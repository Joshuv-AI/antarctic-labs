Spatio-temporal dynamics, trend analysis, and incidence prediction of echinococcosis in Xinjiang, China (2005–2024)
Authors: Lingyu Jiang, Liping Zhang, Yang Yang, Kaisar Tursunjiang, Xuehui Cao, Yarikun Maimaitiyiming, Guangzhong Shi, Adili Simayi, Jiangshan Zhao, Li Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveGiven the persistent transmission and marked spatial heterogeneity of echinococcosis in endemic settings, a comprehensive spatiotemporal assessment is warranted. This study aimed to characterize the spatiotemporal epidemiology of echinococcosis in the Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region from 2005 to 2024, develop a time-series forecasting framework, and inform region-specific prevention and control strategies.MethodsSpatiotemporal clustering analysis was conducted to delineate geographic heterogeneity and dynamic transmission patterns at the county level. An interrupted time series framework was employed to estimate changes in the level and slope associated with the implementation of the universal free health examination policy in September 2016. Subsequently, a seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average (SARIMA) model was fitted to characterize temporal autocorrelation structures and seasonal fluctuations and to produce short-term forecasts of incidence trends.ResultsFrom 2005 to 2024, a total of 23,813 echinococcosis cases were reported in Xinjiang, with an average annual incidence of 5.48 per 100,000 population. Incidence increased significantly from January 2005 to August 2016 (β = 0.0026 per 100,000 per month, P < 0.001), with an immediate increase observed in September 2016 (0.2265 per 100,000, P < 0.05), and then declined significantly through December 2024 (β = −0.0069 per 100,000 per month, P < 0.001). Spatial clustering was observed in the Ili and Altay regions. Incidence increased and then decreased across sex and age groups, with larger declines among males. Significant reductions were observed in the 15–44 and ≥ 45 year groups, but not in the 0–14 year group. The optimal first-stage model was SARIMA(1,1,0)(2,0,0)₁₂, and the second-stage model was SARIMA(0,1,1)(1,0,2)₁₂. The second-stage model produced a relative prediction error of − 14.06% and projected 1,040 cases in 2025.ConclusionEchinococcosis incidence in Xinjiang demonstrated a temporal increase followed by a sustained decline, with pronounced spatial heterogeneity across regions. Despite inherent uncertainty, SARIMA-based projections offer evidence to strengthen early warning systems and guide region-specific control strategies.Keywords: Echinococcosis, Spatiotemporal Clustering, ITSA, SARIMA, Incidence Forecasting

Introduction

Echinococcosis is a zoonotic parasitic disease caused by the larval stage of Echinococcus granulosus [1]. Humans acquire infection through ingestion of parasite eggs from contaminated food or water, or through contact with infected canids [2]. The disease is typically chronic and primarily affects the liver, but it may also involve other organs, leading to progressive and potentially fatal complications [3–6]. Despite decades of control efforts, echinococcosis remains widely distributed across pastoral and semi-pastoral regions of Central Asia, South America, the Middle East, parts of Eastern Europe, and western China. Globally, it is estimated to cause approximately 19,300 deaths and 871,000 disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) annually, resulting in economic losses exceeding US$3 billion [7–10]. In recognition of its substantial and persistent burden, the World Health Organization (WHO) has designated echinococcosis as a neglected tropical disease requiring intensified and sustained control strategies [11, 12]. However, in many endemic settings, rapid livestock expansion, environmental variability, and uneven access to veterinary services continue to challenge efforts to control transmission, resulting in marked spatial heterogeneity and recurrent local transmission [13].

China bears one of the heaviest global burdens of echinococcosis. Between 2004 and 2022, 89,844 cases were reported in m ainland China (average annual incidence: 3.45 per million) [14]. The disease is highly concentrated in pastoral and semi-pastoral areas across seven provincial-level regions—Xinjiang, Qinghai, Tibet, Ningxia, Gansu, Sichuan, and Inner Mongolia—which together cover approximately 44% of the national territory and place an estimated 66 million people at risk [15, 16]. Although overall incidence in western China has declined in recent years, reductions have not been uniform, and moderate-to-high endemicity persists in certain high-altitude pastoral communities [17]. Since its designation as a Class C notifiable infectious disease in 2005, nationwide control measures—including community screening, standardized treatment, and regular canine deworming—have reduced population-level prevalence from approximately 1% to 0.3% [18]. Nevertheless, an estimated 50,000–60,000 active cases remain, with approximately 20 million people still at risk and canine infection rates reaching 10%–20% in key endemic counties, indicating ongoing transmission pressure and the need for more targeted and spatially refined control strategies [19].

Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, one of China’s largest pastoral areas, is characterized by extensive livestock production, frequent human–animal contact, and ecological conditions conducive to parasite transmission [20–22]. Since September 2016, a region-wide free health examination program covering all age groups has enhanced early case detection. However, broad and non-targeted allocation of public health resources may limit intervention precision, particularly in settings with substantial spatial heterogeneity. Although previous studies have described temporal trends or spatial patterns at national or provincial scales, comprehensive analyses integrating long-term temporal dynamics, fine-scale spatial clustering, and short-term forecasting at the county level in Xinjiang remain scarce [23]. This gap constrains the identification of priority areas and limits the ability to anticipate future epidemic trajectories.

Therefore, a systematic investigation of the spatiotemporal characteristics of echinococcosis in Xinjiang from 2005 to 2024 was conducted. Specifically, the study aimed to: (1) characterize county-level temporal trends and spatial clustering patterns; and (2) develop time-series models to predict short-term incidence. By integrating descriptive epidemiology, spatial analysis, and forecasting approaches, this study provides evidence to inform precision-based intervention strategies and optimize resource allocation in endemic regions.

Materials and methods

Study area

The Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, one of China’s five provincial-level autonomous regions, is located in northwestern China in the central Eurasian continent (34°25′–48°10′N, 73°40′–96°18′E). The study area comprised 96 county-level administrative units, including counties, county-level cities, and autonomous counties. Xinjiang covers approximately 1,664,900 km² and had a permanent resident population of 26.23 million at the end of 2024. The region has a temperate continental arid climate and a characteristic topography described as “three mountain ranges surrounding two basins” (Fig. 1).

A Schematic representation of the location of the study area. The map shows the location of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region within China (inset) and the boundaries of the 96 county-level administrative units included in the study

Data sources

Echinococcosis case data for the Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region and its 96 county-level administrative units from January 1, 2005 to December 31, 2024 were obtained from the Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region Center for Disease Control and Prevention. Cases were diagnosed according to the Diagnostic Criteria for Echinococcosis (WS 257–2006) (https://cspd.ipd.org.cn/standardv81.html), which incorporates epidemiological history, clinical manifestations, imaging findings, and laboratory test results. Population data were obtained from the National Bureau of Statistics of China. Base map data were acquired from the National Geographic Information Public Service Platform (Tianditu; map approval number: GS(2024)0650) (https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/pcsj/).

Statistical analysis

Spatial clustering analysis

Spatial clustering analysis was performed to identify geographic and temporal patterns of echinococcosis incidence. Choropleth maps were generated using the natural breaks (Jenks) classification method to visualize spatial variation and highlight high-incidence areas. Kernel density estimati on (KDE), a nonparametric spatial smoothing approach, was applied to produce continuous density surfaces from discrete case data and to identify spatial aggregation hotspots. Three-dimensional trend analysis was conducted to integrate spatial coordinates (X–Y) and incidence values (Z) within a unified framework, enabling visualization of overall spatial trends. For spatio-temporal analysis, incidence data were aggregated into regular space–time cubes stored in NetCDF format, where geographic coordinates (x, y) defined spatial location and time steps (t) represented the temporal dimension [24]. Each cube recorded the corresponding incidence rate. Local outlier analysis was performed using Anselin’s Local Moran’s I to detect significant spatial clusters and outliers (high–high, high–low, low–high, and low–low). Emerging spatio-temporal hotspot analysis was conducted using the Getis–Ord Gi statistic combined with the Mann–Kendall trend test to classify areas into patterns such as sporadic, persistent, intensifying, or diminishing hotspots.

The continuous variable time was coded sequentially from 0 (January 2005), increasing by 1 unit per month. The binary intervention variable was coded as 0 before September 2016 and as 1 thereafter. The variable postslope represented the number of months after the intervention and was coded as 0 before September 2016 and increased sequentially thereafter. Statistical significance of the regression coefficients was assessed to evaluate changes in both incidence level and trend. Autocorrelation was examined using the Breusch–Godfrey test, and when detected, robust standard errors were estimated using the Newey–West method. Residual autocorrelation was assessed using the Breusch–Godfrey test. Significant serial correlation was detected in the ITS models; therefore, heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent standard errors were estimated using the Newey–West correction. Seasonal Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (SARIMA) modeling

The time series exhibited both long-term trends and seasonal patterns. To account for these characteristics and improve forecasting performance, a Seasonal Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (SARIMA) model was constructed. The general form of the model is expressed as SARIMA(p, d, q)(P, D, Q)s, where sss denotes the seasonal periodicity. The non-seasonal parameters include p (autoregressive order), d (order of non-seasonal differencing), and q (moving average order). The seasonal parameters include P (seasonal autoregressive order), D (order of seasonal differencing), and Q (seasonal moving average order). To formally determine whether structural changes existed in the long-term incidence series, breakpoint analysis based on the Bai–Perron methodology was performed using the strucchange package in R. A significant structural change was detected (SupF = 55.24, P < 0.001), with candidate breakpoints occurring around September 2016 and late 2017. Considering the delayed epidemiological response following implementation of the universal free health examination policy and the stabilization of post-intervention incidence trends, January 2018 was selected as the start of Stage 2. Although the ITS analysis identified September 2016 as the policy intervention point, using this same cut-off for SARIMA segmentation would conflate the modeling objectives and include the transitional 2016–2017 period characterized by ascertainment-driven incidence inflation. January 2018 was therefore chosen to ensure Stage 2 reflects the post-stabilization trend. Accordingly, the monthly incidence series was divided into Stage 1 (January 2005–December 2017) and Stage 2 (January 2018–December 2024), and separate SARIMA models were fitted for each stage. Forecasting performance was evaluated using multiple indicators, including the root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and relative prediction error. Model adequacy was assessed using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and Ljung–Box test of residual autocorrelation.

Statistical analysis

Data cleaning and preprocessing were performed using Microsoft Excel 2019 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Spatial clustering analyses were conducted using ArcGIS Pro version 3.5 (Esri, Redlands, CA, USA). Interrupted time series analysis and Seasonal Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (SARIMA) modeling were performed using R software version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The threshold for statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Incidence overview

From 2005 to 2024, a total of 23,813 echinococcosis cases were reported in Xinjiang, corresponding to an average annual incidence of 5.48 per 100,000 population (range: 1.95–11.07 per 100,000). The incidence increased from 1.95 per 100,000 in 2005 to a peak of 11.07 per 100,000 in 2017, followed by a gradual decline. A marked rise was observed after 2016, coinciding with the implementation of the universal free health examination policy. Overall, a significant upward trend was identified during the study period (Z = 71.584, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Echinococcosis incidence in Xinjiang, 2005–2024

During 2005–2024, all 96 counties/districts in Xinjiang reported echinococcosis cases. Low-incidence areas (0.645–3.600 per 100,000 population) were primarily located in southern Xinjiang, including Hotan Prefecture, Kashgar Prefecture, and several counties in Aksu Prefecture. In contrast, medium-to-high incidence areas (14.319–46.003 per 100,000) were predominantly distributed across northern Xinjiang, particularly in Ili Kazakh Autonomous Prefecture, Altay Prefecture, and Tacheng Prefecture, forming evident high-incidence clusters. Kernel density estimation further identified spatial aggregation hotspots. High-density areas (0.166–0.322) were concentrated in western Ili Prefecture and eastern Changji Hui Autonomous Prefecture, forming two core hotspot regions. Medium-to-high density areas (0.080–0.165) exhibited a belt-like distribution along the northern slopes of the Tianshan Mountains, corresponding closely to the pastoral regions of northern Xinjiang (Fig. 3).

Epidemiological characteristics

Diagnostic testing indicated substantial residual autocorrelation in the ITS models. The Breusch–Godfrey test confirmed significant serial correlation (LM = 92.70, df = 4, P < 0.001). Accordingly, Newey–West heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent standard errors were applied to obtain robust estimates of intervention effects.

Temporal distribution

Interrupted time series analysis with Newey–West standard errors (lag = 4) was conducted to assess monthly echinococcosis incidence from January 2005 to December 2024. Before the intervention (January 2005–August 2016), incidence increased significantly by 0.0026 per 100,000 population per month (P < 0.001). In September 2016, a significant immediate increase of 0.2265 per 100,000 population was observed (P = 0.035). After the intervention (September 2016–December 2024), incidence declined significantly by 0.0069 per 100,000 population per month (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4). Overall, the intervention was associated with an immediate rise in incidence followed by a sustained downward trend.

Spatial distribution

A spatiotemporal cube was constructed using 96 counties/districts and annual time steps, generating 1,920 space–time bins with corresponding incidence values. The Mann–Kendall test indicated no significant overall temporal trend (Z = 1.1356, P > 0.05). Incidence was classified into five levels (0–6.649, 6.650–17.065, 17.066–31.869, 31.870–56.520, and 56.521–112.653 per 100,000 population) using the natural breaks method. The three-dimensional trend surface showed a spatial gradient, with incidence increasing from north to south and decreasing from west to east. High-incidence areas were concentrated in Ili and Altay, with higher incidence in the north and west than in the south and east (Fig. 5).

Population distribution

Gender-stratified analysis showed no significant differences in pre-intervention trends (January 2005–August 2016) between males and females. At the time of policy implementation, incidence increased in both groups; this increase was statistically significant in males (β₂ = 0.1383, P < 0.05) but not in females (β₂ = 0.0882, P = 0.0654). After policy implementation (September 2016–December 2024), incidence declined significantly in both males and females, decreasing by 0.0037 and 0.0032 per 100,000 per month, respectively (P < 0.001 for both).

Age-stratified analysis showed no significant differences in pre-intervention trends across age groups. Following policy implementation, no significant change was observed in the 0–14 age group (P > 0.05). In contrast, significant declines were observed in the 15–44 age group (β = −0.0040 per month, P < 0.001) and the ≥ 45 age group (− 0.0029 per month, P < 0.001) (Fig. 8).

Structural break analysis

Structural breakpoint analysis identified significant changes in the monthly echinococcosis incidence series. The Bai–Perron procedure suggested several candidate breakpoints, including September 2016 and late 2017. SupF testing confirmed the existence of statistically significant structural change (SupF = 55.24, P < 0.001). Considering the lagged epidemiological effects of the universal free health examination policy and the stabilization of post-intervention trends, January 2018 was selected as the beginning of Stage 2 for subsequent SARIMA modeling.

SARIMA model

Stationarity and differencing

Based on the structural breakpoint analysis, the monthly incidence series was divided into two stages. The time series was divided into two stages: January 2005–December 2017 (Stage 1) and January 2018–December 2024 (Stage 2). Visual inspection indicated clear seasonal patterns in both stages. Stage 1 showed a persistent upward trend, whereas Stage 2 demonstrated a marked decline after 2018, followed by low-level fluctuations (Fig. 9). Stationarity of the raw series in each stage was assessed using the Augmented Dickey–Fuller, KPSS, and Phillips–Perron tests. Both series were non-stationary (P > 0.05). After first-order differencing (d = 1), stationarity was achieved (P < 0.05). Box–Ljung tests performed on the residuals yielded P-values > 0.05, indicating that the residuals approximated white noise and satisfied the assumptions for SARIMA modeling.

Time series decomposition of echinococcosis incidence in Xinjiang, 2005–2024

Model identification

Model identification was conducted based on the minimization of the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The optimal model for Stage 1 (2005–2017) was SARIMA(1,1,0)(2,0,0)₁₂, whereas the optimal model for Stage 2 (2018–2024) was SARIMA(0,1,1)(1,0,2)₁₂.

Model diagnostics

The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values were 1356.54 for the Stage 1 SARIMA model and 783.83 for the Stage 2 SARIMA model. Box–Ljung tests applied to the residuals were non-significant (Stage 1: P = 0.843; Stage 2: P = 0.570), indicating that the residuals approximated white noise. Maximum-likelihood estimation showed that the seasonal parameters in both models were statistically significant (P < 0.05), suggesting that the selected model structures were appropriate (Table 3).

Forecast performance and application

The SARIMA model was applied to the Stage 2 data (2018–2024) to fit the reported echinococcosis cases in Xinjiang. The fitted values closely followed the observed temporal trend and reproduced the major seasonal peaks and troughs (Fig. 10). For 2024, comparison of observed and predicted monthly cases yielded a relative error of -14.06%, corresponding to an underestimation of 149 cases. Based on the fitted model, the projected number of echinococcosis cases in Xinjiang for 2025 was 1040, representing a decrease of 1.61% (17 cases) compared with 2024. The seasonal peak was projected to occur in spring, with the highest monthly incidence in March (102 cases) (Table 4). The largest monthly deviation occurred in September 2024 (− 47.82%), coinciding with the traditional post-summer pastoral season. This episodic peak likely reflects unmodeled transmission events or post-COVID-19 surveillance normalization that exceeded the historical seasonal pattern captured by the 2018–2023 training data.

Discussion

Echinococcosis remains a significant public health challenge in Xinjiang. Between 2005 and 2024, the annual incidence ranged from 1.95 to 11.07 per 100,000 population, with a mean of 5.48 per 100,000. The overall trend was characterized by an initial increase followed by a decline, peaking in 2017 and subsequently stabilizing at relatively low levels. This pattern is consistent with the national epidemiological profile [25]. The observed trend may reflect the cumulative effects of Xinjiang’s comprehensive control program implemented over the past two decades, which has emphasized source control, improved management of intermediate hosts, and enhancements in water safety and health education [18, 26]. According to the Xinjiang Statistical Yearbook, the average annual growth rate of cattle and sheep stocks from 2005 to 2017 was approximately 4.5%, increasing to 5.6% during the 12th Five-Year Plan period (2010–2015), exceeding the national average for the same period [27]. Rapid livestock expansion may have increased challenges in dog management in pastoral areas, thereby sustaining transmission risk.

The incidence peak in 2017 may be related to enhanced case detection following the implementation of the “Nationwide Free Health Examination” policy in September 2016, which expanded screening coverage and facilitated identification of previously asymptomatic or mild infections. From 2017 to 2024, incidence declined, paralleling the post-intervention decrease reported for brucellosis in Ningxia [28]. During this period, sustained governmental investment supported integrated interventions, including regular dog deworming, livestock vaccination, health education, and active screening [29–31]. Between 2020 and 2022, control activities were partially disrupted due to resource reallocation during the COVID-19 pandemic [32]. Nevertheless, incidence remained relatively low (4.16 per 100,000 in 2020, 3.72 in 2021, and 3.21 in 2022). A slight rebound was observed in 2023 (4.06 per 100,000) and 2024 (4.58 per 100,000). This rebound warrants attention and may reflect restoration of routine surveillance in the post-pandemic period rather than true resurgence. It should be noted, however, that the ITS analysis evaluates the aggregate epidemiological impact of the broader control program rather than the specific contribution of the free health examination policy alone. Given that multiple concurrent interventions — including dog deworming, livestock vaccination, health education, and poverty-alleviation relocation — were implemented during the same period, attributing the observed decline to any single measure is unwarranted. Continued emphasis on early screening, health education, and integrated control measures remains essential [33].

Temporal and spatial patterns were first described using conventional graphical methods, and spatial aggregation was assessed through kernel density analysis. To overcome the limitations of single-dimensional approaches, spatiotemporal clustering (STC) and three-dimensional trend analysis were applied to integrate temporal continuity with spatial structure. A clear spatial gradient was identified, with higher incidence in northern and western Xinjiang and pronounced clustering in pastoral prefectures such as Ili and Altay. This distribution may be associated with livestock density, ecological conditions, and pastoral production systems that favor transmission [30]. Whether the persistent high-high clusters in Ili and Altay reflect differences in the implementation intensity of dog deworming or screening programs warrants further investigation. Future studies with county-level intervention coverage data should formally test whether regional heterogeneity in policy delivery contributes to these spatial patterns. Persistent high-incidence areas require strengthened dog deworming, source control, and standardized slaughter practices. Counties with declining trends should maintain routine management and active screening, whereas areas with recent increases warrant enhanced surveillance and timely adjustments to interventions. Such a tiered approach may support precision-based control of echinococcosis [34–36].

Incidence trends were comparable between males and females, although the decline was more pronounced among males. This difference may reflect greater male participation in high-risk activities, including pastoral work, slaughtering, and animal handling, which increase exposure to infection [37]. The 2016 free health examination policy was associated with a larger reduction in males. Age-stratified analysis showed the greatest decline in the 15–44 year group (β = −0.0040 per month), followed by the ≥ 45-year group (β = −0.0029 per month), whereas no significant decrease was observed among children aged 0–14 years. This finding suggests that, despite the overall reduction in reported incidence following implementation of the free health examination program, transmission risk among children may not have declined to the same extent as in adults. Several explanations may account for this pattern. Children in pastoral and semi-pastoral communities often have frequent contact with domestic dogs and contaminated environments, while preventive behaviors such as hand hygiene and food safety practices may be less consistently maintained. Furthermore, although the free health examination program may improve case detection and treatment, it does not directly interrupt environmental transmission pathways. Consequently, reductions in incidence may occur more slowly among children than among adults. Given that childhood infection may indicate ongoing local transmission, the absence of a significant decline in the 0–14 year age group highlights the need for strengthened prevention efforts. School-based health education, regular ultrasound screening in endemic communities, and sustained canine management programs should be prioritized to reduce exposure and support long-term disease control [38].

In this study, optimal SARIMA models were identified using the auto.arima() function in R (version 4.5.1) based on stage-specific incidence trends. For the first stage (2005–2017), the SARIMA(1,1,0)(2,0,0)₁₂ model was selected, whereas the second stage (2018–2024) was best fitted by the SARIMA(0,1,1)(1,0,2)₁₂ model. Application of the stage-two model demonstrated that predicted values for 2024 were slightly lower than observed counts (relative error ≈ − 14.06%), while accurately capturing seasonal peaks and troughs. Forecasting for 2025 indicated a continued downward trend, with the seasonal peak projected between February and April. Exceedance of the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval may serve as an early warning signal, supporting timely intensification of dog deworming and human screening. Because strong serial correlation was detected in the interrupted time-series models, Newey–West corrected standard errors were used to improve the robustness of statistical inference and reduce the risk of underestimated standard errors.

Several limitations should be considered. First, all analyses were conducted using county-level aggregated surveillance data. Therefore, the observed associations reflect population-level patterns and should not be interpreted as evidence of individual-level risk. Potential ecological fallacy cannot be excluded. Second, changes in diagnostic capacity, reporting practices, screening coverage, and healthcare accessibility over the 20-year study period may have influenced the observed temporal and spatial patterns. In particular, the implementation of the universal free health examination program after 2016 likely increased case ascertainment and may partially explain the temporary rise in reported incidence. Furthermore, age-specific data on screening participation rates, follow-up rates, and dog deworming coverage at the county level were unavailable, limiting our ability to quantitatively examine the mechanisms underlying the non-significant decline in incidence among children aged 0–14 years. Third, the present study focused primarily on descriptive spatiotemporal surveillance analysis. Advanced spatial modeling approaches such as geographically weighted regression, spatial lag models, or Bayesian spatiotemporal models were not applied because the primary objective was to characterize epidemiological patterns rather than identify causal mechanisms. Future studies integrating environmental, livestock, socioeconomic, and intervention-related variables may provide deeper insight into the determinants of spatial heterogeneity. Fourth, the SARIMA framework assumes time-series stationarity and is primarily suitable for short-term forecasting. Although the model demonstrated moderate predictive performance (RMSE = 24.81, MAE = 20.74, and MAPE = 22.12%), prediction errors remained substantial in certain months, indicating that abrupt epidemiological fluctuations may not be fully captured by historical temporal patterns alone. Continuous model updating and incorporation of additional explanatory variables may further improve forecasting accuracy. No formal comparison with alternative time-series models (e.g., ETS, TBATS, or Prophet) or rolling-forecast validation was conducted, which limits assessment of the SARIMA model’s relative performance. Future studies should evaluate these approaches systematically.

Overall, the SARIMA model provides a practical and cost-efficient approach for short-term forecasting of echinococcosis incidence in Xinjiang. Integration of such models into routine surveillance systems may facilitate early detection of abnormal fluctuations and inform targeted prevention and control strategies.

Future research should integrate multi-source data, including meteorological, zoonotic surveillance, and socioeconomic variables [39]. Incorporating interpretable machine learning and mixed-effects models may improve predictive accuracy and clarify the relative contributions of key determinants, such as climate factors, canine infection prevalence, and livestock density [40–42]. Within the One Health framework, strengthened cross-sector collaboration may further enhance coordinated and sustainable control strategies, advancing toward integrated disease prevention and management [43].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that echinococcosis in Xinjiang has undergone a transition from increasing to declining incidence over the past two decades, while maintaining pronounced seasonal patterns and substantial spatial heterogeneity. County-level analyses identified persistent high-risk areas and vulnerable populations, particularly children in dispersed rural settings, underscoring the need for spatially targeted and age-specific interventions.

By integrating spatiotemporal analysis with time-series modeling, this study provides a structured framework for monitoring epidemic dynamics and anticipating short-term trends. The findings support the use of model-informed surveillance to enhance early warning capacity and to guide precision-based prevention and control strategies in endemic regions.
