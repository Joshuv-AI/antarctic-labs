The clinical evidence and strategic use of hyaluronidase in spinal interventions
Authors: Jae Hun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyaluronidase is an enzyme that degrades hyaluronic acid within the extracellular matrix, thereby increasing tissue permeability and facilitating the dispersion of injected agents. Hyaluronidase has been used as an adjunct in spinal interventions to improve drug delivery, enhance adhesiolysis, and overcome therapeutic limitations imposed by epidural fibrosis and scar tissue. This review summarizes the pharmacological properties, clinical evidence, safety considerations, and practical applications of hyaluronidase in spinal pain management. The available evidence suggests that hyaluronidase enhances the distribution of local anesthetics, corticosteroids, and other therapeutic agents within fibrotic or compartmentalized tissues. Clinical studies have demonstrated improved pain relief and functional outcomes when hyaluronidase was incorporated into lumbar interlaminar, caudal, and transforaminal epidural injections, particularly in patients with failed back surgery syndrome. However, evidence regarding its role in percutaneous epidural neuroplasty and adhesiolysis remains inconsistent, with some studies reporting sustained benefits, whereas others have not demonstrated significant independent effects beyond mechanical adhesiolysis. Although generally well-tolerated, rare hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis, have been reported, necessitating careful patient selection and monitoring. Current evidence supports the use of hyaluronidase as a valuable adjunct rather than a stand-alone therapeutic option in selected spinal interventions. Future well-designed prospective studies are needed to clarify its independent therapeutic contribution, optimize dosing strategies, and establish evidence-based clinical guidelines for its use in interventional pain management.

INTRODUCTION

Hyaluronidase is an enzyme that degrades hyaluronic acid (HA), a key component of the extracellular matrix. Reducing tissue viscosity facilitates the dispersion of injected fluids across the connective tissue planes. Originally identified in the venom of various species—including snakes, scorpions, and honeybees—it functions as a “spreading factor” that promotes the dissemination of toxins [1,2].

Addressing epidural fibrosis and mechanical adhesions in interventional pain management remains a persistent clinical challenge. Chronic inflammation, degenerative spinal disease, and previous spinal surgery often result in the formation of dense connective tissue within the epidural space. Fibrosis creates anatomical compartmentalization, acting as a physical barrier that restricts the delivery of therapeutic agents, such as local anesthetics and corticosteroids, to inflamed targeted nerve roots.

Given these challenges, hyaluronidase has emerged as a promising adjunct in spinal interventions. By enhancing drug dispersion within connective tissues and facilitating access to target neural structures, the therapeutic efficacy of co-administered anesthetics and adhesiolytic agents can be augmented [3-5]. Its application spans all spinal segments, including cervical, thoracic, lumbar, and sacral regions [6-8].

Despite its potential, the use of hyaluronidase in spinal procedures remains controversial [9,10]. Although accumulating clinical evidence suggests improvements in outcomes following epidural injections and enhanced adhesiolysis [5,10,11], there is heterogeneity in clinical practice regarding the optimal dosage, preparation type (animal-derived versus recombinant human formulations), and potential pharmacological interactions with co-administered drugs. Furthermore, a standardized consensus regarding its clinical application and precise role in various interventional settings is lacking.

Therefore, this review aims to provide a comprehensive summary of the mechanistic rationale, clinical evidence, and safety considerations, including the hypersensitivity profiles associated with the use of hyaluronidase in spinal interventions.

PHARMACOLOGIC CHARACTERISTICS AND MECHANISMS

The core pharmacological effect of hyaluronidase is the enzymatic degradation of HA, which increases tissue permeability and facilitates fluid dispersion through the connective tissue planes [12,13]. This action can provide a mechanistic basis for the accelerated diffusion and increased absorption observed with many injected drugs, including those commonly used in epidural procedures [13]. Experimental data and a literature review indicate that hyaluronidase has anti-inflammatory effects, including reduced neutrophil infiltration at inflammatory sites, a possible role in reducing scar formation, and reduced post-procedural pain [13-16].

Hyaluronidase has been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for several clinical applications. It is primarily used to increase the absorption and dispersion of other injected drugs and for hypodermoclysis. Furthermore, it is indicated for subcutaneous urography to improve the resorption of radio-opaque agents, and ovine hyaluronidase has been specifically approved for the treatment of vitreous hemorrhage [5]. It is also used off-label for edema reduction and adhesiolysis in spinal interventions [4,5].

The pharmacokinetic profile and metabolic pathways of exogenously administered hyaluronidase remain poorly characterized [5]. Clinical observations indicate a rapid serum clearance, with a half-life (t1/2) of 2.1 ± 0.2 min, followed by subsequent hepatic and renal inactivation [17]. While the systemic elimination pathway is relatively well understood, the specific mechanism of local inactivation within the dermal and interstitial spaces continues to be a subject of investigation. The localized effect of hyaluronidase is transient and is limited by the continuous physiological synthesis of HA [18]. Clinical data have demonstrated that hyaluronidase-induced alterations in the interstitial matrix are fully reversible within 48 h in adults [17].

HYALURONIDASE ACTIVITY AND DRUG INTERACTIONS

In spinal interventions, hyaluronidase is rarely used as monotherapy; it is typically administered in combination with other pharmacological agents. Hyaluronidase activity is not fixed and can be modified by mixing other agents with the injectate [19]. Anti-inflammatory agents such as indomethacin, dexamethasone, salicylates, heparin, vitamin C, and antioxidants have been reported to antagonize hyaluronidase activity, suggesting that larger doses may be required when these compounds are present [5]. According to an in vitro study, contrast media and 10% sodium chloride reduced hyaluronidase activity, whereas local anesthetics, such as ropivacaine, exerted no significant influence, and triamcinolone had a slightly positive influence on enzymatic activity [19]. Normal saline appeared to have the highest hyaluronidase activity among commonly used solutions [19]. Hyaluronidase activity increased in a dose-dependent manner but not proportionally; as the dose increased, incremental increases in activity appeared to diminish [19]. Therefore, the efficacy of hyaluronidase depends on both the dose and the composition of the injectate.

Currently, there is a paucity of evidence-based guidelines regarding the optimal hyaluronidase dosage for spinal interventions. While clinical practice allows for variations in dosage such as 150–3,000 IU [20,21], a dose of 1,500 IU is commonly employed [13,22-29].

In a recent comparative study by Safran et al. [30], the degradation efficacies of animal-derived (ovine) and recombinant human hyaluronidases were evaluated for various HA fillers. The authors demonstrated that both enzymes possessed comparable overall dissolving capacities, although minor variations in dissolution kinetics were observed, depending on the cross-linking profile of the substrate. Although lower concentrations (100 IU) were sufficient for rapidly degradable fillers, highly cross-linked or high-concentration formulations exhibited substantial resistance, requiring higher enzymatic concentrations (300 IU) to achieve complete degradation within 50 to 60 min [30]. The authors demonstrated that animal-derived (ovine) and recombinant human hyaluronidases exhibited comparable overall efficacies in degrading HA fillers [30].

SAFETY AND ALLERGY

In Korea, hyaluronidase is commonly used in nerve blocks and spinal interventions, and some physicians have observed allergic reactions to hyaluronidase in their patients. Hypersensitivity to hyaluronidase is generally uncommon, with a reported rate of < 0.1% [2]. In high-volume clinical settings, the reported incidence was 6 per 100,000 after the first exposure and 16 per 100,000 after repeated exposure, suggesting that prior sensitization may increase the risk of hypersensitivity [31]. Simultaneously, laboratory observations suggested that hyaluronidase itself does not elicit immune responses and that some reactions to animal-derived products may reflect contamination with proteases or immunoglobulins rather than the purified enzyme alone [3,32].

Preclinical studies evaluating the safety of recombinant human hyaluronidase (rHuPH20) in animal models, including rats and monkeys, reported no significant adverse immune reactions across intravenous, ocular, and dermal routes of administration [5]. While minor renal tubule dilation was observed in rats treated with high doses (10,500 units), no systemic toxicity or immunogenic complications were identified [5].

Clinicians must remain vigilant. A Korean case report documented a rare instance of anaphylactic shock following epidural administration of hyaluronidase, even in a patient without prior exposure [33].

Documented allergic complications include localized cutaneous manifestations such as erythema, pruritus, urticaria, and angioedema [32]. Notably, periorbital edema or local swelling is the most frequent clinical presentation, often leading to misdiagnosis as simple postprocedural inflammation [32]. A review of 106 cases revealed a mean age of 54.2 years, female predominance (74%), and a history of previous exposure in 70% of patients, with sensitization intervals ranging from 2 weeks to 5 years [32]. Regarding the timing of hypersensitivity, approximately two-thirds of reactions are delayed, occurring 12–120 h after exposure; 18% are immediate (within 5 min), and 16% are early reactions (within 2 h) [32]. Statistical analysis indicated that the number of prior exposures was not significantly associated with the timing of allergic reactions (P = 0.3) [32].

Management typically involves the oral, topical, or intravenous administration of corticosteroids, often in combination with antihistamines [32]. Given these findings, careful history-taking and post-procedural observation are essential, especially when risk factors are present, and the use of recombinant human preparations should be considered when available [32].

Finally, hyaluronidase-related allergy may be associated with hypersensitivity to Hymenoptera (bee or wasp) venom [2,32]. Therefore, clinicians should avoid administering hyaluronidase to patients with a known history of allergy to bee or wasp venom [2].

EPIDURAL USE IN NERVE BLOCK AND NEUROPLASTY

The rationale for the epidural use of hyaluronidase is to disrupt extracellular ground substances within epidural adhesions, increase capillary permeability, and facilitate the spread of injectates across scarred or compartmentalized epidural spaces [3,34]. These properties are particularly attractive in cases of failed back surgery syndrome (FBSS) and postlaminectomy pain, in which fibrosis may limit the efficacy of standard epidural steroid injections [3,4]. Borg and Krijnen [35] demonstrated the clinical efficacy of hyaluronidase in the management of recurrent radicular pain secondary to postoperative scarring. Their case report showed that the targeted infiltration of hyaluronidase into the scar tissue provided substantial and progressively sustained pain relief, demonstrating its safety and utility as an adjuvant therapy. In this case report, the authors repeated transforaminal injections of hyaluronidase, which resulted in a dose-dependent improvement in both pain intensity and physical function. While the initial 150 IU injection showed limited efficacy, increasing the dose to 600 IU progressively extended the period of pain reduction from 0 to several weeks, with the baseline visual analogue scale (VAS) score decreasing from 7 to 2. Concurrently, the straight leg raising (SLR) restriction, which was initially limited to 30°, completely resolved to an unrestricted range following the final administration [35].

However, apart from the studies discussed below, prospective studies and randomized controlled trials specifically evaluating the direct adhesiolytic effects of hyaluronidase remain scarce.

Interlaminar lumbar epidural block

The clinical evidence regarding the adjunctive use of hyaluronidase in various conventional epidural steroid injections is summarized in Table 1.

Kim et al. [36] conducted a randomized controlled trial to investigate the therapeutic efficacy of adding hyaluronidase to epidural steroid injections for FBSS management. They randomly allocated 60 patients to three distinct treatment arms. Their findings revealed that the combined administration of 1,500 IU of hyaluronidase and triamcinolone achieved the most pronounced and sustained reduction in low back pain and disability at 12 weeks, outperforming the respective monotherapy (triamcinolone or hyaluronidase) groups [36]. At 6 weeks, more than a 50% reduction in pain was observed in 5/20 patients (25.0%) in the triamcinolone group, 1/17 patients (5.9%) in the hyaluronidase group, and 10 patients (43.5%) in the combination group. At 12 weeks, this was observed in 0 patients in the triamcinolone and hyaluronidase groups compared to 12 patients (52.2%) in the combination group [36]. The proportion of patients with > 50% pain reduction was higher in the combination group than in the triamcinolone and hyaluronidase groups at both 6 and 12 weeks [36]. These results underscore a potential synergistic effect when hyaluronidase is combined with corticosteroids, rather than being utilized as a stand-alone agent.

In a related randomized controlled trial involving 61 patients with severe low back pain and sciatica, Kim et al. [37] used a similar three-arm design (triamcinolone, hyaluronidase, and combination groups) to evaluate the additional benefit of hyaluronidase. There was no statistically significant difference in the reduction of VAS scores among the three groups at 2, 4, and 8 weeks of follow-up [37]. However, in the combination group, the functional improvement (Oswestry Disability Index, ODI) remained significantly superior at 8 weeks [37].

Caudal epidural block

In a prospective, double-blind, randomized controlled trial, Yousef et al. [8] evaluated the efficacy of incorporating 1,500 IU of hyaluronidase into fluoroscopically guided caudal epidural injections in patients with FBSS. The study randomized 38 patients to receive either a baseline regimen of bupivacaine, methylprednisolone, and 3% hypertonic saline or the same regimen supplemented with hyaluronidase [8]. The rationale for adjuvant therapy is based on the ability of the enzyme to depolymerize HA and disrupt the proteoglycan matrix of epidural adhesions, thereby enhancing the spread of therapeutic agents and reducing the effects of postoperative perineural fibrosis [8].

While both groups demonstrated significant reduction in pain intensity during the short-term follow-up period (6 weeks to 3 months), sustained clinical benefits at 6 and 12 months were observed only in the hyaluronidase group [8]. Furthermore, patients receiving hyaluronidase exhibited significant long-term improvements in lumbar range of motion and a sustained reduction in opioid consumption throughout the 1-year study period, whereas the clinical gains in the control group were largely limited to the initial three months [8]. Although minor adverse events, such as rash and pruritus, were more frequent in the hyaluronidase group, no serious complications were reported [8]. These findings suggest that when integrated into an adhesiolysis-oriented caudal protocol, hyaluronidase contributes to durable analgesic and functional outcomes by facilitating drug diffusion to the affected neural structures in patients with FBSS [8].

Transforaminal epidural steroid injection

Rahimzadeh et al. [23] conducted a prospective, double-blind, randomized clinical trial to evaluate the efficacy of hyaluronidase as an adjuvant to transforaminal epidural steroid injection (TFESI) in patients with FBSS. Twenty-five patients with persistent radicular pain after lumbar surgery were randomized to receive either 1,500 IU of hyaluronidase or normal saline combined with a standard regimen of triamcinolone, bupivacaine, or hypertonic saline via a fluoroscopy-guided transforaminal approach [23]. While all patients reported immediate postprocedural pain relief, the hyaluronidase group demonstrated significantly superior clinical outcomes during the follow-up period. Patients receiving hyaluronidase exhibited lower VAS pain scores at 2 and 4 weeks, higher overall satisfaction, and a significantly higher proportion of individuals achieving a ≥ 50% reduction in pain intensity [23]. Furthermore, the inclusion of hyaluronidase was associated with a more frequent conversion to a negative SLR test and a decreased requirement for rescue analgesics [23]. Multivariate analysis identified the addition of hyaluronidase as the only independent predictor of favorable treatment response [23]. The authors proposed that hyaluronidase enhanced the efficacy of TFESI by depolymerizing HA within the epidural scar tissue and adhesions, thereby improving the distribution of corticosteroids to inflamed or tethered nerve roots [23]. Given that the transforaminal route targets a confined periradicular space, even modest improvements in the injectate spread can facilitate more effective decompression. Although the study was limited by a small sample size and a relatively short 4-week follow-up period, these findings suggest that adjunctive hyaluronidase is a potentially valuable additive in carefully selected transforaminal procedures, particularly when significant epidural fibrosis is suspected [23].

Percutaneous epidural neuroplasty and adhesiolysis

Heavner et al. [7] conducted a prospective randomized study (Table 2) to evaluate the contribution of 10% hypertonic saline and hyaluronidase to the clinical outcomes of percutaneous epidural neuroplasty in 83 patients with chronic low back pain and radiculopathy. Participants were allocated to four treatment groups based on a 3-day protocol: hypertonic saline with or without hyaluronidase and isotonic saline with or without hyaluronidase, all administered in conjunction with epidural local anesthetics and corticosteroids [7]. The study demonstrated that, while all treatment groups achieved clinically meaningful pain reduction that persisted for up to 12 months, the addition of hyaluronidase did not result in a statistically significant improvement in pain outcomes compared to the groups without the enzyme [7]. Similarly, no significant superiority was observed for hypertonic saline over isotonic saline in terms of the raw pain relief scores [7].

However, secondary analyses indicated that patients treated with hypertonic saline, particularly when combined with hyaluronidase, required fewer subsequent interventional pain procedures than those treated with isotonic saline-based regimens [7]. This finding supports the traditional perspective that hyaluronidase acts as an adjunct to augment chemical adhesiolysis within a multicomponent protocol rather than serving as the dominant therapeutic agent alone [7]. Overall, these results suggest that the principal benefit of percutaneous epidural neuroplasty is primarily derived from the mechanical adhesiolysis procedure itself; nonetheless, hyaluronidase remains of interest for its theoretical role in facilitating drug distribution within scarred or fibrotic epidural tissues [7].

Rapčan et al. [20] conducted a randomized, multicenter, double-blind, parallel pilot study to compare mechanical adhesiolysis alone with adhesiolysis combined with epidural administration of corticosteroid and hyaluronidase during epiduroscopy in patients with FBSS. Both groups showed significant short-term improvements at 6 months; however, these gains were largely diminished by 12 months in the mechanical adhesiolysis-only group. Importantly, the group receiving hyaluronidase plus corticosteroids demonstrated more durable benefits, with sustained improvements in back pain and ODI at 12 months [20]. The authors concluded that the addition of hyaluronidase and corticosteroids during epiduroscopy might provide better long-term functional and analgesic outcomes than mechanical adhesiolysis alone [20].

Hsu et al. [38] retrospectively evaluated 115 patients who underwent epidural lysis of adhesions for FBSS or lumbar spinal stenosis to identify the factors associated with treatment success. In this cohort, hyaluronidase was administered in 40.7% of the cases as an adjuvant based on its theoretical ability to degrade HA, increase tissue permeability, and facilitate the spread of injectates within fibrotic epidural spaces [38]. Although 48.7% of the total patient population achieved successful pain relief (defined as a ≥ 50% reduction in pain intensity for at least one month), the use of hyaluronidase was not significantly associated with improved clinical outcomes [8]. Univariable analysis revealed similar success rates between patients who received hyaluronidase and those who did not (52.2% vs. 47.8%; odds ratio 1.2, 95% confidence interval 0.6–2.5, P = 0.65) [38]. Furthermore, although catheter use showed a strong correlation with treatment success, hyaluronidase administration did not significantly influence the overall response [38]. The authors proposed that the mechanical disruption of adhesions achieved through catheter manipulation and high-volume injectate delivery likely outweighed the relatively modest enzymatic effects of hyaluronidase [38]. These findings are consistent with previous prospective evidence, such as a study by Heavner et al. [7], which similarly failed to demonstrate a clear benefit of hyaluronidase during epidural neuroplasty [38]. Given these results and the associated increases in procedural cost, complexity, and potential risks, the authors concluded that the routine incorporation of hyaluronidase into epidural adhesiolysis protocols requires further rigorous investigation [38]. This ongoing discrepancy between biological plausibility and clinical evidence underscores the controversial role of hyaluronidase in epidural interventions [38].

Regarding the procedural use of hyaluronidase in epidural adhesiolysis, Helm et al. [11] reported, citing the clinical experience of Racz, that their integration of the enzyme into neuroplasty protocols was initially based on anecdotal clinical observations rather than large-scale prospective trials. The authors noted that the use of hyaluronidase was inspired by its demonstrated success in managing arachnoiditis-induced neurological dysfunction, where the enzyme was observed to facilitate the spread of therapeutic agents through dense fibrotic tissues [11]. Consequently, they applied this enzymatic approach to address the challenges encountered in the distribution of viscous contrast media in the scarred epidural regions [11]. While these personal observations highlight the potential utility of hyaluronidase in enhancing drug delivery within compartmentalized epidural spaces, the authors acknowledge that further high-quality randomized evidence is required to confirm these clinical benefits and establish a standardized role for the enzyme in modern interventional protocols [11].

The observed inconsistency in hyaluronidase-related outcomes during percutaneous epidural neuroplasty likely stems from the complex interplay between the fundamental mechanisms of the procedure, including the mechanical, chemical, and hydrostatic forces. Percutaneous epidural neuroplasty is designed to achieve perineural adhesiolysis, primarily through catheter manipulation and injectate administration. Notably, the catheter tip position is a critical determinant of efficacy, and clinical evidence suggests that ventral positioning during lumbar percutaneous epidural neuroplasty provides superior therapeutic outcomes compared with dorsal placement, as it facilitates more localized and selective blocks near the dorsal root ganglion and ventral aspect of the nerve root [39]. Regarding the procedural determinants of clinical efficacy, a systematic review reported a significant positive correlation between larger epidural injection volumes and improved outcomes, suggesting that the volume itself may contribute to the therapeutic mechanism [40]. Furthermore, the administration of large-volume injectates exerts a significant hydrostatic effect, promoting the physical expansion of the epidural space and enhancing drug distribution. Given that these mechanical and hydrostatic factors are potent drivers of clinical improvement, they are likely primary determinants of treatment success, thereby minimizing the independent contribution of hyaluronidase. Hsu et al. [38] noted that the mechanical disruption of adhesions achieved through high-volume injectate delivery and catheter manipulation likely outweighs the relatively modest enzymatic effects of hyaluronidase. Consequently, the variability in outcomes across studies is likely a result of differences in procedural techniques, such as catheter tip position, extent of mechanical lysis, and total injection volume, rather than the enzyme itself.

TRIGGER POINT INJECTION (TPI)

TPIs are frequently used to manage spinal pain. Several studies have investigated the use of hyaluronidase in TPI (Table 3).

In a randomized double-blind study, Choi et al. [41] evaluated the clinical efficacy of hyaluronidase as an adjuvant to lidocaine in TPI for myofascial pain syndrome (MPS). While both lidocaine monotherapy and the combination of lidocaine with hyaluronidase yielded significant improvements in pain intensity, disability, and quality of life over a 14-day follow-up period, no significant intergroup differences were observed in the verbal numerical rating scale (VNRS), neck disability index (NDI), or Brief Pain Inventory-Short Form (BPI-SF) scores at the primary endpoints. Notably, the hyaluronidase group demonstrated a faster onset of analgesia, with significant pain reduction observed as early as one day post-injection compared to four days in the lidocaine-only group [41]. The authors attributed this accelerated analgesic effect to the enzymatic reduction of hyaluronan viscosity, which facilitated the diffusion of local anesthetics and enhanced drug distribution within the trigger-point region. Furthermore, hyaluronidase may mitigate post-injection soreness by promoting the resolution of local tissue edema and inflammatory changes [41].

In a randomized, single-blind study, Ghasemi et al. [42] compared the efficacy of ultrasound-guided TPI of bicarbonate, hyaluronidase, and lidocaine in patients with trapezius MPS. While all three interventions resulted in a reduction in pain intensity over time, the analgesic profiles differed significantly; lidocaine provided the most robust immediate relief, whereas hyaluronidase demonstrated the most sustained analgesic effect throughout the 4-week follow-up period [42]. Interestingly, none of the treatment groups showed a significant improvement in cervical range of motion, indicating that the therapeutic benefits were primarily limited to subjective pain reduction rather than objective functional recovery [42]. These findings suggest that hyaluronidase may offer a more durable therapeutic advantage than the traditional lidocaine monotherapy in the management of MPS.

In summary, current evidence suggests that hyaluronidase does not necessarily provide superior improvement in certain objective functional measures, such as cervical range of motion, compared to lidocaine alone [42]; however, it significantly enhances the temporal quality of analgesia [41,42]. The accelerated symptom resolution observed by Choi et al. [41] and the extended duration of pain relief reported by Ghasemi et al. [42] collectively support the versatile role of hyaluronidase in TPI therapy. These findings suggest that the incorporation of hyaluronidase into clinical practice may be a viable strategy for optimizing patient-reported outcomes, offering a distinct analgesic advantage in MPS management.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, hyaluronidase is a biologically plausible and clinically relevant adjunct for interventional pain management, particularly when the therapeutic objective is to overcome tissue resistance in fibrotic or compartmentalized anatomical spaces. Enzymatic degradation of HA consistently enhances the distribution and accessibility of co-administered therapeutic agents, offering a clear theoretical advantage in treating challenging conditions such as FBSS and chronic epidural adhesions.

However, the clinical evidence supporting its routine use remains heterogeneous. While some studies have reported superior functional outcomes for caudal and transforaminal interventions, the efficacy of hyaluronidase for epidural neuroplasty appears to be inextricably intertwined with technical procedural components, specifically catheter tip positioning, mechanical adhesiolysis, and the hydrostatic force of high-volume injectates. Thus, hyaluronidase should not be viewed as a panacea, but rather as a specialized tool whose success depends heavily on rigorous patient selection, optimal injectate composition, and a precise technical approach.

From both clinical and safety perspectives, a selective strategy that prioritizes its use in patients with dense epidural fibrosis refractory to conventional interventions can be recommended. While mindful of potential hypersensitivity, clinicians might find that recombinant human formulations provide a beneficial alternative for reducing risks linked to animal-derived proteins. Although the current evidence does not justify the universal adoption of hyaluronidase for all spinal interventions, it remains a significant evidence-informed adjuvant for targeted therapy. Future research should prioritize prospective trials that isolate the specific enzymatic contribution of hyaluronidase from the mechanical procedural effects to establish standardized and evidence-based dosing recommendations. Until such a consensus is reached, its integration into clinical practice should be guided by the individual pathophysiological constraints of the patient’s pain state.
