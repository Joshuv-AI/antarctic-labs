Challenges in predicting chromatin accessibility differences between species
Authors: Amy Z M Stephen, Arian Raje, Heather H Sestili, Morgan E Wirthlin, Alyssa J Lawler, Ashley R Brown, Junjie Ma, William R Stauffer, Andreas R Pfenning, Irene M Kaplow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDifferences in enhancer activity between species can help drive phenotypic diversity, yet enhancers often have conserved functions despite rapid sequence evolution, posing a challenge for quantifying their functional differences between species. Previous machine learning models have focused on the binary task of predicting differences in the presence of enhancers between species but have yet to demonstrate an ability to predict continuous differences in enhancer activity. Here, we trained convolutional neural networks on a regression task to predict chromatin accessibility—a proxy for enhancer activity—in the liver across five mammals, and we developed a novel framework to evaluate cross-species performance. We demonstrated that training on multiple species improves model generalization to both species used in training and held-out species. However, the models consistently achieved poor performance in predicting quantitative differences in accessibility between species at orthologous regions. Our study highlights the challenges in using regression models to predict chromatin accessibility changes between species. All data and code are available at http://daphne.compbio.cs.cmu.edu/files/azstephe/liver_regression_resource/ and https://figshare.com/projects/liverRegression/274293.

Introduction

Enhancers are transcriptional regulatory elements that loop in three dimensions to transcription start sites to activate gene expression [1]. Within a given tissue, the regulatory activity of an enhancer is shaped by a combinatorial code of transcription factor (TF) binding motifs and other local sequence features [2–5]. Enhancer activity can be approximated by measures of chromatin accessibility from assays like DNase-seq and ATAC-seq, as regions of the genome that are accessible tend to be easily bound by TFs [6, 7].

Unlike protein-coding sequences, which are often highly conserved, enhancer regions often evolve rapidly at the nucleotide level despite their essential roles in development and adult tissue-specific transcriptional regulation [8]. Many phenotypic differences between species and even within species have been linked to changes in enhancer activity that lead to changes in gene expression rather than changes in protein-coding genes [9–11]. Thus, understanding how enhancer function diverges across species is critical for understanding the mechanisms underlying the gene expression differences and downstream phenotypic differences between species.

Although many enhancers with functional conservation lack strong nucleotide-level conservation [8, 12, 13], the conservation of the presence of TF binding motifs and other local sequence features offers an alternative approach for quantifying enhancer activity conservation. Previous studies were able to train machine learning models on sequences underlying candidate enhancers to predict whether an enhancer will be active. They found that these models could make accurate predictions in species not used in training [13, 14], including at locations where the enhancer activity status differed from that of the orthologous region in the training species [13, 16].

A key limitation of these models is their binary treatment of enhancer activity, which does not reflect the continuous nature of chromatin accessibility data [17, 18]. Even for single-cell chromatin accessibility assays, data is typically analyzed by pooling cells that likely come from the same cell type, thus creating quantitative enhancer activity measurements. Many candidate enhancers are present at orthologs across multiple species but have differences in levels of accessibility at these orthologs. Such differences in accessibility might affect gene expression and downstream phenotypes.

To address these limitations, studies have increasingly trained regression-based frameworks that treat enhancer activity as a continuous variable [19–21]. Unlike binary classifiers that output 0 or 1, regression models output a continuous numerical value. This allows them to capture the full range of chromatin accessibility values, providing more precise predictions of transcriptional regulatory strength. However, these models have not been evaluated for their ability to predict chromatin accessibility differences between species.

For example, previous work by Kelley [20] demonstrated that regression models can be trained to predict chromatin accessibility in human and mouse using input sequences that are over 100 kb long. The study found that a model trained using data from two species performs better in both species than a model trained using data from only one. The study suggested that this performance boost is due to the increased genomic diversity of the training data rather than to the increased number of species in the training set. However, the ability of the models presented in this work to predict quantitative differences in chromatin accessibility between species was not evaluated, as this was not the goal of that study. In addition, long sequences cannot be used as input in many current genome assemblies, as these assemblies often consist of scaffolds that are only a few hundred thousand base pairs long [22, 23].

Large-scale initiatives such as the Vertebrate Genomes Project [24] and the Bat1K Project [25] are in the process of building chromosome-level assemblies for hundreds of mammals, but alignments of these genomes have not yet been completed. In addition, the majority of sequenced mammals are not included and will continue to have fragmented assemblies for likely many years to come due to the difficulties of obtaining high-quality DNA from animals that live in remote locations [23, 26]. Thus, training models with shorter input sequences is essential for making predictions in most available assemblies, which is necessary for finding enhancers involved in the phenotypic differences between species with fragmented genome assemblies. Additionally, predicted enhancer activity can be experimentally validated in massively parallel reporter assays (MPRAs) [27, 28], but these assays are limited to sequences on the order of hundreds of base pairs due to the cost of DNA synthesis and plasmid packaging limits, so predictions from models with longer sequences could not be directly validated experimentally.

Furthermore, a recent study showed that using longer sequences only incrementally improves the performance for machine learning models for predicting gene expression [29]. Since genetic variants associated with chromatin accessibility tend to be within a few hundred base pairs of chromatin accessibility peak centers [30], the sequence signatures underlying chromatin accessibility are likely to be even more localized than those underlying gene expression. This suggests that models trained using shorter input sequences are likely to often work well for predicting chromatin accessibility.

Previous work showed that classification models for enhancer activity prediction that work comparably well on parts of the genome not used in training can vary in their abilities to predict gains and losses in enhancer activity in species not used in training [13, 31]. In one such study, only a strict subset of convolutional neural network (CNN) machine learning models with impressive performance in predicting whether a region would be accessible in the brain on held-out chromosomes had strong performance in predicting the gain or loss of a brain accessible region in a new species, while others performed poorly [13]. In another study, a gapped k-mer support vector machine learning model trained on human sequences from a STARR-seq assay in HepG2, a liver cell line, was unable to predict the difference in STARR-seq activity of enhancers between nonhuman primates and their human orthologs [31]. Furthermore, recent studies have demonstrated that regression models trained to predict gene expression do not accurately predict expression differences between individuals [32, 33], further illustrating that models trained to predict continuous genomic measurements often fail to predict how those measurements change in the presence of changes in sequence. The limitations illustrated in these previous studies demonstrate the need for systematically developing evaluation criteria for machine learning models for predicting enhancer activity differences between species.

We addressed this need by developing a novel framework for evaluating the ability of machine learning models to predict chromatin accessibility differences between species. We then used our framework to evaluate the performance of multiple approaches to training these models, where we focused on approaches that could be used in species with fragmented assemblies so that our models could be applied to the hundreds of assemblies in existing multispecies alignments [26]. Specifically, we created a new dataset for comparing chromatin accessibility between species, which involved generating new ATAC-seq data from the rhesus macaque (Macaca mulatta) and Norway rat (Rattus norvegicus) and uniformly processing it along with publicly available ATAC-seq data from the house mouse [13, 34], cow, and pig [35]. We focused on the liver due to its cell type homogeneity relative to other tissues [36]. We trained our CNNs using primarily 500 base pair (bp) sequences to predict chromatin accessibility so that we could use them to make predictions at orthologous regions in fragmented assemblies. We tested multiple CNN hyper-parameters, multiple strategies for normalizing accessibility signals across species, multiple training sequence input lengths, and multiple subsets of species for model training. We found that many of the models worked well on held-out regions of species used in training and performed better than random when predicting chromatin accessibility differences between species, but none achieved strong performance on this latter task. Consistent with previous studies, we found that training on data from multiple species improved model performance for both species represented in the training data and for held-out species [20], but performance in predicting differences in accessibility between species was poor even for models trained using data from all five species. Our results demonstrate the importance of evaluating models for cross-species enhancer activity prediction for their ability to predict enhancer activity differences between species and that training a model to make such predictions accurately remains a challenge.

Materials and methods

Animal use and sample collection

We obtained tissue samples from corresponding tissues from two rhesus macaques and three Norway rats. Specifically, we obtained liver and multiple brain regions. We chose to focus our analysis on data from the liver samples for this study because recent work has suggested that brain regions may have different cell type proportions in different species [37–39].

We performed all animal procedures in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of Carnegie Mellon University (protocol ID 201600003) and the University of Pittsburgh (protocol ID 19024431). We single- or pair-housed rhesus macaques at the University of Pittsburgh with a 12 h–12 h light–dark cycle. The macaques in this study were a 12-year-old female (8.1 kg) and a 4-year-old male (6.0 kg). Before surgery, we sedated the macaques with ketamine [15 mg/kg intramuscular (IM)] and then ventilated and further anesthetized them with isoflurane. We next transported the animals to a surgery suite and placed them in a stereotaxic frame (Kopf Instruments). We opened the body cavity and excised a sample from the right lobe of the liver. Separately, we removed the calvarium and then perfused the circulatory systems with 3–4 l of ice-cold, oxygenated macaque artificial cerebrospinal fluid (124 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 2 mM MgSO4, 2 mM CaCl2, 3 mM NaH2PO4, 23 mM NaHCO3, 10 mM glucose). We then opened the dura and removed the brain. We sliced the brains into coronal slabs using a brain dissection knife. We excised the following brain regions under a dissection microscope: secondary auditory cortex (2A), premotor area 6V (6V), supplementary motor area, hand and forearm motor cortex (hM1), hand and forearm S1 (hS1), orofacial motor cortex (ofM1), orofacial S1 (ofS1), cerebellum, caudate, nucleus accumbens (NAcc), and putamen. We also collected liver, primary motor cortex (M1), and caudoputamen of the striatum (STR) tissues from three rats (one male Sprague–Dawley, housed in the University of Pittsburgh; two Brown Norway, one male and one female, housed at Carnegie Mellon University) (Supplementary Fig. S1). We euthanized rats by isoflurane overdose followed by decapitation. We collected livers immediately and kept them cold on ice in lysis buffer [6]. We sliced brains into 300 μm sections in a vibrating microtome (Leica VT 1200) in ice-cold, oxygenated rodent artificial cerebrospinal fluid [119 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 1 mM NaH2PO4 (monobasic), 26.2 mM NaHCO3, 11 mM glucose]. We sampled the brain regions from these coronal sections under a dissection microscope and transferred them to a chilled lysis buffer [40].

ATAC-seq

We processed tissue samples as described previously [6, 40] with the following minor differences in procedure and reagents: We isolated nuclei from dissected tissues using 30 strokes of homogenization with the loose pestle (0.005 in. clearance) in 5 ml of cold lysis buffer placed in a 15 ml glass Dounce homogenizer (Pyrex #7722-15). We filtered the nuclei suspensions through a 70 µm cell strainer, pelleted them by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 10 min, resuspended them in water, and filtered them a final time through a 40 µm cell strainer. We stained sample aliquots with DAPI (Invitrogen #D1206) and quantified nuclei concentrations using a manual hemocytometer under a fluorescent microscope. We input ∼50 000 nuclei into a 50 µl ATAC-seq tagmentation reaction as described previously [6, 40]. We amplified the resulting libraries to 1/3 qPCR saturation and estimated fragment length distributions using the Agilent TapeStation System. We shallowly sequenced barcoded ATAC-seq libraries at 600 000–5 million reads per sample on an Illumina MiSeq and processed individual samples through the ENCODE pipeline [41], including running Preseq to estimate library complexity [42]. We used the quality control measures from the pipeline to filter out the lowest-quality samples and re-pool an approximately balanced library for paired-end deep sequencing. We did the deep sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 System, where we used the University of Pittsburgh sequencing core for the first macaque and the first rat and GENEWIZ’s services for the other animals.

Constructing positive and negative sets

We used the bulk ATAC-seq data that we collected from the liver of the rhesus macaque (M. mulatta) and Norway rat (R. norvegicus) as well as publicly available bulk liver ATAC-seq data from house mouse (Mus musculus) [15, 34], cow (Bos taurus), and pig (Sus scrofa) [35]. A summary can be found in Supplementary Table S1. We processed the datasets as we described in [13] (Supplementary Table S2). This involved running the ENCODE ATAC-seq pipeline [41], which uses bowtie2 for mapping reads [43], picard (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/), and SAMtools [44] for filtering reads, MACS2 for calling peaks [45], and the Irreproducible Discovery Rate (IDR) for finding reproducible peaks [46]. Since, for each animal, our samples had substantial differences in sequencing depth, we used the IDR reproducible peaks across pooled pseudo-replicates.

We used the MACS2 peak caller “fold_enrichment” column value as a quantification of chromatin accessibility. Higher MACS2 signal values reflect a higher proportion of cells in which a given regulatory region is accessible. Specifically, the signal in MACS2 is the ratio of read count to the local estimation of background noise within each peak [45]. Although MACS2 does not claim to explicitly normalize for region length in its read count calculations, ATAC-seq peaks are generally of similar lengths on the order of hundreds of base pairs [47, 48], particularly following our filtering step in which we excluded all regions >1 kb [13]. As a result, signal strength estimates from MACS2 should be approximately comparable across peaks and species in our ATAC-seq datasets.

We found that many of the new datasets we generated were high-quality; full data quality reports are linked in the ‘Data availability’ section. We used all of the liver samples except for macaque biological replicate 2, which had poor periodicity, and rat biological replicate 1, which had <90% of reads properly paired in both technical replicates. We should also note that the publicly available cow and pig data had poor periodicity and that the pig data had <90% of reads properly paired in both biological replicates. We included them because we previously found that classification models trained using the data from the other species made accurate predictions for these [13]. In addition, the pig genome assembly we used (susScr3) was of lower quality than the rat genome assembly we used (rn6), so the lower fraction of properly paired reads from the pig is less likely to be indicative of poor data quality.

To identify candidate enhancers in each species and use them to create our positive and negative sets, we used the process described in our previous work [13]. Since we intersected reproducible peaks from two datasets to obtain the mouse liver candidate enhancers, we used the signal value from the peaks generated in our previous work because they were collected from fresh instead of flash-frozen tissue [13]. For model training, we set the signal values for the negative set examples to zero. We evaluated results separately on positive and negative sets to determine the extent to which our performance was caused by the model accurately quantifying chromatin accessibility and not just differences between positives and negatives. We used two examples for each sequence–one with the sequence and another with its reverse complement.

We split our training, validation, and test sets as described in our previous work [13, 15, 49]. Briefly, we split the mouse data based on chromosomes and the data from the nonmouse species based on the chromosomes they mapped to in the mouse to avoid training-test contamination due to chromosomes not mapping one-to-one across species [13]. For training models using only mouse sequences, we used only the mouse training and validation set; for training models using sequences from mouse, rat, and macaque, we used the training and validation sets from all three of these species; for training models using sequences from mouse, rat, macaque, cow, and pig, we used the training and validation sets from all five of these species.

Quantile normalization

We initially trained our models on log-transformed signal values but were concerned that MACS2’s normalization strategy may be insufficient for cross-species comparisons, as signal distributions varied slightly between species (Supplementary Fig. S2A). To address this, we trained models on quantile-normalized data. Quantile normalization normalizes signal values across datasets by ranking samples and then giving all samples at each rank the average signal value across all datasets [50]. Since quantile normalization requires equal sample sizes, we selected the peaks with the strongest signal, hypothesizing they would be most informative for model training. We first log-transformed chromatin accessibility signals, next selected the strongest 20 615 peaks from each species (the number of peaks in the species with the smallest number of reproducible peaks that were candidate enhancers after filtering), and then ranked peaks within each species (rank 1 for the highest signal, rank 20 615 for the lowest). For each rank i, we averaged the log-transformed signal strength across all five species. We then assigned the average as the normalized signal for rank i in each species (Supplementary Fig. S2B).

Extended quantile normalization

One limitation of quantile normalization is that it requires equal sample sizes, and we had different numbers of reproducible peaks for each species (Supplementary Table S2). To address this, we developed a novel method called extended quantile normalization (EQN). This approach applies quantile normalization to a random subset of peaks equal to the number of peaks from the smallest sample. It then assigns normalized values to the remaining peaks by matching each to its nearest neighbor (in log-transformed signal space if using log-transformed signal) within the quantile-normalized subset. This strategy allows us to use the full dataset while obtaining comparable signal distributions across species, with values reflecting relative signal strengths (Supplementary Fig. S2C). The code for this algorithm can be found in the GitHub repository.

Cross-species evaluation set creation

We generated validation and test sets that allowed us to directly evaluate our model’s ability to predict differences in chromatin accessibility between species (Fig. 1). We did this using bedtools v2.30.0 [51]. To explain validation and test set generation, we will describe the process for macaque as an example. Note that we did this for each nonmouse species.

Evaluation criteria for predicting differences in chromatin accessibility between species. Red peaks are accessible sites and colored boxes are TF motifs within them that we expect our CNNs to learn. The purple box highlights the training species, while the evaluation is performed on species that were not included in the initial training set. We evaluated our models using three distinct test sets. (We used corresponding validation sets in hyper-parameter tuning.) Test set 1: Regions in the evaluation species that are not accessible whose orthologs are accessible in the training species. Test set 2: Regions in the evaluation species that are accessible in both the evaluation and training species. For test set 2, we also evaluate accessibility differences between orthologous regions. Test set 3: Regions in the evaluation species that are accessible whose orthologs are not accessible in the training species.

To generate Validation/Test Set 1, we used bedtools subtract with the -A flag to remove all macaque validation/test set peaks (both reproducible and nonreproducible; specifically, we the used peaks called in the pool of replicates for species with multiple replicates and all the peaks at the lenient threshold in the ENCODE pipeline from the species with one replicate) from the set of mouse reproducible peaks mapped to the macaque genome. This produced a set of macaque nonaccessible regions whose orthologs are candidate enhancers in the mouse. To generate Validation/Test Set 2, we used bedtools intersect with the -wa -wb flags to identify unique macaque validation/test set peaks mapped to mouse whose orthologs overlapped with mouse reproducible candidate enhancer peaks. This yielded the set of mouse orthologs of macaque test set peaks whose orthologs are candidate enhancers in the mouse. We then filtered the macaque peaks using this list to produce Validation/Test Set 2. To generate Validation/Test Set 3, we used bedtools subtract with the -A flag to remove all mouse candidate enhancers (both reproducible and nonreproducible) from the set of macaque validation/test set peaks mapped to the mouse. This resulted in mouse orthologs of macaque test set peaks whose orthologs are not candidate enhancers in the mouse. We filtered the macaque peaks using this list to produce Validation/Test Set 3.

Within Validation/Test Set 2, we further evaluated differences in accessibility between orthologous regions, which required establishing one-to-one correspondence between macaque and mouse peaks. To obtain these corresponding orthologs, we used the output of the bedtools intersect -wa -wb step in the Validation/Test Set 2 creation, which was the set of macaque validation/test candidate enhancers whose mapped orthologs overlapped with reproducible mouse candidate enhancers. We created a list of one-to-one matched macaque and mouse candidate enhancers using the following rules: If a mapped macaque candidate enhancer uniquely overlapped with a single mouse candidate enhancer, we considered them a match. If one macaque candidate enhancer’s mouse ortholog overlapped multiple mouse candidate enhancers, we considered the match to be with the mouse peak with the largest overlap. If multiple macaque candidate enhancers’ mouse orthologs overlapped a single mouse candidate enhancer, we considered the match to be the macaque candidate enhancer whose mouse ortholog had the largest overlap. In cases of identical overlap sizes, we considered the match to be the candidate enhancer orthologs with the highest signal value.

To test how our models generalized to unseen clades of the phylogenetic tree, we also used the test set of laurasiatheria-specific enhancers and nonenhancers that we described in our previous work [13, 49]. In brief, the positives were cow enhancers whose pig orthologs were accessible in the pooled reads across pig replicates but whose mouse, macaque, and rat orthologs were not accessible in the pooled reads across mouse, macaque, and rat, respectively. The negatives were mouse enhancers whose macaque and rat orthologs were accessible in the pooled reads across macaque and rat replicates, respectively, and whose cow and pig orthologs were not accessible in the pooled reads across cow and pig replicates, respectively.

Evaluating Enformer

Enformer is a widely-used model that takes 200kb sequences as input. It has been shown to accurately predict both chromatin accessibility on held-out chromosomes, and chromatin accessibility differences between species [19–21]. To assess the ability of Enformer, we used the pre-trained Enformer model to make predictions on our test sets. We downloaded the model from kaggle. We extracted predictions from the output head mapping to Mouse track 5445, which is an ATAC-seq assay on C57BL/6 liver embryo (11.5 days). We chose this because there was no human liver ATAC-seq nor adult mouse liver ATAC-seq track. Enformer outputs chromatin accessibility predictions in bins of 128bp across the input sequence. We assigned the region’s predicted accessibility as the maximum of the four bins overlapping the 500bp candidate enhancer, which yielded the strongest correlation. Alternative aggregation strategies, such as averaging predictions across wider windows (4, 8, and 12 bins), consistently decreased prediction values, decreasing performance. This suggests that relevant regulatory signals are localized around the peak summit. Accessibility differences between species was calculated as the difference between the predicted value at each pair of orthologous loci.

Evaluating AlphaGenome

To assess the ability of AlphaGenome, a model that takes 1 Mb sequences as input and has been shown to achieve state-of-the-art performance for predicting chromatin accessibility on held-out chromosomes, we used the pre-trained AlphaGenome model to make predictions on our test sets. We expanded our input sequence lengths to 1Mb and centered the model’s receptive field on the peak summit. We made predictions for the ATAC-seq, human, liver task as specified by the ontology term UBERON:0 002 107 because this was the most similar task to ours. Since AlphaGenome predicts at base-pair resolution, we averaged the predicted signal strength for the 500 bp summit centered base pairs. As we did for other models, we calculated accessibility differences between species as the difference between the predicted values at each pair of orthologous loci.

Training regression models

Although there is publicly available chromatin accessibility data from multiple tissues, we used only data from the liver for multiple reasons. First, the majority of the liver consists of hepatocytes [36], while other tissues are much more heterogeneous with cell type proportions varying across species [52]. In addition, while related studies used multitask models trained on large numbers of cell lines and tissues [16, 20], multitask models have been shown to have poor performance for predicting or cell type-specific accessibility relative to single-task models, and most chromatin accessibility is tissue- or cell type-specific [53, 54].

We used CNNs [55] as our machine learning models because they have excelled at similar tasks [13, 20, 56]. They can learn combinatorial sequence patterns and pertinent features, including features that were not previously known to be relevant to the biological task, and not all sequence patterns and pattern combinations involved in liver chromatin accessibility are known. Our inputs were one-hot encoded 500 bp sequences, and our outputs were continuous numerical values representing predicted log-transformed signal values, the quantile-normalized log-transformed signal values, or the extended quantile normalized log-transformed signal values. Our loss function was the mean squared error (MSE).

We began training each model with the model architecture for the best classification model trained using the mouse motor cortex data published in our previous work [49], which we chose instead of our previous mouse only liver model [13] because it had better performance. For each model type, we ran an extensive 100-model hyperparameter sweep varying the following hyperparameters: number of filters per convolutional layer, number of filters per fully connected layer, convolutional layer dropout, fully connected layer dropout, convolutional layer L2 regularization, fully connected layer L2 regularization, initial learning rate, number of convolutional layers, and number of fully connected layers. Each convolutional layer had filters with width 7 and stride 1. Each layer had a ReLU activation function except for the last layer, which had a linear activation function. Between the convolutional and fully connected layers was a max-pooling layer with size 26 and stride 26. We initialized both convolutional layer filter weights and fully connected layer neuron weights with Glorot uniform initialization [57]. We used the Adam optimization method [58] with batch size 100. We trained each model until there were 4 consecutive epochs with no improvement on the validation set. The hyperparameters of the best models for each model type are listed in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4. We selected hyperparameters based on their performance on validation set chromosomes in the training species, performance on Validation Sets 1–3, and performance in their ability to predict the differences in chromatin accessibility between species for Validation Set 2. After selecting the hyperparameters for each type of model, we evaluated the performance on all of the test sets.

Motif enrichment analysis

We used motif enrichment to investigate the regulatory code differences between regions with different levels of chromatin accessibility in the mouse. We divided the mouse accessible regions based on their chromatin accessibility levels. We defined strong candidate enhancers as those with log-transformed signal levels above 2.5 (20.8% of peaks), moderate candidate enhancers as those with signal levels between 1.4 and 2 (35.2% of peaks), and weak candidate enhancers as those with signal levels below 1.4 (15.1% of peaks). We removed candidate enhancers with signal levels at the border of moderate and strong. We then ran differential motif enrichment between the strong and weak candidate enhancers as well as between the strong and moderate candidate enhancers. We also ran motif enrichment on each of the 3 groups. To do this, we used bedtools getfasta [51] to retrieve the fasta sequences for summits of the strong, moderate, and weak candidate enhancers ±250 bp. We used MEME-ChIP version 4.12.0 [59] with the JASPAR 2018 database [60] to identify enriched motifs. For differential motif enrichment, we used the strong candidate enhancers as the foreground and the other candidate enhancers as the background.

Results

Generating new comparative genomics data for cross-species chromatin accessibility model training and evaluation

We generated ATAC-seq data for the liver and multiple brain regions from adult rhesus macaque and Norway rat. We collected data from adult tissue because we wanted data that could be directly compared across species, and there is no consensus about how to match developmental stages across mammalian species [61]. The majority of samples were high-quality, but several had poor periodicity; full quality control evaluations can be found in the ‘Data availability’ section. We excluded the brain samples from our investigation, instead focusing on liver tissue due to its much more homogeneous cell type composition [36]. To investigate the ability of regression models to predict chromatin accessibility, we supplement this data with publicly available bulk liver ATAC-seq data from house mouse [15, 34], cow, and pig [35]. Although the read depth and periodicity for the cow and pig ATAC-seq data were inferior to those of the other species, we previously found that a model trained using only data from the other species could make accurate binary classification predictions in these species [13], suggesting that the chromatin accessibility regions are likely reliable.

Enformer and AlphaGenome perform poorly on predicting liver chromatin accessibility differences between species

We initially evaluated the performances of widely used previously trained sequence-to-chromatin accessibility models on the evaluation criteria we developed for predicting chromatin accessibility in new species. We first evaluated the performance of Enformer, a multitask model that takes 200 kb sequences as input and predicts gene expression and chromatin accessibility in a variety of cell lines and tissues. Although Enformer’s long input sequence makes it unusable in the hundreds of mammals with only fragmented assemblies [23, 26] and makes its enhancer activity predictions in species without such data impossible to experimentally validate, we thought that an analysis of its performance would be useful for the many genomics researchers applying Enformer to new tasks and could provide insights into the relationship between input sequence length and model performance. We used the Enformer Mouse track 5445 [ATAC-seq assay on C57BL/6 liver embryo (11.5 days)], as there was no adult liver chromatin accessibility track. We evaluated Enformer on the held-out test set from each species as well as on each of our evaluation criteria. Unfortunately, Enformer yielded poor performance. Across all species, Pearson and Spearman correlations were consistently below 0.4 (Supplementary Table S5). In addition, Enformer performed poorly on our data from mouse held-out chromosomes despite having been trained on the mouse genome, which might be due to Enformer having been trained on embryonic cells instead of adult liver.

We then evaluated the performance of the recently published AlphaGenome model, which takes 1 Mb sequences as input and predicts gene expression and chromatin accessibility at base pair resolution [62]. Although this input sequence length makes AlphaGenome usable in even fewer assemblies than Enformer, its impressive performance on held-out chromosomes led us to hope that it would have better performance in high-quality assemblies. We evaluated AlphaGenome predictions for the human liver ATAC-seq task, which was the most relevant task to ours. For most tasks, AlphaGenome performed even worse than Enformer, with Pearson and Spearman correlations consistently below 0.3 (Supplementary Table S5). In fact, AlphaGenome performed approximately at random for cow and pig with Pearson and Spearman Correlations near 0.

Both Enformer and AlphaGenome predicted the negative sets close to zero (Supplementary Table S6), but both predicted candidate enhancer sequences close to zero as well, with AlphaGenome tending to more severely underestimate the strength of chromatin accessibility than Enformer. Both models predict the direction of fold change correctly around 0.6 of the time (Supplementary Table S7). Since these models with state-of-the-art performance on held-out chromosomes had such poor performance in predicting differences in open chromatin between species and are not usable in the majority of mammalian genome assemblies in current multispecies alignments, we decided to train new models to directly predict adult liver chromatin accessibility from much shorter DNA sequences.

Model training

We trained CNNs to predict chromatin accessibility at candidate enhancers–accessible sites that are distal from transcription start sites (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section) [13]. For most models, we used 500 bp centered on accessible chromatin peak summits because they are short enough to often be mappable to species with fragmented assemblies [26], have previously been shown to be long enough to capture enough local sequence information to predict whether a region is accessible [13, 16], are close to the length of many ATAC-seq peaks, and are short enough to have their activity validated in MPRAs. We used the MACS2 peak caller “fold_enrichment” column to quantify chromatin accessibility, where the log of these quantifications were the target prediction values for most of the models.

New model evaluation criteria

We developed a set of model evaluation criteria specifically for evaluating the ability to predict differences in chromatin accessibility between species. Specifically, we created multiple criteria for evaluating how well a model trained on a strict subset of species performs on datasets from other “evaluation” species, as this is necessary for determining if a model can make reliable predictions in a genome for which chromatin accessibility measurements are not available. First, as was done in previous work [20], we evaluated our models on held-out test set sequences from the training species. In addition to this test set (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section), we created the following three test sets in species not used in training: regions not accessible in the evaluation species whose orthologs are accessible in the training species (Test Set 1), regions accessible in both the evaluation and training species (Test Set 2), and regions accessible in the evaluation species but not accessible in the training species Test Set 3). For Test Set 2, we also evaluated models’ abilities to predict accessibility differences between orthologous regions.

Strong performance on held-out chromosomes within a species does not guarantee generalization to species not used in training

We did an extensive hyperparameter search (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section) and obtained multiple models with strong performance on mouse chromosomes not used in training or hyperparameter tuning (Supplementary Table S3). Our best model trained on only mouse data had comparable performance on the training, validation, and test sequences (Supplementary Table S8), suggesting that the model was not overfit to the training data. We then evaluated the performance of the best model trained on only mouse data in rat, macaque, cow, and pig using our new evaluation criteria. The model successfully predicts negative instances, as their prediction distribution is skewed left towards the target value of 0 (Supplementary Table S9 and Fig. 2). The moderate overlap between the distributions of predictions for positives and negatives distributions indicates that the model would function sub-optimally as a binary classifier. However, the clear separation between the prediction means, and the significantly different distributions suggests that the model has learned meaningful features that distinguish the two classes to some degree, even if the boundary is not distinct.

The distributions of the model’s predicted signal values are shown for the negative (gray) and positive (colored) examples. A Mann–Whitney U test comparing the positive and negative distributions confirmed a statistically significant difference in all cases (P-value < 10−30).

Evaluation set performances for the nonmouse species were worse than for held-out mouse chromosomes. Test Set 3 performance tended to be worse than the overall test set performance for cow and pig, suggesting that the model was less reliable for predicting species-specific quantitative accessibility in species distantly related to the training species (Supplementary Table S8). This demonstrates the importance of evaluating chromatin accessibility prediction regression models in species not used in training.

Performance was especially poor for predicting differences in accessibility between mouse and species not used in training (Fig. 3A). In fact, around 45% of the differences were predicted in the incorrect direction (Supplementary Table S10). For example, the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) between the predicted and real accessibility differences for mouse and cow was only 0.20, and the Spearman correlation coefficient (ρ) was only 0.23. This demonstrates that models with strong performance on predicting chromatin accessibility in held-out chromosomes cannot be assumed to make accurate predictions of chromatin accessibility differences between species.

We previously found that classification models with comparable performance on species used in training vary greatly in their ability to predict binary differences in accessibility between species [13]. Since we had multiple sets of hyperparameters with strong performance on held-out mouse chromosomes, we evaluated models trained with different hyperparameters. Unfortunately, when evaluating these models with our new criteria, the performance was similar to that of the best mouse-only model (Supplementary Table S10).

Normalizing signal across species does not improve performance

Obtaining datasets of the same read depth across species was not feasible for us (Supplementary Table S2) and is not feasible for most researchers due to the challenges of obtaining tissue samples from nonmodel organisms. Since MACS2 does not claim to explicitly normalize for total read depth, we thought that normalizing the signal across species might improve generalization to new species. Although the log-transformed signal distributions are similar across species (Supplementary Fig. S2A), the small differences may be preventing a model trained in one species from making accurate predictions in another. Thus, we quantile-normalized the strongest log-transformed signal values and then trained and evaluated models for predicting these quantile-normalized signals.

We found that training models on mouse quantile-normalized data generally hurt performance (Supplementary Table S11), with model predictions exhibiting lower correlations across all species. Notably, in the pig test set 2, ρ decreased by 0.08 from 0.34 to 0.26. The one exception was that the model trained to predict quantile-normalized signals more accurately predicted that negatives had signals closer to 0.

We hypothesized that the decreased performance in the nonpig species was due to excessive input data loss, as quantile normalization assumes identical dataset sizes, so we removed the weaker peaks for the nonpig species that had more reproducible peaks than pig (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section). For example, to execute quantile normalization, the macaque dataset was reduced by approximately one third, from 32 697 reproducible peaks to 20 615. We therefore created an EQN method that preserves the rank structure within each sample while aligning the overall signal distributions across species and accounts for different species having different numbers of peaks (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section). Unfortunately, models trained to predict EQN signals had comparable performance to those trained to predict log-transformed signals. Both models worked well on mouse chromosomes not used in training or hyper-parameter tuning (Supplementary Table S11) but had sub-optimal performance in nonmouse species. When comparing the models trained to predict quantile-normalized or EQN signals, we observed that the EQN model strongly outperformed the quantile-normalized signal model on the macaque test sets, where macaque had the largest number of peaks. r increased from 0.23 to 0.35, and ρ increased from 0.23 to 0.37. The one exception was that the quantile-normalized signal model tended to predict that negatives had signals closer to 0 than the EQN model, though both models out-performed the models trained on log-transformed signals for this task.

Models trained to predict quantile-normalized, EQN, and log-transformed signals had comparably poor performance on their ability to predict differences in signal between species (Supplementary Table S12). While models trained on log-transformed signal consistently had lower MSE values, no model consistently outperformed the others across all four species, indicating that no single approach is superior for predicting chromatin accessibility across different species. These results demonstrate that normalizing signal across species is not sufficient for enabling models to accurately predict differences in chromatin accessibility between species.

Training models with longer sequences does not improve performance

Large chromatin accessibility differences may be caused by sequence differences near peak summits, while smaller differences may be caused by sequence differences in flanking regions. We therefore trained a model using 2000 bp sequences centered on peak summits, quadrupling the size of our input sequences. Unfortunately, the new models showed no improvement over the original 500 bp models in their ability to predict signal strength or cross-species signal differences (Supplementary Tables S11–S13). In fact, the model trained using 2000 bp sequences made higher predictions for negatives than the models trained using 500 bp sequences. This along with our previous results from Enformer suggests that longer input sequences do not enable models to make accurate predictions of chromatin accessibility differences between species.

Adding more species in training improves performance

We and others previously found that liver chromatin accessibility classification models generalized to species not used in training and suggested that these results were indicative of a shared regulatory code across species [13, 14]. However, in light of our new results, we hypothesized that the poor generalizability of regression models to new species may indicate that the regulatory code is not completely shared. Large differences in chromatin accessibility could reflect disruptions of a shared regulatory code in one species, while small differences may arise from regulatory elements that are active due to disruptions of a species-specific regulatory code. Alternatively, a previous study found that adding an additional species to model training for chromatin accessibility prediction improved performance on the original species, suggesting that predicting quantitative chromatin accessibility may require more training examples than predicting binary accessibility even if the regulatory code is shared [20].

To evaluate the extent to which incorporating more species into model training improves performance, we compared a baseline model trained exclusively on mouse data (“mouse-only model”) against several models trained using data from multiple species. We trained two distinct “three-species models”: one trained on mouse, macaque, and rat data (“MMR model”) to evaluate the performance of a model trained with data from multiple species in species not closely related to any training species and another trained on mouse, cow, and pig data (“MCP model”) to evaluate the value of incorporating data with sub-optimal quality from additional species. Finally, we trained a “five-species model” trained on data from all represented species. We evaluated these models using the criteria described previously.

When evaluating predictions for the negative sets (Supplementary Table S14), the five-species model was the most accurate at predicting values close to zero, with the three-species models performing nearly as well. We observed a larger performance gap between both the three-species models and the mouse-only models. Predictions for both positive and negative sets exhibited a negative shift for the five-species model when compared to the three-species models. However, the improvement from the mouse-only to the three-species models is not accompanied by a similar shift.

We observed a notable improvement in predicted and real signal correlations as well as MSE when comparing the mouse-only model to the three-species models (Supplementary Table S15) across all species. Both models exhibit a wider dynamic range and a line-of-best-fit slope more closely aligned with the identity line (y = x). The five-species model exhibited a slightly larger MSE and a slightly smaller slope, suggesting that the addition of more distantly related species may not always translate to better local predictions.

Interestingly, r and ρ increased by ∼0.1 on the mouse test set (Fig. 4). This suggests that training with more data causes the model to predict a wider range of values rather than clustering around the mean. However, the improvement for the mouse model was smaller when increasing from three to five training species. Similar trends are seen when evaluating macaque, rat, and pig test sets (Supplementary Figs S3–S5). Our results thus suggest a point of diminishing returns in model accuracy as more species are added.

To evaluate the impact of training set composition, we compared the MMR and MCP models. We observed that model performance was highest for species included in the training set. For instance, the MCP model outperformed the MMR model on cow and pig test sets (increasing r by 0.05 and ρ by 0.07, respectively). Conversely, the MMR model maintained superior performance on its training species, even on held out sequences.

For the “laurasiatheria-specific” set, we observed that the three-species models had better validation set but worse test set performance than the mouse-only model and that the five-species model had only slightly better performance than the other models.

In addition, both three-species models failed to predict chromatin accessibility differences between species, including between pairs of species used in training (Supplementary Table S16), and the performance of the five-species model was generally not better for this task (Fig. 3). While all four models had comparable performance on Pearson and Spearman correlations, the mouse-only model had the lowest MSE. This demonstrates that including more training data, including data from the evaluation species, is insufficient for enabling models to predict differences in accessibility between species.

Regulatory code differences between peaks with different levels of chromatin accessibility are often limited

We hypothesized that our models’ consistent poor performance on predicting accessibility differences between species may be a result of limited differences in regulatory code between peaks with different levels of chromatin accessibility. We used MEME-ChIP [59] to perform motif enrichment on mouse candidate enhancers of strong, moderate, and weak strengths as well as differential enrichment between pairs of these enhancer groups (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section). The differential motif enrichment between strong and weak candidate enhancers yielded some motifs, including motifs of TFs known to play important roles in the liver like HNF4A [63, 64] and CEBPA [63, 65]. However, the differential motif enrichment between strong and moderate candidate enhancers yielded a limited number of motifs and only one motif of a TF that is known to be involved in liver-specific functions, RXRA [66]. For the individual motif enrichments, we found that strong and moderate candidate enhancers had comparable enrichment for motifs of TFs with established roles in the liver. In contrast, weak candidate enhancers had fewer enriched motifs; there were none for TFs with known roles in liver-specific functions, and there was no enrichment for CTCF, a TF integral to the chromatin looping that is thought to be required for enhancer function [67] (http://daphne.compbio.cs.cmu.edu/files/azstephe/liver_regression_resource/mouseStrongVsMediumMemechip.zip). The lack of difference in motif enrichment between moderate and strong candidate enhancers may explain our model’s difficulty in predicting fine-grained quantitative chromatin accessibility differences between species at orthologous regions.

Discussion

The ability of sequence-to-function models to predict differences in chromatin accessibility between orthologous regions across species has the potential to help decipher mechanisms underlying phenotype evolution [49] yet has not previously been evaluated. We hypothesized that training regression models to use DNA sequence to predict chromatin accessibility signal strength would enable us to predict continuous differences in chromatin accessibility between species. We trained models that we anticipated would work well on this task and developed a new set of evaluation criteria for it.

Our models as well as Enformer [19] performed well on parts of the training species not used in training. Unfortunately, the new AlphaGenome [62] model consistently substantially underestimated open chromatin signal, leading to poor performance. This may be because the AlphaGenome adult liver ATAC-seq task came from an individual with cirrhosis [68] and therefore may present a regulatory code that is different from the healthy liver of the datasets we used.

Unfortunately, our models as well as the widely used Enformer and AlphaGenome models [19, 62] consistently had poor performance on our cross-species evaluation criteria, especially in predicting chromatin accessibility differences at orthologous regions. Across all models, including those trained with multiple species, performance for predicting accessibility differences between species was suboptimal: Models correctly predicted the direction of chromatin accessibility differences in ∼60% of orthologous sequences, and the correlation between predicted and real differences was under 0.40. We found that multiple sets of hyper-parameters and multiple input sequence lengths led to models with comparable performance. We also found that explicitly normalizing log-transformed chromatin accessibility signals across species did not yield better performance than using log-transformed signal values. We did, however, find that performance increased when predicting the EQN signal values when compared to quantile normalized values in macaque. Given that the quantile normalization procedure discards a substantial portion (approximately one-third) of the macaque dataset, we hypothesize that this performance increase is a direct result of the EQN method’s ability to use the complete dataset from our most deeply sequenced species. While performance was consistently above random chance, it remains too low to be reliably used in applications such as linking quantitative chromatin accessibility differences to phenotypic differences between species [49].

Our results additionally demonstrated that incorporating data from more species into model training generally improves model performance for both species used in training and for held-out species. As previous studies suggested [13, 14, 20], we hypothesize that this occurs because the liver regulatory code is shared across species, so adding data from additional species exposes the models to more diverse yet relevant training examples. However, we found the improvement from incorporating additional species into model training was limited when comparing the three-species to the five-species models. One possibility is that having three species of training data is sufficient for training a model with the best possible performance. Alternatively, the lower quality of the cow and pig data added to model training may be unable to help improve model performance. In addition, the performance on ability to predict differences in chromatin accessibility between species was poor regardless of the number of species used when training the model.

While our regression models can usually correctly identify negative sequences, performance on positive sequences, particularly in terms of accuracy of predicted accessibility signal strength in species not used in training and predicted differences between species in accessibility, remains weak. The limited improvement in predicting quantitative chromatin accessibility differences from extensive model modifications, including hyperparameter tuning, alternative signal normalization, longer input sequences, and multispecies training suggests that the task may be infeasible. This infeasibility may be a result of small differences between liver cell type proportions or differences between percentages of hepatocytes in each liver lobule zone between species [69]. Alternatively, poor performance may reflect other differences between samples from different species, such as slight variations in age (All were adults.) or the time of sacrifice within their circadian cycles. However, the lack of motif enrichment differences between strong and moderate peaks within a species suggests that training a model to distinguish them by sequence alone may be infeasible. This lack of motif enrichment in contrast to the liver motif enrichment between strong and weak peaks may explain why our regression models were unsuccessful despite the success of classification models we previously trained [13, 49].

There are multiple reasons why few sequence patterns differentiate peaks with moderate versus strong levels of accessibility. One is that these differences may not be driven by DNA sequence. Specific combinations of sequence motifs may be required for accessibility, but the extent of accessibility could depend on aspects of the tissue environment not encoded in the genome. For example, the amount of time between animal sacrifice and a chromatin accessibility experiment could lead to DNA unwinding nonrandomly, which could cause some peaks to have reduced signal in a subset of samples. In addition, differences between animals sampled from different species in age or time in the sleep cycle when an animal was sacrificed may affect quantitative chromatin accessibility at more locations than they affect the presence or absence of an accessible site. Another possibility is that proper quantification of differences between moderate and strong accessibility requires deeper sequencing than we had from many of our animals or a different approach to quantifying signals in peaks than the one used by MACS2. Unfortunately, the challenges in obtaining postmortem tissue from nonmodel organisms make a thorough investigation of these possibilities infeasible.

Finally, these differences might be influenced by regulatory sequences located >1 kb from the peak summit—such as flanking regions or distal elements that interact with enhancers through three-dimensional chromatin looping. Although Enformer, which uses 200 kb input sequences, had poor performance in predicting accessibility differences between species, it was not trained on data from adult liver, and a recent study suggested that it often fails to incorporate information from the full input sequence [29]. The upcoming assembly and alignment of hundreds of chromosome-level vertebrate genome assemblies by the Vertebrate Genomes Project [24] and Bat1K Project [25] will provide an opportunity for us to evaluate the ability of models trained using much longer sequences to predict differences between species in chromatin accessibility and mitigate errors caused by sub-optimal genome assembly quality [23], though training such models to use distal sequences may require three-dimensional DNA interaction data, and such data from primary tissues lacks the resolution to identify enhancer interactions that may affect chromatin accessibility [70].

An additional unexpected result was that our mouse-only trained models consistently had the worst performance in the rat, including when evaluating the ability to predict the accessibility signal of peaks in the rat whose orthologs are accessible in the mouse. This was especially surprising given that the rat is more closely related to the mouse than the other species are. To further understand this observation, we re-evaluated the models using a deeper rat dataset [71]. While correlation coefficients improved slightly across these test sets, the gains were marginal (<0.06) and did not alter the overall performance trend (Supplementary Table S17). Potential explanations include that the cell type proportions sampled from the rats might have been different from those sampled for other species or that differences between the way that the rats and the other animals were raised affected the rats’ chromatin accessibility. Finally, there are fewer sequence differences between mouse and rat than between mouse and other more distantly-related mammals. In this context, a lower proportion of the differences in open chromatin could be due to differences in local tissue-specific signals in the genome sequence.

Our evaluation criteria were specifically designed to assess a model’s ability to predict accessibility at orthologs of accessible sites. Evaluating genome-wide chromatin accessibility predictions in a given tissue would require additional criteria, such as verifying that the model correctly predicts that regions inaccessible in the target tissue (but accessible in others) are indeed inaccessible. While many previous classification models have shown strong performance on held-out chromosomes, only some have performed well on this task [13], demonstrating the importance of evaluating regression models for this task. Additionally, genome-wide prediction would require a strategy for defining input sequences. Our models used sequences centered on peak summit orthologs, but such anchors do not exist for regions that are not orthologs of accessible sites.

This work highlights the importance of evaluating machine learning models for genomics using criteria that are directly applicable to how they will be used. We found that models that provide accurate quantitative predictions of chromatin accessibility on held-out chromosomes consistently perform better than random but poorly in predicting chromatin accessibility differences between species. Being able to predict these differences is necessary for using predictions to study differences between species. These results are in line with our previous work that demonstrated that only a strict subset of models that can predict presence of an accessible region in held-out chromosomes can predict difference in accessibility status between orthologous regions [13]. Work from others reveal that models that accurately predict gene expression for held-out chromosomes fail to predict the effects of genetic differences between people on gene expression [32, 33, 72], further demonstrating the importance of directly evaluating machine learning models’ abilities to predict the effects of changes in sequence. However, the lack of sequence patterns enriched for occurring in strong peaks relative to moderate peaks suggest that obtaining precise predictions of chromatin accessibility level above a certain value may be infeasible. In addition, differences in chromatin accessibility between species may have little impact on phenotype evolution, so classification models may be sufficient for identifying most enhancer activity differences between species that affect phenotypic differences.
