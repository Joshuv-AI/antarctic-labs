Blood Levels of Angiotensinogen and Risk of Ischemic Stroke: The Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis
Authors: Soroush Masrouri, Patrick J Trainor, Parag Anilkumar Chevli, João AC Lima, Sotirios Tsimikas, Andrew P DeFilippis, Michael D Shapiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe investigated whether circulating angiotensinogen levels are associated with the risk of ischemic stroke and examined sex‐specific differences.MethodsWe included 5622 participants (mean age, 62.7 years; 52% women) free of cardiovascular disease at baseline (2000–2002) and followed them through 2020. Cox models assessed the association between angiotensinogen and ischemic stroke, including sex‐stratified hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. Mediation analyses estimated the contribution of risk factors to observed associations. Subgroup analyses with multiplicative interaction testing were conducted.ResultsDuring follow‐up, 283 ischemic stroke events occurred. Each 1‐SD increase in log‐transformed angiotensinogen was associated with a 16% higher stroke risk (HR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.00–1.34]). Significant interaction with sex was observed (P interaction<0.001). In men, angiotensinogen showed a positive dose–response association with ischemic stroke (adjusted HR per SD increase, 1.71 [95% CI, 1.33–2.19]). Compared with the lowest tertile (<16.8 μg/mL), adjusted HRs were 1.68 (95% CI, 1.08–2.61) and 1.95 (95% CI, 1.26–3.03) for the second and third (>20 μg/mL) tertiles, respectively (P trend=0.004). Diabetes marginally mediated the association in men (proportion mediated, 0.04 [95% CI, 0.00–0.15]). Significant evidence of an association between angiotensinogen and ischemic stroke in women was not observed (HR, 1.07 [95% CI, 0.88–1.29] per SD). No significant multiplicative interactions were observed across subgroups defined by age, race, smoking status, diabetes, obesity, angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitor/angiotensin II receptor blocker use, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein levels in either sex after Bonferroni correction (all P interaction>0.002).ConclusionsHigher circulating angiotensinogen levels are associated with an increased risk of ischemic stroke in men, independent of blood pressure.Keywords: angiotensinogen, ischemic stroke, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, sex differencesSubject Categories: Cerebrovascular Disease/Stroke, Ischemic Stroke

The renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) cascade has long been recognized as fundamental to the regulation of blood pressure (BP) and circulatory homeostasis. 1 , 2 Over the past 2 decades, data from large‐scale trials have consistently demonstrated the impact of targeted RAAS modulation through angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE‐Is) and angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) in reducing cardiovascular disease (CVD) morbidity and death. 3 , 4 However, therapeutic targets in the RAAS pathway currently focus exclusively on enzymes and peptides downstream of angiotensinogen, which may not effectively suppress RAAS activity or achieve an optimal safety profile. 5 Furthermore, RAAS escape may occur due to incomplete suppression of the pathway 6 or through the generation of angiotensin peptides via alternative mechanisms, such as the chymase pathway. 7

Recently, there has been growing interest in angiotensinogen, the primary precursor in the renin–angiotensin pathway. Human population studies of circulating levels of angiotensinogen and variants in the angiotensinogen gene have provided further insight into its implications for CVD management. 1 , 2 , 8 Therapeutic strategies leveraging gene silencing with hepatocyte‐targeted antisense oligonucleotide or small interfering RNA molecules have shown promising results in directly and specifically modulating angiotensinogen in the RAAS cascade, demonstrating efficacy in reducing angiotensinogen production, with a capacity to ameliorate the effects of RAAS escape and subsequently leading to notable decreases in BP with renal tolerability and safety profile. 9 , 10 , 11

In population‐based studies, due to the lack of standardized methods for measuring serum angiotensinogen levels, most of the knowledge of the role of angiotensinogen in CVD and hypertension has been provided through genetic association studies assessing single‐nucleotide polymorphisms. Three angiotensinogen single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (rs5051, rs699, and rs4762) have been linked to the development of hypertension and its severity. 1 The absolute concentrations of angiotensinogen protein can now be measured using the ELISA kit developed by IBL‐America (Minneapolis, MN). 12 We previously demonstrated that higher plasma angiotensinogen levels are associated with elevated BP and increased likelihood of hypertension. Notably, while men exhibited lower angiotensinogen levels on average, they showed a steeper increase in BP for a given relative difference in angiotensinogen levels. 13

Hypertension affects nearly half of the US population 14 and is strongly, and independently of conventional cardiovascular risk factors, associated with the risk of stroke, 15 , 16 the third‐leading cause of death in 2021. 17 The RAAS cascade is also shown to be involved in the pathology of ischemic stroke and its risk factors. 18 , 19 In addition to regulating BP, inhibition of angiotensin II production or its interaction with angiotensin II type 1 receptor attenuates atherosclerosis development. 20 , 21 Inhibition of angiotensinogen was also shown to decrease BP and atherosclerosis. 22 Although some studies have reported contradictory findings, meta‐analyses suggest that the M235T polymorphism of the angiotensinogen gene is associated with a slightly elevated risk of ischemic stroke in some ethnic populations. 23 , 24 , 25 Despite the angiotensinogen's importance in blood pressure regulation, the magnitude of its contribution to ischemic stroke in a multiethnic population is unclear.

In a large, contemporary, population‐based study, we tested the hypothesis that elevated angiotensinogen levels are associated with an increased risk of ischemic stroke and sought to evaluate sex differences in this association. We further assessed whether conventional cardiovascular risk factors mediate the association between angiotensinogen and ischemic stroke.

METHODS

A detailed description of the methods used in the current study is provided below. The data will be made available to other researchers to reproduce or expand the results after application and approval by the MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) Publications and Presentations Committee (http://www.mesa‐nhlbi.org).

Study Participants and Design

Data used for this analysis came from MESA, a prospective and population‐based study that was designed to examine the characteristics of subclinical CVD and progression to clinically overt CVD among US adults. Details of the MESA are described in earlier publications. 26 In brief, 6814 participants, aged 45 to 84 years, free of clinically apparent CVD, active cancer treatment, or any serious condition that would prevent long‐term participation, were enrolled in 2000 to 2002 from 6 urban and suburban field centers (Forsyth County, NC; Northern Manhattan and the Bronx, NY; Baltimore, MD; St. Paul, MN; Chicago, IL; and Los Angeles County, CA), with ≈38% White, 28% Black, 22% Hispanic/Latino, and 12% Chinese American participants. Each participant provided written informed consent, and the institutional review boards at each site approved the study.

The current ancillary study used data from participants not included in MESA‐1000, a random sample of roughly 1000 individuals who underwent additional characterization with the use of baseline blood samples. To preserve the availability of these specimens, ancillary studies generally exclude the MESA‐1000 participants, unless otherwise justified. As a result, 5786 participants had plasma angiotensinogen measurements available for analysis. The present study included 5622 individuals with complete covariate data and follow‐up for stroke (97.2% of individuals available for analysis).

Sociodemographics, medical history, anthropometric measurements, behavioral, and laboratory data were ascertained as described in previous publications. 26 , 27 , 28

Ascertainment of End Point(s)

Participants were contacted via telephone at 9‐ to 12‐month intervals to gather information on all interim hospital admissions, cardiovascular outpatient diagnoses/procedures, and mortality cases. Additionally, MESA occasionally identified additional events through cohort clinic visits, participant call‐ins, and medical record reviews. To validate self‐reported diagnoses, death certificates and medical records for all hospitalizations, as well as for selected outpatient cardiovascular diagnoses and procedures, were requested by MESA. Also, interviews with next of kin were conducted for out‐of‐hospital CVD deaths. Copies of death certificates and medical records were gathered to verify self‐reported diagnoses. The MESA cerebrovascular adjudication committee adjudicated stroke events. In cases of persistent disagreement between reviewers following review and adjudication, the case was discussed to reach a consensus. The primary end point for the current study was ischemic stroke events, as they accounted for >82% of all stroke events in MESA. Ischemic stroke subtypes included large vessels (extracranial and intracranial), cardioembolic, small vessels, other specific mechanisms, multiple mechanisms, or undetermined. We decided a priori not to include transient ischemic attack events due to their nonspecific and transient clinical presentation, which raises a risk of classification bias and could dilute relevant associations. Further detailed descriptions of the stroke subtype classification and adjudication protocols are available in earlier publications. 29 Incident stroke was defined as the first occurrence of an adjudicated stroke event from baseline through the end of follow‐up on 2020. Follow‐up for all analyses was calculated as the time elapsed from the baseline examination to whichever came first: first stroke event, loss to follow‐up, death, or the end of the study.

Plasma Angiotensinogen Measurements

Angiotensinogen levels were measured in blood plasma from examination 1 (2000–2002) of the included MESA participants. The measurements were made with an ELISA described elsewhere and executed by Medpace Reference Labs (Cincinnati, OH). 12 In brief, the coating antibody used was a rabbit anti‐human immunoglobulin monoclonal antibody directed to human angiotensinogen (IBL‐America; Catalog No. 27412). Total angiotensinogen, including both intact angiotensinogen and des(Ang I) angiotensinogen, was detected in 1:10 000 diluted EDTA plasma using a horseradish peroxidase–conjugated mouse anti‐human angiotensinogen monoclonal Fab' fragment (IBL‐America). Quantification was achieved through a standard curve generated with purified human angiotensinogen. The assay exhibited a coefficient of variation of 9%, with an analytical range of 13.9 to 75.9 μg/mL. 13 Further details about the angiotensinogen measurement are available elsewhere. 9

Definition of Terms

Race and ethnicity were self‐reported as White, Black, Chinese, or Hispanic. Physical activity was measured using a semiquantitative questionnaire, adapted from the Cross‐Cultural Activity Participation Study, and expressed as total moderate and vigorous activity in metabolic equivalent minutes per week. 30 , 31 Diabetes was defined as self‐reported physician diagnosis or the use of insulin/oral hypoglycemic medications or fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL. Hypertension was defined according to the 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guideline as systolic BP ≥130 mm Hg, diastolic BP ≥80 mm Hg, or use of antihypertensive medications 32 ; this definition was chosen to reflect contemporary clinical practice and evidence demonstrating increased cardiovascular and stroke risk at BP levels below the previously used ≥140/90 mm Hg threshold. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 Obesity was defined as having a body mass index (BMI) of ≥30 kg/m2. Chronic kidney disease was defined with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <60 mL/min per 1.7 m2 or urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio ≥30 mg/g. The typical diet over the past year was assessed using a 120‐item food‐frequency questionnaire based on the Insulin Resistance Atherosclerosis Study instrument, modified to include foods typically eaten by Chinese individuals. 37 Diet was categorized as “poor,” “intermediate,” and “ideal” on the basis of Life's Simple 7 criteria, with intermediate and ideal combined for analysis. 38 Menopausal status in MESA was determined by self‐report using responses to “Have you gone through menopause?” and “Are you going through menopause?”; also, women aged >55 years were classified as postmenopausal. 39 , 40

Statistical Analysis

Characteristics of the study cohort were stratified by sex and angiotensinogen levels (above versus below the sex‐specific median). Categorical variables were summarized as frequency (%), approximately normally distributed continuous variables as mean (SD), and skewed continuous variables as median (interquartile range). Comparisons of characteristics across angiotensinogen levels were performed separately for men and women using the χ2 statistic for categorical variables, the t test for approximately normally distributed continuous variables (Welch's t test when the assumption of homogeneity of variance was violated), and the Mann–Whitney U test for skewed continuous variables.

Incidence rates with 95% CIs were calculated per 1000 person‐years in total and across tertile categories. Kaplan–Meier analysis examined survival across tertiles of angiotensinogen levels in men and women. Cox regression was used to examine the relationship between a 1‐SD increase in log‐transformed angiotensinogen and the risk of ischemic stroke. Four models were constructed: Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, race and ethnicity, and study site; model 2 included additional adjustments for education level, health insurance, smoking status, alcohol consumption, diabetes, BMI, triglycerides, low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, use of lipid‐lowering medications, eGFR, and moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity. Model 3 was further adjusted for systolic BP and the use of any antihypertensives. As a sensitivity analysis, model 4 included additional adjustments for poor diet score, hs‐CRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein), glycoprotein acetylation, coronary artery calcium, and urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio among those with available data (n=5349). Covariates were selected a priori on the basis of clinical relevance, prior literature, and their potential to confound the association between plasma angiotensinogen levels and ischemic stroke. 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 As prespecified, in all of the models, we examined how sex modifies the relationship between angiotensinogen and the risk of incident ischemic stroke by including interaction terms (angiotensinogen × sex) in our analyses. We found significant effect modifications by sex on the association between angiotensinogen and ischemic stroke (all P interactions<0.001). As a result, we conducted the main analysis in the overall population and stratified the results by sex. In addition, we repeated all models with angiotensinogen as a categorical variable (tertiles with percentiles calculated within each sex). The proportional hazard assumptions in the Cox models were verified using Schoenfeld's global test of residuals. Trend analysis P values were calculated by modeling angiotensinogen tertiles as continuous variables. Multivariable‐adjusted restricted cubic splines with knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the distribution of plasma angiotensinogen levels were used to model and visualize the relationship between angiotensinogen and ischemic stroke among men and women, with the top 1% of outlier values truncated. The reference point was set as the median of the reference group from the categorical analysis. As exploratory analyses, effect modification was tested in sex‐stratified Cox models by including multiplicative interaction terms between angiotensinogen levels and age (45–60 years [middle‐aged] and 60–84 years [older adults], also approximating the study population's median age of ≈63 years), race and ethnicity (White, Chinese, Black, and Hispanic), current smoking, diabetes, obesity, ACE‐I/ARB use, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and hs‐CRP (<2 and ≥2 mg/L 46 , 47 ). We also performed a sensitivity analysis by accounting for the competing risk of death using the Fine–Gray method. 48 In a second sensitivity analysis, we repeated the main analyses after excluding participants who experienced interim atrial fibrillation (AF) or coronary heart disease (CHD) events. We used the R “mediation” package (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) to test whether prevalent hypertension, diabetes, BMI, and hs‐CRP mediated the association between plasma angiotensinogen and ischemic stroke. 49 This model‐based causal mediation approach uses separately fitted mediator and outcome models to estimate the average causal mediation effect (indirect effect), average direct effect, total effect, and proportion mediated. For each mediator, both models were fitted with full covariate adjustment. The 95% CIs were estimated using nonparametric bootstrap resampling with 1000 simulations. These analyses were conducted under the sequential ignorability assumption. 49 Further, we repeated the main analyses for incident hemorrhagic stroke events as the end point in a secondary analysis. Finally, E‐values were used to assess how likely the findings could be explained by residual confounding. An E‐value represents the minimum HR that an unmeasured confounder would need to have to explain the observed results.

All analyses were conducted using STATA version 14 SE (Stata Corp LP, TX) and R version 4.4.0. A 2‐tailed P value of <0.05 indicates statistical significance. Given the exploratory nature of the current study, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied 50 , 51 ; thus, findings should be interpreted cautiously, with consideration of potential type I error. However, for the effect modification analysis, given the significant number of interaction tests (22 comparisons), we adjusted the P values for multiple comparisons using a Bonferroni correction, setting the significance threshold at 0.0023 (0.05/22).

RESULTS

Compared with those not included in the analyses (1192 individuals without angiotensinogen measurements, follow‐up data, or covariate data), included individuals were older (mean, 62.67±10.26 versus 59.71±9.76 years; P<0.001) and comprised fewer women (52.06% versus 56.54%; P=0.005) and White individuals (37.21% versus 44.46%) and more Chinese (12.20% versus 9.90%) and Black individuals (28.78% versus 22.99%; P<0.001). Included individuals had lower total cholesterol (193.39±34.84 versus 197.83±39.58 mg/dL; P<0.001), triglycerides (median, 110 [interquartile range, 77–159] versus 119 [interquartile range, 84–173] mg/dL; P<0.001), and prevalence of current smoking (12.47% versus 15.90%; P=0.002) but had slightly higher systolic BP (126.92±21.55 versus 125.05±21.10 mm Hg; P=0.006), and lower lipid‐lowering medication use (16.79% versus 13.68%, P=0.008). BMI, HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, diastolic BP, fasting plasma glucose, antidiabetic medication, and antihypertensive medication use were comparable between the 2 groups (all P>0.05).

As shown in Table S1, men and women had comparable age and race and ethnicity distribution. Current alcohol intake (62.2% versus 48.6%) and current smoking (14.2% versus 10.9%) were more prevalent among men. The prevalence of hypertension and any antihypertensive medication use, including ACE‐I/ARB use, was not significantly different. Angiotensinogen levels were higher among women (26.0±11.5 μg/mL) compared with men (18.7±4.2 μg/mL). Women also had higher BMI, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, systolic BP, and urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio levels. Conversely, men had higher triglyceride levels, diastolic BP, and eGFR. Inflammatory markers, including hs‐CRP and glycoprotein acetylation, were higher in women.

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of participants stratified by sex and angiotensinogen levels. Of the 5622 participants (mean age, 62.7±10.3 years) included in this analysis, 2695 (48.0%) were men. There were 2092 (37.2%) White, 1618 (28.8%) Black, 1226 (21.8%) Hispanic, and 686 (12.2%) Chinese individuals. Among both sexes, those with above‐median angiotensinogen were younger and included a higher proportion of White individuals. Smoking history did not differ significantly among men; however, women with higher angiotensinogen had lower rates of current smoking. Among both sexes, a higher proportion of participants with above‐median angiotensinogen reported currently drinking alcohol. BMI was higher in men with above‐median angiotensinogen, whereas women showed no significant difference. Both men and women with above‐median angiotensinogen had higher hs‐CRP and glycoprotein acetylation. Among women, those above the median were more likely to use antihypertensive medication.

Time to ischemic stroke for tertiles of angiotensinogen during a median follow‐up of 18.7 (interquartile range, 12.8–19.4) years in men and women are displayed in Kaplan–Meier curves (Figure 1); during follow‐up, there were 283 ischemic stroke events (148 in men and 135 in women). Figure 2 shows the association between a 1‐SD increase in plasma angiotensinogen levels (log scale) and incident ischemic stroke, both overall and stratified by sex. Multivariable Cox regression analysis showed that each SD higher angiotensinogen level (log scale) was significantly associated with incident ischemic stroke events, with an HR of 1.16 (95% CI, 1.00–1.34; P=0.04), adjusted for age, sex, race and ethnicity, study site, education, smoking, health insurance, diabetes, alcohol use, BMI, triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, lipid‐lowering medication use, eGFR, physical activity, systolic BP, and any antihypertensive medication use (model 3). In sex‐stratified analysis, each 1‐SD greater angiotensinogen level was significantly associated with a higher risk of incident ischemic stroke among men, with an HR of 1.71 (95% CI, 1.33–2.19; P<0.001) in model 3. The association among men remained statistically significant when we further adjusted for poor diet, hs‐CRP, glycoprotein acetylation, coronary artery calcium, and urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio in a subset of individuals with available data (model 4). Evidence for higher plasma angiotensinogen levels being associated with incident ischemic stroke events in women in the fully adjusted model was not observed, with a corresponding HR of 1.07 (95% CI, 0.88–1.29; P=0.50). In model 4, for the overall population, the HR was 1.14, with an E‐value of 1.54. Among men, the HR was 1.72, with an E‐value of 2.83, indicating a low likelihood of findings due to residual confounding alone. Among women, the HR was 1.07, with an E‐value of 1.34. Further adjustment for ACE‐I/ARB use, instead of any antihypertensive medication use, did not alter the results (data not shown).

Throughout the follow‐up, 1653 death events were considered in the competing risk analysis. As shown in Table S3, after accounting for the competing risk of death, higher plasma angiotensinogen levels remained significantly associated with incident ischemic stroke among men. Specifically, the subdistributionHR for ischemic stroke per 1‐SD increase in angiotensinogen was 1.38 (95% CI, 1.18–1.61) in men, whereas no significant association was observed in women (subdistribution HR per 1‐SD increase, 1.08 [95% CI, 0.88–1.35]; P=0.45). After excluding individuals with interim atrial fibrillation or coronary heart disease (n=1568), the multivariable‐adjusted HR of a 1‐SD increase in angiotensinogen for ischemic stroke was 1.60 (95% CI, 1.22–2.11) among men (Table S4).

During follow‐up, 51 hemorrhagic stroke events occurred. In secondary analyses, we did not observe a significant association of angiotensinogen with incident hemorrhagic stroke (Table S5). The HR of hemorrhagic stroke associated with 1‐SD higher angiotensinogen (log scale) was 0.95 (95% CI, 0.70–1.30; P=0.74) with model 3 multivariate adjustment.

Mediation analysis was performed to assess the proportional contribution of prevalent hypertension, diabetes, BMI, and hs‐CRP to the risk of incident ischemic stroke in men associated with angiotensinogen, adjusting for other covariates, including age, race and ethnicity, study site, education, smoking, health insurance, alcohol use, triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, lipid‐lowering medication use, eGFR, and physical activity, in addition to hypertension, diabetes, BMI, and hs‐CRP. Results of the mediation analyses showed that diabetes accounted for 4% of the association between angiotensinogen and incident ischemic stroke (95% CI, 0%–15%; P=0.05). No significant mediation effects were observed for hypertension, BMI, or hs‐CRP (Table S6).

DISCUSSION

This study presents the first associative evaluation of plasma angiotensinogen concentrations and ischemic stroke in a multiethnic human population. Our findings indicate that the impact of angiotensinogen on ischemic stroke differs by sex. Specifically:

After adjusting for multiple covariates, including age, sex, race and ethnicity, study site, education, smoking, health insurance, diabetes, alcohol use, BMI, triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, lipid‐lowering medication use, eGFR, physical activity, systolic BP, and antihypertensive medication use, each SD increase in plasma angiotensinogen levels was associated with a 16% higher risk of ischemic stroke.

To date, no prior studies have investigated the relationship between plasma angiotensinogen and 2‐decade ischemic stroke risk in a multiethnic population not known to have CVD. The important role of RAAS activation in the pathology of CVD is supported by basic science research, genetic association studies, and clinical trials using various RAAS inhibitors. 1

The absence of standardized methods for measuring serum angiotensinogen has limited its use in population studies. As a result, genetic association studies of angiotensinogen single‐nucleotide polymorphisms and CVD have been the main investigative approach. 1 , 2 With inconsistent findings, several studies link the angiotensinogen M235T polymorphism to 30% to 160% higher ischemic stroke risk, with more pronounced associations in some ethnic groups. 23 , 24 , 25 , 52 A meta‐analysis of 17 studies (16 030 participants) found a significant association in Asian individuals (odds ratio, 1.44 [95% CI, 1.21–1.74]; P<0.05) but not White individuals. 23 Similar associations were notably observed among Asian populations in a comprehensive meta‐analysis that included 490 case–control studies with a total of 138 592 cases and 159 314 controls. 25 Moreover, limited studies among patients with diabetes have linked higher levels of urinary angiotensinogen to increased risk of renal–cardiovascular events, 53 including stroke. 54 Complementing existing evidence, this study explored the relationship between plasma angiotensinogen levels and the risk of ischemic stroke over 2 decades of follow‐up. We identified a strong, significant association in men, while the association in women was not statistically significant. Among conventional risk factors, diabetes demonstrated a marginally significant mediating effect in men, which is unlikely to be clinically meaningful. This underscores that the association between angiotensinogen and ischemic stroke is primarily independent of conventional metabolic risk pathways.

The association between plasma angiotensinogen levels and ischemic stroke in men was independent of systolic BP and antihypertensive medication use and was evident in both individuals with hypertension and individuals without hypertension as defined by 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association criteria. This finding is considerable since a meta‐analysis of 26 clinical trials found that ACE‐Is/ARBs might have BP‐independent effects on the risk of stroke. 55 In line with our findings, results from the LIFE (Losartan Intervention for Endpoint Reduction in Hypertension) and MOSES (Midregional Proatrial Natriuretic Peptide to Guide Secondary Stroke Prevention) trials have shown some BP‐independent effects for ARBs on cerebrovascular and cardiovascular events. 56 , 57 Similarly, results derived from animal models indicate a detrimental effect of angiotensin II type 1 receptor signaling on ischemic stroke through BP‐independent mechanisms such as neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration, reactive oxygen species generation, and apoptosis. 58 , 59 Although we assessed the mediating effects of commonly addressed variables in population‐based studies (hypertension, diabetes, BMI, and hs‐CRP), important RAAS‐related pathways may indeed be overlooked. Additional RAAS pathway biomarkers were not available in our population; however, preclinical research has shed light on the regulation of angiotensinogen and its remnant, des(Ang I) angiotensinogen, revealing a direct physiological role beyond its effects through the RAAS. 22 , 60 , 61 A substantial body of work has investigated the role of the RAAS in experimental atherosclerosis, and it does not appear that the rise in BP, per se, is the cause of the atheroma progression. 62 Importantly, the liver‐derived angiotensinogen appears to be the key source driving these atherosclerotic effects. 22 , 63 Preclinical in vivo studies have also revealed additional pathways through which angiotensinogen contributes to CVD, including direct promotion of hepatic steatosis and adipose tissue expansion, as well as inhibition of angiogenesis via suppression of endothelial cell proliferation. 1 , 2 Although the BP‐independent effects of angiotensinogen are profound, their underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood and are fertile ground for future studies. Furthermore, additional studies are needed to explore the mediating roles of RAAS‐related biomarkers and markers of endothelial dysfunction, to better understand the pathways linking angiotensinogen to ischemic stroke, and to investigate whether sex differences in these factors may modify the associations.

Another key finding of this study was the observed sex differences in the relationship between plasma angiotensinogen levels and ischemic stroke. In previous research with MESA participants, men demonstrated a more pronounced increase in BP in response to a given relative difference in angiotensinogen levels than women. 13 A systematic review of randomized controlled trials indicated that ACE‐Is and ARBs are slightly more effective in men compared with women. 64 Similarly, a post hoc analysis from the RENAAL (Angiotensin II Antagonist Losartan study and the IDNT (Irbesartan Diabetic Nephropathy Trial) revealed that ARBs reduce the risk of composite outcomes of stroke, myocardial infarction, CVD death, and heart failure only in men. 65 Recent evidence suggests that ACE‐Is/ARBs and other antihypertensive agents may upregulate angiotensinogen synthesis in the liver. 9 However, the proportion of ACE‐I/ARB use was comparable between men and women in our cohort, making it unlikely to explain the observed sex differences. Moreover, subgroup analysis showed that the association between angiotensinogen levels and ischemic stroke in men persisted irrespective of ACE‐I/ARB use. The mean age of women at baseline was 62 years. Subgroup analysis by age revealed 83% significantly higher risk of ischemic stroke among women aged <60 years, although the interaction was not statistically significant (P=0.37). The limited number of events in this subgroup (28 events in women aged <60 years) may have contributed to the lack of statistical significance due to reduced power. These findings highlight the need for further studies in younger populations with higher event rates to validate our observations. Evidence from animal studies suggests that estrogen administration increases hepatic angiotensinogen mRNA expression and plasma angiotensinogen levels. 66 , 67 Similarly, in postmenopausal women, estradiol administration has been associated with elevated plasma angiotensinogen. 68 In this cohort, older women had higher circulating angiotensinogen levels; however, only 258 women (8.8%) were premenopausal, so our findings in women largely reflect postmenopausal participants, and larger studies with greater representation of premenopausal women are needed to assess effect modification by menopausal status. To further evaluate whether exogenous hormone exposure might influence these associations, we examined hormone replacement therapy (HRT) use among women: Of 2927 women, 816 reported current HRT use, and 1845 reported no use. In analyses stratified by HRT use among postmenopausal women, although the associations varied according to HRT use, they remained statistically nonsignificant in both strata (nonusers: HR, 1.17 [95% CI, 0.84–1.65]; users: HR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.62–1.35]), suggesting that HRT use is unlikely to explain the overall nonsignificant associations observed in women. However, confirmation in larger studies is needed. To examine whether the sex difference was driven by body composition, we replaced BMI with estimated fat mass and fat‐free mass, as described elsewhere. 69 In the fully adjusted model, the HRs were 1.14 (95% CI, 0.98–1.33) overall, 1.73 (95% CI, 1.32–2.25) in men, and 1.07 (95% CI, 0.88–1.31) in women, suggesting that the observed sex differences were not entirely attributed to body composition measures.

Upstream RAAS inhibition, through suppression of hepatic angiotensinogen, may overcome compensatory RAAS activation and enhance treatment efficacy. 9 A number of approaches have been developed to target liver‐derived angiotensinogen. The small interfering RNA (Alnylam Pharmaceuticals, Cambridge, MA) and antisense oligonucleotide (Ionis Pharmaceuticals, Carlsbad, CA) approaches, currently in human trials, work through degrading angiotensinogen mRNA in hepatocytes. 8 , 10 , 70 , 71 These drugs are conjugated to an N‐acetyl galactosamine moiety, ensuring selective targeting of liver‐derived angiotensinogen. 8 , 10 IONIS‐AGT‐LRx (AGT‐757) is an antisense oligonucleotide that reduces liver angiotensinogen protein by promoting angiotensinogen mRNA degradation. In 3 Ionis Pharmaceuticals trials, it was given via weekly subcutaneous injections, and the highest dose (80 mg) was well tolerated and led to significant reductions in plasma angiotensinogen levels both as monotherapy and add‐on therapy. 9 However, our findings are observational and do not establish that upstream suppression of liver‐derived angiotensinogen reduces cerebrovascular events; extended follow‐up and randomized outcome trials will be needed to determine whether such strategies confer benefits beyond conventional downstream inhibition via ACE‐Is/ARBs.

Limitations

Several limitations merit consideration in the interpretation of the results of the present study:

The observational design limits causal inference, and despite extensive adjustment, residual confounding (eg, dietary sodium, additional RAAS markers, broader inflammatory measures, or socioeconomic factors) may still influence the observed associations. To further evaluate the potential impact of residual confounding, we expanded the fully adjusted model in a subsample with available data (n=3513) to additionally account for smoking pack‐years, ankle–brachial index, carotid intima‐media thickness Z score (defined as the measured maximum intima‐media thickness across the internal and common carotid sites), left ventricular mass, left ventricular ejection fraction, marital status, total annual family income, and circulating biomarkers including insulin, total homocysteine, interleukin‐6, plasmin–antiplasmin complex, fibrinogen antigen, d‐dimer, and calibrated factor VIII. Results were materially unchanged after this additional adjustment; the HRs for ischemic stroke were 1.17 (95% CI, 0.97–1.43) overall, 1.77 (95% CI, 1.25–2.50) among men, and 1.17 (95% CI, 0.91–1.51) among women. Also, the E‐value for the observed estimate among men was 2.83, indicating a low likelihood of findings due to residual confounding alone.

Conclusions

In conclusion, circulating angiotensinogen levels were positively associated with the incidence of ischemic stroke in men within a diverse, population‐based cohort of US adults, independent of hypertension. With confirmation from larger studies, future research should focus on elucidating the potential mechanisms by which angiotensinogen levels contribute to ischemic stroke risk in men.
