 1. Executive Summary
 This final technical report summarizes the key accomplishments of the HyMarc Seedling
project to develop new materials based on metal-organic framework (MOF) incorporated with
frustrated Lewis pairs (FLP) termed as FLP@MOF, as next-generation of hydrogen storage

 Page 1 of 19

sorbent that can meet or exceed DOE’s 2025 performance target of hydrogen storage gravimetric
density of 5.5 wt.% and volumetric density of 0.040 kg H2/L.[1] The project was carried out by the
PI Shengqian Ma at the University of North Texas (UNT) along with Argonne National Laboratory
(ANL) to develop FLP@MOF systems that exhibit high hydrogen storage capacity at ambient
temperature under pressure <100 bar, as well as to obtain improved fundamental understanding
of hydrogen adsorption mechanism in FLP@MOF system.
 The motivation for this project stems from the urgent need for clean energy carriers that can
replace fossil fuels in transportation and grid storage. Hydrogen, while abundant and clean-
burning, requires efficient storage methods to be practical at scale.[2] MOFs offer high surface
area and tunable chemistry, while FLPs provide strong hydrogen binding interactions.[3-14]
 Combining these properties, the project utilized these FLP@MOF systems for hydrogen
storage and the research provided three major contributions: (1) it advanced fundamental
understanding of hydrogen adsorption mechanisms, showing how these frustrated Lewis acid-
base pairs enhance binding; (2) it demonstrated technical effectiveness by achieving reproducible
hydrogen uptake capacities of up to 2.6 wt% at ambient temperatures and pressures below 100
bar, a significant improvement over pristine MOFs and promising leap towards the DOE target of
5.5 wt%; and (3) it established a library of new FLP@MOF systems demonstrating high hydrogen
uptake capacities that were further optimized by modulation of the FLP loading amounts. Overall,
this work laid the foundation to provide a framework for systematic evaluation of these
FLP@MOF systems for hydrogen storage. Moreover, the methods developed for the synthesis of
FLP@MOFs and post-synthetic modifications are scalable and cost-effective compared with
other advanced hydrogen storage technologies, establishing economic feasibility.
 These achievements benefit the public by advancing hydrogen technologies that can enable
clean energy, zero-carbon transportation and contribute to U.S. energy independence.
 2. Comparison of Accomplishments with Goals
The project’s goals included:
 (1) Design and synthesis of FLP@MOF:
 To design and synthesize six FLP@MOF systems using step-wise anchoring approach as
 well as to complete the structural studies and high-pressure hydrogen measurements.
 (2) Development of FLP@MOF with improved hydrogen storage capacity:
 To synthesize four FLP@MOF systems using de novo synthesis approach, followed by the
 structural studies and high-pressure hydrogen adsorption isotherm measurements.
 (3) Enhancement of volumetric density for FLP@MOF and mechanistic understanding of
 hydrogen adsorption in FLP@MOF:
 To conduct mechanistic studies on H2 adsorption in FLP@MOF using several advanced in
 situ characterization techniques and computational methods.

 Page 2 of 19

While the ultimate DOE gravimetric target was not fully reached, the project met key milestones
of demonstrating proof-of-concept; validating improved hydrogen uptake performance for
FLP@MOFs compared to pristine MOFs; and providing mechanistic insights into the adsorption
mechanisms. Moreover, it successfully advanced material design strategies to inform future
work. The key accomplishments of the project were:
 (1) Design and Synthesis:
 Synthesized a wide range of frameworks including MIL-101(Cr),[15] Ni2Cl2(BTDD),[16]
       V2Cl2.8(BTDD),[17] PCN-333(Fe/Cr),[18] NU-1501(Fe),[19] NU-1000,[20] Fe-soc-MOF[21] and
 PCN-222.[22] It was observed that the Fe-based frameworks showed less stability upon FLP
 incorporation, and more stable Cr-based analogs were obtained by post-synthetic
 modification.
 Various N/B based FLP systems labeled FLPs1-4 were optimized and utilized to obtain
       FLPs1-4@MIL-101(Cr), FLPs1-4@PCN-333(Cr), FLP@Ni2Cl2(BTDD), FLP@V2Cl2.8(BTDD)
 systems. The FLP loading amounts were also optimized to enhance the H2 uptake
 capacities.
 (2) Measurement and Validation:
 High uptake capacities were observed for the FLP@MOF systems, reaching up to 2.6 wt%
 for 0.5mmolFLP4@PCN-333(Cr). The results were reproducibly measured at UNT, also
 validated with partner laboratories (NREL, Montana State). The activation protocols for
 solvent removal were optimized with the aid of variable temperature in-situ FTIR
 spectroscopy.
 (3) Mechanistic Insights:
 In-situ IR spectroscopy confirmed direct H2-FLP interactions via n(H–H) shifts studied for
 FLPs@MIL-101(Cr) and FLPs@PCN-333(Cr) systems, while computational simulations on
 FLP1@NU-1501 projected saturation uptakes exceeding 10 wt% at cryogenic conditions
 (77K, 100 bar) and showed a great promise towards achieving 5 wt% total uptake capacity
 at ambient conditions (298K, 100 bar).
Problems and Challenges: Initial discrepancies in results at NREL led to re-calibration using NREL
reference samples. After the calibration of the procedures and the sample amounts, the data
collected showed good consistency and reproducibility.
 3. Summary of Project Activities
The project unfolded across two main phases: Phase I and Phase II.
 • Phase I focused on synthesizing and characterizing diverse FLP@MOF systems, studying
 FLP loading effects, evaluating H2 uptake performance, validating hydrogen uptake data
 at different labs (NREL, Montana State University), and instrument calibration. Go/No-go
 Milestone 1: At least one FLP@MOF with reversible total gravimetric capacity ≥ 1.5 wt %

 Page 3 of 19

 and total volumetric capacity ≥ 0.012 kg H2/L at H2 pressure of £ 100 bar at room
 temperature.
 • Phase II involved optimization of the measurement protocols calibrated using the
 reference Norit Row sample and re-measurement of data based on the optimized
 procedures, accessing high surface-area MOFs and evaluation of the FLP loading amounts
 on H2 uptake capacities, assessment of the activation conditions using in-situ FTIR
 characterization, and mechanistic understanding through in-situ and computational
 studies. Go/No-go Milestone 2: a) At least one FLP@MOF with controlled activation, well-
 characterized composition and structure, and fully reversible total gravimetric capacity ≥
 2.0 wt % (total volumetric capacity ≥ 0.015 kg H2/L) at H2 pressure of £ 100 bar at room
 temperature, and b) results from computational modeling of hypothetical FLP@MOF
 structures which confirm that there is a feasible pathway to materials that can adsorb 5
 wt% H2 at room temperature.
The project activities for the two phases were as follows:
Phase I activities:
 (a) Design and synthesis of FLP@MOF systems
Phase I of the project was dedicated to exploring the breadth of potential host frameworks,
identifying and optimizing the FLP systems, refining post-synthetic strategies, and data
validation. Several MOF families were screened to identify hosts with high porosity, chemical
robustness, and the ability to retain framework stability upon functionalization. The selected
candidates included MIL-101(Cr), PCN-333(Cr), V2Cl2.8(BTDD) and Ni2Cl2(BTDD) as shown in Figure
1.

 Figure 1. The selected MOF candidates featuring high surface areas and stabilities.
In parallel, several N/B based FLP systems were evaluated and down-selected with Lewis bases:
1,4-diazabicyclo[2.2.2]octane (DABCO), hexamethylenetetramine (HMTA) and 2,2',6,6'-
tetramethyl-4,4'-bipyridine; and Lewis acids: tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane [B(C6F5)3] and
mesityl(bis-pentafluorophenyl)borane [MesB(C6F5)3]. The resultant FLPs1-4 based on the Lewis
acid/base combinations are depicted in Figure 2. A step-wise anchoring approach was utilized to
prepare different kinds of FLP@MOFs by varying the coordinating sites, Lewis base or Lewis acid
to generate FLPs1-4@MIL-101(Cr), FLPs1-4@PCN-333(Cr), FLP@Ni2Cl2(BTDD) and
FLP@V2Cl2.8(BTDD).

TEM/EDS mapping confirmed the successful incorporation of FLPs in the MOFs. Taking
FLP1@MIL-101(Cr) as an example (Figure 3), the EDS element mapping showed the existence
and uniform distribution of Cr, F, N indicative of successful incorporation of FLP1 into MIL-
101(Cr); while the XPS analysis suggested the existence of C, N, O, Cr, F in FLP1@MIL-101(Cr).
Also, N, F were not present in pristine MIL-101(Cr), indicative of successful introduction of FLP1
into MIL-101(Cr).

measurement protocols (sample amount, equilibration time) needed to be optimized.

 (b) Assessment of activation conditions using in-situ FTIR
aximizing hydrogen uptake capacity requires complete removal of residual solvents to achieve
effective pore activation. To identify the optimal activation conditions and verify solvent
elimination, variable-temperature in-situ FT-IR spectroscopy was conducted on FLP@MOF
samples. As shown in Figure 10, activation under dynamic vacuum at temperatures above 100 °C
was necessary to fully remove residual toluene from FLP1@MOF, with the FLP moieties
remaining stably incorporated within the pores even after activation at 180 °C. Guided by these
findings, an activation temperature of 120 °C was selected for FLP@MIL-101(Cr) samples. The
corresponding hydrogen adsorption isotherms for FLP1@MIL-101(Cr) and FLP4@MIL-101(Cr)
(Figure 11) revealed an approximate 15% increase in excess uptake capacity following activation,
confirming that the improved performance was attributable to the effective removal of residual
toluene molecules from the pores

 (e) Mechanistic understanding using in-situ and computational studies
 in-situ studies: To probe the direct interaction between H2 and FLP@MIL-101(Cr) samples, in-
situ IR measurements were conducted with the dry sample upon exposure to gaseous H2. Figure
20a clearly showed that H2 is adsorbed in FLP1@MIL-101(Cr), as evidenced by the occurrence of
a distinct band at 4133 cm-1 which is attributed to H-H stretching vibration (ν). Compared with
the value of gas phase ortho-H2 at 4155 cm-1, ν(H-H) in FLP1@MIL-101(Cr) displayed a red-shift
of 22 cm-1, indicating the interaction between H2 and functional groups. The assignment of 4133
cm-1 to adsorbed H2, mostly likely bound to FLP, was further confirmed by measuring FLP1@MIL-
101(Cr) sample after exposure to N2, which did not show such a peak, and by the observation
that it is also absent in H2-exposed pristine MIL-101(Cr) sample. Examination of difference
spectrum (Figure 20b) revealed that upon loading H2, the absorption bands associated with
stretching of C-F bond at different positions on B(C6F5)3, were primarily perturbed, as typified by
the derivative-like features, which provided further evidence that H2 mainly interacts with FLP
groups.

 bar, 25 °C. Each spectrum is referenced to the activated (empty) sample. (b) Difference
 spectrum in the 1800-800 cm-1 region after exposure of MIL-101(Cr)-FLP1 to H2 (top), and IR
 absorbance spectrum of empty FLP1@MIL-101(Cr) (bottom).

In-situ infrared spectroscopy was subsequently conducted on 0.5 mmol FLP1@PCN-333(Cr) to
probe hydrogen adsorption behavior (Figure 21). The ν(H₂) band exhibited a broadened profile
with a doublet-like feature, indicative of multiple adsorption environments within the
framework. This spectral characteristic is consistent with the enhanced hydrogen uptake
observed for 0.5mmolFLP1@PCN-333(Cr) relative to 0.5 mmolFLP4@PCN-333(Cr), which
displayed only a single, sharp ν(H₂) band corresponding to a uniform adsorption environment.
Notably, significant perturbations in the C–F vibrational modes were observed, strongly
suggesting that hydrogen preferentially interacts with the B(C₆F₅) units in the FLP moiety. By
contrast, pristine PCN-333(Cr) revealed only a weak absorption at 4020 cm⁻¹, which can be
tentatively assigned to ν(H₂).
Computational studies: To evaluate the potential of achieving a 5 wt% hydrogen uptake in
FLP@MOF systems under room temperature conditions, computational modeling studies were
conducted in collaboration with the Space group at NCSU. In these preliminary simulations, FLP1
was positioned near an open metal site in NU-1501 (Figure 22) and optimized using the CP2K
simulation package. REPEAT charges were assigned to the resulting structure, followed by
classical Monte Carlo simulations within the Grand Canonical ensemble using the CH4-PHAST
model. In order to obtain a saturation loading, the simulation was undertaken at 77K and 100
bar, producing a projected saturation uptake of over 10 wt%. Moreover, The initial results at 298
K and 100 bar for FLP1@NU-1501 indicated an excess uptake capacity exceeding 2.5 wt%.

Importantly, given the large pore volume of FLP1@NU-1501 (>3.0 cm³/g), the models suggest
strong potential for achieving a total uptake capacity approaching 5.0 wt% at room temperature.

 4. Conclusion
The project demonstrated that FLP@MOFs are technically viable and economically feasible as
hydrogen storage materials. While DOE’s ultimate gravimetric target of 5.5 wt% has not yet been
achieved, the project:
 o Delivered down-selected materials showing reproducible, validated results reaching ~2.6
 wt% uptake
 o Established scalable synthetic protocols
 o Provided deep mechanistic understanding through advanced in-situ characterization, and
 o Confirmed feasibility via computational modeling.
These results lay the groundwork for continued progress to create a coherent roadmap for
achieving higher capacities capable of reaching the DOE targets. With targeted optimization of
FLP loading, framework stability, and activation, FLP@MOFs can meet or exceed DOE storage
goals, advancing hydrogen as a clean, secure, and sustainable energy carrier.
