Healthcare-associated transmission and early IPC system vulnerabilities during the 2026 Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo
Authors: Ben Bepouka, Mardoche Kafua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The 2026 Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak in eastern DRC highlighted important early infection prevention and control (IPC) challenges, including healthcare-associated transmission, healthcare worker infections, unsafe triage systems, limited isolation capacity, and shortages of IPC supplies. This commentary argues that pathogen-specific preparedness may remain insufficient in settings characterized by diagnostic uncertainty and proposes an “IPC-first” outbreak response framework based on rapid syndromic IPC activation before definitive laboratory confirmation becomes available.

Commentary

The re-emergence of Bundibugyo ebolavirus disease (BDBV) in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) in 2026 highlights the persistent vulnerability of resource-constrained health systems to filovirus outbreaks. While major progress has been achieved in Ebola preparedness following previous epidemics in West Africa and eastern DRC, the early phase of the current outbreak suggests that infection prevention and control (IPC) weaknesses remain an important contributor to outbreak amplification, particularly within healthcare settings. According to preliminary WHO AFRO operational reports, healthcare worker infections, healthcare-associated transmission, critically low IPC assessment scores, inadequate triage systems, shortages of IPC supplies, and limited isolation capacity were reported as major operational challenges during the initial response phase [1]. These findings raise important concerns regarding the ability of frontline health facilities to rapidly detect and contain emerging filovirus outbreaks before laboratory confirmation becomes available. In endemic settings such as the Democratic Republic of the Congo, suspected filovirus cases should prompt immediate implementation of core IPC measures and syndromic isolation procedures pending diagnostic confirmation. Because the early clinical manifestations of filovirus diseases are frequently non-specific, continuous frontline awareness and IPC preparedness should be maintained beyond declared outbreak periods, recognizing that delayed IPC activation may be as dangerous as delayed pathogen detection itself.

Healthcare-associated transmission has historically played a central role during Ebola outbreaks. During the 1995 Kikwit outbreak in DRC and the 2014–2016 West African epidemic, inadequate IPC measures, delayed recognition of cases, unsafe triage systems, and insufficient protection of healthcare workers substantially amplified transmission within hospitals and communities [2, 3]. Healthcare worker infections often represent one of the earliest operational indicators of IPC system vulnerabilities during filovirus outbreaks.

The current Bundibugyo outbreak also illustrates the limitations of pathogen-specific preparedness approaches. Initial diagnostic investigations reportedly focused primarily on Zaire ebolavirus, while the circulating pathogen was later identified as Bundibugyo ebolavirus. This delayed etiologic confirmation may have complicated early outbreak recognition; however, core IPC measures should be implemented immediately whenever filovirus infection is suspected, regardless of the specific viral species involved. Pathogen identification remains important for outbreak characterization and vaccination strategies, but frontline IPC activation should not depend on definitive etiologic confirmation.

This commentary discusses the early IPC vulnerabilities identified during the 2026 Bundibugyo outbreak and argues for an “IPC-first” outbreak response framework in epidemic-prone settings.

Healthcare-associated transmission as an early outbreak amplifier

Early operational evidence from the outbreak suggests that healthcare-associated transmission contributed to the initial amplification of the epidemic. According to preliminary WHO AFRO operational reports, four healthcare worker deaths and eight additional healthcare worker infections were reported during the early outbreak phase, further supporting the role of healthcare-associated transmission in outbreak amplification [1]. Such findings are particularly concerning because healthcare worker infections may represent early indicators of IPC system vulnerability during filovirus outbreaks [2, 3].

Unsafe triage systems likely played a major role during the earliest outbreak phase. In many resource-limited healthcare settings, febrile patients with nonspecific symptoms are initially managed in general consultation areas without rapid syndromic isolation procedures. This creates opportunities for exposure of healthcare workers, caregivers, and other hospitalized patients before Ebola virus disease is clinically suspected [4].

The persistence of healthcare-associated transmission during Ebola outbreaks demonstrates that IPC vulnerabilities frequently precede laboratory confirmation and may become the earliest operational signal of outbreak vulnerability. Consequently, IPC should not be viewed merely as a supportive intervention implemented after pathogen confirmation, but rather as a frontline emergency response mechanism activated immediately when suspicious syndromic clusters emerge.

IPC vulnerabilities identified during the Ituri outbreak

Preliminary WHO AFRO operational reports described multiple IPC weaknesses affecting the outbreak response. One of the most concerning findings was the critically low IPC assessment scores reported in affected health facilities. Low IPC performance scores may indicate insufficient preparedness capacity, inadequate staff training, weak supervision systems, poor adherence to standard precautions, a lack of patient triage systems in healthcare facilities; and limited operational readiness. Previous studies have shown that low IPC compliance in fragile health systems significantly increases the risk of healthcare-associated outbreaks during emerging infectious disease emergencies [5]. The reported shortages of IPC kits and supplies further illustrate the operational vulnerability of outbreak preparedness systems. Sustainable IPC preparedness requires uninterrupted availability of gloves, gowns, masks, chlorine solutions for environmental decontamination, hand hygiene materials, and environmental cleaning supplies. Human spraying is not recommended and should not be part of IPC practice. In outbreak settings, supply chain disruptions can rapidly compromise healthcare worker safety and increase nosocomial transmission risk.

Limited isolation capacity also likely contributed to early outbreak amplification. In many peripheral health facilities, dedicated isolation areas remain unavailable or insufficient. Consequently, suspected Ebola patients may initially remain in general wards while awaiting referral or diagnostic confirmation, thereby facilitating transmission to other patients [6].

Inadequate personal protective equipment (PPE) availability and training may further worsen transmission risks. Even when PPE is technically available, inappropriate donning and doffing procedures, fatigue, understaffing, inadequate supervision, and insufficient simulation-based training can compromise healthcare worker protection [14–18].

Together, these findings suggest that early outbreak vulnerability was not driven by a single operational failure, but rather by cumulative weaknesses across frontline IPC systems.

Why IPC vulnerabilities emerged early

Several contextual factors likely contributed to the early IPC vulnerabilities observed during the outbreak.

First, delayed clinical suspicion probably played an important role. Bundibugyo ebolavirus disease often presents with nonspecific symptoms such as fever, fatigue, gastrointestinal symptoms, and malaria-like syndromes during the early phase of illness [8, 9]. Such nonspecific presentations may delay Ebola suspicion, particularly in malaria-endemic regions where febrile illnesses are extremely common. Delayed recognition of non-Zaire filoviruses may therefore postpone IPC activation during the earliest outbreak stages. Delayed clinical suspicion may be reduced through systematic assessment of geographical exposure history, including recent travel, residence in outbreak-prone areas, cross-border movements, and contact with rural forested environments where zoonotic spillover may occur.

Second, vulnerable healthcare infrastructure remains a major challenge in many rural areas of eastern DRC. Overcrowding, inadequate water and sanitation systems, understaffing, limited laboratory decentralization, insufficient IPC supervision, and weak referral systems may all reduce outbreak response capacity [7].

Third, insecurity and population mobility may complicate IPC implementation. Conflict-affected settings frequently experience disruptions in healthcare delivery, limitations in field supervision, delays in transportation of supplies, reduced continuity of IPC monitoring activities, and reduced healthcare accessibility [10, 11, 19].

Together, these factors illustrate how IPC vulnerability is often multifactorial and deeply interconnected with broader health system vulnerability.

The need for an “IPC-first” outbreak response framework

Over the past three decades, IPC priorities during filovirus outbreaks have progressively evolved. The 1995 Kikwit outbreak highlighted the consequences of inadequate barrier nursing [3], while the 2014–2016 West African epidemic led to standardized PPE protocols and dedicated treatment units [1, 13, 14]. The 2018–2020 outbreak in eastern DRC integrated IPC with vaccination strategies [4].

However, the current Bundibugyo outbreak also illustrates the limitations of pathogen-specific preparedness approaches. Preliminary operational investigations initially focused primarily on Zaire ebolavirus diagnostic algorithms before Bundibugyo ebolavirus was subsequently identified. Unlike Zaire Ebola outbreaks, Bundibugyo and Sudan virus outbreaks continue to rely predominantly on rapid IPC implementation because licensed vaccines remain limited or unavailable.

The current outbreak therefore highlights the urgent need to reconsider how IPC activation is integrated into outbreak preparedness frameworks in Africa. In many epidemic-prone settings, reinforced IPC measures are only fully activated after laboratory confirmation of a specific pathogen. However, during rapidly evolving outbreaks, transmission may already be occurring within healthcare facilities before etiologic confirmation becomes available.

The Bundibugyo outbreak demonstrates the limitations of relying exclusively on pathogen-specific preparedness approaches.

An “IPC-first” response framework could help reduce early outbreak amplification. Under such a model, reinforced IPC measures would be triggered by syndromic signals rather than waiting for definitive pathogen identification.

Frontline IPC preparedness may therefore represent one of the most critical determinants of outbreak containment during the earliest response phase [12, 13]. Broader implications for epidemic preparedness

The implications of the current outbreak extend beyond Ebola virus disease alone. Similar IPC vulnerabilities may affect preparedness for Sudan virus disease, Marburg virus disease, mpox, and future “Disease X” scenarios characterized by diagnostic uncertainty during the earliest outbreak stages.

The Bundibugyo outbreak therefore reinforces the importance of syndromic preparedness models capable of triggering rapid frontline IPC activation before pathogen confirmation becomes available. Future epidemic preparedness frameworks may increasingly require integration of IPC readiness, healthcare worker protection, rapid triage systems, robust clinical assessment including geographical exposure history, and decentralized outbreak detection capacity within frontline healthcare systems.

Implementation of strengthened IPC preparedness strategies may be challenging in resource-constrained settings because of financial limitations, workforce shortages, supply-chain vulnerabilities, and competing health priorities. The feasibility of specific interventions may vary according to local epidemiological risk, health system capacity, and available resources. Sustainable progress will likely require long-term domestic investment together with continued support from international partners and donors. Increased financial support from global health financing mechanisms may also help strengthen frontline IPC readiness and rapid diagnostic capacity in epidemic-prone regions. Such investments may represent a cost-effective strategy when compared with the substantial human, economic, and health-system costs associated with large-scale Ebola outbreaks or future emerging infectious disease threats.

Without sustained investment in frontline IPC readiness, healthcare systems in epidemic-prone settings risk becoming amplification hubs rather than barriers against emerging infectious threats.

Conclusion

The 2026 Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak in eastern DRC demonstrates that early operational vulnerabilities during filovirus outbreaks are frequently linked to early IPC vulnerabilities occurring before definitive pathogen confirmation becomes available.

Healthcare-associated transmission, healthcare worker infections, unsafe triage systems, inadequate isolation capacity, limited PPE preparedness, and shortages of IPC supplies may substantially amplify transmission during the critical early outbreak phase.

These findings also demonstrate the limitations of pathogen-specific preparedness approaches in settings characterized by diagnostic uncertainty and resource-constrained healthcare systems.

Future preparedness frameworks in Africa should strengthen early activation of standard and transmission-based IPC measures whenever filovirus infection is suspected. Syndromic recognition and rapid implementation of IPC precautions may help reduce healthcare-associated amplification and improve early outbreak containment in resource-constrained settings.

Because operational data from the ongoing outbreak remain limited and rapidly evolving, some observations discussed in this commentary should be interpreted cautiously pending further epidemiological investigations.

Without rapid syndromic IPC activation, healthcare systems in epidemic-prone settings risk becoming amplifiers rather than barriers against future epidemic threats.
