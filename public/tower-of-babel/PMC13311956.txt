Positive impact of training and support on improving the proficiency of nurses providing palliative care to babies, children and young people in hospital

Context

Children with life-limiting conditions are living for longer and with more complex conditions than ever before. It is estimated that there are more than 21.6 million babies, children and young people (children) in need of palliative care internationally.1 There is worldwide agreement that access to palliative care is a right of these children, and that care should be provided by appropriately trained and experienced professionals within multidisciplinary teams. Despite the acceptance of these guidelines, access to child-specific palliative care training remains unclear, as do the levels of knowledge and experience of nurses who work exclusively with children.

Methods

This study2 was designed to explore the knowledge of children’s palliative care (CPC) in nurses working in a tertiary children’s hospital in Madrid, Spain. A stratified convenience sampling approach was taken to participant recruitment. Participation was open to any nurse caring for children receiving palliative care, including a range of specialities working in both inpatient and homecare settings. A Spanish translated version of the Palliative Care Quiz for Nurses (PCQN-SV)3 completed anonymously, alongside sociodemographic and professional information. Approval for the study was granted by the Ethics Committee for Research on Medicines (CEIm). Convenience sampling was used for participant recruitment. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw appropriately. Statistical analysis of data was conducted using STATA (V.17).

Findings

53 nurses participated in the study. Experience varied between 3 months and 37 years (mean=14 years), with most participants having between 3 months and 5 years of clinical experience. In terms of training, 42.2% of participants working in non-specific CPC areas had no training in CPC, compared with 18.2% of nurses working in CPC. Nurses working in CPC scored higher on the PCQN-SV, as did those with more experience of CPC nursing and with higher levels of education. The study concluded that CPC education and clinical experience are paramount to the development of knowledge acquisition of nurses caring for children with life-limiting conditions.

Commentary

This study2 acknowledged the powerful impact of CPC education on the quality of hospital-based care of children who require palliative care, and their families. The authors suggest the principles of CPC should be included in undergraduate, preregistration nursing education, with higher levels of CPC education being available through postgraduate continuing education courses and master’s degrees, depending on the role of nurses in the provision of CPC.

Anecdotal, practice-based evidence suggests that misunderstandings around the definition and scope of CPC are common in less experienced nurses, in particular, the differences between palliative care and end-of-life care. This is highlighted in a study by Shavlev et al4 exploring the understanding of palliative and hospice care. They found that 73.9% of participants associated palliative care with dying. This reinforces the need for appropriate training to support less experienced nurses when caring for children with life-limiting or life-threatening conditions. An understanding of the differences between palliative care and end of life care is vital to create a trusting, positive experience for children and families, to manage expectations and to break down some of the barriers that persist around the association of palliative care and death.

Understanding the differences between palliative care and end of life care is a pertinent issue for healthcare professionals, as research shows that numbers of babies, children and young people living with life-limiting and life-threatening conditions are increasing worldwide.5 The association of death and palliative care is frequently cited as a barrier to families accessing palliative care services for their child.6 Enabling healthcare professionals to understand that hospice and palliative care services are not only for dying children will help reduce misunderstandings of, and result in timelier referrals to CPC services. Benini et al5 recommend that education (including condition-specific training) is included in undergraduate education, as well as continuing professional development for practitioners. As nurses, preparation and adequate training are essential to ensure both palliative and end-of-life care are delivered the correct way: with care, compassion, patience and understanding, as there is only one chance to get this right.
