Screening for sarcopenia risk using SARC-F and its relationship with laboratory parameters in primary care
Authors: İsmail Çifçi, Bilal Durmaz, Esra Meltem Koç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/aimSarcopenia screening in primary care is limited by time and workload. The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between SARC-F (Strength, Assistance with walking, Rise from a chair, Climb stairs, and Falls)-defined sarcopenia risk and routine laboratory parameters in primary care. Additionally, the study aimed to determine the prevalence of sarcopenia risk, as defined by the SARC-F, in the study population.Materials and methodsData were collected from individuals aged 65 and older (n = 396) registered at two family health centers in İzmir who had complete SARC-F and laboratory data. Sarcopenia risk was defined as a SARC-F score of 4 or higher. Demographic, clinical, and routine laboratory data were obtained from electronic records. Multivariate logistic regression, Pearson chi-square test, and Mann–Whitney U test were used for statistical analysis. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe study included 396 individuals with an average age of 70 ± 6.3 years, of whom 55.1% were female. Sarcopenia risk was present in 16.9% of participants (20.6% in women and 12.3% in men). Multiple logistic regression analyses showed that increased age (OR = 1.250, 95% CI = 1.177–1.327), female sex (OR = 2.284, 95% CI = 1.057–4.934), higher BMI (body mass index) (OR = 1.106, 95% Cl = 1.026–1.193), lower ferritin (OR = 0.992, 95% Cl = 0.985–1.000), and lower albumin (OR = 0.777, 95% Cl = 0.693–0.871) levels were associated with an increased risk of sarcopenia.ConclusionThe results of our study indicate that the risk of sarcopenia can be quite a common clinical condition in primary care. When increased clinical demands and limited time hinder the use of tests such as SARC-F, routine laboratory tests may help identify individuals at risk of sarcopenia.Keywords: Sarcopenia risk factors, family practice, serum albumins, older adults, ferritins

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a progressive muscle disorder characterized by loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength, and function. It poses a serious threat to the independence of older adults [1]. The reduction in muscle tissue negatively affects aerobic capacity and metabolic functions. Clinical signs may include difficulty walking, repeated falls, difficulty rising from a chair, difficulty climbing stairs, and involuntary weight loss. Sarcopenia is often overlooked in clinical practice because of competing demands and limited time. Failure to identify at-risk individuals early can delay preventive interventions and increase the risk of falls, fractures, decreased quality of life, functional dependence, hospitalization, and death. The etiology of sarcopenia is multifactorial: physical inactivity, endocrine disorders (especially diabetes), chronic diseases, inadequate protein intake, and systemic inflammation are the leading causes [2–4].

Recently, sarcopenia risk assessment using SARC-F has been integrated into older adults’ health screenings in primary care settings in our country. The European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP2) recommends using the SARC-F test to identify individuals at risk of sarcopenia. EWGSOP2 and the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) guidelines also recommend SARC-F for case-finding, with a similar cut-off score of ≥4 [5,6]. The SARC-F questionnaire is a practical screening tool for primary healthcare because it is easy to use, affordable, and saves time. It inquires about muscle strength, walking ability, standing up from a chair, climbing stairs, and a history of falls. Previous meta-analyses have shown that SARC-F has relatively low sensitivity but moderate-to-high specificity (approximately 69–91%), making it a useful screening tool for identifying individuals who require further diagnostic evaluation for sarcopenia [7,8]. Although the SARC-F test is easy to administer, the numerous assessments involved in comprehensive older adults’ health monitoring by family physicians in our country, combined with other chronic disease screenings, make it unfeasible to conduct these tests on every outpatient. This also necessitates prioritizing which patients should be screened first [9].

In another recent community-based study from the literature, the risk of sarcopenia (SARC-F score ≥4) was found to be 23.0% in women and 12.8% in men [10]. In Turkey, a study conducted at a university hospital’s geriatrics clinic, using the EWGSOP2 criteria, found a prevalence of sarcopenia of 12.7% [11]. In a recent study, the risk rate of sarcopenia screened with SARC-F among hospitalized patients in our country’s geriatric clinics was found to be 35.0% in university hospitals, 29.6% in training and research hospitals, and 23.5% in private hospitals (41). However, the lack of recent community-based research on sarcopenia risk in primary healthcare settings remains a gap in the literature.

Including biochemical markers in case-finding protocols for identifying individuals at higher SARC-F-defined risk would assist primary care physicians in managing the condition. Although many laboratory parameters, such as albumin, aspartate aminotransferase/alanine aminotransferase ratio (De Ritis ratio), and systemic inflammation markers, have been linked to sarcopenia in previous studies, laboratory parameters for individuals at risk of sarcopenia have not been established [12–14]. Common laboratory tests can help primary care physicians assess sarcopenia risk.

The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between SARC-F-defined sarcopenia risk and routine laboratory parameters in primary care. Additionally, the study aimed to determine the prevalence of sarcopenia risk, as defined by the SARC-F, in the study population.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at two family health centers (FHCs) in Izmir, Turkey. The study population consisted of 2,146 individuals aged 65 years or older who were registered at these centers. Using a known-population formula (Cochran’s), with an estimated prevalence of 18%, a 95% confidence level, and a 5% margin of error, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 205. Of these, individuals who had attended the FHCs for routine screening within the previous six months and had complete SARC-F and laboratory data were assessed for eligibility (n = 422). A total of 26 participants were excluded for meeting the predefined exclusion criteria. The final analytic sample consisted of 396 participants. Participants in the study were evaluated as part of the group with chronic diseases, including hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, hypothyroidism/hyperthyroidism, asthma, COPD, chronic kidney disease, rheumatic diseases, and inflammatory diseases.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria were severe kidney failure (GFR <30%) or dialysis treatment, active cancer, diseases causing malnutrition, dementia, and a previous diagnosis of sarcopenia.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Committee of İzmir Katip Çelebi University (approval no: 0316), and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection

This study was a cross-sectional study that used clinical and laboratory data obtained from electronic medical records, including the SARC-F questionnaire administered during routine screening visits. The SARC-F test and laboratory parameters were collected simultaneously during the individual’s same visit. Laboratory tests and the SARC-F questionnaire were part of routine screening for people registered at the FHC, regardless of whether or not they had a chronic illness.

Assessment of sarcopenia risk

The SARC-F questionnaire was used to assess sarcopenia risk. SARC-F is a self-report assessment tool developed for identifying individuals at risk of sarcopenia. It provides a quick, practical screening tool in clinical practice. SARC-F is generally considered a screening tool for identifying individuals who require further diagnostic evaluation rather than a diagnostic tool itself. The SARC-F questionnaire consists of 5 questions regarding strength (lifting/carrying 10 kg), walking, getting up from a chair/bed, climbing 10 stairs, and fall history in the last year. Each question is scored 0–2, and a total score of 4 or higher indicates an increased risk of sarcopenia. Family physicians administered the questionnaire during routine visits. The validity and reliability of the scale in Turkish were examined by Bahat in 2018 [15].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25. Frequencies and percentages were used to analyze categorical variables. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median [IQR], depending on distribution. We used the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality and Levene’s test for homogeneity of variance. Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical comparisons, and independent-sample t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were used for continuous variables.

Additionally, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with SARC-F-defined sarcopenia risk. Results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants’ demographic, clinical, and biochemical characteristics

A total of 396 individuals (mean age, 70 ± 6.3 years) were included in the study. Of the participants, 55.1% were female, the mean BMI was 28.1 ± 4.4 kg/m2, and 83.1% had at least one chronic disease. The prevalence of SARC-F-defined sarcopenia risk (SARC-F score ≥4), was 16.9% (20.6% in women and 12.3% in men).

The risk of sarcopenia was found to be significantly higher among older individuals, women, individuals with a high BMI, and individuals with chronic disease (Table 1).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

In the study, multiple regression was used to identify relationships. Variables were chosen based on clinical importance, prior research, and our study findings, ensuring minimal redundancy among highly correlated markers. Significant PLR and NLR ratios were used as hemogram parameters. Regression analysis of all hemogram parameters showed that highly correlated values adversely affected the results. Additionally, age, BMI, sex, albumin, ferritin, total cholesterol, De Ritis ratio, and chronic disease were included in the model. According to our model, each additional year age increased the risk of sarcopenia by 25%, and a one-unit increase in BMI increased the risk by 10.6%. Each one-unit increase in albumin decreased the risk of sarcopenia by 22.3%, and each one-unit increase in ferritin decreased it by 0.8% (Table 4).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional primary care study, older age, higher BMI, female sex, lower albumin, and lower ferritin levels were associated with SARC-F-defined sarcopenia risk, while the estimated prevalence of SARC-F-defined risk was 16.9%.

In the literature, the global prevalence of sarcopenia risk in population-based studies conducted among older adults using the EWGSOP2 criteria is reported to range from 7% to 30% [10,16–18]. There are a limited number of studies in Turkey reporting the prevalence of sarcopenia risk according to the EWGSOP2 criteria. In a recent study, the risk rate of sarcopenia screened with SARC-F among hospitalized patients in our country’s geriatric clinics was found to be 35.0% in university hospitals, 29.6% in training and research hospitals, and 23.5% in private hospitals [19]. The main reasons for the wide range of results in this study are that the study is not population-based and that the participants’ existing illnesses make them more susceptible to sarcopenia. Our study differs from others in that it reflects the general population in primary healthcare settings and uses the current screening algorithm.

Our study demonstrates an increasing prevalence of sarcopenia risk with age. This finding is consistent with previous studies. Progressive muscle loss associated with aging has been documented in many studies [20]. The increasing prevalence can be attributed to natural aging processes, including increased protein breakdown, increased fat infiltration into muscle tissue, impaired body homeostasis, decreased circulating hormones, and elevated inflammatory cytokines [21]. In community-based studies previously conducted using SARC-F to assess sarcopenia risk, women were found to have a higher prevalence of sarcopenia risk. In a study conducted in Southampton, the sarcopenia risk was 23.0% in women and 12.8% in men. İn another study conducted in Korea, the percentage in women was nearly four times higher than in men [10,22]. In our study, consistent with the literature, a significant relationship between female gender and the risk of sarcopenia was identified in univariate analyses. This finding persisted in the multivariate logistic regression analysis.

Obesity, considered a worldwide metabolic pandemic, is a risk factor for many diseases. In geriatric patients, increased adipose tissue and muscle loss are age-related. This leads to increased morbidity and mortality rates, disability, and hospitalizations [23]. Sarcopenic obesity, which occurs due to aging, is a common clinical condition but is rarely detected in clinical practice [24]. Our study findings, from both univariable and multivariable analyses, demonstrate a significant association between higher body mass index and an increased risk of sarcopenia. This connection is probably related to the high obesity rates in our country. Sarcopenia is frequently overlooked because it is linked to thinness. Hence, it is crucial to screen obese individuals for sarcopenia as well.

Several previous studies have found an association between higher ferritin levels and the risk of sarcopenia [25,26]. In our study, multiple regression analyses revealed that low ferritin levels were associated with an increased risk of sarcopenia. This difference may be due to the population-based nature of our research. Earlier studies in the literature have been conducted on patients with cirrhosis and those undergoing hemodialysis. Elevated ferritin levels in these groups are likely caused by chronic inflammation. The study on the hemodialysis group showed evidence of anemia of chronic disease in the sarcopenia-risk group.

Serum albumin has previously been identified as a valuable indicator of sarcopenia risk in hemodialysis patients and those with cirrhosis [27]. Based on our data, no studies have evaluated the relationship between sarcopenia risk and albumin in a population-based group. In our research, we observed a significant reduction in serum albumin levels in the sarcopenia-risk group compared to the non-risk group. This association remained significant in the logistic regression analysis. Additionally, there was no notable difference in serum albumin levels between the chronic disease and non-chronic disease groups. This finding suggests that serum albumin is also a valuable marker in population-based groups screened using the SARC-F.

In a study evaluating the relationship between sarcopenia risk and ALT and AST, it was observed that these parameters alone were not an indicator. In this study, the sarcopenia group showed high AST levels and low ALT levels. However, the De Ritis ratio was not calculated in that study [28]. To our knowledge, no study has examined the relationship between the SARC-F-defined sarcopenia risk group and the De Ritis ratio. In our study, no statistically significant relationship was found between sarcopenia risk and the De Ritis ratio. This finding needs to be supported by larger-scale studies.

Previous studies have found a significant association between high NLR and the risk of sarcopenia in a group of patients with gastrointestinal diseases and in a group of hospitalized cancer patients [29,30]. In our study, no statistically significant relationship was found between sarcopenia risk and NLR or PLR ratios. This situation may be explained by previous studies conducted among hospitalized patients at high risk of inflammation. The limited number of population-based studies in this area highlights the need for further research on these ratios in relation to sarcopenia risk. It is expected that future studies will establish cut-off values for these ratios.

There is no approved specific medication for sarcopenia. The only intervention proven to prevent and mitigate sarcopenia is the continuation of resistance exercises and physical activity. Clinicians should also encourage physical activity rather than solely focusing on laboratory tests when identifying potential sarcopenia cases [31].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, because it was conducted with individuals who applied to two FHCs in a specific region, the results’ generalizability may be limited. Important variables such as nutritional status, alcohol use, liver disease, medications, acute illness, and physical activity could not be evaluated. Because only participants with complete SARC-F and laboratory data were included, selection bias cannot be ruled out. Given the number of SARC-F-positive cases relative to the candidate predictors, overfitting cannot be entirely excluded. Furthermore, since SARC-F is a screening tool rather than a diagnostic standard, our results should be interpreted as indicating SARC-F-defined risk rather than a confirmed diagnosis of sarcopenia.

Implications for practice and research

In primary care, routine laboratory tests can help clinicians identify older adults who require closer assessment for SARC-F-defined sarcopenia risk, especially when time constraints limit thorough screening. However, laboratory markers should supplement, not replace, clinical screening and advice on physical activity. Future prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate laboratory-supported case-finding strategies and establish reference standards.

Conclusion

The results of our study indicate that the risk of sarcopenia can be quite a common clinical condition in primary care. Routine laboratory tests may support the identification of individuals at risk of sarcopenia in situations where tests like SARC-F cannot be performed due to increased clinical demands and time limitations. However, as this condition has no evidence-based pharmacological treatment, clinicians should not overlook providing basic recommendations such as physical activity, even while focusing on laboratory tests.
