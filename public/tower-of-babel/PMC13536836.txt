Mental health literacy among nurses at community health centers in rural Rwanda
Authors: Marie Chantal Ingabire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimTo assess mental health literacy and its association with professional variables among community health center (CHC) nurses.BackgroundMental health problems are highly prevalent in post-conflict countries, but access to treatment is acutely limited. The integration of mental health care into primary healthcare (PHC) is one of the key recommended strategies to scale up access to treatment. However, little is known about PHC nurses’ mental health literacy (MHL).MethodSeventy-four nurses (46 women, 28 men) were recruited from 13 CHCs in the western province of Rwanda. Participants answered a questionnaire about their professional characteristics and MHL, namely, the recognition of mental disorders depicted in clinical vignettes, knowledge of risk factors for mental disorders, and knowledge of helpful people and interventions.ResultsMost participants correctly identified Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (63.5%), followed by depression (60.8%), Somatic Symptom Disorder (51.3%), schizophrenia (51.3%), and Generalized Anxiety Disorder (25.7%). The majority of participants correctly recognized the risk factors for mental disorders (e.g., death of a loved one), but a considerable proportion endorsed false causes (e.g., evil possession, weakness of character). Most participants indicated that psychologists/counselors were helpful for all the disorders. Education and in-service mental health training were not associated with MHL.ConclusionsThe mental health literacy of CHC nurses is limited in different ways. Strengthening both academic and continual mental health training programs may enhance nurses’ MHL and, consequently, early detection and treatment of mental disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13033-026-00715-y.

Background

Mental disorders are common in Sub-Saharan Africa, with a heightened severity in countries with a history of political violence and armed conflicts [1]. However, the availability of and access to mental health care are strikingly inadequate relative to the needs, with as many as 85% of individuals with common mental disorders not receiving treatment [2, 3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) [4] recommended integrating mental health into primary healthcare (PHC) to bridge this treatment gap. Rwanda has made progress in this area by incorporating mental health care into services provided at community health centers (CHC) [5]. However, little is known about the level of mental health knowledge among CHC professionals. This study aimed to address this gap by investigating the mental health literacy of nurses working at CHCs.

Mental disorders are highly prevalent in sub-Saharan Africa, especially in conflict-affected countries [1, 6]. For example, estimates of Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) reach 30% or higher [1, 7]. The 1994 genocide perpetrated against the Tutsi in Rwanda led to long-term mental health problems, principally PTSD (26% − 37%) [8, 9], depression (13% − 33%) [10], and Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD; 37%) [11]. Several challenges hinder access to mental health care in Rwanda, including the availability of services, financial constraints, poor mental health literacy, and pervasive social stigma [12–14]. WHO recommended integrating mental health into PHC as a strategy to overcome barriers to mental health treatment [4, 15]. In Rwanda, the integration of mental health services into primary healthcare was first articulated in the national mental health policy, after which general nurses began receiving continuous training in mental health care [5, 12]. Evidence regarding the nature of this training remains scarce, particularly with respect to its content and duration. Moreover, although mental health is one of the subjects covered in nursing school, the specific curriculum is not publicly available. Therefore, research is needed to understand nurses’ level of mental health knowledge, which is essential for recognizing and treating mental disorders.

The Rwandan health system comprises community health workers (trained volunteers), community health centers and health posts, district hospitals, provincial referral hospitals, and national referral and teaching hospitals [16]. At the community health center level, services are provided by nurses. When necessary, patients, including those with mental health conditions, are referred to district, provincial, and national hospitals, where physicians of various specialties provide more advanced services [12]. Over the past 20 years, nursing education in Rwanda has undergone substantial reforms. Until 2007, a high school diploma was sufficient to practice; currently, nurses are trained through advanced university diplomas, bachelor’s, and master’s programs in various specialties, including mental health nursing [17, 18]. Although mental health is included in nursing education, information on the curriculum and its evolution alongside these reforms remains publicly unavailable.

Mental health literacy (MHL) indicates knowledge and beliefs about mental health that help identify, manage, or prevent mental disorders [19]. MHL includes several core components, namely the ability to recognize specific mental disorders, knowledge of risk factors and causes of these disorders, awareness of sources of mental health information, knowledge of self-management strategies, familiarity with available professional help, and attitudes that support recognition and help-seeking behaviors [19].

Studies have shown that less than 40% of primary healthcare (PHC) workers in Sub-Saharan Africa could correctly identify common mental disorders [20, 21]. Among Kenyan PHC workers, 36% correctly identified depression, while only 16% recognized schizophrenia [20]. A similar study conducted in Ethiopia found that, before receiving mental health training, only 32% and 34% of PHC nurses could recognize psychosis and depression, respectively.

Overall, African MHL studies have reported low levels of knowledge of risk factors for mental disorders and appropriate professional help. In one study, before being trained, Ethiopian PHC workers showed overall poor knowledge of risk factors for mental disorders [21]. Korhonen and colleagues [22] found that the level of knowledge of risk factors and causes of mental disorders varied from low to high among South African and Zambian PHC workers. Kenyan PHC workers believed that people with a mental disorder should seek help from a psychiatrist or a mental hospital (45%), despite those specialists being scarce in rural areas in Kenya and across sub-Saharan Africa [20, 23]. Notably, only 12% of participants in that study believed PHC workers could offer a helpful intervention [20]. These beliefs that privileged help from mental health specialists may not support the integration of mental health into PHC.

Furthermore, some factors have been shown to influence MHL, though these findings have been inconsistent. A higher education level has been linked to better MHL among South African and Zambian PHC workers and Ghanaian laypeople [22, 24], while no such link was found in Kenya [20]. Receiving in-service mental health training was associated with higher MHL among Ethiopian and Nigerian PHC workers [21, 25] but not among PHC workers in Kenya, South Africa, and Zambia [20, 22].

In sum, studies conducted among PHC workers in Sub-Saharan African countries suggest limited MHL. PHC workers’ MHL is crucial for effectively integrating mental health care into PHC [26, 27], as they are often the first health professionals people encounter and, therefore, have a unique opportunity to diagnose and treat illnesses, including mental disorders, at an early stage. This study aimed to assess the MHL of CHC nurses in a rural area of Rwanda. First, we examined nurses’ ability to recognize common mental disorders, namely PTSD, depression, GAD, somatic symptom disorder (SSD), and schizophrenia; their knowledge of risk factors for these disorders; and helpful people and interventions. Second, we examined whether education and in-service mental health training were associated with MHL.

Methods

Setting and design

This study used a cross-sectional design to assess the MHL of nurses working at CHCs. Participants were recruited from 13 CHCs in one district of the western province of Rwanda.

Participants

The target population was relatively small, with 120 nurses across the 13 CHCs. A convenient, total population sampling strategy was used. Out of 120 potential participants, 20 nurses (17%) declined to participate in the study, whereas 26 nurses (22%) did not participate because they were on leave. The remaining sample included 74 nurses who had been in their positions at CHCs for an average of seven years.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire, developed based on prior research [28] was piloted among ten nurses from a rural and urban CHC. Before piloting, the questionnaire was developed in English and translated into Kinyarwanda (the local language) by the author. Participants in the pilot study recommended translating the questionnaire into French, as it was the language of their formal education and was routinely used for technical medical content. The author translated the questionnaire into French, and a mental health clinician conducted a back translation into English. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved. The questionnaire had two sections. The first covered basic socio-demographic and professional information, and the second included the different components of MHL.

Demographic and professional profile:

Participants answered questions about their age, gender, education, and professional background, namely, their formal training area, years of professional experience, and prior in-service mental health training.

Mental Health Literacy (MHL): Three components of MHL: [1] recognition of mental disorders [2], knowledge of their risk factors, and [3] beliefs about helpful people and interventions were assessed. A vignette-based methodology frequently used in MHL studies [28, 29] was adopted to measure the recognition of mental disorders. Participants were presented with five vignettes measuring GAD, PTSD, depression, SSD, and schizophrenia. The depression and GAD vignettes were adopted from the Mental Health Literacy Scale [29]. PTSD, SSD, and schizophrenia vignettes (see Table 1 for examples) were developed by the author based on the diagnostic criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-IV [30]. The vignettes did not contain names to avoid gender-based or other social biases. Participants had to read each vignette and choose the most likely diagnosis from nine options: [1] depression [2], schizophrenia [3], GAD [4], SSD [5], PTSD [6], personality disorder [7], obsessive-compulsive disorder [8], substance use disorder, and [9] other. A score of 1 was allocated for a correct response, a score of zero was given for an incorrect response, and a total score was computed (possible range: 0 –5). The median (= 3) was used to categorize the level of recognition as low (three or fewer correctly recognized vignettes) or high (four or five correctly identified vignettes).

To assess the knowledge of risk factors and causes of mental disorders, participants were presented with 24 statements adapted from prior research [28, 31]. They were asked to indicate if each statement was true or false. Examples of those statements included financial problems, brain chemical imbalance, curses, possession by evil spirits, and the assertion that “Middle-aged or elderly individuals are unlikely to develop psychological problems and mental disorders.” Participants obtained a score of 1 for a correct response and zero for an incorrect response. One item (“virus or infection”) was removed from the list of risk factors for mental disorders, as only some viruses or infections are associated with the development of mental disorders. Therefore, this item could not be clearly classified as entirely true or false. A total score of 23 items was calculated; participants’ scores ranged from 11 to 21. The median (= 16) was used as a splitting point to determine the level of knowledge of risk factors for mental disorders (11–16: moderate; 17–21: high). Finally, participants were asked to indicate people and interventions they believed to be helpful for people suffering from depression, GAD, PTSD, SSD, and schizophrenia. The frequency of indicated people and interventions was calculated for each mental disorder.

Data collection

Data were collected between January and February 2018 by the author, who was then a master-level psychologist experienced in research and clinical practice in Rwanda. Participants were recruited at their respective CHCs. They completed a pencil-and-paper questionnaire that took approximately 30 min to complete. Before participating in the study, each participant received an information letter outlining the study’s purpose, potential benefits, possible inconveniences, the voluntary nature of participation, and assurance of data confidentiality. Informed consent was obtained in writing from all participants. Participants received no financial incentive for their participation in the study. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Global Health Equity (Protocol # 0038).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize all demographic information and MHL components. Chi-square tests of independence were used to examine associations between MHL and professional characteristics. The significance level was set at 0.05. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.

Most participants were middle-aged women trained in general nursing. On average, they had worked at the CHC for seven years. Only a small proportion (n = 20; 27%) had received in-service training on mental health.

Knowledge of the risk factors for mental disorders

Participants’ knowledge about the risk factors for mental disorders is presented in Tables 4 and 5. Almost all participants identified the combination of biological, psychological, and social factors (98.6%), chemical imbalance in the brain (93.2%), and death of a loved one (90.5%) as risk factors for mental disorders. However, a high proportion of participants believed that thinking too much (79.9%), evil possession (71,6%), witchcraft (64.9%), and weakness of character (56,8%) could lead to the development of mental disorders (see Table 4 for full results). Furthermore, most participants correctly recognized that middle-aged or elderly people could suffer from mental disorders (85.1%) and that medications for schizophrenia should be taken on a long-term basis (85.1%). However, a considerable number of participants thought that mental disorders could not be prevented (41.9%) or cured (44.6%; see Table 5 for full results). Thirty-nine participants (52.7%) had moderate knowledge, while 35 participants (47.3%) demonstrated high knowledge of risk factors for mental disorders.

Perceived helpful people and interventions

As outlined in Table 6, psychologists and counselors were most commonly identified as the go-to professionals for help for all mental disorders, followed by mental health nurses, psychiatrists, and medical doctors. Religious leaders, family members, and friends were named least often. The majority of participants considered counselling or psychotherapy to be a helpful intervention for all disorders except schizophrenia. For schizophrenia specifically, 79.7% of participants viewed medication as the most beneficial intervention. A very low percentage endorsed prayer as a helpful intervention for any mental disorder.

Factors associated with MHL

The Chi-square test of independence showed that education (high school versus university) was not related to the level of recognition of mental disorders (low versus high), X2 (1,74) = 1.49, p = .22; or the knowledge of risk factors for mental disorders (moderate versus high), X2 (1,74) = 0.80, p = .37. In-service mental health training (training/no training) was not associated with the level of recognition of mental disorders (low versus high), X2 (1,74) = 0.47, p = .54, or the knowledge of risk factors for mental disorders (moderate versus high), X2 (1,74) = 0.08, p = .77.

Discussion

The integration of mental health into primary healthcare is one of the key strategies recommended to scale up access to mental health care in low-and middle-income countries. The present study sought to evaluate mental health literacy (MHL) among nurses who worked at community health centers in a rural area of Rwanda. The findings revealed that the overall MHL level was moderate, with variations ranging from low to high depending on specific components of MHL. Education and in-service mental health training were not significantly associated with MHL levels.

PTSD was the most frequently recognized disorder (63.5%). This recognition level was higher than reported among hospital workers (primarily nurses) in the United Arab Emirates (47%) [32]. This finding is not surprising given the high prevalence of PTSD in Rwanda, particularly among genocide survivors [9, 33]. Further, during the annual genocide commemoration period, which is a triggering event, some people who suffer from PTSD exhibit intense re-experiencing and dissociative symptoms [34] and are referred to community health centers for care [35]. Therefore, one can argue that nurses may have encountered people presenting PTSD symptoms in their workplace. It is worth noting that prior African MHL studies did not assess the recognition of PTSD [20, 24]. In those countries (e.g., Ghana) with no history of widespread mass violence, PTSD is not as common as in Rwanda [36].

Slightly more than 60% of participants were able to identify depression. This number is higher than those reported among Kenyan PHC workers (36%) [20], Ghanaian lay people (47%) [24], Arabic Emirates hospital workers (54%) [32], and Chinese doctors (58%) [37]. Even though this number is comparatively higher, it suggests that four of ten cases of depression may go undetected when depression is highly prevalent in Rwanda as a stand-alone diagnosis and as a comorbid disorder of PTSD [8, 10].

Only half of the participants (51%) correctly identified the SSD. In non-Western cultures, including African cultures, psychological distress is sometimes expressed in the form of somatic complaints, which tend to be culturally and socially more acceptable than emotional symptoms [38]. This result suggests that half of the cases of SSD may go undetected and untreated. Only 25.7% of participants correctly identified GAD. In a study of Chinese non-mental health professionals, 32% correctly recognized GAD [37]. This low level of recognition of GAD among Rwandan nurses sounds the alarm, given its high prevalence in different groups, such as genocide survivors and young people [39, 40]. Similar to other mental disorders, untreated GAD is associated with marked disability [3], overutilization of PHC services [41], and economic burden [42].

Herein, 51% of participants recognized schizophrenia, a proportion that is higher than that reported among Kenyan PHC workers (24%) [20], Chinese doctors (49%) [37], and Ghanaian lay people (16%) [24]. Schizophrenia is the most severe mental disorder that one would expect nurses to know, given that many schizophrenia symptoms (incoherent speech, poor physical presentation) can be externally observed. A delayed schizophrenia diagnosis, or worse, untreated schizophrenia, can lead to a poor prognosis [43] and premature mortality [44].

Overall, nurses had good knowledge of risk factors for mental disorders and mental disorder-related epidemiology. Strikingly, however, a considerable number of nurses simultaneously hold sociocultural beliefs about the causes of mental disorders. For example, more than 50% of participants thought that witchcraft, curses, and evil possession could lead to the development of mental disorders. Moreover, a number of participants thought that mental disorders could not be cured (44.6%) or prevented (41.9%). This finding suggests that these health professionals are not immune to the prevailing social and cultural beliefs about mental health [11].

Regarding helpful people, psychologists were most frequently indicated as helpful persons for all disorders, including schizophrenia. Two findings related to helpful people were noteworthy. First, a low number of participants (18% − 31%) indicated psychiatrists as helpful people for mental disorders, including schizophrenia. This finding suggests that nurses were not aware of the vital role of psychiatrists in treating mental disorders. Second, a small proportion of participants named trained nurses (22% − 32%) or doctors (9% − 15%) as helpful people in treating mental disorders. This finding implies that most nurses may not have internalized their role in mental health care.

In this study, a few nurses (27%) had received in-service mental health training. The same finding was reported among Zambian and South African PHC workers [22], while in Kenya, only 9% of PHC workers had received continual mental health training [20]. Further, no significant associations were found between education level, in-service mental health training, and MHL. Similar findings were reported among Kenyan PHC workers [20]. In this sample, training varied in terms of duration and provider. These variations, combined with the small number of trained nurses, prevent any definitive conclusions about the effectiveness of continuous mental health training in enhancing MHL.

Taken together, these findings have practical implications for mental health policy and service delivery. To strengthen MHL among nurses in Rwanda, policy efforts should prioritize the integration of standardized mental health content into nursing curricula at all levels of training, with clear competencies and measurable outcomes. The curricula could include risk factors for mental disorders (biological, psychological, socio-cultural), the symptomatology of common mental disorders, their assessment, and existing psychological and pharmacological treatments. Additionally, structured, evidence-based in-service training programs should be implemented and regularly updated and evaluated to ensure the sustained acquisition of knowledge and skills.

This study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The sample was conveniently recruited from community health centers under the catchment of one rural hospital district. Consequently, this sample is not representative of Rwandan nurses, and the results are not generalizable. We did not collect the characteristics of those who declined to participate or those on leave; the selection bias cannot be ruled out. Additionally, a larger number of trained nurses and a greater variation in educational level (e.g., high school, bachelor’s, and master’s levels) would have permitted a more reliable assessment of the relationship between in-service training, education, and MHL. Future studies should strive to recruit larger and more representative samples of PHC nurses. To identify the correct diagnosis and risk factors for mental disorders, participants were presented with multiple choices. It is possible that some participants would have been unable to correctly diagnose the disorders if they had been asked to generate the diagnosis by themselves. Consequently, this study may have overestimated nurses’ level of MHL. Future studies could adopt a methodology that requires participants to independently formulate diagnoses for mental disorders presented in vignettes [32]. Future studies should assess cultural influences on nurses’ MHL and the extent to which MHL affects clinical practice. It is also pertinent to investigate MHL among the general population.

Conclusion

Notwithstanding these limitations, to our knowledge, this study is the first to examine mental health literacy among primary healthcare nurses in Rwanda. Results showed that nurses’ MHL is limited in different areas. This topic merits consideration as these nurses constitute the first point of contact with the healthcare system and have the unique opportunity to detect and treat mental disorders early. These findings have practical implications for mental health delivery in resource-constrained settings. They suggest that sustained efforts are needed to integrate mental health into primary healthcare successfully. Strengthening mental health-related academic curricula and continuous training programs may enhance nurses’ mental health literacy.
