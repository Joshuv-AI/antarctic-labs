Real‐World Effectiveness and Safety of Finerenone in People With Type 2 Diabetes and Chronic Kidney Disease: A Spanish Multicentre Retrospective Observational Study (MEDFINE‐RWD)
Authors: Oscar Moreno‐Pérez, Antonio Tejera‐Muñoz, Patricia Tomás‐Gómez, Federico Vázquez‐San Miguel, Virginia Soria‐Utrilla, Ada María Roldán‐Sánchez, Maya Sánchez‐Baya, Jersy Cárdenas‐Salas, Blanca Timón‐Vázquez, Juan J Gorgojo‐Martínez, Ines Modrego‐Pardo, Irea Fernández‐Xove, Jose Carlos Fernández‐García, Rebeca Reyes‐García
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimAlthough finerenone has shown efficacy in randomized trials, evidence from routine clinical practice remains limited. We aimed to assess the real‐world effectiveness and safety of finerenone in people with type 2 diabetes (PWT2D) and chronic kidney disease (CKD), with a particular focus on residual cardiorenal risk as reflected by changes in urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR).Materials and MethodsMEDFINE‐RWD was a multicentre, retrospective study across eight Spanish centres, enrolling patients initiating finerenone (June 2024–December 2025). The primary endpoint was a ≥ 30% reduction in UACR. Multivariable logistic regression identified response predictors. Secondary outcomes included safety (hyperkalaemia ≥ 5.5 mmol/L, major kidney [MAKE] and cardiovascular events [MACE]).ResultsAmong 844 participants (median age 72 years; median follow‐up 184 days; 76.8% male; baseline eGFR 49 mL/min/1.73 m2; UACR 285 mg/g; 77.8% were at high/very high KDIGO risk), finerenone was associated with a 34.5% median UACR reduction at 6 months (p < 0.001), despite intensive SGLT2 inhibitors (88.4%) and GLP‐1 receptor agonists (35.7%) use. The primary endpoint was achieved by 52.7%, consistently across background renoprotection or baseline KDIGO risk strata. Predictors of response were age ≥ 70 (aOR 2.12), baseline UACR > 245 mg/g (aOR 1.94), and BMI > 27 kg/m2 (aOR 1.84), while insulin use was associated with lower response odds. Treatment persistence was 89.5%, with low incidences of hyperkalaemia (4.8%), MAKE (0.9%), and MACE (1.1%).ConclusionsIn real‐world practice, finerenone was associated with a substantial reduction in albuminuria. Finerenone demonstrated high persistence and a favourable safety profile, irrespective of baseline renoprotective regimens or KDIGO risk.Keywords: chronic kidney disease, finerenone, MEDFINE, observational study, real world data, type 2 diabetes

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are primary drivers of global morbidity, with an estimated prevalence rate of 13% and 10% in the adult population, respectively [1]. Approximately 40% of people with T2D (PWT2D) develop CKD, a phenotypic overlap that significantly escalates clinical risk [2, 3, 4]. PWT2D with CKD experience a 40% higher risk of cardiovascular events and all‐cause mortality [5, 6], which reinforces the important need for integrated cardiorenal management strategies to mitigate the cumulative risk of heart failure, myocardial infarction, and premature mortality in this high‐risk scenario [7].

In T2D‐associated CKD, mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) overactivation promotes proinflammatory and profibrotic damage, escalating cardiorenal risk [8, 9]. Finerenone, a selective non‐steroidal MR antagonist, significantly reduced renal progression by 18% in the FIDELIO‐DKD and composite cardiovascular outcomes by 13% in the FIGARO‐DKD [3, 10]. The clinical efficacy of finerenone was also synthesized in the FIDELITY pooled analysis [11], providing the evidence base that underpins current international guideline recommendations [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. These guidelines now establish finerenone as a cornerstone of nephroprotection in PWT2D, integrated into a comprehensive therapeutic framework alongside renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system inhibitors (RAAS), sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs).

The therapeutic synergy of finerenone as part of multidrug regimens has become a central focus in optimizing nephroprotection. Evidence from the landmark CONFIDENCE trial demonstrates that the simultaneous initiation of finerenone and empagliflozin provides a superior reduction in the urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR) compared to either monotherapy alone, irrespective of baseline GLP‐1 RAs utilization [17, 18]. Despite these promising findings from randomized controlled trials (RCTs), large‐scale data characterizing the effectiveness and safety of finerenone alone or in combination with other therapies in routine clinical practice remain notably scarce. In line with this, RCTs may not provide adequate estimates of efficacy or safety that reflect real clinical settings [19], and therefore, insights into the real‐world use of finerenone are needed [20]. Indeed, subjects classified as low‐to‐moderate risk by KDIGO criteria remain a secondary focus in RCTs, representing a mere 10%–11% of the population analysed in FIDELITY or CONFIDENCE [18, 21]. This creates a critical evidence gap, as the majority of RCTs data come from high or very high‐risk individuals, while patients with lower risk are underrepresented. Consequently, further characterizing the response to finerenone in ‘lower‐risk’ real‐world settings would help inform treatment strategies before significant renal damage occurs.

While awaiting the definitive renal outcome data from the prospective, phase 4 FINE‐REAL study [22, 23], current real‐world evidence is primarily derived from large‐scale population‐based database analyses [24, 25] and small retrospective cohorts, with even the most substantial retrospective reports frequently involving cohorts of barely more than one hundred participants [26, 27] and only one small study focusing on combination therapy [28].

Hence, we designed the MEDFINE‐RWD study to evaluate the real‐world effectiveness and safety of finerenone in a large multicentre cohort of PWT2D in Spain. Additionally, we sought to identify factors associated with treatment response, addressing the need for evidence reflecting heterogeneous clinical practice beyond the populations typically represented in RCTs.

Methods

Study Population

We conducted a multicenter, retrospective, observational cohort study in routine clinical practice across 8 tertiary health centres of the Spanish National Health System between June 2024 and December 2025. Eligible participants were adults aged ≥ 18 years with type 2 diabetes and CKD who initiated finerenone during the study period and for whom baseline clinical data were available from electronic health records. Finerenone was available through public reimbursement within the Spanish National Health Service for patients meeting national funding criteria [12]—adult patients with CKD associated with T2DM, eGFR ≥ 25 mL/min/1.73 m2, UACR ≥ 30 mg/g, prior stable optimized doses of RAAS inhibitors (angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin II receptor blockers) and/or SGLT2 inhibitors, or intolerance to these agents—served as a broad external eligibility filter. Participants were consecutively recruited from the drug prescription registry of each health centre. Follow‐up extended from treatment initiation to the pre‐specified 6‐month visit (±45‐day analytical window), permanent treatment discontinuation, loss to follow‐up, or administrative censoring at database lock on 1 April 2026, whichever occurred first. All laboratory values reflect a single routine‐care determination per scheduled visit, measured at each centre's local accredited biochemistry laboratory.

This report corresponds to a pre‐specified interim analysis at 6 months of FU. Given the single‐arm, retrospective, pre–post nature of MEDFINE‐RWD, all subsequent references to ‘effectiveness’ refer to real‐world associations between finerenone initiation and the studied outcomes, not to causal estimates of drug efficacy. This distinction reflects the analytical constraints inherent to uncontrolled observational designs and aligns with STROBE guidance [20].

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Dr. Balmis General University Hospital, Alicante, Spain (2025‐035).

Outcomes: Effectiveness and Safety

The primary outcome was a clinically significant renal response, defined as a ≥ 30% reduction in the UACR from baseline to 6 months, a validated surrogate endpoint for CKD progression [29, 30]. UACR was quantified from first‐morning void samples utilizing turbidimetry or direct colorimetry.

Secondary endpoints included absolute and relative changes in UACR and eGFR, the latter calculated using the CKD‐EPI equation, as well as identification of clinical and demographic factors associated with response.

Safety outcomes included the cumulative incidence of hyperkalaemia (defined as a serum potassium concentration ≥ 5.5 mmol/L) [31] and ≥ 30% eGFR decline. Major Adverse Kidney Event (MAKE) and Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events (MACE) [32] are reported as descriptive safety data only. MAKE was defined as progression to end‐stage renal disease (dialysis, kidney transplantation or confirmed eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2), a sustained decrease in eGFR of at least 50% from baseline [33] or death from renal causes. Treatment modifications, including dose reduction or discontinuation, were also recorded. Potassium monitoring was performed according to routine clinical practice, with no protocol‐mandated monitoring schedule, dose‐titration algorithm, or discontinuation rule.

Treatment persistence and safety outcomes were additionally analysed according to baseline CKD severity. For the purposes of baseline risk stratification, CKD was evaluated using the single most recent eGFR and UACR values available within the 3 months preceding finerenone initiation in each patient's local hospital laboratory.

Four pre‐specified, nested analytical populations were defined a priori. The full prescribing‐pattern cohort—all consecutive adults who initiated finerenone with retrievable baseline data, regardless of subsequent follow‐up completion—served as the denominator for baseline demographic and clinical characterization and for cumulative safety events without time restriction. The 6‐month safety analysis set, restricted to patients with a documented clinical visit within the pre‐specified month‐6 analytical window, was used for time‐window‐bound safety outcomes, including sustained hyperkalaemia, major adverse cardiovascular events, the MAKE composite, and ≥ 30% eGFR decline. The active‐treatment UACR‐evaluable subset comprised patients from the safety set who remained on active finerenone at the visit and had paired baseline and 6‐month UACR measurements available; this subset constituted the population for the primary efficacy endpoint. A complementary eGFR‐evaluable subset, comprising patients with paired baseline and 6‐month eGFR values, was used for renal‐function secondary endpoints. Patients without a documented 6‐month visit at database lock—because their visit window had not yet opened—remained under active clinical follow‐up and were reserved for the planned complete‐cohort update [20].

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Between‐group comparisons were performed using Mann–Whitney U, χ 2, or Fisher's exact test as appropriate. Multivariable modelling. A multivariable logistic regression identified factors associated with the primary endpoint achievement (≥ 30% UACR reduction at 6 months). The full universe of available clinical variables—demographics, comorbidities, baseline biochemistry, background pharmacological therapy, and treatment‐related characteristics—was evaluated a priori for its bivariate association with the primary endpoint (see Supporting Information). Variables reaching p < 0.10 in bivariate analysis were eligible for the multivariable model. Age, sex, and finerenone dose were maintained as fixed covariates based on their clinical relevance and the established dose‐exposure‐response relationship that characterizes finerenone's effectiveness, particularly concerning UACR reduction [34]. Variables with more than 20% missing data among patients eligible for the primary endpoint analysis were not considered for multivariable modelling. No imputation procedures were applied due to sample size constraints and the potential for non‐random missingness.

Model stability was assessed using a pre‐specified minimum events‐per‐variable ratio of 10 to reduce the risk of overfitting. Multicollinearity was assessed a priori by variance inflation factor and its reciprocal, the tolerance statistic, with pre‐specified acceptability thresholds of tolerance > 0.10 and VIF < 5, with values close to 1 indicating the absence of meaningful collinearity. Continuous associated factors were dichotomised only when a clinically recognized, guideline‐anchored threshold existed. The optimal baseline UACR threshold for predicting the primary endpoint was determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The cut‐off point was defined according to the Youden Index, maximizing discriminatory performance. Model calibration was assessed by the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test and discrimination by the area under the ROC curve with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Cumulative incidence of hyperkalaemia was calculated using simple time‐to‐event analysis, accounting for differences in FU duration.

All statistical tests were two‐tailed, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 25.

Results

Cohort Flow and Data Completeness

Of the 844 patients who initiated finerenone with retrievable baseline data, 621 (73.6%) had a documented clinical visit at a median follow‐up of 184 days (IQR 147–218) and constituted the safety analysis set for the 6‐month outcomes reported here. Among these attendees, 65 (10.5%) had permanently suspended finerenone before the visit; the remaining 556 were still on active treatment at the time of assessment. Of these 556, 500 had both baseline and 6‐month UACR measurements obtained on treatment and contributed to the primary efficacy analysis, while paired eGFR values were available in 617 of the 621 attendees. The 223 patients without a documented 6‐month visit at database lock remained under active clinical follow‐up, with their visit window not yet reached. No deaths were recorded during the follow‐up window.

Endocrinologists and nephrologists were the leading prescribers, together accounting for 85.8% of all initiations, with the remaining 14.2% distributed across other specialties (cardiology, internal medicine, primary care, etc.).

Baseline Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

The cohort was predominantly male (76.8%), with a median age of 72 years, a median diabetes duration of 14 years, and a median HbA1c of 7.1%. Baseline cardiorenal risk was high, with a median eGFR of 49 mL/min/1.73 m2 and UACR of 285 mg/g. Regarding baseline CKD categories, 27.5% and 50.3% of the cohort were stratified into high and very high KDIGO risk, respectively. Most patients presented with A2‐stage albuminuria, while approximately half the population exhibited eGFR stages G2 or G3a. Cardiovascular Kidney Metabolism Treatment Patterns

Baseline pharmacological therapy included SGLT2 inhibitors (88.4%), antihypertensives (87.2%), and lipid‐lowering agents (82.6%). GLP‐1 RAs were used in 35.7% of patients, with 33.3% (281/844) receiving combined SGLT2 inhibitor and GLP‐1 RA therapy. Potassium binders were used in 10% of patients during FU (Table S1).

Finerenone was initiated at a dose of 10 mg in 82.3% of patients and at 20 mg in 17.7%, with limited up‐titration over time (14.7% at 1 month and 17.2% at 6 months).

Changes in UACR and eGFR

Finerenone therapy was associated with a median UACR reduction of 23.9% at 1 month and 34.5% at 6 months (p < 0.001 for both). Sensitivity analyses using log‐transformed values were performed, yielding consistent results. These antiproteinuric benefits occurred alongside modest, hemodynamically‐driven reductions in eGFR, which reached 3.4% [−1.5 (−6.9 to 1.8) mL/min/1.73 m2] and 5.6% [−2 (−7 to 1) ml/min/1.73 m2] at the 1‐month and 6‐month FU, respectively (p < 0.001 for both).

Renal Outcome and Associated Factors

Of the 500 UACR‐evaluable patients, 461 (92.2%) had complete covariates for multivariable modelling. The 383 excluded (344 without paired UACR + 39 with incomplete covariates) showed no baseline differences versus included across any characteristic (all p > 0.05; all standardized mean differences < 0.10), confirming administrative rather than disease‐driven missingness. At 6 months, 52.7% of patients achieved the primary endpoint (≥ 30% reduction in UACR). Responders had higher baseline UACR, lower prevalence of advanced CKD, and were less frequently treated with insulin. Response rates were consistent across background‐therapy strata: SGLT2 inhibitor users achieved 52.5% (235/442) vs. 50% (29/58) in non‐users (p = 0.72); GLP‐1 RA users 55.5% (111/200) vs. 50% (150/300) in non‐users (p = 0.22); and combined SGLT2 inhibitor/GLP‐1 RA therapy 56.5% (100/177) vs. 50% (153/306) without such combination (p = 0.17) (Table S2).

Adjusted multivariable analysis identified age ≥ 70 years [aOR 2.121 (95% CI 1.301–3.458); p = 0.003], baseline UACR > 245 mg/g [aOR 1.940 (1.219–3.089); p = 0.005], and a BMI > 27 kg/m2 [aOR 1.842 (1.106–3.069); p = 0.019] as independent associated factors of renal response. In contrast, insulin therapy [aOR 0.456 (0.289–0.717); p = 0.001] was associated with lower odds of achieving the primary endpoint (see Figure 2). Finally, higher finerenone dose [aOR 1.948 (95% CI 0.978–3.883); p = 0.059], although not statistically significant, showed a positive trend towards improved renal outcomes. Variance inflation factors and tolerance statistics were computed for all covariates retained in the final multivariable model. The tolerance values ranged from 0.719 to 0.984 and the VIF values ranged from 1.016 to 1.515, comfortably within the pre‐specified acceptability thresholds (tolerance > 0.10 and VIF < 5) and confirming the absence of meaningful collinearity. No candidate variable was excluded from the multivariable analysis because of missing data.

In stratified analyses, response rates were similar across KDIGO risk strata (50.5% in low‐to‐moderate vs. 52.4% in high‐to‐very high risk) and background therapy (56% in subjects with SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP‐1 RA combined therapy). Higher baseline UACR and older age remained consistent predictors, whereas baseline insulin therapy, diabetic retinopathy, and lower eGFR were associated with reduced response in specific subgroups (Table S3).

Safety Outcomes and Drug Persistence

Among the 621 patients included in the 6‐month safety analysis set, 612 (98.6%) had at least one valid on‐treatment serum potassium measurement available. The cumulative incidence of hyperkalaemia was 4.8% (29/612), distributed as 2.2% at 1 month and a further 2.6% emerging between 1 and 6 months, leading to dose down‐titration in 3 patients. No fatal hyperkalaemia event was recorded. A ≥ 30% decline in eGFR occurred in 6% of patients, while MAKE and MACE were infrequent (0.9% and 1.1%, respectively).

Treatment persistence was high, with discontinuation rates of 5.2% at 1 month and 10.5% at 6 months. The distribution of discontinuation causes was led by hyperkalaemia (64.3% at 1 month; 50.0% at 6 months), followed by loss to follow‐up (6 of 65 patients who discontinued, 9.2% within this subgroup; corresponding to 6 of 621 patients with a documented 6‐month visit window, 0.97% of the 6‐month safety analysis set), gastrointestinal intolerance (6.2%), and perceived lack of efficacy (4.6%).

Discussion

In this large, multicentre, real‐world cohort of PWT2D and CKD, we observed that finerenone was associated with an early and sustained reduction in albuminuria, with more than half of subjects achieving a clinically significant ≥ 30% reduction in UACR by 6 months. These renal benefits were observed independently of baseline SGLT2 inhibition or GLP‐1 RAs use. Advanced age, higher baseline albuminuria, and higher BMI emerged as independent predictors of renal response. Conversely, baseline insulin therapy was associated with significantly lower odds of achieving the primary endpoint. In this unselected population, treatment persistence was high, with the cumulative incidence of hyperkalaemia remaining below 5%.

The magnitude of UACR reduction observed in our cohort (34.5% at 6 months) aligns closely with, and slightly exceeds, results from pivotal Phase III trials and recent population large‐scale real‐world data [3, 10, 24]. In the FIDELIO‐DKD and FIGARO‐DKD trials, finerenone demonstrated a robust 30%–32% reduction in UACR at 4 months, with these early and sustained effects mediating a substantial portion of the observed cardiorenal benefits [3, 10]. Our results align with real‐world evidence from the FOUNTAIN platform, which underscored finerenone's clinical benefit across diverse clinical settings, yielding median UACR reductions of 33% at 4 months and 38% at 12 months [24, 25]. Notably, our cohort achieved these outcomes despite a significantly higher baseline utilization of SGLT2 inhibitors (88.4%) and GLP‐1 RAs (35.7%) compared to previous trial populations. Together with findings from the CONFIDENCE trial [17, 18], subgroup analyses from pivotal studies [21, 35], and smaller real‐world cohorts [28], our results suggest that finerenone provides additive antiproteinuric benefits beyond SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP‐1 RAs [14, 15, 16]. Collectively, these findings support the integration of finerenone into contemporary multimodal nephroprotective strategies for PWT2D [13, 33], in which complementary mechanisms target a CKD substrate defined by MR overactivation, FGF23 excess, and Klotho deficiency, which converge on inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrosis [36]. Also, finerenone may reverse diabetes‐related GLP‐1 receptor downregulation, supporting combination with GLP‐1 RAs [37].

The identification of independent response predictors offers key insights for clinical stratification. Mirroring the CONFIDENCE trial [38]—where finerenone's UACR‐lowering effect rises linearly with age—the stronger response observed in elderly patients in our cohort indicates meaningful benefit without added safety risk [31], despite their persistent under‐representation in RCTs. This likely reflects ‘inflammaging’: chronic low‐grade inflammation with progressive cardio‐renal fibrosis, driven by MR signalling through aldosterone‐dependent and aldosterone‐independent (Rac1, oxidative stress) pathways, both amplified by ageing. The resulting relative MR overactivation in older individuals plausibly explains their more pronounced albuminuria response to finerenone through attenuation of MR‐driven inflammation and age‐related fibrotic remodelling [39, 40, 41, 42].

The BMI > 27 kg/m2 finding aligns with MR overexpression in adipose tissue of individuals with overweight or obesity, where MR activation drives oxidative stress, low‐grade inflammation, adipokine dysregulation and pro‐fibrotic signalling [43], with pre‐clinical evidence that finerenone activates brown adipose tissue via the AMPK–ATGL–UCP1 pathway [44]; the prespecified FINE‐HEART analysis confirmed that absolute cardiorenal benefit of finerenone scales with higher baseline BMI [45]. The lower odds of response associated with insulin therapy likely reflect more advanced, long‐standing disease with established structural damage, which may limit the degree of rapid hemodynamic and anti‐inflammatory response; furthermore, hyperinsulinemia per se drives a self‐perpetuating GSK3β–IRS1 desensitization circuit in podocytes, producing microalbuminuria [46]. This finding should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating, since the prespecified FIDELIO‐DKD subgroup analysis by Rossing et al. showed that the relative UACR reduction is consistent across insulin users and non‐users (p‐interaction = 0.56) [47]; the apparent discrepancy with our binary ≥ 30% reduction endpoint more likely reflects the greater sensitivity of the binary threshold to advanced structural kidney disease, rather than a genuine biological difference attributable to insulin.

Along these lines, the higher probability of response in patients with higher baseline UACR (≥ 245 mg/g) reflects the drug's potent mechanism in targeting MR overactivation, which is more pronounced in patients with significant albuminuria [48]. However, the association between higher baseline UACR and greater response magnitude may partly reflect regression‐to‐the‐mean phenomena inherent to albuminuria variability. Finally, stratified analysis further identified diabetic retinopathy as a marker of diminished renal response. Often described as a ‘clinical biomarker and window into the kidney,’ diabetic retinopathy reflects systemic microvascular burden, showing strong clinical correlations with albuminuria and eGFR, as well as histological parallels with glomerular lesions [49].

Model performance characteristics were consistent with study expectations: calibration was adequate and multicollinearity was negligible. As expected for a moderately sized real‐world cohort, discrimination was modest (AUC‐ROC 0.689, 95% CI 0.634–0.744), inherent to the rank‐based construction of the C statistic, which is documented to provide a conservative estimate of model discrimination when incremental covariates are added to established models [50]. Although limited discrimination precludes individualized prediction, these findings may help characterize population‐level clinical profiles associated with greater likelihood of UACR response in routine practice.

Regarding the safety profile, the 4.8% cumulative incidence of hyperkalaemia in our cohort is highly encouraging and aligns closely with contemporary real‐world evidence. This rate is consistent with the interim findings of the FINE‐REAL study, where the incidence of serum potassium levels > 5.5 mmol/L was reported at 4.2% at 7 months, with only 0.4% of patients exceeding 6.0 mmol/L [23]. Similarly, the FOUNTAIN platform in Japan demonstrated a favourable safety profile in clinical practice, with hyperkalaemia incidence proportions ranging between 2.16% and 2.70% across different database cohorts [25].

These real‐world figures contrast favourably with the higher rates observed in pivotal Phase III clinical trials. In the FIDELIO‐DKD trial, 21.7% of the finerenone‐treated group reached potassium levels > 5.5 mmol/L [51]. Similarly, in the FIGARO‐DKD study—which enrolled a population with higher baseline eGFR—the incidence of hyperkalaemia was lower than in FIDELIO, yet remained at 11% [11]. The somewhat higher proportion of low‐to‐moderate risk patients in MEDFINE‐RWD (22.2%) and FINE‐REAL (22.6%) [23] versus FIDELITY (10.6%) [11] may partly account for the clinical differences observed between real‐world evidence and pivotal trials, although the absolute number of low‐risk patients remains modest and limits firm subgroup‐level inferences. Also, the low incidence of hyperkalaemia observed in our cohort may partly reflect the extensive background utilization of SGLT2 inhibitors (88.4%), which are known to mitigate the risk of MR antagonist‐induced hyperkalaemia [30]. Overall, these findings support the favourable safety profile of finerenone when integrated into contemporary standard‐of‐care therapy.

The eGFR decline (−2.0 mL/min/1.73 m2) mirrors pivotal finerenone trials [52]. However, without serial sampling in the first 4 weeks, we cannot mechanistically confirm any hemodynamic dip. Approximately 75% of this decline had occurred by month 1, followed by near‐plateau.

Dosing patterns in MEDFINE‐RWD show strong concordance with the international FINE‐REAL study, reinforcing finerenone's real‐world safety and tolerability profile [23]. At initiation, 82.3% of our cohort received 10 mg and 17.7% received 20 mg—almost mirroring FINE‐REAL's 80.8%/19.2%—and attainment of the 20 mg target dose reached 39.3% locally versus 40.2% globally, confirming regimen reliability while signalling clear room for improvement in dose titration [23]. Persistence was likewise robust; discontinuation was 11.2% at 6 months in MEDFINE‐RWD and 13.1% at a median follow‐up of 12 months in FINE‐REAL. Altogether, these data indicate that finerenone performance in routine practice is reproducible across healthcare settings, supporting its embedding in current standard‐of‐care frameworks.

MEDFINE‐RWD has limitations but equally relevant strengths. Constraints include the retrospective, observational design without a control group; the absence of adherence data; reliance on a single biochemical determination per visit rather than triplicate or research‐grade UACR. The relatively low incidence of hyperkalaemia observed in MEDFINE‐RWD should be interpreted in light of the retrospective, non‐interventional design, the absence of protocolised serum potassium monitoring, and variable inter‐visit intervals in routine care, which may have led to under‐ascertainment of mild or transient hyperkalaemia episodes. However, this limitation is partly mitigated by the high availability of on‐treatment potassium measurements (612/621; 98.6%) and by the pre‐specified definition of hyperkalaemia as any serum potassium value > 5.5 mmol/L during follow‐up. Other limitations are the inherent variability of UACR, a relatively short follow‐up—likely underestimating long‐term kidney events—and the fact that this report corresponds to a pre‐specified interim cut of MEDFINE‐RWD. Laboratory‐related constraints are partly mitigated by using a categorical ≥ 30% UACR reduction, anchored to each patient's paired baseline rather than continuous mean shifts; this binary endpoint is an established regulatory‐grade surrogate for CKD progression [29]—more robust to extreme individual values—and was applied to first‐morning void samples, the modality with the lowest within‐person variability [53]. Despite adjustment for measurable confounders, selection bias and confounding by indication remain inherent to non‐interventional data. In addition, national reimbursement criteria for finerenone during the study period may have further contributed to the selection of patients fulfilling approved treatment indications, potentially limiting generalizability to patients outside these criteria.

The strengths of MEDFINE‐RWD rest on the detailed characterization of a large cohort managed under contemporary standard of care, with ~90% on SGLT2 inhibitors and ~40% on GLP‐1 receptor agonists. Although FINE‐REAL [23] prospectively collects UACR, quantitative evidence on albuminuria reduction is still limited, and background cardiorenal therapy there is markedly lower (52.9% SGLT2 inhibitors; 28.7% GLP‐1 RAs)—underscoring the relevance of findings from a more intensively treated population. We adopted a ≥ 30% UACR reduction as the primary endpoint—a validated surrogate for long‐term kidney protection [29, 30, 54] filling a real knowledge gap in comparative real‐world data. Finally, our analysis of factors linked to renal response offers a granular view of drug performance in daily practice and supports rapid‐sequence up‐titration of guideline‐directed medical therapy for PWT2D and CKD [54].

The MEDFINE‐RWD study provides pragmatic evidence that finerenone is associated with a favourable benefit–risk profile when integrated into cutting‐edge multimodal therapeutic frameworks. Within this contemporary real‐world setting, finerenone therapy was associated with a substantial and consistent reduction in albuminuria, independent of background therapy or KDIGO risk stage. The low incidence of severe hyperkalaemia and high treatment persistence further reinforce its favourable safety profile in routine clinical practice, aligning with recent large‐scale observational findings. From a clinical perspective, these findings advocate for the early consideration of finerenone as a cornerstone of comprehensive nephroprotective strategies designed to bridge the ‘residual risk’ gap—particularly in people with persistent albuminuria despite optimized background care.
