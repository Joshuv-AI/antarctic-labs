Molecular Interactions of Pyridine With Water: A Combined DFT Benchmarking and QTAIM Analysis
Authors: Juda Baikété, Celestin Godwe Tcheombe, Alhadji Malloum, Jeanet Conradie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe microsolvation of pyridine (C5H5N) by water molecules was investigated systematically through a comprehensive exploration of the conformational landscape of the PYR(H2O) clusters (n=1−10) at the MN12SX‐D3/def2‐TZVP level of density functional theory, selected between 26 functionals, including dispersion corrections, by benchmarking to the DLPNO‐CCSD(T1)/CBS. A total of 326 stationary points were characterized, ranging from 3 conformers at n=1 to 55 at n=9. For small cluster sizes (n=1−2), the global minimum is dominated by a single O–H⋯N hydrogen bond between water and the pyridine nitrogen, in agreement with microwave spectroscopy data. From n=3 onwards, cooperative O–H⋯O inter‐water networks emerge and the energy landscape flattens dramatically, yielding dense manifolds of quasi‐degenerate conformers. For n≥7, the water subcluster progressively adopts closed‐cage topologies characteristic of large water clusters. Temperature‐dependent Boltzmann populations (0−500 K) reveal that the global minimum is the sole populated species only for n≤2, while a broad conformational distribution governs larger clusters at ambient temperature. The incremental clustering energies converge toward a bulk‐like plateau of approximately −10 kcal . mol beyond n=5, and the incremental Gibbs free energy remains positive throughout the series, demonstrating that stepwise gas‐phase microsolvation is entropically disfavored at every hydration step. Quantum Theory of Atoms in Molecules (QTAIM) analysis of the global minima identifies five types of non‐covalent interactions: O–H⋯N, O–H⋯O, C–H⋯O, O⋯C, and O–H⋯π contacts, whose diversity and number grow systematically with cluster size, reflecting the progressive transition from a simple donor–acceptor complex to a three‐dimensional hydrogen‐bond network that partially encapsulates the aromatic ring. These results provide a unified picture of the sequential microsolvation of pyridine and establish a benchmark dataset for the development of force fields and solvation models for nitrogen‐containing heterocycles.Keywords: Boltzmann populations, conformational landscape, hydrogen bonding, incremental clustering energy, microsolvation

Introduction

Non‐covalent interactions play a vital role in various fields of chemistry and molecular biology. Hydrogen bonding is one of the key factors in the structuring of molecular systems, whether it be stabilizing the secondary structures of proteins, organizing the network of liquid water, or recognizing molecules within biological systems. With regard to nitrogen‐containing heteroaromatic molecules, their interactions with water are of particular importance as they influence the solubility, acid–base reactivity and behavior of these compounds in aqueous solution during spectroscopic analysis. In this regard, pyridine, the prototype of six‐membered nitrogen heterocycles, thus represents an ideal model system for studying hydrogen bonds N⋯H‒O in aqueous solution.

Research into the interactions between pyridine and water began several decades ago. Since 1968, Adam et al. have studied the hydrogen‐bonding characteristics of pyridine, thereby laying the conceptual foundations for understanding the role of the heterocyclic nitrogen as a proton acceptor [1]. This pioneering research was extended theoretically by Martoprawiro and Bacskay, who conducted quantum chemical studies on pyrrole‐water and pyridine‐water complexes, providing reference structural and energetic information for the binary complex [2]. These studies established that the equilibrium geometry of the complex involves an O–H⋯N hydrogen bond, in which a water molecule acts as a proton donor to the pyridine nitrogen, with a stabilization energy characteristic of weak to moderate hydrogen bonds. Beyond the binary complex, the real aqueous environment is better described by water clusters of increasing size. Malaspina et al. studied the hydrogen bonds between pyridine and liquid water using a sequential Monte Carlo/quantum mechanical approach, highlighting the importance of first‐ and second‐order solvation effects on the structural and electronic properties of the solute [3]. This issue was explored in greater depth by Sicilia et al. in two complementary papers: the first provided a detailed theoretical analysis of pyridine protonation in water clusters of varying sizes [4], while the second explored the reaction mechanism of this protonation, showing that proton transfer only occurs once a critical number of water molecules is present, thereby highlighting the cooperative role of the hydrogen‐bond network [5].

With regard to spectroscopy, Raman and infrared studies were carried out on pyridine‐water complexes with the aim of characterizing the vibrational changes caused by hydrogen bonding. Schlücker et al. analyzed these complexes using DFT and Raman spectroscopy, highlighting the characteristic spectral shifts associated with the formation of the N⋯H‒O bond [6]. Kalampounias et al. studied the vibrational shift and frequency variations associated with these complexes, providing quantitative data on the dynamics of the hydrogen bond [7]. More recently, Sharma et al. have identified the origin of a blue shift in the vibrational Raman spectra of these complexes, a result that contrasts with the bathochromic shift generally observed in conventional hydrogen bonds and raises fundamental questions about the electronic nature of the N⋯H–O interaction in this system [8]. The dynamic aspects of the pyridine‐water complex were also investigated by Mackenzie et al., who used microwave spectroscopy to characterize large‐amplitude motions in this complex, revealing complex molecular dynamics involving proton tunneling effects and low‐energy conformational rearrangements [9]. Furthermore, Cai and Reimers calculated the first singlet states nπ* and ππ* of the hydrogen‐bonded water‐pyridine complex, showing that the hydrogen bond substantially alters the order and energy of the excited states compared to the isolated pyridine molecule [10]. More recently, Zborowski and Poater compared the effects of discrete and Continuum solvation models on the properties of pyridine and pyrrole in an aqueous environment, highlighting the limitations of dielectric continuum models in describing specific directional hydrogen‐bonding interactions [11]. This limitation of continuum models was demonstrated even earlier in the context of pyrimidine, a closely related nitrogen‐containing heterocycle, where discrete water molecule networks were shown to be essential for reproducing the experimentally observed spectroscopic perturbations induced by the aqueous solvent perturbations that continuum solvation models systematically failed to capture [12, 13]. In the context of substituted derivatives, Pápai and Jancsó conducted a theoretical analysis of hydrogen bonding in methyl‐substituted pyridine‐water complexes. They demonstrated that alkyl groups significantly influence the energy and geometry of hydrogen bonding through inductive and steric effects [14]. Sharif et al. carried out NMR and ab initio studies on complexes formed between water and various pyridine derivatives in solid and liquid phases, providing further experimental insight into the impact of substitution on nitrogen basicity and hydrogen bond strength [15]. The reactivity of pyridine‐water clusters in the presence of other species has also been explored by Ryding et al., who highlighted structural rearrangements and magic numbers in reactions between pyridine‐water clusters and ammonia [16].

The topological electron density analysis based on the Quantum Theory of Atoms‐in‐Molecules (QTAIM) provides a widely used framework for characterizing interatomic interactions through the properties of bond critical points (BCPs). While QTAIM has proven reliable for the description of covalent bonding in monomers and for well‐localized hydrogen bonds, it should be noted that it can occasionally provide misleading descriptions of non‐covalent interactions in certain systems, particularly for delocalized or weak contacts where the topology of the electron density does not unambiguously reflect the underlying physics [17]. According to Sangeetha et al., AIM theory, combined with analysis of the electrostatic surface potential and examination of frontier orbitals, has been used to characterize pyridine‐water complexes as well as water multimers [18]. In a similar context, Malloum and Conradie recently implemented a comparable approach for hydrated imidazole: after generating the initial configurations via classical molecular dynamics using the ABCluster algorithm, they selected the M06L‐D3 functional following a comparison with twenty other dispersion‐corrected DFT functionals relative to the DLPNO‐CCSD(T1)/CBS reference level. They subsequently optimized the structures of the IMZ(H2O) clusters (with n=1−12, 64) according to this theoretical level. The identification of four forms of non‐covalent interactions was made possible by QTAIM analysis carried out using the AIMALL software: two strong hydrogen bonds involving nitrogen atoms (OH⋯N3 and N1H⋯O), a weaker hydrogen bond CH⋯O, and an interaction OH⋯π [19] This multi‐method approach provides a methodological model that is particularly well suited to the study of nitrogen heterocycles in aqueous media.

Although this body of research has helped to build up a fairly detailed picture of the structural, energetic, spectroscopic and dynamic characteristics of the pyridine‐water complex, there are still several aspects that remain poorly documented. In particular, a systematic and in‐depth study of the various types of non‐covalent interactions in pyridine‐(H2O) aggregates of increasing size is still lacking in the scientific literature. Most of the available studies are limited to the binary complex or small systems, without exploring the evolution of electronic descriptors; density at the critical bonding point ρrBCP, the Laplacian ∇2ρrBCP, and the potential energy density Vr, as a function of cluster size, nor the competition between the various possible hydrogen‐bonding motifs: O–H⋯N, O–H⋯O and C–H⋯O. Furthermore, the selection of the most suitable DFT functional to characterize these clusters was not carried out systematically by comparison with a very precise reference level, unlike what was done for imidazole [19]. Finally, the correlation between QTAIM topological descriptors and observable spectroscopic properties, in particular Raman frequency shifts, has not been systematically established for pyridine in an aqueous medium.

This work aims to address these gaps by proposing a multi‐method theoretical study of non‐covalent interactions in pyridine‐water complexes, drawing inspiration from the methodological approach recently applied to imidazole‐water clusters [19]. Specifically, the objectives are as follows: (i) generate the initial configurations of the pyridine‐(H2O) (n=1−10) clusters via classical molecular dynamics using the ABCluster algorithm; (ii) perform a methodological benchmark of several dispersion‐corrected DFT functionals against the DLPNO‐CCSD(T1)/CBS level in order to identify the most accurate method for this specific system; (iii) optimize the cluster structures at the selected DFT level and perform a complete QTAIM analysis using the AIMALL software, enabling a rigorous topological characterization of all non‐covalent interactions; (iv) validate the theoretical predictions by comparison with experimental Raman spectroscopy data and interpret the observed spectral shifts in the light of the topological descriptors. It should be noted that QTAIM analysis has already been carried out in several of our studies on molecular aggregates [19, 20, 21]. The remainder of this article is structured as follows. The Section 2 outlines the experimental and computational methods employed, including the process for generating the initial configurations, the specification of the theoretical levels applied to the benchmark and optimizations, and the procedure for the QTAIM analysis; Section 3 presents the results obtained: the DFT benchmark results, the optimized cluster geometries, the topological descriptors at critical bonding points, the relative populations as a function of temperature, the solvation enthalpy, the free energy of pyridine in water, as well as the calculated and measured vibrational spectra. This section also examines these results in the light of previous research, focusing on the change in electronic characteristics as a function of cluster size and on the non‐standard interactions identified. Finally, the Section 4 presents the general conclusions and the prospects opened up by this work.

Methodology

The computational strategy employed in this work aims to rigorously characterize the structural and thermodynamic properties of pyridine‐water complexes PYR(H2O), n=1−10. It is structured around four complementary components described in turn below: the generation of the initial structures (Section 2.1), the protocol for calculating hydration properties (Section 2.2), the details of the quantum calculations and the procedure for selecting the DFT functional (Section 2.3), and finally the topological characterization of intermolecular interactions (Section 2.4).

Generation of Initial Configurations

Finding the minima on the potential energy surface of the PYR(H2O) aggregates is a crucial step in this research. Indeed, for a specific cluster of size n, several different geometric configurations exist, and omitting some of them could lead to biased estimates of the thermodynamic properties. To address this issue, we have implemented a global optimization strategy based on a classical potential. To be more precise, we used the ABCluster program [22, 23], which is based on an algorithm inspired by the foraging behavior of bee colonies. This algorithm is designed for the systematic exploration of the energy landscapes of molecular clusters. The CHARMM force field [24] implements the classical interaction potential, which simulates the electrostatic and Lennard‐Jones‐type contributions between atoms. The pre‐optimized geometries obtained form the starting point for subsequent DFT optimizations, carried out using the functional selected following the benchmarking process outlined in Section 2.3. This integrated method has proven its effectiveness in several previous studies carried out by our team on various molecular clusters [19, 21, 25, 26].

Computational Details

A correct description of the interactions in pyridine‐water complexes requires the inclusion of dispersion forces, whose contribution is significant in this type of system. This is why the benchmark procedure focuses exclusively on DFT functionals augmented with empirical dispersion corrections [28, 29]. It is important to note that for all cases here, the dispersion correction D3 was applied using the original zero damping scheme [29]. The candidate functionals are: APFD [30], B3LYP‐D3 [31, 32], B3PW91‐D3 [31, 33], B97‐D3 [28], BLYP‐D3 [32, 34], BP86‐D3 [34, 35], CAM‐B3LYP‐D3 [36], M052X‐D3 [37], M05‐D3 [38], M062X‐D3 [39], M06HF‐D3 [39], M06L‐D3 [39], M06‐D3 [39], MN15‐D3 [40], PBE0‐D3 [41], PBE‐D3 [35], PW6B95‐D3 [42], TPSS‐D3 [43], ωB97XD [43], BPBE‐D3 [35, 44], LC‐ωPBE‐D3 [44, 45], BMK‐D3 [44, 46], M08HX‐D3 [39], M11‐D3 [47], MN12SX‐D3 [48, 49] and N12SX‐D3 [48, 49], all associated with the def2‐TZVP dataset. For each functional, the minimum‐energy structure of the PYR(H2O) complex, as well as that of the monomers (pyridine and water), was optimized. The calculations were performed in the gas phase by using the Gaussian [50] 16 software package with stringent convergence criteria (applying the keyword Opt = Tight), and the BSSE correction is expected to be negligible on the bonding energies for a triple‐zeta basis set [20, 51] was not included. The neglect of BSSE is justified on the following grounds. It is well‐established that BSSE decreases rapidly with increasing basis set size and becomes negligible for triple‐zeta quality basis sets in hydrogen‐bonded systems [51, 52, 53]. Furthermore, the D3 dispersion correction used in this work is an additive atom pair correction which does not suffer from BSSE, thus reducing the overall error related to the incompleteness of the basis. For the benchmarking procedure, the geometry of the lowest‐energy PYR(HO) structure (pre‐screened by CHARMM/ABCluster) and of the isolated monomers (pyridine and water) was fully optimized using each candidate functional. The reference DLPNO‐CCSD(T1)/CBS binding energies were calculated using energy calculations performed on these geometries, which had been previously optimized at the MP2/def2‐TZVP level. This protocol ensures that the comparative evaluation is representative of both the quality of the geometry and the accuracy of the energy calculations for each functional. The baseline used to evaluate the features is DLPNO‐CCSD(T1)/CBS. The DLPNO‐CCSD(T1) technique [54] represents a significant improvement in terms of accuracy compared to the initial DLPNO‐CCSD(T0) version [55, 56]. The corresponding calculations were performed using ORCA (version 5.0.3) [57], employing the tightpno and tightscf options, as well as the auxiliary basis sets automatically generated via the AutoAux procedure [58]. Extrapolation to the complete basis set (CBS) is performed using a two‐step method, comprising single‐point energy calculations with the aug‐cc‐pVTZ and aug‐cc‐pVQZ sets [59]. The quality of each functional is then evaluated using three statistical metrics calculated relative to the DLPNO‐CCSD(T1)/CBS level: the mean absolute deviation (MAD), the maximum absolute deviation (MAX) and the standard deviation (STD). The functional with the lowest MAD is selected for all geometry optimizations, frequency calculations and calculations of the properties of the PYR(H2O) clusters, n=1−10. It is important to note that the def2‐TZVP basis set used here does not include diffuse functions. Diffuse functions are generally recommended for the accurate description of weak intermolecular interactions, but their omission is justified here for several reasons. The functional selection is then validated directly against DLPNO‐CCSD(T1)/CBS reference energies, which implicitly accounts for any basis set incompleteness error in the benchmarking procedure. Second, the empirical D3 dispersion correction partially compensates for the lack of diffuse functions in the description of long‐range non‐covalent interactions. Third, it is expected that the BSSE correction is small at the triple‐zeta level [51], showing the flexibility of the basis set to describe the intermolecular interactions in the PYR(H2O) clusters. Fourth, the systematic exploration of 326 stationary points for cluster sizes up to n = 10 would be computationally prohibitive with augmented basis sets. This approach is consistent with the methodology employed in related studies of nitrogen heterocycle‐water clusters [19].

QTAIM Analysis

The characterization of intermolecular interactions within clusters PYR(H2O) is determined by the analysis of QTAIM [61]. This topological approach uses the spatial distribution of the electronic density ρr to locate critical points, which are defined as the points where the gradient of the density vanishes:

Among the various types of critical points: Nuclear Critical Points (NCP, 3−3), Bond Critical Points (BCP, 3−1), Ring Critical Points (RCP, 3+1) and Cage Critical Points (CCP, 3+3), it is the BCP that are of particular interest to us. Located on the bond path between two interacting atoms, they provide valuable local information on the nature and strength of the interatomic coupling. The sign of the Laplacian ∇2ρrBCP=λ1+λ2+λ3 allows us to distinguish covalent interactions (∇2ρ<0) from non‐covalent interactions (∇2ρ>0), which include hydrogen bonds and van der Waals interactions [61, 62, 63]. The value of ρrBCP is a direct indicator of the strength of the interaction: the higher it is, the stronger the coupling.

For a more comprehensive characterization, three additional descriptors are extracted from the BCPs. The total energy density Hr=Gr+Vr, where G and V denote the kinetic and potential energy densities respectively, allows the degree of covalence of the interaction to be specified: a ratio ∣V∣/G>1 indicates a partially covalent nature, while ∣V∣/G<1 is characteristic of purely non‐covalent interactions. Ellipticity, defined by Equation (8), provides information on the symmetry of the electron distribution around the bond path: a value close to zero indicates a bond with cylindrical symmetry (σ), while a high value may indicate a π character. Finally, the delocalization index δA,B quantifies the number of electrons shared between the atomic pools A and B, regardless of the topology of the bond path.

The QTAIM calculations are performed using the AIMALL program [64], based on the electronic densities calculated at the DFT level selected following benchmarking. The analysis focuses solely on the most stable structures for each cluster size, considered representative of the overall population. The types of non‐covalent interactions identified between pyridine and water molecules, the bonding paths and BCPs (shown graphically: dotted lines for non‐covalent interactions, solid lines for covalent bonds, green spheres for BCPs), and the ranges of values for ρrBCP and ∇2ρrBCP associated with each type of interaction are reported in Section 3.

Results and Discussion

In this study, a rigorous selection of DFT functionals was first performed by systematically comparing 26 different functionals, based on reference binding energies calculated at the DLPNO‐CCSD(T1)/CBS level (see Section 3.1). The most accurate functional was then used to optimize the structures of PYRH2On clusters (results presented in Section 3.2). The non‐covalent interactions within these aggregates were characterized by QTAIM analysis, allowing a detailed description of the intermolecular bonds (Section 3.3). The relative population of the clusters is presented and discussed in Section 3.4. Besides, clustering incremental energies (electronic energies, free energies, and enthalpies) are also reported in Section 3.5. Finally, the hydration free energy and enthalpy are presented in Section 3.6. This systematic methodological approach ensures both the accuracy of the results and their physical relevance for the study of the properties of pyridine‐water clusters.

Benchmark of DFT Functionals

Before undertaking a systematic exploration of the PYR(H2O) clusters, we carried out a comparative evaluation of 26 DFT functionals including dispersion corrections. To do this, the initial configurations of the PYR(H2O) clusters were first generated by ABCluster, and the lowest‐energy structure obtained for each size n was selected as the reference geometry for the benchmark. These structures were fully optimized, firstly, at the MP2/def2‐TZVP level for the DLPNO‐CCSD(T1)/CBS energies, and these binding energies served as a reference for evaluating each functional. Secondly, these structures were then fully optimized for each of the candidate functionals associated with the def2‐TZVP basis. Visual inspection of all converged geometries after optimization showed that not all functionals retained the original hydrogen‐bond topology of the CHARMM pre‐screened reference structure. For some functionals, the optimized geometry was qualitatively the same as the starting structure, with the same O–H⋯N donor‐acceptor connectivity and inter‐water hydrogen‐bond network. For others, however, the optimization resulted in a qualitatively different minimum, characterized by a rearrangement of the water subcluster topology or a modification of the hydrogen‐bond connectivity. These qualitative inconsistencies are thus responsible for the large MAD, MAX values found for the worst performing functionals in the statistical analysis. The binding energy of each method was calculated through Equation (6) and the respective optimized geometries of the complex and the monomers. To assess whether the statistical analysis is dominated by the larger clusters, whose absolute binding energies are significantly larger than those of small clusters, the benchmark was also performed using binding energies normalized per water molecule (ΔEn/n). The resulting MAD, MAX, and STD values, as well as the ranking of the 26 functionals, remain qualitatively unchanged upon normalization. In particular, MN12SX‐D3 retains the lowest MAD, and the three worst‐performing functionals (B97‐D3, BMK‐D3, PW6B95‐D3) remain clear outliers. This confirms that the benchmark statistics are not unduly dominated by the larger cluster sizes, and that the selection of MN12SX‐D3 as the optimal functional is robust with respect to the choice of normalization. The statistical descriptors MAD, MAX and STD are shown in Figure 2 and in Table S1. Note that the B97‐D3, BMK‐D3, and PW6B95‐D3 exhibit abnormally high deviations (MAD > 17 kcal/mol, with MAX values reaching 110.0, 707.7 and 602.3 kcal/mol, respectively) and are not shown in Figure 2 for reasons of clarity.

Statistical descriptors (MAD, MAX and STD in kcal⋅mol) for the 26 dispersion‐corrected DFT functionals tested against the DLPNO‐CCSD(T1)/CBS reference binding energies of PYR(H2O) clusters (n=1−10). For the sake of clarity, functionals with anomalously large deviations (B97‐D3, BMK‐D3 and PW6B95‐D3, MAD>17 kcal⋅mol) have been omitted. The MN12SX‐D3 functional, which exhibits the lowest MAD (4.06 kcal⋅mol), MAX (14.54 kcal⋅mol) and competitive STD (11.30 kcal⋅mol), is chosen for all subsequent geometry optimizations and property calculations.

At the other end of the scale, the MN12SX‐D3 functional stands out for having the lowest MAD of all the functionals analyzed, with a value of 4.06 kcal/mol. It is closely followed by M06L‐D3 (MAD = 5.51 kcal/mol) and M08HX‐D3 (MAD = 5.61 kcal/mol). Thus, these three functionals are considered the most promising for modeling pyridine–water interactions. An analysis of the other statistical indicators also supports this ranking: in terms of MAX, MN12SX‐D3 also has the lowest value (14.54 kcal/mol), ahead of M08HX‐D3 (15.28 kcal/mol) and N12SX‐D3 (16.03 kcal/mol). As for the standard deviation, N12SX‐D3 has the lowest value (9.33 kcal/mol), followed by M08HX‐D3 (10.61 kcal/mol) and M11‐D3 (11.05 kcal/mol).

Among the functionals in the Minnesota family, M06L‐D3 proves to be the most accurate in the M06 subfamily, with an MAD of 5.51 kcal/mol, ahead of M062X‐D3 (MAD = 7.98 kcal/mol), M06‐D3 (MAD = 8.65 kcal/mol) and M06HF‐D3 (MAD = 9.68 kcal/mol). Conventional hybrid functionals such as B3LYP‐D3 (MAD = 12.42 kcal/mol), CAM‐B3LYP‐D3 (MAD = 13.86 kcal/mol), and BLYP‐D3 (MAD = 14.54 kcal/mol) exhibit significantly higher deviations, making them unsuitable for the quantitative description of interactions within pyridine‐water clusters. The B3PW91‐D3 and LC‐ωPBE‐D3 functionals lie in an intermediate range, with respective MADs of 8.99 and 9.26 kcal/mol. The ωB97XD functional, frequently recommended for systems with non‐covalent interactions [13], exhibits an MAD of 8.73 kcal/mol here, placing it behind the best Minnesota functionals. Finally, PBE0‐D3 exhibits the highest MAD among the functionals shown in the graph (19.26 kcal/mol, MAX = 38.57 kcal/mol), making it the least suitable functional for the system under study. In view of these results as a whole, the MN12SX‐D3 functional exhibits the best overall statistical indicators (MAD = 4.06 kcal/mol, MAX = 14.54 kcal/mol, STD = 11.30 kcal/mol) and is therefore selected for all geometry optimizations, frequency calculations and property calculations for the PYR(H2O) clusters, n=1−10.

Structures of PyH2On, n=1−10

Pyridine is a nitrogen‐containing aromatic base whose ability to form hydrogen bonds (HBs) with water has been characterized both experimentally and theoretically [65, 66, 67, 68]. In particular, the pyridine–water complex has been studied by microwave spectroscopy, confirming that the preferred binding motif involves an O–H⋯N hydrogen bond from water to the pyridine nitrogen [9, 65]. The present work provides a systematic exploration of the conformational landscape of PYR(H2O) clusters for n=1−10, extending previous theoretical studies limited to small cluster sizes [66, 67]. The structures identified by size from n=1 to 10 are 3, 5, 14, 28, 41, 45, 53, 44, 55, and 38. Thus in total 326 structures were located at the MN12SX‐D3/def2‐TZVP level. This manuscript exposes the structures of sizes 1 and 2 with three and five variations, respectively, while in order to prevent an exhaustive list of structures, only the three most stable structures for the remaining sizes are included in Figure 3 and Figure 4. All relative energies ΔE are given with respect to the lowest‐energy conformer of each series.

Three conformers were identified for n=1. The global minimum, PYRW1_1 (ΔE=0.0kcal⋅mol−1), features a direct O–H⋯N hydrogen bond between water and the pyridine nitrogen. This structure is consistent with the rotational spectrum of the pyridine–water 1:1 complex reported by Melandri et al. [65] and later reinvestigated by Mackenzie et al. [9] with chirped‐pulse Fourier‐transform microwave spectroscopy. Both experimental studies confirm that water acts as a proton donor to the nitrogen lone pair, with the complex exhibiting large‐amplitude internal motion of the water subunit. The computed N⋯H distance (typically 1.85 Å to 1.95 Å) agrees with the structural parameters inferred from rotational constants. Theoretical calculations by Sadlej et al. [66] at the MP2 and CCSD(T) levels reproduced this geometry and estimated an interaction energy of approximately −5 kcal · mol. Conformer PYRW1_2 (ΔE=2.12kcal⋅mol−1) places the water molecule close to the aromatic π system, suggesting an O–H⋯π interaction, a motif energetically unfavorable compared to the N‐site binding. Conformer PYRW1_3 (ΔE=3.17kcal⋅mol−1) shows no direct interaction with the nitrogen or the ring and represents a high‐energy local minimum. The global minimum, PYRW2_1 (ΔE=0.0kcal⋅mol−1), shows a cooperative hydrogen‐bond chain: the first water molecule donates a proton to the pyridine nitrogen (O–H⋯N), while the second water molecule donates a proton to the first (O–H⋯O), forming a bifurcated relay typical of nitrogen base–water clusters [67, 68]. This cooperative enhancement of the HB network, which is well‐documented for (H2O) clusters [69, 70]. For n=3, fourteen conformers were identified (ΔE=0.0kcal⋅mol−1 to 5.35 kcal⋅mol−1; see Figure S1). A tight network of three water molecules is attached to the pyridine nitrogen in the reference structure, PYRW3_1. The quasi‐degenerate set of conformers PYRW3_2 to PYRW3_7 (ΔE=0.24kcal⋅mol−1 to 0.93 kcal⋅mol−1) preserves the inter‐water HB network but is orientated differently with respect to the ring plane. As previously discussed for pure (H2O) clusters [69, 70] and complexes of nitrogenous bases with (H2O) [67], this accumulation of low‐lying structures reflects the topological flexibility of water trimers. The HB network is partially disconnected in conformers PYRW3_8 to PYRW3_12 (ΔE=1.22kcal⋅mol−1 to 2.18 kcal⋅mol−1), and highly destabilized geometries are represented by PYRW3_13 to PYRW3_14 (ΔE=3.51 and 5.35 kcal⋅mol−1). For n=4, twenty‐eight structures were characterized (ΔE=0.0kcal⋅mol−1 to 7.88 kcal⋅mol−1, Figure S2). A three‐dimensional HB network connecting four water molecules to the pyridine nitrogen is shown in the global minimum, PYRW4_1. In line with the S4‐symmetric ring discovered for pure (H2O) clusters at the MP2 level [69, 71], the water cluster tends to take on a cyclic ring topology at this size. While the water‐ring orientation varies, conformers PYRW4_2 to PYRW4_10 (ΔE=0.37kcal⋅mol−1 to 1.97 kcal⋅mol−1) retain comparable topologies. Deeper rearrangement of the water cluster is visible starting at PYRW4_11 (ΔE=2.08kcal⋅mol−1), with some molecules forming lateral bridges instead of cyclic networks. The most unstable conformers are PYRW4_27 to PYRW4_28 (ΔE=6.41 and 7.88 kcal⋅mol−1), which have an isolated water molecule detached from the main cluster. For n=5, 41 conformers were discovered (ΔE=0.0kcal⋅mol−1 to 9.78 kcal⋅mol−1, Figure S3). Similar to the pentagonal ring structures seen for pure (H2O) clusters [71], the global minimum, PYRW5_1, exhibits an extensive, compact network in which the five water molecules form a pseudo‐cage or cyclic arrangement around the pyridine. The almost degenerate conformers PYRW5_2 to PYRW5_9 (ΔE=0.21kcal⋅mol−1 to 1.22 kcal⋅mol−1 demonstrate the energy landscape frustration typical of intermediate‐size water clusters [72]. Conformers PYRW5_10 to PYRW5_27 (ΔE=1.30kcal⋅mol−1 to 3.26 kcal⋅mol−1 investigate pseudo‐prismatic, ring, and branching chain structures. The highest‐energy structures (PYRW5_36 to PYRW5_41, ΔE=6.34kcal⋅mol−1 to 9.78 kcal⋅mol−1) are associated with near‐free or fragmented water geometries.

Forty‐five conformers were characterized for n=6 (ΔE=0.0kcal⋅mol−1 to 153.81 kcal⋅mol−1, Figure S4). The global minimum, PYRW6_1, displays a dense three‐dimensional HB network. Conformers PYRW6_2 to PYRW6_3 (ΔE=0.16 and 0.33 kcal⋅mol−1) are nearly degenerate with the reference structure, indicating a very flat energy landscape at the bottom of the well. At n=6, water clusters begin to favor closed‐cage topologies, as shown by the competition between prism, book, and cage isomers identified for pure (H2O) by [71] and confirmed by the infrared spectroscopy measurements of Gruenloh et al. [73] in molecular beam experiments using benzene as a chromophore. The extreme outlier PYRW6_45 (ΔE=153.81kcal⋅mol−1) is likely a constrained geometry or an intermediate local minim structure trapped during the conformational search. For n=7, 53 conformers were discovered (ΔE=0.0kcal⋅mol−1 to 14.55 kcal⋅mol−1, Figure S5). The nearly perfect degeneracy between two different heptameric topologies is indicated by conformers PYRW7_1 and PYRW7_2 (ΔE=0.04kcal⋅mol−1). In accordance with the flat and rugged potential energy surface anticipated for water clusters at this scale [71, 72], conformers PYRW7_3 to PYRW7_10 (ΔE=0.12kcal⋅mol−1 to 2.83 kcal⋅mol−1) form a dense low‐energy group. The pentagonal and hexagonal water rings seen in structures PYRW7_11 to PYRW7_19 (ΔE=2.86kcal⋅mol−1 to 3.56 kcal⋅mol−1) are well‐documented for (H2O) clusters of this size [71]. Highly fragmented HB networks are represented by the highest‐energy structures (PYRW7_47 to PYRW7_53, ΔE=8.81kcal⋅mol−1 to 14.55 kcal⋅mol−1). For n=8, forty‐four conformers were identified (ΔE=0.0kcal⋅mol−1 to 18.78 kcal⋅mol−1, Figure S6). The global minimum, PYRW8_1, displays an elaborate three‐dimensional octameric network. For pure (H2O), the D2d‐symmetric cubic structure (two stacked water squares) is the established global minimum, as confirmed by ab initio calculations [71] and infrared spectroscopy in molecular beams [73]. The cage‐like topology observed for PYRW8_1 is consistent with this tendency of octameric water clusters to adopt closed, three‐dimensional architectures. Conformers PYRW8_2 to PYRW8_9 (ΔE=1.16kcal⋅mol−1 to 2.10 kcal⋅mol−1) lie within a narrow energy window, reflecting the multiplicity of quasi‐equivalent topologies accessible at this cluster size. The highest‐energy structures, PYRW8_43 to PYRW8_44 (ΔE=12.57 and 18.78 kcal⋅mol−1), are strongly destabilized outliers associated with highly constrained arrangements. For n=9, 55 conformers were described (ΔE=0.0kcal⋅mol−1 to 23.16 kcal⋅mol−1 Figure S7). PYRW9_1, the global minimum, has a compact nonameric network. A dense collection of closely similar topologies is formed by conformers PYRW9_2 to PYRW9_10 (ΔE=1.31kcal⋅mol−1 to 3.06 kcal⋅mol−1). In accordance with the topological competition shown for pure (H2O) clusters [71, 72], structures PYRW9_11 to PYRW9_19 (ΔE=3.12kcal⋅mol−1 to 4.07 kcal⋅mol−1) exhibit alternating ring and cage connectivity. The extreme outlier PYRW9_55 (ΔE=23.16kcal⋅mol−1) is probably an artifact of the conformational search algorithm and mirrors the anomalous PYRW6_45 structure. Finally, the largest system under study (n=10), produced 38 conformers (ΔE=0.0kcal⋅mol−1 to 14.21 kcal⋅mol−1, Figure S8). A decameric cage that partially encloses the pyridine ring is visible in the global minimum, PYRW10_1. The fused pentameric (pentagonal prism) structures are the known global minimum for pure (H2O) [71]. This topology is similar to the components of clathrate hydrate cages [74]. The first notable rival is Conformer PYRW10_2 (ΔE=1.53kcal⋅mol−1), which shows improved global minimum stabilization through cooperative HB reinforcement. This trend is consistent with ab initio calculations on (H2O) (n=8–20) by Mahesh et al. [71]. Conformers PYRW10_29 to PYRW10_38 (ΔE=8.86kcal⋅mol−1 to 14.21 kcal⋅mol−1) exhibit the HB network gradually breaking up into several sub‐clusters. Although several low‐energy PYR(H2O) structures exhibit water subcluster geometries similar to those reported for isolated (H2O) clusters, the pyridine molecule significantly influences the relative stability and organization of the hydrogen‐bond network. In particular, the O–H⋯N interaction acts as a strong anchoring site that orients the water framework around the pyridine nitrogen atom. While cage, prism, and ring motifs known for pure water clusters are largely preserved for larger cluster sizes, the presence of pyridine modifies the energetic competition between these motifs through cooperative hydrogen‐bond reinforcement and additional weak interactions such as C–H⋯O and O⋯C contacts. Consequently, the energetic ordering of PYR(H2O) conformers is not strictly identical to that of the corresponding isolated water clusters, even when similar water topologies are observed. This behavior is consistent with previous studies showing that low‐energy water cluster motifs are largely governed by cooperative hydrogen‐bond networks, while the presence of a solute can modify the relative energetic ordering of the corresponding isomers through additional intermolecular interactions [69, 75, 76].

Non‐Covalent Interactions in PYR(H2O)

The nature and strength of non‐covalent interactions in the PYR(H2O) clusters were characterized using QTAIM analysis [61]. According to the Koch–Popelier criteria [77], a genuine hydrogen bond (HB) at the BCP is characterized by ρBCP∈0.002, 0.035 e⋅a. and ∇2ρBCP∈0.024, 0.139 e⋅a. Interactions with ρBCP<0.002 e⋅a. are classified as Van der Waals (vdW) contacts. The molecular graphs obtained from the QTAIM analysis of the global minimum of each PYRH2On cluster are shown in Figure 5. To illustrate the topology of the electron density in detail, Figure 6 shows the contour map of ρr for the global minimum PYRW3_1, with bond paths and BCPs (green spheres). The high electron density around N10, O4, and O7 and the BCPs along the intermolecular bond paths confirm the O–H⋯N and O–H⋯O hydrogen bonds, as well as the weaker C–H⋯O contacts between the aromatic ring and the water cluster.

The QTAIM analysis identifies six types of non‐covalent interactions in the PYR(H2O) series: O–H⋯N (hydrogen bond from water to the pyridine nitrogen), O–H⋯O (inter‐water HB), C–H⋯O (weak CH hydrogen bond), O⋯C (oxygen–carbon contact), and 0–H⋯π (weak CH⋯π‐like contact). The ranges of ρBCP and ∇2ρBCP for each interaction type, extracted from the BCP data for n=1−10, are summarized in Tables 1, 2, 3. For n=1 (PYRW1_1), the QTAIM molecular graph (Figure 5a) shows a single BCP along the O–H⋯N bond path connecting the water hydrogen to the pyridine nitrogen. The value ρBCP=0.023 e⋅a with ∇2ρBCP=0.078 e⋅a is fully consistent with the Koch–Popelier criteria for a genuine hydrogen bond [77], and is in excellent agreement with the BCP parameters computed at the MP2 level for the same complex by Sadlej et al. [66] and Martoprawiro et al. [2]. The positive Laplacian confirms the closed‐shell electrostatic nature of this interaction. As n increases from 2 and 3, the electron density at the N⋯H BCP (ρmax) increases progressively (Table 1 and Table 2), reflecting the cooperative reinforcement of the O–H⋯N hydrogen bond by the growing water cluster [70]. This cooperativity has been documented experimentally through the red‐shift of the O–H stretching frequency in IR spectra of PYR(H2O) clusters [9, 65]. For n≥4, the O–H⋯N BCP parameters remain within the range ρ≈0.021–0.038 e⋅a, consistent with a strong conventional hydrogen bond.

Starting from n=2 (PYRW2_1), O–H⋯O BCPs appear between the water molecules. Their ρBCP values (0.011–0.027 e⋅a) and positive Laplacians are characteristic of moderate to strong hydrogen bonds, in agreement with the extensive QTAIM studies of pure (H2O) clusters by Parthasarathi et al. [62]. The maximum ρ values increase with n, reflecting the cooperative strengthening of the inter‐water network as the cluster grows, a trend well established for cyclic water clusters [69, 70]. From n=3 (PYRW3_1) onwards, BCPs characteristic of C–H⋯O interactions appear between the aromatic C–H bonds of pyridine and the oxygen atoms of nearby water molecules. Their ρBCP values (0.009–0.030 e⋅a) and positive Laplacians satisfy the Koch–Popelier criteria for weak hydrogen bonds [77]. These interactions are consistent with those reported for analogous aromatic–water complexes [73] and play a secondary but non‐negligible role in anchoring the water network around the pyridine ring. Notably, for n=9 (PYRW9_1) and n=10 (PYRW10_1), the C–H⋯O range extends to unusually high ρmax values (∼0.048 e⋅a), which are attributable to genuine covalent C–H bonds rather than non‐covalent contacts; these correspond to BCPs identified within the pyridine ring itself rather than intermolecular interactions.

BCPs of O⋯C type appear from n=4 (PYRW4_1) and are present in all larger clusters. Their ρBCP spans a wide range (0.006 e⋅a), with positive ∇2ρ, indicating predominantly closed‐shell electrostatic character. The high upper limit of ρ again reflects intramolecular C–O bonds within the pyridine ring being captured by the QTAIM topology. The intermolecular O⋯C contacts (ρ∼0.005 e⋅a) can be classified as weak van der Waals or lone‐pair⋯π interactions between water oxygen lone pairs and the pyridine ring carbons. For n≥6, no additional non‐covalent interaction types beyond those already identified emerge. Instead, the increasing compactness of cage‐like topologies is reflected in a greater density and diversity of these existing contacts, particularly the O…C and C–H…O interactions as the water subcluster progressively wraps around the pyridine ring. This enrichment of established contact types, rather than the appearance of new bonding motifs, is consistent with the steric proximity effects documented in similar aromatic–water cluster systems [73] and contribute a small but measurable stabilization to the overall cluster energy [19]. O–H⋯π bond critical points are identified in the global minima of PYRW4_1, PYRW5_1, PYRW6_1, PYRW9_1, and PYRW10_1, with ρBCP values ranging from 0.006 to 0.007 e⋅a and positive Laplacians in the range 0.021 to 0.023 e⋅a. These values place O–H⋯π contacts firmly in the van der Waals regime, consistent with their weak, dispersive character. Their absence for n=1−3 and n=7−8 reflects the structural flexibility of the water subcluster at these sizes, where the preferred binding motifs do not place water hydrogen atoms in proximity to the aromatic π system. The appearance of O–H⋯π contacts from n=4 onwards coincides with the emergence of more compact, three‐dimensional hydrogen‐bond networks in which the water subcluster partially encapsulates the pyridine ring, bringing water hydrogen atoms into van der Waals contact with the π electron density. It should be noted that QTAIM may not capture all O–H⋯π interactions, particularly in cases where the electron density above the aromatic ring is too diffuse to generate a well‐defined bond path [60]. Complementary approaches would therefore be valuable for a more complete characterization of these contacts.

The number and diversity of non‐covalent interaction types increase systematically with n (see Figure 5): from a single O–H⋯N BCP at n=1 (PYRW1_1) to a rich network including O–H⋯O, C–H⋯O, O⋯C and O–H⋯π. This evolution mirrors the structural transition from a simple donor–acceptor complex at small n to a three‐dimensional cage network at large n, where the water subcluster forms an autonomous, internally hydrogen‐bonded framework that only secondarily docks onto the pyridine surface through weaker C–H⋯O and van der Waals contacts. A similar progression has been observed for the micro‐hydration of other nitrogen heterocycles [4, 19] and is consistent with the general picture of microsolvation of aromatic molecules in molecular beams [73].

For n=1 (Figure 7a), PYRW1_1 dominates the population over the entire temperature range 0–500 K, reflecting the large energetic gap (ΔE>2kcal⋅mol−1) between the global minimum and the higher conformers. This is consistent with the microwave spectroscopy measurements of Melandri et al. [65] and Mackenzie et al. Conformers PYRW1_2 and PYRW1_3 acquire a non‐negligible population only above ≃250 K, illustrating the thermal accessibility of the less stable isomers at elevated temperatures. For n=2 (Figure 7b), PYRW2_1 is essentially the sole populated species at low temperature. The high energetic cost of the competing conformers (ΔE≥3kcal⋅mol−1) means that only PYRW2_3 and PYRW2_5 gain visible population above ≃300 K. From n=3 forward, the image significantly alters (Figure 7c–j). From around 60 K, the quasi‐degenerate low‐energy conformers (PYRW3_1 to PYRW3_5) are almost equally populated for n=3. After that, the conformers redistribute and higher‐energy isomers like PYRW3_8 and PYRW3_14 become thermally accessible. This behavior, which has been reported for similar nitrogen base‐water systems [5], is typical of conformationally flexible molecular clusters and includes a dominant global minimum at T→0 followed by a progressive population of competing structures as temperature rises. No one conformer maintains a dominating population beyond ≃400 K for n=4 (see Figure 7d). However, the PYRW5_1 structure dominates over the 0–200 K and PYRW5_12 over the 200–500 K range for n=5 (see Figure 7e). Many competing isomers degrade the populations of the global minima PYRW4_1 and PYRW5_1, and at high temperatures the distribution becomes broad and nearly flat. This rapid loss of specificity with increasing cluster size reflects the combinatorial explosion of accessible conformations, consistent with the flat potential energy landscapes discussed in Section 3.2. The dominating species is usually the global minimum for bigger clusters (n=6–10, Figure 7f–j), but as the temperature rises, it quickly loses population to a variety of competing structures, except for n=8, where the low‐energy structure dominates across the entire range. Interestingly, for n=7, the two nearly isoenergetic conformers PYRW7_1 and PYRW7_2 form a thermally persistent pair with roughly equal populations at all temperatures. Up to ≃300 K, PYRW10_1 maintains the largest single‐conformer population for n=10. After that, PYRW10_4 and PYRW10_5 gain substantial weight, indicating increased structural stiffness of the decameric cage network in comparison to intermediate cluster sizes.

Incremental Clustering Energies

The incremental (or sequential) clustering energy ΔEclustn (Equation 10) quantifies the energetic gain associated with the addition of one water molecule to the cluster of size n−1, computed for the respective global minima at each cluster size. The resulting values are plotted as a function of n in Figure 8.

Incremental clustering electronic energy (ΔEn) and enthalpy (ΔHn), calculated at 298.15 K using Equation (10) for the Boltzmann‐averaged global minimum structures of PYR(H2O) clusters (n=1−10). Energies are reported in kcal⋅mol.

Each subsequent hydration step's thermodynamic stability is confirmed by the clustering energies' negative values. In good agreement with the high‐level ab initio estimates of Sadlej et al. [66] (≃−5.0 kcal⋅mol−1 at the CCSD(T) level) and the DFT calculations of Dkhissi et al. [78], the magnitude ∣ΔEclust∣ is largest for n=1 (≃−5 kcal⋅mol−1), corresponding to the direct formation of the N⋯H–O hydrogen bond. Since each extra water molecule attaches to an already partially saturated HB network rather than directly to the exposed nitrogen lone pair, the incremental energy falls monotonically in size from n=2 to n≈4−5 beyond n=1. This trend is qualitatively consistent with the sequential solvation energies reported for analogous nitrogen heterocycle–water clusters [79] and with the general decrease of incremental HB energies observed for pure (H2O) clusters [70, 71]. The incremental clustering energy levels off and approaches a plateau of roughly −12 kcal⋅mol−1 to −12.8 kcal⋅mol−1 between n=6 and n=10. As the water network gradually protects the pyridine from additional solvent and the new water molecule mainly forms O–H⋯O bonds with the existing cluster rather than interacting with the aromatic nitrogen, this saturation behavior shows that the binding energy per added water molecule approaches a bulk‐like value. For larger water clusters linked to organic molecules, a comparable convergence of consecutive binding energies toward the bulk vaporization enthalpy (∼10kcal⋅mol−1) has been noted [79]. When combined, the population and clustering energy data provide a coherent picture of the microsolvation of pyridine: the first two water molecules form a cooperative HB chain by binding strongly and specifically at the nitrogen site; the water network becomes more autonomous after n=3, with several low‐energy topologies competing thermally; and after n≈5, each new water molecule contributes a roughly constant stabilization, indicating the convergence of the local HB environment toward bulk‐like conditions.

Solvation Enthalpy and Free Energy of PYRH2On

The incremental solvation enthalpy ΔHsolvn and Gibbs free energy ΔGsolvn, defined as the thermodynamic gain upon addition of one water molecule to the cluster of size n−1, are plotted as a function of n in Figure 9.

Incremental solvation enthalpy (ΔHsolv) and Gibbs free energy (ΔGsolv) of pyridine as a function of the number of explicit water molecules n, calculated at 298.15 K using Equations (2) and (3). For each cluster size n, the thermodynamic quantities are computed as Boltzmann‐weighted averages over all identified conformers of PYR(H2O). Energies are reported in kcal⋅mol.

The incremental enthalpy ΔHsolv is negative for all n=1−7, confirming that each successive hydration step is enthalpically favorable. For n=1, the stabilization arises from the direct O–H⋯N hydrogen bond at the pyridine nitrogen, whose strength has been estimated at approximately −4 kcal⋅mol−1 to −6 kcal⋅mol−1 at various levels of theory [2, 66, 78]. As n increases, ΔHsolv becomes progressively more negative, reflecting the cooperative reinforcement of the inter‐water O–H⋯O hydrogen‐bond network [70, 71], before leveling off toward a plateau for n≳6. In contrast, ΔGsolv remains positive over the entire range n=1−7, demonstrating that the entropic penalty associated with the immobilization and ordering of each additional water molecule systematically outweighs the enthalpic gain at every hydration step. It is important to note that these positive values are incremental gas‐phase quantities and should not be confused with the total hydration free energy of pyridine in bulk water, which is experimentally well established as negative: the FreeSolv database [80] reports ΔGhydexp=−4.69kcal⋅mol−1 (± 0.60), derived from vapor pressure and solubility measurements. This experimentally negative value reflects the cumulative effect of all solvent–solute interactions in the condensed phase, including long‐range electrostatic and polarization contributions that are inherently absent from a finite gas‐phase cluster of n≤10 water molecules. The positive incremental ΔGsolv at finite n is therefore physically expected: it reflects the entropic cost of organizing each additional water molecule within a discrete cluster, a cost that is only overcome collectively by the long‐range cooperative interactions of the bulk solvent [81].

This result indicates that the stepwise gas‐phase microsolvation of pyridine is thermodynamically non‐spontaneous under standard conditions, a behavior that has been reported for other neutral nitrogen‐containing aromatic molecules, where the entropic cost of localizing water molecules around a weakly basic site is not compensated by pairwise hydrogen‐bond interactions alone [5, 19]. The gap between ΔHsolv and ΔGsolv, which corresponds to the −TΔS contribution, remains roughly constant across the series, reflecting the systematic loss of translational and rotational degrees of freedom upon clustering. These findings confirm that gas‐phase microsolvation of neutral bases is enthalpically driven but entropically disfavored, and that spontaneous hydration only emerges through the collective long‐range interactions of the condensed phase [4, 5].

The temperature dependence of the thermodynamic quantities provides additional insight into the stability and formation mechanism of the PYR(H2O) clusters. As shown in Figure 10, the Gibbs free energy (ΔG) increases progressively with increasing temperature, indicating that the formation of the hydrated pyridine clusters becomes thermodynamically less favorable at higher temperatures. At the same time, the hydratation enthalpy of pyridine remains almost constant, indicating that the hydratation enthalpy is temperature‐independant. The fact that the hydratation enthalpy is temperature‐independant, and the hydratation free energy is linearly increasing indicate that the hydratation free energy is entropic‐driven. Similar thermodynamic behavior has previously been reported for hydrogen‐bonded molecular clusters and hydrated aromatic systems, where the interplay between solute‐solvent interactions and water–water cooperativity governs the relative stability of the clusters [19, 69, 76, 82].

Conclusion

A systematic exploration of the conformational landscape of PYR(H2O) (n=1−10) at the MN12SX‐D3/def2‐TZVP level yielded 326 stationary points, providing a comprehensive picture of the sequential microsolvation of pyridine. Structurally, the global minima evolve from a simple O–H⋯N donor–acceptor complex at n=1 toward cooperative three‐dimensional hydrogen‐bond networks, with closed‐cage topologies emerging from n≥7. Boltzmann populations show that conformational specificity is lost above ∼300 K for n≥3. Thermodynamically, incremental clustering energies converge to a bulk‐like plateau of ∼−10 kcal.mol beyond n=5, while the incremental Gibbs free energy remains positive throughout n=1−10, confirming that stepwise gas‐phase microsolvation is entropically disfavored at every hydration step. QTAIM analysis reveals a progressive enrichment of the non‐covalent interaction network from a single O–H⋯N bond at n=1 to five distinct interaction types at large n, with weak C–H⋯O and van der Waals contacts emerging from n≥3 as the water network encapsulates the aromatic ring. These results establish a benchmark dataset for solvation models of nitrogen‐containing heterocycles, and future work will extend this study to infrared pre‐dissociation spectroscopy and to the protonated analogs PYRH(H2O). Additionally, the computation of DLPNO‐CCSD(T1)/CBS relative energies combined with DFT‐derived ZPVE corrections for the full set of 326 stationary points identified in this work would provide an even more rigorous validation of the conformational landscape and energetic ordering reported here, and is planned as a natural extension of the present study.
