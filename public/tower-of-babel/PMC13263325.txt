Extracellular flux analyses indicate low ATP yield and require refinement for accurate determination of maximal oxygen consumption rate
Authors: Celine Ransy, Mathieu Boissan, Noureddine Hammad, Abdelouhab Bouaboud, Tarik Issad, Maud De Dieuleveult, Benoit Miotto, Manuela Ye, Eric Pasmant, Frédéric Bouillaud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Agilent Seahorse XF Analyzer has become a central tool for investigating cellular oxidative metabolism. Most studies employ the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test, which measures the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) under sequential pharmacological modulation of mitochondrial respiration. This assay partitions basal OCR into its functional components and estimates maximal OCR through uncoupler-driven stimulation of respiration, with the resulting value interpreted as the spare respiratory capacity. However, maximal OCR is obtained only after complete inhibition of ATP synthesis by oxidative phosphorylation, and its reliable determination requires appropriate uncoupler titration. Studies have underscored the limitations of this approach. To evaluate its reliability, we examined 97 recent publications reporting 530 Cell Mito Stress Tests. Strikingly, 17% of assays yielded a maximal OCR lower than the basal OCR, raising concerns about the accuracy of the method. To improve maximal OCR determination, we introduce the Cell Stimulation Test, which – analogous to maximal VO₂ assessments in patients – stimulates respiration directly from the basal state across a broad (1–8x) range of uncoupler concentrations. Using the same Seahorse plate, we compared results from both assays. Combined application of the Cell Mito Stress Test and the Cell Stimulation Test enhances the accuracy of maximal OCR assessment and eliminates the need for prior optimization of uncoupler concentration. Our survey further indicates that oxygen consumption not coupled to ATP synthesis constitutes a substantial fraction of basal cellular OCR (median ≈50%), with an unexpectedly large contribution from OCR typically classified as non-mitochondrial. We briefly discuss the implications and limitations of this observation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46479-w.

Introduction

Mitochondrial respiration is the primary source of energy in animal cells. During oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), the energy released from metabolic reactions is converted into ATP by the coordinated activity of the mitochondrial respiratory chain (complexes I–IV), which consumes oxygen, and the phosphorylating system (complex V together with nucleotide and phosphate transporters). Consequently, measurements of cellular oxygen consumption are widely used as a reliable quantitative indicator of aerobic energy metabolism and an indirect estimate of ATP turnover. However, ATP can also be generated through anaerobic pathways – glycolysis followed by lactic fermentation – which can be quantified by measuring lactate released by cells. Both mitochondrial respiration and lactic fermentation acidify the extracellular medium: mitochondrial respiration produces CO₂, which generates carbonic acid, whereas lactic fermentation releases lactic acid directly. The amount of acid produced per ATP molecule differs markedly between these pathways: lactic fermentation yields one molecule of lactic acid per ATP, while mitochondrial respiration produces only about 0.2 molecules of carbonic acid per ATP (or even less depending on the substrate, such as glucose or lipids). Despite this, medium acidification is typically interpreted solely as a proxy for lactic fermentation, and contributions from carbonic acid generated during mitochondrial respiration are generally not taken into account.

Measurement of oxygen consumption rate

Quantitative assessment of real-time cellular respiration kinetics requires dedicated instrumentation. The earliest approaches relied on manometric techniques, most notably the Warburg respirometer1. Although this device made a major historical contribution to metabolic research, it was quite complicated to use and offered limited time resolution.

The introduction of the Clark electrode greatly facilitated oxygen consumption rate (OCR) measurements in cells and isolated mitochondria by enabling accurate, continuous monitoring of oxygen levels. However, systems based on the Clark electrode generally required well-stirred, homogeneous suspensions, which limited their applicability to mammalian cells growing adherently on solid substrates. The development of the Seahorse XF Analyzer (Agilent), which uses oxygen- and proton-sensitive fluorophores, enabled intermittent measurements (approximately every 5 min) of OCR and extracellular acidification rate in live adherent cells cultured in multi-well plates. This technology has made metabolic studies of attached cells far more accessible to many researchers (https://www.agilent.com/en/products/cell-analysis/how-seahorse-xf-analyzers-work). Comparative studies have shown that cellular OCR values obtained with the Seahorse platform closely match those obtained using Clark-type electrodes2,3.

The cell Mito stress test

The Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test (https://www.agilent.com/en/product/cell-analysis/real-time-cell-metabolic-analysis/xf-assay-kits-reagents-cell-assay-media/seahorse-xf-cell-mito-stress-test-kit-740885) involves the sequential addition of mitochondrial inhibitors – oligomycin, rotenone, and antimycin – which block complex V (ATP synthase), complex I, and complex III, respectively, as well as the uncoupler FCCP (carbonylcyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone). The uncoupler increases proton permeability across the inner mitochondrial membrane and, according to Mitchell’s chemiosmotic theory (Additional file 1), is expected to stimulate mitochondrial respiration to its maximum possible rate.

In this assay, OCR is measured after each successive addition. First, the basal OCR is recorded in the absence of any compound. Oligomycin, an inhibitor of OXPHOS, is then added. Following this inhibition, the assay seeks to re-stimulate respiration to its maximal rate by adding FCCP. Finally, the mitochondrial respiratory chain is fully blocked by the combined addition of rotenone and antimycin. The resulting OCR profile is interpreted as follows. The decrease in OCR observed after oligomycin reflects the proportion of cellular respiration dedicated to ATP production via OXPHOS. The OCR measured after FCCP addition is assumed to represent, in intact cells, the maximal rate of electron transport achievable by the mitochondrial respiratory chain. The residual OCR in the presence of rotenone and antimycin corresponds to oxygen consumption that is independent of the mitochondrial respiratory chain and is therefore considered as the non-mitochondrial OCR. The difference between the OCR measured after oligomycin addition and that recorded following respiratory chain inhibition reflects the endogenous proton leak (ionic cycling) across the inner mitochondrial membrane, and is thus referred to as the “Leak.”

The physiological relevance of the maximal OCR measured in the presence of an uncoupler and, by extension, of the spare respiratory capacity as defined above, has been questioned by some authors4. Nevertheless, many researchers consider it as a useful indicator for identifying cells in which OXPHOS operates close to its limit, that is, when basal and maximal OCR values are similar or identical. Under such circumstances, cells may be unable to respond adequately to an energetic challenge (such as increased ATP demand or partial impairment of OXPHOS) or may already be experiencing an energy deficit. This situation can lead to a marked restriction of ATP availability for non-immediately essential processes, such as protein synthesis5–7. A loss of spare respiratory capacity is therefore viewed as a marker of a pathological or pre-pathological condition resulting from inadequate cellular oxidative metabolism. Accordingly, the Cell Mito Stress Test is expected to offer essential information on cellular energy status, assuming that the comparison of basal and maximal mitochondrial OCR is reliable.

At each step of the protocol, the OCR is measured several times (typically 2–4) to verify that a steady state has been reached, after which the mean of these measurements can be considered. The assay employs saturating concentrations of oligomycin, rotenone, and antimycin, generally in the low micromolar range, which are expected to fully inhibit their respective mitochondrial respiratory complexes. By contrast, a saturating concentration of the uncoupler cannot be used: excessively high concentrations suppress mitochondrial respiration, whereas suboptimal concentrations fail to stimulate mitochondrial respiration maximally. For this reason, the manufacturer’s instructions for the Cell Mito Stress Test recommend determining or validating the optimal uncoupler concentration prior to performing the assay.

The standard Cell Mito Stress Test protocol involves three sequential additions: oligomycin, uncoupler, and respiratory chain inhibitors (Additional file 1c). However, the Seahorse XF Analyzer allows up to four injections per well, making it possible to introduce an alternative or supplementary substrate prior to initiating the assay, or to add a metabolic inhibitor that perturbs pathways such as glucose or fatty acid metabolism. As a result, cells are exposed to only a single concentration of uncoupler during the Cell Mito Stress Test. Determining the optimal uncoupler concentration therefore becomes not merely a recommendation but an essential requirement, as this concentration is influenced by numerous factors. It cannot be assumed to be constant even within a defined cell line, as it may vary in derived or genetically modified variants, and according to experimental conditions such as medium composition or cell density. The sequence of additions used in the Cell Mito Stress Test is designed to extract as much information as possible from a cell preparation while maintaining a protocol applicable to a broad range of cellular models. This versatility explains its widespread adoption, but it also introduces inherent limitations. More precise assessments of cellular oxidative metabolism and mitochondrial function are achievable8,9; such approaches, established before the advent of Seahorse technology, include uncoupler titration, which forms the conceptual basis for the stimulation protocol proposed here.

The cell stimulation test

Established cell lines typically rely on a combination of OXPHOS and lactic fermentation to fulfill their ATP requirements. Following the addition of oligomycin, lactic fermentation is expected to increase sufficiently to maintain cell viability; however, this response depends strongly on the composition of the culture medium and may not always occur.

A major limitation of the Cell Mito Stress Test is that maximal OCR is assessed in cells that have been poisoned with oligomycin – i.e., in cells in which OXPHOS has been completely inhibited10. This entails a risk of cell death, meaning that the population contributing to subsequent OCR measurements may be smaller than the one contributing to the initial basal rate. Stimulation of glycolysis acutely suppresses mitochondrial oxidation through the “Crabtree effect,” in which glycolytic intermediates directly inhibit respiratory chain complexes. This interference can prevent full manifestation of maximal OCR when the uncoupler is added after oligomycin11. Furthermore, the initial steps of glucose and fatty acid oxidation require ATP, and this requirement increases proportionally with OCR, reaching its maximum when OCR is maximal. Thus, the absence of OXPHOS during the uncoupler-stimulated phase constitutes an additional barrier to achieving the true maximal metabolic rate. Collectively, these considerations suggest that the presence of oligomycin likely interferes with the development of the cell’s native maximal OCR – an effect already documented in experimental studies12. Recent reviews have revisited the interpretation of OCR measurements13,14. While the consequences of oligomycin exposure, as highlighted in10, have been discussed, the provided examples and guidelines continue to rely on the standard Cell Mito Stress Test protocol, as does much of the current literature.

For these reasons, we propose a Cell Stimulation Test (Additional files 1c, 2), which is based on the sequential addition of increasing concentrations of uncoupler to cells starting from their basal rate of oxygen consumption. Incorporating this Cell Stimulation Test alongside the Cell Mito Stress Test within a single Seahorse experiment is straightforward and offers several advantages. First, it enables the measurement of maximal cellular respiration without exposing the cells to the stress associated with complete inhibition of OXPHOS by oligomycin. Second, less uncoupler is required to achieve maximal OCR when stimulation begins from the basal state. Third, the use of four successive uncoupler injections allows a broad range of concentrations to be tested, making it possible to assess the optimal uncoupler concentration directly within the experimental run. Fourth, even if the optimal concentration is not achieved in an initial experiment, comparisons across cell types or experimental conditions remain valid, as the relative increase in OCR above the basal rate can still be evaluated. Thus, incorporating the Cell Stimulation Test together with the Cell Mito Stress Test eliminates the need for prior determination of the optimal uncoupler concentration for each experimental condition and makes the search for this optimal concentration explicit within the experimental dataset.

To support our proposal, we present two sets of observations. First, an examination of the published literature using the Seahorse Mito Stress Test reveals a substantial proportion of experiments in which the presumed maximal OCR is paradoxically lower than the basal rate; the number of studies reviewed also allows additional comments regarding other OCR parameters. Second, we compare the results obtained when the two tests are performed in parallel on the same cellular preparations within the same Seahorse plate.

It should be emphasized at the outset that our aim is not to resolve all the issues associated with the use of OCR measurements to quantify cellular energy metabolism8,9,13,14. Rather, we seek to offer an improvement relative to the current situation as reflected in the existing literature.

Materials and methods

The cell stimulation test

The Cell Stimulation Test differed from the Cell Mito Stress Test only in the sequence of reagent injections (Additional file 2). The assay medium and the concentrations of pharmacological agents followed Agilent recommendations, except that all solutions were prepared in-house using reagents from Sigma-Aldrich: oligomycin (O4876, MW ≈800), rotenone (R8875, MW = 394), antimycin A (A8674, MW ≥500), and the uncoupler CCCP (C2759 MW ≈ 205). Stock solutions were prepared in DMSO at 1000x the final working concentration. Working concentrations for inhibitors (oligomycin, rotenone, and antimycin) were in the low micromolar range (Additional file 3). Cell plating procedures and measurement programs were identical for the two assays. Both tests were performed on a single Seahorse plate, using at least six wells per cell type or plating density (three wells per assay). We propose an eight-well layout (Additional file 3), which includes designated empty wells (shown in black) for background correction. In the Cell Stimulation Test, each successive injection doubled the final concentration of uncoupler in the well (0.25, 0.5, 1, and 2 µM CCCP). Ten-fold dilutions of the stock solution in Seahorse medium (2.5, 5, and 10 µM) were loaded into injectors. To account for the increasing well volume over the course of the experiment, injection volumes were progressively adjusted to 16, 17, 19, and 21 µL for wells initially containing 140 µL. Data analysis followed the same principles as for the Cell Mito Stress Test, except that maximal OCR values were derived from the Cell Stimulation Test.

Cells

Wild-type (WT) mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) were kindly provided by John A. Hanover (NIH, USA). Cells were transfected with cDNA encoding GFP fused to protein A (MEF A), GFP fused to protein B (MEF B), or control GFP alone (MEF C) using FuGENE 6 Transfection Reagent (Promega). Stably transfected cell lines were selected using neomycin. MEFs were maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum. Mouse embryonic stem cells were obtained from Kengo Homma and Hidenori Ichijo (The University of Tokyo, Japan) and Stephen A. Wood (Griffith University, Queensland, Australia). These cells were derived from the inner cell mass of C57BL/6 N mouse blastocysts. Cells were cultured on feeder layers in DMEM (high glucose, with pyruvate) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum, 1% L-glutamine (Gibco, 25030-081), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Life Technologies, 15140163), 100 µM β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, M7522), and 500 U/mL leukemia inhibitory factor (Chemico, ESG1107). Immortalized human Schwann cell lines, either wild-type or harboring SUZ12 mutations, were used as previously described15.

Analysis of reported maximal OCR values in the literature

We examined 100 publications (2021–2022) identified in April 2023 in the Agilent Life Science Publication Database (https://www.agilent.com/search/?N=4294836537&No=0&Nrpp=20), irrespective of cell type or research field. Two inclusion criteria were applied to extract Cell Mito Stress Test data: (i) OCR data had to be presented in the main figures (not in supplementary or extended data), and (ii) the experimental profiles had to be shown in full so that variation across successive OCR measurements within each state could be evaluated. Three publications did not meet these criteria and were excluded. The remaining 97 publications encompassed multiple cell types (Additional file 4a) and yielded 530 Cell Mito Stress Test profiles. Quantitative OCR values were extracted by superimposing an appropriate scale (0–60 to 0–600) onto each profile of each Cell Mito Stress Test. Only the four states of the Cell Mito Stress Test were analyzed: the basal state (prior to oligomycin injection) and the three states following the sequential injections of oligomycin, uncoupler, and respiratory chain inhibitors. For each state within a Mito Stress Test profile, the several measurements shown on the figure were extracted, and averaged. The term OCR value will therefore denote these mean values, unless when examining measurement drift.

Results

Maximal OCR values in the literature

The OCR values from Cell Mito Stress Test profiles extracted from the literature (Additional file 4b) were expressed as percentages of the corresponding basal rates (Fig. 1). This representation allows a direct comparison of the distribution of the different OCR parameters. For instance, median values were 143% for uncoupled OCR, 46% for OCR in the presence of oligomycin, and 29% for OCR measured after addition of rotenone and antimycin. Half of the uncoupled OCR values fell within the 110–200% range. Most studies included three measurements for each OCR condition, resulting in a total of twelve measurements over a period of 80–100 min, corresponding to intervals of 6–8 min between measurements. Information about the uncoupler concentration was occasionally missing or could not be retrieved (Additional file 4c). FCCP was used in the vast majority of cases, with one publication reporting the use of CCCP. While most studies employed a single uncoupler concentration, a few presented a concentration range, indicating experimental optimization for the cell type(s) examined. More than 80% of the reported FCCP concentrations were less than or equal to 2 µM.

To assess whether the OCR measured in the presence of an uncoupler provides a reliable estimate of the maximal achievable OCR, a minimal requirement is that the uncoupled OCR exceeds the basal rate (> 100 in Fig. 1). This condition was not met in 17% of the tests examined. Even when decreasing the threshold to 90, 13% of the tests still failed to meet this criterion. Values within the 90–110 range reflect cases in which the basal OCR is close to the maximal achievable rate, indicating little or no respiratory reserve. In comparison, analysis of OCR values obtained after addition of oligomycin or respiratory chain inhibitors revealed far fewer inconsistencies with the expected behavior of the Cell Mito Stress Test: only 1.5% of the tests (8 instances) showed inhibited OCR values higher than the basal rate. Control traces were included in the Seahorse experiments and did not show significant differences overall (Additional file 4d). Still, when values were stratified into lower (0–50%) and upper (50–100%) ranges, small but significant differences were detected (Additional file 4e). To increase compliance with the test’s principles, several studies compared the last basal measurement with the highest uncoupled OCR and the lowest inhibited OCR. Applying this approach to our dataset – comparing the highest uncoupled value with the lowest basal value – reduced the percentage of experiments in which the uncoupled OCR was lower than the basal OCR from 17% to 14%. However, the improvement was modest, and this treatment ignores the majority of the measurements collected during the experiment.

We interpreted these measurements (Fig. 2a) to estimate several OCR components: mitochondrial OCR in the basal state (Mito basal); OCR attributable to oxidative phosphorylation (ATP); mitochondrial OCR not due to oxidative phosphorylation (Leak); non-mitochondrial OCR (non-Mito), corresponding to the OCR measured in the presence of respiratory chain inhibitors (OCR inhib. in Fig. 1); and the respiratory reserve (Reserve), defined as the difference between the maximally uncoupled OCR and the basal OCR (reference = 100). All experimental values are reported here, including negative respiratory reserve values (17% of experiments) and non-mitochondrial OCR values exceeding 100% (7 out of 530 experiments), i.e., cases in which the non-mitochondrial OCR was higher than the basal OCR, indicating that mitochondrial oxidative metabolism could not be detected. Experiments in which the uncoupled OCR was < 100% revealed anomalous measurements that may arise from inappropriate uncoupler concentration, from the consequences of oligomycin-induced poisoning, or from a time-dependent decline in cellular OCR for unknown reasons. The latter two scenarios would justify rejecting the entire experiment. Thus, a prerequisite for reliable estimation of maximal OCR is the establishment of a steady state during basal OCR measurements. The basal OCR measurement typically occupies roughly one quarter of the total duration of the Cell Mito Stress Test. A 20% drift during this interval, if maintained throughout the experiment, would correspond to an overall variation of 80%. To investigate such OCR drifts in our dataset, we analyzed the differences between successive measurements within the same condition – basal, oligomycin, uncoupled, or inhibited (Additional files 4f-h).

Regarding the stability of the basal OCR – always represented by at least three measurements – the absolute drift between the first and second measurements was consistently greater than that between the second and third (Fig. 3). In experiments with four or more basal OCR measurements (23% of all cases), the values generally suggested convergence toward a steady state (Additional file 4 g), with the majority showing a decrease relative to the first measurement. Drifts observed in OCR measurements obtained in the presence of oligomycin, uncoupler, or inhibitors (Additional file 4f) may reflect the effects of prolonged exposure to these toxic compounds, or a loss of reagent efficacy, for example due to binding of these hydrophobic molecules to the plastic surface of the plates. No correlation was found between the drift in basal OCR and that in uncoupled OCR (Additional file 4 h).

Overall, 78% of the tests in our dataset met the prerequisites for producing OCR values consistent with the principles of the Cell Mito Stress Test and exhibited an acceptable drift (< 20%) in basal OCR, enabling interpretation as shown in Fig. 2b. A small number of experiments yielded negative “Leak” values, which consequently resulted in “ATP” values exceeding the “Mito basal” rate. This may be due to imprecise measurements or low absolute rates subject to variability. Alternatively, such cases may indicate limitations in using these data to determine the individual contributors to the basal OCR (see Discussion).

The cell stimulation test

Strict application of the conductance model to the dose-dependent effect of proton transport by uncouplers implies that cells with different maximal OCRs require different optimal uncoupler concentrations. In contrast, all Cell Mito Stress Tests reported in the literature rely on a single uncoupler injection. This practice, combined with the potential effects of oligomycin poisoning, raises concerns about the reliability of the reported maximal OCR values. The Cell Stimulation Test addresses both limitations. First, maximal OCR is stimulated in cells that have not been exposed to oligomycin. Second, four successive uncoupler injections allow examination of a broad concentration range, with each injection doubling the uncoupler concentration. In our literature survey of Cell Mito Stress Tests, 83% of studies used FCCP at concentrations ≤ 2 µM (Additional file 4c), and the lowest reported concentration was 0.25 µM – corresponding to a relative range of 1–16. The Cell Stimulation Test, with its four successive two-fold increases (relative range 1–8), can therefore cover most concentrations previously used, within a single experiment. Here, we compare data obtained with the Cell Stimulation Test to those obtained using the Cell Mito Stress Test, performed simultaneously in a Seahorse XF96 Analyzer. These experiments involved mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cell lines (Fig. 4), mouse embryonic stem cells (Fig. 5), and human immortalized Schwann cells (Fig. 6). In the Cell Mito Stress Test (Figs. 4c, 5c and 6b), the first uncoupler concentration (0.25 µM CCCP) restored an OCR close to basal levels. The second uncoupler addition (0.75 µM final concentration) further increased OCR, but often failed to produce a stable value above the basal OCR (e.g., MEF lines B and C in Fig. 4c), thereby compromising determination of a stable maximal OCR. In contrast, in the Cell Stimulation Test, the same initial uncoupler addition (0.25 µM CCCP final concentration) produced a sustained increase above the basal OCR in all experiments and across all cell lines (Figs. 4d, 5d and 6c), typically reaching 120–150% of the basal rate. The second uncoupler addition differed between the two tests: 0.75 µM final in the Cell Mito Stress Test versus 0.5 µM in the Cell Stimulation Test. This choice was based on the observation that 0.25 µM CCCP restored OCR to approximately basal levels in the Cell Stimulation Test; according to the conductance model, 0.75 µM in the Cell Mito Stress Test should therefore correspond to the effect of 0.5 µM in the Cell Stimulation Test, making the two situations theoretically comparable. Occasionally, OCR values were indeed identical (blue trace in Fig. 4c, d), but in all other cases they were either lower in the Cell Mito Stress Test (Figs. 5c and 6b) or unstable (Fig. 4c). Doubling the uncoupler concentration in the Cell Stimulation Test carried the risk of transitioning abruptly from a suboptimal to an excessive concentration. For example, between 0.5 and 1 µM, OCR frequently decreased. Thus, the optimal uncoupler concentration for these cell lines fell within the 0.5–1 µM range, and precise determination of maximal OCR would require additional experiments. Optimal uncoupler concentrations should yield a plateau response; modest differences between two concentrations then indicate proximity to the optimum. Such patterns were observed at 1–2 µM (Fig. 4d, blue; Fig. 6c, red) and at 0.5–1 µM (Fig. 5d, blue and purple). In some cases, tolerance to supra-optimal concentrations appeared relatively high, as seen across 0.25–1 µM (Fig. 5d, blue, green, and red). It is important to note that the units used here are appropriate for assessing respiratory reserve relative to basal OCR, but do not allow comparison of absolute fluxes, which require normalization – a process that substantially increases error bars (Figs. 4b and 5b). Taken together, the Cell Stimulation Test enables comparison of uncoupler-stimulated OCR across different cell lines without requiring prior determination of the optimal uncoupler concentration.

The Cell Stimulation Test is more sensitive than the Cell Mito Stress Test for detecting modest spare respiratory capacity. In particular, the OCR measured after the first uncoupler addition produced an increase of approximately 20% (Figs. 4, 5 and 6). We observed that CCCP is two- to three-fold less potent than FCCP (Additional file 5). Accordingly, 0.25 µM CCCP corresponds to ≤ 0.125 µM FCCP, a concentration not encountered in Cell Mito Stress Test protocols reported to date (Additional file 4c). In the Cell Mito Stress Test, the oligomycin step makes the comparison between uncoupled OCR and basal OCR indirect, and this is more than a graphical inconvenience, as the oligomycin step carries additional potential consequences (see Discussion). In all cases shown here (Figs. 4, 5 and 6), cells reached a maximal OCR that exceeded the basal rate by 30% or more. Moreover, the stimulated OCR could be maintained over extended periods (≈100 min in Figs. 4, 5 and 6), allowing a more accurate assessment of the cells’ capacity to sustain elevated energy expenditure over the mid to long term.

Expression of results

OCR values are typically expressed as absolute values normalized to protein content, DNA content, or cell number. In our case, a practical constraint arises from the combined use of the Cell Mito Stress Test and the Cell Stimulation Test: determining the basal OCR for each cell type relies on twice as many wells as those needed for measuring the other OCR parameters (Additional file 3). For this reason, we expressed basal OCR values as absolute measurements, whereas inhibited and stimulated OCR values were normalized to an internal reference – the basal rate measured in the same well. In standard Seahorse configurations, this corresponds to the last basal measurement (Figs. 4, 5 and 6). Consequently, differences observed between cell types or experimental conditions may originate from two distinct sources: (i) variations in normalized basal OCR values, reflecting differences in basal oxidative metabolism, and (ii) differences in relative responses to uncouplers and mitochondrial inhibitors, reflecting the distribution between mitochondrial and non-mitochondrial OCR as well as the mitochondrial status with respect to minimal (Leak) and maximal OCR. Interpretation of these parameters must be made with awareness of the inherent limitations of these estimations, as discussed in13,14.

Discussion

The Cell Mito Stress Test provides two main types of information: the relative contributions of different processes (OXPHOS, proton leak, and non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption) to the basal OCR, and the maximal mitochondrial OCR. Most published Seahorse studies seek to correlate variations in cellular respiratory or glycolytic fluxes with differences in other phenotypic properties – such as motility, tumorigenic potential, or endocytic capacity – characterized through independent methods. The parameters of greatest interest are typically the basal OCR, the proportion attributable to OXPHOS, and the maximal mitochondrial activity (or respiratory reserve), the latter being analogous to maximal VO₂ measurements in physiological studies.

Maximal OCR in the Cell Mito Stress Test

Before developing the Cell Stimulation Test, we observed that a two-fold variation from the optimal concentration of uncoupler – whether FCCP, CCCP, or dinitrophenol – consistently resulted in markedly reduced OCRs (Additional file 5). Similar relative ranges for optimal concentrations of FCCP and dinitrophenol have been reported previously9, although CCCP was not included in those analyses. The experiments presented here remain fully consistent with this two-fold variation window. They further show that derivatives of the same cell line may require different optimal uncoupler concentrations, in agreement with the conductance model (i.e., higher maximal OCRs requiring higher uncoupler concentrations). These observations imply that maximal OCR values become uncertain when derivatives of a given cell line (or different experimental conditions) are compared using a single uncoupler concentration – a practice that dominates the literature, despite the feasibility of using two uncoupler injections. Indeed, a substantial fraction of Cell Mito Stress Tests produced the paradoxical outcome in which the basal OCR exceeded the presumed maximal OCR. The proportion of such cases represents a lower-bound estimate of incorrect maximal OCR determinations in the analyzed literature. The literature provides illustrative examples. In one study (PMID: 34825138), the authors employed a range of FCCP concentrations (0.5–2 µM) to compare different cellular models, indicating appropriate optimization for each cell line. Consistent with this approach, the uncoupled OCR values were stable and equal to or greater than their corresponding basal rates (1.0–1.8x). In contrast, another study (PMID: 34169549) compared derivatives of a single cell line (HCT116) using a fixed FCCP concentration (0.5 µM). The seven control traces showed an orthodox pattern, with uncoupled OCRs around 110–130% of the last basal measurement and oligomycin/inhibitor responses consistent with a typical Mito Stress Test. However, among the 24 derivatives – expected to differ in mitochondrial respiration – responses were highly heterogeneous: eight derivatives behaved as expected; seven produced uncoupled OCRs similar to basal OCR; eight showed signs of impaired mitochondrial respiration, with pronounced downward drift beginning at the initial basal measurement, indicating incompatibility with the conditions of the Mito Stress Test; and one derivative showed minimal response to either oligomycin or uncoupler. This example underscores that the stability of OCR measurements over time is essential for accurate interpretation. Our literature survey revealed that basal OCR frequently declined toward a lower, stable value after two or three measurements. Given that the Cell Mito Stress Test includes a substantial equilibration period before measurements begin, this decline is unlikely to reflect adaptation to the assay medium. Instead, it likely results from the measurement cycles themselves. For instance, agitation steps expose adherent cells to shear stress; early cycles may detach weakly attached cells, which would no longer contribute to subsequent OCR measurements. A sustained decline in basal OCR may therefore indicate a cell line unable to tolerate repeated measurement cycles, or progressive mitochondrial impairment during the assay.

Consequences of oligomycin use in the Cell Mito Stress Test

It is counterintuitive to assess maximal physiological performance in cells that have been poisoned. This limitation of the Cell Mito Stress Test is often overlooked, largely because it is assumed that once OXPHOS is inhibited by oligomycin, mitochondria within intact cells behave similarly to isolated mitochondria examined in vitro – i.e., mitochondria prepared from tissues or cells and suspended in a dedicated respiration buffer, where the effects of substrates, ADP, oligomycin, and uncouplers on oxygen consumption are studied. In cells, the effects of oligomycin, respiratory chain inhibitors, or uncouplers on OCR appear within seconds. The consequences for ATP turnover are expected to be equally rapid, as cellular ATP reserves sustain function for only a few minutes. Indeed, intracellular ATP content is widely considered as a reliable indicator of cell viability16. In our own experience, using cells grown in galactose medium – thus fully dependent on OXPHOS – addition of oligomycin collapsed the ATP/ADP ratio from 16 to zero; in glucose medium, the ratio decreased from 10 to 417. These changes occurred in under five minutes. In another model, we observed a decline in ATP/ADP ratio within one minute18. This is in stark contrast to the duration of a typical Cell Mito Stress Test following oligomycin addition, which lasts 60–75 min. Most Seahorse experiments use glucose-rich media, allowing many cell lines to survive to the suppression of OXPHOS by switching to lactic fermentation to sustain ATP production. However, cell lines that rely heavily on OXPHOS would be expected to die following oligomycin exposure. Direct evidence comes from a study (PMID: 34644150) comparing cells metabolizing glucose versus fatty acids. With glucose, OCR profiles were orthodox, with uncoupled OCR values 1.5–1.7-fold of basal OCR. In contrast, when fatty acids were the primary substrates, uncoupled OCR fell below basal values (0.45–0.47-fold of basal OCR). Seahorse experiments are frequently used to characterize the energy metabolism of cells whose dependence on OXPHOS is unknown. Yet high dependence on OXPHOS is a hallmark of many post-mitotic, terminally differentiated cells, raising further concerns about interpreting maximal OCR measurements made under conditions of enforced OXPHOS inhibition.

Unlike oligomycin, uncouplers do not suppress OXPHOS; instead, they increase proton leak across the inner mitochondrial membrane (Additional file 1a). When uncouplers are added during the Cell Stimulation Test, the conductance they induce is superimposed onto that of OXPHOS. This has been confirmed experimentally by comparing uncoupler addition in the presence and absence of oligomycin19, demonstrating that OXPHOS remains active up to the point at which OCR reaches its maximal value. Thus, during the Cell Stimulation Test, maximal OCR is observed at the moment when OXPHOS becomes fully impaired or shortly thereafter. In contrast, during the Cell Mito Stress Test, OXPHOS is completely suppressed immediately following the first oligomycin injection – i.e., 18–24 min before the first uncoupled OCR measurement. Based on our findings, the major issues requiring improvement are oligomycin-induced poisoning and the use of inappropriate uncoupler concentrations. A further complication arises from the lack of specificity of classical uncouplers: as protonophores, they can increase proton permeability in membranes other than the mitochondrial inner membrane. This off-target effect may well interfere with measurement of cellular OCR; a mitochondrial-targeted uncoupler with minimal plasma membrane effects has been proposed20 but is not yet widely implemented. An alternative approach would be to increase cellular ATP demand substantially, thereby stimulating oxidative metabolism through more physiological means. However, a broadly applicable strategy that achieves this without undesirable side effects has yet to be established.

In addition to concerns regarding the impact of oligomycin on cellular physiology and viability, it is important to question the assumption that mitochondria in oligomycin-poisoned cells behave like isolated mitochondria. Experiments using isolated mitochondria rely on optimal, saturating concentrations of substrates that are immediately accessible for mitochondrial respiration. In intact cells, however, extracellular substrates – such as sugars, lipids, and amino acids – must undergo multiple metabolic and transport steps before reaching mitochondria, and some of these steps require ATP. Consequently, unlike isolated mitochondria provided with saturating substrates, cells in which OXPHOS is suppressed may experience impaired substrate delivery to mitochondria, thereby reducing the maximal achievable OCR. Indeed, the maximal cellular OCR measured in a Seahorse analyzer reflects the fastest achievable rate of the entire sequence of events involved in oxidative metabolism – from substrate uptake at the plasma membrane to oxygen reduction by mitochondrial complex IV. This makes the Seahorse platform highly valuable for studying metabolic regulation. For example, the effects of the carnitine palmitoyltransferase-1 inhibitor etomoxir (PMID: 34610308) or of glucose availability (PMID: 35526742) on cellular OCR emphasize that steps upstream or distal to the mitochondrial respiratory chain can strongly influence cellular respiration. For these reasons, the conditions employed in the Cell Mito Stress Test do not ensure accurate determination of maximal OCR in cells that rely heavily on oxidative metabolism.

The risk of underestimating maximal OCR when using the Cell Mito Stress Test has been noted by others10. More generally, when determining an optimal concentration of any compound, titration is the standard approach8,9, and the Cell Stimulation Test effectively provides a four-point titration. Our experiments show that performing the Cell Mito Stress Test and the Cell Stimulation Test in parallel yields higher and more stable estimates of maximal OCR – even in the presence of high glucose concentrations (25 mM). Because the Cell Stimulation Test minimizes cellular damage, it is expected to provide a more physiologically relevant assessment of maximal OCR (Fig. 7).

A high electron flux to oxygen not linked to OXPHOS

This discussion must be nuanced by the consideration that the different contributors to basal OCR can only be accurately identified through successive inhibitor treatments if these inhibited states do not trigger compensatory mechanisms that restore electron flux to oxygen. In this regard, uncoupling can counteract the inhibitory effects of oligomycin on complex V, and alternative oxygen-reducing pathways can counteract respiratory chain inhibitors, as illustrated in several of our previous studies21–24. Cellular OCR reflects the entire electron flux from the oxidation of carbon substrates to the reduction of oxygen. This oxidative process provides animal cells with the energy required for survival, growth, and differentiated functions, most of which ultimately rely on ATP hydrolysis. Within this electron flux, the portion that supports ATP synthesis via OXPHOS is considered of high energetic value, whereas the remaining represents direct dissipation of redox energy as heat. OCR measured in the presence of oligomycin is commonly interpreted as an estimate of this energy dissipation. Our analysis of the literature revealed rather high values with a median of 46% of total cellular OCR. This oligomycin-resistant OCR is composite in nature, comprising both non-mitochondrial OCR and mitochondrial “Leak.” The latter, in intact cells, includes proton-coupled ion transport processes within mitochondria that have physiological relevance. OXPHOS is not the only contributor to ATP production. Carbon oxidation also involves several steps of substrate-level phosphorylation (SLP). In theory, the complete oxidation of glucose yields 30 ATP from OXPHOS and 4 ATP from SLP (4/34 = 9%). When OCR is interpreted as a proxy for ATP turnover, these calculations assume a 100% ATP yield per unit of oxygen consumed. If this yield falls to 50%, the proportional contribution of SLP increases to 21% (4/19), and rises from 7% to 13% during fatty acid oxidation. Thus, the energetic advantage conferred by OXPHOS may be less substantial than suggested by classical biochemical equations. Alternatively, these observations may reflect a cellular requirement for maintaining a high electron flux that is not strictly coupled to ATP turnover, potentially serving functions such as redox balance regulation or thermogenesis. Whether the cellular models examined in these studies accurately represent in vivo conditions is a separate question, beyond the scope of the present work.

Other pathways besides the mitochondrial respiratory chain can reduce oxygen, including those that generate reactive oxygen species such as superoxide and hydrogen peroxide. In the Cell Mito Stress Test, inhibition of the respiratory chain is expected to reveal these alternative electron fluxes leading to oxygen reduction. This fraction is commonly, though somewhat inaccurately, labeled as the “non-mitochondrial” OCR (“non-Mito”). For example, in PMID 35,698,260, loss of mitochondrial DNA function affected all OCR components of the Cell Mito Stress Test – including the so-called non-mitochondrial OCR – highlighting the mitochondrial contribution to this parameter. In the literature analyzed here, this “non-Mito” OCR represented a substantial component of redox energy loss, with a median value of approximately 30% of the basal OCR and values above 20% in 80% of tests. These proportions appear higher than those typically measured using Clark electrodes. Historically, little attention has been paid to this inhibited OCR fraction, which has often been subtracted from total OCR or treated as negligible. Our own earlier studies, based mostly on Clark electrode measurements, provided a more limited set of inhibited OCR values (24 measurements), yet comparison with the larger literature dataset (530 profiles) suggests a true discrepancy (Additional file 4i). This discrepancy affects the inferred relative contributions of mitochondrial Leak versus non-mitochondrial OCR to the basal rate. Differences in experimental conditions between Seahorse assays and Clark electrode measurements may help explain these divergences.

Conclusion and future developments

The present study leads to three main conclusions regarding the use of the Seahorse analyzer for determining cellular OCRs. First, evaluation of basal OCR should rely on more than three measurements. Although we employed four measurements for each OCR (Figs. 4, 5 and 6), some may prefer to limit the time of cell exposure to toxic compounds and thus retain only three measurements for inhibited or stimulated rates. In that case, five measurements for the basal rate would represent the most robust and consistent choice. Second, incorporating the Cell Stimulation Test enhances detection of respiratory reserve and improves the determination of maximal OCR. The uncoupler injection of the Cell Mito Stress Test can be retained, if desired, to verify consistency with the conductance model, but its inclusion is not essential. Third, the type and concentration of inhibitors may require adjustment; if the risk of cell damage is substantial, the assay design should be revised.

In their current format, Seahorse protocols quantify fluxes. However, the physiologically relevant parameter for energy expenditure is power, which, in our present model for the bioenergetic circuit, is proportional to the product of the current (I, corresponding to OCR) and the mitochondrial membrane potential. Moreover, cellular ATP concentration and the adenine nucleotide energy charge (AEC), defined as (ATP + ½ADP)/(ATP + ADP + AMP), are key parameters linking cellular energy metabolism to homeostasis, repair, growth, renewal, differentiation, and function within a multicellular organism. Accurate measurement of these variables will require the development of new protocols to refine the assessment of energetic profiles in cells, organoids, or organisms analyzed using the Seahorse platform.
