Pericardial hemangiosarcoma in a cat
Authors: Lezith D Chavez, Giulio Menciotti, Santiago Diab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Case summaryA 5-year-old, male castrated cat was presented with lethargy, tachypnea, and pleural and pericardial effusion. Echocardiographic assessment revealed an irregular hyperechoic structure that occupied a large portion of the thoracic cavity, expanding from the pericardium and surrounding the heart. Postmortem evaluation, histopathology and immunohistochemistry for factor VIII-related antigen confirmed the diagnosis of pericardial and splenic hemangiosarcoma.Relevance and novel informationThis case report highlights that hemangiosarcoma, despite being rarely reported, should be considered as a differential for pericardial neoplasms in cats.

Case description

A 5-year-old, male castrated American Bobtail cat was referred to the emergency service of our institution for hyporexia, progressively increasing respiratory effort over 2 days, lethargy, pleural effusion and abnormal SNAP feline N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide. Upon presentation, the patient had muffled heart sounds, diminished bronchovesicular sounds bilaterally and pale mucus membranes. The patient’s respiratory rate was 60 breaths/min, with increased abdominal effort and nostril flaring. The patient was administered furosemide (2 mg/kg IV) and supplemental oxygen via an oxygen-cage set at 40% oxygen concentration. After approximately 35 mins, his respiratory rate decreased to 36 breaths/min. Preliminary blood work consisting of packed cell volume and total solids yielded 21% and 8.8 g/dl, respectively.

The following morning, the cat was transferred to the cardiology service for continued management. At the time of transfer, the respiratory rate was 48 breaths/min with continued increased abdominal effort and nostril flaring. Upon auscultation, muffled heart sounds and decreased airway sounds were appreciated bilaterally. Mucus membranes were pale. The cat was administered another intravenous injection of furosemide (1 mg/kg) and butorphanol (0.2 mg/kg), resulting in a marked decrease in respiratory rate and effort.

A moderate amount of pericardial effusion and a marked amount of pleural effusion were observed on the echocardiogram. An abnormal hyperechoic structure surrounding the heart was visualized and presumed to be thickened pericardium. The structure was heterogeneous, cavitated, had irregular margins and displayed variable thickness in the range of approximately 4.27–13.47 mm (Figure 1). The right atrium was subjectively normal in size and did not collapse for more than one-third of the cardiac cycle, suggesting the absence of tamponade physiology. The left atrium:aorta ratio was 1.10. End-diastolic dimension of the left ventricle on M-mode was 15.60 mm and 13.18 mm on two-dimensional echocardiography. The thickness of the interventricular septum and left ventricular free wall were 5.68 mm and 8.91 mm, respectively, reflective of mild to moderate thickening. Given the reduced left ventricular internal diameter, the thickening was attributed most likely to pseudohypertrophy secondary to recent diuretic therapy administration, although other causes, like hyperthyroidism, systemic hypertension and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, could not be excluded at that time. Nonetheless, the echocardiographic findings ruled out the suspicion of congestive heart failure, and diuretic therapy was discontinued.

Complete blood count and serum biochemistry panels revealed a normocytic, normochromic, regenerative anemia and azotemia (Table 1). Thoracic radiographs displayed pleural effusion and subsequent atelectasis of the left and right cranial lung lobes and middle lobe, with suspicion of a cranial thoracic mass within the cranial mediastinum. Thoracic ultrasound reported a cranial thoracic lobular mass that tapered caudally and was confluent with the pericardium. Echogenic pleural effusion and pericardial effusion were also noted. Transthoracic fine-needle aspiration of the cranial thoracic mass was performed with a 22 G needle and submitted for cytopathologic assessment. The aspirate smears displayed low nucleated cellularity. The nucleated cells were primarily foamy macrophages, non-degenerate neutrophils, and many nucleated red blood cells and polychromatophils. There was also an abundant amount of peripheral blood (erythrocytes, lymphocytes and platelet clumps). A thoracocentesis was also performed with a 22 G needle yielding approximately 5 ml of non-clotting hemorrhagic fluid. No further fluid could be removed from the puncture site; given that the patient’s respiratory rate and effort had remained improved, another thoracocentesis was not performed before obtaining the cytologic results. On fluid analysis, the direct smear 100-cell differential count resulted in 67 non-degenerate neutrophils, 17 small lymphocytes, 15 macrophages and one eosinophil. The fluid characteristics displayed 6990 white blood cells/µl, 2,490,000 red blood cells/µl and 6.6 g/dl total protein. No infectious agents or overtly neoplastic cells were identified in any of the samples. Both mass and fluid sample interpretations concluded chronic active hemorrhage.

After communication with the owners, humane euthanasia was elected. Postmortem gross evaluation observed approximately 110 ml of uncoagulated blood within the thoracic cavity (severe hemothorax) and a small amount of sanguineous fluid within the pericardial sac (mild hemopericardium). The pericardium was greatly expanded by a dark red, multinodular, cavitated, blood-filled mass measuring 8 × 6 × 5 cm that surrounded, but was not adherent to, the heart and occupied approximately 50% of the thoracic cavity, causing caudodorsal displacement of the lungs (Figure 2). On cross-section, the thickness of the neoplastic pericardial wall was in the range of 0.5–1.3 cm. In addition, the spleen had two dark red to black, well-circumscribed, raised nodules measuring 11 × 8 mm and 3 × 2 mm. Tissue samples from the pericardial and splenic masses, thoracic lymph nodes, heart, adrenal gland, pancreas, lungs, kidneys, liver and gastrointestinal tract were fixed by immersion in 10% buffered formalin, pH 7.2, for 72 h and routinely processed for histopathology to obtain 4 µm-thick tissue sections that were stained with hematoxylin and eosin and subjected to histologic evaluation. The splenic and pericardial masses were compatible with the diagnosis of hemangiosarcoma (HSA).

(a) Gross pathology image of the thoracic cavity (right hemithorax) after transection depicting a large, irregular, mottled, cavitated and nodular mass effacing the pericardium. (b) Large irregular, heterogeneous, cavitated, blood-filled pericardial mass that is reflected dorsally

Briefly, the neoplasm was composed of solid areas of pleomorphic neoplastic cells arranged in loose nests and cords surrounded by scant fibrovascular stroma and neoplastic cells lining irregular vascular channels filled with erythrocytes and fibrin. The cells had a small to moderate amount of variably distinct eosinophilic cytoplasm, oval nuclei with finely stippled chromatin and a prominent magenta nucleolus. Anisocytosis and anisokaryosis were moderate. There were five mitotic figures in 10 fields at 400× (2.37 mm2) (Figure 3a). To confirm the neoplastic cell of origin, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections of the neoplastic mass were further assessed using standard immunohistochemical staining (Ultraview universal red kit; Roche) for the detection of factor VIII-related antigen, which is present in endothelial cells and used for the diagnosis of vascular neoplasms, such as HSA. 1 Neoplastic cells stained strongly positive for factor VIII-related antigen, which confirmed the diagnosis of HSA (Figure 3b). Additional histologic findings included moderate acute myocardial degeneration and necrosis of the right and left ventricular free walls interpreted as possibly associated to hypoxia (secondary to severe hemothorax).

Discussion

HSA is a highly malignant neoplasm traditionally thought to originate from vascular endothelium.2,3 However, recent evidence may support the origination from a pluripotent bone marrow progenitor.4,5 Reports of HSA in cats are limited, with a reported incidence of less than 2% of feline neoplasia.3,4,6 –9 There have been reports of primary feline HSA in the right atrium, right ventricle, subcutaneous tissues of the caudal abdomen, cranial mediastinum, mesentery, spleen, forelimb phalanx, nasal cavity, conjunctiva, tongue and hard palate.3,8–12 In dogs, HSA can occur concurrently in the spleen, subcutaneous tissues and the right atrium of the heart, but it is unclear whether this pattern represents metastases or multicentric origin. 7 Right atrial HSAs are common in dogs, 7 while there have been only three reported cases of primary feline cardiac HSA.3,8,12 The most recent described an HSA obliterating the right ventricular free wall with multiple pulmonary masses. 8 The second reported a cardiac HSA that adhered to the right atrium and occupied the pericardial space, leading to compression of the right atrium and cardiac tamponade. 12 The third reported primary feline cardiac HSA originating within the right atrium, involving the right auricle along with suspected metastasis to the liver. 3 Other reports of metastatic feline HSA have involved the lymph nodes, liver, lungs, spleen and kidneys.3,10 In this case report, there was no evidence of cardiac HSA. Furthermore, thoracocentesis was not carried out before performing the thoracic radiographs; this could have increased the diagnostic yield of the radiographs both for the primary mass and, potentially, for identification of secondary neoplastic lesions, although none were found at necropsy.

Reported prognosis for feline HSA varies among the diverse origination sites, metastasis, concurrent diseases and treatment options. Generally, the reported prognosis for feline non-visceral HSA is variable, while feline visceral HSA is poor.4,10 In this patient, the owners elected humane euthanasia upon communication of the diagnostic findings; therefore, prognostic information cannot be extrapolated.

Conclusions

It is likely that this case is a primary pericardial HSA with metastases to the spleen, considering the large size of the pericardial mass, the presence of two small nodules in the spleen and the usual metastatic patterns of tumors. This case supports that hemangiosarcoma should be considered as a differential for feline pericardial neoplasia.
