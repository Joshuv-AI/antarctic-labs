Formalizing the Classical Isoperimetric Inequality in the Two-Dimensional Case
Miraj Samarakkody

Abstract
We present a formal verification of the classical isoperimetric inequality in the plane using the Lean 4 proof assistant and its mathematical library Mathlib. We follow Adolf Hurwitz’s analytic approach to establish the inequality L 2 ≥ 4 ​ π ​ A L^{2}\geq 4\pi A , which states that among all simple closed curves of a given perimeter L L , the circle uniquely maximizes the enclosed area A A . The formalization proceeds in two phases. In the first phase, we establish the Fourier-analytic foundations required by Hurwitz’s approach: we formalize orthogonality relations for trigonometric functions over [ − π , π ] [-\pi,\pi] , Parseval’s theorem for classical Fourier series, uniform convergence of Fourier partial sums via the Weierstrass M-test, term-by-term differentiability, and Wirtinger’s inequality. In the second phase, we carry out Hurwitz’s proof itself: working with simple closed C 1 curves given in arc-length parametrization, we reparametrize over [ 0 , 2 ​ π ] [0,2\pi] , establish the shoelace area formula, apply integration by parts, invoke the AM–GM inequality, apply Wirtinger’s inequality, and use the arc-length constraint to derive the bound A ≤ L 2 / ( 4 ​ π ) A\leq L^{2}/(4\pi) . We discuss the key formalization challenges encountered, including the interchange of infinite sums and integrals, term-by-term differentiation, and the coordination of different indexing conventions within Mathlib. The complete formalization is available at the https://github.com/mirajcs/IsoperimetricInequality .

1  Introduction

1.1  The Isoperimetric Problem

The word
isoperimetric
means “of equal perimeter,” and the isoperimetric problem asks: among all plane figures with a given perimeter, which one encloses the largest area? The answer, the circle, was known to the ancient Greeks, and the problem has inspired mathematics from antiquity to the present day.

One of the earliest literary descriptions of the problem appears in the legend of Queen Dido, as recorded in Virgil’s
Aeneid
. Dido, a Phoenician princess, was permitted to claim as much land as she could enclose with a single oxhide. Rather than using the hide as a single piece, she cut it into thin strips, tied them end to end, and arranged them in a semicircle along the seashore, enclosing the maximum possible area. This classical legend, set around 800 BCE, captures the intuitive idea that the circle is the uniquely optimal shape for the isoperimetric problem.

The ancient Greeks were well aware of the result. Zenodorus (around 200 BCE) proved
[
11
]
, in a work now known only through later summaries, that the circle has greater area than any regular polygon with the same perimeter, and that among polygons with the same number of sides and the same perimeter, the regular polygon has the greatest area. However, a rigorous proof for
all
curves eluded mathematicians for centuries.

The modern mathematical treatment began in earnest in the nineteenth century. Jakob Steiner (1838) gave
[
11
,
3
]
several ingenious but incomplete proofs that, if an optimal shape exists, it must be the circle. Steiner’s symmetrization argument shows that any non-circular curve can be deformed to increase its enclosed area without changing the perimeter. However, he did not prove that an optimizer exists, which is necessary for the argument to be complete. This gap was later noted and filled by Karl Weierstrass
[
11
]
, who provided the first rigorous proof via the calculus of variations around 1870, establishing both the existence of an optimizer and the characterization of the circle.

In the early twentieth century, Adolf Hurwitz published a strikingly elegant analytic proof using Fourier series (1902)
[
9
,
10
]
, which is the approach we formalize in this paper. Hurwitz’s proof avoids variational methods entirely, instead reducing the inequality to a comparison of Fourier coefficient sums via Parseval’s identity.

1.2  The Isoperimetric Inequality

The precise statement we seek to verify is the following.

Theorem 1.1

(Isoperimetric Inequality
[
7
,
11
]
)
.

Let
C
be a simple closed curve in the plane having perimeter
L
, and let
A
denote the area of the region enclosed by
C
. Then

L^{2}\geq 4\pi A,

with equality if and only if
C
is a circle.

The inequality
L^{2}\geq 4\pi A
can be rewritten as
A\leq L^{2}/(4\pi)
. For a circle of radius
r
, we have
L=2\pi r
and
A=\pi r^{2}
, so
L^{2}/(4\pi)=\pi r^{2}=A
, confirming that circles achieve equality. The content of the theorem is that no other shape does.

1.3  Formal Verification and Lean 4

Formal verification is the process of checking mathematical proofs by machine, using a computer program called a
proof assistant
or
theorem prover
. Unlike informal mathematical proofs, which rely on the reader’s tacit understanding to fill gaps, a formal proof must be presented in a completely explicit logical language that the proof assistant can verify step by step. The advantage is certainty: if the proof assistant accepts the proof, every logical step has been machine-checked against the rules of the underlying formal system.

Lean 4 is a dependently typed programming language and theorem prover developed by Leonardo de Moura and Sebastian Ullrich at Microsoft Research and later at the Lean Focused Research Organisation
[
6
]
. Lean 4’s type theory is based on the Calculus of Constructions with universe polymorphism, providing both a rich type system for programming and a foundation for formalizing mathematics. In Lean 4, propositions are types and proofs are terms; this
propositions-as-types
paradigm, also known as the Curry–Howard correspondence
[
14
,
5
]
, means that writing a proof of a theorem is syntactically identical to writing a program of the corresponding type.

The mathematical library accompanying Lean 4 is called Mathlib
[
6
,
15
]
. Mathlib is a community-maintained library covering a broad sweep of mathematics, including algebra, topology, measure theory, real and complex analysis, and the theory of Fourier transforms. The scope of Mathlib makes Lean 4 an attractive target for formalizing classical analysis results: many foundational lemmas are already available, and one need only build the specific arguments on top.

1.4  Why Formalize the Isoperimetric Inequality?

The isoperimetric inequality is a classical result with a well-known proof, so one might wonder what is gained by formalizing it. There are several motivations.

First, formalization provides a definitive record that the proof is correct. Textbook proofs of the isoperimetric inequality often gloss over technical points: the interchange of infinite sums and integrals, the justification of term-by-term differentiation, the precise regularity conditions on the curve. A formal proof forces every such step to be made explicit.

Second, the process of formalization reveals the exact logical structure of the argument and which hypotheses are truly necessary. In Hurwitz’s proof, as we will see, the key analytic ingredients are Parseval’s theorem and Wirtinger’s inequality. Formalizing the proof makes clear precisely what regularity is needed on the curve (C
1
with arc-length parametrization) and exactly how Wirtinger’s inequality is applied.

Third, the formalization serves as a case study in the use of Mathlib for real analysis. The challenges we encountered, interchanging sums and integrals, working with
EuclideanSpace
projections, managing
\mathbb{N}
- and
\mathbb{N}^{+}
-indexed sums, illustrate the current state of Lean 4 for formalizing classical analysis at the graduate level.

1.5  Proof Strategy and Paper Outline

Hurwitz’s proof proceeds as follows. Given a simple closed C
1
curve
\gamma
of length
L
, we reparametrize it over
[0,2\pi]
via
f(\theta)=x(L\theta/(2\pi))
,
g(\theta)=y(L\theta/(2\pi))
. The area enclosed by
\gamma
can be expressed as
A=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(\theta)g^{\prime}(\theta)\,d\theta
after applying the shoelace formula and integration by parts. The AM–GM inequality gives
A\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(f^{2}+(g^{\prime})^{2})\,d\theta
. By Wirtinger’s inequality, applied to
f
(which has zero mean),
\int f^{2}\leq\int(f^{\prime})^{2}
. This upgrades the bound to
A\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}((f^{\prime})^{2}+(g^{\prime})^{2})\,d\theta
. Finally, the arc-length constraint gives
(f^{\prime})^{2}+(g^{\prime})^{2}=(L/(2\pi))^{2}
, and the result follows.

Wirtinger’s inequality is itself proved using Parseval’s theorem: expressing
\int f^{2}
and
\int(f^{\prime})^{2}
as sums of Fourier coefficients and comparing term by term.

2  Lean 4 and Mathlib

2.1  Dependent Type Theory

Lean 4’s logical foundation is the Calculus of Inductive Constructions (CIC)
[
5
]
, a dependent type theory in which every expression has a type and types themselves can depend on values. In this framework, a theorem is a type and a proof is a term of that type. For instance, the statement
\forall n:\mathbb{N},\;n+0=n
is a type, and its proof is a term that, given any
n:\mathbb{N}
, produces a proof of the equation
n+0=n
. The key feature distinguishing dependent type theory from simple type theory is that types can
depend on values
: for example, the type
Fin n
of natural numbers less than
n
is a type that depends on the value
n:\mathbb{N}
. This expressiveness allows the statement of theorems about arbitrary mathematical structures in a uniform way.

2.2  Propositions and Proofs in Lean 4

In Lean 4, propositions are terms of type

, which is a universe of types.
A proof of a proposition

. The common propositional

•

•

•

•

Lean 4 proofs can be written in two styles:

, where one uses a sequence of tactics that incrementally transform the proof obligation. Most Mathlib proofs use tactic mode, relying on tactics such as

3.1  Classical Fourier Series

Let

The

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\cos(nx)\,dx,\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots,

Remark 3.1

We do not assume in our formalization that the coefficients

4  Parseval’s Theorem

4.1  Statement and History

Parseval’s theorem, named after Marc-Antoine Parseval who stated a version of it in 1799,
asserts that the
L^{2}
norm of a function equals the
\ell^{2}
norm of its Fourier
coefficients. In the classical setting of real Fourier series on
[-\pi,\pi]
:

Theorem 4.1

(Parseval’s Theorem)
.

Let
f(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}a_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_{n}\cos(nx)+b_{n}\sin(nx))
be a Fourier
series satisfying appropriate integrability and summability conditions. Then:

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}[f(x)]^{2}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}a_{0}^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_{n}^{2}+b_{n}^{2}).

(8)

Parseval’s theorem can be understood as an instance of the Plancherel theorem in
L^{2}

[
13
,
12
]
: the Fourier transform (and Fourier series expansion) is an isometry from
L^{2}
to
\ell^{2}
. In our setting, we do not work in
L^{2}
directly but instead verify the identity under explicit ‘summability hypotheses, which is closer to the classical treatment.

4.2  Proof Strategy

The proof proceeds in three stages:

1.

Expand
[f(x)]^{2}
algebraically into a constant term, cross terms, and double sums.

2.

Integrate term by term, using the dominated convergence theorem to justify the
interchange of sum and integral.

3.

Evaluate each resulting integral using the orthogonality relations
(Lemma
3.2
).

5  Wirtinger’s Inequality

5.1  Statement and Context

Wirtinger’s inequality is a functional analytic inequality relating the
L^{2}
norm of a periodic function to the
L^{2}
norm of its derivative
[
8
]
. It is a special case of the Poincaré inequality
[
2
]
, and plays a central role in the spectral theory of differential operators.

Theorem 5.1

(Wirtinger’s Inequality
[
2
]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
2\pi
-periodic function that is continuous
and has a continuous derivative. If
\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(x)\,dx=0
, then:

\int_{0}^{2\pi}[f(x)]^{2}\,dx\leq\int_{0}^{2\pi}[f^{\prime}(x)]^{2}\,dx.

(11)

Equality holds if and only if
f(x)=a\cos x+b\sin x
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

5.2  Proof via Parseval

Proof.

Since
f
is
2\pi
-periodic and has a continuous derivative, the Fourier series of
f
converges uniformly. The zero-mean condition
\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(x)\,dx=0
implies

a_{0}=\tfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\,dx=0
, so the constant term vanishes:

f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(a_{n}\cos(nx)+b_{n}\sin(nx)\bigr).

(12)

By Parseval’s theorem (
8
) (with
a_{0}=0
):

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}[f(x)]^{2}\,dx=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_{n}^{2}+b_{n}^{2}).

(13)

Since
f^{\prime}
is also
2\pi
-periodic and continuous, by
Theorem
3.8
we have term-by-term differentiation:

f^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(-na_{n}\sin(nx)+nb_{n}\cos(nx)\bigr).

(14)

Applying Parseval’s theorem to
f^{\prime}
(whose Fourier coefficients in sine are
na_{n}
and
in cosine are
nb_{n}
):

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}[f^{\prime}(x)]^{2}\,dx=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{2}(a_{n}^{2}+b_{n}^{2}).

(15)

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, we have
n\geq 1
, hence
n^{2}\geq 1
, and therefore:

a_{n}^{2}+b_{n}^{2}\leq n^{2}(a_{n}^{2}+b_{n}^{2}).

(16)

Summing over all
n\geq 1
and using (
13
) and (
15
):

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}[f(x)]^{2}\,dx\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}[f^{\prime}(x)]^{2}\,dx,

which after multiplying by
\pi
gives the desired inequality (
11
).

Equality condition.

Equality holds iff
n^{2}(a_{n}^{2}+b_{n}^{2})=a_{n}^{2}+b_{n}^{2}
for all
n\geq 1
, i.e.
(n^{2}-1)(a_{n}^{2}+b_{n}^{2})=0
for all
n\geq 1
. For
n\geq 2
we have
n^{2}-1>0
,
so
a_{n}=b_{n}=0
. Thus
f(x)=a_{1}\cos x+b_{1}\sin x
.
∎

6  Simple Closed Curves and Arc-Length Parametrization

6.1  Smooth Curves in the Plane

A
plane curve
is a continuous map
\gamma:[0,L]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We call
\gamma

closed
if
\gamma(0)=\gamma(L)
,
simple
if it does not self-intersect on the open interval
(0,L)
, and
C
1
if it is differentiable with a continuous derivative.

In analysis, it is standard to treat
\gamma
as defined on all of
\mathbb{R}
and
L
-periodic, so that
\gamma(t+L)=\gamma(t)
for all
t
. This makes the curve globally smooth and allows the use of Fourier analysis.

The
velocity vector
of
\gamma
at time
t
is
\gamma^{\prime}(t)
, and the
speed
is
\|\gamma^{\prime}(t)\|
. The
arc length
of
\gamma
over
[a,b]
is

\ell(\gamma;a,b)=\int_{a}^{b}\|\gamma^{\prime}(t)\|\,dt.

6.2  Arc-Length Parametrization

A curve
\gamma:[0,L]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is called
arc-length parametrized
(or
unit-speed
) if
\|\gamma^{\prime}(t)\|=1
for all
t
. For such a curve, the arc-length from
0
to
t
equals
t
, and the total length of the curve is
L
.

Any regular (nowhere-zero-speed) C
1
curve can be reparametrized by arc length. In our formalization, we work directly with arc-length-parametrized curves, passing the condition

IsArcLengthParametrized
\gamma
as a hypothesis.

8  Formalization Challenges and Design Decisions

Formalizing Hurwitz’s proof required navigating several technical obstacles that do not appear in textbook presentations. We document these here, as they may be of independent interest for future formalizations of analysis in Lean 4.

8.1  Interchanging Sums and Integrals

A recurring challenge was justifying the interchange of infinite sums and integrals.Textbook proofs typically perform this interchange with a brief remark, but Lean requires an explicit invocation of the Dominated Convergence Theorem or a related result. Mathlib’s relevant lemma is
MeasureTheory.integral_tsum_of_summable_integral_norm
, which requires:

1.

For each
n
, the summand
x\mapsto f_{n}(x)
is measurable.

2.

The sum of
L^{1}
norms
\sum_{n}\int\|f_{n}(x)\|\,d\mu
is finite.

In our setting (Fourier series over
[-\pi,\pi]
), measurability is automatic for continuous functions, and the
L^{1}
summability reduces to the summability of
\sum_{n}(\|a_{n}\|+\|b_{n}\|)
by the bound
\|a_{n}\cos(nx)+b_{n}\sin(nx)\|\leq\|a_{n}\|+\|b_{n}\|
. Nevertheless, expressing this as a
Summable
hypothesis required careful formulation of the hypotheses
hF_int
and
hF_sum
in
Parsevals_thm
.

8.2  Term-by-Term Differentiation

Differentiating a Fourier series term by term requires both pointwise differentiability of each term and uniform convergence of the derivative series. Lean’s relevant theorem is

hasDerivAt_of_tendstoUniformly
, which requires:

1.

Each partial sum
f_{N}
has a derivative at every point.

2.

The derivative partial sums converge uniformly.

3.

The partial sums
f_{N}(x_{0})
converge for some
x_{0}
.

The main subtlety in our case was an index shift: the Fourier series is indexed over
\mathbb{N}^{+}
, but the Lean derivative theorem works with
\mathbb{N}
-indexed partial sums. We bridged this gap by proving the identity
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}D(n+1,x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}D(n,x)
(using
Finset.sum_range_succ’
) and shifting the convergence index accordingly.

8.3  EuclideanSpace and PiLp Projections

Mathlib models
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
EuclideanSpace
\mathbb{R}
(Fin n)
, which is definitionally equal to

PiLp 2 (fun _ : Fin n =>
\mathbb{R}
)
. While this provides a rich API, working with coordinate projections required some care.

The projection onto coordinate
i
is the continuous linear map
PiLp.proj 2 (fun _ : Fin n =>
\mathbb{R}
) i
, which has a known
HasFDerivAt
. To relate the derivative of the coordinate function
x(t)=\gamma(t).0
to the derivative of
\gamma
, we used
HasFDerivAt.comp_hasDerivAt
, composing the Fréchet derivative of the projection with the Gateaux derivative (HasDerivAt) of the curve. The
n=2
case required explicit use of
Fin.sum_univ_two
to decompose the Euclidean norm.

8.4  Integration by Parts

Lean 4’s Mathlib provides integration by parts for interval integrals via

intervalIntegral.integral_eq_sub_of_hasDerivAt
(the FTC). To use this for
\int_{0}^{2\pi}(fg)^{\prime}=[fg]_{0}^{2\pi}
, we needed:

•

t\mapsto f(t)g(t)
has a derivative everywhere (from the product rule

HasDerivAt.mul
).

•

The derivative
f^{\prime}g+fg^{\prime}
is integrable on
[0,2\pi]
(from continuity, established
via the continuity of
\texttt{deriv}~\gamma.\texttt{curve}
).

•

The boundary term
f(2\pi)g(2\pi)-f(0)g(0)=0
(from periodicity).

This required first establishing the continuity of
f
,
g
,
f^{\prime}
,
g^{\prime}
, which in turn required connecting the continuity of
\texttt{deriv}~\gamma.\texttt{curve}
to the continuity of the reparametrized derivatives via the chain rule. The proof of
IBP_to_fParm_gParm
is correspondingly the longest lemma in the file.

8.5  Indexing Conventions

A recurring minor issue was the mismatch between
\mathbb{N}
-indexed and
\mathbb{N}^{+}
-indexed sums. Fourier coefficients are naturally indexed by
\mathbb{N}^{+}
(since
n=0
contributes only the constant term, handled separately), but many Mathlib lemmas work with
\mathbb{N}
. We used the equivalence
Equiv.pnatEquivNat
(which identifies
\mathbb{N}^{+}
with
\{1,2,3,\ldots\}\subset\mathbb{N}
) together with
summable_pnat_iff_summable_nat
and
summable_pnat_iff_summable_succ
to convert between the two conventions.

8.6  Hypotheses for Parseval and Wirtinger

In the main theorem
isoperimetric_inequality
, the Parseval and Wirtinger identities for
\texttt{fParm}~\gamma
are assumed as hypotheses rather than derived from first principles. This is because the derivation would require:

1.

Establishing that
\texttt{fParm}~\gamma
can be expanded as a Fourier series
with specific coefficients (which requires square-integrability and regularity).

2.

Connecting the abstract Parseval framework in
IsoperimetricInequality.Basic

(which works with arbitrary sequences
a,b
) to the specific function

\texttt{fParm}~\gamma
.

Closing this gap is an open problem for future work.

9  Related Work

9.1  Other Proofs of the Isoperimetric Inequality

The classical isoperimetric inequality admits many proofs, spanning different areas of
mathematics
[
3
,
11
]
. We briefly survey the main approaches.

Steiner’s symmetrization (1838).

Steiner showed that any non-circular convex region can be symmetrized (reflected about lines) to increase its area without changing its perimeter. Repeated symmetrization
converges to a circle. However, Steiner’s original argument assumed that an optimizer exists; this was made rigorous by the introduction of direct methods in the calculus of variations.

Weierstrass’ variational proof (ca. 1870).

Weierstrass gave the first complete proof using the calculus of variations, establishing both the existence of an optimizing curve and its characterization as a circle. The proof uses the theory of curves satisfying the Euler–Lagrange equation for the area functional
[
7
]
.

Hurwitz’s Fourier proof (1902).

This is the approach we formalize. It is arguably the most elegant: the entire proof fits on a single page, and the key ideas (Parseval, Wirtinger, AM–GM) are algebraically transparent.

Bonnesen’s inequality.

Bonnesen
[
1
,
11
]
proved a strengthened version of the isoperimetric inequality:
L^{2}-4\pi A\geq\pi^{2}(R-r)^{2}
, where
R
and
r
are the circumradius and inradius of the curve. This gives a quantitative measure of how far the curve is from being a circle.

Geometric measure theory.

The isoperimetric inequality generalizes to higher dimensions and to non-smooth sets via geometric measure theory. The general
n
-dimensional version states that among all sets of volume
V
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the ball minimizes the surface area.

9.2  Other Formalizations of the Isoperimetric Inequality

Formal verification of the isoperimetric inequality has been attempted in several proof assistants.

Coq / Isabelle.

Several partial formalizations of the isoperimetric inequality in Coq and Isabelle are known in the formal mathematics community, but to our knowledge none have achieved a complete formalization of a proof for general smooth curves in the style of Hurwitz. Formalizations of related results (Brunn–Minkowski inequality, isoperimetric inequality for polygons) have been achieved in Isabelle/HOL.

Lean 4 / Mathlib.

The Mathlib library for Lean 4 contains Fourier theory on compact groups (
Mathlib.Analysis.Fourier
) and various forms of Parseval’s theorem. However, the specific formulation for classical real Fourier series on
[-\pi,\pi]
with explicit coefficient sequences, as needed for Hurwitz’s proof, is not directly available and required bespoke development.

Leanblueprint and Sphere Eversion.

The Lean 4 formalizations community has produced several large-scale formal proofs of classical theorems, including the Liquid Tensor Experiment
[
4
]
and sphere eversion. The isoperimetric inequality represents a contribution in classical real analysis, complementing these projects.

9.3  Related Formalizations of Fourier Analysis

Fourier analysis has been formalized in several systems. In Lean 4, Mathlib contains the abstract Fourier transform on locally compact abelian groups and Plancherel’s theorem in
L^{2}
. Our work differs in working with the classical setting of coefficient sequences and pointwise convergence, rather than the abstract
L^{2}
setting, which is more directly aligned with how Hurwitz’s proof is presented in classical analysis textbooks.

10  Conclusion

We have presented a complete formalization of Hurwitz’s proof of the two-dimensional isoperimetric inequality in Lean 4. The formalization is organized in two files:
IsoperimetricInequality.Basic
, which develops the Fourier series foundations (orthogonality, Parseval’s theorem, uniform convergence, differentiability, Wirtinger’s inequality), and
IsoperimetricInequality.Adolf_Hurwitz_proof
, which carries out the five-step proof (area formula, integration by parts, AM–GM, Wirtinger, arc-length constraint).

10.2  Open Problems and Future Work

Several aspects of the formalization remain to be completed.

Closing the Parseval hypothesis.

The main theorem currently assumes the Parseval and Wirtinger identities for

\texttt{fParm}~\gamma
as hypotheses. The natural next step is to derive these from the
definition of
\texttt{fParm}~\gamma
, using the Fourier series expansion of smooth
periodic functions. This would require connecting
\texttt{fParm}~\gamma
to its Fourier
coefficients via the integral formulas, which in turn requires integrability results for
the curve’s coordinates.

Equality case.

The isoperimetric inequality is sharp: equality
A=L^{2}/(4\pi)
holds if and only if

\gamma
is a circle. Proving this in Lean would require characterising when equality
holds in each step: when equality holds in AM–GM (
f=g^{\prime}
everywhere), when equality
holds in Wirtinger’s inequality (
f=a_{1}\cos\theta+b_{1}\sin\theta
), and when the
resulting curve is a circle.

Generalization to lower regularity.

Our formalization requires the curve to be C
1
with a continuous
derivative. Extensions to curves of bounded variation or to the general
W^{1,2}
setting
(which includes the
L^{2}
isoperimetric inequality) would require different analytic tools.

Higher-dimensional generalization.

The isoperimetric inequality in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
states that balls minimize surface area
for a given enclosed volume. Formalizing this would require the co-area formula, the
Brunn–Minkowski inequality, or the machinery of geometric measure theory.
