Zero Cancellation and Equation Structure in Kiselman’s Semigroup
Luka Andrenšek

Abstract
We investigate equations in Kiselman’s semigroup K n K_{n} , generated by a 1 , … , a n a_{1},\dots,a_{n} . Let f f denote the zero element of K n K_{n} . We prove that if y ∈ K n y\in K_{n} lies in the subsemigroup generated by a 2 , … , a n a_{2},\dots,a_{n} , then x ​ y = f xy=f implies x = f x=f . In contrast, the equation x ​ a 1 = f xa_{1}=f admits non-trivial solutions. We describe the solution set of this equation, show that its cardinality is 1 + | K n − 1 | 1+|K_{n-1}| , and study its algebraic structure. Moreover, we show that | K 2 ​ n + 1 | |K_{2n+1}| is even, whereas | K 2 ​ n | |K_{2n}| is odd.

1
Introduction

Let
n
be a positive integer.
The
Kiselman’s semigroup

K_{n}
is defined by

K_{n}=\langle a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\mid a_{i}^{2}=a_{i},a_{i}a_{j}a_{i}=a_{j}a_{i}a_{j}=a_{i}a_{j},1\leq j<i\leq n\rangle.

We denote by
e
the unit element of
K_{n}
.

This family of semigroups arose as a natural
generalisation of a semigroup of operators in convex analysis introduced
by Kiselman
10
.
Fundamental structural properties of
K_{n}
were established by
Kudryavtseva and Mazorchuk
11
.
The semigroup arises in graph dynamics, as discussed by Collina and D’Andrea
6
,
and exhibits rich combinatorial structure.

A more general family of monoids was introduced by Ganyushkin and Mazorchuk
9
.
They defined the
Hecke–Kiselman monoid

\mathrm{HK}_{\Theta}
associated to a finite simple digraph
\Theta
.

Identities in certain families of these monoids were studied by Ashikhmin, Volkov, and Zhang
4
, who solved the corresponding finite basis problem, and
further semigroup identities were investigated by Wiertel
18
.
Normal forms for two particular families were described by
Lebed
12
, yielding solutions to the word problem in
those cases, while normal forms for another family were established by Aragona and D’Andrea
3
.

Conditions for finiteness were analysed by
Aragona and D’Andrea
2
, who established necessary conditions on

\Theta
and provided a full
classification of simple digraphs on at most four vertices for which

\mathrm{HK}_{\Theta}
is finite.
The finiteness of
K_{n}
was proved in
(
11
, Theorem 3)
, and
D’Andrea and Stella
7
showed
that the cardinality of
K_{n}
grows
double-exponentially as
n
tends to
\infty
.

Effective representations of Hecke–Kiselman monoids were discussed by Forsberg
8
.
We determined all endomorphisms of
K_{n}
in
1
.

Representations, properties, and structure of
the corresponding Hecke–Kiselman algebras
were studied in
13
;
14
;
15
;
16
;
17
.

In
(
11
, Section 10)
, deletion properties were established in
K_{n}
.
The present paper continues the study of these deletion properties, revealing further combinatorial consequences.
In particular, we investigate equations involving the zero element
f
. We analyse equations of the form

xy=f
under various restrictions on
y\in K_{n}
.
The paper relies heavily on the concept of canonical words, which was introduced in
(
11
, Section 3)
.

2
Main Results

In Section
3.2
, we recall that
K_{n}
has a zero element, which we denote by
f
.
Let
\langle a_{2},a_{3},\dots,a_{n}\rangle
be the subsemigroup of
K_{n}
generated by
\{a_{2},a_{3},\dots,a_{n}\}
.
We prove the Zero Cancellation Theorem, which states the following.

\text{If }y\in\langle a_{2},a_{3},\dots,a_{n}\rangle\text{ and }xy=f,\text{ then }x=f.

In contrast, the equation
xa_{1}=f
admits non-trivial solutions. We show that

\bigl|\{x\in K_{n}\mid xa_{1}=f\}\bigr|=1+|K_{n-1}|

and give an explicit description of its solution set.
We further prove that this set is a subsemigroup of
K_{n}
and derive an explicit multiplication rule.

Using ideas from the proofs of these results, we show that

|K_{2n+1}|\text{ is even},\qquad|K_{2n}|\text{ is odd}.

The paper proceeds as follows.
In Section
3
, we summarise the main definitions and results on
K_{n}
.
In Section
4
, we state and prove the Zero Cancellation Theorem, and discuss some of its consequences.
In Section
5
, we analyse solutions to the equation
xa_{1}=f
, determine the cardinality of the solution set, give its description, and study its algebraic properties.
The paper concludes with Section
6
, in which we determine the parity of
|K_{n}|
.

3
Preliminaries

For a set
X
, we denote by

W(X)
the set of all finite words over
X
and we denote by
\mathbf{e}
the empty word.
The set
W(X)
equipped with the binary operation of
concatenation
becomes a monoid with unit element
\mathbf{e}
.
We denote
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
.
Define the
canonical epimorphism

\varphi:W(A)\to K_{n}
by

\varphi(\mathbf{a}_{i_{1}}\mathbf{a}_{i_{2}}\dots\mathbf{a}_{i_{k}})=a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\dots a_{i_{k}},

for all
k\geq 1
and
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, and
\varphi(\mathbf{e})=e
.
In this paper, elements of
W(A)
are written in boldface, for example
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{a}_{i}
, whereas elements of
K_{n}
are written in standard mathematical italic, for example
x,y,z,a_{i}
.

We recall the following lemma from
(
11
, Lemma 1)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The following statements hold.

(i)

Let
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}

and
\mathbf{w}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{i-1}\})
. Then we
have
a_{i}\varphi(\mathbf{w})a_{i}=a_{i}\varphi(\mathbf{w})
.

(ii)

Let
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and
\mathbf{w}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{i+1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})
. Then we have

a_{i}\varphi(\mathbf{w})a_{i}=\varphi(\mathbf{w})a_{i}
.

We define the
length
of a word as the map
l:W(A)\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
given in the natural way.
In
(
11
, Corollary 2)
, the authors proved that there is an upper
bound on the number of occurrences of the letters
\mathbf{a}_{i}
in words of shortest possible
length that represent the same element of
K_{n}
.
They later showed that these words correspond to
canonical words
(see Section
3.1
).

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
and let
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
be a word of shortest
possible length such that
x=\varphi(\mathbf{w})
. Then we have

(i)

For
i\leq\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
, the number of occurrences of
\mathbf{a}_{i}
in
\mathbf{w}
is less than or equal to
2^{i-1}
.

(ii)

For
i\geq\lceil\frac{n+1}{2}\rceil
, the number of occurrences of
\mathbf{a}_{i}
in
\mathbf{w}
is less than or equal to
2^{n-i}
.

3.1
Canonical Words

Let
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
be a word and write
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{a}_{i_{1}}\mathbf{a}_{i_{2}}\dots\mathbf{a}_{i_{k}}
. A word of the form

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{a}_{i_{j}}\mathbf{a}_{i_{j+1}}\dots\mathbf{a}_{i_{j+l}}
, where

j\geq 1
and
j+l\leq k
, is called a

subword
of
\mathbf{w}
.

Definition 3.3
.

We call a word
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)

canonical
over
A
, or simply

canonical
, if for every
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
such that
\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{u}\mathbf{a}_{i}
is a subword
of
\mathbf{w}
for some
\mathbf{u}\in W(A)
, there exist
j>i
and
k<i
such that
\mathbf{a}_{j}
and
\mathbf{a}_{k}
occur in
\mathbf{u}
.

Furthermore, for a word
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
, we define the set

\overline{\mathbf{w}}=\{\mathbf{v}\in W(A)\mid\varphi(\mathbf{v})=\varphi(\mathbf{w})\}=\varphi^{-1}(\{\varphi(\mathbf{w})\})
.

Observe that if
\mathbf{v}
is a subword of a canonical word, then

\mathbf{v}
is also canonical.
In
(
11
, Theorem 6)
, the following theorem was proved.

Theorem 3.4
.

The following statements hold.

(i)

For every
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
, the set
\overline{\mathbf{w}}
contains a unique element of minimal length.

(ii)

A word
\mathbf{v}\in\overline{\mathbf{w}}
is the unique element of minimal length if and only if
\mathbf{v}
is canonical over
A
.

For a word
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
, we call the unique word of minimal length in
\overline{\mathbf{w}}
the
canonical form
of
\mathbf{w}
and we denote it by
\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})
.
Since
\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})
is the word of minimal length such that
\varphi(\mathbf{w})=\varphi(\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w}))
, Proposition
3.2
may be applied to
\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})
for any
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
.

Definition 3.5
.

We define the binary relation
\to
on
W(A)
as follows. For
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in W(A)
, we write
\mathbf{w}\to\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
if and only if
there exist
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and words
\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2},\mathbf{s}\in W(A)
such that
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{s}\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{w}_{2}
and
one of the following holds:

1.

\mathbf{w}^{\prime}=\mathbf{w}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{s}\mathbf{w}_{2}
and
\mathbf{s}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{i-1}\})
,

2.

\mathbf{w}^{\prime}=\mathbf{w}_{1}\mathbf{s}\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{w}_{2}
and
\mathbf{s}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{i+1},\mathbf{a}_{i+2},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})
.

We say that
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
is obtained from
\mathbf{w}
by a

right deletion
if
\mathbf{w}\to\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
and the first condition in Definition
3.5
holds.
Similarly, we say that
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
is obtained from
\mathbf{w}
by a

left deletion
if
\mathbf{w}\to\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
and the second condition in Definition
3.5
holds.
Furthermore, applying Lemma
3.1
(
i
) corresponds to using a right deletion, and applying Lemma
3.1
(
ii
)
corresponds to using a left deletion.

For
k\geq 1
, define the binary relation
\overset{k}{\to}
as follows.
For words
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in W(A)
, we write
\mathbf{w}\overset{k}{\to}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
if and only if there exist words

\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k-1}\in W(A)
such that
\mathbf{w}\to\mathbf{v}_{1}\to\dots\to\mathbf{v}_{k-1}\to\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
.
We also define
\overset{0}{\to}
as
\mathbf{w}\overset{0}{\to}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
if and only if
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
.
Observe that the binary relations
\overset{1}{\to}
and
\to
coincide.
Denote by
\overset{*}{\to}=\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\overset{k}{\to}
the reflexive-transitive closure of
\to
on
W(A)
.

The following proposition collects basic facts about canonical forms and the binary relation
\to
, which are used throughout the paper without explicit reference.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
.
The following statements hold.

(i)

If
\varphi(\mathbf{v})=\varphi(\mathbf{w})
, then
\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{v})=\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})
.

(ii)

The word
\mathbf{w}
is canonical if and only if
\mathbf{w}=\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})
.

(iii)

If
\mathbf{v}\overset{*}{\to}\mathbf{w}
, then
\varphi(\mathbf{v})=\varphi(\mathbf{w})
.

(iv)

We have
\mathbf{w}\overset{*}{\to}\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})
.

Proof.

(
i
) If
\varphi(\mathbf{v})=\varphi(\mathbf{w})
, then
\overline{\mathbf{v}}=\overline{\mathbf{w}}
, and hence
\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{v})=\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})
.
(
ii
) Since
\mathbf{w}\in\overline{\mathbf{w}}
, this follows from Theorem
3.4
(
ii
).
(
iii
) If
\mathbf{v}\to\mathbf{w}
, then
\varphi(\mathbf{v})=\varphi(\mathbf{w})
by Lemma
3.1
. Hence, if
\mathbf{v}\overset{*}{\to}\mathbf{w}
, then
\varphi(\mathbf{v})=\varphi(\mathbf{w})
.
(
iv
) This follows from the proof of
(
11
, Theorem 6)
;
\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})
is the
normal form
of
\mathbf{w}
with respect to the binary relation
\to
on
W(A)
.
See
(
5
, Chapter 2.1)
for a general reference on term rewriting.
∎

For
x\in K_{n}
, we define its
canonical form
, denoted by
\zeta(x)
, to be
\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})
, where
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
satisfies
\varphi(\mathbf{w})=x
.
This is well defined, since Proposition
3.6
(
i
) implies that all such words
\mathbf{w}
have the same canonical form.
We then have
x=\varphi(\zeta(x))
for every
x\in K_{n}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
x,y\in K_{n}
.
If
\zeta(x)=\zeta(y)
, then
x=y
.

Proof.

Write
x=\varphi(\mathbf{w})
and
y=\varphi(\mathbf{v})
for some
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v}\in W(A)
.
Since
\zeta(x)=\zeta(y)
, we have
\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})=\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{v})
.
Then we have
\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})\in\overline{\mathbf{w}}
and
\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})=\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{v})\in\overline{\mathbf{v}}
.
This implies
\varphi(\mathbf{w})=\varphi(\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w}))=\varphi(\mathbf{v})
, which yields
x=y
.
∎

In
(
11
, Proposition 29)
, the following proposition was proved.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{a}_{3},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})
be canonical words.
Assume that
\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{v}
are both canonical.
If
\mathbf{u}\neq\mathbf{v}
,
then

\varphi(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{u})\neq\varphi(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{v})
.

This proposition implies that if

\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{a}_{3},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})
are canonical words such that

\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{v}
are both canonical, then the equality

\varphi(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{u})=\varphi(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{v})
implies that
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}
.

3.2
Idempotents

Let
X\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. If
X=\emptyset
, define
e_{X}=e
, the unit element in
K_{n}
, and
\mathbf{e}_{X}=\mathbf{e}
, the empty word.
Otherwise, write
X=\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}\}
with
i_{1}>i_{2}>\dots>i_{k}
and set

e_{X}=a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\dots a_{i_{k}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{e}_{X}=\mathbf{a}_{i_{1}}\mathbf{a}_{i_{2}}\dots\mathbf{a}_{i_{k}}.

Then we have
\varphi(\mathbf{e}_{X})=e_{X}
and the word
\mathbf{e}_{X}
is canonical for every
X\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.

In
(
11
, Remark 16)
, it was established that
e_{\{1,2,\dots,n\}}
is the zero
element of
K_{n}
. We denote

e_{\{1,2,\dots,n\}}
by
f
, and
\mathbf{e}_{\{1,2,\dots,n\}}
by
\mathbf{f}
.

Remark 3.9
.

For every
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
, we have
\mathbf{f}\mathbf{w}\overset{*}{\to}\mathbf{f}
.
Therefore, the word
\mathbf{f}\mathbf{w}
is canonical if and only if
\mathbf{w}
is the empty word.

Throughout the paper, we often use the trivial
equalities

e_{\{2,3,\dots,n\}}a_{1}=f\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{e}_{\{2,3,\dots,n\}}\mathbf{a}_{1}=\mathbf{f}.

In
(
11
, Proposition 11)
, the authors showed that
e_{X}
is an idempotent in

K_{n}
for every
X\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.
Furthermore, they showed that
e_{X}\neq e_{Y}
if
X\neq Y
,
and proved that
every idempotent in
K_{n}
is of the form
e_{X}
for some
X\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.
As a result,
K_{n}
contains exactly
2^{n}
idempotents.

Define the
content
map on
K_{n}
as
c:K_{n}\to\mathbbm{2}^{\{1,2,\dots,n\}}
, where
c(x)
is the set of
all
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
such that
\mathbf{a}_{i}
occurs
in the canonical form of
x
.
In
(
11
, Lemma 10)
, it was shown that the following holds.

Proposition 3.10
.

The map
c
is a semigroup epimorphism from
K_{n}
onto the semigroup
(\mathbbm{2}^{\{1,2,\dots,n\}},\cup)
.

In
(
11
, Proposition 20)
, the following was proved.

Proposition 3.11
.

The only automorphism of
K_{n}
is the identity. The map
a_{i}\mapsto a_{n-i+1}
extends uniquely to an
antiautomorphism of
K_{n}
. This is the only antiautomorphism of
K_{n}
.

We denote the unique antiautomorphism of
K_{n}
by
\tau
.

Remark 3.12
.

Observe that
\tau(f)=\tau(a_{n}a_{n-1}\dots a_{2}a_{1})=\tau(a_{1})\tau(a_{2})\dots\tau(a_{n-1})\tau(a_{n})=a_{n}a_{n-1}\dots a_{2}a_{1}=f
. Hence

\tau
fixes
f
.

4
Zero Cancellation Theorem

In this section, we prove the Zero Cancellation Theorem, which we now state.

Theorem 4.1

(Zero Cancellation Theorem)
.

Let
x,y\in K_{n}
satisfy
xy=f
.

(i)

If
c(y)\subseteq\{2,3,\dots,n\}
, then
x=f
.

(ii)

If
c(x)\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,n-1\}
, then
y=f
.

The proof proceeds as follows. We first recall the notion
of a quasi-subword. We then establish several technical results
concerning canonical forms, exploiting the key role of the letter
\mathbf{a}_{1}
, and
use these to prove the Zero Cancellation Theorem.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
be a word. Write
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{a}_{i_{1}}\mathbf{a}_{i_{2}}\dots\mathbf{a}_{i_{k}}
.
A word of the form
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{a}_{i_{j_{1}}}\mathbf{a}_{i_{j_{2}}}\dots\mathbf{a}_{i_{j_{l}}}
, where
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\dots<j_{l}\leq k
, is called a
quasi-subword
of
\mathbf{w}
.
In this case, we write
\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{w}
.

It follows immediately from the definition that if

\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{w}
, then

\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{w}
.
Moreover, we have
\mathbf{w}\leq\mathbf{w}
for every word
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For every
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
, we have
\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})\leq\mathbf{w}
.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{w}=\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})
, the statement follows from
\mathbf{w}\leq\mathbf{w}
.
Otherwise, there exist
k\geq 1
and words
\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k-1}\in W(A)
such that

\mathbf{w}\to\mathbf{v}_{1}\to\dots\to\mathbf{v}_{k-1}\to\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})
.
Since
\leq
is transitive, it suffices to show that for any

\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\in W(A)
, if

\mathbf{u}\to\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
, then
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\leq\mathbf{u}
.
This follows immediately from the definition of
\to
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Let
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
. If the letter
\mathbf{a}_{i}
occurs in
\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})
,
then
\mathbf{a}_{i}\leq\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w})\leq\mathbf{w}
, which implies
that
\mathbf{a}_{i}\leq\mathbf{w}
.
Therefore,
\mathbf{a}_{i}
occurs in
\mathbf{w}
.

The following lemma describes the canonical form of
words of the form
\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}
. The essence of this result is used implicitly
in the proof of
(
11
, Proposition 28)
. For the sake of
completeness, we state and prove it here.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{a}_{3},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})
and assume that
\mathbf{w}
is canonical.
Then

\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}^{*},

for some
\mathbf{u}^{*}\leq\mathbf{u}
.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}
is already canonical, the conclusion follows from
\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{u}
.
Otherwise, there exist
k\geq 1
and words

\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k-1}\in W(A)
such that

\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}\to\mathbf{v}_{1}\to\dots\to\mathbf{v}_{k-1}\to\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}).

Since
\leq
is transitive, it suffices to show that whenever

\mathbf{v}^{\prime}=\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
with
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\leq\mathbf{u}
and

\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\to\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}
, there exists
\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}\leq\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
such that
\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}=\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}
.

Assume
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}=\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
with
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\leq\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\to\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}
.
By the definition of
\to
, there exist
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and words

\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2},\mathbf{s}\in W(A)
such that

\mathbf{v}^{\prime}=\mathbf{w}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{s}\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{w}_{2}
and one of the following holds:

1.

\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}=\mathbf{w}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{s}\mathbf{w}_{2}
and
\mathbf{s}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{i-1}\})
,

2.

\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}=\mathbf{w}_{1}\mathbf{s}\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{w}_{2}
and
\mathbf{s}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{i+1},\mathbf{a}_{i+2},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})
.

Since
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\leq\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{u}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{a}_{3},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})
, the letter
\mathbf{a}_{1}
does not occur in
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
.
Since
\mathbf{a}_{1}
also does not occur in
\mathbf{w}
, it occurs exactly once in
\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}=\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
, and hence
i>1
.
Both occurrences of
\mathbf{a}_{i}
from the definition of
\to
cannot lie in
\mathbf{w}
, since this would produce the word

\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{s}\mathbf{a}_{i}
with
\mathbf{s}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{i-1}\})
or
\mathbf{s}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{i+1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})

as a subword of
\mathbf{w}
, which would contradict the assumption that
\mathbf{w}
is canonical.
Therefore, at least one of the two occurrences of
\mathbf{a}_{i}
from the definition of
\to
lies in
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
.

If both occurrences of
\mathbf{a}_{i}
from the definition of
\to
lie in
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
, the reduction
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\to\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}
deletes
one occurrence of
\mathbf{a}_{i}
from
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
, while the prefix
\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}
remains unchanged.
Let
\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}
be the word obtained from
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
by deleting this occurrence of
\mathbf{a}_{i}
.
Then we have
\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}=\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}
and
\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}\leq\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
.

If only one occurrence of
\mathbf{a}_{i}
from the definition of
\to
lies in
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
, then it must be the right occurrence, and the left occurrence of
\mathbf{a}_{i}
lies in
\mathbf{w}
, since
i>1
.
This implies that
\mathbf{a}_{1}
occurs in the subword
\mathbf{s}
.
Therefore, we cannot have
\mathbf{s}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{i+1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})
, and hence we are in the first case of the definition of
\to
.
Thus, in the reduction
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\to\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}
, we use a right deletion to delete the occurrence of
\mathbf{a}_{i}
in
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
,
while the prefix
\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}
remains unchanged.
Let
\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}
be the word obtained by deleting the mentioned occurrence of
\mathbf{a}_{i}
from
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
.
We then have
\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}=\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}
and
\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}\leq\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

The following theorem provides a key step in proving the main result of this section.
It asserts that if
\mathbf{w}
is canonical and contains
\mathbf{a}_{1}
, and
\mathbf{u}
does not contain
\mathbf{a}_{1}
, then the prefix preceding
\mathbf{a}_{1}
in
\mathbf{w}
coincides with the prefix
preceding
\mathbf{a}_{1}
in
\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{u})
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
be canonical and let
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{a}_{3},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})
. If we have

\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{v}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{v}_{2}
,
then
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{v}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
for some
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{a}_{3},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})
.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{v}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{v}_{2}
, Lemma
4.3
implies
\mathbf{v}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{v}_{2}\leq\mathbf{w}\mathbf{u}
.
Since
\mathbf{a}_{1}\leq\mathbf{v}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{v}_{2}\leq\mathbf{w}\mathbf{u}
, Remark
4.4
further implies that the letter
\mathbf{a}_{1}
occurs in
\mathbf{w}\mathbf{u}
.
As
\mathbf{u}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{a}_{3},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})
, the letter
\mathbf{a}_{1}
occurs at least once in
\mathbf{w}
.
Since
\mathbf{w}
is canonical, the letter
\mathbf{a}_{1}
occurs
exactly once in
\mathbf{w}
by Proposition
3.2
(
i
).

Since
\mathbf{a}_{1}
occurs exactly once in
\mathbf{w}
, we can write
\mathbf{w}
as

\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{w}_{2},

for some
\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})
. Hence, the word
\mathbf{w}\mathbf{u}
is of the form

\mathbf{w}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{w}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}(\mathbf{w}_{2}\mathbf{u})
. Since
\mathbf{w}_{1}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})

is canonical, as it is a subword of the canonical word
\mathbf{w}
, and since
\mathbf{w}_{2}\mathbf{u}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})
,
we can use Lemma
4.5
to conclude that
\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{w}_{2}\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{w}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}^{*}
for some
\mathbf{u}^{*}\leq\mathbf{w}_{2}\mathbf{u}
.
Thus, we have

\mathbf{w}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}^{*}=\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{v}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{v}_{2}.

Since
\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{1}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{a}_{3},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})
, we must have
\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{v}_{1}

and
\mathbf{u}^{*}=\mathbf{v}_{2}
. Hence,
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{v}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{w}_{2}
. Setting
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}=\mathbf{w}_{2}
yields
the desired conclusion.
∎

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
be canonical and let
\mathbf{u}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{a}_{3},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})

\mathbf{v}_{2}

∎

In this section, we study the equation

.

Proposition 5.1
.

The equation

admits only the trivial solution

if and only if

Proof.

By Theorem

(

is the unique solution to

.

Conversely, if
1\in c(y)
, Proposition
3.2
(
i
) implies that the canonical form of

is of the form

\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}
, where
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{a}_{3},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})

Setting
z=\varphi(\mathbf{w})
and
y^{\prime}=\varphi(\mathbf{u})

and

By Lemma
3.1
(

We focus on the case when

.

.
Hence, defining

The following lemma determines solutions to

Lemma 5.2
.

Let

.

x=e_{\{2,3,\dots,n\}}
.

Proof.

We write
x=\varphi(\mathbf{w})
,
where
\mathbf{w}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{a}_{3},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})
is the canonical form of
x
.
As

, we have
\varphi(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1})=xa_{1}=f=\varphi(\mathbf{f})
, and since
\mathbf{f}
is canonical, we obtain
\operatorname{can}(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1})=\mathbf{f}
.
Since
\mathbf{w}
is canonical and
\mathbf{a}_{1}
does not occur in
\mathbf{w}
, the word
\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}

Hence
\mathbf{w}\mathbf{a}_{1}=\mathbf{f}
.
It follows that
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{e}_{\{2,3,\dots,n\}}
, which implies

.

By Lemma
5.2
, the set
R
is equal to

R=\{e_{\{2,3,\dots,n\}}\}\cup T

(1)

and this union is disjoint.
We now turn to the study of the set

.
Observe that if

and
\mathbf{w}

, then by Proposition

(
i
), the letter

\mathbf{a}_{1}
occurs exactly once in

Definition 5.3

Define the map
\pi:K_{n}^{1}\to\langle a_{2},a_{3},\dots,a_{n}\rangle

For

, let

.

The map

and let

We have that
\pi(T)=\langle a_{2},a_{3},\dots,a_{n}\rangle

Let

In what follows, we interpret the set

Theorem 5.8

In Section

Theorem 6.1

Let

Observe that

We also define the map

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)

\mathbf{w}
is canonical if and only if

is canonical.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathbf{w}
is canonical.
Suppose there exists a subword of the form
\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{s}\mathbf{a}_{i}
in

Then

t^{-1}(\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{s}\mathbf{a}_{i})=t(\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{s}\mathbf{a}_{i})=\mathbf{a}_{n-i+1}t(\mathbf{s})\mathbf{a}_{n-i+1}

Since

is canonical, there exist

and

and

such that
\mathbf{a}_{k}
and
\mathbf{a}_{j}
occur in

.

occur in

.

is canonical.

is canonical, then applying the above argument to
