Genomic analysis of Tulasnella spp. associated with Cypripedium macranthos var. rebunense
Authors: Kota Kambara, Chunyi Jin, Kaien Fujino, Hanako Shimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrchid mycorrhizal (OM) fungi are essential for seed germination and seedling development in orchids. Although research on the molecular mechanisms underlying orchids-OM fungi interactions is progressing, detailed analyses, including fungal genomics, are limited. In this study, we performed short-read genome sequencing of two Tulasnella isolates obtained from the terrestrial orchid Cypripedium macranthos var. rebunense―both capable of inducing seed germination. Using sequencing data, we assembled genomes and predicted gene sets, each demonstrating over 95% completeness based on Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs analysis, indicating high-quality assemblies and annotations. Phylogenetic analysis incorporating existing Tulasnella genome data revealed that these isolates belonged to Group IV, an early-diverging lineage distant from the other Tulasnella groups. Comparative genomic analyses demonstrated that Group IV Tulasnella possessed a distinct repertoire of genes encoding carbohydrate-active enzymes and lineage-specific small secreted proteins. Our results indicate that these genes are possibly involved in establishing symbiosis with Cypripedium species. These findings offer novel insights into the molecular basis of orchid-mycorrhizal interactions.Keywords: comparative genomics, genome sequencing, mycorrhizal fungi, orchid-mycorrhizal interaction

1. Introduction

Orchids, the family with the largest number of monocotyledonous species, form unique symbiotic associations with mycorrhizal fungi. Their seeds are small and lack sufficient nutrient reserves for germination; therefore, in natural environments, they rely on carbon nutrition provided by mycorrhizal fungi until they become photoautotrophic (Leake, 1994; Rasmussen et al., 2015). Orchid mycorrhizal (OM) fungi form intracellular hyphal coils called pelotons that serve as interfaces for nutrient exchange (Perotto & Balestrini, 2024). Recent studies have clarified the molecular mechanisms underlying orchid-OM fungi interactions. For example, common symbiotic genes (CSGs) essential for arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) symbiosis are also upregulated during OM symbiosis (Miura et al., 2018). Additionally, metabolomic analysis has revealed significant alterations in both orchids and OM fungi during symbiosis (Ghirardo et al., 2020). Although commonalities with other mycorrhizal relationships are becoming apparent, the detailed mechanisms underlying mycorrhizal interactions between orchids and diverse fungal species remain largely unknown.

The first whole-genome sequencing of a mycorrhizal fungus―an ectomycorrhizal (ECM) species, Laccaria bicolor―was performed in 2008 (Martin et al., 2008). Since then, whole-genome sequencing and comparative genomic analyses of various mycorrhizal fungi have identified the primary genes essential for plant-fungal symbiosis on a genome-wide scale (Kohler et al., 2015; Miyauchi et al., 2020). Notably, genomic studies have revealed variations in the copy number repertoire of genes encoding carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) among fungal species, reflecting ecological adaptations to specific nutrient acquisition strategies and lifestyles (Floudas et al., 2020; Kobayashi et al., 2023; Kohler et al., 2015; Martin et al., 2024). Comparative genomic analyses have demonstrated that ECM fungi generally possess a reduced CAZyme-coding gene repertoire compared with that of saprotrophic or pathogenic fungi, indicating genomic adaptations for symbiosis-associated carbohydrate metabolism (Kohler et al., 2015). However, among mycorrhizal fungi, OM and ericoid mycorrhizal fungi retain broader CAZyme-coding gene repertoires than that of ECM fungi, indicating potential functional differences in organic matter degradation and nutrient acquisition strategies against host plant cells (Miyauchi et al., 2020). Additionally, genomic analyses have facilitated the identification of small secreted proteins (SSPs) in mycorrhizal fungi. Fungal SSPs play crucial roles in the virulence of pathogenic fungi and symbiosis of mycorrhizal fungi (Kohler et al., 2015; Lo Presti et al., 2015). Several functional SSPs―Rhizophagus irregularis small LysM domain-containing effector (RiSLM) (Zeng et al., 2020) and mycorrhizal-induced small-secreted protein 7 kDa (MiSSP7) in the ECM fungus L. bicolor―have been characterized in mycorrhizal fungal genomes (Plett et al., 2014). These findings highlight the general role of fungal SSPs as molecular signals governing plant-fungal interactions (Martin et al., 2008; Teulet et al., 2023); however, there have been no reports on the SSPs of OM fungi.

Fungal genome data have been used to analyze phylogenetic relationships, primarily based on nuclear ribosomal DNA (rDNA) internal transcribed spacer (ITS) sequences. However, fungal genomes typically contain multiple ITS copies, and intragenomic variations are detected in several fungal groups (Kovács et al., 2011; Nilsson et al., 2008; Simon & Weiss, 2008). In certain cases, these variations make it challenging to distinguish the ITS sequences of closely related species, and analysis based solely on ITS sequences has limitations in accurately classifying them (Kiss, 2012). To address these issues, concatenating multiple genes has been proposed and applied to enhance the resolution and accuracy of fungal phylogenetic analyses (Brown et al., 2001; Fitzpatrick et al., 2006; Robbertse et al., 2006; Rokas et al., 2003).

Molecular phylogenetic analysis is increasingly being used to classify OM fungi, and research into the relationship between ecological characteristics and taxonomy is also advancing. The genus Tulasnella is one of the representative OM fungal genera found in orchid mycorrhizal tissues, specifically in temperate terrestrial orchids. Even among strains classified in Tulasnella based on the morphological characteristics of their basidiomata, diversity exists in ITS sequences. Studies that combined basidiomata morphology with molecular phylogenetic approaches revealed the presence of numerous cryptic species (Cruz et al., 2014). Rammitsu et al. (2021) demonstrated that the 5.8S-ITS region of Tulasnella species is highly diverse and that the genus comprises multiple groups with distinct phylogenetic relationships. Furthermore, some Tulasnella species have a wide host range, regardless of their ecological location, while others primarily interact with orchids that grow in specific regions (Girlanda et al., 2011). Consequently, the genus Tulasnella represents a phylogenetically and ecologically complex taxonomic group; notwithstanding this complexity, comparative genomic studies remain limited. In our previous study, Rhizoctonia-like fungi were isolated from the mycorrhizal tissues of the endangered lady slipper orchid Cypripedium macranthos var. rebunense (Shimura et al., 2009). Phylogenetic analysis using rDNA and ITS sequences classified these fungi as the genus Tulasnella; however, they were distinct from taxonomically identified Tulasnella species (Shimura et al., 2009), indicating the presence of novel genetic lineages within this genus. In this study, we sequenced the whole genomes of two fungal strains isolated from C. macranthos var. rebunense using a short-read sequencing approach. Phylogenetic and comparative genome analyses were conducted using their genomic data, along with 13 publicly available Tulasnella isolates. Additionally, we identified genes specific to a subset of Tulasnella, including the two strains, and discussed their potential roles in OM interactions.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Fungal material

Two strains of Tulasnella sp. WO97 and FT061 were isolated from roots and protocorms of Cypripedium macranthos var. rebunense, respectively in a wild population on Rebun Island, Hokkaido, Japan (Shimura et al., 2009). Both isolates were confirmed to induce the in vitro germination of C. macranthos var. rebunense (Kambara et al., 2023; Shimura & Koda, 2005). For DNA or RNA extraction, an agar block of either WO97 or FT061 culture was placed on an oatmeal liquid medium (2 g/L fine oatmeal powder without agar), and the propagated hyphae were collected after 10 weeks of culture period. DNA was extracted from frozen hyphae using the QIAGEN DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (QIAGEN K.K., Tokyo, Japan) and subjected to genome sequencing. For RNA sequencing, total RNAs were extracted and treated with DNase according to the previously described protocol (Shimura et al., 2018).

2.2. DNA/RNA sequencing, assembly, and gene annotation

Library preparation using the TruSeq DNA PCR-free (Illumina) or TruSeq stranded mRNA (Illumina) for genome and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), respectively, and sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq platform were outsourced to Macrogen (Korea). Each library yielded 40-50 million 151 bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were pre-processed using fastp v0.23.1 (Chen et al., 2018).

The genome sizes of WO97 and FT061 were estimated from the cleaned short reads using GenomeScope 2.0 (Ranallo-Benavidez et al., 2020). The cleaned reads were assembled using Platanus v1.2.4 (Kajitani et al., 2014), and contigs less than 300 bp were excluded using SeqKit v2.4.0 (Shen et al., 2016). The completeness of the genome assembly was assessed with Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) v5.4.4 (Manni et al., 2021), using the “fungi_odb10” database (No. of BUSCO = 758). Repetitive elements were predicted using RepeatModeler v2.0.1 (Smit et al., 2013, https://www.repeatmasker.org) and soft-masked using RepeatMasker v4.1.1 (Smit et al., 2013, http://www.repatmasker.org). Genes were predicted from a soft-masked genome assembly using BRAKER3 v3.0.2 (Gabriel et al., 2024; Stanke et al., 2008), with fungal protein sets from OrthoDB v10.1 (Zdobnov et al., 2021) used as the extrinsic evidence.

The RNA-seq reads from the free-living mycelium of WO97 or FT061 were pre-processed with fastp and de novo assembled using Trinity v2.15.0 (Grabherr et al., 2011). Cleaned RNA-seq reads were mapped to the de novo transcriptome assembly using HISAT2 v2.2.1 (Kim et al., 2019), and only the mapped reads were extracted using SAMtools v1.6 (Danecek et al, 2021). Subsequently, they were mapped to the genome assembly using HISAT2 to predict genes with GeMoMa v1.9 (Keilwagen et al., 2018) and MMseqs2 v14.7 (Steinegger & Söding, 2017). For gene set prediction, Tulasnella calospora AL13/4D (Kohler et al., 2015) from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (Sayers et al., 2021) used as the reference. Coding sequence (CDS) was predicted from the de novo transcriptome assembly using TransDecoder v5.7.0 (https://github.com/TransDecoder/TransDecoder), and their CDS regions were predicted using Exonerate v2.72.4 (Slater & Birney, 2005). Three predicted gene sets obtained from BRAKER3, GeMoMa, and Exonerate were integrated using EVidenceModeler v2.0.0 (Haas et al., 2008) with weighting ratios of 1:2:5, respectively. The completeness of the predicted gene set was assessed using BUSCO with the “fungi_odb10” database (No. of BUSCO = 758). The predicted genes were annotated using EnTAP v1.0.1 (Hart et al., 2020) and amino acid sequences from the RefSeq fungi (O'Leary et al., 2016) and UniProt databases (The UniProt Consortium, 2023)―both downloaded on Apr 17, 2023. Gene Ontology (GO) terms were annotated using InterProScan v5.59 (Jones et al., 2014). The sequencing reads, genome assembly, and annotations were deposited in the NCBI database under the BioProject accession numbers PRJNA1335393 (WO97) and PRJNA1335395 (FT061).

2.3. Datasets of genome assemblies and phylogenetic analysis

Genome assemblies and predicted gene sets of Acaromyces ingoldii (Kijpornyongpan et al., 2018), Botryobasidium botryosum (Riley et al., 2014), Ceratobasidium sp. (Miyauchi et al., 2020), Filobasidium floriforme (Guerreiro et al., 2022), Hydnum rufescens (Miyauchi et al., 2020), Kwoniella newhampshirensis (Coelho et al., 2024), Kwoniella shandongensis (Coelho et al., 2024), Mixia osmundae (Toome et al., 2014), Pseudozyma flocculosa (Lefebvre et al., 2013), Rhizoctonia solani (Li et al., 2021), Sebacina vermifera (Kohler et al., 2015), Tremella mesenterica (Floudas et al., 2012), Tulasnella sp. 330, Tulasnella sp. 331, Tulasnella sp. 332, Tulasnella sp. 408, Tulasnella sp. 417, Tulasnella sp. 418, Tulasnella sp. 419, Tulasnella sp. 424, Tulasnella sp. 425, Tulasnella sp. 427, Tulasnella calospora UAMH9824 (Unruh et al., 2019), and Tulasnella calospora AL13/4D (Kohler et al., 2015) were downloaded from the NCBI database (Sayers et al., 2021). Accession numbers and other data regarding these genome assemblies are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Single-copy orthologs were identified using OrthoFinder v2.5.5 (Emms & Kelly, 2019). These orthologs were aligned using MAFFT v7.520 (Katoh & Standley, 2013), and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE v2.2.2.7 (Minh et al., 2020), with 1,000 bootstrap replicates and 1,000 SH-aLRT (Shimoda-Hasegawa approximate likelihood ratio test) replicates under the ‘Q.yeast + F + R6’ model.

2.4. Comparative analyses

CAZyme-coding genes were identified using the dbCAN3 web server (Zheng et al., 2023). Genes predicted to encode CAZymes by more than two methods were considered as CAZyme-coding genes. When counting the genes encoding each CAZyme, a single gene assigned to multiple CAZyme families was counted redundantly for each assignment. The Mann-Whitney U test was performed in R v4.2.3 (R Core Team 2023, https://www.R-project.org/) for statistical testing in the number of CAZyme-coding genes. Secretory proteins were predicted using SignalP v6.0 (Teufel et al., 2022). Genes predicted to encode secretory proteins with less than 300 amino acids were regarded as SSPs. Ortholog analyses were performed using a bidirectional best hit search with BLASTP v2.5.0 from the BLAST + suite (Camacho et al., 2009) with an e-value cutoff set to 1e-10. To compare the presence of nitrate assimilation genes in the Tulasnella genomes, orthologs of nitrilase, nitrate reductase, nitrate/nitrite transporter, and nitrite reductase genes from Ceratobasidium sp. and R. solani, and ammonium transporter genes (Tulasnella calospora ammonium transporter 1 [TcAMT1] and TcAMT2) of T. calospora AL13/4D (Fochi et al., 2017) were also searched.

2.5. Transcriptome analysis

To compare transcriptomes of mycelium in free-living and those in mycorrhizal interactions with an orchid (C. macranthos var. rebunense), RNA-seq data from WO97-inoculated C. macranthos var. rebunense roots (Jin et al., 2025) were downloaded from the NCBI database (accession number PRJNA1231755). The reads were pre-processed with fastp and mapped to the WO97 genome assembly using the HISAT2. Read counts were obtained using featureCounts v2.0.3 (Liao et al., 2014). For differential expression genes (DEG) analysis, RNA-seq data from the free-living WO97 mycelia of WO97 was used as the control. DEGs were identified using EdgeR v3.40.2 (Robinson et al., 2010) with a log2FC > 2 or < −2, and a false discovery rate of < 0.05. GO enrichment analysis was performed using GOATOOLS (Klopfenstein et al., 2018).

2.6. Analysis of chordin (CHRD) domain-containing protein genes

A BLASTP search was conducted on the BLASTP web server (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) to identify genes with high sequence similarities to TuCHRD using the RefSeq fungi database (e-value cutoff was set to 1e-10; accessed on Nov 18, 2024). The TuCHRD domain was predicted using the HMMER v3.4 web server (http://hmmer.org; accessed on Nov 15, 2024). A phylogenetic tree of fungal CHRD genes was constructed using MEGA 11 (Tamura et al., 2021) with the maximum-likelihood method and 1,000 bootstrap replicates.

3. Results

3.1. Genome sequencing and assembly of WO97 and FT061

We sequenced the genomes of two OM fungal strains belonging to Tulasnella sp., WO97 and FT061, isolated from Cypripedium macranthos var. rebunense (Shimura et al., 2009) using the Illumina short-read sequencer. Before assembly, the genome sizes were estimated using Genome Scope 2.0 at 38.5 and 34.0 Mb for WO97 and FT061, respectively. The short reads were assembled using Platanus, yielding 3,091 and 1,990 contigs for WO97 and FT061, respectively (Table 1). The completeness of these genome assemblies was assessed using BUSCO, revealing that 96.1 and 96.5% of complete BUSCOs were present in the WO97 and FT061 genome assemblies, respectively (Table 1). From these genome assemblies, 10,404 and 9,782 genes were predicted for WO97 and FT061, respectively (Table 1). Additionally, the completeness of the predicted genes was assessed using BUSCO, yielding high complete BUSCO values of 96.0 and 97.6% for WO97 and FT061, respectively. Moreover, most complete BUSCOs were present as single-copy genes, with 95.6 and 97.2% in WO97 and FT061, respectively (Table 1). These results indicate that our genome assemblies and predicted gene sets were highly complete with minimal redundancy.

3.2. Phylogenetic relationship and CAZyme-coding gene content of WO97 and FT061

In our previous study, WO97 and FT061 were classified as an unidentified species in the genus Tulasnella based on the rDNA-ITS sequence and ultrastructure of the septal pore (Shimura et al., 2009). Therefore, we downloaded the genomes of 13 available Tulasnella spp. isolates from the NCBI database for phylogenetic analysis. Phylogenetic analysis using 318 single-copy orthologs in Tulasnella assigned these 15 isolates to four groups (Fig. 1). FT061 formed a well-supported monophyletic clade with Tulasnella sp. 418 and Tulasnella sp. 419. WO97, however, was placed in a more basal position relative to this clade. These four taxa forming the basal lineage of Tulasnella were designated Group IV in this study. Supplementary Table S2 shows the correspondence between the 13 Tulasnella strains with genomic data used in Fig. 1 and the Tulasnella strains shown in previously reported rDNA-ITS-based phylogenetic trees (Rammitsu et al., 2021; Novotná et al., 2023). Tulasnella strains showing homology with those included in Group IV in Fig. 1 were also positioned in the early-branching group in the phylogenetic tree presented by Rammitsu et al. (2021) and Novotná et al. (2023).

The copy number repertoire of CAZyme-coding genes in fungi often exhibits unique patterns associated with specific ecological adaptations (Floudas et al., 2012, 2020; Kobayashi et al., 2023; Kohler et al., 2015; Martin et al., 2024). Therefore, we compared the copy number repertoire of CAZyme-coding genes in the Tulasnella isolates. The total number and composition of CAZyme-coding genes varied across isolates (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S3). Principal component analysis (PCA) with the copy numbers of CAZyme-coding genes explained variance―PC1 (46.7%) and PC2 (24.6%), demonstrating that Group IV Tulasnella clustered separately from the other groups (Fig. 2A). Comparison of copy numbers of individual CAZyme revealed that for 26 CAZymes, the copy numbers differed significantly between Group IV and other groups (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Fig. S1). Specifically, Group IV Tulasnella had more copies of enzymes crucial for plant lignocellulose degradation categorized in AA1 (multicopper oxidases), AA9 (lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases; LPMOs) and AA16 (LPMOs) (Fig. 2B). Group IV Tulasnella also harbored more copies of GH25 (lysozymes) than the other Tulasnella groups (Fig. 2B). In contrast, the copy number of genes coding for GH28 (polygalacturonase), whose expression was induced during mycorrhizal symbiosis in L. bicolor (Zhang et al., 2022), was significantly lower in Group IV Tulasnella. These findings indicate that Group IV Tulasnella differs from other Tulasnella groups in terms of CAZyme-coding gene copy number repertoires.

3.3. Genes for nitrate assimilation and SSP

Ceratobasidium species use both ammonium and nitrate as nitrogen sources, whereas Tulasnella species use only ammonium (Nurfadilah et al., 2013). Additionally, most genes involved in nitrate assimilation were absent in the T. calospora AL13/4D genome, whereas they were present in the Ceratobasidium genome (Fochi et al., 2017). Because T. calospora and WO97/FT061 were distantly related within the genus Tulasnella based on their genome sequences (Fig. 1), we assessed whether genes involved in nitrate assimilation were absent in WO97 and FT061, similar to T. calospora AL13/4D. Our analysis revealed that genes encoding nitrate reductase, nitrate/nitrite transporter, and nitrite reductase were absent in WO97, FT061, and other Tulasnella isolates examined (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Table S4). In contrast, these genes were detected in Ceratobasidium sp. and S. vermifera. Orthologs of functional ammonium transporters (TcAMT1 and TcAMT2) in T. calospora AL13/4D (Fochi et al., 2017) were present in all Tulasnella isolates examined (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Table S4). These findings indicate that the absence of nitrate assimilation genes is a common characteristic of Tulasnella, suggesting that Tulasnella species primarily use ammonium as the nitrogen source.

To compare the differences in SSP-coding genes between Tulasnella isolates, we identified genes annotated as SSPs in the WO97 and FT061 assemblies. In WO97, 361 SSP-coding genes were identified, of which 48, 60, and 35 were conserved in T. calospora AL13/4D, Ceratobasidium sp., and S. vermifera, respectively (Fig. 3B). In FT061, 268 SSP-coding genes were identified, of which 72 (26.9%) were shared with WO97 (Fig. 3B). Additionally, 47 SSP-coding genes were shared within Group IV Tulasnella, including WO97 and FT061, four of which were specific to Group IV in the genus Tulasnella (Supplementary Table S5). These results indicate that SSPs were diverse in each isolate, with at most 20% shared within Group IV―as observed between WO97 and FT061―and that Group IV Tulasnella possesses specific SSPs absent in other OM fungi.

3.4. Expression patterns of WO97 genes in mycorrhizal interaction with Cypripedium

To assess fungal gene expression specific to mycorrhizal interactions, transcriptome data from WO97 free-living mycelium and WO97-inoculated C. macranthos var. rebunense mycorrhizal roots were compared. In the mycorrhizal tissues at two weeks post inoculation (2 wpi), 812 and 397 genes were upregulated and downregulated, respectively, compared to the free-living mycelium, whereas 949 and 462 genes were upregulated and downregulated at 4 wpi, respectively (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table S6). Among the 1718 DEGs, 638 genes were upregulated at both 2 and 4 wpi, whereas 264 genes were downregulated (Fig. 4A). In the GO enrichment analysis of upregulated DEGs, GO terms related to “oxidoreductase activity” and “steroid metabolic process” were detected at 2 wpi, whereas “transporter activity” were detected at 4 wpi (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Among the 456 CAZyme-coding genes in WO97, 95 were detected as DEGs under mycorrhizal interaction than that of free-living mycelium, with 54 and 41 genes upregulated and downregulated at 2 or 4 wpi, respectively (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Table S6). Among the 361 genes coding for SSPs in WO97, 127 genes were detected as DEGs, with 86 and 41 genes upregulated and downregulated at 2 or 4 wpi, respectively (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Table S6). Based on their expression patterns, the 127 SSPs were classified into six groups: SSP group 1 (SSP1) containing 57 genes upregulated at both 2 and 4 wpi, SSP group 2 (SSP2) containing 19 genes upregulated only at 4 wpi, SSP group 3 (SSP3) containing 10 genes upregulated only at 2 wpi, SSP group 4 (SSP4) containing 16 genes downregulated at both 2 and 4 wpi, SSP group 5 (SSP5) containing 16 genes downregulated only at 4 wpi, and SSP group 6 (SSP6) containing nine genes downregulated only at 2 wpi (Fig. 4B). Among the six groups, SSP1 had the largest number of SSPs. In the upregulated SSP genes, there are few informative functional annotations for genes assigned to SSP3, (Supplementary Table S6), which makes their biological roles difficult to infer at present. Within SSP1 and SSP2, genes encoding common in fungal extracellular membrane (CFEM) domain-containing proteins were identified (WO_scaffold1651_cov158.19, WO_scaffold667_cov145.27, WO_scaffold433_cov159.98, WO_scaffold127_cov154.43, and WO_scaffold127_cov154.44; Supplementary Table S6). In previous studies, CFEM domain-containing proteins were characterized as effectors in pathogenic fungi, including Melampsora lini, Magnaporthe grisea, and Colletotrichum fructicola (Catanzariti et al., 2006; Kulkarni et al., 2003; Shang et al., 2024). Chitin deacetylase, an effector of soil-borne fungi, suppresses plant immune responses (Gao et al., 2019). Three chitin deacetylase genes were identified in SSP1 (WO_scaffold1651_cov158.24, WO_scaffold637_cov158.10, and WO_scaffold1651_cov158.16) and one in SSP2 (WO_scaffold583_cov159.80), although one chitin deacetylase gene was identified in SSP5 (WO_scaffold1651_cov158.17; Supplementary Table S6). Additionally, genes encoding LysM domain-containing proteins were identified in SSP1 (WO_scaffold153_cov158.39; Supplementary Table S6) and are known to function as fungal effectors in R. irregularis (Zeng et al., 2020). These results suggest that functional effectors from pathogenic or mycorrhizal fungi are involved in the mycorrhizal interactions between WO97 and C. macranthos var. rebunense.

3.5. SSPs specific to Group IV Tulasnella

Among the four SSPs specific to Group IV Tulasnella, two SSP-coding genes were identified as DEGs―WO_scaffold127_cov154.94 and WO_scaffold1539_cov150.31 (Supplementary Tables S5 and S6): WO_scaffold127_cov154.94 was classified in SSP1, and WO_scaffold1539_cov150.31 was classified in SSP6. WO_scaffold1539_cov150.31 was annotated as peptidoglycan-binding domain-containing protein. However, its role in mycorrhizal interactions remains unclear. WO_scaffold127_cov154.94 was annotated as CHRD domain-containing protein-coding gene that encodes a 229-amino acid protein predicted to contain a CHRD domain and signal peptides (Supplementary Fig. S3). In Fusarium oxysporum, a CHRD domain-containing protein was identified as a candidate for a secreted effector (Lou et al., 2024; Sperschneider et al., 2013). The expression of WO_scaffold127_cov154.94 (referred to as TuCHRD) was upregulated at both 2 and 4 wpi than that in free-living mycelium (Supplementary Table S6), suggesting that TuCHRD functions as a secreted effector involved in the mycorrhizal interaction with C. macranthos var. rebunense.

Phylogenetic analysis of 33 mycorrhizal or pathogenic fungi with 302 single-copy orthologs revealed that genes with high sequence similarity to TuCHRD were absent in other OM fungi, such as Ceratobasidium sp. and S. vermifera (Fig. 5A). Similarly, no homologous genes were present in ECM fungi, such as Suilus luteus and Laccaria bicolor, or in AM fungi, such as R. irregularis and R. clarus (Fig. 5A). To further explore TuCHRD homologs, a BLASTP search was performed on basidiomycete genes (taxon ID = 5204) registered in RefSeq Fungi, identifying 18 organisms with a high sequence similarity to TuCHRD (Supplementary Table S7). A similar analysis using ascomycete genes (taxon ID = 4890) revealed 150 organisms with homologous genes, including certain pathogenic fungi, such as F. oxysporum (Supplementary Table S8). Phylogenetic analysis of CHRD domain-containing protein genes demonstrated that Group IV Tulasnella genes formed a distinct cluster, separate from other fungi (Fig. 5B). These results indicate that CHRD domain-containing protein genes conserved in Group IV Tulasnella are present in other basidiomycetes and ascomycetes, including plant-pathogenic fungi, but are absent in other mycorrhizal fungi, including OM fungi.

4. Discussion

Genome analysis of two Tulasnella isolates (WO97 and FT061), the OM fungi of Cypripedium macranthos var. rebunense, revealed that these fungi belong to Tulasnella Group IV, an early-diverging group (Fig. 1). Within Group IV, FT061, Tulasnella sp. 418, and Tulasnella sp. 419 formed a well-supported monophyletic clade, while WO97 was placed more basally. Tulasnella sp. 418 was isolated from Platanthera leucophaea, and Tulasnella sp. 419 was isolated from Cypripedium candidum (Unruh et al., 2019). Of the 15 Tulasnella isolates assessed, only WO97, FT061, and Tulasnella sp. 419 were isolated from Cypripedium, belonging to Group IV. Phylogenetic analysis of the 5.8S-ITS region of Tulasnella operational taxonomic units (OTU) demonstrated that five out of six isolates from Cypripedium clustered together within an early-diverging clade, except for one isolate from C. californicum (Rammitsu et al., 2021). Another phylogenetic study using the 5.8S-ITS region of fungal isolates from three Cypripedium species and three Paphiopedilum species in the subfamily Cypripedioideae revealed that most fungal isolates from Cypripedium belonged to early-diverging clades within the genus Tulasnella, whereas isolates from Paphiopedilum primarily belonged to late-diverging clades (Yuan et al., 2010). The specificity of mycorrhizal fungal partners in Cypripedium is narrow within the Tulasnellaceae, although exceptions, such as C. californicum and C. acaule exhibit broader specificity (Shefferson et al., 2007). These findings indicate that Cypripedium specifically interacts with the early-diverging group in Tulasnella. Notably, within the Cypripedioideae subfamily, Cypripedium is unique because it has expanded into temperate regions, whereas species in other genera, such as Paphiopedilum, are restricted to tropical regions (Guo et al., 2012). In the genetic differentiation within the species C. macranthos, adaptation to cold environments is considered the primary environmental factor; in particular, C. macranthos var. rebunense has been reported as a typical example of founder speciation derived from a coastal population in the Russian Far East (Chang et al., 2024). Therefore, it would be interesting to consider the possibility that the adaptation and expansion of Cypripedium to temperate regions and/or cold environments is related to its specific mycorrhizal relationship with Tulasnella Group IV. Further assessment, including the examination of whether Group IV Tulasnella species are more adaptable to low temperatures than that of other Tulasnella species, is needed to confirm this.

The copy number repertoire of CAZyme-coding genes differed significantly between Group IV and other groups in Tulasnella (Fig. 2A). Specifically, 26 CAZyme-coding genes demonstrated variations in copy numbers between these groups (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Fig. S1). For example, Group IV Tulasnella possessed only a single copy of the GH28-coding gene, whereas most of the other Tulasnella isolates had multiple copies, including seven copies in T. calospora AL13/4D and 16 copies in Tulasnella sp. 330 and 331 (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Table S3). Our transcriptome analysis of WO97 revealed that the expression level of the gene in GH28 (WO_scaffold442_cov153.7), encoding polygalacturonases, did not differ in 2 wpi and 4 wpi compared to the free-living mycelium (Supplementary Table S6). In L. bicolor, the expression of Laccaria bicolor glycoside hydrolase family 28 enzyme A (LbGH28A) is induced early during symbiosis (Veneault-Fourrey et al., 2014), and LbGH28A accumulates on the surface of both fungal and plant cell walls to facilitate hyphal root penetration and hartig net formation through pectin degradation (Zhang et al., 2022). These results raise the possibility that polygalacturonase (GH28) plays a crucial role in Tulasnella species possessing multiple gene copies and ECM fungi; however, it is not involved in mycorrhizal interactions in Group IV Tulasnella. In contrast, the GH25-coding genes were expanded in Group IV Tulasnella compared with the other groups (Fig. 2B). Although the role of GH25 in OM interaction remains unclear, the expansion of this family, together with the observation that a GH25-coding gene (WO_scaffold583_cov159.91) was included in SSP1 (Fig. 4) and its expression was upregulated during colonization in orchid tissues (Supplementary Table S6), suggests that the GH25 expansion in Group IV Tulasnella reflect lineage-specific ecological adaptation rather than simple functional redundancy. Additionally, the copy numbers of AA1 (multicopper oxidases), AA9 (LPMOs) and AA16 (LPMOs) were higher in group IV Tulasnella than that in other groups (Fig. 2B). Seven genes in AA9 were upregulated at 2 and 4 wpi than that of the free-living mycelium (WO_scaffold2231_cov166.39, WO_scaffold807_cov121.2, WO_scaffold911_cov148.26, WO_scaffold1708_cov160.6, WO_scaffold3396_cov119.5, WO_scaffold946_cov123.12, and WO_scaffold633_cov149.30; Supplementary Table S6). AA1, AA9, and AA16 are crucial for lignocellulose degradation in the plant cell walls (Lebreton et al., 2021; Miyauchi et al., 2020). Because saprotrophic fungi generally have more LPMO-encoding genes than that of mycorrhizal fungi (Kobayashi et al., 2023; Kohler et al., 2015), it is conceivable that Tulasnella in Group IV also possesses greater saprophytic ability than other Tulasnella groups. However, Novotná et al. (2023) found that Tulasnella strains belonging to the early-branching clade in their phylogenetic tree grew more slowly and had a lower capacity for carbohydrate utilization than strains in other clades. Therefore, the observed differences in the copy number and expression patterns of CAZyme genes may reflect distinct saprophytic strategies or substrate utilization patterns, rather than an inherently high saprophytic capacity.

On the other hand, some strains in Groups I-III were found to possess CAZyme genes with copy numbers comparable to those of strains in Group IV. Thus, it should be noted that the CAZyme gene repertoire exhibits diversity even within phylogenetically related groups, and further analysis is needed to understand the relationship between the CAZyme gene repertoire, phylogenetic classification, and physiological characteristics.

Fungal SSPs are essential for the interaction with plants. We predicted SSP-coding genes in Tulasnella isolates and revealed that the expression levels of 127 SSP-coding genes identified in WO97 were significantly altered during mycorrhizal interactions compared to those in free-living mycelium (Supplementary Table S6). Chitin, a major structural component of fungal cell walls, functions as an elicitor in plant immune responses. Plants secrete chitinases to hydrolyze fungal cell wall components, releasing chitin oligomers (Sánchez-Vallet et al., 2015) that trigger plant immune responses to defend against fungal infections (Kombrink et al., 2011). In contrast, fungal chitin deacetylases deacetylate chitin oligomers to produce chitosan―that is ligand-inactive and therefore fails to elicit plant immune responses (Sánchez-Vallet et al., 2015). Soil-borne fungi use chitin deacetylases to prevent the activation of chitin-triggered plant immunity (Gao et al., 2019). In a metabolomic analysis involving T. calospora AL13/4D and S. vomeracea, short chitosan oligomers were enriched in the symbiotic protocorm of S. vomeracea than that in the free-living mycelium of T. calospora AL13/4D, and the expression of chitin deacetylase genes in T. calospora AL13/4D was upregulated in the symbiotic protocorms (Ghirardo et al., 2020). Similarly, in WO97, the expression of four chitin deacetylase genes (WO_scaffold583_cov159.80 [SSP2], WO_scaffold1651_cov158.16 [SSP1], WO_scaffold1651_cov158.24 [SSP1], and WO_scaffold2372_cov128.12 [SSP1]) was upregulated during mycorrhizal interactions (Supplementary Table S6). These findings suggest that chitin deacetylases in T. calospora AL13/4D and WO97 play a regulatory role to suppress chitin-triggered immunity in orchid tissues. In the AM fungus R. irregularis, a representative fungal SSP, the LysM domain-containing effector, RiSLM, suppresses chitin-triggered immunity in host plants (Zeng et al., 2020). Consistently, in WO97, a gene encoding a LysM domain-containing protein (WO_scaffold153_cov158.39, SSP1) was upregulated in the mycorrhizal tissues (Supplementary Table S6). This suggests that the suppression of chitin-triggered immunity is essential for establishing mycorrhizal interactions and is modulated by fungal chitin deacetylases or LysM domain-containing proteins in the Cypripedium-WO97 interaction.

Additionally, we identified four SSP-coding genes unique to Group IV Tulasnella. Specifically, the expression of a gene encoding CHRD domain-containing protein (WO_scaffold127_cov154.94, SSP1) was upregulated in mycorrhizal tissues than that in free-living mycelium (Supplementary Table S6). The putative homolog of TuCHRD in the plant pathogenic fungus Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. lycopersici was predicted as a candidate for secreted effector (Sperschneider et al., 2013). In the plant pathogen F. oxysporum f. sp. niveum, FonCHRD―a putative homolog of TuCHRD―contributes to virulence and suppresses plant immune responses (Lou et al., 2024). These results indicated that CHRD domain-containing protein functions as a secreted effector that suppress plant immune responses. Because TuCHRD expression was upregulated during mycorrhizal interactions (Supplementary Table S6), TuCHRD likely plays a crucial role in the Cypripedium-WO97 interaction. Unexpectedly, genes with high sequence similarity to TuCHRD were absent in other mycorrhizal fungi examined, such as OM fungi Ceratobasidium and Sebacina, ECM fungi L. bicolor, and AM fungi R. irregularis (Fig. 5A). In contrast, TuCHRD homologs were identified in evolutionarily distant fungi, including pathogenic fungi in ascomycetes (Fig. 5B). The CHRD domain-containing protein may be a key factor in establishing the mycorrhizal interaction between Group IV Tulasnella and Cypripedium. However, knowledge of fungal CHRD domain-containing protein is limited, and further analysis is required to understand its functions in the mycorrhizal relationship.

In conclusion, our phylogenetic analysis using genomic data demonstrated that Group IV Tulasnella, including WO97 and FT061, is phylogenetically distant from other Tulasnella groups by early divergence. Comparative genome analysis revealed differences in the copy number repertoire of CAZyme-coding genes between Group IV Tulasnella and other groups. Additionally, we identified an effector candidate that was specifically present in Group IV Tulasnella. These findings offer insights into Tulasnella diversity and the molecular mechanisms underlying orchid-OM fungal symbiosis. It has been reported that there are significant differences in mycelial production and nutrient utilization capacity between Tulasnella species in the early-diverging group and those in the late-diverging group (Novotná et al., 2023), but the cause of these differences remains unclear. In addition to focusing on the CAZyme gene, a detailed comparative analysis of genomic data and transcriptomes between early-diverging groups and other groups is likely to shed light on the diverse ecological characteristics of Tulasnella species.
