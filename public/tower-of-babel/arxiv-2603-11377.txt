Highly homogeneous and isotropic universes: quasi-dust models and the apparent dark-energy evolution arising from the local gravitational potential
Leandro G. Gomes

Abstract
In this manuscript, we develop a class of inhomogeneous relativistic cosmological models with the following properties: (i) They contain cosmological observers to whom the spatial geometry and the expansion are homogeneous and isotropic; (ii) Matter behaves closely to dust, as it is formed by an ensemble of massive particles whose number density 4 4 -vector is conserved and reacts viscously to the local tidal forces; (iii) They generalize the dust FLRW model; (iv) They give rise to effective models on large scales that reproduce the FLRW behaviour with dust and a running dark-energy term, which appears as a backreaction effect from the local gravitational potential; (v) By suitably setting the distribution of energy in the current universe, the effective large-scale equation-of-state parameter can reproduce, in principle, any polynomial on the scale factor whose term of order zero is negative; (vi) The luminous distance observations imply an apparent large-scale acceleration, as the deceleration parameter is negative, while in reality the universe is decelerating.

III
Quasi-dust and the tidal viscosity

We shall deal with the description of matter content in the
\mathrm{u}
-observers point of view. Our approach will follow closely the phenomenological aspects of the theory of transport phenomena, where the coefficients, such as viscosity or thermal and electrical conductivity, are introduced by hand under suitable physical justifications, rather than derived by statistical arguments. Hence, we assume matter to be a self-gravitating ensemble of particles whose energy density is composed of the “rest” mass and the internal counterparts, as

\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\rho$}=m_{0}\,\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathsf{n}$}+\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{1.0}[1.4]{$\varepsilon$}}\,,

(7)

where
\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathsf{n}$}(t,x)
is the number density of particles. The number-flux
4
-vector
\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathsf{n}$}^{\mu}=\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathsf{n}$}\,\mathrm{u}^{\mu}+\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\hat{\mathsf{n}}$}^{\mu}
, the last term the particle flux density

\hat{\mathsf{n}}

orthogonal to
\mathrm{u}
, is conserved, that is,

\gradient_{\mu}\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathsf{n}$}^{\mu}=0\,.

(8)

The energy flows along with the particles, that is, with
m_{0}\,\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathsf{n}$}^{\mu}
. Hence, the energy flux density satisfies the law

q^{i}=m_{0}\,\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\hat{\mathsf{n}}$}^{i}\,.

(9)

Concerning the stress tensor
T^{i}_{j}
, our fluid displays two distinct parts. The first, isotropic, corresponds to a global thermal equilibrium, which is quite often given by a linear barotropic equation of state
\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathsf{P}$}_{T}=\mathrm{w}\,\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\rho$}
. We shall assume it negligible,
\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathsf{P}$}_{T}\approx 0
. In this sense, our model is a generalisation of the FLRW’s dust solution, where
\mathrm{w}=0
.

The second component of the stress corresponds to the reaction to the local gravitational forces acting on the characteristic matter element, which might cause compression, stretching, or shearing. As it is so long considered in physics, like in elasticity with the elastic modulus tensor or in fluid dynamics with the bulk and shear viscosities
[
45
]
, in cosmological models with imperfect fluids it is often assumed that matter responds linearly to the expansion, an effect usually interpreted as the viscosity in fluid dynamics, with bulk
-3\,\zeta\,\mathrm{H}
and shear
-\lambda\,\sigma_{\mu\nu}
components (Sec. 5.2 in Ref.
[
26
]
). However, this approach does not take into account the effects of local gravitational tides, which impinges the same kind of effect into matter. Hence, we should expect an analogous response against these local forces, that is, the total stress should contain the tidal counterpart composed by the pressure

\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathsf{P}$}=-\,\zeta_{B}\,\scalebox{0.8}[0.8]{$\mathfrak{M}$}=-\,\frac{\zeta_{B}}{3\,\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathrm{a}$}^{2}}\,\left(\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{1.2}[1.0]{${\mathrm{D}}$}}^{2}\scalebox{1}[0.8]{$\phi$}+|\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{1.2}[1.0]{${\mathrm{D}}$}}\scalebox{1}[0.8]{$\phi$}|^{2}\right)\,,

(10)

where
\zeta_{B}
is the tidal bulk viscosity, and the anisotropic stress

\pi_{\mu\nu}=-\,\zeta_{A}\,\scalebox{0.8}[0.8]{$\widehat{\mathfrak{M}}$}_{\mu\nu}\,,

(11)

with
\zeta_{A}
the tidal anisotropic viscosity, both dimensionless and non-negative constants.

In this manuscript, we neglect thermodynamic effects, such as heat conduction, for the sake of simplicity. A more detailed analysis shall be presented elsewhere. Hence, the final form of the total energy-momentum tensor entering into Einstein’s equations will be

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{1.0}[1.4]{$\varepsilon$}}\,\mathrm{u}_{\mu}\mathrm{u}_{\nu}+m_{0}\,\left(\mathrm{u}_{\mu}\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathsf{n}$}_{\nu}+\mathrm{u}_{\nu}\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathsf{n}$}_{\mu}\right)

(12)

\displaystyle-\,\zeta_{B}\,\scalebox{0.8}[0.8]{$\mathfrak{M}$}\,\left(\mathrm{g}_{\mu\nu}+\mathrm{u}_{\mu}\mathrm{u}_{\nu}\right)-\,\zeta_{A}\,\scalebox{0.8}[0.8]{$\widehat{\mathfrak{M}}$}_{\mu\nu}\,.

V
Solutions which are homogeneous and isotropic on large scales

V.1
The periodic boundary conditions

The concept of large-scale homogeneity and isotropy of the universe follows from the intuitive idea of tiling space into congruent parts of characteristic length
L_{0}
and demanding periodic boundary conditions to Einstein’s equations
[
7
,
29
,
28
]
, so that it is homogeneous and isotropic as we look at it on scales much larger than

. We assume that all the geometric entities and their time derivatives are invariant by a free and properly discontinuous action on
\mathsf{S}
of a discrete group
\Gamma
of
\mathfrak{g}
-isometries whose quotient
\mathcal{K}=\mathsf{S}/\Gamma
is a compact manifold of constant curvature
K
and volume
L_{0}^{3}

[
52
]
. Here,
I\times\mathcal{K}
is called the cosmological cell and is naturally identified with the region in the spacetime
I\times\mathsf{S}
it represents. In this context, our spacetime satisfies a weaker version of the cosmological principle as compared to its usual implementation through the FLRW framework
[
47
]
. A natural large-scale model emerges after taking spatial averages of the
\Gamma
-periodic scalars of the theory
[
29
,
28

VI
The large-scale FLRW model and the apparent dark-energy component

VI.1
The large-scale FLRW model

We now turn to the fitting problem, that is, decide which FLRW model will represent the large-scale dynamics. Since our background is closely related to it, this is accomplished by two suitable gauge choices, namely, deciding which discrete subgroup of symmetries
\Gamma
of
\mathfrak{g}

[
52
]
fits better to represent the cosmological cell
\mathcal{K}=\mathsf{S}/\Gamma
and the homogeneous scale
L_{0}
, and picking the gauge for the potential

\phi

, and therefore fixing the global time
t
of the universe. We shall leave the former to be decided by observations, while for the fitting of the cosmological time, we choose the FLRW gauge, defined as

\langle e^{-2\scalebox{1}[0.8]{$\phi$}}\rangle=1\,,

(49)

that is, the cosmic time
t
is chosen such that

\left(\frac{\dot{\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathrm{a}$}}}{\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathrm{a}$}}\right)^{2}=\langle\mathrm{H}^{2}\rangle\,.

(50)

Here, we have recovered the “dot” notation for the
t
-time derivative,
\dot{f}=df/dt
and set
t_{0}
for our current era, that is,
\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathrm{a}$}(0)=0
and
\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathrm{a}$}(t_{0})=1
.

The large-scale expression for the total energy is given by its mean value
\langle\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\rho$}\rangle

\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\rho$}_{c}=3\,\langle\mathrm{H}_{0}^{2}\rangle=3\,\left(\frac{\dot{\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathrm{a}$}}}{\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathrm{a}$}}\right)^{2}_{t=t_{0}}

(51)

as our reference scale, where
\mathrm{H}_{0}(x)=\mathrm{H}(t_{0},x)
. We introduce the cosmological parameter for the matter content

\Omega_{m}=\frac{N\,m_{0}}{\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\rho$}_{c}\,L_{0}^{3}}\,,

(52)

just as in the standard model of Cosmology
[
49
]
.

In the FLRW gauge, the large-scale dynamics emulates an FLRW universe with energy density given by the averaged value of (
7
), that is,

\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\rho$}_{L}(t)=\langle\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\rho$}\rangle=\left(\frac{\Omega_{m}}{\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathrm{a}$}(t)^{3}}+\Omega_{L}(t)\right)\,\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\rho$}_{c}\,,

(53)

where, from (
43
) and (
44
),

\Omega_{L}(t)=\frac{\langle\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{1.0}[1.4]{$\varepsilon$}}\rangle(t)}{\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\rho$}_{c}}=\frac{(2+\zeta_{B})}{\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\rho$}_{c}\,\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathrm{a}$}(t)^{3}}\,\int_{0}^{\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathrm{a}$}(t)}\langle|\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{1.2}[1.0]{${\mathrm{D}}$}}\scalebox{1}[0.8]{$\phi$}|^{2}\rangle(\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathrm{a}$})\,d\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathrm{a}$}

(54)

\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathsf{P}$}_{L}(t)=-\,\frac{(2+\zeta_{B})}{3\,\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathrm{a}$}(t)^{2}}\,\langle|\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{1.2}[1.0]{${\mathrm{D}}$}}\scalebox{1}[0.8]{$\phi$}|^{2}\rangle(t)\,,

(55)

which is negative except for the FLRW case, where it vanishes. Note that
\langle\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathsf{P}$}\rangle\neq\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathsf{P}$}_{L}

(56)

Note that divergent terms do not contribute to the mean values, an implication of the periodic boundary condition and Gauss integral theorem.

As we take the mean value along the equations (
13

42

(57)

(58)

\displaystyle\Omega_{L}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2+\zeta_{B})}{3\,\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\rho$}_{c}}\langle|\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{1.2}[1.0]{${\mathrm{D}}$}}\scalebox{1}[0.8]{$\phi$}^{(1)}|^{2}\rangle\,,

The aceleration of the

VII
Final considerations

In this manuscript, we have introduced a cosmological model with matter inhomogeneously distributed in space as part of an effort to expand our mathematical understanding of the cosmological principle, namely, models beyond the FLRW spacetimes that are homogeneous and isotropic on large scales. We have focused on novelties for the late time regimen, while we kept the earlier epochs as homogeneous and isotropic as in the standard picture of Cosmology. The matter content has been described phenomenologically by a kind of dust capable of reacting viscously to the local tidal forces. We have shown that the problem of Einstein’s equations in this case is well-posed, and each solution gives rise to an FLRW spacetime on large scales, where the equation of state parameter has the form

\mathrm{w}(\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathrm{a}$})=\mathrm{w}_{0}+\mathrm{w}_{1}\,(\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathrm{a}$}-1)+\frac{\mathrm{w}_{2}}{2!}\,(\scalebox{1.1}[1.1]{$\mathrm{a}$}-1)^{2}+\ldots\,,

(82)

with
\mathrm{w}_{0}\leq 0
and each of the coefficients
\mathrm{w}_{i}
determined by the current distribution of the energy and particle densities along space. This turns out to be a significant result in the literature of backreaction in Cosmology for the following reasons:(i) It is an explicit model where the late-time acceleration of the universe appears as an apparent effect caused by the local gravitational field; (ii) An effective FLRW spacetime appears naturaly on large-scales; (iii) The coefficients in the Taylor series (
82
) are quite arbitrary to fit the observational data; (iv) The deceleration parameter is negative for
\mathrm{w}_{0}<-1/3
, indicating a large-scale apparent acceleration, while the observers who witness an homogeneous and isotropic expansion are typically decelerating relative to each other. In particular, this last feature tells us that the global time used to describe the large-scale dynamics cannot be realised as the proper time of almost any observer to whom the expansion and geometry are homogeneous and isotropic, except in the FLRW case, of course. This fact has important implications, as a natural Hubble tension due to the different time parameters used to measure the CMB and the luminous distance. This will be discussed elsewhere in a forthcoming manuscript.

We claim that the mathematical implementation of the cosmological principle is not exclusive to the FLRW spacetimes. In this sense, models with homogeneous and isotropic expansion
[
27
]
have a lot to contribute to theoretical Cosmology by realising physically meaningful universes where this tenet is still valid. Their conceptual development and subsequent observational scrutiny could turn them into serious candidates for the most suitable mathematical framework underlying our explanation of the Cosmos.
