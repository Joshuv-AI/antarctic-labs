Suspected myocarditis resulting from leptospirosis: A case report from Iran
Authors: Lotfollah Davoodi, Hasti Asdaghi Jahromi, Eissa Soleymani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Leptospirosis is a potentially fatal disease that can lead to dysfunction across multiple organ systems. Although cardiovascular complications are relatively rare, they are frequently underestimated and may result in severe outcomes if not addressed promptly. A 46-year-old farmer presented with complaints of palpitations and chest pain. He reported a history of fever, chills, rigors, diaphoresis, myalgia, nausea, low back pain, and headaches that began 7 days prior. An electrocardiogram showed diffuse T-wave inversions and sinus tachycardia; a troponin test was positive, indicating myocardial injury. Echocardiography revealed generalized wall motion abnormalities and left ventricular systolic dysfunction. After 5 days of antibiotic treatment and a 3-day course of corticosteroids, the patient fully recovered and was discharged without any complications. His angiography was normal, and leptospiral IgM was highly positive. Severe leptospirosis should be considered a potential cause of myocarditis, especially in patients with occupational risk factors and those living in endemic regions. Given the non-specific clinical features and diagnostic findings of leptospirosis, it is essential to maintain a high level of suspicion. Timely clinical recognition and laboratory confirmation are crucial, as delays can lead to higher mortality.

Introduction

Leptospirosis is an infectious disease caused by Leptospira bacteria, transmitted from animals to humans. It can be contracted through direct exposure or contact with contaminated water.1,2 WHO states that the occurrence of leptospirosis in temperate areas that are not endemic ranges from 0.01 to 1 case/100,000 individuals. In contrast, endemic tropical areas see an incidence of 10–100 cases/100,000 people. 3 The bacteria primarily infect various animals, particularly rodents, and human infections typically occur through accidental contact with water or soil contaminated with infected animals’ urine. 1 The clinical presentation can vary widely, ranging from a mild, undifferentiated febrile illness to severe multiorgan dysfunction syndrome, which can be life-threatening. 4 Cardiac complications are well-documented in cases of leptospirosis. These manifestations can include non-specific electrocardiographic changes, arrhythmias, myocarditis, pericarditis, endocarditis, and, in severe cases, cardiogenic shock. 5 Here we present the case of a middle-aged male who presented with severe leptospirosis with cardiac involvement.

Case presentation

In August 2025, a 46-year-old farmer presented to Fatemeh Zahra heart center in Northern Iran with complaints of palpitations and chest pain radiating to his left shoulder for the past 7 days. He reported a history of fever, chills, rigors, diaphoresis, myalgia, nausea, low back pain, and headaches that began 7 days prior. He had been working in a paddy field recently before admission and had also had contact with farm animals. Upon admission, he was conscious and alert. The examination revealed a respiratory rate of 20 breaths/min and an oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. His blood pressure was measured at 100/60 mmHg, and his pulse rate was 84 beats/min. Notably, there were petechiae observed on his back, as well as bilateral erythema and conjunctivitis in his eyes. Additionally, his fingers appeared pale and cold (Figure 1). Cardiac, abdominal, and central nervous system examinations were normal, with no focal neurological deficits noted. The last investigations revealed an electrocardiogram (ECG) showing generalized T-wave inversions and ST depression with a normal sinus rhythm and normal corrected QT interval (QTc) interval. In light of generalized T-wave inversion, a bedside 2D echo was done, in which there was moderate to severe systolic left ventricle dysfunction, his left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was between 35% and 40% and he had a global wall motion abnormality. Also ECG showed left anterior hemi block, left anterior deviation, T-wave inversion, and R wave progression (Figure 2).

The results of his complete blood count indicated a white blood cell count of 10,800 mg/dL, a hemoglobin level of 13.7 g/dL, and a platelet count of 21,000/μL. Moreover, his troponin test was positive, and he had elevated levels of alkaline phosphatase 526 IU/L, C-reactive protein (CRP) (61 IU/L), and Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) 77 mm/h. He was admitted to the heart center with a diagnosis of non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (MI) and myocarditis. Although he was a candidate for angiography, the procedure was canceled due to his low platelet count. After 2 days, his platelet count decreased further to 25,000/μL. His lab tests also revealed an elevated pro-Brain Natriuretic Peptide (BNP) level of 132 pg/mL. An infectious disease consultation was requested due to suspected leptospirosis.

During the admission, the platelet count decreased further to 14,000/μL, while the troponin test returned negative. The patient was started on ceftriaxone at a dosage of 1 g twice daily and received 8 mg of dexamethasone daily. Complete blood count (CBC) test was repeated and monitored daily. After 3 days, dexamethasone was discontinued, and the platelet count increased to 30,000/μL. Although the PCR test for leptospirosis was positive, an elevated leptospirosis IgM level of 18.9 mg/dL was observed, within the range of <9 mg/dL. The patient received ceftriaxone for a total of 5 days. Gradually, the patient’s symptoms improved, and by the seventh day of hospitalization, both the ECG and echocardiography results returned to normal. Angiography was performed, and the findings were normal. He was discharged with a platelet count of 201,000/μL (Table 1). Before discharging the patient, we requested a cardiology consult, which recommended that he take bisoprolol 2.5 mg daily. The patient was discharged a week later in an asymptomatic state after having recovered from acute illness.

The report of this article adheres to the CARE checklist. We obtained the patient’s consent before initiating treatment for leptospirosis and myocarditis, as well as conducting other diagnostic procedures as per our institutional protocols (Supplemental Material).

Discussion

Leptospirosis is an uncommon infection, yet it is the most widespread zoonosis and is reemerging globally. 5 Most infections are asymptomatic and resolve spontaneously. However, in certain cases, the infection may progress to the icteric phase, commonly known as “Weil’s disease.” This stage may be characterized by symptoms such as kidney failure, jaundice, pulmonary hemorrhage, acute respiratory distress syndrome, myocarditis, rhabdomyolysis, or septic shock. The mortality rate in severe cases ranges between 3% and 5%, with the majority of deaths due to acute kidney or liver failure, myocarditis, pulmonary hemorrhage, or multiorgan failure.5,6 The precise mechanism underlying cardiac involvement in leptospirosis remains incompletely understood. It is believed to stem from the complex interplay between the host’s immune response and the infectious agent.

One proposed mechanism for organ dysfunction in this context is systemic vasculitis. The broad activation of the immune system can lead to a sepsis-like syndrome that directly affects the heart or, as a consequence of leptospirosis, induces systemic vasculitis. 7 Leptospirosis can cause various typically transient ECG abnormalities, including bundle branch block, atrial fibrillation, varying degrees of AV block, ventricular and supraventricular extrasystoles, and low-voltage QRS complexes. Elevated troponin levels do not necessarily indicate acute coronary syndrome; they can also occur in conditions such as perimyocarditis and sepsis. In sepsis, myocardial and microvascular dysfunction may result in troponin release even in the absence of significant coronary artery obstruction. Although this is rare, potential explanations include transient hypoperfusion, coronary vasospasm, and localized endothelial injury due to the systemic inflammatory response associated with sepsis. 8

The patient presented with generalized T-wave inversions and ST-segment depression in the anterior leads, along with a positive troponin test result. Our initial diagnosis was a non-ST-elevation of MI, prompting us to place the patient on the list for reperfusion strategies. However, this plan was later rescinded due to the patient’s severe thrombocytopenia and our suspicion of leptospirosis. Careful evaluation of clinical findings, laboratory results, and imaging studies is essential to differentiate myocarditis from coronary events, thereby mitigating the risk of potentially harmful interventions, such as unnecessary dual antiplatelet therapy or even reperfusion procedures.9,10 Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was utilized to aid in the diagnosis and monitoring of the patient’s condition. Initially, the transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) revealed a diminished LVEF; however, this measurement subsequently improved and returned to normal as the patient recovered from leptospirosis. This progression underscores the importance of conducting serial TTE assessments to monitor cardiac improvement throughout the management of leptospiral myocarditis.

The TTE findings were consistent with previous case series, which reported global hypokinesia and reduced LVEF. 11 There was a significant improvement in cardiac function following the initiation of targeted antibiotic therapy and supportive care. This underscores the potential for recovery in leptospiral myocarditis, even in the presence of initial hemodynamic instability, and helps differentiate it from MI. One limitation of our case was the exclusion of the microscopic agglutination test (MAT), which is considered the gold standard for diagnosing leptospirosis. Instead, we utilized the IgM/IgG anti-Leptospira test, which has lower sensitivity and specificity compared to MAT. 12 Currently, there are no specific treatment guidelines for leptospiral myocarditis other than treating the underlying sepsis and supportive therapy. 5 Currently, a range of broad-spectrum antibiotics such as doxycycline, azithromycin, erythromycin, penicillin G, cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, ampicillin, and amoxicillin are used. 13 We used ceftriaxone 1 g twice daily for 5 days, along with a 3-day course of 8 mg dexamethasone. Our patient responded well to this treatment, with no complications.

Conclusion

Severe leptospirosis can lead to multiple organ dysfunction and is associated with a significant mortality rate. It should be considered a possible cause of myopericarditis, especially in patients with occupational risk factors or those residing in endemic areas. Considering that the clinical manifestations and diagnostic findings of leptospirosis lack specificity, it is essential to maintain a heightened level of suspicion for this condition. Early clinical identification and laboratory confirmation of leptospirosis are critical, as a late diagnosis may elevate the risk of mortality.
