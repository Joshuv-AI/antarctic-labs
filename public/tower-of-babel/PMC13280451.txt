Pediatric pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of guanabenz for the treatment of vanishing white matter
Authors: Linda G. Franken, Bart van Lieshout, Joshua Eugenio, Renate Verbeek, Marije Voermans, Elske van den Berg, Pierre Bet, Ron A. A. Mathôt, Dennis van der Laan, Truus E. M. Abbink, Marjo S. van der Knaap, Imke H. Bartelink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vanishing White Matter (VWM) is a leukodystrophy with cystic cerebral white matter degeneration, resulting in neurological decline and premature death. There is currently no treatment. Guanabenz, a central α2-adrenergic receptor agonist, ameliorates the disease in VWM mouse models. We aimed to evaluate the validity of a translational guanabenz pediatric pharmacokinetic model, to explore the concentration-toxicity association, and to estimate brain guanabenz concentrations using a biomarker of biological effect and toxicity. Translational modelling was performed to determine the potentially effective guanabenz dose. During a phase 1/2 clinical trial for pediatric VWM patients, this model was evaluated and the correlation between guanabenz concentrations and side-effects was explored. Translating rodent PK/PD models and adult PK models, the estimated pediatric target dose of guanabenz was 2 mg/kg/d, with a minimum effective dose of 1 mg/kg/d and a maximum of 10 mg/kg/d. PK modelling was performed with 32 trial participants, aged 2.4–12.9 years. Evaluation of the translational model showed an underprediction with a median prediction error of -50% with oral doses between 1.04 and 1.68 mg/kg/d. Hallucinations were an unexpected side-effect. A trend was seen in the correlation between the decrease in mean arterial blood pressure and hallucinations. The developed translational model was able to predict plasma concentrations within acceptable range of 2-fold prediction error. The plasma concentrations reached were above the predicted minimal effective dose. Hallucinations were a dose limiting adverse event and the predicted optimal dose may have been underestimated. Blood pressure may be useful as a non-invasive biomarker in future studies. This clinical trial was registered under number: 2017-001438-25 on 28/11/2018.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47959-9.

Introduction

Vanishing White Matter (VWM) is a leukodystrophy that most often presents in children between two and six years of age1–4. It causes cystic degeneration of the cerebral white matter, leading to motor and cognitive decline and premature death4. The disease course and age at death are extremely variable, with age of onset as best predictive variable of disease course, especially in very early onset4. Currently, there is no approved therapy for this severe disease.

VWM is caused by pathogenic variants in the genes EIF2B1-5, encoding the 5 subunits of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2B (eIF2B)5,6. eIF2B is conditional for mRNA translation per se and central in regulating the rate of mRNA translation and protein synthesis. It is pivotal to downregulate protein synthesis upon activation of the integrated stress response (ISR). Various types of physiological stress activate enzymes that phosphorylate initiation factor eIF2, which then inactivates eIF2B, activating its downstream targets and inhibiting protein synthesis7–11. A feedback loop in the ISR via stress-induced growth arrest and DNA damage-inducible protein (GADD34) restores eIF2B activity and reduces ISR activation. In VWM, pathogenic variants in eIF2B reduce its activity, leading to constitutive activation of the downstream ISR. In mouse models for VWM, the degree of ISR activation correlates with severity of clinical disease and white matter pathology. Inhibition of ISR with the eIF2B activator ISRIB resulted in improvements in neuropathology and motor skills in these VWM mice12. We therefore postulated that the abnormal activation of the ISR plays a central role in VWM pathophysiology and that the ISR is potential target for treatment of VWM12.

Guanabenz is a central acting α2-adrenergic receptor agonist, which has been trialed and registered in 1973–1983 as a treatment for hypertension in adults. Additionally, it has a known effect on GADD34 in the ISR. We showed in VWM mice that guanabenz reduces ISR activation and ameliorates the clinical disease and brain pathology1,13. A clinical trial was, therefore, initiated in pediatric patients with VWM. Guanabenz is lipophilic drug that is well absorbed (75%) after oral administration. The Cmax is observed around 2–5 h after a single oral dose. Guanabenz is 90% bound to plasma proteins is metabolized via CYP1A2 and passes the blood brain barrier via passive transport. No data on guanabenz were available for the pediatric population. Translational pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) modelling was performed to determine the safe and potentially effective doses of guanabenz for children. This PKPD model was then used to design the dosing schedule and PK-sampling in the phase 1/2 clinical trial, which investigates the safety, tolerability and efficacy of guanabenz in children with VWM.

The PKPD aims of this currently ongoing trial were to evaluate the validity of the translational guanabenz pediatric PK-model, to explore the concentration-toxicity association and, to assess the possibility of estimating the guanabenz concentration in the brain using an early non-invasive biomarker of biological effect and toxicity. For the latter, blood pressure measurements were chosen, as guanabenz’ antihypertensive effect is due to binding to α2-adrenergic receptors in the brain14. Thus, the relationship between guanabenz and the decrease in blood pressure was investigated.

Materials and methods

Translational PKPD

First, relevant PK and efficacy and toxicity data were extracted from preclinical models (for details see supplementary material). Studies in VWM mice showed that a 4.5 mg/kg daily dose resulted in a better clinical efficacy than a 10 mg/kg weekly dose, suggesting that the total area under the curve (AUC) is the driver of clinical efficacy rather than the Cmax13. Therefore, we translated the possible effective dose on AUC from mice to children. Previous studies in rats showed accumulation and some delayed absorption of guanabenz in the brain15. To scale optimal dose from brain AUC from rodents to children, plasma PK parameters from mice, brain ratio’s from rats and adult plasma PK data15–17 were combined to infer the PK profile and accumulation of guanabenz in the brain in children at the optimal predicted dose level (figure S1). In short, and explained in more detail in the supplementary materials, linear compartmental models were used to approximate the observed mouse and rat PK-data in plasma and brain15,17. The observed data in mice and rat were adequately estimated using two-compartment models, with equilibrium force function with two parameters, the rate of distribution (KPT) and ratio between plasma and the brain (RPT) from plasma into the brain, to describe the delayed transfer of Guanabenz from rat-plasma to brain (Supplemental 1.1.1, Eq. 8).

For plasma-PK, allometric scaling was used (equations in supplementary materials) among rodents, adults and children. Two methods were used to predict brain concentrations in children. In the first, the rate and extent of penetration from plasma into the brain (KPT & RPT) were directly translated from the rat model, without adjustment for differences in body composition15. In the second, differences in brain mass ratio between mice and humans were taken into account to correct both parameters (Table 1), as recent research showed that the permeability surface area product (PS) in ml/min/g tissue is well conserved among species18.

Trial and patricipants

The trial is an ongoing open-label single-center study at the University Medical Center Amsterdam in children with DNA-confirmed VWM with onset ≤6 years and disease duration ≤8 years, with approval of the Dutch Central Ethics Committee (CCMO) (https://www.clinicaltrialsregister.eu/ctr-search/trial/2017-001438-25/NL) and written informed consent from the participant’s parents or legal guardians. Each participant’s age, body weight, body temperature, heart rate and blood pressure were recorded during the trial. Side-effects and adverse events were recorded and severity graded, applying NCI CTCAE version 5.0.

Dosing and sampling

On the first day of the trial, VWM patients were administered approximately 0.5 mg/kg guanabenz in oral capsules: approximately 5-fold higher than the adult 4 mg BID antihypertensive starting dose21. Subsequently, doses were given once daily in the evening to minimize possible guanabenz’s sedative effects during the day. Under daily clinical supervision, the dose was titrated upwards in 1 mg/kg increments every three days or smaller increments over a longer period if mild or moderate side effects occurred. Alternatively, doses were administered twice daily if better tolerated. The dose titration ended if the maximum individual tolerated dose or the maximum dose, determined by translational analysis was reached (Fig. 1a)

For the pharmacokinetic analysis, once a year venous samples were taken at time points of just before and 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, and 12 h after administration of Guanabenz. Additionally, dried blood spot (DBS) samples of 30µL each were taken annually on the same day as the venous sampling before intake and 2 and 3 h after, as well as every three months just before and occasionally 2 h after administration (Fig. 1b). Per protocol, full PK-sampling, was performed in 15 patients with a body weight below 15 kg and in 15 with a body weight of 15 kg and higher. After completion of these cohorts, PK-sampling was minimized. The pharmacodynamic properties of guanabenz were assessed through adverse events, diastolic and systolic blood pressure, heart rate, and body temperature, which were measured frequently during periods of pharmacokinetic assessment and the drug titration.

Bioanalytical method

The DBS and venous samples were analyzed using a validated LC-MS/MS method in an ISO-certified pharmaceutical laboratory at the Amsterdam University Medical Centre. The lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) of this method was 0.1 µg/L and the upper limit was 500 µg/L. The between-day coefficient of variation percentage (CV%) was less than 11.4%. Agreement between DBS and plasma concentrations was evaluated using linear modelling. Additional covariates of age, body weight, height, sex, time after dose, and lag time between the sampling times of both sampling types were evaluated in a stepwise fashion. A resulting model and its predicting variables were considered significant if the p-value of ANOVA’s F-test, compared to the null or parent model, was lower than the α-level of 0.05. The final model’s structure was thereafter incorporated into the pharmacokinetic analysis to assess both sampling types’ data simultaneously.

Pharmacokinetic modelling

A population pharmacokinetic analysis was conducted using nonlinear mixed-effects modeling with NONMEM® (version 7.4.2, ICON PLC, Dublin, Ireland) with first-order conditional estimation (FOCE-I), supported by Pirana (version 3.0.0, Certara, Princeton, NJ, USA). SCM and bootstrap analysis were conducted using Perl-speaks-NONMEM (version 5.3.0, Uppsala University, Uppsala, Sweden). Data processing and graphical imaging of data were performed in R (version 4.2.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with Rstudio (version 2022.02.3, Rstudio, Boston, MA, USA) and the ggplot2 and cowplot packages. Linear modeling of PK data was done in R using the stats package and visualized with the ggfortify package. Prediction-corrected visual predictive checks were generated in R with the vpc package. The Kaplan-Meier analysis and visualization were performed in R using the survival and survminer packages.

For the translational modelling animal the effective and toxic doses from literature13,22 were translated to brain AUC values using previously described data in rodents studies15,17. This was then comibned with human adult data combined to predict the PK profile and accumulation of Guanabenz in the brain in children with VWM at clinical doses (Figs. 1 and 2)19.The animal PK-data were extracted from 2 preclinical models using a plot extractor tool15,17. Parameter estimation was performed using NONMEM® (version 7.4.2, ICON PLC, Dublin, Ireland) with first-order conditional estimation (FOCE-I), supported by Pirana (version 3.0.0, Certara, Princeton, NJ, USA). To evaluate the accuracy of the model, goodness-of-fit plots PRED vs. observed and CWRES vs. time were made. R (4.3.2) was used with Rstudio (2023.12.1.402) for data processing and visualization. For the model building, one- and two-compartment models were compared to determine which structural model best described the guanabenz PK. Afterwards, inter-individual variability (IIV) on each of the model’s parameters was assessed in a stepwise matter and residual variability was assessed in both additive, proportional and combined error models. The detailed description of model development and evaluation can be found in the supplementary methods.

Pharmacodynamic analysis

Sequential PKPD analyses were used to investigate the concentration-response relationships. The PK profile of each individual for the entire study’s duration was simulated by using the dose adjustment logs and the final individual’s PK parameter estimates. Subsequently, the concentration-toxicity relationship was analyzed and compared to adult data to estimate the PD translation.

Categorical measures– adverse events

The recorded adverse events during the study were gathered and events that were considered not or unlikely related to guanabenz were omitted from the dataset. The prevalence of each adverse event was calculated as the percentage of the population that had experienced that specific adverse event. The results were compared to adult adverse event data in the literature, describing the occurrence of adverse events after a period of daily guanabenz intake for six months24,25.

Hallucinations were observed as unexpected adverse events. Explorative time-to-event (TTE) population modelling, using Cox Proportional-Hazards Modeling was performed to investigate a concentration-response relationship or discover covariates that could help identify at-risk populations26. Covariate selection was performed using backward elimination approach, starting with a full model and iteratively removing covariates to improve the AIC. Pearson correlation analysis was done to exclude covariates with potential multicollinearity prior to selection (Supplemental figure S6).

Furthermore, mainly for visualization purposes, Kaplan-Meier analysis on the time-to-event dataset was performed after dichotomization of the data, with an α-level of 0.05.

Model evaluation

The pediatric PK-predictions using rodent and adult data were evaluated using the percentage prediction error (PE%) through Eq. 8 and prediction-corrected visual predictive check (pcVPC). If the PE% of the rodent or adult extrapolated model was within 2-fold of the observed data, with adequate pcPVC, the model was considered adequate for translational predictions27. The median PE% of the adult extrapolated model was also compared to the median PE% of the final model to demonstrate the improvement after re-estimation of all PK-parameters using the observed plasma PK-data.8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${PE\% = \frac{{Population \:Predicted - Observed}}{{Observed}}\times 100\% } $$\end{document}

Furthermore, a comparison was made between PK-parameters of the final fitted model and the model that was developed based on rodent or adult PK parameters that were extrapolated by allometric scaling (Eq. 1 in the supplements)19.

Results

Translational model development

To approximate the observed mouse and rat pharmacokinetic data in plasma6,11, linear compartmental models were applied to the median of the observed plasma PK data Both rat and mice plasma PK-data showed a bilinear shape, indicating the presence of a distribution and a terminal phase, characteristic of a two-compartment model. Initially, the PK parameters for mice were allometrically scaled from the estimated rat PK parameter values. However, this shows an underprediction of the observed concentrations in mice. Therefore, parameter estimation was done in NONMEM.

For the initial estimations preclinical PK parameters from rodents (mouse and rat) as well as from adult patients were scaled to paedicatric PK as described in Table 1. Simulations were performed for a number of doses (0.25 mg/kg/day–10 mg/kg/week) and the predicted guanabenz concentrations and AUCss in both plasma and brain compartments are summarized in supplemental Table 1.

Translational PKPD analysis

The developed two-compartment model showed adequate predictions of the plasma and brain concentration time profile (AUC and Cmax) in both mice and rats, when compared with data after a single dose of 32 and 100 µg/kg in rats, and 10 mg/kg in mice (Supplemental Fig. 2)15,17. These models were therefore used for further simulation purposes. 2 mg/kg and 4.5 mg/kg daily doses were effective in previous tissue and mouse models13), and produced predicted brain AUCss of 7.71 mg*h/L and 17.36 mg*h/L respectively.

Simulations were performed with the current pediatric model for children 3 years of age (supplemental Figs. 3 and 4), which showed steady state concentrations much higher in brain than in plasma. A predicted brain AUCss of 8.5 mg*h/L is reached with a daily dose of 1 mg/kg, and a brain AUC of 17.1 is reached with a dose of 2 mg/kg (Supplemental Table 1). The latter dose was therefore estimated as target dose and 1 mg/kg daily as minimum effective dose. Additional simulations were done for children of ages 2, 5, and 10 years, which showed a decrease of Cmax in the brain with increasing age. (Supplemental Figs. 3 and 4). Rodent studies showed acute toxicity at dosages of 40 mg/kg22. At similar plasma AUC, a daily dose of 10 mg/kg was estimated to be the maximum dose in children. In conclusion, these analyses suggested 2 mg/kg as target dose, with a minimal dose of 1 mg/kg and maximum of 10 mg/kg (Fig. 3).

Clinical characteristics

At the time of the current PKPD analysis, a total of 32 VWM patients had been included in the clinical trial. In 10 patients, one year follow up PK sampling data was available. The participants’ characteristics are shown in Table 2. At inclusion, ages ranged between 2.4 and 12.9 years. The initial doses ranged between 0.15 and 0.5 mg/kg. The doses at year one, which were the highest individual tolerated doses, ranged between 1.04 and 1.68 mg/kg. These titrated doses fell short of reaching the preferred target of 2 mg/kg, but all were above the 1 mg/kg threshold of an effective dose.

Pharmacokinetic sample analysis results

The Guanabenz PK dataset comprised 521 measurements, of which 56 were below the LLOQ of 0.1 µg/L, all but one taken before the patients received the first guanabenz dose and therefore omitted from the analysis. The remaining 466 consisted of 190 and 138 venous samples from the titration phase and year 1 sampling, respectively. For the DBS samples, 90 were taken at baseline, 24 at 1 year, and a total of 79 in the 3-monthly sampling times.

For the validation of DBS measurements, a total of 84 venous measurements were matched to DBS measurements at similar sampling times, where the median difference in time (range) was 0.12 (−0.45–0.67) hours (supplemental Figure S5A). DBS concentrations correlated linearly with venous concentrations, with a correction factor of 0.78 (p<.001 compared to 1, adjusted R2 = 0.9832). No other covariate resulted in a significant improvement. This analysis shows that DBS measurements could serve as a valuable addition to the PK dataset and its analysis as they reflected the venous measurements well, albeit with the following considerations. The diagnostic plots’ residuals (supplemental Figure S5B) indicated increased variability in DBS samples. DBS samples were taken at home by parents or legal guardians, and the absence of hematocrit measurements to normalize the DBS measurements may explain the additional variability. Therefore, all population PK model predictions including DBS samples were optimized by adding a parameter for the ratio between the two sampling types, and two separate error models.

Pharmacokinetic modeling

The model developed using only rodent PK data showed a prediction error of 100% when extrapolating to children. By extrapolating adult PK parameters an overall under-prediction of the measured concentrations (Fig. 4C) was observed, with a median PE% of −50.0, within the defined limits (Table 1)28.

For the population PK model, the structural model that best described the data was a one-compartment model with a first-order absorption rate (Ka) and CL. A two-compartment model was not able to be adequately fitted to the data, giving relative standard error percentages (RSE%) for the peripheral volume and inter-compartmental clearance parameter estimates in excess of 100%. The model that best described the RUV was a proportional error model for both venous and DBS measurement types.

The following IIVs were incorporated, in the following order, on CL (∆OFV = −117.7), V (∆OFV = −68.1), and Ka (∆OFV=−7.81). The subsequent SCM showed body weight to be a significant covariate of CL (∆OFV=−18.12, p<.001) and V (∆OFV=−36.2, p<.001). The parameter estimates of the final PK model can be seen in Table 1. PcVPCs and goodness-of-fit plots (Fig. 4A,B) showed that the parameter estimates and corresponding IIVs were able to describe the PK data well and could explain its variability. The final model’s predictions showed a median PE% of −4.5. The original adult extrapolated PK parameters of CL, V, and Ka were within the 95% CI of the final PK-parameters. Likewise, the allometric scaling exponents showed to be adequately within the 95% CI, with the exponent for body weight on V being near the lower range.

Pharmacodynamic analysis

Mean arterial pressure modelling

We hypothesized that mean arterial pressure could be an early non-invasive biomarker of biological effect and toxicity, if the MAP associated with the central adverse events incidence observed in children. If this hypothesis is correct, MAP may be suitable as a biomarker for guanabenz brain concentrations.

First, MAP was modeled using a dataset consisting of 2106 systolic and diastolic blood pressures during the titration phase. For the structural model, the concentration-response relationship was best described by the Emax function. Thereafter, the addition of an effect compartment (∆OFV=−26,806 and an exposure-driven tolerance through EC50 (∆OFV = −104.3) significantly improved the model (combined ∆OFV=−422.008) compared to the base model. Following this, IIVs were assigned on the following parameters: EC50,BL (∆OFV=−61.5) and the effect compartment’s rate constant, ke (∆OFV=−58.4). The final PD model’s parameter estimates are shown in Table 3. The pcVPCs (Fig. 5), showed a good fit to the data, although the variability was under-explained. They also showed a decrease in effect after several days of guanabenz intake, albeit partly due to sparse sampling during the time point of maximum effect. The goodness-of-fit plots (Fig. 5B) overall supported the model’s fit, although they did show predictions with high CWRES.

Adverse events

An overview of the adverse events observed in trial patients is shown in Table 4, together with adult guanabenz adverse event data, as published24,25 the full list of adverse events is shown in Table S2. For the adverse events, three were graded as severe, one being constipation and the other urinary incontinence. Among the adverse events, which were also present in the adult data, were dry mouth (82%), drowsiness (82%), feeling weak (35%), headaches (24%), dizziness (18%), and insomnia (18%). An unexpected adverse event was hallucinations (41%). These hallucinations were analyzed in more detail to uncover potential predicting covariates. Regarding the other adverse events, it was observed that the pediatric participants experienced them more frequently than reported from adults.

Concerning hallucinations, a total of 16 events were observed in 15 participants. The majority of hallucinations occurred in the first months of the study, especially during the drug titration phase at the start (Fig. 6). The final multivariate model includes the maximum brain concentration, and the predicted baseline MAP, and the predicted maximum decrease after 1 day of treatment. Although there was some difference in the TTE, this effect was insignificant (p >.05). The brain Cmax at day 1 and the maximum decrease of MAP at day 1, have positive regression coefficients (Table 3), indicating direct proportionality with the hazard. Additional univariate analysis of each covariate in the final model also resulted in a similar, yet insignificant results (Fig. 6). Overall, our simulations of brain concentrations using the BMR suggest that, although plasma concentrations are similar across patients of different body weights at a dose of 1 mg/kg/day, brain concentrations may be substantially higher in younger children. Consequently, to achieve the same brain AUC, the minimum effective dose may be lower in this population, as shown in Fig. 7.

Discussion

In this study, we showed that by using rodent and adult PK/PD data, we were able to adequately derive an possible effective dose for a phase 1 clinical trial of guanabenz in children with VMW. Observed concentrations were less than two-fold higher than those predicted on basis of the adult model. During the clinical trial the potentially minimal effective dose was reached in all patients through dose titration, but the potentially optimal dose of 2 mg/kg was not reached, due to off target toxicity. The occurrence of tolerance prevents the use of MAP as biomarker for long-term response, but a trend was observed of early onset hypotensive drug effects and risk of hallucinations future dose. Further concentration-efficacy and -toxicity analyses are needed to evaluate the balance between efficacy and toxicity and may guide further treatment optimization.

The fact that toxicity was seen at a dose below the predicted optimal dose may indicate that brain concentrations in children were underpredicted, even though we corrected for the difference in brain mass between rodents, humans and children specific. Guanabenz is passively transported into the brain and we know from literature that PS values for these drugs are conserved between species18. This means that the permeability-surface area product (PS) for passive diffusion is conserved when adjusted for differences in brain microvascular endothelial surface area across species. As this PS value is expressed as ml/min/g tissue we performed additional analysis correcting for the brain weight of children with different ages. However, this does not take into account structural differences in lipophilicity of the brain between species. In fact in humans, white matter account for around half of the total brain mass while this is only around 10% in mice29” Guanabenz is a lipophilic drug and human brains have much more white matter than rodents, which could result in accumulation of guanabenz in the human compared to the rodent brain. As a result, it is possible that we underpredicted brain concentrations in humans, particularly in this pediatric population, leading to a lower-than-anticipated effective dose. This may also have contributed to the unexpected occurrence of hallucinations, which limited the use of higher doses in children.

Direct measurements of brain concentrations in children are not feasible, therefore we evaluated if MAP could be used as a non-invasive biomarker for guanabenz brain concentrations. To this end we developed a population PD model and explored its correlation with the occurrence of hallucinations. This PD model consisted of an EMAX concentration-response relationship, an effect compartment, and a tolerance component. The effect compartment was warranted as the time point of the maximum decrease in MAP followed after the time point of the maximum guanabenz concentration. Tolerance was best described by increasing EC50, not by decreasing EMAX. This was primarily due to the fact that while the dosages were titrated upwards, the effect size remained stable and the effect duration did not increase. The effect duration remained below 24 h, so a persistent effect was not observed as Ctrough increased. The tolerance might be attributed to the homeostatic regulation in response to the altered hemodynamics30,31 or by downregulation of α2-adrenergic receptors with spare receptors, as with spare receptors the decrease in the amount of available receptors decreases the EC50, while the EMAX can remain largely unaffected32–34. The occurrence of tolerance prevents the use of MAP as biomarker for long-term response. However, the PD model used to associate the individual hypotensive drug effects with the occurrence of hallucinations. In this small dataset, a rapid decrease in MAP appeared to trend toward an increased risk of hallucinations. Further investigation is needed to determine whether early MAP changes could serve as a biomarker for toxicity.

Our modeling approach has some limitations. First of all although the prediction error was within the defined limits for pediatric studies it was still relatively large considering that enzyme ontogeny should not play a major role especially as guanabenz is metabolized via CYP1A2 which matures before the age of 2. This may be explained by the fact that that the literature on pharmacokinetics of guanabenz is very limited. The study that was used for the extrapolation was the study of Lasseter et al. from 1984 which analyzed the pharmacokinetics of guanabenz in 20 patients, of which 10 had liver insufficiency. Therefore the adult PK was based only on 10 adult patients who received a lower dose. However by re-estimating the model we have been able to accurately describe the PK of guanabenz in children. However it should be noted that the youngest patient in our study was 2 years of age. At this age the blood brain barrier permeability as well as the metabolic route of guanbenz (via CYP1A2) has matured, as a result our findings cannot be readily extrapolated to younger children35,36.

In addition in the PK translation only age with respect to brain mass was implemented, but no other adjustments were performed for species or disease state. This may have affected the results since the amount of white matter may influence the brain concentrations of guanabenz due to its high lipophilicity. Future research should try to take this into account by incorporating this effect possibly by the use of MRI data. Another limitation of our study is that only total concentrations were measured while guanabenz is highly protein bound, with about 90%. However there is no information available about interspecies variability in protein binding of guanabenz, and all available literature is on total concentrations. Furtheremore, it is important to note that because VWM is a rare disease the population size was very small. As a result analysis to determine covariates are likely to be underpowered and only trends could be observed. Finally, the hematocrit values of DBS samples were not measured and the measured guanabenz concentrations could therefore not be normalized with a standard hematocrit value37,38. This, alongside the fact that DBS samples could be sampled at home, resulted in a higher proportional error compared to venous samples. However, the linear modelling between the two sampling types showed a good correlation between the two methods, albeit with a correction factor. DBS guanabenz PK-samples can therefore be considered suitable alternative to venous samples in an outpatient setting. Second, the final PK model showed under-predictions of the steady state CMAX values. Given that the model showed a high CV% and shrinkage for the IIV on Ka, this is likely due to limited sampling in the absorption phase (< 2 h after dosing). Future PK-sampling schemes in children should allow assessing maturation of the absorption processes. The PD-modeling was hypothesis driven only. For MAP predictions, the circadian rhythm was specifically sampled protocol, nor implemented into the model, which might have affected the determination of the concentration-response relationship as doses, except those taken during PKPD assessments, were taken in the evening. The occurrence of hallucinations as dose limiting effect allowed us to use a TTE modeling, but further modeling of other AEs would require repeated event modeling and allow further dose optimization. With the data on MAP we made optimal use of all the information to predict brain concentrations. For future perspective it would be of interest to combine this information in a physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modelling, like the one by Verscheijden et al. and be able to possibly link CSF concentrations to actual brain concentrations. In conclusion, we were able to translate preclinical and adult data to adequately predict plasma concentrations in children. This data was used to predict an minimal and optimal effective dose, of which the minimal effective dose was reached during the clinical study. The fact that the optimal effective dose was not reached may indicate an underprediction of brain concentrations in our translational model. Therefore, the optimal effective dose may be lower than initially predicted. Indeed, brain AUC predictions using the final re-estimated model showed a generally higher AUC for all doses (Fig. 3). Therefore in future research this needs to be evaluated further possibly using a biomarker, like early onset hypotension, to evaluate the concentration - toxicity/efficacy relationships of guanabenz.
