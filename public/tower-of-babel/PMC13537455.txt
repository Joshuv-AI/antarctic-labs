Sleep and neurocognitive performance in middle-aged Latina women in a rural agricultural community: insights from the CHAMACOS Maternal Cognition Study
Authors: Nadia A Rojas, Lucia Calderon, Katherine Kogut, Yesli Perez-Rocha, Jenny S Guadamuz, Mark Fleming, Marcella Warner, Brenda Eskenazi, Hector M González, Julianna Deardorff, Jacqueline M Torres
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesTo evaluate the connection between sleep measures and neurocognitive function among middle-aged Latinas in rural settings.Materials and MethodsWe analyzed cross-sectional data from the 2022–2024 CHAMACOS Maternal Cognition study of middle-aged (mean age: 49) Latinas (n = 500) residing in a rural agricultural community in California. We measured nightly sleep duration, sleep quality, and weekly nap frequency via self-report. Participants completed the SOL-INCA-AD neurocognitive battery, yielding global and domain-specific (memory, executive function, verbal fluency) cognitive z-scores. We estimated associations between sleep measures and cognitive scores controlling for socio-demographic and health-related covariates.ResultsParticipants averaged 8.5 hours of sleep per night. Overall, 65% of participants reported no naps, 22% 1–2 naps, and 13% 3+ naps per week; 21% reported “restless or very restless sleep.” There was an inverted U-shaped association between sleep duration and memory z-scores (linear 𝛽: 0.45, SE: 0.20; quadratic 𝛽: -0.03, SE: 0.01), but no statistically significant associations with other cognitive scores. Reporting 1–2 weekly naps (vs. none) was associated with higher memory (𝛽: 0.21, SE: 0.10) and lower verbal fluency (𝛽: -0.18, SE: 0.09) z-scores, but no statistically significant association with executive function or global z-scores. Reporting 3+ weekly naps (vs. none) was associated with lower memory z-scores (𝛽: -0.30, SE: 0.12). We observed no statistically significant associations between sleep quality and neurocognitive scores.ConclusionsOur results suggest the importance of sleep as a modifiable factor for ADRDs risk reduction among US Latinos and provide a foundation for longitudinal research on sleep and cognitive decline.Keywords: sleep/wake cognition, cognitive function, women’s health, aging

Statement of Significance

Our study provides novel insights into sleep and cognitive function among Latinas in a rural agricultural community. While sleep disturbances and short and long sleep duration have been linked with worse neurocognitive function and increased ADRDs risk, existing research on Latinos focuses on urban or suburban areas of the United States, limiting our understanding among those in rural settings who face unique social, environmental, and occupational exposures influencing sleep. Our findings support the role of sleep duration in shaping cognitive outcomes beginning in middle-age. Our study provides a foundation for future longitudinal research on sleep and cognitive decline as follow-up cognitive assessments are completed and suggests the importance of identifying early sleep-related interventions to reduce dementia risk, including in rural settings.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease is one of the top ten leading causes of death among adults over 65, with healthcare costs exceeding over $300 billion annually in the United States [1]. Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRDs) disproportionately impact the Latino population. Estimates from nationally representative data indicate an age-adjusted dementia prevalence of 13.6% for Latina women and 8.4% for Latino men in 2016 as compared to 8.7% and 6.6% for their non-Hispanic Whites (hereafter, Whites) counterparts [2]. There is currently no widely accepted treatment available to halt or reverse the course of disease after ADRDs onset. Even if an effective treatment is identified, Latinos may not benefit equitably from such interventions due to limitations in high-quality healthcare access that contribute to delayed diagnosis [3, 4]. The lack of available treatment and inequitable access highlights the pressing need for a focus on identifying modifiable risk factors and early life prevention of ADRDs. Moreover, increasing evidence suggests that the pre-clinical trajectory of Alzheimer’s disease begins decades earlier than onset, indicating that critical prevention windows may begin in middle-age [5].

Sleep disturbances, characterized by difficulties in initiating and maintaining sleep, have been associated with worse neurocognitive function and a higher risk for ADRDs [6–15]. Two frequently studied dimensions of sleep disturbances are duration and quality, both of which have been linked to cognitive function and decline, albeit in complex ways. Studies have shown associations between self-reported short and long average sleep duration and lower cognitive function and steeper cognitive decline in cognitively unimpaired and community-dwelling middle-aged and older adults [8–13]. Additionally, studies have found an association between subjective reports of poor sleep quality and cognitive performance and decline in cognitively unimpaired older adults [14, 15].

Naps can be a valuable metric for assessing the quality of sleep, as ​​frequent naps may indicate short sleep duration or poor sleep quality [16, 17]. However, studies on nap frequency have yielded differing results, depending on the length of the nap. Two reviews - one systematic and one narrative - have established a positive association with cognitive function and napping, particularly brief naps (15–30 minutes), as compared to no naps across a range of different populations, including middle-aged adults [18, 19]. Consistent with this evidence, a study of community-dwelling middle-aged and older adults that employed a Mendelian randomization approach to address confounding bias found that habitual napping was associated with larger total brain volumes. This suggests that napping regularly could be protective against neurodegeneration, including by compensating for poor sleep [20]. In contrast, a systematic review found that taking longer naps (>30 minutes vs. no naps), whether measured objectively or subjectively, was associated with worse cognitive functioning across a range of different study populations, including middle-aged adults [21]. While mechanisms underlying the relationship between longer naps and cognitive function are not well understood, it is thought that longer naps might be suggestive of excessive daytime sleepiness, which can be an early, preclinic symptom of declining cognitive function [22].

Sleep disruption is thought to influence cognitive function through multiple mechanisms. One mechanism involves the accumulation of the 𝛽-amyloid peptide, a metabolic “waste product” found in the interstitial fluid that is cleared from the brain during slow-wave sleep, an important component of restorative sleep [23–26]. As such, even one night of sleep deprivation substantially increases 𝛽-amyloid in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and brain regions connected with Alzheimer’s disease in healthy adults, including middle-aged adults [27, 28]. Short sleep duration and poor sleep quality are associated with increased 𝛽-amyloid burden in cognitively unimpaired middle-aged and older adults, with findings supported by subjective and objective sleep measurements [29–34]. Another mechanism involves the accumulation of hyperphosphorylated tau protein in the brain, leading to the formation of neurofibrillary tangles, a major neurological hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease [32, 35–37]. Both objective and subjective sleep disruption have been linked to tau accumulation in cognitively unimpaired middle-aged and older adults [32, 36, 37]. Evidence also suggests these relationships may be bidirectional as accumulation of 𝛽-amyloid and tau can disrupt sleep cycles [38, 39].

While past studies have provided valuable insights into the relationship between sleep and cognitive function, they were predominantly conducted within white, middle-class populations. A focus on the Latino population is pertinent as they experience sleep inequities in comparison to Whites, including higher odds of sleep disordered breathing (SDB) and short sleep [40]. These inequities could be rooted in social determinants of health that can impact sleep, such as income inequality [41], limited access to healthcare [42], low health literacy [43], and discrimination [44].

Past studies on sleep and neurocognitive function among Latinos have focused on individuals living in urban or suburban regions of the United States, limiting our understanding of sleep and cognitive function among rural Latino populations [13, 45, 46]. The rural agricultural Latino population faces distinct health challenges compared to urban Latino populations, including occupational factors, environmental threats, and heightened vulnerability to food insecurity, leading to associated health risks [47]. These factors can elevate levels of stress, which has been known to impact sleep [48]. Lastly, there is limited research examining the relationship between sleep and neurocognitive function in middle-aged adults.

To fill these gaps, we evaluated the association between sleep duration, sleep quality, and nap frequency with cognitive function among middle-aged Latinas residing in a rural agricultural region. The focus on middle-aged women is partly motivated by the unique sleep dynamics that occur during this development period for women. Sleep disturbances are particularly common among women throughout the menopausal transition and postmenopausal period, affecting 40 to 60% of women in this period [49], owing at least in part to hormonal changes (e.g. declining estrogen and progesterone levels) [50]. Women undergoing this transition also report cognitive difficulties more frequently than premenopausal women [51], also believed to be a result of hormonal changes [52].

We hypothesized that long and short sleep duration, poor sleep quality, and frequent naps would be each associated with lower domain-specific and overall cognitive function scores.

Materials and Methods

Study participants and recruitment

Participants were part of the Center for Health Assessment of Mothers and Children of Salinas (CHAMACOS) Maternal Cognition Study, which is built on a well-established longitudinal birth cohort of predominantly low-income Latino families living in the rural agricultural Salinas Valley of California. Study details have been published elsewhere [53]. Briefly, participants were recruited and screened for eligibility in 1999–2000, and 597 pregnant women were enrolled in a prospective pregnancy cohort. Eligibility criteria included being 18 years or older, less than 20 weeks’ gestation, English- or Spanish-speaking, and eligible for California’s Medicaid program, Medi-Cal (<200% Federal Poverty Level [54]). Nine years later, when their index child was turning 9 years old, 348 of these participants were still engaged in the study. Between 2009 and 2011, to refresh the cohort of 9-year-old children, 18 active CHAMACOS mothers enrolled a second child, and 287 unique women newly enrolled with their 9-year-old child. New mother–child dyads were recruited and enrolled based on similar criteria to the initial cohort. In total, 635 unique maternal participants participated in the study once their child reached age 9, between 2009 and 2011.

The CHAMACOS Maternal Cognition study launched recruitment and data collection between 2022 and 2024. In addition to the neurocognitive battery described in detail below, study visits included a detailed questionnaire facilitated aloud by a bilingual and bicultural interviewer in the participants’ language of choice. Questionnaires were administered in person or over the phone. Interviewers read each questionnaire item and response option aloud and recorded the participants’ verbal response in the electronic questionnaire. Visits also included a fasting blood draw (unless the participant was unable to fast) and anthropometric measurements. Recruitment for the baseline Maternal Cognition visit focused on recruiting 577 women who had participated in a relatively recent data collection wave (i.e. from 2014 onward) and who therefore had updated contact information. In total, 519 women completed the baseline Maternal Cognition visit between 2022 and 2024.

Measurements

Sleep

Participants’ average self-reported nightly sleep duration and nap frequency were determined via questionnaire with measures derived from the Sleep Heart Health Study Sleep Habits Questionnaire [55]. The sleep quality question was adapted from the Women’s Health Initiative Insomnia Rating Scale [56].

Participants self-reported typical sleep and wake times separately for weekdays and weekends using the following questions: “What time do you usually go to bed?” and “What time do you usually wake up?” Sleep duration was calculated as the length between bedtime and wake time, separately for weekdays and weekends. Average nightly sleep duration was calculated as the weighted average of weekday and weekend sleep (5/7 weekday +2/7 weekend) [13]. We modeled sleep duration as a continuous measure but also created a three-category sleep duration measure: <6 hours, 6–9 hours, and >9 hours per night. We defined categories based on previous literature that identified these thresholds as most consistently associated with adverse health outcomes [57, 58].

Participants self-reported sleep quality using the following question: “Overall, was your typical night’s sleep during the past 4 weeks: Very sound or restful, sound or restful, average quality, restless, or very restless.” The five categories were condensed into three categories (restful, average, or restless) to account for a limited sample (n < 50) within the “very” restful/restless categories.

Finally, participants self-reported the number of weekly naps via a single-choice question: “During a usual week, how many times do you nap for 5 minutes or more: No naps, 1 or 2 times per week, 3 or 4 times per week, and 5 or more times per week.” Responses of 3-4 times per week and 5+ times per week were combined to account for the limited sample (n < 50) in the individual categories.

Cognitive function

Study participants were administered an adapted version of the Study of Latinos-Investigation of Neurocognitive Aging-Alzheimer’s Disease (SOL-INCA-AD) assessment from the Hispanic Community Health Study [59]. The battery included nine tasks designed to evaluate performance across multiple neurocognitive domains. These included tests of verbal learning, recall, and delayed recall from the Brief Spanish English Verbal Learning Test (B-SEVLT) [60], the Digit Symbol Substitution (DSS) test [61], Digit Span Test (DST) Forwards and Backwards [62], the Trails Making Tests (TMT) A and B [63, 64], and letter (A and F) and category (animal naming) verbal fluency tasks [65]. Scores on each of these tasks were transformed into z-scores (i.e. with a mean of zero and a standard deviation of 1). We combined measures into three domain-specific composite z-scores (memory, executive function, verbal fluency) as well as a global composite z-score that combined information on all tasks. A sensitivity analysis excluded the TMT B test (with low successful completion at 60.0% due to participants quitting from difficulty [64]) from the executive function and global cognitive scores. See Supplemental Materials Table S4 and S5 for a detailed summary of the neurocognitive tasks completion rates.

Covariates

Covariates were chosen a priori based on their potential to act as confounders in the relationship between sleep and cognitive function based on a directed acyclic graph (DAG; see Supplemental Materials Figures S1–S3). Covariates include age in years, education (≤6th grade, 7–12th grade, ≥ HS graduate), country of birth (categories: United States, foreign-born, and missing), currently married or living as married (Yes, No), currently working (Yes, No), and household income level (at or below the federal poverty level poverty, above the federal poverty level, missing income information).1

In a sensitivity analysis, we also controlled for participants’ chronic health conditions, menopause status, and chronic stressors, all reflecting their status at the same visit in which sleep and cognitive function were measured. We created a variable counting the total number of self-reported doctor-diagnosed health conditions (asthma, heart disease, cancer, and thyroid disorders), as well as diabetes and hypertension. For most participants, diabetes was defined as either self-report of doctor diagnosis (“Has a doctor ever told you that you had diabetes or high blood sugar?”) or fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% [66] as measured through their blood samples, which were analyzed at LabCorp. For 13 participants without blood samples, only self-report was used.

Hypertension was defined as either self-reported prior diagnosis (“Has a doctor ever told you that you had high blood pressure or hypertension?”) or in-office blood pressure measurements of ≥130 mm Hg systolic or ≥ 80 mm Hg diastolic (as defined for stage 1 hypertension) [67]. For 6 participants without blood pressure measurements, we relied solely on self-report. The health condition count (0-6) was then categorized into a three-category measure: 0, 1, or 2 or more conditions.

For menopause status, we used the Stages of Reproductive Aging Workshop (STRAW+10) criteria to categorize women into four groups: premenopausal, perimenopausal, natural menopausal, or surgical menopausal [68]. Participants were classified as premenopausal if, within the last 12 months, they reported having regular periods, habitually irregular periods, or no period due to pregnancy, breastfeeding, or contraception. Women who reported having irregular periods in the last year that were atypical were categorized as perimenopausal. Lastly, women were classified as having menopause if they reported having no period in the previous year due to natural or surgical menopause.

For chronic stressors, participants were asked the following questions with yes or no response options [69]: “Have you experienced ongoing stress that lasted six or more months related to…” with the following eight stressors listed: personal health problems, health problems in someone else, job difficulties, financial strain, difficulties in relationships, taking care of a friend or relative with a health condition, difficulties with parenting, or something else. We created a continuous variable totaling the number of chronic stressors (0–8).

In a separate sensitivity analysis, we additionally included anxiety and depressive symptoms as covariates. We used continuous variables for both the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10) [70] to assess past-week depressive symptoms and the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale [71] to assess anxiety symptoms experienced within the past 2 weeks.

Statistical analyses

We generated descriptive statistics for the overall analytic sample. We then fit multivariate linear regression models to test the relationships between each of the sleep measures and each of the four composite neurocognitive z-scores. Sleep duration was modeled both as a three-categorical variable and as a continuous variable with its quadratic term given prior evidence of a non-linear association between sleep duration and cognition [8]. The categorical and continuous sleep duration terms were tested independently.

In the analysis of nap frequency and the four neurocognitive domains, we controlled for average nightly sleep duration, along with its quadratic term, as an additional covariate to better isolate the specific contribution of nap frequency to cognitive function (see Supplemental Materials Figure S2 for our DAG).

For each model, we sequentially controlled for covariates as follows: In our primary model, we accounted for age, country of birth, educational attainment, marital status, occupational status, and household income (Model 1). In a sensitivity analyses, we further adjusted for the number of chronic conditions, menopause status, and chronic stressors (Model 2), and additionally, for anxiety and depressive symptoms (Model 3). Covariates added in Models 2 and 3 could serve as potential confounders of the relationship between sleep and neurocognitive measures, but may also mediate this association, in which case covariate adjustment could lead to overcontrol [72–75].

Finally, due to low successful completion rates on the TMT B test (60.0%), we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis using an alternative version of executive function and global composite z-scores, excluding this task.

Analyses were conducted in STATA Version 19.5. In an effort to move towards best practices in the field of epidemiology and highlight findings of public health significance, we report meaningful associations regardless of statistical significance arbitrarily defined by p = .05 [76, 77] but have included P-values in our tables for interested readers.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Of 519 women who completed a study visit, 19 were excluded from the primary (Model 1) analyses due to incomplete data on sleep variables (n = 3) or neurocognitive outcomes (n = 16) for a total analytic sample of 500 participants. The same 500 participants were included in the Model 2 sensitivity analyses. For the Model 3 sensitivity analyses, an additional 11 women were excluded due to missing data on depressive or anxiety symptoms (n = 489). See Supplemental Materials Figure S4 for a study flow diagram.

Table 1 shows descriptive statistics for the overall analytic sample. Participants’ age range was 39–63 and mean age was 49 years (SD: 5.3), and the majority of participants (89%) were foreign-born (majority born in Mexico). Forty-three percent had educational attainment of less than or equal to 6th grade, and a large majority were married or partnered (71%). More than half of the participants (55%) were currently working. More than a third (35%) of participants had a household income at or below the federal poverty level. In comparison, 58% had an income above the poverty threshold, with the majority falling below 200% of the federal poverty level.

Sleep duration

After adjustment for socio-demographic variables, we observed a quadratic association between sleep duration and the memory composite z-score, adjusted for socio-demographic characteristics (Table 2, Model 1), with a linear coefficient of 0.45 standard deviations (SE: 0.20) and a quadratic coefficient of -0.03 (SE: 0.01). Prior to the peak of 8.0 hours, more average hours of sleep is associated with a higher mean memory composite z-score. Beyond this threshold, the estimated mean memory composite z-score declines with increased average sleep duration.

These non-linear patterns were also supported by the analysis that modeled a three-category measure of short, average, and long sleep duration (Table 2). That is, both <6 hours/night (𝛽: -0.14, SE: 0.21) and >9 hours/night (𝛽: -0.11, SE: 0.09) were each associated with lower memory composite z-scores relative to average 6–9 hours of sleep per night. However, these estimates lacked precision, particularly the estimate of <6 hours/night, possibly due to the small numbers of participants who reported sleeping less than 6 hours. Associations between the three-category sleep measure and other neurocognitive performance scores were not statistically significant. We note that the point estimates for the association between the three-category sleep measure and verbal fluency z-scores were of similar magnitude as those observed for memory z-scores [<6 hours (β: 0.11, SE: 0.19), >9 hours (β: -0.08, SE: 0.08)], but estimates were imprecise.

Nap frequency

Our findings revealed that taking 1 or 2 naps per week (versus no naps) was associated with a higher average memory composite z-score (𝛽: 0.21, SE: 0.10) after adjusting for socio-demographic variables (Table 2, Figure 2). Conversely, napping 3 or more times per week (vs. zero weekly naps) was associated with a lower average memory composite z-score (𝛽: -0.30, SE: 0.12). We found a similar association, albeit of smaller magnitude, between napping 3 or more times per week (vs. zero times) and lower global composite z-scores (𝛽: -0.12, SE: 0.08) but no evidence of a protective association between 1-2 weekly naps and global cognitive z-scores (𝛽: -0.01, SE: 0.06).

In contrast, we found that napping 1 or 2 times (vs. zero times) per week was associated with a lower verbal fluency z-score (𝛽: −0.18, SE: 0.09) but no evidence of an association between 3 or more naps per week and the verbal fluency score (𝛽: 0.01, SE: 0.11). Associations between weekly naps and executive function z-scores were not statistically significant.

Sleep quality

Associations between sleep quality (i.e. “very restful or restful sleep” and “restless or very restless sleep” vs. “average quality sleep”) and cognitive performance z-scores were not statistically significant after adjusting for socio-demographic characteristics (Table 2, Figure 2). Coefficients corresponding to “restless or very restless” sleep vs. “average sleep” were in the expected direction (i.e. associated with lower cognitive z-scores), while coefficients corresponding to “very restful sleep or restful sleep” vs. “average sleep” were in the unexpected direction (i.e. also associated with lower cognitive z-scores).

Sensitivity analyses

All of the results remained consistent after further adjustment for health and stress covariates, menopause status, anxiety, and depressive symptoms (Models 2 and 3/Supplemental Materials Table S2), and after excluding TMT B from the executive function and global composite z-scores (Supplemental Materials Table S3).

Discussion

This study examined the relationships between sleep duration, sleep quality, nap frequency, and neurocognitive function in a cohort of Latina women residing in a rural agricultural community. Our findings revealed important relationships between two different aspects of sleep health—sleep duration and weekly nap frequency —and neurocognitive scores in middle-aged women, decades before the typical onset of memory impairment [78]. These associations remained robust even after taking into consideration a range of physical and mental health indicators and chronic stressors related to socioeconomic conditions (e.g. financial strain, caregiving, and interpersonal dynamics), which were highly prevalent among this cohort. To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the relationship between sleep and cognitive function in a rural Latino population.

Aligned with other studies [8], including among urban, suburban, and older US Latinos [46], our findings revealed an inverse U-shaped association between sleep duration and neurocognitive function. We found that longer sleep duration was associated with better memory function scores up to 8.0 hours of average daily sleep. After this threshold, additional sleep was associated with decreased memory function. To give our estimates context, we found that each year of additional age was associated with a 0.03 lower global cognition z-score (SE: 0.01) in our sample. This means that compared to those who slept 8 hours, those who slept 6 hours had estimated cognitive scores equivalent to someone 4 years older and those who slept 9 hours had estimated cognitive scores equivalent to someone one year older.

A cross-sectional study focusing on middle-aged Latino adults in urban settings found a similar inverted U-shaped association with sleep duration [46]. Their reported associations between sleep duration and scores on verbal learning and memory tasks were comparable in magnitude to our observed relationships. Previous studies have demonstrated an inverted U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and neurocognitive function across various domains, including executive function and verbal fluency [8]. We observed associations in our study that were consistent in direction with prior findings, but the estimated magnitudes were modest with large standard errors. Our results extend previous findings to a rural agricultural Latino population.

The biological mechanisms underlying the association between short and long sleep durations and poorer cognitive function remain a subject of ongoing research. As previously noted, a leading theory suggests that short sleep prevents the clearance of 𝛽-amyloid and tau proteins from the brain, a process that largely occurs during deep, slow-wave sleep [27, 37]. The buildup of these proteins in the brain can then disrupt sleep patterns, leading to a vicious cycle [38, 39]. The risks associated with long sleep duration are less clearly understood; however, they may reflect underlying issues such as poor sleep quality [79], chronic inflammation [80], or early-stage neurological changes [81].

We also found a non-linear association between weekly nap frequency and memory scores. Compared to reporting no weekly naps, taking 1 or 2 naps per week was associated with better average memory scores, while taking 3+ naps weekly was associated with lower memory scores. Prior studies on napping and neurocognitive performance typically focus on nap duration, making direct comparisons to our study challenging, as we did not collect data on nap duration. These studies suggest a positive association between napping and cognitive function, particularly in the case of short naps (15–30 minutes vs. no naps) [18, 19]. Longer naps (over 30 minutes) have been associated with worse cognitive functioning [21]. Frequent naps could be reflective of both short sleep duration or poor sleep quality [16, 17], which could contribute to daytime sleepiness. The timing of naps is emerging as an important focus in research. A study of older adults found that more morning naps (measured objectively) are linked to a higher risk of Alzheimer’s disease, as opposed to more early afternoon naps that are linked to reduced 𝛽-amyloid levels [82]. Again, as we did not collect information on timing of naps, we cannot make direct comparisons. Interestingly, while we found a protective association with memory function, we also found that taking 1–2 naps per week (compared to zero) was linked to lower verbal fluency scores. This could reflect the fact that participants who took 1–2 naps per week had lower levels of educational attainment than participants who did not take naps (47% vs 44%; education ≤6th grade). While we controlled for level of educational attainment, residual confounding due to related factors (e.g. educational quality) could be driving the adverse association with verbal fluency scores [83].

Despite significant overlap in estimate of association found in other studies, we underscore the unique nature of our sample. For example, in comparison to a study conducted with Latinos living in urban regions [13], our study had a higher percentage of participants with long (>9 hours) self-reported sleep duration (32% vs 15%). Long sleep duration can be indicative of underlying health conditions [84] and has been associated with elevated levels of inflammatory markers [80] and neurocognitive decline [85]. Indeed, like others, we observed lower memory scores among participants with long (or short) versus average sleep durations. In addition, our study population is 90% overweight or obese based on measured body mass index, compared to 78% of Latina women in the US [86]. High body fat has been associated with poor sleep quality through mechanisms such as compromised airway passages leading to sleep-disordered breathing [87], poor metabolism and endocrine function [88], and reduced physical activity during the daytime [89]. Further, our study population faces unique sleep-related challenges that may impact sleep quality. For example, a total of 48% of participants in our study work in agriculture, which can require long hours, particularly during the harvest season [90], and 41% of participants reported needing to wake up before 6 a.m. to begin their days. In addition, participants in our study face other structural and socio-economic stressors that have been associated with poorer sleep outcomes. Over one-third of participants in our study have household incomes that place them at or below the federal poverty line; another 22% report living in crowded households (defined as living in a house with more than one person per habitable room). Poverty has been strongly associated with poor sleep quality through mediators such as anxiety and stress over financial instability [91], as well as environmental factors at the household- and neighborhood-levels such as overcrowding, noise, and safety [92].

Although the cross-sectional design of this study limits our ability to establish temporality, these findings have important implications for public health practice. The association between sleep and cognitive function in this population suggests that interventions targeting sleep may be effective in improving cognitive outcomes for this population. At the individual level, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia has been shown to be an effective treatment to improve sleep disturbances, however, access remains limited [93].

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we use self-reported questionnaire data for the sleep measures. A recent review found that objective measures of sleep (i.e. electroencephalography spectral analysis, total sleep time and sleep efficiency as measured through polysomnography) and subjective measures of sleep quality are not as closely related as one might expect in healthy individuals, raising concerns about the accuracy of self-reports [94]. Also, our questions on sleep duration focused on time in bed and time awake, as opposed to time actually spent asleep. Our data does not account for the time it takes participants to fall asleep or for nighttime awakenings, and previous research has shown that participants tend to overestimate sleep duration in self-reported questionnaires [95, 96]. Second, recall bias is a notable limitation, as participants could under- or over-estimate their bedtime or awake time based on recent sleep experiences. In a study of Latino adults, correlations between self-reported and actigraphy-recorded sleep were lower when sleep efficiency (i.e. the percentage of time asleep while in bed) was below 85% and when there was night-to-night variability in sleep duration, indicating reduced accuracy of self-reported measures under these conditions [95]. This may result in a differential misclassification and lead to a bias towards the null. Third, as mentioned, our study examined nap frequency and not nap duration, limiting comparison to studies on napping that typically focus on nap duration. Fourth, we cannot exclude the possibility of reverse causation due to preexisting disease or other confounders. The relationships between sleep and 𝛽-amyloid, and sleep and tau accumulation, may be bidirectional as a higher 𝛽-amyloid and tau burden in the brain can also lead to disruption of the sleep cycle [38, 39]. Fifth, we assessed sleep quality through a single question which may not fully capture participants’ sleep quality as comprehensively as validated instruments such as the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. Future studies would benefit from incorporating more detailed sleep quality measures to provide a more nuanced understanding of this relationship. Sixth, our results may not be generalizable to other subgroups (e.g. men, nulliparous women, and women with incomes above 200% poverty level) based on our enrollment criteria. Seventh, we did not collect information around use of sleep medication, which influences sleep architecture and duration [97]. In a subsequent wave of data collection for this study, only 10% of participants reported taking any prescription sleep medication. Lastly, we studied sleep duration and sleep quality, which are practical measures in large cohort studies, but evidence more consistently links obstructive sleep apnea [98], sleep fragmentation [99], and slow wave sleep [100] to ADRD risk. Future studies on this population should focus on studying these measures in relation to cognitive function and decline.

Strengths

Despite these limitations, our study has key strengths. This study fills a critical research gap by exploring the relationship between sleep and cognitive function in Latinas living in a rural agricultural setting. Additionally, our study focuses on middle-aged adults, a crucial period when sleep disturbances can begin to affect long-term cognition. We also used multiple, comprehensive neurocognitive tests that allowed for domain-specific neurocognitive scores in memory, verbal fluency, executive function, and global cognition. Lastly, our findings provide a foundation for conducting longitudinal research to examine the associations between sleep and cognitive decline in this population, which may ultimately contribute to identifying modifiable risk factors and reducing the risk of ADRDs.
