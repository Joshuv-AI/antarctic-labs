Exploring perceived awareness, barriers and enablers of pregnant women towards physical activity in Ethiopian cities: a qualitative study
Authors: Enatfenta Sewmehone Endalew, Gudina Terefe Tucho, Kristoffer Mattisson, Anna Oudin, Eleni Papadopoulou, Aderajew Mekonnen Girmay, Zewditu Abdissa Denu, Muluneh Getachew Garedew, Ibrahim Kedir, Tsedey Moges, Masresha Tessema, Ebba Malmqvist

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccording to the World Health Organization, all healthy pregnant women are recommended to engage in at least 150 min of regular moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week. This level of physical activity is associated with numerous physical and psychological health benefits and helps to reduce the risk of being overweight, gestational diabetes, and preeclampsia. Despite its importance, adherence to the recommended levels of physical activity is low among pregnant women in Ethiopia, particularly in urban areas. Therefore, there is a need for a better understanding of barriers and enablers that affect physical activity levels. Thus, this study explored the perceived awareness, barriers, and enablers that influence pregnant women’s physical activity in their everyday lives.MethodsSeven focus group discussions were conducted with 45 pregnant women who attended routine antenatal care visits at selected health centers in four Ethiopian cities. The data were transcribed and exported to NVivo version 14 software for data management and analysis. An inductive process of thematic analysis was employed, and themes were developed.ResultsMost of the pregnant women had awareness about the health benefits of physical activity. However, some lacked awareness regarding safety precautions and the types of physical activities that can be done during pregnancy. Additionally, some had misconceptions about physical activity throughout the first trimester, believing that it results in abortion. Background

Physical activity refers to any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that requires energy expenditure, for instance during leisure time, transport, or as part of a person’s work or domestic activities [1]. World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that all healthy pregnant women undertake regular physical activity throughout pregnancy [2]. Regular physical activity has numerous maternal and fetal health benefits with no evidence of adverse outcomes for the fetus or the newborn when exercise is performed at mild to moderate intensity [3].

Physical activity during pregnancy is associated with a lower risk for excessive gestational weight gain, risk of pre-eclampsia, gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes, delivery complications, postpartum depression, and fewer newborn complications [2, 4].

Evidence shows that participation in physical activity during pregnancy in Africa is generally low [5].

Despite the importance of physical activity during pregnancy, prior research shows it is also very low among Ethiopian pregnant women. According to a systematic review done in Ethiopia, the pooled prevalence of good antenatal physical exercise practice in the country is 34% [6]. Even so, there is variation between cities, for example only 20.7% of pregnant women in Mekelle [7], 25% in Hawassa city [8], 30% in Gonder [9], 47.2% in southern Ethiopia [10], and 50% in the Harar region were physically active during pregnancy [11].

Physical inactivity is higher in urban areas due to more sedentary jobs among adults and increased screen time spent on mobile devices and television [12]. Physical inactivity in cities is also associated with several built environment factors, such as crowding, high traffic volumes, a lack of parks or green spaces, and lack of footpaths, which collectively reduce opportunities for outdoor physical activity [12–14].

Different studies has mentioned different barriers to physical activity: such as tiredness or lower energy during pregnancy, health related or somatic factors, lack of time and motivation [15, 16], discouragement from family members [17], no exercise habits and fear of miscarriage [18], whereas the enablers included maternal and fetal health benefits, social support [17] and availability of specialized health personnel and policy for physical activity in pregnancy [19].

Although several studies have explored barriers and enablers of physical activity during pregnancy in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), evidence from the Ethiopian context remains limited particularly from urban settings. Sociocultural beliefs, family influences, misconceptions about the safety of physical activity during pregnancy, and health system factors may vary across contexts and influence women’s experiences and behaviors differently. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to explore pregnant women’s perceived awareness, barriers, and enablers to engaging in regular physical activity in urban Ethiopian contexts. The findings of this study will contribute to the development of effective, context-tailored interventions to promote physical activity during pregnancy.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in four Ethiopian cities: Addis Ababa, Adama, Harar and Jimma. The cities have 122 health centers in total, of which 100 are found in Addis Ababa, 13 are in Adama, 5 are in Harar, and 4 are in Jimma. Out of these, 7 health centers were randomly selected, one health center from each city for Adama, Harar and Jimma, and 4 health centers from Addis Ababa. The study was conducted from October 18 to December 18, 2024. This study is conducted within the ENABLE project that aims to reduce the burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) by lowering the lifelong prevalence of major NCD risk factors among pregnant women in urban Ethiopia [20].

Study design

This study employed an exploratory qualitative research design to explore pregnant women’s perceived awareness, barriers and enablers to engaging in physical activity in their everyday lives across four Ethiopian cities.

Sampling strategies and study participants

A purposive sampling technique was employed to recruit participants for focus group discussions, hereafter referred to as focus groups. Pregnant women who attended routine antenatal care (ANC) and resided in the catchment areas of the urban public health centres were eligible for inclusion in the study if they had received at least one ANC service and were 18 years of age or older. Those who consented to be part of the study were included.

Data collection

Data were collected through a focus group discussion (FGD) guide developed by the research team (see supplementary file 1 for the full English version). This was a semi-structured guide that included questions assessing participants background information, awareness of the importance of physical activity, perceptions of barriers and enablers, and physical activity practices. The guide was developed in English and then translated into the two local languages spoken at the study sites, namely Amharic and Affan Oromo. Further, the guide was piloted prior to data collection through one FGD with 6 pregnant women attending routine ANC at a health center in Addis Ababa and iteratively refined within the research team to ensure contextual relevance and clarity. The health center was selected for convenience. Lastly the project members back translated it into English to check for consistency. The data collectors were the research team members, including two postdoctoral fellows and four PhD students with experience in qualitative data collection. Data collectors received training from an expert in qualitative research to ensure consistent interview conduct and adherence to the study protocol.

A total of seven FGDs with 45 pregnant women were conducted face-to-face in conference rooms of the health facilities until the data saturation point was reached. Saturation was determined by the research team members when no new themes or relevant insights emerged from subsequent interviews and the information obtained became repetitive. The focus groups took 60–90 min.

Each focus group was conducted by 2 data collectors: the moderator and the assisting moderator. Whenever possible, at least one female data collector conducted interviews with pregnant women to help ensure participant comfort and openness during discussions. Before the discussion, they welcome the participants and warmly greet them. They introduced themselves and briefly clarified the study’s aims to the participants prior to data collection. The moderators informed all participants that there were no right or wrong answers, encouraged more reserved participants to share their views and ensured that no single participant dominated the discussion. FGDs were recorded using audio recorder, and field notes were taken by the assisting moderator.

Research team and reflexivity

The research team involved in data collection and analysis had backgrounds in maternal health Nursing and public health. They were aware that their professional experiences could influence data collection and interpretation of findings. To address this, the team used semi- structured interview guide to ensure consistency during data collection, and discussions were held throughout the study and analysis.

Data analysis

The audio-recorded FGDs were transcribed by the research team members who collected the data themselves, with assistance from external transcribers. The audio data were transcribed verbatim into English and then exported to NVivo version 14 software for data management and analysis. An inductive thematic analysis process, as described by Braun and Clarke [21], was employed. The analysis began with reading, familiarization, reflection, and annotation of the transcripts to understand their overall content. The first few interviews were coded line by line with collaborative discussion among the research team to develop initial codebook. This codebook was iteratively updated as the team progressed through the different transcripts. Once the codebook was developed, the remaining transcripts were analyzed individually by one researcher. The team had regular discussions to review emerging codes, and any discrepancies in interpretation were resolved through consensus. The codes were then regrouped into similar clusters, with their meanings and patterns of occurrence examined. Using this grouping method, themes were developed to present pregnant women’s perceived awareness, barriers, and enablers to physical activity. Direct quotes were selected to illustrate the interpretations.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from Ethiopian Public Health Institute Institutional review board (reference number: EPHI-IRB-573-2024). Ethical approval for this study was waived by the Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics (REK South-East) in Norway (reference number: 656164) as it was confirmed to be outside the scope of the boards mandate. In addition, all necessary permissions were obtained from the four respective city administration health offices and each health center. All study participants were informed of the study’s purpose and that they could withdraw from the study at any time. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before conducting the discussions. In addition, permission to audio-record conversations was obtained before data collection. Participants’ confidentiality was maintained through the use of codes. All personal identifiers were removed from the transcripts and quoted texts.

Trustworthiness

The credibility, dependability, transferability, and confirmability of the study were insured by different techniques.

To ensure credibility, which refers to confidence in how well data and processes of analysis address the intended focus [ss22], the study gathered data from participants across different age groups, educational status, gestational age, and parity.

Dependability was ensured by including multiple authors in data collection and analysis. We have coded the data in a group and received feedback from additional research team members.

Transferability is ensured by including four cities of Ethiopia, thereby increasing the transferability of the findings.

Detailed methodologies and research processes were used to organize and assist interpretations of the findings. The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) checklist [23] was used to guide the reporting of this study.

Theme 1 pregnant women’s perceived awareness of physical activity

Most of the pregnant women reported several health benefits of physical activity, indicating high awareness, while some had misconceptions and unaware of which activities should be avoided during pregnancy and the safety precautions to take during physical activity.

Subtheme 1: health benefits

Pregnant women mentioned that doing physical activity is important for the general health of both pregnant and non-pregnant women, and it helps to prevent non-communicable diseases like high cholesterol levels, hypertension, diabetes, stress, depression, and difficulties in labour.

“…helps to prevent depression…, diabetes and high blood pressure” (participant, 5).

Furthermore, they also identify the adverse health consequences of sedentary behaviour.

“…if we always sit, our cholesterol level may increase, (which increases the risk of getting) diabetes and hypertension” (participant,16).

Subtheme 2: types of activities

Pregnant women identified activities they believe should be avoided during pregnancy, including strenuous activities such as jumping, lifting heavy objects, and running.

“…there are some activities that are not recommended for pregnant women like jumping….” (participant, 16).

However, several reported that they didn’t know which types of physical activities to do during pregnancy, and they mentioned that they have not received counselling in this regard.

“I don’t know what kinds of activities can be done and what cannot. No one has previously told me” (participant, 17).

Subtheme 3: safety precautions

The participants also mentioned that pregnant women should avoid slippery floors to prevent falls, underscoring the safety precautions to follow when engaging in physical activity.

“When I wash cloths, I walk carefully if there is water on the floor which is slippery and may cause me to fall.” (participant, 19).

Subtheme 4: misconceptions

This study revealed that some of the pregnant women held misconceptions about physical activity. They believed that doing physical activity and driving on cobblestone roads in the 1st trimester was harmful.

“Women in the first trimester are not allowed to do much of physical activity.” (participant,16).

Theme 2 physical activity practice

The study participants reported that their preferred form of physical activity was walking, both for transportation and recreation. They actively chose walking over public transportation for their commute whenever feasible, and later in the evening for refreshment.

“My house isn’t very far from my workplace, and it takes about 30 minutes to walk.” (participant,4).

Additionally, they mentioned that they meet their daily physical activity requirements by doing household chores.

Theme 3 barriers of physical activity

Pregnant women had mentioned health-related, personal, societal and environmental barriers to physical activity.

Subtheme 1: health related

Pregnant women mentioned that their health could be one issue that might be a barrier to being physically active. Pregnant women who have conditions related to pregnancy or other comorbidities tend to refrain from physical activity.

Subtheme 2: personal barriers

Pregnant women who worked at offices mentioned that it is exhausting to do physical activity or go for a walk after long hours of office work.

“I work until 11:30 (5:30 PM in the afternoon), it can be exhausting to walk home after a long day at the office. I am more likely to go home and rest instead of walking. Walking is usually more feasible when I stay at home all day.” (participant, 2).

The respondents also mentioned that a lack of time is one reason they don’t engage in physical activity, despite knowing the benefits.

“…I know (there is separate physical activity) for pregnant, but I didn’t do it due to time constraints.” (participant, 24).

Some women mentioned that a lack of commitment was a barrier. They stated that even if the neighborhood is uncomfortable for those committed to exercising, they might consider going to another park or green space where they can exercise comfortably.

“The neighbourhood may not be comfortable, but we can go to comfortable areas or green areas and walk. Even if it is not comfortable, we can walk carefully if we really want to walk.” (participant,13).

Subtheme 3: societal

The pregnant women mentioned that fear of judgement by society hinders them from going for a walk or being physically active.

“The society sometimes discourages (me) from exercising with comments such as are you still pregnant? How long will you stay pregnant? When will you give birth? Such comments may give a bad feeling…due to fear of such comments, I prefer to stay at home. (participant,13)

The respondents mentioned that their culture doesn’t support being physically active during pregnancy. Pregnant women must walk slowly, and women are not expected to reveal their stomach.

“Regarding our culture, it does not support pregnant women to be physically active” (Participant,13).

While some of them mentioned that it is not a barrier, in that it supports doing household chores and moving around.

“… being physically active is recommended by our culture. They say move around, do household chores.” (participant,16).

Subtheme 4: environmental

Pregnant women also mentioned that lack of paved sidewalk is another barrier.

“I find it challenging because the sidewalk isn’t paved and can be unsafe to walk on at times, so I have to walk slowly.” (participant, 2).

Some pregnant women reported unfavourable weather conditions as a barrier. They avoided outdoor activities during the hottest times of day. To mitigate this, they prioritized walking in the early mornings or after 5 PM when the temperature cooled.

“I prefer to walk when it is not sunny…” (participant,43).

Theme 4 enabling factors of physical activity

The sub-themes identified as facilitators of physical activity were access to green space, partner support, and hearing the experiences of other pregnant women who had previously exercised.

Subtheme 1: social support

The study participants mentioned that having a partner who engages in physical activity is one of the factors that motivate them to be physically active.

“If I stay home, I’m usually involved in household chores. However, in the evening when my husband comes home from work, we go out for a walk.” (participant,4).

Subtheme 2: experiences of other women

The study participants also reported that hearing about the experiences of women who were physically active during their pregnancy motivates them to engage in physical activity.

“…women who have previously given birth also share their experiences about how regular activity helped them.” (participant, 5).

Subtheme 3: presence of green spaces and walking lanes

Pregnant women indicated that new parks and walking lanes are being built in Ethiopia, especially in Addis Ababa and Jimma cities, and this was mentioned as a positive influence on pregnant women’s physical activity.

“There are a few parks around here. There’s Meles Foundation (park), where I can go for fresh air. Also, there’s a recently renovated area around Piazza that anyone can visit.” (participant,4).

Discussion

In this study, we found that Ethiopian pregnant women in urban areas understand the health benefits of doing physical activity and the consequences of sedentary behavior. The study findings are consistent with other studies done in Ibadan, Nigeria, in which the majority of the study participants agreed that physical activity improves general health, enhances sleep, improves, and the labor and delivery process [24]. This may be attributed to the increasing awareness of maternal health and exposure to health information in urban areas.

In contrast the findings of this study are inconsistent with study conducted in Bahir Dar city, North of Ethiopia, which reported that only about half of the pregnant women were knowledgeable about exercise during pregnancy [25]. This discrepancy may be explained by the difference in the study setting and methodologies. Most of the participants in the present study were from Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia, where access to health information and awareness may be higher. In addition, the current study employed a qualitative approach which allows for in-depth exploration of participants perspectives, while the other used a quantitative method using a structured questionnaire with predefined response options, which may limit participants’ ability to fully express their understanding. Furthermore, Majority of the study participants in the current study had high school and above level of education; whereas one third of participants in the other study had only primary level of education, which may have contributed to the lower level of knowledge reported.

We also found that most of the pregnant women had limited awareness of safety precautions for physical activity, mentioning being careful on slippery floors to prevent falls. They did not mention other WHO-recommended safety precautions to be taken while pregnant, such as avoiding physical activity during excessive heat, especially with high humidity, staying hydrated by drinking water before, during, and after physical activity, and avoiding activities in the supine position, especially after the first trimester of pregnancy [2].

Despite their awareness on the importance of physical activity and the consequences of sedentary behaviour, there were some women who were unaware of the types of physical activities to do and with misconceptions, similarly the qualitative study done in Sri Lanka mentioned that majority of the mothers realized the importance but, they had inadequate knowledge about the exercises and some women strongly trust that exercises are not good in the first 3 months of pregnancy [26]. This might be due to traditional beliefs and lack of adequate exposure to structured counseling related to physical activity during ANC follow ups.

The study participants reported that their preferred form of physical activity was walking for transportation and recreation. This finding is in line with a quantitative study conducted in Hawassa, Ethiopia, which found that walking was practiced by 95.9% of physically active study participants [27]. This might be due to limited awareness of other safe types of physical activities that can be done during pregnancy, and walking is simple, socially acceptable, and easy to incorporate into their daily routines.

Some of the pregnant women in the present study mentioned engaging in physical activity to complete household tasks. This finding is similar to other studies that found pregnant women mentioned that they were involved in rigorous household activities [19]. This may be due to time constraints and existing cultural expectations that require pregnant women to engage in domestic activities.

In this study all pregnant women mentioned that illness is one of the barriers to engaging in physical activity. This finding is supported by a study done in Nigeria, where ill health or comorbid conditions in pregnancy were considered as barriers [19]. This might be explained by women’s perception that physical activity could exacerbate existing symptoms, leading women to refrain from physical activity during pregnancy or during periods of illness.

Some respondents mentioned that a shortage of time and a lack of commitment are one of the reasons they don’t engage in physical activity despite knowing the benefits. Previous studies also show a lack of time due to family responsibilities [15, 28], a lack of motivation to exercise [29], feeling exhausted, and having low energy that is not enough to engage in physical activity are barriers [16]. This may be explained by the demanding household and caregiving responsibilities as well as pregnancy-related discomforts, which collectively reduce women’s perceived ability and motivation to prioritize physical activity in their daily routines.

Some of the pregnant women mentioned that their culture is not supportive of physical activity for pregnant women. This finding is consistent with a study done in Bahir Dar, Ethiopia, in which only 12.6% of mothers agreed that exercise during pregnancy is suitable for their culture [25]. Some pregnant women also mentioned that culture is not a barrier. This might be due to Ethiopia’s diverse nations and nationalities with different cultures.

Pregnant women also mentioned environmental-related issues, such as lack of paved sidewalks and unfavourable weather conditions, as a barrier. This finding is consistent with a study done in Zimbabwe, which stated the exercise environment as a salient perceived barrier [30]. This might be explained by a lack of accessible walking lanes, as well as weather-related discomfort which can reduce their motivation and perceived safety to engage in outdoor physical activity during pregnancy.

This study found out that having a partner’s support and hearing experience of other physically active women are facilitators. This finding is supported by a study done in Tehran, Iran, which reported that enrolling husbands in prenatal classes and hearing the experiences of women who were physically active during their pregnancy were also facilitators [31].This may be due to partners emotional support and encouragement, and reassurance from peers can reduce fear and normalize engagement in physical activity.

While several findings of the study are consistent with earlier qualitative studies, this study contributes to existing literature by providing context-specific insights into cultural norms, misconceptions about exercise safety, social support, societal and environmental barriers to physical activity during pregnancy that are particularly relevant to urban Ethiopian context. Additionally, the findings of this study informed a subsequent randomized controlled trial aimed at assessing behavioral change following counselling on physical activity during pregnancy.

Strengths and limitations

The present study has several limitations. First the findings are based on a small number of pregnant women selected from urban areas and may therefore not fully represent the experiences of all pregnant women in Ethiopia, particularly those in rural areas. Second, since the data were collected through FGDs, participants’ responses may have been influenced by group dynamics, which could have limited the expression of divergent opinions and introduced social desirability bias [32]. Third, there is a potential for selection bias, as participants were recruited from ANC clinics. Women who attend ANC services may have greater health awareness than those who don’t. Therefore, the findings may not fully represent the perspectives of all pregnant women in the community. Fourth, the data were collected with other non-communicable diseases (NCDs) risk factors, such as unhealthy diet and exposure to air pollution, which might make pregnant women to lose focus in one of the risk factors. Fifth, since it is a qualitative study, some degree of subjectivity might be introduced; although efforts were made to ensure reflexivity, complete elimination of bias is not possible [32].

Despite these limitations, the study’s inclusion of pregnant women from four different Ethiopian cities increases the transferability of the results to other similar settings and provides an insightful, context-specific understanding of pregnant women’s perceived awareness, barriers, and enablers in urban Ethiopian settings. The results will be utilized to develop counselling intervention messages on physical activity that will be given to pregnant women in ANC clinics.

Conclusions

This study concludes that most pregnant women were aware of the importance of physical activity and the consequences of sedentary behavior.

Health conditions, occupation, time constraints, lack of commitment, fear of judgement, culture, walkability of the neighborhood, and weather condition (specifically hot weather) were identified as barriers, and having access to green space, partner support, and experience sharing from pregnant women who had been exercising previously were identified as enablers of physical activity during pregnancy. Culture was ambiguous, with some seeing it as a barrier and others as an enabler.

These findings can inform policy makers in prioritizing integration of physical activity promotion into routine maternal health services. By addressing identified barriers, policy makers can create more enabling conditions for women to engage in safe, regular physical activity during pregnancy, which will contribute to reducing NCDs and improving maternal well-being and pregnancy outcomes.

Health care providers working in ANC clinics can provide counseling on physical activity to pregnant women as part of routine ANC services.
