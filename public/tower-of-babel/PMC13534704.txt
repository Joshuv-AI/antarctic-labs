Evaluation of CNS xenograft brain tumour response to MRI-guided focused ultrasound in combination with radiation therapy
Authors: Deepa Sharma, Evan McNabb, Benjamin Geraghty, Colleen Bailey, Murtuza Saifuddin, Wenyi Yang, Anoja Giles, Cassie Lim, Peter Albanese, Martin Stanisz, Arjun Sahgal, Gregory J. Czarnota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn recent years, treating solid tumours with focused ultrasound (FUS) has emerged as a novel therapeutic technique because of its noninvasive nature. Recent work has demonstrated the ability of focused ultrasound-stimulated microbubble (FUS + MB) treatments to enhance radiation effects on tumours significantly. In this study, a rabbit model of brain metastasis was used to evaluate the therapeutic effect of FUS + MB-based therapy and radiation therapy (XRT).MethodsExperiments were performed with brain-tumour bearing rabbits generated using human prostate cancer xenografts (PC3). Animals were randomized into four groups: control (untreated), FUS + MB alone, XRT alone, and a combined therapy (FUS + MB + XRT). Single treatment regimens and multiple-treatment regimens were evaluated with single-dose and fractionated radiotherapy, respectively. Tumour response was evaluated 24 hours and for up to 1 week after treatment to evaluate longitudinal responses.ResultsTumour cell death and vascular damage was found to be minimal within 24 hour following treatment. However, results obtained following 1 week of multiple treatments demonstrated that combined treated tumour xenografts exhibited a greater extent of cellular and vascular damage confirmed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and factor VIII immunohistochemical staining, respectively. Lesser number of proliferative cells were also observed in the combined treated group as compared to the other groups. Additionally, significant increase in acid sphingomyelinase (ASMase) staining was observed following a combined treatment of FUS + MB and XRT confirming the involvement of ASMase/ceramide pathway in enhanced tumour response.ConclusionThese results indicate enhancement of radiation effect to CNS-based tumours through ultrasound-stimulated microbubbles.

Introduction

Over the years, cancer survival rates have increased imminently. Advancement in technology to treat cancer has led to a successful cure for cancer. However, treatment failure in cancer patients has been reported frequently and the primary cause for this is due to cancer metastasis. It is estimated that 90% of deaths from cancer are a result of metastases [1]. Almost all types of cancer have the potential to metastasize. Brain metastases, by far the most fatal, are estimated to occur in 24-45% of all cancer patients. Treatment options for brain metastases often include radiation therapy (XRT), chemotherapy, immunotherapy, corticosteroids or antiepileptic medications, gamma knife surgery, and stereotactic radiosurgery [2–7]. Despite the significant advances in treatment of modality for brain metastases, prognosis remains poor due to the complex heterogeneity of the brain microenvironment. Out of several biological aspects, the role of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) in the management of brain metastasis remains ultra-challenging. In contrast to the normal BBB, the blood–tumour barrier (BTB) which is often a result of abnormal blood vessels formed within tumours causes hindrance in uptake and absorption of chemotherapeutic agents. Thus, several mechanisms have been proposed to overcome the obstacle of chemotherapy delivery across the BBB-BTB in primary and metastatic brain tumours [8], one of which includes a focused ultrasound -combined with microbubbles (FUS + MB) mediated disruption [9, 10].

MB are tiny bubbles that range between the sizes of 1-10 μm in diameter. MB in contact with ultrasound energy undergoes several series of contraction and expansion, inducing a phenomenon of cavitation [11]. Two types of cavitation, namely stable and inertial, take place depending upon the ultrasound pressure applied. The former occurs upon low ultrasound pressure, whereas the latter occurs upon high ultrasound pressure [12]. FUS + MB is a noninvasive, innovative technique that uses focused ultrasound waves and circulating bubbles to disrupt the tumour. Using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in conjunction with FUS + MB ensures real-time guidance, allowing precise usage of ultrasound energy with stimulated MB to the targeted area without harming healthy tissue [13]. Preclinical findings suggest FUS in conjunction with MB is highly effective for the non-invasive and regional opening of the BBB that allows drug uptake to the targeted area through a process of cavitation [14]. In the past few years, cavitation-induced histotripsy has emerged as a non-invasive and non-thermal treatment option for the brain in preclinical studies [15]. This technique uses high-focused ultrasound pulses to induce acoustic cavitation to destroy the targeted area into acellular debris. A recent study by Choi et al. studied the impact of histotripsy treatment on mice bearing brain tumours or mice without tumours [16]. The study assesses the correlation between MRI radiomics features and histological analysis. Their results demonstrated that the histotripsy ablated area appeared to be on T2 and T2* MR images, suggesting the correlation of MRI images with histology might be useful in transcranial histotripsy brain therapy. [16]. Another non-thermal treatment option for preclinical brain models includes transcranial MRI-guided focused ultrasound (TcMRgFUS) which uses ultrasound waves to precisely create a lesion within the area [17]. This study used rhesus macaques to treat the skull base using focused ultrasound operating at 220 kHz; sonicated for 5 minutes with MB. Their results showed that it is feasible to use this technique to ablate the brain’s area by inducing inertial cavitation; however, if parameters are not controlled precisely or when inertial cavitation is surpassed, tissue damage is seen [17].

A study by Arvanitis et al. demonstrated a seven-fold greater uptake of the chemotherapeutic agent doxorubicin by the endothelial cell in BT474-Gluc brain tumour mouse model when FUS was used in combination with MB [10]. Another study by Park et al. utilized MRI-FUS + MB to evaluate tumour response in a breast cancer brain metastases xenograft model [18]. Rats inoculated with HER2/neu-positive human breast cancer cells (BT474) receiving FUS + MB with trastuzumab showed that out of ten animals, four demonstrated complete disappearance of tumour. In addition, this combined treatment resulted in higher survival animal rates as compared to control-grouped animals, suggesting FUS + MB can be used for improving treatment outcomes in breast cancer brain metastases [18].

There has been a plethora of data suggesting the effectiveness of MRI-FUS + MB regarding the drug uptake in brain tumours via the opening of the BBB, resulting in enhanced tumour cure; however, very few studies have explored the effects of FUS + MB and XRT in BBB opening. A study by Chen et al. investigated the effects of FUS + MB and XRT in BBB opening and evaluated tumour response following these combined treatments [19]. Their preclinical study carried out with the glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) model demonstrated improved tumour control and enhanced survival rates following a combination of treatment of FUS + MB and XRT as compared to single radiation treatment. In addition, a clinical study performed on six patients with recurrent malignant high- grade glioma showed a rapid disease progression in three patients, while another three patients demonstrated stable disease after multiple treatments of FUS + MB and XRT [19]. In another study by Fletcher et al. a healthy rat brain and F98 glioma model were used to investigate the effects of FUS + MB and XRT on tumour response [20]. A combined treatment of FUS + MB and XRT demonstrated a significant reduction in tumour volume by 45-57% with histology analysis showing a significant increase in cell death and vessel damage by 396% and 336%, respectively, compared to single modality treatments [20].

In the present study, we attempt to treat prostate cancer brain metastasis model in rabbits using MRI-FUS + MB following radiation treatment. XRT is one of the most used treatment modalities for brain metastases. The treatment plan for whole-brain radiation is highly individualized, and several aspects are considered to minimize neurotoxicity. However, using high-dose radiation to treat brain metastases usually leaves patients with long-term side effects including neurocognitive dysfunction [21]. Treatment that might enhance overall survival and elevate the patient’s quality of life is of utmost importance. In this study, we intend to establish a rabbit brain metastasis model and explore the safe applicability of MRI-FUS + MB and XRT in the implanted tumours. Our results demonstrated the successful implantation of a metastatic brain tumour in rabbits. Tumour response assessed within 24 hours where animals were treated only once demonstrated no significant changes in tumour cell death, vascularity, and proliferation index. Furthermore, animals receiving multiple treatments (for a week) of FUS + MB + XRT showed increased tumour cell death, reduced vascularity and cellular proliferation confirmed using histopathology analysis. An increase in acid sphingomyelinase (ASMase) staining was seen in the combined group treated multiple times, confirming the involvement of the ASMase/ceramide pathway in tumour response following FUS + MB + XRT.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human prostate cancer cells (PC3) from American Type Culture Collections (ATCC CRL-1435, Manassas, VA, USA) were cultured and maintained in RPMI1640 medium from Multicell (cat# 350–000) containing 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Gibco 15140) and 10% fetal bovine serum (Hyclone, characterized, GE Life Sciences). Cells were then incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 conditions to reach confluence. Afterward, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and trypsinized using 0.05% Trypsin-EDTA at room temperature. Subsequently, the detached cells were collected and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 7 minutes at 4°C. Pellets were isolated and re-suspended by adding 100 µL of Mg + /Ca+ Dulbecco’s Phosphate Buffered Saline (DPBS) per 5 ×106 cells. Tumour viability was assessed using trypan blue for quantification. Cells were diluted immediately preceding intracranial injection at a ratio of 50:50 Matrigel for a final concentration of 1.0 ×106 cells/20 µL.

Animal model

Animal work completed in this study was approved by the Sunnybrook Research Institute Animal Care Committee. New Zealand White rabbits (Charles River, Canada) were intracranially injected with a cell suspension of approximately 1.0 ×106 cells to induce tumours. After cell injection, the tumours were left to grow for approximately 2 weeks, or until visible tumours were seen in MRI scans before their respective treatments. The rabbits were injected intramuscularly with cyclosporin A (10–25 mg/kg daily) for three days including the day of cell injection, to induce immunosuppression (for details see [22]). Before cell injection and treatment administration, rabbits were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane and 2 L/min of O2 through continuous inhalation. The body temperature of animals during treatment was kept normal using heating pads. Vitals were monitored throughout the entirety of the treatment.

Group size was determined by the number of conditions tested and number of animals requested per condition for us to see a real difference in the treatment.

All our current and previous studies (published) use a minimum of 3-4 rabbits per group/condition for data analysis.

To ensure the statistical power (1 - β) > 0.8, minimum experimental group sizes were calculated (n = 3-4) from the minimum effect size in animal models at the selected therapy parameters (this is based on our previous study).

Animals were randomly assigned to each treatment group by an expert senior technician. Ear notching was used to identify animals. To avoid selection bias, the technician was unaware of which animals were assigned to which groups (blinded).

Animals that reached humane endpoint during the experimental period was euthanized. Humane endpoints followed in this study included animal's weight loss greater than 20%, lack of feeding, bleeding, or ulcerations of the tumour area, lack of ambulation, and self-mutilation.

Tumour cell injection/implantation

For the preparation of intracranial injection, rabbits were first anesthetized using 5% isoflurane in oxygen for induction and 3% for maintenance (all anesthetics were obtained from manufacturer (Fresenius Kabi, DIN 02237518)). Furthermore, 2% of oxygen was given in a continuous inhalant form. Rabbit heart rate and blood oxygen levels were monitored with a pulse oximeter. Rabbits were under complete anesthetic while being intubated with a 2.5 mm endotracheal tube (Teleflex). Afterward, eye lubrication Systane Ointment (Alcon, DIN 02444062) was applied, and the animal was gently secure to a stereotactic apparatus. Once a rabbit was in the apparatus, the skull was exposed by shaving the top of the head with an electric razor. Marcaine (stock concentration 2.5 mg/ml) was added (few drops to site of surgery (before surgery)) atop the exposed section, and a 2 cm sagittal cut was made along the midline of the head to expose the skull. Coordinates for injection were mapped using the bregma as the reference point (AP = 3.0 mm; ML = 4.0 mm, DV = 8 mm). Once the point was marked, a small burr hole was drilled at the potential injection site to expose the brain. For injection, a 26 Gauge Hamilton syringe (Hamilton Co., Reno Nevada) was used to inoculate 20 µL of 106 PC3 cells (1:1 mixture of cells and Matrigel at a rate of approximately 1 µL/min into the site of interest). After injection, the needle was kept in place for 5 min and then was withdrawn slowly over another 1 mm/min. The skull was cleaned and dried using a sterile dry cotton swab (Medline). The hole in the skull was then covered with bone wax, and cut skin was sutured back together with 5–0 silk sutures. For postoperative pain relief and to prevent bacterial infections in animals, buprenorphine (0.3 mg/ml concentration, given 0.1 mg/kg, as necessary, Vetergesic, Ceva) and Baytril (Enrofloxacin, 50 mg/ml bottle, DIN 02169428, Bayer Inc. Dose: 5 mg/kg) were administered respectively three days via subcutaneous injection at doses, respectively. Animal incisions were assessed post-operatively to ensure that sutures remain intact until they are fully healed. Tumour growth was monitored every week by a 3 T Bruker MRI (Achieva, Philips Healthcare, Best, Netherlands)) using gadolinium contrast (Gadovist 1.0, DIN 02241089, Bayer Inc. (3 ml gadolinium chased by 4 ml saline)).

Animals cohort and treatment regimen

Animals were divided into two cohorts: a 24 hour cohort (in which treatment was administered only once following tumour response assessed at 24 hours) and a multifraction 1- week cohort (in which treatment was administered thrice during a one week period, specifically on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, with tumour response assessed at 72 hours following the last treatment day). All treatment parameters were kept the same for 24 hours and a 1-week study. A total of 3-6 animals were used in each of the treatment conditions for 24 hours and 1 week (control, FUS + MB, XRT, and FUS + MB + XRT). A treatment plan for 24 hours and a one-week study is presented in Fig. 2.

Ultrasound-mediated microbubble treatment

Rabbits treated with FUS + MB were under complete anesthetic before hair above the tumour region was removed using a 50:50 ratio of Nair lotion (Nair, TM Trademark of/ MC Marque de commerce de Church & Dwight Co Inc). Definity® MB (perfluoropropane gas/liposome shell, Lantheus Medical Imaging, Inc., North Billerica, MA, USA) were used in this study. A Vialmix ® device (Lantheus Medical Imaging, Inc., North Billerica, MA, USA) was used to activate MB by shaking at 3000 rpm for 45 seconds. MB once prepared was placed in the tubing attached to the catheter. Animals (~ 2 Kg) received MB (2 mL Definity MB plus 4 mL saline (total 6 mL), corresponding to a total dose of approximately 9 × 10^9 MB/kg, or 0.75 mL/kg. A power injector was used for injecting the MB. This was delivered at 1ml/min during ultrasound treatment, that usually lasted for 6-7 minutes. Ultrasound exposure included 16 cycles of a tone burst over 50 milliseconds. The peak acoustic pressure was set to approximately 0.1MPa. For the FUS + MB only and combined treated groups, the FUS + MB treatment was administered on both Monday and Friday of the treatment week. Ultrasound treatment was delivered using a FUS LP-100 system with transducer frequency working at 1.1 MHz (Focused Ultrasound Instruments, Toronto, Canada) in a 3 T MRI system. The transducer was submerged in degassed deionized water and attached to a FUS system. During the treatments, rabbits were anesthetized with isoflurane and intubated. They were positioned supine on top of an MRI-compatible FUS system, with a catheter/stopcock attached to tubing and a syringe for an automatic power injector, for delivery of contrast agent and/or MB. A catheter was inserted into the ear vein with a four-way stopcock secured by tape. The pulse oximeter was secured to the toe to monitor oxygen and heart rate. MRI guided the ultrasound-mediated microbubble treatment. T1-weighted MR imaging was used to identify targets in the brain along with gadolium-based contrast. A custom miniature brain coil (a single-loop linearly polarised transmit/receive custom design for rabbits) was used in place for treatments.

Radiation therapy

Rabbits were anesthetized during radiation procedures. For irradiation rabbits were immobilized using tape to an animal holder which was then placed in an x-ray irradiator (Faxitron Cabinet X-ray, Faxitron X-ray, LLC, IL) with a lead sheet covering the entire body except for the brain area where the tumour were located as confirmed by MRI (a skin marking was made in the brain tumour area). Lead sheets contained a small circular opening (2.5 cm diameter) that allowed exposure to the tumour-bearing region during treatment. Animals were treated on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday of the treatment week. Irradiator settings were as follows: 160 kV, 6.3 mA for 3 minutes for a dosage of 6 Gy per treatment day for a total dose of 6 Gy for the single treatment cohort and for the multiple treatment cohort a total of 18 Gy in three fractions.

Histology

At the end of the studies (24 hours and 1 week), animals were euthanized under anesthesia with intravenous sodium pentobarbitol (Euthanyl) via the ear vein immediately at endpoints and the brain was removed and fixed in 10% (v/v) neutral buffered formalin (10% formalin in sodium phosphate buffer (0.075 M)) (Fisher Scientific (Fisher Chemicals), SF93-4 (4 L)). Three days thereafter, each specimen was cut into approximately three sections (labeled as A, B, and C) and placed into cassettes. The cassettes were put into buffered formalin (10% (v/v) at room temperature for another four days before further processing. Afterwards, cassettes were embedded in paraffin and sectioned for staining. All sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to determine the tumour area/location and size. Once the tumour area/location was determined, corresponding factor VIII staining, a vascular disruption marker, Ki-67 staining, a cell proliferation marker, and ASMase, a cell stress marker was carried out using immunohistochemistry. For factor VIII quantification, from each stained section, 11 random regions of interest (ROIs) were selected at 20X magnification, and the stained blood vessels within the confirmed tumour area were counted and averaged. The proliferative fraction was quantified from digitized Ki-67 image files using an in-house MATLAB program (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Quantification of ASMase staining was performed using QuPath (v0.5.1), an open-source bioimage analysis software [23]. ASMase staining was carried out using Invitrogen ASM monoclonal Antibody (OTI3H7) (Product # MA5-26615) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For this, ROIs were manually annotated using either default or marker-specific optimized parameters. All the staining was performed at Pathology Research Program, University Health Network, Toronto, ON, Canada). Images of whole mount tumour stains were acquired at 20X magnification, resulting in a resolution of 0.5 µm/pixel using the TissueScope LE scanner (Huron Digital Pathology, St. Jacobs, ON, Canada).

Statistics

A one-way ANOVA followed by Sidak selected comparison statistical test was performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc, La Jolla, USA). Each treatment condition was compared with the untreated control group. Each group was also tested against the others for statistical significance. A significant P-value was indicated by *P ≤ 0.05.

Discussion

This study aimed to achieve the objective of treating a metastatic brain tumour model with single or multiple treatments of FUS + MB and XRT and assess the tumour response within 24 hours or 1 week, respectively. The establishment of the brain metastasis model here can have significance for research lending its applicability to clinical settings as a preclinical model. In this study, a rabbit brain metastasis model was first established by inoculating human prostate cancer cells (PC3) in the right hemisphere of the rabbit brain using stereotactic neurosurgical procedures. This was carried out using a mixture of cells and Matrigel injected at a pre-defined location using a three-dimensional coordinate system. We were able to successfully implant brain metastasis tumours in 60 plus rabbits. The procedure was minimally invasive and found to be useful for preclinical model generation. Rabbit weight and behavior were monitored and recorded every day to assure animal vitality. The protocol here, to our knowledge, represents the first in enabling rabbit models of metastatic brain tumours and exposing them to various treatments.

Several studies have successfully demonstrated the implantation of glioblastoma in various rodent models, since it is easier for brain cells to grow in the brain [24–27]. Recently, a study by Li et al. showed successful implantation of rabbit liver metastasis as confirmed by computed tomography (CT). For this, the VX2 tumour was implanted into the right hind limb muscle of the rabbit and once the tumour was developed, tumour was removed from the hind limb muscle of the rabbit, and its suspension was made for further experiments. The presence of tumour cells was assessed using H&E staining [28]. A prior study by Gregor et al. showed successful implantation of VX2 lung tumour models forming early nodal metastases in rabbits. For this, VX2 tumour cells were injected in rabbit hind limb muscle followed by CT imaging performed to detect the lymph node after which the lymph node was dissected and stained with H&E and cytokeratin AE1/AE3 immunohistochemistry for metastasis confirmation [29]. The work here demonstrates the use of human tumour model in rabbit brain in contrast to rabbit work which to date has mostly relied on the leporine VX2 tumour model.

The major component of our study investigated whether single or multiple treatments with FUS + MB, XRT, or FUS + MB + XRT could enhance radiation treatment tumour effect in a rabbit brain tumour model using a human prostate cancer line. The histological findings in the study here suggest that tumour response was more prominent than expected with multiple treatments of FUS + MB + XRT as compared to tumour response assessed acutely after one treatment. At 24 hours, an increase in tumour cell death was detected however, no significant reduction in vascular density and cell proliferation was observed. Whereas treating animals for a week with multiple treatments demonstrated a greater tumour cell death with vascular density and cellular proliferation rate reduced significantly as compared to untreated control. Previous studies have demonstrated enhanced tumour response within 24 hours following FUS + MB and XRT using an in vivo xenograft model [30]. However, in those studies, the tumour was induced mainly in the hind limbs of animals. Most of the in vivo studies conducted with brain tumour were mainly focused on testing the feasibility and safety of FUS and MB. In other work the opening of the BBB using FUS and MB to deliver chemotherapeutics drugs has gained attraction over the past few years and scientists and clinicians have been successful in achieving this treatment effect. However, treatment response enhancement using radiation to treat brain tumours has still not been fully explored. There have been important findings that suggest an increase in tumour response following FUS combined with other treatment modalities. A longitudinal study was conducted by Alkins et al. using athymic nude rats bearing brain metastatic tumour induced by injecting human HER2-expressing MDA-MB-231 breast tumour cells. Tumours exposed to FUS, and MB treatment showed improved long-term animal survival. No signs of hemorrhage within the surrounding brain tumour area were reported [31]. Another study conducted with a rat C6 intracranial glioma tumour model demonstrated enhanced tumour response following FUS and sonodynamic therapy (with 5-Aminolevulinic Acid). Increased tumour growth inhibition and survival with increased tumour cell death and less proliferation were observed with combination of FUS and sonodynamic therapy [26].

It is worth noting that from our 1-week results, XRT administered 3 times a week with a total dose of 18 Gy was less effective compared to when it was combined with FUS + MB. A decreasing pattern in microvessel counts and proliferative fraction can be seen with XRT alone; however, it was not significant. A reverse pattern can be seen in ASMase staining. The idea of administering multiple fractions instead of a single dose was to reduce dose toxicity and to use XRT as a radiosensitizer by first sensitizing the tumour with FUS + MB, followed by XRT administration to maximize tumour effects. The goal was to use fractionations like those used clinically for tumours rather than a single large dose. Also, it is important to highlight that the radiation delivered in several doses, 6 Gy three times for a total of 18 Gy, may alter how the tumour responds compared to the radiation dose administered as a single 18 Gy dose.

The actual mechanism involved in enhanced tumour response following FUS treatment is still unclear and likely dependent on ultrasound pressure and frequency parameters, exposure duration, and the use of MB contrast agents. One of the well-studied mechanisms known to cause tumour vasculature damage is the production of ceramide that takes place due to endothelial cell membrane perturbation following ultrasound and MB treatments. This effect is known to be synergistically higher when ultrasound and MB treatment is combined with XRT [32, 33]. Previous studies have shown that the combined treatment of ultrasound and MB plus radiation causes activation of endothelial ASMase, leading to ceramide release, a lipid cell death signaling molecule [34, 35]. Once released, these ceramide molecules can stimulate various other cell death cascades, causing tumour vascular damage [32]. In this study, we investigate whether a similar mechanism of ASMase/ceramide activation is responsible for brain tumour response in rabbits. For this, we used ASMase as a marker for cell stress. ASMase is a pivotal enzyme that hydrolyzes sphingomyelin into ceramide and phosphorylcholine [36]. It resides primarily within lysosomes that, upon various stress stimuli such as irradiation, hypoxia, hyperthermia, interleukin 1ß, tumour necrosis factor, and various antitumour treatments, etc. can translocate to plasma membrane [37]. Once translocated, it can form ceramide, which acts as a second messenger, transmitting a cell death signal inside the cells [38–41]. Here in this study, we showed that treating tumour xenografts multiple times with radiation alone or USMB alone was able to generate notable ASMase compared to the control, untreated group. However, a combined treatment of FUS + MB and radiation administered multiple times caused a significant enhancement of ASMase in brain tumour tissue. Extensive work by Czarnota and group has shown that high doses of radiation ( ≥ 8 Gy) alone can cause a significant amount of ASMase-mediated ceramide that leads to vascular damage and tumour cell death. Similarly, combining low-dose radiation with ultrasound and MB can also cause a similar release of ceramide, mediating tumour vascular destruction [33]. Here, in this study, we combined 6 Gy with FUS + MB to treat a rabbit model of brain metastasis. The use of a lower dose of radiation combined with ultrasound and MB can be useful in clinical settings, as fewer to no side effects of radiation might occur when treating brain tumour patients in future. Even though the results here are promising, this study has some limitations, one of which is the variability in tumour size and shape. The difference in tumour’s size and shape could be due to cell invasion and migration in different parts of the brain. Although the cell injection procedures in all animals were consistent throughout the study, this limitation was not overcome. In addition, the small group sizes used in the study may have resulted in the failure to detect a small tumour effect, as seen mainly in the 24-hour group. Animal response variability was noted within the 24 hour- and 1-week control groups, which may have been due to the different stages of tumour growth that could be attributed to cell cultures initiated on different days. Next, the prostate cancer cell model used may reflect metastatic disease being derived from a bony metastasis with small cell-like features, but primary brain tumours behave differently. Responses are linked to the vascular content of tumours so compensation for that may need to be based on tumour type. Also, PC3, which is a derived bone metastatic cell used in this study, may not fully and accurately represent the biology of prostate cancer that has metastasized the brain. The bone microenvironment of PC3 cells is different from the brain microenvironment, which may alter cancer behavior, leading to variable therapeutic responses. Moreover, as well as in this case with a thin rabbit skull, no major corrections needed to be made for deposition of ultrasound energy and focus. However, for treatments with humans, corrections for skull shape, size, and density will have to be made. The data presented in this study are preliminary, and translation of results to clinical trials will be challenging. The FUS + MB technique used in treating rabbit tumour here provides an important outlook for understanding tumour mechanisms; however, using the same method/ parameters for treating sensitive deep-brain human tumours will be difficult. Targeting human deep-brain tumours will require careful planning with highly precise ultrasound parameters. The uncertainty and obstruction in translating preclinical experiments to clinical settings can’t be overlooked [42–44]. Furthermore, a more thorough study of FUS + MB + XRT, including tumour growth assessment over time and survival studies, is needed to evaluate if the treatment benefits can be sustained over the course of a lifetime. Lastly, long-term studies are required to determine clinical targets.

Conclusions

In this study, we established the rabbit model of brain metastasis. New Zealand white rabbits bearing prostate (PC3) metastatic tumour received single or multiple treatments of either FUS + MB, XRT; 6 Gy, or a combination of both treatments (FUS + MB + XRT). Results demonstrated an enhanced tumour response following a combination of FUS + MB + XRT given multiple times a week compared to the single modality treatments. Increased tumour cell death, reduced vascularity, and lesser tumour proliferative fraction were observed validating the treatment efficacy. Furthermore, higher ASMase staining was observed in the combined treated group of FUS + MB and radiation, which can be a leading factor for inhibition of angiogenesis and tumour cell death.
