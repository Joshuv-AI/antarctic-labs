<h3 class="text-align-center"><strong>The Discovery of Pakistan’s Secret Nuclear Program: Implications for U.S. Policy, 1978-1979</strong></h3>

<p class="text-align-center"><strong>By William Burr</strong></p>

<p>During January 1979, U.S. intelligence startled State Department officials when they learned that the Government of Pakistan had secretly initiated a uranium enrichment program using gas centrifuge technology. Pakistan’s objective was a capability to support a nuclear weapons program. A January 1979 telegram published for the first time by the National Security Archive includes a statement by Assistant Secretary of State for Oceans, Environmental, and International Scientific Affairs Thomas R. Pickering that Pakistan was “moving more rapidly toward acquisition of nuclear capability than we had earlier estimated.’</p>for Oceans, Environmental, and International Scientific Affairs Thomas R. Pickering that Pakistan was “moving more rapidly toward acquisition of nuclear capability than we had earlier estimated.’</p>

<p>President Jimmy Carter came to office as a strong opponent of nuclear proliferation, but Pakistan posed a thorny challenge to his administration. In a late January 1979 telegram to Embassies in India and Pakistan, senior State Department officials wrote of the “acute dilemma” posed by the Pakistani nuclear program because “we wish to be more supportive” of Islamabad. With revolution in Iran and growing Soviet influence in Afghanistan, the Carter administration worried that pushing too hard on proliferation concerns could alienate Pakistan – a regional counterweight to Soviet partner India – from the United States and produce greater instability in the region. While not abandoning nonproliferation interests, policymakers gave greater priority to geopolitical and Cold War concerns which they doubled down on after the Soviets invaded Afghanistan later in 1979.</p>not abandoning nonproliferation interests, policymakers gave greater priority to geopolitical and Cold War concerns which they doubled down on after the Soviets invaded Afghanistan later in 1979.</p>

<p>The new intelligence that was coming in indicated that Pakistan had plans to build a uranium enrichment plant with 7,000 gas centrifuges and a smaller plant with a 64-unit cascade. Those were references to the large plant underway at Kahuta and a pilot plant under way at Sihala, both of which are outside of Islamabad. The activities at Kahuta and Sihala, among other sites, were under the direction of A.Q. Khan, the Belgian-educated metallurgist, who had stolen major elements of gas centrifuge technology when he worked in the Netherlands for a contractor to the Anglo-Dutch-German consortium URENCO. Under suspicion, Khan returned to Pakistan in late 1975, where he began to draw upon his European connections to acquire sensitive technology for the nuclear program. It is not known precisely when U.S. and other intelligence agencies had Khan in their sights, but it was certainly by the summer of 1978. A statement made to U.S. officials by a Dutch Foreign Ministry official in early February 1979 that A. Q. Khan “had returned to Europe for [a] time after leaving the Netherlands and had been based in Antwerp” may be the earliest declassified reference to Khan in the declassified record.<a href="#_edn1" name="_ednref1">[1]</a></p>ope for [a] time after leaving the Netherlands and had been based in Antwerp” may be the earliest declassified reference to Khan in the declassified record.<a href="#_edn1" name="_ednref1">[1]</a></p>

<p>While it would be 20 years before Pakistan tested a nuclear weapon, in May 1998, the groundwork for that event was laid in the late 1970s and early 1980s when Islamabad acquired the wherewithal for producing fissile material. By the early 1980s Pakistan was “cold testing” prototype nuclear weapons and a few years later, its gas centrifuges were producing weapons-grade enriched uranium. Moreover, a secret reprocessing plant was producing plutonium in quantities. <a href="#_edn2" name="_ednref2">[2]</a> During that same period, however, U.S. presidents Jimmy Carter and Ronald Reagan treated the Pakistani bomb as a lower priority compared to seeking and receiving Islamabad’s assistance in pursuing a covert war against Soviet forces in Afghanistan.</p>

<p>The Central Intelligence Agency has declassified next to nothing on the issues discussed in this posting. Its releases of documents from 1978 and 1979 on Pakistani nuclear issues are so heavily excised that they do not add significant value. When the editor, out of curiosity, filed a FOIA for documents from 1978 concerning A. Q. Khan and Pakistani nuclear enrichment activities, the Agency “<a href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/nukevault/ebb305/index.htm">glomarized</a>” it, taking a neither confirm-nor-deny position that it had any such records. The derivative but reliable intelligence information in declassified State Department records help somewhat to fill the gap.</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>During the early 1970s Prime Minister Zulfikar Ali Bhutto made formative decisions on Pakistan’s nuclear weapons program. Although he never acknowledged that to U.S. officials, a conversation that Bhutto had with U.S. Ambassador Henry Byroade demonstrated his deep, strongly nationalistic, commitment to the nuclear program and his emotional response to U.S. nonproliferation concerns.<a href="#_edn3" name="_ednref3">[3]</a> Bhutto claimed that he was especially chagrined that President Ford’s March 1976 <a href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve08/d225">letter</a> advising against the purchase of a reprocessing plant had arrived “almost coincidentally with the actual signing of the agreement with France.”<a href="#_edn4" name="_ednref4">[4]</a> By that point, he argued, his decision was “irreversible” and that to “back down now would be disastrous for him as a political leader and would leave him ‘prostrate.’”</p>n4" name="_ednref4">[4]</a> By that point, he argued, his decision was “irreversible” and that to “back down now would be disastrous for him as a political leader and would leave him ‘prostrate.’”</p>

<p>Discussing his motivations, Bhutto observed that the “confidence and morale” of the people depended on Pakistan keeping abreast of modern technology. Just as important, the “general feeling of lack of security” had a deep impact on the “morale of the Pakistani people as a whole, even including the illiterates.” According to Bhutto, the 1974 Indian test made it necessary to raise the “comfort” of the population by showing that Pakistan “could match the technology of others.”</p>

<p>During the conversation, Byroade mentioned the possibility of a “long-term” solution for a more “sensible” world: internationally supervised multinational processing plants as an alternative to reprocessing on nationalist lines. But that would have little bearing on Pakistan’s immediate decision to acquire “sensitive nuclear technology.” Nevertheless, later in his message, Byroade suggests further review of the multilateral approach. “I think that judging from his mood in our discussion Bhutto would be prepared to listen to what we had to say about prospects along these lines, particularly in view of Symington Amendment” (which would cut off aid to countries receiving reprocessing technology).</p>

<p>In his comments to Washington, Byroade acknowledged that “we were … quite delinquent in not letting [Bhutto] know our views in a more timely fashion.” He further observed that there should be a “better Washington-mission relationship on such important matters.” While not questioning the importance of nonproliferation policy, Byroade observed that “it should not be considered alone and in isolation from other foreign policy objectives.”</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>During much of 1978, trying to check Islamabad’s effort to develop a reprocessing capability was a top priority for U.S. policy toward Pakistan.<a href="#_edn5" name="_ednref5">[5]</a> That Pakistan was also seeking an enrichment capability does not appear to have been on the mental map of interested officials, as indicated by the assumptions spelled out in this report of a meeting. The purpose of the discussion was to formulate a request to what became known as the “Z” Division at Lawrence Livermore Laboratory and which, in a few months, produced a <a href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/nukevault/ebb451/docs/3.pdf">study</a> of Pakistani capabilities to take the plutonium route to the bomb.</p>

<p>The Z Division would study two paths. One was to reprocess spent fuel from the KANUPP reactor, which was safeguarded by the International Atomic Energy Agency. The other was to build a secret production reactor as a source for spent fuel.</p>

<p>A note from Smith that was attached to the memorandum indicated that he wanted to make sure that the study was in the works and how long it would take Pakistan to finish the plant.</p>

The Saunders-Pickering memorandum to Vance looks at policy options for responding to the Pakistani nuclear program, including application of the Symington Amendment (aid cut-off to countries that were illicitly enriching uranium). <p>What made policy options necessary were two findings about Pakistani efforts to produce fissile material for nuclear weapons: One was that Pakistan was “building a facility capable of small-scale reprocessing, near the PINSTECH [Pakistan Institute for Science and Technology] research center” that would be capable of producing enough plutonium for at least one nuclear device annually. The other was that a “secret gas centrifuge program is intended to yield significant quantities of HEU.” The Pakistanis had already “succeeded in operating small numbers of centrifuge units.” Moreover, referring to Kahuta, but not naming it, the Pakistanis “have plans for 7,000 units, and have begun to construct buildings to house the facility and to acquire equipment for the plant.” It would take about 2 years of work for the plant to operate and another two years before it could produce HEU.</p>

<p>The discussion of the Symington Amendment, which required an aid cut-off if Pakistan was found to be receiving technology for an enrichment program after 4 August 1977 (when the amendment went into effect), indicated the problems of interpretation. <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>Most of the text is essentially the same as Document 10, although some of the intelligence findings are truncated. It includes, however, an appendix on “Intelligence Relevant to the Timing of Pakistani Imports of Items for Use in Their Gas Centrifuge Program,” listing items that were “reportedly sent or received after August 1977” or “may have been sent after August 1977”—the cutoff for the Symington Amendment. The list included various types of bellows, rotor drives, and inverters. Moreover, to make the point that the Pakistanis had an ongoing enrichment program under the terms of the Symington Amendment, the report listed three continuing activities: an “eight-machine cascade,” a reference to the test installation at Chaklala air force base; a 64-machine cascade planned for June 1979, a reference to the pilot plant at Sihala; and a 1,000-machine cascade projected for 1980, which referred to Kahuta.</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>In this telegram to Ambassadors Hummel and Goheen, senior State Department officials cited the “acute dilemma” posed by the Pakistani nuclear program at a time when “we wish to be more supportive” of Islamabad. Given the role of the 1974 Indian test in stimulating Pakistan’s nuclear efforts, the views of both ambassadors were sought on “whether we can deal with these as related problems.” With its “good intelligence” on the secret enrichment program, the Government of India was “concerned” that domestic politics could make “untenable” Prime Minister Desai’s “principled stand” against nuclear testing. That could lead to pressure to weaponize. Therefore, to prevent a nuclear arms race in South Asia, the Department was considering the merits of asking Ambassador Goheen to meet with Desai to discuss the possibility of an India-Pakistan declaration on “non-development and non-use” of nuclear weapons.</p>

<p>The “non-paper” includes fascinating detail on the pilot enrichment plant at Sihala and the large plant then under construction at Kahuta. To secure equipment for the Kahuta enrichment plant, the Pakistanis had set up “dummy companies using false-end use statements” operating from Dubai and Kuwait. Nevertheless, the Pakistanis appeared to be having “continuing difficulties” with the gas centrifuge enrichment process, which gave the Department “fairly high confidence that sufficient HEU for a [Pakistani] nuclear device could not be available from the Kahuta plant for two or more years.”</p>fuge enrichment process, which gave the Department “fairly high confidence that sufficient HEU for a [Pakistani] nuclear device could not be available from the Kahuta plant for two or more years.”</p>

<p>The non-paper also reviewed Pakistan’s efforts to build plants for reprocessing spent fuel for plutonium. Four projects were in the works, including the “New Labs” facility at PINSTECH [Pakistan Institute for Science and Technology] and the plant at Chasma for which the contract with the French had been broken. U.S. intelligence was uncertain about how far Pakistan had gone in producing plutonium but “it now appears possible that the Pakistanis have made more progress in the area of reprocessing than we previously believed.” According to U.S. intelligence, if New Labs became “fully operational by mid-1980, Pakistan conceivably could separate sufficient plutonium from spent KANUPP reactor fuel for its first nuclear device by early 1981.”</p>

<div class="field field--name-field-title field--type-string field--label-hidden field__item"><a href="/document/24489-state-department-telegram-227353-u-s-embassy-switzerland-paper-activities-swiss" hreflang="en"><strong class=" wrapped-title raw-title">State Department telegram 227353 to U.S. Embassy Switzerland, “Paper on Activities by Swiss Firms,” 26 August 1980, Secret</strong></a></div>

<div class="field field--name-field-date field--type-datetime field--label-hidden field__item"><time datetime="1980-08-26T12:00:00Z" class="datetime">Aug 26, 1980</time>
</div>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-source field--type-text-long field--label-inline">
    <div class="field__label">Source</div>
              <div class="field__item"><p>Smith records, Box 18, Pakistan [May-December 1980]</p>
</div>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>Swiss nuclear-related exports to Pakistan were also of concern to the Carter administration, as was Bern’s general “hands-off” approach. This message consisted entirely of a “non-paper” to be provided to Swiss officials. As Switzerland was not a NATO ally, the information on Pakistan’s nuclear program was more of a general nature than that provided to the West Germans, but the litany of suspicious Swiss commercial activities was detailed and lengthy. The “non-paper” was not the first expression of State Department concern, as its text made clear, because Washington had been monitoring Swiss firms for at least a few years.</p>detailed and lengthy. The “non-paper” was not the first expression of State Department concern, as its text made clear, because Washington had been monitoring Swiss firms for at least a few years.</p>

<p>Like the message to Bonn, this non-paper demonstrated how far the Pakistanis had gone in acquiring technology from the Swiss that was vital to their fissile material production efforts. With respect to the plutonium program, such firms as Sulzer Brothers and Luna AG had been playing significant roles to enable work at “New Labs.” On the enrichment program, two firms were of particular concern: CORA Engineering and VAT (Vakuum Apparat Technik) because they supported the gas centrifuge program. For example, CORA Engineering had “delivered a complete installation for feeding uranium hexafluoride into the uranium enrichment plant.” As for VAT, it had been supplying valves for gas centrifuges.</p>

<p>According to the non-paper, as recently as May both VAT and CORA Engineering, or individuals working with them, had “enter[ed] into contracts worth several millions of dollars.” Considering the previous and ongoing transactions, the U.S. government asked the Swiss government to “take actions to ensure control of exports and other types of assistance to Pakistan’s nuclear programs.” Like West Germany, Switzerland was not an enthusiastic partner in this endeavor. Although a signatory of the NPT, Switzerland’s reluctance to take economic action against another state brought considerable frustration to the Carter administration and its successors.</p>
