Realization of room-temperature magnetism and multistep magnetization switching in 2D metallic ferrimagnets
Authors: Zijing Zhao, Nanshu Liu, Qinghua Zhang, Yuansha Chen, Ziyu Meng, Bailing Li, Guangcheng Wang, Wei Li, Shibo Li, Yunzhong Chen, Baogen Shen, Xiaolei Wang, Yanglong Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Achieving multistep magnetization switching in two-dimensional (2D) ferrimagnets demonstrates substantial promise for advancing high-density non-Boolean logic and memory devices. However, existing 2D ferrimagnets are still hampered by low Curie temperatures and a lack of tunable multiple magnetic states. Here, we report the successful synthesis of a family of 2D Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex alloy with robust room-temperature ferrimagnetism. The key magnetic and electrical properties can be effectively regulated by tailoring the chalcogen ratio in Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex alloy. Notably, multiple magnetic states and resistance plateaus are observed because of the spin canting behavior, enabling the realization of several distinct spin states by adjusting magnetic fields and temperatures, thereby demonstrating great potential for multistate applications. Density functional theory calculations further reveal that the evolution of magnetic anisotropy originates from variations in the orbital occupation of electronic states near the Fermi level. Our work can expand the library of 2D metallic ferrimagnets and pave the way for designing high-density spintronic devices.

INTRODUCTION

Recently, two-dimensional (2D) magnetic materials have provoked a surge of research interest owing to their potential for uncovering exotic physical phenomena and building advanced spintronic devices (1–3). Within this rapidly expanding field, 2D ferrimagnets are particularly promising because they inherit the facile magnetic readout capability of ferromagnets while retaining the low stray magnetic field of antiferromagnets (4–6). They provide ideal platforms for investigating the unusual multistep magnetic switching process with distinct intermediate spin states (a phenomenon rarely observed in ferromagnets) due to the competition between intrinsic antiferromagnetic interactions and ferromagnetic arrangements induced by an external field (7, 8), which is essential both for understanding the spin dynamics in low-dimensional magnetic systems and for designing multivalue storage devices. However, the number of experimentally verified 2D ferrimagnetic materials remains extremely limited. Moreover, even among the few reported 2D ferrimagnets (9–17), their critical temperatures are far below room temperature, severely hindering their practical applications. Furthermore, most ferrimagnets fail to exhibit the desired multistep magnetization transitions, impeding the development of spintronic devices for non-Boolean computing. These limitations collectively underscore the urgent need to explore 2D ferrimagnetic materials with high transition temperatures and tunable magnetic states, as well as to develop effective magnetism manipulation strategies to further expand the library of 2D magnets.

Alloy engineering has been considered as a powerful chemical method to tailor the magnetism of 2D materials and induce innovative spin textures (18, 19). For instance, composition-dependent magnetic behavior has been demonstrated in 2D (Fe1–xCox)3GaTe2 alloy by varying the Co/Fe ratio (11). Similarly, compositional tuning in 2D CrCl3–x–yBrxIy alloy can induce the transition from ferromagnetism to antiferromagnetism (20). Besides, the incorporation of Se into Cr2S3 nanoflakes has been shown to increase the Curie temperature (15). Notably, Fe0.875X (X = S or Se) is a promising 2D ferrimagnetic candidate due to its fascinating room-temperature magnetism (21, 22), excellent electrical conductivity (23, 24), and unique spin reorientation behavior (25). In addition, the structural similarity between Fe0.875S and Fe0.875Se suggests the feasibility of synthesizing Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex alloy without disrupting the crystalline framework, thus enabling effective magnetism modulation in this material system (26).

Nevertheless, the synthesis of ultrathin 2D Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex alloy nanoflakes has yet to be realized. It contains a higher content of intercalated Fe atoms between the c planes and exhibits diverse stoichiometric structural variants (27), which severely impede the 2D anisotropic and phase-controllable growth. Moreover, the impact of Se/S chemical substitution on the magnetic properties and multistep magnetic switching behavior of ultrathin nanoflakes remains unclear, leaving a critical knowledge gap in understanding its low-dimensional magnetic ordering. In addition, the evolution of composition-dependent microscopic magnetic structures, electrical properties, and their underlying mechanisms across the Se/S substitution gradient is poorly understood—yet these insights are critical for optimizing material performance and comprehending the ferrimagnetic behavior.

Here, we realized the controllable synthesis of a series of ultrathin Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex nanoflakes with precisely tunable compositions via the space-confined chemical vapor deposition (CVD) method on mica substrates. The modulated composition and atomic structures of high-quality nanoflakes were characterized by x-ray diffraction (XRD), x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS), and scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM). All samples maintain the room-temperature ferrimagnetism with excellent metallic behavior even down to three–unit-cell thickness. Notably, the magnetic properties can be manipulated by changing the ratio of chalcogens in 2D Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex alloy, involving spin reorientation temperature, easy magnetization axis, coercivity, magnetic domains, and magnetoresistance (MR). Novel magnetic states and multistep magnetization reversal can be induced when x = ~0.9, which originate from the spin canting behavior. The change of magnetic anisotropy was further understood by density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Our work can expand the library of 2D metallic ferrimagnets and bring various fancy properties beyond the fundamental limits of intrinsic 2D magnets.

RESULTS

Crystal structures and controllable synthesis of 2D Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex alloy

Fe0.875X crystallizes in a fundamental NiAs-like structure with alternating layers of iron and chalcogen atoms. It can also be regarded as a structure in which some Fe atoms are intercalated into the van der Waals gaps of the layered FeX2 backbone with the remaining sites being vacancies (fig. S1, A and B). (28, 29). Its ferrimagnetic nature originates from the ferromagnetic coupling within the c plane and antiferromagnetic coupling between adjacent layers, with uncompensated magnetic sublattices yielding a net magnetic moment (29). In particular, bulk 3c-Fe0.875Se exhibits the intriguing spin reorientation behavior, where the spin direction changes from the c plane toward c axis below 130 K (21, 30). On the contrary, the spin easy axis of Fe0.875S remains confined to the c plane across the entire temperature range (Fig. 1A) (31).

Our DFT calculations indicate that Se substitution at S sites is energetically favored in the alloy system (fig. S1, C to E), exhibiting the lowest formation energy among all considered configurations. This pronounced stability arises from the comparable electronegativity and chemical characteristics of S and Se, which enables seamless incorporation of Se into the anion sublattice without disrupting the host crystal framework. Furthermore, calculation results reveal that the charge transfer between Fe and S (with a calculated averaged Bader charge of 0.88 e−) is larger than that between Fe and Se (0.73 e−), due to their distinct electronegativities. By tuning the S/Se atomic ratio, it is possible to modulate both the charge transfer effect and the occupation of p orbitals of S/Se atoms. As a result, the relative contributions from the in-plane px/py orbitals and the out-of-plane pz orbital of the chalcogen atoms around the Fermi level would be changed (Fig. 1B), offering great potential for magnetic anisotropy regulation through compositional engineering. We further calculate the energy difference between magnetic moments along the c axis and a axis of Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex alloy, revealing that out-of-plane spin configuration becomes more stable with increasing Se concentration (Fig. 1C). Detailed spin orientation analysis with more canting angles will be discussed later. Consequently, controlling the chalcogen composition offers an effective strategy for tailoring the magnetic and electrical properties in Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex alloy.

To investigate the magnetism transition in this material system, we used a three-zone CVD setup for the synthesis of ultrathin Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex alloy, as depicted in fig. S2. To facilitate the growth of ultrathin nanoflakes, we used mica substrates and a space-confined growth strategy. Sulfur and selenium powders are loaded separately in the upstream with their volatilization temperatures set at ~120° and ~320°C, respectively, to enable independent control of their concentrations. The chalcogen ratio (S/Se) in the alloy can be systematically regulated by adjusting the relative mass of S and Se sources (table S1). In addition, the elemental contents are analyzed using EDS, with the values averaged from three to five individual nanoflakes on the substrate.

Characterizing the atomic structures

The optical microscope (OM) image of as-synthesized Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex alloy nanoflakes exhibits hexagonal or triangular morphologies (Fig. 2A), which arise from differential growth rates along metal- and chalcogen-terminated edges and are consistent with their hexagonal crystal structure. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements show that the thinnest nanoflakes can reach ~1.73 nm (Fig. 2B), referring to one–unit-cell thickness. Both the lateral size and thickness of the samples can be effectively controlled via tuning the growth time, as detailed in the Materials and Methods section and fig. S2. XRD characterization can reflect the compositional evolution of Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex. For terminal compositions (x = 0 and x = 1), the XRD patterns (fig. S3, A and B) can be indexed to the standard cards of Fe0.875S (Fe7S8) and Fe0.875Se (Fe7Se8), respectively. With increasing Se content, a continuous shift of XRD peaks toward lower diffraction angles is observed (Fig. 2C), which can be attributed to the gradual expansion of lattice parameters due to the larger ionic radius of Se2− compared to S2−. Specifically, the diffraction angle of the (0012) peak gradually decreases from ~65.51° in Fe0.875S to ~63.35° in Fe0.875Se, indicating that the lattice constant c is increased from ~17.08 to 17.60 Å. Similarly, the (203) peak angle shifts from ~34.06° in Fe0.875S to ~32.39° in Fe0.875Se, showing an expansion of the a/b lattice constants from ~6.85 to 7.23 Å (Fig. 2D). XPS is also used to analyze elemental compositions and valence states. As shown in Fig. 2E, the incorporation of Se induces a gradual decrease in the intensities of S 2p1/2 and S 2p3/2 peaks. Simultaneously, the peaks of Se 3p1/2 and Se 3p3/2 come into view and progressively dominate the spectrum. The XPS spectrum of the Fe element depicted in fig. S3C confirms the coexistence of Fe2+ and Fe3+.

Aberration-corrected STEM and selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) are used to identify the atomic and phase structures of the nanoflakes. Figure S4 presents the atomic-resolution STEM image of Fe0.875S. Because of the larger atomic number of Fe relative to S, Fe atoms exhibit brighter contrast, which are octahedrally coordinated by six dimmer S atoms. This atomic arrangement matches well with the atomic structural models of Fe0.875S along the c axis. The SAED pattern of Fe0.875S exhibits a single set of hexagonal diffraction spots corresponding to the [001] zone axis, which confirms the oriented growth along the c axis as well. Notably, the innermost weaker superspots (highlighted by purple circles) verify the stoichiometric ratio and signify the formation of 3c superstructure featuring long-range ordered intercalated Fe atoms or vacancies, which can rule out the formation of 4c-Fe0.875S superstructure and other stoichiometric phases (32).

The aberration-corrected STEM image of Fe0.875S0.62Se0.38 is shown in Fig. 2 (F and G). It exhibits a well-defined honeycomb lattice with the absence of visible structural defects, demonstrating the high crystalline quality of as-synthesized alloy. The hexagonal structure is maintained with no obvious lattice distortion, enabled by the structural similarity between Fe0.875S and Fe0.875Se. SAED patterns of Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex with x > 0.75 or x < 0.2 are similar to those of pristine Fe0.875Se and Fe0.875S, illustrating that the 3c superstructure is well retained after alloying (fig. S5). By contrast, for intermediate compositions such as Fe0.875S0.62Se0.38 (Fig. 2H), SAED patterns exhibit a noticeable attenuation in the intensity of superspots (highlighted by purple circles). This reduction suggests that the excessive incorporation of Se atoms may disrupt the long-range vacancy ordering (33). However, the persistence of faint superspots confirms that the vacancy ordering remains preserved. Compared with Fe0.875S, the line intensity profiles of alloy exhibit enhanced intensity at chalcogen sites (Fig. 2I), which demonstrates the successful doping of Se atoms. Besides, the EDS elemental mappings in Fig. 2 (J and K) further demonstrate the uniform spatial distribution of Fe, S, and Se over the entire nanoflakes.

Property regulation and room-temperature ferrimagnetism

The composition-dependent magnetic properties are systematically investigated via magnetotransport measurements (Fig. 3, A to E), with the applied magnetic field perpendicular to the sample. A representative optical image of the Hall device and the device thicknesses are shown in fig. S6. The elemental composition of the sample under transport testing is determined individually using EDS. For all alloy compositions, the temperature-dependent longitudinal resistances (Rxx) exhibit metallic characteristics, showing a general decreasing trend with reducing temperatures (Fig. 3E), which is caused by the depressed electron-electron scattering and electron-phonon interaction at lower temperatures. Notably, samples with x ≥ 0.78 show an anomalous resistance upturn near ~100 K (marked by grey lines). This phenomenon signals a spin reorientation behavior where magnetic moments rotate from the c plane (in-plane) to the c axis (out-of-plane) direction, which is the distinctive feature of 3c superstructure (21, 30). As the S concentration is augmented, the spin reorientation temperature (defined as the point where the resistance suddenly changes, as shown in fig. S7) decreases gradually (Fig. 3F). Besides, the magnetization curve reveals the antiferromagnetic coupling (fig. S8), consistent with the behavior of bulk samples and further indicating the ferrimagnetic properties (19).

Hall resistance measurements provide more comprehensive evidence for the spin state transition within the alloy system. In Fe0.875S0.27Se0.73 and other S-rich samples (Fig. 3A and fig. S9), in-plane magnetic anisotropy is preferred across all temperatures, as evidenced by the absence of obvious square-shaped Hall hysteresis loop: When the temperature is above 250 K, the Hall resistance (Rxy) of Fe0.875S0.27Se0.73 reaches saturation at high fields (Fig. 3A), indicating that the in-plane–aligned spins are canted toward the out-of-plane direction under the application of a large perpendicular magnetic field. However, Rxy displays a linear dependence on the magnetic field below 200 K because the saturation field exceeds 9 T at lower temperatures. In contrast, distinct Hall resistance behaviors are observed in samples with x ≥ 0.78 (Fig. 3, B to D). At cryogenic temperatures, they exhibit pronounced anomalous Hall hysteresis with square loops, indicative of an out-of-plane magnetic easy axis. As the temperature increases, the square loop gradually diminishes, and the resistance changes steadily before reaching saturation at a larger magnetic field. This temperature-driven transformation is attributed to the change of easy axis from the out-of-plane (c axis) to in-plane (c plane) direction (Fig. 3G). Concurrently, the MR exhibits a temperature-dependent crossover from negative to positive values (fig. S10), which corroborates the spin reorientation dynamics further. At lower temperatures, the spins are aligned along the c axis, and negative MR is dominated due to the suppression of electron-spin scattering by the perpendicular magnetic field. Conversely, at higher temperatures, positive MR emerges because the spins reorient to the in-plane alignments, which result in the transport behavior being dominated by contributions from the Lorentz force and spin disorder. In addition, the sample with higher S content exhibits positive MR across all temperature ranges (fig. S11). In addition, the coercivity exhibits an increasing trend with the enhancement of the Se content, as depicted in fig. S12.

All samples show anomalous Hall effect at 300 K, suggesting the robust room-temperature magnetic ordering. Compared with other 2D ferrimagnets (Fig. 3H) (9–17), Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex shows a notable feature of high magnetic transition temperatures exceeding room temperature. Concurrently, it also demonstrates exceptional electrical conductivity (table S2), displaying excellent metallic behavior.

Multiple magnetic states and resistance plateaus

Distinct magnetic states and multistep magnetization switching can be induced when the alloy content (x) approaches 0.90. For the Fe0.875S0.10Se0.90 sample, steplike hysteresis loops with multiple resistance plateaus emerge during magnetic field sweeping (Fig. 4A), which may arise from the field-driven spin-flopping process from antiferromagnetic coupling to forced ferromagnetic coupling states (34, 35). Figure 4B presents the magnetic field angle-dependent Rxy curves at 10 K. When the angle between the magnetic field and c axis is less than 45°, the Rxy curves show similar shapes, and the coercive field (HC) has an upward trend with increasing angle. However, as the angle approaches 60°, HC undergoes an anomalous decrease, accompanied by a weakening of the multistep magnetization reversal phenomenon. The nonmonotonic angular dependence of HC in Fe0.875S0.10Se0.90 is different from that in Fe0.875Se (fig. S13), implying that the ground-state spin structures of Fe0.875S0.10Se0.90 may have a canting angle toward the in-plane direction. This canting behavior may reduce the interlayer antiferromagnetic coupling strength, facilitating the emergence of ferromagnetic order under higher fields, thereby leading to multiple resistance plateaus. The coercivity (HC) and saturated field (H′) decrease gradually as the temperature rises. By ~80 K, the stepwise behavior vanishes, while H′ becomes larger (Fig. 4C), indicating that the in-plane easy axis is preferred at higher temperatures. In addition, the MR curves also display multiple resistance states at lower temperatures and undergo temperature-dependent negative-to-positive transitions (Fig. 4D and fig. S14), further demonstrating the rich spin states in this alloy. For further clarity, Fig. 4E presents the enlarged Hall curve at 30 K after subtraction of the high-field background, and Fig. 4F provides the specific possible spin configurations of the multistep magnetic states. By extracting the transition fields, the magnetic phase diagram is depicted in Fig. 4G. In total, more than six spin states can be obtained by varying the magnetic fields and temperatures. A similar stepwise hysteresis phenomenon is also observed in another sample with x = 0.93 (fig. S15). The diverse magnetic and resistance states, engineered via alloying, hold substantial potential for advancing multistate storage technologies.

To further reveal the magnetic domain evolution during the multistep spin-flopping, we conducted temperature- and field-dependent magnetic force microscopy (MFM) measurements. As shown in Fig. 4H, the Fe0.875S0.1Se0.9 nanoflake displays out-of-plane multidomain features at ~25 K after a zero-field–cooling (ZFC) process, characterized by distinct alternating bright-dark contrast. The bright regions correspond to domains with magnetization directions opposite to the MFM tip, while the dark contrasts indicate domains with the same direction as the tip. These multidomain structures are evolved into quasi–single-domain states at a perpendicular magnetic field of +7 T (fig. S16). When the external field reduces from +7 to +0.55 T, the single-domain region breaks into multiple domains, which may be attributed to the transition from ferromagnetic to antiferromagnetic interlayer interactions. As the magnetic field is swept to a negative value of −0.69 T (exceeding the coercivity) (Fig. 4, I to K), the domains undergo a gradual flipping process with obvious changes in the bright-dark contrast (marked in blue squares). Further increasing the magnetic field to −3 T leads to complete domain flipping back to ferromagnetically coupled states (Fig. 4L). Notably, relatively weak phase contrast is also observed in the bright or dark large domains (fig. S17), indicating the presence of in-plane spin textures. The coexistence of in-plane and out-of-plane magnetic components in MFM images is consistent with the spin canting behavior inferred from Hall measurements.

In contrast, for samples without the multistep flopping process, all magnetic domains switch to the out-of-plane direction in a single step under a much smaller magnetic field of 5 kOe, as evidenced from fig. S18. As the temperature increases to ~200 K, in-plane magnetic domains with weaker contrast emerge, which maintain in the in-plane direction against perpendicular fields up to 2 T. The alteration of the magnetic domain orientation with temperature also verifies the spin-reorientation behavior in this alloy system. Moreover, the room-temperature magnetic domain structure presents a continuous evolution pattern with variations in chemical composition, as depicted in fig. S19. These composition-dependent magnetic domain structures highlight the effectiveness of alloying as a powerful means to engineer magnetic properties.

Even when the thickness of Fe0.875S(1–x)Sex is reduced to as low as ~5 nm (corresponding to three unit cells), multidomain states are preserved, as shown in fig. S20. The consistent presence of magnetic domains at room temperature across all samples, even in the ultrathin regime, strongly validates the existence of room-temperature magnetic ordering in Fe0.875S(1–x)Sex alloy.

Theoretical calculations

To elucidate the magnetism evolution induced by alloying, we further perform DFT calculations. The optimized lattice parameters for Fe0.875S and Fe0.875Se are a = b = 6.91 Å, c = 17.11 Å and a = b = 7.28 Å, c = 17.79 Å, respectively, which are close to the XRD results. The calculations reveal that both Fe0.875S and Fe0.875Se exhibit a ferrimagnetic ground state, where magnetic moments are ferromagnetically aligned within each Fe sublayer while being antiferromagnetically coupled between adjacent Fe layers (fig. S21), in accordance with our experimental data and previous literature (29). This magnetic structure can be understood through the competition between intralayer and interlayer exchange interactions (fig. S22). The intralayer ferromagnetic coupling is characterized by the positive exchange parameter J1 of 0.55 and 9.54 meV and J2 of 4.04 and 9.15 meV, for Fe0.875S and Fe0.875Se, respectively. The average intralayer Fe─X─Fe bond angles are close to 90°, which is consistent with near-orthogonal superexchange coupling. In contrast, the interlayer magnetic coupling, supported by average interlayer Fe─X─Fe bond angles of ~128° or 70° and a shorter interlayer Fe-Fe distance (2.89 Å) relative to the full intralayer distance (3.45 Å), suggests the coexistence of interlayer antiferromagnetic superexchange and direct exchange interactions (36). Both the intra- and interlayer exchange parameters show increasement for intermediate doping contents (fig. S23).

We then investigate the magnetic anisotropy energies (MAE) of Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex by calculating the relative energies of various spin orientations, which are defined by the angles relative to the z axis (θ) and x axis (φ). As shown in Fig. 5A, the purple color represents the lowest energy, whereas the red color denotes the highest energy. The magnetic moments of Fe atoms in Fe0.875S (x = 0) prefer lying in the xy plane (θ = 90°, independent of φ) with an easy-plane anisotropy, while those in Fe0.875Se (x = 1.0) are oriented to the out-of-plane z direction (θ = 0°). For the alloy with x = 0.50, 0.71, 0.75, and 0.83, the easy axis (corresponding to the minimum MAE value) gradually rotates from the in-plane to out-of-plane direction with enhancing Se content. This distinct anisotropy is also quantitatively reflected in the opposite signs of the single-ion anisotropy parameter Az for Fe0.875S (−2.20 meV) and Fe0.875Se (1.52 meV). Quantitatively, we calculated energy difference between magnetic configurations with moments along the easy axis (Eea) and the z (Ez) direction (Fig. 5B). The energy difference is negative for x < 0.75 and approaches zero for larger doping contents, verifying the spin reorientation behavior happens at x = ~0.75, which is consistent with our experimental observations as well (Fig. 3F). Moreover, systems with higher Se content exhibit larger MAE values (fig. S24), providing a theoretical explanation for the observed trend of coercivity rising with increasing Se concentration. To further illustrate the spin transition mechanism, we calculated the density of states (DOS) at the G point. As shown in Fig. 5C, the px/py orbitals of S atoms contribute significantly near the Fermi level, leading to the in-plane MAE. For Fe0.875Se (Fig. 5D), the densities of px/py orbitals shift toward higher energies, resulting in them being unoccupied and the pz orbital appearing near the Fermi level (indicated by the purple arrow). Therefore, Se doping changes the electronic states near the Fermi level and enhances the occupation of out-of-plane orbitals, thus altering the magnetic easy axis. These electronic structure transitions can explain the transition of MAE from in-plane to out-of-plane easy axis, aligning well with the transport measurement results (Fig. 3, A to F).

Notably, when the Se concentration is approaching ~0.90, the easy axis slightly deviates from the reference z axis, signifying a slight deviation of the magnetic easy axis from the crystalline c axis with a small canting angle. To clarify the origin of this spin canting, we calculated the contributions of Fe d orbital hybridization to MAE. As shown in Fig. 5E and fig. S25, the contribution from hybridization between dx2–y2 and dxy orbitals dominates in Fe0.875S, corresponding to the easy plane anisotropy in the xy plane. In Fe0.875Se and Fe0.875S0.12Se0.88 alloy, however, the contribution from the dz2 and dyz orbitals is at least 2.5 times of magnitude larger than that from other orbitals, resulting in the out-of-plane easy axis. For Fe0.875S0.10Se0.90 (x = 0.90), the contribution from dz2 and dyz orbitals remains dominant, while that from dx2–y2 and dxy orbitals also becomes comparable. The competition between these orbital hybridization channels thus induces a canting of magnetic moments away from the z axis. As a result, the canted magnetic states become the most stable, which may explain the spin-flopping process and rich resistance states observed in Fig. 4A.

DISCUSSION

In conclusion, we successfully synthesized ultrathin Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex nanoflakes with precisely tunable compositions down to single–unit-cell thickness via the space-confined CVD method. The elemental composition and atomic structures of the nanoflakes were systematically characterized by XRD, XPS, EDS, and STEM analyses, confirming the high quality and atomic-level uniformity of the obtained nanoflakes. All compositions exhibit excellent ferrimagnetic behavior with high electrical conductivity and robust room-temperature magnetic ordering, which breaks the current record. We demonstrate that the key magnetic properties—including spin reorientation temperature, easy magnetization axis, coercivity, magnetic domain structure, and electrical characteristics—could be effectively modulated by varying the chalcogen ratio in the 2D Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex alloy. Notably, distinct spin states and multiple resistance plateaus can be induced at specified alloy compositions, holding great potential for applications in multistate memory devices. DFT calculations provide fundamental insights into the underlying mechanisms of the magnetic moment transition. More promising research avenues can be explored to modulate the magnetic states and spin orientations in 2D ferrimagnets. This work not only expands the family of 2D metallic ferrimagnetic materials but also advances the understanding of low-dimensional magnetism, opening avenues for designing functional spintronic devices.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation

The synthesis was performed in a three–temperature-zone tubular furnace. All materials and reagents were purchased commercially and used without additional purification. Selenium (Se; Alfa Aesar: 99.5%) and sulfur (S; Alfa Aesar; 99.5%) powders were separately loaded in the upstream zones, with the volatilization temperature rigorously controlled at ~320° and ~120°C, respectively, to precisely regulate the chemical potential of chalcogen atoms and avoid the formation of impurity phases. Meanwhile, 50 mg of FeCl2 (Alfa Aesar; 99.5%) was placed in the downstream zone, the temperature of which was kept at ~580°C. A gas mixture of 10 standard cubic centimeter per minute (SCCM) of H2 and 100 SCCM of Ar served as the carrier gas. Freshly cleaved fluorophlogopite mica substrates (Taiyuan Fluorphlogopite Mica Company Ltd.) were stacked with each other above the FeCl2 powders to create a confined spatial environment, which promotes the ultrathin growth of nanoflakes. The chalcogen ratio (S/Se) in the alloy can be systematically regulated by adjusting the relative mass of S and Se sources (table S1).

Characterizations

The size and morphology of Fe0.875S(1−x)Sex alloys were observed using an OM (Nexcope NM910). Thickness characterization was conducted via AFM (Bruker, Dimension Icon). XRD measurement was performed on a Rigaku D/Max-2400 x-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation. XPS (Kratos Analytical AXIS-Ultra with monochromatic Al Kα x-ray) was used to reveal the element compositions. Room-temperature MFM was carried out using a Bruker Dimension Icon. Low-temperature MFM (attocube, attoAFM I) was performed at temperatures ranging from 2 to 300 K with a magnetic field applied perpendicular to the sample surface. Atomic-resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) imaging was performed on an aberration-corrected JEOL ARM200F microscope operating at 60 kV. EDS mapping (FEI, Tecnai F30) was performed to show the element compositions.

Device fabrication and electrical transport measurement

The Hall bar devices were fabricated using standard e-beam lithography. A 10-nm Ti and a 90-nm Au were used as the contact electrodes, which are deposited by e-beam evaporation. Magnetotransport measurements were conducted using the Electronic Transport Option (ETO) modes in the physical property measurement system (Quantum Design, DynaCool) under a maximum magnetic field of 9 T. Hall and MR data were antisymmetrically or symmetrically processed with magnetic field.

DFT calculations

DFT calculations were performed using the generalized gradient approximation parametrized by Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof for the exchange-correlation potential (37), the projected augmented wave method (38), and a plane-wave basis set as implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (39). Dispersion correction was made at the van der Waals density functional level, using the optB86b functional for the exchange potential, which was proved to be accurate in describing the structural properties. A uniform Monkhorst-Pack k mesh of 9 × 9 × 4 was adopted for integration over Brillouin zone. A kinetic energy cutoff for the plane waves was set to 700 eV for geometric optimization and 500 eV for electronic structure calculations. All atoms and lattice volumes were allowed to relax until the residual force per atom was less than 0.01 eV/Å. A DFT + U method was used in all calculations where on-site Coulomb interaction of Fe d orbital with U and J values of 2.6 and 0.6 eV. The calculated lattice parameters using this choice of U and J values are well consistent with the experiment. Charge transfer between Fe and S/Se atoms was calculated using the Bader charge analysis(40). Positive values of J and Az mean ferromagnetic coupling and single-ion anisotropy along the z direction.
