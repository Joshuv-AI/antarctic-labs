Frequency-domain multi-scale hybrid attention for pathological image classification
Authors: Yang Zhang, Junjie Li, Qiushi Wang, Luyan Zhang, Lingjiang Deng, Qiyun Tian, Feixu Lu, Gang Li, Chuanyun Xu, Fanghong Zhang, Hualiang Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPathological image classification is critical for early cancer diagnosis and precise subtyping. However, pathological images exhibit significant heterogeneity and complex textures. Existing methods often fail to fully exploit local details, while frequency-domain approaches lack effective inter-subband interaction, hindering the fusion of global context and local fine-grained information. To address this, this study propose a Multi-scale Frequency-domain Hybrid Attention mechanism (MFHA). MFHA uses wavelet transform to decompose images into low-frequency subbands (global structure) and high-frequency subbands (microscopic details). By integrating multi-scale convolutions with subband fusion, it enhances high-frequency feature representation, enabling effective joint modeling of global structure and local texture. This study further introduce a spatial attention module with cosine similarity and multi-dimensional statistics to improve feature robustness and discriminability. Experiments show that our method outperforms baselines on multiple pathological datasets, with accuracy gains of 1.97% and 2.71%. This work provides a novel perspective for pathological image feature co-modeling, boosting classification accuracy and robustness. Introduction

Pathological image classification serves as a core technical foundation for early cancer screening, precise subtyping, and prognostic assessment. Its diagnostic accuracy directly determines the scientific validity of clinical treatment strategies and the improvement of patients’ quality of life1. According to global cancer burden data released by the World Health Organization’s International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), cancer incidence and mortality rates remain persistently high2. As the “gold standard” for tumor diagnosis, pathological classification can accurately determine critical information such as tumor benign or malignant status, histological type, and staging, thereby providing the essential basis for personalized treatment3,4.With the maturation and widespread adoption of whole slide imaging (WSI) technology5,6, digital pathology has laid a solid data foundation for algorithmic models, propelling the extensive application of artificial intelligence in translational medicine and clinical practice7.

Early pathological image classification primarily relied on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), which, leveraging their unique advantages, became the core technical architecture in this field8–11. However, CNNs mainly focus on extracting local features. While they can effectively capture detailed information such as cell morphology, they struggle to establish long-range global dependencies. This limitation constrains their analytical capability when processing pathological images characterized by complex spatial structures and contextual dependencies8,12. To overcome these limitations, researchers have attempted to enhance multi-scale feature representation capability through methods such as multi-receptive-field adaptive path aggregation13,14 and graph convolutional networks fused with adaptive filters and aggregators15.

By introducing attention mechanisms to optimize feature selection and expand the receptive field, the representation of key regions is strengthened16; however, such improvements are still confined to spatial-domain feature reconstruction and struggle to overcome the inherent bottleneck of spatial-domain modeling.

The emergence of Transformer in the field of pathological imaging has fundamentally reshaped the local perception-based modeling paradigm of CNNs17–19. By leveraging the self-attention mechanism, Transformer inherently possesses a global field of view across the entire image, enabling efficient capture of long-range feature dependencies between tissue regions. Supported by large-scale pre-training data, it demonstrates powerful cross-scene generalization capability, propelling pathological image analysis from local perception into a new stage of global contextual reasoning. However, Transformer-based models lack the strong inductive bias inherent to CNNs, resulting in insufficient sensitivity to local image features. This limitation makes them prone to errors in tasks requiring precise identification of lesion boundaries or detection of subtle pathological changes20.

In recent years, frequency-domain analysis techniques have garnered increasing attention in pathological imaging and emerged as a significant direction to compensate for the limitations of spatial-domain modeling21–23. Through techniques such as the Fourier transform and wavelet decomposition, frequency-domain analysis maps images from the spatial domain to the frequency domain. Signals of different frequencies can capture the periodic textures and multi-scale structural features of tissues, effectively addressing the shortcomings of CNNs and Transformers in collaboratively capturing global structures and local details24,25. The low-frequency components possess excellent high-frequency noise suppression characteristics, enabling them to mitigate the impact of interfering factors such as staining variations and imaging device noise in pathological images. This enhances the model’s adaptability to data distribution differences and alleviates generalization challenges caused by clinical data heterogeneity. The high-frequency components are highly sensitive to subtle textures and abrupt structural changes in images, allowing them to effectively extract key discriminative features for pathological diagnosis, such as nuclear atypia and glandular arrangement disorders26.

However, the majority of existing research methods tend to focus on the global modeling and interaction of pathological images27–31, often falling short in sufficiently mining the discriminative high-frequency detailed features within the images, such as nuclear morphology and local textures. This inadequacy makes it difficult to effectively capture fine-grained differences. Furthermore, current frequency-domain analysis methods mostly remain at the level of single-scale decomposition or simple subband concatenation25,32,33, lacking a mechanism for deep interaction and optimization of multi-scale frequency-domain features. This limitation hinders flexible adaptation to the complex clinical scenarios inherent in pathological images, which are characterized by strong heterogeneity in cell morphology and varying lesion sizes, consequently constraining the improvement of classification accuracy and robustness.

To address the aforementioned challenges, this study proposes a Multi-scale Frequency-domain Hybrid Attention (MFHA) mechanism. It aims to decouple and fuse multi-scale information from frequency subbands through frequency-domain techniques, thereby achieving efficient synergistic representation of cytomorphological information and global context, and enhancing the recognition accuracy for small lesions and subtle morphological variations. Figure 1 illustrates the core concept of the MFHA framework. Specifically, MFHA employs the wavelet transform to decompose the input into low-frequency subbands carrying global information and high-frequency subbands containing detailed information. By fusing information from different subbands and leveraging the Multi-scale Hybrid Attention (MHA) module, it achieves multi-level feature integration. This process simultaneously enhances fine-grained image features and filters out noise, thereby strengthening the expressive capability for detailed characteristics. Furthermore, this study introduces a fusion strategy incorporating cosine similarity and multi-dimensional statistical features. Related work

This section primarily reviews and summarizes wavelet-based methods in pathological image analysis, aiming to discuss their limitations and challenges. Wavelet decomposition, as an image preprocessing technique that achieves feature enhancement through spatial-frequency domain transformation, has provided robust support for anomaly recognition and classification in pathological images through its ingenious integration with convolutional operations34–38. Furthermore, the need to reduce false positive/false negative detection rates has further propelled the application of wavelet decomposition39. Early studies, such as the application of the Curvelet transform, demonstrated that wavelet decomposition can effectively preserve edge details of malignant pulmonary nodules, significantly reducing classification error rates. Further advancing this line of research, the Non-Subsampled Dual-Tree Complex Contourlet Transform (NSDTCT) captured micro-calcifications and spiculation signs within high-frequency subbands, improving the discrimination accuracy for benign and malignant nodules to 98.37%40, highlighting the critical value of high-frequency components for pathological diagnosis. Addressing the core challenges of feature enhancement and network optimization in breast cancer detection, Oyelade O N et al.41 introduced a Wavelet-CNN-Wavelet architecture. This framework employs a hybrid preprocessing mechanism combining Seam Carving and wavelet decomposition for feature selection, and designs a novel WT function to replace the ReLU activation function, achieving an accuracy of up to 99% on multiple relevant datasets.

Meanwhile, Das A et al.42 employed a Wavelet Convolutional Neural Network (WCNN) to address the limitation of traditional CNNs relying solely on spatial domain features. Their approach integrates wavelet filters and convolutional filters in parallel at each layer, simultaneously capturing both frequency-domain and spatial-domain features of pathological images. After four layers of hybrid feature extraction, their method achieved a classification accuracy of 98.4% on the Kaggle Breast Histopathology dataset. Trivizakis E et al.43 extracted multi-scale pathological features by combining Wavelet Packet Transform (WPT) with Local Binary Patterns (LBP), Gabor filters, and Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM), realizing a 10-fold cross-validation accuracy of 95.3% in colorectal cancer (CRC) histopathological image classification. Ataky S T M et al.44 extracted information-theoretic features from DWT subbands while also deriving biodiversity metrics from original channels. These features were then combined and classified using Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (GBDT). Their method leverages ecological models to capture multi-scale non-linear interactions within textures, enabling the efficient characterization of complex textural properties in histopathological images without requiring data augmentation. This approach achieved outstanding performance on CRC (AUC=0.994) and BreakHis (AUC=0.98) datasets. Addressing the issues of strong subjectivity and low efficiency in manual assessment for prostate cancer Gleason grading, Ali U et al.45 overcame the limitation of traditional DWT decomposing only low-frequency subbands. They performed a 3-level full subband decomposition using Discrete Wavelet Packet Decomposition (DWPD), extracted texture features such as contrast and correlation from each subband using GLCM, and subsequently employed an SVM for cancer grading.

While the aforementioned methods have improved model performance to some extent, most fail to fully exploit the information within the subbands obtained from wavelet decomposition, particularly the high-frequency subbands containing subtle morphological features. These subbands are often merely used as auxiliary features or fused through simple channel concatenation, without adequately mining the discriminative details such as cell edge textures embedded within them. Secondly, these methods lack effective mechanisms for cross-subband interaction and multi-scale fusion, making it difficult to achieve synergistic optimization between local details and global structure. This results in an inability to balance global semantic comprehension with local detail enhancement. Furthermore, when confronted with challenges like staining variations and noise interference, the robustness of feature representations in existing methods still proves insufficient. These limitations collectively constrain the model’s holistic understanding and discriminative capability regarding the multi-scale features in pathological images.

Inspired by Hwang S et al.46, this study proposes a Multi-scale Frequency-domain Hybrid Attention (MFHA) mechanism. This mechanism employs the wavelet transform to achieve frequency-domain decomposition of the image structure. It designs cross-subband interaction and multi-scale fusion strategies aimed at enhancing high-frequency detailed features and promoting the deep integration of local and global information, thereby improving the model’s ability to discern subtle differences and its classification accuracy for pathological images. Furthermore, the mechanism incorporates spatial attention to integrate cosine similarity and multi-dimensional statistical features, achieving a more robust multi-scale feature representation in complex pathological scenarios.

Proposed method

This section details the specifications of the MFHA mechanism. To address the issues of insufficient utilization of subband information and the difficulty in achieving synergy between local details and global structure in existing methods, the MFHA mechanism employs the wavelet transform to decompose the input features in the frequency domain. The image is decomposed into low-frequency subbands, which carry the global structure, and high-frequency subbands, which contain cellular textures and edge information, thereby enabling explicit modeling of detailed features. Building upon this, a multi-branch convolutional architecture and a hybrid attention mechanism are introduced. These components extract multi-scale local features from receptive fields of varying sizes, while simultaneously enhancing the discriminative details within the high-frequency subbands. Furthermore, through a high-frequency information-guided upsampling process and a spatial attention module, the detail reconstruction capability and feature representation robustness are further strengthened, and noise interference is suppressed.

Spatial attention block

To address issues such as weak semantic and structural correlations among multi-scale features after wavelet transform and the susceptibility of detailed information to noise interference, this study designs a spatial attention module that integrates similarity measures and multi-dimensional statistical features. This module aims to enhance the robustness and discriminative power of feature representation by quantitatively assessing semantic consistency between features and reinforcing discriminative regions through multi-dimensional statistical characterization. As illustrated in Figure 5, the module adopts a dual-branch parallel architecture: one branch calculates the correlation between local and global features via cosine similarity to measure the degree of semantic matching. The other branch extracts multi-dimensional statistical features to characterize the distribution properties and saliency of the features. These two streams of information are then concatenated and fused through multi-scale convolution to generate a spatial attention weight map. This map is used to recalibrate the input features, highlighting crucial regions and suppressing irrelevant responses.

Experiments

Datasets

This study utilizes two datasets to evaluate the performance of MFHA: the publicly available Sipakmed dataset49 and a set of 2012 H&E-stained whole-slide images (WSIs) of lung cancer pathological specimens for gene mutation prediction, provided by a medical center in Chongqing, China.

SipakMed is a classic public dataset of cervical cytology smears, comprising five classes of samples: Normal, Inflammation, Light Dysplasia, Moderate Dysplasia, and Severe Dysplasia/Carcinoma in situ. The dataset consists of 4,049 isolated cell images cropped from 966 cell cluster images and is primarily designed for cell-image-level classification tasks to evaluate a model’s ability to distinguish the subtle morphological differences among cervical cells at various lesion grades. The core challenges of this dataset lie in the blurred morphological boundaries between lesional and normal cells, the high morphological heterogeneity among cells of the same lesion class, and the noise interference introduced by uneven smear staining.

The lung cancer gene mutation pathological image dataset is shown in Table 1. This dataset is divided into 12 categories, encompassing various types of gene mutations. However, it is a highly class-imbalanced dataset (for instance, the EGFR genotype accounts for nearly 50% of the entire dataset). Therefore, to ensure experimental rationality and fairness, this study processes the dataset into two distinct binary classification subsets: one classifying cases with any gene mutation versus those without any gene mutation, and another specifically classifying cases with an EGFR mutation versus those without an EGFR mutation (as detailed in Table 2). This approach aims to balance the class distribution as much as possible and to thoroughly evaluate the model’s performance.

Experimental on gene mutation

Implementation details on gene mutation

WSI pathological image classification adopts the mainstream two-stage processing method, consisting of a patch-level feature encoder and a slide-level global aggregation encoder50,51. To effectively preserve and enhance high-frequency detail information before the Transformer performs feature aggregation, this study integrates MFHA with Swin Transformer52 to construct a novel hybrid encoding network, MFTrans, which serves as the feature encoder (Figure 6). Compared to traditional shallow sub-band concatenation methods or single-channel/spatial attention mechanisms, MFHA employs explicit cross-sub-band attention modeling and multi-receptive-field feature fusion, enabling the MFTrans network to capture subtle morphological features at the cellular level through MFHA while leveraging the Transformer architecture to model long-range dependencies and global contextual relationships. The global aggregation encoder adopts a LongNet-based53 global aggregation encoder54 for feature integration across the whole slide.

WSI pathological analysis and diagnosis pipeline: (a) Feature encoder incorporating the MFHA mechanism; (b) Global aggregation encoder built upon LongNet.

Specifically, background processing is first performed on the input WSI to remove blank background regions without pathological significance through edge detection, retaining only effective tissue regions that contain tumor and normal tissue. To control the H&E staining heterogeneity and batch effects caused by differences in slide preparation batches, staining equipment, and operators, this study applies the Macenko stain normalization method to standardize all effective tissue regions. This method estimates the reference stain vectors of hematoxylin (nuclear stain) and eosin (cytoplasmic/stromal stain) in the image via non-negative matrix factorization, transforms the original image into a unified standard stain space, effectively eliminates non-pathological interference such as staining intensity variations, hue deviations, and contrast differences, enhances the feature consistency of pathological images from different sources, and provides stable and reliable input for subsequent feature extraction. Subsequently, the standardized tissue regions are divided into multiple 224×224 pixel patches (MPP=1.0).

These patches are fed into a feature encoder based on the MFTrans architecture (Figure 6a), which first enhances texture information and global correlations through MFHA, then extracts features via a 4-stage Swin Transformer to generate patch-level embedding representations. This encoder was not pre-trained using contrastive learning; instead, it was first pre-trained on ImageNet-1K with an image size of 224×224 and a batch size of 128 for 120 epochs, and then further trained on the pathology dataset LDCH55 to adapt to pathological features. Subsequently, the patch-level embeddings are passed into a global aggregation encoder (Figure 6b), which is loaded with pre-trained LongNet weights54. During encoding, the Dilated Attention53 mechanism is adopted to capture long-range cross-patch dependencies, ultimately yielding target tile embeddings and completing the extraction of semantic embeddings from local to global.

It should be noted that, due to computational resource constraints, this experiment only trains the final classification head on the lung cancer gene mutation WSI dataset, without performing end-to-end fine-tuning of the patch-level feature encoder and the slide-level global aggregation encoder. Although this experimental setup allows for a fair comparison of the inherent representational capacity of different feature extractors under strictly controlled variables, eliminating the interference caused by differences in fine-tuning strategies, it also introduces certain limitations. Since the pre-trained encoder has not undergone targeted domain adaptation to the lung cancer gene mutation pathology data in this study, it may fail to fully capture morphological features specific to lung cancer tissue, such as glandular structural variations and nuclear atypia, as well as the subtle pathological patterns corresponding to different gene mutation types, thereby limiting the upper bound of the model’s classification performance to some extent.

Moreover, this frozen-encoder training approach weakens the model’s ability to learn the distribution characteristics of the current dataset, which may adversely affect its cross-domain generalization capability on heterogeneous lung cancer pathology data collected from different medical centers and using different staining equipment. Future research will, when computational resources permit, carry out hierarchical joint fine-tuning of the encoder and the classification head, progressively unfreezing network layers and adopting a low learning rate optimization strategy, so as to further tap the model’s performance potential and enhance its clinical utility while avoiding overfitting.

The classification task employs the Adam56 optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.002, a batch size of 1, and cross-entropy as the loss function, and is trained for 40 epochs on a 4×DCU 16G computing node. To rigorously prevent patient-level data leakage, a common issue in pathological image analysis, this study adopts a strict patient-wise two-fold Monte Carlo Cross Validation (MCCV)57 strategy: in each fold, the patient cohort is independently and randomly partitioned into a training set and a test set at a ratio of 7:3, ensuring that all whole slide images and their derived image patches from the same patient belong exclusively to a single set, thereby completely eliminating the possibility of the same patient’s data appearing in both the training and test sets. A total of 2,012 independent patients (each corresponding to one H&E-stained lung cancer WSI) are included in this study, with approximately 1,408 patients in the training set and 604 patients in the test set per fold. During training, a weighted sampling strategy based on class distribution is adopted to mitigate the inherent class imbalance problem of the dataset.

Specifically, identical WSI partitions were processed to extract pathological features using MFTrans and the baseline model, respectively, as the patch-level encoder. These features were then passed through the same slide-level encoder for final classification.

It is noteworthy that in the EGFR mutation dataset, despite a more balanced sample ratio (approximately 1:1.12) compared to the general gene mutation dataset, the magnitude and absolute values of the model’s performance improvement are lower than those observed in the general gene mutation task. An in-depth analysis suggests that this phenomenon may be related to the complexity of pathological features and the inherent differences in task nature. Specifically, in the general gene mutation classification task, the positive class encompasses various types of gene mutations. These mutations may manifest through significant common pathological features (such as abnormal cell proliferation, disorganized tissue structure, and nuclear morphological distortion), and the differences between positive samples and the negative class (wild-type, WT) samples are more pronounced in terms of both global semantics and local details. The MFHA mechanism in MFTrans effectively captures these shared characteristics by decomposing and fusing low-frequency global information and high-frequency details via the wavelet transform, learning multi-level semantic features. Simultaneously, the dual upsampling fusion reinforces the perception of high-frequency components, further amplifying the morphological differences associated with mutations, leading to superior performance in this task.

In contrast, EGFR mutation represents a single type of genetic alteration. Its pathological features might be more subtle and exhibit a higher degree of overlap with the characteristics of negative class samples. For instance, pathological patterns of other mutations like KRAS or ALK might partially resemble those of EGFR mutations, increasing the difficulty of feature discrimination. Secondly, the composition of the negative class in this dataset is more complex. This category includes not only wild-type (WT) samples but also samples with other gene mutations. These samples do not present a single pathological pattern; instead, they exhibit diverse morphological features, resulting in a more dispersed feature distribution. This effectively requires the model to identify a single target feature from a variety of non-target characteristics, necessitating handling more complex feature distribution overlaps. Consequently, the absolute values of the overall performance metrics are lower in this task compared to the previous one.

Experimental on sipakmed

Implementation details on sipakmed

This experiment focuses on the comparative validation of backbone networks and attention mechanisms. Given the lightweight and plug-and-play nature of MFHA, it was simply embedded into the backbone networks of ResNet58 and Vision Transformer59 (Figure 7a,b) for performance comparison with mainstream attention mechanisms and wavelet-transform-related methods in recent years.

MFHA mechanism embedding location.

To ensure that integrating MFHA into the backbone network effectively enhances the extraction of detailed features, this study designed corresponding embedding strategies tailored to the architectural characteristics of ResNet and Transformer. Specifically, as the bottleneck layers of ResNet are key nodes for feature transformation and information fusion, MFHA was embedded between these bottleneck layers. This utilizes MFHA to augment local details during the feature flow process, preventing the dilution of detailed information caused by deep networks. Conversely, ViT primarily relies on the self-attention mechanism to model global dependencies and is relatively less sensitive to local high-frequency details in the input features. Therefore, MFHA was placed at the beginning of the network to perform frequency-domain decomposition and detail enhancement on the raw input before global feature extraction, thereby providing richer and more discriminative feature representations for subsequent attention layers.

The dataset was divided into training and test sets in an 8:2 ratio. During training, a batch size of 128 was used with the Adam optimizer, a learning rate of 0.001, and weight decay of 1e-4. The cross-entropy loss function was employed for 120 training epochs. Evaluation was conducted using four metrics: Accuracy, F1 Score, Precision, and Recall.

Specifically, lesion features in cervical tissue, such as nuclear enlargement and disordered cell arrangement, are primarily reflected in high-frequency components, whereas the overall tissue structure constitutes low-frequency information. MFHA employs wavelet decomposition to separate features into low-frequency global (LL) sub-bands and high-frequency edge-detail (LH/HL) sub-bands. It then fuses these different sub-bands to capture multi-level semantics. Its dual upsampling fusion mechanism selectively enhances high-frequency details while simultaneously filtering out staining noise. The spatial attention module integrates cosine similarity with multi-dimensional statistical features, which helps measure the alignment between global and local characteristics while capturing cell density fluctuations, thereby strengthening the features within lesion regions. In comparison, mechanisms like CBAM rely solely on spatial-domain weighting and struggle to distinguish meaningful high-frequency details from noise. Transformer variants such as ViT, although effective at modeling global dependencies, exhibit limited capability in resolving high-frequency microscopic features, a gap that MFHA is designed to address.

Furthermore, the increase in parameter count and computational cost of MFHA compared to other methods is minimal and almost negligible. This indicates that its performance improvement does not rely on additional computational overhead but stems from its unique feature enhancement mechanism and local-global collaborative modeling capability.

Ablation experiments and interpretability

To validate the effectiveness of each core module in MFHA, this study conducts an ablation experiment using ResNet as the backbone network on the Sipakmed pathological dataset. Performance changes are compared by incrementally adding the wavelet-guided up/downsampling module (Wavelet) and the spatial attention module (Spatial).

The experimental results are presented in Table 5. It can be observed that when only the Wavelet module was added, the model’s accuracy increased by 2.22% and the F1-score improved by 2.43%, indicating that wavelet decomposition enhances high-frequency details and that multi-scale fusion effectively captures pathological features. When only the Spatial module was added, accuracy improved by 3.21% and the F1-score increased by 3.33%, validating that the spatial attention mechanism constructs robust representations through cosine similarity and multi-dimensional statistical features. When both the Wavelet and Spatial modules were combined, accuracy further increased to 95.56%, and the F1-score reached 0.9573, significantly outperforming the individual modules. This demonstrates that the frequency-domain features provided by wavelet decomposition offer more precise guidance for spatial attention, while spatial attention reinforces the weighting of key areas in the frequency domain, resulting in a synergistic optimization of frequency-domain analysis and spatial focusing.

Conclusion

This study addresses the key issues of insufficient utilization of detailed information and the lack of multi-scale interaction in frequency-domain methods for pathological image classification, proposing a Multi-scale Frequency-domain Hybrid Attention mechanism (MFHA). This mechanism employs the wavelet transform to decompose images into low-frequency global structural and high-frequency local detail sub-bands. By integrating multi-scale convolution and sub-band fusion strategies, it effectively enhances high-frequency discriminative features, achieving deep synergistic representation of global semantics and local details. Furthermore, a spatial attention module that incorporates cosine similarity and multi-dimensional statistical features is introduced to enhance the robustness and discriminative power of features in complex pathological scenarios. Experiments conducted on the public Sipakmed dataset and a lung cancer gene mutation pathological dataset demonstrate that MFHA outperforms existing methods across multiple evaluation metrics, validating its effectiveness in fine-grained pathological image classification tasks.

Despite the promising performance demonstrated by MFHA in the experiments, this study has several limitations. First, although the wavelet decomposition and reconstruction processes in MFHA preserve most high-frequency information, the completeness of detail reconstruction could be further improved under conditions of extreme noise or significant staining variation. Second, the current method primarily processes individual pathological images and does not yet fully consider the spatial contextual relationships and multi-scale semantic continuity across different regions within a whole-slide image, leaving room for further refinement in the coherence of the overall diagnostic logic.

Future work will explore the following directions in depth. First, we aim to extend the applicability of MFHA to heterogeneous pathological data collected from multiple centers and devices, further enhancing the model’s clinical generalization capability through domain adaptation or meta-learning strategies. Second, we will pursue deeper integration of MFHA with architectures such as Vision Transformers and Graph Neural Networks to construct a unified framework capable of simultaneously modeling local details, long-range dependencies, and topological relationships. Third, we will explore the application of MFHA to a wider range of fine-grained pathological classification tasks, such as tumor subtype grading, immunohistochemical prediction, and prognostic assessment, to promote its practical implementation in precision medicine.
