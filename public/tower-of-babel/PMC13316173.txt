What drives family physicians to rural practice? A discrete choice experiment in Vietnam
Authors: Cam Anh Thi Pham, Nguyen Quynh Trang Le, Dohyeong Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite commune health stations serving as the backbone of Vietnam's primary healthcare, severe physician maldistribution persists. To explore factors influencing the recruitment and retention of family physicians in rural areas, we conducted a discrete choice experiment among 315 family physicians in Vietnam. Mixed logit regression was used to estimate the relative importance of individual characteristics and key job attributes, which included income change, career promotion, living condition, and government support for private clinics. Our findings indicate that a 50% increase in salary has the most significant influence on rural job uptake for key subgroups, including those under 50, assistant doctors, and those with prior rural exposure. However, the marginal utility of financial incentives diminishes rapidly beyond a specific threshold. Subgroup analysis further reveals heterogeneous preferences: physicians who transitioned from commune health stations to higher-level facilities, and those working in primary health care areas, respond more strongly to non-financial incentives such as career promotion and private practice support than to additional income increases. Physicians would forgo 48.36% of their salary to secure an urban posting, yet would accept a rural position if offered expedited promotion or private practice support, each valued at approximately 38% of monthly income. Achieving sustainable rural deployment therefore requires differentiated rather than uniform intervention: moderate salary increases for younger and rural-exposed physicians, combined with accelerated promotion pathways and private practice facilitation for those in primary health care and higher-level facilities. Translating stated preferences into actual rural uptake ultimately hinges on government credibility in delivering promised benefits.Keywords: Discrete choice experiment, Family physicians, Management Reform, Public Utility

Introduction

Background

Approximately 4.5 billion people, which is more than half of the world's population, currently lack access to essential healthcare services, partly due to a projected shortfall of 18 million health workers by 2030 1., [2], 3.. One recommendation by the WHO to alleviate the shortage of skilled health professionals and enhance the accessibility of frontline services is to maximize the presence of health workers in primary health care and family medicine [4], [5], [6]. Nonetheless, in developing and underdeveloped countries, a severe shortage of healthcare personnel in primary care, particularly in disadvantaged areas, remains a major barrier to achieving comprehensive national health coverage [7], [8], 9..

This study investigates factors influencing healthcare workers' job placement decisions, focusing on family doctors in Vietnam. As a lower-middle-income country, Vietnam's healthcare services are provided by both public and private sectors, with private medical facilities predominantly concentrated in urban areas 9., [10]. As a core public utility service, commune-level primary healthcare, including family medicine, places the state under an obligation to ensure equitable access, particularly for rural and disadvantaged populations. Attracting and retaining qualified personnel is therefore central to Vietnam's public service management reform agenda. Since the reunification of North and South Vietnam in 1975 and the subsequent market reforms of Doi Moi in 1986, Vietnam's healthcare system has evolved with its political and economic changes. Yet, the maldistribution of health workers across these tiers is acute. Commune health stations (CHSs) at the grassroots tier manage approximately 80% of local primary care demand, yet fewer than 18% of the total health workforce operates at this level [10]. In the poorest 62 districts, only 30% of CHSs are staffed with a physician, well below the government's target of 80% [11]. Urban public hospitals, by contrast, operate at 150-250% patient capacity, reflecting the downstream effect of primary care avoidance [10], [12]. In response to these systemic weaknesses, the government has increasingly positioned family physicians as a cornerstone of the primary care network. While family medicine has been a recognized specialty since 2001, supported by ongoing initiatives like the Family Physician Scheme 2025-2030, training more doctors hasn't yet solved the staffing crisis. Yet only 40-65% of graduates return to CHSs after specializing, and retention among those who do remains a persistent challenge [13].

Despite several targeted policies, none specifically addresses family physicians, and none has produced measurable improvements in CHS staffing levels [10], [11]. Moreover, total income of rural doctors, when informal earnings are excluded, remains substantially lower than that of urban counterparts even when official salaries are higher [11], suggesting that purely financial approaches face structural limitations. These repeated failures underscore the need for evidence-based approaches that move beyond isolated financial incentives.

Literature review

Extant literature has extensively examined the drivers behind health workers' location preferences. Generally, these determinants are classified into three broad categories: personal characteristics, living conditions, and work-related incentives.

Among work factors, financial compensation is the most universally cited determinant. In Vietnam, qualitative research across multiple provinces identifies inadequate salary as the primary driver of rural attrition [14], [15]. The income gap is structural rather than merely administrative: although rural doctors receive slightly higher official salaries (VND 3.1 million vs. VND 2.9 million/month), their total income is roughly half that of their urban peers once informal earnings, such as patient gifts, hospital profit-sharing, and dual employment, are factored in [11]. In a national cross-sectional study, approximately 74% of health workers reported that monthly income does not cover living expenses [16]. Consistent with prior findings, DCE evidence from China confirms income as the dominant predictor of rural uptake, with a salary increase from ¥3000 to ¥5000 raising rural job acceptance by 33% among township health physicians [17]. While incentive effects are generally consistent in direction, their magnitude varies across contexts, making uniform policies ineffective. Studies across Tanzania and rural China confirms that salary increases raise rural job acceptance substantially up to a threshold, beyond which additional increments yield diminishing returns and non-financial attributes become relatively more cost-effective [17], [18].

Career development represents the second most influential incentive category, yet its importance is systematically underweighted in Vietnam's policy framework. Witter [15] rank training and promotion third and fourth among factors motivating rural doctors in Vietnam, after salary and job stability. This ranking aligns with DCE studies in Tanzania, China, Mozambique, and Australia [17], [18], [19], [20]. The literature also suggests that age plays a crucial role in these preferences, for instance, young physicians place a higher value on training opportunities and fewer working hours compared to older doctors. Notably, for physicians who have already transitioned from commune health stations to higher-level facilities, career promotion outperforms additional salary as a retention lever [11], [15].

Personal characteristics, particularly geographical origin and rural training experience, are among the most robust predictors of willingness to accept rural postings across settings. Rural-born physicians practice rurally at substantially higher rates than their urban-born counterparts, a finding replicated across multiple settings [18], [21], [22]. Rural-trained graduates also show markedly higher local retention rates than urban-trained peers [23].

Given the complex interplay of these factors, single-intervention policies are often insufficient. Van Lerberghe [8] and others [2], [24], [25] emphasize that durable retention requires multilevel strategies that integrate career development, working conditions, and administrative support.

Research objectives

Although the literature offers numerous retention strategies, their applicability to the Vietnamese context remains limited as incentive effectiveness is highly contingent on local contexts and demographics. Within Vietnam, local evidence is constrained on two methodological grounds. First, existing qualitative and cross-sectional studies identify relevant factors but fail to quantify their relative importance or the income trade-offs physicians are prepared to accept [14], [15]. Second, the only prior DCE in Vietnam [21] lacked a focus on the emerging specialty of family medicine and did not conduct systematic subgroup analyses. Consequently, there is a critical lack of evidence on how preferences differ across demographic profiles or professional trajectories.

This study therefore addresses these gaps by quantifying how individual characteristics and job attributes interact to shape family doctors' location preferences in Vietnam, a lower-middle income country navigating the transition toward a family medicine-based primary care system. While the findings directly inform targeted, fiscally sustainable interventions under Vietnam's Family Physician Scheme 2025-2030, the evidence on preference heterogeneity and threshold effects of financial incentives is likely transferable to other low-middle-income countries where family medicine is emerging and rural physician shortages persist.

Methods

We employed a Discrete Choice Experiment (DCE) to quantify how job attributes and individual characteristics jointly shape family doctors' location preferences.

Defining attributes and levels

Initially, a comprehensive list of attributes and their levels was gathered from literature and policy reviews. This was followed by focus group discussions with six family doctors and in-depth interviews with key national and provincial decision-makers to determine the most influential attributes affecting family doctors' job location choices: (a) income change, (b) career development, (c) living condition, and (d) ease of starting medical practice. Especially, the ease of establishing a private clinic, an attribute unique to Vietnam's regulatory environment (where family physicians hold the legal right to open multi-specialty practices), only emerged from focus group discussions as a key career motivation. Attributes and levels.

The suggested levels for three attributes: career development, living condition, and ease of starting medical practice were deemed suitable by both the focus groups and key policymakers, with two mutually exclusive levels for each. Income change was purposely structured to explore trade-offs: a 30% decrease, no change, and 50% and 100% increases, revealing the threshold at which income became less crucial compared to factors such as career advancement, living conditions, and opportunities for private practice. To avoid any overlap between the income and career development attributes, monetary benefits associated with promotion, such as management allowances, were explicitly excluded from the income definition and treated solely as a benefit of career advancement.

Construction of choice sets

Following guidelines of the ideal number of choice tasks, 10 choice tasks per respondent were used, administered in two randomized blocks to reduce burden while maintaining statistical adequacy [26], [27].

With four attributes at varying levels, the total number of possible attribute-level combinations was 32 (23 × 41 = 32), yielding 496 unique choice sets. Since presenting all possible sets was infeasible, a D-efficiency design was generated using Sawtooth Choice-Based Conjoint (CBC) software, which prohibited dominated alternatives and balanced the frequency of each attribute-level across the overall design.

Of the 10 choice cards in each block, two duplicate choice cards per block served as internal consistency checks. Each choice set presented two unlabelled hypothetical jobs (Job A vs. Job B) alongside the respondent's current job as a status quo option. Jobs were labelled generically as Job A and Job B rather than as “rural post” and “urban post” to elicit attribute-level trade-offs unconfounded by prior label associations [18].

The current job scenario for each respondent was constructed from their responses to the supplementary questionnaire, ensuring that no information was lost even when respondents chose to remain in their current position. Specifically, living conditions were derived from the respondent's current institution location. Career development was inferred from the average number of years until promotion eligibility. Ease of starting private medical practice was derived from the respondent's self-reported level of government support for opening a private clinic. Income was assigned the “no change” level.

Alongside the choice sets, a supplementary questionnaire gathered data on demographics and personal experiences for two key reasons: (a) to examine how individual factors interact with incentives to influence job location decisions, and (b) to obtain data for the status quo option [19]. The questionnaire sought details on (i) personal information of family doctors, (ii) current employment, and (iii) work history. A sample choice set is shown in Fig. 1.

Data collection

The study was conducted across five major medical universities including Hanoi Medical University, Haiphong University of Medicine and Pharmacy, Hue University of Medicine and Pharmacy, Ho Chi Minh University of Medicine and Pharmacy, and Pham Ngoc Thach University of Medicine. These five institutions are located in the four largest cities, and together cover all geographic regions: north, central, and south, enabling the study to capture characteristics of family physicians from diverse regional contexts. The final sample consists of 315 family physicians and physician assistants with active clinical experience. Subgroups were categorized based on professional status, work history, and demographic factors, with an age threshold of 50 reflecting Vietnamese retirement norms.

The survey instruments were validated through one-on-one pretesting and a pilot study to ensure technical clarity and cognitive feasibility. Three enumerators completed two days of standardized training, which included a cheap talk script designed to mitigate hypothetical bias by emphasizing the importance of realistic responses. Surveys were administered face-to-face in private settings to encourage authentic decision-making. Participants first completed a supplementary questionnaire regarding their demographics and work history, which provided the data necessary to personalize the status quo option. This was followed by 10 randomly ordered DCE choice tasks.

Econometric model

Discrete choice modeling is grounded in Random Utility Theory [28], which posits that a rational individual n selects the alternative i that maximizes their utility. Since survey respondents (n) are assumed to receive utility (Uni) from selecting option i in a choice situation, the utility Uni that respondent n obtains from alternative i is Uni=βixni+ϵni, where xni is the vector of alternative-specific variables of alternative i relative to respondent n and the individual-specific variable characteristics of respondent n. βiis the vector of coefficients, and ϵni is an unobserved error term.

Three logit specifications were estimated sequentially to identify the best-fitting model. The conditional logit model treats job attributes as the sole determinants of choice, assuming a fixed coefficient vector β constant across all respondents. The multinomial logit model extends this by incorporating respondent-specific characteristics as additional predictors, allowing coefficients to vary across alternatives. The mixed logit (MXL) model was ultimately selected as the primary analytical framework, as it produced the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values among the three specifications. This specification allows the coefficients β to be randomly distributed among respondents and accommodates individual-specific characteristics, thereby capturing preference heterogeneity across physicians. In this study, the attributes living condition, career development, and ease of starting private medical practice were specified as random parameters following a normal distribution, while income was specified as fixed to ensure a theoretically consistent sign on its utility estimate, with 500 Halton draws used for simulation [29].

Results

Descriptive statistics

Among the 315 valid responses, 116 were collected in Haiphong, 99 in Ho Chi Minh, 65 in Hue, and 35 in Hanoi (Table 3). Gender distribution was nearly equal, with an average participant age of 45 years. Half of the respondents were born in rural areas, worked as assistant doctors, and had around 20 years of experience in the health sector. Family doctors in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh were more commonly employed at high-level urban health facilities than those in Haiphong and Hue. In terms of income, Ho Chi Minh family doctors earned the most, averaging VND 11 million (USD 500), followed by Hue (USD 400), while Hanoi and Haiphong doctors earned about USD 300 monthly.

Choice modeling with choice set attributes

We employed multinominal logit, conditional logit, and mixed logit models to estimate job choice probabilities. The reference job for salary regression was defined by: (a) placement at a rural commune health station (CHS), (b) over one year requirement for promotion eligibility, and (c) difficulty starting private practice due to limited government support.

All three models confirmed that career promotion, private clinic support, living conditions, and income were positive and significant predictors of job choice (p < 0.01). The MXL model was selected as the primary framework based on its superior AIC and BIC values (Table 4). Significant standard deviations across attributes confirmed substantial preference heterogeneity among respondents.

Policy impact by age. An age threshold of 50 was selected based on focus group insights and confirmed by significant heterogeneity in subgroup and interaction-term analyses. In Vietnam's five-year promotion cycle, physicians over 50 have insufficient time remaining before mandatory retirement to benefit from promotion, shifting their priority toward private practice as a post-retirement income source. Quantitatively, family physicians aged 50 and older are more responsive to financial incentives than their younger counterparts, though the impact of salary increases diminishes beyond a 50% boost. For those 50 and older, a 50% salary increase boosts rural job acceptance by 22% points, with an additional 50% increase adding 21% points. For those under 50, the boosts are 18-20% points. Once a certain wage level is reached, alternative policies should be considered. Fast-track promotions are equally effective as financial incentives for younger physicians, while older physicians prefer private clinic opportunities, which increase acceptance by 28% points.

Policy impact by rural exposure. Policies are more effective for those with rural backgrounds such as being rural-born or having rural work experience with monetary incentives proving most impactful. For rural-born physicians, a 50% increase in wages raises the likelihood of accepting a CHS job by 26% points, while an additional 50% increase adds 23% points. In contrast, urban-born physicians see increases of 15% points each. Fast-track promotions and private clinic opportunities raise the acceptance rate by 16-19% points, respectively.

Policy impact by leaving CHSs to higher level health facilities. Providing a fast-track career promotion and opportunities to launch a private clinic increased the likelihood of family doctors accepting a CHS job by 21-25% points, respectively. This effect is significantly greater than the 16% points increase resulting from a 50% boost in total income.

Policy impact by primary health care area. Family doctors involved in primary health care value both financial and non-financial incentives more than others. Offering a fast-track career promotion and opportunities to open a private clinic increased the likelihood of these doctors accepting a CHS job by 24-27% points, respectively.

Discussion

This research is the first known study to quantify family doctors' preferences for job attributes in Vietnam. It is unsurprising that financial incentives have the most significant influence on family physicians' job location decisions in Vietnam. Nearly all respondents reported feeling undercompensated relative to their workload and qualifications, consistent with evidence from rural Vietnam [11], rural China [17], and transitional health systems in Central Asia [30].

Remarkably, the study suggests that relying solely on income to attract and retain health workers in rural areas may be ineffective. Existing policies which offer allowances of 30-50% of salary have failed to meaningfully shift job location decisions [10], [11]. Our study reveals a clear threshold effect regarding monetary incentives: increasing current income by 50% was the most effective factor influencing job location decisions for many subgroups, including those under 50, with rural internship experience, assistant doctors, and those with commune-level work experience. Beyond this point, the effectiveness of wage increases sharply diminishes. Consequently, targeting a 50% income increase would yield the optimal effect, suggesting that the 70% allowance stipulated in Decree 64/2009/ND-CP may be unnecessarily costly without providing proportional retention benefits.

Furthermore, this study highlights that preference for job attributes varies significantly across subgroups, rendering a “one-size-fits-all” approach impractical and costly [12], 31., [32], [33]. While financial incentives are highly effective for the aforementioned groups, they are less impactful for others. Doctors transitioning from CHSs to higher-level facilities, national-level employees, and specific primary care physicians place a much higher value on non-pecuniary benefits, specifically rapid career advancement and opportunities to open private clinics. Non-financial incentives should therefore be prioritized as a cost-effective complement to financial measures, particularly for subgroups where monetary incentives show diminishing returns.

Consistent with previous studies [19], [22], [23], a physician's background and early exposure are strong predictors of their future practice location. Rural-born health workers as well as those with prior rural internship experience, exhibit a significantly higher willingness to accept rural posts under the same incentive conditions. To capitalize on this, the Ministry of Health and the Ministry of Education and Training should jointly establish a formalized rural pipeline. This involves two key actions: first, implementing preferential medical school admission quotas for applicants with rural backgrounds; and second, mandating rural clinical rotations and internships within the core medical curriculum to build familiarity and confidence in primary care settings early in a doctor's career.

A critical insight from this study is the inefficiency of applying generic incentives to higher-level physicians. Family doctors currently working at provincial, national, or university hospitals exhibit a pronounced preference for urban settings, which substantially diminishes the impact of financial incentives. When these urban doctors do consider relocating to rural areas, it is rarely due to government incentive policies. When relocation does occur among this group, it is driven primarily by personal factors, notably retirement planning and urban quality-of-life concerns among those aged 50 and above, rather than policy incentives. Consequently, an efficient allocation of the healthcare budget requires shifting the policy focus away from mid-career urban specialists. Resources should be strategically directed toward subgroups that demonstrate a higher baseline propensity for rural practice, namely assistant doctors, district-level physicians, and the existing commune-level workforce. For primary healthcare physicians, who show the greatest inclination toward rural practice, targeted residency-period support, including streamlined private clinic pathways and structured promotion tracks, is likely to improve long-term retention.

Ultimately, the simulated uptake of rural jobs in a discrete choice experiment relies on the assumption that promised policies will be enacted. However, our qualitative feedback indicates skepticism about whether authorities will keep their incentive promises, which could lead to an overestimation of job uptake. Hence, alongside financial and non-financial incentives, it is essential for authorities to establish credibility by fulfilling their commitments to providing the promised benefits.

Conclusion

There is a notable gap in empirical research concerning health workers' decision-making regarding job locations. This research adds to the dearth of literature by investigating the strategies for attracting and retaining family doctors in rural settings within low- and middle-income countries. Utilizing a discrete choice experiment approach, the study explores the intricate factors influencing health professionals' decision-making.

Policymakers should replace uniform subsidies with targeted incentive bundles. Retention is best achieved when a 50% salary increase is paired with non-financial benefits, such as accelerated promotion and private practice support. Additionally, prioritizing rural-oriented medical education and recruiting subgroups with a higher propensity for rural practice, rather than mid-career urban specialists, will ensure sustainable coverage. Finally, translating these policy preferences into actual rural deployment requires the government to establish credibility by strictly fulfilling its promised incentives.

This study is subject to a number of restrictions worth acknowledging. First, as with all DCE studies, stated preferences may not fully translate into actual job uptake, though this was partially mitigated by the cheap talk script administered during data collection. Secondly, this study primarily examines the impact of incentive policies on labor market decisions, rather than their implementation costs. While the findings offer guidance on feasible policies, a thorough cost analysis is essential due to budget constraints. Additionally, limited sample size in some subgroups restricts the statistical analysis of family doctors' labor market decisions. Future research should address these gaps through cost-benefit analyses of incentive policies, longitudinal tracking of actual practice decisions, and qualitative work to capture physicians' lived experiences.
