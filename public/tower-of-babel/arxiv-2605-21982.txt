Abstract
. We investigate the category of “matricial order operator spaces,” which generalize operator systems, being equipped with both matricial norms and matricial order. For these objects, we develop duality theory. Taking a cue from the theory of ordered normed spaces, we introduce two important properties describing the interplay between order and norm – “normality” and “generation,” and show that they are dual to each other. As examples, we consider operator systems (in particular, C ∗ -algebras), and Schatten spaces. We also describe the minimal and maximal matricial order structures (which, again, turn out to be in duality), and show how Banach lattices can be equipped with such structures.



1.
Introduction

The present paper is devoted to investigating matricial order operator spaces (defined below). Our goal is to unify two streams of Functional Analysis: (i) the theory of ordered normed spaces, and (ii) operator space theory.

Ordered spaces (such as function spaces) have been studied since the dawn of functional analysis. For a general approach, we refer the reader to e.g.
[
6
]
or
[
4
]
. Below, we recall the facts and definitions relevant to this work.

Operator spaces arose in the late 1980s, as subspaces of
C^{*}
-algebras. Alternatively, one equips a given normed space with a matricial structure, satisfying the so called Ruan’s Axioms. Accessible monographs on the topic include
[
26
]
,
[
28
]
, and
[
14
]
; again, the most pertinent results are recalled below.

From the beginning of operator space theory, people were interested in (
unital
)
operator systems
– that is, involution-invariant subspaces of
B(H)
which contain the identity, and hence naturally carry “matricial order” (see e.g.
[
26
]
).
Instead of concrete embeddings into
B(H)
, one can give “abstract” descriptions of such objects, involving interactions of the unit, the matricial norms, and the matricial order, see
[
11
]
.

Recently, general (not necessarily unital) ordered operator spaces have come into focus, due to their connections to non-commutative convexity (see e.g.
[
21
]
).
Here, the order is required to satisfy certain conditions (which we make explicit below), but doesn’t need to arise from a specific embedding into
B(H)
.
In fact,
[
20
]
already notes the difficulty of obtaining such embeddings of duals of
C^{*}
-algebras.

All this points to the necessity of developing a general theory of “matricial order” on operator spaces, which is the purpose of the present work.
We begin this paper by laying the necessary foundation. In
Section

2
, we briefly introduce the notion of an ordered Banach space, and review “order” versions of local reflexivity.
Apart from being of independent interest, local reflexivity will also be used to describe the duality between minimal and maximal matricial order structures in
Section

7
.

Section

3
examines matricially normed spaces (operator spaces), and also matricial convexity and duality.

We then move on to the core of the present work – namely, the examination of matricial order.
Section

4
defines
matricial order operator spaces
, and introduces their basic properties, such as normality and generation (their classical counterparts are described in
Section

2
).
In
Section

5
we describe a way to renorm a normal, geneating matricial order space to make it
1
-normal and
1
-generating.

It is easy to observe that the dual of a matrical order operator space can be equipped with a matricial order operator space structure. In
Section

6
we show that the dual of
\mathcal{X}
is normal if and only if
\mathcal{X}
itself is generating, and vice versa.

Taking a cue from the theory of operator spaces, in
Section

7
we define the maximal and minimal matricial order structures on a given ordered Banach space, and show that they are in duality. We also develop criteria for normality and generation of these “maximal” and “minimal” matricial order operator spaces.

Section

8
is devoted to investigating “natural” examples of matricial order operator spaces – namely,
C^{*}
-algebras (and operator systems more generally) and Schatten spaces. In
Section

9
we study a new class of matricial order operator spaces obtained by equipping Banach lattices with their minimal or the maximal structures.

2.
Ordered vector spaces and local reflexivity

In this section, we discuss ordered real Banach spaces, and, in particular, the specifics of local reflexivity therein. For unexplained facts and notation, we refer the reader to
[
4
]

[

]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is a
real
topological vector space. Following
[
4
]
, we say that a closed set
\mathcal{X}^{+}\subset\mathcal{X}
is a
wedge
in
\mathcal{X}
if (i)
\mathcal{X}^{+}+\mathcal{X}^{+}\subset\mathcal{X}^{+}
, (ii)
\mathbb{R}^{+}\mathcal{X}^{+}=\mathcal{X}^{+}
.

\mathcal{X}^{+}
is called a (
positive
)
cone
if, in addition, it is
pointed
– that is,
\mathcal{X}^{+}\cap(-\mathcal{X}^{+})=\{0\}
.

Such a wedge turns
\mathcal{X}
into an
ordered space
: we say that
x\geq y
if
x-y\in\mathcal{X}^{+}
. If
\mathcal{X}^{+}
is a cone, then
x=y
iff
x\geq y
and
y\geq x
.

For

, we use a shorthand
A^{+}
for
A\cap\mathcal{X}^{+}
.

The dual
\mathcal{X}^{\flat}
of an ordered space
\mathcal{X}
is turned into an ordered space by defining
\mathcal{X}^{\flat+}
to be the set of all
x^{\flat}\in\mathcal{X}^{\flat}
for which
\langle x^{\flat},x\rangle\geq 0
for any
x\geq 0
. Note that
\mathcal{X}^{\flat+}
is
\sigma(\mathcal{X}^{\flat},\mathcal{X})
-closed (hence norm closed if
\mathcal{X}
is a normed space).
Clearly

is a wedge; by
[
4
, Theorem 2.13]

is a cone iff
\mathcal{X}^{+}-\mathcal{X}^{+}
is
\sigma(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\flat})
-dense in
\mathcal{X}
.

By
[
4
, Theorem 2.13]
,
x\in\mathcal{X}^{+}
iff
\langle x^{\flat},x\rangle\geq 0
for any
x^{\flat}\in\mathcal{X}^{\flat+}
. Consequently, an element
x\in\mathcal{X}
belongs to
\mathcal{X}^{+}
if and only if it belongs to
\mathcal{X}^{\flat\flat+}
(here and throughout the paper,
\mathcal{X}
is viewed as canonically embedded into
\mathcal{X}^{\flat\flat}
).

Banach-Alaoglu Theorem states that a Banach space
\mathcal{X}
is weak
∗
-dense in
\mathcal{X}^{\flat\flat}
. Likewise,
[
4
, Theorem 2.13]
gives us:

Proposition 2.2
.

If
\mathcal{X}
is an ordered Banach space, then
\mathcal{X}^{+}
is weak
∗
dense in
\mathcal{X}^{\flat\flat+}
.

Later (
2.13
) we shall see that an analogue of Goldstine Theorem holds as well.

Definition 2.3
.

An ordered space
\mathcal{X}
is said to be:

•

C
-
normal
if, for any

and

•

We say that a space is

(

-normal (resp.

In

Note that the normality of

A straightforward compactness argument shows that a finite dimensional ordered space

, find

We claim that the operator

1-\varepsilon<\|S\|-\|U-S\|\leq\|U\|\leq\|S\|+\|U-S\|<1+\varepsilon.

Further, for every

Similarly, we verify that

for

and therefore,

.
∎

3.
Matricial structures and matricial polars

The useful facts collected here will be used throughout the paper.

3.1.
Operator spaces: an introduction

An (abstract)
operator
space, or a
matricially normed space
, is, for us, a normed space
\mathcal{X}
, equipped with its sequence of
matricial norms

\|\cdot\|_{n}
on
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
(the space of
\mathcal{X}
-valued
n\times n
matrices, with
n\in\mathbb{N}
; we identify
\mathcal{X}
with
S_{\infty}^{1}(\mathcal{X})
); these norms have to satisfy
Ruan’s axioms
:

(1)

For any
a,b\in S_{\infty}^{m,n}
and
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
,
\|a\cdot x\cdot b^{*}\|_{m}\leq\|a\|\|b\|\|x\|_{n}
. Here,
a\cdot x\cdot b^{*}
is the shorthand for
(a\otimes I_{\mathcal{X}})\cdot x\cdot(b^{*}\otimes I_{\mathcal{X}})
.

(2)

For any
x\in S_{\infty}^{m}(\mathcal{X})
and
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
,
\|\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&y\end{pmatrix}\|_{m+n}=max\big\{\|x\|_{m},\|y\|_{n}\big\}
.

The prototypical example of an operator space is
B(H)
(
H
is a Hilbert space); the matricial norms come from the identification
S_{\infty}^{n}(B(H))=B(\ell_{2}^{n}(H))
.
Importantly, every operator space
\mathcal{X}
has a concrete representation: there exists a Hilbert space
H
and a map
J:\mathcal{X}\to B(H)
so that, for every
n
,
I_{S_{\infty}^{n}}\otimes J:S_{\infty}^{n}(X)\to S_{\infty}^{n}(B(H))
is an isometry.

For an operator
T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
(
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
are operator spaces), we define its
c.b. norm

\left\lVert T\right\rVert_{\text{cb}}
as
\|I_{S_{\infty}}\otimes T:S_{\infty}(\mathcal{X})\to S_{\infty}(\mathcal{Y})\|
.
Equivalently,
\left\lVert T\right\rVert_{\text{cb}}=\sup_{n}\|T_{n}\|
, where
T_{n}
stands for
I_{S_{\infty}^{n}}\otimes T
.
T
is
completely bounded
if its c.b. norm is finite.
For more information on operator spaces, and maps between them, we refer the reader to
[
14
]
,
[
26
]
, or
[
28
]
.

If
\mathcal{X}
is a vector space, and
\mathcal{X}^{\flat}
is its dual, then, for
x=(x_{ij})\in S_{\infty}^{m}(\mathcal{X})
and
x=(x^{\flat}_{k\ell})\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
, we define
\langle\langle x^{\flat},x\rangle\rangle=\big(\langle x_{k\ell}^{\flat},x_{ij}\rangle\big)\in S_{\infty}^{mn}
(the rows and the columns of the resulting
mn\times mn
matrix are indexed by pairs
(i,k)
and
(j,\ell)
, respectively).
This permits us to view
x^{\flat}
as an operator
\mathcal{X}\to S_{\infty}^{n}
. If
\mathcal{X}
is equipped with matricial norms, we define
\|x^{\flat}\|
as the c.b. norm of the above operator, or in other words,
\|x^{\flat}\|=\sup\big\{\|\langle\langle x^{\flat},x\rangle\rangle\|_{nm}:\|x\|_{m}\leq 1\big\}
; this expression turns out to equal
\sup\big\{\|\langle\langle x^{\flat},x\rangle\rangle\|_{nm}:\|x\|_{m}\leq 1\big\}
(Smith’s Lemma, see e.g.
[
26
, Proposition 8.11]
).

3.2.
Matricial polars

Duality between matricially normed spaces was investigated in
[
13
]
. We recall some of the important results here, and refer the reader to the original paper for more detail.

If
\mathcal{X}
is a vector space, then
K=(K_{n})
is a
matricial convex set on
\mathcal{X}
if, for every
n
,
K_{n}\subset S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
, and (i) for any
m,n
,
K_{m}\oplus K_{n}\subset K_{m+n}
, (ii)
\alpha^{*}\cdot K_{n}\cdot\alpha\subset K_{m}
for any
\alpha\in\ball(S_{\infty}^{n,m})
. Clearly, in this setting, each
K_{n}
is convex, and contains
0
.

Note that our definition differs slightly from the one given in
[
13
]
, where the inclusion
\alpha^{*}\cdot K_{n}\cdot\alpha\subset K_{m}
was only required for
\|\alpha\|=1
. However, it is easy to see that a matricial convex sets “in the original sense”
(K_{n})
satisfies
0\in K_{1}
iff
(K_{n})
has the properties listed in the preceding paragraph.

Suppose
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\flat})
is a dual pair of vector spaces, and
K=(K_{n})
is a matricial convex set on
\mathcal{X}
. Define the
matricial polar
of
K
, denoted by
K^{\pi}=(K_{n}^{\pi})
:

x^{\flat}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})
belongs to
K_{n}^{\pi}
iff
\Re\langle\langle x^{\flat},x\rangle\rangle\leq I_{mn}
(the identity on
\ell_{2}^{mn}
) for any
x\in K_{n}
. Actually, checking
m=n
suffices:

Proposition 3.1
.

In the above notation,
x^{\flat}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})
belongs to
K_{n}^{\pi}
iff
\Re\langle\langle x^{\flat},x\rangle\rangle\leq I_{S_{\infty}^{n^{2}}}
for any
x\in K_{n}
.

Further, we shall say
K=(K_{n})
is
weakly-closed
or
\sigma(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\flat})
-closed if for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
(x_{i})_{i\in I}\subset K_{n}
such that
\langle x^{\flat},x_{i}\rangle\to\langle x^{\flat},x\rangle
for every
x^{\flat}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})
, then
x\in K_{n}
.

Theorem 3.2

(Separation theorem)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\flat}
are as above,
K=(K_{n})
is weakly closed, and matricial convex on
\mathcal{X}
, and
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
doesn’t belong to
K_{n}
.
Then there exists
x^{\flat}\in K_{n}^{\pi}
so that
\Re\langle\langle x^{\flat},x\rangle\rangle\not\leq I_{n^{2}}
.

4.
Matricial order operator spaces: definitions

In the 1970s, operator systems (that is, self-adjoint subspaces of
B(H)
, for some Hilbert space
H
) appeared in the literature (such as e.g.
[
11
]
) as a natural generalization of the GNS construction for
C^{*}
-algebras.
As with operator spaces, for an operator system
\mathcal{X}
we identify
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
with a subspace of
B(\ell_{2}^{n}(H))
, with the inherited order. This is an elementary example of a matricial order space, which we define in the following manner:

Definition 4.1
.

A
*
-vector space
is a complex topological vector space
\mathcal{X}
, equipped with a continuous involution
*
. If our space is normed, then the involution is assumed to be isometric.
The
Hermitian
part of
\mathcal{X}
, denoted by
\mathcal{X}_{h}
, is the real-linear subspace of
\mathcal{X}
, consisting of elements invariant under involution.

Following
[
18
]
, and earlier works
[
30
,
31
]
, we consider
matricial order structures
on
*
-vector spaces:

Definition 4.2
.

A
matricial order space
is a pair
(\mathcal{X},\{S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}})
where
\mathcal{X}
is a
*
-vector space, and the
positive wedge

\{S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies:

(1)

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
is closed, and is a wedge in
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
.

(2)

For any
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
, and
a\in S_{\infty}^{m,n}
,
a\cdot x\cdot a^{*}\in S_{\infty}^{m}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
.

(3)

For any
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in S_{\infty}^{m}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
and
y\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
,
x\oplus y:=\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&y\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{m+n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
.

(4)

For any
n
,
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}\subset S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})_{h}
(the set of
hermitian
, or
self-adjoint
, elements of
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
).

If, in addition,
\alpha
is an operator space semi-norm (that is, a sequence of semi-norms
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, satisfying Ruan’s axioms), we call a triple
(\mathcal{X},\{S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\alpha)
a
matricial order operator space.

Here and throughout this paper, we define the
adjoint
of
x=\sum_{i,j}E_{i,j}\otimes x_{ij}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
as
x^{*}=\sum_{i,j}E_{i,j}\otimes x_{ji}^{*}
, with
E_{i,j}
being matrix units. In other words,
(a\otimes x)^{*}=a^{*}\otimes x^{*}
. We assume that
\|x\|=\|x^{*}\|
for any
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
. We shall need a simple linear algebra result.

Lemma 4.3
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{X}
as above,
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})_{h}
coincides, as a real vector space, with
[S_{\infty}^{n}]_{h}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{h}
.

Proof.

Suppose
x=x^{*}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})_{h}
. Write
x=\sum_{k}a_{k}\otimes x_{k}
, with
a_{k}\in S_{\infty}^{n},x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
. Split both
a_{k}
and
x_{k}
into their real and imaginary parts. That is, write
a_{k}=b_{k}+\iota c_{k}
, with

b_{k}=\frac{a_{k}+a_{k}^{*}}{2},\,\,c_{k}=\frac{a_{k}-a_{k}^{*}}{2i}

(here
b_{k}=b_{k}^{*},c_{k}=c_{k}^{*}
). Likewise, write
x_{k}=y_{k}+\iota z_{k}
. Then

\displaystyle a_{k}\otimes x_{k}

\displaystyle=\big(b_{k}\otimes y_{k}-c_{k}\otimes z_{k}\big)+\iota\big(b_{k}\otimes z_{k}+c_{k}\otimes y_{k}\big),

\displaystyle(a_{k}\otimes x_{k})^{*}

\displaystyle=\big(b_{k}\otimes y_{k}-c_{k}\otimes z_{k}\big)-\iota\big(b_{k}\otimes z_{k}+c_{k}\otimes y_{k}\big).

Therefore,

x=\frac{x+x^{*}}{2}=\sum_{k}\frac{a_{k}\otimes x_{k}+(a_{k}\otimes x_{k})^{*}}{2}=\sum_{k}\big(b_{k}\otimes y_{k}-c_{k}\otimes z_{k}\big).\qed

In the context of
4.2
, we say that
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
is the
positive cone
(and not merely a wedge) if
-S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}\cap S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}=\{0\}
. It turns out that being a cone is determined “on the lowest level.”

Lemma 4.4
.

If
\mathcal{X}^{+}
is a cone, then
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
is a cone for every
n
.

Proof.

Suppose
x=(x_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
is such that
x,-x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
. Then for any
a\in\ell_{2}^{n}
,
\pm a^{*}\cdot x\cdot a\in\mathcal{X}^{+}
(here we view
a
as an
n\times 1
matrix).

Fixing
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and taking
a=|i\rangle
, we conclude that
\pm x_{ii}\in\mathcal{X}^{+}
, hence
x_{ii}=0
, for any
i
.
Next, for
i\neq j
, take
a=|i\rangle+|j\rangle
and
|i\rangle+\iota|j\rangle
. This gives us
\pm(x_{ij}+x_{ij}^{*}),\pm\iota(x_{ij}-x_{ij}^{*})\in\mathcal{X}^{+}
. This can only happen when
x_{ij}+x_{ij}^{*}=0=x_{ij}-x_{ij}^{*}
, so
x_{ij}=0
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

As noted above, any operator system (in particular, any
C^{*}
-algebra) is a matricial order operator space; we further investigate these examples in
Section

8
.
While operator systems can be realized as unital self-adjoint subspaces of
B(H)
, this is false for general matricial order operator spaces, see
[
34
]
and
[
32
]
.

We also need to define positivity properties of maps between matricial order spaces:

Definition 4.6
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
are matricial order spaces. Then an involution-preserving map
T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is called
completely positive
if
I_{S_{\infty}^{n}}\otimes T:S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})\to S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{Y})
is positive for every
n
– that is, the positivity of
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
implies that of
I_{S_{\infty}^{n}}\otimes T(x)
.
If
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
if and only if
I_{S_{\infty}^{n}}\otimes T(x)\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{Y})^{+}
for every
n
, we say that
T
is
completely bipositive
.

It is easy to see that, if
\mathcal{X}_{h}
is generating, and
T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
satisfies
T(\mathcal{X}^{+})\subset\mathcal{Y}^{+}
for any
n
, then
T
is automatically involution-preserving.

A classical result states that, for an operator space
\mathcal{X}
, any
x^{\flat}\in\mathcal{X}^{\flat}
is completely bounded, with
\|x^{\flat}\|=\|x^{\flat}\|_{cb}
. Reasoning as in
[
26
, Proposition 3.8]
, we easily establish:

Proposition 4.7
.

If
\mathcal{X}
is a matricial normed space,
x^{\flat}\in\mathcal{X}^{\flat}
is completely positive if and only if it is positive.

Often, positive cones of our matricial order operator spaces have certain convenient properties:

Definition 4.8
.

A matricial order operator space
(\mathcal{X},\{S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\alpha)
is said to be:

(1)

C
-
matricial normal
: if
\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}&u\\
u^{*}&u_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
, then
\alpha_{n}(u)\leq C\alpha_{n}(u_{1})\vee\alpha_{n}(u_{2}).

(2)

C
-
matricial generating
: for any
\varepsilon>0
, and any
u\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
, there exist
u_{1},u_{2}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
such that
\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}&u\\
u^{*}&u_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
, and
\alpha_{n}(u_{1})\vee\alpha_{n}(u_{2})\leq C\alpha_{n}(u)+\epsilon
. If the above condition also holds for
\varepsilon=0
, we shall say that our cone is
C
-
strongly matricial generating
.

(3)

matricial normal
(
matricial generating
) if it is
C
-matricial normal (resp.
C
-matricial generating) for some
C
.

As in
Section

2
, we see that, if
\mathcal{X}
is matricial normal, then

is a cone, for every
n
.

Remark 4.9
.

The preceding definition is motivated by normality and generation of ordered Banach spaces, introduced in
Section

2
. To see the connection, suppose
(\mathcal{X},\|\cdot\|)
is an ordered normed space. Observe first that, if
-y\leq x=x^{*}\leq y
, then
\begin{pmatrix}y&x\\
x&y\end{pmatrix}\geq 0
. Indeed,
y-x\geq 0
, hence

\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix}\otimes(y-x)=\begin{pmatrix}y-x&x-y\\
x-y&y-x\end{pmatrix}\geq 0.

Likewise,
y+x\geq 0
, hence

\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\otimes(y+x)=\begin{pmatrix}y+x&x+y\\
x+y&y+x\end{pmatrix}\geq 0.

Averaging the two centered expressions, we obtain
\begin{pmatrix}y&x\\
x&y\end{pmatrix}\geq 0
.

Now suppose
x
is self-adjoint, and
\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&x\\
x&y_{2}\end{pmatrix}\geq 0
. Letting
y=(y_{1}+y_{2})/2
, we obtain

\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix}^{*}\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&x\\
x&y_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix}=2(y+x)\geq 0.

Also,

\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}^{*}\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&x\\
x&y_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}=2(y-x)\geq 0.

Thus,
-y\leq x\leq y
, and
\|y\|\leq\|y_{1}\|\vee\|y_{2}\|

Remark 4.10
.

The above reasoning shows that, for a matricial generating
\mathcal{X}
,
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
is hermitian if and only if it is a difference of two positive elements. Indeed, any positive element is hermitian, hence real linear combinations of such are hermitian as well. On the other hand, if
x=x^{*}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
, then matricial generation implies the existence of
y\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
so that
y\geq x\geq-y
. Then
x=(y+x)/2-(y-x)/2
; both terms are positive.

Lemma 4.11
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is a matricial order operator space.

(1)

\mathcal{X}
is
C
-matricial normal if and only if, for any
u\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})_{h}
and
v\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
with
v\geq\pm u
, we have
\alpha_{n}(u)\leq C\alpha_{n}(v)
.

(2)

Proof.

(1) If
\mathcal{X}

and

It remains to establish the converse. Suppose

\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&u\\
u^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}

\alpha_{n}(x_{i})\leq 1
for

First note that

\alpha_{n}(u)=\alpha_{2n}(\overline{u})

\overline{x}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&0\\
0&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}
is positive, and
\vee_{i}\alpha_{n}(x_{i})=\alpha_{2n}(\overline{x})
. A simple computation shows that

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\overline{x}&\overline{u}\\
\overline{u}&\overline{x}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&0&0&u\\
0&x_{2}&u^{*}&0\\
0&u&x_{1}&0\\
u^{*}&0&0&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\\
0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&u\\
u^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\\
1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&u\\
u^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1&0\\
0&1&0&0\end{pmatrix}\geq 0,

By

\overline{x}\geq\pm\overline{u}
, which leads to

. In light of our earlier discussion on
\alpha_{2n}(\overline{x})
and
\alpha_{2n}(\overline{u})

(2) If
\mathcal{X}
is
C
-matricial generating, then

implies that for any
u\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})_{h}
and any
\varepsilon>0

v\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}

v\geq\pm u
and
\alpha_{n}(v)<C\alpha_{n}(u)+\varepsilon
.

Conversely, suppose for any self-adjoint

and positive

and
\varepsilon>0

\vee_{i}\alpha_{n}(x_{i})<C\alpha_{n}(u)+\varepsilon
, and
\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&u\\
u^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}

\overline{u}=\begin{pmatrix}0&u\\
u^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}

\alpha_{2n}(\overline{u})=\alpha_{n}(u)

The following result re-states matricial normality and generation.

Lemma 4.12
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is a matricial order operator space.

(1)

\mathcal{X}
is
C
-matricial normal if and only if, for any
a,b\in S_{\infty}^{n}({\mathcal{X}})^{+}
, we have
C\alpha_{n}(a+b)\geq\alpha_{n}(a-b)
.

(2)

\mathcal{X}
is
C
-matricial generating if and only if, for any
\varepsilon>0
and
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}({\mathcal{X}})_{h}
, there exist
a,b\in S_{\infty}^{n}({\mathcal{X}})^{+}
so that
x=a-b
and
C\alpha_{n}(a+b)\leq C\alpha_{n}(x)+\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We only prove (1), as (2) is similar. By
4.11
, it suffices to check for normality on self-adjoint elements.

(i) Suppose the above formula holds. To show that
y\geq x\geq-y
implies
C\alpha_{n}(y)\geq\alpha_{n}(x)
, apply it with
a=(y+x)/2,b=(y-x)/2
; then
y=a+b,x=a-b
.

(ii) Conversely, suppose
\mathcal{X}
is
C
-matricial normal. For positive
a,b
, take
y=a+b,x=a-b
. Then
y\geq x\geq-y
, hence
C\alpha_{n}(a+b)\geq\alpha_{n}(a-b)
.
∎

We show that, for operators between normal and generating spaces, the c.b. norm can be calculated on the positive cone.
More specifically, for an operator
u:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
(where
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
are normed matricial order spaces), define
\|u\|_{cbc}
(the

as
\sup\big\{\|u_{n}x\|:x\in\ball(S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}))^{+}\big\}
. Clearly,
\|u\|_{cbc}\leq\|u\|_{cb}
. We show that the converse is also true if the spaces in question possess certain properties.

Lemma 4.13
.

Suppose the matricial order operator spaces
(\mathcal{X},\alpha_{n})
and
(\mathcal{Y},\beta_{n})
are
C_{1}
-matricial generating and
C_{2}
-matricial normal, respectively. Then a completely positive

is completely bounded iff

; in this case,
\|u\|_{cbc}\leq\|u\|_{cb}\leq C_{1}C_{2}\|u\|_{cbc}
.

Proof.

The inequality
\|u\|_{cbc}\leq\|u\|_{cb}
is straightforward. For the converse, we need to show that, if
\|u\|_{cbc}\leq 1
, and
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
satisfies
\alpha_{n}(x)<1
, then
\beta_{n}(u_{n}x)<C_{1}C_{2}
.

As
\mathcal{X}
is
C_{1}
-matricially generating, we can find
x_{1},x_{2}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
so that
\alpha_{n}(x_{1}),\alpha_{n}(x_{2})<C_{1}
, and
\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x\\
x^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
.
Then
\begin{pmatrix}u_{n}x_{1}&u_{n}x\\
(u_{n}x)^{*}&u_{n}x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(\mathcal{Y})^{+}
(recall that
u
is completely positive, hence in particular

.
Further,
\beta_{n}(u_{n}x_{1}),\beta_{n}(u_{n}x_{2})<C_{1}

-matricial normality of

\beta_{n}(u_{n}x)<C_{1}C_{2}
.
∎

We briefly address subspaces and quotients of matricial order spaces.

It is easy to see that a subspace of a matricial order space inherits the matricial order. Generation may be lost (a subspace may have trivial intersection with the positve cone), but normality is preserved.

To define quotients, we follow
[
18
]
. A subspace
J\subset\mathcal{X}
(we assume
\mathcal{X}^{+}
is a cone) is called a
kernel
if it is a kernel of a bounded completely positive map into a matricial order operator space
\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathcal{Y}^{+}
is a cone. The matricial norms on
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}/J)
are defined in the usual manner.
As
J=J^{*}
, we can define involution on
\mathcal{X}/J
via
(x+J)^{*}=x^{*}+J
. Let
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}/J)^{+}
be the closure of
q_{n}(S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+})
, where
q:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}/J
is the quotient map. By
[
18
, Section 3]
, if
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
is a cone, then so is
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}/J)^{+}
.

Proposition 4.14
.

In the above notation, if
\mathcal{X}
is
C
-matricial generating, then so is
\mathcal{X}/J
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that, if
u\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}/J)_{h}
satisfies
\|u\|<1
, then there exists
v\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}/J)_{h}
so that
\|v\|\leq C
, and
\pm u\leq v
.

We can represent
u=x+S_{\infty}^{n}(J)
, with
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}),\|x\|<1
. As the involution preserves
J
, we also have
u=x^{*}+S_{\infty}^{n}(J)
, hence
u=x_{0}+S_{\infty}^{n}(J)
, where
x_{0}=(x+x^{*})/2
is self-adjoint, and
\|x_{0}\|<1
. Then there exists
y_{0}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
so that
\pm x_{0}\leq y_{0}
and

∎

5.
Positivisation in matricial order spaces

Now suppose a matricial order space
\mathcal{X}
is equipped with a family of semi-norms
\alpha=(\alpha_{n})
. Further, assume that the matricial order on
\mathcal{X}
is
majorizing
: given any
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
, there exists
x_{i}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
such that
\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x\\
x^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
.
Inspired by a construction from
[
12
]
, define a new sequence of semi-norms, consistent with the order: for
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
, let

(5.1)

\alpha^{+}_{n}(x):=\inf\Big\{\alpha_{n}(x_{1})\vee\alpha_{n}(x_{2}):\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x\\
x^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}\Big\}.

Note that, for every
n
,
\alpha_{n}^{+}
is a semi-norm, and, for
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
, we necessarily have
\alpha_{n}^{+}(x)\leq\alpha_{n}(x)
.
If
(\alpha_{n})
turns
\mathcal{X}
into a matricial generating, matricial normal space, then
\alpha_{n}^{+}=\alpha_{n}
. Also, reasoning as before, one can show that, for
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})_{h}
,
\alpha_{n}^{+}(x)=\inf\big\{\alpha_{n}(y):y\geq\pm x\big\}
.

The most essential properties of
(\alpha_{n}^{+})
are given below.

Proposition 5.1
.

(1)

(\alpha_{n}^{+})
satisfy Ruan’s axioms.

(2)
The family of semi-norms
(\alpha_{n}^{+})
on
\mathcal{X}
is
1
-matricial normal and
1
-matricial generating.

(3)
For every
n
,
\alpha_{n}^{+}=(\alpha^{+})_{n}^{+}
.

Proof.

(1) We need to show that

(i)

For any
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
and
a,b\in S_{\infty}^{n,m}
, we have
\alpha_{m}^{+}(a^{*}\cdot x\cdot b)\leq\|a\|\alpha_{n}^{+}(x)\|b\|
.

(ii)

For any
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
and
y\in S_{\infty}^{m}(\mathcal{X})
,
\alpha_{n+m}^{+}(x\oplus y)=\alpha_{n}^{+}(x)\vee\alpha_{m}^{+}(y)
; here
x\oplus y=\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&y\end{pmatrix}
.

For (i), we can assume (by replacing
a
and
b
by
ta
and
t^{-1}b
if necessary) that
\|a\|=\|b\|
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
, and find
x_{1},x_{2}
so that
\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x\\
x^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
, and
\alpha_{n}(x_{1}),\alpha_{n}(x_{2})<\alpha_{n}^{+}(x)+\varepsilon
. Then

\begin{pmatrix}a^{*}x_{1}a&a^{*}xb\\
(a^{*}xb)^{*}&b^{*}x_{2}b\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{pmatrix}^{*}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x\\
x^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2}(\mathcal{X})^{+},

By Ruan’s axioms,
\alpha_{m}(a^{*}\cdot x_{1}\cdot b)\leq\|a\|^{2}\alpha_{n}(x_{1})
, and
\alpha_{m}(b^{*}\cdot x_{2}\cdot b)\leq\|b\|^{2}\alpha_{n}(x_{2})
; recalling

, we obtain
\alpha_{m}^{+}(a^{*}\cdot x\cdot b)\leq\|a\|\|b\|\alpha_{n}^{+}(x)
.

To establish (ii), fix
\varepsilon>0
, and find
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}
so that

\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x\\
x^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(\mathcal{X})^{+},\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&y\\
y^{*}&y_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2m}(\mathcal{X})^{+},\alpha_{n}(x_{i})<\alpha_{n}^{+}(x)+\varepsilon,\alpha_{m}(y_{i})<\alpha_{m}^{+}(y)+\varepsilon(i=1,2).

Then

\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&0&x&0\\
0&y_{1}&0&y\\
x^{*}&0&x_{2}&0\\
0&y^{*}&0&y_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x\\
x^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&y\\
y^{*}&y_{2}\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2(n+m)}(\mathcal{X})^{+},

hence

\alpha_{n+m}^{+}\Big(\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&y\end{pmatrix}\Big)\leq\vee_{i=1,2}\alpha_{n+m}\Big(\begin{pmatrix}x_{i}&0\\
0&y_{i}\end{pmatrix}\Big)<\alpha_{n}^{+}(x)\vee\alpha_{m}^{+}(y)+\varepsilon.

(2) We establish matricial normality, since generation is similar. In light of
4.11
, it suffices to show that, for every
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})_{h}
and
y\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
, with
y\geq\pm x
we have
\alpha_{n}^{+}(x)\leq\alpha_{n}^{+}(y)
.
By the preceding reasoning,
\alpha_{n}^{+}(y)=\inf\{\alpha_{n}(z):z\geq y\}
. But any
z
as above satisfies
\pm x\leq z
, hence
\alpha_{n}^{+}(x)\leq\inf\{\alpha_{n}(z):z\geq y\}=\alpha_{n}^{+}(y)
.

(3) For a
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
,

\alpha^{+}_{n}(x)=\inf\Big\{\alpha_{n}(x_{1})\vee\alpha_{n}(x_{2}):\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x\\
x^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}\Big\}.

Clearly
x_{1},x_{2}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
, hence
\alpha_{n}^{+}(x_{i})\leq\alpha_{n}(x_{i})
, for
i=1,2
. Therefore,

(\alpha^{+})^{+}_{n}(x)=\inf\Big\{\alpha_{n}^{+}(x_{1})\vee\alpha^{+}_{n}(x_{2}):\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x\\
x^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}\Big\}\leq\alpha^{+}_{n}(x).

Conversely, suppose
(\alpha^{+})^{+}_{n}(x)<1
. Find
x_{1},x_{2}
so that
\vee_{i}\alpha_{n}^{+}(x_{i})<1
, and
\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x\\
x^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
. For
i=1,2
find

so that
\alpha_{n}(y_{i})<1
. Then

\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&x\\
x^{*}&y_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x\\
x^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}-x_{1}&0\\
0&y_{2}-x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(\mathcal{X})^{+},

6.
Duality of matricial order spaces

Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is a matricial order space, and
\mathcal{X}^{\flat}
is its topological vector space dual (for instance,
\mathcal{X}^{\flat}
can be the space of norm continuous functionals on
\mathcal{X}
, or the space of weak
∗
-continuous functionals, that is, the pre-dual of
\mathcal{X}
).
Define the
*
-operation on
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})
by taking, for each
x^{\flat}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})
,
x^{\flat*}
to be the unique element of
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})
so that the equality
\langle\langle x^{\flat*},x\rangle\rangle:=\langle\langle x^{\flat},x^{*}\rangle\rangle^{*}
holds for any
x\in S_{\infty}^{m}(\mathcal{X})
.
Equivalently,
\langle u^{\flat*},u\rangle=\overline{\langle u^{\flat},u^{*}\rangle}
for any
u^{\flat}\in\mathcal{X}^{\flat},u\in\mathcal{X}
, and
(a\otimes u^{\flat})^{*}=a^{*}\otimes u^{\flat*}
.

Similarly, define the positive wedge: a self-adjoint
x^{\flat}
belongs to
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})^{+}
iff
\langle\langle x,x^{\flat}\rangle\rangle\in S_{\infty}^{mn+}
for any
x\in S_{\infty}^{m}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
. By
[
13
]
, it suffices to verify this condition for
m=n
. Clearly all conditions of
4.2
are satisfied.

Remark 6.1
.

4.4
shows that
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})^{+}
is a cone for every

iff
\mathcal{X}^{\flat+}
is a cone.
For different approach to proving this, recall
[
4
, Theorem 2.13]
that
\mathcal{X}^{\flat+}
is a cone iff
\mathcal{X}^{+}-\mathcal{X}^{+}
is weakly dense in
\mathcal{X}_{h}
. Assuming this density, one can show that
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}-S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
is also weakly dense in
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})_{h}
.
Using
Section

2
, we conclude that, if
\mathcal{X}_{h}
is matricial generating, then
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})^{+}
is a cone, for every
n
.

Remark 6.2
.

[
31

(see also
[

]
) gives several alternative descriptions of positivity in

is a matricial order space, then

.

Theorem 6.5
.

Suppose

Next we establish a self-adjoint matricial version of the polarity between intersections and convex hulls. Consider

Proof.

Lemma 6.8
.

Suppose

As a by-product of the above reasoning, we obtain:

Lemma 6.9
.

Suppose

Proof of

C

In the direction opposite to

Proposition 6.10
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is a matricial order operator space.

(1)

If
\mathcal{X}
is
C
-matricial normal, then
\mathcal{X}^{\flat}
is
C
-matricial generating.

(2)

If
\mathcal{X}^{\flat}
is
C
-matricial normal, and
\mathcal{X}

\mathcal{X}
is
C
-matricial generating.

Proof.

We first establish (1), and then briefly describe the changes one needs to introduce into the proof to handle (2).

(1) In light of
4.12
, we are reduced to showing: if
C\alpha_{m}(a+b)\geq\alpha_{m}(a-b)
for any
a,b\in S_{\infty}^{m}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
, then for any
x^{\flat}\in\ball(S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat}))_{h}
there exists
y^{\flat}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})^{+}
so that
y^{\flat}\geq x^{\flat}\geq-y^{\flat}
, and
\alpha_{n}(y^{\flat})\leq C
.

Equip the operator space
{\mathcal{X}}^{2}:={\mathcal{X}}\oplus_{\infty}{\mathcal{X}}
with componentwise order. For each
n
consider
U_{n}=\{(a,b)\in S_{\infty}^{n}({\mathcal{X}}^{2})^{+}:\alpha_{n}(a+b)\leq 1/C\}
. One can check that
U=(U_{n})
is matricial convex.

Define
T:{\mathcal{X}}^{2}\to{\mathcal{X}}:(a,b)\mapsto a-b
. Our assumption states that, for any
n
,
T_{n}(U_{n})\subset\ball(S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}))_{h}
(we use shorthand
T_{n}=I_{S_{\infty}^{n}}\otimes T
). For the self-adjoint matricial polar (defined and described earlier in this section), we obtain:
(T_{n}(U_{n}))^{o}\supset\ball(S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat}))_{h}
.
It remains to show that for any
x^{\flat}\in(T_{n}(U_{n}))^{o}
there exists
y\in C\ball(S_{\infty}^{n}({\mathcal{X}}^{\flat}))_{h}
with
y^{\flat}\geq x^{\flat}\geq-y^{\flat}
.

Note that
x^{\flat}
belongs to
(T_{n}(U_{n}))^{o}
if, and only if, for any
x\in U_{k}
we have
\langle\langle x^{\flat},T_{k}x\rangle\rangle=\langle\langle T_{n}^{\flat}x^{\flat},x\rangle\rangle\leq I_{kn}
(here
T_{n}^{\flat}=T^{\flat}\otimes I_{S_{\infty}^{n}})
), that is,
T_{n}^{\flat}x^{\flat}\in U_{n}^{o}
.
It is easy to verify that
T_{n}^{\flat}x^{\flat}=(x^{\flat},-x^{\flat})
. Thus,
x^{\flat}\in(T_{n}(U_{n}))^{o}
iff
(x^{\flat},-x^{\flat})\in U_{n}^{o}
.

Further,
U=V\cap({\mathcal{X}}^{2})^{+}
, where
({\mathcal{X}}^{2})^{+}
is a shorthand for
(S_{\infty}^{k}({\mathcal{X}}^{2})^{+})_{k}
, and
V=(V_{k})
, where
V_{k}
is the set of all
(a,b)\in S_{\infty}^{k}({\mathcal{X}}^{2}_{h})
with
\alpha_{k}(a+b)\leq 1/C
.

The set
V=(V_{k})
is matricial convex;
(y^{\flat},z^{\flat})\in V_{n}^{o}
iff
\langle\langle(y^{\flat},z^{\flat}),(a,b)\rangle\rangle=\langle\langle y^{\flat},a\rangle\rangle+\langle\langle z^{\flat},b\rangle\rangle\leq I_{kn}
whenever
(a,b)\in V_{k}
. Note that
(a,-a)\in V_{k}
for an arbitrary

, hence
\langle\langle y^{\flat}-z^{\flat},a\rangle\rangle\leq I_{kn}
for any such
a
; this is possible only if
y^{\flat}=z^{\flat}
.
In this situation,
\langle\langle y^{\flat},a+b\rangle\rangle\leq I_{kn}
whenever
\alpha_{k}(a+b)\leq 1/C
, which happens iff
\alpha_{n}^{\flat}(y^{\flat})\leq C
. To summarize,
V_{n}^{o}
is the set of all pairs
(y^{\flat},y^{\flat})
, with
\alpha_{n}^{\flat}(y^{\flat})\leq C
.

By
6.6
,

U^{o}=\big(V\cap({\mathcal{X}}^{2})^{+}\big)^{o}=\overline{\text{nco}}(V^{o}\cup-(({\mathcal{X}}^{2})^{\flat})^{+})=\overline{V^{o}-(({\mathcal{X}}^{2})^{\flat})^{+}}

(as before,
\overline{\text{nco}}(\cdot)
denotes the level-wise
\sigma(\mathcal{X}^{\flat},\mathcal{X})
closure).
Consequently,
(x^{\flat},-x^{\flat})\in U_{n}^{o}
iff there exist nets
y_{i}^{\flat}\in C\ball(S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})_{h})
and
u_{i}^{\flat},v_{i}^{\flat}\in\mathcal{X}^{\flat+}
so that
(y_{i}^{\flat}-u_{i}^{\flat})_{i}
and
(y_{i}^{\flat}-v_{i}^{\flat})_{i}
converge to
x^{\flat}
and
-x^{\flat}
, respectively, in
\sigma(\mathcal{X}^{\flat},\mathcal{X})
.
A compactness argument shows that, by passing to a subnet, we can assume that
(y_{i}^{\flat})
converges to
y^{\flat}\in C\ball(S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})_{h})
, in
\sigma(\mathcal{X}^{\flat},\mathcal{X})
.
For any

,

\liminf_{i}\langle\langle y_{i}^{\flat}-x^{\flat},x\rangle\rangle=\liminf_{i}\langle\langle u_{i}^{\flat},x\rangle\rangle\geq 0,

hence
y^{\flat}\geq x^{\flat}
. Likewise
y^{\flat}\geq-x^{\flat}
.

(2) Next we outline the changes needed to prove that, if
\mathcal{X}^{\flat}
is matricially normal, then
\mathcal{X}
is matricially generating. We need to establish: if
C\alpha_{m}^{\flat}(a^{\flat}+b^{\flat})\geq\alpha_{m}^{\flat}(a^{\flat}-b^{\flat})
for any

, then for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1/3)
, and
x\in\ball(S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})_{h})
there exists
y\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})^{+}
so that
y\geq x\geq-y
, and
\alpha_{n}(y)\leq C(1+\varepsilon)
.

Define the matricial sets
U
,
V
, etc., interchanging the roles of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\flat}
. For instance,
U_{n}=\{(a^{\flat},b^{\flat})\in S_{\infty}^{n}(({\mathcal{X}}^{\flat})^{2})^{+}:\alpha_{n}^{\flat}(a^{\flat}+b^{\flat})\leq 1/C\}
.
We arrive at
U^{o}=\overline{V^{o}-({\mathcal{X}}^{2})^{+}}
, where, in the right side, we take the closure in the norm (equivalently,
\sigma(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\flat})
) topology.

For a moment, consider
a\in\ball(S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})_{h})
. Then
(a,-a)\in U_{n}^{o}
, hence, for every

\alpha_{n}((z-u)-a),\alpha_{n}((z-v)+a)<\delta

Let
z^{\prime}=(u+v)/2

-z^{\prime}\leq a^{\prime}<z^{\prime}

Now take

x_{0}=x_{1}+x_{0}^{\prime}

, and there exists a positive

\alpha_{n}(z_{0})<(1+\varepsilon/3)C

x_{1}=x_{2}+x_{1}^{\prime}

\alpha_{n}(x_{2})<(\varepsilon/3)^{2}

\alpha_{n}(x_{k})<(\varepsilon/3)^{k}

\alpha_{n}(z_{j})<(\varepsilon/3)^{j}(1+\varepsilon/3)C

Also,

7.
Minimal and maximal matricial order spaces

It is well-known (see e.g.
[
9
]
,
[
14
, Section 3.3]
, or
[
28
, Section 3]
) that a Banach space can be equipped with different operator space structures, among them the maximal and minimal ones.
In this section, we endow a given ordered Banach space with operator space
and
matricial order structures – specifically, the maximal and minimal ones – and study these structures.

Throughout, a Banach space
\mathcal{X}
is assumed to be complex, with its positive wedge
\mathcal{X}^{+}
, and an antilinear involution
x\mapsto x^{*}
, which leaves
\mathcal{X}^{+}
invariant.

For future use, we need to observe that self-adjoint elements of
\mathcal{X}
can be characterized using positive functionals.

Proposition 7.1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}^{+}
is the positive cone of an ordered Banach space
\mathcal{X}
. Then
x\in\mathcal{X}
is self-adjoint if and only if
\langle x^{\flat},x\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
for any
x^{\flat}\in\mathcal{X}^{\flat+}
.

Proof.

If
x\in\mathcal{X}_{h}
, then clearly
\langle x^{\flat},x\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
for any
x^{\flat}\in\mathcal{X}^{\flat+}
. For the opposite implication, note that, by
[
4
, Theorem 2.13]
,
\mathcal{X}^{\flat+}-\mathcal{X}^{\flat+}
is weak
∗
dense in
\mathcal{X}^{\flat}_{h}
. Thus, if
\langle x^{\flat},x\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
for any
x^{\flat}\in\mathcal{X}^{\flat+}
, then the same holds for any
x^{\flat}\in\mathcal{X}_{h}
, which shows the self-adjointness of
x
.
∎

Begin with describing the minimal structure on an ordered Banach space
\mathcal{X}
.

Definition 7.2
.

For an ordered Banach space
\mathcal{X}
, equip
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
with its injective Banach space tensor product (that is,
\mathcal{X}
has the minimal operator space structure).
That is, for
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
, let
\|x\|=\sup_{a\in\ball(S_{1}^{n})}\|{\mathrm{tr}}\otimes I_{\mathcal{X}}(a\cdot x)\|_{\mathcal{X}}
(here
{\mathrm{tr}}\otimes I_{\mathcal{X}}(a\cdot(b\otimes y))=\mathrm{tr}{(}ab)y
).
An
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\MIN(\mathcal{X}))
belongs to the positive wedge if
{\mathrm{tr}}\otimes I_{\mathcal{X}}(a\cdot x)\in\mathcal{X}^{+}
whenever
a\in S_{1}^{n+}
.

By an extreme point argument,
\|x\|=\sup_{\xi,\eta\in\ball(\ell_{2}^{n})}\|\langle\xi|x|\eta\rangle\|_{\mathcal{X}}
.
Similarly, a self-adjoint
x
is positive iff
\langle\xi|x|\xi\rangle\in\mathcal{X}^{+}
for any
\xi\in\ell_{2}^{n}
. By
7.1
, the self-adjointness is automatic if
\mathcal{X}^{+}
is a cone (and not merely a wedge).

Clearly, the object described here is indeed a matricial convex wedge;

S_{\infty}^{1}(\MIN(\mathcal{X}))^{+}
is identified with
\mathcal{X}^{+}
. By
4.4
,
S_{\infty}^{n}(\MIN(\mathcal{X}))^{+}
is a cone whenever
\mathcal{X}^{+}
is.

Any
x=\sum_{i,j}E_{ij}\otimes x_{ij}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\MIN(\mathcal{X}))^{+}
is self-adjoint. Indeed, the positivity of
\langle\xi|x|\xi\rangle
with
|\xi\rangle=|i\rangle
tells us that
x_{ii}=x_{ii}^{*}\geq 0
. Further, taking
|\xi\rangle=|i\rangle+|j\rangle
, we obtain
x_{ij}=x_{ji}^{*}
.

In yet another reformulation,
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
iff
\langle a\otimes x^{\flat},x\rangle\geq 0
whenever
a\in S_{\infty}^{n+},x^{\flat}\in\mathcal{X}^{\flat+}
(see
[
4
, Theorem 2.13]
).
By the self-adjointness noted earlier, the positive cone in
S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
is nothing but the “injective cone”
C_{i}\subset(S_{\infty}^{n})_{h}\otimes\mathcal{X}_{h}
from
[
35
]
.
In other words,
C_{i}
consists of those
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
which define a positive operator
\mathbf{op}({x})
from
S_{1}^{n}
to
\mathcal{X}
. Then
\|x\|_{S_{\infty}^{n}(\MIN(\mathcal{X}))}=\|\mathbf{op}({x})\|
.

Some simple properties of the
\MIN
structure are noted below.

Proposition 7.3
.

(1)
Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is an ordered complex Banach space, and
\mathcal{Y}
is its involution-invariant subspace. Then
\MIN(\mathcal{Y})
embeds into
\MIN(\mathcal{X})
as a matricial order operator space – that is, it inherits the matricial norms and the matricial positive wedges from the latter.

(2)
If
\mathcal{Y}
is an ordered Banach space,
\mathcal{X}
is a matricial order operator space, and
T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is positive, then it is completely positive as a map from
\mathcal{X}
to
\MIN(\mathcal{Y})
.

Sketch of a proof.

Recall that
y\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\MIN(\mathcal{Y}))^{+}
iff
\langle\xi|y|\xi\rangle\in\mathcal{Y}^{+}
for any
\xi\in\ell_{2}^{n}
. From this, (1) immediately follows. For (2), note that, if
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
, then
\langle\xi|x|\xi\rangle\in\mathcal{X}^{+}
for any
\xi\in\ell_{2}^{n}
.
∎

Next we show that the matricial order space
\MIN(\mathcal{X})
has certain desirable properties for a class of ordered spaces
\mathcal{X}
.

Definition 7.4
.

We say that an ordered Banach space
\mathcal{X}
is
\MIN
-nice
if
\|x\|\leq\|x_{1}\|\vee\|x_{2}\|
whenever

(7.1)

x_{1},x_{2}\geq 0,\,\,{\textrm{ and }}\,\,t^{2}x_{1}+s^{2}x_{2}\geq 2ts\Re(\omega x)\,\,{\textrm{ for any }}\,\,t,s\in\mathbb{R},\,|\omega|=1.

As an example of an ordered space with desired properties, consider
\mathcal{X}=S_{\infty}^{n}
, with a unitarily invariant norm
\|\cdot\|
and natural order. The condition (
7.1
) is equivalent to
\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x\\
x^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\geq 0
.
In this case
\|x\|^{2}\leq\|x_{1}\|\|x_{2}\|
(see
[
17
]
for this, and more results in the same vein). This give us one example of
\MIN
-niceness. In
Section

9
below, we shall see other instances of this, coming from Banach lattices.

Remark 7.5
.

If
\mathcal{X}
is
\MIN
-nice, then
\mathcal{X}_{h}
is
1
-normal, as a real ordered Banach space. Indeed, suppose
x,y\in\mathcal{X}_{h}
satisfy
\pm x\leq y
. Then
t^{2}y+s^{2}y\geq 2ts\Re(\omega x)
for
t,s,\omega
as in (
7.1
), hence
\|x\|\leq\|y\|
, per definition.

Proposition 7.6
.

An ordered Banach space
\mathcal{X}
is
\MIN
-nice if and only if the matricial order space
\MIN(\mathcal{X})
described above is
1
-matricial normal.

Proof.

Suppose first
\mathcal{X}
is
\MIN
-nice. Consider
\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x\\
x^{*}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
, and
\|x\|>1
; show that
\|x_{1}\|\vee\|x_{2}\|>1
.

Find
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\ball(\ell_{2}^{n})
so that
\|u\|>1

x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
as
\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}E_{ij}\otimes x_{ij}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
, with
x_{ij}=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{m}[x_{ij}]_{k\ell}E_{k\ell}
). We say that
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
if the matrix
\sum[x_{ij}]_{k\ell}E_{ij}\otimes E_{k\ell}
, where the rows and the columns are indexed by pairs
(i,k)
and
(j,\ell)
, respectively, is positive.
Clearly our requirements are satisfied: (i) if
x\geq 0
and
-x\geq 0
, then
x=0
; (ii) if
x\geq 0
, then
a\cdot x\cdot a^{*}\geq 0
for any matrix
a
, and (iii) if
x\in S_{\infty}^{m}(S_{p})^{+},y\in S_{\infty}^{n}(S_{p})^{+}
, then
x\oplus y\in S_{\infty}^{m+n}(S_{p})^{+}
.

Proposition 8.3
.

In the above notation,
\mathcal{X}^{\flat}=S_{q}
,
1/p+1/q=1
, with the matricial order on
S_{q}
defined as for
S_{p}
.

Proof.

It is well known that such identification holds for operator space structure. To handle the matricial order, consider
x^{\flat}=(x^{\flat}_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}E_{ij}\otimes x^{\flat}_{ij}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})
. As we did with
x
, write
x^{\flat}_{ij}=\sum_{k\ell}[x_{ij}^{\flat}]_{k\ell}E_{k\ell}
, then
x=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{k\ell}[x_{ij}^{\flat}]_{k\ell}E_{ij}\otimes E_{k\ell}
– that is, we view
x
as a matrix with entries
\big([x_{ij}^{\flat}]_{k\ell}\big)
, with pairs
(i,k)
and
(j,\ell)
indexing the rows and columns, respectively.

We shall show that
x^{\flat}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})^{+}
iff the corresponding infinite matrix
\big([x_{ij}^{\flat}]_{k\ell}\big)
is positive.
Indeed,
x^{\flat}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})^{+}
iff the operator
T:\mathcal{X}\to S_{\infty}^{n}:x\mapsto\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\langle x_{ij}^{\flat},x\rangle E_{ij}
(where
(E_{ij})
stand for the matrix units), is completely positive.
This, in turn, is equivalent to the corresponding “formal” Choi matrix
C_{T}=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{\infty}T(E_{k\ell})\otimes E_{k\ell}
being positive (see e.g.
[
5
, Section 2.3]
). Note that
T(E_{k\ell})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}[x_{ij}^{\flat}]_{k\ell}E_{ij}
(where
[x_{ij}^{\flat}]_{k\ell}
denotes the
(k,\ell)
entry of
x_{ij}^{\flat}
), hence

C_{T}=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}[x_{ij}^{\flat}]_{k\ell}E_{ij}\otimes E_{k\ell}=U^{*}\cdot\Big(\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}[x_{ij}^{\flat}]_{k\ell}E_{k\ell}\otimes E_{ij}\Big)\cdot U=U^{*}\cdot x^{\flat}\cdot U,

where
U
is the “flip” map – that is,
U|j\ell\rangle=|\ell j\rangle
. Clearly
U^{*}\cdot x^{\flat}\cdot U
is positive iff
x^{\flat}
is.
∎

Proposition 8.4
.

For
1\leq p\leq\infty
, the Schatten space
S_{p}^{m}
is
1
-matricial normal and
1
-matricial generating.

Proof.

Show first that
S_{p}
is matricial normal. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, there exist
x,x_{1},x_{2}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(S_{p})
so that
\|x\|>1>\|x_{1}\|\vee\|x_{2}\|
, and
\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x\\
x^{*}&x_{1}\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(S_{p})^{+}
.
Find
a,b\in S_{2p}^{n}
so that
\|a\|_{2p},\|b\|_{2p}<1
, and
\|a\cdot x\cdot b\|_{p}>1
(here
a\cdot x\cdot b
is viewed as an element of
S_{p}^{n}[S_{p}]=S_{p}(\ell_{2}^{n}\otimes_{2}\ell_{2})
). Now note that

\begin{pmatrix}ax_{1}a^{*}&axb\\
b^{*}x^{*}a^{*}&b^{*}x_{2}b\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a^{*}&0\\
0&b\end{pmatrix}^{*}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x\\
x^{*}&x_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a^{*}&0\\
0&b\end{pmatrix}\geq 0.

By
[
17
]
,
\|axb\|_{p}^{2}\leq\|ax_{1}a^{*}\|_{p}\|b^{*}x_{2}b\|_{p}
. However,
\|axb\|_{p}>1
by our choice of
a
and
b
, while
\|ax_{1}a^{*}\|_{p}\leq\|a\|_{2p}^{2}\|x_{1}\|<1
, and similarly,
\|b^{*}x_{2}b\|_{p}<1
. This is the desired contradiction.

Duality (explained in
8.3
) shows that
S_{p}=S_{q}^{\flat}
for
1\leq p<\infty
and
1/p+1/q=1
. Consequently, for finite
p
,
S_{p}
is generating, as a dual of a normal space. The case of
p=\infty
has been handled in
8.2
.
∎

.

Remark 8.6
.

It is easy to see that, for
n\geq 2
,
S_{\infty}^{n}(S_{p})^{+}
(here
S_{p}
is equipped with its “natural” matricial order structure, described above) does not coincide with
S_{\infty}^{n}(\MIN(S_{p}))^{+}
. Indeed, take
x=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}E_{ij}\otimes E_{ji}\in S_{\infty}^{n}(S_{p})
.
This
x
is represented by a
4\times 4
matrix which is not positive, hence
x\notin S_{\infty}^{n}(S_{p})^{+}
, on the other hand, for any
\xi=(\xi_{i})\in\ell_{2}^{n}
,
\langle\xi|x|\xi\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}|\xi_{1}|^{2}&\overline{\xi_{1}}\xi_{2}\\
\xi_{1}\overline{\xi_{2}}&|\xi_{2}|^{2}\end{pmatrix}\geq 0
, so
x\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\MIN(S_{p}))^{+}
.
By duality,
S_{\infty}^{n}(S_{p})^{+}
does not coincide with
S_{\infty}^{n}(\MAX(S_{p}))^{+}
.

Similarly, if
A
is a non-abelian
C^{*}
-algebra, then, for
n\geq 2
,

(8.1)

S_{\infty}^{n}(\MAX(A))^{+}\subsetneq S_{\infty}^{n}(A)^{+}\subsetneq S_{\infty}^{n}(\MIN(A))^{+}

(the cone in the middle refers to the canonical matricial order structure of
A
). Indeed, by passing to the bidual, we can assume that
A
is a von Neumann algebra;
then it must contain
S_{\infty}^{2}
as a subalgebra, which is the range of a completely positive, completely contractive projection.
The reasoning we applied for
S_{p}
can be “recycled” to establish (
8.1
).

The situation is different if
A
is an abelian
C^{*}
-algebra, that it, a
C(K)
space (a Banach lattice). By
[
35
, Theorem 3.1]
, the maximal and the minimal matricial cones coincide.

9.
Matricial order structures on Banach lattices

Here we describe how to equip a Banach lattice with a matricial operator space structure, and examine some properties of the said structure. For Banach lattice background see, for instance,
[
1
]
,
[
3
]
, or
[
23
]
.

Following
[
1
, Section 3.2]
(where more detail can be found), we say that the
complexification

\mathcal{X}
of a real Banach lattice
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}}
consists of formal linear combinations
x+\iota y
(
x,y\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}}
), with
|x+\iota y|:=\vee_{\theta}(\cos\theta\,x+\sin\theta\,y)
, and
\|x+\iota y\|=\||x+\iota y|\|
. The
*
-operation (involution) takes
x+\iota y
to
x-\iota y
.

We should note that, for Banach spaces, a number of “reasonable” complexification procedures exist, see
[
24
]
.

If
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are complex Banach lattices, we say that a map
T
is a
lattice homomorphism
if it takes
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}}
to
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbb{R}}
, and preserves lattice operations.
T
is a
lattice isomorphism
if it is bijective, and both
T
and
T^{-1}
are lattice isomorphisms.

Below, we discuss the minimal and maximal matricial order structures on Banach lattices. Although we do not use this, let’s nevertheless recall a result of
[
35
, Section 3]
:
S_{\infty}^{n}(\MIN(\mathcal{X}))^{+}
coincides with the closure of finite sums
\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}\otimes x_{k}
, with
a_{k}\in S_{\infty}^{n+},x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}^{+}
. Consequently, the “minimal” and “maximal” matricial cones over a Banach lattice coincide.

Lemma 9.1
.

Any Banach lattice
\mathcal{X}
is
\MIN
-nice.

Proof.

We only need to verify that (
7.1
) implies
\|x\|\leq\|x_{1}\|\vee\|x_{2}\|
. Krivine functional calculus (see e.g.
[
22
, Section 1.d]
) shows that
\sup_{|\omega|=1}\sup\Re(\omega x)=|x|
, hence (
7.1
) implies that, for any
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
,
t^{2}x_{1}+s^{2}x_{2}\geq 2ts|x|
. Restricting to
t=1/s
, we obtain

|x|\leq\frac{1}{2}\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big(t^{2}x_{1}+\frac{1}{t^{2}}x_{2}\Big)=\sqrt{x_{1}x_{2}},

where the right hand side is again defined using Krivine functional calculus. By
[
22
, Proposition 1.d.2]
,
\|x\|\leq\|\sqrt{x_{1}x_{2}}\|\leq\sqrt{\|x_{1}\|\|x_{2}\|}
.
∎

Lemma 9.2
.

Any Banach lattice
\mathcal{X}
is
\MAX
-nice.

It may be possible to deduce this result from
9.1
by duality; however, we present an independent proof.

Proof.

Consider
x\in\mathcal{X}
, with
\|x\|<1
. Denote by
I_{x}
the principal ideal generated by
x
– that is, the linear (but not necessarily closed) subspace of
\mathcal{X}
consisting of elements
y
for which there exists
c\geq 0
with
|y|\leq c|x|
.
By
[
1
, Corollary 3.21]
, there exists a Hausdorff compact
K
and a bijective contractive lattice homomorphism
T:(C(K),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\to(I_{x},\|\cdot\|)
, taking
\bf 1
(the identity on
K
) to
|x|
; so,
|T^{-1}x|={\bf 1}
.
A “partition of identity” argument allows us to find non-negative
g_{1},\ldots,g_{N}\in C(K)
and
t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}\in K
so that
\sum_{k}g_{k}=\bf 1
, and

max_{t\in K}\big|[T^{-1}x](t)-\sum_{k}[T^{-1}x](t_{k})g_{k}(t)\big|\leq\varepsilon.

Let
\alpha_{k}=[T^{-1}x](t_{k})
,
\omega_{k}=\alpha_{k}/|\alpha_{k}|
,
\eta_{k}=\sqrt{|\alpha_{k}|}
,
\xi_{k}=\omega_{k}\sqrt{|\alpha_{k}|}
(so
\alpha_{k}=\xi_{k}\overline{\eta_{k}}
), and
x_{k}=Tg_{k}
.
Then
\sum_{k}|\xi_{k}|^{2}x_{k}=\sum_{k}|\eta_{k}|^{2}x_{k}=|x|
. Applying
T
to
\big|T^{-1}x-\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}g_{k}\big|\leq\varepsilon\bf 1
, we obtain
\big|x-\sum_{k}\xi_{k}\overline{\eta_{k}}x_{k}\big|\leq\varepsilon|x|
, hence
\big\|x-\sum_{k}\xi_{k}\overline{\eta_{k}}x_{k}\big\|\leq\varepsilon\|x\|<\varepsilon
.
∎

Combining the above lemmas with results of
Section

7
, conclude:

Corollary 9.3
.

For any Banach lattice
\mathcal{X}
,
\MIN(\mathcal{X})
is
1
-matricial normal, and
\MAX(\mathcal{X})
is
1
-matricial generating.

Proposition 9.4
.

If
\mathcal{X}
is a Banach lattice, then
\MIN(\mathcal{X})
is matricial generating if only if it is lattice isomorphic to an AM-space.

Recall that a
real AM-space
is a sublattice of a real-valued
C(K)
space; a
complex AM-space
is a complexification of such. By
[
23
, Theorem 2.1.12]
, a Banach lattice
\mathcal{X}
is lattice isomorphic to an AM-space iff there exists a constant
C
so that the inequality
\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\|\leq Cmax_{i}\|x_{i}\|
holds whenever
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{X}
are disjoint.

For future use, we define a dual object: a
real AL-space
is a Banach lattice
\mathcal{X}
where
\|x_{1}+x_{2}\|=\|x_{1}\|+\|x_{2}\|
whenever
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
are disjoint; a
complex AL-space
is a complexification of a real one.
Theorem 1.b.2 of
[
22
]
tells us that any real AL-space can be identified with
L_{1}(\mu)
, for some measure
\mu
. In light of
[
1
, Section 3.2, Exercise 5]
, the same holds in the complex setting.
Further, by
[
22
, Theorem 1.b.12]
,
\mathcal{X}
is lattice isomorphic to an AL-space iff there exists a constant
c>0
so that, for any pairwise disjoint
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathcal{X}
, we have
\|\sum_{i}x_{i}\|\geq c\sum_{i}\|x_{i}\|
.
By e.g.
[
3
, Theorem 4.23]
,
\mathcal{X}
is a real AL-space iff its dual is a real AM-space, and vice versa. By
[
1
, Corollary 3.26]
,
(\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}}+\iota\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}})^{\flat}
is naturally identified with the complexification of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}}^{\flat}
, hence the duality between AL and AM spaces remains true in the complex setting.

Proof of
9.4
, backward direction.

[
22
, Theorems 1.b.5-6]
show that any real AM-space has a canonical representation as a sublattice of
C(K)
, for some Hausdorff compact
K
: there exists a collection of triples
(t_{i},s_{i},\lambda_{i})_{i\in I}
, with
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
and
t_{i},s_{i}\in K
so that
\mathcal{X}
consists of all
x\in C(K)
which satisfy
x(t_{i})=\lambda_{i}x(s_{i})
for any
i\in I
. A combination of Lemma 3.18 and Exercise 3.2.3 from
[
1
]
extends this result to the complex setting.

For
a\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})_{h}
, define
b:K\to S_{\infty}^{n}:t\mapsto(a^{*}(t)a(t))^{1/2}
.
Clearly
b(t_{i})=\lambda_{i}b(s_{i})
for any
i\in I
, so
b\in S_{\infty}^{n}(\mathcal{X})
. It is easy to see that
\|b\|=\sup_{t\in K}b(t)=\sup_{t\in K}|a(t)|=\|a\|
. Finally,
\begin{pmatrix}b(t)&a(t)\\
\overline{a(t)}&b(t)\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n+}
for any
t\in K
, hence
\begin{pmatrix}b&a\\
a^{*}&b\end{pmatrix}\in S_{\infty}^{2n}(\mathcal{X})^{+}
. From this, the
1
-matricial generation follows.
∎

For the forward direction we need a lemma.

Lemma 9.5
.

Suppose
p
is an orthogonal projection onto a subspace of
\ell_{2}^{n}
, and
p^{\perp}
is its orthogonal complement. If
a\in S_{\infty}^{n}
is such that
a\geq\pm(p-p^{\perp})
, then
\mathbb{E}\langle\xi|a|\xi\rangle\geq 1
; here,
\mathbb{E}
is the expected value computed when
\xi
is uniformly distributed over the unit sphere of
\ell_{2}^{n}
.

Proof.

Clearly
\langle\xi|a|\xi\rangle=\langle p\xi|a|p\xi\rangle+\langle p^{\perp}\xi|a|p^{\perp}\xi\rangle+\langle p\xi|a|p^{\perp}\xi\rangle+\langle p^{\perp}\xi|a|p\xi\rangle
.
Our probability measure on the sphere is invariant under the orthogonal transformation
\xi\mapsto p\xi-p^{\perp}\xi
, hence
\mathbb{E}\langle p\xi|a|p^{\perp}\xi\rangle=\mathbb{E}\langle p\xi|a|-p^{\perp}\xi\rangle
, and consequently,
\mathbb{E}\langle p\xi|a|p^{\perp}\xi\rangle=0
. Likewise,
\mathbb{E}\langle p^{\perp}\xi|a|p\xi\rangle=0
.

For any
\xi\in\ell_{2}^{n}
, we have

\langle p\xi|a|p\xi\rangle\geq\langle p\xi|(p-p^{\perp})|p\xi\rangle=\|p\xi\|^{2}.

Similarly,
\langle p^{\perp}\xi|a|p^{\perp}\xi\rangle\geq\|p^{\perp}\xi\|^{2}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\langle\xi|a|\xi\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\langle p\xi|a|p\xi\rangle+\mathbb{E}\langle p^{\perp}\xi|a|p^{\perp}\xi\rangle+\mathbb{E}\langle p\xi|a|p^{\perp}\xi\rangle+\mathbb{E}\langle p^{\perp}\xi|a|p\xi\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big(\langle p\xi|a|p\xi\rangle+\langle p^{\perp}\xi|a|p^{\perp}\xi\rangle\big)\geq\mathbb{E}\big(\|p\xi\|^{2}+\|p^{\perp}\xi\|^{2}\big)=1.\qed

Proof of
9.4
, forward direction.

If
\mathcal{X}
is not an AM-space, then, by
[
10
, Lemma 4]
, for any
K>0
we can find disjoint
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{+}
so that
\|\sum_{k}x_{k}\|>K
, while
\sum_{k}|\langle x^{\flat},x_{k}\rangle|^{2}\leq 1
for any
x^{\flat}\in\ball(\mathcal{X}^{\flat})
.
The second condition means that the operator
\ell_{2}^{n}\to\mathcal{X}:\delta_{i}\mapsto x_{i}
(
(\delta_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
is the canonical basis of
\ell_{2}^{n}
) is contractive.

By
[
5
, Section 11.1]
, for
N
large enough
S_{\infty}^{N}
contains a real subspace of dimension
n
consisting exclusively of real scalar multiples of symmetric orthogonal matrices.
Let
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
be a normalized basis in this space, so that
{\textrm{tr}}(U_{i}U_{j})=0
for
i\neq j
. Then
\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}\otimes x_{i}\|\leq 2
, since
\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}\otimes x_{i}
can be viewed as an operator from
\Span[U_{i}:1\leq i\leq n]
to
\mathcal{X}
, and the former space is
2
-isomorphic to
\ell_{2}^{n}
. We shall show that, if
S_{\infty}^{N}(\mathcal{X})\ni a\geq\pm\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}\otimes x_{i}
, then

(9.1)

\mathbb{E}\langle\xi|a|\xi\rangle\geq\sum_{i}x_{i},

with the expected value taken for
\xi
uniformly distributed over the unit sphere of
\ell_{2}^{N}
. For any such
\xi
,
\langle\xi|a|\xi\rangle\leq\|a\|
, hence
\|a\|\geq\|\sum_{i}x_{i}\|\geq K
, showing that
\MIN(\mathcal{X})
cannot be matricial generating.

Write
a=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}E_{k\ell}\otimes y_{k\ell}
. As in the proof of
9.2
, we can consider
x_{i},y_{k\ell}
as “living” in
C(K)
, corresponding to the ideal
I_{x}
with
x=\sum_{i}x_{i}+\sum_{k,\ell}|y_{k\ell}|
.
In particular, we view
x_{i}
’s as disjoint positive functions on
K
, and
a
is a matrix-valued function on
K
. For any
t\in{\textrm{supp}}x_{i}
,
a(t)\geq\pm x_{i}(t)U_{i}
. Write
U_{i}=p_{i}-p_{i}^{\perp}
, where
p_{i}
is an orthogonal projection. By
9.5
,
\mathbb{E}\langle\xi|a(t)|\xi\rangle\geq x_{i}(t)
for
t\in{\textrm{supp}}x_{i}
. Summing over
i
, we obtain (
9.1
).
∎

By duality, conclude:

Corollary 9.6
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is a Banach lattice. Then
\MAX(\mathcal{X})
is matricial normal if and only if
\mathcal{X}
is lattice isomorphic to an AL-space.
