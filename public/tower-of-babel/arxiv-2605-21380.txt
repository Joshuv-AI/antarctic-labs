Modeling and Resource Optimization for Quantum Oracles
Zhihang Li, Bo Zhao, Chuanbing Han, Jie Zhao, Jinchen Xu, Guoqiang Shu, Yimin Gao, Woji He, Zheng Shan

Abstract
Quantum computing has demonstrated its significant advantage over supercomputing for specific applications and shown promising prospect, such as machine learning, cryptography, finance, etc.. Quantum oracles are very common in many quantum algorithms and oracle resource consumption directly affects algorithm performance. However, existing oracle designs often exhibit high resource overhead and limited compatibility. Moreover, structured description tools and complexity analysis methods are lacked. In this work, we introduces a Hierarchical Recursive Synthesis-Evaluation (HRSE) model, enabling formal description and precise quantum gate complexity analysis of oracles. Based on this model, we propose an Adaptive Space-Depth Trade-off (ASDT) algorithm for generating oracle structures under a fixed qubit constraint. We provide a theoretical proof showing that the ASDT algorithm achieves the optimal gate count for a given number of qubits. Experimental results show that the ASDT algorithm reduces the average quantum circuit depth by 53.99% compared with the W-cycle approach, with the number of variables being 10, 15, and 20, respectively.

Preliminaries: Qubit Multiplexing Technique

Qubit multiplexing allows a limited number of auxiliary qubits to evaluate numerous functions. This technique has been adopted in oracle constructions in many previous studies
[
12
,
13
,
22
,
27
]

Each function is first computed and then encoded into computed and stored in an auxiliary qubit. An MCX gate then merges the results of all auxiliary qubits into a single target qubit, indicating if all functions are satisfied. Finally, the auxiliary qubits are uncomputed and returned to their original states for reuse, while the target qubit records whether the input satisfies all functions.

The XOR oracle is a special case of the shift oracle, which simplifies the presentation and avoids unnecessary technical details. The modeling framework and algorithms proposed in this work are equally applicable to general shift oracles.

As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), the XOR oracle and the phase oracle can be converted into each other using multi-controlled X (MCX) and multi-controlled Z (MCZ) gates. More generally, shift oracles and phase oracles can be transformed into each other using the quantum Fourier transform

In this paper, we use an abstract representation of functions to develop a general methodology. Our focus is not on how to encode a specific problem function, but on how the computations of multiple functions should be organized and composed once their quantum circuit implementations are given. In practice, user’s only need to provide the quantum circuit implementation of each function, which can then be incorporated into the proposed framework to construct and analyze the corresponding oracle structure.

III
Hierarchical Recursive Synthesis-Evaluation Model

Constructing an optimal oracle structure requires tools for quantum gate complexity analysis. The recursion depth of an oracle is a key factor affecting its complexity. To address this, we introduce a formal model for oracle structures, termed the Hierarchical Recursive Synthesis and Evaluation (HRSE) model.

To provide an intuitive understanding, we briefly outline the modeling philosophy of the HRSE model. The HRSE model aims to provide a structured analytical framework for the complex architecture of multi-function oracles. The key observation is that the composition of functions within a multi-function oracle typically exhibits a recursive structure, which can be naturally represented using a recursive tree.
Specifically, each function evaluation unit is modeled as a node in the tree, while the containment relationships between computation units are represented as edges.
To enable complexity analysis and optimization, each node is associated with several attributes, such as its depth and the number of child nodes.
Under this modeling approach, even if an oracle appears highly complex at the quantum circuit level, it can be represented by progressively constructing the corresponding HRSE tree: each function computation unit forms a node, and edges are introduced according to the relationships among these computations.
By traversing and analyzing this tree structure, the quantum gate complexity of the oracle can be systematically evaluated and its structure can be further optimized.

In Section
III-A
, the HRSE model and its validity constraints are defined. Section
III-B
then demonstrates how the model can be used to analyze the quantum gate count of an oracle. The HRSE model thus provides a systematic foundation, identifies the key factors affecting gate complexity, and establishes a theoretical basis for further optimization.

IV
Adaptive Space-Depth Trade-off algorithm

Building upon the HRSE model and its complexity framework, we introduce the Adaptive Space-Depth Trade-off (ASDT) algorithm, which incrementally generates HRSE tree structures to minimize root-node complexity under a given qubit count constraint. Section
IV-A
details the ASDT algorithm’s procedure, and Section
IV-B
provides a proof of its optimality in terms of quantum gate count.

IV-A

Algorithm Process

In the ASDT algorithm, the HRSE model tree
T_{k}(m)
is constructed incrementally by progressively adding function nodes. To efficiently select the node for expansion, the algorithm employs two prioritization strategies. Strategy 1 (the minimum-depth strategy) selects the node with the smallest depth, thereby controlling the growth scale of the tree. When multiple nodes share the same depth, Strategy 2 (the maximum-size strategy) is applied, selecting the node with the largest size to maximize its capacity for accommodating child nodes. The rationale for these strategies stems from the complexity formula in Equation
5
. Strategy 1 minimizes node depth. Because expanding a leaf node (i.e., splitting it) incurs a fixed overhead per expansion, Strategy 2 minimizes the total number of such expansions required for a given number of constraint functions. This results in fewer non-leaf nodes contributing to the sum in Equation
5
, thereby reducing the total cost. The combined use of these strategies guarantees the optimality of the generated structure
T_{k}(m)
; a rigorous proof is provided in Section
IV-B
. Upon completion of the tree, a single post-order traversal computes the complexity
c(v_{i})
and the number of leaf nodes

for each node
v_{i}

IV-B

Optimality Proof for Leaf Node Cost

In multi-function oracle construction, the computational cost in our model is naturally divided into leaf-node cost and non-leaf-node cost. Leaf nodes represent function evaluations, so the leaf-node cost measures the number of repeated function computations, whereas non-leaf nodes correspond to multi-controlled Toffoli operations used to combine intermediate results. Since function computation constitutes the primary computational cost in oracle construction, the main optimization objective is therefore to minimize the number of repeated function evaluations. Accordingly, the optimality of the proposed algorithm is defined with respect to the leaf-node cost. In this proof, we focus on the leaf-node cost term
\sum_{\kappa(v)=0}2^{d(v)}\cdot\delta
.

We prove by contradiction that the cost
\sum_{\kappa(v)=0}2^{d(v)}\cdot\delta
is minimized. Assume, for contradiction, that for the HRSE tree
T_{k}(m)
constructed by the ASDT algorithm, there exists a valid tree

such that
c(T_{k}^{\prime}(m))<c(T_{k}(m))

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\{v\mid v\in V(T_{k}(m)),\,v\notin V(T_{k}^{\prime}(m))\},

(13)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\{u\mid u\in V(T_{k}^{\prime}(m)),\,u\notin V(T_{k}(m))\}.

(14)

IV-C

Complexity Analysis for Non-Leaf Node Cost

This section provides a supplementary complexity analysis of the non-leaf-node cost term
\sum_{\kappa(u)\neq 0}2^{d(u)}\cdot\Gamma(u)
to better understand the overall complexity behavior. While we do not provide a formal optimality proof for the non-leaf-node term, this term is still reduced to some extent under the ASDT algorithm.

The ASDT algorithm minimizes the leaf-node term
\sum_{\kappa(v)=0}2^{d(v)}\cdot\delta
, i.e., the exponential sum over leaf depths. This optimization also benefits the non-leaf-node term. Since every non-leaf node lies on a path to a leaf, its depth cannot exceed that of its descendant leaf nodes. Therefore, reducing leaf-node depths directly constrains the exponential factor
2^{d(u)}
in the non-leaf-node cost.

for

Its main objective is to combine the evaluation results of multiple functions in a hierarchical manner, thereby reducing the number of required auxiliary qubits. In our experiments, the Boolean equation systems are encoded into phase oracles using the ASDT algorithm and the W-cycle approach, respectively. The oracle takes the form

In our evaluation, the oracle structures generated by the ASDT algorithm are compared against the fixed W-cycle structures, demonstrating a significant reduction in quantum circuit depth. A key limitation of the preset W-cycle scheme is its susceptibility to discretization effects, often resulting in suboptimal gate counts. In contrast, as proven in Section
IV-B
, the ASDT algorithm achieves theoretical optimality in gate count. To ensure a fair comparison with the W-cycle approach, we adopt the same problem sizes (
n=15,20,25
) and construct systems of Boolean equations from the test sets available in its public code repository. For each problem size, 15 uniquely solvable equation instances are used. The circuit depth is evaluated using a two-tier averaging method. First, the average depth per Grover iteration is computed for each individual equation instance. Subsequently, these averages are aggregated across all 15 instances to yield the final depth metric for each problem size. The circuit depth is measured after decomposing the circuits into single-qubit gates and multi-qubit gates such as CNOT and CCNOT, with MCZ gates further decomposed into H and MCX gates.

A detailed analysis of the results reveals specific scenarios where the advantages of ASDT become apparent. V-B

Space-depth Trade-off of ASDT Algorithm

The performance of the ASDT algorithm is governed by a key parameter: the number of auxiliary qubits
k
, which is in a direct trade-off relationship with the resulting quantum circuit depth. Our experimental analysis, summarized in Figure
5
, systematically explores this space-depth trade-off by varying
k
while holding the number of variables constant. The results reveals a distinct two-phase behavior. Initially, increasing
k
leads to a significant reduction in circuit depth, as evidenced by the sharp decline observed from
k=5
to
k=9
for all variable sizes (e.g., for
n=16
, the depths drops from 23,975 to 11,816). This reduction occurs because additional qubits reduce the need for costly qubit reuse and the serialization of operations, thereby enabling a higher degree of parallel execution and resulting in shallower circuits.

However, this improvement plateaus once
k
exceeds the number of functions
m
required for the problem instance. Beyond this critical point, further increases in
k
yield diminishing returns, and the circuit depth stabilizes. For example, for
n=9
, the depth reaches its minimum of 1448 at
k=9
and remains unchanged for
k=10
. This saturation indicates that the circuit has achieved its maximum parallelism under the given functions; supplying additional qubits beyond what is functionally necessary does not further reduce the depth. This observed threshold, therefore, identifies the minimal resource allocation required to achieve the optimal circuit depth for a given problem size.

VI
Conclusion

This paper introduces the Hierarchical Recursive Synthesis-Evaluation (HRSE) model, specifying its structural definition, attribute correspondences, and the constraints required for model validity. The framework provides a comprehensive representation of XOR-oracle recursive structures, enabling an intuitive characterization of complex recursive relationships. Building on the HRSE model, we propose a quantum gate complexity analysis approach that quantifies the number of quantum gates in the oracle circuit by aggregating the costs associated with nodes and edges in the model. This approach reveals the primary sources of quantum gate complexity and provides an analytical foundation for subsequent optimization algorithms. The HRSE model is applicable not only to the ASDT algorithm but also serves as a theoretical foundation for future studies on oracle optimization.

Based on the HRSE model, we propose the Adaptive Space-Depth Trade-off (ASDT) algorithm, which achieves an optimal quantum gate count. Our analysis shows that increasing the number of auxiliary qubits reduces the depth of the resulting oracle circuits. We provide a theoretical proof that the HRSE tree constructed by the ASDT algorithm achieves optimal quantum gate count , with the optimization objective of minimizing
\sum_{\kappa(v)=0}2^{d(v)}\cdot\delta
. Experimental results on solving systems of nonlinear Boolean equations under uniform auxiliary qubit constraints demonstrate that for problem sizes of
n=15,20,25
, the average circuit depth of oracles constructed by our method is reduced by 53.99% compared to the W-cycle approach.

Several open issues and directions for future work remain. The current HRSE model and ASDT algorithm do not account for specific function encoding methods or decomposition strategies for multi-controlled X (MCX) gates. The depth of the circuit after MCX gate decomposition is significantly influenced by the number of auxiliary qubits available for the decomposition process. Future work will explore the joint optimization of auxiliary qubit allocation between the constraint function computation and MCX gate decomposition stages to achieve further reductions in circuit depth.
Another important direction is to investigate the asymptotic behavior of the proposed model and algorithm under large-scale parameters, and to establish more general analytical results for complexity characterization.
The proposed framework is highly general and can be applied to a broader class of constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs).
In particular, evaluating the HRSE model and ASDT algorithm on more complex problems, such as AES cryptanalysis, may further demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework and optimization strategy.
In addition, since the proposed approach focuses on structural modeling and optimization, it is naturally complementary to pebbling-based methods that optimize the scheduling of intermediate computations.
Combining the HRSE-based structural optimization with advanced pebbling strategies, such as the divide-and-conquer approach proposed by Zhang et al.
[
25
]
, may further reduce resource consumption in practical oracle implementations.
These directions will be investigated in future studies.
