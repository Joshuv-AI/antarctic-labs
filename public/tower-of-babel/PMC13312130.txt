Effects of dietary exemestane administration on reproductive performance, hepatic function, and lipid metabolism in Japanese quail
Authors: Mohammad Amin Hadavi, Abouzar Najafi, Seyed Davood Sharifi, Shokoufe Ghazanfari, Hasan Rouhanipour, Esmaeil Pourhosseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEstrogen is a central regulator of avian reproduction and metabolism, with key functions in vitellogenesis and hepatic lipid mobilization. This study evaluated the physiological effects of dietary exemestane administration, using a compound pharmacologically classified as an irreversible steroidal aromatase inhibitor, on reproductive performance, serum lipid indices, hepatic enzyme activity, egg quality, yolk lipid peroxidation, and yolk fatty acid composition in Japanese quail (Coturnix japonica). A total of 192 28-day-old female quail were acclimated for 14 days on a basal diet and subsequently fed experimental diets containing 0 (control), 0.5, 1.0, or 1.5 mg/kg exemestane from 43 days of age for 56 days. Reproductive performance traits were recorded throughout the trial, whereas serum biochemical variables, hepatic enzyme activities, egg-quality traits, and yolk oxidative and fatty acid indices were evaluated at the end of the experimental period. Dietary exemestane did not significantly affect overall egg production or egg weight. However, birds receiving 1.5 mg/kg exemestane exhibited reduced eggshell percentage, altered serum HDL cholesterol, increased aspartate aminotransferase activity, and markedly higher yolk lipid peroxidation, suggesting dose-related metabolic and oxidative responses at the highest inclusion level. Exemestane administration also produced selective alterations in yolk fatty acid composition, including changes in monounsaturated and n-3 fatty acid fractions. Overall, these findings indicate that dietary exemestane administration is associated with dose-dependent changes in metabolic and egg-quality traits without marked impairment of reproductive output. Because circulating sex steroids, gonadotropins, tissue expression of cytochrome P450 family 19 subfamily A member 1 (CYP19A1), and aromatase activity were not directly measured, these results should be interpreted as physiological responses compatible with estrogen-sensitive pathway modulation rather than as direct empirical confirmation of aromatase inhibition.Keywords: Exemestane, Aromatase inhibition, Lipid metabolism, Oxidative stress, Japanese quail

Introduction

Estrogens, synthesized primarily from androgens through the action of the aromatase enzyme (encoded by CYP19A1), play essential roles in the regulation of reproductive and metabolic processes across vertebrate species. In avian physiology, 17β-estradiol (E2) is a central regulator of follicular development, vitellogenesis, and hepatic lipid mobilization required for yolk precursor synthesis (Rosati et al., 2021). Beyond its reproductive functions, estrogen also influences lipid metabolism, hepatic enzyme activity, and overall energy homeostasis. Consequently, disruption of aromatase activity may have broad physiological consequences that extend beyond reproduction to metabolic and hepatic regulation in birds.

Japanese quail (Coturnix japonica) is a well-established avian model for investigating steroid hormone regulation due to its short generation interval, clearly defined reproductive physiology, and relevance to commercial poultry production (Hassan et al., 2013). In birds, aromatase expression is not confined to the gonads, where it modulates feedback regulation of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH), but is also present in extragonadal tissues such as the liver, adipose tissue, and brain (Grzegorzewska, 2020). Because these extragonadal sites participate in lipid transport, hepatic metabolic activity, calcium utilization, and oxidative balance, aromatase inhibition may be expected to affect not only reproductive output but also circulating lipid fractions, liver-associated enzymes, eggshell traits, and yolk lipid stability. These integrated endpoints therefore provide a physiologically relevant framework for evaluating systemic responses to altered estrogen synthesis in avian species (Johnson, 2014; Mills et al., 2021; Stocco, 2012).

Exemestane is a steroidal, irreversible aromatase inhibitor structurally analogous to androstenedione, which binds covalently to the active site of the aromatase enzyme, resulting in sustained suppression of estrogen synthesis (Lombardi, 2002). In mammalian systems, exemestane has been widely used in clinical oncology and pediatric endocrinology, and its effects on lipid metabolism and hepatic function including alterations in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglyceride concentrations are well documented (Palmisano and Zhu, 2017; Untch and Jackisch, 2008). These metabolic responses are largely mediated through estrogen-dependent regulation of hepatic apolipoprotein synthesis, bile acid metabolism, and lipoprotein lipase activity. However, despite extensive mammalian evidence, the physiological implications of long-term dietary aromatase inhibition in avian species remain insufficiently characterized.

Beyond its mechanistic relevance, modulation of estrogen synthesis through aromatase inhibition may have experimental and cautiously applied relevance for poultry physiology and management. Previous avian studies have shown that aromatase inhibitors can alter reproductive endocrine function, sex differentiation, semen quality, and reproductive performance in birds (Abdulateef et al., 2021; Ali et al., 2017; Bazyar et al., 2019). However, the translation of aromatase inhibition to poultry production requires careful evaluation because changes in estrogen signaling may influence eggshell quality, lipid utilization, hepatic metabolism, and welfare-related physiological responses. In addition, reproductive biology has broader relevance to avian conservation and population management, although pharmacological endocrine manipulation should be interpreted cautiously and should not be inferred as a direct management recommendation without safety and efficacy validation (Elphick et al., 2007). Nevertheless, few studies have concurrently evaluated reproductive performance, lipid metabolism, egg quality, and hepatic function following aromatase inhibitor administration in avian models such as Japanese quail (De Bournonville et al., 2019; Ottinger and Quinn, 2024).

Despite these considerations, a critical knowledge gap remains regarding the chronic, dose-dependent physiological responses of birds to dietary exemestane exposure. In particular, limited information is available on the integrated responses of lipid homeostasis, hepatic function, egg quality, and reproductive performance following prolonged exposure to this steroidal aromatase-inhibiting compound. Direct measurements of estradiol, testosterone, LH, FSH, tissue CYP19A1 expression, and aromatase activity were not included in the present experimental design; therefore, the study was structured as a physiological evaluation rather than as a direct endocrine validation of aromatase suppression. The concurrent assessment of reproductive tissues, follicular populations, serum biochemical indices, yolk lipid peroxidation, and fatty acid composition was intended to provide an integrated profile of endpoints relevant to estrogen-sensitive regulation in birds. Accordingly, the objective of the present study was to evaluate the effects of dietary exemestane supplementation (0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mg/kg feed) over an eight-week period in female Japanese quail, with specific emphasis on reproductive performance, egg quality, circulating lipid profiles, and markers of hepatic function. We hypothesized that dietary exemestane administration would induce dose-dependent alterations in metabolic and egg-quality traits plausibly associated with estrogen-sensitive regulation, particularly lipid metabolism, hepatic enzyme activity, oxidative status, and eggshell formation, with more pronounced systemic responses at higher inclusion levels.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Tehran (approval number No. 2024.01.21), ensuring minimal distress to the birds throughout the study.

Bird management and treatments

A total of 192 28-day-old female Japanese quail (Coturnix japonica; body weight 120 ± 5 g) were used in a 56-day feeding trial following a 14-day acclimation period. Birds were first subjected to the acclimation period during which they received a basal diet; thereafter, the experimental diets were offered from 43 days of age and continued for 56 days. Birds were randomly allocated to 16 cages in a completely randomized design with four dietary treatments, each consisting of four replicates with 12 birds per replicate. Exemestane (≥98% purity; Sigma-Aldrich, Y0001747) was finely ground and dissolved in 95% ethanol. The resulting solution was emulsified in soybean oil (5 mL/kg feed) and thoroughly mixed into the basal diet to obtain final dietary concentrations of 0 (control), 0.5, 1.0, or 1.5 mg exemestane/kg feed. Control diets received the same ethanol-soybean oil vehicle without exemestane. Experimental diets were prepared weekly, air-dried for 24 h at room temperature under a fume hood to allow evaporation of residual ethanol, homogenized, and stored in sealed opaque containers at room temperature until use. The feed-mixing route was selected to model chronic dietary exposure under poultry-relevant husbandry conditions and to avoid repeated handling stress associated with daily gavage or injection. Because direct chemical recovery of exemestane from the mixed feed was not performed, diet preparation was standardized by weekly preparation, vehicle control, protected storage, and pen-level feed-intake monitoring. Based on average daily feed intake during the experimental period and the initial mean body weight, the estimated daily intake of exemestane was approximately 0.12, 0.24, and 0.36 mg/kg BW/day for the 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mg/kg feed groups, respectively. Diet formulations (Table 1) were prepared according to the Brazilian Tables (Rostagno et al., 2011), and were formulated to be isocaloric (2,973 kcal/kg metabolizable energy) and isonitrogenous (20% crude protein). Prior to and throughout the experimental period, birds were maintained under controlled environmental conditions, including a photoperiod of 16 h light and 8 h dark, ambient temperature of 22 ± 2°C, relative humidity of approximately 60%, and drinking water temperature maintained between 20 and 24°C. Quail were housed in cages measuring 90 × 60 × 40 cm, lined with paper bedding, within an environmentally controlled room. Each cage was equipped with a feeder and a manual bell-shaped drinker. Lighting was provided using standard white light-emitting diode (LED) lamps (6 W, 365 lm, 6,200 K; 220-240 V, 50-60 Hz). Light intensity was maintained at 20-30 lx during the first three days of the experiment and subsequently reduced to 5-10 lx until the end of the trial, as measured using a digital lux meter (TES 1335, Taiwan). Feed and fresh water were provided ad libitum throughout the experiment. The amount of feed allocated to each replicate was weighed at the beginning of each week and stored in a designated container. Feed was offered daily at 08:00 h. At the end of each week, residual feed was collected from the feeders and weighed. Weekly feed intake was calculated as the difference between the feed offered and feed remaining. Average daily feed intake was then calculated by dividing weekly feed intake by the number of bird-days per replicate and expressed as grams per bird per day (g/bird/day).

Production performance

Egg number, average egg weight, and egg mass were recorded daily and summarized on a biweekly basis. The date of the first egg was monitored during daily egg collection, but time to first egg was not analyzed as an independent endpoint because the experiment was designed primarily to evaluate laying performance after transfer to the experimental diets. Data on feed intake obtained as described above were used to calculate egg production rate, egg mass output, egg shape index, and feed conversion ratio (FCR), expressed as kilograms of feed consumed per kilogram of egg mass produced (kg feed/kg eggs), according to Vasdal et al. (2019).

Egg physical properties

Egg physical properties, including eggshell thickness, eggshell weight, yolk height, yolk diameter, and eggshell percentage, were evaluated at weekly intervals. At the end of each week, four eggs were randomly collected from each replicate for analysis, following the methodology described by Schreiweis et al. (2003). Each egg was individually weighed to the nearest 0.01 g using an electronic digital balance. Egg length and width were measured using a digital caliper, and eggshell strength was determined using a digital eggshell strength tester (Egg Shell Strength Tester II, model EG-001, OSR 13473R, OGAWA SEIKI Co., Ltd.) with an accuracy of ±0.2%. For the assessment of internal egg quality traits, eggs were broken onto a flat surface. Albumen height was measured using an electronic height gauge (Technical Services and Supplies QDC Egg Quality System, TSS, York, UK). Measurements were taken on the thick albumen, avoiding contact with the yolk and chalazae, and care was taken to ensure that the albumen was not ruptured during the procedure. The albumen index was calculated as the ratio of albumen height to albumen width, as described by Heiman and Carver (1936). Albumen width was determined by averaging the minimum and maximum diameters measured using dial calipers (Bel-Art Products, Pequannock, NJ). Albumen and yolk were separated and weighed individually using an electronic balance. Yolk diameter was measured with dial calipers, and yolk height was determined using a standard tripod micrometer. The yolk index was calculated as the ratio of yolk height to yolk diameter, with measurements taken while the yolk remained in its natural position after breaking the egg (Funk, 1948). Following separation, eggshells were rinsed with tap water to remove residual albumen and then dried in an oven at 65°C for 12 h. Eggshell thickness was measured using an Ames micrometer (model 25, BC Ames Company, Waltham, MA). Thickness measurements were taken at seven locations: three at the equator, two at the pointed end, and two at the blunt end. The average of these measurements, expressed in micrometers (µm), was considered the eggshell thickness. Finally, dried eggshells were weighed using a digital balance with a precision of 0.01 g to determine eggshell weight.

Physiological and reproductive parameters

At 12 weeks of age, two female quail were randomly selected from each replicate, resulting in a total of 8 birds per treatment (n = 8). Selected birds were humanely euthanized in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines, local regulations. Immediately after euthanasia, the ovary and oviduct were carefully excised, freed from surrounding connective tissues, and weighed using an electronic digital balance with a precision of 0.01 g. Relative organ weights (ovary and oviduct) were calculated as the ratio of organ weight to final body weight and expressed as a percentage. These parameters were used as indicators of estrogen-dependent reproductive tissue development in response to dietary aromatase inhibition. To assess follicular development, ovarian follicles were carefully separated and initially counted by visual inspection. Follicles were subsequently measured and classified using a dissecting stereomicroscope, following established procedures (Mfoundou et al., 2021). Based on morphology and diameter, follicles were categorized into three groups: small white follicles (SWFs; 3–5 mm), small yellow follicles (SYFs; 5–10 mm), and large yellow follicles (LYFs; 10–12 mm), as described by Hassan et al. (2013).

Malondialdehyde assay

To assess lipid peroxidation, egg samples were collected during the final two days of the 12th week of age. A total of eight eggs per treatment (n = 2 eggs per replicate) were collected and broken, and yolks were carefully separated from the albumen. Fresh yolks were stored at −20°C until analysis. Prior to analysis, yolk samples were refrigerated at 4°C for 3 days to allow controlled oxidative development. Lipid peroxidation was then evaluated by measuring MDA concentration using a spectrophotometric method, as described by Botsoglou et al. (1994). Briefly, 1 g of yolk sample was transferred into a 15-mL centrifuge tube, and 4 mL of 5% (w/v) aqueous trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and 2.5 mL of 0.8% (w/v) butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) in hexane were added sequentially. Samples were homogenized using an Ultra-Turrax homogenizer for 30 s at high speed and then centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 3 min. The upper hexane layer was discarded, and the lower aqueous phase was filtered through Whatman filter paper and adjusted to a final volume of 5 mL with TCA solution. Subsequently, 3 mL of 0.8% (w/v) aqueous thiobarbituric acid (TBA) was added to each sample. The reaction mixture was incubated in a water bath at 70°C for 30 min. After cooling to room temperature, absorbance was measured at 521 nm using a spectrophotometer. MDA concentration was quantified using a standard curve prepared with 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane (TEP) and expressed as nanomoles of MDA per gram of yolk (nmol/g).

Yolk fatty acid analysis

To determine egg yolk fatty acid profiles, a total of 16 eggs per dietary treatment were collected at the end of the experimental period (n = 4 eggs per replicate). Yolk samples corresponding to each replicate were separated, pooled, and homogenized to form a composite sample for each pen. These replicate samples (n = 4 per treatment) were transferred into 50-mL Falcon tubes and stored at −20°C until further analysis. Fatty acid profiles of both the experimental diets and egg yolk samples were determined according to the method described by Mazalli and Bragagnolo (2007) using gas chromatography. Briefly, 0.5 g of each sample (diet or egg yolk) was mixed with 5 mL of 2% methanolic solution and incubated in a water bath for 10 min. Subsequently, 175 µL of the reaction mixture was transferred into a test tube, and 2 mL of boron trifluoride–methanol solution (20% BF₃) was added. The tubes were then placed in a boiling water bath for 3 min to facilitate fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) formation. After cooling, 1 mL of n-hexane and 1 mL of saturated NaCl solution were added to each tube. The mixture was vortexed, and the upper hexane layer containing the FAMEs was carefully collected using a micropipette and transferred into 1.5-mL microtubes. Fatty acid methyl esters were reconstituted in 1 mL of hexane following the boron trifluoride-methanol esterification approach described by Metcalfe et al. (1966). Fatty acid separation and quantification were performed using gas chromatography (GC; YL 6100, Young Lin, Korea) equipped with a flame ionization detector and a 60-m capillary column (0.25 mm internal diameter; Dikmacap-2330). Hydrogen was used as the carrier gas. The oven temperature program was set from an initial temperature of 170°C to a final temperature of 230°C. Injector and detector temperatures were maintained at 260°C and 300°C, respectively. A volume of 2.0 µL of each sample was injected for analysis. Individual fatty acids were identified by comparing their retention times with those of known standards, and results were expressed as a percentage of total identified fatty acids (Metcalfe et al., 1966).

Blood sample collection

At the end of the experimental period (12 weeks of age), blood samples were collected to evaluate serum biochemical parameters. One female quail was randomly selected from each cage, resulting in four experimental-unit observations per dietary treatment (n = 4 pens per treatment). This sampling scheme was selected to avoid pseudo-replication and to align biochemical analyses with the pen-based experimental design; however, the relatively small number of replicate observations for serum variables was recognized as a limitation for statistical power and was considered when interpreting lipid and enzyme responses. Birds were subjected to a 2-h feed withdrawal prior to sampling to minimize postprandial variation. Approximately 5 mL of blood was collected from the wing vein using sterile syringes. Blood samples were immediately transferred into non-heparinized vacuum tubes and allowed to clot at room temperature for 2 h. Samples were then centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 min to separate the serum. The resulting serum was carefully harvested, transferred into labeled microtubes, and stored at −20°C until analysis. Serum concentrations of triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c) were determined using commercial colorimetric diagnostic kits according to the manufacturer's instructions (Diagnostic Products Corporation, Pars Azmoon, Tehran, Iran). Activities of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were measured using commercial kinetic ultraviolet enzymatic assays, and absorbance was monitored at 340 nm using a spectrophotometer (Genesys 10 s UV-Vis, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For each assay, 10 µL of serum was added to 1,000 µL of the corresponding reagent (AST: AST-110100; ALT: ALT-10100). Samples were incubated at room temperature for 1 min before kinetic reading. All biochemical assays were performed in duplicate. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were less than 5%, indicating acceptable analytical precision (Xie et al., 2023).

Statistical analysis

Prior to statistical analysis, all data were examined for normality and homogeneity of variances. Residual normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test with the UNIVARIATE procedure, whereas homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene's test through the HOVTEST option in the GLM procedure of SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Data were analyzed according to a completely randomized design with four dietary levels of exemestane. The pen was considered the experimental unit for all measured variables. For variables measured on individual birds or eggs within a pen, including malondialdehyde concentration, blood biochemical parameters, reproductive organ traits, and egg yolk fatty acid profiles, observations were averaged by pen before statistical analysis to generate one replicate value per pen. Accordingly, serum biochemical variables and yolk fatty acid variables were analyzed using four pen-level observations per treatment, and this modest number of experimental units was considered when interpreting treatment effects, mean separation results, and polynomial contrasts. Egg physical properties, which were measured repeatedly over time, were analyzed as repeated measures using the MIXED procedure (PROC MIXED) of SAS, with dietary treatment, time, and their interaction included as fixed effects and pen specified as the subject effect. The covariance structure was selected according to the lowest Akaike information criterion among candidate structures, including compound symmetry, autoregressive, and unstructured covariance matrices. All other endpoint data were analyzed using the General Linear Model procedure (PROC GLM). For the one-way endpoint analyses, the omnibus treatment effect was evaluated with 3 numerator and 12 denominator degrees of freedom, corresponding to four treatment levels and 16 pen-level experimental units. Each preplanned orthogonal polynomial contrast (linear, quadratic, and cubic) was evaluated with 1 numerator and 12 denominator degrees of freedom. For repeated-measures egg-quality analyses, denominator degrees of freedom were estimated within PROC MIXED according to the selected covariance structure. When significant treatment effects were detected (P ≤ 0.05), mean comparisons were performed using Tukey's post hoc test. Orthogonal polynomial contrasts were used to describe dose-response patterns; however, higher-order polynomial responses were interpreted cautiously as descriptive indicators and not as independent mechanistic evidence. Results were considered statistically significant at P ≤ 0.05 and are presented as least squares means ± standard error of the means (SEM). Individual pen-level observations for serum biochemical outcomes were additionally visualized using dot plots with mean ± SD overlays to facilitate transparent assessment of within-treatment dispersion and potential influential observations.

Results

Production performance

Dietary administration of exemestane at 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mg/kg did not significantly affect overall laying performance of Japanese quail during the 56-day experimental period (Table 2). No significant differences were observed among treatments with respect to egg weight, egg mass, feed conversion ratio, or hen-day egg production compared with the control group. During the first biweekly period after birds transitioned to the experimental diets (42-56 d of age), egg production did not differ among treatments, suggesting that the reduced feed intake observed in the 0.5 mg/kg group was not accompanied by a detectable delay in early laying performance. Daily feed intake varied in response to the experimental diets during specific periods (P < 0.05). From 42 to 56 d of age, birds fed 0.5 mg/kg exemestane consumed less feed than those in the control group (P < 0.05). Likewise, during the 85-98 d period, feed intake declined as dietary exemestane levels increased, with birds receiving 1.5 mg/kg exemestane showing lower feed consumption than control birds (P < 0.05).

Egg physical properties

The effects of varying dietary levels of exemestane on egg quality traits are presented in Table 3. Egg specific gravity was significantly higher in quail receiving 1.5 mg/kg exemestane compared with the lower supplementation levels and the control group (P < 0.05). In contrast, eggshell percentage was reduced in birds fed 1.5 mg/kg exemestane relative to the other treatments (P < 0.05). Shell weight itself was not significantly affected by dietary treatment, indicating that the significant response in eggshell percentage reflected the proportional contribution of the shell to total egg weight rather than a statistically detectable treatment effect on absolute shell weight. In addition, Haugh unit, yolk index, shell weight, yolk height, yolk diameter, and albumen thickness were significantly affected by time across all exemestane levels (P < 0.05). However, egg shape index, egg diameter, egg length, and shell thickness were not significantly influenced by dietary treatment.

Serum metabolic parameters

Dietary exemestane supplementation significantly affected HDL cholesterol concentration and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) activity in female quail (Table 4). Serum HDL cholesterol was lowest in birds receiving 0.5 mg/kg exemestane, highest in birds receiving 1.0 mg/kg, and intermediate in the control and 1.5 mg/kg groups. The overall treatment effect was significant (P = 0.0002), with significant linear (P = 0.014) and cubic (P < 0.0001) components. AST activity differed among treatments (P < 0.0001), with the highest value observed at 1.5 mg/kg exemestane and the lowest value observed at 1.0 mg/kg. Significant linear, quadratic, and cubic contrasts were detected for AST activity (P ≤ 0.0003). LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, total cholesterol, and ALT activity were not significantly affected by treatment (P > 0.05). Yolk fatty acid profiles

Dietary exemestane supplementation resulted in selective alterations in the egg yolk fatty acid profile (Table 6). Exemestane significantly affected myristic acid (C14:0), palmitic acid (C16:0), palmitoleic acid (C16:1), oleic acid (C18:1n-9), linoleic acid (C18:2n-6), alpha-linolenic acid (C18:3n-3), gamma-linolenic acid (C18:3n-6), gadoleic acid (C20:1n-9), eicosatrienoic acid (C20:3n-3), eicosapentaenoic acid (C20:5n-3), and docosapentaenoic acid (C22:5n-3) (P < 0.05). In general, exemestane increased the proportions of oleic acid and selected monounsaturated fatty acids, whereas linoleic acid was reduced in all supplemented groups relative to the control. Several fatty acids showed significant nonlinear dose-response patterns, as indicated by quadratic and/or cubic contrasts (P < 0.05). No significant treatment effects were detected for arachidonic acid, docosatetraenoic acid, docosapentaenoic acid n-6, docosahexaenoic acid, or several other identified fatty acids.

As shown in Table 7, dietary exemestane significantly affected the main yolk fatty acid classes and ratios. Total saturated fatty acids (Σ SFA) decreased at 0.5 and 1.5 mg/kg, whereas total unsaturated fatty acids (Σ UFA) increased in all supplemented groups compared with the control (P < 0.05). The UFA/SFA ratio was also higher in the 0.5 and 1.5 mg/kg groups. Total omega-3 fatty acids (Σ n-3) were lower at 1.0 mg/kg than in the other treatments, whereas total omega-6 fatty acids (Σ n-6) were reduced in all exemestane-supplemented groups. The Σ n-6/Σ n-3 ratio was highest at 1.0 mg/kg and lower in the control, 0.5 mg/kg, and 1.5 mg/kg groups (P < 0.0001). These variables showed significant linear, quadratic, and/or cubic contrasts depending on the specific fatty acid class.

Discussion

The production-performance findings indicate that dietary exemestane, at the inclusion levels evaluated, did not substantially disrupt the normal laying trajectory of female Japanese quail. The maintenance of egg production despite exposure to a pharmacological aromatase-inhibiting compound may reflect compensatory physiological mechanisms; however, this interpretation remains provisional because circulating estradiol, testosterone, LH, and FSH concentrations were not measured. Partial endocrine modulation is a plausible explanation for the preservation of folliculogenesis and oviduct function, but direct endocrine measurements would be required to substantiate this mechanism. Extragonadal aromatization in tissues such as the liver and adipose tissue may also contribute to estrogen availability and may buffer reproductive tissues against complete functional suppression (Grzegorzewska, 2020). In addition, reduced estrogen-mediated negative feedback could increase gonadotropin secretion, including FSH and LH, thereby stimulating ovarian steroidogenesis and partly offsetting the inhibitory effects of exemestane (Ali et al., 2017). These explanations should be regarded as mechanistic hypotheses rather than demonstrated causal pathways. The present findings suggest that the tested inclusion levels (0.5-1.5 mg/kg feed) were associated with physiological changes compatible with partial endocrine modulation rather than overt reproductive suppression. This pattern differs from reports using more potent aromatase inhibitors or higher pharmacological doses. Zandi et al. (2019), for example, reported that letrozole administration at 5 mg/kg in laying quail completely suppressed egg production and induced severe ovarian atrophy, with marked reductions in circulating estradiol and concomitant increases in testosterone and progesterone. In contrast, comparable reproductive tract atrophy and cessation of yolk deposition were not evident in the present experiment. This discrepancy may reflect the lower effective exposure, differences between steroidal and nonsteroidal aromatase inhibitors, and species- or tissue-specific compensatory responses. Similarly, de Bournonville et al. (2019) demonstrated site-specific effects of aromatase inhibition on male sexual behavior in Japanese quail, indicating that the magnitude of reproductive response depends on both dose and endpoint. The present study extends these observations to female quail by showing that dietary exemestane exposure can be associated with metabolic and egg-quality changes even when overall egg production is preserved.

The selective effect of exemestane on eggshell percentage, in the absence of a significant treatment effect on shell thickness or absolute shell weight, suggests that dietary exemestane altered the proportional allocation of shell material rather than producing a generalized impairment of shell structure. This observation is consistent with the established role of estrogen-sensitive pathways in regulating calcium metabolism and shell gland function. Estrogen promotes duodenal calcium absorption, mobilization of calcium from medullary bone reserves, and activation of carbonic anhydrase in the shell gland, processes that are essential for calcium carbonate deposition during eggshell formation (Wistedt et al., 2014). Therefore, partial suppression of estrogen synthesis may reduce the efficiency of calcium-related shell deposition or the proportional contribution of shell to total egg mass. The response becoming significant only at the highest supplementation level (1.5 mg/kg) suggests a threshold-like pattern. Under moderate exposure (0.5-1.0 mg/kg), compensatory homeostatic mechanisms may be sufficient to maintain shell traits, whereas higher exposure may exceed the capacity of these adaptive responses. From a practical poultry perspective, the reduction in eggshell percentage at 1.5 mg/kg is relevant because even subtle deterioration in shell-related traits can affect egg handling, breakage risk, hatchability-related assessments, and marketability. However, because the present study did not measure shell ultrastructure, calcium transporters, or circulating hormones, these interpretations should be viewed as physiological hypotheses requiring targeted validation.

The elevation of AST at the highest exemestane dose suggests possible mild hepatocellular stress or altered hepatic metabolic activity. Nevertheless, ALT activity was not significantly affected, and serum LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, and total cholesterol did not differ significantly among treatments. The serum profile therefore indicates selective changes in HDL cholesterol and AST activity rather than a generalized biochemical disturbance. The HDL response was non-monotonic, with lower values at 0.5 mg/kg, higher values at 1.0 mg/kg, and intermediate values in the control and 1.5 mg/kg groups. This pattern may reflect interactions among hepatic lipid transport, yolk precursor synthesis, and steroid-sensitive lipoprotein regulation, but this interpretation remains inferential because sex steroids and gonadotropins were not measured. The more pronounced AST and MDA responses observed at 1.5 mg/kg feed may reflect greater exposure at the highest dietary inclusion level. Collectively, these findings are consistent with previous evidence that aromatase inhibitors can influence lipid metabolism, but confirmation of the avian-specific endocrine and hepatic mechanisms will require direct hormonal, enzymatic, and molecular assessment.

Reproductive organ and folliculogenesis findings contrast with the pronounced ovarian and oviduct atrophy reported in studies employing more potent aromatase inhibitors or higher dosage levels. For example, Zandi et al. (2019) documented significant reductions in ovarian weight and follicular development in quail treated with letrozole, effects that were attributed to profound suppression of circulating estradiol. In the present study, the preservation of ovarian and oviduct relative weights and follicular populations suggests that the dietary exemestane levels tested did not produce overt regression of reproductive anatomy. Because direct endocrine data were not collected, this result should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of a specific degree of aromatase inhibition. Rather, it indicates that the measured reproductive structures were maintained under the dietary exposure conditions used here. Possible explanations include incomplete endocrine suppression, compensation through hypothalamic-pituitary feedback, or tissue-specific sensitivity to exemestane exposure, but these alternatives require future confirmation by measuring circulating steroid hormones, gonadotropins, tissue CYP19A1 expression, and aromatase activity.

Elevated MDA concentrations reflect increased oxidative degradation of PUFAs within yolk lipids, thereby compromising both the nutritional quality and structural integrity of the egg. The pronounced elevation observed at the highest exemestane supplementation level suggests that excessive aromatase inhibition may overwhelm endogenous antioxidant defenses in the oviduct and developing ovarian follicle. Estrogens are known to exert antioxidant effects through multiple mechanisms. In particular, the phenolic hydroxyl group of estradiol enables direct scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS), while estrogen-mediated signaling pathways upregulate the expression and activity of key antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase, glutathione peroxidase, and catalase. Collectively, suppression of estrogen synthesis at high exemestane doses may therefore predispose yolk lipids to oxidative damage. In addition, estrogen stabilizes mitochondrial membrane potential and attenuates ROS generation while suppressing pro-oxidant inflammatory signaling pathways (Chainy and Sahoo, 2020). Aromatase inhibition by exemestane reduces circulating and tissue-level estradiol concentrations, thereby weakening these protective mechanisms. Moreover, the elevation of circulating androgens resulting from inhibited aromatization may further exacerbate oxidative stress in certain tissues, as androgens lack the antioxidant properties of estrogens and, under specific physiological conditions, may promote ROS generation (Goss et al., 2004a). These findings are consistent with previous reports linking estrogen deficiency to increased oxidative stress in reproductive and metabolically active tissues. Qasem (2020) reviewed the estrogenic activity of resveratrol and highlighted the critical role of estrogen receptor signaling in maintaining cellular antioxidant capacity, noting that estrogen deficiency is associated with enhanced lipid peroxidation across multiple tissues. Similarly, Dotas et al. (2023) in their investigation of egg yolk fatty acid composition and oxidative stability in laying hens supplemented with aromatic plant essential oils, demonstrated that dietary antioxidants can mitigate lipid peroxidation and improve egg quality. Their findings underscore the importance of antioxidant protection in egg production systems and support the interpretation that the elevated MDA levels observed in the present study reflect reduced antioxidant defense under conditions of estrogen deprivation. Moreover, Goss et al. (2004b) reported increased oxidative stress markers in various tissues of ovariectomized rats subjected to aromatase inhibition, further corroborating the loss of estrogen’s antioxidant and cytoprotective effects.

The observed shifts in yolk fatty acid composition may reflect indirect effects of exemestane on hepatic lipogenesis, fatty acid desaturation, and yolk lipoprotein assembly. Estrogen participates in the regulation of hepatic lipid metabolism in oviparous vertebrates, including processes involved in vitellogenesis and lipid transport (Palmisano and Zhu, 2017; Tramunt et al., 2021). In the present study, exemestane increased oleic and palmitoleic acid proportions while reducing linoleic acid, suggesting that fatty acid partitioning and desaturation-related pathways may have been altered. However, because hepatic expression of lipogenic enzymes, desaturases, elongases, and estrogen-responsive genes was not measured, these mechanistic interpretations should be regarded as plausible explanations rather than demonstrated causal pathways. Collectively, these findings suggest that dietary exemestane influenced the balance among yolk fatty acid classes, particularly by altering the relative proportions of major unsaturated fatty acid.

The nonlinear response of the omega-6 to omega-3 fatty acid ratio provides additional evidence that yolk lipid balance was altered by dietary exemestane. At 0.5 mg/kg, the omega-6 to omega-3 ratio declined, whereas the 1.0 mg/kg group showed a marked increase, and the 1.5 mg/kg group decreased again. Such non-monotonic patterns are common in endocrine-sensitive systems, but in the absence of hormonal and hepatic molecular data, the U-shaped response should be interpreted descriptively rather than as definitive evidence of a specific regulatory mechanism. The nutritional implications of the fatty acid shifts appear to be dose dependent. Changes in linoleic acid and the omega-6 to omega-3 balance may be relevant for breeder nutrition and embryonic development because these fatty acids contribute to membrane structure and bioactive lipid mediator synthesis. Therefore, pronounced alterations in yolk PUFA balance warrant further investigation in studies assessing fertility, hatchability, and embryonic outcomes.

Direct endocrine confirmation of aromatase inhibition was beyond the scope of the present experimental design, as circulating E2, testosterone, LH, and FSH concentrations, tissue aromatase activity, and CYP19A1 expression were not measured. Accordingly, the effects of dietary exemestane were interpreted using an integrated physiological response profile rather than by direct quantification of estrogen suppression. The evaluated endpoints, including reproductive organ development, follicular populations, eggshell traits, serum lipid fractions, hepatic enzyme activity, yolk lipid peroxidation, and yolk fatty acid composition, are closely related to estrogen-sensitive reproductive and metabolic pathways in birds. However, these endpoints do not by themselves establish the magnitude of aromatase inhibition or exclude direct chemical effects of exemestane. Therefore, the observed responses should be considered compatible with, rather than diagnostic of, altered estrogen-sensitive regulation. Future studies incorporating circulating hormone profiles, tissue CYP19A1 expression, aromatase activity, and feed-recovery measurements are warranted to validate the endocrine mechanisms underlying these responses.

Conclusions

This study indicates that dietary exemestane administration is associated with dose-dependent changes in several physiological endpoints plausibly related to estrogen-sensitive regulation in female Japanese quail. Overall egg production was maintained across all treatments, but exemestane supplementation was associated with alterations in eggshell percentage, HDL cholesterol, AST activity, yolk lipid peroxidation, and yolk fatty acid composition. The highest inclusion level tested (1.5 mg/kg feed; approximately 0.36 mg/kg BW/day based on feed intake and initial body weight) produced the most unfavorable response profile, including reduced eggshell percentage, increased AST activity, and markedly elevated yolk MDA concentration. These findings suggest that high dietary exemestane exposure may affect hepatic metabolism, oxidative balance, and egg quality even in the absence of overt reductions in laying performance. Because direct hormonal measurements and tissue aromatase assays were not performed, the results should be interpreted as physiological responses consistent with estrogen-sensitive pathway modulation rather than as direct confirmation of endocrine suppression. Future studies should incorporate circulating estradiol, testosterone, LH, and FSH measurements, tissue CYP19A1 expression, aromatase activity, feed-recovery assays for exemestane stability, and larger biochemical sample sizes to confirm the endocrine basis of these responses and define safe exposure thresholds for avian applications.
