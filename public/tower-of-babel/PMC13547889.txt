Thermal Ecology and Thermoregulatory Behavior of the Invasive Turtle, Trachemys scripta elegans, in the Mediterranean Basin
Authors: Afsar Murat, Çelik Çetin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe success of invasive species, which pose a significant threat to global biodiversity, hinges on their physiological and ecological adaptive capacities to the environments into which they are introduced. Trachemys scripta elegans (T. scripta elegans), recognized as one of the world's 100 most invasive species, has spread across a wide geographic range and exerts considerable pressure on native ecosystems. To investigate this, we studied the thermal ecology of an invasive population of T. scripta elegans in a wetland located in the province of Manisa, in Western Anatolia. Our objective was to assess the species' level of pre‐adaptation to local climatic and habitat conditions and to identify its thermoregulatory strategies. Between March and October 2024, we measured individual body temperatures (Tb) and environmental temperatures (Te) in the study area; concurrently, we recorded data on water temperature (Tw), air temperature (Ta), solar radiation intensity, and wind speed. We also measured preffered body temperatures (Tset) in the laboratory. We found that the thermal quality of the habitat (de) was suboptimal during the species’ primary activity period, thereby presenting a thermally challenging environment. Despite this, the invading population exhibited high thermoregulatory accuracy (db), successfully maintaining its body temperatures in close alignment with its preferred temperature range. This resulted in a high index of thermoregulatory effectiveness (E = 0.79) and a positive thermoregulation index value (I = 3.79 > 0). Furthermore, Pearson correlation analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between Tb and Tw, as well as between Tb and Ta. Our results indicate that the invasive T. scripta elegans population prefers an effective thermoregulation option rather than being thermoconformist. This high thermoregulatory capability represents a key pre‐adaptation, enabling the species to spread successfully even in thermally suboptimal habitats and providing a competitive advantage over native species. The findings from our study support projections that in the face of a warming climate, thermal stress tolerance and behavioral thermoregulation can facilitate the expansion of invasive species.Keywords: body temperature, climate change, conservation biology and environmental management, habitat thermal quality, thermoregulation indices, thermoregulatory accuracy

Introduction

Freshwater turtles are key vertebrates in the functioning of aquatic ecosystems (Lovich et al. 2018). However, in recent decades, this long‐lived group has faced increasing pressure from accelerating climate change, habitat fragmentation, and biological invasions (Stanford et al. 2020). The red‐eared slider (Trachemys scripta elegans), a species native to the southern United States, has proven remarkably successful against these threats. Consequently, through unwanted releases, it has spread to numerous countries across Asia, Europe, Australia, and Africa, becoming notable for its high invasive capacity (Iverson 1992; Ernst et al. 1994; Newberry 1984; Uchida 1989; Da Silva and Blasco 1995; Ota 1995; Luiselli et al. 1997; Chen and Lue 1998; Cox et al. 1998; Cadi and Joly 2004; Yakın et al. 2024). Indeed, the species is listed among the 100 of the world's worst invasive species (Lowe et al. 2000).

In ectotherms, body temperature (Tb) is the fundamental factor governing the rate and limits of metabolic processes, thereby influencing vital functions such as digestion (Parmenter 1980), locomotion (Claussen et al. 2002), growth (Frazer et al. 1993), predator‐avoidance (Weetman et al. 1998), habitat selection (Dubois et al. 2009), and competition (Cadi and Joly 2003). This central role of Tb necessitates behavioral thermoregulation, especially considering the limited physiological control in most ectotherms (Weathers and White 1971; Seebacher and Franklin 2005). Consequently, individuals strive to operate within a narrow temperature range that optimizes their performance (Avery 1982; Huey 1982; Angilletta et al. 2002b). The close link between behavioral thermoregulation and the thermal sensitivity of performance has supported the idea of their co‐evolution (Huey and Bennett 1987; Angilletta 2006); selected body temperatures (Tb) directly influence fitness via physiological performance (Huey and Bennett 1987).

In reptiles, the most frequently observed thermoregulatory mechanism is allocating time to aerial basking to elevate body temperatures (Pough et al. 1998; Cadi and Joly 2003; Lillywhite 2014). A specific challenge for aquatic turtles arises from the high thermal conductivity of water, which causes their Tb to rapidly equilibrate with the ambient water temperature after basking (Ben‐Ezra et al. 2008). As time spent in the water column for foraging or mating increases, the thermal advantage gained from basking rapidly diminishes; thus, individuals are expected to prefer warmer microhabitats during diurnal activities (Picard et al. 2011). Therefore, the environmental thermal regime shapes not only instantaneous behaviors but also habitat use, foraging strategies (Brow and Brooks 1991; Nieuwolt 1996), and even population structure and geographic distribution (Litzgus et al. 1999; Ewert et al. 2005). In amphibians and reptiles, which exchange heat via radiation, conduction, and convection, basking is considered the primary thermoregulatory behavior (Zug et al. 2001; Auth 1975; Boyer 1965; Gibbons 1990).

Reptiles exhibit a continuum of strategies from thermoconformity to precise thermoregulation: in some species, Tb passively conforms to the environment (Huey 1982; Manning and Grigg 1997; Souza and Martins 2006; Ibargüengoytía et al. 2010), while in others, it is actively regulated within a narrow range (Huey 1982; Edwards and Blouin‐Demers 2007; Hill et al. 2012). On this continuum, species can be eurytherms, tolerant of a wide temperature range, or stenotherms, requiring narrow Tb ranges to maintain performance (Huey 1982; Hill et al. 2012). While basking is the most common method of elevating Tb (Pough et al. 1998; Cadi and Joly 2003; Lillywhite 2014), thermoregulation entails costs as well as benefits. Factors such as sacrificing other behaviors, increased predator exposure, and elevated metabolic demands can reduce the net gain of thermoregulation (Huey and Slatkin 1976). Consequently, dynamic trade‐offs arise between a turtle's thermal “goals” and other ecological imperatives such as foraging, mating, and predator avoidance (Crawford et al. 1983).

Turtles evolved under different thermal constraints compared to lizards and snakes. Because aquatic turtles are exposed to higher rates of heat loss via conduction (Ernst and Barbour 1989; Fitzgerald and Nelson 2011), they may have evolved divergent behavioral and physiological strategies. Dominant frameworks for reptile thermal ecology, based largely on data from lizards and snakes (see Angilletta et al. 2002), may not fully reflect thermal decision‐making in turtles. Indeed, Trachemys scripta elegans individuals spend a considerable portion of the day basking, using solar radiation as the primary means of elevating Tb above the water (Boyer 1965; Moll and Legler 1971). Within this framework, disentangling the environment–behavior–physiology interaction is critical for understanding the species' thermal ecology (Huey 1982; Hertz et al. 1993).

In the current study, we examined the thermal ecology of a naturalized, dense population of Trachemys scripta elegans that has invaded a wetland in the province of Manisa (Western Anatolia). We identified the thermoregulation strategy for pre‐adaptation to this wetland and its environmental conditions, along with the species' local thermal requirements. We determined whether the species actively thermoregulates, estimated its thermoregulatory accuracy and effectiveness, and evaluated the thermal quality of the occupied habitat. This study thus provides the first thermal ecology data for this species from a densely invaded region in Turkey exhibiting a typical Mediterranean climate.

Materials and Methods

Study Species

The current study focused on an invasive population of Trachemys scripta elegans (Thunberg in Schoepff, 1792), an aquatic turtle in the family Emydidae. Native to the southeastern USA and northeastern Mexico, this species has become a globally significant invader as a result of the intense exotic pet trade, particularly in Mediterranean countries (Luiselli et al. 1997; Chen and Lue 1998; Pleguezuelos et al. 2002). Rödder et al. (2009) modeled the potential distribution of T. scripta elegans, demonstrating large suitable habitats across Central and South America; Europe; West and Central Africa; the coasts of East Africa; East Asia; eastern and western Australia; and Turkey. Its invasive success, evidenced by its colonization of every continent except Antarctica, is attributed to its high adaptive capacity (Gibbons 1990). Consequently, it has been emphasized that this species can have significant impacts on ecosystems, even causing the displacement of native fauna (Cadi and Joly 2003).

Fieldwork

We conducted fieldwork in a 7.4‐hectare wetland densely invaded by T. scripta elegans, located in a region exhibiting the characteristic features of a Mediterranean climate (Figure 1). According to the Köppen‐Geiger classification, the region experiences a Csa‐type Mediterranean climate, marked by hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters (Peel et al. 2007). This wetland, situated in a narrow valley (100–240 m elevation) and fed by the nearby Bozköy Stream, features shorelines intermittently shaded by earthen banks, thorny shrubs, oleander, and pine trees, with some areas supporting willow (Salix spp.) trees. Aquatic vegetation is sparse. The significance of this site, located within the protected Mesir Nature Park at the foothills of Mount Spil, is underscored by its co‐occupation by the native turtle species, Mauremys rivulata (Ayaz et al. 2008b).

Location of study area in Western Anatolia and a view of the fieldwork site.

Field Measurements

Fieldwork was conducted between March 2024 and October 2024. Upon capturing (via fishing net) a turtle, the “capture–immediate measurement” technique (Taylor et al. 2021) was employed to measure body temperature (Tb) via cloacal insertion (1–10 mm) using a fast‐reading thermocouple (Hanna Checktemp 1 Digital Thermometer; 0.1°C resolution), and values were recorded within 10 s (Barroso et al. 2016). Subsequently, body size (plastron and carapace length, plastron and carapace width, and plastron height) was measured with calipers to the nearest 0.1 mm, and body mass was measured with a digital scale to the nearest 0.5 g. Concurrently, the water temperature (Tw) of the microhabitat at the exact point of capture was measured using the same thermocouple. Air temperature (Ta) at the time of capture was measured at a height of 1–3 cm above the immediate capture substrate (e.g., water surface, ground, or basking log) using a Uni‐T UT‐320D Mini Thermometer (Ortega et al. 2016). Solar radiation was recorded with an Apogee MP‐200 pyranometer (Crawford et al. 1983), while wind speed was measured using a Skywatch Explorer 1 anemometer (Dodd 2016). Wind speed was also measured at a height corresponding to the turtle's position (1–10 cm above the ground) to ensure the collection of ecologically relevant data (Crawford et al. 1983).

Thermoregulation Analyzes

To characterize the thermoregulation of T. scripta elegans, we calculated the indices and variables commonly used to describe thermoregulation in free‐ranging animals (Hertz et al. 1993; Taylor et al. 2021). We conducted all statistical analyzes in PYTHON using the NumPy (Gupta and Bagchi 2024), Pandas (Bernard 2016), and Matplotlib libraries (Barrett et al. 2005).

Preferred Body Temperature (Tset) and Thermoregulatory Accuracy (db)

We determined the preferred body temperature (Tset) in a laboratory thermal gradient, an environment devoid of the costs of thermoregulation (Huey and Slatkin 1976; Hertz et al. 1993). We established this gradient (15°C–35°C) in a four‐chamber tank design, allowing turtles to move freely in water at a depth of 14 cm. We measured the turtles’ body temperatures every 10 min for a 60‐h period using a HOBO MX2202 4‐Channel Data Logger equipped with four thermocouple probes. Following a 12‐h acclimation period (data excluded), we used the subsequent 48‐h portion of the temperature data for analysis. We defined the Tset range as the interquartile range (25th–75th percentiles) of the obtained Tb values and the point Tset as the mean of these values (Nutting and Graham 1993; Edwards and Blouin‐Demers 2007).

Building upon this, we calculated thermoregulatory accuracy (db) as the absolute difference between field‐active body temperatures (Tb) and the point Tset (Hertz et al. 1993). Low db values represent high thermoregulatory accuracy (field Tb is near Tset), whereas high db values represent low accuracy (field Tb deviates significantly from Tset) (Taylor et al. 2021).

Operative Temperature (Te) and Habitat Thermal Quality (de)

We measured operative temperature (Te) using physical operative temperature models (OTMs) designed to mimic the thermal properties of the turtles (Mizsei et al. 2025; Bakken 1980). Our OTMs consisted of hollow galvanized boxes (15 × 10 × 5 cm), painted to match the turtle's coloration, and fitted with an internal thermocouple, which was sealed airtight with silicone. We connected these OTMs to data loggers (HOBO MX2205) and placed them in locations most frequently used by the turtles for basking, ensuring a mix of partially shaded and full‐sun positions. The loggers were set to record temperatures every 15 min for 120 h (Blouin‐Demers and Weatherhead 2001; Hertz et al. 1993).

We then used these Te data to calculate the thermal quality of the environment (de), defined as the absolute difference between Te and Tset (Hertz et al. 1993). Therefore, high de values represent poor thermal quality (available temperatures are far from Tset), whereas de values close to zero represent good thermal quality, allowing animals to thermoregulate near their Tset (Taylor et al. 2021).

Thermoregulation Indices

We calculated two primary indices. First, the index of thermoregulation effectiveness, E = 1 – (mean db/mean de), where a value close to zero indicates thermoconformity, and high values indicate active thermoregulation (Hertz et al. 1993). Second, the thermoregulation index, I = de – db, where a value of 0 indicates thermoconformity, positive values indicate active thermoregulation, and negative values suggest avoidance of thermally suitable habitats (Blouin‐Demers and Weatherhead 2001).

Results

The mean field‐active body temperature (Tb) of the turtles was 24.9°C ± 5.7 with a range of 11.6°C to 35.4°C. This was recorded concurrently with a mean water temperature (Tw) of 22.7°C (min = 11.5°C, max = 32.5°C) and a mean air temperature (Ta) of 29.3°C (min = 8.5°C, max = 42.0°C).

A Pearson correlation analysis revealed strong positive correlations among all thermal variables (Figure 2). We identified the strongest relationship between body temperature (Tb) and water temperature (Tw) (r = 0.791, p < 0.001). A similarly strong correlation was observed between Tb and air temperature (Ta) (r = 0.781, p < 0.001). Furthermore, Ta and Tw were also highly correlated (r = 0.778, p < 0.001), indicating that water and air temperature are primary factors determining the turtles’ body temperature.

Data obtained from the results of the Pearson correlation analysis of the Tb, Ta, and Tw variables.

Trachemys scripta elegans individuals in the study area were predominantly diurnal, with activities observed in both aquatic and terrestrial microhabitats. To determine whether the population tended towards thermoregulation or thermoconformity, we evaluated their thermoregulatory accuracy (db).

Following the protocol of Hertz et al. (1993), we calculated the mean thermoregulatory accuracy (db)—defined as the mean deviation of field body temperatures (Tb) from the preferred temperature range (Tset). Our analysis revealed a db of 1.0085 (Table 1, Figure 3). This means that, on average, the body temperatures of the T. scripta elegans population in the study area deviated from the preferred temperature range (Tset) by only 1.0085°C.

An analysis of the 148 individual Tb measurements demonstrated that the majority (57.4%) fell within the Tset range. Only 19.6% were recorded below this range, and 23% were above it. This distribution, combined with the low mean deviation (db), strongly supports the conclusion that these turtles were not passive thermoconformers; rather, they actively regulate their body temperatures.

We assessed the thermal quality of the habitat (de), calculated as the mean deviation of operative temperatures (Te) from the preferred Tset range. An analysis of 1,324 Te measurements revealed that only 37.9% of available temperatures fell within the Tset range; 25.2% were below this range, and 36.9% were above it. This distribution yielded a de value of 4.8011 (Table 2, Figure 3).

This value, being far from zero, indicates that the habitat's thermal quality is challenging for T. scripta elegans. Consequently, to maintain their body temperatures within the ideal range, individuals must actively engage in thermoregulatory behaviors, such as shuttling between basking sites and cool water or shaded areas.

We assessed thermoregulation effectiveness using two separate indices. The effectiveness index (E) (Hertz et al. 1993) yielded a high value (E = 1 – (db/de) = 1 – (1.0085/4.8011 = 0.79). This result, approaching 1.0, indicates that the turtles were highly effective thermoregulators.

Moreover, the thermoregulation index (I) (Blouin‐Demers and Weatherhead 2001) was strongly positive (I = de – db = 4.8011 – 1.0085 = 3.7926), which confirms that the animals actively thermoregulate. The positive results from both calculations demonstrate that T. scripta elegans individuals in the study area made a relatively high investment in thermoregulation (Blouin‐Demers and Weatherhead 2001; Edwards and Blouin‐Demers 2007).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that the preferred body temperature (Tset) range for T. scripta elegans in this Mediterranean wetland is 19.2°C–28.7°C. These findings are comparable to ranges reported for populations in the United States (e.g., 18°C–30°C, Cagle 1946; 16.8°C–32.1°C, Nutting and Graham 1993). Although our findings are largely consistent with this literature, variation in Tset is known to be influenced by factors such as life stage, sex, body size, and basking behavior (Grayson and Dorcas 2004; Bulté and Blouin‐Demers 2010; Maia‐Carneiro and Rocha 2013). Consequently, the observed variation across regions likely reflects local adaptation. This view is supported by Spotila et al. (1984), who found that T. scripta in a thermally polluted river adopted an opportunistic thermoregulatory strategy, using aquatic basking and shuttling between warm surface and cool bottom water to maintain a Tset of 26°C–34°C without resorting to atmospheric basking.

The high thermoregulatory accuracy (db = 1.0085) we observed indicates a high degree of precision. This ability to maintain Tb close to its Tset range likely allows the invader to sustain high physiological performance within the invaded habitat, a finding consistent with research on other reptile species (Mizsei et al. 2025).

Rather than behaving like the thermoconforming populations in Panama (Moll and Legler 1971) or adopting the opportunistic strategies observed by Spotila et al. (1984), the individuals in our study area preferentially engaged in atmospheric basking. The calculated habitat thermal quality (de) of 4.80 is indicative of this behavior; a value this far from zero signifies a thermally challenging environment. However, through its high thermoregulation effectiveness, the invasive T. scripta demonstrates it can successfully reach its target temperatures by exploiting the available thermal environment. Nonetheless, atmospheric thermoregulation in aquatic turtles entails costs, such as energy expenditure and predation risk (Huey 1974; Huey and Slatkin 1976). While T. scripta faces numerous predators in its native range, a released adult in this novel wetland has little to fear from predation due to its size, powerful bite, and thick shell, and it is not consumed as a food source in Turkey.

Our analysis, following the methodology of Hertz et al. (1993), yielded a thermoregulation effectiveness index (E) of 0.79. This value, approaching 1.0, strongly indicates the invasive species is a highly effective thermoregulator. This conclusion is further corroborated by the thermoregulation index (I) (Blouin‐Demers & Weatherhead 2001), which resulted in a strongly positive value (I = 3.7926 > 0), confirming active and effective thermoregulation.

In our measurements, Tb was generally lower than Ta, a phenomenon that may result from thermoregulatory behavior to avoid overheating in full sunlight. Our Pearson correlation analysis detected the strongest relationship between Tb and water temperature (Tw), a finding consistent with Souza and Martins (2006). The literature confirms that basking activity and, consequently,Tbs are influenced by complex interactions between air and water temperatures (Auth 1975; Spotila et al. 1984). Indeed, T. scripta is known to thermoregulate through vertical movements within the water column in response to Tw (Moll and Legler 1971).

Building upon the understanding that T. scripta competes with and may displace native aquatic turtles on the Iberian Peninsula, such as Emys orbicularis and Mauremys leprosa (Polo‐Cavia et al. 2009b), our findings have direct conservation implications. Polo‐Cavia et al. (2009b) demonstrated that T. scripta's advantages include aggression and dominance over food and basking sites. Our own finding—that this population relies heavily on atmospheric basking—leads to the prediction of intense competition for these specific sites with the native turtle in our area, M. rivulata. Indeed, we observed in the present study that T. scripta was more aggressive and dominant than the native M. rivulata in competition for basking sites, giving it a clear advantage. This competitive superiority could facilitate the spread of this alien species to the detriment of the native population. At the population scale, this ongoing displacement could lead to reduced reproductive success and lower survival rates for M. rivulata, potentially triggering local population declines or extirpations. Furthermore, the monopolization of optimal basking sites and resources by T. scripta may alter the spatial distribution patterns of native turtles, thereby disrupting the overall community dynamics and energy flow within the wetland ecosystem.

Global climate change and biological invasions pose interacting threats to global biodiversity (Sorte et al. 2013; Padilla et al. 2023). Climate change may favor invasive species if they possess higher physiological performance or thermal stress resistance than native species (Bates et al. 2013; Penk et al. 2016). Due to such differential resistance, native species may reach their physiological limits sooner, especially during extreme climatic events (Diez et al. 2012), leading to shifts in community composition (Zerebecki and Sorte 2011). These dynamics appear relevant to T. scripta; Zhang et al. (2023) stated that T. scripta exhibited higher resistance to thermal stress compared to two native turtle species, suggesting a warming climate favors the invader. Our study reveals that even though the thermal quality of the habitat was suboptimal, the invasive Trachemys scripta elegans overcame this disadvantage through active thermoregulation. This ability to behaviorally mitigate a thermally challenging environment suggests active thermoregulation is a key trait that may enhance its resilience and invasive success in the face of climate change.

In Anatolia, it is crucial to monitor the competitive interactions between this invasive turtle and the native Anatolian turtles—Emys orbicularis (European pond turtle), Mauremys rivulata (Balkan terrapin), and Mauremys capsica (Caspian terrapin)—and the resulting consequences for these native populations. Competitive interactions between T. scripta and the European pond turtle (Emys orbicularis) are particularly important because Emys orbicularis has been reported as an endangered species Ayaz et al. (2007, 2008a, 2008b) as T. scripta spreads into natural habitats of high ecological value, it is also vital to monitor its impacts on the broader local fauna. Furthermore, future long‐term studies in thermal ecology will provide a more comprehensive understanding of how seasonal changes, life‐history traits, and ecological interactions affect the thermal ecology of both the invasive T. scripta and co‐occurring native species.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that the invasive T. scripta population in this wetland, rather than being a passive thermoconformer, is able to maintain its performance through highly effective thermoregulation using behavioral mechanisms. This high thermoregulatory capability can be considered a key pre‐adaptation that enables the species to spread successfully even in thermally suboptimal habitats, providing a competitive advantage over native species. The findings from our study support projections that in the face of a warming climate, thermal stress tolerance and behavioral thermoregulation can facilitate the expansion of invasive species.
