Bilateral rhinoliths causing long-standing nasal obstruction: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Mohammedsefa Arusi Dari, Sara Abdulkadir Memhur, Zelalem Tadesse Wondimu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRhinoliths are uncommon calcified masses in the nasal cavity, typically unilateral. Bilateral rhinolithiasis is extremely rare—with fewer than 10 bilateral cases documented in the literature—and can present with chronic nasal obstruction and significant diagnostic challenges.Case presentationA 55-year-old Ethiopian man presented with a 10-year history of bilateral nasal obstruction, foul-smelling discharge, epistaxis, facial heaviness, and headache, previously treated as chronic rhinosinusitis with only temporary relief. Background

A rhinolith (derived from the Greek rhino, meaning nose, and lithos, meaning stone) is a rare nasal finding. It consists of a calcareous concretion formed when salts deposit around a foreign body within the nasal cavity. This foreign body, which may enter the nose either accidentally or incidentally, acts as a nucleus around which mineral encrustation occurs [1]. Nasal foreign bodies can be classified as either endogenous (originating within the body) or exogenous (introduced from outside) [2].

The precise cause of rhinoliths remains unclear. They can, however, develop around endogenous substances such as dental fragments or shed epithelial cells, as well as exogenous objects like plastic pieces, fruit seeds, batteries, or stones [3]. Interestingly, a noticeably higher number of individuals under the age of 40 are diagnosed with this condition when compared with those over 40 years old [4].

Rhinoliths can present with symptoms such as headache, loss of smell, nasal obstruction, nasal discharge, swelling, foul nasal odor, bad breath, nosebleeds, localized pain, and fever. These symptoms are observed in roughly 1 in 10,000 patients seeking Ear, Nose, Throat care and can last for months or even years [5].

Bilateral rhinolithiasis is exceptionally rare, with fewer than 10 cases documented in the English-language literature to date. The majority of reported bilateral cases have occurred in females, and complications such as septal perforation have been described in some instances. Each additional case report contributes to understanding the pathogenesis and clinical spectrum of this uncommon condition. Due to the rarity of bilateral rhinoliths, we present our clinical experience and successful treatment of a bilateral rhinolith in a 55-year-old patient. This case is particularly noteworthy as it occurred in an Ethiopian man, demonstrated an unusually prolonged 10-year course without complications despite large stone size, and showed predominant calcium oxalate composition on analysis—adding valuable epidemiological and clinical data to the existing literature. This case is unique in several aspects: it represents one of the very few reported bilateral rhinolithiasis cases in Sub-Saharan Africa, demonstrates an unusually prolonged 10-year clinical course without complications despite large stone size (3 × 4 cm), and shows predominant calcium oxalate composition—features that remain rarely described in the existing literature.

Case presentation

A 55-year-old Ethiopian man presented to the ENT outpatient department with a 10-year history of persistent bilateral nasal obstruction, associated with foul-smelling nasal discharge, episodic epistaxis (occurring 2–3 times monthly, self-limited), facial heaviness, and bifrontal headache. He denied intermittent sneezing, facial pain, anosmia, fever, or other systemic symptoms. Over the years, he had received multiple courses of antibiotics, intranasal steroids, and nasal decongestants for presumed chronic rhinosinusitis, which provided only transient relief. There was no history of nasal trauma, prior nasal surgery, or remembered foreign body insertion during childhood or adulthood. His past medical history was unremarkable for diabetes, hypertension, or allergic disorders, and he took no regular medications. The patient was a farmer from a rural area, married with children, with no significant family history of ENT disorders. He denied tobacco or alcohol use.

Clinical examination

On examination, the patient was afebrile with stable vital signs (blood pressure 120/80 mmHg, pulse 76 bpm, respiratory rate 16/min). Systemic examination was otherwise unremarkable. Anterior rhinoscopy revealed bilateral whitish, hard masses, partially obscured by mucopurulent discharge. The nasal septum was midline, with mild mucosal edema noted. Diagnostic nasal endoscopy following topical decongestion and anesthesia identified stony, irregular masses along the nasal floor bilaterally, with posterior extension into the choana and nasopharynx (Fig. 1). Intraoperatively, the anterior left nasal cavity demonstrated a stony-hard mass beneath the inferior turbinate. The mucosa overlying the masses appeared inflamed but intact, with no ulceration, bleeding, or granulation tissue. Diagnostic assessment and challenges

The 10-year delay in diagnosis in this case likely resulted from several factors: (1) the nonspecific nature of symptoms mimicking chronic rhinosinusitis, (2) bilateral presentation which diverted clinical suspicion away from foreign body-related pathology (typically unilateral), (3) limited access to diagnostic nasal endoscopy and CT imaging in primary care settings, and (4) repeated empirical treatment for presumed sinusitis without definitive investigation. In resource-limited settings, such delays are not uncommon, underscoring the importance of early specialist referral for persistent unilateral or bilateral nasal symptoms with malodor.

Imaging

Non-contrast CT of the paranasal sinuses revealed well-defined hyperdense masses within both nasal cavities, extending into the nasopharynx (Fig. 2). There was no evidence of bony erosion, sinus expansion, remodeling, or involvement of adjacent sinuses or orbital structures. Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for calcified sinonasal masses includes osteoma (typically arising from sinus walls with bony attachment), odontoma (associated with dental structures), calcified polyps (usually have associated soft tissue component), fungal concretions (often with sinus opacification and characteristic microcalcifications), ossifying fibroma (expansile with characteristic ground-glass matrix), and malignant neoplasms with calcification (accompanied by destructive features). The clinical and CT findings favored rhinolithiasis based on: (1) typical location along the nasal floor, (2) intranasal origin without sinus wall attachment, (3) absence of bony destruction or remodeling, (4) lack of associated soft tissue mass, and (5) bilateral symmetrical distribution without sinus involvement.

Management

A diagnosis of bilateral rhinolithiasis was established. The patient underwent endoscopic surgical removal under general anesthesia with preoperative nasal preparation using topical oxymetazoline. The rhinoliths were brittle, stony-hard, and measured approximately 3 × 4 cm each. Using a 0° rigid endoscope, Blakesley forceps, and suction, the stones were carefully fragmented and extracted in pieces (en bloc removal was not possible due to their large size and the narrow nasal passages). The underlying mucosa was inflamed but intact, without necrosis or granulation tissue. Total procedure time was 65 min, with minimal blood loss (< 50 mL).

Histopathology and analysis

Histopathological examination confirmed laminated mineralized structures with pigmented cores, consistent with rhinoliths. Chemical analysis using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy revealed that the masses were predominantly composed of calcium oxalate (approximately 85%), with smaller amounts of calcium phosphate and magnesium.

Outcome and follow-up

Postoperatively, the patient experienced immediate relief of nasal obstruction and resolution of malodor. He was discharged the following day with instructions for nasal saline irrigation, a 7-day course of oral amoxicillin–clavulanate, and analgesics as needed. At 6-month follow-up, nasal endoscopy demonstrated healthy mucosa with complete mucosal healing and no evidence of recurrence. The patient remained completely asymptomatic and expressed satisfaction with the outcome.

Discussion

Rhinolithiasis was first described by Thomas Bartholin, a Danish physician, in the seventeenth century [6, 7]. The cause of rhinolithiasis is not well understood, but it may arise from a foreign body serving as a nidus. This can trigger chronic inflammation and the accumulation of mineral salts, ultimately resulting in stone formation [8].

Rhinoliths develop gradually and often produce symptoms slowly over time. The most frequent clinical features include nasal obstruction and foul-smelling nasal discharge, while other possible signs are cacosmia, nosebleeds, headache, facial pain, and excessive tearing [9]. Our patient presented with persistent bilateral nasal blockage, accompanied by malodorous discharge, epistaxis, facial pressure, and headache.

Several studies suggest that rhinoliths may be more common in females and in individuals from rural areas, indicating these factors could increase susceptibility. For instance, Kharoubi [10] reviewed 20 patients over 17 years, finding that 55% were female, while Benhoummad et al. [11] reported 16 cases, 68% of whom were female. Cases of bilateral rhinolithiasis, however, are extremely uncommon. In the series by both Kharoubi [10] and Benhoummad et al. [11], only one bilateral case was reported in each, and additional isolated instances documented by Yaşar and Öner [12], Subbanna et al. [13], Sadri [14], and Zafar et al. Synthesis of bilateral rhinolithiasis literature

The review of the limited bilateral cases reveals several patterns. The age range for bilateral rhinolithiasis spans from young adults to the elderly, and review of previously reported bilateral rhinolithiasis cases demonstrates a clear female predominance, with approximately 5 of 6 reported cases occurring in female patients. Presenting symptoms consistently include chronic nasal obstruction and fetid discharge, with symptom duration ranging from several months to decades—our patient's 10-year history being among the longest documented. Complications reported in bilateral cases include septal perforation [10, 12] and choanal obstruction [14]; however, our case is remarkable for the complete absence of such complications despite prolonged duration and large stone size. Treatment in all reported cases was surgical removal, with endoscopic approaches predominating and universally favorable outcomes without recurrence, supporting endoscopic removal as the standard of care.

The formation of rhinoliths is thought to result from local inflammatory reactions around the foreign body, leading to the gradual deposition of salts such as calcium phosphate, magnesium, calcium carbonate, and aluminum. This process hardens the mass and allows it to enlarge over time [3]. Histopathological examination in our case showed laminated mineralized structures with pigmented cores, characteristic of rhinoliths, while chemical analysis indicated that the stones were primarily composed of calcium oxalate—a finding less commonly reported than calcium phosphate predominance, possibly reflecting dietary or metabolic factors.

Nasal foreign bodies are most commonly found on the nasal floor, near the junction of the anterior and posterior nares, likely because they normally migrate posteriorly from an initially anterior position [4]. In our patient, the foreign body was located on the nasal floor and grew upward, consistent with this typical pattern.

For diagnosis, nasal endoscopy allows direct visualization of the lesion, while CT scans provide detailed information about its size, density, extent, and relation to adjacent structures [13]. The diagnostic pathway in our case—proceeding from clinical suspicion to endoscopic confirmation and CT characterization—illustrates the optimal approach to evaluating such patients.

The differential diagnosis for calcified sinonasal masses includes osteoma, odontoma, calcified polyps, nasal glioma, enchondroma, hemangioma, ossifying fibroma, calcifying angiofibroma, septal desmoid tumors, malignant neoplasms, as well as infectious causes such as syphilis and calcified tuberculomas [12]. The distinction from these entities is typically possible through combined endoscopic and imaging findings, as demonstrated in our case.

Although rhinoliths can lead to complications such as destruction of the nasal mucosa, palatal perforation, recurrent sinusitis, chronic middle ear inflammation, maxillary bone erosion, fistula formation, and septal deviation or perforation [2, 9], no such complications were observed in our case despite the long-standing nature of the condition. This may relate to the location of the stones along the nasal floor without lateral pressure against the septum, the absence of sharp irregular edges, or host factors limiting inflammatory bone resorption.

Treatment of rhinolithiasis involves removal of the stone using endoscopic guidance with a rigid endoscope. The procedure can be carried out under either local or general anesthesia, depending on the medical team’s approach and stone characteristics [11]. In our case, general anesthesia facilitated comfortable extraction of large bilateral stones with adequate airway protection.

Clinical learning points

This case underscores several important lessons for clinicians. First, rhinolithiasis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of chronic nasal obstruction with foul discharge, even when symptoms are bilateral. Second, failure of empirical medical therapy for presumed chronic rhinosinusitis should prompt early referral for nasal endoscopy. Third, CT imaging is invaluable for confirming diagnosis, assessing extent, and excluding complications or alternative pathologies. Fourth, prolonged duration does not necessarily predict complications, as demonstrated by our patient's 10-year symptom history without mucosal erosion or septal perforation. Finally, endoscopic removal is safe, effective, and associated with excellent outcomes and minimal recurrence risk.

Conclusion

Bilateral rhinolithiasis is an exceptionally rare condition that can remain undiagnosed for years because of its slow and subtle progression, nonspecific symptoms, and clinical mimicry of chronic rhinosinusitis. Persistent nasal obstruction with foul-smelling discharge should raise suspicion, particularly when symptoms are longstanding, unilateral, or fail to respond to medical therapy—though bilateral presentation does not exclude the diagnosis. Nasal endoscopy and CT imaging play a key role in establishing the diagnosis and defining the extent of disease. Endoscopic removal under general or local anesthesia remains the treatment of choice and is both safe and effective, with excellent outcomes and no reported recurrence after complete extraction. This case contributes to the limited literature on bilateral rhinolithiasis by documenting an unusual occurrence in an Ethiopian man, a prolonged 10-year course without complications, large stone size, predominant calcium oxalate composition, and successful endoscopic management. The key take-home message for clinicians is to maintain a high index of suspicion for rhinoliths in patients with chronic, malodorous nasal obstruction unresponsive to medical therapy, and to pursue early endoscopic evaluation to prevent prolonged morbidity and diagnostic delay.
