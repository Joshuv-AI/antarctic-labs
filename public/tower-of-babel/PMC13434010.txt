Stabilizing stiffness is the most limiting factor in human force exertion
Authors: F. Tessari, N. Hogan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Humans have a remarkable ability to manage physical interaction despite a complex musculoskeletal system, slow muscles and substantial neural delays. Most physical interaction tasks require force generation, and that can be influenced by body configuration and task stability. In this work we investigate the human limits of force generation with respect to these factors. We devised an experiment in which healthy individuals performed a maximum voluntary pushing task using a custom-designed gimbal apparatus which allowed us to change the ability to transmit torque at the wrist. The experiment was repeated in two different arm positions to assess the effect of limb configuration. The results showed a substantial decrease in maximum force with less wrist ability to generate stabilizing stiffness, and minimal difference in maximum pushing force between the two arm configurations. Our findings confirm our hypothesis that stiffness production appears to be a prominent factor limiting force exertion.

Introduction

Humans are remarkably good at managing physical interaction despite: (i) a complex musculoskeletal system with more than 200 degrees of freedom and 600+ muscles, (ii) slow neural communication (~\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${10}^{2}$$\end{document}102 m/s), (iii) substantial transmission delays (~100–200 ms), and (iv) limited muscle control bandwidth (łe 10≤10 Hz)1,2.

Most physical interaction tasks require the production of force. Imagine, for example, hammering a nail, opening a bottle, or holding a mug in your hand. However, we often overlook the fact that force production can be compromised by both (i) the mechanical stability of the task, and (ii) the body configuration (posture). Two simple examples are pushing a screwdriver or bench pressing with free weights. In both cases, the generation of force also produces a destabilizing action which has the tendency to push the limbs away from their nominal posture. Small deviation around the direction of force generation – when pushing a screw driver or lifting a dumbbell – will generate a de-stabilizing force which, if not compensated, will tend to increase the deviation, leading to catastrophic failure. Moreover, the way you hold the screwdriver or the bench press bar also matters. In fact, there are some postures that are considered more preferable than others. These are sometimes known as ‘correct forms’ by trainers (in the gym) or skilled workers (in the screwdriver case). Humans can compensate for the destabilizing effect of force production by generating mechanical impedance, and specifically stiffness3. Several studies have investigated the ability of humans to generate and regulate impedance, and particularly stiffness, during physical interaction4–16. Note that in this work we always refer to mechanical impedance, therefore we frequently omit the ‘mechanical’ prefix.

Existing experimental results have quantified the human ability to tune muscle, joint, and end-effector impedance to maintain stability during physical interaction in both perturbed10,17 and unperturbed16 conditions. In a previous work from our group16, we showed how healthy individuals are capable of predictively regulating their arm end-effector stiffness to succeed in a challenging physical interaction task. Moreover, we also observed that subjects tended to use the minimum allowable stiffness to do so. Note, however, that not all physical interaction tasks are intrinsically destabilizing. Simple examples include holding a mug of coffee or pushing against a flat wall.

In terms of posture, we know that arm pose and joint configuration play a key role in transmitting – for example – muscle tension to hand force. Moreover, humans appear to exhibit a preference for certain poses over others. In fact, most upper-limb manipulation tasks are preferentially performed about a half meter below the shoulder and about a half meter in front of the sternum. This suggests a “motor fovea” (by analogy with the visual fovea18) where dexterity and manipulability are maximized. However, we also know that the redundancy of the human musculoskeletal system allows for multiple solutions to emerge for the same motor task with equal performance19–21.

In this context, two experimentally unanswered questions arise: (i) Is the human ability to generate force limited by the ability to stabilize posture? (ii) How critical is the posture chosen to perform a force-exertion task? Does deviation from a preferred posture affect the ability to recruit muscles and limit the ability to exert force? In simpler words, what are the factors limiting force generation?

To address these questions, we designed an experiment in which healthy individuals were asked to perform a simple pushing task with maximum effort. They pushed on an experimental apparatus that resembled a hand tool but included a lockable gimbal device that allowed us to selectively reduce the wrist’s ability to generate torque about different physiological axes: flexion-extension, pronation-supination, ulnar-radial deviation. With it, we tested different levels of inhibition of the wrist’s ability to generate stabilizing torques in two different arm configurations: a preferred arm pose and an undesired arm pose. The experimental design and related apparatus, allowed us to probe the human ability to generate force in spite of instability and postural changes without disrupting—for example, by means of perturbations – participants’ ecological behavior.

Our results showed that (i) human maximum force production during a pushing task is significantly and substantially limited by the ability to generate stabilizing stiffness, while (ii) substantial deviations of the arm configuration from its preferred posture had a negligible effect on performance.

It is observable (from Fig. 2) that the total force magnitude ‘FF’ ramped up from zero to near a steady-state value at about 3 seconds, and was maintained for the rest of the task. It is also notable that the total force magnitude and the force along the ‘z’ axis were very similar both in terms of temporal evolution as well as magnitude. The forces along the other two orthogonal axes (‘x’ and ‘y’) were considerably smaller. Moreover, these forces oscillated around zero for all conditions except the FLAT one. This is expected, since in all cases – except the FLAT condition – a ball joint connected the gimbal with the sensor, which made the generation of lateral forces much harder. More importantly, a pronounced decrease in total force is observable when moving across conditions from FLAT to ULCK.

The effect of pushing condition and arm configuration on maximum force

Subjects presented a diverse range of forces across conditions. The strongest subject was number 5 with a maximum median force of 162.3 N, while the weakest subject was number 9 with a maximum median force of 59.6 N—almost a 3:1 ratio in performance. Despite the diversity in force generation ability, all subjects experienced across conditions an overall loss of performance in terms of maximum voluntary isometric force generation—independently of arm configuration. Each condition, moving from FLAT to ULCK, progressively complicated the stabilization of the task by reducing the wrist’s ability to generate torque or stiffness in specific directions. When going from FLAT to LCK, the task goes from being a stable pushing task to an unstable one, i.e., pushing on a stick. Then, when moving from LCK to FEX, subjects lose their ability to generate wrist flexion-extension torque or stiffness. In the URD case, subjects additionally lose their ability to generate wrist ulnar-radial deviation torque or stiffness. At last, in the ULCK case, subjects lose their ability to generate wrist torque and stiffness in pronation-supination.

It is interesting to observe how the maximum voluntary force has a significantly and almost-monotonically decreasing trend in all subjects and in both arm configurations when going from the FLAT condition i.e., pushing directly against the robot, to the ULCK one i.e., pushing in the most challenging condition with all the gimbal degrees of freedom unlocked. However, exceptions in some specific cases are observable. For example, subject 9 has a non-monotonic behavior between the FLAT and LCK conditions. However, the force still shows a significantly decreasing maximum force going from the fully locked (LCK) to the fully unlocked (ULCK) gimbal conditions.

Our results demonstrate that going from a stable to an unstable pushing task (FLAT → LCK) significantly reduced the maximum voluntary isometric force performance. By continuously impairing the wrist’s ability to stabilize the task (LCK → FEX → URD → ULCK), subjects continued to significantly reduce their maximum voluntary isometric force. The effect of removing the wrist’s ability to generate torque and stiffness decreased non-linearly with an approximately exponentially decaying trend, i.e., the more wrist ability to generate torque was removed, the less the maximum voluntary isometric force was reduced. This was evident in all subjects and clearly observable in Fig. 3. We refrained from interpolating the data between task conditions since the scale is ordinal, but the intervals’ distance cannot be numerically defined.

As mentioned previously, arm configuration was found to have a significant effect on maximum force performance. However, as observable in Fig. 3, the use of the two different arm configurations did not seem to substantially change the amount of generated force in each condition. Discussion

In this paper we investigated two research questions: (i) Is human maximum force production limited by the ability to generate stabilizing stiffness? (ii) Does arm configuration influence maximum force generation?

To answer these two research questions, we developed an experiment in which subjects were asked to perform a simple physical interaction task, i.e., pushing as hard as possible. By avoiding perturbations, the experiment was designed to permit the investigation of our research questions without disrupting natural, i.e., ecological, behavior.

The pushing task was performed in both stable (FLAT) and unstable conditions (LCK—fully locked, FEX—flexion/extension unlocked, URD—ulnar/radial deviation and flexion/extension unlocked, ULCK—fully unlocked). The unstable conditions progressively decreased the ability to generate a stabilizing action at the wrist. These conditions were tested in both the subjects’ preferred arm configuration, and in a highly undesirable arm configuration i.e., maximum supination.

This experiment extended the work by ref. 23, which showed preliminary evidence that maximum force magnitude was affected by the degrees of freedom of the tool. The present study examined force components in all three directions as well as two distinct postures. A diverse group of subjects, with maximum force exertion ranging from 59.6 N to 162.3 N, all showed a progressive and substantial reduction of maximum force as the tool degrees of freedom were increased, ranging from 23.1% to 53.7%.

Subjects were free to select their preferred posture and took advantage of that freedom. Figure 6 clearly shows qualitative differences between subjects’ preferred postures. It is interesting to observe that – even if subjects consistently used a configuration with their wrist facing downward – the rest of their body posture (fingers, arm, trunk) varied considerably between subjects.

On any single trial, the arm configuration remained essentially constant. Consequently, the moment arms relating muscle tension to externally exerted force were essentially unchanged. Yet the maximum force decreased with an approximately exponential trend (Fig. 3) as the unusable wrist degrees of freedom increased.

To better understand the influence of posture, we studied the effect of an extreme change in arm configuration, maximizing forearm supination. We did find a significant effect of arm configuration in all subjects and most conditions. However, the magnitude of this effect was well within the range of the Just Noticeable Difference of human perception. The small effect of this extreme variation of pose indicates that the skeletal configuration played a minimal role in the observed drop in maximum pushing performance. The subtle but significant differences, in favor of the preferred arm configuration, might still represent a possible functional advantage with respect to the undesired arm posture. However, their contribution was negligible compared to the loss of ability to exert wrist torque and hence stiffness.

One might argue that the wrist muscles, and thus wrist torque, could contribute to force production; hence, eliminating wrist torque might account for the observed reduction of the force exerted. However, if the tool axis is perpendicular to the surface of the force transducer (the nominal situation), wrist torque can only generate shear force with no axial compression force. If the tool axis is misaligned, wrist torque can contribute to axial force, but that component is dwarfed by the contribution to shear force. For example, a misalignment of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5^\circ$$\end{document}5∘ would yield an axial component of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tan \left(5^\circ \right)=0.087$$\end{document}tan5∘=0.087 of the shear component, i.e., more than 10 times smaller. Even an extreme misalignment of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$15^\circ$$\end{document}15∘ would yield an axial component of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tan \left(15^\circ \right)=0.268$$\end{document}tan15∘=0.268 of the shear component, i.e., almost 4 times smaller.

In fact, the measured shear forces, especially with degrees of freedom unlocked, fluctuated about zero, with a maximum range of about 10 N (see Fig. 2). The corresponding axial force contribution would be between 1 and 3 N—a negligible fraction of the observed pushing force. To check, we repeated our statistical analysis by considering only the ‘z’ direction (refer to the Supplementary Material), and found the same results obtained for the total force magnitude—the force significantly decreased with increasing gimbal degrees of freedom, while it was significantly but negligibly affected by the two arm configurations.

Torque alone is not sufficient to stabilize this unstable pushing task. The fundamental requirement is stiffness, a relation between torque and angular displacement. Releasing a degree of freedom of the gimbal set the corresponding component of wrist torque to zero but also constrained the corresponding component of wrist stiffness to zero. In principle, a loss of stabilizing stiffness at the wrist might be compensated by other joints e.g., the shoulder, but this compensation was evidently insufficient. A plausible mechanistic interpretation is that the muscles previously used to maximize pushing, would now need to be used to stabilize the task. The overall result was a drop in performance i.e., the maximum output force decreased.

An obvious concern with our study was that we did not monitor muscle activity directly e.g., via EMG. That was a deliberate choice. While EMG can signal when a muscle is active, it does not provide a calibrated quantitative measure of its mechanical action. Furthermore, in practice only a subset of the relevant muscles is accessible. In contrast, measurements of end-effector force and motion reflect the action of all recruited muscles.

Nevertheless, we acknowledge that EMG might play a role in future studies. For example, it might be informative to test whether subjects show evidence of co-activating antagonist wrist muscles even when the unlocked tool prevented the wrist from generating any stabilizing torque or stiffness. Subjects might still co-activate wrist muscles out of habit. These interesting questions go beyond the purpose of this work, but represent potential avenues of future research.

We also investigated the effect of time or experience (pre- vs. post-) on maximum voluntary isometric force exertion. This was initially intended to test for possible functional effects of fatigue. Serendipitously, we found that, with exposure, healthy individuals improved their performance of this very familiar and common activity, pushing. The pre-post analysis showed that all subjects but one significantly improved their pushing performance (on average by +25.5%) at the end of the experiment—this despite the fact that most subjects self-reported some level of fatigue. How can we explain this? One hypothesis is that during the pushing task, two opposing processes occurred: a fatiguing process, which should reduce overall performance, and a learning process, which might improve overall performance. In our experiment, learning appeared to dominate. However, this is speculation. Further study is needed to understand this phenomenon.

Our study may inform therapeutic treatments aimed at restoring motor functions. For example, if generating stabilizing stiffness is a higher priority, maybe we should focus on restoring this ability before teaching users how to generate force. Additionally, our findings may also affect the way we design control strategies for assistive and rehabilitative robots; e.g., we should focus on control strategies that consider stabilization rather than just force generation.

In conclusion, our results confirm that, in some important functional contexts such as an unstable pushing task, force generation is limited by our ability to generate stabilizing stiffness. This holds true across subjects, even if multiple skeletal configurations are used (Fig. 6) to perform the same motor task, i.e., pushing.

Two important observations can be made from this equation. First, an increase in muscle stiffness will cause a unique increase in joint stiffness. Second, since the mapping from joint space to muscle space is not unique, multiple combinations of muscle stiffness can achieve the same joint stiffness. In other words, different subjects may generate joint stiffness in different ways to accomplish the same task. Some approaches might be more preferable (e.g., efficient) than others, but all can lead to the same result. For more details on this mapping, see ref. 20. The extent to which each individual muscle contributes to the end-effector stiffness was not considered in this study, as we focused on the global effect of instability and posture on the musculoskeletal system.

It is also worth noting that the contributions to muscle stiffness (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${K}_{m}$$\end{document}Km) originate from a combination of passive muscle tissues (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${K}_{{m}_{p}}$$\end{document}Kmp)—the natural viscoelastic response of muscle tissues in absence of neural activation – with neuromuscular activation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${K}_{{m}_{a}}$$\end{document}Kma), i.e., the stiffness obtained through alpha neuromotor action. How much passive and active muscle stiffness contribute to the task was beyond the purpose of this study. However, both terms (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${K}_{m}={K}_{{m}_{p}}+{K}_{{m}_{a}}$$\end{document}Km=Kmp+Kma) contribute to the generation of muscle-space, joint-space and, thus, task-space stiffness. Moreover, it also worth stressing that while muscles are capable of generating force only in one direction, i.e., tension, their stiffness acts bidirectionally to stabilize the skeleton. Please refer to the Supplementary Material for a detailed explanation of this aspect.

An important consequence of this analysis is that—in all cases – force and stiffness at the end-effector, and at the joint and muscle levels, are intrinsically connected. If a higher end-effector stiffness is required, that requires higher joint stiffness, and in turn higher muscle stiffness.

Experimental setup

Nine healthy individuals, (5 F, 4 M), age 24± 424±4 years old, were recruited to perform the experiment. All subjects but one were right-handed; subject number 5 was ambidextrous. All subjects performed the experiment with the right hand. They all received a comprehensive briefing on the experimental procedures and signed a consent form approved by MIT’s Institutional Review Board (IRB protocol nr. 2408001378).

Subjects were instructed to sit on a stool with adjustable height and no back support. The height of the stool was adjusted such that the subject could comfortably maintain both feet in contact with the ground at all times. Subjects were instructed to maintain an upright posture. Nonetheless, leaning back or forth during the experiment was allowed. The stool was positioned in front of a Kuka LBR iiwa robot equipped at its end-effector with an ATI 6-axis force-torque sensor (US-30-100 Gamma series). The sensor was used to measure the interaction forces and torques exerted by the subjects during the task. The decision to mount the sensor on a programmable robot was motivated by the need to adjust the placement of the sensor with respect to the subject.

A custom gimbal device was designed and manufactured to progressively modify the ability of the wrist to generate torque by removing constraints, i.e., mechanical pins, on the gimbal axes of rotation. Figure 8 provides a detailed view of the apparatus. It consists of three axes of rotation, mutually orthogonal and intersecting at one point. This allowed the gimbal to reproduce the behavior of a spherical joint. Each axis of rotation could be locked by means of mechanical pins which could be manually inserted in the system. When an axis of rotation was unlocked, the wrist was unable to transmit torque about that axis, thanks to the negligible resistance offered by the bearings. Alternatively, when an axis of rotation was locked, the wrist could fully transmit torque about that axis. The gimbal was connected to the robot by means of a ball joint. This was implemented to closely reproduce the common task of pushing on a drill or screwdriver against a wall – as depicted in Fig. 1. The distance between the ball joint and the intersection of the 3 axes was 238.44 mm. Experimental protocol

Before initiating the experiment, subjects completed a brief survey reporting their age, weight, height and handedness. The length of the upper arm (acromion to olecranon), forearm (olecranon to styloid process), and hand (styloid process to middle finger knuckle) were manually measured using a measuring tape.

After completing the survey, subjects were placed in front of the experimental setup (shown in Fig. 7) and instructed to push directly into the force sensor i.e., along the ‘z’ axis, – either directly with the palm or via the gimbal device—using their right arm. They were tasked to generate their maximum isometric force for a total of 7 s. No visual feedback was provided about the amount or direction of the generated force. The beginning and end of each trial was communicated verbally by the experimenter.

The test was repeated in different conditions organized in the protocol presented in Fig. 9. Each subject started by performing a single trial of maximum pushing force task (pre) using their preferred arm configuration. This was done to measure subjects’ initial performance. Then, each subject was randomly assigned to perform either the preferred – first – and the ‘undesired’ – second – arm configuration blocks, or vice-versa. The preferred arm configuration allowed each subject to perform the pushing task in whatever posture they considered best for them. The ‘undesired’ arm configuration required subjects to push while maintaining what is generally considered an uncomfortable pushing configuration i.e., pushing with the wrist facing upward, maximizing supination.Fig. 9The experimental protocol.Each subject started by performing a single maximum pushing force trial (pre) in their preferred arm configuration. We decided to use as a metric the magnitude of the force vector—rather than the ‘z’ direction force component—to have a measure of the total applied force independently of any eventual misalignment between the direction of force generation and the ‘z’ direction. A closer look at each force component revealed—as expected—that most of the force was concentrated along the ‘z’ direction, though some misalignment was observable. This further justified the decision to investigate the total force magnitude.

Two research questions were investigated in this work (1): Is the maximum voluntary isometric force limited by the ability to generate stiffness during an unstable physical interaction task? (2) Does arm configuration influence maximum voluntary isometric force generation?

To statistically address these two research questions, we used a 2-way repeated-measures ANOVA design. We analyzed the effect of arm configuration (preferred, undesired), and pushing conditions (FLAT, ULCK, LCK, FEX, URD) on the generated steady-state maximum isometric force F(t)F(t). A significance threshold of α=0.05α=0.05 was used. Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc t-tests were conducted in case of significant effects. The analysis was performed—on an individual subject basis—pooling each tested condition across the 5 trials. The decision to perform individual subjects’ analysis was taken to observe any individual preference in force generation. This was motivated by the Synergy Expansion hypothesis, which indicates that different humans are not guaranteed to converge to the same muscle recruitment for the same motor task20.

An additional 2-way repeated-measures ANOVA was performed to assess the effect of individual subjects and time (pre- and post-) on the maximum voluntary isometric force. A significance threshold of α=0.05α=0.05 was used. Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc t-tests were conducted in case of significant effects. This additional test was included to investigate the presence of fatigue or learning in individual subjects.
