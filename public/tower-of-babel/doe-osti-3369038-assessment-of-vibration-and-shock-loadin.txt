ABSTRACT
Studies that examine the combined effects of hydrogen and rail loading environments in the context
of hydrogen embrittlement of steels have not been performed. Large-scale hydrogen storage requires
either gaseous hydrogen stored in pressure vessels and tubing or liquid hydrogen in cryogenic,
insulated tanks. Since the focus of this study is on steels, we examine the influence of hydrogen on
stainless steel used for high pressure tubing and cryogenic tanks and Cr-Mo steel liners used in
gaseous pressure vessels. Literature studies show that despite hydrogen degradation, these steels can
be used effectively and safely if the stresses are managed. The shock and vibration environment on
railcars present unique environments where stresses and loading rates can be high. A literature
review was conducted to understand the accelerations experienced in rail by examining instrumented
railcars of nuclear fuel casks. Hydrogen studies were examined that focused on steel pressure
hardware with an emphasis on fatigue, fast loading rates, and low temperatures. A simplified fatigue
assessment was performed to examine design cycles for three different hydrogen components:
stainless steel tubing, stainless steel cryogenic tanks, and gaseous pressure vessels. The assessments
were idealized and used bounding cases of accelerations in both normal rail conditions and coupling
events. In normal shock and vibration environments when accelerations were less than 2 g, infinite
design life was calculated. In extreme shock or coupling environments, finite design life was
calculated which was shown to be dependent on unsupported lengths of pressure components. It
was shown that adjusting the unsupported length can reduce the bending stress thereby increasing
design life.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Hydrogen can have detrimental effects on mechanical properties of most materials, particularly
steels. Hydrogen can reduce the mechanical property performance of steels through a phenomenon
known as hydrogen embrittlement where it reduces ductility and fatigue life. Hydrogen will enter
nearly all materials and in the presence of tensile stresses, can reduce the stress or strain necessary
for fracture.
The mechanical loading that occurs in the rail environment is unique, and literature on hydrogen
effects in environments directly relevant to rail is scarce. Most high-pressure hydrogen containment
steel vessels and hardware are stationary, and the stresses are driven predominantly by the pressure
cycles of the hydrogen gas. DOT specification hydrogen steel cylinders in truck transportation do
experience external mechanical loading due to road vibration; however, the design does not require
fatigue analysis from external sources suggesting that normal road vibration is not of concern.
However, the shock and vibration environment in rail environments can be more severe and should
be examined.
A literature review to understand the rail environment was conducted and contrasted to the truck
environment using data where trucks or railcars of nuclear fuel casks were instrumented with
accelerometers. It was found that the rail environment is more severe in terms of accelerations
measured in g. The vibration in both modes of transportation (truck and rail) appeared to be bound
by 2 g, and shock and vibration was most severe in the vertical direction. The location of the
measurement was critical as acceleration measurements on the ends of the truck or rail platform
were significantly higher compared to cargo areas, where dampening reduced acceleration.
Instrumented railcars experienced shock events, excluding coupling, up to 8.9 g on tests performed
at the Transportation Technology Center (TTC) and peaks of 7.97 g on commercial railroads, with
both occurring on the platform ends. The cargo regions (e.g., where hydrogen vessels and hardware
would be located) experienced much lower accelerations bound by 2 g. In contrast, on-road vehicles
experienced a maximum of 4.5 g at the platform end in one study and 7 g in another, but most
events were less than 0.5 g. Rail coupling was the most severe shock environment and similarly the
location of greatest severity was the platform end. Accelerations up to 26 g were recorded at the
TTC tests at speeds up to 8 mph. In addition, coupling events were recorded at railyards along a
cross-country route with a maximum of 22 g; however, most coupling events were less than 10 g at
the platform end and were near 1 g in the cargo regions.
A literature review was performed on steel performance in hydrogen in mechanical loading
environments most relevant to rail. Emphasis was placed on austenitic stainless steels (304L or
316L) as these materials are commonly used for gaseous and liquid hydrogen storage components.
In addition, martensitic steels (4130 or 4140 steels) were examined as they are commonly used as
metal liners in gaseous pressure vessels. Three important environments were prioritized: 1) fatigue,
2) loading rate effects, 3) low temperature. The following highlights the overlap between available
hydrogen literature and the environments mentioned above.
 • Hydrogen affects fatigue life of steels, but the influence is only apparent at high stresses and
 no effect of hydrogen on the fatigue limit was observed, where fatigue limit is the stress
 below which infinite life is observed. This means that if stresses are managed, there may be
 minimal effect of hydrogen on fatigue life.

 9

 • Faster loading rates appear to reduce the effects of hydrogen as hydrogen does not have
 sufficient time to diffuse to tensile stressed regions. The effects are not completely removed
 but appear to be less than at slow rates.
 • Low temperatures reduce ductility of austenitic stainless steels. The effects of hydrogen
 appear to be reduced at liquid hydrogen temperatures (20 K or -424°F), but still show
 degradation effects through secondary cracking and lower reduction of area in tensile
 samples.
Four shock and vibration environments were selected to perform simplified calculations of fatigue
design cycles based on ASME VIII-3 fatigue design curves on steel pressure vessels, cryogenic tanks,
and piping. ASME fatigue design curves (e.g., S-N curves) are commonly used for pressure vessel
design to evaluate a permissible number of cycles (N) based on applied stress (S) with built in
reduction factors. The environments and corresponding accelerations (g) used for the fatigue design
analysis were extreme coupling (26 g), extreme shock (8.9 g), normal shock (2 g), normal vibration (1
g). The two extreme environments used accelerations from rail platform ends, which makes the
accelerations conservative as hydrogen hardware is not likely to be placed at these locations. The
two normal environments are more representative of where hydrogen hardware would be placed as
the accelerations were from instrumented components in the cargo storage areas. The accelerations
were used to determine vertical bending stress on horizontal-oriented pressure vessels, cryogenic
tanks, and pipes. The three components analyzed were 1) pressurized tubing (stainless steel), 2)
cryogenic tank (stainless steel), 3) gaseous hydrogen pressure vessel (Cr-Mo steel). It should be
noted that cryogenic tanks can maintain pressures slightly above atmospheric and often have
pressure relief set to <14.2 bar (206 psi). The design cycle calculations have many embedded
assumptions to establish the boundary conditions for the analysis; however, it provides a means of
assessing magnitude of stress level and subsequent design cycles. In all cases, the normal
environments with accelerations of 1 to 2 g resulted in infinite life as the stresses were below the
fatigue limits. The extreme shock (8.9 g) resulted in infinite life for the tubing and cryogenic tank
and a limited life of 160,000,000 cycles for the gaseous hydrogen pressure vessel operated at ambient
temperatures. For the extreme coupling event (26 g) a finite life was typically calculated. It is unclear
how often an event of this magnitude occurs during normal rail operations and is an area where
more data should be collected. However, additional analysis showed that reducing the unsupported
length would reduce bending stress, thereby increasing design cycles. Therefore, one means to
extend life of pressure hardware is to reduce distances between supports.
Recommended further research:
 - Measure accelerations on tanks and tubing relevant to hydrogen rolling stock to improve
 fidelity of fatigue assessments as instrumented data are limited
 - Measure both high loading rate fracture and fatigue performance of fully hydrogen-saturated
 austenitic stainless steels to mimic long-term exposure to either gaseous or liquid hydrogen.
 - Evaluate effects of shock and vibration on hydrogen-exposed non-metallic liner materials.

1. INTRODUCTION
This report includes a literature review that was performed between January 2021 and December
2022 by Sandia National Laboratories, under interagency agreement 693JJ620N000070 with the
Federal Railroad Association (FRA), to identify where existing hydrogen materials compatibility
studies on steel overlap mechanical loading conditions experienced in normal railroad operations.
This report summarizes the findings from studies to help inform FRA how steels will respond in
shock and vibration environments common to rail systems. In addition, a fatigue assessment was
conducted using 4 different shock and vibration conditions which impose bending stresses on
hydrogen containment vessels and tubing to assess design life based on relevant ASME fatigue
design curves.

1.1. Background
As new technologies such as hydrogen fuel cell powered locomotives are considered, it is important
to understand how hydrogen might degrade the mechanical properties of the materials used to store
it, as hydrogen is known to cause deleterious effects to mechanical properties of steels through a
phenomenon known as hydrogen embrittlement. Rolling stock are subjected to unique mechanical
loading conditions of shock and vibration compared to stationary storage applications of hydrogen,
for which much of the previous mechanical testing has been performed to address. Therefore, there
are questions as to how hydrogen may degrade materials in the shock and vibration environment
and this report seeks to identify overlaps in literature data on hydrogen materials studies on steels
with rail environments.

1.2. Objectives
The objective of this project was to identify potential mechanical loading environments experienced
in railroad operations that may lead to hydrogen embrittlement. This was done via a literature review
of steel compatibility with hydrogen to identify overlaps in mechanical test data of steels with
environments applicable to rail and identify potential areas, if any, where further experimental
research would be beneficial. An idealized fatigue assessment was performed based on rail
conditions found in literature and were used to evaluate how shock and vibrations might influence
fatigue life of steel components.

1.3. Overall Approach
A literature survey was conducted of common mechanical loading conditions experienced in rail
environments during transportation. Rail environments were contrasted with data collected on truck
environments to assess shock and vibration differences between the two transportation modes.
Next, a literature survey was performed to assess the effects of hydrogen in different mechanical
loading environments that are relevant to rail environments. A comparison was made of the rail
environments and the hydrogen compatibility of steels studies to identify what overlaps exist among
the data. The materials of interest for hydrogen containment include austenitic stainless steels (e.g.,
304L, 316L) commonly used in high pressure tubing and cryogenic storage at ambient pressures (i.e.,
<206 psi) as well as conventional pressure vessel steels (e.g., Cr-Mo (chromium molybdenum
martensitic steels)) used in gaseous hydrogen storage. Cr-Mo steels such as 4130 or 4140 are quench
and tempered martensitic steels that can be tuned to a particular strength for the application.
Literature data on hydrogen compatibility of these steels were prioritized but studies of other
materials that included mechanical loading environments similar to rail applications were included.
Finally, several fatigue analyses were performed to assess design cycles of standard sized steel storage

 13

tanks and tubes using ASME fatigue design curves for stresses induced by shock and vibration
commonly incurred in railcars. The ASME fatigue design curves were shown to be bounding curves
compared to literature data for materials tested in hydrogen; therefore, the ASME design curves
were used for the analyses.

1.4. Scope
The focus of this report is specifically on effects of hydrogen on steel components subject to
pressure and mechanical loading. For gaseous or liquid hydrogen service these steels components
include austenitic stainless steel tubing and cryogenic vessels. In addition, we examined the effects
on metal liner gaseous pressure vessels which are conventionally Cr-Mo martensitic steels often used
in Type 1 or Type 2 pressure vessels and operated at ambient temperatures. Type 4 vessels which are
composed of a plastic liner and non-metallic fiber overwrap were outside the scope of this work as
they are not affected by conventional hydrogen embrittlement and do not contain steel. Table 1 lists
the different components and materials considered for rail applications as well as common materials

The literature used to develop an understanding of the mechanical loading environment for rail
focused on select studies in which a nuclear fuel cask was instrumented with a variety of
accelerometers and strain gauges and put through a series of tests. The conditions assessed includes
travel across commercial rail and specific tests conducted at the Transportation Technology Center
(TTC) in Pueblo, CO. The results from these instrumented rail cars were used to bound the
acceleration forces experienced on rail cars which were subsequently used to calculate stresses due to
acceleration. Vertical acceleration of components was the orientation considered as the bounding
case, as it consistently had the highest recorded accelerations. The focus of the current report is on
shock and vibration experienced by rail systems during normal operations; however, the truck
environment is of relevance as hydrogen is routinely transported in both liquid and gaseous forms

 14

by truck and has been transported safely for many decades using this mode of transportation.
Therefore, the vibration and shock environments of truck and rail were compared to identify the
magnitude of differences between the two modes of transportation.
Rolling stock with hydrogen storage may be different in length, weight, or design compared to the
simplified study cases in this report; however, the max accelerations were assumed to be translatable
from the nuclear fuel cask car to hydrogen rolling stock. Accelerations are measured in g which is
the acceleration of the object divided by the acceleration of gravity (g = 9.8 m/s2 [32.2 ft/s2]).
Therefore 2 g would mean an object had an acceleration of 2 x 9.8 m/s2. A fatigue assessment was
included to provide a comparison of the acceleration magnitudes on fatigue life for idealized
bending stresses imposed by vertical acceleration. These assessments are not intended to deem the
components safe for use or identify precise design cycles. A more detailed stress assessment should
be performed based on actual design and operating conditions to evaluate component life.

1.5. Organization of the Report
There are 6 sections in this report. Section 1 introduces an overview of objectives, approach and
scope of the project. Section 2 provides a literature review of the common mechanical loading
conditions experienced by railcars and contrasts that with truck loading environments. Section 3
provides an overview of steel degradation by hydrogen and relates the mechanical environments of
rail to literature data on hydrogen compatibility. Section 4 provides fatigue design life assessments of
steel pressure vessels and piping commonly used in liquid and gaseous hydrogen storage using
common mechanical loading conditions in railcars using ASME design curves. It was found that the
ASME design curves bounded the literature fatigue-life data for tests in hydrogen gas and therefore
the ASME design curves were used for the fatigue analysis. Section 5 identifies gaps in research that
could be pursued. Section 6 provides a summary of conclusions.

2. RAIL AND TRUCK MECHANICAL LOADING ENVIRONMENTS
Rolling stock are subjected to a variety of loading conditions caused by normal travel on railroads
consisting of crossings, switches, coupling, etc. Shock and vibration environments are innate, and
the magnitude and frequency of these events are caused by the route, speed, and weight of the
rolling stock. In this report, vibration is defined as a repetitive vertical movement, as opposed to
shock which is a higher magnitude isolated event. In this section, a literature review was conducted,
and a summary of the mechanical loads experienced during rail operations are included in which rail
cars were instrumented with accelerometers and strain gauges. These rail cars were subjected to
various tests performed at the TTC in Pueblo, CO and measurements were also performed during
normal travel routes on commercial rail. Environments on rail cars may vary due to weight, size, and
design (e.g., built-in dampening), however, the environments from the following studies were used
to identify common loading environments and provide bounding cases for accelerations based on
shock and vibration.

2.1. Magnuson Report on Large Shipping Containers
Shock and vibration environments were examined by Magnuson [1] on shipping containers
responsible for handling fissile material. Data were analyzed from U.S. Energy Research and
Development Administration and U.S. Department of Defense Transportation data banks. The
shipping containers were instrumented and transported by both truck and rail and the accelerations
due to vibration and shock were reported. The measurements were taken between the cargo and the
cargo floor.

2.1.1.     Truck Data
The shipping container weights ranged from no load to 15 tons (133 kN) and the trucks traveled
over highways and 2-lane roads with varying speed. The peak acceleration amplitudes categorized as
vibrations were measured at 2 g in the vertical direction. The shock environments were related to the
truck encountering potholes, railroad crossings, and bridge approaches. Similar to the vibration
measurements, the shock measurements were highest in the vertical direction and reached a
maximum acceleration of 7 g.

2.1.2.     Rail Data
Vibration data were obtained on the shipment of a 15 ton (133 kN) fuel cask from Kentucky to
Tennessee. The measurements were taken on 3 different trains and the length of trains varied from
65 to 120 cars. Similar to the truck data, the vertical axis acceleration was the greatest and for
vibration, reached a peak acceleration amplitude of 0.37 g. Rail coupling shock was measured as a
function of speed on a cargo weighing 5 tons (44.5 kN) ranging from 8.45 to 17.78 km/hr (5.25 to
11.05 mph). The data reported for shock measurements consisted of 15,648 switching and coupling
events. The peak acceleration in the vertical direction at the highest coupling speed of 17.78 km/hr
(11.05 mph) was recorded at 26 g. The peak acceleration at this coupling speed in the longitudinal
direction was 39 g. These data were represented by simple half-sine input pulses which are
conservative measurements that represent the expected shock severity. The data were unfiltered and
did not include concurrent strain measurements, therefore it is uncertain if these higher accelerations
translated to real strain on the components.

2.2. Kalinina Reports on Nuclear Fuel Cask Transport
A study [2] was conducted in 2017 which instrumented a surrogate pressurized water reactor nuclear
fuel assembly in a rail cask. Data were collected over 3 different modes of transportation: heavy-haul
truck, ship, and rail. The ship was the least severe mechanical shock/vibration environment and not
relevant to this current study, so it is not discussed further. A total of 40 accelerometers and 37
strain gauges were placed on the cask, the cradle, the transport platforms, and inside the cask on the
fuel assembly to measure the mechanical loading environment and identify differences between
shock and vibration at specific locations on the instrumented assembly. For each shock event, strain
gauge data were compared to the accelerations to confirm that strain was imposed on the location
during the shock event. The most severe loading environments, regardless of the mode of
transportation, was on the transport platform. Figure 1 shows a schematic of the cask, cradle, and
platform used in the rail transport along with corresponding accelerometer locations. At each
location, accelerations were measured in the axial, lateral, and vertical directions. In the subsequent
sections, a summary of the most severe events is included which provide a comparison for truck
versus rail environments.

Figure 1. Schematic of the cask, cradle, and transport platform accelerometer locations. Adapted
 image from Kalinina et al. [2].

2.2.1.     Truck Data
A heavy haul truck was used to transport the fuel cask over 245 miles of road. The maximum
vertical acceleration measured was 4.52 g on the platform ends, whereas the max acceleration of
cradle and cask was only 0.52 g. Both events were associated with crossing bridge abutments. Over
the entire 245 mile trip, the cask and cradle experienced significantly lower accelerations than the
platform demonstrating that dampening occurs in the system. Normal vibration accelerations for the
truck transport were significantly below 1 g.

2.2.2.     Rail Data
Rail environments were evaluated on both commercial rails and at controlled tests at the TTC in
Pueblo, CO. Tests on commercial rail were taken along a Class 1 Railroad from Port of Baltimore,
MD to Pueblo, CO. The tests at the TTC were taken on FRA Class 2 rail tracks and consisted of
twist and roll, pitch and bounce, dynamic curve, PCD (a lower class track with tight turns), single
bump, crossing diamond, hunting (e.g., high speed stability tests), and coupling impact. It was found

2.3. Comparison of Rail vs Truck Mechanical Loading Environments
The shock environment is more severe on the rail application compared to truck applications based
on the available literature data shown in previous sections. The vibration in both modes of
transportation seems to be bound by 2 g and vibration and shock in the vertical direction is typically
the most severe. In addition, the acceleration on the ends of the platform is the greatest, where there
is no load. Beneath the cargo, the accelerations are dampened by nearly half. In addition, the cask
and cradle (e.g., area representing the cargo) all experience significantly reduced accelerations
compared to the ends of the platform. In rail, the maximum shock events, excluding coupling, were
8.9 g in the TTC tests and 7.97 g on the recorded commercial railroads and both occurred on the
platform ends. The cargo region on the TTC rail tests was bound by an acceleration of 2 g and on
the 1,950 mile trip along commercial rail only experienced a maximum acceleration of 1 g. On the
truck, the accelerations were a maximum of 4.5 g at the platform end in one study [2] and 7 g

majority were less than 10 g. In the cargo region, specifically inside the cask, the maximum recorded

the 8 mph coupling events recorded at the TTC are likely conservative.

3. LITERATURE REVIEW OF HYDROGEN EMBRITTLEMENT OF
 STEELS FOR ENVIRONMENTS RELEVANT TO RAIL
Hydrogen is known to degrade the mechanical properties of structural materials at ambient
temperatures through a process known as hydrogen embrittlement. Hydrogen degradation consists
of accelerated fatigue crack growth, reduction of fracture toughness, and ductility loss in the form of
reduced elongation to failure or less reduction of area in tensile properties. A common
misconception though is that hydrogen embrittlement will occur anytime hydrogen is present, when
in fact three key factors are necessary for hydrogen embrittlement to occur: susceptible material,
hydrogen environment, critical tensile stress. As shown in Figure 3, hydrogen embrittlement only
occurs at the intersection of these three. If any one of the three factors is removed or reduced, the
effects of hydrogen embrittlement will not occur. An example is common laboratory gas cylinders
which are used to store hydrogen at 15 MPa (2,176 psi). They are made from 4130X Cr-Mo
martensitic steel which is highly susceptible to hydrogen and has been reported to show accelerated
fatigue crack growth rates in hydrogen 30 times that in air [4], however, this material is used
routinely and safely because the stresses are kept rather low (e.g., hoop stresses are less than 50% of
yield strength) which is accomplished by using thick-walled pressure vessels, thus mitigating
hydrogen embrittlement.

 Figure 3. Schematic showing that hydrogen embrittlement occurs at the intersection of specific
 materials, environments, and stress conditions.

Based on the literature review of the rail environments, the shock and vibrations encountered at the
nuclear fuel cask and cradle (e.g., referred to as the cargo area) are most relevant at the locations
where hydrogen would be stored in rail applications. In addition, storage in pressure vessels at high
pressures (350 bar (5,076 psi) and above) and distribution piping locations and their supports are
critical in examining the susceptibility to vibration and shock loads. The rail platform ends
experienced the greatest accelerations, but it was clear that accelerations were reduced in the cargo
area, if such cargo areas are in-between the trucks. If the exact accelerations are unknown on a
component of interest, one could use the accelerations measured at the platform ends as extremes
were measured at these locations.

 21

As the concept of using hydrogen for rail motive power is relatively new, mechanical testing of
components used for hydrogen (fuel) storage and distribution piping systems subjecting them to the
severe environments encountered in rail operations has not been explicitly performed. Much of the
data in past tests have been generated with stationary storage hydrogen systems at high pressures.
These have focused on fatigue loading resulting from the pressurization/depressurizations of tanks
at a relatively slow frequency, e.g., once per day. At these slower rates, hydrogen diffusion in steel
can play a significant role in the hydrogen embrittlement effects as slower rates provide time for
hydrogen to diffuse to regions of elevated stress such as at a crack tip and enable hydrogen
embrittlement to occur. Loading frequency, temperature and material all affect how far hydrogen
can diffuse in a given amount of time which can have an influence on hydrogen effects. The
following sections summarize hydrogen effects on steel materials that would be used in storage of
gaseous or liquid hydrogen in a rail application. The summary focuses specifically on fatigue, loading
rates, and low temperatures.

3.1. Fatigue
This section will discuss fatigue, specifically the effects of hydrogen on fatigue-life and fatigue crack
growth rates in steels such as austenitic stainless steels or chromium-molybdenum martensitic steels,
which are commonly used for steel liners in pressure vessels, cryogenic tanks and piping1. Fatigue is
the repeated cycling of loads that can initiate and extend cracks until failure occurs. There are two
main laboratory testing methods to quantify fatigue behavior: fatigue life (S-N or e-N curves) or
fatigue crack growth rate (FCGR). Fatigue life curves are a stress (S) or strain (e) controlled test that
impose repeated cycles until samples fail at which point the number of cycles (NF) is reported. This
can be visualized in Figure 4 where more cycles are required to cause failure for the lower stress
amplitude condition compared to the higher stress amplitude condition. The stress or strain level
can be varied to develop a set of data points that is referred to as fatigue life curve which can be
used for design. The fatigue limit is defined as a stress at which an infinite number of cycles can be
applied without fatigue failure.
FCGR is used in a fracture mechanics-based design approach where an assumed initial flaw (e.g.,
crack) is present and then the rate at which the crack grows is measured as a function of stress
intensity factor range (K), which is a fracture mechanics parameter that represents the stress, size,
and geometry of a crack and is transferrable between tests in a lab and engineering structures. Both
approaches (i.e., stress variations and strain variations) are used in engineering designs and the
fatigue method used is dictated by the design code. The rate at which the loading is applied can
influence the crack growth rates and should be considered when translating data from laboratory
tests to application, however, in many cases, it is not practical to cycle in laboratory experiments at
the same frequency as application due to testing efficiency. However, frequency effects are often
evaluated and appear to only increase crack growth rates by a factor of 2x [5] on pressure vessel Cr-
Mo steels and this influence diminishes when crack growth rates are low.

3.1.1. Fatigue-Life
Fatigue life has been measured in hydrogen gas on austenitic stainless steels (often used in high-
pressure hydrogen tubing and cryogenic liners) and ferritic and martensitic-based materials (such as
those used for pipelines and DOT pressure vessels). Figure 5 shows fatigue life (S-N-) data for 316
austenitic stainless steel tested in 100 MPa (1,000 bar, 14,504 psi) hydrogen gas pressure at various
temperatures and applied stress amplitudes [6]. In these tests, the gas pressure is held constant (i.e.,
100 MPa, 14,504 psi) and the stress is varied mechanically through a load frame to achieve a variety
of stress amplitudes. Hydrogen appears to have only a minor effect on the cycles to failure at these
applied stresses. The arrows indicate the test was stopped and failure did not occur, and it can be
assumed infinite life. In ferritic and martensitic steels [7], hydrogen appears to have a more
significant effect on cycles to failure, particularly at higher applied stresses. However, hydrogen does
not have a significant effect on the fatigue limit in both austenitic and martensitic steels. The fatigue
limit is defined as the stress below which infinite life occurs. In Figure 5, the majority of the tests in
100 MPa (1,000 bar, 14,504 psi) hydrogen were performed at 25°C (77 °F) and at 1 Hz; however,
select tests were performed at lower temperatures of -45°C (-49°F) and -80°C (-112°F) for 316
stainless steels. Tests at lower temperatures (e.g., -45°C (-49°F) and -80°C (-112°F)) resulted in an
improvement of fatigue life which was attributed to the improved strength that 316 experiences at
lower temperatures.

3.1.2. Fatigue Crack Growth Rate
Fatigue crack growth rate (FCGR) data in hydrogen gas of pressure vessel steels are more ubiquitous
in the literature. Stationary pressure vessels that are used to store hydrogen at high pressure are
designed according to ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code (BPVC) VIII-3 Article KD-3 [8]
which requires a fracture mechanics approach and therefore FCGR curves. One set of FCGR curves
is shown in Figure 6 for pressure vessel steels (SA-372 and SA-723) commonly used in high-pressure
hydrogen vessels tested in 103 MPa (14,939 psi) pressure hydrogen gas. The FCGR curves were
observed to be very repeatable among different strength levels and a design curve was developed
and published as ASME Code Case 2938 which can be used for design [9]. As K increases, the
crack growth rate (da/dN) increases and shows a distinct increase compared to da/dN in air. The
difference can be over one order of magnitude greater in hydrogen compared to air, particularly at
the higher K; however, this difference appears to diminish at lower K where the crack growth
rates in hydrogen approach those in air.

3.2. Effects of Loading Rate
This section discusses the effects of mechanical loading rate on hydrogen embrittlement of steels.
Hydrogen embrittlement can be limited by kinetics as hydrogen needs to diffuse to regions of higher
stress, such as ahead of the crack tip. Therefore, if loading rates (i.e., the increase in applied load

 25

over a specific time) are too high or if hydrogen diffusion rates are too low, the effects of hydrogen
embrittlement may be diminished.

3.2.1. Monotonic Tensile Tests
One clear example of how loading rates can affect mechanical response in the presence of hydrogen
is shown in Figure 8 for tensile tests performed on a low-carbon martensitic steel. Tensile tests were
performed at strain rates ranging from 10-1 to 10-6 s-1 which revealed significant changes in tensile
behavior in hydrogen precharged materials [11]. The fast strain rates (8.3 x 10-1 s-1) exhibited
significantly more ductility than the slow rates (8.3 x 10-6 s-1) where the effects of hydrogen were
most pronounced. Similar behavior was observed in Depover et al. [12] and Rehrl et al. [13] for
hydrogen exposed steels which exhibited less than 10 percent loss in ductility at higher strain rates
compared to more pronounced losses at slower rates. The materials tested were either ferritic or
martensitic steels and would be representative of high-pressure hydrogen storage tanks used for
gaseous hydrogen.

 Figure 8. Tensile curves of hydrogen precharged low-carbon martensite tested at different strain
 rates. Figure from Momotani et al. [11].

3.2.2. Impact Testing (High Rate)
Charpy testing is a high-rate impact test commonly performed on structural metals to evaluate their
ability to absorb energy and assess their fracture performance. Charpy tests are performed via a
hammer on a pendulum swinging at around 5 m/s which provides an estimated strain rate between
101 to 103 s-1, although a true value of strain rate is difficult to quantify due to the higher strain rate in
the notch and lower strain rate in the plastic hinge of the sample [14]. Regardless, these rates are
very fast compared to other mechanical tests such as tensile testing or monotonically loaded fracture
tests, which tend to be much slower (e.g., <10-3 s-1).
Hydrogen precharged ferritic steels have been tested via Charpy methods and minimal effect was
measured on the upper shelf energies compared to non-charged conditions particularly as the tests
approached room temperature [15, 16]. This is likely due to the fast loading rates imposed by the
Charpy test where the strain rates are estimated to be 103 s-1, which does not allow time for
hydrogen to diffuse to the region of higher stress ahead of the notch. In [16], the ductile to brittle
transition temperature (DBTT) shifted higher by approximately 30°C (54°F) in the hydrogen
charged samples but this shift was from -120°C (-184°F) to -90°C (-130°F), however, the upper
shelf maintained similar impact energy values. Both studies were of pipeline ferritic steels which

 26

would have comparable hydrogen diffusion rates to Cr-Mo pressure vessel steels and therefore
would be expected to have a similar response. However, the steels above were electrochemically
precharged and it is challenging to directly relate the hydrogen environments to gaseous hydrogen
exposure as there can be significant hydrogen concentration gradients as a result of the high
hydrogen fugacity near the surface from electrochemical charging. Charpy testing has also been
performed on hydrogen-precharged 304 stainless steel as shown in Figure 9. The impact energy
absorbed decreases as the temperature is reduced and the effects of hydrogen appear to increase at
lower temperatures. In 304 stainless steel, low temperatures can result in the formation of a
martensitic phase which is more susceptible to hydrogen and could be the reason for this larger
degradation at low temperatures.
In general, it appears that the effects of hydrogen are reduced at higher testing rates compared to
slower testing rates; however, there still appears to be some measurable degradation of properties.
At higher rates, hydrogen mobility is limited and cannot diffuse and accumulate at regions of high
tensile stress (e.g., crack tips). Hydrogen was introduced to these materials via electrochemical or
gaseous precharging which overcomes some of these kinetic barriers but also raises questions about
the relevancy of the hydrogen contents as they may not be representative of applications. Fielded
components will be exposed to hydrogen for long durations and kinetic limitations may become less
of an issue. More work is needed to understand these behaviors.

 charged and H-charged conditions. Figure from Nam et al. [17].

3.2.3. Frequency Effects in Fatigue
Experiments to evaluate hydrogen effects on fatigue are typically biased towards slower frequencies
as diffusion of hydrogen to the region of elevated stress is necessary for the hydrogen embrittlement
effects to manifest. In stationary hydrogen storage components, hydrogen
pressurization/depressurizations occur at a very slow frequency, e.g., perhaps once a day or 10-5 Hz.
However, laboratory tests cannot be performed efficiently at these frequencies and therefore must
be accelerated. Test frequencies for laboratory tests are typically between 10-1 and 101 Hz. In a study
by Somerday et al. [5], fatigue crack growth rates were measured in high pressure hydrogen gas for
Cr-Mo pressure vessel steels at different frequencies ranging from 10-3 to 101 Hz. The highest crack
growth rates were measured at the slowest frequencies although the effect appears to saturate below
10-2 Hz and the difference was 2-5 times compared to the fastest rate of 101 s-1. Above this
frequency, the crack growth rates appear to decrease, meaning the effects of hydrogen are reduced at

 27

higher frequencies and the da/dN starts to approach behavior in air. This is consistent with the
tensile and Charpy results mentioned above. Frequency effects have also been evaluated in fatigue
life (S-N) curves for a 304 stainless steel tested at rates ranging between 10-2 and 1 Hz [6, 7]. The
effects of frequency were negligible or at least within the scatter of the data over these frequency
ranges which could be due to the slower diffusion in austenitic stainless steel compared to
martensitic steels. Fortunately, hydrogen-precharging offers the benefit of pre-saturating the
austenitic stainless steels with hydrogen thereby removing the kinetic barrier that is often a limitation
for tests performed in-gas. Recent work on a high strength austenitic stainless steel showed
negligible effects of test frequency on hydrogen-precharged fatigue life tests from 1 to 80 Hz [18].

3.3. Cryogenic Temperature Effects
Low temperatures reduce ductility of most steels and can be detrimental to ferritic and martensitic
steels that go through a ductile to brittle transition. Liquid hydrogen is stored at 20 K (-424°F) at
atmospheric pressure and therefore will be in contact with the steel which needs to be compatible at
these temperatures. For this reason, austenitic stainless steels are typically used at sub-ambient
temperatures as the effects of temperature are less significant than for ferritic or martensitic steels.
This section discusses both cryogenic tensile properties and fatigue-life.

3.3.1. Cryogenic Tensile Properties
Previous work by Caskey [19] showed that tensile ductility reaches a minimum near 220 K (-64°F)
for 304N and 304L stainless steel as shown in Figure 10 for deuterium (a hydrogen isotope)
precharged steels. In this study, samples were thermally precharged with deuterium prior to testing
in air. The ductility loss or plastic strain ratio of the material when precharged with deuterium
compared to non-charged remains high at ambient temperatures (e.g., 300 K (80°F)) as well as very
low temperature (e.g., < 100 K (-280°F)). The reason for this minimum around 220 K (-64°F) is
related to both the stability of the austenite phase and mobility of hydrogen-isotopes at these
temperatures. Less stable alloys like 304 can transform to martensite at low temperatures when
strained, creating a more susceptible microstructure thereby increasing diffusion of hydrogen-
isotopes. Hydrogen diffuses faster through martensite than austenite. At extremely low temperatures
(e.g., < 100 K (-280°F)), martensite will still form in these less stable alloys, but the diffusivity of
hydrogen is so low at these temperatures, the hydrogen effects are often diminished compared to
room temperature. Conversely, the more stable austenitic stainless steels such as 316 and 310 have
much less temperature sensitivity. Recent work at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory on 304L
precharged with hydrogen shows that reduction of area in tensile samples is reduced between 30-50
percent at 20 K (-424°F) and the samples show secondary cracking on the surface which is absent in
the noncharged condition [20]. However, the stress strain curves do not show distinguishable
differences with and without hydrogen. This same behavior is shown in 304L tensile curves tested
down to 25 K (-415°F) in hydrogen in work by Ogawa [21]. Hydrogen did not appear to affect total
elongation at 25 K (-415°F) but did have a more pronounced effect at 190 K (-118°F). The
reduction in area was not reported in Ogawa’s work [21]. Therefore, hydrogen may have some
effects at these low temperatures. Similarly, the 304 stainless steel Charpy impact results shown in
Figure 9 suggest that low temperatures can have a more pronounced effect with hydrogen, although
these tests were limited to above 77 K (-321°F). In summary, tensile data is informative for relative
comparison of hydrogen versus non-hydrogen environments, but it is not directly used in design of
pressurized components as compared to fatigue or fracture data.

3.3.2. Cryogenic Fatigue Life
Fatigue life curves (S-N) are shown in Figure 11 for SUS 304L stainless steel in 190 K (-118°F) and
20 K (-424°F) for both hydrogen and helium pressurized environments. It is clear that at 190 K (-
118°F) the number of cycles to failure in hydrogen is reduced compared to helium particularly at
higher stress amplitudes. This trend of reduced cycles in hydrogen is consistent with behavior at
room temperature in other alloys as shown in Figure 11. However, at 20 K (-424°F), the effect of
hydrogen is negligible compared to the helium tests suggesting no significant effect at temperatures
where liquid hydrogen would be stored. It should be noted that the curve at 20 K (-424°F) is
significantly higher than at 190 K (-118°F) due to the greatly improved strength of the 304L at low
temperatures. Low temperatures increase the strength of austenitic stainless steels significantly which
means that their fatigue performance typically improves. It is worth noting that the tests on
austenitic stainless steels in Figure 11 were performed in gas which means the samples were only
exposed for a relatively short time frame (hours to days). Hydrogen diffusion rates are limited in
austenitic stainless steels at room temperatures and further limited at low temperature. Therefore,
the results at very low temperatures should be interpreted with this diffusion limitation in mind. The
fielded components will have significantly longer hydrogen exposure times potentially permitting the
hydrogen to diffuse deeper into the material and the fatigue life behavior may be affected.

3.4. Summary of Effects of Hydrogen on Steels Used in Rail Environments
Rolling stock experience varying fatigue loading on a regular basis during normal travel conditions as
well as faster rate shock loads during more severe events like couplings. The magnitude of the stress
will depend on the specific events, but the highest acceleration conditions appear to occur during
coupling, which happens on a much lower frequency than vibration loading. Based on the literature
review presented above, the following summarizes the overlaps between the available hydrogen
literature on steels and the relevant rail application environments.
 • The most prominent loading environment on the rail is fatigue as the repeated shocks and
 vibrations induce a fluctuation in the loads that can lead to crack growth and failure. The
 occurrence of the shock and vibration varies depending on the event. Normal vibrations
 have low accelerations (<2 g) but occur continuously, whereas shock events result in higher
 accelerations (>2 g) but occur less frequently. Coupling events result in the highest
 accelerations (up to 26 g) but occur the least. The larger the acceleration, the larger the strain
 (or stress) therefore it is necessary to understand how hydrogen affects fatigue life over a
 range of stresses. Hydrogen does affect fatigue life, but the effect is only apparent at high
 stresses as the effect on fatigue limit is negligible in austenitic stainless steels and Cr-Mo
 pressure vessel steels at ambient temperatures. Therefore, if stresses are managed, there may
 be minimal effect of hydrogen on fatigue life. Fatigue crack growth rate is affected by
 hydrogen, but similar to fatigue life, if stresses are low, the differences between crack growth
 rate in air and hydrogen can be minimal.
 • Loading rate is important as hydrogen needs to diffuse to the region of higher stress, such as
 a crack tip, for hydrogen embrittlement to occur. Shock events occur at very high strain rates
 therefore the effects of hydrogen at higher strain rates are of interest for rail environments.
 Faster loading rates appear to reduce the effects of hydrogen as tensile data and Charpy
 impact testing showed diminishing effects of hydrogen as the loading rate increased. These
 tests were performed on hydrogen-precharged samples where hydrogen was saturated into
 the material prior to mechanically loading the sample. During a fast loading rate test such as
 would be experienced during shock and vibration, hydrogen does not have sufficient time to
 diffuse to tensile stressed regions, thereby hydrogen effects are reduced. Similarly,
 accelerated fatigue crack growth rates appear to diminish as frequency increases and crack

 30

 growth rates approach those in air. Pressurization/depressurization of vessels will occur at a
 much slower rate where hydrogen has greater time to diffuse. Conservatively, it should be
 expected that crack growth rates will be accelerated due to hydrogen. This accelerated crack
 growth rate can be accounted for in design and is accounted for in stationary pressure vessel
 design for hydrogen vessels. As will be shown in Section 4.4, the total number of pressure
 fills will be relatively low in application as filling may occur every few days/weeks.
• Low temperature can greatly affect mechanical performance and in combination with
 hydrogen can further degrade materials. Hydrogen vessels on rolling stock will be exposed to
 extreme weather and in the case of liquid hydrogen, in contact with hydrogen at
 temperatures as low as 20 K (-424°F). Low temperatures, as required for transporting liquid
 hydrogen, do not appear to drastically degrade austenitic stainless steel elongation to failure
 or fatigue life compared to inert conditions (e.g., helium), but do show degradation in
 reduction of area. Tensile tests showed similar elongation to failure between hydrogen and
 helium at temperatures of 20 K (-424°F) and 25 K (415°F), but some secondary cracking
 was observed which suggests hydrogen is reducing plasticity. Charpy impact energy of
 hydrogen precharged 304 stainless did decrease at low temperatures compared to non-
 charged. The fatigue life curves at low temperatures all showed improved cycles to failure
 compared to room temperature due to the improved strength of the austenitic stainless
 steels at low temperature. Despite limited diffusion at low temperatures, fielded components
 will be exposed to hydrogen for long durations and therefore it should be assumed that
 hydrogen can diffuse throughout the material. Therefore, hydrogen precharged conditions
 would be the more conservative approach at representing fielded components.

4. ASSESSMENT OF DESIGN CYCLES FOR HYDROGEN STORAGE
 ON RAIL
In any application where repeated load cycles will be incurred, a fatigue assessment can be done to
calculate cycles to failure at a given stress amplitude. This assessment can then help define the
allowable design cycles which are reduced based on safety factors. Depending on the design code, a
safety factor for number of cycles or cycles to reach some critical crack length may be imposed. The
design approach would depend on the material, usage, and design code requirements. Stainless steel
tubing would be used in all cases to transport smaller volumes of hydrogen through the hydrogen
storage system with the large storage provided by steel or non-metallic pressure vessels or cryogenic
storage. The scope of this current review is on metallic vessels and therefore the analysis below
focuses on stainless steel and Cr-Mo steel vessels.
A short summary of some of the potentially relevant codes and regulations is included below. Using
the bounding accelerations found in the literature from the instrumented nuclear fuel cask
transportation, a simplified assessment was performed to calculate bending stresses on steel pressure
vessels and tubing that are commonly used for hydrogen storage. The results include estimated
design cycles based on different shock and vibration conditions.

4.1. Transportable Hydrogen Pressure Vessel Codes and Regulations
For cryogenic liquid, the 49 CFR 179 has general guidelines and design requirements for tank cars.
49 CFR 179.400 is specific to cryogenic liquid tank car tanks and seamless steel tanks transportation.
The DOT specific car would be 113A60W for liquid hydrogen and has a 304 or 304L stainless steel
liner and a carbon-steel outer shell. A minimum inner liner plate thickness 3/16” is required which is
identical to the liquefied natural gas (LNG) liquid natural gas tank car of 113C120W. The burst (240
psi), vent (60 psi) and test (60 psi) pressures are lower for the liquid hydrogen (LH2) tank compared
to the LNG tank according to 49 CFR 179.401-1. A hydrostatic test is required up to the test
pressure of the tank. Charpy impact testing is required of the materials at the service temperature but
there is no mention that the materials need to be exposed to hydrogen. In 49 CFR 179.400-13 there
is mention that the support system for the inner tank must be able to survive impacts without
loading up to 3 g vertical acceleration. There is no mention of fatigue analysis or testing required.
For gaseous hydrogen storage vessels, ISO 11120 [23] is an international standard used for design,
construction, and testing of refillable seamless tubes used to transport up to 3000 liters (water
volume) and would be applicable for hydrogen fuel storage in rail applications if steel liner vessels
were to be bundled in a multiple element gas container (MEGC). ISO 11120 [23] is used for
transportation cylinders commonly used on trucks and specifically states that the standard “does not
include consideration of any additional stresses that may occur during service or transport, e.g.
bending stresses, etc.,” and has no mention of fatigue in the standard.
Seamless steel cylinder 3AA and 3AAX (49 CFR 178.37) are common pressure vessels used in
transportation of gaseous hydrogen on trucks. This regulation does not discuss any fatigue
assessment. 3AAX cylinders are seamless steel cylinders with water capacity greater than 1000
pounds. The specification only requires that twice the static bending stress in filled condition plus
the longitudinal stress due to pressure must not exceed 80 percent of minimum yield strength.
Additionally, a static pressure test must be performed at 5/3 the service pressure. Therefore, only
static tests are employed.
However, the Specification for MEGCs found in 49 CFR 178.75, states that the tanks must be able
to withstand a vertical acceleration of 2 g, which is twice the maximum permissible gross mass (e.g.,

 33

the mass of the tank plus contents multiplied by the acceleration due to gravity). 49 CFR 178.75
requires the tank design take into account dynamic loading and fatigue due to repeated application
of loads. This identifies that a fatigue assessment should be performed.
As there does not appear to be clear guidance on fatigue analysis for DOT cylinders, a simplified
assessment of the design cycles to failure using ASME design curves was used on the major
hydrogen storage components (e.g., steel tanks and tubing). More detailed analysis incorporating
stress concentrations and finite element analysis should be performed to ensure a safe design.

4.2. Mechanical Loading Environments use for Fatigue Assessment of
 Hydrogen Storage Vessels in Rail
Based on the literature data for the rail transportation of the nuclear fuel cask in Section 2,
accelerations measured on the instrumented railcar were used to perform fatigue analyses. The
environments were based on upper bound accelerations for either the platform end (extreme case)
or cask location (more representative of hydrogen storage location). All accelerations used were
vertical accelerations and a bending stress was calculated based on the size, weight, and pressure
contained within the hydrogen pressure component. The four acceleration categories are:
 • Extreme coupling: 26 g was the overall max acceleration recorded as measured on the end
 of platform during coupling. This is an extreme event and does not account for any
 dampening that was measured in the cargo area where hydrogen components would likely be
 located.
 • Extreme shock: 8.9 g was measured on the Pitch and Bounce test at the TTC tests and was
 the most extreme shock condition other than coupling measured on the platform ends. This
 is an extreme event and does not account for any dampening that was measured in the cargo
 area where hydrogen components would likely be located.
 • Normal shock: 2 g would be an upper bound value for shock acceleration as experienced
 by the cradle and cask which is where hydrogen components would likely be positioned. The
 2 g is also consistent with the Specification for MEGCs found in 49 CFR 178.75 mentioned
 in Section 4.1.
 • Normal vibration: 1 g would be an upper bound case for normal vibration in both truck
 and rail for the cradle and cask which is where hydrogen components would likely be
 positioned.

4.7. Summary of Fatigue Design Cycle Assessments
A simplified fatigue assessment was performed on three different hydrogen components: gaseous
tubing (stainless steel), gaseous pressure vessel (Cr-Mo steel), cryogenic tanks for liquid hydrogen
(stainless steel). Four shock and vibration environments were considered which ranged from
extreme accelerations of 26 and 8.9 g to normal conditions of 2 and 1 g. The normal shock and
vibration environments all resulted in stress amplitudes that were well below the fatigue limit
suggesting infinite life. The extreme shock environments resulted in either infinite or finite design
life with the limiting case being the gaseous pressure vessel at 8,000 cycles at 26 g.
It should be noted that the stresses were based on bending which is directly dependent on the
unsupported length of the vessels or tubing. A reduction in the support distance would reduce the
bending stress. Similarly, the extreme accelerations were bounds taken from platform ends where no
dampening occurred. Normal shock and vibration acceleration values of 1 or 2 g are more
representative of the environment that would be experienced by hydrogen components as some
dampening would be expected. At these lower accelerations, infinite life was calculated for all
components.

5. GAPS IN RESEARCH
This section discusses additional research needed to understand the effects that the rail environment
can have on degradation of materials considered for hydrogen storage. A brief discussion of the
need for high fidelity stress measurements, testing at low temperatures and faster rates, and non-
metallic tanks is included.

5.1. Higher Fidelity Stress Measurements
Most of the literature for hydrogen pressure vessels is focused on understanding the stresses
imposed from pressure cycling of the component. These stresses are repeatable and bound by the
system operations. Additionally, the studies are typically focused on stationary vessels, therefore no
external sources of stress are considered in their fatigue assessments. When the external
environmental loading conditions become more extreme, it may be necessary to have higher fidelity
measurements of stresses imposed by shock, as will occur in rail applications. There is a notable gap
in the literature on instrumented tanks on different types of rolling stock such that accurate
estimates of accelerations and strains can be used for fatigue assessment. The literature does include
instrumentation of specific locations of nuclear fuel casks transported via rail, and the results can be
leveraged to some degree. However, these rail cars are unique compared to a hydrogen storage
rolling stock (including locomotives) and therefore uncertainties remain in interpreting the results.
 Recommendation: A study on the accelerations and strains of different types of rolling stock
 equipped with instrumented hydrogen fueling hardware (e.g., pressure vessels, tubes) would
 provide much needed data and greatly increase confidence and reduce conservatism in
 design. In addition, understanding the number of pressurizations that will occur in the life of
 a rail application will help determine how significant the filling procedure is in total life of
 pressurized components.

5.2. Testing Rate and Temperature Effects
Laboratory testing should mimic operating environments, when possible. This is particularly
important for assessing hydrogen embrittlement as it requires accumulation of hydrogen at tensile
stresses which means diffusion is critical. Both temperature and testing rate can influence hydrogen
accumulation at crack tips and test results may be influenced by limited hydrogen diffusion. Low
temperature testing with hydrogen, particularly in the liquid hydrogen temperature regime (e.g., 20 K
(-424°F)) is scarce in the literature; however, the majority of the results suggest the impact of
hydrogen is diminished at these temperatures, but it should be noted that laboratory tests are in
effect, short-term tests, so hydrogen-diffusion may be limited. Components in real-world
applications will be exposed to hydrogen continuously and hydrogen may be able to saturate the
material given the long-term exposures.
Similarly, testing at fast loading rates can result in insufficient time for hydrogen to accumulate in
tensile stress regions and the effects of hydrogen may be mitigated. The literature does suggest that
the influence of hydrogen is reduced at faster rates, but this does not mean it is completely removed.
The majority of hydrogen storage applications view the critical stress resulting from the
pressurization/depressurization of the pressure vessel which typically occurs at relatively slow rates,
where hydrogen effects are most pronounced. In rail applications, the external loading environment
becomes more extreme which may mean larger stresses may come from bending of the component
and these may occur at faster rates. Additionally, coupling of rolling stock is known to occur at
faster rates.

 45

 Recommendation: Research should be focused on understanding rate and temperature effects
 on damage of hydrogen containing components (i.e., does hydrogen embrittlement manifest
 when shock loading is applied?). It is recommended that test samples are hydrogen
 precharged prior to performing tests at variable testing rates or at low temperatures to
 ensure hydrogen diffusion is not the limiting factor. Faster rate impact testing should be
 performed in a manner to ensure the material has been exposed to hydrogen long enough to
 mimic conditions in the field (i.e., long-term exposure). Experiments should be performed at
 different loading rates to confirm that loading rates are not affecting testing results when in
 the presence of hydrogen. Fatigue-life curves could be developed on hydrogen-precharged
 materials at liquid hydrogen temperatures (i.e., 20 K (-424°F) which could be used for fatigue
 assessment of cryogenic tanks.

5.3. Non-metallic Lined Vessels
 Recommendation: Research on non-metallic lined vessel material in shock and vibration
 environments should be performed to evaluate if further degradation occurs in these
 extreme environments with hydrogen. This research could be combined with instrumented
 vessels to better understand the loading environment. The strength and toughness of
 polymers are significantly lower than steel, and shock may reduce life of the components
 differently than steels. Polyethylene material has not shown effects of hydrogen degradation
 in fatigue/fracture testing in gaseous hydrogen, but research is needed on variables such as
 frequency and shock loading rates. Polymers could be pre-exposed to hydrogen or
 performed in-situ in gas followed by testing over a range of loading rates to explore the
 influence of hydrogen and shock on mechanical performance.

6. CONCLUSION
In this report, a literature review was performed to identify loading environments typical of railroad
applications to investigate if there are overlaps with hydrogen embrittlement studies on steels. Three
important environments were identified that should be examined when considering large scale
hydrogen storage on rail: fatigue, loading rate effects, low temperature behavior. Austenitic stainless
steels and Cr-Mo pressure vessel steels are commonly used for high pressure tubing and liquid or
gaseous hydrogen storage. It was found that hydrogen has the most pronounced effect on fatigue
life at higher stresses, but the fatigue limits of steels are not affected by hydrogen; therefore, the
effects of hydrogen may be managed if stresses are kept low. Hydrogen embrittlement relies on
hydrogen diffusing to regions of tensile stress; therefore, in short-term laboratory experiments faster
rates or lower temperatures can make hydrogen diffusion a rate limiting step and reduce hydrogen
embrittlement effects. Ductility loss is reduced at faster loading rates. Similarly, as the temperature
approaches liquid hydrogen, 20 K (-424°F), the degradation of mechanical properties of austenitic
stainless steels is attributed more to the low temperature than the hydrogen. However, hydrogen still
results in some measurable degradation at both faster rates and low temperatures. Furthermore, as
materials in application will be in contact with hydrogen for extended periods of time, hydrogen
diffusion may not be a rate limiting step so efforts such as hydrogen precharging should be used to
ensure hydrogen is present throughout the entire thickness of material being analyzed.
Shock and vibration environments obtained from studies on instrumented nuclear fuel cask rail cars
were used to identify vertical accelerations associated with events common in rolling stock. Four
events (extreme coupling, extreme shock, normal shock, normal vibration) were categorized and a
fatigue assessment was performed to determine the design cycles based on simplified boundary
conditions where vertical acceleration imposed bending stresses on hydrogen components in the
horizontal position. Three components were examined: gaseous tubing (stainless steel), gaseous
pressure vessel (Cr-Mo steel), cryogenic liquid tanks (stainless steel). Design curves found in ASME
BPVC VIII-3, which do not explicitly account for hydrogen effects but have safety factors imposed,
were used for the stress-life fatigue analysis which were shown to bound the hydrogen fatigue-life
data in the literature. In low acceleration events (less than 2 g) such as normal shock and vibration,
the components all show infinite life as the stresses are well below the fatigue limit. The extreme
coupling event (26 g) resulted in a finite life of 8,000 cycles in the gaseous pressure vessel, which
represents the limiting design cycles of the components evaluated. It should be noted that these
fatigue assessments are based on simplified stress analysis. In addition, the fatigue assessments are
completely dependent on inputs like support spacing, which directly affects bending stress, and
accelerations, which were taken to be the extreme on platform ends. It was shown that reducing the
support spacing greatly improved design life. Acceleration values of <2 g were more typical on the
nuclear fuel cask and cradle which are most likely where hydrogen components would be located in
rail applications. Further analysis incorporating stress concentrations and finite element analysis
should be performed to obtain stresses more specific to actual designs.
Recommendations for further research:
 - Measure accelerations and strains on pressure components at relevant locations on railcars to
 improve the fidelity of stress analysis.
 - Quantify the magnitude of hydrogen embrittlement at liquid hydrogen temperatures,
 ensuring long-term exposure conditions are achieved during testing.
 - Evaluate fast rate loading effects on materials pre-exposed to hydrogen to determine if
 hydrogen degrades mechanical properties.

- Evaluate non-metallic liner materials exposed to hydrogen in shock and vibration
 environments.
