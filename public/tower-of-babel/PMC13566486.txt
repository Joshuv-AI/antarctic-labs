Can placental histology reveal preexisting hypertension in patients who develop preeclampsia?
Authors: Lydia Hadj Youcef, Clemence Jacquin, Pauline Orquevaux, Philippe Rieu, Rene Gabriel, Olivier Graesslin, Benjamin Birene, Vincent Dupont
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Hypertension can be both a cause and a consequence of preeclampsia, complicating diagnosis and postpartum management. Placental histopathology has been proposed as a tool to distinguish preeclampsia superimposed on chronic hypertension from de-novo disease, yet specific and reliable markers remain poorly defined.Objective:To evaluate the association between placental histological features and preexisting hypertension in a cohort of patients who developed preeclampsia.Methods:We conducted a retrospective analysis of 321 preeclamptic pregnancies at a tertiary center between January 1, 2020, and October 31, 2023. Preexisting hypertension was defined as the use of antihypertensive medication at the beginning of pregnancy. Placental histopathological evaluation was available for 223 patients. Histological lesions were classified according to established guidelines, with a focus on maternal vascular malperfusion features. Associations between placental lesions and preexisting hypertension were assessed using univariate and multivariate analyses.Results:The absence of placental infarction (47.4 vs. 61.1%, P = 0.02) and the presence of decidual arteriopathy (31.6 vs. 16.8%, P = 0.03) were significantly associated with preexisting hypertension. The combination of these two features was independently associated with preexisting hypertension (odds ratio = 5.90; 95% confidence interval 1.88–18.60; P < 0.01). This pattern demonstrated high specificity (96.2%) but low sensitivity (18.4%) for identifying preexisting hypertension.Conclusion:The presence of decidual arteriopathy in the absence of placental infarction is strongly associated with preexisting hypertension in preeclamptic patients. Although its low sensitivity limits its use as a screening tool, its high specificity suggests that it may serve as a useful indicator of underlying chronic hypertension when present. These findings support the potential role of placental histopathology as an adjunct to clinical evaluation in the postpartum assessment of hypertensive disorders.

INTRODUCTION

Preeclampsia is a multifactorial hypertensive disorder of pregnancy and remains a leading cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality worldwide, constituting a major public health concern [1–3]. Beyond its acute complications, preeclampsia is increasingly recognized as a marker of long-term cardiovascular risk for both mothers and their offspring [4]. Notably, women who develop preeclampsia have a tenfold increased risk of subsequent hypertension compared to those with uncomplicated pregnancies [3].

Among the numerous risk factors implicated in the development of preeclampsia, preexisting hypertension is particularly significant [5]. Preexisting hypertension not only predisposes women to preeclampsia but also raises important questions about the underlying nature and origin of their hypertensive state. In women of reproductive age, especially those presenting with preeclampsia, the possibility of secondary hypertension must be carefully evaluated. Common causes include chronic kidney disease, primary aldosteronism, pheochromocytoma, and renovascular hypertension [6].

Hence, hypertension may act as both a cause and a consequence of preeclampsia, complicating the clinical picture and making it difficult to discern the primary pathology. This challenge is especially pronounced during postpartum follow-up, particularly within nephrology and internal medicine settings. The diagnostic process is further hindered by overlapping clinical features and the absence of specific biomarkers to aid in evaluation [7,8].

In recent years, placental histopathology has emerged as a valuable tool for studying preeclampsia. This advancement has been facilitated by the development of standardized and reproducible classification systems for placental lesions [9]. Among the most widely adopted frameworks are the Amsterdam Placental Workshop Group Consensus Statement [10] and guidelines from the Society for Pediatric Pathology. These systems aim to harmonize diagnostic criteria across studies by categorizing key placental lesions, including maternal vascular malperfusion (MVM), fetal vascular malperfusion (FVM), and inflammatory lesions. Such classifications enable correlations between specific placental findings and distinct clinical phenotypes of preeclampsia [11,12].

The objective of this study was to investigate the association between placental histology and preexisting hypertension in a well characterized cohort of patients who developed preeclampsia.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and patients

We performed a retrospective study at University Hospital of Reims (France). All women hospitalized between January 1, 2020, and October 31, 2023, with a diagnosis of preeclampsia according to the National Hospital Discharge Database (PMSI) were screened. All medical charts, including both singleton and multiples pregnancies, were screened by two clinicians to only include patients who developed preeclampsia defined as the onset of hypertension after 20 weeks of gestation (SBP >140 mmHg and/or DBP >90 mmHg), associated with significant proteinuria (>300 mg/24 h). Women who did not meet this definition or for whom data were incomplete were excluded from the study.

Preexisting hypertension was defined as the use of antihypertensive medication at baseline, that is, from the beginning of the pregnancy. Blood pressure at the first prenatal visit was reviewed when available to confirm preexisting hypertension. Accordingly, we compared two groups of patients who developed preeclampsia: those with preexisting hypertension and those without.

Data collection

A comprehensive review of medical charts was conducted to collect the following data: maternal characteristics, including age, BMI, smoking status, and any history of cardiovascular, renal, or autoimmune diseases, thyroid disorders, prior miscarriage, fetal growth restriction (FGR), or preeclampsia. Pregnancy-related information was also gathered, such as whether the pregnancy was singleton or multiple, spontaneous or medically induced, and whether prophylactic aspirin and/or anticoagulants were used. Fetal outcomes included gestational age at birth, birth weight, and the occurrence of intrauterine or perinatal death. Clinical and biological parameters at admission were recorded, including the presence of key symptoms (headache, epigastric pain, edema, visual disturbances), the highest documented blood pressure, peak serum creatinine level during hospitalization, lowest platelet count, and highest aspartate aminotransferase (AST) level. Peripartum complications were assessed, including postpartum hemorrhage (defined as blood loss of ≥500 ml for vaginal delivery and ≥1000 ml for cesarean section), the occurrence of HELLP syndrome (Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, and Low Platelet count), eclampsia (defined as new-onset seizures in the context of preeclampsia), retroplacental hematoma, and maternal death. Finally, postpartum follow-up data included results from biological testing and placental pathological examinations.

Placental pathology

In accordance with a local standardized protocol, all placentas from pregnancies with pathological outcomes undergo systematic anatomopathological evaluation, following the guidelines recommended by the Collège Français de Pathologie. Approximately six to eight tissue sections per placenta were analyzed. This evaluation comprises both macroscopic and microscopic analyses. The macroscopic assessment includes measurements of placental weight and dimensions, evaluation of general appearance, description of the umbilical cord (including length, site of insertion and number of vessels), and documentation of any infarctions, massive Perivillous fibrinoid deposition, intervillous thrombosis, subchorionic thrombohematomas, basal decidual hematoma or marginal decidual hematoma [10]. All histological examinations were performed by a trained and experienced pathologist.

Statistics

For continuous variables, normality was assessed using Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous and categorical variables are reported as median (interquartile range), mean ± standard deviation or number (percentage), when appropriate. For continuous and categorical variables, univariate analysis was performed using unpaired two-tailed Mann–Whitney test or χ2 test with Yates correction, respectively. Multivariate analysis was performed using a multiple logistic regression model including all noncollinear variables with a P value less than 0.1 in univariate analysis and/or when considered clinically relevant. All estimations were performed using GraphPad Prism Version 9.5.1. (GraphPad Software). A P value less than 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Ethics and data availability

This study was conducted in accordance with current French legislation (CNIL and MR004). All data used in this study are openly available and appropriately cited.

Regarding obstetric history, patients with hypertension had a higher number of previous pregnancies, whereas the history of miscarriage and intrauterine growth restriction did not differ between groups.

Symptoms at admission, including headache, edema, and epigastric pain, were similarly reported in both groups (Table 1). SBP was slightly lower in patients with preexisting hypertension, although this difference was not statistically significant. In contrast, dBP was significantly higher in this group. Kidney function and aspartate aminotransferase levels at admission were comparable between groups, while platelet counts were higher among patients with preexisting hypertension.

Maternal and neonatal outcomes

No significant differences were observed in maternal outcomes between groups (Table 1). The incidence of postpartum hemorrhage was similar, as were rates of HELLP syndrome, eclampsia, placental abruption, and maternal death. Neonatal outcomes were likewise comparable, with no significant differences in gestational age at delivery, birth weight, or neonatal mortality.

Placental histopathology

Placental histopathological analysis was available for 223 patients, including 38 with preexisting hypertension (Table 2). Features of MVM were frequently observed. Decidual arteriopathy was significantly more common in patients with preexisting hypertension (Fig. 2), whereas placental infarctions were more prevalent among patients without preexisting hypertension.

Diagnostic performance of histological markers

The diagnostic performance of decidual arteriopathy and absence of placental infarction-evaluated individually and in combination-for identifying preexisting hypertension is shown in Table 4. Decidual arteriopathy alone demonstrated a sensitivity of 31.6%, specificity of 83.2%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 27.9%, and negative predictive value (NPV) of 85.6%. Absence of placental infarction showed a sensitivity of 57.9%, specificity of 62.2%, PPV of 23.9%, and NPV of 87.8%. When combined, the presence of decidual arteriopathy without placental infarction achieved a high specificity (96.2%) and PPV (50.0%), although sensitivity remained low (18.4%).

DISCUSSION

This study identifies a specific histopathological pattern, decidual arteriopathy in the absence of placental infarction, as significantly associated with preexisting hypertension in patients who developed preeclampsia. This combination, when present, may serve as a retrospective placental signature of underlying maternal vascular disease, distinct from the lesions typically observed in de novo preeclampsia.

The clinical differentiation between de-novo and preexisting hypertension after preeclampsia remains a persistent diagnostic challenge, particularly when preexisting hypertension is undiagnosed or subclinical at the onset of pregnancy. Our findings suggest that placental histology, long considered descriptive and confirmatory, may have previously underutilized potential in refining diagnostic clarity in such cases. In particular, the combination of decidual arteriopathy without infarction demonstrated high specificity, supporting its role as a marker of chronic rather than acute placental pathology. Importantly, although the combination of decidual arteriopathy in the absence of placental infarction demonstrated high specificity for preexisting hypertension, its sensitivity was low. Therefore, it should not be considered a screening marker but rather a highly specific indicator that may help “rule in” underlying chronic hypertension when present.

These results align with and extend prior research documenting a higher prevalence of MVM features in women with preexisting hypertension [11,13]. However, most previous studies have focused on individual lesions in isolation. By emphasizing the diagnostic value of lesion combinations, our study provides a new interpretive framework that may help distinguish phenotypes within the heterogeneity of preeclampsia. Specifically, we propose that the absence of infarction, an acute, often catastrophic lesion, may indicate a more stable, chronic vascular insult, while the presence of decidual arteriopathy reflects longstanding maladaptation of the spiral arteries.

Histologically, decidual arteriopathy encompasses fibrinoid necrosis, mural hypertrophy, and atheroma, features indicative of sustained endothelial injury and impaired vascular remodeling. These changes are typical in women with preexisting hypertension, but can also occur in other conditions such as diabetes, thrombophilia, or autoimmune syndromes [10][14]. Thus, while not pathognomonic, their interpretive value increases in the absence of acute features such as infarction or thrombosis. Our findings therefore support a nuanced, context-dependent reading of placental pathology. It is also important to note that similar histopathological changes may be observed in other maternal conditions, including diabetes, thrombophilia, and autoimmune diseases. Due to the limited sample size, we could not perform a subgroup analysis excluding these comorbidities. As such, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and future studies with larger cohorts should aim to assess the robustness of these associations after exclusion of major comorbid conditions.

From a translational perspective, these observations hold clinical relevance. First, they may offer retrospective insight into maternal cardiovascular risk, particularly in patients whose hypertensive status was ambiguous during pregnancy. Second, they underscore the potential utility of placental examination not only as a diagnostic adjunct but also as a risk stratification tool. Identifying patients with histopathological signs of chronic vascular disease could prompt timely referral to internal medicine or nephrology services, ensuring adequate follow-up and intervention to mitigate long-term cardiovascular morbidity [4,8]. The potential implication of these findings for the identification of secondary hypertension deserves further consideration. In women of reproductive age, secondary causes of hypertension are not uncommon and may remain undiagnosed prior to pregnancy. The identification of a histopathological signature suggestive of preexisting hypertension could therefore serve as an additional trigger for targeted etiological work-up in the postpartum period.

Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration. The retrospective and single-center design may introduce selection bias and restrict generalizability. Although our population represents a regional cohort consistent with national epidemiological data [3], validation in larger and more diverse populations is necessary. Furthermore, the lack of postpartum blood pressure monitoring limits our ability to confirm the persistence or resolution of hypertension in individual cases. It is plausible that some women classified as normotensive had undiagnosed or masked hypertension, potentially blurring distinctions between study groups. Interobserver variability in placental histology is another concern, particularly for lesions like arteriopathy, which can exhibit focal or regressive patterns. Also, although all placenta samples were analyzed by an expert pathologist following standardized protocols, full concordance in interpretation is rarely achievable in practice [10]. Additionally, placental lesions are temporally indeterminate: decidual arteriopathy may represent either chronic baseline disease or early placental maladaptation. Without longitudinal data on maternal vascular function, establishing causality remains speculative. Finally, several limitations related to data availability should be acknowledged. First, we could not systematically differentiate between maternal and fetal indications for delivery, as this information was not consistently documented in the medical records. In addition, key fetal parameters such as umbilical cord pH and detailed Doppler findings were not consistently available in this retrospective dataset, limiting our ability to explore associations between placental histology and fetal condition.

In conclusion, our study identifies a specific placental histopathological pattern-decidual arteriopathy in the absence of infarction-that is strongly associated with preexisting hypertension in preeclamptic patients. While its low sensitivity limits its utility as a screening tool, its high specificity suggests that it may serve as a useful retrospective indicator of underlying chronic hypertension when present. These findings support the potential role of placental pathology as an adjunct tool in refining the phenotypic classification of preeclampsia and guiding postpartum evaluation. Further prospective studies are needed to validate these results and to determine their clinical applicability, particularly in the context of systematic cardiovascular and etiological assessment after pregnancy.
