Universal purification dynamics in real non-unitary quantum processes
Federico Gerbino, Donghoon Kim, Guido Giachetti, Andrea De Luca, Xhek Turkeshi

Abstract
We study purification dynamics in monitored quantum processes governed by ensembles of quantum circuits in different random-matrix symmetry classes. We analyze the universal aspects that emerge away from the measurement induced phase transition and inside the volume/weak measurement phase and in the scaling limit of large time and Hilbert space dimension. We present two toy models that reveal two complementary visions and provide quantitative access to universal scaling: i) a discrete-time dynamic in which each time step corresponds to multiplication by a Gaussian random matrix; ii) weak continuous-time monitoring that induces a Dyson brownian motion of the eigenvalues of the density matrix. The first approach provides an algebraic characterization based on rotational invariance emerging in Kraus’s operator space, focusing in particular on the unitary and orthogonal cases, respectively β = 2 \beta=2 and β = 1 \beta=1 , with β \beta the Dyson random-matrix index. The second approach, on the other hand, allows for a unified treatment for any β \beta , thanks to the mapping of the Fokker-Planck evolution of eigenvalues onto the Calogero-Sutherland integrable Hamiltonian diagonalized in terms of Jack polynomials. We provide explicit expressions for the universal decrease of Rényi entropies. We show that, approaching the universal scaling limit, numerical simulations of different models agree with each other and with our theoretical predictions. Our results clarify the existence of different classes of universality for the purification process in hybrid quantum systems, accessible in random circuit architectures and weak measurement protocols.

In the weak-measurement phase, purification occurs on a timescale

A useful way to rationalize the exponential growth of the purification time in the weak-measurement phase is to interpret measurement-induced phase transitions as genuine second-order transitions in an enlarged space of replicated degrees of freedom.
Within this perspective, the competition between measurements and coherent dynamics can be viewed as a phenomenon of symmetry breaking in the group of permutations that exchange replicas of the system.
The strong-measurement phase corresponds to a symmetric (disordered) phase, in which measurements efficiently couple different replicas and rapidly drive the system toward a pure state.
By contrast, in the weak-measurement phase coherent dynamics dominates, leading to a symmetry-broken phase in replica space, where different replicas effectively decouple.
In such a broken-symmetry phase, purification requires rare collective fluctuations that restore the symmetry by coupling replicas over the entire system.
These processes play a role analogous to instanton events connecting distinct vacua in symmetry-broken phases, and their probability is therefore exponentially suppressed in the system size.
As a consequence, the characteristic purification time (
1

This exponential separation of timescales has been analyzed from several complementary viewpoints, including quantum trajectory approaches, statistical-mechanics mappings, and information-theoretic arguments

Physically, it reflects the extreme inefficiency of measurements in extracting global information when unitary scrambling remains effective over long times.

The existence of such a long purification timescale suggests that, in the weak-measurement phase, purification becomes the slowest relevant dynamical process in the system.
All microscopic relaxation and scrambling mechanisms occur on parametrically shorter timescales, while the system remains close to maximally mixed for an exponentially long time.
This observation naturally leads to the expectation that purification dynamics in this regime should exhibit a form of universality, becoming insensitive to microscopic details such as lattice geometry, spatial dimensionality, or the precise structure of local interactions.
Indeed, recent work has argued that, upon rescaling time by the purification time

A central message of this work is that the universal features of purification dynamics are controlled by an emergent symmetry group associated with this effective random-matrix description.
In the most generic situation, coherent dynamics explores the full unitary group acting on the many-body Hilbert space, leading to an effective symmetry group

The primary goal of the present work is to show that this reduction of symmetry gives rise to a distinct universality class of purification dynamics.
We demonstrate that purification processes invariant under orthogonal transformations exhibit scaling behavior that is qualitatively different from that of the unitary class, despite sharing the same microscopic phenomenology of exponentially slow purification.
These differences are directly visible in the scaling behavior of spectral observables, such as the Rényi entropies.
As a representative example, we find that in the scaling regime the average Rényi entropies behave at short rescaled times

These differences ultimately originate from the symmetry constraints imposed on the effective stochastic dynamics.

To establish these results, we develop two complementary theoretical descriptions of universal purification dynamics.
The first is based on a discrete-time random-matrix formulation, in which the effect of monitoring is encoded in random Kraus operators drawn from appropriate matrix ensembles.
In this approach, universal scaling functions emerge from the algebraic and combinatorial structure of the effective interactions between replicated degrees of freedom, with the nature of the symmetry group determining the relevant mathematical framework.
The second description relies on a continuous-time weak-measurement limit, in which monitoring induces an infinitesimal stochastic evolution of the density matrix.
In this limit, the dynamics of the density matrix eigenvalues is governed by a stochastic process closely related to Dyson Brownian motion, whose associated Fokker–Planck operator maps onto the Hamiltonian of the Calogero–Sutherland model.
This formulation naturally encompasses different symmetry classes through the Dyson index
\beta

In addition to developing the theoretical framework, we provide analytical and numerical evidence that purification dynamics with real-valued monitoring protocols converges to the same universal scaling functions across different microscopic models, confirming the existence of a well-defined orthogonal universality class.

I
Framework, averages, and scaling limit

We study the purification dynamics of a finite-dimensional quantum system undergoing monitored evolution.
The state of the system is described by a density matrix
\rho
, initially taken to be highly mixed.
Throughout this work we focus on systems without conservation laws, for which the natural initial condition is the maximally mixed state
\rho(0)=\mathbb{1}/q
, where
q
denotes the dimension of the many-body Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, exponentially large in the system size
L
. (We refer to 1D systems for simplicity’s sake, but the discussion is not limited to them.)
Purification is then defined as the stochastic process by which
\rho
evolves toward a rank-one projector at long times.

At the microscopic level, the evolution over a finite time-step
\Delta t
is described by a quantum channel
\mathcal{C}
, which can always be written in terms of a set of Kraus operators
\{\hat{K}_{a}\}_{a}
as

\mathcal{C}_{\Delta t}[\rho]=\sum_{a}\hat{K}_{a}\rho\hat{K}_{a}^{\dagger},\qquad\sum_{a}\hat{K}_{a}^{\dagger}\hat{K}_{a}=\mathbb{1}.

(3)

Here the index
a
labels the outcomes of generalized measurements, as well as possible sources of classical randomness or noise.
The specific nature of these outcomes will not be important in the following, and the sum over
a
should be understood in a generalized sense, possibly including integrations over a continuous probability space, as we will see in practical cases.

Conditioning on a given outcome
a
leads to an unraveling of the quantum channel, in which the density matrix undergoes the stochastic update

\rho\;\longrightarrow\;\rho_{a}=\frac{\check{\rho}_{a}}{\operatorname{Tr}(\check{\rho}_{a})},\qquad\check{\rho}_{a}=\hat{K}_{a}\rho\hat{K}_{a}^{\dagger},

(4)

where
\check{\rho}
denotes the unnormalized density matrix and the probability
\mbox{Prob}(a)=\operatorname{Tr}(\check{\rho}_{a})
, in accordance with Born’s rule.
By iterating this evolution for several time-steps, we obtain a quantum trajectory conditioned on the time series of outcomes
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{\mathsf{t}})
up to time
t=\mathsf{t}\Delta t
, with
\mathsf{t}
the (integer) number of time-steps, whose unnormalized density matrix

\check{\rho}(t=\mathsf{t}\Delta t):=\check{\rho}_{\mathbf{a}_{\mathsf{t}}}=K_{a_{\mathsf{t}}}\ldots K_{a_{2}}K_{a_{1}}\rho(t=0)K_{a_{1}}^{{\dagger}}K_{a_{2}}^{{\dagger}}\ldots K_{a_{\mathsf{t}}}^{{\dagger}}

(5)

from which
\rho_{\mathbf{a}_{\mathsf{t}}}=\check{\rho}_{\mathbf{a}_{\mathsf{t}}}/\operatorname{Tr}[\check{\rho}_{\mathbf{a}_{\mathsf{t}}}]
and the probability of the whole trajectory is
\mbox{Prob}(\mathbf{a}_{\mathsf{t}})=\operatorname{Tr}[\check{\rho}_{\mathbf{a}_{\mathsf{t}}}]
.

To quantify the purity of the state along the quantum trajectory, one can look at the spectrum of
\rho_{\mathbf{a}_{\mathsf{t}}}
. A general set of indicators consists of the moments

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}[\rho]:=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho]^{r_{1}}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{2}]^{r_{2}}\ldots=\prod_{j=2}^{\infty}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{j}]^{r_{j}}

(6)

where we introduce the notation for integer partitions

\mathbf{r}=\{r_{1},r_{2},\ldots\}=1^{r_{1}}2^{r_{2}}\ldots,\quad\mbox{with }r_{j}\in\mathbb{N}\;,\quad|\mathbf{r}|:=\sum_{j}jr_{j}\;.

(7)

In the last equality of (

), we used that

is irrelevant for normalized density matrices with
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho]=1
. The moment
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}[\rho]
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree

in the entries of
\rho

These moments provide a

e^{(1-n)kS_{n}[\rho]}:=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{n}]^{k}=\mathcal{P}_{n^{k}}[\rho]

(8)

where, in agreement with (
6

7
), the subscript

denotes a sequence

whose only non-zero element is

Consistently, the von Neumann entropy can be interpreted as the formal limit
S_{1}[\rho]\equiv\lim_{n\to 1}S_{n}[\rho]
.
One is interested in analyzing the behavior of the moments
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}[\rho]
on a typical quantum trajectory. Quite generally, we can say that for a highly mixed state,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}[\rho]
is exponentially small in the system size, e.g. for the maximally mixed state
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}[\mathbb{1}/q]=q^{\sum_{j}r_{j}(1-j)}
. In contrast, for a pure state

During the non-unitary stochastic dynamics (
5
), the density matrix evolves from a mixed state to a pure one and the purification dynamics can be characterized by the growth in time of the moments
M_{\mathbf{r}}[\rho]
and

represents the characteristic time scale for such a process.
The details of this process of growth of moments (and therefore of purification itself) depend on how the different realizations of trajectories
\mathbf{a}
are weighted. Two natural choices are

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}(t)\right\rangle_{\rm BR}:=\sum_{\mathbf{a}_{\mathsf{t}}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}[\rho_{\mathbf{a}_{\mathsf{t}}}]\mbox{Prob}(\mathbf{a}_{\mathsf{t}})\;,

Born’s rule (BR)

(9a)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}(t)\right\rangle_{\rm FM}:=\frac{1}{Z_{\rm FM}}\sum_{\mathbf{a}_{\mathsf{t}}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}[\rho_{\mathbf{a}_{\mathsf{t}}}]\;,

Forced measurements (FM)

(9b)

where

is chosen so that

. In practice, in Eq. (

As explained in the introduction,

\mathcal{S}_{n}^{(N)}(x):=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,L\to\infty\\

(12)

A crucial assumption underlying this framework is the persistence of strong scrambling throughout the purification process.
Because measurements are inefficient in the weak-measurement phase, unitary dynamics thoroughly scrambles the system between successive purification events.
More specifically, let us assume that the time window
\Delta t
in (
3
) is such that
t_{\rm micro}(L)\ll\Delta t\ll t_{P}(L)
, where

refers broadly to a microscopic scale of scrambling and relaxation. For example, estimating

\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{U}(q)

\mathcal{G}
is reduced to just a subgroup of

\mathcal{G}

\mathcal{G}
and the calculation of the scaling functions
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}^{(N)}(x)

This symmetry structure admits two complementary theoretical descriptions that are well-captured by the toy models we will introduce in the next sections.

) amounts to choose independent matrices at different timesteps, we have

|{\check{\rho}^{(N)}(t=\mathsf{t}\Delta t)}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\!\left[(K_{\mathsf{t}}\otimes K_{\mathsf{t}}^{\ast})^{\otimes N}\right]}_{=:T}|{\check{\rho}^{(N)}((\mathsf{t}-1)\Delta t)}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle=T^{\mathsf{t}}|{\check{\rho}^{(N)}(0)}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle\,.

(23)

where
T\in\operatorname{End}((\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{\ast})^{\otimes N})
can be interpreted as a transfer matrix. In principle, the transfer matrix
T
has huge size
q^{2N}\times q^{2N}
. However, the random matrix structure of this problem allows us to highlight that the effective size of the matrix
T
is actually much smaller. The details of this reduction depend on the ensemble of random matrices under consideration, so we will consider separately the fundamental case
\beta=2
(already analyzed in Ref.
27
) and then the case
\beta=1
.

II.1
Complex Ginibre ensemble
(\beta=2)

In this section, we consider the case where all entries of
K
are complex numbers and the only non-vanishing correlations can be read in Eq. (
14
). Thus, the matrix elements of the transfer matrix
T
can be computed by a simple application of the Wick’s theorem: this amounts to all possible ways to contract one entry from the set of
N

K
’s with one entry from the set of
N

K^{\ast}
,
leading to

T:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[(K_{\mathsf{t}}\otimes K_{\mathsf{t}}^{\ast})^{\otimes N}\right]=q^{-N}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathsf{S}_{N}}|{\sigma}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle\langle\hskip-2.27621pt\langle{\sigma}|

(24)

where
|{\sigma}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle
labels the permutation states defined in Eq. (
20
). We see that the transfer matrix effectively spans a set of
|\mathsf{S}_{N}|=N!
states. However, a simple inspection shows that these states are not orthogonal and give rise to the
N!\times N!
Gram matrix

G_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}^{(\beta=2)}:=\langle\hskip-2.27621pt\langle{\sigma}|{\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle=q^{N-d_{N}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})}\;,\qquad\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathsf{S}_{N}

(25)

where
d_{N}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
is the transposition distance, i.e. the minimal number of transpositions connecting
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathsf{S}_{N}
. In the following we will omit the superscript
\beta=2
when there is no ambiguity.
In Fig.
1
we graphically represent the evolution of a single instance
|{\check{\rho}_{t}^{\otimes N}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle
, and its average
|{\check{\rho}_{t}^{(N)}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle
, leading to the transfer matrix
T
Eq. (
24
) and the Gram matrix elements
G^{(\beta=2)}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}
(
25
) for the symmetry class
\beta=2
.

Inserting Eqs. (
23
) and (
24
) in Eq. (
22
), we see that the calculation of the moments reduces to

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}[\check{\rho}]\operatorname{Tr}[\check{\rho}]^{N-|\mathbf{r}|}\right]=q^{-N(\mathsf{t}+1)}\sum_{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{\mathsf{t}}}G_{\sigma(\mathbf{r}^{(N)}),\sigma_{\mathsf{t}}}\ldots G_{\sigma_{2},\sigma_{1}}G_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{0}=\mathbf{1}}=q^{-N(\mathsf{t}+1)}[G^{\mathsf{t}+1}]_{\sigma(\mathbf{r}^{(N)}),\mathbf{1}}\;,

(26)

We thus see that the calculation of the moments has been reduced to the calculation of the matrix elements of the Gram matrix of reduced (and
q
-independent) size
N!\times N!
. The emergence of permutation states from the average on an ensemble of random matrices is a crucial mechanism underlying scrambling in ergodic quantum dynamics. In this case, there is a second fundamental ingredient towards universality: we are interested in studying purification dynamics in the scaling limit (
11
) where both the size of the system and time are large. From Eq. (
25
) we have the expansion

G_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}^{(\beta=2)}=q^{N}\left(\delta_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{q}A_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}^{(\beta=2)}+O(q^{-2})\right)

(27)

where
A
denotes the adjacency matrix of the transposition graph, i.e.

A_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}^{(\beta=2)}=\begin{cases}1&d_{N}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=1\;,\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}\;.

(28)

We define the purification time scale as the cost associated to the elementary step from
\mathbf{1}
to the transposition
(12)
, in units of
\Delta t
, thus

\frac{\Delta t}{t_{\rm P}}=\frac{G_{(12),\mathbf{1}}^{(\beta=2)}}{G_{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}}^{(\beta=2)}}=q^{-1}\;.

(29)

Therefore, in the scaling limit (
11
),
x=t/t_{\rm P}=\mathsf{t}/q
and accounting for the normalization factor, we obtain the expression

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}^{(N)}(x;\beta=2)=\frac{[e^{xA^{(\beta=2)}}]_{\sigma(\mathbf{r}^{(N)}),\mathbf{1}}}{[e^{xA^{(\beta=2)}}]_{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}}}\;,\qquad\sigma\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{r}^{(N)}}

(30)

where
[e^{xA^{(\beta=2)}}]_{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}}
is a normalization corresponding to the
N
-th moment of the trace.
We can therefore see that the calculation of moments is reduced to the one of the elements of an
N!\times N!
matrix. In this way, the combinatorial interpretation becomes clear: expanding in powers of
x
, we obtain terms of the type
[A^{\ell}]_{\sigma(\mathbf{r}^{(N)}),\mathbf{1}}
, with
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
; since
A
is the adjacency matrix of the transposition graph, these terms are clearly identifiable as the number of paths of length
\ell
connecting two specific nodes, a quantity also known as Hurwitz multiplicity
127
;
126
.

Eq. (
30
) provides an operational way to calculate moments in generalized ensembles defined by the index
N
, whenever
N>|\mathbf{r}|
, in terms of finite-dimensional matrix traces. However, the most physically interesting cases are the specific cases corresponding to Born’s rule (
N\to 1
) and forced measurements (
N\to 0
). Extracting these behaviors from the integer values calculable via Eq. (
30
) is a non-rigorous and often heuristic procedure called the replica trick.
As explained in Ref.
27
, there are various ways to obtain predictions in these regimes: one possibility is to consider the perturbative expansion at small
x
in terms of Hurwitz multiplicities. As we will see, in some cases, the latter admit a simple functional expression in terms of the parameter
N
(e.g., a polynomial
128
;
103
) for which it is straightforward to take the limit
N\to 0
or
N\to 1
. An alternative approach is to consider appropriate generating functions based on the summation of all integer values of
N
. This approach has the advantage of providing a prediction for arbitrary values of the scaling parameter
x
, but it is technically more difficult. An example of its use is provided in
27
for the calculation of the von Neumann entropy in the Born’s rule case, using that, in that case, both the Rényi index
n
and the replica index
N
need to be sent to
1
, so one can consider
\lim_{N\to 1}\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(N)}
in Eq. (
12
).

) are represented by blueish rectangles.

II.2
Real Ginibre ensemble
(\beta=1)

We will now extend the previous discussion to the case where the matrix elements of each matrix
K
are real numbers, still assuming that Eq. (
14
) holds for the covariance between the matrix elements. However, since the elements are real,
K^{\ast}=K
and thus we have that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[K_{\mu\nu}K_{\gamma\delta}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[K_{\mu\nu}^{\ast}K_{\gamma\delta}\right]=\frac{1}{q}\delta_{\mu\gamma}\delta_{\nu\delta}\;,\qquad\forall\ \mu,\nu,\gamma,\delta=1,\dots,q\,.

(31)

The effect of this additional non-zero term manifests itself in the calculation of the transfer matrix
T
using Wick’s theorem: while for
\beta=2
in Eq. (
24
), the only relevant contractions were those between a matrix
K
and its complex conjugate
K^{\ast}
, in this case, all possible contractions between the
2N
matrices must be considered.
Applying Eq. (
31
), one has

T:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[(K_{\mathsf{t}}\otimes K_{\mathsf{t}}^{\ast})^{\otimes N}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[K_{\mathsf{t}}^{\otimes 2N}\right]=q^{-N}\sum_{p\in\mathsf{P}_{N}}|{p}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle\langle\hskip-2.27621pt\langle{p}|\,,

(32)

where we introduced the pairing states

|{p}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle=\sum_{a_{1},\dots,a_{2N}=1}^{q}\left(\prod_{(i,j)\in p}\delta_{a_{i},a_{j}}\right)\ket{a_1,\dots, a_{2N}}\quad\in\quad\mathcal{(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{\ast})^{\otimes N}}\,,

(33)

where
p=\{(i_{1},i_{2}),\dots,(i_{2N-1},i_{2N})\}
is an element of the set of pairings
\mathsf{P}_{N}
of
I
in (
19
).
There are two crucial observations about the set of pairings. First, the set of permutation states
|{\sigma}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle
in (
20
) are a subset of the set of pairings
|{p}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle
, corresponding to pairs
(i,i^{\prime})
only joining starred and unstarred elements in
I
, so formally
\mathsf{S}_{N}\subset\mathcal{P}_{N}
. Secondly, the representation of a pairing
p=\{(i_{1},i_{2}),\ldots\}
can be interpreted as the decomposition into cycles of a permutation of
\mathsf{S}_{2N}
composed exclusively of
2-
cycles. In other words, this implies

\mathsf{P}_{N}=\mathcal{C}_{2^{N}}\subset\mathsf{S}_{2N}\quad\to\quad|\mathsf{P}_{N}|=\frac{(2N)!}{N!2^{N}}\;.

(34)

where
|\ldots|
denotes the cardinality of a finite set and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{r}}
denotes the conjugacy class in the permutation group with cycle structure
\mathbf{r}
.

Repeating the derivation in the previous section, we see that the moments can still be expressed by Eq. (
26
), but with a larger Gram matrix whose expression takes the form

G_{p,p^{\prime}}^{(\beta=1)}=\langle\hskip-2.27621pt\langle{p}|{p^{\prime}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle=q^{N-d_{2N}(p,p^{\prime})/2}\;,\qquad p,p^{\prime}\in\mathsf{P}_{N}\,.

(35)

where the pairings
p,p^{\prime}
are interpreted as elements in
\mathsf{S}_{2N}
and

d_{2N}(p,p^{\prime})
is the corresponding transposition distance.
To better clarify the origin of this equation, a graphical analysis of the case
N=2
is depicted in Fig.
2
.
Note that Eq. (
35
) reduces to Eq. (
25
) when the pairings
p=\sigma,p^{\prime}=\sigma^{\prime}
are permutations in
\mathsf{S}_{N}
, so that
G^{(\beta=2)}
is a submatrix of
G^{(\beta=1)}
.
In Fig.
1
, moreover, we represent the transfer matrix
T
Eq. (
32
) and the Gram matrix elements
G_{p,p^{\prime}}^{(\beta=1)}
for the real case
\beta=1
, as compared to the complex case
\beta=2
previously described.
Then, the calculation of the moments can once again be performed in terms of matrix elements of powers of
G
, using Eq. (
26
).

Similarly to Eq. (
27
), also for
\beta=1
, the Gram matrix admits the large-
q
expansion

G_{p,p^{\prime}}^{(\beta=1)}=q^{N}\left(\delta_{p,p^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{q}A_{p,p^{\prime}}^{(\beta=1)}+O(q^{-2})\right)

(36)

where we introduced the adjacency matrix in the pairing graph

A_{p,p^{\prime}}^{(\beta=1)}=\begin{cases}1&d_{2N}(p,p^{\prime})=2\;,\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}\;.

(37)

Note that conservation of parity in
\mathsf{S}_{2N}
imposes that the distance between pairings
p,p^{\prime}\in\mathsf{P}_{N}\subset\mathsf{S}_{2N}
is necessarily even; correspondingly,
the matrix
G^{(\beta=1)}
only depends on integer powers of
q
and
A^{(\beta=1)}
connects elements with the minimal non-vanishing distance, being
2
. As a consequence, consistently with Eq. (
29
), even in this case we can identify the purification time
t_{\rm P}=q\Delta t
and obtain in the scaling limit of fixed
x=t/t_{\rm P}
an expression analogous to Eq. (
30
)

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}^{(N)}(x;\beta=1)=\frac{[e^{xA^{(\beta=1)}}]_{\sigma(\mathbf{r}^{(N)}),\mathbf{1}}}{[e^{xA^{(\beta=1)}}]_{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}}}\;,\qquad\sigma\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{r}^{(N)}}

(38)

where the elements
\sigma(\mathbf{r}^{(N)})
and
\mathbf{1}\in\mathsf{S}_{N}\subset\mathcal{P}_{N}
.

III
General
G
-invariant ensembles and the commutant structure

We now present a unified formulation that explains the origin of the effective transfer matrices derived in the previous sections and further clarifies why their structure is universal. The style and content of this section are more mathematical, and readers who are less interested in these aspects may choose to skip reading it.
The key ingredient is invariance of the Kraus ensemble under a compact subgroup
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{U}(q)
. Once again, we assume to be in the volume phase of a measurement-induced phase transition emerging in the limit of large
q
, where the dimension of the group
\mathcal{G}
becomes large. We will keep the discussion about a generic group
\mathcal{G}
, specifying how it specializes to the situations treated in the cases
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{U}(q)
and
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{O}(q)
. We assume that each timestep is described by a set
\{K_{a}\}
of Kraus operators, with the crucial property, justified in the previous sections, that the ensemble is invariant under the action of a compact group
\mathcal{G}
sampled with Haar measure:

\{K_{a}\}\;\overset{d}{=}\;\{UK_{a}V\},\qquad U,V\sim\text{Haar}(\mathcal{G}).

(39)

where the equality holds in distribution sense.
Then, one can once again compute the moments from the powers of the transfer matrix

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}^{(N)}}[\check{\rho}]\right]=\frac{1}{q}\langle\hskip-2.27621pt\langle{\sigma(\mathbf{r}^{(N)})|T^{t}}|{\mathbf{1}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle\;,\qquad T=\mathbb{E}\!\left[(K\otimes K^{\dagger})^{\otimes N}\right]\;.

(40)

We introduce the
commutant algebra

\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}:=\{X\in\operatorname{End}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N})\;:\;[X,U^{\otimes N}]=0\ \ \forall U\in\mathcal{G}\}\simeq\{X\in(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{\ast})^{\otimes N}\;:\;(U\otimes U^{\ast})^{\otimes N}|{X}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle=|{X}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle\ \ \forall U\in\mathcal{G}\}

(41)

where in the second equality we rewrote the same notion using the vectorized notation (
16
) (after replication) to show that
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}
can be interpreted as the space left invariant by the replicated action of the group
\mathcal{G}
.
In general, using (
39
), one has immediately that the transfer matrix

R(U)T=TR(V)\;,\quad\forall U,V\in\mathcal{G}\quad\Rightarrow\quad T\;\in\;\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(N){\dagger}}\simeq\operatorname{End}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)})

(42)

This already implies the already observed reduction for the action of the transfer matrix from the huge space
(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{\ast})^{\otimes N}
to the commutant vector space
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}
.

To proceed further, it should be noted that both the transfer matrix
T
in Eq. (
40
) and the symmetry transformations
(U\otimes U^{\ast})^{\otimes N}
have a structure consisting of the repetition of the exact same object across different replicas. This property allows for an additional symmetry consisting of replica exchanges. In the most general case,
K
and
K^{\ast}
(and analogously for
U
and
U^{\ast}
) are different from each other, but it is still possible to freely permute the replicas corresponding to the starred/unstarred indices in (
19
). This identifies a group of discrete symmetries equal to
S_{N}\times S_{N}
. This group of symmetries can be enlarged in certain cases, such as in the case of real Kraus matrices
K=K^{\ast}
, in which case it is possible to permute freely all
2N
replicas indiscriminately, giving rise to
S_{2N}
. In general, we assume that there exists a discrete symmetry group
\mathsf{G}_{N}\supseteq S_{N}\times S_{N}
that acts on
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}
through a
representation
R:\mathsf{G}\to\operatorname{End}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)})
and such that

[(U\otimes U^{\ast})^{\otimes N},R(g)]=[T,R(g)]=0\;,\quad\forall g\in\mathsf{G}_{N}\;,\quad U\in\mathcal{G}

(43)

A natural consequence of this is that the action of
R(g)
preserves the invariant states. Starting from the reference state
|{\mathbf{1}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle
, one must have
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}\supseteq\operatorname{span}[R(g)|{\mathbf{1}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle,\;g\in\mathsf{G}_{N}]
. We will assume that in practice, the equality holds, i.e. the entire structure of the commutant space can be obtained by using the action of the group
\mathsf{G}_{N}
on the reference vector
|{\mathbf{1}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle
.
This is a compatibility requirement between the group
\mathcal{G}
and the discrete symmetry group among replicas
\mathsf{G}_{N}
, that in the cases
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{U}(q)
and
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{O}(q)
follows from Schur-Weyl duality.

In practice, the basis consisting of the states
R(g)|{\mathbf{1}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle
contains redundancies, for example because there may exist
g,g^{\prime}\in\mathsf{G}_{N}
such that
R(g)|{\mathbf{1}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle=R(g^{\prime})|{\mathbf{1}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle
. To take this into account, we introduce the stabilizer group

\mathsf{H}_{N}:=\{h\in\mathsf{G}_{N}\;|\;R(h)|{\mathbf{1}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle=|{\mathbf{1}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle\}

(44)

We assume that for sufficiently large
q
, there are no other linear dependencies between elements of the type
R(g)|{\mathbf{1}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle
. In this case, we can identify

\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}\equiv\mathbb{C}[\mathsf{G}_{N}/\mathsf{H}_{N}]=\operatorname{Span}[|{g\mathsf{H}_{N}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle\;,g\in\mathsf{G}_{N}]

(45)

i.e. the commutant vector space can be seen as the span of the left cosets of the group over the stabilizer.
For instance, in the case
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{U}(q)
, as we stated above
\mathsf{G}_{N}=\mathsf{S}_{N}\times\mathsf{S}_{N}
, with the action
R((\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}))|{\mathbf{1}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle=|{\sigma\sigma^{\prime-1}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle
; thus
|{\mathbf{1}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle
is left invariant by choosing
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma
, with
\mathsf{H}_{N}=\{(\sigma,\sigma)\in\mathsf{G}_{N}|\sigma\in\mathsf{S}_{N}\}\equiv\mathsf{S}_{N}
and for
q\geq N

\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{U}(q)}^{(N)}=\mathbb{C}[\mathsf{S}_{N}\times\mathsf{S}_{N}/\mathsf{S}_{N}]\equiv\mathbb{C}[\mathsf{S}_{N}]\simeq\operatorname{Span}[|{\sigma}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle\;,\sigma\in\mathsf{S}_{N}]\;,

(46)

the already introduced vector space of permutations. Instead, the case
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{O}(q)
, all replicas can be permuted and
\mathsf{G}_{N}=\mathsf{S}_{2N}
. In this case, seeing a pairing
p\in\mathcal{C}_{2^{N}}\subset\mathsf{S}_{2N}
, the group
\mathsf{S}_{2N}
acts on the pairings as
R(\sigma)|{p}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle=|{\sigma p\sigma^{-1}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle
for
\sigma\in\mathsf{S}_{2N}
. The whole set of pairings can be generated acting on the state
|{\mathbf{1}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle
, seen as the reference pairing
p_{\mathbf{1}}=((1,1^{\ast}),(2,2^{\ast}),\ldots,(N,N^{\ast}))
. However the mapping
\sigma\to\sigma p_{0}\sigma^{-1}
is not one-to-one due to the existence of a non-trivial stabilizer group, corresponding to the wreath product i.e
\mathsf{H}_{N}=S_{2}^{N}\rtimes S_{N}
. Intuitively, it corresponds to the exchanges within each pairing, or by permutations of the
N
pairings.
As a consequence,

\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{O}(q)}^{(N)}=\mathbb{C}[\mathsf{S}_{2N}/S_{2}^{N}\rtimes S_{N}]\equiv\mathbb{C}[\mathsf{P}_{N}]\equiv\operatorname{Span}[|{p}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle\;,p\in\mathsf{P}_{N}]\;,

(47)

also known as the Brauer algebra
17
. Note that the dimension matches consistently with Eq. (
34
). Moreover, as
\mathcal{G}
is a subgroup of
\mathcal{U}(q)
,
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{U}(q)}^{(N)}=\mathbb{C}[\mathsf{S}_{N}]\subset\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}
as a subalgebra.

Eq. (
43
) alone allows us to simplify the calculation of the powers of
T
required to determine the moments in Eq. (
40
). Indeed, Schur’s lemma ensures that
T
is the identity in each irreducible representation of
\mathsf{G}_{N}
in
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}
. But we can proceed further as we are interested in exploring the scaling limit where
q
and
t
become large, under the assumption of a long purification time. Due to Eq. (
43
), we know that
T
is in the center of
\mathsf{G}_{N}
and therefore it is natural to consider an expansion in terms of the conjugacy classes of
\mathsf{G}_{N}
. Let us consider the conjugacy class in
\mathsf{G}_{N}
associated with the swap
[(12)]=\{g(12)g^{-1}|g\in\mathsf{G}_{N}\}
and the linear sum over all its elements leads to the operator

M=\sum_{h\in[(12)]}R(h)\;,

(48)

also belonging to the center of
\mathsf{G}_{N}
, i.e.

[R(g),M]=0\qquad\forall g\in\mathsf{G}_{N}\;.

(49)

We assume that
T=\lambda_{0}\mathbb{1}+\lambda_{1}M+\ldots
, where the neglected terms correspond to more complex transitions suppressed within the scaling limit.
The structure of the operator
M
can be further clarified using the coset description

\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}\simeq\mathbb{C}[\mathsf{G}_{N}/\mathsf{H}_{N}]
.
In the natural basis
\{|{g\mathsf{H}_{N}}\rangle\hskip-2.27621pt\rangle\}
, the matrix elements of
M
are given by

M_{g^{\prime}\mathsf{H}_{N},\,g\mathsf{H}_{N}}=\#\big([(12)]\cap g^{\prime}\mathsf{H}_{N}g^{-1}\big),

(50)

where
\#
denotes the cardinality of the finite set. So, all the entries are integers and in particular, the diagonal contribution is independent of the coset and equals

m=|[(12)]\cap\mathsf{H}_{N}|,

(51)

which yields the decomposition

M=m\,\mathbb{1}+A,

(52)

where
A
has vanishing diagonal and non–negative integer entries.
The matrix
A
admits a natural interpretation as the adjacency matrix of the
Schreier graph
87
associated with the action of
\mathsf{G}_{N}
on the coset space

\mathsf{G}_{N}/\mathsf{H}_{N}
, with generating set given by the conjugacy class
[(12)]
.
Vertices correspond to cosets, and two vertices are connected by as many edges
as there exist elements of the class mapping one coset into the other. It provides a natural generalization of the matrices introduced in Eqs. (
28
,
37
).

Therefore, within the scaling regime where higher conjugacy classes are suppressed,
the transfer matrix takes the form consistent with Eqs. (
27
,
36
)

T=\lambda_{0}\mathbb{1}+\lambda_{1}M+\ldots=(\lambda_{0}+m\lambda_{1})\,\mathbb{1}+\lambda_{1}A+\dots\;.

(53)

Consistently with (
29
), we can define the time scale for purification from the (normalized) matrix element

\frac{T_{(12),\mathbf{1}}}{T_{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}}}\simeq\frac{\lambda_{0}+m\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{1}}=\frac{\Delta t}{t_{\rm P}}

(54)

In the scaling limit of interest this ratio is expected to become exponentially small in the system size. For the moments, we therefore obtain

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}^{(N)}(x;\mathcal{G})=\frac{[e^{xA}]_{\sigma(\mathbf{r}^{(N)}),\mathbf{1}}}{[e^{xA}]_{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}}}\;,\qquad\sigma\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{r}^{(N)}}

(55)

formally analogous to Eqs. (
30
,
38
), which therefore justifies their validity and generality.

III.1
Spectrum of the adjacency matrix

In general, replica limits require analytical treatment for generic
N
and therefore benefit from the possibility of diagonalizing the adjacency matrix
A
and thus simplifying the calculation of the matrix elements in Eq. (
55
). This can be done on the basis of a purely algebraic general treatment based on the considerations of the previous section.

We now give a general and abstract discussion of the diagonalization of the
adjacency operator
A
, valid for any discrete symmetry group

\mathsf{G}_{N}
acting transitively on the invariant space

\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}\simeq\mathbb{C}[\mathsf{G}_{N}/\mathsf{H}_{N}]
. It is convenient to consider directly the operator (
48
), trivially related to
A
via (
52
).

By Schur’s lemma, Eq. (
49
) implies that on each irreducible block of
\mathsf{G}_{N}
inside
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}
,
M
is a multiple of the identity.
We write the decomposition into irreducible representations of
\mathsf{G}_{N}
as

\mathbb{C}[\mathsf{G}_{N}/\mathsf{H}_{N}]=\bigoplus_{\Lambda\in\operatorname{Irreps}(\mathsf{G}_{N})}m_{\Lambda}\,V_{\Lambda},

(56)

where
V_{\Lambda}
runs over irreducible representations of
\mathsf{G}_{N}

and
m_{\Lambda}

is the corresponding multiplicity.
Let
P_{\Lambda}
denote the projector onto the
\Lambda
-isotypic component, i.e. onto the
m_{\Lambda}
copies of
V_{\Lambda}
corresponding to the same irrep
\Lambda
.
Then one has the spectral decomposition

A=\sum_{\Lambda\in\operatorname{Irreps}(\mathsf{G}_{N})}\nu(\Lambda)\,P_{\Lambda},\qquad e^{xA}=\sum_{\Lambda\in\operatorname{Irreps}(\mathsf{G}_{N})}e^{x\nu(\Lambda)}P_{\Lambda}\;,

(57)

and eigenvalue
\nu(\Lambda)
then appears with multiplicity

m_{\Lambda}d^{\Lambda}
and
d^{\Lambda}=\dim V_{\Lambda}
, the dimension of each irreducible representation
\Lambda
of
\mathsf{G}_{N}
.

The eigenvalues can be expressed in terms of characters, by simply considering
\operatorname{Tr}[MP_{\Lambda}]
.
Let

\chi^{\Lambda}(g)
be the character of
V_{\Lambda}
.
Denoting by
d_{[(12)]}
the size of the conjugacy class
[(12)]
, one finds

\nu(\Lambda)=\frac{d_{[(12)]}\chi^{\Lambda}(g=(12))}{d^{\Lambda}}-m

(58)

where the integer
m
is defined in Eq. (
51
).
This formula provides the spectrum of
A
in complete generality.

In order to compute matrix elements of functions of
A
, such as (
55
), it is convenient to describe the spectral projectors
P_{\Lambda}
. They can be written explicitly in terms of
characters
\chi^{\Lambda}(g)
of
\mathsf{G}_{N}
, relative to
\Lambda\in\operatorname{Irreps}(\mathsf{G}_{N})
on the element
g\in\mathsf{G}_{N}
.
Using the orthogonality of characters, one has

P_{\Lambda}=\frac{d^{\Lambda}}{|\mathsf{G}_{N}|}\sum_{g\in\mathsf{G}_{N}}\chi^{\Lambda}(g)^{\ast}\,R(g).

(59)

Using (
57
), this expression can be used to compute the matrix elements of
e^{xA}
required for the moments.

A particularly relevant case is the one where
(\mathsf{G}_{N},\mathsf{H}_{N})
forms a Gelfand pair
90
, i.e. if the multiplicities

m_{\lambda}\in\{0,1\}
. We will see that this is true in the unitary/orthogonal cases considered here. In this case,
for those
\Lambda
such that
m_{\Lambda}=1
,
the projector
P_{\Lambda}
reduces to the projector onto the single irrep
V_{\Lambda}
.
As a consequence, one can introduce the spherical functions
90

\omega^{\Lambda}(g):=\frac{1}{|\mathsf{H}_{N}|}\sum_{h\in\mathsf{H}_{N}}\chi^{\Lambda}(gh^{-1})^{\ast}

(60)

and obtain finally

[e^{xA}]_{\sigma(\mathbf{r}^{(N)}),\mathbf{1}}=\frac{|\mathsf{H}_{N}|}{|\mathsf{G}_{N}|}\sum_{\Lambda\in\operatorname{Irreps}(\mathsf{G}_{N})}e^{x\nu(\Lambda)}d^{\Lambda}\omega^{\Lambda}(\sigma)\;.

(61)

IV
Continuous-time model: Dyson Brownian Motion and Calogero-Sutherland mapping

We now turn to a complementary description of purification dynamics in continuous time. This approach provides a powerful analytical framework by mapping the purification process onto the dynamics of an integrable many-body system.
While the discrete-time model highlighted the combinatorial structure of the commutant algebra acting on replicated spaces, the continuous-time limit reveals the connection with Dyson Brownian motion and the Calogero-Sutherland model.
The formulation is analogous to the one introduced in Refs.
44
;
19
for the unitary class, but we will extend it in a way that applies to a generic Dyson index
\beta
, thus encompassing the
\beta=2
-unitary case
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{U}(q)
and the
\beta=1
-orthogonal case
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{O}(q)

1
1

1

Our discussion embraces naturally also the symplectic
\beta=4
case. However, that does not have a simple interpretation as a many-body monitored dynamics. So we do not analyze it in detail in this work.
.

IV.1
Stochastic evolution and eigenvalue dynamics

We consider a model of monitoring dynamics in continuous time, as before for a macroscopic quantum dot whose Hilbert space has dimension
q
.
Let us start by briefly discussing the generalization of projective measures to weak measures. One way to introduce them is to consider coupling the system to an auxiliary system (ancilla) and performing projective measurements exclusively on the latter. This allows, by means of an experimentally relevant protocol, the effect of the measurement on the system to be tunable and arbitrarily small; see the Appendix
A
for details. By choosing the ancilla as a spin
1/2
and considering a generic observable
\hat{O}
, labeling with
s=\pm 1
the measurement outcome on the ancilla, we have the hermitian Kraus operators

K_{s}(\hat{O};\lambda,\Delta t)=\cos\left(\lambda\hat{O}\Delta t+s\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\,,

(75)

satisfying
\sum_{s=\pm 1}K_{s}(\hat{O};\lambda,\Delta t)^{2}=\mathds{1}
. In the Equation above,
\Delta t
is the time interval during which the ancilla and the system interact, and
\lambda
is a coupling constant.
The famous stochastic Schrödinger equation
21
;
29
;
46
;
20
is obtained by iterating
this procedure several times, leading to a quantum trajectory (see (
4
)), with the continuous time limit emerging taking
\Delta t\to 0
,
\lambda\to\infty
at fixed rate
\Gamma=\lambda^{2}\Delta t
; then, the sequence of measurement outcomes along the trajectory

\sqrt{\Delta t}\sum_{\mathsf{t}^{\prime}<\mathsf{t}}s_{\mathsf{t}^{\prime}}\to W(t=\mathsf{t}\Delta t)\;,\qquad\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma}{\Delta t}}

(76)

converges to a continuous Wiener process
W(t)
, with a measurement rate
\Gamma
. In the absence of further effects, these dynamics leads for
t\to\infty
to the projection onto one of the eigenstates of
\hat{O}
. In our approach, however, we assume that at
each time-step
, the choice of operator
\hat{O}
is also randomized. Setting
\hat{O}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{q}o_{\alpha\beta}\ket{\alpha}\bra{\beta}
, written in terms of an orthonormal basis of states
\{\ket{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{q}
, we consider
o_{\alpha\beta}
a
q\times q
random matrix with Gaussian entries, and with given symmetry parameterized by a Dyson index
\beta
. Here, Gaussianity is not crucial as long as rotational invariance is maintained, but we assume it for simplicity of the derivation.
Then, the probability distribution for a matrix
o_{\alpha\beta}
drawn from a Gaussian Ensemble at given
\beta
, and the corresponding correlations, are

P(o)=\frac{1}{z}e^{-\frac{q\beta}{4}\operatorname{Tr}(oo^{\dagger})}\,,\qquad\mathbb{E}\!\left[o_{\alpha\beta}o_{\gamma\delta}^{*}\right]=\frac{1}{q}\left(\delta_{\alpha\gamma}\delta_{\beta\delta}+\frac{2-\beta}{\beta}\delta_{\alpha\delta}\delta_{\beta\gamma}\right)\,.

(77)

Crucially, this distribution is invariant under the symmetry transformation
o\to U^{\dagger}oU
, with
U
an element of the unitary (
\beta=2
) or orthogonal (
\beta=1
) group. From the combined choice
a=(s,o)
, we obtain a continuous family of Kraus operators. Formally, with respect to the notation of Sec.
I
, one has to interpret the sum over the generalized measurements’ outcomes as

\sum_{a}(\ldots)\longrightarrow\int do\;P(o)\sum_{s=\pm 1}(\dots)\;.

(78)

We can now consider the limit
\Delta t\to 0
as in Eq. (
76
); in this case it is convenient to introduce the matrix-valued
Wiener process
do_{\alpha\beta}=o_{\alpha\beta}(t)\ dW
, satisfying
\mathbb{E}\!\left[do_{\alpha\beta}do_{\gamma\delta}^{*}\right]=\frac{dt}{q}\left(\delta_{\alpha\gamma}\delta_{\beta\delta}+\frac{2-\beta}{\beta}\delta_{\alpha\delta}\delta_{\beta\gamma}\right)
.
The notation
do_{\alpha\beta}=o_{\alpha\beta}(t)\,dW
can be regarded as a polar (direction–magnitude) decomposition of an isotropic multidimensional Wiener increment in the continuous-time limit, where only the second moments are retained.
Accordingly, we can define
d\hat{O}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{q}do_{\alpha\beta}\ket{\alpha}\bra{\beta}
. Then, the evolution equation (
4
) can be rewritten as a stochastic equation in the continuous time limit, which reads

d\check{\rho}=e^{\sqrt{\Gamma}dO-\Gamma dO^{2}}\check{\rho}\,e^{\sqrt{\Gamma}dO-\Gamma dO^{2}}-\check{\rho}=-\Gamma dt\left(\check{\rho}-\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\check{\rho}}{q}\mathds{1}\right)+\sqrt{\Gamma}\left\{dO,\check{\rho}\right\}\,,

(79)

where the Itô convention is assumed, and where
\check{\rho}=\check{\rho}(t)
hereafter for simplicity.
This dynamics can be extended by considering in each
\Delta t
also a unitary evolution (deterministic or stochastic), by simply replacing
d\check{\rho}\to d\check{\rho}+i[H,\rho]dt
. However, provided that
e^{-iHdt}\in\mathcal{G}
(holding for any hermitian Hamiltonian for
\beta=2
and any purely imaginary one for
\beta=1
), such an evolution does not modify the spectrum of the density matrix but only acts as a symmetry transformation of its eigenvectors. As we consider the infinite-temperature initial state
\check{\rho}(t=0)=\rho(t=0)=q^{-1}\mathds{1}
and
since our ensemble of Kraus operators generated by the measurements is automatically invariant under
\mathcal{G}
, we can omit a Hamiltonian term without loss of generality (see also Refs.
44
and Appendix
A
). Also, being the initial state and the distribution of observables (
77
) invariant under
\mathcal{G}
, the distribution of
\check{\rho}
will be itself invariant under
\check{\rho}\to U\check{\rho}U^{-1}
for any
U\in\mathcal{G}
. This point is fundamental, in as much it allows us to decouple the dynamics of the eigenvalues
\{y_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{q}
of
\check{\rho}
from the one of the eigenvectors.
Following second-order perturbation theory, from (
79

dy_{\alpha}=\frac{4\Gamma}{q}dt\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\neq\alpha}\frac{y_{\alpha}y_{\alpha^{\prime}}}{y_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha^{\prime}}}+\sqrt{\frac{8\Gamma}{\beta q}}dB_{\alpha}\,y_{\alpha}

(80)

where
dB_{\alpha}
are independent standard Wiener increments,
dB_{\alpha}dB_{\alpha^{\prime}}=dt\delta_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}

44
.
It is useful to connect this formalism with the moments introduced in (
10

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}[\check{\rho}]=\prod_{j}(\sum_{\alpha}y_{\alpha}^{j})^{r_{j}}
and thus

\left\langle\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}(t)\right\rangle^{(N)}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[p_{\mathbf{r}^{(N)}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[p_{1^{N}}\right]}\;,\qquad p_{\mathbf{r}}:=\prod_{j}\bigl(\sum_{\alpha}y_{\alpha}^{j}\bigr)^{r_{j}}

(81)

where
\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[\ldots\right]
represents the average over
\{y_{\alpha}\}
solutions of Eq. (
80

t

\mathbf{a}
; in the second equality, we introduced the power-sum symmetric polynomials
p_{\mathbf{r}}(y)
associated to the partition
\mathbf{r}
.

Summing over
\alpha
, one clearly sees that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{\alpha}y_{\alpha}\right]
is kept constant by the dynamics (
80
), ultimately a consequence of the preservation of total probability
\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\operatorname{Prob}(\mathbf{a})
by each quantum channel (
3
). So in the case
N=1
, we can get rid of the denominator by simply normalizing the initial condition
y_{\alpha}(t=0)=1/q
.
From a qualitative point of view, in (
80
), purification results from the combined action of multiplicative noise, which tends to increase eigenvalues exponentially, together with level repulsion, which keeps them well separated from each other.
Indeed, a pure state corresponds to a single maximal eigenvalue being much larger than all the others.
To handle the multiplicative noise and identify the stationary properties, it is convenient to switch to logarithmic variables

w_{\alpha}=\log y_{\alpha}-x\left[1/\beta+(q-1)/2\right]

with a suitable constant drift accounting for center-of-mass motion, and where we introduced the rescaled time
x=4\Gamma t/q
. It will be clear later that
t_{P}=q/(4\Gamma)
is the purification time for the weak measurement protocol in continuous time. As the dynamics emerges from a multiplicative random matrix process, the variables
\{w_{\alpha}\}
can be interpreted as Lyapunov exponents
47
;
52
;
5
;
82
.
Using Itô’s lemma, the evolution maps onto a gradient flow in a potential

dw_{\alpha}=-\frac{2}{\beta}\frac{\partial V^{0}_{\beta}}{\partial w_{\alpha}}dx+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\ dB_{\alpha}\,.

(82)

The potential
V^{0}_{\beta}
governing this Dyson Brownian motion is given by

V^{0}_{\beta}(\vec{w})=-\frac{\beta}{4}\sum_{\alpha\neq\gamma}\log\sinh\left|\frac{w_{\alpha}-w_{\gamma}}{2}\right|\,.

(83)

It represents a pairwise logarithmic repulsion typical of random matrix ensembles, but with a hyperbolic deformation (
\sinh
) arising from the multiplicative nature of the noise; with standard methods, one can write down the associated Fokker-Planck equation for the probability distribution of the Lyapunov exponents
P(\vec{w},\tau)

\beta\ \partial_{x}P(\vec{w},x)=-H^{\rm FP}_{\beta}P(\vec{w},x)\,,\qquad H_{\beta}^{\rm{FP}}=-\sum_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\left(\partial_{\alpha}+2\partial_{\alpha}V_{\beta}^{0}\right)\,.

(84)

For clarity, we stress here that the
Hamiltonian

we introduced here is an effective one that emerges in the description of the joint pdf of the singular values through the Fokker-Planck. So it should not be confused with the quantum dynamics of the original quantum system in (
79

IV.2
Mapping to the Calogero-Sutherland model

The Fokker-Planck operator
H_{\beta}^{\rm{FP}}
is non-Hermitian. However, it can be mapped onto a well-known Hermitian Hamiltonian through a similarity transformation.
First, one observes that the stationary solution of (
84
) admits the simple exponential form

\Psi_{0}(\vec{w})=e^{-V_{\beta}^{0}(\vec{w})}=(\tilde{\Delta}(\vec{w}))^{\beta/2}\;,\qquad\tilde{\Delta}(\vec{w})=\prod_{\alpha<\alpha^{\prime}}\sinh\frac{w_{\alpha}-w_{\alpha^{\prime}}}{2}\;,

(85)

where
\tilde{\Delta}
plays the role of a generalized Vandermonde determinant.
By performing the transformation
P(\vec{w},x)=\Psi_{0}(\vec{w})\psi(\vec{w},x)
, the evolution takes the form of an imaginary-time Schrödinger equation

-\beta\ \partial_{x}\psi=\left(\Psi_{0}^{-1}H_{\beta}^{\rm{FP}}\Psi_{0}\right)\psi=\left(H_{\beta}^{\rm CS}+E^{0}_{q,\beta}\right)\psi\,.

(86)

where the shift with
E^{0}_{q,\beta}=\beta^{2}q(q^{2}-1)/48
enforces the zero-energy ground state and, remarkably, the resulting operator is the Hamiltonian of the hyperbolic Calogero-Sutherland (CS) model

H_{\beta}^{\rm CS}=-\sum_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}^{2}+\frac{\beta}{4}\left(\frac{\beta}{2}-1\right)\sum_{\alpha<\alpha^{\prime}}\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{w_{\alpha}-w_{\alpha^{\prime}}}{2}\right)}\,.

(87)

This mapping establishes a profound connection: the universal purification dynamics of a monitored quantum system is governed by the same integrable Hamiltonian describing interacting particles in one dimension. The symmetry class of the monitoring enters simply as the coupling constant
\beta
in the CS model. Note that for
\beta=2
, the interaction term in Eq. (
87
) vanishes, recovering free diffusion for the eigenvalues
44
;
19
.
For
\beta=1
, however, the non-trivial interaction persists, leading to the distinct universality class studied here.

We can now further exploit the integrability of the CS model

;
32
to derive exact expressions for the finite-time value of the moments (
81

can be put in correspondence with integer partitions, already introduced in Eq. (

Then, the eigenvector

associated to a given partition

\alpha

\Psi_{\lambda,\beta}(w):=\tilde{\Delta}^{\beta/2}(\vec{w})\ J_{\lambda}^{(2/\beta)}(e^{w})\;,\quad(H_{\beta}^{\rm CS}\cdot\Psi_{\lambda,\beta})(w)=\epsilon^{\lambda}_{q,\beta}\Psi_{\lambda,\beta}(w)\,,\quad\epsilon^{\lambda}_{q,\beta}=-\sum_{j=1}^{q}\lambda_{j}\left(\lambda_{j}+\frac{\beta}{2}(q+1-2j)\right)\,,

(88)

where
\epsilon^{\lambda}_{q,\beta}
denotes the corresponding eigenvalue.
We refer to
the Appendix
B
for the definition and a summary of the properties of Jack polynomials. Here, it suffices to note that they form a complete basis for symmetric polynomials. Therefore, it is possible to derive linear relationships with another basis, that of power-sum symmetric polynomials

J_{\lambda}^{(\alpha)}=\sum_{\mu\vdash N}\theta_{\mu}^{\lambda}(\alpha)p_{\mu}\,,\qquad p_{\mu}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash N}\gamma_{\mu}^{\lambda}(\alpha)J^{(\alpha)}_{\lambda}\,.

For clarity, we stress again that we are using a different notation for integer partitions with respect to Eq. (

p_{\lambda}(y)=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\left(\sum_{\alpha}y^{\lambda_{j}}\right)\;.

(90)

The evaluation of the change-of-basis coefficients

,
\gamma_{\mu}^{\lambda}(\alpha)
can be done iteratively but as analytical expressions are not always available, it represents a technical challenge and additional details are provided in Appendix
B

We recall that our aim is to evaluate averages of the moments
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}
expressed in terms of power-sum symmetric polynomials of the eigenvalues
y
’s. Crucially, we have converted the averages over the realization of the Wiener processes
dB_{\alpha}
in terms of the probability density

\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[p_{\mathbf{r}^{(N)}}\right]:=\int_{[0,\infty)^{q}}d^{q}y\ p_{\mu}(y)\ P(y,t)=e^{-\left[N\left(\frac{\beta}{2}(q-1)+1\right)+E^{0}_{q,\beta}\right]\frac{x}{\beta}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}d^{q}w\,\tilde{\Delta}^{-\beta/2}P(\vec{w},0)\left(e^{-H^{\rm CS}_{\beta}\ \frac{x}{\beta}}\right)\left(p_{\mu}(e^{w})\,\tilde{\Delta}^{\beta/2}\right)\,,

(91)

where

and
we restored explicitly the shift of the center-of-mass thanks to the homogeneity

Crucially, we can use Eq. (

\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[p_{\mu}\right]=\sum_{\lambda\vdash N}\gamma_{\mu}^{\lambda}(2/\beta)\,e^{-\left[N\left(\frac{\beta}{2}(q-1)+1\right)+\epsilon_{q,\beta}^{\lambda}\right]\frac{x}{\beta}}\,J^{(2/\beta)}_{\lambda}(q^{-1})\,.

In this last expression, we

We observe that for this continuous-time evolution model, thanks to the integrability of the evolution of the eigenvalues of the density matrix, we have obtained an explicit and compact expression for the moments that holds for finite time

IV.3
Scaling limit

We now analyze the behavior of Eq. (
92
) in the large
q
limit, showing how the universal form for the moments emerges in the scaling limit. First of all, we recall that

denotes the Jack’s polynomial involving

\theta_{\mu}^{\lambda}
and use that, from the definition (

p_{\mu}(q^{-1})=q^{\ell(\mu)-N}
.
Thus, in the limit of large

, which maximizes

, so that explicitly

(93)

where we used the identity
\theta^{\lambda}_{(1^{N})}(\alpha)=1
independently of
\alpha
and
\lambda
, proper of the normalization chosen in Appendix
B
(see also standard references
90
;
129
).
Additionally, we can simplify the exponent in (

\nu_{\beta}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\beta}\left[-N\left(\frac{\beta}{2}(q-1)+1\right)-\epsilon_{q,\beta}^{\lambda}\right]=\sum_{j}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\lambda_{j}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(\lambda^{\prime}_{j})^{2}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\beta}\right)N\,,

where the last equality is based on

(compare with Eq. (74) of

Notice that the values

correspond exactly to the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix of transpositions

as given in Eqs. (

Finally, we consider the scaling limit. In this case, the appropriate scaling
t,q\to\infty

x=4\Gamma t/q
finite.
Using the definition (
11
) and Eq. (
81

\lim_{q\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[p_{\mu}\right]=\sum_{\lambda\vdash N}\gamma_{\mu}^{\lambda}(2/\beta)\,e^{x\,\nu_{\beta}(\lambda)}\,.

(95)

This equation provides a nice generalization of Eqs. (
69
,
74
) for arbitrary values of
\beta
.
Indeed, as we show in Appendix
B
(see also Refs.
90
;
129
), for the case
\beta=2
, the Jack polynomials

are proportional to the well-known Schur polynomials

(see Eq. (

and

are simply related to the characters of the irreducible representations of

(see Eq. (

The fact that these two approaches based on discrete Gaussian models and on a continuous model of Dyson Brownian motion, through mapping onto the Calogero-Sutherland model, provide identical results in the scaling limit is a direct manifestation of the universality we discuss in this paper.

Small-

We focus in particular on the case

We specialize the calculation of moments to the interesting case of Rényi entropies

Thus, using Eq. (

Expanding Eq. (

\left[e^{xA}\right]_{\sigma(r_{n}=k,r_{1}=N-nk),\mathbf{1}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\ell}}{\ell!}\left[A^{\ell}\right]_{\sigma(r_{n}=k,r_{1}=N-nk),\mathbf{1}}\,.

(96)

Therefore, our goal reduces to compute the number of paths of length
\ell
along the graph with adjacency matrix

, connecting
the identity with
\sigma=\sigma(r_{n}=k,r_{1}=N-nk)
, a representative of the conjugacy class

(97)

It is well-known that the minimal number of transpositions to decompose a

distinct decompositions of such a cycle

. Since the final state consists of

of these

k

We therefore obtain

\left[e^{xA^{(\beta=2)}}\right]_{\sigma(r_{n}=k,r_{1}=N-nk),\mathbf{1}}=\left(\frac{n^{n-2}}{(n-1)!}\right)^{k}x^{(n-1)k}\left(1+\frac{a_{N,n,k,1}}{(n+1)}x^{2}+O(x^{4})\right)\;.

The next order corresponds to decompositions longer than the minimal ones and, due to parity conservation, this necessarily requires an even number of additional transpositions. The coefficient

can be determined by considering separately the cases in which the two extra transpositions i) remain within one of the
k

n

n

a_{N,n,k,1}^{(\beta=2)}=\frac{1}{4}(N-1)N(n+1)-\frac{1}{12}(n+1)(5n+6)Y+Y^{2}+NY\;,\qquad Y=\frac{n(n-1)k}{2}

(99)

The crucial aspect is the resulting purely polynomial dependence on the parameters

We now move on to the case
\beta=1

The adjacency matrix

Simulation Protocol for the Continuous Process

The weak-measurement model defined in Sec.

(i)
the explicit forward evolution of a discrete weak-measurement (DWM) protocol, as given by the Kraus operators Eq. (

(ii)

The equivalence of the two approaches provides a further proof of universality, as the two models collapse onto the same description after appropriate rescaling of the time variable through the function
\mathsf{f}
.
In both approaches we compute the BR-averaged (
9a

\hat{O}
. Notice that for BR dynamics the choice of

is also conditioned by the ancilla measurement outcome

, which is realized with probability
\operatorname{Tr}\left(K_{s}\rho K_{s}\right)

can be seen to be conditioned to random outcomes

, we use the general expression

VII
Conclusions

In this work, we provide a comprehensive theoretical and numerical characterization of universal purification dynamics in monitored quantum processes, focusing on the scaling limit deep in the weak-measurement (volume-law) phase. In this regime, the exponential separation of timescales ensures that scrambling dominates over information extraction, enabling an effective
0+1
-dimensional replica statistical-mechanics description.

Specifically, we establish that restricting the system’s effective symmetry group from the unitary group
\mathcal{U}(q)
to the orthogonal group
\mathcal{O}(q)
—as realized in systems with real-valued Hamiltonians or circuits with real gates—qualitatively alters the scaling behavior of purification. To capture these universal features, we develop two complementary theoretical frameworks:

•

Discrete-time random-matrix dynamics:
By mapping purification to products of matrices drawn from the real Ginibre ensemble (
\beta=1
), we use the combinatorial structure of the commutant space to characterize effective interactions between replicated degrees of freedom. In the real-matrix case, the basis of invariant states is enlarged to all pairings of
2N
elements, generating a distinct universal transfer matrix.

•

Continuous-time weak monitoring:
We show that infinitesimal stochastic evolution of the density matrix maps eigenvalue dynamics to Dyson Brownian motion. Crucially, this mapping is formally equivalent to an imaginary-time Schrödinger equation for the integrable Calogero–Sutherland model, naturally accommodating different symmetry classes through the Dyson index
\beta
.

A central quantitative outcome of our analysis is the distinct scaling behavior of the Rényi entropies at short rescaled times
x=t/t_{P}
. In contrast to the unitary class (
\beta=2
), which exhibits an
O(x^{2})
leading-order correction, the orthogonal class (
\beta=1
) displays a linear
O(x)
correction. This indicates that purification is inherently more effective in the orthogonal case, a direct consequence of the reduced parameter space of real matrices. These theoretical predictions were strongly corroborated by numerical simulations of both discrete and continuous protocols, which exhibited excellent data collapse onto our derived scaling functions.

Looking forward, this work opens several compelling avenues for future research.
From an analytical standpoint, important subtleties of the replica formalism remain to be clarified. Although our perturbative expansion is highly accurate at small rescaled times, describing larger values of
x
requires an all-orders resummation. Likewise, controlling the analytic continuation to
N\to 0,1
at late times, where entropies are set by only a few dominant transfer-matrix singular values, remains challenging and mathematically rich.

It is also natural to investigate additional dynamical universality classes generated by further constraints on the monitored dynamics. For example, monitored Clifford circuits
are associated with a different invariant-state structure
73
;
51
;
138
;
14
;
72
;
92
;
3
;
2
;
93
;
91
;
56
;
73
;
30
;
31
, while circuits with continuous symmetries (such as
U(1)
conservation
84
;
23
;
9
;

113
;
7
;
8
;
49
) introduce additional timescales such as charge sharpening before universal purification scaling sets in.

Finally, because the characteristic purification timescale
t_{P}
grows exponentially with the number of qubits
V
, the scaling regime can already emerge at modest system sizes. This makes direct observation of purification universality classes a realistic target for near-term quantum simulators and cross-entropy-benchmarking platforms.

Appendix A
Weak measurement

In this Appendix, we provide a derivation of the weak measurement protocol introduced in Subsec.
IV.1
, in order to show the origin of the Kraus operators
K_{s}(\hat{O};\lambda)
in Eq. (
75
).
The basic observation for introducing this protocol, is that projective measurements do not naturally fit a continuous-time limit, as their effect on the system cannot be made arbitrarily small. Weak measurements solve this problem with an experimentally relevant protocol that allows for a continuous-time mathematical description: the idea is to couple the system with an ancilla, a spin
1/2
, for a small time
\Delta t
, so that the system and the ancilla become entangled. A projective measurement of the ancilla is then performed. The crucial aspect of this procedure is that the coupling between the system and the ancilla remains tunable, so that the effect on the system of the projective measurement on the ancilla can be made arbitrarily small.

To be specific, we denote by
\hat{X},\hat{Y},\hat{Z}
the Pauli matrices acting on the ancilla, by
\ket{\pm}
the eigenstates of
\hat{Z}

and assume that the ancilla is initially prepared in the eigenstate
\ket{\to}=(\ket{+}+\ket{-})/\sqrt{2}

\displaystyle m_{N}^{(\beta=1,N+3)}=N^{N-2}(N+3)(N+2)\frac{N^{3}(N^{2}-1)}{362880}\Big[120+7N\big(200+9N(-67+185N)\big)

(166e)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt-32\big(3+2N(16+21N(-4+15N))\big)\,C_{N}\Big]\,.

(166f)

We conjecture that the form of these coefficients is always of the type
N^{N-2}[P_{\ell}(N)+C_{N}Q_{\ell}(N)]
, where
P_{\ell}(N)
and
Q_{\ell}(N)
are polynomials in
N
.

C.1
Small-
x
expansion of
\Omega_{N}^{(\beta=1)}(x)

In this Subsection we focus on

\Omega^{(\beta=1)}_{N}(x)\equiv\left[e^{xA^{(\beta=1)}}\right]_{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}}=2^{N}N!\sum_{\lambda\vdash N}\frac{1}{c(2\lambda,1,1)}\,e^{\nu_{1}(\lambda)x}\,.

(167)

Notice that the trace of
e^{xA^{(\beta=1)}}
is exactly

\operatorname{Tr}\left(e^{xA^{(\beta=1)}}\right)=\sum_{p\in\mathsf{P}_{N}}\left[e^{xA^{(\beta=1)}}\right]_{p,p}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash N}\frac{(2N)!}{c(2\lambda,1,1)}\,e^{\nu_{1}(\lambda)x}=|\mathsf{P}_{N}|\ \Omega^{(\beta=1)}_{N}(x)\,,

(168)

namely the contributions from each pairing
p\in\mathsf{P}_{N}
are all equivalent. This can be understood expanding

\Omega^{(\beta=1)}_{N}(x)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\ell}}{\ell!}\left[\left(A^{(\beta=1)}\right)^{\ell}\right]_{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}}=:\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\ell}}{\ell!}\omega_{N}^{(\beta=1,\ell)}\,.

(169)

The matrix element
\omega_{N}^{(\beta=1,\ell)}=\left[\left(A^{(\beta=1)}\right)^{\ell}\right]_{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}}
counts the number of loops of
\ell
steps starting and ending at the reference pairing
\mathbf{1}
. Since the graph is invariant under global transformation of the pairing states, one has
\omega_{N}^{(\beta=1,\ell)}=\left[\left(A^{(\beta=1)}\right)^{\ell}\right]_{p,p}
,
\forall p\in\mathsf{P}_{N}
, and the contributions to the trace are all equivalent.
Our task is now to evaluate the coefficients
\omega_{N}^{(\beta=1,\ell)}
exactly in
N
for the first few
\ell
’s.
As a useful property used in the explicit calculation, we recall that the flip graph of perfect pairings in
\mathsf{P}_{N}
is regular of degree
\rm d
.

First coefficients. —
First, the trivial walk of length
0
is unique. Then, since the graph has no loops, we cannot come back to
\mathbf{1}
in only one step. Moreover, in two steps, we can instead choose one of the
\rm d
neighbors of
\mathbf{1}
and then come back via the closed walk
\mathbf{1}\to p\to\mathbf{1}
. Therefore

\omega_{N}^{(\beta=1,0)}=1\,,\quad\omega_{N}^{(\beta=1,1)}=0\,,\quad\omega_{N}^{(\beta=1,2)}={\rm d}=N(N-1)\,.

(170)

Computation of
\omega_{N}^{(\beta=1,3)}
. —
To compute the third coefficient, fix an edge
\mathbf{1}\sim p
connecting vertex
\mathbf{1}
to a vertex
p
. Then
p
differs from
\mathbf{1}
only on four indices

\{a,b,c,d\}
, where the matching in
\mathbf{1}
is
(a,b)(c,d)

and the matching in
p
is
(a,c)(b,d)
or
(a,d)(b,c)
.
But on the set
\{a,b,c,d\}
there are exactly three perfect pairings:

(a,b)(c,d),\qquad(a,c)(b,d),\qquad(a,d)(b,c).

(171)

Therefore, every edge
\mathbf{1}\sim p
lies in a unique triangle

\mathbf{1}\sim p\sim q\sim\mathbf{1}\,,

(172)

where
q
is the third pairing on those same four indices. For the remaining
2N-4
indices, the vertices
\mathbf{1}
,
p
,
q
are equal.
Thus, for each first step
\mathbf{1}\to p
, there is exactly one 2-step return path
p\to q\to\mathbf{1}
, and

\omega_{N}^{(\beta=1,3)}={\rm d}=N(N-1)\,.

(173)

Computation of
\omega_{N}^{(\beta=1,4)}
. —

We use the fact that

\omega_{N}^{(\beta=1,4)}=\left[\left(A^{(\beta=1)}\right)^{4}\right]_{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}}=\sum_{p\in\mathsf{P}_{N}}\left[\left(A^{(\beta=1)}\right)^{2}\right]_{\mathbf{1},p}^{2}\,.

(174)

So we classify the vertices
p
that can be reached from
\mathbf{1}
in two steps.

•

Type I:
p=\mathbf{1}
.
This means the second move undoes the first one. There are exactly
{\rm d}
such 2-step paths (i.e.,
L_{2}(N)
), so this contribution is
{\rm d}^{2}
.

•

Type II:
p
adjacent to
\mathbf{1}
.
Fix
p\sim\mathbf{1}
. Since the edge
\mathbf{1}\sim p
lies in a unique triangle, c.f. the discussion above, there is exactly one 2-step path from
\mathbf{1}
to
p
. Thus

\left[\left(A^{(\beta=1)}\right)^{2}\right]_{\mathbf{1},p}=1\,,\quad p\ {\rm neighboring\ to}\ \mathbf{1}\,.

(175)

Since there are
\rm d
such vertices
p
, the contribution from this type is
{\rm d}\cdot 1^{2}={\rm d}
.

•

Type III: three reference pairs involved.
Choose
3
pairs of
\mathbf{1}
. After two flips, these three pairs may become fully mixed, so that
\mathbf{1}\cup p
is a single alternating 6-cycle. For a fixed triple of reference pairs, there are
8
such final matchings
p
. Hence the total number of such vertices is

N_{(3)}=8\binom{N}{3}.

(176)

For each such
p
, there are exactly
3
possible intermediate neighbors of
\mathbf{1}
, according to which two of the three pairs are flipped first. Therefore

\left[\left(A^{(\beta=1)}\right)^{2}\right]_{\mathbf{1},p}=3\,,\quad p\ {\rm has\ flipped}\ 3\ \rm pairs\,.

(177)

and

N_{(3)}\cdot 3^{2}=8\binom{N}{3}\cdot 9.

(178)

•

Type IV: four reference pairs involved.

Choose
4
pairs of
\mathbf{1}
. After two independent flips on two disjoint pairs of pairs,
the union
\mathbf{1}\cup p
is a disjoint union of two alternating 4-cycles.
For a fixed set of
4
reference pairs:
(i)
there are
3
ways to partition them into
2
unordered couples;
(ii)
for each couple, there are
2
possible flips. Hence the number of such vertices is

N_{(2,2)}=3\cdot 2^{2}\binom{N}{4}=12\binom{N}{4}.

(179)

For each such
p
, the two flips can be performed in either order, so

\left[\left(A^{(\beta=1)}\right)^{2}\right]_{\mathbf{1},p}=2\,,\quad p\ {\rm has\ flipped}\ 4\ \rm pairs\,,

(180)

and

N_{(2,2)}\cdot 2^{2}=12\binom{N}{4}\cdot 4.

(181)

Summing all contributions,

\omega_{N}^{(\beta=1,4)}={\rm d}^{2}+{\rm d}+9\cdot 8\binom{N}{3}+4\cdot 12\binom{N}{4}=N(N-1)\left(3N^{2}+N-11\right)\,.

(182)
