Effects of surgical timing on inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and immune modulation in Wagyu calves following castration
Authors: Watcharapon Promsut, Thanyakorn Chalalai, Benjawan Saechue, Worapol Aengwanich, Arthittaya Nueangnong, Watthanaporn Janchai, Areeya Saoweang, Piyarat Srinontong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTime-of-day-related physiological variation may influence immune, oxidative, and endocrine responses to surgical stress. This study evaluated the effects of morning versus afternoon surgical castration on inflammatory cytokine expression, oxidative stress markers, T-lymphocyte subpopulations, and cortisol levels in Wagyu calves. Twelve 4-month-old male Wagyu calves were randomly assigned to morning (10:00–11:30 h) or afternoon (14:30–16:00 h) castration groups (n = 6 per group). Blood samples were collected before castration and at 6, 24, and 72 h post-surgery. Inflammatory cytokine mRNA expression (IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α) was quantified by RT-qPCR, and oxidative stress markers were assessed using TBARS and FRAP assays. T-lymphocyte subpopulations were analyzed by flow cytometry. Serum cortisol concentrations were measured by ELISA. Data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with Šidák adjustment for pairwise comparisons.ResultsMorning castration was associated with greater cytokine response, with IL-6 expression peaking at 143-fold at 6 h post-surgery, accompanied by concurrent IL-10 upregulation (13.47-fold) and a transient increase in TNF-α expression. The afternoon group showed minimal cytokine changes throughout the study period. CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-lymphocyte proportions were higher in the afternoon group before surgery, but were lower than those in the morning group at later post-surgical time points. γδ T cells increased at 24 and 72 h in both groups without significant surgery timing effects. Malondialdehyde concentrations were significantly higher in the afternoon group (P < 0.05), whereas total antioxidant capacity (FRAP) values were also significantly higher in the afternoon group (P < 0.05). Serum cortisol did not differ significantly between surgery time groups or sampling intervals.ConclusionsMorning surgery was associated with greater acute inflammatory cytokine responses, whereas afternoon surgery was associated with higher oxidative stress and antioxidant capacity. These findings suggest that surgical timing may influence acute postoperative inflammatory, oxidative, and immune responses in cattle and may therefore be relevant to postoperative management and animal welfare.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05670-x.

Background

Beef serves as an important source of high-quality protein, essential amino acids, vitamins, and minerals. Over recent decades, global meat demand has risen sharply due to population growth, urbanization, and rising incomes. However, the current beef cattle production system cannot meet this rising demand, and sustainable livestock systems therefore require improved management efficiency to support future market expansion [1]. Castration is a routine management practice in beef production systems worldwide. Male cattle are typically castrated before puberty to reduce testosterone-driven aggression and mounting behavior, lower injury risk, and improve carcass and meat quality [2]. Surgical castration remains among the most widely performed methods [3].

Physiological and immune responses vary across the day, with time-of-day influencing endocrine function, inflammatory signaling, and cellular metabolism [4, 5], as well as immune cell activity and redox balance [6]. Disruption of these endogenous physiological patterns can impair immune function and compromise cellular coordination at multiple biological levels [7]. Several studies have demonstrated that anesthesia and surgery alter physiological mechanisms in a time-dependent manner [8–10], and the timing of surgical procedures has been associated with differences in recovery duration and modulation of endogenous physiological rhythms [4, 8, 11].

Multiple immune cell populations, including mast cells, neutrophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes, exhibit time-of-day-dependent variation in activity [7], influencing leukocyte trafficking, cytokine production, and the interplay between innate and adaptive immunity [6]. CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-lymphocyte counts in peripheral blood fluctuate across the day in both humans and experimental animals [7, 12]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-1, IL-2, IL-6, and IL-12, also show diurnal variation, with concentrations differing between day and night [13–16]. Antioxidant enzyme activities, including superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase, similarly fluctuate throughout the day [17], and diurnal patterns have been described for malondialdehyde, catalase activity, and total antioxidant capacity in both rodents and humans [18, 19].

Despite this evidence from human and experimental animal research, critical knowledge gaps remain in livestock species. No study has directly examined whether surgical timing affects T-cell subpopulations, oxidative stress markers, or stress biomarkers in cattle. Prior research in this species has focused predominantly on stress physiology and inflammation following castration, without considering whether the time of day influences postoperative immune modulation or oxidative balance. Consequently, the relationships among surgical timing, T-lymphocyte dynamics, cytokine expression, oxidative stress, and endocrine responses in calves undergoing routine procedures remain poorly characterized. This gap is practically relevant because common livestock management procedures, such as surgery, transport, feeding, and vaccination, are routinely performed at different times of day and may differentially affect animal welfare, immune recovery, and productivity.

This study, therefore, investigated how surgical timing (morning versus afternoon) influences postoperative inflammatory cytokine mRNA expression (IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α), T-lymphocyte subpopulation profiles (CD3⁺CD4⁺, CD3⁺CD8⁺, and CD3⁺WC1⁺), oxidative stress biomarkers (MDA and FRAP), and serum cortisol concentrations in Wagyu calves following surgical castration, evaluated at multiple postoperative intervals. The study was designed to assess surgically relevant daytime periods rather than to characterize circadian biology comprehensively. The findings are intended to provide foundational evidence on time-dependent physiological responses to surgical stress in cattle and to inform the development of evidence-based scheduling strategies for improving postoperative recovery and animal welfare in beef production systems.

Results

Effect of surgery time on inflammatory cytokines following castration in calves

The effects of surgery time on inflammatory cytokine mRNA expression following castration are shown in Table 1. Surgery time, sampling intervals, and their interaction significantly affected TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10 expression (P < 0.05). TNF-α mRNA expression was significantly higher in the morning group than in the afternoon group overall (P < 0.05). TNF-α expression peaked at 6 h in the morning group and was significantly higher than at all other time points (P < 0.05). In contrast, the afternoon group showed the lowest TNF-α expression at 24 h, which was lower than that of the morning group at 6 h (P < 0.05; Fig. 1A). IL-6 mRNA expression increased markedly in morning-castrated calves at 6 h post-surgery relative to baseline, whereas afternoon-castrated calves showed minimal change across all sampling intervals. The 6 h value in the morning group was significantly higher than all other groups and sampling intervals (P < 0.05; Fig. 1B). For IL-10 mRNA expression, morning-castrated calves showed a marked increase at 6 h post-surgery, followed by a return toward baseline at later sampling intervals. Afternoon-castrated calves showed consistently lower expression than the morning group throughout the study period (Fig. 1C).

Effect of surgery time on oxidative stress markers following castration in calves

The effects of surgery time on oxidative stress markers following castration are presented in Table 2. Surgery time significantly influenced both MDA and FRAP concentrations (P < 0.05), whereas sampling intervals significantly affected only MDA levels (P < 0.05). No interaction between surgery time and sampling intervals was detected for either parameter (P > 0.05). MDA concentrations were significantly higher in afternoon-castrated calves at all time points (P < 0.05). In both groups, MDA levels increased progressively from baseline and reached their highest values at 72 h post-surgery, which were significantly higher than the corresponding pre-surgical values within each group (P < 0.05). No other within-group pairwise comparisons reached significance. FRAP values were also significantly higher in afternoon-castrated calves at all sampling intervals (P < 0.05). However, FRAP values did not differ significantly across sampling intervals within either group (P > 0.05).

Effect of surgery time on T-cell subpopulations following castration in calves

T-lymphocyte subpopulation data are presented in Table 3. A significant surgery time × sampling interval interaction was observed for CD3⁺CD4⁺ cells. Before surgery, afternoon-castrated calves showed significantly higher CD3⁺CD4⁺ proportions than morning-castrated calves (P < 0.05). After surgery, however, CD3⁺CD4⁺ levels in afternoon-castrated calves were significantly lower at 6, 24, and 72 h post-surgery (P < 0.05). Within both groups, CD3⁺CD4⁺ levels declined at 6 and 24 h relative to baseline and increased again at 72 h, reaching levels significantly higher than those at earlier sampling intervals (Fig. 2A and D). CD3⁺CD8⁺ cells showed significant effects of surgery time, sampling intervals, and their interaction. CD3⁺CD8⁺ levels increased after surgery and reached their highest values at 24 h post-surgery in both groups. Afternoon-castrated calves had significantly lower CD3⁺CD8⁺ levels than morning-castrated calves at 24 and 72 h post-surgery (P < 0.05), whereas no significant differences between groups were observed before surgery or at 6 h post-surgery (P > 0.05; Fig. 2B and E). CD3⁺WC1⁺ γδ T cells were affected by sampling interval (P < 0.05) but not by surgery time, and no interaction was detected (P > 0.05). In both groups, CD3+WC1⁺ proportions increased significantly at 24 and 72 h compared with before surgery and 6 h post-surgery (P < 0.05; Fig. 2C).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the timing of surgical castration influences inflammatory cytokine mRNA expression, oxidative stress markers, and T-lymphocyte dynamics in Wagyu calves. Morning-castrated calves exhibited greater cytokine responses, whereas afternoon-castrated calves showed attenuated cytokine expression but elevated oxidative stress markers. Taken together, these findings suggest that the time of day may be a significant physiological factor affecting postoperative responses in cattle.

The most pronounced finding was the 143-fold increase in IL-6 mRNA expression in morning-castrated calves at 6 h post-surgery, compared with a 2-fold rise in the afternoon group. This difference was statistically significant and was the interaction effect between surgery time and sampling interval. IL-6 is a central mediator of the acute-phase response and of immune cell differentiation following tissue injury [20]. The present findings are consistent with previous work in Korean calves, where IL-6 mRNA expression increased markedly within 6 h after castration [21]. The morning-castrated group also showed a 13.47-fold elevation in IL-10 mRNA at the same time point, whereas the afternoon-castrated group exhibited no significant change. The concurrent upregulation of both the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6 and the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in the morning group is consistent with a coordinated acute inflammatory response, in which regulatory pathways are activated alongside pro-inflammatory signaling to limit excessive tissue damage [22, 23]. TNF-α mRNA expression followed a similar pattern, with a transient 4.25-fold increase at 6 h observed only in the morning-castrated group. In addition, morning-castrated calves showed higher baseline TNF-α fold-change values than afternoon-castrated calves. The concurrent increase in TNF-α and IL-10 at 6 h in the morning group is also compatible with rapid negative feedback regulation, in which anti-inflammatory signaling is activated to counterbalance intensified pro-inflammatory activity [24, 25]. In contrast, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α mRNA expression remained consistently low across all sampling intervals in the afternoon group, indicating a globally attenuated inflammatory response rather than selective modulation of individual cytokines. The mechanisms underlying this reduced inflammatory response were not directly investigated in the present study and require further clarification. The substantial inter-animal variability in IL-6 mRNA expression at 6 h in the morning group should also be considered. Marked variability in IL-6 responses following surgical stress is a biological phenomenon that can be observed, with IL-6 expression during the acute-phase response reported to increase by approximately 10–100 fold [26]. Human surgical studies have similarly documented pronounced inter-individual variation in IL-6 responses, which may reflect heterogeneous inflammatory responsiveness among individuals [27]. Nevertheless, individual-animal data demonstrated a consistent pattern of increased IL-6 expression at 6 h in the morning-castrated group, accompanied by concordant increases in IL-10 and TNF-α, supporting the presence of a group-level acute inflammatory response. The wide confidence intervals and small sample size do, however, limit the precision of effect-size estimates, and larger cohorts are needed to confirm these findings. Because cytokine expression was presented as fold change relative to the Morning baseline group, baseline values in the Morning group were fixed at 1.00 by definition and do not represent observed biological measurements. Consequently, apparent baseline differences between groups may partly reflect the normalization strategy rather than true biological differences. To facilitate interpretation, individual animal-level ΔCt values and model-estimated means with 95% confidence intervals are provided in the supplementary materials. Therefore, baseline fold-change differences between groups should be interpreted cautiously.

Oxidative stress biomarkers exhibited a pattern distinct from that of the cytokines. MDA, a direct product of lipid peroxidation, was significantly higher in the afternoon-castrated calves than in the morning-castrated calves at all sampling intervals, and both groups showed progressive increases peaking at 72 h post-surgery. The only significant pairwise difference was observed between the afternoon-castrated calves at 72 h and the morning pre-surgical values, indicating that the most pronounced lipid peroxidation occurred during the later postoperative period in calves castrated in the afternoon. Persistent elevation of MDA at 72 h in both groups indicates that lipid peroxidation had not returned to baseline by the end of the observation period and may reflect ongoing inflammatory activity or incomplete tissue repair [18, 28]. FRAP values were also significantly higher in the afternoon group, whereas no significant temporal variation was detected across sampling intervals. These findings suggest that the observed group difference in FRAP may be related more to differences in antioxidant status associated with surgery timing than to dynamic postoperative changes in antioxidant responses. The concurrent elevation of MDA and FRAP in the afternoon group further indicates that antioxidant defenses were insufficient to fully counterbalance the oxidative burden associated with surgical stress during this period. This pattern may be related to cumulative metabolic stress developing throughout the day [29] or to higher ambient temperatures during afternoon hours in tropical regions, both of which can directly increase oxidative stress biomarkers. However, these mechanisms were not directly examined in the present study.

T-lymphocyte dynamics differed significantly between surgery time groups for both CD3⁺CD4⁺ and CD3⁺CD8⁺ cells. Before surgery, calves castrated during the afternoon showed higher proportions of CD3⁺CD4⁺ cells than those castrated during the morning. Following castration, however, the CD3⁺CD4⁺ proportion in the afternoon group declined markedly and remained significantly lower than that in the morning group at 6, 24, and 72 h post-surgery. By 72 h, CD3⁺CD4⁺ proportions in both groups exceeded pre-surgical levels, consistent with immune mobilization during the recovery phase [28, 30]. CD3⁺CD8⁺ cells peaked at 24 h in both groups, with significant between-group differences at 24 and 72 h. The higher pre-operative CD3⁺CD4⁺ and CD3⁺CD8⁺ proportions in the afternoon group, followed by a post-castration decline, could reflect time-of-day-associated differences in peripheral lymphocyte distribution [6, 31], though this interpretation cannot be confirmed without a non-surgical control group. CD3⁺WC1⁺ γδ T cells increased significantly at 24 and 72 h in both groups, with no significant differences between surgery periods, indicating a consistent tissue-repair response independent of castration timing. This pattern is in accordance with the established role of γδ T cells in epithelial injury responses in ruminants [32–34]. Calf age is another variable that may have influenced the T-cell responses observed here [35]. At approximately 4 months of age, bovine immune and endocrine systems are still undergoing maturation, and substantial variability in lymphocyte subpopulations has been reported during early development [36]. Incomplete maturation of time-of-day immune-regulatory mechanisms could partly explain why WC1⁺ γδ T cell proportions did not differ between surgery time groups [32, 37]. In addition, the lack of a non-surgical control group limits the ability to distinguish surgery-induced T-cell redistribution from normal time-of-day physiological variation. Future studies incorporating non-surgical controls together with older animals may help clarify the influence of developmental stage on post-surgical immune responses in cattle.

Serum cortisol did not differ significantly between surgery time groups or across sampling intervals. Previous studies report that cortisol typically peaks within 0.5–3 h of castration and returns to baseline within 6–12 h [38, 39]. It is therefore likely that the acute activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis had already resolved before the first post-surgical sampling point at 6 h, rather than indicating the absence of endocrine activation. Similar transient increases in cortisol following castration have been reported previously in calves [40–42], supporting that castration is an acute physiological stressor followed by relatively rapid endocrine recovery [43]. Age-related differences in endocrine responsiveness may also be relevant; younger calves reportedly have lower cortisol responses and recover more quickly than older animals following surgical castration [44]. Although a non-surgical control group was not included, previously published baseline cortisol profiles in cattle provide a useful context for interpretation of the present data. Circulating cortisol concentrations in cattle are generally highest during morning hours and decline during the afternoon [45]. Cortisol values at 6, 24, and 72 h in both groups fell within the basal ranges reported for unstressed cattle, supporting the interpretation that the acute endocrine response had largely resolved by the time sampling was performed rather than indicating prolonged postoperative stress activation [45]. Pre-operative cortisol did not differ between groups, indicating comparable baseline HPA axis activity before surgery. However, the sampling design could not determine whether the magnitude or duration of the acute cortisol peak differed between morning and afternoon castration. Future studies incorporating blood sampling within the first 1–3 h post-castration, together with non-surgical control groups, are needed to resolve this question.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the two surgery periods represented closely spaced daytime windows rather than clearly distinct physiological phases. Second, cortisol sampling did not capture the acute post-castration endocrine peak. Third, environmental temperature and light intensity were not continuously monitored throughout the study period. Afternoon temperatures were generally higher than morning temperatures and may have contributed to differences in oxidative stress biomarkers, although THI values remained within the mild stress range for both periods. Fourth, the relatively small sample size reduced statistical power for high-variance variables. Fifth, analgesics were not used to avoid interference with primary outcome measures, which may limit the direct clinical application of the research findings. Sixth, the absence of a non-surgical control group is a limitation; however, comparable designs have been used in previous livestock research [46, 47]. Future studies incorporating non-surgical controls, continuous environmental monitoring, early post-operative endocrine sampling, and larger animal numbers will be important for more fully characterizing time-of-day-dependent post-surgical responses in cattle.

Conclusion

This study suggests that the timing of surgical castration is associated with differences in postoperative physiological responses in Wagyu calves. Morning castration was associated with greater IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α mRNA expression at 6 h post-surgery, whereas afternoon castration was associated with higher lipid peroxidation, greater antioxidant capacity, and distinct T-lymphocyte changes following surgery. CD3⁺WC1⁺ γδ T cells increased during the later postoperative period regardless of surgery timing. Cortisol responses could not be fully evaluated because early postoperative sampling was not performed. These findings indicate that surgical timing is associated with differences in acute inflammatory, oxidative, and immune responses following castration in cattle. These findings suggest that consideration of surgical timing may be relevant for postoperative management and animal welfare in beef production systems. Further studies incorporating additional surgical time points, early endocrine sampling, environmental monitoring, and larger sample sizes are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying these temporal physiological differences.

Methods

All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Mahasarakham University (IACUC-MSU-057-038/2025). The experimental protocol, including the decision to withhold analgesic agents, was explicitly reviewed and approved by the ethics committee because analgesic administration would substantially confound the primary outcome measures. All procedures were performed by a licensed veterinarian in accordance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of animals in research.

Animals and housing

This study was conducted between February and August 2025, with surgical castration performed at a commercial farm in Kalasin Province, northeastern Thailand (16°26’N, 103°30’E). Environmental conditions on the day of surgery were obtained from weather.com historical records for Kalasin Province. The ambient temperature ranged from 21 to 29 °C with relative humidity estimated between 55 and 65%. Based on these values, the temperature-humidity index (THI) was estimated at approximately 75–76 during the morning surgery period (10:00–11:30) and 78–79 during the afternoon surgery period (14:30–16:00), both within the mild heat stress range (THI 72–79). Continuous on-site environmental monitoring and direct light intensity measurements were not performed during the study. Calves were housed in open-sided shelters exposed to natural daylight under the same ambient photoperiod conditions. Twelve male Wagyu calves, four months of age and weighing 100–190 kg, were used in this study. The calves were raised on a farm in Kalasin Province, northeastern Thailand. All study animals had received a trivalent foot-and-mouth disease vaccine (O, A, and Asia1 serotypes) and a live attenuated lumpy skin disease virus Neethling strain vaccine (KEMIN, Mevac, Cairo, Egypt). All calves were kept on concrete flooring with straw bedding and had free access to water and pangola grass. They also received a supplemental concentrate feed (CP 972®, CPF (Thailand) Public Company Limited, Saraburi Province, Thailand). Prior to the experiment, a veterinarian examined all the calves and found them healthy, with no signs of disease or anomalies.

Study design and procedure

This study consists of two experimental factors: (A) surgical timing with two periods, 10:00–11:30 h (morning group) and 14:30–16:00 h (afternoon group), and (B) surgical interval time points, including before castration (Time 0) and 6, 24, and 72 h post-surgery. The calves were randomly assigned to two experimental groups according to surgical timing: morning castration and afternoon castration. All six calves in the morning group were castrated sequentially within the 10:00–11:30 h window, whereas all six calves in the afternoon group were castrated sequentially within the 14:30–16:00 h window. The minimum time interval between the last morning surgery and the first afternoon surgery was approximately 3 h, ensuring a meaningful separation between the two experimental periods. The sample size was determined according to the method described by Ilyas et al. [48]. Thirty minutes before surgical castration, xylazine hydrochloride (X-Lazine®, L.B.S. Laboratory Co., Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand) was administered intramuscularly at a dose of 0.02 mg/kg body weight. Each calf was gently restrained using a halter. The surgical castration procedure followed the method described by Marquette et al. [3]. Briefly, a scalpel was used to make an incision through the distal one-third of the scrotal wall; the testicles were exposed, the spermatic cord on each side was crushed, and the testicular cords along with the tunica vaginalis were severed. Analgesic agents were not administered to all animals in this study. This decision was explicitly reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. The scientific rationale was as follows: (1) non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), the most commonly used analgesics in bovine castration, directly inhibit cyclooxygenase enzymes and suppress prostaglandin synthesis, thereby substantially altering the inflammatory cytokine profile including IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α, which were primary outcome measures in this study [49]; (2) NSAIDs have been shown to reduce oxidative stress markers including MDA and to modulate antioxidant capacity in cattle, which would have directly confounded the FRAP and MDA measurements [50, 51]; (3) local anesthetics such as lidocaine have been reported to alter T-lymphocyte proliferation and cytokine production, potentially confounding the T-cell subpopulation data [52, 53]. To minimize suffering, all surgical procedures were performed as rapidly as possible by a single experienced veterinarian using a standardized technique, and all animals were monitored closely for signs of distress throughout the post-operative period.

Blood sampling

Blood samples were collected from the jugular vein of each calf via jugular venipuncture immediately before castration (time 0) and at 6, 24, and 72 h post-surgery. Samples were drawn into one 5 mL clot activator tube (Meditube®, Hebei Xinle Sci & Tech Co., Ltd, China) for serum separation and three 3 mL EDTA tubes (Meditube® K3EDTA, Hebei Xinle Sci & Tech Co., Ltd, China), all kept on ice during handling. Two EDTA tubes were used for peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) isolation, and the other for flow cytometry. Serum separations were collected after centrifugation for 15 min at 3000 × g, aliquoted, and stored at − 20 °C for analysis of cortisol and oxidative stress profiling.

Cortisol analysis

Serum cortisol concentrations were determined using a bovine cortisol ELISA kit (Cusabio Biotech Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China; Catalog No. CSB-E13064B) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Tecan Infinite®200 microplate reader (Tecan Trading AG, Switzerland), according to the relevant standard curves.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cell isolation

PBMCs were isolated from whole blood as previously described [54]. In brief, blood samples were diluted 1:2 with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and carefully layered onto 3 mL of Ficoll-Paque Plus (Cytiva, MA, USA) in a conical tube. After centrifugation at 800 × g for 30 min, the PBMC layer at the interphase was collected, transferred to a new tube containing 10 mL of PBS, and centrifuged again at 1,400 × g for 10 min. Red blood cells were removed using RBC lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The remaining PBMC pellet was washed twice with PBS and preserved in RNAlater® solution (Invitrogen, USA) for downstream analysis.

Flow cytometric analysis of T-lymphocyte subsets

Blood leukocytes were isolated after red blood cells were removed by RBC lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) and resuspended in PBS. The tube was then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 10 °C. RBC lysis was repeated until a clear white cell pellet was obtained. Finally, the pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of PBS containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS). To block nonspecific antibody binding, cells were incubated with polyclonal rat IgG (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 15 min at 4 °C, followed by staining with fluorescently labeled antibodies: CD3–Alexa Fluor 647, CD4–Pacific Blue, CD8–RPE, and WC1–FITC (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., UK). Stained cells were incubated for 30 min at 4 °C, washed with PBS, and finally resuspended in 200 µL of PBS containing 2% FBS. T lymphocyte subpopulations were analyzed using a FACSCelesta flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA) with FlowJo Software. RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from PBMCs using the Nucleospin RNA Kit (Macherey-Nagel, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the isolated RNA using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (TOYOBO, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was subsequently performed with the Maxima SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) on a QuantStudio™ 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). All reactions were performed in duplicate; wells with Cq deviation > 0.5 between technical replicates were excluded. Primer amplification efficiencies were validated from 5-fold serial dilutions and ranged from 96 to 102% (R² > 0.99) for all targets. The stability of GAPDH as a reference gene was confirmed using geNorm analysis (M-value < 0.5). ΔCq values (ΔCq = Cq_target − Cq_GAPDH) were used directly for statistical analysis. Results are reported as fold change (2(ΔCq_baseline − ΔCq_sample)) back-transformed from estimated marginal means, calculated relative to the Morning group at baseline (Time 0). Serum malondialdehyde analysis

Serum malondialdehyde levels were assessed using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substance (TBARS) method with modifications based on our previous research [55]. For each sample, 450 µL of 0.9% NaCl (QRëC™, Auckland, New Zealand), 200 µL of 0.12 M thiobarbituric acid (Fluka, Steinheim, Germany), and 1000 µL of 10% trichloroacetic acid (QRëC™, Auckland, New Zealand) were added to the serum sample (100 µL). This mixture was thoroughly mixed and placed in a boiling water bath for 30 min. After the samples were cooled, 2000 µL of distilled water was added, and the tubes were centrifuged at 1,100 ×g for 10 min. The supernatant was placed in a 96-well plate and read at 532 nm in a Tecan Infinite®200 microplate reader (Tecan Trading AG, Switzerland). Malondialdehyde concentrations were calculated using a 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane standard curve (Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany).

Total antioxidant capacity analysis

Total antioxidant power was analyzed using the ferric reducing ability (FRAP) assay with modifications based on our previous research [46]. In brief, 300 mM sodium acetate buffer (Ajax Finechem Pty. Ltd.), pH 3.6, 10 mM tris(2-pyridyl)-s-triazine (Aldrich) in 40 mM HCl (QRëC™), and 20 mM of FeCl3.6H2O (PanReac AppliChem) were prepared in a volume ratio of 10:1:1 to generate the FRAP working reagent. Subsequently, 20 µL of serum was mixed with 180 µL of the FRAP reagent in a 96-well plate. After incubation for 5 min at room temperature, absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a Tecan Infinite® 200 microplate reader (Tecan Trading AG, Switzerland). Calibration was performed using FeSO₄·7 H₂O standards in the range of 100–1000 µM.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2; R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Distributional assumptions were assessed using a two-stage approach. Raw data were initially screened for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, followed by simulation-based residual diagnostics using the DHARMa package (version 0.4.6) after model fitting. Model adequacy was evaluated based on residual uniformity, dispersion, and outlier patterns. For cytokine gene expression (IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α), analyses were performed on ΔCt values (ΔCt = Cqtarget − CqGAPDH) rather than fold-change values to reduce distributional skewness. DHARMa diagnostics confirmed acceptable residual behavior for all cytokine models. WC1⁺ γδ T-cell data showed non-normality and were therefore log-transformed before analysis. Results for this variable are presented as back-transformed values. All other variables met the Gaussian model assumptions without transformation.

All outcome variables were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models fitted with the lme4 and lmerTest packages. Surgery time (morning vs. afternoon), sampling interval (before surgery, 6, 24, and 72 h post-surgery), and their interaction were included as fixed effects, with individual calf included as a random effect to account for repeated measures. Degrees of freedom and P-values were estimated using Satterthwaite’s method. Estimated marginal means and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the emmeans package with Kenward–Roger correction. Pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Šidák method. Cytokine gene expression results are presented as fold change [2(ΔCtbaseline− ΔCtsample)] back-transformed from estimated marginal means relative to the morning group at baseline (Time 0). Compact letter displays were generated using the multcomp and multcompView packages, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.
