Implementation of decentralized clinical trials in Korea from a resource-based perspective
Authors: Ki Young Huh, Gaeun Kang, Kye Hun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clinical trials in South Korea are heavily concentrated in Seoul, limiting patient access in other regions. We conducted a cross-sectional ecological study of 17 Korean administrative divisions to assess regional infrastructure readiness for decentralized clinical trial (DCT) implementation, using data from the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (2024), the National Health Insurance Service (2024), and the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (2024 to 2025). Provincial densities for four resource domains (specialists, nurses, beds, and medical equipment) were calculated per 100,000 patients and normalized to Seoul (= 1.00). Area-specific composite indices for four therapeutic areas were computed as the capped geometric mean of specialist and equipment densities, and an overall infrastructure index as the capped geometric mean of all four domain densities. Trial density was derived from 1,924 approved trials yielding 7,760 trial-center pairs (99% mapping rate). Among the 16 non-Seoul provinces, bed density was the most dispersed (coefficient of variation [CV] = 0.39, range 0.58 to 2.91), whereas specialist density was the most Seoul-concentrated of the four domains: no non-Seoul province reached 80% of Seoul’s level (CV = 0.23, range 0.42 to 0.79). The four therapeutic-area composite indices showed comparable dispersion (CV 0.15 to 0.23; composite infrastructure CV 0.16; Pearson r = 0.72). Gwangju exhibited the largest gap between infrastructure capacity (index 0.94) and trial activity (density ratio 0.18). Clinical trial activity in Korea is disproportionately low relative to infrastructure, and region-specific DCT strategies matched to local resource profiles can provide a practical framework for balanced trial participation.

INTRODUCTION

Clinical trials are essential for new drug development, yet geographical access to trials remains unequal. Patients living in rural or underserved regions often have limited access to trial sites, which tend to be concentrated in academic hospitals in metropolitan areas [1]. This geographic concentration restricts participation and reduces the generalizability of trial populations [2]. In South Korea, the problem is particularly pronounced; approximately 30% of trials are conducted in only five centers located in the Seoul metropolitan area [3]. Despite growing recognition of this regional concentration, it has not been adequately addressed.

The use of decentralized elements in clinical trials may help reduce geographical barriers [45]. Decentralized elements refer to trial-related activities conducted outside of traditional trial sites [6]. These elements can be categorized as either remote or face-to-face. Remote components include electronic consent, remote source data verification, and telemedicine visits, none of which require facility-based interaction. Face-to-face activities, on the other hand, involve delegation of trial-related tasks to local healthcare facilities or laboratories. Effective implementation of these elements requires careful evaluation of the existing healthcare system, available trial infrastructure, and trained staff [7].

A resource-based approach to decentralized clinical trials (DCTs) can optimize the real-world implementation of decentralized elements. Whether and how such elements should be adopted in a given country depends critically on the underlying distribution of clinical trial resources, yet this issue has received relatively little systematic attention in the Korean context. Conducting clinical trials requires various elements that are distinct from routine clinical practice. Successful trials require dedicated sites [8], well-trained workforce [9], disease-specific facilities [10], and these resources are often unevenly distributed across a country. Beyond physical capital, systematic governance, education, and investigator engagement are equally important. In short, successful clinical trials require a balanced distribution of trial-related resources.

The challenge becomes more complex in certain therapeutic areas, as the resource requirements differ substantially across major fields of clinical research. Oncology trials, for instance, frequently depend on multidisciplinary tumor boards, advanced imaging, pathology services for central review specimens, and pharmacy capabilities for handling cytotoxic agents. Cardiovascular trials may demand catheterization laboratories, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, and continuous monitoring capabilities. Neurological studies often rely on specialized cognitive assessment tools and neuroimaging infrastructure. Regions without this infrastructure cannot attract trials, which in turn discourages investment in building it [1].

Decentralized trial elements can address several of these barriers. Home-based interventions, including nursing visits and direct-to-patient shipping, reduce the dependence on traditional facility infrastructure [11]. Telemedicine and collaborative trial networks can enable sites with less experience to participate meaningfully in trials. This approach was exemplified by the concept of the ‘teletrial’ model, which emphasized a hub-and-spoke network for trial collaboration in oncology rather than the application of fully decentralized elements [12].

The present study was designed to propose an implementation strategy for decentralized elements through a resource-based analysis of clinical trial infrastructure. Previous studies have typically relied on location-based counts of trial activity, which effectively demonstrate quantitative regional concentration in trial distribution but offer limited insight for interventions focused on resource allocation [31314]. We evaluated the geographical distribution of trial-related resources in the context of major therapeutic areas and proposed optimal strategies for implementing decentralized elements.

METHODS

Data sources

This cross-sectional ecological study analyzed the geographic distribution of clinical trial infrastructure across all 17 first-level administrative divisions of South Korea: Seoul, Busan, Daegu, Incheon, Gwangju, Daejeon, Ulsan, Sejong, Gyeonggi, Gangwon, Chungcheongbuk, Chungcheongnam, Jeollabuk, Jeollanam, Gyeongsangbuk, Gyeongsangnam, and Jeju. Data were obtained from four national sources. First, healthcare workforce and facility data were extracted at the provincial level from the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) via the Korean Statistical Information Service, including the number of medical specialists by specialty, registered nurses, licensed hospital beds, and medical equipment counts by type, based on 2024 reporting year data [15]. Second, patient utilization statistics and regional healthcare resource indicators by province were obtained from the Regional Medical Service Utilization Statistical Yearbook (2024) published by the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) [16] and supplemented with data from the NHIS National Health Insurance Statistical Yearbook (2024) [17]. Third, clinical trial infrastructure data were compiled from the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety, comprising a registry of 208 institutions designated to conduct clinical trials as of 2026 [18] and 1,924 clinical trial approval records spanning 2024 to 2025 [19]. All data were publicly available administrative records. The study relied exclusively on aggregated, de-identified administrative statistics with no individual-level patient information; accordingly, institutional review board approval was not required.

Therapeutic area scenarios and resource requirements

We defined four therapeutic area scenarios based on the multidisciplinary team composition typically required for trial conduct. Each scenario specified a set of primary departments, whose specialist availability was essential for trial feasibility, and support departments, whose presence contributed to but was not strictly required for trial conduct. The four scenarios were as follows.

The first scenario, Oncology, required at least one primary department (internal medicine, general surgery, neurosurgery, thoracic and cardiovascular surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, urology, or radiation oncology) plus pathology, at least one imaging department (diagnostic radiology or nuclear medicine), and laboratory medicine. A province was considered capable of supporting oncology trials only if all four functional groups were adequately represented. The second scenario, Cardiovascular, required at least one primary department (internal medicine or thoracic and cardiovascular surgery) plus diagnostic radiology and laboratory medicine. The third scenario, Neurological, required at least one primary department (neurology or psychiatry) plus diagnostic radiology and laboratory medicine. The fourth scenario, Endocrine, Immunology, and Hematology, required internal medicine and laboratory medicine (Supplementary Table 1).

These configurations reflected the decreasing complexity of multidisciplinary coordination from oncology trials, which demand the broadest specialist involvement, to single-specialty endocrine or hematology studies. Because national statistics on subspecialty fellowship training (e.g., hematology-oncology within internal medicine) were not systematically reported at the provincial level, all specialty-based analyses relied on board-certified specialist counts aggregated at the parent specialty level. Resource requirements for each scenario encompassed 25 items organized into four categories: laboratory equipment (13 items), diagnostic imaging (4 items), and therapeutic devices (3 items). The complete specification of resource items by therapeutic area is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Density normalization and composite index construction

All infrastructure indicators were first converted to density measures expressed per 100,000 patients using the NHIS provincial patient count as the denominator. This normalization accounted for differences in healthcare demand across regions, rather than relying on resident population alone. To enable direct comparison across provinces and resource types, each density measure was then normalized to the Seoul metropolitan area, which was assigned a reference value of 1.0.

For each resource domain (specialists, nurses, beds, and medical equipment), provincial densities were calculated per 100,000 patients and normalized to Seoul as the reference (Seoul = 1.00) [313], and the four Seoul-normalized density ratios were reported separately by province. The geometric mean was chosen over the arithmetic mean because it penalizes extreme imbalance across resource domains: a province with abundant beds but few specialists receives a lower score than one with moderately adequate levels across all four domains, reflecting the practical requirement that clinical trial conduct depends on a minimum threshold in each resource category [78]. The capped geometric-mean construction follows the methodological precedent of the Human Development Index, in which geometric aggregation across normalized domain indices penalizes imbalance and prevents full substitutability between domains [20]. The equal-weight scheme implies that no single domain dominates the composite index; the robustness of this choice was further examined in a sensitivity analysis comparing three alternative weighting schemes.

Each province was then assigned to one of four DCT implementation strategies based on its infrastructure index, specialist density, and trial density. The association between infrastructure capacity and clinical trial activity was examined using Pearson correlation. Direct-to-Patient was assigned when the infrastructure index was at or below 0.50 or when specialist density was at or below 0.50 and trial density was at or below 0.20, on the grounds that conventional site-based trial conduct is infeasible in provinces lacking adequate specialist coverage regardless of capacity in other resource domains. Community-Based Hybrid was assigned to remaining provinces with an infrastructure index above 0.50 and below 0.80, representing moderate infrastructure that could support trial participation through engagement of local healthcare providers. Site Activation was assigned when the infrastructure index was at or above 0.80 but trial density remained below 0.80, indicating adequate infrastructure that had not yet been mobilized for clinical research. Integrated Hub-Spoke Network was reserved for provinces meeting both an infrastructure index and trial density ratio at or above 0.80, identifying regions capable of serving as coordinating centers for multi-site decentralized trials. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Spatial data were handled with the sf package, and multi-panel figures were assembled using the patchwork package.

Cardiovascular composite values ranged from 0.47 (Sejong) to 0.92 (Daegu; CV = 0.22). Neurological composite values ranged from 0.43 (Sejong) to 0.89 (Daejeon; CV = 0.23). Clinical trial activity and DCT implementation strategy

A total of 1,924 clinical trials registered between 2024 and 2025 were mapped to provincial-level site locations, generating 7,760 trial-center pairs (99.0% mapping rate; Table 3). Clinical trial density, normalized to Seoul (= 1.0), varied dramatically across provinces (CV = 0.64): Daegu exhibited the highest non-Seoul density at 0.33, followed by Gyeonggi at 0.21, while Gyeongbuk recorded 0.00 and Sejong 0.03. The CV for clinical trial density (0.64) was substantially higher than that for composite infrastructure (0.16), confirming that trial activity is far more concentrated than the underlying resource base. The Pearson correlation between the composite infrastructure index and trial density was 0.72, indicating a moderate positive association and implying that existing trial site selection is partially explained by measurable infrastructure (Table 3, Fig. 3A). Exclusion of Phase 1 and bioequivalence trials (n = 1,020, 53.0% of all approved trials) yielded a near-identical dispersion (CV 0.65) and a slightly stronger infrastructure-trial correlation (Pearson r = 0.76), indicating that the observed regional concentration is robust to trial-type heterogeneity (Supplementary Table 2). Province rankings of the composite infrastructure index were also robust to weighting choice, with Spearman rank correlations of 0.94 to 0.98 against the equal-weight baseline across three alternative weighting schemes (Supplementary Table 3).

DISCUSSION

This study provides the first systematic, quantitative assessment of clinical trial infrastructure across South Korea, with the quantitative evaluation of implementing DCT strategies. We demonstrated that distribution of clinical trial-related resources varied across regions, and specialist density was the most Seoul-concentrated of the four domains (no non-Seoul province reached 80% of Seoul's level). Industry-driven site selection and logistical optimization, in addition to resource availability, likely contribute to the observed regional concentration of trial activity. The moderate correlation between composite infrastructure scores and trial density (Pearson r = 0.72) indicates that resource availability is a relevant but insufficient predictor of clinical trial conduct. All 16 non-Seoul provinces exhibit trial density ratios below 0.33, with 13 provinces below 0.20, demonstrating extreme concentration of trial activity in the capital far exceeding what infrastructure differences alone would predict.

The results demonstrated that implementation of decentralized elements requires accounting for both resource distribution and other supportive aspects such as investigator training and regulatory education. The decentralization of clinical trials has been framed primarily as a technological and regulatory challenge [21]. However, our data indicate that implementation requires a more nuanced approach. In rural regions such as Gyeongbuk and Sejong, both hospital and specialist densities are critically low, and therefore hospital-independent decentralized elements play an important role. In contrast, regional metropolitan areas such as Busan, Gwangju, and Daejeon maintain infrastructure scores comparable to or exceeding the national median, yet trial activity remains disproportionately low. Gwangju, with a composite score of 0.94, hosts trial activity at only 0.18 relative to Seoul.

In regions where infrastructure is available but trial activity remains low, the primary barriers are structural, including limited institutional experience, insufficient trained research personnel, and lack of established trial workflows. These factors often outweigh patient-level barriers [1] and may discourage sponsors from engaging otherwise resource-adequate sites. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle in which experience concentrates at metropolitan centers while infrastructure-adequate regions are progressively excluded.

The Australian Teletrial Model addresses this gap through a hub-and-spoke cluster in which a primary site supervises satellite sites via telehealth. As satellite sites gain experience through participation, they build capacity to act independently as primary sites, addressing the systemic shortage of trained investigators in regional communities [22]. Canada and New Zealand have since adopted teletrial frameworks as national models for extending trials into regional areas [23]. This approach has been most actively applied in oncology, where protocol complexity demands close hub supervision yet patient populations are widely dispersed. The U.S. NCI Community Oncology Research Program operates on a similar principle, with research bases serving as scientific hubs while community sites accrue patients from underserved and rural populations [24].

Translating this hub-and-spoke logic to the Korean context requires a phased approach reflecting the current resource distribution. Under a Stage 1 configuration with Seoul as the primary hub and non-Seoul provinces functioning strictly as satellites, 10 of the 16 non-Seoul provinces (Busan, Daegu, Incheon, Gwangju, Daejeon, Ulsan, Gyeonggi, Gangwon, Jeonbuk, and Jeju) meet a minimum threshold of 0.50 in both Seoul-normalized specialist density and equipment density, indicating sufficient on-site capacity to host protocol-mandated assessments under remote supervision from Seoul. Under a more demanding Stage 2 configuration in which a non-Seoul province acts as its own regional hub (composite infrastructure index at or above 0.80 and trial density at or above 0.15 of Seoul), only four metropolitan cities qualify: Busan, Daegu, Gwangju, and Daejeon. Across the remaining provinces, specialist density rather than equipment density is the limiting factor (Table 1), indicating that targeted investment in specialist workforce is the principal lever for advancing additional regions to Stage 2. This phased framing reconciles the absence of a non-Seoul Integrated Hub-Spoke province in the main analysis (Fig. 4) with the practical feasibility of an Australian-style network by identifying Seoul as the initial primary hub and characterizing a graduation pathway for regional centers as their infrastructure and trial-conduct experience accumulate.

Where infrastructure itself is fundamentally lacking, a different strategy is required. The teletrial model may be insufficient in these regions because satellite sites lack the clinical departments, equipment, or specialist workforce to function as credible trial partners. Decentralized trial elements allow at-home measurements to substitute for in-site visits, extending participation to rural and mobility-limited populations while reducing coordination workload [25]. Telemedicine enables remote screening, recruitment, care coordination, and outcome assessment, with preliminary evidence suggesting improved diversity and retention [26]. These approaches differ fundamentally from hub-and-spoke networks in that they bypass the need for an equipped satellite site altogether. For infrastructure-deficit regions identified in our analysis, combining direct-to-patient telemedicine, mobile nursing, and wearable-based remote monitoring may represent a more realistic entry point than building conventional trial site capacity.

The resource-based analysis shows that effective DCT implementation cannot follow a uniform national strategy. Korea has been actively pursuing regulatory reform to advance DCT adoption, including the establishment of a public-private consultative body and the launch of a government-backed pilot project [27]. However, our findings suggest that regulatory modernization alone may be insufficient. Geographic concentration in clinical trial infrastructure constitutes an independent barrier that would persist even under an optimally permissive regulatory environment. Previous research has documented the geographic concentration of clinical trial activity in the Seoul metropolitan area and the resulting regional concentration of patient access across Korean regions [3], and this pattern is consistent with international findings that DCT strategies must be tailored to local resource profiles rather than deployed as a single standardized model [28]. Our infrastructure index, which integrates nurse, specialist, bed, and equipment densities into a single composite measure, provides a practical tool for identifying which strategy is appropriate for a given region. Therefore, effective DCT implementation in Korea will require a multi-dimensional approach addressing trial networks, human capital development for research methodology, and IT infrastructure for remote monitoring.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our resource metrics capture quantity rather than quality: specialist counts do not distinguish clinicians with clinical trial training from those without, and conflate clinical practice capacity with research capacity. Second, trial counts treated all 1,924 trials equally despite differing resource demands across phases, although a sensitivity analysis excluding Phase 1 and bioequivalence studies confirmed that the regional concentration pattern was unchanged (Supplementary Table 2). Third, the therapeutic scenarios are necessarily generalized and cannot capture all protocol-specific requirements. Fourth, the illustrative Hub-Spoke impact estimates (Supplementary Fig. 1) assume satellite provinces gain access to a presumptive 30% of the Seoul hub portfolio; actual outcomes will depend on protocol transferability, satellite-site training, and sponsor engagement. Future work could refine the specialist metric using HIRA claim codes (e.g., subspecialty-specific targeted therapy prescriptions) as a proxy for active subspecialty practice. Despite these limitations, this study offers the first resource-based quantitative analysis of geographic resource distribution in Korean clinical trial infrastructure with actionable DCT implementation recommendations.

This study demonstrates that clinical trial infrastructure in South Korea is unevenly distributed across regions and that infrastructure capacity alone does not account for the disparity in trial activity. These findings support a differentiated DCT implementation strategy tailored to each region's resource profile, ranging from hub-and-spoke trial networks to direct-to-patient home-based approaches. Future studies should incorporate investigator experience data, patient accessibility metrics, and pilot evaluations of DCT elements in different regional settings.
