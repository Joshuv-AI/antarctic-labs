Dual CCR2/CCR5 Antagonism with Cenicriviroc Alleviates Nerve Injury-Induced Hypersensitivity and Prevents Morphine Tolerancevia Modulation of Neuroimmune Signaling
Authors: Anna Piotrowska, Joanna Starnowska-Sokół, Katarzyna Ciapała, Justyna Barut, Katarzyna Pawlik, Agata Ciechanowska, Klaudia Kwiatkowski, Grzegorz Kreiner, Joanna Mika
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMounting evidence implicates that chemokine receptors type 2 and 5 play a pivotal role in neuropathic pain. Here, we report for the first time that cenicriviroc, a dual CCR2/CCR5 antagonist, exerts strong, long-lasting, and morphine-potentiating effects in a mouse model of chronic constriction injury (CCI) to the sciatic nerve. The analgesic effect of cenicriviroc was comparable to that of morphine in the initial phase of neuropathy (Day 3) and, rewardingly, superior to the effect of morphine on Days 8 and 16 of twice-daily intraperitoneal administration. Moreover, on Day 16, tolerance to the analgesic effect of morphine was less pronounced in the group that received cenicriviroc before every morphine injection. Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed that CCR2 colocalizes with microglia/macrophages. Luminex analyses revealed time-dependent CCI-induced spinal increases in several kinases (p38, ERK, JNK, Akt), transcription factors (NF-κB, STAT3), and cytokines (IL-10, IL-1β, TNFα, CCL2/3/5). No less importantly, there was a pronounced elevation of pronociceptive mediators (TNFα, IL-1β, CCL2/3/4/5, pERK, and pNF-κB) following prolonged morphine administration over 16 days; all these factors can potentially contribute to the emergence of tolerance and tactile hypersensitivity. In contrast to morphine, cenicriviroc on Day 3 increased the level of antinociceptive IL-10, while decreasing pERK. Furthermore, on Day 8, cenicriviroc increased the level of Akt/STAT3, and decreased pp38/pERK and pronociceptive CCL2/3/5. The obtained results indicate that simultaneous targeting of CCR2/5 by cenicriviroc may have great potential for neuropathic pain therapies, especially since the drug is already in clinical trials for other conditions.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10571-026-01761-3.

Introduction

Neuropathic pain is a serious public health problem due to its diverse etiology and long-term persistence (Baron 2009; Finnerup et al. 2016; Colloca et al. 2017; Zilliox 2017). The multifactorial pathogenesis and complex mechanisms underlying the generation and maintenance of painful symptoms remain the factors that seriously limit the options of a successful treatment (Baron 2009; Mika et al. 2013; van Hecke et al. 2014; Finnerup et al. 2016; Attal 2019). Unfortunately, it is estimated that less than 50% of patients achieve satisfactory pain relief in the course of their treatment (Finnerup et al. 2016; Colloca et al. 2017). Therefore, the need to develop and establish new efficient therapies remains a priority of current research.

In recent years, the importance of the cytokine family as a crucial factor in the development and persistence of neuropathic pain symptoms has been widely recognized (Mika et al. 2013; Kwiatkowski and Mika 2018; Pawlik and Mika 2023; Ciechanowska and Mika 2024). Nerve injury typically triggers the release of various cytokines, including IL-1β, TNFα, CCL2, CCL3, and CCL5; these can intensify neuronal hyperexcitability and add to the phenomenon of pain sensitization, while also exerting anti-opioid effects (Mika et al. 2013; Pilat et al. 2015; Kwiatkowski et al. 2017, 2019; Plein and Rittner 2018; Bogacka et al. 2020a; Pawlik and Mika 2023; Ciechanowska and Mika 2024; Bober et al. 2024a; Pawlik et al. 2025). An activation of cytokine receptors contributes to the initiation of several intracellular signaling cascades (e.g. p38, ERK, JNK, Akt) and transcription factors (e.g. STAT3, NFκB) that are already known to play a significant role in neuropathic pain and thus represent promising targets for therapeutic strategies (Popiolek-Barczyk et al. 2015; Rojewska et al. 2015; Popiolek-Barczyk and Mika 2016; Piotrowska et al. 2016a; Ciapała et al. 2023, 2024; Zajączkowska et al. 2023; Ciapała and Mika 2023). Importantly, some cytokine receptor antagonists are able to effectively attenuate the hypersensitivity that follows the activation of these receptors (Mika et al. 2013; Pilat et al. 2015; Piotrowska et al. 2016a, b; Kwiatkowski and Mika 2018; Kwiatkowski et al. 2020a; Bogacka et al. 2022; Pawlik and Mika 2023).

Among the chemokine receptors, two deserve special attention as potential therapeutic targets: in recent years, it has been proven that the blockade of CCR2 (Zhang et al. 2012; Pevida et al. 2013; Zhu et al. 2014; Piotrowska et al. 2016b; Kwiatkowski et al. 2017, 2019, 2020a) and CCR5 (Kwiatkowski et al. 2016, 2020a; Piotrowska et al. 2016a; Ciechanowska et al. 2022; Zhong et al. 2023; Ruiz-Cantero et al. 2024) provides robust analgesic effects in animal models of neuropathy. Within this area of research, we were the first to prove that intrathecal (i.t.) injection of RS504393 (CCR2 antagonist) reduces tactile/thermal hypersensitivity in a mouse/rat chronic constriction injury (CCI) model of neuropathic pain (Piotrowska et al. 2016b; Kwiatkowski et al. 2017, 2020a). Moreover, others have reported that intracisternal or subcutaneous administration of RS504393 alleviates pain symptoms induced by nerve transection (Zhang et al. 2012) and lumbar disc herniation (Zhu et al. 2014). In parallel, we have shown that CCR5 antagonists - maraviroc (Kwiatkowski et al. 2016, 2020a; Piotrowska et al. 2016a), AZD5672 (Ciechanowska et al. 2022), and TAK-220 (Ciechanowska et al. 2022) - administered intrathecally and/or intraperitoneally, diminish hypersensitivity in the CCI model (Kwiatkowski et al. 2016; Ciechanowska et al. 2023). Clinical trials have also explored blockers of these receptors; BMS-741,672 (CCR2 antagonist) has been tested in individuals with diabetic neuropathic pain (NCT00683423), while maraviroc (CCR5 antagonist) has been approved in HIV-infected patients (Lisi et al. 2012). Importantly, current findings indicate that CCR2 and CCR5 can form heterodimers, particularly upon co-stimulation with shared ligands (Mellado 2001; Salanga et al. 2009; Yuan et al. 2012), which appears to be a noteworthy feature, especially in the context of testing the new drug candidates. For instance, in the CCI model, we have shown that a single injection of cenicriviroc—a drug known to block both receptors in a simultaneous manner—produces greater analgesic efficacy than inhibition of either receptor alone (Kwiatkowski et al. 2020a).

In parallel, in vitro studies have suggested that chemokine and opioid receptors can form heterodimers (Parenty et al. 2008; Arnatt et al. 2016); this shared trait of the two receptors can potentially enable their cross-desensitization and lead to substantial changes in the balance between analgesia and algesia (Szabo et al. 2002), and in consequence be one of the reasons for the insufficient effectiveness of opioids in neuropathic pain. Coherently, we have already proven that repeated i.t. injections of RS504393 (Kwiatkowski et al. 2017), maraviroc (Kwiatkowski et al. 2016) and cenicriviroc (Kwiatkowski et al. 2020b) not only relieve pain, but also improve the analgesic properties of morphine after its single administration on Day 7 in the rat CCI model, with cenicriviroc showing the greatest efficacy among the compounds tested. Therefore, cenicriviroc represents a promising pharmacological strategy for pain relief and a noteworthy drug candidate, especially since recent literature data indicate that it has beneficial effects in other various disease states, including HIV infection (Riviere-Cazaux et al. 2022; Umumararungu et al. 2024), and liver fibrosis in non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (Friedman et al. 2016; Soto and Lim 2020; Anstee et al. 2024), and has been evaluated in related clinical trials related to them (Thompson et al. 2016; Tacke 2018).

Given the available literature and promising results obtained by our group (Piotrowska et al. 2016a, b; Kwiatkowski et al. 2017, 2020a, b; Bober et al. 2024b), we sought to continue this line of research and determine the molecular effects of the dual CCR2/CCR5 antagonist, cenicriviroc, in neuropathic pain. In the present work, we examined the influence of its repeated administration on pain-related behaviors and morphine tolerance/opioid-induced hypersensitivity (OIH) development in mice on Days 3, 8, 12, 14, and 16 after CCI. In addition, we assessed the levels of IBA-1-positive cells in the spinal cord and investigated the colocalization of CCR2 and CCR5 with markers of microglia/macrophages. To define and compare the molecular mechanisms of cenicriviroc and morphine action, we studied the changes in the expression of nociceptive cytokines (IL-10, TNFα, IL-1β, CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5), kinases (p38, ERK, JNK, Akt) and transcription factors (NF-κB, STAT3) at the spinal cord level in the course of the 16-day treatment, taking into consideration the phosphorylated and total forms of the studied proteins after repeated administration of drugs, and their combination. Altogether, the employment of behavioral and biochemical methods allowed us to study the molecular correlates of the observed phenomena (analgesic effects under nerve injury conditions, tolerance development, opioid-induced hypersensitivity in long-term treatment) in a time- and drug-dependent manner.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Ethics Statement

Male Albino Swiss CD-1® IGS mice (weighing 30–35 g), sourced from Charles River Breeding Laboratories in Germany, were kept in the cages, lined with sawdust and maintained on a standard 12-hour light/dark cycle (lights on at 6:00 a.m.), with unrestricted access to food and water. All experimental procedures complied with the guidelines of the International Association for the Study of Pain (Zimmermann 1983) and the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and were approved by the local Ethics Committee in Krakow, Poland (approval numbers: 215/2021 and 111/2023).

Neuropathic Pain Model - Surgical Procedure

A mouse model of neuropathic pain was established using the chronic constriction injury (CCI) of the sciatic nerve, following the protocol originally described by Bennett and Xie (Bennett and Xie 1988) and based on a previously standardized laboratory procedure (Starnowska-Sokół et al. 2020; Bogacka et al. 2020b). The surgery was conducted under isoflurane anesthesia. An incision was made below the right hip of the mouse to expose the sciatic nerve, around which three ligatures were loosely tied using 4/0 silk thread, spaced approximately 1 mm apart, until a brief contraction of the corresponding hind limb was observed.

Compounds and Administration Procedure

The experiment utilized cenicriviroc (CVC; Axon MedChem, Groningen, the Netherlands) and morphine hydrochloride (M; Fagron, Krakow, Poland). Both compounds were administered via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection. Cenicriviroc was freshly prepared each day by dissolving it in sterile water for injections containing 1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), while morphine was dissolved in sterile water for injections. Experimental design and tissue sampling strategy for tolerance development assessment were as follows: Male mice received i.p. injections of tested compounds twice daily—once in the morning and once in the evening—with an 8 to 12-hour break between doses. Animals were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 10–12 per group): (1) naive controls (N); (2) vehicle controls receiving 1% DMSO and water for injection (V1 + V2); (3) cenicriviroc with water for injection (CVC+V2); (4) 1% DMSO with morphine (V1 + M); and (5) cenicriviroc combined with morphine (CVC + M). The doses of cenicriviroc (1 mg/kg) and morphine (20 mg/kg) were selected based on previous studies (Kwiatkowski et al. 2020a, b; Bober et al. 2024b). Pharmacological tools administration began with a preemptive dose given in the evening before CCI surgery, in line with the previously established experimental scheme, to inhibit early glial activation and limit the development of neuropathic pain (Mika et al. 2009; Pawlik et al. 2025). This was followed by continued administration twice daily starting on the day of surgery (designated as Day 0). Behavioral assessments of pain hypersensitivity were performed on Days 3, 8, 12, 14, and 16 after CCI. Subsets of animals from each group were euthanized for tissue collection on Days 3, 8, and 16, while the naive group was sacrificed on Day 16 for protein analysis.

Behavioral Tests

Von Frey Test

Mechanical sensitivity to non-noxious stimuli was assessed using a series of calibrated nylon monofilaments with increasing force values ranging from 0.16 to 6 g (Ugo Basile, Gemonio, Italy), following the protocol outlined previously (Mika et al. 2007, 2009; Ciapała et al. 2024). Before testing, mice were acclimated for 5 min in individual plastic cages. Each filament was then applied manually to the plantar surface of the affected hind paw until a behavioral reaction was elicited. Pain-like responses were defined as rapid paw withdrawal, shaking, or licking. The minimum force (in grams) that caused the paw withdrawal was recorded, with a maximum cutoff force set at 6 g. In the case of naive animals, both hind paws were tested.

Cold Plate Test

Thermal sensitivity to painful stimuli was evaluated using the Cold/Hot Plate Analgesia Meter (Ugo Basile, Gemonio, Italy), following the method described previously (Ciapała et al. 2024). The plate temperature was kept constant at 2 °C. Mice were placed on the cold surface, and the latency to exhibit a pain-related response was measured. Pain-like behaviors included quick withdrawal, shaking, or licking of the affected limb. A cutoff time of 30 s was applied to prevent tissue damage. In the case of naive animals, both hind paws were tested.

Biochemical Tests

Immunofluorescence Staining

Immunofluorescence analysis was used to qualitatively verify the localization and distribution of receptors in microglia/macrophages. At several time points after CCI, additional groups of mice were sacrificed, their spinal cords were isolated, and fixed overnight at 4 °C in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), as previously described (Rojewska et al. 2018; Ciechanowska et al. 2022). After dehydration, the tissues were embedded in paraffin and cut into 7 μm sections using a Leica RM45 microtome. Consecutive coronal sections from the L4–L6 region of the spinal cord, obtained from both naive and CCI-treated mice, were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-CCR2 (1:100, Novus Biologicals, Cat# NBP1-48337, RRID: AB_10011101), rabbit anti-CCR5 (1:100, Novus Biologicals, Cat# NBP2-31374, RRID: AB_3278584), and rat anti-IBA1 (1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab283346, RRID: AB_3065282). Antigen-antibody complexes were detected using Alexa Fluor 488 (1:100, Invitrogen, Cat# A-21206; RRID: AB_2535792) or 594-conjugated (1:100, Invitrogen, Cat# A-21203, RRID: AB_2535789) donkey secondary antibodies. Nuclear staining was performed using Hoechst 33,342 (Invitrogen, Cat# R37165). Antibody validation follows the available guidelines (Bordeaux et al. 2010; Uhlen et al. 2016) and other requirements for authentication of key biological resources (National Institutes of Health), with validation data available in the links provided in the Antibody Validation section. Panoramic images of the lumbar L4-L6 area of the spinal cord were obtained at RT temperature with a widefield microscope (Leica Thunder widefield fluorescence microscope; Leica Microsystems CMS GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) at 10x magnification (HC PL FLUOTAR 10x/0.30 DRY) with DA/FI/TX, FITC, and TXR filter sets. The detailed sample set was imaged at room temperature using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems TCS SP8 X; Leica Microsystems CMS GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) at 63x magnifications (HC PL APO CS2 1.40 NA OIL). Image acquisition was performed bidirectionally along the X-axis with a scanning frequency of 200 Hz, an image format of 2048 × 2048 (pixel size 90 nm), and a digital magnification of 1.0. Analysis was performed using ImageJ v1.54p software (Wayne Rasband, National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) with default parameters. In the present study, sections collected on day 7 after CCI were used for representative imaging. Immunofluorescence experiments were performed only to qualitatively assess the localization of CCR2 and CCR5 within spinal cord microglia/macrophages.

Luminex Analysis of the Inflammatory Biomarker Panel

For the analysis of protein level changes using MILLIPLEX® Multiplex Assays with Luminex® Technology, tissues were collected from the ipsilateral lumbar section (L4–L6) of the spinal cord. Samples were obtained from both CCI-exposed and naive mice following decapitation on Days 3, 8, and 16 post-CCI. The time points were chosen based on observed behavioral changes related to the development of tolerance following repeated administration of morphine and cenicriviroc. Tissue samples were initially placed into 2 ml plastic Eppendorf tubes containing RIPA buffer supplemented with a broad-spectrum protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The samples were then homogenized by centrifugation following previously established protocols described (Liu and Fagotto 2011; Baghirova et al. 2015; Ciechanowska et al. 2022). Total protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay. Equal amounts of total protein were used for each assay according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. All samples were analyzed in duplicate, and the amount of protein loaded per well was consistent across all samples. Cell signaling multiplex assays (MILLIPLEX®MAP 9-plex Multi-Pathway Signaling Mag Bead Kit - Phosphoprotein, 48-680MAG; and MILLIPLEX®MAP 9-plex Multi-Pathway Total Magnetic Bead Kit, 48-681MAG; Merck, Burlington, MA, USA) were employed to measure the levels of phosphoproteins and total proteins involved in the following intracellular signaling pathways in each sample, according to the manufacturer’s instructions: p38, pp38, ERK, pERK, JNK, pJNK, NF-κB, pNF-κB, Akt, pAkt, STAT3, and pSTAT3. Additionally, concentrations of cytokines IL-1β, IL-10, TNFα, CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, and CCL5 were quantified using the MILLIPLEX® MAP Mouse Cytokine Chemokine Magnetic Bead Panel Immunology Multiplex Assay, MCYTOMAG-70 K (Merck) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Data and Statistical Analyses

The pain-related behavioral data (von Frey test and cold plate test, Fig. 1) are presented as the means ± SD. The Luminex results (Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7) are presented as fold changes relative to those of the controls (naive) ± SD. To distinguish injury-related effects from treatment-related effects, statistical analyses comprised comparisons of all groups versus naïve animals as well as comparisons among CCI-treated groups, with the V1 + V2 group defined as the injured control. An a priori sample size calculation was performed using the method described (Kadam and Bhalerao 2010) to ensure adequate statistical power (power = 0.8, α = 0.05). The number of replicates for each experiment is specified in the Methods section and in all figure legends. The group sizes (n = 10–12 mice per group) were determined based on preliminary data variability and expected effect sizes, which indicated that this sample size is sufficient to detect medium to large effects. Post-hoc power analyses confirmed that n = 12 provides adequate power for the observed differences between groups. The adequacy of sample sizes was determined based on preliminary data variability and expected effect sizes. Data were first tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test (α = 0.001) and for homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. If data did not meet the assumptions of normality and/or homogeneity (Fig. 1), nonparametric tests (Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc correction (B, D) or Mann-Whitney test (C, E) were applied. For normally distributed data with homogeneous variances (Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7), parametric tests (one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post hoc test) were used as appropriate. Statistical analysis of the hypersensitivity measurements performed in the von Frey (Fig. 1C) and cold plate (Fig. 1E) tests between the two groups (V1 + V2 and V1 + M) at 3 time points was performed using the Mann-Whitney test. The results of the biochemical experiments were statistically evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The differences between the treatment groups throughout the study were further analyzed with Bonferroni’s post hoc correction. Capital P values refer to the overall significance of the Kruskal–Wallis, Mann–Whitney, or ANOVA tests, whereas lowercase p values indicate post hoc comparisons between groups; post hoc significance is reported in the Results section and indicated on the figures using symbols. Randomization was performed when assigning samples or animals to experimental groups to minimize bias. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Results are reported in APA format, including the test statistic, degrees of freedom, and exact p-values (Results sections). For statistical analyses, GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Prism Software Inc., USA) was used.

Results

The Influence of the Repeated Twice-Daily Intraperitoneal Administration of Cenicriviroc on Tactile and Thermal Hypersensitivity Development and the Analgesic Effects of Morphine in Male Mice After Chronic Constriction Injury of the Sciatic Nerve (Fig.1; Scheme Fig. 1A)

Compared with naive animals, the V1 + V2-treated CCI-exposed group displayed pain-like behaviors manifesting as a significantly (***p < 0.001) lower response threshold to tactile (Fig. 1B) and thermal (Fig. 1D) stimuli at all time points tested (Days 3, 8, 16 after CCI). Morphine (V1 + M) showed high analgesic potential, observed at the first time point (Day 3) in both the von Frey (Fig. 1B; ###p < 0.001) and cold plate (Fig. 1D; ###p < 0.001) tests. However, in the following days, its analgesic activity gradually decreased, which indicates the development of tolerance to its analgesic effects. From Day 8, its analgesic effect was no longer observed in both tests, and additionally, on Day 16, there was a paradoxical increase in hypersensitivity to tactile stimuli (known as opioid-induced hypersensitivity; OIH) after long-term morphine administration (Fig. 1B). OIH was confirmed in the von Frey, but not in the cold plate test, on Days 12 (##p < 0.01), 14 (###p < 0.001), and 16 (###p < 0.001) after CCI and long-term morphine (V1 + M) administration (Fig. 1C, E). However, the analgesic effect of cenicriviroc was maintained at all time points studied (3, 8, 16 Days after CCI), both when it was administered alone (CVC+V2) and in combination with morphine (CVC + M), which indicates its high analgesic potential and ability to improve the efficacy of morphine (Fig. 1B, D). CCL5 was another chemokine that increased in the V1 + V2 group only at later time points—namely on Day 8 (***p < 0.001) and Day 16 (**p < 0.01)—in comparison with naïve animals (Fig. 7D). Interestingly, the administration of cenicriviroc in both experimental groups (CVC+V2 and CVC + M) increased CCL5 levels as compared to the naive (***p < 0.001) as early as on Day 3. In contrast, in the V1 + M group, no effect on CCL5 levels on Day 3 was observed. On Day 8 after CCI, there was a significant increase in CCL5 release in all groups tested (***p < 0.001); however, the mentioned effect was stronger in the V1 + M than the V1 + V2 group (#p < 0.05). Importantly, at this time point, the decrease of CCL5 expression was detected in animals receiving CVC+V2 compared to the V1 + V2 group (#p < 0.05). On Day 16, CCL5 remained elevated versus naive in all CCI groups, albeit to a lesser extent, and the highest level was again observed in the V1 + M (***p < 0.001), and the lowest (*p < 0.05) in the CVC + V2 group. Discussion

Our findings provide the first preclinical evidence that cenicriviroc not only attenuates the development of morphine tolerance but also exerts a strong and long-lasting analgesic effect on its own after repeated administration under nerve injury conditions, being particularly efficient in reducing tactile hypersensitivity. These results offer new insights into the potential utility of CCR2/5 blockade as a strategy in neuropathic pain treatment. Following the available literature (Mika et al. 2013; Popiolek-Barczyk and Mika 2016; Pawlik and Mika 2023; Ciapała and Mika 2023; Ciechanowska and Mika 2024), we observed the time-dependent spinal increases in kinases (p38, ERK, JNK, and Akt), transcription factors (NF-κB, and STAT3), and cytokines (IL-10, IL-1β, TNFα, CCL2, CCL3, and CCL5). The repeated morphine administration was associated with overactivation of intracellular signaling cascades, as evidenced by elevated levels of pERK, pNF-κB, and all examined pronociceptive cytokines (IL-1β, TNFα, CCL2-5), highlighting a possible mechanistic basis for the development of tolerance and OIH observed on Day 16. In contrast to morphine, cenicriviroc enhanced antinociceptive IL-10 while concurrently suppressing pERK on Day 3. Notably, on Day 8, cenicriviroc, as compared to vehicle, sustained low pERK and pp38 activity, boosted Akt and STAT3 signaling, and further attenuated the expression of pronociceptive chemokines CCL2, CCL3, and CCL5. The temporal specificity of these effects suggests that cenicriviroc modifies both the early inflammatory response and subsequent signaling adaptations that shape pain trajectories over time. Altogether, these findings provide the rationale for further investigation of cenicriviroc in neuropathic pain states and suggest that targeting chemokine signaling may represent a promising avenue for the development of more effective, non-opioid-based pain therapies. As several of the detected effect endpoints show time × treatment interactions, the observed benefits are unlikely to reflect a uniform shift in baseline nociception and instead indicate true capacity to positively modulate disease progression.

The idea of using cenicriviroc, the dual CCR2/5 antagonist, emerged based on our previous pharmacological results obtained in rat/mice neuropathic pain models (Kwiatkowski et al. 2020a, b; Bober et al. 2024b). Currently, many studies have highlighted the significant role of CCR2 and CCR5 in nociceptive transmission (Gao et al. 2009b; Kwiatkowski et al. 2016; Piotrowska et al. 2016a, b), and their presence on neurons is well established in the literature (Yagura et al. 2020; Komiya et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2025). Importantly, it has been shown that mice lacking CCR2 (Abbadie et al. 2003) or CCR5 (Lee et al. 2013) develop hypersensitivity to a lesser extent. Furthermore, not only is the expression of CCR2 and CCR5 increased in the spinal cord during neuropathy, but also their pharmacological blockade with single-target antagonists has been shown to have analgesic properties (Gao and Ji 2010; Zhu et al. 2014; Kwiatkowski et al. 2016, 2017; Piotrowska et al. 2016b). Taking the above into account, we hypothesized that due to the complex pathophysiology of neuropathy, the use of cenicriviroc may affect the function of multiple pronociceptive ligands of these receptors, e.g. CCL2-5 (Campbell and Meyer 2006; Baron et al. 2010; Colloca et al. 2017; Finnerup et al. 2021). Notably, in our previous studies, a single administration of cenicriviroc demonstrated greater analgesic potential than the single-target antagonists RS504393 (CCR2) and maraviroc (CCR5), and was effective at significantly lower doses than either of those compounds (Kwiatkowski et al. 2020a). In our current studies, we demonstrate for the first time the long-lasting effectiveness of cenicriviroc administered i.p. in alleviating both mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity and its impact on enhancing the effectiveness of morphine after CCI. It should be noted that these experiments were conducted exclusively in male mice. However, our previous work showed that cenicriviroc produces long-lasting analgesia and may delay morphine tolerance in female mice in a diabetic neuropathy model (Bober et al. 2024b), suggesting that the beneficial effects observed here could potentially extend across sexes. The present study examined molecular changes associated with combined drug treatment. These findings underscore the importance of considering sex as a biological variable in neuropathic pain research and highlight the need for future studies assessing treatment efficacy in both males and females, as well as under conditions that more closely reflect established neuropathy.

Characteristically, cenicriviroc exhibited a particularly strong effect in reducing tactile hypersensitivity, whereas its impact on thermal was comparatively weaker. This is an important finding because tactile allodynia is a common and challenging symptom in patients with peripheral nerve injury. It is characterized by pain in response to normally innocuous tactile stimuli, where light touch activates pathways that typically convey the sensation of touch but, due to central sensitization, instead elicit pain. Our findings align with existing literature, which suggests that chemokine receptor antagonists act more effectively against tactile hypersensitivity, for instance, RS504393 in sickle cell disease mice with chronic pain (Sadler et al. 2018). Moreover, it was shown that CCL2, the main ligand of CCR2, to a greater extent induces tactile than thermal hypersensitivity (Thacker et al. 2009; Kwiatkowski et al. 2019). Importantly, repeated morphine administration in the CCI model also induced tactile, but not thermal OIH, indicating modality-specific regulation of pain pathways. In line with this observation, we found that the levels of pronociceptive chemokines (CCL2–CCL5) were increased in neuropathy. Studies to date have shown that, of these chemokines, CCL2 contributes most to tactile hypersensitivity (Kwiatkowski et al. 2019). However, the literature data do not allow us to clearly attribute this effect solely to the signaling of this chemokine (Araldi et al. 2019; Kearns et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2022; Han et al. 2024; Yao et al. 2024; Sypek et al. 2024; Yin et al. 2024), and further studies are required to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Our data confirmed CCR2 colocalization with microglial/macrophage markers. For CCR5, however, colocalization appeared weak and inconsistent, making it difficult to draw firm conclusions about its cellular localization. At the same time, we observed pronounced activation of microglia/macrophages in the ipsilateral spinal cord. This is—at least regarding the CCR2 data—consistent with the available literature, which indicates a role of these cells in neuropathic pain and OIH (Gao et al. 2009b; Vallejo et al. 2010; Todd 2010; Mika et al. 2013; Kwiatkowski et al. 2017; Reiss et al. 2022; Terminel et al. 2022). Taking into account the available literature data (Kwiatkowski et al. 2016, 2017, 2019, 2020a, b; Thompson et al. 2016; Piotrowska et al. 2016a, b; Pawlik and Mika 2023; Bober et al. 2024b) and our current results, we hypothesize that cenicriviroc may exert its analgesic effects by directly inhibiting pronociceptive signaling in microglia/macrophages mainly through CCR2 blockade, and by silencing neuronal excitability via antagonism of CCR2 and—putatively—CCR5 as well. CCR2 is robustly upregulated on microglia and infiltrating monocyte-derived macrophages after nerve injury, which favors clear colocalization with microglial/macrophage markers. By contrast, CCR5 expression in the spinal cord is lower, more cell-type diverse (reported on subsets of neurons, astrocytes, and immune cells), and more dynamically regulated by ligand exposure. CCR5 also undergoes rapid ligand-induced internalization and recycling and can heterodimerize with other G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs; including CCR2 and opioid receptors), all of which can reduce membrane signal and epitope accessibility in fixed tissue (Liebick et al. 2016). These receptor-specific features, together with timing (snapshot vs. transient peaks), antibody sensitivity, and potential species/antibody differences, provide a plausible explanation for strong CCR2–glia colocalization and weaker, less consistent CCR5-glia colocalization in our study.

Endogenous CCR2 and CCR5 ligands lead to the activation of certain kinases and transcription factors during the development of neuropathy. It has been known for years that nerve damage increases spinal MAPKs levels (Wen et al. 2009; Gao et al. 2009b). Several studies, including our own, have shown that intrathecal injection of p38 inhibitors [SB203580 (Jin et al. 2003; Tsuda et al. 2004; Rojewska et al. 2014), FR167653 (Wen et al. 2009)], ERK [PD198306 (Ciruela et al. 2003), U0126 (Han et al. 2011; Popiolek-Barczyk et al. 2014), PD98059 (Zhuang et al. 2005a; Rojewska et al. 2015)] and JNK [D-JNKI-1JNK (Zhuang et al. 2006; Gao et al. 2009a), SP600125 (Hervera et al. 2012; Okerman et al. 2021)] reduces pain-related behavior in models of neuropathy. Our results demonstrate a consistent upregulation of total p38 expression at all examined time points following CCI, with the most pronounced increase observed on Day 16. This late upregulation may indicate a prolonged or secondary recruitment of microglia and macrophages in response to CCI, potentially playing a role in the maintenance of enhanced nociceptive processing underlying neuropathy. Interestingly, at the early time point (Day 3), total p38 levels were downregulated by both cenicriviroc and morphine administered alone; however, this regulatory effect was not maintained at later time points. One possible explanation is that pharmacological intervention administered before or soon after injury is effective before strong activation of microglial cells occurs. It is worth noting that p38, particularly in its phosphorylated form (pp38), is widely recognized as a microglia/macrophages activation marker in the context of neuropathic pain (Jin et al. 2003; Ji and Suter 2007; Ji et al. 2014). In our study, pp38 levels significantly increased on Days 3 and 8 after CCI, which was reduced by the administration of morphine, cenicriviroc, or their combination. It should be mentioned that we have already demonstrated in rats that repeated cenicriviroc administration diminishes microglia/macrophage activation during neuropathy (Bober et al. 2024b). Interestingly, despite a persistent increase in total p38 expression in all CCI-exposed mice, no differences in pp38 levels were observed among the study groups by Day 16, which may suggest the involvement of other cell populations, such as infiltrating macrophages (Jin et al. 2003; Takeura et al. 2019) and neutrophils (Pawlik et al. 2022). Moreover, our study has shown that another MAP kinase, ERK1/2, is consistently elevated following CCI from the early to late phases of the experiment. Surprisingly, morphine treatment can slightly reduce ERK1/2 levels on Day 16. In contrast, pERK was consistently downregulated at all examined time points post-CCI, aligning with previous reports suggesting that pERK activation may peak within hours after injury (Ji et al. 2002, 2009) and subsequently stabilize or decline over the following Days (Song et al. 2005). This apparently opposite pattern between pERK and total ERK1/2 likely reflects independent regulation of ERK activity and protein levels: in this view, phosphorylation is a rapid, early response to injury, while total ERK protein may remain elevated or increase over time to support longer-term cellular adaptations and regeneration, potentially also reflecting infiltration or recruitment of additional cells to the spinal cord (Zhuang et al. 2005b; Costigan et al. 2009; Orr and Gensel 2018), as particularly described for microglia and macrophages following sciatic nerve injury (Mika et al. 2013). On day 16, phosphorylated forms of p38 and JNK remained high, while pERK1/2 levels showed the reverse pattern. This may reflect a temporal shift in cellular contributions, with microglia and macrophages playing a reduced role (Mika et al. 2013), and strongly activated astrocytes assuming an increasingly prominent function, although the mechanisms underlying this transition require further investigation. It is also worth considering that the elevated total ERK levels may trigger negative feedback mechanisms via MAP kinase phosphatases, leading to the observed reduction in pERK1/2; such regulation could serve as an adaptive response to prevent excessive neuronal damage, although this phenomenon in neuropathic pain remains largely unexplored, and inconsistent data and gaps in the literature highlight the need for further investigation. The issue appears even more complex when the effects of pharmacological intervention are considered. Cenicriviroc, after CCI, reduced p-ERK levels and produced analgesia without signs of tolerance, likely by inhibiting inflammation. In contrast, prolonged morphine increased pERK compared to the vehicle-treated CCI group, and the increase correlated with the emergence of tolerance and OIH. However, the precise mechanisms underlying these observations remain unclear and require further investigation.

Among MAPKs, JNK also likely plays a significant role in neuropathic pain (Gao and Ji 2008; Gao et al. 2009b), and this hypothesis is further strengthened by our current findings. Herein, we have shown that both phosphorylated and total JNK exhibited a generally similar time-dependent pattern of expression after CCI, with elevated levels at early (Day 3) and late (Day 16) time points, but with no apparent changes in comparison to naive animals on Day 8. Previous studies have demonstrated that JNK activation shows distinct temporal profiles in different cell populations after nerve injury, with transient activation in microglia/macrophages and more sustained activation in spinal astrocytes, differentially engages JNK signaling at different stages of development and maintenance of neuropathic pain (Zhuang et al. 2006). Shortly after the injury, in the early phase of neuropathy, the JNK activation is primarily associated with microglial and macrophages activation/influx and the acute release of pronociceptive cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, CCL2; (Clark et al. 2013; Huang and Tsai 2016). Around Day 8, microglial signaling is at its peak, influencing astrocyte activation, while compensatory anti-inflammatory mechanisms begin, preceding full astrocyte-driven neuroinflammation (Clark et al. 2013; Donnelly et al. 2020). In the late phase of neuropathy increase in pJNK and total JNK (Day 16) is likely due to astrocyte-driven neuroinflammation, sustaining neuropathic pain (Gao et al. 2009b). Thus, the Day 8 pattern may represent a transitional phase between early microglial-driven initiation and later astrocyte-mediated maintenance (Donnelly et al. 2020). Notably, on Day 3 after CCI, we observed a reduction in pJNK levels in all drug-treated mice, which may be associated with analgesic effects provided by the treatments. Nevertheless, the expression pattern of all total MAPKs on Day 16 after CCI appears to be similar despite the different pharmacological effects of the compounds used, which is probably related to the dualistic role of MAPKs, as their activation may influence the production of both pro- and antinociceptive factors. On the whole, the divergence between phospho-signaling and total protein abundance highlights the importance of spatiotemporal and cell-type-specific readouts when interpreting MAPK data in the context of chemokine receptor modulation.

The PI3K/Akt signaling cascade is also activated during neuropathy, and early studies suggested that its inhibition reduces pain symptoms (Popiolek-Barczyk and Mika 2016; Ciapała and Mika 2023). The Akt inhibitor, MK-2206, has been shown to attenuate pain-like behaviors in a rat model of chemotherapy-induced neuropathic pain (Li et al. 2016). However, emerging evidence suggests that the role of the PI3K/Akt pathway is more complex. Although activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway has been shown to reduce CCI-induced pain behaviors in mice (Ciapała et al. 2023), different in vitro studies have shown that, under certain conditions, activation of this pathway can also suppress pronociceptive mediators while simultaneously promoting antinociceptive responses (Jana et al. 2007; Tarassishin et al. 2011; Park et al. 2012; Saponaro et al. 2012). In the present study, nerve injury alone did not increase total Akt levels at early time points (days 3 and 8), whereas morphine, cenicriviroc, or their combination transiently increased total Akt levels, likely reflecting early drugs-induced activation of intracellular signaling pathways. Although there are no direct reports describing the effects of cenicriviroc on Akt/PI3K signaling in neuropathic pain models, cenicriviroc, as a CCR2/CCR5 antagonist, modulates neuroinflammatory processes that are closely linked to microglial activation and proinflammatory cytokine release, and these are known regulators of pPI3K/Akt signaling (Guo et al. 2017). Thus, cenicriviroc may indirectly influence Akt activity through modulation of the inflammatory microenvironment. In contrast, morphine has been shown to activate PI3K/Akt signaling in several experimental models, both after acute and repeated administration (Cunha et al. 2010; Xu et al. 2014). From Day 8, the analgesic effect of morphine diminished, indicating the development of tolerance, whereas cenicriviroc maintained its antinociceptive efficacy; this behavioral profile corresponded to persistently elevated total Akt levels across treatment groups. This temporal pattern supports the notion of dynamic, phase-dependent regulation of PI3K/Akt signaling, in which early activation may contribute to pronociceptive mechanisms, while later Akt upregulation may reflect adaptive or compensatory responses limiting inflammation and promoting cell survival, as suggested by in vitro studies demonstrating anti-inflammatory and antinociceptive effects of Akt activation (Jana et al. 2007; Tarassishin et al. 2011; Park et al. 2012; Saponaro et al. 2012). In addition, on Day 16, total Akt levels were increased in the CCI group, but treatment with morphine or cenicriviroc alone did not further modify Akt expression. This lack of effect may result from desensitization and internalization of MOR and CCR2/CCR5, as chronic stimulation of these GPCRs is known to attenuate downstream signaling, including the PI3K/Akt pathway. Supporting this interpretation, chronic morphine exposure engages adaptive mechanisms that limit further Akt activation (Cunha et al. 2010; Xu et al. 2014). In contrast, combined treatment with morphine and cenicriviroc significantly reduced total Akt and pAkt levels compared with the cenicriviroc group on Day 16. This effect may reflect interference with receptor crosstalk or heterodimerization, thereby overcoming adaptive regulatory mechanisms engaged during monotherapy. Taken together, these findings indicate that simultaneous modulation of opioid and chemokine receptors can differentially affect Akt signaling in the late phase of neuropathy. Given that PI3K/Akt signaling may exert both pronociceptive and protective effects depending on timing, cell type, and injury context, the precise mechanisms underlying these interactions require further investigation.

It is well established that hypersensitivity is associated with elevated levels of transcription factors such as NF-κB and STAT3 (Sakaue et al. 2001; Dominguez et al. 2008; Tsuda et al. 2011; Ciapała and Mika 2023). Accordingly, modulation of these factors has been shown to exert beneficial analgesic effects (Meunier et al. 2007; Rojewska et al. 2015; Ciapała et al. 2023; Ciapała and Mika 2023). NF-κB is a well-characterized pleiotropic transcription factor that plays a crucial role in nociceptive processing, as it regulates the expression of both pro- and anti-nociceptive mediators (Ma and Bisby 1998; Lee et al. 2004; Meunier et al. 2007; Miyoshi et al. 2008; Tchivileva et al. 2009; Pan et al. 2010; Popiolek-Barczyk et al. 2014, 2015; Rojewska et al. 2015). Preclinical studies have demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB reduces hypersensitivity in animal models (Meunier et al. 2007; Zhang et al. 2017). We also confirmed that NF-κB level is elevated at all examined time points following CCI; however, cenicriviroc and morphine diminished its level only on Day 3, which correlates well with their analgesic properties in the initial phase. Despite the loss of morphine’s analgesic efficacy by Day 16 - unlike cenicriviroc - NF-κB and pNF-κB levels remained comparable between these groups, a finding that warrants further investigation. A similar regulation pattern as in the case of NF-κB was also detected for STAT3, and the results obtained are consistent with reports by others (Yamauchi et al. 2006; Dominguez et al. 2008, 2010; Wang et al. 2014; Popiolek-Barczyk et al. 2015). It is known that in neuropathy, STAT3 activation is primarily associated with microglial/macrophage activation (Dominguez et al. 2010; Molet et al. 2015), and its pharmacological inhibition has been suggested to reduce hypersensitivity (Dominguez et al. 2008). Based on these data, we hypothesized that cenicriviroc would reduce STAT3 levels, given that our previous study demonstrated its ability to suppress microglial/macrophage activation/infiltration (Kwiatkowski et al. 2020b). Unexpectedly, at the early time point (Day 3), phosphorylation of STAT3 was reduced by drug treatment, which coincided with their robust analgesic effects. In contrast, at later stages (Days 8 and 16), STAT3 phosphorylation appeared to be driven predominantly by nerve injury and was no longer significantly modulated by pharmacological intervention. Notably, total STAT3 showed a distinct time-dependent pattern in all CCI groups that did not strictly parallel changes in STAT3 phosphorylation and behavioral outcomes. On Day 3, early increases in total STAT3 following morphine or combined treatment likely reflect acute transcriptional activation, corresponding to strong antinociceptive effects. On day 8, an injury-induced increase in STAT3 expression was observed, together with an additional rise after cenicriviroc administration, which may reflect adaptive or compensatory changes contributing to the upregulation of analgesia-related factors, as supported by the concomitant upregulation of IL-10. By Day 16, total STAT3 remained elevated due to nerve injury; however, treatment with morphine alone or in combination with cenicriviroc significantly reduced STAT3 levels, suggesting the involvement of adaptive feedback mechanisms, including receptor desensitization and changes in transcriptional regulation. Indeed, in vitro studies provide a potential explanation for this finding, as they suggest that under certain conditions, STAT3 activation may promote polarization of microglia toward the M2 phenotype (Koscsó et al. 2013; Cianciulli et al. 2015; Popiolek-Barczyk et al. 2015). Importantly, these observations highlight the dual role of STAT3, which can contribute both pronociceptive and antinociceptive signaling. For instance, STAT3 activation has been linked to M2 polarization induced by parthenolide, accompanied by increased expression of IL-10 (Popiolek-Barczyk et al. 2015), which is consistent with the elevated IL-10 levels observed in the present study. Similarly, treatment with maraviroc, a CCR5 antagonist, increased IL-10 in parallel with elevated STAT3 in the dorsal root ganglia, suggesting the promotion of antinociceptive signaling (Kwiatkowski et al. 2016, 2020a; Piotrowska et al. 2016a; Ciechanowska et al. 2022; Zhong et al. 2023; Ruiz-Cantero et al. 2024). Thus, increased total STAT3 expression at later time points may represent a compensatory or protective response rather than a direct driver of pain symptoms. Taken together, STAT3 can play a dualistic role in drug effectiveness in neuropathic pain. This complexity may underlie the lack of direct correlation between behavioral outcomes and transcription factors expression following drug administration in our experiment. Further studies are needed to clarify the precise contribution of transcription factors to the analgesic effects of cenicriviroc.

The kinases and transcription factors discussed above are key drivers of cytokine production by highly activated microglial cells in neuropathic conditions and during the development of morphine tolerance, as demonstrated by numerous publications, including several from our group (Song and Zhao 2001; White et al. 2007; Matsushita et al. 2014; Pilat et al. 2015; Kwiatkowski et al. 2017, 2019; Eisenstein 2019; Pawlik et al. 2020, 2022; Pawlik and Mika 2023). Given our previous findings that cenicriviroc effectively suppresses microglial activation (Kwiatkowski et al. 2016), we investigated whether it also modulates nociceptive factors released by these cells. For this study, we selected cytokines that are well-documented in the literature as being produced by activated microglia—specifically TNFα (Nakamura et al. 1999; Popiolek-Barczyk et al. 2015; Piotrowska et al. 2017), IL-1β (Nakamura et al. 1999; Popiolek-Barczyk et al. 2015; Piotrowska et al. 2017), CCL2 (Kwiatkowski et al. 2016; Piotrowska et al. 2016b), CCL3 (Kwiatkowski et al. 2016; Piotrowska et al. 2016b), CCL4 (Kwiatkowski et al. 2016), and CCL5 (Kwiatkowski et al. 2016; Piotrowska et al. 2016b). It has long been known that TNFα (Sorkin et al. 1997; George et al. 1999; Lindenlaub et al. 2000; Dubový et al. 2006) and IL-1β (Ren and Torres 2009; Makuch et al. 2013) are important pronociceptive factors in neuropathy. Both are considered as key mediators of pain since they can promote neuronal hypersensitivity during neuropathy (Malcangio et al. 1996; Sorkin et al. 1997; George et al. 1999; Dubový et al. 2006; Ren and Torres 2009; Makuch et al. 2013). Their injection has been shown to induce neuropathic pain symptoms in healthy animals (Malcangio et al. 1996; Wagner and Myers 1996). Moreover, it was published that in neuropathic pain models, administrations of TNFα (Sommer et al. 2001; Svensson et al. 2005) and interleukin-1 receptor (Pilat et al. 2015) antagonists attenuate hypersensitivity. In our study, we observed a significant increase in TNFα and IL-1β levels on Day 16 in mice treated repeatedly with morphine, both alone and in combination with cenicriviroc. This upregulation may contribute to the development of morphine tolerance in both groups. Remarkably, cenicriviroc coadministration did not significantly reduce IL-1β and TNFα, suggesting a potential limitation in counteracting this specific inflammatory pathway.

As for the pronociceptive ligands of CCR2 and/or CCR5 analyzed in our study, we observed a significant upregulation of CCL2, CCL3, and CCL5 expression on Day 8 following CCI. Importantly, CCL3 appears to play a pivotal role in the initiation of neuropathic pain, while CCL5 may be more critically involved in its maintenance. CCL2 and CCL5 are the primary ligands for CCR2 and CCR5, respectively, and their role in neuropathic pain have been widely implicated. Initially, CCL2 was shown to be secreted by spinal astrocytes and neurons, leading to pronounced microglial activation via CCR2 expressed on their surface (Zhang et al. 2007; Thacker et al. 2009; Kiguchi et al. 2012). Subsequent studies revealed that CCL2 can also be produced by microglia, macrophages, and neutrophils (Tecchio and Cassatella 2016; Matsuda et al. 2021; Errede et al. 2022). We have previously demonstrated that intrathecal administration of CCL2 induces long-lasting pain symptoms (Pawlik et al. 2022), and that mice overexpressing CCL2 display increased hypersensitivity (Menetski et al. 2007). Furthermore, intrathecal injection of a CCL2-neutralizing antibody effectively reverses neuropathic pain-related behavior (Kwiatkowski et al. 2019), likely through inhibition of microglial activation (Thacker et al. 2009). Similarly, siRNA-mediated CCL2 knockdown has been shown to reduce pain-like behavior (Zhu et al. 2022). CCL5 is produced by microglial and astroglial cells (Kwiatkowski et al. 2016), and its intrathecal administration elicits neuropathic pain symptoms (Pevida et al. 2014; Pawlik et al. 2022). Upregulation of CCL5 during neuropathy has been observed in both rats (Yin et al. 2015) and mice (Pawlik et al. 2022). Curiously, CCL5 knockout mice exhibit reduced hypersensitivity in neuropathic conditions (Liou et al. 2012), and antibody-mediated neutralization of CCL5 alleviates pain-like behaviors (Yin et al. 2015; Malon and Cao 2016). Therefore, the Day-8 reduction of CCL2 and CCL5 in cenicriviroc-treated mice likely contributes to its analgesic action.

Other CCR5-acting chemokines include CCL3 and CCL4. CCL3 likely plays a larger role in nociception than previously acknowledged (Kiguchi et al. 2010; Ochi-ishi et al. 2014; Kwiatkowski et al. 2016; Rojewska et al. 2018; Kwiatkowski and Mika 2018; Pawlik et al. 2022; Ciechanowska et al. 2023). It can be secreted by a variety of cell types, including microglia, macrophages, neurons, neutrophils, and lymphocytes (Hanisch 2002; De Haas et al. 2007; Blidberg et al. 2012; Wallace et al. 2013; Kwiatkowski et al. 2020b). Intrathecal administration of CCL3 elicits pronounced pain-like behaviors in naive mice (Matsushita et al. 2014; Pawlik et al. 2022), while its neutralization during neuropathy reduces hypersensitivity (Saika et al. 2012; Ochi-ishi et al. 2014; Rojewska et al. 2018; Ciechanowska et al. 2023). The elevated spinal levels of CCL3 have been detected in both the early and intermediate phases of neuropathy (Pawlik et al. 2020; Kwiatkowski et al. 2020b), which aligns well with our current findings. Previously, we demonstrated that intrathecal administration of cenicriviroc on Day 7 effectively prevented the CCI-induced increase in spinal CCL3 levels in rats (Kwiatkowski et al. 2020b). In the present study, we show that i.p. administration of cenicriviroc also reduces CCL3 by Day 8. These results suggest that CCL3 may be one of the key mediators of the therapeutic effects of cenicriviroc in neuropathic pain. In contrast, the involvement of CCL4 in neuropathic pain appears to be less critical (Pawlik and Mika 2023). Although in vitro studies have indicated that microglia and astrocytes can produce CCL4 (Kwiatkowski et al. 2016), in vivo evidence is limited. To date, only an increase in spinal CCL4 mRNA expression has been reported following CCI (Rojewska et al. 2018; Pawlik et al. 2020, 2022), with no corresponding increase in protein levels (Pawlik et al. 2022; Ciechanowska et al. 2023), which is consistent with our present findings. Interestingly, in our research, mice receiving repeated morphine exhibited a significant increase in CCL4 levels on Day 16 compared to vehicle-treated, CCI-exposed mice, suggesting that this chemokine may also contribute to the development of morphine tolerance and hypersensitivity.

We obtained particularly interesting results regarding the levels of IL-10, a cytokine known for its potent antinociceptive properties (Sawada et al. 1999; Mika et al. 2013). Although the precise mechanisms of IL-10 action in the nervous system are not fully understood, existing evidence suggests that it plays a key role in silencing activated microglia and thereby inhibiting the release of cytokines such as IL-1β (Sawada et al. 1999). Moreover, IL-10 is closely linked to the polarization of microglial cells toward the M2 phenotype, which is considered neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory. M2-polarized microglia promote resolution of inflammation, in part through the release of IL-10 itself, creating a feedback loop that supports analgesic effects (Cherry et al. 2014; Guo et al. 2022; Li et al. 2023). Previous studies have demonstrated that intrathecal administration of IL-10 alleviates neuropathic pain symptoms in various animal models (Milligan et al. 2006; Watkins et al. 2007; Ledeboer et al. 2007), highlighting its therapeutic potential. In our study, we observed a marked increase in IL-10 levels on Day 3 in mice treated with cenicriviroc, both alone and in combination with morphine. This increase in IL-10 concentration may contribute to the restoration of immune homeostasis disturbed after CCI, which may both underlie the long-lasting effects of this antagonist and the improved efficacy of morphine when co-administered. These findings highlight a unique mechanism of action of cenicriviroc—it was already proposed that targeting the production of cytokines by intrathecal IL-10 therapy may be a promising therapeutic strategy for the relief of neuropathic pain (Ledeboer et al. 2007).

The results of our study indicate that repeated administration of morphine leads to the development of tolerance. This is consistent with our previous observations showing that the loss of morphine efficacy is accompanied by strong activation of microglia/macrophages (Bober et al. 2024b; Pawlik et al. 2025; Piotrowska et al. 2026). Importantly, from Day 12 we observed clear tactile hypersensitivity, which by Day 16 was accompanied by a statistically significant increase in spinal levels of TNFα, IL-1β, and CCL2-5. In line with the extensive literature (Mika et al. 2008, 2013; Rojewska et al. 2018; Kwiatkowski et al. 2019, 2020a; Pawlik et al. 2022; Pawlik and Mika 2023; Ciechanowska and Mika 2024) these pronociceptive cytokines are likely responsible for the development of hypersensitivity and may also contribute to the reduced efficacy of cenicriviroc.

Since day 8 treatment with cenicriviroc was associated with a general trend toward reduced inflammatory signaling compared with morphine, suggesting that its analgesic efficacy may involve attenuation of chemokine-dependent responses triggered by nerve injury and repeated opioid exposure. Importantly, although the Luminex analysis did not show persistent suppression of inflammatory biomarkers by cenicriviroc as compared to vehicle-treated animals, this does not preclude its biological relevance. Transient early immunomodulatory effects of cenicriviroc may be sufficient to initiate longer-lasting neuroimmune and transcriptional adaptations that shape pain-related plasticity, even if cytokine differences are no longer detectable at later stages. Interestingly, by Day 16, cenicriviroc monotherapy produced a stronger analgesic effect than the combination with morphine. This observation may serve as indirect evidence of functional cross-talk between opioid receptors and CCR2/CCR5 signaling pathways, and suggests that chronic morphine exposure, through sustained neuroinflammatory activation, may limit the effectiveness of chemokine receptor antagonism. Mechanistically, opioid and chemokine receptors are both GPCRs and interact at multiple levels. Chronic MOR activation can enhance chemokine receptor expression and activate microglia/macrophages, increasing pronociceptive cytokine release, which can counteract analgesic signaling (Szabo et al. 2002). At the same time, chemokines such as CCL2 may interfere with MOR-mediated signaling, further attenuating inhibitory nociceptive transmission (Heles et al. 2021). Our data support this interaction, as repeated administration of morphine in combination with cenicriviroc did not inhibit the increase in pronociceptive mediators, whereas cenicriviroc monotherapy effectively reduced CCL2, CCL3, and CCL5 levels, contributing to its sustained analgesic efficacy. Furthermore, on Day 16, morphine treatment was associated with a marked increase in chemokine levels, whereas these levels remained lower in animals receiving cenicriviroc alone but not in those receiving combined treatment. GPCR interactions, including heterologous desensitization and potential receptor heterodimerization, may contribute to this effect: prolonged MOR activation during combination therapy could dampen downstream CCR2/CCR5 signaling, limiting the efficacy of co-administration. Similarly, chemokines elevated by chronic morphine administration may interfere with opioid signaling, creating a bidirectional cross-tolerance. These observations indicate that cenicriviroc itself has potent analgesic effects, but chronic opioid exposure may offset some of its benefits, highlighting the need for further research into the molecular basis of drug efficacy in the treatment of neuropathy.

Conclusions

Our study provides the first comprehensive preclinical evidence that cenicriviroc, a dual CCR2/CCR5 antagonist, not only significantly reduces neuropathic pain symptoms on its own but also enhances and prolongs the analgesic effects of morphine while attenuating tolerance and OIH after nerve injury. Notably, these effects are particularly pronounced in the context of tactile hypersensitivity. Cenicriviroc’s action, including suppression of MAPKs, modulation of Akt, and regulation of transcription factors (NF-κB, STAT3), converges on the regulation of key pronociceptive chemokines (CCL2, CCL3, CCL5) while promoting the antinociceptive cytokine IL-10; all these observations altogether suggest the restoration of neuroimmune balance. Moreover, our findings reveal differential engagement of core signaling pathways (p38, ERK, JNK, Akt, NF-κB, and STAT3) underlying cenicriviroc’s analgesia and its interplay with morphine, a topic that warrants further mechanistic study. Collectively, these results underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting CCR2/CCR5 in neuropathic pain management. They also highlight the neuroimmunological complexity of chemokine signaling in modulating nociception and morphine tolerance. The translational potential of cenicriviroc is further supported by its ongoing evaluation as an experimental treatment for HIV infection and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT02128828), strengthening the rationale for its repurposing in neuropathic pain therapy. Further studies are needed to define the precise cellular and molecular mechanisms involved and to evaluate cenicriviroc’s clinical applicability as a novel non-opioid or adjunctive therapy for neuropathic pain. Prospective trials should consider biomarker-guided designs using CCL2, CCL3, CCL5, and IL-10 as pharmacodynamic readouts to link target engagement with analgesic outcomes. Given known differences in neuropathic pain etiology, future studies should also include different neuropathy models to refine dosing, timing, and combination strategies with opioids.
