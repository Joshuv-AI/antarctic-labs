Performance-driven utilization of industrial wastes as fine aggregates in ambient-cured alkali activated concrete
Authors: Prabhjot Singh, Harpal Singh, Sagar Paruthi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rapid urbanization and the growing demand for sustainable construction materials have encouraged the development of low-carbon alkali-activated concrete (AAC). This study investigates the utilization of finely burnt clay brick dust (BD) and waste foundry sand (WFS) as alternative fine aggregates in ambient-cured cement-free AAC. Natural river sand was replaced from 25 to 100% using BD and WFS to evaluate their influence on fresh, mechanical, and durability properties. The binder system consisted of Class-F fly ash and ground granulated blast furnace slag, activated using an 8 M sodium-based alkaline solution with a constant solution-to-binder ratio of 0.50. Results indicated that incorporation of BD reduced compressive strength due to its porous structure and higher water absorption, with reductions of 18.3% and 24.2% at 28 and 90 days, respectively, at full replacement. In contrast, WFS significantly enhanced the performance of AAC because of its micro-filler effect and improved particle packing. The optimum performance was achieved at 50% WFS replacement, where compressive strength increased by 41.5% and 21.5% at 28 and 90 days, respectively, while split tensile and flexural strengths also improved considerably. Durability performance was substantially enhanced at the optimum WFS level, with reductions of 60.9% in chloride penetrability, 62.7% in carbonation depth, 45.7% in water permeability, and 42% in sorptivity compared to the control mix. Furthermore, the 50WFS mix exhibited superior resistance against acidic and sulfate environments. The findings demonstrate that WFS can effectively replace natural sand up to 50% in ambient-cured AAC, producing a sustainable and durable construction material, while BD can be utilized at moderate replacement levels for effective waste valorization.

Introduction

Concrete is one of the most commonly used materials in construction industry, however its increasing demand has raised concern related to environmental sustainability and depletion of natural resources1. Ordinary Portland cement (OPC) production involves high temperature calcination of limestone, during which nearly one ton of CO2 is released per ton cement produced, which contributes significantly to atmospheric degradation2. To minimize this impact, researchers have explored the use of industrial by-products as alternative construction material for conserving natural resources3. In this context, alkali-activated materials emerged as eco-friendly and low-carbon alternative to conventional cement-based systems. Reuse of industrial waste in geopolymer composite not only reduces construction cost but also help in reducing environmental damage and supports sustainable development4. The concept of alkali-activated concrete was first systematically developed by Viktor Glukhovsky in the 1950s, who introduced the idea of activating aluminosilicate materials with alkaline solutions to produce cementitious binders, laying the foundation for modern alkali-activated materials5. The term ‘Geopolymer’ was first introduced by Joseph Davidovits in 1978 to describe inorganic polymer materials6. Geopolymer material generally refer to low-calcium aluminosilicate binders that predominantly form sodium aluminosilicate hydrate (N-A-S-H) gel through polymerization reactions. In contrast, alkali-activated materials incorporating calcium-rich precursors such as ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBFS) generate a combination of calcium-aluminosilicate-hydrate (C-A-S-H) and N-A-S-H type gels, resulting in a more complex reaction mechanism7. The reaction mechanism of the alkali-activated concrete (AAC) and geopolymer concrete (GPC) is shown in Fig. 17. Binders are fundamental to the performance and durability of AAC, as they control the geopolymerisation process and formation of strengthening reaction products8. Unlike conventional concrete, where Portland cement act as the primary binder, alkali-activated systems rely on aluminosilicate-rich natural materials, agricultural or industrial by-products such as fly ash, GGBFS, metakaolin, rice husk ash and other agro-industrial ashes9–12. These materials, rich in silica and alumina, react with alkaline activators to form a three-dimensional polymeric network, eliminating the need for cement. The utilization of such alternative binders not only enhance resource efficiency but also enable effective reuse of industrial waste, making GPC/AAC a sustainable and low-carbon construction material13. The geopolymerization process begins with the alkaline activation of aluminosilicate source materials using sodium or potassium-based activators14. Under alkaline conditions, reactive precursors such as fly ash and GGBFS dissolve after activation, releasing silicate, aluminate, and calcium species into the pore solution. These species undergo gel nucleation, followed by gelation and polycondensation, forming N-A-S-H, C-A-S-H, and/or C-(N)-A-S-H gels depending on the precursor chemistry. The progressive development of these gels creates a two- and three-dimensional alkali-activated backbone, as shown in Fig. 2, which densifies the microstructure and forms a durable hardened alkali-activated matrix. Owing to its improved strength, durability, and resistance to chemical and thermal actions, alkali activated GPC is considered a promising sustainable alternative to conventional cement-based materials15. Unlike Portland cement hydration, geopolymerization or alkali-activation can occur under ambient or slightly elevated curing condition, making the process more energy-efficient and suitable for practical construction applications16,17.Fig. 1Reaction mechanism of AAC and GPC7.

Finely burnt clay brick dust (BD), locally known as Surkhi, is a waste material generated from brick kilns where clay bricks are produced using high-draft kiln technology or the traditional Bull’s Trench Kiln technology, which is widely used in India. Surkhi mainly consists of pulverized burnt clay brick residues and is rich in silica and alumina content, making it suitable for geopolymer production18. Traditionally, Surkhi was used as a pozzolanic material which, when mixed with lime and water, forms a strong and water-resistant hydraulic mortar, and prior to the widespread use of Portland cement it served as one of the primary construction material19. Brick dust is a silica and alumina rich material that has been determined to be a partially reactive material that could contribute to alkali activation. Chatveera et al. have found that geopolymer systems with refractory brick dust have a better mechanical performance in case of optimal curing conditions and mix ratios are considered, and compressive strength is highly determined by pore structure and microstructural densification20. In the same way, Li et al. showed that denser microstructure and better compressive and flexural strength up to 31 MPa are achieved when higher doses of alkali are used, whereas more porous ones are achieved with lower doses21.

Nevertheless, Wong et al. have found that additions of more than 10% percent of recycled brick powder results in lower compressive strength and higher sorptivity because of the developing heterogeneous microstructure and higher porosity22. Similarly, Yurt and Bayraktar pointed out that despite the fact that high content of powder brick can lead to compressive strength of up to 66 Mpa given the right conditions, performance is very sensitive to mix composition and curing temperature making mixing optimization very important23. Moreover, El-Seidy et al. showed that the waste brick aggregates could optimize the mechanical and durability performance at the optimal replacement levels (approximately, 50%), yet overuse might result in higher water absorption and a decrease in microstructure strength caused by porous particle properties24.

Waste foundry sand (WFS) is a high-silica sand including 85–95% SiO2 produced after casting operations; it may contain residual binders, fines, and trace metals from molding sands. WFS particle size distribution can be comparable to natural fine aggregates but often has higher fines content and different surface chemistry, which affects water demand and fresh behavior25. Several authors report that WFS is largely inert silica and thus behaves as a filler unless pretreated or activated. Partial replacement by BD or WFS usually reduces workability (lower slump/flow) because of higher fines, angular particles, and increased surface area leading to higher water/alkali activator demand in geopolymer mixes26,27. The incorporation of definite dosage of superplasticizer and higher activator liquid-to-solid ratio are commonly required to achieve the desired workability. Study indicate effective replacement levels of WFS up to 20% in geopolymer matrices, WFS often behaves as filler, so strength retention depends on the binder reactivity and particle–matrix bonding28. Geopolymer binders generally offer improved chemical durability (acid/sulfate/chloride resistance) compared to OPC; incorporating BD or WFS has mixed effects: if the fines enhance packing and reduce connectivity of capillary pores, durability can improve; conversely, additional fines or contaminants in wastes can raise permeability or introduce deleterious phases29. Durability outcomes therefore track porosity, binder activation degree, and contaminant content. Bhardwaj and Kumar also noted that inclusion of WFS up to 60% leads to a significant increase in compressive strength up to almost 43%, decreased sorptivity, and refinement and densification of the microstructure30. Other study also reported similar results with the strength and durability enhancement due to enhanced particle packing and a decrease in capillary porosity, but the workability declined with increased levels of replacement27. Moreover, Khalaf et al. revealed that WFS geopolymer stabilization during ambient curing conditions significantly enhances mechanical properties and microstructure where compressive strength is notably improved by the increase in geopolymer gel formation and decrease in voids31. Previous study by Joy and Mathew also revealed that WFS addition enhances compressive, tensile and flexural strength to moderate replacement levels, beyond which its performance can deteriorate because of excessive fines and loss of workability32. Likewise, as Devi and Kumar indicated, WFS in geopolymer concrete helps in the enhancement of strength properties and sustainability advantages because it is an efficient way of utilizing industrial wastes33.

In general, existing literature indicates that BD and WFS influence AAC properties through different mechanisms. BD mainly affects the matrix through its porous structure and partial aluminosilicate reactivity, whereas WFS acts predominantly as a micro-filler that enhances particle packing and pore refinement. Although several studies have investigated the individual utilization of BD or WFS in geopolymer or alkali-activated systems, most available research has primarily focused on partial replacement levels, heat-cured systems, or limited mechanical characterization. Comprehensive comparative studies evaluating both BD and WFS as fine aggregate replacements within the same ambient-cured AAC system are still very limited. Furthermore, relatively less attention has been given to long-term durability characteristics such as permeability, sorptivity, carbonation resistance, and chloride penetrability under high replacement levels. In addition, this study also incorporates microstructural investigation, statistical analysis and environmental impact analysis to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the developed AAC mixes. Therefore, the present study introduces a comprehensive and comparative investigation on the utilization of BD and WFS as partial and full replacements of natural fine aggregate in ambient-cured AAC. The originality of this work lies in: (1) the direct comparative assessment of two industrial waste-derived fine aggregates within the same alkali-activated system, (2) the evaluation of replacement levels up to 100%, (3) the integration of both mechanical and durability performance analysis under ambient curing conditions, and (4) the correlation of macro-level performance with microstructural characteristics. Unlike many previous studies that rely on heat curing, the present work focuses on ambient curing to improve the practical applicability of AAC for field construction.

Accordingly, the specific objectives of the present study are: (1) to evaluate the influence of BD and WFS on the workability and mechanical properties of AAC, including compressive, split tensile, and flexural strength; (2) to investigate the durability performance through permeability, sorptivity, carbonation resistance, chloride penetrability, and chemical resistance tests; (3) to identify the optimum replacement level for achieving balanced mechanical and durability performance; and (4) to examine the microstructural characteristics governing the observed behavior. The outcomes of this study are expected to provide practical insights into the sustainable utilization of industrial waste materials for the development of durable and environmentally friendly ambient-cured AAC.

Alkali activators

Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and sodium silicate (Na2SiO3) solution were adopted as alkaline activators for initiating alkali activation reaction shown in Fig. 3c and d respectively. The concentration of NaOH solution plays a critical role in governing the alkali activation process. Previous studies have demonstrated that NaOH concentrations in the range of 8–10 M provide an optimal balance between dissolution of reactive species, gel formation, and workability, particularly under ambient curing conditions21,22. Therefore, 8 M NaOH was adopted in this study to ensure efficient alkali activation while maintaining practical handling and consistent performance. The sodium silicate to hydroxide ratio of 2.0 was fixed by maintaining a constant solution to binder ratio of 0.50. The sodium hydroxide is used in flakes form and it is mixed with water one day before the day of casting as an exothermic reaction takes place during the mixing of NaOH in water.

Brick dust

At present, large quantities of BD are disposed as waste; for instance, a medium-sized High-Draft brick kiln in Punjab, India produces nearly 4.5 m3 of BD per one lakh bricks, resulting in about 238 m3 of waste annually from a single kiln. With around 2000 brick kilns operating in Punjab, approximately 4.76 × 105 m3 of BD waste is generated every year, which occupies large land area and causes environmental issues. Therefore, effective valorisation of BD as an artificial fine aggregate in geopolymer concrete is proposed to reduce waste disposal and overall construction cost. Superplasticizer

A polycarboxylate ether (PCE) based high-range water-reducing admixture was used in the present study to improve the workability of AAC mixtures shown in Fig. 3d. Although PCE is primarily developed for cement-based systems, previous studies have shown that it can also be effectively used in geopolymer systems, particularly those containing fly ash and GGBFS37,38. In alkali-activated geopolymer systems, PCE improves workability by dispersing aluminosilicate particles and reducing inter-particle friction, thereby enhancing flowability. The presence of calcium in GGBFS further improves the compatibility of PCE by facilitating adsorption mechanisms similar to those in cementitious systems. A constant dosage of superplasticizer (by weight of binder) was maintained for all mixes to ensure comparability of results. The incorporation of PCE helped in partially compensating for the reduction in workability caused by higher fines and water absorption of BD and WFS.

Microstructural characterization of raw materials

The microstructural characteristics of the raw materials used in the present study were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) in order to understand their surface morphology, particle texture, and potential influence on the behavior of alkali-activated concrete. The SEM images of FA, GGBFS, BD, and WFS are shown in Fig. 6. The SEM image of FA revealed predominantly spherical particles with smooth surfaces and varying particle sizes, which contribute to improved workability and particle packing within the alkali-activated matrix. Some hollow and porous cenospheres were also observed, which are characteristic features of Class-F fly ash. In contrast, GGBFS exhibited irregular and angular particles with relatively rough surfaces, indicating its highly reactive amorphous nature and greater potential for calcium-based reaction product formation under alkaline activation. The SEM morphology of BD showed highly angular and porous particles with rough surface texture and visible microvoids. This porous microstructure explains the higher water absorption and reduced workability observed in BD-incorporated mixes. The irregular surface characteristics of BD also influence the interfacial transition zone and matrix continuity at higher replacement levels. On the other hand, WFS particles appeared comparatively denser and finer with relatively sub-angular to rounded surfaces and compact morphology. The fine particle distribution and dense texture of WFS support its micro-filler behavior, which contributes to improved particle packing, reduced pore connectivity, and enhanced matrix densification in alkali-activated concrete. The observed SEM morphology of WFS is consistent with the improved mechanical and durability performance achieved at optimum replacement levels.Fig. 6SEM images of the material used (a) FA, (b) GGBFS, (c) BD, (d) WFS.

Methodology

In the current study natural river sand was replaced with BD and WFS at replacement levels of 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% by mass of fine aggregate. Separate mix series were prepared for BD and WFS to evaluate their individual influence on fresh properties, mechanical strength, and durability characteristics of AAC. The mix design proportion used for the controlled AAC with 100% natural river sand (RS) and the AAC with different dosages of BD and WFS is shown in Table 3. The mix design was developed using a modified rational approach based on the guidelines of IS 10262:201939. Since no specific standard mix design method is currently available for AAC, IS 10262:2019 was used as the reference framework for aggregate proportioning and volume calculations, while the binder and liquid phases were modified to suit the AAC system. Accordingly, FA and GGBFS were used as the binder, and the alkaline activator solution was incorporated by maintaining constant activator concentration, sodium silicate-to-sodium hydroxide ratio, solution-to-binder ratio, and water-to-solid ratio. The target strength was selected to achieve performance comparable to M25 grade conventional concrete, which represents a commonly used structural-grade concrete and provides a practical benchmark for evaluating the suitability of BD and WFS as fine aggregate replacements under ambient curing conditions. Based on previous extensive experimental investigations on various parametric influences affecting both fresh and hardened properties of AAC, to achieve strength comparable to M25 grade conventional concrete, the present study adopts an overall binder content of 375 kg/m3 to ensure sufficient alkali activation, strength development, and durability performance under ambient cured conditions. Previous study have reported that comparatively higher binder contents are often required in ambient-cured fly ash–GGBFS alkali-activated geopolymer systems to compensate for slower reaction kinetics and to achieve stable mechanical performance comparable to conventional structural-grade concrete40. In the present study, a fly ash to GGBFS ratio of 80:20 was adopted to achieve an optimal balance between workability, strength development, and curing efficiency. Fly ash, being rich in silica and alumina, contributes to the formation of geopolymer gel and enhances long-term strength, while its spherical particle morphology improves workability41. In contrast, GGBFS contains a significant amount of calcium, which accelerates alkali activation and promotes early strength development, particularly under ambient curing conditions42. Previous studies have reported that incorporating fly ash and GGBFS in 80:20 significantly improves the reactivity and mechanical performance of alkali activated systems under ambient conditions by facilitating the formation of additional calcium-aluminosilicate hydrate (C-A-S-H) gel along with geopolymeric gel43,44. The binder consists of alkaline activator concentration (8 M NaOH), sodium silicate to sodium hydroxide ratio (2.0), solution-to-binder ratio (0.50) and water-to-solid ratio of 0.28 were kept constant throughout the study to isolate the effect of fine aggregate replacement. The sodium hydroxide solution of 8 M concentration was prepared by dissolving 320 g of NaOH pellets in distilled water and making up the total volume to 1 L. The solution was prepared at least 24 h prior to mixing to allow for temperature stabilization and complete dissolution. All kind of aggregates, including natural sand, BD, and WFS, were used in air-dried condition rather than saturated surface-dry condition. A constant quantity of extra water (19.98 kg/m3) and identical activator-to-binder ratio were intentionally maintained for all mixes to isolate the influence of fine aggregate replacement on the performance of alkali activated concrete. Although BD exhibited higher water absorption compared to natural sand, no additional water compensation was introduced for individual replacement levels in order to maintain consistent mix design parameters across all mixtures. Consequently, mixes containing higher BD contents may have experienced reduced effective free water and activator availability, which could have influenced workability and alkali activation efficiency. This aspect should be considered as a limitation of the present study and may partially contribute to the observed reduction in mechanical and durability performance at higher BD replacement levels.

Mixing and casting

The mixing procedure of AAC was carried out in a systematic manner to ensure uniformity and proper alkali activation. Prior to mixing, the alkaline activator solution was prepared by mixing sodium hydroxide solution with sodium silicate solution in the required proportions. The prepared activator solution was allowed to equilibrate for approximately 24 h at room temperature to ensure complete dissolution and stabilization. Initially, all dry materials including coarse aggregates, natural sand, and fine aggregates including river sand or brick dust or waste foundry sand as per the mix proportions were mixed in a pan mixer for about 2–3 min to achieve uniform distribution. Subsequently, the binder materials such as fly ash and ground granulated blast furnace slag were added to the dry mix and further mixed for an additional 2 min. After achieving a homogeneous dry mixture, the pre-prepared alkaline activator solution was gradually added while the mixer was in operation. Mixing was continued for approximately 3–5 min to ensure proper coating of all particles and uniform consistency. Then the superplasticizer was then added during this stage to improve workability. The total mixing duration was maintained at approximately 8–10 min to ensure complete homogenization of the mix. The freshly prepared AAC was then immediately placed into pre-oiled moulds in layers. Each layer was compacted using a table vibrator to eliminate entrapped air and achieve proper compaction. After casting, the specimens were kept at room temperature for 24 h to allow initial setting. Subsequently, the specimens were demoulded and cured under ambient conditions until the testing age. All specimens were cured under controlled ambient laboratory conditions at a temperature of 27 ± 2 °C and relative humidity of 60 ± 5% throughout the curing period. The specimens were stored on laboratory racks without external heat curing or water curing in order to simulate practical field curing conditions applicable for ambient-cured AAC. For each experimental test, a minimum of three specimens were prepared and tested for every mix proportion and curing age. The reported results represent the average values obtained from the tested specimens. In cases where significant variation was observed, additional specimens were tested to ensure consistency and reliability of the experimental results.

Workability

Workability of the fresh AAC was assessed using the slump cone test as per IS 1199 (Part 2):201845. The slump test was performed immediately after mixing to minimize the effect of rapid alkali activation. Since BD is slight coarse than natural sand and WFS possesses higher fines content and water absorption compared to natural sand, slump variation was used as an indicator of fresh-state rheological sensitivity due to fine aggregate replacement. All mixes were tested under identical environmental conditions (27 ± 2 °C) to ensure consistency. Split tensile strength

Split tensile strength was determined in accordance with IS 5816:199947. Three cylindrical specimens of 150 mm diameter and 300 mm height were cast and tested for each mix and curing age as shown in Fig. 8b and cured for 7 and 28 days under ambient conditions. After curing, positioned horizontally between the compression platens of the CTM as shown in Fig. 9b.

Flexural strength

Flexural strength was evaluated as per IS 516:1959 using a three-point loading arrangement48. Three prismatic specimens of size 100 × 100 × 500 mm were cast and tested for each mix and curing age. After curing, beams were tested under three-point loading with an effective span of 400 mm as shown in Fig. 9c.

Water permeability test

Permeability characteristics were assessed through a constant flow method. The basic concept of this method is to make water flow through concrete under pressure and measure the flow rate under steady—state flow condition. The specimens were placed in the test cells and the annular space was filled with sealing compound so that the flow occurred only in one direction i.e. from top to bottom. Measurements were taken at the bottom surface by collecting the quantity of water over the entire time period after achieving the steady-state flow condition. Darcy’s law has been used to determine the co-efficient of permeability under the average pressure head that was maintained during the steady state as per IS 3085-196549. Cube specimens (150 mm) cured for 7 and 28 days were sealed in the permeability moulds and then subjected to water pressure of 5 kg/cm2 to 15 kg/cm2 for 100 h after the steady flow is attained using a permeability apparatus shown in Fig. 9d.

Sorptivity test

The sorptivity of AAC specimens was determined in accordance with ASTM C1585, which measures the rate of water absorption by capillary action50. Prior to testing, the specimens were oven-dried at 50 °C until a constant mass was achieved and then allowed to cool to room temperature. The sides of the specimens were sealed using epoxy coating to ensure unidirectional water flow through the exposed surface. The specimens were then placed in contact with water at a depth of approximately 3–5 mm as shown in Fig. 10a.Fig. 10Physical appearance of the test performed (a) Sorptivity, (b) RCPT.

Rapid chloride penetrability test

Rapid chloride penetrability test (RCPT) was conducted as per ASTM C120251. After 28 days of curing, cylindrical specimens of 100 mm diameter were sliced to obtain 50 mm thick discs. The specimen sides were sealed and placed between two cells, one containing 3% NaCl solution, other containing 0.3 N NaOH solution as shown in Fig. 10b. A constant voltage of 60 V DC was applied for 6 h. Current was recorded at 30-min intervals, and total charge passed (in coulombs) was calculated.

Resistance to harmful chemicals

Chemical resistance was evaluated by exposing 28-day cured cube specimens to 3% H2SO4 solution, 3% HCl solution, 3% NaCl solution, 3% Na2SO4 solution. Specimens were immersed for 90 days in respective solutions. Solutions were replaced weekly to maintain concentration. After exposure, specimens were surface cleaned and dried, weighed to determine mass loss, and tested for residual compressive strength.

Carbonation test

The carbonation test was carried out to evaluate the depth of carbon dioxide (CO2) penetration of AAC with partial and full replacement of natural sand by BD and WFS for assessing environmental impact on durability and porousness of concrete, as the ingression of CO2 reduces the pH of concrete and can cause corrosion to the reinforcement. Accelerated carbonation testing was performed as per RILEM CPC-1852 recommendations. Cubical specimens of 100 × 100 × 100 mm were cast for each mix and ambient cured for 28 days. After curing, four side faces were waxed to allow unidirectional CO2 ingress. Specimens were then placed in an accelerated carbonation chamber maintained at a CO2 concentration of 3 ± 0.5%, temperature of 20 ± 2 °C, and relative humidity of 65 ± 3% for a duration of 28 days to simulate accelerated long-term carbonation exposure as shown in Fig. 11a. These environmental conditions were selected in accordance with commonly adopted accelerated carbonation testing procedures to ensure reliable assessment of carbonation resistance. After removal, cubes were kept in open air, then split and phenolphthalein solution applied and left for 1 h ± 10 min as shown in Fig. 11b. Statistical analysis

Compressive strength was measured on three specimens for each mix at each curing age, and the average value was reported. For inferential analysis, the individual specimen values were used instead of the averages. Since compressive strength testing is destructive, statistical comparison was performed separately at each curing age using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with mix type as the factor. Tukey’s HSD post hoc test was applied for pairwise comparison of means at a significance level of 0.05. Normality and homogeneity of variance were checked using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. In addition, a two-way ANOVA was performed using mix type and curing age as factors to examine their main and interaction effects on compressive strength.

Environmental impact analysis (EIA)

In the present study, a simplified environmental impact analysis was carried out to evaluate the sustainability performance of the developed AAC mixtures in comparison with conventional OPC concrete. The assessment was performed on the basis of a functional unit of 1 m3 concrete, which allowed direct comparison between the AAC and OPC concrete systems. A cradle-to-gate system boundary was adopted, with the gate defined as the hardened concrete specimen stage. The assessment therefore considered the production or acquisition of constituent materials, their processing, relevant transportation of constituent materials within the assumed local sourcing distance, batching, mixing operations and curing prior to testing, whereas construction stage activities, service-life impacts, demolitions, recycling and end-of-life phases were not considered in this evaluation. The OPC reference mixture used in this investigation correspond to M25 grade concrete, consisting of 375 kg/m3 cement, 640 kg/m3 natural sand, 1245 kg/m3 coarse aggregates and 168.75 kg/m3 water (w/c = 0.45). This OPC reference concrete was water cured without inclusion of additional steam or accelerated curing energy. In contrast, the AAC specimens were cured under ambient laboratory conditions without external heat curing, no additional thermal curing energy was considered in the environmental calculations. Mixing energy consumption was assumed to be comparable for both systems and therefore was not differentiated separately in the simplified environmental assessment. Similarly, curing was considered as part of the system boundary, but separate numerical impacts for curing-water use, water replacement/circulation or curing-related energy were not calculated because primary consumption data were not recorded. Therefore, the difference between water curing of OPC concrete and ambient curing of AAC was addressed qualitatively within the boundary description. Other processes such as reinforcement, maintenance, demolition, recycling, equipment manufacture and laboratory testing were excluded from the present environmental assessment. Four environmental impact indicators were considered including abiotic depletion (AD), global warming potential (GWP), human toxicity (HT) and acidification potential (Ac), which are commonly used indicators for evaluating environmental burdens associated with construction materials and low carbon concrete systems54–56. The environmental impact results are based on midpoint-oriented indicators rather than endpoint damage assessment methodology. The analysis focuses on direct environmental burden indicators, including GWP, Ac-related impacts and associated emissions during material production and concrete preparation. Endpoint damage categories such as ecosystem quality deterioration, long-term resource depletion, etc. were not evaluated. Therefore, the reported values should be interpreted as comparative midpoint indicators for assessing the relative sustainability performance of conventional OPC concrete and AAC mixtures under the adopted system boundary. Environmental inventory factors for individual concrete constituents were obtained from previously published literature where the impact values represent environmental burden associated with production of 1 kg of material57–59. No dedicated LCA software or commercial environmental database was used in this study. The environmental impacts were calculated using the literature-derived characterization factors, where the quantity of each constituent per 1 m3 concrete was multiplied by its corresponding impact factor and summed for each environmental indicator. The reported AD, GWP, HT and Ac values correspond to midpoint impact indicators; endpoint damage assessment, weighting and aggregation into a single score were not performed. Brick dust (BD) and waste foundry sand (WFS) were treated as industrial waste materials generated from brick kiln and metal casting industries respectively; therefore these materials were assigned zero upstream environmental burden under the no-allocation approach, which is widely adopted for secondary industrial by-products in environmental impact studies60. Since these materials does not require additional processing for their utilization as fine aggregates, only transportation could normally contribute to their environmental impacts. Result and discussion

Workability

The workability result of different AAC mixes prepared is shown in Fig. 12. The controlled AAC exhibited the slump value of 145 mm, indicating good workability under the selected design mix proportion. A significant reduction in the slump was noticed with increase in the dosage of BD and WFS as replacement of natural sand in AAC. The slump decreased by 17.2%, 41.4%, 58.6%, and 65.5% after incorporation of 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% BD respectively into the AAC. Similar trend was noticed after incorporation of WFS in AAC i.e. reduction of slump about 20.7%, 34.5%, 62.1%, 66.9% was observed with incorporation of 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% WFS respectively. The reduction in the slump in BD incorporated AAC is due to higher water absorption in comparison with natural sand which increases the demand for alkaline activator. The rough texture and porous internal structure of the BD is responsible for the enhancement of the internal friction and absorbing alkali liquid from the mix, thereby reducing the effective lubrication between the particles of BD. Similarly, the reduction in the slump in the WFS incorporated AAC is due to cumulative fines effect and particle packing densification characteristics.Fig. 12The workability result of different AAC mixes prepared (error bars represent ± 1 standard deviation (SD)).

The workability result of different AAC mixes prepared (error bars represent ± 1 standard deviation (SD)).

Ayaz Khan et al.26 also reported reduction in workability after incorporation of brick dust due to increased surface area and higher water demand, which is consistent with the observations of the present study. Angel et al.61 and Mehta62 reported that incorporation of WFS reduces workability because of higher fines content and increased internal friction between particles, which agrees well with the current findings. Despite the maximum reduction was noticed with full replacement of natural sand with the BD and WFS, no segregation was noticeable and superplasticizer were used to improve the workability of AAC.

Compressive strength

The compressive strength result of different AAC mixes prepared is shown in Fig. 13. The controlled mix exhibited a compressive strength of 32.22 MPa and 41.25 MPa after curing for 28 days and 90 days, respectively. This increment in the strength with curing time shows the ongoing alkali-activation and secondary gel formation in the AAC. The gradual reduction in the compressive strength of AAC incorporated with BD was noticed for all the curing periods. During the curing period of 28 days, the compressive strength fall by 2.5%, 6.1%, 13.5%, and 18.3% after incorporation of 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% BD in the AAC. On the other hand, during the curing period of 90 days, the compressive strength is decreased by 5.2%, 7.3%, 22.1%, and 24.2% after for the same dosage of replacement of natural sand with BD. The marginal reduction in the strength at 25–50% replacement level indicates the feasibility of incorporating the BD as replacement of natural sand without severe compromising the strength performance. This slight loss in strength is due to weaker interfacial transition zone and porous internal structure of BD. The incorporation of BD also helps in geopolymeric gel network formation due to alumina rich composition and similar particle size as that of RS which enhance the densification of GPC19. Maaze and Shrivastava63 and Rovnanik et al.64 reported that brick-based geopolymer systems can contribute to geopolymeric gel formation because of their reactive aluminosilicate composition, which partially supports the moderate strength retention observed in the present study63,64.Fig. 13The compressive strength result of different AAC mixes prepared (error bars represent ± 1 SD).

The compressive strength result of different AAC mixes prepared (error bars represent ± 1 SD).

On the other hand, the compressive strength of AAC cured for 28 days is increased by 17.6% and 41.5% after incorporation of 25% and 50% WFS. A similar improvement in compressive strength was observed in WFS incorporated AAC for 90 days curing period i.e. 2.7% and 21.5% with 25% and 50% WFS as replacement of natural sand. This trend indicates that the control alkali-activated concrete continued to gain strength more significantly at later ages under ambient curing conditions, thereby progressively reducing the performance gap. The enhanced early-age strength of the 50WFS mix can primarily be attributed to the micro-filler effect, improved particle packing, and refinement of the interfacial transition zone (ITZ), which accelerate matrix densification during the early stages of alkali-activation. The fine silica-rich particles of WFS also provide additional nucleation sites that facilitate early gel formation and improve load transfer efficiency. However, at later curing ages, the control mixes likely experienced continued alkali-activation and gradual dissolution of unreacted binder particles, resulting in additional formation of alkali-activated reaction products such as N-A-S-H and C-A-S-H gels. Since WFS behaves predominantly as a physically acting filler material rather than a highly reactive pozzolanic precursor under ambient curing conditions, its relative contribution to long-term reaction kinetics becomes comparatively less pronounced. Consequently, although the 50WFS mix maintained the highest absolute compressive strength at 90 days, the percentage improvement relative to the control mix decreased over time. This behavior suggests that the primary contribution of WFS is associated with early-age matrix densification and pore refinement rather than sustained long-term chemical reactivity. The continued strength development observed under ambient curing conditions is consistent with the findings of Naghizadeh et al.65, who reported that fly ash-based geopolymer systems continue undergoing gradual geopolymerization and gel densification at later curing ages under ambient environments. The result also shows the optimum dosage of replacement of natural sand with WFS is 50% and the highest compressive strength with the optimum dosage of replacement was 45.58 MPa and 50.12 MPa for curing period of 28 days and 90 days. The improved performance observed with WFS incorporation can be primarily attributed to its physical micro-filler effect rather than chemical reactivity which is also supported by the dense microstructure. The fine particle size and relatively smooth surface texture of WFS contribute to enhanced particle packing, leading to reduced voids and improved matrix densification30,32.

Bhardwaj and Kumar reported approximately 43% improvement at 60% WFS under ambient curing conditions, indicating similar behavior governed by micro-filler effects and improved particle packing30. However, most studies suggest optimum replacement levels between 20 and 40% due to excessive fines at higher dosages leading to strength reduction28,66. The present study demonstrates that 50% WFS achieves superior performance without compromising strength, highlighting improved interfacial transition zone (ITZ) and pore refinement due to the synergistic action of FA–GGBFS binder and silica-rich WFS. Similar improvements in strength and densification have also been attributed to enhanced geopolymer gel formation and reduced porosity in ambient-cured systems31. In contrast, BD incorporation resulted in strength reduction, which aligns with previous study where excessive brick powder leads to heterogeneous microstructure and reduced strength beyond optimum levels22. Wong et al. reported that strength decreases beyond 10–20% brick powder replacement due to inhomogeneous matrix formation and increased porosity22. However, the present study shows relatively moderate strength reduction up to 50% BD, suggesting partial alkali activated reactivity of BD due to its aluminosilicate composition, as also supported by microstructural densification observed in brick-based geopolymers21.

Split tensile strength

The split tensile strength result of different AAC mix prepared is shown in Fig. 14. A consistent decrement in the split tensile strength was noticed with incorporation of BD at all the replacement levels. The split tensile strength is reduced by 20.7%, 23.7%, 25.2%, and 27.7% for the AAC cured for 28 days with incorporation of 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% BD, respectively. This reduction in the split tensile strength is more pronounced than the compressive strength reduction because the tensile strength is more sensitive to microcracks and particles’ angularity. This high water absorption capacity of BD results in the slightly weaker aggregate paste bonding, which reduces the crack-bridging capability67. El-Seidy et al.24 also observed reduction in tensile strength at higher brick aggregate replacement levels due to increased microcracking and weaker aggregate–matrix bonding, which is consistent with the present results.Fig. 14Split tensile strength of different AAC mixes prepared (error bars represent ± 1 SD).

Split tensile strength of different AAC mixes prepared (error bars represent ± 1 SD).

On the other hand, the split tensile strength is increased by 12.8% and 19.2%, respectively, after incorporation of 25% and 50% WFS in the AAC cured for 28 days. The highest split tensile strength of 3.17 MPa was achieved after the replacement of natural sand with WFS in the AAC cured for 28 days. The incorporation of WFS more than the optimum dosage results into the decrement in the split tensile strength of AAC, which is due to weak bonding and early crack propagation, as excess dosage of replacement increases the internal friction of concrete particles68. Kumar et al.69 reported improvement in tensile strength of WFS-incorporated geopolymer concrete due to matrix densification and micro-filler effects, which agrees with the enhancement observed at moderate WFS replacement levels in the current study.

Flexural strength

The flexural strength result of different AAC mixes prepared is shown in Fig. 15. It was clearly noticed from the result that flexural strength is decreased with the incorporation of BD at all the replacement levels. The flexural strength is decreased by 5%, 10.5%, 12.3%, and 15.1% after the incorporation of 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% BD as a replacement of natural sand in the AAC. The reduction in the flexural strength of BD incorporated AAC is lower than the tensile strength reduction, indicating that BD does not severely affect the bending resistance and crack propagation resistance is also reasonably intact. It clearly shows that the introduction of micro voids and angular interface due to the usage of BD does not influence the resistance to bending stresses. Wang et al.70 reported that moderate incorporation of brick-derived materials can retain flexural performance due to acceptable matrix continuity, which is in agreement with the present findings.Fig. 15Flexural strength results of different AAC mix prepared (error bars represent ± 1 SD).

On the other hand, the enhancement in the flexural strength was observed after incorporation of WFS in to the AAC up to the optimum dosage. The flexural strength of AAC cured for 28 days is increased by 6.5% and 11.6% after incorporation of 25% and 50% WFS respectively in the AAC. The highest flexural strength of 4.44 MPa was achieved after 50% replacement of natural sand with WFS in AAC cured for 28 days. The flexural strength increased with WFS incorporation, which can be attributed to the reduction in microvoids and improved matrix continuity. This is consistent with the observed improvement in compressive strength and supported by the dense microstructure seen in SEM images. Devi and Kumar33 and Manikandan and Murali71 reported enhancement in flexural strength after WFS incorporation due to improved particle packing and microstructural densification, which supports the current observations.

The improvement in flexural strength at 50% WFS (11.6%) is consistent with previous findings where enhanced crack resistance and stress transfer efficiency were attributed to improved particle packing and microstructure densification27. The presence of fine silica-rich particles in WFS reduce microvoids and delay crack propagation, thereby improving flexural performance27. Compared to a previous study where improvements are generally limited to moderate replacement levels (20–40%), the present study demonstrates higher efficiency at 50% WFS, likely due to optimized binder composition and ambient curing conditions30.

On the other hand, the reduction in flexural strength with BD incorporation aligns with reported studies where porous and angular brick particles weaken matrix continuity and reduce stress transfer efficiency22,72. However, another study also reported that optimized brick-based geopolymer systems can achieve high strength (up to 60 MPa) under controlled conditions, indicating that performance depends strongly on activation conditions and mix design23.

The increased in the permeability was noticed for all the replacement level of BD except 25%. At 25% replacement level of BD with natural sand, permeability of the AAC decreases about 13.98% at 7 days and 17.46% at 28 days due to improved particle packing and blockage of the pores due to high water holding capacity of BD. Sorptivity

The sorptivity test result of different AAC mixes prepared is shown in Fig. 17. The controlled AAC were achieved a sorptivity of 0.072 mm/min1/2 at 120 min of exposure, which later shows a gradual increase in absorption with time, indicating normal capillary behaviour typical of ambient cured AAC. After the incorporation of 25% BD in the GPC, sorptivity is decreased by 10% in comparison with the controlled AAC. This improvement in the sorptivity is due to the filler effect of the particles of BD and the improved particle packing capacity of AAC at a low replacement level. But on the other hand, the sorptivity is increased with a higher dosage of BD in the AAC due to the higher water absorption capacity of BD and an increase in the capillary pore connectivity after fully replacing natural sand with BD in the AAC. Mixes incorporating BD show a progressive increase in sorptivity with increasing replacement levels. The 100BD mix exhibited the highest initial sorptivity (0.120 mm/min1/2) and secondary sorptivity (0.070 mm/min1/2), indicating higher water absorption. Another study on geopolymer-stabilized WFS also confirm improved microstructure and strength due to enhanced geopolymerization under ambient conditions31. In contrast, BD incorporation increases sorptivity at higher replacement levels, which aligns with previous finding where porous brick particles increase water absorption and capillary suction. The chemical resistance performance observed in the present study compares favorably with previously reported alkali activated or geopolymer concrete systems under similar aggressive exposure conditions. Previous studies on ambient-cured alkali-activated or geopolymer concrete incorporating industrial waste materials generally report strength losses ranging from 20–35% under sulfuric acid exposure and approximately 10–20% under hydrochloric acid exposure depending on binder composition and curing conditions81,82. In contrast, the present study exhibited only 16% strength loss under H2SO4 exposure and 8.5% under HCl exposure for the optimum 50WFS mix, indicating comparatively improved resistance against chemical attack. The enhanced performance can be attributed to the dense alkali-activated matrix, refined pore structure, and reduced permeability resulting from the micro-filler effect of WFS83. Acid degradation in AAC can be associated with decalcification of calcium-bearing reaction products, dissolution/dealumination of aluminosilicate phases, pore enlargement and microcracking, while sulfate exposure may promote sulfate-bearing expansive products. However, post-acid SEM/XRD analysis was not conducted in the present study; therefore, the degradation mechanism is interpreted from residual strength, mass loss, permeability-related results and established chemical mechanisms rather than direct post-exposure microstructural confirmation. In OPC-based WFS concrete, sulfur-containing impurities may promote ettringite-related deterioration under sulfate exposure79,80; the comparatively better response of the present 50WFS-AAC mix indicates that the dense alkali-activated matrix effectively limited ingress and damage propagation.

This improvement in the carbonation depth at moderate dosage of WFS is due to fine silica particles filling micro voids, reduced capillary pore diameter and lower permeability and sorptivity, which directly restricted CO2 diffusion and reduced carbonation depth85.

Environmental impact assessment

The environmental hotspot contribution of different materials used in OPC and AAC mixtures for the impact categories of AD, GWP, HT and Ac are illustrated in Fig. 21. The results indicates that the environmental burden of OPC concrete is strongly dominated by cement production. Nearly 98% of the global warming impact of OPC concrete originates from cement manufacturing due to limestone calcination and high energy consumption during clinker production. Similar dominance of cement in the environmental footprint of conventional concrete systems has been widely reported in literature2,86. In contrast, AAC mixtures exhibit a different environmental contribution pattern where the impacts are distributed among several constituents including alkaline activators and industrial by-product binders such as fly ash and GGBFS. The results shows that fly ash contributes approximately 24% of the GWP, while GGBFS contributes about 5% in the geopolymer control mixture. However, the production of alkaline activators, particularly sodium silicate, represents the major environmental hotspot in alkali activated systems. Sodium silicate contributes about 48–56% of GWP, 48–49% of AD, and nearly 74% of HT in alkali activated mixtures. Sodium hydroxide also contributes noticeable impacts, especially in AD and HT categories. Similar observations were reported by Habert et al. and McLellan et al., who indicated that alkali activators are the primary contributors to environmental impacts (EI) in GPC production57,60. The contribution of aggregates such as sand and gravel remains comparatively small in all environmental categories, generally below 5% of the total impacts. In the acidification category, the replacement of natural RS with BD and WFS gradually reduces the contribution of fine aggregates because the environmental burden associated with natural sand extraction, heavy machinery operation and long-distance transport of RS decreases. As BD and WFS replacement increases, the share of acidification impact shifts mainly toward the binder and activator components, resulting in an overall reduction of atmospheric acidification contribution from aggregates. Furthermore, BD and WFS were assumed to have zero upstream environmental burden under the no-allocation approach because they are industrial by-products that do not require additional processing. Therefore, replacing RS with these materials helps reduce extraction of natural aggregates while simultaneously promoting valorisation of industrial waste resources in concrete production.Fig. 21Contribution of individual materials to EI categories for OPC and AAC mixtures.

Contribution of individual materials to EI categories for OPC and AAC mixtures.

The relative EI of AAC compared with OPC concrete are presented in Fig. 22, where the impact values of OPC concrete were normalized to 100% for comparison purposes. The results clearly demonstrate environmental benefit of AAC that exhibit significantly lower GWP approximately 79–80% of OPC concrete, indicating a reduction of nearly 20% in greenhouse gas emissions. This reduction is mainly attributed to the elimination of cement clinker production and utilization of industrial by-products such as fly ash and GGBFS as binder materials. Similar reductions in carbon emissions for AAC/GPC have been reported by several researchers, who demonstrated that replacement of Portland cement with industrial by-products can significantly reduce the carbon footprint of concrete production57,87. Interestingly, the acidification potential showed a decreasing trend with increasing replacement of natural sand by BD and WFS. The acidification impact decreased from 167.8% for alkali-activated control mix to 150.6%, 132.9%, 116% and finally 98.3% for 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% replacement respectively, indicating nearly 41% reduction in acidification impact and gradual environmental improvement as natural sand consumption decreased. This behavior demonstrates that utilization of industrial waste materials as fine aggregates can partially offset the environmental burden associated with alkaline activators. The AD of alkali-activated mixtures was approximately 2.8–2.9 times higher than OPC, while HT was nearly 6.8–6.9 times higher, but these impacts remained nearly constant for all BD and WFS replacement levels. This increase is primarily associated with the production of alkaline activators such as sodium hydroxide and sodium silicate, which involve energy-intensive industrial processes and extraction of rare chemical mineral resources. Similar findings were reported by Turner and Collins, who noted that geopolymer systems may exhibit higher impacts in certain environmental categories due to activator production87. Overall, although AAC may show higher impacts in some categories due to activator production, it provides significant reduction in global warming potential while enabling effective utilization of industrial waste materials. Among all mixtures, the 50% replacement level exhibited the most balanced performance, with improved mechanical strength, enhanced durability and lower environmental burden, indicating a promising pathway toward sustainable low-carbon concrete production.Fig. 22Relative EI of AAC mixtures normalized with respect to OPC concrete.

Microstructure analysis

SEM was used to examine the morphology of the hardened AAC matrix and to relate visible pore structure, particle packing and ITZ quality with the mechanical and durability results. As SEM does not provide standalone chemical phase identification, gel-related interpretations were made cautiously and supported by XRD results. The control mix in Fig. 23 showed a moderately compact alkali-activated matrix with embedded unreacted fly ash particles and visible microvoids, explaining its intermediate strength and sorptivity. The BD mixes in Fig. 24 showed increasing heterogeneity with replacement level; at higher BD contents, visible voids, microcracks and weaker particle–matrix bonding were observed, consistent with the higher water absorption and reduced performance of BD-rich mixes. In contrast, WFS-incorporated mixes in Fig. 25 showed improved packing and lower visible porosity up to 50% replacement. The 50WFS mix exhibited the most compact matrix and improved ITZ, supporting the observed enhancement in compressive strength, sorptivity, permeability, RCPT and carbonation resistance. At 75WFS and 100WFS, localized agglomeration and excess fines reduced packing efficiency, which explains the decline beyond the optimum level. The XRD patterns of the controlled AAC, 50BD, and 50WFS AAC mixes are shown in Fig. 26. The XRD results provide additional evidence for the formation of alkali-activated reaction products and support the SEM-based interpretation of matrix densification. All selected mixes exhibited a broad diffuse region within approximately 20°–35° 2θ, which indicates the presence of poorly crystalline aluminosilicate and calcium-aluminosilicate reaction products. In fly ash–GGBFS based alkali-activated systems, this broad region is generally associated with the formation of N-A-S-H, C-(A)-S-H, and C-A-S-H type gels. Therefore, the XRD results support the occurrence of geopolymerization and calcium-assisted gel formation in the developed ambient-cured AAC mixtures. The controlled AAC showed a broad amorphous hump along with crystalline reflections mainly related to residual quartz and aluminosilicate phases. The persistence of crystalline peaks suggests that part of the fly ash and aggregate-related crystalline phases remained unreacted, which is common in ambient-cured fly ash–slag alkali-activated systems. This observation agrees with the SEM image of the controlled mix, where unreacted fly ash particles and visible micro voids were observed within the alkali-activated matrix. The 50BD-AAC pattern also showed the broad amorphous region, indicating the formation of alkali-activated gel products. However, compared with the 50WFS-AAC mix, the gel-related region appeared less effective in producing a dense and continuous matrix. The XRD pattern of 50BD-AAC contained crystalline phases associated with quartz, mullite/aluminosilicate phases, and iron-bearing phases, which can be related to the silica-, alumina-, and iron-rich composition of brick dust. The presence of these residual crystalline phases indicates that BD acted partly as an aluminosilicate-bearing material but did not fully participate in the reaction under ambient curing. This supports the SEM observation that BD mixes contained more heterogeneous regions, microvoids, and weaker particle–matrix bonding. Therefore, the XRD findings explain why 50BD-AAC retained acceptable strength but did not achieve the same densification and durability improvement as 50WFS-AAC. The 50WFS-AAC mix showed the most favorable XRD response among the selected mixtures. The broad diffuse region in the 20°–35° 2θ range, together with the reduced dominance of unwanted crystalline features, indicates a denser matrix containing poorly crystalline alkai-activated reaction products. The silica-rich nature of WFS contributed mainly through a micro-filler and nucleation effect rather than strong chemical reactivity. This effect improved particle packing, reduced void connectivity, and supported the development of a compact binding matrix. The XRD result is consistent with the SEM observation of 50WFS-AAC, where a dense alkali-activated matrix and improved interfacial transition zone were observed. This also agrees with the mechanical and durability results, where 50WFS-AAC showed the highest compressive strength and the lowest permeability, sorptivity, chloride penetrability, and carbonation depth.

Influence of curing conditions on AAC performance

Curing conditions are very critical in regulating alkali-activation process and mechanical and durability properties of AAC. It is a well-documented fact that the dissolution of aluminosilicate precursors is faster in heat curing and that it improves the formation of activation gel, which in turn increases the early-age strength. Indicatively, geopolymer systems based on the use of brick powder have shown compressive strengths of more than 50 Mpa at high curing temperatures owing to improved reaction kinetics and formation of gels21. Likewise, with the help of optimized curing regimes and the appropriate composition of activators, strength of up to 60 Mpa in waste-based geopolymer systems can be obtained23. However, despite these advantages, heat curing presents practical limitations for large-scale and in-situ applications. In contrast, ambient curing conditions provide a more feasible and field-applicable approach, although the alkali-activation process occurs at a relatively slower rate. Previous studies have shown that ambient-cured geopolymer concrete incorporating WFS can achieve significant strength enhancement, with improvements up to approximately 40–45% due to improved particle packing and microstructural densification30. Additionally, the incorporation of calcium-rich materials such as GGBFS facilitates strength development under ambient conditions by promoting the formation of additional binding phases and improving early-age reactivity31. In contrast, brick dust (BD) incorporation exhibited a reduction in strength at higher replacement levels, which is consistent with literature findings where porous particle morphology and increased water absorption limit the efficiency of geopolymerization under ambient conditions22. The quantitative comparison of the current study with existing studies is shown in Table 12, indicates that while several studies achieve high compressive strength under heat curing conditions, the present study demonstrates comparable performance under ambient curing. For instance, the systems based on the brick powder report the strengths up to 66.5 MPa with the optimized curing conditions, and the current study demonstrates the 41.5 percent increase in strength under the ambient conditions. Likewise, the WFS-based studies have shown strength gains of up to 40–45 which fits well with those of the current study. This underscores the fact that a lack of heat curing can be well addressed by optimized mix design and binder composition. It is noted that the direct comparison of studies depends on the differences in the binder composition, activator concentration, and curing conditions. Limitations and future research directions

It is also important to note that the present study maintained a constant extra-water dosage for all mixtures irrespective of the water absorption characteristics of the alternative fine aggregates. Since BD possesses significantly higher water absorption than natural sand, higher replacement levels may have reduced the effective liquid availability within the alkali activated matrix. Future studies should investigate the influence of SSD conditioning and absorption-compensated mix design approaches to better optimize the performance of BD-incorporated alkali activated concrete. A limitation of this study is that SEM analysis was carried out only for 28-day cured samples, and no analysis was done after acid exposure. Although the SEM observations provided important insight into matrix densification, pore structure, and interfacial transition zone development, the long-term evolution of alkali activation products and microstructural changes at later curing ages could not be directly evaluated. In particular, the time-dependent interaction between WFS particles and the alkali activated matrix under prolonged ambient curing conditions requires further investigation. Therefore, future studies should include detailed long-term microstructural analysis at 90 days and beyond using techniques such as SEM, EDS, and XRD to better understand gel formation, acid damage, pore structure changes, and overall durability.

Although these findings were made, it is advisable to conduct further studies to explore the long-term durability beyond 90 days, performance in different environmental conditions and comparative analysis in different curing regimes. Moreover, microstructural characterization and field validation study, at large scale, would give more information about the long-term usefulness of such materials in real construction. Although leaching tests were not conducted in the present investigation, previous studies have reported that alkali activated or geopolymer matrices incorporating waste foundry sand (WFS) exhibit effective immobilization of potentially hazardous elements due to the dense aluminosilicate gel network formed during alkali activation. Sabour et al.28 reported that geopolymer mortars containing WFS satisfied Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP) limits for heavy metal release, indicating effective stabilization of contaminants within the geopolymer matrix. Similarly, Moni et al.29 observed significantly reduced leaching of metallic ions in fly ash-based geopolymer systems incorporating foundry-related waste materials, attributing the improved environmental stability to matrix densification and chemical encapsulation mechanisms. These findings suggest that incorporation of WFS in alkali activated or geopolymer concrete can potentially provide environmentally safe waste valorization in addition to mechanical and durability benefits. Nevertheless, dedicated long-term leaching assessment under different environmental exposure conditions is still recommended for comprehensive environmental performance evaluation of the developed alkali activated systems.
