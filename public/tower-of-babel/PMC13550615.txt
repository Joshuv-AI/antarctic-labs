Study on failure law and repair technology of weak surrounding rock in adjacent roadway under excavation disturbance
Authors: Xiaoping Wang, Longyu Hu, Lin Guo, Xiangqian Wang, Xue Li, Xiang Cheng, Junlong He, Peiluo Wang, Qingheng Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Based on a case study of the track cross-cut in Liuzhuang Coal Mine’s western shaft bottom yard, this research investigates the deformation and failure behavior of weak surrounding rock in adjacent roadways subjected to excavation disturbance. A combined methodology was applied, including field measurement, similarity simulation, and numerical modeling, to analyze failure mechanisms and design a remediation strategy for the affected rock zone. Results indicate that roadway deformation primarily exhibits as cross-section convergence, influenced by surrounding rock strength, support resistance, and excavation disturbance. Stress affected zones from adjacent excavations overlap, expanding the unloading range, with the initially excavated roadway shielding stress redistribution in subsequent ones. Compared to shed support alone, the combination of full cables, steel sets, and an inverted arch reduced displacement growth by 95.31%. Numerical simulation validated that after repair, the maximum horizontal displacement increased by 63 mm while the vertical stress peak dropped by4.05 MPa. The integrated support system—combining active cables with passive shed and inverted arch—effectively stabilized the weak rock by enhancing self-bearing capacity, transferring stress deeper, and optimizing the stress environment. The findings offer practical guidance for repairing adjacent roadways under similar conditions.

Introduction

The growing demand for coal resources in China has necessitated the development of deeper mines1,2. Under the deep mining environment characterized by “five high and two disturbances” conditions, surrounding rock often degrades, leading to deformation, failure, and repeated repairs of roadways3–6. As critical junctions connecting shaft hoisting with main roadway transport, shaft bottom yard roadways feature large cross-sections, multiple free surfaces, and dense layouts. These characteristics make them especially susceptible to instability and deformation under excavation disturbance7,8. Therefore, understanding the deformation and failure mechanisms of surrounding rock in these areas is essential for ensuring roadway stability and maintaining safe mine operations.

A thorough understanding of the deformation and failure behavior of roadways under excavation disturbance is fundamental to ensuring their stability. Recent engineering cases have demonstrated that deep soft rock roadways under high structural stress or adjacent excavation disturbance often experience severe deformation, and combined active–passive support systems have been proven effective in controlling such instability9,10. The principles and rapid advancement of distributed fiber optic sensing (DFOS) provide a powerful tool for this purpose11–13. Numerous scholars have successfully employed fiber-optic monitoring techniques, yielding significant insights through both physical similarity simulations and field measurements14–17. Liu et al.18 found that the disturbance still exists when the roadway is greater than 5 times the diameter of the tunnel. Li et al.19 revealed that tunnel excavation induces stress redistribution and abrupt strain changes in the surrounding rock, emphasizing the critical role of the rock-lining system in ensuring long-term stability. Cheng et al.20 employed distributed fiber optic sensing to reveal that coal mining induces dynamic deformation and crack propagation in overburden strata.

Chai et al.21,22 analyzed the application of optical fiber bed technology in the monitoring of surrounding rock deformation and failure, summarized the optical fiber sensing theory and its application characteristics. Sun et al.23 employed distributed optical fiber sensing based on Brillouin scattering to achieve dynamic in-situ monitoring of overburden deformation in a coal mining face. Nowak et al.24 demonstrated the feasibility of deploying a low-cost, high-sensitivity fiber optic sensing network for geomechanical monitoring in large excavation areas using a novel DOFS method. Li et al.25 applied PPP-BOTDA fiber optic sensing in similar model tests to accurately map stope support pressure, demonstrating its high precision and strong anti-interference capability.

In summary, while fiber-optic monitoring has been extensively applied and yielded significant results in coal mining research, studies specifically targeting the excavation-induced deformation and failure mechanisms of shaft bottom yard track cross-cuts remain scarce. In particular, one key problem needs to be addressed: the deformation control mechanism and effectiveness of repair technologies under different surrounding rock lithologies and support conditions. Addressing this gap, this study investigates the Liuzhuang Coal Mine’s western shaft bottom yard as a case study. A comprehensive methodology was employed, integrating three-dimensional physical modeling, numerical simulation, and field trials to analyze the deformation and failure behavior induced by track cross-cut excavation. The findings provide a practical basis for designing effective roadway rehabilitation and stability control strategies.

Engineering geological survey

Geological conditions of roadway

The shaft bottom yard in the west area of Liuzhuang Coal Mine contains three track cross-cuts. No. 1 track cross-cut primarily traverses carbonaceous mudstone, mudstone, sandy mudstone, along with the 17 − 1 and 16 − 2 coal seams. The surrounding rock of No. 2 track cross-cut consists mainly of mudstone, sandy mudstone, and the 17 − 1 and 17 − 2 coal seams. No. 3 track cross-cut is opened in the sandy mudstone interbedded with mudstone of the 17 − 1 coal seam roof, exposing lithologies including sandy mudstone, 17 − 1 coal, and siltstone. The comprehensive geological columnar section of No. 3 track cross-cut, partial roadway failure conditions, layout of the track cross-cuts, and monitoring data of surface displacement and support are summarized in Fig. 1.

The No.1 and No.2 track cross-cuts are the first excavation roadways, and the No.3 track cross-cuts are the post-excavation roadways that meet the requirements of ventilation and auxiliary transportation. Due to the excavation disturbance from No. 3 track cross-cut, significant deformation occurred in No. 2 and No. 1 track cross-cuts. To monitor roadway stability and ensure mine safety, displacement monitoring points were installed in the shaft bottom yard and related chambers. In No. 1 track cross-cut, eight measuring points (J1 ~ J2 to J15 ~ J16) were set west of the intake shaft. No. 2 track cross-cut was equipped with nine points (F1 ~ F2 to F17 ~ F18) arranged from west to east. The monitoring results are presented in Fig. 2.

This section, composed mainly of carbonaceous mudstone, mudstone, and the 17 − 1 coal seam, features relatively weak surrounding rock. Its proximity to a chamber and the larger cross-section further contributed to deformation under excavation disturbance from No. 3 track cross-cut.As shown in Fig. 2(b), points F1 ~ F2 exhibited a maximum convergence of 36 mm in sandy mudstone, supported by a combined “inner anchoring–external framing” system with an inverted arch. The frequency, amplitude, and waveform characteristics of the reflected signals are interpreted to determine the structure, position, lithology, and geometry of the target formation26,27. Taking No. 2 track cross-cut as an example, the roadway is divided from east to west into three zones based on support type: a normally-supported section with internal anchoring and external steel sets, a weakly-supported section with only steel sets, and a strongly-supported section incorporating both steel sets and an inverted arch.

Based on comprehensive field measurements, the deformation and failure pattern of the track cross-cut is characterized by sidewall convergence and floor heave. The failure mechanism involves continuous rheological behavior of the weak surrounding rock under high deep-ground stress and excavation-induced disturbances. The deformation is attributed to poor rock conditions, insufficient support resistance, and significant disturbance from adjacent cross-cut excavation.

Analysis of horizontal optical fiber results

According to three horizontal optical fibers, the contour cloud map of the model z = 28 section is drawn, and the results are shown in Fig. 7 .

Fig. 7Contour cloud map in vertical direction.

Contour cloud map in vertical direction.

Following the excavation of No. 1 track cross-cut, tensile deformation developed along both sidewalls. A similar deformation pattern was observed in No. 2 track cross-cut, with sidewalls also exhibiting tensile deformation. The surrounding rock between the two cross-cuts experienced an expanded tensile stress zone due to superimposed excavation disturbances. After the connecting roadway was excavated, the tensile stress zones on both sides of the joint roadway became interconnected, further influencing the rock between No. 1 and No. 2 track cross-cuts. The subsequent excavation of No. 3 track cross-cut intensified the disturbance in this region, while the rock between No. 2 and No. 3 track cross-cuts displayed a similar deformation response.

During adjacent roadway excavation, stress disturbance zones interact and superimpose. The surrounding rock immediately adjacent to the tunnel surface enters a plastic state under unloading. As excavation progresses, particularly near roadway intersections, the combined effect of stress redistribution from neighboring tunnels enlarges the unloading range and intensifies the disturbance. The overlapping stress zones may eventually connect, potentially initiating progressive failure. Additionally, the initially excavated roadway exerts a shielding effect on subsequent excavations, reducing stress concentration in later-driven roadways due to the pre-existing opening.

Simulation of deformation and failure law of track cross-cut

Establishment of numerical model

Based on the deformation and failure characteristics observed in the deep shaft bottom yard track cross-cut under various influencing factors, this study accounts for the significant variation in surrounding rock conditions and supporting forms along different sections of the same roadway. The investigation focuses on the deformation behavior of surrounding rock under excavation disturbance, considering lithological diversity and support configurations. Displacement and stress evolution following roadway excavation are analyzed to inform subsequent repair strategies and stability control measures33,34. The simulation schemes are designed as follows:

Scheme 1: The model considers three track cross-cuts at a burial depth of 740 m. Focusing on the section between the intersection of No. 2 track cross-cut and the waiting chamber—where the lithology is poorest—variations in stress and displacement are analyzed under different surrounding rock types: mudstone, interbedded sandy mudstone, sandy mudstone, and siltstone, by adjusting their mechanical parameters.

Scheme 2: Using the same model as Scheme 1, this scheme examines the influence of support systems in the weakly supported section between the intersection of No. 2 track cross-cut and the waiting chamber. Stress and displacement responses are evaluated under four support configurations: steel set support only; combined bolt-cable and steel set; bolt-cable with floor anchor cables and steel set; and full cable support with an inverted arch and steel set.

In order to simulate the disturbance and failure of the surrounding rock of the No.3 track and the No.2 track, the field deformation area is selected for simulation. The model size is 150 m×200 m×80 m.

The three-dimensional cavern group model established in Rhino will be generated into a grid, and the grid will be transformed into a numerical model through Griddle2.0 (Itasca Consulting Group, Inc. https://www.itascacg.com/software/griddle.). The numerical model grid is imported into FLAC3D 6.0 (Itasca Consulting Group, Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA. The official website is: https://www.itascacg.com/software/flac3d.)numerical simulation software for simulation operation. The measuring lines are 1 m, 2 m, 5 m and 10 m away from the left side of the No.2 track, and Y = 40 m, 120 m and 150 m are selected to arrange the measuring lines, which are located in the strong support area, the weak support area and the normal support area respectively. Horizontal displacement contourprofile of No.3 track cross-cut excavation under different surrounding rock conditions.

Under different surrounding rock conditions, the maximum horizontal displacement at 1 m into the left side of the No. 2 track cross-cut increased as follows: in mudstone, from 256 mm to 320 mm; in sandstone-mudstone interbeds, from 105 mm to 132 mm; in sandy mudstone, from 70 mm to 85 mm; and in siltstone, from 52 mm to 60 mm. Analysis of horizontal displacement trends reveals that both the initial displacement before the excavation of No. 3 track cross-cut and the displacement increment induced by its excavation decreased progressively with increasing rock strength. In the section between the intersection and the waiting chamber of No. 2 track cross-cut, the distribution of excavation-induced horizontal displacement varied significantly with lithology. In mudstone, displacements near the middle of the section were comparable to those close to the intersection. For sandstone-mudstone interbeds, mid-section displacements were slightly lower, while in sandy mudstone and siltstone, they were substantially lower—indicating negligible excavation disturbance in the central zone under these conditions.

Fig. 10Stress distribution surface diagram of No.3 track cross-cut excavation under different surrounding rock conditions.

Stress distribution surface diagram of No.3 track cross-cut excavation under different surrounding rock conditions.

Figure 10 illustrates the vertical stress distribution at the Z = 37 m plane before and after excavating No. 3 track cross-cut under different surrounding rock conditions. The maximum vertical stress initially increases with rock strength up to sandy mudstone, beyond which it declines with further strength improvement, with stress concentration most pronounced near the roadway intersection. Under mudstone conditions, the maximum vertical stress increased from 41.6 MPa to 42.7 MPa, with the stress concentration factor rising from 2.64 to 2.71; for sandstone-mudstone interbeds, these values changed from 44.5 MPa to 44.68 MPa and 2.82 to 2.84; in sandy mudstone, from 51.42 MPa to 51.57 MPa and 3.26 to 3.27; while in siltstone, from 47.06 MPa to 47.13 MPa and 2.987 to 2.99.The reason why the stress concentration factor in sandy mudstone is higher than that in siltstone lies in the interaction between rock strength and stress redistribution capacity. Although both rocks are relatively strong, sandy mudstone has a moderate elastic modulus of 13.6 GPa, which limits lateral stress transmission and causes stress to concentrate more intensely near the roadway. Siltstone, with a higher modulus of 15.5 GPa and a greater tensile strength of 5.57 MPa, allows stress to dissipate over a wider range and transfer to deeper zones, thereby reducing the local peak stress and the corresponding concentration factor. Therefore, a higher rock strength does not always lead to a higher stress concentration factor; the efficiency of stress propagation plays a key role.

By changing the support in this area, the horizontal displacement and vertical stress of the area after the excavation of the No.3 track under different support methods are analyzed. Fig. 11Horizontal displacement contour profile before and after excavation of No.3 track cross-cut under different support schemes.

Under different support schemes, the maximum horizontal displacement at 1 m into the left side of No. 2 track cross-cut exhibited distinct responses, as shown in Fig. 11. Under Support Scheme 1, displacement increased from 256 mm to 320 mm; Scheme 2 showed an increase from 132 mm to 171 mm; Scheme 3 from 108 mm to 132 mm; and Scheme 4 demonstrated the most controlled behavior, with displacement rising only from 29 mm to 32 mm. Analysis reveals that both the initial horizontal displacement before No. 3 track cross-cut excavation and the subsequent displacement increment induced by its excavation decreased progressively with enhanced support intensity. In the section between the intersection and waiting chamber of No. 2 track cross-cut, the distribution of excavation-induced displacement varied significantly with support conditions. Under basic steel set support, mid-section displacements approached those near the intersection. In contrast, under reinforced support schemes, mid-section displacements were substantially lower, indicating effective containment of excavation disturbance. The performance comparison demonstrates that roadway supported solely by steel sets experienced significant displacement both during initial excavation and under subsequent adjacent excavation. The incorporation of bolt-cable systems substantially improved support resistance, reducing sensitivity to both inherent and external excavation disturbances. The addition of an inverted arch further enhanced stability, resulting in lower displacements in reinforced sections compared to roadway ends. These findings confirm that enhanced support intensity not only improves inherent roadway stability but also reduces susceptibility to adjacent excavation effects. Although the bypass section of No. 2 track cross-cut initially exhibited greater displacement than the ends due to lower support intensity, this pattern reversed under reinforced support conditions. This demonstrates that horizontal deformation is primarily governed by support configuration, while remaining subject to influencing factors such as surrounding rock conditions.

Figure 12 illustrates the vertical stress distribution before and after excavation of No. 3 track cross-cut under varying support conditions. As support intensity increases, the maximum vertical stress exhibits a progressive decline, with peak stress concentrations consistently localized around the roadway intersection zone. Under Support Scheme 1, the maximum vertical stress rose from 41.6 MPa to 42.7 MPa, accompanied by an increase in the stress concentration factor from 2.64 to 2.71. Scheme 2 recorded an increase from 39.82 MPa to 41.30 MPa, with the concentration factor advancing from 2.53 to 2.62. For Scheme 3, stresses increased from 37.78 MPa to 40.07 MPa while the concentration factor grew from 2.40 to 2.54. Notably, Scheme 4 demonstrated minimal variation, with stress values remaining nearly constant between 31.42 MPa and 31.49 MPa and maintaining a stable concentration factor of 1.99. The analysis indicates that reinforced support structures effectively absorb excavation-induced energy, thereby mitigating dynamic instability risks. Enhanced support configurations reduce both the stress magnitude within disturbed zones and the peak vertical stress values, while simultaneously lowering stress concentration factors. Following excavation of No. 3 track cross-cut, robust support systems demonstrated significant resistance to disturbance stresses. The incorporation of an inverted arch structure proved particularly effective, virtually eliminating excavation-induced impacts in the study area. These reinforced support mechanisms substantially improve the load-bearing capacity of surrounding rock and facilitate stress transfer to deeper geological formations.

These strain distributions directly reflect mine pressure manifestations in the surrounding rock at each fiber location, as shown in Fig. 14.

Fiber-optic monitoring results for the normally supported section in Fig. 14(a) reveal minimal strain variation along the roadway sidewalls. Consecutive measurements showed strain changes below 200 microstrain, indicating both instrument stability and consistent deformation behavior in the surrounding rock. The absence of significant mine pressure manifestations suggests that excavation disturbances from No. 3 track cross-cut did not substantially affect this area. The weakly supported section, shown in Fig. 14(b), experienced considerable excavation impact. During the initial 20-day monitoring period, significant strain developed in two distinct zones: within 2.9 m of the borehole collar and at depths between 4 and 7 m. Long-term monitoring confirmed stabilized shallow strains but revealed expanding tensile strain at 6–7 m depth, demonstrating continued rheological activity in this stress-sensitive zone. In the strongly supported section presented in Fig. 14 (c), minimal strain variation with consistent patterns across monitoring cycles reflects stable conditions without significant mine pressure. This performance results from favorable geological conditions combined with enhanced support resistance, including the additional reinforcement provided by an inverted arch structure in the floor area.

The brush excavation enlarged the roadway cross-section, triggering stress redistribution around the opening. This expansion caused the original stress concentration zone to extend outward, increasing the unloading range within the surrounding rock and leading to continued deformation. Although such sectional enlargement could potentially raise stress peaks, the implemented support system—integrating active reinforcement through full-length anchor cables with passive support from steel sets and an inverted arch—enhanced the surrounding rock’s self-bearing capacity. This composite system successfully transferred stresses deeper into the rock mass, reducing the vertical stress peak by 9.4%. The results demonstrate that this rehabilitation support scheme effectively controls roadway deformation, optimizes the stress environment, and ensures surrounding rock stability.

Limitations of the study

(1) This study is based on a single mine case. Although the geological conditions and support schemes are representative of many deep soft rock roadways, the generalizability of the results may be limited. Multi-site validation is needed to confirm the broader applicability of the proposed repair scheme.

(2) The numerical simulations employed the Mohr-Coulomb constitutive model, which is suitable for describing the instantaneous strength and failure of rock masses. However, this model does not capture long-term creep or time-dependent deformation, which may be significant for weak surrounding rock over extended periods. Future work should adopt creep models to assess long-term stability.

(3) The on-site monitoring duration after repair was 14 months. While this period has been sufficient to observe stabilization of deformation rates, it may not cover the full service life of the repaired roadway. Continued monitoring is recommended to verify long-term performance.

Conclusions

(ⅰ) The deformation and failure of weak surrounding rock in adjacent roadways under excavation disturbance are primarily controlled by three factors: surrounding rock lithology, support structure, and disturbance intensity. The weakly supported section with only shed support experienced severe cross-section convergence, while the strongly supported section with an inverted arch remained stable with a loose zone of only 1.6 m.

(ⅱ) By applying OFDR and DIC monitoring techniques in physical similarity simulation experiments, it was found that adjacent roadway excavation enlarges the unloading zone through stress superposition, and the initially excavated roadway exerts a “shielding effect” that reduces stress concentration in later-driven roadways.

(ⅲ) Compared to shed support alone, the combined support system of full cables, steel sets, and an inverted arch reduced displacement growth by 95.31%. After repair, the maximum horizontal displacement increased by 63 mm as controlled deformation, while the peak vertical stress decreased by 4.05 MPa, reflecting the yield-resistant support concept.

(ⅳ) For the stability control of surrounding rock disturbed by excavation in similar deep soft rock roadways, a combined active–passive support system should be adopted. For weakly supported sections, full cable support with an additional inverted arch can be applied to ensure long-term stability.
