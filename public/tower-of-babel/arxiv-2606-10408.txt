A Modular Approach to Succinct Arguments for QMA
James Bartusek, Jiahui Liu, Giulio Malavolta

Abstract
Succinct argument systems are of central importance to modern crytpography, enabling the efficient verification of computational claims. In the classical setting, Kilian (STOC 92) established that any probabilistically checkable proof for NP can be transformed into a succinct argument system for NP using only collision-resistant hash functions. In the quantum setting, recent works have established the feasibility of (classically-verifiable) succinct arguments for QMA, capturing statements that require quantum proofs. However, known constructions all rely on the highly structured assumption of learning with errors (LWE), which stands in stark contrast with the unstructured assumptions that suffice for NP. In this work, we develop a new framework that broadens the cryptographic foundations of succinct arguments for QMA. We assume the existence of (i) an oblivious state preparation (OSP) protocol, which in turn can be constructed from plain trapdoor claw-free functions, and (ii) collapsing hash functions, the quantum analogue of collision-resistance. In particular, we obtain the first succinct, classically-verifiable argument system for QMA which does not rely on the hardness of LWE. Our construction proceeds in two steps. First, we design a round-efficient classically-verifiable argument system for QMA based only on the assumption of OSP. Second, we introduce a generalized communication compression compiler , which, assuming collapsing hash functions, transforms any T T -round interactive protocol into one in which the communication size is bounded by T ⋅ poly ⁡ ( λ ) T\cdot\poly(\lambda) for some fixed poly \poly independent of the original size of each message. Our compiler extends a quantum rigidity-based communication compression technique of Zhang (QCrypt 25), and may be of independent interest.

Introduction

The ability to convince a skeptic of the validity of a mathematical statement in time much less than it takes to
prove
the statement is a widely celebrated result in cryptography
[

]
, inspiring decades of foundational and applied research on the topic of

. Remarkably, Kilian’s initial feasibility result utilized only lightweight “unstructured” cryptography. In particular, Kilian showed how to compile any probabilistically checkable proof system (PCP)
[

,
10.1145/273865.273901
]

More recently, the growing interest and investment in quantum information processing has given rise to natural related questions: Can we efficiently verify statements that require
quantum
proofs? And under what cryptographic assumptions?

Intriguingly, a quantum analogue of Kilian’s compiler was worked out by
[
10.1145/3564246.3585198
]
, establishing that PCPs for QMA can be compiled into succinct argument systems for QMA, utilizing only unstructured cryptography (in fact, utilizing assumptions that are plausibly even weaker than one-way functions). Unfortunately, it is unknown whether PCPs for QMA exist! Indeed, fundamental properties of quantum information such as unclonability suggest that quantum PCPs may not exist at all (see e.g.
[

Despite this gap, the community has still managed to demonstrate the feasibility of succinct arguments for QMA

1.1
Results

In this work, we introduce a novel framework for building (classically-verifiable) succinct arguments for QMA, and leverage our approach to diversify the sources of cryptographic hardness under which succinct arguments for QMA exist. In more detail, we utilize the following objects.

•

Oblivious state preparation.
OSP, recently introduced by
[
bartusek2025power
]
, is an interactive protocol between a classical client and a quantum server that prepares a BB84 state on the server’s side without revealing the choice of basis to the server. It can be constructed from any “plain” trapdoor claw-free function (without assuming extra properties such as dual-mode or the adaptive hard-core bit), and is known from LWE
[
BCMVV
]
, cryptographic group actions
[
AMR22
]
, and the lattice isomorphism problem
[
cryptoeprint:2025/993
]

•

Collapsing hash functions.
Collapsing is the post-quantum analogue of collision-resistance, and is a standard “unstructured” hardness assumption that holds in the quantum random oracle model
[
unruh2016computationally
]
, or from a variety of computational assumptions
[
zhandry2022new
]
. Collapsing hash functions are known to imply post-quantum succinct arguments for NP (by adapting the proof of Kilian’s protocol to the post-quantum setting)
[
CMSZ
]
, though it remains a fascinating open problem whether collapsing hash functions alone (or other sources of unstructured hardness) suffice to build succinct arguments for QMA.

Our main result can be summarized as follows.

Theorem 1

(Informal)
.

There exists a fixed polynomial
\poly
such that, assuming OSP and collapsing hash functions, there exist a classically-verifiable argument system for QMA with communication size
\poly(\lambda)
and verifier complexity
|x|\cdot\poly(\lambda)
, where
|x|
is the instance size.

We note that some prior work (see e.g. definitions in

We note that, like previous work on classically-verifiable succinct arguments for QMA

]
, our result is interesting even when applied to BQP languages, establishing that (bounded-error) polynomial-time quantum computations can be classically verified in time much less than the time it takes to execute the quantum computation.

Our main result can also be viewed in light of recent work

establishing that either OSP or “plain” trapdoor claw-free functions alone are enough to obtain (non-succinct) classical verification of quantum computation. We show that making use of collapsing hash functions along with OSP suffices to realize the additional property of

Finally, it is straightforward to extend our methods to obtain a succinct protocol for

There exists a fixed polynomial

We note that this statement requires assuming the hardness of LWE but, to the best of our knowledge, succinct arguments for sampling problems were not known from any assumption prior to this work. We also mention that, as a byproduct of our techniques, we obtain a general compiler that allows us to compress the communication and the verifier-complexity of

Techniques

Our approach is quite modular, and can be broken into two central steps.

1.

Step I: First, we design a
\poly(\lambda)
-round

2.

Step II: Second, we develop a “generalized communication compression compiler”, and apply it to our

In more detail, we establish the following result. Fix any

\ket{x_0}+\ket{x_1}

2.1
Round-Efficient Classical Verification for QMA

Background.

Our first, intermediate, goal en route to succinct arguments for QMA is the following: We aim to construct a classically-verifiable
\poly(\lambda)
-round protocol, but where the amount of communication in each round may be non-succinct. That is, we focus first on optimizing for
round
complexity.

By now, there exist a few approaches to classical verification of quantum computation, so we begin by reviewing some of these approaches, and explain why known results fall short of our goals.

•

The original approach of
[
Mahadev2018
]
is centered around the idea of a “measurement protocol”, and in fact it is known how to leverage this approach to obtain a
constant
-round protocol
[
ACGH
]
. However, the underlying measurement protocol requires the use of a TCF with the adaptive hard-core bit property, which we aim to avoid in order to diversify the set of assumptions from which our approach can be instantiated.

•

More recently, the framework of compiled non-local games
[
kalai2023quantum
]
has been successfully utilized to obtain classical argument systems for QMA
[
natarajan2023bounding
]
, even from more-general assumptions such as OSP
[
bartusek2025power
]
or plain TCFs
[
BKM+25
]
. Unfortunately, both of these instantiations suffer from round complexity that depends on the size of the QMA witness. This is due to the fact that they compile a non-local game with very small completeness-soundness gap, and thus inherit a prohibitively large round complexity blow-up from sequential repetition.

Our protocol.

We follow the non-local games approach, but begin with a game that already enjoys
constant
completeness-soundness gap, so that sequential repetition will not be too costly.

In more detail, we begin with a simplified version of the two-round two-prover non-local game from
[
metger2024succinct
]
, where both questions and answers may grow with the size of the witness.
4
4

4

In particular, we do not require their “question-reduction” machinery of sampling questions from
\varepsilon
-biased sets and subsampling the Hamiltonian terms.
However, we require that this game satisfies two particular properties.

i)

Alice’s operation has low depth. That is, the quantum circuit that operates on the quantum witness, her halves of EPR pairs, and her question, and outputs her answer, has depth that is bounded by some fixed polynomial
\poly(\lambda)
of the security parameter. We observe that this holds for (our simplified version of)
[
metger2024succinct
]
’s protocol.

ii)

The game is sound against
computationally
non-local strategies
[
bartusek2025power
]
. That is, soundness holds against two-party strategies that may share the same Hilbert space but where the left-over state after Alice’s operation is computationally independent of her question. We confirm that this holds for
[
metger2024succinct
]
’s protocol, as their proof in the “compiled” setting only makes use of the computational indistinguishability of Alice’s questions.

We then compile the two-prover game into a single-prover protocol using a “generalized” KLVY compiler
[
kalai2023quantum
,
bartusek2025power
,
BKM+25
]
, which performs Alice’s operation under a
blind
classical delegation of quantum computation protocol, followed by Bob’s operation in the clear. Blind classical delegation of quantum computation can be constructed from OSP
[
bartusek2025power
]
, where the number of rounds grows only with the
depth
of the delegated operation. Thus, our compiled protocol still has a strict
\poly(\lambda)
number of rounds (though the communication in each round may grow with the size of the witness).We refer to the reader to
for further details.

2.2
A Generic Approach to Communication Compression

At the heart of our approach to succinctness is an inherently quantum technique for constructing simulation-based secure computation with succinct communication. The basic idea was pioneered by Zhang
[
cryptoeprint:2023/1492
]
, and we extend his work to obtain our general compiler.

Background.

[
cryptoeprint:2023/1492
]
addresses the following setting. Let
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{m}
be a function with
m\gg n
, and suppose that a client wants to send
f(x)
to a server for a random
x\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
, without revealing the value of
x
. In the fully classical setting, it is provably
impossible
to obtain a succinct (i.e. much less than
m
) communication protocol for this functionality with simulation-security
[
HW
]
. Interestingly,
[
cryptoeprint:2023/1492
]
shows that quantum “rigidity” techniques can be applied to obtain a protocol with succinct
quantum
communication.

The basic, simplified, idea is as follows. The client first samples random
x_{0},x_{1},r_{0},r_{1}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
and sends the claw state

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0,x_0,r_0}+\ket{1,x_1,r_1})

to the server. An honest server will first compute the function
f
on the input register in superposition, yielding

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0,x_0,r_0,f(x_0)}+\ket{1,x_1,r_1,f(x_1)}),

and then “delete” the input by measuring the “
x
” register in the Hadamard basis to obtain a string
c\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. Then, the client selects one of three options.

•

Standard basis test:
The server measures the remaining registers in the standard basis to obtain the (large) string
(b,r_{b},f(x_{b}))
. It then engages in a succinct “commit-reveal-prove” protocol (based on a collapsing hash function) with the client to argue that this information was honestly generated.

•

Hadamard basis test:
The server measures the remaining registers in the Hadamard basis to obtain the (large) string
d
. It then engages in a similar succinct “commit-reveal-prove” with the client to prove honest behavior.

•

Output generation:
The server measures the first two remaining registers in the standard basis to obtain the (short) string
(b,r_{b})
, which it sends back to the client. The server’s state collapses to
f(x_{b})
, while the client knows
x_{b}
.

The standard and Hadamard basis tests allow
[
cryptoeprint:2023/1492
]
to make a rigidity argument on any (successful) server’s system, enabling a quantum simulation strategy that has no classical counterpart. Using garbled circuits,
[
cryptoeprint:2023/1492
]
then shows how to extend the above “random-input” functionality to a “chosen-input” functionality, where the client is able to choose any value of
x
, and delegate
f(x)
to the server.

Committed secure function evaluation.

In this work, we adapt the basic idea outlined above to the following setting. Consider any protocol that takes place between a (potentially quantum) server and a classical client, where the goal of the server is to try to convince the client to accept the interaction. For example, any classical interactive argument for QMA fits this description. We aim to replace each round of communication in such a protocol with a fixed
\poly(\lambda)
-communication sub-protocol, regardless of how many bits were communicated in the round.

Prior work
[
BKL+
]
has shown how to generically compress the server’s answers using succinct commitments to these messages followed by (state-preserving) arguments of knowledge
[
LMS
]
. In order to also compress the client’s messages, we propose the following high-level strategy.

•

The client samples random coins
s\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\lambda}
that it will use throughout the protocol. Note that this randomness can be expanded using a PRG, and thus the remaining client’s strategy can be taken to be completely deterministic. In particular, the client’s
next message
function in each round
i
can be expressed as
f_{i}(s,m_{1},\dots,m_{i})
, where
m_{1},\dots,m_{i}
are the prover’s previous messages.

•

Each of the prover messages are replaced with
succinct commitments

\mathsf{Com}(m)
.

•

Each of the verifier messages
f_{i}(s,m_{1},\dots,m_{i-1})
are computed via a succinct “committed secure function evaluation” protocol with public inputs
\mathsf{Com}(m_{1}),\dots,\mathsf{Com}(m_{i-1})
, prover inputs
m_{1},\dots,m_{i}
and (private) verifier input
s
. This protocol is an extension of
[
cryptoeprint:2023/1492
]
’s secure function evaluation protocol described above, where we additionally allow for
large
server inputs that have previously been
succinctly committed
.

As one of our main technical contributions, we develop the required succinct committed secure function evaluation protocol, by extending the protocol of
[
cryptoeprint:2023/1492
]
to handle (committed) prover inputs, and provide a new self-contained proof of security. We refer to
Section

5
for further details.

Restoring classical communication.

Finally, we replace the quantum claw-state communication with classical communication, again relying on the assumption of OSP. We do this via two steps adapted from the prior work of
[
ZhaRSP
]
, and worked out in
Section

4
.

1.

First,
[
ZhaRSP
]
argues that (plain) trapdoor claw-free functions imply a notion of
verifiable
state preparation (VSP) for BB84 states, satisfying a simulation-based notion of security. This strategy can be adapting to showing the OSP implies VSP for BB84 states.

2.

Second,
[
ZhaRSP
]
shows that VSP for BB84 states implies VSP for claw states, which is what we require to instantiate the above with only classical communication.

Quantum Information

In this section, we provide a brief overview of quantum information. For a more detailed introduction, we refer to

A

A

\mathcal{H}_{A}=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
. Given two registers
A

B

The corresponding Hilbert space is given by the tensor product
\mathcal{H}_{AB}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
. The
(quantum) state
of a register
A
is described by a density operator
\rho_{A}
on

, which is a positive semi-definite Hermitian operator with trace equal to one.
A state is called

Thus, pure quantum states can be represented by unit vectors
\ket{\psi}_{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}
, with
\rho_{A}=\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}_{A}
.
For a quantum state
\rho_{AB}
on
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
, we denote
\rho_{A}=\Tr_{B}(\rho_{AB})\in\mathcal{H}_{A}
the reduced state of
\rho_{AB}
on
A
.

A
projective measurement
is defined by a set of projectors
\{\Pi_{j}\}_{j}
such that
\sum_{j}\Pi_{j}=\mathsf{Id}
.
A projector
\Pi
is a Hermitian operator such that
\Pi^{2}=\Pi
, that is, an orthogonal projection.
Given a state
\rho
, the measurement yields outcome
j
with probability
p_{j}=\Tr(\Pi_j \rho)
, upon which the state changes to
{\Pi_{j}\rho\Pi_{j}/p_{j}}
.
A basis measurement is one where
\Pi_{j}=\outerproduct{e_j}{{e_j}}
and the
\{\ket{e_j}\}_{j}
(necessarily) form an orthonormal basis.
A
positive operator-valued measure
(POVM) is a generalization of a projective measurement.
A POVM is defined by a set of positive semi-definite operators
\{\mathsf{E}_{j}\}_{j}
such that
\sum_{j}\mathsf{E}_{j}=\mathsf{Id}
(that is, the
\mathsf{E}_{j}

As before, given a quantum state
\rho
, the probability of obtaining outcome
j

p_{j}=\Tr(\mathsf{E}_j \rho)
, but the state after the measurement is no longer uniquely specified.

For two observables
A,B
, the commutator is denoted as
[A,B]:=AB-BA
and the anticommutator is
\{A,B\}:=AB+BA

The

between two states
\rho
and
\sigma
, denoted by

\mathsf{TD}(\rho,\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}\norm{\rho- \sigma}_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left(\sqrt{(\rho-\sigma)^{\dagger}(\rho-\sigma)}\right).

The trace distance can be equivalently defined as the maximal advantage of any (possibly unbounded) quantum algorithm in distinguishing the two states
\rho
and
\sigma
. Sometimes we use the shorthand
\rho\approx_{\varepsilon}\sigma
to denote
\mathsf{TD}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\varepsilon
.

We recall the following version of the gentle measurement lemma.

Lemma 3

(Gentle Measurement)
.

Let
\rho
be a density matrix and
\Pi
be an orthogonal projection. If
\Tr(\Pi\rho)\geq 1-\varepsilon
, then

\rho\approx_{\varepsilon}\frac{\Pi\rho\Pi}{\Tr(\Pi\rho)}.

We will also consider the notion of

.
Recall that a
nonuniform QPT algorithm

\mathcal{A}=\{\mathcal{A}_{\lambda},\tau_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
consists of a family of quantum channels that can be implemented by a polynomial-size quantum circuit
{\cal A}_{\lambda}
initialized with a quantum advice state

of polynomial number of qubits (we will often drop the explicit mention of
\tau_{\lambda}

We call

Definition 4

(Computational Indistinguishability)
.

We say that two families of states

are computationally indistinguishable if for every nonuniform QPT distinguisher
\mathcal{A}=\{\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
there exists a negligible function

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{ \Pr\left( \mathcal A_\lambda(\rho_\lambda) = 1 \right) - \Pr\left( \mathcal A_\lambda(\sigma_\lambda) = 1 \right) }\leq\negl(\lambda).

3.2
Classically-Verifiable Argument Systems

The class QMA consists of all (promise) languages that are decidable by a polynomial-size quantum circuit, on input a non-deterministically generated witness state. We recall the definition of a classical verification protocol for QMA.

Definition 5
.

A classically-verifiable argument for QMA is an interaction between a QPT prover, with input an instance
x
and state
\ket{\psi}
, and a PPT verifier, with input an instance
x
,

\{\top,\bot\}\leftarrow\langle P(1^{\lambda},\ket{\psi},x),V(1^{\lambda},x)\rangle,

where
\{\top,\bot\}
is the verifier’s output. We say that the protocol is an
(\alpha(\lambda),\beta(\lambda))
argument for QMA if for any language
{\cal L}=({\cal L}_{\mathsf{yes}}\cup{\cal L}_{\mathsf{no}})
in QMA, the following hold.

•

Completeness.
For any
x\in{\cal L}_{\mathsf{yes}}
, there exists a state
\ket{\psi}
such that

\Pr[\top\leftarrow\langle P(1^{\lambda},\ket{\psi},x),V(1^{\lambda},x)\rangle]\geq\alpha(\lambda).

•

Soundness.
For any
x\in{\cal L}_{\mathsf{no}}
, and any QPT adversary
\{{\cal A}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
,

\Pr\left[\top\leftarrow\langle{\cal A}_{\lambda},V(1^{\lambda},x)\rangle\right]\leq\beta(\lambda).

Moreover, we say that the protocol is succinct if there exists a fixed polynomial
p(\lambda)
such that the total communication is bounded by
p(\lambda)
and
V
’s run-time is bounded by
|x|\cdot p(\lambda)
.

.

For any
\varepsilon=1/\poly(\lambda)

\mathsf{TV}\left(\left\{z\leftarrow Q_{\lambda}\right\},\left\{z\leftarrow\langle P(1^{\lambda},|Q|,\varepsilon),V(1^{\lambda},|Q|,\varepsilon)\rangle\right\}\right)=\negl(\lambda).

\{\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}

\bigg|\Pr[1\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}\left(Z[Q,p]\right)]-\Pr[1\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}\left(\langle{\cal A}_{\lambda},V(1^{\lambda},|Q|,\varepsilon)\rangle\right)]\bigg|\leq O(\varepsilon).

Moreover, we say that the protocol is succinct if there exists a fixed polynomial
p(\lambda)
such that, for any
\varepsilon=1/\poly(\lambda)
, the total communication is bounded by
p(\lambda,1/\varepsilon)
and
V
’s run-time is bounded by
|Q|\cdot p(\lambda,1/\varepsilon)
.

3.3
Collision-Resistant and Collapsing Functions

Let
{\cal H}=\{H_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
be such that each
H_{\lambda}
is a distribution over functions
h\colon\{0,1\}^{\ell(\lambda)}\to\{0,1\}^{\lambda}
. We define the standard notion of collision resistance for functions.

Definition 7

(Collision Resistance)
.

{\cal H}
is post-quantum collision resistant if for every QPT adversary
{\cal A}
,

\Pr\left[\begin{array}[]{c}x\neq x^{\prime}\;\wedge\\
h(x)=h(x^{\prime})\end{array}\middle|\begin{array}[]{r}h\leftarrow H_{\lambda}\\
(x,x^{\prime})\leftarrow{\cal A}(h)\end{array}\right]=\negl(\lambda).

The notion of collapsing
[
unruh2016computationally
]
, which we define next, generalizes collision resistance in the context of quantum algorithms.

Definition 8

(Collapsing)
.

{\cal H}
is collapsing if for every QPT adversary
{\cal A}
,

\Big|\Pr[\mathsf{HCollasingExp}(0,\lambda,{\cal A})=1]-\Pr[\mathsf{HCollasingExp}(1,\lambda,{\cal A})=1]\Big|\leq\negl(\lambda).

For
b\in\{0,1\}
the experiment
\mathsf{HCollasingExp}(b,\lambda,{\cal A})
is defined as follows:

1.

The challenger samples
h\leftarrow H_{\lambda}
and sends
h
to
{\cal A}
.

2.

{\cal A}
replies with a (classical) binary string
y\in\{0,1\}^{\ell(\lambda)}
and a
\lambda
-qubit quantum state on register
{\cal X}
. (The requirement that
y
is classical can be enforced by having the challenger immediately measure these registers upon receiving them.)

3.

The challenger computes
h
in superposition on the
\lambda
-qubit quantum state, and measures the bit indicating whether the output of
h
equals
y
. If
h
does not equal
y
, the challenger aborts and outputs
\bot
.

4.

If
b=0
, the challenger does nothing. If
b=1
, the challenger measures the
\lambda
-qubit state in the standard basis.

5.

The challenger returns contents of the registers
{\cal X}
to
{\cal A}
.

6.

{\cal A}
outputs a bit
b^{\prime}
, which is the output of the experiment.

Claim 9

(
[
unruh2016computationally
]
)
.

If
{\cal H}
is collapsing then
{\cal H}
is post-quantum collision resistant.

Remark 10
.

In an abuse of notation, we sometimes refer to collision resistant and/or collapsing functions as
succinct commitments
, where we use
\mathsf{Com}
to refer to the description of the hash function
h
(which is still sampled from some family
H_{\lambda}
).

3.4

We recall the definition of state-preserving arguments of knowledge (AoKs)
[
BKL+
,
LMS
]
. It is shown in

Definition 11

(State-Preserving AoKs for NP)
.

A state-preserving argument of knowledge (AoK) for NP is an interaction between a QPT prover and PPT verifier on input an instance
x

\{\top,\bot\}\leftarrow\langle P(1^{\lambda},x),V(1^{\lambda},x)\rangle,

where
\{\top,\bot\}
is the verifier’s output. For any language
{\cal L}
in NP, the following properties hold.

•

Succinctness.
When invoked on a security parameter
\lambda
and instance size
n
and a relation decidable in time
T
, the communication complexity of the protocol is
\poly(\lambda,\log T)
. The verifier computational complexity is
\poly(\lambda,\log T)+\tilde{O}(n)
.

•

Extraction.
There exists an extractor
\mathsf{Ext}(x,\varepsilon)
with running in time
\poly(n,\lambda,1/\varepsilon)
outputting a classical transcript
\tau
and a classical string

, such that the following holds. For any
\varepsilon=1/\poly(\lambda)
, any QPT adversarial prover
{\cal A}=\{{\cal A}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
, any QPT distinguisher
{\cal D}=\{{\cal D}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
, and any advice state
\rho_{A,E}=\{\rho_{\lambda,A,E}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
, consider the following games.

\mathsf{ExpREAL}[{\cal A},\rho]

–

Run
\langle{\cal A}(A,x),V(1^{\lambda},x)\rangle
and let
\tau
be the classical transcript and
A
the residual state on the input register.

–

Output
(\tau,A,E)
.

\mathsf{ExpIDEAL}[\mathsf{Ext},\varepsilon,\rho]

–

Run
(\tau,w,A)\leftarrow\mathsf{Ext}(x,\varepsilon)^{\mathcal{A}(A,x)}
.

–

Output
(\tau,A,E)
.

Then,

\bigg|\Pr[{\cal D}(\mathsf{ExpREAL}[{\cal A},\rho])=1]-\Pr[{\cal D}(\mathsf{ExpIDEAL}[\mathsf{Ext},\varepsilon,\rho])=1]\bigg|\leq\varepsilon.

Furthermore, the probability that
\tau
is an accepting transcript but

is not a witness for

is bounded by

.

3.5
Remote State Preparation

We define remote state preparation with two flavors of security: (1) an indistinguishability-based notion, called oblivious state preparation (OSP), and (2) a simulation-based notion, called verifiable state preparation (VSP).

Definition 12

(Oblivious State Preparation)
.

Oblivious state preparation (OSP) is a protocol that takes place between a PPT sender
S
with input
b\in\{0,1\}
and a QPT receiver
R
:

(s,\ket*{\psi})\leftarrow\mathsf{OSP}\langle S(1^{\lambda},b),R(1^{\lambda})\rangle,

where
s\in\{0,1\}
is the sender’s output and
\ket*{\psi}
is the receiver’s output. It should satisfy the following properties.

•

Correctness.
For any
b\in\{0,1\}
, let

\Pi_{b}\coloneqq\sum_{s\in\{0,1\}}\outerproduct*{s}{s}\otimes H^{b}\outerproduct*{s}{s}H^{b}.

Then for any
b\in\{0,1\}
,

\E\left[\|\Pi_{b}\ket*{s}\ket*{\psi}\|:(s,\ket*{\psi})\leftarrow\mathsf{OSP}\langle S(1^{\lambda},b),R(1^{\lambda})\rangle\right]=1-\negl(\lambda).

•

Security.
For any QPT adversary
\{{\cal A}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\Big|

\displaystyle\Pr\left[b_{\cal A}=0:(s,b_{\cal A})\leftarrow\mathsf{OSP}\langle S(1^{\lambda},0),{\cal A}_{\lambda}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle-\Pr\left[b_{\cal A}=0:(s,b_{\cal A})\leftarrow\mathsf{OSP}\langle S(1^{\lambda},1),{\cal A}_{\lambda}\rangle\right]\Big|=\negl(\lambda).

Definition 13

(Verifiable State Preparation)
.

Verifiable state preparation (VSP) for a state family
\{\ket{\psi_i}\}_{i\in[K]}
is a protocol that takes place between a PPT sender
S
and a QPT receiver
R
:

(i,\ket*{\psi_i})\leftarrow\mathsf{VSP}\langle S(1^{\lambda},\varepsilon),R(1^{\lambda},\varepsilon)\rangle,

where
\varepsilon
is an error parameter,
i\in[K]\cup\{\bot\}
is the sender’s output, and
\ket*{\psi_i}
is the receiver’s output. It should satisfy the following properties.

•

Correctness.
For any
\varepsilon=1/\poly(\lambda)
,

\mathsf{TD}\left(\mathsf{VSP}\langle S(1^{\lambda},\varepsilon),R(1^{\lambda},\varepsilon)\rangle,\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i\in[K]}\outerproduct{i}{i}\otimes\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}\right)=\negl(\lambda).

•

Security.
For any
\varepsilon=1/\poly(\lambda)
, any QPT adversarial receiver
{\cal A}=\{{\cal A}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
, and any advice state
\rho_{A,E}=\{\rho_{\lambda,A,E}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a QPT simulator
\mathsf{Sim}=\{\mathsf{Sim}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for any QPT distinguisher
{\cal D}=\{{\cal D}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
, the following holds. Define the following games.

\mathsf{ExpREAL}[{\cal A},\varepsilon,\rho]

–

Run
(i,A)\leftarrow\mathsf{VSP}\langle S(1^{\lambda},\varepsilon),{\cal A}(A)\rangle
.

–

Output
(i,A,E)
.

\mathsf{ExpIDEAL}[\mathsf{Sim},\varepsilon,\rho]

–

Run
(b,A)\leftarrow\mathsf{Sim}(1^{\lambda},\varepsilon,A)
.

–

If
b=1
, sample
i\leftarrow[K]
, otherwise if
b=0
, set
i=\bot
and
\ket{\psi_i}=0
.

–

Run
A\leftarrow\mathsf{Sim}(1^{\lambda},\varepsilon,A,\ket{\psi_i})
.

–

Output
(i,A,E)
.

Then,

\bigg|\Pr[{\cal D}(\mathsf{ExpREAL}[{\cal A},\varepsilon,\rho])=1]-\Pr[{\cal D}(\mathsf{ExpIDEAL}[\mathsf{Sim},\varepsilon,\rho])=1]\bigg|\leq\varepsilon.

Generalized Communication Compression Compiler

In the following we present our compiler to generically reduce the communication complexity of interactive protocols between QPT provers and PPT verifiers. For an interactive protocol, we denote its communication complexity as the number of bits exchanged during the interaction. In this section, we will prove the following main theorem.

Theorem 18

(Communication Compression)
.

where

such that the following holds.

•

Completeness.
For any
\chi\in X
and any
\varepsilon=1/\poly(\lambda)
,

\mathsf{TV}\left(\left\{z\leftarrow\langle P(1^{\lambda},\chi),V(1^{\lambda},\chi)\rangle\right\},\left\{z\leftarrow\langle\tilde{P}(1^{\lambda},\chi,\varepsilon),\tilde{V}(1^{\lambda},\chi,\varepsilon)\rangle\right\}\right)=\negl(\lambda).

•

Soundness.
For any
\chi\in X
,
\varepsilon=1/\poly(\lambda)
, and QPT adversary

, there exists a QPT adversary

such that for any QPT distinguisher

\absolutevalue{\Pr\left[1 \gets\D_\secp\left(\langle\cA_\secp,V(1^\secp,\chi)\rangle\right)\right] - \Pr\left[1 \gets\D_\secp\left(\langle\tilde\Adv_\secp,\tilde V(1^\secp,\chi, \varepsilon)\rangle\right)\right]}\leq O(\varepsilon).

•

such that the communication complexity of the protocol is bounded by

and the runtime of the verifier is bounded by

.

Moreover, additionally assuming OSP (
Definition

12
), there exists a compressed protocol where the verifier
\tilde{V}
is completely classical.

We note that the notion of soundness as defined above immediately implies that any bound that one can establish for the non-compiled protocol immediately carries over to the compiled protocol, up to arbitrarily small inverse-polynomial factors.

5.1
Definition: Committed Secure Function Sampling

A Secure Function Sampling (SFS) protocol
[
cryptoeprint:2023/1492
]
allows a prover and a verifier to interactively sample a random input/output pair for a given function. Here we define an extension of this primitive, where the function takes an additional input that corresponds to the pre-image of a commitment. We assume that the commitment is
deterministic
and satisfies the notion of collision-resistance (see
Section

3.3
).

Definition 19

(Committed SFS)
.

Committed Secure Function Sampling (SFS) for a function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}^{m}
and a commitment
\mathsf{Com}
is a protocol that takes place between a PPT verifier
V
and a QPT prover
P
:

(z,x)\leftarrow\mathsf{cSFS}\langle P(1^{\lambda},\varepsilon,y),V(1^{\lambda},\varepsilon,c)\rangle,

where
\varepsilon
is an error parameter,
z
is the prover’s output, and
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}\cup\{\bot\}
is the verifier’s output. It should satisfy the following properties.

•

Correctness.
For any
\varepsilon=1/\poly(\lambda)
and all pairs
(y,c=\mathsf{Com}(y))
,

\mathsf{TD}\left(\mathsf{cSFS}\langle P(1^{\lambda},\varepsilon,y),V(1^{\lambda},\varepsilon,c)\rangle,\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\outerproduct{x}{x}\otimes\outerproduct{f(x,y)}{f(x,y)}\right)=\negl(\lambda).

•

Security.
For any
\varepsilon=1/\poly(\lambda)
, any QPT environment
{\cal E}=\{{\cal E}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
, and any QPT adversarial prover
{\cal A}=\{{\cal A}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a QPT simulator
\mathsf{Sim}=\{\mathsf{Sim}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for any QPT distinguisher
{\cal D}=\{{\cal D}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
, the following holds. Define the following games.

\mathsf{ExpREAL}[{\cal E},{\cal A},\varepsilon]

–

Run
y,\rho_{A,E}\leftarrow{\cal E}(\mathsf{Com})
.

–

Run
(A,x)\leftarrow\mathsf{cSFS}\langle{\cal A}(A),V(1^{\lambda},\varepsilon,\mathsf{Com}(y))\rangle
.

–

Output
(x,A,E)
.

\mathsf{ExpIDEAL}[{\cal E},\mathsf{Sim},\varepsilon]

–

Run
y,\rho_{A,E}\leftarrow{\cal E}(\mathsf{Com})
.

–

Run
(b,A)\leftarrow\mathsf{Sim}(1^{\lambda},\varepsilon,A)
.

–

If
b=1
, sample
x\leftarrow X
and set
z=f(x,y)
, otherwise if
b=0
, set
x=z=\bot
.

–

Run
A\leftarrow\mathsf{Sim}(1^{\lambda},\varepsilon,A,z)
.

–

Output
(x,A,E)
.

Then,

\bigg|\Pr[{\cal D}(\mathsf{ExpREAL}[{\cal A},\varepsilon,\rho,y])=1]-\Pr[{\cal D}(\mathsf{ExpIDEAL}[\mathsf{Sim},\varepsilon,\rho,y])=1]\bigg|\leq\varepsilon.

•

Efficiency.
There exists a fixed polynomial
p(\lambda)=\poly(\lambda)
such that the communication complexity of the protocol is bounded by
p(\lambda,1/\varepsilon,n)
and the runtime of the verifier is bounded by
|f|\cdot p(\lambda,1/\varepsilon,n)
, where
|f|
is the description size of
f
(as opposed to the time it takes to execute).

From Random-Input to Chosen-Input.

A variant of the above primitive allows the verifier to choose an arbitrary input
x
, while attaining the same security guarantees. To differentiate between these two primitives we refer to them as
random-input
committed SFS and
chosen-input
committed SFS. It is shown in
[
cryptoeprint:2023/1492
]
that random-input SFS and chosen-input SFS are equivalent assuming the existence of one-way functions. The same transformation works in the committed settings, which we sketch here.

Let
g(\cdot,y)
be the function that takes as input some random coins
r\in\{0,1\}^{\lambda}
and returns a garbling
[
yao1986generate
]
of the circuit computing
f_{y}(\cdot)=f(\cdot,y)
. Running a random-input committed SFS results in the verifier receiving a random
r
and the prover receiving the garbled circuit
g(r,y)=\tilde{f}_{y}
. The verifier can then expand
r
into pairs of labels and, for each pair, send to the prover the
x_{i}
-th label. Security follows by first invoking
Definition

19
to program the output of the prover and then substituting it with a simulated garbled circuit, which is indistinguishable by the security of garbled circuits. Since this is a standard transformation and it is already proven (up to trivial syntactical modifications) in
[
cryptoeprint:2023/1492
]
, we omit a formal proof and we directly state the following lemma.

Lemma 20

(Random-input implies chosen-input committed SFS)
.

Assuming one-way functions, the existence of random-input committed SFS implies the existence of chosen-input committed SFS.

5.2
Random-Input Committed SFS

We present a self-contained description of our random-input committed SFS protocol, which is itself a variant of the protocol proposed in
[
cryptoeprint:2023/1492
]
. We assume the existence of the following ingredients:

•

A state-preserving succinct AoK for NP (
Section

3.4
).

•

A succinct commitment
\mathsf{Com}
satisfying collision resistance (
Section

3.3
), whose output size is a fixed
\poly(\lambda)
.

We describe the protocol in the settings where the verifier is entirely classical, except that in the first round it sends a
claw state
to the prover. A claw state is a state of the form

\frac{\ket{0,z_0}+\ket{1,z_1}}{\sqrt{2}}

for randomly sampled
z_{0},z_{1}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\poly(\lambda)}
. Looking ahead, in
Section

4
we shall see how to make the verifier entirely classical, but to keep things simple, here we present the protocol with explicit claw states sent by the verifier.It is a straightforward consequence of the
distinguishing implies swapping
principle
[
AAS
]
(see also
[
HMT23
,
MW24
]
) that the following states are negligibly close in trace distance

\frac{\ket{0,z_0}+\ket{1,z_1}}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{\bra{0,z_0}+\bra{1,z_1}}{\sqrt{2}}\approx_{\negl(\lambda)}\frac{\outerproduct{0,z_0}{0,z_0}+\outerproduct{1,z_1}{1,z_1}}{2}

(1)

that is, a claw state is close to the classical mixture over each claw. Naturally,
Eq.

1
implies that it is hard for any (possibly unbounded) algorithm to return both
(z_{0},z_{1})
given a claw state, with non-negligible probability.

The protocol is parametrized by a function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}^{m}
and a commitment scheme
\mathsf{Com}
. In addition to the public description of
f
and a parameter
\varepsilon
, the prover is given a string
y\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
and the verifier is given
\mathsf{Com}(y)=c
.

We describe the following protocol between the prover and the verifier.
