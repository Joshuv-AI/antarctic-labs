Upper brachial plexus lesions in adults: long term results of direct infraspinatus muscle reinnervation with a radial nerve branch transfer
Authors: Mario Gilberto Siqueira, Roberto Sergio Martins, Klever Forte de Oliveira, Bruno Spindola Amaral Garcia de Freitas, Carlos Otto Heise
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough shoulder external rotation is crucial for upper limb functionality, attempts to restore it are not well represented in traumatic brachial plexus palsy literature.MethodsA series of seven patients underwent a modified technique for direct reinnervation of the infraspinatus muscle using a branch of the radial nerve. They were evaluated after long-term follow-up.ResultsAll seven patients recovered some degree of shoulder external rotation, and in five cases (71.42%) the recovery was equal to or greater than 45°, which was considered a good result. The median follow-up was 4.7 years (range 3-6 years).ConclusionsThe transfer of a branch of the radial nerve supplying the triceps muscle to the branch of the suprascapular nerve supplying the infraspinatus muscle is effective and may increase the possibility of functional recovery of shoulder external rotation. This technique should be included in the surgical armamentarium for nerve surgeons.Keewords: Shoulder external rotation, Brachial plexus injury, Nerve surgery technique

Introduction

Most publications on traumatic brachial plexus palsy in adults focus on restoring shoulder abduction and elbow flexion. However, the functionality of the upper limb is severely compromised without shoulder external rotation, as the forearm remains close to the abdomen, preventing the performance of most activities of daily living (ADLs). Ideally, the hand should be positioned more anteriorly, away from the body, as much as possible (Elhassan et al., 2010). Despite the recognized importance of shoulder external rotation recovery, it is underrepresented in the traumatic brachial plexus palsy. After elbow flexion is restored, shoulder external rotation should be a priority (Webber et al., 2019).

Kuhlman and colleagues (Kuhlman et al., 1992) reported that the infraspinatus muscle is the most important glenohumeral external rotator, being responsible for 75% of this movement. The commonly used technique for reinnervation of the infraspinatus muscle is transferring the distal spinal accessory nerve to the suprascapular nerve. While this technique generally provides good reinnervation of the supraspinatus, satisfactory infraspinatus reinnervation is less frequent (Alnot, 1999; Malessy et al., 2004; Merrell et al., 2001; Siqueira et al., 2019).

In this paper, we present the outcomes of external rotation using a recently proposed technique.

Materials and methods

The study has been carried out in accordance with the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki) for experiments involving humans and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of São Paulo Medical School. We selected the medical records of 11 consecutive patients with C5/C6 lesions and attempted to contact them for a follow-up evaluation. One patient had died (cancer), and three were lost to follow-up. Consequently, seven patients underwent final evaluation. Informed consent was obtained from all patients.

To assess external shoulder rotation, patients stood with the shoulder fully internally rotated. The forearm, flexed at 90° either actively or passively, was placed transversally over the abdomen. Patients were then instructed to move the forearm laterally, away from the abdomen in an arc-like motion, while keeping the arm at the side of the body (Fig. 1). The range of external rotation of the shoulder was measured with a goniometer.

Patients presenting a complete C5/C6 lesion, aged over 18 years, with less than one year between injury and surgery, no history of upper extremity pathology, and no shoulder range-of-motion restriction were included in the study.

The surgical technique consisted of the coaptation of a branch of the radial nerve (commonly targeting the medial head of the triceps) to the infraspinatus branch of the suprascapular nerve. With the patient in the prone position, a large incision was made, extendind laterally from the medial limit of the scapula, parallel to the superior border of the scapula, until close to the acromion. From this point, the incision curved inferiorly,and acquired a distal straight course of approximately 12 cm, along the posterior border of the deltoid muscle and lateral border of the long head of the tríceps muscle, and then between the superficial heads of the triceps muscle (long and lateral) (Fig. 2). The infraspinatus branch was identified after opening the superior transverse scapular ligament (Fig. 3), separated from the supraspinatus branch and dissected in the supraspinatus fossa as far as the spinoglenoid notch. Next, the infraspinatus muscle was partially detached from the scapula in the infraspinous fossa to locate the infraspinatus branch of the suprascapular nerve. It was dissected proximally to the spinoglenoid notch, connecting dissections in the supraspinous and infraspinous fossae (Fig. 4), and distally until the infraspinatus-teres minor interval. After confirming the infraspinatus muscle had no contractility upon electrical stimulation, its branch was severed just distal to the bifurcation from the suprascapular nerve and turned inferiorly to the infraspinous fossa. It was then connected to a branch of the radial nerve (typically to the medial head of the triceps) (Fig. 5). The coaptation used two 10-0 Ethilon sutures (Ethicon, Inc., Somerville, NJ, USA) reinforced with Tissucol fibrin sealant (Baxter Healthcare Corporation, Deerfield, IL, USA). Postoperatively, the limb was immobilized in a sling for three weeks. Rehabilitation then commenced to prevent internal rotation contracture and maintain good passive external rotation. Once the infraspinatus began to contract, the focus shifted to strengthening. The surgical protocol also included three other nerve transfers: a fascicle of the ulnar nerve to the biceps branch of the musculocutaneous nerve, the medial pectoral nerve to the axillary nerve, and the spinal accessory nerve to the suprascapular branch for the supraspinatus muscle. Shoulder external rotation of ≥45° was considered a good functional outcome. An independent assessor measured the outcome, reducing potential observer bias.

Results

All but one patient were male, and the average age was 26 years. The mean interval between injury and surgery was 6.5 months, with all patients operated on within a year post-injury.

In six patients, the branch of the triceps muscle to the medial head was used as donor and in one patient, it was the branch to the lateral head.

All seven patients achieved some degree of external rotation, with five (71.42%) reaching ≥45°, which was considered a good outcome. The external rotation angle obtained by the nerve transfer varied from 20° to 90° from the abdominal wall (Table 1). The mean shoulder external rotation achieved was 62°, with a 95% confidence interval from 37 to 86°. Recovery typically began more than four months after surgery. Surprisingly, in one patient, recovery only began in the fourteenth month post-surgery. The median follow-up was 4.7 years (range 3-6 years).

Discussion

The goal of reconstructive surgery for brachial plexus injuries is to produce a limb with sufficient independent function to assist the contralateral upper limb in two-handed tasks.

Shoulder stability and mobility are necessary for positioning the hand away from the body, maximizing usefulness in activities of daily living. The most common technique used for the reinnervation of the shoulder is the transfer of the spinal accessory nerve to the suprascapular nerve with highly variable results. Data from the literature shows that in average a good outcome for stabilization and abduction is achieved with this technique in 66.4% of cases, but only 37.4% of patients presented a good external rotation of the shoulder (Malessy et al., 2004; Merrell et al., 2001; Siqueira et al., 2019; Allieu and Cenac, 1988; Baltzer et al., 2017; Bertelli and Ghizoni, 2016; Bathia et al., 2011; Bhandari et al., 2009; Birch, 2011; Emamhadi et al., 2016; Estrella, 2011; Kostas-et al., 2013; Leechavengvongs et al., 2003; Narakas and Hentz, 1988; Socolovsky et al., 2014; Souza et al., 2014; Sulaiman et al., 2009; Suzuki et al., 2007; Terzis and Kostas, 2006; Venkatramani et al., 2008; Vekris et al., 2010). The cause for the poor results in the reinnervation of the infraspinatus muscle is still a matter of discussion. It may be related to a second distal injury of the suprascapular nerve after it branches out to the supraspinatus nerve (i.e. two-level lesions) (Terzis and Kostas, 2006; Vekris et al., 2010) or may be consequent to more intense reinnervation of the first muscle that is reinnervated (i.e. the supraspinatus) (Bertelli and Ghizoni, 2016; Vekris et al., 2010). Another technique used to restore external rotation of the shoulder is the combination of spinal accessory nerve transfer to the suprascapular nerve with the triceps branch transfer to the axillary nerve. The degree of recovery of external rotation in the literature with this combination of techniques varies from 58 to 93° (Kostas-et al., 2013; Song et al., 2020; Jerome, 2012; Bertelli and Ghizoni, 2004). Comparing our results with data from the literature is difficult because there are several ways to clinically evaluate external rotation of the shoulder, with specific results for each type of technique. Furthermore, in most studies, the authors do not provide how external shoulder rotation was clinically evaluated.

In June 2013, the possibility of a nerve transfer directed to the infraspinatus muscle was presented for the first time by Mishra and colleagues (Mishra et al., 2013) from Birmingham, UK during the Conference of The Federation of European Societies for Surgery of the Hand in Antalya, Turkey. The authors dissected eight cadavers to study the feasibility of transfer a motor branch from the radial nerve to the axillary nerve and to the infraspinatus muscle. The suprascapular nerve was transected after passing the spinoglenoid notch and mobilized to the region between the scapular spine and the infraspinatus muscle, The average length of the motor branch to the infraspinatus muscle following the transection was 4 cm (with a range of 4 to 6 cm).

Wyles and colleagues (Wyles et al., 2019), in 2019, proposed a modification of the original technique based on an anatomical study in 7 specimens. The branch of the suprascapular nerve to infraspinatus was transected immediately distal to the motor branch to supraspinatus near the superior transverse scapular ligament and delivered through the spinoglenoid notch and deep to the infraspinatus for emergence in the infraspinatus-teres minor interval. With this route the length of the branch to infraspinatus was increased (average 59 mm - range 46 to 80 mm). In all 14 dissected shoulders the proposed nerve transfer was feasible.

In 2020, Tavares and colleagues (Tavares et al., 2020) presented a technique similar to that proposed by Mishra et al. (2013) using the more proximal transection of the infraspinatus branch, as proposed by Wyles and colleagues (Wyles et al., 2019). The radial nerve branch to the medial head of the triceps was directly transferred to the infraspinatus branch in 29 adult cadavers. In 1 specimen there was no sufficient length of any of the branches to the heads of the triceps muscle to carry out the transfer.

The possibility of neurotization of the infraspinatus branch with the spinal accessory nerve as donor was studied by Kaiser et al. (2021) in 25 adult cadavers and the results were published in 2021. The spinal accessory nerve was routed through a bone canal opened in a weakened area under the spine of the scapula to shorten distances. The technique was feasible in all specimens.

Paulos (2021) in 2021, proposed a new technique of neurotization from the inferior subscapular nerve to the motor branch of teres minor to recover the external rotation of the shoulder and at the same time providing some decrease in the internal rotation force. She could perform the technique in all 20 cadavers dissected.

In 2023, Krajcová and colleagues (Krajcov et al., 2023) studied the possibility of a transfer from the inferior subscapular nerve to the branch to the infraspinatus muscle dissecting 18 cadavers. The technique consisted in the transection of the infraspinatus branch at the spinoglenoid notch and mobilization of the branch to the space between teres major, teres minor and the long head of the triceps, where the inferior subscapular nerve was found. The transfer was feasible in 17 specimens (94.4%).

Finally, in 2024, Veronesi (2024) dissected 20 adult cadavers and demonstrated that it is anatomically feasible to transfer radial nerve branches destined to the triceps (especially for the medial head) to the motor branches of the infraspinatus and teres minor muscles. The motor branch to the infraspinatus was transected immediately after the origin of the motor branch to the supraspinatus from the suprascapular nerve and mobilized through the spinoglenoid notch and then under the infraspinatus until the interval between the infraspinatus and teres minor. The proposed nerve transfers were possible in all specimens.

Probably the first clinical attempt of a nerve transfer to the infraspinatus branch was made in 2013 by Dominic Power (Peripheral Nerve Injury Unit, Queen Elizabeth Hospital, Birmingham, UK). Using radial nerve branches to the triceps as donors he achieved good external rotation of the shoulder (4 MRC) in the two cases operated. These results were not published (personal communication -February 2024).

In 2015, Sommarhem and colleagues (Sommarhem et al., 2015) from the Helsinki University Hospital reported 8 cases of brachial plexus birth injury treated using the spinal accessory nerve to reinnervate the infraspinatus. All the patients were 1.5 years of age or older. At 1-year follow-up, active external rotation of the shoulder improved in all patients, with a mean improvement of 47° (range, 20 to 85°).

Five years later (2020) Grahn and colleagues (Grahn et al., 2020) from the same institution published a paper with the mid-term outcome of the same procedure in 14 patients with birth injuries. Shoulder external rotation was assessed after a mean follow-up of 3.8 years and improvement was observed in 12 children, with a mean of 57°.

Abou-Al-Shaar and colleagues (Abou-Al- and Shaar, 2020) reported, in 2020, the case of a 48 year-old patient with a bilateral lesion of the suprascapular nerve. A selective nerve transfer of the distal part of the spinal accessory nerve to the motor branch of the infraspinatus was performed on both sides. A good recovery of the shoulder external rotation was observed 16 months after surgery.

Also in 2020, Soldado and colleagues (Soldado, 2020) published the results of a double nerve transfer to restore active external rotation of the glenohumeral joint in 20 children with neonatal brachial plexus injury with more than 18 months of age. The spinal accessory nerve was transferred to the infraspinatus motor branch through a posterior approach and the long head of the triceps motor branch to the teres minor motor branch through an axillary approach. All children, except two, recovered shoulder external rotation 4-6 months after surgery (70 ± 32.4°).

In 2015, Gates and colleagues (Gates et al., 2016) using an electromagnetic three-dimensional tracking system quantified the motion requirements necessary to perform eight key activities of daily living in fifteen healthy young adults. It was determined that a minimum of 55 degrees of external shoulder rotation is required to carry out the specified upper-limb activities of daily living.

Applying this standard, Tavares et al. (2020) achieved a good result in only 20,2% of their patients submitted to a direct reinnervation of the infraspinatus. However, after that publication we discussed the external shoulder rotation movement with a group of rehabilitators (physiatrists, physiotherapists and occupational therapists) and concluded that even small degrees of external rotation could decrease patient disability and increase the ability to perform ADLs, agreeing with Langer and colleagues (Langer et al., 2012). This group observed normal individuals performing 12 common ADLs wearing a custom shoulder orthosis designed to selectively limit shoulder external rotation in different settings and concluded that restoration of even small amounts of external rotation in brachial plexus palsy patients can improve patient's function and reduce their disability in most ADLs. Based in this findings and in our observations we considered, in the current series, 45 degrees of shoulder external rotation as the minimum required to perform many, though not all, daily activities (Fig. 1). Using this new standard we observed that 71.42% of our patients achieved a good result on infraspinatus muscle reinnervation. Although any branch to the triceps muscle could be used in the proposed nerve transfer, we always tried to use the radial branch to the medial head of triceps muscle as the donor because it usually has a long length and has the largest number of fibers (1030.8 ± 258.5) for reinnervation (Tavares et al., 2020). We succeeded in using the branch to the medial head of triceps in all cases, except one.

To evaluate the efficacy of the proposed technique in the reinnervation of the infraspinatus muscle we compared its result with our series of spinal accessory to supraspinal nerve transfer (Siqueira et al., 2019) and the external shoulder rotation achieved with multiple nerve transfers (unpublished personal data). The amount of good results with the new technique (71.42%) was bigger than the result with multiple nerve transfer (57.1%) and even bigger than the good results with the spinal accessory to the supraspinal nerve transfer (21.7%).

The lack of infraspinatus function has been also treated with teres major, latissimus dorsi or lower trapezius tendon transfer (Borschel and Clarke, 2009; Elhassan, 2014). The results are usually good, but the benefit from tendon transfers may decrease in long-term follow-ups (Werthel et al., 2018).

The proposed strategy's effectiveness in restoring shoulder external rotation also depends on the glenohumeral joint's stability. If this joint is unstable, the neurotization will provide limited effect as the joint itself is unable to make the movement.

Although direct reinnervation of the infraspinatus muscle by nerve transfer has been described in some anatomical studies, clinical cases have only been published by our group, in previous work (Tavares et al., 2020) and in the current study.

The present study had some limitations in addition to its retrospective design: the lack of control group and the small sample size. Given the complexity and novelty of the procedure, this relatively small sample size is consistent with other published series in the field. Future studies with larger cohorts and randomized controlled trials would further strengthen the evidence base for this intervention. Nonetheless, despite these limitations, we believe that this surgical series provides important information for the nerve surgeon.

Conclusions

The transfer of a branch of the radial nerve going to the triceps muscle, to the branch of the suprascapular nerve going to the infraspinatus muscle seems to be effective and may increase the possibility of functional recovery of external rotation of the shoulder.
