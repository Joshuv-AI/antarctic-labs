Replacement of soybean meal with water-soluble corn protein improves early growth performance of broilers via regulating intestinal microbiota and metabolites
Authors: Changzheng Guo, Shiyong Liao, Shourong Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the effects of 5% dietary replacement of soybean meal (SBM) with water-soluble corn protein (WCP) or cottonseed protein (CotP) on growth performance and intestinal development of starter broilers, with the underlying microbial and metabolic mechanisms of WCP further explored. A total of 504 one-day-old male Arbor Acres broilers were randomly assigned to three isonitrogenous and isocaloric dietary treatments: a basal diet control group (CON), a WCP group (5% of SBM replaced by WCP), and a CotP group (5% of SBM replaced by CotP). The results showed that WCP significantly increased the 14-day body weight, average daily gain and average daily feed intake of broilers, improved duodenal villus morphology, and lowered duodenal digesta pH (P < 0.05) without damaging intestinal barrier function. In contrast, CotP inhibited early growth and reduced duodenal goblet cell density (P < 0.05). Further cecal microbial and metabolomic analyses were performed between the CON and WCP groups. Although WCP did not affect the alpha diversity of cecal microbiota, it substantially altered microbial community structure, increased the relative abundances of beneficial Clostridium, Romboutsia and Anaerofilum, and decreased Anaerobutyricum (P < 0.05). Metabolomic profiling revealed significant differences in cecal metabolites, and protein digestion and absorption was identified as the key enriched pathway. WCP supplementation upregulated isovaleric acid accumulation and decreased the level of harmful indole metabolites. Spearman correlation analysis showed that Clostridium and Romboutsia were positively correlated with body weight, while Anaerofilum was positively correlated with average daily feed intake. Moreover, isovaleric acid was positively associated with Clostridium and Anaerofilum, and exhibited highly significant positive correlations with duodenal villus height, body weight and average daily gain. Collectively, WCP specifically promotes early broiler growth by modulating cecal microbial structure and improving protein fermentation metabolism, suggesting its potential as a high-quality alternative protein source for starter broiler diets.Keywords: Broilers, Soybean meal replacement, Water-soluble corn protein, Microbiota, Metabolites

Introduction

Feed costs account for 70% of total livestock and poultry production costs (Mallick et al., 2020). Global animal production relies heavily on unsustainable plant-based protein sources (FAO, 2018), among which soybean meal is the most widely used and one of the most expensive feed ingredients (Čumplík et al., 2026). Geographic imbalances in global soybean supply and demand force long-distance intercontinental trade, increasing soybean meal costs and supply chain risks, which are exacerbated by volatile global trade and energy prices (Taghizadeh-Hesary et al., 2019). Therefore, developing alternative ingredients to partially replace soybean meal can both address the sustainability challenges of protein feed supply and strengthen regional food security (FAO, 2018; Yu et al., 2025; Liang et al., 2026a).

While cottonseed protein (CotP) has emerged as the most widely adopted commercial soybean meal substitute in poultry production, its inconsistent performance and free gossypol-related health risks (e.g., iron deficiency and reduced blood haemoglobin) have driven the search for superior alternative protein sources (Świątkiewicz et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2021). Building on the abundant corn wet-milling by-product corn steep liquor (annual production is ∼4.3–4.8 million dry tons) (Guo et al., 2026; Vilpoux and Junior, 2023; Zhang et al., 2021), a novel water-soluble corn protein (WCP) was developed through a proprietary integrated purification and modification process (Guo et al., 2026). This technology has achieved industrial-scale production with official feed safety certification in China, a unit protein cost equivalent to commercial soybean meal, and demonstrated stable application in multiple livestock and aquaculture species.

In our previous study using a unified experimental design, we evaluated the effects of 5% soybean meal replacement with WCP or commercially detoxified CotP on market-age (40-day) broilers. The results confirmed that WCP maintained growth performance comparable to the soybean meal control, whereas CotP significantly reduced final body weight (BW) and feed efficiency (Guo et al., 2026). The underlying mechanisms for this CotP-induced growth retardation remain unclear, but emerging evidence indicates that broilers are most susceptible to gossypol toxicity during the early post-hatch period, and subclinical organ and metabolic damage occurring at this stage can persistently impair growth potential throughout the entire production cycle (Chen et al., 2023). The early growth phase (1–14 days) represents a critical developmental window for broilers, during which intestinal tract maturation, immune system establishment, and gut microbiota colonization occur (Zhang et al., 2022). These early physiological changes exert long-term deterministic effects on growth performance, disease resistance, and production efficiency throughout the entire rearing cycle (Zhang et al., 2022). To date, the impacts of WCP and CotP on broiler early development remain uncharacterized, which hinders the optimization of their application strategies across different growth stages.

Therefore, the present study, as a systematic continuation of our previous work using the same batch of Arbor Acres (AA) broilers, aimed to investigate the effects of partial soybean meal replacement with WCP or CotP on growth performance and intestinal development of starter broilers, with the underlying microbial and metabolic mechanisms of WCP further explored. The findings will fill the knowledge gap regarding the early-stage efficacy of these protein sources and provide more comprehensive scientific support for their rational and precise application in broiler production.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

This study was approved by the institutional animal care and use committee of the Jiangsu Institute of Poultry Science (Permit Number: CNP20220323).

Animals, experimental design, and management

A total of 504 one-day-old healthy male AA broilers were enrolled and randomly divided into 3 dietary treatments, with 7 replicates per treatment and 24 broilers per replicate. Birds in the control group (CON group) were fed a corn-soybean meal basal diet, while those in the experimental groups were fed diets in which 5% of soybean meal was replaced with CotP (CotP group) or WCP (WCP group), respectively. All diets were formulated to be iso-nitrogenous and iso-energetic, with consistent nutrient specifications across all treatment groups. The WCP used in this study was a commercial product provided by Zhucheng Haotian Pharm Co., Ltd. Its key nutritional indicators were as follows: crude protein 54.57%, acid-soluble protein 49.1%, and total amino acids 44.2%. The entire experiment lasted for 40 days, and starter feed (crumbles for days 1–7 and complete pellets for days 8–21) and grower pelleted diets (days 22–40) in accordance with the guidelines specified in Nutrient Requirements of Poultry (NRC, 1994). The composition and nutritional levels of the diet are presented in Table 1. Crude protein contents of experimental diets were determined following GB/T 6432-2018 (General Administration of Quality Supervision, Inspection and Quarantine of the People’s Republic of China, 2018). All broilers were reared in a floor-based housing system and had ad libitum access to feed and water. During the initial rearing phase, the ambient temperature inside the house was maintained at approximately 34°C. From day 4 onwards, the temperature was reduced by 1°C every 48 h until a stable temperature of around 23°C was achieved. A staged lighting program with graded photoperiod, dark interval and light intensity was implemented throughout the trial. Chicks were exposed to continuous 24 h illumination at 100 lx with no dark period on day 1. From days 3 to 7, a 23 h light / 1 h dark cycle (00:00–01:00) at 100 lx was used. Days 10 to 21 received 18–20 h light and a 4 h dark period (00:00–04:00), with light intensity reduced to 10 lx at day 10 and further lowered to 7 lx at day 15. For days 28 to 35, the 23 h light / 1 h midnight dark cycle was restored, and light intensity was adjusted to 5 lx.

Growth performance

Feed consumption and mortality of broilers were recorded at 8:00 a.m. every morning. The number of dead birds in each treatment group was counted, and the mortality rate of each group was calculated accordingly. The BW of broilers in each replicate was collectively weighed on day 1, day 14 and day 40, and the total feed consumption of each replicate during the periods of day 1 to 14 and day 15 to 40 was determined. Based on these data, the average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed-to-gain ratio (F/G) of broilers in each treatment group were calculated. For F/G calculation, feed consumption of dead birds in each replicate was estimated according to average individual feed intake and deducted from total feed supply. The final F/G was the corrected feed intake of surviving broilers divided by the total weight gain of surviving birds.

Relative intestinal weight

On day 14 of the feeding trial, one broiler with a BW close to the average BW of the corresponding replicate was selected from each replicate. The entire small intestine was dissected immediately after slaughter, with pancreatic tissue and adipose tissue adherent to the intestinal wall removed. Intestinal contents were then gently scraped off using a glass slide, and the segments were weighed. The relative weight of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum was calculated separately using the following formula: Relative weight of intestinal segment (g/kg) = Weight of intestinal segment (g) / Live BW of bird (kg). All measurements were performed blind to dietary treatments after random re-coding of birds.

Gastrointestinal tract digesta pH

The target segments of the digestive tract (proventriculus, gizzard, duodenum, jejunum, and ileum) were immediately isolated individually. Each segment was incised longitudinally along the intestinal wall, and the digesta inside the segment was gently scraped into a centrifuge tube using a glass slide. The pH value of the digesta from the proventriculus, gizzard, duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were measured respectively using a precision pH meter (PH-STAR, Matthaus, Germany).

Intestinal Histomorphology and Goblet Cell Quantification

After collection, duodenal, jejunal, and ileal tissue samples were immediately fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin solution, followed by dehydration using an ascending graded ethanol series. The tissues were then cleared with xylene and subsequently embedded in paraffin wax. The paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were sectioned into 5 μm-thick serial slices using a microtome, and the sections were mounted onto glass slides. Afterwards, the sections were subjected to deparaffinization and rehydration through a descending graded ethanol series. Subsequently, the sections were stained separately with two methods: hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining. For HE-stained sections, the villus height and crypt depth of each intestinal segment were measured, and the villus height to crypt depth ratio (V/C ratio) was calculated. For PAS-stained sections, the number of goblet cells per intact villus was quantitatively analyzed. To ensure the representativeness and accuracy of the data, 10 intact, well-oriented villi with their corresponding crypts were randomly selected from each section for measurement. The V/C ratio was defined as follows: V/C ratio = Villus height (μm)/Crypt depth (μm).

DNA extraction and 16 s rRNA analysis

On day 14, one broiler with a BW close to the average BW of the corresponding replicate was selected from each replicate of the CON group and WCP group. Following humane euthanasia, the intestinal tract was isolated, and approximately 1 g of cecal digesta was collected under aseptic conditions, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored for subsequent 16S rRNA gene sequencing of cecal microbiota as described previously (Zhang et al., 2022). Briefly, total microbial genomic DNA was extracted from cecal digesta samples using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, the V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene, which have high amplification specificity and comprehensive taxonomic annotation in reference databases, were amplified using barcode-specific primer pairs (forward primer 341F: CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG; reverse primer 806R: GGACTACHVGGGTATCTAAT). The purified amplicons were used to construct sequencing libraries with the TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Finally, paired-end sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

After sequencing, raw reads were quality-filtered to remove low-quality sequences. Paired-end reads were merged into tags using FLASH, and further quality-filtered. Clustering was performed with Usearch (v10.0) to define operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at a 97% similarity threshold, followed by chimera removal to obtain representative OTU sequences and abundance profiles. Multi-dimensional bioinformatic analyses were conducted: taxonomic annotation against the Silva database (v138), microbial community composition analysis; alpha diversity assessment using Chao1 and Shannon indices, beta diversity analysis via principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on unweighted UniFrac distances, identification of differentially abundant taxa using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe), functional prediction using PICRUSt2, and spearman correlation analysis between microbiota and environmental factors for visualization. All statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.0.3).

Metabolomics analysis

On day 14, 1 g of cecal digesta sample was collected. The sample was immediately transferred into a pre-cooled 2 mL cryopreservation tube, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then stored in an ultra-low temperature freezer at −80°C for subsequent non-targeted metabolomics analysis.

Approximately 50 mg of cecal digesta was homogenized with 500 μL of pre-chilled 80% (v/v) methanol aqueous solution. After vortexing for 30 s and incubating on ice for 10 min, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected and diluted with LC-MS grade water to a final methanol concentration of 53% (v/v). Following filtration through a 0.22 μm organic membrane, samples were analyzed using a Vanquish UHPLC system coupled to a Q Exactive™ HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). Raw LC-MS/MS data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.1 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for feature detection, retention time alignment, and signal filtering. Mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), retention time (RT), and peak area were extracted for all features detected in both positive and negative ion modes. Peak area data were normalized using the median centering method to minimize systematic variation.

Multivariate statistical analysis was performed using SIMCA 14.1 software (Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden). Principal component analysis (PCA) was first conducted to visualize overall metabolic variation among samples. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was then applied to maximize the separation between the FR and CON groups and identify differential metabolites. Significantly differential metabolites were defined by meeting all three criteria: variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1.0 from the OPLS-DA model, false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05, and fold change (FC) > 1.5 or < 0.67 (WCP/CON). Volcano plots were generated to visualize the distribution of differential metabolites. Finally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed to identify biologically relevant pathways associated with the differential metabolites.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were initially collated using Microsoft Excel 2019. For phenotypic data (growth performance, gastrointestinal tract digesta pH, intestinal morphology, and goblet cell quantification), outliers were removed using the 95% confidence interval method in SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Then, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to compare differences among the three treatment groups, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons of means. Results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical methods for 16S rRNA gene sequencing and non-targeted metabolomics are described in detail in their respective methodological sections.

Results

Growth performance

The growth performance of 40-day-old broilers has been reported in our previous study (Guo et al., 2026). Briefly, 5% WCP replacement for soybean meal maintained growth performance comparable to the CON group with no significant differences in mortality rate among treatments, while the same level of CotP significantly reduced final BW, ADG, ADFI and European Production Index (EPI) (P < 0.001; Table S1). This study focused on the early growth responses (1–14 d) of broilers to the same dietary treatments. The growth performance of broilers at 14 d is shown in Table 2. Compared with CON, WCP significantly increased 14 d BW, 1–14 d ADG and ADFI (P < 0.001), while CotP significantly decreased these indices (P < 0.001). The F/G in WCP was comparable to CON, but significantly higher in CotP (P = 0.001).

Cecal microbiota analysis

The rarefaction curves reached a plateau with increasing sequencing reads, confirming that the sequencing depth was sufficient to adequately capture the diversity of the cecal microbial communities (Fig. S1). Venn diagram analysis showed that the CON group contained a total of 4124 OTUs, including 3464 unique OTUs, while the WCP group contained 4864 OTUs, including 4207 unique OTUs. A total of 657 OTUs were shared between the two groups (Fig. 5A). No significant differences in alpha diversity indices (Observed species, Shannon, Simpson, and Chao1) were detected between the CON and WCP groups (Fig. S2, P > 0.05). However, PCoA revealed a significant separation in the overall structure of the cecal microbiota between the two groups (P = 0.032; Fig. 5B and 5C).

Effects of dietary WCP on cecal microbiota of 14-day-old broilers. A, venn diagram of shared/unique OTUs in CON and WCP groups. B, β-diversity Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) plot. C, adonis analysis. D, differential bacterial families. E, differential bacterial genera; F: LEfSe analysis. CON, control group; WCP, water-soluble corn protein group.

At the family level, the dominant cecal bacteria (relative abundance > 2%) in both the CON and WCP groups were Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Incertae_Sedis, Oscillospiraceae and Erysipelatoclostridiaceae (Fig. S3). The relative abundances of these families were as follows: Ruminococcaceae (30.83% in CON vs. 24.87% in WCP), Lachnospiraceae (15.75% vs. 17.63%), Incertae_Sedis (14.30% vs. 16.71%), Oscillospiraceae (9.14% vs. 12.26%), and Erysipelatoclostridiaceae (2.66% vs. 2.22%). The WCP group had significantly lower abundance of Ruminococcaceae (P = 0.011) and higher abundances of Peptostreptococcaceae (P = 0.040) and Clostridiaceae (P < 0.001) compared with the CON group (Fig. 5D).

At the genus level, the dominant cecal bacteria (relative abundance > 2%) in both the CON and WCP groups were Incertae_Sedis, Faecalibacterium, UCG-005, Mediterraneibacter, Negativibacillus, and Thomasclavelia (Fig. S4). Specifically, the relative abundances of these genera were as follows: Incertae_Sedis (17.57% in the CON group vs. 19.43% in the WCP group), Faecalibacterium (20.36% vs. 8.25%), UCG-005 (2.85% vs. 5.63%), Mediterraneibacter (3.01% vs. 4.82%), Negativibacillus (4.00% vs. 2.40%), and Thomasclavelia (2.04% vs. 1.89%). The WCP group showed significantly increased abundances of Clostridium (P = 0.002), Romboutsia (P = 0.026), and Anaerofilum (P = 0.018), and a significantly decreased abundance of Anaerobutyricum (P = 0.004) relative to the CON group (Fig. 5E).

LEfSe analysis (LDA score > 3.0) further identified key discriminatory taxa (Fig. 5F). The CON group was enriched in taxa such as Clostridia, Anaerobutyridia, Oscillospirales, Ruminococcacea, and Incertae_Sedis, while the WCP group was characterized by higher levels of Actinomycetota, Peptostreptococcales_Tissierellales, Clostridiales, Clostridiaceae, Peptostreptococcaceae, Planococcaceae, Kurthia, Romboutsia, Anaerofilum, CHKCI002, Frisingicoccus, Clostridium, and Ruminococcus.

Cecal metabolomic profiling

The overall changes in cecal metabolites were first analyzed using PCA (Fig. 6A). The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained 28.8% and 14.0% of the total variance, respectively. Samples from the CON and WCP groups formed distinct clusters with no overlap in their 95% confidence ellipses, indicating a clear separation in metabolite profiles between the two treatments. To further identify the differential metabolites, OPLS-DA was performed (Fig. 6B). The score plot showed a marked separation between the CON and WCP groups, with each group clustering within its respective confidence ellipse, confirming significant differences in their cecal metabolite composition (Fig. 6B). The permutation test validated the reliability of the OPLS-DA model, with R² = 0.95 and Q² = −0.04 (Fig. 6C). The low Q² value indicated that the model was not overfitted, demonstrating that the observed group separation was statistically robust.

C, OPLS-DA model validation. D, volcano plot of differential metabolites. E, classification of differential metabolites. F, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment of differential metabolites. G, protein digestion and absorption metabolic pathway. A total of 1627 metabolites were identified in the cecal contents of broilers in the CON and WCP groups. Based on the criteria of FDR < 0.05, FC > 1.5 or < 0.67 (WCP/CON), and VIP > 1, 190 significantly differential metabolites were screened between the two groups. Compared with the CON group, 28 metabolites were significantly upregulated and 162 were significantly downregulated in the WCP group, as visualized in the volcano plot (Fig. 6D). Functional classification revealed that these differential metabolites were mainly involved in multiple metabolic pathways, including carbohydrate metabolism (13 metabolites), lipid metabolism (43 metabolites), protein and amino acid metabolism (81 metabolites), and other metabolic processes (53 metabolites) (Fig. 6E, Table S2).

To explore the functional implications of the differential metabolites, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed. The top 20 enriched pathways are shown in the bubble plot (Fig. 6F), including pyrimidine metabolism, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, prolactin signaling pathway, glycerolipid metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, biosynthesis of plant secondary metabolites, selenocompound metabolism, longevity regulating pathway-worm, metabolic pathways, phosphonate and phosphinate metabolism, caffeine metabolism, mineral absorption, microbial metabolism in diverse environments, biosynthesis of amino acids, biosynthesis of phenylpropanoids, ubiquinone and other terpenoid-quinone biosynthesis, and pentose and glucuronate interconversions. Notably, consistent with both the majority of differential metabolites being annotated to protein and amino acid metabolism and the fact that the dietary treatment involved the substitution of soybean meal as a protein feed ingredient, the significantly enriched protein digestion and absorption pathway emerged as a functionally relevant and biologically meaningful response to the dietary intervention, warranting further investigation.

Focusing on the significantly enriched protein digestion and absorption pathway, we further analyzed the key differential metabolites involved. As shown in Fig. 6G, compared with the CON group, the WCP group exhibited significant changes in multiple metabolites related to protein and amino acid metabolism: the abundance of isovaleric acid was significantly increased (P < 0.05), while propanoic acid, l-alanine, indole, glycine, and DL-threonine were significantly decreased (P < 0.05). These results provide direct metabolic evidence that the dietary substitution of soybean meal altered the end products of protein metabolism in the cecum, with marked changes in both branched-chain fatty acids and amino acid-derived metabolites, indicating a clear shift in the patterns of microbial proteolysis and amino acid fermentation.

Spearman correlation analysis

Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed to explore the relationships between the differential metabolite isovaleric acid, differential cecal microbiota at the genus level, growth performance, and intestinal development indicators. As shown in Fig. 7, Anaerofilum was positively correlated with ADFI (P < 0.05). Clostridium showed significant positive correlations with BW, ADG and relative jejunal weight (P < 0.05). Romboutsia was positively associated with BW, ADG, duodenal villus height and relative jejunal weight (P < 0.05). By contrast, Anaerobutyricum had significant negative correlations with BW, ADG and ADFI (P < 0.05). Isovaleric acid was significantly and positively correlated with Anaerofilum (P < 0.01) and Clostridium (P < 0.05), but negatively correlated with Anaerobutyricum (P < 0.05). In terms of intestinal development, isovaleric acid showed a highly significant positive correlation with duodenal villus height (P < 0.01) and a significant positive correlation with relative jejunum weight (P < 0.05). Furthermore, isovaleric acid was significantly positively correlated with growth performance parameters including BW (P < 0.01), ADG (P < 0.01), and ADFI (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Our previous work reported that 5% WCP substitution for soybean meal did not alter broiler growth performance at 40 d (Guo et al., 2026). However, early-stage analyses revealed obvious stage-specific effects of WCP. Notably, 14-d BW, and 1–14 d ADG and ADFI were markedly increased in the WCP group (P < 0.001), demonstrating a pronounced growth-promoting effect during the starter phase. In contrast, CotP exhibited consistent inhibitory effects on broiler growth across different stages. Dietary CotP supplementation significantly decreased 14-d BW, 1–14 d ADG and ADFI (P < 0.001) and increased F/G (P = 0.001), which was consistent with its adverse impacts on growth performance at 40 d.

The stage-dependent growth responses of broilers to WCP could be explained by the dynamic physiological development and shifting nutritional requirements of broilers during different growth periods. It is well established that newly hatched chicks have immature intestinal function and limited protease secretion, making their early growth highly dependent on the solubility and absorbability of dietary protein rather than total protein level (Eits et al., 2003). As a high-quality protein material rich in acid-soluble protein, small peptides, and free amino acids (Guo et al., 2026), WCP may be more efficiently utilized by the underdeveloped gut, thereby improving the early growth performance of broilers. Consistent with the present findings, Wang et al. (2019) also found that increased small peptide levels in feed benefited nutrient utilization and growth of broilers. In contrast, CotP exhibited stable adverse effects on broiler growth across both early and late growth stages. The consistently reduced BW, ADG and ADFI, as well as elevated F/G at 14 d and 40 d, suggested that the inferior nutritional properties or potential anti-nutritional factors of CotP impaired nutrient utilization in a stage-independent manner. In summary, broilers in the starter phase are sensitive to dietary protein quality, and easily digestible protein sources can effectively promote early growth.

The relative weight of the jejunum was significantly higher in both the WCP and CotP groups compared with the CON group (P < 0.05). The elevated jejunal weight in the WCP group may be linked to the stimulatory effect of small peptides and highly absorbable nutrients on intestinal development. It has been well documented that small peptides can nourish intestinal epithelial cells and promote the normal development of intestinal tissues in broilers (Wang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2022). Nevertheless, the increased relative jejunal weight in the CotP group may result from reduced BW, which consequently elevated the relative organ index.

Broilers in the WCP group exhibited significantly greater duodenal villus height relative to the CON and CotP groups (P < 0.05). As a key indicator, villus height reflects the maturation degree and nutrient absorption capability of the intestinal mucosa (He et al., 2025; Liang et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2025). Consistent with the present study, Abdollahi et al. (2017) also demonstrated that dietary soybean peptides at 3.0 and 6.0 g/kg increased duodenal villus height in broilers. This improvement further verifies that ingredients rich in peptides exert favourable effects on intestinal morphology (Zhao et al., 2022). Conversely, CotP showed no positive influence on duodenal villus development, which corresponded to its suppressive effects on broiler growth.

Both WCP and CotP significantly decreased duodenal digesta pH compared with the CON group (P < 0.05). The changes in duodenal pH were closely linked to the inherent physicochemical properties of experimental diets (Pearlin et al., 2020). As previously observed, the WCP diet had the lowest feed pH and acid-binding capacity at pH 3 (ABC-3) values among the three groups (Guo et al., 2026). A moderate drop in duodenal pH suppresses pathogenic proliferation and stabilizes intestinal microflora (Cisek and Binek, 2014). Combined with intestinal morphology data, the lower digesta pH and abundant small peptides in WCP collectively enhanced villus development and nutrient utilization, partly accounting for the improved growth performance. While CotP also lowered duodenal pH, this favourable effect was offset by its poor nutritional value and potential anti-nutritional factors, resulting in no beneficial impacts on growth and intestinal development.

Duodenal goblet cell density showed obvious differences among treatments. No significant difference was found between the WCP group and the CON group, while the CotP group had markedly lower duodenal goblet cell counts than the other two groups (P < 0.05). Goblet cells are critical for intestinal health, as they secrete mucus to protect the intestinal epithelium and maintain intestinal barrier function (Gustafsson et al., 2022). The comparable goblet cell density between WCP and the CON indicated that WCP exerted no adverse impacts on duodenal physiological status. In contrast, the decreased goblet cell density in the CotP group implied impaired duodenal barrier function. Consistent with our results, Liu et al. (2020) observed reduced intestinal goblet cell density in fish fed cottonseed meal containing gossypol. Additionally, gossypol has been proven to damage intestinal mucosa and epithelial cells in poultry (Özdoğan et al., 2012). Thus, the decreased goblet cells in the CotP group were likely caused by gossypol and anti-nutritional components, which ultimately inhibited broiler growth.

Dietary composition is the primary factor driving the assembly of gut microbiota, and alterations in dietary nutrients can rapidly reshape microbial community structure (David et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2025). In the present study, no significant differences were found in alpha diversity indices between the two groups, indicating that WCP did not change the overall species richness and uniformity of cecal microbiota in young broilers. However, PCoA based on beta diversity showed clear segregation of microbial profiles between CON and WCP groups, demonstrating that WCP substantially remodelled the community composition of cecal microbiota without altering overall microbial diversity. A well-organized gut microbial community is essential for maintaining intestinal physiological homeostasis (Guo et al., 2018). At the family and genus levels, Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Faecalibacterium and other taxa dominated the cecal microbiota of 14-day-old broilers, which was consistent with the microbial characteristics of young broilers reported previously (Xiao et al., 2021). These predominant microbes are functionally involved in the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), regulation of carbohydrate and protein metabolism, maintenance of intestinal barrier and inhibition of pathogenic bacteria (Eeckhaut et al., 2016; Esquivel-Elizondo et al., 2017).

Further analysis of differential taxa revealed that WCP significantly reduced the relative abundance of Ruminococcaceae, while increasing the abundances of Clostridiaceae and Peptostreptococcaceae. As common taxa in broilers fed corn-soybean meal diets, most members of Clostridiaceae produce abundant SCFAs to supply energy for intestinal epithelial cells, alleviate intestinal inflammation and competitively inhibit the colonization of pathogens (Gong et al., 2002; Lu et al., 2003). Peptostreptococcaceae is capable of degrading dietary proteins and peptides to generate amino acids and SCFAs, playing an important role in host nutrient metabolism (Maki et al., 2020). At the genus level, Clostridium, Romboutsia and Anaerofilum were significantly enriched in the WCP group, while the abundance of Anaerobutyricum decreased. Clostridium can synthesize various SCFAs and facilitate the proliferation of beneficial gut bacteria (Huang et al., 2019). Romboutsia is recognized as a potential biomarker for intestinal health and microbial homeostasis (Mangifesta et al., 2018; Ricaboni et al., 2016), and Anaerofilum participates in SCFA biosynthesis (Zellner et al., 1996). LEfSe analysis further identified these genera as the characteristic microbial taxa of the WCP group. Collectively, WCP selectively enriched beneficial functional bacteria related to protein metabolism and SCFA production in the cecum of early-stage broilers. Correlation analysis revealed associations between differential microbes and broiler phenotypes. Anaerofilum, enriched in the WCP group, was positively correlated with ADFI, suggesting a potential link to feed intake. The WCP-upregulated Clostridium and Romboutsia correlated positively with growth traits and intestinal morphology, which may be partially attributed to their capacity for SCFA production and intestinal homeostasis maintenance.

Metabolomics can dynamically reflect the metabolic phenotypes of organisms in response to external dietary interventions (Feng et al., 2024). Multivariate statistical analyses including PCA and OPLS-DA revealed distinct separation of cecal metabolites between CON and WCP groups, and the OPLS-DA model passed permutation test with no overfitting, confirming the reliability of metabolomic data. A total of 1627 metabolites were annotated, and 190 differential metabolites were screened according to strict criteria. Functional classification indicated these metabolites were mainly associated with protein/amino acid metabolism, lipid metabolism and carbohydrate metabolism. In line with the property of WCP as a peptide-rich protein source replacing soybean meal, KEGG pathway enrichment identified protein digestion and absorption as the significantly enriched pathway. Undigested dietary protein can be fermented by gut microbes into a series of metabolites, which exert divergent effects on intestinal physiology (Blachier and Kong, 2025). Excessive protein fermentation produces toxic metabolites such as indole, which disrupt intestinal metabolism and homeostasis (Alvarenga et al., 2024). The decreased indole content in the WCP group indicated that WCP optimized intestinal nitrogen metabolism and reduced the accumulation of harmful fermentation products in early broilers. Notably, isovaleric acid, a branched-chain fatty acid (BCFA) derived from microbial fermentation, was significantly upregulated in the WCP group. Existing studies have demonstrated that isovaleric acid can enhance growth performance and antioxidant capacity in broilers (Wang et al., 2023).

Correlation analysis verified that isovaleric acid was positively correlated with Clostridium and Anaerofilum. Previous studies have confirmed that these two genera are major producers of isovaleric acid via amino acid fermentation (Ran-Ressler et al., 2011; Topping, 1996). Therefore, the increased abundance of these functional bacteria was the direct cause of elevated isovaleric acid in the WCP group. Moreover, isovaleric acid showed strong positive correlations with duodenal villus height and relative jejunal weight. Evidence suggests that isovaleric acid can mitigate intestinal inflammation by reducing antigen-presenting cells in intestinal villi (Wang et al., 2023), which partly explains the improved intestinal morphology in WCP-fed broilers at day 14. In addition, isovaleric acid was positively associated with early growth indices (BW, ADG, ADFI). Mechanistically, isovaleric acid activates the cAMP/PKA signaling pathway to promote growth hormone secretion, thereby accelerating animal growth (Blakeney et al., 2019). It should be noted that these Spearman correlation results merely reflect associations instead of causal relationships. Subsequent exogenous isovaleric acid dietary supplementation trials are needed to further validate its regulatory effects.

In summary, WCP remodels cecal microbial structure and microbial metabolic patterns in early-stage broilers. The enrichment of SCFA- and BCFA-producing bacteria increases the content of isovaleric acid, which improves intestinal morphology, nutrient digestion and absorption, and ultimately promotes early growth of broilers.

Conclusion

This study identified distinct stage-specific effects of WCP and CotP on broiler growth and intestinal health. Dietary WCP supplementation improved early growth performance and intestinal morphology of 14-day-old broilers by remodeling cecal microbiota and optimizing microbial metabolism, increasing beneficial isovaleric acid and reducing harmful indole metabolites. In contrast, CotP persistently impaired intestinal barrier and growth. These findings indicate that WCP serves as a promising alternative protein source for promoting early gut health and growth of broilers.
