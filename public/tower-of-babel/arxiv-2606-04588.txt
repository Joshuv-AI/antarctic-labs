VCIFBench: Evaluating Complex Instruction Following for Video Understanding
Huangchen Xu, Yuan Wu, Yi Chang

Abstract
Multimodal large language models have made rapid progress in video understanding, yet existing benchmarks largely rely on simple prompts and provide limited evidence about whether models can satisfy explicit output constraints. We introduce VCIFBench, a benchmark for evaluating complex instruction following in video understanding. VCIFBench constructs constraint-rich instructions from both benchmark-adapted and directly video-grounded prompts, covering content, format, style, and structure requirements, and evaluates model outputs with a hybrid verification pipeline. The benchmark contains 306 satisfiable test instructions, a 540-pair DPO preference dataset, and a 30-item conflict diagnostic subset. Experiments on 10 MLLMs show that joint constraint satisfaction remains challenging. We further show that DPO training on VCIFBench data can improve instruction-following performance.

1
Introduction

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have shown strong capabilities in understanding images, videos, and other heterogeneous inputs
6
;
4
. A growing set of benchmarks evaluates multimodal reasoning, long-video understanding, and general multimodal competence
29
;
35
;
19
;
3
. However, these benchmarks mainly assess task-level performance under relatively simple prompts, leaving unclear whether MLLMs can reliably follow complex instructions with explicit constraints in video-grounded settings.

This gap matters in practical use. A model often needs not only to understand what happens in a video, but also to satisfy user requirements on output content, format, style, and structure. Such requirements are especially important in agentic or tool-use settings, where schema violations or uncontrolled outputs may cause downstream failures
30
. Although instruction following has been widely studied in language models
13
;
15
;
26
, multimodal instruction-following benchmarks remain more limited, often focusing on images
7
;
2
or narrow video tasks such as captioning
17
. Such benchmarks capture only part of the broader task design space. Practical video instructions may also require prediction, temporal or causal reasoning, option selection, or structured generation. As a result, constraints such as length limits, evidence requirements, and ordering rules can behave differently across task types and lead to different failure modes.

Evaluation is another challenge. Rule-based checks are reliable for programmable requirements such as length, format, or exact surface forms
34
, but they cannot cover many semantic or video-grounded constraints. LLM-based judging is more flexible, but may introduce biases such as length or positional bias
9
;
18
. A useful benchmark therefore needs both diverse constraint coverage and reasonably reliable verification.

We introduce
VCIFBench
, a benchmark for
V
ideo
C
omplex
I
nstruction
F
ollowing. VCIFBench constructs constraint-rich instructions from both benchmark-adapted prompts and directly video-grounded prompts, using videos from TempCompass
20
, MMWorld
14
, NExT-QA
27
, and YouCook2
36
. The benchmark covers 20 task types and 40 constraint types across content, format, style, and structure. The current release includes 306 satisfiable test instructions, a 540-pair DPO preference split, and a separate 30-item conflict diagnostic subset for unsatisfiable constraint combinations.

To evaluate model outputs, we develop a hybrid verification pipeline that combines rule-based verification, hybrid parameter extraction with executable checkers, and LLM-based judging. For semantic content constraints, we further decompose high-level requirements into targeted sub-constraints, enabling finer-grained diagnosis of video-grounded failures. Experiments on 10 proprietary and open-source MLLMs show that reliable joint constraint satisfaction remains challenging, especially for video-grounded content constraints. We also show that data derived from the same construction pipeline can improve instruction-following performance through DPO. Anonymous code and data are available at
https://anonymous.4open.science/r/annoym0
.

2
Related Work

2.1
Multimodal Large Language Models and Benchmarks

Recent multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have demonstrated strong capabilities in understanding and reasoning over images, videos, and other heterogeneous inputs
6
;
4
. To evaluate these capabilities, many multimodal benchmarks have been proposed, covering settings such as multimodal reasoning
29
, long-video understanding
35
, spatial reasoning
12
, and video summarization
16
. These benchmarks have been valuable for tracking model progress and exposing failure modes across tasks and modalities.

Many multimodal benchmarks still emphasize task-level performance under relatively simple prompts, offering limited insight into whether models can satisfy fine-grained requirements on content, format, style, and structure
8
. This motivates our focus on complex instruction following in video settings, where task completion must be coupled with explicit constraint satisfaction.

2.2
Instruction Following

Instruction following has been studied extensively in language models. Early work focused on explicit and verifiable requirements. For example, IFEval
34
evaluates programmatically checkable format constraints, while more recent benchmarks study complex instruction following across diverse task types and composition patterns, including combinations of semantic and format constraints
13
;
26
. EifBench further considers settings with multiple instructions and multiple constraints
38
. Prior work has also explored methods for improving compliance in such scenarios, including in-context learning
37
, supervised fine-tuning
31
, and DPO
7
.

In contrast, multimodal instruction following remains less developed, particularly for video. Existing studies mostly examine image-based settings
7
;
2
, while video introduces temporally distributed evidence and more varied response goals. IF-VidCap
17
is an important step toward video-based instruction following, but its scope is mainly caption-oriented. Less is known about how models handle constraints across broader video tasks, such as prediction, option selection, reasoning, and structured response generation. Our work studies this broader setting by combining diverse video task types, multiple constraint categories, and a hybrid evaluation pipeline with rule-based, executable, and judge-based checks.

3
VCIFBench

3.1
Data Collection

We construct VCIFBench from four complementary video sources, focusing on short videos with durations under two minutes: TempCompass
20
, MMWorld
14
, NExT-QA
27
, and YouCook2
36
. These sources cover diverse video scenarios and reasoning demands: TempCompass provides temporal and state-change cases, MMWorld covers scientific and general video-understanding scenarios, NExT-QA contributes causal and temporal reasoning over daily activities, and YouCook2 provides procedural cooking videos. The satisfiable test split contains 306 items. The overall construction and evaluation pipeline is shown in Figure
1
.

3.2
Instruction Generation

Task taxonomy.

We organize video-grounded tasks into three broad categories:
perception
,
reasoning
, and
expressive generation
. Unlike benchmarks that mainly evaluate task completion, VCIFBench evaluates whether models can satisfy explicit constraints while performing the task. Detailed task definitions and examples are provided in Appendix
A.7
.

Constraint taxonomy.

We build on prior taxonomies of practical instruction constraints
15
;
38
;
17
and adapt them to video instruction following. As shown in Figure
2
, VCIFBench contains 40 constraint types organized into four dimensions.
Format
constraints specify surface requirements such as serialization, length, and fixed patterns.
Content
constraints require models to select, include, exclude, or emphasize video-supported information.
Style
constraints control communicative form, such as tone, target audience, persona, and rhetoric.
Structure
constraints specify organization requirements, such as chronological, causal, or hierarchical ordering. Importantly, although content-related constraints depend on video understanding, faithfulness to the video content is itself a core aspect of instruction following, as a helpful response must remain grounded in the video rather than rely on unsupported assumptions.

Figure 2:
Hierarchical taxonomy of constraints in VCIFBench.

Compositional instruction types.

Following prior work
26
, we construct instructions with several composition patterns:
And
for jointly satisfying multiple constraints,
Chain
for combining subtasks,
Selection
for conditioning the response on explicit alternatives, and
Nested
for hierarchical combinations. We do not report separate performance for these patterns because they are not mutually exclusive; for example,
And
commonly appears inside
Chain
and
Selection
instructions.

Instruction construction.

We construct instructions from two complementary sources, using GPT-5.2
22
as the primary generator.

Benchmark-derived prompts.

For source datasets with QA-style task descriptions, we rewrite suitable prompts into open-ended video tasks and combine them with compatible constraints. We discard cases that cannot be meaningfully transformed, such as questions asking about content absent from the video. TempCompass also provides original–reverse video pairs, which naturally support binary
Selection
instructions and allow us to test whether models follow video-grounded alternatives rather than passing a single branch by chance.

Direct video-grounded prompts.

For videos without suitable annotations, we select tasks compatible with observable video evidence and then compose them with candidate constraints. GPT-5.2 generates and revises the instruction to improve fluency. We further use Gemini-2.5 Pro
4
to check whether content-related constraints are supported by the visible video, followed by manual revision to correct residual inconsistencies. Appendix
A.2
reports the full constraints map.

3.3
Evaluation

VCIFBench uses a hybrid evaluation pipeline because constraints differ in how directly they can be verified.

Rule-based verification.

For constraints with deterministic criteria, such as JSON validity or required surface forms, we directly implement checking functions and compute binary outcomes.

Hybrid verification.

Some constraints are rule-checkable only after their parameters are identified from the instruction.
For example, a word-count constraint requires extracting the target range before applying a checker.
Following prior work on constrained and tool-use evaluation
33
;
26
, we ask the judge model to extract the required parameters from the instruction and then execute the corresponding checker.

LLM-based judging.

For constraints that depend on semantic interpretation, discourse organization, or video-grounded reasoning, we use GPT-5
22
to produce binary constraint-level judgments, following prior work on LLM-as-a-judge, multimodal LLM-based evaluation, and complex instruction evaluation
32
;
28
;
38
;
26
.
To make video-grounded evaluation more reliable, we further decompose content-related constraints, such as summarization, focus, tracking, and content inclusion, into explicit evidence-grounded sub-constraints. We report judge–human alignment in Appendix
A.5
.

Metrics.

VCIFBench is constraint-centric. We report both constraint-level and instruction-level metrics. Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of samples. For each sample
i\in\mathcal{S}
, let
\mathcal{C}_{i}
denote its annotated constraints, and let
p_{i,c}\in\{0,1\}
indicate whether constraint
c
is satisfied.

CPR (Constraint Pass Rate).

For a set of annotated constraints
\mathcal{A}
, we define

\mathrm{CPR}(\mathcal{A})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{(i,c)\in\mathcal{A}}p_{i,c}.

\mathcal{A}
can correspond to all constraints of a given type or dimension.

IPR (Instruction Pass Rate).

IPR measures whether all constraints in an instruction are satisfied jointly:

\mathrm{IPR}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\forall c\in\mathcal{C}_{i},\;p_{i,c}=1\right].

This all-constraints criterion reflects practical settings where violating one critical requirement can invalidate the output.

Conflict diagnostic evaluation.

The 30 conflict diagnostic items are excluded from standard IPR/CPR aggregation because they are intentionally unsatisfiable. For this subset, the desired behavior is to recognize that the constraint set is inconsistent, identify the conflicting requirements, and avoid inventing a normal answer.

3.4
DPO Dataset Construction

We construct a 540-pair DPO preference split from the same instruction construction pipeline.
Chosen responses are generated by GPT-5.2 and then processed through the same evaluation pipeline and manual revision to ensure constraint satisfaction and video grounding.

The rejected responses are constructed in two ways.
Inspired by MM-IFEval
7
, we first remove the explicit constraints from each instruction and ask MiniCPM-o-4.5 to answer the underlying video task, producing plausible but constraint-insensitive responses.
Because this strategy may introduce superficial length or style differences, we also generate targeted negatives by prompting MiniCPM-o-4.5 to corrupt one specific constraint in the chosen response while preserving the rest of the answer as much as possible.
We manually revise samples from the second strategy where MiniCPM-o-4.5 only introduces superficial changes without creating a clear instruction-following violation.

Open-source models show substantially lower instruction-level success.
Most achieve CPR around 69–74%, but their IPR remains only around 24–29%, suggesting that they can satisfy many individual constraints but are less stable when constraints are composed.
InternVL3.5-8B is the clearest outlier, with low scores across all dimensions.

Since IPR only indicates whether an instruction fails, we further report near-miss rate and the average number of violated constraints per failed instruction.
The severity metrics show that failures are not homogeneous.
For Gemini-3-Pro and GPT-5.4, more than 73% of failed instructions are near misses, and each failed instruction violates only 1.44 and 1.35 constraints on average.
By contrast, mid-tier open-source models have lower near-miss rates and higher violation counts.
Thus, stronger models often remain close to satisfying all requirements, whereas weaker models more often exhibit cascading failures across multiple constraints.

Manual inspection supports this distinction.
Gemini and GPT failures are usually narrow boundary errors: the semantic answer is often coherent and video-grounded, but one explicit constraint boundary is violated, such as a word budget, a CSV/JSON rule, a missing visual detail, or an excluded extra detail.
Some semantic judgments are inherently more interpretation-sensitive, so strong models may be closer to the practical ceiling on these cases.
However, many remaining failures are still concrete compliance errors, suggesting that multi-constraint instructions place additional pressure even on otherwise simple constraints.
In contrast, InternVL3.5-8B failures more often indicate global breakdowns, including mixed languages, answering both branches of a selection instruction, or uncontrolled long continuations.

Option-order robustness.

We also test selection robustness by swapping the display order of Option A and Option B.
This diagnostic asks whether models follow the video-grounded branch condition, rather than relying on the surface position or label of an option.
As shown in Table

By contrast, Qwen3-VL-8B and MiniCPM-o-4.5 show much lower robustness: they sometimes select the correct path under one option order, but fail to preserve the same decision after the options are swapped.
This indicates that
selection_accuracy

This trend supports the view that instruction-level failures accumulate under multi-constraint settings. However, constraint count alone does not determine difficulty. Some model-level curves show local rebounds because different
k

Overall, the results suggest that both constraint count and constraint composition matter: instructions with more constraints are generally harder to satisfy, but local variations depend on which constraint types appear together.

4.4
Constraint Failure Mechanisms

We further analyze why models fail on Base-306. Rather than treating all failed instructions as the same type of error, we distinguish between constraints that are difficult in themselves and constraints that become fragile mainly when composed with other requirements. This distinction helps explain why relatively high constraint-level performance does not necessarily translate into high instruction-level success.

From hard constraints to failure mechanisms.

The hardest constraints in Base-306 come from several sources. Some require video-grounded evidence selection and semantic compression, such as
summarize
,
content_inclusion
, and
focus
. Others involve negative or boundary control, such as
content_exclusion
and
lexical_diversity
, where the model must avoid prohibited or repeated information while still producing a useful response. We also find that seemingly simple surface requirements, such as
word_count
and
csv
, can remain fragile, especially for weaker models.

Interaction patterns.

We further examine whether some co-occurring constraints fail more often than expected from their individual failure rates. This analysis is diagnostic, since constraint types are not randomly assigned and some pairs have limited unique samples. Still, manual inspection suggests several recurring patterns.

•

Length/count budget
\times
required content or structure.

Length and count constraints become fragile when paired with content or structure requirements. Models often exceed the budget to include visual evidence, causal links, or required markers; conversely, they may satisfy the budget by omitting required visual anchors. This pattern suggests a trade-off between being complete and staying within strict output limits.

•

Selection branch.

selection_accuracy
can act as a gatekeeper constraint. Once a model chooses the wrong branch, preserves both branches, or leaves option labels in the answer, subsequent constraints often fail as a cascading consequence.

•

Style or rhetoric
\times
rigid surface format.

Persona, tone, and rhetorical constraints can encourage greetings, labels, explanatory framing, or narrative openings. These additions may improve naturalness, but they can violate exact openings, fixed closings, plain-text requirements, or label placement. In creative writing or role-play tasks, models may produce more readable responses while breaking exact surface constraints.

•

Output language/rhetoric
\times
lexical exclusion or diversity.

A model may avoid the exact prohibited word while reintroducing the excluded content through paraphrase, adjacent concepts, or plausible elaboration, especially when instructed to produce the response in another language.

Overall, Base-306 failures should not be interpreted as isolated constraint errors. Some constraints are intrinsically difficult because they require video-grounded evidence selection or semantic compression. Others become difficult mainly under composition, where content, format, style, and task-specific response tendencies compete with one another.

4.6
Conflict-Aware Instruction Following

Conflict-30 tests whether models can recognize conflicting user requirements instead of forcing a normal video answer.
The conflicts are deliberately explicit, such as requiring and forbidding the same phrase, requiring all-lowercase output while requiring an uppercase token such as
NASA
.
Such conflicts may arise from erroneous or inconsistent user instructions; a reliable model should identify the problem and ask for clarification rather than blindly proceed.

Figure 5:

Conflict-30 diagnostic results.

Detected
denotes basic conflict detection;
Localized
denotes identifying the conflicts;
Strict
additionally requires not giving a normal answer;
Blind
denotes blind compliance.

Figure
5
shows a clear gap between detecting a conflict and handling it strictly.
Top proprietary models perform well: they usually identify the instruction as unsatisfiable, point to the conflicting requirements, and avoid producing a normal video answer.
Our strict criterion intentionally requires localization of the conflict, because merely saying that the instruction is “impossible” gives the user little guidance on how to revise it.
By contrast, several weaker open-source models show high blind-compliance rates, meaning that they often treat an impossible instruction as an ordinary video task and generate an answer anyway.

Other models reveal a more nuanced pattern.
Qwen3-VL-30B and MiniCPM-o-4.5 detect conflicts in many cases, but their strict handling is lower because they sometimes fail to point out the exact contradictory requirements or continue answering after acknowledging a problem.
Qwen3-VL-8B is a notable intermediate case: despite much lower Base-306 IPR, it achieves strong Conflict-30 handling, suggesting that conflict awareness is related to but distinct from ordinary multi-constraint compliance.

The
visible_absent_content
cases further show why conflict awareness matters in video-grounded settings.
These instructions deliberately ask the model to mention an object that is not visible in the video, such as a blue elephant, while also requiring the answer to use only visible evidence.
A correct response should reject this contradiction.
However, Gemini-3-Flash and InternVL3.5-8B sometimes describe the absent object as if it were visible.
This failure is more consequential than a formatting error, because blind compliance directly turns an inconsistent instruction into hallucinated visual evidence.

5
Conclusion

We present
VCIFBench
, a benchmark for evaluating complex instruction following in video understanding, together with a construction pipeline and a hybrid evaluation pipeline. Experiments on 10 MLLMs show that even capable models still struggle to satisfy multiple explicit constraints jointly: substantial weaknesses remain in fine-grained format control, adherence to video-grounded content, and robustness under instructions that combine multiple explicit constraints. We further show that DPO training on data derived from our pipeline can improve overall instruction-following performance. We hope VCIFBench will serve as a useful step toward more reliable, controllable, and practically deployable multimodal systems.

6
Limitations

First, our current benchmark primarily centers on English instructions and outputs. Broader multilingual coverage would be a useful extension for studying complex video instruction following across languages and linguistic conventions.

Second, VCIFBench intentionally covers a focused subset of video instruction following.
Consequently, VCIFBench should be viewed as a controlled stress test for constraint-rich video instruction following.
Its results are informative about whether models can jointly satisfy explicit requirements, but should not be used as a standalone measure of overall video understanding, factual grounding, or deployment safety.

Finally, our evaluation pipeline combines rule-based verification, hybrid verification, and LLM-based judging to balance coverage and reliability. Although this design improves practical coverage, variations in judge behavior and interpretation cannot be fully ruled out, especially for more semantic or subjective constraints.

Potential Risks.

VCIFBench is a controlled evaluation resource and does not introduce direct deployment risks. A possible indirect risk is that optimizing models primarily for explicit constraint satisfaction could encourage formal compliance at the expense of broader reliability, such as factual grounding, safety, or appropriate refusal behavior. We therefore recommend using VCIFBench alongside broader evaluations of video understanding, factuality, and safety.

A.4
Residual Failure Modes in Instruction Construction

After automated verification, we manually inspect residual inconsistencies in the satisfiable split. Despite multi-stage filtering, several recurring failure modes remain.

First, some instructions contain mismatches between content-related constraints and visual evidence, e.g., requiring details or events that do not appear in the video. We also observe occasional accidental cross-constraint conflicts, such as exact-word requirements that are incompatible with the required output language. These accidental cases are removed or repaired in the satisfiable split; by contrast, the separate conflict diagnostic subset in Appendix
A.3
deliberately contains hard conflicts for diagnostic evaluation.

Second, some
Selection
-type instructions remain underspecified. In these cases, the unselected branch is not described clearly enough, or the branching condition is framed too subjectively (e.g., whether the response should be written “as a news report” or “as a tutorial”), rather than being tied to an objectively identifiable alternative in the video. This weakens the intended design of
Selection
, where the correct branch should be uniquely determined by visual evidence.

A.6
Task–Constraint Interaction Diagnostics

The main text summarizes recurring constraint–constraint interactions.
Here we provide additional task–constraint diagnostics, focusing on cases where a constraint becomes harder because the task itself encourages a competing response style.

•

state_change_detection

\times

tone
.

Although
tone
is generally not a hard constraint, it becomes more fragile when the task asks for state changes.
In one sample, models are asked to describe a visible facial change in a neutral and objective tone instead of interpreting the emotion, but outputs such as “he appears to be happy” or “broad, happy smile” turn visual change into affective interpretation.
This suggests that task semantics can pull models away from neutral description and make style control harder.

•

practical_writing

\times

rhetoric
.

Practical writing favors direct, actionable steps, while rhetorical constraints require comparison, contrast, or simile.
In YouCook2 examples, some models produce clear cooking instructions but omit the required rhetorical device, while others add figurative language that is not clearly grounded in the visible action.
This reflects a tension between practical clarity and rhetorical elaboration.
