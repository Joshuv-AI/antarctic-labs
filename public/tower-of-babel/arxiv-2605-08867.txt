Abstract
. We compare Picard–Lefschetz theory and resurgence in three basic one-dimensional exponential integrals: the Airy model, the Bessel model, and the Gamma model. On the Picard–Lefschetz side, we describe the Lefschetz thimbles and compute the connecting trajectories between critical points appearing at Stokes phases. On the resurgent side, we analyze the Borel singularities of the saddle expansions and use alien operators to recover the same Stokes coefficients. These examples serve as explicit finite-dimensional test cases for the dictionary between thimble wall-crossing and alien calculus.



1.
Introduction

Complex exponential integrals form a central object of study in asymptotic analysis, complex geometry, and mathematical physics. A prototypical example is an integral of the form

I_{\Gamma}(\hbar)=\int_{\Gamma}e^{-S(z)/\hbar}\,\mu

where
S(z)
is a holomorphic function on a complex manifold,
\mu
is a holomorphic form, and
\Gamma
is a contour (or cycle) in a suitable relative homology class
[\Gamma]
. We take
\hbar
to be valued in
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. Such integrals naturally arise in semiclassical analysis, in the study of differential equations via Laplace-type representations, and in complexified path integrals in quantum field theory.

The asymptotic behavior of
I_{\Gamma}(\hbar)
for small
\hbar
is controlled by the critical points of the phase function

dS=0.

Near non-degenerate critical points
\{p_{\sigma}\}
, one obtains a formal asymptotic expansion of the form

I_{\Gamma}(\hbar)\sim\sum_{\sigma}e^{-S(p_{\sigma})/\hbar}\hbar^{n/2}\left(c_{\sigma,0}+c_{\sigma,1}\hbar+c_{\sigma,2}\hbar^{2}+\cdots\right)

where the coefficients are determined by the local Taylor expansion of
S
(see for example
[
1
,
16
]
). However, this expansion is typically divergent and only asymptotic in nature, reflecting the global complexity of the integral.

A powerful framework to capture the full analytic structure is provided by Picard–Lefschetz theory, which describes how integration cycles can be decomposed into a basis of Lefschetz thimbles. Each thimble
\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}
is obtained by downward gradient flow of
\operatorname{Re}(S/\hbar)
associated to a critical point
p_{\sigma}
. If we decompose
\Gamma
into Lefschetz thimbles in a suitable relative homology class

[\Gamma]=\sum_{\sigma}n_{\sigma}[\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}],

then one obtains a representation of the integral by

I_{\Gamma}(\hbar)=\sum_{\sigma}n_{\sigma}\int_{\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}}e^{-S(z)/\hbar}\,\mu.

We collect basics of this construction relevant to our discussion in Section
2
.

A crucial feature of this decomposition is that it depends on the phase of the parameter
\hbar
. As
\arg\hbar
varies, the gradient flow structure changes, and the thimble decomposition undergoes discontinuous jumps across certain rays, known as Stokes lines. These jumps of
\{n_{\sigma}\}
are governed by the Picard–Lefschetz formula: the discontinuous change in asymptotic expansions corresponds to a change in the basis of integration cycles. Similar phenomenon appears if we vary parameters in defining the phase function
S
.

On the other hand, the theory of resurgence developed by Écalle (
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
) provides a general framework to study global analytic natures of divergent asymptotic expansions. The key idea is that the Borel transform of a divergent formal series often defines a function with controlled singularities in the complex Borel plane, and these singularities encode the global analytic structure of the original problem. The process of Borel–Laplace resummation provides a systematic way to reconstruct actual functions from their asymptotic expansions, at least within suitable sectors of the complex plane. More concretely, given a formal power series with
\operatorname{Re}\alpha>0
,

\tilde{\phi}(\hbar)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}\hbar^{n+\alpha}

its Borel transform is defined by

\hat{\phi}(\xi)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\Gamma(n+\alpha)}\xi^{n+\alpha-1}

which may be holomorphic on the local logarithmic Riemann surface at
\xi=0
and admit analytic continuation beyond it. If the resulting function has sufficiently mild growth, one can define the Laplace transform

\mathcal{L}_{\theta}\hat{\phi}(\hbar)=\int_{0}^{e^{i\theta}\infty}e^{-\xi/\hbar}\hat{\phi}(\xi)d\xi

yielding a resummed function depending on a direction
\theta
.

However, when singularities of the Borel transform lie on certain directions,
one has to pass around them from one side or the other, leading to possible
ambiguities in the resummation.
These ambiguities are precisely governed by the Stokes phenomenon. A central insight of resurgence theory is that the discontinuities across Stokes lines are not arbitrary but are organized by a rich algebraic structure encoded in alien operators. These alien operators measure the singular behavior of the Borel transform near its singular points. See Section
2.5
for a review.

In this paper, we explain this correspondence by a case study of three concrete examples: the Airy, Bessel, and Gamma models. We perform explicit computations from both the Picard–Lefschetz theory and the resurgence theory, and illustrate how the alien/Stokes coefficients reproduce the
Picard–Lefschetz trajectory counts. These three chosen examples represent three typical situations on connecting trajectories between critical points when crosing the Stokes line. The airy model has a unique connecting trajectory; the Bessel model has a multiple connecting trajectories; and the Gamma model has infinite many critical points and connecting trajectories. The phenomenon of broken lines appears in the example of Gamma model, and we illustrate how this is captured by the iterated alien operators on the resurgence side.

All the above models are finite dimensional. They are used to make explicit
the dictionary between Picard-Lefschetz theory and alien calculus. In the infinite dimensional case, this dictionary gives a useful strategy for studying quantum field theory, where the Picard–Lefschetz geometry is difficult to compute directly. In the forth-coming work
[
13
]
, we will generalize such analysis

\text{Picard--Lefschetz trajectory count}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{alien coefficient}

to an infinite dimensional model for the path-space problem.

The relation among Stokes phenomena, exponential integrals, and wall-crossing structures has been explored from several complementary perspectives. In particular, the work of Kontsevich and Soibelman
[
10
,
11
]
investigate analyticity and resurgence phenomena in the context of Lefschetz thimbles and their wall-crossing behavior, and develop a Floer-theoretic framework for exponential integrals. One aim of the present work is to introduce the viewpoint of alien calculus into the geometric setting of Picard–Lefschetz theory and wall-crossing, and to show that the interplay with vanishing cycles admits a natural interpretation in terms of the algebraic structures underlying resurgence.

2.
Exponential integrals: Picard-Lefschetz v.s. alien calculus

This section recalls the basic framework that will be used throughout the Airy, Bessel, and Gamma examples below: the Morse flow of exponential integrals, the associated Lefschetz thimbles and their wall-crossing, and the corresponding resurgent interpretation via Borel singularities and alien operators. We refer to classical references for further details, such as Milnor
[
14
]
on the local geometry of vanishing cycles, Pham’s vanishing-homology approach to the saddle-point method
[

[

2.1.
The basic data

Given

•

a complex manifold
X
with
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}X=n
;

•

a holomorphic Morse function
S:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
with suitable growth at
\infty
;

•

a holomorphic volume form
\mu
on
X
, locally,
\mu=\mu(z)\mathrm{d}^{n}z
;

•

an oriented cycle
\Gamma
with
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\Gamma=n
such that

\operatorname{Re}(S)\big|_{\Gamma}\rightarrow+\infty\text{ at }"\infty"\text{ of }\Gamma.

We shall study the exponential integrals of the form

(1)

I_{\Gamma}(\hbar)=\int_{\Gamma}e^{-S(z)/\hbar}\,\mu.

The main goals are:

\diamond

to recall the finite-dimensional
Picard–Lefschetz description of exponential integrals in terms of
thimbles, dual thimbles, Stokes phases, and connecting trajectories;

\diamond

to compute explicitly, in the Airy, Bessel, and Gamma
models, the same Stokes matrices from both the Picard–Lefschetz
wall-crossing of thimbles and the resurgent Stokes automorphisms of
saddle expansions;

\diamond

to compare the two computations and verify that the
alien/Stokes coefficients reproduce the Picard–Lefschetz trajectory
counts.

For these purposes, it is convenient to fix a positive parameter
|\hbar|>0
and to rotate the action instead of the small parameter. Thus we write

\hbar=|\hbar|e^{i\theta},\qquad S_{\theta}:=e^{-i\theta}S,

so that

e^{-S/\hbar}=e^{-S_{\theta}/|\hbar|}.

The steepest-descent geometry is therefore governed by the pair of real-valued functions

F_{\theta}:=\operatorname{Re}(S_{\theta})=\operatorname{Re}(e^{-i\theta}S),\qquad G_{\theta}:=\operatorname{Im}(S_{\theta})=\operatorname{Im}(e^{-i\theta}S).

2.2.
Negative gradient flow

Choose a Hermitian metric
h
on
X
and consider the negative gradient flow of
F_{\theta}
,

(2)

\frac{dz}{ds}=-\nabla_{h}F_{\theta}(z).

Let
g_{h}:=\operatorname{Re}h
be the underlying Riemannian metric, and let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{h}
and
\|\cdot\|_{h}
denote the corresponding inner product and norm. The gradient
\nabla_{h}f
of a real-valued function
f
is characterized by

g_{h}(\nabla_{h}f,\xi)=df(\xi),\qquad\forall\,\xi~\in TX.

In a local holomorphic chart
z=(z^{1},\dots,z^{n})
, write

h=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}h_{i\bar{j}}(z)\,dz^{i}\otimes d\bar{z}^{j},\qquad(h^{i\bar{j}})=(h_{i\bar{j}})^{-1}.

Then the negative gradient flow of
F_{\theta}=\operatorname{Re}(S_{\theta})
takes the coordinate form

(3)

\frac{dz^{i}}{ds}=-2\sum_{j=1}^{n}h^{i\bar{j}}(z)\,\partial_{\bar{j}}F_{\theta}(z)=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}h^{i\bar{j}}(z)\,\overline{\partial_{j}S_{\theta}(z)}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}h^{i\bar{j}}(z)\,\overline{e^{-i\theta}\partial_{j}S(z)}.

In one complex dimension, if
h=h_{z\bar{z}}(z)\,dz\,d\bar{z}
, this becomes

\frac{dz}{ds}=-h^{z\bar{z}}(z)\,\overline{e^{-i\theta}S^{\prime}(z)}.

For the flat metric
h_{z\bar{z}}\equiv 1
, we arrive at

\dot{z}=-\overline{e^{-i\theta}S^{\prime}(z)}.

The fundamental identities along (
2
) are the following.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
z(s)
be a solution of the gradient flow (
2
). Then

(4)

\frac{d}{ds}F_{\theta}(z(s))=-\lVert\nabla_{h}F_{\theta}(z(s))\rVert_{h}^{2}\leq 0,

and

(5)

\frac{d}{ds}G_{\theta}(z(s))=0.

Thus every flow line lies in a level set of
G_{\theta}
, while
F_{\theta}
decreases strictly along every non-stationary flow line.

Proof.

S_{\theta}=F_{\theta}+iG_{\theta}
is holomorphic, hence
\partial_{j}F_{\theta}=i\partial_{j}G_{\theta}
. By equation (
3
)

\frac{dS_{\theta}(z(s))}{ds}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{dz^{i}}{ds}\frac{\partial S_{\theta}(z)}{\partial z^{i}}=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}h^{i\bar{j}}(z(s))\,\partial_{i}S_{\theta}(z(s))\,\overline{\partial_{j}S_{\theta}(z(s))}=-\lVert\nabla_{h}F_{\theta}(z(s))\rVert_{h}^{2}.

The lemma follows by comparing the real and imaginary part of this equation.
∎

2.3.
Lefschetz thimbles: local and global descriptions

Definition 2.2
.

For
p\in\operatorname{Crit}(S)
, define the (global) stable and unstable manifolds for the negative gradient flow (
2
) by

W^{s}_{\theta}(p;-\nabla_{h}F_{\theta}):=\{z_{0}\in X\mid z(s;z_{0})\to p\text{ as }s\to+\infty\},

W^{u}_{\theta}(p;-\nabla_{h}F_{\theta}):=\{z_{0}\in X\mid z(s;z_{0})\to p\text{ as }s\to-\infty\}.

We also denote them by
\mathcal{J}_{p}^{\theta}
and
\mathcal{K}_{p}^{\theta}
respectively, and call
\mathcal{J}_{p}^{\theta}
Lefschetz thimble and
\mathcal{K}_{p}^{\theta}
dual thimble attached to a critical point
p
.

Let us analyze the local structure first. Let
p\in\operatorname{Crit}(S)
be a nondegenerate critical point. By the holomorphic Morse lemma, there exist holomorphic coordinates
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
centered at
p
such that

(6)

S_{\theta}(w)=S_{\theta}(p)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}^{2}.

Writing
w_{j}=x_{j}+iy_{j}
, one gets

F_{\theta}(w)-F_{\theta}(p)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(x_{j}^{2}-y_{j}^{2}),\qquad G_{\theta}(w)-G_{\theta}(p)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}y_{j}.

To first order at
p
, after choosing normal coordinates for the metric, the negative gradient flow is

(7)

\dot{w}_{j}=-\overline{w_{j}},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad\dot{x}_{j}=-x_{j},\quad\dot{y}_{j}=y_{j}.

Thus
F_{\theta}
has real Morse index
n
at every nondegenerate critical point of a holomorphic function on an
n
-dimensional complex manifold.

Proposition 2.3

(Local stable and unstable models)
.

Near a nondegenerate critical point
p
, the local stable and unstable manifolds for
-\nabla_{h}F_{\theta}
are middle-dimensional real submanifolds. In the leading holomorphic Morse coordinates (
6
), they have tangent spaces

T_{p}\mathcal{J}_{p}^{\theta}=\mathbb{R}^{n}=\{y=0\},\qquad T_{p}\mathcal{K}_{p}^{\theta}=i\mathbb{R}^{n}=\{x=0\}.

More precisely, for a sufficiently small ball
B_{\varepsilon}(p)
, one has local stable and unstable disks

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{p,\operatorname{loc}}^{\theta}

\displaystyle=\{x+i\psi_{s}(x):|x|<\varepsilon\},\qquad\psi_{s}(0)=0,\quad D\psi_{s}(0)=0,

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{p,\operatorname{loc}}^{\theta}

\displaystyle=\{\psi_{u}(y)+iy:|y|<\varepsilon\},\qquad\psi_{u}(0)=0,\quad D\psi_{u}(0)=0.

Both are contained in the level set
G_{\theta}=G_{\theta}(p)
.

Proof.

The linearized flow is hyperbolic, with stable space
\{y=0\}
and unstable space
\{x=0\}
. The stable and unstable manifold theorem gives the smooth disks (
8
) and (
9
). By Lemma
2.1
,
G_{\theta}
is constant on every flow line. Since every point of the local stable or unstable disk flows to
p
in one time direction, the value of
G_{\theta}
on these disks must equal
G_{\theta}(p)
.
∎

When
n=1

S_{\theta}=S_{\theta}(p)+\frac{1}{2}w^{2},\qquad w=x+iy.

Then

\mathcal{J}_{p,\operatorname{loc}}^{\theta}=\{y=0\},\qquad\mathcal{K}_{p,\operatorname{loc}}^{\theta}=\{x=0\}.

The stable thimble has two local half-branches

A_{p}^{\theta}=\{x>0,\ y=0\},\qquad B_{p}^{\theta}=\{x<0,\ y=0\},

and the unstable thimble has two local half-branches

C_{p}^{\theta}=\{x=0,\ y>0\},\qquad D_{p}^{\theta}=\{x=0,\ y<0\}.

With the usual orientation convention, one may write locally

(10)

\mathcal{J}_{p}^{\theta}\sim A_{p}^{\theta}-B_{p}^{\theta},\qquad\mathcal{K}_{p}^{\theta}\sim C_{p}^{\theta}-D_{p}^{\theta}.

Here each half-branch
A_{p}^{\theta},B_{p}^{\theta},C_{p}^{\theta},D_{p}^{\theta}
is
oriented outward from the critical point
p

A_{p}^{\theta}-B_{p}^{\theta}
and
C_{p}^{\theta}-D_{p}^{\theta}
determine the induced

Let
\phi_{s}
denote the flow of
-\nabla_{h}F_{\theta}
. With the above analysis, the global stable and unstable manifolds are also given by

\mathcal{J}_{p}^{\theta}=\bigcup_{s\geq 0}\phi_{-s}\bigl(\mathcal{J}_{p,\operatorname{loc}}^{\theta}\bigr),\qquad\mathcal{K}_{p}^{\theta}=\bigcup_{s\geq 0}\phi_{s}\bigl(\mathcal{K}_{p,\operatorname{loc}}^{\theta}\bigr).

Again, by Lemma
2.1
, one has

\mathcal{J}_{p}^{\theta}\subset\{G_{\theta}=G_{\theta}(p)\},\qquad\mathcal{K}_{p}^{\theta}\subset\{G_{\theta}=G_{\theta}(p)\}.

Moreover, along
\mathcal{J}_{p}^{\theta}
, the forward flow decreases
F_{\theta}
to
F_{\theta}(p)
, while the backward flow increases
F_{\theta}
. Therefore, under the usual tameness/no-critical-points-at-infinity hypotheses, the outgoing end of
\mathcal{J}_{p}^{\theta}
lies in the region

F_{\theta}\to+\infty.

Similarly, the outgoing end of
\mathcal{K}_{p}^{\theta}
lies in the region

F_{\theta}\to-\infty.

For
R\gg 0
, define the positive and negative ends

X_{+R}^{\theta}=\{x\in X:F_{\theta}(x)\geq R\},\qquad X_{-R}^{\theta}=\{x\in X:F_{\theta}(x)\leq-R\}.

The truncated thimble
\mathcal{J}_{p}^{\theta}\cap\{F_{\theta}\leq R\}
has boundary in
X_{+R}^{\theta}
, and the truncated dual thimble
\mathcal{K}_{p}^{\theta}\cap\{F_{\theta}\geq-R\}
has boundary in
X_{-R}^{\theta}
. Hence they define relative cycles

[\mathcal{J}_{p}^{\theta}]\in H_{n}(X,X_{+R}^{\theta};\mathbb{Z}),\qquad[\mathcal{K}_{p}^{\theta}]\in H_{n}(X,X_{-R}^{\theta};\mathbb{Z}).

For different sufficiently large
R

H_{n}(X,X_{+\infty}^{\theta};\mathbb{Z}),\qquad H_{n}(X,X_{-\infty}^{\theta};\mathbb{Z}).

The phase
\theta
determines which trajectories are visible, because every trajectory is constrained to a level set of
G_{\theta}
. For simplicity, assume that the critical values of
S
are pairwise distinct, then for two critical points
p,q
, a necessary condition for a gradient trajectory from

G_{\theta}(p)=G_{\theta}(q),

that is,

\operatorname{Im}\bigl(e^{-i\theta}(S(p)-S(q))\bigr)=0.

Thus the exceptional phases are precisely the Stokes phases determined by differences of critical values.

Definition 2.4

(Regular phase)
.

A phase
\theta
is called regular, or non-Stokes, if

G_{\theta}(p)\neq G_{\theta}(q)\qquad\text{for all distinct }p,q\in\operatorname{Crit}(S).

Equivalently, no two distinct critical values of
S
lie on a line of direction
e^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}
in the
S
-plane.

For a regular phase,
\mathcal{J}_{p}^{\theta}
and
\mathcal{K}_{q}^{\theta}
can intersect only when
p=q
. Indeed, if
x\in\mathcal{J}_{p}^{\theta}\cap\mathcal{K}_{q}^{\theta}
, then
x
lies on a trajectory with forward limit
p
and backward limit
q
. Conservation of
G_{\theta}
forces

G_{\theta}(p)=G_{\theta}(x)=G_{\theta}(q),

so regularity implies
p=q
.

(Tame Lefschetz data)

We say that

Remark 2.6

2.4.
Connecting trajectories and Picard-Lefschetz formula

At a Stokes phase
\theta_{*}
, where
G_{\theta_{*}}(p)=G_{\theta_{*}}(q)
for some
p\neq q
, connecting trajectories may appear. When
\theta
crosses such a wall, the thimble basis changes by a Picard–Lefschetz transformation.

More explicitly, fix a Stokes phase
\theta_{*}
, and let
p_{0},\dots,p_{r}
be the critical points whose critical values lie on the same Stokes line (i.e. along the line in the direction
e^{i\theta_{*}}
), ordered so that

F_{\theta_{*}}(p_{0})>\cdots>F_{\theta_{*}}(p_{r}).

Write the two one-sided thimble bases as

\mathbf{J}^{<}:=([J_{p_{0}}^{<}],\dots,[\mathcal{J}_{p_{r}}^{<}]),\qquad\mathbf{J}^{>}:=([\mathcal{J}_{p_{0}}^{>}],\dots,[\mathcal{J}_{p_{r}}^{>}]).

Here
<
(resp.
>
) means the direction
\theta_{*}-\varepsilon
(resp.
\theta_{*}+\varepsilon
) with small positive
\varepsilon
.
Then there are unique unitriangular matrices
S_{+}
and
S_{-}
such that

\mathbf{J}^{>}=\mathbf{J}^{<}S_{-},\qquad\mathbf{J}^{<}=\mathbf{J}^{>}S_{+},

hence
S_{-}=S_{+}^{-1}
. If either
S_{+}
or
S_{-}
has the elementary form

\begin{pmatrix}1&&&&&\\
&\ddots&&&&\\
&&1&N&&\\
&&0&1&&\\
&&&&\ddots&\\
&&&&&1\end{pmatrix},

with all omitted off-diagonal entries equal to
0
, then the elementary Picard–Lefschetz jump formula implies that
N
is the signed count of direct connecting trajectories from
p_{i}
to
p_{i+1}
, where
N
sits in the
(i,i+1)
-position; in complex dimension one,
|N|
is the number of such trajectories. See
[
20
, Sec. 3.1.2]
; cf. also
[
1
,
16
,
19
]
.

This is exactly the situation relevant for the examples below.

•

For Airy and Bessel, one has
r=1
, so the relevant matrix is

\begin{pmatrix}1&N\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

with
N=1
in the Airy case and
N=2
in the Bessel case.

•

For the Gamma model, the same discussion applies with an infinite unitriangular matrix in place of the finite one; in that example, one of
S_{+}
and
S_{-}
is not elementary, while the other is of nearest-neighbor form, and it is this latter matrix from which the direct connecting trajectories are read off.

Remark 2.8
.

If we do not assume that the critical values of
S
are pairwise distinct, then Stokes walls should be defined for distinct critical values rather
than for individual critical points. For two critical values
A\neq B
, the
phase
\theta_{*}
is Stokes if

\operatorname{Im}\bigl(e^{-i\theta_{*}}(A-B)\bigr)=0.

At such a phase the Picard–Lefschetz jump is a block transformation between the
critical clusters
\operatorname{Crit}_{A}(S)
and
\operatorname{Crit}_{B}(S)
.
If
p_{\alpha}\in\operatorname{Crit}_{A}(S)
, then

[\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{\theta_{*+}}]=[\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{\theta_{*-}}]+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\neq A\\

where

is the signed count of
F_{\theta_{*}}
-gradient
trajectories from
p_{\alpha}
to
p_{\beta}
. Critical points with the same
critical value

form a resonant block. They do not produce an ordinary
Stokes jump among themselves, since any finite-energy trajectory between two

2.6.
The Hopf algebra of pointed alien operators

We now record the Hopf-algebraic structure carried by the pointed alien
operators
\dot{\Delta}_{w}^{+}
, where
w\in\Lambda_{d}\subset d
and

\Lambda_{d}
is the additive semigroup generated by the nonzero Borel
singularities on the Stokes ray
d
. For more details on the algebraic structures behind alien operators and
mould calculus, we refer the reader to
[
5
]
and

[
12
]
. The Stokes automorphism in the
direction
d
is written as

(16)

\mathfrak{S}_{d}^{+}=\Id+\sum_{w\in\Lambda_{d}}\dot{\Delta}_{w}^{+}.

For a word

\underline{w}=w_{1}\cdots w_{r},\qquad w_{i}\in\Lambda_{d},

we write

(17)

\dot{\Delta}_{\underline{w}}^{+}:=\dot{\Delta}_{w_{r}}^{+}\cdots\dot{\Delta}_{w_{1}}^{+}.

For the empty word
\emptyset
, we set

\dot{\Delta}_{\emptyset}^{+}:=\Id,\qquad|\emptyset|=0.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{d}
be the completed noncommutative algebra formally generated
by the operators
\dot{\Delta}_{w}^{+}
, with unit

\dot{\Delta}_{\emptyset}^{+}=\Id
, completed with respect to the total weight

|\underline{w}|:=w_{1}+\cdots+w_{r}.

The product is operator composition. With the convention
(
17
), one has

\dot{\Delta}_{\underline{w}}^{+}\,\dot{\Delta}_{\underline{v}}^{+}=\dot{\Delta}_{\underline{v}\underline{w}}^{+}.

The coproduct is determined by the modified Leibniz rule for

\dot{\Delta}_{w}^{+}
. Since the full Stokes automorphism is an algebra
automorphism, we have

\mathfrak{S}_{d}^{+}(FG)=\mathfrak{S}_{d}^{+}(F)\mathfrak{S}_{d}^{+}(G).

Using (
16
) and comparing the homogeneous
part of total weight
w
, we obtain

\dot{\Delta}_{w}^{+}(FG)=\bigl(\dot{\Delta}_{w}^{+}F\bigr)G+F\bigl(\dot{\Delta}_{w}^{+}G\bigr)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{1}+w_{2}=w\\
w_{1},w_{2}\in\Lambda_{d}\end{subarray}}\bigl(\dot{\Delta}_{w_{1}}^{+}F\bigr)\bigl(\dot{\Delta}_{w_{2}}^{+}G\bigr).

This motivates the coproduct

(18)

\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}\bigl(\dot{\Delta}_{w}^{+}\bigr)=\dot{\Delta}_{w}^{+}\otimes 1+1\otimes\dot{\Delta}_{w}^{+}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{1}+w_{2}=w\\
w_{1},w_{2}\in\Lambda_{d}\end{subarray}}\dot{\Delta}_{w_{1}}^{+}\otimes\dot{\Delta}_{w_{2}}^{+}.

It is extended to
\mathcal{H}_{d}
as an algebra homomorphism:

\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}(XY)=\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}(X)\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}(Y).

Consequently the Stokes automorphism is group-like:

\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathfrak{S}_{d}^{+})=\mathfrak{S}_{d}^{+}\otimes\mathfrak{S}_{d}^{+}.

The counit is defined by

\varepsilon(\dot{\Delta}_{\emptyset}^{+})=1,\qquad\varepsilon(\dot{\Delta}_{\underline{w}}^{+})=0\quad\text{for every nonempty word }\underline{w}.

The antipode is the map

\mathsf{S}_{\mathcal{H}}:\mathcal{H}_{d}\to\mathcal{H}_{d}

defined on homogeneous generators by

\mathsf{S}_{\mathcal{H}}(\dot{\Delta}_{w}^{+})=\sum_{r\geq 1}(-1)^{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{1}+\cdots+w_{r}=w\\
w_{i}\in\Lambda_{d}\end{subarray}}\dot{\Delta}_{w_{1}}^{+}\dot{\Delta}_{w_{2}}^{+}\cdots\dot{\Delta}_{w_{r}}^{+}.

Note that
0\notin\Lambda_{d}
. The antipode is extended anti-multiplicatively:

\mathsf{S}_{\mathcal{H}}\left(\dot{\Delta}_{\underline{w}}^{+}\right)=\mathsf{S}_{\mathcal{H}}\left(\dot{\Delta}_{w_{r}}^{+}\cdots\dot{\Delta}_{w_{1}}^{+}\right)=\mathsf{S}_{\mathcal{H}}(\dot{\Delta}_{w_{1}}^{+})\cdots\mathsf{S}_{\mathcal{H}}(\dot{\Delta}_{w_{r}}^{+}).

Equivalently,
\mathsf{S}_{\mathcal{H}}
is characterized by the convolution
inverse identity

m(\mathsf{S}_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes\Id)\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}(X)=\varepsilon(X)1=m(\Id\otimes\mathsf{S}_{\mathcal{H}})\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}(X).

Since
\mathfrak{S}_{d}^{+}
is group-like, the antipode sends it to its inverse:

\mathsf{S}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathfrak{S}_{d}^{+})=(\mathfrak{S}_{d}^{+})^{-1}=\mathfrak{S}_{d}^{-}.

Thus, on the level of Stokes matrices, the antipode corresponds to inverse
wall-crossing.

Finally, the logarithmic pointed alien operator is obtained by taking the
logarithm of the Stokes automorphism:

(19)

\dot{\Delta}_{d}:=\log\mathfrak{S}_{d}^{+}=\log\left(\Id+\sum_{w\in\Lambda_{d}}\dot{\Delta}_{w}^{+}\right).

We write

\dot{\Delta}_{d}=\sum_{w\in\Lambda_{d}}\dot{\Delta}_{w}

for its decomposition into homogeneous weights. Comparing the homogeneous

\dot{\Delta}_{w}=\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{r-1}}{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{1}+\cdots+w_{r}=w\\
w_{i}\in\Lambda_{d}\end{subarray}}\dot{\Delta}_{w_{1}}^{+}\dot{\Delta}_{w_{2}}^{+}\cdots\dot{\Delta}_{w_{r}}^{+}.

Remark 3.1
.

We call this the Airy model because it is the specialization at
x=1
of
the standard Airy phase

S_{x}(z)=\frac{z^{3}}{3}-xz.

If
x
is kept as a parameter, then for suitable rapidly decaying cycles, the integral

I_{\Gamma}(x,\hbar)=\int_{\Gamma}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\hbar}\left(\frac{z^{3}}{3}-xz\right)\right)\,dz

satisfies the semiclassical Airy equation

\hbar^{2}\,\frac{d^{2}I_{\Gamma}}{dx^{2}}=x\,I_{\Gamma}.

The critical points

p_{+}=1
and
p_{-}=-1
are non-degenerate and the critical values are

S(p_{+})=-\frac{2}{3},\qquad S(p_{-})=\frac{2}{3}.

Choosing the standard hermritian metric, the negative gradient flow is given by

(21)

\dot{z}=-\overline{e^{-i\theta}(z^{2}-1)}.

Since

S(p_{-})-S(p_{+})=\frac{4}{3},

the Stokes phases are

\theta_{*}=0\pmod{\pi}.

3.1.
Lefschetz thimbles near the Stokes phase

For a fixed regular phase
\theta
, that is,
\theta\notin\pi\mathbb{Z}
, the shape of the stable and unstable manifolds is determined by two pieces of data: the fact that every flow line is contained in a level set of

G_{\theta}=\operatorname{Im}\bigl(e^{-i\theta}S)

and the local flow directions at each critical point.

•

The stable and unstable manifolds through
p_{-}
and
p_{+}
lie in the level sets

G_{\theta}(p_{-})=-\frac{2}{3}\sin\theta\qquad\text{and}\qquad G_{\theta}(p_{+})=\frac{2}{3}\sin\theta.

•

Linearizing (
21
) at the two critical points gives

\dot{\xi}=2e^{i\theta}\overline{\xi}\qquad\text{at }p_{-}=-1;\qquad\dot{\xi}=-2e^{i\theta}\overline{\xi}\qquad\text{at }p_{+}=1.

Hence locally the flow directions are

\displaystyle\text{at }p_{-}=-1:

\displaystyle\arg\xi=\frac{\theta}{2}\pmod{\pi}\text{ unstable},

\displaystyle\arg\xi=\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{2}\pmod{\pi}\text{ stable},

3.3.
Resurgence and the Stokes matrix

We now run the resurgent analysis for the Airy type. For the two generic phases adjacent to
\theta_{*}=0

in the corresponding adjacent sectors. Since
\mathcal{J}_{+}^{<}
and
\mathcal{J}_{+}^{>}

\mathcal{J}_{-}^{<}
and
\mathcal{J}_{-}^{>}

where

\widetilde{\phi}_{+}(\hbar)=\sum_{m\geq 0}c_{m}\hbar^{m},\qquad\widetilde{\phi}_{-}(\hbar)=\sum_{m\geq 0}(-1)^{m}c_{m}\hbar^{m},\qquad c_{m}=\frac{(6m)!}{576^{m}(2m)!(3m)!}.

z=1-\sqrt{\hbar}\,t
near
p_{+}=1
and
z=-1-i\sqrt{\hbar}\,t
near
p_{-}=-1
, followed by expansion of the cubic correction. We now pass to the Borel plane. The hypergeometric Borel transform written below is the standard explicit form of the Airy saddle expansion; see, for example, the saddle-integral computations in
[
2

3

. For the reduced formal series

we use the ordinary Borel transform

3

In this subsection we use the ordinary Borel transform of the

corresponds in the Borel plane to a universal fractional

\mathcal{B}_{1/2}(\sqrt{\hbar}\,\widetilde{\phi})=\partial_{\xi}\left(\frac{1}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})}\int_{0}^{\xi}(\xi-\eta)^{-1/2}\widehat{\phi}(\eta)\,d\eta\right).

This universal factor has no singularity at any nonzero action difference

\omega

\omega
passes through it;
equivalently, alien derivatives are derivations for the product/convolution

\Delta_{\omega}^{+}(\sqrt{\hbar}\,\widetilde{\phi})=\sqrt{\hbar}\,\Delta_{\omega}^{+}\widetilde{\phi}\qquad(\omega\neq 0).

After restoring the outside Gaussian and exponential factors, the ordinary

\widetilde{I}_{\pm}
.

\widehat{\phi}(\xi):=\sum_{m\geq 0}a_{m}\frac{\xi^{m}}{m!}.

Thus the first Borel singularities occur at the expected action differences. More precisely, there exist holomorphic germs

\displaystyle\dot{\Delta}_{4/3}^{+}\widetilde{I}_{+}

4.
The Bessel model

We now consider the Bessel-type exponential integral

I_{\Gamma}(\hbar)=\int_{\Gamma}e^{-S(z)/\hbar}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z},\qquad S(z)=\frac{1}{2}\left(z-\frac{1}{z}\right),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{*}.

It is convenient to formulate this example on the logarithmic
cylinder. Let

w=\log z,\qquad z=e^{w},

and view
w
as a coordinate on
\mathbb{C}/(2\pi i\mathbb{Z})
.
Then the exponential integral becomes

I_{\Gamma}(\hbar)=\int_{\Gamma}e^{-\sinh w/\hbar}\,\mathrm{d}w.

Remark 4.1
.

We call this the Bessel model because, if one keeps a parameter
x
, then

Y_{\Gamma}(x)=\int_{\Gamma}\exp\left(\frac{x}{2}\left(z-\frac{1}{z}\right)\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z}

is the standard contour integral representation of a Bessel-type solution.
For the unit circle

\Gamma=\{|z|=1\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{*}

using the generating function

\exp\left(\frac{x}{2}\left(z-\frac{1}{z}\right)\right)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}J_{n}(x)z^{n},

one obtains

Y_{\Gamma}(x)=2\pi i\,J_{0}(x).

Hence
Y_{\Gamma}(x)
satisfies the order-zero Bessel equation

x^{2}Y_{\Gamma}^{\prime\prime}(x)+xY_{\Gamma}^{\prime}(x)+x^{2}Y_{\Gamma}(x)=0.

In the present note we specialize to the large-parameter value

x=-1/\hbar
.

The critical points

w_{+}=i\pi/2\quad\text{and}\quad w_{-}=-i\pi/2

are non-degenerate and
the corresponding critical values are

S(w_{+})=\sinh\!\left(\frac{i\pi}{2}\right)=i,\qquad S(w_{-})=\sinh\!\left(-\frac{i\pi}{2}\right)=-i.

Solving the equaiton

\operatorname{Im}(2ie^{-i\theta_{*}})=0

gives the Stokes phases

\theta_{*}=\frac{\pi}{2}\quad\pmod{\pi}.

4.3.
Resurgence and the Stokes matrix

We now run the resurgent analysis for the Bessel type, in parallel with the Airy case.
Since the Stokes phase is
\theta_{*}=\frac{\pi}{2}
, it is convenient to rotate the action by this phase and set

\mathcal{A}(w):=e^{-i\pi/2}S(w)=-\,i\,\sinh w.

Then

\mathcal{A}(w_{+})=1,\qquad\mathcal{A}(w_{-})=-1,\qquad\mathcal{A}(w_{+})-\mathcal{A}(w_{-})=2.

For the two generic phases adjacent to
\theta_{*}=\frac{\pi}{2}
, the four thimble integrals

I_{\mathcal{J}_{+}^{<}}(\hbar),\quad I_{\mathcal{J}_{-}^{<}}(\hbar),\quad I_{\mathcal{J}_{+}^{>}}(\hbar),\quad I_{\mathcal{J}_{-}^{>}}(\hbar)

admit saddle-point expansions for
\hbar\to 0
in the corresponding adjacent sectors. Since

\mathcal{J}_{+}^{<}
and
\mathcal{J}_{+}^{>}
have the same local germ at
w_{+}
, and likewise

\mathcal{J}_{-}^{<}
and
\mathcal{J}_{-}^{>}
at
w_{-}
, there are only two formal objects:

I_{\mathcal{J}_{+}^{\gtrless}}(\hbar)\sim\widetilde{I}_{+}(\hbar),\qquad I_{\mathcal{J}_{-}^{\gtrless}}(\hbar)\sim\widetilde{I}_{-}(\hbar).

With the local orientations fixed as in Figure
8
, one obtains

\widetilde{I}_{+}(\hbar)=\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}\,e^{-1/\hbar}\,\widetilde{\phi}_{+}(\hbar),\qquad\widetilde{I}_{-}(\hbar)=i\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}\,e^{1/\hbar}\,\widetilde{\phi}_{-}(\hbar),

where

\widetilde{\phi}_{+}(\hbar)=\sum_{m\geq 0}(-1)^{m}a_{m}\hbar^{m},\qquad\widetilde{\phi}_{-}(\hbar)=\sum_{m\geq 0}a_{m}\hbar^{m},

and

a_{m}=\frac{((2m-1)!!)^{2}}{m!\,8^{m}}=\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)_{m}^{2}}{2^{m}m!}.

These formulas are obtained by the standard saddle-point substitutions

w=w_{+}+\sqrt{\hbar}\,t\qquad\text{and}\qquad w=w_{-}+i\sqrt{\hbar}\,t,

followed by expansion of
\cosh(\sqrt{\hbar}\,t)
and
\cos(\sqrt{\hbar}\,t)
. By using the ordinary Borel transform similar to that for equation (
3.3
), we have

\widehat{\phi}_{+}(\xi)={}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2};1;-\frac{\xi}{2}\right),\qquad\widehat{\phi}_{-}(\xi)={}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2};1;\frac{\xi}{2}\right).

Hence
\widehat{\phi}_{-}
has its first singularity at
\xi=2
, while
\widehat{\phi}_{+}
has its first singularity at
\xi=-2
. More precisely, there exists a holomorphic germ
H_{-}(\xi)
near
\xi=2
such that

\widehat{\phi}_{-}(\xi)=H_{-}(\xi)-\frac{2i}{2\pi i}\log\!\left(\xi-2\right)\widehat{\phi}_{+}(\xi-2).

Therefore, with the convention of Section
2
for the alien operator,

\Delta_{2}^{+}\widetilde{\phi}_{-}=-\,2i\,\widetilde{\phi}_{+},\qquad\Delta_{2}^{+}\widetilde{\phi}_{+}=0.

Similarly,
\widehat{\phi}_{+}
has its first singularity at
\xi=-2
. More precisely, there exists a holomorphic germ
H_{+}(\xi)
near
\xi=-2
such that

\widehat{\phi}_{+}(\xi)=H_{+}(\xi)-\frac{2i}{2\pi i}\log\!\left(\xi+2\right)\widehat{\phi}_{-}(\xi+2).

Therefore

\Delta_{-2}^{+}\widetilde{\phi}_{+}=-\,2i\,\widetilde{\phi}_{-},\qquad\Delta_{-2}^{+}\widetilde{\phi}_{-}=0.

We thus have

\begin{split}\dot{\Delta}_{2}^{+}\widetilde{I}_{-}&=e^{-2/\hbar}\Delta_{2}^{+}\Bigl(i\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}\,e^{1/\hbar}\widetilde{\phi}_{-}(\hbar)\Bigr)=2\,\widetilde{I}_{+}(\hbar),\qquad\dot{\Delta}_{2}^{+}\widetilde{I}_{+}=0.\\
\dot{\Delta}_{-2}^{+}\widetilde{I}_{+}&=e^{2/\hbar}\Delta_{-2}^{+}\Bigl(\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}\,e^{-1/\hbar}\widetilde{\phi}_{+}(\hbar)\Bigr)=-2\,\widetilde{I}_{-}(\hbar),\qquad\dot{\Delta}_{-2}^{+}\widetilde{I}_{-}=0.\end{split}

Hence, if we write

\widetilde{\mathbf{I}}:=(\widetilde{I}_{+},\widetilde{I}_{-}),

then the Stokes automorphism along the positive Borel ray is

\mathfrak{S}^{+}\widetilde{\mathbf{I}}=\widetilde{\mathbf{I}}\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

By (
13
) and Proposition
2.9
, this gives

\mathbf{I}^{<}=\mathbf{I}^{>}\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{I}^{>}=\mathbf{I}^{<}\begin{pmatrix}1&-2\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

where

\mathbf{I}^{<}:=\bigl(I_{\mathcal{J}_{+}^{<}},I_{\mathcal{J}_{-}^{<}}\bigr),\qquad\mathbf{I}^{>}:=\bigl(I_{\mathcal{J}_{+}^{>}},I_{\mathcal{J}_{-}^{>}}\bigr).

Since the integral is linear in the cycle, the same coefficients govern the wall-crossing of the thimbles:

(\mathcal{J}_{+}^{<}\ \ \mathcal{J}_{-}^{<})=(\mathcal{J}_{+}^{>}\ \ \mathcal{J}_{-}^{>})\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad(\mathcal{J}_{+}^{>}\ \ \mathcal{J}_{-}^{>})=(\mathcal{J}_{+}^{<}\ \ \mathcal{J}_{-}^{<})\begin{pmatrix}1&-2\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

This agrees with (
25
). In particular, the resurgent Stokes coefficient is
2
, exactly matching the two connecting trajectories found in Section
4.2
.

5.
The Gamma model

We now turn to a third example, whose main new feature is that the holomorphic action has infinitely many critical points. Following
[
8
, Appendix C]
, we consider

S(\phi):=e^{\phi}-\phi,\qquad\phi\in\mathbb{C},

and the associated exponential integral

I_{\mathcal{C}}(\hbar):=\int_{\mathcal{C}}e^{-S(\phi)/\hbar}\,d\phi.

Here
\hbar\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
is small and
\mathcal{C}
is an oriented contour in the
\phi
-plane.

Remark 5.1
.

For the distinguished contour
\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{R}
and
\hbar>0
, one has

I_{\mathbb{R}}(\hbar)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-(e^{\phi}-\phi)/\hbar}\,d\phi=\hbar^{1/\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\frac{1}{\hbar}-1}e^{-t}\,dt=\hbar^{1/\hbar}\Gamma(1/\hbar),

where we use the change of variables
t=e^{\phi}/\hbar
. Thus, up to the elementary prefactor
\hbar^{1/\hbar}
, the present model is exactly the Gamma function.

The critical points of
S(\phi)
are

\{p_{n}:=2\pi in\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\},

which are all non-degenerate. The corresponding critical values are

S_{n}:=S(p_{n})=1-2\pi in,

so the critical values form an infinite arithmetic progression in the imaginary direction.

For any two distinct critical points
p_{n}
and
p_{m}
, one has

S_{n}-S_{m}=-2\pi i(n-m).

It follows that the Stokes phases are

\theta_{*}=\pm\frac{\pi}{2}.

The case
\theta_{*}=-\frac{\pi}{2}
is obtained from the case

\theta_{*}=\frac{\pi}{2}
by reversing the relevant ordering of the critical values, so below we analyze
\theta_{*}=\frac{\pi}{2}
in detail.

5.1.
Lefschetz thimbles near the Stokes phase

In real coordinates
\phi=x+iy
,
S(\phi)
reads

S(\phi)=e^{x}\cos y-x+i(e^{x}\sin y-y),

hence

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,y)

\displaystyle=e^{x}\cos(y-\theta)-x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta,

\displaystyle G_{\theta}(x,y)

\displaystyle=e^{x}\sin(y-\theta)+x\sin\theta-y\cos\theta.

With respect to the standard hermitian metric, the negative gradient flow is

\begin{cases}\dot{x}=-e^{x}\cos(y-\theta)+\cos\theta,\\[2.84544pt]
\dot{y}=\,e^{x}\sin(y-\theta)+\sin\theta.\end{cases}

Now we study the Lefschetz thimble and its dual thimble attached to the critical point
p_{n}=2\pi in
.

•

The stable and unstable manifolds through
p_{n}
are the connected components of the level set

G_{\theta}(x,y)=e^{x}\sin(y-\theta)+x\sin\theta-y\cos\theta=-\sin\theta-2\pi n\cos\theta

selected by the local stable and unstable directions.

•

Linearizing the flow equation at
p_{n}
, we obtain

\dot{\eta}=-e^{i\theta}\overline{\eta}\qquad(\phi\sim p_{n}+\eta),

the local stable and unstable directions are

\text{stable:}\quad\arg\eta=\frac{\theta}{2}\pmod{\pi},\qquad\text{unstable:}\quad\arg\eta=\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\pi}{2}\pmod{\pi}.

Remark 5.2
.

This local picture of
p_{m}
is the same as the one of
p_{n}
, up to vertical translation by
2\pi(n-m)
. This is due to the translation invairance of the flow equation under

.

It remains to identify the ends of these branches. We have the following result.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let

\theta_{-}:=\frac{\pi}{2}-\delta,\qquad\theta_{+}:=\frac{\pi}{2}+\delta,\qquad 0<\delta\ll 1.

For
p_{n}=2\pi in
, the stable and unstable manifolds have the following ends.

(1)

For

, the asymptotics are given by

W^{s}_{\theta_{-}}(p_{n}):\quad\begin{cases}x\to-\infty,\quad y=x\tan\theta_{-}+\tan\theta_{-}+2\pi n+o(1),\\[2.84544pt]

and

W^{u}_{\theta_{-}}(p_{n}):\quad\begin{cases}x\to+\infty,\quad y=\theta_{-}-\pi+2\pi n+O(xe^{-x}),\\[2.84544pt]
x\to+\infty,\quad y=\theta_{-}+\pi+2\pi n+O(xe^{-x}).\end{cases}

(2)

For
\theta=\theta_{+}
, the asymptotics are given by

W^{s}_{\theta_{+}}(p_{n}):\quad\begin{cases}x\to+\infty,\quad y=\theta_{+}+2\pi n+O(xe^{-x}),\\[2.84544pt]
x\to+\infty,\quad y=\theta_{+}+2\pi(n-1)+O(xe^{-x}),\end{cases}

and

W^{u}_{\theta_{+}}(p_{n}):\quad\begin{cases}x\to-\infty,\quad y=x\tan\theta_{+}+\tan\theta_{+}+2\pi n+o(1),\\[2.84544pt]
x\to+\infty,\quad y=\theta_{+}-\pi+2\pi n+O(xe^{-x}).\end{cases}

Proof.

The case
x\rightarrow+\infty
:

Fix
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Along the stable and unstable manifolds of

p_{n}=2\pi in
, the function
G_{\theta}
is constant and equal to

G_{\theta}(p_{n})
. Hence the relevant level equation is

(26)

H_{\theta,n}(x,y):=G_{\theta}(x,y)-G_{\theta}(p_{n})=e^{x}\sin(y-\theta)+(x+1)\sin\theta-(y-2\pi n)\cos\theta=0.

Note that for our
\theta=\theta^{\pm}

H_{\theta,n}(x,2\pi n)=\sin\theta(1+x-e^{x})\leq 0,

with equality only at
x=0
, while for
x>0
,
\theta
sufficiently close to
\pi/2
,

H_{\theta,n}(x,2\pi n+\theta)>0,\qquad H_{\theta,n}(0,y)>0\quad(2\pi n<y<2\pi n+\theta).

Hence the branch leaving
p_{n}
in the right-up stable direction cannot leave the region

x>0,\quad 2\pi n<y<2\pi n+\theta.

Which means, for
\theta=\theta_{\pm}

and
0<\delta\ll 1
, the signs of
H_{\theta,n}
on the neighboring
horizontal lines give barriers. Hence each right-going half-branch remains,
for
x\geq 0
, in one fixed horizontal strip. In particular
y=O(1)

along that right end. Hence
(
26
) gives

e^{x}\sin(y-\theta)=-(x+1)\sin\theta+(y-2\pi n)\cos\theta=O(x).

Thus

\sin(y-\theta)=O(xe^{-x}).

Consequently, on any such right end,
y-\theta
must approach a zero of

\sin
, and hence there exists
k\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

y=\theta+k\pi+\varepsilon(x),\qquad\varepsilon(x)\to 0\qquad(x\to+\infty).

Substituting this form into (
26
), we obtain

e^{x}\sin(k\pi+\varepsilon)+(x+1)\sin\theta-(\theta+k\pi+\varepsilon-2\pi n)\cos\theta=0.

Since

\sin(k\pi+\varepsilon)=(-1)^{k}\sin\varepsilon=(-1)^{k}\left(\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{3})\right),

the equation becomes

\bigl((-1)^{k}e^{x}-\cos\theta\bigr)\varepsilon+(x+1)\sin\theta-(\theta+k\pi-2\pi n)\cos\theta+O(e^{x}\varepsilon^{3})=0.

The preliminary estimate
\sin(y-\theta)=O(xe^{-x})
gives

\varepsilon(x)=O(xe^{-x}),

and hence

e^{x}\varepsilon^{3}=O(x^{3}e^{-2x}).

Solving the previous equation for
\varepsilon
, and using

\frac{1}{(-1)^{k}e^{x}-\cos\theta}=(-1)^{k}e^{-x}+O(e^{-2x}),

we obtain

\varepsilon(x)=(-1)^{k}e^{-x}\left(-(x+1)\sin\theta+(\theta+k\pi-2\pi n)\cos\theta\right)+O(x^{2}e^{-2x}).

Therefore every right end of
H_{\theta,n}=0
in the
k

(27)

y=\theta+k\pi+(-1)^{k}e^{-x}\left(-(x+1)\sin\theta+(\theta+k\pi-2\pi n)\cos\theta\right)+O(x^{2}e^{-2x}).

In particular,

y=\theta+k\pi+O(xe^{-x}),\qquad x\to+\infty.

It remains to identify whether such a right end is stable or unstable. Along
(
27
), we have

F_{\theta}(x,y)=e^{x}\cos(y-\theta)-x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta.

Since
y-\theta=k\pi+\varepsilon(x)
and
\varepsilon(x)=O(xe^{-x})
,

\cos(y-\theta)=\cos(k\pi+\varepsilon)=(-1)^{k}+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

Therefore

F_{\theta}(x,y)=(-1)^{k}e^{x}+O(x).

Hence, if
k
is even, then

F_{\theta}(x,y)\to+\infty,

so this is a stable, rapid-decay end. If
k
is odd, then

F_{\theta}(x,y)\to-\infty,

so this is an unstable end.

Thus the stable right ends are precisely the even horizontal asymptotic lines

y=\theta+2\pi\ell+O(xe^{-x}),\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{Z},

whereas the unstable right ends are precisely the odd horizontal asymptotic
lines

y=\theta+(2\ell+1)\pi+O(xe^{-x}),\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{Z}.

Specializing these alternatives to the two adjacent strips containing the
branches issuing from
p_{n}
gives exactly the
x\to+\infty
endpoint
statements in this proposition.

The case
x\rightarrow-\infty
:

Fix the critical point
p_{n}=2\pi in
. As before, the stable and unstable
manifolds through
p_{n}
are contained in the level set (
26
).

We now consider the left end, namely an end on which

. Since

\theta=\theta_{\pm}=\frac{\pi}{2}\pm\delta
with
0<\delta\ll 1
, we have

\cos\theta\neq 0
. Solving (

) for

The last term is

, because

is bounded and

\theta
is fixed away from

by the small nonzero parameter

. Therefore

In particular,

y=x\tan\theta+\tan\theta+2\pi n+o(1),\qquad x\to-\infty.

This proves the claimed left-end asymptotic line.

For completeness, let us also record the corresponding asymptotic behavior of

F_{\theta}
, since this determines whether the left end is stable or unstable.
Along the left end, (
28
) gives

y=x\tan\theta+\tan\theta+2\pi n+O(e^{x}).

Substituting this into

we obtain

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,y)

\displaystyle=e^{x}\cos(y-\theta)-x\cos\theta-\left(x\tan\theta+\tan\theta+2\pi n+O(e^{x})\right)\sin\theta

\displaystyle=-x\left(\cos\theta+\frac{\sin^{2}\theta}{\cos\theta}\right)+O(1)=-\frac{x}{\cos\theta}+O(1).

Hence, as

and

) or as the nearest-neighbor relation
(
33
).

Following the general rule explained in Section
2
, direct connecting
trajectories should be read from the elementary, or nearest-neighbor, part
of the Stokes matrix. In the Gamma model, the finite-difference relation
(
33
) isolates precisely this
nearest-neighbor part: with the present ordering, it gives one elementary
entry coupling
\mathcal{J}_{n-1}
to
\mathcal{J}_{n}
. Hence the corresponding direct
Picard–Lefschetz trajectory is

p_{n-1}\longrightarrow p_{n},

with signed coefficient
1
. This agrees with
Proposition
5.4
.

The full triangular relation (
32
)
contains more information, but its non-nearest-neighbor entries should be
interpreted as broken-line contributions. For example, on a finite block

(\mathcal{J}_{m},\mathcal{J}_{m+1},\ldots,\mathcal{J}_{n})
, the full Stokes matrix has the form

\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&\cdots&1\\
0&1&1&\cdots&1\\
0&0&1&\cdots&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}.

The nearest-neighbor entries of this matrix are the elementary coefficients, and hence
encode the direct trajectories

p_{r}\longrightarrow p_{r+1}.

The entries farther above the diagonal are obtained by multiplying these
elementary nearest-neighbor matrices. Thus the entry relating
\mathcal{J}_{m}
to

\mathcal{J}_{n}
, with
m<n
, records the broken chain

p_{m}\longrightarrow p_{m+1}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow p_{n},

not a new direct trajectory from
p_{m}
to
p_{n}
. This is the
broken-line interpretation used in
[
8
, Appendix C]
.

5.3.
Resurgence and the Stokes matrix

We now compute the resurgent data for the Gamma model. Since the Stokes phase under consideration is
\frac{\pi}{2}
, we rotate the action by this phase and set

\mathcal{A}(\phi):=e^{-i\pi/2}S(\phi)=-iS(\phi).

We study the integrals

I_{n}^{<}(\hbar):=\int_{\mathcal{J}_{n}^{<}}e^{-\mathcal{A}(\phi)/\hbar}\,d\phi,\qquad I_{n}^{>}(\hbar):=\int_{\mathcal{J}_{n}^{>}}e^{-\mathcal{A}(\phi)/\hbar}\,d\phi,

where now
\hbar>0
is real and small. The critical points are still

p_{n}=2\pi in,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},

and their rotated critical values are

A_{n}:=\mathcal{A}(p_{n})=-iS(p_{n})=-i-2\pi n.

Now we show that, as
\hbar\to 0^{+}
,

I_{n}^{\lessgtr}(\hbar)\sim\widetilde{I}_{n}(\hbar):=e^{-A_{n}/\hbar}\,e^{i\pi/4}\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}\,\widetilde{\phi}_{\Gamma}(\hbar),\qquad\widetilde{\phi}_{\Gamma}(\hbar)=\widetilde{\phi}_{\mathrm{St}}(i\hbar).

Here

\widetilde{\phi}_{\mathrm{St}}(\hbar)=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{B_{2k}}{2k(2k-1)}\hbar^{2k-1}\right).

Indeed, for
n=0
, the reduced asymptotic series is fixed by the classical Gamma-function asymptotics. The classical Stirling expansion, written directly in the small parameter
\hbar
, is

\Gamma(1/\hbar)\sim\sqrt{2\pi}\,\hbar^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\hbar}}e^{-1/\hbar}\widetilde{\phi}_{\mathrm{St}}(\hbar).

Therefore by (
5.1
),

I_{\mathbb{R}}(\hbar)\sim e^{-1/\hbar}\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}\,\widetilde{\phi}_{\mathrm{St}}(\hbar).

We have

I_{0}^{\lessgtr}(\hbar)\sim\widetilde{I}_{0}(\hbar)=e^{-1/i\hbar}\sqrt{2\pi i\hbar}\,\widetilde{\phi}_{\mathrm{St}}(i\hbar)=e^{-A_{0}/\hbar}\,e^{i\pi/4}\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}\,\widetilde{\phi}_{\Gamma}(\hbar).

For general
n
, the saddle-point method and the vertical translation symmetry show that the series (except the exponential part) does not change from one saddle to another. Indeed, the translation
\phi\mapsto\phi+2\pi in
sends the saddle
p_{0}
to
p_{n}
, and changes
\mathcal{A}
only by the constant
-2\pi n
. Hence it only changes the exponential factor
e^{-A_{n}/\hbar}
, while the Gaussian phase and the reduced series remain the same:

I_{n}^{\lessgtr}(\hbar)\sim\widetilde{I}_{n}(\hbar)=e^{-A_{n}/\hbar}\,e^{i\pi/4}\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}\,\widetilde{\phi}_{\Gamma}(\hbar).

We now pass to the Borel plane. We use the known alien calculus for the Stirling series in the book
[
15
]
(Formula (6.99)):

\Delta^{+}_{2\pi im}\widetilde{\phi}_{\mathrm{St}}(\hbar)=\begin{cases}\widetilde{\phi}_{\mathrm{St}}(\hbar),&m=-1,1,2,3,\ldots,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Since

\widetilde{\phi}_{\Gamma}(\hbar)=\widetilde{\phi}_{\mathrm{St}}(i\hbar),

the Borel singularities are rotated from
2\pi im
to
2\pi m
. Hence

(34)

\Delta^{+}_{2\pi m}\widetilde{\phi}_{\Gamma}(\hbar)=\begin{cases}\widetilde{\phi}_{\Gamma}(\hbar),&m=-1,1,2,3,\ldots,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Now recall that

\widetilde{I}_{n}(\hbar)=e^{-A_{n}/\hbar}e^{i\pi/4}\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}\,\widetilde{\phi}_{\Gamma}(\hbar),\qquad A_{n}=-i-2\pi n.

Using
A_{n-m}=A_{n}+2\pi m
, (
34
) gives

(35)

\dot{\Delta}^{+}_{2\pi m}\widetilde{I}_{n}(\hbar)=e^{-2\pi m/\hbar}\Delta^{+}_{2\pi m}\widetilde{I}_{n}(\hbar)=\begin{cases}\widetilde{I}_{n-m}(\hbar),&m=-1,1,2,3,\ldots,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For the wall-crossing at
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
, the relevant Stokes ray in the rotated Borel plane is the positive real ray. Therefore only the singularities
2\pi m
with
m\geq 1
contribute. We obtain

\mathfrak{S}^{+}\widetilde{I}_{n}=\widetilde{I}_{n}+\sum_{m\geq 1}\widetilde{I}_{n-m}=\sum_{\ell\leq n}\widetilde{I}_{\ell}.

Equivalently, if
T\widetilde{I}_{n}=\widetilde{I}_{n-1}
, then

\mathfrak{S}^{+}=1+T+T^{2}+\cdots.

Remark 5.5
.

Taking the logarithm of the Stokes automorphism gives

\log\mathfrak{S}^{+}=-\log(1-T)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{T^{k}}{k}.

By (
19
),

\log\mathfrak{S}^{+}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\dot{\Delta}_{2\pi k}
, we have
\dot{\Delta}_{2\pi k}=\frac{1}{k}T^{k}
, and

\dot{\Delta}_{2\pi k}\widetilde{I}_{n}=\frac{1}{k}\widetilde{I}_{n-k},\qquad\Delta_{2\pi k}\widetilde{I}_{n}=\frac{1}{k}\widetilde{I}_{n}.

This is the computation of alien derivation
\Delta_{2\pi k}
in Gamma model.

The inverse Stokes automorphism is

(36)

\mathfrak{S}^{-}=(\mathfrak{S}^{+})^{-1}=1-T.

Equivalently,

\mathfrak{S}^{-}\widetilde{I}_{n}=\widetilde{I}_{n}-\widetilde{I}_{n-1}.

Thus the two Stokes automorphisms act on the formal objects by

\mathfrak{S}^{+}\widetilde{I}_{n}=\sum_{m\leq n}\widetilde{I}_{m},\qquad\mathfrak{S}^{-}\widetilde{I}_{n}=\widetilde{I}_{n}-\widetilde{I}_{n-1}.

By the general relation between lateral Borel sums and Stokes automorphisms,

\mathcal{S}^{<}=\mathcal{S}^{>}\circ\mathfrak{S}^{+},\qquad\mathcal{S}^{>}=\mathcal{S}^{<}\circ\mathfrak{S}^{-},

we obtain

I_{n}^{<}=\sum_{m\leq n}I_{m}^{>},

and conversely

I_{n}^{>}=I_{n}^{<}-I_{n-1}^{<}.

Equivalently, if the formal series are ordered by decreasing index,

\cdots,\widetilde{I}_{2},\widetilde{I}_{1},\widetilde{I}_{0},\widetilde{I}_{-1},\cdots,

then
\mathfrak{S}^{+}
is represented by the upper triangular matrix

R_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}\ddots&&&&\\
&1&1&1&\cdots\\
&0&1&1&\cdots\\
&0&0&1&\cdots\\
&&&&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

while
\mathfrak{S}^{-}
is represented by the inverse upper bidiagonal matrix

R_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}\ddots&&&&\\
&1&-1&0&\cdots\\
&0&1&-1&\cdots\\
&0&0&1&\cdots\\
&&&&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

By Proposition
2.9
, the matrix relations obtained
from the resurgent Stokes automorphisms translate directly into the
wall-crossing relations for the Lefschetz thimbles. Thus

[\mathcal{J}_{n}^{<}]=\sum_{m\leq n}[\mathcal{J}_{m}^{>}],

and

[\mathcal{J}_{n}^{>}]=[\mathcal{J}_{n}^{<}]-[\mathcal{J}_{n-1}^{<}].

These are precisely the two Picard–Lefschetz wall-crossing formulas
obtained above in (
32
) and
(
33
). Hence the resurgent Stokes
automorphisms recover the same jump formula.

5.4.
Hopf-algebraic interpretation

We finally interpret the Gamma computation in the Hopf-algebraic language of
Subsection
2.6
and explain Table
2
. In the normalization used above, the
relevant Stokes ray is

d=\mathbb{R}_{>0},

and the corresponding semigroup is

\Lambda_{d}=2\pi\mathbb{N}^{\ast}.

By (
35
), the pointed alien operators act on the
formal objects in Gamma model by

(37)

\dot{\Delta}_{2\pi k}^{+}\widetilde{I}_{n}=\widetilde{I}_{n-k},\qquad k\geq 1.

Therefore the product in the Hopf algebra, which is operator composition,
becomes the composition of these shifts. For
a,b\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
,

\dot{\Delta}_{2\pi a}^{+}\dot{\Delta}_{2\pi b}^{+}\widetilde{I}_{n}=\dot{\Delta}_{2\pi a}^{+}\widetilde{I}_{n-b}=\widetilde{I}_{n-a-b}=\dot{\Delta}_{2\pi(a+b)}^{+}\widetilde{I}_{n}.

In particular,

\dot{\Delta}_{4\pi}^{+}\widetilde{I}_{n}=(\dot{\Delta}_{2\pi}^{+})^{2}\widetilde{I}_{n}=\widetilde{I}_{n-2}.

Thus the Stokes-matrix entry which shifts the sector by two steps is the
product of two nearest-neighbor shifts. On the Picard–Lefschetz
side, this is interpreted as the broken chain

p_{n-2}\longrightarrow p_{n-1}\longrightarrow p_{n},

rather than as a new primitive trajectory from
p_{n-2}
to
p_{n}
.

The coproduct records a different operation. For example,

\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}(\dot{\Delta}_{4\pi}^{+})=\dot{\Delta}_{4\pi}^{+}\otimes 1+1\otimes\dot{\Delta}_{4\pi}^{+}+\dot{\Delta}_{2\pi}^{+}\otimes\dot{\Delta}_{2\pi}^{+}.

This should be interpreted through product thimbles. If two Gamma thimble
integrals are multiplied, the corresponding cycle is the product thimble

\mathcal{J}_{a}\times\mathcal{J}_{b}
. The total action difference
4\pi
may then be split
between the two factors as

(4\pi,0),\qquad(0,4\pi),\qquad(2\pi,2\pi).

The three terms correspond respectively to a jump in the first factor, a
jump in the second factor, and simultaneous nearest-neighbor jumps in both
factors. Thus the coproduct encodes the product rule for Stokes
automorphisms on product thimbles, rather than another broken chain in the
same one-dimensional Gamma system.

Finally, the antipode gives the inverse Stokes transformation already computed
in (
36
):

\mathsf{S}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathfrak{S}^{+})=(\mathfrak{S}^{+})^{-1}=\mathfrak{S}^{-}.
