Self-deploying reinforced concrete structures
Authors: Ezra Ben-Abu, Anna Zigelman, Hod Lipson, Amir D. Gat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Formwork for concrete construction remains one of the most resource-intensive, polluting, and constraining components of the construction sector, relying on prefabricated molds that demand extensive material, labor, and on-site preparation. We introduce a self-deploying formwork system based on multistable tubes that transform from a compact configuration to a full-scale structural mold with integrated steel reinforcement. We demonstrate this concept by deploying a steel-reinforced 2.36 m high structure within 14 seconds. We further observed that the polymer shell of the multistable tubes increases the structural strength by a factor of three, significantly enhancing their load-bearing capacity. The demonstrated concept paves the way for the creation of concrete castings with minimal on-site intervention, enabling a scalable pathway toward rapid, low-carbon construction methods.

Introduction

The reduction of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions is one of the most challenging and pressing missions humanity faces today. The construction industry alone accounts for 37% of total GHG emissions1, with nearly one-third of these emissions coming from the Cross-Laminated Timber (CLT) used in formwork for casting concrete columns, beams, and floors2–4. Additionally, up to 75% of building construction time is devoted to formwork5, and the associated material and labor costs may comprise 35–60% of total building expenses6. Therefore, finding alternative construction solutions that do not rely on CLT formwork could significantly reduce global GHG emissions and shorten construction times and costs, making these solutions more environmentally friendly and cost-effective. Concrete construction is typically performed using either cast-in-place methods, which rely on on-site formwork5,6, or precast methods, where elements are manufactured off-site and assembled on-site7,8. While precast construction reduces on-site time, it introduces transportation and lifting constraints, whereas cast-in-place construction remains highly dependent on formwork.

Several formwork methods have been developed as alternatives to CLT formwork, including metal formwork9–11, which can be also composed of corrugated metallic plates wrapped in helical patterns that enhance shear and axial resistance12–16, fabric formwork17–20, CNC-milled styrofoam formwork21,22, 3D-printed plastic formwork23–25, mesh mold formwork26–28, sand formwork29–31, ice formwork32–34, and climbing formwork systems designed to improve construction efficiency in repetitive vertical structures35–37. The selection of the specific formwork type depends on factors including expected lifespan, geometric complexity, and construction conditions (e.g., weather)10. The implementation of such systems in modern architecture often necessitates strategic adaptations, including pre-defined concrete pour points to prevent high-drop casting and mitigate segregation38–40, or designing formwork to be reused across floors and even between buildings41–44. These strategies help streamline on-site preparation and reduce the amount of cross-laminated timber (CLT) required in each project. However, conventional formwork systems face significant limitations, as they are inherently time consuming for standard geometries, and become particularly inefficient when applied to complex configurations. In such cases, they require highly customized designs that are difficult to implement, further increasing construction time and cost.

More recently, construction techniques that do not rely on any formwork have emerged. One of the most recognized methods is the 3D printing of small houses45–49, for which reinforcement solutions have also been proposed50–54. Another approach involves the use of pneumatic actuation to inflate the entire reinforced concrete shell of a structure, followed by concrete spraying after inflation55–58. Additional methods have been developed for creating complex structures without concrete, using deployable systems that can be inflated with fluids59–61, deployed manually62–64, or supported by polyurethane foam65–67, which solidifies upon drying and provides structural stability. These structures are typically based on inflatable structures such as origami-based structures68–73 or thin-walled corrugated tubes74–77. In spite of the aforementioned advances, the existing approaches rely on external actuation, lack integration between formwork and structural function, or are limited in scalability and structural performance, making it imperative for a method that can deploy rapidly and maintain structural integrity while maintaining geometric flexibility.

In this study, we present a self-deploying formwork concept for reinforced concrete structures. Specifically, we investigate whether shape-morphing interconnected multistable tubes can (i) rapidly deploy into predefined structural configurations, (ii) maintain sufficient structural performance after concrete casting and curing, and (iii) provide practical advantages over conventional formwork approaches in terms of construction efficiency and sustainability. This method relies on shape-morphing interconnected multistable tubes, guided either by director field theory60 to achieve complex geometries, or by a constrained ceiling, where columns expand due to gravity to form more conventional 3D shapes. We experimentally demonstrate the deployment of such structures in the meter scale in less than a minute. Such rapid deployment does not only accelerate the construction process, but also minimizes concrete segregation, which simplifies the casting procedure. To ensure structural integrity, we incorporate flexible steel cables to accommodate elastic deformation during deployment and use helical tube designs, that improve shear and axial resistance. We also derive a theoretical model for the final configuration based on the amount of concrete and the initial conditions. In addition, experimental measurements showed that the polymer shell surrounding the multistable tubes increases the structural strength of the final reinforced concrete element by a factor of three, substantially enhancing its load-bearing capacity without adding significant weight.

Results

As illustrated in Fig. 1, our approach consists of the following stages. First, a compact, closed structure is assembled in a factory with integrated steel reinforcement, according to a predefined requirements, which refer to the desired final geometry of the structure. Specifically, based on the target geometry, the arrangement of the multistable tubes and the placement of the connectors are determined to ensure that the structure deploys into the intended configuration. The steel cables are incorporated as part of the reinforcement system, providing the necessary tensile strength for the cured concrete. The structure is then delivered and installed at the construction location. Next, concrete is poured by a concrete pump, which then pours it into the structure that is automatically self-deployed due to gravity. Note that although in the present study, we used a water-cement mixture, the proposed method is fully compatible with self-compacting concrete. Note that, in centimeter-scale cases, we use an external pressure to achieve the deployment, since at smaller scales, gravity is not sufficiently dominant. However, in the meter-scale experiment, the gravity is very dominant and the structure has the ability to self-deploy without the need for any external forces.

In particular, Fig. 3A illustrates the load stability of the centimeter-scale spherical structure by showing in (A1) its ability to support a 95 kg man standing and jumping on top of it. In panels (A2)-(A4), we demonstrate the jumping test, where the maximum jumping height in (A3) was 15 cm (for the jumping test, see Movie S1). Figure 3B presents the experimental setup for both compression and buckling tests under axial loading. The graphs in Fig. 3C1–C3 show the applied axial load versus the displacement of the tested columns in various cases, including buckling and compression for helical folding tubes (HFTs) and conical frusta tubes (CFTs) (sheathed and bare), each measurement repeated 3 times. The options “sheathed” and “bare” refer to the tested columns in which the thin plastic shell was left as is and removed after concrete solidification, respectively. To minimize imperfections in the tested columns and to ensure loading conditions that approximate pure compression and buckling, we selected slenderness ratios of l/d = 1 and l/d = 12, respectively, where l denotes the column length and d is the outer diameter of the tested columns. All measurements were performed four weeks after the concrete casting. The tests were conducted under displacement-controlled conditions, with a loading rate of 3 mm/min. For the bare specimens, the tests were continued until failure. For the sheathed specimens, the tests were terminated upon reaching a plateau in the load-deformation response. At this stage, the internal concrete was already significantly crushed and confined by the polymer shell, such that the measured response no longer reflected the true load-bearing capacity of the structural element. The solid curves in Fig. 3C1–C3 represent the mean result over 3 experiments and the shaded areas correspond to the regions between minimum and maximum of these measurements. It is possible to see that the sheathed samples are approximately three times stronger than the bare samples and that the HFT samples are approximately twice as strong as the CFT samples, suggesting that sheathed HFTs provide the best performance for reinforced concrete columns. The observed increase in load-bearing capacity of sheathed samples can be qualitatively attributed to a confinement effect. In the unconfined case, the concrete fails once a critical displacement is reached, associated with crack initiation and localized deformation. When enclosed by the polymer shell, lateral deformation is constrained, limiting the displacement modes that lead to failure. This confinement delays crack development and failure, allowing the structure to sustain higher loads. Thus, the polymer shell does not increase the intrinsic material strength, but rather delays the failure mechanism, resulting in an enhanced load-bearing capacity. Discussion

First, let us compare the proposed deployment method with the construction methods mentioned in the Introduction. Specifically, the proposed method differs from the existing approaches such as 3D printed construction, pneumatic membrane based systems, and advanced formwork techniques used in the construction sector. While these methods rely on external processes such as layer-by-layer deposition, air pressure, or rigid molds to define the geometry, the present approach achieves shape morphing through the deployment of a multistable structure during casting. This enables rapid transformation from a compact configuration to the final geometry while maintaining control over the structure throughout the process. Moreover, in the current study, the structure is deployed onto a temporary support system that can be easily removed after curing. In future work, the system can be designed such that the multistability of the tubes, together with the connection design, provides sufficient stability to maintain the structural configuration throughout the curing process. This would eliminate the need for external support by a suspension system such as a temporary stand or a crane after the deployment is complete.

Note that several additional aspects will be investigated in future work, as discussed below. A more detailed analysis of the achievable tolerances, as well as the design of connections and joints will be performed. These studies will define the acceptable ranges that must be maintained and will ultimately determine the required tolerances for a manufacturing process. Once these tolerances and design constraints are established, it will be necessary to ensure that the activation threshold of the multistable tubes remains significantly lower than the forces applied by the concrete during casting. This will ensure reliable deployment of the structure, even in the presence of variability between different tubes. Moreover, in order to meet the fire safety requirement, in future implementations, we will replace the flammable polymer tubes with metallic multistable tubes, which could also serve as an additional structural reinforcement component. Furthermore, this replacement to metallic multistable tubes will allow to maintain load-bearing capacity for larger diameters.

Next, let us discuss the expected environmental and economic advantages of the proposed system. As cited in the Introduction, conventional formwork typically accounts for approximately 35–60% of total construction cost and up to 75% of construction time, while also contributing significantly to material waste and carbon emissions due to the extensive use of temporary molds. Contrary, the proposed system eliminates the need for external formwork by integrating the formwork function into the deployable structure itself. This leads to several key advantages: a substantial reduction in material (mainly CLT molds) usage, a significant decrease in labor and assembly time due to rapid deployment (seconds to minutes instead of hours to days), and reduced environmental impact associated with formwork production, transportation, decreasing the need for trees harvesting, and disposal.

The reported cost and CO2 emission values in Table 1 represent first-order estimates based solely on material consumption and production. Costs associated with transportation, installation, labor, maintenance, reuse, and end-of-life processing were not considered. Based on material costs alone, the proposed PP-based formwork used for concrete is estimated to cost approximately 11 USD/m3, compared with approximately 193 USD/m3 for conventional CLT formwork used for concrete. Furthermore, conventional formwork systems typically require additional supplies and substantial on-site labor for assembly, installation, and removal. In the reference study of conventional formwork used for concrete6, the total formwork cost, including supplies and labor, reached approximately 1710 USD/m3. By contrast, the proposed self-deploying formwork requires minimal on-site formwork operations, suggesting additional potential cost savings beyond the material costs considered here. For the construction-time estimate, the conventional formwork value was normalized from a reported 43.3 h formwork-installation task performed by four workers for 13.6 m3 of concrete6, corresponding to approximately 12.7 labor-h/m3 for concrete. Finally, let us discuss possible failure scenarios during the deployment process. Specifically, asymmetric filling may lead to uneven deployment of different tubes. This failure can be mitigated by maintaining a controlled and continuous inflow, which promotes a stable propagation of the filling front. The geometric constraints of the structure further help to guide the deployment properly. Local blockage failure may lead to air pocket regions which can affect the overall structural strength. The occurrence of this failure may be significantly reduced by using sufficiently large tube diameters and appropriate concrete mixtures with high workability. The current deployment model does not explicitly account for the rheological behavior of fresh concrete, including viscosity and yield-stress effects. Nevertheless, the deployment process occurs on a timescale of seconds, whereas concrete setting and significant rheological evolution typically occur over substantially longer timescales. A premature setting of the concrete failure is not expected to occur at all. This is because the deployment process timescale is much shorter than the typical setting time of the concrete. In our meter-scale experiments, the structure fully deploys within seconds, while concrete setting occurs over much longer timescales. Therefore, the system reaches its final configuration well before any significant stiffening takes place.

Conclusions

In this study, we demonstrated that shape-morphing interconnected multistable tubes can serve as self-deploying formwork for reinforced concrete structures. We showed that such structures can rapidly deploy into predefined configurations, maintain structural performance after casting and curing, and provide practical advantages over conventional formwork approaches. We further demonstrated the feasibility of deploying both traditional structural configurations, such as rectangular box-shaped structures, and more complex nontraditional geometries, including conical and spherical configurations. The proposed design enables rapid deployment of reinforced concrete structures through a single inlet, while its self-leveling nature enhances robustness and adaptability to various terrains.

Here, we presented experimental demonstration for the deployment of up to meter-scale structures of various shapes, where all experiments lasted less than a minute.

Furthermore, our self-deploying formwork method minimizes segregation effects due to the expansion of the multistable tubes during deployment, thus simplifying the design of the concrete pouring procedure relative to the traditional formwork methods. Moreover, in order to ensure structural strength, we employed the following two strategies. First, we installed flexible steel cables, which allow complex elastic distortions during the deployment of the structure, thus providing additional reinforcement beyond the outer reinforcement of the multistable tubes. Second, we used helical multistable tubes, which, according to refs. 14–16, provide a better shear and axial resistance of the reinforced concrete columns. Furthermore, we discovered that a thin plastic shell of the multistable tubes increases the structural strength of the structure by a factor of three, substantially enhancing its load-bearing capacity without adding significant weight. In addition, we proposed a theoretical model and compared the simulation and experimental results, which yielded a good agreement. Thus, our concept is accompanied by a validated theoretical tool, which should be capable of predicting stability and final configuration as a function of the initial setup.

The structures demonstrated here are still relatively small compared to typical buildings. Additional research will be needed to establish the viability of this approach to structures tens of meters height. Similarly, additional research is needed to understand the durability of the proposed method under dynamic loading, as well as alternative sheathing and reinforcement materials. Note that since the concrete that will be used in real building systems is the same as used in standard construction techniques, our methodology is expected to sustain at least the same load-bearing capacity as the traditional buildings. The present study does not address the response of meter-scale constructions under lateral loads, and investigating such behavior remains an important direction for future work. The applicability of the proposed methodology to multi-story buildings has not been established in the present study and remains conceptual at this stage. Future work should investigate the effects of structural height on stiffness characteristics, potential buckling limits, and overall structural stability in multi-story configurations. An additional limitation of the current implementation is the use of single-use polymer tubes, whose environmental impact has not yet been fully assessed. A comprehensive life cycle assessment will be required in future work to evaluate the overall sustainability of the proposed approach and to explore alternative materials or reusable configurations. Moreover, future work will address inter-floor connections, reinforcement continuity, and overall structural stability. Similarly, constraints related to architectural features, such as windows and doors, will be addressed through careful design of the structural layout.

Beyond technical advances, our work has the potential to redefine the construction methodology of reinforced concrete structures, particularly in situations requiring rapid and scalable assembly, such as large-scale infrastructure projects. Overall, our approach offers a more adaptable, sustainable, and cost-effective model for future architecture compared to 3D printed construction, pneumatic membrane based systems, and advanced formwork techniques used in the construction sector.

Methods

In this study, we propose a self-deploying formwork method for the rapid deployment of reinforced concrete structures. Our approach is based on a structure, which consists of multiple multistable tubes. One end of all tubes is attached to a 3D-printed circular part, so that the whole configuration is axisymmetric. The other end of the tubes is partially sealed by a 3D-printed component, so it allows excess water to drain from the system. Inside all of the multistable tubes there are two (overlapping) regions with two steel cables in each region, so that when the tube deploys there exist at least two cables along its length. The diameter of these cables is equal to 10% of the tube’s radius. This configuration ensures reinforcement along the entire length of the tube in the deployed state. Each steel cable consists of flexible multi-strand steel cables (twisted wire ropes), made of several thin steel wires helically wound together. The cables were used without any special surface treatment. Note that the interaction between the cementitious material and the steel cables is mainly based on friction and mechanical interlocking due to the multi-strand geometry of the cables.

Modeling details

Fabrication and experimental setups

All 3D-printed components were fabricated using SLA 3D printer (Form 3BL). The zip ties and connectors were made of stainless steel. The concrete used in all experiments consisted of a 50:50 mixture of water and standard Portland cement. The fluid reservoir was constructed from two 3D-printed parts and a Perspex tube with inner and outer diameters of 190 mm and 200 mm, respectively. The connectors were made from M6 threaded rods.

Centimeter-scale experiments were constructed using 40 mm polypropylene either conical or helical multistable tubes connected to the fluid reservoir, which was connected to a pressure controller (ELVEFLOW OB1 MK3+) and, in turn, to a compressor (CompAir L07).

The meter-scale experiment was constructed using 80 mm polypropylene helical multistable tubes connected to the fluid reservoir, which was connected to a pump (Afec LBV 40A 400W 7m) submerged in a 240 L container. The closed structure was placed on a stand with a height of 2.36 m and a base of 2.4 m, made from pine wood rods. Note that the multistable tubes with various diameters up to 16 cm are commercially available. However, there are no technical limitations for producing such tubes at larger diameters, according to demand.

The axial loading experiment was conducted using MTS machine retrofitted with Instron 250 kN system. Each type of column was tested three times and all measurements were performed 28 days after the concrete casting. The samples consisted of sheathed and bare HFTs and CFTs with outer diameters of 40 mm and 80 mm, as well as a standard bare circular column.

The photos in Figs. 2 and 3, and the frames of the experimental setup shown in Figs. 2 and 4 were extracted from videos, all of which were captured using iPhone 15 Pro Max camera in 4K resolution at 60 fps.
