The major cluster merger in Abell 2034 as seen by XRISM : strong turbulence and spectral anomalies?
Annie Heinrich 1, Irina Zhuravleva 1, Eugene Churazov 2,3, Congyao Zhang 4,1, Daniele Rogantini 1, Hannah McCall 1, Reinout J. van Weeren 5, William R. Forman 6

Abstract
XRISM observations to date have shown that gas kinetic pressures in the intracluster medium (ICM) tend towards the low end of predictions from cosmological simulations. Here, we present a XRISM observation of the merging cluster Abell 2034, which exhibits the broadest emission lines yet observed in a galaxy cluster. We measure a velocity dispersion of ∼ 470 \sim 470 km s -1 , corresponding to a kinetic pressure fraction of ∼ 15 % \sim 15\% . This places A2034 at or above the high end of the theoretical predictions for similar-mass clusters. This large velocity dispersion may reflect Mach ∼ 0.5 \sim 0.5 turbulence in the ICM and/or result from a core disruption driven by the ongoing head-on merger. We also detect a ∼ 380 \sim 380 km/s gas bulk velocity gradient along the merger axis with an opposite sign to the galaxy velocity gradient, indicating a decoupling of the cluster galaxies (and dark matter) from the ICM. Finally, we report tentative evidence of several spectral anomalies, including a suppressed Fe He α \alpha - z z line, an enhanced Fe Ly α \alpha -2 line, and a potential absorption feature at ∼ 8.7 \sim 8.7 keV. The first two features may be explained by the combination of a multi-phase ICM and a non-equilibrium ionization state in the wake of a merger shock. Deeper XRISM observations of this cluster are required to confirm these features. This work highlights the importance of kinematic measurements across a large sample of merging clusters as well as the need for deep XRISM observations to unveil more exotic physics in the ICM.

We note that the northern/southern ICM is traveling in the opposite direction along the LoS as the northern/southern galaxies (i.e., the northern gas is approaching us while the northern galaxies are receding and vice-versa).

Given this velocity structure, we can be certain that the two subclusters in A2034 have undergone pericenter passage as the dark matter/galaxies have passed each other but the gas has not.

Diverging bulk motions along the LoS may also contribute to

As discussed in Section

It has been proposed that the core of the infalling subcluster (A2034N) may have been disrupted by the impact between the two main ICM components in this merger

In this scenario, the two gas components will spread outwards, driving gas motions perpendicular to the merger axis (i.e., along the east-west axis and the LoS).

The high

However, we cannot rule out the possibility that no compact core existed in either subcluster prior to the ongoing merger.
Distinguishing between these possibilities may be possible with further

4.1
Comparison to other merging clusters and numerical simulations

These kinematic results raise the possibility of A2034 housing significantly stronger ICM turbulence than in other merging clusters observed with

(

10

, A2319

We demonstrate this by comparing

For consistency, velocity dispersions and temperatures are taken from single-component

These measurements are plotted against the length scale associated with the measured gas motions in Figure

This associated scale is the size of the region that dominates the emission observed by

Defining this scale is nontrivial.
Here we use two approaches: either the size of the region observed in the plane-of-sky or the size of the region along the LoS that contributes the majority of X-rays to the spectrum, which we call the effective length scale
\ell_{\rm eff}

The former scale is defined as the size of the
Resolve

3^{\prime}

We note that, due to the

However, the spectrum is dominated by emission from a region the size of or smaller than the FoV.
For instance, in A2034
\gtrsim 50\%

2.2^{\prime}
(
\sim 270

The calculation of

We note that the gas motions in each of these clusters may be injected on scales smaller than either scale presented here.
However, cluster mergers are expected to drive gas motions on scales of a few 100s kpc or larger

23

The size of the

The length scales associated with the gas motions in A2034 are comparable to or slightly larger than the scales probed in other clusters.
Despite the similarity in scale, A2034 has a higher kinetic pressure fraction than any other cluster in this sample (
\sim 15\pm 3\%

We note that sub-FoV fits in other clusters can yield regions with turbulent Mach numbers similar to A2034, e.g.

4.2
Spectral anomalies

While the one and two-component models described above provide reasonable fits to the A2034 spectrum, there are a few features that are not explained by this baseline model:

•

Ly
\alpha
anomaly:

A2034 shows a brighter Fe Ly
\alpha
-2 line than is predicted by the baseline model.
This is visible as a positive residual from the baseline model (black curve) at
\sim 6.25
keV in Figure
3
, detected at
\sim 2\sigma
significance.
While the value of the Ly
\alpha
-2/1 ratio for this temperature gas is expected to be
\sim 0.5
, the observed ratio in A2034 is higher.
We measure the ratio by freezing the temperature, metallicity, and velocity dispersion of the single-component model and replacing the Ly
\alpha
lines with two Gaussians.
The width and redshift of each Gaussian are tied to the
bapec
model, while the normalizations are free.
In the full FoV, the ratio Ly
\alpha
ratio is
{1.3}_{-0.4}^{+0.8}
.
A similar anomalously high Ly
\alpha
ratio was observed in the Coma and A2029 clusters
(
44
;
45
)
; however, the difference is more prominent in A2034.

•

He
\alpha
-
z
anomaly:

We detect a suppression of the Fe He
\alpha
forbidden (
z
) line relative to the baseline model (Figure
3
at
\sim 5.95
keV).
In optically-thin CIE plasmas with typical ICM temperatures, the forbidden-to-resonance ratio (
z/w
) of the Fe He
\alpha
triplet is expected to be
\sim 0.3
.
The ratio observed in A2034 is
\sim 2\sigma
lower, at
{0.16}_{-0.06}^{+0.07}
.
This anomaly appears more strongly in the southern half of the FoV, where the
z
line is not detected at the
1\sigma
level.

Modifying the baseline model by including multiple temperature, metallicity, or velocity components cannot explain the anomalous
z/w
line ratio, though it can partially reduce the Ly
\alpha
-2 residual (see Figure
A2
).
Resonant scattering
(see, e.g.,
6
;
12
, for reviews)
is unlikely to be the cause either.
The optical depth of the potentially optically thick
w
-line is
\sim 0.3
(assuming a spherically-symmetric ICM and no turbulent broadening)—too small to create a noticeable distortion due to resonant scattering.
Moreover, resonant scattering should increase the
z/w
ratio relative to the optically-thin prediction (assuming the ICM is not extremely asymmetric along the LoS).
Resonant scattering cannot explain the anomalous Ly
\alpha
ratio either, as the optical depth for both Ly
\alpha
lines is
<0.1
.
Charge exchange is also unlikely to be significant in this cluster as there is no cool gas to supply neutral atoms
(
11
, e.g.,)
.

Though the significance of these features is low (
\sim 2\sigma
, individually), their presence, if statistically confirmed, points to some intriguing possibilities for ICM physics.
One (rather exotic) explanation for the observed line ratios is a departure from CIE
(see, e.g.,
12
, for review)
.
A non-equilibrium ionzation state has been previously reported in A754
(
15
)
and A3667
(
33
)
using
Suzaku
observations.
However, these spectra do not resolve the individual He
\alpha
and Ly
\alpha
lines as
XRISM
does.
At
Suzaku
’s spectral resolution an apparent deviation from CIE could also be explained by multiple temperature/velocity components, which is reasonable to expect given the mergers ongoing in both clusters.
Notably, both A754 and A3667 have been observed with
XRISM

(
27
;
28
)
, yielding spectra which do not show signs of a suppressed
z/w
ratio.
However, both have residuals in the Ly
\alpha
lines, which may indicate a secondary hot ICM component or a mixture of the CIE and NEI plasmas.

To explore this idea, we consider a model where a merger shock heats the ICM.
In the immediate wake of the shock, the plasma temperature has risen substantially, but the gas has not yet reached ionization equilibrium, leading to specific spectral signatures
(
22
;
19
)
.
In the shocked ICM condition, the collisional excitation of the
w
line increases while the main channels leading to
z
line emission (H-like ions recombination or Li-like ions innershell ionization) become subdominant, causing a decrease in the
z/w
ratio
(
5
)
.

This NEI state is short lived; however, it is not unrealistic to suggest we may observe it in this cluster.
The ionization parameter
\tau=n_{e}\cdot t
(where
n_{e}
is the electron density and
t
is the ionization timescale) for an NEI ICM is expected to be
\lesssim
a few
10^{12}
s cm
-3

(
5
)
.
For typical ICM densities of
\sim 10^{-3}
cm
-3
, the NEI state can last up to
\sim 30
Myr.
However, as the shock passes through the ICM more gas will be brought out of CIE, effectively trailing an NEI region behind the shock.
Therefore, finely tuned merger timing is not required to expect that we may detect emission from the NEI gas.

If a merger shock is within the
Resolve
FoV we may expect three ICM states to contribute to the X-ray emission: a pre-shock, low-temperature (
\sim 6-7
keV) gas in CIE; a post-shock NEI gas; and a post-shock, high temperature (
>10
keV) gas that has returned to CIE.
We probe this configuration by modeling the full-FoV
Resolve
spectrum with a combination of three plasma components: two
bapec

The temperature of the pre-shock

brnei

bapec
and final
brnei

We additionally tie the abundances and velocity dispersions between each component.
The normalizations and redshifts of each component are free.

This model is fit to the full-FoV spectrum in the
5-7

\alpha

\alpha

We find a best-fit pre-shock temperature of
7_{-3}^{+1}

12_{-2}^{+5}
keV, with a velocity dispersion of
300_{-60}^{+80}
km/s.
This temperature jump is produced by a
\mathcal{M}\sim 1.8

\tau

\lesssim 10^{11.6}

The best-fit model is plotted in Figure

These figures show that the low
z/w

\alpha

The NEI component is the brightest of the three, followed by the post-shock CIE component, while pre-shock component is
\sim 4
times dimmer than the others.

Fitting the
3-11
keV spectrum with the same combination of CIE and NEI components results in a model with a bright pre-shock CIE and dim NEI component, which does not address the anomalous
z/w
ratio.
Therefore, additional
XRISM
observations of this cluster that will increase the significance of the tentatively detected deviations are needed to confirm this solution.

To further probe the ionization state of the ICM, we employ line ratio diagnostics that are sensitive to the excitation and ionization temperatures
(
26
;
14
;
31
)
.
With the current data, we are able to measure the Fe He
\alpha

(x+y+z)/w
and He
\alpha

z
/Ly
\alpha
line ratios, which are respectively sensitive to the excitation and ionization temperatures.
The latter ratio is
0.19\pm 0.08
, consistent with an ionization temperature of
11^{+3}_{-2}
keV in a CIE plasma.
This is similar to the post-shock temperature found in the previous narrow-band modeling exercise.
The former ratio is
0.31^{+0.12}_{-0.05}
, which is inconsistent with any CIE emission in the ICM temperature range.
The
(x+y+z)/w
ratio is expected to be
\sim 0.55-0.7
for a plasma with a similar temperature to A2034, which is in
\gtrsim 2\sigma
tension with the observed ratio.
This further supports the notion that the ICM in A2034 deviates from the ionization equilibrium.

We note the possibility that an NEI plasma has not reached ion-electron temperature equilibrium.
In the wake of the shock, the ion temperature may be higher than the electron temperature.
If that is the case, the Fe emission lines would be thermally broadened more than expected and therefore a CIE model would overestimate the velocity dispersion based on the line widths.
Independently measuring the ion temperature requires resolving lines from multiple elements, which is not possible with the current data.

The NEI model we consider requires the presence of a merger shock within or near the
Resolve
FoV, yet the only reported shock in A2034 is the Mach
\sim 1.8
shock located
\sim 1^{\prime}
north of the FoV
(
29
)
.
A southern shock is expected in/near the FoV based on the merger configuration
(
25
)
.
It is also expected to be weaker and cover a smaller region than the northern shock, given the measured mass ratio of the merging components.
Thus, the shock may be hidden by projection effects due to the bright core or a shock orientation along the LoS.
Further evidence for a shock in this region is given by the presence of a hot (
\sim 12
keV) region near the southern edge of the FoV
(
29
)
.
Finally, a radio structure just south of the FoV
(
32
, which is likely either a radio relic or phoenix,)
also indicates a recent shock passage.

4.3
Potential absorption feature

Careful examination of the A2034 spectrum has revealed another unique anomaly: a “dip” in the spectrum between
\sim
8.5 and 8.9 keV (Figure
3
, the right panel).
The exact shape of this feature is unclear, given the low photon counts in the hard X-ray band.
At low resolution (the spectrum shown in Figure
3
is visually binned to
>5\sigma
significance), it can be modeled as a Gaussian absorption line (
gabs
model in
xspec
).
Modeling this feature in the full FoV spectrum, we find a detector-frame centroid of
E_{\mathrm{abs}}=8.70\pm 0.03
keV with a width of
\sigma_{\mathrm{abs}}=80\pm 30
eV and an optical depth of
\tau_{\mathrm{abs}}=0.8\pm 0.4
.
This feature is detected at
\sim 4\sigma
significance.
We also fit this feature in the north/south half-FoV spectra, finding no significant variance in any of the
gabs
parameters.

The presence of such an absorption feature in the spectrum of a galaxy cluster is puzzling.
To the best of our knowledge, no such features have been reported previously, and they are not expected on physical grounds unless rather unusual conditions are invoked.
We verified that the NXB spectrum shows no emission lines in the 8-9 keV band that could have been incorrectly subtracted to produce an artificial absorption feature.
We also confirmed with the
XRISM
support team that this feature is very unlikely to be associated with any instrumental or calibration effects (private communication).

The energy and width of the feature are consistent with an ultrafast outflow (UFO) from an AGN
(
43
, e.g.,)
.
Although no AGN is detected within the detector FoV, based on the
Chandra
image, there are several bright point sources near the FoV (see Figure
A1
) that could, in principle, contribute to the observed spectrum as light scattered by the PSF.
The presence of a UFO would also require a significant contribution of the AGN continuum to the spectrum.
It is possible to add an AGN component to the model described in Section
3
, effectively reducing the continuum emission from the ICM.
However, this will increase the ICM metallicity to
\gtrsim 0.8
, which is rather high compared to typical values of
\sim 0.3-0.5
in the ICM and inconsistent with the metallicity measured by
Chandra

(
16
)
.
Additionally, the feature would have to absorb essentially all of the AGN continuum emission from
8.5-8.9
keV range, meaning the UFO would need to have an extreme optical depth.
Therefore, we consider this explanation unlikely.

Another rather exotic possibility is that the feature comes from a shock in the outskirts of A2034, where the gas density is very low.
With a particular shock geometry (e.g., a shock front close to be parallel to the LoS), the column density of the shocked region could be significant, while the total density (and therefore X-ray emissivity) remains low.
In this case, the feature could be attributed to absorption by a high energy atomic transition.
The highest energy atomic transition expected to be strong in the ICM is the Ni Ly
\alpha
lines, at
\sim 8.1
keV.
For this line to reach 8.7 keV, it must be blueshifted by
\sim 50\cdot 10^{3}
km/s (or
\sim 0.16c
) relative to the cluster mean, which is unrealistic to expect in a galaxy cluster.
There are weaker lines at higher energies that would require a smaller blueshift (e.g., the Fe He
\gamma
-
\iota
lines); however, these would be paired with stronger, lower-energy lines (e.g., He
\alpha
) that we do not see in absorption in the
Resolve
spectrum.

In conclusion, we cannot identify a physically motivated explanation for the absorption feature.
Given its current
\sim 4\sigma
significance, it may still be a statistical fluctuation.
Deeper
XRISM
observations are required to confirm its presence, investigate possible multi-component structure, and explore its spatial variation within the cluster.
We note that if the
4\sigma
detection of the feature is due to mere statistical fluctuations, the temperature of the ICM in A2034 reduces from
\sim 8.1
keV to
7.9\pm 0.3
keV, while all other parameters are unchanged.

5
Conclusions

We conducted a detailed study of the high-resolution X-ray spectrum measured by
XRISM
/
Resolve
in the merging cluster A2034, revealing several unique features that could not have been discovered without
XRISM
’s spectral resolution.
The standout feature is the velocity dispersion, which is higher than in other merging clusters observed by
XRISM
so far:
\sim 400-500
km/s in the full FoV, corresponding to a 3D turbulent Mach number of
\sim 0.56
.
As expected for a near-plane-of-sky merger, the average bulk velocity of the ICM in the full FoV is consistent with 0 km/s.
The main conclusions can be summarized as follows:

•

We probe the spectrum for evidence of multiple bulk velocity components along the line-of-sight.
We find that the combination of a hot (
kT\approx 11
keV) component that is redshifted with respect to the cluster mean (
{V_{\mathrm{bulk}}}\approx+500
km/s) with a cooler blueshifted component (
kT\approx 6.5
keV,
{V_{\mathrm{bulk}}}\approx-100
km/s) provides a better fit to the spectrum.
When separating these bulk velocity components, the velocity dispersion of both components is
\sim 390
km/s.

•

By splitting the FoV along the north-south merger axis, we show that the hot component is localized in the south and the cooler component in the north.
The bulk velocity gradient between the two regions is
\sim 380
km/s, consistent with the near-plane-of-sky, head-on merger in A2034.

•

The southern/northern gas are moving in the opposite directions along the LoS of the southern/northern galaxies, demonstrating a decoupling between the two, which is expected in dissociative mergers.
The gas is also moving slower compared to the BCGs of the northern/southern subclusters, consistent with the collisional nature of the ICM.
This indicates the merger in A2034 is just past pericenter passage as the galaxies have had time to pass each other but the gas has not.

•

We discussed that diverging bulk motions along the LoS associated with the disruption of the subcluster cores during the merger (“gas squashing”) may be partially responsible for the high velocity dispersion.

•

We compare the turbulent Mach number (
{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{3D}}}
) in A2034 to other merging clusters observed by
XRISM
, calculating the gas motions’ associated length scale in each.
While the length scales probed in A2034 (i.e., a few hundred kpc) are similar to other clusters, it has a turbulent Mach number of
\sim 0.56
and kinetic pressure fraction of
\sim 15\%
, higher than any other merging cluster yet observed.
This kinetic pressure fraction puts A2034 on the high end of the
P_{\mathrm{kin}}/P_{\mathrm{tot}}
distribution found in analogous clusters in cosmological simulations
(
42
)
.
Thus, A2034 helps to balance the distribution of clusters observed by
XRISM
, which have so far skewed to low kinetic pressures.

We additionally find several tentative residuals in the
Resolve
spectrum that are not captured by an optically thin, collisionally-ionized equilibrium plasma.
Namely, these are: a low He
\alpha

z/w
ratio, a high Ly
\alpha
2/1 ratio, and a dip in the spectrum at
\sim 8.7
keV indicating an unexplained absorption feature, which are detected at
\sim 2
, 2, and
4\sigma
, respectively.

The anomalous line ratios could be explained if the ICM is in a non-equilibrium ionization state following the passage of a merger shock.
The low
z/w
ratio, if confirmed with longer
XRISM
observations, would indicate an NEI state in the ICM, the first robustly discovered via this diagnostic criterion.
The high Ly
\alpha
ratio can be explained by a redshifted, hot (
kT>10
keV) ICM component.
We model the narrow-band spectrum of A2034 with a combination of NEI and CIE components, finding that a
\mathcal{M}\approx 1.8
shock that heats the gas from
\sim 7
to 12 keV can reproduce the observed He
\alpha
and Ly
\alpha
emission lines.
Both line ratio anomalies require additional
XRISM
exposure time to statistically confirm.

We fit the hard X-ray absorption feature with a Gaussian absorption line, finding a centroid of
\sim 8.7
keV, width of
\sim 70
eV, and an optical depth of
\sim 0.8
.
We do not detect significant variation in the absorption feature’s spectral properties across the FoV or over the time interval of the observation.
We speculate on the possible origin of this feature, however we cannot find a physically-convincing explanation without further data.

A2034 stands out from other
XRISM
-observed merging clusters due to the higher-than-previously-observed gas kinetic pressure fraction and the anomalous spectral features it produces.
This work demonstrates the need for deep
XRISM
observations of massive merging clusters—as well as supporting theoretical studies aimed at
Resolve
’s capabilities—to understand the physics that produce these and other spectral anomalies.
