Patrolling cop vs omniscient robber
Nina Chiarelli, Paul Dorbec, Miloš Stojaković, Andrej Taranenko

Abstract
We study a variant of the classical Cops and Robbers game with one cop and one robber, in which the cop follows a fixed walk on the graph, a patrol, that is chosen before the game begins, while the robber is omniscient, he knows the entire patrol in advance. A capture occurs when the robber comes within a given radius of capture of the cop. This model arises naturally at the intersection of recent work on limited-visibility games and offline versions of pursuit-evasion problems. By ρ ~ ​ ( G ) \tilde{\rho}(G) we denote the minimum radius of capture that the cop must have to always capture the robber on G G in this setting, under optimal play, where G G is a connected graph. We initiate a systematic study of this parameter for several graph classes. We determine the exact value of ρ ~ ​ ( G ) \tilde{\rho}(G) for trees, establish upper and lower bounds for grids, and analyze the parameter for various families of chordal graphs, including interval graphs and caterpillars. Along the way, we develop general tools and structural results that may be of independent interest for the study of pursuit-evasion games with predetermined patrols and limited information.



Key words:
Cops and Robbers game, pursuit-evasion game, radius of capture

Introduction

Introduced by Nowakowski and Winkler
[
20
]
and Quilliot
[
21
]
, the now classical cop and robber game has gained massive popularity and was intensively studied by numerous authors, see, e.g.,
[
5
,
15
]
. Also see the book
[
6
]
for a comprehensive review of the topic. The cop and robber game is a pursuit-evasion game played on the vertices of finite undirected graphs. First, the cop chooses his starting position, then the robber occupies another vertex. They alternately either move to an adjacent vertex or stay in the same vertex. Both players are aware of the position of the opposing player throughout the game. The aim of the cop is to capture the robber, i.e., to occupy the vertex that is occupied by the robber, while the robber wants to evade the cop. A graph
G
is a
cop-win
graph if the cop has a strategy to capture the robber after a finite number of moves regardless of the robber’s playing strategy.

Since the introduction of this game, many variants appeared in the literature. The first and most natural variant replaces a single cop with a set of cops, and then it makes sense to look at the minimum number of cops needed to capture the robber in
G
, the
cop number
of a graph
G
,
c(G)
, introduced by Aigner and Fromme
[
2
]
. Catching the robber from a distance variant, the distance
k
cop and robber game was introduced by Bonato and Chiniforooshan
[
4
]
. This variant changes the condition for the cop’s win, in this case he wins if he is at a distance at most
k
from the robber. The
distance
k
cop number
of a graph
G
,
c_{k}(G)
, is the minimum number of cops necessary to win for a given distance
k
. This game was further studied in
[
8
]
under the name:
the cop and robber game with radius of capture
k
. Denote by
{\cal{CWRC}}(k)
the set of graphs in which the cop has a winning strategy in the cop and robber game with radius of capture
k
regardless of how robber is playing. Chalopin et al.
[
8
]
considered the cop and robber game with radius of capture
1
on bipartite graphs, and characterized the bipartite graphs in
{\cal{CWRC}}(1)
. Graphs in
{\cal{CWRC}}(1)
were also studied in
[
11
]
, where the cop and robber game with radius of capture 1 was considered on square-grids,
k
-chordal graphs, and outerplanar graphs. In
[
12
]
the
radius of capture number
of a graph
G
,
\rc(G)
, was defined as the smallest
k
such that
G\in{\cal{CWRC}}(k)
, i.e.,
\rc(G)=\min{\{k\mid G\in{\cal{CWRC}}(k)\}}
and some results on this invariant were presented.

Clarke et al.
[
9
]
introduce and explore another variant of the cops and robber game, fundamentally departing from the classical model primarily through restrictions on the cops’ information regarding the robber’s location. Namely, in the classical game, both cops and the robber possess perfect information, always knowing each other’s precise positions throughout the game. In contrast,
[
9
]
delves into
zero-visibility
and
\ell
-visibility
variants. In the zero-visibility game, the robber is completely invisible to the cops unless a cop lands on the same vertex. Then in the
\ell
-visibility game
, studied also in
[
3
]
and
[
19
]
, cops can only see the robber if the distance between them is at most a fixed parameter
\ell
, while the robber has perfect information about the cops’ positions. This key modification allows for a new layer when designing cops’ winning strategies, having them to maneuver to first see the robber (i.e., move within distance
\ell
), and only then can they progress to capture him. A crucial and counter-intuitive result of this limited visibility is that seeing the robber does not automatically imply his capture, which is a significant departure from the classical game’s dynamics; for instance, on a
C_{4}
cycle in the 1-visibility game, one cop can see the robber, but two are required for capture, and this can also be true even on cop-win graphs. Finally, a particularly interesting finding is that the difference between the number of cops required in the
\ell
-visibility game and the number needed in the classical game can be arbitrarily large, demonstrating how profoundly the visibility constraint can increase the resources necessary for success.

A watchman’s walk of a graph
G
is any minimum closed dominating walk of a single watchman traversing
G
; in other words, any minimum closed walk such that every vertex is either visited or seen from a visited neighbor. The watchman’s walk was introduced by Hartnell, Rall, and Whitehead in 1998
[
18
]
.

Dumitrescu, Suzuki and Zylinski
[
13
]
study a variant of the cops and robber game in which the cops choose their walks in advance and publicly announce them before the game starts. Their work arises from the study of a discretized, offline version of the Lion and Man game (see
[
10
]
) on graphs. These results were later extended by Brass et al.
[
7
]
.

Motivated by these developments, we consider a variant of the game that combines the two mentioned features – limited visibility, and a predefined and publicly announced walk of the cop. We play the game on a connected graph
G
with exactly one cop and one robber. The cop has a fixed walk on
G
, his
patrol
, a radius of capture
\rho\geq 0
and no information on the robber’s position at any time. On the other hand the robber is
omniscient
, meaning he knows all of the cop’s movements in advance (the cop’s predetermined patrol plan was “stolen” by the robber), and he tries to not be caught by the cop on the graph using this knowledge. The cop wins if he can capture the robber, assuming the cop’s radius of capture is
\rho\geq 0
. The minimum radius of capture that the cop must have to always capture the robber on
G
in this setting is denoted by
\tilde{\rho}(G)
.

Our concept generalizes the cops and robbers problem with zero-visibility, in the sense that the cop cannot adapt his strategy to the moves of the robber. Most results with zero-visibility use probabilistic strategies, so that there is a non zero probability that the robber gets caught. With our setting, we force the strategy to be deterministic. Clearly, for the cop to have a possibility of a win, he must visit or at least see every vertex of the graph, otherwise the robber wins, hence a similarity with the watchman’s walk.

We proceed as follows. In Section
3
, we determine the exact value of
\tilde{\rho}(G)
for trees, as well as for some unicyclic graphs. In Section
4
, we establish upper and lower bounds for
\tilde{\rho}(G)
on grids. Finally, in Section
5
, we analyze this parameter for chordal graphs, covering in particular the caterpillars and interval graphs. Along the way, we develop general tools and structural results that may be of independent interest in the study of pursuit-evasion games with predetermined patrols and limited information.

Preliminaries

A
weak homomorphism
from a graph
G
to a graph
H
is a mapping
\varphi:V(G)\rightarrow V(H)
such that for any two adjacent vertices
u,v\in V(G)
, it holds true that
d(\varphi(u),\varphi(v))\leq 1
, i.e., they are mapped to the same vertex or two adjacent vertices.
A subgraph
H
of a graph
G
is a
weak retract
of
G
if there exists a weak homomorphism
\varphi:V(G)\rightarrow V(H)
with
\varphi(u)=u
, for all
u\in V(H)
. The map
\varphi
is called a
weak retraction
.

We first prove the following property that we extensively use in the rest of the paper.

Theorem 2.1
.

If
H
is a weak retract of a connected graph
G
and
\tilde{\rho}(H)>\rho
,
then
\tilde{\rho}(G)>\rho
.

Proof.

Let
H
be a weak retract of a connected graph
G
, and
\varphi:V(G)\rightarrow V(H)
a weak retraction.
Robber uses his strategy on
H
, restricting his movement to
H
, imagining for every position
v\in V(G)
that the cop occupies
that he is actually on
\varphi(v)
.

Since
H
is a subgraph of
G
, robber’s strategy can be played on
G
.
The cop’s strategy remains consistent in
H
since to every edge the cop uses in
G
,
there is a corresponding move on
H
(either not moving, or along an edge).

The robber’s strategy prevents him from being caught by the cop because for every
position
v_{c}
of the cop and
v_{r}
of the robber, by assumption

d_{G}(v_{r},v_{c})\geq d_{H}(v_{r},\varphi(v_{c}))>\rho
.
∎

Trees and unicyclic graphs

A
triod
is a tree
T
with three leaves, and a unique vertex
v
of degree three that we refer to as the
origin
. We define
\ell_{3}(T)
to be equal to the distance of the closest of the three leaves to the origin and call it
the triod size
.

For a general graph
G
and a vertex
v
of degree at least 3, we define
\ell_{3}(v)
to be the maximum
\ell_{3}(T)
over all triods
T
with origin
v
induced in
G
. If
v
is of degree less than 3,
\ell_{3}(v)=0
.

A
clique-triod
is a graph made from a clique on at least
3
vertices,
referred to as the
clique-origin
, and three vertex-disjoint paths, each attached to
a different vertex of the clique-origin.
Given a clique-triod
G
, we define
\ell_{3}(G)
as the length of the shortest of the three paths.

We say that the cop
checks
a vertex
v
if he comes in range of at most
\rho
from
v
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a triod with the origin
v
. Assuming the cop has range
\rho
, he can check any vertex at distance at most
2\rho+1
from
v
. On top of that, if at any time the robber moves to
v
, then the cop can catch the robber.

Proof.

Let
u
be a vertex at distance at most
2\rho+1
from
v
.
The cop moves to the vertex
v^{\prime}
at distance
\rho
from
v
which is closest to
u
,
during which time the robber clearly does not enter
v
.
The vertex
u
is then at distance at most
\rho+1
from
v^{\prime}
.
The cop moves one more step towards
u
, and in his next turn, he moves back to
v^{\prime}
.
This means that
v
was not guarded during precisely one turn, which would allow the robber to enter
v

but would not give him enough time to leave it. So the robber doesn’t enter
v
, as required.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

(i)

If the cop has range
\rho
, then the robber can win on a triod provided
\ell_{3}(G)\geq 2\rho+2
.

(ii)

If the cop has range
\rho
, then the robber can win on a clique-triod
G
provided
\ell_{3}(G)\geq 2\rho+1
.

Proof.

In both parts of this lemma, the strategy of the robber will consistently be to stay as close as possible to the cop without getting caught. Hence, if in the next turn the cop moves towards the robber, then the robber moves to a vertex at a distance
\rho+2
from the cop. If the cop’s next move is to move away from the robber, the robber moves to a vertex at distance
\rho+1
. Finally, if the cop’s next move does not change the distance to the robber, then the robber does not move.

(i)

The origin of the triod is denoted by
o
, and the three paths joining
o
to the leaves will be called
branches
, and denoted by
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}
. The
\rho+1
vertices furthest from
o
on each path will be called
houses

h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}
, respectively.

When choosing his initial position, the robber follows the same principle as while moving: he places himself on a vertex at distance either
\rho+2
or
\rho+1
from the cop, depending on whether the cop’s first move will be towards the robber or not.
If there are multiple such vertices, the robber chooses a branch that is neither the cop’s current branch nor the branch containing the next house that the cop will visit. If the cop is at the origin, we arbitrarily choose a branch that does not contain the next house the cop visits to be the cop’s branch.

Following this strategy, if the robber can choose between two possible moves, he has to be at the origin, and furthermore, the cop moved to a vertex at distance
\rho+1
from the origin with the intention to continue moving towards the origin. In such a case, if the cop is on the branch
P_{i}
, for some
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, and the next house the cop is going to visit is on the branch
P_{j}
, for some
j\in\{1,2,3\}
, then the robber moves to a branch
P_{k}
, for
k\not\in\{i,j\}
. Note that, if
i=j
then the robber can move to any of the two remaining branches, otherwise there is only one branch left that he can move to. E.g., if the cop comes from branch
P_{1}
and the next house he is going to visit is
h_{2}
, the robber goes toward
h_{3}
.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that the cop catches the robber with this strategy. This means that prior to the final turn, the cop moves to a vertex at distance
\rho+1
from the robber, the robber cannot shadow him at distance
\rho+2
, and the cop makes one more move towards the robber, thus decreasing the distance to
\rho
. But that means that the robber has reached a leaf, and the cop entered the house in which the robber is hiding. This further implies that the last time the robber had to leave the origin he chose the wrong direction. Indeed, the cop could not have visited any other house in the meantime, because the origin is at distance at least
\rho+2
from any other house, a contradiction.

(ii)

The vertices of degree three in the clique are denoted by
o_{1},o_{2},o_{3}
, and the three paths joining them to the leaves are referred to as
branches
,
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}
, respectively. For each
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, the
\rho+1
vertices furthest from
o_{i}
on the path
P_{i}
we will call the
house

h_{i}
.

Similarly to (i), When choosing his initial position, the robber places himself on a vertex at distance either
\rho+2
or
\rho+1
from the cop, depending on whether the cop’s first move will be towards him or not.
If there are multiple such vertices, the robber chooses a branch that is neither the cop’s current branch nor the branch containing the next house the cop will visit.

Following his strategy, the robber may have to choose between two possible moves only if he is in
o_{i}
, for some
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, and one of the following two cases occurs.

Case 1. The robber is at
o_{i}
and the cop is on the branch
P_{i}
at distance
\rho+1
, with an intention to move towards the robber in the next move. The robber considers the next house
h_{j}
different from
h_{i}
that the cop is going to enter, and then he moves to
o_{k}
, where
k\not\in\{i,j\}
.

Case 2. The robber is at
o_{i}
, and the cop is on
P_{j}
,
j\not=i
, at distance
\rho+2
from the robber, with intention to move towards the robber in his following move. The robber considers the next house
h_{x}
different from
h_{j}
that the cop is going to enter, and then he either stays on
o_{i}
, if
i\not=x
, or he moves to
o_{k}
,
k\not\in\{i,j\}
, if
i=x
.

Note, that whenever the cop enters a house, the robber positions himself in such a way that, if the cop moves towards the robber, the robber can retreat to the branch that does not contain the house that the cop will enter next.

Now again, as the robber maintains a safe distance from the cop at all times, the only way for him to get caught is if he is cornered in a leaf with the cop approaching within a distance of
\rho
. But that means that the cop entered the house the robber is in, which cannot happen.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

For a tree
T
,

\tilde{\rho}(T)=\max_{v\in V(T)}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\ell_{3}(v)}{2}\right\rfloor\right).

Proof.

Let
\rho=\max_{v\in V(T)}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\ell_{3}(v)}{2}\right\rfloor\right)
.

We provide a winning strategy for the cop when his range is
\rho
.
Let
\mathcal{L}=\max_{v\in V(T)}(\ell_{3}(v))
and let
S=\{v\in V(T)\mid\ell_{3}(v)=\mathcal{L}\}
. In words, the set
S
contains the origins of all triods in
T
with the maximum triod size. By
[
17
, Lemma 3]
all vertices of
S
lie on the same path in
T
. Take a longest path
P
in
T
, say
v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
, such that it includes all vertices of
S
.

The cop starts on
v_{0}
and makes a depth first search (DFS) traversal of the tree such that the vertices of
P
are always chosen when there is no other neighbor to choose from. Note that in this manner
v_{m}
will be the last visited vertex. At each vertex
v
of the tree, the cop checks all the vertices at distance at most
2\rho+1
from
v
without letting the robber occupy
v
during this time. By Lemma
3.1
this is possible.

Indeed, there is no subtree attached to
v_{i}
of depth more than
2\rho+1
because:

•

When
i\leq 2\rho
, that would induce a path longer than
m
.

•

When
2\rho<i<m-2\rho
,
\ell_{3}(v_{i})
would be more than
2\rho
.

•

When
i\geq m-2\rho
, that would induce a path longer than
m
.

Thus, using this strategy, the cop checks all the vertices of
T
, without ever letting the robber go from an unchecked vertex to an already checked one without being caught. At the latest, the robber is caught when the cop reaches
v_{m}
. Hence,
\rho(T)\leq\rho
.

To prove that
\rho(T)\geq\rho
, let
v\in V(T)
be such that
\ell_{3}(v)=\max_{u\in V(T)}(\ell_{3}(u))
and let
T^{\prime}
be any induced triod with the origin
v
that maximizes the triod size. Define the function
p:V(T)\rightarrow V(T^{\prime})
to map any vertex
u\in V(T)
to the unique vertex in
V(T^{\prime})
which is closest to
u
. It is easy to see that
p
is a weak homomorphism. Using Lemma
3.2
and Theorem
2.1
, the assertion follows directly.
∎

Theorem 3.4
.

\tilde{\rho}(T_{k,\ell})=\max\left\{\left\lceil\frac{\ell}{2}+\frac{k-2}{4}\right\rceil,\left\lceil\frac{3k-2}{2}\right\rceil\right\}.

Let

on paths

\rho<\left\lceil\frac{\ell}{2}+\frac{k-2}{4}\right\rceil

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\ell>0
be an integer, and let
G
be a clique-triod with three vertex-disjoint paths of length

, each attached to a different vertex of the clique-origin. Then

\tilde{\rho}(G)=\left\lceil\frac{\ell}{2}\right\rceil.

Proof.

Using Lemma
3.2
(ii) one immediately obtains that
\tilde{\rho}(G)\geq\left\lceil\frac{\ell}{2}\right\rceil
. For the upper bound, namely
\tilde{\rho}(G)\leq\left\lceil\frac{\ell}{2}\right\rceil
one can use the cop’s strategy described in the proof of Theorem
3.4
with the following changes. In the proof of Theorem
3.4
set

(thus the cycle is of length 3, i.e. a clique of length 3). Since

is a positive integer,
\tilde{\rho}(G)\geq 0
. Also,
\left\lceil\frac{\ell}{2}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{\ell}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right\rceil
, hence the values from this theorem and Theorem

coincide. Moreover, if the clique-origin of

is a clique of order larger than

, following the same line of thought as in the proof of Theorem

one can show that the robber cannot either be on the clique-origin or move through it without being detected by the cop.
∎

For grids

.
We can embed a tree to prove that with range less than

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
n\leq m
,

\min\left\{\frac{n-3}{2},\frac{2m+n-10}{8}\right\}\leq\tilde{\rho}(P_{n}\Box P_{m})\leq\frac{n}{2}

Proof.

Obtaining the upper bound is straightforward.
Denote by
u_{1},\ldots u_{n}
the vertices of the
P_{n}
factor, and by
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
the vertices of the
P_{m}
factor.
The cop starts from the vertex
(u_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil},v_{1})

and follows the
P_{m}
-fiber along
(u_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil},v_{2})
up to
(u_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil},v_{m})
.

When on

5
Chordal graphs

In this section, we consider two special subclasses of chordal graphs, namely caterpillars and interval graphs. We considered these classes as initial cases while we hoped for a general result on chordal graphs. We conclude this section with a general conjecture for chordal graphs that would generalize the first results.

A graph
G
is a
caterpillar
if it is a tree and all vertices are within distance 1 of a central path called the
backbone
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
G
be a connected graph. Then
\tilde{\rho}(G)=0
if and only if
G
is a caterpillar.

Proof.

If
G
is a caterpillar, let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
be the longest induced path in
G
, its backbone. The cop starts from
u_{1}
, goes toward
u_{k}
while systematically visiting the legs of the caterpillar. The cop is situated on the backbone every second move, thus the robber situated on the path cannot move past the cop toward
u_{1}
without being caught.

Suppose now that
G
is not a caterpillar. If
G
is a tree, then it contains a subdivided
K_{1,3}
as a retract and
\tilde{\rho}(G)\geq 1
by Theorem
3.3
.
If
G
contains a cycle, then its minimum cycle is a weak retract of
G
.
For the cop to catch the robber on a cycle on
k
vertices, he needs a range of at least
\lceil\frac{k}{2}\rceil-1
so
\tilde{\rho}(G)\geq 1
, this completes the proof.
∎

An
interval representation
of a graph is a family of real line intervals assigned to vertices in such a way that two distinct vertices are adjacent if and only if the corresponding intervals intersect. A graph is an
interval graph
if it has an interval representation. One may assume that all intervals in any interval representation of an interval graph are closed, have positive length, and no intervals agree on any endpoint. Caterpillars have been characterized as connected triangle-free interval graphs
[
14
]
.

Let
G
be an interval graph. Given an interval representation of
G
, for all
v\in V(G)
let
I_{v}=[b_{v},e_{v}]
be the interval corresponding to
v
. A vertex
v
of
G
is called an
end-vertex
if there exists an interval representation of
G
such that
b_{v}=\min\{b_{u}\mid u\in V(G)\}
or
e_{v}=\max\{e_{u}\mid u\in V(G)\}
, the corresponding interval
I_{v}
is called an
end-interval

[
16
]
. A maximal clique of
G
is an
end-clique
if it contains an end vertex of
G
. It is easy to see that an end-vertex
v
of
G
is always simplicial, i.e.,
N[v]
is a clique.

Theorem 5.2
.

If
G
is a connected interval graph, then
\tilde{\rho}(G)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $G$ is a caterpillar}\\
1,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
.

Proof.

If
G
is a caterpillar, then the claim follows from Theorem
5.1
.

Assume that
G
is not a caterpillar. Choose an arbitrary interval representation of
G
and for all
v\in V(G)
let
I_{v}=[b_{v},e_{v}]
be the interval corresponding to
v
. Order the vertices of
G
with respect to the left end-points in their interval representation, say
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}
where
b_{v_{1}}\leq b_{v_{2}}\leq\ldots\leq b_{v_{n}}
.

We claim that if the cop with a radius of capture equal to 1 uses a walk through all vertices, starting from
v_{1}
, then he can catch the robber. Let the cop start in
c_{1}=v_{1}
and say the robber chooses to be in any other vertex of
G
, say
r_{1}\not=c_{1}
(otherwise he is immediately caught). If
d(c_{1},r_{1})=1
then
\tilde{\rho}(G)\leq 1
.

Assume now
d(c_{1},r_{1})>1
. Therefore
e_{c_{1}}<b_{r_{1}}
. Since in our strategy the cop visits all vertices of
G
, at some point in time, say
t+1
, it must hold that
b_{r_{t+1}}\leq b_{c_{t+1}}
, and for all
t^{\prime}<t+1
it holds true that
b_{r_{t^{\prime}}}>b_{c_{t^{\prime}}}
.

If
b_{r_{t}}\leq e_{c_{t}}
, then
d(c_{t},r_{t})=1
and
\tilde{\rho}(G)\leq 1
, again we are done. Otherwise, since
b_{c_{t}}<b_{r_{t}}
and
b_{r_{t+1}}\leq b_{c_{t+1}}
,
c_{t+1}\in N[c_{t}]
and
r_{t+1}\in N[r_{t}]
it follows that
I_{c_{t+1}}\cap I_{r_{t+1}}\not=\emptyset
. Which means that
d(c_{t+1},r_{t+1})\leq 1
(they are on the same clique) and therefore,
\tilde{\rho}(G)\leq 1
. By Theorem
5.1
and the fact that
G
is not a caterpillar we have that
\tilde{\rho}(G)>0
and the assertion follows.
∎

A graph
G
is
chordal
if it has no induced cycles of length greater than
3
.
Equivalently, chordal graphs admit a perfect elimination ordering, i.e., a bijection

\sigma:V\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that the neighborhood of each vertex

v
within
\{u\mid\sigma(u)>\sigma(v)\}
induces a clique.
Chordal graphs also admit a clique-tree decomposition,
i.e., a treelike representation in which each node (usually called
bag
)
is a maximal clique of
G
.

All interval graphs are chordal graphs that admit a tree decomposition that form a single path.
Our intuition is that the strategy we adopted for trees can also be applied
on chordal graphs, with a similar strategy.
However, there is no natural link between the distance separating two vertices in
a chordal graph and their distance in the tree decomposition.
We were thus not able to prove it, but we suggests the following conjecture that would settle the case of chordal graphs.

Conjecture 5.3
.

Let
G
be a connected chordal graph and
\rho\geq 2
. Its range
\tilde{\rho}(G)\geq\rho
if and only if there is a triod
T
with
\ell_{3}(T)\geq 2\rho
or a clique-triod
G
with
\ell_{3}(G)\geq 2\rho-1
that is a weak retract of
G
.
