Preventive effects and underlying mechanisms of Ilex rotunda Thunb.-Cyperus rotundus L. herb pair extract on avian colibacillosis in chickens
Authors: Huixin Liu, Anqi Yang, Xiaofang Wei, Hongjie Hu, Chenchen Wang, Jiesheng Liao, Liangyu Yang, Hongbin Si
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a major bottleneck limiting the sustainable development of global animal husbandry. Avian colibacillosis, caused by Avian Pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC), incurs severe economic losses in the poultry industry due to its high pathogenicity, multidrug resistance, and potential zoonotic risk. This study aimed to clarify the material basis of the Ilex rotunda Thunb.-Cyperus rotundus L. herb pair and its preventive mechanisms against avian colibacillosis in chickens. Volatile oil from C. rotundus and water-soluble components of the herb pair were co-extracted via steam distillation combined with water extraction, and compounds were identified using GC-TQMS and UHPLC-Q-Orbitrap HRMS. An avian colibacillosis model was established with the multidrug-resistant APEC strain E-37 (ST350), and eight experimental groups were set to evaluate the extract’s effects on chick growth performance, immune function, antioxidant capacity, hepatic injury, bacteremia, and intestinal microbiota. Results showed that 93 bioactive compounds were identified in the herb pair extract (CIRC), with cyperotundone, α-cyperone, caffeic acid, and oleanolic acid as core components. Prophylactic administration of CIRC significantly reduced chick mortality (30%∼45% vs. 60% in the model group), regulated immune organ indices (decreased spleen index, increased thymus and bursa of Fabricius indices), inhibited pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), upregulated the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, and elevated serum IgM/IgY levels. Additionally, CIRC decreased serum malondialdehyde (MDA) content, restored antioxidant enzyme activities, downregulated hepatic transaminase levels, alleviated liver damage, reduced blood bacterial load (3.75×10² vs. 2.82×10³ CFU/mL), lowered lipopolysaccharide (LPS) bioactivity and procalcitonin (PCT) levels, increased the abundance of probiotics (Firmicutes, Bacteroides), reduced pathogenic bacteria (Escherichia-Shigella), and restored intestinal microbiota diversity. This study confirms that CIRC prevents avian colibacillosis through immune regulation, antioxidation, bacteremia inhibition, and intestinal microbiota remodeling, providing experimental evidence for developing green veterinary drugs as antibiotic alternatives.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance (AMR), Avian Pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC), Ilex rotunda Thunb., Cyperus rotundus L., Herb pair extract

Introduction

Avian colibacillosis, caused by Avian Pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC), is a globally prevalent bacterial infectious disease in poultry (Newman, et al., 2021). Chicks are highly susceptible to APEC, which result in high morbidity and mortality (Gomis, et al., 2000; Someya, et al., 2007). In addition to impairing poultry growth and increasing carcass condemnation rates—leading to millions of US dollars in annual economic losses—APEC poses a clear zoonotic risk (Helmy, et al., 2018; Kathayat, et al., 2021). This risk stems from the homology of virulence genes between APEC and human extraintestinal pathogenic Escherichia coli (ExPEC), such as uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC) and neonatal meningitic Escherichia coli (NMEC) (Kathayat, et al., 2021).

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has further compounded the problem. Long-term overuse of antibiotics in animal husbandry has increased APEC drug resistance, with rising resistance rates to commonly used antibiotics (tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, β-lactams) (Mellata, 2013). The widespread dissemination of multidrug-resistant APEC strains not only impairs the efficacy of traditional treatments but also triggers food safety and public health hazards (Denamur, et al., 2021; Nawaz, et al., 2024). Vaccination is a key strategy for APEC prevention and control (Hu, et al., 2022; Joseph, et al., 2023); however, APEC exhibits high serotypic heterogeneity (over 160 serotypes identified to date), and significant differences in virulence factor combinations between strains limit the cross-protective effect of single vaccines (Ghunaim, et al., 2014; Guabiraba and Schouler, 2015; Pokharel, et al., 2023). Thus, developing safe and efficient antibiotic alternatives that do not create or worsen antimicrobial resistance is critical for the green development of the poultry industry (Al-Mnaser, et al., 2022).

Plants of the genus Ilex are known for their antibacterial and anti-inflammatory activities (Paluch, et al., 2021). Ilex rotunda Thunb., a medicine-food homologous traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) — i.e., a substance classified as both food and medicine in TCM — refers to the dried bark or root bark of Ilex rotunda Thunb. (Aquifoliaceae) (Tan, et al., 2024). It contains triterpenoids, phenylpropanoids, and phenols, exhibiting pharmacological effects such as antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and hepatoprotective activities (Paluch, et al., 2021; Su, et al., 2025; Yang, et al., 2018; Zeng, et al., 2022). Previous studies have shown that the 70°C aqueous extract of Ilex leaves and stems has significant antibacterial activity (Noureddine, et al., 2018). Cyperus rotundus L. is the dried rhizome of Cyperus rotundus L. (Cyperaceae); its volatile oil components (e.g., α-cyperone, cyperotundone) exert significant anti-inflammatory and antibacterial effects by regulating signaling pathways such as NF-κB and MAPKs (Amamra, et al., 2026; Pirzada, et al., 2015; Xue, et al., 2023). As a common Asian herbal medicine, Cyperus rotundus L. has attracted increasing attention as a potential source of antibacterial agents (Sharma, et al., 2025). TCM herb pairs, the basic form of TCM compatibility (i.e., the practice of combining two herbs to achieve enhanced efficacy and reduced toxicity), achieve synergistic efficacy and toxicity reduction through the interaction of two herbs. Moreover, co-extraction of two TCMs may induce chemical reactions between components (degradation of original components or formation of new compounds), conferring unique advantages in controlling bacterial infections without contributing to antimicrobial resistance. (Li, et al., 2022; Liu, et al., 2022). Although both Ilex rotunda Thunb. and Cyperus rotundus L. are effective antibacterial agents, no studies have investigated their combined effects in a chick model of drug-resistant APEC bacteremia. Their synergistic role in APEC treatment and the specific mechanism of their components remain unclear.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

Ilex rotunda Thunb. was produced in Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region and identified as the dried bark of Ilex rotunda Thunb. (Aquifoliaceae). Cyperus rotundus L. was produced in Henan Province and identified as the dried rhizome of Cyperus rotundus L. The positive control drug was Shanhua Scutellaria baicalensis Extract Powder, purchased from Shengtailer Technology Co., Ltd.

The multidrug-resistant APEC strain E-37 was isolated and purified from diseased chickens by our research group, belonging to ST type 350. It carries resistance genes (rmtD, tetA, tetM, CTX-M9, OXA-10, qnrB, flor, oqxA, Sul1, Sul2, marA) and virulence genes (iucD, csgA, tsh, aatA, fimC, iss3, ompA, sodA, iss2, kspM, ibeB, yijp, mat, R1, R2), and was preserved in glycerol at -20°C (Zhang, et al., 2022).

A total of 224 one-day-old male Guangxi local chicken chicks (body weight 35±3 g) were purchased from Guangxi Fufeng Animal Husbandry Group Co., Ltd. and reared in the Experimental Animal Room of the Animal Hospital, Guangxi University (Ethics Approval No.: GXU-2023-271). The rearing environment was maintained at 28∼32°C, 60%∼70% humidity, and a 16 h/8 h light/dark cycle. Chicks had free access to 510-type chick feed (Nanjing Fukailai Animal Husbandry Co., Ltd.; nutritional composition shown in Table 1) and sterile drinking water, with a 1-week adaptive feeding period.

Preparation and component identification of Ilex rotunda Thunb.-Cyperus rotundus L. herb pair extract

Extraction and chemical component detection of Cyperus rotundus L. volatile oil

A total of 100 g of Cyperus rotundus L. was weighed, each rhizome cut in half, and soaked in 1 L pure water overnight at a solid-liquid ratio of 1:10 (no crushing or sieving to avoid sedimentation and charring during extraction). Steam distillation was performed: soaked Cyperus rotundus L. was poured into a 2000 mL round-bottom flask, and an electric heating mantle and volatile oil extraction device were assembled. Extraction parameters: heated at 1/3 of 100V for 10 min, adjusted to full 100V for 25 min until boiling; immediately after boiling, voltage was reduced to 1/3 of 100V, and gentle boiling was maintained for 5.5 h until no additional oil was collected. After standing for 1 h, the oil-water mixture was collected and dried with anhydrous sodium sulfate to obtain golden yellow translucent Cyperus rotundus L. volatile oil.

To prepare the extract for GC-TQMS analysis, an appropriate amount of Cyperus rotundus L. volatile oil was diluted to 1.0 mg/mL with n-hexane, centrifuged at 13000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was subjected to GC-TQMS analysis. Gas chromatography conditions: Agilent 122-5532UI DB-5ms Ultra Inert quartz capillary column (30 m×250 μm×0.25 μm); initial column temperature 45°C for 2 min, followed by program heating: 20°C/min to 130°C (held 3 min), 20°C/min to 165°C, 4°C/min to 190°C, 30°C/min to 250°C (held 2 min); carrier gas helium (2.25 mL/min), collision gas nitrogen (1.5 mL/min); injection volume 1 μL, injection port temperature 250°C, split ratio 120:1, split flow rate 144 mL/min. Mass spectrometry conditions: electron impact ion source (EI+) with extractor, ion source temperature 230°C, emission current 35.0 µA, ion focusing voltage -90.0 V, electron energy 70 eV; MS1 and MS2 quadrupole temperatures 150°C; scanning range m/z 69∼502, data acquisition mode: full scan. Compounds were identified using MassHunter software, combined with NIST 2017 mass spectral database search (absolute peak area ≥1 million, matching degree ≥75), and verified by mass spectral fragments, molecular formula, and structure.

Preparation and chemical component detection of Ilex rotunda Thunb.-Cyperus rotundus L. aqueous extract

A total of 100 g of Ilex rotunda Thunb. was weighed and soaked in 1 L pure water for 6 h at a solid-liquid ratio of 1:10. The soaked Ilex rotunda Thunb. was mixed with Cyperus rotundus L. residue (after volatile oil extraction) and the remaining medicinal water (solid-liquid ratio maintained at 1:10). The mixture was boiled over high heat (170∼180°C), adjusted to gentle heat (120°C) for 1.5 h, and the first filtrate was collected by gauze filtration. The filter residue was re-extracted twice: 1.6 L pure water (1:8 solid-liquid ratio) boiled over high heat then gentle heat for 1 h (second filtrate), and 1.2 L pure water (1:6 solid-liquid ratio) boiled over high heat then gentle heat for 0.5 h (third filtrate). The three filtrates were combined, concentrated to 100 mL by rotary evaporation, pre-frozen at -80°C, and lyophilized to powder. The light brownish yellow to brownish yellow lyophilized powder (1 g equivalent to ∼ 4.8 g crude herb pair (1:1)) was stored for use.

To prepare the extract for UHPLC-Q-Orbitrap HRMS analysis, the lyophilized powder was reconstituted as follows. A 10 mL sterile centrifuge tube was used to accurately weigh 10.05 mg of lyophilized extract (after peeling), dissolved in 10 mL 50% methanol (1.0 mg/mL), transferred to a volumetric flask, and sealed (weight 143.69 g). The flask was ultrasonicated (44°C, 250 W, 40 kHz) for 30 min, reweighed after cooling to room temperature (143.59 g), and the weight loss was supplemented with 50% methanol. After thorough mixing, the solution was centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane for UHPLC-Q-Orbitrap HRMS analysis. Liquid chromatography conditions: ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (50 mm×2.1 mm, 1.7 μm), column temperature 30°C, autosampler temperature 10°C; negative ion (ESI-) mode, mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in water) and B (methanol); gradient elution: 0∼2 min 95% A, 2∼13 min 95% A→0% A, 13∼16 min 0% A, 16∼16.1 min 0% A→95% A, 16.1∼19 min 95% A; flow rate 0.3 mL/min, injection volume 2 μL. Mass spectrometry conditions: heated electrospray ionization (HESI) at 350°C; spray voltage 3.0 kV (negative ion mode), transfer capillary temperature 320°C, sheath gas 35 psi, auxiliary gas 10 psi; Full MS/ddMS2 scanning mode, mass range 200∼2000 m/z, primary/secondary resolution 70000/17500; collision gas: high-purity nitrogen. Compounds were identified using Compound Discoverer 3.3 SP1 software, combined with accurate relative molecular mass, mass spectral fragments, retention time, and error value.

Establishment of a multidrug-resistant APEC infection model in chicks

Activation of APEC and determination of bacterial solution concentration

Glycerol-preserved APEC E-37 was inoculated into LB broth and cultured overnight. The overnight culture was then streaked onto LB agar plates and incubated at 37°C to obtain single colonies. A single colony was selected, inoculated into fresh LB broth, and cultured to the logarithmic growth phase (expanded). The bacterial culture was then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min, and the pellet was resuspended in sterile PBS. The original bacterial solution was serially diluted to 10⁻⁶, 10⁻⁷, 10⁻⁸, and 10⁻⁹, with four LB agar plates per gradient. After 15 h of culture at 37°C, colonies were counted, and the original bacterial concentration was calculated as 5.0×10⁹ CFU/mL.

Determination of median lethal dose (LD₅₀)

A total of 64 15-day-old healthy chicks (body weight 80±7 g) were randomly divided into 4 groups (16 chicks/group): Challenge Group 1 (E37-1 group, 5.0×10⁹ CFU/mL, 1 mL/chick), Challenge Group 2 (E37-2 group, 5.0×10⁸ CFU/mL, 1 mL/chick), Challenge Group 3 (E37-3 group, 5.0×10⁷ CFU/mL, 1 mL/chick), and Blank Control Group (1×PBS, 1 mL/chick), all injected subcutaneously under the wing (0.5 mL/side). After challenge, mortality was recorded every 2 h, and symptoms were observed for 24 consecutive hours. LD₅₀ was calculated using the improved Karber’s method: LD₅₀=lg⁻¹[Xm - i (ΣP - 0.5)] (Xm: logarithm of the maximum dose, i: difference between logarithmic doses of adjacent groups, P: mortality of each group, ΣP: sum of mortalities).

Model evaluation

After challenge, chick mental state, and fecal traits were observed, and mortality was counted at 24 h. Mental state was assessed based on activity level (active, depressed, or moribund), response to external stimuli (normal, sluggish, or unresponsive), and posture (upright, hunched, or recumbent). Fecal traits were classified as normal (formed, brown), loose (semi‑formed, yellowish), or watery (unformed, yellow‑green). Appetite was evaluated by feed intake relative to the control group (normal, reduced, or anorexia). Dead chicks were dissected to check for typical lesions (pericarditis, perihepatitis, turbid air sacs) in the liver, heart, and air sacs to confirm successful model establishment (Robinson, et al., 2025; Usman, et al., 2022).

Preventive administration experiment of Ilex rotunda Thunb.-Cyperus rotundus L. herb pair extract on avian colibacillosis in chickens

Animal experimental design

After 1 week of adaptive feeding, 160 chicks were randomly divided into 8 groups (20 chicks/group) with the following treatments: Blank Control Group (Control): subcutaneous injection of 1.0 mL 1×PBS, basic diet + sterile water; Infected Model Group (Model): subcutaneous injection of 1.0 mL 1.0×10⁹ CFU/mL APEC E-37, basic diet + sterile water; Positive Drug Group (Positive): same challenge as Model Group, basic diet + 0.5 g/kg Shanhua Scutellaria baicalensis Extract Powder (mixed feeding) + sterile water; CIRC Low-Dose Group (CIRC-L): same challenge as Model Group, basic diet + sterile water + 50 mg/L Cyperus rotundus L. volatile oil + 0.25 g/L lyophilized powder (drinking water); CIRC Medium-Dose Group (CIRC-M): same challenge as Model Group, basic diet + sterile water + 100 mg/L Cyperus rotundus L. volatile oil + 0.50 g/L lyophilized powder (drinking water); CIRC High-Dose Group (CIRC-H): same challenge as Model Group, basic diet + sterile water + 200 mg/L Cyperus rotundus L. volatile oil + 1.00 g/L lyophilized powder (drinking water); High-Dose Control Group (CIRC): subcutaneous injection of 1.0 mL 1×PBS, basic diet + sterile water + 200 mg/L Cyperus rotundus L. volatile oil + 1.00 g/L lyophilized powder (drinking water); Vehicle Control Group (Vehicle): subcutaneous injection of 1.0 mL 1×PBS, basic diet + sterile water + surfactant (anhydrous ethanol:Tween 80=1:2, same concentration as CIRC-H).

Cyperus rotundus L. volatile oil emulsion was prepared with Tween 80 (surfactant) and anhydrous ethanol (co-surfactant) at a mass ratio of volatile oil:ethanol:Tween 80=1:2:4, diluted with sterile water to form a water emulsion, and mixed with lyophilized powder to obtain CIRC. Preventive administration was performed continuously for 7 days (8∼14 days of age): CIRC via drinking water, positive drug via mixed feeding (per instructions). On day 15, chicks were challenged with the LD₅₀ dose of 1.027×10⁹ CFU/mL (determined in section “Determination of Median Lethal Dose (LD₅₀)”). This sublethal dose was chosen to induce significant pathology and bacteremia while allowing sufficient survival for sample collection. At 24 h post-challenge, all surviving chicks per group (n = 11–20) were used for blood, immune organ, and liver sample collection. At 36 h post-challenge, 6 randomly selected surviving chicks per group were used for bacteremia indicator detection (blood bacterial load, serum/liver LPS, and serum PCT).

Detection of growth performance

On the mornings of day 8 and day 15, chicks were weighed individually after fasting, and body weight data were recorded. Daily feed supply and residual feed were recorded to calculate actual feed intake. Average Daily Gain (ADG) = total weight gain / (feeding days × number of chicks per group); Average Daily Feed Intake (ADFI) = total feed intake / (feeding days × number of chicks per group); Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR) = ADFI/ADG.

Detection of immune function

Twenty-four hours post-challenge, mortality was calculated as (number of dead chicks / total chicks per group) ×100%. Spleen, thymus, and bursa of Fabricius from surviving chicks were collected, blotted dry to remove surface moisture and fat, and weighed. Immune organ index = immune organ weight (mg) / body weight (g). Jugular vein blood was collected, coagulated at room temperature for 1 h, centrifuged at 3000 rpm (4°C) for 10 min to obtain serum, and stored at -80°C. Serum IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, IgM, and IgG levels were detected using ELISA kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) per instructions. OD values were measured with a microplate reader, and sample concentrations were calculated using standard curves.

Detection of antioxidant and hepatic function

Serum ALT, AST, GSH, MDA, CAT, and T-SOD levels were detected using ELISA kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) as described for immune function. Liver tissue was fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 48 h, dehydrated with gradient ethanol (70%∼100%, 1∼2 h/grade), cleared with xylene (twice, 30 min each), embedded in paraffin (twice, 1∼2 h each), sectioned into 4∼6 μm slices, baked at 60∼65°C for 30 min∼1 h, HE-stained, and mounted with neutral gum. Liver pathological changes were observed under a microscope.

Detection of bacteremia indicators

Thirty-six hours post-challenge, 6 chicks per group were selected, and sterile jugular vein blood was collected into heparin sodium anticoagulant tubes. A 60 μL aliquot of blood was spread evenly on MacConkey agar plates, cultured at 37°C for 24 h, and colonies were counted. Serum and liver lipopolysaccharide (LPS) bioactivity (expressed as endotoxin units, EU) was measured using a chromogenic endpoint assay (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute). Serum procalcitonin (PCT) concentration was measured using an ELISA kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute). All procedures were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Detection of intestinal microbiota diversity

Cecal contents were collected from 5 randomly selected chicks per group (Control, Model, and CIRC‑H) at 24 h post‑challenge. Total DNA was extracted, quantified by Nanodrop, and qualified by 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 338F (5′‑ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA‑3′) and 806R (5′‑GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT‑3′). Amplified products were purified with magnetic beads, quantified using the PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit and microplate reader, and sequenced using the Illumina TruSeq Nano kit (Suzhou Panomic Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd.). Raw data were quality-screened and de-redundant, then clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity using QIIME2 (the OTU‑based approach remains widely accepted in microbiome studies). Alpha diversity (rarefaction curve), beta diversity (principal coordinate analysis), and species composition (phylum/genus level relative abundance) were analyzed. Alpha diversity indices (Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, Pielou_e) were compared among groups using one‑way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Beta diversity was assessed by PCoA based on Bray‑Curtis distances with PERMANOVA (999 permutations). Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis was used to identify differential species between groups. The raw sequencing data will be deposited in a public repository (NCBI SRA or CNGBdb) upon acceptance of the manuscript, and the accession number will be provided in the revised version.

Data statistics

Experimental data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA using SPSS 22.0 software. A P-value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results were expressed as Mean ± SD. Asterisks in figures indicate P < 0.05 (*) or P < 0.01 (**).. Graphs were plotted using GraphPad Prism 8.3 and Origin 2025 software. For intestinal microbiota analysis, alpha diversity indices (Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, and Pielou_e) were compared among groups using one‑way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Beta diversity was assessed by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distances, and statistical differences were evaluated using PERMANOVA (permutational multivariate analysis of variance) with 999 permutations. All microbiota data analyses were performed on the Biodeep cloud platform (https://www.biodeep.cn).

Successful establishment of multidrug-resistant APEC infection model in chicks

After activation, the original APEC concentration was 5.0×10⁹ CFU/mL (plate counting). Infection route screening showed that subcutaneous injection under the wing resulted in mortality, pathological characteristics, and symptoms consistent with natural avian colibacillosis, thus selected as the infection route. Post-challenge, Challenge Group 1 (5.0×10⁹ CFU/mL) began dying at 2 h (24 h mortality 100%); Challenge Group 2 (5.0×10⁸ CFU/mL) began dying at 6 h (24 h mortality 18.75%); no deaths occurred in Challenge Group 3 (5.0×10⁷ CFU/mL) or the Blank Control Group within 24 h (Fig. 2). The LD₅₀ of E-37 in 15-day-old chicks was calculated as 1.027×10⁹ CFU/mL (improved Karber’s method, Table 4). Model Group chicks exhibited typical symptoms (mental depression, anorexia, yellow-green loose stools), and pathological dissection showed "pericarditis, perihepatitis, and turbid air sacs", confirming successful model establishment.

Survival Curves of Chicks Challenged with Different Doses of Multidrug-Resistant APEC. Chicks were challenged via subcutaneous injection under the wing with E37 at concentrations of 5.0×10⁹ CFU/mL (E37-1), 5.0×10⁸ CFU/mL (E37-2), and 5.0×10⁷ CFU/mL (E37-3), with a blank control group (Control). The E37-1 group showed 100% mortality within 24 h, the E37-2 group showed 18.75% mortality, while no deaths occurred in the E37-3 or Control groups within 24 h.

Improvement effect of Ilex rotunda Thunb.-Cyperus rotundus L. herb pair on growth performance of infected chicks

Mortality and clinical symptoms after challenge

Twenty-four hours post-challenge, Model Group mortality was 60%, with symptoms including huddling, closed eyes, immobility, anorexia, and yellow-green loose stools. Mortalities of CIRC low, medium, and high dose groups were 45%, 40%, and 30%, respectively, and 35% in the Positive Drug Group, with significantly alleviated symptoms compared to the Model Group (Fig. 3A).

Growth performance during preventive administration (before challenge)

During the preventive administration period from 8 to 14 days of age, the growth performance of chicks in each group is shown in Fig. 3B-D. Compared with the Control group (3.11 ± 0.35 g/d) and the Positive group (3.10 ± 0.17 g/d), the low-dose (CIRC-L) and high-dose (CIRC-H) CIRC groups exhibited significantly higher ADG (3.43 ± 0.12 g/d and 3.68 ± 0.02 g/d, respectively; P < 0.05 or P < 0.01). No significant differences in ADFI were observed between the CIRC-treated groups and the Control group (P > 0.05), with ADFI values of all CIRC groups ranging from 10.48 ± 0.41 g/d (CIRC-M) to 11.05 ± 0.13 g/d (CIRC-H), while the Positive group showed a slightly higher ADFI (11.26 ± 0.85 g/d) compared with the Control group (10.33 ± 0.44 g/d, P < 0.05). In terms of feed conversion ratio (FCR), all CIRC-treated groups (CIRC-L, CIRC-M, and CIRC-H) exhibited significantly lower FCR values (3.22 ± 0.18, 3.21 ± 0.14, and 3.01 ± 0.02, respectively) compared with the Positive group (3.63 ± 0.19, P < 0.05 or P < 0.01); notably, the CIRC-H group also showed a significantly lower FCR compared with the Control group (3.35 ± 0.31, P < 0.01). These results indicate that CIRC can effectively improve the growth performance of chicks by increasing ADG and reducing FCR without significantly affecting ADFI.

Pathological changes of liver tissue

To more intuitively observe the extent of liver tissue damage, we prepared pathological sections of chick liver tissue. Histopathological evaluation was performed by a certified pathologist at Servicebio (Wuhan, China), a company specializing in histological analysis. No formal scoring system was applied; the assessment was based on descriptive observation as shown below. As shown in Fig. 8, the liver tissues of chicks in the Control, CIRC, and Vehicle groups were normal. Hepatocytes were irregularly polygonal with large, round nuclei, and hepatic sinusoids showed no obvious compression or dilation; no significant inflammatory cell infiltration was observed. In the Model group, occasional hepatocyte edema (black arrows) was noted, characterized by cellular swelling, loose and pale cytoplasm, and compression of surrounding hepatic sinusoids, with rare focal perivenous lymphocyte infiltration (blue arrows). In the Positive group, a small number of hepatocytes showed vacuolar degeneration (yellow arrows), with circular vacuoles of varying sizes visible in the cytoplasm. In the low-dose CIRC group (CIRC-L), a small number of hepatocytes exhibited edema (black arrows), with cellular swelling, loose and pale cytoplasm, and compression of surrounding hepatic sinusoids, accompanied by a few hepatocytes showing vacuolar degeneration (yellow arrows) with tiny circular vacuoles in the cytoplasm. In the medium-dose CIRC group (CIRC-M), a small number of hepatocytes displayed mild edema (black arrows), with loose and pale cytoplasm. In the CIRC-H, a small number of hepatocytes exhibited vacuolar degeneration (yellow arrows), with circular vacuoles of varying sizes visible in the cytoplasm. These results indicate that CIRC can effectively reduce the liver tissue damage caused by Escherichia coli infection in chicks.

(C, D) Species relative abundance at the phylum and genus levels. ##P < 0.01 vs Control; *P < 0.05 vs Positive; **P < 0.01 vs Positive.

LEfSe analysis showed significant enrichment of Lactobacillus and its affiliates (Lactobacillaceae, Lactobacillales) in the Model Group; the Control Group was characterized by Clostridia, Clostridiales, and Bifidobacterium; only Enterococcus showed differences in the CIRC-H group, suggesting a targeted regulatory effect on specific microbial groups (Fig. 10B).

Species relative abundance plots at different taxonomic levels provided a direct visualization of compositional differences at the phylum and genus levels. In terms of species abundance, the Model group exhibited a significantly increased relative abundance of Firmicutes, a decreased relative abundance of Bacteroidetes, an increased abundance of potential pathogens such as Escherichia-Shigella, and a decreased abundance of beneficial genera including Ruminococcus and Bacteroides. In contrast, the microbiota structure of the CIRC-H group approached that of the Control group, with a reversion in the relative abundance of Firmicutes, a recovery in the abundance of beneficial genera, and a reduction in the abundance of pathogens (Figs. 10C–D).

Microbiota diversity analysis

Community structure indices, including the Chao1, Simpson, Shannon, and Pielou_e indices, were compared among different groups using box plots. These indices reflect the richness and evenness of the microbial community from different perspectives, and the results are shown in Fig. 11A.

Effects of CIRC-H on Gut Microbiota Diversity in Infected Chicks. (A) Box plots of α-diversity indices (Chao1, Simpson, Shannon, Pielou_e, etc.). (B) Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) of microbial community structure based on Bray-Curtis distance.

Alpha-diversity analysis revealed that the Shannon, Simpson, and Pielou_e indices were significantly decreased in the Model group (P < 0.01). The high-dose CIRC group restored the evenness and diversity of the gut microbiota, showing no significant difference compared with the Control group (P > 0.05). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was conducted to reveal differences in microbial community structure among groups. As presented in Fig. 11B, the three groups formed distinct clusters. The microbial community structure of the high-dose CIRC group was significantly separated from that of the Model group (P < 0.01) and partially overlapped with the Control group, suggesting that CIRC can reshape the gut microbial community structure.

Discussion

The global spread of AMR is a critical challenge threatening animal husbandry sustainability and public health security (Arbab, et al., 2025; Chandra Deb, et al., 2023). As the primary pathogen of avian colibacillosis, multidrug-resistant APEC not only fails clinical treatment but also enhances zoonotic risk due to virulence gene homology with human extraintestinal pathogenic Escherichia coli (ExPEC) (Hu, et al., 2024; Sgariglia, et al., 2019). Vaccination is limited by high serotypic heterogeneity and insufficient cross-protection, making antibiotic alternatives an urgent priority (Kathayat, et al., 2021; Redweik, et al., 2020). Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) herb pairs, with synergistic pharmacological effects, offer unique advantages in controlling bacterial infections without contributing to AMR. Unlike single antibiotics that target a single pathway and readily select for resistance, TCM herb pairs exert multi‑target actions including direct antibacterial activity, immune modulation, anti‑inflammatory effects, and gut microbiota regulation. Additionally, the combination of two herbs can enhance efficacy while reducing toxicity of individual components, and the co‑extraction process may induce chemical transformations that generate new bioactive compounds (Liu, et al., 2022; Millar, et al., 2021). Clarifying their material basis and mechanism is key to their modernization and industrialization (Chi, et al., 2026; Liu, et al., 2022; Millar, et al., 2021; Wang, et al., 2024).

Ilex rotunda Thunb. and Cyperus rotundus L. are potent antibacterial agents, yet their combination application lacks systematic validation in target animal models. The core finding of this study is the identification of the material basis of the CIRC and verification of its preventive efficacy in chicks. CIRC contains 93 identified bioactive compounds, with cyperotundone, α-cyperone, caffeic acid, and oleanolic acid as core components. Their effects form the material basis for CIRC’s multi-target efficacy. Cyperotundone (26.51% of Cyperus rotundus L. volatile oil) and α-cyperone (4.85%) inhibit inflammatory factor release by downregulating NF-κB (Jung, et al., 2013; Setiawan, et al., 2024) and MAPKs signaling pathways (Edres, et al., 2025; Zhang, et al., 2021), while caffeic acid and oleanolic acid (from Ilex rotunda Thunb. aqueous extract) exert antioxidant and hepatoprotective activities (Han, et al., 2022; Pan, et al., 2025; Shao, et al., 2020). The combined steam distillation-water extraction method enriches both volatile and water-soluble components, ensuring activity integrity (Sun, et al., 2017). CIRC significantly reduces multidrug-resistant APEC-induced chick mortality, improves growth performance and organ function, and constructs a quadruple synergistic prevention network: "immune regulation - antioxidant protection - bacteremia blockage - intestinal microbiota remodeling". This confirms the scientific rationale of TCM for multi-pathway bacterial disease control, providing a new paradigm for antibiotic alternative development.

Growth performance improvement is an intuitive manifestation of CIRC’s preventive effect. CIRC significantly increased chick ADG and reduced FCR without affecting ADFI, consistent with studies showing baicalin improves poultry growth performance (Liu, et al., 2025; Zhou, et al., 2019). We speculate that multidrug-resistant APEC infection induces intestinal mucosal inflammation(Indhuprakash, et al., 2024), impairs epithelial integrity (Zha, et al., 2025), and reduces nutrient absorption efficiency (Cao, et al., 2025; Li, et al., 2025). Cyperus rotundus L. in CIRC alleviates the TCM concept of disordered intestinal qi movement, which leads to poor nutrient absorption (Lu, et al., 2022). Ilex rotunda Thunb. reduces mucosal inflammatory damage through its anti‑inflammatory activity (Kim, et al., 2023; Li, et al., 2024). Together, they may help mitigate intestinal inflammation-induced nutrient absorption interference via the “dampness-eliminating and diarrhea-stopping” and “anti-inflammatory and repairing” pathways (Hu, et al., 2025; Zhou, et al., 2025), which could contribute to the improved growth performance observed during the preventive phase (8–14 days of age). However, because feed conversion was not measured after infection, we cannot directly conclude that CIRC improved feed conversion efficiency during the infection period. The potential economic benefits of CIRC in commercial settings, as well as its long-term effects on growth performance, require further investigation (Deng, et al., 2025).

Immune regulation is the core pathway of CIRC’s preventive effect, reflected in synergistic improvements in immune organs, cytokines, and antibody responses. The 15-day-old chick immune system is immature, and multidrug-resistant APEC infection easily triggers "immune disorder - inflammatory storm", characterized by spleen swelling (immune organ compensatory hyperplasia), thymus and bursa of Fabricius atrophy (immune cell production impairment), elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, and decreased antibody levels (Chen, et al., 2025; Sun, et al., 2015; Ye, et al., 2025). Prophylactic CIRC significantly reverses this pathology: reduced spleen index may alleviate immune organ inflammatory edema, while increased thymus and bursa of Fabricius indices may promote T/B lymphocyte maturation (Shi, et al., 2022). This is similar to Xiaochaihu Decoction’s (XCHD, a classic TCM formula for anti-inflammation and immune regulation) regulation of avian Salmonella-infected chick immune organ indices (Yang, et al., 2024), but CIRC exhibits a more significant reversing effect on bursa of Fabricius atrophy, which is speculated to relate to α-cyperone’s unique activity in promoting lymphoid tissue development (from Cyperus rotundus L. volatile oil) (Zhang, et al., 2024). Notably, APEC infection’s effects on immune organ indices vary across studies, potentially due to strain serotype, virulence factor combination, and infection dose (Chen, et al., 2025; Nawaz, et al., 2026; Sun and Li, 2019). The multidrug-resistant APEC strain E-37 (carrying multiple virulence genes such as iss3 and ompA) may exacerbate lymphoid organ damage, indirectly confirming CIRC’s protective value against highly virulent strains (Zhou, et al., 2016). Additionally, CIRC rebalances cytokines by inhibiting excessive IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α release and increasing IL-10 levels. This mechanism is similar to luteolin’s inhibition of the HMGB1/TLR4/NF-κB signaling axis (Cao, et al., 2022) and baicalin’s regulation of the IL-17 pathway (Wu, et al., 2020), indicating evolutionary conservation of TCM compound-mediated inflammatory signaling regulation (Zhang, et al., 2025). For immunologically immature chicks, early infection-induced rapid antibody consumption increases mortality (Ji, et al., 2021); CIRC slows IgM and IgY decline, potentially enhancing adaptive immunity by promoting B cell proliferation/differentiation and inhibiting antibody degradation, thereby complementing innate immunity. This is consistent with resveratrol’s improvement of infected broiler antibody levels (He, et al., 2023).

Oxidative stress and liver damage protection are important supplements to CIRC’s preventive effect, forming a "damage - repair" synergistic regulatory network (Yu, et al., 2024). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) released by multidrug-resistant APEC infection induces oxidative stress via TLR4/NF-κB pathway activation, leading to MDA (lipid peroxidation product) accumulation and inhibited antioxidant enzyme (GSH, CAT, T-SOD) activities (Xing, et al., 2021). As the core antioxidant enzyme synthesis organ, liver damage exacerbates the oxidative stress vicious cycle. The CIRC high-dose group significantly reduces MDA levels and restores antioxidant enzyme activities, potentially via Nrf2-Keap1 pathway activation (Ding, et al., 2023). Nrf2 (core oxidative stress transcription factor) upregulates antioxidant enzyme (SOD, CAT) gene expression (de Castro Trigueira, et al., 2025; Janko, et al., 2023); oleanolic acid is a natural Nrf2 activator (Hong, et al., 2023; Yuan, et al., 2025), and caffeic acid directly scavenges reactive oxygen species (Kalthoff, et al., 2020). Meanwhile, CIRC downregulates ALT and AST levels and alleviates hepatocyte edema and inflammatory infiltration, which is similar to dihydromyricetin’s protection against LPS-induced chicken hepatocyte damage, blocking the "oxidative stress - hepatocyte necrosis - inflammatory infiltration" cascade via "antioxidation - anti-inflammation" synergy (Yuan, et al., 2025). Liver function protection reduces organ damage and ensures metabolic/immune auxiliary functions, supporting chick infection resistance (Tanaka, et al., 2025).

In this study, APEC directly entered chick blood via subcutaneous wing injection (bypassing natural infection invasion/colonization steps), inducing bacteremia or sepsis (Holmes, et al., 2021). Previous studies show α-cyperone inhibits APEC-O78-induced actin cytoskeleton polymerization by downregulating Nck-2, Cdc42, and Rac1 expression, reducing APEC-O78 adhesion to chicken type II lung cells (Zhang, et al., 2014). In LPS-induced RAW 264.7 macrophages, Ilex rotunda Thunb.-derived caffeic acid derivatives significantly inhibit pro-inflammatory mediator (nitric oxide) and cytokine (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-8) production, and reduce inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), and phosphorylated extracellular signal-regulated kinase (pERK1/2) expression (Kim, et al., 2023). This experiment shows CIRC effectively reduces chick blood APEC counts, indicating its active components may directly or indirectly inhibit APEC survival/proliferation in the blood. Further bacteremia evaluation (serum/liver LPS, serum PCT) shows CIRC reduces LPS bioactivity and PCT concentration, confirming its ability to block avian colibacillosis-induced bacteremia.

This study clarifies CIRC’s regulation of chick intestinal microbiota via Venn diagram, LEfSe, species relative abundance, and α/β diversity analyses. Significant microbial community differences exist between the Model and Control groups; the CIRC-H group has more unique OTUs, indicating CIRC reshapes specific microbiota structures—consistent with Mirjam et al.’s finding that probiotic intervention alters intestinal microbiota composition (unique OTUs) to improve microecology (Bloemendaal, et al., 2021). The Model Group is enriched with Lactobacillus and affiliates, the Control Group with Clostridia and affiliates, and the CIRC-H group with differential Enterococcus levels (Ren, et al., 2023). During intestinal infection, the enrichment of Lactobacillus likely represents a dysregulated response to infection rather than a beneficial change. The CIRC-induced shift of dominant microbiota toward a profile more similar to the Control group aligns with Rui et al.’s study showing TCM compounds regulate and restore intestinal dominant microbiota balance (Rui, et al., 2021). Additionally, CIRC improves infected chick intestinal microbiota structure (closer to healthy hosts), characterized by increased Firmicutes relative abundance, decreased potential pathogens (Escherichia-Shigella), and recovered probiotics (Ruminococcus, Bacteroides). CIRC also increases intestinal microbiota α/β diversity, restoring infection-induced imbalance—similar to berberine’s microbiota-regulating, health-promoting effects (an isoquinoline alkaloid with known microbiota-regulating effects) (Yan, et al., 2022). Probiotics enhance intestinal barrier function, reduce pathogen invasion, and activate the immune system via short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production (Wang, et al., 2023); increased Firmicutes correlates with improved metabolic health and immune function (Milano, et al., 2022). Thus, CIRC may indirectly enhance chick immune function via microbiota regulation, alleviating APEC-induced inflammatory responses and immune organ pathology.

As a key link between intestinal health and systemic homeostasis, intestinal microbiota imbalance impairs intestinal barrier function, triggering endotoxemia and systemic inflammatory responses, which are closely linked to oxidative stress. In this study, Model Group intestinal microbiota imbalance coincided with increased serum MDA and decreased antioxidant enzyme (GSH, CAT, and T-SOD) activities, which further confirms the close interaction between intestinal microbiota imbalance and oxidative stress (Mahdavi-Roshan, et al., 2022). This mutual promotion forms a vicious cycle that exacerbates chick tissue damage and infection severity: intestinal microbiota disorder impairs the intestinal barrier, leading to increased LPS absorption and systemic oxidative stress; in turn, oxidative stress disrupts the growth environment of intestinal microorganisms, further aggravating microbiota imbalance (Mahdavi‑Roshan et al., 2022). CIRC breaks this vicious cycle by reshaping the intestinal microbiota structure and enhancing antioxidant capacity, achieving synergistic protection against multidrug-resistant APEC-induced damage. This finding is consistent with previous studies that intestinal microbiota and oxidative stress are mutually regulated in bacterial infections, and further expands the understanding of TCM herb pairs in multi-pathway synergistic regulation (Erega, et al., 2021; Kiousi, et al., 2023).

Despite these findings, this study still has several limitations. First, the specific contribution of individual active components in CIRC and the synergistic mode between components have not been clarified. In the future, it is necessary to analyze the weight of core components such as cyperotundone and caffeic acid in different effects such as immune regulation and antioxidation through single-component verification and combination experiments. Second, this study only targeted the ST350 multidrug-resistant APEC strain, and the cross-protective effect of CIRC on other serotypes of APEC still needs to be evaluated, which is the key to its wide application. Finally, there is a lack of long-term administration safety data and field application effect verification. It is necessary to clarify its applicability in actual production through large-scale breeding experiments, and pay attention to its compatibility with other feed additives. Future research can focus on the screening of core components and formulation optimization, explore its prevention and control mechanisms against different serotypes of APEC, and further analyze the molecular details of CIRC regulating signaling pathways such as Nrf2 and NF-κB, providing more comprehensive support for its industrial production and clinical application.

In summary, through the synergistic effect of its bioactive components, CIRC constructs a multi-dimensional prevention network of "immune regulation - antioxidant protection - bacteremia blockage - intestinal microbiota remodeling", which effectively prevents avian colibacillosis caused by multidrug-resistant APEC. This study not only provides a scientific basis for the modernization development of TCM herb pairs but also offers a feasible solution for the animal husbandry industry to reduce antibiotic use and cope with AMR, which is of great significance for promoting green and healthy breeding and safeguarding public health security.

Conclusion

This study clarified the material basis of CIRC, with a total of 93 compounds identified and cyperotundone, α-cyperone, caffeic acid and oleanolic acid as the core bioactive components. It was confirmed that CIRC exerts a significant preventive effect on avian colibacillosis induced by multidrug-resistant APEC, and its efficacy is achieved through multiple pathways including regulating immune function, alleviating oxidative stress and hepatic injury, blocking the progression of bacteremia, and remodeling the balance of intestinal microbiota. This study provides a new candidate for green veterinary drugs to replace antibiotics, and the multi-pathway preventive mechanism of CIRC also provides a theoretical basis for the development of TCM herb pairs for the control of multidrug-resistant bacterial diseases in poultry.
