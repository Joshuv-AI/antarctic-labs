Dynamic tumor tracking for locally advanced pancreatic cancer: precision in motion
Authors: Jason Gurewitz, Jennifer B. Permuth, Sarah Hoffe, Jessica Frakes
Published: 2026

The primary challenge in definitive radiotherapy (RT) for locally advanced pancreatic cancer (LAPC) is no longer whether we can deliver dose precisely, but whether we can deliver ablative dose safely. We commend Yoshimura and colleagues for their rigorous work presenting the first multi-institutional phase II study evaluating dynamic tumor tracking (DTT) with moderately hypofractionated intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) for LAPC post-induction systemic therapy (1). This prospective trial enrolled 25 patients with LAPC across four Japanese institutions who had up to one course of induction gemcitabine or S-1, had a range of respiratory motion ≥10 mm, and confirmation that DTT-IMRT was feasible after insertion of a single gold fiducial marker. Patients were treated to 48 Gy in 15 fractions using the Vero4DRT gimbal-mounted linear accelerator system with concurrent gemcitabine or S-1.

The study met its primary endpoint, achieving a 1-year freedom from locoregional progression (FFLP) rate of 75.3%. They reported acute grade 3 gastrointestinal (GI) toxicity occurring in 8% of patients, 8% of patients having late grade 3 GI toxicity, and no grade 4 or 5 nonhematologic toxicities were observed. The median overall survival (OS) of 13.9 months reflects the systemic nature of this disease, with 76% of patients having distant metastases at first recurrence. In a disease dominated by systemic failure, what is the marginal value of increasingly sophisticated local motion management?

The imperative of motion management

The argument for respiratory motion management in pancreatic RT is strong. The pancreas exhibits substantial respiratory excursion, with the authors reporting a median respiratory motion range of 9.8 mm (range: 3.5–27.3 mm) during treatment. The proximity of radiosensitive organs at risk (OARs), the duodenum, stomach and small bowel, combined with respiratory motion, challenge the delicate balance between tumor dose escalation and coverage vs. normal tissue protection.

The clinical importance of dose escalation in LAPC has been demonstrated in multiple retrospective analyses. Krishnan et al. (2) reported that delivering a biologically effective dose (BED10) greater than 70 Gy improved OS compared to lower doses (17.8 vs. 15.0 months, P=0.03). A National Cancer Database analysis confirmed that escalated-dose RT (BED10 >70 Gy) is associated with improved survival, with median OS of 14.5 months for escalated-dose vs. 13.0 months for conventional-dose RT [adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) 0.85, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.80–0.91, P<0.001] (3). The NRG Oncology International Consensus Contouring Atlas has now codified target volumes and dosing strategies for dose-escalated pancreatic RT, reflecting the growing acceptance of this approach (4). While we await the results of large randomized controlled trials comparing dose escalation to conventional, dose escalation has moved further from the experimental, and closer to a standard we should strive for when anatomy permits. So the question becomes, how do we safely dose escalate?

The Vero4DRT system: technical considerations

The Vero4DRT system represents a novel approach to real-time tumor tracking, and understanding its capabilities is necessary to evaluate whether it can support treating to ablative dosing. The system features a gimbaled X-ray head capable of pan and tilt rotations of ±2.4° (approximately 4 cm) excursion at isocenter plane, along two orthogonal axes, enabling the therapeutic beam to follow tumor motion in real time (5). The system utilizes a correlation model [four-dimensional (4D) model] between infrared markers on the abdominal wall and the implanted gold fiducial marker to predict tumor position, with the gimbaled head continuously adjusting to track the target. The gimbaled X-ray head is fixed on an O-ring gantry allowing rotation along the O-ring structure, and rotation around the vertical axis provides the ability for non-coplanar beam delivery. On-board imaging with dual kV X-ray and electronic portal imaging device (EPID) allows for cone-beam computed tomography (CT) for image-guided setup, as well as intra-fraction visualization and verification of the dynamic treatment field with respect to the target (6,7). This surrogate-based approach introduces a layer of uncertainty absent from direct visualization. While the system does everything technically possible to minimize this uncertainty, it is unclear whether potential phase decoupling, baseline shifts, or drifting correlation between external and internal motion, could be clinically meaningful at ablative dose levels.

Previous phantom studies of the X-ray-based tracking system demonstrated root-mean-squared total tracking errors of 1.2 mm (range 0.9–1.8 mm) for patient respiratory patterns, with mechanical errors contributing negligibly (0.1–0.5 mm) to total system error (8). In this study, the reported median tracking accuracy was 2.6 mm, with tracking error ≤3 mm in 80.8% and ≤4 mm in 96.5% of fractions. From a practical standpoint, this level of performance justifies tighter planning target volume (PTV) margins. The limiting factor is not the tracking itself, but rather our willingness to escalate dose adjacent to OARs, which is further complicated by the natural peristaltic motion of GI organs.

Comparison with alternative dynamic tracking systems

Multiple other platforms can achieve accurate dynamic tracking. The CyberKnife Synchrony (9) system is an established alternative, using a lightweight 6 megavoltage (MV) linear accelerator mounted on a robotic arm, with respiratory tracking accomplished through correlation between external infrared markers and internal fiducials detected on periodic stereoscopic kilovoltage images. CyberKnife has demonstrated submillimeter tracking errors while treating pancreatic cancer (10) as well as demonstrating clinical safety and efficacy. A recent retrospective analysis of 88 patients with inoperable pancreatic cancer treated with ablative 5-fraction CyberKnife stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) (BED10 ≥100 Gy) reported a median OS of 18.1 months from diagnosis, with grade 3 GI bleeding in 4.5% of patients (11).

Multileaf collimator (MLC) tracking constitutes a third approach, adapting the MLC aperture in real time to follow tumor motion. The AAPM Task Group 264 has provided guidelines for safe clinical implementation of MLC tracking (12). Couch tracking is an additional investigational system, relying on shifting of the patient relative to the gantry (13). A multi-institutional dosimetric comparison between all four systems showed comparable dosimetric accuracy with mean 2%/2 mm γ-fail rates less than 3% for all systems with adaptation for both lung and prostate motion patterns (14). When appropriately commissioned, all of these approaches can accurately deliver the prescribed dose. The more important question for an institution may not be which system is superior, but which one a center can realistically implement.

Alternative motion management strategies

Beyond dynamic tracking, several other motion management strategies should be considered. Deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH) and end-expiratory breath-hold can effectively immobilize the target but require patient cooperation and may prolong treatment time. Respiratory gating, which delivers radiation only during specific phases of the respiratory cycle, reduces motion uncertainty but extends treatment duration compared to free-breathing delivery. Abdominal compression can reduce respiratory excursion but may alter the anatomic relationship between tumor and OARs in unpredictable ways, cause patient discomfort, and does not eliminate motion entirely.

The fundamental limitation of surrogate-based tracking is that it addresses intra-fraction motion while not adapting to the significant inter-fraction anatomic changes, the limitations that online adaptive platforms were designed to address. Schiff et al. (15) conducted a prospective in silico imaging trial using the Ethos platform (Varian Medical Systems) evaluating CT-guided stereotactic adaptive radiotherapy (CT-STAR) for LAPC prescribing 50 Gy in 5 fractions under a strict isotoxicity approach. The study demonstrated that use of the non-adapted plan would have resulted in at least one hard OAR constraint violation in all 40 fractions evaluated, whereas 39 of 40 adapted plans met all constraints. Early clinical implementation has had comparable findings, demonstrating improved target coverage and reduced dose to critical GI OARs, with online adaptation completed in modest treatment times (16). This suggests that for pancreas SBRT, addressing the inter-fraction changes is necessary in order to deliver ablative dosing.

Magnetic resonance-guided adaptive radiotherapy (MRgART) goes even further, providing direct soft-tissue visualization of the tumor and adjacent OARs without reliance on surrogate markers or fiducials. The multi-institutional stereotactic MR-guided on-table adaptive radiation therapy (SMART) trial (17) demonstrated that 5-fraction ablative SBRT (50 Gy in 5 fractions) could be delivered safely using SMART, with no acute grade ≥3 GI toxicity definitely attributed to treatment and 1-year OS of 65%. Daily on-table adaptation was performed for 93.1% of all delivered fractions, highlighting the frequency of anatomic changes that necessitate plan modification.

While the current study primarily focused on intra-fractional motion management, it does not diminish the contribution of DTT. The question is not whether we can track or adapt, but how we can combine tracking and online adaptation? MR-linac based DTT or gating approaches this ideal, but at substantial cost and limited availability. The more pragmatic path may be CT-guided online adaptation with intra-fraction motion management.

Dose considerations and optimization

The dose regimen employed in this study, 48 and 36 Gy in 15 fractions via simultaneous integrated boost (SIB) to the PTV-planning organ at risk volume (PRV) and PTV, respectively, yields a SIB BED10 of approximately 63.4 Gy. The authors suggest that the shortened overall treatment time suppresses tumor repopulation, bringing the effective BED10 closer to 70 Gy. While directionally plausible, the repopulation parameters underlying this claim are poorly characterized for pancreatic adenocarcinoma, the data that do exist report a median volume doubling time of 144 days (18), far slower than the head and neck cancers in which repopulation corrections are best validated. Furthermore, the BED10 >70 Gy threshold associated with improved survival (2) was derived using the standard formula without repopulation correction, making selective application of this correction methodologically inconsistent. This regimen remains comparable to conventional fractionation (50–54 Gy in 25–30 fractions, BED10 60–63.7 Gy), falls short of the BED10 >70 Gy threshold associated with improved survival (2), and well below the regimens increasingly employed today approaching a BED10 of 100 Gy.

Current National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines acknowledge that hypofractionated regimens delivering 67.5 Gy in 15 fractions or 75 Gy in 25 fractions are acceptable for locally advanced disease (19). Reyngold et al. (20) reported outcomes for 119 patients with inoperable pancreatic cancer treated with ablative radiation therapy, 67.5 Gy in 15 fractions (BED10 97.9 Gy) for tumors ≥1 cm away from luminal organs and 75 Gy in 25 fractions (BED10 97.5 Gy) for tumors <1 cm away, demonstrating 2-year OS of 38% and 2-year local failure of 32%.

Ultra-hypofractionated, SBRT regimens (e.g., 50 Gy in 5 fractions) have suggested further improved local control (LC). The previously mentioned SMART trial reported 2-year LC of 78.2%. A recent multi-institutional comparison of ablative 5-fraction MRgART vs. 15/25-fraction CT-guided moderately hypofractionated approaches found that SMART achieved significantly lower 2-year local failure rates (6.5% vs. 32.9%), though OS was similar between groups (21). This suggests that the superior LC achievable with MRgART may not yet translate to survival benefit, likely due to the predominance of distant metastatic failure in LAPC. The 3-fraction approach has also been explored, with Reyngold et al. (22) establishing 33 Gy in 3 fractions (BED10 =69.3 Gy) as the recommended phase 2 dose, with comparable LC and toxicity.

These findings suggest that the dose employed in the current study, while safe and effective for achieving the primary endpoint, is a conservative approach. The technology’s demonstrated capability to support dose escalation leaves room for further optimization, particularly in patients who have already demonstrated good tolerance of multi-agent chemotherapy.

Evolution of systemic therapy

A notable limitation of this study is the use of gemcitabine or S-1 monotherapy as induction chemotherapy, reflecting the treatment standards at the time of trial design (2015–2018) and the trial population. Current NCCN guidelines recommend FOLFIRINOX, modified FOLFIRINOX, or gemcitabine plus albumin-bound paclitaxel as preferred induction regimens for patients with good performance status (19).

The choice of concurrent chemotherapy has also evolved. The SCALOP trial compared capecitabine-based vs. gemcitabine-based chemoradiation following induction chemotherapy. It showed that capecitabine-based chemoradiation had comparable or improved efficacy with an initial median OS of 15.2 vs. 13.4 months (aHR 0.39, P=0.01) (23), though long-term follow-up showed a non-significant difference (17.6 vs. 14.6 months; aHR 0.68, P=0.19) (24). It was also less toxic, with lower rates of severe hematologic toxicity. This has led to a contemporary practice that increasingly favors fluoropyrimidine-based concurrent regimens.

The LAP07 trial (25) provided important context for the role of chemoradiotherapy in LAPC. This randomized trial found no OS benefit for adding chemoradiotherapy after 4 months of gemcitabine with or without erlotinib in patients with controlled disease (16.5 vs. 15.2 months, P=0.83). However, chemoradiotherapy did reduce local progression (32% vs. 46%, P=0.03). The optimal integration of improved systemic therapy with dose-escalated RT remains an active area of investigation.

The 13.9-month median survival in the current study likely reflects both the systemic therapy limitations and a population that included patients who might today be identified as poor candidates for local intensification. Future studies of DTT should mandate modern induction regimens and require adequate systemic disease control before enrollment.

Future directions and clinical implications

The Vero4DRT system’s commercial discontinuation in 2016 might suggest this technology has reached a dead end. However, the authors note that 18 systems remain operational worldwide, and a second-generation O-ring-shaped system (OXRAY) with gimbal-mounted capabilities was launched in Japan in 2023. The OXRAY system enables biaxially rotational dynamic radiation therapy (BROAD-RT), which combines gantry-ring synchronized rotation with dynamic MLC optimization to create non-coplanar treatment plans without requiring couch movement (26). A dosimetric study in pancreatic cancer comparing BROAD-RT and coplanar volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) suggests the non-coplanar trajectories can improve OAR sparing compared to coplanar VMAT (27).

The rigorous quality assurance (QA) program implemented in this multi-institutional study, all four facilities undergoing IROC Houston credentialing, with mandated dose-volume indices reporting and log file analysis of tracking accuracy, provides a valuable template for other motion management approaches.

Conclusions

Yoshimura and colleagues have demonstrated that the multi-institutional implementation of DTT-IMRT for LAPC is feasible and safe, with excellent tracking accuracy, and rigorous QA that should serve as a model for future trials evaluating new technology. The low rates of severe toxicity suggest that further dose escalation is possible with this level of motion management precision. As a single-arm phase II study conducted in a selected population at experienced centers using earlier systemic therapy regimens, the results primarily demonstrate feasibility rather than comparative effectiveness across platforms or dose strategies, and warrant validation in larger, contemporary populations.

The current study is a meaningful contribution to the field, demonstrating a novel treatment system that can deliver precision RT safely across multiple institutions with rigorous QA, but the path forward is not another single-arm feasibility study of a tracking platform. NRG GI-011 is now directly testing whether dose-escalated RT improves outcomes compared to standard-dose chemoradiation in patients who have completed modern multi-agent induction chemotherapy, the randomized evidence the field has long needed. Regardless of which motion management platform is used, the answer to that trial will define the value of every tracking, gating, and adaptive system discussed in this editorial. Dose escalation already improves LC; if GI-011 demonstrates that this translates to a survival benefit, then the precision these technologies enable becomes not just useful, but essential infrastructure for standard-of-care delivery.
