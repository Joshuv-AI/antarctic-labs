Respecting Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion: Pluralism and Human Rights in Civil Society
Authors: Laurence J. Kirmayer, Dörte Bemme, G. Eric Jarvis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) are foundational values for cultural psychiatry. The current assault on DEI in the United States and other countries represents a direct challenge to the scientific, ethical, and clinical commitments that have guided progress in mental health over recent decades. This paper examines the history, rationale, and successes of DEI frameworks in health care and psychiatry, discusses legitimate critiques, documents the scope and consequences of the current anti-DEI movement in the United States and other jurisdictions, and draws comparisons with Canadian and United Kingdom contexts. We revisit each component of DEI—diversity, equity, and inclusion—to clarify its scientific grounding and moral significance for mental health. We argue that, rather than dismantling DEI, cultural psychiatry and allied disciplines must respond with renewed commitment to the values of DEI as well as those of dignity, empathy, and integrity, as ways to advance social justice and pluralistic civil society.

Diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) are at the heart of cultural psychiatric research, policy, and practice. The current attacks on DEI in the United States and other societies are thus an attack on the core values, principles, and concerns of cultural psychiatry. In this paper, we revisit each of these concepts to clarify their meaning and implications for mental health and well-being, examine the political context in which they are being challenged, and consider the implications for cultural psychiatry and for the patients and communities we serve.

The language of DEI arose as part of efforts to recognize structural violence and inequities in society as enduring legacies of historical wrongs, such as transatlantic slavery, colonialism and scientific racism, and to redress them through education, public policy, laws and regulations. The current assault on DEI in the US—and similar attacks in other places—now casts the values of diversity, equity and inclusion as somehow restrictive or harmful to public welfare, societal norms and collective values and actively dismantles the processes, infrastructures and vocabularies supporting them. This rejection of DEI is an explicit rejection of the facts of history and demography as well as foundational ethical and political values in pluralistic, democratic societies that were renewed and rearticulated in response to the horrors of World War II, the civil rights movement in the US, and decolonization movements around the world. In place of diversity, equity and inclusion, the aim appears to be to create a social regime characterized by enforced homogeneity, entrenched inequity and systematic exclusion. This is not simply a policy disagreement; it represents a profound ethical and political regression to a pre-civil rights era of racialized hierarchies, violence, and oppression.

The rhetorical and political strategies underlying this assault deserve careful examination. Support for anti-DEI policies draws partly from deeply entrenched racism, sexism, and White supremacist ideologies, but also from the deliberate mobilization of fear, resentment, and misrepresentation. 1 A major strategy has been the redefining of language, including terms like “liberal,” “woke,” and “feminist,” to carry negative connotations, while those who seek to radically dismantle hard-won social protections describe themselves (with unintended irony) as “conservatives.” Being fully awake and aware of the legacies of historical harms, entrenched inequities, and the ways that the status quo serves a wealthy minority is vital for seeing reality as it is and taking compassionate action towards more equitable futures. The virtues of being tolerant, generous, flexible, and open to the variegated and changeable nature of the world are worth defending against the cooptation and distortion of political rhetoric that characterizes such core values as “wokeness.” George Orwell recognized this political use of language in 1984, describing “Newspeak” as a tool for maintaining oppression by constraining thought. The use of acronyms like “DEI” (or “EDI” in Canada and the UK) can similarly deflect attention from the core ethical, social, and political concerns those words represent, especially when normalized through bureaucratic routines (Dobbin & Kalev, 2022; Pizarro Milian & Wijesingha, 2023).

In making sense of these developments, a first and essential step is to unpack the acronym, restoring the words—diversity, equity, inclusion—to their intended meaning and use. This not only enables more meaningful critique but opens up conceptual space for clarity, moral imagination, debate, and action. The goal of the present paper is precisely this: to trace the origins, goals, and real-world contexts and impacts of DEI; to address the legitimate critiques of DEI implementation; to document the scope of the current assault; to reaffirm, from the perspective of cultural psychiatry, why these values matter so profoundly; and to identify some ways forward in response to the challenges of this historical moment.

The Goals and History of DEI

The concepts that inform DEI are grounded in the long history of social movements for civil rights, labor rights, gender equality, disability rights, and Indigenous sovereignty in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and globally (Oppenheimer, 2026). DEI policies were progressively developed and institutionalized as responses to documented, persistent inequities that financial markets, informal social norms, and goodwill alone had consistently failed to address.

In the United States, the formal architecture of DEI emerged most visibly from the civil rights era. The Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Voting Rights Act of 1965, and subsequent legislation established legal prohibitions on discrimination in employment, education, housing and public accommodations. Affirmative action policies, first introduced in the 1960s, sought to actively promote the inclusion of historically excluded groups in higher education and the workforce. Over subsequent decades, these principles were extended to address gender equity (including Title IX in education), disability rights (Americans with Disabilities Act, 1990), and, more recently, protections for LGBTQ+ individuals.

The concept of intersectionality developed by Black feminists in the US further shaped the evolution of DEI by demonstrating that inequalities are not experienced along single axes but emerge through overlapping systems of power and oppression. Kimberlé Crenshaw (1989) coined the term, showing how anti-discrimination frameworks failed to account for the experiences of Black women when treating race and gender separately. Her work built on earlier Black feminist scholarship, including the Combahee River Collective (1977), which argued that race, gender, class, and sexuality are inseparable, and Patricia Hill Collins (1990), whose “matrix of domination” conceptualized how these systems interact. Together, these thinkers pushed DEI practice beyond identity politics and representation toward examining structural barriers, institutional cultures, and compounded inequalities.

In mental health and psychiatry, the history of DEI is inextricably bound to a parallel history of exclusion and harm. Psychiatric institutions were sites of ongoing racial segregation in the United States, with Black Americans systematically denied equal access to care or provided with inferior, segregated facilities. The diagnostic categories of psychiatry were themselves implicated in systems of oppression: the invention of “drapetomania” in the 19th century—a supposed mental illness causing enslaved people to desire freedom—represents perhaps the most grotesque example, but the racialization of dangerousness and schizophrenia diagnoses persists in subtler forms to the present day (Jarvis, 2008; Metzl & Roberts, 2014; Shim, 2021). Women, LGBTQ+ individuals, and people with disabilities were similarly subjected to pathologizing, over-medicating, or coercive “treatment” (Butler, 2004; Harrington, 2019).

DEI frameworks in health care and psychiatry thus represent an attempt to reckon honestly with this history and to build institutions that serve all people equitably (Hayvon, 2024). In Canada, these efforts have developed within a parallel legislative framework, including the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982), the Employment Equity Act (1986), federal multiculturalism policy, and the ongoing process of reconciliation with Indigenous peoples following the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2015). The CanMEDS 2025 framework for physician competency explicitly integrates equity, diversity, inclusion, and social justice as core professional requirements (Barnabe et al., 2023). The Canadian Psychiatric Association has supported position statements on training in cultural psychiatry (Kirmayer et al., 2021), the training of international medical graduates (Kirmayer et al., 2018), and taking action on racism and structural violence (Jarvis et al., 2023), all of which speak directly to equity, diversity, and integration issues.

DEI in Health Care Systems, Institutions, and Training

Within health care systems and medical education, DEI initiatives have taken many forms: diversifying the health workforce, developing curricula in structural competency and anti-racism, implementing cultural safety protocols, expanding community-based participatory research, and creating institutional accountability mechanisms for equitable care delivery (Blanchet Garneau et al., 2026; Hansen & Metzl, 2019; Jarvis et al., 2023; Metzl & Hansen, 2014). In psychiatry specifically, DEI efforts have addressed racial disparities in diagnosis and treatment, barriers to access for marginalized communities, the underrepresentation of racialized and Indigenous clinicians and researchers, and the need for culturally responsive and linguistically accessible care (Amonoo et al., 2022; Eckstrand et al., 2022; Kaslow et al., 2021; Woodhead et al., 2022). These efforts are not merely aspirational: the evidence base demonstrating both the health costs of inequity and the positive impact of DEI-aligned interventions in mental health is now substantial and spans multiple domains, where it has become accepted practice. Even for people with severe mental disorders, culturally adapted, DEI-aligned services confer better outcomes in terms of more accurate diagnosis (Adeponle et al., 2012), better family participation (Oluwoye et al., 2020), and improved treatment engagement (Kline & Thomas, 2018). When diverse service users with mental health problems feel respected and included by the treatment teams that are meant to help them, they and their families trust their clinicians, participate in treatment, and are more likely to recover (Knight et al., 2024).

Workforce Diversity and Health Outcomes

One of the clearest bodies of evidence regarding the effects of DEI concerns the impact of a racially and ethnically diverse healthcare workforce on the health outcomes of minority patients. In the UK, racial inequalities in the health services workforce (e.g., discrimination in training, career progression, and employment conditions) have been found to contribute to racial health inequities in patient outcomes, especially in mental health, maternal health, and neonatal health (Kapadia et al., 2022). A landmark randomized study in the US by Alsan et al. (2019) assigned Black men in Oakland, California to either Black or non-Black physicians for preventive care visits. Black patients seen by Black physicians were significantly more likely to accept preventive services, particularly invasive procedures such as blood draws and diabetes screening. The authors estimated that expanding access to racially concordant Black physicians could reduce the Black–White male gap in cardiovascular mortality by approximately 19%. A broader systematic review and meta-analysis of patient–provider racial and ethnic concordance in mental health found that concordance is associated with improved rapport, stronger therapeutic alliance, and better communication (Jackson et al., 2025). Race concordance has also been associated with improved patient-centeredness in clinical encounters, higher patient satisfaction, and greater willingness to disclose sensitive clinical information (Alegría et al., 2013). It is important to note that racial concordance alone does not guarantee superior therapeutic outcomes, and the evidence is heterogeneous. A meta-analytic review of 53 studies of client outcomes in mental health treatment found only a small average benefit of racial/ethnic matching per se (d = 0.09), though preferences for concordant providers were moderately strong (d = 0.63 across 52 studies) and perceptions of racially concordant therapists were systematically more positive (d = 0.32 across 81 studies) (Cabral & Smith, 2011). The clinical implication is not that race concordance is a necessary condition for effective care, but that it operates as one component of a broader system of culturally responsive practice, increasing trust in the system and overall engagement with services when it is an option for members of racialized minorities. When patients cannot access providers who share their background, other DEI-aligned strategies—cultural humility training, structural competency, and linguistically accessible services—become especially critical.

Language Access and Psychiatric Care

Language barriers represent one of the most consequential and best-documented inequities in mental health service access (Arpin-Gemme et al., 2023; Brisset et al., 2014). A systematic review of 18 studies from four countries found consistent evidence that limited language proficiency was significantly associated with underutilization of psychiatric services, with 15 of 18 studies reporting significant negative associations (Takeuchi et al., 2015). Among Latino immigrants with diagnosed psychiatric disorders, limited English proficiency independently reduced the odds of mental health service use even after controlling for insurance status, number of psychiatric diagnoses, and other background characteristics (Bauer & Alegria, 2010). A study of inpatient psychiatric units in the United Kingdom found that patients with limited English language proficiency underwent significantly more consultant reviews and received different discharge diagnoses than language-proficient patients, providing evidence of measurable effects on both service utilization and clinical outcomes (Daly et al., 2019). In Swiss psychiatric hospitals, low shared language proficiency was found to be a significant risk factor for coercive measures during hospitalization, underscoring that language barriers do not merely limit access but can shape the character of care received in ways that compromise safety and dignity (Miteva et al., 2022). A recent systematic review indicated lack of language access in services was an important barrier for mental health services for refugee and asylum-seeking youth (Abou Seif et al., 2026). Multilingual psychiatric encounters without interpreters can lead to the erasure and misinterpretation of culturally specific linguistic expressions of distress (Fowler, 2010), with potentially severe consequences for the service user ranging from alienation and withdrawal from care to misdiagnosis and coercive psychiatric admission (Miteva et al., 2022). Language-concordant care (the provision of psychiatric services in patients’ primary language) is associated with greater disclosure of psychological symptoms and trauma, more patient-centered communication, and improved therapeutic alliance (Alegría et al., 2013; Bauer & Alegría, 2010), while reducing treatment errors and improving patient satisfaction (Bowen, 2015). These findings have direct implications for DEI: building a linguistically diverse mental health workforce and providing professional interpreter services are not merely matters of courtesy but of clinical efficacy and ethical obligation.

Structural Racism and Mental Health Disparities

The evidence that structural racism and systemic exclusion are determinants of poor mental health is now robust and convergent across multiple methodologies. Structural racism—the totality of ways in which societies foster racial discrimination through mutually reinforcing systems of housing, education, employment, policing, and health care—has been shown to operate as a chronic stressor with measurable effects on mental health outcomes including depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress, and psychosis risk (Galán et al., 2021; Shim, 2021; Wallace et al., 2016). Minority stress theory, developed initially in relation to sexual minority individuals and extended to racialized communities, posits that stigma-related stressors—including anticipated discrimination, concealment of identity, and internalized stigma—are distal causes of elevated rates of mental disorder in marginalized groups (Meyer, 2003). This framework has been empirically validated across multiple marginalized populations and represents a key theoretical foundation for the argument that addressing the structural conditions producing minority stress (the core aim of DEI) is itself a mental health intervention.

A systematic review of the relationship between discrimination experiences and mental health found consistent and significant associations across a broad range of study designs, populations, and outcome measures, with perceived discrimination independently predicting depression, anxiety, and overall psychological distress (Pascoe & Smart Richman, 2009). Structural racism also shapes diagnostic practice in psychiatry: Black patients in the United States and the United Kingdom continue to be overdiagnosed with schizophrenia relative to White patients with equivalent symptoms, while being underdiagnosed with mood disorders that would make them eligible for certain treatment resources—disparities that reflect both clinician bias and the racially stratified structure of psychiatric institutions (Bansal et al., 2022; Metzl & Roberts, 2014). DEI frameworks in psychiatric training and practice, including the Cultural Formulation Interview (CFI) and training in structural competency, are designed precisely to interrupt these diagnostic and therapeutic disparities.

Culturally Adapted Interventions

A strong body of evidence for the impact of attention to diversity in mental health care comes from research on culturally adapted psychotherapy. Across more than a decade of meta-analytic research, culturally adapted mental health interventions have consistently demonstrated moderate-to-large effect sizes compared to unadapted treatments or no-treatment controls. A meta-analysis by Griner and Smith (2006) of 76 studies found a weighted average effect size of d = 0.45 favoring culturally adapted interventions; crucially, interventions targeted to a specific cultural group were four times more effective than those provided to mixed cultural groups, and interventions conducted in clients’ native language were twice as effective as those conducted in English. A subsequent meta-analysis by Hall et al. (2016) of 78 studies involving nearly 14,000 participants (95% non-European American) found that culturally adapted interventions produced an overall effect size of g = 0.67 (p < .001) over comparison conditions, with a medium effect size of g = 0.52 when compared directly to unadapted versions of the same intervention—indicating that cultural adaptation itself, not merely the availability of intervention, drives improvements in outcomes. The effects were largest for treatment (g = 0.76) versus prevention, and for mood and anxiety symptom outcomes. These findings have since been replicated in specific populations, including a systematic review of 67 randomized trials of culturally adapted psychological interventions for people of Chinese descent, which demonstrated significant benefits for depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (Li et al., 2023). A separate systematic review and meta-analysis of culturally adapted digital mental health interventions found a large significant effect (g = 0.90) favoring culturally adapted platforms over waitlist and treatment-as-usual controls (Ellis et al., 2022).

Taken together, this evidence base makes a compelling case that attention to DEI in mental health is not merely a set of institutional aspirations but a scientifically grounded program of action with demonstrated impacts on access, engagement, diagnostic accuracy, therapeutic alliance, and clinical outcomes. The appropriate response to this evidence is investment, refinement, and rigorous evaluation, not dismantling of efforts that have had clearly beneficial effects, but have only begun to redress centuries of dispossession and marginalization.

Critiques of DEI

Any reasoned defense of DEI must acknowledge that there are legitimate critiques of how DEI principles have been articulated and implemented in practice. These critiques deserve serious engagement, both because they are sometimes correct and because failing to address them cedes rhetorical ground to those whose actual goal is to dismantle equity efforts entirely.

The first and most easily remedied critique is of superficial or purely performative implementations. DEI has sometimes been reduced to symbolic gestures: diversity photographs in institutional brochures, one-off cultural competency trainings that tick a box without changing practice, or diversity statements that bear no relationship to actual hiring, promotion or resource allocation decisions. Sara Ahmed's (2012) ethnographic work on diversity in universities documents how the institutional performance of diversity can actively substitute for material change, allowing organizations to claim DEI commitments while leaving underlying power structures untouched. DEI can hardly deliver on its promise if it is limited to “check-box” approaches or cosmetic changes to institutional practice.

A second legitimate and more fundamental critique concerns the failure to address structural and systemic issues. DEI initiatives that focus primarily on representation—on the visible diversity of organizational membership—without confronting the structural conditions that produce inequity can be insufficient or even counterproductive. Increasing the number of racialized individuals in an institution while leaving discriminatory practices, wage gaps, and hostile climates intact may produce token inclusion without substantive justice (Berrey, 2015; Henry et al., 2017; Kahn, 2025). This has also led to the paradoxical experience for racialized employees of becoming both hypervisible and invisible through diversity initiatives (Settles et al., 2019). Moreover, key structural determinants of health like poverty and social marginalization may be hard to address through DEI policies and practices. Indeed, the lack of attention to such structural inequities, which affect most sectors of society, has fueled the backlash against DEI measures that focus on minoritized groups that are designated “equity-deserving.” 2

Critics from both left and right have argued that certain DEI framings can entrench identity categories in ways that undermine solidarity, erode shared civic identity, and produce new forms of exclusion. These concerns deserve careful consideration: the goal of DEI should always be to broaden inclusion and strengthen the common good, not to create rigid identity hierarchies or to suppress open inquiry and debate (Neiman, 2023). Lilla (2018) argues that some forms of identity politics risk eroding the very solidarities required for effective democratic action. When political discourse is organized primarily around discrete and essentialized group identities, it can fragment the public sphere into mutually suspicious constituencies, each mobilizing its own injuries rather than cultivating a shared sense of citizenship and common good. This may weaken the capacity to build broad coalitions across differences and to articulate universal programs of social and economic justice that address structural inequality affecting many groups simultaneously. In this reading, a politics that foregrounds identity without simultaneously foregrounding shared institutions, rights, and responsibilities risks narrowing the scope of moral concern, and may inadvertently confirm the caricature promoted by the far right that equity projects are inherently particularistic and exclusionary rather than universalist.

Related worries are expressed in debates about so-called “cancel culture,” where social sanctions, online shaming, and ostracism are deployed in response to perceived moral or political transgressions (Hanafi, 2025; Ng, 2022; Norris, 2023). Proponents understand these practices as necessary forms of accountability in contexts where formal institutions have long failed to address racism, sexism, and other harms. Critics counter that, when deployed punitively or indiscriminately, they can generate a climate of fear and self-censorship, narrowing the range of expressible viewpoints and discouraging good-faith disagreement. From the perspective of a pluralistic, deliberative democracy, the danger is that moral outrage displaces reasoned argument, passionate but respectful contestation, and the slow work of persuasion, thereby undermining precisely the conditions of public debate that are needed to advance equity. For cultural psychiatry, these tensions highlight the importance of defending both the substantive aims of DEI and the procedural norms of open inquiry, fallibilism, and dialogical engagement that sustain inclusive, epistemically diverse institutions.

Finally, there are concerns about poorly designed implementations that function as “reverse discrimination” or that undermine meritocracy (Stein, 2025; Totenberg, 2023). Where DEI initiatives set numerical targets without attention to qualifications, or where they create institutional cultures of fear and self-censorship, they can damage both institutional effectiveness and the broader social legitimacy of equity projects. In most cases, of course, what is called “reverse discrimination” is simply providing some of the consideration, privileges, and advantage taken for granted by the few, to those previously excluded.

Each of these critiques identifies real failure modes in DEI implementation. They are, however, vastly overstated by those who invoke them not to improve DEI but to abolish it. Instead of dismantling institutions and undermining nascent efforts at redress of historical wrongs and persistent structural violence, the appropriate response to poorly implemented DEI or unintended consequences is more thoughtful, evidence-based, and structurally attentive DEI.

The Assault on DEI

The attack on DEI in the United States under the second Trump administration (2025–present) is unprecedented in scope and speed. Within days of taking office, President Trump signed executive orders eliminating DEI offices across the federal government, terminating federal DEI programs, and directing federal contractors to abandon DEI commitments as a condition of receiving government contracts. The explicit framing of these orders, with the claim that DEI constitutes “illegal discrimination” and is “anti-American,” inverts the historical and legal record.

The effects on mental health and health equity infrastructure have been severe and concrete. Federal funding for behavioral health services has been dramatically curtailed: the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) experienced the termination of approximately $2 billion in grants in late 2025, alongside major staff reductions, undermining federal capacity for technical assistance, suicide prevention, addiction treatment, homelessness prevention, and service delivery to vulnerable populations. School-based mental health grants were cancelled. Proposals were advanced to reorganize or significantly reduce SAMHSA itself.

Scientific infrastructure has also been targeted (Aggarwal et al., 2026). The NIH's scientific integrity policy was rescinded in March 2025, removing formal protections against political interference in research. NIH grants linked to DEI-related health disparities research were systematically disrupted, affecting pipelines for mental health disparities, LGBTQ+ health, and related fields. Communications freezes at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) delayed release of overdose mortality data and disrupted the publication of evidence-based public health guidance. Federal government websites containing information on LGBTQ+ health, HIV prevention, and other equity-relevant topics were removed or altered.

The administration has also moved to restrict clinical practice. Proposed rules would tie Medicare and Medicaid participation to the prohibition of gender-affirming care for youth—care that major professional bodies, including the American Psychiatric Association and the American Academy of Pediatrics, regard as evidence-based. These proposals directly challenge clinical autonomy and the evidentiary foundations of psychiatric care.

These are the instrumental effects of exclusionary policy. However, what is missing in this list of depredations is the overt attack on identity and self-esteem: the official erasure of one's being; the effects on children and youth of being told—both explicitly and implicitly—that your group has accomplished nothing and does not have “entitlements.” At its worst, this is an assault on the very foundations of hope.

Taken together, these actions represent a systematic and politically orchestrated dismantling of the public health and equity infrastructure that has been constructed over decades. These destructive interventions are not based on evidence of the ineffectiveness of DEI programs; they arise from an explicit political agenda that, as Neiman (2023) and others have argued, is fundamentally anti-Enlightenment in its rejection of reason, universalism, and the idea of shared human dignity (Boehm, 2025).

Comparison with Canada

In contrast to the United States, Canada has thus far maintained its formal institutional commitments to EDI (equity, diversity, and inclusion—the terminology more commonly used in Canada). Federal policies including the Employment Equity Act, the Pay Equity Act, and the Federal Anti-Racism Strategy provide a legislative backbone for national EDI efforts. Research funding bodies, including the Tri-Agency and the New Frontiers in Research Fund, require applicants to demonstrate EDI commitments in research design and implementation.

Canadian universities generally maintain formal EDI policies, though implementation varies considerably by institution and discipline. Persistent representation gaps remain. According to Statistics Canada (2020,2022), in 2019 women held 37% of tenured positions in Canadian universities while comprising 41% of full-time university faculty overall, and they remain less likely than men to be tenured (63% of female faculty versus 75% of male faculty). Indigenous scholars are sharply underrepresented, with available data indicating that no more than 1.4% of researchers self-identify as Indigenous. Racialized faculty, while comprising roughly 22% of the academic workforce overall (in line with the broader population), remain underrepresented in senior and leadership positions. In the federally regulated private sector, the Employment Equity Act Annual Report (2025) reported that as of December 31, 2023, persons with disabilities represented 5.2% of the workforce and Indigenous peoples 2.4%, both still below their labour market availability.

In Canadian medicine and psychiatry, national professional associations have articulated EDI principles, and many medical faculties have established equity offices and anti-racism training programs. The CanMEDS 2025 framework integrates equity, diversity, inclusion, and social justice as professional competencies. Nevertheless, persistent inequities in physician compensation, career advancement, and cultural and linguistic access to care indicate that implementation continues to lag normative commitments in important respects.

Of course, Canada has its own history of racism and inequality (Satzewich, 2011), and is not immune to the political currents emanating from south of the border. Some institutions have begun reframing or retreating from explicit EDI language. The University of Alberta, for example, shifted from “EDI” terminology toward “access, community, and belonging” citing concerns about political polarization. These shifts, while perhaps well-intentioned in specific institutional contexts, risk normalizing a broader retreat from accountability. Canada has a particular responsibility in this moment to ensure that evidence-based, equity-committed governance remains viable and effective.

Comparison with the United Kingdom

From the 1970s onward, EDI practices in the UK evolved from early anti-discrimination statutes such as the Sex Discrimination Act 1975 and Race Relations Act 1976 into a more consolidated framework culminating in the Equality Act 2010, which unified protections across nine key characteristics and placed a Public Sector Equality Duty on institutions. Yet throughout this period, class remained the dominant idiom of British social stratification, shaping public discourse and often obscuring how race, gender, disability, and migration status structure unequal opportunity. In mental health care, cultural and social psychiatry exposed the limits of class-only analysis by documenting how racism, migration stress, and institutional practices contributed to coercive treatment and diagnostic disparities.

In the 2020s, however, EDI has come under intensified scrutiny. High-profile gender-critical campaigners won legal cases that centered on the protection of “philosophical beliefs” under the Equality Act, challenging institutional EDI frameworks on the grounds of compelled speech, freedom of belief, and academic freedom. Universities stood accused of unlawful EDI-based hiring practices, arguing that requiring applicants to demonstrate commitment to EDI may breach free-speech and anti-discrimination laws. The introduction of free-speech laws for higher-education providers in 2025 exacerbated the tensions between equality protections and freedom of expression, prompting new guidance on balancing these legal duties. Against this backdrop, EDI practices are increasingly viewed as an obstacle to “free speech” rather than a core part of safeguarding minoritized communities (Bacevic, 2025). These developments have unfolded against a backdrop of rising right-wing rhetoric, anti-migration sentiment, and a political climate in which EDI policies have remained in place but are also portrayed as ideological rather than grounded in structural inequities intimately connected to Britain's historical role in promoting colonization and scientific racism to justify it.

Rethinking DEI

Collapsing and conflating major social structural, political, and ethical concerns into an acronym makes them an easy target for reactionary politics. We can best respond to these regressive attacks by unpacking the acronym and examining each of its components, thereby addressing valid critiques while reaffirming the paths toward social justice and efficacy in mental health care.

Diversity

The term “diversity” recognizes a fundamental empirical reality: human beings vary widely in their identities, cultures, histories and experiences, both as individuals and as groups. Demographically, the United States, Canada, and most contemporary societies are highly diverse. In the United States, this diversity reflects the many different cultures of the original inhabitants of the Americas; a long and brutal history of the transatlantic slave trade and its multigenerational consequences; waves of voluntary and involuntary migration; and generations of intermixing with the emergence of new identities and communities. In Canada, diversity is similarly constituted by Indigenous peoples, French and English settler colonialism, the diverse peoples present and participating in the early building of the country, and successive waves of immigration from around the world.

This diversity has profound implications for mental health and well-being. The erasure of diversity through the dominance of majority or elite cultures causes harm: the suppression of languages, the delegitimization of spiritual and cultural practices, and the invalidation of identities. The racialization of identity—the social process by which racial categories are imposed and made consequential—has pervasive effects on mental health through stigma, discrimination, and the embodied stress of navigating a society organized around racial hierarchy (Bryant et al., 2022; Steele, 2011; Waldron, 2024). Isabel Wilkerson's (2020) analysis of American racialization as a caste system documents the psychological and physical health costs of caste across racialized groups in ways that resonate deeply with the psychiatric literature on minority stress and structural racism.

Recognition of diversity extends beyond race and ethnicity to include sexual orientation, gender identity, disability, social class, and the multiple axes of difference that intersect in individual lives. This diversity produces various forms of intersectionality that require individualized recognition while also manifesting patterns common enough to allow some degree of evidence-based generalization (Kelly et al., 2022; Zurn et al., 2022). Importantly, when imposed identity categories derived from census or dominant social frameworks do not fit individual experience, this mismatch itself generates significant distress and obscures the diverse experience of minorities (Ghanem et al., 2025).

The recognition of diversity is not merely an expression of liberal sentiment; it is a public health and clinical imperative. Research consistently shows that health outcomes improve when clinical care is responsive to patients’ cultural contexts, languages, values, and explanatory models. Lack of attention to diversity in clinical services results in misunderstanding, mistrust, and disengagement from clinical care with serious health consequences (Ahmad et al., 2022; Nikolitch et al., 2018; Sacha et al., 2022). Evidence-based cultural psychiatry depends on taking diversity seriously as a feature of the social world that affects pathways to illness, help-seeking, therapeutic relationships, and recovery (Kirmayer, 2019). On a macro-social level, diversity may improve resilience and the response to changing social systems (Page, 2010).

Equity

Beyond the recognition of diversity, the evidence is clear that there are major, systematic differences in individual and collective health and well-being that reflect differences in social status, exposure to adversity, and access to resources for coping and adaptation. These differences are not random; they are systematic, reflecting structures and practices that stratify society and segregate groups. They do not merely represent relative disadvantages for some: they represent corresponding advantages and privileges for others—privileges that may be invisible to those who benefit precisely because they do not experience the barriers that others continue to face. The goal of equity is not dragging down a privileged group but ensuring the same access to resources and opportunities for all.

Equality of opportunity, equality before the law, and equal rights are foundational to human dignity and to just societies at the local and global level. Equity is not the same as equality, however, because people are not the same and do not need the same things to achieve comparable outcomes. As Amartya Sen (1995) has argued in his capabilities approach, justice requires attending to the profoundly unequal conditions in which people are able to develop and exercise their human capacities. There is evidence that economic inequity has been increasing both within and between societies in the last several decades (Chancel et al., 2022; Milanovic, 2016; Neckerman & Torche, 2007). Thomas Piketty's (2024) analysis of the historical dynamics of inequality demonstrates that capital accumulation systematically concentrates wealth across generations in ways that no market mechanism corrects without deliberate redistribution. The inheritance of generational wealth, in particular, is a powerful amplifier of economic inequality; the racialization of poverty—the ways in which racial hierarchies concentrate disadvantage among racialized groups across generations—compounds this effect. These inequities harm everyone in society by exacerbating wealth disparities, increasing poverty, decreasing social cohesion, and making the overall society a less healthy and desirable place to live (Jones, 2000; Wilkinson & Pickett, 2010).

Maldistribution and inequities in access to resources and opportunities arise from multiple sources: geographic and distributional challenges, differential power and control of resources, and ongoing prejudice and discrimination. Addressing them requires accurate recognition of where inequities exist, how they are distributed, and how they are tied to particular social identities, which is precisely the kind of empirical work attentive to identity that the anti-DEI movement aims to suppress (Fraser & Honneth, 2003).

In psychiatry and mental health, inequities manifest as disparities in access to care, in the quality of care received, in diagnostic practices, in treatment outcomes, and in life expectancy (Das-Munshi et al., 2023). Structural racism shapes who is referred for what kind of care, who is seen as credible and worthy of therapeutic investment, and whose suffering is medicalized versus criminalized (Jarvis, 2008; Metzl et al., 2018; Shim, 2021). Addressing these inequities is not a matter of political preference; it is a matter of clinical efficacy, ethics, and social reality that affects everyone in a community and society. This realization comes from an understanding of the health, social, economic, and environmental consequences of inequity.

Inclusion

The recognition of and respect for diversity does not aim at separate, homogeneous enclaves, but at civil societies in which there is ongoing exchange among diverse peoples within shared institutions and shared public life. Inclusion is about the quality of participation—where diverse individuals and communities can meaningfully engage in the institutions, decisions, rewards, and cultural life of the broader society on terms that respect their dignity and identity (Yanicki, 2021).

The political philosophy of deliberative democracy (Habermas, 2018, 2023) provides one framework for thinking about what inclusive civil society requires: a public sphere in which multiple perspectives can be articulated, debated, and brought to bear on collective decisions, and in which the conditions for genuinely free and equal participation are actively maintained. The erosion of these conditions, through polarization, echo chambers in social media, the weaponization of disinformation, and the systematic delegitimization of minority voices, is thus a threat not merely to DEI but to democratic governance itself (Aubert, 2021).

Exclusion occurs for many reasons: misunderstanding, implicit bias, structural barriers, and deliberate discrimination driven by fear, hatred, prejudice, and the desire to preserve power and advantages for those who have historically benefitted from these inequities and exclusions. The fear of the other—which ranges from everyday apprehension or unease with the unfamiliar to virulent xenophobia—is a powerful force that has been harnessed by political actors who use divisiveness to advance their partisan interests. Understanding the psychological dynamics of exclusion, and developing evidence-based interventions to address them, is itself an important domain of mental health research and practice as well as a vital intervention to protect civil society. An inclusive society is one in which social bonds and sense of belonging benefit everyone (Allen et al., 2021; United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, 2009).

Social psychiatry and the study of social determinants of health are essential to addressing the sources of inequity and injustice that contribute to marginalization and exclusion. Cultural psychiatry also has a distinct contribution to make here. By demonstrating that cultural context shapes the very categories through which we understand the self, illness, healing, and community, cultural psychiatry challenges the ethnocentric universalism that underlies much exclusion. Showing that multiple ways of being human are not only compatible with health but may represent adaptive strengths undermines the premise that diversity is a problem to be managed rather than a reality to be respected and engaged.

The Role of Cultural Psychiatry in Defending DEI

Cultural psychiatry has a specific and important role to play in the current crisis. As scholars and clinicians who work at the intersection of culture, society and mental health, we have the expertise and the ethical obligation to defend the values and the evidence that DEI represents.

In research, this means continuing to prioritize health equity, to center the experiences of marginalized communities, to develop and validate culturally responsive assessment and intervention tools, and to produce the kind of evidence that can sustain policy commitments to equity even in hostile political climates (Varma et al., 2023). It means defending the funding and infrastructure for this research, including pushing back against politically motivated restrictions on grant-making, publication, and data dissemination.

In training and education, it means strengthening curricula in structural competency, anti-racism, cultural safety and culturally adapted care, and ensuring that the next generation of mental health professionals has the knowledge and the skills to work effectively and ethically with diverse populations. The Canada Research Chairs’ EDI requirements and the CanMEDS framework provide structural support for this in Canada; similar frameworks need to be defended and strengthened elsewhere.

In advocacy, it means speaking clearly and publicly about the health consequences of anti-DEI policies, providing expert testimony and public commentary, and supporting the professional organizations and civil society institutions that are committed to equity. It means engaging with the public sphere—through op-eds, public lectures, podcasts, and social media—to counter the disinformation and misrepresentation that fuel anti-DEI politics. Professional organizations including the Section on Transcultural Psychiatry of the World Psychiatric Association, the World Association of Cultural Psychiatry, and Society for the Study of Psychiatry and Culture can serve as platforms for this advocacy.

In clinical practice, it means continuing to provide equitable, culturally responsive, evidence-based care to all patients, resisting clinical pressure to suppress relevant diversity frameworks, and actively supporting colleagues who face institutional or political pressure to abandon equity commitments (Todic et al., 2022). This also means attending to the mental health needs of the communities most directly affected by the current political assault. LGBTQ+ youth, racialized communities, immigrants and refugees, Indigenous peoples, and people with disabilities face heightened risks of discrimination, violence, and mental health crisis as anti-DEI politics intensify. So too do professionals who stand up to anti-DEI politics. Cultural psychiatry has the knowledge and the ethical obligation to respond by helping to protect, provide care, and advocate for those, including our patients, colleagues, friends, and families, who bear the brunt of structural violence and discrimination in our societies.

Cultural psychiatry can contribute to pluralistic civil society first by insisting on the centrality of culture in human development, experience and flourishing, and countering biologically reductionist accounts that obscure the social and historical conditions of mental health. By treating cultural diversity as a constitutive demographic fact rather than a deviation from a presumed norm, cultural psychiatry reinforces the view that contemporary societies are irreducibly plural and that institutions must be designed with this pluralism in mind. Empirically documenting how cultural meanings, practices, and structures shape risk, resilience, symptom expression, help-seeking, and recovery clarifies that culture is not merely a source of difference or difficulty in the clinical encounter but also a resource for coping, collective action, and healing. This orientation supports the design of services that recognize and strengthen local forms of knowledge, mutual aid, and community solidarity rather than treating them as obstacles to standardized care.

At the same time, cultural psychiatry can help build “places of recognition” and “spaces of inclusion” and “mutual learning” within and across mental health systems and other institutions by foregrounding experiences of misrecognition, marginalization, and epistemic injustice as clinically and politically salient (Bemme et al., 2024; Kirmayer, 2011, 2019; Kirmayer & Jarvis, 2019). This includes attending to the anxieties, projections, and deeper psychosocial dynamics that fuel mistrust, polarization, and scapegoating, and developing interventions that address these dynamics at interpersonal, institutional, and societal levels. Drawing on work in social psychology and political philosophy, cultural psychiatry can also underscore the value of diversity for collective problem-solving and institutional resilience: heterogeneous groups, when supported by norms of reciprocity and equal respect, tend to generate more creative solutions and more robust responses to complex social challenges (Page, 2008). Work on diversity in complex systems shows that cognitive and social diversity can improve performance at the level of teams and organizations, providing an additional pragmatic rationale for pluralistic institutions beyond moral arguments alone (Page, 2010). Cultural psychiatry is well positioned to articulate how recognizing, respecting, and sustaining diversity is integral not only to individuals’ mental health but to the health of democratic, pluralistic societies.

Conclusion: Dignity, Empathy, and Integrity as Ways Forward

Instead of dismantling DEI, we need to affirm the values it reflects and recognize the persistence and changing forms of the problems it aims to address. The appropriate responses in social policy and clinical practice need to be carefully thought through and empirically evaluated for their impacts, but the goal should not be the wholesale dismantling of efforts to redress historic wrongs. In the process of refining societal approaches to ongoing structural violence, we need to improve our understanding of the systemic effects of interventions. In some cases, this may uncover unintended consequences that require a shift in approach. This is the work of evidence-based social policy: ongoing, critical, and humble inquiry into what works and for whom.

Approaching this from a positive direction, we suggest that cultural psychiatry can complement DEI by orienting itself around an additional set of foundational values: dignity, empathy, and integrity.

Dignity stands for the recognition of every person as an ethical being, an end in themselves and not merely a means. As Kateb (2014) has argued, the assault on human dignity—through slavery, torture, arbitrary cruelty, the denial of rights—represents the most serious form of moral wrong. Defending dignity means refusing to accept the dehumanization of any person or group on the basis of race, ethnicity, gender, religion, sexuality, disability, immigration status, or any other dimension of identity (Boehm, 2025).

Empathy stands for deepening our engagement with each other across difference: putting ourselves in the other's position while remaining attentive to the limits of our mutual understanding. This is not a simple or sentimental gesture. Genuine empathy requires intellectual and emotional work—learning about the histories, contexts, and experiences of those who are different from us, tolerating the discomfort of recognizing our own privilege and complicity, and remaining open to having our assumptions challenged (Kirmayer, 2015). Empathy across difference is both a clinical skill and a civic virtue.

Integrity stands for creating the conditions, both locally and globally, in which people can live in ways consistent with humane values: with honesty, coherence between values and actions, and resistance to corruption and self-dealing. Institutional integrity requires accountability, transparency, and the willingness to acknowledge and correct failures. Scientific integrity requires protecting research from political interference and maintaining the standards of evidence that distinguish knowledge from ideology.

A renewed commitment to human rights, human dignity, and diversity should be the measure of the moral worth of our societies, our institutions, and our discipline. The assault on DEI is, at its core, an assault on these values (Kirmayer, 2012). Cultural psychiatry's response must be clear, principled, and evidence-based: to defend what we know, to insist on what we value, to maintain humility to learn what we do not know, and to work with determination and compassion toward strengthening pluralistic, inclusive, and just societies in which all people can flourish.
