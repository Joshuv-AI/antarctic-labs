Cost-effectiveness analysis of molecular reflex testing-based targeted therapy in non-small cell lung cancer from a German healthcare payer perspective using a decision tree model
Authors: Leonie Schumacher, Melina Sophie Kurte, Tabea Poos, Markus Thalheimer, Frank Griesinger, Florian Kron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReflex testing (RT) - pathologist-initiated molecular testing performed at non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) diagnosis - enables rapid identification of actionable genomic alterations for targeted therapy (TT) selection. Despite national and international guideline recommendations, approximately 15% of patients with advanced NSCLC in Germany remain untested for driver mutations. The portion of untested patients in early stages is likely to be even higher.ObjectiveThis study simulated the clinical and economic impact of introducing RT for untested NSCLC patients under different implementation scenarios, using a decision-tree model from the German statutory health insurance (SHI) perspective.MethodsA decision-tree model simulated four RT cases (C): (C1) no molecular testing, (C2) testing per German S3 guideline recommendations, (C3) testing according to current TT approvals, and (C4) comprehensive panel testing (standard in Austria). PD-L1 expression was tested in all cases. The untested NSCLC population was projected for 2025 based on SHI demographics. Model inputs comprised RT sensitivity, mutation and stage distribution, drug acquisition costs (Lauer-Taxe, April 2025), and median overall/progression-free survival (mOS/mPFS) per therapeutic strategy. A scenario analysis was conducted assuming NGS test costs of €3,000 per panel to assess their potential influence on cost-effectiveness versus no testing.ResultsAll RT cases (C2–C4) substantially increased actionable mutation detection, enabling earlier TT initiation and lower mean treatment costs versus no molecular testing. C2-C4 were clinically dominant, driven by increased mOS by 5.48–5.47 months and mPFS by 4.89–5.42 months. Model yielded annual cost savings versus no testing of €740 (C2) and €535 (C3/C4) per patient (annual cost savings of €1,175 – €1,621 per life-year gained). When €3,000 next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel costs were included, annual costs per patient increased modestly (C2: €2,260; C3/C4: €2,465 versus no testing), at annual incremental costs of €4,946 – €5,409 per life-year gained.ConclusionsReflex testing across implementation cases, by enabling earlier identification of actionable biomarkers and receipt of matched targeted therapy, improves clinical outcomes and is cost-effective for untested NSCLC patients from the German SHI perspective. Structured initiatives, such as national Network for Genomic Medicine (nNGM), could further expand equitable access to precision oncology in Germany.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12962-026-00803-3.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with approximately 57,000 new cases diagnosed annually in Germany [1, 2] and around 2.5 million globally [3, 4]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 80% of these cases [5] and is frequently driven by genetic alterations, many of which are actionable with targeted therapies (TT), particularly tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) [5, 7–10]. Most European and German guidelines recommend the molecular testing of mutations such as epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), B-Raf proto-oncogene serine/threonine kinase (BRAF) V600, anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) fusions, ROS proto-oncogene 1 (ROS1) fusions, rearranged during transfection (RET) mutations, neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase 1–3 (NTRK1–3) fusions, Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) G12C, MET exon 14 skipping mutations (MET ex14), neuregulin 1 (NRG1) fusions, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2/ERBB2) mutations. The testing of programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) by immunohistochemistry is obligatory [8, 11, 12]. Furthermore, most guidelines reflect the local situation of approved TT for the respective disease stage and molecular target [5, 16]. These therapies are well established in clinical practice, provided the relevant alterations are identified through comprehensive molecular testing [8, 10, 11].

Reflex testing (RT) refers to the immediate initiation of panel-based molecular analysis of clinically relevant genes by a pathologist at the time of lung cancer diagnosis. In Germany, however, pathologists are required to obtain a formal laboratory order before performing these tests, introducing additional administrative steps and causing delays in the diagnostic process [8, 13, 14]. Despite the growing number of approved targeted therapies, comprehensive molecular profiling is not yet routinely integrated into diagnostic workflows in Germany, leaving a significant proportion of NSCLC patients without comprehensive testing for actionable driver mutations [8, 11, 13–15]. However, the German National Network of Genomic Medicine (nNGM) offers access and remuneration for standardized next generation sequencing (NGS) diagnostics and supports the implementation of RT workflows [15, 16]. Data from the clinical research platform into molecular testing, treatment and outcome of non-small cell lung carcinoma patients (CRISP) registry show that around ~ 22% of patients in stage IV receive first-line therapy before all molecular markers were analysed, limiting access to potentially beneficial therapies [8, 17]. Moreover, only 72.5% of patients with advanced non-squamous NSCLC were tested, for example, for EGFR mutations prior to first-line therapy [8, 11]. Although international and German guidelines recommend broader testing panels and RT, implementation in Germany lags behind other countries such as Austria, where automatic RT for lung cancer has been standard practice since 2020. Consequently, 97% of patients with adenocarcinoma are tested in Austria, 95% of which are examined using NGS [10, 18]. Moreover, lung cancer cases are documented and evaluated by the LALUCA registry with particular focus on molecular biomarker testing and NGS of patients before starting the first-line treatment [19].

Across settings, RT improves testing completeness, reduces turnaround times, and increases delivery of matched targeted therapies, leading to more efficient care pathways [15, 23–25, 28, 29]. National and international programs show scalable feasibility with acceptable turnaround and survival gains, while German CRISP data reveal implementation gaps that reflex testing could address [8, 11, 15]. International economic analyses consistently find NGS-based reflex testing to be cost-effective compared with serial single-gene approaches [26–29]. However, in Germany a proportion of patients remain untested before starting their first line therapy, unlike other countries like Austria [8, 10, 17–18]. Thus, the aim of paper is to depict the potential health economic benefits of RT in the treatment of untested NSCLC patients in Germany.

To quantify the potential benefits of RT, this study simulated the clinical and economic impact of introducing molecular RT in previously untested NSCLC patients across different implementation cases from the perspective of German statutory health insurance (SHI). The analysis employed a deterministic decision tree model capturing the diagnostic branching decision at the time of NSCLC diagnosis, using drug acquisition costs (Lauer-Taxe list prices) as the primary cost input from an annual SHI reimbursement perspective and life-years gained (derived from median overall survival) as the effectiveness metric. Key modelling assumptions include 100% testing uptake as a theoretical best-case scenario defining an upper bound on the potential benefit of universal reflex testing, guideline-concordant targeted therapy receipt by all mutation-positive patients, and mutually exclusive driver biomarker categories. These design choices reflect the German HTA context and the diagnostic decision-point focus of the study.

Methods

Model approach

A cost-effectiveness analysis was conducted using a decision tree model, to analyze effectiveness in terms of median progression-free survival (mPFS) and median overall survival (mOS), and costs measured as the mean annual therapy expenses per case. A decision tree structure was selected because the primary research question concerns a single, time-limited diagnostic decision point, whether and how to test patients for actionable molecular alterations at NSCLC diagnosis, rather than the long-term management of disease progression or multi-line treatment sequencing. The model captures the branching probability that a patient receives a positive test result, is assigned to the corresponding first-line therapy, and accrues the associated annual costs and survival benefit. The survival parameters (mOS, mPFS) used as effectiveness inputs are derived directly from published clinical trial evidence for each assigned treatment strategy. Consistent with the model’s scope, these values represent the survival outcomes associated with the initial treatment phase following NSCLC diagnosis and reflex testing; the model does not extend to subsequent-line therapies or explicitly model disease progression beyond the reported trial endpoints. In this sense, the decision tree functions as a cross-sectional cost-effectiveness comparator of testing strategies at the point of treatment initiation, rather than as a longitudinal model of disease management. Possible RT cases were defined based on the current clinical practice, the German S3 guideline, available targeted therapeutics, and the molecular testing approach implemented in Austria. The Austrian test model was used as a template for case 4, as the complete gene panel is tested in Austria regardless of the NSCLC stage [29, 61, 62].

The decision tree model compared four diagnostic scenarios / patterns and the therapeutic decisions based on them (C1-C4) for NSCLC in Germany (Fig. 1):

C1 is included in the calculation and served as the null point for the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) calculation. C2 included two branches - differentiated by stage - according to the German S3 guideline recommendations: Stages IB-IIIB were tested for alterations in EGFR and ALK, whereas stage IV was tested for the full molecular panel including EGFR, BRAF V600, ALK, ROS1, RET, NTRK1-3, KRAS G12C, MET ex14, NRG1, HER2 (ERBB2) alterations. C3 comprised two branches: Stages IB-IIIA were tested for alterations in EGFR and ALK, and stages IIIB and IV were tested for the full panel. C4 represented comprehensive panel testing for all genes mentioned in stages IB-IV. Patients were assigned to the respective therapeutic options only after testing. The respective target population for each case was simulated depending on the pathway of the patient flow model. For each step, corresponding input is included, such as test sensitivity, mutation distribution, annual therapy costs of TTs, immunochemotherapies, and alternative therapy options, as well as survival rates of the therapy option, were included. Finally, the incremental cost- effectiveness- ratio consisting of costs and benefits was calculated. This principle was applied to all three cases and the base case. This calculation uses the base case as a comparison scenario. All calculations were performed in Excel (Microsoft Corporation, USA). Model validity was assessed through face validity review and clinical expert oversight. Face validity was established by deriving all model inputs directly from published German epidemiological data, published clinical trials, and registry sources: Mutation prevalence estimates were bootstrapped from the published literature and are consistent with real-world molecular testing data from the German CRISP registry [8], and survival parameters (mOS and mPFS) were drawn from published randomized controlled trials and health technology assessments for each therapy, ensuring consistency with the best available clinical evidence. Model structure, scenario definitions, and key assumptions were reviewed by clinical co-investigators with specialized expertise in NSCLC molecular diagnostics and targeted therapy in Germany. Formal external validation, comparing model-predicted aggregate population outcomes against independent registry datasets, was not performed and is acknowledged as a limitation.

Model input

Population

The target population was defined as NSCLC patients in Germany who have not undergone testing for genetic alterations (hereafter referred to as “untested population”) and were insured under SHI. The standardized rate of new cases (SRNC) for women and men between 2006 and 2022 reported in the annual publications of the German Cancer Registry [2, 30] was extrapolated to 2025 (see Additional file 1: Figure S1). This projected SRNC was applied to the forecasted 2025 population of Germany, as published by the German Federal Statistical Office [31], to estimate the number of lung cancer cases in 2025. The proportion of NSCLC among all lung cancer cases was derived from published literature and applied to this estimate [5]. To reflect the population covered by Germanys public health insurance system, the proportion of SHI patients [32], was then applied to the projected number of NSCLC cases. Finally, the percentage of NSCLC patients not tested for genetic alterations, as identified through literature research, was applied to this SHI-NSCLC patient group to determine the final study population, hereafter referred as the “target population” [8, 16, 17, 20]. It was assumed that the proportion of untested patients is uniformly distributed across all disease stages. This assumption was adopted in the absence of stage-specific testing rate data for Germany: Published data from the CRISP registry primarily characterize molecular testing rates in stage IV disease [8], and systematic stage-specific testing rates for earlier NSCLC stages in Germany are not currently available. The implications of this simplification are discussed in the limitations.

The stage distribution of NSCLC (stages IA to IV) was determined based on literature [5, 6, 30, 33–36]. Literature screening was also used to determine the distribution of the molecular markers (EGFR mutation, BRAF V600 mutation, ALK fusion, ROS1 fusion, RET mutation, NTRK1-3 fusion, KRAS G12C mutation, MET ex14 mutation, NRG1 fusion and HER2 (ERBB2) mutation) as well as the distribution of PD-L1 expression > 1% [7, 8, 10–12, 20–22, 34, 37–49, 52–53]. These distributions were pooled using bootstrapping in R with 1,000 repetitions, and the mean value obtained from the bootstrapping was used as model input. The distribution of molecular markers was assumed to be uniform across all disease stages. Furthermore, molecular markers were assumed to be mutually exclusive, meaning each patient was assigned to a single biomarker category for the purpose of therapy assignment; this is supported by published evidence that co-occurrence of two primary driver mutations in NSCLC is exceedingly rare and does not typically alter first-line treatment decisions [5, 33]. Detection rates for genetic alterations were derived from the testing method (NGS) recommended by the German S3 Guideline and relevant literature [8, 33], assuming 100% of the target population receives molecular RT; this defines a theoretical best-case scenario to quantify the maximum potential benefit of universal reflex testing for the currently untested population, rather than modelling real-world adoption rates. Test sensitivity was set to 95% for NGS-based mutation testing and 96.5% for PD-L1 IHC testing [8, 33]; test specificity was assumed to be 100% for all assays. Based on the number of patients with detected mutations, therapy assignment and cost calculations were simulated. Patients with a detected mutation were assumed to receive a respective TT; this reflects guideline-concordant care in organized molecular testing pathways and is consistent with high targeted therapy uptake rates observed in structured programmes such as the German nNGM, where mutation-positive patients predominantly receive matched first-line treatment [15]. Patients without detected genetic alterations were determined by subtracting the number of patients with identified mutations from the total target population. This group was divided into two groups: (1) patients with elevated PDL-1 expression eligible for immunochemotherapy, and (2) patients not eligible for TT nor for immunochemotherapy (“others”) (see Fig. 2). The latter received alternative treatment according to the German S3 guideline.

Therapy-relevant biomarkers and their respective therapy options were defined according to European Medicines Agency’s European Public Assessment Reports (EPARs) and medical guidelines. Only TTs – predominantly TKIs - approved for first-line treatment were included in the model, as the analysis focused on the initial treatment phase following diagnosis of NSCLC and RT. The initial treatment phase was defined as first therapeutical treatment after NSCLC diagnosis, including adjuvant and neoadjuvant treatment.

Clinical Inputs

Clinical inputs for each therapy were defined as mPFS and mOS, based on published product information, health technology assessments (HTAs), and clinical studies. In the model, mOS and mPFS were used as measures of effectiveness and were normalized to the stage distribution to reflect a realistic clinical setting. Stage-specific values were calculated by multiplying mOS or mPFS by the proportion of patients in each stage. Monetary inputs

Annual therapy costs of TTs were calculated based on pharmacy list price information (Taxe-VK) of Lauer-Taxe (as of April 1, 2025). Costs were calculated for the recommended medication regime of each targeted therapy (including TKIs and other approved agents), per cycle and per year. Administration cycles and dosages were obtained from the EPAR product information and Engelhardt et al. [50]. Calculations were based on an average patient based on mean German population data (weight = 80 kg, height = 180 cm, age > 50 years, body surface area = 2 m2) [63]. Obligatory pre- and concomitant medication such as sodium chloride, paracetamol, or clemastine were not included in the price calculation. If the medication was combined with chemotherapy or cytostatics, these costs were included in the price calculation according to the respective product information. If applied intravenously, the price of a whole flask was used for the calculation of the cycle. Where multiple treatment options were approved for a given driver mutation or patient group, costs and clinical outcomes (mOS and mPFS) were averaged equally across all available options, assuming equal use. For survival parameters (mOS and mPFS), this equal-weight arithmetic mean was applied to the reported median values from individual agent trials. Sample-size-weighted pooling was not performed because trial-level sample sizes and individual patient-level data were not uniformly available across all agents; furthermore, time-to-event data required for more rigorous survival synthesis, such as parametric pooling of survival curves or restricted mean survival time estimation, were not accessible from published summary statistics alone. This limitation is discussed further in the limitations section. Real-world market shares were not applied, as weighing by market share would have required excluding agents for which market share data were unavailable, compromising data completeness. Equal weighting is a conservative and transparent approach that preserves data completeness; however, it represents a simplification that is most consequential for biomarker groups in which individual agents differ substantially in both efficacy and cost, most notably ALK-positive disease, where second- and third-generation inhibitors demonstrate markedly superior outcomes compared with crizotinib in both randomised trials and real-world settings. Additionally, costs associated with NGS testing were not included in the base case, owing to substantial variability across laboratories and differences in the number of bases analyzed.

Immunochemotherapy was restricted to checkpoint-inhibitors that are approved for PD-L1 expression > 1%, since PD-L1 testing by IHC is obligatory in NSCLC diagnostics according to the German S3 guideline and reimbursement requirements under the SHI. Patients with PD-L1 < 1% and no detectable driver mutation were therefore assigned to alternative (non-immunotherapy) treatment options. This approach reflects the German regulatory and guideline context and may differ from international settings where checkpoint inhibitor-based regimens are used regardless of PD-L1 expression level. PD-L1-specific immunochemotherapy options were defined according to EPAR product information and supporting literature [5, 33]. Alternative therapies were defined as all other treatment options according to the S3 guideline that are not TKIs or PD-L1-specific checkpoint-inhibitors (see Additional file 1: Table S1) and were differentiated by disease stage. Drug-related costs of alternative therapies were calculated using pharmacy list price (Taxe-VK) of Lauer-Taxe (1. April 2025). Outpatient alternative therapy costs were determined according to the Uniform Value Scale (Einheitlicher Bewertungsmaßstab, EBM; Q2 2025), while inpatient costs for resection were approximated using diagnosis related group (aG-DRG) revenue: aG-DRG: E05B, State base case value North Rhine-Westphalia 2025: € 4,394.22) [60].

Model output

The model output was defined as incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), calculated as annual therapy costs per patient divided by effectiveness (mean mOS and mPFS) per patient (see Fig. 2). Discounting of costs and outcomes was not applied, as the cost component reflects a single year of drug acquisition expenditures and effectiveness is derived from published clinical trial summary statistics rather than multi-year modelled projections; the implications are discussed in the limitations. Life-years gained (LYG), derived by converting mOS from months to years, were used as the effectiveness unit in the ICER denominator. Quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) were not used, as health-state utility weights were not available across all biomarker subgroups and disease stages included in the analysis. This approach is consistent with the German statutory health insurance context, in which IQWiG does not apply QALY-based willingness-to-pay thresholds [64]; the absence of utility weights is acknowledged as a limitation.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:ICER=\:\frac{\begin{aligned}&Costs\:Case\:2-4\cr &-Costs\:Base\:Case\end{aligned}}{\begin{aligned}&Effects\:Case\:2-4\cr &-Effects\:Base\:Case\end{aligned}}$$\end{document}

Annual therapy costs were expressed as drug acquisition expenditures per patient per year (€/year); clinical effectiveness parameters (mOS and mPFS) were derived from published clinical data in months and converted to years as the ICER denominator. The resulting ICER is thus reported in € per life-year gained.

Annual therapy costs represent drug acquisition expenditures over one year of treatment per patient; clinical effectiveness values (mOS and mPFS) are derived from published clinical evidence and reflect median survival outcomes in months, which are converted to years for the ICER denominator. The resulting ICER thus expresses the annual cost difference per life-year gained, that is, the incremental annual expenditure (or saving) associated with each additional life-year of survival benefit. As the cost numerator reflects a one-year treatment period while the effectiveness denominator captures median survival outcomes from clinical data, the ICER is to be interpreted as an annual cost-per-benefit metric rather than as a fully integrated lifetime cost-effectiveness estimate; this methodological characteristic is discussed further in the limitations.

Scenario and sensitivity analysis

Deterministic sensitivity analyses were performed using the upper and lower bounds of the proportion of untested patients (10–20%), which was the parameter varied to define the range of the estimated target population, as well as the 95% confidence intervals of mOS and mPFS sourced from the literature for each individual targeted therapy, which were subsequently aggregated across biomarkers within the model to derive the upper and lower bounds applied in the analysis.

An additional scenario analysis was conducted to explore the impact of mandatory ALK and EGFR exclusion testing, which is required prior to initiation of several PD-L1-based immunotherapies under their approved labels. While C1 assumes no molecular testing, this requirement means that some degree of ALK and EGFR testing may occur even in routine practice outside of structured reflex testing programmes. To reflect this, C1 was adjusted to incorporate routine ALK and EGFR testing across all disease stages before initiating PD-L1 immunochemotherapy; patients with detected driver mutations were then assigned to the respective TT, while only ALK/EGFR-negative patients proceeded to PD-L1-based treatment. This scenario was not adopted as the base case because the study aims to quantify the incremental value of structured RT programmes relative to a no-testing comparator, and the extent to which mandatory exclusion testing is consistently applied in practice for the untested population remains uncertain. In an additional scenario analysis, NGS test costs of €3,000 per panel were assumed [51] to assess the potential influence on cost-effectiveness.

To assess the sensitivity of the base-case results to the equal-weighting assumption applied within each biomarker group, an additional scenario analysis was conducted in which, for each biomarker, the single currently guideline-preferred first-line agent was applied instead of the equal-weighted average of all approved options: alectinib (ALK), osimertinib (EGFR), sotorasib (KRAS G12C), dabrafenib plus trametinib (BRAF V600), selpercatinib (RET), tepotinib (MET ex14), entrectinib (ROS1), the mean of larotrectinib and entrectinib (NTRK1-3), trastuzumab deruxtecan (HER2), afatinib (NRG1), and pembrolizumab (PD-L1). This scenario reflects a more clinically realistic prescribing pattern than the equal-weighted base case and was compared against the base-case results to assess the robustness of the cost-effectiveness conclusions.

A further scenario analysis explored the impact of sub-maximal testing uptake, addressing the base-case assumption of 100% molecular RT uptake among the target population. In this scenario, 80% of the target population were assumed to undergo molecular testing; the remaining 20% were assigned to immunochemotherapy according to the PD-L1 expression distribution (57.96% PD-L1 positive) or to alternative non-TT, non-immunotherapy treatment, consistent with the treatment assignment logic applied to the untested comparator (C1). This 80% testing uptake scenario was also combined with the NGS cost scenario described above; in this combined analysis, NGS panel costs of €3,000 were applied only to the 80% of the target population assumed to undergo testing, rather than to the full target population.

Scenario and sensitivity analysis

The deterministic sensitivity analysis showed that per-patient cost-effectiveness outcomes were invariant to population size assumptions across the tested range of 10% to 20% of untested patients. Model outcomes were sensitive to changes in mOS and mPFS parameters. In the base case analysis, mean mPFS ranged from 8.35 months (C1) to 13.77 months (C3/C4) and mean mOS from 19.74 months (C1) to 25.23 months (C2). At the lower limit of the 95% CI inputs, absolute mPFS and mOS values across C2–4 decreased (mPFS = 2.1–2.5 months; mOS = 2.9–4.4 months); C2–4 remained both less costly and more effective than C1, but the incremental clinical benefit versus C1 was reduced compared with the base case (ICER mPFS: -€4,188.36, -€2,565.07 for C2 and C3–4 respectively; ICER mOS: -€3,032.07, -€1,492.74, -€1,456.35 for C2, C3 and C4 respectively). At the upper limit, absolute mPFS and mOS values increased (mPFS/mOS = 5.9–6.1 months); C2–4 remained both less costly and more effective than C1, with the incremental clinical benefit versus C1 increased compared with the base case (see Additional file 1: Table S2 and Additional file 1: Figure S3).

In the base case analysis, NGS test costs were not included, yielding annual cost savings of €740 (C2) and €535 (C3/C4) per patient versus C1. When NGS test costs of €3,000 per patient were included, these cost savings were offset, resulting in net annual cost increases of €2,260 (C2) and €2,465 (C3/C4) per patient versus C1. As C2–4 were no longer less costly than C1 in this scenario, the incremental cost per life-year gained was €5,541.17 (C2) and €5,461.35 (C3/C4) for mPFS, and €4,945.61 (C2) and €5,408.95 (C3/C4) for mOS, while incremental survival benefits versus C1 remained unchanged (Table 6 and Additional file 1: Table S2 and Additional file 1: Figure S2).

In a scenario analysis applying the single guideline-preferred first-line agent for each biomarker (alectinib for ALK-positive disease, osimertinib for EGFR-mutated disease, sotorasib for KRAS G12C, dabrafenib plus trametinib for BRAF V600, selpercatinib for RET, tepotinib for MET exon14, entrectinib for ROS1, the mean of larotrectinib and entrectinib for NTRK 1–3, trastuzumab deruxtecan for HER2, afatinib for NRG1, and pembrolizumab for PD-L1) instead of the equal-weighted average of all approved agents, mean incremental mOS was 6.16 months (C2), 5.77 months (C3), and 6.50 months (C4), and mean incremental mPFS was 6.80 months (C2/C3) and 7.66 months (C4). Annual cost savings versus C1 were €1,670 (C2) and €1,567 (C3/C4), corresponding to €2,895–€3,258 per life-year gained (mOS) (Table 6).

A further scenario analysis reduced testing uptake from 100% to 80% of the target population, with the remaining 20% assigned to immunochemotherapy according to the PD-L1 expression distribution (57.96% PD-L1 positive) or to alternative therapy, consistent with the treatment assignment logic applied to C1. Incremental mOS and mPFS values versus C1 were unchanged from the base case (Table 6). Annual cost savings versus C1 were €1,557 (C2) and €1,393 (C3/C4), corresponding to €3,058–€3,409 per life-year gained (mOS). When this 80% uptake scenario was combined with NGS panel costs of €3,000 applied to 80% of the target population, C2–C4 showed a net annual cost increase of €843 (C2) and €1,007 (C3/C4), corresponding to €1,844–€2,209 per life-year gained (mOS) (Table 6).

Discussion

This health economic study demonstrated that RT-related TT-use in NSCLC is clinically beneficial and a dominant strategy from the German SHI perspective. In the base case analysis, all modelled RT scenarios showed lower overall costs and higher effectiveness compared with C1 (no testing) in the initial treatment phase. Among these, guideline-based testing (C2) achieved the largest absolute cost reduction, whereas broader panel testing (C3 and C4) showed marginally higher mPFS (13.77 vs. 13.25 months) but marginally lower mOS (25.21 vs. 25.23 months) compared with C2, at slightly higher expenditure, a pattern explained by the composition of targeted therapies accessed through broader testing. When NGS test costs of €3,000 per patient were included in a scenario analysis, the cost advantage of C2–4 versus C1 was reversed, though incremental survival benefits were maintained. The sensitivity analysis indicated that per-patient cost-effectiveness outcomes were insensitive to population size assumptions, but sensitive to changes in mOS and mPFS parameters. These findings should be interpreted within the scope of the decision tree model, which was designed to compare reflex testing strategies at the point of first-line treatment initiation rather than to model the full NSCLC disease trajectory, including progression and multi-line treatment sequencing.

The findings suggest that broader molecular testing coverage is associated with improved survival outcomes and potential cost efficiencies, primarily by supporting more appropriate treatment selection between targeted therapies and immunochemotherapy. In the model, patients in C2 (guideline-based testing) still received immunochemotherapy more frequently than in the broader testing scenarios and thus derived most of their clinical benefit from optimized use of these regimens rather than from targeted therapies. This explains the slightly lower survival gains, but greatest overall cost reduction observed in C2. This pattern, the shift from cost savings to survival gains as RT is moved earlier in the disease course, reflects the stage-specific testing, whereas in C2 comprehensive molecular testing and the associated targeted therapies were only introduced from stage IV onward, while C3 extended full panel testing from stage IIIB onward and C4 to all disease stages. Early access to broad testing in C3 and C4 allowed more patients to access targeted therapies, slightly improving survival but also increasing overall expenses in earlier stages. Similar results of C3 and C4 were anticipated, as these scenarios differ only by the inclusion of NRG1-targeted therapy, which currently lacks first-line approval and therefore had no influence on the modeled outcomes. It is noteworthy that C3 and C4 showed marginally lower mean mOS (25.21 months) than C2 (25.23 months), despite higher mean mPFS (13.77 vs. 13.25 months). This reflects a composition effect: Broader testing in C3 and C4 detects additional mutations whose associated targeted therapies confer stronger PFS than OS benefits relative to the agents predominating in C2. The absolute difference of 0.02 months (less than one day) is clinically negligible and does not contradict the overarching finding that all RT scenarios substantially improve survival compared with the no-testing base case (C1: 19.74 months mOS). Within the German HTA system, IQWiG’s “General Methods” does not specify a general ICER threshold across indications; cost-effectiveness is instead assessed on an indication-specific basis, and no normative willingness-to-pay threshold analogous to NICE’s exists in this framework [64]. Thus, since all RT cases were dominant and cost-effective compared with the base case, the ideal case cannot be determined in a German setting [54]. Accordingly, no willingness-to-pay threshold line is depicted in the supplementary cost-effectiveness plane (Additional file 1: Figure S2): In the base-case analysis, C2–4 fall in the southeast quadrant (lower costs and higher effectiveness than C1), representing unambiguous dominance irrespective of any threshold. When NGS panel costs of €3,000 are included, the ICERs of approximately €4,946–€5,541 per life-year gained cannot be formally evaluated against a German threshold but are contextually low relative to cost-effectiveness benchmarks applied in other European healthcare systems. The additional scenario analyses further support the robustness of the base-case conclusions. Applying guideline-preferred single agents rather than equal-weighted averages within each biomarker group increased both the survival benefit and cost savings associated with RT relative to the base case, indicating that the equal-weighting simplification is, if anything, conservative and that the base-case findings are robust to this assumption. Reducing testing uptake to 80% did not change the modelled mOS and mPFS deltas, as these effectiveness parameters are calculated as branch-level averages that are independent of the number of patients assigned to each treatment arm; only the cost outcome, which reflects the treatment-mix composition of the population, changed under this scenario. The larger absolute cost savings observed under 80% uptake likely reflect the larger proportion of patients who remain untested and are assigned to the comparatively inexpensive alternative-therapy pathway rather than the full RT-enabled treatment pathway. When this scenario was combined with NGS panel costs applied pro rata to the tested proportion, the resulting cost-effectiveness ratio was substantially more favorable than under full-population NGS costing, consistent with NGS costs being incurred for a smaller share of the target population under sub-maximal uptake. Together, these analyses indicate that the deterministic base-case conclusion, that RT-enabled targeted therapy is a highly cost-effective strategy relative to no testing, driven by early and adequate use of targeted therapy, is not contingent on the equal-weighting or 100%-uptake assumptions and holds under more conservative and clinically realistic alternative specifications.

Evidence across multiple settings shows that reflex (pathologist-initiated) testing increases testing completeness, shortens turnaround times, and raises the proportion of patients receiving matched targeted therapy, thereby improving clinical pathways and resource utilization [15, 23–25, 28, 29]. National programs demonstrate feasibility at scale with acceptable turnaround (median 11 days) and measurable survival benefits, while German registry data (CRISP) highlight heterogeneous implementation that reflex pathways could mitigate [8, 11, 15]. Economic evaluations consistently indicate that NGS-based reflex strategies are cost-effective versus serial single-gene testing [26–29], although prior studies have emphasized different dimensions of value. Hospital-level analyses demonstrated that implementing rapid in-house NGS for metastatic NSCLC can offset upfront infrastructure and workflow costs through reduced send-out testing and more efficient treatment allocation [55]. Pathway-level models further showed that upfront NGS generates cost savings by reducing delays to effective therapy and avoiding ineffective interim treatments, a mechanism central to reflex testing [56]. Broader outcomes-based evaluations, including the analysis reported survival gains alongside cost differences for comprehensive genomic profiling strategies, while system-level interventions aimed at increasing effective NGS use have also been shown to be cost-effective [57, 58]. Real-world evidence additionally suggests that upfront NGS is associated with improved overall survival compared with alternative testing strategies in advanced NSCLC [59]. Building on this literature, our analysis extends the economic perspective from diagnostic and implementation efficiency to the downstream treatment value of reflex testing by quantifying survival gains and treatment-related cost savings attributable to targeted therapies enabled by RT, thereby providing payer-relevant evidence within the German SHI context.

Nonetheless, RT-implementation in Germany faces structural, legal, and reimbursement constraints. Although the nNGM already provides a quality-assured reflex framework covering > 85% of the statutory-insured population-with observed survival advantages-payers emphasize that similar benefits outside specialized networks remain unproven and call for additional German-specific outcome and cost-effectiveness evidence before wider rollout [15, 23]. Operational challenges include inconsistent ordering, variable acceptance of cytology material, and workflow bottlenecks such as order routing and electronic health record (EHR) integration [25]. Reimbursement in Germany is fragmented: Hospital reimbursement via DRG flat rate system financing covers only a small share of NGS costs, shifting financial burden to hospitals or outpatient settings, while mandatory written consent for coverage and data use increases administrative workload and historically contributes to data gaps [16, 60].

Proposed solutions include stage-independent RT algorithms, the strengthening of regionally coordinated genomic medicine networks with shared digital infrastructures (e.g., nNGM), and cross-sector financing instruments [15, 65, 66]. These encompass established care models such as Ambulante spezialfachärztliche Versorgung (ASV) as well as innovation-oriented reimbursement mechanisms, in particular §64e model projects under German Social Code Book V (SGB V), which allow time-limited, evaluation-based remuneration of innovative diagnostic pathways outside the regular benefit catalogue to generate evidence for permanent coverage decisions [67–69]. Pilot initiatives within nNGM structures could facilitate the evaluation of automated reflex NGS in routine care, supported by prospective real-world data on outcomes, timelines, guideline adherence, and costs [65, 66]. Quality-linked remuneration with defined service levels may help ensure consistently high-performing diagnostic structures. Persisting challenges include underdiagnosis, uneven access beyond established networks, and resistance to structural consolidation. While international experience supports the feasibility of reflex NGS, differences in governance and financing limit direct transferability, highlighting the need for solutions tailored to the German healthcare system [8, 11, 12, 15, 16, 22–29, 55–59].

Several limitations must be considered. First, we assumed a true-positive detection rate of approximately 95%, although diagnostic performance varies across laboratories, assays, and sample types; false-positive or false-negative results could therefore alter both clinical outcomes and the cost-effectiveness-balance. Second, three key modelling assumptions may not fully reflect routine clinical practice. The model assumed (i) 100% uptake of molecular RT among the target population; (ii) 100% receipt of guideline-matched targeted therapy by all mutation-positive patients; and (iii) mutual exclusivity of driver biomarker categories. The 100% testing and treatment uptake assumptions define ideal-case scenarios establishing an upper bound on the potential benefit of universal reflex testing: in practice, sub-maximal uptake rates would proportionally attenuate the absolute magnitude of cost savings and survival gains, though the per-patient ICER is expected to remain directionally similar if costs and effectiveness scale proportionally. The mutual exclusivity assumption is supported by published evidence that co-occurrence of primary driver mutations in NSCLC is exceedingly rare, limiting the aggregate impact of this simplification on model outcomes [5, 33]. A scenario analysis varying testing uptake (80% versus 100% of the target population) was performed to address this assumption (see Scenario and sensitivity analysis and Results); however, treatment uptake and biomarker co-occurrence rates are embedded within the decision tree’s branching logic itself, rather than represented as scalar input parameters that can be varied through simple scenario substitution; formal sensitivity analyses on these two parameters were therefore not performed in the current analysis. Future analyses should incorporate these remaining parameters to more fully characterize the sensitivity of results to these structural assumptions. Additionally, the model assumed that untested patients are uniformly distributed across disease stages and are clinically representative of the broader NSCLC population. In practice, untested patients may differ systematically: In the German context, the S3 guideline mandates comprehensive molecular testing primarily from stage IV onward, suggesting the untested population may be disproportionately concentrated in earlier stages (IB–IIIA), where structured testing is less routinely initiated. Conversely, some untested patients with advanced disease may represent a subgroup with rapidly progressing disease or poor performance status, for whom testing was not completed before treatment initiation. Systematic differences in age, performance status, or comorbidity could also alter both the costs and the expected survival benefits associated with targeted therapy in this population. The direction and magnitude of this selection bias cannot be quantified from currently available data; future analyses should incorporate stage-specific testing rate data, ideally from the CRISP registry or nNGM, to derive a more representative population structure for the untested patient group. Third, test costs were excluded, as they vary widely depending on the laboratory and the number of bases determined; however, real-world variability, repeat testing, or workflow inefficiencies may increase overall costs. Sensitivity analyses incorporating NGS costs of €3,000 still yielded cost-effectiveness results, with ICERs of approximately €4,946 – €5,409 per life-year gained, consistent with the primary analysis metric. A range of NGS panel costs was not separately modelled, as NGS cost enters the model as a fixed additive term in the cost numerator: the ICER–NGS-cost relationship is linear and fully characterized by the two reported cost points (€0 and €3,000), from which the ICER for any alternative cost assumption can be derived by interpolation, analogous to the population-size parameter shown to be invariant in the deterministic sensitivity analysis. Fourth, only drug acquisition costs were included in the model, whereas full therapy costs, including administration, monitoring, adverse event management, and downstream care, were not considered. This omission is particularly relevant for comparisons between targeted therapies and immunochemotherapy: oral targeted therapies (TKIs) and intravenously administered checkpoint inhibitors differ substantially in administration route, monitoring intensity, and adverse event profiles, resulting in meaningfully different resource use beyond drug acquisition. The direction and magnitude of the resulting bias on comparative cost estimates cannot be determined without comprehensive costing data. Specifically, costs not captured include toxicity management, hospitalization, subsequent-line therapies, and palliative care. Importantly, these omissions do not straightforwardly bias results in favor of targeted therapies: immune-related adverse events (IRAEs) associated with checkpoint inhibitor-based immunochemotherapy, the principal comparator in the model, can require hospitalization, prolonged corticosteroid treatment, and specialist management, generating substantial healthcare resource use that is also excluded. Future analyses should incorporate comprehensive cost-of-care data to produce a full payer-perspective estimate. Costs were calculated assuming full vial use, consistent with German SHI reimbursement under Lauer-Taxe, where billing reflects the listed package price rather than the quantity administered; a no-wastage sensitivity scenario was therefore not performed, as full-vial billing reflects actual German practice rather than a conservative modelling choice. In addition, the deterministic sensitivity analysis was restricted to target population size and survival parameters (mOS and mPFS); biomarker prevalence and test sensitivity were not varied, despite uncertainty ranges being available for both. Given that the model’s cost-effectiveness results were primarily driven by survival outcomes, these parameters were not expected to materially alter the conclusions; however, future analyses should incorporate them to more fully characterise parameter uncertainty. Furthermore, health-state utility weights were not incorporated into the model; effectiveness is therefore measured as life-years gained (LYG) rather than quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs). Utility-weighted outcomes would capture the differential impact on health-related quality of life of targeted therapies versus immunochemotherapy, which may differ meaningfully between regimens due to differences in adverse event profiles, route of administration, and treatment burden. As IQWiG does not apply QALY-based thresholds within the German statutory health insurance framework, this limitation primarily affects comparability with international cost-effectiveness analyses conducted in QALY-based systems. Furthermore, no probabilistic sensitivity analysis was performed. As the principal sources of uncertainty raised related to structural modelling assumptions, agent selection within biomarker groups and testing uptake, rather than distributional parameter uncertainty, and as robust distributional data were not uniformly available across all parameters (e.g., rare-biomarker survival variance), the two targeted scenario analyses described above were prioritised as a more directly interpretable and pragmatic alternative; strict conclusions regarding cost-effectiveness cannot be drawn, and full parameter uncertainty is not captured. Drug prices are also dynamic and subject to market forces such as patent expiration and generic entry, which may substantially alter relative costs over time. These potential pricing changes were not simulated, limiting insight into the long-term robustness of the model. Fifth, we used average treatment costs rather than explicitly modeling dynamic market share shifts, which may influence cost estimates as prescribing patterns evolve. This simplification is most consequential for the ALK-positive subgroup, where approved inhibitors, including crizotinib, alectinib, brigatinib, and lorlatinib, differ substantially in both acquisition cost and clinical outcomes. Furthermore, the use of an arithmetic mean of reported median survival values, rather than a sample-size-weighted pooled estimate or a parametric synthesis of survival curves, represents a methodological approximation. The mean of medians from individual trials does not equal the median of the combined patient population and does not account for differences in trial sample sizes, follow-up duration, or censoring patterns across studies. Individual patient-level data and trial sample sizes were not uniformly available across all agents to support a more rigorous synthesis approach. Real-world comparative evidence consistently demonstrates the superiority of second- and third-generation ALK inhibitors, particularly alectinib, over crizotinib in progression-free survival in ALK-positive NSCLC [70]; pooling agents with such divergent efficacy and cost profiles under equal-weighting assumptions may therefore bias the average survival and cost parameters attributed to this subgroup. To address this, an additional scenario analysis was conducted applying the single currently guideline-preferred first-line agent for each biomarker (e.g., alectinib for ALK, osimertinib for EGFR) instead of the equal-weighted average, providing a check on the robustness of the base-case conclusions to this assumption (see Scenario and sensitivity analysis and Results). Moreover, the model applied a limited time horizon of one year and therefore does not account for longer-term changes in pricing, market dynamics, or the introduction of new treatment options. Relatedly, the cost numerator of the ICER reflects one-year drug acquisition expenditures, whereas the effectiveness denominator is based on mOS and mPFS values reported across multi-year clinical trials. The resulting ICER therefore represents an annual incremental cost per life-year gained, that is, the incremental annual drug expenditure (or saving) associated with each additional life-year of survival benefit, rather than a conventional lifetime cost-effectiveness ratio in which cumulative costs and outcomes are modelled over a consistent time horizon. This non-standard structure means that the reported values cannot be directly compared with threshold-based assessments derived from lifetime models (e.g., Markov state-transition analyses) and may underestimate total treatment expenditures accrued over the full survival period. A state-transition model with a lifetime horizon would be required to capture the full cost–outcome relationship and is recommended for future analyses. Additionally, in the 80% testing uptake scenario analysis, costs and effectiveness outcomes were aggregated asymmetrically: annual costs were calculated across the full target population, with the untested 20% assigned costs consistent with the no-testing comparator, whereas the reported incremental mOS and mPFS values reflect the branch-level average benefit among tested and treated patients only and were not diluted by the untested proportion. Consequently, the resulting ICER for this scenario should be interpreted as the cost-effectiveness of the tested subgroup rather than as a fully consistent population-level estimate; a population-level estimate would additionally require weighting the survival benefit by the proportion of the target population actually tested. Such an analysis should incorporate discounting of both costs and outcomes at the recommended rate for Germany (3% per annum, consistent with IQWiG general methods), as the present model’s annual cost perspective does not include temporal discounting. Finally, clinical inputs were primarily derived from advanced-stage NSCLC data due to limited evidence in earlier disease stages. This limitation is particularly consequential for scenarios C3 and C4, which extend molecular testing and targeted therapy use to stages IB–IIIA (C3) and IB–IV (C4). Patients in these stages predominantly receive curative-intent treatment, including surgical resection and adjuvant or neoadjuvant systemic therapy, rather than the palliative first-line systemic therapy from which published mOS and mPFS values are primarily derived. Early-stage patients have substantially longer survival than patients with metastatic disease: for example, adjuvant osimertinib in EGFR-mutated stage IB–IIIA NSCLC (ADAURA) demonstrated a 5-year disease-free survival rate of 65% versus 26% with placebo [71], and adjuvant alectinib in ALK-positive stage IB–IIIA NSCLC (ALINA) showed markedly improved disease-free survival versus chemotherapy [72]. Applying advanced-stage mOS/mPFS values to early-stage targeted therapy recipients therefore likely underestimates true survival in the C3 and C4 arms, which may in turn underestimate the incremental survival benefit of extending reflex testing to earlier stages. The direction and magnitude of net bias on incremental outcomes are difficult to quantify, however, because C1 (the no-testing base case) applies the same advanced-stage survival inputs to its early-stage patients, meaning a degree of cross-scenario cancellation may occur. Future analyses should incorporate stage-stratified survival inputs, in particular adjuvant-setting data for EGFR- and ALK-targeted therapies, to substantially strengthen the validity of C3 and C4 cost-effectiveness estimates.

Furthermore, the model’s aggregate predictions were not formally validated against independent real-world datasets. While mutation prevalence estimates and survival inputs are directly derived from published clinical evidence and German registry sources, a cross-validation of model-predicted outcomes, particularly the base-case (C1) overall survival estimates, against observed outcomes for untested NSCLC patients in the CRISP or nNGM registries was not performed. Such external validation would strengthen confidence in the model’s calibration and is recommended for future analyses.

Taken together, the model provides evidence that increased RT has the potential to be cost-effective, with survival benefits and cost savings attributable to earlier identification of actionable driver mutations and receipt of matched targeted therapy. This addresses key reimbursement concerns and offers decision-grade evidence supporting a financed, standardized reflex pathway in Germany. Future research should incorporate full therapy costs, probabilistic sensitivity analyses, dynamic pricing scenarios, real-world adoption rates, laboratory performance variability, reimbursement frameworks, and stage-specific data to validate and refine these findings.
