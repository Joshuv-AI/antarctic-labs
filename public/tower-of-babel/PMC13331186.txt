Suturing maneuvers and technical insufficiencies in microvascular anastomosis dry laboratory training analyzed with the skill decomposition approach
Authors: Evgenii Belykh, Arevik Abramyan, Dara S. Farhadi, Xiaochun Zhao, Ali Tayebi Meybodi, Leandro Borba Moreira, Mark C. Preul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: This study applied a skill decomposition approach to identify and quantify technical nuances of performing subtasks of a microvascular anastomosis by analyzing suturing maneuvers, the duration of each step, and technical insufficiencies in a controlled setting.Methods: Four microneurosurgery trainees performed 16 end-to-side microanastomoses, alternating between the interrupted technique (IT) and the continuous technique (CT) of suturing on standardized artificial vessels. At the subtask level, 24 reproducible suturing techniques and 42 technical insufficiencies were identified and analyzed.Results: Significant contributors to faster suture included biting both walls in a single move, dominant-hand suturing, and suturing directed toward the participant. Total anastomosis time correlated negatively with the sequential attempt order (p < 0.05) and correlated positively with the total number of technical insufficiencies (p < 0.05). The CT was 19% faster than the IT (mean [standard deviation (SD)], 15.7 [3.7] vs. 19.1 [4.7] min, p = 0.02). The IT was associated with 40% more technical insufficiencies per anastomosis than the CT (mean [SD], 37 [23.3] vs. 26.5 [12.2], p = 0.04). The CT throws were associated with insufficiencies related to controlling the suture thread, whereas the IT was associated with insufficiencies related to knot tying.Conclusion: By converting well-known microsurgical concepts into measurable performance subtasks, the skill decomposition approach offers a foundation for objective skill assessment and targeted training intervention. Identified maneuvers and insufficiencies that influenced the anastomosis subtasks form the foundation for evidence-based microsurgery training. A better understanding of the most effective microsurgical suturing techniques will contribute to their refinement, improving anastomosis quality and decreasing performance time.

INTRODUCTION

Proficiency in microvascular anastomosis is a crucial aspect of neurosurgery training and can be mastered only through repetitive laboratory practice.[18,19] The majority of cerebrovascular pathology is now treated endovascularly, leaving only the most complex cases for neurosurgeons.

Although the basic techniques of microvascular anastomosis are widely described in the neurosurgery literature, technical nuances, timing, and suturing insufficiencies vary among individual surgeons. Both the interrupted technique (IT) and the continuous technique (CT) have been conventionally used in extracranial-intracranial bypass surgery, yet which technique is superior remains inconclusive.[3,7] Interestingly, no studies have quantitatively evaluated which anastomosis steps, surgical maneuvers, and nuances are associated with fewer mistakes, better suture integrity, and shorter anastomosis time.

In response to this variability, we designed a study of anastomosis techniques to analyze different bypass methods through the use of a skill-decomposition approach. Skill decomposition is an established conceptual framework that subdivides complex procedures into smaller, discrete subtasks that can be objectively evaluated to identify specific strengths and weaknesses and optimize surgical performance. The goals of this project were to demonstrate that technical insufficiencies affect the timing of the corresponding subtasks in CT or IT and to estimate the time cost of various types of insufficiencies and individual subtypes. Individual aims of this study were to (1) quantitatively analyze IT and CT anastomosis in a controlled laboratory setting, (2) identify and quantitatively assess specific anastomosis subtasks, (3) assess the technical maneuvers used to complete subtasks and their effect on subtask efficiency, and (4) identify and assess individual technical insufficiencies and their effect on subtask efficiency.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Recruitment of participants

Neurosurgical residents and neurosurgery fellows at Barrow Neurological Institute who had completed advanced laboratory microsurgery training were recorded as they performed each anastomosis, with <2 weeks between sessions. Institutional review board approval was not required because no personal or patient information was used, no identifying physical characteristics were recorded, and the study was conducted in a training setting. Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines were followed.

Preparation of vessels

We trimmed 2-mm-diameter polyvinyl alcohol artificial vessels (Takayama Instrument, Inc., Japan) into 2-cm fragments. Recipient vessels underwent a 2.5-mm arteriotomy, and donor vessels were prepared with a 30° angled fish-mouth end. Each participant received 16 numbered, sealed envelopes, each containing a donor–recipient pair and a 10-0 nylon suture.

Anastomosis exercise

Participants alternated between IT and CT end-to-side anastomoses [Figure 1]. The first 6 anastomoses were for practice and were excluded from analysis. After completion of the anastomosis exercises, the videos were stopped, and the samples were sealed with their identification cards for quality inspection.

Steps involved in completing a microvascular anastomosis using the interrupted technique (IT) (left) and the continuous technique (CT) (right) of suturing. The fish-mouth vessel was oriented toward the participant’s dominant hand. Participants applied methylene blue to the vessel edges and prepared the donor vessel with 2 sutures (heel and toe), which served as a common starting position for both the IT and the CT. Instructions for IT suturing required placing a center suture first at the midpoint between the heel and toe sutures. Next, 4 sutures were placed, with 2 on each side of the center suture. The opposite side of the anastomosis was completed similarly, resulting in a total of 12 interrupted sutures. In CT suturing, running continuous sutures were placed by making 5 suture passes through the vessel wall on one side, following the standard corner heel-and-toe stitch steps. CT suturing ended by tightening the suture line, then tying the knot connecting the thread to the other corner stitch. The opposite side of the anastomosis was then completed similarly, resulting in a total of 12 stitches. Used with permission from Dr. Arevik Abramyan.

Video analysis

A neurosurgery fellow proficient in microsurgery and a trained medical student jointly evaluated 40 randomized, anonymized videos to quantitatively assess timing, suturing maneuvers, and technical insufficiencies with the use of the skill decomposition approach. The analysis measured the time required for each anastomosis subtask, identified the suturing maneuvers (24 categories), and detailed the number and type of technical insufficiencies (42 categories). A subtask was defined as a discrete action, such as placing, tightening, or tying a knot or transitioning between sutures. Technical insufficiency refers to a deviation from the intended suturing maneuver that compromises the efficiency, precision, or quality of the anastomosis subtask. Anastomosis time was recorded from the first needle puncture to the final suture cut.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Statistica 6.1 (Dell Inc.). Given the small sample size (n = 40) and nonnormal distribution, Mann–Whitney U and Wilcoxon matched-pairs tests were used. Data are presented as mean (SD). To account for repeated measures within participants and interindividual variability, we performed mixed-effects modeling using the “Variance Components and Mixed Model ANOVA/ANCOVA” module. Participant number was treated as a random effect, whereas suturing technique, subtask maneuvers, insufficiency types, and attempt number were included as fixed effects. The statistical significance of model terms was evaluated using F-tests, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Data will be made available upon request. Absolute increase in time was calculated in relation to the ideal time for a subtask.

RESULTS

Four male neurosurgery fellows (age range, 26–31 years) who had completed neurosurgery residency training and were proficient in microsurgery participated in the study. Each performed 6 practice anastomoses and 10 anastomoses for analysis (5 with the IT and 5 with the CT) within 3 days. One video was corrupted, leaving 39 videos available for total time analysis and 38 for technical insufficiencies analysis (1 video was excluded due to poor visibility). The 38 videos had a mean (SD) of 22.7 (4.1) steps for the IT and 33 (3.9) steps for the CT, for an overall total of 1132 steps [Figure 2].

Timing of suturing techniques

After adjusting for repeated measures, interparticipant variability, and total technical insufficiencies per anastomosis using mixed-effects modeling, CT suturing remained faster than IT suturing (15.7 [3.7] vs. 19.1 [4.5] min), yielding a 3.5-min reduction (95% CI 0.8–6.1 min, p = 0.01, [Table 1]). A significant random effect of participant was observed (F = 6.6, p = 0.001), indicating variance across individuals. Three participants showed significantly faster anastomosis completion times with CT (16.5 [3.5] vs. 20.6 [4.2] min, p = 0.008 by Wilcoxon matched pairs test), whereas 1 participant showed no difference (13.0 [2.8] vs. 13.6 [1.1] min, p = 0.90 by Wilcoxon matched pairs test). Attempt number had a significant effect (F = 14.5, p < 0.001), demonstrating a learning curve. Total technical insufficiencies were strongly associated with anastomosis time (F = 10.4, p < 0.001).

Timing of suturing subtasks

The mean (SD) time to complete a CT throw was 72% faster than that needed to complete an IT suture (18 [18] vs. 65 [31] s), with a mean reduction of 47 s (95% CI 42–52 s, F = 216.4, p < 0.001). This was adjusted for interparticipant variability, repeated measures, and total insufficiencies using a mixed-effects model.

Anchor sutures (either CT or IT) were completed more slowly than IT sutures on the side of the anastomosis (78 [34] s for a toe suture, 13 s slower [95% CI 2–24 s]; 95 [56] s for a heel suture, 30 s slower [95% CI 13–48 s], p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in anchor suture timing between CT and IT (p = 0.2) when adjusted for participants (F = 0.8, p = 0.4), attempt number (F = 1.5, p = 0.4), and total insufficiencies (F = 49, p < 0.001).

The duration of transition intervals was weakly associated with suturing technique (IT 29 [22] s for the IT vs. 25 [24] s for the CT, F = 4.1, p = 0.04) when adjusted for the sequential order of anastomosis (F = 0.3, p = 0.6), participant (F = 2.5, p = 0.06), and total insufficiencies (F = 82.6, p < 0.001).

When performing anastomosis using the CT, tightening of the suture line required a mean of 51 s (range 17–110 s) and was significantly associated with the sequential order of anastomosis (F = 6.7, p = 0.02), individual participant (F = 17.7, p < 0.001), and total insufficiencies (F = 4.1, p = 0.05). Placing the connecting suture required a mean of 46 s (range 16–108 s) and was associated with interparticipant variability (F = 3.6, p = 0.02) and total insufficiencies (F = 40, p < 0.001), but not with attempt number (F = 0.3, p = 0.6).

We analyzed the maneuvers used to accomplish subtasks and their effects on timing [Table 2]. We identified 4 categories of knot-tying maneuvers [Video 1]: Classic, tail pull, alternating, and other.[2] After adjusting for participant and anastomosis order using mixed-effects modeling, the classic knot-tying technique was associated with faster completion of individual sutures when using the IT [Table 2]. The classic knot-tying technique and “thread tail pulled constantly in same direction” were associated with mean (95% CI) reductions in suture time of 37 (16–58) s and 30 (7–53) s, respectively, compared with the alternating grab-and-release technique (p = 0.02).

Suturing with the dominant hand was faster during CT throws and IT sutures (p = 0.004 and p = 0.02, respectively). Suturing toward the participant was associated with a reduction in individual throw time when using the CT (38% reduction; mean [95% CI] difference, 10 [−0.6–20] s; p = 0.002), but the effect on the duration of individual sutures when using the IT was not significant (18% reduction; mean [95% CI] difference, 15 [−10–40] s; p = 0.9).

When using the CT, biting both vessel walls in a single motion was associated with a mean (95% CI) reduction in throw time of 24 (11–37) s (60%) compared with donor-to-recipient sequential biting and 31 (17–79) s (66%) compared with recipient-to-donor biting (p < 0.001). When using the IT, single-motion biting was associated with a mean (95% CI) reduction in suture time of 41 (22–60) s (40%) compared with recipient-to-donor sequential biting and 32 (14–50) s (34%) compared with donor-to-recipient biting (p < 0.001).

Cutting both threads simultaneously was associated with a mean (95% CI) advantage of 10 (3–18) s (15%) over sequential cutting; however, this was not significant after adjustment for participant and sequential attempt number (p = 0.93).

Accounting for technical insufficiencies in the mixed-effects model did not alter the significant effects of sequential attempt number and interparticipant variability. For IT sutures, handedness and suture bite technique remained significantly associated with subtask completion time, whereas the association with knot-tying technique was no longer significant (F = 1.9, p = 0.11), suggesting that technical insufficiencies partially explained the effect of knot-tying technique. Similarly, for throws using the CT, suture bite direction and suture bite technique remained significant predictors, whereas handedness was no longer significantly associated with completion time (F = 2.3, p = 0.12).

Technical insufficiencies

We identified 42 types of technical insufficiencies and maintenance maneuvers, which were then grouped into 6 categories [Table 3]. In mixed-effects modeling, the mean (SD) total number of insufficiencies per procedure was significantly associated with the suture technique (37 [23.3] total insufficiencies with the CT vs. 26.5 [12.2] total insufficiencies with the IT, F = 5.2, p = 0.03) and with individual participant (F = 5.4, p = 0.004), but not with the sequential order of anastomosis (F = 2.6, p = 0.1). Total anastomosis time correlated positively with the total number of technical insufficiencies (RAll = 0.708, RCT = 0.758, RIT = 0.719, p < 0.05 for every group) and had a moderate negative correlation with the number of anastomosis attempts (RAll = −0.435, RIT = −0.473, RCT = −0.606, p < 0.05 for every group) [Figure 3].

Maintenance and readjustment

Maintenance and readjustments were encountered more frequently during transition subtasks (n = 181/472) than during CT throws (n = 4/226) or IT sutures (n = 29/197) and had a significant effect on subtask timing when adjusted to the sequential order of anastomosis (F = 1.1, p = 0.9) and on interparticipant variability (F = 16.0, p < 0.001). Adding water, grabbing a new suture, adjusting microscope position or focus, delays in getting an instrument, and adjusting pin position all significantly affected transition time [Table 4]. The ideal mean (SD) duration of the transition subtask without insufficiencies for the IT (n = 156) and CT (n = 234) was 26 (16) s and 20 (18) s, respectively. The absolute increase in transition time ranged from 16 s with a delay in getting an instrument to 30 s for microscope refocusing and 44 s when adding water [Table 4].

Knot-tying insufficiencies when using the IT

Specific insufficiencies associated with increased individual suture time during IT included repeated attempts to grasp a needle, repeated attempts to grasp a thread tail, chasing a thread tail, difficulty opening the recipient or donor vessel lip, restarting a knot tie, placing too many knots, letting go of a thread when inappropriate, the thread being pulled out, a tissue tear, restarting a bite, and the needle jumping out of the instrument [Table 5]. The ideal mean (SD) IT suture time with no insufficiencies was 48 (14) s (n = 30, range 26–86 s). The time cost of these insufficiencies ranged from 22 extra s for repeated tail-grab attempts to 3.5 extra min for searching for a needle that had jumped out of the instrument.

Thread-related insufficiencies when using the CT

CT throw placement efficiency was associated with insufficiencies such as difficulty opening the recipient and donor vessel lips and the thread being pulled out. DISCUSSION

Skill decomposition approach for microvascular anastomosis training

Despite numerous studies on microvascular anastomosis performance, no formal assessment has been conducted at the subtask level.[3,7,8,14] Microsurgical training parallels high-performance disciplines such as elite athletics, specialized military training, and professional musicianship, in which skills are broken into components, linked to memory, and refined through repetition [Figure 4].[4,5,16,20,23] This approach requires decomposing skills as input.[23] For example, baseball players refine their pitching and batting through focused practice on individual components of their skill set, such as their grip on the ball, stance, and batting mechanics.[5] Tennis players refine the nuances of their serve through isolated exercises that improve the consistency of its components, so that, when the parts are reassembled, this consistency carries over to the serve itself.[16] Swimmers perfect elements of the stroke by adjusting individual components such as kick angle, sole rotation, entry dive, and turns.[4,20] Accomplished pianists and cathedral organists similarly execute complex musical performances from memory by associating movements with “chunks” of the composition.[17]

An artist’s depiction of skill development in tennis illustrates the stepwise analytical skill decomposition approach. Elite tennis players refine the nuances of their serve (e.g., the task) by practicing isolated exercises to build consistency in subtasks (e.g., stance, swing mechanics, and muscle strengthening), then reassemble these subtasks into the serve to improve overall performance. Similarly, in microvascular anastomosis training, focusing on isolated subtask-level exercises (e.g., knot tying and needle passes) can improve the efficiency of individual maneuvers and minimize deficiencies. Moreover, skill decomposition is a powerful, general principle in artificial intelligence (AI) that has been successfully applied in machine learning.[23] Machine learning techniques are increasingly used to analyze surgical motions to improve surgical training, skills assessment, and outcome prediction. Gonzalez-Romo et al.[6] developed an AI-driven hand motion detector to assess microvascular anastomosis, and On et al.[15] used deep learning to analyze expert cerebrovascular surgeons’ hand movements during simulation. Likewise, convolutional neural networks can classify surgical skills by identifying patterns in neurosurgery trainees’ movements during robotic surgery.[10,22] Despite their promise, these models remain complex and somewhat opaque in their input-output interpretation. Simpler metrics, such as objective tracking and recording of specific observable actions, might yield actionable feedback and provide identifiable, labeled data for future machine learning.

Subtask-based microsurgery training allows practitioners to gradually develop precision, dexterity, and coordination in discrete subtasks, leading to more proficient performance of the complex task as a whole, such as microvascular anastomosis. Hafez et al.[8] described 5 phases of bypass creation: (1) Anchoring sutures (takeoff); (2) increasing speed (acceleration); (3) reaching maximal pace; (4) decelerating for assessment; and (5) completion (finish). Although microsurgical operating techniques are well described, there is a paucity of objective data that dissect microvascular suturing at the level of individual maneuvers, transitions, and observable insufficiencies, and that quantitatively link these elements to time and performance.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically and quantitatively evaluate microsuturing maneuvers and technical insufficiencies, determine their individual time contributions, and identify the most efficient ways to accomplish each subtask. Laboratory training is essential because many key factors that can affect the quality of an anastomosis are difficult to assess during surgery.[7] Coordination, speed, agility, flexibility, and reaction time are critical to successful suturing and require synchronization of the surgeon’s gross motor movements, fine motor skills, and mental acuity to achieve optimal brain–body–hand coordination.[8]

Comparison of IT and CT

IT and CT suturing are commonly used techniques for end-to-side microvascular anastomoses. Although neurosurgeons often prefer one technique over the other, studies have shown no major differences in outcomes, and only a few have compared these techniques in controlled settings.[13] Bot et al.[3] and Hafez et al.[7] compared the IT and CT in end-to-end and end-to-side anastomoses, respectively. Topalan et al.[21] and Levi et al.[12] studied the effects of vessel torsion, and Jung and Jeong[11] analyzed vessel twisting in a side-to-side bypass. However, earlier research has not focused on the quantitative evaluation of microsuturing at the subtask level to identify time-efficient maneuvers, optimal knot-tying maneuvers, technical errors, or common insufficiencies.

Our assessment of subtask variations identified knot-tying time for interrupted sutures as the primary factor affecting the efficiency of the CT relative to the IT. The CT shortens transition periods and overall completion time, making it a more time-efficient approach. Furthermore, the CT was associated with 40% fewer technical insufficiencies. Our analysis identified specific patterns of technical insufficiencies. IT suturing involved more knot-tying errors, including repeated suture tail grabbing, tail chasing, difficulty opening the vessel lip, and knot restarting, which were likely related to the higher number of knots required. CT suturing involved more thread-handling errors, including repeated attempts to grab the thread and more frequent thread tangling, which were attributed to manipulating a continuous thread. Although fewer total errors were associated with CT suturing, unique errors such as thread tangling or thread-tightening insufficiency can compromise the entire anastomosis line and require time-consuming repair. These findings support the value of partial task simulation, such as knot-tying exercises under high magnification, to improve proficiency in IT suturing by reducing knot-related errors.[9]

Value of technical nuances

Although they have a negligible impact in isolation, small technical nuances can cumulatively improve anastomotic efficiency. Biting both vessel walls in a single motion with the dominant hand and directing the needle toward the participant markedly shortened subtask completion time in our study. Therefore, to create operative conditions conducive to dominant-hand suturing toward the participant, vessels should be oriented before suturing, or the operator’s position should be adjusted ahead of time. An unexpected finding was the absence of significant differences in subtask completion times across various knot-tying and thread-cutting maneuvers, suggesting that these maneuvers have a limited effect on total time.

Anastomosis completion time showed a negative correlation with the number of attempts, confirming a learning curve consistent with findings from Mokhtari et al.[14] These results underscore the importance of repetitive practice in mastering bypass procedures. Many of the observed results align with established microsurgical teachings and the concept of movement economy. This study bridges a fundamental gap by translating tacit expert opinion into measurable, reproducible performance elements for objective skill assessment, structured feedback, and future integration with AI-based video evaluation tools.

Technical insufficiencies

Identifying common errors can help trainees and educators target weaknesses during practice. This study found differences in the frequency of specific insufficiencies, including tissue tear, suture loss, biting too deeply, and an uneven bite. Specific insufficiencies observed include “grabbing” and “chasing.” Grabbing refers to repetitive, unsuccessful attempts to squeeze the forceps to grasp a thread end (i.e., suture tail) with the instrument. This insufficiency can be attributable to poor instrument quality or an inadequate forceps position relative to the tail, causing the tail to escape from the forceps tip. Chasing refers to bringing the forceps into position to hold or grab the suture end. In this situation, if the suture tail is located inconveniently, time is wasted moving the forceps into position to grab a thread. The chasing problem is usually a planning mistake. The tail is left too long, so it is out of the field of view, or it is tied during a previous maneuver in a direction that is not conducive to a subsequent progressive maneuver. Grabbing and chasing can become intertwined, forming a recurring cycle.

Variations in technical insufficiencies among the participants can be explained by individual differences in suturing proficiency. Variations were observed in the sum of “thread or suture handling” and “overall performance” insufficiencies for the IT and the CT, both individually and combined. Participants with more experience in a particular suturing technique (IT) demonstrated greater control and fewer mistakes during knot tying and thread management. The strong effects of the total number of insufficiencies and total suturing time at the anastomosis and subtask levels highlight the importance of mistake avoidance. Too often, neurosurgery trainees are sent to the laboratory to practice microsurgical procedures, performing an entire procedure without adequately attending to its constituent parts or subtasks. They and their supervisors might believe that practicing the entire procedure is the best way to increase proficiency. However, this is not how successful elite athletes or highly specialized military forces train. Technical insufficiencies often consume time, as demonstrated by specific instances in which a particular insufficiency prolonged the associated subtask. Conversely, prolonged suturing time can lead to fatigue, decreased concentration, and increased susceptibility to mistakes.

Study limitations

The small sample size limited statistical power, yet significant findings underscore the value of technical details in anastomosis training. All participants worked under standardized conditions (vessel size, arteriotomy, suture instructions), reducing procedural bias. Other unobserved errors or maneuvers may exist, and findings may not fully generalize to all trainees. A key limitation is reliance on detailed video-based assessment by an educated reviewer and an experienced fellow, without independent external adjudication or formal interrater reliability testing, which could introduce bias; however, a uniform, objective, second-by-second, structured assessment minimized this risk. Multiple reviewers are unnecessary because we have not used any subjective scales, such as the Northwestern Objective Microanastomosis Assessment Tool,[1] but instead used objective, measurable events. Importantly, this study did not aim to establish the definitive superiority of one suturing technique over another, but rather to demonstrate the feasibility of a granular, subtask-level analytical framework for microsurgical training. Unlike a method that uses instrument tracking to record tremor and movement fluidity and “black box” machine learning for evaluation,[6] our approach identifies observable, actionable subtasks and records variations and deficiencies in their performance that can be emphasized in training. Moreover, it produces a large library of labeled video data that could be used for machine learning video analysis.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates the feasibility of a novel skill-decomposition, video-based framework for analyzing microsurgical anastomosis at the subtask level and identifies optimal maneuvers and observable insufficiencies for 2 anastomosis techniques. Such an approach could help future trainees improve microsurgery skills (e.g., bypass techniques) and serve as the basis for subtask-oriented exercises in evidence-based microvascular anastomosis training. Across studies of microsurgical proficiency, the old adage “practice makes perfect” remains relevant. As with elite athletes, neurosurgeons require many hours of deliberate practice to acquire and master neurosurgical skills, especially microneurosurgical techniques. Attention to the specific parts of a procedure that require improvement or are most critical to a successful outcome is key to more efficient and effective training.
