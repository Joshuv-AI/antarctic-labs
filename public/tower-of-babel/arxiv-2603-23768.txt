CAT ( 0 ) (0) spaces quasi-isometric to Euclidean spaces
Nicola Cavallucci, Andrea Sambusetti

Abstract
. We show that if a proper, geodesically complete, CAT ​ ( 0 ) \textup{CAT}(0) homology manifold is quasi-isometric to the Euclidean space ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} then it is homeomorphic to ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} . On the other hand, we show that there exist proper, geodesically complete, CAT ​ ( 0 ) \textup{CAT}(0) spaces quasi-isometric to ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , which are not homeomorphic to it. We prove that our example is sharp in a suitable sense. Finally, we provide an example of a sequence of proper, geodesically complete, CAT ​ ( 0 ) \textup{CAT}(0) spaces that are not homology manifolds and that converge in the Gromov-Hausdorff sense to a topological manifold: this shows that the set of topological manifolds is not open in the class of proper, geodesically complete, CAT ​ ( 0 ) \textup{CAT}(0) spaces.

1.
Introduction

Given
L\geq 1
and
C\geq 0
, two metric spaces
({\rm X},{\sf d}_{\rm X}),({\rm Y},{\sf d}_{\rm Y})
are
(L,C)
-quasi-isometric if there exists a not necessarily continuous function
f\colon{\rm X}\to{\rm Y}
such that:

(i)

\frac{1}{L}{\sf d}_{\rm X}(x,x^{\prime})-C\leq{\sf d}_{\rm Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))\leq L{\sf d}_{\rm X}(x,x^{\prime})+C
for every
x,x^{\prime}\in{\rm X}
;

(ii)

for every
y\in{\rm Y}
there exists
x\in{\rm X}
such that
{\sf d}_{\rm Y}(y,f(x))\leq C
.

Two metric spaces are
quasi-isometric
if they are
(L,C)
-quasi-isometric for some
L\geq 1,C\geq 0
.
They are
L
-biLipschitz homeomorphic
if the same holds for
C=0
.

In this paper we address the following:

Question 1
.

If
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space which is
(L,C)
-quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then is it homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
?

We notice that the constant
C
in the definition of quasi-isometry plays no role in Question
1
.Indeed, for every proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space
({\rm X},{\sf d})
which is
(L,C)
-quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for every
c>0
, a suitable rescaling
({\rm X},\lambda{\sf d})
is
(L,c)
-quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
On the other hand, we will see that the value of the constant
L
matters.

A first, positive answer to Question
1
under the additional assumption that
({\rm X},{\sf d})
has a discrete, cocompact group of isometries is essentially known because of
[
1
, Corollary C]
. In fact, applying an unpublished result in
[
9
]
, the following result holds as soon as a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space has the full isometry group which is cocompact.

Theorem A
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space such that
\textup{Isom}({\rm X},{\sf d})
acts cocompactly on
{\rm X}
. If
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In Section
4
we will provide a short, self-contained proof of this fact.

One of the first results of this paper answers Question
1
in the affirmative for every space which is a homology manifold.

Theorem B
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper,
\textup{CAT}(0)
homology manifold. If
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
{\rm X}
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We stress the fact that the statement of Theorem
B
is definitely not trivial, since
the class of proper,
\textup{CAT}(0)
homology manifolds contains many spaces with topological and metric properties extremely different to the ones of Riemannian manifolds of non-positive curvature. For instance, for every
n\geq 5
there are
\textup{CAT}(0)
topological
n
-manifolds that are not simply connected at infinity, hence not homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, see
[
17
, Theorem 5b.1]
. On the other hand, it has been proved in
[
24
, Theorem 1.1]
that every
\textup{CAT}(0)
topological
n
-manifold with
n\leq 4
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This is no longer true for
\textup{CAT}(0)
homology
4
-manifolds: for that, it is enough to consider the Euclidean cone over the Poincaré homology sphere. A more detailed description and more examples of
\textup{CAT}(0)
homology and topological manifolds are presented in Section
2.5
.

A general, positive answer to Question
1
, without any cocompacity or topological assumption is the following statement,
which is essentially contained in
[
32
]
, and which deal with the case when
L
is small enough, see again Section
4
.

Theorem C
.

Given
\varepsilon\!>\!0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
\delta\!=\!\delta(\varepsilon,n)\!>\!0
such that the following holds.
Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space.

(i)

If
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is
(1+\delta,C)
-quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
({\rm X},{\sf d})

is
(1+\varepsilon)
-biLipschitz homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(ii)

Moreover, if
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is
(1,C)
-quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

A sharper, general affirmative answer to Question
1
is provided by the following result, that highlights again the crucial role of the multiplicative constant
L
of the quasi-isometry.

Theorem D
.

If
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space which is
(L,C)
-quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
L<2^{1/n}
, then
{\rm X}
is
homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We stress that Theorem
B
is a fundamental step for Theorem
D
, as we will explain in
1.1
.

Let us briefly explain how the constant
2^{1/n}
appears here.
In Section
2.6
, we will prove that if a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is
(L,C)
-quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then the Euclidean cone over the Tits boundary
\partial_{T}{\rm X}
is
L
-biLipschitz homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This leads to the inequality

(1)

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial_{T}{\rm X})\leq L^{n}\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1}).

Then, the reason for the constant
2^{1/n}
appearing in Theorem
D
lies in the following deep result, of which Theorem
D
is a direct consequence.

Theorem E
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space which is quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial_{T}{\rm X})<2\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
then
{\rm X}
is
homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The last main result of our paper is to show that Question
1
has a
negative
answer in general,
and that Theorem
E
is sharp.

Theorem F
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
there exists a proper, geodesically complete, purely
n
-dimensional,
\textup{CAT}(0)
simplicial complex
({\rm X}_{k},{\sf d}_{k})
which is
(2,\frac{1}{k})
-quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, but is not a homology manifold. Moreover,
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial_{T}{\rm X}_{k})=2\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
for every
k
.

Finally, we point out that the sequence of spaces
({\rm X}_{k},{\sf d}_{k})
of Theorem
F
converge in the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff sense to a proper,
\textup{CAT}(0)
topological manifold. This shows the following result.

Corollary G
.

The class of proper,
\textup{CAT}(0)
homology (or topological) manifolds is not an open subset of the class of all proper, geodesically complete
\textup{CAT}(0)
spaces, with respect to the Gromov-Hausdorff topology.

1.1.
Outline of the proofs

Our main contribution is in the proof of Theorems
B
,
D
,
E
,
F
and Corollary
G
; here, we will focus on Theorem
B
and
E
to explain the main ideas in the paper.

The proof of Theorem
B
is based on a combination of topological and geometric arguments.
Because of classical topological results, Theorem
B
is equivalent to the following statement:
every proper
\textup{CAT}(0)
homology manifold
{\rm X}
which is quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a topological
n
-manifold which is simply connected at infinity
(see
[
37
]
for
n\geq 5
,
[
19
]
for
n=4
, and
[
13
]
for
n=3
).
So, we have to show that
{\rm X}
is a topological
n
-manifold and is simply connected at infinity.

A deep theorem of Lytchak-Nagano (cp.
[
26
, Theorem 1.1 and Proposition 2.3]
) says that a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space is a topological
n
-manifold if and only if for every
x\in{\rm X}
, every small enough metric sphere
S_{r}(x)
is homotopy equivalent to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.

On the other hand, as
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we deduce that it has cone at infinity
C_{\infty}{\rm X}

bi-Lipschitz homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see Proposition
2.21
).
Moreover,
C_{\infty}{\rm X}
is isometric to the Euclidean cone
\textup{Cone}(\partial_{T}{\rm X})
over the Tits boundary
\partial_{T}{\rm X}
of
{\rm X}
(
[
32
, Proposition 3.4]
),
and the latter is
homotopy equivalent to the standard sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, see again Proposition
2.21
.
Then, a result of Nagano (
[
32
, Theorem 4.6]
) implies that, for every fixed
x\in{\rm X}
and every radius
R
big enough, the metric sphere
S_{R}(x)
is homotopy equivalent to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
; in particular
S_{R}(x)
is a simply connected, homology
(n-1)
-sphere.

Therefore, large metric spheres around every point of
{\rm X}
are homotopy equivalent to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, while we would like to have that very small metric spheres are homotopy equivalent to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. The next step is to relate the homology of large metric spheres to the homology of small ones, and this is where the assumption of having a homology manifold plays a role. In order to do that, we use the natural contraction map from large balls to small balls. Using an argument of Thurston (
[
38
, Corollary 2.10]
) we can prove that the contraction maps have acyclic fibers. This is enough to apply Vietoris-Begle’s Theorem as in Davis-Januszkiewicz
[
17
]
to conclude that small metric spheres have the same homology of large metric spheres, and so they are homology spheres. Moreover, the same construction gives that small metric spheres are simply connected. But a simply connected homology sphere is homotopy equivalent to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, which concludes the proof of the fact that
{\rm X}
is a topological manifold.

The simply connectedness at infinity of
{\rm X}
essentially comes from the same argument, since large metric spheres are simply connected.

We remark that the above argument cannot be applied to a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space which is not a homology manifold. Indeed, the spaces constructed in Theorem
F
have points
x_{k}\in{\rm X}_{k}
and radii
0<r<R
with the following properties:

•

one fiber of the contraction map
\phi^{R}_{r}\colon B_{R}(x_{k})\to B_{r}(x_{k})
centered at
x_{k}
is not acyclic, actually it is not connected;

•

the metric sphere
S_{R}(x_{k})
is homotopy equivalent to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, while
S_{r}(x_{k})
is not;

•

there exists no continuous map
\sigma\colon B_{r}(x_{k})\to B_{R}(x_{k})
such that
\phi^{R}_{r}\circ\sigma=\text{id}
, i.e. there is no way to extend continuously geodesics.

On the other hand, the proof of Theorem
E
is based on a combination topological, geometric and analytical arguments. As discussed above, a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space which is quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
has a cone at infinity isometric to the Euclidean cone over the Tits boundary
\partial_{T}{\rm X}
, and
\partial_{T}{\rm X}
is a
(n-1)
-homology manifold homotopy equivalent to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Our goal is to show that
{\rm X}
is a homology
n
-manifold. By a characterization proved in
[
26
]
, see Proposition
2.16
, it is enough to show that each space of direction
\Sigma_{x}{\rm X}
,
x\in{\rm X}
, has the same homology of
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. For every
x\in{\rm X}
we have the natural
1
-Lipschitz surjective map
\partial\log_{x}\colon\partial_{T}{\rm X}\to\Sigma_{x}{\rm X}
that sends a point at infinity
[\mathfrak{r}]
to the starting direction of the unique geodesic ray issuing at
x
and pointing towards
[\mathfrak{r}]
. The conclusion then follows by the next result, applied to
{\rm Y}=\partial_{T}{\rm X}
,
{\rm Y}^{\prime}=\Sigma_{x}{\rm X}
and
f=\partial\log_{x}
.

Theorem H
.

Let
f\colon({\rm Y},{\sf d})\to({\rm Y}^{\prime},{\sf d}^{\prime})
be a
1
-Lipschitz, surjective map between compact, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(1)
spaces of dimension
n
.
Assume that
{\rm Y}
is a homology manifold homotopy equivalent to
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, with
\mathcal{H}^{n}({\rm Y})<2\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
: then,
{\rm Y}^{\prime}
is also a homology
n
-manifold which is homotopy equivalent to
\mathbb{S}^{n}
.

The proof of Theorem
H
is by induction on the maximal spherical factor that
{\rm Y}^{\prime}
splits in its unique join decomposition, see
[
27
, Corollary 1.2]
. Again, to prove that
{\rm Y}^{\prime}
is a homology manifold it is enough to show that
\Sigma_{y^{\prime}}{\rm Y}^{\prime}
has the same homology of
\mathbb{S}^{n}
for every
y^{\prime}\in{\rm Y}^{\prime}
.

For this, we define a natural
1
-Lipschitz, surjective map
g_{y^{\prime}}\colon{\rm Y}^{\prime}\to\Sigma_{y^{\prime}}{\rm Y}^{\prime}\ast\mathbb{S}^{0}
in order to use the induction hypothesis on the map
g_{y^{\prime}}\circ f\colon{\rm Y}\to\Sigma_{y^{\prime}}{\rm Y}^{\prime}\ast\mathbb{S}^{0}
, and conclude that
{\rm Y}^{\prime}
is a homology manifold.
The control of the homotopy type
of
{\rm Y}^{\prime}
is more delicate.
As recalled before, it is enough to prove that
{\rm Y}^{\prime}
is a simply connected homology sphere. In order to do this, we relate the topological degree between oriented homology manifolds to the Jacobian of maps between rectifiable metric spaces, and then use the area formula to infer that
{\rm Y}^{\prime}
is simply connected
and orientable. Moreover, the map
f
satisfies

2\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathbb{S}^{n})>\mathcal{H}^{n}({\rm Y})\geq|\textup{deg}(f)|\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n}({\rm Y}^{\prime})\geq\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathbb{S}^{n}),

(where the last inequality is a standard property of geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(1)
spaces), so applying again the area formula yields that
f
must have degree one. Now, the fact that
{\rm Y}^{\prime}
has the same homology of
\mathbb{S}^{n}
follows by Poincaré duality.

1.2.
Relation to the literature

The proof of Theorem
B
uses crucially the deep analysis of
[
38
]
. Recently, the same ideas have been used in
[
24
]
to prove that
\textup{CAT}(0)
topological
4
-manifolds are homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. We also use intensively some ideas of
[
26
]
, especially when we need to improve infinitesimal topological properties to global ones.

Theorem
E

might be compared with
[
32
, Theorem 1.2]
where (building on
[
25
]
) it is proved that if a proper, geodesically complete CAT
(0)
-space
X
satisfies

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial_{T}X)<\tfrac{3}{2}\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})

then
X
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
provided that

({\rm X},{\sf d})
is purely
n
-dimensional. On the other hand, in our statement, besides the better constant
2
, pure
n
-dimensionality is part of the conclusion of the theorem.
The proof of this seemingly innocent conclusion requires the assumption on the volume of the Tits boundary: we do not know if the same is true
for every proper, geodesically complete CAT
(0)
space which is quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, cp. Remark
2.22
.

Theorem
E
also shares similarities with
[
33
, Theorem 1.2]
, where it is proved that there exists
c>3/2
such that any
\textup{CAT}(1)
homology
n
-manifold
({\rm Y},{\sf d})
satisfying
\mathcal{H}^{n}({\rm Y})\leq c\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. In
[
33
, Problem 1.1]
it is asked for the optimal value for
c
.

Remark 1.1
.

The following example shows that the optimal constant
c
is smaller than
2
.

Let
2\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
be the real projective plane equipped with the metric induced by
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, multiplied by a factor of
2
, which is a compact
\textup{CAT}(1)
space. For every
n\geq 3
, consider the spherical join
{\rm Y}:=\mathbb{S}^{n-3}\ast 2\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Then,
{\rm Y}
is a compact,
\textup{CAT}(1)
topological
n
-manifold which is not homeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, but satisfying

\mathcal{H}^{n}({\rm Y})=\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\cos\vartheta)^{n}(\sin\vartheta)^{2}dv_{\mathbb{S}^{n-3}}\,dv_{2\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}}\,d\vartheta=2\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathbb{S}^{n-3}\ast\mathbb{S}^{2})=2\,\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathbb{S}^{n})

(as
dv_{2\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}}\!=\!4\!\cdot\!dv_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
and integrating on
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
is equivalent to integrating on a hemisphere of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
).

This example, together with Theorem
E
, suggests that
c=2
might indeed be the right threshold for the validity of the statement above. However, the techniques we develop here do not yet give an affirmative solution to this conjecture.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout this paper, we will denote the standard
n
-dimensional Euclidean space by
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the round
n
-sphere by
\mathbb{S}^{n}
and the
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure by
\mathcal{H}^{n}
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a metric space. The open (resp. closed) ball of center
x\in{\rm X}
and radius
r>0
is denoted by
B_{r}(x)
(resp.
\overline{B}_{r}(x)
). The metric sphere around
x\in{\rm X}
of radius
r>0
is denoted by
S_{r}(x)
. Given
{\rm Y}\subseteq{\rm X}
and
r>0
, we denote by
\textup{Cov}({\rm Y},r)\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
the minimal number of balls of radius
r
needed to cover
{\rm Y}
. The metric space is said to be
metrically doubling
if there exists
C_{D}\geq 1
such that
\textup{Cov}(B_{2r}(x),r)\leq C_{D}
for every
x\in{\rm X}
and every
r>0
.
Finally, we will write
({\rm X},{\sf d})\cong({\rm X}^{\prime},{\sf d}^{\prime})
for two isometric metric spaces.

A
geodesic segment
is an isometric embedding
\mathfrak{g}\colon[a,b]\to{\rm X}
for some compact interval
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
.
A geodesic
ray
is an isometric embedding
\mathfrak{r}\colon[0,\infty)\to{\rm X}
, and a geodesic
line
is an isometric embedding
\mathfrak{g}\colon\mathbb{R}\to{\rm X}
. A
reparametrized
geodesic is the composition of a geodesic
\mathfrak{g}
with a monotone reparametrization of
[a,b]
.
A
local
geodesic is a map
\mathfrak{g}\colon[a,b]\to{\rm X}
such that for every
t\in[a,b]
there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that the restriction of
\mathfrak{g}
to
[a,b]\cap(t-\varepsilon,t+\varepsilon)
is a geodesic segment.

A metric space
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is
geodesically complete
if every local geodesic can be extended, i.e. if for every local geodesic
\mathfrak{g}\colon[a,b]\to{\rm X}

there exists
\varepsilon>0
and a local geodesic
\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}\colon[a-\varepsilon,b+\varepsilon]\to{\rm X}
such that the restriction of
\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}
to
[a,b]
coincides with
\mathfrak{g}
.
A metric space
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is
R
-geodesic
, for
R\in[0,+\infty]
, if for every
x,y\in{\rm X}
with
{\sf d}(x,y)<R
there exists a geodesic segment joining the two. If
R=\infty
we say that
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is geodesic.

The
Euclidean cone over
{\rm X}
is the space
\textup{Cone}({\rm X})=[0,+\infty)\times{\rm X}
modulo the equivalence relation that identifies all the points
(0,x)
for every
x\in{\rm X}
, equipped with the metric

(2)

{\sf d}^{2}((t,x),(t^{\prime},x^{\prime}))=t^{2}+t^{\prime 2}-2tt^{\prime}\cos(\min\{\pi,{\sf d}_{\rm X}(x,x^{\prime})\}).

The
section
of the Euclidean cone
\textup{Cone}({\rm X})
is the metric space
({\rm X},\min\{{\sf d}_{\rm X},\pi\})
, while its
vertex

o
is the equivalence class of the points
(0,x)
,
x\in{\rm X}
.

We will assume in the following that the reader is familiar with the concepts of Gromov-Hausdorff convergence and ultralimits. Among the many references in the literature we mainly refer to
[
16
, Section 3]
, where precise definitions are given and a comparison between the two convergences is made. A similar discussion, which does not take into account group actions, can be found in
[
22
]
(unpublished).

2.1.
Geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(\kappa)
spaces

Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and let
D_{\kappa}:=\pi/\sqrt{\kappa}
. For basics of
\textup{CAT}(\kappa)
spaces we refer to
[
12
]
. Here, we will recall some properties of proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(\kappa)
spaces. The main reference for this part is
[
25
]
. We recall that a
\textup{CAT}(\kappa)
space is
D_{\kappa}
-geodesic.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(\kappa)
space. For every
x\in{\rm X}
there exists an integer
n_{x}\in\mathbb{N}
such that the Hausdorff dimension of every small enough ball
B_{r}(x)
is
n_{x}
. The integer
n_{x}
is called the
dimension of
{\rm X}
at
x
and it is denoted by
\dim(x;{\rm X})
. The dimension of
{\rm X}
is correspondingly defined as

\dim({\rm X}):=\sup_{x\in{\rm X}}\dim(x;{\rm X}).

The space
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is said to be
pure-dimensional
if
\dim(x;{\rm X})=\dim({\rm X})
for every
x\in{\rm X}
. It is said to be purely
n
-dimensional if it is pure-dimensional and
\dim({\rm X})=n
.

Let
x\in{\rm X}
. The
space of directions
at
x
is

\Sigma_{x}{\rm X}:=\{\mathfrak{g}\colon[0,\varepsilon]\to{\rm X}\,:\,\mathfrak{g}\text{ geodesic with }\mathfrak{g}(0)=x\}/\sim,

where
\mathfrak{g}\sim\mathfrak{g^{\prime}}
if and only if
\angle_{x}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}^{\prime})=0
; for the definition of the
Alexandrov angle
between two geodesics, we refer to
[
12
]
. The space
\Sigma_{x}{\rm X}
equipped with the distance
\angle_{x}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a compact,
\textup{CAT}(1)
space of diameter
\pi
. The Euclidean cone over the space of directions, namely
T_{x}{\rm X}:=\textup{Cone}(\Sigma_{x}{\rm X})
is a proper
\textup{CAT}(0)
space which is called the
tangent space
at
x
. It is the Gromov-Hausdorff limit of every sequence of pointed metric spaces
({\rm X},\lambda_{i}{\sf d},x)
with
\lambda_{i}\to\infty
.
A deep result of
[
25
]
says that the space of directions is homotopy equivalent to small enough metric spheres:

Proposition 2.1
.

[
25
, Theorem 1.12]

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(\kappa)
space. For every
x\in{\rm X}
there exists
r_{x}>0
such that
\Sigma_{x}{\rm X}
is homotopy equivalent to
S_{r}(x)
for every
0<r<r_{x}
.

Throughout the paper we will be mainly interested to the case
\kappa=0
. However, we will also need to consider
\textup{CAT}(1)
spaces like the spaces of directions and the Tits boundary of a
\textup{CAT}(0)
-space. Regarding the special case
\kappa=0
, we recall that in every geodesically complete CAT
(0)
space
{\rm X}
, for every
x\in{\rm X}
and
0<r\leq R
, there exists a well-defined
contraction map
:

(3)

\phi^{R,r}_{x}:\overline{B}_{R}(x)\to\overline{B}_{r}(x)

obtained by sending a point
y\in\overline{B}_{R}(x)
to the unique point
y^{\prime}
along the geodesic
[x,y]
satisfying
d(x,y^{\prime})=\frac{r}{R}d(x,y)
; we will use the notation
\varphi^{R,r}_{x}
for the restriction of the above map to the sphere
S_{R}(x)
.
By the geodesic completeness of
{\rm X}
and the
\textup{CAT}(0)
assumption, the map

is surjective and
\frac{r}{R}
-Lipschitz, see

or

. This implies that the

\log_{x}:{\rm X}\to\Sigma_{x}{\rm X}

sending any

to the initial direction of the geodesic

, is
1
-Lipschitz and surjective, cp.
[

, Lemma 2.3]
.

2.2.
Tits boundary and asymptotic cone

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space. Two geodesic rays
\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}

are said to be equivalent if
\sup_{t\geq 0}{\sf d}(\mathfrak{r}(t),\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}(t))<\infty
. This is an equivalence relation on the set of geodesic rays. The resulting space is called the
ideal boundary
of
{\rm X}
and it is denoted by
\partial{\rm X}
. For every
x\in{\rm X}
and every geodesic ray
\mathfrak{r}
, there exists a unique geodesic ray
\mathfrak{r}_{x}
which is asymptotic to
\mathfrak{r}
and such that
\mathfrak{r}_{x}(0)=x
(see
[
12
, Proposition II.8.2]
). Given two elements
[\mathfrak{r}],[\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}]\in\partial{\rm X}
and
x\in{\rm X}
we define

\angle([\mathfrak{r}],\mathfrak{[r^{\prime}]}):=\sup_{x\in{\rm X}}\angle_{x}(\mathfrak{r}_{x},\mathfrak{r}_{x}^{\prime}).

This defines a distance on
\partial{\rm X}
, called
angular metric
. The Tits boundary of
{\rm X}
is the set
\partial{\rm X}
equipped with the length metric induced by the angular metric
\angle(\cdot,\cdot)
and it is denoted by
\partial_{T}{\rm X}
. It is a
\textup{CAT}(1)
space, see
[
12
, Theorem II.9.20]
).

For every
x\in{\rm X}
, the map

(4)

\partial\log_{x}\colon\partial_{T}{\rm X}\to\Sigma_{x}{\rm X}

assigning to each
[\mathfrak{r}]\in\partial_{T}{\rm X}
the direction of the geodesic ray
\mathfrak{r}_{x}
at
x
is
1
-Lipschitz, since

{\sf d}_{T}([\mathfrak{r}],[\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}])\geq\angle([\mathfrak{r}],[\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}])\geq\angle_{x}(\mathfrak{r}_{x},\mathfrak{r}_{x}^{\prime})=\angle_{x}(\partial\log_{x}([\mathfrak{r}]),\partial\log_{x}([\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}])).

and surjective because of geodesic completeness.

Following
[
32
]
, we will say that
{\rm X}

admits the cone at infinity
if there exists a pointed metric space
(C_{\infty}{\rm X},{\sf d}_{\infty},x_{\infty})
such that for every sequence
\lambda_{i}\to 0
and every
x\in{\rm X}
, the sequence of pointed metric spaces
({\rm X},\lambda_{i}{\sf d},x)
converges in the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff sense to
(C_{\infty}{\rm X},{\sf d}_{\infty},x_{\infty})
; then,
C_{\infty}{\rm X}
is called the
cone at infinity
of
{\rm X}
.
Notice that if the convergence is true for every sequence
\lambda_{i}
going to
0
and some fixed point
x\in{\rm X}
, then it is also true for every sequence and every other point of
{\rm X}
. Moreover, if
C_{\infty}{\rm X}
exists, then it is proper, by definition of pointed Gromov-Hausdorff convergence; so, if it exists, it is a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space.

The existence of the cone at infinity is equivalent to several natural asymptotic conditions and bounds on the growth of
{\rm X}
, as we now recall.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space. The following properties are equivalent:

(i)

({\rm X},{\sf d})
admits the cone at infinity;

(ii)

\omega
-
\lim({\rm X},\lambda_{i}{\sf d},x)
is proper for some
x\in{\rm X}
, some sequence
\lambda_{i}\to 0
and some non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
;

(iii)

the Tits boundary
\partial_{T}{\rm X}
is compact;

(iv)

({\rm X},{\sf d})
is metrically doubling.

If any of these conditions is satisfied, then
\omega
-
\lim({\rm X},\lambda_{i}{\sf d},x)
is isometric to
(C_{\infty}{\rm X},{\sf d}_{\infty},x_{\infty})
for every choice of
x\in{\rm X}
,
\lambda_{i}\to 0
and non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
; moreover,
C_{\infty}{\rm X}
is isometric to the Euclidean cone
\textup{Cone}(\partial_{T}{\rm X})
over
\partial_{T}{\rm X}
, and
\dim(C_{\infty}{\rm X})=\dim({\rm X})
.

Furthermore, if
{\rm X}
is purely
n
-dimensional, conditions (i)-(iv) are also equivalent to:

(v)

the
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{n}
is doubling on
({\rm X},{\sf d})
.

The fact that
C_{\infty}{\rm X}
is isometric to
\textup{Cone}(\partial_{T}{\rm X})
justifies the name
cone at infinity
.

Proof.

Recalling the relation between ultralimits and Gromov-Hausdorff convergence proved in
[
16
, Section 3]
, the equivalences (i)-(ii)-(iii) simply follow from
[
32
, Proposition 3.4]
. The fourth equivalence is also proved in
[
32
, Proposition 3.7]
, under the assumption that
({\rm X},{\sf d})
has pure dimension, which is unnecessary. Actually, if
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is metrically doubling then
\omega
-
\lim({\rm X},\lambda_{i}{\sf d},x)
is proper for every
x\in{\rm X}
, every
\lambda_{i}\to 0
and every non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
, by
[
16
, Proposition 3.13]
, because it satisfies the assumptions of Gromov’s precompactness Theorem. Now, suppose that
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is not metrically doubling. Therefore, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a point
x_{n}\in{\rm X}
and a radius
r_{n}>0
such that
\textup{Cov}(B_{2r_{n}}(x_{n}),r_{n})>n
. We fix
x\in{\rm X}
. By
[
15
, Proof of Lemma 4.5]
we can suppose without loss of generality that
r_{n}\geq 1
, so that
{\sf d}(x_{n},x)=:d_{n}\to\infty
. We set
\lambda_{n}:=d_{n}^{-1}
and we observe that
\textup{Cov}(B_{2d_{n}}(x_{n}),d_{n})>n
again by
[
15
, Proof of Lemma 4.5]
. It is now easy to see that the space
\omega
-
\lim({\rm X},\lambda_{n}{\sf d},x)
is not proper.

If the conditions (i)-(iv) hold then
C_{\infty}{\rm X}=\textup{Cone}(\partial_{T}{\rm X})
by
[
32
, Proposition 3.4]
.
The equality
\textup{dim}(C_{\infty}{\rm X})=\textup{dim}({\rm X})
comes from
[
15
, Proposition 6.5]
noticing that
\textup{dim}({\rm X})
is finite since
{\rm X}
is metrically doubling, by
[
15
, Theorem 4.9]
.

Finally, suppose that
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is purely
n
-dimensional. Then
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is metrically doubling if and only if
\mathcal{H}^{n}
is doubling by
[
15
, Corollary 5.5]
.
∎

2.3.
\textup{CAT}(1)
spherical joins

Let
({\rm Y},{\sf d})
,
({\rm Y}^{\prime},{\sf d}^{\prime})
be two metric spaces. The
spherical join

{\rm Y}\ast{\rm Y}^{\prime}
is the set
[0,\pi/2]\times{\rm Y}\times{\rm Y}^{\prime}
modulo the equivalence relation which identifies
(\vartheta,y,y^{\prime})
and
(\varphi,z,z^{\prime})
if (
\vartheta=\varphi=0
and
y=z
) or (
\vartheta=\varphi=\pi/2
and
y^{\prime}=z^{\prime}
). The equivalence class of
(\vartheta,y,y^{\prime})
will be denoted by
y\cos\vartheta+y^{\prime}\sin\vartheta
and we identify
Y,Y^{\prime}
to the subsets of
{\rm Y}\ast{\rm Y}^{\prime}
corresponding respectively to the classes of
[0,y,\cdot]
and
[\frac{\pi}{2},\cdot,y^{\prime}]
.
We equip
{\rm Y}\ast{\rm Y}^{\prime}
with the metric
{\sf d}_{\ast}
which is at most
\pi
and that satisfies, for given
x_{1}=y\cos\vartheta+y^{\prime}\sin\vartheta
,
x_{2}=z\cos\varphi+z^{\prime}\sin\varphi
,

\cos({\sf d}_{\ast}(x_{1},x_{2}))=\cos\vartheta\cos\varphi\cos(\min\{\pi,{\sf d}_{\rm Y}(y,z)\})+\sin\vartheta\sin\varphi\cos(\min\{\pi,{\sf d}_{{\rm Y}^{\prime}}(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\}).

Spherical joins are related to Euclidean cones in the following way.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
12
, Proposition I.5.15]
)
.

Let
({\rm Y},{\sf d}_{\rm Y}),({\rm Y}^{\prime},{\sf d}_{{\rm Y}^{\prime}})
be two metric spaces.

Then
\textup{Cone}({\rm Y}\ast{\rm Y}^{\prime})\cong\textup{Cone}({\rm Y})\times\textup{Cone}({\rm Y}^{\prime})
.

In particular, we have
\mathbb{S}^{n}\ast\mathbb{S}^{m}\cong\mathbb{S}^{n+m+1}
for every
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
.

In the sequel we will need some basic facts about the geometry of spherical joins and Euclidean cones.
Recall that a Euclidean cone is
\textup{CAT}(0)
if and only if its section is
\textup{CAT}(1)
, see
[
12
, Proposition II.3.14]
, and it is geodesically complete if and only if its section is geodesically complete by
[
12
, Proposition I.5.10]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
{\rm X}=\textup{Cone}({\rm Y})
be the Euclidean cone over a
\textup{CAT}(1)
space
({\rm Y},{\sf d}_{\rm Y})
. For every
y\in{\rm Y}
we have
T_{(1,y)}{\rm X}\cong\mathbb{R}\times T_{y}{\rm Y}
and
\Sigma_{(1,y)}{\rm X}\cong\mathbb{S}^{0}\ast\Sigma_{y}{\rm Y}
.

Proof.

First,
T_{(1,y)}\textup{Cone}({\rm Y})=T_{(1,y)}\left(\mathbb{R}\times\!{\rm Y}\right)
, for
\mathbb{R}\times\!{\rm Y}
endowed with the product metric
{\sf d}_{\times}
.

In fact, any geodesic from
x=(1,y)
in
\textup{Cone}({\rm Y})\!\setminus\!\{o\}
or in
\mathbb{R}\times\!{\rm Y}
can be reparameterized as

(t(s),y(s))
where
y(s)
is a geodesic on
{\rm Y}
(cp.
[
12
, Prop. I.5.3 and I.5.10]
) and
t(0)=1
.
Now, for any germ of curve
\gamma(s)=(t(s),y(s))
we have, with respect to the cone metric,

{\sf d}(\gamma(s),x)=1+t(s)^{2}-2t(s)\cos{\sf d}_{\rm Y}(y(s),y)=1+t(s)^{2}-2t(s)+t(s){\sf d}_{\rm Y}(y(s),y)^{2}+t(s)O(s^{4})

which, for
s\to 0
, behaves like

{\sf d}_{\times}(\gamma(s),x)=(t(s)-1)^{2}+{\sf d}_{\rm Y}(y(s),y)^{2}
,
by definition of product metric
{\sf d}_{\times}
.
Then, for any two such curves
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
from
x
, their (upper) Alexandrov angle

\angle_{x}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
is the same when measured in
({\rm X},{\sf d})
or in
(\mathbb{R}\times{\rm Y},{\sf d}_{\times})
.
This implies that
\Sigma_{(1,y)}\textup{Cone}({\rm Y})=\Sigma_{(1,y)}\left(\mathbb{R}\times{\rm Y}\right)
, hence that

T_{(1,y)}\textup{Cone}({\rm Y})=T_{(1,y)}\left(\mathbb{R}\times{\rm Y}\right)
.

Moreover, it is well-known that, for any metric product
{\rm Y}^{\prime}\times\!{\rm Y}
, we have

\Sigma_{(y^{\prime},y)}({\rm Y}^{\prime}\times{\rm Y})\cong\Sigma_{y^{\prime}}{\rm Y}^{\prime}\ast\Sigma_{y}{\rm Y}\hskip 5.69054pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 5.69054ptT_{(y^{\prime},y)}({\rm Y}^{\prime}\times{\rm Y})\cong T_{y^{\prime}}{\rm Y}^{\prime}\times T_{y}{\rm Y}

therefore
\Sigma_{(1,y)}\textup{Cone}({\rm Y})\cong\Sigma_{1}\mathbb{R}\ast\Sigma_{y}{\rm Y}\cong\mathbb{S}^{0}\ast\Sigma_{y}{\rm Y}
, and in turns
T_{(1,y)}{\rm X}\cong\mathbb{R}\times T_{y}{\rm Y}
.
∎

A consequence of the above lemmas is the computation of some spaces of directions of a spherical join.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
({\rm Y},{\sf d})
,
({\rm Y}^{\prime},{\sf d}^{\prime})
be two compact, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(1)
spaces and consider the spherical join
{\rm Y}\ast{\rm Y}^{\prime}
. For every
y\in{\rm Y}
it holds
\Sigma_{y}({\rm Y}\ast{\rm Y}^{\prime})\cong\Sigma_{y}{\rm Y}\ast{\rm Y}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The point
y\in{\rm Y}
can be seen as the point
(0,y,\cdot)\in{\rm Y}\ast{\rm Y}^{\prime}
. Applying several times Lemma
2.3
and Lemma
2.4
we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\times\textup{Cone}(\Sigma_{y}({\rm Y}\ast{\rm Y}^{\prime}))\cong\mathbb{R}\times T_{y}({\rm Y}\ast{\rm Y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\cong T_{(1,y)}\textup{Cone}({\rm Y}\ast{\rm Y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\cong T_{(1,y)}\textup{Cone}({\rm Y})\times T_{o^{\prime}}\textup{Cone}({\rm Y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\cong\mathbb{R}\times\textup{Cone}(\Sigma_{y}{\rm Y})\times\textup{Cone}({\rm Y}^{\prime}),

where
o^{\prime}\in\textup{Cone}({\rm Y}^{\prime})
denotes the vertex. Hence,

\textup{Cone}(\Sigma_{y}({\rm Y}\ast{\rm Y}^{\prime}))\cong\textup{Cone}(\Sigma_{y}{\rm Y})\times\textup{Cone}({\rm Y}^{\prime})\cong\textup{Cone}(\Sigma_{y}{\rm Y}\ast{\rm Y}^{\prime})

since the
\mathbb{R}
-factor clearly is sent into the
\mathbb{R}
-factor by the above isometries (see also
[
18
, Theorem 1.1]
) and by Lemma
2.3
. We conclude that
\Sigma_{y}({\rm Y}\ast{\rm Y}^{\prime})\cong\Sigma_{y}{\rm Y}\ast{\rm Y}^{\prime}
. ∎

Another application is the following useful fact, which will be crucial to perform induction in the proof of Theorem
H
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
({\rm Y},{\sf d})
be a geodesically complete
\textup{CAT}(1)
space of diameter
\pi
and
y\in{\rm Y}
. Then, there exists a
1
-Lipschitz surjective map
g\colon{\rm Y}\to\mathbb{S}^{0}\ast\Sigma_{y}{\rm Y}
.

Proof.

We consider the space
{\rm X}:=\textup{Cone}({\rm Y})
and the point
x=(1,y)\in\textup{Cone}({\rm Y})
.
Lemma
2.4
implies that
\Sigma_{x}\textup{Cone}({\rm Y})\cong\mathbb{S}^{0}\ast\Sigma_{y}{\rm Y}
. Since
{\rm X}
is a
\textup{CAT}(0)
Euclidean cone (as
{\rm Y}
is
\textup{CAT}(1)
), it admits the cone at infinity and its Tits boundary
\partial_{T}{\rm X}
is isometric to
({\rm Y},{\sf d})
. Then, the
1
-Lipschitz, surjective map
\partial\log_{x}\colon\partial_{T}{\rm X}\cong{\rm Y}\to\Sigma_{x}\textup{Cone}({\rm Y})\cong\mathbb{S}^{0}\ast\Sigma_{y}{\rm Y}
defined in (
4
) gives the thesis.
∎

2.4.
Strainer maps and local contractibility of spheres

Strainer maps play a crucial role in the description of the geometry of geodesically complete spaces with upper curvature bounds, see
[
25
]
. In the sequel we will need only the case of
(1,\delta)
-strainer maps in
\textup{CAT}(0)
spaces, so we will focus on this case.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
(\Sigma,{\sf d})
be
\textup{CAT}(1)
space, and let
\delta>0
. A point
v\in\Sigma
is called
\delta
-spherical
if there exists
\bar{v}\in\Sigma
such that

(5)

{\sf d}(v,w)+{\sf d}(w,\bar{v})<\pi+\delta

for every
w\in\Sigma
. The points
v
and
\bar{v}
are said to be
\delta
-spherical opposite.

The condition above implies that every two
antipodes
of
v
, i.e. points with distance
\pi
from
v
, are at mutual distance at most
2\delta
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper, geodesically complete
\textup{CAT}(0)
space, let
p\in{\rm X}
,
\delta>0
. We say that the distance map
{\sf d}_{p}\colon{\rm X}\to\mathbb{R}
,
x\mapsto{\sf d}(p,x)
is
(1,\delta)
-strainer
on a subset
U\subseteq{\rm X}
if for every
x\in U
the direction
\log_{x}(p)
is
\delta
-spherical in
\Sigma_{x}{\rm X}
.

The only example of
(1,\delta)
-strainer map we will use is the distance from the vertex of an Euclidean cone:

Example 2.9
.

Let
({\rm Y},{\sf d}_{\rm Y})
be a geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(1)
metric space. Consider
({\rm X},{\sf d}):=\textup{Cone}({\rm Y})
and let
o\in{\rm X}
be the vertex. Then
{\sf d}_{o}
is a
(1,\delta)
-strainer map on
U={\rm X}\setminus\{o\}
, for every
\delta>0
. Indeed, for every
x\in{\rm X}\setminus\{o\}
, the direction
\log_{x}(o)\in\Sigma_{x}{\rm X}
has a unique antipode.

Fibers of strainer maps are locally contractible and locally path connected.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space, let
p\in{\rm X}
and
0<\delta\leq 1/20
. Suppose
{\sf d}_{p}\colon{\rm X}\to\mathbb{R}
is
(1,\delta)
-strainer on
U\subseteq{\rm X}
. Then, for every
t\in[0,+\infty)
the set
{\sf d}_{p}^{-1}(t)\cap U=S_{t}(p)\cap U
is locally contractible and locally path connected.

Proof.

By
[
25
, Theorem 9.1]
, for every
x\in S_{t}(p)\cap U
we can find
\varepsilon_{x}>0
and a continuous map
G\colon B_{\varepsilon_{x}}(x)\to B_{\varepsilon_{x}}(x)\cap S_{t}(p)
with
G(x)=x
and
{\sf d}(G(y),x)\leq{\sf d}(y,x)
for every
y\in B_{\varepsilon_{x}}(x)
. We now define the map

\Psi\colon(B_{\varepsilon_{x}}(x)\cap S_{t}(p))\times[0,\varepsilon_{x}]\to B_{\varepsilon_{x}}(x)\cap S_{t}(p)

sending
(y,t)
to
G(\phi_{x}^{\varepsilon_{x},(1-t)\varepsilon_{x}}(y))
, where the maps
\phi
are the contraction maps defined in (
3
). By construction,
\Psi
is continuous,
\Psi(\cdot,0)=\textup{id}
and
\Psi(\cdot,1)=x
. This shows that
B_{\varepsilon_{x}}(x)\cap S_{t}(p)
is contractible. Moreover, the path
t\mapsto\Psi(y,t)
is contained in
B_{\varepsilon_{x}}(x)\cap S_{t}(p)
, proving that every point
y\in B_{\varepsilon_{x}}(x)\cap S_{t}(p)
can be connected via a path in
B_{\varepsilon_{x}}(x)\cap S_{t}(p)
to
x
. Therefore,
B_{\varepsilon_{x}}(x)\cap S_{t}(p)
is path connected.
∎

Remark 2.11
.

A more refined version of Proposition
2.10
has been proved in
[
34
, Proposition 8.3]
, but we do not need it here.

As an application of the stability of strainer maps under Gromov-Hausdorff convergence we obtain the following result.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space and let
x\in{\rm X}
.

(i)

There exists
r_{x}>0
such that
S_{r}(x)
is locally contractible and locally path connected for every
0<r<r_{x}
.

(ii)

If
({\rm X},{\sf d})
admits the cone at infinity then there exists
R_{x}>0
such that
S_{R}(x)
is locally contractible and locally path connected for every
R>R_{x}
.

Proof.

The tangent space
(T_{x}{\rm X},o_{x})
is a metric cone with base point given by the vertex
o_{x}
, and it is the pointed, Gromov-Hausdorff limit of the sequence
({\rm X},\lambda_{i}{\sf d},x)
for every
\lambda_{i}\to\infty
. The distance map from
o_{x}
is
(1,\delta)
-strainer for every
\delta>0
, as explained in the Example
2.9
. Now,
[
25
, Theorem 13.1]
implies that
{\sf d}_{x}\colon{\rm X}\to\mathbb{R}
is
(1,\delta)
-strainer on a neighbourhood of
S_{r}(x)
, for
r
sufficiently small. So, (i) follows from Proposition
2.10
.

If
({\rm X},{\sf d})
admits the cone at infinity, then the sequence of spaces
({\rm X},\lambda_{i}{\sf d},x)
converges in the Gromov-Hausdorff sense to
C_{\infty}{\rm X}
for every
\lambda_{i}\to 0
. Proposition
2.2
says that
C_{\infty}{\rm X}
is a metric cone. Arguing exactly as before we conclude the proof of (ii).
∎

The stability of strainer maps has been also used in
[
25
, Theorem 1.12]
to deduce homotopy properties of small spheres. In the same way, homotopy properties of large spheres in case of the existence of the cone at infinity have been deduced in
[
32
]
:

Proposition 2.13

(
[
32
, Theorem 4.6]
)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space admitting the cone at infinity. For every
x\in{\rm X}
there exists
R_{x}>0
such that
S_{R}(x)
is homotopy equivalent to
\partial_{T}{\rm X}
for every
R\geq R_{x}
.

2.5.
\textup{CAT}(\kappa)
homology and topological manifolds

A homology
n
-manifold (over
\mathbb{Z}
) is a Hausdorff, locally compact topological space
{\rm X}
with finite
\mathbb{Z}
-cohomological dimension (see Definitions 16.3-16.7 in
[
11
]
)
such that

H_{\ast}({\rm X},{\rm X}\setminus\{x\})\simeq H_{\ast}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
for every
x\in{\rm X}
, where
\simeq
denotes an isomorphism of algebraic structures.

3.
More on homology manifolds

We discuss here orientability and degree of maps between homology manifolds.

All the results stated in the first part are well-known for topological manifolds. Since it is hard to find a clean reference for the same results on homology manifolds, we try to give a unifying presentation. In the second part we will relate the topological notion of degree of a Lipschitz map to the analytical notion of its Jacobian by exploiting the rectifiability of
\textup{CAT}(1)
homology manifolds.

3.1.
Orientability and degree of maps

The orientation sheaf of a homology
n
-manifold
X
is the sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
generated by the presheaf
U\mapsto H_{n}(U)
, with stalks
{\mathcal{O}}_{X,x}=\varinjlim_{U}H_{n}(U)
, which coincide with the groups
H_{n}(X,X\setminus\{x\})
see
[
11

.
A system of neighbourhoods for
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
is given by the sets

{\mathcal{U}}(U,\xi_{U})=\{j_{x}(\xi_{U})\hskip 2.84526pt\,:\,\hskip 2.84526ptx\in U\}

for
U
varying among all open subsets of
{\rm X}
,
\xi_{U}\in H_{n}({\rm X},{\rm X}\setminus U)

j^{U}_{x}:H_{n}({\rm X},{\rm X}\setminus U)\to H_{n}({\rm X},{\rm X}\setminus\{x\})

It is well-known that
{\mathcal{O}}_{\rm X}
is a locally constant sheaf, that is for every

U
such that
{\mathcal{O}}_{\rm X}|_{U}\simeq U\times\mathbb{Z}
; this is usually subsumed by saying that homology manifolds are always
locally orientable
, see
[
10
]
and
[
11
, Theorem 16.15]

. A connected homology manifold
{\rm X}
is called

if the orientation sheaf is constant, that is
\mathcal{O}_{\rm X}\simeq{\rm X}\times\mathbb{Z}
;
an

of
{\rm X}
is a global section
\xi_{\rm X}\in\Gamma({\rm X},\mathcal{O}_{\rm X})
corresponding to a generator of
\mathbb{Z}
.

The orientation sheaf of
{\rm X}
over
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}

The same statement holds for any proper continuous map

between connected homology
n
-manifolds with the

Proof.

Then, the commutative diagram

\textstyle{\hskip 5.69054pt\oplus_{i=1}^{m}H_{n}({\rm X},{\rm X}\setminus\{x_{i}\})\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{m}\hskip 5.69054pt\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\hskip 14.22636ptf_{\ast}}

3.2.
Degree, Jacobian and Area Formula

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. A metric space
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is
n
-rectifiable if there exist countably many biLipschitz maps
f_{i}\colon A_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\to{\rm X}
such that
\mathcal{H}^{n}({\rm X}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}f_{i}(A_{i}))=0
. The next statement presents the only example of rectifiable spaces which is relevant for us.

Proposition 3.5

(
[
25
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper, geodesically complete, purely
n
-dimensional,
\textup{CAT}(1)
space. Then there exists a subset
M\subseteq{\rm X}
which is locally biLipschitz equivalent to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and such that
\mathcal{H}^{n}({\rm X}\setminus M)=0
. In particular,
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is
n
-rectifiable.

The theory of Jacobian of Lipschitz maps on rectifiable spaces is developed in
[
2
]
. Firstly, they consider two metric spaces
({\rm X},{\sf d}_{\rm X})
,
({\rm Y},{\sf d}_{\rm Y})
that are subsets of duals of separable Banach spaces
V^{\ast}
and
W^{\ast}
respectively.
Namely, let
V,W
be separable Banach spaces, let
({\rm X},{\sf d}_{\rm X})\subseteq(V^{*},\|\cdot\|_{V^{*}})
be a
n
-rectifiable metric space and let

f:({\rm X},{\sf d}_{\rm X})\to(W^{\ast},\|\cdot\|_{W^{\ast}})

be a Lipschitz map. Then, for
\mathcal{H}^{n}
-a.e.
x\in{\rm X}
there exists a suitable
n
-dimensional vector subspace of
V^{*}
called the
approximate tangent space
at
x
and denoted by
\textup{Tan}(x,{\rm X})
(see
[
2
, §5]
for the properties of this space). The dual
W^{*}
of a separable Banach space
W
carries the norm metric
\|\cdot\|_{W^{*}}
and also, for any choice of a dense subset
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of
W
, the metric

{\sf d}_{W^{*}}(T,T^{\prime}):=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{-i}\langle T-T^{\prime},w_{i}\rangle,

inducing the weak-
\ast
topology.
Then,
[
2
, Theorem 8.1]
gives that for
\mathcal{H}^{n}
-a.e.
x\in{\rm X}
there exist a linear and weak-
\ast
continuous map
A\colon V^{*}\to W^{*}
and a Borel subset
S_{x}\subseteq{\rm X}
such that

(7)

\lim_{y\in{\rm X}\setminus S_{x}\to x}\frac{{\sf d}_{W^{*}}(f(y),f(x)+A(y-x))}{\|y-x\|_{V^{*}}}=0,

where
S_{x}
has the property that for every
v\in\textup{Tan}(x,{\rm X})\cap\{\|\cdot\|_{V^{*}}=1\}
there exists a sequence
\{y_{i}\}\in{\rm X}\setminus S_{x}
such that
y_{i}\to x
and
\frac{y_{i}-x}{\|y_{i}-x\|}
converges weakly-
\ast
to
v

[
2
, Proposition 5.7]
.

The restriction of
A
to
\textup{Tan}(x,{\rm X})
is uniquely determined and it is denoted by

{\mathbf{d}}_{x}f\colon\textup{Tan}(x,{\rm X})\to W^{*}\;.

The Jacobian of
f
at
x
is defined as the number

\mathbf{J}f(x):=\frac{\omega_{n}}{\mathcal{H}^{n}(\{v\in\textup{Tan}(x,{\rm X})\,:\,\|{\mathbf{d}}_{x}f(v)\|_{W^{*}}\leq 1\})},

where
\omega_{n}
denotes the
\mathcal{H}^{n}
-measure of the unit ball
\mathbf{B}_{1}
in
(\textup{Tan}(x,{\rm X}),\|\cdot\|_{V^{*}})

(which coincides with the volume of the unit ball
in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
[
23
, Lemma 6]
).

Remark 3.6
.

In the situation above, if
f
is
L
-Lipschitz then
0\leq\mathbf{J}f(x)\leq L^{n}
. Indeed, using the definition of
{\sf d}_{W^{*}}
and (
7
) we get that

\displaystyle\lim_{y\in{\rm X}\setminus S_{x}\to x}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{-i}\left\langle A\left(\frac{y-x}{\|y-x\|_{V^{*}}}\right),w_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{y\in{\rm X}\setminus S_{x}\to x}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{-i}\left\langle\frac{f(y)-f(x)}{\|y-x\|_{V^{*}}},w_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{y\in{\rm X}\setminus S_{x}\to x}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{-i}L\left\langle\frac{f(y)-f(x)}{\|f(y)-f(x)\|_{W^{*}}},w_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{y\in{\rm X}\setminus S_{x}\to x}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{-i}L\|w_{i}\|_{W}

Choosing
v\in\mathbf{B}_{1}

such that
\|A(v)\|_{W^{*}}=\|{\mathbf{d}}_{x}f\|
, there exist
points
y\in{\rm X}\setminus S_{x}\to x
such that
\|A(\frac{y-x}{\|y-x\|_{V^{*}}})\|
converges in the weak-
\ast
sense to
A(v)
, the property of the set
S_{x}
recalled above.
Applying the weak-
\ast
convergence we get

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{-i}\|{\mathbf{d}}_{x}f\|\|w_{i}\|_{W}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{-i}L\|w_{i}\|_{W},

which implies that
\|{\mathbf{d}}_{x}f\|\leq L
.
Then, the
\|\cdot\|_{V^{*}}
-ball of radius
\frac{1}{L}
in
\textup{Tan}(x,{\rm X})
satisfies

\mathbf{B}_{1/L}\subseteq\{v\in\textup{Tan}(x,{\rm X})\,:\,\|{\mathbf{d}}_{x}f(v)\|_{W^{*}}\leq 1\},

so

\mathcal{H}^{n}(\{v\in\textup{Tan}(x,{\rm X})\,:\,\|df_{x}(v)\|_{W^{*}}\leq 1\})\geq\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathbf{B}_{1/L})=\frac{1}{L^{n}}\omega_{n}.

Therefore, the definition of Jacobian gives
0\leq\mathbf{J}f(x)\leq L^{n}
.

To define the Jacobian in the general case of metric spaces which are not subsets of Banach spaces, we proceed as follows. Let
f\colon({\rm X},{\sf d}_{\rm X})\to({\rm Y},{\sf d}_{\rm Y})
be a Lipschitz map between separable metric spaces and assume that
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is
n
-rectifiable. Consider the Kuratowski embeddings
\iota_{\rm X}\colon{\rm X}\to\ell^{\infty}
and
\iota_{\rm Y}\colon{\rm Y}\to\ell^{\infty}
, and the induced map
\tilde{f}:=\iota_{\rm Y}\circ f\circ\iota_{\rm X}^{-1}\colon\iota_{\rm X}({\rm X})\to\iota_{\rm Y}({\rm Y})\subseteq\ell^{\infty}
.
Then, we define
\mathbf{J}f(x):=\mathbf{J}\tilde{f}(\iota_{\rm X}(x))
.

Even if the definition of Jacobian is extrinsic, it has an intrinsic nature that is highlighted by the area-formula.

Proposition 3.7

(
[
2
, Theorem 8.2]
)
.

Let
f\colon({\rm X},{\sf d}_{\rm X})\to({\rm Y},{\sf d}_{\rm Y})
be an
L
-Lipschitz map and assume that
({\rm X},{\sf d}_{\rm X})
is
n
-rectifiable. Then

\int_{\rm X}g(x)\,\mathbf{J}f(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{n}(x)=\int_{\rm Y}\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(y)}g(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{n}(y)

for every Borel function
g\colon{\rm X}\to\mathbb{R}
. In particular,
\int_{\rm X}\mathbf{J}f(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{n}(x)=\int_{\rm Y}\#f^{-1}(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{n}(y)
.

Moreover,
0\leq\mathbf{J}f(x)\leq L^{n}
.

Proof.

The first statement is
[
2
, Theorem 8.2]
, the second one follows by Remark
3.6
.
∎

Clearly, for any map
f
between open subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which is differentiable at a point
x
, the differential
\mathbf{d}_{x}f
and the Jacobian
\mathbf{J}f(x)
defined above coincide with the usual notions in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular, the following lemma holds.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
f:U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\to V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a Lipschitz map between open sets, and let
x
be a point
where
f
is differentiable such that
\mathbf{J}f(x)>0
. Then,
x
is an isolated point of
f^{-1}(y)
and
|\textup{deg}_{x}(f)|=1
.
The same is true for the
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-degree.

Proof.

Since
x
is a point where
f
is differentiable, the determinant of
df_{x}
coincides with the Jacobian
\mathbf{J}f(x)
; moreover, since
\mathbf{J}f(x)>0
, this implies that there exists
\lambda>0
such that

(8)

\|df_{x}(v)\|\geq\lambda\|v\|\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{ for every }v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

In particular,
\frac{1}{\lambda}f
is expanding in a neighbourhood of
x
,
hence
f
is proper on
B_{r}(x)-\{x\}
for
r
small enough and
x
is the only preimage of
y
in
B_{r}(x)
. Let now
g(x^{\prime}):=x+(df)_{x}(x^{\prime}-x)
, let
V_{y}
be a convex neighbourhood of
y
, and choose
0<\varepsilon<\lambda
and a possibly smaller
r>0
such that
B_{r}(x)\cap f^{-1}(y)=\{x\}
,

f(B_{r}(x))\cup g(B_{r}(x))\subset V_{y}
and

\|f(x^{\prime})-g(x^{\prime})\|<\varepsilon\|x^{\prime}-x\|
for all
x^{\prime}\in B_{r}(x)
.
Then, the maps
f
and
g
are properly homotopic as maps of pairs
\left(B_{r}(x),B_{r}(x)\setminus\{x\}\right)\to\left(V_{y},V_{y}\setminus\{y\}\right)
.
Actually, calling
h_{t}=tf+(1-t)g
, we have that

h_{t}(x^{\prime})\neq y
for all
x^{\prime}\in B_{r}(x)\setminus\{x\}

(in fact, setting
\Delta(x^{\prime})=f(x^{\prime})-g(x^{\prime})
, by the differentiability of
f
at
x
we have

{\sf d}(h_{t}(x^{\prime}),y)=\|(d_{x}f)(x^{\prime}-x)+t\Delta(x^{\prime})\|\geq(\lambda-t\varepsilon)\|x^{\prime}-x\|>0
for all

, by (
8

It follows that
f
and
g
induce the same map in relative homology
H_{n}\left(U,U\setminus\{x\}\right)=H_{n}\left(B_{r}(x),B_{r}(x)\setminus\{x\}\right)\to H_{n}\left(V_{y},V_{y}\setminus\{y\}\right)=H_{n}\left(V,V\setminus\{y\}\right)
(with coefficients
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
), hence
\textup{deg}_{x}(f)=\textup{deg}_{x}(g)
and
\textup{deg}_{2}(f)=\textup{deg}_{2}(g)
as well. But the degree of
g
is clearly
\pm 1
as
g
is a linear isomorphism.
∎

We recall the statement of Theorem

Theorem
A
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space such that
\textup{Isom}({\rm X},{\sf d})
acts cocompactly on
{\rm X}
. If
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Since
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
then its Tits boundary
\partial_{T}{\rm X}
is compact, by Proposition
2.21
. Therefore,
[
9
, Proposition 7]
gives the thesis.
∎

Since
[
9
]
is not published, we propose an alternative proof.

Alternative proof of Theorem
A
.

Set
G:=\textup{Isom}({\rm X},{\sf d})
and fix a non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
. Consider the group
G_{\omega}
acting by isometries on the geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space
C_{\omega}{\rm X}
.

This action is transitive, being the limit of cocompact actions with codiameter going to
0
. By Proposition
2.21
,
C_{\omega}{\rm X}
is proper and it is a topological manifold.
Therefore, by
[
6
, Theorem 3]
,
C_{\omega}{\rm X}
is a smooth Lie group whose metric coincides with a sub-Finsler Carnot-Caratheodory metric. Since the
metric is
\textup{CAT}(0)
, then it is indeed Finsler.
This is due to the fact that the Hausdorff dimension of a sub-Finsler Carnot-Caratheodory metric that is not Finsler is strictly larger than the topological dimension of the Lie group, while the Hausdorff dimension of a geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
metric coincides with the topological dimension, by
[
25
, Theorem 1.1]
. A Finsler manifold which is
\textup{CAT}(0)
is indeed Riemannian by
[
14
, Theorem 1.2]
. Hence, since
C_{\omega}{\rm X}
is biLipschitz to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we deduce that
C_{\omega}{\rm X}
has to be an abelian Lie group by
[

\textup{CAT}(0)

is isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This implies that the Tits boundary
\partial_{T}{\rm X}
is isometric to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.

From this, one immediately infers that
{\rm X}
is flat, by induction on
n
, as follows.

If

, then
\partial_{T}{\rm X}\cong\mathbb{S}^{0}
and one gets that

by geodesic completeness.

If

, where

); then, by Lemma

where each factor is again a proper, geodesically complete

and
{\rm X}_{2}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n-1}

∎

. This implies that
G
has polynomial growth, so it is amenable. Since
G
has no fixed points at infinity then
[
1
, Theorem A]
implies that
{\rm X}
is flat.
∎

Let us now turn to the proof of Theorem
C
.

Theorem
C
.

Given
\varepsilon\!>\!0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
\delta\!=\!\delta(\varepsilon,n)\!>\!0
such that the following holds.
Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space.

(i)

If
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is
(1+\delta,C)
-quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
({\rm X},{\sf d})

is
(1+\varepsilon)
-biLipschitz homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(ii)

Moreover, if
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is
(1,C)
-quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In order to prove it,
we recall the definition of the
n
-asymptotic volume of a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space
({\rm X},{\sf d})
introduced in
[
32
]
. It is the quantity

\textup{as-vol}_{n}({\rm X}):=\limsup_{R\to+\infty}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n}(B_{R}(x))}{\omega_{n}R^{n}}\in[0,+\infty],

where
\omega_{n}
is the
n
-dimensional volume of the ball of radius
1
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
x
is a point of
{\rm X}
(notice that
\textup{as-vol}_{n}({\rm X})
does not depend on the choice of the point
x
, by the triangular inequality).
Nagano proved in
[
32
, Proposition 3.5]
that, if the asymptotic cone
C_{\infty}{\rm X}
exists, then

(9)

\textup{as-vol}_{n}({\rm X})=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial_{T}{\rm X})}{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}

and that if
{\rm X}
is purely
n
-dimensional and
\textup{as-vol}_{n}({\rm X})
is close to
1
, then
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is bi-Lipschitz equivalent to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Actually, looking at his proof, it is enough to assume that
\partial_{T}{\rm X}
is compact and purely
(n-1)
-dimensional.
So, we state his result in this form.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
32
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
\delta_{0}\!=\!\delta_{0}(\varepsilon,n)>0
with the following property. If
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space with
\textup{as-vol}_{n}({\rm X})\leq 1+\delta_{0}
and such that
\partial_{T}{\rm X}
is compact and purely
(n-1)
-dimensional, then
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is
(1+\varepsilon)
-biLipschitz equivalent to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Assuming that
{\rm X}
is purely
n
-dimensional, Nagano proved that one can relax the asymptotic volume bound and still obtain that
{\rm X}
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 4.3

(
[
32
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

If
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is a proper, geodesically complete, purely
n
-dimensional,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space with
\textup{as-vol}_{n}({\rm X})<3/2
, then
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The constant
3/2
is sharp, as shown in
[
32
, Theorem 1.3]
.

Assuming further that
{\rm X}
is a homology
n
-manifold, then the conclusion of Theorem
4.3
remains true if
\textup{as-vol}_{n}({\rm X})<3/2+\varepsilon_{n}
, where
\varepsilon_{n}
is a small positive constant (cp.
[
32
, Theorem 1.4]
).
This is the best result one can obtain using the methods of
[
32
]
. In particular, Theorems
B
and
D
are out of reach, and that is why we follow a different strategy in Sections
6
and
7
.

Here, we focus on the proof of Theorem
C
, which follow directly from Theorem
4.2
, Proposition
2.21
and the following observation.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space admitting the cone at infinity. Then
\textup{as-vol}_{n}({\rm X})=\textup{as-vol}_{n}(C_{\infty}{\rm X})
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.2
, we have
C_{\infty}{\rm X}=\textup{Cone}(\partial_{T}{\rm X})
. Let us denote by
o_{\infty}
the vertex of the cone at infinity. The cone structure, together with
[
31
, Lemma 6.4]
, gives that
\mathcal{H}^{n}(B_{R}(o_{\infty}))=\frac{R^{n}}{n}\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial_{T}{\rm X})
for every
R>0
. The same formula applied to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
gives
\omega_{n}R^{n}=\mathcal{H}^{n}(B_{R}(0))=\frac{R^{n}}{n}\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
, where
0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore, using (
9
) we have

\textup{as-vol}_{n}(C_{\infty}{\rm X})=\limsup_{R\to+\infty}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n}(B_{R}(o_{\infty}))}{\omega_{n}R^{n}}=\limsup_{R\to+\infty}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n}(B_{R}(o_{\infty}))}{\mathcal{H}^{n}(B_{R}(0))}=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial_{T}{\rm X})}{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}=\textup{as-vol}_{n}({\rm X}).

∎

Proof of Theorem
C
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, let
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(\varepsilon,n)>0
be the costant given by Theorem
4.2
, and set
\delta:=(1+\delta_{0})^{\frac{1}{n}}-1>0
.
Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(0)
space which is
(1+\delta,C)
-quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Proposition
2.21
implies that
C_{\infty}{\rm X}
exists and it is
(1+\delta)
-biLipschitz equivalent to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and that
\partial_{T}{\rm X}
is compact and purely
(n-1)
-dimensional. Then, by Lemma
4.4
, we have

(10)

\textup{as-vol}_{n}({\rm X})=\textup{as-vol}_{n}(C_{\infty}{\rm X})\leq(1+\delta)^{n}\leq 1+\delta_{0}.

Therefore, Theorem
4.2
implies that
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is
(1+\varepsilon)
-biLipschitz equivalent to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Finally, if
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is
(1,C)
-quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can apply (i) to
\varepsilon_{k}=1/k
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and find a
(1+\varepsilon_{k})
-biLipschitz homeomorphism
f_{k}\colon{\rm X}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. After composing these
f_{k}
with suitable translations of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can suppose that all of them send a fixed point
x\in{\rm X}
into the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The sequence
\{f_{k}\}
is then equi-Lipschitz and equi-bounded, so by Ascoli-Arzelà we can extract a converging subsequence. The limit map is therefore the desired isometry.

(Remark: assertion (ii) can be deduced directly, without invoking Theorem
4.2
, since if
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is
(1,C)
-quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\partial_{T}{\rm X}
is isometric to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
by Proposition
2.21
, which easily implies that
{\rm X}
is flat, as explained at the end of the proof of Theorem
A
.)
∎

5.
The proof of Theorem
B

In this section we prove Theorem
B
by developing the main tools needed for the study of asymptotic properties of
\textup{CAT}(0)
homology manifolds.
We recall the statement of the theorem.

Theorem
B
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper,
\textup{CAT}(0)
homology
m
-manifold. If
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
{\rm X}
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The proof is mainly inspired by the works of Thurston
[
38
]
and Davis-Januszkiewicz
[
17
]
. We will actually extend some of the results of
[
17
]
beyond the PL-case.
The two main tools are provided by the following classical results.

Proposition 5.1

(
[
38
, Corollary 2.10]
)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper,
\textup{CAT}(0)
homology
n
-manifold. Let
x\in{\rm X}
and
0<r\leq R
. Then:

(i)

the metric sphere
S_{r}(x)
is a homology
(n-1)
-manifold;

(ii)

the natural contraction map
\varphi_{x}^{R,r}\colon S_{R}(x)\to S_{r}(x)
is acyclic, that is for every
y\in S_{r}(x)
, calling
F_{y}=(\varphi_{x}^{R,r})^{-1}(y)
the fiber of
y
, we have
\tilde{H}^{*}(F_{y};\mathbb{Z})=0
, where
\tilde{H}^{*}
is the reduced cohomology.

Remark 5.2
.

The proof of the above result, in
[
38
]
, is based on
[
38
, Proposition 2.8]
which uses singular homology, and is a form of Alexander duality.
Proposition
5.1

still holds if one considers Borel-Moore homology in place of the singular homology considered there.

Indeed, following the proof of
[
38
, Proposition 2.8]
, we have that the closed balls
\overline{B}_{r}(x)
are homology
n
-manifolds with boundary, and their boundary are the metric spheres
S_{r}(x)
, which are indeed homology
(n-1)
-manifolds (defined via Borel-Moore homology) by
[
30
]
.
However, the sphere
S_{r}(x)
might be not locally contractible (see Remark
2.14
), so the singular and Borel-Moore homologies may differ.
The double of
\overline{B}_{r}(x)
along its boundary is, in any case, a (Borel-Moore) homology
n
-manifold without boundary and a (Borel-Moore) homology sphere. This follows from references [48] and [58] in
[
38
]
, where these results are stated for cohomology manifolds, and so they hold for homology manifolds as well by Remark
2.14
. The final step in Thurston’s proof is an Alexander duality result for homology spheres; the correct reference for this is
[
39
, Theorem VIII.6.4]
.

Thurston’s result is particularly useful in conjunction with Vietoris-Begle’s Theorem.

Theorem 5.3

(Vietoris-Begle’s Mapping Theorem,
[
36
]
, Chpt.6, Theorem 9.15)
.

Let
{\rm X}
and
{\rm Y}
be two compact metric spaces, and let
f\colon{\rm X}\to{\rm Y}
be surjective and continuous. Suppose that the fibers

f^{-1}(y)
are acyclic for every
y\in{\rm Y}
. Then, the induced homomorphism
f^{*}\colon H^{*}({\rm Y};\mathbb{Z})\to H^{*}({\rm X};\mathbb{Z})
is an isomorphism.

The first consequence is a generalization of
[
17
, Theorem 3d.1]
for homology manifolds (not necessarily PL).

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper,
\textup{CAT}(0)
homology
n
-manifold. Then:

(i)

the metric sphere
S_{r}(x)
is a homology
(n-1)
-manifold which is a homology
(n-1)
-sphere for every
r>0
;

(ii)

if
X
admits the cone at infinity, then for every
x\in{\rm X}
there exists
r_{x}>0
such that the contraction maps
\varphi_{x}^{R,r}\colon S_{R}(x)\to S_{r}(x)
induce surjective maps between fundamental groups, for every
r\in(0,r_{x})
and every
R\geq r
.

Proof.

Let us call
f=\varphi_{x}^{R,r}:S_{R}(x)\to S_{r}(x)
the contraction, for simplicity.

Applying Vietoris-Begle Mapping Theorem
5.3
to
f
, and using Proposition
5.1
, we deduce that
f^{\ast}:H^{k}(S_{R}(x))\to H^{k}(S_{r}(x))
is an isomorphism, for every
0<r<R
and every
k
. Then, the two metric spheres
S_{r}(x)
and
S_{R}(x)
are homology
(n-1)
-manifolds, by Proposition
5.1
, with the same integral cohomology groups, hence same homology,
by Poincaré duality. Moreover, Propositions
2.16
and
2.1
imply that
H_{\ast}(S_{r}(x))\simeq H_{\ast}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
for every
r
small enough, so
H_{\ast}(S_{R}(x))\simeq H_{\ast}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
for every
x\in{\rm X}
and every
R>0
. This shows (i).

To show (ii),
notice that by the universal coefficient theorem we have a commutative diagram (see
[
11
, Chapter V]
)

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\text{Ext}\big(H^{n}(S_{R}(x)),\mathbb{Z}\big)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{H_{n-1}(S_{R}(x))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Large f_{\ast}}
\textstyle{\text{Hom}\big(H^{n-1}(S_{R}(x)),\mathbb{Z}\big)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0}
\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\text{Ext}\big(H^{n}(S_{r}(x)),\mathbb{Z}\big)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{H_{n-1}(S_{r}(x))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\text{Hom}\big(H^{n-1}(S_{r}(x)),\mathbb{Z}\big)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0}

and since
S_{r}(x),S_{R}(x)
are
(n-1)
-homology spheres, the Ext groups are trivial and
f_{\ast}
is an isomorphism too; therefore
|\textup{deg}(f)|=1
.
Now recall that, by Proposition
2.12
, for every
x
there exist
r_{x},R_{x}>0
such that all metric spheres centred at
x
with radii in
(0,r_{x})\cup(R_{x},\infty)
are locally path connected and locally contractible, so we can apply covering theory to these spheres.
Then, choose first
R_{0}\geq R_{x}
and
r\leq r_{x}<R_{0}
, let
p:\widehat{S}_{r}(x)\to S_{r}(x)
be the covering associated to the subgroup
f_{\ast}\pi_{1}(S_{R_{0}}(x))
, and let
\hat{f}:S_{R_{0}}(x)\to\widehat{S}_{r}(x)
be a lift of the contraction map.
The space
\widehat{S}_{r}(x)
is a homology manifold too (since it covers a homology manifold) which is still compact and connected, since
\hat{f}
is surjective by covering theory; moreover,
\widehat{S}_{r}(x)
is still a homology sphere, again by Vietoris-Begle’s theorem applied to
\hat{f}
, which has the same fibers as
f
; so it is also orientable.
Since
1=|\textup{deg}(f)|=|\textup{deg}(\hat{f}\circ p)|=|\textup{deg}(\hat{f})\cdot\textup{deg}(p)|
by Lemma
3.3
, we deduce that
the degree of the (finite) covering
p
is
\pm 1
. This in turns implies that
f_{\ast}:\pi_{1}(S_{R_{0}}(x))\to\pi_{1}(S_{r}(x))
is surjective.

Finally, for any
r\leq R<R_{0}
we know that
f_{\ast}=(\varphi^{R,r}_{x})_{\ast}\circ(\varphi^{R_{0},R}_{x})_{\ast}
is surjective, hence
(\varphi^{R,r}_{x})_{\ast}
is surjective too.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

It is likely that item (ii) of Theorem
5.4
holds without assuming that
{\rm X}
admits the cone at infinity. In order to use our strategy one would need to prove that each metric sphere
S_{r}(x)
is locally contractible. While this is always true for small enough spheres, and also for large enough spheres when
{\rm X}
has the cone at infinity, by Proposition
2.12
.

Alternatively one could try to follow a purely algebraic topology proof, but this goes beyond the scope of this paper.

A corollary is the following generalization of
[
32
, Theorem 4.7]
to homology manifolds.

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
n\geq 3
. Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a proper,
\textup{CAT}(0)
homology
n
-manifold that admits the cone at infinity. If
\pi_{1}(\partial_{T}{\rm X})=\{0\}
then
{\rm X}
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

A combination of Proposition
2.13
and item (ii) of Theorem
5.4
implies that for every
x\in{\rm X}
and
r>0
small enough, the metric sphere
S_{r}(x)
is simply connected. Therefore, it is homotopy equivalent to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, being a homology
(n-1)
-manifold with the same homology of
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, by item (i) of Theorem
5.4
. By Propositions
2.17
and
2.1
,
{\rm X}
is indeed a topological manifold. The thesis follows by
[
32
, Theorem 4.7]
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
B
.

By Proposition
2.21
,
{\rm X}
admits the cone at infinity
C_{\infty}{\rm X}
, which is moreover bi-Lipschitz equivalent to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore,
\dim(C_{\infty}{\rm X})=\dim(\mathbb{R}^{n})=n
. Furthermore, by Proposition
2.2
,
\dim(C_{\infty}{\rm X})=\dim({\rm X})=m
, hence
n=m
. If
n\geq 3
, Proposition
2.21
also implies that
\partial_{T}{\rm X}
is homotopy equivalent to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, hence simply connected. Therefore, Corollary
5.6
implies that
{\rm X}
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
n=2
, then
{\rm X}
is already a topological manifold and every contractible topological

-manifold is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
∎

We end this section with a comment on Theorem
5.4
. If
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is a proper,
\textup{CAT}(0)
homology
n
-manifold then for every
x\in{\rm X}
and every
0<r<R
, the contraction map
c_{r}^{R}\colon S_{R}(x)\to S_{r}(x)
induces a map in homology which is an isomorphism. In general, this map is not a homotopy equivalence, actually the map induced at the level of fundamental groups is not an isomorphism in general. By point (ii) of Theorem

this means that the map induced at the level of fundamental groups is not injective.
There are examples of topological manifolds where this happens: in
[

, Theorem 5b.1]
it is presented a proper,
\textup{CAT}(0)
topological manifold which is not simply connected at infinity. This is precisely due to the fact that the contraction map between two metric spheres induces a map at the level of fundamental groups which is not injective.

6.
The proof of Theorems
D
,
E
and
H

We start with Theorem
H
, which is the key to proving the Theorems
D
and
E
.

It might be helpful, for the reader, to visualize first the idea of proof of Theorems
H
and
E
in dimension
n=2
, which we give separately in the Appendix.

Theorem
H
.

Let
f\colon({\rm Y},{\sf d})\to({\rm Y}^{\prime},{\sf d}^{\prime})
be a
1
-Lipschitz, surjective map between compact, geodesically complete,
\textup{CAT}(1)
spaces of dimension
n
.
Assume that
{\rm Y}
is a homology manifold homotopy equivalent to
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, with
\mathcal{H}^{n}({\rm Y})<2\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
: then,
{\rm Y}^{\prime}
is also a homology
n
-manifold which is homotopy equivalent to
\mathbb{S}^{n}
.

Proof.

For every
{\rm Y}^{\prime}
as in the statement, we define

\textup{sph}({\rm Y}^{\prime}):=\max\{m\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,{\rm Y}^{\prime}\cong\mathbb{S}^{m}\ast{\rm Z}\},

(possibly, with
{\rm Z}=\emptyset
); in other words,
\textup{sph}({\rm Y}^{\prime})
denotes the maximal round spherical factor of
{\rm Y}^{\prime}
in the unique decomposition of
{\rm Y}^{\prime}
in spherical joins provided by
[
27
, Corollary 1.2]
. Notice that the factor
{\rm Z}
is a again a compact, geodesically complete
\textup{CAT}(1)
space, by
[
12
, Corollary II.3.15]
. We will prove the statement by induction on
k:=n-\textup{sph}({\rm Y}^{\prime})
.

If
k=0
then
{\rm Y}^{\prime}
is isometric to
\mathbb{S}^{n}
and there is nothing to prove.

If
k=1
then
{\rm Y}^{\prime}=\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\ast F
, where
F
is a finite set of points since
\dim({\rm Y}^{\prime})=n
, and
{\sf d}(p,q)=\pi
for different
p,q\in F
.
Now,
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\ast F
consists of
\#F
isometric copies of a spherical hemisphere, so

\#F\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathbb{S}^{n})/2=\mathcal{H}^{n}({\rm Y}^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{H}^{n}({\rm Y})<2\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathbb{S}^{n}).

Therefore
\#F\leq 3
. Notice that
\#F\neq 1
, otherwise
{\rm Y}
would not be geodesically complete. Moreover,
\#F\neq 2
or
{\rm Y}^{\prime}\cong\mathbb{S}^{n}
, contradicting the definition of
\textup{sph}({\rm Y}^{\prime})
.

Let us also exclude that
\#F=3
, which will imply that
k\geq 2
necessarily. Actually, if
\#F=3
then the space
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\ast F
would be isometric to the gluing of three spherical hemispheres of
\mathbb{S}^{n}
along their common boundary. Denoting by
H_{i}
these hemispheres, for
i=1,2,3
, let us consider the three spheres
S_{ij}:=H_{i}\cup H_{j}
for every two distinct
i,j\in\{1,2,3\}
, and
consider the
1
-Lipschitz map
\rho\colon{\rm Y}^{\prime}\to S_{12}
,
which is the identity on
S_{12}
and which coincides with the reflection along the equator of
S_{23}
on the hemisphere
H_{3}
.
Then,
\hat{f}=\rho\circ f\colon{\rm Y}\to S_{12}\cong\mathbb{S}^{n}
is a
1
-Lipschitz surjective map between compact, connected, oriented,
\textup{CAT}(1)
homology
n
-manifolds. Corollary
3.10
implies that

2\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathbb{S}^{n})>\mathcal{H}^{n}({\rm Y})\geq|\textup{deg}(\hat{f})|\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathbb{S}^{n})

therefore
|\textup{deg}(\hat{f})|\leq 1
.
Actually,
|\textup{deg}(\hat{f})|=1
necessarily, or
\textup{deg}(\hat{f})=\textup{deg}_{2}(\hat{f})=0
and then we would have
\#\hat{f}^{-1}(y^{\prime})\geq 2
for
\mathcal{H}^{n}
-a.e.
y^{\prime}\in S_{12}
by Proposition
3.9
.(i) (since by construction
\hat{f}
has at least two preimages for every
y^{\prime}
in the interior of
H_{2}
, which has positive
\mathcal{H}^{n}
-measure).
By the area formula
3.7
it would then follow

2\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathbb{S}^{n})>\mathcal{H}^{n}({\rm Y})\geq\int_{\rm Y}\mathbf{J}f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{n}(y)=\int_{S_{12}}\#\hat{f}^{-1}(y^{\prime})\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{n}(y^{\prime})\geq 2\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n}(S_{12})=2\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathbb{S}^{n}),

which is a contradiction.
Proposition
3.9
(i) then would imply that
\#\hat{f}^{-1}(y^{\prime})\geq 3
for every
y^{\prime}\in H_{2}\subseteq S_{12}
, so applying again Proposition
3.7
we would obtain

2\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathbb{S}^{n})>\mathcal{H}^{n}({\rm Y})\geq\int_{S_{12}}\#\hat{f}^{-1}(y^{\prime})\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{n}(y^{\prime})\geq\mathcal{H}^{n}(H_{1})+3\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n}(H_{2})=2\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathbb{S}^{n}),

which is again a contradiction.

Therefore,
k\geq 2

Until now, we gave positive answers to Question

Theorem

For any

\textup{CAT}(0)

Proof.

First, we give an example in dimension

Let
(S,{\sf d})
be a circle of total length
4\pi

\pi
;

\textup{CAT}(1)
space with diameter
\pi
. Let
