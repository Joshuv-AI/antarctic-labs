EchoChain: A Full-Duplex Benchmark for State-Update Reasoning Under Interruptions
Smit Nautambhai Modi, Gandharv Mahajan, Marc Wetter, Randall Welles

Abstract
Real-time voice assistants must revise task state when users interrupt mid-response, but existing spoken-dialog benchmarks largely evaluate turn-based interaction and miss this failure mode. We introduce EchoChain, a controlled benchmark for evaluating full-duplex state-update reasoning under mid-speech interruptions. EchoChain identifies three recurring failure patterns in post-interruption continuations: contextual inertia, interruption amnesia, and objective displacement. The benchmark generates scenario-driven conversations and injects interruptions at a standardized point relative to assistant speech onset, enabling controlled cross-model comparison. In a paired half-duplex control, total failures drop by 40.2% relative to interrupted runs, indicating that many errors are driven by state-update reasoning under interruption rather than task difficulty alone. Across evaluated real-time voice models, no system exceeds a 50% pass rate, showing substantial room for improvement in mid-generation state revision. EchoChain provides a reproducible benchmark for diagnosing state-update reasoning failures in full-duplex voice interaction.

Introduction

Human communication is naturally full-duplex. Listeners interject, revise, and provide information while the speaker is still talking through audio and visual cues
(
12
)
. Spoken dialogue systems are expected to operate under the same conditions, yet evaluation has largely remained half-duplex, assuming that each turn is entirely complete before the other party begins speaking
(
4
)
,
(
11
)
,
(

. In practice, a user can revise a request, add a constraint, or redirect the task while the assistant is mid-response, and the assistant must update its in-progress reasoning without the benefit of clean turn breaks. When new information arrives while the assistant is generating, online state revision becomes a core requirement, and existing benchmarks that assume turn boundaries do not evaluate it.

Interruptions are a natural stressor for full-duplex systems because new information arrives while the assistant is mid-generation, forcing it to revise what it is currently producing
(
12
)
. The difficulty in evaluating this is that failures are often invisible at the surface. A model may acknowledge the interruption yet continue under stale or incorrect assumptions, incorporate the update momentarily before reverting to its original plan, or not complete the main requirements of the user input. The response appears fluent and responsive, but the underlying task state is incorrect.

To assess whether these errors stem from the interruption itself rather than general task difficulty, we conducted a paired comparison against a non-interruption baseline of the same conversation using the same rubric. Under this baseline, with 48 sampled conversations across all 4 models, 92 failures were detected for the interrupted conversations, while 55 failures were detected for the non-interrupted conversations. This corresponds to a net 40.22% reduction in total failures, indicating that a substantial share of errors are related to the interruption event. This finding necessitates the creation of a dedicated benchmark on how models revise in-progress responses as new information arrives during generation.

State-update reasoning is the ability of a model to preserve and revise its task state. In this paper, we focus specifically on when this happens during ongoing speech generation because a user introduces new information mid-utterance. Analysis of these failures reveals three recurring patterns in how models mishandle interruptions during generation, which we use to build a classification taxonomy. In contextual inertia, the model receives the new information but continues generating as if nothing changed. In interruption amnesia, the update is initially incorporated but later overwritten as generation proceeds. In objective displacement, the model pivots entirely to the interruption, abandoning the original task. Each pattern reflects a different breakdown in how the model maintains and revises task state under concurrent input.

To study these failures systematically, we introduce EchoChain, a benchmark evaluating how full-duplex spoken dialogue models handle mid-generation interruptions. The EchoChain framework generates scenario-driven conversations through a planner-simulator loop, where each user message and barge-in is conditioned on the conversation history, ensuring that interruption content is contextually grounded. User speech and the interruption stimulus are pre-synthesized, and barge-in timing is anchored, so the framework can be used to compare various models under the same conditions. All user audio is generated through a Persona Voice Engine that clones voices from our diverse set of recorded actors into synthetic speech, ensuring identical audio input across models and controlled reproducibility across trials. A paired listening study shows that raters cannot reliably tell the cloned voices apart from real recordings. The framework records aligned audio and transcripts for the pre-interruption assistant segment, the barge-in utterance, and the post-interruption continuation, and routes flagged cases through automated classification followed by human review.

We categorize full-duplex state-update reasoning failures along three dimensions. These categories capture common post-interruption failure patterns that arise as a consequence of the interruption. Examples of these failures can be found in Figure
1
.

Contextual Inertia
is a failure that is seen when the assistant does not properly integrate the interruption content into the following response. Oftentimes, it does acknowledge that some interruption happened, even saying it is going to incorporate it; however, in the actual content, no change or an incorrect change is made instead. As in the example, the assistant acknowledges a caffeine constraint from the interruption but still recommends a caffeinated drink. This results in responses where it can appear to shift topic or correct itself superficially, but the actual content remains a failure. To date, established metrics remain insufficient for capturing this error, as they were not developed with this specific vulnerability in mind.

Interruption Amnesia
is a symptom of models simply forgetting past constraints or requirements after being interrupted. It can be seen that initially, constraints are incorporated correctly into earlier model responses; however, due to an onset of ’amnesia’ caused by the interruption, they fail to integrate them correctly after the barge-in. In the example, the assistant correctly applies dual-timezone formatting after the first interruption, but silently drops it after the second. As a result, the system loses contextual alignment in its response once the interruption occurs and generates a failing response.

Objective Displacement
is a unique failure that is caused by how an interruption changes a natural conversation. When the assistant is interrupted before it has completed a user request, in some cases, it is more than reasonable to expect the assistant to finish the request despite a change or modification to the request from the user. This is signaled by the interruption either adding context without introducing a new request, or using additive phrasing such as ’also’ or ’in addition to.’ Yet, it can be observed that most of the time, the assistant responds only to the interruption, entirely forgetting the actual conversation state. The example shows the assistant abandoning a regulatory comparison request when asked for an additional flag in addition to the comparison request. This leaves the conversation broken and missing a completing factor that is necessary for a good response.

3
Related Work

3.1
Single-turn Audio and Speech Reasoning Benchmarks

Existing benchmarks that operate in single-turn settings evaluate speech understanding, instruction following, and paralinguistic or scene cues. VoiceBench,
1
, and AudioBench,
14
, provide broad coverage in these statistics. VoiceBench primarily evaluates text response content, and AudioBench relies heavily on model-based judging and task-specific metrics, preventing both of these benchmarks from measuring audio assistant reasoning directly. BigBenchAudio,
5
, adapted from BIG-Bench Hard,
13
, further highlights a modality-induced speech reasoning gap by comparing speech to speech, speech to text, and text to text settings, while retaining a turn-based evaluation paradigm.

In turn-based benchmarks, user speech is treated as an atomic input, and models are assessed on their response, without probing how new user information arriving during generation affects model reasoning. EchoChain fills this gap by introducing controlled mid-speech interruptions in full-duplex interaction, implemented as a standardized, model-independent barge-in event during assistant speech. The harness records aligned audio and transcripts for the pre-interruption assistant segment, the barge-in utterance, and the continuation. This continuation is then assessed on whether it fails, specifically paying attention to contextual inertia, interruption amnesia, and objective displacement.

3.2
Half-duplex Multi-turn Spoken Dialogue Benchmarks

A parallel line of work evaluates multi-turn spoken dialogue under a half-duplex interaction framework, where the model receives each user’s utterance in its entirety before responding with each output in its entirety. Audio MultiChallenge,
4
, evaluates end-to-end spoken dialogue systems on context tracking, instruction following, and self-consistency across multi-turn interactions using high-fidelity recordings with natural disfluencies. SpokenWOZ,
11
, benchmarks spoken task-oriented dialogue and evaluates whether models can track task-relevant information across multiple exchanges, while MTalk-Bench,
2
, assesses speech-to-speech models across semantic, paralinguistic, and ambient sound dimensions in multi-turn settings. These benchmarks evaluate memory and coherence across exchanges, but each exchange still proceeds with exactly one speaker at a time.

EchoChain instead studies interruptions that modify the task during assistant speech. The focus, therefore, shifts from cross-turn memory and coherence to whether the continuation incorporates the update correctly, a question that half-duplex multi-turn benchmarks can not test.

3.3
Full-duplex Spoken Dialogue Benchmarks

Prior benchmarks for full-duplex spoken dialogue focus on overlap handling through timing and turn-taking metrics. Full-Duplex-Bench,
8
evaluates pause handling, backchanneling, smooth turn-taking, and user interruption using automated behavior metrics, and Full-Duplex-Bench v1.5,
7
, extends this to controlled overlap scenarios with prosodic and speech-quality analyses. These frameworks evaluate when models yield and resume under overlap, but do not test the reasoning of the model in the post-interruption response.

Full-Duplex-Bench v2,
6
, generates multi-turn dialogues with LLMs and moves closer to task-level evaluation by introducing an automated real-time examiner to the framework that scores correction handling and entity tracking. Because the examiner adapts its pacing and stage progression in response to the evaluated model, interruption timing and content can vary through mutual adaptation, complicating controlled cross-model comparison. Moreover, aggregate task-level scoring does not explicitly separate distinct failure mechanisms during ongoing generation, such as contextual inertia, interruption amnesia, and objective displacement, which each describe a different way state-update reasoning can break down under concurrent input and output.

FD-Bench,
9
, similarly constructs conversations with LLMs but takes a different approach: it synthesizes all user-side input into speech and streams this fixed user audio to the assistant before the first user input is given. Because the user script is predetermined, subsequent user utterances, including interruption events, are not adjusted based on the assistant’s realized replies. The benchmark emphasizes interruption detection rates, timing behavior, and aggregate response-quality scores, but does not directly evaluate whether the continuation properly integrates the interrupting content. FLEXI,
3
, benchmarks multiple full-duplex scenarios and introduces a Topic Shift Score that measures whether a response redirects toward the interrupting query. The Topic Shift Score is an incomplete metric because it ignores the quality of reasoning. It rewards any response that pivots to the new topic - even if that pivot is disjointed or fails to reconcile the new data with the existing discussion.

EchoChain takes a complementary approach that addresses each of these limitations. User messages and barge-in content are conditioned on the assistant’s observed responses through a planner-simulator loop. Barge-in timing is scheduled relative to detected assistant speech onset, enabling the same interruption stimulus to be applied across responder models for controlled cross-model comparison. A diagnostic failure taxonomy, which includes contextual inertia, interruption amnesia, and objective displacement, classifies reasoning failures after an interruption. This taxonomy identifies failures that timing-only overlap metrics, aggregate task-level scoring, and topic-shift measures do not, providing a deeper insight into how models are performing.

4
The EchoChain Framework

Figure 2
:
Architecture of EchoChain. Scenario seeds drive a planner–simulator loop that generates context-dependent user utterances and barge-ins, which are synthesized into speech and streamed through a full-duplex orchestrator to a realtime responder model. The orchestrator injects interruptions at a controlled point during assistant speech, and the resulting transcripts and audio are evaluated automatically and, when flagged, reviewed by humans.

Comparing how different models handle interruptions fairly requires controlling for variables unrelated to reasoning. EchoChain normalizes three such variables. Acoustic latency is controlled by pre-synthesizing all user speech and barge-in audio before the interaction begins, so injection timing is the same for all models. Interruption timing is standardized by scheduling each barge-in at a fixed offset from the assistant speech onset, ensuring every model receives the input at a comparable point in its generation. Contextual consistency is maintained by holding system instructions, persona definitions, and conversation history constant across models.

EchoChain’s framework is organized into four components, illustrated in Figure
2
. Each conversation is initialized from a scenario seed that specifies a topic and persona drawn from a pool spanning voice support calls, interview coaching, multi-constraint planning and coordination, etc. To ensure diversity in the data set, these seeds were periodically changed during the creation of the EchoChain benchmark. The Dialogue Planner Module produces a conversation blueprint that structures the interaction to be interruption-appropriate. At each exchange, the User Simulator Module generates the next user message, and after an initial context establishment stage, barge-in text conditioned on the current blueprint and accumulated conversation history. These textual outputs are then passed to the Persona Voice Engine, which synthesizes user speech for both text outputs. The Orchestrator streams the user audio to the Realtime Responder Model, monitors for assistant speech onset, and injects the barge-in at the scheduled offset. After a complete assistant response, the conversational outputs are passed to an evaluation layer, where a failure classifier applies the taxonomy through majority voting. Flagged conversations are routed to human review for final adjudication, and conversations without detected failures continue for another exchange.

4.1
Dialogue Generation

The Scenario Specification and Dialogue Design and Simulation layers of the framework jointly handle conversation generation. The Dialogue Planner Module, implemented with GPT-5.2, takes the seed and accumulated conversation history and produces a blueprint that governs conversational progression while steering the interaction toward states where interruptions are contextually appropriate. The blueprint is updated after each completed exchange, so the planner adapts to the responder’s observed behavior rather than following a fixed trajectory. The User Simulator Module, implemented with Gemini 3 Pro, consumes the current blueprint and conversation history to generate the next user message and, during interruption-eligible exchanges, the barge-in text. Because both modules operate over the live conversation history, the resulting dialogue and its interruptions evolve with the interaction rather than being determined in advance.

4.2
Audio Synthesis and Voice Validation

The dialogue generation stage produces textual user messages and barge-in content, but the system under test operates on streaming audio. The Audio Synthesis and Pre-Generation layer generates all user audio before the interaction begins. This is because converting text to speech at runtime would introduce variable synthesis delays that could shift the effective barge-in timing across models, undermining the controlled comparison established in the framework design. A Persona Voice Engine built on Minimax Voice Cloning and a User TTS Engine built on Minimax Speech-02-HD select a speaker profile from our pool of recorded voice actors and produce speech for the primary user utterance and, during interruption-eligible exchanges, a separate barge-in stimulus. Each conversation is generated using a single cloned voice for all user utterances.

To validate that the cloned voices are a realistic proxy for human speech, we conducted a paired listening study. Each trial paired a real voice actor recording with the corresponding cloned sample, and raters assessed both clips on a short rubric covering perceived human likeness, speech naturalness, and synthesis artifacts. 4.3
Full-duplex Runtime and Failure Detection

The preceding components produce pre-generated user speech and barge-in stimuli, but evaluating how models handle interruptions requires a runtime layer that delivers this audio under controlled streaming conditions. The Realtime Runtime Loop, coordinated by the Full-Duplex Orchestrator, executes each conversation as a live interaction between the pre-generated audio and a Realtime Responder Model operating in full-duplex mode. The framework supports pluggable responder models, with a responder assigned uniformly at random per conversation instance while scenario context and runtime constraints are held constant. The Orchestrator maintains the current planning blueprint and accumulated dialogue context, streams user speech as incremental audio chunks that mirror the delivery pattern of human speech, and aggregates the responder’s output deltas, including both audio and transcripts, into final responses.

Central to the evaluation is controlling when and how interruptions enter the live audio stream. The Barge-in Scheduler is inactive for the first exchange, which proceeds uninterrupted to establish conversational context. For each subsequent exchange, the scheduler monitors for assistant speech onset and, after a configured delay, injects the pre-generated barge-in stimulus so that new input arrives while the responder is actively generating; because the offset is fixed relative to detected onset, every model receives the interruption under the same timing rule, though model-specific buffering may introduce small deviations. The responder then produces a continuation informed by both its partial output and the interruption content, which is then evaluated for a correctly updated task state.

The resulting post-interruption output is routed to the Auto Evaluation layer, where a Failure Classifier, implemented with GPT-5.2, applies the failure taxonomy to the segmented transcripts to produce a pass/fail determination. Transcripts are used instead of raw audio for scalability and because current language models exhibit stronger failure detection over textual discourse,
10
. When the classifier flags a failure, the conversation history up to the preceding exchange is provided as text, and the flagged exchange is replayed as audio to the remaining responder models, enabling cross-model comparison under identical conditions. Flagged conversations are then routed to human review (Section
5
), where reviewers assess both transcripts and aligned audio to produce the final adjudicated labels.

5
Human Review and Rubric-based Evaluation

Conversations that are flagged as failures by the Failure Classifier enter human review with their full transcripts and aligned audio. The review proceeds in two stages: a quality screening and a rubric-based evaluation of each model’s post interruption response. First, a contextual screening that filters out conversations that are unrealistic, internally inconsistent, or otherwise ill-posed is completed. For example, conversations may be removed at this stage because the interruption content is not appropriate, or the user-side synthesized speech was not realistic. Second, conversations that pass this screening proceed to conversation-specific rubric creation and evaluation used to label outcomes and categorize failures. All reviewers are blind to model identity throughout both stages.

Human annotators assess each conversation instance for three criteria to ensure the conversation is a valid evaluation case. Annotators check whether the dialogue is coherent, with each user utterance and interruption consistent with prior context rather than containing abrupt topic discontinuities or incompatible constraints. Second, the interaction must be naturalistic, avoiding test-like phrasing or adversarial constructions that are unlikely in ordinary assistant use. Third, the audio must be high quality, so that the evaluation reflects voice-interface conditions rather than artifacts of synthetic audio. Instances that fail any criterion are rejected and are not included in the benchmark.

For each accepted conversation, annotators author instance-specific discrete rubrics that verify the expected post-interruption behavior for that particular context. The rubric is designed to define correct post-interruption behavior for the local conversational context, independent of any particular failure label. Using this rubric, annotators assign a binary pass/fail label for every model response; failed responses that reflect interruption-driven errors are then labeled using the benchmark failure taxonomy. As mentioned in the framework, we apply the same conversational context and interruption content when evaluating different responder models. All four models are tested on the target evaluation interaction and evaluated using the same conversation-specific rubric.

Each conversation is first screened independently by a rater for quality and validity. The same rater then constructs an instance-specific rubric and applies it, under blinded model identity, to evaluate the four corresponding model responses. After these annotations are completed, an expert reviewer audits the full set of annotations for that conversation to ensure consistency and overall quality.

Across all four models, MPR remains below 50%, indicating that state-update reasoning under mid-generation interruptions remains an open challenge for current full-duplex systems. The highest MPR is observed for Grok Voice Agent at 48.5%. MCP rankings follow the same order as MPR, though the percentage change between GPT-realtime and Amazon Nova Sonic 2 narrows approximately from 70% to 4%. Among failed conversations, 49.09% of GPT-realtime’s involve two or more unsatisfied rubric criteria, resulting in a lower MCP, compared with 42.86% for Nova, suggesting that GPT-realtime tends to fail in multiple ways after the interruption. Beyond aggregate performance, EchoChain reveals distinct failure profiles across models. Gemini Live’s failures proportionally concentrate in interruption amnesia and objective displacement, indicating that it either loses previously established constraints or abandons the original task entirely. Amazon Nova Sonic 2 exhibits the opposite pattern, with contextual inertia accounting for nearly half of its failures, suggesting it tends not to apply or misapply the interruption to its reasoning. GPT-realtime shows the most balanced distribution across the three categories, while Grok Voice Agent’s objective displacement is a notably low proportion, pointing to stronger retention of the original task objective when interrupted.

These results, combined with the paired half-duplex control where 40.22% of total failures became passes when the same content is delivered after a complete response, confirm that EchoChain captures a genuine full-duplex reasoning challenge rather than general task difficulty. The failure taxonomy provides actionable diagnostic signals, as contextual inertia, interruption amnesia, and objective displacement surface different weaknesses across models, offering targeted starting points for improving post-interruption reasoning. These findings establish a controlled, reproducible baseline for tracking progress on mid-generation state revision in full-duplex systems.

7
Limitations and Future Work

EchoChain is designed to maximize experimental control and cross-model comparability, and several scope constraints follow from this design choice. The benchmark evaluates only interruptions that deliver new task-relevant information during assistant generation, leaving other forms of full-duplex input, such as backchanneling, side-speech, and ambient noise, for future work. To ensure consistent timing across models, barge-in is scheduled at a fixed offset from detected speech onset rather than driven by human judgment, which improves reproducibility but means that some interruption points may be more conversationally natural than others. The evaluation relies on discrete, conversation-specific rubrics, which provide clear pass/fail determinations but may exclude edge cases where failure severity is ambiguous or where a more granular scoring scheme would be informative. Finally, the current release evaluates four closed-source full-duplex models and does not yet include open-weight alternatives, and the evaluation focuses on state-update reasoning rather than acoustic dimensions such as response timing or prosodic adaptation.

8
Conclusion

EchoChain presents a controlled benchmark for evaluating state-update reasoning in full-duplex voice models under mid-generation interruptions. The EchoChain framework standardizes acoustic conditions, barge-in timing, and conversational context across models, enabling direct cross-model comparison on a capability that existing benchmarks do not isolate. A diagnostic failure taxonomy classifies post-interruption breakdowns into contextual inertia, interruption amnesia, and objective displacement. The paired half-duplex control further indicates that a substantial share of observed failures are driven by the interruption event rather than task difficulty alone. Across 200 interrupted conversations, no evaluated model exceeds a 50% pass rate. As full-duplex voice interaction becomes more common, the ability to revise in-progress reasoning under concurrent input will become a baseline expectation rather than an edge case. EchoChain provides a method to quantify model performance as it improves in this kind of reasoning.
