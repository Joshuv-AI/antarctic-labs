Conduction-Diffusion in N N -Dimensional settings as irreversible port-Hamiltonian systems footnoteinfo This work has been achieved in the frame of the EIPHI Graduate school (contract “ANR-17-EURE-0002”) and ANID funded projects CIA250006 and FONDECYT 1231896.
Luis Mora, Yann Le Gorrec, Hector Ramirez, Denis Matignon

Abstract
This work extends previous 1 ​ D 1D irreversible port-Hamiltonian system (IPHS) formulations to boundary-controlled N ​ D ND distributed parameter systems describing conduction–diffusion fluid phenomena. Within a unified and thermodynamically consistent framework, we show that conduction and diffusion can be represented through a single coherent structure that preserves global energy balance and ensures a correct characterization of entropy production. The resulting formulation provides a foundation for the systematic modeling and control of complex multi-physical processes governed by coupled transport mechanisms in N N -dimensions. In the longer term, this framework opens the door to structure-preserving numerical schemes capable of enforcing thermodynamic principles directly at the discretized level.

Introduction

Thermodynamic modeling of fluids plays a central role in predicting and analyzing physical and chemical behavior under variations of pressure, temperature, and composition
(
Bird et al. 2006
)
. Such modeling relies on energy-based constitutive laws, equations of state, and transport relations that characterize phase behavior, density, internal energy, entropy production, and reactive transformations
(
De Groot and Mazur 1962
)
. Accurate thermodynamic representations are essential for describing a wide range of engineering processes
(
Kjelstrup et al. 2017
)
, including combustion, reactive mixing, mineral dissolution, aerothermal flows, and multi-phase transport, where both the first and second laws of thermodynamics constrain the admissible system dynamics. In recent years, the need for thermodynamically consistent models has intensified, driven by modern applications where energy fluxes, irreversible phenomena, and multi-physics couplings dominate system behavior
(
Dubljevic 2022
)
.
A promising approach to address these challenges builds on the Port-Hamiltonian system (PHS) framework
(
Duindam et al. 2009
)
, originally developed as an extension of classical Hamiltonian mechanics to open systems interacting with their environment. PHS formulations encode the interconnections between energy storage and power exchange through a skew symmetric geometric structure and power conjugated port variables. These properties make PHS particularly suitable for the modular modeling of complex multi domain systems, where mechanical, thermal, and chemical phenomena are tightly coupled. Generalizations to distributed parameter settings
(
Le Gorrec et al. 2005
;
Rashad et al. 2020
)
have introduced boundary port variables derived from the co-states, enabling the representation of open fluid flows, heat transfer mechanisms, reactive mass fluxes, and fluid-structure interactions.
Within fluid mechanics, PHS and pseudo PHS formulations have been proposed for various models, including inviscid flows, shallow water dynamics, and incompressible Navier-Stokes systems. More recently, energy based descriptions have been extended to compressible and reactive fluids, providing coherent representations of the energy fluxes that govern thermodynamic behavior
(
Califano et al. 2022
;
Mora et al. 2021
)
. These extensions are particularly relevant because modern computational fluid dynamics methods often require artificial corrections to approximate physical energy fluxes or struggle with nonphysical instabilities when thermodynamic consistency is not explicitly enforced. A formulation that inherently incorporates energy flux, such as the PHS framework, offers a systematic alternative that naturally aligns modeling, simulation, and control design
(
Cardoso-Ribeiro et al. 2024
)
.
A further development in this context is the Irreversible Port-Hamiltonian Systems (IPHS) framework, originally introduced in
(
Ramirez et al. 2013a
;
Ramirez et al. 2013b
)
and extended to 1D dimensional non-convective distributed parameter systems in
(
Ramirez et al. 2022
;
Ramirez and Le Gorrec 2022
)
. IPHS generalizes traditional PHS to explicitly incorporate irreversible thermodynamics. Unlike classical PHS, IPHS formulations use the total energy as generating function while embedding the entropy balance directly within the state evolution, thereby guaranteeing consistency with both the first and second laws of thermodynamics. The resulting structure, a modulated skew symmetric operator, captures not only system topology but also the irreversible thermodynamic forces induced by viscosity, heat conduction, diffusion, and chemical reactions. These features make IPHS particularly appealing for passivity based and energy shaping control strategies
(
Ortega et al. 2001
)
, including IDA-PBC approaches
(
Ramirez et al. 2016
)
, which directly exploit the energetic structure for feedback design.
Motivated by the examples in
(
Ramirez and Le Gorrec 2016
)
, and building upon the generalization of IPHS to
ND
systems provided in
(
Mora et al. 2023
)
, this work extends the 1D IPHS formulations of
(
Ramirez et al. 2022
)
to boundary controlled
ND
distributed parameter systems for conduction-diffusion fluid phenomena within a unified, thermodynamically consistent framework. This extension integrates conduction, diffusion and reactive transport into a single coherent formalism that preserves energy balance and entropy production.

The

Consider the heat conduction over an
N

The conserved quantity is the density of internal energy and the state reduces to a unique variable. Choose the internal energy density
u=u(s)

U(s)=\int_{\Omega}u\,\mathrm{d}z

(10)

\lambda

Alternatively, the heat conduction can also be written in terms of the entropy flux

\bm{f}_{s}:=\frac{1}{T}\,\bm{f}_{Q}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial s}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{T}\,\operatorname{div}(T\,\bm{f}_{s})=-\operatorname{div}(\bm{f}_{s})-\frac{1}{T}\,\mathbf{grad}(T)\cdot\bm{f}_{s}

, and the boundary term can be decomposed into
u_{s\partial}=-\bm{f}_{Q}\cdot\bm{n}
, the incoming normal component of the heat flux, and
y_{s\partial}=\beta:=\frac{1}{T}
, the reciprocal temperature.

Proof.

From the last line,

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}s\,\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\Gamma}g_{s}\bm{r}_{s}\cdot\bm{n}\,T\,\mathrm{d}\gamma\,+\,\int_{\Omega}[\bm{r}_{c}\cdot\mathbf{grad}(\frac{\partial u}{\partial c})+g_{s}\bm{r}_{s}\cdot\mathbf{grad}(\frac{\partial u}{\partial s})]\,\mathrm{d}z
,
and upon substitution,
\bm{r}_{c}\cdot\mathbf{grad}(\mu)=\frac{d}{T}\|\mathbf{grad}(\mu)\|^{2}:=\sigma_{c}
first, and
g_{s}\bm{r}_{s}\cdot\mathbf{grad}(T)=\frac{\lambda}{T^{2}}\|\mathbf{grad}(T\|^{2}:=\sigma_{s}
second.
∎

With formulation (
20
) at hand, the first principle of Thermodynamics can be recovered in:

Lemma 6

(First law of Thermodynamics)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}U=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}u\,\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\Gamma}T\,\frac{\lambda}{T}\bm{n}\cdot\mathbf{grad}(T)+\mu\,d\bm{n}\cdot\mathbf{grad}(\mu)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma\,,

where the boundary term can be decomposed into two parts,
u_{\partial}^{s}=-\bm{f}_{s}\cdot\bm{n}
and
u_{\partial}^{n}=-\bm{f}_{c}\cdot\bm{n}
, and
y_{\partial}^{s}=T
and
y_{\partial}^{n}=\mu
, for instance.

Proof.

It is based on the chain rule
\partial_{t}u=\frac{\partial u}{\partial c}\,\partial_{t}c+\frac{\partial u}{\partial s}\,\partial_{t}s
.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}u\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial u}{\partial c}\frac{\partial u}{\partial s}\end{bmatrix}\,{\mathcal{J}}\,\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial u}{\partial c}\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial s}\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}(\mu\bm{r}_{c}T)+T\,\Psi(T)\,\mathrm{d}z\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}[\mu\,\bm{n}\cdot\bm{r}_{c}\,T+T\,g_{s}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{r}_{s}\,T]\,\mathrm{d}\gamma\,,\text{thanks to }\eqref{eq-TPsiT}\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}[\mu\,(d\bm{n}\cdot\mathbf{grad}(\mu))+T\,(\frac{\lambda}{T}\,\bm{n}\cdot\mathbf{grad}(T))]\,\mathrm{d}\gamma\,.

Indeed, since
{\mathcal{J}}
in (
20
) is formally skew symmetric, only boundary terms appear in the result.
∎

4.2
Processes with diffusion of
n
different species

In a similar way as in the diffusion of one species, we write the balance equations on the extensive variables of Thermodynamics, i.e. the
n
molar concentrations of the species involved in the diffusion process plus the entropy density, resulting in the
n+1
following equations:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial c_{1}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{div}(\bm{f}_{c_{1}})\,,

(21)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial c_{n}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{div}(\bm{f}_{c_{n}})\,,

(22)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial s}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{div}(\bm{f}_{s})+\sigma_{s}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{c_{i}}\,,

(23)

where thanks to Fick’s law,
\bm{f}_{c_{i}}:=-d_{i}\,\mathbf{grad}(\mu_{i})
, and
\sigma_{c_{i}}:=-\frac{1}{T}\bm{f}_{c_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{grad}(\mu_{i})=\frac{d_{i}}{T}\|\mathbf{grad}(\mu_{i})\|^{2}\geq 0
for the entropy production.

Let us now define
\bm{r}_{c_{i}}:=-\frac{1}{T}\bm{f}_{c_{i}}=+\frac{d_{i}}{T}\,\mathbf{grad}(\mu_{i})
, the modulating function of the mass diffusion of the
i
-th species, then we can compute
-\operatorname{div}(\bm{f}_{c_{i}})=+\operatorname{div}(\bm{r}_{c_{i}}\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial s})
, and
\sigma_{c_{i}}=\bm{r}_{c_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{grad}(\frac{\partial u}{\partial{c_{i}}})
. Hence, in more compact notations, the following structure has been obtained for (
21
)-(
23
):

\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial c_{1}}{\partial t}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{\partial c_{n}}{\partial t}\\
\frac{\partial s}{\partial t}\end{bmatrix}={\mathcal{J}}_{\text{glob}}\,\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial u}{\partial c_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial c_{n}}\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial s}\end{bmatrix}\,,

(24)

where

{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{glob}}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&\cdots&0&\operatorname{div}(\bm{r}_{c_{1}}\cdot)\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&\operatorname{div}(\bm{r}_{c_{n}}\cdot)\\
[\bm{r}_{c_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{grad}(\cdot)]&\cdots&[\bm{r}_{c_{n}}\cdot\mathbf{grad}(\cdot)]&\Psi(\cdot)\end{bmatrix}.

(25)

We have already identified in Proposition
3.1
that the unbounded operator
\Psi
in (
14
) is formally skew symmetric. Moreover, it is also a classical computation to check that the adjoint of
T\mapsto\operatorname{div}(\bm{r}_{c_{i}}T)
is
\mu\mapsto-\bm{r}_{c_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{grad}(\mu)
; hence the global operator
{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{glob}}
in (
25
) is formally skew symmetric.

With formulation (
25
) at hand, the second principle of Thermodynamics can be recovered in:

Lemma 7

(Second law of Thermodynamics)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}S=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}s\,\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\Omega}(\sigma_{s}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{c_{i}})\,\mathrm{d}z+\int_{\Gamma}g_{s}\bm{r}_{s}\cdot\bm{n}\,(\delta_{s}U)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma\,,

where the volumic term is the total entropy creation
\sigma_{s}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{c_{i}}\geq 0
, and the boundary term can be decomposed into
u_{s\partial}=-\bm{f}_{Q}\cdot\bm{n}
, the incoming normal component of the heat flux, and
y_{s\partial}=\beta:=\frac{1}{T}
, the reciprocal temperature.

Proof.

The proof is very similar to that of Lemma
5
, summing on
i
between
1
and
n
.
∎

With formulation (
25
) at hand, the first principle of Thermodynamics can be recovered in:

Lemma 8

(First law of Thermodynamics)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}U

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}u\,\mathrm{d}z\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}T\,(\frac{\lambda}{T}\bm{n}\cdot\mathbf{grad}(T))+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\,(d_{i}\bm{n}\cdot\mathbf{grad}(\mu_{i}))\,\mathrm{d}\gamma\,,

where the boundary term can be decomposed into
1+n
terms,
u_{\partial}^{s}=-\bm{f}_{s}\cdot\bm{n}
and
u_{\partial}^{c_{i}}=-\bm{f}_{c_{i}}\cdot\bm{n}
for
1\leq i\leq n
, and
y_{\partial}^{s}=T
and
y_{\partial}^{c_{i}}=\mu_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
, for instance.

Proof.

The proof is very similar to that of Lemma
6
, summing on
i
between
1
and
n
.
∎

Let us enlighten the structure which has been obtained above through a factorization result that happens to be very meaningful from the thermodynamical point of view.

Proposition 4.1

The operator
{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{glob}}
in (
25
) can be factorized in the following way. Let

R_{1}:\vartheta\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}\bm{r}_{c_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{r}_{c_{n}}\end{bmatrix}\,\vartheta\,,

and let

{\mathcal{G}}_{1}:\begin{bmatrix}\bm{r}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{r}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{div}(\bm{r}_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\operatorname{div}(\bm{r}_{n})\end{bmatrix}\,,

(26)

then

{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{glob}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&&{\mathcal{G}}_{1}\,R_{1}\\
-R_{1}^{*}\,{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{*}&&\Psi(\cdot)\end{bmatrix}\,.

Proof.

The identification of the formal adjoints gives the structure. Indeed,

{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{*}:\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}\\
\vdots\\
d_{n}\end{bmatrix}\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}-\mathbf{grad}(d_{1})\\
\vdots\\
-\mathbf{grad}(d_{n})\end{bmatrix}\,,

and

R_{1}^{*}:\begin{bmatrix}\bm{g}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{g}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\mapsto\bm{r}_{c_{1}}\cdot\bm{g}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{r}_{c_{n}}\cdot\bm{g}_{n}\,.

∎

Proposition 4.2

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{bmatrix}x(t,z)\\

\frac{\delta H}{\delta s}(t,z)\end{bmatrix}

(27)

where
P_{0}=-P_{0}^{\top}
,
{\mathcal{J}}_{1}=-{\mathcal{J}}_{1}^{*}
and

is a first-order differential operator.

Proof.

5
Conclusion and Future work

The 1D IPHS formulations of
(
Ramirez et al. 2022
)
has been extended to boundary-controlled
ND
distributed parameter systems describing conduction–diffusion fluid phenomena. Within a unified and thermodynamically consistent framework, it has been showed that conduction, diffusion, and reactive transport can be integrated into a single coherent structure that preserves both global energy balance and the proper representation of entropy production. This extension provides a basis for systematic modeling and control of complex multi-physical processes governed by coupled transport mechanisms.

As future work, the inclusion of anisotropy should be done both for conduction and diffusions: in this case, the strictly positive scalar parameters
\lambda
in Fourier’s law and
d_{i}
in Fick’s law will become positive-definite symmetric tensors. Then, the example of a reaction-diffusion process in
ND
should be carried out, based on the 1-D example. The interest of such IPHS structured formulations for numerical simulations should be seen as a longer term perspective, see e.g.
(
Cardoso-Ribeiro et al. 2024
)
: indeed, structure-preserving numerical methods, such as the Partitioned Finite Element Method (PFEM) introduced in
(
Cardoso-Ribeiro et al. 2020
)
, should be able to guarantee both the Thermodynamics principles at the discrete level.
