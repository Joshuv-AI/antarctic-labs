Native-valve Staphylococcus epidermidis endocarditis complicated by destructive pseudoaneurysm of the mitral-aortic intervalvular fibrosa: a case report and review of the literature

Introduction

The mitral–aortic intervalvular fibrosa (MAIVF) is a thin, avascular fibrous structure forming the anatomic continuity between the noncoronary and left coronary cusps of the aortic valve and the anterior mitral leaflet.1 Owing to its limited vascular supply, the MAIVF is particularly susceptible to extension of infection from adjacent left-sided valves during infective endocarditis (IE).2 Periannular involvement of the MAIVF is uncommon but represents one of the most severe complications of IE, manifesting as abscess formation, pseudoaneurysm, or fistulization, with attendant risks of rupture, haemodynamic compromise, and systemic embolization.2,3

Destructive MAIVF involvement is classically associated with highly virulent organisms, most notably Staphylococcus aureus and streptococcal species, and is frequently described in patients with prior aortic valve surgery or prosthetic valve material.3 In contrast, CoNS—particularly Staphylococcus epidermidis—are traditionally regarded as low-virulence skin commensals and are often dismissed as blood culture contaminants. However, accumulating evidence from large multicentre registries indicates that CoNS can cause clinically significant native-valve IE (NVE) with extensive valvular destruction and periannular complications.4

We report a rare case of native-valve S. epidermidis endocarditis complicated by destructive MAIVF pseudoaneurysm in the absence of prosthetic material, underscoring the underappreciated virulence of this organism and the critical importance of early transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) in patients with CoNS bacteraemia.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old man with a history of hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, and recent right knee cellulitis presented to an outside hospital with recurrent syncopal episodes. Blood cultures obtained on admission grew S. epidermidis. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated a large mobile mass on the aortic valve without apparent mitral valve pathology. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain revealed multiple small acute infarcts in the left middle cerebral artery and right cerebellar territories, consistent with septic embolization. The patient was transferred to our institution for further evaluation.

On arrival, physical examination revealed a grade II/VI systolic ejection murmur, diminished breath sounds at the lung bases, and healed abrasions on both knees without evidence of peripheral stigmata of IE. Laboratory studies were significant for an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (74 mm/hr) and C-reactive protein (91.8 mg/L). Intravenous nafcillin was initiated for treatment of methicillin-susceptible S. epidermidis endocarditis.

TEE performed at our institution revealed findings substantially more extensive than those suggested by the initial TTE (Figure 1). A heavily calcified bicuspid aortic valve was identified with severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe aortic regurgitation. A 2.5-cm broad-based homogeneous mass was visualized adjacent to the posterior aspect of the aortic valve annulus, with extension into the left atrium. This lesion demonstrated systolic expansion and diastolic collapse with colour Doppler evidence of flow communication with the left ventricular outflow tract, consistent with pseudoaneurysm formation in the MAIVF region. Additionally, a posterior periannular abscess involving approximately half of the aortic annulus circumference was identified. The mitral valve, which had appeared normal on TTE, demonstrated a probable vegetation associated with a 1.4 × 1.0 cm thrombus in a supravalvular position above the mitral annulus, with associated mild mitral regurgitation. A moderate pericardial effusion was present with fibrinous material and loculations, suggestive of pericardial involvement. Flow reversal in the descending thoracic aorta was noted, consistent with significant aortic regurgitation; however, the pattern suggested a component of chronicity, raising concern for a prolonged subclinical infectious process.

Repeat MRI of the brain demonstrated additional foci of restricted diffusion in the left medial temporal lobe and left anterior frontal lobe consistent with interval embolic infarcts (Figure 2). Computed tomography of the chest revealed moderate-to-large bilateral pleural effusions. Blood cultures obtained at our facility remained negative, likely reflecting the prior initiation of appropriate antimicrobial therapy.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain. Diffusion-weighted imaging demonstrating foci of restricted diffusion in the left medial temporal lobe and left anterior frontal lobe (arrows), consistent with acute embolic infarcts secondary to septic embolization.

Despite aggressive diuretic therapy for the pleural effusions, the patient developed progressive respiratory failure requiring endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation. Cardiothoracic surgery evaluated the patient and determined that operative intervention carried prohibitive risk given the patient's haemodynamic instability, extensive periannular destruction, recent embolic strokes, and overall clinical trajectory. After multidisciplinary discussion with the patient's family, the decision was made to transition to comfort-focused care. The patient died shortly thereafter.

Discussion

Although S. epidermidis is traditionally regarded as a low-virulence skin commensal and frequent blood culture contaminant, increasing evidence supports its role as a true pathogen in NVE. Early series characterized S. epidermidis NVE as uncommon and often indolent, potentially delaying diagnosis, yet capable of significant valvular destruction requiring surgery.5 Data from the GAMES registry, encompassing 842 coagulase-negative staphylococcal IE cases across 44 centres, demonstrate that S. epidermidis accounts for 77% of CoNS IE and is associated with substantial morbidity, including periannular abscess in 29% and surgical intervention in 60%.4

Virulence is mediated by biofilm formation—facilitated by polysaccharide intercellular adhesin and accumulation-associated protein—which promotes adherence to damaged endocardium and resistance to host defences and antimicrobials.6 Phenol-soluble modulins and other secreted factors further contribute to tissue injury and inflammation.6 Although methicillin-resistant strains possess additional virulence determinants, these mechanisms can clearly drive aggressive native-valve disease even in methicillin-susceptible isolates. The patient’s bicuspid, heavily calcified aortic valve likely created a substrate for bacterial adherence and invasive infection.7

Pseudoaneurysm of the MAIVF is a rare but devastating complication of IE due to the region’s avascularity and susceptibility to contiguous spread.2 Prior reviews identified S. aureus and streptococci as predominant pathogens, with no reported CoNS-associated cases.3 To our knowledge, only one prior report describes S. epidermidis IE complicated by MAIVF pseudoaneurysm, occurring postoperatively in the presence of prosthetic material.8 Our patient's case appears to represent one of the first reported instances of native-valve S. epidermidis endocarditis complicated by MAIVF pseudoaneurysm in the absence of any prosthetic material or prior cardiac surgery, expanding the recognized pathogenic potential of this organism.

This case underscores the superior sensitivity of TEE compared with TTE for detecting periannular complications of IE. Although initial TTE suggested isolated aortic valve involvement, TEE identified MAIVF pseudoaneurysm, posterior annular abscess, and a large mitral vegetation with thrombus—findings not visualized on transthoracic imaging. This aligns with prior data showing TTE sensitivity of 28%–50% for periannular abscess vs. 78%–90% for TEE.9 The posterior location and complex anatomy of the MAIVF further limit TTE visualization. Accordingly, current guidelines recommend TEE when IE is suspected or clinical concern persists.10

The VIRSTA score, developed to identify patients with S. aureus bacteraemia at high risk for IE and guide TEE use, has demonstrated excellent sensitivity (98.9%–100%) and negative predictive value (98.8%–99.5%) for ruling out endocarditis.11 While this tool was designed and validated specifically for S. aureus bacteraemia, our case illustrates that similar risk stratification approaches may be worthy of future investigation in CoNS bacteraemia, although such applications remain hypothetical and unvalidated.

Our patient had multiple high-risk features that would have prompted early TEE, highlighting the potential utility of a modified VIRSTA calculator for CoNS bacteraemia (Summary figure). Other factors to consider that have been well documented in the literature to predispose to endocarditis include persistent bacteraemia,12 bicuspid aortic valve,13 presentation with altered mental status/syncope,14 or ekg changes.15

Given the demonstrated capacity of CoNS to cause aggressive native-valve disease with periannular complications, we propose that clinicians maintain a high index of suspicion for complicated IE in patients with CoNS bacteraemia, particularly those with pre-existing valvular abnormalities, persistent bacteraemia, or embolic events. Early TEE should be strongly considered in such patients, as TTE may significantly underestimate disease extent.
