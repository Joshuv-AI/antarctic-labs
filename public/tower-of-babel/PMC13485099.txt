Impact of contrast sensitivity on driving behaviors in individuals with moderate-to-severe glaucoma
Authors: Navdeep Kaur, Parul Ichhpujani, Suresh Kumar, Varnika A Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:This study aimed to investigate the relationship between contrast sensitivity (CS) and driving performance in individuals with moderate to severe glaucoma, using the Pelli–Robson (PR) chart and Spaeth/Richman Contrast Sensitivity (SPARCS) test.Design:Cross-sectional observational study.Methods: Setting:A hospital-based study was conducted in a multi-specialty tertiary care institute.Study Population:Forty-five non-professional drivers (90 eyes) diagnosed with moderate-to-severe glaucoma using Hodapp–Anderson–Parrish classification. Mean age (± standard deviation, in years) was 61.76 ± 10.02 with a significant male preponderance (97.8%).Observation Procedures:Participants underwent a comprehensive clinical assessment, and CS was assessed using both the PR chart and SPARCS. Driving-related parameters were evaluated using the Driving Habits Questionnaire, which assessed driving difficulty, driving space, and dependency on others. Statistical analyses were performed to identify correlations between CS scores and driving parameters.Primary Outcome:Patients with lower peripheral contrast experienced significant driving-related difficulties.Results:Significant correlations were observed between CS and driving behaviors in the worse eye. Lower peripheral SPARCS scores (particularly upper and lower right quadrants; ρ = 0.35–0.51, P < 0.05) were associated with reduced driving space (ρ = 0.44–0.50) and increased dependency (ρ = −0.32) and lower right scores (ρ = −0.32, P = 0.035). Driving difficulty scores showed positive correlations with SPARCS upper right quadrant (ρ = 0.34, P = 0.023) and lower right quadrant scores (ρ = 0.51, P < 0.001). Driving space scores were positively correlated with PR (ρ = 0.33, P = 0.027) and total SPARCS scores.Conclusion:CS, especially in peripheral regions, is significantly associated with driving difficulties, reduced driving space, and dependency on others in individuals with glaucoma. The worse eye’s CS measures have a stronger correlation with these driving-related factors.Clinical Implications:SPARCS correlates with structure and behavior, making it a holistic functional biomarker. Peripheral CS loss predicts functional self-regulation even before driving cessation. CS assessment could enhance the understanding of driving capabilities and safety, guide interventions, and driving restrictions more effectively to improve patient safety on the road.

Contrast sensitivity (CS) is a crucial aspect of visual function that significantly impacts daily activities, including driving. Unlike visual acuity (VA), which measures the ability to discern fine details under optimal conditions, CS assesses the ability to detect subtle differences in lightness and darkness between an object and its background.[1] This capability is particularly vital for navigating complex and variable driving environments, where detecting low-contrast objects and recognising visual cues can influence driving safety and performance.

Glaucoma, a chronic and progressive eye disease characterized by damage to the optic nerve, often results in a gradual loss of peripheral vision and can impair CS. Its global age-adjusted prevalence is 3–5% among individuals aged 40 and older.[2] At present, there are 80 million individuals with glaucoma worldwide, and this number is anticipated to grow to more than 111 million by 2040.[3] These numbers make it reasonable to infer that the probability of on-road drivers having this disease is high.

The International Council of Ophthalmology suggests that three vision functions should mainly be assessed for driving license procurement, namely VA, visual field (VF), and CS.[4] The suggested criteria include a VA of at least 6/12 in both eyes, a binocular VF of 120° horizontally, and a vertical field extending 20° above and below fixation (totaling 40°).[4] Meanwhile, there is no set criterion mentioned for CS assessment.

Despite the importance of CS in driving, traditional assessment methods often rely on chart-based tests (like Pelli–Robson [PR] chart) that measure only central CS and are subject to various limitations, such as memorisation effects, surface reflections, and uneven illumination. The Spaeth/Richman Contrast Sensitivity (SPARCS) test is an internet-based, inexpensive test that overcomes these drawbacks and measures both central and peripheral contrast thresholds of an individual.[5] SPARCS is grating based, online test with no issues pertaining to chart fading and is not dependent on the literacy of the patient, therefore it is ideal for a heterogeneous, multilingual population with variable literacy such as ours.

However, despite its recognised importance, CS testing remains underutilised in glaucoma care and rarely integrated into assessments of driving fitness, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. This study aims to bridge that gap by exploring the relationship between CS (central as well as peripheral) and driving in individuals with moderate-to-advanced glaucoma using SPARCS, PR chart, and Driving Habits Questionnaire (DHQ) in the Indian population.

Methods

This hospital-based cross-sectional analytical study was conducted at a multi-specialty tertiary care institute in North India that enrolled 45 patients with moderate-to-severe glaucoma as per Hodapp–Anderson–Parrish classification. The study was registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI) prior to enrolment of the first participant (CTRI/2023/10/059228). The study was conducted in congruence with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The study population included non-professional drivers with a valid driving license who had been driving for at least 1 year and had best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) ≥ 6/12 (20/40) or better, which likely excluded individuals who had stopped driving because of severe visual impairment.

Patients with other ocular diseases, such as visually significant cataract, active ocular surface disease, disorders affecting the retina like diabetic retinopathy, age-related macular degeneration, and diseases that could cause VF defects other than glaucoma, were excluded. Patients with any medical condition that prohibited them from giving reliable and valid data (e.g., cognitive impairment, neurological, or musculoskeletal disease) were also not considered.

Sample size calculation

Based on a study by Ayala, who reported the proportion of subjects with failure as 50%.[6]

Proportion of subjects with failure: P = 0.50 (50%) and precision: δ = 0.15 (15%)

Type I error: α = 0.05 (5%), = 1.96

Based on the values given above sample size required:

N = [1.96² × 0.50 × (1–0.50)]/0.15² = 42.68 ≈ 43 patients. We enrolled 45 patients.

Clinical assessment

All patients underwent a detailed clinical examination that included VA assessment, CS assessment using PR chart and SPARCS, slit lamp examination, +90 D fundus examination, intraocular pressure measurement using calibrated Goldmann applanation tonometer, gonioscopy (using a four mirror Zeiss goniolens; Modified Schaffer’s grading), VF testing using Humphrey Field analyser (HFA 860III) (Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA) monocularly by Swedish interactive thresholding algorithm (SITA) Fast 24-2 strategy, Spectral Domain Optical coherence tomography (CIRRUS 500, Carl Zeiss). A DHQ, was administered after taking a written, informed consent prior to enrolment. VA values were converted to the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution scale for statistical analysis.

SPARCS was accessed via https://www.sparcscontrastcenter.com where each patient got a unique identification number.

Standard automated perimetry with the Humphrey’s field analyzer

All subjects underwent standard automated perimetry on the Humphrey’s field analyzer (HFA) 860 III. The VF was considered satisfactory if false positive and false negative errors did not exceed 33% and fixation errors did not exceed 25%.

Driving habits questionnaire

The DHQ designed by Owsley et al.[7] was used to obtain information about driving during the past 1 year. The DHQ was designed to be interviewer-administered, and it addressed six domains.

Current driving status and miscellaneous issues: Items 1–10 established current driving status, general driving practices (e.g., spectacle and seatbelt use, driving speed), and self-assessed quality of driving.

Driving exposure: Items 11–14 asked about the average number of days driven per week and where the respondent drives in a typical week. The latter generated an estimate of the number of places travelled to, number of trips made, and number of miles driven in a typical week.

Dependence on other drivers: Items 15 and 16 provide a detailed assessment of who the respondent travels with in a car on a regular basis and who usually drives with that person. From this interview, an estimate of “dependency” on other drivers was generated, which ranged from 1 to 3, with higher scores meaning greater levels of dependency on others to drive.

Driving difficulty: Items 17–24 asked respondents to rate the degree of visual difficulty experienced in specific driving situations. Ratings were made on a 5-point scale (5 = no difficulty, 4 = a little difficulty, 3 = moderate difficulty, 2 = extreme difficulty, 1 = so difficult I no longer drive in that situation). A composite score of driving difficulty was computed based on the responses to all eight items and scaled on a 100-point scale [(mean score − 1) × 25]. Lower composite scores indicated a greater degree of difficulty.

Driving space: Items 29–34 addressed the distance respondents typically drove into their environment away from their home base over the past year (e.g., within the neighborhood, outside the state). Subjects answered “yes” = 1 or “no” = 0 as to whether they have driven to the designated region in the past year. A summary score of driving space was computed by summing scores across all items (0–6), where lower scores indicated a smaller driving space.

Self-reported crashes and citations: Items 25–28 asked respondents to report the number of crashes incurred and the number of citations received during the past year. Certain modifications were made in this questionnaire to make it suitable for our context. “miles” was changed to “kilometers”, “church” was changed to “religious place”, and “left-handed turns” was changed to “right-handed turns”.

Contrast sensitivity assessment

PR Test: This CS test uses a wall-mounted chart positioned 1 meter from the patient. It features large Sloan letters, each spanning about one cycle per degree of vision. The letters are grouped into triplets, with the contrast decreasing by 0.15 log units per triplet. The range of contrast tested varies from 100% to 0.56% (log CS 0.00–2.25).[8] Patients receive credit for a contrast level if they correctly identify two out of three letters in a triplet. CS is measured using PR scoring sheets, employing a “letter-by-letter” scoring system where each correctly identified letter scores 0.05 log units (except for the first triplet, which has 100% contrast). Testing concludes when the patient fails to identify two of the three letters in a triplet.

SPARCS Test: SPARCS is designed to be used on a monitor set to 1024 × 768 resolution, 256 grey levels, and a size of at least 22 cm width and 26.5 cm height. In our study, SPARCS was accessed via https://www.sparcscontrastcenter.com, where each patient got a unique identification number.[9] The website provided instructions on how to take the test [Fig. 1a]. The patient was seated 50 cm from the computer monitor. At this testing distance, the test occupied 30° of vision horizontally and 23.5° of vision vertically. The central test area subtends 5° horizontally and 3.5° vertically [Fig. 1b]. Patient was then instructed to fixate on the central area of the testing screen and identify which of the areas appears different. When the patient was ready, they clicked on the central area. To avoid learning effects, one demonstration and one practice trial were conducted before documenting the final SPARCS values.

SPARCS test screen: (a) Initial patient information screen with eye selection check-box. (b) Testing dimensions for the SPARCS test with a grating display in the upper right quadrant. (c) Result screen. SPARCS: Spaeth/Richman Contrast Sensitivity

Vertical square wave gratings with a spatial frequency of 0.4 cpd appear for 0.3 seconds in one of the five tested areas. Patients then temporarily break fixation to select the area. Subsequently, patients fixate again on the central area and click it to activate the program to show the next image. The area with the gratings appears at random. Correct and incorrect responses are recorded by SPARCS until the contrast threshold is determined in each area.

The contrast threshold is determined using a staircase strategy with reversals. Initial correct responses advance four levels until an incorrect response is made. After the incorrect response, the contrast level presented is two levels easier. Thereafter, the algorithm advances or regresses one level at a time until two incorrect responses are made at a specific level, which establishes the threshold. The test defines consecutive clicking on the same quadrant more than four times as a refusal to guess, in which case the test is automatically terminated, no score given, and an explanation provided before retesting. The range of contrast tested is from 100% to 0.45% (log CS 0.00–2.35) and decreases by approximately 0.15 log units between levels. The contrast value is calculated by the Weber contrast. The central area and four peripheral areas each receive separate scores.

The log-based score of each of the five testing areas is scaled out of 20, making a maximum SPARCS score of 100 [Fig. 1c].

SPARCS scores in individual areas can be converted to log CS using the equation:

Log CS score = (SPARCS score × 2.346353)/20.

Total SPARCS scores can be converted to log CS using the equation:

Log CS score = (SPARCS score × 2.346353)/100

Statistical analysis: Data from 45 patients were coded and recorded in an MS Excel spreadsheet program. SPSS v23 (IBM Corp.) was used for data analysis. The association between two categorical variables was explored using Chi-squared test. The association between variables where one is continuous, and one is categorical, was explored using an independent sample ‘t-test’ when the categorical variable had two categories. If data were found to be non-normally distributed, appropriate non-parametric tests in the form of the Wilcoxon Mann–Whitney U Test/Kruskal–Wallis test were used for these comparisons. Linear correlation between two continuous variables was explored using Pearson’s correlation (if the data were normally distributed) and Spearman’s correlation (for non-normally distributed data). Statistical significance was kept at P < 0.05.

Results

The mean age (years) was 61.76 ± 10.02, and the majority of the participants belonged to the age group 60–69 years (33.3%). The proportion of cases with moderate glaucoma was 42.2%, and that with severe glaucoma was 57.8%. Nearly two-third (71.1%) of the participants were diagnosed with primary open-angle glaucoma, 13.3% had primary angle closure glaucoma, 8.9% had secondary open-angle glaucoma, and the rest (6.7%) had normal tension glaucoma. There was a marked predominance of males (97.8%) in our study.

Although all participants were driving at the time this study was conducted, nearly half (22/45; 48.9%) had been advised in the preceding year to limit or stop driving, suggesting self-selection of functionally capable individuals who continue driving despite moderate-to-severe disease. Participants reported driving on average 4.3 days per week (4.31 ± 1.94; range: 1–7), covering about 83.64 ± 43.15 km weekly (range: 20–200), which corresponds to modest but regular driving exposure.

Driving behavior: Mean of dependency score was 1.96 ± 0.93 (range: 1-3), suggesting mild dependence on others for transport. Nearly a third (13/45) of the participants had difficulty driving in rush-hour traffic. Nearly half (20/45) of them faced difficulty in driving at night and in the rain, consistent with known challenges related to reduced contrast and glare sensitivity in glaucoma. Only one of the 45 participants was involved in an accident, implying either cautious self-restriction or underreporting. The mean driving difficulty score was 61.88 ± 18.43 (range: 0–100; lower score indicates greater difficulty). This indicates mild-to-moderate self-perceived difficulty among the cohort.

During the past year, all participants had travelled beyond their neighborhood. Around two-thirds (31/45; 68.9%) of the participants drove to their neighboring towns. One-fourth (12/45; 26.7%) of the participants drove to more distant towns, but only one (2.2%) participant drove outside the state. None of the participants had driven to places outside the northern region. The mean driving space score was 2.98 ± 0.84 (range: 0–6; lower score indicates smaller driving space). Together, these findings indicate self-regulation of driving habits, shorter distances, limited conditions, and avoidance of difficult settings as the disease severity increases.

CS findings: The mean PR score in the better eye was 2.08 ± 0.24 and 1.95 ± 0.27 in the worse eye. Meanwhile, the mean total SPARCS score was 66.58 ± 9.07 in the better eye and 58.51 ± 9.67 in the worse eye, confirming reduced CS in the worse eye. Around one-fourth of the participants (10/45; 3: moderate glaucoma and 7: severe glaucoma) had a peripheral SPARCS score of less than 10 (out of 20) in at least one quadrant. A single patient with severe disease had a central score of less than 10. This pattern aligns with the known peripheral predilection of glaucomatous damage. While some (7/45; 2: moderate glaucoma and 5: severe glaucoma) had lesser scores in both central and peripheral quadrants. Our study revealed a moderate positive correlation between total SPARCS and PR scores in the better eye (ρ = 0.54, P <0.001) and a strong positive correlation in the worse eye (ρ = 0.61, P <0.001), validating SPARCS as a reliable measure of functional CS comparable to PR.

Structure–function relationships: On correlation of functional parameters, HVF 24-2 and visual field index (VFI) showed positive correlation with each quadrant (upper left, lower left, center, upper right, and lower right) of SPARCS score (P <0.001) as well as PR score (P <0.001). Along with this, average retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness showed moderate positive correlations with SPARCS scores (upper left: P <0.001; lower left: P <0.001; center: P = 0.004; upper right: P <0.001; lower right: P <0.001) and PR score (P <0.001). Average macular ganglion cell complex (GCC) thickness also revealed moderate positive correlations with SPARCS scores (upper left: P <0.001; lower left: P = 0.003; center: P <0.001; upper right: P = 0.004; lower right: P <0.001) and PR score (P <0.001). Significant correlations between SPARCS quadrant scores and VF indices (mean deviation, VFI) reinforce the sensitivity of CS testing as a functional marker of glaucomatous damage [Table 1].

CS and driving performance: When linked to driving outcomes, lower SPARCS and PR scores, particularly in the worse eye, were associated with poorer driving performance.

Driving difficulty score: Participants reporting greater difficulty in night-time, rainy, or high-traffic driving (i.e., lower scores) had lower total and quadrant SPARCS scores, especially in the upper- and lower-right quadrants.

Dependency score: Those more dependent on others for transport (higher scores) showed reduced SPARCS sensitivity in the right-sided quadrants (significant negative correlations for UR and LR quadrants, ρ = –0.32, P ≈ 0.03–0.035), suggesting that right-side peripheral CS loss translated to greater dependence on others for mobility—an important quality-of-life indicator [Table 3].

Discussion

CS in the human visual system is primarily mediated by two parallel pathways—the magnocellular (MC) and parvocellular (PC) systems. The MC pathway, originating from parasol ganglion cells, is highly sensitive to luminance contrast and temporal frequency, enables the detection of subtle brightness differences and motion. Its function is typically assessed using low-spatial-frequency, flickering, or moving stimuli, such as those employed in frequency-doubling perimetry. The PC pathway, arising from midget ganglion cells, is more sensitive to color and high-spatial-frequency static patterns. It exhibits a relatively linear response with increasing contrast, contributing to a detailed perception of fine texture and form. Both pathways project through the lateral geniculate nucleus to the primary visual cortex (V1), where visual contrast information is integrated for spatial interpretation.[1011]

Previous research by McKendrick et al.[11] has shown that normal ageing results in reduced CS across both MC and PC channels, and glaucoma further exacerbates this decline without preferentially affecting one pathway over the other. Thus, CS loss in glaucoma reflects generalised impairment of visual information processing rather than selective cellular damage. Our study reinforces this concept by demonstrating a consistent reduction in CS across both PR and SPARCS measures, particularly in the worse eye. The strong positive correlation between PR and SPARCS scores (ρ = 0.61, P < 0.001) validates SPARCS as a reliable, practical alternative to the PR chart for glaucoma populations, consistent with prior findings by Thakur et al.[12] who reported a similar correlation (ρ = 0.80, P < 0.001). The ability of SPARCS to capture both central and peripheral CS confers a unique advantage in understanding functional loss patterns, as glaucoma often manifests initially in the peripheral VF.

Importantly, our results demonstrate that peripheral CS—especially in the upper and lower right quadrants of the worse eye—was significantly correlated with self-reported driving performance. Lower SPARCS scores in these quadrants were associated with higher driving difficulty (ρ = 0.34–0.51), smaller driving space (ρ = 0.44–0.50), and greater dependency on others (ρ = –0.32). These findings suggest that localized peripheral CS impairment contributes meaningfully to the real-world challenges faced by drivers with glaucoma. The right hemifield association may be particularly relevant in the Indian context, where right-sided oncoming traffic and overtaking lanes demand continuous visual scanning of that field.

Previous work by Haymes et al.[13] and Lee et al.[14] similarly reported that reduced CS is linked with poorer on-road driving performance and greater difficulty under low-contrast conditions such as night or rain. Moreover, the larger cross-sectional study of 153 drivers found that low-contrast peripheral simulation tasks were better discriminators of motor vehicle collision history than standard perimetry or VA, suggesting the real-world relevance of low-contrast functional tests.[15] Conversely, Kwon et al.[16] found that VF loss, rather than CS, predicted crash involvement. The divergence across studies may arise from methodological differences—particularly between self-reported versus performance-based driving assessments. Our findings bridge these perspectives by showing that even in active, licensed drivers with preserved VA (BCVA ≥ 6/12), subtle CS deficits correlate with perceived difficulty and self-imposed driving restriction, implying early functional compensation rather than frank disability.

Our quadrant-specific finding (upper right/lower right worse-eye CS) gains biological plausibility given the recent finding that CS correlates variably with hemifield VF defect location, supporting the hypothesis that localized CS impairment may map to region-specific functional performance.[17] The observed quadrant-specific effects also provide insight into adaptive driving strategies. Glaucomatous damage in superior or inferior VF regions may limit detection of road hazards or pedestrians, prompting compensatory head and eye movements. SPARCS-measured CS correlated with VF indices, RNFL, and GCC thickness, validating its link to disease severity, yet explained additional variance in driving performance not captured by traditional perimetry—indicating that CS tests reflect real-world functional integrity.

Nonetheless, the reliance on self-reported measures limits direct inference about behavioral performance. Future studies using driving simulators or on-road testing could validate whether these perceptual deficits translate into measurable performance impairments.

A recent systematic review of glaucoma and driving pointed out that despite consistent evidence of driving impairment in glaucoma, CS remains underutilised in driving-fitness assessments.[18] Real-world data as cited by Owsley et al.[19] (naturalistic crash data) and Jones et al.[20] (simulator hazard detection) show CS predicts crash risk and hazard detection better than high-contrast acuity, supporting the fact that CS matters for driving safety. Recent studies linking CS to driving performance are elucidated in Table 5. These recent reviews and studies cite the need for standardised, functionally relevant CS testing (including peripheral/mesopic tests), an unmet need that SPARCS addresses.

Our cohort’s predominantly male (97.8%) demographic profile, comprising only active drivers, reflects sociocultural driving patterns in India and introduces selection bias. The exclusion of individuals with advanced vision loss or those who had already ceased driving likely underestimates the true functional impact of CS loss in the broader glaucoma population. Despite this, the consistent associations between peripheral CS loss and restricted driving behavior underscore the importance of including CS testing in functional visual assessment.

Conclusion

Taken together, our results indicate that even among visually competent, active drivers with moderate-to-severe glaucoma, reduced peripheral CS—particularly in the worse eye—is associated with higher self-reported driving difficulty, smaller driving range, and greater dependence on others. These findings suggest that CS loss subtly affects confidence and performance under demanding visual conditions, prompting self-regulatory behaviors rather than abrupt driving cessation. Incorporating a comprehensive CS evaluation may enhance the assessment of driving fitness and help identify patients who could benefit from targeted visual rehabilitation or adaptive driving interventions.
