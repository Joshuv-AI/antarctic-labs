Application of Artificial Intelligence in the Diagnosis, Prediction, and Management of Metabolic Syndrome: A Systematic Review
Authors: Aynaz Esmailzadeh, Narges Norouzkhani, Sara Sezavar Dokhtfaroughi, Alireza Rasoulian, Mohammad Reza Mazaheri Habibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsMetabolic syndrome (MetS) is associated with increased risks of cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes, with recent global prevalence estimates of approximately 28%–31% in adults. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning offer potential to improve risk stratification beyond conventional statistical methods. This systematic review aims to synthesize evidence on AI applications for the diagnosis and prediction of MetS, while assessing the limited evidence on extensions for prevention and management.MethodsFollowing PRISMA and SAMPL guidelines, a systematic literature search was conducted in 2025 in PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar without date restrictions. Search terms included “artificial intelligence,” “machine learning,” “metabolic syndrome,” “diagnosis,” “prediction,” “prevention,” and “management.” Only English‐language original studies were included. Titles/abstracts and full texts were screened independently by two reviewers. Extracted data comprised study characteristics, sample sizes, AI algorithms, feature types, performance metrics, and key findings.ResultsOf 1178 identified records, 64 full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility; 12 studies (n = 12/64) met inclusion criteria. The majority addressed diagnostic or predictive tasks using algorithms such as support vector machines (SVMs), artificial neural networks (ANNs), k‐nearest neighbors (KNNs), and random forests (RFs). Most models utilized non‐invasive features and reported area under the curve (AUC) values ranging from 0.82 to 0.99. Evidence for long‐term management or behavioral prevention was present in only a minority of studies (n = 3/12) and remained largely exploratory. Across the reviewed literature, formal reporting of uncertainty measures (e.g., 95% confidence intervals) and model explainability techniques (e.g., SHAP, permutation importance) was consistently absent.ConclusionAI demonstrates promise for non‐invasive diagnosis and risk prediction of MetS. However, applications in prevention and personalized management remain underdeveloped. Realizing the full clinical potential of AI will require rigorous external validation, enhanced model explainability, and educational promotion to bridge the current implementation gap.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, diagnosis, machine learning, metabolic syndrome, prediction, systematic review

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is characterized by a cluster of interrelated metabolic abnormalities, including central obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia (often with elevated triglycerides and reduced high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL‐C]). It is widely recognized as a significant risk factor for the premature development and progression of cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. MetS is also frequently linked to non‐cardiometabolic conditions, such as hepatic steatosis, cholesterol gallstones, obstructive sleep apnea, gout, depression, and polycystic ovary syndrome [6].

The global prevalence of MetS is substantial and varies by diagnostic criteria (e.g., IDF, ATP III), age, sex, ethnicity, and region. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), approximately 25% of adults meet the criteria, though recent systematic analyses and epidemiological data indicate rates ranging from 20% to 45% in many populations, with a global average around 28%–31% in recent years [3, 7, 8]. Prevalence is particularly high in older adults (20%–30% or more in those aged 60 + ), contributing significantly to all‐cause mortality [9]. Projections suggest further increases due to ongoing lifestyle transitions and demographic shifts [8]. Alcohol consumption [10, 11, 12, 13, 14] and smoking [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20] further exacerbate MetS risk in overweight and obese individuals, a dynamic particularly evident in recent prospective evidence highlighting the complex associations between detailed body composition metrics and long‐term cardiometabolic outcomes [21].

Accurate and timely identification of at‐risk individuals is essential to enable early intervention and reduce the long‐term health and economic burden of MetS. Its multifactorial etiology, encompassing rapid lifestyle changes, socioeconomic disparities, and genetic predispositions [22, 23], poses challenges for traditional risk assessment tools, such as linear regression models or simple risk scores, which often struggle with nonlinear interactions, heterogeneous data sources, and complex multidimensional parameters.

In recent years, the rapid expansion of electronic health records (EHRs), wearable devices, genomic sequencing, and large‐scale clinical datasets has generated vast, heterogeneous data that exceed the analytical capacity of conventional statistical approaches. Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly machine learning (ML), has increasingly been proposed as a robust alternative to address these limitations by identifying complex, nonlinear patterns in high‐dimensional data [24, 25]. AI applications in healthcare have refined diagnostic pattern recognition (e.g., in imaging) [26, 27, 28] and predictive modeling, with algorithms such as artificial neural networks (ANN), decision trees (DT), support vector machines (SVM), and random forests (RF) showing potential in MetS contexts [29, 30, 31, 32]. These tools may also support prevention programs, clinical decision support, and individualized applications [33, 34, 35]. However, the interpretability of many “black‐box” models remains a key concern for clinical trust and adoption.

Recent studies have further demonstrated the potential of machine learning models to predict MetS using non‐invasive, easily measurable features. For instance, Choi et al. developed and validated an anthropometric and bioelectrical impedance‐based ML model that accurately identified pediatric MetS, suggesting the broader applicability of AI‐driven risk prediction frameworks in clinical settings [36]. Similarly, recent advancements in computational health have highlighted the shift towards personalized medicine; for instance, Clarós et al. proposed an AI‐based model to predict and prevent non‐communicable diseases specifically linked to MetS, emphasizing the necessity of patient‐centered AI interventions [37]. However, these advancements highlight the need for a systematic synthesis to determine if such performance is consistent across the literature. Despite these promising individual applications, the literature on AI in MetS remains fragmented, with substantial heterogeneity in algorithms, datasets, populations, diagnostic criteria, and reporting standards. Previous reviews have often focused on traditional statistical methods or limited aspects of diagnosis, leaving a critical gap in a comprehensive, systematic synthesis of AI applications across diagnosis and prediction in MetS. Moreover, few have systematically examined whether studies adequately address model explainability (e.g., via techniques such as SHAP or permutation importance) to facilitate clinical translation.

“This systematic review aims to synthesize the current evidence on the role of AI algorithms in the diagnosis and prediction of MetS, evaluating performance across diverse algorithmic approaches, feature types (invasive and non‐invasive), and populations.” By integrating available studies in a structured manner and highlighting methodological gaps, it seeks to clarify the current state of AI in MetS care and inform future directions for robust validation and implementation. To guide this synthesis, we developed a conceptual framework (Figure 1) illustrating the integration of diverse potential data sources, including clinical, imaging, genomic, biochemical, and lifestyle data, into machine learning models for the diagnosis, prediction, and personalized management of MetS, reflecting the broad scope of current and emerging AI applications analyzed in this review.

Methods

Study Design

This systematic review was conducted in 2025 in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement. The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 2). The review protocol was not prospectively registered, as this was an exploratory narrative synthesis rather than a formal meta‐analysis. The literature search was performed in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar using the keywords “artificial intelligence,” “prediction,” “diagnosis,” “treatment,” and “metabolic syndrome,” combined with appropriate Boolean operators and their corresponding MeSH terms where applicable. The detailed search strategies for each database are presented in Appendix A.

Diagram of article selection based on flowchart (PRISMA).

Eligibility Criteria

The primary research question was: What is the current evidence on the application of AI algorithms in the diagnosis, prediction, or management of MetS? Eligibility criteria included peer‐reviewed, English‐language original research articles that investigated the use of AI algorithms in the diagnosis, treatment, or prevention of MetS. Studies were required to report original empirical data on AI model performance (e.g., accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, specificity) in human participants. Full‐text access was required for inclusion.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) articles not available in English; (2) non–peer‐reviewed sources, including conference abstracts, books, review papers, letters, and editorial correspondence; (3) lack of conceptual alignment between the study objective and the title, abstract, or full text; (4) unavailable full‐text access; and (5) retracted publications (checked via PubMed retraction tags and Retraction Watch database prior to data extraction).

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Duplicate records were removed using EndNote version 21. Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts against the predefined eligibility criteria, with discrepancies resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer. Full‐text articles were then assessed for eligibility. Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized form that captured: study title, publication year, first author, country, study design, participant characteristics (e.g., age, gender, population type), dataset size, study objective, AI algorithms/techniques applied, validation methods, types of input features, variable importance approach (if reported, including any use of impurity‐based measures, permutation importance, or Shapley Additive Explanations [SHAP]), and performance metrics (e.g., accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, specificity). Where original studies reported hypothesis testing, the specific test, a priori significance level (typically α = 0.05, two‐sided), and any multiplicity adjustments were noted as described by the authors. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Data Synthesis

Extracted data were synthesized narratively, with findings summarized in tabular form (see Appendix B for a comprehensive summary table of the 12 included studies). Qualitative synthesis focused on the types of AI techniques employed, their reported performance, participant populations, and implications for MetS management. All analyses were prespecified as descriptive and narrative; no exploratory inferential statistical tests were conducted in this review. No formal meta‐analysis was performed due to heterogeneity in study designs, populations, outcomes, and reporting metrics. Reporting of performance metrics followed general principles outlined in the SAMPL guidelines [38] and the Guidelines for reporting of statistics for clinical research in urology [39], including emphasis on effect sizes, uncertainty measures (where available), and avoidance of over‐reliance on hypothesis testing alone. Data management and synthesis were conducted in Microsoft Excel, and narrative compilation was used; no specialized statistical software was required.

Transparency statement: The corresponding author had full access to all data and takes responsibility for the integrity of the data and the accuracy of the analysis.

Conceptual Framework for AI in MetS Management

To synthesize the findings of this review, we developed a conceptual framework (Figure 1) illustrating how diverse data sources clinical, imaging, genomic, biochemical, and lifestyle are integrated into machine learning models (e.g., ANN, LR, KNN, DT, RF, and SVM) to support diagnosis, prediction/risk stratification, prevention, and treatment/management of MetS. This framework highlights potential benefits, including increased diagnostic accuracy, early detection, personalized interventions, reduced disease burden, and improved patient outcomes, drawing directly on patterns observed across the included studies.

Risk of Bias and Applicability Assessment

The risk of bias and applicability of the included prediction models were systematically evaluated using the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool [40]. This validated instrument assessed four key domains: participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis. For each included study, two reviewers independently rated the risk of bias (low, high, or unclear) and applicability concerns in each domain, with disagreements resolved by consensus. The PROBAST assessment focused on aspects such as study design (e.g., retrospective vs. prospective), participant selection, measurement of predictors, outcome definition, sample size adequacy, handling of missing data, overfitting, and calibration. Results of the PROBAST evaluation are summarized in Appendix C, highlighting common sources of potential bias across the reviewed literature and their implications for the clinical applicability of AI models in MetS management.

Results

A total of 1178 records were identified. Following the initial screening of titles and abstracts, 64 articles were deemed eligible for full‐text review. After assessing the full texts, 33 articles were excluded, leaving 12 studies for the final analysis. One potentially eligible study [41] was excluded following a retraction check via the Retraction Watch database due to authorship integrity issues. The study selection process is illustrated in Figure 2 using the PRISMA flow diagram [42].

Study Features

Among the 12 included studies, five were conducted in China [35, 43, 44, 45, 46], three in Taiwan [30, 47, 48], and two in Iran [31, 34]. The remaining studies were conducted in Saudi Arabia [49] and Greece [50]. A comprehensive summary of the characteristics, methodology, and performance of these studies is provided in Appendix B.

The reviewed studies encompassed a range of study designs, including cross‐sectional, retrospective cohort, prospective cohort, and longitudinal cohort designs. Dataset sizes varied substantially, ranging from 124 participants in a genetic association study [30] to 27,415 participants in a large‐scale longitudinal cohort [48]. Most studies included adult populations of mixed sex, with participant characteristics varying according to study scope, such as general population samples, clinical cohorts, or high‐risk groups.

Regarding analytical approaches, the most frequently applied machine learning algorithms were SVMs [31, 34, 44, 45, 49, 50], logistic regression (LR) models [30, 44, 45, 46, 47, 49], DTs [31, 44, 45, 49], RF models [30, 44, 47, 49], k‐nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithms [44, 45, 49], and ANNs [30, 43, 44, 48].

Input features varied across studies and included non‐invasive anthropometric measures, clinical and laboratory variables, dietary intake data, lifestyle and socioeconomic indicators, and genetic single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Most studies focused on predicting or diagnosing MetS, with validation strategies primarily based on train–test splits, cross‐validation, or external validation, depending on data availability and study design.

Cai et al. (2024, China) identified age‐specific nutrient intake patterns associated with MetS using ML‐based classification applied to NHANES data [35]. While performance metrics were reported via age‐specific nutrient–MetS associations, the study did not explicitly report a specific variable importance method (e.g., SHAP or Permutation Importance).

Chen et al. (2014, China) developed an ANN model to identify MetS risk in a cohort of 2074 staff members of the Lanzhou Grid Company using only physical signs [43]. No biochemical indices were utilized, and formal variable importance methods were not reported. The ANN model outperformed PCLR, achieving a sensitivity of 0.8843, specificity of 0.8367, PPV of 0.5661, NPV of 0.9677, and an AUC of 0.9043, compared to PCLR's AUC of 0.8873.

Xu et al. (2023, China) applied a panel of seven supervised ML classifiers to non‐invasive variables from 9171 adults undergoing routine physical examinations [44]. Variable importance analysis was not reported in this screening‐focused study. In external validation, the ANN performed best, with a sensitivity of 71.98%, specificity of 99.38%, AUC of 0.989, and overall classification accuracy of 0.956, supporting its role as a low‐cost screening tool.

Duan et al. (2024, China) proposed a GP‐CCBLS model for MetS diagnosis in 1849 adults using basic, non‐invasive clinical and demographic risk factors [45]. Despite the complexity of the GP‐CCBLS framework, the authors did not provide a formal ranking of feature importance. The model achieved an accuracy of 0.8054, sensitivity of 0.9189, specificity of 0.6351, precision of 0.7907, and AUC of 0.7770, indicating a reliable and user‐friendly early‐diagnosis framework.

Yu et al. (2021, Taiwan) evaluated several ML algorithms across different MetS definitions (ATPIII, JIS, NHLBI, and IDF) [47]. The RF model under the IDF criterion provided the best results, with an accuracy of 0.947, sensitivity of 0.571, specificity of 0.985, and AUC of 0.921. Although RF inherently provides feature ranking, specific variable importance scores or rankings were not reported for the different diagnostic criteria.

Sghaireen et al. (2022, Saudi Arabia) examined ten ML algorithms in a dataset of 12,012 adults to reduce the cost of predictive diagnosis [49]. While the study used metaheuristic feature selection (e.g., GA and Bat algorithms) to optimize the models, it did not report post‐hoc explainability metrics such as SHAP values. The best average accuracy was 0.7814, with optimized KNN reaching 0.9449, illustrating the benefit of algorithm tuning for cost‐efficient prediction.

Panagoulias et al. (2022, Greece) developed a cascaded SVM‐based system to predict MetS and its defining factors in an adult clinical cohort [50]. The study's focus was on architecture rather than feature contribution analysis, and no variable importance method was reported. The system yielded an average accuracy of approximately 84% for MetS prediction and 74% for systolic blood pressure classification.

Across the included studies, point estimates of model performance (AUC and Accuracy) were predominantly reported; however, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were not explicitly provided in the majority of the original publications. Furthermore, no formal hypothesis testing with p‐values was reported for direct model comparisons within these studies, as performance evaluations remained primarily descriptive.

Hsu et al. (2022, Taiwan) investigated the association between circadian genes and MetS in a small cohort of 124 Taiwanese adults. Using a feature selection approach on significant SNPs, they compared four models (LR, RF, AdaBoost, and NN) [30]. The Neural Network (NN) model with feature selection achieved the best overall performance with an AUC of 0.85, sensitivity of 0.699, and specificity of 0.834. While feature selection was applied to SNPs, no formal post‐hoc variable importance method (e.g., SHAP) was reported.

Karimi‐Alavijeh et al. (2016, Iran) used data from 2107 adults in the Isfahan Cohort Study to predict the 7‐year incidence of MetS [31]. They evaluated SVM and DT models; SVM slightly outperformed DT across all metrics, with a sensitivity of 0.774 versus 0.758, specificity of 0.740 versus 0.720, and accuracy of 0.757 versus 0.739. Although formal variable importance metrics were not reported, TG was identified as a critical feature within the DT analysis.

Mohseni‐Takalloo et al. (2024, Iran) explored the prediction of MetS using non‐invasive and dietary parameters in a general adult population [34]. The study used SVM and reported high predictive accuracy; however, the available summary did not detail specific performance metrics or variable importance methods.

Ma et al. (2024, China) developed a predictive nomogram for MetS risk among 3073 adults in the high‐altitude region of Qinghai Province [46]. The LR‐based nomogram demonstrated high discriminative power, achieving an AUC of 0.918 in the training set and 0.925 in the validation set. Formal ML‐based variable importance techniques were not reported.

Wang et al. (2020, Taiwan) conducted a large‐scale longitudinal study on 27,415 adults to predict MetS onset based on socioeconomic and lifestyle changes [48]. They implemented an ANN using an oversampling approach to handle data imbalance. The balanced model achieved superior performance at the third screening stage, with an AUC of 0.930, sensitivity of 84.0%, and specificity of 85.8%. No specific variable importance method was reported.

As noted in the diagnostic section, studies focusing on MetS prediction predominantly reported point estimates of performance. 95% CIs and formal hypothesis testing (p‐values) for direct model comparisons were largely absent from the reported results in the original publications, limiting the assessment of statistical precision and comparative significance.

Cai et al. (2024, China) utilized ML‐based classification to identify nutrient intake patterns across different age groups, comparing MetS patients with healthy individuals [35]. The study functioned as both a diagnostic and predictive tool by establishing age‐specific nutrient–MetS associations. However, formal variable importance methods (e.g., SHAP or Gini impurity) were not reported, and the performance metrics remained focused on age‐stratified nutritional associations rather than a single global accuracy score.

Panagoulias et al. (2022, Greece) developed a system of cascaded SVM‐based classifiers for automated diagnosis and prediction of MetS‐defining factors using biochemical and anthropometric data [50]. Although the exact dataset size was not specified in the abstract, the models were tested on clinical blood exam subsets. The cascaded architecture achieved an average accuracy of approximately 84% for MetS prediction. Furthermore, it demonstrated a specific accuracy of 84% for BMI classification and 74% for systolic blood pressure classification. No explicit variable importance method, such as permutation importance or SHAP, was reported, as the study prioritized the architectural efficiency of cascaded classification over individual feature contribution analysis.

Consistent with the findings in Tables 1 and 2, the studies summarized in Table 3 primarily reported point estimates for model accuracy. Measures of uncertainty, such as 95% CIs and inferential statistical comparisons (p‐values), were not provided by the original authors, which limits the ability to statistically contrast the performance of the cascaded SVM models against other diagnostic frameworks.

Risk of Bias and Applicability Assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was rigorously evaluated using the PROBAST tool. As summarized in Appendices 3 and 8, 8 of the 12 studies (66.7%) were classified as having a low overall risk of bias, indicating robust study designs and analytical frameworks. Conversely, 3 studies (25.0%) were rated as high risk, primarily due to convenience sampling methods [30], retrospective single‐center designs [47], or unclear data sourcing from public repositories [49]. One study (8.3%) was categorized as having an unclear risk due to insufficient detail regarding diagnostic criteria.

Regarding applicability, the majority of studies (n = 10, 83.3%) demonstrated high relevance to the clinical question, with only 2 (16.7%) rated as unclear. Notably, while model performance was generally high, 95% CIs were not explicitly reported in most studies for key metrics such as AUC and accuracy. The values presented in this review reflect the point estimates provided in the original publications; no additional measures of uncertainty or variability were available for extraction. Furthermore, no formal hypothesis testing or inferential p‐values were reported for direct head‐to‐head model comparisons in the reviewed literature, as performance evaluations remained strictly descriptive.

Discussion

This systematic review highlights a steadily growing interest in the use of AI across different stages of MetS care. Among the 12 studies that strictly met our inclusion criteria, the majority focused on diagnostic applications, with consistently high levels of accuracy and clinical relevance reported. A smaller portion of these investigations explored the predictive potential of AI, identifying individuals at elevated risk based on non‐invasive behavioral and sociodemographic factors. Notably, only a limited number of our 12 reviewed studies extended beyond risk prediction to address personalized management, thereby indicating a relative underdevelopment of AI applications for prevention. Beyond the scope of our primary synthesis, external evidence further supports AI's diagnostic potential. For instance, Choi et al. (2025) validated machine learning models for pediatric MetS [36], while Deng et al. (2024) developed a framework to assess the risk of metabolic‐associated fatty liver disease [51]. These studies, although not included in our review, reinforce the broader applicability of AI‐driven metabolic risk assessment beyond the adult MetS populations analyzed here.

The capacity of AI to process large volumes of complex information has facilitated more granular risk stratification. In our systematic review, most included studies (n = 7 out of 12) primarily addressed diagnostic applications of MetS [43, 44, 45, 47, 49, 50], while the remaining investigations focused on risk prediction or mixed diagnostic and predictive objectives (Table 1 and Appendix B). Across these studies, algorithms such as SVM, ANN, KNN, and LR were most frequently applied, with performance generally ranging from acceptable to high across both invasive (laboratory‐based) and non‐invasive feature sets, as summarized in Tables 1, 2, 3. In particular, SVMs tended to perform strongly in high‐dimensional settings combining anthropometric and biochemical predictors [31, 50], whereas ANN models frequently achieved higher discrimination when modeling complex, nonlinear relationships among lifestyle, sociodemographic, and clinical variables [20, 48]. However, as reflected in our qualitative synthesis and assessment, ANN performance was occasionally constrained by small or imbalanced samples, thereby increasing the risk of overfitting and limiting generalizability in specific diagnostic and predictive contexts. Although beyond the scope of our 12 included studies, cloud‐based retraining systems [52] represent a shift toward dynamic AI solutions that can be periodically updated as new data accrue. In our review, studies such as Wang et al. (2020) demonstrate that ANN‐based models can leverage non‐invasive metrics (e.g., waist circumference and socioeconomic status) to develop feasible screening tools, which may be particularly advantageous in resource‐limited primary care settings by enhancing health system efficiency through early intervention [48]. Notably, despite the frequent use of complex machine learning models, most reviewed studies did not formally report variable importance or explainability analyses, thereby limiting insight into the relative contribution of individual predictors and constraining clinical interpretability.

Several predictive models identified within our core 12 studies demonstrated AI's capacity to transition from static diagnosis to dynamic risk forecasting. For example, the longitudinal modeling by Karimi‐Alavijeh et al. (2016) and the large‐scale socio‐behavioral analysis by Wang et al. (2020) exemplify a recurring pattern across multiple studies, suggesting that AI models can effectively synthesize temporal lifestyle changes and routinely collected clinical variables to predict incident MetS [31, 48]. By contrast, findings such as the algorithmic adaptation to high‐altitude physiology reported by Ma et al. (2024), or the integration of advanced body composition metrics described in emerging external work [21, 46], remain exploratory signals that have not yet been replicated across diverse populations or standardized MetS definitions. Accordingly, the former group of results provides more consistent, prespecified evidence supporting AI‐enhanced risk stratification within conventional clinical pathways, whereas the latter should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating and warrant confirmatory investigation. To facilitate the transition from exploratory signal detection to robust clinical tools, adherence to structured reporting and appraisal frameworks, such as the SAMPL guidelines [53] and PROBAST‐AI [54], is essential to ensure transparent performance estimates and minimize bias.

By contrast, a significant finding of this review is the relative scarcity of AI applications dedicated to long‐term MetS management. Among the included studies, isolated efforts, such as the incorporation of dietary and lifestyle features into models like those of Duan et al. (2024), suggest an early transition toward precision prevention [45]. However, actual AI‐driven intervention or management frameworks remain largely absent. To contextualize these gaps, we also examined supplemental literature [20, 55, 56] that illustrates the potential of AI for web‐based lifestyle interventions and weight‐loss optimization. These “proof‐of‐concept” tools, however, remain a minority in the current literature. The comparative analysis across our 12 reviewed studies reveals a fundamental trade‐off between predictive power and clinical interpretability. While high‐capacity models like ANNs may provide superior accuracy, they often lack the transparency required for routine clinical trust. To address this, recent hybrid approaches, such as the integration of an ANN with LR [20], aim to balance performance with clinician‐trusted interpretability.

Evidence from our reviewed studies suggests that while AI‐based systems are increasingly reliable for disease identification, a significant implementation gap remains in transitioning from reactive screening to proactive, long‐term management. This integrated trajectory, moving from diverse clinical, genomic, and lifestyle data toward personalized interventions, is synthesized in our conceptual framework (Figure 1). By harmonizing multidimensional inputs using machine learning, this framework demonstrates the potential to advance MetS care beyond isolated diagnostic snapshots, aiming to reduce the global disease burden through scalable, evidence‐based AI solutions.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths

This systematic review offers several notable strengths. It represents one of the few comprehensive syntheses examining the trajectory of AI algorithms across the diagnostic, predictive, and management domains of MetS. By assessing a wide range of studies that employ diverse machine learning techniques, including SVM, ANN, KNN, and hybrid models, this review captures the breadth of current AI applications. A key strength is the absence of geographic or economic filters, enabling the inclusion of evidence from both high‐income countries (HICs) and low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), thereby enhancing the global generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, in line with the latest reporting guidelines, this review synthesizes diverse clinical, sociodemographic, genomic, and behavioral predictors, offering a multidimensional perspective essential for future policy formulation and precision medicine.

Limitations

Nonetheless, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the exclusive inclusion of English‐language publications may have introduced language bias. Second, methodological heterogeneities were evident across the 12 included studies. Many utilized relatively small or imbalanced datasets, which increases the risk of overfitting and limits statistical power, particularly for high‐capacity models like ANN. As identified through our PROBAST‐based assessment (Appendix C), a significant limitation is the lack of external validation in most models; reliance on internal cross‐validation alone undermines generalizability across diverse healthcare settings. Moreover, the geographic concentration of research primarily in China, Iran, and Korea may limit applicability to other ethnic cohorts.

Furthermore, following the recommendations of Assel et al. (2018), we noted a widespread lack of reported uncertainty measures (e.g., CIs) and effect sizes in the source literature, which complicates the quantification of measurement error. Finally, the limited interpretability of “black‐box” models remains a barrier. The absence of explainable AI (XAI) frameworks, such as SHAP or permutation‐based variable importance analyses, in most reviewed studies impedes clinical transparency and practitioner trust.

Implications for Clinical Practice and Public Health

The integration of AI into MetS care may offer meaningful opportunities to enhance early detection and risk stratification. However, translating algorithmic performance into clinical utility requires strategic implementation. Prioritizing the embedding of AI tools into interoperable EHRs and national screening initiatives is essential for scalability. To promote clinical adoption, clinician training must focus on interpreting AI outputs, supported by explainability features that demystify model logic. Additionally, following the SAMPL guidelines, clinical practice should move toward reporting actual proportions (n/N) and precision estimates to ensure that AI‐driven decisions are grounded in robust statistical evidence. Ethical considerations, including data privacy, equity in access, and the mitigation of algorithmic bias, must be systematically addressed to ensure that AI serves as a cornerstone of equitable, proactive chronic disease management.

Future Research Directions

To realize the full public health potential of AI in MetS care, future research must transition from pilot studies to high‐level evidence. Adherence to standardized reporting protocols, such as TRIPOD‐AI and PROBAST‐AI [54], is critical for ensuring methodological rigor. Conducting randomized controlled trials (RCTs) is essential to determine whether AI‐assisted interventions yield measurable improvements in clinical outcomes compared to standard care. Furthermore, comprehensive health economic analyses, particularly in resource‐limited settings, are vital for evaluating cost‐effectiveness and guiding global policy. Future studies should prioritize the development of interpretable and explainable models to secure regulatory approval and clinical trust. Lastly, expanding validation efforts to multiethnic and multisite populations will be instrumental in strengthening generalizability and informing the design of equitable, AI‐powered healthcare solutions.

Conclusion

This systematic review reveals that AI and machine learning hold promise for improving the diagnosis and early risk prediction of MetS, primarily by leveraging non‐invasive, routinely collected variables. However, applications remain heavily concentrated on diagnostic tasks, with substantial gaps in long‐term management, personalized prevention, and behavioral interventions.

The evidence indicates that AI is not yet mature for standalone clinical use or routine preventive strategies. To advance, future research must prioritize multiethnic and multisite validation to enhance global generalizability, along with consistent adherence to updated standardized frameworks such as TRIPOD + AI and PROBAST + AI to ensure methodological rigor and policy relevance.

Addressing these limitations, particularly the imbalance between diagnostic saturation and underdeveloped prevention‐focused efforts, will be essential for AI to meaningfully contribute to reducing the global burden of MetS through equitable, proactive, and patient‐centered care.
