Hydroxyurea (hydroxycarbamide) use in adults with haemoglobin SC disease: A real‐world study in Quebec
Authors: Alice Girard, Laurianne Charette, Mélissa Haouchine, Laurence Bozec, Mohamed Zakaria Bachir Bouyadjera, Mariane Poitras, Vincent‐Thierry Taillefer, Chloé Trudeau, Yves Pastore, Nathalie Letarte, Stéphanie Forté
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHaemoglobin SC (HbSC) disease is the second most prevalent form of sickle cell disease, but evidence for hydroxyurea (hydroxycarbamide; HU) to prevent pain episodes was limited until the prospective identification of variables as outcomes for treatment (PIVOT) trial. To assess HU effectiveness and safety in adults with HbSC, we conducted a retrospective, single‐centre, pre–post cohort study at the Centre hospitalier de l'Université de Montréal. Patients initiated HU between January 2010 and July 2025. Effectiveness analysis required ≥12 months of pre‐HU follow‐up; patients with shorter follow‐up were included for the safety analysis only. The primary end‐point was the change in the annual frequency of a vaso‐occlusive crisis (VOC) composite (VOCC: VOC, acute chest syndrome, priapism, splenic/hepatic sequestration) from 12 months pre‐HU to months 4–16 post‐HU. Secondary end‐points included adverse events (AEs), treatment effectiveness and reasons for HU discontinuation. Of 263 patients assessed, 110 were included in the safety analysis and 75 in the effectiveness analysis. HU reduced mean VOCC by 56% (0.57–0.25; Δ = −0.32 ± 0.84; 95% confidence interval [CI] [−0.51, −0.13]; p = 0.0015). Haematological AEs were mostly mild (thrombocytopenia, 32.7%; neutropenia, 23.6%; leucopenia, 20.9%); bilirubin elevations were the most frequent non‐haematological AE (10.9%), all asymptomatic and without clinical hepatotoxicity. In conclusion, HU was associated with a meaningful reduction in VOCC and was generally well tolerated.

INTRODUCTION

Haemoglobin SC (HbSC) disease is the second most prevalent formof sickle cell disease (SCD) in Canada, resulting from a composite heterozygous state of the HbS and HbC point mutations in the beta‐globin gene. 1 Although considered milder than Haemoglobin SS disease, many patients experience serious complications, including vaso‐occlusive crises (VOC), acute chest syndrome (ACS), priapism and progressive organ damage. 2 Despite these complications, hydroxyurea (hydroxycarbamide; HU) was not routinely recommended in HbSC until recently, due to a lack of robust evidence. 3 , 4 Most clinical trials have excluded patients with HbSC and concerns persist about potential hyperviscosity resulting from HU‐induced haemoglobin, thereby increasing the risk of VOC and possibly of other SCD‐related complications. 5 Nonetheless, in the province of Quebec, HU is used off‐label in adults with HbSC, yet its real‐world impact on safety has not been systematically evaluated. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9

Recent studies have attempted to investigate the efficacy and safety of HU in this population. In a US cohort of 133 HbSC patients, Luchtman‐Jones et al. reported a 38% reduction in pain events, with the greatest reductions observed in patients over 15 years of age and those receiving doses exceeding 20 mg/kg/day. 10 From a safety perspective, 20% of patients experienced cytopenias, all of which were reversible. In 2022, a Cochrane systematic review found limited evidence specific to HbSC disease. Five of the nine studies included, only one focused exclusively on patients with HbSC (n = 44), assessing the haematological effects of HU. 11 Due to the small sample size, statistical power was insufficient for conclusions. In 2023, Ghunney et al. published a 1‐year retrospective study in Ghana that reported significant underuse of HU in HbSC disease. 12 Despite high disease burden, fewer than 1% received HU due to safety concerns and misconceptions about HbSC. 12 Most recently in 2024, the prospective identification of variables as outcomes for treatment (PIVOT) trial demonstrated that HU, at 20 mg/kg/day, reduced VOCs and hospitalizations by over 50% compared with placebo, although mild, transient haematological dose‐limiting toxicities occurred more frequently with HU than with placebo (33% vs. 11%). No hyperviscosity‐related events were observed in the 12 patients on HU with high haemoglobin (>12.0 g/dL). 13 , 14 Finally, the study by Connes et al. 15 showed no significant difference in haemoglobin levels between HbSC patients with or without HU. HbSC patients receiving HU also had significantly lower blood viscosity than those without HU (p < 0.05). These studies underscore both the potential benefit and the persistent uncertainty surrounding HU in HbSC. Data are particularly scarce in adults and in North American settings, where patient demographics, healthcare infrastructure and comorbidity profiles differ from African cohorts. Furthermore, most published cohorts included many paediatric patients and had limited follow‐up, restricting the assessment of long‐term safety and treatment acceptability. Considering these studies, there is an urgent need for additional real‐world data on the effectiveness and safety of HU in patients living with HbSC SCD.

We therefore conducted a retrospective study to describe the effectiveness of HU in reducing the annual rate of VOCs. We also assessed its safety based on the incidence of laboratory toxicities and adverse events and described reasons for HU discontinuation in adults with HbSC disease. We hypothesized that HU is associated with a reduction in vaso‐occlusive crisis composite (VOCC) and a favourable safety profile in a real‐world setting.

METHODS

The manuscript was prepared in accordance with the strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology guidelines. 16

We conducted a retrospective observational cohort study with a pre–post design, where each patient served as their own control. The pre‐HU period corresponded to 12 months before HU initiation. Time zero (T = 0) corresponded to the HU start date recorded in the clinical notes or the first prescription date if unavailable. The post‐HU period extended from month 4 (T4) through month 16 (T16) following T = 0. The pre‐ and post‐treatment periods were selected to ensure that baseline VOC frequency was stable and measured over a sufficiently long period to capture the natural variability of the disease. A 3‐month initiation period was excluded to account for the pharmacological onset of HU and to avoid capturing variability related to early titration. A timeline is shown in the Supplementary Appendix (Figure S1). The study was approved by the local ethics committee.

Patients

Patients with HbSC SCD were eligible if they were ≥18 years old, had not taken HU 12 months prior to the start of a new HU prescription and had at least three follow‐up visits at the Centre hospitalier de l'Université de Montréal (CHUM) between 1 January 2010 and 31 July 2025. Follow‐up visits were conducted according to standard‐of‐care recommendations, in line with current CanHaem consensus. 4 For the effectiveness analysis, patients required a ≥12 months follow‐up with a haematologist at a Quebec hospital, to be treated at the CHUM at HU initiation and have documentation of the number of VOCs in the 12 months prior to HU initiation. Patients who did not have a 12‐month follow‐up prior to HU prescription and/or who did not complete the 12‐month post‐HU period were still eligible for safety analysis. Exclusion criteria included concomitant disease‐modifying therapies (e.g. l‐arginine, crizanlizumab), ongoing transfusion programmes or pregnancy during treatment (limited to the pregnancy period). Patients undergoing apheresis or on a transfusion programme within 3 months prior to HU initiation were excluded from the effectiveness analysis but remained eligible for the safety analysis. Although the inclusion of these patients could potentially influence safety outcomes, they were retained to better reflect real‐world clinical practice.

Data collection and variables

Data were extracted from electronic health records. A pilot review of 20 charts assessed data completeness and inter‐rater reliability using kappa statistics. 17

Collected variables included demographics, clinical characteristics and baseline medications that can potentially affect patients' outcomes such as antiplatelets, anticoagulants, hypoglycemics, folic acid and iron supplements. History of transfusions and apheresis was also collected. Laboratory values included haemoglobin (g/L), fetal haemoglobin (HbF, %), mean corpuscular volume (MCV, fL), platelets (×109/L), leucocytes (×109/L), reticulocytes (×109/L), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, U/L), creatinine (μmol/L), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, U/L), bilirubin (μmol/L), ferritin (μg/L) and electrolytes. Mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration was not measured, as prior studies (PIVOT et al., Connes et al.) have reported no difference between HbSC patients with and without HU treatment. Laboratory values were collected at baseline (T0), at 3 months (±1 month) after HU initiation or dose adjustment and subsequently at routine follow‐up visits. Blood samples were taken during appointments and were coordinated by the nurses if further blood samples were needed between appointments. Confounding variables were defined a priori, with attention to time‐varying factors such as smoking and comedications. Potential effect modifiers were assessed in subgroup analysis. However, due to limited statistical power, no conclusions could be drawn about concomitant medications as potential effect modifiers.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the annual frequency of the VOCC, defined as events managed in a day hospital, emergency department or through hospitalization and compared before and after HU initiation over a 4‐ to 16‐month follow‐up period. VOCC was defined by episodes of pain‐related VOCs, ACS, priapism or episodes of splenic or hepatic sequestration.

Secondary outcomes aimed to describe the early biological effects of HU, as reflected by laboratory markers, at 3 months post‐initiation or dose adjustment. Laboratory abnormalities were graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. The frequency and severity of HU‐related adverse events were documented. Additionally, complications commonly associated with hyperviscosity were evaluated, including deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, hearing impairment and vertigo, stroke and retinopathy. Finally, reasons for HU discontinuation and treatment duration were assessed.

Exploratory outcomes assessed initial variations in treatment effectiveness, safety and reasons for HU discontinuation among subgroups, including those defined by age, sex, ethnicity, glucose‐6‐phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency and alpha‐thalassaemia status. Given the lack of statistical power, the exploratory outcomes were limited to variations in treatment effectiveness within patient subgroups.

Sample size calculation

A sample size of 200 patients, representing almost the entire HbSC population treated at the CHUM, would allow the detection of a minimal clinically significant reduction of 27.5% in VOCC with a statistical power of 80% and a significance level of 5%, based on a lognormal distribution and a two‐tailed one‐sample t‐test. The sample size calculation was based on experts' clinical experience with the HbSC population treated at the CHUM, estimating the annual number of VOCs in the pre‐HU period to range from 0 to 14, with an average of two crises. The PIVOT trial revealed a 60% reduction in VOCs in comparable subpopulation receiving HU. 13 Using this 60% reduction as a reference, at least 75 pre–post patients would be required to achieve 80% power with an alpha significance level of 5%. Calculations were performed by an independent statistician using power analysis & sample size 2023 software, version 23.0.3.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses included means, standard deviations and frequencies, as appropriate. Skewed distributions were described by the median and extreme values. Adverse events (AEs) were reported proportionally according to their severity. Mean differences were calculated for parameters related to the effectiveness of HU, namely MCV and HbF. The primary analysis compared the number of VOCC before and after initiation of HU using an intention‐to‐treat (ITT) approach, using a mixed‐model with repeated measures to estimate crude and adjusted effects. Sensitivity analyses included per‐protocol (PP) subsets of ‘likely adherent’ patients. Patients with incomplete pre‐ or post‐HU follow‐up were included only in the safety analysis. Missing data were examined and, if deemed not at random missing not at random (MNAR), imputation using the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method was applied as a sensitivity analysis. A missing data rate <10% was anticipated.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore heterogeneity of treatment effects by age, sex, ethnicity, G6PD and alpha‐thalassaemia status. Comparisons were performed using a repeated measures model with a between–subject factor. It was determined that the observations in subgroups with fewer than 30 patients likely lacked statistical power.

RESULTS

Between 1 January 2010 and 31 July 2025, 263 patients with HbSC disease were screened; 110 met the safety analysis criteria and 75 met effectiveness analysis criteria (Figure 1). The mean age was 33 years, 51.8% were female. Most patients originated from North America and the Caribbean (63.6%), followed by sub‐Saharan Africa (24.5%). At baseline, 7% had G6PD deficiency and 37% carried one or two α‐thalassaemia gene deletion(s). Effectiveness analysis

Among the 75 patients included in the effectiveness analysis, HU was associated with a 56% reduction in the mean number of annual VOCC, whether managed in the day hospital, the emergency department or through hospitalization. During the pre‐HU period, 43 VOCC components were reported, of which 36 were pain‐related and 7 were ACS. Of the 36 pain‐related VOC, 80% required hospitalization. In the post‐HU period, 18 VOCC components were reported, of which 17 were pain‐related VOCs and 1 was a splenic sequestration. Of the 17 pain‐related VOC, 76% required hospitalization. The mean number of annual VOCC decreased from 0.57 to 0.25 (–0.32 ± 0.84; 95% confidence interval [CI] [–0.51; –0.13]; p = 0.0015) in ITT analysis. The mean number of annual VOCC in the pre‐HU period was 0.57 ± 0.81 (median = 0; min = 0; max = 5) compared to 0.25 ± 0.62 in the post‐HU period (median = 0; min = 0; max = 3). Safety analysis

Among the haematological AEs, thrombocytopenia was the most frequent (32.7%), but neutropenia accounted for the highest number of grade 3 events (5.4%). 26.3% of patients suffered at least one episode of neutropenia. Most haematological AEs were grades 1‐2. Haemoglobin elevation occurred at least once in 10.9% of patients, with no interventions needed and no hyperviscosity‐related complications observed. For non‐haematological AEs, bilirubin elevation was the predominant event with 15 events in 12 patients (10.9%), followed by ALT elevation in 6 patients (5.5%). No liver injury and no intervention was needed. Also, no creatinine elevation occurred. Neutropenia and clinical symptoms account for the highest frequencies of treatment intervention. Initiation and discontinuation analysis

Reasons for HU initiation and discontinuation were collected from all patients included (n = 110). The mean treatment duration during the observation window was 13.6 months (standard deviation 3.67; minimum 1.20; maximum 17.60). Discontinuation occurred in 27% of patients (n = 30/110). The most common reason encountered (n = 10) was lack of awareness about prescription renewal (belief that the duration of treatment was limited to a few months, failure to renew upon prescription expiration or miscommunication with the pharmacy). The second most common reason for discontinuation was adverse events (n = 8). Other reasons for treatment discontinuation are detailed in Figure S3. Prevention of VOC recurrence was the main reason for initiating HU (n = 57), followed by history of ACS with or without pulmonary hypertension (n = 16), retinopathy (n = 15), cerebral involvement (n = 14) and renal complications (n = 11). Other documented reasons for initiation included an attempt to reduce transfusion/apheresis requirements (n = 6), hearing loss (n = 3), cardiac involvement (n = 2), chronic lower limb ulcers (n = 1) and avascular necrosis (n = 1). More than one reason for initiation could be documented per patient.

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective, single‐centre pre–post cohort study, we observed a significant reduction in VOCC requiring day hospital, emergency or inpatient management among patients with HbSC SCD treated with HU. A trend towards an increase in HbF and MCV is also observed 16 months after HU initiation, which is consistent with prior studies in HbSC disease and suggests that mechanisms beyond HbF induction may contribute to the observed clinical benefits. Overall, the absolute number of VOC was relatively low at baseline, indicating that while HU was associated with a significant reduction in VOCC most patients already experienced a limited number of events prior to starting therapy.

An exploratory per‐protocol analysis restricted to ‘likely adherent’ patients showed a 46% (–0.28; 95% CI [–0.58; 0.02]) VOCC reduction, consistent with the ITT findings, although not statistically significant. This could be explained by the reduced sample size and greater variability, as well as the paradoxical amplification of the ITT effect from partial or irregular HU use among non‐adherent patients. These results underscore the importance of adherence and the need for larger confirmatory studies.

Haematological toxicities, including thrombocytopenia, neutropenia and leucopenia, were common but mostly mild (grade 1–2), reversible and manageable with dose adjustments. Severe events (grade 3–4) were rare, and no life‐threatening complications occurred. Importantly, no hyperviscosity‐related complications were observed, reinforcing the existing reassuring data in this regard. Non‐haematological events, including mild liver enzyme elevations, gastrointestinal symptoms and hypomagnesaemia, were infrequent and clinically limited, with no significant hepatic, renal or dermatological toxicity observed. Only a small proportion of patients required treatment modification (Figure 3), suggesting HU's overall safety when combined with regular monitoring and patient education.

The mean HU dose was 17.8 mg/kg (standard deviation 5.14; minimum 4.20; maximum 38.00), which is consistent with doses reported in other studies. 14 As there are no clear recommendations regarding HU dose titration, dose escalation was individualized according to the clinical judgement and patient discussion during follow‐up visits approximately every 1–3 months. Although the maximum dose could reach up to 30 mg/kg, the reasons limiting further dose escalation in our study remain unknown. Nevertheless, statistically significant reductions in VOCCs were still observed, suggesting that lower doses may still be sufficiently effective.

HU discontinuation occurred in 27.3% of patients over a mean 13.5‐month treatment period. Although most initiated therapy to prevent VOCC recurrence, long‐term adherence was limited. The main reason for discontinuation was unawareness of prescription renewal, underscoring systemic gaps in education and communication among prescribers, patients and pharmacists. AEs accounted for only 7.3% of discontinuations and HU's overall good tolerability supports this interpretation. Other reasons such as pregnancy, fear of treatment or perceived ineffectiveness highlight the strong influence of psychosocial and personal factors.

Compared with other chronic therapies, HU discontinuation is within the range observed for commonly prescribed medications. In Quebec, limited data suggest that approximately 14% of prescriptions are never filled and an additional 13% are not renewed, suggesting that treatment persistence challenges reflect broader systemic and behavioural barriers common to chronic disease management. 18 A second phase of the project will aim to develop resources for healthcare professionals, enhancing their ability to support patients in maintaining long‐term HU therapy.

The subgroup analyses revealed larger reductions of VOCC in patients aged 18–30 years and those with coexisting α‐thalassaemia gene deletion consistent with the influence of genetic and age‐related modifiers. Co‐inherited α‐thalassaemia has been previously recognized as a modest modifier in SCD, with data suggesting it alters the magnitude of laboratory changes (smaller MCV rise, higher baseline Hb). 19 , 20 The apparent greater benefit among patients without G6PD deficiency could not be reliably interpreted due to the small subgroup size (n = 3), limiting statistical comparison. Future studies with larger samples are needed to clarify these associations.

Our findings align with other available evidence supporting the benefit of HU in HbSC disease. The PIVOT trial demonstrated a 62% reduction in vaso‐occlusive pain events and a 58% reduction in hospitalizations. 13 Similarly, a US multicentre cohort of 133 children and adults reported comparable results. 10 In our Quebec cohort, HU reduced VOCC by 56%, with a safety profile marked by mostly mild, reversible haematological toxicities.

This observational study has several strengths, including its within‐subject pre–post design reducing interindividual variability and enhancing internal validity. AEs were systematically assessed by pharmacists and haematologists using CTCAE 5.0, ensuring objective evaluation. 21 Additionally, the 3‐month titration period after HU initiation helped avoid premature misclassification of early events.

Despite these strengths, the study also faces limitations. Information bias may arise from data extracted from non‐standardized clinical notes, and measuring adherence in retrospective data is challenging. Adherence was evaluated only when explicitly documented; patients without documentation were classified as ‘likely adherent’, which may have led to an underestimation of HU effectiveness and an overestimation of its safety profile. Also, confounding time‐varying variables remains a concern, particularly for factors that are difficult to quantify or incompletely recorded, such as smoking habits, seasonal effects or nutritional deficiencies. Nonetheless, this risk is reduced by the stability of the clinical management practices for HbSC disease at the CHUM in recent years, which are consistent with current CanHaem consensus and American society of hematology guidelines. 3 , 4 Furthermore, the absence of an untreated control group limits exclusion of indication bias. Although the pre–post design offers internal control, it cannot adjust for differences between HU initiators and non‐initiators. 22 It also assumes disease stability over time, which may not hold in cases of unexpected progression, potentially undermining comparability of pre‐ and post‐treatment periods. Another limitation is that only severe VOCC requiring medical care were included, while less severe crises managed at home were missed, risking underestimation of benefit.

CONCLUSION

This study adds to the evidence on use of HU in HbSC disease by demonstrating its association with real‐world effectiveness and safety but limited adherence in a North American context. HU was associated with a significant reduction in VOCC and showed a favourable safety profile, with generally mild and reversible adverse events. The relatively high discontinuation rate highlights the need for improved patient education and adherence‐support strategies. These findings may help guide future recommendations and promote greater pharmacist involvement in sickle cell disease care. Further studies with longer follow‐up are needed to confirm the long‐term effectiveness and safety of HU in routine practice.
