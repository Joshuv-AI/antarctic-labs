Near-Resolution of the Tradeoff Conjecture in Distributed Proof Labeling Schemes
Arnold Filtser, Orr Fischer

Abstract
In the t t -Proof Labeling Scheme model ( t t -PLS model), our goal is to certify that a network of nodes satisfies a given property P P . A prover assigns a label to each node, and each node decides to accept or reject based on its labeled t t -hop neighborhood. If P P holds, there exists a labeling that makes all nodes accept. If P P does not hold, in all labelings at least one node rejects. The cost of a scheme is its maximum label size. The Tradeoff Conjecture [Feuilloley, Fraigniaud, Hirvonen, Paz, and Perry, DISC 18, Dist. Comput. 21] hypothesizes that the existence of a 1 1 -PLS for a property P P with cost p p implies the existence of a t t -PLS for P P with cost O ⁡ ( ⌈ p / t ⌉ ) O(\lceil p/t\rceil) . The conjecture was initially shown to hold for specific graph classes, such as trees, cycles, and grids. Later, a weaker O ~ ​ ( ⌈ Δ ​ p / t ⌉ ) \widetilde{O}(\lceil\Delta p/\sqrt{t}\rceil) cost was shown for fixed minor-free graphs, where Δ \Delta is the maximum degree. In this work we resolve the Tradeoff Conjecture, up to a single logarithmic factor. In general graphs, we show that the existence of a 1 1 -PLS with cost p p implies the existence of an O ⁡ ( t ​ log ⁡ n ) O(t\log{n}) -PLS with cost O ⁡ ( ⌈ p / t ⌉ ) O(\lceil p/t\rceil) for the same property. For fixed minor-free graphs (which include e.g. planar graphs), we show that the existence of a 1 1 -PLS with cost p p implies the existence of a t t -PLS with cost O ⁡ ( ⌈ p / t ⌉ + log ⁡ n ) O(\lceil p/t\rceil+\log{n}) for the same property. We also refute a previously suggested stronger variant of the Tradeoff Conjecture, and show that having very large t t -hop neighborhoods is an insufficient condition for obtaining a tradeoff better than O ⁡ ( ⌈ p / t ⌉ ) O(\lceil p/t\rceil) .

1.1
Technical Overview

We begin by presenting a key framework for obtaining tradeoff theorems, used both in prior works and in our work, which shows how the existence of certain graph decompositions directly yields tradeoff theorems. We then introduce a new type of decomposition, called
Two-Separated partitions
(TS partitions), which extends the prior decompositions while implying similar tradeoff theorems.

Following this, we show three constructions of TS partitions in graphs:

(a)

a warmup construction which yields a weaker tradeoff theorem for general graphs (cf.
Theorem

1.2
), but its ideas are used throughout the paper,

(b)

constructing TS partitions from padded decompositions (introduced later), which implies our results for minor free graphs (
Theorem

1.4
), and

(c)

a construction which yields our tradeoff theorem for general graphs (
Theorem

1.2
).

Finally, we discuss the techniques for refuting the stronger variant of the Tradeoff Conjecture (
Theorem

1.6
).

Challenge:

As it turns out, constructing decompositions such that the fraction of boundary vertices is small in all the clusters simultaneously is quite challenging. In the following, we describe how we can relax the requirements from the decomposition to get considerably better results.

Attempt 1 - Ball carving with advice:

Definition 2

(TS partition)
.

A
(t,\varepsilon)
-
Two-Separated partition
(TS partition) of a graph

•

\forall C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}

•

\forall C\in\mathcal{C}

. We say that a TS partition is

is connected.

•

The

of

R(\mathcal{C},X)=\max_{i}|C_{i}\cap X|/|C_{i}|
, holds

.

Similarly, define for a TS partition

the cost ratio of a cluster

as

.

Next, we introduce the notion of a decomposition into clusters with

-TS partition. This notion provides a convenient approach for constructing TS partitions in graphs, which we use in both

Given a ordered partition

Definition 3
.

A partition of

\forall i,\quad\left|\left\{v\in C_{i}\mid\dist_{G}\left(v,V\setminus\cup_{j=1}^{i}C_{j}\right)\leq 2\right\}\right|\leq\varepsilon\cdot|C_{i}|.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume a graph

Proof.

Let

, and
V_{>k}=\emptyset
. Setting

-TS partition, i.e. it fulfills the following three properties:

•

•

Cost ratio:
We note that

•

Two Separation:
Assume for the sake of contradiction that the two separation property fails. Let

Proof.

We sample a partition

5
Tradeoff Theorem for General Graphs

Our goal in this section is to show a near resolution of the Tradeoff Conjecture in general graphs, up to a single multiplicative logarithmic factor, and with only constant additive factors. In particular, the cost of this scheme converges to a constant number of bits as
t
grows.

See
1.2

5.1
Helpful Sequential Algorithm for Finding a
(O(t\log{n}),1/t)
-TS Partition

Before proving
Theorem

1.2
, we describe and analyze a sequential randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on a graph
G
, which obtains a
(O(t\log{n}),1/t)
-TS partition with high probability, and is later used as part of the labeling scheme. For ease of notation, we treat
\log{n}
as an integer throughout the section, and note that this assumption can be easily removed using appropriate rounding functions.

Description of algorithm
\mathcal{A}
:

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
be an ordering of
V
according to lexicographic order. Initially, we initialize our set of clusters as
\mathcal{C}=\emptyset
, and the set of active nodes as
L_{1}=V
. We iterate over the nodes according to the ordering. In step
i\geq 1
, we check whether
L_{i}\cap B_{2t\log{n}}(v_{i})=\emptyset
, and if so, we continue to step
i+1
. Otherwise, we choose a random even integer
r_{i}\in\{2t\log{n}+2,2t\log{n}+4,\dots,8t\log{n}\}
, and define
C_{i}=B_{r_{i}}(v_{i})\cap L_{i}
. We note that
C_{i}
does not necessarily contain
v_{i}
, and also might be disconnected. We add
C_{i}
to
\mathcal{C}
if

\frac{|B_{r_{i}}(v_{i})\cap L_{i}|}{|B_{r_{i}-2}(v_{i})\cap L_{i}|}\leq 1+\frac{1}{t}~.

(1)

If
C_{i}
was added to
\mathcal{C}
, we set
L_{i+1}=L_{i}\setminus C_{i}
, set
X_{i}=C_{i}\cap B_{2}(L_{i}\setminus C_{i})
. Otherwise, we set
X_{i}=\emptyset
and
L_{i+1}=L_{i}
. Following this, we continue to step
i+1
. We conclude at the end of step
n
, i.e. after the final vertex
v_{n}
is processed. Let
X=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
. We output
(\mathcal{C},X)
as our TS partition.

Analysis of algorithm
\mathcal{A}
:

In the analysis, our goal is to show that with some high probability the resulting
(\mathcal{C},X)
is an
(O(t\log{n}),1/t)
-TS partition of
G
. Specifically, we need to show that
\mathcal{C}
is a partition of
V
(i.e., every node is assigned to exactly one cluster in
\mathcal{C}
), that the clusters are weakly
t
-bounded, have cost ratio at most
1/t
, and are two separated. We first show that
L_{n+1}=\emptyset
, which implies that
\mathcal{C}
is a partition. Following this, we show that conditioned that
\mathcal{C}
is a partition, then
(\mathcal{C},X)
is a
(O(t\log{n}),1/t)
-TS partition.

We next show that in each step
i
, if we have some node in
L_{i}
in the vicinity of
v_{i}
(i.e.
L_{i}\cap B_{2t\log{n}}(v_{i})\neq\emptyset
), then with some constant probability we create a new cluster in
\mathcal{C}
that contains these nodes. We later argue that since each node has
\Theta(t\log{n})
nodes in its
\Theta(t\log{n})
-neighborhood (unless the graph is smaller than
\Theta(t\log{n})
), then with high probability each node is added to a cluster in some step, and the correctness of the algorithm follows.

Lemma 5.1
.

If at the start of step
i
we have
L_{i}\cap B_{2t\log{n}}(v_{i})\neq\emptyset
, then Eq. (
1
) holds with probability at least
1/2
over the choice of
r_{i}\in[2t\log{n}+2,8t\log{n}]
.

Proof.

Fix a step
i
, and denote

\Bad_{i}=\{r\in\{2t\log{n}+2,2t\log{n}+4,\dots,8t\log{n}\}\mid r\text{ does not satisfy }Eq.~(\ref{eq:general_expands})\text{ in step $i$}\}.

Assume by contradiction that
|\Bad_{i}|\geq t\log{n}+1
, and let
\{r^{\prime}_{1},\dots,r^{\prime}_{t\log{n}+1}\}\subseteq\Bad_{i}
, ordered in ascending order. Therefore, for all
j\in[2,t\log{n}+1]
we have that

|B_{r^{\prime}_{j}}(v_{i})\cap L_{i}|\geq(1+\frac{1}{t})|B_{r^{\prime}_{j}-2}(v_{i})\cap L_{i}|\geq(1+\frac{1}{t})|B_{r^{\prime}_{j-1}}(v_{i})\cap L_{i}|,

where the first inequality holds by definition of
\Bad_{i}
, and the second inequality holds due to the fact that we considered only even indices, hence
r^{\prime}_{j-1}\leq r^{\prime}_{j}-2
, and the fact that a ball can only increase in size as the radius grows. By induction on
j
, we get that

|B_{r^{\prime}_{t\log{n}+1}}(v_{i})\cap L_{i}|\geq(1+\frac{1}{t})^{t\log{n}}|B_{r^{\prime}_{1}}(v_{i})\cap L_{i}|\geq(1+\frac{1}{t})^{t\log{n}}|B_{2t\log{n}}(v_{i})\cap L_{i}|>n,

where the last inequality follows by the fact
(1+1/t)^{t}\geq 2
for any
t\geq 1
, and by the lemma’s assumption that
|B_{2t\log{n}}(v_{i})\cap L_{i}|\geq 1
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Assume
n\geq 2t\log{n}
. Then
\Pr(L_{n+1}\neq\emptyset)\leq 1/n
.

Proof.

We consider a node
v
and bound the probability of
v\in L_{n+1}
. Let
S=B_{2t\log{n}}(v)
, denote
k=|S|
, and let
v_{i_{1}},\dots,v_{i_{k}}
be the vertices of
S
in lexicographic order. Since we assume
n\geq 2t\log{n}
, it is easy to see that
k\geq 2t\log{n}
. When processing each
v_{i_{j}}
for
j\in[k]
, if
v
is active in this step (i.e.
v\in L_{i_{j}}
), then by
Lemma

5.1
,
v
is still active at the end of the step (i.e.
v\in L_{i_{j}+1}
) with probability at most
1/2
. Therefore,

\Pr(v\in L_{n+1})\leq\prod_{j=1}^{k}\Pr(v\in L_{i_{j}+1}\mid v\in L_{i_{j}})\leq(1/2)^{k}\leq(1/2)^{2t\log{n}}\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}.

and
\Pr(L_{n+1}\neq\emptyset)\leq 1/n
follows by the union bound on all vertices
v\in V
.
∎

Next, we show that if
\mathcal{C}
is a partition of
V
, then it is an
(O(t\log{n}),1/t)
-TS partition.

Lemma 5.3
.

If
L_{n+1}=\emptyset
, then
(\mathcal{C},X)
is a
(16t\log{n},1/t)
-TS partition.

Proof.

Recall that if
C_{i}
is added to
\mathcal{C}
, then it is non-empty, and that
C_{i}=B_{r_{i}}(v_{i})\cap L_{i}
. Since
r_{i}\leq 8t\log{n}
, it follows that
\mathrm{weak\text{-}diam}(C_{i})\leq 16t\log{n}
. Next, we show that the cost ratio and two separation conditions hold. The cost ratio follows by:

\displaystyle|X_{i}|

\displaystyle=|C_{i}\cap B_{2}(L_{i}\setminus C_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq|B_{r_{i}}(v_{i})\cap L_{i}|-|B_{r_{i}-2}(v_{i})\cap L_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq(1+\frac{1}{t})\cdot|B_{r_{i}-2}(v_{i})\cap L_{i}|-|B_{r_{i}-2}(v_{i})\cap L_{i}|

\displaystyle=\frac{|B_{r_{i}-2}(v_{i})\cap L_{i}|}{t}\leq\frac{|C_{i}|}{t}~.

We note that indeed
X_{i}=C_{i}\cap X
, since
X_{i}\subseteq C_{i}
and the clusters are disjoint. Finally, we show the two separation property.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that the two separation property fails. Let
P_{u,v}
be the shortest path between two vertices
u,v\notin X
that belong to different clusters in
\mathcal{C}
such that
P_{u,v}
does not have two consecutive vertices from
X
. By minimality
\dist_{G}(u,v)\leq 2
(as otherwise we could have taken a shorter path). Let
C_{i},C_{j}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
u\in C_{i}
,

, and suppose w.l.o.g. that
i<j
. As
\dist_{G}(u,v)\leq|P_{u,v}|\leq 2
, it follows that
u\in C_{i}\cap B_{2}(L_{j})
, and thus

, a contradiction.

∎

Combining
Lemma

5.2
and
Lemma

5.3
, we immediately get the following as a corollary.

Corollary 5.4
.

Assume
n\geq 2t\log{n}
. Then
(\mathcal{C},X)
is a
(16t\log{n},1/t)
-TS partition with probability at least
1-(1/n)
.

This concludes the description and analysis of
\mathcal{A}
. We call a randomness string
R

good
w.r.t. graph
G
if by running
\mathcal{A}
using randomness
R
, we have that the resulting clusters
(\mathcal{C},X)
are a
(16t\log{n},1/t)
-TS partition. In particular,

5.4
implies that for any graph

G
.

Encoding the randomness string of
\mathcal{A}
and reducing its size:

Let us consider the randomness used by
\mathcal{A}
, and its encoding. Naively, we can represent it as a choice of
n
even indices
r_{i}\in[2t\log{n}+2,8t\log{n}]
for each
i\in[n]
. However, in our labeling scheme we run a sort of simulation of
\mathcal{A}
in a distributed manner, where nodes have access to the shared randomness string. The naive representation does not lend itself well to that sort of distributed implementation, as it requires knowledge of the global ordering of node identifiers (i.e., knowing the lexicographic rank of each node
u
among all identifiers). Instead, we view the randomness as a function that assigns each possible identifier a random index, namely
R:[\mathrm{poly}(n)]\rightarrow[2t\log{n}+2,8t\log{n}]
. With this representation, a node
u
can locally compute its random index from its identifier alone. The drawback is that describing
R
explicitly requires
\mathrm{poly}(n)
random bits. We therefore apply a randomness reduction argument to reduce it to
O(\log{n})
bits, which we show later can be shared among all nodes using only
O(1)
label bits.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{n}
be the family of all graphs of size
|V(G)|=n
. Next, we prove using a standard argument that for graphs in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
there exists a set of randomness strings
\{R_{1},\dots,R_{n^{2}}\}
such that for each
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
there exists an
R_{i}
that is a good randomness string for
G
.

12

12

In the literature, this type of argument is often referred to as “Newman’s trick”
[
20
]
. It was originally used in communication complexity to show that public coin protocols can be simulated by private coin protocols, and as a general method to reduce randomness.

Lemma 5.5
.

For any integer
n
, there exists a set
F_{n}:[n^{2}]\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{*}
such that for any graph
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
there exists an index
i\in[n^{2}]
such that
F_{n}(i)
is a good randomness string for
G
.

Proof.

We notice that algorithm
\mathcal{A}
uses for graphs in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
at most

uniformly at random from
\{0,1\}^{O(n\log{n})}
, we have that for a fixed

that

\Pr(R\text{ is good randomness for }G)\geq 1-(1/n).

Therefore, if we choose
n^{2}

uniformly at random, we have that

\Pr(\exists_{i}R_{i}\text{ is good randomness for }G)\geq 1-(1/n)^{n^{2}}.

By union bound over all graphs in

we have that

\Pr(\forall_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}\exists_{i}R_{i}\text{ is good randomness for }G)\geq 1-2^{\binom{n}{2}}\cdot(1/n)^{n^{2}}>0.

Therefore, there exists a set of
n^{2}
randomness strings

such that for all
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
, there exists an index

such that

is a good randomness string for

the function

, the claim follows.
∎

.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
r\geq 1
be an integer. Then for any

, there exists an
O(r)
-PLS

Proof sketch of
Lemma

5.6
.

The key idea of the proof is to show the existence of a vertex set
U\subseteq V
that splits the graph into connected components
C_{1},\dots,C_{k}
of
G[V\setminus U]
such that

(a)

\forall_{i\in[k]}|C_{i}|=r
, and

(b)

for every
v\in V
there exists
i\in[k]
such that
\dist_{G}(v,C_{i})\leq r
.

Given this decomposition, we encode the string
S
on each of the connected components

, assigning
O(1)
bits of
S
to each node in the component. Therefore, every vertex
v
can retrieve

from its closest component

. Moreover, we can guarantee that, as long as
G
is connected, all nodes output the same string. To achieve this, we define an auxiliary graph

whose vertices correspond to the clusters, and where two clusters are adjacent in

if there exists a short path between them in

. We then observe that

is connected, meaning that to ensure all nodes output the same string, it suffices to guarantee that neighboring clusters encode the same string.

Theorem

1.2
. Let

Description of

For convenience, we describe the verifier as a distributed algorithm from the viewpoint of

Lemma 5.7

. Then necessarily

, and by the triangle inequality we have
\dist_{G}(u,v)\leq\dist_{G}(u,w)+\dist_{G}(w,v)\leq 16t\log{n}
.

We next formalize the fact that

Lemma 5.8
.

Fix a randomness string

,

\mathrm{FindMyCluster}(u,R)=\min\left\{\ID(w)\mid\ w\in B_{8t\log{n}}(u),\ \mathrm{InT}(w)=1,\ \dist_{G}(w,u)\leq R(\ID(w))\right\},

\mathrm{FindMyCluster}(u,R)=\bot

Proof.

This follows by definition of

, which returns the smallest identifier among nodes satisfying the two conditions above.

(2)

.

(3)

For every node

(4)

Refuting Stronger Variants of the Tradeoff Conjecture

In this section, we refute a stronger variant of the Tradeoff Conjecture, and show a problem where every node has
|B_{t}(v)|=\Omega(n)

t

See
1.6

In our proof, we use a reduction to 2-party non-deterministic communication complexity. We begin with a brief overview of the 2-party non-deterministic communication model and the results relevant for our reduction. For simplicity, instead of describing the standard non-deterministic communication model, we describe a slight (but standard) variant. This variant is equivalent to the standard model up to an additive
O(1)
term in their respective complexity measures.

Non-deterministic communication complexity:

For our purposes, the non-deterministic 2-party communication model consists of two players, Alice and Bob, with private inputs
X\in\mathcal{X}
and
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
, respectively, for some input domain
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. A protocol with cost
p
in this setting is defined as follows. Initially, both Alice and Bob are given a shared string
W\in\{0,1\}^{\leq p}
, called the
witness string
. Without communicating, both Alice and Bob output either
accept
or
reject
based solely on their private input and the witness
W
. For a predicate
P:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, we say that a non-deterministic
p
-cost protocol solves
P
if:

•

Correctness:
For any
(X,Y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
such that
P(X,Y)=1
, there exists a witness string
W
with
|W|\leq p
for which both players accept.

•

Soundness:
For any
(X,Y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
such that
P(X,Y)=0
, and for every witness string
W
with
|W|\leq p
, at least one of Alice or Bob rejects.

We briefly remark that in the standard model, nodes are allowed to communicate, and the cost is the size of
W
plus the total number of bits communicated. Though, using a standard argument, the difference in cost between the two models is at most two bits. Informally, this is because instead of having lengthy communication, we can always encode the transcript inside
W
and have both nodes verify the transcript using a single bit each (See
[
19
]
for an overview).

Finally, we describe the equality function and the lower bound needed for our results. The equality function
\mathrm{EQ}_{k}:\{0,1\}^{k}\times\{0,1\}^{k}\to\{0,1\}
is defined by
\mathrm{EQ}_{k}(X,Y)=1
if and only if
X=Y
.

Lemma B.1

Let

be an integer parameter, and let

be a connected graph with

(b)

Proof.

We use the following simple iterative procedure to construct the set

, proving this decomposition’s existence. At each step we have our set of active nodes

, initially set at

. At the

, we terminate the process. Otherwise, set

to be an arbitrary connected subgraph of such a component, of size exactly

.

Next we prove correctness of this process.

•

Property (

•

For Property (

Lemma B.2

Let
