Taxonomic reevaluation of Eryx jaculus in Anatolia with a monotypic perspective
Authors: Mehmet Zülfü Yıldız, Abdullah Altunışık, Fatma Üçeş, Nedret Yakalı Evsen, İsmail Yıldız
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe taxonomic status of Eryx jaculus is contentious. E. jaculus was known as a polytypic species in the past with the subspecies E. j. jaculus, E. j. turcicus, E. j. familiaris. However, some researchers suggested E. jaculus as a monotypic species. The aim of this study was to contribute to the understanding of the taxonomic status of the species through morphological data from Anatolian populations. A total of 25 specimens were collected and examined during field studies conducted between 2004 and 2019, and nine additional photographic materials obtained from local people and herpetologists were also assessed. These localities have filled the gaps within the previously known distribution range of the species. The taxonomy of the species has been discussed in light of the relevant literature data and it was concluded that E. jaculus should be considered as a monotypic species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57012-4.Keywords: Anatolia, Biodiversity, Javelin Sand Boa, Monotypic, Snake, SystematicsSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Evolution, Zoology

Introduction

The genus Eryx Daudin, 1803 belongs to the family Erycidae, which is distributed from southwestern Europe, North and East Africa, and the Middle East to southwestern Asia, India, and Sri Lanka1–4. The genus also extends northward to Caucasia and Afghanistan and eastward from Turkestan to southern Mongolia and western China5–7. Eryx jaculus (Linnaeus, 1758), the Javelin Sand Boa, was originally described by Linnaeus (1758) as Anguis jaculus and subsequently assigned to the genus Eryx by Daudin (1803). Eryx jaculus is distributed in West Asia, Caucasus, Southeast Europe, and North Africa8–14. Tokar15 carried out a comprehensive study with 327 specimens from a wide range of its distribution and opined that E. jaculus should be monotypic species and placed the names turcicus and familiaris into junior synonymy. However, Tokar and Obst8 concluded that the Caucasian population of E. jaculus, described by Eichwald16 as E. familiaris was similar to those population of E. jaculus from southeast Europe and Türkiye and referred to European-Anatolian populations as E. j. turcicus. Therefore, they recognized only two subspecies, the nominal subspecies in the east to the Suez Canal, North Africa and E. j. turcicus in the southeastern Europe, including the Mediterranean islands, Anatolia, the Caucasus with foreland, extending east to Iran and Iraq. According to Tokar and Obst8, the nominal subspecies has 3 postinternasal scales, 5 interorbital scales, 170–198 ventralia, and the type 2 (standard) color pattern predominates. While the other subspecies, E. j. turcicus, has 2 postinternasal scales, 4 interorbital scales, 161–200 ventralia, and the type 3 (negative) color pattern predominates. Furthermore, Tokar and Obst8 shows clinal variation in the frequency of the number of postinternasal scales, and indicate the North Africa population as the outermost limit of the species distribution. Abbas‑Rhadi et al.17 reported that the only morphological character they used to distinguish E. j. jaculus, E. j. familiaris and Eryx cf. miliaris was number of internasal plates. Eskandarzadeh et al.18 used two multivariate analyses; principal component analysis (PCA) and discriminative cluster analysis (DCA). It was concluded that the plots of PCA and DCA indicated that the specimens of E. jaculus from Egypt, western Iran, Iraq and specimens of E. jayakari are not distinguishable from each other18. But, based on the results of the cluster analysis (using multistate characters) on morphology, only E. jayakari is well separated from other populations and no separation was detected between other clades. They showed that the Egyptian population may differ in only three morphological characters (BE: Number of scales between the eyes-PIN: Number of scales posterior to internasal and DS: Number of dorsal scales in mid-body) from the Iraqi population and in four morphological characters (the same three characters and RE: Number of scales around eye) from the western Iranian population by multivariate analysis. However, they also reported that the characters they found to be statistically different also overlapped. Despite the low number of individuals compared morphologically by Eskandarzadeh et al.18 (Iraqi population: 12, western Iran population: 31, Egypt population: 10), the overlap of characters actually proves Tokar15 clinal variations idea (n: 327). Although Eskandarzadeh et al.18 found that the Iraqi population was molecularly different from the specimens of West Iran, they considered it appropriate to name the population from Iraq as Eryx cf.. jaculus because they did not have tissue samples from the type locality (Egyptian population) for comparison. Besides the high morphological similarities among the studied erycids groups, their results highlighted a significant genetic differentiation and concluded that Eryx jayakari is the only species that can be morphologically distinguished from the other studied erycids in the region by key morphological characters18. The Javelin Sand Boa has been reported from Anatolia by various researchers19–31. The first specimen of Eryx jaculus was collected by Günther from the Xanthos ruins in Fethiye, Muğla province in 1880. Then, various researchers, such as Boettger, Boulenger, Werner, Venzmer and Cyren, collected some specimens from Anatolia32. Bodenheimer19 reported that Türkiye was occupied by E. jaculus and not by E. familiaris and proposed that more specimens should be collected to clarify the species boundaries. Mertens20 stated that E. j. familiaris occurred in Türkiye. Besides, Fuhn and Vancea33 also accepted E. j. familiaris occurrence in Türkiye and expanded the range of E. j. turcicus from the Balkan Peninsula to eastern Türkiye. Yet, Başoğlu and Bilgin21 suggested that the Anatolian specimens were intermediate between E. j. turcicus and E. j. familiaris. Then, Başoğlu and Baran22 accepted that three subspecies are distributed in Anatolia; E. j. jaculus in Şanlıurfa province (Southeastern Anatolia), E. j. turcicus in western and southern Anatolia, and E. j. familiaris in central and eastern Anatolia. Tokar15 suggested E. jaculus was a monotypic species and that turcicus and familiaris subspecies should be considered as synonyms. Previous studies were mainly focused on pholidosis and coloration to recognize the subspecies7,15,34. Eskandarzadeh et al.18 have claimed that Iraqi and western Iran populations of Eryx which were known previously as Eryx jaculus belong to different species.

As explained above, the taxonomy of the Anatolian Javelin Sand Boa populations is still problematic and specimens from those populations should be taxonomically re-evaluated. This study aims to provide detailed descriptions of the specimens from Anatolia to contribute to clarifying the taxonomic status of the species, as well as fill gaps its geographic distribution. The working hypothesis of this study is that the morphological variation observed among Anatolian populations of Eryx jaculus reflects clinal and intraspecific variability rather than discrete subspecific differentiation. This hypothesis was evaluated through comparative meristic, morphometric, and pattern-based analyses across geographically distant localities.

Materials and methods

A total 25 specimens (6 juveniles and 19 adults) were collected during the field studies carried out by the authors, colleagues, and contributors from 2004 to 2019 (Table 1). Most specimens were collected within the framework of the National Biodiversity Inventory and Monitoring Projects (Adana, Şanlıurfa, Batman, Adıyaman, Muş, Siirt, and Elazığ provinces) coordinated by the Republic of Türkiye Ministry of Forestry and Water Affairs General Directorate of Nature Conservation and National Parks. The authors have read the ARRIVE guidelines and the study was carried out in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, and were approved by the local ethics committee of Dicle University (protocol code E-35582840-020-705137, date of approval: 16.05.2024). All collected specimens were initially fixed with 96% ethanol and later preserved in 70% ethanol. The specimens were deposited in the Zoology Department of Adıyaman University, Adıyaman province, Türkiye. Direct examination of historical type specimens representing objective synonyms was not possible within the scope of the present study; therefore, taxonomic comparisons involving nominal taxa were based on original descriptions, high-resolution published plates, and subsequent authoritative revisions.

Three pattern types were considered according to Tokar and Obst8:

Type 1 (Discreet): The background color is light, with scattered dark spots without touching each other. The spots are often spread out lengthways, rarely rounded. The distance between the spots is larger than the spot width. The upper side and the flanks covered with isolated, very small spots that only take up 1–2 scales.

Type 2 (Standard): The background as in type 1, only the dark spots are more frequent. The distance between them is approximately the same as the width of the spots. The outer edges flow into one another, forming a zigzag or checkerboard-like dark pattern. The smaller spots on the flanks are very dense and form almost a closed dark line.

Type 3 (negative): The dark spots on the back merge with each other and with the dark base color of the flanks. The light base color, as in type 1 and type 2, is only preserved in the form of light spots. This creates a new base color that, with its light spots, looks like a negative imprint of type 1.

Results

Eryx jaculus is distributed commonly along Thrace, West, South and East Regions of Anatolia and recorded rarely in central Anatolia in Türkiye. There is no record on the north-side of mountains in the Black Sea region (North of Anatolia). With this study, 25 specimens of Eryx jaculus that were collected from Şanlıurfa, Gaziantep, Adıyaman, Elazığ, Batman, Muş, Kars, Mersin, Kırklareli, Adana and İzmir provinces (Fig. 1), in Türkiye were evaluated morphologically (Table 3). The number of internasals were 2 (100%) in all specimens. Postinternasal scales was predominantly two (88% of specimens), with three specimens (12%) (ZMADYU 186/2007, 34/2019-1, 2) exhibiting three scales. The number of scales between eyes was predominantly four (80% of specimens; n = 20), with four specimens (16%; n = 4) exhibiting three scales and only one specimen had five (4%). In most specimens (80%; n = 20), the number of scales around the left eye was eight (40%; n = 10) or nine (40%; n = 10), whereas in five specimens (20%; n = 5) it was ten. whereas the number of scales around the right eye was eight in ten specimens (40%; n = 10), nine in fourteen specimens (56%), and ten in only one specimen (4%). In all specimens, number of scales between eye and nasal was counted as three on both sides. The number of left supralabial scales was eight in eight specimens (32%), nine in twelve specimens (48%), and ten in five specimens (20%), whereas number of right supralabial scales was eight in five specimens (20%), nine in sixteen specimens (64%), and ten in four specimens (16%). The second upper labials (2nd LAB) are higher than the third one in 20 specimens (83%), but Kurudere (Kars, ZMADYU 132/2012) and Serinbayır (Bitlis, Photographical material) specimens have equal or shorter second upper labial relative to the third ones. Ventrals ranged 153–182 (Mean: 169.08 ± 7.23), Dorsals 41–49 (45.46 ± 2.57) and subcaudals 15–34 (mean: 26.21 ± 5.20). Discussion

The nominate race of E. jaculus is claimed to be distributed in Şanlıurfa (in Türkiye) in the literature records23. Başoğlu and Baran22 stated that the venter of a sample collected from Şanlıurfa province was spotless and included in nominate form. In this study, it was found that 5 of the collected specimens were spotless and 3 specimens were spotted with 1 spotted venter and all of them were pigmented under head and tail in Şanlıurfa population. Therefore, it was concluded that the nominal subspecies could not be separated according to the ventral spotted pattern in Anatolia as suggested by Başoğlu and Baran22.

Most of the specimens in this study have 2 postinternasal scales (88%), but one Birecik (ZMADYU, 186/2007, Şanlıurfa province) and two Bostankaya specimens (ZMADYU, 34/2019, Malazgirt, Muş province) have 3 postinternasals (12%). The third specimen of Bostankaya (ZMADYU, 34/2019) has also 2 postinternasals. Some Iraqi specimens (37.5%) also have 3 postinternasals and the other ones have 2 from the same locality17. Abbas-Rhadi et al.17 classified Eryx specimens from Iraq as E. j. jaculus, E. j. familiaris, and Eryx cf. miliaris, using the number of postinternasal scales (3 or 2) as a diagnostic character but this variation falls within the normal range for E. jaculus. Furthermore, Abbas-Rhadi’s definition of E. miliaris does not align with descriptions by Latifi36 and Eskandarzadeh et al.37, and it lacks morphological distinction from E. jaculus. Thus, we argue that Abbas-Rhadi’s separation does not contradict our monotypic view of E. jaculus. Tokar and Obst8 reported that “about 70% of North African E. jaculus have 3 postinternasals, some even 4. However, 37.5% of Iraqi specimens also have this characteristic. In the neighboring populations, the percentage is gradually decreasing: 18% in Türkiye, 7% in the Middle East and the Balkans, 7% in northwestern Iran. Only in the Caucasus and in the Zagros Mountains only 2 Post-Internasalia occur.” (Fig. 5). According to the results of Tokar and Obst8, the number of postinternasal scales exhibits clinal variation, increasing gradually from north to south, with the North African population representing the outermost limit of the species’ distribution (Fig. 5). Although Tokar and Obst8 drew the boundaries of the North African population through geographical evaluation and defined the E. j jaculus subspecies. Tokar and Obst8 noticed that nominate subspecies has 5 interocular scales while E. j. turcicus has 4, whereas Iraqi17 and Iranian populations37 were recorded to have 5–8 and 6–7, respectively. Interocular scale ranged from 3 to 5 (mean:3,88 ± 0,44) in Anatolian species (n = 25). It is evident that interoculars show considerable geographical variability. The number of ventrals and dorsals overlap with all populations so that it is impossible to use them as diagnostic characters. The two most important diagnostic characters for subspecies were accepted as the number of postinternasals and interoculars8,17. When describing this subspecies, the authors ignored Mayr’s three rules for defining subspecies. Because the two populations don’t separate taxonomically, in clinal variations, populations at the outermost limits of the distribution of taxa are not called taxonomically separate38. For a character to be considered a diagnostic character at the subspecies or species level, it is generally accepted that there should be 75% and 95% difference, respectively38. Since approximately 70% of North African Eryx jaculus specimens possess three postinternasal scales, this character alone is insufficient to warrant subspecific differentiation. We think that the suggestion of Tokar15 that the clinal variance for postinternasalia and interoculars, is more rational. According to Mayr38. “the most practical diagnostic characters are those that relate to some easily visible structure with but slight variability”. Therefore, we think that interoculars and postinternasals could not be diagnostic characters to distinguish subspecies of E. jaculus. On the other hand, Mayr38 states that “The difference must be sufficiently great so that it is possible to identify the great majority of specimens without knowledge of their provenience” so he advised adhering to “75% rule” to taxonomists. Northern African population, recognized as E. j. jaculus by Tokar and Obst8, has about 70% 3 postinternasals (Fig. 5). This is not compatible with Mayr’s “75% rule”. On the other hand, it is not possible to identify some Iraqi and Anatolian specimens without knowledge of their provenience. If a diagnostic character is seen at 20% in one population and 70% in another, it is normal for a significant difference to occur when compared statistically. However, this statistical difference is not taxonomically sufficient to define a new taxon. Though, according to Mayr39 “not more than one subspecies of any one polytypic species can exist in breeding condition in any one area … Some authors began to name as subspecies every population they could prove distinguishable. Although such population may be statistically different, they are not necessarily taxonomically different, for the two terms do not coincide. This is important, because the work of the population geneticist has proved that in sexually reproducing species no two natural populations are genetically identical”. Unfortunately, in recent years, there has been a trend to define new species or subspecies, ignoring the taxonomic rules related to taxon definition.

Tokar and Obst8 determined 3 dorsal type patterns for E. jaculus. For our specimens, pattern type 3 (negative) is predominant with 18 specimens and dorsal pattern type 2 (standard) 7 specimens which is compatible with Tokar and Obst8 and, Tokar15.

Based on our findings, in this study because of not having enough taxonomic character differences to distinguish at the species or subspecies level, we suggest that E. jaculus should be monotypic species as previously suggested by Tokar15 until new evidence. It should be noted that the present study is primarily morphology-based and does not incorporate osteological, hemipenial, or molecular datasets. Although integrative taxonomy approaches are increasingly encouraged, the consistent overlap observed in external morphological characters across Anatolian populations already provides strong evidence against stable subspecific boundaries. Future studies combining genomic, osteological, and reproductive morphology data will undoubtedly provide a more comprehensive systematic framework.
