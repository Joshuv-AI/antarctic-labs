Is Tori seeing red? A lesson for first-year biology that unveils the complexity of phenotypic variation with pedigree analysis
Authors: Ahmad Mohammed Kamal, Si-ah Choi, Aoniya Colynn, Ashleigh Wood, Blaire J. Steinwand, Pamela Kalas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductory biology instructors contend with the tension between the inherent complexity of the discipline and cognitively overloading their students. While oversimplification may promote misconceptions, without sufficient simplification, students may fail to grasp a basic understanding of disciplinary concepts. In genetics education, evidence is mounting that the risks associated with oversimplifying far outweigh the benefits, as emphasizing Mendelian principles while neglecting the role of environment not only fails to reflect our current understanding of genes and genomes but can also inadvertently reinforce the misinformed belief that genes alone determine people’s physical, cognitive, and behavioral characteristics. Examples and activities featuring human pedigrees can be particularly insidious. While excellent tools for helping students apply fundamental genetics concepts and practice analytical skills, they reduce phenotypic variation to a dichotomous “affected” vs. “unaffected” model with perfectly Mendelian inheritance and the underlying, unspoken assumption that phenotype is entirely determined by genotype. There is a need for instructional resources that present genetics in a way that better reflects present-day knowledge, make light of the complexities hidden behind pedigree charts, and allow students to appreciate the multifactorial nature of phenotypic variation, and the challenges of classifying individuals into discrete categories. To help fill this gap, we offer an engaging lesson that promotes critical pedigree analysis skills and honors the complexities of phenotypic variation.

INTRODUCTION

Biology is inherently complex, nuanced, and still full of unknowns. As a result, biology educators constantly face a dilemma: should they present biology as the complex science that it is and risk cognitively overloading their students, or should they simplify it to give students a chance to develop a basic understanding despite misrepresenting biology and possibly reinforcing misconceptions (1)? Within genetics education, mounting evidence demonstrates that the risks associated with oversimplifying and avoiding or minimizing complexity are substantial. For instance, an overemphasis on Mendelian principles fails to reflect our current understanding of genetics (2–6) and can even inadvertently reinforce genetic essentialism (the belief that individuals belonging to a given social group have a characteristic genetic makeup that defines and distinguishes them from other groups physically, cognitively, and behaviorally), which can, in turn, exacerbate prejudice and discrimination (7–12). In contrast, highlighting the complexity of genetics and the crucial role of environment in phenotypic variation counteracts genetic essentialist and deterministic thinking (13–15). Explicit reference to environmental factors in genetics questions increases the likelihood that students will consider the influence of environments on phenotypes (16). Unveiling the multifactorial nature of phenotypic variation will aid students in making sound, informed decisions regarding public policy and healthcare, whereas a naive, oversimplified understanding of genetics may reinforce stereotypes and lead them to support discriminatory policies (17, 18). An understanding of the real-world complexity of our genes, genomes, and the relationship between genotypes and phenotypes is especially important as genetic information rapidly gains power in social and legal spheres, and the general public at large believes that genetic predispositions are deterministic (19).

Human genetics instruction at the undergraduate level often involves the analysis of pedigrees, which can promote analytical thinking and allow students to apply the fundamental principles of genetics (20, 21). However, pedigree analysis is typically presented from a strictly Mendelian perspective, reducing phenotypic variation to an unambiguous, dichotomous, “affected” vs. “unaffected” model with the underlying assumptions of complete penetrance, equal expressivity, and phenotypes being solely determined by genotypes. In reality, hereditary conditions are complex, nuanced, and almost always variable in expressivity (2, 3, 22). While simplified models and assumptions can certainly be useful, students are rarely aware that they are using them when drawing conclusions and making claims, despite decades-old recommendations to emphasize these “hidden” aspects when teaching (23). In addition, students do not realize that individuals are classified as affected or unaffected based on often arbitrary diagnostic thresholds, delineating “affected” and “unaffected” phenotypic categories, which are neither discrete nor homogeneous (24). While nearly 40 years of curriculum development have still not addressed the multifactorial nature of genetics in introductory biology (22, 25–27), this lesson expands the small but hopefully growing collection of existing lessons that highlight the complexity of genetics in the context of human pedigrees (22, 28–31).

Here, we share a tried-and-tested lesson that allows introductory biology students to practice pedigree analysis while also exploring the intricacies of protanopia (the color vision deficiency often referred to as “red colorblindness,” Appendix 1) and discovering the complexities and underlying assumptions that hide behind pedigrees. Unsurprisingly, when we initially presented this lesson to biology faculty, we faced skepticism and concern. Instructors were worried that students would not have the capacity to handle this level of complexity and that de-simplifying pedigree analysis would hinder their understanding of basic principles and overwhelm students. These concerns, albeit reasonable in a first-year biology course, never materialized when the lesson was implemented. Over 1,100 introductory biology students have now productively participated in it over the past three semesters, demonstrating critical pedigree analysis skills and an awareness of the multifactorial nature of phenotypic variation.

In this lesson, students explore the inheritance of a red-green color vision deficiency (CVD), protanopia (Appendix 1). Red-green CVDs are often featured in human pedigree problems, where they are generally oversimplified and presented as entirely Mendelian, sex-linked traits. This lesson combines the analysis of a traditional protanopia pedigree with the “discovery” of the complexities concealed behind it. CVDs are relatively common in the global human population, with a pooled prevalence estimate of 2.59%, which varies with sex, regional, and ethnic-based differences (32). As a result, in a large class, it is not unusual to have multiple students who either have a CVD or a color vision anomaly themselves or know someone who does, making these (largely benign) conditions relatable and affording opportunities for students to contribute personal experiences to the group discussions.

This lesson is organized into three parts (Fig. 1). In Part I, students examine a traditional pedigree for protanopia (Fig. 2A) and decide which mode of inheritance model best fits the data, practicing pedigree analysis and evaluation. Part II both provides information about the cellular and molecular biology of protanopia and reveals an apparent contradiction with the pedigree in Part I. It extends the analysis, challenging students to propose alternative hypotheses to explain this inconsistency. Finally, Part III introduces a “de-simplified” version of the pedigree (Fig. 2B) presented in the format of a real-world pedigree that one would find in a medical chart (complete with diagnostic test results and other annotations), encouraging students to think about protanopia as a spectrum with somewhat arbitrary diagnostic thresholds, which more accurately reflects the complexity of genetic conditions and the diversity of phenotypes in psychology, medicine, and biology in general (33–37). This lesson promotes critical thinking as students contend with the challenges associated with classifying people dichotomously as “affected” or “unaffected,” and they make their own diagnoses as they evaluate the relevance and efficacy of different diagnostic tests.

Intended audience

This lesson is designed for introductory biology or introductory genetics courses. It has been implemented multiple times in a large, first-year biology course for science majors at a research-intensive university. Depending on the amount of detail and depth instructors wish to include in the discussions, the lesson is also suitable for upper-level genetics courses—we have used a prior version of this lesson in a fourth-year genetics elective course.

Learning time

The lesson as described takes two 50-min class periods, in addition to the 75 min that students spend outside of class completing the pre-class assignment (45–55 min) and the post-class reflections (15–20 min). However, on multiple occasions, instructors have extended it to up to 120 min of the total class time to allow more time for discussion.

Prerequisite student knowledge

Students require a basic understanding of classical genetics, including how chromosomes and genes are inherited across generations and the fact that different variants (alleles) of a gene can influence phenotype by changing the structure, function, or amount of gene product in the cell. They should be familiar with the symbols used in pedigrees and know how to conduct a pedigree analysis (reviewed in the pre-class assignment). Ideally, students will have experience supporting the conclusions they make with reasoning and working in small groups collaboratively toward a shared goal. In our case, these topics had been covered in previous lectures.

PROCEDURE

The lesson, described in Table 1, introduces students to protanopia in advance of the in-class activity (Appendix 2) using interactive websites to explore various types of diagnostic tests for CVD. Once they have a basic understanding of the biology of protanopia and the tests used to diagnose it, students read through Part I of the lesson and attempt the first three questions (Appendix 3, pp. 1–3). In class, the lesson’s structure largely follows the interrupted case study format, which is dialogic, promotes critical thinking, and allows instructors to uncover misconceptions (38, 39). It is structured around small group work to promote collaboration and facilitate knowledge co-construction, as this has been shown to increase favorable attitudes toward learning and promote peer-teaching/peer-learning (40–42). Moreover, group-based discussions allow students to communicate their understanding without the pressure of speaking in front of the entire class, promoting inclusivity (40). While the students work, instructors circulate among groups, providing encouragement, feedback, and asking prompting and probing questions. If they observe common misunderstandings or points of confusion, instructors interrupt the group discussions to offer clarification and feedback to the entire class. At the end of each part of the activity, instructors conduct a “debrief” where they invite groups to share their answers, highlight key ideas, and facilitate a whole-class discussion around the ideas that emerged, providing iterative feedback and prompts for further reasoning—an effective strategy to stimulate higher-level discussions (43). In class, we used clickers to gauge students’ understanding throughout the lesson. After class, we used a set of reflection questions (post-class assignment; Appendix 3, p. 8) and the two-part exam question (Appendix 7) as a summative assessment of learning. In Table 2, we include suggestions on what specific questions are suitable to assess (formally or informally) students’ achievement of each lesson goal and/or learning objective.

DISCUSSION

To date, this lesson has been implemented in six cohorts of a large, multi-section first-year biology course that dedicates about one-third of its 13 weeks to introductory genetics, as well as in an upper-level course. Here, we report on our observations and the potential impact of the lesson during its very first implementation.

Observations of students’ reactions and engagement with the lesson

By and large, students came to class prepared, having completed the pre-class assignment, and enthusiastic about the lesson. This was evident by the lively discussion on the assigned questions in Part I of the lesson, with most students highly engaged with the activity, sharing and debating ideas in their small groups, and asking questions as the teaching team circulated around the room. As the lesson progressed and students transitioned into Part II (which coincided with the second half of the first class period), the volume in the room decreased as students processed new information and grappled with the emerging contradiction that protanopia is considered X-linked recessive, the only mode of inheritance model that, just minutes earlier in Part I, they had confidently ruled out! In Part II of the lesson, several students needed clarification of the terms “cellular” and “molecular” in the context of the activity (generally, they correctly guessed that it referred to genes, chromosomes, and proteins in the cone cells), and many of them were unfamiliar with the concepts of X-chromosome inactivation and monoallelic expression; hence, they sought explanations and clarification from the teaching team (e.g., “does it mean that in one cell you may express the good copy, and in another the bad one?”). Although most groups were able to tackle the questions in both Part I and Part II successfully, students were somewhat reticent to share their thoughts with the whole class as the lesson became more challenging. To facilitate contributions from everyone throughout the second day of the lesson (which covered Questions 8 and 9 from Part II, as well as all of Part III; Appendix 3), we invited students to share their ideas through the “open response” feature of the polling platform. This proved successful, as a variety of ideas flooded in for each of the questions covered in the whole-class discussion.

Part III is where the lesson truly came alive. Contrary to our many colleagues’ concerns, the “de-simplified” pedigree (Fig. 2B) did not frighten the students at all—instead, they enthusiastically embraced the challenge, and we observed many rich, animated discussions within groups. Several groups also developed their own criteria to characterize each family member’s red color vision, such as using only the results of one specific test that they deemed relevant or reliable or considering just the color vision of one eye over the other. In some cases, they assigned family members to categories of their own making (e.g., those who passed all diagnostic tests, those who failed all of them, those who barely passed some and failed some, and those who passed some with a margin but also failed some), which they sometimes represented with elaborate color-coding, further de-simplifying the pedigree. As a testament to students’ engagement with the questions in Part III, it was challenging to have them pause their discussions and get their attention back to the front of the room for the debrief. We could have easily extended the amount of time allotted for small group discussions in this part of the lesson and collected more contributions for our whole class discussion, as students had numerous ideas to share. In fact, for subsequent implementations, the lesson took place over about one and a half 80-min class periods so that students could spend more time in discussion and instructors could give space to more ideas and explanations during the debriefs.

Additional insights from implementation in the upper-level course

We used a version of the lesson at the beginning of a fourth-year genetics/epigenetics course. Although designed to be accessible to students at the introductory level, we found that the lesson generated enthusiasm and lively discussions even with a more experienced audience, who had already completed at least two genetics courses. Students were highly engaged throughout, and we observed a substantial amount of peer instruction, with students reflecting with their group members on what they had learned in previous courses or through co-curricular experiences. Topics that arose in these discussions included specific aspects of gene regulation, X-chromosome inactivation, details about retinal cells, personal experiences with color vision variation, and techniques to test not only genotype but also gene expression levels. Thus, this lesson could likely be implemented at any year level (see Improvements, Adaptations, and Extensions).

Effectiveness of the lesson and student achievement of learning objectives

Overall, based on our observations, we found that in the classroom context, with the opportunity to collaborate with peers and receive support, feedback, and clarifications from the teaching team, students were able to achieve, by and large, all the Learning Objectives (LOs) and the two Lesson Goals (LGs). This conclusion is supported by their performance on a summative assessment and final exam question on a pedigree, and by students’ responses to reflection and discussion questions following the lesson.

Final exam performance on relevant questions

As a formal, individual assessment for LOs 1, 2, and 3, we included a “de-simplified pedigree” question on the final exam (Appendix 7). Focused on synpolydactyly, Part A of the question consisted of a “traditional” pedigree analysis, where the students performed well (mean score 84%; median 100%; N = 229) and demonstrated achievement of LO1, providing corroborating evidence that learning the real-life complexities of inheritance does not impede students’ ability to learn how to conduct a straightforward pedigree analysis and support their answer with sound reasoning. In Part B of the final exam question, students are informed that three specific family members (all of them affected) are heterozygous, which contradicts what they deduced in Part A of the pedigree analysis, where the data would support an autosomal recessive mode of inheritance. In Part B, students are asked to identify inconsistencies, provide an explanation for them, and possibly revise the mode of inheritance model proposed in Part A, directly testing LOs 2 and 3. Student performance (mean score 78%, median 87%, N = 229) suggests that for the most part, LOs 2 and 3 were achieved.

In their explanations for Part B, the majority of students (170/229 or 74%) demonstrated a solid grasp of the first overarching lesson goal (LG a), in addition to LOs 2 and 3, and showed different (valid) lines of reasoning. Some students were stuck with the autosomal recessive mode of inheritance model inferred from the pedigree and proposed that although the gene in question was a major contributor to the phenotype (synpolydactyly), it was not the sole determinant, and they offered an explanation for the recessive inheritance pattern observed. Others decided that an autosomal dominant mode of inheritance model with incomplete penetrance would be a better fit for the pedigree and provided some explanations for what might be causing the incomplete penetrance, and some students even proposed a third mode of inheritance model where heterozygotes may or may not display the phenotype, depending on other genetic and environmental factors. While some of the explanations and propositions were more complete and more rigorous than others, all of them included elements illustrating how phenotypic variation is complex, multifactorial, and nuanced—not dichotomous and determined by a single gene.

Among the factors influencing the synpolydactyly phenotype, students talked about polygenic inheritance, aneuploidy, various environmental aspects that could either exacerbate or counteract the effects of the genotype, the possibility that some family members with the rare “affected” genotype may be non-symptomatic, and even possible gene regulation and epigenetic effects. Interestingly, a few of the answers specifically discussed the diagnosis of the phenotype (e.g., if someone has very mild synpolydactyly, it may be missed or overlooked, and the person be inaccurately classified as unaffected) or the validity of the DNA testing technique (e.g., maybe the test used is not very accurate or reliable, and it would be better to confirm the results using different tests). These answers align with LO4 and overarching LG b, showing that their authors were thinking critically about the possible limitations of the diagnostics in relation to the “affected” or “unaffected” status attributed to the members of the family in question.

We acknowledge that we cannot directly attribute the students’ performance on these final exam questions or the types of explanations that they proposed solely to our lesson, as several weeks of the course were dedicated to genetics, students had been exposed to the concept of phenotypes being influenced by multiple genes and environmental factors, and they had heard about epigenetics in separate lessons. However, this was the first (and only) occasion in which they spent an extended amount of time applying their collective knowledge of these concepts to a specific case, delving into the nuanced and multifactorial nature of phenotypic variation, and exploring its relevance to monogenic traits and pedigree analysis.

Responses to reflection/discussion questions

Students were asked to reflect on this lesson (Appendix 3, p.8) either individually or in groups, with instructions to answer 1–4 questions that resonated with them. There were 152 submissions—the majority of them were individual. While most students responded to a total of four questions, some only answered one or two, and others chose to answer all nine of them—for a total of 563 responses. To gain insight into students’ understanding, two of the co-authors conducted a thematic analysis on these responses using an inductive, semantic approach. They worked iteratively (first independently and then collaboratively) to build a codebook and resolved any discrepancies through discussion and clarification until a consensus was reached. Using the finalized codebook, we looked for alignment of the themes with the LOs/LGs.

The vast majority of responses demonstrated an appreciation for the complexity and nuances associated with hereditary conditions (including protanopia) and/or with their diagnosis and classification. For each of the reflection and discussion questions, between three and seven major themes emerged from the students’ responses, with some of these themes being particularly relevant to our learning objectives (Appendix 6). Themes emerging from students’ responses to several questions (Ai, Aiii, Bii, Cii, and Di) highlighted how protanopia is multifactorial, complex, nuanced, and part of a spectrum of red color vision—these aspects were explicitly present in a total of 251 (or 69%) out of the 365 responses to the aforementioned questions. Moreover, 51 (or 98%) of the 52 students who chose to answer Question Ci demonstrated an understanding that the continuous variance in phenotype (being “on a spectrum”) is not only unique to protanopia but also applies to other genetic conditions. Thirty-seven of these students (71%) used examples of specific conditions to illustrate their point, with a few choosing monogenic conditions, and described their variable expressivity and/or environmental contributions. As multiple questions prompt students to think about diagnostic thresholds, themes around this concept and around the factors that may affect test results also emerged. A few questions unexpectedly elicited suggestions for improving testing procedures, diagnostic protocols, and classification of individuals with ambiguous status.

Student reflections from the upper-level course

In the fourth-year course, students submitted responses to two course-specific reflection questions pertaining to their learning and to how the lesson might relate to the rest of the course. The most common theme emerging from 32 students who submitted their reflections was learning (either more or for the first time) about the complexity of genetics: 23 students (71%) mentioned it in a variety of ways. They talked about the complexity of genetic conditions, the importance of considering environmental factors, expressivity, penetrance, the limits of Mendelian genetics, how different causes can result in the same phenotype, and the difference between genetics as seen in (previous) classes vs. what some of them dubbed “real life genetics” (the former relatively simple, the latter more complex). These themes align with overarching LG a and LO3. Eight students (25%) also highlighted their realization that genetic conditions/phenotypes are not dichotomous but instead exist on a continuum, creating challenges when one tries to classify people as “affected” or “unaffected” (LG b and LO5). Nineteen students (59%) reported gaining new knowledge (e.g., learning the mechanisms behind protanopia, learning about X-chromosome inactivation, uniparental disomy, and monoallelic gene expression) or developing/practicing relevant skills (e.g., developing and testing hypotheses and thinking about experiments), while 15 (46%) thought that the lesson promoted critical thinking. These responses suggest that even students with a substantial prior genetics background can benefit from the lesson and that at our institution, prior to the advent of this lesson, many biology students were not exposed to the real complexities and nuances of genetics until their final year of study.

Improvements, adaptations, and extensions

The major adaptation that took place in our more recent implementations of this lesson is an increase in the time allotted to the lesson: one and a half 80-min (or 2 and a half 50-min) class periods. The extra time was primarily devoted to group discussions, providing more time for students to develop their ideas, have richer conversations, formulate questions for their instructor, and extend whole class discussions. This adaptation provided more groups with the opportunity to share their answers, thoughts, or ask questions, affording richer dialogic discourse in the classroom.

Although the use of our polling platform’s “open response” feature was highly successful, an alternative would be to use an app such as Padlet, where each group would post its answers as they develop them during their discussions. These could be displayed on the classroom screen in real time, giving students the opportunity to read answers from other groups and helping the instructor select a few to discuss together. The Padlet could remain accessible after class, and students would be encouraged to continue the conversation. For example, they could share the comments and questions that they did not have a chance to ask in class. If students do not have access to digital devices, each group can nominate a reporter, who is tasked with sharing with the rest of the class the main ideas that the group developed. Alternatively, each group can synthesize its main ideas on a small sheet of paper, crumple it up, and (when instructed to do so) throw it to the instructor, who then reads them out, comments, and solicits further contributions. For multiple-choice questions, the polling platform can be substituted by cards or sheets of paper with the letters corresponding to the various answers, which students/groups can raise to indicate their choice.

In courses that have labs or discussion sessions, instructors could implement at least part of the lesson in these smaller, more informal environments, where students may feel more comfortable sharing their group’s ideas with everyone, commenting, or asking questions about other groups’ answers. The instructional personnel-to-students ratios in labs/discussion sessions are also usually higher than in large lectures, allowing for more and quicker feedback when students are working in their groups. If the lab or discussion sessions are taught by Teaching Assistants (TAs), adequate preparation and training would be an important consideration.

The lesson can be expanded (and the learning objectives modified accordingly) for more advanced courses or students. For example, Question 6 could be expanded to have students consider the effects of skewed X-chromosome inactivation in people who have multiple X chromosomes carrying different variants of the OPN1LW gene. For Questions 8 (proposing hypotheses for the inconsistencies identified) and 9 (identifying evidence that would allow them to reject the hypotheses), students could be asked to design a full experiment that would test their hypotheses and complete with predicted results and a justification for the technique selected. Question 10 could go beyond asking to identify useful information on the pedigree and challenge students to do a bit of research on each of the color vision tests; learn about their respective sensitivities, validities, reliabilities, and applications; and consider that information to help them answer Question 11.

Concluding remarks

Developing this lesson has been an opportunity for us to learn about the many complexities associated with protanopia and color vision variation in humans. Its implementation has highlighted how a demanding activity that honors the complexity of genetics and emphasizes the multifactorial nature of phenotypic variation is completely within the scope of first-year students’ abilities—a sentiment that was echoed by an instructor who implemented the lesson but does not have a background in genetics and was not involved in any aspect of the lesson’s development (B. Clarkston, personal communication). We also suspect that students’ ability to handle biological complexities extends well beyond genetics. Since simplifications are contributing to misconceptions related to a wide variety of topics, including, but not limited to, ATP hydrolysis, cardiac physiology, photosynthesis, and DNA stability (44–47), we hope that biology instructors will consider moving away from simplification by not only honoring the complexity of genetics but also progressively “de-simplifying” their lessons across other aspects of the curriculum. Dauer and Dauer (1) offer a helpful overview of problematic simplifications and an excellent framework and suggestions for those wishing to “de-simplify” their favorite biological concepts.
