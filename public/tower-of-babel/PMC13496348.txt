American Geriatrics Society Position Statement: Advancing the Healthcare of Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual, Transgender, Queer, Intersex, and More Older Adults
Authors: Carl H. Burton, Maria H. van Zuilen, Chelsea Namahana Wong, Angela Primbas, Michael Danielewicz, Timothy W. Farrell, Rohin Aggarwal, Carl G. Streed, Aruna V. Josyula, Reena Karani, Alexia Mary Torke, Bronwyn Keefe, Joseph Shega, Noelle Marie Javier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn the decade since the American Geriatrics Society (AGS) released a position statement on Care of Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual, and Transgender Older Adults, we have seen significant progress toward achieving equal health and civil rights for lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, intersex, and more (LGBTQI+) individuals. Advances have included increased visibility, legalization of same‐sex marriage, and legal protections against discrimination. Even so, for LGBTQI+ older adults, substantial gaps, fears of discrimination, and legal uncertainty persist. Older LGBTQI+ adults experience the intersection of ageism with structural discrimination associated with their sexual and gender minority status—homophobia and transphobia. These experiences occur in many sectors of society, including health care, exacerbating disparities, impacting social determinants of health, and leading to accumulated discrimination across time. The potential for setbacks to progress remains as lawsuits are filed and policymakers consider legislation that would roll back rights at the state and federal levels. Recognizing this evolving landscape, AGS established a writing group to update the 2015 position statement with the goals of supporting progress on eliminating discrimination against LGBTQI+ older adults in healthcare and supporting integration of evidence‐based approaches to caring for these older adults across care settings. By providing recommendations on inclusive education, policy reform, and focused research, AGS aims to promote the health, independence, quality of life, and dignity of LGBTQI+ older adults. Within education, we recommend standardized training for all healthcare staff, with specialized instruction for clinicians caring for LGBTQI+ older adults. On the policy front, we emphasize maintaining nondiscriminatory measures, supporting chosen families in caregiving, promoting completion of advance directives, and collecting and ensuring privacy of sexual orientation and gender identity (SOGI) information. Research priorities include continuing to advance SOGI data collection, understanding mechanisms driving disparities, developing health promotion interventions, and furthering research into long‐term services and supports.

Introduction

The journey toward achieving equal health and civil rights for lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, intersex, and more (LGBTQI+: see Figure 1 and Table 1) individuals has seen significant progress since 2015, when the American Geriatrics Society (AGS) released a position statement on the care of lesbian, gay, bisexual, and transgender older adults. In that statement, the AGS recognized the importance of pursuing equal healthcare outcomes, educating healthcare professionals, emphasizing culturally competent care, and prioritizing further research for LGBTQI+ older adults [10]. In a 2022 news release, reaffirmed commitment to nondiscrimination of LGBTQI+ people in 2022 [11]. In 2024, the AGS established a writing group to update and build upon the prior statement.

Following publication of the 2015 AGS position statement, the Supreme Court issued a ruling in Obergefell v. Hodges, which affirmed the fundamental right of same‐sex couples to marry [12]. This decision was a watershed moment for LGBTQI+ rights, affirming marriage equality and granting same‐sex couples the same dignity and legal recognition as their opposite‐sex peers. This assertion of equality likely not only reduced minority stress and stigmatization but also expanded access to federal and state health‐related benefits [13]. The ruling led to a measurable increase in both private health insurance coverage and a gain in employer‐sponsored insurance coverage for same‐sex couples [14, 15]. Before this legalization of marriage equality, same‐sex couples were excluded from essential lifetime benefits that accrue over time to develop financial security, including Social Security spousal retirement, survivors' benefits, and disability benefits [16].

In 2020, the Supreme Court ruling in Bostock v. Clayton County further protected the rights of LGBTQI+ adults. This decision determined that employers could not discriminate based on sexual orientation and gender identity (SOGI) (see Table 1), in line with the Civil Rights Act of 1964 [17]. The implications of this ruling are profound, particularly in ensuring that LGBTQI+ individuals have equal access to employment opportunities that help ensure associated health insurance benefits [18].

The past decade has brought other legal rulings, policy changes, and evolving societal attitudes that have paralleled increasing identification of LGBTQI+ people [19]. In 2020, however, despite this progress, 1 in 3 LGBTQI+ people still reported discrimination of some kind [20]. Even in medicine, more than 20% of LGBTQI+ people postpone or avoid medical treatment because of discrimination or disrespect from healthcare providers [21]. For LGBTQI+ people, 61% report at least one negative experience with a medical provider, compared with 31% of non‐LGBTQI+ patients. In April 2024, the Kaiser Permanente Family Foundation reported that LGBTQI+ adults were twice as likely as their non‐LGBTQI+ counterparts to report negative experiences while receiving healthcare in the last 3 years, leading to further avoidance of care [22]. Even with leaps forward toward equality, we are not achieving parity in outcomes for some of the most vulnerable older adults in our society, their chosen families, and care partners.

This update to the 2015 position statement arrives at a time when LGBTQI+ rights are increasingly at risk. Federal grants addressing LGBTQI+ health have been rescinded and research regarding older LGBTQI+ is deeply impacted by halted federal funding [23, 24, 25, 26]. The recognition of diverse gender identities is under threat with an executive order stating there are solely two sexes, identified at conception [27]. A petition has been filed asking the Court to overturn Obergefell v. Hodges [28, 29]. Transgender troops have been banned from military service [30]. The Supreme Court has upheld a state‐level ban on gender‐affirming care for minors [31]. A federal court stopped certain provisions in a US Department of Health and Human Services rule implementing regulations to prohibit discrimination based on gender identity, which can affect coverage for gender‐affirming care [32, 33].

The AGS affirms its enduring commitment to nondiscrimination for all older adults and to the belief that everyone deserves respectful care, regardless of SOGI. AGS remains steadfast in its dedication to person‐centered care and to dignity for all of us as we age.

Addressing the health disparities affecting LGBTQI+ older adults and advocating for nondiscrimination is crucial, not only for this population, but also because it illuminates areas in which we can improve care for everyone, regardless of their SOGI. By focusing on the specific needs of LGBTQI+ older adults, we better understand the intersection of their identities and historical experiences across the lifespan, leading to insights that benefit all of us as we age [34]. The intersection of various forms of discrimination, including homophobia (discrimination related to sexual orientation), in addition to ageism and racism, can contribute to healthcare disparities [35]. These intersections can intensify marginalization and, in healthcare, limit access to affirming, equitable care. Homophobia and racism further erode trust in institutions that have historically failed LGBTQI+ communities [36, 37].

We assert the importance of building upon the progress we have made toward improving care of LGBTQI+ older adults—progress that has included the legalization of same‐sex marriage and employment nondiscrimination based on SOGI [12]. These steps include continued efforts to improve clinical care through education, policy, and research.

Methods

The AGS Ethics Committee is charged with addressing issues that improve public and professional understanding of ethical and moral issues that are fundamental to caring for older adults. The Ethics Committee led the development of the 2015 AGS Care of Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual, and Transgender Older Adults Position Statement [10]. In 2024, the AGS Board asked the Ethics Committee to update and build upon that original statement. The Committee established a writing group that is inclusive of members of the Ethics Committee, in addition to content experts in LGBTQI+ geriatrics. A structured literature review and examination of relevant reports and studies were conducted from the completion of the last position statement until the end of 2024 to guide this position statement update.

About This Position Statement

This statement represents the official policy position of the AGS. It is intended to inform stakeholders including healthcare organizations, healthcare educational institutions, and policymakers to enhance the health and healthcare of LGBTQI+ older adults (Table 2). The writing group comprised current and former members of the AGS Ethics Committee, members of the AGS Board, and content experts in LGBTQI+ older adult health and social care. The AGS Executive Committee reviewed and approved the statement in February 2026.

The AGS is a nationwide, not‐for‐profit society of geriatrics healthcare professionals dedicated to improving older adults' health, independence, and quality of life. Our 6000+ members are pioneers in advanced illness care for older adults, with a focus on championing interprofessional teams, eliciting personal care goals, and treating older people as whole persons. AGS is an anti‐discriminatory organization. AGS believes in a society in which we are all supported by and able to contribute to communities where ageism, ableism, classism, homophobia, racism, sexism, xenophobia, and other forms of bias and discrimination no longer impact healthcare access, quality, and outcomes for older adults and their caregivers. AGS advocates for policies and programs that support health, independence, and quality of life for all of us as we age.

Education

Recommendation 1 All staff, clinicians, and trainees in healthcare organizations caring for older adults should receive education about the care of LGBTQI+ older adults, including knowledge, skills, and attitudes focused on cultural humility and responsiveness.

All staff, clinicians, and trainees in healthcare organizations caring for older adults should receive education about the care of LGBTQI+ older adults, including knowledge, skills, and attitudes focused on cultural humility and responsiveness.

The AGS recommends that all staff receive education specific to LGBTQI+ older adults, focused on sexual and gender minority (SGM) population‐specific knowledge. In addition, AGS recommends that all clinicians and clinical trainees receive further education in the four areas detailed in Recommendation 2 (see Table 2). Educating clinical and nonclinical healthcare staff on the care of LGBTQI+ older adults is crucial for fostering nondiscrimination and delivering patient‐centered care [38]. Nonclinical staff include administrative personnel (e.g., receptionists, technology support), housekeeping, food service, other support staff, and other health and social care providers. Addressing underlying biases and understanding the history of discrimination can equip all staff with the tools to provide more inclusive, respectful, and affirming care.

The AGS recommends that training include fundamental concepts such as trauma‐informed care, the impact of historical discrimination, cultural humility and responsiveness, and behavioral health concerns. Training clinical and nonclinical staff on LGBTQI+ identities and health concerns creates a more inclusive healthcare environment [39].

Knowledge, Skills, and Attitudes Focused on Culturally Responsive Care

The AGS recommends training for all clinical and nonclinical team members in delivering culturally humble and responsive care to LGBTQI+ older adults. Many different people interact with LGBTQI+ older adults across various care settings, yet there are no national standards for staff education. Training models for LTSS (see Table 1) have focused on avoiding assumptions and invasive questions, appropriately including chosen family, upholding nondiscrimination policies, and correctly using pronouns, all in alignment with cultural humility [40]. Through a lens of cultural humility, staff should aim to continually develop skills in caring for LGBTQI+ older adults through their own curiosity and internal reflection. By providing people with a mechanism to challenge their own biases and to continue to learn from the diverse communities they work with, the AGS hopes this will improve care for not only LGBTQI+ older adults, but also for all of us as we age.

Beyond nonclinical staff requiring basic training, more extensive training should be considered for individuals involved in clinical care (i.e., staff providing direct care, such as medical assistants, nursing assistants, dietary assistants, and other team members). Unfortunately, during clinical care, care can be affected by negative attitudes, including microaggressions or discrimination [41]. Education inclusive of staff providing direct care across healthcare settings to improve cultural humility may enable these staff members not only to develop competency, but also to learn from their patients. Such efforts are recognized by organizations like the Human Rights Campaign, which quantifies the cultural competency training provided to healthcare workers [42].

Healthcare professionals frequently lack knowledge of the specific health needs of distinct and diverse subgroups of LGBTQI+ adults. People may also exhibit biases and prejudices. Indeed, many clinicians and trainees report feeling unprepared to provide inclusive and affirming care, starting with an unfamiliarity with the basic terminology used to refer to LGBTQI+ persons and communities [43, 44].

Despite the growing literature on educational interventions over the past decade, LGBTQI+ education in health professions training programs remains limited and focused mainly on young and middle‐aged adults or adolescents, with sparse to no content specific to older adults [45, 46, 47, 48]. In residency and fellowship programs, exposure to older adults, let alone older LGBTQI+ individuals, may be limited. Even in training programs focused on care for older adults, such as geriatric medicine fellowships, coverage of content is minimal, especially for topics related to care of transgender and gender diverse (TGD) (see Table 1) older adults [49]. Older adults in LGBTQI+ communities have unique lived experiences (including traumatic ones), and the intersectionality (see Table 1) of the aging process creates distinct challenges and care needs. We view intersectionality as an essential component of inclusivity training for LGBTQI+ older adults with multiple identities. These various identities then impact healthcare experiences and encourage the integration of diverse, intersectional identities into training curricula [50]. Trauma‐informed, culturally inclusive, and affirming care can help mitigate the physical, mental, and sexual health disparities that impact the LGBTQI+ community and empower resiliency [51, 52]. Such person‐centered care is appropriate for all older adults to help provide care aligned with what matters most.

Health professions organizations such as the American College of Physicians [53], the National Association of Social Workers [54], and the American Nurses Association [55] have advocated for strengthened educational content to raise knowledge and skills in addressing the care needs of LGBTQI+ patient populations. Although a competency framework was published by the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) in 2014 [56], it offers limited guidance specific to older adults. Other attempts at establishing discipline‐specific competencies or milestones focused on LGBTQI+ individuals have been isolated and sporadic [57]. There is no clear evidence that exists that training programs have widely adopted and incorporated these recommendations [45].

There are currently no established national standards and accreditation requirements for health professions training programs regarding core curricular content or competencies for care of LGBTQI+ individuals of any age. Discipline‐appropriate competencies should extend across the spectrum of healthcare trainees, including social work, nursing, pharmacy, physical therapy, occupational therapy, and medical school into residency. Understanding the interprofessional nature of geriatrics teams, we further recommend exploring an interprofessional education program as a forum to share perspectives and collaborate on improving care for this vulnerable population. Barriers to implementing education initiatives focused on LGBTQI+ healthcare include the limited availability and lack of awareness of training resources and the lack of topical expertise [49].

Developing LGBTQI+ training areas can incorporate the Age‐Friendly 5Ms framework in caring for all older adults [58, 59]. LGBTQI+ focused 5Ms feature considerations such as the mentation domain, inclusive of the prevalence of historical trauma experienced by LGBTQI+ older adults and how this contributes to disparities in mental health care [60]. Medication concerns include the potential for adverse effects from gender‐affirming care such as aldosterone antagonists and hormone therapy (inclusive of treating hypogonadism), polypharmacy, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) medication interactions for PLWH (see Table 1), and certain other medications such as preexposure prophylaxis. Concerns for assistance needed at home (e.g., mobility issues) or accessing LTSS as another area in which LGBTQI+ older adults may fear discrimination. What Matters Most remains particularly relevant to LGBTQI+ older adults to ensure that patient preferences are respected and the most relevant decision‐maker is identified. Finally, multicomplexity helps guide care for LGBTQI+ older adults and promotes a comprehensive approach to clinical reasoning and communication, acknowledging how historical context, lived experience, social structures, and both medical conditions and geriatric syndromes intersect to shape an LGBTQI+ older adult's overall health. Important social considerations include understanding families of choice and using inclusive language for the loved ones of LGBTQI+ community members. Medical multicomplexity may also be influenced by minority stress across the lifespan, requiring effective clinical care for geriatric syndromes like frailty, which can occur at higher rates in LGBTQI+ older adults [61]. AGS recommends incorporating this content across the full spectrum of health professions education.

The AGS supports efforts to establish training requirements for the care of LGBTQI+ individuals that consider the unique needs of older adults. Several accessible platforms offer age‐related information and training resources, including organizations such as SAGE (www.sageusa.org) [62], the National Resource Center on LGBTQ+ Aging (www.lgbtagingcenter.org) [63], the Fenway Institute (www.fenwayhealth.org/the‐fenway‐institute) [64], AARP (www.aarp.org/voices/lgbtq) [65], and the World Professional Association of Transgender Health website (www.wpath.org) [66]. The Howard Brown Health Center developed the Nursing HEAL Elders (HEALE) curriculum (www.nursesheale.org) that offers continuing education credits [67]. Healthcare institutions and training programs can make these educational resources available in their continuing education library. We note these training programs as potential resources and do not intend to advocate for one training program over another.

The AGS encourages educators who have developed effective training materials to publish these in open‐access peer‐reviewed journals such as MedEdPORTAL (the AAMC Journal for Teaching and Learning Resources) to provide programs with limited resources with options for curricular expansion. 2a. The Health and Healthcare Needs of TGD Older Adults

Medical trainees and clinicians have significant gaps in knowledge, preparedness, and training related to the care of TGD and intersex individuals compared with knowledge of the care of lesbian, gay, and bisexual (LGB) individuals [45, 49, 68]. TGD older adults are more likely than their cisgender LGB peers to report poorer physical and mental health outcomes [69]. They often have unique medical histories (e.g., long‐term hormone use, gender‐affirming surgeries) that clinicians should integrate into the framework of what matters most in age‐friendly care for older adults and guiding the care of complex older adults with multiple chronic conditions. Gender‐affirming care has been associated with improved quality of life, mental health, and physical health for TGD across the life span, including in older adults [70]. There is no expert consensus on specific consideration of gender‐affirming hormone cessation by age, and much like many other parts of geriatric medicine, gender‐affirming care is guided by patient goals and patient experience [71]. Many geriatrics clinicians remain uncertain about specific components of gender‐affirming care, including age‐related hormone adjustments, surgical aftercare in older age, and the psychosocial needs of individuals with intersex variations [69, 72]. Distinct considerations include cardiovascular and bone density monitoring in older TGD individuals on gender‐affirming hormone therapy, along with consideration of whether to continue hormones [73].

Clinicians caring for this community should be aware of how health and healthcare trust of TGD patients has been impacted by past experiences of discrimination [74]. Given these impediments to quality care, we advocate for better preparation of clinicians in caring for TGD and intersex older adults for culturally responsive care. Core education topics include the management of hormone therapy, the long‐term effects of hormone therapy, and psychological and physical safety when transitioning to assisted living or long‐term care facilities.

2b. Mental Health and Substance Use Screening and Interventions

LGBTQI+ older adults experience higher rates of depression, anxiety, post‐traumatic stress disorder, substance use disorder, and other mental health conditions than their cisgender heterosexual counterparts thought to be due to a history of cumulative traumatic experiences and minority stress [60]. These experiences include negative encounters with the healthcare system and mistreatment by healthcare professionals [22]. As LGBTQI+ adults age, they may experience other forms of discrimination in the form of ageism, with important LGBTQI+ community‐wide effects of ageism on mental health [75].

AGS recommends educating clinicians in behavioral health screening and interventions and incorporating trauma‐informed practices into patient care [76]. Clinicians should foster an affirming environment for patients to feel comfortable discussing mental health concerns. Gender‐affirming care also goes hand in hand with mitigating these health disparities, helping improve mental health and quality of life for TGD people [69]. Beginning by addressing these needs of older LGBTQI+ adults, a group who have experienced medical culture and culture at large ignoring their needs, medical providers can also attempt to mitigate the effects of ageism [75].

Research indicates that the LGBTQI+ population also experiences higher rates of substance use disorders than their non‐LGBTQI+ peers [77, 78]. Guided by an age‐friendly framework, training programs should emphasize the increased prevalence of substance use, how substance use affects older adults, the risks of various substances interacting with polypharmacy, and the need for treatment programs that address rather than perpetuate minority stress and trauma.

2c. Caregiving, End‐of‐Life Care, Advance Care Planning, and LTSS

LGBTQI+ older adults face additional burdens when their physical and/or cognitive functions decline and their personal care needs increase. They express a broad range of concerns and fears about navigating care with the lack of affirming social and health services and disclosure to healthcare professionals due to fear of stigma and discrimination [79]. These fears of discrimination are often based on their lived experience, which is further magnified as vulnerability increases. Moreover, LTSS present a setting where LGBTQI+ older adults may be confronted with ageism that many older adults face [80].

Declines in physical and cognitive function increase the need for family and professional care partners, either in the home or in a residential facility such as a nursing home. These LTSS may be coordinated by Area Agencies on Aging (AAA) that work with home health agencies and hospice agencies to provide services or may involve transitions to an assisted living facility or nursing home setting. These circumstances may lead to detransitioning from their gender identity and/or hiding their gender or sexuality due to concerns about privacy and/or disrespectful treatment, the loss of control and independence, and greater isolation [81, 82].

LGBTQI+ older adults are also more likely to rely on their “chosen family,” often comprising loved ones other than married partners or blood relatives. Because of fear of discrimination, LGBTQI+ individuals may not disclose relationships with those who know them best, which prevents participation in daily care and decision‐making. Respecting patients' choices of medical decision‐makers is essential for understanding values and preferences, including post‐acute care, long‐term disposition planning, and end‐of‐life care [83, 84].

The AGS recommends that clinician education address advance care planning. Studies report a low prevalence of advance care discussions between LGBTQI+ people and their primary provider [85]. Only 10% of LGBTQI+ people report having had such a discussion. TGD people were even less likely than LGB counterparts to have a living will or an appointed healthcare proxy for health care [86]. Some of the barriers cited to advance care planning include a lack of understanding of its need as well as the feeling of discrimination and fear of disclosing SOGI [84]. Besides reported patient concerns, low completion rates may also be reflective of a lack of clinician knowledge about advance directives and end‐of‐life care decisions in LGBTQI+ people [87]. These findings affirm the importance of educating health care professionals on advance care planning with nondiscrimination and cultural humility.

Person‐centered care and planning are guiding principles in LTSS and strive to ensure that older adults, their identities, and their chosen families are supported effectively. Training should focus on staff sensitivity in developing care plans, providing personal care, clearly documenting medical decision‐makers, and identifying locally available support resources and care facilities equipped to meet the unique needs of LGBTQI+ older adults. The Older Americans Act, which guides services and supports for older adults through State Departments on Aging and Area Agencies on Aging, included in its 2020 reauthorization the importance of providing affirming and inclusive care for older LGBTQI+ adults [88]. Training in community‐based settings that serve older adults may help staff provide culturally sensitive programming and support to foster psychologically safe spaces that affirm the ability of older adults to express themselves freely.

2d. Aging With HIV

HIV as a chronic illness is a key emerging area requiring further education of clinicians. HIV disproportionately affects older LGBTQI+ adults. Across the world, more women are living with HIV than men, but in the United States and other developed countries, men who have sex with men and TGD women are disproportionately affected [89]. In 2022, adults aged 50 years and older account for 54% of PLWH. Although advances in antiretroviral therapy have enabled PLWH to live a near‐normal lifespan, older adults with diagnosed HIV continue to experience a healthspan gap given greater comorbidities than HIV‐seronegative peers [90]. HIV also disproportionately affects people of color, who experience greater comorbidity burdens throughout their life [91, 92]. Data also demonstrate that among those newly diagnosed, older adults received their diagnosis later in the course of the disease. HIV infection, even with adequate medical management, is associated with accelerated aging, cognitive impairment, multimorbidity, and earlier onset of frailty [92, 93, 94, 95, 96]. Polypharmacy becomes a key concern, especially for people with a longer duration of HIV infection and high burden of comorbidities and for those with active substance use [94, 97, 98]. Many older adults with HIV also experience loneliness and isolation due to multiple factors, including the devastating loss of their social network during the early AIDS epidemic, the intersection of HIV and aging stigma [99], and the impact of multimorbidity on function [100, 101]. These factors also contribute to low uptake of advanced care planning and hospice services [102].

AGS recommends that HIV educational efforts should be directed toward current HIV epidemiology as it relates to the sexual health of older adults; risk mitigation, including HIV prevention inclusive of measures like pre‐exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), and messaging that an undetectable HIV viral load significantly lowers the risk of transmission (undetectable = untransmittable) and discussion around substance use; consideration of early onset frailty; the impact of multimorbidity and polypharmacy; and evidence‐based prescribing and deprescribing guidelines. Training must also address the intersectionality of HIV, aging, race, poverty, and other social determinants of health and how this impacts stigma, discrimination, and access to social and health services.

2e. Respectful Collection of Comprehensive SOGI Data

Training is lacking across medical specialties on how to approach LGBTQI+ care respectfully, which includes collection of SOGI data. Physician understanding and patient behavior can be disparate unless SOGI data is collected in a respectful manner [103]. Historically, low SOGI data collection is often attributed to provider discomfort, despite patients reporting high levels of acceptance and satisfaction with collection of SOGI information, although disclosure also may be limited by patient fear [104, 105, 106]. This underscores the importance of providing training to increase SOGI data collection in older LGBTQI+ adults.

Education in appropriate SOGI collection is important because the collection of SOGI data from all patients is paramount for characterizing, treating, and researching the growing population of LGBTQI+ older adults. This is particularly relevant in the realm of multimorbidity and facing chronic illness, when understanding family structures is important. When healthcare professionals do not have access to accurate SOGI information and make assumptions of identity, their assumptions can be wrong and potentially imperil care [106]. At the individual level, SOGI information enhances the ability to target critical patient conditions, facilitate clinical decision support, and promote inclusive and affirming care [107]. Research indicates that routine SOGI data collection, when conducted respectfully, is generally well‐received by patients and is not offensive [108, 109]. This process should encompass details such as assigned sex at birth, chosen name, pronouns, current gender identity, sexual orientation, and other demographic information in alignment with 2024 Joint Commission recommendations [110]. Consistent SOGI data collection is essential not only for identifying health disparities but also for accurately defining the unique needs and concerns of the LGBTQI+ population. Education must also include privacy protections for SOGI data collected within the healthcare setting. Like nearly all health information, SOGI information is private information that is protected by both law and ethical standards of practice [111, 112].

A just healthcare system necessitates not only the protection of existing nondiscrimination policies but also the advancement of new structural reforms to address the needs of older LGBTQI+ individuals. Such efforts are vital in mitigating the longstanding disparities faced by LGBTQI+ people—a commitment underscored by the Healthcare Equality Index in 2024 [113]. Equally important is extending nondiscrimination protections to LGBTQI+ healthcare employees, creating a work environment that ultimately benefits all of us through more compassionate and culturally humble and responsive care.

While court rulings have continued to advance progress, including a landmark 2024 decision mandating state‐run insurance programs cover gender‐affirming surgery, gaps still exist [114]. In 2020, a federal committee identified the critical gap that only 50% of the US population lives in a state where there is a law that explicitly protects LGBTQI+ people from discrimination [115]. Although many states and localities have expanded protections, these protections, unfortunately, do not yet cover all US citizens. Explicit protections for SOGI are not currently encoded in federal law, which has made progress reliant on more precarious court rulings and state laws that are at higher risk of being rescinded [116].

Some state legislatures have voted on policies to allow religious‐based discrimination against TGD people and to prevent TGD people from acquiring their gender identity‐affirming documents [117]. Recent policies have called gender‐affirming care and medical treatment of TGD patients into question and raise discrimination concerns, even at the federal level. The recognition of transgender identity has changed dramatically, with a 2025 executive order reclassifying sex as defined at conception—a definition that specifically excludes gender identity, which is inclusive of transgender identity [27, 118]. Other federal actions have included the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) removing gender‐affirming care, referred to as “sex‐trait modification,” as an essential health benefit that affects insurance coverage and out‐of‐pocket payments for gender‐affirming care for calendar year 2026 [119]. CMS has also rescinded collection of SOGI information from Medicaid, stating they no longer intended to collect SOGI information [120]. An executive order also directed the HHS Secretary to take action to end gender‐affirming care through Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act (ACA) [27, 121], the Veterans Administration has phased out initiating gender‐affirming care for transgender veterans [122], and the State Department also restricted the ability of transgender people to obtain passports reflecting their gender identity [123, 124].

The AGS affirms existing civil rights protections as essential for the health and well‐being of older adults and continued legal protections that forbid discrimination based on LGBTQI+ identities. The following AGS recommendations address several interrelated issues arising from the literature review of LGBTQI+ focused health: nondiscrimination and privacy policy for SOGI data, caregiving and the Family Medical Leave Act (FMLA), nondiscrimination policies, medical insurance coverage, and advance care planning.

3a. Nondiscrimination and Privacy of All Personal Data, Including SOGI Data

The AGS advocates for informed consent regarding disclosure of SOGI data to healthcare providers and for protecting the privacy of this information. Comprehensive SOGI data collection aims for an intersectional understanding of individuals' needs, concerns, and priorities across all identities. This approach recognizes that disparities in access to care can lead to undesirable physical and mental health outcomes. Robust SOGI data collection is instrumental in obtaining accurate data to inform public health initiatives, clinical care, and research, making this collection an important policy initiative. These datasets guide healthcare organizations in improving care for LGBTQI+ individuals and play a crucial role in shaping local, state, and national policies. These data delineate the needs of the LGBTQI+ population and support efforts to advocate for protective policies, appropriate funding, and research.

Despite the importance of collecting this data, there is also fear in disclosing SOGI data because of historical and ongoing concerns with discrimination [105, 125]. LGBTQI+ people have experienced discrimination within healthcare based on their LGBTQI+ identity and, as a result, fear disclosing SOGI information [106]. By carefully discerning if it is safe to disclose their SOGI and, if necessary, hiding their identity, many patients have enacted a defensive strategy against discrimination [126]. Because of fear of discrimination and disclosure of SOGI data, establishing nondiscrimination and privacy policies and creating affirming environments is crucial. Beyond nondiscrimination, concerns with privacy persist, particularly given the history of mistrust from LGBTQI+ people in healthcare, and may lead to LGBTQI+ people hesitating to share their identity [103]. Measures such as data encryption, consent processes, and clear communication with patients about how their information is used or disclosed could further address patient concerns and should be part of institutional healthcare policy [127]. While a 2022 HHS priority report and 2022 executive order focused on protection of SOGI data, current law does not protect the privacy of this data nationally [128, 129].

As of 2024, only a few states actively collect SOGI information, and none use the CMS‐recommended format, underscoring the urgent need for improved data collection practices [130]. This is a notable deficiency because in localities with more robust nondiscrimination policies, SOGI data is more openly shared [131]. Unfortunately, the routine collection of SOGI data are imperiled by a 2025 executive order eliminating the recognition of gender identity, particularly pertaining to federal data sets [118, 132]. Such orders enact change within federal institutions and directly affect data collection, threatening to halt its collection and also may reinforce fears within the community of sharing data, particularly if privacy is not guaranteed. The AGS affirms the importance of maintaining nondiscrimination and privacy pertaining to SOGI patient data. Without policy that protects the privacy and rights of LGBTQI+ patients, this critical information may not be shared, impacting patient care, as well as larger policy and research.

3b. Family Medical Leave and Support for Care Partners

According to a 2015 AARP report, approximately 9% of care partners for older adults—around three million people—identify as LGBTQI+ [133]. This number has likely increased in recent years, especially considering LGBTQI+ individuals serve as care partners at a higher rate (1 in 5) than their non‐LGBTQI+ peers (1 in 6) [133]. Despite this significant role in caregiving, LGBTQI+ care partners often face disparities in workplace leave protections. Unfortunately, gaps in these leave protections can jeopardize the physical, emotional, and financial well‐being of both care partners and care recipients [134]. By enabling employment‐protected family and sick leave, an underutilized resource in caring for older adults, care partners may be able to spend more time caring for their loved ones [135, 136].

The FMLA currently offers employees unpaid, employment‐protected leave to attend to family and medical needs. However, LGBTQI+ individuals remain vulnerable because of the law's limited definition of family structures and general definition of “serious health conditions.” In one 2017 survey, only 45% of respondents indicated that their employers provided inclusive leave policies for LGBTQI+ workers, often excluding families of choice—longstanding, nonbiological, and nonlegal relationships [137].

The FMLA's narrow recognition of “family” is a key shortcoming. FMLA protections do not extend to domestic partnerships—regardless of whether these partnerships are same‐sex or different‐sex [138, 139]. This exclusion significantly impacts LGBTQI+ individuals who often depend on “chosen family,” because fewer than half would rely on a biological family member during illness, and less than one‐third would rely on a legal spouse [20].

FMLA currently applies the following definition of a serious health condition: an illness, injury, or impairment or physical or mental condition that involves either inpatient care or continuing treatment by a healthcare provider. This serious health condition can be satisfied by a condition requiring continual treatment by a healthcare provider [140]. The AGS supports inclusive FMLA that applies regardless of sex, gender, sexual orientation, or familial legal status.

Beyond the FMLA, some states offer paid sick leave or expanded family leave. However, most do not explicitly account for chosen family, limiting LGBTQI+ care partners' ability to access paid time off [141]. A few states—California, Massachusetts, New Jersey, and Rhode Island—have enacted more inclusive protections, providing partial wage replacement for employees caring for family members (including chosen family) [142].

The AGS advocates for protecting and strengthening current FMLA provisions for LGBTQI+ people. The AGS also advocates for including gender‐affirming care under the definition of a serious medical condition. Finally, the AGS encourages the adoption of federal‐ and state‐paid family leave laws that can include families of choice. These efforts align with the American Medical Association's support for more comprehensive workplace leave protections [143].

3c. Nondiscrimination Policies Including Family and Visitor Policies and Access to LTSS

The AGS advocates for explicit nondiscrimination policies related to LGBTQI+ identities in all healthcare settings—an essential step in ensuring that older LGBTQI+ adults feel affirmed and welcome. Current federal policies and laws, including the Public Health Services Act and section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act, prohibit health care discrimination and refusal based on various identities, including sex [144]. These nondiscrimination policies protecting LGBTQI+ people should extend beyond hospital settings to other care settings, including LTSS, assisted living facilities, residential care programs, Programs of All‐Inclusive Care for the Elderly, home‐based primary care, and other care settings.

Federal regulations established in 2011 stipulate that any hospital receiving Medicare or Medicaid funding must have written policies designating a patient's chosen visitors and prohibiting discrimination based on SOGI [145, 146]. Similar protections exist for nursing home residents, who cannot be denied visitors or face visitor discrimination based on SOGI [147]. In court cases, the Fair Housing Act has applied to both nursing homes and assisted living (Montano v. Bonnie Brae Convalescent Hospital Inc., 79 F. Supp. 3d 1120) (C.D. Cal. 2015); Fair Hous. Just. Ctr. Inc. v. Cuomo, No. 18‐CV‐3196 (VSB), 2019 WL 4805550 (S.D.N.Y. Sept. 30, 2019). The Fair Housing Act provides some nondiscrimination protection for SOGI in these legal interpretations. Several states have enacted additional protections [148]. The AGS remains committed to ensuring housing that is free from discrimination for LGBTQI+ older adults.

3d. Medical Insurance Coverage for Gender‐Affirming Care

Health insurance can provide coverage for critical, inclusive, and affirming care for TGD individuals. As of October 2024, 25 states have no law providing LGBTQI+ inclusive insurance protections, leaving TGD older adults particularly vulnerable [149]. Some states ban insurance plans from excluding gender‐affirming care. Medicare, as of 2024, also authorized coverage for gender‐affirming care. For older TGD adults, life‐critical gender‐affirming care contributes to physical and mental well‐being and quality of life [150, 151, 152, 153]. In concurrence with the American Medical Association, the American Academy of Family Physicians, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, and the American Psychiatric Association, the AGS supports global health insurance coverage for medically necessary gender‐affirming care.

High‐quality research and data collection are essential to improving care for LGBTQI+ older adults. Advancing research strengthens the evidence base for education, enhances understanding of patient needs, and informs healthcare interventions, policy, and law. Engaging LGBTQI+ community members also helps shape research priorities to ensure that studies reflect lived experiences and address pressing health concerns. Once collected, data can guide policymakers in prioritizing issues that impact larger populations over time. The AGS strongly supports continued research on the health of LGBTQI+ older adults and advocates for expanded, high‐quality data collection to drive meaningful improvements in care. Areas of particular interest include advancing data collection, understanding disparities and factors contributing to healthy aging, evaluating disease management and health promotion, and improving care in LTSS.

4a. Advancing SOGI Data Collection, Longitudinal Data Collection, Community Partnerships, and Research Registries and Protecting Privacy

Standardized and universal collection of SOGI data is a fundamental first step toward health equity for LGBTQI+ older adults. AGS advocates for uniform collection of SOGI data; there is broad consensus across medical specialties and care settings that routine assessment and collection of SOGI information are essential to develop a more in‐depth understanding of LGBTQI+ older adults [153].

The sensitive nature of SOGI data requires adherence to strict Institutional Review Board (IRB) guidelines and regulations to safeguard individual privacy and confidentiality, which should be expressed to study participants. By following these appropriate research guidelines, researchers can ensure that participants' LGBTQI+ identities are protected, and that data are handled with the utmost care. This not only enhances the integrity of the research process but also promotes inclusivity and trust, which are essential for accurate representation, research participation, and improved understanding of health outcomes for LGBTQI+ older adults.

Effective SOGI data collection enables disaggregation of the LGBTQI+ population, which acknowledges and affirms the diversity within this population. Beyond collecting SOGI data, sustained support, development, and funding are needed for longitudinal studies and registries dedicated to the older LGBTQI+ population, such as the PRIDE Study; the National Health, Aging, and Sexuality/Gender Study [154]; RISE Registry [155]; and All of Us Research Program [156]. Collecting demographic data and increasing LGBTQI+ participation in research are essential to better understand and characterize LGBTQI+ older adults [157]. Without this understanding, we fail to appreciate the intersection of LGBTQI+ identities with other identities as they influence aging [158, 159].

4b. Understanding Mechanisms Underpinning Health Disparities and Healthy Aging

Older adults face distinct SSDOH (see Table 1) across the lifespan, which may reduce the ability of LGBTQI+ older adults to respond to stressors and may even result in reduced life expectancy and greater burden of frailty [61, 160, 161]. With further research, we may be able to more fully understand certain SSDOH affecting LGBTQI+ older adults, with the ultimate goal of early intervention.

These experiences across the lifespan can be compounded by the discrimination faced by LGBTQI+ older adults both inside and outside of medicine, which may result in avoidance of health care [162]. These experiences are likely compounded by age‐based discrimination, or ageism, leading to a cumulative adverse effect [163]. To better address these inequities, research is needed to understand how these issues translate into outcome differences. Regarding pathophysiology, we hypothesize but do not fully understand how minority stress translates into observed health disparities. To better protect and treat LGBTQI+ older adults, the need for further research into minority stress is urgent [164].

Unfortunately, this lens is a deficit approach that does not understand the mechanisms that may mitigate stress complications over time [165, 166]. Through adversities faced earlier in life, many LGBTQI+ older adults have developed resiliency and coping strategies [157]. Further research is needed to explore LGBTQI+ older adults experiencing healthy aging and to identify individual and community assets that foster such thriving. In line with shifting away from a deficits model, we also advocate for further research into community structures and families of choice that have promoted healthy aging [167]. We note that beyond individual experiences of minority stress or resilience, we must also examine systemic factors that influence health specific to LGBTQI+ populations [168].

The AGS advocates for further research into subgroups as well. Age‐group specific analysis is needed to provide better‐targeted interventions to care for older PLWH, TGD older adults, and intersex adults and to identify the underlying etiologies of the health disparities these populations face.

There is a research gap in general in older PLWH [169]. Over half of PLWH are now older than 50, and they experience higher rates of multimorbidity, frailty, and cognitive impairment, even when on appropriate treatment with virologic suppression [170, 171]. More research is required to better understand aging in PLWH, knowing that for many older adults, HIV has become a chronic disease.

For TGD older adults, much of their care is governed by expert opinion because of a lack of research [172]. For example, there is no evidence to guide continuing or stopping hormones in older transfeminine adults on affirming therapy [173]. Likewise, for transgender men, there is no upper limit of treatment with masculinizing therapy [174]. Other areas that would benefit from further research in TGD older adults include osteoporosis, risk of venous thromboembolism, cardiovascular risk, and mental health [175, 176, 177, 178].

Research is also severely lacking in person‐centered healthcare for intersex people, especially in older age [179]. More research is required to understand the effect of interventions earlier in life on their physical and mental health in later life.

4c. Developing and Implementing Health Promotion Interventions

The AGS recommends developing and evaluating health promotion interventions tailored specifically to the unique needs of LGBTQI+ older adults. Despite recognized health disparities, intervention studies focused on LGBTQI+ adults are scarce and even fewer focus on LGBTQI+ older adults, leaving a critical gap in evidence‐based strategies to support their health and well‐being.

Health disparities among LGBTQI+ adults, such as higher rates of smoking, lower physical activity, and higher rates of overweight or obesity among sexual minority women, contribute to adverse health outcomes. A scoping review of 22 smoking cessation interventions found that most relied on individual‐level behavior change frameworks, with LGBTQI+‐tailored interventions showing no significant improvement over nontailored approaches [180]. For physical activity, a review of four intervention studies found that two successfully increased activity levels [181]. The Women's Health and Mindfulness intervention [182], designed for older sexual minority women with obesity or overweight, included weekly support group sessions over 12 weeks focusing on mindfulness‐based stress reduction, nutrition, exercise, and health education. The intervention improved health, including reduced cholesterol, enhanced mindfulness, mindful eating, and increased vegetable consumption.

The Creating Access to Resources and Economic Support Study [183] is an innovative multi‐level intervention combining microfinance and peer support to improve mental health among TGD adults. The study hypothesizes that providing microgrants ($150/month for 6 months) alongside peer mentoring will reduce psychological distress by alleviating financial hardship and enhancing social connectedness. Although this study does not explicitly focus on older adults, the framework holds promise for adaptation to the LGBTQI+ older adult population, who often face significant economic and social vulnerabilities.

The Innovations in Dementia Empowerment and Action (IDEA) intervention, the first randomized controlled trial specifically designed for LGBTQI+ older adults living with dementia and their care partners, demonstrates the potential of culturally responsive health interventions to fill critical care gaps [184]. The IDEA intervention engages dyads consisting of a person with dementia and their care partner, one of whom must identify as LGBTQI+. It adapts the evidence‐based Reducing Disability in Alzheimer's Disease program by integrating LGBTQI+‐specific empowerment practices and trauma‐informed care. This innovative approach holds promise in addressing the complexities of geriatric syndromes among LGBTQI+ older adults, a population often underserved in dementia care. This study provides a compelling model for future research efforts to enhance care in areas facing disparities.

Finally, the AGS suggests more rigorous research to evaluate the long‐term impacts of training for the care of LGBTQI+ older adults, particularly on patient‐reported behavioral change and outcomes.

4d. Improving Care of LGBTQI+ Older Adults in Long‐Term Care Settings

The growing population of LGBTQI+ older adults, coupled with a higher burden of frailty, comorbid conditions, functional limitations, and behavioral health concerns, as well as reliance on families of choice, may lead to an increased need for LTSS. Despite these needs and documented history of discrimination, little is known about the experiences of LGBTQI+ older adults receiving LTSS. It is imperative to understand the prevalence of LTSS use among LGBTQI+ older adults, specifically characterizing the use of home health, assisted living, and nursing home services. Areas to explore include the quality of care received and the subjective experience of LGBTQI+ older adults in terms of safety, sense of belonging, and discrimination experienced in LTSS, in addition to staff cultural competency and humility and their effect on resident experience. Further research documenting these healthcare outcomes and experiences of LGBTQI+ adults in nursing homes is crucial to guide healthcare interventions.

Conclusion

LGBTQI+ older adults embody the intersection of two historically marginalized groups: older adults and sexual and gender minorities.

Healthcare systems should recognize and accommodate the distinct challenges and strengths of these groups to meet their needs. In the US, despite important legal and societal strides since the 2015 AGS Position Statement was published, LGBTQI+ older adults still face pronounced disparities in care delivery, policy protections, and research inclusion, all within the context of a rapidly changing political climate. The rights of LGBTQI+ individuals are increasingly at risk. Furthermore, previous strides toward nondiscrimination in healthcare are precarious, and the AGS believes these must be preserved and strengthened. Clinicians must remain educated and culturally humble in their care of LGBTQI+ individuals, particularly older adults who often confront intersecting forms of marginalization.

The AGS vision for the future is a society in which we are all able to contribute to our communities and maintain our health, safety, and independence as we age. The Society believes everyone should have access to high‐quality, person‐centered care informed by geriatrics principles and free of ageism and other forms of discrimination. We are committed to preserving and enhancing protections and rights for LGBTQI+ people as they age and as we look to a future in which healthcare is free of all forms of discrimination and bias. The AGS calls on policymakers, healthcare institutions, and healthcare professionals to implement the recommendations outlined in this position statement. By fostering collaborative interprofessional efforts and embedding these principles into training programs, organizational policies, and research agendas, we can create a more just and inclusive system of care. In championing these reforms, the AGS affirms that LGBTQI+ older adults, like all older adults, deserve healthcare services that respect their identities, honor their histories, and safeguard their dignity at every stage of life.
