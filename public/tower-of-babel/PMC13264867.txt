Association of respiratory viral detection with complex febrile seizures and hospitalisation in children after the COVID-19 pandemic: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Ho-Young Song, So-Hyun Paek, Jae-Hyun Kwon, Soo Hyun Park, Min-Jung Kim, Young-Hoon Byun, Jin Hee Kim, Sung-Ha Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-COVID-19 changes in respiratory viral circulation may influence febrile seizures (FS) presentation, but associations between viral detection, co-detection and outcomes remain unclear. We evaluated associations between respiratory viral detection and clinical outcomes in children.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed children aged 6–60 months with FS who underwent multiplex respiratory virus reverse transcriptase-PCR (RT-PCR) in a tertiary paediatric emergency department between April 2023 and March 2025. Logistic regression assessed the associations of individual viruses and viral co-detection (≥2 viruses) with the risks of complex febrile seizures (CFS) and hospitalisation, adjusting for covariates (age, sex, season, Korean Triage and Acuity Scale, mental status, body temperature, peripheral oxygen saturation and family history).ResultsAmong 558 children, 95 (17.0%) had CFS. Parainfluenza virus (PIV) (adjusted OR 0.38, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.89) and seasonal coronaviruses (adjusted OR 0.33, 95% CI 0.12 to 0.89) were associated with lower odds of CFS. PIV (adjusted OR 0.37, 95% CI 0.17 to 0.80) and respiratory syncytial virus (adjusted OR 0.32, 95% CI 0.13 to 0.78) were associated with lower odds of hospitalisation. Viral co-detection occurred in 47.8%, and each additional virus was associated with lower odds of CFS (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.44 to 0.88) and hospitalisation (OR 0.66, 95% CI 0.49 to 0.90).ConclusionsDetection of certain respiratory viruses and viral co-detection was associated with lower odds of CFS and hospitalisation. Viral co-detection did not uniformly indicate greater severity in FS, supporting cautious interpretation of multiplex RT-PCR results. Residual confounding and selection bias related to the retrospective design and clinician-directed testing should be considered.Keywords: COVID-19; Child; Seizures, Febrile; Neurology; Virology

Introduction

Febrile seizures (FS) are defined as seizure events accompanied by fever (≥38℃) in children without underlying neurodevelopmental disorders, occurring in the absence of central nervous system (CNS) infection or metabolic abnormalities.1 FS represent one of the most common seizure disorders in children aged 6 to 60 months, affecting 2%–5% of the paediatric population worldwide.1 These seizures are typically classified as either simple febrile seizures (SFS)—brief (<15 min) and generalised—or complex febrile seizures (CFS), which last longer than 15 min, recur within a single febrile episode, or present with focal features. In the literature, CFS lasting longer than 30 min have been delineated as a distinct clinical entity, termed febrile status epilepticus (FSE).2 While SFS are generally benign and rarely require hospitalisation, CFS warrant particular attention because they require differentiation from CNS infections or intracranial events and, in severe cases, urgent stabilisation for FSE.3 FS are a common reason for paediatric emergency department (PED) visits and are often highly distressing for caregivers, particularly during the first episode, contributing to increased healthcare utilisation.4

Although genetic predisposition is an important risk factor, respiratory viral infections are widely recognised as the primary triggers of FS.1 Viral infections may influence seizure susceptibility through factors such as fever kinetics and host inflammatory responses, which can modulate neuronal excitability, although these mechanisms are not fully understood.5 With the advent of multiplex respiratory virus reverse transcriptase-PCR (RT-PCR), clinicians have been able to detect a broad spectrum of pathogens with greater accuracy and speed. However, the clinical interpretation of these results remains challenging. Detection of respiratory viruses in nasopharyngeal samples does not necessarily imply causation, as it may reflect prolonged viral shedding or incidental carriage. In this context, only a few studies have examined differences in the clinical characteristics of FS according to viral aetiology prior to the COVID-19 pandemic.6,8 The COVID-19 pandemic profoundly disrupted viral epidemiology. In addition to FS associated with COVID-19 itself, the pandemic reshaped the timing, distribution and predominance of common respiratory viruses.9 10 This shift was evident in South Korea, where the nationwide indoor mask mandate—except in hospitals and high-risk facilities—was lifted on 20 March 2023.11 The subsequent resolution of the so-called ‘immunologic debt’ was followed by a sharp resurgence of respiratory viral infections and markedly altered circulation patterns, distinct from pre-pandemic trends.12 Similar resurgences have also been reported in other countries following the relaxation of pandemic-related public health measures, suggesting that this phenomenon is global rather than region-specific.13 14 These changes may have important implications for the clinical presentation and interpretation of FS in the current era.

The clinical implications of respiratory viral co-detection in children with FS remain unclear. In particular, it is uncertain whether viral co-detection confers greater clinical significance than single-virus detection (mono-detection) or whether specific respiratory viruses are associated with more severe presentations. Previous studies in children with respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infection have examined the impact of viral co-detection on clinical outcomes, but findings have been inconsistent.15,17 In the context of FS, however, these associations have not been sufficiently explored.

Against this background, this study aimed to address the following research questions: (1) whether specific respiratory viruses are associated with the risk of CFS and hospitalisation and (2) whether viral co-detection and the number of detected viruses are associated with clinical severity in children with FS. As a secondary objective, we explored temporal changes in the viral aetiologies associated with FS to provide epidemiological context for the clinical findings.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a retrospective analysis of electronic medical records of children presenting with FS to a tertiary PED in South Korea between April 2023 and March 2025. The PED manages approximately 32 000 paediatric visits annually. The study period corresponded to the post-COVID-19 era, following the lifting of indoor mask mandates in South Korea. FS were defined as seizure events accompanied by fever in children aged 6–60 months without underlying neurodevelopmental disorders.

Management pathways were determined by clinical presentation. Children with preserved consciousness were managed in standard examination beds, whereas those with ongoing seizures or postictal confusion received care in a resuscitation unit, where initial stabilisation of airway, breathing and circulation was performed, along with neurological evaluation and administration of anti-seizure medications. Lumbar puncture was selectively performed to rule out CNS infection in the presence of meningeal signs or persistent altered mental status. Respiratory virus RT-PCR was performed at the discretion of the treating physician as part of routine clinical care, and parental consent was obtained for clinical testing.

Most children with SFS were discharged after recovery to normal mental status and 2–3 hours of observation without neurological abnormalities, with parental education regarding seizure recurrence and indications for return to the PED. Admission for conservative management was selectively considered when overall condition or oral intake was reduced. In cases of CFS, hospitalisation was generally considered to allow for extended observation and additional evaluations, such as electroencephalography. However, short-term outpatient follow-up was occasionally arranged when the child was fully alert and in good overall condition at the time of PED presentation.

Patients were excluded if seizures were attributable to CNS infection confirmed by lumbar puncture, metabolic abnormalities (eg, electrolyte imbalance or hypoglycaemia) or pre-existing neurological disorders. Among eligible patients, only those with at least one respiratory virus identified by multiplex RT-PCR were included in the analysis to focus on the clinical interpretation of detected respiratory pathogens rather than the absence of viral detection.

For included patients, the following data were collected: (1) demographic characteristics (age, sex, season of presentation, family history of FS), (2) clinical information (Korean Triage and Acuity Scale (KTAS) level at triage, a five-level national emergency triage system; mental status at presentation; initial vital signs including body temperature and peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (SpO2) and hospitalisation status) and (3) virological and seizure characteristics (type of virus detected by multiplex RT-PCR and classification as simple or complex FS). Seasons were defined as spring (March–May), summer (June–August), autumn (September–November) and winter (December–February).

The primary outcomes were CFS and hospitalisation in relation to individual viruses and viral co-detection (≥2 viruses). Secondary outcomes included annual and monthly trends in FS-associated respiratory viruses during the post-COVID-19 period (from April 2023 onward).

Detection of pathogens

For FS patients in the PED, multiplex RT-PCR was performed during the PED stay or within 12 hours of admission when parental consent was obtained. Testing was conducted on nasopharyngeal swab specimens using the BIOFIRE Respiratory 2.1 plus panel (bioMérieux, Salt Lake City, UT, USA), which detects a wide range of respiratory pathogens, including adenovirus (AdV), rhinovirus/enterovirus (RV/EV), influenza virus A/B, parainfluenza virus (PIV), RSV, human metapneumovirus (hMPV), SARS-CoV-2, Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus, seasonal coronaviruses (229E, HKU1, NL63 and OC43), Bordetella pertussis, Bordetella parapertussis, Chlamydia pneumoniae and Mycoplasma pneumoniae. In addition, BioFire Meningitis/Encephalitis panel (bioMérieux, Salt Lake City, UT, USA) was performed in all patients who underwent lumbar puncture due to suspected CNS infection. This panel included bacterial pathogens (Escherichia coli K1, Haemophilus influenzae, Listeria monocytogenes, Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus agalactiae, Streptococcus pneumoniae) and viral/fungal pathogens (cytomegalovirus, enterovirus, herpes simplex virus type 1/2, human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6), human parechovirus, varicella-zoster virus and Cryptococcus neoformans/gattii).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as medians with IQRs, and categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed for continuous variables, and either the t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test was applied depending on whether the normality assumption was satisfied. For categorical variables, expected cell counts were examined to determine whether the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was appropriate.

Logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate associations of respiratory viral variables with CFS and hospitalisation. Exposure variables were defined as follows: each respiratory virus was coded as a binary variable (present vs absent), viral co-detection was defined as the detection of two or more viruses and virus count was defined as the total number of detected viruses per patient and treated as a continuous variable. Model 1 included each virus individually, while Model 2 included all eight viruses simultaneously; both models were adjusted for relevant covariates. We also evaluated whether the count of detected viruses, after adjusting for covariates, was associated with CFS or hospitalisation.

Covariates included age, body temperature and SpO₂, which were entered as continuous linear terms without categorisation. Sex (male vs female), season (spring, summer, autumn, winter) and family history of FS (yes vs no) were included as categorical variables, with male, spring and no as the respective reference categories. KTAS was dichotomised into two groups (levels 1–2 vs 3–4) and entered as a binary variable, with levels 1–2 as the reference category; KTAS level 5 was not present in the study population. Mental status was classified into three categories (alert, postictal confusion and ongoing seizure status) and entered as a categorical variable, with alert as the reference category. The assumption of linearity was not formally assessed. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. No missing data were present, and all analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach. Because respiratory virus RT-PCR testing was performed at the discretion of the treating clinician in this retrospective study, the analysis may be subject to selection bias and residual confounding.

Temporal trends were assessed using logistic regression, with year and month as independent variables and virus occurrence at each time point as the dependent variable. Trends were summarised using beta coefficients. Two-sided p values were reported where appropriate.

All analyses were performed using R V.4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study.

Ethics statement

This retrospective observational study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of CHA Bundang Medical Center (approval number: CHAMC 2024–12-023; approval date: 30 December 2024), and the requirement for informed consent for retrospective research use of anonymised clinical data was waived by the Institutional Review Board because of the retrospective study design. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

The patient flow diagram is depicted in figure 1. From 1 April 2023 to 31 March 2025, a total of 627 children aged 6 to 60 months who presented with fever-associated seizure to the PED and underwent multiplex RT-PCR testing were screened. Of these, four were excluded due to underlying neurological disorders (two with Dravet syndrome, one with Wolf–Hirschhorn syndrome and one with hydrocephalus secondary to Arnold–Chiari malformation). Lumbar puncture was performed in 54 patients, and seven patients (1.1% of the total cohort) were excluded following detection of pathogens in the CSF (five HHV-6 and two enterovirus). In addition, 58 patients (9.3%) were excluded because multiplex RT-PCR was negative for all pathogens. Among the remaining, 558 patients with at least one pathogen detected, 463 (83.0%) were diagnosed with SFS and 95 (17.0%) with CFS.

Most patients (95.7%) were triaged as KTAS levels 3–4, while 4.3% were classified as levels 1–2. The proportion of KTAS 1–2 (indicating higher severity) was greater in the CFS group (SFS 1.9% vs CFS 15.8%). The majority was alert at presentation; however, the CFS group had a lower proportion of alert mental status (SFS 96.8% vs CFS 87.4%) and higher proportions of postictal confusion (6.3%) and ongoing seizures (6.3%). The proportion of patients with SpO2<95% at PED presentation was higher in the CFS group (3.2%) than in the SFS group (0.4%).

A family history of febrile seizures in first-degree relatives was reported in 36.9% of the cohort and was more common in children with SFS than in those with CFS (38.9% vs 27.4%). Overall, 20.3% of children with FS were hospitalised; the hospitalisation rate was markedly different between groups, at 4.3% in the SFS group and 97.9% in the CFS group. Within the CFS group (n=95), 22 patients (23.2%) had the prolonged type (seizure duration>15 min), 68 (71.6%) had the repetitive type (≥ 2 seizures within 24 hours) and 5 (5.3%) had the focal type. Among the 113 hospitalised patients, all 18 intensive care unit (ICU) admissions occurred exclusively in the CFS group; of these, 6 were the prolonged type and the remaining were the repetitive type.

Association of detected virus count with complex febrile seizures and hospitalisation

Among the 558 patients, 267 (47.8%) had two or more viruses detected on multiplex RT-PCR. Of these, two-virus detections were most common (187/267, 70.0%), followed by three-virus (61/267, 22.9%), four-virus (15/267, 5.6%), five-virus (3/267, 1.1%) and eight-virus detections (1/267, 0.4%). In logistic regression analyses modelling the number of detected viruses as a continuous variable (table 4), each additional detected virus was associated with lower odds of both CFS (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.44 to 0.88) and hospitalisation (OR 0.66, 95% CI 0.49 to 0.90).

Comparison of complex febrile seizure subtypes according to viral detection patterns

Within the CFS group, prolonged and repetitive subtypes were compared according to viral detection patterns using Fisher’s exact tests (online supplemental tables 3 and 4). The focal subtype was excluded because of its small number (n=5). Among virus-positive CFS cases, the distribution of prolonged and repetitive subtypes was broadly similar across individual respiratory viruses, with no clear predominance of either subtype observed. When stratified by viral detection status, the proportions of prolonged and repetitive subtypes were comparable between mono-detection and co-detection (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.31 to 2.39).

Discussion

This study provides an updated characterisation of the virological and clinical features of FS in the post-COVID-19 era, demonstrating several noteworthy distinctions between simple and complex FS as well as virus-specific differences in seizure severity. Children with CFS were younger and presented with higher acuity, altered mental status and transient desaturation, consistent with previous literature describing CFS as a more severe phenotype requiring prompt recognition and management.18

The first notable finding of this study was the inverse association between certain respiratory viruses—particularly PIV, CoV and RSV—and the risk of CFS or hospitalisation. While previous studies have largely focused on the relationship between respiratory viruses and FS occurrence or febrile responses, evidence regarding associations with CFS or clinical disposition remains limited.5,8

FS are thought to occur in young children with an immature CNS in the context of fever, where infection-induced inflammatory responses and cytokine release increase neuronal excitability.19 Higher peak body temperatures have generally been associated with an increased likelihood of FS.20 21 In our cohort, the repetitive subtype accounted for the majority of CFS cases (70.5%), suggesting that viruses associated with higher fever peaks or prolonged febrile illness may increase susceptibility to recurrent seizures within 24 hours.

Consistent with this, previous studies have reported that AdV and influenza are associated with higher fever peaks and longer fever duration compared with viruses such as PIV and RSV.22 23 In contrast, evidence regarding CoV remains limited, although some reports suggest that CoV-NL63 may be associated with lower fever peaks and shorter fever duration compared with influenza.24 Taken together, these observations may partially explain the lower frequency of CFS among children with PIV, CoV and RSV detection in our cohort, although causal interpretations cannot be drawn.

These findings suggest several potential biological mechanisms, which should be considered hypothesis-generating. One plausible explanation is that differences in virus-specific immune responses, including cytokine profiles and fever kinetics, may influence neuronal excitability and seizure susceptibility. Viruses associated with more intense or sustained inflammatory responses may increase the likelihood of FS, whereas others may induce milder or shorter febrile responses.25

In addition to virus-specific effects, we observed that each additional detected virus was associated with progressively lower odds of CFS and hospitalisation. The interpretation of viral co-detection is complex. While the presence of multiple viruses could theoretically reflect a greater pathogen burden and more severe disease, it may also represent incidental detection due to prolonged viral shedding or viral interference rather than active co-pathogenic processes.26,28 Notably, our findings do not support the assumption that viral co-detection reflects increased clinical severity.

Virus–virus interactions may further modulate clinical manifestations. The clinical significance of viral co-detection can be interpreted through competing hypotheses. On one hand, detection of multiple viruses may reflect a greater pathogen burden, potentially contributing to more severe illness through additive inflammatory effects or cooperative viral interactions. On the other hand, co-detection may represent viral interference, whereby one virus suppresses the replication of another through interferon-mediated antiviral responses, or incidental detection related to prolonged viral shedding rather than active co-pathogenic processes.29,35 However, as this study was not designed to establish causal or mechanistic relationships, these interpretations remain speculative and require further investigation.

Most previous studies evaluating viral co-detection have focused on respiratory outcomes, such as symptom severity or ICU admission, rather than seizure phenotype or clinical disposition.15,17 A pre-pandemic study in Germany reported no significant differences between mono-detection and co-detection in the proportions of simple versus complex FS or in hospitalisation rates,36 although differences in age range and sample size limit direct comparability with our cohort. A relatively large study conducted in Hong Kong in 2007 likewise reported no significant differences in the risk of CFS among children with influenza, AdV, PIV, RSV or rotavirus infection.6 However, as these data were generated nearly two decades ago, their applicability to the post-pandemic era may be limited.

When analyses were restricted to prolonged and repetitive subtypes of CFS, the distribution of subtypes appeared broadly similar across viral detection patterns, and the relative proportions were comparable between mono-detection and co-detection. These findings should be interpreted with caution, given the relatively small number of CFS cases in this study (n=95).

From a clinical perspective, the detection of multiple respiratory viruses on multiplex RT-PCR should not be automatically interpreted as a marker of increased disease severity in children with FS. These findings highlight the need for cautious interpretation of multiplex RT-PCR results. Clinical decision-making should instead be guided by the patient’s overall clinical status—such as mental status, oxygenation, seizure characteristics and the level of supportive care required—rather than the number or combination of detected viruses.

Beyond these clinical distinctions, our findings underscore meaningful changes in the viral landscape of FS. While AdV and RV/EV remained common, the relative contribution of influenza was lower than historically reported, whereas CoV, RSV and hMPV demonstrated increasing temporal trends.37 These observations mirror global reports of altered viral ecology following the COVID-19 pandemic—including asynchronous resurgences and shifts in circulating patterns—and they also reflect the broader pathogen detection capacity of modern multiplex RT-PCR assays compared with older diagnostic platforms.38 39

A distinguishing strength of our study is the systematic use of lumbar puncture to exclude CNS infection. Many prior FS-associated virus PCR studies did not explicitly clarify exclusion of CNS pathology; ours did, thus reducing potential misclassification and strengthening the validity of the virological correlations.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-centre study conducted in a tertiary PED, which may limit the generalisability of our findings to other clinical settings. In addition, as the analysis was restricted to patients who underwent RT-PCR testing, our findings may be subject to selection bias and potential collider bias. Second, HHV-6, a well-recognised trigger of FS, was not included in the multiplex respiratory RT-PCR panel used in this study. Third, RV and EV were not differentiated but were analysed as a single group because of their high sequence homology within the 5′ untranslated region and the limited primer and probe specificity of current PCR assays. This combined analysis may have obscured potential differences in their individual clinical effects, which should be considered when interpreting these findings in clinical practice. Fourth, the high analytical sensitivity of multiplex RT-PCR raises the possibility of detecting incidental or ‘bystander’ viral presence rather than true causative pathogens. In this context, viral load—which may provide important insights into pathogen causality—could not be incorporated into the analysis. Fifth, key clinical decisions, including the performance of respiratory virus RT-PCR testing, were made at the discretion of the treating physician with parental consent rather than according to a standardised protocol. While this approach reflects real-world clinical practice in a paediatric emergency setting, it may have introduced selection bias, particularly toward patients perceived as more severely ill.

In addition, hospitalisation, although clinically meaningful, is a disposition outcome that may be influenced by physician decision-making and institutional practice patterns. Finally, because respiratory viral epidemiology has substantially changed in the post-COVID-19 era, evidence guiding interpretation of viral mono-detection versus co-detection in FS remains limited. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as exploratory associations rather than evidence of causal relationships.

Conclusion

In children with FS in the post-COVID-19 era, detection of certain respiratory viruses and viral co-detection was associated with lower odds of CFS and hospitalisation. Viral co-detection did not indicate increased clinical severity, supporting cautious interpretation of multiplex RT-PCR results. Further multicentre studies with standardised testing protocols are needed to clarify the role of respiratory viruses in FS.
