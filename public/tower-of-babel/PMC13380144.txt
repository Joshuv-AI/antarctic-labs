DNA barcoding Diplostomum (Digenea: Diplostomidae) through the lens of taxonomy: Status and issues
Authors: Ana Pérez-del-Olmo, Aneta Kostadinova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Molecular barcoding is causing fundamental changes in our understanding of the species diversity in a large number of digenean families. Yet, after 16 years of barcoding, we are still far from reaching a global coverage and a clearer picture of the taxonomic diversity of Diplostomum, the most species-rich genus of the digenean family Diplostomidae, comprising important fish pathogens. Here, focusing on taxonomy, we provide an overview of the important developments during this period (2009–2025), an update of Diplostomum taxonomy and nomenclature for 49 species and species-level lineages barcoded to date, an assessment of the progress in several specific areas of current interest, such as species richness and taxonomic diversity, geographical distribution, life cycles, host specificity, and the status of barcode libraries, and identify problems with the use and interpretations of the new data gathered that may affect future work.

Introduction

Diplostomum Nordmann, 1832, the type-genus of the digenean family Diplostomidae Poirier, 1886, represents the most species-rich genus within the family, comprising parasites of fish-eating birds and mammals that utilise complex, three-host life cycles involving freshwater lymnaeid snails and fishes as intermediate hosts (Dubois, 1961, 1970; Shigin, 1986, 1993; Niewiadomska, 2002). Both the infective dispersal stage (cercaria) and the larval stage in the second intermediate fish hosts (metacercaria) are important pathogens that are implicated in substantial impacts on both natural and farmed fish populations (reviewed in Shigin, 1986). The metacercarial stage has also been the most studied life cycle stage because of its economic importance in aquaculture, and because fishes are easier to sample than birds. However, the identification and taxonomy based on the metacercaria are most problematic and confused due to its morphological features being few, variable and unreliable for taxonomy; yet many nominal species were described based on metacercarial morphology (Shigin, 1986). Before the “molecular era”, the only way to ensure confident identification of the life cycle stages of Diplostomum spp. was the experimental completion of the life cycle and morphological characterisation of the adult stage (e.g. Niewiadomska, 1984, 1986, 1987, 1989; Niewiadomska and Kiselienė, 1990; Shigin, 1986, 1993). However, this stage is difficult to sample in nature and exhibits phenotypic plasticity, so that a substantial taxonomic expertise is required to establish links between adults from experimental and natural infections (for several examples, see Shigin, 1993).

The scarcity of unequivocal morphological criteria for species discrimination at all life cycle stages, and the potential cryptic diversity, place Diplostomum among the candidates for exploration of species boundaries with the aid of molecular methods. DNA-based approaches are a promising source of independent data for the assessment of species diversity and thus enhance fast and reliable identification of species. The pioneer molecular studies have focused on sequencing of the internal transcribed spacers of the ribosomal rRNA gene cluster, ITS1 (Niewiadomska and Laskowski, 2002) and ITS1-5.8S-ITS2 (Galazzo et al., 2002). The first study using diplostomid-specific primers flanking the cytochrome oxidase c subunit 1 (cox1) “barcode” region was published with a lag of six years since Hebert et al. (2003a, b) proffered DNA barcoding as a rapid and effective means to identify species and assess biodiversity (Moszczynska et al., 2009). This was soon followed by several barcoding studies of Diplostomum spp. in North America and Europe.

After 16 years of barcoding, significant progress has been made in the assessment of molecular diversity within Diplostomum, and we now possess a relatively large barcode library for a range of species worldwide; however, some setbacks have also been identified (Schwelm et al., 2021). We feel it is now time for a “data and system cleanup” to help organise the existing sequence data and interpretations, focusing on optimising and improving the efficiency of barcoding as a species identification tool for this digenean group. Such a “cleanup” and assessment of the current state of Diplostomum barcoding enabled us to address the question of whether the accumulated knowledge from molecular data translates effectively into taxonomic progress and to identify errors and gaps in knowledge.

Our goal with this review is to explore through the lens of taxonomy several specific areas of current interest resulting from 16 years of barcoding Diplostomum, such as species richness and taxonomic diversity, geographical distribution, life cycles, host specificity, and the status of barcode libraries. We did not aim for a detailed historical survey of the literature but rather to present our points of view on the status of Diplostomum taxonomy resulting from barcoding studies. We highlight the important developments in the field made in recent years, provide an update of Diplostomum taxonomy and nomenclature, and identify problems associated with the interpretation of the data that may affect future work.

The pioneer molecular studies

The pioneer molecular studies on species of Diplostomum provided sequences of the internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS1) of the ribosomal rRNA gene cluster; this marker has been used in a few subsequent studies. Niewiadomska and Laskowski (2002) obtained partial ITS1 sequences for six species from larval stages (predominantly cercariae) collected in Poland. These authors identified the six forms as D. baeri Dubois, 1937, D. mergi Dubois, 1932, D. paracaudum (Iles, 1959), D. parviventosum Dubois, 1932, D. pseudospathaceum Niewiadomska, 1984 and D. spathaceum (Rudolphi, 1819). Unfortunately, this first molecular study of European Diplostomum spp. has created a problem since the authors reported identical ITS1 sequences for D. spathaceum and D. parviventosum, which they considered morphologically distinct. Nolan and Cribb (2005) suggested the possibility that the sequences of Niewiadomska and Laskowski (2002) for D. spathaceum were reported in error; Georgieva et al. (2013) agreed with this and suggested using the name D. parviventosum for the four identical sequences (GenBank: AF419275-AF419278).

Galazzo et al. (2002) obtained adult worms experimentally for three North American species identified as “D. huronense” and “D. indistinctum” (both ex chicks of Larus delawarensis fed lenses of Catostomus commersoni and Moxostoma anisurum), and “D. baeri” (ex chicks of L. delawarensis fed metacercariae from the “humour” of Perca flavescens). These authors generated ITS1-5.8S-ITS2 sequences and found an interspecific divergence range of 1.7–4.4% over the entire region for the three species but failed to distinguish a cryptic species of “D. indistinctum” (“Diplostomum sp. 1”; see Locke et al., 2010a). Galazzo et al. (2002) also found “unexpected” differences (at 23 nt positions, uncorrected p-distance of 3.8%) in the partial ITS1 sequences from isolates identified as “D. baeri” from Canada and Europe (an isolate sequenced by Niewiadomska and Laskowski, 2002) and suggested that the two forms are not conspecific. Based on phylogenetic analysis of the partial ITS1 sequences available at the time, Galazzo et al. (2002) concluded that the North American and European species sequenced by Niewiadomska and Laskowski (2002) represent divergent groups within the genus. However, irrespective of the large genetic differences, Galazzo et al. (2002) used the name D. baeri, a species originally described and recorded in Europe, for the North American species. Regrettably, the three species sequenced by Galazzo et al. (2002) were misidentified (see below and discussions in Schwelm et al., 2021; Achatz et al., 2022).

Criticism apart, the works by Niewiadomska and Laskowski (2002) and Galazzo et al. (2002) have value even if only as a stimulus for others to clarify the taxonomic status of the North American and European species identified as D. baeri and the distinction of the European D. spathaceum and D. parviventosum sensu Niewiadomska and Laskowski (2002) using additional evidence and molecular loci.

Three other studies used ITS loci alone for molecular identification. Rellstab et al. (2011) developed a pyrosequencing assay to determine the frequency of species-specific (interspecific) SNP alleles in naturally pooled samples (fish lenses) of Diplostomum spp. The assay was based on ITS1 sequences from 82 specimens of 5 species identified following Niewiadomska and Laskowski (2002). These, in fact, belong to 6 species recovered from 3 snail species, 10 fish species (including one hybrid), and one bird species (see below and Supplementary Table S1 for details). Haarder et al. (2013) published ITS1-5.8S-ITS2 sequences for two Diplostomum spp. (in fact, three species, see below) from cercarial isolates in two snail species in Denmark (see Supplementary Table S1 for details). These authors justified their identifications through phylogenetic analysis based solely on ITS1. Lebedeva et al. (2020) published ITS1-5.8S-ITS2 sequences for four species from metacercariae in four fish species sampled in Mongolia (Supplementary Table S1).

In a comparative analysis of ITS1, Georgieva et al. (2013) clarified the synonymy of D. paracaudum sensu Niewiadomska and Laskowski (2002) with D. spathaceum, assigned the sequence for D. mergi sensu Niewiadomska and Laskowski (2002) to D. mergi Lineage 3, and named a new Clade Q (questionable) comprising the sequences labelled as D. parviventosum and D. spathaceum by Niewiadomska and Laskowski (2002), D. cf. parviventosum/D. cf. spathaceum by Rellstab et al. (2011), and D. mergi by Behrmann-Godel (2013). Faltýnková et al. (2014) updated the analysis with new data available at the time (Locke et al., 2010a; Haarder et al., 2013; Blasco-Costa et al., 2014).

However, although ITS1 had value at the beginning of molecular prospecting of Diplostomum spp., the low interspecific divergence levels, the lack of a barcoding gap (Georgieva et al., 2013; Locke et al., 2015), and intraspecific variation (Locke et al., 2010a) make species identification using this marker complicated (Georgieva et al., 2013; Blasco-Costa et al., 2014; Faltýnková et al., 2014; Locke et al., 2010a, 2015). Our overall conclusion is that ITS1 and the ITS1-5.8S-ITS2 cluster are less suitable to barcode Diplostomum spp.

Sixteen years of barcoding

The study by Moszczynska et al. (2009) was the first to employ a dedicated cox1 barcoding approach to Diplostomum spp. These authors developed diplostomid-specific primers flanking the cox1 barcode region and generated sequences yielding good species-level resolution within the Diplostomidae and Diplostomum. Moszczynska et al. (2009) published in the GenBank database single cox1 sequences for seven species: D. adamsi (as “D. baeri”), Diplostomum sp. “huronense” sensu Galazzo et al. (2002) (as “D. huronense”), Diplostomum sp. “indistinctum” sensu Galazzo et al. (2002) (as “D. indistinctum”), D. huronense (as “Diplostomum sp. 1”), D. alascense Dubois, 1937 (as “Diplostomum sp. 2”), D. gavium (Guberlet, 1922) (as “Diplostomum sp. 3”), and D. indistinctum (as “Diplostomum sp. 4”) (see Section 6 and Supplementary Table S1).

Supplementary Table S1 provides details for 46 molecular surveys considered in this review. Of these, five reported only partial or complete ITS rDNA sequences of Diplostomum spp. (see Section 2). Of the 41 surveys reporting cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1 or “barcode gene”) sequences, some also generated complete ITS rDNA sequences (n = 17) or 28S rRNA gene sequences (n = 6) for all or selected isolates of the species or species-level lineages identified using cox1, and three described the mitochondrial genomes of five species of Diplostomum (Brabec et al., 2015; Landeryou et al., 2020; Locke et al., 2020). Overall, 16 years after the pioneer study by Moszczynska et al. (2009) cox1 sequences have been published for a large number of species and species-level lineages as described below. Notably, associated cox1 and 28S rDNA data are available for 19 species: 12 named species (D. ardeae Dubois, 1969, D. alarioides Dubois, 1937, D. alascense, D. gavium, D. huronense, D. indistinctum, D. marshalli Chandler, 1954, D. phoxini (Faust, 1918), D. pseudospathaceum, D. rauschi, D. scudderi (Olivier, 1941), and D. spathaceum) and 7 species-level lineages (Diplostomum sp. Clade Q sensu Georgieva et al. (2013), Diplostomum sp. lineages VVT1-VVT5 sensu Achatz et al. (2022), and Diplostomum sp. 14 sensu Locke et al. (2015)). These data collectively allow for both biodiversity studies based on species delineation at a fine population/life-cycle stage scale and the assessment of phylogenetic relationships within the genus Diplostomum.

How many species?

The design of the first primers to amplify the cox1 barcode region for the Diplostomidae and Diplostomum led to significant engagement at a global scale, as indicated by the steep species accumulation curves and the number of surveys during the first five years, followed by a gradual increase during the next 10 years (Fig. 1A). As shown in Fig. 1B, a large number of surveys generated sequences for 1–3 species (n = 25, 61%), nearly a third generated sequences for 4–10 species (n = 13, 32%), and only 3 surveys (7%) generated sequences for more than 10 species: 12 spp. (Locke et al., 2010a, 2010b); 15 spp. (Achatz et al., 2022); and 24 spp. (Locke et al., 2015) (Fig. 1B). Of note, the large number of species sequenced by Achatz et al. (2022) was not reflected by a steep increase in the cumulative number of species (Fig. 1A) because their study clarified the taxonomy and nomenclature of several previously sequenced unidentified lineages of Diplostomum.

The geographical extent of cox1 surveys is currently biased towards Europe (25 surveys) and North America (12 surveys), and this contrasts with the scarce number of surveys from Asia (4 surveys), Africa and South America (2 surveys each) (Supplementary Table S1).

Despite the significant sequencing effort over the 16 years of barcoding, the detection probability of the individual species differs substantially. Of the 49 species/lineages, 15 (31%) were recorded in single surveys. These include nine species/lineages represented by singletons (see Section 6) and six species represented by more sequenced isolates: Diplostomum spp. 12, 15 and 17 sensu Locke et al. (2015); Diplostomum sp. 21 sensu McPhail et al. (2026); Diplostomum sp. VVT1 sensu Achatz et al. (2022); and Diplostomum sp. MT sensu Lebedeva et al. (2021). Further 13 species (27%) were recorded in two surveys each: D. lunaschiae; D. marshalli; D. parviventosum; Diplostomum spp. 6, 7 and 9 sensu Locke et al. (2010a); Diplostomum spp. 16, 18 and 19 sensu Locke et al. (2015); Diplostomum spp. VVT4 and VVT5 sensu Achatz et al. (2022); Diplostomum sp. sensu Chibwana et al. (2013); and Diplostomum sp. sensu Komatsu et al. (2019). Finally, only two species (4%) were found in more than 10 surveys (14 for D. spathaceum and 13 for D. pseudospathaceum). This uneven distribution may partially reflect species rarity or commonness, but we consider it to represent sampling bias due to both heterogeneous sampling effort and poor geographical/host coverage.

Which ones? The taxonomy lens

Progress highlights

As illustrated above, DNA barcoding has been instrumental in revealing substantial unrecognised genetic diversity of Diplostomum spp. in the areas of intensive research. Here, we highlight selected surveys with significant contributions towards the advancement of our knowledge of Diplostomum diversity and taxonomy rather than provide an account of all investigations (see detailed data listed in Supplementary Table S1).

The first attempt to address barcoding as an approach of molecular prospecting for the diversity of the Nearctic species of Diplostomum generated a sequence database linking cox1 and ITS1-5.8S-ITS2 sequences for isolates from Canada. Locke et al. (2010a, b) used the primers designed by Moszczynska et al. (2009) to distinguish Diplostomum spp. in a large sample of metacercariae from 20 fish and one amphibian host, plus experimentally obtained adults in gulls (including two species from the study of Galazzo et al., 2002). Locke et al. (2010a, b) detected 12 species, three named (as “D. baeri”, “D. huronense” and “D. indistinctum”) and nine unidentified species (Diplostomum spp. 1–9). Their findings based on cox1 data (79 sequences available on GenBank) were corroborated with ITS1-5.8S-ITS2 sequences from sub-samples of the same specimens (21 sequences available on GenBank). The authors concluded that cox1 sequences were superior to – and more suitable for delineating species of Diplostomum – than ITS markers.

Locke et al. (2015) substantially expanded the DNA barcode database for Diplostomum spp. generated by Moszczynska et al. (2009), Locke et al. (2010a, b) and Désilets et al. (2013) by adding sequences predominantly for metacercariae from fishes in North America, Europe and Asia. The consolidated database comprised sequences for 6 named species and 18 species-level lineages sampled from 2 snail species, 58 fish species, and 3 bird species (see Supplementary Table S1 for details). However, the taxonomic framework of the consolidated database by Locke et al. (2015) was based solely on molecular data, except for the three named species reported by Galazzo et al. (2002) (but see below). Additionally, the sequence data reported from larval stages, predominantly metacercariae, require further taxonomic scrutiny.

The first barcoding studies carried out in Europe augmented the representation of Palaearctic species of Diplostomum by adding 141 cox1 and 64 ITS1-5.8S-ITS2 sequences for isolates from snail (3 species) and fish intermediate hosts (8 species), and bird definitive hosts (3 species) (Georgieva et al., 2013; Blasco-Costa et al., 2014; Pérez-del-Olmo et al., 2014; see Supplementary Table S1 for details). These studies characterised molecularly 11 species or species-level lineages. Matching multiple sequences for isolates from natural infections identified based on the morphology of all life-cycle stages resulted in molecular elucidation of the life cycles of the type-species Diplostomum spathaceum (syn. D. paracaudum) and D. pseudospathaceum. Analyses of cox1 and partial or complete ITS sequences helped delineate four complexes of genetically distinct lineages, “D. baeri”, “D. huronense”, “D. mergi”, and “Diplostomum sp. Clade Q”. The first study integrating character-based barcoding, phylogenetic analyses, estimates of genetic divergence, morphological examination of live larval stages (cercariae and metacercariae), precise detection of microhabitat specialisation and host use of the metacercariae, revealed that at least 6 species of Diplostomum (D. spathaceum and 5 “putative new species”) complete their life cycles in southern Iceland (Blasco-Costa et al., 2014). Collectively, the results of the first European studies raised the number of molecularly characterised Diplostomum spp. from the Palaearctic to 15 species or species-level lineages (58% out of 26 molecularly characterised species and lineages of Diplostomum globally at the time). However, sequences from larval stages (cercariae and metacercariae) predominated in the newly established European sequence database.

In a series of studies, Tkach, Achatz and colleagues carried out worldwide sampling and integrated morphological and molecular characterisation of representatives of the superfamily Diplostomoidea Poirier, 1886. Their integrated approach made a substantial contribution to our knowledge of the diversity, taxonomy, systematics and phylogenetic relationships of diplostomoideans (reviewed in Kostadinova and Pérez-del-Olmo, 2024). Regarding Diplostomum, Achatz et al. (2022) provided new ribosomal (28S rRNA gene) and mitochondrial (cox1) DNA sequence data for 15 species/species-level lineages of Diplostomum (10 named species and 5 species-level lineages) sampled from 14 bird species, 1 mammal species, 5 fish species, and 3 snail species in North and South America and Europe (see Supplementary Table S1 for details). Importantly, this study has significantly expanded the available sequence data from morphologically identified adult stages of Diplostomum, an approach that helped clarify the taxonomy of a large number of taxa. Current cox1 libraries: Status and issues

As of 22 November 2025, there were 1452 cox1, sequences for Diplostomum spp. deposited in the NCBI GenBank database and 2564 published records with sequences (2468 cox1, 80 ITS, 12 18S rDNA, and 4 28S rDNA sequences) publicly available in the Barcode of Life Data System (BOLD) database; of these, 525 are mined from GenBank, and 2471 “have species names and represent 160 species”.

The most recent BOLD data regarding Diplostomum spp. (as of 16 May 2026) can be found in two different web pages (Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2), which provide different statistics. According to the first web page (https://bench.boldsystems.org/index.php/Taxbrowser_Taxonpage?taxid=70375), there are 4127 specimen records, 2938 specimens with sequences (586 mined from GenBank), 2563 public records, 169 species (159 “public species”), and 30 “public BINs” (Supplementary Fig. S1). The second web page (https://portal.boldsystems.org/result?query=Diplostomum[tax]) lists 2394 specimens, 2460 sequences, 2302 records with species, 151 species and 32 BINs (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Irrespective of these inconsistencies, the BOLD specimen records in the Public Data Portal are currently reported to form 30 (or 32) barcode clusters (Barcode Index Numbers, BINs) that “show high concordance with species; this system can be used to verify species identifications as well as document diversity when taxonomic information is lacking” (see BOLDSYSTEMS, 2023). Importantly, the statement on the Public Data Portal home page (as of 22 November 2025), i.e. “Of these records, 2471 have species names, and represent 160 species”, is contradictory, as are the numbers for “species” in the two most recent web pages (as of 16 May 2026), i.e. 169 and 151, respectively (see Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2). Clearly, current BOLD data do not “represent 160 (or 169 or 151) species” (see Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2) but 31 species or species-level lineages (BIN clusters) plus eight unidentified to the species-level singletons: Diplostomum sp. 5, Diplostomum sp. 8 and Diplostomum sp. 11 of Locke et al. (2010a); Diplostomum spp. A-C of Kudlai et al. (2017); Diplostomum sp. VVT1 of Achatz et al. (2022); and Diplostomum sp. of Chibwana et al. (2013). Of these 39 species/lineages, only 10 represent named species, i.e. D. ardeae, D. baeri, D. huronense, D. indistinctum, D. lunaschiae Locke, Drago, Núñez, Rangel e Souza & Takemoto, 2020, D. mergi, D. parviventosum, D. phoxini, D. pseudospathaceum, and D. spathaceum. A further complication is that sequence data published by Achatz et al. (2022) for eight named species (D. alarioides, D. alascense, D. gavium, D. huronense, D. indistinctum, D. marshalli, D. rauschi, and D. scudderi) and five species-level lineages (Diplostomum spp. VVT1 to VVT5, see Achatz et al., 2022) are currently missing in BOLD (as of 16 May 2026). This omission seems strange, because three sequences for unidentified and otherwise unpublished isolates submitted to GenBank in 2022 were mined from this database: Diplostomum sp. HY-2022 (OP297848, 382 bp); Diplostomum sp. SKP1S (GenBank: OP293112: 448 bp); Diplostomum sp. SKP2B (GenBank: OP293111, 234 bp) (Supplementary Table S2). Consequently, the BOLD algorithm for GenBank data mining appears unpredictable, and the sequences from the important study by Achatz et al. (2022) released by GenBank shortly after the publication of the paper (online version published on 6 August 2021; version of record published on 4 January 2022) are not yet mined by BOLD. A time lag of nearly 5 years is unacceptable as it results in an outdated and unreliable taxonomy in the BOLD database, thereby directly affecting the species identification of novel sequences by the BOLD Identification Engine. Regarding the most recent data, the sequences for two lineages (Diplostomum sp. 20 and Diplostomum sp. 21) published in GenBank on 25 August 2025 (McPhail et al., 2026) are not yet mined by BOLD. Just before the submission of the manuscript, we found out that the sequences of D. numericum Niewiadomska, 1988, published by Sokolov et al. (2023) were mined by BOLD, i.e. with a 2-year time lag. Therefore, we suggest that anyone using BOLD for molecular identification should be aware that not all sequenced species/lineages of Diplostomum are represented in the database, and in case an “identification” is achieved using the BOLD algorithms, the updated nomenclature provided in this review should be applied (see below). These and other issues make the use of BOLD database cumbersome.

The GenBank database contains a smaller number of cox1 sequences due to large numbers of sequences for some BINs (BOLD:AAC3222, BOLD:AAF5054, BOLD:AAU1249, BOLD:AAV1834 and BOLD:AAZ0959) present in BOLD alone, but is more inclusive regarding species diversity. The user-friendly NCBI Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) practically aligns a query sequence against all GenBank libraries in one go; this tool should be used as a first, but not the only step, in the identification of newly sequenced isolates. A general warning is compulsory here, as the GenBank database contains otherwise unpublished sequences uploaded by authors with poor knowledge of the taxonomy of the group. Importantly, the updated nomenclature provided here should also be applied because, like in BOLD, most sequence labels and definitions in GenBank do not reflect the current state of species/lineage taxonomy.

The above considerations indicate that the knowledge of the current status of Diplostomum taxonomy and the nomenclature of the sequenced lineages is of major importance for further development of the field.

A note of caution: Questionable identifications and taxonomic decisions

The ultimate goal of the integrative taxonomy approach is to associate molecularly and morphologically characterised lineages with formal names of species defined via traditional digenean taxonomy, based on adult morphology (Georgieva et al., 2013; Pérez-del-Olmo et al., 2014; Achatz et al., 2022) or to recognise and delineate new species (Locke et al., 2020). Adding ecological life-cycle data is an advantage and an important application of barcoding. Establishing links between the larval stages (cercaria and/or metacercaria) and the adult stage based on molecular data helps elucidate the life cycles and enables the assignment of formal names to molecularly distinct lineages of Diplostomum represented by either larval stage, cercaria or metacercaria (Supplementary Table S1). However, the identification of molecularly characterised lineages of Diplostomum is not as straightforward as we may wish and requires a combination of substantial molecular and taxonomic expertise; the study by Achatz et al. (2022) provides support to our statement (see Section 5.1, Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1). In this section, we highlight three additional problematic cases based on sequenced larval and adult material from the Palaearctic.

Diplostomum baeri species complex

Recently, two studies provided additional sequence data for two of the closely related lineages belonging to the D. baeri species complex, i.e. Diplostomum sp. Lineage 3 and Diplostomum sp. Lineage 4, delineated by Blasco-Costa et al. (2014) based on material from Iceland. Both studies documented and provided statistical evidence questioning the identification of the metacercariae based on morphology, due to intermediate host-related differences in morphometric characters. Nevertheless, the authors associated their metacercarial isolates with named species.

Metacercaria: Diplostomum petromyzifluviatilis sensuLebedeva et al. (2022)

Lebedeva et al. (2022) sequenced metacercariae isolated from the brain of lampreys, Lampetra fluviatilis (4 cox1 sequences) and Lethenteron camschaticum (2 cox1 sequences), collected in two rivers of the Baltic Sea and White Sea basins, Russia. The authors provided morphometric data illustrating host-related size differences, reflected in the different means for 9 out of 12 metrical characters, and via discriminant analysis, and concluded that “morphological data alone cannot be used for the identification” of the newly collected metacercariae. Phylogenetic analysis based on the cox1 gene revealed that the material of Lebedeva et al. (2022) belongs to a strongly supported monophyletic clade comprising sequences for Diplostomum sp. Lineage 4 sensu Blasco-Costa et al. (2014) from one snail (A. balthica) and two fish hosts (Perca fluviatilis and Gasterosteus aculeatus). Although Lebedeva et al. (2022) provided several examples of lampreys hosting non-specific parasites, they named their material as D. petromyzifluviatilis Diesing, 1850 (nomen nudum), presumably based on the fact that this is the only name used for metacercariae from the brain of lampreys to date (Shigin, 1986, 1993), i.e. based on Shigin’s concept of strict host- and microhabitat specificity of this species. Finally, Lebedeva et al. (2022) placed D. gasterostei Williams, 1966 in synonymy with D. petromyzifluviatilis, and after combining the hosts of the sequenced materials, concluded that the metacercariae of this species show “no strict specificity to the second intermediate host and the microhabitat within it”.

While the phylogenetic results of Lebedeva et al. (2022) based on cox1 data are valid, we should highlight two problems with the name used by these authors. First, the name D. petromyzifluviatilis Diesing, 1850 used for Diplostomum sp. Lineage 4 sensu Blasco-Costa et al. (2014) is a nomen nudum perpetuated in the literature for a long time. The name was unavailable until the paper by Sweeting (1976), who described the cercaria, metacercaria and adult and applied the name (as D. petromyzi-fluviatilis) and attributed it to Diesing, thus making the name available. However, the species described by Sweeting (1976) was shown experimentally to develop in Bithynia tentaculata and not in Lymnaea stagnalis, L. peregra, or Planorbis contortus, and is thus the only species of Diplostomum with a non-pulmonate snail intermediate host (Shigin, 1986, 1993). Secondly, although part of the sequenced material belonging to Diplostomum sp. Lineage 4 has been identified as D. baeri (ex Perca fluviatilis; see Behrmann-Godel, 2013), Lebedeva et al. (2022) did not consider this species based on a wrong premise, i.e. “D. baeri has been recently re-described and the concept of “D. baeri species complex” has been shown to be invalid” referring to the paper by Faltýnková et al. (2022) (but see detailed discussion in Section 7.2). Based on the above considerations, we believe that the identification of Diplostomum sp. Lineage 4 sensu Blasco-Costa et al. (2014) should await the sequencing of the adult stage and detailed analysis of its morphology and taxonomy.

Metacercaria: Diplostomum numericum sensu Sokolov et al. (2023)

Sokolov et al. (2023) sequenced 15 metacercariae isolated from the eye vitreous humour of three cyprinid fishes, Scardinius erythrophthalmus, Alburnus alburnus and Rutilus rutilus, collected in several lakes in Karelia, Russia. These authors recorded morphometric differences between their material from S. erythrophthalmus and R. rutilus (reflected in the different means for 10 out of 20 characters, 6 metrical and 4 ratios), and between their material and the descriptions of D. numericum by Niewiadomska (1988) (reflected in the different means for 18 characters, 11 metrical and 7 ratios) and D. vitreophilum by Shigin and Stanislavetz (1989) (reflected in the different means for 16 characters, 11 metrical and 5 ratios). These differences may be associated with the different methods of fixation of the metacercariae of D. numericum, as rightly suggested by Sokolov et al. (2023); however, the material described as D. vitreophilum Shigin & Stanislavez, 1989 was fixed and processed using the same techniques. Irrespective of these findings, and based on previous synonymies, Sokolov et al. (2023) resurrected the name D. numericum (syn. D. vitreophilum) for the identification of their material based on “the site of infection, the number of excretory bodies, and the mean and/or range values of the indices describing the body proportions or organ ratios”.

Phylogenetic analysis based on the cox1 gene revealed that the material of Sokolov et al. (2023) from cyprinid fishes in Karelia identified as D. numericum and the metacercariae of Diplostomum sp. Lineage 3 ex Salmo trutta and Salvelinus alpinus from Iceland, sequenced by Blasco-Costa et al. (2014) and S. trutta and Gobio gobio from Germany (‘Diplostomum baeri’ 1 or “trout lineage”), sequenced by Georgieva et al. (2013), formed a strongly supported monophyletic clade.

The problem with the identification of the metacercariae sequenced by Sokolov et al. (2023) appears to stem from the questionable practice of describing new species of Diplostomum based on the morphology of the larval stages, in this case, D. numericum and D. vitreophilum. Notably, D. numericum was described based on composite material regarding both the intermediate hosts (Scardinius erythrophthalmus (L.) (Cyprinidae) and Perca fluviatilis L. and Gymnocephalus cernua (L.) (both Percidae)) and the site in the fish host (“eyeball and vitreous humour”, see Niewiadomska, 2003). Notwithstanding, Shigin (1993) considered that the metacercariae studied in Poland (D. numericum) and Karelia (D. vitreophilum) belong to the same species based on the location in the vitreous humour of the fish host, similarity of the morphometric features (irrespective of the type of fixation), similar second intermediate host range (cyprinid fishes), and geographical distribution. Shigin (1993) also justified the synonymy of D. numericum (syn. D. vitreophilum) with D. gavium based on comparisons of a specimen raised in experimental infection of Anas platyrhynchos dom. and adult worms from natural infections in Gavia arctica and Gavia stellata collected from the waterbody in Chukotka (Russia) where the metacercariae used in Shigin’s experiment were collected.

In brief, the link between D. numericum, D. vitreophilum and D. gavium established by Shigin (1993) was based on the location of the metacercariae in the eye vitreous humour of the fish and the strict specificity to the definitive host, Gavia spp. However, Shigin (1993) uncritically accepted the synonymisation of the Palaearctic (European) D. colymbi (Dubois, 1928) with the Nearctic (North American) D. gavium by Dubois and Rausch (1967). Recently, Achatz et al. (2022) sequenced D. gavium (syn. Diplostomum sp. 3 sensu Moszczynska et al., 2009) from two snail, one fish and one bird species (Gavia immer) collected in North Dakota and Minnesota (USA), thus allowing justification of the distinct species status of this lens-infecting species and the form from the vitreous body sequenced by Sokolov et al. (2023).

The above considerations indicate that identification based on the site of infection and morphology of the metacercariae by Sokolov et al. (2023) appears too preliminary. Although Shigin (1993) believed that a single species of Diplostomum parasitises the vitreous humour of the freshwater fish in the Palaearctic, there are at least three species sharing this microhabitat: Diplostomum sp. Lineage 3 sensu Blasco-Costa et al. (2014) (syn. D. numericum sensu Sokolov et al., 2023); D. gavium sensu Shigin (1993); and Diplostomum sp. Type 2 sensu Brady (1989). Sokolov et al. (2023) hypothesised that their material may well belong to D. colymbi, currently a synonym of D. gavium (Dubois and Rausch, 1967; Dubois, 1970; Shigin, 1993), but decided that “… at this point, the species D. numericum must be resurrected”. We consider this decision hasty and not supported by sufficient morphological or ecological evidence. Therefore, the identification of Diplostomum sp. Lineage 3 sensu Blasco-Costa et al. (2014) should await the sequencing of the adult stage and detailed analysis of its morphology and taxonomy.

Adult: What is D. baeri sensuFaltýnková et al. (2022)?

The morphology of the adult provides numerous and indispensable features allowing species identification and delineation in digeneans in general and Diplostomum in particular, as evidenced by the important taxonomic decisions and nomenclatural acts based on the combination of molecular data and identification based on the morphology of the adult stage (Georgieva et al., 2013; Pérez-del-Olmo et al., 2014; Locke et al., 2020; Achatz et al., 2022) highlighted in our review. However, this process largely depends on the authors’ taxonomic expertise. Here, we highlight a case of erroneous identification by Faltýnková et al. (2022) that, unfortunately, was subsequently followed by some authors (Lebedeva et al., 2022, 2025; Sokolov et al., 2023; Martinek and Hernández-Orts, 2024).

Faltýnková et al. (2022) sequenced two samples of cercariae emerging from Ampullaceana balthica (as Radix balthica) collected in Ireland and five adult worms ex Larus canus collected in Karelia, Russia, and provided morphological data and illustrations of the sequenced material. Phylogenetic analyses based on cox1 and ITS1-5.8S-ITS2 data revealed that seven and four isolates, respectively, cluster in strongly supported monophyletic clades with other published sequences for Diplostomum sp. Clade Q sensu Georgieva et al. (2013) from snail and fish hosts in Europe. Although Faltýnková et al. (2022) found that the morphology of the cercariae agrees well with the description of Diplostomum sp. Clade Q sensu Georgieva et al. (2013) by Selbach et al. (2015), they identified the adult worms as D. baeri, while disregarding the extensive knowledge about the biology, life cycle stages, hosts and host-parasite relationships of this species (and its possible synonym, D. volvens Nordmann, 1832) in the Palaearctic.

Faltýnková et al. (2022) summarised that their results “contradict those of the previous studies reporting that percids (and P. fluviatilis in particular) serve as the main fish hosts for D. baeri … and that the metacercariae are located between the sclera and retina or even deeper in the eye, under the retina (Shigin, 1993)” and concluded that Shigin (1977, 1993), Niewiadomska and Kiselienė (1990), and Niewiadomska and Laskowski (2002) “were dealing with a species different from D. baeri”. Further, Faltýnková et al. (2022) stated that sequenced lineages belonging to the D. baeri species complex sensu Georgieva et al. (2013), Blasco-Costa et al. (2014), and Schwelm et al. (2021) “do not represent lineages of D. baeri” and that “the isolate identified as D. baeri in Canada by Galazzo et al. (2002) does not cluster with the ‘true’ D. baeri”.

But what is D. baeri of Faltýnková et al. (2022) that they believed to represent the “true D. baeri”? Regrettably, this is a case of misidentification of a lens-infecting species of Diplostomum due to examination of limited and improperly handled adult material, which was inadequately compared with morphologically and/or ecologically similar species, and to a lack of consideration of the parasite’s life cycle inferred from molecular phylogenies (Georgieva et al., 2013; Faltýnková et al., 2014, 2022), including careful consideration of the morphology of the sequenced metacercaria published by Pérez-del-Olmo et al. (2014). First, Faltýnková et al. (2022) collected 154 adult worms but based their description on only 5 sequenced worms; this limited sample size does not permit an adequate examination of intraspecific morphological variability. Secondly, the description and metrical data of the adult form were based on contracted worms collected from a dead bird, as clearly illustrated for the two specimens probably considered “best-looking” by the authors ( figure 1 in Faltýnková et al., 2022). This material, in poor condition, is unsuitable for identification to the species level. Thirdly, the morphological data for the adult stage of the “true D. baeri” were interpreted in comparison with the descriptions of non-lens-infecting species, i.e. D. baeri, D. volvens, and D. adamsi, in more detail, but only perfunctorily with the morphology of just two, i.e. D. mergi and D. nordmanni, of the many lens-infecting species described in the Palaearctic.

Finally, Faltýnková et al. (2022) stated that the most typical characters of the material they identified as D. baeri are: (i) the prosoma being “always longer than the opisthosoma or almost of the same size, but never shorter than opisthosoma”; (ii) “the extent of vitelline follicles not far in front of the ventral sucker”; and (iii) “a relatively stout body”. These characters all depend on the fixation of the material and exhibit variation in different definitive hosts (see several examples in Shigin, 1993). Further, several other species possess these “most typical characters” but were not considered during the identification: (i) D. gasterostei, D. pungiti Shigin, 1965, and D. pusillum (Dubois, 1928) (all species with metacercariae located in non-lens tissues of the fish eye); (ii) D. colymbi (Dubois, 1928) and D. pseudomergi Belopolskaja, 1975 (species with unknown life cycles); and (iii) D. gobiorum Shigin, 1965 (species with lens-infecting metacercariae). It is worth noting that the metacercaria of the morphologically similar lens-infecting species, D. gobiorum, is the smallest form known to date (Shigin, 1986), as is the metacercaria of Diplostomum sp. Clade Q sequenced and described by Pérez-del-Olmo et al. (2014). The above considerations justify our opinion that the “true D. baeri” of Faltýnková et al. (2022) is a misidentified lens-infecting species of Diplostomum, probably associated with the D. mergi complex (see, e.g. phylogenies in Georgieva et al., 2013; Pérez-del-Olmo et al., 2014; Lebedeva et al., 2022, 2025, Fig. 2). The identification of Diplostomum sp. Clade Q should await morphological and molecular evidence from newly collected, well-fixed adult specimens.

Geography of barcoding: Have we sequenced them all?

Have we sequenced them all? The answer is “Yes” and “No” depending on the geographical region. To assess the progress achieved during 16 years of barcoding, we examined how the species richness of Diplostomum revealed by molecular data relates to richness based on historical data on a continental level. A full list of the 69 nominal species of Diplostomum described between 1850 and 2020, but predominantly during the 20th Century (66 species), and their continental distribution is provided in Supplementary Table S3. Of note, the most recent new species description was published in 2020 (Locke et al., 2020), i.e. 25 years after the previous description (Shigin and Kostadinova, 1995). These data illustrate both a greatly diminished taxonomic expertise and a change of focus towards generating molecular data without the much-needed link to parasite taxonomy.

A synthesis of geographical and life-cycle data available to date (Fig. 3A and B) indicates that regional knowledge of the diversity of Diplostomum has been historically uneven at the continental scale, with the greatest richness values for Europe (38 nominal species/26 species considered valid), followed by Asia (29 nominal species/24 species considered valid) and North America (19 nominal species/16 species considered valid), and very low richness (1–2 species) for the distinctly understudied Africa, Antarctica, Australia, and South America where more species remain to be discovered (see Supplementary Table S3 for details). The richness pattern based on molecular data is similar, but with a notably greater species diversity discovered in North America (26 species/species-level lineages) where sampling effort has been intensive (Moszczynska et al., 2009; Locke et al., 2010a, 2010b, 2013, 2015; Désilets et al., 2013; Gordy et al., 2016; Ubels et al., 2018; Gordy and Hanington, 2019; Marcogliese and Locke, 2021; Achatz et al., 2022), followed by Europe (16 species/species-level lineages) and Asia (9 species/species-level lineages) (Fig. 3A). Regarding the sequential hosts involved in the life cycles, the greatest number of species revealed via barcoding was found in fishes and snails because these aquatic hosts are widespread, abundant, and also easy to access and sample. Finally, evidence for intercontinental distribution exists for a very small number of molecularly characterised species/lineages (Fig. 3A): 4 species (D. mergi Lineage 2 sensu Georgieva et al. (2013), D. pseudospathaceum, D. rauschi, and D. spathaceum) for Europe and Asia; 2 species (Diplostomum sp. 14 and Diplostomum sp. 16 sensu Locke et al. (2015)) for Asia and Africa; and 1 species (D. huronense) for North and South America (Fig. 3A).

A comparison of the species diversity revealed by morphology and molecules shows a much greater number of species barcoded than previously described from North America (26 species/lineages vs 16 named species considered valid) (Fig. 3A). This indicates that the North American Diplostomum fauna is completely sampled in the barcoding studies, with sequences available for nearly half of the named species, i.e. D. alarioides, D. alascense, D. gavium, D. huronense, D. indistinctum, D. marshalli, and D. scudderi, and a large number of previously unknown species. In contrast, the European fauna is still poorly studied (16 species/lineages vs 26 named species considered valid) (Fig. 3A), with molecular data generated for 5 named species, i.e. D. parviventosum, D. phoxini, D. pseudospathaceum, D. rauschi, and D. spathaceum. The Asian fauna is distinctly understudied (9 species/lineages vs 24 named species considered valid), as are the faunas of the remaining continents, where both taxonomic and barcoding studies are too scarce (Fig. 3A).

It is too early a stage for credible comparisons regarding the intercontinental distribution of Diplostomum diversity. An expected result is that both molecular and morphological data indicate an overlapping distribution of Diplostomum spp. within the Palaearctic (4 and 13 species, respectively, in common for Europe and Asia; Fig. 3A). However, although molecular data do not support the distribution of North American Diplostomum spp. in Europe and Asia and vice versa, 6 nominal species considered valid have been reported based on morphological data in both North America and the Palaearctic, i.e. D. commutatum (Diesing, 1850), D. gavium, D. huronense, D. indistinctum, D. rauschi, and D. repandum Dubois & Rausch, 1950 (Shigin, 1993). Further molecular data from the Palaearctic and taxonomic research are required to assess the distribution of these species.

One of the key uses of barcoding is establishing links between morphologically indistinguishable larval stages and the adult stages of known species. Importantly, the intensive sampling and improved species delineation, identification and taxonomy during the 16 years of barcoding have significantly increased our knowledge of Diplostomum spp. life cycles. The updated nomenclature provided here helped track the intermediate and definitive hosts of Diplostomum spp. The aggregation of all data in the papers considered in this review show that molecular elucidation of the life cycles was achieved for a total of 22 species/lineages, with a continental distribution that reflects the distribution of species richness (Fig. 3B). Overall, life cycles of only 9 species were fully elucidated based on molecular data: 5 in North America (D. gavium, D. huronense, D. indistinctum, Diplostomum sp. “indistinctum” sensu Galazzo et al. (2002), and D. scudderi); and 4 in Europe (D. pseudospathaceum, D. rauschi, D. spathaceum, and Diplostomum sp. Clade Q sensu Georgieva et al., 2013). The relation of partially elucidated life cycles is similar, i.e. for 7 species in North America and 5 species in Europe, but only in North America, molecularly elucidated life cycles include definitive hosts (4 species: D. alascense (syn. Diplostomum sp. 2 sensu Moszczynska et al., 2009); D. marshalli (syn. Diplostomum sp. A sensu Gordy and Hanington, 2019); Diplostomum sp. “huronense” sensu Galazzo et al. (2002); and Diplostomum sp. VVT3 (syn. Diplostomum sp. 10 sensu Locke et al., 2015), thus highlighting the importance of focusing on barcoding the adult stage (Achatz et al., 2022). This is supported by the only partially elucidated life cycle in South America (D. lunaschiae (syn. Diplostomidae gen. sp. sensu Pelegrini et al., 2019; see Locke et al. (2020)) (Fig. 2B). Overall, the lack of life cycle matches in Asia and Africa reflects the poor state of Diplostomum research in general and highlights the need for substantial intensification of barcoding and taxonomic studies in these regions.

As indicated above, the data compiled here significantly expand our knowledge of host specificity towards the fish intermediate hosts. Thus, Locke et al. (2010a) evaluated the host specificity of seven of the 12 species sequenced and considered that five (“D. huronense, D. indistinctum, and Diplostomum spp. 1, 3 and 4”) are generalist species parasitising multiple unrelated fish species and two (“D. baeri and Diplostomum sp. 2”) are host specialists.

The updated nomenclature and combined data provided here support the idea of generalist status for these species with an increase in the number of fish hosts: D. indistinctum (syn. Diplostomum sp. 4 sensu Moszczynska et al., 2009): 26 vs 15 fish species; D. huronense (syn. Diplostomum sp. 1 sensu Moszczynska et al., 2009): 25 vs 17 fish species; D. gavium (syn. Diplostomum sp. 3 sensu Moszczynska et al., 2009): 11 vs 7 fish species; Diplostomum sp. “huronense” sensu Galazzo et al. (2002): 7 vs 5 fish species; Diplostomum sp. “indistinctum” sensu Galazzo et al. (2002): 4 vs 2 fish species. To this list we add three Palaearctic species: D. spathaceum: 24 fish species of 11 families; D. pseudospathaceum: 22 fish species of 8 families; and D. rauschi (syn. Displostomum sp. Lineage 2 sensu Blasco-Costa et al., 2014): 6 fish species of 5 families (Supplementary Table S1).

Furthermore, the present data indicate that the two species (“D. baeri and Diplostomum sp. 2”) listed by Locke et al. (2010a) cannot currently be considered host specialists. Diplostomum alascense (syn. Diplostomum sp. 2 sensu Moszczynska et al., 2009) was found in 8 (vs 2) fish species of 5 families (Atherinopsidae, Centrarchidae, Ictaluridae, Leuciscidae, and Percidae), and D. adamsi (syn. D. baeri sensu Galazzo et al., 2002) is hosted by 4 (vs 1) fish species of 3 families (Lentrarchidae, Leuciscidae, and Percidae) (see Supplementary Table S1 for details). Further barcoding studies will shed more light on Diplostomum spp. specificity to the second intermediate host. The present data indicate that strict host specificity is largely an artefact of incomplete, insufficient sampling effort and taxonomic bias, i.e. incomplete or incorrect identification of the metacercariae.

Although it is early days to consider the specificity of Diplostomum spp. towards the snail intermediate host, some patterns emerge from the 15 surveys reporting sequences from cercarial isolates for 22 species/lineages of Diplostomum (Fig. 4; see Supplementary Table S1 for details). First, 11 North American Diplostomum spp. were reported to use fewer snail species to complete their life cycles compared with 11 European Diplostomum spp. (3 vs 7 snail species). Secondly, snail hosts belong to the family Lymnaeidae, except for two species (D. indistinctum and D. scudderi) also reported from a planorbid snail, Planorbella trivolvis (Gordy and Hanington, 2019; McPhail et al., 2026); to our knowledge, these are the first species of Diplostomum reported to infect hosts of both families, the Lymnaeidae and Planorbidae. Thirdly, 10 species (45%) were recorded to infect two or more snail species: D. pseudospathaceum (4 snail species); D. scudderi, D. spathaceum and Diplostomum sp. Clade Q sensu Georgieva et al. (2013) (3 snail species); plus 6 Diplostomum spp. (3 North American and 3 European) (2 snail species) (Fig. 4). Finally, the North American L. elodes and the European A. balhica support the greatest Diplostomum diversity (9 species each), followed by L. stagnalis and R. auricularia (6 species each), with L. stagnalis acting as host predominantly in North America (5 vs a single species in Europe) (Fig. 4). The way forward

DNA barcode data have proven valuable for accurate species identification and assessment of genetic diversity within Diplostomum, but the major application of barcoding, biomonitoring, has yet to be addressed. Aquatic parasites involved in sequential host-parasite systems are particularly sensitive indicators of ecological perturbation, such as biological invasions, environmental contamination and other causes of habitat degradation, and environmental change associated with global warming (Sures et al., 2023). This is also true for Diplostomum, an important group of globally distributed aquatic parasites whose larval life cycle stages are also easy to access. However, although this review highlights significant progress in revealing the diversity of Diplostomum spp. and establishing rich cox1 libraries for the Nearctic and Palaearctic species, substantial gaps in our knowledge exist, with important implications for accurate biodiversity assessment and monitoring, and the use of epidemiological data for environmental impact assessment. This is why the examples of the use of barcoding for fully or almost completely censused assemblages of Diplostomum spp. in community ecology research and environmental impact assessment are still few (Désilets et al., 2013; Locke et al., 2013; Marcogliese and Locke, 2021; McPhail et al., 2026) and typically, pooled data for morphologically and molecularly identified isolates of Diplostomum spp. are treated as a single entity (Kuhn et al., 2015; Rochat et al., 2022). A recently developed targeted metabarcoding approach using diplostomid-specific cox1-fragment for screening of entire parasite communities in fish eyes may revolutionise the field (Noreikiene et al., 2020; Diaz-Suarez et al., 2024, 2025). The first applications of metabarcoding to host-parasite and host-parasite-environmental interactions, as well as to microhabitat selection and interspecific interactions, are very promising, especially because metabarcoding allowed the detection of both high intraspecific molecular diversity (Noreikiene et al., 2020) and sampling the potentially entire regional species diversity (up to 6 species of Diplostomum: D. pseudospathaceum, D. rauschi, D. spathaceum, Diplostomum sp. Lineage 4 sensu Blasco-Costa et al. (2014), Diplostomum sp. Clade Q sensu Georgieva et al. (2013), and a species of the D. mergi species complex, see Diaz-Suarez et al., 2024).

The accumulation of barcoding/metabarcoding data and ongoing resolution of taxonomic and nomenclatural problems will provide opportunities for the “Diplostomum community” of parasitologists to address biomonitoring as well as fundamental questions in ecology; this is a promising avenue of research.

The answers to the questions “How many species?” and “Which ones?” addressed here highlight at least two important areas for future research. First, there is a need for a drastic increase in sampling effort, both with respect to the continental distribution and the host range of Diplostomum spp., especially in Africa, Asia, Australia, and South America. Regional knowledge of species diversity is vital for the identification of species in biological inventories, enhancing environmental impact assessment and monitoring biodiversity changes. For Diplostomum spp., the regional scale largely depends on the continental scale because of the wide distribution and high mobility of the principal definitive hosts, fish-eating birds.

The current data indicate optimistic answers to both questions above for the Nearctic, i.e. that the North American Diplostomum fauna is completely sampled in the barcoding studies, with more species discovered than we were aware of, including a large proportion (44%) of named species. However, the Palaearctic fauna is insufficiently clarified with respect to both questions, and the remaining continental Diplostomum faunas are definitely understudied. Increased molecular sampling and inclusion of more than 15 “missed” species from Europe, Asia and Africa would contribute substantially to our understanding of the taxonomic diversity, geographical distribution and phylogenetic relationships within Diplostomum.

We still know very little about the intercontinental distribution of Diplostomum spp. Molecular data support the intercontinental distribution of a very few species, and currently, there is no genetic evidence for a species with a Holarctic distribution. Therefore, the species from the Nearctic and Palaearctic can be treated in isolation before additional data are gathered. Although the Palaearctic species require taxonomic scrutiny, sampling of Diplostomum spp. from Nearctic percids, salmonids, and gasterosteids may uncover additional species/lineages, including some with a Holarctic distribution.

Secondly, the taxonomic progress and issues highlighted in this review clearly indicate that the focus of barcoding should be moved from larval stages in snail and fish intermediate hosts, which cannot be reliably identified, to a systematic search and morphological and molecular characterisation of the adult stages of Diplostomum. This is supported by the substantial contribution to Diplostomum taxonomy of a single integrative taxonomic study focused on well-fixed adult stages (Achatz et al., 2022) (see updated nomenclature in Table 1 and Section 5.1). A similar approach and increased taxonomic scrutiny are required for the European species of Diplostomum, especially because of the discovery of two complexes of potentially cryptic species (D. mergi complex and D. baeri complex, see Fig. 2) (Georgieva et al., 2013; Blasco-Costa et al., 2014; Selbach et al., 2015; Schwelm et al., 2021).

Thirdly, sequencing and cox1 barcoding, in particular, have become a cost-effective and accessible gold standard for rapid identification of species of Diplostomum. The most important current challenge is how to integrate the new data into the existing taxonomic framework. As illustrated in this review, as sequences accumulate, data curation is becoming increasingly important for delineation and accurate identification of the genetic lineages of Diplostomum. This is a critical issue and one of the weaknesses of barcoding (and metabarcoding), because the molecular databases are under-curated, especially with respect to taxonomy and nomenclature, leading to inaccurate identifications. As this state of affairs is unlikely to change, we suggest a solution used in a recent study (Schwelm et al., 2021), i.e. generating an a priori in-house comprehensive and well-curated DNA barcode library with taxonomy based on the updated nomenclature and reference sequence sources listed in Table 1 rather than using sequences (and the associated metadata) retrieved from BOLD or NCBI; new species/lineages can be easily incorporated in the alignment.

Finally, the increased taxonomic resolution resulting from barcoding integrated with morphological, ecological (life cycles, host ranges, microhabitats in the second intermediate hosts), and geographical evidence is expected to fulfil the prerequisites for a global taxonomic revision of Diplostomum that would help clarify the taxonomy of the molecular species-level lineages within this species-rich group. Further, parallel sequencing of the large ribosomal subunit (28S or LSU) rRNA gene for representatives of the newly typed species/lineages will allow comprehensive phylogenetic reconstructions at the supraspecific and suprageneric levels and improvement of the classification system of the family Diplostomidae.

Conclusions

The achievements and setbacks of barcoding Diplostomum spp. reviewed here indicate that despite 16 years of study revealing substantial richness in species and intermediate (predominantly fish) host diversity, a great deal of further work is awaiting. Until recently, DNA barcoding of Diplostomum has been predominantly used for biodiversity discovery associated with genetic data alone. It is high time for a coordinated effort towards integrating morphological data for the adult stage with molecular and ecological evidence for all life cycle stages. The few examples highlighted here and the significant progress in the taxonomy of a large number of diplostomoidean species and genera achieved by Tkach, Achatz and colleagues (reviewed in Kostadinova and Pérez-del-Olmo, 2024), which ultimately led to a new classification system for the Diplostomoidea (Achatz and Tkach, 2025), hold a promise for the development of a modern “next-generation” taxonomy of Diplostomum and its constituent species.
