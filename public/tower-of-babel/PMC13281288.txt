Overcoming fratricide of CD86 targeting CAR-T cells by defined logic-gate CAR strategy
Authors: Tobias Riet, Simon Lennartz, Niklas Bösing, Lars Fabian Prinz, Luise Dähn, Christof Scheid, Michael Hallek, Markus Martin Chmielewski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite its remarkable success in the treatment of several hematological malignancies, cellular immunotherapy using chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells faces significant challenges when treating refractory/relapsed or myeloid malignancies such as AML, CML or multiple myeloma. To overcome the pronounced heterogeneity of such diseases, the search for suitable tumor-associated antigens recently led to surface antigens of the B7 protein family as potential CAR-T cell targets. Using the example of CD86-targeting, we highlight a considerable pitfall of CAR-T therapy which utilizes B7-family proteins as a prominent group of T cell (activation) associated antigens. In a consistent set of in vitro and in vivo experiments we could demonstrate that fratricide among pre-activated CD86-specific CAR-T cells strongly reduces their overall survival and efficacy in tumor killing as recently shown for B7-H3/CD276-targeting CARs. Therefore, we propose a defined combinatorial antigen recognition concept via dual-chain CAR-T cells that incorporate an “AND-gate” mechanism through CD19 primary CAR activation and CD86 conditional co-stimulation to circumvent a severely limited outcome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40164-026-00791-3.Keywords: Cellular immunotherapy, CAR-T cell, CD86, Fratricide, Hematological malignancies, Dual-chain CAR, AND gate

To the editor

A safe target-antigen is crucial for CAR-T cell therapy, as disease-associated antigens are frequently expressed on healthy cells. Here, we focus on the B7-family protein CD86 as a recently identified, attractive target-antigen example in hematologic malignancies [1–3]. Our recently published “AND-gate” CAR with superior safety and efficacy compared to a second-generation CAR [1] uses CD86 as a conditional co-stimulator. In this context, we highlight characteristics of T cells expressing both CD86 and CD86-specific CARs.

While transcriptomics confirmed elevated CD86 in NHL lymph nodes [4] (Fig. 1A), we verified this at protein-level on primary DLBCL cells and human leukemia/lymphoma lines (SuppFig.1 A, B). Furthermore, we observed a continuous increase of CD86 expression on healthy human T cells (Fig. 1B), which reached densities after initial activation similar to those of primary lymphoma cells (Fig. 1C).

We designed a second-generation CAR with CTLA4-binding-domain (SuppFig.1 C) to investigate how CD86-expression on T cells affects CD86-CAR-mediated activation. IFN-γ release was induced not only by specific CAR-activation, but also in the absence of activating agents (Fig. 1D). After transduction, the proportion of apoptotic CAR-T cells increased (Fig. 1E) and CD86+ T cells were eliminated accordingly (Fig. 1F), suggesting CTLA4-BBz CAR-induced fratricide.

As a potential solution, we tested dual-chain CAR-variants (SuppFig.2 A):

“19-BB/CTLA4-z”: CD86-binding activates via CD3z, CD19-binding conditionally co-stimulates via 4-1BB.

“19-BB/CTLA4-z”: CD86-binding activates via CD3z, CD19-binding conditionally co-stimulates via 4-1BB.

“19-z/CTLA4-BB”: vice versa composition [1].

“19-z/CTLA4-BB”: vice versa composition [1].

T cells with 19-BB/CTLA4-z CAR killed fewer co-cultured CD19+/CD86+ tumor cells and produced less IFN-γ than 19-z/CTLA4-BB CAR-T cells (SuppFig.2B). Unstimulated 19-BB/CTLA4-z and CTLA4-BBz CAR-T cells secreted more IFN-γ via auto-activation compared to 19-z/CTLA4-BB and 19-BBz CAR-T cells (SuppFig. 2C). Blocking of CTLA-4 during the transduction of T cells (SuppFig.2D) revealed the impact of CD86-binding on potential fratricide: apoptosis rates decreased in 19-BB/CTLA4-z and CTLA4-BBz CAR-T cells while CD86-expression was increased in presence of Ipilimumab (Fig. 2A, B). To further investigate fratricide, we co-cultivated CAR-T cells with re-activated autologous PBMCs as CD86+ “victim cells”. 19-BB/CTLA4-z and CTLA4-BBz CAR-T cells eliminated these PBMCs, whereas 19-z/CTLA4-BB and 19-BBz CAR-T cells did not (Fig. 2C; SuppFig.2E). CAR-activation in CAR-transduced Jurkat reporter-cells [5] led to distinct expression-levels of NFAT-eGFP and NF-kB-CFP. In contrast to second-generation CARs, both dual-chain CARs only fully activate when both CAR-antigens are present. Interestingly, the 19-BB/CTLA4-z CAR induced lower levels of co-stimulatory transcription factor NF-kB (SuppFig. 2F). Deeper mechanistic insights were gained from bulk RNA-sequencing of CAR-T cells after transduction. T cells with the 19-BB/CTLA4-z CAR exhibited potent activation, proliferative signaling and inflammatory response in comparison with 19-z/CTLA4-BB CAR (Fig. 2D).

To demonstrate in-vivo relevance in a xenograft mouse model, we first verified efficacy of 19-z/CTLA4-BB CAR-T cells (SuppFig.3 A) and then conducted a long-term comparison of both dual-chain CAR-T variants in Raji-engrafted mice (SuppFig.3B). Treatment with 19-z/CTLA4-BB CAR-T cells prolonged survival, confirming limited efficacy of 19-BB/CTLA4-z CAR-T cells (Fig. 2E; Supp. Figure 3 C, D). To determine whether these findings are consistent with fratricide, we investigated persistence and CD86-expression of dual-chain CAR T cell variants in another xenograft model. Despite some effects regarding tumor burden and hCD19+ cells in peripheral blood (Fig. 2F), only a few human T cells were detected in the 19-BB/CTLA4-z CAR group (Fig. 2G). Most remarkably, T cells in the 19-BB/CTLA4-z CAR group vanished and the few remaining cells were CD86-negative (Fig. 2G; SuppFig. 4A). Spleen, bone marrow and liver tumors (w/o CAR group) showed no traces of CD86+ 19-BB/CTLA4-z CAR T cells (SuppFig.4B).

While CD86 has been suggested as a promising CAR-T target [2, 3], our findings add an important caveat to these conclusions by demonstrating potential off-tumor reactivity. We observed insufficient selectivity towards lymphoma cells using CTLA4-BBz or 19-BB/CTLA4-z CAR-T cells, resulting in elimination of activated T cells and other CD86+ immune cells like CD14 + monocytes (SuppFig.5 A-C).

Although using a CTLA-4-derived binding-domain, we suggest that antibody-derived CD86-specific second-generation CARs may insufficiently distinguish between tumor cells and CD86-expressing immune cells. Different CAR-designs (CD28 vs. 4-1BB) [3] might explain variations in fratricide susceptibility and need further comparative investigation. Limitations of CAR-T cell efficacy due to fratricide are well-known when targeting T cell-associated antigens [7–11], and a gene knock-out might be a successful bypass strategy [12]; however, for B7-family proteins impact on T cells needs to be carefully investigated.

Therefore, our data suggest that targeting CD86 via CD3zeta-activating CARs requires caution due to its expression on immune cells, but it can serve as a safe target for conditional co-stimulation. The success of the “AND-gate” CAR architecture depends on strict separation of activation and co-stimulation. This distinction is biologically important and highly relevant for future CAR-T design in order to develop safe and efficient CAR-T cells and circumvent limited efficacy due to fratricide.
