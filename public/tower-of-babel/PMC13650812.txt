The Family Perspectives Project: development and preliminary feasibility of a chaplain-delivered emotional support intervention for families of critically ill patients
Authors: Matthew E Modes, Erin K Kross, O Kenrik Duru, Aleksandra Sherman, Sangeeta C Ahluwalia, Anne M Walling, Pamela Lazor, Teryl K Nuckols
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile guidelines recommend that families of critically ill patients receive emotional support from intensive care unit (ICU) clinicians, few receive it. To address this gap, we designed the Family Perspectives Project, an intervention that utilizes hospital chaplains to enhance the emotional support that ICU teams provide to families. This study’s objective was to refine and preliminarily test the feasibility and acceptability of the intervention. The work occurred in two steps. First, we conducted qualitative interviews with stakeholders (n = 12) to refine the proposed intervention and design. Second, we conducted a single-arm feasibility study of the refined intervention in one ICU (9 patient-family dyads) and assessed feasibility and acceptability with validated measures on participant surveys. The intervention was delivered with high fidelity. Chaplains provided emotional support to families and created structured reports about family perspectives that clinicians reviewed. Participants found the intervention feasible and acceptable. A larger pilot randomized trial is warranted.Keywords: Communication, chaplain, emotional support, intensive care unit, spiritual support

Introduction

Yearly, over 5 million U.S. adults receive intensive care, including 1 in 3 older adults in their last month of life (Teno et al., 2018). Families of these patients often experience distressing emotions (Nelson et al., 2017; Schenker et al., 2012), which can negatively impact their psychological health and ability to guide treatment decisions for critically ill loved ones (Kon et al., 2016; Nelson et al., 2017; Power et al., 2011; Schenker et al., 2012). Recognizing this, critical care guidelines recommend that ICU teams provide emotional support to families, in addition to medical information (e.g., prognosis, treatment options) (Davidson et al., 2017; Hwang et al., 2025; Kon et al., 2016). Emotional support means responding to a person’s emotions with empathy, caring, and non-abandonment (Cohen, 2004; Cyranowski et al., 2013; Tan et al., 2021; Wenrich et al., 2003). When families receive emotional support from ICU clinicians, they report less psychological distress, greater satisfaction with communication and care, greater trust in clinicians, and higher quality surrogate decision making (Curtis et al., 2005; Lincoln et al., 2023; Neville et al., 2023; Selph et al., 2008; Torke et al., 2018). Despite this, families receive little emotional support from ICU clinicians and report unmet needs for emotional support (Curtis et al., 2005; Lincoln et al., 2023; Modes et al., 2025; Scott et al., 2019; Selph et al., 2008).

Chaplains have the training and skills to spearhead strategies to enhance emotional support for families in the ICU – including when emotional and spiritual distress overlap and also when emotional distress exists without concomitant spiritual distress. Yet, chaplains are underutilized in ICUs. About 70% of hospitals offer chaplain services (Weiner et al., 2017). However, families and ICU providers widely misunderstand chaplains, including that chaplain services are conflated with end of life, chaplains only provide religious-specific care and rituals, and chaplains preach and proselytize (Cunningham et al., 2017; Klitzman et al., 2022, 2024; Purvis et al., 2019; Stavig et al., 2022). As a result, distressed families who might benefit from chaplain expertise and support may not connect with them at all or connect only when a patient is dying (Choi et al., 2015, 2019).

Chaplains are highly trained to provide emotional support, with or without concomitant spiritual support, to individuals of all faiths, including the 28% of U.S. adults who are religiously unaffiliated (ACPE, 2023; APC, 2015, 2016; Smith et al., 2024). Moreover, training in cultural competency helps chaplains provide equitable support to individuals of all cultures (ACPE, 2023). Chaplains often provide emotional support through the lens of perspective taking, a concept underlying many aspects of clinical pastoral education (ACPE, 2023). Perspective taking is actively adopting another person’s thoughts, feelings, and expectations – putting oneself in another’s shoes – and is essential for empathic communication and care (Galinsky et al., 2005; Norfolk et al., 2007; Sherman et al., 2020).

In addition to directly supporting families, chaplains may also be ideally positioned to help catalyze ICU clinician perspective taking and provision of emotional support. Well-meaning ICU clinicians regularly interact with families without understanding their perspectives because they lack the time or skill to elicit these perspectives. Chaplains often gain a deep understanding of family perspectives while providing support (Klitzman et al., 2022; Roze Des Ordons et al., 2022), yet commonly operate in isolation from ICU clinicians. Communication between clinicians and chaplains is often poor (Choi et al., 2019; Garland et al., 2025), limiting transmission of this important knowledge to the ICU team.

To address gaps in emotional support for these families of critically ill patients, we designed the Family Perspectives Project. This intervention utilizes the skills of hospital chaplains to transform the emotional support that ICU teams provide to families. Chaplains are paired with families to provide emotional support, and structured chaplain-clinician communication is designed to promote ICU clinician perspective taking and provision of emotional support. Developing novel interventions that aim to change human behavior in real-world clinical settings can be challenging and experts recommend a structured approach to development (Czajkowski et al., 2015). Stages include basic design, iterative refinement and preliminary testing, and subsequent efficacy testing. Here, we describe the refinement and preliminarily test for feasibility and acceptability of the Family Perspectives Project.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study consisted of two steps. First, we conducted qualitative interviews with key stakeholders (families, hospital chaplains, ICU clinicians) to refine the basic intervention and study design originally crafted by our research team. Early stakeholder feedback is essential to optimize the feasibility of real-world interventions that operate within the workflow of busy healthcare providers (Hamilton et al., 2013; Melles et al., 2021). Second, we conducted a small single-arm feasibility study of the refined intervention. Small-scale testing can quickly identify major barriers to feasibility and acceptability, thereby facilitating intervention refinements or supporting a decision not to proceed to larger and more expensive studies (Czajkowski et al., 2015).

Setting

We conducted this study at a 915-bed quaternary academic medical center in Los Angeles, California. The hospital has a robust Spiritual Care Department that employs board-certified chaplains. We chose to conduct the feasibility study in the medical ICU, a 24-bed unit with capacity for overflow. At the time of feasibility testing, the unit was not regularly staffed by an assigned chaplain, though one had been consistently present in prior years.

Step 1: Stakeholder feedback of proposed intervention and pre-implementation refinement

To define challenges and design solutions to the content, implementation, and study design of the intervention, we conducted semi-structured interviews and a focus group with key stakeholders and refined the intervention based on their feedback (Hamilton et al., 2013; Hamilton & Finley, 2019; Melles et al., 2021). This step was approved by the medical center Institutional Review Board (STUDY00002260).

Recruitment

We used purposive sampling to recruit end-users at our institution (hospital chaplains, ICU physicians) and individuals with prior experience as a family member of a patient in the ICU (henceforth referred to as family members). To recruit chaplains, the Director of Spiritual Care arranged a focus group with senior chaplains in June 2022. We recruited physicians from the medical ICU by email in August-October 2022. To recruit family members, we worked with the Department of Patient Experience at our institution to send recruitment emails in October 2022 to a listserv of volunteer Patient Family Advisors.

Data collection

We conducted a 90-minute focus group with senior chaplains in June 2022. We conducted 30–60-minute one-on-one interviews with ICU physicians from September-November 2022. We conducted 60–120-minute one-on-one interviews with family members from October-November 2022. For each participant group, we developed a semi-structured interview guide designed to elicit participants’ feedback overall as well as on the content and implementation of each proposed step of the intervention. We used open-ended questions and follow-up probes and shared drafts of study materials in real-time by video to help identify potential challenges and solutions. We collected demographic information from family members. A critical care physician with qualitative research experience (MEM) conducted both the focus group and interviews via videoconference. All were audio recorded, and the recordings were transcribed.

Data analysis and intervention refinement

To enable us to identify problems and implement solutions quickly, we conducted a rapid qualitative content analysis (Hamilton & Finley, 2019). A team member (MEM) used structured summary matrices based on our interview guides to identify areas of agreement and disagreement within each participant group. Research team members reviewed feedback in a summary table and/or in discussions. The team included two critical care physician-researchers (MEM, EKK), two general medicine physician-researchers (TKN, OKD), a PhD-trained palliative care physician-researcher (AMW), a PhD-trained qualitative researcher (SCA), an Association for Clinical Pastoral Education (ACPE) Certified Educator in clinical pastoral education and professional chaplain (PL), and a PhD-trained cognitive psychologist (AS). Together, the team was experienced in conducting ICU-based communication research, developing and testing real-world behavioral interventions, and eliciting and incorporating stakeholder feedback. Guided by stakeholder perspectives, the team reached consensus on changes to the content, implementation, and study design of the intervention.

Step 2: Preliminary feasibility testing

We then conducted a small single-arm study to assess our ability to implement the refined intervention with fidelity and participant acceptability and feasibility (Czajkowski et al., 2015). The study was approved by the medical center Institutional Review Board (STUDY00002813) and registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06039501). All participants provided informed consent.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible chaplains were those employed in our institution. Eligible ICU physicians, nurses, and social workers were those who worked in our medical ICU. Eligible patients were ≥18 years old, placed on invasive mechanical ventilation within 36 hours of ICU admission, and expected to need continued ventilation for ≥72 hours as determined by the patient’s treating ICU physician. We excluded patients whose treating ICU physician indicated that they had capacity to make their own decisions, were expected to die within 24 hours, or had a clear preference for comfort-focused care. We also excluded patients who were imminently awaiting organ transplant, chronically dependent on a ventilator, lacked a surrogate decision maker, or had a preexisting relationship with a nonparticipating chaplain. We chose these exclusion criteria to identify patients who were expected to live long enough to have time to implement the intervention, whose families were experiencing the added emotional distress of making surrogate decisions, and in whom participation would not disrupt continuity of prior chaplain care. Eligible family members were ≥18 years old, proficient in English, and self-identified as the person responsible for making medical decisions for the patient. Dyads that did not meet inclusion criteria for both the patient and family member were not eligible for the study.

Description of the Family Perspectives Project

The intervention involves four steps. First, board-certified hospital chaplains meet with families to provide emotional support, with or without concomitant spiritual support. Second, chaplains write one-page structured reports that include summaries of family perspectives and suggestions for ICU clinicians to provide families with tailored emotional support and meet their communication needs (see Supplementary Appendix for report template). Third, study staff relay these one-page reports to ICU clinicians before planned ICU physician-led meetings with families. Families commonly express emotions to ICU clinicians during these meetings, making them key opportunities for clinicians to provide emotional support (Curtis et al., 2005; Lincoln et al., 2023; Modes et al., 2025; Seaman et al., 2017; Selph et al., 2008). Fourth, ICU clinicians review the reports, prompting them to take family perspectives and empowering them with tailored coaching. Reports include instructions and prompt actions designed to promote perspective taking. The four-step cycle repeats as needed over the course of the ICU stay to provide ongoing support for evolving family perspectives and needs that may result from changes in the clinical status of the patient.

Chaplain training

To standardize how chaplains use their communication skills to elicit and summarize family perspectives, chaplains have a semi-structured discussion guide for meetings and a templated report sectioned into different aspects of the family experience that may drive clinician provision of emotional support. The guide and report were developed with Norfolk’s conceptual model of clinician empathy and refined with stakeholder feedback (see Step 1) (Norfolk et al., 2007). Participating chaplains received a 1-hour training session in intervention content and delivery, including the chaplain discussion guide and structured report template, in October 2023.

Data collection

Chaplains, ICU physicians, nurses, and social workers completed enrollment questionnaires. Chaplains completed questionnaires after they met with families. Physicians completed questionnaires after they met with families, as did nurses and social workers for any meetings they attended. Family members completed enrollment questionnaires, questionnaires after ICU physician-led family meetings, and at 1- and 6-months after enrollment. Study staff abstracted patient demographics and clinical characteristics from patient electronic health records and documented occurrence, length, and participants in chaplain-family and ICU physician-led family meetings.

Monitoring fidelity

Fidelity was monitored as part of intervention delivery activities and through chaplain questionnaires. Fidelity measures of intervention delivery were: (1) did the chaplain meet with the family; (2) did they provide emotional support; (3) did they create a structured report; (4) did the study staff deliver the report to clinicians; and (5) did clinicians review reports before ICU physician-led family meetings. Chaplains documented the emotional and spiritual activities they delivered after each of their family meetings, which we adapted from a taxonomy of chaplain activities (Massey et al., 2015; Sharma et al., 2021). To prompt active engagement, reports included instructions to “circle what you read above that was most helpful to you” and to describe “briefly, why was it helpful?” in an open box. When ICU clinicians either marked up reports (circling, underlining) or handwrote what was most helpful, we considered this actively engaging with reports.

Measures

As a feasibility study, our primary outcomes were the validated 4-item acceptability of intervention measure (AIM) and 4-item feasibility of intervention measure (FIM) (Weiner et al., 2017). AIM was administered to all participants on respective post-meeting questionnaires. To minimize survey burden for families, FIM was withheld from their questionnaire, but included for all other participants. Participants were also asked if they would participate in the Family Perspectives Project again. To learn how families shared their perspectives, we asked chaplains on post-meeting questionnaires: “what best describes the family’s communication with you in the meeting?”, with four response options ranging from “family did not share their perspectives” to “family opened up and offered deep insights into their perspectives”. To learn how ICU clinicians valued reports, we asked clinicians on post-meeting questionnaires: “Now that the meeting is over, how helpful was reviewing the family perspectives report before the meeting?”, using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “not at all” to “extremely”. We asked families to assess if they felt supported by the ICU support counselor (chaplains were called ICU support counselors in the study, see Step 1 results below for details), they would recommend the ICU support counselor, they met with the ICU support counselor too often, and the ICU support counselor took up too much of their time on 5-point Likert scales, ranging from “completely disagree” to “completely agree”, on post-meeting and 1-month questionnaires. To assess survey burden, respondents answered a final question on each questionnaire: “overall, how much of a burden was it for you to complete this questionnaire”, with a 0 (“not at all burdensome”) to 10 (“extremely burdensome”) scale. Finally, we collected demographic information from all participants on respective enrollment questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

We present data as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables and median (25th- 75th or range) for continuous variables. As a feasibility study, we did not power to test for statistical significance and did not conduct inferential testing (Leon et al., 2011; NCCIH, n.d.). We performed all analyses using Microsoft Excel.

Results

Step 1: Stakeholder feedback of proposed intervention and pre-implementation refinement

Three senior chaplains participated in the focus group. Five ICU physicians were approached and all agreed to participate in an interview. Nine family members responded to recruitment emails, of which four agreed to participate in an interview. Family members had a median age of 56.5 years (range 42–72), all were female, and most experienced having a parent in the ICU (Table S1). Family members rated the importance of faith or spirituality in their day-to-day life as a median 80 (range 40–100) on a scale of 0 (“not important at all”) to 100 (“very important”), but only one recalled meeting with a chaplain when their loved one was in an ICU.

Overall impressions

Families liked the proposed intervention and felt it had potential to address gaps in communication and support for families in the ICU. Families described doctors and staff as well-meaning but busy. They valued the chaplain’s role in the proposed intervention as a source of extra support and as an advocate or “bridge” to the medical team to help get their communication needs met.

“You can’t get this [support] from your traditional doctor because they’re busy. The nurses are busy. In ICU, they’re monitoring you very carefully and closely for any kind of changes. They don’t have time to be this kind of support.”

(Family Member #1)

“I know families don’t always have access to doctors or information all of the time. So if they had a chaplain that they could talk to, where the chaplain could help calm them down if they are having fears or feel like they’re not getting enough information or they have questions, that may be more appreciated.”

(Family Member #2)

Chaplains found the spirit of the proposed intervention appealing and thought it was important to study. Chaplains described how their training, skills, and nonjudgmental and person-centered approach help families share thoughts and feelings in ways that families don’t share with medical teams, including about end of life, advance care planning, feeling seen and heard by hospital staff, and sensitive topics like perceived racial biases. They welcomed the prospect of improving communication of this information to the medical team through the proposed intervention.

“There are so many times when I’m trying to communicate this to the team and they’re not interested. Like, the family wants the patient to be terminally extubated tomorrow. And nobody cares. They want to keep going, the team. And the family wants to let go. So this could be a great resource to communicate that better.”

(Chaplain #1)

ICU physicians also welcomed the proposed intervention, particularly noting potential benefits from receiving summaries of family perspectives on the ICU situation. Physicians described how it could help them tailor their conversations with family to better and more efficiently meet family needs.

“If you knew, as the clinician, what the family members [are] expecting and wanting and needing, you could go about giving that to them differently than if you’re just basing on your own bias and guesses, which is why I think this whole thing is great.”

(ICU Physician #5)

Several also described potential benefits to ICU teams beyond receiving summaries. One felt chaplain-family meetings could help families process the ICU experience and prime them to better engage in difficult decision making with ICU physicians. Another thought family frustrations could be shared and worked through with chaplains, as opposed to being “taken out on the nurse”.

Framing

Families generally perceived chaplains as a different layer of care than the medical team. They valued their complete focus on patients and families, contrasting that with other hospital staff who were often felt to have ulterior motives related to insurance and finances, processing patients, or asking families to sign forms. Despite that, all families expressed concern that the title “chaplain” carries religious connotations, and this could negatively impact the reach of the proposed intervention. Instead, families suggested reframing the chaplain as a support person and changing their title.

“You immediately think of religion. And if anybody has a negative feeling about religion, it may be something that’s refused immediately.”

(Family Member #4)

“I would call them ICU support counselors. I would frame it ICU because that’s your audience, that’s your demographic. ICU. I would frame it as an ICU support counselor.”

(Family Member #3)

Chaplains shared that misperceptions about their role and scope of work are common among patients, families, and hospital staff. They noted intentionally interacting with families in ways to avoid stirring these misperceptions, especially that a chaplain’s presence indicates the patient is dying. They highlighted the importance of intentionally framing the proposed study this way as well.

“This [study] is for the purposes of improving support and improving communication, not related to end of life necessarily, not related to delivering bad news.”

(Chaplain #2)

ICU physicians had misperceptions and little knowledge of chaplain training and scope of work and described minimal interaction with chaplains in the ICU at present. They also expressed concerns about religious connotations of the title “chaplain”, and one even wondered about alternative labels.

“I think a lot of this is labels. If someone told me the chaplain was coming to talk to me, I would think one of two things. One, I need to tell them that I’m not Christian. And two, maybe this means that things aren’t going well and they’re here to give me my last rites or something to that extent. And the problem is, I think the word is just a little bit loaded, where it is not a neutral word. So, I don’t know if there is a more bland word we can use to describe them?”

(ICU Physician #3)

Refining the proposed intervention

Based on stakeholder feedback (described above, in Table 1, and in Supplementary Appendix), our research team made changes to the content, implementation, and study design of the proposed intervention (examples in Table 1). With support of the Director of Spiritual Care, we reframed chaplains as “ICU support counselors” and made badges for them to wear during the feasibility study. We revised the chaplain discussion guide, rewording questions and adding questions about prior ICU experience and hope. We decided study staff would schedule initial chaplain-family meetings. We shortened physician-facing materials, including structured reports and questionnaires.

Step 2: Preliminary feasibility testing

Participants

We first approached 16 hospital chaplains about serving as ICU support counselors in our study and 5 (31%) enrolled. Ultimately, 4 were paired with families in the study. Those 4 had a median age of 39 (range 37–65), half were female, and they were racially and religiously diverse (Table S2). They were all board-certified chaplains and had worked for a median of 12 years (range 7–16). We then recruited and enrolled ICU clinicians who rotate through the medical ICU and could potentially care for an eligible patient and family. We approached 43 ICU physicians (attendings and critical care fellows) and 35 (81%) enrolled. Ultimately, 6 experienced the intervention. Those 6 had a median age of 31.5years (range 29–39), half were female, and all were fellows (Table S3). Recruitment and enrollment data and characteristics of nurses and social workers who experienced the intervention are in Table S4. Subsequently, we recruited and enrolled 9 patients and their family members (Figure S1); their demographic characteristics and patient clinical outcomes are in Table 2.

Intervention delivery

The intervention was delivered with high fidelity (Table 3). All 9 families met with a chaplain. Chaplains provided emotional support in all meetings and spiritual support in most meetings (Table 4). Chaplains reported that families offered deep insights into their perspectives in 89% of meetings. Meetings lasted a median of 55 minutes (IQR 30–61) and occurred a median of 4.1 days (IQR 3.2–5.4) after ICU admission. Chaplains wrote summaries of family perspectives and suggestions using the format of the structured report after all meetings. Reports were hand-delivered by study staff to clinicians before all subsequent ICU physician-led family meetings; seven families had such a meeting, while two did not. Enrolled physicians reviewed and actively engaged with reports before all these meetings. Physicians rated 67% of reports as quite a bit or extremely helpful. Enrolled social workers and a nurse also reviewed reports before all meetings they attended. Social workers rated 80% of reports as quite a bit or extremely helpful, as did a nurse who reviewed one. Three families had a follow-up meeting with their assigned chaplain. These lasted a median of 37 minutes (range 21–60) and occurred a median of 7.5 days after ICU admission (range 6.1–7.6). Chaplains provided emotional support in and wrote follow-up reports for all 3 meetings. Follow-up reports were delivered by study staff to enrolled clinicians before all follow-up ICU physician-led family meetings and were reviewed by all enrolled clinicians.

Outcomes

Family members, chaplains, ICU physicians, a nurse, and social workers all found the intervention acceptable and would participate again (Table 5). Chaplains, ICU physicians, a nurse, and social workers all found the intervention feasible (Table 5). Family members recommended ICU support counselors and felt they did not take up too much of their time (Table 6). Family members, ICU physicians, a nurse, and social workers reported minimal survey burden, while chaplains reported moderate survey burden (Table S5).

Discussion

The Family Perspectives Project is a novel complex behavioral intervention that utilizes hospital chaplains to transform the emotional support provided to families of critically ill patients. The current study involved pre-implementation refinement of the proposed intervention through interviews with stakeholders and subsequent preliminary feasibility testing in a single-arm trial. Stakeholders valued the proposed intervention and identified ways to enhance its reach and alignment with the workflow of healthcare providers and preferences of families during a stress-filled time. The feasibility study revealed that the intervention was implemented with high fidelity and reaffirmed the feasibility and acceptability of the intervention to stakeholders.

A few prior studies have used chaplains as interventionists to enhance communication or support for families of critically ill patients, and our study contributes to this growing body of work (Alghanim et al., 2021; Torke et al., 2019, 2023). Some unique aspects of the Family Perspectives Project that stakeholders found feasible are worth highlighting for other investigators who are considering ICU-based research or quality improvement projects that utilize chaplains.

First, we aimed to improve chaplain-clinician communication through a structured approach. Our stakeholder interviews aligned with other studies that suggest communication between chaplains and ICU clinicians is often limited and poor (Choi et al., 2019; Garland et al., 2025). Findings from our preliminary feasibility study suggest structured approaches may improve communication. Chaplains were willing to put in the time to write more detailed notes since they believed ICU clinicians would read them. And indeed, ICU clinicians actively reviewed all reports they were given. More importantly, ICU clinicians valued the kinds of information chaplains were learning about patients and families and sharing with the ICU medical team. Our findings also help affirm that structured interventions are acceptable to chaplains and feasible within their professional roles, as other studies have noted (Ames et al., 2021; Sprik et al., 2024; Torke et al., 2019).

Second, chaplains in our study were balancing study activities with their normal workload. Concerns have been raised about the scalability of prior ICU-based communication interventions that are delivered by hired and trained interventionists (Courtright & Downar, 2025). Our study took a more real-world approach, both intentionally and out of necessity, as we were unable to hire dedicated study chaplains. Thus, we developed the intervention to be more pragmatic and with future scalability in mind. Chaplains found the intervention feasible and acceptable, even while balancing their normal workload. Eliciting and being as responsive as possible to their feedback in the pre-implementation step likely facilitated this success. While preliminary, our findings suggest that reaching emotionally distressed families in need with this collaborative and efficient use of existing support resources has potential for scalability in real-world contexts.

Third, we reframed chaplains as “ICU support counselors”. Our interview findings were consistent with other studies that have found misperceptions about chaplain roles and behaviors to be common among patients, families, and medical providers (Choi et al., 2019; Klitzman et al., 2024). We chose to rename chaplains because doing so may help to connect families in need with experts, emphasizes the chaplains’ primary function in our study, and does not limit their ability to provide spiritual care. Indeed, most families in our study also received spiritual care from their assigned ICU support counselor. Importantly, families found the intervention acceptable and had positive experiences with chaplains serving as ICU support counselors. The success we had with this name change is consistent with studies that have found increased reach with a name change for palliative care clinicians, another commonly misunderstood healthcare provider. A name change from “palliative care” to “supportive care” consultation services has been associated with more favorable impressions from patients, more inpatient referrals, and earlier outpatient referrals (Dalal et al., 2011; Maciasz et al., 2013). While infrequent, when families asked for more details about ICU support counselors, we informed them that they were chaplains by training and named and dispelled common misperceptions about chaplains.

Our study has several limitations. First, though participants were diverse, it was conducted in a single unit in one hospital with strong investment from ICU staff and the Department of Spiritual Care and may not generalize. Our future work aims to expand to additional units and hospitals. Second, small single-arm feasibility studies preclude assessment of efficacy and participant experience with randomized design that is the gold-standard to demonstrate efficacy. As a next step, we aim to conduct a larger pilot randomized trial to assess the proportion of families we enroll, family acceptability of random assignment, differential dropout by arm, ability to collect data per protocol in each arm as well as continuing our assessment of intervention fidelity and participant acceptability and feasibility. Third, though we used validated measures to assess intervention fidelity and acceptability, we did not interview participants to more deeply explore their experience. We plan to do this in our next step pilot randomized trial. Fourth, the use of study staff to deliver reports limits scalability. In this early stage, we felt it was more important to understand how ICU physicians interact with and value the reports. In future stages, we aim to explore alternative delivery strategies that are automated and/or use the electronic health record.

In conclusion, the Family Perspectives Project intervention shows early promise as an approach that utilizes hospital chaplains to transform the emotional support that ICU teams provide to families of patients with critical illness. This study shows how pre-implementation refinement through stakeholder engagement can help lead to an intervention that is feasible and acceptable to stakeholders. A larger pilot randomized trial is warranted. Finally, we hope our feasibility and acceptability findings build on other studies to encourage larger and more collaborative roles for chaplains in ICUs.
