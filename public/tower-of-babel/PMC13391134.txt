The Gut-Joint Axis in Hip Osteoarthritis
Authors: Pablo A. Slullitel, Florencia A. Lohmann, Agustín F. Albani-Forneris, Martin Buljubasich, Agustín M. García-Mansilla, Francisco Salagoity, Carlos M. Lucero, Fernando Comba, Gerardo Zanotti, Tamara A. Piñero, Martin A. Buttaro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Intestinal dysbiosis and systemic microbial translocation potentially contribute to chronic joint inflammation. However, the role of the gut-joint axis in the genesis of osteoarthritis still needs to be elucidated. This investigation characterized taxonomic signatures and proinflammatory metabolic pathways within the hip joint to define their contribution to the pathophysiology of osteoarthritis relative to nonarthritic controls.Methods:A prospective cohort of 48 patients undergoing hip arthroplasty was enrolled. Specimens including synovial fluid, articular cartilage, and acetabular fossa tissue were collected from patients with primary hip osteoarthritis (n = 20) and femoral neck fracture (n = 20). Metagenomic profiling was performed using 16S-rRNA gene sequencing (V3–V4 region). Alpha and beta diversity, taxonomic composition, and predicted functional pathways (PICRUSt2) were compared based on diagnosis (arthritis vs. fracture) and sample location.Results:Osteoarthritic samples demonstrated reduced alpha diversity evenness compared with fracture controls (p = 0.031). While beta diversity was primarily driven by specimen type rather than diagnosis, significant taxonomic differences were observed at the genus level. Pseudomonas, Atopostipes, and Staphylococcus showed significant differential abundance between groups, both by specimen location and diagnosis. Functional predictive analysis revealed a marked enrichment of the KDO2-lipid A biosynthesis pathway in osteoarthritic specimens, specifically within the genus Pseudomonas. Key genes involved in lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis and export, including lpxB, lpxL, and lpxM, exhibited significantly higher median abundances in osteoarthritic joints compared with controls (p < 0.00000001).Conclusions:Patients with hip osteoarthritis exhibited specific taxonomic and predicted lipopolysaccharide-related pathways differences compared with nonarthritic controls, consistent with microbial molecular signatures in a noninfectious inflammatory joint environment, despite the absence of major diagnosis-driven community-level differences.Level of Evidence:Diagnostic Level III. Introduction

In recent years, the role of the microbiome in the pathogenesis of OA has gained attention1,2. Beyond systemic influences, recent evidence has challenged the long-held assumption that synovial joints are sterile environments. Studies using next-generation sequencing (NGS) have identified microbial DNA in synovial fluid and tissues, even in the absence of overt infection3-6. Systemic inflammation, driven in part by gut dysbiosis, has been implicated as a potential contributor to OA progression, a concept encapsulated in the ‘gut-joint axis’2,7,8. Studies have shown that dysbiosis—an imbalance in gut microbial composition—can alter systemic immune responses, increase gut permeability, and facilitate the translocation of microbial components, such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS), into the circulation, triggering joint inflammation2. While minor portal translocation of LPS is physiological, sustained systemic exposure induces chronic low-grade inflammation, a shared pathomechanism also observed in OA, rheumatoid arthritis, obesity, and inflammatory bowel disease9. In addition, gut permeability may also have implications in the development of complications following joint arthroplasty, especially periprosthetic joint infection10.

The presence of microbial communities within the joint, termed the intra-articular microbiome, has been detected in both healthy and osteoarthritic joints, with distinct profiles observed in OA patients3,11. While these studies primarily focused on the knee joint, there are limited data on the hip joint12, with only Goswami et al. describing a hip microbiota4.

Furthermore, microbial diversity and composition may vary across joint compartments, reflecting localized differences in immune responses, tissue architecture, and metabolic activity2,3. For instance, swab samples have demonstrated distinct microbial profiles and abundance compared with tissue and synovial fluid samples, suggesting that location-specific factors influence microbiota within the joint4.

The aim of this study was to characterize the microbiota within different areas of the hip joint in cases with OA relative to nonarthritic controls, followed by identification of enriched proinflammatory metabolic pathways to define their functional contribution to the intra-articular inflammatory pathophysiology of OA. We hypothesized that arthritic hips may exhibit a distinct microbiome profile as compared with nonarthritic hips.

Methods

Source of Funding

This study was supported by the 2021 Société Internationale de Chirurgie Orthopédique et de Traumatologie (SICOT) research grant, with support from the institutional Center of Translational Medicine and Biomedical Engineering (IMTIB). Genetic sequencing was performed at the National Institute of Agricultural Technology (INTA, Argentina).

Patient Recruitment

This prospective-cohort study, approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB00010193, protocol #3372), enrolled 48 patients with either primary hip arthritis or femoral neck fracture who received a joint arthroplasty at our institution between December 2021 and June 2022. Patients with prior surgery involving the same joint, signs of local or systemic infection, known inflammatory arthritis or autoimmune disease, metastatic disease, or a history of intravenous drug use were excluded from enrollment. Of the original cohort, 8 patients were excluded for having incomplete medical charts, leaving 40 patients for analysis. The cohort was separated into 2 groups, “osteoarthritis” (n = 20) and “femoral neck fracture” (n = 20) depending on the diagnosis that led to the joint replacement. Patients with intracapsular hip fractures served as the control group (i.e., without osteoarthritis), as previous studies have shown that older adults with these fractures rarely develop hip osteoarthritis13,14. In fact, studies suggest that OA is a protective factor against intracapsular femoral neck fractures, as they typically exhibit increased bone density in the femoral neck and decreased density in the trochanteric region, potentially increasing the risk of extracapsular fractures in this population15,16.

All “osteoarthritis” patients were treated with total hip arthroplasty, while “femoral neck fracture” fracture cases received either hemiarthroplasty (n = 12) or total hip arthroplasty (n = 8) depending on the preoperative functional status. Demographic data, including dietary habits and a history of any intra-articular corticosteroid (or other drug) injections into the joint within the past 12 months were collected (Table I). All elective cases were encouraged to cease smoking 1 month before surgery, as previously suggested17. Chronic conditions were classified with the Charlson Comorbidity Index18. The modified Harris Hip score (mHHS)19 and the preoperative Parker Mobility score20 were used to classify patients' preoperative functional status.

Demographic Characteristics of the Series

Surgical Characteristics and Sample Collection

All cases were operated in a laminar-flow theater through a posterolateral approach. One-gram of preincisional tranexamic acid was used in all cases. The surgical site (i.e., skin) was prepped with a standard combined betadine (povidone-iodine) and alcoholic solution, and iodine-impregnated sterile drapes (3M Ioban) were used in all cases. None of the patients was allergic to iodophor solutions. Strict cleanliness and sterility protocols were followed as per the standard institutional guidelines21. Samples were obtained from synovial fluid (SF), cartilage (CA), and acetabular fossa (FO). Before arthrotomy, at least 2 mL of synovial fluid was aspirated with an 18-gauge needle. Deep tissue samples (∼4 mm3) were collected from the femoral head cartilage and the pulvinar using a size 16-blade to prevent thermal artifact. A total of 120 samples were collected. The former sites were selected for their relevance to articular microbiota and local inflammation4,12, whereas samples from the acetabular fossa were additionally included since the pulvinar has a demonstrated role in inflammation, immune regulation, and stem cell activity22,23. All samples were placed in sterile containers and stored at -80°.

DNA Extraction, Amplification, and Sequencing

Samples were processed for metagenomic profiling by 16S-rRNA gene sequencing. DNA extraction was performed using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen), yielding variable concentrations according to sample type. The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the 16S-rRNA gene was selected for amplification due to its well-established taxonomic resolution, including maximum nucleotide heterogeneity and discriminatory power24,25. Paired-end sequencing was performed using Illumina technology.

Statistical, Bioinformatic Analysis, and Outcome Measures

Paired-end reads were demultiplexed and imported into QIIME 2 (v2024.2)26. Following quality assessment via the demux plugin, sequences were processed using the DADA2 workflow to generate high-resolution amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and a feature table. ASVs were aligned, and a rooted phylogenetic tree was constructed via the MAFFT/FastTree pipeline. Taxonomic classification was performed using a Naive-Bayes classifier trained on the SILVA-138 database (515F/806R region)27. ASVs with fewer than 5 total reads and samples with a sequencing depth below 1,000 reads were excluded. Unassigned, eukaryotic, mitochondrial, and chloroplast sequences, as well as environmental contaminants, were removed before diversity and abundance analyses.

Alpha diversity was assessed using the Shannon, Simpson, Chao1, and observed features indices, with between-group comparisons performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Beta diversity was evaluated using Bray-Curtis, Jaccard, and weighted and unweighted UniFrac distance metrics. Community structure was visualized by principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), and group-level differences were assessed using PERMANOVA by specimen type and underlying diagnosis.

Microbial profiling was conducted in R-software (v4.4.2) by integrating taxonomic annotations with ASV abundances. Differential relative abundance was determined via Wilcoxon rank sum tests with Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Phylum-level and genus-level compositions were visualized using stacked bar plots.

Functional prediction based on 16S-rRNA taxonomic profiles was performed to estimate the relative abundance of KEGG Orthologs (KOs)28 linked to core bacterial functions, including active transport, membrane lipid biosynthesis, adaptive responses to environmental stress, and nutrient uptake. Functional inference used PICRUSt2 on 16S-rRNA V3–V4 ASVs, with abundances annotated via the KO database28. Analysis focused on biosynthesis, assembly, and transport pathways of LPS due to their proinflammatory relevance9. Clinical differences in KO abundances were evaluated using Wilcoxon rank sum tests.

Ethical Review Statement

This study was REB approved IRB00010193, protocol #3372. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. The authors affirm that human research participants provided informed consent for publication.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

Table I shows the demographic baseline characteristics. None of the included individuals was either vegan or vegetarian, and all had White ethnicity.

Sequencing, Processing, and Taxonomic Assignment

Fifty-nine of the 120 samples yielded high-quality sequencing data, comprising 15-SF, 6-AF, and 38-CA specimens. The median sequencing depth was 14,355 reads per sample (range, 3,268-108,958). Sequence denoising yielded 4,372 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) with a mean length of ∼440bp. Following removal of low-depth samples and contaminant taxa—including Chloroflexi, Planctomycetota, Cyanobacteria, Hydrogenophilus, and Massilia—the final data set was established. AF specimens were excluded from subsequent statistical analyses, as only 2 samples (n = 2) met all filtering criteria.

Diversity Analyses

Alpha diversity did not differ significantly by specimen type across Shannon (H = 2.38, p = 0.12), Simpson (H = 2.07, p = 0.15), Observed Features (H = 1.73, p = 0.19), or Chao1 indices (H = 1.73, p = 0.19) (Fig. 1-A). When stratified by diagnosis, only Simpson diversity differed significantly, with reduced evenness in OA samples (H = 4.66, p = 0.031) (Fig. 1-B).

Taxonomic Composition

At the phylum level, Proteobacteria (p = 0.0005), Firmicutes (p = 0.028), and Acidobacteriota (p = 0.040) showed significant between-group differences across specimen types, whereas other phyla did not (Fig. 2-A). When stratified by diagnosis, only Proteobacteria (p = 0.014) and Firmicutes (p = 0.027) differed significantly (Figs. 2-B and 3-A).

At the genus level, 7 of the 20 most abundant genera differed significantly by specimen location: Pseudomonas (p = 1.93 × 10-19), Staphylococcus (p = 2.61 × 10-12), Atopostipes (p = 6.55 × 10-4), Enhydrobacter (p = 0.025), Corynebacterium (p = 0.031), Sphingomonas (p = 0.037), and Micrococcus (p = 0.041) (Fig. 2-C). Differences by diagnosis were limited to Pseudomonas (p = 4.56 × 10-8), Atopostipes (p = 2.02 × 10-6), and Staphylococcus (p = 0.033) (Figs. 2-D and 3-B).

Metabolic Profiling

The most representative ortholog was K03088 (rpoE), encoding an extracytoplasmic function sigma factor central to envelope stress regulation. High abundances were additionally observed for ABC-type transport system components (K01990, K01992, K02003, K02004), iron acquisition permeases (K02014–K02016), enzymes involved in biosynthesis of fatty acid (K00059, K00626), and regulatory or sensory proteins such as galR (K02529) and mcp (K03406).

Functional curation identified KOs associated with the biosynthesis, assembly, and transport of LPS, including lipid-A biosynthetic genes (K02535–K02542), core oligosaccharide assembly (K03273–K03278), O-antigen biosynthesis (K02845–K02848), and LptA–G export system (K09694–K09700), along with accessory genes involved in LPS maturation (K00748, K00973, K03271).

Functional profiling across Gram-negative genera revealed significant enrichment of the KDO2-lipid A biosynthesis pathway in OA specimens. Specifically, Pseudomonas exhibited higher median abundances of K00748 (LpxB), K02535 (LpxL), K02536 (LpxM), and K00973 compared with controls p < 1 × 10-8, where these orthologs were nearly absent. These results highlight a distinct proinflammatory metabolic potential within the OA joints. Conversely, genera such as Acinetobacter and Roseomonas presented median abundances p > 0.05, indicating no significant functional differences. Similarly, no relevant variation was observed for Veillonella, Atopostipes, Sphingomonas, or Enhydrobacter (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Our study provides novel insights into the microbial composition of OA-hip joints compared with nonarthritic controls, emphasizing the role of intra-articular microbiota in joint health. In addition, this study provides a foundational framework for interpreting NGS results from “aseptic” hips when evaluating patients with suspected periprosthetic joint infection. Although a previous study showed that positivity of cultures obtained during primary total hip arthroplasty may predict the likelihood of revision surgery29, it remains unknown if this may be due to intraoperative bacterial contamination from the skin microbiota or presence of a true intra-articular microbiota, since most revisions occurred due to aseptic reasons. Therefore, and in line with Goswami et al.’s findings4, we confirmed the existence of a native, unique microbiota in the hip joint. However, this may have implications in a future failure of an arthroplasty, either for infectious or aseptic reasons, still needs to be proven10. While the clinical relevance of these findings remains to be fully elucidated, characterizing microbial DNA signatures in native joints under different clinical conditions may help improve understanding of the joint microenvironment.

Community-level diversity metrics provide a broad overview of microbiome organization and should be interpreted as exploratory. In our study, alpha diversity did not differ significantly between specimen types, indicating comparable richness and evenness between SF and CA samples. Similar findings were observed when comparing diagnoses, with the exception of the Simpson index, which showed reduced evenness in OA samples compared with controls. This isolated finding suggests that OA samples may show increased dominance of selected taxa, although the overall alpha-diversity signal was modest. Beta diversity differed significantly according to specimen type but not diagnosis, indicating that anatomical location influenced overall intra-articular microbial community structure more strongly than OA status itself. Instead, the OA-related signal appears to be more selective, involving specific taxonomic differences and predicted functional enrichment of lipopolysaccharide-related pathways, rather than large-scale restructuring of the intra-articular microbiome.

While most joint-specific studies analyzed the presence of microbiome in the synovial fluid only12,30, this study additionally analyzed cartilage and pulvinar tissue. Goswami et al. also performed tissue sampling4, analyzing deep tissue specimens (2 mm3) taken from the synovium, tibia, and femur (in knee arthroplasties) or from the synovium, acetabulum, and femur (in hip arthroplasties). Also, swabs were obtained from the medullary canals of the femur and tibia (in knee arthroplasties) or the femur and acetabulum (in hip arthroplasties)4. However, joint location did not seem to affect bacterial prevalence. Conversely, we showed that sample location significantly influenced microbial presence, as reflected in the beta diversity, providing relevant microbiological data as per interpretation of samples.

Goswami et al. reported that Escherichia, Cutibacterium, Staphylococcus, Acinetobacter, and Pseudomonas were the 5 most prevalent genera in arthritic joints, most of them belonging to the phylum of Proteobacteria4. In their study, hospital location (i.e., the different participating centers) rather than demographic factors was significantly associated with beta diversity in bacterial composition4. In our study, taxonomic distribution also showed between-group differences, with Pseudomonas being more frequent in the arthritic group as compared with nonarthritic controls. Although Pseudomonas were linked to joint inflammation in microbiota-driven arthritis models, their detection does not prove infection, while they may represent a dysbiotic local sign of immune activation31. In the knee joint, a markedly high abundance of Proteobacteria at the phylum level was reported12, previously recognized as a key factor in dysbiosis both in intestinal and nonintestinal tissues32. Also, previous NGS analyses of the gut microbiota linked Proteobacteria with knee osteoarthritis as compared with nonarthritic controls33. Staphylococcus and Atopostipes, more prevalent in the fracture group, have also been associated with intestinal dysbiosis in systemic inflammatory/immune disorders34. Fernández-Rodríguez et al. showed that Staphylococcus, among other species, were dominant in native, sterile, nonosteoarthritic knees as compared with arthritic controls, depicting a difference in prevalence of organism profiles12. This finding parallels our observation of such microbial communities being more prevalent in femoral neck fracture cases (i.e., nonarthritic controls).

Functional profiling revealed marked differences in KO abundances between OA and fracture groups, driven in part by Pseudomonas. OA samples showed significantly greater representation of genes involved in LPS biosynthesis and export—including lpxB (K00748), lpxL (K02535), lpxM (K02536), and related orthologs within the biosynthesis pathway (M00060) of KDO2-lipid A. LPS are potent activators of inflammatory signaling pathways, including TLR4/NF-kB, implicated in synovial inflammation and cartilage degradation35. This is in line with the concept of para-sterile inflammation, whereby persistent exposure to microbial components contributes to chronic joint inflammation in the absence of overt infection2. Gleason et al. and Chisari et al. highlighted the impact of gut dysbiosis on systemic inflammation and its downstream effects on joint health2,7, including increased Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratios and LPS levels leading to OA progression. While these observations align with emerging hypotheses regarding the gut-joint axis, further studies are required to establish causality and clinical relevance.

This study included certain limitations. First, the cohort size of 40 patients may not fully capture the heterogeneity of microbial profiles across a broader population and limited the ability to perform demographic subanalyses, including age-related or sex-related comparisons. Second, while 16S-rRNA sequencing effectively identifies bacteria, it misses other microbial contributors to OA. Shotgun metagenomics would provide broader taxonomic and functional resolution; however, this approach was beyond the scope and resources of this study. Third, the analysis involved low-biomass intra-articular samples, which are inherently susceptible to contamination. Although standardized sterile protocols were used, contamination cannot be entirely excluded. Some of the detected genera, including Pseudomonas and Staphylococcus, have been reported as potential contaminants in sequencing workflows. Therefore, these taxa should not be interpreted as evidence of viable bacteria or active colonization. Rather, they represent microbial DNA signatures whose biological significance requires validation with shotgun metagenomics, microbial viability assessment, and complementary multiomics approaches. Fourth, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish temporal or mechanistic relationships between microbial DNA signatures and OA progression. Furthermore, the functional profiles described were based on predicted gene content using 16S-rRNA data; as such, they reflect microbial metabolic potential rather than active protein expression or transcriptomic activity. Osteoarthritis is characterized by a chronic inflammatory microenvironment. Thus, it is plausible that some microbial DNA signatures reflect cumulative consequences of long-term host-microbe interactions rather than direct contributors to disease initiation. From a microbiological perspective, the persistence of microbial DNA within joint tissues may result from multiple nonmutually exclusive processes, including exposure to microbial-derived molecules, low-level microbial translocation, and altered clearance of microbial products over time. Finally, this study focused exclusively on articular specimens without analyzing paired intestinal samples from the same patients.

In conclusion, this study identified detectable microbial DNA signatures in osteoarthritic and nonarthritic hip joints, with substantial variability according to intra-articular sample location. Osteoarthritis was associated with selected taxonomic differences and predicted enrichment of lipopolysaccharide-related pathways, rather than with a broad diagnosis–driven restructuring of the intra-articular microbiome. These exploratory findings support further investigation of the gut-joint axis in hip OA but do not establish a causal role for microbial signatures in OA pathogenesis.
