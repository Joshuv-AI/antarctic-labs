Microwave-induced modulation of intracellular distribution of peptides based on mitochondrial targeting sequences
Authors: Fumihiro Kayamori, Takuto Kariya, Momo Hirata, Yonejiro Arimoto, Tamaki Endoh, Nobuhiro Nakanishi, Kenji Usui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrowave (MW) irradiation has recently emerged as a promising physical approach for modulating the intracellular behavior of bioactive molecules. However, its potential effects on intracellular distribution and spatial association remain largely unexplored. In this study, we investigated MW-induced changes in the intracellular distribution of peptides designed based on previously reported mitochondrial targeting sequences under MW irradiation at 2.45 GHz. Peptide-derived fluorescence and its intracellular distribution were analyzed using confocal laser scanning microscopy and flow cytometry. MW irradiation induced sequence-dependent changes in cellular fluorescence intensity, with (Cha-r)2-R4 showing a more pronounced increase compared with non-irradiation conditions, whereas maintaining high cell viability. Colocalization analysis indicated an increase in spatial association between peptide-derived fluorescence and mitochondrial staining, although Pearson correlation coefficients remained low. These results suggested MW-induced modulation of peptide delivery to mitochondrial regions, although further quantitative and/or detailed analyses, such as mitochondrial function assays would be required. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate the potential of MW for delivering peptides conjugated with a signal sequence to the mitochondrial region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55313-2.Keywords: peptides, mitochondrial targeting sequences, cellular uptake, intracellular distribution, microwave, 2.45 GHzSubject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Cancer, Cell biology

Introduction

Microwaves (MWs) are electromagnetic waves widely used in various fields, including science, industry, and medicine. In scientific and industrial applications, MWs are particularly useful for the synthesis of organic1–6 and inorganic compounds7–10 because they can reduce reaction times and improve yields via MW-specific effects11,12. In addition to their utility in chemical synthesis, MWs have been shown to influence the behavior and reactions of bio-related molecules. For example, we previously investigated calcium carbonate (CaCO3) mineralization using peptides under MW irradiation13,14, and demonstrated that MWs significantly affected the morphology of CaCO3 precipitates, particularly when the intrinsic precipitation ability of the peptides was low.

Because reactions involving chemicals and bio-related molecules often occur within living cells, the application of MWs in cellular systems has attracted increasing attention. Several studies have reported the diverse cellular effects of MW irradiation, including the induction of apoptosis through DNA damage15–18 and enhancement of cell growth19. In addition, MWs have been shown to influence the cellular uptake of exogenous substances. For example, MW irradiation at 18 GHz enhanced the uptake of fluorescein isothiocyanate-conjugated dextran by Escherichia coli20 and silica nanospheres by Gram-positive cocci21. Increased uptake of silica nanospheres under 18 GHz MW irradiation has also been observed in rabbit red blood cells and rat adrenal medulla-derived pheochromocytoma cells22,23. In mammalian cells, MW irradiation at 18 GHz has been reported to enhance peptide uptake24,25, whereas MWs at 2.45 GHz have been shown to increase the cellular uptake of the anticancer drug doxorubicin by MCF-7 human breast cancer cells26. Consistently, we recently reported that MW irradiation at 2.45 GHz enhances the intracellular uptake of oligoarginine, a representative cell-penetrating peptide, by HeLa cells, enabling efficient internalization of short oligoarginines that are otherwise poorly taken up under non-irradiation conditions27.

Collectively, these studies suggest that MW irradiation induces subtle but functionally relevant perturbations in cellular membranes, thereby facilitating intracellular access to a wide range of substances. Although the precise molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood, transient increases in membrane permeability or the formation of pore-like structures have been proposed as plausible contributors to MW-assisted uptake23. However, for most therapeutic agents, cellular entry alone is insufficient for pharmacological activity. Subsequent trafficking to specific intracellular organelles such as the nucleus or mitochondria is often required. Signal peptides, which direct proteins to defined subcellular compartments, are widely used for organelle-specific delivery. The conjugation of signal peptides to target molecules would allow precise delivery to the organelles of interest. Despite the extensive studies on MW-enhanced cellular uptake, it remains unclear whether MW irradiation can facilitate signal peptide–mediated organelle targeting.

In this study, we investigated MW-assisted intracellular distribution of peptides designed based on previously reported mitochondrial targeting sequences under MW irradiation at 2.45 GHz. By combining physical stimulation with mitochondria-related sequence features, we explored whether MW irradiation modulates the delivery of peptides to mitochondria in living cells. This study provides a preliminary characterization of MW-induced changes in the intracellular distribution of peptides in a sequence-dependent manner. This work suggests a potential strategy for physically modulating peptide delivery in cells.

Results

Design of peptides

Peptides are attractive delivery vectors because of their synthetic accessibility and ease with which their cellular uptake properties can be systematically tuned through sequence modification28–30. By designing peptides with varying intrinsic uptake efficiencies, even modest effects of MW irradiation on mitochondrial delivery can be effectively evaluated. In particular, peptides with relatively low permeability are well-suited for assessing whether MW irradiation alters intracellular accumulation and spatial distribution patterns. Previous studies have shown that incorporation of both hydrophobic and cationic amino acids into peptide sequences significantly improves mitochondrial delivery31–33. Based on these findings, L-cyclohexylalanine (Cha) and D-arginine (r) were selected as the hydrophobic and cationic residues, respectively. Cha possesses strong hydrophobicity and has been reported to promote mitochondrial delivery, whereas r provides a positive charge and enhances resistance to proteolytic degradation, because D-amino acids are generally poor substrates for proteases. Herein, five peptides were designed by alternating Cha and r residues within the sequence (Table 1). Although an increase in the number of repeating units generally enhances cellular uptake, it is often accompanied by increased cytotoxicity. Therefore, peptides containing two or three repeat units were selected as they were expected to possess low cytotoxicity while retaining the potential for mitochondrial localization, despite their relatively low intrinsic uptake efficiency. For the peptide containing two repeat units, (Cha-r)2, a series of derivatives was additionally prepared by introducing two to four L-arginine residues at the C-terminus to modulate baseline cellular uptake. This panel of peptides with varied uptake efficiencies enabled a systematic investigation of MW-induced changes in intracellular distribution. To visualize peptide uptake and intracellular distribution, 5(6)-carboxyfluorescein was conjugated to the N-terminus of each peptide (Table 1 and Figure S1).

Intracellular distribution of peptides under MW irradiation

A previously developed MW irradiation system operating at 2.45 GHz with a semiconductor-type oscillator was used in this study (Figure S2a)13. To evaluate the temperature variations induced by MW irradiation, the temperature of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (D-MEM) in cell culture dishes was monitored using a thermocouple thermometer. The dishes were positioned 35 mm above the antenna and irradiated with MWs at either 10 W for 30 min or 20 W for 5 min. Under both conditions, the temperature increased in a time-dependent manner and reached approximately 38 °C, which was close to the physiological temperature and therefore considered unlikely to induce significant heat stress (Figures S2b and S2c). Based on these measurements, MW irradiation conditions of either 10 W for 30 min or 20 W for 5 min were selected for subsequent experiments.

Cellular uptake and fluorescence signals overlapping with mitochondrial staining of peptides (10 or 20 µM) under MW irradiation at 10 W for 30 min were initially evaluated by confocal laser scanning microscopy. Under these conditions, no substantial differences in intracellular peptide fluorescence were observed between MW-irradiated and non-irradiated cells at either peptide concentration (Figs. 1 and S3). To further investigate the effects of MW irradiation, experiments were conducted under MW irradiation at 20 W for 5 min. At a peptide concentration of 10 µM, MW irradiation induced only minor sequence-dependent changes in intracellular fluorescence distribution, and (Cha-r)2-R4 showed a slight increase in fluorescence signals in regions overlapping with mitochondrial staining (Figure S4). When the peptide concentration was increased to 20 µM, both (Cha-r)2-R3 and (Cha-r)2-R4 showed a modest increase in intracellular fluorescence intensity under MW irradiation compared with non-irradiation conditions (Fig. 2), accompanied by increased fluorescence signals overlapping with mitochondrial staining.

To further evaluate the spatial relationship between peptide-derived fluorescence and mitochondria, colocalization analyses were performed using the Pearson correlation coefficient and the overlap coefficient based on confocal images under MW irradiation conditions (Figure S5). The overlap coefficient indicated a moderate increase in spatial overlap between peptide-derived fluorescence and mitochondrial staining. In contrast, the Pearson correlation coefficients remained low.

To quantitatively evaluate these observations, cellular fluorescence intensity was analyzed using flow cytometry (Figs. 3 and S6). For (Cha-r)2-R3, MW irradiation at 20 W for 5 min did not produce a noticeable change in total cellular fluorescence intensity relative to the non-irradiation condition. In contrast, (Cha-r)2-R4 exhibited a statistically significant increase in cellular fluorescence intensity under MW irradiation compared with the non-irradiation condition (p < 0.05). This quantitative result for (Cha-r)2-R4 was consistent with the trends observed by confocal microscopy.

Flow cytometric analysis of intracellular fluorescence of HeLa cells treated with 20 µM (Cha-r)2-R4 under MW irradiation. Two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to compare conditions with and without MW irradiation (*p < 0.05).

Next, we assessed whether MW irradiation under these conditions affected cellular viability and morphology. HeLa cells exposed to MW irradiation at 20 W for 5 min in the presence of (Cha-r)2-R4 maintained a high viability comparable to that of the controls, including cells cultured under non-irradiation conditions with or without the peptide (Fig. 4). In addition, confocal microscopy of plasma membrane-stained cells revealed no detectable morphological alterations following MW irradiation (Figure S7). Consistently, flow cytometric analysis of forward and side scatter profiles showed no significant shifts between MW-irradiated and non-irradiated cell populations, indicating that MW irradiation at 20 W for 5 min was unlikely to substantially affect cell size or intracellular complexity (Figure S8).

HeLa cell viability after treatment with 20 µM (Cha-r)2-R4 under MW irradiation at 20 W for 5 min. Control experiments were conducted in the absence of peptide and MW irradiation. Peptide-induced cytotoxicity was also evaluated under non-irradiation conditions. Error bars represent the standard deviation of three biological replicate experiments.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effects of MW irradiation on the intracellular distribution of peptides designed based on previously reported mitochondrial targeting sequences. Confocal microscopy and flow cytometry analyses showed that MW irradiation at 2.45 GHz increased intracellular fluorescence intensity in a sequence-dependent manner, with (Cha-r)2-R4 exhibiting a more pronounced increase compared with the other designed peptides. These findings suggest that the effects of MW irradiation on intracellular accumulation depend on the intrinsic internalization capacity of the peptide. Specifically, MW irradiation appears to have limited influence when the intrinsic internalization ability is low, such as in peptides with lower L-arginine content. In contrast, within the range of peptides examined in this study, MW irradiation appears to enhance intracellular accumulation of peptides with a moderate intrinsic capacity for cellular internalization.

To further characterize intracellular distribution, colocalization analysis was performed. Under MW irradiation, the overlap coefficient between peptide-derived fluorescence and mitochondrial staining increased, whereas the Pearson correlation coefficients remained low. These results suggest that peptide-derived fluorescence spatially overlaps with mitochondrial regions. Therefore, MW irradiation may modulate the potential for delivering peptides to mitochondrial regions.

MW irradiation may enhance peptide uptake through perturbations in cellular structures23, but the underlying mechanisms were not directly investigated in this study. In particular, thermal and non-thermal effects were not distinguished. Therefore, mechanistic interpretations remain speculative.

Taken together, our results suggest that MW irradiation enhances intracellular accumulation of peptides and modulates the potential for delivering peptides to mitochondrial regions in a sequence-dependent manner, although further quantitative and/or detailed analyses, such as mitochondrial function assays, will be required.

Conclusion

We investigated the effects of MW irradiation on the intracellular distribution of peptides. MW irradiation induced sequence-dependent changes in cellular fluorescence intensity, with (Cha-r)2-R4 showing a more pronounced increase without apparent short-term effects on cell viability. Colocalization analysis indicated an increase in spatial association between peptide-derived fluorescence and mitochondrial staining, although Pearson correlation coefficients remained low. These results suggest that MW irradiation modulates peptide delivery to mitochondrial regions. To the best of our knowledge, this study demonstrates the potential for delivering peptides conjugated with a signal sequence to mitochondrial regions using MW for the first time. However, the precise mechanisms underlying these effects remain unclear, and additional studies, including mechanistic validation and evaluation using cargo-conjugated peptides, should be conducted in future experiments. Moreover, studies using multiple cell lines beyond HeLa cells will be important to determine whether cell-specific behavior of the peptides can be observed under MW irradiation. These future findings would suggest that MW-assisted peptide delivery can be developed as a more efficient strategy for mitochondrial targeting, thereby contributing to advances in peptide-based intracellular drug delivery systems.

Materials & methods

General remarks

All chemicals and solvents were of reagent or high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade and were used without further purification. HPLC was performed on a GL7410 HPLC system (GL Sciences Inc., Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an Inertsil ODS-3 column (10 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm, GL Sciences) for preparative purification. A linear gradient was applied using 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) in Milli-Q water (Solution A) and 0.08% TFA in acetonitrile (Solution B). Peptides were analyzed by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) using an Autoflex III mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA, USA) with 3,5-dimethoxy-4-hydroxycinnamic acid as the matrix. Amino acid analysis was performed using an Inertsil ODS-2 column (4.6 mm × 200 mm, 5 μm, GL Sciences) after hydrolyzing peptides in 6 M HCl at 110 °C for 24 h in a sealed tube, followed by derivatization with phenylisothiocyanate. Nα-(((9H-fluoren-9-yl)methoxy)carbonyl) (Fmoc))-Nω-(2,2,4,6,7-pentamethyldihydrobenzofuran-5-sulfonyl)-L-arginine (Fmoc-L-Arg(Pbf)-OH), Nα-Fmoc-Nω-(2,2,4,6,7-pentamethyldihydrobenzofuran-5-sulfonyl)-D-arginine (Fmoc-D-Arg(Pbf)-OH), and Nα-Fmoc-β-cyclohexyl-L-alanine (Fmoc-Cha-OH), Fmoc-NH-SAL PEG resin, 2-(1H-benzotriazole-1-yl)-1,1,3,3-tetramethyluronium hexafluorophosphate (HBTU), 1-hydroxy benzotriazole monohydrate (HOBt·H2O), 1-((dimethylamino)(dimethyliminio)methyl)-1H-1–3 triazolo[4,5-b]pyridine 3-oxide hexafluorophosphate (HATU), N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIEA), TFA, and piperidine were obtained from Watanabe Chemical Industries, Ltd. (Hiroshima, Japan). 5(6)-Carboxyfluorescein was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). N-Methylpyrrolidone (NMP), triisopropylsilane and diethyl ether were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan).

Peptide synthesis

The peptides were manually synthesized on Fmoc-NH-SAL PEG resin using standard Fmoc solid-phase synthesis34. Coupling of Fmoc-L-Arg(Pbf)-OH was carried out for 30 min using the amino acids (10 eq.), HBTU (10 eq.), HOBt⸱H2O (10 eq.), and DIEA (8 eq.). For Fmoc-D-Arg(Pbf)-OH, Fmoc-Cha-OH, and 5(6)-carboxyfluorescein, coupling was performed for 30 min with the amino acids or fluorophore (4 eq.), HATU (4 eq.) and DIEA (8 eq.). Fmoc deprotection was achieved using 25% piperidine in NMP. Cleavage of the synthesized peptides from the resins and simultaneous removal of side-chain protecting groups (Pbf) were performed using a TFA/H2O/triisopropylsilane (95/1/4, v/v/v). The crude peptides were precipitated with cold diethyl ether, collected by centrifugation, and dried under vacuum. Purification was performed by reverse-phase HPLC, and the purified peptides were characterized by MALDI-TOF MS: (Cha-r)2, m/z 994.2 ([M + H]+ calcd. 995.2); (Cha-r)3 m/z 1304.5 ([M + H]+ calcd. 1304.6); (Cha-r)2-R2, m/z 1307.7 ([M + H]+ calcd. 1307.5); (Cha-r)2-R3, m/z 1463.6 ([M + H]+ calcd. 1463.7); (Cha-r)2-R4, m/z 1619.7 ([M + H]+ calcd. 1619.9). The HPLC purities of (Cha-r)2, (Cha-r)3, (Cha-r)2-R2, (Cha-r)2-R3, and (Cha-r)2-R4 were 98.8%, 96.6%, 99.7%, 99.8%, and 99.4%, respectively. These peptides were dissolved in Milli-Q water at a concentration of 1 mM, and their concentration was determined by amino acid analysis. All peptide solutions were stored at 4 °C.

MW irradiation system

The MW irradiating system, manufactured as a medical apparatus by Minato Medical Science Co., Ltd. (Osaka, Japan), was used. The system generated MWs at a frequency of 2450 ± 3 MHz using a semiconductor-type oscillator, with an adjustable output power ranging from 10 to 60 W in 10 W increments. The output was delivered to a linearly polarized patch antenna. Cell culture dishes were placed 35 mm above the antenna and irradiated with MWs.

A thermocouple thermometer (Card Logger MR5300, MR9302, CHINO Corp., Tokyo, Japan) was used to measure the temperature of D-MEM (800 µL) in a cell culture dish during MW irradiation at 10 W for 30 min or 20 W for 5 min. We previously confirmed that temperatures measured using the thermocouple thermometer were nearly identical to those obtained using thermography (OPTXI40LTF20CFT090, Optris GmbH, Berlin, Germany) on the time-point measurements13.

In a previous study using the same irradiation system and experimental setup, the electric field (E-field) was directly measured at 10 W using an E-field sensor (ES-100, Seikoh Giken Co., Ltd.)27. Under identical conditions (cell culture dishes positioned 35 mm above the antenna), the measured E-field components were 355 V/m (x-axis), 1616 V/m (y-axis), and 211 V/m (z-axis), indicating the electromagnetic exposure conditions used in this system. It should be noted that the system output is controlled by irradiation duration rather than E-field strength. Therefore, the E-field intensity is expected to remain nearly constant even when the output power is increased to 20 W.

Cell culture

HeLa cell line was obtained from Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources Cell Bank (JCRB; No. JCRB9004 Lot NO. 11062023). HeLa cells were cultured in D-MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Intracellular distribution of peptides analyzed by confocal microscopy

HeLa cells (3.3 × 10⁴ cells) were seeded in cell culture dishes (CELLview, Greiner Bio-One International GmbH, Kremsmünster, Austria) containing D-MEM supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. After incubation, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed twice with 1× PBS buffer (200 µL per wash). Peptide solutions were prepared in D-MEM supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin and 20 mM HEPES buffer, and 800 µL of the solution was added to each dish to achieve a final peptide concentration of 10 or 20 µM. MW irradiation was then performed at either 10 W for 30 min or 20 W for 5 min. Following MW irradiation, cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 90 min (10 W for 30 min) or 55 min (20 W for 5 min). For non-irradiation conditions, cells were maintained at 38 °C for 30 min or 5 min to match the corresponding irradiation periods, followed by incubation at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 90 min or 55 min, respectively. Temperature control under non-irradiation conditions was performed using an incubator (A0601-2 V, Ikuta Sangyo Co., Ltd., Ueda, Japan). After incubation, the cells were washed once with 20 mM HEPES buffer (200 µL) and then again with 20 mM HEPES buffer (100 µL). Mitochondria, plasma membranes, and nuclei were stained with MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Thermo Fisher Scientific K. K., Tokyo, Japan), PlasMem Bright Red (Dojindo Laboratories, Mashiki, Japan), and Hoechst 33342 (Dojindo Laboratories), respectively, for 10 min. Cells were subsequently imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM-700, Carl Zeiss Inc., Jena, Germany) equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 40×/1.3 oil DIC M27 objective. For the observation of peptides, MitoTracker Red CMXRos, PlasMem Bright Red, and Hoechst 33342, excitation/emission wavelengths were 488/521 nm, 555/599 nm, 555/730 nm, and 405/461 nm, respectively. Pearson correlation coefficients and overlap coefficients for (Cha-r)2-R4 were calculated using Zen software (version 2.3 SP1, Carl Zeiss Inc., https://www.zeiss.com/microscopy/en/products/software/zeiss-zen.html). For colocalization analysis, whole-cell ROIs were used as the primary dataset. In addition, subcellular regions showing apparent spatial overlap between fluorescence channels were analyzed separately to assess local enrichment. The criteria for region selection were based on predefined morphological features, defined as regions showing consistent co-distribution patterns of peptide-derived and mitochondrial fluorescence signals across imaging fields, rather than colocalization intensity. Subcellular ROI analysis was performed only as an exploratory evaluation and was not used for primary statistical comparisons. ROIs were manually defined based on consistent spatial patterns observed across multiple imaging fields using identical acquisition and analysis settings for all conditions. For all analyses, images were acquired and processed using identical imaging and analysis settings across all experimental groups to ensure comparability. No additional background subtraction was applied. Therefore, the fluorescence values represent raw integrated intensities under consistent acquisition conditions. This approach allows relative comparison of fluorescence signals between experimental conditions under identical measurement parameters.

Quantification of intracellular fluorescence by flow cytometry

HeLa cells (3.4 × 105 cells) were seeded in 35 mm cell culture dishes (Wuxi NEST Bio-technology Co. Ltd., Wuxi, China) and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. After incubation, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed twice with 1× PBS buffer (500 µL per wash). Peptide solutions (10 or 20 µM, 800 µL) were then added, followed by MW irradiation at either 10 W for 30 min or 20 W for 5 min. Following MW irradiation, the cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 90 min (10 W, 30 min) or for 55 min (20 W, 5 min). For non-irradiation conditions, cells were maintained at 38 °C for 30 or 5 min to match the corresponding irradiation periods and subsequently incubated at 37 °C for 90 or 55 min in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Temperature control under non-irradiation conditions was performed using an incubator (A0601-2 V, Ikuta Sangyo Co., Ltd.). After incubation, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed twice with 1 × PBS buffer (500 µL per wash). Cells were then detached by treatment with 0.25 w/v% trypsin (200 µL) at 37 °C for 1 min. Trypsinization was quenched by adding 500 µL of phenol red-free D-MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and the resulting cell suspension was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 3 min at 25 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of phenol red-free D-MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cellular fluorescence was analyzed using a flow cytometer (FACS Aria Ⅱ, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) equipped with a 488 nm excitation laser and a 530/30 nm emission filter. Live cells were gated based on forward- and side-scatter parameters and quantified for fluorescence intensity. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using BD FACSDiva software (version 9.0.1, Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA, https://www.bdbiosciences.com/en-us/products/software/instrument-software/bd-facsdiva-software), and fluorescence intensity was quantified as the mean fluorescence intensity of gated live-cell populations. All gating strategies were kept identical across samples.

Cytotoxicity test

HeLa cells (3.4 × 105 cells) were seeded in 35 mm cell culture dishes (Wuxi NEST Bio-technology Co. Ltd.) and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. After removal of the culture medium, the cells were washed three times with 1 × PBS buffer (1 mL per wash). The cells were then treated with 800 µL of culture medium containing a 20 µM peptide solution and subjected to MW irradiation at 20 W for 5 min. Following MW irradiation, the cells were further incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 for 55 min. For control experiments, cells were incubated at 37 °C for 60 min in the absence of both peptide treatment and MW irradiation. In parallel, peptide-induced cytotoxicity under non-irradiation conditions was evaluated by incubating cells with 800 µL of culture medium containing a 20 µM peptide solution at 37 °C for 60 min in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Temperature control under non-irradiation conditions was performed using an incubator (A0601-2 V, Ikuta Sangyo Co., Ltd.). After incubation, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with 1 × PBS buffer (1 mL per wash). Cell viability was subsequently assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo Laboratories).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests in Microsoft Excel (version 2019, Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/excel). Data were assumed to be normally distributed, and homogeneity of variances was assessed using an F-test prior to applying the t-tests. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.
