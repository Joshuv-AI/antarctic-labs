 ABSTRACT

A study was conducted to evaluate sorbent technologies that can mitigate the presence of elemental
mercury (Hg0) in waste containers for mercury-contaminated debris (MCD). Decontamination and
demolition (D&D) activities at the Y-12 National Security Complex (Y-12) and other U.S. Department of
Energy (DOE) Oak Ridge Reservation (ORR) facilities generate MCD requiring offsite disposal. The
debris is packaged in appropriate waste containers and may be temporarily stored onsite prior to transport
for treatment and/or disposal. During transportation of loads that had no visible liquid Hg at the point of
origin, temperature changes can cause Hg0 to evaporate, condense, and form droplets on container walls.
Furthermore, vibration during transportation could cause beads of Hg to be released from the debris,
container walls, and ceiling, resulting in pools of liquid Hg0 on the container floor. Waste acceptance
criteria (WAC) limitations for commercial disposal facilities, the Nevada National Security Site (NNSS),
and ORR mixed low-level waste landfills prohibit the presence of any free liquids in containers identified
as a solid waste form. Potential solutions to mitigate the presence of residual liquids that could be formed
through vapor condensation include the use of sorbents or similar materials to capture and stabilize
volatile Hg0 vapors and thus ensure compliance with landfill WAC requirements.

This report summarizes data from small-scale laboratory experiments conducted to evaluate sorbent
materials for Hg0 vapor suppression and sorption of liquid Hg0 that could form under relevant
transportation and disposal conditions. A series of experiments was conducted to evaluate commercial
sorbent materials and their effectiveness for Hg0 sorption across a temperature range from 19.4°C to
60°C. The impact of residual moisture on sorption was investigated under relevant conditions, and
leaching tests were performed to assess the stability of Hg0 captured sorbent materials. The results
provide estimates for sorbent quantities needed for a given Hg 0 mass loading based on experimental
results exposing sorbents to gaseous and liquid Hg0 at various mass ratios. Overall, sorbents that were
most effective for Hg0 vapor suppression were brominated activated carbons, mackinawite-based sorbents
coated on vermiculite, and sulfur-modified granular activated carbon. Elevated temperatures and moisture
conditions did not result in significant increases of Hg0 headspace concentrations, and the materials also
demonstrated high sorption capacities for the sorption of liquid Hg0.

 1. INTRODUCTION

The ongoing and planned operations for decontamination and demolition (D&D) of facilities at the Y-12
National Security Complex (Y-12) and other US Department of Energy (DOE) Oak Ridge Reservation
(ORR) facilities produce mercury-contaminated debris (MCD) that requires offsite transportation and
disposal (Zacha, 2013).

The MCD is packaged in various types of waste containers, including but not limited to barrels, drums,
B-25 waste containers, and roll-off dumpsters. The containers may be kept temporarily onsite prior to off-
site transportation for treatment and/or disposal. During onsite storage or transportation, temperature
changes can cause elemental mercury (Hg0) to vaporize, condense, and form droplets on container walls.
In addition, vibrations during transport can cause beads of Hg to be released from the debris or from the
container walls and ceiling, resulting in pools of liquid Hg0 on the container floor. Waste acceptance
criteria (WAC) limitations for commercial disposal facilities, the Nevada National Security Site (NNSS),
or ORR mixed low-level waste (MLLW) landfills prohibit the presence of any free liquids in containers
identified for packing and transport of solid waste forms. Potential solutions to mitigate the presence of
residual liquids that could form after packaging include the use of sorbents or similar materials to capture

 1

and stabilize volatile Hg0 vapors and liquid Hg0 (Teng & Altaf, 2022; Worthington et al., 2017), ensuring
compliance with landfill WAC requirements.

The current standard practice is to pack MCD into containers with vermiculite (25% to 40% MCD and
60% to 75% vermiculite, by volume). This practice of packing the container with vermiculite serves two
purposes: (1) the vermiculite reduces the shifting of the MCD within containers, and (2) it fills void space
in the containers. Potential issues arise from the high vapor pressure of Hg 0 causing its evaporation from
MCD as the temperature of the container increases, followed by condensation on the container walls as
temperatures drop. These temperature fluctuations can result in the accumulation of liquid Hg 0 at the
bottom of the container.

The Environmental Sciences Division (ESD) at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, in close collaboration
with United Cleanup Oak Ridge (UCOR), conducted laboratory bench-scale studies to evaluate sorbents
for their ability to capture and stabilize Hg0 to prevent condensation and pooling of liquid Hg0 inside
waste containers during staging and transport.

This report summarizes the physical and chemical characteristics of Hg0 and descriptions of the evaluated
sorbent materials, describes experimental methods and conditions, and provides detailed results of the
laboratory studies. The effectiveness of sorbent materials was evaluated under temperature and moisture
conditions considered pertinent to the transport of MCD inside waste containers. The results provide
insights into the sorption kinetics, capacity, and effectiveness of each sorbent material in immobilizing
liquid or vapor Hg0. In addition, a protocol adapted from methods outlined in the Toxicity Characteristic
Leaching Procedure (TCLP) was used to evaluate the stability of Hg after sorption.

The following disclaimer should be noted: the performance results and conclusions presented for the
sorbent materials are strictly limited to the specific experimental conditions under which they were
evaluated. These tests were designed and conducted exclusively at laboratory scales to assess sorbent
efficacy under controlled conditions that were based on informed assumptions about MCD inside waste
containers. The findings herein should not be generalized, extrapolated, or applied to any other scenarios,
use cases, or environmental conditions. The data carry no implications for sorbent performance in
different applications, and any such inference is not supported by this evaluation.

1.1 PHYSICAL AND CHEMICAL CHARACTERISTICS OF ELEMENTAL MERCURY

Elemental Hg, with a melting point of −38.83°C and a boiling point of 356.6°C, stands out among the
metals as the only element that remains liquid at standard ambient temperature and pressure. Mercury is
almost entirely monomeric in the gas phase, which is unique among metals. These attributes stem from
relativistic contraction as electrons fill the orbitals around the nucleus, leading to a decrease in the radius
of the inner atomic orbitals. In Hg, the 1s and 2s orbitals show relativistic contraction, which leads to a
corresponding contraction in the valence 6s orbital, bringing 6s electrons much closer to the nucleus and
making them behave more like a filled shell than would be expected from its valence alone, resulting in
weak metal–metal bonding (Gonzalez-Raymat et al., 2017; Norrby, 1991). Physically, it possesses an
exceptionally high density of 13.534 g/cm3 at 20°C, a very high surface tension of approximately 485
mN/m (water 72 mN/m), and good thermal and electrical conductivity (thermal 8.3 W·m −1·K−1, electrical
10.4 kS·m−1). For a comprehensive summary of physical parameters, see Table 1.

Liquid Hg0 forms amalgams with most metals, except a small group of transition metals (iron, platinum,
tungsten, tantalum, cobalt, and nickel). Amalgams with gold, silver, copper, zinc, and alkali metals have
thus been used historically in various industrial processes (Deiseroth, 1997). Chemically, Hg 0 exhibits
low reactivity and remains stable in dry air, but oxidation to HgO can occur slowly at elevated
temperatures. It resists dissolution in non-oxidizing acids like HCl but reacts with concentrated HNO 3 to

 2

form Hg(II). The high surface tension of liquid Hg0 causes it to disperse into fine droplets that can
become trapped in small pores or at interfaces. Entrapment effects can become particularly pronounced if
the debris includes porous media such as concrete, fine particulates, or a substantial amount of moisture.

1.1.1 Equilibrium Vapor Pressure of Mercury

Equilibrium vapor pressure is defined as the pressure exerted by a substance in the vapor phase in
equilibrium with its liquid and solid phases in a closed system at a given temperature. The equilibrium
vapor pressure of pure Hg0 is primarily determined by temperature. Equilibrium vapor pressures have
been determined for Hg experimentally from below the triple point (i.e. the temperature and pressure
where Hg’s solid, liquid, and gas phases coexist in equilibrium) to the critical point (i.e. the temperature
and pressure above which Hg’s liquid and gas phases become indistinguishable, forming a supercritical
fluid) (Huber et al., 2006). At room temperature (25°C) and ambient air pressure, the partial vapor
pressure of Hg0 is approximately 0.261 Pa. As the temperature increases, the partial vapor pressure of Hg 0
rises exponentially, reaching approximately 51 Pa at 100°C (Figure 1). The evaporation rate of Hg 0 is
generally determined by the temperature and the surface area of liquid Hg, where a larger surface area
(higher degree of Hg0 dispersion) results in faster evaporation rates.

1.1.3 Evaporation and Condensation of Elemental Mercury in Closed Containers

A closed waste container holding MCD with liquid Hg0 undergoes a dynamic vapor–liquid equilibrium,
which is controlled by changes in temperature. The saturation vapor pressure for elemental Hg, or the
pressure exerted by Hg vapor in equilibrium with its liquid phase in a closed container, is relatively low at
temperatures below 20°C but increases exponentially as temperature increases (Figure 3). As vapor
pressure builds with increasing temperature, the net evaporation rate inside the closed container
decreases. If the container is not perfectly isothermal (e.g., hot or cold surfaces create thermal gradients),
then convection currents may arise and accelerate mixing of Hg0 vapor throughout the container. At
steady state, the system assumes a vapor–liquid equilibrium such that the rates of evaporation and
condensation are equal. However, as temperature decreases and the vapor pressure reaches the saturation
limit at a given (lower) temperature, condensation occurs, preferentially on cooler surfaces (purple arrow
in Figure 3). Nucleation on container walls may form satellite droplets if supersaturation occurs
transiently, leading to Ostwald ripening (Madras & McCoy, 2004) whereby smaller droplets evaporate in
favor of larger ones due to the Kelvin effect (elevated vapor pressure of droplets with higher interfacial
curvature). The quantity of Hg subject to condensation as a liquid is determined by the temperature
change (ΔT = T2 – T3), the resulting change in Hg0 vapor concentration (ΔcHg), and the total volume of the
headspace and open voids inside the MCD and container. Thus, minimizing the headspace volume can
lower the quantity of liquid Hg0 formed by condensation.

 2. MATERIALS AND METHODS

2.1 SORBENTS

The sorbents evaluated in this study represent different categories of materials (Table 2), which broadly
include activated carbons (brominated and non-brominated), vermiculite (a phyllosilicate clay mineral),
and a proprietary mackinawite-based colloidal suspension coated on vermiculite. Activated carbons are
broadly used for the immobilization of contaminants due to their high specific surface area, porous
structure, and excellent adsorption properties for a wide range of contaminants. Brominated and sulfur-
impregnated activated carbons are routinely used for the removal of Hg0 vapor from industrial flue gas or
natural gas streams to protect downstream equipment and the environment. Biochars represent a
sustainable alternative to engineered activated carbons. They provide lower surface areas, but abundant
surface functional groups (e.g. -OH, -COOH, -SH, etc.). In addition to carbon-based materials, we include
two variants of a proprietary reactive iron sulfide material (FerroBlack H40 and FerroBlack Fe+)
specifically developed for in situ remediation targeting heavy metals. Vermiculite is not primarily used
for Hg0 capture but is used as an inexpensive waste packaging material with a high sorption capacity for
liquids. It can also be used as a substrate for the application of reagent suspensions, such as FerroBlack
H40 and FerroBlack Fe+.

Generally, the addition of sorbents to waste packaging systems requires minimal processing, can be
applied at scale, and is compatible with other approved packing materials, such as vermiculite. A suitable
sorbent must capture Hg0 in a form that cannot easily be released under the physicochemical conditions of

 7

transport and long-term storage to prevent evaporation, condensation, or pooling of Hg 0. The goal of
sorbent addition is to prevent the formation of liquid Hg0 inside MCD by minimizing (1)
mobility/volatility, (2) corrosiveness (e.g., by amalgamation), (3) toxicity, and (4) solubility, thereby
averting the release of hazardous Hg species at the disposal site.

2.1.1 Activated Carbon

Activated carbon is a highly porous form of carbon that has been processed via various thermochemical
methods from carbonaceous feedstocks, such as coal and lignocellulosic biomass. The activation process
involves heating the precursor material in the absence of oxygen to produce a carbon-rich char, followed
by an activation step. Physical activation utilizes oxidizing gases such as steam or carbon dioxide at
elevated temperatures (700–1100°C) to create a highly porous structure with exceptionally high specific
surface areas, often ranging from 500 to over 2000 m²/g. Activated carbons sorb a wide range of
contaminants, including many Hg species (Abel & Akkanen, 2019; Gai et al., 2019; Hadi et al., 2015).
The surface chemistry of the activated carbon facilitates chemisorption of Hg0. Detailed analyses of Hg0
sorption to activated carbons suggest that the primary adsorption mechanism is the oxidation of Hg 0 to
Hg2+ mediated by sulfide, chloride, and oxygen anions present on the sorbent, which results in the
formation of stable complexes like mercuric sulfide (HgS) or Hg-halide species (Huggins et al., 2003;
Sasmaz et al., 2012).

The primary use case for activated carbons in the context of mercury remediation is their application in
coal-fired power plants with flue gas stream injection systems to control Hg 0 emissions (Sjostrom et al.,
2010; Yang et al., 2007, 2018). The spent adsorbent is then removed downstream in particulate collection
devices such as fabric filters or electrostatic precipitators. Activated carbon can also be treated with
oxidizing agents (e.g., halogens such as chlorine or bromine) that convert Hg 0 to Hg2+, which is adsorbed
on the sorbent surface (Sasmaz et al., 2012). Sulfur-impregnated activated carbon was shown to increase
sorption of Hg species and can be obtained by treating activated carbon with polysulfides, sulfur, or
organic thiols (Hsi et al., 2001; Kim et al., 2011). Modified activated carbons generally enhance Hg 0
removal efficiencies by introducing functional groups that sorb Hg0 by forming a strong covalent bond.

Fluepac SPR MAXX is manufactured by Calgon Carbon Corp. and is a non-brominated product
formulated to enhance Hg capture in flue gas treatment applications across a wide range of temperatures.
The product is described as particularly effective in combination coal additives such as calcium bromide
and flue gas streams with elevated sulfur trioxide levels, where Hg removal is significantly more
challenging.

2.1.1.4 Calgon Carbon FILTRASORB 300

FILTRASORB 300 is a non-brominated granular activated carbon (GAC) for the removal of
contaminants from water and wastewater as well as industrial and food processing streams.

2.1.2     Biochar

Biochar is a sustainable and cost-effective alternative to activated carbon because it is derived from
biomass or organic waste products (Gomez-Eyles et al., 2013; Moreira et al., 2017). Depending on the
feedstock and preparation method, biochars typically exhibit lower adsorption capacities compared to
chemically treated activated carbon. However, biochar may be a cost-effective option due to its high
surface area and sorption properties, which are similar to those of activated carbons. Disadvantages of
using biochar-based sorbents are potentially inconsistent product characteristics and variable
performance, depending on the feedstock and preparation method.

2.1.3 Vermiculite

Vermiculite (A-4 Extra Coarse) is manufactured by Palmetto Vermiculite Co. and is approved for use as
an absorbent and filler material for packing low-level waste (LLW) and MLLW in containers on the Oak
Ridge Reservation for onsite staging and offsite transportation and disposal. Containers with LLW or
MLLW may contain up to 75% vermiculite by volume depending on the waste stream and moisture
content.

For the laboratory-scale experiments described here, the vermiculite was sieved using 8 mesh (2.38 mm)
and 35 mesh (0.5 mm) sieves to obtain a uniform size fraction suitable for small-scale laboratory tests.

2.1.4 FerroBlack H40 and FerroBlack Fe+ Coated on Vermiculite

FerroBlack H40 and FerroBlack Fe+ are manufactured by Redox Solutions and were received as colloidal
suspensions based on mackinawite (iron sulfide) in a proprietary formulation (Cheng et al., 2016; Jeong et
al., 2007). For the laboratory tests, the suspensions were coated on vermiculite
(0.5–2.38 mm size fraction) following recommendations by Redox Solutions. FB-H40 or FB-Fe+ were

2.2 ANALYTICAL INSTRUMENTATION AND ANALYSIS METHODS

2.2.1 Hg0 Vapor Measurements

Experiments were conducted in 40 mL amber borosilicate vials closed with polypropylene caps equipped
with PTFE-lined silicone septa (Figure 4). Liquid elemental Hg0 was added to the vials and was allowed
to equilibrate with the air within the vial, and the gaseous Hg that evolved within the headspace of the vial
was sampled with gastight syringes (Hamilton 1800 series, Hamilton Company, Reno, NV). Gaseous Hg 0
concentrations were analyzed using a cold vapor atomic absorption spectroscopy (CV-AAS) Zeeman
effect Hg analyzer (RA-915+, Ohio Lumex Company, Inc., Twinsburg, OH, USA). Typical volumes of
air sampled within the headspace of the vials were 50 µL. Calibration was performed using a Tekran
Model 2505 Hg vapor calibration unit (Tekran Instruments Corporation, Toronto, Canada).

2.2.2 Relative Humidity

Relative humidity inside sample vials was measured using an Elitech GSP-6 data logger equipped with
temperature and humidity probes (Elitech Technology, Inc., San Jose, CA).

2.2.3 Solid Sample Digestion

To assess the efficiency of different sorbent materials at immobilizing liquid and gaseous Hg, sorbents
were added to the glass vials at different sorbent: Hg ratios. After experimentation, in order to measure
how much Hg was associated with the sorbent materials, microwave digestions of solid sorbent samples
were performed using a Milestone ETHOS UP microwave digestion system (Milestone Inc., Shelton, CT)
equipped with an SK-15 high-pressure digestion rotor and with 12 polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) vessels
(100 mL each). Before each digestion run, the vessels were initially cleaned with 10 ml of ~35% (w/w)
HNO3 using a pre-set cleaning cycle in the microwave digestion system, rinsed with Milli-Q water to
remove any residual acid, and air-dried. Between subsequent same-day runs, each digestion vessel was
sonicated with 40 mL of 25% (w/w) HNO3 for 30 minutes, rinsed with ultrapure water, sonicated again
for 30 minutes with 40 mL of 25% (w/w) HCl, rinsed again with ultrapure water, and then air-dried.

For sample digestions, approximately 100 mg of Hg-containing sorbent material was added to a PTFE
digestion vessel with 9 mL of trace-metal-grade 37% (w/w) HCl and 3 mL of trace-metal-grade 70%
(w/w) HNO3 (aqua regia). Samples were digested using a microwave digestion protocol following EPA
method 3051A (U.S. EPA, 2007), as implemented by the equipment manufacturer. The resulting digest
was transferred into a 50 mL polypropylene centrifuge tube. To ensure complete Hg recovery, the vessel
was rinsed, and the rinsate was added to the digest and diluted to a final volume of 50 mL. The final
solution weight was recorded (average: 52 ± 0.5 g). The diluted samples were preserved with 0.5% (v/v)
bromine monochloride (BrCl) and stored for at least one week before analysis. Finally, 15–200 μL
aliquots were drawn from the supernatant and diluted by a factor of 1–200 for total Hg analysis.

2.2.4 Total Mercury Analysis

Total Hg concentrations in aqueous samples obtained from solid sample digests were analyzed following
EPA Method 1631 (U.S. EPA, 2002). For analysis, a 0.5% v/v BrCl-acidified sample solution was

 10

diluted, and the resulting aliquot was transferred into a borosilicate vial containing Milli-Q water, along
with stannous chloride (SnCl2) and hydroxylamine hydrochloride (NH2OH·HCl). Mercury species were
reduced and purged using ultra-high-purity nitrogen gas (N2), enabling the quantification of Hg0 via cold
vapor atomic absorption spectroscopy (CV-AAS), employing a Zeeman-effect Hg analyzer (RA-915+,
Ohio Lumex Company, Inc., Twinsburg, OH, USA). Calibration of the instrument was performed using a
series of Hg(NO3)2 standards (Brooks Rand Instruments, Seattle, WA, USA). The method detection limit
was 10 ng/L.

2.2.5 Electron Microscopy

Physical sorbent characteristics, such as surface morphology, sorbent porosity, and surface roughness can
directly influence the sorbent performance. These structural features can be visualized by scanning
electron microscopy (SEM). In addition, energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) enables elemental
mapping to locate Hg after sorbent exposure and reveal the distribution of other elements that may be
relevant for sorption on the sorbent surface. A Hitachi S4800 scanning electron microscope equipped for
EDS was used to analyze the surface morphology of sorbents. The EDS spectra and elemental maps were
obtained at a 20 kV acceleration voltage. FerroBlack H40 and FerroBlack Fe+ samples were dried in
vacuum before SEM sample preparation. Samples were affixed to the aluminum SEM stage using double-
sided carbon tape and prepared for SEM/EDS analysis.

3.1 MITIGATION OF LIQUID ELEMENTAL MERCURY BY SORBENTS

The aim of these experiments was to evaluate the effectiveness of sorbent materials in suppressing Hg 0
vapor formation after adding liquid Hg0 directly to the sorbents, simulating the condensation or release of
liquid Hg0 from MCD. The concentration of Hg0 vapor in the headspace was measured over time to detect
the presence of residual Hg0 that is subject to evaporation inside the sample container. The goal was to
identify Hg0:sorbent ratios that are adequate to maintain the Hg0 vapor headspace concentrations below
the saturation limit to prevent condensation.

Known quantities of sorbents were transferred into 40 mL amber borosilicate vials (Figure 4). Liquid
elemental Hg0 was added to the sorbent materials at various Hg0:sorbent mass ratios determined in a
series of exploratory experiments (section 3.1.1). The vials were closed with polypropylene caps equipped
with PTFE-lined silicone septa and placed on an end-over-end rotator for mixing, and the Hg 0 vapor
pressure in the headspace of the vials was tracked periodically for up to 973 hours. The temperature
during the experiments was 19.4 ± 0.4°C.

3.1.1 Results from Preliminary Experiments

A series of preliminary experiments were conducted to determine the Hg0:sorbent mass ratios required for
effective Hg0 vapor suppression after combining each sorbent with liquid Hg0. This empirical approach
was necessary due to the absence of sorption data for liquid Hg0 and the inherent variability of sorbent
properties, such as surface area, chemical affinity, and porosity, that influence sorbent interactions with
liquid Hg0. Testing a broad range of Hg0:sorbent ratios (1:3 to 10:1) identified a set of conditions needed
for Hg0 vapor suppression. The results guided subsequent experiments assessing sorption capacity and the
impact of moisture and temperature on sorbent performance.
The data (Figure 5) show the evolution of Hg0 vapor concentrations in experimental vials filled with the
different sorbent materials at various Hg0:sorbent ratios. Among all the sorbents tested and at all
Hg0:sorbent ratios, MercLok™ P-640 was the most effective, consistently achieving vapor Hg0
concentrations below 1.4 ng/mL for the duration of the experiment (877 hours) (Figure 5D). This level
corresponds to a reduction of approximately 90% relative to the theoretical Hg 0 saturation concentration
of 13.754 ng/mL at 19.4ºC. FerroBlack H40-vermiculite showed slightly different kinetics, with elevated
vapor Hg0 concentrations at the beginning of the experiment, but achieved results similar to MercLok™
P-640 after about 250 hours. At higher Hg0:sorbent ratios of up to 4.6, Hg0 vapor suppression reached a
maximum of 50% of the saturation concentration and further improved over time (Figure 5A). Norit
DARCO Hg-LH showed similar kinetics to FerroBlack H40-vermiculite, with higher concentrations at
the beginning of the experiment and concentrations generally decreasing over time for Hg 0:sorbent ratios
of 2.57 and 5.08. At higher Hg0 mass loadings (Hg0:sorbent ratio of 9.5), headspace Hg0 increased over
time but remained at least ~40% below the theoretical saturation limit at the end of the experiment (877
hours) (Figure 5C). The remaining sorbent materials (Filtrasorb 300, Fluepac SPR MAXX) showed
varying degrees of vapor suppression, but headspace Hg0 reached the theoretical saturation limit in some
instances (Figure 5B and E).

3.2.2 Impact of Temperature and Moisture on Sorbent Performance

Increased temperature significantly reduced the efficiency of several of the sorbents. This was especially
true for Biochar, Filtrasorb 300, and Fluepac SPR MAXX under dry conditions but was also true of
FerroBlack Fe+ during wet conditions by the end of the 14 day experiment. Under both dry and moist
conditions and for both temperatures, vermiculite was not an effective sorbent for Hg 0 vapor, as the
headspace Hg0 vapor concentrations were not significantly different between the vermiculite treatment
and the Hg control (no sorbent). This is likely attributable to the saturation of available sorption sites,
suggesting that the Hg0 load exceeded total sorption capacity of vermiculite. While Fluepac SPR MAXX
performed well at 20°C under dry and moist conditions, its sorption capacity appeared to become
depleted after 7 days at 60°C under dry conditions. This same trend was not observed at 60°C under moist
conditions, suggesting a potential role of moisture in increasing sorption or limiting the evaporation rate
of Hg0. All other sorbents (MercLok™ P-640, FerroBlack H40, Norit DARCO Hg-LH, MERSORB)
reduced Hg0 headspace concentrations under all evaluated conditions with high effectiveness. Across all
conditions, MercLok™ P-640 resulted in the overall lowest Hg0 headspace concentrations.

3.3 PRELIMINARY CHARACTERIZATION OF SORBENT SURFACES

In general, sorbents are effective due to high surface area, porosity, and specific surface chemistry. Here
we describe preliminary measurements made to characterize the surface of the sorbents used in this study.

3.3.1 SURFACE AREA MEASUREMENTS

The specific surface areas of the sorbent materials were determined through nitrogen (N 2) adsorption-
desorption analysis at 77 K using a Micromeritics Gemini VII 2390a surface area analyzer. Prior to
analysis, samples were degassed for 4 hours at 150°C under a stream of N 2 to remove any physisorbed
contaminants. The determination of surface area is critical for sorbent characterization (Bardestani et al.,
2019), as a higher surface area generally corresponds to a greater number of available active sites for Hg
binding, thereby enhancing sorption capacity (Min et al., 2017).

This analysis was conducted using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method, a widely accepted
technique for characterizing porous materials (Sinha et al., 2019). The BET theory extends the Langmuir
model of monolayer adsorption to account for multilayer physisorption of gas molecules on a solid
surface. The volume of nitrogen gas adsorbed was measured at several partial pressures. The resulting
adsorption isotherm data for the relative pressure (P/P0) range of 0.05 to 0.30 were then linearized using
the BET equation. The slope and intercept of this linear plot were used to calculate the monolayer
adsorption capacity and the total surface area of each material.

The results show significant variations in surface area among the tested sorbents (Figure 14). Activated
carbons exhibited the highest BET specific surface areas, with Filtrasorb 300 (781 m 2/g), Fluepac SPR
MAXX (736 m2/g), and MercLok™ P-640 (691 m2/g) showing the highest values. In comparison, the
surface area of the Biochar sample was approximately an order of magnitude lower (95 m 2/g). In contrast,

 23

the BET surface areas of vermiculite, FerroBlack H40 and FerroBlack Fe+ coated on vermiculite showed
significantly lower surface areas (1.6 to 4.7 m2/g). In all cases, the Langmuir surface area values were
higher than the BET values, which is consistent with theoretical models, as the Langmuir equation
assumes a perfect monolayer and thus tends to overestimate surface area compared to the more realistic
multilayer model of the BET theory (Lowell et al., 2004).

Generally, a greater surface area is expected to offer more potential active sites for Hg binding. However,
the wide range in surface area shows no correlation with Hg0 sorption capacity (Figure 15) under all
tested environmental conditions.

3.4 LEACHING TESTS TO DETERMINE MERCURY STABILITY

The Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP) as outlined in EPA Method 1311 is a
standardized laboratory test developed by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) to determine
whether a waste material is hazardous due to its potential to leach toxic substances into the environment
when land-disposed. This method is employed to determine whether a waste exhibits the toxicity
characteristic, as defined under the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA). The TCLP is
specifically designed to assess the mobility of both organic and inorganic contaminants in solid waste,
simulating disposal conditions in a municipal solid waste landfill (U. S. EPA, 1992).

 25

In the present study, a modified TCLP was used due to limited sample quantities available from the
small-scale laboratory experiments.

TCLP extraction fluids:

 Extraction fluid 1 (TCLP 1): 0.1 M sodium acetate, pH 4.93 ± 0.05
 5.7 mL glacial acetic acid and 64.3 mL of 1N NaOH were added to 500 mL of reagent water and
 diluted to 1 L, and the pH was verified.

 Extraction fluid 2 (TCLP 2): 0.1 M acetic acid, pH 2.88 ± 0.05
 5.7 mL glacial acetic acid was diluted with reagent water to a final volume of 1 L, and the pH was
 verified.

Each extraction fluid (4 mL) was added to approximately 200 mg of sorbent materials in 15 mL
polypropylene centrifuge tubes to obtain a sample-to-fluid ratio of 1:20 (w/v). The tubes were agitated in
an end-over-end rotator at 30 ± 2 rpm for 18 ± 2 hours at ambient temperature (22 ± 3°C). After
extraction, the leachate was separated from the solid residue via filtration through a 0.7 µm glass fiber
filter (Whatman Puradisc syringe filters, GF/F, 0.7 µm, Ø 25 mm). The pH values for all samples
extracted with the extraction fluids were determined to range from pH 5.0 to 5.8 for TCLP 1 and from 3.2
to 4.7 for TCLP 2. The filtered leachate was collected and analyzed for total Hg as described in Section
2.2.4.

The procedure involves mixing samples with two extraction fluids (e.g., acetic acid or sodium acetate
solutions) to mimic the acidic environment of a landfill. After a specific period of mixing (e.g., 18 hours),
the liquid was separated and analyzed for the presence of leached Hg. The concentrations may be
compared to regulatory limits set by the EPA under the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act
(RCRA). The toxicity characteristic limit (TCL) as defined in 40 CFR 261.24 is a regulatory threshold
established by the EPA under the RCRA. It is used to determine if a waste is hazardous due to its
potential to leach toxic chemicals into groundwater. The Hg TCL of 0.2 mg/L (40 CFR 261.24, D009) is
set at 100x the maximum contaminant level (MCL) specified in 40 CFR 141.62.

Briefly, the results revealed that certain sorbents, such as FB-H40, FB-Fe+, and Fluepac SPR MAXX,
released significant quantities of sorbed Hg during both TCLP 1 and TCLP 2 extractions (Figure 16).
Conversely, MERSORB consistently demonstrated the lowest leached Hg concentrations under TCLP
conditions. The extent of Hg leaching was determined by the pH of the TCLP extraction fluid, with
released Hg higher in TCLP 2 than TCLP 1. Minor differences were observed as a result of dry/moist
conditions. MERSORB and MercLok™ P-640 were highly resistant to Hg release under the TCLP and
did not exceed the Hg TCL of 0.2 mg/L under any of the tested conditions, based on sorbed quantities of
4.02 mg Hg/g sorbent and 4.17 mg Hg/g sorbent, respectively. All other sorbents exceeded the TCL under
at least one of the tested conditions.

 4. CONCLUSIONS

Laboratory-scale studies to evaluate the effectiveness of sorbent materials to mitigate the presence of Hg 0
vapors that may lead to liquid Hg formation in waste containers for MCD were conducted. The materials
evaluated included multiple types of powdered or granular activated carbons (brominated, sulfur-
modified, and unmodified), biochar, mackinawite-based sorbents, and vermiculite.

Experiments to evaluate the mitigation of secondary formation of liquid Hg0 in direct contact with
sorbents showed that a decrease in Hg0 vapor pressure relative to the theoretical saturation limit can be
achieved across a wide range of Hg0:sorbent ratios. Adding a sorbent to liquid Hg0 that has formed from
the vapor phase may not result in rapid and complete sorption, which suggests that the sorbent tends to
form a surface layer that likely reduces the surface area available for evaporation, thereby limiting the
evaporation rate and lowering the headspace concentration inside the container. A second set of
experiments where the liquid Hg0 was mechanically mixed with the sorbent showed higher initial
headspace concentrations, followed by a decrease over time. In these experiments, the sorption of liquid
Hg0 was most rapid with MercLok™ P-640 and Norit DARCO Hg-LH at Hg0:sorbent ratios below 20%
(w/w).

A series of vapor phase equilibration experiments (drop of liquid Hg0 separate from sorbent) were
conducted to evaluate the ability of sorbents to suppress Hg0 vapor concentrations in the headspace of
closed containers. Generally, sorbents that were most effective for Hg0 vapor suppression were
brominated activated carbons, mackinawite-based sorbents coated on vermiculite, and sulfur-modified
granular activated carbon. While MercLok™ P-640, FerroBlack H40, Norit DARCO Hg-LH,
MERSORB, and Calgon Fluepac effectively suppressed Hg0 vapors at room temperature and under high

 27

moisture conditions, MercLok™ P-640, FerroBlack H40, Norit DARCO Hg-LH, and MERSORB were
found to be the materials with consistently effective vapor suppression at 60°C with or without moisture
present (< 5 mg/m3). The highest quantities of Hg0 captured by sorbents in vapor phase equilibration
experiments were ~5 mg Hg per g FerroBlack H40 and ~4 mg Hg per g MERSORB over a period of 14
days. These results suggest relatively high uptake rates for Hg0 while the sorption capacities for these
materials were not exceeded.

Experiments to evaluate the stability of spent sorbents to Hg leaching revealed that sulfur-modified
granular activated carbon and brominated powdered activated carbon were highly resistant to Hg release
in aqueous solutions containing mild acids. Specifically, MercLok™ P-640 and MERSORB did not
exceed the Hg TCL of 0.2 mg/L, based on sorbed quantities of 4.02 mg Hg/g sorbent and 4.17 mg Hg/g
sorbent, respectively.

Overall, sorbents that were most effective for Hg0 vapor suppression were brominated activated carbons,
mackinawite-based sorbents coated on vermiculite, and sulfur-modified granular activated carbon.
Elevated temperatures and moisture conditions did not result in significant increases in Hg 0 headspace
concentrations, and the materials also demonstrated high sorption capacities for the sorption of liquid Hg 0.

 APPENDIX A. ELECTRON MICROSCOPY

Scanning Electron Microscopy/Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (SEM/EDS) is a powerful,
combined analytical technique that uses an electron beam to create high-resolution images of the sample
surface while simultaneously identifying and mapping its elemental composition. The technique offers a
comprehensive view of microstructure and spatial correlations between specific elements and their
relative concentrations. A Hitachi S4800 scanning electron microscope equipped with energy-dispersive
spectroscopy was used to examine sorbent morphology. Sorbent samples suitable for SEM/EDS were
prepared by drying the samples in a vacuum oven at room temperature to remove residual moisture and
volatile compounds. The samples were then affixed to the SEM stage with conductive carbon tape and
inserted into the SEM chamber for analysis.

Accurate elemental identification via Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) relies on the energy-
resolved detection of characteristic X-ray photons emitted, which are classified as K, L, or M series based
on the specific electron shell transitions involved. Typically, K-series peaks represent high-energy
transitions to the innermost electron shell, while L-series peaks correspond to lower-energy transitions. In
complex samples, the low-energy L-peak of a heavier element may overlap with the high-energy K-peak
of a lighter element resulting peaks overlap. Under these circumstances, relying on a single spectral line
can lead to misinterpretation. Thus, validating the presence of an element requires cross-referencing of
secondary, higher-energy K-peaks to resolve overlapping signals unambiguously.

Figure 17 displays the SEM/EDS elemental maps of FerroBlack H40. Figure 17a, b, and c show the dried
compound at three magnification levels (×300, ×1000 and ×3000), revealing both amorphous and
crystalline features. EDS confirms the presence of Fe, S, O, Na, and Cl as shown in Figure 17.d. The
cubic crystalline structure was identified as NaCl based on the analysis of several EDS elemental maps.

Figure 18 shows the SEM and EDS elemental distribution maps of the dried FerroBlack Fe+. Figure 18.a,
b, and c illustrate the compound in different magnifications. Similar to the elemental constituents in
FerroBlack H40, EDS also confirms the presence of Fe, S, O, Na, and Cl in FerroBlack Fe+. Iron and S
follow a strong spatial correlation, which is consistent with the presence of FeS phases. Oxygen appears
to follow the distribution pattern of both Fe and S, which may suggest the formation of oxidation products
or oxide derivatives of these elements. However, further investigation is required to confirm this. Na and
Cl also show a similar spatial correlation.

Figure 19 displays the SEM/EDS elemental distribution maps of MercLok™ P-640. The SEM analysis
reveals that particle sizes range from 100 nm to approximately 50 µm, and considerable variation in
shapes and morphologies was observed; some particles are granules, others are plates, layered, or exhibit
random shapes, and some contain pores. The EDS spectrum reveals the presence of C and Br, as well as
traces of Si and O. The Br L peak (1.48 keV) and the Al K peak (1.486 keV) have very similar energy
levels, resulting in overlapping and indistinguishable signals in EDS analysis. However, the Br K peak at
11.922 keV provides additional confirmation for the presence of bromine.

Figure 20 illustrates the SEM/ EDS elemental distribution maps of a MERSORB pellet as received from
the manufacturer. Figure 20.a, b, and c show the surface of MERSORB pellets in several magnifications.
MERSORB EDS spectra appear complex with several trace elements; therefore, for simplicity, only the
C, S, and O elemental distribution maps are presented. As illustrated in Figure 20d, carbon and sulfur
showed a similar distribution pattern on the pellet surface.

Figure 21 shows the SEM/ EDS elemental distribution maps of a MERSORB pellet after Hg 0 exposure at
60°C. Interestingly, in addition to the C, O, and S, the presence of Hg was confirmed as seen in Figure
21.c. Figure 22.a shows a comparison of EDS spectra of MERSORB samples at 19.4ºC and the
