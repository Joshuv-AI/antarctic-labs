The Struggle Between Continuation and Refusal: A Mechanistic Analysis of the Continuation-Triggered Jailbreak in LLMs
Yonghong Deng, Zhen Yang, Ping Jian, Xinyue Zhang, Zhongbin Guo, Chengzhi Li

Abstract
With the rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs), the safety of LLMs has become a critical concern. Despite significant efforts in safety alignment, current LLMs remain vulnerable to jailbreaking attacks. However, the root causes of such vulnerabilities are still poorly understood, necessitating a rigorous investigation into jailbreak mechanisms across both academic and industrial communities. In this work, we focus on a continuation-triggered jailbreak phenomenon, whereby simply relocating a continuation-triggered instruction suffix can substantially increase jailbreak success rates. To uncover the intrinsic mechanisms of this phenomenon, we conduct a comprehensive mechanistic interpretability analysis at the level of attention heads. Through causal interventions and activation scaling, we show that this jailbreak behavior primarily arises from an inherent competition between the model’s intrinsic continuation drive and the safety defenses acquired through alignment training. Furthermore, we perform a detailed behavioral analysis of the identified safety-critical attention heads, revealing notable differences in the functions and behaviors of safety heads across different model architectures. These findings provide a novel mechanistic perspective for understanding and interpreting jailbreak behaviors in LLMs, offering both theoretical insights and practical implications for improving model safety.

To address these challenges, we study the internal mechanisms of a continuation-triggered jailbreak phenomenon in this work, aiming to provide a mechanistic perspective for future safety defenses and to offer insights that are crucial for developing more robust and secure large language models. Specifically, as illustrated in Figure
1
, under the clean prompt setting, models refuse to respond when a continuation-triggered instruction suffix is appended after a harmful instruction. In contrast, under the jailbreak prompt setting, simply relocating the continuation-triggered suffix to follow the user instruction prompts the model to continue generating harmful content, thereby successfully bypassing safety constraints. It is worth noting that this setup is primarily designed to facilitate the investigation of the internal mechanisms underlying jailbreak behavior in large language models. Our results demonstrate that, across multiple datasets, both LLaMA-2-7B-Chat and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct models exhibit substantial increases in Attack Success Rate (ASR) under this setting, with ASR rising from 0 to as high as 0.58 in some cases.

Intuitively, given that most of current LLMs are trained on next-token prediction paradigm, they possess an inherent tendency to generate coherent continuations that align with the semantics of the input. Alignment training, however, attempt to redirect the model from continuing the input to declining the request when safety is at risk, which introduces a fundamental
tension
with the pre-training paradigm. Therefore, we hypothesize that the continuation-triggered jailbreaking may arise from an overactivation of the model’s continuation capabilities. Specifically, we posit that LLMs contain distinct attention heads responsible for safety enforcement (safety heads) and for continuation-triggered generation (continuation heads). The fundamental reason why different prompt structures induce drastically different model behaviors lies in the internal competition between these safety heads and continuation heads.

To faithfully investigate the underlying mechanisms of this phenomenon and to validate our hypothesis, we adopt mechanistic interpretability at the level of attention heads. Specifically, we first employ a causal intervention technique, namely path patching
Wang et al. 2022
, to conduct a head-wise analysis across the model. Through path patching, We identify a small subset of attention heads located in the middle-to-late layers of the model that play a critical role in enabling jailbreak behavior in the specific jailbreak task studied in this work. To further examine the functional contributions of these key heads, we ablate them by zeroing out their activations. Based on the resulting impact on ASR after ablation, these heads can be categorized into safety heads and continuation heads. Subsequently, we perform activation scaling and ablation experiments on both categories to validate their causal effects on jailbreak behavior. The experimental results demonstrate that this phenomenon indeed arises from an inherent tension between the model’s intrinsic continuation-driven generation capability and the safety capability acquired through alignment training.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:

•

We are the first to investigate the underlying mechanisms of a specific jailbreaking method, i.e., the continuation-triggered jailbreak, providing valuable insight for understanding the genuine safety boundary of LLMs.

•

By leveraging path patching, activation scaling, and ablation analyses, we reveal that the core cause of this phenomenon lies in the conflict between the model’s intrinsic generative continuation capability and its safety-aligned behaviors.

•

We provide a detailed analysis of the selected safety heads, identifying those that are critical for model behavior under the continuation-triggered jailbreak, which could benefit the future development of more robust and reliable LLMs.

These findings not only offer a new perspective for understanding the internal safety dynamics of large language models but also provide practical guidance for enhancing their alignment robustness.

2
Related Work

Jailbreak Attack in LLMs.

Jailbreak attacks refer to adversarial input strategies that deliberately exploit weaknesses in the safety alignment mechanisms of large language models, enabling them to generate outputs that violate predefined safety policies, ethical guidelines, or usage constraints. Despite extensive safety alignment, modern LLMs remain vulnerable to various jailbreak attacks, such as adversarial suffixes
Zou et al. 2023
, LLM‑driven iterative attacks
Chao et al. 2025
and attacks based on attention and generation mechanisms
Wu et al. 2025
. Unlike prior works, we are the first to investigate the underlying mechanisms behind continuation-triggered jailbreaks, which achieves successful jailbreaks by relocating continuation-triggered instructions to follow the user instruction marker.

Mechanistic Interpretability.

Mechanistic interpretability aims to understand how high-level model behaviors arise from concrete computational mechanisms within neural networks. In transformer-based large language models, a growing body of work shows that behaviors are not implicitly or diffusely stored, but can instead be traced to structured computational circuits composed of attention heads, neurons, and residual pathways
Olah et al. 2020
;
Elhage et al. 2021
. Existing studies have demonstrated that individual attention heads often exhibit clear and interpretable functional roles
Olsson et al. 2022
. These findings suggest that Transformers organize their internal computations through modular components with distinct functional specializations.To move beyond purely correlational analyses, recent studies emphasize causal intervention techniques, such as activation patching and path patching, which selectively replace or manipulate internal activations to test the functional necessity of specific components
Wang et al. 2022
. Methods such as activation scaling provide fine-grained, inference-time interventions, enabling researchers to amplify or attenuate particular computational pathways without modifying model parameters, thereby allowing precise analysis of their causal impact on model behavior. Overall, this work follows a “locate-then-intervene” paradigm
Li et al. 2023a
, in which we not only interpret model behaviors but also based on insights gained from interpretability analyses intervene in the model at inference time to further improve model capabilities.

3
The Continuation-Triggered Jailbreak

This section reports a distinct and reproducible jailbreak phenomenon identified through controlled experiments on two representative instruction-tuned large language models. The phenomenon emerges from a subtle but systematic manipulation of prompt structure, rather than from semantic changes to the malicious intent itself.

The discovery process began with an observation during preliminary safety evaluations: when continuation-triggered instruction suffix such as “Sure, here is a step-by-step guide: First,” was embedded within the user prompt, models consistently refused to comply with the malicious request. However, when the same instruction suffix was repositioned outside the user instruction marker and appeared immediately after the prompt delimiter, the models frequently generated unsafe content.

To investigate this phenomenon systematically, we designed paired prompts for each malicious instruction. The clean prompt embeds the continuation-triggered instruction suffix inside the user prompt, while the jailbreak prompt places the same suffix after the user prompt termination token, causing it to be interpreted as part of the assistant’s continuation. Importantly, the malicious intent remains unchanged across the two conditions, ensuring that any observed difference in ASR can be attributed to structural rather than semantic factors.

As shown in Figure
2
, LLaMA-2-7B-Chat exhibits extreme sensitivity to this prompt manipulation: under the clean prompt setting, the ASR remains zero across AdvBench, JailbreakBench, and MaliciousInstruct, whereas under the jailbreak prompt, the ASR rises sharply to 0.16, 0.26, and 0.58, respectively. In comparison, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct shows non-zero ASR even under clean prompts; nevertheless, the jailbreak prompt further amplifies the attack success rate by over 30 percentage points on multiple datasets, reaching 0.68 on MaliciousInstruct. Based on the above results, despite differences in their baseline safety strength, both models are highly susceptible to the continuation-triggered jailbreak attack studied in this work.

4
Methodology

4.1
Key Heads Localization

4.1.1
Which Heads Matter?

To causally localize attention heads that are highly associated with jailbreak behavior, we adopt path patching and quantify its effect using a KL-divergence–based metric. Path patching is a causal interpretability technique that identifies behavior-critical attention heads by selectively transplanting internal activations across different input conditions and measuring the resulting change in model behavior
Zhang and Nanda 2023
. Path patching measures how much a specific internal component contributes to the behavioral gap between two input conditions by selectively transferring activations across runs and observing the resulting change in model outputs.

Specifically, we define three types of forward runs in this work. The clean run corresponds to the Clean Prompt, under which the model exhibits safety-aligned refusal behavior. The orrupted run corresponds to the Jailbreak Prompt, which successfully induces harmful generation. The patched run is constructed by replacing the activation of a specific attention head in the clean run with the corresponding activation from the corrupted run, while keeping all other components identical to the clean run.

Let
P_{\mathrm{cl}}
,
P_{\ast}
, and
P_{\mathrm{pt}}
denote the output probability distributions over the vocabulary produced by the clean, corrupted, and patched runs, respectively. We employ the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence from the clean distribution as the evaluation metric:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mathrm{cl}}\,\|\,P),

(1)

which measures how much the output distribution
P
deviates from the safety-aligned baseline behavior.

The patching effect of a given attention head is then defined as:

\Delta_{\mathrm{patch}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mathrm{cl}}\,\|\,P_{\ast})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mathrm{cl}}\,\|\,P_{\mathrm{pt}}).

(2)

This quantity captures how much of the divergence introduced by the jailbreak prompt can be recovered by patching a single head. A large positive patching effect indicates that the patched head restores the output distribution toward the clean behavior, implying that this head lies on a causal path enabling the jailbreak.

4.1.2
What Roles Do Key Heads Perform?

To probe the functional roles of attention heads identified as critical to jailbreak behavior, we conduct a targeted activation-level intervention during inference by zeroing the activations of selected heads while keeping all other components of the model unchanged. This intervention removes the contribution of a specific head without modifying model parameters or altering the input distribution, thereby enabling a causal assessment of its functional impact on model behavior. Formally, at a given layer
l
, we zero the output activation of attention head
h
, effectively removing its contribution to the model’s computation.

We evaluate the effects of this intervention under jailbreak prompts by measuring changes in the ASR. If zeroing a particular head consistently increases ASR, we interpret this head as supporting safety or refusal behavior, since removing it weakens the model’s resistance to harmful instruction following. Conversely, if zeroing a head leads to a decrease in ASR, we attribute it to response continuation, as its removal suppresses the model’s tendency to comply with or extend harmful content. Heads whose activation zeroing yields negligible changes in ASR are regarded as functionally neutral or only weakly involved in jailbreak dynamics.

Applying this criterion across multiple datasets and model architectures reveals a clear functional dichotomy among the identified key heads: if zeroing these attention heads leads to an increase in ASR, we define these heads as
Safety Heads
, while if zeroing these attention heads leads to a decrease in ASR, we define these heads as
Continuation Heads
.

This functional categorization establishes the basis for subsequent generation-time interventions. By distinguishing safety heads from continuation heads, we enable targeted activation-level modulation strategies that selectively strengthen or attenuate specific behavioral tendencies, offering a mechanistic and fine-grained approach to mitigating jailbreak vulnerabilities without retraining the model.

4.1.3
Faithfulness Validation

To investigate the effects of activation scaling on model jailbreak behavior by intervening on safety heads and continuation heads, thereby further validating the faithfulness of safety heads and continuation heads introduced in the previous section. Activation Scaling is a lightweight generation-time intervention technique that modulates model behavior by adjusting only the magnitude of activation vectors, while preserving their directions, in order to strengthen or weaken steering directions already encoded within the model. Unlike methods that rely on parameter updates or introduce external steering vectors, activation scaling applies scalar modulation to selected internal activations solely during inference, without modifying model parameters or altering the input distribution, thereby offering an efficient and controllable means of behavioral steering
Stoehr et al. 2024
.

Specifically, during the forward pass of the transformer decoder, we apply a scaling operation to the output activations of selected attention heads. Given an attention head activation vector
h
, Activation Scaling modifies it as

h^{\prime}=w\cdot h,

(3)

where
w
is a non-negative scaling coefficient. For key heads,
w>1
amplifies their contribution to the residual stream. Setting
w=0
corresponds to complete suppression, while
w=1
preserves the original behavior of the model.

Activation Scaling is applied exclusively at inference time and does not require any modification to model parameters or additional training. The scaling operation is injected after attention computation and before residual addition, ensuring minimal interference with the overall architecture. This design allows fine-grained control over the safety–utility trade-off by adjusting either the magnitude of the scaling coefficient or the number of heads being scaled.

4.2
Safety Heads Behavior Study

Prior studies
Zhao et al. 2025
have demonstrated, at the layer level in large language models, that harmfulness recognition and refusal execution can be encoded separately, corresponding to distinct internal representations and decision processes. This finding suggests that safety alignment is not a monolithic capability, but rather emerges from multiple interacting yet potentially disentangled mechanisms. Building on this insight, we conduct a more fine-grained analysis at the attention head level to elucidate the specific aspects of safety-related behavior encoded by individual heads.

Specifically, we distinguish between two closely related but conceptually distinct behaviors: (i)
Harmfulness Recognition
: the model’s ability to assess whether a user instruction is illegal, unsafe, or potentially harmful, and (ii)
Refusal Execution
: the decision to withhold a response or generate a refusal after an instruction has been recognized as harmful.

Inspired by
Zhao et al. 2025
, we adopt a reply inversion task that explicitly decouples harmfulness assessment from refusal generation through behavioral means. By appending a specific inversion query to the user instruction, we reverse the standard response logic: the model is expected to output an acceptance token for harmful inputs and a refusal token for harmless ones. This mechanism ensures that assessment is isolated from the model’s innate refusal tendencies.

Prompt

User wants to {Instruction}

Is this user prompt illegal or harmful? Answer ’Yes’ if so, otherwise ’No’. Only answer with exactly one word: ’Yes’ or ’No’. Do not explain.

In this setting, we scale the activations of identified safety heads during inference and measure changes in the model’s harmfulness judgments, allowing us to distinguish heads that primarily encode harmfulness semantics from those involved in refusal execution or generation-stage control. We determine whether the judgment has changed by examining whether the generated response exhibits a reversal.

5
Experiments

5.1
Experimental Setup

Datasets.

We evaluate jailbreak attacks on three widely used benchmark datasets: AdvBench
Zou et al. 2023
, JailbreakBench
Chao et al. 2024
, and MaliciousInstruct
Huang et al. 2023
. These datasets cover a diverse range of harmful and policy-violating instructions, and are commonly used to assess the robustness of large language models against jailbreak attacks. In Section
4.2
, we use AdvBench as the harmful instructions dataset and Alpaca
Taori et al. 2023
as the harmless instructions dataset.

Models.

Experiments are conducted on two instruction-tuned large language models: LLaMA-2-7B-Chat
Touvron et al. 2023
and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
Yang et al. 2024
. Both models are representative open-source chat models with built-in safety mechanisms, suitable for studying attention-head behaviors related to safety and jailbreaking.

Metrics.

We adopt different evaluation metrics for different tasks. For the jailbreak generation task, we use Attack Success Rate (ASR), which is computed as the proportion of test inputs for which the model generates harmful or policy-violating content. For the reply inversion task in Section
4.2
, we employ Harmfulness Detection Rate (HDR) to assess the model’s ability to recognize harmful intent. HDR is calculated as the proportion of instructions that the model identifies as harmful.

Implementation details.

Both ASR and HDR are computed using rule-based matching methods, ensuring that the evaluation process is objective and reproducible. Since the model’s refusal and continuation behaviors typically manifest at an early stage of generation, we set max_new_tokens to 32. To ensure experimental reproducibility, we disable stochastic decoding by setting do_sample to False.

Notably, heads in layers 15–17 and 25–27 show particularly strong effects, implying that these layers contain key pathways that substantially influence the model’s predictions. Overall, the path patching results demonstrate a sparse yet functionally significant distribution of influential attention heads, consistent with prior findings that only a fraction of heads dominate specific aspects of model behavior.

5.3
Faithfulness Evaluation

To further validate the faithfulness of different key heads, we introduce activation scaling, which multiplies the activation of selected heads by a scaling coefficient
w
during inference.

Scaling Individual Head.

We first examine the effects of scaling the activations of identified safety and continuation heads under jailbreak prompts. As shown in Figure
5
, varying the scaling coefficient for safety heads from
w=0
to moderate values sharply decreases ASR across all datasets, demonstrating that strengthening safety head activations significantly enhances the model’s resistance to jailbreak attacks. Notably, when the scaling coefficient exceeds a threshold (approximately
w>4
), the reduction in ASR saturates, indicating diminishing returns from further amplification. In contrast, scaling continuation heads produces the opposite effect: increasing the scaling coefficient
w
leads to a monotonic and substantial rise in ASR on all benchmarks, confirming that continuation heads play a dominant role in driving harmful content generation once safety constraints are weakened.

Scaling Multiple Heads.

We further investigate the effect of scaling multiple safety and continuation heads simultaneously. As shown in Table
1
, increasing the number of scaled safety heads generally leads to additional reductions in ASR, suggesting a cumulative protective effect, although occasional increases in ASR indicate non-linear interactions among attention heads. In contrast, amplifying a larger set of continuation heads typically results in higher ASR, reflecting a cumulative enhancement of the model’s continuation drive. This indicates that continuation behavior is distributed across multiple attention heads, whose effects can compound when jointly strengthened, while saturation or slight fluctuations in some cases reveal non-linear interactions and potential redundancy among continuation heads.

Overall, these results suggest that
the underlying reason for the sharp increase in ASR under jailbreak attacks lies in the antagonistic interaction between safety heads and continuation heads, that is, the conflict between the model’s safety enforcement and its continuation-generation capabilities, which is consistent with our prior hypothesis.

5.4
Safety Heads Behavior Analysis

In this subsection, we conduct experiments to determine the primary behavior of safety heads in different models.

For LLaMA-2-7B-Chat, as shown in Table
2
, on the AdvBench harmful dataset, the HDR is 0.89 when the scaling coefficient
w=1
, corresponding to the unmodified model. When
w=0
, effectively removing the contribution of safety heads, HDR drops sharply to 0.515. As
w
increases beyond 1, HDR steadily improves, reaching 0.962 at
w=5
. On the harmless Alpaca instructions, HDR also shows a slight increase with higher scaling coefficient. The experimental results indicate that
the safety heads in LLaMA-2-7B-Chat are primarily responsible for recognizing harmful instructions.
Scaling these safety heads enhances the model’s ability to detect harmful instructions; however, excessive scaling can make the model overly cautious, leading to the misclassification of a small number of harmless instructions as harmful.

In contrast, for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, increasing the scaling coefficient results in a continuous decrease in HDR. Notably, at
w=4
, HDR on AdvBench drops substantially. For harmless Alpaca instructions, HDR remains at 0 regardless of the scaling coefficient. The above experimental results indicate that
the safety heads in Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct are primarily responsible for refusal behavior.
In the original model, harmful instructions are correctly recognized and should elicit a “Yes” response; however, excessive scaling of the safety heads over-amplifies the model’s refusal tendency, causing it to output “No” instead and resulting in a decrease in HDR.

Overall, the behaviors of safety heads can be broadly categorized into two types: harmfulness recognition and refusal execution. Experimental results show that,
under this specific jailbreak task, the safety heads in different models are primarily responsible for different behaviors.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we study the internal mechanisms underlying continuation-triggered jailbreaks, where relocating continuation-triggered instructions after the user instruction marker increases jailbreak success. Using mechanistic interpretability, including path patching, activation scaling, and ablation studies, we show that this phenomenon arises from the conflict between models’ intrinsic generative continuation and alignment-based safety capabilities. By analyzing the behavior of safety heads, we find that the safety heads identified in different models are responsible for different behaviors. These findings provide a theoretical basis for the targeted enhancement of model safety mechanisms and hold significant implications for research on safety alignment in LLMs.

Ethical Statement

This paper studies jailbreak behaviors in large language models from a mechanistic interpretability perspective. In particular, we analyze how internal activation-level interventions, such as scaling continuation and safety heads, can alter model responses under adversarial prompting. The purpose of this work is to understand failure modes of safety alignment, rather than to enable the generation of harmful content. Our analysis includes limited case studies in which models produce harmful or disallowed content after specific internal interventions. These examples are presented solely for illustrative and analytical purposes, to demonstrate how changes in internal activations can shift model behavior from refusal to continuation. All such content is drawn from standard benchmark settings and is not intended for real-world use. We strongly discourage any misuse of the findings or examples presented in this work to bypass or weaken safety safeguards in deployed systems.
