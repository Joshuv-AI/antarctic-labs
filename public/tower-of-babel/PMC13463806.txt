Green and efficient ultrasound-assisted extraction of Banqiao Codonopsis Radix polysaccharides: Process optimization, extraction mechanism, and in vivo anti-ulcerative colitis efficacy with mechanistic insight
Authors: Yiheng Jiang, Fei Wang, Siyu Zhong, Kexin Xue, Rui Deng, Hui Gao, Gangqiang Dong, Jingjing Zhang, Chong Yuan, Yifei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Banqiao codonopsis radix polysaccharides, Ultrasound-assisted extraction, Extraction mechanism, Ulcerative colitis, Multi-omics

Introduction

Banqiao Codonopsis Radix (BCR), a geo-authentic cultivar of Codonopsis tangshen Oliv. predominantly cultivated in Enshi, Hubei Province, China, has been traditionally used for invigorating the spleen, replenishing the lung, nourishing blood, and is a typical medicine–food homologous plant [1]. Accumulating pharmacological evidence demonstrates that polysaccharides (BCRPs) isolated from the dried roots of BCR possess potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities, rendering them promising candidates for functional food ingredients and pharmaceutical intermediates [2], [3]. Currently, the cultivation area of BCR in the Enshi exceeds 10,000 mu (approximately 667 ha), with a comprehensive output value surpassing 120 million RMB, indicating broad industrial development prospects. However, the current mainstream extraction method for BCRPs remains hot water (HW) extraction, which has inherent limitations including prolonged extraction duration (2–4 h), elevated extraction temperatures (90–100 °C), low extraction yields (15–20%), and excessive energy consumption [4]. These drawbacks make HW extraction unsuitable for large-scale green production. More critically, prolonged high-temperature treatment inevitably induces glycosidic bond cleavage and degradation of heat-sensitive bioactive groups in many plant polysaccharides, which not only reduces polysaccharide yield but also significantly impairs their biological activities [5], [6]. The aforementioned drawbacks of HW extraction have become the core technical bottleneck restricting the high-value utilization of BCRPs.

Ultrasound-assisted (UA) extraction, as a representative non-thermal process intensification technology, offers an ideal solution to the core limitations of the aforementioned conventional HW extraction. Its most prominent advantages lie in enhancing extraction efficiency while reducing energy consumption. Compared with traditional HW extraction, reported UA extraction processes can shorten polysaccharide extraction time by more than 40%, reduce energy consumption by more than 60% [7]. Although UA extraction technology has been widely applied to extract various plant polysaccharides from Choerospondias axillaris peels [8], and Ginseng leaves [9], no study to date has systematically applied this technique to the green preparation of BCRPs. Elucidating the mechanism by which UA extraction improves polysaccharide yield is a critical step toward the rapid optimization of process parameters and therefore merits in-depth investigation. It is currently widely accepted that transient cavitation effects generated by ultrasound propagating in a liquid medium can create localized extreme microenvironments with temperatures of approximately 5,000 K and pressures of approximately 100 MPa, accompanied by microjets with velocities of up to 100 m/s and intense turbulence [10]. These unique physicochemical effects generate powerful mechanical shear forces and shock waves that directly disrupt plant cell walls and membrane structures, thereby promoting the rapid release of intracellular polysaccharides [7]. Meanwhile, ultrasound-induced medium vibration can significantly enhance mass transfer at the solid–liquid interface, reduce boundary layer resistance, and increase the dissolution rate of polysaccharides [11]. Nevertheless, the specific mechanism by which UA extraction interacts with BCR powder to enhance polysaccharide extraction yield has not been reported.

In addition to high efficiency and low energy consumption, UA extraction technology can also modify the structural features, physicochemical properties, and biological activities of polysaccharides. This may be attributed to the selective effects of ultrasound on the molecular weight distribution and chain conformation of polysaccharides, a phenomenon confirmed in polysaccharides from Lentinus edodes [12], Flammulina velutipes [13], and Ganoderma lucidum [14]. BCRPs from BCR have been demonstrated to possess favorable anti-ulcerative colitis (anti-UC) effects. However, whether anti-UC efficacy differs between UA extraction derived BCRPs (UBCRPs) and HW extraction-derived BCRPs (HBCRPs) remains to be further elucidated, as this will determine the suitability of UA extraction for BCRPs preparation. UC is a chronic idiopathic inflammatory bowel disease with increasing global prevalence, and currently available clinical drugs are limited by significant adverse effects and high recurrence rates, creating an urgent need for novel therapeutic agents [15]. Gut microbiota dysbiosis, disordered serum corticosterone metabolism and aberrant activation of the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway play pivotal roles in UC pathogenesis and progression, and have been recognized as important therapeutic targets for novel anti-UC drug screening [[16], [17], [18]]. Nevertheless, whether UBCRPs can exert anti-UC effects through modulating these pathways remains unclear.

Accordingly, this study aimed to establish an efficient and green UA extraction process that maximally preserves the bioactivity of BCRPs. First, single-factor experiments combined with response surface methodology (RSM) were used to optimize key process parameters including ultrasonic power, extraction time, and liquid-to-solid ratio to maximize BCRPs yield. Subsequently, the optimized UA extraction process and conventional HW extraction process will be comprehensively and quantitatively compared in terms of extraction efficiency, energy consumption, and carbon emissions to substantiate the green extraction advantages of UA extraction. On this basis, scanning electron microscopy will be used to observe microstructural changes in BCR materials before and after extraction, thereby revealing the mechanism by which UA extraction enhances BCRPs yield at the cellular level. Furthermore, a combination of physicochemical and structural characterization techniques was employed to analyze the chemical composition, molecular weight distribution, crystalline structure, and thermal properties of UBCRPs and HBCRPs, to clarify the influence of ultrasound treatment on BCRPs structural characteristics. Finally, the therapeutic effects of UBCRPs and HBCRPs on dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) induced UC mice were comparatively evaluated, and multi-omics and molecular biology experiments were conducted to elucidate the anti-UC mechanism of UBCRPs.

Overall, this study aims to develop a green extraction process for BCRPs characterized by high efficiency and low energy consumption, which will accelerate its industrial implementation and facilitate further development and utilization.

Materials and methods

Materials

Banqiao Codonopsis Radix, the dried root of Codonopsis tangshen Oliv. was collected from Banqiao Town, Enshi, Hubei Province, China in October 2024. Wushan, Wuxi, and Fengjie Codonopsis Radix samples were collected from Wushan County, Wuxi County, and Fengjie County, respectively, all in Chongqing, China, in October 2024. Jianshi Codonopsis Radix was collected from Jianshi County, Hubei Province, China, during the same period. All plant materials were taxonomically authenticated as Codonopsis tangshen Oliv. by Prof. Yifei Liu from the College of Pharmacy, Hubei University of Chinese Medicine, Wuhan, China. Vancomycin, ampicillin sodium, metronidazole, and neomycin sulfate were purchased from MCE Bioscience Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). ELISA kits for the inflammatory cytokines TNF–α, IL–1β, IL–6, and IL–10 were supplied by Wuhan Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

UA extraction optimization of the UBCRPs

The ultrasonic-assisted water extraction and ethanol precipitation of polysaccharides from BCR was optimized through a single-factor investigation followed by RSM. All extractions were performed using an ultrasonic cleaner (KQ-500DE, Kunshan Ultrasonic Instruments Co., Ltd., China). In the single-factor experiments, the effects of ultrasonic power (250, 300, 350, 400, and 450 W), extraction time (10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 min), liquid-to-solid ratio (10:1, 20:1, 30:1, 40:1, and 50:1 mL/g), and final ethanol concentration in the precipitation step (50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, and 90%, v/v) on the polysaccharide yield were evaluated. When each factor was examined, the other three factors were held constant at their levels (400 W, 30 min, 10:1 mL/g, and 80% ethanol, respectively).

Following the single-factor experiments, a three-level factorial design was employed to further optimize the significant parameters. RSM was subsequently implemented via Design-Expert software (version 13.0, Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) using a Box-Behnken design. A quadratic regression model was built with the polysaccharide extraction yield as the response variable, and ultrasonic power (350, 400, and 450 W), liquid-to-solid ratio (20:1, 30:1, and 40:1 mL/g), extraction time (20, 30, and 40 min), and final ethanol concentration (70%, 80%, and 90%, v/v) set as the independent variables.

Extraction of HBCRPs

1g of BCR powder was dispersed in deionized water (1:10, w/v), and the mixture was then heated at 80 °C for 1 h, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was collected, while the residue was re-extracted under identical conditions. The two supernatants were combined, and 95% ethanol was added to reach a final ethanol concentration of 80% (v/v). After standing at 4 °C overnight, the resulting precipitate was collected by centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 15 min) and redissolved in deionized water. Protein impurities were removed by three cycles of Sevag treatment (chloroform: n-butanol, 4:1, v/v). Finally, HBCRPs was obtained after low-temperature drying

Comparison of polysaccharide yields of C. tangshen from different geographical origins

The crude polysaccharides (HBCRPs) obtained from the five origins (Banqiao, Wushan, Wuxi, Fengjie, and Jianshi) following the procedure described in Section 2.2 were compared for their extraction yields. The yield was expressed as a percentage and calculated using the following equation: Polysaccharide extraction yield (\%) = (mass of extracted crude polysaccharide / dry weight of the original sample) × 100

Energy consumption and environmental impact comparison between HW and UA extraction

The yields, energy consumption, and environmental impacts of HW extraction and UA extraction were compared. HW extraction was conducted using a digital constant-temperature water bath (heating power: 1.2 kW; temperature range: ambient to 99.9 °C; HH-4S, Shanghai Yiyou Instrument Co., Ltd., China). The extraction was performed at a temperature of 80 °C with a liquid-to-solid ratio of 10 mL/g for a duration of 120 min. UA extraction was carried out under the optimal conditions determined in Section 2.4, namely, an ultrasonic power of 400 W, a liquid-to-solid ratio of 29 mL/g, an extraction time of 34 min, and a final ethanol concentration of 87% (v/v) in the precipitation step. The subsequent steps were identical for both methods, following the procedure described in Section 2.2. Productivity was expressed as the yield of UBCRPs and HBCRPs produced per hour. Energy consumption was evaluated based on total energy and specific energy, where total energy (kJ) = power (kW) × treatment time (s), and specificenergykJ/kg=totalenergykJ÷massofBCRPsextractedfrom1kgofdriedBanqiaoCodonopsisRadix [19]. Furthermore, the environmental impact of energy consumption was quantified in terms of carbon dioxide emissions associated with the extraction process, assuming an emission factor of 800 g CO2 per kWh of electrical energy consumed [20].

Characterization of extraction residues from UA and HW extraction

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

For FT-IR spectroscopy analysis, 20 mg of each residual powder obtained from UA extraction and HW extraction was separately ground and thoroughly mixed with potassium bromide (KBr) powder. The mixture was then pressed into a clear pellet. Infrared spectra were then acquired with a Nicolet iS50 FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) in the range of 4000 to 400 cm−1.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

XRD patterns of the residual powders obtained after UA extraction and HW extraction were recorded at room temperature using an X-ray diffractometer (SmartLab SE, Rigaku, Japan) with a step size of 0.02°.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric analysis coupled with differential scanning calorimetry (TGA/DSC) was performed using a TGA/DSC 3 + system (Mettler Toledo, Greifensee, Switzerland). The samples were heated from 25 °C to 900 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under an argon flow of 20 mL/min.

Particle size analysis

The particle size of the residues remaining after UA and HW extraction was measured with a Mastersizer 3000 laser diffraction analyzer (Malvern Instruments, England). The UA extraction and HW extraction powders were each dispersed in deionized water to form suspensions, and measurements were performed according to the instrument's standard operating procedure.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

The microstructural morphology of the residual powders obtained after UA extraction and HW extraction were examined using SEM. An accelerating voltage of 15 kV was applied, and surface morphological characteristics were captured at various magnifications.

Characterization of UBCRPs and HBCRPs

The analytical procedures for FTIR, TGA, XRD, and SEM were performed following the protocols described in Section 2.6. Other methods are as follows.

Ultraviolet absorption spectroscopy analysis

UBCRPs and HBCRPs were separately dissolved in deionized water to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL, and their UV spectra were recorded from 200 to 800 nm with a UV–Vis spectrophotometer.

Monosaccharide composition analysis

Monosaccharide composition of UBCRPs and HBCRPs were determined by high–performance anion–exchange chromatography with pulsed amperometric detection (HPAEC–PAD). Thirteen monosaccharide standards (fucose, rhamnose, arabinose, galactose, glucose, xylose, mannose, fructose, ribose, galacturonic acid, glucuronic acid, mannuronic acid, guluronic acid; Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were individually dissolved in ultrapure water to obtain stock solutions (1.0 mg/mL). A mixed working standard solution was prepared by pooling appropriate volumes of each stock solution and serially diluting to five concentration levels. Polysaccharide samples (UBCRPs and HBCRPs, 5 mg each) were hydrolyzed with 2 mol/L trifluoroacetic acid at 121 °C for 2 h in sealed ampoules. After hydrolysis, the residual acid was removed by repeated evaporation with methanol under a nitrogen stream. The dried hydrolysates were reconstituted in 2 mL of ultrapure water and filtered (0.22 μm). Both the mixed standard solutions and the sample hydrolysates were derivatized under identical conditions and analyzed on a Thermo ICS 5000 + ion chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with an electrochemical detector. Determination of molecular weight

Molecular weights of UBCRPs and HBCRPs were determined using SEC-MALLS-RI (size exclusion chromatography coupled with multi-angle laser light scattering and refractive index detection). Samples (5 mg each of UBCRPs and HBCRPs) were dissolved in 0.1 M NaNO3 containing 0.02% NaN3 to give 1 mg/mL, then filtered (0.45 µm) prior to injection. The SEC-MALLS-RI setup comprised a Thermo U3000 HPLC system coupled to an Optilab T–rEX refractive index detector and a DAWN HELEOS II multi-angle laser light scattering detector (both from Wyatt Technology, Santa Barbara, CA, USA). Two serially connected Shodex OH–pak columns (SB–805 and SB–803, 300 × 8 mm; Showa Denko K.K., Tokyo, Japan) were used for separation. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1 M NaNO3 and 0.02% NaN3, delivered at 0.6 mL/min with the column maintained at 45 °C. The injection volume was 100 µL, and isocratic elution was continued for 75 min.

Zeta potential

The zeta potentials of the UBCRPs and HBCRPs stock solutions were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments, England).

Evaluation of the in vivo anti-UC effect of UBCRPs and HBCRPs

Animal grouping and drug administration

Male BALB/c mice (SPF, 6–8 weeks old, 20–22 g) were obtained from the Animal Center of China Three Gorges University (Experimental Animal Use License No.: SYXK (E) 2022–0012). All animal procedures adhered to ethical guidelines and were approved by Hubei University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No.: HUCMS 00307357, Hubei, China). Following a one-week acclimatization period, 56 specific pathogen-free (SPF) male BALB/c mice were randomly and equally divided into seven groups (n = 8/per group): Control group (gavaged with an equivalent volume of normal saline), DSS group (3.5% DSS), HBCRPs low-dose group (DSS + HBCRPsL, 250 mg/kg), HBCRPs high-dose group (DSS + HBCRPsH, 1000 mg/kg), UBCRPs low-dose group (DSS + UBCRPsL, 250 mg/kg), UBCRPs high-dose group (DSS + UBCRPsH, 1,000 mg/kg), and Positive control group (DSS + mesalazine, 200 mg/kg). The doses of HBCRPs and UBCRPs were determined based on the C. tangshen dosage stipulated in the Pharmacopoeia of the People's Republic of China, the actual extraction yield of crude polysaccharides, and the human-to-mouse dose conversion.

During days 1–7 of the experimental period, mice in the DSS group received sterile drinking water only, while from days 8–14, they were provided with a 3.5% DSS solution ad libitum to induce the murine UC model. Treatment groups were gavaged daily with their respective doses from day 1 to day 14, following the same drinking water protocol as the DSS group. Bedding was changed every day, and body weight as well as general health status were monitored throughout this experiment. On day 15, serum, spleen, colon, and fecal samples were harvested under ethical guidelines, and then weighed, photographed, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Disease activity index (DAI) scoring

Body weight was recorded daily, and feces were examined for consistency and visible blood. Fecal occult blood was detected and scored using a commercial test kit (Shanghai Yunaye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Based on the body weight loss, fecal consistency, and fecal occult blood score, the Disease Activity Index (DAI) for each mouse was calculated as a composite score according to the criteria described in [21] and summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Histopathological assessment

Colonic tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin by standard procedures. Sections were cut at 5 µm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Alcian blue-periodic acid-Schiff (AB-PAS) for light microscopy. For MUC2 detection, the sections were incubated with a primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, washed three times with cold PBS, and then treated with a secondary antibody for 30 min at 37 °C. To visualize ZO-1 and Occludin, separate sections were labeled with the corresponding primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, rinsed with PBS, and incubated with fluorescence–conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After washing, nuclei were stained with DAPI mounting medium, and the slides were coverslipped with an anti–fade reagent. Histopathological images were captured using a digital slide scanner, and the acquired images were analyzed using ImageJ software (v1.8.0, USA).

Determination of inflammatory cytokines

Colon tissues were homogenized in cold PBS and centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatants were collected, and the levels of IL–6, TNF–α, IL–1β, and IL–10 were measured using commercial ELISA kits. All steps were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Sequencing and analysis of the 16S rRNA gene

Fecal DNA was extracted using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide method, and its integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR, purified, and then used to construct sequencing libraries with the TruSeq® DNA PCR–Free Sample Preparation Kit. High–throughput sequencing was carried out on the NovaSeq6000 platform. The raw reads were denoised into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), and alpha diversity, beta diversity, and taxonomic annotation were analyzed to evaluate changes in gut microbiota structure and composition.

Fecal microbiota transplantation experiment

After a one-week acclimatization period, 24 specific pathogen-free (SPF) male BALB/c mice were randomly and equally divided into three groups (n = 8/per group): a fecal microbiota transplantation control group (Control-FMT), a DSS-induced colitis model FMT group (DSS-FMT), and a UBCRPs high-dose FMT group (UBCRPsH-FMT). To establish a pseudo-germ-free model, all mice were gavaged daily with an antibiotic cocktail (ABX) solution for seven consecutive days. From day 8 to day 14, mice in each group were orally gavaged once daily with the corresponding donor fecal microbiota suspension. The fecal microbiota suspension was freshly prepared immediately before each administration by suspending fecal material (1 g feces per 10 mL pre-cooled sterile PBS), vortexing thoroughly, and centrifuging at low temperature; the resulting supernatant was collected and used as the donor inoculum for gavage [22], [23]. During this period, mice in the Control-FMT group were provided with sterile drinking water ad libitum, whereas mice in the DSS-FMT and UBCRPsH-FMT groups received a 3.5% DSS solution ad libitum to induce acute ulcerative colitis. After treatment, colon and serum samples were collected following animal ethics guidelines, weighed, photographed, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Detailed procedures for DAI scoring, histopathological evaluation, and inflammatory cytokine determination were performed as described in Section 2.8.

Validation of a pseudo-germ-free model established by antibiotic cocktail

After a one-week acclimatization period, 24 specific pathogen-free (SPF) male BALB/c mice were randomly and equally divided into three groups (n = 8/per group): an antibiotic-treated control group (Control-ABX), an antibiotic-treated DSS induced colitis DSS group (DSS-ABX), and an antibiotic-treated UBCRPs high-dose intervention group (UBCRPsH-ABX). To establish a pseudo-germ-free model, all mice were gavaged daily with an antibiotic cocktail (ABX) solution containing metronidazole (25 mg/mL), neomycin sulfate (25 mg/mL), ampicillin sodium (25 mg/mL), and vancomycin hydrochloride (12.5 mg/mL) for seven consecutive days [24], [25]. From day 8 to day 14, the Control-ABX group received sterile drinking water ad libitum, while the DSS-ABX and UBCRPsH-ABX groups were provided with a 3.5% DSS solution and libitum to induce colitis. Mice in the Control-ABX and DSS-ABX groups were orally administered an equivalent volume of normal saline, whereas the UBCRPsH-ABX group received the corresponding dose of UBCRPsH working solution. After treatment, colon and serum samples were collected following animal ethics guidelines, weighed, photographed, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Detailed procedures for DAI scoring, histopathological evaluation, and inflammatory cytokine determination were performed as described in Section 2.8.

Serum metabolomics analysis

Blood from the Control, DSS, and UBCRPsH groups was centrifuged, and 50 µL of serum was mixed with 300 µL of internal standard solution (20% acetonitrile/methanol, v/v). After vortexing and centrifugation, the supernatant was kept at −20 °C for 1 h, centrifuged again, and passed through a 0.22 µm membrane. Serum samples were analyzed using a QTRAP® LC–MS/MS system (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B), delivered at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The injection volume was 2 μL. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–2.0 min, 95%-80% A; 2.0–5.0 min, 80%-40% A; 5.0–6.0 min, 40%-1% A; 6.0–7.5 min, 1% A; 7.5–7.6 min, 1%-95% A; 7.6–10.0 min, 95% A. Mass spectrometric detection was conducted in both positive and negative ESI modes. The key MS parameters were set as follows: ion spray voltage, 5000 V in positive mode and −4000 V in negative mode; source temperature, 500 °C; ion source gas 1 (GS1), 50 psi; ion source gas 2 (GS2), 50 psi; curtain gas (CUR), 25 psi; mass scan range, m/z 25–1000; and collision energy, 30 V in positive mode and −30 V in negative mode. Data were acquired in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode and processed using Analyst 1.6.3 software (AB Sciex).

Transcriptomic analysis

Colonic RNA from the Control, Model, and UBCRPsH groups was isolated using the phenol-guanidine isothiocyanate method. The extracted RNA was dissolved in diethyl pyrocarbonate (DEPC)-treated water. RNA concentration was accurately quantified using a Qubit fluorometer, and RNA integrity was assessed by determining the RNA Quality Number (RQN) using a Qsep400 high-throughput bio-fragment analyzer to ensure that sample quality met the requirements for library construction.

Integrated multi-omics analysis

Correlation analyses between differential gut microbiota, metabolomic profiles, and transcriptomic data were performed using both Spearman's rank correlation and Pearson's product-moment correlation coefficients.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and graphical representation were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software (v8, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Normality and homogeneity of variance were checked before one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA). For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post–hoc test was used. For comparisons against a single model group, Dunnett's post-hoc test was used. Analysis and visualization of multi-omics raw data were conducted using the Metware Cloud platform (https://cloud.meta.cn). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. The graphical abstract was drawn using the BioGDP online website (https://biogdp.com/).

Results and discussion

RSM-based optimization results of UA extraction process for UBCRPs

RSM coupled with a Box-Behnken design (BBD) was used to optimize the UA extraction process of UBCRPs (Fig. 1A). Single factor experiments demonstrated that the UBCRPs yield reached its maximum at an ultrasonic power of 400 W, an ethanol precipitation concentration of 80%, a liquid-to-solid ratio of 30 mL/g, and an extraction time of 30 min (Fig. 1B-E). Based on the results of the single-factor experiments, a three-level factorial design was established to further optimize the significant variables. As shown in Table 1, a total of 29 experimental runs were designed, including 24 factorial points and five center points based on four independent variables. The independent variables investigated were ultrasonic power (A), ethanol precipitation concentration (B), ultrasonic time (C), and liquid-to-solid ratio (D). Subsequently, a second-order polynomial regression analysis was performed to establish the mathematical relationship between the response variable (Y, extraction yield) and the four independent variables, resulting the following regression equation: Y=19.01+1.34A+3.95B+0.9158C-1.02D+0.0425AB-0.1275AC-0.3150AD+0.7100BC+1.03BD+0.1450CD-2.16A2-2.94B2-1.72C2-1.20D2. The ANOVA results (Fig. 1L) demonstrated that the regression model was highly significant (F = 11.73, P < 0.0001). The lack-of-fit test returned F = 1.61 and P = 0.3433, which was not significant relative to the pure error, indicating no significant systematic bias, adequate fit to the experimental data, and reliable applicability for predicting and analyzing the UA extraction of UBCRPs. Furthermore, the significance of individual regression coefficients and their interaction terms was evaluated (Fig. 1L). Within the tested parameter ranges, all quadratic terms (A2, B2, C2, D2) were statistically significant (P < 0.05), with the quadratic term of ethanol precipitation concentration (B2) showing the highest significance (P < 0.0001). Based on the significance levels of these terms, the relative influence of the four factors on UBCRPs extraction yeild was ranked as follows: ethanol precipitation concentration (B) > ultrasonic power (A) > ultrasonic extraction time (C) > liquid-to-solid ratio (D).

Given the significant interactions between different factors, we further investigated the effects of any pair of factors on the yield of UBCRPs obtained via UA extraction. As shown in Fig. 1F-K, with increasing levels of each of the four factors, the yield exhibited a consistent trend of initial increase followed by a subsequent decrease (or plateauing). The contour plots for each interaction were elliptical in shape (Fig. S1), indicating the presence of interactions between any two variables. Moreover, steeper slopes reflected more pronounced interaction effects. The distinct curvature observed, especially for ethanol precipitation concentration (B) and ultrasonic power (A), indicated their greater impact on the yield. RSM optimization predicted the theoretical optimal UA extraction conditions for UBCRPs: ultrasonic power of 415.766 W, ethanol precipitation concentration of 86.9769%, ultrasonic time of 33.9278 min, and liquid-to-solid ratio of 28.5818 mL/g, with a predicted theoretical yield range of 18.6707% to 23.031%. Considering the discrete nature of the operational settings on the laboratory ultrasonic equipment, the parameters were adjusted to experimentally feasible values: ultrasonic power of 400 W, ethanol precipitation concentration of 87%, ultrasonic extraction time of 34 min, and liquid-to-solid ratio of 29 mL/g. Triplicate verification experiments performed under these adjusted operational conditions afforded an average UBCRPs extraction yield of 21.31 ± 1.01%, which lies entirely within the 95% predicted confidence interval and shows good quantitative agreement with the model prediction. According to the related quality standard, the total polysaccharide content of Banqiao Codonopsis Radix should be no less than 20%, as determined by a direct colorimetric assay of the raw material. It should be noted that the extraction yield reported in this study refers to the mass of crude polysaccharide powder recovered after the complete UA extraction, ethanol precipitation, centrifugation, and drying workflow, expressed as a percentage of the initial dry powder weight. Therefore, the extraction yield should be regarded as a process-recovery value rather than a direct measurement of the total polysaccharide content in the raw material. Under the optimized UA extraction conditions, the average crude polysaccharide yield reached 21.31 ± 1.01%, which was higher than the yield of 18.67 ± 1.23% previously reported for conventional hot water extraction [4], confirming that the extraction process is capable of recovering the majority of the polysaccharides present. However, the fact that the isolated yield was only moderately above the standard threshold suggests that polysaccharide loss or operational factors during extraction and post-treatment may have affected the final recovery. Possible losses may occur during filtration, incomplete ethanol precipitation, co-removal of polysaccharide and protein complexes during Sevag deproteinization, and possible sonomechanical scission of very high molecular weight fractions. These factors may collectively explain why the isolated yield was not substantially higher. Accordingly, the potential food-processing application of Banqiao Codonopsis Radix should be interpreted as a preliminary implication based on the improved extraction performance rather than as a definitive conclusion.

Overall, this part successfully established the optimal UA extraction conditions for BCRPs and validated the predictive accuracy of the RSM model. The extraction yield obtained under these optimized parameters agreed well with the predicted value, confirming the feasibility and industrial scalability of the process.

UA extraction achieved higher BCRPs extraction yield and superior environmental benefits

Building on the optimized UA extraction process for BCRPs established in Section 3.1, we first applied this standardized protocol to extract polysaccharides from C. tangshen Oliv. samples collected from five geographical origins: Banqiao, Wushan, Wuxi, Jianshi, and Fengjie. As shown in Fig. 1N, under the optimized UA extraction conditions, the polysaccharide yield from Banqiao remained significantly higher than those from the other four origins, consistent with our preliminary HW extraction screening results (Fig. 1M). Accordingly, BCRPs extracted by the optimized UA extraction process (UBCRPs) and conventional HW extraction process (HBCRPs) were selected as the core materials for comparative analysis, with a focus on extraction yield, energy consumption, and environmental performance.

The analysis results showed that UA extraction process achieved a productivity of 18.86 ± 0.90%/h with a specific energy consumption of 6.91 ± 0.34 kWh/kg, while HW extraction process yielded only 7.50 ± 0.29%/h and consumed 16.01 ± 0.61 kWh/kg (Table 2). Compared with the HW extraction, UA extraction exhibited a 2.38–fold higher productivity, a 43.5% shorter extraction time, and an energy consumption equal to only 43.16% of that required by HW extraction. These improvements suggest that UA extraction is a considerably more efficient and energy-saving approach for extracting BCRPs. Furthermore, the environmental impact of the extraction processes was quantified by calculating carbon dioxide emissions based on energy consumption. It is estimated that approximately 800 g of CO2 is emitted per kilowatt-hour (kWh) of electrical energy consumed in China [20]. For the production of 1 kg of UBCRPs, UA extraction generated only 5.52 kg of CO2, which accounted for merely 43.09% of the 12.81 kg CO2 emissions from HW extraction. This significant reduction in carbon footprint highlights that UA extraction is an environmentally responsible extraction method, consistent with global sustainable development goals. Similar environmental advantages of UA extraction have also been reported in the extraction of lignans from sesame cake [20]. Considering the widespread commercial availability of large-scale ultrasonic equipment, the optimized UA extraction process demonstrated in this study possesses substantial potential for industrial-scale production of high-quality BCRPs.

Mechanism underlying the enhanced extraction efficiency of BCRPs by UA extraction

Based on the demonstrated superiority of UAE over HWE for BCRPs extraction, we further elucidated the underlying mechanisms responsible for the enhanced extraction efficiency.

UA extraction altered the cell wall composition of BCR powder

FTIR spectroscopy analysis revealed distinct structural differences between UA and HW extraction residues of BCR powder (Fig. 2A). Both samples exhibited characteristic absorption bands of cell wall components, including O-H stretching (3423 cm−1), C-H stretching (2925 cm−1), and carboxyl or ester group vibrations [26]. However, UA extraction altered the 1737 cm−1 band corresponding to hemicellulose C=O stretching, indicating structural modifications to hemicellulose [27]. Notably, the characteristic crystalline cellulose peaks at 874 cm−1 and 818 cm−1 disappeared entirely after UA extraction, confirming disruption of the crystalline cellulose structure [28]. The increased overall absorption intensities of key functional groups in UA extraction residues of BCR powder were further indicated greater exposure of cell wall components. Collectively, these results demonstrated that UA extraction enhanced BCRPs yield more effectively than HW extraction primarily through disrupting crystalline cellulose architecture and promoting cell wall matrix disintegration [29].

Alterations in cell wall characteristics of residues obtained after HW and UA extraction. A: Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra. B: X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns. C: Thermal properties. D: Particle size analysis. E: Surface morphological analysis.

UA extraction altered the crystalline structure of the cell wall in BCR powder

Since cellulose is the main structural component of the cell wall of BCR powder, XRD was used to examine changes in its crystalline structure induced by UA extraction. As shown in Fig. 2B, the diffraction signals recorded at 2θ values ranging from 20.95° to 21.8° were assigned to the typical lattice plane of type I cellulose [28]. This result confirmed that the cell wall matrices of both UA and HW extracted residues were dominated by type I cellulose, which inherently determines the mechanical and thermal behaviors of plant cell walls [30]. Compared with the HW extraction, the diffraction peak of UA extraction residues presented noticeable variations in peak intensity and width. This results implied that UA extraction decreased cellulose crystallinity and induced the partial transition of crystalline cellulose into amorphous fractions[31]. Such structural rearrangement favors the liberation of intracellular UBCRPs [32].

UA extraction altered the cell wall thermal properties of BCR powder

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was further performed to verify the thermally structural variations between UA and HW extraction residues of BCR powder (Fig. 2C). The minor mass loss occurring at 40 °C to 200 °C was primarily derived from the evaporation of absorbed moisture and certain volatile substances [33]. Within the range of 200 °C to 400 °C, the pyrolysis of lignin, hemicellulose, cellulose, and pectin, which involved dehydration, decarboxylation, and the cleavage of glycosidic and hydrogen bonds, generated substantial quantities of gases and volatile substances, resulting in considerable mass loss [34]. UA extraction showed a higher temperature at the maximum weight loss rate, which was probably related to the partial removal of hemicellulose and pectin during ultrasonic processing [35]. Macromolecular carbonization and residual lignin decomposition continuously occurred in the temperature range of 400 °C to 800 °C [36]. Moreover, the HW extraction possessed a higher residual mass than UA, demonstrating that UA extraction aggravated the structural decomposition of cell wall compositions [33]. Collectively, these thermal variations further verified that UA extraction effectively modulated the thermal properties of cell wall matrices and intensified the structural destruction of cellulose fractions.

UA extraction altered the particle size and micromorphology of BCR powder extraction residues

Particle size analysis compared macroscopic morphological differences between UA and HW extraction residues of BCR powder. UA extraction exhibited a 22.467% decrease in particle size compared to HW extraction (Fig. 2D), resulting from UA extraction removal of pectin, hemicellulose, and lignin, which enlarged the specific surface area and improved the dissolution accessibility of intracellular polysaccharides [28]. SEM images further showed distinct microstructural differences: HW extraction residues showed a compact, dense surfaces with abundant attachments and minimal pores (Fig. 2E1-E3), whereas UA extraction residues, displayed prominent cracks and fragmented textures (Fig. 2E4-E6). This morphological evidence indicated that UA extraction exerted stronger destructive effects on cell wall structures than HW extraction, reducing mass transfer resistance and promoting UBCRPs release. UA extraction enhanced the extraction yield of UBCRPs primarily through physical disruption of the plant tissue and cell wall architecture, including cellulose decrystallization, particle size reduction, and surface morphology alteration. This physical extraction mechanism was mainly driven by acoustic cavitation and the resulting mechanical effects, which facilitated the release of polysaccharides from the plant matrix [37]. Similar microstructural alterations have been reported for UA extraction of polysaccharides from the epidermis of Allium fistulosum and Kangxian flowers [7], [38].

The multi-dimensional characterization collectively elucidates the mechanism underlying UA extraction of BCRPs. Specifically, UA extraction induces acoustic cavitation and associated mechanical forces, which synergistically creates porous cell wall structures, reduces particle size, enlarges specific surface area, disrupts the ordered crystalline structure of cellulose, and promotes the breakdown of insoluble cell wall components. Such cumulative structural changes facilitate the efficient release of intracellular polysaccharides, thereby significantly enhancing the extraction yield of UBCRPs.

Effects of UA extraction on the structural characteristics and physicochemical properties of BCRPs

Given that UA extraction significantly improved the extraction yield and environmental performance of BCRPs, we next investigated whether it compromised the structural integrity and fundamental properties of UBCRPs. Therefor, the structural and physicochemical properties of UBCRPs were systematically characterized and directly compared with those of HBCRPs in this section.

UA extraction preserved the molecular weight and functional group profiles but altered the monosaccharide compositional ratios of BCRPs

As shown in Table 3, the weight-average molecular weights (Mw) of UBCRPs and HBCRPs were 3.386 kDa and 3.292 kDa, with polydispersity indices (Mw/Mn) of 1.219 and 1.274, respectively. These nearly identical parameters demonstrated that UA extraction did not cause significant chain scission or degradation of the BCRPs polysaccharide backbone. FTIR spectroscopy revealed that UBCRPs and HBCRPs shared identical characteristic absorption bands, including hydroxyl stretching (approximately 3420 cm−1), C-H stretching (approximately 2930 cm−1) [39], amide C=O stretching (near 1600 cm−1), pyranose ring vibrations (1000 to 1200 cm−1), and β type glycosidic linkages (approximately 880 cm−1 and 599 cm−1) (Fig. 3A) [40], [41]. These results demonstrated that UA extraction preserved the primary structure and functional group profiles of BCRPs. However, monosaccharide composition analysis showed that UBCRPs contained a lower glucose proportion (75.30% versus 80.63%) and higher proportions of arabinose (10.42% versus 7.26%), rhamnose (1.60% versus 1.26%), and galactose (12.68% versus 10.85%) compared to HBCRPs (Table 3 and Fig. 3B). Therefore, UA extraction altered the monosaccharide compositional ratios without affecting molecular weight or functional group integrity (Table 3 and Fig. 3C and D). Collectively, UA extraction altered the monosaccharide compositional ratios of BCRPs without significantly affecting their molecular weight. However, the present study mainly characterized the overall structural features of crude polysaccharide fractions. Detailed glycosidic linkage patterns were not fully resolved because methylation analysis and advanced NMR characterization of purified homogeneous fractions were not performed. Therefore, future work should focus on fraction purification, methylation analysis, and advanced NMR characterization to clarify the fine structures responsible for the observed bioactivities.

UA extraction reduced protein impurities, enhanced solution stability, and did not alter crystallinity or thermal stability of BCRPs

UV–Vis absorption spectra showed that HBCRPs had a characteristic absorption peak at 260 nm, indicating the presence of nucleic acid or protein impurities, while no such peak was detected in UBCRPs (Fig. 3E). This result suggested that, under the same Sevag deproteinization process, UA extraction enabled more efficient removal of proteinaceous impurities, likely by cleaving the non–covalent bonds between polysaccharides and bound proteins. XRD analysis revealed that both UBCRPs and HBCRPs showed similar sharp diffraction peaks at approximately 20° (Fig. 3F), indicating relatively high crystallinity [42]. Moreover, the two samples exhibited almost identical peak positions, intensities, and widths, demonstrating that UA extraction did not alter the crystalline structure of BCRPs.

Zeta potential measurements were performed to assess the colloidal stability of BCRPs in aqueous solutions. Generally, an absolute zeta potential greater than 10 mV indicates a stable dispersion [43]. As shown in Fig. 3G, UBCRPs had a zeta potential of −14.6 mV, while HBCRPs exhibited −13.7 mV. The larger absolute value of UBCRPs suggested that UA extraction improved the colloidal stability of BCRPs, consistent with previous findings for UA extraction of polysaccharides from Artemisia argyi leaves [44], frosted fig pectin [45], and Allium Chinese G. Don [7]. Furthermore, the thermal properties of UBCRPs and HBCRPs were detected by TGA, and the corresponding thermograms showed that both samples exhibited three distinct thermal degradation stages (Fig. 3H). The first stage occurred between 40 °C and 100 °C, with an approximately 10% loss of the initial mass, corresponding to the evaporation of free and bound water as well as volatile compounds [46]. The second stage took place between 250 °C and 300 °C, with an approximately 55% mass loss, attributed to the decomposition of the polysaccharide backbone, including sugar ring degradation and side chain cleavage [35]. The third stage extended from 400 °C to 900 °C, with an approximately 15% mass loss, ascribed to the carbonization of residual organic matter, resulting a final char residue of approximately 20% for both samples [47]. The thermal degradation profiles of UBCRPs and HBCRPs were nearly identical across all temperature ranges. These results confirmed that both UBCRPs and HBCRPs had good thermal stability, and that UA extraction did not alter the thermal properties of BCRPs.

UA extraction altered the surface morphology and spatial conformation of BCRPs

The structural morphology of polysaccharides is intimately associated with their solubility, viscosity, and biological activities. As shown in Fig. 3I and J, HBCRPs exhibited a dense, compact structure with large, irregular, and heterogeneously sized particles, whereas UBCRPs displayed a rough, porous architecture composed of small, loosely stacked lamellar fragments interspersed with uniformly distributed cake-like aggregates. These distinct morphological alterations may be directly attributable to the intense cavitation effects, turbulent shear forces and transient high-pressure shocks generated during UA extraction [35]. Such physical forces disrupt inter-molecular hydrogen bonds and disintegrate large polysaccharide aggregates, resulting in the formation of smaller, more uniform particles and a loosely stacked lamellar structure. The presence of sparsely cross-linked cake-like aggregates further indicated that UA extraction partially disrupted the inter-molecular cross-linking network within BCRPs. Collectively, these findings confirmed that UA extraction significantly altered the surface morphology and spatial conformation of BCRPs. Such a porous, fragmented structure with increased specific surface area may facilitate the interaction of BCRPs with biological targets, thereby potentially contributing to their biological activities [48].

UA extraction did not compromise the anti-UC effects of BCRPs

Building on the findings that UAE altered the surface morphology of BCRPs without affecting their molecular weight or functional groups, we systematically compared the anti-UC effects of HBCRPs and UBCRPs using a DSS induced murine UC model (Fig. 4A). Compared with the control group, mice in the DSS group exhibited significant weight loss, colon shortening, and elevated DAI scores (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01) [49]. Treatment with HBCRPs, UBCRPs, or mesalazine (the positive drug) significantly alleviated these UC symptoms (P < 0.01 or P < 0.001, Fig. 4B-E). Notably, no significant differences in therapeutic efficacy were observed between HBCRPs and UBCRPs across all dosage groups (P > 0.05). Neither polysaccharides affected the organ coefficients of the liver, kidney, spleen, or thymus (Fig. 4F-I), indicating no obvious systemic toxicity under the tested conditions. ELISA results showed that DSS administration significantly up-regulated the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6, while down-regulating the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (P < 0.001). Treatment with HBCRPs or UBCRPs markedly reversed these abnormal cytokine levels (P < 0.05 or P < 0.001), with no significant difference in regulatory effects between the two polysaccharide groups (P > 0.05, Fig. 4J-M). H&E staining analysis demonstrated that all treatment groups significantly attenuated the severe colonic damage induced by DSS, including intestinal barrier degeneration, crypt loss, goblet cell depletion, and epithelial cell sloughing (Fig. 5A).

To further evaluate the protective effects of UBCRPs and HBCRPs on the intestinal mucosal barrier, AB-PAS staining, MUC2 immunohistochemistry and ZO-1/Occludin immunofluorescence were conducted. AB-PAS staining showed markedly reduced mucin secretion in the DSS group, indicating impairment of the mucus chemical barrier. Both UBCRPs and HBCRPs treatment significantly increased the production of acidic and neutral mucins (Fig. 5B). Consistently, MUC2 protein expression was significantly decreased in the DSS group but effectively restored by both UBCRPs and HBCRPs treatments (Fig. 5C and F). Immunofluorescence results further demonstrated that DSS significantly down-regulated the tight junction proteins Occludin and ZO-1, while both UBCRPs and HBCRPs markedly up-regulated their expression (Fig. 5D-H). No significant differences were observed in any of these mucosal barrier protective effects between the UBCRPs and HBCRPs treatment groups.

Collectively, these results demonstrated that both UBCRPs and HBCRPs alleviated DSS induced UC by regulating inflammatory cytokine expression and repairing intestinal mucosal barrier damage. Importantly, UA extraction did not alter the anti-UC effects of BCRPs.

UBCRPs exerted anti-UC effects not solely via regulating gut microbiota

UBCRPs reshaped the disordered gut microbiota diversity and community structures in UC mice

Gut microbiota is critical for maintaining intestinal mucosal barrier integrity and is involved in the pathogenesis of UC [50]. 16S rRNA sequencing analysis showed that DSS significantly decreased Chao1 and Shannon indices (alpha diversity) compared with the control group, which were partially reversed by UBCRPsH treatment (P < 0.05, Fig. 6A and B). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was used to assess the overall structural changes of gut microbiota. The analysis revealed significant separation of microbial communities among different groups (P < 0.001), with UBCRPsH group clustering much closer to the control group than the DSS group (Fig. 6C). This finding confirms that UBCRPsH partially ameliorated the DSS induced structural dysbiosis of gut microbiota.

At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were the dominated bacterial taxa across all experimental groups (Fig. 6D). Compared with the control group, the DSS group exhibited a marked decrease in the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio, an alteration partially reversed by UBCRPsH treatment (Fig. 6E). Given that Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes are primary producers of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) in the gut, their reduced relative abundance aligns with the lower fecal SCFA levels typically observed in UC patients. Therefore, a decreased F/B ratio is widely recognized as a hallmark of gut dysbiosis [[51], [52], [53]]. Notably, UBCRPs appeared to ameliorate gut microbiota dysbiosis in UC mice, at least partly through normalizing the F/B ratio. At the family level, DSS treatment induced clear shifts in the abundances of Lachnospiraceae and Muribaculaceae. UBCRPsH intervention restored the levels of these two beneficial bacterial families to near-normal levels (Fig. 6F). At the genus level, UBCRPsH decreased the abundances of potentially pathogenic bacteria including Colidextribacter, Oscillibacter and unidentified Enterobacteriaceae. Meanwhile, it increased the abundances of beneficial bacteria such as Ligilactobacillus and unidentified Lachnospiraceae (Fig. 6G). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was further performed to identify key bacterial taxa associated with the therapeutic effect of UBCRPsH. The results showed that Firmicutes and Lachnospiraceae were significantly enriched in the UBCRPsH group compared with the DSS group (Fig. 6H and I). Collectively, these findings indicated that UBCRPsH exerted its anti-UC effects primarily by modulating gut microbiota composition, particularly by enriching beneficial SCFA-producing bacteria and reducing pathogenic taxa.

Transplantation of UBCRPs-modulated gut microbiota alleviated UC symptoms

To determine whether the anti-UC effects of UBCRPsH are dependent on gut microbiota, we first performed FMT experiments, as outlined in Fig. 7A. As confirmed, ABX treatment successfully eliminated the gut microbial community compared to the control group (Fig. S2). On day 15, the DSS-FMT group showed significant body weight loss, elevated DAI scores, and colon shortening compared with the control-FMT group (P < 0.01 or P < 0.001, Fig. 7B-E). UBCRPsH-FMT intervention significantly improved DAI scores and colon length (P < 0.05 or P < 0.0001) but did not reverse body weight loss (P > 0.05). Moreover, UBCRPsH did not alter liver, kidney, spleen, and thymus organ coefficients (P > 0.05, Fig. 7F- I), indicating it has no systemic toxicity under microbiota-depleted conditions. ELISA analysis showed that DSS-FMT significantly up-regulated pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α, and down-regulated anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01), whereas UBCRPsH-FMT markedly normalized these abnormal cytokine levels (P < 0.001 or P < 0.0001, Fig. 7J-M).

ABX treatment did not abolish the anti-UC effects of UBCRPs

Having established that UBCRPsH-modulated microbiota possesses anti-UC activity, we further performed antibiotic depletion experiments (Fig. 8A) to determine whether gut microbiota is the exclusive mediator of UBCRPsH's therapeutic effects. UBCRPs ameliorated DSS induced UC in mice by modulating corticosterone metabolism

Having demonstrated that UBCRPsH exerts anti-UC effects through both microbiota-dependent and independent pathways, we further performed widely targeted metabolomics to identify key metabolic mediators linking gut microbiota and UC pathogenesis. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) revealed clear separation of serum metabolite profiles between the control and DSS groups, as well as between the DSS and UBCRPsH groups (Fig. 9A and D). Permutation tests confirmed the reliability of both models, with R2/Q2 values of 0.999/0.875 and 0.999/0.902, respectively (Fig. 9B and E). A total of 67 and 103 differential metabolites were identified in the two comparisons based on variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 and P < 0.05, and Venn diagram analysis revealed 16 common differential metabolites across the three groups (Fig. 9C). Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the associations between these key metabolites and the previously identified differential bacterial taxa. The results showed that corticosterone was negatively correlated with Lachnospiraceae and positively correlated with Bacteroides, unidentified Clostridia and Oscillospiraceae (P < 0.05, Fig. 9F). Previous studies have reported that UC is associated with elevated serum corticosterone concentrations, and pro-inflammatory cytokine release can further increase blood corticosterone levels [54], [55]. Collectively, these findings suggest that UBCRPsH may ameliorate UC by modulating serum corticosterone metabolism through its regulatory effects on gut microbiota (Fig. 10).

UBCRPs ameliorated DSS induced UC by regulating the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction signaling pathway

To further explore the downstream molecular mechanisms underlying corticosterone-mediated UC amelioration, transcriptomic sequencing was performed on colonic tissues from the control, DSS and UBCRPsH treatment groups. Principal component analysis (PCA) results showed clear intergroup separation and good intragroup consistency, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 32.92% and 17.03% of the total variance respectively (Fig. 9G). A total of 632 up-regulated and 709 down-regulated genes were identified in the DSS group compared with the control group, while 361 up-regulated and 85 down-regulated genes were found in the UBCRPsH group relative to the DSS group (Fig. 9H and I). Venn diagram analysis revealed 249 common differentially expressed genes across the three groups (Fig. 9J). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of these 249 common genes identified three significantly enriched UC-related pathways: cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, retinol metabolism, and mucin type O-glycan biosynthesis (P < 0.05, Fig. 9K). Spearman correlation analysis between genes in these pathways and the 16 common differential metabolites showed that corticosterone was significantly positively correlated with multiple genes in the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway (including TNF, THPO, IL1RN, BMP2, IL34 and IL1RL2) and negatively correlated with the PRLR gene (Fig. 9L). Given that inhibition of the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway has been well documented to alleviate UC [18], [56], these results suggested that UBCRPsH might ameliorate UC by modulating corticosterone metabolism to regulate this key pathway, a hypothesis that warrants further functional validation of this corticosterone-cytokine signaling axis through in vitro and in vivo experiments in our future studies.

Conclusion

In this study, an optimized UA extraction process for BCRPs was developed using RSM. Under the optimal conditions of 400 W ultrasonic power, 34 min extraction time, 29 mL/g liquid-to-solid ratio and 87% ethanol concentration, UA extraction achieved a BCRPs yield of 21.31 ± 1.01%, which was 42.07% higher than that obtained by conventional HW extraction. Compared with HW extraction, UA extraction reduced extraction time by 43.5%, energy consumption by 38.79%, and CO2 emissions by 56.91%, while also improving impurity removal efficiency by eliminating nucleic acid and protein contaminants. Mechanistic investigations suggested that ultrasonic cavitation and mechanical effects reduced the material particle size by 22.47%, altered the crystalline cellulose structure, thereby reducing mass transfer resistance and facilitating the release of BCRPs. Structural characterization further demonstrated that UA extraction caused only limited changes in the physicochemical properties of BCRPs, with minor variations in molecular weight (2.8%) and polydispersity index (4.5%), suggesting that the main polysaccharide chain was largely preserved. Most importantly, UA extraction did not compromise the anti-UC effects of BCRPs, as UBCRPs exhibited anti-UC effects comparable to those of HBCRPs by regulating gut microbiota composition, modulating corticosterone metabolism, and inhibiting the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway. These findings indicate that UA extraction is an efficient, low-carbon, and sustainable alternative for BCRPs production, and provide a technical basis for the high-value utilization of BCRPs.
