Association between organisational patient safety practices, safety culture and adverse event reporting in paediatric emergency departments in Spain: a multicentre cross-sectional survey study
Authors: Blanca Collado-González, María Victoria Navarta-Sánchez, Ancor Sanz-Garcia, Ana María Palmar-Santos, Emergency Safety Culture Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo analyse the association between organisational patient safety practices, including training in patient safety and clinical simulation, and safety culture and adverse event (AE) reporting in paediatric emergency departments.DesignObservational analytical multicentre cross-sectional study.Setting19 paediatric emergency departments at different levels of healthcare.ParticipantsHealthcare professionals working in paediatric emergency departments.InterventionsNot applicablePrimary and secondary outcomesSafety culture was analysed using the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture, following the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality methodology. Logistic regression analyses were performed. Organisational patient safety practices included the presence of patient safety specialists, leadership walkarounds, additional reporting systems and training in patient safety and clinical simulation.ResultsA total of 1176 professionals participated (response rate 63.8%). The presence of patient safety specialists was associated with more favourable scores in ‘supervisor/manager expectations and actions’, ‘organisational learning’ and ‘teamwork’ (p<0.001). Additional reporting systems were associated with higher scores in ‘frequency of events reported’ and ‘feedback’ (p<0.001). Leadership walkarounds were associated with higher response rates in ‘supervisor/manager expectations and actions’ (p=0.013) and ‘organisational learning’ (p=0.048). Training in patient safety and clinical simulation were associated with higher positive response rates across most questionnaire dimensions. Higher AE reporting was observed in centres with clinical simulation training, presence of patient safety specialists, leadership walkarounds and additional reporting systems in bivariate analyses. However, in the multivariable model, only other reporting systems (OR 2.690; 95% CI 1.565 to 4.662) and annual emergency department visit volume (OR 1.023; 95% CI 1.1013 to 1.033) were independently associated with AE reporting. The presence of more than two patient safety specialists was associated with lower reporting (OR 0.553; 95% CI 0.306 to 0.998). Paediatricians were more likely to report AEs.ConclusionsOrganisational patient safety practices were associated with favourable safety culture dimensions. However, after the adjustment, AE reporting was primarily associated with reporting infrastructure and organisational characteristics. The implementation of these practices varied across centres, highlighting differences in the availability of organisational safety practices. Further research is needed to better understand these associations in paediatric emergency departments.Keywords: Adverse events, Emergency Departments, PAEDIATRICS, Health & safety

Introduction

Ensuring patient safety (PS) is essential in healthcare. Over the last decade, numerous strategies and measures have been implemented to prevent errors and promote PS.1–3 However, adverse events (AEs) remain a serious public health problem worldwide.2 Safety culture (SC) is defined as the product of individual and group values, attitudes, perceptions, competencies and behaviour patterns that determine commitment, style and competence in managing an organisation’s health and safety.4

Assessing and measuring patient SC is a key strategy for identifying areas for improvement, promoting change and monitoring progress,5 with SC questionnaires being the most widely used and accepted method for this purpose.6

Emergency departments (EDs) are complex clinical settings characterised by a high degree of uncertainty. Factors such as frequent interruptions, sudden increases in patient pressure, rapid and complex decision-making, communication failures and high staff turnover contribute to an increased risk of AEs.7 8 Although critical illness is less common in paediatric patients than in adult patients,9 incidents occurring during their care coexist as they do in adults.10–12 As a result, the WHO has placed special emphasis on the paediatric population, focusing on the 2025 PS campaign on this age group.13

Despite advances in PS, achieving zero harm remains a challenge that requires a systemic and coordinated approach.2 Promoting SC is key to improving PS and quality. For this reason, different strategies have been established to promote SC, including promoting teamwork, active leadership and manager involvement, as well as creating non-punitive reporting systems.14 Likewise, PS training and clinical simulation stand out as key strategies for identifying risks, reducing errors and improving quality of care.15

In Spain, in line with international strategies, a range of strategies have been promoted to enhance PS through organisational practices, such as AE reporting systems, functional risk management units with the establishment of PS specialists (PSSs), continuing education in PS, clinical simulation and leadership walkarounds (LWs).16 SC is a multidimensional construct influenced by individual, team and organisational factors. Organisational PS practices represent conceptually distinct but complementary domains, including structural organisational resources (such as PSSs), leadership behaviours (LWs), reporting and communication infrastructure and educational interventions (training and clinical simulation). From a theoretical perspective, these practices are expected to influence SC and AE reporting through shared mechanisms such as organisational learning, leadership visibility, reporting climate and improved teamwork and communication.14 17

The implementation of these systems has been gradual.18 In a previous study conducted in paediatric EDs (PEDs), differences in PS were analysed according to professional category and sex, identifying different perception profiles through cluster analysis.19 However, evidence on the association between the implementation of different organisational PS practices and SC, as well as voluntary reporting of AE, is limited.

Therefore, this study adds to knowledge in this area by exploring the association between different organisational practices implemented in hospitals and the dimensions of the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (HSOPSC) questionnaire and voluntary reporting of AE.

Methodology

Study design

Observational, analytical, cross-sectional, multicentre comparative study.

The study was conducted and described in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines and the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) (online supplemental material 1 and 2).

Setting and study population

The study was conducted in 19 of the 26 hospitals in the public network of the Community of Madrid with paediatric emergency units, offering different levels of care. The participating hospitals were H.U. Gregorio Marañón, H.U. de la Paz, H.U. del Suereste, U. Puerta de Hierro, H.U. Severo Ochoa, H. Clínico San Carlos, H.U. de Getafe, H.U. Infanta Leonor, H.U. Infanta Sofía, H.U. Ramón y Cajal, H.U. Fundación Jiménez Díaz, H.U. Fuenlabrada, H.U. del Escorial, H.U. 12 de Octubre, H.U. del Tajo, H.U. Niño Jesús, H.U. Príncipe de Asturias, H.U. Fundación Alcorcón and H.U.G de Villalba. The general design of the study, selection of centres and population characteristics were described previously.19 At the time of the study, the participating centres had a total of 1843 healthcare professionals, ensuring a sufficient sample size and adequate variability between hospitals.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All healthcare professionals (paediatricians, specialty training in paediatrics (STP), nurses, paediatric nurse residents (PNR) and nursing assistants) were included. Non-healthcare professionals and doctors and resident nurses from medical specialties other than paediatrics, as well as healthcare degree students, were excluded. The detailed criteria were published previously.19

Instrument and data collection

The self-administered HSOPSC questionnaire from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) was used in its validated Spanish version.20 The characteristics of the instrument, its dimensional structure and the data collection procedure were described previously.19

Two additional items assessed whether participants had received PS training and clinical simulation training. Responses were self-reported at the individual level (yes/no). Non-responses to these items were treated as missing data and excluded from the corresponding analyses.

Additional variables related to organisational PS practices were collected at the centre level through a designated contact person at each participating hospital. These variables included the presence of PSSs, LWs and AE reporting systems. In addition, the number of annual ED visits was recorded.

PSSs: PSSs were defined as healthcare professionals officially designated as PS reference persons within PED. This role is established in the Community of Madrid by regional regulations (Decree 4/2021 of 20 January), which specifies the designation of professionals responsible for coordinating PS activities within clinical units.21 In participating centres, PSSs included paediatricians, nurses and nursing assistants who performed this role alongside their routine clinical duties. Their responsibilities included supporting incident reporting processes, promoting PS training, facilitating communication on safety issues between frontline staff and management and fostering a culture of safety. The number of PSSs in each centre was collected via self-reporting by the participating unit, based on professionals who were formally designated and actively performing this role at the time of data collection. The centres were classified into four categories: no PSS, one PSS, two PSSs or >2 PSSs.

Information regarding the presence of LWs, participating professionals and frequency of implementation was collected at the centre level. Considerable heterogeneity was observed across participating hospitals in both composition and implementation characteristics. LWs ranged from annual to trimestral initiatives and involved different combinations of hospital leadership, quality and safety units, medical and nursing managers, frontline clinicians and multidisciplinary representatives. In some centres, LWs were conducted specifically within the PED, whereas in others, they formed part of paediatric-area initiatives. Data analysis

The HSOPSC questionnaire was analysed using the methodology proposed by the AHRQ, described previously.19 Responses were recoded as negative, neutral or positive, and relative frequencies were calculated by dimension, considering strengths and weaknesses according to the criteria established by the AHRQ.

A secondary analysis of the data was performed to explore the association between organisational PS practices and the dimensions of the HSOPSC, as well as their relationship with AE reporting.

The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to verify the assumption of normality of the distribution of the variables. None of the variables analysed followed a normal distribution.

To analyse the differences in the number of reported AE according to structural and sociodemographic variables, the Mann–Whitney U test was performed for dichotomous variables, whereas the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to the remaining variables. If statistically significant, a post hoc analysis was performed using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

To analyse the dimensions of the HSOPSC questionnaire, the percentages of responses (positive, negative and neutral) for each dimension were compared between the structural variables of the SC. Initially, the χ2 test of independence was used. In cases where significant differences were found, post hoc tests were performed between pairs of groups with Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons for those variables with more than two groups. In the case of dichotomous variables, comparisons of proportions between pairs were performed using the proportions test with continuity correction.

Finally, a binary logistic regression model was constructed to identify independent factors associated with AE reporting. Variables were dichotomised (yes/no), and non-responses were treated as missing data and excluded from the corresponding analyses. Independent variables comprised personal characteristics (age and sex), professional characteristics (professional category, PS training and clinical simulation) and organisational variables related to SC: PSSs, LWs and other AE reporting systems; annual ED visit volume was included as a continuous variable (number of annual visits divided by 1000) in the multivariable logistic regression model. Multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with adjusted generalized VIF (GVIF) values for categorical variables.

Potential sources of bias

Several strategies were implemented to minimise potential sources of bias. The use of a validated and widely used instrument (HSOPSC) reduced information bias, and data were collected anonymously to minimise social desirability bias. Selection bias was mitigated by including all healthcare professionals working in participating PEDs and by achieving a high response rate. In addition, multivariable analysis was used to adjust for potential confounding variables.

Calculations were performed for a 95% CI. The level of statistical significance was considered to be ≤0.05. Excel (Microsoft 365, Excel 2016) was used to organise the data. Statistical analysis was performed using R software (V.4.4.2; R Studio).

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the ethics committees of participating hospitals: Gregorio Marañón (CSUP_ENF), Fuenlabrada (CSUP_ENF 24/11), Doce de Octubre (CSUP_ENF), Tajo (CSUP_ENF), Fundación Jiménez Díaz (2024/2), Villalba (2024/2), Clínico (CSUP_ENF), Puerta de Hierro (CSUP_ENF), El Escorial (CSUP_ENF), Severo Ochoa (CSUP_ENF), La Paz (CSUP_ENF), Sureste (2/2024), Infanta Leonor (CSUP_ENF), Ramón y Cajal (006/24), Infanta Sofía (CSUP_ENF), Getafe (CV23/57), Niño Jesús (R-0009/24), Príncipe de Asturias (CSUP_ENF OE 24/2024) and Fundación Alcorcón (24/53). All participants agreed to participate in the study. The data collected were pseudonymised, coded with a numerical sequence for each subject and by hospital.

Results

The 19 participating hospitals had a total staff of 1843 professionals during the study period. A total of 1176 questionnaires were collected, representing an overall response rate of 63.8%. Among the participants, 83.25% (n=979) were women, and 12.93% (n=152) were men, while 3.83% (n=45) did not respond or did not identify with any of the responses.

The majority of participants were nurses, accounting for 33.3% (n=434), which was also the group of professionals with the highest response rate (70.8%, n=434).

Regarding the number of PSSs, we found significant differences among groups. Significant differences in AE reporting were observed according to the number of PSSs. Higher reporting levels were observed in centres with at least one PSS compared with centres with none (p<0.001), with the highest reporting levels observed in centres with two PSSs (p=0.02). Significant differences were observed between hospitals with and without other reporting systems, with higher AEs reporting in hospitals with additional systems (p<0.001). Similar differences were observed for LW, with higher AE reporting in hospitals conducting regular LW (p<0.001).

Professionals who had received PS training reported a higher number of AEs; however, the differences between groups were not statistically significant. In contrast, higher AE reporting was observed among professionals who reported clinical simulation training. Similarly, we found significant differences according to job category, observing that consultants report more AEs than the rest (p<0.001).

Discussion

In this study, we examined the associations between organisational PS practices and healthcare professionals’ perceptions of SC as well as voluntary AE reporting. SC is influenced by multiple factors, both external and intrinsic to each professional.22

Based on the conceptual framework proposed in this study, organisational PS practices were considered heterogeneous but complementary domains that may influence safety-related behaviours and perceptions of the safety environment through different organisational mechanisms, including reporting climate, teamwork, open communication and organisational learning. The structural factors analysed in this study could be placed at a meso–micro level of influence on the SC of professionals.

Changes in SC are slow and require time and commitment from all parties. Strengthening SC remains a major concern, both internationally and in the Spanish context, with voluntary AE reporting systems widely promoted as mechanisms for organisational learning. These voluntary systems, designed to learn from near misses and reported incidents to minimise their occurrence, are one of the main strategies for reactive risk management.23 Importantly, within voluntary reporting systems, higher reporting rates should not be interpreted as a direct indicator of better PS performance but as reflecting a more positive reporting climate and greater organisational willingness to identify, discuss and learn from safety events. Understanding which factors are associated with higher levels of reporting may help inform strategies to identify and prevent failures, which may be relevant for understanding how organisations identify and respond to safety-related events.

A previous study24 reported that structural and cultural factors at each hospital influence AE and have been linked to more favourable SC perceptions among professionals.25

Leadership and management support have been established as critical elements in promoting SC.26 Creating and maintaining a strong SC requires the direct involvement of management and a personal commitment to maintaining change and continuing to move towards zero harm. Our results suggested that the presence of LWs was associated with more favourable perceptions of leadership commitment among managers and with higher AE reporting. This association should be interpreted as reflecting a more open reporting climate rather than differences in the underlying occurrence of AEs. These practices may increase the visibility of leadership engagement in the clinical setting. However, substantial variability was observed across hospitals, with different combinations of leadership involvement and varying frequencies of these activities. This variability may influence how professionals experience and perceive these initiatives and should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. Nevertheless, our results are consistent with the results of López-Picazo et al27 who reported an increase in AE reporting following the implementation of structured LWs.

A study examined the influence safety specialists on SC and PS.28 Within our proposed conceptual framework, this role may function as an organisational resource that facilitates safety coordination, knowledge exchange and organisational learning. In our bivariate analysis, the presence of PSSs was associated with more favourable SC perceptions; however, having >2 PSSs was not associated with additional benefits in the SC dimensions assessed. This finding suggests a possible non-linear or saturation pattern, whereby the association of this role may depend less on the absolute number of specialists and more on factors such as role integration, coordination and available resources. It would be necessary to examine whether the presence of PSSs may be associated with a more critical attitude towards PS among professionals, potentially leading to a more cautious assessment of SC.

Martin et al28 highlighted the importance of the role played by PSSs in motivating and promoting change within the organisation, with a broad understanding of PS, the organisation and their influence over professionals. Most of those PSSs had a high level of qualification (band ≥8). In Spain, such job segments do not exist, and specialists are not governed or identified by these levels but arise from personal motivation, involving time, generally their own, dedicated to both monitoring PS policies and continuing education in PS.29 30 The non-linear pattern observed in our results may be related to the personal motivation and level of engagement of safety specialists, as well as their professional background, which could be explored in future studies.

Discordant findings were observed for the handoffs and transitions dimension, with higher positive scores in centres without PSSs. Collado et al25 also reported less favourable perceptions in this dimension in studies examining settings with implemented safety initiatives. Although increased critical awareness associated with PSS involvement may partly explain this pattern, alternative explanations such as differences in organisational complexity, workflow structures or workload should also be considered. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution.

With regard to training, previous research has highlighted its relevance in relation to PS, with training in clinical simulation and PS being associated with the development of clinical and non-clinical skills, including leadership, communication and teamwork.26 31 In line with our conceptual framework, these educational interventions could represent a mechanism through which individual and collective safety-related capabilities are strengthened, favouring more positive perceptions of SC. In this study, PS training and clinical simulation showed a strong association with different SC dimensions in the bivariate analysis. However, this association was not maintained in the fully adjusted model in relation to voluntary AE reporting. This finding could indicate that although training may be related to greater safety awareness and perceptions of safety, it may not necessarily translate into an increase in AE reporting, which may be related to other factors.

More than half of the professionals in this study had not reported an AE in the last year. In the bivariate analysis, we observed higher levels of AE reporting in centres implementing at least one organisational PS practice. However, in the fully adjusted model, only centres that had established additional reporting mechanisms, specifically daily morning safety huddles, showed a significant increase in voluntary AE reporting. Similarly, annual ED volume was associated with higher levels of AE reporting.

According to our conceptual framework, reporting infrastructure and communication-based practices may represent organisational mechanisms that influence the reporting climate by facilitating the identification, discussion and learning from safety events. Daily morning safety huddles may therefore provide a structured opportunity for teams to share safety concerns and promote open communication, which could facilitate the identification and communication of safety concerns and may support reporting behaviours. Nevertheless, the absence of associations with other organisational practices in the adjusted model suggests that the implementation of safety initiatives alone may not be sufficient to modify reporting behaviours, which may depend on how these practices are integrated into daily clinical work. Nevertheless, fostering voluntary AE reporting remains an essential component of organisational learning and continuous PS improvement. Therefore, the higher reporting observed in centres implementing these organisational practices should be interpreted primarily as reflecting a more positive reporting climate and greater organisational willingness to identify, discuss and learn from safety events, rather than differences in the underlying occurrence of AE or PS performance. Within the occupational categories, PNR and STP (professionals in training) report fewer AEs. It is likely that their lesser work experience and knowledge of reporting systems are related to these findings, as SC and PS are still pending subjects in formal academic training.32 33 Likewise, concerns about blame, judgement or being perceived as lacking professional competence.

Several methodological considerations should be acknowledged. Given the cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be inferred. No interventions were implemented; instead, existing organisational differences and their associations with perceived SC and voluntary AE reporting were examined within a relatively homogeneous clinical setting. Therefore, the proposed framework should be interpreted as a conceptual model describing hypothesised pathways and potential mechanisms underlying these associations rather than as a causal model. In addition, predictors and outcomes were derived from the same self-reported survey, introducing the potential for common method bias. Although multivariable analysis was used to adjust for confounding, the hierarchical structure of the data—professionals clustered within hospitals—was not explicitly modelled, which may have influenced the precision of the estimates. As such, the results should be interpreted as identified factors associated with variations in perceived SC and AE reporting, which may help in determining areas for further exploration or the generation of hypotheses in future research. In addition, response rates varied across centres. Although the overall response rate was acceptable, some centres showed relatively low participation (as low as 21.1%), which may introduce non-response bias and affect the representativeness of the findings. Despite these limitations, the findings provide a snapshot of organisational practices and SC perceptions in PEDs in the Madrid region. Finally, healthcare professionals bring diverse personal and professional backgrounds that may shape their perceptions of PS. Likewise, patients and families bring their experiences and perspectives. Further exploration of these individual and relational dimensions may provide additional insights into SC in future research.

Conclusions

Different organisational PS practices were associated with more favourable perceptions of SC among healthcare professionals. However, few centres had implemented the full range of organisational PS practices examined. The presence of LWs, PSSs and additional reporting systems was associated with more favourable safety-related perceptions. PS training and clinical simulation were also associated with more favourable SC perceptions, supporting their relevance as educational strategies to strengthen safety-related knowledge, teamwork and communication.

Voluntary AE reporting may reflect an organisation’s willingness to identify, discuss and learn from safety-related events and opportunities for improvement rather than as a direct measure of PS performance. We found that additional reporting mechanisms, such as daily morning safety huddles, may be linked to a more open reporting climate by facilitating communication and organisational learning. Nevertheless, the implementation of these practices varied across centres, suggesting that their potential contribution may depend on how they are integrated into routine clinical practice and organisational processes. Further research is needed to better understand how different organisational PS practices interact and how contextual factors influence their relationship with safety culture and voluntary AE reporting.
