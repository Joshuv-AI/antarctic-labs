Executive Summary
The overall objective of this project was to produce a coal-based construction material that has up
to ~95 weight percent (wt. %) coal with physical, chemical, and thermal properties exceeding those
of ordinary Portland cement (OPC)-based construction materials. Additionally, the project aimed
to minimize external binders by implementing novel mixing techniques, while exceeding the
performance/cost ratio of OPC. Finally, the project was to demonstrate production of precast
products via the design and fabrication of products via a bench scale process. Consistent with some
of these objectives, the project successfully fabricated samples of coal-based composite materials
with >80 wt% coal with physical, chemical and thermal properties on par with cement-based
concrete. Select samples demonstrated compressive strengths with >7,000 psi and flexural strength
of >420 psi. The composite materials minimized external binders and also demonstrated durability,
as evidenced by resistance to acidic and basic solutions. Finally, larger slab and beam type samples
were produced using a process developed by the Recipient, although, the process was not semi-
continuous in nature. Taken together, the results of this project suggest that domestic coal has
potential to serve as a replacement for cementitious materials utilized in incumbent construction
technologies, which could significantly reduce the energy and emissions of the construction
industry.

1. Introduction and State of the Art
Current construction materials predominantly make use of Ordinary Portland cement (OPC), and
other calcium aluminates, which require extensive thermal treatments to produce reactive
precursors. For example, the average manufacturing energy of OPC is ~5 GJ/ton1. On the other
hand, due to environmental policies and adoption of eco-friendly practices in energy production
and delivery, the demand for coal has plummeted in the last decade. Therefore, the opportunity is
ripe to directly convert domestic coals into building materials, which would leverage coal’s low-
cost and strong carbon-carbon bonds to offer superior chemical and thermal resistance, as
compared with OPC. Simultaneously, application of coal in building materials could provide an
alternative market for this low cost and abundant domestic resource.

Coal-derived building materials have existed for several centuries in the form of carbon-magnesia
bricks, which utilize powdered coal in combination with MgO and other oxides sintered at 1,500°C
for over a day. Such bricks show exceptional temperature tolerance, exceeding 2,000°C, with high
resistance towards acids and alkali, making them the best candidates for refractory linings.2
However, coal powder is not the dominant component in such bricks. There are several other
patented works that claim direct utilization of powdered coal to produce bricks and foams through
chemical treatments and high temperature firing,3,4 though these technologies have not become
key commercial products that consume large amounts of coal. For example, a domestic company
has recently claimed coal core composites, where powdered coal is mixed with a proprietary resin
(i.e., polymer derived ceramic) to produce high strength and chemically resistant bricks, with
temperature tolerance up to 1,100°C.5 However, there is no publicly available information on the
quantity of coal utilized in their formula. Further, the technology is not yet commercial and appears
to be limited in application to only to precast construction markets (e.g., roofing tiles). Other recent
work utilized a blend of coal and Portland cement to create structural components, but the strength
drops dramatically to a mere 30% with 65% addition of coal into Portland cement.6 Academic
efforts are also exploring novel approaches to coal valorization to building materials via pyrolysis
and other thermal treatments,7 however, the scalability and economic feasibility of these
approaches have yet to be demonstrated.

Apart from the above mentioned examples, there is limited work on direct utilization of coal in
building materials. Thus, there is a significant opportunity for the direct application of coal into
construction materials, especially for cast-in-place applications, which accounts for >80% of the
OPC market.8 However, to achieve this in a cost-effective and eco-friendly manner, and compete
with the mature incumbent technologies currently used by the construction industry, where
processes and resources are optimized over several decades, any up-conversion processes of coal
must be minimized, curing conditions must be energy-efficient, and potential binders must be very
inexpensive, and preferably be sourced from other industrial waste steams to lower the cost. These
considerations were directly addressed in this project, the outcomes of which will be discussed in
the following sections of this report.

2. Proposed Technology
The popularity of cement in construction rests on the fact that it is a low-cost “glue” that can bind
other low-cost raw materials together, such as sand and gravel. To enable direct utilization of coal
in large quantities in construction, similarly we need a low-cost glue that when mixed with coal,

can create a composite can rival the performance of cement-based materials. The ability of an
arbitrary material to bind with coal is a direct function of its chemistry. As coal is predominantly
a carbon-based material, the binders must either form strong covalent bonds with carbon or exert
strong adhesion with carbonaceous surfaces. Keeping in mind the necessary economy of the ideal
glue, we recognize that chemically functionalizing non-carbon materials to achieve covalent bonds
with carbon (e.g., organometallics) may be expensive and involve hazardous materials and
protocols. Therefore, we have explored materials that have a natural tendency to adhere with coal,
are scalable, and can be further tailored to the coal matrix via advanced mixing and energy efficient
thermal treatment.

Implementing the above strategies in an economical and eco-friendly fashion requires extensive
engineering developments to i) minimize binder requirements and ii) merge the coal and glue to
make a high performance composite in an energy-efficient manner. Our core technology can use
atomizing nozzles to well-distribute (i.e., vaporize) the binder and mix it with a pre-treated ground
coal to form an extremely thin binder coating on the majority of coal particles. This concept is
borrowed from the pharmaceutical industry and dramatically reduces solvent requirements. Here
this concept ensures that the volume of the binder is low, hence the volume of the coal is
maximized. Moreover, the coal particle size(s) must be optimized to achieve the desired
performance. Further, given that carbon is an excellent electromagnetic (EM) absorber, it is
envisioned that we can further enhance this approach to achieve high temperature curing in minutes
(vs. days in carbon bricks) and in much better quality via microwave (MW) curing. As compared
to conventional curing, which works based on convective heat transfer and entails significant
energy losses (e.g., compression molding), our approach employs the more than 70% ground coal
all as “nanostructured heaters” embedded in the mix to self-heat under MW radiation and cure all
inter-particle binders/films rapidly. This strategy, combined with a low-power MW magnetron
source such as a waveguide, results in high quality in-situ curing and excellent structural,
mechanical, and longevity properties, all of which are highly desired for construction materials.
Our technology can be adopted in precast constructions materials and with minor modifications in
cast-in-place applications (Figure 1).

The coal + epoxy recipe (Alt I) was a mix of coal and minimal epoxy dispensed via atomizing
nozzle. Preliminary tests showed exceptional strength and toughness (ductility), making it
competitive in many applications. The coal + thermoplastics recipe (Alt II) used recyclable waste
thermoplastics as a binder for coal by implementing high temperature compression molding to
achieve a high degree of compaction. This alternative produced impressive strength based on
preliminary results and uses 100% recycled materials. The coal + tar pitch recipe (Alt III) produced
a ~100% carbon brick by curing coal and coal tar pitch via a microwave-kiln based process to
eliminate low efficiency heat transfer in conventional furnaces.

Alt I, II, and III were prepared as follows:

 • Alt I: To prepare this composite material, first, we ground anthracitic coal to 500 µm
 particle size. Then, we mixed it for 30 min with our proprietary epoxy and hardener in
 80:10:10 ratio wt. % using a high speed mixer to emulate an atomizing nozzle effect. With
 no compaction, we obtained a compressive strength of 3,650 ± 235 psi at 72 hrs., which is
 on par with or higher than conventional concrete (3,500 psi) while its density was 1.37
 g/cm3, more than 38% lower than conventional concrete (density = 2.25 g/cm3). With a
 200 psi compaction pressure and 20 compression-release cycles, the material’s
 compressive strength was determined to be ~7,160 ± 238 psi with a density of 1.93 g/cm3,
 which greatly exceeds the strength of concrete and the material is ~15% lighter. Similar
 grinding and compression molding on bituminous coal using Alt I yielded a material with
 a strength of 4,180 ± 196 psi and a density of 1.8 g/cm3, suggesting that our strategy would
 work well even with lower ranks of coal. The mixing energy was minimal and only ~250
 Wh for 1 kg of the coal and binder, translating to only ~$10/ton cost of processing,
 assuming an industrial cost of $0.04/kWh for electricity. The compaction was short and its
 energy was insignificant.

 • Alt II: To prepare this composite material, we mixed 80% ground coal with 20 wt. %
 shredded waste plastic, common high density polyethylene (HDPE). After compression
 molding and curing at 300°C for 30 min, the compressive strength of the material was
 determined to be 768 ± 186 psi with a density of 1.68 g/cm3. While the strength is
 significantly lower vs. the epoxy-based mixture, this alternative is still feasible.

 • Alt III: To prepare this composite material, we mixed 90% ground coal with 10 % wt. %
 tar pitch and cured the mixture in an 800 W silicon carbide microwave kiln. The resulting

 cast was determined to have a compressive strength of 369 ± 159 psi and was the weakest
 of our initial trials. However, we could observe that the tar binds the coal powder (without
 microwave curing the powders with tar do not strengthen over time) and performing
 microwave curing at higher powers could mimic kiln firing in an energy-efficient manner.

4. Project Accomplishments by Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO) Task/Subtask
Approach
The Recipient will couple a collection of advanced synthesis, fabrication, characterization, and
engineering to create novel coal-based construction materials. The project’s strategy is to apply
and further develop current data on binders, activators, ready mix design, curing, and modular
industrial design through a bottom-up approach. A set of systems-scale analyses will be performed
to understand the technoeconomics and determine the economic viability of the proposed

technology and the market penetration possibilities. Task 1-2 will shortlist several candidates from
a wide (coarse) of experiments on different recipes. The down selection criteria will be a
preliminary performance/cost ratio index (figure of merit) defined in Task 3. This will be achieved
through a series of mechanical, thermal, and chemical tests on all the recipes. Task 4 will optimize
the top five candidates by fine tuning the composition and curing parameters (a second refined
Taguchi design) to achieve properties that exceed those of OPC. At the end of Task 4, the Recipient
will demonstrate three refined recipes that surpass a comprehensive performance/cost index of
OPC. Task 5 involves the design and fabrication of a small bench scale semi-continuous process
to demonstrate relevant prototypical production in simulated environments.

Task 2. Pretreatment and Mixing
A key goal of the project is achieving a thin film of the binder over the coal particles while
minimizing the overall binder quantity. In order to achieve a cost-effective, strong recipe, the
Recipient will first investigate the coal particle size effect. Coal will be ground in a ball mill, and
a particle size analyzer will be used intermittently, to produce two rough range of particle sizes.
Next, the Recipient will mix them with the binder and cast several recipes via Taguchi design of
experiments.
Subtask 2.1 Mixing
Approach
The conventional mode of dispensing binders can be dramatically improved for homogenous
mixing. The Recipient will modify a conventional bulk cement mixer to achieve homogenous
mixing for all coal particles and binders.

Figure 2 shows a typical bulk cement mixer used for this project. We tried a smaller mixer as well
(Figure 5). Additionally, the sieves shown in Figure 2 were used to explore two different particle
sizes of coal. We modified the mixers to add an atomizing nozzle, such that the atomizing nozzle
disperses the epoxy solution to the coal while coal is being rotated in the mixer (Figure 5). A key
challenge was getting the viscosity of the epoxy solution right, such that it is able to be dispersed
from the very small orifice of the nozzle. For this purpose, we used back pressure using an air
pump. A second challenge was that the orifice of the nozzle frequently became clogged and we
had to clean it very regularly. While the unclogging of the nozzle could work for lab scale
experiments, eventually, we realized that this approach would not be scalable for industrial use.
Hence, we switched to simpler strategies, such as adding epoxies without atomizing nozzles but
doing the mixing with a higher speed via drill fan or spraying with a pressure pot (see Figure 2).

Task 3. Screening and Down selection of Recipes
Subtask 3.1 Dimensionless Figure of Merit (FM) for All Recipes
Approach
Due to the wide phase space of recipes, performance metrics, and cost of each ingredient in the
D.O.Es, the Recipient will define and use a universal dimensionless FM that incorporates both
technical performance and cost to rapidly screen realistic scenarios to concurrently evaluate
technical and commercial viabilities. Cost is an important factor since the majority of construction
materials’ success revolves around cost. At a preliminary level, the FM is defined based on a few
normalized parameters such as compressive strength (CS), flexural strength (FS), thermal stability
(TS) and material cost (MC). The normalization is with respect to OPC performance and cost
(FMCoal).
At this early stage, the capital cost is excluded, although processing costs will be added in Task 4.
Dimensionless FM provides an easy and rapid screening tool to discard the candidates that do not
have real potential. Given that none of the ingredients in the formula (except coal) are produced at
the levels comparable to an omnipresent commodity like OPC (~4 bn/yr.), initially the “cost” may
be a major penalty for all binders. Thus, all scenarios with FM >0.7 will be initially accepted -
while maintaining certain minimum values for key individual properties with a goal of further
refinement and property enhancement to increase CFM >1.2 in Task 4.

We define Figure of Merit (FM) based on four normalized parameters: compressive strength (CS),
flexural strength (FS), thermal stability (TS) and material cost (MC) as,

Subtask 3.2 Performance Tests
Approach
The compressive strength of 4”x 8” cylindrical samples from all D.O.Es will be measured. The
target strength is 4,500 psi exceeding residential concrete (~4,000 psi). Flexural strength (modulus
of rupture) will be tested via bending tests. The thermal degradation will be evaluated by recording
compressive strengths after exposing the specimen up to heat. There should be no sign of thermal
degradation at least up to 100°C. The Recipient will replicate all OPC data in identical lab
conditions as those of the D.O.Es, to ensure reliable benchmark data and eliminate unwanted
variability for comparisons. After completing all tests and tabulating feedstock costs from
wholesale vendors, the recipes that do not meet the 0.7 FM criteria will be discarded. The top five

candidates from the remaining recipes will be down selected for further performance optimization
in the next task.

Task 4. Rigorous Testing and Development of Final Recipes.
Subtask 4.1 Refined Taguchi Design of Experiments
Approach
The top five recipes obtained from the initial screening provides the opportunity to further tune
the composition, cost, processing conditions and improve their performance to match/exceed
OPC’s FM. Thus, the Recipient will design a new Taguchi D.O.E for each of the five candidates.
The base factors (F) for this optimization would be (i) binder quantity; (ii) casting pressure; (iii)
curing time; and (iv) curing temperature. Five levels (L) will be explored for each factor where the
mean of the levels is centered around the base recipe with the range spanning up to around 20% ±
mean. The refined FM includes a few additional normalized parameters for a comprehensive FM
(CFM) such as using CR as the chemical resistance against certain harsh chemicals, using OR as
the oxidation resistance, or using PC as an estimation of processing cost per ton and N as the net
carbon footprint per ton of product.

be fairer we need to consider carbon footprint and hence the Comprehensive Figure of Merit

Subtask 4.2 Comprehensive Testing and Final Down Selection
Approach
In addition to tests in Subtask 3.2, the Recipient will perform a series of comprehensive chemical
resistance tests for acids (e.g., HCl, HNO3), common alkali (e.g., NaOH, KOH), and corrosive
elements (e.g., sulfur). Chemical resistance tests will have three facets. First, after exposure to
concentrated chemicals either liquid or gaseous environments, the appearance (i.e., the
dimensional shrinkage and density) will be recorded. Second, the compressive strength and
flexural strength of the specimen will be tested. Third, the cross section of the specimen will be
obtained to visually inspect the penetration depth. The resistance to each chemical is scored on a
qualitative scale from one to five where one is poor resistance and five is full resistance. The
normalized average of resistance values for a recipe against the chemicals constitutes the chemical
resistance score for that recipe. The Recipient will also perform oxidation resistance for recipes
that exceed a thermal stability of 1,000°C by heating the specimen to 1,300°C and inspecting the
cross section.
Processing costs and net carbon footprint per ton will be estimated roughly based on literature
studied on the same or similar materials. The energy cost associated with curing, grinding,
shredding, etc. (and their associated CO2 footprints) will be estimated based on commercial
machines with projected production capacities (with ~60% inefficiency factor). This analysis will
be done carefully for all processes.

Task 5. Semi-continuous Bench Scale Technology Development
Strategies for coal-derived building materials require casting and curing requirements to achieve
the desired CFM. The Recipient will demonstrate that the overall process can be designed by
adapting commercial assembly/production line practices through carefully planned bench scale
testing. This establishes quantitative performance data and design criteria to move the bench-scale
technology to the next TRL, update the TMP, and perform technology gap analysis for pilot
manufacturing.
Subtask 5.1. Design of bench-Scale Production Rig
Approach
The Recipient will construct a semi-continuous bench scale test plant to mimic a typical assembly
and production line in relevant simulated environments. The bench-scale plant consists of three
major sectors: pre-treatment, casting, and curing. Raw materials are fed into the grinding units to
prepare the input coal. The raw materials then move onto the casting unit which mixes the slurry
homogeneously. The mixed slurry is then fed onto casting molds. The filled molds are then
compression molded between plates using a hydraulic press to achieve adequate compaction. The
molded specimens then undergo an oven curing that has a tunable holding time to apply controlled
temperatures to the molds. Convective radiation applied on the molds will homogeneously cure
the samples. Cooled samples are then demolded and stored to be used as representatives of precast
construction materials. The individual processes proposed are based on commercial assembly line

practices pertaining to materials processing, moving, measured delivery, and thermal treatment.
This enables rapid scaling of protocols making these strategies a truly disruptive technology in the
precast industry.

Subtask 5.2 Technology Gap Analysis
Approach
The Recipient will use Technology Gap Analysis (that includes data such as performance,
emissions cost, market, and safety metrics) and assess the TRL for all key components of the
technology including testing needed for scale-up and commercialization. The Recipient will
identify perceived technology gaps and R&D needed for future commercialization.

Based on our technology gap analysis, given the high cost of epoxy (for 80/20 mixes or even 90/10
mixes), there should be additional research on identifying lower cost epoxies to make the product
economically feasible while targeting lowering the wt. % of epoxy via new techniques.
Additionally, given the fire-prone attribute of coal, a fire resistant coating for this product is
recommended, to ensure safety. The current technology readiness level (TRL) is assessed to be
around 5.

Conclusion
In summary, this project successfully fabricated coal-based construction materials that have >80%
wt. % coal with physical, chemical and thermal properties on par with cement-based concrete.
Select samples demonstrated compressive strengths >7,000 psi, flexural strength >420 psi, and
excellent durability and resistance with respect to acidic and basic solutions. The project was able
to minimize external binders in the composite materials by implementing novel mixing techniques
while aiming towards the performance/cost ratio of cement. The project also successfully produced
larger laboratory scale slab and beam samples using a batch processes. Despite these successes,
the project did not meet all of its original objectives, which included leveraging microwave
technologies in a semi continuous process to develop coal derived building materials. Overall, the
results of the project suggest that coal may offer promise as a replacement in construction
applications for cement based materials, which could ultimately provide aid in the reduction of the
massive energy and emissions profiles of the construction industry.
