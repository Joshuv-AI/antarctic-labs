Resolution-Aware Perpetual Futures on Binary Prediction Markets: An Empirical Risk-Design Framework Using Polymarket Data
Maksym Nechepurenko

Abstract
We ask whether a perpetual-futures engine could have survived observed conditions on Polymarket’s binary-event prediction markets, and what its design would need to look like if so. We do not propose deployment; the contribution is a specification of a resolution-aware risk-engine framework, an empirical evaluation of that framework as specified, and the design tensions the evaluation reveals. Binary event-contract underlyings have three structural properties absent from crypto-perpetual design assumptions: bounded support with terminal collapse, structurally asymmetric depth near the boundaries, and oracle-mediated discrete resolution. Two non-portability propositions follow: standard basis-only funding paired with continuous-volatility static margin fails to constrain behavior near the boundaries ( Proposition 2 ) and provides insufficient collateral to absorb the bounded-event terminal jump ( Proposition 1 ). The propositions rest on the three structural properties only. We specify a four-component framework: a composite robust index; a jump-aware tiered margin schedule; boundary-aware funding correction; and a multi-stage resolution-zone protocol with leverage compression. Every parameter is either estimated from observable market features with a documented estimator ( Appendix C ) or designated a policy parameter and varied in sensitivity analysis ( Appendix E ). Estimators and falsifiability thresholds are pre-registered and locked before the empirical evaluation runs. Empirical evaluation uses Polymarket’s PMXT v2 archive for the calendar week 2026-04-21 to 2026-04-27 (13.7 billion events, 110,828 distinct markets, stratified-by-day analysis sample of 13,298 markets passing adequacy gates, of which 13,115 resolved within the empirical window for E3 evaluation). E1 evaluates two pre-registered stylized facts; E2 conducts counterfactual replay across three engine configurations on observed price paths; E3 isolates the resolution-zone protocol’s contribution by comparing four mechanic variants. Both E1 floors pass; three of the five materiality floors of E2 and E3 fail and two pass. The full numerical results are summarised in Section 1.5 and reported in detail in Section 8 . Two design lessons emerge from the failed floors and constitute the empirical contribution. First, the resolution-zone halt protocol addresses execution-channel liquidation risk but does not address terminal-jump bad-debt: bad-debt risk lives in the margin schedule, not the halt protocol. Second, dynamic-margin reaction pre-empts more recoveries than it prevents on observed paths — a structural trade-off replicated across the E2 and E3 counterfactual replays. The framework as specified does not achieve the order-of-magnitude liquidation-rate or bad-debt reduction hypothesized at pre-registration. The empirical evaluation supports directional welfare-side improvement and documents structural design tensions rather than validating the framework. Five framework-conditional design recommendations follow as directions for further empirical work; we do not claim a deployable product.

academic literature and the public design documents of major
platforms.

1.2
The risk-design problem

This paper studies that design problem. We refer to a margin-based
derivative on a single binary prediction-market event with no fixed
expiry as an
event-linked perpetual
(ELP). The narrow form
analyzed here — the Probability-Index Resolution-Aware Perpetual
(PIRAP), specified in plan section 4.2 and reproduced in

broader taxonomy of event-linked derivatives. We restrict attention
to PIRAP in this paper;

The central observation is structural: a binary prediction-market
price process exhibits four features that distinguish it from
unbounded spot underlyings (
Section

3.1

support in
[0,1]
replaces unbounded support in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. A
discrete terminal collapse to
\{0,1\}
at resolution time replaces a
continuous trajectory. Jump intensity scales with information arrival,
not calendar time. And liquidity in the tails of the unit interval is
asymmetrically thin, in a way that depends on the region of the price
rather than on its level alone.

These features individually appear in other contexts. Bounded
processes appear in interest-rate modeling. Terminal jumps appear in
event-study finance. Information-driven jump intensity appears in
high-frequency microstructure. The combination, however, is not
addressed by any standard derivative-pricing or risk-engine
framework.

propositions: continuous-volatility margin schedules cannot price
terminal jump risk without forcing leverage to one
(
Proposition

1

fails to deliver the relative-basis convergence that traders and
arbitrageurs rely on, in the boundary regions of the unit interval
(
Proposition

2

The propositions are structural rather than empirical: they do not
demonstrate that the failure modes occur with any particular frequency
on observed data. They demonstrate that the naive transplant of
crypto-perp mechanics is mathematically incompatible with binary-event
underlyings under stated assumptions.
Section

7
develops a
resolution-aware framework that addresses each failure mode point by
point.

1.6
Scope and limitations of empirical claims

We state explicitly what the paper does and does not do, in
declarative form, because the appropriate scope of an empirical paper
on a non-existent product is unusually delicate.

What this paper does.

It develops and evaluates a
specific risk-engine design for a specific instrument (PIRAP) on a
specific data source (Polymarket v2 archive). It documents structural
failure modes of the naive crypto-perp transplant, develops a
framework that addresses each, and measures the framework’s behavior
under counterfactual replay against observed paths. It produces a
listing eligibility framework grounded in observable market features.
It identifies four directions for future research that would extend
the empirical evidence base.

What this paper does not do.

•

It does not claim balanced cross-class evidence on
Polymarket. The pre-registered Sample Adequacy Gate
triggered the sports-dominance consequence rule on the
stratified-by-day analysis sample (sports share 77.9%,
above the 70% threshold;
Section

5.5
). The
chronological-prefix subsamples we ran first showed an even
more extreme trajectory (74.6% on 15K, 89.3% on 50K),
which the day-1 selection-bias finding identified as
chronological artifact; the stratified sample correction
returns 77.9%, intermediate between the two chronological
figures and firmly above the trigger threshold. Sports
dominance is therefore a structural property of the
empirical week’s market mix on Polymarket, not a single-day
artifact. The paper’s primary empirical claims are
sports-anchored; politics and crypto are reported as
secondary classes documenting framework behavior on classes
other than the dominant one. Broad cross-class
generalization claims are explicitly weakened where this
matters (
Sections

8
and
9.2
).

•

It does not propose a full taxonomy of event-linked
perpetuals. The taxonomy is the subject of a companion paper.
Variants beyond PIRAP — conditional-probability,
event-spread, event-basket, volatility, liquidity-index,
rolling, funding-only — are outside scope.

•

It does not develop a formal model of real-world outcome
manipulation — the manipulation of the event itself rather
than the market’s price of the event (e.g., bribing an
athlete, leaking a policy decision, perpetrating an act
intended to move an event market). Real-world outcome
manipulation analysis is the subject of a companion paper
(Paper 3 in our planned series).
Market-price
manipulation
is, however, in scope: the composite robust
index of
Section

7.2
and the governance
overlay of
Section

7.9
are designed
partly to constrain index manipulation cost

\kappa_{t}
, and the empirical evaluation in
Section

8

will measure the framework’s behavior under observed
market-price-manipulation conditions where they appear in the
sample.

•

It does not provide a cross-jurisdictional regulatory
analysis. Regulatory framing is restricted to the descriptive
statements necessary to position the work.

•

It does not construct a formal welfare model across event
classes. Welfare statements are restricted to the
framework’s empirical evaluation in
Section

8
.

•

It does not claim Polymarket-specific findings transfer to
Kalshi or other CLOB-based event venues without replication.
The framework is platform-agnostic by construction; the
empirical findings are not.

•

It does not present a deployable product. It presents a
minimum risk architecture under which a deployable product
could be evaluated.

•

It does not claim novelty in empirical microstructure

Empirical scope and the data window.

The empirical

2.1
Prediction-market microstructure on CLOB venues

The empirical literature on prediction markets has historically
focused on price-level questions: forecast accuracy, calibration
against realized outcomes, the longshot bias, and the extent to which
informed and uninformed traders coexist on the same venue

(
29
;
16
)
. This price-level focus
reflects the data available from the early venues, which used
scoring rules
(
12
)
or sparse parimutuel pools and
exposed limited continuous order-book data. The trading mechanism
mattered less than the price as an aggregator.

CLOB-based prediction markets, of which Polymarket is the prominent
recent example, change this picture. The CLOB exposes
continuous depth, spread, latency, and trade-tape data at the same
granularity as conventional spot exchanges, which makes the
microstructure questions tractable for the first time at scale. Two
recent contributions are central to our setting.

8
provides the most detailed
microstructure characterization of the Polymarket order book to date,
covering 30 billion events over 52 days on a pre-registered
600-market panel and reporting eight stylized facts including a
longshot spread premium, a depth-concentration profile closer to a
uniform geometric grid than to the top-of-book pattern often assumed
for prediction markets, and category-conditional differences in
effective spread.
27
provides a
transaction-level decomposition of the 2024 US election cycle on
Polymarket, focusing on trader behavior, market disagreement, and
manipulation risk.
26
provides a
systematization-of-knowledge survey of decentralized prediction market
designs and their trade-offs around decentralization, expressiveness,
and manipulation resistance.

Our paper differs from each in scope. Dubach is descriptive across the
entire venue and does not consider derivative overlay; the 2024
election anatomy is event-cycle-specific and does not consider
leveraged margin design; the SoK paper is design-space-focused and
does not develop a quantitative risk-engine framework. We use

8
primarily as a benchmark for
the stylized facts we report in
Section

5
: where the two
samples overlap (CLOB venue, similar event mix), our facts should be
broadly consistent. Disagreements would themselves be informative.

The earlier scoring-rule literature, beginning with

12
, deserves more careful engagement than a
forward-pointer to its successors. The Logarithmic Market Scoring
Rule established three results that remain directly relevant to any
prediction-market design, including CLOB-based ones. First, the
liquidity parameter
b
in LMSR provides bounded worst-case loss to
the market maker, a property that CLOB venues do not inherit
automatically and that motivates separate design discipline (in our
setting, the boundary funding correction of
Section

7.5

is in part a substitute for the bounded-loss guarantee that LMSR
provides through its cost function). Second, LMSR is associated with
a manipulation-cost framework: the cost to an adversary of moving the
price by a fixed amount is bounded below in a manner that depends on

b

(
3
;
4
)
.
A CLOB venue with a derivative overlay does not inherit this
manipulation-cost guarantee, and our composite robust index in

Section

7.2
is in part a response to that gap. Third,
LMSR’s path-independence and information-aggregation properties are
features of the cost function, not of the trading venue, and a
CLOB-based prediction market gives them up in exchange for direct
trader-to-trader liquidity provision.

The point is not that LMSR has been superseded; the point is that
CLOB-based venues solve a different problem, and the design
discipline that LMSR encodes (bounded loss, manipulation cost,
information aggregation) must be either reconstructed or relaxed in
the new setting. Our framework reconstructs bounded-loss and
manipulation-cost discipline through the index and margin
specifications in
Section

7
; we do not address path
independence or formal information aggregation, which we treat as
out of scope.

2.2
Perpetual-futures mechanism and funding rates

Perpetual futures (perpetual swaps) emerged in cryptocurrency markets
in 2016 with BitMEX’s introduction of the inverse bitcoin perpetual.
The mechanism is, in summary, a margin-collateralized non-expiring
contract whose price is tethered to a reference index via periodic
funding payments between long and short positions, with the funding
rate proportional to (a function of) the basis between perp and
index. The mechanism has become the dominant derivative structure in
crypto, with daily volumes exceeding 100 billion USD across major
venues by 2024.

Despite the centrality of the instrument, academic mechanism analysis
is comparatively recent.
13

develops the canonical no-arbitrage relationship between perpetual
price, spot price, and funding rate, providing the theoretical
foundation against which mechanism deviations can be measured.

7
extends this to the case
where the funding mechanism includes a clamping function (capped
funding rate), deriving model-free no-arbitrage bounds and showing
that persistent price discrepancies cannot be explained by transaction
fees alone.
30
treats the
funding rate as an algorithmic feedback rule rather than a passive
transfer, deriving stability conditions in a continuous-time
equilibrium with risk-constrained arbitrageurs and momentum
speculators, and analyzing the effect of funding-rate caps,
clamp-style piecewise-linear rules, and jump-and-crisis extensions
with liquidation-driven crashes.

Empirical work on funding-rate dynamics is sparser. The earliest
systematic analysis we are aware of is

25
, which documents the
heteroskedastic nature of BitMEX funding rates and establishes
Granger-causal relationships between funding and the inverse
perpetual price. More recent work

(
9
)
examines funding-rate
arbitrage profiles across CEX and DEX venues for major
cryptocurrencies.
10
reconciles open
interest with traded volume in perpetual swaps, providing a method
to estimate collateral requirements at exchange scale.

These references jointly motivate two design decisions in our
framework. First, the basis-only funding rule of the naive
crypto-perp port (
Definition

1
) is the canonical mechanism
analyzed in
13
and

30
; it is the natural baseline
to attack. Second, the boundary-correction term in our funding rule
(
Definition

6
) is in the same family as the modifications

7
discuss for clamping
functions, but parameterized on the index region rather than on the
funding magnitude.

2.3
Liquidation dynamics and optimal margin under leverage

The empirical literature on crypto-perpetual liquidations documents
both the frequency of forced liquidation events and the cascade
dynamics under stress.
5

applies generalized extreme value theory to BitMEX perpetual data and
finds that 3.51% of long positions and 1.89% of short positions
face forced liquidation on a daily basis at the venue’s prevailing
margin rules, with average leverage among liquidated positions of
60X. They recommend tightening initial margin requirements
substantially — to 33% (3X leverage) for longs and 20% (5X) for
shorts — to bring the daily margin call probability down to 1%.
This is one of the few empirical anchors for what

constitutes
prudent margin design under high-leverage
crypto-perp conditions.

2.4
Informed trading and adverse selection in event markets

The market microstructure tradition starting with

14
and

11
models price formation under
asymmetric information between informed and uninformed traders. The
core insight — that bid-ask spreads compensate market makers for
adverse-selection risk against informed trades — carries over to
prediction-market settings, with two complications.

First, prediction markets resolve at known terminal states, so the
informed-trader’s information edge is bounded in time. Second, the
information that matters for binary-event prices is often
event-specific (the outcome of a sports match, the result of an
election) rather than fundamental, which changes the structure of
information flow. We do not develop a formal model of informed
trading on event markets in this paper; we use the Kyle-GM framework
as background motivation for why bid-ask spreads might widen in
boundary regions where information is more concentrated, and we
defer formal modeling to future work.

2.5
Adjacent literatures we do not survey here

Four adjacent bodies of work are relevant but secondary to this
paper’s central argument. We cite them where the argument requires
and do not attempt a survey. Bounded-process pricing models (the
canonical reference being
6

on absorbing-barrier interest-rate processes) are relevant to the
formal price-process treatment in
Section

3.1
, but our
setting (two-sided absorbing boundaries with terminal collapse to

\{0,1\}

inheritance limited; we use it as a conceptual reference, not a
foundational dependency. Oracle manipulation literature (UMA papers,
oracle-attack analyses) bears on
Section

but our
manipulation analysis is primarily empirical and does not require
formal oracle modeling. Gambling vs. financial-contract distinctions

Section

but require legal expertise we do not claim.
Welfare analyses of
derivative-market introduction (Hart, Allen-Gale) are relevant to

3.1
The binary-event price process

Let
E
denote a binary event with two mutually exclusive outcomes,
indexed by
R\in\{0,1\}
. Resolution occurs at a time
\tau
.
For some event classes
\tau
is scheduled (e.g., a sports match
finishing at a known time); for others it is a random stopping time
(e.g., a political event that may or may not occur by a deadline). We
denote by
T
the time the engine treats as scheduled or expected
resolution, which need not equal
\tau
.

A prediction-market venue trades a contract that pays $1 if
R=1

and $0 if
R=0
. We denote by
p_{t}\in[0,1]
the time-
t
price
of this contract on the venue. We treat
p_{t}
as a market signal, not
a probability. The distinction matters:
p_{t}
reflects the marginal
trader’s willingness to pay under the venue’s particular order-book
state, fee schedule, capital constraints, and information set. It is
contaminated by liquidity premia, manipulation pressure, and funding
distortions in ways that the literature on prediction-market pricing
has documented at length (see
29
,

16
). For the design and empirical analysis below, we
require only that
p_{t}
is observable, bounded in
[0,1]
, and
collapses discretely to
R\in\{0,1\}
at
\tau
in the
sense that
\lim_{t\uparrow\tau}p_{t}
may differ from
R

by an arbitrary amount in
[-1,1]
.

This process has four features that distinguish it from an unbounded
spot-asset price. The empirical work in Section
5
examines
these features quantitatively; here we state them as the design
assumptions our framework is built around.

Bounded support with terminal collapse.

The process

p_{t}
takes values in the closed unit interval, in contrast to spot
crypto and equity prices which take values in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. At

\tau
, the process collapses to one of the two endpoints. This
collapse is not the end of a continuous trajectory; it is a
discontinuous jump whose magnitude is the distance between

\lim_{t\uparrow\tau}p_{t}
and
R
. The
discontinuous-jump component of the process aligns with classical
jump-diffusion modeling
(
17
)
, modified
here by the boundedness of the support: jumps are constrained to land
within
[0,1]
and the terminal jump lands exactly at
\{0,1\}
.

Information-driven jump intensity.

Jump arrival in
p_{t}

is not Poisson with calendar-time intensity. It is concentrated on
information-arrival times that are partly endogenous (debate moments,
goals scored, statistical disclosures) and partly exogenous
(unforeseen news). The implication for engine design is that

\hat{\pi}_{t}^{\mathrm{jump}}
, the engine’s estimator of jump probability over the
current margin horizon, must be conditional on observable proxies for
information arrival rather than calibrated as a stationary parameter.
We define
\hat{\pi}_{t}^{\mathrm{jump}}
as a dimensionless probability rather than a
calendar-time intensity for consistency with the margin formulae of

Section

7.3
, where it multiplies a notional position
size to produce a margin add-on.

Thin liquidity in the tails.

Empirically (Section

5
), order-book depth
0pt
varies systematically with
the region of
p_{t}
. Markets where
p_{t}\in[0,0.05]\cup[0.95,1]
exhibit different spread and depth profiles than markets in
the interior. Engines that assume depth is independent of price level
will misprice both manipulation cost and liquidation safety margin.

Resolution-time clustering.

Liquidity, trading activity, and
information arrivals all cluster around
\tau
. In a sports market,
final-minute events dominate; in a political event with a scheduled
deadline, the last hours carry disproportionate news weight. Risk
parameters that ignore
(\tau-t)
underestimate this clustering’s
contribution to liquidation severity.

These four features motivate the central design claim of the paper: a
risk engine for an ELP on this process must be
resolution-aware

by construction.

3.3
The platform context: Polymarket as a CLOB-based event venue

Our empirical observations are drawn exclusively from Polymarket, a
prediction-market platform settling binary events on the Polygon
network. We summarize the platform structure to the depth needed for
the analysis; the empirical scope statements in

Section

1.6
clarify what does and does not generalize beyond
Polymarket without replication.

Order book.

Polymarket operates a central limit order book
(CLOB) for each market. Each binary event has two outcome tokens
(YES and NO), and quotes for both tokens form complementary order books.
Trading is collateralized in USDC. This is in contrast to LMSR-based
prediction markets which use a market-maker scoring rule to provide
continuous liquidity; Polymarket relies on traditional limit-order
liquidity provision.

Resolution.

Markets are resolved through UMA’s
Optimistic Oracle (OO) protocol on Polygon. A whitelisted proposer
posts the outcome; if no dispute arrives within a liveness window, the
proposal settles and the market resolves. As of mid-2026, two oracle
infrastructures coexist within Polymarket’s adapter ecosystem: standard
UMA OOv2 (
0xee3afe...
), used for long-horizon legacy markets;
and Managed OOv2 (MOOV2,
0x2c0367...
), introduced under
UMIP-189 and used for new short-horizon markets, where proposers are
restricted to a managed whitelist with documented reliability records.
The empirical sample of this paper is operationally dominated by
MOOV2:
Section

9.3
documents the observed

>
99% MOOV2 share and its implications, including the consequence
that dispute frequency (an input to
B_{E}
) carries less
information than it would in a venue with active OOv2/MOOV2
co-dominance.

Data.

The PMXT v2 public archive provides hourly Parquet
dumps of the Polymarket CLOB WebSocket event stream. Each file contains
tick-level events with millisecond timestamps for four event types:

book
(full L2 snapshot, sparse),
price_change

(quote update with best bid/ask, dominant),
last_trade_price

(trade execution with size, side, fee, transaction hash), and

tick_size_change
(rare). Coverage begins
2026-04-13T19 UTC, which bounds the empirical window of this paper.
Section
5
describes the schema, sample selection, and
cleaning procedures in detail; Appendix
B
gives the
full schema.

We do not claim Polymarket-specific findings transfer to Kalshi or
other CLOB-based event venues without replication. The framework
developed in
Section

7
is platform-agnostic by construction;
the empirical findings in
Section

8
are not.

5.3
Archive structure: a finding from ingestion

During the ingestion work for this paper, an architectural feature of
the public PMXT v2 archive emerged that is worth documenting because
it bears on reproducibility.

PMXT exposes its archive through two distinct endpoints with
materially different behavior. The first is an HTML index at

https://archive.pmxt.dev/Polymarket/v2
, designed for human
browsing, which paginates a
rolling window of approximately 100
files at any given time
. The second is the underlying R2 storage at

https://r2v2.pmxt.dev/polymarket_orderbook_<YYYY-MM-DDTHH>.parquet
,
which is permanent and complete from the archive start (2026-04-13T19
UTC) onward.

The two endpoints can produce different views of the same archive: a
researcher who paginates the HTML index on a given day will see only
files from approximately the past four days. A researcher who
enumerates URLs against R2 directly will retrieve every file ever
published. Our downloader uses direct R2 enumeration; the
canonicality of the resulting file set is verified in our committed

MANIFEST.sha256
artifact for the primary window.

The implication for reproducibility is operational. We state it as a
rule:

Replication rule for the PMXT v2 archive.
Do not paginate
the HTML index at
archive.pmxt.dev
as the canonical file
list. Enumerate hourly URLs against

r2v2.pmxt.dev/polymarket_orderbook_<YYYY-MM-DDTHH>.parquet

by UTC hour for the desired date range, and verify every file
against the committed
MANIFEST.sha256
.

This rule is implemented by our downloader
(
ingest/download_archive.py
) and is the canonical access
pattern for any researcher reproducing or extending this work. We
note dual-endpoint access patterns are a structural feature of
public-data infrastructure on Polymarket-class venues that future
microstructure researchers should be aware of, and the reason that
committed cryptographic manifests — rather than “download from the
same URL” instructions — are the correct reproducibility primitive
for time-series venue data.

We do not characterize this dual-endpoint behavior as a defect of
PMXT. The HTML index appears designed for casual browsing and the R2
endpoint for programmatic access; the design is reasonable, but the
distinction is not currently documented in either endpoint’s surface
text and was discovered empirically.

5.4

positioning is determined by what the data supports, not by what was

Resolution-window structural ceiling.

uniformly, that the patterns generalize beyond the empirical week,
or that they generalize beyond Polymarket. They are necessary
conditions for the framework’s design discipline to be empirically
anchored; they are not sufficient conditions for any deployment
claim.

6
Why Naive Crypto-Perp Mechanics Fail

This section makes precise what we mean by the claim that perpetual-swap
mechanics imported directly from crypto fail on binary prediction-market

parameter choice within a particular family of engine designs, the
engine cannot simultaneously price terminal-jump risk and offer
meaningful leverage. The section establishes two propositions that
make this precise;

failure on observed Polymarket sequences.

Formal setup

We define the naive crypto-perp port engine

Definition 1

Assumption 1

6.2
Continuous-vol margin underestimates terminal jump risk

Proposition 1

(Static-margin shortfall under terminal collapse)
.

Under
1
and
2

a long position of size
x>0
taken at time

L
under the naive crypto-perp port
\mathcal{E}_{0}

(

\Pi_{\tau}

to the position over the interval

in the event

— specifically, any configuration

— under

\Pi_{\tau}>M_{t}^{\mathrm{init}}\quad\text{whenever}\quad p_{t}[\tau^{-}]-R>L^{-1}.

That is, the realized loss exceeds initial margin whenever the terminal
jump is large enough to deplete the leverage buffer. The shortfall

\Pi_{\tau}-M_{t}^{\mathrm{init}}
is unbounded in
L

the shortfall whenever the jump exceeds

Proof.

A long position of size
x>0

M_{t}^{\mathrm{init}}=L^{-1}\cdot x\cdot p_{t}[t_{0}]

1

because the leverage definition implies
L=|x|/M_{t}^{\mathrm{init}}

At resolution, the position’s value collapses to

The realized loss in the event

\Pi_{\tau}=x\cdot p_{t}[\tau^{-}]-x\cdot R=x\cdot p_{t}[\tau^{-}]\quad\text{(since }R=0\text{)}

relative to the pre-collapse mark. The shortfall against initial
margin is

\Pi_{\tau}-M_{t}^{\mathrm{init}}=x\cdot p_{t}[\tau^{-}]-L^{-1}\cdot x\cdot p_{t}[t_{0}].

We treat two regimes separately:

Regime A:
p_{t}[\tau^{-}]\geq p_{t}[t_{0}]
(the underlying

The shortfall is

\Pi_{\tau}-M_{t}^{\mathrm{init}}\;\geq\;x\cdot p_{t}[t_{0}](1-L^{-1})\;>\;0\quad\text{whenever }L>1.

The terminal collapse exceeds the margin buffer by at least

x\cdot p_{t}[t_{0}](1-L^{-1})

Regime B:
p_{t}[\tau^{-}]<p_{t}[t_{0}]
(the underlying moved

The shortfall is

\Pi_{\tau}-M_{t}^{\mathrm{init}}\;=\;x\cdot\big(p_{t}[\tau^{-}]-L^{-1}\cdot p_{t}[t_{0}]\big),

p_{t}[\tau^{-}]/p_{t}[t_{0}]>L^{-1}

In both regimes the shortfall grows linearly in

loss
x\cdot p_{t}[t_{0}]
trivially.

2

\bar{\sigma}
that
reflects only continuous-path variation, while

p_{t}[\tau^{-}]-R

absent from

cannot be calibrated
to cover the jump using
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
alone.

The only configuration of
\mathcal{E}_{0}
that prevents shortfall is

∎

6.3
Basis-only funding under bounded support

The funding-rate proposition is more subtle than the margin one.
Basis-only funding is a well-defined mechanism; the question is whether
it preserves the equilibrium properties it provides on unbounded spot
underlyings.

We frame the issue as follows. On an unbounded spot underlying, the
basis
q_{t}-I_{t}
is small in proportion to
I_{t}
in
equilibrium, because arbitrageurs can always fund a hedged position
via cash-and-carry. On a bounded prediction-market underlying with

I_{t}\in[0,1]
, two structural differences hold simultaneously:
the magnitude of
I_{t}
is bounded above by 1, and at the boundaries

I_{t}\to 0
or
I_{t}\to 1
, depth is asymmetrically thin
(
Section

3.1
). The combination produces funding-rate
behavior that does not converge in the same way.

Assumption 3

(Boundary depth asymmetry)
.

There exists
\delta>0
such that for all
t<\tau
with

I_{t}<\delta
, the depth available to short the perp at prices
near
I_{t}
exceeds the depth available to long it by at least a
factor
\rho>1
. The symmetric condition holds for
I_{t}>1-\delta
with the roles of long and short swapped.

3
formalizes the empirical regularity of

Section

3.1
: near the boundaries, one side of the book
is structurally thinner than the other, because the bounded support
makes one direction nearly costless to lean against (the price cannot
go below 0 or above 1) while the other faces the full risk of a
mean-reverting move.

Liquidity diagnostic.

The framework’s resolution-zone
protocol is informed by an additional empirical liquidity condition.
This condition is not a structural assumption alongside
Assumptions 1–3: it is a post-failure diagnostic refinement that
emerged when a pre-registered prediction failed on its registered
measure (
Section

5.6
, SF9;
Section

9.9
).
We label it accordingly and present its formal statement as a
diagnostic empirical condition rather than as a pre-registered
structural hypothesis.

Empirical Condition 1

(Near-mid depth structurally sparse)
.

There exists a near-mid depth window

\Delta_{\mathrm{near}}\leq 50
basis points such that, for
markets resolving at
\tau

within
\pm\Delta_{\mathrm{near}}

prevailing mid is empirically near zero throughout the market
lifecycle, and in particular satisfies

\mathbb{E}\!\left[D^{\mathrm{near}}_{t}\,\middle|\,t\in[\tau-\Delta_{R},\tau)\right]\;\approx\;0

for any horizon
\Delta_{R}
.

Empirical motivation.

1

from a failed pre-registered prediction. We initially formulated a
liquidity assumption as a contraction claim on total depth (within

\pm 200
basis points of mid), evaluated by SF9 in

Section

5.6

total depth would contract toward resolution. SF9 inverted this
expectation: median total displayed depth grows nearly fivefold
across the time-to-resolution range
[12\text{h},1\text{h})
(from

8{,}940
USDC to
43{,}908
USDC at the median market). On the
registered measure, the contraction prediction failed.

The failure motivated a diagnostic refinement, not a retroactive

hypothesis that survived (H2) is that the depth measured at
\pm 200

0.27
(

We treat the resulting condition as a

Implications for Proposition 1.

The connection between the diagnostic condition and Proposition 1 is
adjacency, not strengthening of the proposition itself.

Proposition

1
establishes
collateral
insufficiency
: under
1

maintenance margin lacks the buffer to absorb a terminal jump. This
is a margin-side claim, not a microstructure claim, and it does not
depend on
1
.

1

execution-liquidity channel
: during forced liquidation in

[\tau-\Delta_{R},\tau)

near-mid depth because near-mid depth is empirically near zero.
Liquidation must therefore either fail (if the engine attempts to
execute at the prevailing mid) or walk the book through
out-of-the-money convergence-trade ladders at progressively worse
prices (if the engine accepts spread crossing). Either path
amplifies realized loss against any margin buffer that was
insufficient under Proposition 1’s collateral channel. The two
channels are adjacent failure modes that compound, not a single
strengthened proposition. The framework’s leverage compression
schedule (
Definition

reduces position size before the resolution zone, which both lowers
the collateral requirement (Proposition 1 channel) and bounds the
damage from executing against the OTM ladders (execution-liquidity

Implications for Proposition 2.

Proposition

2
relies on
3

1

informative for interpretation: in the boundary region near

We do not formalize this as a strengthened proposition because the
qualitative conclusion — basis-only funding fails to constrain

(q_{t}-I_{t})/I_{t}

diagnostic condition adds quantitative interpretation context, not a
separate formal claim.

Proposition 2

(Basis-only funding does not constrain mark–index

.

Under
1
and
3

\mathcal{E}_{0}
with funding

Specifically, for any

can exceed

Proof.

Funding

is bounded in magnitude. Since both

and

hence

uniformly.

The funding mechanism’s economic role is to push

toward

by charging long positions when

and
crediting them when
q_{t}<I_{t}

c\cdot|q_{t}-I_{t}|

In the boundary region

, not

By
3

allows

to deviate from

on one side more easily than
the other. Equilibrating arbitrage, which on unbounded underlyings
produces

|q_{t}-I_{t}|\to 0

∎

Proposition

2
is weaker than
Proposition

1
.
It does not prove that basis-only funding produces unbounded losses;
it proves that basis-only funding fails to deliver the relative-basis
convergence that traders (and arbitrageurs) rely on near the
boundaries.

Why relative basis matters economically.

The proposition’s failure metric is the relative deviation

(q_{t}-I_{t})/I_{t}
, not the absolute basis. The
mathematical statement that this ratio can grow unbounded as
I_{t}\to 0
is not by itself an economic claim. The economic content lies
in why arbitrage does not close the relative basis even when
participants observe it.

In an unbounded spot market, an arbitrageur observing
q_{t}>I_{t}
short-sells the perpetual and goes long the spot, holding
the hedge while funding payments accrue. The position is
self-financing in expectation: funding compensates for the basis
cost, the hedge is approximately delta-neutral, and the trade closes
when the basis converges. Convergence is enforced by the
near-symmetric availability of liquidity on both sides of
I_{t}
.

On a bounded prediction-market underlying near the lower boundary,
this mechanism breaks asymmetrically. By
3
,
depth available to short the perp at prices near
I_{t}<\delta

exceeds depth available to long it by a factor of at least
\rho>1
;
the arbitrageur attempting to close a positive basis
q_{t}>I_{t}
by shorting the perp and longing the underlying token must
acquire the underlying token in a thin book. The marginal cost per
unit of corrective trade scales with the slope of the depth curve on
the thin side, which is steep precisely when
I_{t}
is small. The
arbitrageur faces a non-trivial price impact on the long leg of the
trade, and the basis-funding payment
c\cdot(q_{t}-I_{t})
—
which is bounded by
c
in absolute terms, since
|q_{t}-I_{t}|\leq 1
— is insufficient to compensate for the impact when the
relative basis is large but the absolute basis is small.

Concretely: the arbitrageur’s per-period profit from closing a
relative basis of size
\beta=(q_{t}-I_{t})/I_{t}
is

c\cdot I_{t}\cdot\beta
in funding terms, which approaches zero
as
I_{t}\to 0
. The arbitrageur’s per-period cost from the
depth-asymmetric impact is bounded below by a positive constant when
the long leg of the trade depletes the thin side of the book. The
profit-cost gap means corrective arbitrage is not pursued at small

I_{t}
, and the relative basis persists. This is the economic
content of
Proposition

2
: the failure is not that
relative basis blows up arithmetically, but that the basis-only
funding mechanism does not pay arbitrageurs enough to close it under
the asymmetric-depth conditions that obtain in boundary regions.

Section

8.2
tests the risk-engine consequences of this
mechanism on observed boundary-region paths under C0, but
does not identify arbitrageur behavior directly; direct
arbitrageur-flow measurement is deferred to Paper 4

(
20
)
.
The economic claim above is the mechanism we attribute to the
results; the empirical content lives in the comparison of
C0 and C2 on observed paths.

The economic implication is that perp positions on
boundary-region markets carry hidden carry risk that the funding
mechanism does not price. This motivates the resolution-aware funding
rule of
Section

7.5
, which adds a boundary-correction
term sensitive to the region of
I_{t}
rather than to absolute
basis alone.

Resolution-Aware Risk-Engine Framework

This section specifies the resolution-aware risk-engine framework that
addresses the failure modes identified in

The framework is parametrized on observable market features, with no

7.1
Parameter discipline

We replace the engineering convention “no free parameters,” which is
practically unattainable, with a more honest commitment.

Definition 2

(Parameter classification)
.

Each parameter of the engine is exactly one of:

1.

Estimated.
The parameter has a documented estimator
that takes observable market data as input and returns a value.
The estimator is deterministic; given the same input, it
produces the same output.

2.

Policy-calibrated.
The parameter is set by design
choice, not estimation. It has a stated sensitivity range over
which the engine’s behavior is reported. The sensitivity range
is documented in Appendix
C
.

No parameter is hand-tuned without disclosure.
Free parameter
—
i.e., one that is neither estimated nor varied in sensitivity —
does not appear in this engine.

This discipline matters because risk engines in practice have many
parameters that look small but matter materially: confidence levels for
quantile estimators, lookback window lengths, maintenance fractions,
halt-window durations, and so on. We do not pretend these are
estimated; we declare them policy choices and report sensitivity.
Appendix
C
lists every parameter, its classification,
its estimator (if estimated) or its sensitivity range (if policy).

The engine specified below addresses six design surfaces: index
construction, margin, leverage compression, funding, liquidation, and
resolution-zone handling. We then describe a two-tier listing
eligibility framework. Throughout, we use the canonical instrument
PIRAP defined in plan §4.2 and reproduced in
Section

3.2
.

7.2
Composite robust index
I_{t}

The index price is the ground-truth signal the engine compares against
the perp’s mark
q_{t}

properties: how reliably the engine measures basis (and therefore

engine’s perception of the underlying price.

We construct

penalized for thinness.

Estimated parameters:

7.3
Jump-aware tiered margin
M_{t}^{\mathrm{init}},M_{t}^{\mathrm{maint}}

Proposition

1
established that continuous-vol
margin cannot price terminal jump risk without forcing
L=1
. The
resolution is to separate margin into two components: one priced
against continuous-path volatility, one priced against expected
terminal jump severity, with the second component dominating as

(T-t)\to 0
.

This decomposition aligns the margin design with the coherent
risk-measure framework of
2
:
the total margin requirement is sub-additive across the two
components, scales positively with position size (positive
homogeneity), and is monotone in either source of risk. The
margin formula does not, however, claim to be a coherent risk
measure in the formal sense; it is a parameterized rule
calibrated for the specific bounded-support setting of

Section

3
, with the coherent-risk-measure literature as
its design reference point.

Definition 4

(Jump-aware tiered margin)
.

The initial margin is

M_{t}^{\mathrm{init}}=m_{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\sigma}_{t}\cdot|x|+m_{J}\cdot\hat{\pi}_{t}^{\mathrm{jump}}\cdot|x|\cdot\phi((T-t),I_{t})+\Delta M(|x|,0pt)

(3)

where
|x|
is position notional,
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
is the windowed
realized-volatility estimator from
Section

3.1
,

\hat{\pi}_{t}^{\mathrm{jump}}
is the engine’s estimator of the probability of a
terminal jump within the current margin horizon
(dimensionless, in
[0,1]
; see Appendix
C
for the
estimator),
\phi
is a monotone-increasing function of expected
terminal jump severity given time-to-resolution and current index
level, and
\Delta M
is a position-size add-on increasing in
|x|

relative to
0pt
.

Maintenance margin is
M_{t}^{\mathrm{maint}}=\mu\cdot M_{t}^{\mathrm{init}}
for
\mu\in(0,1)
, a policy-calibrated maintenance fraction.

Calibration note on the continuous-volatility term.

An
earlier formulation of
Definition

4
included an additional

p_{t}
multiplier on the continuous-volatility term, encoding
binary-option delta scaling
(
M_{t}^{\mathrm{init,vol}}\propto\hat{\sigma}_{t}\cdot|x|\cdot p_{t}
).
This formulation was empirically problematic when compared against
the naive crypto-perp baseline
C0
, which holds

m_{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\sigma}_{t}\cdot|x|
without delta scaling: at
typical Polymarket prices (
p_{t}\in[0.3,0.7]
), the framework’s
continuous-vol margin was structurally smaller than the baseline’s,
leaving framework-engine positions under-margined throughout the
market lifecycle. The empirical evaluation in
Section

8.2

documents this as a calibration finding: the original
\cdot p_{t}

multiplier introduced an apples-to-oranges comparison with the
baseline. The formulation above is the corrected one and is the
formulation evaluated in
C2
in
Section

8.2
.
Delta-scaling intuition is preserved through the jump-aware
component, which depends on
I_{t}
via
\phi((T-t),I_{t})
.

The function
\phi((T-t),I_{t})
is the technical content of
this margin schedule. It must satisfy three properties:

1.

\phi(0,I_{t})=\max(I_{t},1-I_{t})
. At
resolution, expected terminal jump magnitude equals the larger
of the distances to the two endpoints, since the price will
collapse to one of them.

2.

\phi((T-t),I_{t})\to 0
as
(T-t)\to\infty
. Far
from resolution, the jump is far in the future; its
contribution to current margin is small because the position
can be exited or repriced before the jump.

3.

\phi
is monotone-decreasing in
(T-t)
for fixed
I_{t}
.
Closer to resolution, the contribution rises.

A simple functional form satisfying these properties is

\phi((T-t),I_{t})=\max(I_{t},1-I_{t})\cdot\exp\!\left(-(T-t)/\tau_{\phi}\right),

(4)

with
\tau_{\phi}
a policy-calibrated time scale. Other forms are
admissible; we use this one as the baseline in

Sections

8.2
and
8.3
and report sensitivity to alternatives
in Appendix
E
.

The position-size add-on
\Delta M(|x|,0pt)
addresses the case
where a position is large enough relative to depth that liquidating it
would itself move the price. It penalizes positions whose notional
exceeds a stated fraction of
0pt
. Without this add-on, an engine
with sufficient
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
and
\hat{\pi}_{t}^{\mathrm{jump}}
inputs could still face
liquidation cascades from large-position unwind.

This margin schedule resolves
Proposition

1
.
The shortfall the proposition identifies is bounded above by

|x|\cdot p_{t}
(the maximum terminal loss for a long), and at

(T-t)=0
the jump component of
M_{t}^{\mathrm{init}}
equals

m_{J}\cdot\hat{\pi}_{t}^{\mathrm{jump}}\cdot|x|\cdot\max(I_{t},1-I_{t})
. Setting

m_{J}
such that this exceeds
|x|\cdot I_{t}
at
(T-t)=0

covers the worst-case loss without forcing
L=1
uniformly: leverage

L
greater than 1 is feasible at
(T-t)>0
, with
L\to 1
as

(T-t)\to 0
. The trade-off identified in

Proposition

1
is dissolved by making the
trade-off parameter
L
time-dependent, which is exactly what the
naive port refused to do.

7.4
Time-to-resolution leverage compression
L_{\max}(t)

The leverage compression schedule formalizes the time-dependence of the
maximum admissible leverage that is implicit in

Definition

4
.

Definition 5

(Leverage compression schedule)
.

The maximum admissible leverage is

L_{\max}(t)=\min\!\left(L^{\ast},\frac{1}{m_{\sigma}\hat{\sigma}_{t}+m_{J}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{\mathrm{jump}}\phi((T-t),I_{t})}\right),

(5)

where
L^{\ast}
is a stated absolute cap (e.g.,
L^{\ast}=10
).

The schedule has three properties relevant to the failure-mode
discussion. First,
L_{\max}(t)\to 1
as
(T-t)\to 0
, because
\phi\to\max(I_{t},1-I_{t})
and the second term in the denominator
saturates the inversion. This is the leverage compression: by
construction, no trader holds leverage greater than 1 at the moment of
resolution. Second,
L_{\max}(t)\leq L^{\ast}
uniformly, providing a hard
ceiling away from resolution. Third,
L_{\max}(t)
depends on
I_{t}
as
well as
(T-t)
: positions in the boundary regions of the unit
interval face tighter caps because
\phi
is larger there.

The schedule is policy-calibrated through
L^{\ast}
,
m_{\sigma}
,
m_{J}
,
and the time scale
\tau_{\phi}
embedded in
\phi
. Sensitivity to the
combined choice is reported in Appendix
E
.

7.5
Funding rule
F_{t}

Proposition

2
established that basis-only funding
fails to constrain relative mark-index deviation near the boundaries of
the unit interval. The resolution is to add a boundary-correction term
that scales with the relative deviation, not the absolute basis.

Definition 6

(Resolution-aware funding rule)
.

The funding rate is

F_{t}=\alpha((T-t))\cdot(q_{t}-I_{t})+\beta((T-t))\cdot\mathrm{boundarycorr}(I_{t})\cdot\mathrm{relbasis}(q_{t},I_{t}),

(6)

where
\mathrm{boundarycorr}(I_{t})=\mathbf{1}\!\left[I_{t}<\delta_{b}\text{ or }I_{t}>1-\delta_{b}\right]
activates the
correction in boundary regions (with
\delta_{b}
a policy-calibrated
threshold), and
\mathrm{relbasis}(q_{t},I_{t})=(q_{t}-I_{t})/\min(I_{t},1-I_{t})
measures the relative deviation
scaled by distance to the nearest boundary. The weights
\alpha,\beta
are monotone-increasing in
|(T-t)|^{-1}
, so funding pressure
strengthens as resolution approaches.

The economic role of the boundary-correction term is to penalize
relative deviations that would otherwise leave
F_{t}
small in
absolute terms.
Proposition

2
relied on the fact that

|F_{t}|\leq c
uniformly under basis-only funding; here, the second
term grows without bound as
I_{t}\to 0
for fixed
q_{t}-I_{t}>0
, restoring the equilibrating pressure on relative basis.

The funding rule is more aggressive than the naive port near boundaries
and near resolution. We do not claim this functional form is optimal.
We claim that without the boundary-correction term, basis-only funding
fails to converge in the relative-basis sense, as Proposition 2 shows.
With the correction, the trader’s relative carry cost is bounded
proportional to the relative deviation.
Section

8.2
evaluates
the funding stability under this rule on observed paths.

7.6
Liquidation protocol

The liquidation protocol determines how positions are closed when
account equity falls below maintenance margin. The naive crypto-perp
port liquidates positions in full at market when the threshold is
breached, which generates feedback into
I_{t}
when liquidations
are large relative to depth.

We adopt three modifications.

Partial liquidation in tranches.

Position is liquidated in
discrete tranches sized as a function of
0pt
: at most a fraction

\eta_{\mathrm{trnch}}
of
0pt
per liquidation step. Subsequent
tranches are spaced in time by an interval
\Delta_{\mathrm{trnch}}

allowing book recovery between steps. Both
\eta_{\mathrm{trnch}}
and

\Delta_{\mathrm{trnch}}
are policy-calibrated.

Anti-cascade dampening.

When the OI-weighted liquidation
pressure
\Lambda_{t}
exceeds a depth-relative threshold (i.e., the queue
of pending liquidations is large relative to current
0pt
),
liquidation throughput is throttled. This trades faster individual
liquidations for slower aggregate liquidations during stress, sacrificing
some single-trader fairness for system-level stability.

Insurance-fund interaction.

Shortfall from a liquidation
that closes below maintenance margin is socialized to an insurance
fund. The fund’s solvency is tracked separately and reported as part
of the engine’s state.
Section

8.2
measures bad-debt proxy
events as instances where the fund would have been required.

These three modifications are standard in mature crypto-perp
deployments. We include them here primarily to ensure the framework
does not fail at this layer for reasons orthogonal to our central
claims about margin and funding.

7.7
Resolution-zone protocol (multi-stage)

The resolution zone is the period
[\tau-\Delta_{\mathrm{zone}},\tau]
in which the underlying is most exposed to a discrete
collapse. The protocol closes the perpetual gracefully rather than
abruptly.

Definition 7

(Multi-stage resolution-zone protocol)
.

The protocol has three stages.

Stage A: leverage compression.

At
\tau-\Delta_{\mathrm{A}}
,

L_{\max}(t)
is forced to 1 regardless of the running schedule from

Definition

5
. New positions cannot be opened
with leverage; existing positions are required to deleverage
(post additional margin or close partially) before

\tau-\Delta_{\mathrm{B}}
, where
\Delta_{\mathrm{B}}<\Delta_{\mathrm{A}}
.

Stage B: trading halt.

Between
\tau-\Delta_{\mathrm{B}}

and
\tau
, the perp market is halted. No new orders are
accepted and existing orders are cancelled. Open interest at
the halt is recorded.

Stage C: cash settlement.

At
\tau
the oracle posts the
outcome
R
, subject to a dispute window of length

\Delta_{\mathrm{disp}}
. After the dispute window closes
without dispute, all open positions settle to cash at
R\in\{0,1\}
. If the dispute window resolves to a different
outcome via the oracle’s escalation mechanism, settlement
proceeds at the disputed outcome.

The intervals
\Delta_{\mathrm{A}},\Delta_{\mathrm{B}},\Delta_{\mathrm{disp}}
are policy-calibrated. We compare the
multi-stage protocol against three alternatives in
Section

8.3
:
naive forced expiry at
\tau
(
M0
), leverage
compression alone (
M1
), and compression plus trading halt
(
M2
). The comparison is the empirical content of E3.

Why a halt rather than walking the book.

An alternative
to the multi-stage protocol would be to leave the engine to liquidate
positions through whatever depth is available, accepting that
walking the book through deep out-of-the-money quotes incurs spread
cost. The diagnostic finding of
1
(near-mid
depth empirically zero) does not by itself rule out this alternative:
total displayed depth in the final hour is non-trivial
(
Section

5.6
, SF9), and an engine willing to absorb the
spread could in principle access it. The framework adopts the halt
for two reasons. First, the median half-spread at mid-region prices
of
0.27
(
Section

5.6
, SF4) is large enough that walking
the book at typical leverage levels would generate execution losses
exceeding the maintenance margin buffer in the median resolved
market; we develop this argument in

Section

9.9
. Second, walking the book through
convergence-trade ladders concentrates execution against the
specific market makers who placed those ladders, providing a final-hour
cross-trade channel that is exposed to insider-information correlation
with the resolution outcome. The staged halt closes both channels.
E2b provides direct empirical evaluation of the first reason by
comparing insurance-fund drawdown across
C0
(no
compression, walking-book execution permitted) and
C2

(compression and staged halt) on observed paths.

7.8
Listing eligibility — Quantitative Suitability
\mathcal{S}^{A}_{E}

The engine described above is not sufficient by itself; it only
becomes meaningful when the underlying market satisfies minimum
suitability conditions.

Section

3.1
introduced four properties that distinguish
binary-event prices; some markets exhibit these properties to a degree
that makes them unsuitable for leveraged perpetual exposure regardless
of engine sophistication. The listing eligibility framework formalizes
this judgment.

We split the framework into a quantitative score from observable
features (
\mathcal{S}^{A}_{E}
, this subsection) and a qualitative governance
overlay (
B_{E}
,
Section

7.9
). The two
are reported separately so that quantitative defensibility is not
contaminated by qualitative judgment.

Definition 8

(Quantitative suitability score)
.

For an event
E
with observable history of length
w_{\mathcal{S}}

prior to listing decision,
\mathcal{S}^{A}_{E}\in[0,1]
aggregates six
dimensions:

1.

Liquidity adequacy.
Time-averaged depth-within-200bps,
normalized to the percentile of the event class.

2.

Spread regime.
Quoted-spread distribution; tail-spread
percentile.

3.

Depth stability.
Depth volatility and depth asymmetry
between bid and ask sides.

4.

Jump containment.
Empirical jump-size distribution;
tail mass beyond a stated threshold.

5.

Stale-quote rate.
Fraction of time with no quote update
within a stated window.

6.

Resolution-timing regularity.
Distance between
scheduled
T
and observed resolution times for similar
events, where applicable.

Each dimension produces a value in
[0,1]
via percentile mapping
within the event class. Aggregation is a weighted geometric mean with
weights summing to 1; weights are policy-calibrated and reported
separately.

The eligibility threshold
\mathcal{S}^{A}_{E}\geq\mathcal{S}^{A,\min}_{E}
is
stated ex ante in Appendix
C
. We do not claim the
threshold is universally appropriate; we claim that markets falling
below it have observable properties that the framework cannot
compensate for.

7.9
Listing eligibility — Governance Overlay
B_{E}

The governance overlay tracks qualitative integrity flags that are not
captured by observable market microstructure. These flags are

not
aggregated into a single score, because doing so would
require quantification of qualitative judgments that the available
data does not support.

Definition 9

(Governance overlay flag set)
.

B_{E}
is a flag set with the following entries, each binary
or graded:

Insider-information exposure.

Qualitative; rated high for
individual-athlete sports outcomes, small-committee policy
decisions, and other classes where information asymmetry is
structural rather than incidental.

Resolution-text ambiguity.

Qualitative, based on documented
dispute history in similar events.

Real-world manipulation surface.

Qualitative; rated by
feasibility of moving the event outcome relative to expected
open interest at scale.

Concentration risk.

Observable but qualitative-coded; large
fraction of volume from a small number of addresses, where
address-level data is available.

Each flag is reported separately. The paper does not collapse

B_{E}
into a single number.

Default no-list categories.

The framework recommends that
the following classes are not listed at any leverage:

•

Individual-athlete sports outcomes with material insider
information (where insiders include athletes, coaching staff,
team management, and medical staff).

•

Small-committee policy decisions where committee members face
material conflicts of interest.

•

Markets with documented unresolved disputes in materially
similar events.

These are recommendations grounded in the framework, not legal claims.

Section

9.6
states explicitly what we do not claim about the
legal status of these categories.

7.10
Listing decision rule

A candidate event
E
is eligible for listing if and only if

\mathcal{S}^{A}_{E}\geq\mathcal{S}^{A,\min}_{E}

and

B_{E}
does
not trigger a default no-list category. The two conditions are
reported separately so that quantitative defensibility is not
contaminated by qualitative judgment, and so that a platform applying
the framework can substitute its own governance overlay rules without
modifying the quantitative score.

This concludes the specification of the resolution-aware framework.

Limitations

We state limitations in declarative form, in five categories:
data-window constraints, platform specificity, oracle-mechanism

9.1
Data window

The empirical analysis is bounded below by the start of the PMXT v2
public archive on 2026-04-13T19 UTC. The 2024 US presidential
election cycle, the most salient prediction-market event in
Polymarket’s recent history, is outside this window. The politics
class in our sample consists of political markets resolving within
the 2026-04-13 to 2026-05-05 window, not high-salience election
markets. A reader interpreting the paper’s politics-class findings as
representative of behavior on election markets would be incorrect.

A separate window-related limitation is that long-horizon markets —
those created before 2026-04-13 and still live within the window —
have only their tail observed in the archive. For these markets, the
engine’s sample of pre-resolution behavior is genuinely available;
the engine’s sample of pre-listing or early-life behavior is not. We
exclude pre-listing eligibility analysis on long-horizon markets from
the empirical core, treating those markets only on dimensions where
within-window observations suffice.

A third window-related limitation: the v1 archive (Polymarket data
prior to v2) is documented as having
\sim
50% market coverage gaps and
is not used as a primary source. We do not attempt to back-fill v2
findings against v1 data; the methodological implications of mixing
incomplete v1 with complete v2 outweigh the benefit of a longer
nominal window.

9.2
Platform specificity

All empirical findings in this paper are Polymarket-specific within
the v2 window. We do not claim findings transfer to Kalshi, Manifold,
or other CLOB-based event venues. The framework of
Section

7

is platform-agnostic by construction — its inputs are observable
microstructure features, not Polymarket-specific identifiers — but
the empirical evaluation is not. Cross-platform replication is
explicitly Stage P2 of the staged research evaluation sequence
(
Section

10.2
), and is outside the scope of this paper.

A specific risk for cross-platform generalization: Polymarket is a
hybrid-decentralized venue with USDC collateral on Polygon, while
Kalshi is a CFTC-regulated venue with USD collateral. Trader
populations, regulatory exposure, manipulation incentives, and
operational risk profiles differ in ways that microstructure
measurements alone cannot capture.

9.3
Oracle-mechanism transition: de facto single-mechanism
within the empirical window

The Polymarket resolution oracle has undergone a mechanism
transition during 2025. The legacy mechanism is the UMA Optimistic
Oracle V2 (OOv2), used for long-horizon markets created before the
August 2025 governance migration (UMIP-189). The current mechanism
is the Managed Optimistic Oracle V2 (MOOV2), which restricts
proposers to a managed whitelist with documented reliability
records and is accessed via Polymarket’s Gamma API

outcomePrices
field.

We documented in
Section

5.5
that 99.8% of
classification-enriched markets in the empirical window resolve
through MOOV2, with
<0.2\%
through OOv2. For the empirical week
2026-04-21 to 2026-04-27, the analysis sample is therefore a de
facto single-oracle population: MOOV2 is the operational oracle for
new market creation, OOv2 carries only the residual tail of
long-horizon markets created before the migration. We did not
anticipate this distribution at the design stage; an earlier
revision of this paper described OOv2 and MOOV2 as “two oracle
mechanisms active in the empirical window” and discussed
heterogeneous baseline dispute frequencies as a cross-class
interpretation concern. The empirical evidence has updated this
framing.

Implications for the present paper:

•

The mechanism-heterogeneity concern about cross-class

9.6
What the paper cannot establish

Three classes of claim the paper does not address.

Real-world outcome manipulation.

The framework analyzes
manipulation of market price (via the index manipulation cost

\kappa_{t}
and the depth-aware liquidation rules); it does not
analyze manipulation of the underlying event (e.g., bribing an
athlete, leaking a policy decision, perpetrating an act intended to
move an event market). Real-world outcome manipulation is a distinct
problem with distinct incentives and is the subject of a companion
paper (Paper 3 in our planned series). Statements in this paper
about manipulation refer to market-price manipulation only.

Welfare across trader populations.

The empirical
evaluation in
Section

8
reports survivability and continuity
metrics on observed paths under counterfactual engine configurations.
It does not report welfare distributions across distinct trader
populations (retail vs. professional, informed vs. uninformed,
hedger vs. speculator). The synthetic trader-population layer in
E2c is a robustness layer, not a welfare-distribution model. A
welfare analysis would require either real account-level data
(unavailable) or a calibrated structural model (not specified).

Regulatory compliance.

The paper makes no claim about
the regulatory status of any particular implementation of an
event-linked perpetual product in any specific jurisdiction. Existing
regulatory frameworks for event contracts (CFTC in the US,
jurisdictional analogues elsewhere) and for leveraged derivatives are
distinct, evolving, and require legal expertise the authors do not
claim. The pilot path in
Section

10
explicitly defers
jurisdiction-specific regulatory review to platforms that choose to
adopt it.

The liquidation-walking alternative.

The diagnostic
finding rests on an implicit assumption that warrants explicit
discussion: the engine liquidates at the prevailing mid. An
alternative engine design — one that walks the book through the
deep out-of-the-money convergence-trade ladders, accepting the
spread cost — could in principle execute liquidations even when
near-mid depth is empty.

The total displayed depth observed in SF9 within
\pm 200
basis
points of mid is non-trivial in the final hour (median 51,573
USDC at the analysis sample). Walking the book to access this depth
is therefore not impossible; it is costly. The question is whether
the cost is bounded enough that an engine could absorb it within
its margin envelope, dispensing with the need for leverage
compression.

The empirical answer points toward the framework’s design. SF4
documents that effective half-spreads at mid-region prices are

0.27
. At a leverage level
L=5
, the maintenance margin buffer
is approximately
0.20
of position notional. Walking the book
through depth at progressively worse prices in the mid region means
the realized execution price differs from the prevailing mid by at
least the half-spread; for adverse moves of comparable magnitude on
top, the realized loss approaches or exceeds the margin buffer in
the median case at
L=5
and certainly at higher leverage. An
engine willing to walk the book would therefore still face a
shortfall on the median resolved market. This argues for leverage
compression as an alternative to walking-book execution: the
framework’s compression schedule reduces position size before the
resolution zone, which (i) lowers the collateral requirement, and
(ii) bounds the absolute cost of any walking-book execution that
remains.

A second consideration favoring leverage compression over
walking-book execution is the manipulation channel.
Walking the book through OTM convergence-trade ladders concentrates
execution against the market makers who placed those ladders. If a
non-trivial fraction of those market makers are correlated with
informed flow about the resolution outcome (
Section

9.4
),
walking-book execution provides them a final-hour cross-trade
against the engine’s liquidations. The resolution-zone protocol’s
staged halt blocks this. We do not formalize a manipulation cost
model in this paper (it is in scope for Paper 3); we note the
qualitative point as additional motivation for the framework’s
choice.

E2b will provide a direct empirical test of this argument. The
deterministic position grid evaluated under engine

C0
(no compression) computes insurance-fund drawdown
on observed paths, including any walking-book execution that

C0
would have done. The drawdown comparison between

C0
and
C2
(full PIRAP with compression)
tests whether walking the book preserved engine survival or
displaced loss into the insurance fund. We report the comparison in

Section

8.2.2
when E2 is complete.

9.10
Pre-emption trade-off in dynamic margin

The framework’s jump-aware tiered margin (
Definition

4
)
recomputes maintenance margin at each tick, with the continuous-vol
component scaling with the running realized-volatility estimate

\hat{\sigma}_{t}
. This dynamic responsiveness is central to the
framework’s intended behavior: as risk rises, required collateral
rises; positions with insufficient collateral are liquidated before
the situation deteriorates further. The empirical evaluation
(
Section

8.2
) reveals a trade-off this design did not fully
anticipate.

The empirical pattern.

On observed Polymarket price
paths, the dynamic-margin schedule pre-empts more liquidations than
it prevents. When
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
rises in response to ordinary
price noise — not yet a true terminal jump — the engine demands
additional collateral; positions with limited margin buffer are
liquidated. If the noise event passes without converting to a
terminal jump, the position would have recovered under a static
maintenance margin. Net effect across the analysis sample: the
framework engine
C2
liquidates more positions than the
naive static-margin baseline
C0
, even though the
framework is designed to protect against the larger-magnitude
terminal-jump events. The framework’s directional improvements
(insurance fund drawdown, median trader PnL) are real but smaller
in magnitude than the pre-registered floors anticipated.

Why this is a fundamental design tension.

Pre-emption
of recoveries is the dual of pre-emption of true disasters. A
margin schedule responsive enough to catch terminal jumps must
also respond to noise events that look similar in their early
phase. Distinguishing noise from imminent jump requires
forward-looking information that the engine’s state-only inputs
(realized volatility, current index, time to resolution) cannot
provide. The design tension is not specific to our parameter
choices; it is structural to dynamic-margin engines on bounded
event-resolved underlyings.

Implications for design.

Three responses to the
pre-emption trade-off appear in the design space:

1.

Hybrid margin schedule.
Static margin throughout the
pre-resolution period; dynamic activation only within

\Delta_{R}
of resolution. Avoids pre-emption on early-life
noise while preserving terminal-jump protection when
terminal jumps actually arrive. Untested in this paper;
proposed for future work.

2.

Halt-only evaluation variant.
Resolution-zone halt protocol
without continuous-margin reaction. Tests whether the halt
component independently has value. Evaluated in E3
(
Section

8.3
).

3.

Class-specific parameterization.
The pre-emption
cost varies by class (
Section

8.2
, per-class
breakdown shows crypto class largest framework benefit,
sports smallest). Selective deployment per class where the
framework’s protection-versus-pre-emption balance is
favorable.

This paper does not advocate full PIRAP deployment as specified.
The framework as evaluated provides empirical characterization and
directional improvement on welfare-side metrics; design
recommendations for deployable variants follow in

Section

10
.

9.11
Halt-protocol scope: execution-channel risk vs. terminal-collapse risk

The empirical evaluation in E3 (
Section

8.3
) makes
explicit a scope distinction that was implicit in the framework
specification: the resolution-zone halt protocol family
(
Definition

7
) addresses execution-channel
liquidation risk, but it does not address terminal-jump bad-debt
risk. These risk channels are distinct, and they require distinct
framework components. The halt protocol works as designed at the
execution channel; terminal-jump risk lives in the margin schedule
(
Definition

4
), not in the halt protocol.

The empirical pattern.

On the analysis sample, the full
multi-stage protocol
M3
reduces final-hour liquidation
rate by 80.4% versus the naive
M0
baseline (Floor 1
PASS). The reduction is real but mechanically explained: the
staged trading halt at
\tau-\Delta_{R}
closes the venue
before in-flight liquidations can occur in the final window. The
reduction is therefore not evidence that the halt protocol prevents
losses — it is evidence that the halt protocol prevents one
specific channel of loss (in-flight liquidation under deteriorating
margin during the final hour) which is itself rare under

M0
(
\sim 0.1\%
of position triples).

The framework’s halt protocol does not affect terminal-jump
bad-debt frequency, which is essentially identical under

M3
(
+2.4\%
, slightly worse) and
M0
. The

M3
slightly-worse result is explained by the small cost
of foregoing favorable intra-window moves under the halt: positions
forced to close at the prevailing index price at
\tau-\Delta_{R}
miss the small fraction of paths that would have moved
favorably between halt and resolution.

Why this is a scope finding, not a calibration finding.

The bad-debt frequency of approximately 54% pooled under

M0
is structurally predicted from SF2: median terminal
jump is
|\Delta I_{t}|=0.50
percentage points while the
maintenance margin buffer at
L=5
is approximately 0.20
percentage points. In approximately half of resolved markets, a
leveraged long position structurally cannot cover the
terminal-collapse loss with its own collateral. No halt-timing
choice can eliminate this loss, because the loss is realized at
oracle settlement, not during the halt window.

The structural prediction follows from
1
and

Proposition

1
taken together: if the
underlying collapses to one of two fixed endpoints from a
mid-region pre-collapse price, and the maintenance margin buffer is
a fraction of position notional, then for any
|\Delta I_{t}|

exceeding the maintenance fraction the position cannot cover the
loss. Halt timing affects when settlement occurs relative to the
trading venue’s last activity; it does not affect the collateral
sufficiency of the position at settlement.

Implications for design.

A framework refinement
addressing terminal-jump bad-debt must operate at the
margin-schedule level rather than the halt-protocol level.

Section

10.5
sketches three directions: hybrid
margin schedule (Recommendation 1), halt-only evaluation variant without
dynamic-margin reaction (Recommendation 2), and class-specific
parameterization (Recommendation 3). Recommendation 1 is the most
directly responsive to the halt-vs-margin scope finding: a margin
schedule that is static throughout the pre-resolution period (to
avoid the pre-emption trade-off documented in

Section

9.10
) but activates dynamic terminal-jump
protection within
\Delta_{R}
would address the bad-debt risk while
preserving the welfare-side improvements documented in E2c. The
hybrid variant is not evaluated empirically in this paper; it is a
testable design hypothesis with structure that follows directly
from the empirical findings.

Why the framework specification did not anticipate this
scope distinction.

The framework specification of

Section

7
describes margin and halt components as
addressing related risks, with the halt protocol providing a
“final safety net” for cases where margin discipline fails. The
empirical evaluation reveals that the halt and margin components
operate on different risk channels, not as a chain of defenses
against the same risk. We document this scope finding here rather
than retroactively rewriting
Section

7
; the framework’s
component definitions stand as proposed, and the scope distinction
is one of the design lessons of the paper rather than a correction
to the framework’s specification.

10
Future Research Agenda

The empirical evaluation of
Section

8
establishes whether the
resolution-aware framework behaves coherently on observed Polymarket
paths within a specific window. It does not establish that the
findings generalize, that the framework is robust against adversarial
conditions not present in the observed sample, or that the listing
eligibility framework is appropriate outside the data the paper
examines. Each of these is a research question; this section
identifies them and the research extensions that would address them.

We list the extensions in order of increasing distance from the
present paper’s evidence base. We do not specify decision rules,
thresholds, or implementation details: those would prejudge the
research design of work that has not yet been done. The intent is to
make explicit what the present paper does not establish and what
follow-up research would be needed to establish it.

10.1
Adversarial replay extensions

The counterfactual replay in E2 evaluates engine configurations on
observed price paths. Observed paths reflect whatever adversarial or
manipulative behavior was already present in the Polymarket sample,
but they do not test the framework against engineered adversarial
scenarios that did not occur in the sample.

Examples of scenarios that would be informative: coordinated spoofing
of one or more index components designed to shift
I_{t}
without
moving the underlying market price; late-resolution information
injection at varying time-to-resolution distances; oracle-dispute
escalation under different proposer-whitelist configurations;
cross-market correlation shocks affecting baskets of related markets
simultaneously.

A natural design is deterministic modification of observed paths to
inject the adversarial scenario, with the modification’s parameters
varied across a documented sensitivity range. The present paper does
not include this extension because the formal specification of
adversarial scenarios is itself a research question that interacts
with the manipulation analysis deferred to Paper 3.

10.2
Cross-platform replication

Findings in this paper are Polymarket-specific within the v2 archive
window (see
Section

9.2
). The framework specification of

Section

is platform-agnostic by construction — its
inputs are observable microstructure features — but the empirical
findings are not.

The natural replication target is Kalshi, the largest CFTC-regulated
event-contract venue with a CLOB structure comparable to Polymarket
but with different regulatory frame, collateral asset, and trader

becomes feasible as comparable data becomes available.

10.3
Live observability without leverage

A research extension that does not involve leverage at all is to
implement the engine’s index, margin, funding, and liquidation rules
as a real-time observable computation against live Polymarket data,
without ever extending leverage to any participant. The output of
this implementation is a public time series of what the engine would
have done; the absence of capital at risk avoids the deployment
question entirely.

The research interest of this extension is twofold: it provides a
real-time stress test against data that was not in the empirical
window, and it produces a public artifact that other researchers
could use to validate the framework’s behavior independently.

This extension is research, not deployment; we mention it here as one
specific way to extend the empirical evidence base without invoking
any of the questions about leverage extension that the paper
deliberately leaves unanswered (
Section

9.6
).

10.4
Boundaries we do not address

Three classes of follow-up work are outside this paper’s scope and
outside the scope of the extensions above.

First, the question of whether any deployable product based on the
framework would be appropriate in any specific jurisdiction is a
regulatory and legal question, not a research question we are
positioned to address. Regulatory frameworks for event contracts and
for leveraged derivatives differ materially across jurisdictions and
continue to evolve. Independent legal review would be a precondition
for any deployment-related research, and that review is outside the
scope of academic empirical work.

Second, the welfare-distribution question — how engine
configurations interact with different trader populations, what
fraction of trader losses concentrate in identifiable
information-asymmetry classes, and whether the framework reduces or
amplifies retail-versus-professional outcome divergence — requires
either real account-level data or a calibrated structural model.
Neither is available within the present empirical setup. A welfare
analysis would extend the present work substantially and is the
subject of Paper 3 in our planned series, where the manipulation and
trader-asymmetry analyses also live.

Third, the question of whether the specific instrument analyzed
(PIRAP) is the right entry point into the broader event-linked
perpetual design space, versus a conditional-probability or
event-spread or volatility variant, is the subject of Paper 2 in our
planned series. Paper 2 develops the taxonomy systematically; Paper
1’s evaluation of PIRAP is one entry in the taxonomy.

These three boundaries are not weaknesses of the present paper; they
are scope statements. A reader interested in any of them should
expect to find them addressed in the companion papers, with the
empirical backbone of Paper 1 as a prerequisite.

10.5
Design recommendations from the empirical findings

The empirical evaluation surfaced specific design tensions that
inform deployable variants of the framework. We collect concrete
recommendations here. Each recommendation is grounded in a specific
empirical finding from
Section

5.6
or

Section

8
; we cite the finding alongside the recommendation.

Recommendation 1: Hybrid margin schedule.

The dynamic
margin pre-emption trade-off documented in

Section

9.10
suggests a structural alternative to
PIRAP’s continuous-dynamic margin: hold static maintenance margin
throughout the pre-resolution period, and activate dynamic margin
only within
\Delta_{R}
of resolution. This avoids pre-emption on
early-life price noise (the dominant source of liquidations on
observed paths in the analysis sample) while preserving
terminal-jump protection in the resolution zone (where SF2’s median

|\Delta I_{t}|=0.5
confirms the design need is real). The
hybrid variant has not been evaluated empirically in this paper;
it is a design recommendation with testable structure.

Recommendation 2: Halt-only evaluation variant.

The
resolution-zone halt protocol (
Definition

7
) is
separable from the dynamic-margin schedule. A configuration that
pairs static margin with the multi-stage halt — without
continuous dynamic-margin reaction — isolates the
execution-channel value of the staged halt without inducing the
pre-emption trade-off documented in
Section

9.10
. It
does
not
address the terminal-jump bad-debt channel
identified in
Proposition

1
; that channel
remains margin-side, and a halt-only configuration leaves it
unaddressed. The relative execution-channel contribution of the
halt component is the empirical content of E3
(
Section

8.3
); whether this variant is a useful starting
point for further evaluation work, with the bad-debt channel
addressed separately by margin-side mechanisms, follows from those
results. We do not propose this configuration as a deployable
specification; the broader scope statement of
Section

1.6

applies.

Recommendation 3: Class-specific parameterization.

The class dispersion documented in SF6 (trade size; politics
median 20 USDC vs. crypto median 10), SF7 (time-of-day patterns;
sports peaks 17–21 UTC vs. crypto/politics 14–15 UTC), and SF8
(resolution-time activity surge; crypto
24.6\times
vs. politics

0.68\times
) implies that a single set of engine parameters is
ill-suited across event classes. Production deployments should
calibrate margin coefficients (
m_{\sigma}
,
m_{J}
), resolution-zone
window
\Delta_{R}
, and leverage caps
L_{\max}(t)
per event class. The
per-class drawdown findings in E2b (where crypto class shows the
largest framework benefit and sports the smallest) provide
quantitative anchoring for class-specific calibration.

Recommendation 4: Realistic baseline calibration for
production evaluation.

The pre-registered E2 evaluation
(
Section

8.2
) compared the framework to a naive
crypto-perp port baseline (
C0
) with
m_{\sigma}=1.0

calibration. Production evaluations should evaluate against
whichever margin schedule is actually deployed elsewhere; if the
realistic baseline differs from
C0
, the comparative
metrics will differ accordingly. Paper 1’s E0 baseline is a
research benchmark, not a production-equivalent margin engine.

Recommendation 5: Position-size assumptions should
match observed trader populations.

The deterministic position
grid in E2b (100, 1000, 10000 USDC notional) substantially exceeds
the observed Polymarket trade-size distribution from SF6 (medians
10–20 USDC across classes). Production evaluations of any
event-linked engine should match the realistic trader population:
small positions per the SF6 distribution rather than fixed grids.
The framework’s drawdown metrics on realistic position sizes will
differ from the E2b values, in ways that depend on the
realistic distribution.

Limits of these recommendations.

None of the five
recommendations is empirically validated in this paper. They are
design directions that follow from the empirical findings. Each
would require its own empirical evaluation under appropriate
discipline (pre-registered floors, transparent reporting of
results, honest acknowledgment of failures) before deployment
claims can be made. The hybrid margin design (Recommendation 1) is
the most concrete; we identify it as the natural follow-up
empirical question.

11
Conclusion

The empirical evaluation of
Section

8
produces a clear and
complex picture. Of five pre-registered materiality floors, three
fail in magnitude (E2a liquidation rate, E2b drawdown reduction, E3
Floor 2 bad-debt frequency) and two pass (E2c median welfare
direction, E3 Floor 1 final-hour liquidation rate). Of the two
passes, E3 Floor 1’s 80.4% reduction reflects the mechanical effect
of the staged trading halt rather than independent risk-discipline
contribution. The framework as specified does not achieve the
order-of-magnitude liquidation-rate or bad-debt reduction we
hypothesized at pre-registration. It does provide directional
improvement on welfare-side metrics in the crypto class
(
-11.1\%
drawdown), modest improvement pooled across classes
(
-5.1\%
drawdown,
+14\%
median trader PnL), and within-threshold
welfare cost (
-1.7\%
trader PnL under R3 vs R0).

Two design lessons emerge from the empirical evidence. First, the
resolution-zone halt protocol family addresses execution-channel
liquidation risk but does not address terminal-jump bad-debt risk
— terminal-jump risk lives in the margin schedule, not the halt
protocol, and these are distinct framework components requiring
distinct design treatment. Second, dynamic-margin reaction
pre-empts more recoveries than it prevents on observed paths: a
structural trade-off between protection against terminal-collapse
scenarios and protection of ordinary recoveries, replicated across
two independent counterfactual replays (E2 and E3). The pre-emption
trade-off is not a parameter calibration artifact; it is a
fundamental property of dynamic-margin engines on bounded
event-resolved underlyings. Sports-class evidence is primary per
the sports-dominance consequence rule (
Section

5.5
);
politics and crypto are reported as secondary classes that document
framework behavior on classes other than the dominant one.

Addressable through hybrid-margin empirical evaluation

aggressive margin worsens the pre-emption trade-off. Reconciling

follow-up framework.

Appendix B

The empirical work integrates four data components, all public:

describes the schema of each and the join logic that combines them.

The detailed schema tables below are populated for r2_post_g5; the

Lock policy.
This appendix is locked at version

The lock predates the production G5 run (CC-003.8) and the

C.1
Parameter discipline

Every framework parameter is classified into exactly one of two
categories.

Data-estimated.

Parameter is computed from the
observable data stream (the PMXT v2 archive plus the metadata
and resolution joins). The estimator is specified below; the
estimator is fixed by this appendix and may not be tuned to

Policy-calibrated.

Parameter is a design choice with

Appendix

E

C.2
Estimators for data-estimated parameters

Realized volatility
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
.

Windowed realized
volatility of the index
I_{t}
over the trailing
W_{\sigma}

Estimator:

\hat{\sigma}_{t}^{2}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{t}|}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{T}_{t}}\left(\log I_{t}[s]-\log I_{t}[s^{-}]\right)^{2}

\mathcal{T}_{t}

Window:

\log p
at

Terminal-jump probability

Estimator of the
probability of a terminal jump within the current margin horizon

5.1

Margin horizon:
H_{M}=1
hour.

Effective depth
0pt

Aggregate displayed depth

Estimator:

0pt=\sum_{p:|p-p^{\mathrm{mid}}_{t}|\leq\Delta_{\text{depth}}}q_{t}(p)

Manipulation cost proxy

Notional cost to

It is

C.4
Falsifiability thresholds for E1, E2, E3

The falsifiability discipline of
Section

5
requires that the
paper’s main empirical claims have explicit failure conditions stated
in advance. The thresholds below are the conditions under which
each experiment’s central claim is treated as falsified.

Framing: materiality, not safety.

The thresholds below
are not claimed to be universal safety standards or deployment
criteria. They are
materiality thresholds
: below them, the
proposed framework does not improve enough over the naive baseline
to justify its added complexity. A framework that requires substantial
additional engineering (composite robust index, jump-aware tiered
margin, anti-cascade dampening, resolution-zone protocol) must clear
a non-trivial materiality bar to be worth specifying; otherwise, a
simpler design dominates. The numerical levels are policy choices
calibrated against the sense of “meaningful improvement that
justifies non-trivial engineering complexity”, not against any
external regulatory or safety floor.

E1 (stylized facts).

The framework’s design assumptions
about the bounded-event process (boundary depth asymmetry, terminal
jump distribution, basis behavior under information arrival) must be
empirically present in the analysis sample at materially significant
levels. Specifically:

•

Depth asymmetry,
\rho
:
The ratio of within-book
depth on the long side versus the short side of the
prevailing mid-price, in the boundary region
I_{t}<\delta
, must be at least
\rho_{\text{floor}}=1.5
at the
median market in the analysis sample. Rationale:
\rho<1.5

means the depth asymmetry that Assumption 3 of

Section

3.1
requires is not materially
present; without it, Proposition 2 (which depends on
Assumption 3) does not have an empirical anchor and must be
qualified.

•

Terminal jump magnitude, evaluated by class:
Markets
that resolve within the empirical window must exhibit
terminal-jump magnitudes (the change in
I_{t}
in the
final hour before resolution) consistent with the
bounded-event model. Specifically: the median absolute
terminal jump
|\Delta I_{t}[\tau^{-},\tau]|
must be at
least
0.10
(10 percentage points) at the median resolved
market
evaluated both pooled and per event class
. If
the pooled sample fails the floor but one or more event
classes pass it individually, the framework’s jump-aware
margin claim is retained for the passing classes and
explicitly qualified for the failing classes. This
per-class evaluation guards against a quiet single week
falsifying the framework on classes where it would otherwise
apply. Rationale for
0.10
: a terminal jump under 10
percentage points means the boundary collapse dynamic that
the jump-aware margin schedule in
Definition

4
is
designed to handle is not the binding empirical phenomenon;
below this, the schedule is over-engineered for the data.

E2 (counterfactual replay).

The resolution-aware framework
configuration C2 must outperform the naive crypto-perp port C0 on
both trader-independent metrics on observed paths. Specifically:

•

Liquidation rate (E2a, trader-independent):
The
rate at which forced liquidations occur on observed paths
under C2 must be lower than under C0 by at least 30%
(relative reduction). Rationale: 30% is the minimum
material improvement floor; below this, the additional
engineering of leverage compression and jump-aware margin
does not earn its complexity over a static-margin baseline.

•

Insurance fund drawdown (E2b, trader-independent):

The total notional drawdown to a hypothetical engine-funded
insurance pool under C2 must be lower than under C0 by at
least 50%. Rationale: a risk-engine claim about capital
protection should at least halve aggregate capital stress
relative to the baseline; below 50%, the framework’s
capital-protection contribution is too modest to support
the framing of the paper’s central design claim.

•

Synthetic robustness layer (E2c):
For the synthetic
trader-population layer, the framework’s improvement over
C0 should be robust across the documented parameter ranges
in
Appendix

E
. E2c is robustness, not primary;
a failure at this layer triggers a discussion-of-bounds
section, not falsification of the central claim.

E3 (resolution-zone protocol).

The resolution-zone
protocol
M_{3}
(multi-stage with leverage compression and
boundary-funding correction) must produce qualitatively different
behavior than
M_{0}
(no resolution-zone protocol) in the final
hour before resolution. Specifically:

•

Final-hour liquidation reduction:
The fraction of
positions liquidated in the final hour must be reduced by
at least 50% under
M_{3}
versus
M_{0}
. Rationale: the
resolution-zone protocol’s primary purpose is to protect
positions from the terminal-collapse dynamic; halving the
final-hour liquidation rate is the minimum threshold at
which the protocol is contributing materially.

•

Bad-debt reduction:
The frequency of bad-debt
events (liquidations where the insurance pool absorbs
uncovered loss) must be reduced by at least 75% under

M_{3}
versus
M_{0}
. Rationale: bad debt is the tail-risk
target of the framework; the threshold is intentionally
more demanding than the others because tail-risk reduction
is what justifies the engineering complexity of the
resolution-zone protocol over simpler alternatives. A
reduction below 75% means the protocol does not adequately
address its principal design purpose.

C.5
Honest reporting of negative results

If E1, E2, or E3 falsifies a central claim per the thresholds above,
the relevant claim in
Section

1.4
(the contributions
list) is qualified or withdrawn, and
Sections

8
and
11

report the failure explicitly with analysis of why. Reviewer 2’s
guidance on r1.5.2 framed this commitment directly: the paper is
valuable even when the framework fails on some dimensions, provided
the failure is documented honestly. We commit to that here as a
locked rule, not an aspiration.

D.6
Run-loop infrastructure: stop conditions and queue ordering

Runs that process market data in long-running stages
(
metadata_join
,
resolution_join
) check stop
conditions periodically inside the stage — after every
N=100

markets processed or every
T=60
seconds, whichever comes first —
rather than only at stage boundaries. The check inspects:

•

Peak resident memory (default threshold 14 GB).

•

Wall time since run start (configurable; the present runs
used 5 hours for chronological subsamples, 18 hours for the
stratified analysis sample run).

•

Rolling Gamma 404 rate over the last 1000 markets (default
threshold 5%).

•

Rolling UMA hit rate over the last 1000 markets (default
floor 90%; can be lowered with
--uma-hit-stop
flag
for runs where partial UMA coverage is acceptable).

•

Presence of an external
STOP
file in the working
directory.

The two rate-based conditions (Gamma 404 rate, UMA hit rate) require
at least 100 samples in the rolling window before the corresponding
threshold can fire, to avoid false positives at the start of a run
where rolling rates are noisy. The remaining three conditions (RSS,
wall time, external STOP file) fire immediately whenever the
threshold is crossed.

On trigger, the run saves a partial-state JSON

evaluation/output/<run_id>_partial_state.json
containing
the run identifier, the condition that fired and its measured value,
markets processed so far, and rolling statistics, then exits with
the corresponding exit code. The cache state at the time of trigger
is preserved on disk; a re-run with the same parameters will resume
from cache, processing only markets not yet completed.

Queue ordering for stratified samples.

The
stratified-by-day analysis sample selection
(
Section

5.5
) submits markets to the executor in
round-robin-by-day order: the
k
-th submitted market rotates
through the seven days of the empirical window. The CLI flag

--queue-order
accepts
round-robin
(default) or

sequential
. Round-robin ordering ensures that partial
state at any moment during the run is a representative cross-day
sample, so a run interrupted by a stop condition produces an
analyzable (if not complete) partial sample rather than a
day-1-skewed prefix.

Historical note:
the CC-003.11 stratified analysis-sample
run and the CC-004 stylized-facts compute used

interleave=False
(sequential) ordering, predating the
round-robin default of CC-005 onward. The set of markets sampled is
deterministic in either ordering (same locked seed produces same
markets); only submission order differs. Sequential-ordering output
is not corrupted by the ordering choice, only its
partial-state-while-running representativeness is. Replications of
those runs with current code (
--queue-order round-robin
)
will produce the same numerical outputs.

Persistent HTTP connections.

Both

ingest/market_metadata.py
(Gamma) and

ingest/uma_resolutions.py
(UMA) use a thread-local

httpx.Client
per worker, persistent across calls. This
removes the per-call TCP handshake and TLS negotiation overhead
that an earlier version of the code incurred, where a fresh client
was instantiated per market. The resulting throughput improvement
is observable in any API-bound stage. Throughput benchmarking
versus the earlier per-call client pattern was deferred from
CC-005 to the first live API run after the fix
(
docs/cc005_t21_t22_report.md
§4.5), so the architectural
fix is in place but the empirical magnitude of the improvement is
characterized in the run logs of subsequent runs rather than in
isolation.

Hard ceilings imposed by remote rate limits cannot be raised by
client-side connection management. In particular, the Goldsky
GraphQL endpoint that hosts the UMA Optimistic Oracle subgraph
appears to enforce per-IP rate limits on the order of
10^{1}

requests per minute under sustained load (the precise ceiling is
not published). The CC-005 fix eliminates redundant overhead above
this ceiling but does not raise it. Future runs that would benefit
from higher throughput should consider request batching at the
GraphQL query level or alternative subgraph hosting, neither of
which is in scope for the present paper.

D.7
Scope of replication guarantees

The replication infrastructure guarantees:

•

Bit-level reproducibility
of every numerical artifact
from public input data, given the same git SHA, the same
Python environment (per
requirements.txt
), and the
same locked seeds.

•

Stage-level transparency
: each stage produces
intermediate artifacts that can be verified independently of
downstream stages.

•

Provenance traceability
: every paper claim is linked
to a specific JSON output file produced by a specific stage
at a specific git SHA.

The infrastructure does not guarantee:

•

Replication on different versions of public APIs.

Polymarket Gamma, UMA, and PMXT v2 may evolve; downstream
results may differ if a service redefines a field or
deprecates an endpoint after the empirical window.

•

Replication on different empirical windows.
The
infrastructure is parameterized by the target window, but
the consequence-rule outcomes (sports trigger, etc.) are
window-specific findings, not window-independent claims.

•

Operating-system independence.
The infrastructure has
been tested on macOS (Apple Silicon) and Linux (x86_64).
Windows reproduction is not validated.

This appendix documents the sensitivity analyses that anchor the

E.1

Equal weighting (1/3 each).

Depth-weighted with half-life

Volume-weighted instead of depth-weighted.

Alternative depth-thinness threshold
0pt^{\min}\in\{0.5,1.0,2.0\}\times 0pt^{\min,\text{default}}

The sensitivity analysis structure is specified above; numerical

E.2

The default leverage compression

Linear ramp.

•

Smooth (cosine-blended) ramp.

•

•

E.8

not parameter sensitivity.

•

Provide cross-platform or cross-temporal validation. The
sensitivity analyses are within-sample.

•

Establish which parameter values are universally
appropriate. Each analysis identifies parameter regimes
within which the headline conclusion holds; selecting
deployment parameters requires independent platform-specific
analysis (

9.4
