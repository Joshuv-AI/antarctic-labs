DysonNet: Constant-Time Local Updates for Neural Quantum States
Lucas Winter, Andreas Nunnenkamp

Abstract
Neural quantum states (NQS) provide a flexible variational framework for many-body wavefunctions, but suffer from high computational cost and limited interpretability. We introduce DysonNet, a broad class of NQS that couples strictly local nonlinearities through global linear layers. This structure is analogous to a truncated Dyson series which gives an intuitive interpretation of local wavefunction updates as scattering from static impurities. By resumming the scattering series, single-spin-flip updates can be computed in 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathcal{O}(1) time, independent of system size, using an algorithm we call ABACUS. Implementing DysonNet with the state-space model S4, we obtain up to 230 × 230\times speedups over Vision-Transformers for computing the local estimator. This corresponds to an asymptotic 𝒪 ⁡ ( N 2 ) \mathcal{O}(N^{2}) improvement in training-time scaling, reaching 𝒪 ⁡ ( N ​ log 2 ​ N ) \mathcal{O}(N\log^{2}N) total training complexity in area-law phases. Benchmarks on the 1D long-range Ising model and frustrated J 1 J_{1} - J 2 J_{2} chains show that DysonNet matches state-of-the-art NQS accuracy while removing the dominant local-update overhead. More broadly, our results suggest a route to scalable NQS architectures where physical interpretability directly enables computational efficiency.

II
DysonNet: Physically motivated neural network architecture

A key feature of many quantum many-body ground states is a natural separation of scales:
long-wavelength behavior is often universal and can be described by a few collective modes, the basis for effective field theories and the renormalization group.
In contrast, short-range physics can be highly complex and non-universal, depending sensitively on microscopic details of the Hamiltonian.

We will start by introducing DysonNet, a class of NQS Ansätze that explicitly builds the separation of correlation scales into their architecture. Each block factorizes the representation into (i) a linear, translation-invariant propagator
G
that captures long-wavelength response and (ii) a local nonlinearity
D(\bm{\sigma})
that captures short-range structure. Stacking such blocks yields a representation that is both efficient and interpretable, rather than asking a generic ansatz to discover this structure from scratch.

We propose a Metropolis sampler that addresses two bottlenecks: standard Metropolis updates are inherently sequential and thus underuse GPUs, and our link-tensor construction must be amortized to achieve sublinear cost in system size. The key idea is to treat a batch of spatially separated spin flips as a dilute gas of defects. When the defects are well separated, their scattering signatures do not interfere. We can then evaluate the flips as independent proposals in parallel and reuse the same background link tensors across the batch solving both problems. This approximation introduces a small error in the acceptance ratio, but a screened acceptance rule restores exact detailed balance
67
.

Proposals are single-site flips, with occasional global spin inversions to aid mixing. A proposal
\bm{\sigma}_{0}\to\bm{\sigma}

F

\bm{\sigma}_{0}

Independent Scattering Approximation

. We sweep first over even blocks, then odd blocks. In each active block, we select one site uniformly. This creates a superlattice of defects separated by the spacing
s
. We build the link tensors once per sweep (costing
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
) and reuse them for all proposals in the batch. Because the proposals are spatially separated, the independent scattering approximation is highly accurate and the error
\bm{\epsilon}
can be bounded (see appendix
D
).

Because
\epsilon
is bounded, the Metropolis acceptance rule can be enforced exactly even though the wave-function amplitude is approximate. We recover exact detailed balance using a screened acceptance rule
67
. We accept a proposal based on the approximate ratio
\tilde{R}=|\Psi_{\mathrm{ISA}}(\bm{\sigma})|^{2}/|\Psi(\bm{\sigma}_{0})|^{2}
and error bound
\epsilon
.
For a random number
u\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]

1.

Fast Accept: If
u<\tilde{R}-\epsilon
, accept immediately.

2.

Fast Reject: If
u>\tilde{R}+\epsilon
, reject immediately.

3.

Correction: Only in the rare window
u\in[\tilde{R}-\epsilon,\tilde{R}+\epsilon]
do we evaluate the exact interference terms (recomputing the full amplitude).

This scheme allows parallel evaluation without statistical bias; proof see appendix
C
.

The efficiency depends on the physical correlation length of the system.
The decay of correlations determines the required spacing
s
to keep
\epsilon
small.
In appendix
E
, we derive the amortized per proposal cost

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(N)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}N)&\text{Area-law (Gapped)},\\
\mathcal{O}(N^{\gamma}\log N)&\text{Power-law (Critical)},\\
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)&\text{All-to-all}.\end{cases}.

(13)

For gapped phases (area-law), the Green’s function decays exponentially, allowing dense packing of updates and polylogarithmic runtime. For critical systems (power-law), the spacing
s
must grow with system size, leading to sublinear scaling
N^{\gamma}
(where
\gamma<1
depends on the critical exponent
\alpha
). Only in the all-to-all limit does the speedup vanish. In our numerics, we find the empirical error
\epsilon(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}_{0})
to be small up to very large system sizes
N\leq 1000
and even on criticality. For practical system sizes, adjusting the spacing
s
is unnecessary. This means the method yields a speedups of
\mathcal{O}(N)
significantly exceeding the worst case analytical estimates (see appendix
F
). Another significant practical advantage is that the screened acceptance rule enables reduced precision sampling. While neural quantum states typically require high floating-point precision, this rule permits lower-precision formats, which can yield additional constant speedups of
2\times
to
4\times
.

We noted in the algorithm section that ABACUS applies to any NQS with global linear layers. For dense, unstructured linear mixers, a forward pass typically costs
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})

\mathcal{O}(N^{2})

\mathcal{O}(N)
connected configurations typically incurs an
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})

Implementation of DysonNet

So far we have treated the free propagator
G_{0}
and the coupling vertex

as abstract objects, since our algorithms only require their general properties (linearity for
G_{0}

Parameterizing the Green’s function
G^{(l)}
.

State space models are a natural choice as they allow to interpret
G^{(l)}
as a free propagator. This is consistent with viewing the network as
a truncated Dyson series. A state space model is a sequence-to-sequence model where the output is obtained by computing the response of a linear state-evolution equation to the input. We implement a bidirectional state-space model mapping a sequence of
\bm{h}_{j}\to\bm{\tilde{h}}_{j}
by evolving the state vector
\bm{x}_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{s\times d}
via the following discretized differential equation

\displaystyle x_{j,c}\;

\displaystyle=\;A_{c}\,(x_{j+1,c}+x_{j-1,c}-x_{j,c})+B_{c}\,h_{j,c},\qquad

(15)

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{j,c}\;

\displaystyle=\;C_{c}\,x_{j},

(16)

where
A_{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{s\times s},B_{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times s},C_{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{s\times 1}
are the learned state matrices. The first term in (
16
) is the lattice Laplace operator spreading an excitation. As the system is translationally invariant, the response can be calculated using the Fast Fourier transform
\bm{\tilde{h}}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\bigl(\,G(k)\;\odot\;\mathcal{F}(\bm{h})\,\bigr)
and the Green’s function

\displaystyle G_{c}(k)=C_{c}\,(I-e^{i2\pi k/N}A_{c})^{-1}B_{c}\;.

(17)

When
A_{c}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{s})
is diagonal, the resolvent and

G(k)
reduce to a sum of Cauchy kernels (simple poles)

\displaystyle\;G_{c}(k)\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{s}\frac{C_{c,j}B_{c,j}}{\,1-e^{i2\pi k/N}\lambda_{c,j}\,}.

(18)

Physically, these are damped/oscillatory
modes with poles
\lambda_{c,j}
inside the unit disk (
|\lambda_{c,j}|\leq 1
) on the complex plane. The Green’s function corresponds to the free propagation of an excitation (for example domain wall or magnon mode) in space. Note as the number of modes grows,
G_{c}(k)
can also approximate power laws over finite range.

In our paper we use the S4 Ansatz for
A=\Lambda+\bm{p}\bm{q}^{T}
consisting of a diagonal matrix
\Lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{s\times s}
and a rank one perturbation
\bm{p},\bm{q}\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times s}

24
. We provide the analytical Green’s function
G(k)
in appendix
J
. Making this choice is physically analogous to considering the interacting Green’s function of an excitation scattered at a static impurity.

Parameterizing the local nonlinearity
D(\bm{\sigma})
.
For parameterizing the local vertex
D(\bm{\sigma})
we use a short-ranged CNN kernel, as this naturally enforces locality via a finite receptive field. From the input configuration we construct a second data stream
\bm{\phi}^{(l)}
(see Fig.
1c
). Each layer applies (i) a site-wise dense map, (ii) a short-range convolution, and (iii) a nonlinearity. The dense map
D_{j}(\bm{\sigma})
is then conditioned on the data stream
\bm{\phi}^{(l)}

\displaystyle D_{j}(\bm{\sigma})

\displaystyle=U^{(l)}\,\mathrm{diag}\!\big(\chi(\bm{\phi}_{j}^{(l-1)})\big)\,V^{(l)}.

(19)

Here,
\chi
is a nonlinearity that acts locally on each vector
\bm{\phi}_{j}^{(l-1)}
. The matrices
U^{(l)},V^{(l)}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are layer-specific and configuration-independent. Thus
D_{j}(\bm{\sigma})\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
has fixed eigenvectors but spin-configuration–dependent eigenvalues through
\bm{\phi}^{(l-1)}_{j}
. Besides the multiplicative gating
D(\bm{\sigma})
(
19
) we also inject the
\bm{\phi}_{j}^{(l)}
stream into the
\bm{\sigma}^{(l)}\to\bm{\sigma}^{(l)}+\bm{\phi}^{(l)}
stream via addition before applying the SSM (see Fig.
1c
).

VII
Conclusion

We introduce ABACUS, an efficient local update algorithm, and DysonNet, a physically interpretable architecture. ABACUS updates the wavefunction amplitude for any NQS with global linear layers and strictly local non-linearities in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time given cached link tensors. Designed for ABACUS, the architecture DysonNet constructs wavefunction amplitudes from local non-linearities connected by global convolutions. It is interpretable as a truncated Dyson series and allows efficient link tensor construction. To amortize caching costs, we employ a screened typewriter sampler that preserves tensors over multiple flips. We derive worst-case runtime bounds and demonstrate a linear speedup. Benchmarks against the long-range transverse-field Ising and frustrated
J_{1}-J_{2}
models show that DysonNet matches Vision Transformer accuracy while reducing runtime by two orders of magnitude—an
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
asymptotic improvement. Finally, critical exponent calculations confirm that DysonNet captures correlations at criticality and scales to large systems.

The ABACUS update is straightforwardly compatible with advanced Monte-Carlo strategies, including cluster moves and parallel tempering, opening the door to further runtime improvements
73
;
17
;
70
.
Because the link-tensor construction is agnostic to lattice geometry, DysonNet and ABACUS extend naturally to 2D, inviting applications to the 2D systems
7
;
39
;
25
;
57
. The same structure also accommodates dynamics: DysonNet is compatible with different approaches to time evolution such as the time dependent variational principle

;
65

;

;

;
2
;
59
.

Beyond DysonNet, this work points to a general NQS design principle: build long-range correlations by wiring strictly local nonlinearities through global linear layers. This might preserve the expressivity of deep networks while adding algebraic structure that ABACUS can exploit for efficient local updates, pushing variational Monte Carlo to larger system sizes. At the same time, the resulting ansätze admit a diagrammatic interpretation as a scattering series, offering a route to theoretical understanding and characterization via field theory and the renormalization group. We believe this offers a path towards architectures where computational efficiency is enabled by physical interpretability.

Appendix A
DysonNet can exactly represent power law decay

For interpreting the DysonNet Ansatz and showing it can express power law decay we will consider the simplifying case of one layer, activation function
f(x)=e^{x}
and embedding dimension
d=1
. The log amplitude of the network is

\displaystyle\log\Psi(\bm{\sigma})=\frac{A}{N}\sum_{i,j}D(\sigma_{i-w:i+w})\,G_{i-j}\sigma_{j},

(21)

To interpret the local vertex we will apply a Fourier-Walsh expansion. Let the window be
\mathcal{W}=\{-w,\dots,w\}
with
W=2w+1
.
Any function
D:\{\pm 1\}^{W}\to\mathbb{R}
has the exact Walsh expansion

\displaystyle D(\sigma_{i-w:i+w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal{W}}D_{S}\,\prod_{u\in S}\sigma_{i+u}

\displaystyle=D_{\varnothing}+\sum_{u\in\mathcal{W}}D_{\{u\}}\sigma_{i+u}

(22)

\displaystyle+\sum_{u<v}D_{\{u,v\}}\sigma_{i+u}\sigma_{i+v}+\cdots\ .

Inserting (
22
) into (
21
) gives

\displaystyle\log\Psi=\frac{A}{N}\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal{W}}\sum_{I,j}D_{S}\,G_{I-j}\,\bigg(\prod_{u\in S}\sigma_{i+u}\bigg)\,\sigma_{j},

(23)

so a Walsh monomial of degree
|S|
contributes an interaction of degree

|S|+1
in the exponent (coupling order).

We first consider the Jastrow limit for
W=1
.
For
W=1
(
w=0
) only
S=\varnothing
and
S=\{0\}
appear, hence

D(\sigma_{i})=D_{0}+D_{1}\sigma_{i}
. Then (
21
) reduces to

\displaystyle\log\Psi

\displaystyle=\frac{A}{N}\sum_{I}(D_{0}+D_{1}\sigma_{i})\sum_{j}G_{i-j}\sigma_{j}

\displaystyle=h\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}+\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\text{const},

(24)

where (for even
G_{r}
and absorbing
i=j
into the constant)

\displaystyle h=\frac{AD_{0}}{N}\sum_{r}G_{r},\qquad J_{ij}=\frac{2AD_{1}}{N}\,G_{|i-j|}\ .

(25)

Configuration-sum correlator and small-
\lambda
expansion.

For the
W=1
Jastrow limit, diagonal correlators are computed as explicit
configuration sums with respect to the induced classical measure

p(\bm{\sigma})\propto|\Psi(\bm{\sigma})|^{2}
,

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}\hat{\sigma}_{i+r}^{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+r}\,\exp\!\Big[\,2h\sum_{\ell}\sigma_{\ell}+2\sum_{\ell<m}J_{\ell m}\sigma_{\ell}\sigma_{m}\Big],

(26)

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\exp\!\Big[\,2h\sum_{\ell}\sigma_{\ell}+2\sum_{\ell<m}J_{\ell m}\sigma_{\ell}\sigma_{m}\Big].

To expose the long-distance scaling we set
h=0
(or, equivalently, consider the
connected correlator), write
J_{\ell m}=\lambda\,\bar{J}_{\ell m}
, and expand
around
\lambda=0
, where the weight becomes uniform.
Denote averages with respect to the uniform measure by
\langle\cdot\rangle_{0}
,
i.e.
\langle F\rangle_{0}:=2^{-N}\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}F(\bm{\sigma})
.
Then

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+r}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\Big\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+r}\,\exp\!\big[2\lambda\sum_{\ell<m}\bar{J}_{\ell m}\sigma_{\ell}\sigma_{m}\big]\Big\rangle_{0}}{\Big\langle\exp\!\big[2\lambda\sum_{\ell<m}\bar{J}_{\ell m}\sigma_{\ell}\sigma_{m}\big]\Big\rangle_{0}}

(27)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Big\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+r}\Big\rangle_{0}+2\lambda\sum_{\ell<m}\bar{J}_{\ell m}\Big\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+r}\sigma_{\ell}\sigma_{m}\Big\rangle_{0}+O(\lambda^{2})}{1+2\lambda\sum_{\ell<m}\bar{J}_{\ell m}\Big\langle\sigma_{\ell}\sigma_{m}\Big\rangle_{0}+O(\lambda^{2})}.

where we expanded the exponential up to second order in the second line. Since under
\langle\cdot\rangle_{0}
spins are independent with

\langle\sigma_{a}\rangle_{0}=0
and
\langle\sigma_{a}\sigma_{b}\rangle_{0}=\delta_{ab}
.
For
r\neq 0
one also has
\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+r}\rangle_{0}=0
.
Moreover, the only non-vanishing four-spin averages are those where the indices
pair up; in particular,

\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+r}\sigma_{\ell}\sigma_{m}\rangle_{0}=1

iff
\{\ell,m\}=\{i,i+r\}
(and vanishes otherwise). Hence the sum in
(
27
) collapses to the single edge
(\ell,m)=(i,i+r)
.

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+r}\rangle=2\lambda\,\bar{J}_{i,i+r}+O(\lambda^{2})=2J_{i,i+r}+O(J^{2}).

(28)

Using the
W=1
identification
J_{ij}\propto G_{|i-j|}
and a power-law kernel

G_{r}=r^{-\alpha}
therefore yields the leading asymptotic behavior

\displaystyle\langle{\sigma}_{i}{\sigma}_{i+r}\rangle=2J_{r}+O(J^{2})\sim r^{-\alpha}+O(J^{2}).

(29)

Higher orders in
\lambda
correspond to multi-edge contributions (paths and
graphs) and dress the prefactor, while the leading long-distance tail is set by
the direct long-range coupling inherited from
G_{r}
.

For
W>1
, higher Walsh sectors
D_{S}
in (
22
)
generate controlled multi-spin vertices of order
|S|{+}1

via (
23
), adding non-Gaussian short-range dressing
beyond a pure Jastrow factor. Stacking
L
DysonNet blocks increases the
effective coupling order through the Dyson-series structure of repeated

D(\bm{\sigma})G
insertions, while preserving the separation of scales:

G
fixes the long-distance response (e.g. algebraic kernels suitable for
criticality) and
D
learns non-universal local structure. Allowing complex
parameters further enables nontrivial sign/phase structure beyond strictly
positive Jastrow states.

Appendix B
Induction Proof of ABACUS

In this section we prove Theorem
I
and the recursion relations
(
10
) and (
11
) via induction.
For a local update
\bm{\sigma}=\bm{\sigma}_{0}+\Delta\bm{\sigma}
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\bm{\sigma})

\displaystyle:=\prod_{l=1}^{L}\!\bigl(D^{(l)}_{0}+\Delta D^{(l)}\bigr)M^{(l)},

(30)

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}(\bm{\sigma})

\displaystyle=A\,P_{M}\,\mathcal{M}(\bm{\sigma})\,\bm{\sigma}.

(31)

A
matrix word
is one summand in the expansion of
\mathcal{M}(\bm{\sigma})
.
Its
pivot
is the largest layer index
l
for which
\Delta D^{(l)}
appears.
Let
\mathcal{W}_{l}
be the set of words with pivot
l
, and define

\tilde{\bm{h}}^{(l)}:=P_{S,j}\sum_{W\in\mathcal{W}_{l}}W\,\bm{\sigma}\quad\in\mathbb{R}^{W\times d}.

(32)

Invariant 1

(after line 3 of Algorithm
II
)
.

For every
l\leq L
, the recursion (
10
) equals the sum over all words with pivot
l
.

\bm{\tilde{h}}^{(l)}=P_{S,j}\sum_{W\in\mathcal{W}_{l}}W\,\bm{\sigma}\;.

(33)

Proof.
We use strong induction over
l
.

Base case
l=1
.

For pivot
1
, the only scattering layer is the first one.

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{h}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=P_{S,j}\,\Delta D^{(1)}M^{(1)}(\bm{\sigma}_{0}+\Delta\bm{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\Delta D^{(1)}\!\left(M^{(1)}\Delta\bm{\sigma}+\tilde{\bm{h}}^{(1)}_{0}\right),

(34)

which is exactly the initialization in Algorithm
II
.

Induction step.

Assume (
33
) holds for all
k<l
. Any word with pivot
l
is obtained by
applying
\Delta D^{(l)}M^{(l)}
to either:
(i) the unscattered incident field at layer
l
, or
(ii) a word with pivot
m<l
, propagated from
m
to
l
through background factors

D_{0}^{(n)}M^{(n)}
,
n=m+1,\dots,l-1
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{W\in\mathcal{W}_{l}}W\bm{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\Delta D^{(l)}M^{(l)}\bigg[\bm{h}^{(l)}_{0}

(35)

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=1}^{l-1}\left(\prod_{n=m+1}^{l-1}D_{0}^{(n)}M^{(n)}\right)\sum_{W\in\mathcal{W}_{m}}W\bm{\sigma}\bigg].

Projecting to the slice and using the induction hypothesis gives

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{h}}^{(l)}=\Delta D^{(l)}\!\left(\tilde{M}^{(l)}\tilde{\bm{h}}^{(l-1)}+\sum_{m=1}^{l-2}L^{(l,m)}\tilde{\bm{h}}^{(m)}+\tilde{\bm{h}}_{0}^{(l)}\right),

(36)

where
L^{(l,m)}
is exactly the link tensor from Eq. (
9
).

\displaystyle L^{(l,m)}=P_{S,j}M^{(l)}\left(\prod_{n=m+1}^{l-1}D_{0}^{(n)}M^{(n)}\right)P_{S,j}^{\top}.

(37)

This is Eq. (
10
), so the invariant is preserved.

Termination:
After the loop (
l=L
), (
33
) holds for every pivot class.
Grouping the full expansion by pivot and contracting each class with its output link
tensor
T^{(l)}
yields

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}(\bm{\sigma})=A\!\left(P_{M}\,\tilde{\bm{h}}^{(L)}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}T^{(l)}\tilde{\bm{h}}^{(l)}\right),

(38)

which is Eq. (
11
). Hence ABACUS produces the desired matrix-vector
product exactly.

Complexity.
Using only the principal blocks of the Toeplitz matrix, contracting a vector
\bm{\tilde{h}}^{(l)}
with
G^{(l)}
and then applying the slice projection
P_{S,j}
requires
\mathcal{O}\!\left(d\,W^{2}\right)
operations, where
W=2w+1\ll N
denotes the slice width and
d
is the hidden‐state dimension. If
W
becomes large, the same contraction can instead be performed with a state-space model (SSM) in
\mathcal{O}(W\,d\,s^{2})
time, which scales strictly linearly in
W
.

Multiplying
\bm{\tilde{h}}^{(l)}
by the sparse diagonal update
\Delta D^{(l)}
costs
\mathcal{O}\!\left(W\,d^{2}\right)
, again linear in the slice width. The link tensors at the final layer,
T^{(l)}
, incur the same
\mathcal{O}\!\left(W\,d^{2}\right)
cost, while the most expensive step is contracting with the multi-layer link tensors, which scales as
\mathcal{O}\!\left(W^{2}\,d^{2}\right)
yet remains independent of the full system size
N
.

Since every individual contraction is free of any
N
-dependence, the entire update can be carried out in constant time with respect to
N
. For a network of
L
layers, roughly
L(L-1)/2\sim\mathcal{O}(L^{2})
such contractions are required, giving the overall worst-case complexity
\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(d^{2}W^{2}L^{2}\bigr),

which is constant in
N
and quadratic in both the number of layers and the slice width.

By a strong-induction loop-invariant argument we have shown that
Algorithm
II
constructs every matrix word
exactly once, associates it with the correct pre-contracted linear
environment, and thus evaluates the network output
\bm{\Omega}(\bm{\sigma})

at
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}W^{2}L^{2})
constant in
N
cost for a local spin flip.

\blacksquare

Appendix C
Screened Metropolis sampler is exact

Let
\pi(\bm{\sigma})\propto|\Psi(\bm{\sigma})|^{2}
be the target distribution and consider a symmetric proposal

q(\bm{\sigma}\to\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})=q(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\to\bm{\sigma})
.
Define the exact Metropolis–Hastings ratio

R(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}):=\frac{|\Psi(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})|^{2}}{|\Psi(\bm{\sigma})|^{2}},\qquad\alpha(\bm{\sigma}\to\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}):=\min\{1,R(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})\}.

(39)

Assume we can compute deterministic bounds
L(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})
and
U(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})
such that

L(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})\leq R(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})\leq U(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})\qquad\text{for all }(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}).

(40)

Consider the screened acceptance rule: draw
u\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]
and
(i) accept if
u<\min\{1,L\}
;
(ii) reject if
u>\min\{1,U\}
;
(iii) otherwise (the ambiguous window) compute
R
exactly and accept iff
u<\min\{1,R\}
.
Then the resulting accept/reject decision coincides with the standard MH decision for every triple

(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime},u)
, hence the induced Markov chain satisfies detailed balance with respect to
\pi
and is exact.

Proof.

By (
40
) we have
\min\{1,L\}\leq\min\{1,R\}\leq\min\{1,U\}
.
If
u<\min\{1,L\}
then
u<\min\{1,R\}
and both screened MH and MH accept.
If
u>\min\{1,U\}
then
u>\min\{1,R\}
and both reject.
Otherwise
u\in[\min\{1,L\},\min\{1,U\}]
and the screened rule evaluates
R
and applies the MH test

u<\min\{1,R\}
, which is exactly the MH decision.
Therefore the transition kernel is identical to MH, and detailed balance

\pi(\bm{\sigma})q(\bm{\sigma}\to\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})\alpha(\bm{\sigma}\to\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})=\pi(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})q(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\to\bm{\sigma})\alpha(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\to\bm{\sigma})

holds. ∎

Appendix D
Upper bound error scaling for independent scattering approximation

In this section we will explicitly derive an upper bound for the error in the Metropolis acceptance ratio under the
independent scattering approximation
introduced in section
IV
.
For a configuration
\bm{\sigma}
and a fixed reference configuration
\bm{\sigma}_{0}
, define

\epsilon(\bm{\sigma}):=\left|\frac{\Psi_{\mathrm{ISA}}(\bm{\sigma})-\Psi(\bm{\sigma})}{\Psi(\bm{\sigma}_{0})}\right|.

(41)

Let the wavefunction be of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Psi(\bm{\sigma})&=f\!\big(\mathbf{z}(\bm{\sigma})\big),\\
\mathbf{z}(\bm{\sigma})&:=A\,P_{M}\!\Bigg(G^{f}\sum_{M=0}^{L}\prod_{l=1}^{M}D^{(l)}(\bm{\sigma})\,G^{(l)}\Bigg)\,E\,\bm{\sigma},\end{split}

(42)

where
P_{M}
is the mean-pooling projection vector,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
D^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times N}
(block diagonal along the input sequence), and
G^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N\times d}
(Toeplitz, block diagonal in
d
).
Fix the reference
\bm{\sigma}_{0}
and set

\mathbf{z}_{0}:=\mathbf{z}(\bm{\sigma}_{0}),\quad\Delta\mathbf{z}(\bm{\sigma}):=\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{ISA}}(\bm{\sigma})-\mathbf{z}(\bm{\sigma}),\quad\bm{\sigma}=\bm{\sigma}_{0}+\Delta\bm{\sigma}.

(43)

with

\displaystyle z_{\text{ISA}}(\bm{\sigma})=z(\bm{\sigma}_{0})+\sum_{k}\text{ABACUS}(\Delta\bm{\sigma}_{k},\bm{\sigma}_{0},\mathcal{T}(\bm{\sigma}_{0}))\ ,

(44)

with the link tensors
\mathcal{T}(\bm{\sigma}_{0})=\text{LinkTensor}(\bm{\sigma}_{0})
and the flips
\Delta\bm{\sigma}_{k}
. Then (
41
) can be written as

\epsilon(\bm{\sigma})=\frac{\big|\,f(\mathbf{z}(\bm{\sigma})+\Delta\mathbf{z}(\bm{\sigma}))-f(\mathbf{z}(\bm{\sigma}))\,\big|}{f(\mathbf{z}_{0})}.

(45)

For the choice

f(\mathbf{z})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\cosh(z_{j})

(with
f(\mathbf{z}_{0})>0
), one can bound the numerator of (
45
) without any linearization.
Using the identity

\cosh(u+v)=\cosh(u)\cosh(v)+\sinh(u)\sinh(v)

and the inequalities
|\sinh(u)|\leq\cosh(u)
and
\cosh(v)+|\sinh(v)|=e^{|v|}
, we obtain for all
u,v\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\big|\cosh(u+v)-\cosh(u)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\cosh(u)\big(\cosh(v)-1+|\sinh(v)|\big)

(46)

\displaystyle=\big(e^{|v|}-1\big)\cosh(u).

(47)

Applying (
47
) componentwise with
u=z_{j}(\bm{\sigma})
and
v=\Delta z_{j}(\bm{\sigma})
, and using

e^{|\Delta z_{j}|}-1\leq e^{\|\Delta\mathbf{z}(\bm{\sigma})\|_{\infty}}-1
,
yields

\displaystyle\epsilon(\bm{\sigma})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{f(\bm{z}_{0})}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\big(e^{|\Delta z_{j}(\bm{\sigma})|}-1\big)\cosh\!\big(z_{j}(\bm{\sigma})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{f(\bm{z}_{0})}\big(e^{\|\Delta\mathbf{z}(\bm{\sigma})\|_{\infty}}-1\big)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\cosh\!\big(z_{j}(\bm{\sigma})\big)

\displaystyle=\big(e^{\|\Delta\mathbf{z}(\bm{\sigma})\|_{\infty}}-1\big)\,\frac{f(\mathbf{z}(\bm{\sigma}))}{f(\bm{z}_{0})}.

(48)

\displaystyle\leq\big(e^{\|\Delta\mathbf{z}(\bm{\sigma})\|_{2}}-1\big)\,\frac{f(\mathbf{z}(\bm{\sigma}))}{f(\bm{z}_{0})}.

(49)

We note this exact result can be for small
||\Delta\bm{z}||
approximated as

\;\epsilon(\bm{\sigma})\;\lesssim\;\left\lVert\Delta\mathbf{z}(\bm{\sigma})\right\rVert_{2}\frac{f(\mathbf{z}(\bm{\sigma}))}{f(\bm{z}_{0})}.\;

(50)

The acceptance error (
41
) for the mean-field multi-flip approximation is controlled by the feature-space deviation
\Delta\mathbf{z}
:
the linearized estimate (
50
) shows that bounding
\left\lVert\Delta\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}
suffices to bound
\epsilon
.

Scaling of
\left\lVert\Delta\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}
under separated flips

We now derive the scaling of
\left\lVert\Delta\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}
. Decompose the update into spatially disjoint contributions

\Delta\bm{\sigma}=\sum_{j\in F}\Delta\sigma_{j},\qquad F=\{\text{sites flipped relative to }\bm{\sigma}_{0}\},

(51)

with the total number of flips
M:=|F|
. Assume flips are separated by at least a window
w
,

|i-j|>w\quad\text{for all }i\neq j\in F,

(52)

so that the layerwise perturbations admit a separable decomposition with disjoint spatial support,

\Delta D^{(\ell)}(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}_{0})=\sum_{j\in F}\Delta D^{(\ell)}_{j}\!\left(\Delta\sigma_{j},\bm{\sigma}_{0}\right).

(53)

Writing

\tilde{D}^{(\ell)}_{\alpha}:=\begin{cases}D^{(\ell)}_{0},&\alpha=0,\\[3.0pt]
\Delta D^{(\ell)}_{j},&\alpha=j\in F,\end{cases}

(54)

we may expand the exact and approximate features as

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{z}

\displaystyle=A\,P_{M}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\alpha}\in(\{0\}\cup F)^{L}\\
|\{\alpha_{\ell}\in F\}|\geq 2\end{subarray}}\Bigg(\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\tilde{D}^{(\ell)}_{\alpha_{\ell}}G^{(\ell)}\Bigg)E\,\bm{\sigma}.

(55)

In (
55
) we have dropped all “multi-flip within one word” terms with a single flip (
|\{\alpha_{\ell}\in F\}|=1
), i.e. we retain only cross-insertion words involving two or more distinct flips.

By submultiplicativity of the spectral norm,

\left\lVert AB\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}\,\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{2},

(56)

and, for mean pooling

\displaystyle P_{M}:=\frac{1}{N}\,[\,I_{d}\;I_{d}\;\cdots\;I_{d}\,]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times dN},

(57)

\displaystyle\left\lVert P_{M}\right\rVert_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}},\quad\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{N},

(58)

we obtain the cancellation
\left\lVert P_{M}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert_{2}=1
and thus

\left\lVert\Delta\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}\;\bigg\|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\alpha}\in(\{0\}\cup F)^{L}\\
|\{\alpha_{\ell}\in F\}|\geq 2\end{subarray}}\Big(\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\tilde{D}^{(\ell)}_{\alpha_{\ell}}G^{(\ell)}\Big)\bigg\|_{2}\;\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{2}.

(59)

We absorb fixed-depth and fixed-channel constants into the following envelopes. For each layer define

d_{\ell}:=\big\|D^{(\ell)}_{0}\big\|_{2},\quad\delta_{\ell}:=\sup_{j}\big\|\Delta D^{(\ell)}_{j}\big\|_{2},

(60)

Treating each
G^{(\ell)}
as a Toeplitz/convolution operator, a Young–Schur envelope yields, for
n<\ell
and
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle\big\|\Delta D_{i}^{(\ell)}\,G^{(\ell)}\,(\cdots)\,G^{(n+1)}\,\Delta D_{j}^{(n)}\big\|_{2}\;\leq\;\delta_{\ell}\,\delta_{n}\;C^{(n\to\ell)}\;\kappa(|i-j|),

(61)

where
\kappa
is an effective two-point decay kernel in the same class as the
g_{\ell}
(exponential or power law), and
C^{(n\to\ell)}
collects intermediate operator norms between the two insertions.

Absorb all layer bookkeeping into the finite constant (for fixed depth
L
)

K_{2}:=\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}\,\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{2}\sum_{1\leq n<\ell\leq L}C^{(n\to\ell)}\,\delta_{\ell}\,\delta_{n}\prod_{m\notin[n,\ell]}d_{m}.

(62)

Then the contribution from
two-insertion
cross terms obeys

\;\big\|\Delta\mathbf{z}\big\|_{2}^{(r=2)}\;\leq\;K_{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in F\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\kappa\!\big(|i-j|\big).\;

(63)

Higher-order insertions (
r\geq 3
) are controlled by higher convolutions of
\kappa
and extra small factors
\delta_{\ell}
; for summable kernels (e.g. exponential decay or power law with exponent
>1
) or sufficiently large separation
w
they are subleading compared to (
63
). We therefore focus on (
63
) to obtain scaling laws.

Counting pairs under the separation assumption

Let the flips be ordered along the 1D geometry, and assume (
52
). A worst-case upper bound is obtained when flips are arranged with minimal allowed spacing
w
, so that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in F\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\kappa(|i-j|)\;\leq\;2\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}(M-m)\,\kappa(m\,w),

(64)

where
m
counts the (coarse-grained) separation in units of
w
and the factor
2
accounts for ordered pairs
(i,j)
,
i\neq j
.
Combining (
63
) and (
64
),

\big\|\Delta\mathbf{z}\big\|_{2}^{(r=2)}\;\leq\;2K_{2}\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}(M-m)\,\kappa(m\,w).

(65)

Exponential decay.

If
\kappa(r)\leq C_{\exp}\,e^{-r/\xi}
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}(M-m)\,e^{-mw/\xi}

\displaystyle=M\!\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}e^{-mw/\xi}-\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}m\,e^{-mw/\xi}

\displaystyle\leq M\,\frac{e^{-w/\xi}}{1-e^{-w/\xi}},

(66)

whence

\;\big\|\Delta\mathbf{z}\big\|_{2}^{(r=2)}\;\lesssim\;2K_{2}\,C_{\exp}\,M\,\frac{e^{-w/\xi}}{1-e^{-w/\xi}}\;=\;\mathcal{O}\!\big(M\,e^{-w/\xi}\big).\;

(67)

In particular, for
w\gg\xi
the pairwise error is exponentially small in
w
and only linear in the number of flips
M
.

Power-law decay.

If
\kappa(r)\leq C_{\alpha}\,r^{-\alpha}
for
r\geq w
with
\alpha>0
, then from (
65
)

\big\|\Delta\mathbf{z}\big\|_{2}^{(r=2)}\;\leq\;2K_{2}\,C_{\alpha}\,w^{-\alpha}\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}(M-m)\,m^{-\alpha}.

(68)

The sum admits the asymptotics (as

Appendix E
Sampling Complexity

We model premature freezing of an approximate Metropolis chain via a step–dependent stop (“hazard”) probability (see also Ref.
1
;
35
). The objective is to compute the expected number of successful moves before the chain either freezes or reaches a deterministic cap. At the
k

k=1,2,\dots
) the chain freezes with probability

p_{k}\;=\;p_{0}\,k^{\gamma},\qquad\gamma\geq 0,

(71)

otherwise it proceeds to
k{+}1
. Independently of freezing, the chain is capped after

K\;=\;\frac{N}{w}\;=\;\frac{N^{\,1-\beta}}{w_{0}},\qquad w(N)=w_{0}N^{\beta},\;\;0\leq\beta\leq 1,

(72)

accepted moves. We denote by
M
the number of accepted flips before the first of

or

occurs, and we seek
\mathbb{E}[M]
.

General hazard results

Exact expression.

Survival through step
k
requires not freezing in any of the steps
1,\dots,k

\Pr(M\geq k)\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{k}\!\big(1-p_{0}\,i^{\gamma}\big),\qquad k\leq K,

(73)

and by the tail–sum formula

\;\mathbb{E}[M]\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\;\prod_{i=1}^{k}\!\big(1-p_{0}\,i^{\gamma}\big)\;.

(74)

(If
p_{0}\,i^{\gamma}\geq 1
for some
i

When
p_{0}i^{\gamma}\ll 1
up to the relevant
i

\log(1-x)\approx-x

\sum_{i=1}^{k}i^{\gamma}\approx k^{\gamma+1}/(\gamma{+}1)
to obtain

\mathbb{E}[M]\;\approx\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\!\exp\!\Big(-\frac{p_{0}}{\gamma+1}k^{\gamma+1}\Big)\;\approx\;\int_{0}^{K}e^{-at^{\gamma+1}}\,dt,

(75)

with

Cap-limited

Appendix F
Practical implementation of the screened typewriter sampler

Implementing the screened typewriter sampler on GPUs requires modifying Algorithm 2 to maximize parallelism. We process proposals for an entire batch of chains simultaneously. Because the Independent Scattering Approximation (ISA) renders defects non-interacting, we parallelize computationally intensive steps—such as link tensor construction and ABACUS recurrence—both across chains and across the proposals within a single chain. With amplitudes precomputed, a lightweight loop handles acceptance. To accommodate the limited control flow of GPUs, we do not halt execution when a chain freezes; instead, we use masking to prevent updates to the output buffer, preserving batch synchronization. Optimizing this masking scheme could yield further speedups.

The screened typewriter sampler requires exact error bounds to remain exact. These bounds can be computed by evaluating Eq. (
59
) and Eq. (
50
), which guarantees that the screened typewriter sampler is provably unbiased. Additionally, we provide a pragmatic strategy that is easier to implement than evaluating Eq. (
50
) at every step. We allocate a buffer
\epsilon(k)
indexed by the number of accepted flips
k
. We initialize
\epsilon(k)
to a conservative value (e.g., 0.2) to ensure it exceeds the actual error, thereby forcing the chain to freeze prematurely. During these freeze events, we obtain the empirical error
\tilde{\epsilon}
at no additional computational overhead by comparing the exact and approximate acceptance ratios. If
\epsilon(k)
retains its initial default value, we overwrite it with
\tilde{\epsilon}
; otherwise, we update
\epsilon(k)\leftarrow\max\{\epsilon(k),\gamma\tilde{\epsilon}\}
with
\gamma>1
. This heuristic converges rapidly, self-corrects as the wavefunction evolves, and avoids calibration parameters. We note, however, that it is still a heuristic and can underestimate
\epsilon(k)
early in training, which may introduce bias. For this reason, we discard the first few sweeps at the beginning of each training run. Empirically, after this burn-in, the error bounds are accurate and the sampler satisfies the Metropolis–Hastings rule exactly. All final observables we report (ground-state energy, observables, V-score, etc.) are obtained with a provably exact sampler.

Appendix G
Efficient construction of link tensors

Final-stage links
T^{(l)}
.

The final-stage link tensors
T^{(l)}
only have rank
d
. They can therefore be computed using
d
reverse matrix vector products. In all machine learning libraries this can be computed highly efficiently via
d
backwards passes.

Two-layer links
L^{(l,l-1)}
.

The two-layer link tensors
L^{(l,l-1)}_{j,\Delta j}
can be efficiently computed using convolutions. Specifically, we have

\displaystyle L^{(l,l-1)}_{j,\Delta j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\bm{G}_{j+\Delta j-k}^{(l)}D_{k}^{(l-1)}\bm{G}_{k-j}^{(l-1)}\ .

(95)

Let’s define the composite Green’s function
{\tilde{G}}_{j-k,\Delta j}^{(l)}=\bm{G}_{j-k+\Delta j}\otimes\bm{G}_{-(j-k)}
. This is another convolution kernel meaning we can efficiently construct the link tensor via the matrix valued FFT

\displaystyle L^{(l,l-1)}_{j,\Delta j}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\mathcal{F}(\tilde{G}_{\Delta j}^{(l)})\odot\mathcal{F}(D^{(l-1)}))|_{j}\ .

(96)

We need this link tensor for all
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
but for
\Delta j\in\{-w,\dots,w+1\}
. This means the link tensor can be constructed in
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}WN\log N)\subset\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
.

Multi-layer links
|l-m|\geq 3
.

Direct nested FFTs scale as badly for more than two layers. We can instead use randomized SVDs to obtain the link tensors

L^{(l,m)}\approx U^{(l,m)}\,\tilde{\Sigma}^{(l,m)}_{k}\,V^{(l,m)}

using
k\ll Wd
random probes (linear-time in
N
for fixed
k
) and a small
\mathcal{O}(k^{3})
factorization on slice-shaped matrices. In the worst case
k=Wd
this incurs a total cost of
\mathcal{O}(Wd^{2}N\log N+Wd^{3}N)\subset\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
matrix vector multiplication cost and
\mathcal{O}(W^{3}d^{3})
build cost. Note using randomized SVDs the error can be made arbitrarily small. Nevertheless, in this paper we empirically only need two layers to obtain accurate results. Therefore, we do not require this construction, meaning the ABACUS algorithm returns provably exact results without controlling for error.

Appendix H
Exact algorithm to construct link tensors for arbitrary network depth

In our benchmarks, two layers of DysonNet suffice for high performance, allowing us to construct all link tensors efficiently using FFTs. However, because nested FFT contraction scales poorly with depth, this strategy fails for deeper networks. To demonstrate that ABACUS is mathematically exact and asymptotically efficient, we provide an advanced algorithm for constructing link tensors, even though our experiments do not require it.

We leverage additional structure in the long-range token mixer to compute link tensors efficiently. Consistent with our assumption that long-wavelength propagation is controlled by only a few modes, we model each Green’s-function mixer
G^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
as a hierarchical off-diagonal low-rank (HODLR) matrix. HODLR matrix multplication is highly efficient allowing us to build the link tensors at reasonable cost. We show that SSM impulse-response kernels (finite sums of exponentials) are exactly HODLR.

H.1
Definition of HODLR matrices

Fix an ordering of token indices
\{1,\dots,N\}
along a 1D chain (or along a chosen space-filling order in higher
dimensions), so that contiguous index sets correspond to local spatial neighborhoods.
For simplicity assume
N=2^{p}
and define a recursive binary partition of the index interval into contiguous halves.
At the top level,

I_{1}:=\{1,\dots,N/2\},\qquad I_{2}:=\{N/2+1,\dots,N\}.

(97)

Definition II

(HODLR matrix)
.

A matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is
HODLR of rank
\leq r
(with leaf size
n_{0}
) if it can be represented as

\displaystyle A\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}A_{I_{1},I_{1}}&A_{I_{1},I_{2}}\\[2.0pt]
A_{I_{2},I_{1}}&A_{I_{2},I_{2}}\end{pmatrix},A_{I_{1},I_{2}}=U_{12}V_{12}^{\top},\quad A_{I_{2},I_{1}}=U_{21}V_{21}^{\top},

(98)

where
\mathrm{rank}(A_{I_{1},I_{2}}),\mathrm{rank}(A_{I_{2},I_{1}})\leq r
, and where the
diagonal blocks

A_{I_{1},I_{1}}
and
A_{I_{2},I_{2}}
are again HODLR of rank
\leq r
under the next binary split of
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
,
recursively, until blocks reach size
\leq n_{0}
(stored densely).
Importantly, only the
diagonal blocks are refined recursively
; off-diagonal blocks are stored as low-rank factors
at the level where they appear.

H.2
SSM kernels are exactly HODLR

Consider a 1D translation-invariant Green’s function (Toeplitz kernel)

G_{ij}\;=\;g(|i-j|),\qquad i,j\in\{1,\dots,N\}.

(99)

We focus on SSM impulse responses, which in real space are finite sums of (possibly damped/oscillatory) modes.
For clarity we first treat real exponentials, then state the extension.

Proposition III

(Sum of exponentials
\Rightarrow
exact low-rank off-diagonal blocks)
.

Let

g(t)=\sum_{p=1}^{s}\alpha_{p}\lambda_{p}^{t},\qquad|\lambda_{p}|<1.

(100)

Let
I=\{a,\dots,b\}
and
J=\{c,\dots,d\}
be two disjoint contiguous index intervals with
b<c

(i.e.
I
lies strictly to the left of
J
). Then the off-diagonal block
G_{I,J}
has rank at most
s
and factors as

G_{I,J}\;=\;\sum_{p=1}^{s}u^{(p)}_{I}\bigl(v^{(p)}_{J}\bigr)^{\top},\qquad u^{(p)}_{I}(i)=\lambda_{p}^{-i},\quad v^{(p)}_{J}(j)=\alpha_{p}\,\lambda_{p}^{j}.

(101)

The analogous statement holds for blocks strictly below the diagonal. In particular, for any recursive binary partition
into contiguous halves (Definition
II
), every off-diagonal block is exactly rank
\leq s
, hence
G
is an exact
HODLR matrix with rank bound
r\leq s
.

Proof.

If
i\in I
and
j\in J
with
i<j
, then
|i-j|=j-i
and

G_{ij}=\sum_{p=1}^{s}\alpha_{p}\lambda_{p}^{j-i}=\sum_{p=1}^{s}\bigl(\lambda_{p}^{-i}\bigr)\bigl(\alpha_{p}\lambda_{p}^{j}\bigr),

(102)

which is precisely the separable expansion in Eq. (
101
).
∎

Remark IV

(Damped oscillations / complex poles)
.

Many SSM kernels are sums of damped oscillatory modes corresponding to complex poles inside the unit disk.
Writing such modes as complex exponentials (or equivalently as real
\exp(-t/\xi)\cos(\omega t)
and

\exp(-t/\xi)\sin(\omega t)

proportional to the number of modes (counting complex-conjugate pairs appropriately).

H.4
Parallel construction via scans

The previous section outlined how to construct the link tensors using HODLR multiplication. This section targets construction of multiple link tensors in parallel on GPUs. Naivly, one would need
\mathcal{O}(L^{3})
HODLR multiplications to form all link tensors. However, since HOLDR multipication is associative, one can reduce the cost to
O(L^{2}\log L)
using a parallel prefix (Blelloch) scan
3
.

Fix an endpoint
b\in\{1,\dots,L\}
and consider the reversed list

\bigl(B^{(b)},B^{(b-1)},\dots,B^{(1)}\bigr)
.
A parallel prefix-scan over this list yields all prefix products

\displaystyle P^{(b)}_{t}

\displaystyle:=B^{(b)}\odot B^{(b-1)}\odot\cdots\odot B^{(b-t+1)},\qquad t=1,\dots,b.

(107)

These prefixes correspond exactly to consecutive products ending at
b
via the index map

\displaystyle X_{a,b}

\displaystyle=P^{(b)}_{\,b-a+1}.

(108)

Thus, one scan computes the entire family
\{X_{a,b}\}_{a=1}^{b}
. Running the same scan independently for
different endpoints is precisely “parallel scans starting at different nodes” of the layer chain.

To construct all link tensors, for each target layer
\ell
we run a scan with endpoint
b=\ell-1
to obtain
all segments
\{X_{m+1,\ell-1}\}_{m=0}^{\ell-1}
using Eq. (
108
).
For each
m
, we then form
G^{(\ell)}X_{m+1,\ell-1}
(HODLR multiply+recompress) and extract all local blocks

\mathcal{L}^{(\ell,m)}_{j}
using Eq. (
106
); all patch indices
j
are independent and hence parallel.

H.5
Cost

Let one HODLR multiply+recompress cost

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{mm}}(N,r)

\displaystyle=\Theta\!\bigl(Nr^{2}\log(N/n_{0})\bigr).

(109)

A scan of length
b
uses
\Theta(b)
HODLR multiplications (linear work) and has
\Theta(\log b)
scan stages
(logarithmic depth)
3
. Summing the work over endpoints
b=1,\dots,L
gives
\Theta(L^{2})

HODLR multiplications, hence

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{work}}

\displaystyle=\Theta(L^{2})\,T_{\mathrm{mm}}(N,r)=\Theta\!\bigl(L^{2}Nr^{2}\log(N/n_{0})\bigr).

(110)

Running all endpoint scans in parallel yields overall depth

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{depth}}

\displaystyle=\Theta(\log L)\,T_{\mathrm{mm}}(N,r).

(111)

Forming
B^{(\ell)}
in Eq. (
103
) by diagonal scaling is lower order than Eq. (
110
),
and patch-block extraction in Eq. (
106
) is embarrassingly parallel over
(\ell,m,j)
.

This scan-based construction therefore computes all cached link tensors with
\Theta(\log L)
depth and
log-linear HODLR costs in the system size
N
. This shows link tensors for ABACUS can be efficiently constructed independent of network depth.

The 2D token mixer

is a translationally invariant convolution operator (Toeplitz/circulant under periodic boundaries),

(Gh)_{j,c}\;=\;\sum_{r}G_{j-r,c}\,h_{r,c},

(113)

which is implemented with 2D FFTs channel-wise.

Gh\;=\;\mathcal{F}_{2}^{-1}\!\big(\mathcal{F}_{2}(G)\odot\mathcal{F}_{2}(h)\big),

(114)

where
\mathcal{F}_{2}
denotes the discrete 2D Fourier transform over the
L_{x}\times L_{y}
grid and
\odot
is pointwise multiplication.

The local nonlinearity
D(\bm{\sigma})
acts position-wise on each embedding vector, but may depend on a bounded
patch
around
j
. Concretely, for a fixed patch radius
w
define the square patch

S_{j}\;:=\;\{\,k:\ \|k-j\|_{\infty}\leq w\,\},\qquad W:=|S_{j}|=(2w+1)^{2},

(115)

and parameterize the local vertex as

(D(\bm{\sigma})h)_{j}\;=\;D\!\big(\bm{\sigma}|_{S_{j}}\big)\,h_{j},

(116)

i.e.
D(\cdot)
“attends” to the
W
tokens in the patch (via a small CNN/MLP over the patch) and outputs a
d\times d
matrix multiplying the center token
h_{j}
.

For a single spin flip at position
j
relative to a reference configuration
\bm{\sigma}_{0}
, we decompose each layer’s vertex as

D^{(l)}(\bm{\sigma})\;=\;D^{(l)}_{0}+\Delta D^{(l)},\qquad D^{(l)}_{0}:=D^{(l)}(\bm{\sigma}_{0}),

(117)

where
\Delta D^{(l)}
is nonzero only on a bounded region: in 2D it is supported on (a small union of) patches around the flip, rather than on an interval. Let
P_{S,j}
denote the projector that restricts activations to the patch
S_{j}
(so
P_{S,j}h\in\mathbb{R}^{W\times d}
). Then the link tensors are defined exactly as before, with
P_{S,j}
now projecting onto a patch.

\displaystyle T^{(l)}\;

\displaystyle=\;P_{M}\,G^{f}\!\left(\sum_{M=l}^{L}\prod_{n=l+1}^{M}D^{(n)}_{0}G^{(n)}\right)P_{S,j}^{\top},

(118)

\displaystyle L^{(l,m)}\;

\displaystyle=\;P_{S,j}\,G^{(l)}\!\left(\prod_{n=m+1}^{l-1}D^{(n)}_{0}G^{(n)}\right)P_{S,j}^{\top},\quad 1\leq m\leq l-2.

(119)

Defining patch-restricted activations
\tilde{h}^{(l)}=P_{S,j}h^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{W\times d}
,
\tilde{h}^{(l)}_{0}=P_{S,j}h^{(l)}_{0}
, and
\tilde{G}^{(l)}:=P_{S,j}G^{(l)}P_{S,j}^{\top}
, the DysonNet/ABACUS recurrence is unchanged except for interpreting the restriction as a patch.

\displaystyle\tilde{h}^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\Delta D^{(l)}\left(\tilde{h}^{(l)}_{0}+\tilde{G}^{(l)}\tilde{h}^{(l-1)}+\sum_{m=1}^{l-2}L^{(l,m)}\tilde{h}^{(m)}\right),

(120)

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{\Omega}

\displaystyle=A\!\left(P_{M}\tilde{h}^{(L)}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}T^{(l)}\tilde{h}^{(l)}\right).

(121)

Here
\tilde{G}^{(l)}\tilde{h}^{(l-1)}
denotes propagating a patch-supported input through the convolution and then restricting back to the patch; operationally this is a contraction of the local
(W\times W)
block of the Toeplitz operator (or an equivalent SSM restriction), exactly as in 1D.

Complexity statements carry over verbatim upon replacing the slice width
W=2w+1
by the patch size
W=(2w+1)^{2}
. Given precomputed links, the local-update runtime remains independent of
N
and scales as
O(L^{2}W^{2}d^{2})
,
Importantly, the link tensors depend on the
number of tokens in the restricted region
(
W
), not on whether that region is a 1D interval or a 2D area. For DysonNet, link construction remains log-linear in
N
because all required background propagations reduce to 2D convolutions, which are computed with 2D FFTs (and multi-layer links can be handled by the same low-rank probing strategy, with each probe dominated by
O(N\log N)
FFT-based matvecs for fixed
W,d
.

Here we summarize the S4 parameterization from Ref.
24

17

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\bm{y}(x)=\bar{A}\,\bm{y}(x)+\bar{B}\,\bm{h}(x).

(122)

We discretize it with the bilinear transform

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\Bigl(I-\frac{\Delta}{2}\bar{A}\Bigr)^{-1}\Bigl(I+\frac{\Delta}{2}\bar{A}\Bigr),

(123)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\Bigl(I-\frac{\Delta}{2}\bar{A}\Bigr)^{-1}\Delta\bar{B}.

(124)

where
\Delta
is the step size. For
z_{k}=e^{i2\pi k/N}
, Eq. (
17

\displaystyle G_{c}(z_{k})

\displaystyle=C_{c}\,(I-z_{k}A_{c})^{-1}B_{c}

(125)

\displaystyle=C_{c}\,c(z_{k})\,\bigl(g(z_{k})I-\Delta\bar{A}_{c}\bigr)^{-1}\bar{B}_{c}.

(126)

with

\displaystyle c(z)=\frac{2\Delta}{1+z},\qquad g(z)=\frac{2(1-z)}{1+z}.

(127)

For diagonal
\bar{A}_{c}=\Lambda_{c}
, define the scalar Cauchy-kernel contraction

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{0,c}(z;u,v)=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{v_{i}u_{i}}{g(z)-\Delta\Lambda_{c,i}}.

(128)

For the S4 parameterization
\bar{A}_{c}=\Lambda_{c}+\bm{p}_{c}\bm{q}_{c}^{\top}
(matching the main text), Woodbury gives
24

\displaystyle\begin{split}G_{c}(z)&=c(z)\Big(\widehat{G}_{0,c}(z;\bar{B}_{c},C_{c})\\
&\quad-\widehat{G}_{0,c}(z;\bm{p}_{c},C_{c})\,[1+\widehat{G}_{0,c}(z;\bm{p}_{c},\bm{q}_{c}^{*})]^{-1}\widehat{G}_{0,c}(z;\bar{B}_{c},\bm{q}_{c}^{*})\Big).\end{split}

(129)

This is the Green’s function practically implemented in our network.

For parameterizing the local vertex
D(\bm{\sigma})
we use a short-ranged CNN kernel, as this enforces a finite receptive field
D_{j}(\bm{\sigma})=D_{j}(\sigma_{j-w},\dots,\sigma_{j+w})

\bm{\phi}^{(l)}
(see Fig.
1c

\bm{\phi}_{j}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\bm{\phi}^{(1)}=E_{\phi}\bm{\sigma}
with embedding matrix
E_{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 1}
. Each layer applies (i) a site-wise dense map, (ii) a short-range depth-wise convolution, and (iii) an elementwise nonlinearity
g
(we use
\mathrm{SiLU}
).

\displaystyle\bm{\tilde{\phi}}_{j}^{(l)}

\displaystyle=W_{D}^{(l)}\bm{\phi}_{j}^{(l)}+\bm{w}^{(l)},

(130)

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}_{j}^{(l+1)}

\displaystyle=g\!\bigg(\sum_{r=-w}^{w}K_{r}^{(l)}\odot\bm{\tilde{\phi}}_{j+r}^{(l)}+\bm{k}^{(l)}\bigg).

(131)

Here,

W_{D}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\bm{w}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the site-wise
(dense) weight matrix and bias at layer
l
.
The convolution has half-width
w
with offsets
r\in[-w,w]
; indices
j+r
are taken modulo
N

(circular padding).
K_{r}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the kernel at offset
r
and

\bm{k}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
its bias.
The nonlinearity is applied element-wise and chosen as
\mathrm{SiLU}

g(x)=\mathrm{SiLU}(x)=x/(1+e^{-x})

To match Eq. (

\displaystyle\chi(\bm{\phi}^{(l-1)}_{j})

\displaystyle=g\!\big(A_{D}^{(l)}\bm{\phi}^{(l-1)}_{j}+\bm{b}^{(l)}\big),

(132)

\displaystyle D_{j}(\bm{\sigma})

\displaystyle=U^{(l)}\,\mathrm{diag}\!\big(\chi(\bm{\phi}^{(l-1)}_{j})\big)\,V^{(l)}.

(133)

Here
A_{D}^{(l)}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\bm{b}^{(l)}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d}

is an elementwise nonlinearity (we use
\mathrm{SiLU}

\bm{\phi}^{(l-1)}_{j}

stream additively into the mixer stream before applying the SSM.
In this work we take the parameters to be real. We note as this is inside the network this does not limit expressivity to real valued wavefunctions, as a complex vector can be embedded in a real-valued vector space. The final layer determines the sign structure of the network
