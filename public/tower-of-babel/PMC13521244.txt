Cytokine-independent activation of STAT5 promotes the persistence and function of CD4+ regulatory T cells
Authors: Claire E Shudde, Shams Tabrez, Yena Jin, Alejandra Rosario-Crespo, Kora Reid, Atsunori Kaneshige, Longchuan Bai, Yiqing Yang, Shaomeng Wang, Chang H Kim, Adam H Courtney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegulatory T cells (Tregs) prevent autoimmunity by suppressing self-reactive immune cells that can damage healthy tissues. Accordingly, therapeutics designed to promote Treg expansion and function, including IL-2 receptor (IL-2R) agonists and adoptive Treg therapies, have been used to treat autoimmune disease. However, the efficacy of these strategies has been hindered by the effects of IL-2 on activated immune cell populations, which can exacerbate inflammation, and by the limited persistence and functionality of Treg therapies. Because Tregs are dependent on IL-2R signaling that is mediated by STAT5, we evaluated a strategy to bypass IL-2R and activate STAT5 in a cytokine-independent manner in CD4+ T cells. We determined that an engineered variant of the herpesvirus saimiri tyrosine kinase interacting protein induced the phosphorylation of STAT5 in CD4+ T cells in the absence of IL-2 to promote their survival, as well as the differentiation and maintenance of Tregs ex vivo. This STAT5 activator (aSTAT5) enhanced Treg engraftment in mice and prevented the onset of colitis. Tregs that expressed aSTAT5 better maintained their lineage and were more effective at suppressing pathogenic inflammatory T cells. Our findings demonstrate a new approach to equip Tregs with the signals they need to maintain their suppressive capacity.Keywords: T cell, Treg, STAT5, LCK, T cell therapy, cytokine, IL-2, JAK-STAT, autoimmunity

Introduction

Immune tolerance is necessary to maintain tissue homeostasis and prevent autoimmunity. CD4+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) protect healthy tissues against inflammation and unwanted destruction by suppressing both innate and adaptive immune cells through the release of inhibitory cytokines such as TGFβ and IL-10,1,2 competitive binding to stimulatory factors such as interleukin 2 (IL-2) and CD80/86,3,4,5 and other mechanisms such as the degradation of extracellular ATP into adenosine.6,7 These mechanisms endow Tregs with the ability to regulate inflammation and confer tolerance needed to maintain tissue homeostasis.8,9,10 Loss of tissue homeostasis through Treg dysfunction, both in mouse models and in humans, can result in severe autoimmune disease.11,12,13,14,15,16 Accordingly, therapies have been developed that exploit the multi-modal suppressive abilities of Tregs for the treatment of autoimmune diseases, providing an alternative to single-agent immune modulatory drugs. These therapies include the cytokine IL-2, which can promote the survival and expansion of endogenous Tregs in patients,17,18,19,20,21,22 owing to its essential role in maintaining the Treg lineage.23,24 However, IL-2 can also expand or promote the activity of inflammatory populations such as natural killer (NK) cells and activated T cells.25,26,27 Alternatively, autologous Tregs can be administered to patients with autoimmune diseases.28,29,30 These cell-based therapies have been limited by both the loss of persistence and suppressive function of the transferred Tregs because of insufficient IL-2 signaling.31,32,33 We therefore evaluated a strategy to directly activate STAT5, a downstream mediator of IL-2 signaling, in Tregs in order to enhance their persistence and function.34

Tregs arise from a differentiation process controlled by the transcription factor Forkhead box P3 (FOXP3).35,36 FOXP3 is a master regulator of the Treg lineage, and its deletion in mice results in severe autoimmunity.35,37 Similarly, mutations that disrupt FOXP3 in humans result in immunodysregulation polyendocrinopathy enteropathy X-linked syndrome (IPEX), an often fatal inflammatory condition.14,15,16,38 The induction of FOXP3 occurs upon recognition of self-antigen and gives rise to natural Tregs (nTregs) during T cell development in the thymus,39 whereas peripheral FOXP3 induction generates induced Tregs (iTregs).40,41 The conversion of CD4+ T cells to FOXP3+ Tregs requires TGFβ and IL-2.40,42 IL-2 is critical for functional Tregs,24,43,44,45 and mice that lack IL-2 or IL-2Rα (CD25) spontaneously develop lymphoid hyperplasia and severe autoimmunity.24,46,47,48 Thus, Tregs require an essential signal provided by IL-2 that is initiated upon binding to its receptor (IL-2R), causing Janus kinases (JAK1/3) to become activated and phosphorylate tyrosine residues within IL-2R that recruit STAT5.49 The recruited STAT5 is then phosphorylated by JAK1/3, resulting in its activation and translocation to the nucleus where it regulates gene expression. In Tregs, STAT5 binds to the promoter of the Foxp3 gene to control its expression,23 and cooperates with FOXP3 to maintain expression of CD25.50,51,52,53 This establishes a positive feedback loop between FOXP3 and IL-2-induced JAK1/3-STAT5 signaling needed for the survival and function of Tregs.

A clinical trial for type 1 diabetes evaluated whether the persistence of adoptively transferred Tregs could be increased by IL-2 administration. Low doses of IL-2 transiently increased the proportion of transferred Tregs detected in patients’ peripheral blood.22 However, IL-2 was found to exert effects on other cell populations that included endogenous Tregs, as well as inflammatory cells such as activated NK cells and cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. Additionally, IL-2 is limited by its half-life of less than 20 min due to rapid clearance by the kidneys.54,55 The rapid clearance and short half-life can be mitigated by the addition of antibodies and Fc regions to IL-2, creating long-lived IL-2.56,57,58,59 To target this long-lived IL-2 to Tregs, its affinity for the chains that comprise the IL-2R was altered. IL-2 binds to the heterodimeric IL-2Rβγ with lower affinity than the high-affinity IL-2Rαβγ receptor.60 Mutations to IL-2 (muteins) were identified that decreased its affinity to the IL-2Rβγ chains by 3-fold, which more specifically targets Tregs because they constitutively express IL-2Rα (CD25).61 A long-lived IL-2 mutein decreased the incidence of diabetes in NOD mice; however, because CD25 is also expressed by activated T and NK cells, treatment with long-lived IL-2 muteins also induced their expansion in the pancreas, indicative of on-target, off-Treg effects.57,61 These observations highlight a fundamental challenge in the design of IL-2R agonists: the highest affinity form of IL-2R is expressed by both Tregs and activated T cells, which can include those that are self-reactive and inflammatory. An alternative strategy has been used to engineer the IL-2Rβ chain to recognize an orthogonal IL-2 variant (ortho-IL-2).62 In a model of graft versus host disease, when mice received both Tregs that were transduced to express the IL-2Rβ variant and daily ortho-IL-2, Treg engraftment was improved, and survival was extended 4-fold in comparison with IL-2.63 Rather than deliver both an orthogonal agonist and its receptor, the intracellular activation of STAT5 could be decoupled from IL-2R engagement in the desired Treg population specifically.

We recently described a new platform to activate endogenous STAT proteins in a cytokine-independent manner by engineering the herpesvirus saimiri tyrosine interacting protein (TIP).34,64 TIP binds LCK kinase, a non-JAK, and recruits it to STAT proteins to phosphorylate and activate them. A STAT5 activator (aSTAT5) was generated by incorporating the STAT5 recruitment site derived from the IL-2Rβ chain. When aSTAT5 was expressed by activated CD8+ T cells, they could persist in the absence of IL-2, and tumor-specific T cells exhibited enhanced survival and functionality in vivo.34 Because Treg therapies can be limited by insufficient IL-2 receptor signaling,65 we reasoned that the cytokine-independent activation of STAT5 could restore an essential signal to Tregs. We determined that expression of aSTAT5 was sufficient to promote the survival and function of Tregs in the absence of IL-2. These findings translated in vivo where the engraftment and persistence of adoptively transferred Tregs was enhanced by aSTAT5, which prevented the onset of colitis in mice. Overall, our findings demonstrate that the activation of intracellular signaling effector proteins is an effective strategy to promote the identity and function of therapeutic Tregs.

Results

A cytokine-independent STAT5 activator promotes CD4+ T cell survival

We previously demonstrated that expression of aSTAT5 induced the LCK kinase-mediated phosphorylation and activation of STAT5 in primary CD8+ T cells (Figure 1A).34 We therefore sought to determine whether aSTAT5 could induce phosphorylation of STAT5 in CD4+ T cells, as well as its effects on their survival in the absence of IL-2. Primary murine CD4+ T cells were isolated from spleen and lymph node and activated with anti-CD3/CD28 agonistic antibodies for 24 h and then transduced to express aSTAT5 or an empty vector (control) (Figure 1B). Transduced cells were then cultured in the presence or absence of IL-2 for 3 days, or following an overnight washout period, and the degree of STAT5 phosphorylation was determined by immunoblot (Figure 1C). STAT5 was robustly phosphorylated when cells were cultured in IL-2, and phosphorylation was abrogated in control cells by a 16-h washout (W) to remove IL-2. We confirmed that other STAT proteins were not activated by aSTAT5 expression in CD4+ T cells (Figures S1A and S1B). We also determined that the extent of autophosphorylated (active) LCK was increased, consistent with the binding of aSTAT5 to the SH3 domain of LCK. These data support our prior observation in CD8+ T cells; CD4+ T cells that expressed aSTAT5 maintained levels of phosphorylated STAT5 in the absence of IL-2.

Because activated CD4+ T cells are also reliant on IL-2 for their survival in culture,44,45,66 we predicted that aSTAT5-induced phosphorylation of STAT5 in CD4+ T cells would promote their survival in the absence of exogenous IL-2. CD4+ T cells were activated and transduced to express aSTAT5 or control and then cultured in the presence or absence of IL-2 before cell viability was assessed by flow cytometry (Figures 1D and 1E). We observed that CD4+ T cells cultured in medium supplemented with IL-2 remained viable across treatment groups, as anticipated. Whereas when IL-2 was absent, the viability of CD4+ T cells that expressed aSTAT5 was markedly increased compared with control cells and was comparable with cells that were cultured with IL-2. Interestingly, without IL-2, CD4+ T cells that expressed aSTAT5 did not expand appreciably in comparison with cells cultured in IL-2, which expanded over 5-fold in number over the original number of cells that were seeded (Figure 1F). Notably, IL-2 treatment combined with aSTAT5 expression restored the proliferation of cells that expressed aSTAT5. These observations were confirmed by tracking cell viability and live cell numbers over 3 days (Figures 1G and 1H). Consistent with expression of aSTAT5 sustaining CD4+ T cell survival in the absence of IL-2, cells lacking Thy1.1, a marker for aSTAT5 expression, were mostly absent after 2–3 days of culture (Figures S2A and S2B). Expression of a minimal TIP variant that binds LCK, but lacks a STAT5 binding site, was also insufficient to maintain CD4+ T cell viability (Figures S2C and S2D). Together, these observations demonstrated that expression of aSTAT5 maintained CD4+ T cell viability but did not appreciably induce their proliferation ex vivo.

The translational potential of aSTAT5 was evaluated using primary human CD4+ T cells obtained from two healthy donors. CD4+ T cells were isolated from PBMCs and then activated for 24 h with anti-CD3/CD28 prior to lentiviral transduction (Figures 1I and S3A). The transduced human T cells were expanded and then cultured in the presence or absence of IL-2 for 16–18 h. The degree of STAT5 phosphorylation was assessed by immunoblot, and similar to primary mouse CD4+ T cells, we observed that aSTAT5 induced STAT5 phosphorylation in the absence of IL-2 (Figures 1J and S3B). When the viability of the human CD4+ T cells was quantified over several days, it was observed that viability was also maintained in the absence of IL-2 by aSTAT5 expression (Figure 1K). These findings demonstrate that aSTAT5 functions similarly in primary human CD4+ T cells to induce STAT5 phosphorylation and maintain cell viability in the absence of IL-2 ex vivo.

Expression of aSTAT5 supports Treg differentiation

Conventional CD4+ T cells can be activated and then differentiated to become FOXP3+ induced Tregs (iTregs) ex vivo in the presence of TGFβ and IL-2.67 The signaling pathways activated by the IL-2 receptor, notably the JAK1/3-STAT5, PI3K-AKT, and RAS-ERK pathways, were therefore assessed to compare the effects of IL-2 and aSTAT5.68 CD4+ T cells that expressed aSTAT5 or control were cultured for 3 days in the presence or absence of IL-2 and then lysed. The phosphorylation of AKT, ERK, and STAT5 regulatory sites was assessed by immunoblot as a readout of their activation. We observed that AKT, ERK, and STAT5 were phosphorylated when the cells were cultured in medium supplemented with IL-2. However, when IL-2 was washed out or absent, aSTAT5 was observed to induce the phosphorylation of STAT5, but not ERK (Figures 2A and S4C). Minimal AKT phosphorylation was observed in cells that expressed aSTAT5 in the absence of IL-2. Because IL-2 robustly activated STAT5, AKT, and ERK, we investigated the contribution of each pathway to the differentiation of conventional CD4+ T cells into iTregs (denoted as Tregs hereafter) ex vivo using inhibitors that targeted AKT (AKTi VIII, denoted AKTi),69 MEK (trametinib, denoted MEKi),70 and STAT5 through PROTAC-induced degradation of STAT5 (AK-2292, denoted STAT5i).71 The effect of the titrated doses of the inhibitors on CD4+ T cell viability was assessed by imaging cytometry after 24 h of treatment to determine a dose response (Figure S4A). Inhibitor concentrations that did not result in cell death were further analyzed by immunoblot to determine whether phosphorylation of AKT, ERK, and STAT5 was inhibited; each inhibitor was determined to be effective at 5 μM (Figures S4B and S4D). The contribution of AKT, ERK, and STAT5 on Treg differentiation was evaluated by culturing activated CD4+ T cells for 5 days with DMSO (vehicle) or inhibitor in Treg differentiation medium that contained TGFβ, IL-2, and antibodies that neutralized IL-4 and IFNγ. Both AKTi and STAT5i caused a reduction in viability, whereas viability was unaffected by MEKi (Figure S5A). The effects of pathway inhibition on Treg differentiation were determined by flow cytometry to quantify the proportion of FOXP3+ Tregs (Figures 2B and S5B). The majority of the vehicle-treated cells were FOXP3+ Tregs (66% FOXP3+), which was markedly decreased by STAT5i (17% FOXP3+, p = 0.0006). Treatment with MEKi did not appear to impact the proportion of FOXP3+ cells, whereas the proportion of FOXP3+ cells was slightly increased by AKTi as compared with the control. These observations confirmed that STAT5 was needed for Treg differentiation and that the RAS-ERK and PI3K-AKT pathways were dispensable.

We therefore investigated whether cytokine-independent activation of endogenous STAT5 could substitute for IL-2 during Treg differentiation. We first evaluated whether aSTAT5 expression alone was sufficient to induce Treg differentiation in the absence of TGFβ. CD4+ T cells were transduced to express aSTAT5 or control and cultured for 5 days in the presence or absence of IL-2. Control cells cultured in the absence of IL-2 lacked sufficient viability (<5%) by day 5 for analysis (Figures 2C and 2D); the remaining treatment groups were analyzed by flow cytometry, and the proportion of FOXP3+ cells was determined (Figures S6A and S6B). We found that IL-2 alone and expression of aSTAT5 alone were insufficient to induce the differentiation of FOXP3+ cells (Figure S7). We next compared differentiation in the presence of medium that contained TGFβ in the presence or absence of IL-2. Blocking antibodies against both IL-2 and IL-2Rβ (anti-IL-2/R) were used to assess any potential contribution of IL-2 production that could act in a paracrine or autocrine manner. The viability of the cells was assessed after 5 days, and we found that expression of aSTAT5 was sufficient to maintain viability in the absence of IL-2, as well as in the presence of neutralizing anti-IL-2/R (Figure 2D). When activated and transduced CD4+ T cells were cultured in Treg differentiation medium that contained TGFβ with IL-2 (Figure 2C), a robust population of Tregs (75% FOXP3+) was obtained (Figures 2E and 2F). Excitingly, when T cells that expressed aSTAT5 were in Treg differentiation medium that lacked IL-2, we observed comparable levels of FOXP3+ cells. Similarly, 98% of cells that expressed aSTAT5 or control across the culture conditions were positive for IL-2Rα (CD25), which is sustained by IL-2 and constitutively expressed by Tregs (Figures 2G and 2H). Thus, treatment with anti-IL-2/R blocking antibodies did not decrease the proportion of FOXP3+ or CD25+ cells. Together, these data demonstrate that cytokine-independent activation of STAT5 in the presence of TGFβ is sufficient for Treg differentiation ex vivo.

Treg lineage and function is maintained ex vivo by aSTAT5

FOXP3 expression is required to maintain Treg lineage, and ablation of Foxp3 in mature Tregs results in the loss of their immunosuppressive capabilities.37 Because STAT5 is essential for the continued expression of FOXP3,23,72 and aSTAT5 could promote the differentiation of FOXP3+ Tregs when combined with TGFβ, we next assessed whether aSTAT5 could maintain FOXP3 expression by Tregs. CD4+ T cells that expressed aSTAT5 or control were cultured in Treg differentiation medium for 5 days before cells were washed to remove IL-2 and TGFβ. The medium was then replaced with cytokine- and antibody-free medium (CF) or differentiation medium (DM) and cultured for an additional 3 days, with or without the addition of anti-IL-2/R blocking antibodies (Figure 3A). After 3 days, we assessed viability to determine the effects of IL-2 removal and to ensure cells were sufficiently viable across conditions to then assess FOXP3 and CD25 levels (Figures 3B, S6A, and S6C). The cells that expressed aSTAT5 maintained their viability over the course of the 3 days across culture conditions, including in the presence of anti-IL-2/R blocking antibodies, whereas control viability was reduced by anti-IL-2/R to a similar extent as cells maintained in CF medium. Furthermore, aSTAT5 resulted in a markedly greater percentage of FOXP3+ cells when compared with control cells and was comparable with CD4+ T cells continually cultured in DM (Figure 3C). Notably, when aSTAT5 was expressed, anti-IL-2/R did not impact the proportion of FOXP3+ cells, and high levels of CD25 were maintained (Figure 3D). These data are consistent with reports that STAT5 activity is needed to maintain FOXP3 expression in Tregs.72,73 To verify that the observed aSTAT5 lineage maintenance required LCK (Figure 1A), we inhibited its activity with PP2, an SRC family kinase inhibitor. PP2 treatment prevented the ability of aSTAT5 to maintain FOXP3 levels in CF medium similar to treatment with STAT5i (Figure S8). Collectively, these data indicate that aSTAT5 expression can replace IL-2 to maintain FOXP3 ex vivo.

Because aSTAT5 was sufficient to maintain FOXP3+ and CD25+ Tregs in the absence of IL-2, we evaluated whether their suppressive function was also maintained ex vivo. CD4+ conventional T cells (Tconv) (CD45.2+) were isolated from mice and stained with cell trace violet (CTV). Tregs (CD45.1+) were generated that expressed aSTAT5 or control then combined with Tconv cells in CF medium lacking exogenous IL-2. The co-cultures were activated with anti-CD3/CD28 beads and cultured for 3 days. Tconv proliferation was assessed by flow cytometry to quantify the extent of CTV dilution that occurred upon each cell division (Figure 3E). Tregs that expressed aSTAT5 exhibited increased suppressive capacity across Treg:Tconv ratios, which corresponded to decreased Tconv proliferation. Specifically, the proportion of undivided Tconv was greater in the presence of Tregs that expressed aSTAT5 as compared with Tconv cultured with control Tregs (p = 0.0248) (Figure 3F). The percent suppression was also greater when Tconv were co-cultured with Tregs that expressed aSTAT5 as compared with control Tregs (p < 0.0001) (Figure 3G). Thus, when Tregs expressed aSTAT5, Tconv underwent fewer divisions and exhibited decreased proliferation compared with when Tconv were cultured with control Tregs. In aggregate, our ex vivo findings revealed that expression of aSTAT5 was sufficient to maintain Treg viability and FOXP3/CD25 levels, as well as their capacity to suppress Tconv proliferation ex vivo.

Treg engraftment and lineage maintenance in vivo are enhanced by aSTAT5

Treg therapies have been limited by their in vivo persistence.22,28,29 Because Tregs that expressed aSTAT5 remained viable in the absence of IL-2 ex vivo, we investigated whether Treg engraftment could be enhanced by aSTAT5 expression. CD4+ T cells were isolated from BoyJ (CD45.1+) mice and transduced to express aSTAT5 or control, and cultured in Treg differentiation medium for 5 days. The transduced Tregs were characterized to determine transduction efficiency and FOXP3 levels prior to their adoptive transfer into C57BL/6 (CD45.2+) mice that had been conditioned using a non-myeloablative dose of radiation (6 Gy) (Figures 4A and S9A). Five days post-transfer, the lymph nodes and spleens were harvested to quantify the proportion of transferred CD45.1+ Tregs by flow cytometry. Interestingly, aSTAT5 expression increased the proportion of CD45.1+ Tregs compared with control in both the spleen (p < 0.0001) and lymph nodes (p < 0.0001) (Figures 4B and 4C). Because our findings indicate that aSTAT5 could sustain FOXP3 levels in the absence of IL-2 ex vivo, we further evaluated the proportion of FOXP3+ cells. CD45.1+ Tregs that expressed aSTAT5 were to a greater extent FOXP3+ in both the lymph nodes (p = 0.0033) and spleen (p = 0.0149), as compared with the control (Figures 4D and 4E). Similarly, aSTAT5 expression maintained a higher proportion of CD25+ transferred Tregs in both the lymph nodes (p = 0.0027) and spleen (p = 0.0014) (Figures 4F and 4G).

To further investigate whether aSTAT5 influences Treg lineage in vivo over a greater duration, we determined the extent to which Treg identity was maintained after 3 weeks in vivo. Differentiated Tregs that expressed aSTAT5 or control were transferred into Rag1−/− mice (Figures 4H and S9B). After 3 weeks, mononuclear cells were isolated from the spleen or colon lamina propria, and FOXP3+ cells were analyzed (Figures 4I and 4J). The proportion of FOXP3+ cells was markedly increased when Tregs expressed aSTAT5 as compared with the control in both the colon (p < 0.0007) and the spleen (p = 0.0158). Furthermore, the absolute number of Tregs that expressed aSTAT5 was increased in the colon as compared with control Tregs (p = 0.0036). These observations are consistent with aSTAT5 enhancing Treg engraftment and FOXP3+ lineage maintenance in vivo.

aSTAT5 expression by Tregs stabilizes FOXP3 expression in colitis

We sought to test whether cytokine-independent STAT5 activation with aSTAT5 expression could maintain Treg lineage and function in a colitis model. Colitis can be induced by naive CD4+ T cells (Tnv) that differentiate into inflammatory Th1 and Th17 cells in RAG-1 (Rag1−/−) or RAG-2 (Rag2−/−) deficient mice.74,75,76 CD45.1+ CD4+ T cells were transduced and differentiated into FOXP3+ Tregs ex vivo, then characterized to determine transduction efficiency and FOXP3 levels, as well as transcription factors characteristic of inflammatory T cells (e.g., T-bet), prior to adoptive transfer into recipient mice (Figures S9D and S9E). CD4+CD25−CD62L+CD44− naive T cells (Tnv) (CD45.2+) were isolated by FACS and transferred into recipient Rag1−/− mice (Figure S9C). Two cohorts also simultaneously received Tregs (CD45.1+) that expressed aSTAT5 or control (Figure 5A). We performed the in vivo suppression experiment at two different Treg:Tnv cell ratios (0.5:1 for a low Treg dose and 1:1 for a high Treg dose). Weight was monitored as an indicator of colitis onset and severity. Mice that received only Tnv cells experienced weight loss, consistent with the onset of colitis (Figures 5B, S10A, and S10B). Whereas mice that also received Tregs (control or aSTAT5) were able to maintain their body weight, consistent with reduced colitis onset. When a humane endpoint was reached, tissues were isolated, and colon length was measured as a readout of inflammation damage (Figure 5C). Colons obtained from the Tnv only group were shorter than those isolated from the mice that received Tregs that expressed aSTAT5 or control (Figures 5D, S10C, and S10D), indicating that the administered Tregs reduced colonic shortening. Tregs that expressed aSTAT5 prevented colon shortening to a greater extent than controls (p = 0.0366). Colonic tissue was sectioned and assessed by histology to determine the extent of lymphocyte infiltration and tissue hyperplasia (Figures 5E and 5F). At the high Treg dose, no difference was observed between the two Treg treatment groups because even control Tregs were efficient in suppressing tissue inflammation (Figures S10B–S10G). However, at the low Treg dose, when tissue sections were scored in a blinded manner, moderate to severe inflammation (grades 4–5) was observed in the tissue from the Tnv only group, whereas tissues from mice that received Tregs that expressed aSTAT5 or control exhibited reduced inflammation (grades 1–3). Notably, aSTAT5 expression reduced the inflammation score to a greater extent than control (p = 0.0044). Colitis was not induced by transferred Tregs that expressed aSTAT5 or control in the absence of Tnv (Figure S11A). Overall, tissue and histological analysis indicated that aSTAT5 Tregs were functional and able to reduce inflammation in comparison with mice that received only naive T cells, with greater control of inflammation than control Tregs.

Evaluation of adoptively transferred Tregs in a colitis model

To determine whether aSTAT5 supported Treg function, we examined the expansion of CD45.2+ CD4+ T cells (target cells) and phenotype of CD45.1+ CD4+ T cells (administered Tregs) to determine the extent of Treg persistence and their effects on the expansion of inflammatory target cells. Mononuclear cells were isolated from the colon lamina propria and spleens of Rag1−/− mice and analyzed by flow cytometry (Figure S11B). A greater proportion of transferred CD45.1+ cells were FOXP3+CD25+ when aSTAT5 was expressed as compared with the control in both the colon (p = 0.0159) and spleen (p = 0.0002) (Figures 6A and 6C). The degree of Treg suppression was assessed by determining the extent to which CD45.2+ cells expanded in the colon and could produce inflammatory cytokines. The number of CD45.2+ cells was greatly increased in the colons of the Tnv group that did not receive Tregs, and consistent with increased inflammation (Figures 5D–5F), possessed greater numbers of cells positive for TNFα, IFNγ, and IL-17 (Figure 6B). A similar trend was observed in the spleen, where expansion of CD45.2+ cells was also decreased by Tregs that expressed aSTAT5 versus control (p = 0.0159) (Figure 6D), whereas the number of inflammatory CD45.2+ cells was overall much lower in the spleen across treatment groups. When CD45.2+ cells were analyzed for FOXP3, we observed a greater proportion of FOXP3+ cells in the spleens of mice that received Tregs that expressed aSTAT5 versus control (p = 0.0159) (Figure 6E), whereas greater variability was observed in the colon (Figures 6F and S11C). Overall, we observed increased levels of FOXP3+CD25+ Tregs that expressed aSTAT5, which resulted in reduced numbers of inflammatory CD45.2+ cells.

Discussion

We determined that aSTAT5 could induce the phosphorylation of STAT5 in primary mouse and human CD4+ T cells, which was sufficient to maintain their viability in the absence of IL-2. Interestingly, activated CD4+ cells that expressed aSTAT5 did not expand appreciably ex vivo in contrast to our prior observations in CD8+ T cells.34,64 Expansion could be rescued when cells that expressed aSTAT5 were treated with IL-2. These observations suggest that a pathway activated by IL-2 is needed for CD4+ T cell expansion, which is not similarly activated by aSTAT5. IL-2 is known to activate the JAK1/3-STAT5, PI3K-AKT, and MEK-ERK pathways,68 whereas we found that aSTAT5 predominately activated STAT5, but not AKT or ERK to an appreciable extent. STAT5 is known to upregulate anti-apoptotic proteins such as BCL2 in CD4+ T cells,77 which is consistent with the ability of aSTAT5 to maintain CD4+ viability. The PI3K-AKT pathway in particular is known to control T cell proliferation by regulating the activation of cell cycle progression proteins such as CDK1, Cyclin D3, and Cyclin E.78,79,80,81 Altogether, these data show that STAT5 activity is sufficient for CD4+ T cell survival but does not appear to contribute substantially to cell cycle progression ex vivo. These findings indicate that the IL-2 receptor can be bypassed to provide tailored signals to CD4+ T cells by activating intracellular mediators of signaling pathways like STAT5. A potential limitation of this cytokine-independent approach to STAT5 activation is the risk of uncontrolled expansion of transferred cells in vivo due to the pro-survival signal it provides. Although our ex vivo data suggest CD4+ T cell expansion by STAT5 activity is minimal in comparison with IL-2, further studies are needed to assess the potential interplay between aSTAT5 and the homeostatic signals present in vivo to fully assess the potential for uncontrolled proliferation. This risk could be mitigated however by new strategies that have been developed to regulate the survival of T cell therapies, such as inducible caspase-9 expression, and gene expression using synthetic gene circuits.82,83,84 Our prior study also demonstrated that the dependence of aSTAT5 on the activity of the tyrosine kinase LCK, rather than JAKs, rendered T cells sensitive to small molecules inhibitors that block LCK activity, suggesting potential “off switch” strategies.

Current Treg therapies have been limited by poor persistence and loss of their suppressive function due to insufficient IL-2 signaling.22,31,32,33,85 We determined that aSTAT5 alone did not induce FOXP3+ Tregs from conventional CD4+ T cells ex vivo unless it was combined with TGFβ. We further demonstrated that cytokine-independent activation of STAT5 by aSTAT5 expression was sufficient to maintain FOXP3 in the absence of IL-2 and TGFβ ex vivo. Although FOXP3 was maintained by aSTAT5, even in the presence of anti-IL-2/R blocking antibodies, FOXP3 levels were qualitatively reduced in comparison with cells cultured with IL-2. These observations suggest that the level of STAT5 activity influences the extent of FOXP3 expression. Interestingly, when transferred Tregs that expressed aSTAT5 were recovered from mice, their FOXP3 levels were qualitatively higher than control cells. These differences could be driven by cytokines or other signals present in vivo that may combine with aSTAT5 to maintain higher levels of FOXP3 in comparison with control Tregs, consistent with their improved ability to remain FOXP3+ in vivo. In more long-term studies using Rag1−/− mice, although FOXP3 was maintained by aSTAT5 to a greater extent than control, a decrease in the proportion of FOXP3+ cells still occurred. This decrease was comparable with ex vivo derived Tregs that expressed an engineered IL-2R receptor that were expanded with an orthogonal variant of IL-2.63 These observations could be attributed to lineage plasticity and suggest considerations beyond IL-2R-STAT5 signaling to maintain FOXP3+ Tregs in vivo.86,87,88 aSTAT5 reduced inflammatory CD45.2+ cell populations to a greater extent than control in a colitis model. However, the overall number of CD45.2+ cells were also lowered by control Tregs resulting in reduced numbers of inflammatory CD45.2+ cells for comparison. Additional inflammatory models are needed to fully define the effects of aSTAT5 on Treg efficacy.

The degree of FOXP3 stability can be dependent on epigenetic regulation of the Foxp3 gene.89 In thymic-derived nTregs, the Foxp3 locus is stably demethylated on the CpG island in the first intron of the Foxp3 gene, while H4 is acetylated.90,91,92,93,94 These epigenetic alterations confer nTregs with greater lineage stability in comparison with iTregs.95 Accordingly, nTregs have been used therapeutically but face similar limitations to their persistence in vivo, as well as other considerations such ease of genetic modification and obtaining sufficient cell numbers. When nTreg are isolated from patients with autoimmune disease, their therapeutic potential could also vary.30 These observations suggest that realizing the full potential of ex vivo derived Tregs will require both STAT5 activity and epigenetic stability. We predict that reduced FOXP3 following Treg transfer, despite cytokine-independent STAT5 activity, reflects the epigenetic instability of ex vivo derived Tregs, a limitation of our approach that warrants further investigation. We anticipate that expression of aSTAT5 could be combined with strategies to improve Treg epigenetic stability. Recently, T cell activation without co-stimulation of CD28 in conjunction with a CDK8/19 inhibitor resulted in iTregs that had demethylation patterns comparable with nTregs.96 Interestingly, targeted de-methylation of the FOXP3-TSDR was sufficient to induce FOXP3 expression but did not result in a functional Treg phenotype.97 Thus, generating iTregs with favorable epigenetic properties will likely require strategies to activate the IL-2R-STAT5 pathway.

To bolster the efficacy of adoptive Treg therapies, ex vivo derived Tregs have been engineered to target the IL-2R-JAK1/3-STAT5 pathway. To avoid the limitations of systemic IL-2 administration, an orthogonal IL-2Rβ/IL-2 (ortho-IL-2) was expressed by Tregs and determined to enhance their engraftment and persistence.63 However, these Tregs were dependent on daily administration of ortho-IL-2 to mice. Alternatively, a transgenic mouse model where STAT5b was constitutively active in CD4+ T cells was used to promote Treg differentiation in vivo, without requiring additional treatment with IL-2.98 Here, we evaluated an approach to activate endogenous STAT5 in a cytokine-independent manner that can be combined with T cell engineering strategies to bypass requirements for IL-2 receptor agonists in therapeutic Tregs. A potential advantage of this approach is the small size of the aSTAT5 construct (∼100 amino acids), which as a single gene can be more readily combined into retroviral vectors limited by size. Because aSTAT5 recruits LCK to phosphorylate and activate STAT5 intracellularly, it eliminates requirements for administration of cytokines and their effects on other cell populations. Similarly, IL-2R signaling has been enforced by expression of a tethered IL-2R agonist that improved Treg persistence in NSG mice.99 This study tethered the orthogonal IL-2 variant to the engineered IL-2Rβ to cause constitutive signaling in Tregs, which maintained FOXP3 expression and limited the onset of diabetes in NOD mice. Alternatively, an IL-2–TGFβ surrogate agonist that engaged both IL-2 and TGFβ receptor pathways induced Tregs in vivo and was effective in mouse models of allergen-induced allergic inflammation and self-antigen-driven autoimmune neuroinflammation.100 An attractive feature of aSTAT5 is that it only acts on STAT5 and is likely to avoid potential effects of constitutively activating the PI3K-AKT and MEK-ERK pathways. Moreover, as suggested by the induction of FOXP3 when aSTAT5 was combined with TGFβ, the aSTAT5 construct could be further engineered to provide signals that produce tailored Treg phenotypes. Future studies will be needed to address potential barriers to clinical translation, which would include analysis of whether the engineered TIP variant from which aSTAT5 is derived possess immunogenicity and engineering it to mitigate these risks. Additional studies are also needed to assess the effects of aSTAT5 on signaling pathways and gene expression profiles using human iTregs that have been generated using patient samples according to clinical manufacturing procedures being developed for clinical trials.85,101

In aggregate, we demonstrate that cytokine-independent activation of STAT5 can promote CD4+ T cell survival and facilitate Treg differentiation ex vivo in the presence of TGFβ. These observations translated in vivo, where STAT5 activation enhanced the engraftment and FOXP3 levels of adoptively transferred iTregs, which were protective in an inflammatory colitis model. Overall, our findings highlight how intracellular pathways can be directly activated in Tregs to provide the signals needed by these cells to persist and maintain their suppressive function.

Materials and methods

Mice

Mice were housed in a specific-pathogen-free facility at the University of Michigan (U-M). All mice were treated and raised in accordance with NIH guidelines and according to protocols that were approved by U-M animal care ethics and veterinary committees. All mice were bred and maintained on the C57BL/6 genetic background and used between 6 and 12 weeks of age. Both male and female mice were used for in vivo engraftment experiments, and male mice were used for the colitis studies. All mice were age and sex matched. The C57BL/6, BoyJ (CD45.1), and Rag1−/− mice used for this study were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory.

Cell lines

Phoenix cells were obtained from ATCC and kept in culture for no more than 2 weeks. Lenti-X 293T (LX293T) cells were obtained from Takara Bio. Both phoenix and LX293T cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 2 mM glutamine. Primary T cells were cultured in complete RPMI (RPMI 1640 medium, 10% FBS, 2 mM glutamine, 1× penicillin/streptomycin [Gibco], 1× MEM non-essential amino acids [Gibco], 1 mM sodium pyruvate [Gibco], 10 mM HEPES [Corning], 50 μM 2-mercaptoethanol [Gibco]). All cells were cultured under standard conditions at 37°C and 5% CO2.

Plasmids

The plasmids MSCV_IRES_mCherry (no. 52114) and MSCV_IRES_Thy1.1 (no. 17442) were obtained from Addgene and modified by insertion of a Gateway cassette. aSTAT5 constructs were transferred into MSCV vectors with LR clonase as previously described.34 Lentiviral pHR EF1α_GFP and pHR EF1α_aSTAT5_Thy1.1 constructs are previously described.64 The sequences for the aSTAT5 and Minimal constructs are provided in Table S1.

Primary murine T cell purification and differentiation

Primary murine CD4+ T cells were purified from the lymph nodes and spleen of mice with biotinylated antibodies (Table 1) and magnetic streptavidin nanobeads (BioLegend). Lymphoid tissue was ruptured and passed through a 40 μm strainer with cells suspended in complete RPMI. Cells were washed in FACS buffer (PBS [Gibco] supplemented with 2% FBS and 2 mM EDTA). For negative selection, cells were resuspended at a concentration of 100 × 106 cells/1 mL of FACs with 5% rat serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch) and 30 μL CD4+ selection cocktail /100 × 106 cells containing antibodies against CD8α, CD19, CD45R, CD11b, CD11c, CD49b, TER119, and CD24. After 10 min incubation at room temperature (RT), cells were washed in FACS and resuspended at the same concentration with 9% streptavidin nanobeads for a 5 min incubation at RT followed by a 5 min incubation in a magnet. The supernatant that contained the CD4+ T cells was collected for experimental use. For positive selection of CD4+ T cells, the same procedure was followed, with the following modifications: rat serum concentration was increased to 7.5%, CD4+ biotinylated antibodies were used (Mojosort Mouse CD4+ selection kit), magnetic streptavidin beads were used at a concentration of 1% total volume, and the cells were incubated in the magnet twice with the supernatant discarded between each round. Thy1.1 purification after transduction was modified from the positive selection protocol by omitting rat serum and additional washes. Thy1.1 biotinylated antibody was incubated with cells for 10-min before selection with streptavidin beads. Primary T cells were then activated on anti-CD3/CD28 coated plates (1.5 mL/well of PBS supplemented with 5 μg/mL anti-CD3 and 2.5 μg/mL anti-CD28 on a non-TC treated 6-well plate coated at RT for 2 h or overnight at 4°C). For Treg differentiation, primary CD4+ T cells were plated on anti-CD3/CD28 coated plates and supplemented with Treg differentiation medium composed of 2 μg/mL TGFβ (BioLegend), 10 ng/mL IL-2 (BioLegend), 10 μg/mL anti-IL-4 (BioLegend), 10 μg/mL anti-IFNγ (BioLegend). Fresh differentiation medium was administered every day for 5 days (including the day of activation) for differentiation into induced Tregs (iTregs). All ex vivo derived iTregs underwent Thy1.1 positive selection prior to analysis.

Antibodies and reagents

Primary human T cell isolation

The use of human samples in this study was reviewed by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board. Anonymous human whole blood samples obtained from healthy donors were provided by the U-M Platelet Pharmacology and Physiology Core (HUM00258983). Whole blood was diluted 1:1 with PBS and underlaid with Ficoll-Paque PLUS (Cytiva). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were separated by a Ficoll density gradient using centrifugation (400 g, 20 min). PBMCs were washed with FACS buffer and then counted prior to T cell isolation. Primary human CD4+ T cells were purified by positive selection using an EasySep Human CD4 Positive Selection Kit II (StemCell Technologies) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Once purified, human CD4+ T cells were activated with plate-bound antibodies [anti-human CD3 (5 μg/mL) and anti-human CD28 (2.5 μg/mL)] in complete RPMI medium. Primary human CD4+ T cells were activated for 24 h before lentiviral transduction.

Inhibitor assays

IL-2 signaling pathways were inhibited with AKTi VIII (AKTi) (EMD Millipore), Trametinib (MEKi) (Selleck Chem), and AK-2292, a STAT5 specific PROTAC (STAT5i) (provided by the Wang lab).71 CD4+ T cells were purified with positive selection from a C57BL/6 mouse between 8 and 12 weeks of age, and activated with plate-bound antibodies as described above. Cells were harvested 24 h later and plated on a 96-well flat-bottomed plate (Thermo Fischer Scientific) with treated with starting concentrations of 125 μM of AKTi, 0.5 μM of MEKi, and 25 μM of AK-2292 and a DMSO control followed by serial dilutions of the compounds. After a further 24 h, cells were stained with Hoechst and propidium iodide, and viability was measured with imaging cytometry. Concentrations of interest that did not cause cell death or were below the IC50 after the 24 h were further assessed by immunoblot for their ability to inhibit phosphorylation of their targeted pathways. To assess the role of pathway inhibition in Treg differentiation, primary CD4+ T cells were isolated and activated as described previously in the presence of the differentiation medium. 24 h after activation, 5 μM of the AKTi, MEKi, or STAT5i were added to the culture. Treg differentiation medium containing inhibitors was added every 24 h. The cells were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry to assess their differentiation status.

Retroviral transduction

Phoenix cells were plated in a 6-well plate at a density to reach 60% confluency after overnight culture. The next day, these cells were transfected with pCL_Eco and a viral expression vector using TransIT-LT1 (Mirus Bio). After 24 h, the medium was replaced with complete RPMI. On the same day, CD4+ T cells were isolated and activated. A 6-well plate was coated with RetroNectin (Takara Bio) (15 μg/mL in PBS) overnight at 4°C. The next day, the RetroNectin plate was washed twice with PBS and blocked with complete RPMI. Viral supernatant was harvested and plated at 1 mL/well and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. T cells were harvested from their activation plates, washed, and resuspended in fresh media with added differentiation conditions. Cells (0.5–1.5×106 cells/mL) were plated at a 1:1 volume ratio and spun at 2000 g for 1 h at 30°C before culture overnight. The next day, transduced cells were purified by Thy1.1 positive selection to enrich the proportion of transduced cells which typically exceeded 80% (Thy1.1+).

Lentiviral transduction

LX293T cells were seeded on TC-treated 6-well plates and cultured for one to two days before transfection. At 60–70% confluency, LX293T cells were transfected with packaging plasmid pCMV-dR8.91, envelope plasmid pMD2.G, and a viral expression vector using TransIT-LT1 (Mirus Bio). After 18–24 h, the medium containing transfection reagent was replaced with high-bovine serum albumin (BSA) medium (RPMI 1640 medium, 30% FBS, 2 mM glutamine, MEM nonessential amino acids [Gibco], penicillin/streptomycin [Gibco], 1 mM sodium pyruvate [Gibco], 50 μM 2-mercaptoethanol [Gibco], and 10 mM HEPES [Corning]). Viral supernatant was collected 24 h later and syringe-filtered (PVDF 0.45 μm; Millipore Sigma) to remove any packaging cells. 1 mL/well of viral supernatant was added to RetroNectin-coated non-TC-treated 6-well plates and incubated in a tissue culture incubator (37°C, 5% CO2) for 1 h. After incubation, 1 mL of activated CD4+ T cell suspension (400,000–500,000 cells per well) was added. The plates containing both viral supernatant and activated CD4+ T cells were centrifuged (2,000 g, 1 h, 30°C) before culturing overnight. Transduction efficiency was measured 48 h later by GFP fluorescence or Thy1.1 staining. Total cell counts were measured using a LUNA Automated Cell Counter or a hemocytometer.

Immunoblot analysis

Transduced, primary murine and human CD4+ T cells were washed and resuspended in complete RPMI with or without IL-2 (10 ng/mL) 16–18 h prior to lysate preparation. After 16–18 h, CD4+ T cells were washed and resuspended in plain RPMI for 15 min at 37°C. Cells were lysed by addition of lysis buffer containing a final concentration of 1% NP-40 (EMD Millipore), Na3VO4 (2 mM), NaF (10 mM), Halt protease inhibitor cocktail (1×; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and EDTA (0.5 mM; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primary murine CD4+ T cells were lysed at a concentration of 20 × 106 cells/mL. Primary human CD4+ T cells were lysed at a concentration of 15×106 cells/mL. Lysates were placed on ice for 15 min before a 10-min centrifugation at 16,000g at 4°C to remove debris. The supernatant was harvested and combined with 6× SDS sample buffer and heated at 95°C for 3 min before electrophoreses with NuPAGE 4%–12% Bis-Tris gels (Invitrogen). Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore Sigma). Membranes were then blocked in 3% BSA TBS-T (tris-buffered saline supplemented with 3% bovine serum albumin and 0.1% Tween 20) for two hours. The membranes were probed overnight with primary antibodies (1:2,000 or 1:1,000) at 4°C. Blots were washed with TBS-T before and after addition of secondary antibody (1:5,000). Blots were imaged with chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an iBright (Invitrogen) imaging system. Image analysis was performed using iBright imaging software.

Flow cytometric analysis

Cells were washed with PBS and plated in a 96-well plate. Cells were stained at RT with a live/dead stain (1:1,000) for 15 min and then washed with PBS and fixed in 2% PFA at RT for 15 min. After fixation, cells were washed with FACS buffer and permeabilized with 90% methanol on ice for 30 min. Cells were rehydrated with FACS buffer for 30 min on ice or overnight at 4°C. Fixed and permeabilized cells were stained with Fc Block and fluorescently conjugated antibodies for 45 min at RT. Compensation beads (BioLegend) were stained according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Data were collected on BioRad Ze5 flow cytometer or Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software. All antibodies and concentrations are listed in Table 1.

Cell viability analysis

Transduced primary murine CD4+ T cells were cultured in the presence or absence of IL-2 for 3 days post-transduction. 100 μL of cells were removed every 24 h for staining with propidium iodide (BioLegend) and Hoechst (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and viability and total cell numbers were measured with a Celígo Imaging Cytometer. Cells were harvested on the third day post-transduction for analysis by flow cytometry. Transduced, primary human CD4+ T cells were grown out for five days post-transduction to obtain appropriate cell numbers. Primary human CD4+ T cells were washed twice and resuspended in complete RPMI (∼1,000,000 cells/mL). Cells from the same transduction were split in half and plated out into two separate wells. Each well was cultured in the presence or absence of IL-2. Viability was determined by Zombie Violet staining followed by flow cytometry.

Treg differentiation

CD4+ T cells were transduced and differentiated either in differentiation medium ± IL-2 (10 ng/mL). To further verify that the mechanism of aSTAT5 was independent from autocrine/paracrine IL-2, antibodies against IL-2 (10 μg/mL) and IL-2R (10 μg/mL) were supplemented into the culture medium. These conditions were consistent throughout differentiation. Cells were then permeabilized and stained for analysis by flow cytometry. To assess lineage maintenance, fully differentiated Tregs that expressed control or aSTAT5 were washed and split into either a Treg differentiation group where differentiation medium was added daily for 3 days, or a challenge group where cells were washed and cultured in media and that lacked all cytokines. Blocking antibodies against anti-IL-2/R were also added to some samples as described above. Cells were then harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Treg suppression

Naive CD4+ T cells were isolated from BoyJ (CD45.1+) mice via negative selection before retroviral transduction. Transduced cells were selected a day later by positive selection for Thy1.1. After 5 days of differentiation, CD4+ T cells (Tconv) from C57BL/6 (CD45.2+) mice were isolated and stained with CTV [Tag-It Violet (BioLegend)]. Tregs were loaded into a 96-well plate and serially diluted to obtain ratios of Treg:Tconv of 1:1 (50,000 each) through 1:16 in complete medium that was not supplemented with exogenous cytokines or anti-IL-2/R blocking antibodies. Anti-CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added to each sample at a ratio of 2:1 beads to cells. After three days of culture, all cells were fixed and stained for analysis by flow cytometry. Suppression was calculated as % suppression = [1 − (division index with Tregs/division index without Tregs)]×100.102

Irradiation

Animals were irradiated with X-rays at a dose of approximately 6 Gy with a 225-kV beam energy by using a Kimtron IC225 (Kimtron Medical) at the Experimental Irradiation Shared Resource of University of Michigan Rogel Comprehensive Cancer Center. Dosimetry was performed using an ionization chamber connected to an electrometer system that is directly traceable to a National Institute of Standards and Technology calibration.

Treg engraftment

CD4+ T cells were purified via negative selection from BoyJ (CD45.1+) mice, transduced, and differentiated into Tregs. The transduced cells were purified by positive selection for the Thy1.1 marker 24 h post-transduction. On the fourth day of differentiation, cells were analyzed to determine transduction efficiency and differentiation status. C57BL/6 mice were irradiated with 6 Gy on the fourth day of Treg differentiation. Tregs were prepped for adoptive transfer by washing twice with PBS and resuspending at 2.4–2.6 × 106 cells/100 μL PBS. Each treatment group received the same number of cells. The cells were adoptively transferred into irradiated mice via tail vein injection. 5 days post-transfer, mice were sacrificed. Spleens and lymph nodes were isolated and passed through a 40 μm strainer. Lymph node samples were directly fixed and permeabilized for analysis. Spleens were treated with ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) buffer and rinsed with FACS buffer before preparation for analysis by flow cytometry.

Rag1−/− mouse model

For the T cell-induced colitis model, 5 × 105 CD45.2+ CD4+ CD44- naive T cells were administered together with CD45.1+ Tregs that expressed control or aSTAT5 at cell ratios of 0:1, 0.5:1, and 1:1 of Treg:Tnv per mouse. Cells were injected i.v. or i.p. into randomized-by-weight RAG-1-deficient mice. Mice were monitored for weight change and sacrificed when a humane endpoint was reached, which occurred ∼5 weeks after T cell injection. The intestinal tissues were fixed in 10% formalin and embedded in paraffin for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of 6 μm paraffin tissue sections or processed for flow cytometry. Histological scoring was performed in a blinded manner based on the degrees of immune cell infiltration and tissue hyperplasia (0–5).

Quantification and statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using Prism GraphPad software (version 10.4.1). Unpaired t tests with Welch’s correction were performed on comparisons between two groups. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test unless otherwise noted was applied to comparisons between groups with one independent variable. Two-way ANOVA with Šídák’s or Tukey’s multiple comparisons was performed on data with two independent variables. The number of independent experiments performed is denoted by n unless otherwise noted. All error bars represent the mean ± standard deviation.
