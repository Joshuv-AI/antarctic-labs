Influence of trough currents on Permian reef–shoal belts and reef-capping dolomite reservoirs, Damaoping Block, Sichuan Basin, China
Authors: Changrong Wu, Guoqiang Xu, Shihu Wu, Siding Jin, Tairan Ye, Bin Ye, Ziyan Wang, Zheyuan Liao, Xuelian Zhang, Haoyuan Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Large Late Permian reef-shoal mounds and supergiant gas fields have been identified along the margin of the Kaijiang–Liangping trough on the Upper Yangtze Platform. However, the underlying mechanisms of their concentration along the trough margin remain unclear. Herein, the Damaoping Block at the eastern trough end is used as a case study to examine how trough currents influence the development of reef-shoal mounds and reef-cap reservoir distribution. Results show that enhanced seismic facies analysis can identify individual reef-shoal mounds in low-amplitude reef-shoal zones and map small-scale reef-cap dolomite reservoirs. Sponge reefs occur in neritic water below the normal wave base because of upwelling. Rear reef-shoal mounds align directionally and become less developed toward the platform interior, with their orientation following the trough axis—indicating upwelling direction. Nutrient- and oxygen-rich cold bottom currents and upwelling helped sustain the preferential development and directional distribution of reef-shoal complexes, and indirectly controlled dolomite reservoir distribution on reef tops. This study establishes a methodological framework for reconstructing carbonate sedimentary systems and characterizing small-scale reef-shoal facies reservoirs, and provides evidence for deep-water bottom currents and upwelling on the Permian Yangtze Platform, which may contribute to future discussion of Late Permian thermohaline circulation.

Introduction

Geologists identified Changxing Formation reefs in the eastern Sichuan Basin1–3 in the early 1980s. However, their full value became apparent only after the discovery of the Puguang Gas Field in 20024. Over the next decade, oil companies systematically explored the margin of the Kaijiang–Liangping Trough in the northeastern Sichuan Basin5–7, discovering the largest Late Permian reef-shoal group or carbonate buildup zones in the world7,8 (Fig. 1a). This process contributed to the development of several major gas fields. In addition, it introduced such as Puguang and Yuanba, and introduced the reef-shoal hydrocarbon accumulation theory for trough-margin settings9–13. This theory identified the Precambrian–Cambrian Mianyang–Changning extensional trough in the central Sichuan Basin and large Anyue gas field14–17. Specialized technologies were developed to address the unique characteristics of reef-shoal reservoirs, significantly improving exploration and development13,14,17,18. These achievements exhibit a major impact on China’s natural gas supply19.

However, two critical issues affect the formation of large reef-shoal belts and the development of gas reservoirs on reef-shoal tops. The reason for the formation of reef-shoal belts on a large scale along the Kaijiang–Liangping trough margin is unclear, while no such large belts were developed on other platform margins such as the Middle and Lower Yangtze Platform. This suggests that factors beyond wave-resistant conditions at the platform-edge might have played a crucial role in reef belt formation. Large reefs such as the Great Barrier Reef20 typically develop in regions where ocean currents provide a steady nutrient supply, suggesting that oxygen- and nutrient-rich cold bottom currents from northern high-latitude regions might have contributed to this reef belt. Earlier seismic facies analysis could identify high-amplitude reef-shoal mounds and their reef-cap dolomite reservoirs (Fig. 1b), but could not distinguish individual mounds or reservoirs within medium- and low-amplitude reef-shoal belts12 (Fig. 1c). This limitation hinders the efficient exploitation of small-scale reef-top gas reservoirs in low-amplitude reef terraces and the identification of cold water bottom current-upwelling signals during large reef-shoal mound formation.

The Damaoping block, located at the end of a trough with strong currents, is an ideal site for capturing trough current signals. Herein, enhanced seismic lithofacies analysis was used to identify individual reef-shoal mounds and their associated reef-cap dolomite reservoirs within low- to medium-amplitude reef-shoal belts. The link between sponge reefs and cold bottom current-upwelling systems was studied by observing sponge reef water depths. Reef-shoal extension patterns and development intensity in the post-barrier reef zone were used to infer the pathways and direction of trough currents. An integrated assessment of the trough-margin reef-shoal sedimentary system—including lithofacies distribution, depositional environments, and current dynamics—was used to elucidate the influence of trough currents on reef-shoal and reservoir evolution, offering insights into cold bottom currents within thermohaline circulation.

Geological setting

The Kaijiang–Liangping trough (Fig. 1a) lies in the northeast of the Sichuan Basin, between Leshan–Longnvsi and Ankang uplifts8. During the Changshingian Age, it formed an intraplate depression, extending southeast from the Paleo-Tethys Ocean21–23. It began developing during the Wujiaping Age (Fig. 1b) and reached its maximum development in the middle Changshingian Age. By the Early Triassic, it was filled and buried by carbonate and terrigenous clastic deposits. Based on the inclined layer height of the Feixianguan Formation—the sedimentary layer overlying the Changxing Formation—the estimated paleo-water depth at the trough center was ~ 500 m21,24. Reefs were formed along the basin margins, creating a reef mound belt of 2–16 km wide and ~ 720 km long (Fig. 1a), representing the largest known reef mound (carbonate buildup) group from the Changshingian Age. The sponge reefs are dominated by calcareous sponges, accompanied by hydroid worms, tubular shells, and locally bryozoans, forming a sponge-dominated reef framework25. Lithologically, the reef bodies are mainly composed of sponge boundstone and framestone, locally associated with bioclastic grainstone and shoal limestone. In terms of depositional architecture, the reef-shoal system includes barrier-reef, lagoon, rear-reef, patch-reef, and shoal-related facies belts, reflecting environmental differentiation across the trough-margin platform from below wave base to above wave base. This facies framework provides the geological basis for interpreting the spatial distribution of reef growth, shoal development, and reservoir formation. High-quality reservoirs occur in porous dolomites of evaporitic tidal-flat facies developed on reef tops12. Two key factors were responsible for the formation of these reservoirs26: (ⅰ) a high-energy shoal environment that produced granular limestone with abundant primary porosity and (ⅱ) an isolated evaporitic tidal flat setting, promoting freshwater leaching, dissolution, and mixed dolomitization while blocking terrigenous clastic input27.

Materials and methods

Data

This study is based on drilling and seismic data collected from Sinopec and CNPC between 2006 and 2020. The high-quality three-dimensional (3D) seismic data have a dominant frequency of up to 35 Hz within a depth range of 5000–8000 m, with a quarter-wavelength thickness of 38–46 m. This resolution provides clear images of the internal structure of high-amplitude reef-shoal bodies (~ 300 m thick in the Changxing Formation; Fig. 1b), while less clear images of medium- and low-amplitude reef-shoal bodies (< 200 m thick; Fig. 1c). The reef-shoal zone along the trough contains hundreds of available drilling data. In particular, the Southern Exploration Company of CNPC has drilled different seismic anomaly reflectors in the Yuanba area, including strong reflections at the trough center, progradational bodies on the slope, high- and low-amplitude reflections on the top of the reef-shoal body (including fore reef, reef crest, and back reef), and localized strong reflections in lagoons, beaches, and tidal flats. These drilling efforts have provided valuable data for analyzing seismic reflection characteristics of reef-shoal facies along the trough margin. All seismic profiles are displayed in the zero-phase American polarity format.

Methods

We used standardized sequence stratigraphy to define system tracts in the Changxing Formation and clarify the impact of sea-level changes on sponge reef development and reef-cap dolomite reservoir distribution. Enhanced seismic facies analysis identified individual reef bodies and their associated dolomite reservoirs, the lithofacies distribution, and the sedimentary environments of highstand and lowstand stratigraphic units. The presence of cold bottom and upwelling currents in the trough was assessed based on the nutrient and oxygen supply conditions during the Late Permian. Assuming that the orientation of rear reef-shoal bodies behind the barrier reef reflects nutrient transport pathways, the direction of upwelling currents was inferred from their alignment with the trough axis. The tidal current characteristics were interpreted from the trumpet-shaped geometry and orientation of high-energy facies in Early Triassic shallow-marine mudstone deposits. The reef-shoal sedimentary system was reconstructed by integrating the facies, depositional settings, and flow paths. The influence of cold bottom currents and upwelling systems on reef and shoal development and the formation of evaporative tidal flat dolomite reservoirs was analyzed to understand their control on large-scale reef-flat systems and reservoir distribution. Results

Sequence-stratigraphic framework

Traditional sequence stratigraphy divides the Changxing Formation into two sequences, representing its upper and lower members. Standardized studies show that the formation comprises five genetic stratigraphic units (Units 1–5; Fig. 1b). These units form the mentioned systems tracts from bottom to top: (i) first lowstand, (ii) transgressive, (iii) highstand, (iv) forced regressive, and (v) second lowstand. Units 1–3 comprise the lower member and correspond to the lower sequence in the traditional division6, while Units 4 and 5 correspond to the upper member and match the upper sequence.

The sea level change pattern during different periods significantly influences reef and shoal development. (i) Middle transgression to highstand periods (i.e., the first sea-level peak37 during the Changshingian Age): the barrier reef zone was located on the middle slope and developed on 10 m-scale sponge reefs (Well Y27, Fig. 1b; Well Y12-6, Fig. 3). Simultaneously, the upper slope was in a shallow-water environment above the normal wave base, dominated by shoal-type deposits. (ii) The middle stage of the second lowstand period (i.e., the second sea-level peak during the Changshingian Age): the platform behind the barrier reef was below the normal wave base, forming meter-scale sponge reefs, while the barrier reef zone remained in a shallow-water, shoal-dominated environment. (iii) The first lowstand, early stage of transgression, forced regression, and early/late stages of the second lowstand: few sponge reefs developed at the trough margin, where lagoon, beach, and evaporative tidal flat facies dominated instead.

The above phenomenon indicates that sponge reefs were formed in deep water (30–120 m) below the normal wave base during rapid sea-level rise and highstand phases, while shoals and evaporite tidal flats were developed in shallow water above the wave base during rapid sea-level fall and lowstand phases. When other conditions for sponge reef development are fulfilled, a decrease in the water depth above the wave base hinders reef growth and shifts deposition to shoal environments. Conversely, an increase in the depth below the wave base might re-establish conditions that are favorable for reef development. Reef-cap dolomite reservoirs were primarily formed during rapid sea-level fall and the subsequent lowstand phases of the third cycle.

Identification of individual reef-shoal mound and reef-cap reservoirs

Velocity analysis of different types of rock layers

Based on the enhanced seismic lithofacies classification criteria34, the study area can be divided into three main seismic lithofacies: dense carbonate, porous carbonate, and argillaceous rocks. The porosity and acoustic velocity data from the Yuanba block show that dense carbonate rocks exhibit a porosity range of 0–0.5%, with average acoustic velocities between 6500 and 6470 m s− 1 (Fig. 2a). Reef and shoal facies reservoirs exhibit porosities ranging from 0.5 to 16%, with acoustic velocities between 6400 and 5050 m s− 1. At 8% porosity, the velocity is ~ 5800 m s− 1. The reflection coefficient between this layer and dense limestone is 0.06, producing weak to moderate amplitude reflection.

Fig. 2Relation between velocity and porosity (a), mud content (b), and gas content (a) in the Kaijiang–Liangping trough margin area.

Relation between velocity and porosity (a), mud content (b), and gas content (a) in the Kaijiang–Liangping trough margin area.

The muddy rocks of the Changxing Formation along the trough margin can be broadly classified into four types: mud-bearing limestone, marl, calcareous mudstone, and shale. The mud content is negatively correlated with velocity (Fig. 2b). (i) Type 1 (5–25% mud content): sonic logging velocities range from 6430 m s− 1 to 5650 m s− 1, with a median of ~ 6000 m s− 1. (ii) Type 2 (25–50% mud content): velocities range from 5630 m s− 1 to 5000 m s− 1, with a median of ~ 5300 m s− 1. (iii) Type 3 (50–75% mud content): the median decreases to ~ 4800 m s− 1. (iv) Type 4 (75–100% mud content): the median decreases to ~ 4400 m s− 1. These median velocities reflect the characteristic velocities of the four mudstone types. The reflection coefficients between Types 1, 2, 3, and 4 mudstones and dense limestone are 0.04, 0.1, 0.15, and 0.2, producing weak, moderate, strong, and highly strong amplitude reflections, respectively (Fig. 3; weak amplitude at the top of the Changxing Formation in Well 12- 9 and highly strong amplitude in Well T-62).

Velocity correction for gas-bearing layers

Analyses of synthetic seismograms from borehole profiles reveal that gas-bearing rock layers have significantly lower acoustic logging velocity than seismic wave velocity, though the exact cause remains unclear34. This discrepancy hinders accurate phase and amplitude matching between synthetic seismograms developed from acoustic data and nearby seismic records, making it difficult to correlate macroscopic layers (strata with similar acoustic impedance) with seismic phases. This can be addressed by using the Gassmann or Wyllie time-averaging Eq.38 to convert water-layer velocity into gas-bearing layer velocity. However, owing to its complexity, this method is rarely used by seismic interpreters. Alternatively, Xu and Haq34 proposed a simplified formula: gas-bearing velocity ≈ water-layer velocity × (1 − porosity). For example, at 8% porosity, the gas-bearing velocity is 92% of the water-layer velocity, corrected from 5800 to 5336 m s− 1. Using this method, the velocity range for carbonate reservoirs with 0.5–16% porosity can be adjusted to 6360–4400 m s− 1. Based on the Domenico effect32,39 the seismic velocity reduces sharply when gas saturation is between 0 and 0.05. At 0.05 saturation, the velocity reduction in slightly gas-bearing layers exceeds that of fully gas-saturated layers, indicating that even small amounts of gas require careful velocity correction.

The results after correction are mentioned: (ⅰ) the corrected gas-bearing layer velocity curve closely matches the low logging velocity boundary (Fig. 2a), (ⅱ) Following correction, the synthetic record aligns with the well-adjacent seismic trace in the phase, with consistent amplitudes and waveforms (Fig. 4), supporting reliable correlation between macroscopic layers (e.g., porous dolomite) and seismic phases, and (ⅲ) Gas-bearing and slightly gas-bearing layers with porosities of 2%, 6%, 10%, and 16% have seismic velocities of ~ 6000, 5600, 5200, and 4400 m s− 1, respectively. Their reflection coefficients at the interface with dense limestone are 0.04, 0.07, 0.11, and 0.21, corresponding to weak, medium, strong, and highly strong reflections (Figs. 1b and 3, Well Y12-6), confirming that high-porosity, gas-bearing reef-cap reservoirs can generate bright reflections (Fig. 1b).

Wave impedance structure and seismic response of typical borehole profiles

The T-62 well represents the trough margin facies (Fig. 3). The Changxing Formation is a high-velocity layer comprising dense limestone platform facies (the blue line on the right of the well log indicates the wave impedance curve, Fig. 3). At the base of the Feixianguan Formation lies a ~ 30 m thick low-velocity layer comprising mudstone and shale, overlain by high-velocity tidal flat limestone. Consequently, a distinct low-velocity interbed is formed at the bottom of the Feixianguan Formation, producing a high amplitude and right down–dip skew–symmetrical waveform reflection (RD reflection). The top and base of the low-velocity layer correspond to negative and positive reflection interfaces, respectively. The top of the Changxing Formation (P/T interface) is correlated with the crest event in the RD waveform reflection.

Well Y12-6 is located at the top of the reef-shoal body (Fig. 4b) and represents a high-quality reef-cap dolomite reservoir well (Fig. 3). Two porous dolomite reservoirs are developed in the upper Changxing Formation, each generating RD waveform reflection. The reservoir top corresponds to the upper trough of the RD waveform, and the base corresponds to the crest. The P/T interface is correlated with the trough event of the upper RD waveform.

Well Y12-9 is located behind the barrier reef area (Fig. 4b) and represents a well with poor reef-cap reservoir quality. These types of wells are commonly found in pre-reef, back-reef, and inter-reef zones (Fig. 3). Similar to Well Y12-6, dolomite layers are present at the top of the 4th (forced regression system tract) and 5th (second lowstand system tract) units of the Changxing Formation, but they are thin and have low porosity. The dolomite bed at the top of the 5th unit is ~ 6-m thick with a porosity of < 3%, and its wave impedance (blue line on the right of the lithologic column) resembles that of dense limestone. Therefore, no dolomite low-velocity interbed exists at the reef top to produce RD waveform reflections. Moreover, a minor mud content at the bottom of the Feixianguan Formation makes the velocity of the Changxing Formation near the Permian-Triassic interface slightly higher than that of the Feixianguan Formation, which results in a weak positive reflection at the P/T interface. This interface corresponds to a weak wave crest event (Figs. 3 and 4b).

Minimum resolvable thickness of reef-cap reservoirs

Seismic response simulations of the reef-cap dolomite reservoir (thin layer) reveal that seismic observation with a dominant frequency of ~ 35 Hz and < 1/4 wavelength (37.5 m) in the reef-cap dolomite reservoir exhibits typical thin-layer seismic responses (Fig. 5). High-quality reservoirs with > 8% porosity and thicknesses between 1/4 λ and 1/32 λ (37.5–4.7 m) produce strong to moderate amplitude RD waveform reflections. The RD waveform reflections can be visually identified when the thickness is between 1/32 λ and 1/64 λ (4.7–2.3 m), suggesting that reef tops with overlying and underlying dense carbonate rock layers in reservoirs with 2.3–4.7 m thickness can be effectively identified through RD waveform characteristics. Therefore, weak-amplitude RD waveform reflections in reef-top areas (Figs. 4b and 6) indicate the presence of small-scale reef-cap dolomite reservoirs.

Identification of individual reef-shoal mounds and reef-cap reservoirs

In the Upper Yangtze Platform area, the bottom of the Feixianguan Formation generally contains clay and has lower acoustic impedance than the dense limestone in the upper Changxing Formation. Consequently, the P/T interface appears as a positive reflection with a symmetrical waveform. This is shown as a wave crest event in zero-phase American polarity seismic profiles. Traditionally, the P/T interface is interpreted along this crest event (Figs. 1c and 7). However, in the reef-shoal belt along the Kaijiang–Liangping trough margin, dolomite reservoirs related to evaporative tidal flat environments often develop on top of reef-shoal mounds—known as reef-cap dolomite reservoirs. The base limestone of the Feixianguan Formation contains a small amount of mud and behaves as a high-velocity layer, making the reef-cap a low-velocity interlayer. This shifts the P/T interface from positive to negative reflection, aligning with the wave trough phase axis instead of the wave crest. Therefore, moving from the trough margin through the reef-shoal to the lagoon, the interpretation of the P/T interface shifts from the wave crest to the wave trough and the back wave to the wave crest. This phase-jump method makes low-amplitude reef-shoal bodies appear half a trough width higher than the surrounding layers—equivalent to 10–38 m—effectively highlighting them (Fig. 7) and providing a theoretical basis and practical method to identify individual reef-shoal mounds in low-amplitude reef settings.

Seismic reflection characteristics of sedimentary facies units

Edge of the trough

Edge of the trough

Edge of the trough

At the sea trough edge, the Changxing Formation comprises dense limestone with minimal internal wave impedance variation. The bottom of the Feixianguan Formation was developed in a low-energy, negative topographic setting, primarily comprising mudstone and muddy carbonate rock. This forms a low-velocity interlayer, where the P/T interface is mapped along the low wave crest of the RD waveform (Figs. 3 and 4). Owing to the lateral stability of mudstone and limestone, adjacent seismic traces show minimal impedance variation. Consequently, the seismic reflections are smooth, continuous, and regularly shaped (Fig. 4).

(2)Platform

(2)Platform

Platform

The Changshingian platform is in a medium-energy environment, dominated by thick limestone layers with minimal internal wave impedance variation. Thus, the formation exhibits weak or nearly blank seismic reflections (Fig. 6). The base of the overlying Feixianguan Formation comprises subtidal mudstone limestone with lower wave impedance than the underlying Changxing limestone, forming a positive reflection interface with nearly symmetrical waveform characteristics. In zero-phase American polarity seismic profiles, the P/T interface aligns with the in-phase axis of the wave crest, showing weak to moderate amplitude reflections (Fig. 6a and c, left), all with good continuity.

(3)Reef-shoal mound

(3)Reef-shoal mound

Reef-shoal mound

High-resolution analysis of the P/T interface reveals distinct hill-like protrusions in medium- and low-amplitude reef-shoals (Figs. 4 and 6). Medium-amplitude reefs show internal weak-amplitude protrusions and short events, while low-amplitude reef-shoals exhibit nearly blank reflections.

Reef mounds can be divided into three types based on the reef body distribution: frontal barrier (Fig. 4), rear (Fig. 5a), and patch (Fig. 6b) reefs. Frontal reefs occur behind the trough and are marked by prominent protrusions and disordered internal reflections. Their tops exhibit RD waveform reflections with variable amplitudes (low to high) and misaligned phases, indicating minor lateral variations in time depth, amplitude, and waveform. These characteristics are in sharp contrast with the trough edge, which shows a high-amplitude RD waveform with smooth, continuous reflections. The thickness, morphology, and reflection properties change abruptly at the barrier reef–sea trough boundary (Fig. 4). Rear reefs exist behind the lagoon and exhibit noticeable but less abrupt variations in thickness, internal structure, and top reflection continuity (Figs. 6a and 7). Patch reefs appear in the platform area behind rear reefs, with small protrusions and scales. Their tops display distinct RD waveform reflections, a key identifying feature (Fig. 6b).

The front, back, edge, and inter-reef areas of the reef complex (Figs. 4, 6, and 7) are well connected to the open sea and have a relatively low topography. Consequently, these zones struggle to form brine, produce limited brine, or experience low hydrodynamic energy, resulting in weak dolomitization and poorly developed pore-type dolomite reservoirs. Moreover, owing to their high topographic setting, the low Feixianguan Formation contains less mud, exhibiting slightly lower wave impedance than the Changxing Formation limestone. Consequently, the P/T interface in these areas generally shows low-amplitude or no reflections (Fig. 4b). In contrast, the reef flat area shows RD wave patterns owing to low-velocity interbeds of porous dolomite.

(4)Bays and Lagoons

(4)Bays and Lagoons

Bays and Lagoons

Bays and lagoons occur in low-energy, negative topographic settings. A bay refers to a bay-shaped marine area (Fig. 10) between two barrier reefs that becomes a tidal channel during low sea levels. Its seismic reflection pattern matches that of the trench edge (Fig. 4a). Lagoons lie behind barrier reefs and display a distinct concave negative topography (Fig. 7), with a parallel configuration, medium to high amplitudes, medium to low frequencies, and RD waveform reflections. The P/T interface corresponds to the crest phase of the RD waveform, characterized by a smooth, continuous, and clear appearance.

(5)Shoal mound

(5)Shoal mound

Shoal mound

Shoals occur in base-uplift areas behind the trough margin and at the top of trough slopes (Fig. 11b). The seismic profiles show stacked small hills and lateral progradation reflection patterns (Fig. 6b). Larger shoals display flattened mound tops (Fig. 6c), sharply contrasting with the internal blank reflections in rear and patch reefs. These internal structural differences indicate that during transgression and highstand stages, the barrier reef belt is developed in a low-energy slope setting below the normal wave base, forming sponge reefs. In contrast, the slope crest lies above the wave base in a high-energy environment, and shoal facies limestone with primary porosity is formed here (Fig. 11b). The wave impedance contrast between shoal and platform facies limestone results in the mound-like reflection pattern within shoals.

(6)Supratidal mud flat

(6)Supratidal mud flat

Supratidal mud flat

The supratidal flat mudstone at the base of the Feixianguan Formation is formed in a positive topographic area atop the trough slope. The Changxing Formation, comprising platform facies carbonate rocks, lies below this layer, and tidal flat facies limestone of the same formation lies above it. This mudstone has a low-velocity interlayer with RD wave reflection characteristics. Compared with RD waves in other sedimentary units, its key features are as mentioned: (ⅰ) occurrence in highland areas behind the open platform, showing medium- to high-amplitude reflections (Fig. 6b and c), (ⅱ) the Changxing Formation exhibits stacked small-hill patterns in the frontal zone of the supratidal flat (Fig. 6b, right), with the P/T interface aligned to the low crest of the RD wave and horizon continuity intact but lacking smoothness, and (ⅲ) the low-lying areas within the supratidal flat exhibit that seismic reflections match those of the trough and lagoon zones, displaying high amplitude, smooth, and continuous reflections.

Distribution of skeleton lithofacies

Subdivided seismic lithofacies

Based on integrated seismic and geological analysis, five seismic lithofacies types are identified in the study area. (ⅰ) Negative topography, low-energy, low-velocity, and mudstone lithofacies: they are present at the base of the Feixianguan Formation and reflect the paleogeomorphology and sedimentary environments (e.g., sea trough margins, bays, and lagoons) of the Upper Permian Changxing Formation. (ⅱ) Medium-energy and high-velocity carbonate lithofacies: they are dominated by dense limestone and represent the flat sedimentary environment of the Changxing carbonate platform. (ⅲ) Positive topography, high-energy, low-velocity, and porous carbonate lithofacies: they comprise porous limestone and dolomite and reflect the environment of the Changxing reef-shoal complex. (ⅳ) Positive topography, high-energy, and high-velocity carbonate lithofacies: they comprise dense limestone and dolomite and represent the reef complex margins, including fore-, back-, and inter-reef areas. (ⅴ) Positive topography, low-energy, and low-velocity mudstone lithofacies: they comprise mudstone and marl and represents the low-velocity layer at the base of the Feixianguan Formation, reflecting a shallow water platform environment on the slope top of the trough. These five seismic lithofacies can be grouped into two framework lithofacies: (i) positive topography, high-energy, and low-velocity layers and (ii) positive or negative topography, low-energy, and -velocity layers. The lithofacies matrix is medium-energy and high-velocity limestone in a flat topography.

Distinguishing porous dolomite from mudstone layers

Low-velocity dolomite and mudstone are easily distinguishable in outcrops and well logs. However, both exhibit RD waveform reflections in seismic profiles, making them difficult to differentiate. Based on nine seismic lithofacies identification features, the mentioned approaches can be used to distinguish them effectively. (ⅰ) A low-velocity dolomite interlayer occurs at the top of upward-protruding reef-shoal mounds (positive landforms), while low-velocity mudstone is usually found in concave negative landform units such as troughs and lagoons (Figs. 1b and 4). (ⅱ) Argillaceous rocks in low-energy environments show highly consistent acoustic impedance between adjacent seismic traces, resulting in smooth events and neat appearances. In contrast, shoal facies in high-energy environments, although generally consistent, might exhibit slight acoustic impedance variations owing to lateral changes in the thickness and porosity of the dolomite reservoir. These variations cause minor waveform and amplitude changes, reducing reflection smoothness and neatness. (ⅲ) In complex settings such as argillaceous layers overlying reef-cap dolomite differentiation can be achieved via sedimentary system reconstruction using the spatial distribution patterns of sedimentary bodies. For example, the tidal flat facies RD waveform above the reef-shoal complex presented in Fig. 6b (right side) can be misinterpreted as reef-cap dolomite if its extensive lateral distribution is not considered.

Major sedimentary environments

The productivity of the carbonate factory is influenced by factors such as the depth of water, temperature, salinity, nutrients, turbidity, and pH40. Shallow areas are highly productive owing to strong light and high oxygen levels, resulting in thicker carbonate deposits. Deep areas are less productive owing to weak light and less oxygen, resulting in thinner deposits. Carbonate thickness is strongly negatively correlated with water depth. Using Changxing Formation thickness data, we reconstructed the paleogeography of the Damaoping block (Fig. 10). The results indicate two major environments: trough margin and reef-shoal facies. The reef-shoal facies is further divided into seven subenvironments: barrier reefs, bays, lagoons, rear reefs, platforms, patch reefs, and shoals. In a depth model using the barrier reef top as the zero benchmark, the obtained depth ranges are as mentioned: trough margin (120–80 m); barrier reef inner/outer zones [80–30 m (top at 0 m)], lagoon (100–40 m), rear reef inner/outer zones [40–20 m (top, 20–10 m)]; platform (70–30 m), patch reef surroundings [40–20 m (top, 20–10 m)], and shoal [40–30 m (top at 0 m)]. Additionally, individual reef-cap dolomite reservoirs in barrier, rear, and patch reefs cover approximately 1–4 km2, 0.5–2 km2, and 0.1–0.5 km2, respectively.

Flow path

Although studies on the impact of ocean currents on reef growth and high-energy beach formation remains limited41,]42, it should not be considered as evidence of insignificant influence. Analysis of the paleogeomorphology of the Damaoping block shows that sponge reefs in the barrier reef zone—classified as slope-zone accumulative reefs—were initially formed at depths of 80–30 m (Fig. 10). These reefs were developed below the normal wave base in the lower-upper neritic and upper-lower neritic environment, indicating deep-water growth characteristics43–45. Their development depends on oxygen and nutrients supplied by upwelling currents46. Notably, sponge reefs are densely concentrated along the Kaijiang–Liangping trough margin, in contrast to their sparse occurrence on the margins of the Yangtze Platform. This provides paleontological support for the cold-bottom-current/upwelling interpretation adopted here.

Scanning interpretation of the reef-cap reservoir and paleogeographic data shows clear directional trends in the distribution of barrier reefs and rear reefs (Fig. 10). Reefs show two distinct directional trends: the southwest-northeast extension aligns with isobaths at the eastern end of the Kaijiang–Liangping trough, showing that reef distribution correlates with water depths suitable for sponge growth. Meanwhile, the northwest–southeast extension follows the trough axis (Fig. 1a). This alignment may reflect upwelling flow paths. Wave direction, being variable, has minimal influence on reef orientation. Wave energy decreases inward from the platform margin, significantly affecting beach body porosity while having minimal effect on the reef complex size. Contrastingly, upwelling is interpreted to have favored reef growth and influenced reef distribution. Nutrient availability declines from the trough edge toward the platform interior, matching the observed reef size trends. This suggests that upwelling nutrients enable sponge reefs and beach bodies to grow beyond platform sedimentation rates. Their alternating development is controlled by habitat conditions: the reef belt below the wave base shows that sponges dominate and form reefs (Fig. 11b), while the reef belt above the wave base shows that organisms such as calcareous algae, brachiopods, and foraminifera form bioclastic and grain limestones. The preferential distribution and development intensity of reef mounds along the Kaijiang–Liangping trough margin may have been influenced by nutrient supply associated with upwelling, whereas their large-scale development was primarily controlled by the paleogeomorphic and sea-level framework. The directional extension of reef bodies likely records the pathway of upwelling-related currents.

Moreover, skeletal lithofacies distribution indicates a strong northwest–southeast water flow zone in central Damaoping. (ⅰ) Its origin lies in the northwest of Damaoping, ahead of the reversed extension of the barrier reef zone (Fig. 9A). Seismic data show platform-like features, with a thicker Changxing Formation than at the trough margin, and no RD waveform typical of trough-margin mudstones. This suggests an area with a positive topography (with no mudstone) and strong hydrodynamic conditions. The thick Changxing Formation and reef-shoal extension into deep water indicate high carbonate productivity, likely driven by an enhanced nutrient flux owing to fast flow—i.e., stronger upwelling. (ⅱ) Abnormal reef-shoal development in the central zone further supports this phenomenon. (ⅲ) In the southern area, the distribution of tidal flat mudstones at the base of the Feixianguan Formation reveals a trumpet-shaped zone narrowing toward the southeast, indicating strong tidal currents during the Induan. Thus, a major flow corridor exists from the trough margin mud-free zone to this tidal zone, where upwelling and tides are intensified. This flow enhancement is mainly controlled by the trough topography: as trough currents (cold bottom and tidal currents) move southeastward, the narrowing of the trough increases the velocity of the current. The study area lies at the southeastern end of the Kaijiang–Liangping trough, where the central flow zone was formed because the intensified trough currents entering along the sea trench axis.

Reef-shoal sedimentary system at the trough margin

Studies show that sea-level changes drove significant shifts in lithofacies distribution along trough/platform margins and hydrodynamic conditions, resulting in distinct sedimentary systems in each stage. During the highstand period of the Changshingian Age, a complete reef-shoal sedimentary system was developed along the trough margin (Fig. 11). The successive fancies from the trough margin to the platform included the trough margin, reef-shoal zone (barrier reef, lagoon, rear reef, platform, patch reef, and shoal), and open platform. Cold bottom currents transformed into upwelling flows upon contacting reef slopes, moving along the trough axis from front reef mounds to rear reef mounds and supplying nutrients that supported sponge reef growth and reef development. Wave action formed shoal limestones above the normal wave base. Shallow shoals were formed on reef-shoal flats during the lowstand of the fourth-order cycle. Furthermore, the decreasing wave intensity from the barrier reef to the patch reef reduced primary pore development in granular limestone of shoal facies.

During the second lowstand period, the third- and fourth-order cycles remain in a descending state. The significant drop in the sea level formed an isolated evaporative tidal flat environment at the top of the reef-shoal complex. Influence of trough currents on the reef and reef-cap reservoirs

Based on the above analysis, three primary water flows—upwelling, waves, and tidal currents—are identified in the reef-shoal sedimentary system along the trough/platform margin. Each water flow plays a key role in the formation of large reef-shoal systems and reef-cap dolomite reservoirs. Oxygen- and nutrient-rich upwelling likely enhanced the development potential of reef-shoal complexes (Fig. 11b), setting the stage for high-energy reef-top shoals and isolated evaporative tidal flats during lowstand periods. During transgression and highstand periods, wave action enhances primary pore development in granular limestone within intraplatform shoals. In contrast, during forced regression and lowstand periods, deep water in the trough generates strong waves owing to the vast sea area and deep water, while only weak waves are generated on the platform. Consequently, reef-cap dolomite reservoirs in barrier reefs are better developed than those in rear reefs. The study area was located at the end of the trough. Hence, wave and tidal currents intensify in the central zone, significantly developing better reef-cap reservoir than flanks. This might explain the notable difference in reservoir quality between Wells Y12-6 and Y12-8, which are in the reef-shoal belt and have similar reef heights (Figs. 10 and 13).

Discussion

Carbonate rocks exhibit biological factory characteristics, and limited research exists on the impact of water flow on their sedimentary systems48. This study used enhanced seismic lithofacies analysis to clarify the spatial positioning of reef-cap dolomite reservoirs and describe individual reef mounds and reef-cap dolomite reservoirs in low- to medium-amplitude reef-shoal belts. Using this approach, the trough current was identified, and a carbonate sedimentary system incorporating lithofacies, environment, and flow paths was established. The findings reveal the influence of trough currents on the development of reef mounds and reef-cap reservoirs. These results provide valuable insights for reef-shoal oil and gas exploration, paleoenvironment reconstruction, and understanding mass extinction events.

According to seismic stratigraphy, seismic reflection horizons are typically treated as isochronous interfaces49, making phase-crossing interpretations time transgressive. However, the low-velocity nature of gas-bearing dolomite layers at the reef mound top causes the P/T interface to shift from positive to negative reflection polarity. Consequently, the P/T interface should be reinterpreted from the crest phase (traditional P/T interface calibration) to the trough phase of the RD waveform reflection. The trough horizon at the reef mound top and the crest horizon at the lagoon top correspond to the same isochronous interface (Fig. 7). Using this crossphase method, the reef-top P/T interface is redefined from the lower crest horizon to the upper trough horizon, restoring true reef height and clarifying the position of the reef-cap dolomite reservoir in seismic profiles. This approach provides critical technical support for efficient oil and gas development in such reservoirs. After implementing enhanced seismic lithofacies analysis to accurately map reef-cap reservoirs, oil companies utilize horizontal drilling to connect multiple isolated reef-cap reservoirs (e.g., Well Y12-X16-33 in Damaoping block)50,51, enabling the development of previously noncommercial gas fields and significantly improving resource efficiency13,52.

The cold, nutrient- and oxygen-rich bottom current from northern high-latitude regions (North China and Tarim blocks53) contributed to the formation of a large-scale reef-shoal belt around the Kaijiang–Liangping trough. Sponge reefs developed below the wave base, while biologic shoals formed above it. These hydrodynamic conditions likely explain the dominant presence of shoal facies rather than reef mound facies in its western shallow trough (Jiangyou–Nanchong depression21). Moreover, the reason for the significantly small platform-edge reefs in the middle and lower Yangtze Region can also be explained.

The stagnation of ocean circulation and reduced deep-water ventilation have been proposed as possible mechanisms contributing to the end-Permian marine crisis54–57. More broadly, changes in upwelling, euxinia, and marine silica cycling indicate that oceanographic instability had already affected Permian marine systems before and around this interva58,59. However, direct geological evidence for the spatial behavior of such circulation remains limited. In this study, we identify the pathways and flow directions of cold bottom currents and associated upwelling along the Kaijiang–Liangping trough margin. These results provide a geological basis for future discussion of Late Permian ocean circulation and its possible environmental significance, but they should not be taken as direct proof of the extinction mechanism itself.
