Everywhere Valid Bounds on False Discovery Proportions in Conformal Inference
Ziang Song, Ying Jin, Emmanuel J. Candès

Abstract
Modern applications of conformal inference to multiple testing problems—such as outlier detection and candidate selection—often involve selecting test samples whose conformal p-values fall below a threshold. The quality of such methods is often measured by the false discovery proportion (FDP), defined as the fraction of incorrect selections. Existing approaches typically control the expected value of the FDP, using methods such as the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. This approach fails to provide high-probability bounds on the realized false discovery proportion and invalidates statistical guarantees if the rejection threshold is selected after inspecting the data. This paper establishes finite-sample, distribution-free upper bounds on the FDP that hold simultaneously over all possible rejection thresholds, enabling arbitrary post hoc selection of the threshold. Simultaneous validity is achieved by constructing a high-probability envelope for the empirical distribution function of ‘null’ conformal p-values by sampling from their joint distribution. Furthermore, our framework allows practitioners to modulate the envelope’s shape, thereby producing tight bounds in rejection regions of primary interest. We use this flexible approach to derive simultaneous FDP upper bounds for both outlier detection and conformal selection. We demonstrate through synthetic and real-data experiments that the resulting bounds are both valid and substantially less conservative than those derived from existing approaches.



1
Introduction

Conformal p-values have become a fundamental tool for distribution-free hypothesis testing
(
30
;
2
)
. Their appeal is that they can be constructed using arbitrary predictive models while offering finite-sample guarantees. These properties have enabled broad applications ranging from outlier detection
(
2
;
24
)
to scientific discovery
(
17
;
16
;
18
)
, and decision-making in which practitioners wish to select a subset of test samples obeying a user-specified criterion.

A common strategy in these applications is to select test samples whose p-values (or scores) fall below a threshold.
Such a selection rule can be viewed as a binary classifier that predicts whether each test sample is of interest. Thresholding decision rules naturally induce a family of binary decisions; as the threshold varies, the resulting decisions trace out curves of binary error metrics across the test samples, analogous to precision-recall curves in classification
(
5
;
28
)
.

The reliability of such selections is naturally quantified by the False Discovery Proportion (FDP)
(
3
)
, defined as the ratio of false discoveries to the total number of selections. In classification where we would like to single out samples with a label of interest, the FDP would be the fraction of predicted positives that are incorrect. This is simply one minus the precision.
Controlling the FDP/precision is often crucial because each selected unit typically triggers costly follow-up actions, such as experimental validation, manual inspection, or downstream scientific investigation. We illustrate this through two motivating examples.

Outlier Detection

In outlier detection
(
2
;
24
)
, one has access to a calibration set
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{calib}}=\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
where each
X_{i}
is independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) from an unknown distribution
\mathcal{P}_{0}
. Given test data
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}=\{X_{n+j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, the goal is to detect which points are likely to be outliers, i.e., drawn from a distribution different from
\mathcal{P}_{0}
. This task arises in fraud detection, anomaly detection in manufacturing, and the detection of unusual patterns in scientific data. The conformal inference framework provides a principled approach by calculating p-values
\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
that quantify how well each test sample conforms to the calibration data; smaller values indicate stronger evidence of being an outlier. Common procedures
(
2
;
24
)
use the thresholding rule
\mathcal{R}(t)=\{j:p_{j}\leq t\}
in which
t\in[0,1]

is a data-driven threshold computed via the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure
(
3
)
. Let
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\{j:X_{n+j}\sim\mathcal{P}_{0}\}
denote the index set of inliers so that a false discovery means a selection from
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. The FDP is then

\displaystyle\textnormal{FDP}(t)=\frac{|\mathcal{R}(t)\cap\mathcal{H}_{0}|}{\max\{1,|\mathcal{R}(t)|\}}.

(1)

The numerator above
|\mathcal{R}(t)\cap\mathcal{H}_{0}|
is the number of inliers incorrectly selected as outliers.
Maintaining a low FDP is often desired since the identified outliers often call for follow-up investigations, and a low FDP indicates that these costly investigations are rarely wasted
(
3
;
9
)
.

Drug Discovery

Conformal p-values also arise in drug discovery pipelines
(
17
;
16
)
, where early-stage screening aims to identify promising drug candidates from a large library. Machine learning predictions are useful in an initial screening stage to shortlist candidates; in later, more costly stages, only those shortlisted are carefully investigated to confirm promising cases. In this problem, the calibration set is
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{calib}}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
where
X_{i}
is the biochemical feature of the
i
th drug, and
Y_{i}
is the experimentally evaluated property, such as the binding affinity to a disease target. For new test drugs
\{X_{n+j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
with unknown properties
\{Y_{n+j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, the goal is to find those
Y_{n+j}
’s that exceed a pre-specified threshold
c_{n+j}
.

The conformal selection framework
17
constructs conformal p-values to make such selections. Here, a smaller p-value
p_{j}
provides strong evidence for a large outcome. The selection set is given by
\mathcal{R}(t)=\{j:p_{j}\leq t\}
, where
t\in\mathbb{R}
is determined by the BH procedure at a desired nominal level
(
3
)
. A false discovery is here a selected drug whose response
Y_{n+j}
is no larger than
c_{n+j}
, and thus the FDP is as in (
1
) with
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\{j:Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}
. Controlling the FDP ensures a high precision or ‘hit rate’, which is critical to prioritizing experimental validation efforts.

Limitations of existing methods

Existing methods mainly focus on controlling the false discovery rate (FDR), which is the expected value of the FDP averaged over the randomness in the data. For a pre-specified error rate
\alpha\in(0,1)
, these methods determine a data-dependent threshold
t(\alpha)
such that
\mathbb{E}[\textnormal{FDP}(t(\alpha))]\leq\alpha
. These approaches suffer from two major limitations:

•

What’s the realized FDP?
The realized FDP on a particular dataset may substantially exceed its expected value, even when the FDR is controlled
(
20
)
. FDR control only guarantees that the FDP is small
on average
over repeated realizations of the data, and thus provides no guarantee for the dataset at hand. However, practitioners often desire guarantees for the
data at hand
. They might want to know that with 90% or 95% chance, the FDP is below 20%. It may be of little comfort to know that over independent realizations of the data, the FDR may be at most 10% since this does not say anything about
their
study.

•

Fixed-
\alpha
guarantees only.
These methods require the nominal level
\alpha
to be fixed in advance, which limits their flexibility. A practitioner may wish to choose
\alpha
adaptively after inspecting the data. For example, if
\alpha=0.05
yields only one discovery, they may want to increase it to
0.1
to include more discoveries. Such post hoc adjustments, however, invalidate FDR control, see Figure
7
in the Appendix for a concrete illustration.

In this work, we address these limitations by proposing a framework to construct simultaneously valid, high-probability upper bounds on the FDP that remain valid for all thresholds
t\in[0,1]
. This enables flexible, data-driven exploration while maintaining rigorous statistical guarantees. Given a confidence level
\delta\in(0,1)
, we provide a data-driven function
\widehat{\text{FDP}}(t)
obeying

\mathbb{P}\Big(\textnormal{FDP}(t)\leq\widehat{\textnormal{FDP}}(t),~\forall t\in[0,1]\Big)\geq 1-\delta.

That is, with probability 90%, say, the observed curve
\widehat{\textnormal{FDP}}(t)
everywhere dominates the unknown FDP curve
\textnormal{FDP}(t)
.

Such simultaneous upper bounds have been extensively studied in the multiple testing literature, e.g.
13
;
11
;
12
;
19
;
4
;
23
. These approaches typically rely on closed testing procedures or structural assumptions such as independence or permutation validity. By contrast, we leverage the tractable joint distribution of null conformal p-values induced by exchangeability to construct finite-sample simultaneous FDP bounds.

Figure

previews our results on a real drug-target interaction task, with the full experimental setup deferred to Section
5.3
. Briefly, the goal is to select drug-target pairs whose binding affinity exceeds a given threshold. The figure demonstrates that our upper bounds consistently lie above the true FDP across all selection sets, ensuring valid error control regardless of the number of selections. This not only offers practitioners reliable, instance-wise control of FDP but also allows them to adaptively vary the selection threshold while maintaining rigorous guarantees.

Our main contributions are threefold:

•

We establish a novel high-probability upper bound on the empirical cumulative distribution function (CDF) of conformal uniform variables; see Section
3
. Empirically, our bound is tighter than existing bounds based on DKW-type concentration inequalities
10
. As a direct consequence, we obtain simultaneous upper bounds on the false coverage proportion in conformal prediction that are substantially less conservative than existing approaches; see Section
3.4
.

•

For outlier detection, we develop an upper bound for the FDP of selection sets holding at all rejection thresholds simultaneously; see Section
4
. Our approach combines our improved bound on the empirical CDF with two different tightening techniques from
(
15
)
and
(
10
)
, respectively. Through extensive simulation studies, we demonstrate the practical effectiveness of our quantitative results.

•

For conformal selection, we establish the first finite-sample FDP bounds that hold uniformly over all rejection thresholds; see Section
5
. We validate our theoretical results on the DAVIS dataset
(
6
)
, and show that our framework allows flexible exploration while providing rigorous error control guarantees.

Underlying all this is a general framework for deriving high-probability upper bounds on random functions via sampling from the joint distribution of null conformal p-values. Compared with approaches based on concentration inequalities, this approach yields substantially sharper bounds while preserving finite-sample validity.

2
Basic properties of conformal uniform variables

2.1
Conformal uniform variables

Following the split conformal prediction framework
(
30
)
, we assume access to a fully observed calibration set
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{calib}}=\{Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{n}\}
and a set of partially observed test units
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}=\{Z_{n+1},Z_{n+2},\ldots,Z_{n+m}\}
. Each
Z_{i}\in\mathcal{Z}
may represent either a feature-response pair or a feature vector alone depending on the specific context. The conformity score, denoted as
s(\cdot)
, is a function that assigns a real number to each data point that measures its “conformity” with the rest of the dataset. We assume this score function is obtained by a training process independent of both the calibration and test data. For example, in outlier detection,
s(Z_{i})
may be a one-class SVM classifier
(
29
)
assigning a high score to
Z_{i}
if
Z_{i}
is not likely to be an outlier and low score otherwise.
To simplify notation and discussion, throughout, we assume no ties among scores:

Assumption 2.1

(No Tie)
.

\mathbb{P}(s(Z_{i})=s(Z_{j}))=0
for all
i\neq j
.

We rely on the standard exchangeability assumption to characterize the null distribution:

Assumption 2.2

(Exchangeability)
.

The combined set of calibration and test data points,
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n+m}
, is exchangeable. Formally, for any permutation
\sigma
of the indices
\{1,\dots,n+m\}
, the joint distribution of the permuted sequence
(Z_{\sigma(1)},\dots,Z_{\sigma(n+m)})
is identical to that of the original sequence
(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n+m})
.

Under exchangeability, the test scores are exchangeable with the calibration scores. This allows us to define the conformal uniform variables, which are effectively the normalized ranks of the test samples:

Definition 2.3

(Conformal uniform variables)
.

For a test sample
Z_{n+j}
, the conformal uniform variable is defined as:

u_{j}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\left\{s(Z_{i})<s(Z_{n+j})\right\}+U_{j}}{n+1},

(2)

where
U_{1},\ldots,U_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\text{Unif}[0,1]
are independent of the data.

Under Assumption
2.1
and
2.2
, each
u_{j}
is marginally uniformly distributed over
[0,1]
and this is the same quantity as the null conformal p-value from
10
. More importantly, we shall see that the vector
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},...,u_{m})
admits a tractable joint distribution that does not depend on the underlying data-generating mechanism (Proposition
2.4
). We denote this joint distribution by
\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
. This object plays a central role below as it shall serve as the reference distribution for constructing uniform upper bounds on empirical distribution functions.

2.2
Joint distribution of conformal uniform variables

We revisit a key property of the conformal uniform variables (
2
), assuming no ties among the scores.
Proposition
2.4
below is a well-known result
(
10
)
, whose proof is omitted for brevity.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\{T_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n+m}
be independent and identically distributed as

\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
, and let
\{U_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
be independent and
identically distributed as
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
, independent of

\{T_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n+m}
. Set

q_{j}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\{T_{i}<T_{n+j}\}+U_{j}}{n+1},\quad j=1,\ldots,m.

(3)

Then the distribution of
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{m})
is identical to the distribution
\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
of
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})
(
2
).

This result follows from the exchangeability of the scores: the joint distribution of the conformal uniform variables depends only on the relative ranks of the scores and therefore coincides with that obtained when the scores are replaced by independent
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
random variables.

Although it is straightforward, Proposition
2.4
enables convenient sampling from the joint distribution of the conformal uniform variables, regardless of the underlying data distribution and score function.

3
Main results

We introduce a unified strategy for constructing simultaneous upper bounds on the empirical CDF of conformal uniform variables under our exchangeability assumption.

Recall the definition of the conformal uniform variables
\{u_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
in (
2
). The empirical CDF is defined as

\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{u_{j}\leq t\},\quad t\in[0,1].

(4)

The e.c.d.f. satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)]=t
, while its variance has the form
\operatorname{Var}\{\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)\}=c_{n,m}(t)t(1-t)
; see Proposition
C.1
.
Given a confidence level
\delta\in(0,1)
, the goal of this section is to find a (possibly random) function
G\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
obeying

\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)\leq G(t),~~\forall t\in[0,1]\right)\geq 1-\delta.

(5)

We call such a
G(t)
an envelope function.

The problem (
5
) has been investigated in
10
, who applied the Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz (DKW) inequality
(
8
;
25
)
to obtain a deterministic linear function
G(t)
of the form

G_{\mathrm{DKW}}(t)=t+\lambda_{\mathrm{DKW}},

(6)

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{DKW}}\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant depending on
(n,m,\delta)
. We will use their method as a baseline for comparison in our experiments.
An alternative analytic form of
G(t)
is established in

2
when the data
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n+m}
are i.i.d. (stronger than the exchangeability condition here) and
n\to\infty
. In contrast, our idea is to sample from the distribution of conformal uniform variables, which yields tight and finite-sample valid bounds in various applications.

3.1
Uniform upper bounds for increasing random functions

We begin with any one-dimensional summary statistic
T(F)
such that for any function
F\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
and any cutoff
g\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists an envelope function
\mathcal{G}(\cdot;g)\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
obeying

\displaystyle T(F)\leq g\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad F(t)\leq\mathcal{G}(t;g),~\forall t\in[0,1].

(7)

The task then reduces to finding a high-probability upper bound
\widehat{T}\in\mathbb{R}
for
T(\widehat{F}_{n,m})
, since

\mathbb{P}\big(T(\widehat{F}_{n,m})\leq\widehat{T}\big)\geq 1-\delta\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathbb{P}\big(\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)\leq\mathcal{G}(t;\widehat{T})\big)\geq 1-\delta.

We construct such a cutoff
\widehat{T}\in\mathbb{R}
by using the
(1-\delta)
-th quantile of multiple independent samples from the joint distribution of the conformal uniform variables.

For a probability distribution
P
on
\mathbb{R}
and
\beta\in(0,1)
, the quantile function is defined as

\operatorname{Quantile}(\beta;P):=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:P((-\infty,x])\geq\beta\}.

The procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Sampling for Envelope Function Estimation

0:

Distribution
\mathcal{D}
of a random function
F(t),t\in[0,1]
; summary statistic
T(\cdot):C([0,1])\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, envelope function mapping
\mathcal{G}(\cdot;\cdot)
; confidence parameter
\delta
; number
B
of Monte Carlo samples.

1:

Sample
B
i.i.d. replications
\{F^{(b)}\}_{b=1}^{B}
of
F
from the distribution
\mathcal{D}
.

2:

Compute
T^{(b)}=T(F^{(b)})
for
b=1,\dots,B
.

3:

Compute
\widehat{T}=\textrm{Quantile}(1-\delta;\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{1}{B+1}\delta_{T^{(b)}}+\frac{1}{B+1}\delta_{\infty})
.

3:

Envelope function
G(\cdot)
where
G(t)=\mathcal{G}(t;\widehat{T})
for
t\in[0,1]
.

Naturally, the shape of our bound relies on the summary statistic
T
.
Motivated by classical empirical process theory, throughout this paper, we mainly consider summary statistics of the general form

T(F)=\sup_{t\in[\ell,r]}\frac{F(t)-\mu(t)}{\sigma(t)},

(8)

where the mean function
\mu(t)
is typically chosen as the expectation of
F(t)
(known in the case of
F=\widehat{F}_{n,m}
), and
\sigma(t)
is a template function that controls the shape of the bound. Here, we use two additional parameters
\ell,r\in[0,1]
to add flexibility: while it is a natural idea to take the supremum over the entire range
t\in[0,1]
, the fluctuation of
(F(t)-\mu(t))/\sigma(t)
may be large around
t=0,1
, which leads to conservative bounds on
T(F)
.
When
[\ell,r]\neq[0,1]
, the region outside
[\ell,r]
can be extrapolated using the boundedness and monotonicity of the empirical c.d.f.
\widehat{F}_{n,m}(\cdot)
.

The following theorem formalizes the finite-sample validity of Algorithm
1
for a general choice of the summary statistic, and establishes the correspondence in (
7
), hence the form of
G(t)
, based on (
8
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
F(t),t\in[0,1]
be a random function drawn from an arbitrary distribution
\mathcal{D}
. Let
\widehat{T}
be the
(1-\delta)
-quantile of the statistics
T(F)
estimated via
B\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
Monte Carlo samples as in Algorithm
1
. Then

1-\delta\leq\mathbb{P}(T(F)\leq\widehat{T})\leq 1-\delta+\frac{1}{B+1},

(9)

where the randomness is over both
F
and the sampling process.
Furthermore, if
F(t)
is increasing in
t
and bounded above by a constant
M>0
almost surely, then using the summary statistic defined in (
8
), Algorithm
1
produces the piecewise function

\mathcal{G}(t,\widehat{T})=\begin{cases}\min\{\mu(\ell)+\widehat{T}\sigma(\ell),M\},&\mbox{ if }t<\ell,\\
\min\{\mu(t)+\widehat{T}\sigma(t),M\},&\mbox{ if }\ell\leq t\leq r,\\
M,&\mbox{ if }t>r,\end{cases}

which is a valid simultaneous upper bound:

1-\delta\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(F(t)\leq\mathcal{G}(t;\widehat{T}),~\forall t\in[0,1]\Big)\leq 1-\delta+\frac{1}{B+1}.

Flipping the sign of the summary statistic leads to simultaneous lower bounds, which enables two-sided simultaneous bounds on the e.c.d.f. when needed.
We make several remarks on Algorithm
1
.

Sharpness

As
B\to\infty
, the probability
\mathbb{P}(T(F)\leq G(t,\widehat{T}),~\forall t\in[0,1])
converges to
1-\delta
. This indicates that our upper bound is asymptotically sharp. In other words, when
B\to\infty
, it is impossible to find another envelope function that is uniformly smaller than
G(\cdot,\widehat{T})
while still maintaining the same level of confidence.

Beyond conformal uniform variables

While we primarily focus on conformal uniform variables in this work, Algorithm
1
can be applied to other multiple testing problems as long as we can sample from the joint distribution of the p-values. We include in Appendix
C.1
a discussion on extensions to i.i.d. p-values.

Point-wise bound

Our strategy can be easily modified to obtain other types of bounds. For example, if one is interested in an upper bound on
F(t_{0})
at a single point
t_{0}
, we can set
T(F)=(F(t_{0})-\mathbb{E}[F(t_{0})])/\sigma(t_{0})
and still follow Algorithm
1
to obtain the cutoff
\widehat{T}
. Inverting
T(F)\leq\widehat{T}
then yields the point-wise upper bound
\mathbb{P}(F(t_{0})\leq\sigma(t_{0})\cdot\widehat{T}+\mathbb{E}[F(t_{0})])\geq 1-\delta
.

3.2
Summary statistics

The general form (
8
) encompasses many well-studied statistics in the literature. Below, we list some representative ones which will be used in our experiments, followed by a numerical comparison.

One-sided Kolmogorov-Smirnov Statistic

This is the classical statistic defined as

T_{\textrm{KS}}(\widehat{F}_{n,m})=\sqrt{m}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}(\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)-t).

When used in Algorithm
1
, this statistic yields a linear upper bound function, making it computationally efficient but potentially conservative since it is not adapted to the variance of
\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)
.

(Truncated) Higher-Criticism Statistics

The Higher-Criticism framework
7
suggests the following statistic:

T_{\textrm{HC},\ell,r,\beta}(\widehat{F}_{n,m})=\sup_{t\in[\ell,r]}\frac{\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)-t}{(t(1-t))^{\beta}}.

(10)

When
\beta=1/2
, the statistic in (
10
) reduces to
the Higher-Criticism statistic
(
7
)
.
This choice is also motivated by the variance calculation in
Appendix
C.2
. Proposition
C.1

shows that

\operatorname{Var}\{\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)\}=c_{n,m}(t)t(1-t),

where
c_{n,m}(t)
accounts for the dependence induced by the shared calibration sample. Hence
t(1-t)
captures an important component of the
heteroskedasticity of the empirical CDF of conformal uniform variables.
The denominator
(t(1-t))^{\beta}
can therefore be viewed as a
variance-adaptive normalization, with
\beta=1/2
corresponding to
standardization up to the factor
c_{n,m}(t)
. When
n
is moderately large, the remaining factor

c_{n,m}(t)
is comparatively flat over most of the interval; see
Appendix
C.2
.

As we already briefly mentioned, the truncation parameters
\ell
and
r
are crucial. When
\beta=1/2
,
the
supremum over
t\in[0,1]
is typically attained at very small values of

t(1-t)
, that is, near the boundaries of the interval
(
7
;
22
)
, which may lead to overly conservative bounds for mid-range values of
t
. By restricting the range to
[\ell,r]
, we obtain a smaller cutoff
\widehat{T}
and better balanced and practically useful bounds.

The denominator
(t(1-t))^{\beta}
controls the shape of the envelope function.
Additionally, the parameter
\beta
determines the extent to which the upper bound is sensitive to the variance of
\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)
. A smaller
\beta
results in a bound which exhibits a linear behavior. We include an empirical investigation of the choice of
\beta
in Appendix
B
.

Berk-Jones Statistics

This statistic is based on a likelihood deviance:

T_{\textrm{BJ}}=m\cdot\max_{1\leq i\leq m/2}D(p_{(i)},i/m),

where
D(p_{0},p_{1})=p_{0}\log(p_{0}/p_{1})+(1-p_{0})\log((1-p_{0})/(1-p_{1}))
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between Bernoulli distributions with parameters
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
. To understand this, consider the order statistic of the p-values
p_{(1)}\leq p_{(2)}\leq\ldots\leq p_{(m)}
. If we think of the p-values as being i.i.d. uniform p-values (which is not exactly the case here), we would have
p_{(i)}\approx i/m
, so that
D(p_{(i)},i/m)
captures the expected deviation. In our experiments, we find that this statistic leads to bounds that are particularly sensitive to deviations in the middle range of p-values.

The MC-THC (purple) and MC-HC bound (red) adapt to the heteroskedasticity
\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)
(pinching near
t=0
), whereas the Baseline (
6

.

Figure

compares our method (based on various summary statistics) with the approach in

(
Baseline

(n,m)=(100,100)

\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)

The gray curves show that the fluctuation of
\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)

By contrast, our method with the Higher-Criticism statistic with
(\ell,r,\beta)=(0,1,1/2)

The Berk-Jones statistic (

3.3
Computational efficiency

Computing the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and truncated Higher-Criticism statistics is particularly efficient because of monotonicity: Fact

Fact 3.2
.

For any

and

This fact allows us to characterize the computation efficiency of these summary statistics and ensures that our bounds remain scalable even when

Proposition 3.3

(Computational cost of summary statistics)
.

1.

Truncated Higher-Criticism (THC).

Computing the truncated higher-criticism statistic (

(\ell,r,\beta)

reduces to checking the
m
jump points of
\widehat{F}_{n,m}
within
[\ell,r]
. Hence the statistic can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(m)
time.

2.

Evaluating the envelope at a fixed
t
.

Given the cutoff
\widehat{T}
from Algorithm

G(t)=\min\{\mu(t)+\widehat{T}\cdot\sigma(t),\,1\},\quad t\in[\ell,r],

can be evaluated at any fixed value of

in constant time
\mathcal{O}(1)
. For

also takes
\mathcal{O}(1)
time.

3.4
Simultaneous FCP upper bounds

An immediate application of our upper bound on
\widehat{F}_{n,m}
is bounding the false coverage proportion (FCP) for conformal prediction sets, following ideas similar as in
(
10
)
.

In this context, each data point is
Z_{i}=(X_{i},Y_{i})
for
i\in[n+m]
. For a given test instance with only
X_{n+j}
observed and a confidence level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we construct a prediction set
\mathcal{C}(X_{n+j};\alpha)=\left\{y:u_{j}(X_{n+j},y)>\alpha\right\}
, where
u_{j}(X_{n+j},y)
is obtained by replacing
Z_{n+j}
with
(X_{n+j},y)
in (

With exchangeable data, the conformal prediction set is guaranteed to cover the unknown outcome with
\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+j}\in\mathcal{C}(X_{n+j};\alpha))
for each

; see

The FCP is the proportion of prediction sets that fail to cover the true labels among all

\textnormal{FCP}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{Y_{n+j}\not\in\mathcal{C}(X_{n+j};\alpha)\}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{u_{j}\leq\alpha\}=\widehat{F}_{n,m}(\alpha).

(11)

Thus, the FCP at a confidence level

.
Therefore, Figure

4
Simultaneous FDP bounds in outlier detection

In this section, we establish simultaneous FDP upper bounds for the outlier detection problem introduced in
(
2
)
.
Recall that we observe a calibration dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{calib}}=\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i\in[n]}
where the data points are i.i.d. samples from an unknown distribution
\mathcal{P}_{0}
, and a test dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}=\left\{X_{n+j}\right\}_{j\in[m]}
where each test sample is independently sampled from an unknown distribution. (Outliers may be sampled from unequal and arbitrary distributions.)
The goal of outlier detection is to identify test samples whose distributions deviate from
\mathcal{P}_{0}
.

4.1
Setup

The outlier detection problem can be formulated as a multiple testing problem.
For each observation
X_{n+j}
, we test the null hypothesis
H_{n+j}:X_{n+j}\sim\mathcal{P}_{0}
. In other words, under the null hypothesis, the test sample follows the same distribution as the calibration data.
In many standard solutions, a one-class classifier is trained on a separated dataset to obtain a score function
s\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}

(
27
)
, where higher values of
s(X)
indicate stronger evidence that
X
follows the same distribution as the calibration data. As before, we assume
s(X_{1})
is a continuous random variable.

2
shows that conformal p-values wrapped around any score function can be leveraged to test for outliers with FDR control at a specified level.
The conformal p-value takes exactly the form of (
2
):

\displaystyle p_{j}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\{s(X_{i})<s(X_{n+j})\}+U_{j}}{n+1},\quad j\in[m].

A small value of
p_{j}
means that
s(X_{n+j})
is comparably small and thus suggests that
X_{n+j}
is likely to be an outlier.
The joint distribution of these p-values can be characterized under mild assumptions.

Assumption 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{0}\subseteq[m]
denote the index set of true inliers, where
j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
if and only if
X_{n+j}
follows the same distribution
\mathcal{P}_{0}
as the calibration data. We assume that the scores of the calibration samples and inlier test samples
\{s(X_{i})\}_{i\in[n]\cup\{n+j:j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}\}}
are exchangeable random variables.

Beyond pre-trained scores, Assumption
4.1
permits the use of so-called
adaptive
score functions learned from data
10
;
24
. Such adaptive scores are no longer i.i.d., yet they are still exchangeable and obey this assumption.
To avoid unnecessary complication, we again assume the scores have no ties. Proposition
4.2
provides a unified description of the joint distribution of the inlier conformal p-values.

Proposition 4.2
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
and
4.1
, letting
m_{0}=|\mathcal{H}_{0}|
denote the number of true inliers in test samples, the conformal p-values
\{p_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}
follows the joint distribution
\mathcal{P}_{n,m_{0}}
given in Proposition
2.4
.

For a rejection threshold
t\in[0,1]
, the FDP is given by

\displaystyle\mbox{FDP}(t)=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\ind\{j\in\mathcal{R}(t)\}}{\max\{1,|\mathcal{R}(t)|\}}=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\ind\{p_{j}\leq t\}}{\max\{1,\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}\leq t\}\}}.

(12)

Since we observe the denominator in (
12
), the key to establishing a simultaneously valid upper bound for
\mbox{FDP}(t)
is to bound the unobserved number of false discoveries
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\ind\{p_{j}\leq t\}
.

4.2
Bounding the number of false selections

The following lemma connects the distribution of
\{p_{j}\}_{j\in[m]}
to the distribution
\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
. Without loss of generality, we assume
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\{1,\dots,m_{0}\}
.

Lemma 4.3

(Coupling with completed conformal random variables)
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
and
4.1
, there exists a collection of random variables

(p_{1}^{\ast},\dots,p_{m}^{\ast})

such that:

1.

Null preservation
:

(p_{1}^{\ast},\dots,p_{m_{0}}^{\ast})=(p_{1},\dots,p_{m_{0}})
almost surely.

2.

Joint distribution
: The full vector
(p_{1}^{\ast},\dots,p_{m}^{\ast})
has joint distribution

\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
as defined in Proposition
2.4
.

Lemma
4.3
states that we can always couple the null p-values with certain
p_{j}^{*}
’s for those
j\notin\mathcal{H}_{0}
, such that the entire sequence of p-values follows the known, tractable distribution
\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
. The key to establishing simultaneous upper bounds for the FDP is that a valid upper bound for the new p-value sequence leads to a valid upper bound for the original p-value sequence with unknown non-null p-value distributions. Namely,

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\ind\{p_{j}\leq t\}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\ind\left\{p_{j}^{*}\leq t\right\}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\left\{p_{j}^{*}\leq t\right\}.

(13)

The next theorem shows that any simultaneous upper bound for
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\left\{p_{j}^{*}\leq t\right\}

immediately yields a simultaneous FDP bound for the observed p-values.

Theorem 4.4

(Simultaneous FDP bound via completed sequences)
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
and
4.1
, let
(p_{1}^{\ast},\dots,p_{m}^{\ast})
be constructed as in
Lemma
4.3
.
Suppose
G:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is a function independent of
\{p_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{m}
and
\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
which satisfies

\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}\{p_{j}^{\ast}\leq t\}\leq G(t),\ \forall t\in[0,1]\Bigg)\geq 1-\delta.

(14)

Then if we work with the rejection sets
R(t)=\{j:p_{j}\leq t\}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\mathrm{FDP}(t)\leq\frac{m\,G(t)}{1\vee\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}\{p_{j}\leq t\}},\ \forall t\in[0,1]\Bigg)\geq 1-\delta.

The envelope function
G(t)
in Theorem
4.4
can be constructed using
the procedure from Section
3
, by sampling from the known joint distribution

\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
.
Importantly,
G(t)
depends only on
(n,m,\delta)
and on the choice of summary statistic, and not on
the particular realization of the p-values.

The above FDP upper bound can be conservative because the upper bound
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\left\{p_{j}^{*}\leq t\right\}
on the number of false discoveries is rather crude.
Next, we explore two additional techniques to refine this initial impulse.

4.3
Enhancing the FDP upper bound

The main new ingredient in our outlier-detection bound is the envelope construction from Section 3.
The refinements below are modular: the self-refinement step is adapted from
(
15
)
, while the null-proportion tightening is related to the strategy of
(
10
)
. These refinements can be applied on top of any valid simultaneous upper bound on the null empirical process.

4.3.1
A tighter bound on false discoveries

Our first strategy is an improved upper bound on the number of false discoveries, following
(
15
)
.
Given
\delta\in(0,1)
, suppose one has access to a function
B(t)
obeying

\mathbb{P}\big(|\mathcal{R}(t)\cap\mathcal{H}_{0}|\leq B(t),\forall t\in(0,1)\big)\geq 1-\delta.

(15)

We consider a “self-improved” version

B^{\star}(t)=|\mathcal{R}(t)|-\sup_{s\leq t,s\in(0,1)}\{|\mathcal{R}(s)|-B(s)\}_{+}\leq B(t).

(16)

The following result shows that
{B^{\star}(t)}/{(1\vee|\mathcal{R}(t)|})
is also a valid upper bound on the FDP.

Proposition 4.5

(Self-refinement)
.

If the number of false discoveries obeys
\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{R}(t)\cap\mathcal{H}_{0})|\leq B(t),\forall t\in(0,1))\geq 1-\delta
, then

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\textnormal{FDP}(\mathcal{R}(t))\leq\frac{B^{\star}(t)}{1\vee|\mathcal{R}(t)|},\forall t\in(0,1)\bigg)\geq 1-\delta,

where
B^{\star}(t)
is defined in (
16
).
Moreover,
B^{\star}(t)
can be computed efficiently as

\displaystyle B^{\star}(t)=\min_{j:p_{j}\leq t}\{B(p_{j})+|\mathcal{R}(p_{\leq t})|-|\mathcal{R}(p_{j})|\},

(17)

where
p_{\leq t}
denotes the largest p-value not exceeding
t
.

At a high level, the proposition leverages the fact that when enlarging the selection set, the increase in the number of false discoveries cannot exceed the increase in the number of discoveries. Throughout this section and the next, we will apply this refined bound
B^{\star}(t)
when constructing FDP upper bounds.

4.3.2
Upper bound on the number of inliers

Our second strategy is based on upper bounding the number of inliers, that is, the number of true nulls,
m_{0}
. The bound in Theorem
4.4
may be conservative because we substitute the summation over
\mathcal{H}_{0}
with that over the entire set of hypotheses in (
13
), and the number of nulls
|\mathcal{H}_{0}|=m_{0}
with
m
.
Should
m_{0}
be known, one can construct the envelope function
G(t)
based on
\{p_{j}^{*}\}_{j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\sim\mathcal{P}_{n,m_{0}}
instead of
\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
.
This observation suggests developing a more informative bound on
m_{0}
.

We will employ a technique similar to that of
10
to establish such an upper bound. Consider the event

\mathcal{E}=\Big\{\textstyle{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}}\ind\left\{p_{j}^{*}\leq t\right\}\leq G_{m_{0}}(t),\forall~t\in[0,1]\Big\},

where
G_{m_{0}}(\cdot)
is a function which depends on the number of true nulls
m_{0}
.
On the event
\mathcal{E}
, it holds that

\textstyle{\sum_{j=1}^{m}}\ind\left\{p_{j}>t\right\}\geq\textstyle{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}}\ind\left\{p_{j}^{*}>t\right\}\geq m_{0}-G_{m_{0}}(t),\forall~t\in[0,1],

which further implies

m_{0}\leq\max\left\{r:\textstyle{\sum_{j=1}^{m}}\ind\left\{p_{j}>t\right\}\geq r-G_{r}(t),\forall~t\in[0,1]\right\}=:\widehat{m}_{0}.

4.3.3
Integrating the two strategies

With the techniques discussed above, we can now construct a sharper simultaneous upper bound on the FDP.

Theorem 4.6
.

Suppose
G_{k}(\cdot)
obeys

\mathbb{P}(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\ind\left\{p_{j}^{*}\leq t\right\}\leq G_{k}(t),\forall t\in[0,1])\geq 1-\delta
for each
k\in\{1,\dots,m\}
.
Define

\widehat{m}_{0}:=\max\Big\{r\in\{0,1,\dots,m\}\colon\textstyle{\sum_{j=1}^{m}}\ind\{p_{j}>t\}\geq r-G_{r}(t),\forall t\in[0,1]\Big\}.

Let
B(t):=\sup_{k\leq\widehat{m}_{0}}G_{k}(t)
. Then with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that

\textnormal{FDP}(t)\leq\frac{B(t)\wedge|\mathcal{R}(t)|}{1\vee|\mathcal{R}(t)|},\quad\forall~t\in[0,1].

Furthermore, the bound holds when replacing
B(t)
with
B^{\star}(t)=\min_{s\leq t}\{B(s)+|\mathcal{R}(t)|-|\mathcal{R}(s)|\}
.

The computational cost of the proposed procedure is modest. Once the summary
statistics have been computed, evaluating the envelope function
G_{r}(t)
at a
fixed value of
t
requires only
O(1)
operations. Note that the envelope function
G_{r}(t)
can be taken to be nondecreasing in
t

(by replacing it with its monotone envelope if necessary). This property
simplifies the verification step below.

To compute the estimator

\widehat{m}_{0}
, we must verify the condition

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}\{p_{j}>t\}\geq r-G_{r}(t),\qquad\forall t\in[0,1].

Since
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}\{p_{j}>t\}
is a step function with at most
m

discontinuity points and
G_{r}(t)
is nondecreasing, this verification reduces to checking these finitely many
points. For a fixed value of
r
,
this step requires
O(m)
time. As a result, computing
\widehat{m}_{0}
requires

O(m^{2})
operations overall.

Once the functions
\{G_{k}(t)\}_{k\leq\widehat{m}_{0}}
are available, evaluating

B(t)=\sup_{k\leq\widehat{m}_{0}}G_{k}(t)
at a fixed value of
t
costs
O(m)
time. By Proposition
4.5
,

B^{\star}(t)
only needs to be evaluated at
m
candidate p-values at most.
Consequently, computing
B^{\star}(t)
requires
O(m^{2})
time in total.
Therefore, both
\widehat{m}_{0}
and
B^{\star}(t)
can be computed in quadratic time
with respect to
m
.

5
Controlling FDP/precision in conformal selection

In this section, we demonstrate how our method can be used to obtain simultaneous FDP bounds for the selection sets generated by conformal selection
(
17
;
16
)
.
In this problem, one has access to a calibration dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{calib}}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
, where
X_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
are the features, and
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the outcome of interest.
We also have a test dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}=\{X_{n+j}\}_{j\in[m]}
with unknown outcomes
\{Y_{n+j}\}_{j\in[m]}
and wish to identify test samples where the unknown outcomes are substantially large with
Y_{n+j}>c_{n+j}
;
c_{n+j}\in\mathbb{R}
are pre-specified and potentially random thresholds.

5.1
Setup

The above problem can be viewed as a binary classification problem over the test samples. The positive class consists of samples whose outcomes exceed their corresponding thresholds, that is, samples with
Y_{n+j}>c_{n+j}
. A selection set
\mathcal{R}\subseteq[m]
is therefore the set of test samples predicted to be positive. In this language, the precision of
\mathcal{R}
is the fraction of selected samples that are truly positive,

\mathrm{Precision}(\mathcal{R})=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{j\in\mathcal{R},\,Y_{n+j}>c_{n+j}\}}{1\vee|\mathcal{R}|}.

Equivalently, the false discovery proportion is one minus precision:

\mathrm{FDP}(\mathcal{R})=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{j\in\mathcal{R},\,Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}}{1\vee|\mathcal{R}|}.

Thus, an upper bound on the FDP gives a lower bound on the precision of the selected set.

The conformal selection procedure of
17
identifies a selection set
\mathcal{R}
such that the FDR,
\mathbb{E}[\textnormal{FDP}(\mathcal{R})]
, is below a pre-specified level
\alpha\in(0,1)
. This method uses a calibration dataset
\{(X_{i},Y_{i},c_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
. We assume the following threshold observation and exchangeability condition.

Assumption 5.1
.

The random triplets
\{(X_{i},Y_{i},c_{i})\}_{i\in[m+n]}
are exchangeable across
i\in[n+m]
. This condition is automatically satisfied when
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n+m}
are exchangeable and either
c_{i}=c
for every
i\in[n+m]
for some fixed constant
c
, or
c_{i}=g(X_{i})
for every
i\in[n+m]
for some fixed function
g
.

To assess the confidence in rejecting
H_{j}
, conformal selection constructs conformal p-values based on any nonconformity score
V
that satisfies the following monotone property.

Definition 5.2
.

A nonconformity score
V(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot):\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is monotone if
V(x,y,c)\leq V(x,y^{\prime},c)
for any
x\in\mathcal{X},c\in\mathbb{R}
, and any
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
with
y\leq y^{\prime}
.

Generally, the nonconformity score should measure how extreme the value
y
is compared to the typical behavior of the outcome for a given feature value and threshold
c
. For instance, one could define
V(x,y,c)=y-\widehat{\mu}(x)
, where
\widehat{\mu}(\cdot)
is a prediction algorithm estimating the conditional mean of
Y
given
X
.

Conformal selection constructs the p-values

p_{j}^{\textnormal{CS}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\{V(X_{i},Y_{i},c_{i})<V(X_{n+j},c_{n+j},c_{n+j})\}+U_{j}}{n+1},

where
U_{j}\sim\mbox{Unif}(0,1)
are i.i.d. random variables.

As it is natural to select p-values of the smallest magnitudes, we focus on selection sets of the form
\mathcal{R}(t)=\left\{j\in[m]:p_{j}^{\textnormal{CS}}\leq t\right\}
. The conformal selection set in
17
coincides with
\mathcal{R}(t)
when
t\in\mathbb{R}
is the cutoff determined by running the BH procedure on
\{p_{j}^{\textnormal{CS}}\}
at nominal level
\alpha
.
In practice, a uniformly valid upper bound on
\textnormal{FDP}(\mathcal{R}(t))
across
t\in[0,1]
provides useful information on the quality of a series of selection sets, allowing practitioners to flexibly balance power and selection error control.

To prepare, we define the “oracle” conformal uniform variables

\displaystyle p_{j}^{\textnormal{CS},*}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\{V(X_{i},Y_{i},c_{i})<V(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j},c_{n+j})\}+U_{j}}{n+1}.

(18)

Here, the uniform random variables
U_{j}
’s are the same as those in
p_{j}^{\textnormal{CS}}
’s. It is straightforward to verify that the
p_{j}^{*}

\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
from Proposition
2.4
.
While they are unobservable due to the unknown labels
Y_{n+j}
, these oracle p-values are key to constructing our upper bounds.

5.2
Methodology

A key result in conformal selection is that we can bound the number of false discoveries using “oracle” conformal p-values
p_{j}^{*}
, as shown in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

Consider the set of null hypotheses
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\{j\in[m]:Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}
. For any monotone score function
V
and the selection set
\mathcal{R}(t)=\{j\in[m]:p_{j}^{\textnormal{CS}}\leq t\}
, it holds for every
t\in[0,1]
that

|\mathcal{R}(t)\cap\mathcal{H}_{0}|\leq\textstyle{\sum_{j=1}^{m}}\ind\{p_{j}^{\textnormal{CS},*}\leq t\}.

(19)

Furthermore, the above inequality becomes an equality for
t<\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\{Y_{i}\leq c_{i}\}
if for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, one has (1)
V(x,y,c)>V(x,c,c)
for any
y>c
; and
(2)
V(x,y,c)=V(x,c,c)
for
y\leq c
.

Lemma
5.3
shows that it suffices to construct a valid upper bound on the empirical CDF of
\{p_{j}^{\textnormal{CS},*}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, which can be done even though these oracle p-values are not observed, given that their distribution is known.
The second part of Lemma
5.3
hints at the score choice that leads to sharp upper bounds.
An example that satisfies the equality conditions in Lemma
5.3
is the clipped score function

V(x,y,c)=M\ind\{y>c\}+c\ind\{y\leq c\}-\widehat{\mu}(x),

where
M>2\sup_{x}|\widehat{\mu}(x)|
and
\widehat{\mu}(x)
is any predictor of
\mathbb{E}[Y{\,|\,}X=x]
. Such clipped scores are shown to be powerful in
17
and a standard choice in conformal selection methods
1
;
26
;
14
. In general, given any monotone score function
V^{\prime}(x,y,c)
specified by the user, one can always construct

\displaystyle V(x,y,c)=M\ind\{y>c\}+V^{\prime}(x,y,c)\ind\{y\leq c\},

where
M>0
is a sufficiently large constant, or effectively,
M=+\infty
, that preserves the relative ranking of p-values based on
V^{\prime}
while achieving higher power and satisfying the conditions in Lemma
5.3
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
G(\cdot)
be a random function satisfying
\mathbb{P}(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}^{\textnormal{CS},*}\leq t\}\leq G(t),\forall t\in[0,1])\geq 1-\delta
using methods from Section
3
. For selection sets of the form
\mathcal{R}(t)=\{j\in[m]:p_{j}^{\textnormal{CS}}\leq t\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\textnormal{FDP}(\mathcal{R}(t))\leq\frac{mG(t)}{1\vee|\mathcal{R}(t)|},~\forall~t\in[0,1]\bigg)\geq 1-\delta.

The upper bound in Theorem

is particularly tight if the inequality in (

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce a unified framework for simultaneous inference in conformal prediction. Our method builds on constructing high-probability upper bounds for the empirical CDF of conformal uniform variables. We develop simultaneous coverage guarantees for two key applications: false coverage proportion (FCP) in conformal prediction and false discovery proportion (FDP) in multiple testing problems with conformal p-values, such as outlier detection and conformal selection. Through extensive experiments on both synthetic and real datasets, we demonstrate that our method provides valid simultaneous coverage while remaining practically efficient.

Our framework is highly versatile, as it can be applied to many other problems as long as we can simulate from the joint distribution of p-values. Our approach also allows one to choose different summary/test statistics to achieve various inferential goals, making it applicable beyond the specific examples presented in this work.

Our work has several limitations.
A key strength of our approach is that the proposed FDP upper bounds hold simultaneously over all thresholds; however, this generality can lead to conservative bounds when evaluated at a single threshold, such as the threshold selected by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (see Figure
6
). Future work may develop tighter bounds for a specific data-adaptive thresholds, including the Benjamini–Hochberg threshold, and may extend the framework beyond exchangeable settings to handle covariate shift between calibration and test data.

C.2
Variance of the empirical CDF of conformal uniform variables

The conformal uniform variables are generally dependent because they share the
same calibration sample. However, the variance of their empirical CDF has
the same
t(1-t)
shape, up to a multiplicative factor.

Proposition C.1
.

Let
q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}
follow the conformal-uniform distribution
P_{n,m}
in
Proposition
2.4
. Define

\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}\{q_{j}\leq t\},\qquad t\in[0,1].

Then

\operatorname{Var}\{\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)\}=c_{n,m}(t)t(1-t),

where, for
t\in(0,1)
,

c_{n,m}(t)=\frac{1}{m}+\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\rho_{n}(t),

with

\rho_{n}(t)=\frac{(n+1)(k+\gamma^{2})-(k+\gamma)^{2}}{(n+2)(k+\gamma)(n+1-k-\gamma)}.

Here

k=\lfloor(n+1)t\rfloor,\qquad\gamma=(n+1)t-k.

At the endpoints
t=0
and
t=1
, the variance is zero.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.4
, it suffices to work with

q_{j}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{T_{i}<T_{n+j}\}+U_{j}}{n+1},\qquad j=1,\ldots,m,

where
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n+m}
are i.i.d.
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
and

U_{1},\ldots,U_{m}
are i.i.d.
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
, independent of the

T_{i}
’s.

Fix
t\in(0,1)
and write

a=(n+1)t,\qquad k=\lfloor a\rfloor,\qquad\gamma=a-k.

Let
T_{(1)}<\cdots<T_{(n)}
be the order statistics of

T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}
, and define the spacings

D_{0}=T_{(1)},\qquad D_{\ell}=T_{(\ell+1)}-T_{(\ell)},\ \ell=1,\ldots,n-1,\qquad D_{n}=1-T_{(n)}.

Conditional on the calibration variables
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}
, the indicators

I_{j}(t)=\mathbf{1}\{q_{j}\leq t\},\qquad j=1,\ldots,m,

are i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables with success probability

H_{t}=\mathbb{P}(q_{j}\leq t\mid T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}).

Hence

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)\mid T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]=H_{t}

and

\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)\mid T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})=\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}I_{j}(t)\,\middle|\,T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{m}H_{t}(1-H_{t}).

By the law of total variance,

\operatorname{Var}\{\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)\}=\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\mathbb{E}[\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)\mid T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]\right)+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)\mid T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})\right].

Therefore,

\operatorname{Var}\{\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)\}=\operatorname{Var}(H_{t})+\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}[H_{t}(1-H_{t})].

Since
\mathbb{E}[H_{t}]=t
, this can be rewritten as

\operatorname{Var}\{\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)\}=\frac{t(1-t)}{m}+\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\operatorname{Var}(H_{t}).

It remains to compute
\operatorname{Var}(H_{t})
. The spacings

(D_{0},\ldots,D_{n})
follow a Dirichlet distribution with all parameters equal
to one. Using the standard covariance formula for the Dirichlet distribution

[
21
, Chapter 49]
, for any weights
w_{0},\ldots,w_{n}
,

\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}w_{\ell}D_{\ell}\right)=\frac{(n+1)\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}w_{\ell}^{2}-\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}w_{\ell}\right)^{2}}{(n+1)^{2}(n+2)}.

Applying this identity with

w_{\ell}=1\quad(\ell<k),\qquad w_{k}=\gamma,\qquad w_{\ell}=0\quad(\ell>k),

gives

\operatorname{Var}(H_{t})=\frac{(n+1)(k+\gamma^{2})-(k+\gamma)^{2}}{(n+1)^{2}(n+2)}.

Since
k+\gamma=(n+1)t
, we may write

\operatorname{Var}(H_{t})=\rho_{n}(t)t(1-t),

where

\rho_{n}(t)=\frac{(n+1)(k+\gamma^{2})-(k+\gamma)^{2}}{(n+2)(k+\gamma)(n+1-k-\gamma)}.

Substituting this expression into the total-variance decomposition yields

\operatorname{Var}\{\widehat{F}_{n,m}(t)\}=\left[\frac{1}{m}+\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\rho_{n}(t)\right]t(1-t).

The endpoint cases
t=0
and
t=1
are immediate since

\widehat{F}_{n,m}(0)=0
and
\widehat{F}_{n,m}(1)=1
almost surely.
∎

C.3
Constructing calibration-conditional valid p-values

We revisit the calibration-conditional p-values of
[
2
]
and show how our envelope construction
provides a simple and tight way to obtain them with finite-sample guarantees.

Calibration-conditional valid p-values

Classical conformal (marginal) p-values are valid on average across repeated draws of the calibration data,
but may be anti-conservative when conditioning on a specific calibration set. This is unsatisfactory for
a practitioner who only has one calibration sample in hand. Calibration-Conditional Valid (CCV) p-values
remedy this issue: with probability at least
1-\delta
over the randomness of the calibration data, each CCV
p-value is valid conditional on that calibration set. Formally, for any
t\in(0,1)
and inlier test sample

X_{n+1}\sim P_{X}
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathbb{P}\!\left(\widehat{u}^{(\mathrm{ccv})}(X_{n+1})\leq t\,\middle|\,D\right)\leq t\ \text{for all $t$}\right)\geq 1-\delta,

where
D
denotes the calibration sample. Thus, CCV p-values provide guarantees that are specific to the user’s
data, not just “on average” across hypothetical users.

The key idea of
[
2
]
is to construct
a sequence of thresholds
(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
that uniformly lower-bound the order statistics of i.i.d. uniforms.
These thresholds are then used to transform marginal p-values into calibration-conditional valid (CCV) p-values.
The following theorem provides the precise adjustment.

Theorem C.2

(Conditional p-value adjustment)
.

Let
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
with order statistics

U_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq U_{(n)}
, and fix
\delta\in(0,1)
. Suppose
0\leq b_{1}\leq\cdots\leq b_{n}\leq 1
satisfy

\mathbb{P}\big[U_{(1)}\leq b_{1},\ldots,U_{(n)}\leq b_{n}\big]\geq 1-\delta.

(20)

Let
b_{0}=0
,
b_{n+1}=1
, and define
h:[0,1]\to[0,1]
by
h(t)=b_{\lceil(n+1)t\rceil}
. Then

\widehat{u}^{(\mathrm{ccv})}=h\circ\widehat{u}^{(\mathrm{marg})}
is calibration-conditional valid as long as
\widehat{u}^{(\mathrm{marg})}(X_{n+1})
is a valid p-value.

Condition (
20
) is equivalent to requiring a
uniform lower bound
on the empirical CDF

\widehat{F}_{n}(t)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind(U_{i}\leq t)
:

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\widehat{F}_{n}(b_{i})\geq\frac{i}{n}\ \text{ for all }i\in[n]\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Hence, constructing
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
reduces to computing a lower envelope for
\widehat{F}_{n}
.

Envelope-to-threshold construction.

Let
L_{n}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
be any nondecreasing function such that

\mathbb{P}\big(\widehat{F}_{n}(t)\ \geq\ L_{n}(t)\ \text{ for all }t\in[0,1]\big)\geq 1-\delta.

Then

b_{i}:=\inf\{\,t\in[0,1]:L_{n}(t)\geq i/n\,\},\qquad i=1,\dots,n,

satisfies (
20
). In practice, we obtain
L_{n}
via Algorithm
1

lower-tail
summary statistic (e.g., a flipped KS statistic or truncated lower-tail
higher-criticism). This yields:

•

Finite-sample

(1-\delta)
guarantees;

•

Adaptivity
to the shape of
\widehat{F}_{n}(t)
, often yielding tighter bounds near small
t
;

•

Flexibility
to tune the choice of summary statistic for the application at hand.

Overall, this provides a straightforward, plug-and-play method to construct CCV p-values using the same
Monte Carlo algorithm developed in the main text.

, it suffices to verify the inequality at the extreme points
(\beta,x)\in\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}
, which is straightforward.
∎

Proof of Lemma

.

Let
m_{0}=|\mathcal{H}_{0}|
be the number of nulls (inliers). By Proposition

, the vector of null conformal

p

has the same distribution as the first

conformal
p
-values under
\mathcal{P}_{n,m_{0}}
(the law in Proposition
2.4

The family
\{\mathcal{P}_{n,m}\}_{m\geq 1}
is consistent in

2.4
,
each

depends only on
(T_{1},\dots,T_{n},T_{n+j},U_{j})
and is thus unaffected by the presence or

m_{0}
coordinates of
\mathcal{P}_{n,m}

equals
\mathcal{P}_{n,m_{0}}
.
Fix
m
and consider
\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
. Let
\mathcal{K}(\,\cdot\,|\,\cdot\,)
be the conditional
distribution of the remaining coordinates
((p_{m_{0}+1},\dots,p_{m}))
given the first
m_{0}
coordinates
under
\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
. On the probability space carrying the given
(p_{1},\dots,p_{m_{0}})
, define

(p^{*}_{m_{0}+1},\dots,p^{*}_{m})\sim\mathcal{K}(\,\cdot\,|\,p_{1},\dots,p_{m_{0}})
, independently of all else.
By construction, the concatenated vector

(p_{1},\dots,p_{m_{0}},p^{*}_{m_{0}+1},\dots,p^{*}_{m})

∎

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Let
R(t)=\{j\in[m]:\,p_{j}\leq t\}
and write
V(t)=|R(t)\cap\mathcal{H}_{0}|=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\ind\{p_{j}\leq t\}
.
By Lemma
4.3
, there exist
(p_{1}^{*},\dots,p_{m}^{*})\sim\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
such that

V(t)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\ind\{p_{j}\leq t\}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}^{*}\leq t\}.

On the event

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}^{*}\leq t\}\leq G(t)\ \text{for all }t\in[0,1],

we have
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}^{*}\leq t\}\leq m\,G(t)
for all
t
, hence

\mathrm{FDP}(t)=\frac{V(t)}{1\vee|R(t)|}\leq\frac{m\,G(t)}{1\vee\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}\leq t\}}\qquad\text{for all }t\in[0,1].

∎

Proof of Proposition
4.5
.

Let
V(t)=|R(t)\cap\mathcal{N}|
and suppose
\mathbb{P}\big(V(s)\leq B(s)\ \forall s\in(0,1)\big)\geq 1-\delta
.
For any
s\leq t
,

V(t)\leq V(s)+\big(|R(t)|-|R(s)|\big)\leq B(s)+|R(t)|-|R(s)|,

hence

V(t)\leq|R(t)|-\big(|R(s)|-B(s)\big).

Taking the supremum over
s\leq t
and using
V(t)\leq R(t)
yields

V(t)\leq|R(t)|-\sup_{s\leq t}\big(|R(s)|-B(s)\big)_{+}\;=:\;B^{\star}(t).

Therefore
\mathbb{P}\big(V(t)\leq B^{\star}(t)\ \forall t\big)\geq 1-\delta
, and dividing by
1\vee|R(t)|
gives the
stated FDP bound.
For the computable form, note that both
|R(s)|
and
(typically)

B(s)
are right–continuous step functions
that only change when
s
passes an observed
p
-value. Thus the supremum over
s\leq t
is attained at
some
s=p_{j}\leq t
, and a short rearrangement yields

B^{\star}(t)=\min_{j:\,p_{j}\leq t}\Big\{B(p_{j})+|R(p_{\leq t})|-|R(p_{j})|\Big\}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
4.6
.

(i) Let
r=|\mathcal{H}_{0}|
. By the assumed guarantee for
G_{r}(\cdot)
and Lemma
4.3
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\ind\{p_{j}\leq t\}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{r}\ind\{p_{j}^{*}\leq t\}\leq G_{r}(t)\qquad\forall t.

Equivalently,

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\ind\{p_{j}>t\}\geq r-G_{r}(t)
,
and hence

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}>t\}\;\geq\;r-G_{r}(t)\qquad\forall t.

By the definition of
\widehat{m}_{0}
as the largest
r
satisfying the same system of inequalities, it follows
that
\widehat{m}_{0}\geq|\mathcal{H}_{0}|
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

(ii) Let
r=|H_{0}|
and define the event

\mathcal{E}_{r}\;=\;\Bigl\{\sum_{j=1}^{r}\ind\{p_{j}^{\ast}\leq t\}\leq G_{r}(t)\ \text{for all }t\in[0,1]\Bigr\}.

By the theorem’s assumption applied at
r=|H_{0}|
, we have
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{r})\geq 1-\delta
.
On
\mathcal{E}_{r}
, Lemma
4.3
yields the coordinate-wise coupling

\sum_{j\in H_{0}}\ind\{p_{j}\leq t\}\;\leq\;\sum_{j=1}^{r}\ind\{p_{j}^{\ast}\leq t\}\;\leq\;G_{r}(t)\qquad\forall t\in[0,1].

Still on
\mathcal{E}_{r}
, part (i) shows
\widehat{m}_{0}\geq r
, hence

B(t)\;=\;\sup_{k\leq\widehat{m}_{0}}G_{k}(t)\;\geq\;G_{r}(t)\quad\text{for all }t.

Combining the previous displays, on
\mathcal{E}_{r}
we have
|R(t)\cap H_{0}|\leq B(t)
for all
t
, so

\mathbb{P}\bigl(|R(t)\cap H_{0}|\leq B(t)\ \forall t\bigr)\ \geq\ 1-\delta.

Finally, applying Proposition
4.5
with this simultaneous upper bound
B(t)
gives

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\mathrm{FDP}(R(t))\leq\frac{B^{\ast}(t)}{1\vee|R(t)|}\ \text{for all }t\Bigr)\ \geq\ 1-\delta,

where
B^{\star}(t)=\min_{j:p_{j}\leq t}\{B(p_{j})+|R(p_{\leq t})|-|R(p_{j})|\}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.3
.

From the definition of the selection set
\mathcal{R}(t)
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{R}(t)\cap\mathcal{H}_{0}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}\leq t,Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}

\displaystyle\overset{}{\leq}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}\leq t,V(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j},c_{n+j})\leq V(X_{n+j},c_{n+j},c_{n+j})\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}\leq t,p_{j}^{*}\leq p_{j}\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}^{*}\leq t\}.

Here, the first inequality uses the fact that the nonconformity score
V
is monotone.

On the other hand, if the score function satisfies
the conditions in the lemma, then if
p_{j}^{*}\leq t
, we must have
Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}
because otherwise
p_{j}\geq\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\left\{V(X_{i},Y_{i},c_{i})<V(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j},c_{n+j})\right\}\geq\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\left\{Y_{i}\leq c_{i}\right\}=t
. So we have

\ind\left\{p_{j}^{*}\leq t\right\}=\ind\left\{p_{j}^{*}\leq t,Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\right\}=\ind\left\{p_{j}^{*}\leq t,p_{j}^{*}=p_{j}\right\}=\ind\{p_{j}\leq t,Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
5.4
.

Let

\mathcal{E}\;=\;\Bigl\{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}^{\ast}\leq t\}\leq G(t)\ \text{for all }t\in[0,1]\Bigr\},

so
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\delta

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}^{\ast}\leq t\}\ \leq\ m\,G(t)\qquad\forall t.

For the conformal selection set
