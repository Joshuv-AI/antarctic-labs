Protocol to induce memory generalization and stress-like behaviors without memory strengthening using contextual fear conditioning in mice
Authors: Xin Cheng, Yan Zhao, Yubo Hu, Wei-Jye Lin, Xiaojing Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOver-generalization of negative memory is common in psychiatric disorders, including depression and anxiety. Here, we present a protocol to dissociate memory generalization from memory strengthening and induce lasting stress-related behavioral alterations using contextual fear conditioning in adult mice. We describe the steps for repeated conditioning in identical or distinct contexts, followed by memory testing and behavioral assays of stress-related phenotypes. This protocol enables the investigation of the behavioral and biological mechanisms underlying pathological memory generalization and its consequences.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Cheng et al.1

Before you begin

Innovation

This protocol presents a reproducible approach to induce and assess negative memory generalization in mice using a modified contextual fear conditioning (CFC) paradigm.1 A key advance of this design is its ability to double-dissociate memory generalization from memory strengthening. In contrast to conventional paradigms, in which high-intensity foot shocks often elicit both stronger and more generalized fear,2,3,4 or paradigms that require extended incubation periods to reveal generalized memory over time,5,6 our protocol enables efficient induction of robust memory generalization within a defined experimental framework.

A further advance is that the same paradigm that induces memory generalization also produces long-lasting psychological distress-related behaviors, creating an integrated platform to examine how memories of acute aversive experiences give rise to broader behavioral outcomes. In contrast to standard models based on chronic stress, this approach reveals that two training sessions in different, but not identical, contexts are sufficient to induce enduring stress-related phenotypes. Importantly, this contextual differentiation relies on distinct multisensory environmental configurations, including unique chamber geometry, illumination, tactile features, and olfactory cues provided by the cleaning agents used for each context. This feature makes the assay particularly useful not only for studying how the neural substrates underlying negative memory generalization contribute to behavioral abnormalities, but also for preclinical screening and evaluating intervention strategies aimed at reducing memory overgeneralization and its associated psychological stress phenotypes.

This workflow allows the combined evaluation of behavioral, physiological, circuit, and molecular outcomes. It is compatible with other analyses, including circuit tracing and functional manipulation, in vivo miniscope imaging in freely moving mice to identify neural activity patterns, and circuit-specific transcriptomic sequencing to resolve the molecular signatures underlying memory-associated psychological distress.

Expected outcomes

Using this protocol, mice subjected to repeated CFC are expected to exhibit distinct behavioral profiles associated with memory strengthening versus memory generalization. Compared with the “1×Tr” group, mice in the “2×Tr same” group are expected to show stronger, context-specific fear memory, reflected by high freezing levels in the trained context and limited freezing in the novel context. In stress-related behavioral assays, mice with generalized memory in the “2×Tr altered” group are expected to exhibit increased feeding latency in the NSFT, decreased grooming duration in the ST, increased immobility in the TST, reduced social preference in the SIT, and reduced sucrose preference in the SPT (Figures 3C–3G). These behavioral alterations can persist for at least 2 weeks after the final conditioning session (Figures 3H-3L), supporting the use of this paradigm for studying sustained stress-related behavioral changes following acute aversive experiences.

Behavioral outcomes are expected to be reproducible across cohorts when context assignment, handling, and testing conditions are carefully controlled.

Limitations

While this protocol provides a reproducible behavioral framework to dissociate memory generalization from memory strengthening and to examine subsequent stress-related behavioral alterations, several limitations should be considered when applying this paradigm.

First, the performance of the protocol is highly sensitive to the degree of environmental contrast between the training contexts. Successful dissociation between the “2×Tr same” and “2×Tr altered” conditions requires carefully controlled differences in sensory features, such as visual cues, illumination, chamber geometry, and odor. If the contrast between Context A and Context B is too weak, the “2×Tr altered” condition may be less effective in inducing memory generalization and subsequent stress-related behavioral changes. Researchers should therefore calibrate these parameters according to their specific laboratory environment.

Second, the protocol has so far been optimized in a single inbred strain, which may limit generalizability across genetic backgrounds. Future studies should evaluate its robustness in additional inbred strains or outbred mice.

Finally, although this protocol identifies a unique link between memory generalization and stress-related phenotypes, it has not yet been validated with pharmacological interventions. Whether clinically relevant treatments, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors or ketamine, can reverse memory generalization and the associated stress-related phenotypes induced by the “2×Tr altered” condition remains to be determined.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

No obvious behavioral response to foot shock during conditioning (related to Steps 5 and 6).

This problem can arise for several reasons. First, the electrical circuit may be compromised. Grid floors contaminated with feces or urine can increase electrical resistance, and moisture on the grid can cause partial short-circuiting, thereby reducing the effective current delivered to the animal. Second, a small number of mice may reduce effective shock exposure by contacting only a single grid bar during shock presentation.

Potential solution

•Calibrate the shock intensity using an ammeter before each experiment and confirm that all electrical connections are secure.•Thoroughly clean and dry the grid floor between subjects to maintain stable conductivity.

Problem 2

Mice show little or no freezing during memory tests (related to Steps 8-10).

Stress can exert intensity-dependent effects on fear learning and memory. Although moderate stress may facilitate fear conditioning, excessive environmental stress may impair the formation or retrieval of the context-foot-shock association or interfere with the stable expression of freezing during testing. Unintended cues present during training or testing, such as noise or other disturbances, may also interfere with the association between the context and foot shock, thereby reducing subsequent freezing behavior.

Potential solution

•Extend the days of handling before training to reduce baseline anxiety in the animals and improve behavioral stability.•Avoid unintended external cues during training and testing, such as auditory disturbances (e.g., phone alerts or loud conversation), strong odors (e.g., perfume), or environmental disturbances (e.g., nearby construction).•If such disturbances occur, discard the affected trials. Resume the experiment only after the environment has stabilized, or repeat the experiment using a new cohort of animals.

Problem 3

Mice show unusually short or long feeding latencies in the NSFT (related to Step 12).

This is often caused by a poorly controlled hunger state at the time of testing. Extremely short or long latencies may indicate inappropriate fasting durations.

Potential solution

•Standardize the duration of food deprivation (e.g., 18–24 h).•Replace the bedding at the start of food deprivation to minimize access to residual chow or feces.•Use bedding material that is less likely to be ingested during fasting, such as aspen shavings instead of corn cob bedding.

Mice show little, no, or highly variable grooming behavior during the ST (related to Step 13).

Problem 5

Mice climb onto the tops of the cups or the walls during the SIT (related to Step 14).

This may result from insufficient habituation to the testing chamber. It may also reflect elevated anxiety in the subject mouse.

Potential solution

Problem 6

Mice show abnormally low or high sucrose preference during the SPT (related to Step 16).

In naïve mice, the sucrose preference differential index should typically range from 0.7 to 0.85. Values outside this range may suggest that the sucrose concentration is not optimal for the experimental setting. Baseline preference may vary with housing conditions and with the brand or purity of the sucrose used.

Potential solution

•Before the formal experiment, perform a preliminary concentration-gradient test in a small cohort of naïve mice, using 1% sucrose as the starting concentration.•Adjust the sucrose concentration within a range of 0.5% to 2%. Reduce the concentration if naïve mice show excessively high preference, and increase it if preference is too low.
