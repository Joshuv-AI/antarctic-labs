Interrelationships between suicide risk, childhood abuse, depression, hopelessness, and social support in treatment-naïve adolescent depression patients: a network analysis
Authors: Wenxiu Luo, Yuqin Song, Cailin Xie, Qiuyue Fan, Jiarui Shao, Yu Cen, Ping Mo, Xingyu He, Min Liu, Cen Lin, Xianghong Ding, Lu Liu, Jiaming Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuicide caused by depression in adolescents is a major global public health challenge, but the assessment of suicide risk in this population remains inadequate. This study aims to use network analysis to examine the interrelationships between suicide risk, childhood abuse, depression, hopelessness, and social support in treatment-naïve adolescent depression patients.MethodThe study included 518 treatment-naïve adolescent depression patients aged 10–18 (393 females and 125 males). General patient information was collected, and assessments were conducted using the Suicidal Behavior Questionnaire, Childhood Trauma Questionnaire, Self-Rating Depression Scale, Adolescent Social Support Scale, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, and Beck Hopelessness Scale. Network analysis was performed using R language to examine the correlations between various factors, and the centrality and stability of the network structure were tested.ResultsIn this network structure, emotional abuse showed the strongest edge with suicide risk. Furthermore, hopelessness, sleep duration, and depression were also linked to suicide risk.ConclusionsChildhood trauma experiences, especially emotional abuse, are strongly associated with suicide risk in treatment-naïve adolescent depression patients.Trial registrationThis study has been registered with the China Clinical Trials Registry in (Registration No. ChiCTR2200064623; Registration Status: Prospective registration)13/10/2022. Background

Depression is one of the most common mood disorders among adolescents, characterized by prolonged duration and a high recurrence rate, which significantly impacts their academic performance, interpersonal relationships, and social functioning [1]. Of particular concern is its strong association with suicidal behavior, which has become the second leading cause of death among adolescents worldwide [2]. In recent years, both the incidence and the age of onset of adolescent depression have shown a rising trend. According to a report by the World Health Organization, depression is projected to become the leading contributor to the global disease burden by 2030 [3]. The incidence of depression among adolescents has significantly increased in recent years, with 2019 data from the global Burden of Disease showing that the prevalence of depression is 0.98% among 10-14-year-olds, and 2.69% among 15-19-year-olds [4]. Moreover, the 2022 “National Depression Blue Book” indicates that the prevalence of adolescent depression has reached 15–20%. Globally, the lifetime mortality rate from suicide among individuals with depression is 19.7%; while in China, the lifetime incidence of suicidal ideation and attempted suicide among depression patients is 53.1% and 23.7%, respectively [5].

Given the increasing prevalence of depression among adolescents, the earlier onset age, and the high suicide risk, it is both necessary and urgent to further explore the mechanisms linking adolescent depression and suicide. Although depression itself is a well-recognized risk factor for suicide, accumulating evidence suggests that the relationship between depression and suicide is complex and modulated by various socio-psychological and developmental factors [6–9]. Among these risk factors, childhood maltreatment, with emotional abuse in particular, has been increasingly identified as a robust predictor of suicidality [8]. Emotional abuse, as a common yet underrecognised form of maltreatment, may have particularly profound effects on children and adolescents during critical periods of self-identity and social skill development [10]. Emotional abuse often coexists with other forms of neglect or abuse, and cumulative trauma may further increase the risk [11, 12]. The Interpersonal-Psychological Theory of Suicide (IPTS) posits that suicidal desire arises from the simultaneous experience of thwarted belongingness and perceived burdensomeness [13]. Emotional abuse may foster persistent feelings of worthlessness, helplessness, and perceived burdensomeness, thereby increasing the risk of suicide [14].

Moreover, sleep disorders impair the emotional regulation and impulse control functions of the prefrontal cortex [15], lowering the threshold for coping with negative emotions and suicidal impulses. The lack of available support during nighttime further fosters negative thinking, thereby increasing suicide risk [16]. Given its widespread occurrence and potential for intervention, sleep disorders hold significant clinical relevance.

Previous studies have confirmed that social support is negatively correlated with suicidal ideation [17]. According to the stress-buffering hypothesis, social support can buffer the negative effects of mental disorders such as anxiety, depression and suicidal thoughts [18]. As an important mediator between emotional abuse and suicidal ideation, social support may play a crucial role in suicide prevention for adolescents experiencing emotional abuse [17].

Despite decades of research on suicide-related issues, suicidal behavior remains difficult to predict. Rather than declining, the global incidence of suicide has increased by approximately 60% since the 1960s, a trend that may partly reflect the limited predictive power of individual risk factors for suicidal ideation and behavior [19]. This may also be a limitation of current research methods, as traditional linear risk factor models or mediation models fail to capture the dynamic interactions between symptoms. Therefore, it is necessary to incorporate multiple factors and employ diverse research methods to better explore the complex relationships between these factors.

Given the complex and multifactorial nature of suicide, network analysis provides a promising theoretical and methodological framework. The approach can illustrate the complex relationships between diseases and various factors, as well as the influence of different factors on the network [20, 21]. It has been previously used in the field of suicide, with clinically useful and consistent results [22]. A network structure diagram includes two critical elements: “nodes” and “edges”. Nodes represent psychological symptoms, while edges denote the connections between these symptoms. Nodes with higher centrality have more important positions in network structure. The thicker the edge, the stronger the correlation [23]. Within such a network, central nodes (those with the strongest and most numerous connections) represent promising targets for clinical intervention [24].

Current study

Previous network analyses have begun to explore the complex interactions between factors related to adolescent suicide. A network analysis conducted in Spain among adolescents with a history of suicide attempts revealed strong associations between depressive symptoms, childhood trauma, and the number of suicide attempts [25]. However, because it focused exclusively on individuals with documented suicide attempts, its findings may not fully generalize to suicide risk in the broader population of adolescents with depression. Another network analysis involving adolescents with major depressive disorder (MDD) identified a strong association between difficulties in emotional recognition and suicidal ideation [26]. Although these findings offer valuable insights for interventions targeting suicidal ideation, the study did not incorporate key developmental risk factors, such as childhood maltreatment, or protective factors, such as social support, into its network model. To move beyond descriptive symptom co-occurrence, the present study is guided by an integrated stress-diathesis and interpersonal-psychological framework. Within this model, emotional abuse is conceptualized as a distal vulnerability factor that shapes maladaptive cognitive-affective patterns; hopelessness and sleep disturbance are positioned as proximal, modifiable pathways that directly amplify suicidal ideation; and perceived social support is included as a theoretically grounded protective buffer capable of interrupting risk cascades [27]. Clinically, this network is designed to inform early risk stratification and precision intervention planning during the initial assessment of adolescents with depression-a decision point where clinicians must rapidly identify which co-occurring factors exert the greatest systemic influence on suicide risk and should therefore be prioritized for targeted monitoring or intervention. Network analysis is specifically employed here because it captures reciprocal, symptom-level dynamics and identifies structural leverage points (e.g., strength and expected influence) that linear multivariable models cannot detect, thereby translating complex comorbidity into actionable treatment targets.

Critically, to our knowledge, no existing network analysis has specifically investigated the constellation of suicide risk in treatment-naïve adolescent depression patients. This is a significant gap, as this population is unaffected by the confounding effects of prior treatments and represents a critical window for early intervention. Therefore, our study aims to construct a comprehensive network structure linking suicide risk, depressive symptoms, childhood trauma (with its subtypes), hopelessness, sleep quality, and social support specifically within this unique and clinically important group. Identifying core nodes within the network can provide more precise prevention strategies for suicide risk in these patients. The network is intended to inform early risk stratification and targeted intervention planning during initial assessment of depressed adolescents, where clinicians must prioritize among co-occurring risk and protective factors.

Consistent with the stress-diathesis and IPTS framework outlined above, we hypothesize that within the psychological network underlying suicidal vulnerability, emotional abuse will emerge as a central node closely linked to suicide risk, while hopelessness and sleep disturbance will also demonstrate significant connections to suicidal outcomes. Conversely, social support is expected to function as a key protective factor, buffering against these risk pathways and contributing to the network’s overall resilience.

Methods

Participants

This study is a cross-sectional study conducted from September 2022 to August 2023 among treatment-naïve adolescent depression patients at the Mental Health Center outpatient clinic of Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College. Inclusion criteria: (1) Age 10–18 years; (2) Participants diagnosed with depression by two professional psychiatrists according to the International Classification of Diseases, Eleventh Revision (ICD-11); (3) In this study, treatment-naïve depression patients refer to individuals who meet the ICD-11 diagnostic criteria for depression and have not received any psychiatric evaluation, diagnosis, or related treatment (including psychotherapy, physical therapy, or pharmacotherapy) prior to this study. Our inclusion criteria ensure that all patients enrolled in the study had not received any treatment before the current episode of depression. Exclusion criteria: (1) Patients diagnosed with anxiety, depression, hyperactivity, sleep disorders, or other psychiatric illnesses before this visit; (2) Patients who had previously taken anti-anxiety medications, antidepressants, or drugs to improve sleep; (3) Patients with other serious organic diseases; (4) Patients unable to understand the content of the scales. A total of 733 patients were assessed, with 215 excluded based on the exclusion criteria, ultimately enrolling 518 patients in the study.

Measurements

Basic information

General information about the patients was collected using a custom survey, including age, education level, parental marital status, experiences of being left behind, and place of residence.

Suicidal behavior questionnaire-revised (SBQ-R)

The study use the SBQ-R [28] as a tool to assess patients’ suicide risk. This self-report instrument consists of four items: the first item assesses lifetime suicidal ideation and attempts, scoring from “no suicidal ideation” (1 point) to “suicidal ideation” (2 points), “suicidal plan” (3 points), and “suicidal attempt” (4 points). The second item evaluates the frequency of suicidal ideation in the past year, with scores ranging from “never” (1 point) to “rarely (once)” (2 points), “sometimes (twice)” (3 points), “often (3–4 times)” (4 points), and “always (5 or more times)” (5 points). The third item rates the threat of suicidal behavior from mild to severe (1–3 points). The fourth item assesses the likelihood of future suicidal behavior, from “absolutely not” (0 points) to “very unlikely” (1 point), “somewhat unlikely” (2 points), “slightly unlikely” (3 points), “slightly likely” (4 points), “moderately likely” (5 points), to “very likely” (6 points) [29–32]. The total score ranges from 3 to 18 points, with higher scores indicating a higher risk of suicide [32]. In our study, the total score of the SBQ-R was treated as a continuous variable of suicide risk in all primary analyses, including the network analysis. We used the validated Chinese version of the scale [31], which has demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.75) [33]. In our study, its Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.77.

Childhood trauma questionnaire-short form (CTQ-SF)

In this study, the CTQ-SF [34] was used to assess the patients’ experiences of childhood abuse. This instrument is one of the most widely used tools globally for measuring childhood abuse. The CTQ-SF consists of 28 items, including 25 clinical items and 3 validity items, all rated on a 5-point scale based on frequency (“never” scores 1 point; “rarely” 2 points; “sometimes” 3 points; “often” 4 points; “always” 5 points). The questionnaire is divided into five dimensions: physical neglect, emotional neglect, sexual abuse, physical abuse, and emotional abuse [32]. A higher total score indicates a higher degree of childhood abuse [35]. Sexual abuse was defined as “sexual contact or conduct between a child younger than 18 years of age and an adult or older person”. Physical abuse was defined as “bodily assaults on a child by an adult or older person that posed a risk of or resulted in injury”. Emotional abuse was defined as “verbal assaults on a child’s sense of worth or well-being or any humiliating or demeaning behavior directed toward a child by an adult or older person”. Physical neglect was defined as “the failure of caretakers to provide for a child’s basic physical needs, including food, shelter, clothing, safety, and health care” (poor parental supervision was also included in this definition if it places children’s safety in jeopardy). Emotional neglect was defined as “the failure of caretakers to meet children’s basic emotional and psychological needs, including love, belonging, nurturance, and support” [34]. We used the validated Chinese version of the CTQ-SF [36], and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale in our study was 0.84.

Self-rating depression scale (SDS)

This study employs the SDS [37] to assess the severity of depressive symptoms in patients. It consists of 20 items, each rated on a 4-point scale from 1 (“Rarely or none of the time”) to 4 (“Most or all of the time”). The sum of the scores for the 20 items is multiplied by 1.25 to obtain the SDS index score, where a higher total indicates more severe depression [38]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the Chinese version of this scale has been widely used and validated in various populations, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.75 to 0.89 [39–41].

Social support scale (SSS)

The SSS [42], originally developed to measure social support among college students, has also been found applicable to high school students. The scale is divided into three dimensions: perceived support, objective support, and support utilization [43], featuring 17 items rated on a 5-point scale from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly agree”). Higher scores generally indicate higher levels of social support. The scale is known for its good reliability and validity [44]. In our study, its Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.90.

Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI)

The PSQI [45] is used in this study to evaluate patients’ sleep quality. The PSQI consists of 19 items forming seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored from 0 to 3, contributing to a total PSQI score where a higher score indicates poorer sleep quality. Considering that different dimensions of sleep problems may exhibit distinct clinical characteristics and require different intervention strategies in clinical practice [46], the subdimensions of the sleep scale were therefore included as individual nodes in the network analysis. The Chinese version of the PSQI employed in our research has proven to be reliable and valid [47, 48]. In our study, its Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.68.

Beck hopelessness scale (BHS)

This study employs the BHS [49] to assess the level of hopelessness among patients. The BHS consists of 20 items. For each statement, responses are scored as either 0 or 1. The BHS items were introduced in the network analysis using the three factors devised by the scale authors: feelings about the future, loss of motivation, and expectations for the future [50]. The total score is the sum of all item scores, ranging from 0 to 20, with higher scores indicating greater levels of hopelessness. We used the validated Chinese version of the Beck Hopelessness Scale [51]. In this study, its Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.77.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics for general information were conducted using SPSS version 27. Missing data were minimal, with less than 6% of values missing per variable. Missing data were processed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) with predictive mean matching (pmm). Twenty imputed datasets were generated. Descriptive statistics were calculated using the first completed imputed dataset from multiple imputation. Normality was established using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous data that do not conform to a normal distribution are presented as medians and interquartile ranges [M(Q1–Q3)]. Nonparametric tests are used for comparisons between groups. Categorical variables are represented as [n (%)], and comparisons between two groups are performed using the Chi-square test. Based on a two-tailed test, the significance level is preset at 0.05. Given the multiple group comparisons conducted between male and female participants across clinical variables, we applied the False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction to control for Type I error inflation. For continuous variables (non-normally distributed), Cliff’s delta was calculated. For categorical variables, odds ratios (OR) are reported. For three-category variables, Cramér’s V was calculated and reported. All OR values represent the risk ratio for males relative to females.

Network structure

Variable inclusion is theory driven, not data-driven. Select nodes based on their established role in adolescent suicide risk pathways and their potential for clinical modification. Consistent with our pre-set assumptions, the main network metrics are node strength centrality and expected influence. The stability and accuracy of these specific indicators are evaluated using a case descent guidance program, and clinical interpretation is limited to indicators that demonstrate acceptable stability (CS coefficient ≥ 0.25). Given that the variables involved in this study are derived from scales with different scoring ranges, to avoid scale differences introducing bias into the network estimation, we standardized all continuous variables using z-scores prior to the network analysis. For each imputed dataset, an EBICglasso network was estimated (Pearson correlations, LASSO regularization with EBIC, tuning γ = 0.5). The final network was obtained by taking the median edge weight across the 20 imputed networks and symmetrizing. Bivariate associations were estimated using Pearson correlations, which is standard practice for summed subscale scores that approximate continuous distributions and aligns with established protocols in clinical network analysis. We acknowledge that several measures exhibited bounded ranges and mild skewness; however, subscale totals comprising ≥ 4 items typically satisfy Pearson assumptions under the central limit theorem. To ensure robustness to non-normality and ordinal-like scaling, we conducted a prespecified sensitivity analysis re-estimating the network using Spearman rank-order correlations. To assess multicollinearity, pairwise Pearson correlations among all 20 variables were computed, and variance inflation factors (VIF) were derived from a linear model with suicide risk as the dependent variable Pairwise Pearson correlations and variance inflation factors (VIF) indicated no problematic collinearity among the variables (all |r| < 0.7, max VIF = 2.647).

Network analysis is conducted in RStudio using R version 4.4.0. The qgraph package (version 1.9.8) and bootnet package (version 1.6) were used to construct a network model that examines the relationships between suicide risk, depression symptoms, childhood abuse, social support, feelings of hopelessness, and sleep quality in treatment-naïve children and adolescent patients. The model uses the Pearson correlation matrix as input, employs the LASSO, and the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) to reduce spurious associations within the network, thereby creating a sparser symptom network [52], with the EBIC tuning parameter γ set to 0.5. In the network model, depression symptoms, five types of childhood trauma experiences, three dimensions of social support, seven dimensions of sleep quality, three dimensions of hopelessness, and suicide risk are included as nodes. The connections between nodes are represented by edges, where the thickness of an edge indicates the strength of the association (weight) between nodes, and the color of an edge indicates the sign of the relationship (green for positive, red for negative) [52].

Centrality

Network centrality was evaluated using node strength (sum of absolute edge weights) and expected influence (sum of signed edge weights), which are the most stable and clinically interpretable metrics in psychological networks [53]. Closeness and betweenness were computed for methodological transparency but are explicitly excluded from substantive interpretation. Contemporary guidelines caution that these path-based metrics are highly unstable in regularized networks, sensitive to minor weight fluctuations, and often yield artifactual path elongation when sparse structures are estimated [54]. All clinical inferences and target-prioritization recommendations are therefore grounded exclusively in strength and expected influence, with stability evaluated via case-dropping bootstrap procedures (CS-coefficient ≥ 0.25). In the context of research related to mental health, strength is the most commonly used centrality measure. A higher strength value of a node indicates that it has stronger connections and a more influential role within the network structure, thereby identifying it as a core symptom [55]. The “centralityPlot” function in the qgraph package is utilized to compute the centrality indices of nodes. This function provides a visual representation of the relative importance of each node within the network [56].

Subgroup analysis & multiplicity control

Gender-stratified networks were estimated within the same MICE framework (separate imputations for males and females, pooled by the median). It is acknowledged that LASSO regularization penalizes weak associations to zero, which can be exacerbated in smaller sub samples or when variable variance is limited. Therefore, nodes with zero intensity centrality reflect complete regularization of event edges, rather than being essentially unrelated. Case-dropping bootstrap (1,000 iterations) was used to assess the stability of centrality in each gender network, and the correlation-stability (CS) coefficient was reported. The Network Comparison Test (NCT) [57] was performed on the first imputed dataset (1,000 permutations), which evaluated global structural invariance followed by node specific edge/centrality differences. After checking the differences in node level centrality, multiple comparison correction was performed on all test nodes using the Benjamini Hochberg error discovery rate (FDR) program. Given the small male sample and the exploratory nature of the subgroup comparisons, all node-level findings were treated as hypothesis-generating regardless of nominal significance.

Stability

The stability of the network is assessed using the bootnet package in R. Bootstrap confidence intervals are employed to evaluate the accuracy of the edge weights. This process involves performing 1,000 bootstrap iterations to estimate the 95% confidence intervals, which test the significance of differences between edge weights. A plot of edge stability is generated to illustrate the 95% confidence intervals for edge weights following the bootstrapping process. All bootstrap procedures were applied to the first imputed dataset.

For testing the stability of centrality indices, the Correlation Stability (CS) coefficient is used, which measures the maximum proportion of cases that can be dropped such that the centrality estimate remains stable. Centrality indices are considered stable when the CS coefficient is greater than 0.25 and ideally above 0.50 [56]. A plot of centrality stability is also produced, showing how the centralities change when cases are progressively dropped from the bootstrap sample. Bootstrap edge difference tests are used to assess the statistical differences in edge weights.

Sensitivity analysis

Two sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) A sensitivity analysis was conducted to examine the robustness of the main findings. A more parsimonious network model was constructed by aggregating subscales into composite scores, in which the total scores of the PSQI, BHS, and SSS were treated as nodes. The same network estimation method and statistical parameters were applied. (2)To evaluate the robustness of our findings against potential non-normality in the scale scores, we performed a sensitivity analysis using Spearman correlations. The resulting Spearman-based network was compared to the primary Pearson-based network by computing Spearman correlations of the edge weights and of the node strength centralities.

Quality control

To enhance data reliability, all researchers involved in the study received uniform training to ensure qualification and adherence to standard protocols, covering aspects such as research background, design, objectives, scale content, and evaluation criteria. They also assisted the patients in completing the scales, collected the data, and reviewed the questionnaires. Each patient required approximately 30 min to complete all assessments, which were finalized on the day of enrollment.

Results

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of the sample

General and clinical characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. All the continuous variables were not normally distributed. A total of 518 patients participated in the study, with a median age of 15 (interquartile range: 13–16) years, comprising 393 (75.87%) females and 125(24.13%) males. The results indicate that female patients exhibited a higher risk of suicide (FDR-corrected P = 0.005) compared to males. They also reported more severe emotional abuse (FDR-corrected P = 0.005), emotional neglect (FDR-corrected P = 0.001), and physical neglect (FDR-corrected P = 0.005) and received lower objective support (FDR-corrected P = 0.016). In addition, female participants exhibited more severe depressive symptoms, higher levels of non-suicidal self-injury, and were more likely to seek medical help (FDR-corrected P < 0.05).

Network analysis

Figure 1 displays the network structure diagram of suicide risk and related socio-psychological factors among treatment-naïve adolescent depression patients. The strongest correlation with suicide risk is emotional abuse (edge weight = 0.232), followed by feelings about the future (edge weight = 0.147), loss of motivation (edge weight = 0.134), sleep duration (edge weight = 0.077), the severity of depression (edge weight = 0.075) and sleep disturbance(edge weight = 0.037). Among the social support factors, the partial association between objective support and suicide risk was negligible (edge weight =-0.009). Nonparametric bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) yielded a 95% confidence interval of [− 0.019, 0.000], which includes zero. This indicates the pathway is statistically indistinguishable from null and should not be interpreted as a reliable protective link.

Network accuracy and stability

The stability and accuracy of the network were assessed using non-parametric bootstrapping. Figure 4 illustrates the stability of the edges. The narrow gray areas represent the confidence interval (CI) range, with smaller CIs indicating more accurate edge estimates. Additionally, the bootstrapped difference tests showed that many comparisons between edge weights were statistically significant (Fig. 5). Figure 6 demonstrates the stability of centrality by showing changes in centrality as participants are removed from the sample. The small changes in centrality when participants are removed indicate that the centrality values in this study are stable. The estimated CS for strength is 0.672, suggesting that our network structure is sufficiently stable.

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis based on aggregated nodes confirmed the robustness of the original network structure. The results supported the core findings of the original network: emotional abuse remained the central node (Strength = 1.240), and its association with suicide risk remained stable (edge weight = 0.260).

The Pearson and Spearman - based networks showed very high congruence. The Spearman correlation between edge weights was 0.946 and the correlation between node strength centralities reached 0.976. All other centrality indices (expected influence, betweenness, closeness) also correlated above 0.90 between the two networks, demonstrating that the rank - order importance of nodes was essentially unchanged. The overall topology was highly similar: the Jaccard similarity for edge presence was 0.818, indicating that the majority of connections were replicated in both networks (Supplementary Material 2) .

Gender comparison

Figure 7 presents the network graphs constructed for male and female participants. The global strength difference test (P = 0.450) and the network structure difference test (P = 0.623) did not show significant differences between males and females, and after Benjamini - Hochberg FDR correction, none of the node - level strength differences reached significance. We conducted case - dropping bootstrap stability analyses for both the male and female networks. The CS coefficient for strength was 0.595 for the female network, indicating adequate stability, whereas the male network yielded a CS - strength of 0.000, meaning that the order of node centralities in the male network is entirely unstable. Notably, the strength of suicide risk in the male network was estimated at 0.000. This value should be interpreted as a product of LASSO regularization shrinkage (which reduces weak edges to zero to ensure sparse network structure) and the relatively smaller male subsample, rather than a substantive absence of connections between suicide risk and other nodes.

Discussion

Suicide risk in adolescent depression represents a critical clinical challenge, necessitating a nuanced understanding of its complex etiology. By employing network analysis in a unique, treatment-naïve adolescent sample, our study delineates the intricate interplay of psychosocial factors underpinning suicide risk. We identified emotional abuse as the most central node directly linked to suicide risk. In addition, hopelessness, depressive symptoms and sleep formed a cohesive risk architecture, offering novel insights for early, targeted intervention. The current network methods surpass traditional multivariate modeling in two key aspects. Firstly, by modeling interdependence rather than one-way prediction, it reveals how risk and protective factors maintain or disrupt the structure of suicide risk. Secondly, by prioritizing strength and expected influence, the model identifies structural leverage points that can guide precise clinical decision-making: for height centers or bridge nodes (such as sleep disorders, despair), cascading reductions may occur in a wider risk network, and monitoring the changes in these nodes over time can provide information for dynamic grading nursing adjustments. Therefore, the network not only ranks risk factors, but also transforms complex symptom interactions into a target selection framework for early intervention of adolescent depression.

Our study provides compelling evidence for a sex-differentiated clinical presentation in treatment-naïve adolescent depression. Female patients presented with a more severe risk profile, encompassing higher suicide risk and depression symptoms, greater exposure to emotional abuse and neglect. This aligns with broader epidemiological patterns wherein female adolescent patients often report more pronounced psychological symptoms and a higher burden of adverse childhood experiences [58–60]. When confronted with negative life events, females are more likely to attribute the causes to their own shortcomings, thereby experiencing more psychological pressure [59].

We investigated whether the network structure differed between male and female participants using a Network Comparison Test. Neither the global network structure nor any individual node centrality differed significantly between sexes after controlling for multiple testing. The very low strength of suicide risk in the male network is largely driven by LASSO regularization shrinkage and the smaller male sample size, rather than indicating a true lack of association. Overall, the gender-stratified results should be considered explorat ory, and future studies with larger and more balanced male samples are needed to confirm these patterns.

In the network structure of our study, emotional abuse emerges as the most influential node, closely linked to suicide risk. A study on first-episode depression patients in China found that childhood abuse is a potential factor for suicide attempts, but did not analyze the effects of different types of childhood abuse on suicidal behavior [61]. The specificity and severity of different forms of childhood abuse on suicidal behavior suggest that emotional abuse may have the most harmful impact on suicide risk [62]. In a prospective study on adolescents, a three-year follow-up revealed that emotional abuse prospectively increases suicidal ideation in adolescents and serves as a strong predictor of suicidal ideation [63]. Our study is consistent with these results. Emotional abuse often occurs in close relationships and is characterized by its covert and persistent nature. Victims may be subjected to long-term control, humiliation, and neglect, accumulating psychological trauma that gradually diminishes their confidence and hope in life. This can lead to feelings of inferiority, helplessness, and hopelessness, which in turn can result in thwarted belongingness, consistent with the IPTS [13]. Remaining in such a negative emotional state over time can increase the risk of suicide. In research on treatment-naïve adolescent depression patients, the confounding effects of previous antidepressant medication or psychotherapy interventions were eliminated. The results suggest that the significance of emotional abuse is not a product of treatment history, but rather a common characteristic of treatment-naïve adolescent depression patients. This emphasizes the necessity of assessing emotional abuse when first encountering these patients in clinical settings. Early screening for emotional abuse, along with early identification and intervention, may help improve depressive symptoms, increase perceived social support, and reduce suicide risk.

Previous research on the relationship between depression and suicide risk indicates that depression is the strongest risk factor for suicide [64, 65]. In our network analysis of treatment-naïve adolescent depression patients, we observed that emotional abuse exhibited the strongest association with suicide risk among the factors examined, with a connection strength greater than that between depressive symptoms and suicide risk, similar to the network structure analysis of patients with severe depression [66]. Contrary to previous studies on suicide and depression, the reason may be the unique characteristics of treatment-naïve adolescent sample.

Hopelessness is one of the most significant long-term risk factors for suicide among clinical populations [67] and is a common experience among patients with depression [68]. In patients with first-onset severe depression, there is a positive correlation between suicidal ideation and levels of hopelessness, though further analysis of the three factors of hopelessness was not conducted [69]. Hopelessness is closely related to suicidal tendencies in adolescents. As feelings of hopelessness increase, the likelihood of suicidal ideation also significantly rises during short-term acute treatment, emphasizing the importance of early recognition of hopelessness [70]. In our study, the network structure divides the concept of hopelessness into three factors, where feelings about the future and loss of motivation are related to suicide risk in treatment-naïve adolescent depression patients. The loss of hope for the future may indirectly affect suicide risk through negative feelings about the future and loss of motivation. Individuals with high levels of hopelessness tend to face life with a negative attitude, lack goals, are unlikely to seek help on their own, or may even refuse help, leading to a high incidence of psychological problems and increased suicide risk.

Our study found a certain correlation between sleep duration and suicide risk. Sleep disorders are common among adolescents and are associated with depression and various mental illnesses. Sleep disturbances are significantly related to suicidal ideation, planning, and attempts, making them an independent predictor of suicide risk in adolescents [71]. Insufficient sleep is an intervenable and non-stigmatized factor, highlighting its advantages as both a risk and protective factor. This underscores the importance of sleep duration as a crucial factor in assessing suicide risk. For adolescents with emotional disorders and poor sleep quality, it is important to assess their risk of suicide.

Although the estimated network showed a weak negative edge between objective support and suicide risk, bootstrap confidence intervals crossed zero and effect sizes were near null. Consistent with best practices in psychological network analysis, we refrain from labeling this pathway as protective. Larger, longitudinal samples and targeted inferential tests are needed to determine whether objective support meaningfully buffers suicide risk.

Conclusion

Our network structure reveals the interactions between suicide risk, depression symptoms, childhood abuse, sleep, hopelessness, and social support in treatment-naïve adolescent depression patients. Although causal relationships cannot be inferred, these associations suggest that emotional abuse, hopelessness, sleep duration, and depression symptoms may serve as important clinical indicators for suicide risk assessment.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our sample was drawn from a single local center and remains relatively small, which may constrain the generalizability of our findings to broader or more diverse populations. The notably lower number of male participants, while consistent with the higher female prevalence rates in adolescent depression, which, together with the non‑significant global NCT and the lack of surviving node‑level differences after FDR correction, limits the reliability and generalizability of the gender‑comparison results. Future studies should include larger male samples to permit more definitive subgroup analyses. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the relationship between emotional abuse and suicide risk, underscoring the need for longitudinal studies to elucidate temporal and directional pathways. Third, the current network model excluded other empirically relevant adolescent suicide risk factors, such as impulsivity, academic stress, and non-suicidal self-injury, potentially limiting its comprehensiveness and external validity. Collectively, these constraints highlight the importance of future research employing larger, multicenter, longitudinal designs with more balanced gender representation and expanded variable sets to validate, refine, and extend the current network findings.
