Isocyanoalkynes:
Leveraging a High-Energy Interstellar
Lead for Unique Multi-Bond-Forming Cascades

Huan Tian, Tore Brinck, Christopher Absil, Fraser F. Fleming

Abstract

The reactivity of
isocyanoalkynesalkynes bearing a terminal
isocyanideis an unexplored frontier. The absence of any isocyanoalkyne-based
reactions stems from their particularly challenging synthesis, a route
originally developed to determine the possible presence of isocyanoacetylene
in interstellar gas clouds. The difficult flash-vacuum, pyrolysis-condensation
synthesis (240 → −196 °C), the low yield (<10%),
and the rapid polymerization of simple isocyanoacetylenes likely dissuaded
attempts to investigate the chemistry of these highly energetic molecules.
Described here is a robust, scalable synthesis of substituted isocyanoalkynes
bearing aromatic, heterocyclic, and aliphatic substituents, and a
structural analysis complemented by computations that identify molecular
hot spots. These analyses have been particularly important in developing
several exploratory reactions that reveal the high reactivity of isocyanoalkynes
in unique, multi-bond-forming cascades. The synthesis, structural
analyses, and reactivity of isocyanoalkynes provides a robust foundation
for exploring, what augurs to be, a versatile new family of nitrogen-substituted
alkynes.

Introduction

Interstellar molecules provide unique
inspiration for new chemistry
as the frigid vacuum in space can stabilize structures inaccessible
under terrestrial conditions. Fullerene is a case in point. The serendipitous
synthesis of the cage-like, carbon allotrope led to the detection
of fullerene in the interstellar medium triggering a ream of important advances in fields ranging from electronics
to nanoscience.

The identification
of simple isocyanides in the interstellar medium
has sparked an analogous flurry of interest because these high-energy
molecules are postulated as origin-of-life precursors. Speculation over the presence of more energetic sp-hybridized
isocyanides in the interstellar medium led to the synthesis of isocyanoacetylene (HCC–NC, 1a) via a flash vacuum pyrolysis (FVP)-demetallation of the
chromium isocyanide complex 2 (A). The pioneering
synthesis afforded a few milligrams of isocyanoacetylene (1a) whose key spectral signatures unambiguously matched those emitted
from several interstellar gas clouds.

An absence of reactions with isocyanoacetylene
(1a), or any simple derivatives, reflects their challenging
synthesis
and the rapid polymerization of these unstable gases above −80
°C. However, the instability hints
at an untapped opportunity to harness the high reactivity embedded
in the two contiguous triple bonds in new bond-forming reactions.
The potential to discover new reactivity from isocyanoalkynes is high
because most isocyanide-based reactions employ either isocyanoalkanes
or isocyanoarenes (B, compare the frequencies of 3 and 4 with those of 5 and 1); only a few reactions employ isocyanoalkenes, while virtually nothing is known
about the experimental reactivity of isocyanoalkynes.

The important
advance presented here is a reproducible, scalable
synthesis of isocyanoalkynes which allows their unique reactivity
to be explored. The approach is predicated on a rather simple dehydrohalogenation
of bromoisocyanoalkenes (6, C), functionalities whose synthesis and reactions
have only recently been developed. Described
here is a robust synthesis of diverse isocyanoalkynes bearing aliphatic,
aromatic, and heterocyclic substituents, a combined experimental and
computational analysis of their structures, and exploratory forays
to probe their reactivity.

Results and Discussion

Access to
isocyanoalkynes emanated from the naïve idea of
dehydrohalogenating a suitable haloisocyanoalkene (C). As a class, haloisocyanoalkenes are themselves relatively rare:
the first reported congeners were two α-chloroisocyanoalkenes
isolated from soil bacteria whereas the
first syntheses were of β-bromoisocyanoacrylates for an isocyanide-based
approach to heterocycles. Only recently
have β-bromoisocyanoalkenes 6 suitable for dehydrohalogenation,
become readily accessible through a regioselective bromination-dehydration
of the corresponding vinylformamides 7 ().

The ‘simple’ bromoisocyanoalkene dehydrohalogenation
was initially probed with the phenyl-substituted congener 6a (6, R1 = Ph) as a prototype (). The selection of 6a was predicated on forming isocyanoalkyne 1d with extended delocalization between the two π-systems
to improve the stability, while the mass imparted by the phenyl substituent
was anticipated to afford a more easily manipulated non-volatile oil
or solid. Initial eliminations were promising but particularly challenging
because of the instability of the resulting phenylisocyanoalkyne 1d: concentrating or heating relatively clean samples from
the elimination resulted in rapid decomposition to a black tar. By comparing crude reaction mixtures, the solvent,
temperature, and base (LDA, KHMDS, Cs2CO3) were
optimized, which identified t-BuOK in THF at 0 °C
as ideal for cleanly affording 1d (74% yield based on
an NMR analysis of the crude reaction mixture with an internal standard).
However, isolating pure samples of 1d was frustrated
by a strong tendency of neat samples to polymerize. Eventually, a
robust workup and purification was developed using volatile ether-pentane
mixtures to rapidly access concentrated samples for purification.
Combining the optimized workup with chromatography on silanized “C-2
silica” was critical for minimizing
polymerization and preventing irreversible adsorption onto the silica
matrix. Using the optimized workup and chromatography protocol allowed
a reliable synthesis of an array of isocyanoalkynes 1e-p, all of which proved to be significantly more stable
than 1d (). Unwittingly, 1d proved to be anything but prototypical.

Initially, isocyanoalkynes were prepared with modest electronic
changes in the aromatic ring; synthesizing arylisocyanoalkynes with
electron-donating (1e-1h) or neutral substituents
(1i-1j) was uneventful. The naphthyl isocyanoalkyne 1j, prepared on a 1.3 mmol scale in 49% yield, was a particularly
stable crystalline solid. Similarly, crystalline isocyanoalkynes 1g and 1k showed only trace degradation after
storage at −20 °C for several months. Crystalline isocyanoalkynes
and those with electron-rich substituents, seem to be more stable
than those with more electron-deficient substituents, such as 1l and 1m, that were prone to polymerize at room
temperature. Heterocyclic substituents at the sp carbon were tolerated
(1m-1n), while the aliphatic sp3-hybridized substituents with quaternary or tertiary centers adjacent
to the acetylenic carbon were among the more stable congeners (1o-1p, respectively).

Crystallographic
analysis of 1j provided insight into
the isocyanoalkyne structure (A). The carbon–nitrogen triple bond of the isocyanoalkyne 1j is slightly elongated (1.17 Å) compared with the average
isocyanide bond length (1.14 Å) while the C–NC single
bond is shortened compared to the analogous bond in isocyanoalkanes;
,
 the contraction of the C–NC
bond in moving from sp3 to sp2 to sp hybridization,
correlates with a progressive shortening caused by the increasing
p character of the alkyl carbon. Comparing
the structural characteristics of the isocyanoalkyne 1j with the isoelectronic alkynenitrile 8a is particularly
revealing because the bond lengths are quite similar despite the two
functionalities having very different spectral data.

Insight into the interplay of the isocyanide and
alkyne π-systems
was gleaned by comparing the relative 13C NMR shift differences
of the acetylenic carbons of isocyanoalkyne 1j with those
of the alkynenitriles 8 (see values listed in A). The 13C NMR shift difference between the β and α alkyne carbons,
Δδ (β – α), for 1j is
−2.4 with the α-carbon being furthest downfield (55.8
ppm vs 53.4 ppm for the α- and β-carbons, respectively).
Typically, electron-withdrawing substituents on alkynes induce downfield
shifts for the β-carbon as observed for the acetylenic nitrile 8b
 for which Δδ =
+14.5 (81.8 and 67.3 ppm for the β- and α-carbons, respectively);
the relative 13C NMR chemical shift values of the acetylenic
carbons in isocyanoalkynes are closer to electron-rich ynamides and ynamines than
acetylenic nitriles. The upfield 13C NMR chemical shifts
for the alkyne signals in 1j suggests that the isocyanide
nitrogen has shifted some electron density from the terminal isocyanide
carbon into the alkyne π* orbital relative to aliphatic isocyanides,
though overall the alkyne remains strongly polarized by the powerful
electron-withdrawing effect of the isocyanide.

The movement of electron density from the isocyanide
nitrogen into
the alkyne π* correlates with the increasing IR frequencies
within the series of sp, sp2, and sp3-hybridized
isocyanides (B). The low IR stretching frequency for 1d is typical
of the isocyanoalkynes 1 relative to isocyanoethylene
(5a) and isocyanobutane (3a, B). The IR frequency trends
are likewise consistent with some weakening of the triple bond caused
by delocalization of the nitrogen lone pair into the acetylenic π*
orbital.

Computational analyses
complement the electronic profile provided
by crystallographic and spectral data. After scaling by 0.9642 to
compensate for anharmonic effects, the B3LYP/6-31+G­(d,p) IR frequencies
of the nitrogen–carbon triple bond of the isocyanides are in
very good agreement with the experimental frequencies, showing computed
NC bond lengths decreasing with increasing IR frequency [i.e.,
in the order 1d (1.184 Å) > 5a (1.180
Å) > 3a (1.174 Å)].

computed surface electrostatic potentials (V

(r)) of the isocyanoalkyne 1d with 3-phenylpropiolonitrile (8c) indicates
that the isocyano group has an only slightly weaker electron-withdrawing
capacity than the nitrile (), consistent with the reported Hammett σp values of 0.48 and 0.56 for the isocyanide and nitrile, respectively. The local V

S
(r) minima (V
S,min) over the carbon–carbon triple bonds of 1d and 8c are 0.0 and −2.1 kcal/mol, respectively; compared
to methylphenylacetylene (9a), which contains a weakly

S,min is −24.7
kcal/mol which reflects the strong influence of the electron-withdrawing
isocyanide and nitrile groups for the respective alkynes, 1d and 8c. Over the methyl group of methylphenylacetylene
(9a), V

(r) is positive whereas at the isocyano and cyano lone
pairs of 1d and 8c they are strongly negative;
the V

S,min values are
−48.0 and −55.7 kcal/mol, respectively. For comparison,
the corresponding V

consistent with an electron donation from the butyl group to the isocyano

effect on the isocyano group in phenylisocyanoalkyne (1d). The observed variations in V

S
(r) are consistent with the reported Hammett σp values for the substituents, −CN (0.56), -NC (0.48),
−CCH (0.22), CH3 (−0.17) and -C4H9 (−0.19).

The local electron attachment energy (E

S
(r)), an
electrophilicity index derived from unoccupied orbitals with negative
energies, was calculated to provide insight into the reactive sites
of isocyanoalkynes (). The E

S
(r) for phenylisocyanoalkyne (1d) shows the terminal
isocyanide carbon to be particularly electrophilic with an E

S,min of −2.06 eV, while
the acetylenic β-carbon is less activated with an E

S,min of −1.02 eV (); the effect is even more
pronounced in the E

S
(r) map of the p-trifluoromethylphenylisocyanoalkyne 1l, potentially explaining the instability of 1l as the result of nucleophilic attack on the isocyanide carbon. Comparing
the E

S
(r) maps of 1d and 1l with that of 3-phenylpropiolonitrile
(8c) shows that nitrile 8c has a lower E

S,min (−1.30 eV) at
the β-carbon, which correlates well with the facile conjugate
addition of nucleophiles to the β-carbon of alkynenitriles.

A comparison of the total enthalpy (CBS-QB3 level)
of the isocyanoalkyne 1d and the acetylenic nitrile 8c indicates that
the isocyanide is 27.4 kcal/mol less stable than the corresponding
nitrile. In contrast, the enthalpy difference between methylisocyanide
and acetonitrile is 24.3 kcal/mol, consistent with the phenylalkyne
destabilizing the isocyanide compared to the corresponding nitrile,
by around 3 kcal/mol. The high relative enthalpy of the isocyanoalkynes
is consistent with the observed instability of some substrates and
provides a strong thermodynamic driving force in synthetic reactions
(vide infra).

The combined electron-withdrawing effects of the
phenyl and isocyanide
groups increases the electrophilicity of the alkyne, activating the
β-carbon for attack by nucleophiles, but less so than for alkynenitriles.
The electron-rich alkynylimidate 10a is on the other
extreme with a nucleophilic triple bond as indicated by the values
of their associated minima in the surface average local ionization
energy (I

S,min), which
are 9.07, 10.26, and 10.57 eV, respectively.

Preliminary forays to probe the reactivity of isocyanoalkynes
initially
evaluated their reactions with nucleophiles for which the analogous
reactions with alkynenitriles afford conjugate addition products (). Exposing isocyanoalkyne 1j to imidazole and
Et3N.HCl in methanol did not afford a conjugate adduct
but efficiently provided the doubly nitrogen-substituted alkene 13a (a). Presumably the initial acid-promoted isocyanide insertion into
methanol to afford the rare alkynylimidate 11a is followed
by protonation of the electron-rich alkyne to afford 12a that was intercepted by imidazole to afford 13a (11a is anticipated to have the same electronic profile as 10a, ); consistent with this mechanistic proposal was the isolation of 11a in 46% yield when the reaction was performed in the absence
of imidazole. Switching the nucleophile to p-bromothiophenol
generated an analogous double addition product 15a whose
structure was secured by crystallographic analysis (B). Formation of 15a is consistent with an analogous acid-promoted insertion of isocyanoalkyne 1j into the thiol to afford 14a whose protonation
to form a keteniminium is followed by addition of a second equivalent
of thiol (cf. 11a → 12a → 13a) to afford 15a.

The reactions of isocyanoalkynes
with nucleophiles under basic
conditions differ significantly from those of the corresponding alkynenitriles. With sodium methoxide in methanol, the electrophilic
isocyanoalkyne 1j incorporated three equivalents of MeOH
to afford the acetal 18a (A). The reaction is a rare example of two
alkyoxy additions to an isocyanide. Presumably
methoxide addition to the terminal isocyanide is followed by protonation
to afford 11a
 that added
a second equivalent of methoxide to afford 16a; subsequent
protonation followed by methoxide addition to the ketenimine functionality
of 17a would, after protonation, afford 18a.

Under weakly basic conditions with the isocyanoalkyne 1k, Ph3P, malononitrile, and Et3N, (B) a clean, deep-seated
rearrangement occurred to afford the structurally reorganized ylide 21a whose constitution was secured by X-ray crystallography
(B). Attachment
of the phosphonium salt at the benzylic position in 21a suggests an initial conjugate addition of Ph3P to the
β-acetylenic carbon of 1k consistent with the computed
electrophilicity of the alkyne. Subsequent protonation of the intermediate
vinyl anion by malononitrile would afford 19a and malononitrile
anion. The resulting vinylphosphonium
salt 19a would polarize the alkene, favoring addition
of the malononitrile anion at the isocyanide-bearing carbon to then
trigger an ejection of cyanide (19a → 20a); similar addition-eliminations of vinylphosphoniums are known, although this is the first report of cyanide
ejection by cleavage of an isocyanide. Subsequent deprotonation of 20a efficiently generated
the ylid 21a (B), though purification by silica gel chromatography
caused significant mass loss through adsorption as is common with
ylids.

Conclusions

A
diverse array of isocyanoalkynes have been synthesized via a
robust, base-induced dehydrohalogenation of bromoisocyanoalkenes.
The strategy addresses the prior difficulty of synthesizing isocyanoalkynes,
allowing the first mmol synthesis of diversely substituted isocyanides
and investigation of the reactivity profile for this essentially new
functionality. Initial reactions of isocyanoalkynes reveal a propensity
for complex bond-forming cascades with nucleophiles in acidic or basic
media, a reactivity that is distinctly different from most alkynes
with electron-withdrawing substituents. Access to isocyanoalkynes
adds an unexplored functionality to the arsenal of nitrogen-substituted
alkynes whose unique multi-bond-forming cascades are primed for development.
