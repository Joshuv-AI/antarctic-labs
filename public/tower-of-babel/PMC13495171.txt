Circulating DNA reveals nucleosome occupancy patterns that are associated with nucleosome-DNA affinity and are affected in cancer
Authors: Marianne Richaud, Ekaterina Pisareva, Paul Burgat, Alain R Thierry, Jacques Colinge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe study of cell-free circulating DNA (cirDNA) fragments (fragmentomics) from liquid biopsies has received increasing attention to detect biomarkers. CirDNA found in blood plasma originates from cells in diverse tissues with a predominance for hematopoietic cells. Background

Cell-free circulating DNA (cirDNA) can be passively (necrosis, apoptosis, phagocytosis) or actively released from a multitude of tissues and cell types, but it mainly originates from hematopoietic cells [1–3]. Therefore, it may convey information on the whole organism state and hence constitutes a rich source of biomarkers. Landmark applications include non-invasive prenatal genetic tests, based on fetal cirDNA present in the mother’s blood [4], and cancer mutation detection in cirDNA released by tumors [5]. Moreover, upon release in the cytoplasm and in the blood circulation, DNA is actively degraded by different enzymes [6]. As a consequence, the cirDNA fragments observed in blood are strongly enriched in DNA positions that were protected against these enzymes by DNA-binding proteins, such as histones or transcription factors (TFs) [7, 8]. Sequencing of cirDNA fragments and alignment against the reference human genome determines a coverage at the base pair resolution, which hence reveals nucleosome and TF occupancy loci [3, 9]. Moreover, recent studies demonstrated that cirDNA methylation status brings additional information and allows deconvoluting the cells and tissues of origin due to tissue- or cell type-specific methylation patterns [10, 11].

In this general context, fragmentomics is the analysis of cirDNA fragment characteristics, such as their size or the nucleotide sequences at their extremities (end motifs). The cirDNA fragment size distribution is highly reproducible in healthy individuals, whereas it is altered in some diseases thus providing a new biomarker type. For instance, in cancer, the size of the detected cirDNA fragments is typically shorter [7, 12], mostly due to the cancer-related enzymatic profile deregulation [13], and an overall increase in cirDNA release, rather than direct cancer cell contributions [14]. End motif frequencies are also altered in cancer, reflecting a change in enzymatic activities, and they may provide additional biomarkers [13].

This study brings new insights into the fundamental properties of cirDNA fragments. By identifying ~ 5 million of well-positioned nucleosomes (WPNs), we found a relationship between genome coverage, fragment protection against enzyme degradation, and evolution of the genome sequence at WPN loci. Moreover, the genome coverage by cirDNA fragments at WPNs was affected by cancer. This finding could be exploited to develop a highly specific and sensitive cancer detection algorithm, including at early stages. We could also show that these new properties of cirDNA fragments related to nucleosome occupancy were more general. They remained valid in a broader selection of nucleosome positions. Our new cancer detection algorithm performance relation with cirDNA sequencing depth is discussed as well. Lastly, we investigated cirDNA genome coverage and its cancer-related changes globally as well as locally at transcription factor binding sites (TFBS).

Methods

CirDNA sequence data and cohorts

We downloaded cirDNA fragment sequence data from FinaleDB [15] as BED files representing the coordinates of cirDNA fragment alignment against the human genome assembly hg38. We used the data released by Cristiano et al. [16], Jiang et al. [17], and Sun et al. [18]. Cohort sizes are reported in Additional File 1: Table S1 and details regarding samples are available from the original publications. We retained only fragments with alignment quality score > 30, and excluded non-chromosome genome assemblies (contigs).

Software

For this study, a number of Python, R, and Perl scripts as well as C and C + + codes were written [19]. Accordingly, here, we only describe the computation principles and not their implementation. Readers are invited to refer to software for such details. Note that the execution of some programs required significant computer memory and power and disk space. This cannot be done at the project-scale on a personal computer.

Genome cirDNA coverage, fragment U/D ends

As previously described [3], we computed the number of cirDNA fragments that overlap with a given genome position by considering fragments between 120 and 180 bp in length. Such fragments represented the majority of fragments. We used centromere positions listed in Additional File 1: Table S2. We computed the normalized coverage by scaling the coverage between 0 and 1 in a 601 bp-wide sliding window. We obtained the smoothed coverage (actual or normalized values) by applying the Savitzky-Golay filter in 201 bp windows using 4th degree polynomials.

We normalized cirDNA fragment U and D ends as described above and smoothed them by applying the Savitzky-Golay filter in 401 bp windows using 4th degree polynomials. For less smoothing, we applied the Savitzky-Golay filter in 13 bp windows using 4th degree polynomials.

We estimated the spectral density of U/D end positions using the Welsh method [20].

WPS computations and putative well-positioned nucleosome positions

We implemented de novo fast C + + code for WPS computation following the definition by Snyder et al. [3]. In particular, only fragments with lengths between 120 and 180 bp were used. We smoothed WPS values by applying the Savitzky-Golay filter in 101 bp windows using 4th degree polynomials.

We identified putative WPNs (before filtering based on the diameter) as local maxima of the smoothed WPS values. We further imposed normalized cirDNA coverage (see above) to be > 0.15 at such positions and WPS maxima to be at least at 40 bp from the closest, distinct WPS maximum. Lastly, we required a local maximum of the smoothed fragment U end curve to be found between 25 and 120 bp upstream. Similarly, we required a local maximum of the smoothed fragment D end curve between 25 and 120 bp downstream. Bona fide WPS peaks passing these filters provide the genomic coordinates of WPNs, their cirDNA coverage is defined by the smoothed cirDNA coverage curve at these coordinates. WPN widths are defined as the distances between smoothed cirDNA coverage local minima before and after the WPS peaks.

Comparison of WPN positions with cell line-derived nucleosome maps

A map of 21,435,466 stable nucleosome positions of 100 bp each was retrieved from NucPosDB [21], which was generated from seven lymphoblastoid cell lines [22] by MNase-seq. We compared the proportion of our WPN positions included in any of these 100 bp windows with inclusions of an equivalent number of random WPN positions (100 repetitions). A second dataset derived from CD4 + T cells [23], also by MNase-seq, provided nucleosome-bound 25 bp DNA sequences aligned against the genome. Comparison with this broader dataset was achieved by counting how many such 25 bp sequences were included in a WPN (with its estimated width) on average over the whole atlas. Here as well, a null distribution was obtained by generating 100 times random WPN positions keeping the original widths. We did not compute any actual P-value given the very strong separation of the true statistics compared to the randomized ones.

Genome annotation intersection

We downloaded genomic annotations from Vu et al. [24]. For the top and bottom 20% occupancy nucleosomes, using their respective widths, we computed the number of nucleotides that overlapped with each annotation zone. We divided this by the total length for each zone and expressed this ratio as a percentage.

CirDNA fragment protection bias

Given a set of nucleosome positions, we first generated a matching set of randomized positions by adding to each original position a random number between − 500,000 and 500,000 (uniform distribution), while avoiding to hit the centromere or to go beyond the chromosome ends. Then, we collected all cirDNA fragments in the chosen cohort, e.g., Cristiano healthy individuals, and identified those that overlapped with the nucleosome positions or their randomized counterparts. Each position (original or random) was associated with a window of ± 100 bp, and we selected fragments completely included in a window (separately for original and random positions) as well as fragments that were not completely included, but with a non-included part ≤ 20 bp (tolerance).

To determine the bias of the original versus randomized selection, we computed the relative frequencies of each fragment length in the two selections separately, and then computed the difference between the original relative frequency and the randomized relative frequency.

Codons usage bias

We downloaded the list of gene coding sequence positions in the human genome assembly hg38 from the Ensembl database [25] (Homo_sapiens.GRCh38.109.gtf). We filtered the coding sequence positions that intersected the top and bottom 20% occupancy nucleosomes. In both cases, we retrieved the nucleotide sequences, shifted the sequence starts to ensure that they started on an existing ORF, and split them into 3-bp triplets that corresponded to codons. We determined the relative frequencies of all codons for low- and high-occupancy nucleosome-covered gene sequence parts and this allowed us to compute log-odds (base 2 logarithm).

Nucleosome occupancy and normalization in individual samples

We computed and smoothed the genome coverage by cirDNA fragments in individual samples as described above. We obtained the nucleosome occupancy at each WPN location by reading the smoothed coverage value of the individual sample.

We ignored chrY, and we normalized nucleosome occupancy at all the other chromosomes as follows. For each individual, we computed the total of the nucleosome occupancy values at all chromosomes, including the chrX PAR1 and PAR2 regions but not the non-pseudoautosomal part of chrX. Then, we divided the nucleosome occupancy values at these positions by the total and multiplied by the median of all total values. Likewise, we normalized chrX without the PAR1 and PAR2 regions by the individual corresponding total values of nucleosome occupancy and multiplied by the median of these totals.

Model construction, and AUC, sensitivity and specificity computations

Models built after PCA 2D projection. We computed SVM models using the R library e1071. We computed the ROC curves and extracted the AUC values using the pROC package in R [27]. To report balanced specificity (Sp) and sensitivity (Se) values, we determined for each ROC curve the point at the maximum distance from the diagonal. Elementary Euclidean distance computation shows that the distance between a ROC curve point and the diagonal is given by . For a given CV, i.e., a ROC curve, we selected the farthest point that gave Se and Sp. To obtain 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), we applied a bootstrap [28] to the ROC curve points (R package boot, 100 re-samplings, balanced simulation, and CI from basic bootstrap). The pROC package computed directly the AUC 95% CI.

Summation of successive WPN occupancies

For the Jiang dataset, we explored improving the signal-to-noise ratio by summing occupancy values at successive WPN positions by groups of 20, 40, 60, or 80. These groups were restricted to not cross chromosomes. We did not use chromosome X to avoid managing potential intersection with PARs.

Nucleosome occupancy at TSS

We downloaded the annotations of TSS positions from refTSS [29]. We selected nucleosomes that intersected at least one TSS and determined their occupancy by summing over all Cristiano healthy samples at the center of each nucleosome. We obtained RNA-Seq data of healthy hematopoietic cells from the GEO dataset GSE74246. We converted counts to log2 TPM, and computed the mean nucleosome occupancy at all the TSS associated with each gene.

Intersection with ATAC-seq peaks

ATAC-seq peaks were retrieved from ChIP-Atlas we imposed a minimum significance of 50. We selected peaks from PBMC datasets. A WPN position with its estimated cirDNA peak width was considered as overlapping an ATAC-seq peak provided it included at least half the ATAC-seq peak.

Results

Datasets and fundamental cirDNA fragment size patterns

The cirDNA fragment size distribution commonly observed in healthy individuals is described in Fig. 1A. It displays a marked mode at 167–168 bp, which is the size of a nucleosome (145–147 bp) flanked by linker DNA protected by histone H1 (~ 20 bp), i.e., a chromatosome. The distance between the small subpeaks, which are well visible on the left of the main peak, is typically 10.3 bp. This is the distance between two minor grooves along DNA wrapped around nucleosomes. This well-described phenomenon results from an easier access of nucleases to nick DNA at these locations.

To identify novel properties of cirDNA fragments, we retrieved two cohorts of healthy individuals from FinaleDB [15] that were originally released by Cristiano, et al. [16]. and Jiang, et al. [17]. Sun, et al. [18]. data for liver transplant patients were used for validation of some observations as a noncancer dataset. We named these cohorts after their first authors. They included 245, 32, and 13 individuals, respectively. As expected, the cirDNA fragment size distribution profiles in the two healthy cohorts were similar to what is described in Fig. 1A (Additional File 1: Fig. S1). By modeling the overall shape of the cirDNA fragment size distribution profiles, we could subtract this shape to reveal the presence of subpeaks of increased fragmentation also after 168 bp (Additional File 1: Fig. S2), indicative of continued DNA protection against nucleases by binding proteins. In the rest of the study, we used the largest dataset, i.e., Cristiano 245 patient healthy cohort, for discovery and Jiang and Sun data for occasional external validation.

We then computed the genome coverage by Cristiano healthy individual cirDNA fragments. Figure 1B shows the well-defined region of chromosome 12 (chr12) discussed in Ref [18]. where nucleosomes adopted stable positions. As already reported [18], the upstream and downstream locations of cirDNA fragment ends (U ends and D ends, respectively) tended to occur preferentially at linker DNA positions, in agreement with the distribution mode at ~ 167 bp (Fig. 1A). We performed spectral analysis of U and D end position occurrences in a large 65,636 bp window of chr12 that included the portion presented in Fig. 1B. We found a peak frequency at 1/10.3 [1/bp] (Fig. 1C), suggesting the existence of a finer structure. Indeed, by selecting milder smoothing parameters, we revealed local, smaller peaks separated by 10.3 bp (Fig. 1D) that were reminiscent of the subpeaks of the overall distribution structure.

A notion of well-positioned nucleosomes and their occupancy

Different techniques have been proposed to putatively locate nucleosomes from cirDNA data. We based our approach on the window protection score (WPS) [3]. For a given position p, the WPS is the ratio between the number of cirDNA fragments that cover an 80-bp window centered at p and the number of fragments that only intersect the window. As nucleosomes offer protection against nucleases, WPS peaks should reveal the position of nucleosomes. However, in genome regions where cirDNA does not form well-separated peaks, we found that WPS alone might lead to ambiguous localization. Therefore, to locate a subset of clear nucleosome positions [30], we developed an algorithm in which WPS peaks above a minimum height were imposed additional conditions. We required sufficient separation between successive WPS peaks as well as the existence of local U and D end maxima up- and down-stream of a WPS peak (Fig. 2A, Additional File 1: Fig. S3, and Methods).

Applying this algorithm to Cristiano healthy total cirDNA coverage, we found ~ 20 M WPS peaks that were reduced to 9,358,411 bona fide WPS peaks after filtering. We estimated the corresponding cirDNA coverage peak widths (Fig. 2A), which adopted a trimodal distribution (Fig. 2C). The main mode was close to 200 bp, which is a common nucleosome repeat length (NRL) in arrays of well-positioned nucleosomes (Fig. 1B). Larger widths (> 300 bp) were compatible with multi-nucleosomes, or overlapping fuzzy nucleosomes, while smaller structures (< 147 bp) might be the product of TF-DNA complexes or other DNA-associated proteins. As our main objective was to identify a large set of WPN locations, we only retained positions with widths from 147 to 300 bp. This resulted in a set of 4,971,768 WPN positions. We omitted chrY because we wanted sex-independent results. The number of WPN positions was proportional to the chromosome size (Figs. 2C and S4A).

Although cirDNA fragment genome coverage is commonly represented using normalized values between 0 and 1 (Figs. 1B and 2A), actual coverage, i.e., the number of cirDNA fragments above a given genomic position, can vary substantially. At WPN positions, this coverage defined nucleosome occupancy [31], which varied between 200 and 1,200 (total of 245 individuals). This variation was similar across all chromosomes (Fig. 2D and Additional File 1: Fig. S4B). To assess the global coherence of our set of WPN positions and their occupancy, we exploited generic annotations of genome features that were obtained by compiling epigenetic data for > 100 cell types [24]. We hypothesized that this aggregative approach was suitable for cirDNA fragments that originate from different cell types. We defined the top 20% and bottom 20% cirDNA-covered WPN positions as high-occupancy and low-occupancy nucleosomes, respectively. We compared the proportions of their intersection with each zone of genome annotation (Fig. 2E and Additional File 1: Fig. S5), which resulted in significant and expected biases. For instance, high-occupancy nucleosomes were strongly enriched at heterochromatin, and mostly absent at flanking transcription starting sites (TSS) and promoters.

Next, we sought to relate WPN occupancy with cirDNA fragment properties. First, we defined a criterion to identify cirDNA fragments covered by a WPN, i.e., fragments that fully overlapped with a WPN or with a tolerance of 20 bp (Fig. 2F). By comparing the lengths of cfDNA fragments covered by high- and low-occupancy WPNs versus an identical number of random positions in the genome, we discovered that fragments protected by high-occupancy WPNs tended to be longer that those at low-occupancy WPNs (Fig. 2G). We confirmed this observation in the Jiang healthy and Sun liver transplant cohorts using the WPN positions obtained from Cristiano healthy individuals (Additional File 1: Fig. S6). As cirDNA fragments result from enzymatic attacks that are limited by the protection offered by histone binding, this finding suggested that high-occupancy WPNs correspond to DNA positions with increased affinity for histones or reduced enzymatic efficiency. These hypotheses imply local adaptation of the DNA sequence to adjust histone affinity or to change the frequency of motifs recognized by nucleases, and also to maintain the DNA ability to encode diverse features, such as genes. Therefore, we compared the frequencies of codon usage at high- versus low-occupancy WPNs that intersected known human gene exons (Fig. 2H). We found significant differences in the log-odds of codon frequencies at high- versus low-occupancy WPNs (Fig. 2I). This observation was confirmed by making a less marked high-/low-occupancy WPN selection as the top/bottom 40% cirDNA coverage (instead of 20%). We coherently found similar though reduced codon usage bias (Additional File 1: Fig. S7).

Lastly, we challenged the generality of our observations by considering alternative sets of WPN positions. First, we considered the broader selection of 9,358,411 WPS peaks output by our algorithm before restriction to mononucleosome-compatible widths. Observations of codon usage bias, fragment length bias, and coverage of genome feature annotations were all reproduced almost identically (Additional File 1: Fig. S8). Second, we considered alternative WPN sets by applying our algorithm to additional cirDNA datasets or by retrieving publicly available nucleosome position datasets. Our algorithm was applied to Jiang 32 healthy individuals and a few large cancer cohorts: Cristiano breast cancer (BC) 54 patients [16], and two shallow sequencing (0.1x coverage) cohorts (950 BC and 558 prostate cancer) [32]. The numbers of detected WPN positions are reported in Additional File 1: Fig. S9A. They varied modestly in comparison with what we obtained from Cristiano healthy (< 10%), but for Jiang healthy-derived WPNs (~ 20%). This variation was not correlated with the dataset sizes or total genome coverage. CirDNA peak widths displayed similar trimodal distributions with Jiang data leading to a more pronounced mode at ~ 120 bps (Additional File 1: Fig. S9B). The distances between the closest peaks from two distinct atlases were strongly concentrated around zero thus indicating high reproducibility of WPN locations (Additional File 1: Fig. S9C). Comparisons with large-scale nucleosome maps from dedicated studies in specific cell types showed highly significant inclusion of our positions (Additional File 1: Fig. S9D), both in the case of recurrently occupied nucleosome positions [22] and general nucleosome positions [23]. Moreover, checking codon usage bias and genome feature coverage comparing high- and low-occupancy WPNs from Jiang healthy atlas lead to results comparable with what we observed from Cristiano healthy WPNs (Additional File 1: Fig. S10).

Well-positioned nucleosome occupancy and cancer

It is well-known that cell epigenomes are substantially rearranged in cancer [33]. Moreover, the global impact of cancer on the amount of cirDNA released in the circulation [14] could reflect cellular changes that might also modify the epigenomes of non-malignant cell populations. Therefore, we asked whether nucleosome occupancy at WPN positions could be used to probe in individual patients. By reading individual cirDNA genome coverage at cohort-defined WPNs (Fig. 3A), we could associate each individual with roughly 5 million quantitative features that could be submitted to machine learning (ML) for cancer detection. Compared with occupancy values at the cohort level, occupancy for a given individual varied for obvious biological and technical reasons (Fig. 3B). As individual samples were not sequenced at constant depth, and the cohort included women and men, we designed a normalization scheme before modeling WPN occupancy at the individual scale. We hypothesized that correct normalization combined with statistical testing at each WPN position should lead to no significant differences. Specifically, to assess the sex bias, comparison of healthy women and healthy men should find no differences. By applying elementary normalization on the total occupancy over WPN positions, we found many differential occupancies, mostly on chrX as expected (Fig. 3C). However, the PAR1 and PAR2 pseudo-autosomal regions yielded no difference, like in autosomes. Normalizing chrX separately corrected most of the problem, but introduced differences in PAR1 and PAR2. Finally, we opted for a normalization based on the total occupancy in autosomes, including the chrX PAR1/2 regions, and separately, chrX without PAR1/2. This led to controlled false discovery rates (FDR) (Fig. 3C).

Next, we explored nucleosome occupancy in cancer using the seven cancer cohorts (bile duct, breast, colorectal, gastric, lung, ovarian, and pancreatic cancers) released by Cristiano et al. [16]. Before comparing the Cristiano healthy and cancer cohorts, we used principal component analysis (PCA) to exclude potential strong outliers from each sample type. In each cohort, we found 1 or 2 outliers that were always the least cirDNA-covered samples (Additional File 1: Fig. S11). PCA projection of all the remaining samples resulted in very clear separation between healthy and cancer samples (Fig. 3D). Accordingly, we designed an algorithm that combined joint PCA-based dimension reduction of known reference healthy and cancer samples along with new samples to classify, followed by the construction of a support vector machine (SVM) model from reference data only to perform the classification (Additional File 1: Fig. S12 and Methods). Building a fixed projection and a unique SVM model was not possible due to the extremely high dimensionality of the data. Whatever the size of the reference datasets, it is necessary to embed new samples to classify in a common 2D-space with the reference samples before the SVM is trained on the reference data only. Due to the large differences in cohort sizes (244 healthy samples and 25 to 53 samples depending on the cancer type), and to avoid unbalanced learning, we repeated the 10-fold selection of random samples based on the smallest cohort (cancer) 10 times. Each time, a 2D PCA projection was computed and within this projection, 10-fold cross-validation (CV) was repeated 15 times to train/test the SVM models (Additional File 1: Fig. S12). We obtained highly sensitive and selective models (Fig. 3E). Figure 3F shows an example of PCA 2D projection with training and test data and Fig. 3G summarizes the performance for cancer detection in individual samples for the seven cancer types separately. A simple linear model or linear discriminant analysis provided a similar performance, although slightly inferior (data not shown), indicating that other ML models than SVM could be considered.

Since the amount of cirDNA coverage at a given genome position in a particular cancer patient might be changed by copy number variation (CNV), we assessed CNV potential contribution to cancer detection performance. As previously reported, the contribution of cancer cell DNA to cirDNA is generally low and highly variable between tumors [34]. Cristiano et al. provided an experimental estimate of tumoral content in cirDNA by means of targeted mutation deep sequencing for a large number of their cancer samples. As can be seen from Additional File 1: Fig. S13A, the majority of the patients harbored less than a percent of cirDNA of cancer cell origin. If one considers that CNV is gain/loss of one chromosome section, then variation of cirDNA coverage of the same order can be expected, i.e., very little. In Additional File 1: Fig. S13B, we reproduced Fig. 3D projection by color-coding cancer samples according to the tumoral DNA rate. This did not reveal any obvious relation with the distances to healthy samples. To quantify this notion, we regressed distances between healthy and cancer samples against the tumoral rate, which only explained 1.8% of the variance (Additional File 1: Fig. S13C) in line with our estimation of potential impact of CNV on cirDNA coverage.

The 2D projection in Fig. 3D suggested that the separation of distinct tumor types was a more difficult ML task than cancer detection. Indeed, by keeping the first 20 dimensions in the PCA projection, we evaluated random forest (RF) models using 5-fold CV repeated 100 times. The average of the confusion matrices showed that some cancer types could be well separated (bile duct, breast, colorectal, gastric, pancreatic), but others were almost impossible to distinguish (ovarian, lung) (Fig. 3H and G). Overlap between training and test sets occurred even in the case of easily distinguishable tumor types, for instance colorectal and gastric cancer (Fig. 3I, and receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curves in Fig. 3J compared with Fig. 3E).

Next, to determine whether our approach could detect early stage tumors versus healthy controls, we pooled cancer samples (all seven cancer types) in function of their stage (I to IV). This task was again accomplished with very high sensitivity and selectivity by our algorithm (Fig. 3G). Unfortunately, our approach could not separate tumors in function of their stage (Additional File 1: Fig. S14).

Sufficient genome coverage is necessary to be able to extract WPN occupancy data from individual samples as represented in Fig. 3A and B. To explore the limitations of our approach in that regards, we exploited the 90 liver cancer samples that were included in Jiang data, and compared them to their 32 healthy controls. Jiang data were 1.7 times less deep than Cristiano (Additional File 1: Fig. S15A). Interestingly, our detection algorithm performed barely better than random selection on these data with AUC = 0.63, 95%CI = [0.59; 0.67] (Additional File 1: Fig. S15B). Nevertheless, it was possible to restore significant performance through the summation of successive WPN occupancy values (Additional File 1: Fig. S15B), e.g., AUC = 0.95, 95%CI [0.94; 0.96] for sums at 60 successive WPNs. Indeed, summation is a classical procedure to improve signal-to-noise ratios. These results confirmed that less performance is to be expected in lower covered genomes, and hence our approach is not suitable for shallow sequencing. They also indicated that despite the reduced resolution induced by successive WPN occupancy values summation, cancer-specific biases were preserved to a certain extent. This global aspect of cirDNA coverage is further developed below.

Global properties of cirDNA genome coverage

As our WPN set and thus the feature vectors associated with each individual were rather vast (4,971,768 measures of nucleosome occupancy), we explored smaller subsets of WPNs to perform patient classification based on a much-reduced number of features. Namely, we randomly selected 20 sets of N nucleosomes, and for each we performed the two embedded CV steps described above and in Fig. S12 with 3 and 5 repetitions (instead of 10 and 15), which lead to random selections of size N. In the case of the healthy versus pancreatic cancer samples, we used , and we found that only using nucleosome positions instead of almost 5 million, gave the same performance (Fig. 4A). Performance then progressively decreased with and . Breast cancer was easier to detect and we obtained similar performance to full WPN sets with as well (Additional File 1: Fig. S16). We obtained comparable results for the other five cancer types (data not shown), indicating that differences in nucleosome occupancy were rather global. Inspection of the cirDNA coverage for the healthy and cancer cohorts revealed many, rather small differences limited to a single or few consecutive WPN positions (Fig. 4B-D, Additional File 1: Fig. S17). Considering the WPN-wide cirDNA coverage differences, we compared the distances in the dendrogram between the different cohorts after scaling the coverage over each chromosome in each cohort in the [0; 1] range (Fig. 4E). The colorectal and pancreatic as well as the breast and lung cancer samples constituted two rather close pairs. Ovarian cancer was closer to breast and lung cancer samples than the other cancer types, as previously shown by the confusion matrix in Fig. 4H. Gastric cancer was the most different.

Nucleosome occupancy and transcriptional regulation

Nucleosomes play an important role in transcription regulation. They can control DNA accessibility at specific positions, such as TSS and TFBS [9, 36]. Therefore, nucleosome occupancy should reflect DNA accessibility at such loci in cirDNA-releasing cells. Previous studies reported that cirDNA mostly originates from hematopoietic cells, particularly leukocytes, in both healthy individuals and cancer patients [11, 14]. We hence used the Cristiano healthy cohort to correlate cirDNA coverage at the TSS of known genes with transcript abundance in hematopoietic cells [37]. We found a negative correlation (Pearson correlation coefficient = -0.36) (Fig. 5A) in agreement with the fact that nucleosomes repress gene transcription. We also retrieved 4.7 M peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) ATAC-seq peaks from ChIP-Atlas database [38]. We found that 0.12 M out of the 4.7 M peaks overlapped with high-occupancy WPNs, while 3.15 M overlapped with low-occupancy WPNs. These results matched previous work that reported negative correlation between ATAC-seq normalized counts in blood and coverage in healthy cirDNA [39].

The cirDNA fragment size range in the Cristiano healthy and cancer cohorts from FinaleDB (≥ 100 bp) was not adapted to find fragments protected by TFs, which are much shorter [9, 40]. Therefore, by adapting published techniques [9, 41], we inferred DNA accessibility to TFs based on nucleosome occupancy at TFBSs. An example of TFBS with differential nucleosome occupancy is featured in Fig. 5B where the total scaled occupancy was reduced in all seven cancer cohorts compared with the healthy cohort. To perform a systematic analysis, we downloaded the JASPAR database that provided the TFBSs for 644 human TFs [42] and selected only those that overlapped with WPN positions. Then, we compared (Wilcoxon test) the occupancy values in cancer (each type) and healthy samples at each TFBS-overlapping nucleosome to identify TFBSs at WPN positions with differential occupancy. We summed the cirDNA coverage at these TFBSs for each TF in healthy and cancer samples separately over ± 100 bp windows (Fig. 5C). We imposed Pearson correlation coefficient values > 0.6 in absolute value (cirDNA coverage in healthy versus cancer samples) to avoid random signals (Fig. 5D). We found a limited number of prototypical patterns (TFs in Fig. 5C and E-F). The two most recurrent configurations displayed higher cirDNA coverage in healthy than cancer samples and reduced nucleosome occupancy at the TFBSs. In the first case (Fig. 5C), the negative correlation indicated stronger transcriptional regulation in cancer samples. In the second case (Fig. 5E), the positive correlation indicated milder regulation in cancer samples that strengthened a reduction already present in healthy samples. The third configuration (Fig. 5F) was very rare and corresponded to increased TF binding repression in cancer samples (positive correlation).

Overall, we found 250 TFs with higher WPN occupancy in healthy samples and negative correlation in all cancer types and 97 TFs with higher WPN occupancy in healthy samples and positive correlation in all cancer types. The 250 negatively correlated, and thus more strongly regulated TFs were enriched in various KEGG [43] cancer pathways (Fig. 5G). The 97 positively correlated, and thus more mildly regulated TFs did not display any particular pathway enrichment. Inspection of the positively and negatively correlated TFs that were shared by all seven cancer types highlighted several TFs involved in hematopoietic cell differentiation: IKZF1 [44], NFKB1 [45], SOX12 [46], ATF2 [47], BCL6 [48], GATA2 [49], and JunD [50] (Fig. 5H).

Only few TFs adopted a cancer-specific nucleosome occupancy at their TFBS. Gastric cancer harbored 2 TFs with specific positive correlation, and 1 with negative correlation. Bile duct cancer had 1 TF with specific positive correlation, and 2 with negative correlation Pancreatic cancer had 20 TFs with specific positive correlation, and lung and ovarian cancer harbored 3 and 1 TFs with cancer-specific positive correlation, respectively. Colorectal cancer exhibited 51 TFs with specific positive correlation and 3 with negative correlation. Notably, CRC was the only cancer type in which TF binding sites displayed higher coverage in the cancer cohort compared to the healthy cohorts, with 14 TFs showing a specific positive correlation. Among the 54 CRC-specific TFs (n = 54), we found many members of the SP-KLF family that have a role in this cancer regulation [51, 52]. Other examples (Fig. 6I) were SOX2 [53] in ovarian cancer, SP1 [52] in colorectal cancer, MYBL1 [54] in lung cancer, HOXB5 [55] in pancreatic cancer, and ZKSCAN3 [56] in gastric cancer. Colorectal cancer was also the only cancer with TFBS positions more covered in cancer (14 TFs) than healthy samples. Some of these TFs had potential tumor suppressive functions, such as REST (Fig. 5F) [57] and EGR3 [58].

Neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) activation and release (NETosis) is an important source of cirDNA [59] and NF-kB was activated in neutrophils. IKZF1 is expressed in hematopoietic linages including neutrophils. GATA2 is required for survival and proliferation of multipotent progenitor cells that give rise to granulocytes, including neutrophils (patients with GATA2 mutations often have present with neutropenia). SOX2 is a cytoplasmic DNA sensor in neutrophils. SP1 binds to GC-rich motifs in gene promoters, influencing the transcription of genes involved in neutrophil function. Namely, neutrophil elastase, an enzyme released by activated neutrophils, can stimulate the expression of genes, such as MUC1, by increasing SP1 binding to their promoters, indicating a role for SP1 in neutrophil-mediated gene activation. NF-κB signaling pathways cross talk with SP1 [60], and ZKSCAN3 negatively regulates these pathways in neutrophils. Besides these genes, we followed an unbiased approach to extract all genes defined in the KEGG NET formation pathway and then used the ChEA3 tool [61] to infer TFs that may regulate these genes. We found an interesting association between list provided by ChEA3 and our negatively correlated TFs (P = 0.059, hypergeometric test).

Discussion

General properties of cirDNA fragments were searched in the large cohort of 245 healthy individuals by Cristiano et al. [16]. The cirDNA fragment size distribution displayed the commonly observed pattern with a strong mode at 167–168 bp and subpeaks distanced by 10.3 bp on average. We found that these subpeaks also existed after the major mode at 167–168 bp. They are known to be caused by nuclease-facilitated access to protein-bound DNA at minor grooves [7, 8]. Subpeaks after the major mode were hence indicative of continued nucleosome protection in fragments larger than a chromatosome. In addition, the fragment U and D end distributions tended to concentrate in small intervals also separated by 10.3 bp on average, which we explain by a domination of recurrent genome positions for nucleosomes in line with nucleosome positioning literature [22]. Moreover, the probabilities of observation at these small intervals augmented in the vicinity of nucleosome boundaries [18]. That is, we observed both a local structure related to nucleosome positions and a microlocal structure related to nuclease activity and DNA nicks. In a different context, relating DNA methylation and cirDNA fragmentation patterns, other authors observed periodic variations of fragment size probabilities relative to the nucleosome central position [62]. This phenomenon is linked to the microlocal structure we evidenced in this work.

Nucleosome positioning is actively and accurately regulated in time and space through multiple mechanisms, such as DNA sequence, ATP-dependent nucleosome remodeling enzymes, histone and DNA modifications, poly(dA: dT) and poly(dG: dC) tracts, TFs, RNA polymerase II elongation and transcription, and non-histone DNA binding factors [30, 31]. Moreover, nucleosomes assemble and disassemble constantly [63, 64]. Therefore, we hypothesized that high-occupancy nucleosome positions would harbor distinct characteristics compared with low-occupancy positions. To address this question, we first designed a simple algorithm to assemble a large set of well-positioned nucleosomes that we called WPNs. We identified a total of 4,971,768 WPNs using the Cristiano healthy cohort. The number of WPNs per chromosome was proportional to the chromosome size, and occupancy heterogeneity was close to one order of magnitude in each chromosome. High-occupancy WPNs were enriched in heterochromatin, and low-occupancy WPNs were enriched at gene-flanking promoters or TSS. This confirmed the overall relevance of the WPN set as well as the existence of distinct properties in high- and low-occupancy WPNs. To identify physical differences, we exploited a naturally available readout: cirDNA fragment sizes. We discovered that fragments originating from high-occupancy WPNs were longer that those from low-occupancy WPNs. We could confirm this in two independent cohorts [17, 18]. We hypothesized that such a difference was related to variation in DNA-histone affinity. This implied that DNA locations harboring high- versus low-occupancy WPNs would have evolved slightly differently to adjust their affinity with histones and other proteins. However, at protein-coding genes, DNA is constrained to maintain its basic coding function. Therefore, we thought that evidence of DNA adaptation could be found in codon usage biases. Indeed, by computing the log-odds of codon usage in gene exons overlapping with high- and low-occupancy WPNs, we found significant differences. To challenge the inevitable dependencies of a particular set of WPNs on the algorithm specifics as well as parameter values, we considered an even larger, less strictly defined set of ~ 9.3 M WPNs in addition to an independent set of WPNs computed from Jiang healthy data. In both cases, we could reproduce our observations.

Having demonstrated that WPNs associated with distinct DNA physical properties and genomic regions in function of their occupancy, we asked the data whether occupancy was changed in cancer, although most cirDNA does not originate from tumor cells in majority. Indeed, in cancer, malignant clone epigenetic profiles are profoundly affected [33] as well as cirDNA abundance [14], fragment size [7] and end motifs [13], to mention only the best described features. Cristiano et al. released seven cancer cohorts (bile duct, breast, colorectal, gastric, lung, ovarian, and pancreatic) in addition to the healthy individual cohort. From these cancer cohorts, we extracted the individual nucleosome occupancy levels for each patient at each WPN position (4,971,768 measures per individual), and found that PCA 2D-projection could perfectly separate healthy individuals from cancer patients. Therefore, we designed a new algorithm that combined PCA and ML and WPN occupancy data. This approach allowed discriminating between healthy controls and cancer patients, including at early stages (AUC > 0.99, sensitivity > 0.95, specificity > 0.95 in all cases). The separation of distinct cancer types, a more difficult task, resulted in heterogeneous performance. Patients with bile duct, breast, colorectal, gastric, and pancreatic cancer could be reasonably well separated from each other (median overall accuracy = 0.77, 95%CI = [0.68; 0.82]), but not patients with lung and ovarian cancer. In their study, Cristiano et al. achieved an overall accuracy of 0.6 for the same task [16]. By exploiting other datasets and two to six different cancer types, other authors obtained overall accuracy values similar to ours [65–67]. Our approach does not allow separating cancer stages within a dataset, but we are not aware of any method for doing this. For a different, although related purpose, other researchers found a relation between cirDNA genome coverage and CNV [67]. We thus assessed CNV potential contribution to the observed classification performance. Based on experimentally determined cancer cell DNA rate in cirDNA provided by Cristiano et al. for their cancer cohorts, we found no obvious association.

As our PCA/ML algorithm could separate patients with cancer from healthy individuals very easily, cancer might have induced some global changes in nucleosome occupancy. However, due to the PCA step, very specific modifications might be limited in numbers and magnitudes. The discovery that randomly selecting only 10,000 to 100,000 WPNs from the almost 5 million total set was sufficient to obtain excellent cancer detection suggested that differences were abundant. To better understand this, we compared cirDNA genome coverage at the local and global scales. We observed rather modest local variations, limited to few consecutive nucleosomes, but with significant separation of healthy individuals and cancer cohorts. Such WPNs were usually distinct for different cancers. At the global level, differences displayed a faint but clear structure. This structure was cancer-specific and partially different from the basal cirDNA coverage, which was correlated with global genome properties such as closed/open chromatin and transcriptional activity.

To complement this global analysis, we investigated the regulation of access to TFBS through WPN occupancy. We found that the TFBSs of 347 TFs were more available in all seven Cristiano cancer cohorts compared with the healthy cohort. Among other functions, these TFs are involved in cancer development, hematopoietic cell differentiation, and NETosis-related gene regulation. These functions reflect the main cirDNA sources (hematopoietic cells and NETs) as well as the contribution from cancer cells. A small number of TFs had access to their binding sites controlled by WPN occupancy in a cancer-specific manner. Colorectal cancer was the cancer type with the highest number of specifically regulated TFs (n = 54). The 14 TFBS with reduced accessibility in cancer were all detected in this cancer cohort and they included tumor suppressors. The rather homogeneous modulation of TFBS accessibility through well-positioned nucleosome occupancy agreed with the cirDNA global properties that varied little among cancer types. Therefore, we propose that cancer-specific nucleosome occupancy regulation does not occur predominantly at nucleosomes identified as well-positioned in healthy individuals. Their positioning might be deeply coded in our genome, such that even cancer has limited impact on them. Stronger changes might be observed at fuzzy nucleosomes, in cirDNA that directly binds to TFs and that was depleted in Cristiano’s cohorts (fragments were larger than 100 bp), and certainly at the more dynamic levels that are DNA and DNA-binding protein modifications. Furthermore, as we adopted a cohort view due to the limited individual sequencing depth, marked differences in patient subgroups might have been masked.

Lastly, the large set of WPNs we identified along with the computer code we make available to compute them constitute a resource, but it bares limitations that must be appreciated precisely. Limitations mainly originate from the nature if the data, i.e., cirDNA. Indeed, the mixture of cell types contributing to cirDNA may cause cell type-specific WPNs to appear fuzzy by addition. Accordingly and as supported by our results, our WPNs positions are sufficient to sample fragmentomic and DNA properties relative to nucleosomes, but they cannot be regarded as exhaustive.

Conclusions

We identified new fragmentomic features related to well-positioned nucleosome occupancy that led to a highly sensitive and selective algorithm for cancer detection. We also exploited cirDNA as a physical readout to learn about the DNA sequence at well-positioned nucleosome loci.
