Static axisymmetric Einstein spaces with a cosmological constant and the limitation of canonical Weyl coordinates
Sheref Nasereldin

Abstract
The canonical Weyl form for four-dimensional static axisymmetric vacuum metrics is obtained by identifying the area function of the two Killing orbits with a harmonic coordinate on the two-dimensional orbit space. This construction is valid in Ricci-flat vacuum, but it is no longer available in Einstein spaces with nonzero cosmological constant. In this paper, we consider the generalized orthogonally transitive static axisymmetric line element and derive the reduced Einstein– Λ \Lambda field equations. We show that the canonical Weyl choice W = ρ W=\rho is locally admissible if and only if Λ = 0 \Lambda=0 . The Kottler metric gives the simplest explicit example of the resulting equation for the area function. Thus, the statement that “Weyl metrics do not allow Λ ≠ 0 \Lambda\neq 0 ” is precise only when the metric is assumed to be in canonical Weyl coordinates. The issue is not staticity or axisymmetry, but rather the fact that the area function is no longer harmonic.



I
Introduction

The classical Weyl metric gives the general local form of the exterior vacuum field of a static axially symmetric gravitating source in four dimensions
Weyl1919
. It is the static specialization of the Weyl–Lewis–Papapetrou construction for spacetimes with two commuting Killing fields
Lewis1932
;
Papapetrou1966
;
StephaniBook
. In canonical coordinates its line element reads

ds^{2}=-e^{2U}dt^{2}+e^{-2U}\Bigl[e^{2\gamma}(d\rho^{2}+dz^{2})+\rho^{2}d\phi^{2}\Bigr],

(1)

where
U=U(\rho,z)
and
\gamma=\gamma(\rho,z)
. In vacuum,
U
satisfies the flat-space axisymmetric Laplace equation and
\gamma
is obtained by quadratures; for example, see
GriffithsPodolsky2009
.

Static spacetimes with nonzero cosmological constant also exist, with the Kottler family
Kottler1918
being the simplest example. The question now arises as to whether these Einstein spaces can be put in the same canonical Weyl form.

It is important to note that the Weyl line element is not merely a statement about staticity and axial symmetry. It also imposes the canonical Weyl coordinate choice

W\equiv\sqrt{-\det(g_{AB})}=\rho,\qquad A,B\in\{t,\phi\}.

(2)

For Ricci-flat vacuum,
W
is harmonic on the orbit space, and this coordinate choice is admissible. For Einstein spaces with

R_{ab}=\Lambda g_{ab},

(3)

the same function satisfies an equation with a nonzero source term. Therefore, the limitation is not staticity itself, but the use of canonical Weyl coordinates.

The purpose of this paper is to make this statement precise in the purely static axisymmetric setting. We work locally in the orthogonally transitive sector, derive the reduced Einstein–
\Lambda
field equations for a generalized line element with area function
W(\rho,z)
, and show that the canonical choice
W=\rho
is locally admissible in a region away from the symmetry axis,
\rho>0
, if and only if
\Lambda=0
. The Kottler metric then provides the simplest explicit illustration of the resulting equation for
W
.

This question is also connected to earlier work on reductions of Einstein spaces with a cosmological constant and on
\Lambda
-deformed integrability
LeighEtAl2014
;
KlemmNozawaRabbiosi2015
;
CardosoMayorgaPenaNampuri2024
;
CardosoMahapatraNagy2024
. The point emphasized here is more specific: in the static axisymmetric sector, the failure of the canonical Weyl form is controlled directly by the differential equation satisfied by the area function. Thus, the result should be viewed as a local statement about canonical Weyl coordinates, not as a global obstruction to static axisymmetric Einstein spaces.

II
Static axisymmetry beyond canonical Weyl coordinates

To keep the canonical Weyl condition out of the ansatz (
1
), we work with the more general orthogonally transitive static axisymmetric line element

ds^{2}=-e^{2U}dt^{2}+e^{-2U}\left[e^{2\nu}(d\rho^{2}+dz^{2})+W(\rho,z)^{2}d\phi^{2}\right].

(4)

Here
U=U(\rho,z)
,
\nu=\nu(\rho,z)
, and
W=W(\rho,z)
. This is the usual local setting in which the Weyl–Lewis–Papapetrou form is introduced
Papapetrou1966
;
StephaniBook
. The canonical Weyl metric is recovered by the additional specialization

W(\rho,z)=\rho.

(5)

This separates the coordinate specialization from the broader existence of static axisymmetric Einstein spaces.

III
Reduced Einstein–
\Lambda
equations

The line element (
4
) leaves the area function
W(\rho,z)
arbitrary. We now use the Einstein–
\Lambda
field equations to determine whether the canonical Weyl choice
W=\rho
is compatible with the field equations, or whether it is a special feature of the Ricci-flat case.

We work under the assumption of staticity. Namely, there exists a timelike Killing vector
\xi^{\alpha}=\delta^{\alpha}_{t}
satisfying

\xi_{[\alpha}\nabla_{\beta}\xi_{\delta]}=0,

(6)

and all metric functions are independent of
t
. In this case, the line element can be written in the standard static form
Wald1984
;
StephaniBook

ds^{2}=-e^{2U(\rho,z)}dt^{2}+h_{ij}(x)\,dx^{i}dx^{j},

(7)

where
x^{i}=(\rho,z,\phi)
, and
h_{ij}
is the metric induced on the hypersurfaces orthogonal to
\xi^{\alpha}
.

If
R_{\mu\nu}=\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}
, then the lapse potential
U
and the spatial metric
h
satisfy
Note1

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}U+|DU|_{h}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\Lambda,

(8)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ric}(h)_{ij}

\displaystyle=D_{i}D_{j}U+D_{i}UD_{j}U+\Lambda h_{ij}.

(9)

where
D
and
\Delta_{h}
denote the Levi-Civita connection and Laplacian associated with the spatial metric
h
, respectively.

We now make the conformal rescaling

h_{ij}=e^{-2U}\,\bar{h}_{ij}.

(10)

With this choice, the four-metric takes the Weyl-like form

ds^{2}=-e^{2U}dt^{2}+e^{-2U}\bar{h}_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j}.

In the orthogonally transitive axisymmetric setting, the rescaled three-metric can then be written as

\bar{h}=q_{AB}(x)\,dx^{A}dx^{B}+W^{2}(x)\,d\phi^{2},\qquad A,B\in\{\rho,z\},

(11)

where
q
is the metric on the two-dimensional orbit space and
W
measures the size of the axial circles. Finally, choosing local conformal coordinates
(\rho,z)
on the orbit space gives

q_{AB}dx^{A}dx^{B}=e^{2\nu(\rho,z)}(d\rho^{2}+dz^{2}).

(12)

By virtue of the conformal transformation formulas in three dimensions, (
8
) and (
9
) become

\displaystyle\Delta_{\bar{h}}U

\displaystyle=-\Lambda e^{-2U},

(13)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ric}(\bar{h})_{ij}

\displaystyle=2\,\partial_{i}U\,\partial_{j}U+2\Lambda e^{-2U}\bar{h}_{ij}.

(14)

For the three-metric

\bar{h}=q_{AB}dx^{A}dx^{B}+W^{2}d\phi^{2},

(15)

the nonzero Ricci components are

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ric}(\bar{h})_{AB}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ric}(q)_{AB}-W^{-1}\nabla_{A}\nabla_{B}W,

(16)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ric}(\bar{h})_{\phi\phi}

\displaystyle=-W\Delta_{q}W,

(17)

where all derivatives are taken with respect to
q
.

Substituting these expressions into the conformally rescaled static equations gives the reduced Einstein–
\Lambda
system on the two-dimensional orbit space:

\displaystyle\Delta_{q}U+W^{-1}\nabla W\cdot\nabla U

\displaystyle=-\Lambda e^{-2U},

(18)

\displaystyle\Delta_{q}W

\displaystyle=-2\Lambda e^{-2U}W,

(19)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ric}(q)_{AB}

\displaystyle=W^{-1}\nabla_{A}\nabla_{B}W+2\nabla_{A}U\nabla_{B}U

\displaystyle\quad+2\Lambda e^{-2U}q_{AB}.

(20)

The equation for
W
comes from the
\phi\phi
component of (
14
). Since
U
is independent of
\phi
, (
13
) gives

\Delta_{\bar{h}}U=\Delta_{q}U+W^{-1}\nabla W\cdot\nabla U,

(21)

which gives the equation for
U
. The remaining
AB
components of (

q
.

In the conformal coordinates
q_{AB}=e^{2\nu}\delta_{AB}
, the reduced system becomes

\displaystyle U_{,\rho\rho}+U_{,zz}+\frac{W_{,\rho}U_{,\rho}+W_{,z}U_{,z}}{W}

\displaystyle=-\Lambda e^{2\nu-2U},

(22)

\displaystyle W_{,\rho\rho}+W_{,zz}

\displaystyle=-2\Lambda e^{2\nu-2U}W,

(23)

\displaystyle\nu_{,\rho\rho}+\nu_{,zz}

\displaystyle=-\bigl(U_{,\rho}^{2}+U_{,z}^{2}\bigr)-\Lambda e^{2\nu-2U}.

(24)

The off-diagonal and anisotropic parts of (

give the following first-order relations:

\displaystyle W_{,\rho}\nu_{,\rho}-W_{,z}\nu_{,z}

\displaystyle=W\bigl(U_{,\rho}^{2}-U_{,z}^{2}\bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(W_{,\rho\rho}-W_{,zz}\bigr),

\displaystyle W_{,z}\nu_{,\rho}+W_{,\rho}\nu_{,z}

\displaystyle=2WU_{,\rho}U_{,z}+W_{,\rho z}.

(26)

IV
Relation with the canonical Weyl identity

For the canonical Weyl metric one has

g_{\rho\rho}=g_{zz}.

(27)

In addition, the Einstein tensor obeys the identity

G_{\rho\rho}=-G_{zz},

(28)

which is the content behind the footnote in
NasereldinLake2019
. This provides a useful check on the more general reduction derived above.

If one now imposes the Einstein–
\Lambda
equations,

G_{ab}+\Lambda g_{ab}=0,

(29)

then, by virtue of
g_{\rho\rho}=g_{zz}
, we obtain

G_{\rho\rho}=G_{zz}.

(30)

Combining this with (
28
) yields

G_{\rho\rho}=G_{zz}=0,

(31)

and hence necessarily
\Lambda=0
.

The same conclusion follows directly from the reduced equation for
W
. If one imposes the canonical Weyl coordinate choice
W=\rho
, (
23
) reduces to

0=-2\Lambda e^{2\nu-2U}\rho.

(32)

Away from the symmetry axis,
\rho>0
, the exponential factor is nonzero, and therefore
\Lambda=0
. Conversely, when
\Lambda=0
, (
23
) becomes the flat two-dimensional Laplace equation for
W
, so the canonical Weyl coordinates are locally admissible wherever
\nabla W\neq 0
. Thus, within the orthogonally transitive static axisymmetric line element (
4
), the canonical Weyl choice
W=\rho
is compatible with the field equations in a local region away from the axis if and only if
\Lambda=0
.

V
The area function and the correct replacement

Define the area function of the Killing orbits by

W=\sqrt{-\det(g_{AB})},\qquad A,B\in\{t,\phi\}.

(33)

For the general line element (
4
), this is simply the metric function
W(\rho,z)
.
This definition is useful because it is tied to the two Killing directions rather than to a particular choice of the orbit-space coordinates. Under a constant nonsingular linear redefinition of the Killing coordinates,
W
changes only by the absolute value of the determinant of that linear transformation. Therefore, the question of whether
W
is harmonic, and hence whether it can be used as a canonical Weyl coordinate up to a constant normalization, is not an artifact of the coordinates
(\rho,z)
.

In Ricci-flat vacuum,
W
is harmonic on the two-dimensional orbit space. This enables one to choose
W
itself as a coordinate and obtain the canonical Weyl form. For
\Lambda\neq 0
,
W
satisfies an equation with a nonzero source term, so there is no reason to expect the coordinate choice
W=\rho
to remain admissible.

VI
Kottler as the simplest example

Consider the Kottler metric
Kottler1918

ds^{2}=-f(r)dt^{2}+f(r)^{-1}dr^{2}+r^{2}(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\phi^{2}),

(34)

with

f(r)=1-\frac{2M}{r}-\frac{\Lambda r^{2}}{3}.

(35)

This metric is static and axisymmetric, so it must be contained in (
4
). One reads off

e^{2U}=f(r),\qquad W=r\sin\theta\sqrt{f(r)}.

(36)

The two-metric
q
appearing in (
4
) is

q=dr^{2}+fr^{2}d\theta^{2}.

(37)

A direct computation gives

\Delta_{q}W=\frac{\sqrt{f}\sin\theta}{r}\left(f+2rf^{\prime}+\frac{r^{2}}{2}f^{\prime\prime}-1\right),

(38)

and substituting the Kottler expression for
f
yields

\Delta_{q}W=-2\Lambda e^{-2U}W,

(39)

which is in exact agreement with (
19
).

Thus, when
\Lambda=0
,
W
is harmonic and canonical Weyl coordinates are admissible. In contrast, when
\Lambda\neq 0
,
W
is not harmonic and the canonical Weyl coordinate choice fails.
In particular, the
\Lambda=0
member of the Kottler family is the Schwarzschild solution. In that case, the same expression for
W
satisfies
\Delta_{q}W=0
, which is the familiar condition that permits the passage to canonical Weyl coordinates.

VII
Conclusion

The canonical Weyl form is a special feature of the Ricci-flat static axisymmetric sector. For Einstein spaces with nonzero cosmological constant, the natural object to track is the area function
W
, and the reduced field equations show that
W
satisfies an equation with a source term rather than a harmonic equation.

The reduced system (
18
)–(
20
) makes this statement explicit. In particular, (
19
) implies that the canonical choice
W=\rho
is locally compatible with the field equations if and only if
\Lambda=0
. The Kottler metric provides the simplest concrete realization of this result.

Accordingly, the statement that Weyl metrics do not allow a nonzero cosmological constant is correct only when “Weyl metric” is understood in the canonical sense, namely, with the area function itself identified with the coordinate
\rho
. Static axisymmetric Einstein spaces with
\Lambda\neq 0
do exist, but they lie outside that coordinate choice and must be described locally by the more general line element (
4
).

This appendix records the identities used in Sec. III.

A.1
Static Einstein equations

Let

ds^{2}=-N^{2}dt^{2}+h_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j},\qquad N=e^{U},

(40)

with all metric functions independent of
t
. For an Einstein space,

R_{\mu\nu}=\Lambda g_{\mu\nu},

(41)

the standard static decomposition gives

\displaystyle R_{tt}

\displaystyle=ND^{i}D_{i}N=-\Lambda N^{2},

(42)

\displaystyle R_{ij}(g)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ric}(h)_{ij}-N^{-1}D_{i}D_{j}N=\Lambda h_{ij}.

(43)

Since
N=e^{U}
,

\displaystyle N^{-1}D^{i}D_{i}N

\displaystyle=\Delta_{h}U+|DU|_{h}^{2},

(44)

\displaystyle N^{-1}D_{i}D_{j}N

\displaystyle=D_{i}D_{j}U+D_{i}UD_{j}U,

(45)

and hence

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}U+|DU|_{h}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\Lambda,

(46)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ric}(h)_{ij}

\displaystyle=D_{i}D_{j}U+D_{i}UD_{j}U+\Lambda h_{ij}.

(47)

A.2
Conformal rescaling

Now write

h_{ij}=e^{-2U}\bar{h}_{ij}.

(48)

For a conformal rescaling in three dimensions one has

\begin{split}\mathrm{Ric}(h)_{ij}=\mathrm{Ric}(\bar{h})_{ij}+\bar{\nabla}_{i}\bar{\nabla}_{j}U+\partial_{i}U\partial_{j}U\\
+\bigl(\bar{\Delta}U-|\bar{\nabla}U|_{\bar{h}}^{2}\bigr)\bar{h}_{ij},\end{split}

(49)

while for a scalar,

\Delta_{h}f=e^{2U}\bigl(\bar{\Delta}f-\bar{\nabla}U\cdot\bar{\nabla}f\bigr),\qquad|Df|_{h}^{2}=e^{2U}|\bar{\nabla}f|_{\bar{h}}^{2}.

(50)

Applying the scalar identity to

and using the first static equation gives

\bar{\Delta}U=-\Lambda e^{-2U}.

(51)

For the Hessian of

D_{i}D_{j}U=\bar{\nabla}_{i}\bar{\nabla}_{j}U+2\partial_{i}U\partial_{j}U-|\bar{\nabla}U|_{\bar{h}}^{2}\bar{h}_{ij}.

(52)

Substituting these identities into the second static equation yields

\mathrm{Ric}(\bar{h})_{ij}=2\partial_{i}U\partial_{j}U+2\Lambda e^{-2U}\bar{h}_{ij}.

(53)
