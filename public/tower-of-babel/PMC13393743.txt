The impact of managerial styles on medical service quality in Romanian public hospitals: a study of accreditation and performance
Authors: Carmen Cumpăt, Muthana Zouri, Daniela Huţu, Andreea Grădinaru, Nicoleta Zouri, Daniela Tatiana Agheorghiesei, Federico Lega
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The quality of medical services in public hospitals is significantly influenced by managerial styles, which shape organizational efficiency, patient satisfaction, and hospital accreditation outcomes. This study examines the impact of managerial leadership styles and organizational characteristics on accreditation performance in Romanian public hospitals. Using survey data from hospital managers, the study employs a structured analytical approach combining Chi-square tests and cross-tabulations, Kendall’s Tau correlations, the Kruskal–Wallis H test, ordinal regression modeling, and exploratory K-means cluster analysis to examine how hospital size, managerial education, employee consultation, team motivation, and access to information are associated with accreditation outcomes. Results indicate that hospital administration type, field of study, and team motivation are significant predictors of accreditation success, while hospital size negatively correlates with accreditation scores, suggesting larger hospitals face greater challenges in meeting accreditation standards. Conversely, employee consultation and information transparency show no statistically significant impact on accreditation performance. These findings highlight the critical role of managerial education and team engagement in achieving higher accreditation outcomes. Strengthening leadership training and team-based quality initiatives could enhance accreditation performance in public hospitals. Policy makers should prioritize managerial development programs and strategic leadership approaches to improve healthcare quality and compliance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14969-2.

Introduction

The quality of healthcare services is a fundamental component of health systems worldwide, directly influencing patient outcomes, institutional performance, and overall public health. The implementation of quality management systems is essential for ensuring efficiency, performance optimization, and adherence to accreditation standards [1]. In an era marked by demographic shifts, an aging population, and increasing demand for advanced medical services, healthcare institutions must navigate complex challenges while striving to deliver high-quality care [2].

A key determinant of healthcare service quality is the ability of hospitals to meet evolving patient needs while maintaining cost-effectiveness and operational sustainability [3]. To achieve this, hospitals must implement systematic performance monitoring, data-driven decision-making, and leadership strategies that foster continuous improvement [4]. Accreditation serves as a benchmark for hospital quality assessment, ensuring that institutions adhere to rigorous clinical, managerial, and procedural standards [1]. However, the ability to achieve high accreditation scores depends on multiple factors, including hospital size, managerial training, leadership approaches, and team engagement.

Effective hospital leadership plays a central role in coordinating resources, optimizing workflows, and fostering a culture of continuous improvement [5]. The extent to which hospital managers engage with their teams, implement evidence-based decision-making, and use external experts may significantly influence accreditation outcomes. Despite growing recognition of these factors, empirical studies exploring the relationship between managerial characteristics and hospital accreditation in Romania remain limited. This study seeks to address this gap by investigating how hospital size, managerial training, team motivation, consulting employees, and information-sharing practices affect accreditation scores in Romanian public hospitals.

This research tests several hypotheses to determine which organizational and managerial factors contribute to higher accreditation outcomes. First, hospital size (measured by bed count) may significantly impact accreditation performance, as larger hospitals tend to have greater resource availability and specialization, while smaller institutions may exhibit greater agility and personalized patient care. Similarly, the field of study of hospital managers may shape their leadership approach and ability to navigate accreditation requirements, with backgrounds in health administration or business management potentially leading to better institutional performance.

In addition to managerial background, the level of engagement of consulting employees may play a crucial role in enhancing hospital accreditation scores. External consultants provide expertise in quality assurance, compliance, and operational efficiency, yet their impact on accreditation success remains uncertain. Internal team dynamics are also critical; hospitals with highly motivated and engaged teams may demonstrate greater compliance with accreditation standards and improved overall performance. Similarly, the availability of structured and transparent information-sharing mechanisms can strengthen hospital teams’ ability to align with accreditation standards.

Furthermore, managerial training has been widely promoted as essential for improving healthcare leadership, yet its direct influence on accreditation scores requires empirical validation. Given the multifaceted nature of hospital accreditation, it is unlikely that a single factor determines accreditation success. Instead, a combination of institutional characteristics, managerial training, team motivation, engaging in expert consultation within their domain, and information-sharing practices may collectively shape accreditation outcomes. Therefore, this study seeks to identify the best combination of hospital and managerial attributes that contribute to high accreditation scores.

In Romania, the hospital accreditation system is a key policy instrument for improving healthcare quality, yet many public hospitals struggle to meet accreditation requirements due to inefficient management practices, resource constraints, and regulatory challenges [6]. Managerial leadership styles play a crucial role in hospital accreditation, influencing decision-making, team motivation, employee engagement, and the ability to implement quality improvement strategies. However, little is known about the specific managerial attributes that contribute most to accreditation success in Romania’s public healthcare system. This study seeks to fill this gap by examining how hospital size, managerial training, team motivation, consulting employees, and information-sharing practices impact accreditation outcomes.

By employing ordinal regression as the primary analytical approach, complemented by exploratory bivariate and clustering analyses, this study provides a structured empirical assessment of the managerial and organizational factors associated with hospital accreditation outcomes in Romania. The findings contribute to the broader discourse on healthcare governance and quality assurance by highlighting how managerial characteristics and internal organizational processes relate to accreditation performance within a public-sector context. By identifying patterns of association rather than causal effects, the study offers evidence-informed insights for hospital administrators, policymakers, and accreditation bodies seeking to align leadership development and governance practices with accreditation requirements, thereby supporting more context-sensitive quality improvement strategies in the Romanian public healthcare system.

In this study, hospital accreditation is used as an institutional-level proxy for healthcare quality. Rather than directly measuring patient-level clinical outcomes, accreditation reflects the extent to which hospitals comply with standardized managerial, organizational, and clinical processes established by national quality frameworks. As such, accreditation captures dimensions of process quality, governance, and organizational performance, which are widely recognized in the healthcare quality management literature as key components of institutional quality. This distinction is important, as the present analysis focuses on how managerial and organizational factors are associated with these process-oriented quality indicators, rather than with direct clinical outcomes.

Literature review

Hospital accreditation and healthcare quality

Healthcare organizations have increasingly adopted quality management systems such as accreditation in response to rising patient expectations and competitive pressures, as well as evidence suggesting improvements in operational efficiency and organizational performance [4]. Accreditation is generally defined as a voluntary, periodic, and systematic evaluation process through which healthcare institutions are assessed against predefined quality standards, serving as a formal mechanism for demonstrating commitment to quality improvement [7].

The modern quality assurance movement in healthcare can be traced back to 1917, when the American College of Surgeons initiated the first set of minimum hospital standards, aimed at identifying and addressing deficiencies in medical services. This initiative laid the groundwork for the contemporary accreditation process, which has been overseen since 1951 by the Joint Commission on Accreditation of Healthcare Organizations [8].

Accreditation has since become a widely used global strategy for promoting quality and accountability in healthcare organizations. Prior research suggests that accreditation can stimulate organizational change, support the adoption of standardized management practices, and enhance institutional legitimacy and competitiveness [9]. Accredited institutions may also benefit from improved relationships with payers and regulators. Nonetheless, accreditation requires substantial financial and organizational investment, and its overall impact on patient outcomes remains contested [4, 10].

Empirical studies report mixed but generally positive associations between accreditation and healthcare performance. For example, Hussein et al. [11] find that accreditation is linked to improved safety culture, operational efficiency, and reduced length of hospital stay, largely through the implementation of standardized procedures and protocols. However, the extent to which these improvements translate into sustained clinical outcomes continues to be debated.

Within Romania, the healthcare system has undergone significant organizational and managerial reforms, with strategic management increasingly viewed as a key lever for improving service delivery and system performance [5]. Hospital accreditation represents a central policy instrument in these reform efforts, yet its implementation has been challenged by coordination deficits, limited managerial autonomy, and persistent organizational inefficiencies [12].

Romania institutionalized hospital accreditation through the establishment of the National Hospital Accreditation Commission (Co.NAS) in 2009, followed by the creation of the National Authority for Quality Management in Health (ANMCS) in 2015. ANMCS oversees accreditation processes focused on patient safety, infrastructure, staff competencies, clinical processes, and risk management, reflecting a comprehensive approach to quality assurance [6]. Accreditation outcomes classify hospitals into six categories based on compliance with quality standards, ranging from fully accredited institutions to unaccredited facilities. In publicly funded hospitals, accreditation is conducted on a five-year cycle [13].

Although accreditation in Romania serves as a formal validation of compliance with national quality standards and an indicator of institutional commitment to patient care [14], its effectiveness remains contested. Some studies question whether accreditation leads to meaningful improvements in patient outcomes, while others report gains in selected quality indicators [15]. Moreover, limited transparency and public awareness may constrain the ability of accreditation to build patient trust and inform healthcare choices [15].

Lastly, Romanian public hospital managers operate within a constrained governance environment characterized by regulatory rigidity and limited decision-making autonomy. As Iancu and Nica [16] note, these structural constraints complicate quality improvement initiatives and strategic management efforts, underscoring the importance of examining how managerial and organizational factors interact with accreditation processes in this specific institutional context.

Within this context, clinical governance provides an overarching framework for accountability, quality improvement, and patient safety; however, the present study focuses specifically on managerial leadership practices and organizational processes that shape accreditation outcomes within public hospitals.

Managerial leadership style and organizational performance in hospitals

Organizational leadership plays a central role in shaping institutional performance, particularly in complex and dynamic environments such as healthcare systems. Effective leadership supports adaptability, innovation, and strategic alignment in contexts characterized by regulatory pressure, increasing competition, and evolving service demands [17]. In hospital settings, managerial style can be understood as the set of observable leadership and decision-making practices through which managers coordinate teams, allocate resources, and engage staff, drawing on established frameworks such as transformational, participative, and transactional leadership.

Human resources constitute a core organizational asset in healthcare, and leadership effectiveness is closely linked to the ability to motivate, coordinate, and retain skilled professionals. Leadership influences organizational outcomes by shaping communication patterns, decision-making processes, and staff engagement. Sociological research indicates that participative and democratic leadership approaches tend to outperform more authoritarian styles, as they promote collaboration, shared responsibility, and organizational learning [18].

Empirical studies in hospital contexts identify transformational and supportive leadership styles as particularly effective. Aini and Dzakiyullah [19] emphasize the importance of leadership adaptability, while Chen et al. [20] highlight the role of transformational leadership in enhancing intrinsic motivation, clarifying expectations, and providing emotional support. These practices have been associated with reduced burnout and improved organizational resilience.

Organizational culture further mediates the relationship between leadership style and performance. Alsubaie et al. [21] argue that leadership approaches grounded in transparency, open communication, and collaboration enhance employee motivation and productivity. Similarly, Goula et al. [22] emphasize the importance of shared vision and employee empowerment in promoting organizational learning and accountability. Leadership that aligns institutional goals across teams and departments facilitates coordination and improves care delivery, particularly in inter-organizational settings [23].

Leadership effectiveness is also influenced by organizational structures that support teamwork, including access to resources, managerial support, and clearly defined frameworks. Supportive environments enhance team effectiveness and employee commitment, contributing to sustained performance and service quality [24]. Comparative research further indicates that transformational leadership is more prevalent in flexible, innovation-oriented settings, whereas transactional approaches dominate in more hierarchical contexts [25]. While transactional leadership may support stability, it can limit innovation, whereas participative and transformational approaches foster trust, adaptability, and organizational learning [26].

In this context, leadership approaches that emphasize participation, adaptability, and alignment between managerial practices and organizational culture are consistently associated with improved staff engagement and institutional performance. In healthcare settings, where coordination and collaboration are critical, such leadership styles appear particularly well suited to supporting quality improvement and organizational effectiveness.

Managerial style and quality of medical services

Healthcare organizations are complex systems that require effective management to ensure high-quality service delivery. Managers play a central role by overseeing resource allocation, personnel management, and process optimization, particularly in environments characterized by operational and financial constraints [27]. Given the dynamic nature of healthcare systems, managerial effectiveness is critical to sustaining service quality.

Healthcare managers can enhance quality through strategic interventions such as the adoption of lean and risk management frameworks, which have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing inefficiencies and improving performance. At the same time, rising patient awareness and advocacy have increased emphasis on quality standards and accreditation, reinforcing the need for continuous refinement of quality management systems [28]. Evidence suggests that integrating robust quality management approaches and evidence-based decision-making is associated with improved patient outcomes and institutional efficiency [29].

Prior research identifies leadership-related characteristics, such as accessibility, openness to feedback, collaborative decision-making, and support for individual initiative, as key contributors to effective healthcare management [30]. These attributes foster organizational cultures that are responsive to patient needs and capable of continuous monitoring and improvement. More broadly, leadership style has been shown to influence patient safety culture and the quality of medical care, shaping organizational norms and performance outcomes [31, 32]. Leadership approaches grounded in commitment and innovation are consistently associated with improved outcomes and efficiency [33].

These issues are particularly salient in Romanian public hospitals, where constrained resources and a heterogeneous workforce heighten the need for adaptable leadership. Organizational cultures marked by staff demotivation, linked to working conditions, remuneration, and limited professional recognition, pose persistent challenges to quality improvement [34]. In this context, leadership that promotes open communication, learning from errors, and employee support can enhance service quality and patient safety [35]. Participative and transformational leadership styles are especially relevant, as they encourage engagement, strengthen professional commitment, and mitigate burnout through recognition and staff support.

Against this background, the present study examines how managerial leadership style relates to the quality of medical services in Romanian public hospitals and how leadership approaches are associated with accreditation outcomes. By addressing this underexplored context, the study contributes to the literature on healthcare management by clarifying the role of leadership in shaping organizational performance and supporting quality improvement in public hospital settings.

Conceptual framework

To address the fragmented treatment of managerial determinants in hospital accreditation research, this study adopts a structured conceptual framework that distinguishes between managerial characteristics, organizational processes, and institutional outcomes. Rather than assuming a direct relationship between managerial attributes and accreditation performance, the framework posits that managerial characteristics influence accreditation outcomes primarily through their effects on internal organizational processes, consistent with established healthcare leadership and organizational process theories [36].

Within this framework, managerial characteristics, such as educational background, formal management training, seniority, and professional experience, are conceptualized as enabling conditions that shape leadership orientation and decision-making capacity. However, their impact on accreditation performance is indirect and mediated by organizational processes, including team motivation and performance, conflict management, employee consultation, goal alignment, and access to operational information. These processes capture how leadership is enacted in practice and how managerial decisions are embedded within day-to-day organizational routines [36].

Accreditation outcomes, expressed as ordinal accreditation categories, represent cumulative institutional performance and reflect a hospital’s capacity to consistently implement and sustain standardized processes across clinical and managerial domains. As emphasized in the clinical governance and accreditation literature, accreditation functions as an external verification mechanism rather than a quality management system in itself and therefore rewards coordinated organizational behavior rather than individual managerial credentials [37, 38].

Accordingly, the present study examines how managerial characteristics and internal organizational processes are associated with accreditation performance, understood as an external validation of institutional quality management practices.

Methodology

Research design

This study employs a quantitative research design to examine the relationship between managerial leadership styles and the quality of healthcare services in Romanian public hospitals. Quantitative research involves the collection and statistical analysis of numerical data to test theories and explore relationships between variables in a structured and objective manner [39, 40]. By using standardized, closed-ended questionnaires, this approach enables systematic data collection, supports statistical comparison, and allows for the identification of patterns across large samples. Its structured nature ensures that findings are generalizable, replicable, and suitable for drawing conclusions that inform policy and practice in a cost-effective way [41, 42].

To address the research objectives, a structured survey was administered to hospital managers employed in Romanian public hospitals. This method enabled the systematic assessment of managerial leadership styles and their relationship to healthcare service quality. Survey-based research is particularly effective for quantifying attitudes and behaviors that are not directly observable, allowing for the structured analysis of key organizational variables [43]. The questionnaire, serving as the primary data collection instrument, was designed to elicit diverse managerial perspectives relevant to hospital accreditation outcomes. The structured format of the instrument ensured consistent data collection, supporting rigorous statistical analysis and enhancing the generalizability of findings. By capturing managerial perceptions in a standardized manner, the survey approach provided both descriptive insight and empirical evidence on decision-making processes in the public healthcare sector [41, 42].

In the quantitative component of this study, the survey targeted managers of public hospitals in Romania, each operating under a management contract concluded with the hospital’s legal representative, responsible for its administrative and financial oversight. These managers play a critical role in overseeing hospital operations and ensuring compliance with healthcare policies and regulations. The research population consisted of all managers of public hospital units in Romania, with the primary inclusion criteria being active employment in a public hospital and holding a managerial position.

Due to constraints associated with hospitals that did not provide contact details, as well as institutions under the jurisdiction of the Ministry of Administration and Interior or the Ministry of Transport that were not included in publicly accessible databases, the entire research population could not be fully accessed. Accordingly, the study employed a non-probabilistic convenience sampling approach, based on the voluntary participation and accessibility of hospital managers. The target sample size (N = 186) was determined using probabilistic sampling principles, following Rea and Parker [44], with a 95% confidence level and a maximum margin of error of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 3\%$$\end{document}, to ensure adequate statistical power. However, the final sample reflects practical constraints in data collection rather than random selection. This approach allowed for the inclusion of a diverse range of hospitals across regions, sizes, and administrative structures, while acknowledging limitations in statistical generalizability.

Data collection

Defining the research population requires both the statistical delineation of the population and the assessment of its homogeneity. The delineation process is structured around three key elements: the extent of population coverage (spatial dimension), the nature of the population, and the time frame in which data is collected. Additionally, verifying the degree of homogeneity within the studied population is a critical prerequisite for selecting appropriate sampling procedures. This verification involves an analysis of variation indicators in the defining characteristics of the population [45].

A fundamental step in the sampling process is the clear definition of the survey base, the target population to which the study findings will be generalized. This definition must reflect the structural and organizational characteristics of the broader population to ensure validity and relevance [45]. Equally important is the requirement for representativeness, which underpins the generalizability of quantitative research findings. Achieving this involves identifying appropriate sampling units, selecting a suitable survey design, outlining extraction procedures, and calculating an adequate sample size [45].

Adhering to the principle of representativeness in sample selection is essential to ensure that the sample accurately reflects the broader population, thereby supporting the validity and generalizability of research findings [45]. This study targeted managers of Romanian public hospitals due to their pivotal role in healthcare administration. Eligibility criteria included holding a formal managerial position under contract with a public hospital, regardless of the institution’s size, geographic location, or the manager’s professional background.

The survey population was identified using data from the National Institute of Statistics, which reported a total of 368 public hospitals in Romania [46]. Contact information for hospital managers was obtained from publicly available institutional websites, and invitations to participate in the study were distributed via email. Given the reliance on publicly accessible contact details and voluntary participation, the sampling procedure was non-probabilistic, rather than random. Follow-up phone calls were made to confirm receipt and encourage responses. A total of 186 managers were invited to participate, of whom 122 completed the survey, yielding a response rate of 65.6%. Following data cleaning and eligibility screening, 92 responses were retained for statistical analysis. To ensure diverse representation, respondents were drawn from various development regions across Romania, capturing differences in hospital size, governance structures, and regional healthcare challenges. The primary data collection tool in this study was a structured, self-administered questionnaire. Defined by Chelcea as a systematic set of written or graphical questions designed to elicit responses in written form [47], the questionnaire is similarly described by Babbie as a method for gathering data through direct questions or statements requiring participant agreement or disagreement [48]. The questionnaire was distributed electronically via Google Docs to 271 middle and senior hospital managers across Romania. Participants received the investigator’s contact details (phone and email) to confirm participation or seek clarification. Non-respondents were followed up with a second email after one week. Electronic delivery facilitated efficient communication with participants in diverse geographic areas and minimized data entry errors, enhancing the accuracy and speed of database creation [47, 49].

The hospital accreditation status was measured using the national classification system established by the Romanian accreditation authority [6]. Hospitals were categorized into six levels reflecting accreditation outcomes: (1) Accredited; (2) Accredited with recommendations; (3) Accreditation with reservations; (4) Accredited with reduced confidence; (5) Decision to extend the accreditation process; and (6) Unaccredited. Higher values correspond to lower levels of accreditation performance. The accreditation data reported in this study reflect the conditions at the time of collection and were gathered between May 16 and June 2, 2022.

The self-administered questionnaire enabled empirical analysis of managerial leadership styles in Romanian public hospitals, yielding insights that are both generalizable and comprehensive. Hosted on the Google Docs platform, the instrument required participants to self-register, ensuring controlled access and limiting each hospital manager to a single submission, thereby minimizing external interference. These measures collectively contributed to a robust analytical framework for evaluating hospital leadership dynamics.

The data processing methodology adhered to a structured approach, comprising the following stages: (1) data collection, (2) conceptualization of questionnaire items to define key variables, (3) database construction, (4) identification and treatment of missing data, (5) detection of inconsistencies arising either from participant responses or during the database construction phase, (6) database cleaning, and (7) statistical data processing.

Data pertaining to public hospitals were categorized and geographically grouped by counties and regions of Romania, based on the official location of each hospital’s administrative headquarters. Additionally, individuals holding the position of general manager were recorded only once, in accordance with the unit where their primary position is officially registered, as per the definition outlined in the Romanian Fiscal Code (Article 7, Point 2) [50]. The dataset analyzed in this study reflects the organizational landscape during the period from May 16, 2022, to June 2, 2022.

Despite its advantages, the use of an online questionnaire posed certain limitations. Notably, the self-administered format introduced the risk of reduced spontaneity in participant responses. Additionally, misinterpretations of questionnaire items may have led to errors in response accuracy. However, the use of a secure digital platform ensured encrypted access and data integrity throughout the collection process.

To optimize the data collection instrument, the questionnaire underwent a pre-testing phase with a subsample of 19 randomly selected managers from public hospital units in Romania. The primary objective of the pre-test was to assess and enhance the clarity and comprehensibility of survey items before full-scale data collection. Preliminary findings indicated that, while the overall comprehension of the questionnaire was high, certain items exhibited low variability, suggesting that respondents had difficulty differentiating between specific categories.

The questionnaire presented in this study reflects its final, validated form. Prior to data collection, a pretest was conducted to assess clarity, internal consistency, and overall structure. Based on this process, minor revisions were made to improve item wording, eliminate redundancy, and enhance coherence across sections. The instrument was evaluated both before and after pretesting by a panel of experts, including two university professors specializing in Management and Statistics from the Faculty of Economics and Business Administration, “Alexandru Ioan Cuza” University of Iaşi, who confirmed the validity of the final version.

The questionnaire included an introductory section (Section “Introduction”) that outlined the research topic and its objectives. This section also provided participants with an informed consent form, which detailed key aspects of the study, including the estimated time required for completion, potential risks and benefits of participation, the nature of the information requested, and the voluntary nature of participation, emphasizing the right to withdraw at any time. Participants were assured of the confidentiality of their responses, the maintenance of anonymity, and the protection of personal data in accordance with European data protection regulations. The primary function of this section was to familiarize participants with the study’s purpose and to establish trust.

Given the study’s focus on a specific participant group - managers of public hospital units in Romania - a screening question was incorporated to ensure respondent eligibility. The questionnaire comprised 31 closed-ended questions, which provided multiple response options, allowing respondents to select one or more answers. Additionally, 18 open-ended questions were incorporated, enabling participants to provide responses in their own words. Open-ended questions are particularly valuable in qualitative research, as they facilitate the collection of nuanced insights and detailed perspectives from respondents [51].

In formulating the closed questions, we chose the Likert scale, through which respondents were offered the possibility of choosing between six answers: to a very small extent (1), to a small extent (2), to a fairly small extent (3), to a fairly large extent (4), to a large extent (5), to a very large extent (6). In the specialized literature, the Likert scale is seen as an approach to measuring opinions [52].

The questionnaire consists of 7 sections, Sections “Literature review”–7 were dedicated to key concepts. The last part of section 7 was intended to collect socio-demographic data about the managers responding to the study.

Statistical methods used

A structured and theory-driven analytical strategy was employed to examine the relationship between managerial characteristics, organizational processes, and accreditation outcomes in Romanian public hospitals. Descriptive statistics were first used to summarize sample characteristics and variable distributions.

Given the ordinal nature of hospital accreditation categories, ordinal logistic regression was selected as the primary inferential model. This approach is methodologically appropriate for modeling ordered outcomes and was used to test the hypothesized relationships derived from the conceptual framework. Linear regression analyses conducted during preliminary exploration were not retained in the final inferential analysis.

To support model specification and provide descriptive insight into bivariate relationships, a limited set of exploratory analyses was conducted. Chi-square tests were applied to examine associations between categorical managerial characteristics and accreditation categories, while Kendall’s Tau was used to assess monotonic relationships involving ordinal or continuous variables. These analyses were intended to characterize data patterns rather than to establish causal relationships.

Model diagnostics were conducted to assess the validity of the regression estimates. Multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF). All values were below 2, well under the commonly accepted threshold of 5, indicating that multicollinearity does not bias the regression estimates. Additional diagnostic checks were conducted to evaluate key regression assumptions, including tests for autocorrelation (Durbin–Watson statistic) and heteroskedasticity (Breusch-Pagan test), supporting the stability and reliability of the estimated coefficients.

In addition, a Random Forest model was included as a non-parametric robustness check to assess whether variable importance patterns are consistent with the regression results. Given its data-driven nature, this method was not used for hypothesis testing or causal interpretation and is reported for supplementary insight only.

Results

Descriptive analytics

A primary component of the dataset is the hospital accreditation category, which serves as a fundamental indicator of healthcare institutions’ adherence to established quality and performance criteria. Hospitals are classified into predefined accreditation levels, such as “Accredited with Recommendations” or “Accredited with Reduced Confidence”, with corresponding scores reflecting their compliance with national healthcare standards [13]. This ordinal classification enables comparative assessment of hospital performance and provides an empirical basis for examining how managerial and organizational characteristics are distributed across accreditation outcomes.

The descriptive analysis includes only hospitals for which accreditation data were publicly available (92 out of 122 completed surveys). Accreditation information was obtained from the Autoritatea Natională de Management al Calitătii in Sănătate (ANMCS), the national body responsible for healthcare quality management in Romania [13].

Descriptive patterns indicate that education level alone does not show a pronounced association with accreditation status, as hospitals led by managers with different educational backgrounds are relatively evenly distributed across accreditation categories. In contrast, hospital administrative experience and formal management training display more uneven distributions, with higher accreditation levels appearing more frequently among hospitals reporting experienced administrators and structured training programs. Similarly, organizational seniority and managerial seniority suggest that longer institutional tenure is more commonly observed among higher-accredited hospitals, potentially reflecting the benefits of accumulated institutional knowledge and continuity in quality management practices. Gender does not appear to differentiate accreditation outcomes, as distributions remain broadly balanced across categories.

With respect to leadership characteristics, transformational leadership styles were most frequently reported among hospital managers, often accompanied by participative decision-making practices. This pattern is consistent with prior research indicating the prevalence of transformational and participative leadership approaches in healthcare organizations engaged in reform or accreditation processes [53, 54]. Most hospitals in the sample were medium to large in size, a structural feature previously associated with greater administrative capacity and engagement with accreditation initiatives [55]. In terms of team dynamics, a high proportion of respondents reported structured team roles and clear communication channels, aligning with evidence that effective intra-organizational collaboration supports institutional performance and quality outcomes [56]. While these findings are descriptive in nature, they provide an important contextual foundation for subsequent inferential analyses.

The dataset also captures regional variation, enabling the examination of hospital distribution across Romanian counties and development regions. In addition, it includes indicators related to accreditation compliance, such as the proportion of fulfilled standards, identified deficiencies, and corrective actions. Together with leadership style measures—ranging from participative and transformational to more hierarchical approaches—these variables offer a comprehensive descriptive overview of hospital governance and managerial environments in Romanian public hospitals.

Discussion

Key findings

This study provides empirical insights into the relationships between managerial attributes, hospital characteristics, and accreditation performance in Romanian public hospitals. The key findings identify meaningful patterns and associations that contribute to a more nuanced understanding of how leadership-related and organizational factors co-vary with accreditation outcomes.

The results of the Chi-square test shown in Table 6 confirm that the field of study of hospital managers significantly influences accreditation scores (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X^2 = 45.13$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p < 0.001$$\end{document}). Managers with backgrounds in economics demonstrated a balanced distribution across accreditation categories, while legal professionals were concentrated in mid-level accreditation groups. Medical professionals had the highest representation in lower accreditation categories, suggesting that clinical expertise alone may not be sufficient for optimizing hospital accreditation outcomes. These findings emphasize the importance of interdisciplinary training in hospital administration, a point also emphasized by Asiabar and Ardestani [30], who identify leadership effectiveness as closely tied to cross-functional managerial competence in healthcare settings.

Kendall’s Tau correlation analysis revealed a weak but statistically significant negative correlation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau = -0.203$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.022$$\end{document}) between hospital size (measured by bed count) and accreditation scores. This suggests that larger hospitals may encounter greater challenges in achieving high accreditation performance. The finding aligns with organizational complexity theory, which argues that as institutional size increases, so too does bureaucratic inertia, coordination difficulty, and administrative burden. While larger hospitals may benefit from superior resources, these advantages may be offset by reduced organizational agility. Similar scale-related challenges have been documented in prior studies examining quality management systems in large healthcare organizations [4, 29].

The Kruskal-Wallis H test (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H = 18.69$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p < 0.001$$\end{document}) provided strong evidence that hospital administration type significantly influences accreditation scores. Public hospitals managed by Ministry of Health, often constrained by regulatory burdens and financial limitations, tended to have lower accreditation scores compared to hospitals managed by city or county councils. These findings align with Iancu and Nica [16], who describe the operational constraints faced by Romanian public hospitals, particularly those linked to centralized governance and rigid administrative procedures.

Cluster analysis revealed a strong association between team motivation, team performance, and accreditation outcomes, with hospitals characterized by highly engaged teams consistently achieving higher accreditation scores. While these results should be interpreted cautiously given the exploratory nature of the analysis, they underscore the importance of internal organizational processes in shaping institutional performance. This pattern aligns with transformational and participatory leadership theories, which emphasize the role of leaders in fostering engagement, collaboration, and shared commitment to organizational goals [20, 26]. Prior research similarly links supportive leadership styles and positive organizational culture to improved team dynamics and institutional effectiveness in healthcare settings [19, 21, 57].

Ordinal regression analysis using Kendall’s Tau (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p > 0.05$$\end{document}) found no statistically significant relationship between structured access to performance data and accreditation scores. The absence of a meaningful correlation suggests that while information transparency may contribute to overall hospital governance, it does not directly determine accreditation success. This interpretation is consistent with Crema and Verbano [28], who argue that data transparency must be coupled with organizational capacity for interpretation and action in order to support quality improvement.

Multivariate analysis indicates that managerial education, leadership-related variables, team motivation, and hospital size jointly contribute to variation in accreditation outcomes. Notably, managerial training and higher education level exhibited negative associations with accreditation category. Rather than implying a detrimental effect of education per se, this counterintuitive pattern may reflect a contextual misalignment between formal management training and the practical competencies required for effective accreditation governance. In the Romanian public hospital context, management education often emphasizes regulatory compliance, administrative procedures, and formal planning models, while placing comparatively less emphasis on adaptive leadership, interdisciplinary coordination, and process-oriented quality improvement [16]. These findings highlight the institutional specificity of accreditation determinants and suggest that leadership development programs may require recalibration to better support accreditation-related organizational processes, echoing broader arguments on the alignment between training content and healthcare leadership effectiveness [21].

Practical implications for healthcare policy

The findings of this study suggest several practical implications for healthcare policy aimed at strengthening hospital accreditation performance and organizational governance in Romanian public hospitals. These implications should be interpreted cautiously, as the cross-sectional design identifies systematic associations rather than causal effects. Nevertheless, the observed patterns provide insight into institutional mechanisms through which managerial and organizational factors may influence accreditation outcomes.

The negative association between managerial education and formal training and accreditation performance points to a potential misalignment between existing leadership development programs and the practical demands of hospital governance. Rather than indicating that education or training is detrimental, this pattern suggests that many current programs may overemphasize regulatory compliance and administrative procedures at the expense of adaptive leadership, interdisciplinary coordination, and process-based quality improvement. Consistent with Asiabar and Ardestani [30], leadership development initiatives should be redesigned to better reflect contextual realities and the evolving managerial role in public hospitals. Policymakers may therefore consider supporting executive development programs that integrate clinical, strategic, financial, and human resource management competencies, while recognizing that improved training content alone may be insufficient without complementary organizational reforms [19].

The observed negative association between hospital size and accreditation performance suggests that larger institutions may face structural constraints related to organizational complexity, coordination demands, and administrative inertia. While this relationship does not imply that size itself causes lower accreditation outcomes, it highlights the need for accreditation frameworks and support mechanisms that are sensitive to scale-related challenges. As noted by Brzostek and Domagała [29], managing complexity in large healthcare organizations requires differentiated evaluation approaches and targeted support. Policymakers could therefore consider tailoring accreditation requirements or providing additional administrative and financial resources to high-volume hospitals to facilitate effective implementation of quality standards.

The positive association between team motivation and accreditation outcomes underscores the importance of internal organizational processes in shaping institutional performance. Although causality cannot be established, the findings suggest that hospitals characterized by higher levels of staff engagement may be better positioned to coordinate accreditation-related activities. Participatory and supportive leadership approaches that encourage communication, feedback, and collaboration have been linked to improved organizational climate and service quality in prior research [21, 57]. From a policy perspective, this highlights the potential value of initiatives that promote workforce engagement, leadership development focused on relational competencies, and incentive structures aligned with collective performance goals.

Variation in accreditation outcomes across hospital administration types further indicates that governance arrangements are associated with institutional performance. Hospitals operating under centralized Ministry of Health oversight may experience reduced managerial autonomy and procedural rigidity, which can constrain responsiveness to accreditation requirements. As emphasized by Iancu and Nica [16], excessive centralization may limit adaptive management. Policymakers could therefore explore governance reforms that streamline administrative processes, enhance local decision-making capacity, and link resource allocation more closely to performance indicators, while remaining mindful of broader regulatory constraints.

Although structured access to performance data was not significantly associated with accreditation outcomes in this study, this finding should not be interpreted as diminishing the importance of data-informed management. Instead, it suggests that data availability alone may be insufficient to influence accreditation performance in the absence of effective interpretation and integration into managerial decision-making. As Crema and Verbano [28] argue, performance information must be embedded within organizational routines and feedback mechanisms to support continuous improvement. Accordingly, policy efforts should focus not only on expanding data infrastructure but also on strengthening hospitals’ capacity to translate performance information into actionable governance and quality improvement strategies.

Future research integrating patient-level outcome measures would be necessary to establish whether these organizational characteristics translate into measurable improvements in clinical care.

Limitations

While this study provides valuable insights into the relationship between managerial attributes, hospital characteristics, and accreditation performance, several limitations should be acknowledged.

This research is based on a cross-sectional dataset that captures data at a single point in time. While prior studies emphasize that leadership and management practices are dynamic and evolve in response to institutional challenges and reforms [20, 21], our design does not allow for analysis of changes over time or causal inference. As accreditation outcomes and managerial strategies are likely to evolve alongside ongoing structural and organizational reforms in Romania [6], future research could employ longitudinal designs to better capture the temporal evolution of these variables.

The study relies on self-reported survey data provided by hospital managers, which may be subject to reporting bias. Respondents may overstate management effectiveness or under report institutional weaknesses, particularly given the reputation implications associated with accreditation outcomes [15]. This concern is consistent with prior evidence showing that accreditation status is closely linked to perceptions of leadership effectiveness and institutional legitimacy [4, 11]. Future studies could strengthen data validity by triangulating self-reported measures with objective performance indicators, external evaluation reports, or patient-centered outcome measures.

Although the analysis includes a range of managerial and institutional characteristics, it does not capture all contextual factors that may shape accreditation performance. Regulatory interventions, financial constraints, political influence, and regional administrative capacity may also play a significant role in shaping institutional outcomes. While leadership style and participative management have been shown to influence organizational culture and performance [26, 57], these factors operate within broader policy and governance environments. Future research should incorporate macro-level variables—such as funding mechanisms, policy reforms, and external oversight structures—to develop a more comprehensive explanatory model of accreditation determinants.

The study does not incorporate patient-centered outcomes, including patient satisfaction, perceived care quality, or clinical health improvements. This omission was intentional, as these dimensions are examined in a separate, complementary study conducted by the authors, which focuses on patient-reported experiences and outcome measures. The separation of these analyses was intended to preserve conceptual clarity and methodological rigor by examining managerial and institutional processes independently from patient-level outcomes, while recognizing that these domains are closely interconnected.

Leadership style and team motivation were measured using survey-based indicators, which may not fully capture the complexity of interpersonal dynamics and informal organizational practices that influence accreditation processes. While transformational and participative leadership styles have been associated with improved engagement and organizational learning [19, 22, 23], their implementation varies substantially across institutional contexts. Qualitative approaches—such as interviews, focus groups, or case studies—could provide richer insight into how leadership practices are enacted in practice and how they shape accreditation performance.

The study focuses exclusively on Romanian public hospitals, which enables in-depth contextual analysis but limits generalizability to private healthcare providers or health systems operating under different governance arrangements. As noted by Iancu and Nica [16], Romanian public hospitals face specific constraints related to centralized regulation and limited managerial autonomy. Comparative research across private institutions and international settings would be valuable in assessing the extent to which the observed relationships generalize beyond this context.

An important limitation of this study relates to the reliance on self-reported data provided by hospital managers. As respondents evaluate their own practices and organizational processes, the results may be subject to social desirability bias, whereby managers overreport positive behaviors or underreport challenges. In addition, the use of a single data source introduces the potential for common-method bias, which may inflate or attenuate observed associations between variables.

These factors may influence the strength and direction of the reported relationships and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Accordingly, the results reflect perceived organizational practices rather than objective measures of clinical quality or performance. Future research should incorporate multiple data sources, including objective clinical indicators, external evaluations, and patient-reported outcomes, to improve measurement validity and reduce potential bias.

Future research directions

Building on the findings and limitations of this study, several avenues for future research are suggested:

Future studies should adopt a longitudinal design to examine how managerial attributes, including formal leadership training, evolve over time and influence accreditation outcomes. Given the literature’s emphasis on the importance of ongoing professional development, adaptability, and strategic leadership [19–21], tracking managers over several accreditation cycles would allow for assessment of temporal sequencing and provide stronger evidence regarding the causal pathways linking leadership development to institutional performance.

Expanding research beyond Romanian public hospitals to include private institutions, mixed-administration models, and international healthcare systems could further clarify how governance structures and regulatory environments condition the relationship between leadership practices and accreditation outcomes. As noted by Iancu and Nica [16], limited managerial autonomy in Romanian public hospitals may moderate the effectiveness of leadership strategies; cross-system comparisons would help assess the generalizability of the observed associations.

Further research should explore how macro-level factors, such as funding allocation strategies, national healthcare reforms, and policy interventions, influence accreditation outcomes. Prior studies highlight the importance of external policy and financial frameworks in shaping institutional outcomes [4]. Incorporating these contextual variables would support more comprehensive, multi-level models of accreditation determinants.

While this study focuses on managerial and institutional characteristics, future research should explicitly examine how accreditation status relates to patient-centered outcomes, including perceived care quality, patient satisfaction, and clinical effectiveness. As leadership and organizational culture ultimately affect healthcare delivery at the patient level [11, 24, 26], integrating patient-reported and clinical outcomes would help assess whether accreditation-related governance processes translate into meaningful improvements in care.
