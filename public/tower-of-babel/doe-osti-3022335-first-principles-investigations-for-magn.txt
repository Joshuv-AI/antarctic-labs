5 A discussion of what was accomplished under these goals during this reporting
period, including major activities, significant results, major findings or conclusions, key
outcomes or other achievements. This section should not contain any proprietary data or

Research supported by DOE Grant DE-FG02-05ER46237 resulted in 130 publications in
peer-reviewed journals, including Nature, Science, Physical Review Letters, Science
Advances, Nano Letters, and Physical Review B. These works have received over 6,000
citations so far and provided important theoretical advances that helped guide
experimental exploration of magnetic and topological materials. The program established
quantitative links between atomic structure, electronic states, and magnetic interactions
across a broad range of systems, including diluted magnetic semiconductors, magnetic
thin films and interfaces, magnetic flux qubits, topological materials, and two-
dimensional magnets. By combining density functional theory, model Hamiltonians, and
multiscale simulations, the research developed predictive frameworks for understanding
how symmetry, spin–orbit coupling, lattice dynamics, and reduced dimensionality shape
emergent magnetic and electronic phenomena.

A major achievement of the program was the quantitative prediction of magnetic
anisotropy, exchange interactions, and spin–orbit–driven effects in nanoscale materials.
The work clarified how reduced dimensionality, symmetry breaking, and interfacial
coupling control magnetic order and spin textures, providing theoretical insight into non-

collinear magnetism, skyrmions, topological electronic states, and spin-dependent
transport. Early work elucidated the mechanisms of carrier-mediated magnetism in
diluted magnetic semiconductors, establishing how impurity states and carrier
concentration govern magnetic interactions. Subsequent studies predicted quantum spin
Hall states in graphene functionalized with heavy atoms and explored magnetism–
topology interplay in heterostructures, including proximity-induced gaps in topological
surface states and conditions for realizing the quantum anomalous Hall effect.

The program also advanced the understanding of two-dimensional van der Waals
magnets, spin–phonon coupling, and strain or electric-field control of electronic states.
Large-scale simulations using machine-learning potentials further clarified materials
behavior relevant to superconducting qubits, explaining why tantalum outperforms
niobium. Through collaborations with experimental groups and national laboratories, the
project enabled theory-guided interpretation of experiments and accelerated discovery in
quantum and spin-based materials. The grant also supported the training of graduate
students, postdoctoral researchers, and undergraduate researchers, contributing to
workforce development in computational materials science and condensed matter
physics.
