Roadmap to Support International Medical Graduates for Satisfying Rural General Practice Careers: A Realist Evaluation Approach
Authors: Belinda G O'Sullivan, Kim J Omond, Neysan Sedaghat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesDevelop a roadmap of contextualised strategies to support international medical graduates (IMGs) on the pathway into satisfying rural general practitioner careers in Australia.DesignRealist evaluation approach.Setting, ParticipantsOnline semi‐structured interviews, focus groups and intermittent feedback cycles developed, refined and confirmed a contextualised roadmap of strategies between 1 November 2024 and 27 July 2025. Participants were purposefully selected for IMG background and different pathways into rural general practice careers across Australia. This included a 10‐person project advisory group and 31 external participants covering decision‐makers, training teams, supervisors and trainees. Questions explored practical strategies to drive comfort, confidence, competence, belonging and bonding.Main Outcome MeasuresContextualised strategies for IMGs to achieve satisfying rural general practice careers.ResultsThe roadmap identified that when migrating and acclimatising, providing IMGs with centralised resources and information on rural general practice training and careers promotes comfort and empowerment. When moving to new workplaces and communities, providing IMGs with supportive workplaces for early supervised practice, skill bridging and opportunities to connect with other doctors', families and communities stimulates IMG confidence, competence and sense of community belonging. When training to become a general practitioner rurally, providing IMGs with family‐focused, equitable training matched to the IMG and the community, and training that builds on IMG capabilities, promotes a sense of professional belonging and bonding. We identified that early intervention and a continuity of supports are important for more comprehensively supported IMGs.ConclusionsOngoing timely support, when multi‐layered, tailored and integrated, may assist IMGs to gain specialist general practice qualifications, feel valued and to settle in rural general practice roles. The roadmap provides a basis for planning coordinated longitudinal support by distributed agencies.Keywords: general practice, health policy, professional education, rural health services

Introduction

Australia is heavily reliant on international medical graduates (IMGs), who are more likely to become general practitioners compared with locally trained doctors and twice as likely to work rurally, influenced by regulatory conditions, interest and the job market [1, 2]. IMGs make up about two‐thirds of all general practitioners in small rural communities, growing at a faster rate and contributing proportionally more full‐time equivalent hours than general practitioners of local origin [3, 4]. However, despite our reliance on IMGs, there is no national strategy supporting their pathway into rural general practice careers, which contributes to substantial system inefficiencies, lower satisfaction and increased risk of rural turnover [5, 6, 7]. Increased dependence on IMGs coincides with a lower rate of uptake of general practice careers by domestically trained medical graduates in Australia [8, 9]. There are many policies targeting Australia's rural general practice workforce; however, despite incurring a moratorium to work in areas of workforce shortages, specific supports for IMGs in rural general practice careers are under‐developed [10]. This undermines a stable and satisfied workforce for rural communities. It also places Australia in an awkward position relative to global standards expecting migrant health workers to be treated equitably [11].

Major Australian inquiries and independent reviews note that IMGs need more support [12, 13]. The Kruk review in 2023, an independent review of regulatory settings for overseas health practitioners, recommended faster processing of IMGs should be coupled with rural professional supports [13]. Subsequently, the Medical Board of Australia and Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency (Ahpra) implemented an expedited pathway for qualified general practitioners from specific countries to practise in areas of workforce shortages after shortened periods of supervised practice, without a clear support package [14]. Meanwhile, the Australian Medical Council (AMC) has done a cross‐sectional survey of IMG needs but not in relation to a specific professional endpoint like rural general practice careers, where supports need to be quite nuanced [15]. Equally, Australia has many programs and pathways for rural general practice careers, but with no clarity about what strategies matter, when and why, for satisfied IMGs.

Our research aimed to explore the diverse pathways and programs into rural general practice, what works, when and why to support IMGs into satisfying rural general practice careers and develop a roadmap of agreed strategies.

Methods

We drew from a realist evaluation approach using the RAMESES II reporting standards because this topic lacks contextualisation (Box 1) [16]. We engaged a broad research team of a PhD qualified realist evaluation researcher (BOS), general practice academics (KO and NS) and an IMG (NS)—none had prior relationships—to diversify insights. Participatory action research was used to involve end users relevant to informing and translating the findings [17]. A project advisory group of known experts in the industry were engaged through direct email invitation, and we purposefully engaged IMGs and external participants, including decision‐makers, training teams, supervisors and trainees across different rural training pathways into general practice careers to maximise participant diversity for developing and refining the roadmap [17, 18]. Table 1 and further description of each of the phases summarises the processes and dates.

Realist evaluation [16].

Our study used a realist approach rather than a full realist evaluation as we did not include an independent review of the theory. We applied all other aspects of the method.

Realist evaluation aims to describe how and why interventions, when delivered in certain contexts, can drive social and psychological responses in participants to produce outcomes. In this way, realist evaluation helps to understand what works in different scenarios.

These are referred to as contexts (C), intervention (I), mechanisms (M) and outcomes (O), respectively, frequently expressed as C + I + M = O or C(I)MO configurations. Configuring these patterns is the basis for generating theory (or in this case, a roadmap), as the product of realist evaluations.

Phase 1: Initial Roadmap (1 November 2024–16 February 2025)

In Phase 1, the research team developed an initial roadmap (or theory about what contextualised strategies could be effective for IMGs, and in what scenarios) informed by a literature and policy review (Table 1). Drawing on limited research available about IMG strategies, we postulated that effective pastoral support, tailored advice, resources and networking opportunities could drive comfort (to share and receive information), confidence (to practise safely and get real‐time help), competence (to build relevant skills), belonging (to a special professional group) and bonding (to other doctors and families) for satisfied IMGs [19, 20]. We also postulated that IMGs require support before specialist general practice training to develop self‐efficacy, manage stress and prepare for transformative learning [21]. Despite being a useful starting point, our initial roadmap lacked clear links between strategies (I), mechanisms (M) and outcomes (O), in the IMG context (C)—the C(I)MO patterns, which are part of realist evaluation. The question topics for Phase 2 aimed to explore these links to develop the theory and content of the draft roadmap (Table 1).

Phase 2: Draft Roadmap (26 February 2025–1 March 2025)

Developing the draft roadmap involved consulting our 10‐person project advisory group in Phase 2, who were emailed the study information and provided written consent in Google Forms. Two sessions of 2‐h online recorded focus groups were conducted via Zoom—organised based on convenience and to promote collaborative discussions for deeper insights. Broad questions were covered by the realist‐trained qualitative facilitator (BOS), promoting free‐flowing dialogue and interactive discussion centred around the question topics that aimed to develop the vision and content of the roadmap (Table 1). Most participants knew each other but offered different perspectives.

Full verbatim focus group data were transcribed using Turboscribe (https://turboscribe.ai), de‐identified and read by the whole research team who met weekly and worked online to draw out the C(I)MO patterns, which were applied to an updated version of the 3‐page draft roadmap. The draft roadmap included more specific strategies around three emerging contextual challenges of migrating and acclimatising, moving to workplaces and rural communities and specialising as a general practitioner in a rural area. It also provided stronger links showing how strategies in different contexts stimulated mechanisms and outcomes. Gaps in the explanatory aspects of the roadmap were identified and used to inform the Phase 3 question topics (Table 1).

Phase 3: Updated Roadmap (7 March 2025–23 May 2025)

Developing the updated roadmap in Phase 3 sought more granular contextualised strategies through online 2‐h focus groups and 1‐h interviews via Zoom with external participants beyond the project advisory group. Invitations with the study information were sent to key informants known to the research team and the project advisory group, with snowballed invitations. Eligible participants were decision‐makers, training teams, supervisors of IMGs and rural general practice trainees involved in IMG pathways. The research team reviewed the dataset of candidates to purposefully sample for maximum diversity of participants, inclusive of IMGs and different roles across programs and pathways for rural general practice careers [18]. All participants could nominate an interview and bring a support person. Interviews were used to reduce potential power imbalances between groups, such as supervisors and trainees, or for convenience. Most data collection was led by BOS for consistency and KO contributed to facilitating several focus groups with BOS. Free‐flowing dialogue was encouraged (Table 1) to promote deeper reflections about contextualised strategies. Participants were given the roadmap and questions a week in advance of their interviews. The questions were displayed on a PowerPoint slide. All participants received $125 per hour of time contributed.

Full verbatim data were transcribed, de‐identified and read in full by the research team as they were completed. To develop an updated roadmap, the C(I)MO patterns were considered as data were collected, building on the findings of Phases 1 and 2. The contextualised strategies became progressively more populated and nuanced until the research team felt there were different representations of the same patterns by the 31st participant. Data collection continued within the allowable budget, aiming to refine these patterns. The findings were layered to produce the updated 6‐page roadmap (a 1‐page high‐level summary and 5 pages of line listed strategies). Meanwhile, the research team continued deeper analysis of the raw data underpinning the C(I)MO patterns. The researchers reflected on the updated roadmap to set the questions needed to refine and confirm the roadmap in Phase 4.

Phase 4: Final Roadmap (1 June 2025–28 July 2025)

Phase 4 involved refining the updated roadmap by circulating the 6‐page roadmap to the phase 3 respondents via their registration email. They were invited to provide at least 1 h of desktop review time based around specific questions (Google Form), within 2 weeks, with two reminders. The feedback was de‐identified and assigned the same participant ID (date stamped), before being systematically reviewed against the data from Phases 1 to 3, to inform the deeper analysis of the roadmap content. The research team then agreed on actions including places to reduce duplication, improve clarity and temporally arrange the strategies. At this stage, the research team refined the C(I)MO patterns, aggregating and organising the material to accurately represent a theory applicable to any IMG, community and rural general practice pathway. This produced a 27‐page graphically designed roadmap of 3‐levels.

To refine and confirm the roadmap, the project advisory group was emailed the final version with reflective questions. They joined 2‐h‐long online recorded focus groups via Zoom. Full verbatim data were transcribed, de‐identified and read in full as the data came in. At this stage, the roadmap was vastly different from the Phase 2 version, so the review was relatively independent, but it deviated from traditional realist evaluations where theory is confirmed by external parties. All members were invited to mark up and email any further comments on the roadmap within a week of the consultation. The written feedback was applied to refine and confirm the content.

Ethics Statement

This project had ethics approval from the Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee (#44808), 11 November 2024.

Results

Overall, 189 people provided informed consent from which the 10 project advisory group members and 31 external participants contributed to about 57 h and 1140 pages of data across the project; 28 participants were IMGs, 19 were from Modified Monash Model (MMM) 4–7 and 10 from MMM2–3 [22] (Table 2). The aggregate participants covered 62 roles: 14 decision‐making; 15 operational training; 20 supervisors; and 13 IMG trainees. Main roles, rurality and IMG status are included in the sub‐text to quoted material (Table 3) to aid interpretation. Overall, < 5 participants provided additional feedback by email and phone in Phases 2 and 4; however, 22 of Phase 2 participants and 9 project advisory group members, respectively, participated in giving Phase 4 feedback. Those who did not respond were unable to do so within the time available due to other commitments.

Outcomes

The project advisory group indicated that satisfaction is strongly related to recognition of the strengths of IMGs within Australia's multi‐cultural medical workforce: ‘there's a tremendous contribution, I think, that internationals have … [in] a multicultural society’ (8_PAG_IMG_Reg). IMG satisfaction was also strongly linked with family satisfaction. The roadmap was considered valuable for building more clarity about the drivers of success: ‘so that there's a minimum understanding of what support looks like … for success’ (2_PAG_IMG_Rur), improving collective responsibility and efficiency: ‘We can't afford to be duplicating … we all need to have the same focus’ (1_PAG_not IMG_Reg) and ‘improving integrated support’ (2_PAG_IMG_Rur).

Contextualised Strategies

Respondents noted extensive contextualised strategies across the pathway to drive comfort, confidence, competence, belonging and bonding. The C(I)MO patterns expanded over time until researchers consolidated a representation of the findings, centred on early intervention by the end of Phase 3. At this point, it became clear that certain strategies more heavily weighted to drive the required responses and when.

In Stage 1, the IMGs context involves arriving and acclimatising to a new country, and strategies to provide them with resources and advice about life and rural general practice training and career options promote their comfort and empowerment.

In Stage 2, in the context of moving to new workplaces and communities, strategies need to enable supportive workplaces for supervised practice, skill bridging under quality supervision and opportunities to connect with other doctors, families and communities, to promote IMG confidence, competence and their sense of community belonging.

In Stage 3, in the context of training to become a specialist general practitioner in a rural area, strategies for equitable, family‐focused training that matches IMG and community needs and builds on IMG capabilities, triggers in IMGs a sense of professional belonging and bonding.

We also identified that early intervention and continuity of supports are required across the pathway. IMGs with limited holistic and early support in the context of migrating, were prone to choosing poorly tailored employment at later stages, hindering their progress in training: ‘So the IMGs often don't know what the practice really is like … I would prefer to match IMGs to practices before the [training program is] approved … there are too many of these relationships that go sour that shouldn't have ever started’ (22_TD_notIMG_Met). Rather, by promoting a continuous approach to driving comfort and confidence, competence and community belonging across the IMG pathway, not assuming things have been addressed at earlier stages, could achieve more satisfied IMGs at the stage of training to become a specialist rural general practitioner.

The outcomes of the roadmap were also confirmed at this point as achieving qualified, recognised IMGs with career opportunities, who feel settled as a family. The project advisory group also identified that although the roadmap is represented as linear stages, they are highly interactive. One example was that of IMGs moving workplaces many times, requiring that supports for moving to new workplaces needed to be re‐visited. The final roadmap was confirmed by the project advisory group, with minor wording changes and positive feedback as a comprehensive, clear, well‐designed, strength‐based, respectful and useful document: ‘This is the first framework that I know of which endeavours to enable and empower the IMG workforce … right from migration to successful completion of rural training pathway’ (19_sup_IMG_Reg). Others noted the roadmap was useful as a holistic reference point for IMG support in rural general practice: ‘IMGs may feel more supported and hopefully confident that the system is there to work with them and that their service is truly valued’ (23_TD_notIMG_Met). If implemented the roadmap was considered able to: ‘… create confident and successful IMGs contributing to the rural workforce and avoiding the creation of [a] 2‐tier [domestic and IMG] system’ (19_sup_IMG_Reg).

Discussion

This research produced the first known contextualised national strategy for IMGs to achieve satisfying rural general practice careers informed by the rural GP training sector. Its relevance and appropriateness were enhanced by using recurrent rounds of feedback and including IMGs and diverse participants. The final roadmap encompasses integrated strategies to support IMGs, providing a universal reference point for agencies to understand and co‐plan coordinated action across the IMG journey. This expands on other research about a single training pathway or stage [20] or a siloed issue, such as AMC exams [23] or themes about IMGs taken from the AMC survey [15]. Being solution‐focused and centred on the nuance of professional and personal integration in rural general practice, the roadmap provides the AMC with a rurally contextualised, practical tool to better engage and support work‐ready IMGs on moratoriums [24].

The roadmap could be used by the Australian Government's Health Workforce Taskforce to set an integrated national strategy for IMGs to fulfil the objectives of the Kruk review [13]. It could also be used by Rural Workforce Agencies, general practice colleges and employers in rural areas to collaborate around a collective strategy that bridges the challenging transitions into Australian rural general practice and rural living. It is the first integrated framework that hones in on supporting the transitions IMGs make between migratory, regulatory and training systems [14, 15, 25, 26].

It can help training practices to identify areas where IMGs need more personal, place‐based and professional supports as a precursor to improving satisfaction and progression in training. It can also better showcase IMG recognition, with our evidence showing that professional belonging is improved when individual strengths are identified. Supervisors are essential for IMG strengths to be fostered at the prevocational and vocational stages. The roadmap could be applied to developing a unified best practice supervision framework and tailored information and resources for IMGs. Unfortunately, there remains limited research to inform quality supervision and even less for IMGs and the roadmap prompts activity to fill this gap [20, 27].

IMGs are often the focus of criticism for not staying in rural areas. Critically, the roadmap can strengthen attention on the responsibilities of the sector for driving comfort and empowerment, confidence, competence, belonging and bonding to achieve retention, rather than blaming IMGs and seeing them as short‐term workforce. Our research suggests that IMGs have the capacity to contribute to the national agenda of strengthening primary care access in rural communities.

Our research falls short of exploring the full roles and responsibilities for implementing the roadmap strategies. Generative artificial intelligence provides new opportunities to identify resource and information gaps aligned with roadmap strategies; however, it should be supplemented by personal approaches so that IMGs develop a sense of belonging to the profession and community and bonding to other doctors.

Limitations

Participation was capped by the available funding, but we achieved saturation of the strategies and confirmation of the findings, supplemented by multiple rounds of consultation and feedback. The roadmap represents a high‐level systems approach that requires adaptation to individual programs, cohorts and contexts, perhaps setting the scene for ongoing strategy and operational activity. The roadmap needs further testing in practice to confirm its relevance.

Conclusion

This research produced the first national roadmap of contextualised strategies to support IMGs to achieve satisfying rural general practice careers. It identified the importance of early intervention and ongoing support addressing the context of IMG migration, moving communities and training as a specialist general practitioner in rural areas. The strategies can be progressively layered to ensure IMGs are holistically supported, particularly nuancing this for a rural general practice work and training context. The roadmap provides a unique basis for planning more comprehensive and coordinated action across the distributed agencies involved in the IMG pathway.
