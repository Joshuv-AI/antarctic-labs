Optimal State Preparation for Impulse Estimation in Gaussian Quantum Systems This research was funded in part by the Austrian Science Fund (FWF) [10.55776/COE1; 10.55776/PAT9140723; 10.55776/P36236].
K. Schmerling, A. Kugi, A. Deutschmann-Olek

Abstract
We present an optimal control-based strategy to enhance the estimation of impulse-like disturbances in continuously monitored linear classical and quantum systems by exploiting non-equilibrium states. Using optimal estimation techniques for linear Gaussian systems to collect information from the temporal vicinity of the disturbance, we cast the minimization of disturbance estimation uncertainty as a nonlinear optimal control problem over time-dependent system parameters. The resulting method dynamically shapes the estimation covariances through parametric modulation, maximizing information gain at a known impulse time. This differs fundamentally from conventional squeezing protocols using periodic modulation that effectively degrade inference of impulse-like disturbances. Applied to nanomechanical resonators and levitated nanoparticles, optimal parametric driving reduces estimation variance by up to a factor of two relative to steady-state operation.



keywords

Stochastic Systems, Open Quantum Systems, Optimal Control, Optomechanics

1
Introduction

Improving the sensitivity of measurement devices by exploiting non-equilibrium effects in their dynamics is an active research topic across various fields, such as nanoelectromechanical systems (NEMS), quantum optomechanics, trapped ions, and superconducting circuits (
24
;
14
;
10
;
7
). Many of these systems are well described as stochastic linear dynamic systems (
17
;
12
;
19
) with dynamically adjustable parameters, e.g., by external modulation of the system’s resonance frequency or damping.
It is well known that dynamic manipulation of these parameters can influence second-order statistics, which allows a significant enhancement of measurement sensitivity, e.g., using thermomechanic (
20
) or quantum (
6
;
28
) squeezing. However, using such non-equilibrium effects requires to control the nonlinear behavior in these second-order statistics, which makes their analytical treatment challenging.

A particular class of sensing problems involves the detection and estimation of jump processes in continuous systems, in which the system undergoes an instantaneous change in magnitude at a discrete point in time due to an external disturbance (
27
). Examples include frequency jumps induced by nanoscale mass additions in NEMS resonators (
21
;
22
) or momentum kicks imparted to levitated nanoparticles through gas collisions (
17
,
4
). Recently, it has been shown that the disturbance causing such instantaneous changes can be effectively estimated from time traces in the vicinity of the disturbance event using Kalman–Bucy filtering and smoothing techniques, which are well suited for classical linear Gaussian dynamics (
23
). However, it remains unclear how to (optimally) use non-equilibrium effects due to parametric driving and whether existing methods using squeezed states of motion are suitable for this specific scenario.

Hence, the present work extends methods introduced in (
23
) to Gaussian open quantum systems while incorporating non-equilibrium effects to further enhance their sensitivity to impulse-like disturbances. To achieve this, we use the fact that for quantum Gaussian systems, their complete statistical behavior can be explicitly described by the Kalman–Bucy forward and backward filter equations. We then formulate the desired enhancement in the precision of a specific measurement quantity as a nonlinear optimal control problem, which can be efficiently solved using modern optimization techniques. Finally, we illustrate the proposed procedure to enhance the estimation of momentum kicks imparted on two state-of-the-art mechanical sensing devices: classical NEMS resonators and optically levitated nanoparticles in the quantum regime.

2
Open Quantum Systems

The time evolution of a continuously monitored quantum system is given by the stochastic master equation (SME), which extends the Lindblad master equation for open quantum systems by incorporating continuous information gained through measurement and the corresponding measurement back-action into a conditional state described by the density matrix
\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}(t)
.
In the following we set
\hbar=1
for simplicity. For a system with Hamiltonian operator
\hat{H}
and a set of collapse operators
\hat{L}_{i}
corresponding to different interaction channels between the quantum system and its environment, the SME can be written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}(t)=

\displaystyle-\left(i\left[\hat{H}(t),\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}(t)\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{D}[\hat{L}_{i}(t)]\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}(t)\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sqrt{\eta_{i}(t)}\mathcal{H}[\hat{L}_{i}(t)]\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}(t)\mathrm{d}W_{i}(t),

(1)

where the first term describes the deterministic evolution of the quantum state, and the second term accounts for the stochastic disturbance introduced by continuous measurements represented by the Wiener increments
\mathrm{d}W_{i}
with
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{d}W_{i}\right]=0
and
(\mathrm{d}W_{i}\mathrm{d}W_{j})=\delta_{ij}\mathrm{d}t
. The superoperators acting on the density matrix
\mathcal{D}[\hat{L}]
and
\mathcal{H}[\hat{L}]
have the form

\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{D}[\hat{L}]\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}&=\hat{L}\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}\hat{L}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L}\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}+\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L})\\
\mathcal{H}[\hat{L}]\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}&=\hat{L}\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}+\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}\hat{L}^{\dagger}-\text{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}(\hat{L}+\hat{L}^{\dagger})\right]\end{array}

(2)

and account for the unobserved and observed interaction of each channel, see
18
.

The state
\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}(t)
above is conditioned on the continuous (homodyne) measurement outcomes, i.e.,

\mathrm{d}Y_{i}(t)=\sqrt{\eta_{i}(t)}\text{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}(t)(\hat{L}_{i}(t)+\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{i}(t))\right]\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W_{i}(t),

(3)

with the so-called measurement efficiencies
\eta_{i}\in[0,1]
.
A key distinction between quantum and classical dynamics is that any extraction of information from a quantum system inherently disturbs that system, which imposes a fundamental limit on the achievable knowledge of its state. To describe this one can introduce the measurement rates
\kappa_{i}=||\hat{L}||_{o}
defined as some suitable operator norm
||.||_{o}
of the collapse operators. These specify the speed at which information is extracted and, consequently, the strength with which the measurement disturbs the system. The efficiencies
\eta_{i}
specify the fraction of extracted information that is accessible to the observer from each channel.

3
Gaussian state Evolution

Analytic solutions to stochastic master equations are generally intractable; however, one important class of exceptions is formed by systems with Hamiltonians at most quadratic of the form

\hat{H}(t)=\frac{1}{2}{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{H}(t)\hat{\mathbf{x}}+{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{d}(t)+\mathbf{d}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{\mathbf{x}},

(4)

where the
2n
-dimensional vector
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
collects all canonical conjugate operator pairs of a system with
n
bosonic modes, i.e.,
{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{T}}=({\hat{\mathbf{q}}}^{\mathrm{T}},{\hat{\mathbf{p}}}^{\mathrm{T}})
satisfying the canonical commutation relation
[\hat{\mathbf{x}},{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{T}}]=i\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
denotes a symplectic matrix (
9
). Further,
\mathbf{d}(t)
is a vector and
\mathbf{H}(t)
is a symmetric matrix of appropriate dimensions.
Additionally, we assume that the collapse operators are linear operators of the form
\hat{L}_{i}={\mathbf{b}}^{\mathrm{T}}_{i}(t)\hat{\mathbf{x}}
where
\mathbf{b}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}
.
Such systems are populated by Gaussian states (see
1
) which are fully described by a Gaussian phase space distribution (i.e., their Wigner function). Gaussian states can be fully characterized by their first and second statistical moments: the mean vector given by
\mathbf{x}(t)=\text{Tr}\left[\hat{\mathbf{x}}\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}(t)\right]
and covariance matrix given by
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(t)=\text{Tr}\left[\frac{1}{2}\{(\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}),{(\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x})}^{\mathrm{T}}\}\boldsymbol{\hat{\rho}}(t)\right]
with anticommutator
\{\}
.
The uncertainty principle is enforced through the Robertson–Schrödinger matrix inequality (
25
)

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\frac{i}{2}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\succeq 0.

(5)

We will replace
\mathbf{x}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
with
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}
and
\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
to indicate that they are conditioned on past measurements and evolve forward in time. We furthermore omit all explicit time dependencies for readability. Inserting the quadratic Hamiltonian (
4
) and the linear collapse operators
\hat{L}_{i}={\mathbf{b}}^{\mathrm{T}}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{x}}
into the SME (
1
) and the measurement equation (
3
) yields the evolution of the mean

\mathrm{d}\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{A}\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{d}t+(\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{T}}-{\mathbf{N}}^{\mathrm{T}})\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w},

(6)

initial condition
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}(0)=\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
. Here
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}
is called the innovation processes that can be expressed using the differential measurements increment as
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}=\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{C}\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{d}t
. Analogously, the evolution of the covariance matrix obeys the well-known (deterministic) differential matrix Riccati equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\mathbf{A}\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}+\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{T}}+\mathbf{Q}-(\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{T}}-{\mathbf{N}}^{\mathrm{T}})\boldsymbol{\eta}{(\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{T}}-{\mathbf{N}}^{\mathrm{T}})}^{\mathrm{T}},

(7)

which evolves forward in time with symmetric positive definite initial condition
\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(0)=\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}\succ\boldsymbol{0}
. The dynamics of the first and second moments above are related the SME description by the real matrices

\begin{array}[]{cclccl}\mathbf{A}&=&\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\mathbf{H}+\text{Im}\left(\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^{\dagger}\right)),&\mathbf{C}&=&2{\text{Re}\left(\mathbf{b}\right)}^{\mathrm{T}},\\
\mathbf{Q}&=&\boldsymbol{\Omega}\text{Re}\left(\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^{\dagger}\right){\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\mathrm{T}},&\boldsymbol{\eta}&=&\text{diag}\left(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{m}\right),\\
\mathbf{N}&=&\boldsymbol{\Omega}\text{Im}\left(\mathbf{b}\right),&&&\end{array}

(8)

see also (
29
).
These equations are of the same form as the classical Kalman filter, even though they were derived in a different setting (
13
;
8
). Unlike in the classical case, the uncertainty principle in quantum systems due to (
5
) is enforced by the specific form of
\mathbf{Q}
,
\boldsymbol{\eta}
and
\mathbf{A}
in (
8
).

One can also construct an operator
\hat{E}
conditioned on measurement outcomes at future times called the effect operator, which can be interpreted as an effective positive operator-valued measure (POVM) at a past time, see
15
.
The effect operator’s evolution equations are given as the adjoint of the conditional state equation (
1
)-(
3
), propagating backward in time with
\tau=T-t
from some final time
T
.
If we again make the assumptions (
4
)-(
5
), the effect operator is also Gaussian and the evolution of its mean
\overleftarrow{\mathbf{x}}(\tau)=\frac{\text{Tr}\left[\hat{\mathbf{x}}\hat{E}(\tau)\right]}{\text{Tr}\left[\hat{E}(\tau)\right]}
where
\hat{E}(\tau)
is explicitly normalized as it does not necessarily preserve trace. Then its dynamics yields,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{x}}=-\mathbf{A}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{x}}+(\overleftarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{T}}+{\mathbf{N}}^{\mathrm{T}})\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w},

(9)

which is the analog to (
6
) derived for reversed time with terminal condition
\overleftarrow{\mathbf{x}}(0)=\overleftarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
and
the backwards innovation process being
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}=\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{C}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{d}\tau
. Equivalently, the backwards evolution of the second-order moment becomes

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\overleftarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=-\mathbf{A}\overleftarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-\overleftarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{T}}+\mathbf{Q}-(\overleftarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{T}}+{\mathbf{N}}^{\mathrm{T}})\boldsymbol{\eta}{(\overleftarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{T}}+{\mathbf{N}}^{\mathrm{T}})}^{\mathrm{T}},

(10)

with the covariance matrix of the backwards effect operator
\overleftarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\tau)=\text{Tr}\left[\frac{1}{2}\{(\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\overleftarrow{\mathbf{x}}),{(\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\overleftarrow{\mathbf{x}})}^{\mathrm{T}}\}\hat{E}(\tau)\right]
with positive definite terminal condition
\overleftarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(T)=\overleftarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{T}\succ\boldsymbol{0}
, cf.
29
. This procedure is also referred to as state retrodiction or retrofiltering in the physics literature.

By combining forward and backward filtering at some time instance
t_{p}
, one can arguably extend classical state smoothing into the quantum domain. However, this procedure naively yields state estimates that are not Hermitian in general and can violate the uncertainty principle, see
11
;
16
. In our case, however, we aim to estimate an external Dirac-like disturbance, instantaneously changing the state at time
t_{p}
. We can thus use forward filtering and backward retrodiction techniques independently to estimate the states immediately before and after
t_{p}
.

4
Optimal Impulse Estimation

Suppose that a Dirac-like impulse
\alpha\boldsymbol{\delta}(t-t_{p})
with unknown magnitude
\alpha
acts on the Gaussian quantum system at a known time
t_{p}
, as an external coupling, instantaneously changing its first moment
\mathbf{x}
by an offset
\Delta\mathbf{x}
. We assume that
\Delta\mathbf{x}
maps linearly to the impulse magnitude with
\alpha\propto{\mathbf{l}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\mathbf{x}
whereby
\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
with
||\mathbf{l}||_{2}=1
specifies the direction of the impulse along the canonical quadratures. For example, the magnitude of an external momentum kick is directly proportional to the momentum displacement of the observed system.

Building on the concepts of forward and backward filtering introduced above, we now address the optimal estimation of Dirac-like impulse disturbances from measurement data
\mathcal{Y}=\{\mathbf{y}(t)|t\in[0,T]\}
of a system described by (
7
) and (
10
), where the dynamic model
\mathcal{M}=\{\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{p}(t)),\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{p}(t)),\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{p}(t)),\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{p}(t)),\boldsymbol{\eta}(\mathbf{p}(t))|t\in[0,T]\}
is known. Further, without loss of generality, we assume that all matrices can be expressed via the time-dependent vector of parameters
\mathbf{p}(t)\in\mathcal{P}
. The set of admissible parameters
\mathcal{P}
is given by the specifics of the system considered, e.g., constraints on experimentally variable parameters.

The discontinuity, breaks the temporal correlation of the system across
t_{p}
, which makes model (
6
)-(
10
) invalid at that instant. Nevertheless, for the intervals
t\in[0,t_{p})
and
t=T-\tau\in[t_{p},T]
, the model remains valid, allowing the change
\Delta\mathbf{x}
to be estimated by separating the information before and after the impulse into two datasets:

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{Z}_{1}=\{\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{M}|t\in[0,t_{p})\},\\
\mathcal{Z}_{2}=\{\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{M}|t=T-\tau\in[t_{p},T)\}.\end{array}

The change in the first moment at
t_{p}
is then given by

\Delta\mathbf{x}(t_{p})=\mathbf{x}(t_{p})|\mathcal{Z}_{1}-\mathbf{x}(t_{p})|\mathcal{Z}_{2},

(11)

where
\mathbf{x}(t_{p})|\mathcal{Z}_{i}
denotes the first moment conditioned on dataset
\mathcal{Z}_{i}
.

The conditioned first moment
\mathbf{x}(t_{p})|\mathcal{Z}_{1}
depends only on past data and is obtained from (
6
)–(
7
), which gives the forward mean
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{1}}(t)
with covariance
\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{1}}(t)
for
t\in[0,t_{p})
. Its limit as
t\to t_{p}
provides the conditioned estimate,
\mathbf{x}(t_{p})|\mathcal{Z}_{1}=\lim_{t\to t_{p}}\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{1}}(t)
. The effect of the initial condition
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}(0)
and
\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(0)
decays exponentially and becomes negligible for sufficiently long data records.

Conversely,
\mathbf{x}(t_{p})|\mathcal{Z}_{2}
depends only on future data and is obtained by solving the backward equations (
9
) and (
10
) for
T-\tau\in[t_{p},T]
, yielding the backward mean
\overleftarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{2}}(t)
and covariance
\overleftarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{2}}(t)
, with
\mathbf{x}(t_{p})|\mathcal{Z}_{2}=\overleftarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{2}}(t_{p})
. Assuming no prior information about the impulse, the uncertainty in the estimate is fully determined by the forward and backward filters, and the error covariance of the impulse estimate is given by

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Delta\mathbf{x}}(t_{p})=\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{1}}(t_{p})+\overleftarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{2}}(t_{p}),

(12)

assuming that the forward and backward estimates are conditionally independent (
23
).

4.1
Covariance Minimization

As previously noted, the system matrices in (
7
) and (
10
) can be fully time dependent via some parameter vector
\mathbf{p}(t)
. If these time-dependent parameters can be externally controlled, they provide additional degrees of freedom to enhance measurement sensitivity. For Gaussian states, maximizing the information about a quantity is equivalent to minimizing its variance. Therefore, to maximize the sensitivity with respect to the impulse’s magnitude
\alpha
, we seek to minimize the combined forward and backward covariance at the impulse time
t_{p}
in accordance with (
12
). This leads to the following optimization problem

\begin{array}[]{ccl}\min\limits_{\mathbf{p}(t)}&&{\mathbf{l}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Delta\mathbf{x}}(t_{p})\mathbf{l}+\gamma_{\text{reg}}\int_{0}^{T}||\mathbf{p}(t)||_{2}\mathrm{d}t\\
\text{s.t.}&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}&=\mathbf{f}(\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}},\mathbf{p}(t))\quad\text{from }\eqref{eqn:fwrd_riccati},\\
&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\overleftarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}&=\mathbf{g}(\overleftarrow{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}},\mathbf{p}(\tau))\quad\text{from }\eqref{eqn:back_riccati},\\
&&\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P},\end{array}

(13)

which minimizes the impulse estimation uncertainty along the direction
\mathbf{l}
of the canonical quadrature variables at time
t_{p}
. The covariance matrices are constrained by the Riccati dynamics of their respective filters, while the model parameters
\mathbf{p}
are restricted to the set of admissible parameters
\mathcal{P}
, and the cost function is extended with a regularization term with the norm
||.||_{2}
in the parameters and
\gamma_{\text{reg}}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Conceptually, this enhances sensitivity by shaping the covariance ellipse of the quantum state along the direction of the measured quadrature.

This concept is illustrated in Fig.
1
. Since the Riccati equations (
7
) and (
10
) are nonlinear in the covariances, the optimization problem is generally nonlinear and may be nonconvex, necessitating the use of numerical tools for non-convex optimization.

direction, e.g., for the case of a momentum kick.

5
Numerical Examples

In the following section, we investigate the estimation of momentum kicks on two representative systems commonly encountered in ultra-sensitive mechanical experiments: nanomechanical resonators (
20
), limited by thermomechanical noise and optically levitated nanoparticles (
17
) in the quantum regime. For these two simple systems it is sufficient to consider a single mode with single canonical quadrature pair
\hat{\mathbf{x}}={[\hat{q},\hat{p}]}^{\mathrm{T}}
. Further, we consider momentum kicks as impulse-like disturbances, i.e.,
\mathbf{l}={[0\quad 1]}^{\mathrm{T}}
.
Since numerical and experimental ensemble statistics of
\Delta\mathbf{x}
aligned exceptionally well with their theoretical covariances
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\Delta\mathbf{x}}(t_{p})
from (
12
) (see
23
), we omit individual stochastic trajectories due to space limitations.

5.0.1
Nanomechanical Resonators

Thermomechanical noise squeezing in NEMS resonators is a well-understood phenomenon that has been exploited to enhance the sensitivity of force measurements by dynamically shaping the noise statistics (
20
). A simple method to manipulate the second statistical moments of a mechanical oscillator is to vary its harmonic frequency
\Omega(t)
by changing its spring stiffness. Defining a time-varying frequency with a constant base frequency
\Omega_{0}
and a dimensionless modulation parameter
p(t)
as
\Omega(t)=\Omega_{0}(1+p(t))
. Furthermore, viscous damping acts on the system via the damping rate
\Gamma
. We can rewrite the time-varying model of a classical NEMS resonator in the quantum Gaussian framework (
6
)-(
8
) using

\begin{array}[]{clcl}\mathbf{A}=&\begin{bmatrix}0&\Omega_{0}\\
-\Omega_{0}(1+p(t))&-\Gamma\end{bmatrix},&\mathbf{C}=&\begin{bmatrix}\frac{q_{\text{zpf}}}{\sqrt{S_{\text{nn}}}}&0\end{bmatrix},\\
\mathbf{Q}=&\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&\frac{S_{\text{FF}}}{p_{\text{zpf}}^{2}}\end{bmatrix},&\boldsymbol{\eta}=&1,\quad\mathbf{N}=0,\end{array}

(14)

where the quadratures are scaled such that both are unit-less. We further assume that the NEMS resonator resides in a high-temperature thermal equilibrium with the surrounding bath as the dominant noise source, which acts on the resonator by a random force. Hence, the force noise and zero-point motions are given by

\begin{array}[]{clclcl}S_{\text{FF}}&=\frac{4k_{\text{B}}\Theta\Gamma}{m},&q_{\text{zpf}}&=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\Omega_{0}}},&p_{\text{zpf}}&=\sqrt{\hbar m\Omega_{0}}\end{array}

(15)

as defined by the fluctuation-dissipation theorem (
5
) for the high temperature regime (
k_{\text{B}}\Theta\gg\hbar\Omega
), where
\Theta
denotes the bath temperature and
k_{\text{B}}
is the Boltzmann constant and
\hbar
is the reduced Planck’s constant. The measurement noise PSD
S_{\text{nn}}
is given by the photon shot noise of an interferometric measurement (
20
).

5.0.2
Levitated Nanoparticle

The next example concerns a nanosphere confined in a harmonic potential formed by an optical tweezer, i.e., a focused laser beam.
The interaction between the particle and the light field encodes information about the particle’s position as phase modulation of the scattered light, which can be extracted via homodyne detection, while also introducing back-action noise to the particle. The system is well described as an open quantum harmonic oscillator under linear Gaussian measurement, and can therefore be analyzed using our framework. The trapping frequency is proportional to the square root of the laser power,
\Omega(t)\propto\sqrt{P(t)}
. Furthermore, the PSD of the back-action noise resulting from photon recoil is proportional to the laser power via the measurement rate constant
\kappa\propto P(t)
. Again, introducing the modulation as
P(t)=P_{0}(1+p(t))
, the quantum-optical system takes the form (
17
;
12
)

\begin{array}[]{clcl}\mathbf{A}=&\begin{bmatrix}0&\Omega_{0}\\
-\Omega_{0}\sqrt{(1+p(t))}&-\Gamma\end{bmatrix},&\mathbf{C}=&\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{8\kappa_{0}(1+p(t))}&0\end{bmatrix},\\
\mathbf{Q}=&\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&2\kappa_{0}(1+p(t))\end{bmatrix},&\boldsymbol{\eta}=&\eta_{\text{hom}},\quad\mathbf{N}=0,\end{array}

(16)

where the parameter
\kappa_{0}
is the steady state measurement rate for
p=0
. As the trapping laser simultaneously provides the confining potential and enables position readout with measurement efficiency
\eta_{\text{hom}}
, its power determines both the measurement strength and the associated back-action noise. The measurement noise is given by photon shot noise, which itself scales with laser power, see e.g.,
17
.

5.0.3
Solution of the Optimal Control Problem

Based on (
13
), we approximate the continuous-time optimal control problem (OCP) by a discrete-time formulation that can be solved using standard non-convex optimization solvers such as IPOPT (
26
), implemented in the optimal control framework CASADI (
2
). We use multiple shooting with piecewise-constant control inputs
p_{k}
and explicit fourth order Runge-Kutta schemes for forward and backward numerical integration of the differential Riccati equations (
7
) and (
10
).

Conclusion

This paper developed a method to find non-equilibrium protocols for open Gaussian quantum systems that maximize the information about impulse-like disturbances accessible via continuous measurements through tailored parametric modulation.
By coordinating forward filtering and backward retrodiction, the proposed control strategy minimizes the combined estimation variance at the impulse time and outperforms traditional squeezing schemes. In contrast to these approaches, the optimal non-equilibrium solution is explicitly tailored to a single inference time as required for impulse-like disturbances.
