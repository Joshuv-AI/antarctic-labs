Aseptic Failure of Tibial Components With Sleeves in Revision Total Knee Arthroplasty: A Novel Radiographic Assessment of Zonal Fixation
Authors: Eric L Colomb, Trey C Rogers, Barrett B Torre, Alex R Orem, Michael B Sparks, Wayne E Moschetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAseptic sleeve failure in revision total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is rare, and associated risk factors remain poorly understood. Shortcomings exist with current methods for assessing stem fill. We hypothesize that inadequate medial base plate support and undersized stems contribute to aseptic loosening and varus collapse of tibial components with sleeves.MethodsThis retrospective review evaluated TKA cases revised for aseptic loosening of tibial sleeves at a single academic center from 2011 to 2024. Stem-to-endosteal canal fill ratios of knee radiographs were measured using the Parsley method, with adequate fill defined >0.85. A novel area fill ratio was calculated along the sleeve and stem length using ImageJ software comparing implant area to total available area within the tibia. Medial base plate support was considered “unsupported” with gapping underneath the base plate >2 mm or with an excessive cement mantle.ResultsEighteen patients were revised for aseptic loosening of tibial components with sleeves, the majority (55.6%) demonstrating an unsupported medial base plate. Diaphyseal stem AP and lateral fill ratios using traditional Parsley method were undersized at 0.81 ± 0.13 (avg ± SD) and 0.77 ± 0.14, respectively. Using the area fill ratio method, the AP and lateral diaphyseal stems were more undersized measuring 0.74 ± 0.1 and 0.68 ± 0.11, respectively. Sleeve AP and lateral area fill ratios were 0.55 ± 0.1 and 0.51 ± 0.07.ConclusionsUnsupported medial base plates and undersized tibial stems may contribute to aseptic loosening in revision TKA with sleeves. Current stem fill assessments have limitations, whereas the stem and sleeve area fill ratio represents a novel technique for comprehensive measurement of implant-to-endosteal contact.Keywords: Aseptic, Revision, Tibia, Sleeve, Stem

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) has dramatically improved the treatment of severe knee joint osteoarthritis. TKA has led to a considerable increase in patients’ quality of life by relieving joint pain and restoring knee function [1]. Satisfaction rates have consistently remained high following the procedure thus successful outcomes have resulted in a marked increase in the incidence of TKA [[1], [2], [3]]. Projections suggest that by 2030, nearly 2 million TKAs will be performed annually [4]. However, with the ongoing increase in the number of procedures performed, the expanding demographic of younger, more active patients opting for TKA, and the growing life expectancy of living with the prosthetic joint, a substantial rise in revision TKA is expected. Compared to the 55,000 revision TKA performed in 2010, it is projected that the number of revision TKAs performed by 2030 will reach 268,200 cases annually [5,6].

The most common cause for revision following primary TKA is infection; however, aseptic loosening, malalignment, instability and periprosthetic fracture are common causes as well. [5,7] When compared to primary TKA, revision TKA presents additional complexities due to bone loss and soft tissue instability. Reconstruction of bony defects, balancing ligaments, and implant fixation to bone are the primary challenges surgeons face during revision TKA [7]. As a result, patients tend to experience extended hospitalizations, increased rates of perioperative and postoperative complications, and worse functional outcomes when compared to primary TKA [8]. Furthermore, the economic burden of revision TKA is staggering. With the cost of each revision being nearly double that of primary TKA and an increase in consumption of hospital resources due to the complexity of the procedure, the economic burden of revision TKA is projected to exceed $13 billion annually by 2030 [6,9].

To ensure a stable and long-lasting revision construct, Oh et al and Morgan-Jones et al [[10], [11], [12], [13]] have described the importance of obtaining secure fixation in at least 2 of the 3 anatomical zones: joint surface/epiphysis, metaphysis, and diaphysis. Metaphyseal sleeves (Depuy Synthes; Warsaw, Indiana) are frequently used in revision TKA due to their enhanced ability to obtain osseous integration in the metaphyseal anatomical zone, as well as their ability to conform to bony defects and address metaphyseal bone loss [12]. Metaphyseal sleeves may be indicated for patients with Anderson Orthopaedic Research Institute type 2A, type 2B, and type 3 defects [14,15]. Proper implementation of these sleeves reduce torsional and shear stress on the implants and create a stable scaffold for the femoral and/or tibial components. To achieve proper fixation, an appropriately sized sleeve should attain firm contact with the trabecular bone and compensate for metaphyseal bone loss. The surface of the sleeves have a high coefficient of friction to ensure long-term bone in-growth and stability; additionally, a morse taper junction is used to bond the sleeve to the implant which can be mated with a stem [16]. The mid- and long-term clinical outcomes tend to be successful and stable radiographic fixation is generally achieved [17,18]. The aseptic survivorship of metaphyseal sleeves has been reported in recent systematic review as 97.8% at mean follow-up 4.5 years [16]. Given the rarity of revision for aseptic loosening of revision TKA metaphyseal sleeves, the mechanism of failure is currently poorly understood.

Osseointegration of the porous sleeve surface requires rigid stability at the bone-implant interface. Excessive tensile or shear micromotion causes an unfavorable strain environment, stimulating fibrous membrane interposition and inhibiting bony ingrowth [19]. Stable fixation of the epiphyseal base plate and diaphyseal stem limit micromotion of the tibial component immediately upon implantation, providing a favorable microenvironment for the time-dependent osseointegration of the metaphyseal sleeve. Several prior studies reviewing primary TKA have identified undersized tibial implants, medial bone loss, and obesity as significant risk factors for varus collapse, advocating for the addition of stems of sufficient length to mitigate excessive forces at the medial joint surface [[20], [21], [22]]. Thus, aseptic sleeve failures may be explained by inadequate zonal fixation beyond that of the metaphysis.

We hypothesized that revision TKAs utilizing sleeve fixation loosen aseptically secondary to lack of fixation in 2 of 3 zones related to undersized stems and an unsupported medial base plate. The primary objective of this study is to describe a novel technique for thoroughly assessing tibial sleeve and stem fit using an area fill ratio method in the context of a rare cohort of patients with sleeved tibial components revised for aseptic loosening.

Material and methods

Upon receiving approval from the Institutional Review Board, our institutional data base was queried for all patients who underwent revision TKA from January 1, 2011 to January 1, 2024 by using current procedural terminology code 27487 (revision of TKA, with or without allograft; femoral and entire tibial component). This query yielded 1613 patients. All medical records and operative reports were retrospectively reviewed to determine the indication for revision surgery and the implant that was removed during the revision. Patients with a tibial metaphyseal sleeve (Depuy Synthes) removed for aseptic loosening were included; patients with revision surgery for infection, periprosthetic fracture, or if the sleeve was well fixed at the time of revision were excluded. Patient information collected included age, gender, and body mass index. The date of tibial metaphyseal sleeve implantation and later revision was recorded from which implant survival time (in months) was determined. Implant model and stem dimensions were recorded. Anterior-posterior (AP) and lateral knee radiographs obtained following index revision TKA were reviewed. Patients without postoperative radiographs within 1 year of index revision were excluded.

Twenty-one patients met initial inclusion criteria. Upon further review of operative records, 2 patients were excluded as the tibial sleeve components were found to be well-fixed requiring burr and osteotome for extraction, despite gapping under the base plate with a suspected diagnosis of aseptic loosening. One additional patient was excluded due to lack of postoperative radiographs within 1 year of index revision. Eighteen patients were ultimately included in the study analysis. The patients were 61.1% women, average age 63 (range 48-75) at time of index revision, with average body mass index 35.8 (range 24.1-43.7). (Table 1). The average survival of sleeved tibial components was 49 months prior to re-revision for aseptic loosening with an average patient age of 67 years at the time of sleeve revision. The model and size of explanted stems were available for 17 out of 18 patients. Of available data, stem length ranged between 60 and 75 mm. No stem was longer than 75 mm. A total of 94.4% (17/18) of tibial base plates used cemented fixation at the epiphysis. Negative intraoperative cultures confirming aseptic failures were available for 10 out of 18 patients. Preoperative aspiration yielding negative-culture results were available for an additional 5 out of 18 of the remaining patients. For 3 out of 18 patients where perioperative culture data were unavailable, synovial cell count and percent neutrophils was reported consistent with aseptic failure. Manual review of operative reports demonstrated no evidence of gross infection in all 18 cases (Appendix 1).

Diaphyseal stem fill was calculated using the method previously described by Parsley et al [23]. A stem canal fill ratio (CFR) was calculated comparing the distal stem tip diameter prior to stem taper to the endosteal diameter in both AP and lateral radiographs (Fig. 1). Cimen et al [24] describe that the lesser of the AP and lateral measurements most closely matches the cross-sectional area stem fill on computed tomography, thus this value is reported as well. A stem was considered appropriately sized with a CFR >0.85, as Lee et al [25] noted lower aseptic loosening rates in tibial components with stems when CFR >85%.

Sample Parsley stem fill ratio method of distal stem diameter (solid) to endosteal canal diameter (dashed).

To supplement this measurement, a novel technique was developed for measuring stem area fill ratio of the tibial stem in the diaphysis (Fig. 2). ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland) was used to manually trace out and quantify the total endosteal diaphyseal area (Fig. 2b). Subsequently, a grayscale pixel threshold was used in ImageJ to isolate the stem implant area from the surrounding endosteal canal (Fig. 2c). A ratio of the implant area to endosteal canal area was used to calculate the area fill ratio. Gililland et al [26] compared aseptic revision outcomes in cemented stems against diaphyseal engaging cementless stems with at least 4 cm of diaphyseal contact, finding both constructs to achieve reliable fixation. A target of 4 cm of cortical contact for cementless diaphyseal stems has been emphasized, yet previously described methods of assessing stem fill are focal to the stem tip and therefore insufficient. In our study, the stem area fill ratio was calculated over the distal most 4 cm designed for bony contact. A similar ImageJ technique was used to calculate a novel sleeve area fill ratio (Fig. 3a-c), which similarly has not been previously described. Values were reported as arithmetic mean ± 1 standard deviation.

Degree of medial base plate support was determined based on manual review of postoperative radiographs following initial revision TKA. Implants were graded as “unsupported” or “supported.” Implants were considered “unsupported” with gapping underneath the base plate (>2 mm) secondary to bone loss or with excessive cement mantle in place of implant augmentation. (Fig. 4) Implants were considered “supported” with gapping <2 mm between base plate or augment and tibial bone surface.

Results

The average diaphyseal stem AP and lateral fill ratios using Parsley’s method were 0.81 ± 0.13 (avg ± standard deviation) and 0.77 ± 0.14, respectively. Using the smaller of the AP and lateral ratios for each patient as described by Cimen et al the average diaphyseal stem fill ratio was 0.76 ± 0.14. Using the novel diaphyseal stem area fill ratio method over the distal 4 cm of the diaphyseal stem, the average AP and lateral area fill ratios were 0.74 ± 0.1 and 0.68 ± 0.11, respectively. Taking the lesser of the AP and lateral area measurements for each patient, the average was 0.67 ± 0.11. The average metaphyseal sleeve AP and lateral area fill ratios were 0.55 ± 0.1 and 0.51 ± 0.07, respectively (Fig. 5, Appendix 2).

An unsupported medial tibial base plate was present in 55.6% (10/18) of studied patients. Further analysis was performed assessing multizonal fixation of tibial stem and joint surface together. When using the Parsley method to assess stem fill, 33.3% (6/18) patients lacked fixation in both the diaphysis and the epiphysis. When using the area fill method, over half of patients (55.6%, 10 out of 18) lacked fixation in both the diaphysis and the epiphysis. (Table 2).

Discussion

Aseptic failure of tibial sleeves in revision TKA is a rare complication. In a recent study, Chalmers et al [27] reported on 249 tibial sleeves and found 99.5% 5-year survivorship free of revision for aseptic loosening. In another study by Bloch et al [17], 319 tibial sleeves were retrospectively reviewed with follow-up times ranging 2 to 10 years with zero reports of aseptic loosening. To our knowledge, our study of 18 patients represents the largest cohort of aseptic tibial sleeve loosening failures in the literature.

The majority of patients in this cohort (55.6%) had insufficient medial base plate support, suggesting a lack of adequate medial epiphyseal fixation. This may suggest that the use of tibial augments to support the medial base plate could be advantageous over leaving the base plate unsupported. However, augments may not be compatible with larger sleeve sizes, thus a larger tibial cut with a thicker poly or tray may be an alternative option to consider.

In addition, we noted inadequate diaphyseal stem fill, which in the setting of an unsupported medial base plate, may be less likely to resist varus collapse resulting in aseptic loosening. The majority of stems filled ˂85% of the endosteal canal which is considered undersized using the Parsley method.

A limitation of the Parsley method is that it provides a focal measurement at the distal tip of the stem; however, it does not account for a majority of the interface between the medullary canal and distal stem designed for bony contact wherein the value of our proposed novel area fill method lies. Given the taper of the tibial metaphysis, the proximal portion of the relatively uniform tibial stem frequently lacks cortical contact. This suggests the Parsley method overestimates stem fill when calculated at the stem tip (Fig. 6). Therefore, even a stem with a Parsley ratio ˃0.85 distally may have room to toggle and loosen within the medullary canal due to meta-diaphyseal mismatch, especially if the stem is not long enough to engage the true diaphysis of the tibia with more uniform diameter. The area fill ratio integrates the Parsley ratio over a desired stem length, capturing this mismatch. This was reflected in our study cohort, with an overwhelming majority of stems with Parsley ratios measuring larger than respective area fill ratios over the distal 4 cm. All included patients had stems with area fill ratios <0.85 when selecting the minimum value of AP and lateral views. All stems in our cohort were 75 mm or shorter, suggesting the use of longer stems to truly engage the diaphysis may improve stability by increasing the area fill ratio.

Given the taper of the tibial metaphysis, the proximal portion of the uniform tibial stem frequently lacks cortical contact with gapping shown in red (right). This suggests using the Parsley method at the stem tip overestimates stem fill compared to area fill method over portion of stem designed for bony contact. In this case, the Parsley ratio at stem tip (0.91) compared to area fill ratio over distal 4 cm (0.72).

Ferrer-Banus et al [28] recently reported on 67 tibial sleeves with 2 or more years of follow-up data, associating improved component alignment with longer stems and larger Parsley CFRs. While the authors reported an average tibial Parsley ratio of 68.4 ± 12.1% suggesting global stem under sizing similar to our published cohort, no instances of aseptic loosening were recorded. Given the rarity of this outcome, it is possible a larger patient cohort with longer follow-up was needed to capture aseptic failures. The authors also highlight an important limitation of implementing long stems with sleeves. The orientation of long stems are dictated by the geometry of the medullary canal and may lead to mal-positioning of the fixed angle base plate and sleeve attached via morse taper or under sizing of the stem [28].

To date no previous technique for reporting sleeve fill in the metaphysis has been reported. The metaphyseal sleeve area fill ratio (Fig. 3) represents a novel technique for quantifying fit in the metaphyseal zone of fixation. For these aseptic failures the sleeve fill was roughly 50% of the metaphysis. Metaphyseal sleeves are designed to integrate with trabecular bone to reduce torsional and shear stress, address bone loss, and distribute force across the entire bone-implant interface. Currently, there is a lack of literature to describe or measure optimal tibial sleeve sizing. Therefore, there is no target metaphyseal fill ratio at this time. This study serves to establish a method for describing metaphyseal sleeve fill, and to report values in the setting of aseptic loosing. It is assumed that if the sleeve achieves adequate stability, it should obtain ingrowth. In the setting of micromotion though, one would expect potential fibrous ingrowth and aseptic loosening. This study suggests that with an undersized stem or unsupported medial base plate the sleeve may be more prone to loosening. We hope that our metaphyseal sleeve area fill ratio will prove a useful tool in further exploring the relationship between sleeve fill and long-term implant survival.

There are several limitations of our retrospective study design. We recognize this study only reports on a small sample size of 18 patients but given the rarity of aseptic loosening in revision tibial components with sleeves we feel this cohort is sufficient. We do not know of another study quantifying sleeve area fill and hope this technique will provide insight into further investigation. We also have made an assumption that the surgeons at time of sleeve implantation had appropriate axial and rotational stability of the sleeve. Our hypothesis focuses on the lack of medial support and undersized stems which influences sleeve failure as we believe the success of sleeve osseointegration is likely influenced by fixation in the other 2 zones. It is also possible that our retrospective review may have missed some sleeve revisions during the study time period recognizing these are rare events. Additionally, data regarding metaphyseal defect size/Anderson Orthopaedic Research Institute classification at time of revision for our failed cohort were unavailable. Furthermore, culture-negative infections could have been missed and falsely identified as aseptic loosening. Finally, Parsley ratio and area fill calculations were performed manually by a single reviewer; therefore, further study is required to determine inter-rater reliability of this novel method. Having now defined the area fill ratio, the future direction of our study involves validating our hypothesis using a comparator group of patients having undergone revision TKA with sleeves that have performed successfully long term without evidence of aseptic loosening.

Conclusions

Improving revision implant survivorship by better understanding modes of implant failure is critical. Lack of medial base plate support and undersized tibial stems may play a role in aseptic loosening of revision TKA tibial components with sleeves. Care should be taken to maximize base plate support and tibial stem-to-endosteal contact area to achieve fixation in at least 1 if not 2 zones beyond the metaphyseal sleeve. Current methods for assessing stem fill have limitations, whereas a novel area fill ratio may provide a more comprehensive and objective measurement tool. Further investigation is needed to understand appropriate sizing of metaphyseal sleeves.
