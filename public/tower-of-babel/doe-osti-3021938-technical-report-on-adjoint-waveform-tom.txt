Abstract

We present a preliminary version of the East Asia Tomography (EAT) model, an adjoint

waveform tomography model of East and Southeast Asia. We used SPiRaL (Simmons et al.,

2021) as our starting model and source parameters for 250 earthquakes from the Global Centroid

Moment Tensor catalogue (Ekström et al., 2012). Over 198 iterations, we have iterated the EAT

model down to a minimum period of 35 seconds. We plan on continuing our iteration technique

down to 30 seconds period before updating our misfit function to use a normalized cross-

correlation-based misfit functions (e.g., Tao et al., 2018) to better constrain Earth structure. We

hope to iterate the current extent of the model to 25 seconds minimum period before iterating to

shorter periods for a smaller subregion of the full model.

Introduction

The Eurasian continent is a heavily studied region in seismic tomography due to the complex

tectonic processes that have shaped the region. East Asia hosts numerous subduction zones along

the Ring of Fire, spanning from the Kamchatka Peninsula in northeastern Russia to the

Philippine Islands near the Equator. In central Eurasia, the Indian subcontinent collides with

southwestern China, creating the Himalaya Mountains and the extrusion of the Tibetan Plateau to

the east. Intraplate volcanism in northeastern China has debated origins in the deep mantle or

from deep dehydration of the subducted Pacific Slab. These features and more have led to

extensive imaging studies of Eurasia.

While traditional imaging studies have used body wave tomography (e.g., Fu et al., 2023; Fukao

and Obayashi, 2013) and surface wave tomography (e.g., Li et al., 2013; Li et al., 2022; Liu and

Zhao, 2016; Yang et al., 2021), recent imaging studies of East Asia have focused on using adjoint

waveform tomography (AWT) to produce detailed models of the region (e.g., Liu et al., 2024;

Ma et al., 2022; Simute et al., 2016; Tao et al., 2018; Xi et al., 2024). Compared to traditional

techniques, AWT uses more of the seismic waveform by windowing data around certain phases

(e.g., Xi et al., 2024) or by windowing based on similarity between the observed and synthetic

data (e.g., Ma et al., 2022). AWT methods have been shown to improve waveform predictions

over traditional models (Rodgers et al., 2022) and produce more defined subsurface features,

particularly of subducting slabs (Rodgers et al., 2024).

In this preliminary study, we present a long period model of greater East and Southeast Asia.

Even at long periods, we see improvement in slab structure compared to SPiRaL, our starting

model, and reduce the misfit by 45% over 50 iterations. As we begin including shorter period

information, we aim to harness methodological advances of recent studies to improve subsurface

structure by considering relative amplitudes in our misfit function and to simulate seismic waves

through the ocean layer. Though these choices increase computational expense, we hope that

they will improve both waveform predictions and subsurface structure.

Data

Because of its tectonic complexity, East Asia experiences significant moderate-magnitude

seismicity. The Global Centroid Moment Tensor (GCMT) Catalogue (Dziewonski et al., 1981;

Ekström et al., 2012), a global moment tensor catalogue for events over Mw 5 includes over

2400 events between Mw5.0-7.0 between 1 January 2010 and 31 March 2021. Because using all

available events is infeasible, we spatially binned the available events. We used bins that are 100

km by 100 km laterally and 25 km in depth. Because events are scarce in the deep Earth, we

included all events with depths greater than 500 km. Binning the events reduced the number of

events to around 600. We simulated data for all binned events and picked windows for all events

using a modified version of the FLEXWIN algorithm (Maggi et al., 2009). If an event had less

than 20 stations contributing data or if less than 40% of stations contributed windows to an

event, they were removed from the dataset due to their limited data or poor data quality. After

quality control, we converged on a final dataset of 250 events (Figure 1). Data is downloaded

from the EarthScope Data Management Center using the Obspy MassDownloader tool. Around

800 stations from both permanent and temporary networks contributed data to the inversions.

Methods

We used Salvus (Afanasiev et al., 2019) to run inversions of the EAT model. Forward

simulations are run using the spectral element method (Komatitsch & Tromp, 1999; Komatitsch

& Vilotte, 1998). Simulations were run on Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory’s Lassen

high-performance computing system on up to twelve NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs. After we

calculated synthetics, we picked windows using a modified version of the FLEXWIN algorithm

(Maggi et al., 2009) proposed in Krischer et al. (2015). Windows were not restricted to given

phases; instead, data were windowed based on a variety of constraints on the similarity between

the observed and synthetic data (see Doody et al., 2023a for a detailed description). After

window picking, we weighted windows based on the event-dependent station weighting scheme

from Ruan et al. (2019). Station weights were calculated based on distance from the source and

station density in a given region (i.e., clustered stations are downweighted while isolated stations

are upweighted).

We calculated the misfit between the observed and synthetic data using the time-frequency phase

misfit function (Fichtner et al., 2008). The time-frequency phase misfit function first transforms

the time domain data into the time-frequency domain (Kristeková et al., 2006, 2009) then

calculates the weighted phase difference and envelope similarity. The time-frequency phase

misfit function is a phase-based misfit function, so relative amplitudes are not included in the

calculation of the misfit. We then computed sensitivity kernels for each event based on the

interaction of the forward and adjoint wavefields. The sensitivity kernels were smoothed

anisotropically, with 0.5 wavelength smoothing laterally and 0.2 wavelength smoothing

vertically. The smoothing length was chosen by Vsv wavelengths. To minimize source and

receiver effects, we set kernel values to zero around sources and receivers. The source/receiver

cutouts are spherical with 300 km and 75 km radii respectively.

Model updates were computed using the trust-region Limited-memory Broyden-Fletcher-

Goldfarb-Shanno (L-BFGS) algorithm, which is a quasi-Newton optimization algorithm (e.g.,

Conn et al., 2000; Nocedal and Wright, 2006). L-BFGS methods, and the trust-region L-BFGS

method particularly, provide significant computational speed-up over conjugate gradient methods

(Modrak and Tromp, 2016; Thrastarson et al., 2021). We used the trust-region L-BFGS method

of Boehm et al. (2018), which solves an auxillary optimization problem to minimize the

quadratic approximation of the misfit surface. In this approach, the size of model updates are

constrained by the ratio between the predicted and actual misfit reduction of a given model

update. Alignment between the predicted and actual misfit reductions allows us to make many

model updates and avoid being trapped in local minima. We iterated on the model until the

misfits could no longer be reduced; after an iteration failed to reduce the misfit with two target

models, we ended the iteration stage.

Discussion

We present the preliminary EAT model iterated to a minimum period of 35 seconds. In the 50-

150 second period band, we ran 50 iterations before convergence with a misfit reduction of 45%.

At 40-150 seconds, we ran 72 iterations with a total misfit reduction of 29%. Finally, at 35-120

seconds, we ran 76 iterations with a misfit reduction of 23%. Though the first few iterations of

any iteration stage have the largest misfit reductions, previous studies have shown that continued

iteration of the models reduces the likelihood of settling in a local minimum (e.g., Rodgers et al.,

2024).

Figure 2. Absolute and relative time-frequency phase misfit reduction over 198 iterations in three
period bands: 50-150 seconds, 40-150 seconds, and 35-120 seconds.

After 198 iterations, the EAT model shows improvements to large-scale structure compared to its

starting model, SPiRaL (Figure 3). Because the model only includes longer period data in its

current stage, we focus our analysis of the model on the upper- and mid-mantle. At upper mantle

depths (Figures 3b and 3e), EAT constrains slab structure across the Ring of Fire, improving the

continuity of imaged slab structure off the coast of Kamchatka, Russia, along the Ryukyu

Islands, Japan, and in the Bonin and Marianas Trenches from the slab images shown in SPiRaL

(Figures 3a and 3d). Below the 410km discontinuity, the edge of the subducted Pacific Slab

matches the values reported in Slab2.0 (Hayes et al. 2018; maroon dashed line, Figure 3h) and

extends the fast velocities of the Pacific Slab down the length of the Marianas Trench that are

lower absolute velocities in SPiRaL (Figure 3g). Finally, even though we are limited in the

number of deep earthquakes that occur, we still see updates to the deep structure of the model at

600 km (Figure 3l). The EAT model has faster absolute Vs values than SPiRaL under the Pacific

Ocean between the Bonin/Marianas Trench and the Philippines (Figure 3k), which corresponds

to the stagnated Pacific Slab in this region seen in other models (e.g., Fukao and Obayashi,

2013). Since we used the time-frequency phase misfit function, we mainly constrain surface

wave structure since surface waves have larger amplitudes and contribute more to the model

update as their contribution to the gradient is higher. We plan to change to a normalized cross-

correlation misfit function from Tao et al. (2017) at 30 seconds minimum period so that

windowed body wave data is weighted more heavily in the inversion. We expect that this will

help to further constrain slab structure in the EAT model as well as improve Vp predictions

across the domain.

Figure 3. Map view comparisons of SPiRaL (Simmons et al., 2021) and the EAT model after 50
iterations. Voigt-averaged isotropic VS values for the SPiRaL model are plotted in the leftcolumn
and VS values for the preliminary EAT model are plotted in the middle column. The color scale is
the same for the two models. The right column shows the natural log between the ratio of the two
models. The blue regions show areas where the VS of the EAT model is faster than SPiRaL; red
regions show areas where the VS of the EAT model is slower than SPiRaL.

Outside of slab structure, we are also interested in understanding the driver factors of intraplate

volcanism across China and Mongolia. In this report, we will focus on model structure

underneath Chiangbaishan, an intraplate volcano in northeastern China. Figure 4 shows a cross-

section of the EAT model that begins at the outer rise of the subducting Pacific plate off the coast

of eastern Japan. The cross section then travels across the East Sea and terminates close to the

border of China and Mongolia. While the crustal structure of the model is poorly resolved due to

the lack of short periods constrained in the preliminary EAT model (center panel, Figure 4b), the

structure of the downgoing Pacific slab is clearly imaged in the model. The Pacific slab is shown

as a continuous high velocity structure down to the 410 km discontinuity. The slab then begins to

stagnate in the mid-mantle and settles above the 660 km discontinuity. At the edge of the

stagnated slab, there is a narrow section of low velocity that seems to extend from the lower

mantle to the surface. Whether this is related to the upwelling of deep mantle material, slab edge

effects, or an artefact of limited coverage is unclear. Switching to the normalized cross-

correlation misfit function will likely improve our structural resolution of the deep slab, so we

reserve any interpretation of intra-plate volcanism for future publications. We also plan on using

restitution testing (van Herwaarden et al., 2023) to analyze the resolution of our model at depth;

we reserve this analysis until the model is completed at shorter periods.

Figure 4. Cross-section through EAT model. Cross-section begins at the outer rise of the Japan

Trench, passing over the Japanese Arc, Korean Peninsula, Chiangbaishan Volcano, and ending

in northeastern China (Panel a). Panel b shows the relative velocity variations with depth of EAT

across cross-section A-A’. The elevation along the profile is plotted as the grey polygon above

the cross-section; the black line represents sea level. The cross-section is split up by crustal and

Because much of the deep structure of our model relies on data from deep events, we were

interested in how waveform fits for deep events perform. We show one example from a 614 km

deep event that occurred below Mindanao Island, Philippines, on 29 July 2010. 42 stations have

windows picked for this event; we show data for 4 of those stations (Figure 5a). For SPiRaL, the

synthetic seismograms show good fits in the vertical and transverse components, but struggled to

fit the dispersed waves at some stations. There are also time shifts in much of the data in the

SPiRaL model. The preliminary EAT model corrects the time shifts seen in the SPiRaL data and

improves upon the fit of dispersed waves over SPiRaL. We computed the Pearson correlation

coefficient between the observed and synthetic data for both the SPiRaL and EAT models.

SPiRaL’s average Pearson correlation coefficient is 0.49; EAT averages a Pearson correlation

coefficient of 0.82. While the vertical and radial components are significantly improved in most

stations in EAT, the transverse components can still be further improved. We hope that targeting

body wave structure with the normalized cross-correlation coefficient will further improve the fit

of deep earthquakes.

Figure 5. Waveform comparison of SPiRaL synthetics and EAT synthetics for an Mw6.6 event

that occurred at 614 km depth on 29 July 2010 in Mindanao, Philippines. The GCMT moment

tensor solution and paths to stations plotted in the record section are shown in Panel a. Panel b

shows the observed data recorded at all 4 stations for this event (black waveforms) compared to

the synthetics from SPiRaL (blue waveforms). Panel c shows the comparison between the

observed data (black waveforms) and the synthetics from EAT (red waveforms). The Pearson

cross-correlation value between the observed data and the respective synthetics are plotted

below the station information in the record sections.

Conclusions

We present a an update on the East Asian Tomography (EAT) model, an adjoint waveform

tomography model of greater East Asia. The EAT model used SPiRaL (Simmons et al., 2021) as

a starting model and used data from 250 events recorded at over 800 permanent and temporary

seismic stations. We ran 198 iterations over 3 period bands, reaching a minimum period of 35

seconds. The preliminary EAT model shows improved slab structure in the mid-mantle compared

to SPiRaL and improve waveform predicition over SPiRaL for deep events. To further improve

the model, we plan on iterating the model down to a minimum period of at least 25 seconds and

we will switch to a normalized cross-correlation-based misfit function (Tao et al., 2017) to better

constrain the body wave structure of the model.
