Frailty in patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery for lung cancer: a narrative review of assessment, outcomes and prehabilitation
Authors: Yiming Xu, Mingming Cao, Yuting Liu, Xuyang Li, Qiang Zhang
Published: 2026

Background

Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has become an important minimally invasive approach for curative-intent resection in patients with early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Current guidelines support surgical resection for medically operable patients with early-stage NSCLC. Chronological age alone should not exclude older adults from potentially curative treatment [1–3]. Compared with open thoracotomy, VATS may reduce chest wall trauma and cardiopulmonary complications in selected older patients; however, substantial heterogeneity in perioperative risk remains [4, 5].

Frailty is a multidimensional state characterised by reduced physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors. Although these domains frequently overlap, frailty is not synonymous with chronological age, comorbidity, or disability [6]. Frailty has been associated with postoperative complications, prolonged hospitalisation, functional decline, and mortality after surgery, including cancer surgery [7, 8]. In lung cancer surgery, frailty assessment may help identify patients whose cardiopulmonary or oncological operability does not fully reflect their ability to tolerate surgical stress or recover after resection [9, 10].

This review summarises the current evidence on frailty assessment, perioperative outcomes, surgical decision-making, and prehabilitation in patients undergoing lung cancer surgery, with particular emphasis on VATS. Given the heterogeneity of the literature, we explicitly distinguish VATS-specific evidence from evidence extrapolated from mixed thoracic surgery cohorts, open thoracotomy studies, geriatric oncology populations, and non-thoracic surgical populations.

Literature search strategy

This narrative review was conducted to summarise and critically appraise evidence on frailty assessment, perioperative outcomes, and prehabilitation strategies in the context of lung cancer surgery. The review question was structured using a broad PICO framework. The population comprised adults with lung cancer being evaluated for lung resection, particularly older adults undergoing VATS segmentectomy or lobectomy. The exposure of interest was preoperative frailty or related domains of vulnerability, including slow gait speed, impaired physical performance, sarcopenia, malnutrition, and geriatric vulnerability. Comparators included non-frail patients, patients with greater functional reserve, and conventional preoperative assessment based on chronological age, comorbidity burden, pulmonary function, and cardiopulmonary risk evaluation. Outcomes of interest included postoperative pulmonary and cardiac complications, length of stay, discharge disposition, healthcare utilisation, mortality, functional recovery, tolerance of adjuvant therapy, and long-term oncological outcomes.

PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science were searched from their inception to April 2026. Search terms combined keywords and controlled vocabulary terms related to frailty, the Clinical Frailty Scale, gait speed, sarcopenia, malnutrition, geriatric assessment, lung cancer, NSCLC, VATS, thoracoscopic surgery, lung resection, lobectomy, segmentectomy, postoperative complications, pulmonary complications, mortality, prehabilitation, pulmonary rehabilitation, nutrition, and smoking cessation. The detailed search strategy is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, randomised controlled trials, prospective and retrospective cohort studies, registry-based studies, clinically relevant observational studies, consensus statements, and guidelines were considered for inclusion. Priority was given to studies involving VATS or other minimally invasive approaches to lung cancer surgery. When direct VATS-specific evidence was unavailable, evidence from broader lung resection cohorts, mixed thoracic surgery populations, open thoracotomy studies, geriatric oncology populations, and non-thoracic surgical populations was included when clinically relevant and interpreted cautiously.

Studies were eligible if they addressed frailty definitions or assessment tools, associations between frailty and outcomes after lung resection, the feasibility or predictive value of the Clinical Frailty Scale, gait speed, or other instruments, relationships among frailty, sarcopenia, malnutrition, and functional impairment, frailty-informed surgical decision-making, or prehabilitation and perioperative optimisation relevant to lung cancer surgery. Studies were excluded if they were not clinically relevant to frailty or thoracic oncology, focused exclusively on non-surgical cancer treatment without implications for perioperative care, did not report outcomes relevant to frailty assessment or surgical recovery, were conference abstracts lacking sufficient methodological detail, or were not available in English.

Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two authors. Potentially relevant full-text articles were reviewed to determine eligibility and clinical relevance. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, adjudication by a senior author. Because this was a narrative review rather than a systematic review, no meta-analysis was performed, and no formal risk-of-bias assessment was undertaken for all included studies. However, study design, population characteristics, surgical approach, frailty definition, adjustment for conventional risk factors, and key limitations were considered when interpreting the strength of the evidence. The absence of a prospectively registered protocol, such as PROSPERO registration, was acknowledged as a limitation.

Conceptual foundations and evidence hierarchy

In this review, the term “older adults” generally refers to patients aged 65 years or older, whereas “octogenarians” refers to patients aged 80 years or older. The term “elderly” is avoided where possible because it is inconsistently defined and may obscure clinically meaningful heterogeneity in physiological reserve.

Definition of frailty

Several conceptual models have been used to operationalise frailty. The physical frailty phenotype defines frailty using five criteria: unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, weakness, slow gait speed, and low physical activity [7]. The deficit-accumulation model conceptualises frailty as the cumulative burden of age-related deficits across multiple domains [11, 12]. A Delphi consensus also emphasised the multidimensional nature of frailty, incorporating physical function, gait speed, activity level, nutritional status, mental health, and cognition [13]. Although these models differ in measurement, they converge on the concept that frailty is a clinically measurable syndrome of vulnerability rather than a single disease entity.

Distinguishing frailty, sarcopenia, malnutrition and functional impairment

Frailty, sarcopenia, malnutrition and functional impairment are interrelated but distinct constructs. Frailty is the broadest concept and reflects vulnerability across physiological, functional, cognitive, psychological and social domains [6, 13]. Sarcopenia refers more specifically to reduced skeletal muscle strength, muscle quantity or quality, and physical performance; it may contribute to frailty but is not equivalent to multidimensional frailty [14]. Malnutrition reflects deficiency or imbalance in energy, protein or nutrient intake, often influenced by inflammation and cancer-related metabolic changes [15]. Functional impairment describes limitations in activities of daily living, instrumental activities of daily living, mobility or physical performance.

Clarifying these boundaries has practical implications for VATS candidates. A patient with isolated malnutrition may primarily require nutritional optimisation; a patient with sarcopenia may benefit from resistance training and protein supplementation; a patient with slow gait speed may require functional prehabilitation and discharge planning; and a patient with multidimensional frailty may require comprehensive geriatric assessment and multidisciplinary perioperative management. These constructs should therefore be viewed as overlapping domains of vulnerability rather than interchangeable diagnoses.

Why frailty is relevant to VATS lung cancer surgery

Lung resection places substantial demands on cardiopulmonary reserve. Standard preoperative assessment includes pulmonary function, cardiac risk, exercise capacity and predicted postoperative reserve [16, 17]. VATS reduces surgical trauma but should not be interpreted as a low-risk procedure. Postoperative pulmonary complications, prolonged recovery, non-home discharge and functional decline may still occur, particularly in patients with limited reserve [5, 18–20].

Frailty assessment may complement conventional cardiopulmonary evaluation by capturing vulnerability not fully reflected by age, pulmonary function tests or comorbidity indices. Two patients with similar forced expiratory volume in 1 s, tumour stage and planned extent of resection may differ substantially in mobility, nutritional reserve, cognition, muscle strength, social support and ability to participate in rehabilitation. These differences may influence postoperative pulmonary complications, length of stay, discharge destination and longer-term recovery [8–10, 21] (Figs. 1).

VATS-specific versus extrapolated evidence

The evidence linking frailty to outcomes after VATS should be interpreted in layers. First, a limited number of studies have directly evaluated frailty or frailty-related measures in VATS or minimally invasive lung resection populations, providing the most relevant evidence for this review [18, 21–23]. Second, broader lung resection and thoracic surgery studies support the general prognostic relevance of frailty but may include heterogeneous surgical approaches and procedures [9, 10, 19, 20]. Third, evidence from geriatric oncology, general surgery, abdominal surgery and cardiac surgery informs mechanisms and potential interventions but remains extrapolative when applied to VATS candidates with lung cancer [8, 24, 25]. Therefore, VATS-specific studies are prioritised when available, mixed thoracic evidence is used as supportive evidence, and non-thoracic evidence is treated as hypothesis-generating.

Preoperative frailty assessment in VATS candidates

Accurate and efficient identification of frailty is essential for translating frailty assessment from a research concept into a practical component of thoracic surgical care. Frailty screening should complement, rather than replace, conventional evaluation of pulmonary function, cardiac risk, comorbidity burden, performance status and predicted postoperative respiratory reserve [16, 17].

For routine use, the ideal frailty screening tool should be brief, reproducible, inexpensive, interpretable by multidisciplinary teams and feasible in busy outpatient or pre-admission settings. No single instrument is optimal for all patients or clinical contexts. Tool selection should balance feasibility, predictive validity, ease of training, patient burden and the clinical question being addressed. In this review, the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) and 4-metre gait speed (4MGS) are emphasised as pragmatic first-line screening tools because they can be incorporated into thoracic surgery workflows with minimal equipment and limited additional time [21, 23, 26–29].

Clinical frailty scale

The CFS is a 9-point judgement-based scale that categorises patients from very fit to terminally ill [26, 27]. It should be scored according to baseline health and function, preferably before acute deterioration, rather than transient impairment caused by acute illness or hospitalisation. In general, CFS 1–3 indicates fit to managing well, CFS 4 indicates vulnerability, CFS 5–7 indicates increasing severity of frailty, and CFS 8–9 indicates very severe frailty or terminal illness [27]. Many perioperative studies use CFS ≥ 5 to define frailty, although thresholds vary [28].

The main advantages of the CFS are speed, simplicity and broad clinical interpretability. It can be used by surgeons, anaesthesiologists, nurses and geriatric teams after appropriate training [26, 28, 29]. Limitations include subjectivity, potential inter-rater variability, limited granularity and difficulty scoring patients with cognitive impairment, disability unrelated to frailty or limited collateral history. Standardised training and workflow protocols are therefore important when implementing the CFS in thoracic surgery clinics. For practical use, CFS should be interpreted according to the patient’s baseline function before acute deterioration; CFS 1–3 generally indicates fit to managing well, CFS 4 indicates vulnerability, and CFS ≥ 5 is commonly used to identify clinically relevant frailty in perioperative studies.

Four-metre gait speed

Gait speed is a simple objective measure of physical performance and functional reserve [21, 30]. The 4MGS test can be performed in an outpatient clinic, preoperative assessment area or ward corridor. A flat, unobstructed 4-metre walking path is marked, and the patient is instructed to walk at their usual comfortable pace using their regular walking aid if needed. Time is recorded in seconds, and gait speed is calculated as distance divided by time. A patient who walks 4 m in 5 s, for example, has a gait speed of 0.8 m/s.

Common cut-offs in geriatric and surgical literature include < 0.8 m/s or < 1.0 m/s, but the optimal threshold for VATS lung cancer surgery has not been definitively established [30]. Thoracic surgery studies suggest that slower preoperative gait speed is associated with longer hospital stay, postoperative adverse events and greater need for post-discharge support after lung resection [21, 23]. However, 4MGS mainly reflects physical performance and may be infeasible in patients with severe dyspnoea, unstable cardiac symptoms, acute pain, neurological disease, severe arthritis, recent falls or inability to ambulate safely. In routine practice, gait speed is calculated as 4 m divided by walking time in seconds; values below 0.8–1.0 m/s may indicate impaired physical performance, while inability to complete the test safely should itself be considered a clinically meaningful marker of vulnerability.

Practical workflow and complementary assessment

A pragmatic workflow may begin with brief screening at the first surgical consultation or pre-anaesthesia assessment. Patients with CFS 1–3 and normal gait speed may proceed through standard pathways if conventional cardiopulmonary evaluation is acceptable. Patients with CFS 4, borderline gait speed or mild functional limitation may benefit from targeted optimisation, including respiratory training, nutrition assessment, smoking cessation support, medication review and early discharge planning. Patients with CFS ≥ 5, markedly slow gait speed, inability to complete 4MGS, recent functional decline, cognitive impairment or poor social support should be considered for more comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment.

Other instruments may be useful in selected patients or research settings. The Fried phenotype provides a structured physical frailty definition but requires several measurements [7]. Modified frailty indices are useful for database research and risk modelling but may overlap with comorbidity and underrepresent physical performance or cognition [11, 12, 22, 31, 32]. Comprehensive geriatric assessment provides the richest multidimensional evaluation but requires greater resources [33]. Nutritional and sarcopenia assessment may provide complementary information, particularly in patients with weight loss, poor intake, low muscle mass or impaired physical performance [14, 15, 34–36]. Frailty and outcomes after lung resection

Frailty has been associated with adverse postoperative outcomes across surgical populations, including cancer surgery and lung resection [8–10]. In VATS lung cancer surgery, clinically relevant outcomes include postoperative pulmonary complications, cardiac complications, length of stay, delayed functional recovery, non-home discharge, healthcare utilisation, mortality, adjuvant therapy tolerance and long-term oncological outcomes. The strength of evidence differs across outcomes. Direct evidence in VATS-specific frail populations remains limited, and much of the literature comes from mixed lung resection cohorts, open thoracotomy studies or broader geriatric surgical oncology.

Pulmonary and cardiac complications

Postoperative pulmonary complications are particularly relevant because lung resection directly reduces respiratory reserve and may impair cough, secretion clearance and postoperative ventilation. In older patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic pulmonary resection, frailty has been reported as an independent risk factor for postoperative pulmonary complications [18]. This association is biologically plausible because frailty-related sarcopenia, respiratory muscle weakness, low activity and poor nutritional reserve may increase susceptibility to atelectasis, sputum retention and pneumonia [37–40].

Evidence regarding cardiac complications is less well defined in VATS-specific frailty studies. Frailty may increase vulnerability to haemodynamic stress, arrhythmia, myocardial injury and decompensation of pre-existing cardiovascular disease, particularly in patients with limited reserve. However, many thoracic studies report composite morbidity rather than separate pulmonary and cardiac endpoints. Future studies should report organ-specific outcomes and adjust for age, sex, smoking status, pulmonary function, cardiovascular comorbidity, tumour stage, surgical extent, operative time and surgical approach.

Length of stay, discharge disposition and healthcare utilisation

Frailty is consistently associated with prolonged hospitalisation and increased care needs after surgery [7, 8]. In thoracic surgery, slower gait speed appears particularly relevant to recovery trajectory. Singh et al. reported that each 0.1 m/s decrease in preoperative 4MGS was associated with longer hospital stay and higher risk of adverse events after lung resection [21]. This suggests that a simple performance measure may capture recovery capacity not fully reflected by pulmonary function or planned extent of resection.

Discharge disposition is an important patient-centred outcome because it reflects independence, caregiver burden and functional recovery. Slower preoperative 4MGS has been associated with greater need for home support or transfer to a rehabilitation facility after lung resection [21]. Frailty screening may therefore help identify patients who need early physiotherapy input, nutritional support, respiratory training, caregiver preparation, social work involvement or planned rehabilitation after discharge.

Where quantitative estimates were available, we extracted reported associations between frailty-related measures and postoperative outcomes, including pulmonary complications, length of stay, adverse events, discharge disposition and mortality. However, sex-, stage-, and procedure-specific analyses remain inconsistently reported in VATS-only cohorts. Few studies directly compare the incremental predictive value of frailty measures with conventional preoperative cardiopulmonary assessment, and cardiac complications are often included in composite morbidity rather than analysed as separate endpoints.

Patient characteristics, procedure-related factors and long-term outcomes

Variables such as sex, chronological age, tumour stage, procedure type, pulmonary function, respiratory complications, cardiac complications and mortality are clinically important when interpreting frailty evidence. However, current studies do not consistently report these relationships in a VATS-specific manner. Some studies adjust for age, sex, comorbidity or surgical extent, but subgroup analyses by cancer stage, segmentectomy versus lobectomy, lymph node management or sex-specific effects are often unavailable or underpowered [9, 10, 18, 21, 22].

The relevance of frailty also extends beyond 30-day outcomes. Because VATS lung cancer surgery is often performed with curative intent, long-term functional recovery, health-related quality of life, adjuvant therapy tolerance, recurrence, disease-free survival and overall survival are essential endpoints [33, 41, 42]. Frail patients may experience slower functional recovery, higher non-cancer mortality and reduced tolerance of systemic therapy. Direct evidence linking preoperative frailty to recurrence, adjuvant therapy completion or disease-free survival after VATS remains insufficient. Frailty may influence oncological trajectories indirectly rather than through tumour biology alone. Delayed postoperative recovery, reduced tolerance or completion of adjuvant therapy, competing non-cancer mortality and persistent functional decline may all weaken the long-term benefit of curative-intent surgery. Frailty-informed surgical decision-making

Frailty assessment should be understood as an additional layer of risk stratification rather than a replacement for established thoracic surgical evaluation. Standard decision-making for lung cancer resection should continue to prioritise tumour size, location, radiological invasiveness, clinical stage, nodal status, pulmonary function, predicted postoperative lung function, cardiopulmonary exercise capacity, cardiac risk and patient preference [1, 16, 17, 43]. Frailty assessment may complement these parameters by identifying reduced functional reserve, impaired mobility, malnutrition, cognitive vulnerability or limited social support.

Frailty should not be used in isolation to determine the extent of VATS resection. The choice between wedge resection, segmentectomy, lobectomy or more extensive resection should remain grounded in oncological principles, margin adequacy, lymph node evaluation and preservation of pulmonary function. Recent randomized trials support sublobar resection as an oncologically acceptable approach in selected patients with small peripheral NSCLC, but these trials do not establish frailty-based thresholds for choosing segmentectomy rather than lobectomy [44, 45].

For selected patients with early-stage peripheral tumours, limited cardiopulmonary reserve or marked frailty, less extensive resection may be considered when oncologically appropriate and technically feasible. Conversely, patients requiring complex lobectomy, bilobectomy or pneumonectomy may need more detailed multidisciplinary evaluation when frailty suggests disproportionate risk. In shared decision-making, frailty should be presented not as a binary label of fit or unfit, but as information about expected recovery, discharge needs, complication risk, ability to return to baseline function and tolerance of subsequent oncological treatment.

Whether frailty assessment can improve procedure selection remains unresolved. Future studies should test whether incorporating frailty into surgical decision models improves outcomes beyond conventional assessment, with stratification by tumour stage, procedure type, pulmonary function, age group, sex, sarcopenia, nutritional status and frailty severity.

Prehabilitation and perioperative optimisation

The purpose of frailty assessment is not simply to label patients as high risk, but to identify modifiable vulnerabilities and guide perioperative optimisation. Frailty screening becomes clinically meaningful only when linked to a structured pathway including risk communication, targeted intervention, multidisciplinary planning and postoperative support [46, 47]. For VATS lung cancer surgery, such a pathway may include exercise-based prehabilitation, nutritional optimisation, smoking cessation, respiratory training, comorbidity management, medication review, delirium prevention, discharge planning and early rehabilitation [47–50].

Evidence should be interpreted cautiously. Prehabilitation has shown benefits in several surgical populations, but many studies come from abdominal, colorectal or mixed non-thoracic cohorts [51]. Thoracic-specific evidence is growing, yet trials specifically enrolling frail or pre-frail VATS lung cancer candidates remain limited. Therefore, prehabilitation in frail VATS candidates should be regarded as biologically plausible and clinically attractive rather than definitively validated across all frailty subgroups.

Exercise and nutritional optimisation

Exercise-based prehabilitation aims to improve functional reserve through aerobic training, resistance training, inspiratory muscle training, balance training or multimodal programmes. In thoracic surgery, systematic reviews suggest that preoperative exercise training may improve functional capacity and may reduce complications or length of stay in selected patients [50]. Liu et al. reported that a two-week multimodal programme improved perioperative functional capacity in patients undergoing thoracoscopic lobectomy for lung cancer [48]. However, this trial did not specifically enrol frail patients, and frailty-specific effects remain uncertain. More recent studies support the feasibility of multimodal prehabilitation in lung cancer surgery, but intervention components, duration and outcomes remain heterogeneous [49].

Nutritional vulnerability may coexist with frailty, sarcopenia, inflammation and cancer-related weight loss. Nutritional optimisation may include screening for weight loss, low body mass index, poor oral intake, hypoalbuminaemia and sarcopenia; dietitian referral; oral nutritional supplementation; protein optimisation; and management of reversible causes of poor intake. Protein intake may support muscle mass and function in older adults, particularly when combined with resistance exercise, but evidence linking protein intake alone to frailty is inconsistent [35, 36, 52].

Smoking cessation and respiratory optimisation

Smoking cessation is a core component of perioperative care for patients undergoing lung cancer surgery. Longer abstinence is generally preferable when oncologically feasible, and at least four weeks of cessation may reduce pulmonary complications after pulmonary surgery [53]. Nevertheless, cessation should be encouraged at any time before surgery because even short-term cessation may provide physiological and behavioural benefits [54].

Respiratory optimisation may include breathing exercises, inspiratory muscle training, airway clearance, bronchodilator optimisation, treatment of respiratory infection, pulmonary rehabilitation and postoperative mobilisation planning. Meta-analytic evidence suggests that preoperative breathing exercise may improve postoperative outcomes in lung cancer surgery [55]. LIUZIJUE or other culturally specific breathing interventions may be promising, but available evidence appears limited and should not be presented as established standard care for all VATS candidates [56].

Multimodal frailty-guided pathways

Because frailty is multidimensional, single-component interventions may be insufficient for patients with multiple vulnerabilities. A multimodal pathway may combine exercise, nutrition, respiratory optimisation, smoking cessation, medication review, comorbidity optimisation, delirium prevention, psychological support and discharge planning. The components should be selected according to the frailty profile: slow gait speed may trigger functional training; weight loss may trigger nutritional intervention; cognitive impairment may trigger delirium prevention and caregiver engagement; and poor social support may trigger early discharge coordination.

Implementation remains challenging because institutions differ in access to geriatricians, rehabilitation specialists, dietitians, smoking cessation services, pulmonary rehabilitation and community support. A tiered model may be practical: brief screening with CFS and/or 4MGS identifies risk, and abnormal results trigger targeted nutritional, functional, respiratory or geriatric interventions (Fig. 2). Future trials should determine whether frailty-guided multimodal prehabilitation reduces pulmonary complications, shortens hospital stay, increases home discharge, improves functional recovery, enhances adjuvant therapy tolerance and improves quality of life after VATS.

Brief screening with practical tools such as the CFS and 4MGS may identify non-frail, pre-frail and frail patients. Abnormal results may prompt targeted nutritional, functional, respiratory, geriatric or social assessment. Prehabilitation and optimisation should be initiated as early as feasible before surgery when oncologically safe, although the optimal duration and intensity in frail VATS candidates remain undefined. Frailty assessment should guide perioperative planning and shared decision-making, but should not be used in isolation to determine surgical eligibility or extent of resection

Unanswered questions

Several questions remain unresolved. First, VATS-specific evidence remains limited. Future studies should clearly report surgical approach, procedure type, tumour stage, lymph node management, pulmonary function, operative time and perioperative pathways. Second, the optimal frailty instrument for thoracic surgery is uncertain. Direct head-to-head comparisons should assess feasibility, sensitivity, specificity, calibration and incremental predictive value beyond age, comorbidity, pulmonary function, cardiac risk and planned extent of resection.

Third, clinically actionable frailty thresholds are not well established. It remains unclear which CFS score, gait-speed cut-off or multidimensional profile should trigger prehabilitation, comprehensive geriatric assessment, intensified monitoring or altered discharge planning. Fourth, frailty-targeted prehabilitation in VATS candidates requires randomized or pragmatic trials that specifically enrol frail or pre-frail patients and evaluate timing, duration, intensity, adherence and outcome effects.

Fifth, the role of frailty in determining the extent of VATS resection requires further investigation. Current evidence does not support using frailty alone to choose wedge resection, segmentectomy, lobectomy or non-surgical treatment. Sixth, long-term functional and oncological outcomes remain underreported. Future studies should evaluate sustained functional decline, quality of life, non-cancer mortality, recurrence, disease-free survival, overall survival and completion of adjuvant therapy. Finally, implementation studies should assess cost, staff training, workflow burden, patient adherence and equity of access.

Conclusions

Frailty should be regarded as a clinically actionable marker of perioperative vulnerability in older adults undergoing VATS for lung cancer, rather than as a surrogate for chronological age. Although VATS reduces surgical trauma compared with thoracotomy, it does not eliminate heterogeneity in physiological reserve, functional capacity, nutritional status or recovery potential. Current evidence suggests that frailty is associated with postoperative pulmonary complications, prolonged hospitalisation, non-home discharge, increased healthcare utilisation and mortality after lung resection, but direct VATS-specific data remain limited [8–10, 18, 21, 22].

Practical tools such as the CFS and 4MGS may provide feasible entry points for routine preoperative screening [21, 23, 26–29]. Their main value lies in identifying patients who may benefit from more detailed geriatric, nutritional, functional, respiratory or social assessment. These tools should not be interpreted as stand-alone tests for determining surgical eligibility or the extent of resection.

For patients identified as frail or pre-frail, assessment should lead to structured perioperative planning rather than passive risk labelling. Potential strategies include exercise-based prehabilitation, nutritional optimisation, smoking cessation, respiratory training, medication review, comorbidity management, delirium prevention, discharge planning and postoperative rehabilitation [46–50]. Future thoracic oncology research should prioritise VATS-specific prospective cohorts, head-to-head comparisons of frailty instruments, clinically actionable thresholds and randomized or pragmatic trials of frailty-guided interventions. Long-term outcomes, including functional recovery, quality of life, adjuvant therapy tolerance, recurrence, disease-free survival and overall survival, should be incorporated to determine whether frailty-guided care improves the broader cancer care trajectory.
