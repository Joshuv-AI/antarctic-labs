Free energy expansion of determinantal Coulomb gases in the quadratic fields with a point charge
Sung-Soo Byun, Meng Yang, Eui Yoo

Abstract
. We study a determinantal Coulomb gas in the complex plane associated with the external potential Q ⁡ ( z ) = 1 1 − τ 2 ​ ( | z | 2 − τ ​ Re ⁡ z 2 ) − 2 ​ c ​ log ⁡ | z − a | , Q(z)=\frac{1}{1-\tau^{2}}\bigl(|z|^{2}-\tau\operatorname{Re}z^{2}\bigr)-2c\log|z-a|, where τ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ) \tau\in[0,1) , c ≥ 0 c\geq 0 , and a ≥ 0 a\geq 0 . In the regimes where the associated droplet is simply or doubly connected, we derive the free energy expansion up to and including the constant term, with all coefficients computed explicitly, thereby extending recent results in the isotropic case τ = 0 \tau=0 . In particular, we identify the constant term with the Liouville action associated with the droplet. Our result admits a natural interpretation in terms of asymptotic expansions of moments of characteristic polynomials for the elliptic Ginibre ensemble. The proof is based on a deformation framework involving both the singularity location a a and the anisotropy parameter τ \tau , relating variations of the free energy to refined asymptotics of planar orthogonal polynomials. The asymptotic analysis relies on the foliation flow method of Hedenmalm and Wennman, providing an alternative to the Riemann–Hilbert approach used in the isotropic setting. The present work suggests a general framework connecting free energy expansions, refined asymptotics of planar orthogonal polynomials, and conformally invariant geometric functionals, with several intermediate results already formulated for general algebraic Hele-Shaw potentials.



1.
Introduction

One of the distinctive features of probabilistic and statistical physics models in two dimensions is the emergence of conformal geometry as an underlying signature. It often governs the large-scale behaviour of these systems and provides a natural framework for describing the emergent structures of interacting particle systems. A prominent example is given by two-dimensional Coulomb gases
[
97
,
59
,
32
]
, particularly in the study of their free energy expansion.

By definition, for a given external potential
Q:\mathbb{C}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
satisfying suitable assumptions, the determinantal Coulomb gas in the plane (often referred to as a random normal matrix ensemble) is a system of particles
\bm{z}=\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{C}
whose joint probability density function is defined by

(1.1)

\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{N}(\bm{z})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{N}(Q)}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}|z_{j}-z_{k}|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{N}e^{-NQ(z_{j})}\,\mathrm{d}A(z_{j}),

where
\,\mathrm{d}A=\,\mathrm{d}^{2}z/\pi
is the area measure on the complex plane. Here, the normalising constant
\mathcal{Z}_{N}(Q)
is referred to as the
partition function
. For this model, the equilibrium measure plays a central role as the object governing the conformal geometric structure of the system at the macroscopic scale. It characterises the large-scale limit of (
1.1
), and, by Frostman’s theorem, it takes the form

(1.2)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{Q}=\Delta Q\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{K_{Q}}\,\,\mathrm{d}A,\qquad(\Delta:=\partial\bar{\partial}),

where
K_{Q}
is a compact subset of the complex plane, known as the
droplet
. The equilibrium measure can be characterised as the unique minimiser of the energy among probability measures

(1.3)

\displaystyle I_{Q}[\mu]:=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\log\frac{1}{|z-w|}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)+\int_{\mathbb{C}}Q(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z).

A fundamental problem in Coulomb gas theory is to understand the large-
N
expansion of the free energy
\log\mathcal{Z}_{N}(Q)
, which is believed to take the form

(1.4)

\log\mathcal{Z}_{N}(Q)\sim C_{1}N^{2}+C_{2}N\log N+C_{3}N+C_{4}\log N+C_{5},

as
N\to\infty
. (Here, one assumes that the potential
Q
is regular in the sense that it has no logarithmic or jump-type singularities inside the droplet, and that the associated droplet is connected with a smooth boundary; see
[
29
]
for a more detailed discussion.) Such an expansion plays a central role in understanding the behaviour of Coulomb gases.
For instance, the leading-order term
C_{1}
is essential to determine the law of large numbers for the empirical measure
[
67
,
72
]
. Moreover, to capture fluctuations of linear statistics supported in the bulk of the droplet, one needs to identify the expansion up to the term
C_{3}

[
96
]
.

For general Coulomb gases, the first three coefficients
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
, and
C_{3}
were identified in
[
84
]
; see also
[
18
,
15
,
96
]
. These terms admit a potential-theoretic interpretation and are given by

(1.5)

C_{1}=-I_{Q}[\mu_{Q}],\qquad C_{2}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad C_{3}=\frac{\log(2\pi)}{2}-1-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log(\Delta Q)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{Q}.

Here, the coefficients
C_{1},C_{2},
and
C_{3}
represent, respectively, the macroscopic mean-field energy, a universal self-energy contribution at the microscopic scale, and a subleading term encoding entropy effects. Note that, in these first three terms, the geometric and topological structure of the equilibrium measure does not play a significant role. In contrast, a key feature of the remaining terms
C_{4}
and
C_{5}
is that they depend crucially on the underlying geometry and topology of the droplet.

Over the past decades, several conjectures have been proposed for the terms
C_{4}
and
C_{5}
. The coefficient
C_{4}
is a topological term, conjectured in
[
71
]
to be given by

(1.6)

C_{4}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\chi}{12},

where
\chi
denotes the Euler characteristic of the droplet; see also
[
43
,
102
]
.
On the other hand, the constant term
C_{5}
, often regarded as the most significant contribution in the physics literature—particularly from the perspective of conformal field theory
[
108
]
—is expected to be related to the spectral determinant of the droplet. While its general form is quite involved, in the case where
\Delta Q
is constant so that the equilibrium measure is uniform on its support, it was conjectured that

(1.7)

C_{5}=\chi\,\zeta^{\prime}(-1)+\frac{\log(2\pi)}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\log\textup{det}_{\zeta}(\Delta_{\mathbb{C}\setminus K_{Q}}),

where
\zeta
is the Riemann zeta function and
\textup{det}_{\zeta}
denotes the regularised spectral determinant.
We refer to
[
32
, Section 5.3]
,
[
97
, Section 9.3]
and introductions of
[
36
,
39
,
9
,
94
,
29
]
for more on this conjecture. In the next section, we examine this conjecture in detail, replacing the spectral determinant with the Liouville action in the characterisation of
C_{5}
, as the latter admits a more transparent description.

In recent years, the conjectured free energy expansion has been the subject of extensive study. A common starting point is the representation of the partition function as a structured determinant of the form

(1.8)

\mathcal{Z}_{N}(Q)=N!\det\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{C}}z^{j}\bar{z}^{k}e^{-NQ(z)}\,\,\mathrm{d}A(z)\bigg]_{j,k=0}^{N-1},

which follows from the fact that (
1.1
) forms a determinantal point process.
An effective way to analyse the structured determinant (
1.8
) is to work on an orthogonal polynomial basis. More precisely, let
\{P_{j,N}\}_{j=0}^{N-1}
be the family of monic planar orthogonal polynomials satisfying

(1.9)

\int_{\mathbb{C}}P_{j,N}(z)\overline{P_{k,N}(z)}\,e^{-NQ(z)}\,\,\mathrm{d}A(z)=h_{j,N}\,\delta_{j,k},\quad j,k=0,1,\ldots,N-1,

where
h_{j,N}
is the squared norm of
P_{j,N}
.
With this choice of basis, it follows that

(1.10)

\mathcal{Z}_{N}(Q)=N!\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}h_{j,N}.

This representation naturally brings tools from the theory of orthogonal polynomials into the analysis.

In recent studies of free energy expansions based on the representation (
1.8
), the main strategies can be broadly classified into three complementary approaches:
exact solvability
,
duality
, and
deformation of the free energy
.
These approaches are implemented depending on the model and the objectives of the analysis, and together provide a flexible framework for studying the problem. We now describe each of them in more detail; see Table

for a summary.

Summary of complementary approaches

(A) Exact solvability.
The first approach, based on exact solvability, is particularly effective for many well-studied models in non-Hermitian random matrix theory, especially various extensions of the complex Ginibre ensemble
[
32
]
.

It is well known that the eigenvalues of the complex Ginibre matrix follow the law (
1.1
) with the Gaussian potential
Q^{\rm g}(z):=|z|^{2}
.
Several variants of the Ginibre ensemble include potentials of the form

(1.11)

Q^{\rm i}(z):=|z|^{2}-2c\log|z|,\qquad Q^{\rm e}(z):=\frac{|z|^{2}-\tau\operatorname{Re}z^{2}}{1-\tau^{2}},

where
c\geq 0
and
\tau\in[0,1)
. These potentials arise from the induced and elliptic Ginibre ensembles, respectively; see
[
32
, Sections 2.3 and 2.4]
. Further examples include the truncated unitary ensemble and the spherical ensemble; see
[
32
, Sections 2.5 and 2.6]
.
For all such exactly solvable models, the partition function can be expressed in terms of classical special functions—more precisely, in terms of the Barnes
G
-function
[
93
, Section 5.17]
. The large-
N
expansion then follows from classical asymptotic analysis of these functions. This mechanism is closely analogous to Selberg integral-type evaluations in one dimension; see
[
61
]
.

Such an approach has been systematically developed in recent works
[
36
,
37
,
9
]
for radially symmetric potentials of the form
Q(z)=Q(|z|)
. In this setting, the matrix on the right-hand side of (
1.8
) is already diagonal, and the integrals reduce to their radial components. As a result, the partition function can be analysed using the Laplace method combined with Euler–Maclaurin type expansions.
Nevertheless, additional care is required, since the free energy expansion depends sensitively on the geometry of the droplet (which, in this case, is itself radially symmetric). In
[
36
]
, the cases
\chi=0,1
(corresponding to the disc and the annulus) were treated. This was further extended in
[
9
]
to configurations where the droplet consists of concentric annuli, in which case non-trivial oscillatory terms appear as well. Moreover, in
[
37
]
, the spherical geometry was considered, and the conjectural free energy expansion for
\chi=2
was obtained.

(B) Duality.
The second approach is based on certain ensemble interrelations, often referred to as dualities in random matrix theory; see
[
60
]
for a comprehensive review. This perspective has its origins, in part, in the supersymmetry method; see e.g.
[
62
]
.
The central idea is that, for certain models, the associated free energy can be reformulated into alternative integral representations that are more amenable to analysis.

A prominent example in this approach is the potential

(1.12)

Q_{a}^{\mathrm{i}}(z)=|z|^{2}-2c\log|z-a|,

where
c,a\geq 0
. This model naturally arises in the study of moments of characteristic polynomials of the complex Ginibre ensemble. The associated droplet exhibits a topological phase transition
[
16
]
: depending on the parameters
c
and
a
, it may be either simply or doubly connected, with the critical regime characterised by the formation of a double point.

A key feature of this model is a duality relation showing that, up to an explicit multiplicative constant, the corresponding partition function can be identified with the distribution of the extremal eigenvalue of the Laguerre unitary ensemble. Exploiting this connection, the free energy expansion at criticality was derived in
[
39
, Proposition 2.5]
, revealing the emergence of the Tracy–Widom distribution.

This approach has since been extended in
[
31
,
35
]
to generalisations of the potential (
1.12
) arising in the truncated unitary and induced spherical ensembles. In both cases, the dual model is given by the Jacobi unitary ensemble. Owing to the well-developed asymptotic theory of the associated Hankel determinants
[
48
]
, one can again derive the free energy expansion to this order of precision. Furthermore, such duality relations have been extended and are now understood to connect these models with observables in integrable probability, such as the last passage time in geometric last passage percolation
[
31
]
, in line with the philosophy of the seminal work
[
73
]
.

(C) Deformation of the free energy.
The third approach, based on the deformation of the free energy, is one of the most classical and important, yet still actively developing, methods for analysing free energies and conformal invariants in statistical physics. It also constitutes the central framework of the present paper.

From a physical perspective, this approach is related to the Ward identities in conformal field theory, which capture the variations of conformal fields under the insertion of the stress energy tensor; see e.g.
[
76
, Lecture 5, Appendix 6]
. In particular, the deformation of the spectral determinant is governed by the Polyakov–Alvarez formula; see e.g.
[
57
, Proposition 2.3]
. From a mathematical viewpoint, this method has been extensively developed, especially in the context of Hermitian random matrix theory; see e.g.
[
50
,
72
,
80
,
56
]
. Related ideas also appear in the so-called transport method for general Coulomb gases; see
[
97
]
.

The core idea is to compute the variation of the free energy along a suitable deformation, and then recover the free energy itself by integration. More precisely, the method consists of the following two steps:

(i)

Identify a deformation for which the large-
N
expansion of the free energy variation can be computed;

(ii)

Determine an exactly solvable reference free energy serving as the integration constant.

In the present two-dimensional setting, where our goal is to derive the precise asymptotic expansion up to the constant term
C_{5}
, a prototypical example of this approach is again provided by the potential (
1.12
). In this case, the main idea in
[
39
]
is to perform the deformation with respect to the position parameter
a\geq 0
, so that the resulting variation can be identified with the fine asymptotic behaviour of the associated orthogonal polynomials.
The reference free energy is then obtained from a tractable model exploiting radial symmetry. The asymptotics of the corresponding planar orthogonal polynomials are analysed via the Riemann–Hilbert method, which in turn yields the free energy expansion; see
[
39
, Theorem 2.2]
.

Such variational ideas have also been developed further in the recent work
[
94
]
. There, instead of the point charge insertion at a single point
a
, corresponding to the term
\log|z-a|
in (
1.12
), one considers a screened version, namely a globally distributed potential obtained by integrating the point charge. In this framework, the variation induced by moving holes within the droplet—while preserving its topology—is analysed using correlation energy together with a mean-field approximation.

While the above three approaches have been primarily discussed in the context of free energy expansions in the regular setting (
1.4
), there is also a substantial body of work devoted to related problems in the presence of two-dimensional Fisher–Hartwig or hard-wall type singularities. Recent developments in the Fisher–Hartwig (logarithmic singularity) setting include
[
55
,
105
,
29
,
54
,
25
]
, whereas hard-wall type constraints have been studied in
[
5
,
11
,
10
,
44
,
45
,
92
,
38
,
30
,
6
,
75
]
. In addition, we note that there exists another formulation of the free energy expansion, often referred to as the geometric Zabrodin–Wiegmann conjecture. In this setting, one considers ensembles on Riemann surfaces without boundary, which has also been extensively studied in the literature; see e.g.
[
78
,
99
,
26
]
and references therein.

In this work, we extend the results of
[
39
]
to a class of models with more general quadratic potentials (
2.6
) below. This setting is both physically natural and of independent interest in non-Hermitian random matrix theory, and it exhibits a richer geometric structure than that considered in
[
39
,
16
,
81
]
for the case
\tau=0
.

In contrast to the case
\tau=0
, the duality method is no longer applicable; cf. Remark
2.6
. We therefore proceed by employing a deformation-based approach, suitably extended to accommodate the present setting. In particular, the deformation with respect to the position parameter used in
[
39
]
is no longer sufficient, and we introduce a new class of deformations adapted to the present model.

From the perspective of asymptotic analysis, the deformation arguments in
[
39
]
were carried out largely at a computational level, without an explicit identification of the underlying conformal geometry. By contrast, the present approach systematically tracks the geometric content of each variation, thereby uncovering the conformal structure governing the expansion.
Furthermore, instead of relying on Riemann–Hilbert techniques, we make use of the recent general theory of planar orthogonal polynomials developed by Hedenmalm and Wennman
[
66
,
68
,
69
]
, implemented here in a fully quantitative form; see Theorem
5.1
. Altogether, the method developed in the present work points toward a general framework relating free energy expansions, refined asymptotics of planar orthogonal polynomials, and conformally invariant geometric functionals.

2.
Main results and discussions

In this section, we present our main results. In particular, Theorem
2.2
provides the free energy expansion for the potential (
2.6
), defining a Coulomb gas in a general quadratic field with a point charge.
Subsection
2.1
introduces the model and the results in detail, followed by remarks on related work. Subsection
2.2
outlines the proof strategy, including the deformation of the free energy (Proposition
2.3
), which we regard as a key result of independent interest.

As mentioned in the previous section, both the results and their proofs are fundamentally driven by conformal geometric computations. We begin by introducing several notions from conformal geometry that will play a central role in their formulation.

We fix notation used throughout the paper. We write
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
,
\mathbb{D}=\{|w|<1\}
,
\mathbb{D}_{e}=\{|w|>1\}
, and
\mathbb{T}=\{|w|=1\}
.
For a Jordan curve
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{C}
, we denote by
\operatorname{Int}(\Gamma)
the bounded component and
\operatorname{Ext}(\Gamma)
the unbounded component of its complement. We also use notation
D_{e}=\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\bar{D}
for the unbounded component when a Jordan domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
is given.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a Jordan domain. In what follows, we will make use of both the interior and exterior conformal maps associated with
D
.
The
normalised exterior conformal map
is the unique conformal map

(2.1)

\psi:\mathbb{D}_{e}\to D_{e},\qquad\psi(\infty)=\infty,\qquad\psi^{\prime}(\infty)>0.

On the other hand, a
normalised interior conformal map
is a conformal map satisfying

(2.2)

\psi:\mathbb{D}\to D,\qquad\psi^{\prime}(0)>0.

The Liouville action is a fundamental functional in Liouville field theory and a central object in conformal geometry, where it arises naturally in the study of conformal maps and their geometric properties. In this work, we employ a version of the Liouville action adapted to our setting, defined as follows.

Definition: Liouville action

For a normalised exterior conformal map
\psi:\mathbb{D}_{e}\to\mathbb{C}
, we define the
Liouville action
of
\psi
by

(2.3)

\mathcal{S}[\psi]:=\int_{\mathbb{D}_{e}}\Big|\frac{\psi^{\prime\prime}(w)}{\psi^{\prime}(w)}\Big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}A(w)-4\log|\psi^{\prime}(\infty)|.

Similarly, for a normalised interior conformal map
\psi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}
, we define

(2.4)

\mathcal{S}[\psi]:=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\Big|\frac{\psi^{\prime\prime}(w)}{\psi^{\prime}(w)}\Big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}A(w)+4\log|\psi^{\prime}(0)|.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{C}
be a connected compact set whose boundary consists of finitely many Jordan curves. Let
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{d}
denote the bounded components of
K^{c}
, and let
\Omega_{\infty}
denote the unbounded component.
We define the
Liouville action
of
K
by

(2.5)

\mathcal{L}[K]:=\mathcal{S}[\psi_{\infty}]+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{S}[\psi_{j}],

where
\psi_{j}
are normalised interior conformal maps of
\Omega_{j}
and
\psi_{\infty}
is the normalised exterior conformal map of
\Omega_{\infty}
.

By the Polyakov–Alvarez conformal anomaly formula, which describes the variation of the spectral determinant under conformal changes of the metric, the Liouville action naturally emerges as the governing functional.
However, whereas the spectral determinant requires delicate regularisation, particularly on unbounded domains, the Liouville action admits a direct formulation in terms of uniformising conformal maps. We therefore state our results in terms of the Liouville action, albeit the spectral determinant is more common in the physics literature.

We also note that the Liouville action considered here is closely related to the universal Liouville action in the framework of Teichmüller geometry
[
100
,
101
]
.
Its connection to the Löwner energy of Weil–Petersson class loops appears in
[
98
,
104
]
, which was also identified as the constant term of free energy expansion of determinantal Coulomb gases on Jordan curves
[
107
,
51
,
74
]
; see also
[
75
]
for results on Coulomb gases on Jordan domains.

(2.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\log\mathcal{Z}_{N}(Q)&=-I_{Q}[\mu_{Q}]N^{2}+\frac{1}{2}N\log N+\bigg(\frac{\log(2\pi)}{2}-1-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log(\Delta Q)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{Q}\bigg)N\\
&\quad+\frac{6-\chi}{12}\log N+\chi\,\zeta^{\prime}(-1)+\frac{\log(2\pi)}{2}+\frac{1}{24}\mathcal{L}[K_{Q}]-\frac{1}{24\pi}\int_{\partial K_{Q}}\log(\Delta Q)\kappa\,\mathrm{d}s+O(N^{-1}),\end{split}

where
\chi
is the Euler characteristic of
K_{Q}
,
\mathcal{L}[K_{Q}]
is the Liouville action (

), and
\kappa
is the curvature of
\partial K_{Q}
.

Remark 2.1

(Comparison with the
\tau=0
case in
[

)
.

Note that Theorem
2.2
recovers
[

, Theorem 2.2]
in the case

.
In contrast to
[

In particular, it does not rely heavily on explicit expressions for the droplet or related quantities, marking a conceptual shift and rendering the method amenable to extensions, for instance, to algebraic Hele-Shaw potentials.

Remark 2.2

(Explicit formulas of the coefficients)
.

In (

), all coefficients can be made explicit in terms of the parameters

defining the potential (

).
More precisely, the associated energy

was computed in

; see also Proposition

below. Moreover, for the
O(N)
term, since

(2.14)

-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log(\Delta Q)\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{Q}=\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\tau^{2}).

Note also that by definition,

\chi=\begin{cases}0&\textup{for Regime I},\\

Furthermore, it is straightforward to compute the Liouville action; cf. (

\frac{1}{24}\mathcal{L}[K_{Q}]=\frac{1}{12}\log\Big(\frac{c}{1+c}\Big),

while in the simply connected regime,

(2.17)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{24}\mathcal{L}[K_{Q}]&=-\frac{1}{6}\log\Big(R(1-q^{2})\Big)-\frac{1}{24}\log(\lambda+(1-\tau)(1-q)^{2})-\frac{1}{24}\log(\lambda+(1-\tau)(1+q)^{2})\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{6}\log\Big(q^{2}\lambda+(1-q^{2})^{2}(1-\tau q^{2})\Big)-\frac{1}{12}\log\Big((1+\tau q^{2})^{2}\lambda-(1+\tau)(1-q^{2})(1-\tau q^{2})^{2}\Big).\end{split}

Here, the parameters are defined in Theorem

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}\log(1-\tau^{2})&\text{for Regime II.}\end{cases}

Remark 2.3

(Full order expansion)
.

The error term
O(N^{-1})
in (
2.13
) is indeed optimal. Furthermore, in the doubly connected case, the simple form of the droplet (
2.8
) allows one to derive a full asymptotic expansion.
More precisely, in this case, the error term admits the expansion: for any positive integer
k_{0}
,

(2.19)

\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}N^{-k}\mathcal{E}_{k}+O(N^{-k_{0}-1}),\qquad\mathcal{E}_{k}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{B_{k+2}}{k(k+1)}\Big(\frac{1}{(1+c)^{k}}-\frac{1}{c^{k}}\Big),&k:\text{even},\\
\displaystyle\frac{B_{k+1}}{k(k+1)},&k:\text{odd}.\end{cases}

Here,
B_{k}
denotes the
k
-th Bernoulli number defined by

(2.20)

\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}B_{k}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}.

Remark 2.4

(Moments of characteristic polynomials)
.

While we present Theorem
2.2
in the framework of the free energy expansion of two-dimensional Coulomb gases, it admits a natural interpretation in non-Hermitian random matrix theory in terms of moments of characteristic polynomials. This perspective is of independent interest and has attracted considerable attention due to its various connections, for instance, with Gaussian multiplicative chaos
[
82
]
.
In this context, one distinguishes between regimes where the powers are fixed and those where they scale proportionally with
N
. Recent works include
[
105
,
29
,
55
]
for the Ginibre ensemble,
[
31
,
35
,
54
]
for the truncated unitary and spherical ensembles, and
[
25
]
for general determinantal two-dimensional Coulomb gas models with finite moments.

To be more precise, let
X
be the elliptic Ginibre ensemble with non-Hermiticity parameter
\tau\in[0,1)
; see
[
32
, Section 2.3]
. As mentioned earlier, the eigenvalues of
X
follow the distribution (
1.1
) with potential
Q^{\rm e}
defined in (
1.11
). By definition, the moment of the characteristic polynomial of
X
is related to the partition functions via

(2.21)

\mathbb{E}\Big[\,\Big|\det(X-a)\Big|^{2cN}\Big]=\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{N}(Q)}{\mathcal{Z}_{N}(Q^{\rm e})}.

Combining the above, we see that Theorem
2.2
yields, as an immediate corollary, the large-
N
expansion of the moments of the characteristic polynomial of the elliptic Ginibre ensemble; see also
[
41
, Corollary 1.3]
for such formulation deriving the leading order term.

As noted above, while it is natural in the free energy expansion framework for the exponent in the left-hand side of (
2.21
) to be of order
O(N)
, the case of a fixed exponent is also of interest in probabilistic applications. In our setting, this corresponds to replacing
c
by
c/N
in the potential (
2.6
), introducing an
N
-dependence.
Such an expansion was recently obtained in
[
25
]
for general genuinely two-dimensional potentials. The methods developed here, in particular Proposition
2.3
, are also applicable in this regime, and we plan to investigate this problem further, especially in the weak non-Hermiticity regime
\tau\to 1
under an appropriate scaling that interpolates between two- and one-dimensional behaviours.

Remark 2.5

(Multi-component regime)
.

In Theorem
2.2
, we focus on the case where the droplet is either simply or doubly connected. Nevertheless, as previously mentioned, there is a third regime (Regime III) in which the droplet consists of two disjoint simply connected components.

In Hermitian random matrix theory, such a situation where the limiting spectral measure is supported on disconnected sets (the so-called
multi-cut regime
) and the associated free energy expansion have been extensively studied; see e.g.
[
47
,
49
,
24
]
and references therein. In that setting, the constant term in the free energy expansion exhibits an oscillatory contribution described in terms of the Riemann theta function, reflecting the underlying quasi-periodic behaviour of the particle system.

By contrast, in the two-dimensional Coulomb gas setting, an analogous oscillatory structure in the free energy expansion remains largely open, particularly beyond radially symmetric situations
[
9
]
. In fact, even the precise form of the conjectural expansion has not yet been established; we refer to
[
29
]
for further discussion.
In particular, Regime III falls outside the scope of the present work. For instance, the main approach of this paper—based on the general theory of planar orthogonal polynomials developed in
[
68
,
66
]
—does not apply in this setting. Instead, analysing this regime appears to require the development of a suitable Riemann–Hilbert analysis, which we leave for future work.

As a related side remark, we note that the inapplicability of the results in
[
68
,
66
]
to multi-component ensembles is the main reason why the edge universality for determinantal Coulomb gases established in
[
68
]
has thus far been confined to connected droplet geometries. Nevertheless, this universality has recently been extended to the multi-component regime in
[
53
]
by means of an alternative approach that does not rely on orthogonal polynomials.

Remark 2.6

(Duality relation for the elliptic Ginibre matrix)
.

In the previous section, we noted that one of the key tools in recent studies of free energy expansions is the use of duality relations. However, the type of duality that arises from supersymmetric methods, particularly those based on Grassmann integration, appears to be difficult to exploit in the present model.

Let us make this more precise. Recall that
X
denotes an elliptic Ginibre matrix of size
N
with non-Hermiticity parameter
\tau\in[0,1]
, and suppose that
cN
is an integer. Then the associated duality relation is given by (see
[
60
, Remark 5.1]
)

(2.22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\,\Big|\det(X-a)\Big|^{2cN}\Big]\propto\bigg\langle\det\begin{pmatrix}-iaI+\sqrt{2\tau}A&C\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
C^{*}&-iaI+\sqrt{2\tau}B\end{pmatrix}^{N}\bigg\rangle_{\begin{subarray}{c}A,B\in{\rm GUE}_{cN}\\
C\in{\rm GinUE}_{cN}\end{subarray}}.

Here
A
and
B
are independent GUE matrices of size
cN
, and
C
is an independent Ginibre matrix of the same size; the expectation on the right-hand side is taken over all three ensembles.

Observe that when
\tau=0
(corresponding to the setting studied in
[
39
]
), the right-hand side of (
2.22
) simplifies considerably, as the dependence on
A
and
B
disappears. Moreover, since
CC^{*}
is distributed according to the Laguerre unitary ensemble, the right-hand side can be reformulated in terms of a probability involving the smallest eigenvalue. This observation played a crucial role in
[
39
]
, as well as in the subsequent works
[
35
,
31
]
, where the left-hand side of (
2.22
) is replaced by truncated unitary or spherical ensembles, which can likewise be related to extremal eigenvalue statistics of the Jacobi unitary ensemble.

In contrast to these earlier works, the right-hand side of (
2.22
) in the present setting involves three random matrix ensembles, for which a systematic asymptotic theory is currently unavailable. This constitutes the main reason why deformation techniques provide the most effective approach in our analysis. On the other hand, our results yield the asymptotic behaviour of the right-hand side of (
2.22
), which may be of independent interest.

Remark 2.7

(Critical asymptotics and Tracy-Widom distribution)
.

As is evident from the phase diagram in Figure
1
, there exists a critical line separating Regimes I and II. Geometrically, this corresponds to the situation in (
2.9
) where the inner disc meets the outer ellipse tangentially, thereby forming a double point; see
[
41
, Figure 4 (A)]
. In this critical regime, it is expected that the coefficient of the
\log N
term coincides with that of the doubly connected droplet.
Moreover, in analogy with the case
\tau=0
, one expects the Tracy–Widom distribution to appear in the constant term of the free energy expansion. While such a result for
\tau=0
was obtained via a duality argument, as mentioned above, this approach is not applicable in the present setting. We also note that a further indication of the emergence of the Tracy–Widom distribution is given by the universality of the Painlevé II asymptotics for the associated orthogonal polynomial (established in
[
16
,
81
]
for
\tau=0
).

The derivation of the corresponding critical asymptotics appears to require a different approach, or at least the use of the Riemann–Hilbert analysis framework. Nevertheless, similarly to
[
39
, Remark 2.6]
, one can formally observe the emergence of an additional
\frac{1}{12}\log N
term arising from the topological phase transition, which is related to the tail asymptotics of the Tracy–Widom distribution.
More precisely, in this regime we consider the scaling

(2.23)

a=a_{\rm cri}[{\rm I,II}]+O(N^{-2/3}),

where
a_{\rm cri}[{\rm I,II}]
denotes the critical value separating Regimes I and II. This is precisely the scale at which one expects the Painlevé II asymptotics to arise. With this scaling, it follows directly from the explicit formula (
2.17
) that

(2.24)

\mathcal{L}[K_{Q}]=\frac{1}{12}\log N+O(1).

This behaviour captures the critical interpolation in the free energy expansion in (
2.13
): as the Euler characteristic transitions from
\chi=1
to
\chi=0
, an additional
\frac{1}{12}\log N
term emerges.

We further note that, for the model under consideration, there exists an even more singular critical regime, namely the case where all three regimes I, II, and III meet at a single point; see
[
41
, Remark 1.5]
for further discussion of this regime and its geometric interpretation. The analysis of such a highly critical case, referred to as the
triple point
, remains widely open.
One can nevertheless expect that, in this regime, the Painlevé II hierarchy arises. In particular, in the free energy expansion, the constant term is expected to involve not the standard Tracy–Widom distribution, but rather its higher-order analogue, namely the Fredholm determinant associated with a higher-order Airy kernel
[
42
,
83
]
. The analysis of this critical regime lies well beyond the scope of the present work, and we leave it for future investigation.

2.2.
Strategy of the proof

In this subsection, we outline the overall strategy of the proof. The main idea is to relate the free energy to the associated planar orthogonal polynomials defined in (
1.9
). For the reader’s convenience, we summarise the argument in the following steps.

•

We relate the variations of the free energy to the first few leading coefficients of the planar orthogonal polynomials; see Proposition
2.3
. In doing so, we consider deformations with respect to both the insertion point
a
and the non-Hermiticity parameter
\tau
.

•

We then derive the asymptotic behaviour of the coefficients of the planar orthogonal polynomials, in particular the first two subleading coefficients (Propositions
4.2
and
4.3
). For this, we make use of the theory and algorithm developed in
[
68
,
66
]
. Implementing this framework in a fully explicit manner in the present setting requires substantial work, including a number of conformal geometric computations. (From a methodological viewpoint, this marks a key distinction from
[
39
]
, where the coefficients were derived via Riemann–Hilbert analysis combined with a partial Schlesinger transform.)

•

Finally, to integrate the variations, we need to identify appropriate reference partition functions. In particular, in the doubly connected regime, it is essential to combine the deformations with respect to both
a
and
\tau
. The reference partition functions are then obtained from exact solvability (Lemma
4.1
). After this, we interpret all resulting terms within the framework of the general conjecture, leading to the form (
2.13
).

for a schematic summary of the overall strategy.

It follows from the well-known representation (

) that the free energy can be expressed in terms of the corresponding orthogonal norms. However, the right-hand side of (

This presents a difficulty, since even though there exist well-developed methods for analysing the asymptotic behaviour of orthogonal polynomials—such as the Riemann–Hilbert approach or the foliation flow framework—these techniques typically provide precise information only for polynomials of large degree, rather than for those of low degree.

On the other hand, the key idea used in
[
39
]
is that, by suitably deforming the free energy, its variation can be expressed solely in terms of finitely many orthogonal polynomials of highest degree. (This viewpoint is reminiscent of the philosophy in the classical Christoffel–Darboux formula.) We adopt a similar strategy in the present work; however, in our setting, this idea must be developed at a higher level of refinement and precision.

To be more precise, we relate the variations of the free energy with respect to the parameters to the leading-order coefficients of

(2.25)

The variations of the free energy are then expressed in terms of these coefficients as follows.

.

Let

(ii)

(

\tau

(2.27)

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\tau}\log\mathcal{Z}_{N}(Q)=-\frac{N}{1-\tau^{2}}\bigg[\tau(1+2c)N+\tau+(\mathcal{B}_{N,N}+\mathcal{B}_{N+1,N}-\mathcal{A}_{N,N}\mathcal{A}_{N+1,N})-\frac{2\tau a}{1+\tau}\mathcal{A}_{N,N}\bigg].

We note that, in the case
\tau=0

(see also

In contrast, in the present paper we provide a simple and elementary proof of Proposition

Remark 2.8

(Generality of the method)
.

In contrast to the earlier work
[
39
]
on the model (
1.12
), whose method is rather specifically tailored to that particular setting, the approach developed in the present paper has the possibility of being extended to a much broader class of potentials
Q
.

Indeed, although our main results are stated for the particular potential (
2.6
), many of the intermediate steps are developed in the more general framework of algebraic Hele-Shaw potentials of the form (
3.18
). These include the refined asymptotic analysis of planar orthogonal polynomials in Theorem
5.1
and Proposition
6.2
, which we regard as contributions of independent interest in the study of more general potentials.

Nevertheless, extending the free energy expansion to a genuinely broad class of potentials requires several additional ingredients that are not yet fully understood. For instance, one needs an appropriate deformation path preserving the topology of the droplet, as well as finer asymptotic expansions beyond those established in Theorem
5.1
. Once a suitable deformation framework is available, the strategy developed in the present paper strongly suggests that the first subleading correction term should already determine the
O(1)
contribution in the free energy expansion. On the other hand, finer asymptotic expansions still appear to be necessary in order to control the corresponding error terms rigorously.
This is one of the main reasons why, in the present work, we focus on the special potential (
2.6
), for which the earlier work
[
41
]
provides the necessary structural inputs.

At the same time, one of the principal messages of the present paper is that the general strategy developed here—namely, relating the asymptotics of planar orthogonal polynomials to conformally invariant geometric functionals—indeed works in a concrete nontrivial model (
2.6
). We regard the present work as a first step toward developing a more systematic framework for understanding free energy expansions in considerably broader settings.

3.
Preliminaries: conformal geometry, potential theory and orthogonal polynomials

In this section, we introduce several preliminary results that will be used later. While we mainly review well-known facts, we also provide self-contained proofs of certain folklore formulas adapted to our purposes. These include the variational formulas for the Liouville action (Proposition
3.1
) and the harmonic extension of Hele-Shaw potentials expressed in terms of the Schwarz function (Proposition
3.5
).

3.1.
Variational formula of the Liouville action

Recall that
\psi
is the exterior conformal map defined in (
2.1
). Unless stated otherwise, we always assume that the exterior conformal map extends holomorphically to an open neighbourhood of
\overline{\mathbb{D}}_{e}
.
Let
\Gamma=\partial D
denote the boundary of
D
, which we assume throughout to be a smooth, positively oriented Jordan curve.
Let
\phi=\psi^{-1}:D_{e}\to\mathbb{D}_{e}
.
Then the outward unit normal vector
\mathbf{n}
and the unit tangent vector
\mathbf{t}
to
\Gamma
at the point
z=\psi(w)
can be expressed in terms of
\psi
and
\phi
as

(3.1)

\mathbf{n}(z)=\frac{\phi(z)\overline{\phi^{\prime}(z)}}{|\phi^{\prime}(z)|}=\frac{w\,\psi^{\prime}(w)}{|\psi^{\prime}(w)|},\qquad\mathbf{t}(z)=i\mathbf{n}(z)=\frac{i\phi(z)\overline{\phi^{\prime}(z)}}{|\phi^{\prime}(z)|}=\frac{iw\,\psi^{\prime}(w)}{|\psi^{\prime}(w)|},

respectively.

Note that
\mathbf{n}
and
\mathbf{t}
extend from
\Gamma
to real-analytic vector fields in a neighbourhood of
D_{e}
, since
\phi^{\prime}
and
\psi^{\prime}
do not vanish on
D_{e}
.
For functions defined in a neighbourhood of
\Gamma
, the directional derivatives in the normal and tangential directions are given, respectively, by

(3.2)

\partial_{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{n}\partial+\bar{\mathbf{n}}\bar{\partial},\qquad\partial_{\mathbf{t}}=\mathbf{t}\partial+\bar{\mathbf{t}}\bar{\partial}=i(\mathbf{n}\partial-\bar{\mathbf{n}}\bar{\partial}).

We recall that the (signed) curvature
\kappa
of the curve
\Gamma
is given by

(3.3)

\kappa(z)=\kappa(\psi(w)):=-i\partial_{\mathbf{t}}\arg\mathbf{n}=\partial\mathbf{n}-\bar{\mathbf{n}}^{2}\bar{\partial}\mathbf{n}=\frac{1}{|\psi^{\prime}(w)|}\Big(1+\operatorname{Re}\frac{w\psi^{\prime\prime}(w)}{\psi^{\prime}(w)}\Big);

see also (
5.8
) for the derivation of the final identity.
Among the important objects in conformal computations is the
Schwarzian derivative
, defined as

(3.4)

\{\phi,z\}:=\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)}{\phi^{\prime}(z)}-\frac{3}{2}\Big(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\phi^{\prime}(z)}\Big)^{2}=N_{\phi}^{\prime}(z)-\frac{1}{2}N_{\phi}(z)^{2},

where

(3.5)

N_{\phi}(z):=\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\phi^{\prime}(z)}=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}z}\log\phi^{\prime}(z)

is the pre-Schwarzian derivative of
\psi
.

We now investigate the variational formula for the Liouville action associated with an exterior conformal map.
Consider a family of exterior conformal maps
\{\psi_{t}\}_{t\in I}
governed by a
Löwner–Kufarev equation

(3.6)

\dot{\psi}_{t}(w)=w\,\psi_{t}^{\prime}(w)P(t,w),\qquad w\in\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{e}.

Here,
\dot{X}
and
X^{\prime}
denote differentiation of
X
with respect to
t
and
w
, respectively.
We assume that
P(t,w)
is smooth in
t
and bounded and holomorphic on
\mathbb{D}_{e}
, and extends analytically to a neighbourhood of
\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{e}
with respect to
w
.
On the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
, the normal velocity of the evolution is given by

(3.7)

v_{\mathbf{n}}(t,w)=\dot{\psi}_{t}(w)\cdot\mathbf{n}=\operatorname{Re}\Big(\frac{\dot{\psi}_{t}(w)\overline{w\psi_{t}^{\prime}(w)}}{|\psi_{t}^{\prime}(w)|}\Big)=|\psi_{t}^{\prime}(w)|\operatorname{Re}P(t,w),\quad w\in\mathbb{T},

where
\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{n}(\psi_{t}(w))
denotes the outward unit normal vector at
\psi(w)\in\partial D
.

Recall that the Liouville action of an exterior conformal map is given by (
2.3
). Then we have the following.

Proposition 3.1

(
Variational formula of the Liouville action
)
.

Let
\{\psi_{t}\}_{t\in I}
be a family of exterior conformal maps evolving according to the Löwner–Kufarev equation (
3.6
).
Then the variation of the Liouville action with respect to the parameter
t
is given by

(3.8)

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{S}[\psi_{t}]=-\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Big(\operatorname{Re}(w^{2}\{\psi_{t},w\})+|\psi^{\prime}_{t}(w)|^{2}\kappa(\psi_{t}(w))^{2}\Big)\operatorname{Re}P(t,w)\,\mathrm{d}s(w).

Here,
\,\mathrm{d}s
is the arc length measure on curves.

The variational formula (
3.8
) and its derivation can be found in the physics literature; see
[
108
, Appendix B]
. Related variational formulas for the universal Liouville action, arising in the context of the Löwner energy of Weil–Petersson class loops, also appear in
[
98
]
. For the convenience of the reader, we provide here a self-contained and rigorous proof of Proposition
3.1
.

We also note that the special case
\operatorname{Re}P(t,w)=1/|\psi_{t}^{\prime}(w)|^{2}
of the variational formula (
3.8
) appears in
[
65
]
in the context of Hele-Shaw flows. Our argument may be viewed as an extension of the proof of
[
65
, Theorem 7.4.10]
to the general Löwner–Kufarev setting.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

As
\psi^{\prime}(w)
is a bounded holomorphic function on a neighbourhood of
\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{e}
, it follows that

(3.9)

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log\psi^{\prime}(w)\,\mathrm{d}s(w)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\log\psi^{\prime}(w)}{w}\,\mathrm{d}w=\log\psi^{\prime}(\infty).

Recall that
X^{\prime}
denotes the derivative of
X
with respect to
w
. Due to Löwner–Kufarev equation (
3.6
), we have

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\log\psi_{t}^{\prime}(w)=\Big(1+wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)\Big)P(t,w)+wP^{\prime}(t,w),

where
N_{\psi}
is the pre-Schwarzian derivative (
5.4
).
By using (
3.9
) and
\,\mathrm{d}\bar{w}=-i\bar{w}\,\mathrm{d}s(w)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{S}[\psi_{t}]

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{Re}\int_{\mathbb{D}_{e}}\overline{N_{\psi_{t}}(w)}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}N_{\psi_{t}}(w)\,\mathrm{d}A(w)-\frac{2}{\pi}\operatorname{Re}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\log\psi_{t}^{\prime}(w)\,\mathrm{d}s(w)

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{Re}\bigg[\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Big(\overline{N_{\psi_{t}}(w)}+\frac{2}{\bar{w}}\Big)\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\log\psi^{\prime}_{t}(w)\,\mathrm{d}\bar{w}\bigg]=-\operatorname{Re}\big(I_{1}+I_{2}\big),

where

(3.10)

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Big(2+\overline{wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)}\Big)\Big(1+wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)\Big)P(t,w)\,\mathrm{d}s(w),

(3.11)

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Big(2+\overline{wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)}\Big)wP^{\prime}(t,w)\,\mathrm{d}s(w).

Since
P(t,w)
and
N_{\psi_{t}}(w)
are bounded holomorphic functions on a neighbourhood of
\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{e}
with asymptotic behaviour

P(t,w)=O(1),\qquad N_{\psi_{t}}(w)=O(1/w^{2}),

as
w\to\infty
, it follows that

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)P(t,w)\,\mathrm{d}s(w)=\frac{1}{\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}N_{\psi_{t}}(w)P(t,w)\,\mathrm{d}w=0.

Consequently, we obtain

(3.12)

\operatorname{Re}I_{1}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Big(1+\Big|1+wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)\Big|^{2}\Big)\operatorname{Re}P(t,w)\,\mathrm{d}s(w).

On the other hand, since
\,\mathrm{d}P=iwP^{\prime}\,\,\mathrm{d}s
, integration by parts yields

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Big(2+\overline{wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}P(t,w)=-\frac{1}{\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}P(t,w)\overline{(wN_{\psi_{t}}(w))^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{w}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}P(t,w)\Big(\overline{w^{2}\{\psi_{t},w\}+\frac{1}{2}\big(wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)\big)^{2}+wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}s(w),

where we recall that
\{\psi_{t},w\}
denotes the Schwarzian derivative defined in (
3.4
).
Note that

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}P(t,w)\Big(w^{2}\{\psi_{t},w\}+\frac{1}{2}\big(wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)\big)^{2}+wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}s(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}P(t,w)\Big(w^{2}\{\psi_{t},w\}+\frac{1}{2}\big(wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)\big)^{2}+wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)\Big)\frac{\,\mathrm{d}w}{w}=0

since the integrand in the second expression is holomorphic on a neighbourhood of
\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{e}
and satisfies the asymptotic bound
O(1/w^{2})
as
w\to\infty
. Therefore, we obtain

(3.13)

\operatorname{Re}I_{2}=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\operatorname{Re}P(t,w)\operatorname{Re}\Big(w^{2}\{\psi_{t},w\}+\frac{1}{2}\big(wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)\big)^{2}+wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}s(w).

Combining (
3.12
) and (
3.13
), together with the identity

1+\Big|1+wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)\Big|^{2}+\operatorname{Re}\big(wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)\big)^{2}+2\operatorname{Re}\big(wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)\big)=2\Big(1+\operatorname{Re}\big(wN_{\psi_{t}}(w)\big)\Big)^{2}=2|\psi^{\prime}_{t}(w)|^{2}\kappa(\psi_{t}(w))^{2},

where
\kappa
is the curvature defined in (

), we obtain the desired conclusion (
3.8
).
∎

3.2.
Algebraic Hele-Shaw potentials and quadrature domains

The use of conformal mapping methods in the study of the macroscopic behaviour of two-dimensional Coulomb gases has been extensively explored in the physics literature
[
106
,
90
,
79
]
, particularly in connection with integrable hierarchies and string equations. The underlying idea is to consider the conformal map from the droplet onto a reference domain, typically the unit disc, and to characterise this map via a Liouville-type argument; see e.g.
[
28
, Appendix A]
for further exposition.

A central concept in this construction, particularly for defining the analytic continuation of the conformal map to the entire complex plane, is the
Schwarz function
.
To recall its definition, let
\Omega\subsetneq\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a domain, not necessarily bounded, such that
\Omega=\operatorname{Int}\overline{\Omega}
and
\infty\notin\partial\Omega
. By definition, the Schwarz function of
\Omega
is a continuous function
\mathsf{S}_{\Omega}:\overline{\Omega}\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
which is meromorphic in
\Omega
and satisfies

(3.14)

\mathsf{S}_{\Omega}(z)=\bar{z},\quad\text{for }z\in\partial\Omega.

In particular, for an exterior domain
D_{e}
and its conformal map
\psi:\mathbb{D}_{e}\to D_{e}
we have

(3.15)

\overline{\mathsf{S}_{D_{e}}(z)}=\psi(1/\overline{\psi^{-1}(z)}),\quad z\in\bar{D}_{e}.

Using conformal mapping methods, the equilibrium measure associated with external potentials of the form

(3.16)

Q(z)=\frac{1}{t_{0}}\Big(|z|^{2}-2\operatorname{Re}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{t_{j}}{j}z^{j}\Big),\quad t_{0}>0,\;t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\in\mathbb{C},

was derived in
[
58
]
. (For
k\geq 3
, this requires an appropriate localisation to a bounded domain.) Observe that
\Delta Q=1/t_{0}
is constant, which implies that the corresponding equilibrium measure
\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{W}
has uniform density
1/(\pi t_{0})
on its droplet
K
. Moreover, the coefficients
t_{j}
in (
3.16
) encode the area and the exterior harmonic moments of
K
: for
j=1,\dots,k
,

(3.17)

t_{0}=\frac{1}{\pi}\text{area }K,\qquad t_{j}=-\int_{K^{c}}z^{-j}\,\mathrm{d}A(z).

The boundary of the droplet
\partial K
is identified as an algebraic curve, often referred to as a polynomial curve. Equivalently, the exterior conformal map
\psi:\mathbb{D}_{e}\to K_{e}
is a rational function of the form

\psi(w)=R\Big(w+a_{0}+\frac{a_{1}}{w}+\ldots+\frac{a_{k}}{w^{k}}\Big).

In the seminal paper
[
85
]
, Lee and Makarov extended this class of external potentials to a broader family exhibiting similar properties, namely the
algebraic Hele-Shaw potentials
, defined as

(3.18)

Q(z)=\frac{1}{t_{0}}\Big(|z|^{2}-H(z)\Big),\qquad H(z):\text{real harmonic and}\;\;\partial H(z):\text{rational}.

One observes that the polynomial potentials (
3.16
), as well as the potentials with insertions (
1.11
), (
1.12
), (
2.6
), all belong to this class.

Here we recall some basic notions from
[
85
]
. Given an algebraic Hele-Shaw potential
Q
, a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{C}
is called a
local droplet

1
1

1

The definition of local droplets presented here corresponds to
non-maximal
local droplets in
[
85
]
. We will always refer to non-maximal local droplets throughout the paper.

of
Q
if there exists an open neighbourhood
U
of
K
such that
K
is the droplet associated with a localisation of
Q
to
U
:

(3.19)

Q_{\bar{U}}=Q\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\bar{U}}+\infty\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\bar{U}^{c}}.

Note that the (global) droplet of
Q
is, in particular, a local droplet of
Q
. The first part of the Lee–Makarov theorem relates local droplets to domains with a special property, known as
quadrature domains
.

A domain
\Omega
is called a quadrature domain if there exists a rational function
r_{\Omega}
such that the following
quadrature identity
holds:

(3.20)

\int_{\Omega}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}A(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Omega}f(z)r_{\Omega}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z,

for all analytic functions
f
on
\Omega
that are integrable and continuous on
\overline{\Omega}
.
It is evident that if
\Omega
is a quadrature domain, such rational function
r_{\Omega}
is uniquely determined. We call
r_{\Omega}
the
quadrature function
of
\Omega
. One of the main results in
[
85
]
, relating this property to the equilibrium measure problem, is the following.

Theorem 3.2

(
cf. Theorem 3.3 in

[
85
]
)
.

Let
K
be a local droplet associated with an algebraic Hele-Shaw potential
Q
of the form (
3.18
). Then the complement
K^{c}
is a finite union of disjoint quadrature domains
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{q}
, whose corresponding quadrature functions
r_{1},\ldots,r_{q}
satisfy

(3.21)

r_{1}(z)+\ldots+r_{q}(z)=\partial H(z).

It is well known that a domain
\Omega
is a quadrature domain if and only if it admits a Schwarz function. In this case, the Schwarz function and the quadrature function are related by

(3.22)

\mathsf{S}_{\Omega}(z)=r_{\Omega}(z)+\int_{\Omega^{c}}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta)}{z-\zeta},\quad z\in\bar{\Omega}.

Another useful characterisation of local droplets is given as follows.

Lemma 3.3

(
cf. Lemma 3.2 in

[
85
]
)
.

Given an algebraic Hele-Shaw potential
Q
of the form (
3.18
), a compact set
K
is a local droplet if and only if
\mathrm{area}(K)=\pi t_{0}
and there exists a constant
C
such that

(3.23)

C=\int_{K}\log\frac{1}{|z-\zeta|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta)+t_{0}Q(z),

for all
z\in K
.

From the viewpoint of logarithmic potential theory, Lemma
3.3
remains valid for droplets associated with general external potentials; see
[
95
]
. The constant
C
is commonly referred to as the
(modified) Robin constant
. If
K
is not only a local droplet but also the (global) droplet of
Q
, we denote
C=C_{Q}
and refer to it as the Robin constant associated with the external potential
Q
.

We also note that a classical result
[
1
]
asserts that a simply connected domain is a quadrature domain if and only if its conformal map is rational, in agreement with the polynomial curves obtained in
[
58
]
. This characterisation has been used to derive explicit conformal maps of droplets arising in the context of non-Hermitian random matrix theory; see
[
16
,
28
,
35
,
41
,
2
]
.

An alternative, yet equivalent, approach to computing the conformal map is to solve the string equation in terms of exterior moments
[
106
,
58
]
; see also (
5.35
) below. For explicit implementations of this method and its connection to vector equilibrium problems, we refer to
[
23
,
77
]
.

The second part of the Lee–Makarov theorem in
[
85
]
classifies all possible topologies of the droplet for a given degree of the rational function
\partial H
, under the assumption that the boundary is non-singular. This result also played a crucial role in the authors’ previous work
[
41
]
, where it was used to determine all possible topological phases corresponding to the external potential (
2.6
).
For boundaries with singularities, such as cusps or double points, a classification including the number and types of singularities has been recently obtained in
[
91
]
. For further background on quadrature domains, we refer to
[
63
]
and the references therein.

For a simply connected (unbounded) quadrature domain, the Liouville action (
2.3
) admits an explicit representation due to the rationality of the conformal map. Suppose that the normalised exterior conformal map is given by a rational function

(3.24)

\psi^{\prime}(w)=\psi^{\prime}(\infty)\frac{(w-p_{1})\ldots(w-p_{r})}{(w-q_{1})\ldots(w-q_{r})}.

By standard residue computations (see, for instance,
[
108
, Section 6]
), it is straightforward to verify that

(3.25)

\mathcal{S}[\psi]=-4\log\psi^{\prime}(\infty)-\sum_{j=1}^{r}\log\psi^{\prime}(1/\bar{p}_{j})+\sum_{j=1}^{r}\log\psi^{\prime}(1/\bar{q}_{j}).

Using (
3.25
), we obtain explicit expressions for the Liouville action of connected domains; see Remark
2.2
.

3.3.
Determinantal Coulomb gases and planar orthogonal polynomials

As previously mentioned, a remarkable feature of determinantal Coulomb gases is their underlying integrable structure. We briefly review several aspects of this structure, together with the basic properties of the planar orthogonal polynomials (
1.9
).

Given an external potential
Q
satisfying the growth condition

(3.26)

\liminf_{|z|\to\infty}\frac{Q(z)}{2\log|z|}>1,

we define the Bergman polynomial kernel associated with
L^{2}(\mathbb{C},e^{-NQ(z)}\,\mathrm{d}A)
by

(3.27)

\mathbf{K}_{N}(z,z^{\prime})=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{P_{n,N}(z)\overline{P_{n,N}(z^{\prime})}}{h_{n,N}},

where
P_{n,N}
are the monic planar orthogonal polynomials defined via the orthogonality relation (
1.9
). Note that
\mathbf{K}_{N}
is the reproducing kernel of the space of complex polynomials of degree less than
N
, endowed with the inner product

(3.28)

\langle p_{1},p_{2}\rangle_{e^{-NQ}\,\mathrm{d}A}:=\int_{\mathbb{C}}p_{1}(z)\overline{p_{2}(z)}e^{-NQ(z)}\,\mathrm{d}A(z).

The Bergman polynomial kernel encodes the
m
-point correlation functions of the eigenvalues. In particular, the one-point function of the determinantal Coulomb gas (
1.1
) can be expressed as

(3.29)

\rho_{N}(z):=\frac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{N-1}}\mathbb{P}_{N}(z,z_{2},\ldots,z_{N})\prod_{j=2}^{N}\,\mathrm{d}A(z_{j})=\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{K}_{N}(z,z)e^{-NQ(z)}.

The asymptotic behaviour of planar orthogonal polynomials has been extensively studied for special classes of external potentials using the Riemann–Hilbert approach. An incomplete list of works in this direction includes
[
16
,
34
,
22
,
54
,
77
,
87
,
88
,
20
,
17
]
.
In addition, fine asymptotic expansions (up to an error of order
O(N^{-k_{0}-1})
) can be obtained via partial Schlesinger transformations; see e.g.
[
21
,
87
,
88
,
39
,
81
,
40
]
.

For more general potentials, a major breakthrough was achieved by Hedenmalm and Wennman
[
68
]
, who generalised results from special cases within the framework of foliation flow. This approach was later reformulated in
[
66
]
in terms of a soft Riemann–Hilbert problem.
Here we present the main theorem of
[
68
,
66
]
in a form suited to our purposes.

Theorem 3.4

(
cf. Theorem 1.3 in
[
68
]
, Theorem 1.2 in
[
66
]
)
.

Let
Q
be a
C^{2}
-smooth external potential satisfying the growth condition (
3.26
), associated with the connected (possibly multiply connected) droplet
K
.
Let
\Omega_{\infty}
denote the unbounded component of
K^{c}
, and let
\Gamma=\partial\Omega_{\infty}
be the outermost boundary of
K
.

Let
\phi:\Omega_{\infty}\to\mathbb{D}_{e}
be the inverse of normalised exterior conformal map of
\Omega_{\infty}
, and let
\mathcal{Q}
be the unique bounded holomorphic function in a fixed neighbourhood of
\overline{\Omega}_{\infty}
such that
\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{Q}=Q
on
\Gamma
and
\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{Q}(\infty)=0
. We further assume that
Q
is real-analytic and strictly subharmonic in a neighbourhood of
\Gamma
.

Then, for any positive integer
k_{0}
, there exist bounded holomorphic functions
F_{k}
(
k=0,1,\ldots,k_{0}
), defined in a fixed neighbourhood of
\Omega_{\infty}
, such that

(3.30)

P_{N,N}(z)=(\phi^{\prime}(\infty))^{-N-1}(\phi(z))^{N}e^{N\mathcal{Q}(z)/2}\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}}N^{-k}F_{k}(z)+O(N^{-k_{0}-1})\Big),

as
N\to\infty
, where the error term is uniform for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying

(3.31)

\textup{dist}(z,\Omega_{\infty})\leq(N^{-1}\log N)^{1/2}.

Remark 3.1

(Hedenmalm–Wennman theorem for
M=tN
)
.

The asymptotic expansion (
3.30
) also remains valid for the general planar orthogonal polynomial
P_{M,N}(z)
in the regime
M=tN\to\infty
where
t>0
is fixed. Indeed, if the external potential
Q
satisfies the growth condition

(3.32)

\liminf_{|z|\to\infty}\frac{Q(z)}{2\log|z|}>t

for some
t>0
, in place of (
3.26
), then Theorem
3.4
applies after the rescaling
Q\mapsto Q/t
.

More precisely, suppose that the rescaled external potential
Q/t
satisfies (
3.26
) together with the assumptions of Theorem
3.4
. Let
K
,
\Omega_{\infty}
,
\Gamma
, and
\phi
be defined analogously for the potential
Q/t
. Furthermore, let
\mathcal{Q}
denote the unique bounded holomorphic function in a fixed neighbourhood of
\overline{\Omega}_{\infty}
satisfying
\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{Q}=Q
on
\Gamma
and
\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{Q}(\infty)=0
. Then,
there exist bounded holomorphic functions
F_{k}(\cdot;t)

(k=0,1,\ldots,k_{0})
such that

(3.33)

P_{M,N}(z)=(\phi^{\prime}(\infty))^{-M-1}(\phi(z))^{M}e^{N\mathcal{Q}(z)/2}\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}}N^{-k}F_{k}(z;t)+O(N^{-k_{0}-1})\Big),

for
z\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying (
3.31
)
as
M=tN\to\infty

As we explain below, the coefficients
F_{k}(z;t)
are completely determined by the local behaviour of the potential
Q
near
\Gamma
. In particular,
F_{k}(z;t)
depend continuously on
t

\phi
varies continuously with respect to
t
. In this situation, the outermost boundary
\Gamma
deforms continuously, and the droplet
K
undergoes no topological phase transition.

Remark 3.2

(Hedenmalm–Wennman theorem for multiply connected droplets)
.

One aspect of the statement in Theorem
3.4
that differs from its original formulation in
[
68
,
66
]
is that it applies not only to simply connected droplets, but also to multiply connected ones, provided that the droplet itself is connected. Indeed, although the authors state their main results for the simply connected case, the proof extends to the multiply connected setting as well (as confirmed by the authors).

To give an intuitive explanation, we note that the harmonic measure at infinity for
\Omega_{\infty}
, supported on
\Gamma
, is asymptotically given by
|P_{N,N}|^{2}e^{-NQ}
; see, for instance,
[
67
,
16
,
14
]
. Formally, this suggests that the asymptotic behaviour of
P_{N,N}
observed at infinity is governed by data supported in a neighbourhood of
\Gamma
, and is therefore insensitive to the multiple connectedness of the droplet (or the presence of non-trivial inner boundary components); cf.
[
13
]
.

Remark 3.3

(Hedenmalm–Wennman theorem for localised potentials)
.

Although Theorem
3.4
assumes that the external potential is
C^{2}
-smooth on the entire complex plane, this assumption is only needed to ensure the regularity of
\partial K
and of the harmonic continuation
\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{Q}
; see
[
67
]
and
[
68

.

Consequently, Theorem
3.4
also applies to localised algebraic Hele-Shaw potentials (
3.19
), provided that the associated local droplet exists. Indeed, the boundary of a local droplet is a union of algebraic curves
[
63
,
85
]
, and the harmonic continuation
\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{Q}
admits the explicit representation (
3.36
). Furthermore, the localised potential (
3.19
) is real-analytic and strictly subharmonic on a neighbourhood of
K
. See Theorem
5.1
below.

In addition to the asymptotic expansion (
3.30
), a constructive algorithm for deriving the subleading terms

is presented in
[
68
,
66
]

We conclude this subsection by briefly outlining the algorithm for constructing the terms

in Theorem

Proposition 3.5

Let

For completeness, we provide a proof in a more general setting using the quadrature domain framework of

Proof of Proposition
3.5
.

Note that by (
3.34
), it is enough to show that

\mathcal{Q}(z)=Q(z_{0})+\frac{2}{t_{0}}\int_{z_{0}}^{z}\big(\mathsf{S}_{\Omega_{\infty}}(u)-\partial H(u)\big)\,\mathrm{d}u-2\log\phi(z)

for an arbitrarily chosen point
z_{0}\in\Gamma=\partial\Omega_{\infty}
. To establish (
3.36
), it suffices to verify the characterising properties of
\mathcal{Q}
, namely that
\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{Q}=Q
on
\Gamma
and
\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{Q}(\infty)=0
. The first property follows immediately, since
\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{Q}(z_{0})=Q(z_{0})
and, by differentiating the right-hand side of (

), we obtain

\frac{1}{t_{0}}\big(\bar{z}-\partial H(z)\big)=\partial Q(z),\quad z\in\Gamma,

where we used that
\mathsf{S}_{\Omega_{\infty}}(z)=\bar{z}
on
\Gamma=\partial\Omega_{\infty}
. It therefore remains to show that
\mathcal{Q}
is bounded near infinity and
\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{Q}(\infty)=0
.

Since
\partial H
is rational, there exist constants
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
t_{j}
,
s_{0}
, and
s_{1}
such that

H(z)=|z|^{2}-t_{0}Q(z)=2\operatorname{Re}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{t_{j}}{j}z^{j}+s_{0}+s_{1}\log z+O(\frac{1}{z})\bigg),

as
z\to\infty
.
On the other hand, by Theorem
3.2
, we have

(3.39)

K^{c}=\Omega_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\Omega_{d}\cup\Omega_{\infty},

where
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{d}
are disjoint, simply connected, bounded quadrature domains, and
\Omega_{\infty}
is the simply connected unbounded quadrature domain. Let

and

denote their respective quadrature functions.

Note that, by (

), the quadrature function

is uniquely determined by the requirement that it captures the principal part of

at infinity. In particular, it admits the expansion

(3.40)

r_{\infty}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}z^{j-1}+O(\frac{1}{z}),\qquad z\to\infty.

On the other hand, by the quadrature identity (

) for
\Omega_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,d

\frac{1}{\pi}\text{area }\Omega_{j}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Omega_{j}}r_{j}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=-\Res_{z=\infty}r_{j}.

It follows that

has a simple pole at infinity with residue

, and hence

r_{j}(z)=\frac{\text{area }\Omega_{j}}{\pi z}+O(\frac{1}{z^{2}}),\qquad z\to\infty.

Combining the above with (

\mathrm{area}(K)=\pi t_{0}

\displaystyle\mathsf{S}_{\Omega_{\infty}}(z)-\partial H(z)

\displaystyle=r_{\infty}(z)+\int_{\Omega_{\infty}^{c}}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta)}{z-\zeta}-\partial H(z)=\int_{\Omega_{\infty}^{c}}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta)}{z-\zeta}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}r_{j}(z)

Notice that, by definition of the normalised exterior conformal map (

), we have

(3.42)

\log\phi(z)=\log\phi^{\prime}(\infty)+\log z+O(\frac{1}{z}),\qquad z\to\infty.

Therefore,

Remark 3.4

As a byproduct of the above proof, we also verify that (

We proceed to give a brief overview of the algorithm in
[
66
]
for constructing the functions

; a more detailed discussion will be given in Section
5
with a concrete example.
We write

(3.44)

F=\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}}N^{-k}F_{k}+O(N^{-k_{0}-1}),

where
F_{k}
are bounded holomorphic functions in a fixed neighbourhood of
\overline{\Omega}_{\infty}
. Our goal is to determine the coefficients
F_{k}
to the desired order of precision.

In addition, we introduce auxiliary functions
A
and
B
via the asymptotic expansions

(3.45)

A=\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}}N^{-k}A_{k}+O(N^{-k_{0}-1}),\qquad B=\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}}N^{-k}B_{k}+O(N^{-k_{0}-1}),

where
A_{k}
are bounded holomorphic functions in a fixed neighbourhood of
\overline{\Omega}_{\infty}
, while
B_{k}
are real-analytic functions defined in a neighbourhood of
\Gamma
. These functions
A
and
B
arise in the ansatz for the asymptotic expansion of the orthogonal polynomials; see
[
66
, Eq. (1.6.5)]
.
Finally, we impose the normalisation conditions

(3.46)

F(z)=\phi^{\prime}(\infty)+O(\frac{1}{z}),\qquad A(z)=O(\frac{1}{z}),

as
z\to\infty
.

Since
R(z)=0
and
\Delta R=\Delta Q>0
on
\Gamma
, there exists a real-analytic function
\widehat{R}
defined in a neighbourhood of
\Gamma
such that

(3.47)

\widehat{R}^{2}=R

and
\widehat{R}>0
in a neighbourhood outside
\Gamma
. Based on the soft Riemann–Hilbert formulation, the
master equation
governing the asymptotic functions
F
,
A
, and
B
takes the form

(3.48)

\sqrt{2}A\bar{\partial}\widehat{R}+\frac{1}{N}\bar{\partial}B-B\bar{\partial}R=\sqrt{2\pi}\bar{F}

on a fixed small neighbourhood of
\Gamma
.
2
2

2

Note that (
3.48
) differs from
[
66
, Eq. (1.7.3)]
by certain constant factors of

, due to our choice of planar measure

rather than
e^{-2NQ}\,\,\mathrm{d}A
.

The master equation (
3.48
) can be solved iteratively via the following two steps. First, since
\bar{\partial}R
vanishes on
\Gamma
, the equation at order
k
in the
1/N
-expansion of (

) reduces to a Riemann–Hilbert jump problem

(3.49)

\sqrt{2}A_{k}\bar{\partial}\widehat{R}+\bar{\partial}B_{k-1}=\sqrt{2\pi}\bar{F}_{k},

where
B_{k-1}
has already been constructed at the previous stage of the iteration. Since
A
and
F
are bounded and holomorphic in a neighbourhood of
\overline{\Omega}_{\infty}
, the jump problem can be solved using the Szegő projection operator
\mathbf{P}_{H^{2}_{-,0}}:L^{2}(\mathbb{T})\to H^{2}_{-,0}
, defined by

(3.50)

\Big(\mathbf{P}_{H^{2}_{-,0}}f\circ\phi\Big)(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{wf(w)}{\phi(z)-w}\,\mathrm{d}s(w).

Here,

denotes the codimension-one subspace of the conjugate Hardy space

consisting of functions that vanish at infinity. The elements of

are identified with holomorphic functions on

.

Second, the presence of the factor
1/N
in front of

implies that the zeroth-order equation of (

) involves only
A
and
\overline{F}
. Thus, once
A
and
F
have been determined up to order

, the coefficient

can be obtained by substituting

,

, and

into (

Consequently, starting from the zeroth-order equation on
\Gamma
, this procedure yields a constructive scheme for computing

,
B
, and

to arbitrary order.

To solve the master equation (
3.48
), a key step is to compute the derivatives of

in a neighbourhood of
\Gamma
. We note that the Taylor expansion of

near
\Gamma
completely determines the asymptotic expansion of

Moreover, for algebraic Hele-Shaw potentials, the representation (

based on these observations.

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\tau}\log\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}

To compute the first term on the right-hand side, we compare coefficients and observe that

(4.20)

\displaystyle\begin{split}z^{2}\widetilde{P}_{n,N}(z)&=\widetilde{P}_{n+2,N}(z)+(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n,N}-\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n+2,N})\widetilde{P}_{n+1,N}(z)\\
&\quad+\Big(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{n,N}-\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{n+2,N}-(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n,N}-\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n+2,N})\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n+1,N}\Big)\widetilde{P}_{n,N}(z)+O(z^{n-1}).\end{split}

This gives rise to

(4.21)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\quad N\int_{\mathbb{C}}z^{2}\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}_{N}(z,z)e^{-N\widetilde{Q}(z)}\,\mathrm{d}A(z)=N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{z^{2}|\widetilde{P}_{n,N}(z)|^{2}}{\widetilde{h}_{n,N}}e^{-N\widetilde{Q}(z)}\,\mathrm{d}A(z)\\
&=N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\Big(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{n,N}-\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{n+2,N}-(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n,N}-\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n+2,N})\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n+1,N}\Big)\\
&=-N\Big(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{N,N}+\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{N+1,N}-\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{N,N}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{N+1,N}\Big)=-\frac{N}{1-\tau^{2}}\Big(\mathcal{B}_{N,N}+\mathcal{B}_{N+1,N}-\mathcal{A}_{N,N}\mathcal{A}_{N+1,N}\Big).\end{split}

Finally, by combining (
4.17
), (
4.18
), (
4.19
) and (
4.21
) we obtain the desired identity (
2.27
).
∎

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\tau}\log\mathcal{Z}_{N}(0,c,\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau c^{2}}{1-\tau^{2}}N^{2}-\frac{\tau}{1-\tau^{2}}N+O(N^{-\infty}),

(4.30)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}a}\mathcal{Z}_{N}(a,c,\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{2ca}{1+\tau}N^{2}+O(N^{-\infty}).

Combining (

\displaystyle\begin{split}\log\mathcal{Z}_{N}(Q)&=-\Big(\frac{3}{4}+\frac{3c}{2}+\frac{c^{2}}{2}\log\Big(c(1-\tau^{2})\Big)-\frac{(1+c)^{2}}{2}\log(1+c)-\frac{ca^{2}}{1+\tau}\Big)\,N^{2}\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{2}N\log N+\Big(\frac{\log(2\pi)}{2}-1+\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\tau^{2})\Big)N+\frac{1}{2}\log N+\frac{\log(2\pi)}{2}+\frac{1}{12}\log\Big(\frac{c}{1+c}\Big)\\

Here, the integration of the error terms is justified by their uniformity over the relevant parameter range.

It remains to verify that the coefficients appearing in the expansion (

Observe first that the leading

For the constant term, recall that the droplet is given by

Applying (

Furthermore, since

\frac{\log(2\pi)}{2}+\chi\zeta^{\prime}(-1)+\frac{1}{24}\mathcal{L}[K_{Q}]-\frac{1}{24\pi}\int_{\partial K_{Q}}\log(\Delta Q)\kappa\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{\log(2\pi)}{2}+\frac{1}{12}\log\Big(\frac{c}{1+c}\Big),

We now turn to the simply connected case. In contrast to the doubly connected setting, it suffices to consider only the deformation with respect to

By (

),

\log\mathcal{Z}_{N}(Q)=(2c\log a)N^{2}+g_{N}(a),

(4.35)

g_{N}(a):=\log N!+\log\det\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{C}}z^{j}\bar{z}^{k}|1-z/a|^{2cN}e^{-NQ^{\rm e}(z)}\,\mathrm{d}A(z)\bigg]_{j,k=0}^{N-1}.

It follows from Propositions
2.3

(4.36)

g_{N}^{\prime}(a)=-\Big(\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}a}I_{Q}[\mu_{Q}]+\frac{2c}{a}\Big)N^{2}+\frac{1}{24}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}a}\mathcal{S}[\psi]+E_{N}(a),

where the error term

for some bounded continuous function
\mathsf{f}
, by the uniformity of the error estimates. Recall here that these error bounds remain valid in the limit

.

Now, the free energy expansion essentially follows by integrating (

, and then combining the result with (

) and the reference free energy given in Lemma

. However, since the integration is performed over a non-compact domain, one needs to check that the error term

is integrable and that its contribution remains of order

For this purpose, we first claim that

admits an asymptotic expansion in powers of

, i.e. for each non-negative integer

such that

(4.37)

g_{N}(a)=\log\mathcal{Z}_{N}(Q^{\rm e})+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{h_{\ell}(N)}{a^{\ell}}+O\Big(\frac{1}{a^{L+1}}\Big),\qquad a\to\infty.

By the determinantal representation (
4.35
), it suffices to verify that

F(a):=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|1-z/a|^{2cN}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}A(z)

admits an asymptotic expansion in powers of

for every Schwartz function

on
\mathbb{C}
.
Let

denote the Taylor polynomial of degree

such that

\Big||1-u|^{2cN}-P_{L}(u,\bar{u})\Big|\leq C|u|^{L+1},\quad|u|\leq\delta.

It follows that

\displaystyle\bigg|F(a)-\int_{\mathbb{C}}P_{L}(z/a,\bar{z}/a)f(z)\,\mathrm{d}A(z)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|z|\leq\delta a}\Big||1-z/a|^{2cN}-P_{L}(z/a,\bar{z}/a)\Big||f(z)|\,\mathrm{d}A(z)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{a^{L+1}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|z|^{L+1}|f(z)|\,\mathrm{d}A(z)=O\Big(\frac{1}{a^{L+1}}\Big),

where the final integral is finite since

is a Schwartz function.
Note that

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{|z|>\delta a}P_{L}(z/a,\bar{z}/a)f(z)\,\mathrm{d}A(z)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\int_{|z|>\delta a}|z/a|^{L}|f(z)|\,\mathrm{d}A(z)

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\Big(\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}}|z|^{L+3}|f(z)|\Big)\int_{\delta a}^{\infty}\frac{2\,\mathrm{d}r}{a^{L}r^{2}}=O\Big(\frac{1}{a^{L+1}}\Big)

for some constant

. Also, we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{|z|>\delta a}|1-z/a|^{2cN}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}A(z)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\Big(1+\frac{1}{\delta}\Big)^{2cN}\int_{|z|>\delta a}|z/a|^{2cN}|f(z)|\,\mathrm{d}A(z)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(1+\frac{1}{\delta}\Big)^{2cN}\Big(\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}}|z|^{2cN+L+3}|f(z)|\Big)\int_{|z|>\delta a}\frac{2\,\mathrm{d}r}{a^{2cN}r^{L+2}}=O\Big(\frac{1}{a^{L+1}}\Big).

Combining these estimates, it follows that

\bigg|F(a)-\int_{\mathbb{C}}P_{L}(z/a,\bar{z}/a)f(z)\,\mathrm{d}A(z)\bigg|=O\Big(\frac{1}{a^{L+1}}\Big),\qquad a\to\infty.

Therefore, it follows that

admits an asymptotic expansion in powers of
1/a
.
In particular, since
P_{0}(u,\bar{u})=1
, we have

F(a)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}A(z)+O\Big(\frac{1}{a}\Big),\qquad a\to\infty.

Then, by (
4.35
), we obtain the desired expansion (
4.37
).

Differentiating (

) with respect to

, we have

(4.38)

g_{N}^{\prime}(a)=-\frac{h_{1}(N)}{a^{2}}+O\Big(\frac{1}{a^{3}}\Big),\qquad a\to\infty.

Comparing with (

and that

Since these estimates justify the term-by-term integration of the asymptotic expansion of

, integrating (

together with (

(4.41)

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{Z}_{N}(Q)

\displaystyle=\log\mathcal{Z}_{N}(Q^{\rm e})-\Big(I_{Q}[\mu_{Q}]-\lim_{a\to\infty}(I_{Q}[\mu_{Q}]+2c\log a)\Big)N^{2}+\frac{1}{24}(\mathcal{S}[\psi]-\lim_{a\to\infty}\mathcal{S}[\psi])+O(N^{-1}),

.

Observe that by (

\displaystyle I_{Q}[\mu_{Q}]+2c\log a=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\log\frac{1}{|z-w|}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{Q}(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{Q}(w)+\int_{\mathbb{C}}\Big(Q^{\rm e}(z)-2c\log|1-z/a|\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{Q}(z).

Similarly, continuity of the Liouville action yields

since the droplet

Remark 5.1

We expect that Theorem

The proof of Theorem

To facilitate these computations, we first collect several useful identities relating these objects. Although the derivations are fairly elementary, we include them for completeness. In the context of random matrix theory, closely related computations appeared in

) as

(5.4)

\frac{\partial\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{n}}=\frac{\phi^{\prime}}{\phi}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}},\qquad\frac{\bar{\partial}\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{n}}=\frac{1}{2}\overline{\Big(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}}\Big)}.

Furthermore, the derivative of the pre-Schwarzian admits two different representations:

\displaystyle\partial\Big(\frac{\partial\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{n}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi\phi^{\prime\prime}-(\phi^{\prime})^{2}}{\phi^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}}\Big)^{\prime}=-\Big(\frac{\phi^{\prime}}{\phi}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}}\Big)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\Big(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}}\Big)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}}\Big)^{\prime},

\displaystyle\partial\Big(\frac{\partial\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{n}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{n}}-\Big(\frac{\partial\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{n}}\Big)^{2}=\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{n}}-\Big(\frac{\phi^{\prime}}{\phi}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}}\Big)^{2}.

Comparing the two expressions yields the identity

(5.5)

\{\phi,z\}=\Big(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}}\Big)^{\prime}(z)-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}}\Big)^{2}(z)=-2\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{n}(z)}{\mathbf{n}(z)},

valid for
z
in a neighbourhood of
\overline{K^{c}}
. Similarly, using the second identity in (
5.4
), we obtain

(5.6)

\overline{\{\phi,z\}}=\overline{\Big(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}}\Big)^{\prime}}(z)-\frac{1}{2}\overline{\Big(\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}}{\phi^{\prime}}\Big)^{2}}(z)=2\frac{\bar{\partial}^{2}\mathbf{n}(z)}{\mathbf{n}(z)}-4\frac{(\bar{\partial}\mathbf{n})^{2}(z)}{\mathbf{n}^{2}(z)}.

Next, we derive an identity for the tangential derivative of the curvature
\kappa
:

(5.7)

\partial_{\mathbf{t}}\kappa(z)=\operatorname{Im}(\mathbf{n}^{2}(z)\{\phi,z\}),\quad z\in\Gamma.

To verify this identity, observe first that since
|\mathbf{n}(z)|=1
on
\Gamma
, it follows from (
5.5
) and (
5.6
) that

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}^{2}(z)\{\phi,z\}-\overline{\mathbf{n}^{2}(z)\{\phi,z\}}

\displaystyle=-2\mathbf{n}(z)(\partial^{2}\mathbf{n})(z)-2\bar{\mathbf{n}}^{3}(z)(\bar{\partial}^{2}\mathbf{n})(z)+4\bar{\mathbf{n}}^{4}(z)(\bar{\partial}\mathbf{n})^{2}(z).

Furthermore,

0=-i\partial_{\mathbf{t}}|\mathbf{n}|^{2}=\mathbf{n}^{2}(\partial\bar{\mathbf{n}})+\partial\mathbf{n}-\bar{\mathbf{n}}^{2}(\bar{\partial}\mathbf{n})-\bar{\partial}\bar{\mathbf{n}}.

Since
\partial(\bar{\partial}\mathbf{n}/\mathbf{n})=0
by the second identity in (
5.4
), we obtain

\partial\bar{\partial}\mathbf{n}=\partial\Big(\mathbf{n}\cdot\frac{\bar{\partial}\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{n}}\Big)=\frac{\partial\mathbf{n}\bar{\partial}\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{n}}.

Combining all of the above, we have

\displaystyle i\partial_{\mathbf{t}}\kappa

\displaystyle=-(\mathbf{n}\partial-\bar{\mathbf{n}}\bar{\partial})(\partial\mathbf{n}-\bar{\mathbf{n}}^{2}\bar{\partial}\mathbf{n})=-\mathbf{n}(\partial^{2}\mathbf{n})+2\bar{\mathbf{n}}\bar{\partial}\partial\mathbf{n}-\bar{\mathbf{n}}^{3}(\bar{\partial}^{2}\mathbf{n})+2(\partial\bar{\mathbf{n}}-\bar{\mathbf{n}}^{2}\bar{\partial}\bar{\mathbf{n}})(\bar{\partial}\mathbf{n})

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{n}(\partial^{2}\mathbf{n})+2\bar{\mathbf{n}}\bar{\partial}\partial\mathbf{n}-\bar{\mathbf{n}}^{3}(\bar{\partial}^{2}\mathbf{n})-2(\bar{\mathbf{n}}^{2}\partial\mathbf{n}-\bar{\mathbf{n}}^{4}\bar{\partial}\mathbf{n})(\bar{\partial}\mathbf{n})=-\mathbf{n}(\partial^{2}\mathbf{n})-\bar{\mathbf{n}}^{3}(\bar{\partial}^{2}\mathbf{n})+2\bar{\mathbf{n}}^{4}(\bar{\partial}\mathbf{n})^{2},

which gives the desired identity (
5.7
).

All of the identities above can be rewritten in terms of the exterior conformal map
\psi=\phi^{-1}
by using

(5.8)

\phi^{\prime}(\psi(w))=\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}(w)},\qquad\phi^{\prime\prime}(\psi(w))=-\frac{\psi^{\prime\prime}(w)}{(\psi^{\prime}(w))^{2}}.

In particular, the curvature is given by

(5.9)

\displaystyle\kappa(\psi(w))=\frac{1}{|\psi^{\prime}(w)|}\Big(1+\operatorname{Re}\frac{w\psi^{\prime\prime}(w)}{\psi^{\prime}(w)}\Big).

Moreover, by the composition formula

(5.10)

\{f\circ g,z\}=\{f,g(z)\}(g^{\prime}(z))^{2}+\{g,z\}

for the Schwarzian derivative, we have

(5.11)

\{\phi,\psi(w)\}=-\frac{\{\psi,w\}}{\psi^{\prime}(w)^{2}}.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
5.1
.

5.2.
Dispersionless 2D Toda hierarchy and logarithmic energy

From now on, we restrict our attention to the case where the droplet
K
is simply connected. By virtue of the deformation formula (
2.26
), it remains to relate the asymptotic behaviour of
\mathcal{A}_{N,N}
to the variation of the coefficients appearing in the free energy expansion (
2.13
).

The underlying viewpoint of our approach is that the exterior conformal map associated with the droplet may be interpreted through the dispersionless
2
D Toda hierarchy
[
106
,
79
,
103
,
64
]
, an integrable hierarchy describing deformations of planar domains by infinitely many time parameters. More precisely, the coefficients of the potential play the role of the generalised times of the hierarchy, while the free energy corresponds to the logarithm of the associated tau-function. In this framework, variations of the free energy are naturally encoded by deformations of the conformal map and of the harmonic moments of the droplet.

Together with the exterior moments (
3.17
), we also introduce the interior harmonic moments

(5.34)

M_{j}=\int_{K}z^{j}\,\mathrm{d}A(z),\quad j\geq 1.

From the viewpoint of the dispersionless
2
D Toda hierarchy, the relevant integrability relations take the form

(5.35)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{j}}\log\tau=\begin{cases}M_{j}/j,&j\geq 1,\\
-C_{Q},&j=0,\end{cases}\qquad\text{and }\quad\frac{\partial t_{\ell}}{\partial t_{j}}=\delta_{j,\ell},\quad\frac{\bar{\partial}t_{\ell}}{\partial\bar{t}_{j}}=0,\quad j,\ell\geq 0.

Here,
\tau
denotes the tau-function of the dispersionless 2D Toda hierarchy, and
C_{Q}
is the Robin constant appearing in the Euler–Lagrange relation (
3.23
). (As mentioned earlier, we use a slight abuse of notation here: the symbol
\tau
denotes the tau-function depending on the hierarchy time variables, and should not be confused with the anisotropy parameter appearing in (
2.6
). Since these objects arise in entirely different contexts, this notation should not lead to ambiguity.)

In the present context, the tau-function under consideration should be understood as the “large-
N
” or dispersionless tau-function, which differs from the corresponding “finite-
N
” tau-function. A heuristic explanation, originating from
[
90
]
(see also
[
19
]
), is that for finite
N
, the tau-function
\tau_{N}
is essentially the partition function
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
up to multiplicative constants. This perspective played a crucial role in
[
39
]
. Passing to the dispersionless limit
N\to\infty
, the leading contribution of
\log\tau_{N}=\log\mathcal{Z}_{N}
gives rise to
\log\tau
satisfying (
5.35
).

In other words, in view of the leading-order term in the free energy expansion (
1.4
), the dispersionless tau-function associated with determinantal Coulomb gases is naturally identified with the weighted logarithmic energy
I_{Q}[\mu_{Q}]
defined in (
1.3
). For completeness, we now verify this correspondence.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
K
be a simply connected local droplet associated with an algebraic Hele-Shaw potential
Q
of the form (
3.18
), and let
t_{j}
denote the exterior harmonic moments defined in (
3.17
). Assume that, under sufficiently small perturbations of the parameters
t_{j}
in the potential
Q
, the corresponding local droplets remain simply connected and their boundaries
\partial K
vary smoothly within the class of smooth Jordan curves. Then the tau-function of the dispersionless
2
D Toda hierarchy satisfying the integrability relations (
5.35
) is given by

(5.36)

\log\tau=-\int_{K^{2}}\log\frac{1}{|z-\zeta|}\,\mathrm{d}A(z)\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta)-t_{0}\int_{K}Q(z)\,\mathrm{d}A(z).

In particular, if
K
is the droplet of
Q
then

(5.37)

\log\tau=-t_{0}^{2}I_{Q}[\mu_{Q}].

Proof.

By the assumption that the droplets vary smoothly under sufficiently small perturbations of the exterior moments, there exists a point
z_{0}\in\operatorname{Int}K
that remains inside the local droplet throughout such perturbations. Moreover, the potential
Q
can be written in the form

(5.38)

Q(z)=\frac{1}{t_{0}}\big(|z|^{2}-H(z)\big)-\frac{1}{t_{0}}\big(|z_{0}|^{2}-H(z_{0})\big)+Q(z_{0}).

Therefore,
Q(z_{0})
is invariant under perturbation by exterior harmonic moments
t_{j}
for
j\geq 1
. Since
z_{0}\in K
, it follows from (
3.23
) that

(5.39)

C_{Q}=\int_{K}\log\frac{1}{|z_{0}-\zeta|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta)+t_{0}Q(z_{0}).

We emphasise that the Robin constant
C_{Q}
also varies under perturbations of the exterior moments.

Now let
\log\tau
be defined as in (
5.36
). One then observes that

\displaystyle\log\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{K^{2}}\log\frac{1}{|z-\zeta|}\,\mathrm{d}A(z)\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta)-\int_{K}\bigg(2\int_{K}\log\frac{1}{|z-\zeta|}\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta)+t_{0}Q(z)\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}A(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{K^{2}}\log\frac{1}{|z-\zeta|}\,\mathrm{d}A(z)\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta)-t_{0}C_{Q}.

Then, by (
5.39
), we have

(5.40)

\displaystyle\log\tau=\log\widetilde{\tau}-t_{0}^{2}Q(z_{0}),\qquad\log\widetilde{\tau}:=-\int_{K^{2}}\log\Big|\frac{1}{z-z_{0}}-\frac{1}{\zeta-z_{0}}\Big|\,\mathrm{d}A(z)\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta).

Note that
\log\widetilde{\tau}
coincides with the original tau-function associated with exterior conformal maps in the theory of the dispersionless
2
D Toda hierarchy
[
79
,
103
,
64
]
. Since the quantity
t_{0}Q(z_{0})
remains invariant under perturbations of the moments
t_{j}
for
j\geq 1
, the tau-function (
5.36
) satisfies all of the integrability relations (
5.35
), except possibly the variation with respect to the area parameter
t_{0}
. To complete the proof, it therefore remains to verify directly that

(5.41)

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t_{0}}\log\tau=-C_{Q}.

Let
v_{\mathbf{n}}
denote the outward normal velocity associated with the perturbation with respect to the area parameter
t_{0}
, as defined in (
3.7
). This deformation corresponds precisely to the Hele-Shaw flow with source at infinity
[
85
,
67
]
. It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t_{0}}\log\tau

\displaystyle=-2\int_{\partial K}\bigg(\int_{K}\log\frac{1}{|z-\zeta|}\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta)\bigg)v_{\mathbf{n}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}s(z)-\int_{\partial K}t_{0}Q(z)v_{\mathbf{n}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}s(z)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial K}\bigg(\int_{K}\log\frac{1}{|z-\zeta|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta)+t_{0}Q(z)\bigg)v_{\mathbf{n}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}s(z)

\displaystyle=-C_{Q}\int_{\partial K}v_{\mathbf{n}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}s(z)=-C_{Q}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}t_{0}}{\,\mathrm{d}t_{0}}=-C_{Q}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.3

We now specialise to the particular potential

defined in (
2.6
). With the preparatory results established above, we are ready to derive the refined asymptotic behaviour of the coefficient
\mathcal{A}_{N,N}
defined in (

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

Recall that the droplet

where

3.4

(5.43)

P_{N,N}(z)=(\phi^{\prime}(\infty))^{-1}F_{0}(z)e^{Ng(z)}\Big(1+\frac{F_{1}(z)}{F_{0}(z)}\frac{1}{N}+O(\frac{1}{N^{2}})\Big),

as
N\to\infty.

Note that by (
5.1
), we have

(5.44)

F_{0}(z)=\sqrt{\phi^{\prime}(\infty)(\phi^{\prime}(z))}=\phi^{\prime}(\infty)\Big(1+O(\frac{1}{z^{2}})\Big),\qquad z\to\infty.

To derive the asymptotic behaviour of
g(z)
as

, let
\mathsf{S}=\mathsf{S}_{K^{c}}
denote the Schwarz function associated with the exterior domain

. Since

gives

(5.45)

\mathsf{S}(z)-\partial H(z)=\int_{K}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta)}{z-\zeta}.

Therefore, it follows from (
3.36

(5.46)

g(z)=\frac{1}{1-\tau^{2}}\int_{K}\log(z-\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta)=\log z-\frac{1}{1-\tau^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{M_{j}}{j}\frac{1}{z^{j}},

where

are interior harmonic moments (
5.34
).

Then by combining (
3.45
), (
5.43
), (
5.44
) and (

), we obtain

(5.47)

\mathcal{A}_{N,N}=-\frac{M_{1}}{1-\tau^{2}}N+\big[\frac{1}{z}\big]\Big(\frac{F_{1}}{F_{0}}\Big)(z)\frac{1}{N}+O(N^{-2}),

where
[1/z]f(z)
denotes the coefficient of
1/z
of a function
f(z)
as
z\to\infty
. It remains to identify the coefficients appearing in (
4.27
) and (
5.47
), i.e.

(5.48)

\frac{1+\tau}{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}a}I_{Q}[\mu_{Q}]=\frac{M_{1}}{1-\tau^{2}},\qquad\frac{1+\tau}{48}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}a}\mathcal{S}[\psi]=\big[\frac{1}{z}\big]\Big(\frac{F_{1}}{F_{0}}\Big)(z).

We begin with the coefficient of the linear term in
N
.
For this, it is convenient to consider a translation of external potential,

(5.49)

\mathsf{Q}(z;t_{1})=\frac{1}{1-\tau^{2}}\Big(|z|^{2}-2\operatorname{Re}\Big(t_{1}z+\frac{\tau}{2}z^{2}\Big)\Big)-2c\log|z|,\qquad t_{1}=-(1-\tau)a.

Note that

(5.50)

Q(z+a)=\mathsf{Q}(z;t_{1})+\frac{a^{2}}{1+\tau}.

Observe that the droplet
\mathsf{K}
associated with the potential

(5.51)

I_{\mathsf{Q}}[\mu_{\mathsf{Q}}]=I_{Q}[\mu_{Q}]-\frac{a^{2}}{1+\tau}.

Identifying

(5.52)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}}I_{\mathsf{Q}}[\mu_{\mathsf{Q}}]=-\frac{1}{t_{0}^{2}}\int_{\mathsf{K}}z\,\mathrm{d}A(z).

Since

in our present case (cf. (

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}a}I_{Q}[\mu_{Q}]

\displaystyle=\frac{2a}{1+\tau}+\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}a}I_{\mathsf{Q}}[\mu_{\mathsf{Q}}]=\frac{2a}{1+\tau}-2(1-\tau)\operatorname{Re}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}}\Big|_{t_{1}=-(1-\tau)a}I_{\mathsf{Q}}[\mu_{\mathsf{Q}}]

Moreover, since

is real by symmetry with respect to the

5.48

Next, we prove the second identity in (
5.48
). Let
\mathsf{K}(t_{1})
denote the droplet associated with the potential
\mathsf{Q}(\cdot;t_{1})
, and let
\varphi_{t_{1}}
be the corresponding exterior conformal map. Since

(5.53)

\psi(w)=\varphi_{t_{1}}(w)+a.

To establish the desired identity, we analyse the Löwner–Kufarev evolution of the domains
\mathsf{K}(t_{1})
, namely the evolution for which the associated conformal map satisfies the Löwner–Kufarev equation (
3.6

We write the Löwner–Kufarev equation as

(5.54)

\frac{\partial\varphi_{t_{1}}}{\partial\operatorname{Re}t_{1}}=w\varphi_{t_{1}}^{\prime}(w)P(t_{1},w).

Note that the second and third identities in the integrability relations (

(5.55)

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial}{\partial\operatorname{Re}t_{1}}\int_{\mathsf{K}(t_{1})^{c}}z^{-\ell}\,\mathrm{d}A(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\partial\mathsf{K}(t_{1})}z^{-\ell}v_{\mathbf{n}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}s(w)=\begin{cases}0&\textup{if }\ell=0,\;\text{or }\ell\geq 2,\\
1&\textup{if }\ell=1.\end{cases}

Moreover, by using (

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}w^{-\ell}|\varphi^{\prime}(w)|^{2}\operatorname{Re}P(t_{1},w)\,\mathrm{d}s(w)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\partial\mathsf{K}(t_{1})}(\varphi^{-1}(z))^{-\ell}v_{\mathbf{n}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}s(z)=\begin{cases}0&\textup{if }\ell=0\;\text{or }\ell\geq 2,\\
\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)&\textup{if }\ell=1.\end{cases}

(5.56)

|\varphi^{\prime}(w)|^{2}\operatorname{Re}P(t_{1},w)=\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)}{2}(w+\frac{1}{w})=\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)\operatorname{Re}w,\quad w\in\mathbb{T}.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}a}\mathcal{S}[\psi]&=-(1-\tau)\frac{\partial}{\partial\operatorname{Re}t_{1}}\bigg|_{t_{1}=-(1-\tau)a}\mathcal{S}[\varphi]\\

Finally,

\varphi^{\prime}(w)=\psi^{\prime}(w),\qquad\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)=\psi^{\prime}(\infty)=\frac{1}{\phi^{\prime}(\infty)}

\Delta Q=(1-\tau^{2})^{-1}

\frac{1+\tau}{48}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}a}\mathcal{S}[\psi]=\frac{1}{24\pi(\Delta Q)\phi^{\prime}(\infty)}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Big(\operatorname{Re}\big(w^{2}\{\psi,w\}\big)+|\psi^{\prime}(w)|^{2}\kappa^{2}(\psi(w))\Big)\frac{\operatorname{Re}w}{|\psi^{\prime}(w)|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s(w).

Hence the second identity of (

) follows from (

) and (

is real due to the conjugate invariance

∎

Remark 5.2
.

The key idea of the proof is to interpret the deformation with respect to the parameter
a
as a deformation with respect to the generalised time
t_{1}
in the integrable hierarchy. This is achieved by introducing a translated determinantal Coulomb gas model, in the same spirit as in the proof of (
2.26
).

A crucial point is that the integrability relations provide an explicit expression for
\operatorname{Re}P(t_{1},w)
in the Löwner–Kufarev equation with respect to
\operatorname{Re}t_{1}
. Indeed, if one attempts instead to analyse directly the Löwner–Kufarev evolution of
\psi
with respect to the parameter
a
, the resulting expressions become difficult to relate to the Liouville action, and the computation strongly depends on the explicit form of
\psi
. In contrast, the present argument is entirely independent of any explicit formula for
\psi
. This flexibility allows the argument to extend naturally to more general settings in which analogous integrable-hierarchy structures remain available. We plan to return to this direction elsewhere.

6.
Doubly connected case

In this section, we derive the asymptotic behaviour of the orthogonal polynomials
P_{n,N}
in the doubly connected regime and thereby prove Proposition
4.2
. In contrast to the simply connected case, the doubly connected regime requires analysing deformations not only with respect to the parameter
a
, but also with respect to
\tau
; see Figures
1
and
3
.
Note that the deformation formula with respect to
\tau
in (
2.27
) involves the second subleading coefficient
\mathcal{B}_{n,N}
. Consequently, one must compute both
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
in the asymptotic expansion (
3.44
) of
F
.

Although
F_{2}
can in principle be computed algorithmically using the iterative scheme developed in Section
3.3
and implemented in Theorem
5.1
for the computation of
F_{1}
, we instead present a simpler argument based on the following a priori geometric observation: throughout the entire doubly connected regime (namely Regime I in Theorem
2.1
), the outer boundary component of the droplet
K_{Q}
remains fixed and is explicitly given by an ellipse; see (
2.8
). This special geometric structure allows one to derive the free energy variations in a substantially simpler manner; see also
[
94
]
for a recent related study.

For the convenience of the reader, we briefly outline the main idea of this section. In Subsection
3.3
, we observed that the algorithmic computation of the asymptotic expansion of
F
depends only on the behaviour of the external potential in a neighbourhood of the
outermost
boundary component of the droplet. Consequently, two families of planar orthogonal polynomials associated with different external potentials share the same fine asymptotic structure—in particular, the same
O(N^{-1})
correction terms—provided that the corresponding droplets have identical outer boundaries and that the external potentials agree near this boundary component.

In the present setting, the planar orthogonal polynomials associated with the potential
Q
in (
2.6
) share the same fine asymptotic behaviour as the Hermite polynomials, which arise as the planar orthogonal polynomials associated with the elliptic Ginibre potential
Q^{\rm e}
defined in (
1.11
). Hence, the explicit coefficient formulas for the Hermite polynomials given in (
4.9
) determine the fine asymptotic expansion of
P_{n,N}
. In fact, this argument yields not only the expansion up to order
O(N^{-2})
, but also asymptotic expansions to arbitrary precision; see Remark
2.3
.

In Subsection
6.1
, we consider general algebraic Hele-Shaw potentials associated with multiply connected droplets. There, Proposition
6.2
shows that the asymptotic behaviour of the corresponding planar orthogonal polynomials is essentially governed by the geometry of the outermost boundary component of the droplet. Intuitively, this means that the analysis can be reduced to the simply connected case corresponding to the outer boundary via a “hole-filling” procedure. In Subsection
6.2
, we specialise to the model (
2.6
) and prove Proposition
4.2
using the structural observation provided by Proposition
6.2
.

6.1.
Planar orthogonal polynomials with multiply connected droplets

Throughout this subsection, we work under the following setting and assumptions.

Let
K
be a multiply connected local droplet associated with an algebraic Hele-Shaw potential
W
of the form (
3.18
), having
d
holes and total area
\pi t_{0}
. Then, by Theorem
3.2
, we have

(6.1)

K^{c}=\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}\cup\ldots\cup\Omega_{d}\cup\Omega_{\infty},

where
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{d}
are bounded quadrature domains, while
\Omega_{\infty}
is an unbounded quadrature domain.

We assume that all poles of the quadrature functions associated with the bounded domains
\Omega_{j}
are simple. Since the quadrature functions
r_{\ell}
corresponding to the components
\Omega_{\ell}
sum to
\partial H
by Theorem
3.2
, we may write

(6.2)

\partial H(z)=r_{\infty}(z)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{c_{j}t_{0}}{z-a_{j}},

where

is a rational function whose poles lie in
\Omega_{\infty}
, and each point

\Omega_{\ell}

A typical class of external potentials in this setting is given by

(6.3)

Q(z)=Q^{0}(z)-2\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}\log|z-a_{j}|,\qquad c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}>0,

where

(6.4)

Q^{0}(z):=\frac{1}{t_{0}}\Big(|z|^{2}-H^{0}(z)\Big),\qquad\partial H^{0}(z)=r_{\infty}(z).

Clearly, our particular potential (
2.6
) belongs to this class.

In this general setting, we first establish the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Under the assumptions above, the domain
K^{0}:=\Omega_{\infty}^{c}
is a simply connected local droplet associated with the potential
Q^{0}/(1+c)
, where
c=c_{1}+\ldots+c_{m}.

Proof.

By virtue of Lemma
3.3
, it suffices to verify that

(6.5)

\textup{area}(K^{0})=\pi(1+c)t_{0}

and that

(6.6)

\int_{K^{0}}\log\frac{1}{|z-\zeta|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta)+t_{0}Q^{0}(z)=C,\quad z\in K^{0},

C

We begin by computing the area of

(6.7)

\frac{1}{\pi}\text{area }\Omega_{\ell}=\int_{\Omega_{\ell}}\,\mathrm{d}A(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Omega_{\ell}}r_{\ell}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z.

Note that by (
3.21

r_{1}(z)+\ldots+r_{d}(z)=\partial H(z)-r_{\infty}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{c_{j}t_{0}}{z-a_{j}},

Therefore, we obtain

(6.8)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\textrm{area }K^{0}=\frac{1}{\pi}\textrm{area }K+\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\pi}\text{area }\Omega_{\ell}=t_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Res_{z=a_{j}}(r_{1}+\ldots+r_{d})=(1+c)t_{0},

which gives rise to (

).

Next, we show (
6.6
). To prove this, it suffices to show that the derivative with respect to

of the left-hand side of (

) vanishes on
\operatorname{Int}K^{0}
. Equivalently, it is enough to verify that

(6.9)

\bar{z}-\partial H^{0}(z)=\int_{K^{0}}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta)}{z-\zeta},\qquad z\in\operatorname{Int}K^{0},

By Green’s formula, this is equivalent to

(6.10)

-\partial H^{0}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial K^{0}}\frac{\bar{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta}{z-\zeta},\qquad z\in\operatorname{Int}K^{0},

By (
3.22
), the Schwarz function
\mathsf{S}_{\Omega_{\infty}}
satisfies

(6.11)

\mathsf{S}_{\Omega_{\infty}}(z)=r_{\infty}(z)+\int_{K^{0}}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}A(\zeta)}{z-\zeta},\quad z\in\bar{\Omega}_{\infty}.

Thus
\mathsf{S}_{\Omega_{\infty}}-r_{\infty}
is a bounded holomorphic function on
\Omega_{\infty}
. Consequently, (
6.10
) follows from

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial K^{0}}\frac{\bar{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta}{z-\zeta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial K^{0}}\frac{\mathsf{S}_{\Omega_{\infty}}(\zeta)}{z-\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta=-r_{\infty}(z)+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial K^{0}}\frac{\mathsf{S}_{\Omega_{\infty}}(\zeta)-r_{\infty}(\zeta)}{z-\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta

\displaystyle=-r_{\infty}(z)+\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=r}\frac{\mathsf{S}_{\Omega_{\infty}}(\zeta)-r_{\infty}(\zeta)}{z-\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta=-r_{\infty}(z)=-\partial H^{0}(z).

Here we used that
r_{\infty}
is a rational function holomorphic on
(K^{0})^{c}=\Omega_{\infty}
.
This completes the proof of (
6.6
).
∎

We now relate the planar orthogonal polynomials associated with the potentials (

We denote by

e^{-NQ}\mathbbm{1}_{\bar{U}}\,\mathrm{d}A\quad\text{and }\quad e^{-NQ^{0}}\mathbbm{1}_{\bar{U}^{0}}\,\mathrm{d}A,

respectively. Under the assumption that the droplet is multiply connected, these two families of orthogonal polynomials are related by the following proposition.

Proposition 6.2

(
Hole-filling reduction for orthogonal polynomials
)
.

Assume that
c_{1}N,\ldots,c_{m}N
are integers. Under the assumptions above, we have

(6.13)

\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{m}(z-a_{j})^{c_{j}N}\Big)P_{N,N}(z)=P^{0}_{(1+c)N,N}(z)(1+O(N^{-\infty})),\quad N\to\infty,

where

satisfying

(6.14)

\textup{dist}(z,(K^{0})^{c})\leq(N^{-1}\log N)^{1/2}.

Proof.

Let
\phi:(K^{0})^{c}\to\mathbb{D}_{e}
denote the inverse of the normalised exterior conformal map associated with
K^{0}
. Then, by Theorem
3.4
and Remark
3.1
, as

, we have

(6.15)

\displaystyle\begin{split}P_{N,N}(z)&=(\phi^{\prime}(\infty))^{-N-1}(\phi(z))^{N}e^{N\mathcal{Q}(z)/2}F(z),\\
P_{(1+c)N,N}^{0}(z)&=(\phi^{\prime}(\infty))^{-(1+c)N-1}(\phi(z))^{(1+c)N}e^{N\mathcal{Q}^{0}(z)/2}F^{0}(z).\end{split}

Here,
\mathcal{Q},F
and
\mathcal{Q}^{0},F^{0}
denote the corresponding functions associated with the potentials
Q
and
Q^{0}
, respectively.

We observe that
F
and
F^{0}
agree up to arbitrary precision as
N\to\infty
, in the sense that

(6.16)

F(z)=F^{0}(z)(1+O(N^{-\infty})),\quad N\to\infty,

uniformly for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying (
6.14
). By the discussion following Theorem
3.4
, it suffices to verify that
R=R^{0}
in a neighbourhood of
\Gamma=\partial\Omega_{\infty}
. This is immediate, since both functions are given by the same expression (
3.35
). On the other hand, it follows directly from (
6.3
) and (
6.4
) that

(6.17)

\mathcal{Q}(z)=\mathcal{Q}^{0}(z)-2\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}\log(z-a_{j})+2c\log\big(\phi(z)/\phi^{\prime}(\infty)\big).

Therefore,

(6.18)

e^{N(\mathcal{Q}^{0}(z)-\mathcal{Q}(z))/2}(\phi(z)/\phi^{\prime}(\infty))^{cN}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(z-a_{j})^{c_{j}N},\qquad N\to\infty.

Combining (
6.15
), (
6.16
), and (
6.18
), we obtain the proposition.
∎

Potentials of the form (
6.3
) correspond to determinantal Coulomb gas models with point-charge insertions into an algebraic Hele-Shaw potential.
This also naturally arises in the products of characteristic polynomials; see e.g.
[
4
]
.
The asymptotic behaviour of the associated orthogonal polynomials has been studied extensively from the Riemann–Hilbert perspective; see, for instance,
[
16
,
34
,
81
]
. In the context of multiple insertions, Ginibre ensembles with two point charges were recently analysed in
[
77
]
.

Proposition
6.2
can in fact be extended beyond the setting of algebraic Hele-Shaw potentials. In several works, it has been shown that droplets associated with certain non-Hele-Shaw potentials likewise have complements given by unions of disjoint quadrature domains; see, for example,
[
35
,
34
,
27
,
52
]
, where spherical ensembles with point-charge insertions were studied. In many such situations, the multiply connected regime leads to rather complicated motherbody structures, which substantially complicates the corresponding Riemann–Hilbert analysis.

On the other hand, Proposition
6.2
suggests that one can still extract the asymptotic behaviour of the orthogonal polynomials, at least near infinity, by reducing the problem to the outer boundary component. For instance, this perspective applies to the post-critical regime studied in
[
34
]
. We emphasise, however, that in such settings the scaling factor
1+c
must be modified, since the equilibrium measure typically has non-uniform density.

.

Note that if

would follow immediately from Proposition

Recall from (

is given by the inverse Joukowsky transform

\phi_{1+c}(z):=\frac{z+\sqrt{z^{2}-4\tau(1+c)}}{2\sqrt{1+c}},\quad z\in\mathsf{E}^{c},

where we have made the dependence on the parameter

We denote by
F^{\rm e}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\rm e}
the functions appearing in Theorem

associated with the elliptic potential

\mathcal{Q}(z)=\mathcal{Q}^{\rm e}(z)-2c\log(z-a)+2c\log\frac{\phi_{1+c}(z)}{\phi_{1+c}^{\prime}(\infty)}.

Due to the explicit form of

in (

), the asymptotic expansion (

) yields the following whenever

\displaystyle(\phi_{t}^{\prime}(\infty))^{-tN-1}(\phi_{t}(z))^{tN}e^{N\mathcal{Q}^{\rm e}(z)/2}F^{\rm e}(z;t)

Since

depends continuously on the parameter

(cf. Remark

(6.20)

F^{\rm e}(z;t)=\phi_{t}^{\prime}(\infty)\exp\Big(-N\Big(\frac{\mathcal{Q}^{\rm e}(z)}{2}+t\log\frac{\phi_{t}(z)}{\phi^{\prime}_{t}(\infty)z}\Big)\Big)\Big(1-\frac{\tau t(tN-1)}{2}z^{-2}+O(z^{-4})\Big),\quad z\to\infty,

\displaystyle P_{N,N}(z)

\displaystyle=(\phi_{1+c}^{\prime}(\infty))^{-N-1}(\phi_{1+c}(z))^{N}e^{N\mathcal{Q}(z)/2}F(z)

Appendix A
Explicit description of the droplet

Throughout the proof of the main results, we do not rely on the explicit shape of the droplet associated with the potential
Q
in (
2.6
), nor on explicit formulas for the associated logarithmic energy. Nevertheless, for completeness, we record these explicit expressions here, which were analysed in detail in the recent work
[
41
]
.

Recall that the potential (
2.6
) depends on the three real parameters
\tau\in[0,1)
,
c\geq 0
, and
a\geq 0
. As discussed in Theorem
2.1
, the associated droplet exhibits three distinct topological phases, and the corresponding parameter regions are given as follows; see Figure
1
.

•

(Regime I) The droplet is doubly connected if and only if the parameters
(a,c,\tau)
satisfy

(A.1)

a\leq\min\Big\{2\sqrt{\frac{2\tau(1+\tau)}{3+\tau^{2}}},2\sqrt{\frac{\tau(1-\tau-2c\tau)}{1-\tau}}\Big\}\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leq c\leq\frac{1-\tau}{2\tau},

or

(A.2)

2\sqrt{\frac{2\tau(1+\tau)}{3+\tau^{2}}}\leq a\leq(1+\tau)\sqrt{1+c}-\sqrt{c(1-\tau^{2})}\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leq c\leq\frac{(1-\tau)^{3}}{2\tau(3+\tau^{2})}.

•

(Regime II) The droplet is simply connected if and only if the parameters
(a,c,\tau)
satisfy the following conditions. For a fixed
\tau
, the parameters
c
and
a
can be parametrised by two auxiliary parameters
q
and
\lambda
according to

(A.3)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\equiv c(q,\lambda)=\frac{\lambda}{q^{2}}\frac{(1-q^{2})^{2}(1-\tau q^{2})+q^{2}\lambda}{(1-q^{2})^{2}(1-\tau^{2}+2\tau\lambda)-\lambda^{2}},

(A.4)

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\equiv a(q,\lambda)=\sqrt{\frac{1+\tau}{1-\tau}}\frac{(1-\tau)(1-q^{2})(1+\tau q^{2})-(1-\tau q^{2})\lambda}{q\sqrt{(1-q^{2})^{2}(1-\tau^{2}+2\tau\lambda)-\lambda^{2}}}.

Here, the parameters
q
and
\lambda
lie in the range

(A.5)

q\in(0,1),\qquad\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{\rm cri}),

where
\lambda_{\rm cri}
denotes the unique zero of the function
H(q,\cdot)
given by

(A.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}H(q,\lambda)&:=\frac{1-\tau}{q}\Big(1+\tau q^{2}-\frac{1-\tau q^{2}}{1-\tau}\frac{\lambda}{1-q^{2}}\Big)\Big(w_{*}-\frac{1}{w_{*}}\Big)\\
&\quad-2\Big(\frac{1-\tau q^{2}}{q^{2}}\lambda+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(1-q^{2})^{2}}\Big)\log\frac{|qw_{*}-1|}{|w_{*}-q|}-2\Big(1-\tau^{2}+\frac{1+\tau q^{2}}{q^{2}}\lambda\Big)\log|w_{*}|,\end{split}

where
w_{*}
is given by

(A.7)

w_{*}:=\frac{1}{2q}\bigg(q^{2}+1+\frac{\lambda}{1-\tau}+\sqrt{\Big(q^{2}+1+\frac{\lambda}{1-\tau}\Big)^{2}-4q^{2}}\,\bigg).

•

(Regime III) The droplet consists of two simply connected components whenever the parameters
(a,c,\tau)
lie outside both Regime I and Regime II.

We also note that, in Theorem
2.1
(ii), the parameters
R
,
\lambda
, and
q
are characterised by the following coupled equations:

(A.8)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle 1=\frac{R^{2}}{1-\tau^{2}}\Big(1-\tau^{2}+2\tau\lambda-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(1-q^{2})^{2}}\Big),\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
\displaystyle c=\frac{R^{2}\lambda}{1-\tau^{2}}\Big(\frac{1-\tau q^{2}}{q^{2}}+\frac{\lambda}{(1-q^{2})^{2}}\Big),\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
