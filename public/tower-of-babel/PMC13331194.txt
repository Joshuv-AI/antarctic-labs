Knowledge, attitudes and practices towards routine vaccination among COVID-19-positive individuals in Lebanon in the post-pandemic period: a case-control study
Authors: Maryo Baakliny, Nada Ghosn, Nadine Saleh, Patrick Maison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate knowledge, attitudes and practices towards routine vaccination in the Lebanese population in the post-COVID-19 period, and to examine differences between COVID-19 vaccinated and non-vaccinated individuals. The study also aimed to identify sociodemographic and behavioural factors associated with vaccination status, and to explore changes in vaccination practices before and after the pandemic.DesignUnmatched case-control study.SettingCommunity-based survey conducted across all regions of Lebanon between July and October 2023.ParticipantsA total of 730 adults were included, comprising 277 COVID-19-positive individuals who were not vaccinated against COVID-19 (cases) and 453 COVID-19-positive individuals who had received at least one dose of a COVID-19 vaccine (controls). Participants were randomly selected from the national COVID-19 surveillance database. Adults aged ≥18 years were eligible; individuals who tested negative for COVID-19 or were unable to provide consent were excluded.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcomes were vaccination knowledge and attitudes, measured using the Zingg knowledge scale and the 7C vaccination attitude scale. Secondary outcomes included self-reported adherence to routine and influenza vaccination before and after COVID-19. Factors associated with non-vaccination were examined using multivariable regression models.ResultsVaccinated participants were older and more likely to be employed, have higher education, work in healthcare and report financial coverage for vaccines. They had higher knowledge scores, more positive attitudes towards vaccination and increased adherence to routine and influenza vaccination after COVID-19, while adherence declined among non-vaccinated participants. In multivariable analysis, older age (OR 0.98, 95% CI 0.96 to 0.99), higher education (OR 0.68, 95% CI 0.60 to 0.78), number of children (OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.78 to 1.00) and more positive vaccination attitudes (OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.00) were independently associated with lower odds of non-vaccination.ConclusionsOlder age, higher education, greater number of children and more positive attitudes were associated with lower odds of non-vaccination. Vaccinated participants also had higher knowledge scores, more positive attitudes and better adherence to routine and influenza vaccination practices than non-vaccinated participants.

Background

Vaccination is known to be one of the most effective public health measures to prevent infectious diseases and continues to save millions of lives throughout the world.1 2 Despite its proven effectiveness in prevention and therapeutic,3 4 vaccination coverage remains vulnerable to societal, economic and political disruptions, making sustained public trust and access essential for long-term disease control.

In the prepandemic era, the WHO labelled vaccine hesitancy as one of the ‘top 10 threats to global health’ in 20192 and it has emerged as a major public health problem leading to outbreaks of communicable infections such as measles.5 At the end of that year, a new coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2) was identified5 and it was declared a pandemic on 11 March 2020 by WHO.6 The emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic in late 2019 underscored the critical role of vaccination in preventing and controlling infectious diseases on a global scale.7 Many people have become more positive about vaccination and recognise it as a vital tool for protecting themselves and their communities.8 9 On the other hand, the antivaccination movement across the world reached an all-time high after COVID-19.10 The rise of this movement is likely related to concerns about alleged side effects that are widely disseminated on social media from antivaccination groups.11 12

The WHO mentioned that the COVID-19 pandemic years of 2020 and 2021 witnessed the most significant and continuous decline in global immunisation coverage in three decades.13 This decline led to a nearly 40% rise in the number of zero-dose children worldwide that did not receive any dose of routine recommended vaccines, increasing from 13.3 million in 2019 to 18.2 million in 2021.13 A partial rebound occurred in 2022; however, global coverage remains at 2005 levels and many countries like Lebanon could not reach pre-COVID-19 levels.14 According to UNICEF, the Middle East and North Africa region saw a rise in ‘zero-dose’ children in 2023 reaching 1.64 million, compared with 1.2 million in 2022. This marks the highest number ever recorded.15

Vaccine refusal is a multifaceted issue, influenced by varying geographical and cultural contexts. A major contributing factor to this hesitancy is the widespread concern over vaccine safety, particularly fears about potential adverse effects.2 A systematic review, done by Malik Sallam in 2021, identified three epidemiological factors (environmental, agent and host factors) that were analysed as the reason behind the vaccine hesitancy. Environmental factors include health policies, social and media factors. On the other hand, agent factors include the vaccine safety, effectiveness and the susceptibility to the disease, while the host factors deal with knowledge, previous experience, educational and income level.16

Several knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) studies conducted in countries such as Singapore, Pakistan and Switzerland, among others,81719 have evaluated the factors influencing routine vaccination using various methods and scales. In Singapore, one KAP study demonstrated that higher knowledge levels were associated with increased vaccination rates. As for attitude, individuals who held firm beliefs about the vaccine’s efficacy and safety, and demonstrated a willingness to be vaccinated, exhibited increased vaccination uptake. While as for practices, there was no notable link between practice scores and vaccination status.17

Lebanon is facing substantial obstacles in its efforts in dealing with the economic crisis and conflicts, addressing shortages of vital medications, discontinuing subsidies, inflation rates, internal displacement, Syrian refugees, struggling with incidents of cholera, measles and other outbreaks.2022 In recent years, Lebanon’s routine immunisation coverage has deteriorated significantly. According to the recent WHO/UNICEF Estimates of National Immunization Coverage (WUENIC), Lebanon’s coverage for measles-containing vaccine first dose remains considerably below the 95% threshold needed for herd immunity.21 In addition, the influx of refugees and the presence of displaced populations have hindered equitable access to immunisation: studies among Syrian refugees in Lebanon documented that a substantial proportion lacked full routine vaccination coverage compared with host communities.22

In the post-COVID-19 era, COVID-19 vaccination behaviour has increasingly been recognised as a valid indicator of broader vaccine confidence and preventive health behaviour. A large systematic review and meta-analysis examining determinants of COVID-19 vaccine acceptance globally showed that prior uptake of other vaccines (particularly seasonal influenza), trust in healthcare systems, perceived vaccine safety and effectiveness and health-seeking behaviour were among the strongest predictors of COVID-19 vaccination acceptance.23 Similarly, multicountry population-based studies have demonstrated that individuals who are up to date with routine and influenza vaccination are significantly more likely to accept COVID-19 vaccination, whereas those who refuse COVID-19 vaccines often exhibit generalised vaccine hesitancy extending beyond COVID-19 alone.24 In fragile health system settings as Lebanon where routine immunisation monitoring has been disrupted, COVID-19 vaccination status therefore represents a pragmatic and theoretically grounded proxy for broader vaccination attitudes and practices.

In this particular context, it is important to assess the evolution of the main factors contributing to vaccine hesitancy after COVID-19 pandemic in Lebanon. Our main objective in this study is to evaluate the KAP about routine vaccination in the Lebanese population post-COVID-19 between COVID-19 vaccinated and non-vaccinated populations and to identify which individual characteristics are associated with these outcomes. We hypothesise that the levels of KAP about vaccination may be different between COVID-19 vaccinated and non-vaccinated individuals.

Methods

Study design and settings

This study employed an unmatched case-control design to assess KAP towards routine vaccination and their association with COVID-19 vaccination status in Lebanon. In this design, vaccination status was considered the outcome variable. Cases were defined as individuals who tested positive for COVID-19 and were not vaccinated, while controls were individuals who tested positive for COVID-19 and had received at least one dose of any COVID-19 vaccine. Both cases and controls were drawn from the same underlying source population of confirmed COVID-19 cases to ensure comparability between groups. This restriction was intended to align the pathway to COVID-19 testing and related healthcare contact between cases and controls, thereby improving internal comparability. Conducted from 1 July to 31 October 2023, the survey included 56 questions administered by public health officers across all Lebanese regions. Participants were randomly selected from the COVID-19 Lebanese database, this database is hosted on the district health information system 2 (DHIS2) and includes all reported cases, containing 1 245 442 cases, representing 24% of Lebanon’s population. A simple random sampling approach was applied using computer-generated random numbers to select eligible participants from the database. From DHIS2, the following data were extracted: ID number, age, phone number, COVID-19 test result, date and vaccination status. Selected individuals were contacted using the phone numbers recorded in DHIS2. If a selected individual did not answer after three call attempts on different days, they were replaced by another randomly selected participant from the same sampling frame. Missing data were minimal and were handled using complete-case analysis for each model; the denominator for each analysis is reported in the relevant table. Verbal consent was obtained, and data collectors from the epidemiological surveillance unit registered responses using DHIS2. This research received no specific grant from any funding agency in the public, commercial or not-for-profit sectors.

Study population

Sample size and participants

The required sample size for this case-control study was calculated using Epi Info’s StatCalc tool for unmatched case-control studies. The parameters included a 95% confidence level, 80% power, a 1:2 case-to-control ratio and an assumed OR of 0.5. Based on previous studies, the proportion of controls with high knowledge levels was estimated at 37%, while the proportion among cases was 25%. Using these parameters, the calculated sample size was 181 cases (non-vaccinated) and 362 controls (vaccinated), totalling 543 participants. To account for potential non-responses, the sample size was increased by 10%, resulting in a target of 200 cases and 399 controls. After data collection and cleaning, 277 cases and 453 controls were included in the final analysis.

Inclusion criteria

Included participants were aged 18 years or older, gave consent to participate, tested positive before 1 July 2023 for COVID-19 in a health facility and reported on DHIS2. Exclusions included those under 18 years, unable to consent, or who withdrew and those tested negative for COVID-19.

Data collection

During the pandemic, data related to participants’ demographics, health status and COVID-19 exposure was collected and included in a central database once tested positive by PCR. The data collection for our KAP study was carried out by seven trained interviewers who followed standard procedures for informed consent, confidentiality and data protection. Risks to data privacy and confidentiality were mitigated through strict adherence to ethical guidelines and data protection measures. We ensured participants’ privacy by anonymising all data collected, storing it securely on encrypted servers and restricting access to authorised personnel only. An ethics approval of research was granted by the Institut National de Santé Publique, d'Épidémiologie Clinique et de Toxicologie-Liban (INSPECT Lebanon) under the number 2024REC-002-INSPECT-03-06. Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. The study protocol is available as supplementary material (online supplemental file (study protocol)). This study was not prospectively registered as it is an observational case-control study based on secondary surveillance data.

Questionnaire and variables

Data collection was conducted using a structured questionnaire (online supplemental file (questionnaire)) covering demographics, vaccination status, knowledge scale, attitudes scale and practices pre- and post-COVID-19. The questionnaire included the Zingg scale18 to assess vaccine knowledge and a 7-component Likert scale25 to evaluate attitudes towards vaccination. Both scales were translated into Arabic, back-translated and reviewed for accuracy.

The Zingg knowledge scale, developed in 201218 based on misconceptions cited by the Robert Koch Institute, was designed to assess understanding of vaccines, addressing topics like the necessity of vaccines, their historical impact and common misconceptions, including concerns about vaccine safety and potential links to conditions like autism. Originally comprising 11 items, the questionnaire responses were dichotomised for analysis, with ‘correct’ answers coded as 1 and incorrect or ‘do not know’ as 0. Two items, related to the safety of vaccine components and gene technology in vaccines, showed low item-total correlations, negatively affecting reliability. Removing these items improved Cronbach’s alpha from 0.675 to 0.694, leading to a revised 9-item scale used in the final analysis. The knowledge score was calculated using nine items instead of 11 to ensure scale reliability; the same was done in the validated version of the scale.18 This adaptation was undertaken to improve internal consistency in our study context; however, using a modified version may limit direct comparability with studies that applied the original 11-item Zingg scale.

We assessed attitudes towards vaccination using the 7C’s of vaccination scale developed by Geiger et al in 2021. This valid tool measures seven components of vaccination readiness, including confidence in healthcare recommendations, vaccine safety, effectiveness, minimal side effects and overall benefits. Participants rated their agreement with each statement on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The responses were treated as ordinal data, and the scale demonstrated high internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.887.

The final section of the questionnaire focused on participants’ vaccination practices before and after the COVID-19 pandemic, these questions were based on the literature. This section included items assessing whether participants followed the recommended vaccination schedules for themselves and their children, if they had a regular healthcare provider or doctor for vaccination-related consultations, and whether they or their children received the influenza vaccine during these periods.

Statistical methods

The collected data were analysed using descriptive and inferential statistical methods. Continuous variables, such as age and scores for knowledge and attitudes, were summarised as means with SD and compared between vaccinated and non-vaccinated groups using the independent t-test. Categorical variables, such as gender, employment status and education level, were summarised as frequencies and percentages and analysed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A significance level of 0.05 was set for all statistical tests. Knowledge and attitude scores were calculated by summing the correct answers assigning 1 for correct answers and 0 for incorrect ones in the Zingg scale,18 or Likert-scale responses, respectively. Data analysis was performed using SPSS V.25.0. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Given the sample size and the approximate normal distribution of key continuous variables, parametric tests were considered appropriate and independent t-tests were used for group comparisons. Multivariate analyses, including logistic regression, were conducted to identify predictors of vaccination status while controlling for potential confounders. Linear regression was used to examine the association between knowledge and attitude scores. Covariates assessed in univariate analyses included gender, age, residence, marital status, number of children, healthcare worker status, employment, income level, education level, COVID-19 hospitalisation, underlying conditions and financial coverage for vaccines. Variables with p<0.20 were considered for inclusion in the multivariable models. In the practice section, analyses were stratified in pre- and post-COVID-19 practices.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Among the variables analysed, several showed significant associations with vaccination status (table 1). The mean age of vaccinated individuals was higher than that of non-vaccinated individuals (40.1 (12.8) years vs 37.2 (12.2) years). A higher proportion of vaccinated participants were healthcare workers compared with non-vaccinated participants (8.8% vs 4.0%). Employment status also showed a difference, with 65.3% of vaccinated individuals being employed compared with 56% of non-vaccinated individuals. Educational level was associated with vaccination, with a larger proportion of vaccinated participants holding a bachelor’s degree or higher (64.8% vs 46.9). Additionally, financial coverage for vaccines was significantly more common among vaccinated participants than non-vaccinated ones (39.7% vs 26.8%).

Attitudes about vaccination

Table 3 presents a descriptive analysis of participants’ attitudes towards vaccination, measured using 7 Likert-scale statements (1=‘strongly disagree’ to 5=‘strongly agree’). The attitude score was calculated by summing the seven items, with the study population averaging 23.23 (± 6.52) out of 35, indicating a moderate attitude towards vaccination. Vaccinated individuals had a mean attitude score of 23.8 (±6.4), while non-vaccinated individuals scored 22.2 (±6.5), with a mean difference of 1.51 (p=0.002), suggesting more positive attitudes among the vaccinated. More vaccinated individuals (53.9%) agreed that the vaccine is effective compared with non-vaccinated individuals (37.5%). Similarly, 50.8% of vaccinated individuals agreed that the vaccine is safe, versus 36.5% of non-vaccinated individuals. Confidence that the vaccine will protect against disease was higher among vaccinated individuals (55.0%) compared with non-vaccinated (45.1%). Overall, 62.3% of vaccinated participants believed in the vaccine’s benefits, compared with 52.3% of non-vaccinated participants.

Practices about vaccination

The study evaluated vaccination practices for routine vaccination including childhood vaccines and adult preventive vaccines such as seasonal influenza before and after COVID-19, highlighting notable differences between COVID-19 vaccinated and non-vaccinated individuals in table 4. Among vaccinated individuals, strict adherence to the routine national recommended vaccination schedule increased from 73.6% before COVID-19 to 84.3% after COVID-19. In contrast, adherence among non-vaccinated individuals decreased from 26.4% to 15.7% during the same period. For child routine vaccination, among COVID-19 vaccinated and non-vaccinated individuals, adherence to the recommended child vaccination schedule remained stable. The study also found differences in having a regular vaccines healthcare provider. Pre-COVID-19, 80.4% of vaccinated individuals reported having a regular healthcare provider, which decreased to 71.8% post-COVID-19. In contrast, among non-vaccinated individuals, 19.6% reported having a regular healthcare provider pre-COVID-19, increasing to 28.2% post-COVID-19. Similarly, adherence to the influenza vaccination schedule among vaccinated individuals increased from 80.4% to 85.4% post-COVID-19. However, among non-vaccinated individuals, adherence declined from 19.6% to 14.6% during the same period (p<0.001).

Determinants for the vaccination status/knowledge scale/attitude scale

A logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with being non-vaccinated. Adjusted ORs and 95% CIs for all covariates included in the model are presented in table 5. In the multivariable model, older age was associated with lower odds of being non-vaccinated (OR=0.976, 95% CI 0.962 to 0.991). A higher number of children was also associated with lower odds of being non-vaccinated (OR=0.879, 95% CI 0.777 to 0.995). Higher education level was independently associated with lower odds of being non-vaccinated (OR=0.684, 95% CI 0.601 to 0.779). More positive vaccination attitudes were associated with lower odds of being non-vaccinated (OR=0.969, 95% CI0.941 to 0.998). The model’s explanatory power explained 11.9% of the variance in vaccination status. This indicates modest explanatory power and suggests that additional unmeasured determinants may also contribute to vaccination decisions.

The multiple linear regression analysis for knowledge score showed that attitude score was significantly associated with higher knowledge levels (B=0.18, β=0.556). Employment was negatively associated with knowledge score (B=−0.587, β=−0.089). For attitude score, knowledge score emerged as a significant positive predictor (B=1.729, β=0.56) while age showed a significant negative association (B=−0.063, β=−0.114).

Discussion

This is the first study to assess the KAP of vaccination among the Lebanese population and to reveal the factors associated with vaccine perception highlighting significant disparities between vaccinated and non-vaccinated individuals. Overall, the findings suggest that vaccination behaviour in this post-COVID-19 period is associated not only with knowledge but also with confidence in vaccine safety and effectiveness, affordability and engagement with health services, factors that are especially salient in Lebanon’s fragile context.14 Significant associations between vaccination status and various demographic factors were seen. Vaccinated individuals were older on average than their non-vaccinated, consistent with a US survey showing higher vaccination rates among older adults.26 Employment and healthcare worker status were positively associated with vaccination. Employed individuals were more likely to be vaccinated, knowing that unemployed people had a lower acceptance for vaccines in previous studies.27 28 Healthcare workers showed higher vaccination rates aligning with broader trends where vaccine acceptance among healthcare workers tends to be high as evidenced by the 88% acceptance rate for COVID-19 vaccines in a study conducted in USA among healthcare workers.29 Education level was strongly associated, with individuals holding a bachelor’s degree or higher being more likely to be vaccinated. This supports prior research highlighting the role of education in vaccine acceptance, likely due to a better access to information and health literacy.27 Financial coverage for vaccines was associated with vaccination status, consistent with findings that financial barriers significantly affect vaccine uptake.30 In Lebanon, where economic hardship, service disruptions and out-of-pocket costs have increased, the strong associations with education and financial coverage plausibly reflect structural barriers to access and affordability rather than individual hesitancy alone. These structural barriers may disproportionately affect vulnerable groups, including displaced and refugee populations, who often face additional constraints to continuity of care and access to primary health services.14

This study also revealed significant differences in knowledge about vaccination between vaccinated and non-vaccinated groups, measured using the Zingg knowledge scale. Vaccinated individuals demonstrated higher knowledge scores overall. A key interpretation is that misconceptions persisted in both groups, indicating that information gaps and misinformation-related beliefs remain an important target for vaccination communication in Lebanon. These findings align with studies showing that higher knowledge levels are associated with greater vaccine uptake in Europe31 and in recent post-COVID-19 KAP studies, where moderate knowledge levels coexist with persistent misconceptions about vaccine safety and effectiveness.32 However, in a crisis setting, knowledge alone may be insufficient to translate into consistent vaccination practices when affordability and service access remain constrained.

Significant differences in attitudes towards vaccination between vaccinated and non-vaccinated individuals were seen. The overall attitude scores further underscore this disparity, with vaccinated individuals exhibiting significantly more positive attitudes. Many countries are currently facing challenges with groups that refuse recommended vaccinations for themselves or their children.31 33 Categorisation of attitudes revealed that vaccinated individuals were nearly evenly split between positive (50.1%) and negative attitudes (49.9%), while most non-vaccinated individuals (64.6%) exhibited negative attitudes. Positive parents’ attitudes towards mandatory vaccinations varied across studies, with support ranging from 53% to 97% depending on the vaccination programme.34 35 Widespread concerns and misunderstandings about vaccines often negatively impact individual’s attitudes towards vaccination,36 as also reported in recent studies highlighting safety concerns and mistrust as key drivers of vaccine hesitancy in the post-pandemic period.16 In Lebanon, where trust in institutions and health services has been challenged during overlapping crises, strengthening confidence in vaccine safety and effectiveness through trusted healthcare providers and community channels may be particularly important.

Regarding the practices, vaccinated individuals were more likely to strictly follow recommended vaccination schedules both pre- and post-pandemic. Within this study population a similar trend was observed in child vaccination schedules, with adherence higher among vaccinated individuals pre- and post-COVID-19. The role of healthcare providers was notable, with 80.4% of vaccinated individuals having regular providers pre-COVID-19 compared with 19.6% of non-vaccinated individuals. Although this dropped to 71.8% post-pandemic for vaccinated individuals, it increased among non-vaccinated individuals to 28.2%. Influenza vaccination adherence showed similar patterns, increasing from 80.4% to 85.4% among vaccinated individuals and declining from 19.6% to 14.6% among non-vaccinated individuals. The divergence in reported practices post-COVID-19 may be related to broader differences in preventive health behaviour and confidence in vaccination; however, it may also reflect differential access to services and affordability constraints in Lebanon during the postcrisis period.22 These findings support the need for combined interventions that address both demand-side barriers (confidence and misinformation) and access-side barriers (cost and availability).

The logistic regression analysis identified several significant predictors of vaccination status. Age emerged as a key factor, with each additional year reducing the odds of being non- vaccinated by 2.4%. The number of children also had a protective effect, decreasing the odds of non-vaccination by 12.1% for each additional child. Education level was another significant predictor; individuals with higher education levels were 31.6% less likely to be non-vaccinated. Additionally, a more positive attitude score was associated with a 3.1% decrease in the odds of being non-vaccinated for each unit increase in score. The model accounted for a relatively small proportion of variability in vaccination status (~11.9%), suggesting that vaccination decisions are multifactorial and likely influenced by additional unmeasured determinants, including institutional trust, misinformation exposure, service availability and affordability constraints in Lebanon. The multiple linear regression analysis identified significant predictors for knowledge and attitude scores. Attitude score was positively associated with knowledge score (table 6), suggesting that more favourable vaccination attitudes tend to co-occur with higher vaccine knowledge. A study done in Serbia also found that knowledge demonstrated a positive association with attitude.37 Employment status was also associated with knowledge score, which may reflect differences in time availability, information exposure or health-seeking opportunities. Knowledge score was positively associated with attitude score, consistent with the interrelationship between knowledge and attitudes reported in previous studies. This correlation was observed in other studies done by Betsch et al at Frankfurt University and Pelly et al in Canada, they both observed a positive correlation between increased knowledge and a positive attitude.36 38 Age was also associated with attitude score, suggesting that attitudes may vary across age groups in this setting.

Based on the observed associations, recommendations should be tailored to Lebanon’s context and address both confidence and access barriers. Practical actions include: targeted risk communication focusing on vaccine safety and effectiveness concerns among groups with lower education and limited financial coverage; reducing affordability barriers through subsidised/free access to routine and influenza vaccination through primary healthcare (PHC) networks; strengthening provider recommendation and counselling within PHC services, given the role of regular healthcare providers; countering misinformation through trusted local channels and coordinated social media messaging and integrating routine vaccination screening, referral and follow-up into humanitarian health packages serving displaced and refugee populations.

The main limitation of this study was the reliance on self-reported data, which may introduce recall bias, particularly in assessing vaccination practices pre- and post-COVID-19. Future longitudinal studies could provide deeper insights into the evolution of vaccine-related KAP over time. Another limitation is that we have measured self-reported practices and not actual practices. Further studies should therefore examine how the knowledge scale about vaccination is associated with people’s actual practices. In addition, the vaccine knowledge scale by Zingg was adapted by removing two items that reduced internal consistency, increasing Cronbach’s alpha from 0.675 to 0.694. However, internal consistency remained slightly below the conventional 0.70 threshold, which may introduce measurement error and attenuate associations involving knowledge. While this adaptation improved reliability in our sample, it may reduce direct comparability with studies using the original 11-item scale and may influence interpretation of absolute knowledge scores across studies. Third, although a case-control design was applied with vaccination status as the outcome, both cases and controls were restricted to COVID-19 positive individuals captured in DHIS2. While this restriction improved internal comparability between groups, it may limit generalisability to non-tested or non-infected individuals and to the wider Lebanese population. Given the exploratory nature of the univariate analyses and the number of comparisons performed, findings should be interpreted in light of overall patterns and consistency across outcomes rather than isolated statistically significant results. In addition, the phone-based survey approach may have introduced selection bias by under-representing individuals without reliable phone access or updated contact information. The level of accurate knowledge individuals possess about vaccination can differ depending on the sources of information they rely on for making personal health decisions,36 adding sources of information in further studies would provide valuable insights.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of vaccination-related KAP among adults in Lebanon after COVID-19. Older age, higher education levels, greater number of children and more positive attitudes were associated with vaccination status. Vaccinated individuals demonstrated higher knowledge scores, more favourable attitudes towards vaccines and better adherence to routine and influenza vaccination practices, whereas non-vaccinated individuals reported lower adherence after COVID-19. The findings also show a positive association between knowledge and attitudes, suggesting that these dimensions are closely related in this setting.

Overall, the study identifies important differences in vaccination-related KAP between vaccinated and non-vaccinated groups in Lebanon in the post-pandemic period. These findings should be interpreted in light of the study’s reliance on self-reported practices and the restriction of the sample to COVID-19 positive individuals recorded in DHIS2, which may limit generalisability to the broader population.
