Time-Temperature Superposition Principle Serving as a Design Tool for Tailored MOF-Crystal Glass Composite Membranes
Authors: Ying Chen, Yi Yang, Mao Ye, Shuwen Yu, Fan Zhou, Dudu Li, Yuxiu Sun, Lei Tian, Wengang Huang, Jingwei Hou, Zhihua Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metal-organic framework (MOF) glass membranes demonstrate promising potential in the field of gas separation. This study presents the time-temperature superposition (TTS) principle to guide the fabrication of MOF-based crystalline-glass composite (CGC) membranes, addressing the critical challenge of thermal degradation of fillers during processing. Using glassy ZIF-62 as the continuous phase and crystalline UiO-66, Mg-MOF-74, and MOF-801 as dispersed phases, three types of MOF-based CGC membranes were successfully prepared through precise regulation of processing temperature and time, with minimal thermal decomposition. These membranes exhibit high separation efficiency for CO2/N2, CH4/N2, and C4 hydrocarbon mixtures. The TTS-assisted low-temperature remelting strategy establishes a versatile and tailorable platform for developing fresh CGC materials, opening additional pathways for expanding the family of CGC composites.

Introduction

Industrial chemical separation processes, as energy-intensive operations, have long accounted for a significant proportion of global energy consumption. Their high energy demand has become one of the key factors restricting the sustainable development of related industries. Against this backdrop, gas membrane separation technology has emerged as a highly promising sustainable alternative to conventional separation methods—such as cryogenic distillation, adsorption, and absorption—due to its low energy consumption, operational simplicity, environmental friendliness, and ease of modular integration1–4. In recent years, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have attracted extensive research interest in the field of gas separation owing to their precisely designable pore structures, high specific surface area, and tunable functionality, demonstrating significant potential for constructing high-performance separation membranes5–9. Currently, MOF-based membrane materials are primarily used in two forms: MOF crystalline membranes and MOF-polymer mixed matrix membranes. The former often suffers from issues such as grain boundary defects during growth, while the latter typically requires solvent-assisted processing. Moreover, conventional polymer membrane materials frequently face the trade-off between selectivity and permeability, which limits further enhancement of separation performance.

MOF glass, as a type of organic-inorganic hybrid glass, differs significantly from metallic, inorganic, and organic glasses10–12. In recent years, rapid progress has been made in the preparation of MOF glass membranes via the melt-quenching technique13,14. The resulting membranes exhibit dense surfaces, effectively eliminating grain boundary defects commonly found in crystalline materials during membrane processing, thereby significantly enhancing gas separation efficiency15. The solid-liquid-glass transition endows MOF glass materials with unique processability and formability. Bennett et al. first reported the melting phenomenon of ZIF-4 under an argon atmosphere upon heating16,17. Subsequently, studies on various glass-forming MOFs, including ZIF-62, TIF-4, and ZIF-4, have been published. Among these, ZIF-62 has emerged as the benchmark MOF glass material due to its excellent glass-forming ability and well-defined intrinsic physicochemical properties. Its reliable glass transition and favorable viscous flow characteristics in the glassy state serve as fundamental prerequisites for fabricating continuous, defect-free membranes. Furthermore, utilizing this widely characterized and stable model system allows for the effective isolation of variables, providing an ideal platform to accurately dissect the fundamental scientific principles and dynamic processing evolution in subsequent membrane engineering18.

Crystalline-Glass Composites (CGCs), first reported by Li et al., are fabricated by blending crystalline MOFs with glassy MOFs19,20. At a specific temperature, the glassy MOF transitions into a viscous liquid, which encapsulates the crystalline MOF component before being solidified via quenching. This process results in a homogeneous dispersion of crystalline MOFs within the continuous MOF glass matrix, yielding a composite material that combines the gas separation capabilities of crystalline materials with the continuous densification characteristic of glassy materials. This method offers high freedom in shaping, allowing CGCs to be cast into different form factors, thereby providing a pathway for fabricating high-performance self-supported gas separation membranes. The key to preparing CGC membranes lies in the rational utilization of the solid-liquid transition temperature. Most researchers leverage the intrinsic melting temperature of glassy materials for membrane fabrication. For instance, Ao et al. fabricated high-performance CGC membranes at 450 °C based on the melting behavior of ZIF-62, providing an important reference for the development of such membranes21,22. However, traditional melt-processing is fundamentally constrained by a closed thermodynamic window (Td < Tm). Because the matrix’s melting point (Tm) typically exceeds the decomposition onset (Td) of most high-performance MOFs, forcing high-temperature processing inevitably destroys the fillers’ structural integrity and intrinsic porosity. This severe restriction on filler compatibility makes circumventing the Tm barrier to achieve low-temperature membrane formation highly imperative. Notably, the glass transition temperature (Tg) of representative glass-forming materials such as ZIF-62 typically lies around 330 °C, suggesting the feasibility of preparing CGC membranes below 450 °C. For amorphous polymers, as the temperature rises above the Tg, the chain segments gain mobility. This initiates a transition from a glassy state to a rubbery state and eventually to a viscous flow state, endowing the material with processability. This viscoelastic behavior is characterized by a strong dependence on both time and temperature, both originating from a single underlying mechanism: the relaxation time of the molecular chains. Increasing the temperature enhances chain mobility, thereby shortening the relaxation time required for conformational changes. Equivalent mechanical responses can be observed at lower temperatures if the observation time is sufficiently extended to match the prolonged relaxation times. This interchangeability between the time and temperature scales is described by the time-temperature superposition (TTS) principle23. While originally established in polymer physics, its applicability to MOF glasses fundamentally stems from the viscoelastic relaxation behavior of the coordination network near its Tg. Kinetically, higher temperatures provide the thermal energy needed to overcome dissociation barriers, whereas at lower temperatures, extended processing times allow the system to accumulate sufficient thermal excitation probability, achieving equivalent structural evolution. By bridging these microscopic relaxation dynamics with macroscopic interfacial wetting behaviors, we posit that the processing and shaping of MOF glasses—potentially at much milder temperatures—can be rationally achieved through a concerted manipulation of time and temperature.

In this study, by systematically investigating the processing characteristics of glassy materials above their Tg, we propose the application of the time-temperature superposition (TTS) principle to guide the fabrication of MOF-based CGC membranes24. Via a TTS-assisted low-temperature remelting strategy, which involves the concerted manipulation of processing temperature and time, we successfully prepared three types of MOF-based CGC membranes—namely, agZIF-62/UiO-66, agZIF-62/MOF-801, and agZIF-62/Mg-MOF-74—using amorphous glassy ZIF-62 (agZIF-62, ‘ag’ denotes amorphous glass) as the continuous matrix. Crucially, this achievement was realized within a temperature window of 380–400 °C, far below the conventional processing threshold where such MOFs typically undergo thermal decomposition. The resulting membranes demonstrate highly efficient separation for challenging gas pairs, including CO2/N2, CH4/N2, and C4 hydrocarbon mixtures. The TTS-assisted low-temperature remelting strategy developed herein offers operational simplicity. By fundamentally trading time for temperature, it significantly broadens the processing window without requiring complex auxiliary treatments, thereby effectively addressing the longstanding challenge of integrating heat-sensitive, high-performance crystalline MOFs with MOF glassy materials. This approach not only provides a viable membrane technology for difficult separations but, more importantly, establishes a universal and tailorable platform for expanding the family of CGC composite materials.

Results

Figure 1 illustrates the fabrication process of the CGC membranes in this work (detailed procedures are provided in the Supplementary Materials). Initially, ZIF-62 crystals were converted to agZIF-62 at elevated temperatures. This step facilitated the subsequent shaping of CGC membranes at a relatively lower processing temperature, thereby preventing the thermal decomposition of crystalline MOF fillers during molding. The agZIF-62 was then mixed with MOF fillers, pressed into pellets, and sintered to form membranes. Prior to the formal fabrication of CGC membranes, preliminary investigations were conducted to determine the optimal processing window by evaluating the viscoelastic behavior of agZIF-62 over a broad temperature range (Supplementary Fig. 1). As shown in Fig. 2a, under a fixed processing duration, the MOF glass matrix transitions progressively from solid to liquid-like states with increasing temperature, exhibiting temperature-dependent viscoelasticity. However, excessively high temperatures induce thermal degradation of the MOF fillers, which is detrimental to membrane integrity. Notably, in accordance with the time-temperature superposition principle, temperature and time are interdependent variables that can be strategically used to modulate the viscoelastic behavior of the MOF glass. This approach provides a feasible route to avoid thermal degradation of MOF fillers that would otherwise occur at elevated temperatures. From a molecular mechanism perspective (Fig. 2b), ZIF-62 crystals undergo bond breaking and rearrangement upon reaching the melting temperature (Tm ≈ 430 °C), transforming into amorphous agZIF-62. Above the glass transition temperature (Tg ≈ 330 °C), segmental motion of the molecular chains is activated, representing a typical thermally driven relaxation process. The viscoelastic behavior of agZIF-62 near Tg follows the Williams-Landel-Ferry (WLF) equation, indicating that either increasing temperature or prolonging processing time can induce similar chain relaxation effects23. Leveraging this mechanism, processing parameters must be strictly tailored to the intrinsic thermal stability of each filler, defining a specific thermodynamic window. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, the processing temperature must exceed the threshold for matrix viscous flow but remain strictly below the onset of filler thermal decomposition. For instance, the superior thermal stability of UiO-66 allows for processing at 400 °C, maximizing matrix mobility for rapid densification. In contrast, heat-sensitive fillers like Mg-MOF-74, which begin to decompose near 400 °C, necessitate a lowered temperature of 380 °C. ZIF-62 crystals (Supplementary Fig. 3a) were successfully synthesized via liquid-assisted ball milling, exhibiting uniform particle size distribution (average diameter ≈ 2 μm) and regular appearance25,26. Following high-temperature calcination, the crystalline particles melted to form a smooth and dense surface (Supplementary Fig. 3b). XRD patterns (Fig. 3a) revealed the disappearance of characteristic crystalline diffraction peaks and the emergence of a long-range disordered structure, confirming the complete conversion of ZIF-62 into an amorphous material (agZIF-62)27. FT-IR spectra (Supplementary Fig. 4b) demonstrated stability of chemical bonds and functional groups during phase transition, with high consistency between ZIF-62 crystals and agZIF-62 spectra, aligning with literature reports28. To precisely characterize the thermodynamic phase transitions, two-cycle DSC analysis was performed (Fig. 3b). During the first heating cycle (Scan 1), a broad hump was observed, corresponding de solvation process. The melting temperature (Tm ≈ 426 °C) was strictly identified at the end of the endothermic melting peak in this initial scan. To eliminate the sample’s thermal history and prevent relaxation interference, the glass transition temperature (Tg ≈ 338 °C) was accurately determined during the second heating cycle (Scan 2) at the intersection of the extrapolated baseline tangents. Consequently, agZIF-62 glass membranes were typically fabricated by melting of the crystal at 450 °C, followed by quenching29 (Fig. 3b). To comprehensively evaluate the dynamic mechanical properties, both the storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G”) of the agZIF-62 matrix were investigated (Fig. 3c). At temperatures below 300 °C, the material exhibits a relatively high and stable G′, indicating a mechanically rigid solid state. Upon heating, G′ begins to decrease sharply at an onset temperature of 303 °C, marking the transition from a rigid solid to a softened state. Meanwhile, G″ displays a pronounced peak at higher temperature (380 °C), reflecting enhanced mobility in the molten state. The dramatic reduction in G′ together with the strong increase and peak in G″ provides clear rheological evidence of melt formation and viscous relaxation in this temperature range. It is worth noting that upon heating above the softening/melting region (onset 303 °C), the material undergoes deformation. The specimen loses its original geometry and mechanical integrity after the first heating cycle, making it difficult to maintain the required sample dimensions for a second DMA measurement. Therefore, the DMA experiment was conducted using a single heating scan, which is commonly adopted for materials that experience irreversible structural or morphological changes upon heating. Leveraging this viscous flow behavior, agZIF-62 powder was pelletized and re-calcined into membranes at temperatures (380 °C, 390 °C, and 400 °C). Figure 3d shows SEM surface images of sintered agZIF-62 membranes. To enable precise regulation, systematic control experiments were performed using a 10 °C temperature gradient (350-400 °C) at a fixed duration. Macroscopic optical transitions—from opaque below 380 °C to completely transparent at 400 °C (Supplementary Fig. 2)—directly reflect the matrix’s viscoelastic evolution. These transitions, corroborated by microstructural analysis (Fig. 3d), confirm that densification is accurately controlled through specific temperature-time thresholds, where increasing either parameter effectively reduces viscosity to promote flow and form dense membrane surfaces.Fig. 3Rheological characterization based on the Time–Temperature Superposition principle.a XRD patterns of ZIF-62, b DSC curves of ZIF-62 crystals, c the dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) curves of agZIF-62, d surface electron microscope images of agZIF-62 pure membranes under different conditions.

Through regulating thermal treatment time and temperature, we fabricated three types of MOF-based CGC membranes with a focus on characterizing the (agZIF-62)1-x/(UiO-66)x system. Specifically, UiO-66 crystals were synthesized via the solvothermal method (Supplementary Fig. 5), where their regular octahedral morphology confirmed structural integrity. The UiO-66 crystals were calcined at 400 °C, XRD patterns and FT-IR spectra (Supplementary Fig. 6) demonstrated retention of crystalline diffraction peaks and functional group absorption bands, proving that UiO-66 exhibits exceptional chemical stability without molecular/crystalline structural degradation at this temperature.

Given that Hou et al. observed partial decomposition of UiO-66 during MOF crystalline-glass composite fabrication at 450 °C30, this study employed a low-temperature remelting strategy. agZIF-62 was pre-mixed with UiO-66 crystalline powder (Supplementary Fig. 7), followed by calcination at 400 °C to achieve low-temperature integration of MOF crystals with the glass phase. Systematic characterization of CGC composites revealed that the normalized XRD pattern (Fig. 4a) showed complete disappearance of crystalline diffraction peaks from the agZIF-62 matrix (confirming full amorphization), while all peaks originated exclusively from the UiO-66 filler phase, with intensity increasing proportionally to UiO-66 loading—confirming intact preservation of UiO-66’s crystalline structure. FT-IR spectra (Fig. 4b) demonstrated that the primary chemical bonds and functional groups are well-preserved across the CGC membranes. Specifically, the characteristic absorption peaks of UiO-66 at ~1580 cm-1 and ~1390 cm-1 (assigned to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of carboxylate groups) are clearly visible, with their intensities consistently increasing alongside higher UiO-66 loading31. Concurrently, the characteristic peak at ~430 cm-1, corresponding to the Zn-N coordination bond, confirms that the structural integrity of the agZIF-62 matrix is fully maintained during the low-temperature remelting process32. The thermal stability of materials was investigated via TGA experiments. Supplementary Fig. 8 shows the thermogravimetric curve of UiO-66 crystals, which begin decomposition at 400 °C. Figure 4c displays the dynamic thermogravimetric curve of the CGC membrane materials, demonstrating excellent thermal stability within the 400 °C range. Furthermore, to explicitly validate the stability during prolonged TTS-assisted processing, isothermal TGA was conducted on the (agZIF-62)0.90/(UiO-66)0.10 membrane at 380 °C for 8 h (Supplementary Fig. 9). The material retained 97.79% of its mass, confirming that no significant thermal degradation or gradual decomposition occurs during extended sintering. Consequently, this reduced preparation temperature establishes an optimal and highly stable film-forming environment for heat-sensitive UiO-66. To accurately characterize the intrinsic pore structure parameters of the CGC membranes, we utilized Ar as a probe molecule—owing to its suitable kinetic diameter and lack of polar interactions—and performed adsorption measurements at 87 K to provide an objective evaluation of the microporous network. At this cryogenic temperature, the network framework of the pure agZIF-62 matrix adopts a disordered and highly “frozen” rigid state, resulting in low gas uptake33. As confirmed by Ar adsorption measurements at elevated temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 11), this phenomenon stems from low-temperature diffusion limitations rather than an absence of inherent microporosity. Nevertheless, for the CGC composite membranes (Fig. 4e), the incorporation of UiO-66 effectively increases the BET surface area from 7.2 to 20.3 m2/g, even at the extreme temperature of 87 K. The pore-size distributions shown in Fig. 4f reveal a distinct rightward shift in the most accessible pore-size for the (agZIF-62)0.90/(UiO-66)0.10 membrane. This indicates that UiO-66 indeed contributed additional pore spaces for the adsorbate to enter into this composite system (Supplementary Table 1). Mechanistically, while the vitrification of ZIF-62 disrupts its long-range ordered structure and significantly diminishes its intrinsic porosity, the TTS-guided low-temperature processing strictly preserves the rigid, highly porous crystalline framework of UiO-66. Consequently, the observed increase in overall microporosity is directly attributed to the incorporation of these intact UiO-66 pore networks into the dense glass phase, which successfully act as robust gas transport hubs without suffering from thermal collapse. CO2 and N2 adsorption experiments at 298 K (Supplementary Fig. 12 and 13) show that agZIF-62 moderately adsorbs CO2 but negligibly adsorbs N2. UiO-66 exhibits a stronger affinity for CO2 due to the stronger interaction between CO2 molecules and the framework, leading to preferential CO2 adsorption and expected to improve CO2 and N2 separation in the CGC membranes34.

Supplementary Fig. 14 presents scanning electron microscopy (SEM) surface images of (agZIF-62)1-x/(UiO-66)x membranes with increasing filler loading (a to d). Pristine agZIF-62 displays featureless surfaces, while composite membranes exhibit coherent matrix-filler interfaces without discernible defects—indicating effective interphase adhesion post-annealing. Cross-sectional SEM (Supplementary Fig. 16) further confirms membrane densification, with UiO-66 particles uniformly encapsulated within the ZIF-62 glass matrix. These collectively demonstrate dense CGC membranes, prerequisite for gas separation applications. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images (Supplementary Fig. 18) reveal smooth agZIF-62 domains alongside particulate UiO-66 features, demonstrating excellent compatibility at the interface between the filler phase and the matrix phase. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mappings (Supplementary Fig. 19) validate homogeneous filler dispersion without agglomeration. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) images in Supplementary Fig. 20a and 20b characterize agZIF-62 and (agZIF-62)0.90/(UiO-66)0.10 membranes, respectively. Supplementary Fig. 20a displays amorphous halos—characteristics of disordered atomic arrangements in pristine agZIF-62. Conversely, distinct lattice fringes within the yellow-rectangle region of Supplementary Fig. 20b confirm the preserved local crystallinity of UiO-66 in the CGC membrane. To further corroborate the retention of long-range structural order, selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) analysis was performed. The resulting SAED pattern exhibits distinct diffraction spots corresponding to the typical crystal planes of UiO-66 (Supplementary Fig. 21). This provides comprehensive evidence that the macroscopic crystalline framework of the filler remains thoroughly intact, without amorphization or structural collapse, after the 400 °C thermal processing. To explicitly verify that this TTS-assisted processing preserves both membrane integrity and filler functionality for highly heat-sensitive materials, comprehensive characterizations were extended to the (agZIF-62)1-x/(MOF-801)x (processed at 390 °C) and (agZIF-62)1-x/(Mg-MOF-74)x (processed at 380 °C) systems. As detailed in Supplementary Fig. 37, the XRD patterns unequivocally confirm the complete retention of the fillers’ crystalline frameworks, while Argon physisorption isotherms verify the accessibility of their intrinsic pore networks (Supplementary Fig. 38). Furthermore, corresponding surface and cross-sectional SEM images provide direct visual evidence of continuous, dense, and defect-free membrane formation, rigorously validating the universality of this fabrication strategy (Supplementary Fig. 39).

Beyond preserving microstructural integrity, macroscopic mechanical robustness is essential for validating the material’s stability under high-pressure operating conditions. Nanoindentation measurements (Supplementary Fig. 28, Table 2) reveal that incorporating 10 wt.% UiO-66 significantly reinforces the pure agZIF-62 matrix. The Young’s modulus (E) increases from 6.35 GPa to 11.68 GPa, and hardness (H) rises from 0.71 GPa to 1.64 GPa. This substantial mechanical enhancement confirms that the uniformly dispersed, rigid UiO-66 frameworks effectively dissipate applied stress, endowing the CGC membranes with the exceptional physical stability required for demanding industrial gas separations.

Gas separation performance of (agZIF-62)1-x/(UiO-66)x composite glass membranes (Supplementary Fig. 23 and 24) demonstrate concurrent enhancement of CO2 permeability with escalating UiO-66 loading. To clarify the underlying transport mechanism, we based on the dissolution-diffusion model and used the time lag method to decouple the permeation performance into the diffusion coefficient and the solubility coefficient (Supplementary Fig. 25). The CO2 diffusion coefficient of the agZIF-62 membrane is 33.5×10-5 cm3/s, and the N2 diffusion coefficient is 8.5×10-5 cm3/s. Notably, after the incorporation of UiO-66, the CO2/N2 transfer selectivity of the MOF-based CGC membrane increases to 13.0, which is 3.3 times higher than that of the pure agZIF-62 membrane. Combining the permeation coefficient and the diffusion coefficient, the dissolution selectivity of the MOF-based CGC membrane is 4.9, which is 1.63-fold enhancement compared with the pristine agZIF-62 membrane. A systematic evaluation of the gas adsorption isotherms at different filler loadings shows that the CO2 adsorption capacity increases significantly with increasing UiO-66 content. In the composite membrane, the dense agZIF-62 glassy matrix forms the primary transport network and functions as a molecular-sieving phase, while the incorporated UiO-66 introduces fast transport channels of approximately 0.6 nm and preferentially adsorbs CO2 through strong quadrupole interactions. Since the gas solubility within the membrane is essentially determined by its equilibrium adsorption amount, this adsorption enhancement directly corroborates the increase in CO2 solubility. Correspondingly, the dissolution selectivity increases, and together with the increased diffusion selectivity, it confirms that the gas transport within the membrane mainly relies on the dissolution-diffusion mechanism. The positron annihilation lifetime spectrum (PALS, Supplementary Fig. 22) further confirms this synergistic effect: the addition of UiO-66 increases the free volume fraction (FFV), thereby accelerating CO2 diffusion. PALS provides a more direct and quantitative microscopic characterization. As shown in Supplementary Table 3, after adding UiO-66, the positron lifetime (τ3) and its intensity (I3) both increase, which directly reflects the increase in the average size and relative concentration of the sub-nanometer free volume holes. Crucially, the free volume radius of the composite material (3.32 Å) is almost the same as that of the original agZIF-62 (3.27 Å). Such a tiny size difference physically confirms the tight and defect-free interface formation, thereby ensuring the stable maintenance of high gas selectivity. The 10 wt.% UiO-66 formulation ((agZIF-62)0.90/(UiO-66)0.10) exhibits excellent CO2/N2 selectivity. Additionally, we performed performance testing on 10wt.% UiO-66 CGC membranes prepared under three different conditions, with all membranes exhibiting good selectivity (Supplementary Fig. 26). Supplementary Fig. 16a2-d2 displays the thickness of the CGC membrane series, where the (agZIF-62)0.90/(UiO-66)0.10 membrane has a thickness of approximately 226.6 μm. Mixed-gas testing yields CO2 permeance around 82.5 GPU with α(CO2/N2) = 54.9 (Supplementary Fig. 23), while pure-gas conditions elevate CO2 permeance around 158.2 GPU and α(CO2/N2) to 60.3 (Supplementary Fig. 24). However, at a higher loading of 15 wt.%, selectivity declines significantly. This deterioration intrinsically occurs because the finite wetting capacity of the viscous agZIF-62 matrix reaches saturation. As visualized in Supplementary Fig. 15, excessive filler proportions induce severe agglomeration and non-selective interfacial voids. These macroscopic defects bypass the intrinsic sieving pathways, confirming 10 wt.% as the optimal threshold for defect-free integration. Competitive sorption explains marginally lower mixed-gas metrics versus pure-gas values. Critically, all composites surpass the 2019 Robeson upper bound (Fig. 5a2, Tables.S4-S6), with the 10 wt.% membrane demonstrating exceptional 48-hour stability (Supplementary Fig. 30)—validating these prepared glass composites as compelling candidates for CO2/N2 separation. To verify structural integrity over a larger testing area for practical applications, the optimized (agZIF-62)0.90/(UiO-66)0.10 membrane was scaled up to a 1.0 cm testing diameter (effective area ~ 0.785 cm2, Supplementary Fig. 27). Despite a > 40-fold increase in the testing area, it maintained a highly competitive CO2 permeance of 120 GPU and an α(CO2/N2) of 49.8. This consistent performance provides compelling evidence that the TTS strategy effectively eliminates interfacial defects, ensuring uniform encapsulation across larger testing areas. Furthermore, under highly humidified mixed-gas conditions (81.5%–88.5% RH, Supplementary Fig. 29), the membrane maintained robust CO2/N2 selectivity. Applying the time-temperature superposition (TTS) strategy to regulate MOF-glass membrane formation, we synthesized MOF-801 (Supplementary Fig. 31-32) and Mg-MOF-74 (Supplementary Fig. 33-34), fabricating (agZIF-62)0.95/(MOF-801)0.05 (390 °C, 6 h) and (agZIF-62)0.95/(Mg-MOF-74)0.05 (380 °C, 8 h) CGC membranes. Notably, preliminary experiments tested pure membrane performance at varying temperatures with identical holding times, establishing an experimental basis for holding duration (Supplementary Fig. 36). These membranes exhibit homogeneous surfaces, dense cross-sections, and coherent filler-matrix interfaces (Supplementary Fig. 39). Beyond validating processing universality, these specific heat-sensitive fillers were deliberately selected to expand the application scope of the CGC platform based on their unique separation potentials. Specifically, MOF-801 exhibits a preferential adsorption affinity for CH4 over N2 driven by higher polarizability35. Meanwhile, Mg-MOF-74 features a high density of one-dimensional coordinatively unsaturated Mg2+ sites, endowing it with a strong binding affinity for molecules with unsaturated bonds, making it an ideal candidate for challenging C4 hydrocarbon separations36. We used CO2 as the probe and conducted in-situ Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) to reveal the adsorption behavior at these open metal sites. CO2 is primarily adsorbed through coordination between one terminal oxygen atom of CO2 and the unsaturated Mg2+ sites37. FTIR can provide local binding configuration information of gas molecules. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 41, after approximately 15 minutes of exposure to CO2, the spectrum underwent significant changes: the asymmetric stretching vibration ν3 shifted from 2343 cm-1 to approximately 2347 cm-1. This blue shift originated from the formation of a bent-angle complex (Mg2+···OCO) by CO2 coordinating with Mg2+, directly confirming the strong affinity of Mg-MOF-74 for unsaturated molecules38. Furthermore, we used electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) to detect the unpaired electron signals related to unsaturated Mg2+ sites to confirm the retention of coordinatively unsaturated Mg2+ sites (caused by removal of coordinated solvent molecules) in the CGC membrane. The EPR quantitative test (Supplementary Fig. 42) showed that the signal intensity was proportional to the concentration of unsaturated Mg2+. Both the activated Mg-MOF-74 and CGC membranes exhibited significant unsaturated Mg2+ signals, while the pure glass matrix showed almost no response, ruling out the contribution of the matrix. Thus, it can be concluded that the unsaturated Mg2+ in the CGC membrane are entirely from the filler phase. This ultimately confirmed that these unsaturated Mg2+ sites were successfully retained in the CGC material and remained accessible. Consequently, gas separation assessments demonstrate breakthrough performance. For natural gas purification, where N2 contamination severely compromises methane purity39, the MOF-801 composite achieves a CH4 permeance of 9.5 GPU with α(CH4/N2) = 6.15 (Fig. 5b1), exceeding literature benchmarks (Fig. 5b2, Supplementary Table 7). Furthermore, in the highly demanding separation of butadiene from C4 hydrocarbon mixtures35, the Mg-MOF-74 system was systematically evaluated against various alkane isomers. Because isobutane (i-C4H10) presents the largest steric hindrance and interacts with the membrane solely through weak van der Waals forces, its separation from the strongly coordinating butadiene (C4H6) is the most pronounced40. Thus, the Mg-MOF-74 membrane delivers a C4H6 permeance of 164.1 GPU, with α(C4H6/i-C4H10) = 9.65 serving as the representative benchmark (Fig. 5c1). To provide comprehensive scientific support, excellent separation performance for α(C4H6/n-C4H10) = 6.32 is also demonstrated (Supplementary Fig. 40). These results consistently outperform state-of-the-art materials (Fig. 5c2, Supplementary Table 8). This dual-system validation confirms the TTS strategy’s versatility for engineering high-performance CGC membranes across disparate MOF fillers.

Notably, the separation characteristics of these CGC membranes reflect a distinct design philosophy compared to previously reported ZIF-62 glass foams25. While the latter prioritized high permeance through structural engineering (i.e., physical pore-generation), the present TTS-assisted strategy focuses on a paradigm shift in processing technology. This approach addresses the long-standing thermal stability mismatch between heat-sensitive fillers and high-Tg matrices, ensuring robust structural integrity and defect-free interfacial encapsulation. Within this framework, the membranes maintain a dense, continuous glass phase where gas transport is primarily governed by the intrinsic free volumes of the components rather than hierarchical porosity. This deliberate trade-off prioritizes manufacturing universality and precise molecular control, establishing a customizable platform for integrating diverse functional fillers that are otherwise incompatible with conventional high-temperature melting.

Discussion

In summary, this work pioneers a time-temperature superposition (TTS)-guided low-temperature remelting strategy for MOF-based CGC membrane fabrication. By exploiting time-temperature equivalence to trade processing time for lower temperatures, this approach safely circumvents the thermal degradation of heat-sensitive crystalline fillers. By precisely regulating temperature and time, we integrate the processability of glass matrices with functional fillers at 380 °C-400 °C, 0.5 h-8 h—significantly below conventional processing thresholds—to engineer three MOF-based CGC membranes. These achieve efficient separation of CO2/N2, CH4/N2, and C4 hydrocarbon mixtures. Beyond demonstrating exceptional versatility across multiple MOF families, this operationally simple strategy can be extended to other meltable systems (such as coordination polymer glasses) and integrated with commercial substrates. Ultimately, it provides a highly promising and universal paradigm for expanding the fabrication and practical application of CGC membranes.

Methods

Material synthesis

Synthesis of the agZIF-62: In this work, the method used for the synthesis of crystalline ZIF-62 was liquid-assisted ball milling. Zinc acetate dihydrate (3.30 g), Zn(OH)2 (3.48 g), imidazole (5.96 g), and benzimidazole (1.48 g) were accurately weighed and stirred well into ball milling jars, and a total of 30 stainless steel beads of both types were placed in each jar, and 5 ml of DMF was added. The reaction was set to start with a rotational speed of 380 r/min for 1 h. The reaction was then started.

After completion of the reaction, the product was immersed in a beaker using 150 ml of methanol, sealed and allowed to stand for 12 h. The suspension was drained and immersed in methanol again and repeated three times. The samples were collected after washing and placed in a vacuum oven at 80 °C overnight to obtain ZIF-62 crystal powder. The tube furnace program was set: (450 °C, 30 min), the ZIF-62 crystals were calcined at high temperature, the ZIF-62 particles after the first melt were ground into a powder, herein referred to as agZIF-62 powder. ‘ag’ denotes amorphous glass.

Synthesis of UiO-66 crystals: Accurately weighed ZrCl4 (2.12 g) and formic acid (10 ml) were dissolved in 50 ml of DMF and stirred for 30 min and the resulting slurry was poured into the reactor. It was heated at 120 °C for 24 h to form a white powdery UiO-66 pellet, separated by centrifugation and washed with methanol to remove the residual DMF. The samples were collected after washing and placed in a vacuum oven at 100 °C overnight to obtain UiO-66 crystal powder.

Synthesis of MOF-801 crystals: Fumaric acid (10.16 g) and ZrOCl2·8H2O (32 g) were dissolved in a N, N-Dimethylformamide (DMF) / formic acid mixed-solvent (20:7 in volume) in an autoclave. A hydro thermal synthesis is carried out for the autoclave at 130 °C for 6 h. After completion of this step, the supernatant was decanted. The product was then washed three times with DMF, followed by three washes with methanol solution. After washing, the sample was collected and dried in a vacuum oven at 150 °C for 12 hours to obtain MOF-801 crystal powder.

Synthesis of Mg-MOF-74 crystals: 2,5-Dihydroxy-1,4-benzenedicarboxylic acid (0.111 g) and magnesium nitrate hexahydrate, Mg (NO3)2-6 H2O (0.475 mg), were dissolved in a 15:1:1 (44.1 mL: 295 μL: 295 μL) mixture of DMF-ethanol-water (50 mL). The mixture was stirred until complete dissolution and placed in a reactor (125 °C, 24 h). After removal, it was washed with DMF and methanol, dried and collected to obtain Mg-MOF-74 crystal powder.

Preparation of MOF-based CGC membranes

The thickness of the freestanding CGC membranes was precisely regulated using a fixed-area molding technique combined with strict gravimetric control (Supplementary Fig. 16). Total mass of the prepared CGC membrane was 100 mg, and the mass proportion of UiO-66 in the membrane was accurately weighed using an electronic balance (x, x = 5%, 10%, 15%). Different mass ratios of agZIF-62 powder and UiO-66 crystal powder were mixed thoroughly in a mortar. A tablet press equipped with a fixed-diameter circular stainless-steel mold (20 mm) was used to apply a pressure of 1 MPa to the mixed powder for 30 s. The pressurized material was placed in a tube furnace and the program was set (400 °C, 30 min) under He atmosphere. Self-supported CGC glass membranes, named (agZIF-62)1-x/(UiO-66)x membranes (Fig. 1). The (agZIF-62)1-x/(MOF-801)x membranes and (agZIF-62)1-x/(Mg-MOF-74)x membranes were prepared in the same way, and the time and temperature of the MOF-801 membrane was 390 °C for 6 h, and the time and temperature of the Mg-MOF-74 membrane was 380 °C for 8 h.

Characterization

Changes in crystallinity of MOF crystals and CGC membranes before and after calcination were analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, D8 Discover and D8 Advance, Bruker) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15418 nm) at a scanning rate of 0.02 s-1 between 2° and 50° with 5° min-1. The structures of MOF crystals and CGC membranes were analyzed and characterized using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR, Tensor ІІ and Vertex 80 v, Bruker) in the range of 400 cm-1 to 4000 cm-1 with the resolution of 1 cm-1, and the corresponding infrared absorption spectra of the different molecules were used to analyses the chemical bonding, functional groups and structural changes of the materials before and after calcination; Scanning electron microscope (SEM, Gemini 500, Zeiss) and EDS were used to observe the morphology of the prepared crystalline granular materials, and the surface and cross-section morphology of the CGC membrane were observed to analyses the denseness of the membrane and the melting of the crystals, and the distribution of the fillers in the membrane; The morphological structure of the sample was characterized using a high resolution transmission electron microscope (HR-TEM, JEOL JEMF200). The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) mode was applied to investigate the preservation of MOFs’ crystal structure during the preparation process of CGC membrane. The MOF’s crystal planes information corresponding to such pair of diffraction spots can be inferred by the Bragg equation from the distance between the symmetric electron diffraction spots; The dynamic mechanical properties of agZIF-62 were characterized using a dynamic mechanical analyzer (DMA Q800, TA Instruments). Measurements were performed in a dynamic temperature sweep mode under a continuous nitrogen atmosphere, with the oscillation frequency set to 1 Hz. The sample was heated from 100 °C to 500 °C at a constant heating rate of 3 °C/min, during which the temperature-dependent evolution of the storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G”) was continuously monitored. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted under an N2 atmosphere to assess the thermal stability of the samples. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, 200F3, Netzsch) was employed to determine the melting temperature (Tm) and glass transition temperature (Tg) of the CGC membranes. The measurement consisted of two consecutive thermal cycles. In the first scan (Scan 1), the sample was heated from 30 °C to 500 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere. Following a rapid cooling step at a rate of 50 °C/min, the second scan (Scan 2) was immediately conducted using the identical heating program. Collectively, TG and DSC were utilized to investigate the thermal behaviors and physical state changes of the samples upon heating Nano-indentation technique (TI980, BRUKER, Germany) was employed to assess the mechanical property of the membrane. The CO2 and N2 adsorption capacities of the crystal and MOF based CGC membrane materials at 298 K were obtained by Micromeritics; The argon (Ar) adsorption isotherms were measured at 87 K using a specific surface area and pore size distribution analyzer (BSD660 M, Beishide instrument, China), from which the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) specific surface areas and pore structure parameters of the crystalline materials and CGC membranes were calculated. Prior to all adsorption measurements, the samples were degassed under vacuum. In situ Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (in situ IR, INVENIO S) was performed using CO2 as a probe to elucidate the adsorption behavior at unsaturated Mg²⁺ sites. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR, Magnettech ESR 5000, BRUKER) was used to detect signals from unpaired electrons associated with unsaturated Mg²⁺ sites, confirming that the unsaturated Mg²⁺ sites were retained.

Gas Separation Performance Evaluation

The present work utilized the constant pressure method to determine the gas permeance. For systematic evaluation of the gas transport properties of the CGC membrane, all separation measurements were primarily conducted using circular membranes with an effective testing area of 0.018 cm2. To further verify that the results obtained are scalable, an enlarged membrane with a practical testing diameter of 1.0 cm (effective area ≈ 0.785 cm2) was additionally fabricated and evaluated. The upstream gas, a mixture of equal volumes of CO2 and N2, the flow rate kept at 30 cm³/min. For the downstream, dry He served as the purge gas, and the flow rate kept at 5 cm³/min. The pressure of the downstream gas is one standard atmosphere (1 atm). Under the action of the pressure gradient, some of the gas enters from upstream and permeates downstream. The permeate gas is considered to have reached a steady state when the flow and composition of the purge gas remain constant over time. To analyze the gas composition, the downstream gas line was connected to a gas chromatograph (GC, Agilent 7890B). By combining the permeance of helium in the downstream gas with the permeance of the upstream gas permeating through the membrane, the permeance of carbon dioxide and nitrogen can be calculated, along with the CO2/N2 selectivity. The test points are randomly selected from different locations of the CGC membrane for testing, and at least 10 cycles of each test are conducted to ensure the accuracy of the data.
