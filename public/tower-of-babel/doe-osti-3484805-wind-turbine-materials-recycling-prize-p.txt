 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) and AltiSora, LLC., will develop new
 lightweight cementitious composite material technologies that will utilize wind turbine blade
 waste as a raw material to; (1) allow a high value addition (2) at low cost, enabling (3) a
 significant waste consumption volume; while (4) consuming the entire wind turbine blade,
 without (5) creating any waste or emissions and to also (6) offer specific benefits to communities
 involved.

 CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

 • Enhances the laboratory’s core competencies
 • Uses the laboratory’s core competencies
 • Enhances U.S. competitiveness by utilizing DOE developed intellectual property and/or
 capabilities.

 Summary of Research Results:

 The intent of this project was to identify and select one promising material option incorporating
 recycled wind turbine blade (WTB) residuals into a cementitious materials composite in
 collaboration with NLR and AltiSora, LLC. under the Wind Turbine Materials Recycling Prize
 Phase 2 Voucher. Team AltiSora, LLC. conducted material testing and product performance
 related to thermal performance, noise reduction, and roofing applications. A hotspot lifecycle
 assessment of the most promising WTBW-cementitious composite will be performed to
 determine emissions reduction of the AltiSora, LLC. product. NLR will provide technical
 guidance on manufacturing scale-up and commercialization of products. This will include
 recommendations for additives, suitable equipment, manufacturing processes and product
 qualifications.

 Task 1: The Participant will provide the Contractor with initial research data, raw materials,
 experimental formulations, procedures and available performance data, as well as lab-scale cured
 mortar samples for testing. The Participant will also provide new information on additional raw
 materials, such as surfactants and potential new surfactants to be incorporated in ongoing
 collaborative research work. The Contractor will develop optimal cementitious material
 composite designs and coupons incorporating wind turbine blade wastes for baseline composite
 characterization (i.e., physiochemical characterization on raw materials and mechanical testing
 on composite materials). The Contractor will provide the optimized mortar mixture designs to
 the Participant for sample preparation.

 2

 The Participant provided the Contractor with initial research data, raw materials, experimental
 formulations, procedures and available performance data, as a baseline. From the initial baseline
 mixture, the Contractor performed a series of single factorial permutations to assess the
 contributions to the mixture performance from the individual components. The baseline mixture
 contained ASTM C595 Type IL cement, water, a latex polymer with 50% solids, and a
 commercially available surfactant air entraining admixture. All mixtures had a water-to-cement
 (w/cm) ratio of 0.40. Surfactant was dosed as fluid ounces (oz) per 100 lbs of cement (cwt).
 Latex polymer was dosed as mass of latex solids per mass of cement. WTB fibers and powder
 were dosed as percent by volume of the entire cementitious material. Figure 1a describes the
 nomenclature used to name the WTB composites, where the series values indicate the following
 information: Series A – Influence of latex polymer, Series B – Influence of surfactant, Series C –
 Influence of WTB powder, Series D – Influence of WTB fiber. Figure 1b shows an example set
 of cubes of the baseline mixture N-2200 which did not contain any WTB material, but did
 contain 7.0 oz/cwt surfactant and 7.5% polymer.

 Figure 1. a) Nomenclature for the WTB cementitious composites, b) 2 in. by 2 in. mortar cubes for
 the baseline mixture: N-2200.

 Figure 2a shows the WTB powder in the as-received condition. Figure 2b shows an SEM
 micrograph of WTB powder indicating a non-homogenous material containing small fibers and
 residual resin from the WTB composite. Figure 2c shows produced cubes containing 25% by
 volume WTB powder (N-2202).

 Figure 1. a) WTB powder in as-received condition, b) SEM imaging of WTB powder at x30
 magnification, c) N-2202 cementitious 2 in. mortar cubes containing WTB powder.

 An important finding from the initial characterization of the WTB powder was the absorption
 capacity and influence on the workability of the cementitious composite. The strength and
 performance of cementitious materials is directly related to the water-to-cement ratio (w/c) with
 lower w/c producing higher strength. In conventional concrete the absorption capacity of the
 non-cementitious materials (i.e. aggregate) is measured to allow for an appropriate water
 correction to maintain the w/c and fresh workability. However, the standards used to determine
 the absorption capacity of aggregate do not apply to WTB powder and an adapted procedure was
 used.

 4

 The absorption capacity of the WTB powder was determined by measuring an initial mass of the
 powder, fully saturating the material with water for 72 hours, then using a towel to press out as
 much water as possible. A final mass was recorded and compared to the initial dry mass. Using
 this method, the absorption capacity was measured to be 100%. Consequently, all mixes
 containing WTB powder had additional water added equal to the WTB mass to satisfy the
 material absorption. Figure 2 highlights the importance of considering the absorption capacity of
 the WTB powder to allow for a workable mixture and to allow for water to be available for the
 cement hydration reaction. Figure 3a shows the N-2200 mixture without water correction for the
 WTB powder absorption resulting in a mixture with insufficient workability to be molded into
 specimens for testing. Figure 3b is the same mixture with water correction for WTB absorption
 showing a much more workable mixture, although highly influenced by the presence of the WTB
 powder material. Workability of the various mixtures was determined according to ASTM
 C1437. Figure 3 shows the effect on workability of the WTB powder with decreasing flow with
    increasing WTB powder (N-2200, 0%; N-2201-12.5%; N-22015-18.75%; N-2202-25%).

 Figure 2. a) Not water corrected WTB cementitious system, b) water corrected WTB cementitious
 system.

 The incorporation of WTB powder was found to exert a significant influence on the properties of
 the mortar composites. As shown in Figure 6a, increasing the WTB powder dosage from 0% to
 25% by volume resulted in a systematic reduction in the dry density of the mortar cubes. A
 corresponding decrease in compressive strength was also observed, as presented in Figure 6b.
 Collectively, this dosage range provides a representative spectrum of expected performance with
 respect to both density and strength.

 The 7-day compressive strength results for all mixtures, including those containing WTB
 powder, are summarized in Figure 6b. For each mixture, three two-inch mortar cubes were
 prepared in accordance with ASTM C109 and tested at seven days following ASTM C305
 procedures. The results demonstrate a strong correlation between composite dry density and
 compressive strength, suggesting that density serves as a useful predictor of strength
 performance.

 7

 Overall, the dosage of WTB powder plays a critical role in governing both density and strength.
 Accordingly, the proportion of WTB powder can be adjusted to meet application-specific
 performance requirements, whether the objective is to maximize strength, reduce density, or
 achieve an optimized balance between the two.

 Figure 6. a) Dry density for each WTB powder dosage level, b) respective compressive strength
 for WTB powder dosage level.

 The inclusion of WTB fibers resulted in significant reductions in compressive strength relative to
 the WTB powder mixtures. This decrease is attributed to several factors, including the formation
 of weak interfacial transition zones (ITZs) between the smooth fiber surfaces and the
 cementitious matrix, increased entrapped air and porosity associated with fiber addition, and
 localized stress redistributions under compressive loading. While fibers are effective in bridging
 cracks and enhancing tensile and flexural toughness, they do not contribute significantly to load
 bearing in compression and may instead act as stress concentrators when not well bonded.
 Consequently, the incorporation of WTB fibers is more beneficial for improving post-cracking
 ductility and impact resistance than for increasing compressive strength.

 Task 2: The Participant will provide samples of cured mortar samples in suitable form and
 dimensions to allow acoustic evaluation, with the associated compositional and procedural
 information for each. The Contractor will identify the key variables that determine the acoustic
 properties of WTBW in cured mortar samples. The Contractor will measure sound absorption
 and reflection at various frequencies over a relevant frequency range. The Contractor will
 correlate acoustic properties with other physical properties such as density and pore size and
 material composition. The Contractor will provide guidance on scale-up, commercialization and
 manufacturing to the Participant.

 8

 The acoustic response of the composites was further evaluated through measurement of the
 dynamic Young’s modulus using a longitudinal resonance test (ASTM C215). As shown in
 Figure 7, increasing the WTB powder dosage led to a systematic reduction in the dynamic
 Young’s modulus, which is indicative of increased compliance and thus a higher potential for
 sound absorption. This suggests that WTB powder, by reducing stiffness and density, may be
 particularly beneficial in applications where acoustic damping or noise reduction is desired.
 However, as this test serves only as a proxy for acoustic absorption, further dedicated testing is
 required to confirm this hypothesized relationship in materials of practical geometry.

 In contrast, the inclusion of WTB fibers is less likely to enhance sound absorption. Fibers
 typically increase heterogeneity and introduce localized stiff inclusions that may scatter sound
 waves but do not significantly reduce the bulk stiffness of the composite. Moreover, as observed
 in compressive strength testing, fibers tend to reduce strength through poor bonding and higher
 porosity rather than through a controlled decrease in matrix stiffness. Thus, while fibers may
 contribute to crack bridging and toughness, they are not expected to improve acoustic damping
 to the same degree as powder.

 Overall, the results suggest that WTB powder plays a more direct role in modifying acoustic-
 related properties through reductions in dynamic modulus, whereas WTB fibers primarily
 influence mechanical toughness with limited benefit for sound absorption.

 Figure 7. Dynamic Young's Modulus for powder dosage series. Calculated from the longitudinal
 resonant frequency of 3 in. x 6 in. cylinders.

 Task 3: The Participant will prepare cured mortar samples according to Contractor
 specifications, that will allow material evaluation for roofing applications. The Contractor will
 evaluate WTBW cured mortar samples for roofing applications. The Contractor will provide
 guidance to the Participant on the critical specifications and test methods required for a mortar-
 based solution to be qualified and used in roofing applications, including water permeability,
 UV-resistance, impact resistance, thermal expansion and adhesion to typical exiting roofing
 materials may need to be included in the material testing portfolio. The Contractor will provide
 guidance to the Participant on mortar placing equipment and methods, and provide guidance on
 scale-up, commercialization and manufacturing.

 To support the evaluation of WTB-derived mortars for roofing applications, cured mortar beams
 were prepared and tested for flexural performance. Flexural strength and toughness are critical
 parameters for roofing materials, as they govern resistance to bending stresses, crack
 propagation, and long-term durability under service conditions. WTB fibers were incorporated
 into mortar composites at dosages ranging from 0% to 2% by volume, consistent with typical
 maximum loadings used in fiber-reinforced concretes. Center-point bending tests were
 performed on 2 in. × 2 in. × 10 in. beams and representative fractured specimens are shown in
 Figure 8.

 Figure 8. Broken beams after flexural testing.

 Failure mechanisms in fiber-reinforced mortars occur either by fiber pull-out, controlled by the
 fiber–matrix bond, or by fiber rupture, governed by the intrinsic strength of the fiber. The
 fractured beams in this study exhibited evidence of fiber rupture, consistent with the rough WTB
 fiber surfaces observed in Figure 5b, which promote mechanical interlocking.

 The stress–strain behavior for each mixture is presented in Figure 9a, with the corresponding
 Young’s modulus shown in Figure 9b. Both flexural strength and post-cracking toughness (area
 under the stress–strain curve) improved with increasing fiber dosage, with a modest increase in
 stiffness also observed. These findings indicate that WTB fibers can enhance toughness in a
 manner comparable to conventional fibers already used in roofing composites, suggesting
 potential applicability in this sector.

 10

 While flexural testing represents only one element of a broader performance evaluation portfolio
 necessary for roofing qualification. Additional properties, including water permeability, UV
 resistance, impact resistance, thermal expansion, and adhesion to common roofing substrates,
 should be incorporated into future testing campaigns to establish a comprehensive durability
 profile.

 Figure 9. a) 7-day stress-strain curves from 3 pt. bending of 2 in. x 2 in. x 10 in. beams with varied
 fiber dosages, b) the Young's modulus for each trial.

 Task 4: The Participant will prepare cured mortar samples to allow the evaluation of thermal
 properties for potential application in thermal insulation. The Contractor will measure the
 thermal insulation properties of cured mortar samples. The Contractor will provide guidance to
 the Participant on the thermal insulation properties required in various building and construction
 applications.

 Thermal conductivity was evaluated for the WTB powder dosage series using two-inch mortar
 cubes and the transient plane source (TPS) method. As shown in Figure 10, increasing the WTB
 powder content resulted in reduced composite density and a corresponding decrease in thermal
 conductivity. Lower thermal conductivity indicates greater resistance to heat transfer, thereby
 enhancing the insulating capability of the material.

 The range of thermal conductivities observed demonstrates that WTB powder dosage can be
 used as a design variable to tailor the thermal performance of mortar composites for application-
 specific requirements. Materials with reduced thermal conductivity may be advantageous in
 roofing applications where improved thermal insulation contributes to energy efficiency and
 occupant comfort.

 Task 5: The Contractor will conduct a hotspot cradle-to-gate lifecycle assessment (LCA) of the
 WTBW cementitious materials composite, focusing on the environmental impacts from raw
 material extraction to factory gate (stages A1-A3). The Contractor and the Participant will
 quantify and evaluate key environmental indicators, including carbon footprint, energy
 consumption, water usage, and resource depletion, across three primary stages.

 A Lifecycle Assessment (LCA) was performed according to ISO 14040 and 14044 standards to
 determine the greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions of the investigated mixtures. The WTB end of
 life (EOL) handling and preparation were determined for a standard 9000kg blade. EOL
 processing inputs included a crane for removal, an excavator with saw for size reduction and

 cubic yard of final cement-based product in the fresh state which includes water contained within
 the latex polymer, mixing water, and additional water added to satisfy the absorption of the WTB
 powder, when included in the mixture. Inventory analysis is based on the detailed material

 The other factors which contribute to the overall product A3 GWP include transportation of the
 various materials to the production facility (A2) and the mixing/production energy embodied
 within the process. Currently the latex polymer is shipped from the manufacturer to Houston, TX
 at a distance of 1,040 miles and the WTB materials from Cedar Rapids, IA to Houston, TX
 which is 1,050 miles. If trucking is assumed for both materials using a 20 ton (40,000 lb) load, 6
 mpg fuel efficiency, and 10.21 kg CO2e per gallon of diesel, the A2 contribution from the diesel
 ranges from about 1% of the material contribution for the baseline mixture containing no WTB
 material (N-2200) to 6% for the mixture containing the highest amount of WTB powder (N-
 2202). By locating the production facility to within 30 miles of both the latex polymer and WTB
 production, the A2 contribution due to transportation falls to 0.20% to 0.33%. If transport mode,
 total payload, or distance changes, or if a different truck efficiency is used, the emissions will be
 adjusted accordingly. The mixing contribution to A3 for ready mixed concrete is typically 5-10%
 and would be refined once production equipment and location have been finalized. Future
 growth will include WTB materials sourced within Texas, at much reduced distances.
