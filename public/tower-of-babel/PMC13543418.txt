Development and validation of the Emotional Gambling Scale (EGS) in a community sample from Spain with recent history of gambling
Authors: Laura Diaz-Sanahuja, Macarena Paredes-Mealla, Carlos Suso-Ribera, Azucena García-Palacios, Soledad Quero, Juana María Bretón-López
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Gambling disorder (GD) is a non-substance-related addiction characterized by persistent and recurrent gambling behavior that leads to significant financial, occupational, academic, personal, and social impairment [1]. GD constitutes a public health problem, with a yearly prevalence ranging from 0.12% to 5.8% and a lifetime prevalence between 0.7% and 6.5% [2]. It frequently co-occurs with other psychological disorders, such as depression, anxiety, substance use disorders, and behavioral addictions [3, 4].

The development and maintenance of GD is influenced by several transdiagnostic processes, among which deficits in emotion regulation (ER) have received increasing attention [5]. ER encompasses a set of processes aimed at modulating the valence, intensity, and/or duration of affective experiences. According to Gratz and Roemer [6], deficits in ER are multidimensional, including difficulties in emotional awareness and acceptance, problems engaging in goal-directed behavior or inhibiting impulsive behavior when experiencing negative emotions, limited flexibility in employing strategies to modulate emotions, and unwillingness to experience negative emotions while pursuing meaningful goals [7].

Failures in selecting appropriate ER strategies considering long-term goals, as well as underestimating the costs of impulsive behavior, may lead individuals to engage in gambling as a way to escape or manage intense emotional states [5, 8]. This process is closely linked to emotional urgency, a facet of impulsivity reflecting the tendency to act rashly under high emotional arousal [9, 10]. Emotional urgency can be divided into negative and positive urgency, which integrate affective states with impulsive tendencies: negative urgency refers to rash actions under negative emotions (e.g., frustration, sadness, anger), while positive urgency refers to impulsive behavior under intense positive emotions (e.g., excitement, elation) [11–13]. Both negative and positive urgency have been shown to predict GD severity and poorer treatment outcomes, including higher relapse rates and dropout [14]. Negative urgency is particularly related to coping motivation, as gambling may serve as a maladaptive strategy to alleviate distress [11], whereas positive urgency may reinforce gambling in the context of reward-seeking or celebratory behaviors [12].

Several instruments exist to assess ER difficulties and emotional impulsivity. For instance, the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS) evaluates non-acceptance of emotional responses, difficulties engaging in goal-directed behavior, impulse control deficits, lack of emotional awareness, limited access to ER strategies, and lack of emotional clarity [6, 15]. Likewise, the UPPS-P Impulsive Behavior Scale measures facets of impulsivity, including positive and negative urgency [11]. However, to our knowledge, no instruments directly assess the extent to which specific positive and/or negative emotions trigger gambling urges.

We propose the construct of emotional gambling, conceptualized as the increased likelihood of gambling behavior in response to intense emotional states, whether negative or positive. This construct parallels the concept of “emotional eating,” in which eating urges and maladaptive eating behavior occur in response to attempts to regulate both negative (e.g., anxiety, depression, anger, loneliness) and positive emotions [16–19]. Similarly, emotional gambling encompasses urges and gambling behavior that arise as attempts to modulate affective experiences, reflecting the interaction between emotion regulation processes and impulsivity under emotional states.

Method

Aim

The aim of the present study was to develop a psychometrically robust instrument to assess emotional gambling, defined as the degree to which specific emotional states elicit gambling urges. Specifically, we sought to: (1) Develop and refine a parsimonious item pool for the Emotional Gambling Scale (EGS); (2) Examine its factor structure and internal consistency; and (3) Provide evidence of construct validity by examining associations with gambling severity, gambling-related cognitions, impulsivity, affective symptoms, and quality of life.

Unlike prior research, the EGS targets both positive and negative emotional triggers, reflecting evidence that gambling urges are not only a coping response to negative affect but may also arise in positive affective states.

Design

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design for the development and validation of a novel psychological instrument. Data collection was conducted online via Qualtrics, and the study followed recommendations for scale development outlined by Clark and Watson [20], Boateng et al. [21], and Muñiz and Fonseca-Pedrero [22].

Although the study was conducted at Universitat Jaume I (Castellón, Spain), participants were recruited across Spain through social media platforms (LinkedIn, Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, Twitter) and university pamphlets. Data collection occurred between July 2021 and April 2022.

Participants

A total of 371 individuals met the inclusion criteria: (a) being 18 years of age or older, (b) having gambled at least once during the previous three months, and (c) being Spanish-speaking. Participants had a mean age of 28.54 years (SD = 11.59; range = 18–72). Regarding sex distribution, 54.4% identified as male (n = 202), 45.3% as female (n = 168), and 0.3% as intersex (n = 1).

The full sample (N = 371) was used for item analyses and factor analyses, including the exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Of these participants, 328 completed all study measures and were therefore included in the construct validity analyses examining associations between the EGS and external variables.

Gambling severity was characterized using the National Opinion Research Center DSM Screen for Gambling Problems (NODS) [23, 24], with established thresholds indicating that 1–2 affirmative items correspond to at-risk gambling, 3–4 items to problem gambling, and 5 or more items to pathological gambling. Based on these criteria, 47.6% non-problematic gamblers (n = 156), 33.3% at-risk gamblers (n = 109), 9.5% problematic gamblers (n = 31), and 9.7% pathological gamblers (n = 32).

Regarding education, 2.4% had primary or no formal education, 41.5% had completed secondary education, 15.5% had technical studies, and 40.5% had university or higher education. Most participants were students (50.3%) or employed (39.9%), with smaller proportions unemployed (5.2%), on long-term sick leave (1.2%), or retired (2.1%). Most participants (99.4%) had never received treatment for gambling problems, while 30.2% had received psychological help for other non-gambling-related issues.

Item generation for the EGS followed a multi-step, theory-driven process:

Conceptualization: Emotional gambling was defined as gambling urges elicited by specific emotional states. Both positive and negative affective triggers were considered, based on evidence that gambling can function both as an emotion regulation strategy and as a source of positive reinforcement [5, 25].

Procedure

Survey administration

Participants accessed the online survey after reading informed consent. The survey included: Sociodemographic questions; 55-item EGS; and other validity measures. Participants completing the survey and passing attention-control questions received a €5 gift card.

Item analysis and internal consistency

Prior to conducting factor analyses, all 55 emotion-related items were examined for their distributional properties and internal consistency to identify poorly performing indicators. Each item assessed the extent to which a given emotional state triggered an urge to gamble in the past three months, rated on a 5-point Likert-type scale (0 = Not at all, 4 = Very much).

Descriptive statistics (mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis) and reliability indices (corrected item–total correlation and Cronbach’s alpha if item deleted) were computed for each item (see Additional file 2).

Items were considered for removal if they exhibited restricted variance (< 0.60), low mean endorsement (< 0.50), extreme skewness (> 2.00) or kurtosis (> 4.00), or low discrimination (corrected item–total correlation < 0.60). In addition, items were flagged if their deletion did not reduce the overall internal consistency of the scale, thereby reducing redundancy while preserving reliability.

Data analysis

Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses

Sampling adequacy was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) index and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. KMO values above 0.80 were considered indicative of meritorious sampling adequacy. An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) based on the polychoric correlation matrix was conducted to determine the optimal factor structure of the EGS. Exploratory factor analyses were estimated using the minimum residual (minres) extraction method with oblimin rotation in the psych package in R. Solutions ranging from one to six factors were evaluated according to the root mean square residual (RMSR), the overall fit index provided by the minres method, and the explained common variance (ECV).

Although solutions with more than two factors showed small improvements in statistical fit, the two-factor solution was retained on the basis of theoretical interpretability, parsimony, and factor clarity. The retained factors reflected Positive Emotions and Negative Emotions. Factor correlations were also examined to assess the relationship between dimensions.

A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was subsequently conducted on the final 25-item version of the EGS using the lavaan package in R with the WLSMV estimator for ordinal data. The model included two correlated latent factors corresponding to Positive Emotions and Negative Emotions. Model fit was evaluated using conventional psychometric criteria, including χ², Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Standardized factor loadings and communalities were also examined.

To further evaluate dimensionality, a bifactor model including one general emotional gambling factor and two orthogonal group factors (Positive and Negative Emotions) was estimated. Omega hierarchical and explained common variance (ECV) indices were calculated using the BifactorIndicesCalculator package to assess the reliability and interpretability of total and subscale scores.

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.1 and SPSS version 31.

Construct validity

Pearson correlation coefficients between EGS scores and gambling severity, gambling-related cognitions, impulsivity, anxiety, depression, and quality of life were calculated.

Analyses were conducted with R version 4.5.1. and SPSS version 31.

Results

Item analysis and internal consistency

Preliminary item analyses were conducted on the initial 55-item pool of the Emotional Gambling Scale (EGS) using psychometric and conceptual criteria. Thirty items were removed due to limited response variability, marked non-normality, low item–total correlations, or limited conceptual relevance as gambling-related emotional triggers. Specifically, several discarded items reflected emotional states such as fatigue, sadness, guilt, or compassion, which showed weak associations with gambling urges within this sample.

The final version of the EGS retained 25 items with adequate distributional properties and satisfactory item discrimination indices. Internal consistency was excellent for both subscales. The Positive Emotions factor demonstrated Cronbach’s α = 0.96 and McDonald’s ω = 0.97, whereas the Negative Emotions factor showed Cronbach’s α = 0.94 and McDonald’s ω = 0.96. These findings indicate high reliability while reducing redundancy relative to the original item pool.

Exploratory factor analysis

An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) based on polychoric correlations was conducted on the 25 retained items using minimum residual extraction and oblimin rotation (n = 371). Sampling adequacy was excellent (KMO = 0.93), and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was statistically significant, χ²(300) = 11,633.47, p < .001, supporting the suitability of the data for factor analysis.

Fit indices for one- to six-factor solutions are presented in Table 1. The one-factor solution demonstrated inadequate fit (RMSR = 0.09; Fit = 0.939) and explained 58.2% of the common variance. The two-factor solution substantially improved model fit (RMSR = 0.03; df-corrected RMSR = 0.04; Fit = 0.985), accounting for 70.8% of the variance.

Parallel analysis suggested up to four factors; however, inspection of the rotated solutions indicated that solutions beyond two factors produced unstable or conceptually diffuse factors with limited substantive interpretability.

The retained factors represented Positive Emotions and Negative Emotions and were moderately correlated (r = .58). Standardized factor loadings for the Positive Emotions factor ranged from 0.62 to 0.96, whereas loadings for the Negative Emotions factor ranged from 0.59 to 0.94. Most items demonstrated low complexity and moderate-to-high communalities (h² = 0.53–0.85), indicating that the factor solution adequately accounted for item variance.

Confirmatory factor analysis

A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to evaluate the fit of the proposed two-factor structure in the full sample (n = 371) using the WLSMV estimator for ordinal indicators.

The Positive Emotions factor comprised 15 items, whereas the Negative Emotions factor comprised 10 items. Standardized factor loadings were uniformly high and statistically significant (p < .001), ranging from 0.76 to 0.93 for Positive Emotions and from 0.75 to 0.92 for Negative Emotions (Table 2). Item communalities ranged from 0.56 to 0.86, indicating that the latent factors explained a substantial proportion of item variance.

The correlation between the two latent dimensions was moderate to strong (r = .66), suggesting that positive and negative emotional gambling triggers represent related yet distinguishable constructs.

Model fit indices supported the adequacy of the two-factor model: χ²(274) = 947.80, CFI = 0.993, TLI = 0.993, RMSEA = 0.082, 90% CI [0.076, 0.087], and SRMR = 0.070. Overall, the CFA findings supported the structural validity and theoretical interpretability of the proposed EGS factor structure.

Reliability and dimensionality

Reliability analyses indicated excellent internal consistency for both EGS dimensions. The Positive Emotions subscale showed α = 0.96 and ω = 0.97, whereas the Negative Emotions subscale demonstrated α = 0.94 and ω = 0.96 (Table 3). These coefficients support the precision and homogeneity of the retained item set.

Inspection of the EFA results further suggested the presence of a substantial common dimension underlying emotional gambling vulnerability. The first unrotated factor accounted for 58.2% of the common variance, whereas the two-factor solution explained 70.8% of the variance overall. Although the two dimensions were clearly distinguishable, their moderate intercorrelation and consistently high item loadings suggest the presence of a broader underlying emotional gambling propensity construct.

Construct validity evidence of the EGS

Construct validity evidence for the EGS was examined through Pearson correlations between the Positive and Negative Emotions factors and external measures of gambling severity, gambling-related cognitions, impulsivity, emotional distress, and quality of life (n = 328; Table 3).

Both EGS dimensions were positively associated with gambling severity indicators, including the National Opinion Research Center Diagnostic Screen (NODS) and the Problem Gambling Severity Index (PGSI), with correlations ranging from moderate to strong (0.49 ≤ r ≤ .60, p < .001). Similarly, both factors were positively correlated with gambling-related cognitions measured by the Gambling-Related Cognitions Scale (GRCS) (0.57 ≤ r ≤ .65, p < .001).

Associations with impulsivity were positive and moderate in magnitude (0.32 ≤ r ≤ .42, p < .001), supporting the expected relationship between emotional gambling vulnerability and impulsive personality traits. Regarding emotional symptoms, Positive Emotions demonstrated small correlations with anxiety and depressive symptoms (0.21 ≤ r ≤ .23), whereas Negative Emotions showed moderate associations with anxiety and depression (0.34 ≤ r ≤ .40), all statistically significant at p < .001.

Finally, both EGS dimensions were negatively associated with quality of life scores, with correlations ranging from small to moderate magnitude (− 0.29 ≤ r ≤ − .44, p < .001). Overall, these findings provide convergent and criterion-related validity evidence for the EGS.

Discussion

The present study aimed to develop and validate a novel instrument, the Emotional Gambling Scale (EGS), designed to assess the intensity of gambling urges elicited by specific positive and negative emotional states. Emotional urgency traits, particularly the tendency to act rashly in response to intense emotions, are well-established predictors of problem behaviors, including gambling [13]. Although existing instruments evaluate general emotion regulation mechanisms (e.g., DERS) or impulsivity traits such as positive and negative urgency (e.g., UPPS-P), none specifically quantify which emotions trigger gambling urges and the intensity of these urges. The EGS addresses this gap, providing a targeted measure of “emotional gambling” that may advance both research and clinical practice.

Prior to factor analyses, the initial pool of 55 emotion-related items was carefully examined for distributional properties and internal consistency. Thirty items were removed due to limited variability, extreme skewness/kurtosis, or low item-total correlations, resulting in a more psychometrically sound 25-item scale (α ≥ 0.94), which reduced redundancy while preserving reliability.

Exploratory factor analysis supported a two-factor structure corresponding to Positive Emotions and Negative Emotions, explaining 70.8% of variance. Higher-order factor solutions (three to six factors) produced only marginal improvements in model fit and variance explained, indicating diminishing returns. Confirmatory factor analysis confirmed the two-factor model, with standardized factor loadings ranging from 0.747 to 0.928 and communalities (h²) between 0.56 and 0.86. The covariance between positive and negative emotions was moderate (r = .654), supporting the conceptual distinction between emotional valences while acknowledging shared variance.

Additional bifactor indices provided further information regarding the dimensionality of the EGS. The general factor showed strong reliability and accounted for a substantial proportion of common variance (ECV = 0.71; OmegaH = 0.83), supporting the interpretability of a total EGS score reflecting a general emotional vulnerability to gambling urges. At the same time, the multidimensional structure of the EGS remained relevant, particularly for negative emotional triggers. In contrast, the specific negative emotion factor retained moderate unique variance beyond the general factor, whereas the positive emotion factor showed limited reliable variance after controlling for the general factor. These findings suggest that the EGS captures a substantial general tendency to experience gambling urges in emotionally salient contexts, while negative emotional triggers retain additional specific variance beyond the general factor. Accordingly, the total EGS score appears interpretable, although the negative emotion subscale may also provide clinically meaningful information beyond the general dimension.

At the same time, the very high CFI and TLI values observed in the CFA may indicate substantial overlap among some items. To further explore this possibility, we conducted an additional bifactor analysis, which supported the presence of a strong general factor underlying the item set and may partially explain the high inter-item associations. However, RMSEA and SRMR values remained within acceptable ranges, suggesting that model fit was not solely driven by item redundancy. Nevertheless, future research could explore abbreviated versions of the scale and evaluate potential item reduction strategies while preserving the conceptual coverage of positive and negative emotional triggers for gambling.

Construct validity was supported by moderate positive correlations between both EGS factors and gambling severity, gambling-related cognitions, and impulsivity. Negative emotions were also positively associated with anxiety and depressive symptoms, whereas positive emotions showed weaker associations with these variables. Both subscales were negatively correlated with quality of life, consistent with the notion that gambling as an emotional regulation strategy can be maladaptive [5]. These results suggest that the EGS captures a theoretically meaningful construct related to gambling severity, gambling cognitions, impulsivity, and emotional symptoms, while specifically focusing on emotional triggers for gambling urges.

Assessing emotional triggers for gambling urges has practical implications. Gambling behavior is often used to regulate emotional states, yet this constitutes a maladaptive coping strategy that increases the risk of relapse [41–43]. Recent findings also support the relevance of emotional processes in gambling behavior. For example, Theodorou et al. [44] found that, although men and women differed in gambling patterns and motives, gambling to cope was the strongest predictor of gambling severity in both groups. Furthermore, maladaptive emotion regulation predicted greater gambling severity, whereas adaptive emotion regulation appeared to buffer the impact of gambling motives, particularly among men. These findings are consistent with the present study, as they reinforce the idea that gambling is frequently used as a strategy to regulate both distressing and emotionally arousing states. In this context, the EGS may offer added value by identifying the specific positive and negative emotions that trigger gambling urges, beyond broader measures of coping motives or emotion regulation difficulties. Identifying whether positive, negative, or both types of emotions act as triggers can inform personalized interventions. For example, patients whose gambling urges are primarily triggered by negative emotions may benefit from therapeutic strategies emphasizing emotional acceptance, problem-solving, and cognitive reappraisal, whereas those triggered by positive emotions might benefit from interventions focusing on mindful engagement and impulse control [45]. Furthermore, understanding the specific emotional profiles that contribute to gambling urges can refine clinical approaches such as stimulus control and emotion-focused relapse prevention.

This study has several strengths. Items were developed with input from expert judges, enhancing content validity. The final sample size exceeded recommendations for factor analysis [46–50] and included participants with a wide range of gambling severity, allowing us to evaluate the scale’s psychometric properties across different risk levels.

However, limitations should be acknowledged. The study’s cross-sectional design precluded assessment of test-retest reliability or sensitivity to change. Data collection was conducted online, which may underrepresent individuals with limited access to technology. Additionally, although the sample included participants with varying gambling severity, findings may not generalize to clinical populations with severe gambling disorder, warranting future validation in clinical settings.

In sum, the EGS provides a reliable and valid measure of the emotional states that elicit gambling urges. Its focus on positive and negative emotional triggers fills a notable gap in existing assessments, offering a valuable tool for both research and clinical applications in gambling behavior and relapse prevention.

Conclusions

The Emotional Gambling Scale (EGS) is a novel, psychometrically robust instrument for assessing emotional triggers of gambling urges. Its structure supports the assessment of both a general emotional vulnerability to gambling urges and domain-specific positive and negative emotional triggers. The scale demonstrates strong internal consistency and meaningful construct validity, as evidenced by correlations with gambling severity, gambling-related cognitions, impulsivity, and emotional symptoms. By identifying which emotional states are linked to gambling urges, the EGS enables clinicians to tailor interventions to individual emotional profiles, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of relapse prevention and emotion regulation strategies. Despite limitations regarding generalizability and longitudinal properties, the EGS represents an important advancement in the assessment and understanding of gambling-related behaviors.
