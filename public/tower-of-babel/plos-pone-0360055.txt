Spatiotemporal patterns of anticoagulant rodenticide exposure in bobcats across the northeastern United States

Jennifer A Grauer, David B Needle, Krysten L Schuler, Patrick J Connelly, Gretchen Fowles, Erica A Miller, Brehan Furfey, Kim Royar

ABSTRACT
Anticoagulant rodenticides are a pervasive threat to non-target wildlife health, with exposure in predators frequently driven by the interface of human development and wild spaces. Long-term regional data are needed to understand how exposure rates vary in relation to changing regulation and public use of rodenticide compounds. We analyzed opportunistic samples collected from 2013–2024 across the northeastern United States to quantify rates of anticoagulant rodenticide (AR) contamination in bobcats (Lynx rufus) and identify spatial hot spots of exposure. We detected seven AR compounds in 352 bobcats (59% exposed) across 4 states; the highest levels of exposure were in New Hampshire and Vermont. Our spatial clustering algorithm indicated localized hot spots of AR detection across New Hampshire, Vermont, and New Jersey, with New York clusters appearing cold by comparison. Exposed bobcats had 1–4 AR compounds (mean = 1.07, sd = 1.17) in their system and exposure rates varied by state, age, and sampling source (harvest versus roadkill) but not sex. Predicted odds of AR exposure increased by approximately 35%, and the predicted number of compounds increased by approximately 17%, for each year increase in bobcat age. Despite federal restrictions on the use of second-generation ARs going into effect during the course of our study, AR detection rates increased steadily over time at a rate of 25% per year. Our observations were similar to broad spatial patterns of AR exposure in northeastern fisher (Pekania pennanti), reflecting the ubiquity of AR exposure across predators with differing habitat use. This work provides baseline exposure information for a mesocarnivore species of interest, highlights a concerning trend of increasing AR exposure over time, and contributes to the growing evidence of widespread rodenticide contamination of non-target wildlife.

Introduction Environmental contaminants are pervasive stressors that pose a substantial and growing challenge for wildlife management [ 1 ]. Expanding development and encroachment into wildlands fragments habitat [ 2 ], increasingly places wildlife in conflict with humans [ 3 ], and exposes wildlife and their surroundings to deleterious contaminants [ 4 ]. Enacting effective governance to minimize harmful effects to biodiversity is fraught with difficulty [ 5 ], including long bureaucratic timelines and logistical limitations to enforcement, monitoring, and evaluation of success [ 6 ]. Yet, balancing considerations of wildlife health with human health and socioeconomic priorities is a persistent need. A prime example is rodent control using anticoagulant rodenticides, which directly and indirectly impacts the health of non-target wildlife [ 7 ]. Anticoagulant rodenticides (ARs) are used worldwide across agricultural, rural, suburban, and urban environments to kill rodent pests by inhibiting their blood-clotting ability and causing death by fatal hemorrhaging [ 8 ]. Non-target wildlife species are frequently exposed to and can bioaccumulate ARs via direct poisoning or consumption of contaminated prey [ 9 ], leading to both individual and potential population level effects [ 10 , 11 ]. Individuals may succumb to toxic exposure levels [ 12 ] or experience sub-lethal effects including diminished reproduction [ 13 ], reduced response to threats [ 14 ], compromised body condition [ 15 ], and suppressed immune function [ 16 ]. AR residue concentrations and the number of exposures at which deleterious health effects are observed can vary [ 17 ], necessitating further investigation to understand species-specific thresholds of AR toxicity. Exposure to ARs has been documented to increase vulnerability to additional environmental stressors including parasites and pathogens (e.g., notoedric mange in bobcats [ Lynx rufus ] and mountain lions [ Puma concolor ] [ 16 , 18 ]). High rates of exposure and health effects in non-target wildlife have become widespread and widely recognized [ 19 ], though the management of exposure poses a major challenge. Regulation of AR compounds varies greatly and is often lacking [ 19 ], but concerns over unintended consequences of AR use have increased given heightened documentation and awareness of the negative impacts to human health and non-target wildlife [ 20 ]. Since their earliest uses in the 1940’s, commonly used AR compounds have changed over time [ 21 ]. First-generation anticoagulant rodenticides (FGARs) including warfarin, diphacinone, chlorophacinone, and dicoumarol require multiple doses to become lethal and are largely available for public and professional use [ 20 ]. The more acutely toxic second-generation anticoagulant rodenticides (SGARs), which are toxic given a single dose, include bromadiolone, brodifacoum, and difethialone. These were developed to address rodent resistance to FGARs [ 22 ] and in the US have been largely regulated for application by professionals [ 20 ]. Specifically, in 2008 the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) modified where and how SGARs were sold (Federal Register E8-12493) in an effort to reduce risks to non-target wildlife. In 2024, they released a final biological evaluation on the effects of ARs on endangered species (Docket ID EPA-HQ-OPP-2023–0567) and suggested mitigation measures for use in critical habitats. Yet, information on the effectiveness of policies in reducing wildlife AR exposure is limited. Most states follow EPA standards for AR use, though some have enacted additional restrictions. Some of the strictest US regulations were enacted in California, which currently bans the use of both FGARs and SGARs outside of agricultural and public health applications (Poison-Free Wildlife Act, AB 2552, 2024). However, exposure in non-target wildlife continues in the state, likely due to purchase of compounds outside registered brokers and illegal application [ 23 ]. Trends in carnivore AR exposure remain similar across the World despite variation in regulation [ 19 ] and attempts to limit contamination of wildlife and their habitats [ 24 ]. In the northeastern US, AR exposure in non-target wildlife, especially mesopredators, is common. Fisher ( Pekania pennanti ) are widespread across the region and experience frequent exposure with multiple AR compounds, with notably high exposure in Vermont and New Hampshire [ 25 , 26 ]. High AR prevalences have also been observed in birds of prey in Massachusetts [ 27 , 28 ], red-tailed hawks ( Buteo jamaicensis ) in New York [ 29 ], and mesocarnivores in Pennsylvania [ 30 ]. The wildland-urban interface, or interspersing of structures within undeveloped vegetated spaces, appears to be a major driver of AR exposure [ 25 ]. Across the northeastern states a greater proportion of the fisher population detected with ARs in their system was correlated with suppression of population growth potential [ 11 ]. Notably, rates of exposure in New York fisher increased each year over the course of a three-year study [ 31 ]. Regulation of ARs in northeastern states follows federal rules and EPA guidance, though additional regulations banning the sale and distribution of both FGARs and SGARs and prohibiting use around wildlife habitats are currently being considered (New York Senate Bill S9069, Assembly Bill A10012; Vermont H.326; New Hampshire House Bill 1676). Both baseline and longitudinal data are required to evaluate whether regulatory changes produce tangible benefits to wildlife health through reduced rates of exposure or concentrations and how long it takes to see the effects of regulation change. Meanwhile, AR exposure may also change as continued development across the region increases habitat fragmentation [ 32 ], expanding the diffusion of rodenticide compounds in wild spaces with the intermixing of human structures and wildlife habitat [ 11 ]. Bobcat populations across the Northeast generally appear stable or increasing after rebounding from widespread declines [ 33 , 34 ], although some populations remain small or at low-densities [ 35 , 36 ]. Declines in habitat connectivity are expected to negatively impact bobcats in the Northeast, where much of their habitat is on privately-owned land [ 37 ] or has experienced pronounced human modification [ 38 ]. Both habitat fragmentation and urban development remain areas of concern for bobcat population health [ 39 , 40 ], while increased incursion of human-dominated landscapes into bobcat habitat could increase exposure to ARs [ 41 ] and contribute to population decline [ 10 ]. Bobcats are a wide-ranging species frequently monitored by state agencies and sensitive to disturbance. The goal of this study was to leverage long-term data to establish a baseline for bobcat exposure to ARs across a large extent of the Northeast US, with specific objectives to (1) quantify anticoagulant rodenticide contamination in samples from New Hampshire, Vermont, New York, and New Jersey, (2) evaluate changes in AR detection in bobcats across the span of a decade and between different sources of biological samples, and (3) identify spatial hot spots of AR exposure. Based on previous research with fisher in the region [ 20 ], and consistent with studies of AR exposure [ 24 ], we expected to detect frequent exposures of multiple AR compounds and, further, to observe spatial hot spots of exposure in southern Vermont and New Hampshire. Bobcats are obligate carnivores and their reliance on small mammals as a food source may place them at greater risk of secondary poisoning from ARs compared to other mesocarnivores like fisher, red fox ( Vulpes vulpes ), and grey fox ( Urocyon cinereoargenteus ). However, bobcats appear less tolerant of human activities, avoiding urban and developed lands [ 42 , 43 ] and recreationists [ 44 ], although urban bobcats populations have been observed [ 10 ] and tend to have higher concentrations of AR residues [ 16 ]. Nevertheless, sensitivity to human disturbance [ 45 ] potentially reduces the likelihood of bobcats scavenging rodents exposed to ARs compared to fisher [ 30 ]. Indeed, in stable isotope samples collected across the Adirondack Park (NY), bobcats exhibited less use of human food sources than coyotes ( Canis latrans ), gray foxes, red foxes [ 46 ], and fisher [ 47 ]. Thus, although we expected broad-scale patterns in AR exposure to match those observed for fisher across the region, we expected a lower overall magnitude of exposure in bobcats. Materials and Methods Sample collection and laboratory analyses Samples were opportunistically obtained from regulated harvest, incidental harvest, and road-killed bobcats in New Hampshire, Vermont, New York, and New Jersey from 2013–2024. Incidental harvest included those specimens obtained as non-target capture in harvest. Legal trapping and hunting seasons occurred in Vermont and New York during the study period while hunting and trapping for bobcats were closed in New Hampshire and New Jersey. Therefore, samples in New Jersey were largely obtained from roadkill and incidental harvest from counties where bobcats are found in the state. In Vermont, samples were collected from either roadkill or carcasses that trappers and hunters were required to submit to the Vermont Fish & Wildlife Department. On rare occasions, samples in New Hampshire and New York were obtained from sick, injured or human-bobcat conflict scenarios where bobcats were euthanized by state biologists. Additionally, dead bobcats were occasionally found opportunistically, with post-mortem necropsy attributing the ultimate cause of mortality to sources such as predation or pathogen infection. These specimens, along with conflict and sick animals, were included in a category of ‘other’ sources. The township location, year of sampling, and cause of death were recorded for each individual. Carcasses were submitted for necropsy at the New Hampshire Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory (Durham, New Hampshire, USA), New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection Office of Fish and Wildlife Health and Forensics (Hampton, New Jersey, USA), or New York State Department of Environmental Conservation Wildlife Health Unit (Delmar, New York, USA). When no discernable cause of death was identified during necropsy, the source was categorized as unknown. During necropsy, sex was observed and recorded when genital tracts were present. After necropsy, age was determined to the closest year from tooth cementum annuli of a canine [ 48 ] submitted to Matson’s Laboratory (Manhattan, MT, USA). Age was then summarized as either juvenile, including kittens <1 year old and juveniles 1−2 years old, or adult, ≥ 2 years old. We collected 20−100 g of liver tissue from each individual, stored samples at −20 C until analysis, and submitted samples for testing at the New Bolton Center of the Pennsylvania Animal Diagnostic Laboratory System. Samples were screened for 11 ARs using mass spectrometry and processed following the protocol in Facka et al. [ 30 ]. Samples were analyzed using the AB SCIEX API 4000 LC-MS/MS (SCIEX, Framingham, Massachusetts, USA) or the Agilent 6470 LC-MS/MS (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA). We recorded the number of different AR compounds detected in each sample and the concentration of each in mg/kg when levels met or exceeded the method detection limit (MDL). Samples with positive detections below the MDL were considered ‘trace’. Testing equipment changed over time, resulting in a change in MDL for several compounds. For those compounds whose MDL changed, we used the higher MDL value for samples in all years to conservatively categorize trace cases. The detection limit values used were: 0.100 ppm for warfarin, coumafuryl, chlorophacinone, coumachlor, dicoumarol, and bromadiolone, 0.05 ppm for diphacinone and difethialone, 0.025 ppm for brodifacoum and difenacoum, and 0.200 ppm for pindone. Patterns and hot spots in AR detection We considered individuals to be exposed when either a detectable concentration or trace level of a given compound was observed. We then summarized the mean and total concentrations of quantifiable levels of each AR and the total number of AR compounds detected in each individual. To describe patterns of detection in our data, we compared the proportion of cases exposed, the number of ARs, and the mean and total AR concentrations by sex, age, state and sample source using Welch’s t-tests [ 49 ] and Chi-squared tests [ 50 ]. Due to the large number of comparisons, we used a higher p-value threshold of 0.01 for inferring statistical significance. We examined whether exposure rates varied with bobcat age and year of the study using logistic regression, with a binary response variable of 1 indicating exposure and 0 indicating failure to detect an AR compound. We repeated the test for exposure by year of the study with samples from New Jersey alone, where samples were available across all study years, to assess whether the former test was influenced by the pooling of samples from all states and years. We used an additional linear model to assess the relationship between the number of detected AR compounds and bobcat age. We investigated spatial clustering of AR exposure using Getis-Ord Gi* statistics. This approach evaluates the clustering of spatial data and estimates the significance of statistically high (hot) or low (cold) local cluster values relative to mean levels [ 51 ]. Specific sample coordinates were only available for samples from New Jersey. For the remaining states cases were reported by township. For these samples we generated a random location within a given township to represent each individual bobcat sample. This allowed us to represent each case singly with a unique location and binary exposure status (1 exposed, 0 unexposed), rather than pool data for multiple individuals at the township scale. Analysis at the level of township polygons would either involve information loss if township binary exposure status were considered, or would be heavily influenced by sampling and the total number of cases available if the response variable was the number of exposed cases or proportion exposed. We first conducted a General G test to assess the global clustering of high or low values before statistically testing for hot and cold spots. We used k = 8 nearest neighbors to create adequate cluster size, as k = 4 neighbors proved too small to generate clusters and k = 16 neighbors encompassed a large portion of all samples within single clusters. The global test used a binary weighting of 1 for neighbors and 0 for other points. All spatial calculations and visualizations were conducted in program R [ 52 ] with packages sf [ 53 ], spdep [ 54 ], and ggspatial [ 55 ]. We defined spatial neighbors with the st_knn function and calculated spatial weights of those neighbors with the st_weights function. Both neighbors and weights were used to calculate the Gi statistic for each point (function local_g_perm) over 999 conditional permutations with the two-sided hypothesis that local clusters could be hot or cold areas of AR exposure. We characterized the significance of points at alpha levels of 0.1 (90% confidence), 0.05 (95% confidence), and 0.01 (99% confidence) based on permutation p-values, where significant positive Gi values were hot spots and significant negative Gi values were cold spots. We mapped all aforementioned levels of significance and considered locations to be hot or cold if they exceeded 90% confidence. Maps were created using U.S. Census Bureau 2022 TIGER/Line shapefiles and package tigris [ 56 ]. Discussion Our study detected high rates of AR exposure and steadily increasing exposure over time in bobcats sampled across four northeastern US states. The majority of bobcats in our sample (58.5%) were exposed to at least one AR, consistent with but generally at lower magnitudes than observed for fisher (79% [ 25 ]) and raptors (100% [ 27 ]) in the northeastern U.S., bobcats (89% [ 14 ]) and coyotes (98% [ 57 ]) in California, and higher than mesocarnivores in Pennsylvania (44% [ 30 ]). We detected high rates of brodifacoum and bromadiolone exposure, with more detections of SGARs than FGARs. However, our high detection rates of the FGAR diphacinone echo regional differences of high detections in the Northeast [ 25 ] and in urban coyotes in California [ 57 ], but not fisher and bobcat in California [ 14 , 58 ]. Such continued exposure to FGARs could indicate continued use of the compound [ 59 ], persistence on the landscape, or increased use of FGARs as regulations for the more potent SGARs become increasingly strict. We did not detect the FGAR pindone or SGAR difenacoum, reflecting limited use in the US relative to other countries [ 60 , 61 ]. Exposure to multiple AR compounds in bobcats was also consistent with the aforementioned northeastern US studies, with half of our positive samples exposed to one AR and the other half indicating multiple AR exposures. Quantifiable concentrations of ARs spanned the range associated with mortality from notoedric mange [ 18 ], although threshold levels for direct mortality and toxicosis are lacking [ 14 ]. Other studies of bobcats have detected small numbers of direct mortality from AR toxicosis [ 18 ], as well as gastrointestinal bleeding [ 62 ], population-level impacts [ 10 ], and increased susceptibility to environmental stressors [ 16 ] from AR exposure. Although bobcat populations are generally increasing across their range in North America [ 33 ], the threat of ARs to both individual and local population health should continue to be monitored as an important consideration for wildlife management. We detected hot spots of rodenticide exposure across multiple northeastern states, primarily in New Hampshire and portions of Vermont and New Jersey. This spatial pattern and the proportion of sample exposed is broadly similar to exposure status and the number of compounds detected in fisher across the Northeast [ 25 ], although some differences exist because we lacked samples across southern New York and did not interpolate across a continuous spatial surface. Specifically, fisher exposure to ARs was high in southern and western New York, though we did not detect any hot spots for bobcat in the state. We note that Getis-Ord Gi* may be sensitive to unequal sampling and have reduced confidence in areas without adequate neighbors [ 63 ]. The statistic also performs less well than other metrics of spatial autocorrelation when low-value samples are clustered [ 64 ]. Because of these limitations, cold spots in states with relatively sparse sampling (i.e., New York) may underrepresent local hot spots. Additional sampling may help elucidate whether New York is truly as cold, and New Hampshire truly as hot, as they appear given our available samples. Random chance and underlying patterns in sampling or biological variation may also have influenced patterns in states where we had limited sample sizes and temporal variation in sampling. In contrast, extensive sampling in New Jersey revealed a mix of hot and cold spots. Fisher are relatively rare in New Jersey [ 65 ], potentially making bobcat a better sentinel species for AR exposure across the southern edge of fisher range. Broad-scale patterns of exposure for bobcats may be similar to fishers if driven by the intermixing of low-density structures with forested wildland [ 25 ]; fine-scale patterns may differ from fisher due to increased bobcat use of edge habitats [ 37 ] and avoidance of human disturbance [ 46 ]. Edge habitat could be particularly important for AR exposure, considering the interface of housing units and altered open spaces experiences high residential AR use [ 66 ]; however, exposure in remote natural areas also occurs [ 14 ]. The urban-wildland edge was also found to be important for bobcat physiological responses to ARs at fine spatial scales in California [ 16 ]. Yet, studies frequently report limited or no association between habitat types and exposure [ 19 , 67 ], reflecting the ubiquity of ARs across the landscape. Despite increasing awareness of the negative effects of ARs on non-target wildlife and federal restrictions on consumer access to SGARs, exposure to widespread taxa persists and, as we observed, has been steadily increasing over time. EPA modifications of consumer access to SGARs by the EPA in 2008 may not have stalled overall AR exposure risk, though specific compound availability may have changed [ 7 ]. For instance, multiple species of owls were exposed to increasing concentrations of bromadiolone yet decreasing concentrations of brodifacoum over time [ 68 ], while red-tailed hawks saw increasing frequencies of bromadiolone and difethialone detections despite regulation change going fully into effect in 2015 [ 27 ]. Sales of bromadiolone, the compound detected at the highest mean concentration in our study, were reported to have increased during the study period [ 68 ]. Our detected rates of increase were similar to 8% annual increases in exposure of fisher in New York [ 31 ]. We detected a higher rate of increase for SGARs than FGARs despite higher perceived risk and tighter restrictions on their sale and use [ 69 ]. SGARs are often preferred by applicators because of their higher single-dose potency and lower resistance by rodents. While demand for both generations of rodent control products is expected to continue to increase, growth of the FGAR market may surpass that of SGARs [ 59 ] considering potential future restrictions or bans of SGARs. Still, both FGARs and SGARs remain commonly available online, and misuse by the public contributes to exposure even after regulation [ 70 ]. Overall, studies that monitor changes in percentage of samples positive for ARs indicate increases in overall exposure over multiple decades [ 68 , 71 ], with variation in when certain compounds were detected most [ 14 , 27 ]. Increasing fragmentation and development of the Northeast [ 32 ], along with greater intrusion of toxicants into wild spaces, could further increase exposure rates and contamination risk to non-target species [ 67 ]. Importantly, temporal variation in our sample was partially conflated with spatial variation in sample collection; samples from New Hampshire had higher rates of exposure and were collected in later years. However, the trend of increasing concentrations over time was consistent in New Jersey, where samples were obtained across all years. Though the collective sample also points to an increase over time, regional studies that consistently monitor AR exposure would be helpful in assessing trends in relation to legislation changes. Both the mean number of compounds and the proportion of bobcats exposed in our study increased with bobcat age, consistent with patterns observed in mesocarnivores in Chicago [ 72 ] and coyotes in California [ 57 ]. Although ARs are metabolized in animal tissue- typically faster for FGARs than SGARs- concentrations may accumulate if intake outpaces excretion rates [ 9 ] and potentially accumulate over an individual’s lifetime. Still, individuals <1 year old in our sample experienced high rates of exposure, with almost 50% of sampled kittens exposed. This may result from both the consumption of small contaminated prey items, including invertebrates [ 73 ], and maternal transfer [ 14 , 72 ], reflecting not only ubiquitous exposure across the landscape but persistent risk over time. Male bobcats were expected to have higher AR concentrations and exposure rates due to greater dispersal and larger home ranges than females [ 74 ], but we did not detect differences in exposure rates by sex. Differences in exposure were observed across age and sex for fisher in NY [ 31 ], but not detected in bobcats [ 16 ] or coyotes [ 57 ] sampled in California. We report concentrations of AR residues observed within our sample, but primarily analyzed rates of exposure and number of AR compounds, considering concentration does not necessarily reflect time since consumption or probability of negative health consequences [ 7 ]. Sampling for this study was largely opportunistic and varied by state, which could potentially have affected the variation in AR exposure rates observed among states and skewed our sample compared to the regional bobcat population. The sampling of road-killed animals could over-represent AR-exposed animals if they were predisposed to mortality; however, our samples from incidental and regulated harvest showed higher rates of exposure than roadkill samples. Conversely, bobcats that are overtly sick or die from AR exposure could be missed or misrepresented by opportunistic sampling closer to roads and development [ 75 ], potentially leading studies, even with large sample sizes, to fail to estimate true exposure rates or obscure assessments of landscape drivers. Additionally, detections of FGARs in our sample may be underrepresented considering their shorter half-life and lower dose in single exposures [ 8 ]. Difficulties in sampling across large regions and quantifying exposure in available samples has necessitated broad-scale work as in this study. As such, it was necessary to pool information across states to analyze changes over time, potentially conflating spatial and temporal differences. Variation in population densities across broad areas also makes obtaining consistent sample sizes challenging. However, biases of convenience sampling may have been comparable across the sampled states, and the patterns observed are largely consistent with measurements of other taxa [ 25 , 30 ]. Because patterns of exposure were similar to fisher in the Northeast [ 25 ], bobcat could potentially serve as a sentinel species in northern states where they are common and/or harvested [ 11 ]. For states where bobcats and fisher are comparatively rare [ 33 , 65 ], wide-ranging mesocarnivores such as foxes could be monitored as indicators of exposure. Expanded analysis of bobcat samples from adjacent states in New England would help to broaden the scale of inference and inform patterns and drivers of hot spots, particularly those along the southeastern portion of New Hampshire. Additionally, consistent monitoring of collected samples over time would improve our ability to assess temporal trends in addition to spatial variation. Conclusion Anticoagulant rodenticides pose an important yet challenging-to-manage risk to non-target wildlife species through both direct mortality and indirect impacts to individual and population health. Despite continued detection and our increasing knowledge of the deleterious effects of AR exposure, current regulations have failed to mitigate risks to non-target wildlife, as demonstrated by our finding of high and growing exposure in bobcats across the northeastern US. Mitigation measures have not reduced overall exposure to ARs; rather, persistent use for rodent control has led to consistent secondary exposure. While the built environment of the Northeast poses risks of increased incursion of AR compounds into wildlands, public educational campaigns on correct use and rodent carcass disposal, along with restrictions on deployment of SGARs, may help reverse trends of increasing exposure rates. Additional research on the sublethal effects of ARs in bobcats and other wildlife, the additive and interactive effects of ARs with other determinants of wildlife health, and evaluation of educational programs for minimizing wildlife risks are needed.
