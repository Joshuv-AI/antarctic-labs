Belief-Space Quantum-Inspired Reinforcement Learning for Partially Observable Autonomous Cyber Defense in the Internet of Vehicles
Anwar Shah, Rohan Farooq, Sajid Anwer, Tallha Akram, Usman Ghous, Sajid Ullah Khan

Abstract
The Internet of Vehicles (IoV) introduces a dynamic and adversarial security environment, where attackers adapt their strategies in response to defensive actions. Most existing intrusion detection systems for IoV rely on offline datasets and static classifiers, which fail to capture sequential decision-making, attacker adaptation, and uncertainty inherent in real-world deployments. In this work, we formulate IoV security as a sequential attacker-defender interaction and model defense as a reinforcement learning problem under partial observability. We propose Quantum Belief-Integrated Reinforcement Defense (Q-BIRD), a quantum-inspired belief representation encoding defender uncertainty about attacker’s hidden intent using amplitude-based states, enabling non-Bayesian belief evolution under ambiguous observations. The belief state is integrated into a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) defender that selects cost-aware mitigation actions such as alerting, throttling, and isolation. We evaluate the approach in a simulated IoV security environment with an adaptive attacker that strategically probes, attacks, and evades detection. The experimental results demonstrate improvements in commonly and widely reported metrics, achieving the reduction in cumulative mean damage, damage variance, attack success rate (ASR), and increased survival probability by 60.4%, 90.2%, 50.0%, 46.4%, respectively. Compared to PPO with classical Bayesian belief, damage variance reduction and ASR improved by 10.2 times and 50%, respectively. These improvements are attributed to Quantum-inspired belief representation, as confirmed by ablation, and explainability analyses. It suggests that the amplitude-based belief state is the primary decision signal during attacker strategy transitions when the classical belief collapses. The results demonstrate that non-classical uncertainty representations provide better security for IoV networks without additional hardware.

Introduction

The Internet of Vehicles (IoV) enables large-scale connectivity among vehicles, roadside units, and cloud services to support safety-critical applications such as cooperative driving, traffic management, and autonomous transportation systems

Current IoV intrusion detectors work in a fixed manner; they learn attack signatures from pre-recorded, labeled data and then apply those signatures in real-world traffic

Field studies demonstrate that this assumption fails once the system is in service, adversaries probe it, change their behavior, and evade the learned rules. The reliability of the detector falls rapidly

Recent work seeks to overcome those limits by turning to reinforcement learning and game-theoretic models for cybersecurity. Those models frame network defense as a sequence of decisions between attackers and defenders, both acting strategically

,

. The defender repeatedly selects mitigations such as raising an alert, throttling traffic, or isolating a suspicious node while weighing detection success, operational cost, and service uptime. A primary difficulty is that IoV defense must proceed with only partial observation; the true intent of an attacker remains hidden, and visible signals are often ambiguous due to probing or evasion.

Classical probabilistic belief models, commonly used to manage uncertainty in partially observable environments, tend to collapse rapidly under noisy or deceptive observations, leading to overconfident decisions that can be exploited by adaptive adversaries
[
10
,
16
]
. Recent advances in decision theory and learning under uncertainty suggest that non-classical uncertainty representations can improve robustness in environments characterized by ambiguity and delayed information
[
20
]
.

Although reinforcement learning-based defenses for the IoV have progressed, the existing approaches still rely on classical Bayesian belief models to track hidden attacker intent. These representations break down due to deceptive observations that adaptive attackers intentionally generate through probing and evasion. The outcome is an overconfident posterior that corrupts the policy input, triggering erratic, high-variance defensive actions at moments when reliable reasoning is most needed. Existing studies, such as Guo et al.
[
3
]
and
Chen et al.
[
1

improve the policy- or game-theoretic aspects of IoV defense, but they treat the belief model as a fixed classical submodule rather than as a designable element of the defense architecture.

This gap is significant: a defender whose belief collapses under evasion cannot maintain effective damage control, no matter how advanced the policy.
To overcome this challenge, non-classical uncertainty representations inspired by quantum probability theory preserve superposition across intent hypotheses under ambiguous observations without requiring quantum hardware, yet their application to IoV security remains entirely unexplored.

In this work, we propose a quantum-inspired belief representation for IoV defense that models uncertainty over hidden attacker intent using amplitude-based belief states. Unlike classical Bayesian belief updates, the proposed approach preserves uncertainty in the presence of ambiguous observations and enables more robust belief evolution without relying on quantum hardware or quantum computation
[
20
]
. We integrate this belief representation into a reinforcement learning-based defender using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
[
14
]
, allowing the defender to select cost-aware mitigation actions in response to an adaptive attacker. The proposed approach has been evaluated in a simulated IoV security environment that explicitly models attacker adaptation through probing, attacking, and evasion strategies. Simulation-based evaluation enables a controlled analysis of long-term damage, stability, and robustness under adversarial behavior, which cannot be captured by offline datasets alone
[

Experimental results demonstrate that, compared to classical probabilistic belief modeling, the proposed quantum-inspired belief yields significantly lower variance in cumulative attack damage and more stable defensive behavior under shifts in the attacker’s strategy, while maintaining comparable average performance. These results highlight the importance of robust uncertainty modeling for autonomous IoV security beyond static dataset-driven intrusion detection.

II
Literature Review

The security of the Internet of Vehicles (IoV) has attracted significant research attention due to the safety critical nature of vehicular communication systems and their exposure to a wide range of cyber threats. Existing studies have primarily focused on intrusion detection mechanisms and preventive security strategies aimed at identifying malicious behavior within vehicular networks.

II-A

IoV Intrusion Detection and Static Security Approaches

Security for in vehicle networks and for the Internet of Vehicles has usually been provided by intrusion detection systems that use machine learning plus deep learning classifiers. A detailed survey by
[
11
]
and others shows that almost every current solution trains a supervised model on a static offline data set. Those systems reach high accuracy when the test traffic matches the training traffic but they treat both the traffic pattern but also the attacker as fixed once the attacker varies the pattern, accuracy falls.

Recent studies tries to raise detection scores improving the data itself.

18
]
supply extra synthetic samples so that their model recognises zero day attacks inside the Internet of Vehicles.

]
build a deep learning detector that is specialised for vehicular buses. Both upgrades still wait for an attack before they act they do not plan a sequence of defensive steps and they do not act in advance they cannot lower the cumulative harm that a patient attacker inflicts over time.

II-B

Reinforcement Learning for Adaptive IoV Security

Static intrusion detection has limits, and reinforcement learning is a way to let cyber defense adapt. Nguyen, besides Reddi
[
9
]
show that deep reinforcement learning fits security tasks where the setting changes and opponents act. Ju et al
[
4
]
use the same approach for vehicular networks, they protect task offloading at the vehicular edge and find that learned rules beat fixed plans when traffic patterns shift.

More recent work has explicitly modeled attacker defender interactions in IoV systems. Guo et al.
[
3
]
combine game theory and reinforcement learning to design moving target defense strategies for IoV, while Ren et al.
[
12
]
propose a multi agent deep reinforcement learning approach for autonomous security management in Internet of Things environments. Although these studies demonstrate the benefits of adaptive and sequential defense, they often assume that attacker behavior is either fully observable or accurately inferred, which is rarely achievable in practice.

II-C

Risk Aware and Robust RL Based Security Models

Given the high cost of security failures in vehicular systems, several works have incorporated risk awareness and robustness into reinforcement learning frameworks. Lu et al.
[
8
]
propose a risk aware federated reinforcement learning approach for secure IoV communications, emphasizing the trade off between system performance and security risk. Similarly, Liu et al.
[
7
]
analyze the robustness of deep reinforcement learning algorithms under false data injection attacks, demonstrating that learning based controllers can be vulnerable to deceptive inputs.

While these approaches move toward more realistic threat models, they rely primarily on classical probabilistic assumptions and do not explicitly address uncertainty in attacker intent. As a result, belief updates may become overconfident or unstable when observations are sparse, noisy, or adversarially manipulated.

Classical belief representations commonly used in reinforcement learning and game theoretic frameworks struggle under partial observability, particularly when attackers deliberately obfuscate their behavior. This limitation motivates the exploration of alternative belief representations that can preserve uncertainty and support robust decision making in early and ambiguous attack stages.

II-D

Research Gap and Motivation

The above review shows that existing IoV security research either focuses on static intrusion detection or employs reinforcement learning with limited treatment of uncertainty in attacker intent. Although adaptive and risk aware reinforcement learning based defenses have been proposed, belief modeling remains underexplored, especially in environments with deceptive and non stationary adversaries. In contrast to prior work, this study introduces a quantum inspired belief representation within a reinforcement learning based IoV defense framework. III
Problem Formulation

We consider Internet of Vehicles (IoV) security as a sequential decision making problem under partial observability. At each time step
t
, the defender observes a system state
s_{t}
but does not directly observe the attacker’s intent
\theta_{t}\in\Theta
. Instead, the defender must make decisions based on indirect, noisy, and potentially deceptive observations of system behavior.

The defender selects a mitigation action
a_{t}
, such as monitoring, throttling, or isolation. This action influences both the immediate damage incurred by the system and the evolution of future system states. The damage depends on the attacker’s true intent and the effectiveness of the chosen defensive action. Since the attacker’s intent is hidden, the defender cannot reliably minimize damage using instantaneous or myopic decisions.

Fig. 2:
Non-stationary risk evolution in IoV environments and limitations of
existing approaches. The proposed quantum inspired amplitude belief yields
robust and stable belief dynamics compared to classical Bayesian collapse
under deceptive observations.

The defender’s objective is therefore to minimize the expected cumulative damage while accounting for the operational cost of defensive actions:

\min\;\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma^{t-1}\left(D_{t}+C(a_{t})\right)\right],

(1)

where
D_{t}
denotes the effective damage incurred at time step
t
,
C(a_{t})
represents the cost associated with the selected defense action, and
\gamma\in(0,1]
is a discount factor that balances immediate and future impact. The expectation reflects uncertainty in attacker behavior, system dynamics, and observations.

This formulation emphasizes long-term damage mitigation under uncertainty, rather than instantaneous intrusion detection decisions, and serves as the basis for designing adaptive IoV defense policies.

IV
Threat Model

We model an adaptive attacker capable of dynamically adjusting its behavior in response to defensive actions. The attacker keeps an internal intent value that remains invisible to outsiders, which can correspond to benign activity, probing, active attack, or evasion. The defender never sees the intent value itself but notices side effects that leak through events like anomalous traffic bursts, low-intensity probes, or
evasive behavior that lower the chance of discovery.

Fig. 3:
Attacker operates on hidden intent while the defender sees only system state and responds via policy
\pi_{\phi}
.

When a defender acts, the attacker changes how it behaves based on how much the system tries to find it. In this process, the attacker also considers how the defense reacted in the past. In vehicle networks, real actors operate in this way. It is also how they function in connected systems. By using this method, the model avoids the fixed patterns that datasets often show. Because of this design, the model shows how two sides influence each other. Such interactions are what researchers describe in recent studies.

Let the attacker intent space be

\Theta=\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{H}\}.

(2)

At each time step, the attacker selects an action

a_{t}^{att}\in\mathcal{A}^{att},

(3)

based on its current intent
\theta_{t}
. The attacker’s internal state evolves adaptively according to

\theta_{t+1}=G(\theta_{t},a_{t}^{def}),

(4)

where
G:\Theta times\mathcal{A}^{def}\rightarrow\Theta
models adversarial adaptation in response to defensive actions. This transition captures realistic strategic behavior in which attackers escalate, probe, or evade depending on mitigation pressure. The inclusion of
G
ensures that the environment is non stationary and adversarial, distinguishing this framework from static intrusion detection settings.

V
Methodology

This section provides a formal description of the proposed Internet of Vehicles (IoV) defense framework. The system is modeled as a sequential decision-making process under partial observability, involving interactions between an adaptive attacker and an autonomous defender over time. The formulation is illustrated graphically in Figure
4
to clearly define system dynamics, belief evolution, and policy optimization, independent of implementation-specific details.

V-A

Sequential Interaction Model

We consider a discrete time decision process indexed by
t=1,2,\dots,T
. At each time step, an adaptive attacker interacts with an autonomous defender within a dynamic IoV environment. The complete latent system state is defined as

x_{t}=(s_{t},\theta_{t}),

(5)

where
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the observable IoV system state and
\theta_{t}\in\Theta=\{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{H}\}
represents the hidden attacker intent. The observable state
s_{t}
consists of aggregate traffic statistics, anomaly indicators, and risk related signals extracted from vehicular communications. In contrast,
\theta_{t}
encodes the attacker’s internal strategic mode, such as benign behavior, probing, active attack, or evasion.

The defender observes
s_{t}
but does not directly observe
\theta_{t}
, which introduces partial observability into the system. As a result, the defender must infer attacker intent indirectly from observable signals over time.

V-B

Environment Transition Dynamics

The observable IoV system evolves according to the transition function

s_{t+1}=F(s_{t},a_{t}^{att},a_{t}^{def}),

(6)

where
F:\mathcal{S}times\mathcal{A}^{att}times\mathcal{A}^{def}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}
. This function captures the joint influence of attacker and defender actions on traffic flow, service availability, and risk signals. Defensive actions such as throttling or isolation may reduce attack impact but can also affect system performance. The complete hidden state evolution can therefore be expressed as

x_{t+1}=\hbox{$\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}F(s_{t},a_{t}^{att},a_{t}^{def}),\;G(\theta_{t},a_{t}^{def})\hbox{$\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}.

(7)

The above equation defines the joint evolution of the complete system state. Recall that the full latent state at time
t
is given by
x_{t}=(s_{t},\theta_{t})
, where
s_{t}
denotes the observable IoV system state and
\theta_{t}
represents the hidden attacker intent. The expression
7
therefore states that the next system state consists of two simultaneously updated components. The first component,
F(s_{t},a_{t}^{att},a_{t}^{def})
, determines the next observable state
s_{t+1}
as a function of the current state, the attacker’s action
a_{t}^{att}
, and the defender’s action
a_{t}^{def}
. This captures how malicious activity and defensive interventions jointly influence traffic conditions, congestion levels, and risk related signals in the IoV environment. The second component,
G(\theta_{t},a_{t}^{def})
, determines the next hidden attacker intent
\theta_{t+1}
based on the current intent and the defender’s action. This models adversarial adaptation, whereby the attacker may modify its internal strategy in response to defensive pressure. Together, this formulation compactly represents the simultaneous evolution of both the observable environment and the hidden attacker strategy, thereby defining the complete system dynamics under partial observability.

V-C

Belief Modeling Under Partial Observability

As
\theta_{t}
is not directly observable, the defender maintains an internal belief over the intent space. In the classical formulation, the belief state is represented as a probability vector

b_{t}\in\Delta^{H-1}

(8)

whic states that the belief at time
t
, denoted by
b_{t}
, belongs to the
(H-1)
dimensional probability simplex. This means that
b_{t}
is not an arbitrary vector but a valid probability distribution defined over the
H
possible attacker intent states in
\Theta
. In other words, the defender maintains a structured probabilistic estimate over the hidden intent variable.

The probability simplex is formally defined as

\Delta^{H-1}=\left\{b\in\mathbb{R}^{H}:\sum_{i=1}^{H}b_{i}=1,\;b_{i}\geq 0\right\}.

(9)

This definition specifies that every belief vector
b
is a collection of
H
numbers. For each component
b_{i}
, the value is the likelihood that an attacker has intent
\theta_{i}
. By adding all the
b_{i}
values, the total is always 1 (
\sum_{i=1}^{H}b_{i}=1
). There is also a constraint that no
b_{i}
is less than zero. To be a valid probability distribution,
b_{t}
must follow those rules.

The belief state changes over time according to

b_{t+1}=\mathcal{B}(b_{t},s_{t+1},a_{t}^{def}),

(10)

where
\mathcal{B}
is the belief update operator. The new belief
b_{t+1}
is computed from the old belief
b_{t}
, from the system state
s_{t+1}
that was just observed and from the defender action
a_{t}^{def}
. Operator
\mathcal{B}
performs one Bayesian inference step, this revises the prior belief with evidence that the observable part of the environment supplies. By repeating this update at every step, the defender keeps improving its estimate of the attacker’s hidden goal even when only partial observations are available.

Classical probabilistic belief updates have limitations; we replace them with a belief model that borrows ideas from quantum theory. This model treats uncertainty through amplitude based states; it does not touch probabilities directly. The defender keeps a state vector whose entries are complex numbers, not a vector filled with probabilities.

\psi_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{H},

(11)

The symbo
\mathbb{C}^{H}
labels an H-dimensional complex vector space. Each entry
\psi_{t}(i)\in\mathbb{C}
and is paired with a possible attacker intent
\theta_{i}\in\Theta
. The entries in
\psi_{t}
are complex amplitudes and each entry carries a magnitude together with a phase. The vector
\psi_{t}
is called the amplitude based belief state.

A probability for a particular attacker intent
\theta_{i}
is not kept in explicit form. It is recovered by taking the squared magnitude of the related amplitude entry

b_{t}(\theta_{i})=|\psi_{t}(i)|^{2},

(12)

where
|\psi_{t}(i)|
is the complex modulus of
\psi_{t}(i)
squaring the magnitude guarantees that probabilities stay at or above zero. This step from amplitudes to probabilities follows the Born rule of quantum probability theory, where observed likelihoods are computed from amplitude magnitudes instead of being stored outright.

The evolution of the amplitude based belief state is defined as

\psi_{t+1}=U_{t}\psi_{t},

(13)

where
U_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{HtimesH}
is a linear transformation matrix applied at time step
t
. The operator
U_{t}
models how new observations and defensive actions influence uncertainty over attacker intent. Conceptually,
U_{t}
encodes the impact of system observations
s_{t+1}
and mitigation decisions
a_{t}^{def}
on the defender’s internal belief structure. Because the update is linear in amplitude space, multiple hypotheses can interfere constructively or destructively before probabilities are extracted.

After applying the transformation
U_{t}
, normalization is required to ensure that the resulting amplitudes correspond to a valid probability distribution. This constraint is expressed as

\sum_{i=1}^{H}|\psi_{t+1}(i)|^{2}=1.

(14)

This normalization condition ensures that the sum of probability masses on all intent hypothesis after mapping from amplitude to probability is one.

The key difference between classical and amplitude based belief updates is that the uncertainty is manipulated. In the case of the classical version, probabilities are updated directly and may collapse rapidly under noisy observations. The quantum inspired formulation performs updates in amplitude space before squaring, which allows uncertainty to persist under ambiguous observations. Attacker intent hypotheses in this case co-exist in superposition until enough evidence has been accumulated to cause concentration of mass.

It is important to emphasize that this formulation is inspired by the mathematical structure of quantum probability theory but does not require quantum hardware, quantum entanglement, or physical quantum systems. All computations are performed on classical hardware. The inspiration for quantum probability theory is only used for representing and evolving the uncertainty in the defender’s belief state.

V-D

Damage and Reward Modeling

The instantaneous damage incurred at time
t
is defined as

D_{t}=d(\theta_{t},a_{t}^{def}),

(15)

where
\theta_{t}\in\Theta
denotes the true (but hidden) attacker intent at time
t
, and
a_{t}^{def}\in\mathcal{A}^{def}
represents the defensive action selected by the defender. The function

d:\Theta times\mathcal{A}^{def}\rightarrow\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid x\geq 0\}

is the damage function, which quantifies the severity of harm to the IoV system when a specific attacker intent interacts with a given defensive action. This formulation expresses the idea that the damage is not only determined by the attacker’s behavior but also by the success of the defender’s response. For example, an active attacker under insufficient isolation yields a high damage, while the same attack yields only low damage under a better defensive strategy.

Since
\theta_{t}
is unobserved to the defender,
D_{t}
cannot be evaluated at decision time. The defender evaluates the expectation of damage under its belief
b_{t}
. This expectation is defined as

\mathbb{E}[D_{t}]=\sum_{i=1}^{H}b_{t}(\theta_{i})d(\theta_{i},a_{t}^{def}),

(16)

where
b_{t}(\theta_{i})
denotes the belief probability assigned to intent
\theta_{i}
. This expression represents the weighted average of possible damage outcomes across all hypothesized attacker intents. Each possible damage value
d(\theta_{i},a_{t}^{def})
is weighted by the corresponding belief probability. This ensures that decision making is performed under uncertainty in a mathematically consistent manner.

In addition to attack-induced damage, each defensive action incurs an operational cost denoted by

C(a_{t}^{def}),

where
C:\mathcal{A}^{def}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is the cost function. The cost model accounts for trade-offs in latency, availability, computation or user convenience due to countermeasures. Stronger defensive actions may decrease damage but also increase operational cost.

The scalar reward used for reinforcement learning is defined as

r_{t}=-\hbox{$\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}D_{t}+C(a_{t}^{def})\hbox{$\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}.

(17)

The negative sign in the total damage plus cost rewards transforms the minimization target into a reward maximization problem as commonly adopted in reinforcement learning problems, where lower total damage plus cost leads to higher reward values. This reward structure encourages the defender to balance security effectiveness against operational efficiency.

V-E

Policy Representation and Optimization

The defender policy is parameterized by
\phi
and defined as

\pi_{\phi}:\mathcal{S}times\Delta^{H-1}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}^{def}.

(18)

This notation means the policy is a function from the observable system state-space
\mathcal{S}
and belief space
\Delta^{H-1}
to the defender action space
\mathcal{A}^{def}
. The parameter vector
\phi
represents the learnable parameters of the policy, typically implemented as a neural network. The policy depends on both
s_{t}
and
b_{t}
to incorporate current system conditions and uncertainty about attacker intent.

Action selection is performed according to

a_{t}^{def}\sim\pi_{\phi}(s_{t},b_{t}),

(19)

The symbol
\sim
indicates that the policy generates a probability distribution over actions. In settings where policies are stochastic,
\pi_{\phi}(s_{t},b_{t})
is the distribution for possible defensive moves. By sampling from this distribution, the system adds variety to its exploration as it learns.

To succeed the defender is responsible for reducing the expected total damage. To achieve this goal, the model calculates the sum of all future harms over a fixed period, applying a discount factor to each value. This objective is defined as

J(\phi)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma^{t-1}\hbox{$\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}D_{t}+C(a_{t}^{def})\hbox{$\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\right],

(20)

where
\gamma\in(0,1]
is the discount factor. The discount factor controls the relative importance of immediate versus future damage. When
\gamma
is close to 1, long-term consequences are emphasized. The expectation operator accounts for randomness arising from attacker behavior, stochastic policy sampling, and environment transitions.

Policy parameters are updated iteratively using gradient-based optimization. The parameter update rule is given by

\phi_{k+1}=\phi_{k}+\eta\nabla_{\phi}J(\phi_{k}),

(21)

where
\eta>0
denotes the learning rate,
\phi_{k}
represents the parameter vector at iteration
k
, and
\nabla_{\phi}J(\phi_{k})
is the gradient of the objective with respect to the policy parameters. In practice, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used to compute a stable approximation of this gradient, ensuring controlled policy updates and improved convergence properties under adversarial and non-stationary conditions.

V-F

Unified Sequential Defense Process

The complete methodology can now be understood as a unified sequential process that interconnects the system dynamics, belief evolution, damage modeling, and policy optimization components defined in the preceding subsections.

At time step
t
, the full latent system state is given by
x_{t}=(s_{t},\theta_{t})
, where
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
denotes the observable IoV system condition and
\theta_{t}\in\Theta
represents the hidden attacker intent. The defender does not observe
\theta_{t}
directly and instead maintains a belief state
b_{t}\in\Delta^{H-1}
(or equivalently an amplitude based state
\psi_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{H}
in the quantum inspired formulation).

The interaction at each time step proceeds as follows. First, the attacker selects an action
a_{t}^{att}\in\mathcal{A}^{att}
based on its hidden intent
\theta_{t}
. The defender selects a mitigation action
a_{t}^{def}\in\mathcal{A}^{def}
according to the parameterized policy

a_{t}^{def}\sim\pi_{\phi}(s_{t},b_{t}),

which conditions on both the observable state and the current belief over attacker intent.

Given the attacker and defender actions, the observable system state evolves according to

s_{t+1}=F(s_{t},a_{t}^{att},a_{t}^{def}),

while the attacker’s internal intent evolves according to

\theta_{t+1}=G(\theta_{t},a_{t}^{def}).

Together, these transitions define the joint system evolution

x_{t+1}=\hbox{$\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}F(s_{t},a_{t}^{att},a_{t}^{def}),\;G(\theta_{t},a_{t}^{def})\hbox{$\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$},

which captures both environmental dynamics and adversarial adaptation.

Since the updated attacker intent
\theta_{t+1}
remains hidden, the defender updates its belief using the newly observed state
s_{t+1}
. In the classical formulation, this update is given by

b_{t+1}=\mathcal{B}(b_{t},s_{t+1},a_{t}^{def}),

where
\mathcal{B}
denotes the Bayesian belief update operator. In the quantum inspired formulation, the update is performed in amplitude space via

\psi_{t+1}=U_{t}\psi_{t},

followed by probability extraction
b_{t+1}(\theta_{i})=|\psi_{t+1}(i)|^{2}
and normalization. Thus, belief evolution continuously refines the defender’s estimate of the hidden attacker intent.

Simultaneously, the system incurs instantaneous damage defined by

D_{t}=d(\theta_{t},a_{t}^{def}),

where
d:\Theta times\mathcal{A}^{def}\rightarrow\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid x\geq 0\}
quantifies the severity of harm resulting from the interaction between attacker intent and defensive action. Because
\theta_{t}
is not directly observable at decision time, the defender evaluates expected damage under belief:

\mathbb{E}[D_{t}]=\sum_{i=1}^{H}b_{t}(\theta_{i})d(\theta_{i},a_{t}^{def}).

Each defensive action additionally incurs operational cost
C(a_{t}^{def})
, modeling service degradation and resource expenditure. The reinforcement learning reward is defined as

r_{t}=-\hbox{$\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}D_{t}+C(a_{t}^{def})\hbox{$\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$},

so that minimizing cumulative damage and cost becomes equivalent to maximizing cumulative reward.

Over an episode of length
T
, the defender seeks to minimize the expected cumulative discounted damage:

J(\phi)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma^{t-1}\hbox{$\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}D_{t}+C(a_{t}^{def})\hbox{$\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\right],

where
\gamma
controls the trade-off between immediate and long-term impact. Policy parameters are updated iteratively using gradient ascent,

\phi_{k+1}=\phi_{k}+\eta\nabla_{\phi}J(\phi_{k}),

implemented in practice via Proximal Policy Optimization to ensure stable updates under adversarial and non-stationary dynamics.

In summary, the methodology is a closed loop system. In this process, an attacker’s goals determine how they act against a target. As the system moves and defenses react, those factors define how the visible states change. With updated beliefs, the system reduces uncertainty about hidden motives. There are signals for reinforcement that come from how much the system is harmed or what it costs to operate. To make better decisions about defense in the future, the policy needs to be improved. The only structural distinction between classical and quantum-inspired variants lies in the internal representation and evolution of the belief state. At the same time, all other components of the decision-making pipeline remain identical.

Algorithm

The initialization phase establishes the two parallel

the policy network
\pi_{\phi}

b

For the quantum variant, the belief is initialized as
a uniform amplitude vector
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{H}

rather than a probability vector, so that all intent
hypotheses start with equal amplitude and can evolve
through constructive and destructive interference before
probabilities are extracted.

In every episode, lines 15 through 19 are where the two versions differ. The classical defender uses a Bayesian update operator
\mathcal{B}
to change probabilities when it receives new evidence this method fails when the observations are deceptive. The quantum defender uses a linear transformation
U_{t}
within amplitude space. It then gets probabilities through the Born rule
b_{t+1}(\theta_{i})=|\psi_{t+1}(i)|^{2}

x_{t}=[s_{t}\,\|\,b_{t}]
, which ensures that the system bases every action on the state it can observe. With this approach, the policy also treats the current estimate of uncertainty as a primary input.

All other components of the loop environment
interaction, reward computation, trajectory storage,
and PPO update are identical between the classical
and quantum variants, which ensures that any observed
performance difference is attributable solely to the
belief update operator.

VI
Experiments and Discussion

This section presents the experimental design, evaluation metrics, comparative baselines, and explainability analysis framework used to assess the proposed quantum inspired reinforcement learning approach. In this version, the experiments are described to illustrate the evaluation protocol and analysis methodology. Quantitative empirical results and statistical performance comparisons are deferred to a subsequent revision.

VI-A

Simulation Description

To ground the risk estimation component in realistic vehicular communication behavior, we consider IoV intrusion datasets commonly adopted in prior studies, such as CICIoV-23 style traffic captures. These datasets contain labeled instances of benign and malicious vehicular network activities, including DOS, spoofing, replay, and fuzzing attacks
[
17
,
13
]
.

Classic intrusion detectors stop at a one shot accuracy score against those frozen tables. Our approach treats the tables as a calibration source, we read the counts and timing gaps then feed them into a running risk calculator that never stops. The calculator sits inside a simulated Internet-of-Vehicles playground whose rules, traffic density but also attacker arrival process are all set from the same numbers. Raw packet headers and payloads are first condensed into short weekly or hourly statistics mean rate, variance, unique address count, entropy on message type.
Those condensed numbers are next converted into a single risk level that updates every time unit as well as that the red and blue agents observe before they choose their next move.

Evaluation Metrics

VI-B
1
Cumulative Attack Damage

The primary performance metric is cumulative attack damage, defined as

D_{\mathrm{cum}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t},

(22)

where
d_{t}
denotes the effective damage incurred at time step
t
after defensive mitigation. This metric captures long-term system impact rather than instantaneous detection outcomes, aligning with the objectives of autonomous IoV defense.

VI-B
2
Damage Variance

To assess defensive stability and robustness under adaptive adversaries, we evaluate the variance of cumulative damage across episodes:

\mathrm{Var}(D_{\mathrm{cum}})=\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathrm{cum}}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathrm{cum}}]^{2}.

(23)

Lower variance indicates more predictable and reliable defensive behavior, which is critical for safety-critical IoV deployments.

VI-B
3
Conditional Attack Success Rate

The conditional ASR is defined as

\mathrm{ASR}(t)=\mathbb{P}(D_{t}\geq\theta\mid D_{t-1}<\theta),

(24)

where
\theta
is a predefined attack success threshold. Under effective adaptive defense, this conditional ASR is expected to decrease over time as mitigation actions accumulate and attacker capabilities are constrained.

VI-B
4
Survival Probability

System survival probability is defined as

S(t)=\mathbb{P}(D_{t}<\theta),

(25)

which provides a reliability-oriented perspective complementary to ASR by emphasizing time-to-failure rather than binary success.

Experimental Setup

A simulation-based IoV security environment is constructed to model
long-horizon sequential interactions between an adaptive attacker and
an autonomous defender over
T=200
time steps per episode across

N=600
training episodes. The environment maintains a hidden attacker
intent state
\theta_{t}\in\{\text{Benign, Probing, Attack, Evasion}\}

that evolves according to the adaptive transition function
G(\theta_{t},a_{t}^{\text{def}})
, ensuring non-stationary and adversarial dynamics
consistent with realistic IoV threat scenarios
[
1
,
2
]
. The attacker dynamically alternates between probing,
active attack, and evasion strategies in response to defensive pressure,
with forced strategy shifts every 50 steps to stress-test defensive
stability. The observable state
s_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{8}
consists of
aggregate traffic statistics, anomaly indicators, burst signals, and
risk-related features extracted from vehicular communications. The
defender is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization
(PPO)
[
14
]
with a two-layer MLP policy network
(hidden size 64, ReLU activations) conditioned on the concatenation
of
s_{t}
and the belief state
b_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
, yielding a
12-dimensional input. The system supports four defensive actions (monitor, alert, throttle and isolate). By performing an action, the operator incurs costs of 0.00, 0.05, 0.10, or 0.20. These values represent how security measures relate to the availability of a service. In a damage table
d(\theta,a^{\text{def}})
, each pair of a state and a defensive action is linked to a score that describes how much harm occurs. It ranges from 0.0, when a defense is present in a stable environment, to 0.9, when a system is under threat with no protection. As this table exists, it shows how expenses relate to safety, helping the model learn. The discount factor is set to
\gamma=0.95
, the learning
rate to
\eta=3times10^{-3}
, and PPO clipping to
\epsilon=0.2
.

The quantum belief is hypothesized to maintain stable policy inputs during transitions by preserving uncertainty in amplitude space, where the classical Bayesian belief is expected to collapse under the sudden shift in observation statistics, producing inconsistent defensive responses and high variance.

Comparative Analysis

Table

The comparison uses five measures as following average total damage, standard deviation, variance, attack success rate and survival probability. VI-E
1
Cumulative Damage and Variance

The PPO defender that uses quantum amplitude belief reaches an average total damage of 27.495. This value is 72.4% lower than the average total damage of 99.635 seen when no mitigation is applied and 60.4% lower than the 69.348 average total damage observed when the PPO defender uses the classical Bayesian belief. The switch from Bayesian belief update to amplitude based quantum belief state therefore yields a clear and steady reduction in average damage.

More critically, the damage variance of the proposed method is 3.636, compared to 37.054 for PPO with classical belief which is a reduction of approximately 10 times. The standard deviation is similarly reduced from 6.087 to 1.907, a 69% improvement. These findings directly support the primary hypothesis of this work: that quantum inspired belief representations improve the
stability and reliability
of autonomous IoV defense under adaptive adversaries, beyond any improvement in average performance. In safety-critical vehicular deployments, low variance is equally important as low mean damage, since an unpredictable defender can be probed and exploited by an adaptive attacker.

The Q-Learning baseline with classical Bayesian belief achieves a mean damage of 40.621 and variance of 18.236. Although it outperforms the PPO with classical belief on mean damage, its performance relative to the proposed method represents a 47.6% increase in mean damage and a 401% increase in variance, demonstrating that neither the tabular Q-learning algorithm nor the classical belief representation is sufficient to match the proposed approach. The Q-learning baseline also fails to leverage the representational capacity of a neural network policy, limiting its ability to generalize across the continuous observation space.

The random policy achieves a mean damage of 58.665 and variance of 25.609. The fact that random action selection outperforms PPO with classical belief (69.348) on mean damage is a notable finding. This indicates that the Bayesian belief model harms the neural network policy when an adaptive adversary acts. Classical belief collapses while the attacker evades, this means the policy gets false state data and chooses wrong actions with strong certainty. The quantum belief keeps uncertainty in the amplitude space when observations are unclear. It avoids this failure and lets the policy stay consistent in every phase of the attacker strategy.

Attack Success Rate and Survival Probability

The proposed method achieves an ASR of 0.000 across 80 test episodes, indicating that the defender successfully prevents all attack transitions from non-attack to attack states above the defined damage threshold
\theta
. The same ASR is achieved by the Q-Learning with classical belief baseline, though its inferior damage variance (18.236 vs 3.636) indicates that while both methods prevent threshold crossings on average, the quantum PPO does so far more consistently.

The PPO with classical belief achieves an ASR of 0.247 and survival probability of 0.683, meaning the system sustains damage above the threshold in nearly one quarter of all time steps following a sub-threshold period. The random policy similarly achieves ASR of 0.239 and survival of 0.739. Both significantly underperform the proposed method. The no-mitigation baseline records the highest ASR of 0.325 and the lowest survival probability of 0.388, confirming that active defense is necessary and that the quantum PPO provides the most reliable form of active defense evaluated.

Compared to the base paper of Guo et al.

While a direct numerical comparison is constrained by differences in simulation platform, attacker model, and evaluation duration, the proposed method demonstrates competitive or superior attack suppression performance while additionally providing a 10 times improvement in damage variance a stability metric that the MTD framework of Guo et al. does not report. This suggests that quantum inspired belief modeling offers a complementary and potentially superior approach to uncertainty management in IoV defense relative to game-theoretic moving target strategies.

Summarizing the Analysis

The proposed PPO defender with quantum amplitude belief achieves the best performance across all five reported metrics: lowest mean damage (27.495), lowest standard deviation (1.907), lowest variance (3.636), lowest ASR (0.000), and highest survival probability (1.000). No single baseline matches the proposed method on more than one metric simultaneously. These results collectively validate the central claim of this work: that non-classical uncertainty representations can improve both the average effectiveness and the reliability of autonomous IoV defense systems under adaptive and deceptive adversaries.

VI-G

Discussion

The experimental results demonstrate that the
quantum-inspired belief representation consistently
outperforms all evaluated baselines across every reported
metric. Amplitude-based belief updates preserve distributed
uncertainty during deceptive observations whereas classical
Bayesian belief collapses under attacker strategy
transitions. This collapse corrupts the policy input at
exactly the moments when reliable reasoning is most needed.
A notable finding is that the random policy outperforms
PPO with classical belief on mean damage, revealing that
classical Bayesian belief actively harms the neural network
policy under adaptive adversaries. The quantum belief avoids
this failure by maintaining uncertainty in amplitude space,
directly accounting for the
1.52\times
reduction in
damage variance and the reduction in attack success rate
from 0.247 to 0.000. Moreover, Post-hoc explainability via SHAP, LIME, and Grad-CAM independently confirms that quantum belief
features are the primary decision drivers.

Despite these
results, key limitations remain. All experiments are
conducted in a controlled simulation environment that does
not fully capture real vehicular deployments. The attacker
model assumes a fixed set of four intent states whereas
real adversaries may exhibit more complex behavioral
repertoires. The amplitude transformation operator
U_{t}

is fixed rather than learned, which may limit belief
expressiveness under highly non-stationary conditions.

VII
Conclusion

The Internet of Vehicles (IoV) connects vehicles, roadside units, and cloud services to enable autonomous transportation. It increases the attack surface, enabling attackers to probe defences and launch targeted attacks against IoV systems. These attacks modify their strategies while deliberately complicating the behavior. It leads to a collapse of classical probabilistic belief models, resulting in overconfident and unstable defensive decisions. To address these dynamic intrusions, this work introduces a quantum-inspired belief representation. The model encodes defender uncertainty about the hidden attacker’s intent using complex-valued state vectors. A transformation in the amplitude space is used to update these vectors and extract probabilities. It preserves uncertainty under ambiguous observations and integrates this belief state into a proximal policy optimization defender. Experiments are conducted in a CICIoV-23-calibrated IoV security environment over 600 training and 80 test episodes, each with T = 200 time steps. Results show a 60.3% reduction in mean cumulative damage and a 10.2 times reduction in damage variance compared to PPO with classical Bayesian belief. The attack success rate decreases by 0.247, while survival probability improves by 0.317. Moreover, SHAP, LIME, and Grad-CAM are used for model interpretation and validity. In our future work, learned unitary-like transformations will be used in a multi-agent distribution-defender setting, and formal verification of policy safety properties will be performed on resource-constrained on-board units.
