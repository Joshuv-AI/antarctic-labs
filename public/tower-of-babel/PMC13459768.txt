Comparison of biomass and antioxidant phenolic compound of kale under supplemental UV-A and deep-blue LED lights
Authors: Da-Seul Choi, Myung-Min Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of ultraviolet-A (UV-A) and deep-blue light-emitting diodes (LEDs) on the growth and antioxidant phenolic content of kale (Brassica oleracea var. acephala) and to explore potential boundary effects between UV-A and visible light. Five-week-old kale seedlings cultivated in a vertical farm module were exposed to supplemental UV-A (peak wavelength at 365, 385, and 395 nm) and deep-blue (peak wavelength at 405, 415, 430, and 440 nm) light at an intensity of 30 W m⁻² for 7 days.ResultsThe supplemental light treatments enhanced the biomass, showing the most significant improvement at 415 nm. During the light period, photosynthetic rate peaked at 415 nm, and carbon dioxide exchange was observed during the background-light-off period, with higher rates under deep-blue light. The maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II (Fv/Fm) remained above 0.8 under deep-blue treatments but decreased under UV-A treatments. Hydrogen peroxide levels increased significantly across treatments from days 2 to 7. Except for the 365 nm treatment, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase enzyme activity also increased. Total phenolic content and antioxidant capacity significantly increased in the 395, 405, 415, 430, and 440 nm treatments, with values in the 415 nm treatment being 2-fold higher compared to the control.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that deep-blue light, particularly at 415 nm, may enhance biomass and secondary metabolite accumulation in kale. The results also indicate a potential functional transition region between UV-A and deep-blue light around 400 nm.Keywords: Bioactive compounds, Deep-blue light, Growth, Kale, Photosynthetic rate, UV-A light

Background

It has been established that irradiation of plants with ultraviolet -A (UV-A) light (315–400 nm) can induce photomorphogenesis, leading to inhibited hypocotyl elongation and enhanced leaf thickness [1–3]. Recent studies have reported that providing plants with supplemental UV-A radiation can also promote increases in shoot and root biomass [4–7], which is assumed to be attributable to the fact that the UV-A wavelengths in the 400-nm region have high photosynthetic efficiency [8]. However, it has been reported that UV-A does not have any pronounced effects on photosynthesis [9, 10], and generally there is still limited information regarding the growth and photosynthesis of plants exposed to UV-A. It is, nevertheless, well established that visible light in blue wavelength range (400–500 nm) plays roles in regulating photosynthetic-related processes, including stomatal opening and closure [11, 12] and the induction of chlorophyll formation and development [13, 14]. Accordingly, along with red light (600–700 nm), blue light contributes to photosynthetic efficiency and is deemed essential for promoting plant growth [15–17].

To detect light signals, plants employ various photoreceptor proteins, such as phytochromes, cryptochromes, phototropins, ZEITLUPE (ZTL), and UV RESISTANCE LOCUS 8 (UVR8) [17]. Among these, cryptochromes and phototropins are used to detect UV-A and blue light (detected the wavelengths of 370 and 450 nm, respectively). Blue light stimulates cryptochromes and increases the content of flavonoids by enhancing the expression of the chalcone synthase (CHS) gene [18]. Phototropins (particularly phototropin 1) have also been reported to accumulate cryptochrome-mediated anthocyanins in response to illumination with blue light [19]. Additionally, studies that have investigated changes in bioactive compound levels in vegetables exposed to light of different quality have reported that blue light is also effective in increasing the contents of secondary metabolites in plants [20–23]. Furthermore, it has been found that the detection of UV-A light by cryptochromes promotes flavonoid synthesis via its effects on ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 5 (HY5) activity [24]. Similarly, previous studies have reported that UV-A light increases flavonoid and anthocyanin contents [21, 25, 26] and activates the expression of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) and CHS genes involved in their biosynthesis [27, 28]. These findings and those of other studies thus indicate that, in general, UV-A and blue light induce similar plant reactions with respect to the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites via the activation of shared photoreceptors.

Although UV-A and blue light have optically different wavelength ranges, recent studies have indicated that they have comparable effects in terms of promoting growth by stimulating the synthesis of primary metabolites and contribute to the induction of secondary metabolite synthesis [29, 30]. However, there might be a clear functional difference between UV-A and blue light regarding the allocation of photosynthetic products when plants are irradiated with the same energy intensity of UV-A or blue light. For example, given that UV-A light has a higher energy per unit photon than blue light, it can represent a relatively strong source of stress on plants and stimulate the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [27]. Under such circumstances, plants allocate more carbon resources toward secondary metabolism to mitigate ROS damage and counteract the stress induced by UV-A light [31]. In contrast, blue light is known to promote primary metabolism rather than secondary metabolism in plants with less stress response than UV-A light. In previous studies, providing kale plants with various UV-A light-emitting diode (LED) lights (peak wavelength at 365, 375, 385, 395, and 405 nm) resulted in increased growth and phenolic compound accumulation as the wavelength increased. Particularly, the most significant effects were observed at the boundary between UV-A and deep-blue light, at 405 nm [4]. To date, however, relatively few studies have directly compared the changes in plant reactions in response to exposure to different wavelengths in the UV-A and blue light regions.

In this study, we aimed to compare and analyze the effects of supplemental UV-A and deep-blue (near UV-A) radiation on the changes in biomass and phenolic compound content in kale. We hypothesized that UV-A light would elicit a stronger stress response compared to deep-blue light, leading to differential impacts on metabolite production. Additionally, we sought to explore the potential presence of a functional boundary between UV-A and blue light spectra. By clarifying these differences, this research will improve our understanding of how various light wavelengths influence plant physiology and help optimize lighting strategies for better plant growth and the accumulation of bioactive compounds in plants.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Seeds of kale (Brassica oleracea var. acephala, Asia Seed Co., Ltd., Seoul, Republic of Korea) were sown in seed germination packs (CYGTM, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) and grown in a vertical farm module (24 m3) with artificial lighting for 14 days. The following environmental conditions were maintained for 2 weeks: LED lighting systems [(red: white: blue LED = 8:1:1) (Bi-Won Tech Co., Ltd, Cheonan, Korea)], photoperiod of 12-h light/12-h dark, and photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of 150 ± 5 µmol m− 2 s− 1, CO2 concentration of 500 µmol mol− 1, air temperature of 20 °C, relative humidity of 60%. Two-week-old kale seedlings were transplanted into a deep flow technique (DFT) system and cultivated for 3 weeks. The nutrient solution [32] at pH 5.8-6.0 with an electrical conductivity (EC) of 1.0 dS m− 1 was provided and renewed every two weeks.

UV-A and deep-blue light treatments

After 3 weeks of transplanting, kale plants were continuously provided supplemental irradiation with three types of UV-A LEDs (peak wavelength at 365, 385, and 395 nm; Seoul Semiconductor Co., Ltd, Ansan, Korea) or four types of deep-blue LEDs (peak wavelength at 405 and 415 nm, Seoul Semiconductor Co., Ltd, Ansan, Korea; and 430 and 440 nm, ES LEDs Co., Ltd, Seoul, Korea) for 7 days (Fig. 1B). During this treatment period, the background light (red: white: blue LED = 8:1:1) was operated under a 12-h light/12-h off photoperiod, while the supplemental UV-A and deep-blue LEDs were applied continuously (24 h) (Fig. 1A). The control plants were cultivated under the background light without supplementary UV-A or deep-blue LEDs. The lighting for all treatments was maintained at an intensity of 30 W m⁻² through a DC controller (LPS-503DU; NDE, Incheon, Korea). The corresponding photon flux density (PFD) at the plant canopy level was 97.7, 102.2, and 110.8 µmol m− 2 s− 1 for UV-A LEDs (365, 385, and 395 nm, respectively), and 116.5, 121.6, 120.0, and 124.0 µmol m− 2 s− 1 for deep-blue LEDs (405, 415, 430, and 440 nm, respectively), as measured using a spectroradiometer (JAZ-EL 200; Ocean Optics, Dunedin, FL, USA) at the plant canopy level (30 cm below the light sources). Thirty kale plants were used for each treatment in the experiment (a total of 240 plants), and the plants were systematically rotated daily to minimize errors due to uneven light distribution.

The background and supplemental LEDs installed in a vertical farm module (A) and UV-A LEDs with peak wavelength at 365, 385, and 395 nm and deep-blue LEDs with peak wavelength at 405, 415, 430, and 440 nm (B). Light intensity for all lighting sources was measured at the plant canopy level (30 cm below the light sources)

Shoot biomass

To investigate the changes in shoot dry weight, kale plants were harvested after 2, 4, and 7 days of treatment. To measure dry weights, shoots of kale were then dried in a freeze dryer at -70 °C for 72 h. Subsequently, dry weights were measured using an electronic scale (SI-234; Denver Instruments, NY, USA). To investigate the growth rate of kale, relative growth rate (increase in dry matter per day) was calculated for the two periods between treatment days 2 and 4 and 4 and 7, respectively.

Photosynthetic characteristics

Photosynthetic parameters were measured from the fourth fully developed leaf using a LI-6400XT photosynthesis analyzer (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA) along with a transparent leaf chamber (6400-02B LED Light Source; LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA) after 4 days of treatment. During the light period, defined as when the background lighting was on, the photosynthetic rate was measured at 130 µmol m-2 s-1 PPFD within 4 h of turning on background lighting, and at the same point within 4 h of the background-light-off period, defined as when the background lighting was off (while supplemental UV-A and deep-blue LEDs remained on). The incident PPFD (130 µmol m-2 s-1) was set based on the in-chamber PAR (400–700 nm) reading (ParIn) of the LI-6400XT. It should be noted that radiation below 400 nm is not captured by the LI-6400XT PAR sensor; therefore, the displayed in-chamber PPFD represents a PAR-based reference value (400–700 nm) and does not quantify UV-A photon input. The conditions in the chamber wherein photosynthesis was measured were as follows: 20 °C of air temp., 60% RH; 500 µmol mol⁻¹ of reference CO2, 400 µmol s⁻¹ airflow. The electron transport rate (ETR) was measured using an image chlorophyll fluorescence meter (FC800-C; PSI, Brno, Czech Republic) at the same time as photosynthesis and calculated using the equation ETR = Y(II) × PAR × 0.8 × 0.5, implemented in FLUORCAM 7 software, in which UV-A radiation was not included in the estimation. In this equation, Y(II) is the quantum yield of photosystem II; PAR is the photosynthetically active radiation (i.e., the amount of light); 0.8 is the general light absorption rate of higher plants; and 0.5 is the light absorbed in photosystem II.

Maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II photochemistry (Fv/Fm)

The maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II (Fv/Fm) of kale plants was measured using closed-image chlorophyll fluorescence meter (FC800-C; PSI) for 7 days of treatment. The measurement conditions were referred to previous studies [4]. Prior to measuring Fv/Fm, kale plants were subjected to a 30-minute period of dark adaptation. The maximum (Fm) and minimum (Fo) fluorescence values of the dark-adapted plants were measured and used to calculate Fv/Fm. The Fv/Fm ratios of the whole plant were obtained as images using FLUORCAM 7 software.

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) content

Kale leaves (approximately 500 mg) were preserved in deep freezer at -70 °C (Nf-300sf; Nihon Freezer Co., Ltd., Japan) after 2, 4, and 7 days of treatment. Samples were ground in a pestle and mortar with liquid nitrogen and then extracted with 5 mL of 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5). The extracted solution was centrifuged at 18,000 × g at -4 °C for 20 min and then filtered using a 0.22-µL syringe filter (BS20-NY25; Biofact Co., Ltd., Korea). We conducted H2O2 determinations using EZ-Hydrogen peroxide assay kit (Oxidative Stress Assay kit; DoGenBio Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea). Adding 50 µL of the filtered sample and 50 µL of reaction solution to a 96-well plate, the mixture solution was subjected to a 30-minute reaction in darkness. Thereafter, H2O2 contents were read at a wavelength of 560 nm with an ELISA multi-reader (Epoch; BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). Contents were determined using a standard curve established 100 µM H2O2 included in the kit.

Total phenolic contents and antioxidant capacity

After 2, 4, and 7 days of treatment, approximately 40 mg of dried powder was ultrasonically with 4 mL 80% (v/v) acetone using an ultrasonic sonicator (SK5210HP, KUDOS, Shanghai, China) for 15 min. The obtained extracts were stored at either 4 °C or -20 °C for at least 12 h before conducting analyses on total phenolic content and antioxidant capacity. The extracted sample was centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 2 min. The supernatant was analyzed as described by Lee et al. [27]. Total phenolic contents were quantified as gallic acid equivalent per shoot dry weight (mg GAE·shoot− 1 DW), while antioxidant capacity was quantified as trolox-equivalent antioxidant capacity per shoot dry weight (mM TEAC·shoot− 1 DW), using 6-Hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethylchromane-2-carboxyl acid (Sigma-Aldrich Co., LLC, St. Louis, MO, USA) as a standard.

Levels of PAL transcription and PAL activity

To compare the transcript levels of essential genes in the phenolic compound biosynthetic pathway, we amplified genes of interest using oligonucleotide primers sourced from the GenBank database (Table 1). Approximately 200 mg of kale leaves were collected for RNA extraction after 2, 4, and 7 days of treatment, and stored at -70 °C until used for analysis. Total RNA was extracted with a RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (74904; QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) and Tissuelyser (40714; QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). The isolated RNA concentration was assessed with a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, USA). RNA quantified at 100 ng·uL− 1 was employed for cDNA synthesis using a QuantiTect reverse transcription kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Quantitative real-time PCR for all genes utilized the Quantispeed SYBR Kit (PhileKorea, Daejeon, Korea) and a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA). The PAL gene amplification followed the conditions described by Lee et al. [27].

To determine PAL enzyme activity, we employed the method described by Boo et al. [33]. Leaf tissues (approximately 500 mg) were collected and stored at -70 °C and later ground in liquid nitrogen. The ground leaf material was then extracted with 10 mL of 25 mM borate buffer (pH 8.8) and 2 mL of 3 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). After centrifuged at 900 × g for 20 min, the supernatant (0.5 mL) was mixed with 2.5 mL each of 10 mM borate buffer (pH 8.8) and 10 mM 1-phenylalanine (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Following heating at 40 °C for 2 h, the reaction was stopped by adding of 10 µL of 5 N HCl. The absorbance at 290 nm was determined using a UV-1800 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), and the enzyme activities were quantified as trans-cinnamic acid (mM) per hour (h) per shoot weight (FW g).

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the SAS statistical program (Statistical Analysis System, Version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for each parameter using a one-way model with five replicates, except for photosynthetic parameters and PAL gene expression, which used four replicates. Differences among supplemental radiation treatments were assessed using Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) test. The experimental data were presented as mean ± standard error (SE), and significant differences were considered at p < 0.05. Pearson correlation analysis evaluated parameter associations, and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using R statistical software (Version 4.0.3). Graphs were prepared using the GraphPad Prism program (Version 8.0; GraphPad Inc., Boston, MA, USA) to represent the data visually.

Results

Shoot biomass

During the period of treatment from day 2 to 4, we found that the relative growth rate of shoot dry weight of plants subjected to UV-A and deep-blue illumination increased with a lengthening of wavelengths from 365 nm to 440 nm (Fig. 2A). A similar trend was observed during the period from day 4 to 7, with the highest value observed in plants treated with deep-blue LED at a peak wavelength of 415 nm. After 7 days of exposure to supplementary LED illumination, the shoot dry weight of plants under the 395, 405, 415, 430, and 440 nm treatments was 1.63-, 1.97-, 2.48-, 2.11-, and 1.99-fold higher, respectively, than that of control plants (Fig. 2B). The deep-blue LED treatment at 415 nm outperformed the other wavelengths, highlighting its superior efficacy in promoting shoot biomass.

Different letters above bars indicate significant differences at p < 0.05 (n = 5)

Photosynthetic parameters

After 4 days of UV-A and deep-blue light treatments, we found that the photosynthetic rate during the light period increased with longer wavelengths (Fig. 3A). While no significant differences were noted among the UV-A treatments, plants exposed to deep-blue light at 405 and 415 nm showed 1.51-fold and 1.75-fold higher photosynthetic rates than control plants, respectively. During the background-light-off period, compared with control plants, those in all UV-A and deep-blue treatments showed higher rates of carbon dioxide absorption to facilitate photosynthesis, and plants exposed to deep-blue illumination showed higher values than those exposed to UV-A (Fig. 3B).

Photosynthetic rate, transpiration rate, and Electron Transport Rate (ETR) during the light period (A, C, and E) and during the background-light-off period (B, D and F) for kale plants illuminated with different UV-A and deep-blue LED lights for 4 days. Different letters above bars indicate significant difference at p < 0.05 (n = 4)

During the light period of all treatments, no significant differences were detected with respect to the rate of transpiration (Fig. 3C), whereas, with the exception of plants exposed to 415 nm illumination, plants exposed to deep-blue LEDs during the background-light-off period showed significantly higher transpiration rates compared with the control (Fig. 3D).

After 4 days of treatment, we recorded an increase in the rate of electron transfer (ETR) with increasing PPFD during both the light and background-light-off periods (Fig. 3E and F). During the light period, the ETR was found to be lowest values in plants treated with 365 nm, while reaching the highest levels at 405 and 415 nm under light conditions of 700 µmol m− 2 s− 1 or higher. Nevertheless, no statistically significant differences were observed among the treatments. During the background-light-off period, deep-blue light treatments exhibited significantly higher ETR values compared to the control, while UV-A treatments did not show significant differences. The 365 and 385 nm peak wavelengths of the UV-A treatments recorded the lowest ETR values.

Fv/Fm and H2O2

Over the 7-day treatment period, the Fv/Fm value of control plants was maintained at approximately 0.81 or higher. For UV-A and deep-blue treatments, Fv/Fm values tended to be lower as the peak wavelength of treatments shortened. Specifically, when plants subjected to deep-blue LED treatments, Fv/Fm values showed a slight decrease on the initial day (approximately 0.78), whereas from days 2 to 7, values remained above 0.8 (Fig. 4A). Contrastingly, after the first day of exposure to UV-A LEDs, plants were characterized by Fv/Fm values of less than 0.8, and thereafter, plants exposed to the shorter wavelengths showed a more rapid decline in Fv/Fm. Among the UV-A treatments, plants exposed to 365-nm LEDs maintained a low Fv/Fm value of less than 0.75 from day 4 to 7 (Fig. 4C).

Maximum quantum yield (Fv/Fm) (A), H2O2 contents (B), and chlorophyll fluorescence imaging of maximum quantum yield (Fv/Fm) (C) of kale plants exposed to UV-A and deep-blue LED lights for 7 days. Different letters above bars indicate significant differences at p < 0.05 (n = 5)

After 2 days of LED treatment, we noted increases in the H2O2 contents of plants exposed to both UV-A and deep-blue lights compared with control plants, although the differences among all treatments were non-significant (Fig. 4B). After 4 days of treatment, the H2O2 content in plants subjected to the 365 nm treatment was significantly higher than that in the control plants. After 7 days, H₂O₂ levels increased across most treatments compared to the control, with UV-A at 385 nm exhibiting significantly higher levels than the control.

Total phenolic and anthocyanin contents

After 4 days of treatment, we found that the total phenolic contents per shoot dry weight of plants irradiated with deep-blue light were significantly higher than those in control plants (Fig. 5A). Among the UV-A treatments, increasing trends in phenolic contents were detected in plants exposed to wavelengths of 385 and 395 nm. However, these values were lower than those observed with deep-blue treatments. Similar trends in total phenolic contents were observed after 7 days. Plants exposed to UV-A LEDs showed a 1.19- to 1.57-fold increase in total phenolic contents compared to control plants. In contrast, among plants receiving supplementary illumination with deep-blue LEDs, we detected 1.54- to 2.28-fold increases in total phenolic contents compared with control plants, with kale exposed to the 415 nm treatment being characterized by the highest values.

Total phenolic contents (A) and antioxidant capacity (B) per shoot dry weight of kale plants exposed to different UV-A and deep-blue LED lights for 7 days. Different letters above bars indicate significant differences at p < 0.05 (n = 5)

Changes in antioxidant capacity per shoot dry weight showed a trend similar to that observed for total phenolic contents (Fig. 5B), with deep-blue treatments generally showing higher values than UV-A treatments. After 4 days of treatment, kale plants subjected to peak wavelengths between 395 and 440 nm showed at least 1.72-fold higher antioxidant levels than control plants. At the end of the 7-day treatments, the antioxidant capacity recorded in 395 nm-treated plants was found to be 1.8-fold or higher compared with that of control plants. Among the plants illuminated with deep-blue LEDs, those exposed to 405, 415, 430, and 440 nm were found to have significantly higher antioxidant capacities compared with the control plants, which were at least 2.30-, 2.87-, 2.57-, and 2.22-fold higher, respectively.

Levels of PAL gene expression and PAL activity

Over the 7-day treatment period, we detected time-dependent changes in the levels of PAL mRNA expression in UV-A and deep-blue LED illuminated plants compared with the control plants (Fig. 6A). After 2 days of treatment, levels of PAL gene expression were significantly higher in plants exposed to 405, 415, and 430 nm light compared with the control plants, while no significant differences were noted in UV-A-treated plants. After 4 days, only plants exposed to 440 nm supplemental light showed higher transcript levels than the control. In contrast, considerable reductions in expression levels were detected in plants exposed to 385, 395, 405, and 415 nm wavelengths after 7 days.

Levels of Phenylalanine Ammonia-Lyase (PAL) transcription (n = 4) (A) and PAL enzyme activity (n = 5) (B) in kale plants exposed to different UV-A and deep-blue LED lights for 7 days. Different letters above bars indicate significant differences at p < 0.05

With respect to PAL enzyme activity during the 7 days of UV-A and deep-blue light treatments, we found that after 2 days, activity was lowest in plants exposed to UV-A at 365 nm (Fig. 6B), whereas no significant differences were detected among the other treatments. After 4 days, the PAL activity of plants exposed to wavelengths between 395 and 440 nm was at least 1.55-fold higher than that of control plants, whereas after 7 days, we noted increased activity in those plants exposed to longer wavelengths. In general, plants irradiated with deep-blue light showed higher levels of activity compared with the UV-A-treated plants. The highest values were recorded in plants exposed to deep-blue light at 430 nm. The value was 1.69-fold higher than the activity levels observed in control plants.

Principal component and correlation analysis

PCA was carried out to analyze the response of kale to UV-A and deep-blue lights (Fig. 7B). Component 1 accounted for 51.1% of the variation and was mainly influenced by variables related to growth and secondary metabolites such as shoot dry weight, total phenolic content, AOC (antioxidant capacity), and PAL (phenylalanine ammonia-lyase). Component 2 explained 17.79% of the variation and was associated with variables related to ROS, Fv/Fm, and PR (photosynthetic rate). Strong positive correlations were observed among shoot dry weight, total phenolic content, AOC, and PAL, whereas strong negative correlations were observed among Fv/Fm, PR, and ROS. The UV-A and deep-blue treatments were distinct from the control group (in terms of component 1 and 2), and certain UV-A wavelengths (365 nm and 385 nm) were differentiated from the deep-blue treatment group (in terms of component 1) (Fig. 7A). Pearson’s correlation analysis showed that biomass, phenolic content, antioxidant capacity, and PAL activity were positively correlated with each other (Table 2). In contrast, Fv/Fm showed weaker and less consistent associations with these variables. These patterns were consistent with the PCA results.

Discussion

Effect of supplemental UV-A and deep-blue LED illumination on plant biomass

In this study, we demonstrated that supplemental irradiation using UV-A and deep-blue LEDs had positive effects on the growth of kale plants. With respect to UV-A LED treatment, increasing wavelength from 365 to 395 nm increased relative growth rate during both the initial and final 3 days of treatment (Fig. 2A). These observations are consistent with our previous study, in which UV-A at 385 and 395 nm (30 W m− 2) increased the shoot dry weight of ice plants compared with 365 and 375 nm [34]. Similarly, UV-A LED irradiation at 390 nm (12.4 µmol m− 2 s− 1) has been shown to promote basil growth [35]. These results may be explained by the relatively higher photosynthetic light utilization efficiency at UV-A wavelengths close to 400 nm [4, 8, 27]. Consistently, during the light period, photosynthetic rate increased under 395 nm (Fig. 3A). The low electron transport rate under 365 nm (Figs. 3E and F) may be due to reduced electron receptors in the photosystem II pathway caused by light-induced inhibition [36]. During the background-light-off period, when only UV-A LEDs were turned on, there was an increase in gas exchange in all UV-A treatments compared with the control (Fig. 3B).

Although blue light has been reported to affect photosynthetic function, including stomatal opening and conductance, it does not appear to have direct effects on plant biomass [37, 38]. Nevertheless, it has been demonstrated that blue light can have synergistic effects on plant biomass when simultaneously irradiated with red light [16, 39]. Accordingly, in this study, both the control treatment under background light (red: white: blue = 8:1:1, R8W1B1) and supplemental deep-blue LED treatments improved kale growth (Fig. 2A). Among the four deep-blue wavelengths tested, 415 nm was more effective in promoting photosynthesis and growth than 430 and 440 nm (Figs. 2A and 3A and B). This difference would appear to be related to the qualitative characteristics of blue light. In this regard, Zhang et al. [40] reported that when the blue light fraction of light quality is satisfied, there is little effect on single leaf net photosynthesis. In this study, 430 and 440 nm treatments provided higher photon flux than 415 nm to match 30 W m− 2. Consequently, additional blue light at 430 and 440 nm may not have further enhanced photosynthesis beyond the background light, consistent with the similar photosynthetic rates observed among deep-blue treatments during the background-light-off period (Fig. 3B). Similar to the findings obtained in this study, Lee et al. [41] reported that the growth of green perilla was promoted at wavelengths shorter than 450 nm. Nguyen and Oh [21] also showed that LED irradiation at 415 nm (25 W m− 2) enhanced growth in red perilla. In contrast, Mao et al. [20] reported that pak choi growth increased at longer wavelengths (400, 430, and 460 nm) under supplemental lighting (100 µmol m− 2 s− 1). These contrasting results suggest that the effects of supplemental blue light may vary depending on plant species or genotype, as well as light conditions such as wavelength and intensity [24].

Effect of supplemental UV-A and deep-blue LED illumination on bioactive compounds

Both blue and UV radiation have been established to promote the production of bioactive compounds in plants [22]. In the present study, UV-A and deep-blue LED treatments increased the total phenolic contents and antioxidant capacity of kale plants (Fig. 5). Illumination at 395 nm significantly increased total phenolic contents and antioxidant capacity per plant (shoot) (Fig. 5A). Total phenolic contents per unit dry weight showed a similar trend, whereas antioxidant capacity per unit dry weight was higher in the UV-A group than in the control and deep-blue groups at day 2 and remained comparable to the deep-blue treatment at day 7 (data not shown). UV-A is absorbed by photoreceptors such as cryptochromes and phototropins, which stimulate phenolic biosynthesis, including flavonoids [18, 20, 24]. In addition, UV-A can act as an abiotic stress factor, promoting secondary metabolite accumulation through ROS generation and oxidative stress [27, 42]. The ROS thus generated can function as signaling molecules that stimulate the activation of defense mechanisms in plants [43–45]. In this study, H2O2 levels increased in plants exposed to UV-A at 365, 385, and 395 nm (Fig. 4B), with the 365 nm treatment showing the highest and significantly elevated levels at day 4, suggesting a role in inducing antioxidant secondary metabolites. Notably, plants exposed to 365 nm UV-A showed the highest H2O2 levels at day 4, while exhibiting phenolic contents similar to the control, likely due to antioxidant utilization for ROS scavenging. Consistently, Fv/Fm declined progressively under UV-A treatments, particularly at shorter wavelengths, indicating elevated physiological stress (Fig. 4A and C) [27, 44, 46]. Under such conditions, plants may accumulate phenolic compounds that act as UV-absorbing protective compounds [47, 48]. However, despite these stress responses, UV-A irradiation at 30 W m− 2 did not cause visible damage or growth inhibition, indicating that it functioned as a mild stress factor, consistent with previous reports [4].

Compared with the UV-A-treated plants, significantly higher total phenolic contents and antioxidant capacity were observed in plants exposed to deep-blue light for 7 days, with the highest values at 415 nm (Fig. 5). Blue light wavelengths near the ultraviolet region have been reported to induce phenolic biosynthesis by generating signals similar to those induced by UV light [49, 50]. Fv/Fm values in deep-blue-treated plants were similar to those of control plants (Fig. 4A), although ROS contents were significantly elevated (Fig. 4C). The increase in H₂O₂ under deep-blue irradiation is likely attributable to cryptochrome activity. Previous studies have shown that blue light-induced cryptochrome activation stimulates ROS production (H2O2 and O2•⁻) in Arabidopsis thaliana, with ROS accumulating in the nucleus and regulating stress-related gene expression [51]. Consequently, enhanced phenolic synthesis under deep-blue light may be attributed to photoreceptor-mediated ROS signaling, similar to UV-induced responses. Given the higher relative growth rate under deep-blue LED treatments (Fig. 2A), the increases in phenolic content and antioxidant capacity may reflect enhanced allocation of photosynthesis-derived assimilates to both primary and secondary metabolites [52], with 415 nm showing the most pronounced effects on bioactive compound synthesis (Figs. 3 and 5). However, previous studies have reported that phenolic content was highest at 430 nm in Chinese kale and pak choi under different light conditions [20, 53]. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in background light spectra affecting the responses to supplemental deep-blue light. Our results indicate that light wavelength plays an important role in regulating photosynthetic performance and secondary metabolite accumulation in kale under controlled environment conditions. In addition, continuous exposure to UV-A and deep-blue light during background-light-off period may have partially contributed to the observed increases in gas exchange, biomass, and phenolic compound accumulation. Therefore, the responses observed in this study are likely the result of combined effects of wavelength and continuous light exposure, rather than solely wavelength-specific responses.

Both UV-A and blue light regulate the expression of genes such as PAL, a key enzyme in phenolic biosynthesis, as well as CHS and flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H), which are involved in flavonoid and anthocyanin synthesis [18, 27]. These regulatory effects are facilitated through UV-A/blue light photoreceptors, such as cryptochromes and phototropins [18, 54]. The activity of these enzymes contributes to the synthesis of UV-absorbing phenolic compounds that protect plant tissues exposed to UV radiation [27, 55]. UV-A and deep-blue light increased PAL gene expression and enzyme activity in a wavelength-dependent manner, with deep-blue wavelengths showing stronger effects than UV-A, consistent with previous studies [4]. The increase in PAL activity closely paralleled the trends in phenolic content and antioxidant capacity (Figs. 6 and 7), supporting the activation of phenolic biosynthesis by both light types, with enhanced photosynthesis likely contributing to increased secondary metabolite production. This metabolic coupling is corroborated by the strong positive correlation observed between primary and secondary metabolites (Table 2). However, as only PAL gene expression and its enzyme activity were examined in this study, further investigation is needed to clarify the underlying molecular mechanisms of UV-A and deep-blue regulation of secondary metabolite biosynthesis.

Figure 8 illustrates the differential effects of UV-A and deep-blue wavelengths on plant growth, photosynthetic performance, and secondary metabolism, as well as the transition region between these light ranges. Overall, both primary and secondary metabolic responses exhibited a tendency to saturate as the wavelength increased from UV-A (365 nm) to deep-blue (440 nm). Notably, this saturation generally occurred near 415 nm, suggesting that deep-blue light can effectively replace UV-A light as a supplementary light source.

Conclusion

Supplemental radiation of UV-A and deep-blue LEDs for 7 days enhanced kale growth and antioxidant phenolic compound production, with stronger effects observed under deep-blue light. UV-A light induced greater physiological stress than deep-blue light, supporting our initial hypothesis. Wavelength-dependent differences were observed, with longer wavelengths generally promoting biomass, antioxidant capacity, and PAL activity. The 415 nm treatment showed the most consistent positive effects on both photosynthesis and bioactive compound accumulation, suggesting a potential functional transition region between UV-A and deep-blue wavelengths around 400 nm. This result suggests that supplementary lighting with deep-blue light around 415 nm has the potential to significantly enhance crop productivity and quality in controlled environment agriculture (CEA). Because it is used as a supplementary rather than a primary light source, applying it as a short-term, pre-harvest treatment can achieve these goals while minimizing energy consumption. Consequently, it is expected to be applied in commercial settings in the near future.
