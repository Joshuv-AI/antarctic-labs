Symptom Burden After Dialysis Initiation and Its Association With Hospitalization
Authors: Devika Nair, Xuan Cai, Ron D Hays, Olalekan L Aiyegbusi, Rebecca S Frazier, Tamara Isakova, John D Peipert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationale & ObjectiveSymptom burden is distressing for patients living with kidney failure, but there is limited information about the combination of symptoms and individual symptoms that most strongly predict health care use in this group. We classified and summarized patients’ symptom burden levels and changes over time and estimated associations with hospitalizations among patients receiving incident hemodialysis.Study DesignLongitudinal, observational.Setting & ParticipantsIndividuals initiating dialysis in the United States.ExposureKidney Disease Quality of Life-36 (KDQOL-36) measure.OutcomeFirst hospitalization after dialysis initiation.Analytical ApproachLatent transition analysis was used to identify symptom burden classes using the KDQOL-36. Cox regression models were used to assess whether individual KDQOL-36 symptoms and symptom burden groups were associated with hospitalization risk after dialysis initiation, independent of demographics and comorbid conditions.Results1,818 participants were Black (29%), were aged >65 years (59%), were women (42%), had diabetes (49%), and had hypertension (74%). Introduction

Symptom burden refers to the prevalence, frequency, and impact of symptoms on an individual’s daily life.1 Individuals with kidney failure often experience symptoms such as fatigue, pain, and pruritus.2 Quantifying the impact of symptoms on the lives of individuals with kidney failure and designing person-centered interventions to reduce symptom burden have received increasing focus in nephrology.3 Symptoms can be ascertained using patient-reported outcome measures. The Kidney Disease Quality of Life-36 (KDQOL-36) instrument, a patient-reported outcome measure that quantifies aspects of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) for individuals with kidney disease, asks respondents about the presence, frequency, and impact on their life of a wide range of symptoms, including changes in mood, energy, and pain. In the United States, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services mandated the use of a standardized mental and physical assessment tool in dialysis centers for quality performance monitoring, and the KDQOL-36 was selected by the National Quality Forum and the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Clinical Performance Measures guidance to fulfill this purpose.4 Although the KDQOL-36 is not a required tool to fulfill Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services’ Conditions for Coverage, the vast majority of dialysis centers use it for this purpose.

Most existing evidence related to symptom burden in kidney disease has focused on identifying cross-sectional associations with patient characteristics.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Although treating symptoms themselves is a worthy pursuit for individuals with kidney disease, the relationship between symptom burden and adverse health outcomes over time has received limited attention. There is a dearth of evidence on associations between symptoms, such as pain and fatigue, and health outcomes. Characterizing symptom burden as individual symptoms or symptom groups, and evaluating whether they are longitudinally associated with adverse health outcomes, could enhance the feasibility of timely symptom assessment and intervention.

The objectives of the study were as follows: (1) to characterize symptoms among individuals receiving in-center hemodialysis during the first 3 months after dialysis initiation (a time of heightened risk of adverse health outcomes such as hospitalizations)10; (2) to create new classifications of overall symptom severity using KDQOL-36 scores to group patients into low, moderate, or high levels of symptom burden; and (3) to determine whether incident hospitalization was associated with KDQOL scale scores, individual symptoms from the KDQOL-36, and levels of symptom burden (low, moderate, and high).

Methods

Overview and Study Data

This study was deemed to be nonhuman subject research by the Northwestern Institutional Review Board (STU00216921). Because it was a secondary analysis of existing clinical data, informed consent was not required.

The data used in this study were collected during routine dialysis care in the United States by Visonex (now part of Renvio), a company providing tailored electronic health record solutions for dialysis centers. Visonex includes data from hundreds of small and medium dialysis units, as well as hospital-based units, that use Visonex’s Clarity electronic health record system across the United States. We drew a sample of 4,885 patients from Visonex’s database to create an observational, longitudinal database. We extracted records of individuals who initiated dialysis between November 22, 2014, and October 24, 2022, and captured up to 8 years of follow-up data. The KDQOL-36 was administered annually as part of routine clinical care for in-center hemodialysis patients. Patient records were selected to include those with complete KDQOL-36 assessments, defined as having completed at least 1 item on a KDQOL-36 scale. We excluded patients without a dialysis initiation date or KDQOL-36 assessments available for analysis. In addition, we excluded KDQOL-36 assessments administered before the first dialysis receipt and duplicate KDQOL-36 assessments. Other data incorporated into the analyses included demographic characteristics (age, sex, and race) and clinical characteristics (history of diabetes, hypertension, and heart failure).

KDQOL-36

The KDQOL-36 is the most widely used HRQoL measure in clinical care in the United States and has evidenced its fitness for this purpose through its strong reliability, validity, and interpretability.11, 12, 13 This evidence includes national normative standards for the United States dialysis population,12 as well as measurement invariance between Black and White patients.11 It includes generic and kidney disease-targeted measures. The SF-12 version 1 (12 items total) Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) scores were calculated using a T-score metric (mean = 50 and standard deviation [SD] = 10, in the US general population); higher scores indicate better HRQoL. The 3 kidney disease-targeted scales, comprising a total of 24 items, were the Burden of Kidney Disease (4 items; eg, “My kidney disease interferes too much with my life”), Symptoms/Problems of Kidney Disease (12 items; eg, “Washed out or drained?”), and Effects of Kidney Disease (8 items; eg, “Your ability to work around the house”). Burden of Kidney Disease items were preceded by the following statement: “How true or false is each of the following statements?,” with 5 response options ranging from “Definitely true” (1) to “Definitely false” (5). Symptoms and Problems of Kidney Disease items had the following prelude: “During the past 4 weeks, to what extent were you bothered by each of the following?,” with 5 response options from “Not at all bothered” (1) to “Extremely bothered” (5). Effects of Kidney Disease items were asked in terms of “How much does kidney disease bother you in each of the following areas?,” with 5 response options from “Not at all bothered” (1) to “Extremely bothered” (5). In addition to the 3 kidney disease-targeted scales, the KDQOL-36 Summary Score was calculated by averaging the scores of the 3 scales. Higher scores on the kidney disease-specific scales were reported on a T-score metric, with a mean of 50 and an SD of 10 in the US dialysis population. A higher score indicates better HRQoL across KDQOL-36 scales. Calculation of T-scores norm referenced to the US dialysis population used the population means and SDs of the KDQOL scale scores from this population, which were as follows: Burden of Kidney Disease = 52.8 (29.1); Symptoms and Problems of Kidney Diseases = 79.0 (15.4); Effects of Kidney Disease = 74.1 (21.5); and KDQOL-36 Summary Score = 73.0 (16.9).12

We also focused on the following 5 items from the KDQOL-36 that captured symptoms with evidence of burden and frequent occurrence among dialysis patients:14, 15, 16, 17 2 items from the SF-12: a pain interference item (“During the past 4 weeks, how much did pain interfere with your normal work [including both work outside the home and housework]?”; response options: “Not at all” [1] to “Extremely” [5]) and a lack of energy item (“How much of the time during the past 4 weeks … Did you have a lot energy?”; response options: “All of the time” [1] to “None of the time” [6]). From the Symptoms and Problems scale, we focused on the following 3 items: (1) muscle soreness, (2) itchy skin, and (3) numbness in the hands or feet. These individual items were coded for analysis so that higher scores indicated greater symptom severity.

Statistical Analyses

Participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using means, SDs, frequencies, and percentages, as appropriate, for the total dialysis population in our data set, and for the KDQOL analysis subset. We also calculated the means, SDs, and ranges of KDQOL-36 scores and individual item responses. For longitudinal analysis, we grouped follow-up KDQOL-36 assessments into the following time intervals: 1-3 months, 4-6 months, 7-9 months, and 10-12 months. When individual patients had more than 1 KDQOL-36 assessment within the same interval, the first was selected for analysis.

We used latent transition analysis (LTA) to identify KDQOL symptom burden classes. LTA is the longitudinal extension of latent class analysis.18 Latent class analysis provides a model-based approach to identifying underlying subgroups of patients in a population based on observed categorical variables. The groupings focus on patient similarity rather than correlations between variables. Latent class analysis estimates 2 main parameters, latent class membership probabilities and item-response probabilities, conditional on class membership. LTA allows membership of a latent class to change over time, thereby estimating transition probabilities as additional parameters. These transition probabilities refer to the probability of moving between classes over time. Similar to latent class analysis, latent classes are determined by statistical criteria without reference to any predefined grouping of patients.

The Akaike’s Information Criterion, the Bayesian Information Criterion, and the likelihood ratio statistic (G2) were compared to determine the optimal number of latent classes. The optimal model was selected based on adequate model identification and minimum Akaike’s Information Criterion and Bayesian Information Criterion.18 The latent transition model assumes local independence, meaning that indicators of a latent class are independent. A 5% level of significance was used for all statistical tests. All LTA models were fit using the PROC LTA software in SAS Version 9.4, which assumes missing data to be missing at random.

To examine symptom burden and the incidence of hospitalization after dialysis initiation, we applied Cox regression models to test the association between symptom burden groups derived from KDQOL-36 scores and the risk of first hospitalization after dialysis initiation. Subsequent hospitalizations were not included. Participants were censored at loss to follow-up or the end of the study. The median follow-up time was 8 months, ranging from 1-86 months. All covariates were assessed at the start of the survival follow-up. For each outcome, we adjusted the models for demographic and clinical characteristics, including age (below or above 65 years), sex, diabetes, hypertension, and heart failure. We confirmed that there were no violations of the proportional hazards assumption using the Kolmogorov-type supremum test and visual inspection by checking martingale residuals.19

Association Between KDQOL-36 and Incidence of Hospitalization

Table 2 presents the associations between KDQOL-36 scores at the time of hemodialysis initiation, including responses to individual symptom items, and hospitalization incidence. A 5-point reduction (worsening) in PCS, MCS, Burden of Kidney Disease, Symptoms and Problems of Kidney Disease, and the KDQOL-36 Summary Score was significantly associated with a 3.4%-6.5% increase in the hazard of hospitalization, adjusting for patient characteristics. Statistically significant and larger effects were observed for individual symptoms. A 1-category worsening in pain interference was significantly associated with a 12% increase in the hazard of hospitalization. A 1-category worsening in fatigue was significantly associated with an 8% increase in the hazard of hospitalization. A 1-category worsening in numbness was significantly associated with a 6% increase in the hazard of hospitalization. All of these associations were adjusted for patient characteristics.

Classification of Patients into Latent Symptom Burden Groups

We classified patients into the following 3 groups based on their KDQOL scores: (1) low symptom burden, (2) moderate symptom burden, and (3) high symptom burden. Table S2 shows the probabilities of group membership at each time point, which can be interpreted as the estimated proportion in each latent group. Approximately one-third (27%-32%) were in the low symptom burden group, ∼40% (41%-49%) were in the high symptom burden group, and ∼25% (26%-27%) were in the moderate symptom burden group at each time point. Table S3 presents the response probabilities for KDQOL-36 scores by membership in each latent symptom burden group.

For the low symptom burden group, the probabilities were high (81%-98%) of having an above-median score on each KDQOL-36 scale, reflecting better HRQoL. In the moderate symptom burden group, the probabilities of having below-median scores on the PCS and Burden of Kidney Disease scales (57% for both) were higher, indicating that this group tends to experience physical impairment and overall disease burden. Finally, the high symptom burden group had high probabilities of below-median scores on all KDQOL-36 scales (79%-93%). These results were corroborated by the distributions of KDQOL-36 scores within each latent symptom burden group (Table 3). For each KDQOL-36 scale, scores were lowest (indicating worse HRQoL) in the high symptom burden category, except for PCS, which had a slightly lower score in the moderate symptom burden category. For the KDQOL-36 Summary Score, the high symptom burden group score was more than an SD (10 points) lower than the moderate symptom burden group’s score. Finally, the high symptom burden group consistently had the highest scores on the KDQOL single items, with higher scores indicating worse symptoms.

Discussion

Kidney failure and dialysis impose a significant HRQoL burden. Yet, there is insufficient evidence on how dialysis symptoms change over time from the time of treatment initiation and which symptoms are most strongly associated with clinically long-term health outcomes. In this study, we found that 24% of patients initiate in-center hemodialysis with a high level of symptom burden, and this level remained stable over the first year. In addition, using an innovative statistical approach, we found that patients can be risk stratified based on their total and individual symptom burden levels. Critically, we found that pain interference and fatigue, as well as overall symptom burden, were useful prognostic indicators in patients receiving in-center hemodialysis. In light of recent consensus-based recommendations for clinically actionable symptom monitoring to improve HRQoL while receiving dialysis, these findings suggest the potential for enhanced symptom management through more refined symptom assessment using the KDQOL-36,20,21 and that symptom burden should remain a treatment target in hemodialysis.

Few studies have examined changes in symptoms or other aspects of HRQoL over time in dialysis. A study by de Rooij et al2 found that from dialysis start to 12 months later, the number of total reported symptoms reduced little (1 symptom on average), with only a minor reduction in total symptom burden. A second study by Song et al22 found only small changes in symptoms assessed over 12 months, although a cohort of patients who improved over time was identified. This study may have been limited by the use of the Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale, which does not include some relevant dialysis symptoms and impacts (eg, skin dryness, numbness in hands/feet, sexual dysfunction, itching, and specific pain locations). A study by Chilcot et al23 focused on depression trajectories within 12 months of dialysis initiation and found more dramatic changes among a few patients, although most (62%) began dialysis and maintained low levels of depression. In general, our findings align with these studies. Collectively, our findings suggest that the most burdensome symptomatic event associated with kidney failure is starting dialysis itself, after which large changes in symptoms may not occur.24 Determination of the level of symptom burden was facilitated by the use of LTA, a person-centered approach to identification of groups of patients sharing underlying patterns of symptom experience. This contrasts with previous research to identify clusters of symptoms using a variable-centered analysis approach with the KDQOL-36 Symptoms and Problems of Kidney Disease scale.25

Our findings build upon those from a recent study by Hall et al26 that examined the association between KDQOL-36 scores and hospitalization. Similar to our research, Hall et al26 found a significant association between PCS and MCS scores and hospitalization; however, in that study, the KDQOL-36 Burden and Effects of Kidney Disease scales were not associated with this outcome. Differences in the findings of these studies may be attributable to differences in the study populations, because the Hall et al26 study included patients aged ≥75 years who had been receiving dialysis for an average of 5.9 years. However, the addition of symptom burden summary groups from the LTA analysis, along with key individual symptoms, provides new insights into the best way to conceptualize dialysis symptoms in relation to clinically relevant events. Our study suggests that screening for pain, fatigue, and overall moderate to high symptom burden may identify patients at high risk for hospitalization. Symptom-targeted interventions are gaining more prominence in nephrology, including interventions that treat pain.27,28 Early symptom management interventions to prevent hospitalization require further development, with the identification of symptom management clinical pathways for dialysis patients. Moreover, as dialysis symptoms may start high and remain burdensome over time, there is incentive to control symptoms early after patients start dialysis. Controlling symptoms early in the patients’ dialysis experience may reduce future hospitalizations and ultimately health care costs for dialysis patients.29

Our study has several strengths. To our knowledge, ours is among the first studies to examine trends over time in HRQoL in patients receiving hemodialysis immediately after dialysis initiation. Furthermore, we have demonstrated associations between various potentially clinically relevant approaches to symptom burden assessment using the KDQOL-36 and hospitalizations. Because the KDQOL-36 is among the most frequently assessed patient-reported outcome measures in kidney disease,30 there is strong potential to apply our approaches widely. Our study also has limitations. Our data are specific to Visonex participants and thus may not be generalizable to the US in-center hemodialysis population. We did not have complete data related to participants’ self-reported race. However, as there is no biological basis for an association between race and symptom burden, we assert that the lack of race reporting does not undermine our results. In addition, we did not have access to data on patient deaths, which could have been useful to censor in time-to-event analysis. Finally, we note that we only focused on associations of symptoms and the first hospitalization after dialysis initiation; subsequent hospitalizations could be useful to explore in future research.

In conclusion, this study presents a novel application of the KDQOL-36 to assess the level of symptom burden in in-center hemodialysis patients. It helps to risk stratify symptoms that may require clinical attention. This study’s findings should lead to additional efforts to align optimized symptom monitoring in dialysis centers with approaches to reduce the burden of symptoms. Doing so will help improve the HRQoL of a rapidly growing dialysis population internationally.
