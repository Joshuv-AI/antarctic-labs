Computational Identifiability
Lucius E.J. Bynum, Rajesh Ranganath, Kyunghyun Cho

Abstract
Identification conditions describe the computability of a target query or parameter of interest as a function of the type and amount of information available. In causal identification, this information is often expressed in the form of a causal graph, and data are observed or collected for some subset of variables in the graph. Target queries may be for a single effect alone or for a class of effects in a given model. The derivation of an identification algorithm then defines mathematically the process by which the desired causal effect(s) can be uniquely determined, theoretically, in expectation. Identifiability in expectation, or ‘theoretical identifiability,’ generally assumes asymptotic properties, infinite data, or other mathematically idealized conditions. In this paper, we explore a fundamental distinction between this theoretical, idealized notion of identifiability and a proposed alternative that is computation-bound. The framework we propose — ‘computational identifiability’ — is to instead define a finite computational search procedure for an empirical estimator. If this process finds an estimator empirically, within a desired error tolerance, then identifiability is satisfied, conditional on the specified assumptions of the search (i.e., a prior distribution over the parameters) and conditional on the search procedure itself . Through several experiments, we demonstrate how this framework allows us to answer fine-grained, practical identification questions, such as identification with small finite samples, with ambiguous graphical criteria, with mixed observational-interventional data, and across counterfactual data and estimands. Code is available at https://github.com/lbynum/metadentify .

Introduction

A critical step in being able to answer a causal or a statistical query from data is determining whether or not there is sufficient information to determine a unique answer. Identifiability addresses this problem, deriving mathematically what is possible to uniquely determine from a collection of distributions that encode assumptions, in expectation. In causal inference, identification algorithms often make use of combinations of causal graphical criteria, probability axioms, matrix or algebraic equations, bounding arguments, and other mathematical tools. This notion of identifiability in expectation, which we term ‘theoretical identifiability,’ generally assumes asymptotic properties, infinite data, or other mathematically idealized conditions.

While theoretical identifiability is a long-solved problem for many model classes in expectation, with sound and complete algorithms like do-calculus
[
34
,
47
]
, po-calculus
[
29
]
, or
\sigma
-calculus
[
10
]
, identifiability itself is far from a solved problem. Determining theoretical identifiability in new settings requires bespoke analysis
[
37
,
36
]
. Moreover, many important settings involve finite sample sizes, ambiguous graphical criteria, or complex combinations of observational and experimental data that defy the requirements of existing algorithms and known bounds. In such scenarios, theoretical guarantees of identifiability in expectation may offer little guidance on whether a target parameter can actually be estimated. Similarly, many settings that are considered unidentifiable nonparametrically may become identifiable under additional parametric assumptions (such as linearity, non-Gaussianity, or monotonicity
[
45
,
37
]
), but exploring these possibilities analytically, especially for nonlinear settings, remains a significant challenge. Novel settings that do not yet have theoretical identifiability results, or where identifiability assumptions are hard to satisfy, leave the search for new identification algorithms and partial identification bounds open and ongoing
[
39
,
17
,
49
,
23
,
25
]
.

In this work, we introduce an alternative notion of identifiability: ‘computational identifiability,’ that instead frames identifiability as a finite computational search (see
Figure

1
). In lieu of mathematical derivation, computational identifiability starts with (1) a meta-prior over the parameters in question: in this case, a distribution over
structural causal models (SCMs)
; and (2) a hypothesis space of possible estimators. For a given finite sample size
n
, a desired error tolerance
\epsilon
, and a desired confidence bound
\delta
, computational identifiability defines successful identification as the existence of an estimator in the given hypothesis space that is satisfactory, based on the desired error tolerance and desired confidence. In other words, computational identifiability defines a practical and empirical, hence actionable, notion of identifiability.

Complete identification algorithms.

Neural Causal Models.

A related line of work on

also connects identifiability and estimation, but in a fundamentally different manner than our work here. Neural identifiability

Preliminaries

Let capital letter
X

X=x

\mathbf{X}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
denote a set of random variables, with value
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}

P_{X}
denotes the cumulative distribution function of variable
X

denotes the density (or mass) function. Let
P_{Y\mid X=x}
denote the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X=x
and
P_{Y\mid X}
denote the collection of
P_{Y\mid X=x}
for all

Y
given
X

Causal models.

We define a
causal model
as a tuple
\mathcal{M}=(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{U},\mathbf{F})
. In this tuple,
\mathbf{V}
is a set of observed variables,
\mathbf{U}
a set of unobserved (exogenous) variables, and
\mathbf{F}
a set of functions
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{|\mathbf{V}|}
for each
V_{i}\in\mathbf{V}
such that
V_{i}=f_{i}(\textsc{PA}_{i},U_{i})
where
\textsc{PA}_{i}\subseteq\mathbf{V}\setminus\{V_{i}\}
represents the causal parents of
V_{i}
and
U_{i}\subseteq\mathbf{U}
. A causal model can be pictorially represented as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) with nodes for
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}
and directed edges for
\mathbf{F}
. A
probabilistic causal model

(\mathcal{M},P_{\mathbf{U}})
adds distribution
P_{\mathbf{U}}
over the unobserved variables. The 4-tuple
\mathfrak{C}=(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{U},\mathbf{F},P_{\mathbf{U}})
is also commonly referred to as a
SCM

. An
SCM
entails an
observational distribution

P^{\mathfrak{C}}
as well as distributions after interventions, which we define as follows.

Interventions.

We define an
intervention

I
on variable
V_{i}
as the substitution of equation
V_{i}=f_{i}(\textsc{PA}_{i},U_{i})
with a modified mechanism
V_{i}:=\tilde{f}_{i}(\tilde{\textsc{PA}}_{i},\widetilde{U}_{i})
, commonly notated
\text{do}(I)
.
Such ‘soft’ interventions
[
14
]
allow us to express several types of ‘regimes’ under which data are collected, for example allowing us to describe
transportability
, where we may wish to generalize causal knowledge across different environments with combined observational and/or experimental data
[
11
]
. Soft interventions encompass the
idle
or
null
intervention
\emptyset
, which represents what happens naturally (i.e.,
\tilde{f}_{i}=f_{i}
);
atomic
interventions, which set
V_{i}
to a constant value;
conditional
interventions, which set
V_{i}
to a deterministic function of observable parents; and
stochastic
interventions, which set
V_{i}
to a probability distribution conditional on a set of observable parents (see
[
11
]
for detailed discussion). A
submodel

\mathcal{M}_{I}
for intervention
I
is the model
\mathcal{M}
after intervention
\text{do}(I)
. Given a probabilistic causal model, we can derive from
(\mathcal{M}_{I},P_{\mathbf{U}})
the distribution of any subset of variables following intervention
\text{do}(I)
. We denote an
SCM

\mathfrak{C}
after intervention
I
as
\mathfrak{C}^{\text{do}(I)}
, and the resulting
interventional distribution
as
P^{\mathfrak{C};\text{do}(I)}
. Note that
P^{\mathfrak{C};\text{do}(\emptyset)}
is equivalent to the observational distribution
P^{\mathfrak{C}}
.

Counterfactuals.

If, instead of using
P_{\mathbf{U}}
in
(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{x}},P_{\mathbf{U}})
to derive an interventional distribution, we specify a distribution over the exogenous variables that is specific to a particular context or individual, the same mechanics that allow us to define interventions allow us to define
counterfactuals
that model alternate possible outcomes after interventions in a specific context. We use an asterisk to denote counterfactual versions
\mathbf{V}^{*}
of variables
\mathbf{V}
. Counterfactual variable
\mathbf{Y}^{*}
given a factual observation
\mathbf{z}
and intervention
I
(
\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z}\subseteq\mathbf{V}
) can be computed via a three-step procedure often referred to as ‘abduction, action, prediction.’ Abduction uses observed evidence to obtain
P_{\mathbf{U}\mid\mathbf{z}}
from
P_{\mathbf{U}}
. Action performs intervention
I
to obtain
\mathcal{M}_{I}
. Prediction computes the probability of
\mathbf{Y}^{*}
from
(\mathcal{M}_{I},P_{\mathbf{U}\mid\mathbf{z}})
(or analogously conditioning on
\mathbf{Z}\in A
with
P(\mathbf{Z}\in A)>0
in the continuous case rather than on
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{z}
). We denote the
counterfactual distribution

P^{\mathfrak{C}\mid\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{z};\text{do}(I)}
.

3
Computational identifiability

In this section, we introduce the key theoretical components that define computational identifiability as a meta-property of a computational search for a causal estimator.

Definition 1

(Causal query)
.

Let
\mathbb{C}
denote the space of all
SCMs
with a fixed set of endogenous variables
\mathbf{V}
.
We define a
causal query

\Theta[\cdot]
as a functional taking as input an
SCM

\mathfrak{C}\in\mathbb{C}
as well as a query point
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}
for any
\mathbf{X}\subseteq\mathbf{V}
. More formally, let
\Omega_{\mathbf{X}}
denote the sample space for any subset
\mathbf{X}\subseteq\mathbf{V}
and define
\Omega=\bigsqcup_{\mathbf{X}\subseteq\mathbf{V}}\Omega_{\mathbf{X}}
. Then
\Theta:\mathbb{C}\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

For example, average treatment effects such as the population average treatment effect (PATE) and conditional average treatment effect (CATE) can be defined as expectations of interventional distributions specific to a given
SCM

\mathfrak{C}
that either make use of or ignore a given observation
\mathbf{x}
. Specifically, given
SCM

\mathfrak{C}=(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{U},\mathbf{F},P_{\mathbf{U}})
with a binary treatment variable
T\in\mathbf{V}
and outcome variable
Y\in\mathbf{V}

\Theta_{\text{PATE}}\left[\mathfrak{C},\cdot\right]:=\mathbb{E}_{p_{Y}^{\mathfrak{C};\text{do}(T=1)}}[Y]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{Y}^{\mathfrak{C};\text{do}(T=0)}}[Y]
, where the second argument is ignored.
Adding a condition
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}
for conditioning variables
\mathbf{X}\subseteq\mathbf{V}\setminus\{Y,T\}
, the
CATE
can be written as

\Theta_{\text{CATE}}\left[\mathfrak{C},\mathbf{x}\right]:=\mathbb{E}_{p_{Y\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}}^{\mathfrak{C};\text{do}(T=1)}}[Y]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{Y\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}}^{\mathfrak{C};\text{do}(T=0)}}[Y].

Common estimands comprised of counterfactual rather than interventional distributions include individual treatment effects (ITEs) and sample average treatment effects (SATEs). The ITE represents the counterfactual difference in outcomes specific to a given unit with observed features
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{v}
(including realized
T
and
Y

\Theta_{\text{ITE}}\left[\mathfrak{C},\mathbf{v}\right]:=\mathbb{E}_{p_{Y}^{\mathfrak{C}\mid\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{v};\text{do}(T=1)}}[Y]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{Y}^{\mathfrak{C}\mid\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{v};\text{do}(T=0)}}[Y].

Building on the ITE, the SATE represents the average ITE across a given set of observed units, which can be viewed as a composition of a set of causal queries. For example, given a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the SATE can be written as

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Theta_{\text{ITE}}\left[\mathfrak{C},\mathbf{v}_{i}\right].

Traditional ‘theoretical’ definitions of causal identifiability are about being able to uniquely determine a causal query from a given (typically observational) distribution or set of distributions. We define this in our context as follows.

Definition 3

(Theoretical identifiability)
.

Given a space
\Omega_{\mathfrak{C}}
over
SCMs

\mathfrak{C}
with endogenous variables
\mathbf{V}
and a subset of observed variables
\mathbf{O}\subseteq\mathbf{V}
, we say a causal query
\Theta\left[\mathfrak{C},\mathbf{x}\right]
with query point
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}
for
\mathbf{X}\subseteq\mathbf{V}
is

from
\mathbf{O}
if
for any two
SCMs

\mathfrak{C}_{1},\mathfrak{C}_{2}\in\Omega_{\mathfrak{C}}
,
\Theta\left[\mathfrak{C}_{1},\mathbf{x}\right]=\Theta\left[\mathfrak{C}_{2},\mathbf{x}\right]
whenever
P_{\mathbf{O}}^{\mathfrak{C}_{1}}=P_{\mathbf{O}}^{\mathfrak{C}_{2}}
.

Definition 4

(Computational identifiability)
.

Given a prior distribution over

\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{C}\sim\pi}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{O}\sim p^{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathbf{O},mix}}\left[\mathbf{1}\left(\left|\Theta\left[\mathfrak{C},\mathbf{x}\right]-\phi\left(\mathbf{O},\mathbf{x}\right)\right|\leq\epsilon\right)\right]\right]\geq 1-\delta

(Causal query distribution)

Given

Intuitively, computational identifiability (e.g., estimating

•

Prior-conditioning:

•

3.1
Meta-learning an estimator
\phi

In the experiments in this paper, we make use of two CNP variants based on the above setup. The first is a standard mean-pooled CNP, and the second is an attention-based variant utilizing Set Transformers

PATE vs. CATE/ITE meta-estimation.
Architecturally, the distinction between PATE and CATE/ITE meta-estimation reduces to the routing of the query points
\mathbf{x}_{t}
. For population-level estimands like PATE, the decoder exclusively uses the global context, whereas for individual-level estimands like CATE/ITE, the meta-model conditions on
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, processing query features with the global context either via direct concatenation (Q-CNP) or cross-attention (Q-TNP).

Non-monotonic and architecture-specific identifiability.
The counterfactual case above — and in particular two negative results — allow us to demonstrate an important point about computational identifiability and its conditional nature. They can be summarized intuitively as ‘conditional really does mean conditional.’ Two parameters of our prior and hypothesis space demonstrate this point well in the
ITE
case — dataset size
N
and our choice of neural network architecture.

Figure 5
:

Figure

5

CATE

ITE

This demonstrates how computational identifiability can have non-monotonic or surprising relationships with things like increasing dataset size that contradict what we might normally expect from theoretical identifiability. A similar result occurs with architecture choice, in this case whether or not the meta-model processes query points and context sets via cross-attention and set attention (as in the Q-TNP) or via concatenation and mean-pooling (as in the Q-CNP). Taking the (Counterfactual + Empirical),
\sigma^{2}=25,n=100
case as an example, the Q-CNP was only able to achieve

, while the Q-TNP was able to achieve

. In other words, in this experiment, only the latter architecture was able to learn to perform the matching of individual datapoints that recovering the
ITE

This property of being conditional on an empirical search — and its chosen hyperparameters — is a core component of computational identifiability. The notion itself is fundamentally about attaching a practical, hypothesis-constrained search process to the concept of identifiability.

5
Conclusion and future work

Identifiability is not a notion specific only to causal inference, and there is potential to expand the set of definitions we have written in
Section

3
to work beyond the causal inference settings to other settings, like survival analysis
[
40
,
31
]
and missing data
[
26
]
, where assumptions and analysis drive traditional theoretical identifiability.
Another important future direction is to study computational identifiability in high dimensional settings, which we have not considered here. A key benefit of computational identifiability, as we have defined it, is that it makes the notion of identifiability
actionable
. Through additional optimization of the computational search process itself, there is also significant potential to further make such a search for identifiability systematic or automated.

Using the example in

The story about identifiability in the meta-model search is, for example, more optimistic than in the known-algorithm search, especially for small dataset sizes.
