Epithelial cell proliferation on anterior surface of intraocular lens after Descemet stripping automated endothelial keratoplasty (DSAEK) surgery: a case report
Authors: Ali Zare, Mozhgan Rezaei Kanavi, Sepehr Feizi, Fariba Pirsarabi, Faezeh Tajari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe report a rare case of histopathologically confirmed epithelial cell proliferation on the anterior surface of an intraocular lens (IOL) following Descemet stripping automated endothelial keratoplasty (DSAEK).Case presentationA 61-year-old female with a history of multiple ocular surgeries, including phacoemulsification cataract extraction and DSAEK, was referred for progressive visual deterioration. Background

Opacification of an intraocular lens (IOL) is a rare but clinically significant complication that may lead to glare, reduced contrast sensitivity, and decreased visual acuity [1–3]. IOL opacification is broadly classified into primary and secondary forms. Primary opacification results from intrinsic defects introduced during manufacturing or packaging, including material impurities, inadequate production or storage conditions, or interactions with packaging materials, and occurs independently of ocular comorbidities or subsequent surgical interventions [4–7]. Secondary opacification, in contrast, arises from deposits forming on the IOL surface due to environmental or ocular factors. These include disruption of the blood–aqueous barrier, chronic intraocular inflammation, and surgical procedures involving the introduction of air or gas into the anterior chamber [7–9]. Endothelial keratoplasty is the most commonly reported condition associated with secondary IOL opacification, followed by diabetes mellitus, pars plana vitrectomy, glaucoma, and arterial hypertension [10].

Endothelial keratoplasty has become the preferred surgical approach for treating corneal endothelial disorders due to its advantages over penetrating keratoplasty. However, since its introduction, increasing numbers of cases describing a characteristic pattern of hydrophilic IOL opacification have been reported [1–3, 8–18]. These cases typically involve a circular area of opacification on the anterior optical surface of the IOL and are associated with calcium deposition [1–3, 5, 14–16].

Epithelial cell proliferation directly on the anterior surface of an IOL represents a distinct and exceedingly rare cause of opacification. The underlying mechanisms and risk factors remain poorly understood. It is hypothesized that residual epithelial cells from the lens capsule adhere to the IOL surface and subsequently proliferate. Potential contributing factors include intraoperative trauma, IOL material or design characteristics, and chronic inflammation [19]. Clinically, this process may result in progressive visual disturbances, including glare, blurred vision, irregular astigmatism, diplopia, and reduced contrast sensitivity, which are often refractory to conservative management. This report describes the clinical course, diagnostic challenges, and histopathological findings of epithelial cell proliferation on the IOL surface, presumed to originate from the capsular bag, as a rare cause of visual impairment following Descemet stripping automated endothelial keratoplasty (DSAEK).

Case report

A 61-year-old woman with a history of pathologic myopic fundus changes in both eyes had previously undergone bilateral phakic Artisan IOL implantation. Following the development of age-related cataract in the right eye, she underwent phakic IOL removal, phacoemulsification, and implantation of a hydrophilic acrylic posterior chamber IOL (Akreos Adapt AO, Bausch and Lomb Inc., Rochester, NY, USA) in September 2016. Postoperatively, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in the right eye improved from counting fingers at 4 m to 4/10.

Four years after cataract surgery, the patient presented with progressive visual decline, with BCVA reduced to counting fingers at 1.5 m. Clinical examination revealed pseudophakic bullous keratopathy, and she subsequently underwent DSAEK, resulting in restoration of corneal clarity. Six months postoperatively, a neodymium-doped yttrium–aluminum–garnet (Nd: YAG) laser posterior capsulotomy was performed for visually significant posterior capsule opacification. The procedure was uneventful, and no cracks or pits were observed on the IOL optic.

Eighteen months later, the patient again reported decreased vision, with BCVA reduced to 3/10. She reported no history of topical ocular therapies or systemic medications. Slit-lamp examination revealed opacities on the anterior surface of the IOL, confined to the pupillary zone (Fig. 1). Initial therapeutic interventions, including Nd: YAG laser treatment, were unsuccessful in disrupting the deposits. Due to the persistence of the opacities and lack of response to laser therapy, surgical polishing of the IOL surface was performed, but the deposits remained unchanged. Intraoperatively, the IOL surface was noted to be rough. Ultimately, the IOL was explanted along with the entire capsular bag because of firm adhesions between the lens and capsule. An anterior vitrectomy was performed, and an aphakic Artisan IOL (Artisan, Ophtec, the Netherlands) was fixated to the iris. At final follow-up, slit-lamp examination demonstrated a clear endothelial transplant and a well-centered anterior chamber Artisan IOL (Fig. 2). The final BCVA remained 3/10 due to underlying high myopic fundus changes.

Histopathologic evaluation

The explanted IOL was fixed in 10% formalin and submitted for histopathological assessment. Gross examination revealed a localized opaque area on the anterior surface of the IOL optic, within the capsulorhexis zone. One half of the IOL was stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and the other half with von Kossa stain. Light microscopy demonstrated multiple microcracks on the anterior IOL surface, along with a layer of epithelial-like cellular imprints of variable size covering the anterior surface. Some cells contained small nuclei, while others appeared as organelle-free ghost cells. Several large cellular imprints resembled swollen Wedl cells, typically observed in posterior subcapsular cataracts (Fig. 3A). These findings confirmed the diagnosis of epithelial cell proliferation on the anterior IOL surface, most likely originating from the lens capsule epithelium. Von Kossa staining was negative for calcium deposits (Fig. 3B).

Discussion and conclusion

IOL opacification has an incidence of 5%–9.7% following DSAEK and 2.5% after Descemet membrane endothelial keratoplasty [11, 12, 17, 18]. The majority of reported cases involve hydrophilic acrylic IOLs [1–3, 8–12, 14–18], whereas opacification of hydrophobic IOLs or hydrophilic acrylic IOLs with hydrophobic surface properties has been reported only sporadically [12, 13, 17, 18]. Hydrophilic IOLs from different manufacturers appear equally affected, suggesting a general issue with hydrophilic acrylic material rather than with specific manufacturers or material compositions [1].

Known risk factors for this complication are use of SF6 for anterior chamber tamponade and repeated intracameral injections of air (re-bubbling) to reposition a displaced donor graft in the early postoperative period, which increase the duration of air/gas–IOL contact [12, 17, 18]. The development of opacification in the central anterior IOL surface, where the air bubble contacts the lens, further supports the role of intraocular air or gas in this process. Prolonged exposure to air can dehydrate the hydrophilic IOL surface, leading to microdegradation and changes in the physicochemical properties of the lens within the pupillary zone, thereby increasing permeability to anterior chamber factors and facilitating calcium phosphate deposition [1–3, 5, 14–16].

In the present case, calcium deposition was not observed; instead, the deposits consisted of epithelial cells. Although the exact cell origin was not directly determined, the presence of Wedl cell-like large cellular imprints supports the hypothesis that proliferation arose from residual capsular epithelial cells. Differences in the type of deposits between this case and previously reported cases may reflect variations in individual IOL polymer ultrastructure. Other contributing factors may include the indication for endothelial keratoplasty, disruption of the blood–aqueous barrier, and intraocular inflammatory processes.

IOL opacification after endothelial keratoplasty should be differentiated from other types of IOL opacifications (Table). Primary IOL calcification caused by material impurities or manufacturing defects typically involves the entire optical surface, including the posterior surface and sometimes the haptics [4, 5]. In contrast, opacification of hydrophilic IOLs after DSAEK is confined to a circular area of the anterior optical surface, sparing the posterior surface and haptics. Glistenings, another differential diagnosis, occur in hydrophobic IOL materials and correspond to microvacuoles within the lens substance [20, 21]. Capsular bag opacification must also be considered, as inappropriate laser posterior capsulotomy can complicate subsequent IOL exchange and increase the risk of vitreous loss.

Differential diagnoses of intraocular lens (IOL) opacification

In the present case, Nd: YAG laser and surgical polishing were ineffective in clearing the opacity. In symptomatic patients, IOL exchange remains the only intervention to restore visual function. However, explantation carries substantial risks, particularly in eyes with strong adhesions between the IOL and capsular bag or after previous Nd: YAG capsulotomy [22]. In eyes post-DSAEK, IOL exchange is particularly challenging because it may adversely affect long-term endothelial graft survival, especially when iris-fixated IOLs are required due to insufficient capsular support. Consequently, IOL replacement is less frequently performed after DSAEK. Nieuwendaal et al. reported an IOL exchange rate of 2.5% in 160 eyes after DSAEK [11], and none of the eyes in the study by Ahad et al. underwent explantation [12].

Preventive strategies include avoiding hydrophilic acrylic IOLs in eyes with endothelial disorders, as these eyes are likely to require future endothelial keratoplasty. In cases where keratoplasty is performed in eyes with hydrophilic acrylic IOLs, measures such as inducing pupillary miosis and minimizing the duration of high-pressure anterior chamber tamponade with air or gas may reduce the risk of IOL opacification.

The primary limitation of this report is its single-case nature, which limits generalizability. Furthermore, the absence of immunohistochemical testing prevented precise determination of epithelial cell origin. Nevertheless, this case highlights the rarity and clinical significance of epithelial cell proliferation on IOLs as a cause of postoperative opacification and visual impairment after DSAEK.

In conclusion, lens epithelial cell proliferation on an IOL is a rare but clinically important complication following cataract surgery and DSAEK. Diagnosis is challenging, as it can be mistaken for more common IOL surface deposits. Histopathological evaluation of the explanted lens is essential for definitive diagnosis. Early recognition of this entity is crucial to guide appropriate management and avoid ineffective interventions.
