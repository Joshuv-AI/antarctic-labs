A Simpler Analysis for ε \varepsilon -Clairvoyant Flow Time Scheduling
Anupam Gupta, Haim Kaplan, Alexander Lindermayr, Jens Schlöter, Sorrachai Yingchareonthawornchai

Abstract
We simplify the proof of the optimality of the Shortest Lower-Bound First (SLF) algorithm, introduced by Gupta, Kaplan, Lindermayr, Schlöter, and Yingchareonthawornchai [FOCS’25], for minimizing the total flow time in the ε \varepsilon -clairvoyant setting.

1
Introduction

We consider the classical problem
of minimizing the total flow time
in the online setting where jobs arrive over time
and preemption is allowed. The flow time of a job is the total time
that a job spends in the system. We study the objective
of minimizing the total flow time of all the jobs on a single machine.

In
[
Gup+25
]
, the authors of the present paper
studied this problem
in the
\varepsilon
-clairvoyant setting, which is defined as follows.
When a job
j
arrives, we know nothing about its processing time
p_{j}
. However, the value
p_{j}

is revealed when an
\varepsilon
-fraction of job
j
remains to be processed, for a constant
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
.
They showed that a natural algorithm, called Shortest Lower-Bound First (SLF), achieves a competitive ratio of at most
\smash{\left\lceil\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil}
when
\varepsilon>0
, and this bound is best possible for deterministic algorithms.

In this technical note, we present a simplified analysis
of SLF, which yields the same tight bound on the competitive ratio. For the remaining aspects of this model and its
connection to related work, we refer to
[
Gup+25
]
.
In the remainder of this note, we first present the SLF algorithm and then prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
Gup+25
]
)
.

For any constant
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, there is a deterministic

\varepsilon
-clairvoyant algorithm SLF that is
\smash{\left\lceil\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil}
-competitive
for the objective of minimizing the total flow time on a single machine.

1.1
Technical Overview

We prove that SLF is locally
\smash{\left\lceil\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil}
-competitive at any time
t
,
meaning that there are at most
\smash{\left\lceil\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil}
times as many jobs active in the algorithm’s schedule
as in an optimal schedule. This is a necessary property for constant competitive algorithms
for this problem, and we also use it in
[
Gup+25
]
.
However, there are two major places in which we improve our analysis in
[
Gup+25
]
,
which we explain in the following two paragraphs.

Frozen Jobs instead of Early Arrivals.

A key ingredient in the analysis of
[
Gup+25
]
is the Early Arrival Lemma,
which shows that we can make certain jobs arrive earlier
without affecting the set of active jobs at time
t
or their elapsed times
in the algorithm’s schedule.
Clearly, such a modification can only help an optimal
solution to reduce its number of active jobs at some target time
t
, hence never improves the local competitive ratio.
In
[
Gup+25
]
, we iteratively search for what we called
magical
times
s
with the property that during
[s,t]
the algorithm does not work on jobs
j\in J_{s}
whose remaining time
is more than
\varepsilon p_{j}
at time
s
(
p_{j}
is the total length of job
j
).
Then, we move the arrival time of jobs
that arrive after time
s
to arrive earlier at time
s
.
This modification can only help an optimal solution to reduce its load at time
t
and adds structure to the schedule produced by
our algorithm from time
s
onwards, which greatly helps in the analysis.

In the analysis presented here, we completely avoid this technical reduction. Instead, we mark such jobs
J_{s}
as
frozen

as soon as the algorithm no longer works on them, before time
t
.
This is possible due to a more powerful Fast Forward Lemma (cf.
Lemma

3.6
),
which does not require all jobs to arrive at the beginning of the fast-forward interval, but instead allows jobs to arrive
over time until the next job becomes frozen or all jobs are known.

Fixed-Length Prefix Bounds instead of Valid Assignments.

The second major change is that we use a weaker invariant over time.
In
[
Gup+25
]
, we inductively show for any time
t
a fractional assignment
between the active jobs
in the algorithm’s schedule and the active jobs in the optimal schedule.
The value of a node corresponding to job
j
, in this fractional matching,
is its remaining processing time at time
t
in the algorithm’s schedule or the optimal schedule, respectively (we call this the job’s volume at time
t
).
Specifically, we show
that at any time there exists an assignment that is at most
\smash{\left\lceil\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil}
-expanding.
1
1

1

This means that any prefix of length
x
of SLF’s queue sorted in nonincreasing order of remaining time is assigned to at most
\smash{\left\lceil\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil}\cdot x
jobs of
\mathrm{OPT}
.

This
allows us to bound the number of active jobs in the algorithm’s schedule
with
\smash{\left\lceil\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil}
times the number of active jobs in the optimal schedule, thus proving
Theorem

1.1
.
This invariant has also been used in Schrage’s original proof of SRPT’s optimality for minimizing total flow time
[
Sch68
]
.

We use a simpler invariant instead.
As in
[
Gup+25
]
we fix
a time
t
for which we want to prove local competitiveness,
and then only show an invariant (that depends on
t
) over all times
t^{\prime}\leq t
. But
instead of requiring a bounded expansion for all prefixes of active jobs in the optimal solution, ordered by
decreasing volume as in
[
Gup+25
]
,
we only require that the total volume of the
\smash{\left\lceil\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil}\cdot|\mathrm{OPT}(t^{\prime},t)|
jobs with largest volume
in the algorithm’s queue is at least as much as the total
volume of the jobs in
\mathrm{OPT}(t^{\prime},t)
in OPT’s queue.
Here,
\mathrm{OPT}(t^{\prime},t)
denotes the set of jobs that are active in the optimal schedule
at time
t^{\prime}
and time
t
.
This significantly simplifies the proof, as we do not need to maintain a full fractional assignment of bounded expansion
over time, but only need to argue about the volume of a
single set of jobs
.
This technique has previously been used in the context of
online flow time scheduling
[
BD07
,
AT18
,
ALT21
]
.
A major challenge is that our SLF algorithm may process multiple
jobs at the same time (using Round-Robin on a certain subset of jobs) and hence reduce the volume of multiple jobs at the same time.
This is a crucial difference compared to those earlier works, in which an algorithm works at any time on at most one job, and thus, makes inductive arguments about volume changes much
easier to handle.

2
The Shortest-Lower-Bound-First Algorithm

We first restate the Shortest Lower-Bound First (
\mathrm{SLF}
) algorithm, which was introduced in
[
Gup+25
]
.
For any time
t
, let
{\mathrm{SLF}}(t)
denote the set of active (released and unfinished) jobs in the schedule of
{\mathrm{SLF}}
.
We further distinguish between known and unknown jobs.

Definition 2.1

(Known/Unknown Jobs)
.

Let
j
be an active job in
{\mathrm{SLF}}
at time
t
. We say that a job
j
is
known
if
r_{j}(t)\leq\varepsilon\cdot p_{j}
. Otherwise,
j
is
unknown
.

Let
U(t)
and
K(t)
denote the unknown and known jobs in the algorithm’s schedule at time
t
.
At any time
t
and for each job
j\in{\mathrm{SLF}}(t)
, we define an
estimate

\eta_{j}(t)

as

\eta_{j}(t)=\begin{cases}\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot e_{j}(t),&\text{ if }j\in U(t),\\
r_{j}(t),&\text{ else, }\end{cases}

and
\mathrm{SLF}
works on a job
j
with smallest estimate
\eta_{j}(t)
.

More specifically,
\mathrm{SLF}
behaves as follows:

1.

If
\min_{j\in K(t)}\eta_{j}(t)\leq\min_{j\in U(t)}\eta_{j}(t)
,
\mathrm{SLF}
processes an arbitrary known job
j
with minimum
\eta_{j}(t)
.

2.

Otherwise,
\mathrm{SLF}
processes
all
unknown jobs
j
with minimum
\eta_{j}(t)
in parallel at an equal pace. We can achieve this by scheduling them in Round-Robin with infinitesimally small steps.

We assume without loss of generality that the value of
\varepsilon
is known to the algorithm from the start of the instance. Otherwise, the algorithm can compute it from the elapsed times once it is notified of the remaining processing time for the first job that becomes known. Before the first job becomes known, knowing the value of
\varepsilon
is not necessary.

3
Analysis

In the following, we assume that
0<\varepsilon<1
. Note that if
\varepsilon=1
, SLF is equivalent to Shortest Remaining Processing Time (SRPT), which is known to be
1
-competitive for total flow time
[
Sch68
]
.

Our goal is to show that
\mathrm{SLF}
is locally competitive. Let
\mathrm{OPT}(t)
denote the set of active jobs in an optimal solution at time
t
.

Lemma 3.1
.

At any time
t
, it holds that
|{\mathrm{SLF}}(t)|\leq\smash{\left\lceil\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil}\cdot|\mathrm{OPT}(t)|
.

Integrating this inequality over time yields
Theorem

1.1
.

Fix a point in time
t
for which we want to prove
Lemma

3.1
. We can assume without loss of generality that until time
t
the algorithm does not idle. Otherwise, since both the algorithm and the optimal solution do not idle unnecessarily, we can analyze every maximal time interval where the algorithm does not idle independently.

Terminology.

For all times
t^{\prime}\leq t
, let
\mathrm{OPT}(t,t^{\prime}):=\mathrm{OPT}(t)\cap\mathrm{OPT}(t^{\prime})
refer to the set of jobs that are alive at time
t

and
time
t^{\prime}
in the optimal solution. Let
\delta_{t^{\prime}}:=\delta(t,t^{\prime}):=|\mathrm{OPT}(t,t^{\prime})|

denote the number of jobs that are alive at both time
t^{\prime}
and time
t
, in
\mathrm{OPT}
.
Finally, we use
B(t^{\prime},x)
to refer to the
x
alive jobs with the largest remaining volume in
{\mathrm{SLF}}
at time
t^{\prime}
.
Usually, we care only about the set
B(t^{\prime},x)
for
x=\smash{\left\lceil\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil}\delta_{t^{\prime}}
.
Hence, we introduce the shorthand notation
B(t^{\prime}):=B(t^{\prime},\smash{\left\lceil\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil}\delta_{t^{\prime}})
.
For a set of jobs
Q
and a time
t^{\prime}
, let
\operatorname{vol}_{Q}(t^{\prime})=\sum_{j\in Q}r_{j}(t^{\prime})

and
\operatorname{vol}^{*}_{Q}(t^{\prime})=\sum_{j\in Q}r^{*}_{j}(t^{\prime})

denote the remaining volumes of jobs in
Q
at time
t^{\prime}
in
{\mathrm{SLF}}
and
\mathrm{OPT}
, respectively.

The algorithm’s state can change instantaneously due to job arrivals and status changes (e.g., from unknown to known). We use
A(t^{-})
to denote the state of
A(t)
just before the changes are applied at time
t
. Intuitively, one can think of
t^{-}
as the time instant just before
t
. The notation
t^{-}
is also defined the same way for all other functions
f(t)
and
f(t^{-})
.

Our main goal is to show the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

For all times
t^{\prime}\leq t
, we have

\operatorname{vol}_{B(t^{\prime})}(t^{\prime})\geq\operatorname{vol}^{*}_{\mathrm{OPT}(t,t^{\prime})}(t^{\prime})
.

Let
\operatorname{vol}(t):=\operatorname{vol}_{{\mathrm{SLF}}(t)}(t)
be the total remaining volume in
{\mathrm{SLF}}
at time
t
. Note that
\operatorname{vol}(t)
is also equal to the total remaining volume in
\mathrm{OPT}
at time
t
, as neither strategy idles unnecessarily.
We can now prove
Lemma

3.1
.

Proof of
Lemma

3.1
.

Using
Lemma

3.2
for
t^{\prime}=t
, we obtain

\operatorname{vol}(t)\geq\operatorname{vol}_{B(t)}(t)\geq\operatorname{vol}^{*}_{\mathrm{OPT}(t)}(t)=\operatorname{vol}(t)\ .

This implies
\operatorname{vol}(t)=\operatorname{vol}_{B(t)}(t)
, meaning that the
\smash{\left\lceil\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil}\delta_{t}
jobs with the largest remaining volume in
{\mathrm{SLF}}
contain all remaining volume in
{\mathrm{SLF}}
at time
t
. Thus, there are at most
\smash{\left\lceil\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil}\delta_{t}=\smash{\left\lceil\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil}\cdot|\mathrm{OPT}(t)|
active jobs in
{\mathrm{SLF}}
at time
t
, which concludes the proof.
∎

It remains to prove
Lemma

3.2
, which we do by induction over time. More specifically, we start at time
0
and work
our way forward to time
t
. We have two different types of induction
steps to go from time
t_{0}
to time
t_{1}\leq t
: the Fast Forward Lemma (
Lemma

3.6
) and the Suffix Carving Lemma (
Lemma

3.14
).
We distinguish between these two cases based on the following criteria:

1.

If the algorithm works on unknown jobs
or on known jobs while there are active unknown jobs that will
be processed again before time
t_{1}
, then we use the Fast Forward Lemma (
Lemma

3.6
) to go from
t_{0}
to
t_{1}
.

2.

Otherwise, the algorithm only works on known jobs during
[t_{0},t_{1}]
. We use the Suffix Carving Lemma (
Lemma

3.14
) to go from
t_{0}
to
t_{1}
.

Importantly, we choose
maximal
intervals
[t_{0},t_{1}]
, that is, we extend
t_{1}
as far as possible while satisfying one of the two criteria above.
To properly define those intervals, we introduce the following definitions.

Definition 3.3

(Frozen jobs)
.

We say a job
j
is
frozen
at time
t^{\prime}
if
j
is unknown at
t^{\prime}
and not touched by the algorithm during
[t^{\prime},t]
. We use
F(t^{\prime})
to refer to the set of frozen jobs at time
t^{\prime}
.

By definition, we have
F(t_{0})\subseteq F(t_{1})
for all
0\leq t_{0}\leq t_{1}\leq t
.

Definition 3.4

(Leader)
.

Let
s\leq t
be a point in time with
|U(s)\setminus F(s)|\geq 1
. Then, define the
leader

L(s)
at time
s
as some job
L(s)\in U(s)\setminus F(s)
with
e_{L(s)}(s)=\max_{j\in U(s)\setminus F(s)}e_{j}(s)
.

The following lemma proves a useful property of frozen jobs: If some job becomes frozen at time
t^{\prime}
, then all unknown jobs
j
that were released before time
t^{\prime}
(and still active at time
t^{\prime}
) become frozen at time
t^{\prime}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

If at time
t^{\prime}
a set of jobs
J_{f}\subseteq U(t^{\prime-})\setminus F(t^{\prime-})
becomes frozen, that is,
F(t^{\prime-})\cup J_{f}=F(t^{\prime})
, then

J_{f}=U(t^{\prime-})\setminus F(t^{\prime-})
.
In particular, the leader is touched at time
t^{\prime-}
.

Proof.

Since
J_{f}\cap F(t^{\prime-})=\varnothing
and by the definition of frozen jobs,
it must be that every job in
J_{f}
is touched at time
t^{\prime-}
.
Let
j_{f}\in J_{f}
.

Assume that there exists a job
j\in(U(t^{\prime-})\setminus F(t^{\prime-}))\setminus J_{f}
. This means that there exists a time
t^{\prime}\leq t^{\prime\prime}\leq t
at which
j
is touched. Let
t^{\prime\prime}
be the first such time. Hence
e_{j}(t^{\prime-})=e_{j}(t^{\prime\prime})
. By the definition of SLF and using the fact that
j_{f}\in F(t^{\prime\prime})
, it must hold that

e_{j}(t^{\prime\prime})<e_{j_{f}}(t^{\prime\prime})
.
Since
j_{f}
is frozen from time
t^{\prime}
on, we have
e_{j_{f}}(t^{\prime-})=e_{j_{f}}(t^{\prime\prime})
.
This implies
e_{j}(t^{\prime-})<e_{j_{f}}(t^{\prime-})
. But this contradicts that
j_{f}
was touched at time
t^{\prime-}
.
Hence,
J_{f}=U(t^{\prime-})\setminus F(t^{\prime-})
.
Since every job in
J_{f}=U(t^{\prime-})\setminus F(t^{\prime-})
is touched at time
t^{\prime-}
, the leader is touched at time
t^{\prime-}
.
∎

We use this lemma in
Section

3.3
to prove
Lemma

3.2
at the end of this section. Before that, in the following two subsections, we state and prove the Fast Forward Lemma and the Suffix Carving Lemma.

3.1
Fast Forward Lemma

We first state the Fast Forward Lemma.

Lemma 3.6

(Fast Forward Lemma)
.

Let
t_{0}\leq t
be such that
\operatorname{vol}_{B(t_{0})}(t_{0})\geq\operatorname{vol}^{*}_{\mathrm{OPT}(t,t_{0})}(t_{0})
and
U(t_{0})=F(t_{0})
, i.e., all unknown jobs at
t_{0}
are frozen. Let
t_{1}\geq t_{0}
be such that

1.

at time
t_{1}
, the leader

L(t_{1})\in U(t_{1})\setminus F(t_{1})
with

e_{L(t_{1})}(t_{1})=\max_{j\in U(t_{1})\setminus F(t_{1})}e_{j}(t_{1})

is touched,

2.

the set of frozen jobs does not change during
[t_{0},t_{1}]
,
that is,
F(t_{0})=F(t^{\prime})
for all
t^{\prime}\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
, and

3.

during
(t_{0},t_{1}]
, there is always at least one new unknown job, that is,
|U(s)\setminus U(t_{0})|\geq 1
for all
s\in(t_{0},t_{1}]
. This implies that there is a new job arrival during
(t_{0},t_{1}]
.

Then, we have that
\operatorname{vol}_{B(t_{1})}(t_{1})\geq\operatorname{vol}^{*}_{\mathrm{OPT}(t,t_{1})}(t_{1})
.

For the rest of this subsection, we fix
t_{0}
and
t_{1}
as described in
Lemma

3.6
. Let
J_{new}
denote the set of jobs that are released during
(t_{0},t_{1})
and let
\gamma:=e_{L(t_{1})}(t_{1})
denote the elapsed time of the leader
L(t_{1})
at time
t_{1}
.
We start with the following immediate corollary of Conditions 2 and 3 of
Lemma

3.6
.

Observation 3.7
.

At any time
s\in(t_{0},t_{1}]
, there exists an unknown non-frozen job, that is,
|U(s)\setminus F(s)|\geq 1
.

Proof.

This simply follows because Condition 2 and the assumption
yield
F(s)=F(t_{0})=U(t_{0})
for all
s\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
.
Then, Condition 3 yields
|U(s)\setminus F(s)|=|U(s)\setminus U(t_{0})|\geq 1
for all
s\in(t_{0},t_{1}]
.
∎

Observe that Condition 2 of
Lemma

3.6
implies the following useful property, which states that the elapsed time of the leaders never decreases during the interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]
. Note that the identity of the leader can change during
[t_{0},t_{1}]
, but nevertheless, the elapsed time of the leader does not decrease.

Observation 3.8
.

The maximum elapsed time of unknown non-frozen jobs never decreases during
[t_{0},t_{1}]
, i.e., for all
s,s^{\prime}\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
with
s\leq s^{\prime}
we have
\max_{j\in U(s^{\prime})\setminus F(s^{\prime})}e_{j}(s^{\prime})\geq\max_{j\in U(s)\setminus F(s)}e_{j}(s)
.

Proof.

For the sake of contradiction, assume that there are points in time
s,s^{\prime}\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
with
s\leq s^{\prime}
but
\max_{j\in U(s^{\prime})\setminus F(s^{\prime})}e_{j}(s^{\prime})<\max_{j\in U(s)\setminus F(s)}e_{j}(s)
. Then, there must be some time
s\leq\bar{s}\leq s^{\prime}
with
\max_{j\in U(\bar{s}^{+})\setminus F(\bar{s}^{+})}e_{j}(\bar{s}^{+})<\max_{j\in U(\bar{s})\setminus F(\bar{s})}e_{j}(\bar{s})
. This can only happen if the leader
L(\bar{s})
and all jobs in
U(\bar{s})\setminus F(\bar{s})
with the same elapsed time as the leader become known or frozen at
\bar{s}
. In either case, the leader is touched at
\bar{s}
, which implies that all jobs in
U(\bar{s})\setminus F(\bar{s})
have the same elapsed time as the leader. Hence, all jobs in
U(\bar{s})\setminus F(\bar{s})
become known or frozen at time
\bar{s}
. This is a contradiction to
3.7
.
∎

To prove
Lemma

3.6
, we rely on the following observation. This corresponds to
[
Gup+25
, Lemma V.13]
. Compared to
[
Gup+25
]
, the argument slightly changes because we need to use the second assumption of
Lemma

3.6
instead of the early arriving property.

Lemma 3.9

(Switching)
.

The following statements hold:

(i)

For every job
j\in K(t_{0})
,
j\in{\mathrm{SLF}}(t_{1})
if and only if
r_{j}(t_{0})>\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot\gamma
.

(ii)

If
j\in K(t_{1})
, then
r_{j}(t_{0})=r_{j}(t_{1}).

Thus,
K(t_{1})\subseteq K(t_{0})
and
{\mathrm{SLF}}
does not touch
j
during
(t_{0},t_{1}]
.

(iii)

For every job
j\in U(t_{1})\setminus F(t_{1})
, we have
e_{j}(t_{1})=\gamma
.

(iv)

For every job
j\in J_{new}\setminus{\mathrm{SLF}}(t_{1})
, we have
p_{j}=e_{j}(t_{1})\leq\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot\gamma
.

Proof.

We separately prove the four statements:

(i)

Fix an arbitrary job
j\in K(t_{0})\cap{\mathrm{SLF}}(t_{1})
. Since
j
is alive and known at
t_{1}
but the leader
L(t_{1})
is touched at
t_{1}
, we must have
r_{j}(t_{0})>\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot e_{L(t_{1})}(t_{1})=\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\gamma
by the definition of
{\mathrm{SLF}}
.

Fix an arbitrary job
j\in K(t_{0})\setminus{\mathrm{SLF}}(t_{1})
. Let
s
denote the earliest point in time during
(t_{0},t_{1})
at which
j
is touched.
Using
3.8
, we get

r_{j}(t_{0})=r_{j}(s)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}e_{L(s)}(s)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}e_{L(t_{1})}(t_{1})=\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\gamma
.

(ii)

We first argue that there cannot be a job
j\in J_{new}\cap K(t_{1})
. If there was such a job
j
, then there is a point in time
t_{0}<s<t_{1}
at which
j
becomes known. By

3.7
,
there needs to be at least another non-frozen unknown job
j^{\prime}\in U(s)\setminus F(s)
. Consider the leader
L(s)
among those jobs (
e_{L(s)}(s)=\max_{j\in U(s)\setminus F(s)}e_{j}(s)
). Note that we might have
L(s)\not=L(t_{1})
.
Since
j
becomes known at
s
, we have
r_{j}(s)=\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}e_{j}(s)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}e_{L(s)}(s)
. Using that the remaining size of
j
can only decrease over time and that the elapsed time of the (potentially changing) leader only increases over time by
3.8
, we get
r_{j}(t_{1})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\gamma
. If
r_{j}(t_{1})>0
, then this is a contradiction to
L(t_{1})
being touched at
t_{1}
. Hence,
j\not\in{\mathrm{SLF}}(t_{1})
.

The argument for jobs in
K(t_{0})
is essentially the same. If
r_{j}(t_{0})>r_{j}(t_{1})
, then
j
is touched at some point in time
t_{0}<s<t_{1}
. Hence,
r_{j}(s)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}e_{L(s)}(s)
, where the leader
L(s)
exists by the proof of
Lemma

3.2
.
Using
3.8
, we get
r_{j}(t_{1})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\gamma
, a contradiction to
L(t_{1})
being touched at
t_{1}
.

(iii)

Since the leader
L(t_{1})
with
e_{L(t_{1})}(t_{1})=\gamma
is touched at
t_{1}
, all

Recall that

.

\displaystyle|S|

\displaystyle\leq|B(t_{0})\setminus D|+|O^{+}\cap{\mathrm{SLF}}(t_{1})|+\left\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil\cdot|O^{+}\setminus{\mathrm{SLF}}(t_{1})|+|D|+\left(\left\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil-1\right)\cdot|O^{+}\cap{\mathrm{SLF}}(t_{1})|

It remains to show that the volume of

.

If

3.2
Suffix Carving Lemma

We next state and prove the Suffix Carving Lemma, which we can use for time intervals when SLF only works on known jobs.

Lemma 3.14

(Suffix Carving)
.

Let
t_{0}<t_{1}\leq t
be two points in time such that

1.

\operatorname{vol}_{B(t_{0})}(t_{0})\geq\operatorname{vol}^{*}_{\mathrm{OPT}(t,t_{0})}(t_{0})
, and

2.

{\mathrm{SLF}}
only works on known jobs during
[t_{0},t_{1}]
.

Then,
\operatorname{vol}_{B(t_{1})}(t_{1})\geq\operatorname{vol}^{*}_{\mathrm{OPT}(t,t_{1})}(t_{1})
.

Proof.

The assumption that
{\mathrm{SLF}}
works only on known jobs during
[t_{0},t_{1}]
implies that no new jobs are released during that interval. Then,
\mathrm{OPT}(t,t_{0})=\mathrm{OPT}(t,t_{1})
, and thus,
\operatorname{vol}^{*}_{\mathrm{OPT}(t,t_{0})}(t_{0})\geq\operatorname{vol}^{*}_{\mathrm{OPT}(t,t_{0})}(t_{1})\geq\operatorname{vol}^{*}_{\mathrm{OPT}(t,t_{1})}(t_{1})
.

If
{\mathrm{SLF}}
does not touch jobs in
B(t_{0})
during
[t_{0},t_{1}]
, then

\operatorname{vol}_{B(t_{1})}(t_{1})=\operatorname{vol}_{B(t_{0})}(t_{0})\geq\operatorname{vol}^{*}_{\mathrm{OPT}(t,t_{0})}(t_{0})\geq\operatorname{vol}^{*}_{\mathrm{OPT}(t,t_{1})}(t_{1}),

by the first assumption of the lemma, and we are done.

If
{\mathrm{SLF}}
at some point touches a job
j\in B(t_{0})
, then, using the fact that when
{\mathrm{SLF}}
works on a known job
then it is the smallest in its queue, we conclude that
j
is the smallest in SLF’s queue. This implies that the total number of jobs in
{\mathrm{SLF}}(t_{1})
is at most
|B(t_{0})|\leq\smash{\left\lceil\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil}\delta_{t_{0}}=\smash{\left\lceil\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right\rceil}\delta_{t_{1}}
. Hence,
B(t_{1})={\mathrm{SLF}}(t_{1})
. Using the assumption that the machine never idles, we have

\operatorname{vol}_{B(t_{1})}(t_{1})=\operatorname{vol}_{{\mathrm{SLF}}(t_{1})}(t_{1})=\operatorname{vol}^{*}_{\mathrm{OPT}(t_{1})}(t_{1})\geq\operatorname{vol}^{*}_{\mathrm{OPT}(t,t_{1})}(t_{1})\ ,

which concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎
