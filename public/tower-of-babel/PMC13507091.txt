Mapping radiosensitivity in glioblastoma using MR elastography and biomechanical modeling
Authors: Constantinos Harkos, Kyprianos Dimou, Marina Koutsi, Yiannis Roussakis, Siri Fløgstad Svensson, Kyrre E Emblem, Constantinos Zamboglou, Triantafyllos Stylianopoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma multiforme (GBM) remains a devastating disease with a poor prognosis. GBM progression generates mechanical forces that compromise blood vessels’ functionality, impair perfusion, and create hypoxic regions, which in turn reduce radiosensitivity. While the link between hypoxia and radiosensitivity is known, a quantitative methodology to predict this effect is lacking. Here, we developed a patient-specific mechanistic mathematical model of radiotherapy that integrates Magnetic Resonance Elastography (MRE) imaging. The model can translate MRE-derived stiffness into spatial maps of mechanical stress and simulate subsequent events, such as compression of vessels and impaired intratumoral oxygen distribution which impacts radiosensitivity. Our simulations show that biomechanical properties of both tumor and host tissue control patterns of radiosensitivity. Notably, heterogeneous distribution of tumor elastic properties as well as the presence of regions of higher host-tissue stiffness adjacent to the tumor boundary, generate increased and heterogenous mechanical stresses within the tumor. The results are compressed vessels with hypo-perfused and hypoxic tumor tissue, and consequently compromised radiotherapy efficacy. Results show the importance of tumor microenvironment (TME) parameters and suggest that strategies to normalize the TME could improve treatment outcomes and help stratify treatments. Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most aggressive malignant brain tumor in adults and represents a devastating diagnosis owing to its rapid progression, therapeutic resistance, and poor prognosis1. In combination with surgery and chemotherapy, radiotherapy is a cornerstone of GBM treatment2. Heterogeneous response rates among patients, however, remains a challenge3. To this end, the development of patient-specific mathematical models can deepen our understanding of GBM development and potentially allow the optimization of individual patient treatment4.

Mathematical modeling approaches for radiotherapy include simple arithmetic formulas such as the linear-quadratic model (LQM), more complex continuous approaches based on differential equations4, discrete approaches that treat individual cells as distinct entities5, stochastic approaches that introduce randomness via probabilistic methods5, and machine-learning methods6,7. In this work, we focus on continuous models, which consider tumor cell density and tumor evolution at the macroscopic, tissue-level scale. Continuous glioma models have also been extended to incorporate biomechanical effects such as tumor-induced mass effect and intracranial pressure, improving their ability to capture patient-specific disease progression and treatment-relevant tissue deformation8,9. These models are attractive clinically because they can be calibrated to patient non-invasive magnetic resonance images (MRI), yield interpretable parameters such as the proliferation rate of tumor cells and incorporate spatiotemporal properties of the tissue and the effects of the treatment10.

The LQM is an empirical formula relating the proportion of cells that survive exposure to the dose of ionizing radiation and has been extensively used for modeling radiation treatment11. A common practice is to combine continuous reaction diffusion models which predict GBM progression with the LQM to include effects of radiation therapy in an effort to investigate the efficacy of various therapy schedules and dose distributions in GBM12. More recently, patient-specific image-informed models have also been used to forecast spatiotemporal response of high-grade gliomas during chemoradiotherapy, supporting adaptive radiotherapy strategies13. Other studies have employed a modified LQM to account for variations in cellular radio-sensitivity14,15. In that work, a key factor was the influence of oxygen, encompassing that cells are significantly more sensitive to radiation when oxygen is present14,15. This oxygen dependence was quantified using the oxygen enhancement ratio (OER), which compares the radiation dose required for a given effect under hypoxic versus oxygenated conditions14,15. Furthermore, these models integrated cell cycle phase-specific radiation responses, recognizing that sensitivity varies significantly throughout a cell’s life cycle14,15. Oxygen availability in the tumor microenvironment (TME) is determined in large part by biomechanical compressive forces generated during progression16. Indeed, the progression of a solid tumor, like GBM, within the confined space of the host tissue generates intratumoral compression, which can compress blood vessels, impair perfusion, and create hypoxic regions – all affecting radiotherapy sensitivity17–20. Recent patient-specific biomechanical glioma models further support the view that tumor-induced pressure and deformation influence the TME and treatment efficacy, underscoring the need to account for mechanics when predicting radiotherapy response8. However, a methodology that quantifies these sequential events and predicts the effect of radiotherapy is lacking.

To this end, and building on previous mechanistic modeling studies that established the coupling among tumor growth, solid stress, vascular compression, oxygen transport, and treatment response4,21–24, we developed a continuous mechanistic model of radiotherapy, incorporating complex shear modulus measured from magnetic resonance elastography (MRE). MRE is a noninvasive imaging technique, which allows for spatial quantification of the mechanical properties of tissues25,26. Recent work has further highlighted the potential of MRE to noninvasively characterize biomechanical heterogeneity in intracranial tumors, supporting its use for model personalization27. In a previous study, we showed that incorporating MRE-derived complex shear modulus provided a more realistic patient specific distribution of the mechanical stresses in the tumor which induce a distinct vascular compression and a unique intratumoral chemotherapeutic drug distribution4. Here, we extend this approach to simulate radiotherapy. The proposed model employs MRE complex shear modulus maps from a healthy brain and from 5 patients with GBM to simulate tumor progression. Pathophysiological properties of the TME include the functionality of the tumor vessels, the hydraulic conductivity of the tumor, the interstitial fluid pressure and the permeability of the vessels. The employed framework combines reaction diffusion equations with explicit tissue mechanics and radiotherapy. Radiotherapy is modeled using LQM which incorporates the oxygen dependence on the effect of radio-sensitivity to various cell phases. We present a quantitative association between MRE imaging and oxygen-modulated radiosensitivity in GBM, enabling individualized radiotherapy simulations accounting for the biomechanical state of the tumor. Furthermore, we examine the effect of various parameters on radiotherapy response. Finally, we investigate the behavior of our model on various treatment schedules for frail and non-frail patients, as suggested by the American Society for Radiation Oncology (ASTRO) for newly diagnosed WHO grade 4 adult-type diffuse glioma54.

Methods

MRI acquisition

MRI exams were performed on a 3 T clinical MRI scanner (Ingenia, Philips Medical Systems, Best, the Netherlands) using a 32-channel head coil. The MRI was done prior to tumor resection, and the MRE sequence was added to the standard preoperative MRI exam. This included T1-weighted MPRAGE images (3D inversion recovery gradient echo, 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 resolution, 256 × 256 × 368 matrix, TR/TE = 5.2/2.3 ms, shot interval 3000 ms, inversion delay 853 ms) acquired before and after administration of a gadolinium-based contrast agent (Gadovist, Bayer Pharma AG, Germany), with a 0.1 mmol/kg body weight dose, followed by a 20 mL saline flush (BB. Melsungen AG, Germany).

MRE was performed with a vibrational device placed on the side of the subject’s head, inducing shear waves at 50 Hz into the brain28,29. A multi-shot gradient-echo MRE sequence30 was used with bipolar 13 mT/m motion-encoding gradients in three orthogonal directions at 115 Hz. A reference scan without motion encoding gradients was used as a phase reference. The MRE sequence and the external wave generator were synchronized with a transistor-transistor logic trigger signal. Fifteen contiguous transversal slices were scanned, with an isotropic 3.1 mm resolution, a matrix size of 72 × 70 and FOV = 22 cm. Other scan parameters were flip angle = 20°, TR = 384 ms, TE = 12 ms, with Cartesian readout and a sensitivity encoding factor of 2. Eight wave phases were sampled, equally distributed over one oscillation period at 25 Hz. The actual mechanical vibration frequency was then shifted to the second index of the Fourier transform, thereby filtering out potential parasite contributions at 25, 75 and 100 Hz. The total MRE acquisition time was 5.5 min for the patients. For the healthy volunteer, the settings were the same, but 41 slices were acquired, and the MRE duration was 13 min.

Patient characteristics

All patients had histopathologically confirmed IDW wild-type glioblastoma. The patients (three males and two females) ranged from 53 to 75 years (median 60 years). For four of the patients the tumor was located in the temporal lobe, while the tumor of patient B was located centrally, in the basal ganglia/thalamus.

MRI incorporation in the model

Full-brain, anatomical T1-weighted MR images of a healthy subject used in earlier studies were employed to construct the brain geometry23,24. The 3D reconstruction of the images was performed using Simpleware X-2025.06, (Synopsis Inc., Sunnyvale, CA, USA). To reduce computational cost only the gray and white matter regions were considered. The geometry was then imported in COMSOL Multiphysics (COMSOL, Inc., Burlington, MA, USA) where the model equations were solved using the finite elements method. Results

Incorporation of MRE highlights regions of elevated radiosensitivity

To examine the effect of biomechanical heterogeneity derived from MRE, we run two simulations: one simulation that incorporated MRE from a patient with GBM (patient 1) for the tumor region and from a healthy subject for the host tissue (Fig. 2) and a second simulation that incorporated only mean values of the shear modulus for the tumor and healthy brain (Fig. 3). As shown in Fig. 2, incorporating the inhomogeneous distribution of mechanical properties, measured with MRE, into the model causes a heterogeneous distribution of mechanical solid stresses. In the mean modulus case (Fig. 3), we observe a uniform stress distribution where uniformly larger compressive stresses appear inside the tumor. In addition, in the mean value case the vascular density increases from the tumor center to the radial direction as we reach the host tissue. In the case including MRE, inhomogeneous solid stress development induces inhomogeneous vascular compression which results in a distinct vascular density distribution.

Specifically, the MRE complex modulus from the patient’s tumor was applied on the initial tumor seed while the healthy subject’s MRE complex modulus was applied to the surrounding host tissue. The cut plane at the center of the tumor is displayed here for a better visualization of the internal part of the tumor. Inhomogeneous solid stress development causes inhomogeneous vascular compression which results in unique vascular density distribution. Higher vascular density regions result in increased oxygen levels and enhanced radiosensitivity. This results in lower concentrations of tumor cells in these regions after the beginning of therapy.

Specifically, the seed contained a mean value for the shear modulus while the surrounding host tissue contained another mean value. The cut plane at the center of the tumor is displayed here for a better visualization of the internal part of the tumor. The figure shows that when MRE complex shear modulus was not incorporated, uniform stress distribution induced uniformly larger compressive stresses inside the tumor. Also, the oxygen levels increase from the tumor to the host tissue symmetrically in the angular direction before and after radiotherapy.

Also, in the mean value case (Fig. 3), we observe an increase in the oxygen levels from the tumor to the host tissue that is symmetric in the angular direction. On the other hand, in the MRE case (Fig. 2) regions with lower vascularization cause reduced oxygen delivery. Also, regions with higher oxygen levels inside the tumor at the day of therapy, had a lower tumor cell concentration a week after radiotherapy. This indicates that the oxygen distribution, modelled using biomechanical data, can be used to predict regions where radiotherapy will have higher impact on tumor cells. With fewer oxygen-consuming tumor cells in these regions, consumption of oxygen will also decrease, as shown in the oxygen distribution a week after therapy in Fig. 2.

Impact of tumor and host-tissue stiffness on vessel compression, hypoxia and radiosensitivity

To examine the impact of the tumor biomechanics derived from MRE on the model results, we repeated the simulations by replacing the MRE complex shear modulus of the tumor with that of the other four patients. Figure 4 depicts differences in model results such as solid stresses, the vascular density distribution, oxygenation and tumor cell density a week after the beginning of radiotherapy. Although we observe unique distributions of model variables for each patient due to the use of patient-specific MRE, we also observe similarities attributed to the fact that the elastic properties of the surrounding host tissue remain the same and intratumoral mechanical stresses depend not only on the mechanical properties of the tumor but also of the adjacent host tissue. Specifically, in Fig. 4 we observe higher vessel compression in the upper left side of the tumor which induces low levels of oxygenation in this region. This in turn lowers radiotherapy sensitivity, which causes higher concentrations of tumor cells.

To better understand these results, we run the simulation again by placing the initial tumor seed in another position inside of the brain. Figure 5 shows the initial and alternative position of the tumor surrounded by the host tissue’s shear modulus. Higher compressive stresses appear inside the tumor close to host tissue regions with higher shear modulus. This affects vessel compression. Specifically, a region with higher shear modulus on the upper left side of the tumor in position 1 and on the upper right side of position 2 reduced vascular density in the tumor regions adjacent to the stiff areas. Due to fewer vessels, oxygen transport is also reduced. As a result, radiotherapy sensitivity is reduced and thus more tumor cells appear after the radiotherapy compared to other tumor regions. Taken together, these findings indicate that while intratumoral stiffness drives patient-specific heterogeneity, the spatial arrangement of host-tissue stiffness around the lesion governs regional vessel compression and hypoxia, ultimately shaping radiosensitivity and residual tumor burden.

The effect of vascular functionality on oxygenation and radiotherapy response

To examine the effect of the functional vasculature on treatment outcomes, we again run our baseline model (tumor MRE from patient 1) with various values of the functional vascular density inside the tumor region. Figure 6 shows that for higher values of tumor vascular density (that is, more functional vessels), the tumor mass increases more rapidly before treatment due to the increased oxygen transport. On the other hand, after treatment more functional vessels induced a more rapid reduction of tumor mass compared to the less vascularized cases. This is due to the increased oxygen transport, increasing radio sensitivity.

Parameter sensitivity

The presented model is computationally demanding and requires several days for each run. For that reason, a comprehensive sensitivity analysis of the entire patient-specific model, requiring thousands of simulations was not feasible. Thus, to assess which parameters strongly influence the model predictions, we performed a set of complementary analyses.

First, the effect of incorporating MRE-derived elastography was examined as was described in the previous sections and presented in Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5. Second, we examined the effect of vascular functionality by varying the functional vascular density and assessing the resulting changes in oxygenation and treatment response (Fig. 6). Third, we investigated the effect of tumor cell sensitivity on radiotherapy by varying the ratio (Supplementary Figure S1). Finally, we examined the impact of the assumption of the initial concentration of the tumor cells where i) all tumor cells were assigned to the phase, ii) tumor cells were equally distributed among the five cell phases, iii) tumor cells were initially radioresistant (76% S, 6% G1, 6% G2, 6% M, 6% G0) and iv) tumor cells were initially radiosensitive (38% G2, 38% M, 8% G1, 8% S, 8% G0) (Fig. 7). In these cases, examined in Supplementary Figure S1, there is not an obvious effect of the ratio. Also, the initial phase composition affects pre-treatment tumor growth but has only a limited effect after treatment.

In addition, we performed a sensitivity analysis for the oxygen-related parameters. Because repeated simulations of the full patient-specific model were computationally prohibitive, this analysis was carried out using a reduced version of the framework. Specifically, the cell-cycle description was simplified to a single total tumor-cell concentration, following our previous work 4, and the patient-specific brain geometry was replaced by a simplified geometry used in an earlier version of the model60. This allowed multiple simulations to be performed efficiently. The parameters included in this analysis are shown in Supplementary Table S2, and the corresponding sensitivity analysis results are shown in Supplementary Table S3. The results show that among the oxygen-related parameters the strongest effect was associated with the influence of oxygen on tumor-cell proliferation.

Impact of radiotherapy fractionation schemes on tumor control in frail vs. non-frail glioma patient regimens

Our model can provide a spatially and temporally resolved description of the tumor under different radiotherapy schemes rather than only providing a global scalar comparison. Based on that we simulate various treatment schedules suggested by the ASTRO for newly diagnosed WHO grade 4 adult-type diffuse glioma61. Specifically, we modeled the two shorter partial brain radiotherapy schedules for frail patients i) 34 Gy in 10 fractions and ii) 25 Gy in 5 fractions. We also modeled the partial radiotherapy schedule of 40.05 Gy in 15 fractions for non-frail patients older than 70 years and we compared our results with our baseline model of 60 Gy in 30 fractions for non-frail patients younger than 70 years. In addition, motivated by the possibility that treatment breaks might allow transient recovery of vascular functionality and oxygenation, we examined an intermittent schedule of 5000 cGy in 10 fractions, delivered as 5 fractions in the first week, followed by a 1-week break and 5 additional fractions in the third week. Our results (Fig. 8) show that, among the shortened schedules, 2500 cGy in 5 fractions produced the least favorable response, with earlier regrowth than 3400 cGy in 10 fractions. The intermittent 5000 cGy in 10 fractions schedule also resulted in a worse response than the corresponding continuous two-week schedule, due to tumor recovery during the treatment break. Among all schedules examined, the standard 6000 cGy in 30 fractions regimen produced the most favorable tumor-control response.

Overall, the standard 60 Gy in 30 fractions schedule remained the most effective among the schedules examined. Figure was generated in Python using Matplotlib 66.

Discussion

The progression of a solid tumor like GBM generates solid stresses, which can compress blood vessels, impair perfusion, and create hypoxic regions which affect radiotherapy sensitivity18–20. Here we developed a model that quantifies these sequential events by incorporating MRE images into mathematical modelling of tumor progression and radiotherapy treatment. The model builds on earlier mechanistic modeling frameworks that established the coupling among tumor growth, solid stress, vascular compression, oxygen transport, and treatment response4,21–24. It therefore provides a first quantitative pipeline from MRE to spatially resolved, oxygen-aware radiosensitivity.

Incorporating MRE in the model allowed the conversion of patient-specific stiffness data into spatial maps of solid stress and vessel functionality. Those maps induce oxygenation patterns that affect regional radiosensitivity. Furthermore, the model suggests that both intratumoral stiffness and the arrangement of host-tissue stiffness around the lesion shape the vascular and oxygen landscapes relevant to radiosensitivity. Differing biomechanical properties of the surrounding host tissue cause variation in the compressed vessels in the peritumoral regions. Thus, while intratumoral heterogeneity drives patient-specific patterns, host tissue elasticity impacts tumor perfusion and hence radiosensitivity. Taken together, these findings indicate that patient-specific biomechanical and microenvironmental heterogeneity can generate distinct spatial radiosensitivity patterns and may thereby contribute to inter-patient differences in radiotherapy response.

Previous studies have shown that tumor growth induced stress can impair vascular functionality and thereby limit the delivery and efficacy of therapy62,63. In a previous modelling study, we suggested that increasing the functionality of the vessels with TME normalization can increase the transport and the efficacy of chemotherapy4. TME normalization refers to therapeutic strategies aimed at restructuring the tumor microenvironment to enhance the functional vasculature so that it supports anti-tumor immune responses and improved drug delivery64. In this context, the present results extend these earlier findings by suggesting that normalization may also benefit radiotherapy, as improved vascular function enhances oxygen delivery and, consequently, radiosensitivity. It is worth mentioning that for the parametric analysis in Fig. 6, we varied the functional vascular density for the entire simulation which includes the tumor development before and after therapy. A more realistic representation for normalization treatment would be to keep a single value during growth and vary the vascular density at the start of the treatment. In this case, we expect to see overlapping tumor growth before treatment and a clearer reduction after treatment in cases with higher vasculature. These results are also in agreement with clinical trials in GBM patients receiving vascular normalization therapy65.

Additionally, we performed various sensitivity tests to identify which parameters strongly influence radiotherapy response. The results of Figs. 6, 7 and Supplementary Figure S1 should be interpreted together with the results in Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5, which already show that changing the spatial distribution of the shear modulus modifies the resulting stress field, vascular compression, oxygenation and radiosensitivity. Taken together, these results indicate that parameters describing the TME have the strongest effect on the treatment outcome and should therefore be the primary targets for patient-specific personalization. Specifically, the sensitivity analysis shows that the effect of oxygen on tumor-cell proliferation is among the dominant determinants of response. By contrast, other parameters, such as the baseline cellular sensitivity to radiation represented by the ratio, had a smaller effect. Also, the initial phase composition although it affects pre-treatment tumor growth, has only a limited effect on radiosensitivity because tumor cells continuously transit between phases. As a result, the starting enrichment in more radiosensitive or more radioresistant phases is transient and largely lost before radiotherapy begins. This suggests that treatment response could be driven primarily by TME modulation of radiosensitivity rather than by the initial cell-cycle composition or the intrinsic radiosensitivity of the cells.

Furthermore, we used our model to test different radiotherapy schedules. These findings suggest that, among the regimens considered for frail patients, maintaining at least two weeks of treatment may be important for achieving better tumor control when tolerated. In particular, the poorer response observed with 2500 cGy in 5 fractions indicates that a short, one week course may be less favorable than a longer two-week schedule such as 3400 cGy in 10 fractions. Furthermore, although treatment interruption might in principle allow some transient microenvironmental recovery, the intermittent 5000 cGy in 10 fractions schedule was less effective than the continuous two-week schedule in the present simulations because the treatment break allowed surviving tumor cells more time to proliferate between irradiation periods. At the same time, interrupted schedules may still be clinically relevant if they improve treatment tolerability or overall patient condition. Therefore, the present results suggest that, for frail patients, a treatment duration of at least two weeks is preferable to a one-week regimen when it can be tolerated by the patient. Although practical considerations may still justify less intensive schedules in selected cases, continuous, two-week delivery appears more effective from the tumor-control perspective of the present model. Overall, the standard 6000 cGy in 30 fractions regimen remained the best-performing schedule among those examined.

Several simplifying assumptions were made in this study. For the host tissue, MRE of a healthy subject was used because the clinical patient scans did not cover the entire brain. In addition, the elastic properties were assumed to be constant during tumor progression as the patients’ MRE were obtained at one point during tumor development. Moreover, the present framework is not intended to retrospectively reproduce the exact progression of an individual patient’s tumor. In our simulations, tumor growth is initialized from a small spherical seed, onto which the patient-specific tumor elastography measured at that later clinical stage is mapped. As a result, early stage MRE is unavailable, and even if patient specific elastography of the surrounding host tissue was available, the true patient-specific growth trajectory could still not be reconstructed accurately. Therefore, the current framework should be interpreted as a mechanistic, image-informed model for studying the consequences of measured biomechanical heterogeneity on stress, vascular functionality, oxygenation, and radiosensitivity, rather than as a full reconstruction of the exact patient-specific developmental history. However, although incorporating patient specific whole brain data and temporal variations in elastic properties would likely affect the quantitative predictions, the conclusion that both intratumoral stiffness and the arrangement of host-tissue stiffness shape the vascular and oxygen landscapes relevant to radiosensitivity is expected to remain unchanged. Also, the model does not include a limit on how densely tumor cells can be packed together. Since cell concentration increases only modestly in these simulations, this simplification is unlikely to strongly affect the results or the main conclusions.

Furthermore, in this study we assessed the effect of radiotherapy without chemotherapy, while the standard treatment regime for GBM is radiotherapy with concomitant and adjuvant temozolomide52. Incorporation of chemotherapy in a previous model study showed that drug delivery was enhanced in highly vascularized regions and that those regions also had higher oxygen concentration due to the increased functional vasculature4. Thus, adding the effect of chemotherapy to the current study is expected to support the results found here. Specifically, regions we found to have higher oxygenation and increased radiosensitivity will also have increased drug delivery, which further enhances treatment outcomes. Thus, the MRE derived spatial maps of solid stress and vessel compression can be used to identify regions with higher treatment effectiveness to chemotherapy, radiotherapy or a combination. Additionally, the original 60 Gy and 40.05 Gy treatment plans in Fig. 8 include chemotherapy administration. Incorporation of chemotherapy would have resulted in faster reduction of tumor volume for both cases, but arguably this would not have changed our conclusions on the comparison of the two schedules.

Overall, our study demonstrates that MRE-derived maps of tumor stiffness and host tissue elasticity can help describe spatial associations of vascular compression, oxygenation, and radiosensitivity. The results also suggest that patient-specific TME features such as the vascular functionality and oxygenation play the dominant role in shaping radiotherapy response. These findings highlight the potential of integrating biomechanical imaging into treatment planning to optimize and personalize therapeutic strategies for GBM. Finally, this modeling framework is general, and it could be adapted to other tumors types for which MRI and MRE are available.
