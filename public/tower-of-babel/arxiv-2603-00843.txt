Uncertainty quantification using importance-sampled quasi-Monte Carlo with dimension-independent convergence rates Thanks: The work of the third author was funded by the Guangdong Basic and Applied Basic Research Foundation grant 2024A1515011876 and 2025A1515011888.
Zexin Pan, Du Ouyang, Zhijian He

Abstract
Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) integration over unbounded domains ℝ s \mathbb{R}^{s} remains challenging due to the high dimensionality of sampling space and the boundary growth of the integrand. In applications such as uncertainty quantification (UQ), the dimension s s can reach hundreds or even thousands. To restore the efficiency of quadrature rules in high dimensions, constructive QMC methods like lattice rules have been successfully developed within the framework of weighted function spaces. In contrast to designing problem-specific quadrature points, this paper proposes transforming the underlying integrand to accommodate the off-the-shelf scrambled nets (a construction-free randomized QMC method) via the boundary-damping importance sampling (BDIS) proposed by [ 37 ] . We provide a rigorous analysis of the dimension-independent convergence rate of BDIS-based scrambled nets while covering a broader class of unbounded functions than that in [ 37 ] . By exploiting the dimension structure of the parametric input random field, the proposed n n -point quadrature rule achieves a dimension-independent mean squared error rate of O ⁡ ( n − 1 − α ∗ + ε ) O(n^{-1-\alpha^{*}+\varepsilon}) on standard UQ problems in elliptic partial differential equations (PDEs), where ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 is arbitrarily small and α ∗ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha^{*}\in(0,1) reflects the regularity with respect to the parametric variables. Numerical experiments on elliptic PDEs with high-dimensional parameters further demonstrate the effectiveness of the method. Key words: Quasi-Monte Carlo, importance sampling, uncertainty quantification, parametric PDE MSC codes: 41A63, 65D30, 97N40

Introduction

In this paper, we study quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) quadrature rules for computing integrals over unbounded domain
s
of the form

\mu=\int_{\real{}^{s}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\prod_{j=1}^{s}\varphi(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},

(1.1)

where
f:\real^{s}\to\real
is a real-valued integrand and
\varphi:\real\to\real
is a probability density function.
Such a fundamental problem roots in many fields, such as financial engineering
[
11
,
23
,
48
]
and uncertainty quantification (UQ)
[
13
,
17
,
20
,
39
]
. Although QMC has the potential to accelerate the convergence rate of Monte Carlo (MC),
its application to high-dimensional functions is still a challenging task because of the often occurring curse of dimensionality. In last decades, researchers made great efforts to understand how high dimensionality could be tackled successfully by QMC methods. On the one hand, the concept of “low effective dimension” plays an important role to explain the success of QMC in applications with high dimensions, meaning that high-dimensional functions may in fact depend mainly on a few leading variables or the interaction of a small number of variables
[
3
,
35
,
46
]
. On the other hand, weighted function spaces were introduced by
[
41
]
for handling high-dimensional problems, in which a set of parameters (namely “weights”) are built into the function space norm to model the relative importance between different subsets of variables.
Under certain conditions on the weights, a class of constructive QMC methods known as lattice rules with component-by-component (CBC) construction achieves a dimension-independent error bound
[
25
]
and has proven successful in many UQ applications, including elliptic partial differential equations (PDEs) with random diffusion coefficients
[
13
,
14
,
20
]
and elliptic inverse problems
[
15
,
38
]
.
The philosophy behind the applications is to choose the weights according to the dimension structure of the underlying integrands and then construct lattice rules via CBC
[
40
]
or fast CBC
[
29
]
that are amenable to high-dimensional problems. It is crucial to learn the dimension structure of the problems at hand. Rather than constructing quadrature points via CBC, our strategy is to transform the underlying integrands via dimension information-based importance sampling (IS), which can then be easily handled by off-the-shelf QMC methods such as scrambled nets
[
30
,
31
]
. This paper builds upon the boundary-damping IS recently proposed by the present authors
[
37
]
. We first establish an improved bound on the dimension-independent convergence rate of scrambled net integration over
s
under the boundary-damping IS, and then generalize the results to elliptic
PDEs with general random diffusion coefficients and weighted function spaces.

Throughout this paper, we use
\boldsymbol{x}
for coordinates in
s
and
\boldsymbol{u}
for coordinates in
\mathbb{I}^{s}
, where
\mathbb{I}=(0,1)
.
To estimate
\mu
in (
1.1
), we consider IS of the form

\hat{\mu}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}w(\boldsymbol{u}_{i})f\circ T(\boldsymbol{u}_{i}),

(1.2)

where
T(\boldsymbol{u}):\mathbb{I}^{s}\to\real^{s}
is called the transport map,
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{u}_{n-1}\}\subseteq\mathbb{I}^{s}
is a set of quadrature points, and
w(\cdot)
is known as likelihood ratio or
weight function
. The transport map allows us to generate the proposal via standard uniform distribution tailored to QMC quadrature rules. In this paper, we focus on using scrambled digital nets as the quadrature points following the setting of randomized QMC (RQMC) for ease of error estimation
[
22
,
34
]
.

Pan et al.
[
37
]
considered transport maps with independent components, i.e.,
T(\boldsymbol{u})=(T_{1}(u_{1}),\dots,T_{s}(u_{s}))
. If the transport map
T_{j}:\mathbb{I}\to\real
is differentiable, the weight function then has the product form

w(\boldsymbol{u})=\prod_{j=1}^{s}w_{j}(u_{j})\quad\text{for}\quad w_{j}(u)=T_{j}^{\prime}(u)\varphi\circ T_{j}(u).

We thus have

T_{j}(u)=\Phi^{-1}\Big(\int_{0}^{u}w_{j}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\Big),

(1.3)

where
\Phi(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
be the cumulative distribution function (CDF) and
\Phi^{-1}:\mathbb{I}\to\real
be the inverse CDF (quantile function). Rather than choosing IS proposals from a family of distributions as in the setting of MC
[
11
,
34
]
, Pan et al.
[
37
]
proposed to choose weights
w_{j}(\cdot)
from a set of parametrized functions, i.e.,
w_{j}(u)=w_{\theta_{j}}(u)
with
\theta_{j}\in(0,1/2]
. Given the parameter
\theta_{j}
, the transport map
T_{j}
is determined by (
1.3
).
For
\theta\in(0,1/2]
, Pan et al.
[
37
]
particularly took

w_{\theta}(u)=\begin{cases}(1-\theta)^{-1}\eta(u/\theta),&\text{ if }u\in(0,\theta/2]\\
(1-\theta)^{-1}(1-\eta(1-u/\theta)),&\text{ if }u\in(\theta/2,\theta)\\
(1-\theta)^{-1},&\text{ if }u\in[\theta,1/2]\\
w_{\theta}(1-u),&\text{ if }u\in(1/2,1),\\
\end{cases}

(1.4)

and

\eta(u)=\eta_{p}(u)=\begin{cases}2^{-p-2}u^{-p-1}\exp(2^{p}-u^{-p}),&\text{ if }u\in(0,1/2]\\
0,&\text{ if }u=0\end{cases}\text{ for }\ p\geq 1.

(1.5)

We in addition let
T_{j}(u)=\Phi^{-1}(u)
when
\theta_{j}=0
, which is the usual transport map used by inversion methods
[
5
,
10
,
26
,
33
,
47
]
. As shown in
[
37
]
, estimating
\mu
is equivalent to integrating

f^{w}(\boldsymbol{u}):=w(\boldsymbol{u})f\circ T(\boldsymbol{u})

over
\mathbb{I}^{s}
. The weight function
w_{\theta}(u)
damps the boundary growth of the integrand
f^{w}(\boldsymbol{u})
, so that a faster convergence rate of RQMC can be achieved when the original integrand
f(\boldsymbol{x})
grows unboundedly as
\boldsymbol{x}
goes to infinity, hence the name boundary-damping IS (BDIS).
The mean squared error (MSE) convergence rate of the BDIS method is also established when
f\in W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)
with
q=2
or
f\in W^{1,q,\infty}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)
with
q\in(1,2]
, where a smaller
q
indicates more severe boundary growth (see the definitions of function norms in Section
2
). A limitation of the theory developed in
[
37
]
is that the convergence rate does not improve for integrands in
W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)
or
W^{1,q,\infty}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)
with
q>2
, which limits its applicability in UQ problems. It is therefore of interest to investigate the performance of BDIS on integrands with mild boundary growth (i.e., large
q
) or even no significant boundary growth (i.e., arbitrarily large
q
).

The contribution of this paper is threefold. Firstly, we provide an improved upper bound on the dimension-independent convergence of the BDIS method for
f\in W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)
with any
q>1
, making our theory suitable for functions with mild boundary growth. Secondly, we extend our analysis to PDEs with a general parametric input random field. Lattice rule-based QMC methods have proved effective in the context of elliptic PDEs with lognormal random diffusion coefficients
[
13
,
16
]
.
More recently, Guth and Kaarnioja
[
14
]
studied elliptic PDEs with Gevrey regular inputs
[
4
]
, where the parametric inputs follow a generalized
\beta
-Gaussian distribution, and analyzed the QMC integration error for assessing PDE response statistics using randomly shifted rank-1 lattice rules. Our analysis also covers such PDE problems, and shows the BDIS-based scrambled nets with
n
points achieve a dimension-independent MSE rate of
O(n^{-1-\alpha^{*}+\varepsilon})
, where
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrarily small and
\alpha^{*}\in(0,1)
depends on the regularity with respect to the parametric variables. Thirdly, we generalize the convergence results to the setting of weighted function spaces, thereby bridging our method with constructive QMC methods such as lattice rules
[
13
,
14
,
20
]
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
provides the main convergence results, improving those in
[
37
]
. Section
3
gives the proof of the main theorem in Section
2
. Section
4

5

6

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{I}^{s}}|f(\boldsymbol{u})|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}\Big)^{1/q}&0<q<\infty\\
\sup_{u\in\mathbb{I}^{s}}|f(\boldsymbol{u})|&q=\infty\end{cases},

\displaystyle\|f\|_{W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{v\subseteq 1{:}s}\|\partial^{v}f\|^{q}_{L^{q}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}\Big)^{1/q},

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{q}(\real^{s},\varphi)}

\displaystyle=\Big(\int_{\real{}^{s}}|f(\boldsymbol{x})|^{q}\prod_{j=1}^{s}\varphi(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\Big)^{1/q},

\displaystyle\|f\|_{W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{v\subseteq 1{:}s}\|\partial^{v}f\|^{q}_{L^{q}(\real^{s},\varphi)}\Big)^{1/q}.

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{q,\infty}(\real^{s},\varphi)}

To learn the dimension structure of the integrand, we make use of ANOVA decomposition. Following the framework in
[
21
]
, we introduce the generalized ANOVA decomposition. Let
P_{j}:L^{1}(\real^{s},\varphi)\to L^{1}(\real^{s},\varphi)
denote the integration operator

P_{j}(f)(\boldsymbol{x})=\int f(\boldsymbol{x})\varphi(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}\text{ for }\boldsymbol{x}\in\real^{s}.

Notice that
P_{j}(f)
does not depend on
x_{j}
and
P^{2}_{j}=P_{j}
. We further define the iterated integration operator
P_{v}=\prod_{j\in v}P_{j}
. By Fubini’s theorem,
P_{j}
in
P_{v}
can be applied in any order. By convention,
P_{\emptyset}=I
is the identity operator. The ANOVA decomposition of

is given by

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{v\subseteq 1{:}s}f_{v}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(2.1)

where

f_{v}(\boldsymbol{x})=\Big(\prod_{j\in v}(I-P_{j})\Big)P_{1{:}s\setminus v}f(\boldsymbol{x})=P_{1{:}s\setminus v}f(\boldsymbol{x})-\sum_{w\subsetneq v}f_{w}(\boldsymbol{x}).

It is clear that
f_{v}(\boldsymbol{x})
depends only on
\boldsymbol{x}_{v}
and hence we may write
f_{v}(\boldsymbol{x}_{v})
instead.

Lemma 2.2

\displaystyle\|f\|_{q}

\displaystyle=\sup_{v\subseteq 1{:}s}\|f_{v}\|_{W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)},

\displaystyle\gamma_{v}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\|f\|^{-1}_{q}\|f_{v}\|_{W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)}\quad&\|f\|_{q}>0\\

Remark 2.5
.

Under Assumption
2.3
, the CDF
\Phi(x)
and its inverse
\Phi^{-1}(u)
is almost everywhere differentiable and strictly increasing. It is easy to verify the Gaussian density
\varphi(x)=\exp(-x^{2}/2)/\sqrt{2\pi}
satisfies Assumption
2.3
. As shown in
[

]

[
42
]
and the Niederreiter sequence
[

Corollary 2.6
.

Suppose that the
t
-quality parameters
\{t_{\omega}\mid\emptyset\neq\omega\subseteq 1{:}s\}
of the scrambled digital net satisfy (
2.5
) and the ANOVA terms of the function

have adjusted first-order mixed

\gamma_{v}\leq\prod_{j\in v}\Gamma_{j}\ \forall v\subseteq 1{:}s

for
\{\Gamma_{j}\mid j\in 1{:}s\}

Then under the assumptions of Theorem
2.4

,

\mathbb{E}\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f^{w}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i})-\int_{\real{}^{s}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\prod_{j=1}^{s}\varphi(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\Big|^{2}\leq\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{q}}{n^{1+\alpha}}\exp(C_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}m^{\lambda}),

where

C_{\lambda}
is a constant depending on

S_{\lambda}=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\tilde{\Gamma}^{\lambda}_{j}\quad\text{for}\quad\tilde{\Gamma}_{j}=C_{*}b^{(1+\alpha)t_{j}}(\Gamma^{2}_{j}\theta^{-\nu_{\alpha,q}-\varepsilon}_{j}+\theta^{1-\alpha}_{j}).

Proof.

Plugging in the upper bounds for
t_{\omega}
and
\gamma_{v}
into equation (
2.4
), a straightforward calculation shows

\mathbb{E}\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f^{w}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i})-\int_{\real{}^{s}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\prod_{j=1}^{s}\varphi(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\Big|^{2}\leq\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{q}}{n^{1+\alpha}}\sum_{\emptyset\neq\omega\subseteq 1{:}s}m^{|\omega|-1}\prod_{j\in\omega}\tilde{\Gamma}_{j}.

Because

when
x\to 0^{+}
or
x\to\infty

C_{\lambda}
so that
\log(1+x)\leq C_{\lambda}x^{\lambda}

. Therefore,

\sum_{\emptyset\neq\omega\subseteq 1{:}s}m^{|\omega|-1}\prod_{j\in\omega}\tilde{\Gamma}_{j}\leq\sum_{\omega\subseteq 1{:}s}m^{|\omega|}\prod_{j\in\omega}\tilde{\Gamma}_{j}=\prod_{j=1}^{s}(1+m\tilde{\Gamma}_{j})\leq\exp\Big(C_{\lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{s}m^{\lambda}\tilde{\Gamma}^{\lambda}_{j}\Big).

This completes the proof.

is arbitrarily small. To ensure
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\tilde{\Gamma}^{\lambda}_{j}<\infty
for some
\lambda\in(0,1)
, we need

1+\alpha-2\rho(1-\alpha)/(2\alpha/q+1-\alpha)<-1,

or equivalently

F(\alpha):=(q-2)\alpha^{2}-(2q\rho-q+4)\alpha+2q(\rho-1)>0.

Since
F(0)>0
and
F(1)<0
, we have

0<\alpha<\alpha^{*}(q,\rho):=1-\frac{\sqrt{((2\rho-3)q+8)^{2}+24(q-2)}-(2\rho-3)q-8}{2(q-2)}<1.

In conclusion, under the parameter choice (
2.6
), the MSE of
\hat{\mu}
converges at a nearly
O(n^{-1-\alpha^{*}(q,\rho)})
rate with an implied constant independently of the dimension
s
, where

\alpha^{*}(q,\rho)=\begin{cases}\frac{\rho q-2}{\rho q+1},&\text{ if }q\in(1,2]\\
1-\frac{\sqrt{((2\rho-3)q+8)^{2}+24(q-2)}-(2\rho-3)q-8}{2(q-2)},&\text{ if }q>2\end{cases}.

(2.7)

Remark 2.9
.

If
\rho\geq 3/2
, it is clear that

\lim_{q\to\infty}\alpha^{*}(q,\rho)=1.

If
\rho\in(1,3/2)
, we instead have

\lim_{q\to\infty}\alpha^{*}(q,\rho)=2\rho-2.

Suppose that
f
has no significant boundary growth in the sense that
f\in W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)
for every
q\in(1,\infty)
. If
\rho\geq 3/2
, we should set
\theta_{j}=0
and use the usual inversion methods, consistent with the fact that inversion methods attain nearly
O(n^{-2})
MSEs for such integrands
[
10
]
. Otherwise, if
\rho\in(1,3/2)
, we take
\theta_{j}=\theta_{0}j^{-2\rho/(3-2\rho)}
, attaining nearly
O(n^{-2\rho+1})
MSE.
To unify two cases, we conclude that our BDIS attains nearly
O(n^{-1-\min\{2\rho-2,1\}})
dimension-independent MSE if
f\in W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)
for every
q\in(1,\infty)
.

and

The
k

{}_{b}\mathrm{wal}_{k}(u)=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\kappa_{i}v_{i}\right),

(3.1)

where
\mathrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}
. For the multivariate case, the Walsh functions are naturally defined by the product form

{}_{b}\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\prod_{j=1}^{s}{}_{b}\mathrm{wal}_{k_{j}}(u_{j}),\quad\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}.

Note that
\{{}_{b}\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{u})\mid\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}\}
forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{I}^{s})

[
9
]
. For
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{I}^{s})
, we have the Walsh series expanding

f(\boldsymbol{u})\sim\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}}\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k}){}_{b}\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{u}),

where
f\sim g
denotes the equivalence relation for the
L^{2}(\mathbb{I}^{s})
space, and

\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k}):=\int_{\mathbb{I}^{s}}f(\boldsymbol{u})\overline{{}_{b}\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{u})}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}

denotes the
\boldsymbol{k}
-th Walsh coefficient of
f
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we denote
\lceil k\rceil=r
if
k=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\kappa_{i}b^{i-1}
with
\kappa_{r}\neq 0
and
\lceil k\rceil=0
if
k=0
. For
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0}
, we denote
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{k})=\{j\in 1{:}s\mid k_{j}\neq 0\}
and
\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil=(\lceil k_{1}\rceil,\dots,\lceil k_{s}\rceil)
. For
{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}
, we let

\sigma^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\quad\text{for}\quad L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}\mid\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil={\boldsymbol{\ell}}\}.

The next lemma comes from
[
37
, Lemma 2.7]
, which actually collected the results in
[
9
,
12
,
31
,
32
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{I}^{s})
and
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{u}_{n-1}\}
a scrambled
(t,m,s)
-net in base
b\geq 2
with

,

\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(\boldsymbol{u}_{i})-\int_{\mathbb{I}^{s}}f(\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\emptyset\neq\omega\subseteq 1{:}s}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\omega}}\Gamma_{\omega,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\sigma^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}},

(3.2)

where
\mathbb{N}^{v}=\{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{s}({\boldsymbol{\ell}})=v\}
and

\Gamma_{\omega,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\leq\Big(\frac{b}{b-1}\Big)^{|\omega|-1}b^{t_{\omega}}\boldsymbol{1}\{\|{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\|_{1}>m-t_{\omega}-|\omega|\}.

(3.3)

We next divide the proof of Theorem
2.4
into three steps. First, we bound the

-norm of

when

3.1
Step I: Norms of
f^{w}

The next theorem is crucial for characterizing the dependence of
\|f^{w}\|_{W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}
on
\theta_{j},j\in 1{:}s
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Given
\varphi
satisfying Assumption
2.3
,
f\in W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)
for
q>1
,
w_{j}=w_{\theta_{j}}
for
\{\theta_{j}\mid j\in 1{:}s\}\subseteq(0,1/2]
and
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\|f^{w}\|_{W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}\leq\|f\|_{W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)}\prod_{j=1}^{s}C_{\varepsilon,p,q}\theta^{-1/q-\varepsilon}_{j},

where
C_{\varepsilon,p,q}
is a constant depending on
\varepsilon,p
and
q
.

Proof.

A straightforward calculation shows

\displaystyle\partial^{v}f^{w}(\boldsymbol{u})=\Big(\prod_{j\in 1{:}s\setminus v}w_{j}(u_{j})\Big)\sum_{v^{\prime}\subseteq v}(\partial^{v^{\prime}}f)\circ T(\boldsymbol{u})\prod_{j\in v^{\prime}}\frac{w_{j}(u_{j})^{2}}{\varphi\circ T_{j}(u_{j})}\prod_{j\in v\setminus v^{\prime}}w^{\prime}_{j}(u_{j}).

By Hölder’s inequality and
\sup_{u\in\mathbb{I}}w_{\theta}(u)\leq 2
for
\theta\in(0,1/2]
,

\displaystyle\|\partial^{v}f^{w}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{s-|v|}\sum_{v^{\prime}\subseteq v}\int_{\mathbb{I}^{s}}|(\partial^{v^{\prime}}f)\circ T(\boldsymbol{u})|w(\boldsymbol{u})^{1/q}\prod_{j\in v^{\prime}}\frac{w_{j}(u_{j})^{2-1/q}}{\varphi\circ T_{j}(u_{j})}\prod_{j\in v\setminus v^{\prime}}\frac{|w^{\prime}_{j}(u_{j})|}{w_{j}(u_{j})^{1/q}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{s-|v|}\sum_{v^{\prime}\subseteq v}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{I}^{s}}|(\partial^{v^{\prime}}f)\circ T(\boldsymbol{u})|^{q}w(\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}\Big)^{1/q}\prod_{j\in v^{\prime}}I_{1}(\theta_{j},q)\prod_{j\in v\setminus v^{\prime}}I_{2}(\theta_{j},q),

where, by denoting
q^{*}=q/(q-1)
,

I_{1}(\theta,q)=\Big(\int_{\mathbb{I}}\frac{w_{\theta}(u)^{q^{*}+1}}{(\varphi\circ T_{\theta}(u))^{q^{*}}}\,\mathrm{d}u\Big)^{1/q^{*}}

and

I_{2}(\theta,q)=\Big(\int_{\mathbb{I}}\frac{|w^{\prime}_{\theta}(u)|^{q^{*}}}{w_{\theta}(u)^{q^{*}-1}}\,\mathrm{d}u\Big)^{1/q^{*}}.

First, we bound
I_{1}(\theta,q)
. By
[
37
, Lemma 4.3]
, for
u\in(0,\theta)
,

\varphi\circ T_{\theta}(u)\geq c_{\varepsilon,p}\Big(\theta w_{\theta}(u)\min\big((2u/\theta)^{p+1},1\big)\Big)^{1+\varepsilon}\geq c_{\varepsilon,p}\Big(\theta w_{\theta}(u)(u/\theta)^{p+1}\Big)^{1+\varepsilon},

where
c_{\varepsilon,p}
is a constant depending on
\varepsilon
and
p
. When
u\in[\theta,1/2]
, we use Assumption
2.3
to bound

\varphi\circ T_{\theta}(u)\geq c_{\varepsilon}\Big(\Phi\circ\Phi^{-1}\big(\int_{0}^{u}w_{\theta}(u^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}u^{\prime}\big)\Big)^{1+\varepsilon}=c_{\varepsilon}(u-\theta/2)^{1+\varepsilon}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle I_{1}(\theta,q)=

\displaystyle\Big(2\int_{0}^{1/2}\frac{w_{\theta}(u)^{q^{*}+1}}{(\varphi\circ T_{\theta}(u))^{q^{*}}}\,\mathrm{d}u\Big)^{1/q^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big(2\int_{0}^{\theta}\frac{w_{\theta}(u)^{q^{*}+1}}{c^{q^{*}}_{\varepsilon,p}(\theta w_{\theta}(u)(u/\theta)^{p+1})^{q^{*}(1+\varepsilon)}}\,\mathrm{d}u+2\int_{\theta}^{1/2}\frac{(1-\theta)^{-q^{*}-1}}{c^{q^{*}}_{\varepsilon}(u-\theta/2)^{q^{*}(1+\varepsilon)}}\,\mathrm{d}u\Big)^{1/q^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big(2\theta^{1-q^{*}(1+\varepsilon)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w_{\theta}(\theta u)^{1-q^{*}\varepsilon}}{c^{q^{*}}_{\varepsilon,p}u^{q^{*}(p+1)(1+\varepsilon)}}\,\mathrm{d}u+2\int_{\theta/2}^{\infty}\frac{2^{q^{*}+1}}{c^{q^{*}}_{\varepsilon}u^{q^{*}(1+\varepsilon)}}\,\mathrm{d}u\Big)^{1/q^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{1}\theta^{-1/q-\varepsilon},

where
C_{1}
depends on
\varepsilon,p
and
q
, and we have used
w_{\theta}(\theta u)=2^{-p-2}u^{-p-1}\exp(2^{p}-u^{-p})
over
u\in(0,1/2)
in the last inequality.

Next, we bound
I_{2}(\theta,q)
. By
[
37
, Lemma 4.4]
,

|w^{\prime}_{\theta}(u)|\leq c_{p}\theta^{-1}w_{\theta}(u)

for
u\in(0,\theta]
and a constant
c_{p}
depending on
p
. Hence,

\displaystyle I_{2}(\theta,q)=\Big(2\int_{0}^{\theta}\frac{|w^{\prime}_{\theta}(u)|^{q^{*}}}{w_{\theta}(u)^{q^{*}-1}}\,\mathrm{d}u\Big)^{1/q^{*}}\leq

\displaystyle\Big(2c_{p}^{q^{*}}\theta^{-q^{*}}\int_{0}^{\theta}w_{\theta}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\Big)^{1/q^{*}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(2c_{p}^{q^{*}}\theta^{-q^{*}+1}\int_{0}^{1}w_{\theta}(\theta u)\,\mathrm{d}u\Big)^{1/q^{*}}\leq C_{2}\theta^{-1/q},

where
C_{2}
depends on
p
and
q
. Putting the above bounds into equation (
3.4
) and substituting

\int_{\mathbb{I}^{s}}|(\partial^{v^{\prime}}f)\circ T(\boldsymbol{u})|^{q}w(\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}=\int_{\mathbb{I}^{s}}|(\partial^{v^{\prime}}f)\circ T(\boldsymbol{u})|^{q}\prod_{j=1}^{s}\varphi\circ T_{j}(u_{j})\,\mathrm{d}T(\boldsymbol{u})=\|\partial^{v^{\prime}}f\|^{q}_{L^{q}(\real^{s},\varphi)},

we can bound

\displaystyle\|\partial^{v}f^{w}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{s-|v|}\sum_{v^{\prime}\subseteq v}\|\partial^{v^{\prime}}f\|_{L^{q}(\real^{s},\varphi)}\prod_{j\in v^{\prime}}C_{1}\theta_{j}^{-1/q-\varepsilon}\prod_{j\in v\setminus v^{\prime}}C_{2}\theta_{j}^{-1/q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{s-|v|}\Big(\sum_{v^{\prime}\subseteq v}\|\partial^{v^{\prime}}f\|^{q}_{L^{q}(\real^{s},\varphi)}\Big)^{1/q}\Big(\sum_{v^{\prime}\subseteq v}\prod_{j\in v^{\prime}}C^{q^{*}}_{1}\prod_{j\in v\setminus v^{\prime}}C^{q^{*}}_{2}\Big)^{1/q^{*}}\prod_{j\in v}\theta_{j}^{-1/q-\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{s-|v|}\|f\|_{W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)}\prod_{j\in v}(C^{q^{*}}_{1}+C^{q^{*}}_{2})^{1/q^{*}}\theta_{j}^{-1/q-\varepsilon}.

Finally,

\displaystyle\|f^{w}\|_{W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}=

\displaystyle\sum_{v\subseteq 1{:}s}\|\partial^{v}f^{w}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|f\|_{W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)}\sum_{v\subseteq 1{:}s}2^{s-|v|}\prod_{j\in v}(C^{q^{*}}_{1}+C^{q^{*}}_{2})^{1/q^{*}}\theta_{j}^{-1/q-\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|f\|_{W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)}\prod_{j=1}^{s}\Big((C^{q^{*}}_{1}+C^{q^{*}}_{2})^{1/q^{*}}+2\Big)\theta_{j}^{-1/q-\varepsilon}.

∎

3.2
Step II: Bounds on

We need the following two lemmas:

Lemma 3.4
.

For
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
and
g\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{I}^{s})
,

|\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{k})|\leq\|\partial^{1{:}s}g\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}\prod_{j=1}^{s}b^{-\lceil k_{j}\rceil+1}.

Proof.

By
[
37
, proof of Lemma 3.3]
,

\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{k})=\int_{\mathbb{I}^{s}}\partial^{1{:}s}g(\boldsymbol{u})\prod_{j=1}^{s}\overline{\mathcal{I}({}_{b}\mathrm{wal}_{k_{j}})(u_{j})}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u},

where

\mathcal{I}({}_{b}\mathrm{wal}_{k})(u)=\int_{0}^{u}{}_{b}\mathrm{wal}_{k}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

As shown in
[
37
, Example 3.4]
, if
\kappa_{r}b^{r-1}\leq k<(\kappa_{r}+1)b^{r-1}
for
r=\lceil k\rceil
,

\mathcal{I}({}_{b}\mathrm{wal}_{k})={}_{b}\mathrm{wal}_{\mathcal{M}(k)}\mathcal{I}({}_{b}\mathrm{wal}_{\mathcal{N}(k)})

for
\mathcal{M}(k)=k-\kappa_{r}b^{r-1}
and
\mathcal{N}(k)=\kappa_{r}b^{r-1}
, and

\|\mathcal{I}({}_{b}\mathrm{wal}_{\mathcal{N}(k)})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{I})}\leq b^{-r+1}.

Finally, we apply Hölder’s inequality and conclude

|\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{k})|\leq\|\partial^{1{:}s}g\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}\prod_{j=1}^{s}\|\mathcal{I}({}_{b}\mathrm{wal}_{k_{j}})(u_{j})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{I})}\leq\|\partial^{1{:}s}g\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}\prod_{j=1}^{s}b^{-\lceil k_{j}\rceil+1}.

∎

Remark 3.5
.

A similar bound is derived in
[
43
]
given
\partial^{1{:}s}g
is continuous. Our lemma only requires

Lemma 3.6
.

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
and
g\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{I}^{s})
,

\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}|\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\leq C^{s}_{b,\alpha}b^{-\alpha\|{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\|_{1}}\|g\|^{2-2\alpha}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}\|\partial^{1{:}s}g\|^{2\alpha}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I}^{s})},

where
L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}\mid\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil={\boldsymbol{\ell}}\}
and
C_{b,\alpha}
is a constant depending on
b
and
\alpha
.

Proof.

We apply Lemma
3.4
to each
\boldsymbol{k}\in L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}
and obtain

|\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\leq b^{-2\|{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\|_{1}+2s}\|\partial^{1{:}s}g\|^{2}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}.

Because
|L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}|=\prod_{j=1}^{s}(b-1)b^{\ell_{j}-1}

\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}|\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\leq|L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}|b^{-2\|{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\|_{1}+2s}\|\partial^{1{:}s}g\|^{2}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}=b^{s}(b-1)^{s}b^{-\|{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\|_{1}}\|\partial^{1{:}s}g\|^{2}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}.

Meanwhile,

and

\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}|\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\leq\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}.

The conclusion follows by combining the two bounds.
∎

Theorem 3.7
.

Given
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
,
f\in W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)
for
q>1
,
w_{j}=w_{\theta_{j}}
for
\{\theta_{j}\mid j\in 1{:}s\}\subseteq(0,1/2]
and
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\sigma^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}|\hat{f}^{w}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\leq C_{\varepsilon,p,q,b,\alpha}^{|\boldsymbol{s}({\boldsymbol{\ell}})|}b^{-\alpha\|{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\|_{1}}\sum_{v\subseteq\boldsymbol{s}({\boldsymbol{\ell}})}\|f_{v}\|^{2}_{W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)}\prod_{j\in v}\theta^{-\nu_{\alpha,q}-\varepsilon}_{j}\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{s}({\boldsymbol{\ell}})\setminus v}\theta^{1-\alpha}_{j},

where
L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}\mid\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil={\boldsymbol{\ell}}\}
,
\nu_{\alpha,q}=\max(2\alpha/q,2/q+\alpha-1)
and
C_{\varepsilon,p,q,b,\alpha}
is a constant depending on
\varepsilon,p,q,b
and
\alpha
.

Proof.

From
[
37
, Lemma 3.1]
,

\hat{f}^{w}(\boldsymbol{k})=\sum_{v\subseteq\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{k})}\hat{f}^{w}_{v}(\boldsymbol{k}_{v})\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{k})\setminus v}\hat{w}_{j}(k_{j})\quad\text{for}\quad f^{w}_{v}(\boldsymbol{u}_{v})=f_{v}\circ T(\boldsymbol{u})\prod_{j\in v}w_{j}(u_{j})

with
f_{v}\circ T(\boldsymbol{u})
interpreted as a function of
\boldsymbol{u}_{v}
.
Using Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}|\hat{f}^{w}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}2^{|\boldsymbol{s}({\boldsymbol{\ell}})|}\sum_{v\subseteq\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{k})}|\hat{f}^{w}_{v}(\boldsymbol{k}_{v})|^{2}\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{k})\setminus v}|\hat{w}_{j}(k_{j})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{|\boldsymbol{s}({\boldsymbol{\ell}})|}\sum_{v\subseteq\boldsymbol{s}({\boldsymbol{\ell}})}\Big(\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}_{v}\in L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}},v}}|\hat{f}^{w}_{v}(\boldsymbol{k}_{v})|^{2}\Big)\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{s}({\boldsymbol{\ell}})\setminus v}\sum_{k_{j}=b^{\ell_{j}-1}}^{b^{\ell_{j}}-1}|\hat{w}_{j}(k_{j})|^{2},

where
L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}},v}=\{\boldsymbol{k}_{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{v}\mid\lceil\boldsymbol{k}_{v}\rceil={\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{v}\}
.
By identifying
v
with
1:|v|
, Lemma
3.6
implies

\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}_{v}\in L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}},v}}|\hat{f}^{w}_{v}(\boldsymbol{k}_{v})|^{2}\leq C^{|v|}_{b,\alpha}b^{-\alpha\sum_{j\in v}\ell_{j}}\|f^{w}_{v}\|^{2-2\alpha}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{I}^{|v|})}\|\partial^{v}f^{w}_{v}\|^{2\alpha}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I}^{|v|})}.

(3.5)

Theorem
3.2
shows

\|\partial^{v}f^{w}_{v}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I}^{|v|})}\leq\|f^{w}_{v}\|_{W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{I}^{|v|})}\leq\|f_{v}\|_{W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)}\prod_{j\in v}C_{\varepsilon,p,q}\theta^{-1/q-\varepsilon}_{j}.

If
q\geq 2
, we use
[
37
, Lemma 3.1]
to bound

\|f^{w}_{v}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{I}^{|v|})}\leq\|f^{w}_{v}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{I}^{|v|})}\leq 2^{\frac{q-1}{q}|v|}\|f_{v}\|_{L^{q}(\real^{s},\varphi)}\leq 2^{\frac{q-1}{q}|v|}\|f_{v}\|_{W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)}.

If

, we use Lemma
2.1
and the interpolation inequality
[

to bound

\displaystyle\|f^{w}_{v}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{I}^{|v|})}\leq

\displaystyle\|f^{w}_{v}\|^{q/2}_{L^{q}(\mathbb{I}^{|v|})}\|f^{w}_{v}\|^{1-q/2}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{I}^{|v|})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{\frac{q-1}{2}|v|}\|f_{v}\|^{q/2}_{L^{q}(\real^{s},\varphi)}\|f^{w}_{v}\|^{1-q/2}_{W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{I}^{|v|})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{\frac{q-1}{2}|v|}\|f_{v}\|^{q/2}_{W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)}\Big(\|f_{v}\|_{W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)}\prod_{j\in v}C_{\varepsilon,p,q}\theta^{-1/q-\varepsilon}_{j}\Big)^{1-q/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|f_{v}\|_{W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)}\prod_{j\in v}2^{\frac{q-1}{2}}C^{1-q/2}_{\varepsilon,p,q}\theta^{-1/q+1/2-(1-q/2)\varepsilon}_{j}.

Putting the above bounds into equation (

), we get

\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}_{v}\in L_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}},v}}|\hat{f}^{w}_{v}(\boldsymbol{k}_{v})|^{2}\leq C_{3}^{|v|}b^{-\alpha\sum_{j\in v}\ell_{j}}\|f_{v}\|^{2}_{W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)}\prod_{j\in v}\theta^{-\nu_{\alpha,q}-\varepsilon}_{j},

where

depends on
\varepsilon,p,q,b
and
\alpha
. Finally,
[

, Lemma 3.2]
implies
|\hat{w}_{j}(k_{j})|\leq 4\min(\theta_{j},b^{-\ell_{j}+1})
and

\sum_{k_{j}=b^{\ell_{j}-1}}^{b^{\ell_{j}}-1}|\hat{w}_{j}(k_{j})|^{2}\leq 16(b-1)\min(\theta^{2}_{j}b^{\ell_{j}-1},b^{-\ell_{j}+1})\leq 16(b-1)\theta^{1-\alpha}_{j}b^{-\alpha(\ell_{j}-1)}.

We therefore conclude that

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\leq

Application to parameterized PDEs

4.1
Model setting

Consider an elliptic PDEs with a random diffusion coefficient given by

\displaystyle-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\cdot(a(\boldsymbol{y},\omega)\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}p(\boldsymbol{y},\omega))

\displaystyle=g(\boldsymbol{y})\ \text{in }D,

(4.1)

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{y},\omega)

\displaystyle=0\text{ on }\partial D,

with almost every
\omega\in\Omega
in a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
,
where
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

\partial D

be the dual space of

. The duality pairing between

and

as a finite sum parameterized by
\boldsymbol{x}\in\real^{s}

(4.2)

\boldsymbol{y}\in D

Following
[
14

\beta
-Gaussian random variables with density

\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{2\beta^{1/\beta}\Gamma(1+1/\beta)}e^{-\frac{|x|^{\beta}}{\beta}}\text{ with }\beta>0.

For

Given

More generally, we assume that for any

\|\partial^{v}p(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\|_{H_{0}^{1}(D)}\lesssim(|v|!)^{\sigma}\left(\prod_{j\in v}b_{j}\right)\exp\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\alpha_{j}|x_{j}|^{\tau}\right\},

(4.4)

with

,

,

independently following a standard Gaussian distribution, we have

Remark 4.3

For the standard Gaussian distribution, it is known that its Mills ratio satisfies

Lemma 4.6

The proof is inspired by

\theta_{j}=0
). We use scrambled Sobol’ sequences
[
30
]
for RQMC methods. For the BDIS methods, we take
p=1
in (

) and compare several choices of
w_{j}=w_{\theta_{j}}
.

Now consider the elliptic PDE problem (
4.1
) on the unit square domain
D=(0,1)^{2}
with a deterministic source term given by
g(\boldsymbol{y})=y_{2}
for
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},y_{2})
. The diffusion coefficient
a(\boldsymbol{y},\omega)
follows a truncated random field model:

a(\boldsymbol{y},\omega)=a_{s}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})=\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{s}x_{j}\xi_{j}(\boldsymbol{y})\right),

(6.1)

where

are independent
\beta
-Gaussian random variables with density given by (

(6.2)

where
\zeta>0
and
j=1,\dots,s
.
The parameter
\rho^{*}>1
controls the decay rate of the fluctuations, thereby determining the decay rate of
\gamma_{v}
. As shown in
[
13
]
, this model satisfies (
4.4
) with
\tau=\sigma=1

\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{\infty}=\zeta
and

The spatial domain is discretized using the finite element method with continuous piecewise linear (

7
Concluding remarks

As a follow-up study of BDIS, this paper derives an improved error bound for scrambled digital nets and establishes a dimension-independent error rate for UQ problems in elliptic PDEs with random diffusion coefficients. The analysis is further extended to weighted function spaces, thereby bridging the proposed method with existing literature on constructive QMC approaches.

A natural extension of this work is to investigate whether BDIS can be combined with constructive methods such as lattice rules. Theorem
3.2
provides an estimate of the
W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{I}^{s})
-norm of the modified integrand
f^{w}
in terms of the
W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\real^{s},\varphi)
-norm of the original integrand
f
, which itself is bounded as in Theorem
4.7
. These estimates could be used to design suitable weights for constructing lattice rules. An interesting research direction is to explore whether jointly optimizing the transport maps
T_{j}
and the lattice rules can lead to improved convergence and tractability.

Our analysis builds on first-order mixed derivatives of the integrand. A natural question is whether higher-order QMC rules
[
6
]
can be combined with BDIS by exploiting higher-order derivatives. A new transport map is required to eliminate the discontinuities in the second derivative of
T_{j}
defined by (
1.3
) and (
1.4
). We anticipate that a suitable construction would achieve convergence rates matching those of Möbius-transformed QMC rules
[
19
,
44
]
. We leave this question for future research.

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
2.1

Proof.

We first establish the result for the univariate case (
s=1
). For any
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{I})
and any
x,y\in\mathbb{I}
, we have

f(x)=f(y)+\int_{y}^{x}f^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Integrating both sides with respect to
y
over
\mathbb{I}
gives

f(x)=\int_{0}^{1}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{y}^{x}f^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}y.

Taking the absolute value yields

\displaystyle|f(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}|f(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{0}^{1}\left|\int_{y}^{x}f^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right|\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I})}+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|f^{\prime}(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I})}+\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I})}=\|f\|_{W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{I})}.

Taking the supremum over
x\in\mathbb{I}
gives the desired inequality
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{I})}\leq\|f\|_{W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{I})}
.

For the multivariate case (
s>1
), we prove it by induction. Assume the inequality holds for all dimensions less than
s
. Let
\boldsymbol{u}=(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime},u_{s})\in\mathbb{I}^{s}
, where
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{I}^{s-1}
and
u_{s}\in[0,1]
. Applying the univariate inequality with respect to the variable
u_{s}
while holding
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}
fixed yields

|f(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime},u_{s})|\leq\int_{0}^{1}|f(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime},t)|\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{s}f(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime},t)|\,\mathrm{d}t.

Taking the supremum over
\boldsymbol{u}=(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime},u_{s})\in\mathbb{I}^{s}
, we have

\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{I}^{s}}|f(\boldsymbol{u})|\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{I}^{s-1}}\int_{0}^{1}|f(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime},t)|\,\mathrm{d}t+\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{I}^{s-1}}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{s}f(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime},t)|\,\mathrm{d}t.

(A.1)

By the inductive hypothesis,

\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{I}^{s-1}}|f(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime},t)|\leq\|f(\cdot,t)\|_{W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{I}^{s-1})},\quad\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{I}^{s-1}}|\partial_{s}f(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime},t)|\leq\|\partial_{s}f(\cdot,t)\|_{W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{I}^{s-1})}.

We split the subsets
v\subseteq{1{:}s}
into two groups: those not containing
s
, corresponding to
f(\cdot,t)
, and those containing
s
, corresponding to
\partial_{s}f(\cdot,t)
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|f(\cdot,t)\|_{W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{I}^{s-1})}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{v\subseteq\{1{:}s-1\}}\int_{\mathbb{I}^{s-1}}|\partial^{v}_{\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}}f(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime},t)|\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\subseteq\{1{:}s-1\}}\int_{\mathbb{I}^{s}}|\partial^{v}f(\boldsymbol{u})|\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u},

and similarly,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\|\partial_{s}f(\cdot,t)\|_{W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{I}^{s-1})}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{v\subseteq\{1{:}s-1\}}\int_{\mathbb{I}^{s-1}}|\partial^{v}_{\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}}\partial_{s}f(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime},t)|\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\subseteq\{1{:}s-1\}}\int_{\mathbb{I}^{s}}|\partial^{v}(\partial_{s}f)(\boldsymbol{u})|\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}.

Substituting these expressions into (
A.1
) shows that the sum of the two terms covers all subsets
v\subseteq{1{:}s}
:

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\subseteq\{1{:}s\}\\
s\notin v\end{subarray}}\|\partial^{v}f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\subseteq\{1{:}s\}\\
s\in v\end{subarray}}\|\partial^{v}f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}=\|f\|_{W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{I}^{s})}.

∎

Appendix B
Proof of Lemma
4.2

Proof.

First note that for
f\in W^{1,q}(\real,\varphi)
,

\|f-\mu(f)\|_{L^{q}(\real,\varphi)}\leq\|f-f(0)\|_{L^{q}(\real,\varphi)}+\|\mu(f)-f(0)\|_{L^{q}(\real,\varphi)}\leq 2\|f-f(0)\|_{L^{q}(\real,\varphi)},

where in the second inequality, we used the fact that

\displaystyle\|\mu(f)-f(0)\|_{L^{q}(\real,\varphi)}=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}(f(x)-f(0))\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)-f(0)|^{q}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/q}=\|f-f(0)\|_{L^{q}(\real,\varphi)}.

Consequently, to prove the Poincaré inequality (
4.5
), it suffices to prove the following weighted Hardy inequality:

\int\left|\int_{0}^{x}f^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right|^{q}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C_{q}\int|f^{\prime}(x)|^{q}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
C_{q}>0
is a constant independent of
f(x)
.

Denote
g=f^{\prime}
. Due to the symmetry of
\varphi
, it suffices to prove

\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left|\int_{x}^{0}g(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right|^{q}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C\int_{-\infty}^{0}|g(x)|^{q}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

According to
[
24
]
, this inequality holds if and only if

B^{-}:=\sup_{x<0}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{x}\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\left(\int_{x}^{0}\varphi(t)^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{q-1}{q}}<\infty.

(B.1)

Because the Mills’s ratio
m(x)
equals
\Phi(-x)/\varphi(-x)
for
x\geq 0
, our assumption implies there exists a constant
C>0
satisfying
\varphi(t)^{-1}\leq C\Phi(t)^{-1}
for all
t\leq 0
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{0}\varphi(t)^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{x}^{0}\varphi(t)^{-\frac{q}{q-1}}\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C^{\frac{q}{q-1}}\int_{x}^{0}\Phi(t)^{-\frac{q}{q-1}}\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=C^{\frac{q}{q-1}}\int_{x}^{0}\Phi(t)^{-\frac{q}{q-1}}\,\mathrm{d}\Phi(t)

\displaystyle=C^{\frac{q}{q-1}}(q-1)\left[\Phi(x)^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}-\Phi(0)^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}\right].

Substituting this bound into (
B.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle B^{-}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x<0}\Phi(x)^{\frac{1}{q}}\cdot\left(C^{\frac{q}{q-1}}(q-1)\Phi(x)^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}\right)^{\frac{q-1}{q}}=C(q-1)^{\frac{q-1}{q}}<\infty.

This proves the desired result.
∎
