DARLING: Detection Augmented Reinforcement Learning with Non-Stationary Guarantees
Argyrios Gerogiannis, Yu-Han Huang, Venugopal V. Veeravalli

Abstract
We study model-free reinforcement learning (RL) in non-stationary finite-horizon episodic Markov decision processes (MDPs) without prior knowledge of the non-stationarity. We focus on the piecewise-stationary (PS) setting, where both the reward and transition dynamics can change an arbitrary number of times. We propose Detection Augmented Reinforcement Learning (DARLING), a modular wrapper for PS-RL that applies to both tabular and linear MDPs, without knowledge of the changes. Under certain change-point separation and reachability conditions, DARLING improves the best available dynamic regret bounds in both settings and yields strong empirical performance. We further establish the first minimax lower bounds for PS-RL in tabular and linear MDPs, showing that DARLING is the first nearly optimal algorithm. Experiments on standard benchmarks demonstrate that DARLING consistently surpasses the state-of-the-art methods across diverse non-stationary scenarios.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) studies sequential decision making in unknown environments, typically modeled as Markov decision processes (MDPs), with the goal of maximizing cumulative reward
(
40
)
.
Most RL algorithms assume a
stationary
environment, where rewards and transition dynamics are fixed but unknown, implying a time-invariant optimal policy.
In many real-world applications, however, this assumption is violated: environments evolve due to changing populations, markets, or physical conditions.
Such non-stationarity is central to applications including clinical treatment planning
(
39
)
, real-time bidding
(
5
)
, inventory management
(
1
)
, and traffic control
(
7
)
.
In these settings, stationary guarantees no longer apply and performance can degrade significantly
(
34
)
, motivating the development of reliable algorithms for
non-stationary
(NS) RL.

Non-stationarity is often divided into two regimes:
drifting
non-stationarity, where the MDP evolves gradually, and
abrupt
non-stationarity, where the environment changes at discrete times. The latter is captured by the
piecewise stationary
(PS) model, in which the MDP remains stationary on contiguous segments separated by change-points. While drifting models have received substantial attention
(
33
;
11
;
41
;
46
;
31
;
14
)
, the PS setting remains comparatively under-explored in RL
(
16
)
. At the same time, recent results in the NS bandit literature indicate that algorithms designed for PS can be empirically robust even under drift and on experiments that deviate from strict piecewise stationarity, outperforming approaches explicitly tuned for drifting settings
(
18
)
. This suggests that the PS model can yield effective methods beyond the nominal regime of validity.

Existing NS-RL algorithms differ along three key dimensions: (i)
prior knowledge
of the non-stationarity (e.g., change frequency or variation budgets), (ii) the
adaptation mechanism
used to respond to changes, and (iii) whether the method is
model-based
or
model-free
. Model-based approaches attempt to track the underlying dynamics as the environment evolves; while theoretically appealing, they can incur substantial computational and memory overhead and may degrade under drift due to model mis-specification and estimation error
(
11
;
31
)
. To this end, we focus on
model-free
methods.

Within NS-RL, the dominant design axis is the adaptation mechanism, which yields three widely used paradigms: (i)
discounted/sliding window
methods
(
16
;
11
;
41
;
14
)
, (ii)
budget-restart
methods
(
26
;
33
;
31
;
46
)
, and (iii)
detection-restart
methods
(
43
)
. Discounted and sliding-window approaches are
adaptive
, continuously down-weighting or discarding older data, whereas budget-restart and detection-restart approaches are
restarting
strategies that periodically or conditionally reset the learning process. These paradigms are prevalent in the NS multi-armed bandit literature, which has served as a canonical testbed for studying non-stationarity in online learning
(
17
;
3
;
4
)
. An additional discussion on the NS bandit literature is given in the Appendix.

Among these paradigms, detection-restart methods are distinctive in enabling
prior-free
design with optimal guarantees: they do not require knowledge of the timing, frequency, or magnitude of changes, in contrast to discounted, sliding-window, and budget-restart methods whose performance depends on tuned parameters that encode such prior information. Recent results further suggest that restarting strategies can enjoy more favorable worst-case complexity guarantees than fully adaptive schemes
(
35
)
.

Despite the appeal of
prior-free
,
model-free
detection–restart, a theory–practice gap remains. To our knowledge, MASTER
(
43
)
is the only algorithm in this class with order-optimal guarantees, yet recent empirical work shows that its internal detection can be practically unreliable, leading to performance far worse than competing alternatives
(
19
;
18
)
. On the theory side, to our knowledge, minimax lower bounds for PS episodic MDPs have been unavailable, obscuring the difficulty of PS-RL and the optimality landscape. These gaps motivate new PS-RL methods that are simultaneously prior-free, theoretically grounded, and empirically robust.

Contributions
We propose DARLING, a modular and
prior-free
detection-restart framework for PS episodic MDPs. DARLING combines effective mean-shift detection ideas
(
4
;
18
;
24
)
with two tests tailored to identify changes in the reward functions and transition dynamics, and can augment
any
base RL algorithm with order-optimal stationary regret to lift its guarantees to the PS setting. We instantiate DARLING for tabular and linear MDPs, obtaining unified prior-free algorithms with near-optimal dynamic regret under piecewise stationarity. We also derive the
first
, to our knowledge, minimax dynamic-regret lower bounds for PS episodic MDPs in both regimes, and show that under reachability and change-point separation conditions our algorithms are
nearly-optimal
, improving upon the best known prior-free guarantees. Finally, we evaluate extensively on PS and drifting benchmarks against state-of-the-art prior-free and prior-based baselines, where DARLING consistently outperforms existing methods and remains robust beyond its nominal regime.

Interaction Protocol
We study episodic RL over
T
episodes with horizon
H
. We index time by
(t,h)
for episode
t\in[T]
and step
h\in[H]
. The NS environment is an episodic MDP with state space
\mathcal{S}
(
|\mathcal{S}|=S
), action space
\mathcal{A}
(
|\mathcal{A}|=A
), and step-dependent reward and transition components
\{r_{h}^{t},P_{h}^{t}\}_{t\in[T],h\in[H]}
. At time
(t,h)
, after taking action
a_{h}^{t}
in state
s_{h}^{t}
, the agent observes
R_{h}^{t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\in[0,1]
with mean
r_{h}^{t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})
and transitions to
s_{h+1}^{t}\sim P_{h}^{t}(\cdot\mid s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})
. The episode ends at
s_{H+1}^{t}
.

Value Functions and Bellman Equations
A deterministic policy
\pi:[T]\times[H]\times\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
maps the time index and state space to the action space; we let
\pi_{h}^{t}(s)
denote the action chosen in state
s
at time
(t,h)
. Under policy
\pi
, the value function
V_{h}^{t,\pi}:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and the corresponding state-action value function
Q_{h}^{t,\pi}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
at time
(t,h)
are:

\displaystyle V_{h}^{t,\pi}(s):=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{h^{\prime}=h}^{H}r^{t}_{h^{\prime}}\left(s_{h^{\prime}},\pi_{h^{\prime}}^{t}\left(s_{h^{\prime}}\right)\right)\Bigg|s_{h}=s\right],

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{t,\pi}(s,a):=r^{t}_{h}(s,a)+

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{h^{\prime}=h+1}^{H}r_{h^{\prime}}^{t}\left(s_{h^{\prime}},\pi_{h^{\prime}}^{t}\left(s_{h^{\prime}}\right)\right)\mid s_{h}=s,a_{h}=a\right]

where
s_{h^{\prime}+1}\sim P_{h^{\prime}}^{t}\left(\cdot\mid s_{h^{\prime}},a_{h^{\prime}}\right)
. For brevity, let
P^{t}_{h}V_{h+1}(s,a):=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim P^{t}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)}[V_{h+1}(s^{\prime})]
. The Bellman equations give
V^{t,\pi}_{h}(s)=Q_{h}^{t,\pi}(s,\pi_{h}^{t}(s))
and
Q_{h}^{t,\pi}(s,a)=(r^{t}_{h}+P_{h}^{t}V^{t,\pi}_{h+1})(s,a)
, with
V_{H+1}^{t,\pi}(s)=0
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. There exists an optimal policy
\pi^{\star}
providing the optimal
V_{h}^{t,\star}(s):=\sup_{\pi}V_{h}^{t,\pi}(s)
for all
(s,t,h)
. From the Bellman optimality equation,
V^{t,\star}_{h}(s)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{h}^{t,\star}(s,a)
;
Q_{h}^{t,\star}(s,a):=(r^{t}_{h}+P_{h}^{t}V^{t,\star}_{h+1})(s,a)
.

Linear MDP.
We also consider a class of MDPs called
linear MDPs

(
27
)
, with
discrete state space

\mathcal{S}
. Linear MDPs assume both
P^{t}_{h}
and
r^{t}_{h}
are linear in a known feature map
\phi:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that for any
(t,h)\in[T]\times[H]
, there exist

\mu_{h,t}=(\mu_{h,t}^{1},\ldots,\mu_{h,t}^{d})^{\top}
on
\mathcal{S}
and
\theta_{h,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

P_{h}^{t}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)=\phi(s,a)^{\top}\mu_{h,t}(s^{\prime}),\>\>r_{h}^{t}(s,a)=\phi(s,a)^{\top}\theta_{h,t}.

Without loss of generality, we assume
\|\phi(s,a)\|_{2}\leq 1
for all
(s,a)
, and
\max\{\|\mu_{h,t}(\mathcal{S})\|_{2},\|\theta_{h,t}\|_{2}\}\leq\sqrt{d}
for all
(h,t)
.

Tabular and Linear Equivalence
A tabular MDP is a special case of a linear MDP under the canonical one-hot feature map. For finite
\mathcal{S}
, set
d=SA
and
\phi(s,a)=e_{(s,a)}
where
e_{(s,a)}
is the one-hot vector with the

[\theta_{h,t}]_{(s,a)}=r_{h}^{t}(s,a)
and
[\mu_{h,t}(s^{\prime})]_{(s,a)}=P_{h}^{t}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)
recovers the tabular model.

Dynamic Regret
We evaluate performance using
dynamic regret

(
11
;
31
)
, which compares the agent’s policy
\pi
against the optimal policy for each episode in hindsight:

\mathcal{R}(\pi,T):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(V_{1}^{t,\star}(s_{1}^{t})-V_{1}^{t,\pi}(s_{1}^{t})\right),

where the initial state
s_{1}^{t}
for each episode is selected by an oblivious adversary
(
11
;
31
)
. Thereupon, the goal of the agent is to minimize the dynamic regret with respect to the time-dependent policy
\pi
.

Non-Stationarity Measure
In the PS setting, non-stationarity is naturally measured
per episode
rather than
per step
. We assume that the MDP undergoes abrupt changes at unknown episode-level change-points, with a total of
N_{T}

T

\displaystyle 1=:\nu_{0}<\nu_{1}<\dots<\nu_{N_{T}}<\nu_{N_{T}+1}:=T+1,

denote the change-points. Then,

r_{h}^{t}
and
P_{h}^{t}
remain the same over
\{\nu_{k},\dots,\nu_{k+1}-1\}
and change at
\nu_{k+1}
.
Thus, we now conceptualize our algorithm which aims to solve the problem without the knowledge of
N_{T}
.

3
Our Algorithm

What to Detect

A key challenge in prior-free detection-restart design is deciding
what
signal to monitor so that changes are detected quickly in realistic sample regimes.
The only prior-free algorithm with order-optimal guarantees in our setting, MASTER, detects non-stationarity only
indirectly
by checking whether certain stationary-regret guarantees are violated.
While this principle enables theoretical guarantees, it can be overly conservative in practice, requiring unrealistic number of episodes to detect changes even in favorable instances
(
19
)
, which in turn leads to infeasible restarts and poor empirical performance. DARLING takes a different approach: it detects non-stationarity
directly
by monitoring mean shifts in quantities that
characterize the MDP itself
.
The environment in each episode
t
is fully specified by
\{r_{h}^{t},P_{h}^{t}\}_{h=1}^{H}
; therefore, any change must manifest as a change in at least one of these objects. Thus, we run two dedicated tests: one targeting changes in rewards and one targeting changes in transitions.
This “detect-the-model” viewpoint yields interpretable detection statistics and avoids relying on regret-violation.

How to Detect
Monitoring changes in
\{r_{h}^{t},P_{h}^{t}\}_{h=1}^{H}
directly is challenging because informative triples
(s,a,h)
may be infinite and most may be rarely visited under the behavior induced by the base learner or optimal policy. DARLING addresses this by (i) restricting attention to a finite
probe collection

\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times[H]
constructed from step-dependent exploration supports
\{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{e},h}\}_{h\in[H]}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
and
\{\mathcal{A}^{s}_{\mathrm{e},h}\}_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{e},h},\,h\in[H]}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, and (ii) injecting
probing episodes
at a controlled frequency to guarantee samples for the monitored streams. The step-
h
probe slice is defined as
\mathcal{P}_{h}\coloneqq\{(s,a):s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{e},h},\,a\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{e},h}^{s}\}
and the union of probe slices gives
\mathcal{P}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{P}\coloneqq\bigcup_{h=1}^{H}(\mathcal{P}_{h}\times\{h\})
. Since
r_{h}^{t}(s,a)=\phi(s,a)^{\top}\theta_{h,t}
, choosing
\mathcal{P}_{h}
so that
\{\phi(s,a):(s,a)\in\mathcal{P}_{h}\}
spans
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(or maximal independent) ensures that any reward change that is not orthogonal to this span induces a mean shift in at least one monitored reward stream.

Probing episodes and freezing the base learner

Within segment
k
(after the last restart at episode
\tau
), DARLING designates one probing episode every
\lceil 1/\alpha_{k}\rceil
episodes. In a probing episode, the agent may query the base learner
\mathcal{L}
for its suggested actions, but
does not update

\mathcal{L}
. At step
h
, if
s_{h}^{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{e},h}
, DARLING overrides the base action and samples
a_{h}^{t}
uniformly from
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{e},h}^{s_{h}^{t}}
; otherwise it follows
\mathcal{L}
. The resulting rewards and successor-feature observations are recorded only for change detection. Freezing
\mathcal{L}
preserves modularity: the stationary regret guarantees of
\mathcal{L}
generally rely on its own (on-policy) interaction stream, while probing intervenes on the visitation distribution and can corrupt both theory and practice if used for updates.

Detection histories and monitored streams

DARLING performs change detection by maintaining
scalar
histories for the probe triples
(s,a,h)\in\mathcal{P}
, and applying the same univariate mean-shift detector to each history.
Concretely, whenever a probing episode visits an exploration state
s_{h}^{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{e},h}
and takes a probe action
a_{h}^{t}=a\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{e},h}^{s_{h}^{t}}
, we observe a reward sample
R_{h}^{t}(s,a)
and the next state
s_{h+1}^{t}
.
We then update two families of histories:

Reward histories (Test 1).
For each probed triple
(s,a,h)
, we append the scalar reward sample to

\mathcal{H}^{(r)}_{(s,a,h)}
, whose mean equals the probed reward
r_{h}^{t}(s,a)
.

Transition histories (Test 2).
To detect changes in
P_{h}^{t}(\cdot\mid s,a)
, we monitor shifts in
expected successor features
.
For a reference action
a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
define the successor-feature vector

X^{(t;s,a,h,a^{\prime})}\coloneqq\phi(s_{h+1}^{t},a^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad s_{h+1}^{t}\sim P_{h}^{t}(\cdot\mid s,a).

Since our detector is univariate, we append each coordinate
[X^{(t;s,a,h,a^{\prime})}]_{j}
to the scalar history

\mathcal{H}^{(P)}_{(s,a,h,j,a^{\prime})}
for all
(j,a^{\prime})\in[d]\times\mathcal{A}
. Thus, DARLING reduces PS-RL change detection to detecting mean shifts across a finite collection of scalar streams indexed by
(s,a,h)
(rewards) and
(s,a,h,j,a^{\prime})
(successor-feature coordinates). The mean of the successor-feature is linear in
\phi(s,a)
; hence any change in the underlying transition parameters that is visible on the probe span induces a mean shift in at least one successor-feature coordinate stream (formalized in Section
4.1
).

The Detector

DARLING uses the univariate mean-shift detector
\mathcal{D}
in Algorithm
2
.
Given a history
\mathcal{H}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
, the generalized detection test (GDT) checks whether the sequence exhibits a
mean shift
at
any
candidate split point. Specifically, for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
, it forms empirical means on the two segments,
\hat{\mu}_{1:k}
and
\hat{\mu}_{k+1:n}
(as well as
\hat{\mu}_{1:n}
), and evaluates a generalized likelihood-ratio-type statistic
\mathrm{GTS}_{k}
that quantifies how incompatible the two-segment model is with the no-change hypothesis. If
\mathrm{GTS}_{k}
exceeds a threshold
\beta_{\mathrm{GDT}}(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}})
, the test indicates a detected change. The threshold is calibrated to control false alarms uniformly over time at level
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}
, while
\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
parameterizes the desired detection reliability.
Algorithmically,
every time
DARLING appends a new observation to a history, it runs
\mathcal{D}
on the updated history.
Test 1 applies
\mathcal{D}
to reward histories
\mathcal{H}^{(r)}_{(s,a,h)}
.
Test 2 applies
\mathcal{D}
to successor-feature coordinate histories
\mathcal{H}^{(P)}_{(s,a,h,j,a^{\prime})}
for all
(j,a^{\prime})\in[d]\times\mathcal{A}
.
If
any
monitored history triggers, we set a

restart flag
and at the end of the current episode, the base learner
\mathcal{L}
and all detection histories are reset.

Theoretical Analysis

4.1
On Effective Detection

Effective detection in DARLING relies on repeatedly sampling the finite probe collection
\mathcal{P}
. For each step
h
, DARLING is given exploration supports
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{e},h}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{e},h}^{s}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
(with
N_{\mathrm{e},h}^{s}\coloneqq|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{e},h}^{s}|
), inducing
\mathcal{P}_{h}=\{(s,a):s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{e},h},\,a\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{e},h}^{s}\}
and
\mathcal{P}=\bigcup_{h=1}^{H}(\mathcal{P}_{h}\times\{h\})
. Probing episodes occur once every
\lceil 1/\alpha_{k}\rceil
episodes in segment
k
; within a probing episode, conditional on reaching
s
at step
h
, each
a\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{e},h}^{s}
is selected with probability
1/N_{\mathrm{e},h}^{s}
, so per-stream sample accrual scales as
1/N_{\mathrm{e},h}^{s}
.

Reward detection.
Define the step-
h
probe subspace and its orthogonal complement:

\displaystyle U_{h}\;\coloneqq\;

\displaystyle\mathrm{span}\{\phi(s,a):(s,a)\in\mathcal{P}_{h}\}\ \subseteq\ \mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle U_{h}^{\perp}\;\coloneqq\;

\displaystyle\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ u^{\top}v=0\ \ \forall u\in U_{h}\}.

By design, DARLING constructs probe slices to maximize feature coverage at each step. Formally, we define the probe rank
\rho_{h}\coloneqq\dim(U_{h})\leq d
and require that probe slices are
maximal independent
:

Definition 4.1
.

The probe slice
\mathcal{P}_{h}
is
maximal independent
if
\{\phi(s,a):(s,a)\in\mathcal{P}_{h}\}
forms a basis for
U_{h}
and
|\mathcal{P}_{h}|=\rho_{h}
, i.e., it contains as many linearly independent feature vectors as possible at step
h
.

Throughout, all probe slices
\{\mathcal{P}_{h}\}_{h=1}^{H}
are chosen to satisfy this maximality condition. This principle ensures the best possible identifiability given a reachable state-action space.

Proposition 4.2
.

For probe collection
\mathcal{P}
with maximal independent slices, we have
|\mathcal{P}|=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\rho_{h}\leq dH
. For any step
h
and reward parameters
\theta\neq\theta^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\theta-\theta^{\prime}\notin U_{h}^{\perp}\Rightarrow\exists(s,a)\in\mathcal{P}_{h}:\phi(s,a)^{\top}\theta\neq\phi(s,a)^{\top}\theta^{\prime}.

Remark 4.3
.

If
\rho_{h}<d
, then
\dim(U_{h}^{\perp})\geq 1
. Any change
\Delta\in U_{h}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}
satisfies
\phi(s,a)^{\top}\Delta=0
for all
(s,a)\in\mathcal{P}_{h}
, making reward parameter shifts of the form
\theta\to\theta+\Delta
undetectable from data collected on
\mathcal{P}_{h}
.

In tabular MDPs with
\phi(s,a)=e_{(s,a)}
(so
d=SA
), maximal coverage forces
\mathcal{P}_{h}=\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
for all
h
. In linear MDPs, the goal is
\rho_{h}=d
for each
h
, achieved by selecting
d
probed pairs whose features are linearly independent.

Transition detection.
Since DARLING’s detector operates on scalar streams, we cannot directly monitor the high-dimensional distribution
P_{h}^{t}(\cdot\mid s,a)
. Instead, we monitor changes in
expected successor features
. For a probe
(s,a)\in\mathcal{P}_{h}
and reference action
a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
, define the successor-feature random vector in segment
t
by

\displaystyle X^{(t;s,a,h,a^{\prime})}\;\coloneqq\;\phi(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad s^{\prime}\sim P_{h}^{t}(\cdot\mid s,a).

DARLING applies the univariate detector to each coordinate
[X^{(t;s,a,h,a^{\prime})}]_{j}
for
(j,a^{\prime})\in[d]\times\mathcal{A}
. Since
P_{h}^{t}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)=\phi(s,a)^{\top}\mu_{h,t}(s^{\prime})
, the expected successor feature admits a linear representation. Define

\displaystyle M_{h,t}^{(a^{\prime})}\;\coloneqq\;\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mu_{h,t}(s^{\prime})\,\phi(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})^{\top}\ \in\ \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

Then for any
(s,a)\in\mathcal{P}_{h}
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[X^{(t;s,a,h,a^{\prime})}\right]=\big(M_{h,t}^{(a^{\prime})}\big)^{\top}\phi(s,a)
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Fix
h\in[H]
. Suppose there exist episodes
t\neq t^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
M_{h,t}^{(a^{\prime})}\neq M_{h,t^{\prime}}^{(a^{\prime})}
and the difference has a nonzero projection onto
U_{h}
:

\displaystyle\big(M_{h,t}^{(a^{\prime})}-M_{h,t^{\prime}}^{(a^{\prime})}\big)^{\top}u\neq 0\quad\text{for some}\ u\in U_{h}.

Then there exist
(s,a)\in\mathcal{P}_{h}
and
(j,a^{\prime})\in[d]\times\mathcal{A}
such that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\,[\phi(s^{\prime}_{t},a^{\prime})]_{j}\,\right]\neq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\,[\phi(s^{\prime}_{t^{\prime}},a^{\prime})]_{j}\,\right]
,
where
s^{\prime}_{t}\sim P_{h}^{t}(\cdot\mid s,a)
and
s^{\prime}_{t^{\prime}}\sim P_{h}^{t^{\prime}}(\cdot\mid s,a)
, enabling Test 2 to detect the change.

Remark 4.5
.

If
\rho_{h}<d
, transition changes satisfying
\big(M_{h,t}^{(a^{\prime})}-M_{h,t^{\prime}}^{(a^{\prime})}\big)^{\top}u=0
for all
u\in U_{h}
induce no mean shift on any successor-feature stream from
\mathcal{P}_{h}
and are thus undetectable.

These probe conditions capture identifiability constraints inherent to PS-MDPs, not specific to DARLING. Any algorithm collecting data only from
(s,a)\in\mathcal{P}_{h}
observes rewards through
\phi(s,a)^{\top}\theta
and transitions through feature expectations in
U_{h}
. When
\rho_{h}<d
, changes in directions orthogonal to
U_{h}
are information-theoretically invisible. Thus, without sufficient feature coverage and adequate reachability, reliable detection is impossible for any algorithm.

4.2
On Feasible Detection

Section
4.1
addresses
identifiability
: when
\rho_{h}<d
, certain changes are observationally invisible regardless of sample size. A separate issue is
feasibility
: even when informative probes exist, the agent must reach the corresponding probe states often enough within each stationary segment to collect sufficient detection data. This section formalizes reachability conditions that guarantee feasible detection.

Main reachability assumption.
A standard (yet strong) assumption in the NS linear MDP literature is the following:

Assumption 4.6
.

For each round
t
and step
h
, the transition kernel
P_{h}^{t}
satisfies that for any
(s,a,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}
,
P_{h}^{t}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\geq p_{\mathrm{m}}>0
.

Without this assumption many of the analyses of current methods in the literature are impossible (see e.g.
9
). Even though our analysis still holds under this assumption, we consider the following weaker assumption.

Let
\pi_{\mathrm{U}}
denote the
uniform probing policy
: at step
h
, if the current state
s\in\mathcal{S}_{e,h}
is in the exploration support, it samples action
a
uniformly from
\mathcal{A}_{e,h}^{s}
; otherwise, it samples uniformly from all actions
\mathcal{A}
. For a stationary segment indexed by
t
and fixed initial state
s^{t}_{1}
, let
\mathbb{P}_{t}^{\pi_{\mathrm{U}}}(\cdot)
denote probabilities under policy
\pi_{\mathrm{U}}
and the MDP in segment
t
. Define the step-
h
occupancy of state
s
under the uniform probing policy by
p_{h,t}^{\pi_{\mathrm{U}}}(s)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{t}^{\pi_{\mathrm{U}}}(s_{h}=s\mid s_{1})
.
Let
\mathfrak{B}_{h}
denote the family of
\mathcal{P}_{h}
at step
h
such that:

\mathfrak{B}_{h}\coloneqq\{\mathcal{P}_{h}:\mathrm{rank}\big(\{\phi(s,a):(s,a)\in\mathcal{P}_{h}\}\big)=\rho_{h}\}.

Assumption 4.7
.

There exists
p_{\mathrm{m}}>0
such that for every segment
t\in[N_{T}+1]
, initial state
s_{1}^{t}\in\mathcal{S}
, and step
h\in[H]
,

\inf_{\mathcal{P}_{h}\in\mathfrak{B}_{h}}\ \min_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{P}_{h}}\ p_{h,t}^{\pi_{\mathrm{U}}}(s)\;\geq\;p_{\mathrm{m}}.

Assumption
4.7
ensures that
every
maximal independent probe slice at step
h
contains only states that are reachable with probability at least
p_{\mathrm{m}}
under the uniform probing policy. This condition is weaker than Assumption
4.6
—it only needs to hold for the specific (uniform) exploration strategy DARLING employs. The assumption has two implications:

(i) Prior-free sample collection:
DARLING does not need to know which specific probe slice to use—any maximal independent choice guarantees sufficient visitation under uniform probing. When DARLING triggers forced exploration at step
h
and finds
s_{h}^{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{e,h}
, it samples uniformly from
\mathcal{A}_{e,h}^{s}
. After
n
episodes within a segment, each monitored triple
(s,a,h)\in\mathcal{P}
accrues

\tilde{\Omega}\!\left(\frac{\alpha_{k}}{N_{e,h}^{s}}\,p_{\mathrm{m}}\,n\right)

samples in expectation.

(ii) Connection to tabular MDPs:
In tabular settings with
\phi(s,a)=e_{(s,a)}
, maximal independence requires
\mathcal{P}_{h}=\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
for all
h
. Assumption
4.7
then simplifies to: for every state
s\in\mathcal{S}
and step
h\in[H]
, we have
\mathbb{P}_{t}^{\pi_{\mathrm{U}}}(s_{h}=s\mid s_{1})\geq p_{\mathrm{m}}
—exactly the condition needed for prior-free detection in tabular PS-MDPs.

Probe selection in practice.

In tabular MDPs (one-hot features,
d=SA
), identifiability forces
\mathcal{P}_{h}=\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
for all
h\in[H]
; thus, in probing episodes, whenever the trajectory is at state
s
at step
h
, DARLING samples actions (e.g., uniformly over
\mathcal{A}
) and logs the resulting reward/transition observations for detection.
In linear MDPs, for each step
h
we choose a maximal independent slice
\mathcal{P}_{h}=\{(s_{i},a_{i})\}_{i=1}^{\rho_{h}}
such that
\{\phi(s_{i},a_{i})\}_{i=1}^{\rho_{h}}
are linearly independent (ideally
\rho_{h}=d
), which can be built greedily by adding reachable
(s,a)
whose features increase rank.
During probing episodes, if
s_{h}^{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{e},h}
, DARLING samples
a_{h}^{t}
uniformly from the corresponding probe actions so the associated histories accrue samples; under Assumption
4.7
, any maximal independent choice yields the required coverage.

4.3
On Detector Selection

Any detector aiming to identify distribution shifts inherently requires a certain number of samples, both before and after the change. Ideally, this sample complexity should scale appropriately to avoid negatively impacting the total regret. The stopping time
\tau
of a change detector
\mathcal{D}
denotes the time (episode) at which a change is identified. Let
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}
be the probability and expectation with change-point at
\nu
, and
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
be the ones with no change-point.
The
latency

\ell_{\mathcal{D}}
is the length of time post-change within which a change is declared with a probability
1-\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
, i.e.,
\ell_{\mathcal{D}}
is defined as:

\inf\{t\in[T]:\mathbb{P}_{\nu}(\tau\geq\nu+t)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{D}},~\forall\,\nu\in[m_{\mathcal{D}}+1,T-t]\}

where
m_{\mathcal{D}}
is the length of the pre-change window at which no changes occur.
A detector seeks to minimize
\ell_{\mathcal{D}}
while ensuring low false-alarm probability over horizon
T
, namely
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}(\tau\leq T)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{F}}
with
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\in(0,1)
. To ensure order-optimal regret for DARLING, the detector
\mathcal{D}
must satisfy:

Property 4.8
.

\ell_{\mathcal{D}},m_{\mathcal{D}}=\mathcal{O}\Big(\log T+\log(1/(\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\delta_{F}))\Big)
.

This property has been widely used in the NS bandit, detection-restart literature
(
4
;
18
;
24
)
, due to its good regret properties. Specifically, with
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}=\delta_{\mathrm{D}}=T^{-\gamma}
for any
\gamma>1
, Property
4.8
implies

m_{\mathcal{D}}+\ell_{\mathcal{D}}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
, so detection overhead per stationary segment is polylogarithmic and does not affect the leading-order regret rates.

Regarding the existence of detectors satisfying Property
4.8
, prior work shows that certain variants of the
\mathrm{GDT}
can achieve logarithmic pre-/post-change sample complexity (up to problem-dependent signal terms).
In particular, the Generalized Likelihood Ratio (GLR) and Generalized Shiryaev–Roberts (GSR) tests

(
25
)
satisfy
Property
4.8
. Due to space constraints, we provide the details of the GLR and GSR in the Appendix. We emphasize that DARLING is
detector-agnostic
:
our regret analysis depends only on Property
4.8
, not on any specific implementation of
\mathcal{D}
, Algorithm
2
is presented to enhance clarity.

From sample complexity to episode separation.
Let
N_{\mathrm{e}}\coloneqq\max_{h\in[H],s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{e},h}}N^{s}_{\mathrm{e},h}
.
Property
4.8
is stated in terms of the
number of samples in a monitored stream
.
In DARLING, samples for a fixed probed triple
(s,a,h)
arrive only during probing episodes, and only when (i) the probing-policy trajectory visits
s
at step
h
, and (ii) the probing policy samples action
a\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{e},h}^{s}
(uniformly).
In each probing episode, each monitored stream
(s,a,h)
is sampled with probability at least
p_{\mathrm{m}}/N_{\mathrm{e},h}^{s}\geq p_{\mathrm{m}}/N_{e}
.
Therefore, over
n
episodes in segment
k
, each probed stream receives on the order of
\tilde{\Omega}(p_{\mathrm{m}}\alpha_{k}n/N_{e})
samples with high probability.
Consequently, to guarantee
m_{\mathcal{D}}
pre-change samples and
\ell_{\mathcal{D}}
post-change samples per monitored stream, it suffices that segment lengths scale proportionally.

Definition 4.9
.

Define
m_{k}\coloneqq\lceil 1/\alpha_{k}\rceil\lceil m_{\mathcal{D}}N_{\mathrm{e}}/p_{\mathrm{m}}+(N_{\mathrm{e}}^{2}\log T)/(4p_{\mathrm{m}}^{2})+\sqrt{(m_{\mathcal{D}}\log(T)N_{\mathrm{e}}^{3})/(2p_{\mathrm{m}}^{3})+((\log T)^{2}N_{\mathrm{e}}^{4})/(16p_{\mathrm{m}}^{4})}\rceil
and
\ell_{k}\coloneqq\lceil 1/\alpha_{k}\rceil\lceil\ell_{\mathcal{D}}N_{\mathrm{e}}/p_{\mathrm{m}}+(N_{\mathrm{e}}^{2}\log T)/(4p_{\mathrm{m}}^{2})+\sqrt{(\ell_{\mathcal{D}}\log(T)N_{\mathrm{e}}^{3})/(2p_{\mathrm{m}}^{3})+((\log T)^{2}N_{\mathrm{e}}^{4})/(16p_{\mathrm{m}}^{4})}\rceil
for
k\in[N_{T}]
.

Thereupon, to ensure that there are enough samples between change-points, we consider the following assumption.

Assumption 4.10
.

Assume
\nu_{1}

\geq

m_{1}
and
\nu_{k}

-

\nu_{k-1}

\geq

\ell_{k-1}

+

m_{k}
for
k\in\{2,\dots,N_{T}\}
.

4.4
DARLING’s Regret

Given the probe construction in Section
4.1
, the detector requirements in Section
4.3
, and the feasibility conditions in Section
4.2
, we can now characterize DARLING’s regret.

Theorem 4.11
.

Consider DARLING with a detector
\mathcal{D}
that satisfies Property
4.8
, a stationary RL algorithm
\mathcal{L}
with regret upper bound
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{L}}
concave and increasing with
T
, a probe set
\mathcal{P}
and forced exploration frequencies
(\alpha_{k})_{k=1}^{T}
.
If Assumptions
4.7
,
4.10
hold,
\alpha_{k}=\sqrt{kdH}/(2\sqrt{T}\log^{2}T)
,
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}=\delta_{\mathrm{D}}=T^{-\gamma}
, with
\gamma>1
, then DARLING’s regret satisfies,
\mathcal{R}(\pi,T)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(({N_{T}+1})\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{L}}(T/(N_{T}+1))\right)
.

The proof of Theorem
4.11
is given in the Appendix. In particular, if
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{L}}(T)=c^{\prime}\sqrt{T}
for
c^{\prime}>0
, then Theorem
4.11
yields
\mathcal{R}(\pi,T)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(c^{\prime}\sqrt{N_{T}T})
, showing that DARLING lifts the stationary guarantee of
\mathcal{L}
to the PS setting. By instantiating
\mathcal{L}
with state-of-the-art stationary algorithms, e.g., UCB-MQ
(
32
)
with
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{SAH^{3}T})
in tabular MDPs and LSVI-UCB++
(
20
)
with
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d\sqrt{H^{3}T})
in linear MDPs, we recover the upper bounds in Table
1
. We next complement these guarantees by establishing minimax lower bounds for PS episodic RL.

4.5
Lower Bounds

We provide information-theoretic lower bounds of the dynamic regret to characterize the fundamental limits in PS-RL in finite-horizon tabular and linear MDPs.

Theorem 4.12
.

For any algorithm, there exists an episodic, finite-horizon, tabular PS-MDP such that the dynamic regret of the algorithm is at least
\Omega(\sqrt{SAH^{3}N_{T}T})
.

Proof Sketch
We construct a family of
2^{N_{T}+1}
hard instances indexed by binary vectors, where each MDP contains a waiting state, a root, an
A
-ary tree with
A^{D-1}
leaves, and absorbing states. In each stationary segment
k
, the optimal state-action-step triple depends adversarially on the
k
-th index bit: when
i_{k}=0
it is fixed, while when
i_{k}=1
it is the least-visited triple under any policy, forcing exploration or high regret. We lower bound regret on paired instances differing in one bit using the Bretagnolle–Huber inequality, relating it to KL divergence controlled by visit counts to the least-visited triple. Optimizing the bias parameter and averaging over all instances yields the lower bound. The full proof of Theorem
4.12
is given in the Appendix.

Theorem 4.13
.

For any algorithm, there exists an episodic, finite-horizon, linear PS-MDP such that the dynamic regret of the algorithm is at least
\Omega(d\sqrt{H^{3}N_{T}T})
.

Proof Sketch
We follow the linear-mixture MDP hard instance and reduction of
45
.
We construct a piecewise-stationary MDP with
N_{T}{+}1
segments, where within each segment the value gap in one episode lower bounds (up to constants) the sum of
H/2
independent Bernoulli linear-bandit gaps.
Applying the Bernoulli linear-bandit lower bound in each segment and stage yields per-segment regret and summing over segments leads to the final result. The proof of this theorem is provided in the Appendix.

Hence it is evident that according to Theorems
4.12
,
4.13
, DARLING is nearly-optimal under Assumptions
4.7
,
4.10
.

6
Conclusion

We introduced DARLING, a modular,
prior-free
detection–restart framework for piecewise-stationary (PS) episodic RL. Unlike prior-free baselines that detect changes indirectly via regret-violation, DARLING
detects the model
by monitoring mean shifts in probed reward streams and successor-feature streams, and can wrap
any
stationary RL algorithm with optimal regret, while preserving its on-policy learning guarantees via probing episodes that freeze the base learner. Under identifiability and reachability conditions, and with a broad class of logarithmic-sample-complexity detectors, DARLING attains near-minimax dynamic regret in both tabular and linear MDPs. We also establish, to our knowledge, the first minimax dynamic-regret lower bounds for PS episodic MDPs, showing our rates are nearly optimal. Empirically, DARLING consistently outperforms state-of-the-art prior-free and tuned restart baselines on exploration-hard PS benchmarks and remains robust under drifting non-stationarity, while retaining practical runtime. An interesting direction, would be to adapt the detection mechanism or propose a novel detector to handle uncountably infinite spaces. Finally, future work also includes the extension of DARLING to infinite-horizon MDPs.

Appendix A
Related Work in Non-Stationary Bandits

Non-stationary (NS) bandits are a canonical testbed for studying learning under distribution shift, and they have strongly influenced how NS-RL algorithms are designed and analyzed.
A useful way to organize the NS bandit literature is along two largely orthogonal axes:
(i) the
adaptation mechanism
—
adaptive
methods that continuously emphasize recency versus
restarting
methods that explicitly reset the learner—and
(ii) the extent of
prior knowledge
required about the non-stationarity—
prior-based
(requiring tuned parameters linked to variation/breakpoints) versus
prior-free
(not requiring such tuning).
This taxonomy parallels the dominant paradigms in NS-RL (discounting/windowing, budget-restart, detection-restart), and helps clarify which assumptions are needed to obtain guarantees and practical performance
(
17
;
3
;
4
)
.

Adaptive methods: discounting and sliding windows.
Adaptive approaches track change by continuously down-weighting or discarding older samples, typically via exponential discounting or fixed-length sliding windows.
These methods are conceptually simple and widely applicable, but their performance depends on selecting a discount factor or window length that matches the (unknown) timescale of non-stationarity, rendering them typically
prior-based
.
In NS-MABs, classical examples include discounted UCB and sliding-window UCB
(
28
;
17
)
.
This paradigm has been extended to structured bandits, including NS linear bandits (NS-LBs)
(
10
;
38
;
42
)
, NS generalized linear bandits (NS-GLBs)
(
15
;
36
;
42
)
, and NS self-concordant bandits (NS-SCBs)
(
37
;
42
)
.
Analogous ideas also appear in non-parametric settings such as kernelized bandits (NS-KBs), where recency-weighted or windowed estimators are combined with optimism
(
12
;
47
)
.
Overall, discounting/windowing provides a general-purpose route to adaptivity, but introduces a non-trivial tuning problem: too much forgetting increases variance, while too little forgetting yields bias under shift.

Restarting methods: budgeted restarts.
A second family of approaches explicitly
restarts
the learning process, typically on a schedule designed to control the amount of stale data.
In NS bandits, the most common restarting template is the
budget-restart
strategy, which restarts at predetermined times (or on epochs of increasing lengths) selected using a variation/breakpoint budget.
This yields strong theoretical guarantees when the budget is known or can be tuned, but again is usually
prior-based
.
Representative results include the classical NS-MAB framework in
3
, as well as extensions to structured settings such as NS-LBs/NS-GLBs
(
44
)
and NS-KBs
(
47
)
.
Conceptually, budget-restart trades off two error sources: within-epoch learning (stationary regret) and cross-epoch mismatch (stale data), and the schedule is tuned to balance these terms.

Restarting methods: detection-based restarts.
Detection-restart methods aim to remove the explicit dependence on a known non-stationarity budget by
testing
for change and restarting only when evidence accumulates.
This is particularly natural in abrupt (piecewise-stationary) models, where changes are sparse but impactful.
In NS-MABs, prior-based detection-restart methods include algorithms that rely on thresholds calibrated to the change budget or minimal gap assumptions
(
29
;
6
)
.
More recent prior-free approaches emphasize modular change detection primitives (e.g., GLR/CuSum-type tests) coupled with bandit exploration policies, enabling guarantees without knowing the number/timing of changes
(
2
;
4
;
23
)
.
Beyond MABs, related detection-restart ideas have been developed for richer structured classes, including NS linear and kernelized bandits
(
21
)
and NS contextual bandits (NS-CBs)
(
30
;
8
)
.
At a high level, these methods separate concerns: a base algorithm drives exploration/exploitation within a segment, while a statistical test monitors for distributional shifts and triggers a reset. Importantly, in the detection-based restart literature there exist two black-box, prior-free methodologies which are applicable to all the general bandit settings DAL
(
18
)
and MASTER
(
43
)
.

\displaystyle\zeta_{h}^{t}\coloneqq(s_{1}^{1},a_{1}^{1},\ldots,a_{H}^{1},s_{H+1}^{1},\;s_{1}^{2},a_{1}^{2},\ldots,a_{H}^{2},s_{H+1}^{2},\ldots,a_{h-1}^{t},s_{h}^{t}).

(18)

Then,

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\|\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\mathbf{j}},\pi}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\!\left[\log\frac{\prod_{l=1}^{N_{T}+1}\prod_{t=\nu_{l-1}}^{\nu_{l}-1}\prod_{h=1}^{H}\pi(a_{h}^{t}\mid\zeta_{h}^{t})\,P^{l}_{h,\tilde{\mathbf{i}}}(s_{h+1}^{t}\mid s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}{\prod_{l=1}^{N_{T}+1}\prod_{t=\nu_{l-1}}^{\nu_{l}-1}\prod_{h=1}^{H}\pi(a_{h}^{t}\mid\zeta_{h}^{t})\,P^{l}_{h,\tilde{\mathbf{j}}}(s_{h+1}^{t}\mid s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=\nu_{k-1}}^{\nu_{k}-1}\log\frac{P^{k}_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}},\tilde{\mathbf{i}}}\left(s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}+1}^{t}\Big|s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t},a_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}\right)}{P^{k}_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}},\tilde{\mathbf{j}}}\left(s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}+1}^{t}\Big|s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t},a_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}\right)}\right].

(19)

In step
(a)
, we use the fact that the transition kernel of
\mathcal{M}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}}}
and that of
\mathcal{M}_{\tilde{\mathbf{j}}}
differ only at step
\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}
during the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
stationary segment.
Expanding log-likelihood-ratios yields

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\|\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\mathbf{j}},\pi})

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\sum_{t=\nu_{k-1}}^{\nu_{k}-1}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\!\Bigl[-\log(1+4\varepsilon_{k})\,\mathbf{1}\left\{s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},\ a_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}},\ s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}+1}^{t}=s_{\mathrm{g}}\right\}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{t=\nu_{k-1}}^{\nu_{k}-1}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\!\Bigl[-\log(1-4\varepsilon_{k})\,\mathbf{1}\left\{s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},\ a_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}},\ s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}+1}^{t}=s_{\mathrm{b}}\right\}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=\nu_{k-1}}^{\nu_{k}-1}-\log(1+4\varepsilon_{k})\,\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\bigl(s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},\ a_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}},\ s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}+1}^{t}=s_{\mathrm{g}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{t=\nu_{k-1}}^{\nu_{k}-1}-\log(1-4\varepsilon_{k})\,\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\bigl(s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},\ a_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}},\ s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}+1}^{t}=s_{\mathrm{b}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}\sum_{t=\nu_{k-1}}^{\nu_{k}-1}-\frac{1}{2}\log(1+4\varepsilon_{k})\,\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\bigl(s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},\ a_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{t=\nu_{k-1}}^{\nu_{k}-1}-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-4\varepsilon_{k})\,\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\bigl(s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},\ a_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=\nu_{k-1}}^{\nu_{k}-1}-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-16\varepsilon_{k}^{2})\,\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\bigl(s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},\ a_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-16\varepsilon_{k}^{2})\,\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=\nu_{k-1}}^{\nu_{k}-1}\mathbf{1}\{s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},\ a_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}}\}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-16\varepsilon_{k}^{2})\,\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\!\bigl[n_{k}(\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}},\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}})\bigr].

(20)

Step
(a)
follows from the fact that
P^{k}_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}},\tilde{\mathbf{i}}}(s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}+1}^{t}=s_{\mathrm{g}}|s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},a_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}})/P^{k}_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}},\tilde{\mathbf{j}}}(s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}+1}^{t}=s_{\mathrm{g}}|s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},a_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}}))=1/(1+4\varepsilon_{k})
and that
P^{k}_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}},\tilde{\mathbf{i}}}(s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}+1}^{t}=s_{\mathrm{b}}|s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},a_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}})/P^{k}_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}},\tilde{\mathbf{j}}}(s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}+1}^{t}=s_{\mathrm{b}}|s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},a_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}}))=1/(1-4\varepsilon_{k})
.
Step
(b)
stems from the fact that
P^{k}_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}},\tilde{\mathbf{i}}}(s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}+1}^{t}=s_{\mathrm{g}}|s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},a_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}})=P^{k}_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}},\tilde{\mathbf{i}}}(s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}+1}^{t}=s_{\mathrm{b}}|s_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},a_{\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}}}^{t}=\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}})=1/2
.
Using
\log(1-x)\geq-\tfrac{x}{1-x}
for
x\in[0,1)
gives

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\|\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\mathbf{j}},\pi})\leq\frac{8\varepsilon_{k}^{2}}{1-16\varepsilon_{k}^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\!\bigl[n_{k}(\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}},\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}})\bigr].

(21)

Recall that
L
is the number of leaf nodes. By the definition of
(\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}},\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}},\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}})
in (
8
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\!\bigl[n_{k}(\mathrm{leaf}_{\tilde{\ell}_{\mathbf{i}}},\tilde{a}_{\mathbf{i}},\tilde{h}_{\mathbf{i}})\bigr]\leq\frac{\nu_{k}-\nu_{k-1}}{AL\bar{H}-1},

(22)

and hence

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\pi}\|\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\mathbf{j}},\pi})\leq\frac{8\varepsilon_{k}^{2}}{1-16\varepsilon_{k}^{2}}\,\frac{\nu_{k}-\nu_{k-1}}{AL\bar{H}-1}.

(23)

Combining the Bretagnolle–Huber lower bound with the above KL upper bound yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{i},k}(\pi)+\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{j},k}(\pi)

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon_{k}(H-\bar{H}-D)\,\frac{\nu_{k}-\nu_{k-1}}{2}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{8\varepsilon_{k}^{2}}{1-16\varepsilon_{k}^{2}}\frac{\nu_{k}-\nu_{k-1}}{AL\bar{H}-1}\right).

(24)

Since
H\geq 3D
, we have
H-\bar{H}-D\geq\frac{2}{3}H-\bar{H}
. Then, set
\varepsilon_{k}=[16+8(\nu_{k}-\nu_{k-1})/(AL\bar{H}-1)]^{-1/2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{i},k}(\pi)+\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{j},k}(\pi)\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{16+8(\nu_{k}-\nu_{k-1})/(AL\bar{H}-1)}}\Bigl(\frac{2}{3}H-\bar{H}\Bigr)\,\frac{\nu_{k}-\nu_{k-1}}{2e}.

(25)

Notice that
\varepsilon_{k}\leq 1/4
, ensuring that the transition probabilities remain in
[0,1]
. Recall that
L=A^{D-1}=(S-3)(1-1/A)+1/A
. Then, by setting
\bar{H}=H/3
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{i},k}(\pi)+\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{j},k}(\pi)\geq\frac{H}{6e}\sqrt{\frac{(\nu_{k}-\nu_{k-1})^{2}}{16+8(\nu_{k}-\nu_{k-1})/((S-3)(A-1)H/3+H/3-1)}}.

(26)

There are
2^{N_{T}}
disjoint pairs
(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})
for each fixed
k
, hence

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\{0,1\}^{N_{T}+1}}\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{i},k}(\pi)\geq 2^{N_{T}}\cdot\frac{H}{6e}\sqrt{\frac{(\nu_{k}-\nu_{k-1})^{2}}{16+8(\nu_{k}-\nu_{k-1})/((S-3)(A-1)H/3+H/3-1)}}.

(27)

Without loss of generality, we assume that
T
is divisible by
N_{T}+1
, meaning that
\nu_{k}-\nu_{k-1}=T/(N_{T}+1)
. Thereupon, this implies that,

\displaystyle 2^{-(N_{T}+1)}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\{0,1\}^{N_{T}+1}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{T}+1}\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{i},k}(\pi)\geq\frac{H}{12e}\sqrt{\frac{1}{16/T^{2}+8/T(N_{T}+1)((S-3)(A-1)H/3+H/3-1)}}.

(28)

Consequently, there exists
\hat{\mathbf{i}}\in\{0,1\}^{N_{T}+1}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N_{T}+1}\mathcal{R}_{\hat{\mathbf{i}},k}(\pi)\geq\frac{H}{12e}\sqrt{\frac{1}{16/T^{2}+8/T(N_{T}+1)((S-3)(A-1)H/3+H/3-1)}}.

(29)

Since
\pi
was arbitrary, it follows that for any algorithm (possibly history-dependent) there exists a PS tabular
MDP instance with exactly
N_{T}
changes such that the expected dynamic regret (where
T
is the number of episodes) satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\pi,T)=\Omega(\sqrt{SAH^{3}N_{T}T}).

(30)

This completes the proof.
∎

B.3
Proof of Theorem
4.13

Proof.

Assume
d\geq 4
,
H\geq 4
,
T\geq(d-1)^{2}H(N_{T}+1)/8
, and
N_{T}\geq 0
. Since we construct a single hard instance, we may assume without loss of generality that
H
is even: if
H
is odd, append one dummy step with deterministic transition and zero reward. This increases the horizon by
1
and cannot decrease regret. We consider the standard protocol in the main text indexed by
(t,h)\in[T]\times[H]
, and we take the oblivious adversary to always select the same initial state
s_{1}^{t}=x_{1}
.

Fix change-points
1=\nu_{0}<\nu_{1}<\cdots<\nu_{N_{T}}<\nu_{N_{T}+1}=T+1
and define segment lengths

L_{k}\coloneqq\nu_{k}-\nu_{k-1}
for
k\in[N_{T}+1]
. We set all segments equal:

L_{k}=L\coloneqq\frac{T}{N_{T}+1}\qquad\forall k\in[N_{T}+1].

By the assumption on
T
, we have
L\geq(d-1)^{2}H/8
. Define the amplitude

\Delta\coloneqq\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{HL}}=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{N_{T}+1}{HT}}.

For each segment
k\in[N_{T}+1]
, define parameters

\mu^{(k)}=\big(\mu^{(k)}_{1},\ldots,\mu^{(k)}_{H}\big),\qquad\mu^{(k)}_{h}\in\{-\Delta,+\Delta\}^{d-1}\ \ \forall h\in[H/2],\qquad\mu^{(k)}_{h}=\mathbf{0}\ \ \forall h>H/2,

and write
\mu=(\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\mu^{(N_{T}+1)})
.

We now define a piecewise-stationary episodic MDP
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
(as in the hard instance of

(
45
;
22
)
). Let the state space be

\mathcal{S}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{H},x_{H+1},x_{H+2}\},\qquad s_{1}^{t}=x_{1}\ \ \forall t\in[T],

so
S=H+2
. Let the action space be
\mathcal{A}=\{-1,+1\}^{d-1}
, so
A=2^{d-1}
. Let
\iota\coloneqq 1/H
.
Define deterministic rewards (hence
R_{h}^{t}(s,a)=r_{h}^{t}(s,a)
):

r_{h}^{t}(s,a)=\mathbf{1}\{s=x_{H+2}\},\qquad\forall(t,h)\in[T]\times[H],\ \forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},

and let
x_{H+1}
and
x_{H+2}
be absorbing. For each segment
k\in[N_{T}+1]
, for all episodes

t\in\{\nu_{k-1},\ldots,\nu_{k}-1\}
and all steps
h\in[H]
, define the transitions from
s=x_{h}
by

P_{h}^{t}(x_{H+2}\mid x_{h},a)=\iota+\langle\mu^{(k)}_{h},a\rangle,\qquad P_{h}^{t}(x_{h+1}\mid x_{h},a)=1-\iota-\langle\mu^{(k)}_{h},a\rangle,

(31)

and all other next-state probabilities are
0
; from
x_{H+1},x_{H+2}
the process stays put deterministically. This makes

(r_{h}^{t},P_{h}^{t})
constant within each segment
\{\nu_{k-1},\ldots,\nu_{k}-1\}
, hence the environment is piecewise-stationary
with
N_{T}
changes.

We check that (
31
) defines valid kernels. For
a\in\{-1,+1\}^{d-1}
and

\mu^{(k)}_{h}\in\{-\Delta,+\Delta\}^{d-1}
,
|\langle\mu^{(k)}_{h},a\rangle|\leq(d-1)\Delta
. Using

L\geq(d-1)^{2}H/8
and the definition of
\Delta
,

(d-1)\Delta=(d-1)\cdot\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{HL}}\leq\frac{1}{2H}=\frac{\iota}{2},

so
\iota+\langle\mu^{(k)}_{h},a\rangle\in[\iota/2,3\iota/2]\subseteq[0,1/2]
(since
H\geq 4
) and

1-\iota-\langle\mu^{(k)}_{h},a\rangle\in[0,1]
.

Fix any algorithm
\pi
(as defined in the main text), and let
\mathbb{P}_{\mu,\pi}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mu,\pi}
denote the
induced probability measure and expectation when interacting with
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
. The dynamic regret in your notation is

\mathcal{R}(\pi,T)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(V_{1}^{t,\star}(s_{1}^{t})-V_{1}^{t,\pi}(s_{1}^{t})\right)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(V_{1}^{t,\star}(x_{1})-V_{1}^{t,\pi}(x_{1})\right),

and for each segment
k\in[N_{T}+1]
define

\mathcal{R}_{k}(\pi)\coloneqq\sum_{t=\nu_{k-1}}^{\nu_{k}-1}\left(V_{1}^{t,\star}(x_{1})-V_{1}^{t,\pi}(x_{1})\right),\qquad\mathcal{R}(\pi,T)=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{T}+1}\mathcal{R}_{k}(\pi).

For each episode
t
and step
h
, let
a_{h}^{t}
be the action taken at time
(t,h)
, and define

\bar{a}_{h}^{t}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mu,\pi}\!\left[a_{h}^{t}\mid s_{h}^{t}=x_{h},\ s_{1}^{t}=x_{1}\right]\in[-1,1]^{d-1}.

By Lemma 24 of
(
45
)
, for every episode
t
in segment
k
,

V^{t,\star}_{1}(x_{1})-V^{t,\pi}_{1}(x_{1})\;\geq\;\frac{H}{10}\sum_{h=1}^{H/2}\left(\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\langle\mu^{(k)}_{h},a\rangle-\langle\mu^{(k)}_{h},\bar{a}_{h}^{t}\rangle\right).

(32)

Summing (
32
) over
t=\nu_{k-1},\ldots,\nu_{k}-1
gives

\mathcal{R}_{k}(\pi)\;\geq\;\frac{H}{10}\sum_{h=1}^{H/2}\mathcal{B}_{k,h}(\mu_{h}^{(k)}),

(33)

where

\mathcal{B}_{k,h}(\mu_{h}^{(k)})\coloneqq\sum_{t=\nu_{k-1}}^{\nu_{k}-1}\left(\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\langle\mu^{(k)}_{h},a\rangle-\langle\mu^{(k)}_{h},\bar{a}_{h}^{t}\rangle\right).

For fixed
(k,h)
,
\mathcal{B}_{k,h}(\cdot)
is the pseudo-regret of an
L
-round Bernoulli linear bandit with action set

\mathcal{A}=\{-1,+1\}^{d-1}
and unknown parameter
\mu_{h}^{(k)}\in\{-\Delta,+\Delta\}^{d-1}
(the mean is

\iota+\langle\mu_{h}^{(k)},a\rangle
, and
\iota
is a constant offset).

By Lemma 25 of
(
45
)
(Bernoulli hypercube linear-bandit lower bound), for any algorithm there exists a
choice of
\mu_{h}^{(k)}\in\{-\Delta,+\Delta\}^{d-1}
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\mu,\pi}\!\left[\mathcal{B}_{k,h}(\mu_{h}^{(k)})\right]\;\geq\;\frac{(d-1)\sqrt{L\iota}}{8\sqrt{2}}=\frac{(d-1)}{8\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{L}{H}}.

(34)

We now fix a single hard instance
\mu
by choosing, for every
k\in[N_{T}+1]
and
h\in[H/2]
, a parameter
\mu_{h}^{(k)}

satisfying (
34
), and setting
\mu_{h}^{(k)}=\mathbf{0}
for
h>H/2
.

Taking expectations in (
33
) and using (
34
), for each
k
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mu,\pi}\!\left[\mathcal{R}_{k}(\pi)\right]\geq\frac{H}{10}\sum_{h=1}^{H/2}\mathbb{E}_{\mu,\pi}\!\left[\mathcal{B}_{k,h}(\mu_{h}^{(k)})\right]\geq\frac{H}{10}\cdot\frac{H}{2}\cdot\frac{(d-1)}{8\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{L}{H}}=\frac{(d-1)}{160\sqrt{2}}\,H^{3/2}\sqrt{L}.

Summing over
k=1,\ldots,N_{T}+1
and substituting
L=T/(N_{T}+1)
yields

\mathbb{E}_{\mu,\pi}\!\left[\mathcal{R}(\pi,T)\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{T}+1}\mathbb{E}_{\mu,\pi}\!\left[\mathcal{R}_{k}(\pi)\right]\geq(N_{T}+1)\cdot\frac{(d-1)}{160\sqrt{2}}\,H^{3/2}\sqrt{L}=\frac{(d-1)}{160\sqrt{2}}\,H^{3/2}\sqrt{(N_{T}+1)T}.

which completes the proof.
∎

B.4
Proof of DARLING regret bound

Proof.

Consider a piecewise stationary episodic MDP over
T
episodes with horizon
H
and
N_{T}
change-points. Recall that the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
change-point is denoted by
\nu_{k}
, and that
\nu_{0}\coloneqq 1
and
\nu_{N_{T}+1}\coloneqq T+1
.
Over a stationary segment
\{\nu_{k-1},\dots,\nu_{k}-1\}
, the environment remains
stationary in the sense that there exist transition kernels
\{P_{h}^{(k)}\}_{h\in[H]}
and mean reward functions

\{r_{h}^{(k)}\}_{h\in[H]}
such that, for all
h\in[H]
and
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle P_{h}^{t}(\cdot\mid s,a)=P_{h}^{(k)}(\cdot\mid s,a),\qquad r_{h}^{t}(s,a)=r_{h}^{(k)}(s,a).

(35)

Equivalently, one may denote the stationary MDP over the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
stationary segment by
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},H,P^{(k)},r^{(k)})
with

P^{(k)}=\{P_{h}^{(k)}\}_{h\in[H]}
and
r^{(k)}=\{r_{h}^{(k)}\}_{h\in[H]}
. Let
\tau_{k}
be the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
episode at which DARLING restarts, i.e., for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\tau_{k}\coloneqq\inf\{t>\tau_{k-1}:\textbf{Restart}=\texttt{True}\}

(36)

with
\tau_{0}\coloneqq 0
. We then define the following events:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{\forall\,l\in[k-1],\;\tau_{l}\in\left\{\nu_{l},\dots,\nu_{l}+\ell_{l}-1\right\}\right\}\cap\left\{\tau_{k}>\nu_{k}\right\},\,k\in\left[N_{T}\right].

(37)

The event
\mathcal{G}_{k}
represents the “good event” up to the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
restart time-step
\mathcal{G}_{k}
in which the first
k
changes are detected within the latencies
\ell_{l}
’s.
For notational convenience, we define
\mathcal{G}_{0}
to be the universal space. In this section, let
\mathbb{E}
denote the expectation under the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
induced by executing policy
\pi
on the NS episodic MDP, and let
\pi^{*}
be the optimal policy over the piecewise stationary episodic MDP. For brevity and clarity of the notations, we omitted the conditioning on
s_{1}^{t}
’s, as
s_{1}^{t}
are fixed states chosen by an oblivious adversary. Then, we have the following:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\pi,T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(V_{1}^{t,\star}(s_{1}^{t})-V_{1}^{t,\pi}(s_{1}^{t})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{T}+1}\sum_{t=\nu_{k-1}}^{\nu_{k}-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{h=1}^{H}r^{t}_{h}\left(s_{h},(\pi^{*})_{h}^{t}\left(s_{h}\right)\right)-r^{t}_{h}\left(s_{h},\pi_{h}^{t}\left(s_{h}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{T}+1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=\nu_{k-1}}^{\nu_{k}-1}\sum_{h=1}^{H}r^{t}_{h}\left(s_{h},(\pi^{*})_{h}^{t}\left(s_{h}\right)\right)-r^{t}_{h}\left(s_{h},\pi_{h}^{t}\left(s_{h}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{T}+1}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{G}^{c}_{k}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=\nu_{k-1}}^{\nu_{k}-1}\sum_{h=1}^{H}r^{t}_{h}\left(s_{h},(\pi^{*})_{h}^{t}\left(s_{h}\right)\right)-r^{t}_{h}\left(s_{h},\pi_{h}^{t}\left(s_{h}\right)\right)\Bigg|\mathcal{G}^{c}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\kern 5.0pt+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{T}+1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\left\{\mathcal{G}_{k}\right\}\sum_{t=\nu_{k-1}}^{\nu_{k}-1}\sum_{h=1}^{H}r^{t}_{h}\left(s_{h},(\pi^{*})_{h}^{t}\left(s_{h}\right)\right)-r^{t}_{h}\left(s_{h},\pi_{h}^{t}\left(s_{h}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{T}+1}H\left(\nu_{k}-\nu_{k-1}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{G}^{c}_{k}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{T}+1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\left\{\mathcal{G}_{k}\right\}\sum_{t=\nu_{k-1}}^{\nu_{k}-1}\sum_{h=1}^{H}r^{t}_{h}\left(s_{h},(\pi^{*})_{h}^{t}\left(s_{h}\right)\right)-r^{t}_{h}\left(s_{h},\pi_{h}^{t}\left(s_{h}\right)\right)\right]

(38)

where step
(a)
follows from the fact that the rewards are bounded in
[0,1]
. Now, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{\forall\,l\in[k-1],\;\tau_{l}\in\left\{\nu_{l},\dots,\nu_{l}+\ell_{l}-1\right\}\right\},\,k\in\left[N_{T}\right].

(39)

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{G}^{c}_{k}\right)
is upper bounded by the following modified union bound, which decomposes the bad event into false alarm events and late detection events:

C.1
General Formulations of GLR and GSR

For completeness, we summarize the general forms of the Generalized Likelihood Ratio (GLR) and Generalized Shiryaev–Roberts (GSR) tests for sequential change detection.
Let
(X_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of real-valued observations generated from a parametric family
\{f_{\theta}:\theta\in\mathbb{R}\}
.
Both tests compare the no-change hypothesis (a single parameter
\theta
for all samples) against the single change-point alternative (parameters
\theta_{0}
before the change and
\theta_{1}
after).

GLR test.

The GLR stopping time is defined as

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{GLR}}\;\coloneqq\;\inf\Big\{n\in\mathbb{N}:G_{n}\geq\beta(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}})\Big\},

where the GLR statistic is

\displaystyle G_{n}\;\coloneqq\;\sup_{t\in[n]}\;\log\!\left(\frac{\ \sup_{\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}}\sup_{\theta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}f_{\theta_{0}}(X_{i})\prod_{i=t+1}^{n}f_{\theta_{1}}(X_{i})\ }{\ \sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{\theta}(X_{i})\ }\right).

GSR test.

The GSR stopping time is

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{GSR}}\;\coloneqq\;\inf\Big\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\ \log W_{n}\geq\beta(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}})+\log n\Big\},

with statistic

\displaystyle W_{n}\;\coloneqq\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\ \sup_{\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}}\sup_{\theta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}f_{\theta_{0}}(X_{i})\prod_{i=t+1}^{n}f_{\theta_{1}}(X_{i})\ }{\ \sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{\theta}(X_{i})\ }\right).

Anytime-valid threshold.

In the general case, for any target false-alarm level
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\in(0,1)
, we use the threshold

\beta(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}})=6\log\!\big(1+\log n\big)+\frac{5}{2}\log\!\left(\frac{4n^{3/2}}{\delta_{\mathrm{F}}}\right)+11.

(62)

Empirical-mean (Bernoulli / Gaussian) specialization.

For the families used in our experiments and implementations (Algorithms
3
–
4
), following
4
;
23
, the log-likelihood ratio at a candidate split
t\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
admits the closed form

\displaystyle\log\!\left(\frac{\ \sup_{\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}f_{\theta_{0}}(X_{i})\;\sup_{\theta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=t+1}^{n}f_{\theta_{1}}(X_{i})\ }{\ \sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{\theta}(X_{i})\ }\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=t\,\mathrm{kl}\!\big(\hat{\mu}_{1:t},\hat{\mu}_{1:n}\big)+(n-t)\,\mathrm{kl}\!\big(\hat{\mu}_{t+1:n},\hat{\mu}_{1:n}\big),

(63)

where
\hat{\mu}_{a:b}
denotes the empirical mean of
\{X_{a},\ldots,X_{b}\}
.
For sub-Bernoulli observations we use

\mathrm{kl}(x,y)=x\ln\!\frac{x}{y}+(1-x)\ln\!\frac{1-x}{1-y},

and for
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian observations (Gaussian mean-shift proxy),

\mathrm{kl}(x,y)=\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}.

C.2
Experimental Environments

Exploration-hard “combination-lock” benchmarks.

We evaluate on two standard
combination-lock
MDPs
(
31
;
46
)
which are designed to require
deep exploration
: a near-optimal return is only obtained by executing a long, specific action prefix of length
H
, while most other action sequences quickly lead to a “trap” region with small but dense rewards. This creates a strong local optimum (exploiting the trap) that can dominate naÃ¯ve exploration, even though the globally optimal behavior is to keep searching for the rare high-reward chain. In particular, under uniform exploration the probability of reaching the high-reward endpoint scales as
A^{-H}
, so identifying the correct action sequence can be exponentially hard in
H
without principled exploration or structure. We use these benchmarks to stress-test adaptation under both PS and drifting non-stationarity.

Tabular MDP: Bidirectional Diabolical Combination Lock.

We follow the Bidirectional Diabolical Combination Lock construction
31
in which each episode starts from a fixed initial state. The first action routes the agent into one of two “locks” (paths), each a chain of length
H
; along each chain, at every step there is a
unique
correct action that advances to the next state on that path, whereas any of the other
A-1
actions sends the agent to an absorbing sinking state. The MDP is mildly stochastic: even when the agent selects the correct action, the intended transition succeeds with probability
0.98
, and with probability
0.02
the agent still falls into the sink. Rewards are sparse on the optimal behavior: taking correct actions yields reward
0
, and the only large reward occurs at the endpoint of a path (one endpoint gives reward
1
, the other gives
0.25
). By contrast, entering the sink yields a small reward of
\frac{1}{8H}
at the transition step and then a per-step reward of
\frac{1}{8H}
thereafter, making the sink a tempting locally-optimal attractor.

Non-stationarity.
For drifting experiments we use the original gradual protocol in
31
: the routing dynamics at the initial state are linearly morphed over time so that an action that initially reaches path 1 with probability
0.98
(and path 2 with probability
0.02
) is gradually transformed to reach path 1 with probability
0.02
(and path 2 with probability
0.98
), with the symmetric change applied to the other action. For abrupt PS experiments, we use the same endpoint-swap mechanism as
31
, but replace their fixed-period switching with a geometric change-point model
19
: segment lengths are i.i.d. geometric with parameter
T^{-\xi}
for
\xi\in\{0.4,0.6,0.8\}
over a horizon of
T=50000
episodes, and at each change-point the two endpoints swap identities (the
1
and
0.25
rewards exchange). Unless otherwise stated, we use the benchmark parameterization
(
31
)

H=5
,
S=10
,
A=2
, and report cumulative reward averaged over multiple random seeds.

Linear MDP: Synthetic Chain Combination Lock.

We follow the synthetic linear-MDP “chain lock” construction
46
with
S=15
states,
A=7
actions, horizon
H=10
, feature dimension
d=10
, and
5
special candidate chains. The MDP is linear: transitions factor as

P^{(k)}_{h}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)=\langle\phi(s,a),\mu_{h,k}(s^{\prime})\rangle
,
where the known feature map
\phi:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
one-hot
, so each
(s,a)
deterministically selects a latent index in
[d]
. The feature map is constructed so that for each special chain index
i\in\{1,\dots,5\}
, there is a designated “correct” action
a_{i}
at state
s_{i}
with
\phi(s_{i},a_{i})=e_{i}
; taking any other action at
s_{i}

[d]\setminus\{i\}
(uniformly). For all remaining (“normal”) states
s_{i}
with
i\geq 6
, every action maps to a uniformly random latent coordinate in
[d]
. Given this
\phi
, the vectors
\mu_{h,k}
are chosen so that in each episode there is exactly one

special chain
g\in[5]
that behaves like a combination lock: the latent index
g

0.99
vs.
0.01

0.8/0.2
split between two random next states). Rewards are also linear,
r^{(k)}_{h}(s,a)=\langle\phi(s,a),\theta_{h,k}\rangle

g

h\leq H-1

Non-stationarity.
For drifting experiments we use the original gradual protocol in
(
46

, which continuously shifts the identity of the good chain by interpolating (via convex combinations) between successive base MDPs over fixed windows (e.g.,
100

46
