On a nonlocal non-linear inverse scattering problem
Saumyajit Das, Susovan Pramanik

Abstract
. In this article, we study the inverse scattering problem for the nonlinear fractional Helmholtz equation with cubic nonlinearity in three dimensions, where we recover a compactly supported potential from scattering amplitude.



1.
Introduction

The inverse scattering problem concerns the recovery of unknown potentials or media from scattering amplitude measurements, with applications in quantum optics for modeling nonlocal photon-atom interactions and geophysical electromagnetics for anomalous responses in self-similar formations. Physically, these arise in wave propagation through heterogeneous media exhibiting nonlocal and nonlinear effects, such as fractional elasticity models. For classical background, we refer readers to
[
3
,
16
,
17
]
. Beyond physics, inverse scattering exhibits mathematical richness. For detailed study, we refer to
[
6
,
2
,
4
,
15
,
25
]
.

In recent years, studies have examined the nonlinear Helmholtz equation in the scattering of electromagnetic waves through localized nonlinear Kerr media
[
1
,
7
,
27
]
. Here, the nonlinear Helmholtz equation arises from reducing Maxwell’s equations for a linearly polarized electric field after eliminating the magnetic field. In
[
27
]
, the following equation emerges:

-\Delta u-k^{2}u=\rho 1_{\Omega}|u|^{2}u\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
\Omega
is the support of the nonlinear Kerr medium and
\rho
is the Kerr constant. Motivated by the above, in this article, we consider the fractional Helmholtz equation with potential and cubic nonlinearity (
1.1
). In particular, when
s=1
, it reduces to the Helmholtz equation, which frequently arises in scattering theory and quantum mechanics
[
22
,
3
]
; the fractional Helmholtz equation also appears in geophysical electromagnetics
[
26
,
9
]
and quantum optics to describe the nonlocal characteristics of photon-atom interactions
[
11
,
13
]
. Furthermore, when
k=1
and
s=1
, the equation (
1.1
) becomes Ginzburg-Landau equation (for details see
[
10
,
8
]
). In this article, for
k=1
, our model (
1.1
) exactly becomes fractional Ginzburg-Landau equation. Motivated by its physical relevance across diverse phenomena in physics, we study the inverse scattering problem for the fractional Helmholtz equation to recover the compactly supported potential from the measurement of scattering amplitude. In particular we study the following Helmholtz equation: for
j=1,2

(1.1)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u_{j}-k^{2s}u_{j}=Q_{j}(x)|u_{j}|^{2}u_{j},\quad\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d},

associated with the following Sommerfield radiation condition:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R}\int_{1\leq\|x\|\leq R}|D^{s}_{x}u_{j}^{sc}-k^{2s}u_{j}^{sc}\hat{x}|^{2}\,{\rm{d}}x=0,

where
\hat{x}=\frac{x}{\|x\|}
,
D_{x}^{s}:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}|\xi|^{s}\mathcal{F}
and
u_{i}^{sc}
is the scattering part of the solution
u
. For
j=1,2
, the potential
Q_{i}(x)\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is assumed to be compactly supported in
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
s\in(\frac{d}{d+1},1)
. The solution
u_{j}
can be expressed as: for
j=1,2

(1.3)

\displaystyle u_{j}=u_{j}^{\rm{in}}+u_{j}^{\rm{sc}},

where
u_{j}^{\rm{in}}
satisfy the initial wave equation

(1.4)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u_{j}^{\rm{in}}-k^{2s}u_{j}^{\rm{in}}=0,\quad\mbox{in}\ {\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

given by the Herglotz wave function

u_{j}^{\rm{in}}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}e^{ikx\cdot\theta}g(\theta){\rm{d}}\theta,

for any function
g\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathrm{S}^{d-1})
, whereas
u_{j}^{\rm{sc}}
satisfy the scattering part of the solution, given by

(1.5)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u_{j}^{\rm{sc}}-k^{2s}u_{j}^{\rm{sc}}=Q_{j}|u_{j}|^{2}u_{j},\qquad\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}.

We would like to note that the initial wave solutions are the same for both the equations, i.e.,
u_{1}^{\rm{in}}=u_{2}^{\rm{in}}
. Thanks to the Stein-Tomas theorem
[
24
,
23
]
, we have the following estimate

(1.6)

\displaystyle\|u_{i}^{\rm{in}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2\frac{d+1}{d-1}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq C_{ST}\|g\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})},\quad\mbox{for}\ j=1,2,

where
u_{i}^{\rm{in}}
is given by (
1.4
) and
C_{ST}
is a positive constant depending on the dimension.
The following existence result we borrow from
[
22
, Theorem 1.3]
:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
22
]
)
.

Let
u
satisfies

(1.7)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u-k^{2s}u=Q(x)|u|^{t-1}u,\qquad\mbox{in}\ {\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

where
k>0
and
Q(x)\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is compactly supported. Let the initial wave function is given by

u^{\rm{in}}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}e^{ikx\cdot\theta}g(\theta)\,{\rm{d}}\theta,

where
\|g\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq\epsilon
for some
\epsilon>0
. Furthermore, assume

s\in\left(\frac{d}{d+1},1\right)\ \text{and}\ \frac{d-1+4s}{d-1}<t<\frac{d+1}{d+1-4s}.

Then, there exists a unique solution ‘
u
’ to the equation (
1.7
), satisfying

u\in\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\ \text{such that}\ \|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq a,

provided
u
satisfy the following Sommerfield radiation condition:

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R}\int_{1\leq\|x\|\leq R}|D^{s}_{x}u^{sc}-k^{2s}u^{sc}\hat{x}|^{2}\,{\rm{d}}x=0.

Here
a>0
some positive constant depeding on
\epsilon
and the exponent ‘
q
’ satisfies

\frac{d(t-1)}{2s}<q<\frac{2dt}{d+1}.

In particular, for the spatial dimension
d=3
and
t=3
, we obtain that

u\in\mathrm{L}^{4}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

Remark 1.2
.

The uniqueness follows from the Sommerfeld radiation condition, formulated through the local estimate in
[
22
, Theorem 1.2]
. Additionally, when
s=1
,
k=1
, (
1.1
) reduces to the Ginzburg–Landau equation studied in
[
10
]
. Our nonlinear term in (
1.1
) derives from the Ginzburg–Landau equation. Building on the work of Gutiérrez in
[
10
]
, the authors of
[
22
]
study its fractional version.

To state our result, let us define the
scattering amplitude
(or
far-field pattern
) by (see Section
2.2
for details)

(1.8)

u^{\infty}(\hat{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\mathsf{i}k\hat{x}\cdot y}Q(y)|u(y)|^{2}u(y)\,dy,\quad\hat{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}.

Now the question is: can one recover the potential from a priori knowledge of the scattering amplitude
u^{\infty}
? Here we solve this problem in dimension
d=3
. In particular, we have following result:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
s\in\left(\frac{4}{5},\frac{3}{2}\right)
and
k>k_{0}>0
. For
j=1,2
, let
Q_{j}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and compactly supported. Let
u_{j}
satisfies: for
j=1,2

(-\Delta)^{s}u_{j}-k^{2s}u_{j}=Q_{j}|u_{j}|^{2}u_{j}\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{3}.

The solution
u_{j}
is given by

u_{j}=u_{j}^{\rm{in}}+u_{j}^{\rm{sc}},

where
u_{j}^{\rm{in}}
is the incident wave and
u_{j}^{\rm{sc}}
is the scattering wave as defined in (
1.4
) and (
1.5
). Let the scattering amplitude corresponding to the scattering waves are the same i.e., for all
(k,\hat{x},\theta)\in(k_{0},+\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2},\,k_{0}>0
,

(1.9)

u^{\infty}_{1}(k,\hat{x},\theta)=u^{\infty}_{2}(k,\hat{x},\theta),

where the scattering amplitude as defined in (
1.8
). Then

Q_{1}=Q_{2}.

Remark 1.4
.

Due to the lack of resolvent estimates for all fractional exponents
s
(see Proposition
2.3
), and the restriction arising from the Sobolev embedding (see Lemma
3.3
), we restrict our analysis to
s\in\left(\frac{4}{5},\frac{3}{2}\right)
. For other values of the fractional exponent
s
, the problem remains open.

Remark 1.5
.

Note that here we vary all three variables
(k,\hat{x},\theta)\in(k_{0},\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
in the scattering amplitude to determine the potential
Q
. This corresponds to data of dimension
2d-1
. A more challenging question arises when
k
is fixed and only
(\hat{x},\theta)\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
are varied: can the potential
Q
still be uniquely recovered? In this case, the dimension of the data is
2d-2
. This problem is related to the Ginzburg-Landau equation when
k=1
is fixed. In the local case, i.e., when
s=1
, this problem has been studied in
[
8
]
. To the best of our knowledge, the corresponding scattering problem for the fractional Ginzburg-Landau equation remains open, which corresponds to the case
k=1
in (
1.1
). We state the open problem below.

Open problem: Ginzburg-Landau equation

\bullet

For
j=1,2
, let
Q_{j}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and compactly supported. Let
u_{j}
satisfies: for
j=1,2

(-\Delta)^{s}u_{j}-u_{j}=Q_{j}|u_{j}|^{2}u_{j}\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{3}.

The solution
u_{j}
is given by

u_{j}=u_{j}^{\rm{in}}+u_{j}^{\rm{sc}},

where
u_{j}^{\rm{in}}
is the incident wave and
u_{j}^{\rm{sc}}
is the scattering wave as defined in (
1.4
) and (
1.5
). Let the scattering amplitude corresponding to the scattering waves are the same i.e., for all
(\hat{x},\theta)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
,

u^{\infty}_{1}(1,\hat{x},\theta)=u^{\infty}_{2}(1,\hat{x},\theta).

where the scattering amplitude as defined in (
1.8
). Then

Q_{1}=Q_{2}.

2.
Resolvent Estimate and Limiting Absorption Principle

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that
\frac{d}{d+1}\leq s<\frac{d}{2}
, and
u
satisfies

(-\Delta)^{s}u-k^{2s}u=f,

with
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
p\in(1,\infty)
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
k
, such that

(2.1)

\sup\limits_{R\geq 1/k^{s}}\Big(\frac{1}{R}\int_{1<|x|<R}|u^{\rm{sc}}(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{x}\Big)^{1/2}\leq Ck^{ds(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2})-\frac{3}{2}s}\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})},

whenever
\frac{1}{d+1}\leq\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}<\frac{s}{2}
for
d=3,4
or
\frac{1}{d+1}\leq\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}<\frac{s}{d}
for
d\geq 5
. Moreover,

(2.2)

\sup\limits_{R\geq 1/k^{s}}\Big(\frac{1}{R}\int_{1<|x|<R}|D_{x}^{s}u^{\rm{sc}}(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{x}\Big)^{1/2}\leq Ck^{ds(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2})-\frac{s}{2}}\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})},

whenever
\frac{1}{d+1}\leq\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}<\frac{s}{d}
for
d\geq 3
.

The following result yields an estimate of the scattering part of the solution.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
22
]
)
.

Let
s\in\left[\frac{d}{d+1},\frac{d}{2}\right)
, and let
u
satisfies

(-\Delta)^{s}u-k^{2s}u=f,

with
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
p\in(1,\infty)
. Let us fix some
1<p<q<\infty
satisfying

(2.3)

\frac{2}{d+1}\leq\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\leq\frac{2s}{d},\qquad\frac{1}{p}>\frac{d+1}{2d},\qquad\frac{1}{q}<\frac{d-1}{2d}.

Let the scattering field of the solution is given by

(2.4)

\displaystyle u^{\mathrm{sc}}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi_{s}(x,y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\ \ \text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

Then there exists a positive constant
C_{p,q}>0
, such that

\|u^{\rm{sc}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq C_{p,q}\big(k^{2s}\big)^{\frac{d}{2s}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}-1\right)}\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}.

In particular for spatial dimension
d=3
and for the special choice of exponent
p=\frac{4}{3}
and
q=4
, we have that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\|u^{\rm{sc}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}\leq C_{4,\frac{4}{3}}\big(k^{2s}\big)^{-\frac{3}{4s}}\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\frac{4}{3}}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}.

The above proposition is a direct consequence of the following resolvent estimate and limiting absorption principle.

Proposition 2.3

(
[
5
,
22
]
)
.

Let
d\geq 3
,
\frac{d}{d+1}\leq s<\frac{d}{2}
,
1<p<q<\infty
are Lebesgue exponents satisfying (
2.3
) and
\lambda>0
. Then, for all
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the following estimate holds

(2.6)

\displaystyle\|((-\Delta)^{s}-(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\epsilon))^{-1}\|_{L^{p}-L^{q}}\leq C|\lambda+i\epsilon|^{\frac{d}{2s}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q})-1}.

where the constant
C>0
is uniform w.r.t.
\lambda
and
\epsilon
.

Furthermore, there exists a
\lambda_{0}>0
, such that for
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\sup_{0<\epsilon<1}\|((-\Delta)^{s}-(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\epsilon))^{-1}\|_{L^{p}-L^{q}}\leq C|\lambda|^{\frac{d}{2s}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q})-1},\,\,\lambda>\lambda_{0},

where the constant
C(\lambda_{0})>0
is uniform with respect to
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
.

Rest follows from limiting absorption principle, which says, if
u_{\epsilon}
satisfies

(-\Delta)^{s}u_{\epsilon}-(\lambda+i\epsilon)u_{\epsilon}=f,

where
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with
\frac{1}{p}>\frac{d+1}{2d}
, then

u_{\epsilon}\to u\ \ \ \text{distributionally},

where
u
satisfies

(-\Delta)^{s}u-\lambda u=f\qquad\mbox{in}\ {\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Proof of this limiting absorption principle can be found in
[
22
,
5
]
. Next we analyze the scattering wave scattering amplitude corresponding to the equation (
1.1
) through fundamental solution.

2.1.
Fundamental Solution

Let us investigate the fundamental solutions since they are crucial to our analysis.
Thanks to limiting absorption principle, the fundamental solution
\Phi_{s}(x)
can be defined by

(2.8)

\Phi_{s}(x):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\Phi_{\pm,\,s,\,\varepsilon}(x),

where
\Phi_{\pm,\,s,\,\varepsilon}
are given by

(2.9)

\Phi_{\pm,\,s,\,\varepsilon}(x)=(2\pi)^{-d}\int\displaylimits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\frac{e^{\mathsf{i}x\cdot\xi}}{|\xi|^{2s}-(k\pm i\varepsilon)^{2s}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi}.

\Phi_{\pm,\,s,\,\varepsilon}
is a fundamental solution of

(2.10)

\left[(-\Delta)^{s}-(k\pm\mathsf{i}\varepsilon)^{2s}\right]\Phi_{\pm,\,s,\,\varepsilon}(x)=\delta_{0}(x)\quad\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

and
\Phi_{s}
is a fundamental solution of

(2.11)

\left[(-\Delta)^{s}-k^{2s}\right]\Phi_{s}(x)=\delta_{0}(x)\quad\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Here
\delta_{0}
signifies the Dirac delta measure located at the origin. We have the following lemma from
[
5
]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
s>0
and
k>0
, it holds that

(2.12)

\displaystyle\Phi_{s}(x)=\frac{k^{d-2s}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\bigg[\mathrm{P.V.}\int\displaylimits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\frac{e^{\mathsf{i}kx\cdot\xi}}{|\xi|^{2s}-1}\mathrm{d}\xi}\pm\frac{\mathsf{i}\pi}{2s}\int\displaylimits_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}{e^{\mathsf{i}kx\cdot\omega}\,\mathrm{d}S(\omega)}\bigg],

where
\mathrm{P.V.}
stands for the Cauchy principle value.

Let us denote

(2.13)

\mathcal{S}_{d}(t):=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int\displaylimits_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}{e^{\mathsf{i}te_{1}\cdot\omega}\,\mathrm{d}S(\omega)},

where
e_{1}
stands for the unit vector with its first component being
1
and rest
0
, then it can be seen that
\mathcal{S}_{d}(t)
is continuous on
[0,\infty)
and

(2.14)

\mathcal{S}_{d}(t)=\begin{cases}\frac{J_{d/2-1}(t)}{t^{d/2-1}},&t\neq 0,\\[2.84526pt]
\frac{2^{-d/2+1}}{\Gamma(d/2)},&t=0,\end{cases}\quad d\geq 2,\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{S}_{3}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{\sin t}{t}

where
J_{\alpha}
is the usual Bessel’s function of the first kind.

We continue from (
2.12
) by using the functions
\mathcal{S}_{d}(t)
to write further

(2.15)

\displaystyle\Phi_{s}(x)=\frac{k^{d-2s}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\bigg[\mathrm{P.V.}\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\infty}{\frac{t^{d-1}\mathcal{S}_{d}(k|x|t)}{t^{2s}-1}\,\mathrm{d}t}\pm\frac{\mathsf{i}\pi}{2s}\;\mathcal{S}_{d}(k|x|)\bigg],\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For
s=1
, we get the expression of the fundamental solution for the Helmholtz operator
(-\Delta)-k^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

(2.16)

\Phi_{1}(x)=\frac{k^{d-2}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\bigg[\mathrm{P.V.}\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\infty}{\frac{t^{d-1}\mathcal{S}_{d}(k|x|t)}{t^{2}-1}\,\mathrm{d}t}\pm\frac{\mathsf{i}\pi}{2}\;\mathcal{S}_{d}(k|x|)\bigg],\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For any
d\geq 2
, let us mention the standard asymptotic behavior of
\Phi_{\pm,1}
, this can be found in
[
6
, Lemma 19.3]
:

(2.17)

\Phi_{1}(x)=k^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\frac{e^{-\frac{\mathsf{i}\pi(d-3)}{4}}}{2^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\,\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\,\,\frac{e^{\pm\mathsf{i}k|x|}}{|x|^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\right),\quad\text{ as }|x|\to\infty.

In particular for
d=3
, we have

(2.18)

\Phi_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\frac{e^{\pm\mathsf{i}k|x|}}{|x|},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Next we introduce scattering amplitude corresponding to (
1.1
).

2.2.
Scattered Field and Scattering Amplitude

Let us recall (see (
1.5
)) that the
scattered field
is given by: for
j=1,2

(2.19)

u^{\mathrm{sc}}_{j}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi_{s}(x,y)Q_{j}(y)|u_{j}(y)|^{2}u_{j}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\ \ \text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We use the following result from
[
21
]

(2.20)

\displaystyle\Phi_{s}(x-y)=\frac{k^{2(1-s)}}{s}\Phi_{1}(x-y)+\frac{\sin{s\pi}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{s}(\lambda-\Delta)^{-1}\delta_{y}(x)}{\lambda^{2s}-2\lambda^{s}k^{2s}\cos{s\pi}+k^{4s}}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda,

and we are interested in providing a decay for the second term when
|x|\to\infty.

Following the ideas presented as in
[
5
]
, we will show that

(2.21)

\Phi_{s}(x)\sim\frac{k^{2(1-s)}}{s}\Phi_{1}(x),

where
f\sim g
is defined through
\mathrm{L}^{2}
integrability at infinity, i.e.,

(2.22)

f\sim g\Longleftrightarrow\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R}\int_{1<|x|<R}|f(x)-g(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

By using the following estimates

(2.23)

\Big|(\lambda-\Delta)^{-1}\delta_{y}(x)\Big|\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}C\lambda^{\frac{d-2}{4}}|x-y|^{-\frac{d-2}{2}}e^{-\sqrt{\lambda}|x-y|},\,\sqrt{\lambda}|x-y|>1,\\
C|x-y|^{2-d},\,\sqrt{\lambda}|x-y|\leq 1,\end{array}\right.

a direct computation which can be found in
[
14
, page 12-13]
yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big|\,\frac{\lambda^{s}(\lambda-\Delta)^{-1}\delta_{y}(x)}{\lambda^{2s}-2\lambda^{s}k^{2s}\cos{s\pi}+k^{4s}}\,\Big|\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\leq C|x-y|^{-d-2s},\quad\text{if}\,\,|x-y|>1.

Hence (
2.21
) follows. Further by taking (
2.17
) into account, we have

(2.24)

\displaystyle\Phi_{s}(x)\sim\frac{k^{2(1-s)}}{s}\,k^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\frac{e^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi(d-3)}{4}}}{2^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\,\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\,\,\frac{e^{\pm ik|x|}}{|x|^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}.

The following proposition is taken from
[
5
]
.

Proposition 2.5

Let

u^{\infty}_{j,g}(\hat{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\mathsf{i}k\hat{x}\cdot y}Q_{j}(y)|u_{j}(y)|^{2}u_{j}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\quad\hat{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}.

Following the calculation as presented in

(2.27)

u^{\mathrm{sc}}_{j}(x)\sim\frac{k^{2(1-s)}}{s}\,k^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\frac{e^{-\frac{\mathsf{i}\pi(d-3)}{4}}}{2^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\,\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\,\,\frac{e^{\pm\mathsf{i}k|x|}}{|x|^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\,u^{\infty}_{j,g}(\hat{x}).

Hence the solution corresponding to fractional Helmholtz equation (

) can be expressed as

(2.28)

u_{j}(x)\sim u^{\mathrm{in}}_{j}(x)+\frac{k^{2(1-s)}}{s}\,k^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\frac{e^{-\frac{\mathsf{i}\pi(d-3)}{4}}}{2^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\,\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\,\,\frac{e^{\pm\mathsf{i}k|x|}}{|x|^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\,u^{\infty}_{j,g}(\hat{x}).

Note that, for
j=1,2
, the scattering amplitude
u^{\infty}_{j,g}(\hat{x})
corresponds to the Herglotz wave as an incident field, can be viewed as

(2.29)

u^{\infty}_{j,g}(\hat{x})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}u^{\infty}_{j}(k,\hat{x},\theta)g(\theta)\,ds(\theta),\quad\text{ for }\hat{x}:=\frac{x}{|x|}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\ g\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),

where for
(k,\hat{x},\theta)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,

(2.30)

u^{\infty}_{j}(k,\hat{x},\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\mathsf{i}k\hat{x}\cdot y}Q_{j}(y)|u_{j}(y)|^{2}\underbrace{\left(e^{iky\cdot\theta}+u^{\mathrm{sc}}_{j}(y)\right)}_{u_{j}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y,

and the scattering amplitude corresponds to the incident plane waves is
e^{\mathrm{i}kx\cdot\theta}
.

3.
Inverse Problem

In this section we devout ourselves to recover the potential from the scattering amplitude. We start with the following integral estimate.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
s\in\left(\frac{3}{4},\frac{3}{2}\right)
, and let
u
satisfies

(-\Delta)^{s}u-k^{2s}u=f,

with
f\in\mathrm{L}^{\frac{4}{3}}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
. Let
u^{\rm{sc}}
be the scattering field of the solution as defined in (
2.4
). Then there exists a positive constant
C_{\frac{4}{3},q}>0
, such that, the scattering field satisfy the following estimate

\|u^{\rm{sc}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}\leq C_{\frac{4}{3},q}\big(k^{2s}\big)^{\frac{3}{2s}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}-1\right)}\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\frac{4}{3}}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})},

This is a direct application of Proposition
2.2
with
p=\frac{4}{3}
and
q
satisfies

\frac{1}{2}<\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}<\frac{4s+3}{12}<\frac{2s}{3},

with
s\in(\frac{3}{4},\frac{3}{2})
in spatial dimension
d=3
. Note that

4<q<\frac{6}{3-2s}.

Our next goal is to obtain a local integrability estimate of the scattering field
u^{\rm{sc}}
. We use the following proposition from
[
22
]
which yields a local second order regularity estimate.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
22
]
,Lemma 5.1 )
.

Let the dimension
d\geq 3
and let
s\in\left(\frac{d}{d+1},\frac{d}{2}\right)
. Let
p\in\left(\frac{2(d+1)}{d-1},\frac{2d}{d-2s}\right)
and
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap\mathrm{L}^{q}_{\rm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, where
p^{\prime}
is the Hölder conjugate of
p
, i.e.,
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1
and
q\in(1,\infty)
. Let
u
satisfy

(-\Delta)^{s}u-k^{2s}u=f,\quad\text{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}.

Let the scattering field of the solution
u^{\rm{sc}}
(
2.4
) belongs to the space
\mathrm{L}^{q}_{\rm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Then
u^{\rm{sc}}\in W^{2s,q}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and satisfies the following estimate

\|u^{\rm{sc}}\|_{W^{2s,q}(B_{r}(0))}\leq C_{r,d,p^{\prime},q}\left(\|u^{\rm{sc}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(B_{2r}(0))}+\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(B_{2r}(0))}\right),

here
C_{r,d,p^{\prime},q}
is a positive constant depending on the radius
r
, dimension
d
and the exponents
p^{\prime},q
only.

The above proposition yields the following local integrability estimate of the solution corresponding to (
1.1
).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
s\in\left(\frac{4}{5},\frac{3}{2}\right)
. For
j=1,2
, let
u_{j}
satisfies the fractional Helmholtz equation (
1.1
) in spatial dimension
d=3
. Then for any
r>0
, we have that
u_{j}\in\mathrm{L}^{6}(B_{r}(0))
for
j=1,2
.

Proof.

For
j=1,2
,
u_{j}
satisfies

(-\Delta)^{s}u_{j}-k^{2s}u_{j}=Q_{j}|u_{j}|^{2}u_{j}.

We have that
Q_{j}|u_{j}|^{2}u_{j}\in\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
for any
p^{\prime}\leq\frac{4}{3}
. Applying Lemma (
3.1
), we have that
u_{j}^{\rm{sc}}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
, where
4<q<\frac{6}{3-2s}
. As
u_{j}^{\rm{in}}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})\cap\mathrm{L}^{4}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
, we have that
u_{j}^{\rm{in}}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
. Applying Proposition
3.2
, we have that

\|u_{j}^{\rm{sc}}\|_{W^{2s,\frac{2}{3-2s}}(B_{r}(0))}\leq C_{r,d,p^{\prime},q}\left(\|u_{j}^{\rm{sc}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\frac{2}{3-2s}}(B_{2r}(0))}+\left\|Q_{j}|u_{j}|^{2}u_{j}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\frac{2}{3-2s}}(B_{2r}(0))}\right).

Sobolev embedding theorem yields

W^{2s,\frac{2}{3-2s}}(B_{r}(0))\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{6}(B_{r}(0)),\quad\forall\,s\in\left(\frac{4}{5},\frac{3}{2}\right),\,d=3.

Hence, we have
u_{j}\in\mathrm{L}^{6}(B_{r}(0))
for
j=1,2
and for all
r>0
.
∎

Before begin the proof of the main result we start with the classical Rellich lemma which can be found in e.g.
[
6
, Lemma 35.2]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
k\neq 0
,
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and with compact support.

u\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
solves

(3.1)

\Delta u+k^{2}u=f,\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{d},

Then
u
has a compact support, and
\supp u\subseteq\supp f
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
w_{j}~(j=1,2)
be the solution of

(3.2)

\Delta w_{j}+k^{2}w_{j}=-Q_{j}|u_{j}^{\rm{sc}}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta}|^{2}(u^{\rm{sc}}_{j}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta})\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{3},

where
u_{j}^{\rm{sc}}
’s are the scattered fields solving (
1.1
) with
s\in\left(\frac{4}{5},\frac{3}{2}\right)
,
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
,
k>0
and
Q_{j}
’s are the bounded potentials with supports in
\overline{\Omega}
, where
\Omega
is a smooth bounded domain.
Then
w_{1}-w_{2}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with compact support.

Proof.

Using the fundamental solution of the Helmholtz operator, we write down the solution as

(3.3)

w_{j}(x)=e^{ikx}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi_{1}(x,y)Q_{j}(y)Q_{j}|u_{j}^{\rm{sc}}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta}|^{2}(u^{\rm{sc}}_{j}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta})\,\mathrm{d}y,\quad\text{ for }x\in\ \mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\Phi_{1}
is given in (
2.16
) and (
2.18
), with its asymptotics in (
2.17
) and the scattered field is

w_{j}^{\rm{sc}}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi_{1}(x,y)Q_{j}|u_{j}^{\rm{sc}}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta}|^{2}(u^{\rm{sc}}_{j}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta})\,\mathrm{d}y,\quad\text{ for }x\in\ \mathbb{R}^{d}.

Thanks to Proposition
2.2
, we have that

\|w^{\rm{sc}}_{j}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq C_{4,\frac{4}{3}}\big(k^{2s}\big)^{-\frac{d}{4}}\left\|Q_{j}|u_{j}^{\rm{sc}}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta}|^{2}(u^{\rm{sc}}_{j}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta})\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\frac{4}{3}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}<+\infty,

where the finiteness is due to Theorem
1.1
. Similar to deriving (
2.27
), we can further infer that
[
6
]

(3.4)

w_{j}(x)\sim-k^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\frac{e^{-\frac{\mathsf{i}\pi(d-3)}{4}}}{2^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\,\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\,\,\frac{e^{\pm\mathsf{i}k|x|}}{|x|^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\,u^{\infty}_{j}(k,\hat{x},\theta)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\right),\quad j=1,2,

and using the hypothesis
u^{\infty}_{1}=u^{\infty}_{2}
, we have

w_{1}-w_{2}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Denote
f:=\left(Q_{1}|u_{1}^{\rm{sc}}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta}|^{2}(u^{\rm{sc}}_{1}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta})-Q_{2}|u_{2}^{\rm{sc}}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta}|^{2}(u^{\rm{sc}}_{2}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta})\right)
has compact support in
\overline{\Omega}
. Furthermore,
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, thanks to Lemma
3.3
.
Moreover,
w_{1}-w_{2}
solves

(3.5)

\Delta(w_{1}-w_{2})+k^{2}(w_{1}-w_{2})=f\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{d},

so applying Lemma
3.4
to
(w_{1}-w_{2})
gives

(3.6)

w_{1}-w_{2}=0\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.

Let
\Omega\Subset B(0,\sigma)=B
for some
\sigma>0
. Note that,
(w_{1}-w_{2})\in H^{2}(B)
by the elliptic regularity of the solution of (
3.5
). Furthermore, thanks to (
3.6
), we can conclude
w_{1}-w_{2}\in H^{2}_{0}(B)
.
The proof is done.
∎

We are ready to prove Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be arbitrary.
Choose
l\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
m\cdot l=0
and
|m+l|>k_{0}
.
Denote

(3.7)

\rho:=m+l,\quad k:=|\rho|=\sqrt{|m|^{2}+|l|^{2}},\quad\theta:=\frac{m-l}{k}.

It can be checked that
k\in(k_{0},+\infty)
and
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

Let
w_{j}~(j=1,2)
solve

(-\Delta-k^{2})w_{j}=Q_{j}|u_{j}^{\rm{sc}}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta}|^{2}(u^{\rm{sc}}_{j}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta}),\ \text{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3}.

By Lemma
3.5
we see
w_{1}-w_{2}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with compact support.
Then integration by parts give

\displaystyle\int_{B}e^{\mathrm{i}\rho\cdot x}\Big(Q_{1}|u_{1}^{\rm{sc}}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta}|^{2}(u_{1}^{\rm{sc}}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta})-Q_{2}|u_{2}^{\rm{sc}}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta}|^{2}(u_{2}^{\rm{sc}}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta})\Big)

\displaystyle=\int_{B}e^{\mathrm{i}\rho\cdot x}(\Delta+k^{2})(w_{1}-w_{2})=\int_{B}(\Delta+k^{2})e^{\mathrm{i}\rho\cdot x}(w_{1}-w_{2})=0.

Simplifying the above relation, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B}e^{\mathrm{i}\rho\cdot x}\Big(Q_{1}

\displaystyle(1+|u_{1}^{\rm{sc}}|^{2}+\bar{u}_{1}^{\rm{sc}}e^{ikx\cdot\theta}+u_{1}^{\rm{sc}}e^{-ikx\cdot\theta})(u_{1}^{\rm{sc}}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta})\Big)

\displaystyle=\int_{B}e^{\mathrm{i}\rho\cdot x}\Big(Q_{2}(1+|u_{2}^{\rm{sc}}|^{2}+\bar{u}_{2}^{\rm{sc}}e^{ikx\cdot\theta}+u_{2}^{\rm{sc}}e^{-ikx\cdot\theta})(u_{2}^{\rm{sc}}+e^{ikx\cdot\theta})\Big).

Simplifying further, we obtain

(3.8)

\displaystyle\int_{B}e^{ix\cdot(\rho+k\theta)}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{B}e^{ix\cdot\rho}(Q_{2}u_{2}^{\rm{sc}}-Q_{1}u_{1}^{\rm{sc}})+\int_{B}e^{ix\cdot\rho}(Q_{2}|u^{\rm{sc}}_{2}|^{2}u_{2}^{\rm{sc}}-Q_{1}|u^{\rm{sc}}_{1}|^{2}u_{1}^{\rm{sc}})

\displaystyle+\int_{B}e^{ix\cdot\rho}(Q_{2}|u_{2}^{\rm{sc}}|^{2}e^{ikx\cdot\theta}-Q_{1}|u_{1}^{\rm{sc}}|^{2}e^{ikx\cdot\theta})+\int_{B}e^{ix\cdot\rho}(Q_{2}({u}_{2}^{\rm{sc}})^{2}e^{-ikx\cdot\theta}-Q_{1}({u}_{1}^{\rm{sc}})^{2}e^{-ikx\cdot\theta})

\displaystyle+\int_{B}e^{ix\cdot\rho}(Q_{2}|u_{2}^{\rm{sc}}|^{2}e^{ikx\cdot\theta}-Q_{1}|u_{1}^{\rm{sc}}|^{2}e^{ikx\cdot\theta})+\int_{B}e^{ix\cdot\rho}(Q_{2}\bar{u}_{2}^{\rm{sc}}e^{2ikx\cdot\theta}-Q_{1}\bar{u}_{1}^{\rm{sc}}e^{2ikx\cdot\theta})

\displaystyle+\int_{B}e^{ix\cdot\rho}(Q_{2}u_{2}^{\rm{sc}}-Q_{1}u_{1}^{\rm{sc}}).

We show that the each of the terms appearing on the right hand side actually tends to zero as
k\to+\infty
. We present our calculation only for one term and rest will follow in a similar manner. Let us consider the second term:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B}e^{ix\cdot\rho}(Q_{2}|u^{\rm{sc}}_{2}|^{2}u_{2}^{\rm{sc}}-Q_{1}|u^{\rm{sc}}_{1}|^{2}u_{1}^{\rm{sc}})\right|

\displaystyle\leq|B|^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\|Q_{1}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}+\|Q_{1}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}\right)C_{4,\frac{4}{3}}\big(k^{2s}\big)^{-\frac{3}{4s}}\Big(\|u_{1}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}+\|u_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}\Big),

where we use the resolvent estimate as described in (
2.5
) in Proposition
2.2
. Furthermore, using the fact that
\|u_{j}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}<+\infty
for
j=1,2
(see Theorem
1.1
), we have that

\left|\int_{B}e^{ix\cdot\rho}(Q_{2}|u^{\rm{sc}}_{2}|^{2}u_{2}^{\rm{sc}}-Q_{1}|u^{\rm{sc}}_{1}|^{2}u_{1}^{\rm{sc}})\right|\to 0\ \text{as}\ k\to\infty.

Similarly, we can show, all the terms appearing in the right hand side in (
3.8
) will vanish as
k\to\infty
. Hence from (
3.8
), we have that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{B}e^{ix\cdot(\rho+k\theta)}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{B}e^{2ix\cdot m}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})=0.

Since
m
is arbitrary, we conclude

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}e^{2ix\cdot m}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})=0,\quad\forall\,m\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}.

As
m\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
was arbitrary, we conclude that
Q_{1}=Q_{2}
.
∎
