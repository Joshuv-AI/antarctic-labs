Immune checkpoint inhibitors with neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy in proficient MMR rectal cancer: systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Reza Ghalehtaki, Romina Abyaneh, Mohammad-Mahdi Aghaei, Fatemeh Mahmoudi, Amirreza Arab, Nazanin Seddighi, Kasra Kolahdouzan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy plus immune checkpoint inhibitors (CRT-ICI) for mismatch repair proficient (pMMR) locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC).MethodsFive databases were systematically searched. Pooled pathological complete response (pCR) and composite clinical response (CR) and grade 3–4 neoadjuvant-related adverse events (AEs), surgical AEs, and immune-related AEs (ir-AEs) were calculated, and comparative results (CRT-ICI vs. CRT alone) were conducted on randomized clinical trials (RCTs) via risk ratios (RRs). Also, subgroup analyses were conducted in pMMR-exclusive cohorts for all available endpoints.ResultsEighteen studies (1,619 patients) were included. The pooled pCR rate was 41% (95% CI: 36–47%) overall and in the pMMR-exclusive group (95% CI: 35–48%). Composite CR rates were 49% (95%CI: 38–60%) overall and 54% (95%CI: 41–66%) in pMMR cohorts. Analysis of comparative RCTs showed CRT-ICI significantly improved both pCR (RR 1.7, 95%CI:1.38–2.09) and CR rates (RR 1.76, 95%CI:1.43–2.17) compared to CRT alone. The pMMR-exclusive group confirmed a significant CR (RR 1.76, 95%CI:1.3–2.37, p < 0.01) and pCR (RR 1.66, 95%CI:1.15–2.4; p < 0.01) benefit. Grade 3–4 neoadjuvant-related AEs occurred in 23% of patients, with a significant increase over CRT alone (RR 1.26, 95%CI:1.01–1.57). Surgical complications (6% overall; 4% in the pMMR-exclusive group) and ir-AEs (4% overall; 3% in the pMMR-exclusive group) were infrequent. A subgroup analysis of RCTs showed that short-course ICI containing regimen (SCRT + ICI) significantly improved pCR (RR 1.90, 95%CI: 1.36–2.65) and CR (RR 1.84, 95%CI: 1.37–2.47) over SCRT alone. However, long-course regimen (LCRT + ICI) did not show a benefit over LCRT alone (pCR: RR 1.31, 95%CI 0.94–1.81, CR: RR 1.17, 95%CI 0.53–2.59). Additionally, SCRT + ICI led to higher pooled pCR/CR rates than LCRT + ICI (CR: 59% vs. 36%, p = 0.031; pCR: 51% vs. 33%, p < 0.0001).ConclusionIn pMMR LARC, our results suggested that neoadjuvant CRT-ICI was associated with higher pCR/CR rates than CRT alone based on the analysis of available trials. This effect, however, appears confined to SCRT-ICI, with no corresponding gain seen in LCRT-ICI. Although treatment‑related toxicity is increased, severe immune‑related and surgical adverse events are infrequent. These findings suggest that selective use of CRT‑ICI may be considered, particularly when downstaging or organ preservation is a goal, but confirmatory trials and continued longitudinal follow‑up are warranted to assess response durability, survival benefits, and late toxicity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13014-026-02876-z.

Introduction

The management of locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) has evolved significantly from a purely surgical approach to a multimodal strategy incorporating neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy followed by surgery [1–3]. More recently, total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) has emerged as a promising treatment paradigm, demonstrating improved pathological response rates, disease-free survival, and reduced distant metastasis rates in LARC [4, 5]. Despite these advances, pathological complete response (pCR) rates with TNT remain suboptimal, reaching only around 30% [6, 7]. However, determining pCR as a predictor of better survival outcomes is challenging, but it still serves as a meaningful prognostic marker at the individual level [8]. Importantly, when treatment strategies demonstrate improved pCR rates, clinicians may be more confident in selecting patients for non-operative management [9].

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are a class of cancer immunotherapies that enhance anti-tumor immune responses by blocking key immunologic receptors on T-lymphocytes, including cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1). These targeted therapies have demonstrated significant clinical efficacy across multiple solid tumor types [10, 11]. While ICIs have revolutionized treatment for mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) colorectal cancers, their role in mismatch repair proficient (pMMR)/microsatellite stable (MSS) LARC remains investigational [12]. Given that pMMR/MSS tumors represent 90–95% of rectal cancer cases [13] exploring rational combination strategies to overcome immune resistance in this predominant subset represents a critical unmet need.

Emerging preclinical evidence supports two synergistic mechanisms underlying the combination of ICIs and radiotherapy in pMMR rectal cancer. First, radiotherapy potentiates ICI efficacy by activating CD8-positive T cells to secrete tumor necrosis factor (TNF), thereby depleting myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and alleviating tumor immunosuppression [14]. Additionally, radiotherapy upregulates PD-L1 expression on tumor cells via interferon regulatory factors and NF-κB signaling pathways, enhancing tumor immunogenicity and susceptibility to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade [15].

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to quantify the pooled pCR and complete response (CR) rates of ICI-augmented neoadjuvant therapy in pMMR LARC populations, and compare the efficacy of ICI combinations with CRT versus CRT alone using data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs); it also seeks to report the safety profile of ICI-based regimens, including neoadjuvant-related AEs, immune-related AEs (irAEs), and surgical complications.

Materials and methods

The study adhered to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251026381).

Literature search strategy

A systematic literature search was performed across five major electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library) from their inception to November 2025, with no language restrictions. The search strategy utilized MeSH terms and keywords related to immune checkpoint inhibitors, locally advanced rectal cancer, chemoradiotherapy, and their synonyms. The whole search strategy is provided in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary File 1).

Study selection

The screening process was conducted independently by four reviewers (F.M., A.A., N.S., R.A.), with any discrepancies resolved through discussion or adjudication by a fifth reviewer (R.G.) when necessary. Studies were eligible for inclusion if they enrolled patients with primary, non-metastatic, LARC (clinical stage II-III). The investigational intervention consisted of neoadjuvant CRT or radiotherapy combined with ICIs, administered in any sequence (concurrent, induction, consolidation, or mixed approaches), followed by either surgical resection or watch and wait for patients achieving a clinical complete response (cCR). The comparator arm included standard neoadjuvant CRT or radiotherapy alone, followed by the same management strategies. Both RCTs, which provided comparative efficacy data, and single-arm trials, which contributed to pooled outcome analyses, were included in the review. Given the novel nature of ICI application in pMMR rectal cancer, a field characterized by numerous preliminary trial reports, we implemented strict inclusion criteria to ensure robust findings. Our analysis was restricted to peer-reviewed, full-text publications.

Exclusion criteria were applied to studies that exclusively focused on recurrent or metastatic disease, those that enrolled only dMMR or microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) patients, and studies reporting outcomes for dMMR subgroups without separate data for pMMR or MSS populations. Additionally, conference abstracts and studies lacking extractable data on pCR or CR rates were excluded.

Our inclusion criteria for MMR status were based on its prevalence in rectal cancer (90–95% pMMR/MSS) [13]. We included studies with mixed/unknown MMR status if: ≥80% of the cohort was confirmed pMMR/MSS, or outcomes were reported separately for the pMMR subgroup. Studies that did not report MMR status were included, as their populations were presumed to be predominantly pMMR in the absence of selective enrollment.

Data extraction and study outcomes

Two authors (R.A. and M.A.) performed systematic data extraction using a standardized form, with any disagreements resolved through consensus or consultation with a third reviewer (R.G.) when necessary. The extraction captured comprehensive study- and patient-level data across several domains: study characteristics included publication year, first author, and study design (RCTs or single-arm trials). Patient demographics comprised age, sex distribution, baseline clinical staging (T/N categories), tumor location from the anal verge, ECOG performance status, and pMMR confirmation status when reported. Treatment-specific variables were meticulously documented, including the type of ICI (anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, or anti-CTLA-4) with corresponding dosing regimens, the sequencing strategy relative to chemoradiation (concurrent, induction, or consolidation), and the proportion of patients undergoing surgery. Outcome measures focused on pCR rates among surgically treated patients and composite CR rates, defined as pCR in surgical patients plus cCR in the watch and wait subgroup. Safety outcomes included grade 3–4 irAEs specific to ICI-containing regimens in the experimental arm, grade 3–4 surgical complications, and grade 3–4 toxicities attributable to neoadjuvant therapy (either conventional or experimental).

Statistical analysis

Risk ratio (RR) was used as the primary effect size for comparative analyses. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using I2, τ2 (from REML), and the Cochran Q-test (p < 0.10 indicating significance), with I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity. Based on heterogeneity, fixed effects models or random effects models were used to generate the results. All pooled estimates were reported with two-tailed 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For studies directly comparing ICI + CRT versus CRT alone, pooled RRs were calculated. In cases of zero events in one treatment arm, a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to both arms. Subgroup analyses were performed for studies in which 100% of patients were pMMR, with between-subgroup heterogeneity assessed using the Q-between statistic.

For studies without the CRT arm, a meta-analysis of proportions was performed to estimate the rates of outcomes in the ICI + CRT arm. To include all evidence for the response rates, we also included the ICI + CRT arm data from comparative studies. The logit transformation was applied to stabilize variances, with the arcsine square-root transformation used when proportions approached 0 or 1, and pooled estimates were back-transformed for interpretability.

A prespecified subgroup analysis was conducted to evaluate the impact of radiotherapy schedule on pCR and CR. We compared RCTs assessing short-course ICI containing regimen (SCRT + ICI) versus short-course (chemo)radiotherapy (SCRT) alone (with SCRT defined as 25 Gy delivered in 5 fractions) and trials evaluating long-course ICI containing regimen (LCRT + ICI) versus long-course (chemo)radiotherapy (LCRT alone (with LCRT defined as 45–54 Gy delivered in 25–30 fractions). Utilizing a random-effects model, we calculated pooled estimates within each subgroup. We also formally tested the differences in treatment effects between these subgroups. Additionally, we computed the overall CR and pCR rates for LCRT, SCRT, LCRT + ICI, and SCRT + ICI arms separately.

Publication bias was assessed through funnel plot inspection, Egger’s regression test, and P-values for funnel plot asymmetry. For significant publication bias (p < 0.1), a trim-and-fill test was performed. Both qualitative and quantitative evaluations of small-study effects were conducted.

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the metafor [16] package.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias and study quality were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB 2.0) for RCTs [17] and the methodological index for non-randomized studies (MINORS) for single-arm studies (Supplementary File 1, Table S1 and S2) [18].

Results

Study selection

The systematic search identified 1,689 records from electronic databases (PubMed: 115, Scopus: 645, Embase: 681, Web of Science: 221, Cochrane: 27). After removing 538 duplicate records, 1,115 unique records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 1,046 records were excluded as they did not meet the inclusion criteria. The remaining 105 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. A further 87 articles were excluded with reasons reported in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure S1). The final analysis included 18 studies.

Study characteristics

As detailed in Table 1, this systematic review included 18 clinical trials published between 2021 and 2025. The analysis included a total of 1,619 patients across all study arms, with 1,095 cases specifically receiving (C)RT plus ICI. Eleven studies (n = 61.1%) were Phase I-II trials reporting CRT + ICI results [19–29], while the other seven (n = 38.9%) were RCTs comparing CRT + ICI vs. CRT alone [30–36] (five Phase II, one Phase III, and one Phase II/III). Geographically, 12 studies (66.7%) were conducted in China, with others from the USA (n = 1), Europe (n = 2), Japan (n = 1), Australia (n = 1), and the Middle East (n = 1).

Among the 18 included studies, pCR outcomes were reported in 17 (94.4%), with 11 (64.7%) providing pMMR-specific pCR data. Of the six RCTs reporting pCR, two (33.3%) focused exclusively on pMMR populations. Composite CR rates (combining pCR and cCR) were documented in eight studies (44.4% of total), including five (62.5%) with pMMR-specific results; among the five comparative RCTs reporting CR outcomes, two (40%) provided pMMR-stratified data. Safety reporting was reported in 15 studies (83.3%) detailing neoadjuvant treatment-related adverse events (AEs), including 7 (46.7%) restricted to pMMR cohorts, while only six RCTs reported these AEs (1 exclusively in pMMR cases). Surgical complications were reported in nine studies (50%), though six (66.7%) of these provided pMMR-specific data. Publication bias was assessed through funnel plot inspection (Supplementary Material, Figures S2, S3, S4,S5). Significant asymmetry was detected only for composite CR rates in the RCT subgroup analysis. To address this, a trim-and-fill sensitivity analysis was performed.

Outcome findings from the meta-analysis

Pathological complete response (pCR)

The pooled analysis of 17 studies (n = 854 patients) receiving CRT+ICI demonstrated an overall pCR rate of 41% (95% CI 36–47%), with considerable heterogeneity observed (I2 = 57.75%, p < 0.01). Response rates varied substantially across studies, ranging from 19% [23] to 64% [34] (Fig. 1a).

Forest plots of pooled pathological complete response (pCR) rates for neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy plus immune checkpoint inhibitors (CRT-ICI). (a) Pooled pCR rate across all included studies (n = 854 patients), (b) Subgroup analysis of studies with exclusively mismatch repair proficient (pMMR) populations (n = 447)

Across 11 pMMR-exclusive studies (n = 447), CRT+ICI demonstrated a pooled pCR rate of 41% (95% CI 35–48%), with moderate heterogeneity (I²=47.81%, p = 0.04). Response rates varied from 19% [23] to 62% [26], with three studies achieving pCR rates ≥ 50% [26–28] (Fig. 1b).

RCT subgroup analysis: Analysis of six RCTs (n = 852 evaluable patients) revealed a relatively 70% higher pCR rate with CRT+ICI compared to CRT alone (RR 1.70, 95% CI 1.38–2.09; p < 0.01), with low, non-significant heterogeneity (I2 = 26.1%, p = 0.24) (Fig. 2a). Analysis of two RCTs of pMMR-exclusive cohorts (n = 267 patients) showed a significant relative 66% increase in pCR rates favoring CRT+ICI (RR 1.66, 95% CI 1.15–2.4; p < 0.01), with moderate, non-significant heterogeneity (I2 = 38.49%, p = 0.2) (Fig. 2b).

Composite Complete response (CR)

Analysis of eight studies (n = 568 patients) showed a pooled CR rate (combining pCR and cCR) of 49% (95% CI 38–60%), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 84.75%, p < 0.01). CR rates varied among studies from 22% [30] to 79% [27] (Fig. 3a).

Forest plots of pooled composite complete response (CR) rates for neoadjuvant CRT-ICI. Composite CR is defined as the combination of pathological complete response (pCR) and clinical complete response (cCR). (a) Pooled CR rate across all included studies (n = 568 patients). (b) Subgroup analysis of studies with exclusively mismatch repair proficient (pMMR) populations (n = 356)

Pooled analysis of five pMMR-specific studies (n = 356) revealed a CR rate of 54% (95% CI 41–66%); results varied from 44% [21] to 79% [27] showing high heterogeneity (I²=80.04%, p < 0.01) (Fig. 3b).

RCT subgroup analysis: The analysis of five RCTs (n = 761 patients) demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in CR rates with CRT+ICI compared to CRT alone (RR 1.76, 95% CI 1.43–2.17; p < 0.01), with moderate heterogeneity observed (I2 = 37.92%, p = 0.17) (Fig. 2c). Egger’s regression test indicated publication bias. Thus, a trim-and-fill sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the potential impact of funnel plot asymmetry. The trim-and-fill procedure did not impute any additional studies, and the pooled effect estimate and its confidence interval remained unchanged compared with the primary meta-analysis (Figure S6).

Additionally, in pMMR-exclusive cohorts, analysis of two RCTs (n = 352 patients) demonstrated a statistically significant 76% improvement in CR rates with CRT+ICI compared to CRT alone (RR 1.76, 95% CI 1.3–2.37; p < 0.01), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, p = 0.78) (Fig. 2d).

Neoadjuvant-related adverse events

Across 15 studies (n = 916), the pooled incidence of grade 3–4 neoadjuvant-related AEs was 23% (95% CI 15–31%), with considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 88.63%, p < 0.01). The highest rates reported by Rahama et al. (48%) [32], while Yang Z et al. [29] and Yang Y et al. [36] reported the lowest (4%) (Fig. 4a).

Forest plots depicting adverse event rates for neoadjuvant CRT-ICI. (a) pooled rates of grade 3–4 neoadjuvant-related adverse events for CRT-ICI across all included studies (n = 916), (b) Subgroup analysis of pMMR-exclusive studies for grade 3–4 neoadjuvant-related adverse events (n = 391), (c) Pooled rates of grade 3–4 immune-related adverse events for CRT-ICI across all included studies (n = 842), (d) Subgroup analysis of pMMR-exclusive studies for grade 3–4 immune-related adverse events (n = 315)

The pooled grade 3–4 neoadjuvant-related AE rate across seven pMMR studies (n = 391) was 23% (95% CI 11–39%), with high heterogeneity (I²=90.51%, p < 0.01). Extreme values ranged from 4% [26] to 44% [28](Fig. 4b).

RCT subgroup analysis: Pooled data from six RCTs (n = 958 patients) showed a statistically significant difference in neoadjuvant-related AEs between CRT+ICI and CRT alone (RR 1.26, 95% CI 1.01–1.57; p = 0.04), with low heterogeneity (I2 = 3.65%, p = 0.39) (Fig. 2e).

Immune-related adverse events

The pooled analysis of 15 studies (n = 842) revealed a 4% incidence of grade 3–4 irAEs (95% CI 2–9%, I2 = 84.48%, p < 0.01). Rahma et al. [32] reported the highest rate (37%, 95% CI 27–48%), while four studies observed no severe irAEs [20–23] (Fig. 4c).

Analysis of seven pMMR-exclusive studies (n = 315) showed a low pooled incidence of grade 3–4 irAEs (3%, 95% CI 1–7%), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 48.64%, p = 0.06). No severe irAEs were observed in 2 studies [20, 21], while the highest rate was 8% [25] (Fig. 4d).

Surgical adverse events

Nine studies (n = 466) reported grade 3–4 surgical AEs with a pooled incidence of 6% (95% CI 2–10%, I2 = 69.11%, p < 0.01). Michael et al. [23] documented the highest rate (19%, 95% CI 7–34%), while two studies reported no surgical complications [21, 26] (Figure S7a).

Results from six studies reporting grade 3–4 surgical AEs in pMMR cases showed an incidence of 4% (95% CI: 1–9%), with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 67.7%, p = 0.02). Two studies reported no adverse events [21, 26], while Tsukada et al. reported the highest proportion at 11% [25](Figure S7b).

Efficacy by radiotherapy schedule: comparative and single-arm analyses

To determine if the radiotherapy schedule influenced the benefit of adding ICI, we performed a comprehensive subgroup analysis, including both direct comparisons from RCTs and pooled response rates from all available cohorts.

Figure 5 summarizes the analysis of RCTs directly comparing CRT+ICI versus CRT alone, stratified by radiotherapy type. The addition of ICI to SCRT significantly increased pCR rates compared to SCRT alone (RR 1.90, 95% CI 1.36–2.65). For LCRT, a positive but non-significant trend was observed (RR 1.31, 95% CI 0.94–1.81) (Fig. 5a). A similar analysis for CR rates is presented in Fig. 5b. The combination of SCRT+ICI significantly increased CR rates compared to SCRT alone (RR 1.84, 95% CI 1.37–2.47). In contrast, the results for LCRT+ICI were inconsistent and showed no significant benefit over LCRT alone (RR 1.17, 95% CI 0.53–2.59), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 69%).

To contextualize the absolute response rates, we pooled data from all available CRT+ICI cohorts. The pooled pCR rate for SCRT + ICI was 51% (95% CI: 42–61%), significantly surpassing the 33% (95% CI: 29–38%) rate for LCRT + ICI (p for subgroup difference < 0.0001) (Figure S8). Similarly, the pooled CR rate for SCRT + ICI was 59% (95% CI: 37–80%), which was significantly higher than the 36% (95% CI: 8–71%) rate observed for LCRT + ICI (p for subgroup difference = 0.031) (Figure S9).

Discussion

In this largest meta-analysis to date of ICI-augmented neoadjuvant therapy in pMMR LARC, which included 18 clinical trials, we found a doubling of composite CR rates and a substantial improvement in pCR compared with standard CRT, at the cost of higher toxicity. Since the prevalence of dMMR in rectal cancer is very low (less than 5%) [13, 37], studies involving unselected or MMR-unknown populations were presumed to be mostly pMMR and included to provide a comprehensive evidence base; however, results from pMMR-exclusive cohorts were also analyzed separately. Our analysis demonstrates that the CRT-ICI strategy achieves a pooled composite CR rate of 54% in pMMR-specific cohorts, more than doubling the 23.2% CR rate reported for TNT regimens in a recent systematic review [38]. This finding is reinforced by comparative data from randomized trials, which showed that ICI-augmented therapy increased the likelihood of CR by 76% relative to standard CRT alone (RR 1.76, p < 0.01).

The efficacy observed in our meta-analysis can be explained by the profound immunomodulatory effects of radiotherapy on the tumor microenvironment and is a direct clinical reflection of this underlying synergy. Radiotherapy is known to upregulate PD-L1 expression on tumor cells, an adaptive resistance mechanism that simultaneously sensitizes the tumor to ICI [39]. In pMMR tumors, which are typically immunologically “cold” with low T-cell infiltration, radiotherapy

Radiotherapy also induces immunogenic cell death and depletes MDSCs, thereby converting the tumor microenvironment into a “hot”, ICI-responsive state. Together, these synergistic effects overcome the inherent immune resistance of pMMR cancers and explain the marked improvement in pathological and clinical responses observed in our analysis [40–42].

Given the heterogeneity in treatment protocols across the studies, we sought to determine whether specific modalities influenced outcomes. Our subgroup analysis suggests that the radiotherapy schedule may modulate the efficacy of immunotherapy. The addition of ICI to SCRT yielded a statistically significant improvement in pCR/CR rates, whereas its benefit with LCRT was nonsignificant. This differential effect may be explained by the distinct immunogenic profiles of each schedule. The findings by Salarvand et al. provide a compelling biological rationale, demonstrating that SCRT is associated with a significantly greater increase in CD8 + tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes than LCRT, after adjusting for confounders [43]. This suggests that the potent, high-dose fractions of SCRT may create a more favorable tumor microenvironment for subsequent immune checkpoint inhibition. Nevertheless, this observation underscores the need for careful appraisal of long-term outcomes, given that the durability of response with SCRT may be inferior to that of LCRT, as suggested by the extended follow-up of the RAPIDO trial [44].

Our findings are broadly consistent with other recent meta-analyses. Yang et al. analyzed 14 prospective trials (2021–2023) and reported a pooled pCR rate of 36% and a grade 3–4 AE rate of 20%; their pMMR/MSS subgroup analysis showed a pCR rate of 38% (95% CI: 28–49%) [45]. Yu et al.’s meta-analysis of 21 prospective studies from 2021 to 2024 reported a pooled pCR rate of 30.8% (95% CI: 26.8–34.8%) for PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor combinations and a grade ≥ 3 AE rate of 33.9% (95% CI: 21.1–48.0%). The pCR rate for the pMMR cohort was 33.2% [46]. Wang et al. performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of 19 studies (2021–2024), evaluating the efficacy of adding ICIs to neoadjuvant therapy. At the trial level, their analysis of four RCTs comparing neoadjuvant CRT-ICI with CRT alone demonstrated significantly higher pooled odds ratios (ORs) for both overall CR (1.72, 95% CI 1.21–2.44) and pCR (1.68, 95% CI 1.08–2.62), with low-to-moderate heterogeneity favoring the CRT-ICI combination. At the treatment arm level, which included all 19 trials, the pooled CR and pCR rates for CRT-ICI were 42% (36–47%) and 37% (32–42%), respectively, with moderate heterogeneity. These outcomes were significantly superior to CRT alone, which yielded CR and pCR rates of 24% (P < 0.001 for CR; P = 0.008 for pCR), with negligible heterogeneity for CR but moderate heterogeneity for pCR in the CRT-only studies [47].

Our analysis includes recent trials and uses a composite outcome (CR: pCR + cCR) that reflects the increasing use of watch-and-wait strategies for organ preservation. In addition, we specifically evaluated pMMR populations across all endpoints and included direct RCT comparisons. These methodological differences may explain the slightly higher pooled response rates observed in our study. However, the core conclusion remains the same: adding ICI to neoadjuvant CRT improves response rates in pMMR LARC. The inclusion of newer trials reinforces rather than fundamentally changes the existing evidence base.

While our analysis suggests that CRT-ICI combinations increase the incidence of grade 3–4 adverse events compared to CRT alone (RR 1.26, 95% CI 1.01–1.57), this observed toxicity profile must be interpreted in the context of modern, more intensive treatment paradigms. The absolute incidence of grade 3–4 neoadjuvant-related adverse events in the CRT-ICI arm (range: 4–48%) falls within the spectrum reported for TNT regimens (range: 9–41%), which have emerged as a new standard of care for LARC, in prior systematic reviews [5]. Therefore, the incremental increase in toxicity, largely consisting of conventional chemotherapy/radiotherapy-related AEs with a low rate of severe irAEs (3%), should be viewed as a familiar clinical challenge. Thus, the risk-benefit assessment for CRT-ICI appears to favor the combination, given the improvement in pCR/CR rates. The observed increase in toxicities, while significant, does not outweigh the improvement in pCR/CR rates. Therefore, this combination may be considered an acceptable option for the clinical benefit in appropriate pMMR/MSS LARC patients.

This systematic review offers several strengths, including a comprehensive search strategy up to November 2025. By focusing on RCTs, we conducted a comparative analysis of CRT alone versus CRT-ICI, and separately evaluated outcomes for pMMR cases to ensure precision. A key methodological strength is our subgroup analysis based on radiotherapy dose (SCRT vs. LCRT), which provides novel insights into how the treatment schedule may influence the efficacy of immunotherapy combinations. Additionally, our assessment of the composite CR outcome, incorporating both pathological and clinical responses, improved data validity by accounting for studies that employed watch-and-wait strategies. However, this review has various limitations. A significant portion of data from ongoing studies and conference abstracts was excluded due to incomplete key outcomes to ensure robust findings. The characteristics and findings of the ongoing trials are summarized in Table S3 (supplementary file), which provides a comprehensive overview of the current research landscape that may soon address unanswered questions. Heterogeneity was further compounded by variations in patient demographics, mainly including Asian populations. These issues require further validation in other subpopulations. Moreover, since nCRT combined with ICI remains a novel approach, our analysis primarily focused on early outcomes, as long-term survival data are still maturing and require further follow-up. In the future, there is a clear need to conduct adequately powered pMMR-exclusive phase III RCTs, with biomarker-driven selection (such as tumor mutational burden and possibly PD-L1) and dose/fractionation optimization to maximize immunogenicity.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta‑analysis suggests that adding ICI to nCRT is associated with higher pCR and CR rates in pMMR LARC compared with CRT alone. Data from randomized trials show a relative 76% improvement in CR rates, translating to pooled pCR and CR rates of 41% and 54% in pMMR-exclusive cohorts, respectively. Notably, this benefit was significant when ICI was combined with SCRT over SCRT alone and not in LCRT so far, suggesting that the radiotherapy schedule may influence efficacy. Although treatment‑related toxicity is increased, severe immune‑related and surgical adverse events are infrequent. These findings support selective use of ICI‑augmented CRT, particularly when downstaging or organ preservation is a goal. However, larger, more geographically inclusive phase III trials with longer follow‑up are needed to confirm these results and define optimal patient selection.
