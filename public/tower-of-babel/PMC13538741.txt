Diabetic Foot Ulcers Are Clinical Marker of Disease Burden in People With Diabetes and Heart Failure
Authors: A. Rumanes, G. C. M. Linssen, J. J. van Netten, J. E. W. C. van Gemert‐Pijnen, J. G. van Baal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionDiabetic foot ulcers and heart failure are severe complications of diabetes mellitus, both associated with high morbidity and mortality. Whether diabetic foot ulcers worsen prognosis in people with heart failure remains unclear. This study evaluated the association between diabetic foot ulcers, mortality and heart failure hospitalisations in people with diabetes and heart failure.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study including adults with diabetes and heart failure treated at a large, single centre in the Netherlands (2002–2021). People were stratified by diabetic foot ulcer status. Primary outcomes were all‐cause mortality and hospitalisation for decompensated heart failure during 3 years of follow‐up, with additional 5‐year mortality analyses. Multivariable Cox regression was adjusted for age, renal disease, neuropathy, hypertension and other significant comorbidities. To address immortal time bias, an additional time‐varying Cox model was performed.ResultsAmong 4937 people with diabetes and heart failure, 908 had a current or previous diabetic foot ulcer. These people had more comorbidities, including renal disease and neuropathy. Mortality differences were modest during the first 3 years after heart failure diagnosis but became significant at 5 years (32.0% vs. 25.1%, p < 0.001). When follow‐up started at ulcer diagnosis, mortality was substantially higher in the diabetic foot ulcer group (1‐year: 32.9% vs. 6.0%, p < 0.001). Multivariable analysis showed no independent association, whereas the time‐varying Cox model demonstrated an independent association between diabetic foot ulcer occurrence and mortality (HR 2.88, 95% CI 2.26–3.66; p < 0.001). Hospitalization rates showed similar patterns.ConclusionsIn people with diabetes and heart failure, diabetic foot ulcer occurrence was independently associated with increased mortality in the time‐varying analysis. Diabetic foot ulceration may therefore represent an important clinical marker of disease burden and identify people with advanced cardiometabolic disease who may benefit from intensified multidisciplinary management.

Introduction

Among the most disabling complications of diabetes are heart failure and diabetic foot ulcers [1, 2]. Both complications as such are associated with a lower quality of life, an increased risk of hospitalizations and premature mortality [1, 2, 3, 4].

Diabetic foot ulcers are chronic wounds, with peripheral neuropathy and peripheral artery disease among the causative factors [5]. These underlying pathologies are both associated with increased cardiovascular risk, underscoring the systemic nature of diabetic foot ulcers [5]. The yearly incidence of diabetic foot ulcers in people with diabetes is estimated at 2%–6%, while the cumulative lifetime risk can be as high as 34% [2]. The prognosis for people with a diabetic foot ulcer is poor. It is estimated that almost half of these people die within 5 years after diagnosing this condition, which in fact leads to a survival rate worse than many prevalent forms of cancer [6, 7, 8]. In addition to local complications, increasing evidence suggests that the presence of a diabetic foot ulcer is also a marker of extensive systemic atherosclerosis and chronic inflammation [9, 10, 11]. These processes play a central role in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular disease, particularly in the development and progression of heart failure [9, 10, 11].

The relation between diabetes and heart failure is complex and bidirectional: diabetes increases the risk of heart failure at least twofold, while heart failure, in turn, can further disrupt glucose regulation [12]. The prevalence of diabetes among people with heart failure varies in the literature from 4.3% to 28%, while heart failure is present in 12%–57% of people with type 2 diabetes [13].

Recent research suggests that diabetic foot ulceration is not only associated with poor limb‐related outcomes but also with increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.

Meta‐analyses have demonstrated that people with diabetic foot ulcers have a substantially increased risk of cardiovascular events and premature death compared with people with diabetes without ulceration [9, 10, 11, 14]. Furthermore, heart failure has been identified as one of the most frequent and prognostically important cardiovascular comorbidities in people with diabetic foot ulcers, contributing to both reduced survival and increased healthcare utilization [15]. These findings suggest that the coexistence of diabetic foot ulcers and heart failure identifies a particularly vulnerable population, although the prognostic implications of diabetic foot ulcers in people with established heart failure remain poorly understood [15].

There is reason to believe that the presence of a diabetic foot ulcer further worsens the prognosis of heart failure. The combination of systemic inflammation, endothelial damage and impaired microcirculation can contribute to progressive myocardial dysfunction and an increased risk of cardiovascular mortality [1]. Nevertheless, to date, only limited research has been conducted directly comparing people with diabetes and heart failure with and without a diabetic foot ulcer with regard to cardiovascular outcomes [14].

The aim of our study is to investigate whether the presence of a diabetic foot ulcer is associated with worse clinical outcomes in people with both heart failure and diabetes mellitus, compared with people without an ulcer, specifically focusing on all‐cause mortality and heart failure hospitalization. A better understanding of this relationship can contribute not only to improved risk stratification, but also to the development of integrated, multidisciplinary care strategies that address the metabolic, cardiovascular and peripheral complications of diabetes.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Setting

An observational, retrospective cohort study was conducted in a large general hospital in the Eastern part of the Netherlands in which people were included in the period 2002–2021. Our hospital is a regional referral centre for diabetic foot care. We included all people with heart failure and diabetes (type 1 and type 2) in a single cohort. Within this cohort, people were categorized based on the presence or absence of a diabetic foot ulcer. People could have a current ulcer (present at the time of heart failure diagnosis), a history of ulceration prior to heart failure diagnosis, or develop an ulcer after the diagnosis of heart failure during follow‐up. People with a current or previous ulcer were classified in the ulcer group, while people without a history of ulceration were classified in the non‐ulcer group.

All people with heart failure were diagnosed and treated according to the guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology at that point in time [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. All people with diabetic foot ulcers were treated according to protocols based on the Dutch Guidelines [21] and the International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot Guidelines [22].

Participants

Data Collection

Data for this study were obtained from the electronic health records (EHRs) of Ziekenhuisgroep Twente (ZGT). All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Dutch General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). This retrospective observational study used routinely collected clinical data and did not fall within the scope of the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO). Therefore, formal review by a Medical Research Ethics Committee and the requirement for informed consent were waived according to Dutch legislation.

Data were collected using CTcue (CTcue B.V., Amsterdam, The Netherlands), a clinical data retrieval and text mining software used to extract structured and unstructured information from EHRs. Additional data that could not be retrieved through CTcue were collected by a data steward using SQL‐scripts on a copy database of the EHRs of the hospital. Data extraction was performed using predefined rule‐based queries, including keyword searches and regular expression filtering, with iterative manual validation of samples to optimize query performance. No artificial intelligence, deep learning, or neural network–based methods were used.

Diabetes people above 18 years were included. People with gestational diabetes and an age below 18 years were excluded. Diabetes diagnoses were identified through both structured and unstructured data. Structured data were extracted from the national Diagnosis Treatment Combination system, which registers and reimburses healthcare services in The Netherlands based on classified people care episodes. However, as not all diabetes cases are recorded in these structured fields, an additional open‐text search was conducted. A keyword‐based search in CTcue used diabetes‐related terms and synonyms (e.g., Diabetes Mellitus, DM, NIDDM, IDDM, Dm2, Dm1, Diabetes, Type 1 Diabetes, Type 2 Diabetes), yielding approximately 88 000 initial results. Data extraction queries were iteratively refined and manually verified to optimize retrieval performance and minimize false‐positive classifications.

Using this data mining technique, an initial population was identified based on a diagnosis of diabetes mellitus and all people registered within our hospital with diabetes mellitus type 1 or type 2 were included. Subsequently, within this population, people diagnosed with heart failure were identified using data mining and Diagnosis Treatment Combination coding. Based on this selection, the study database was constructed.

Within the group of people with both diabetes mellitus and heart failure, the presence of a foot ulcer was assessed. Because foot ulcers are not always explicitly registered as a ‘diabetic foot’—particularly in cases where concomitant ischemia is present and the condition may be coded as peripheral vascular disease—additional data mining procedures were applied via CTcue. This approach was used to determine whether people had a (history of a) foot ulcer and, if applicable, to identify the date of the first recorded ulcer. Importantly, the final study cohort therefore consisted of people with diabetes mellitus and heart failure, with or without a history of a foot ulcer.

Mortality data were obtained from the EHRs and verified through linkage with national municipal population registry data. Hospital admissions were identified through data extraction of the EHR. All admissions for the study population were retrieved if they met the following criteria: admission to a clinical ward or intensive care unit, cardiology or intensive care specialty and availability of both admission and discharge dates. For each admission, admission and discharge dates, length of stay, admission type, specialty and available treatment and diagnosis information were extracted.

Because the reason for admission was not recorded in a structured format, treatment and diagnosis notes were used to identify the clinical indication for admission. Given the heterogeneous terminology used to describe decompensated heart failure, we extracted all admissions for the included people for which the criteria described above applied, and then applied a text‐filter on the treatment and diagnosis notes. If any of the following terms were present, the clinical indication for admission was determined to be heart failure: ‘decomp’, ‘HF’, ‘orthopneu’, ‘cordis’ or ‘DC’.

Variables

People Demographics

We obtained data on demography, cardiovascular disease conditions, renal failure and peripheral neuropathy (defined as loss of protective sensation based on 10‐g monofilament tests) [22] from the EHRs. An ulcer was defined as a full‐thickness lesion of the skin [23]. Heart failure diagnosis was based on the ESC Guidelines on heart failure (i.e., based on symptoms, blood biomarker (NT‐proBNP) and echocardiographic parameters) at that point in time. Heart failure diagnoses were established by the treating cardiologist according to the applicable ESC heart failure guidelines [16, 17, 18, 19, 20].

Outcomes

Clinical outcomes of interest were hospital admission for decompensated heart failure and all‐cause mortality. All‐cause mortality was evaluated over a follow‐up period of 5 years, whereas hospital admission for decompensated heart failure was assessed over 3 years of follow‐up. Follow‐up started from the defined index date in each analytical scenario and ended at the time of first hospital admission, death, or at the end of the predefined follow‐up period.

As described above, two groups were defined based on the presence or absence of a diabetic foot ulcer. To account for the bidirectional temporal relationship between heart failure and diabetic foot ulcers, three analytical scenarios were constructed. These scenarios allowed evaluation of outcomes according to the timing of ulcer occurrence relative to the diagnosis of heart failure.

Scenario 1 included all people from both groups. Follow‐up started at the date of heart failure diagnosis for both groups.

Scenario 2 evaluated people who developed a diabetic foot ulcer after the diagnosis of heart failure. People without an ulcer entered follow‐up at the time of heart failure diagnosis. People in the ulcer group were included only if the ulcer diagnosis occurred after the diagnosis of heart failure, with follow‐up starting at the date of ulcer diagnosis. People with a previous ulcer at the time of heart failure diagnosis were excluded from this scenario.

Scenario 3 evaluated people with a history of diabetic foot ulcer preceding heart failure diagnosis. People in the ulcer group were included only if the ulcer diagnosis occurred before the diagnosis of heart failure. People without ulcers entered follow‐up at the time of heart failure diagnosis. In both groups, follow‐up started at the date of heart failure diagnosis. People who developed an ulcer on or after the diagnosis of heart failure were excluded from this scenario.

Statistical Methods

Descriptive statistics were used to analyse baseline people characteristics and outcomes. Continuous variables were presented as mean with standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or median with interquartile range (IQR) for non‐normally distributed data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Differences between groups were assessed using the Chi‐square test, Student's t‐test, or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate.

Univariable Cox proportional hazards analyses were performed to evaluate associations between individual clinical variables and all‐cause mortality. Subsequently, a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was constructed to assess the association between diabetic foot ulcers and mortality. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and previous literature and included age, sex, renal disease, neuropathy, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), prior cerebrovascular accident and hypertension. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p value < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

To address potential immortal time bias in people who developed diabetic foot ulcers after heart failure diagnosis, an additional Cox proportional hazards analysis was performed modelling diabetic foot ulcer status as a time‐varying exposure. All people entered follow‐up at the time of heart failure diagnosis. People contributed person‐time to the non‐ulcer state until the date of first diabetic foot ulcer diagnosis and to the ulcer state thereafter. The model was adjusted for age, gender, renal disease, neuropathy, COPD, prior cerebrovascular accident and hypertension. This analysis was performed using R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the survival package.

Discussion

In this study of people with diabetes mellitus and heart failure, the occurrence of a diabetic foot ulcer was associated with increased mortality, particularly when the ulcer developed during follow‐up. These findings suggest that the development of a diabetic foot ulcer may represent a clinically important phase of deterioration in people with advanced cardiometabolic disease. The association between diabetic foot ulcers and clinical outcomes differed depending on the timing of follow‐up initiation. When follow‐up started at the time of heart failure diagnosis, mortality differences between people with and without diabetic foot ulcers were modest during the first years of follow‐up, but became more apparent over longer‐term follow‐up. In contrast, when follow‐up started at the time of ulcer diagnosis, mortality was substantially higher among people with diabetic foot ulcers, with clear differences observed early after ulcer occurrence. A similar temporal pattern was observed for hospital admissions for decompensated heart failure. We suggest that the occurrence of a diabetic foot ulcer may represent a clinically important deterioration phase in people with diabetes mellitus and heart failure.

The additional time‐varying Cox analysis provided important insight into the temporal relationship between diabetic foot ulcer occurrence and mortality. Whereas the conventional multivariable model evaluated diabetic foot ulcer status as a baseline characteristic, the time‐varying approach incorporated the timing of ulcer occurrence during follow‐up. This analysis demonstrated that diabetic foot ulcer occurrence was independently associated with a nearly threefold increased risk of all‐cause mortality. These findings suggest that diabetic foot ulceration identifies people entering a particularly vulnerable stage of cardiometabolic disease. The occurrence of a diabetic foot ulcer may therefore reflect progression to a later stage of systemic cardiometabolic disease, characterized by progressive vascular damage, inflammation and multimorbidity [9, 10, 11]. Consequently, the high mortality observed after ulcer diagnosis likely reflects disease progression rather than a direct causal effect of the ulcer itself.

Conventional multivariable analysis suggested that the excess mortality associated with diabetic foot ulcers was largely explained by differences in baseline characteristics. However, because people developing diabetic foot ulcers after heart failure diagnosis are susceptible to immortal time bias, a time‐varying Cox analysis was performed. After accounting for the timing of ulcer occurrence, diabetic foot ulceration was independently associated with mortality. People with diabetic foot ulcers also had significantly higher rates of comorbidities, including renal disease and neuropathy, which are established prognostic factors in heart failure and leading to higher costs of healthcare utilization [24]. Therefore, diabetic foot ulceration appears both to reflect advanced systemic disease and to identify transition to a markedly higher‐risk clinical state [15, 25, 26, 27, 28]. The results of the time‐varying analysis suggest that the timing of ulcer occurrence carries important prognostic information beyond baseline comorbidity alone.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated increased mortality among people with diabetic foot ulcers compared with those with diabetes alone, with five‐year mortality rates comparable to several malignancies [6, 7, 8]. Recent meta‐analyses also report increased cardiovascular risk in people with diabetic foot ulcers [14]. However, most studies evaluated general diabetes populations rather than people with established heart failure. Our findings suggest that diabetic foot ulcer occurrence provides important prognostic information in people with established heart failure and that ulcer development marks a particularly vulnerable phase of disease.

Several pathophysiological mechanisms may explain the association between diabetic foot ulcers and adverse outcomes. The relationship between diabetes and heart failure is complex and involves multiple metabolic disturbances. Hyperglycaemia, insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia and lipotoxicity contribute to structural myocardial changes such as hypertrophy, fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction, as well as to microvascular damage and reduced myocardial perfusion [1]. These processes may promote the development and progression of heart failure [1]. In addition, diabetic foot ulcers are strongly associated with peripheral artery disease, endothelial dysfunction and chronic inflammation, which also contribute to heart failure progression through microvascular damage, myocardial fibrosis and impaired perfusion [1, 9, 10, 11]. Chronic infection and inflammation may further promote catabolic states and frailty, thereby worsening prognosis [9, 10, 11]. Nevertheless, our results suggest that these mechanisms mainly reflect shared underlying pathology rather than a direct causal pathway. Accordingly, diabetic foot ulceration should not be interpreted as the direct cause of mortality, but rather as a manifestation of progressive cardiometabolic deterioration associated with a substantially increased risk of adverse outcomes.

A similar pattern was observed for hospital admissions due to decompensated heart failure. No differences were seen when follow‐up started at heart failure diagnosis, whereas admission rates were higher when follow‐up started at ulcer diagnosis or among people with a history of diabetic foot ulcers. This supports the concept that diabetic foot ulcers represent a marker of disease progression and vulnerability.

From a clinical perspective, the occurrence of a diabetic foot ulcer in people with diabetes and heart failure should be considered a signal of increased risk and advanced systemic disease. Intensified cardiovascular monitoring and multidisciplinary management may therefore be warranted. Integrated care models addressing metabolic, cardiovascular and peripheral complications simultaneously have also been emphasized in recent guidelines and reviews [5, 15].

Strengths of this study include the large real‐life cohort and the evaluation of different temporal scenarios between heart failure and ulcer development. A further strength of this study is the additional time‐varying analysis, which allowed the temporal relationship between heart failure and diabetic foot ulcer development to be evaluated while accounting for immortal time bias. Several limitations should be acknowledged. Despite adjustment for clinically relevant covariates, residual confounding cannot be excluded because information on heart failure phenotype, left ventricular ejection fraction, diabetes duration, ulcer severity (e.g., Wagner/University of Texas classification), glycaemic control, body mass index, smoking status and medication use was unavailable. Furthermore, diabetes subtype (type 1 versus type 2 diabetes) and diabetes duration were not consistently available throughout the study period and could therefore not be incorporated into the analyses. Consequently, potential differences in prognosis between diabetes phenotypes could not be evaluated and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Furthermore, the retrospective single‐centre design limits causal inference and generalizability. In addition, hospital admissions and neuropathy may have been underestimated because of limitations in the availability and completeness of recorded clinical information.

In conclusion, in people with diabetes mellitus and heart failure, diabetic foot ulcer occurrence was independently associated with increased mortality in the time‐varying analysis. These findings suggest that diabetic foot ulceration is an important clinical marker of advanced disease burden and identifies people with advanced cardiometabolic disease who may benefit from intensified cardiovascular monitoring and multidisciplinary management.
