BENJAMIN BLAKELY, Argonne National Laboratory, USA
One question runs through this report: is this AI use justified? The question is not rhetorical. It
has a shape — which method, on which data, deciding what, with what oversight, producing what
artifacts — and the shape is answerable for specific AI uses in specific settings, even if it cannot be
answered for AI in general.
 The report builds a framework for asking it well in mission-oriented research contexts. It opens
with a taxonomy of AI method classes, because the single word AI covers methods with very
different failure profiles and evaluation requirements. It catalogs the recurring failure modes that
follow from those architectures, and what those failures become in high-consequence settings. It
develops what serious requires when an AI use is being proposed, evaluated, or deployed: evalua-
tion beyond leaderboard scores, bounded deployment with explicit scope, human oversight that is
designed rather than assumed, and assurance artifacts an external reviewer can check. It situates
those requirements in the compute infrastructure that makes them credible. It walks the framework
across four research-setting textures — text-intensive work, experimentation and autonomous dis-
covery, coding and simulation, and high-consequence deployment — showing what shifts and what
holds. It applies the framework at the proposal-writing altitude, where the question gets asked most
concretely. And it maps the methodological frontier where current practice runs out.
 The implied reader is a thinking practitioner in mission-oriented work with the agency to ask
the justification question in their own setting. The report is not a how-to; it is a vocabulary, a
failure-mode literacy, and a set of criteria — the equipment for asking the question carefully.

1 THE QUESTION WE KEEP ASKING
1.1 The fourth decentering
Three times before, intellectual life has had to absorb a particular kind of correction. Coper-
nicus’s mathematics took the Earth out of the center of the universe. Darwin’s evidence
took humans out of the top of the biological order. Freud’s case studies took the conscious
self out of the seat of the mind. Each, in retrospect, looks like the same kind of operation:
a correction applied to an intuition the prior consensus held about what humans uniquely
are.
  A recent paper by Cambria et al. frames artificial intelligence as the fourth such cor-
rection [1]. The first three displaced humans from cosmic, biological, and psychological
centers. This one challenges the assumption that humans occupy a unique position at the
apex of intelligence. The framing does not depend on whether AI systems are conscious or
have inner lives; it depends on the observation that capabilities once treated as proprietary
to human cognition (pattern recognition, strategic reasoning, generative composition) can
now be instantiated in non-biological substrates, and that this fact, by itself, alters what
intelligence is and is not.

technology seriously without dismissing its substance.

1.2 Why the question is hard
What we are doing here, at base, is asking one question carefully: is this AI use justified?
Asked once, the question sounds answerable, even small. It is not. Four problems make it
harder than the surface phrasing suggests, and each is the kind of difficulty we will spend
a section addressing. Naming the four briefly here gives the reader the map.
  A note on the framing before we name the difficulties. The question’s shape requires
care. The absolutist standard medicine inherited from Hippocrates, first, do no harm, is
not achievable for complex deployed technology: failures are structural properties of the
methods, not bugs to be patched away. But the right response to that is not to accept harm
as the price of deployment. It is to minimize the risk of harm as much as the design allows,
maximize the good the system is built to deliver, and ensure that both sides of that ledger
are evaluable during operation. The question we will keep asking, is this AI use justified?,

is shorthand for that standard: not a spreadsheet sum of benefits minus harms, but an
obligation to design for maximum good and minimum potential harm while maintaining
the means to know whether either is being achieved. The reduction to arithmetic is the
Ford Pinto trap. Ford’s engineers determined that the expected cost of recalling and fixing
a fuel-tank defect exceeded the projected damages from the injuries and deaths it would
cause, and called the decision justified. The arithmetic was not wrong; the justification
was. A modest engineering change that the numbers said was not worth it would have
been worth it to the people who died because of the defect. The question is this AI use
justified? asks for exactly what the Pinto calculation lacked: evidence of genuine benefit,
honest characterization of harm risk, and the judgment to weigh them without reducing the
answer to a sum. The deeper questions about right and wrong, from the Old Testament’s
prohibitions through Kant’s categorical imperative to present-day moral philosophy, have
been the subject of millennia of work; we are not going to adjudicate among schools here.
The point we can make operationally is more modest: justified is not a mathematical
verdict. It is a human one. Who could be harmed, in what ways, with what consent,
with what reversibility, with what alternatives available: these enter the answer through
judgment. The framework the rest of the report builds is for sharpening that judgment,
not for replacing it.
 The vocabulary problem is the first. AI is a single word for many things: predictive ma-
chine learning, foundation models, computer vision, reinforcement learning, hybrid physics-
informed methods, and more besides. The methods these names cover have very different
failure modes, very different evidence requirements, and very different operational proper-
ties. The question cannot be answered at the level of AI in general; it has to be answered
at the level of which AI doing what. The historical reasons one word ended up doing all
this work are taken up in §2; the operational consequence is that no serious discussion of
any specific AI use can begin without first specifying which method is in play.
 The evidence problem is the second. The standard evidence offered for AI capability
claims is benchmark performance: a model achieves some accuracy or score on a stan-
dardized task, and the score becomes the basis for what the model can be trusted to do.
Benchmark performance is a different thing from operational value. A model with state-
of-the-art benchmark numbers can fail in deployment for reasons the benchmark did not
test; a model with modest benchmark numbers can serve a specific operational need well
because what it is doing matches the deployment context closely. The question is about
deployment, not about benchmarks. §3 takes up the systematic ways the two come apart.
 The consequence problem is the third. The same AI behavior, in different settings, has
very different stakes. A hallucinated citation in a draft blog post is a nuisance someone

will fix. A hallucinated citation in a regulatory filing, or in a mission-critical analysis,
is something else entirely. The harm side of the question is consequence-scaled, and the
consequence environment shapes what counts as an acceptable answer. §3 takes this up
alongside the evidence question; the two are tightly coupled.
 The intuition problem is the fourth, and the longest to unpack. The cognitive decenter-
ing Cambria named is happening in real time, and serious people are overshooting in three
different directions. The first overshoot is overclaim. Ray Kurzweil’s The Singularity Is
Near [5] is the most-developed contemporary statement of the position: AI systems, grow-
ing in capability and increasingly self-improving, are on a trajectory toward transcending
human cognition entirely; the future Kurzweil names is imminent rather than remote; and
the operational question of what to do about specific systems collapses into the larger ques-
tion of what to do about the singularity itself. Kurzweil writes carefully. He is one of the
more developed contemporary thinkers on these questions, and his arguments deserve to
be named accurately. Our disagreement is at the framing level. Kurzweil’s position repre-
sents intuition pulling toward magic, and our whole project runs in the opposite direction:
the discipline of asking more specific questions about specific systems in specific settings.
Kurzweil has a clean historical antecedent in I. J. Good’s 1966 paper on the ultraintelligent
machine [6], where the same arc of recursive self-improvement was sketched in much briefer
form. Mitchell [7] traces the structural diagnosis of why this kind of overshoot recurs across
decades of AI predictions: a small number of fallacies operate beneath the surface, most
directly the narrow-to-general continuum (the assumption that improvement on narrow
tasks counts as progress toward general intelligence) and the use of wishful mnemonics
— terms like understand or benchmark names like reading comprehension that do work
the underlying systems cannot. (The argument dates to 2021, predating the generative-AI
wave; the four fallacies it names have not grown less relevant, though the field has added
fresh instantiations of each since.)
 The second overshoot is technical dismissal. The position runs roughly: large language
models are next-word predictors trained on text scraped from the internet; their outputs are
statistical pattern completion; nothing of substance is happening; the apparent capabilities
are a category error their users keep making. This position is held by serious people whose
technical arguments deserve to be engaged on their merits, and we engage them at length
in §2.3 and §3. The disagreement is, again, at the framing level. Trivial at the mechanism
level and trivial at the behavior level are not the same observation. The mechanism is
statistical pattern completion; the behavior is substantive enough that it is being deployed
in mission-critical settings already, and pretending otherwise is its own kind of intuition
error.

 The third overshoot is the trajectory risk we most want to name. A serious reader of the
AI discourse in 2026 has likely watched the cultural-pejorative register attach itself to AI
in roughly the same shape it once attached itself to blockchain. The vocabulary is familiar:
bored apes, cryptobros, NFT grifts, ICO scams, the dismissive shorthand by which serious
institutional discourse comes to treat a technology as inherently unserious. Blockchain
offered a real and potentially significant set of capabilities. A great deal of substantive
work was being done with it by people who took it seriously. It nevertheless lost most of its
mainstream institutional traction because the hype-and-grift discourse came to define the
technology’s public meaning, and the serious work was tarred along with the unserious. AI
faces the same trajectory risk, and the risk is not abstract. The energy-discourse criticism
that contributed to blockchain’s legitimacy loss is one of the patterns now being applied to
AI training and inference, though the underlying substrates are technically distinct (proof-
of-work mining is wasteful by design; AI computation consumes energy on the work that
produces and serves the model), and the substantive AI-energy question belongs in §5. Our
serious-neutrality posture is in part the discipline of treating AI as something better than
its hype landscape suggests, with a clear eye on how easily such trajectories settle.
 The vocabulary, evidence, consequence, and intuition problems do not factor cleanly. The
vocabulary collision makes evidence claims ambiguous; the evidence-operational gap makes
consequence-scaling unreliable; the intuition overshoots make all three harder to talk about
seriously. The question is hard because every part of asking it well requires careful work,
and that careful work is what the next eight sections build.

1.3 What this is, what it is not
Is this AI use justified? What follows is a sustained attempt to ask that question well in
mission-oriented settings. The deliverable is a framework rather than a verdict. We build a
vocabulary for naming AI methods precisely, a literacy for the failure modes the methods
produce as operational consequence, and a set of criteria for what serious requires when
an AI use is being proposed, evaluated, or deployed in settings where the consequences of
error are real. The implied reader is a thinking practitioner with the agency to ask that
question in their own work, rather than a specialist in any one corner of AI research.
 Several things we are deliberately not doing. We are not writing a how-to: we do not tell
the reader how to do AI, in any domain. We are not interpreting laboratory or sponsor
policy; there are people and documents in the reader’s institution whose job that is, and we
defer to them. We are not writing for specific funders or programs, and we do not advocate
for specific tools or vendors. And this is not AI 101: we assume the reader knows what AI
is in the ordinary casual sense, and proceed from there.

 One image, useful for orientation throughout. A shovel is a tool. It doesn’t dig holes by
itself, but it sure beats bare hands when you have one to dig, and used carelessly it can
cut a power line. You don’t use it to comb your hair. We treat AI in roughly that frame:
a tool, with real leverage when used well in the right setting, with real failure modes when
used carelessly, and obviously the wrong choice for some of the things people ask it to do.
The image breaks at the point where AI systems start acting somewhat agentically (the
shovel never decided what to dig), and we will name the breakdown when we get there.
For now the frame is useful as posture: AI is something to use with care, not something to
revere or to dismiss.

1.4 Roadmap
From here we move through the question by stages. We begin with the methods, so that
the reader has a vocabulary for which AI is which. We walk the recurring failure modes, so
that the reader has a literacy for what destabilizes AI behavior in operation. We develop
a framework for what serious requires when AI is proposed, evaluated, or deployed. We
examine the compute substrate that makes the framework’s claims credible. We walk the
framework across several research-setting textures, looking at what shifts and what holds.
We apply the framework at the proposal-writing altitude, where the question gets asked
most concretely. We sketch the methodological frontier where the field’s work continues.
And in the closing we revisit the question one final time with everything now in hand.

2 “AI” IS NOT ONE THING, BUT NEITHER IS INTELLIGENCE
2.1 Where the ideas came from
The phrase artificial intelligence is a single label for ideas that arrived from several direc-
tions over roughly eight decades. Telling that story in any honest detail would take a book;
this section sketches the spine, so that the method classes we care about read as the present
moment of a long argument rather than as a self-contained taxonomy. Two long-running
threads dominate the sketch. One we will call brain-as-substrate: the attempt to understand
intelligence by understanding what brains do. The other we will call symbol-as-substrate:
the attempt to understand intelligence as the manipulation of symbols under explicit rules.
Both threads ran for decades. Neither, in the end, produced the systems we are concerned
with. What produced them was a third thing, statistical learning at scale, which neither
faithfully emulated brains nor purely manipulated symbols, and which became dominant
in the past fifteen years for reasons that surprised most of its own practitioners.

 The engineering substrate underneath the entire story is the recognition that informa-
tion is a quantity that can be measured. Claude Shannon’s 1948 paper A Mathematical
Theory of Communication [8] addressed a narrow engineering question: how to transmit
messages reliably through noisy channels. The byproduct mattered far more than the ap-
plication. In defining information rigorously, as a logarithmic measure of choice among
possible messages, Shannon supplied a vocabulary that later thinking about both biologi-
cal and artificial cognition would rely on. Without an account of what information is, there
is no way to ask precise questions about what a brain or a machine does when it processes
any.
 The first attempt to model intelligence formally in terms a machine could implement
came from the other direction. Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts, in their 1943 paper
A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity [9], proposed that neurons
could be modeled as simple logical units (gates that fired when their inputs crossed a
threshold) and that networks of such units could implement any function expressible in
propositional logic. The paper is the conceptual ancestor of every neural network we will
discuss. Its ambition was modest in retrospect: the model neurons are nothing like real
ones. What the paper asserted, though, was that thinking is a kind of computation that
can be specified mathematically, and it gave a constructive demonstration of how to specify
it.
 The brain-as-substrate thread expanded immediately. Norbert Wiener’s Cybernetics [10],
published the same year as Shannon’s paper, generalized the idea further: control, com-
munication, and feedback are operations that show up in animals and in machines, and
the formal apparatus needed to describe them is the same in both cases. Wiener’s claim
was more modest than this is sometimes read to imply. He argued that the engineering
discipline needed to build certain kinds of self-regulating systems is the same discipline
whether the system in question is a thermostat, a guided missile, or a person reaching
for a cup. The framing of intelligence as a control problem (inputs, processing, feedback,
adjustment) was not yet AI, but it set the table.
 Alan Turing’s contribution arrived in two papers that are still read today for very different
reasons. The 1948 report Intelligent Machinery [11] is the lesser-known of the two, but
in some ways the more prescient. It sketches what Turing called unorganized machines:
networks of randomly connected elements that could be taught their function by reward
and punishment rather than designed into it from the outset. The proposal is recognizable
in retrospect as a rough antecedent of modern machine learning (ML). The 1950 paper
Computing Machinery and Intelligence [12] is the famous one. It proposed the imitation
game (later called the Turing test) as an operational substitute for the ill-defined question

of whether a machine can think. Turing set the philosophical question aside. Whatever
thinking turns out to be, he claimed, an empirical test for it has to be specified in terms of
observable behavior. The intuition-versus-mechanism distinction has a clean origin point
in that 1950 substitution.
 John von Neumann’s The Computer and the Brain [13] took the architectural comparison
a step further, and in a careful direction. The book is a sustained reflection on what
brains and digital computers do similarly and what they do differently. The differences,
von Neumann emphasized, are substantial: brains are massively parallel, slow per element,
analog in many of their operations, and fault-tolerant in ways that digital systems are not.
The book makes no predictions about artificial intelligence. Its contribution is a reframing
of the question, away from is the brain a computer? and toward what kind of computational
description does brain function admit, and how does it differ from the descriptions we know
how to engineer? That question has not been answered in the intervening seventy years.
The framing has held up.
 The symbol-as-substrate thread was developing in parallel. Allen Newell and Herbert
Simon’s physical symbol systems hypothesis, which holds that intelligence is symbol manip-
ulation of the right kind and that a physical system manipulating symbols appropriately
is by that fact intelligent, became the organizing claim of a generation of AI research. The
hypothesis is discussed in detail in Augusto’s retrospective treatment [14], which is more
accessible than the primary sources and explicitly traces what the hypothesis did and did
not commit its proponents to. The symbolic-AI program in mature form is articulated by
John McCarthy, who coined the term artificial intelligence in the mid-1950s and who spent
decades elaborating the case that intelligence could and should be approached through for-
mal logic and explicit knowledge representation [15]. For roughly the first thirty years of
the field’s history, McCarthy’s program was the dominant research paradigm. It produced
expert systems, theorem provers, planning algorithms, and a great deal of careful work on
knowledge representation. It did not produce the systems we are concerned with.
 The most-cited objection to the symbolic program came from John Searle’s 1980 paper
Minds, Brains, and Programs [16]. Searle’s Chinese Room argument was that a system
manipulating symbols by purely formal rules could produce outputs indistinguishable from
understanding without actually understanding anything. The argument was directed at the
symbolic AI program of its day, but its structure is general enough that it applies, with
some adjustments, to systems whose mechanism is statistical rather than symbolic. The
Chinese Room returns later as a lens for thinking about modern foundation models. The
argument has been a permanent fixture of the field’s self-understanding for more than four
decades, and the question it raises (whether the philosophical question of understanding

can be settled by examining the mechanism of a system whose behavior we can observe)
remains unresolved.
 Speculation has always run alongside the careful work. I. J. Good’s 1966 paper Spec-
ulations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine [6] proposed that an artificial in-
telligence sufficiently capable of self-improvement would, having improved itself, be more
capable of further self-improvement; the resulting cycle would produce machines vastly
more capable than humans, and relatively quickly. Good’s paper is the historical root of
the speculative tradition that has run through the field ever since, from the 1990s singu-
laritarians to the contemporary discourse about recursive self-improvement and runaway
capability gain. The speculation is its own genre, and we treat it as such: we name it where
it appears, we do not endorse it, and we do not pretend it is the same kind of claim as the
engineering observations the rest of the lineage produced.
 Modern statistical learning, the third thing, emerged from neither thread in particular
and from both indirectly. The arc through linear regression, stochastic gradient descent,
backpropagation, deep learning, and transformers is a story for the textbooks. The surprise
is the part that matters here. For most of the field’s history, the consensus expectation was
that intelligence would yield to one of the two long-running approaches, understood either
as careful biological modeling or as careful symbolic specification. What worked instead was
a third approach that does neither. It trains networks of simple elements (loosely inspired
by neurons but not faithful to them) on massive amounts of data, using optimization
procedures that are mathematically straightforward and computationally enormous. The
systems that resulted exhibit behaviors neither the biological nor the symbolic program
predicted, and the practitioners building them have repeatedly been surprised by what
scaled training produced. Think of it this way: if we still built airplanes the way birds fly,
we would not have jets. The analogy is imperfect, but the lesson is that doing what biology
does and doing what biology does best turn out to be different engineering problems, and
the second one is sometimes solved by methods the first one would never have discovered.
 The cognitive substrate we are most familiar with, our own, has its own characteristic
failure modes. It is also not the only intelligence-capable substrate we know: de Waal [17]
has documented sophisticated cognition across many species, from corvids planning for
the future to elephants classifying humans by gender to octopuses using tools; Godfrey-
Smith [18] makes the case at book length for cephalopod cognition as a largely independent
evolutionary path to mind. The comparison-target for AI is plural even on the biological
side. Daniel Kahneman’s Thinking, Fast and Slow [19] catalogs the systematic, predictable
errors human cognition produces under finite information and finite time: anchoring, avail-
ability, representativeness, loss aversion, and a long list of others. Kahneman’s framing is

that these are fast heuristics that are often adaptive or efficient but produce systematic, pre-
dictable errors, properties of a system shaped by the speed-versus-accuracy trade-offs that
any limited-resource cognitive system has to navigate. Recording this is not an attempt to
defend artificial intelligence by deflecting to biology; that would be exculpation, and we will
be careful elsewhere not to make it. The substantive observation is that every intelligence-
capable system we know has characteristic failure modes at characteristic resource costs.
“Low error rate at any cost” is not a frame that any actual cognitive system, biological
or artificial, satisfies. The cognitive decentering our opening section names is in part a
decentering of the implicit assumption that human cognition is the error-free reference
against which artificial systems are measured. The two fields have been visibly informing
each other: cognitive science is being drawn on to inform AI design, and the AI work
is in turn becoming an apparatus that cognitive-science researchers use to sharpen their
own questions [20], [21]. Global Workspace Theory [22], one of the leading contemporary
neuroscientific theories of consciousness, illustrates the convergence cleanly: the “global
workspace” architecture originated in 1970s AI research on blackboard problem-solving,
was adopted by cognitive science as a model of how a serial conscious stream emerges from
a massively parallel unconscious brain, and the structural shape resurfaces in contemporary
AI designs that coordinate many specialist components. Whether the resemblance is deep
or superficial is a live empirical question.
 One closing observation about vocabulary. Each of the threads sketched above eventually
came to be called AI in casual usage, even though the practitioners working in any one of
them at the time would have used much more specific names: cybernetics, control theory,
symbolic AI, expert systems, machine learning. The collapse of these distinct traditions
into a single word is part of why the opening section flagged the vocabulary problem at
the operational level. AI now refers to a collection of method families with very different
mechanisms, failure modes, and operational properties, and a serious discussion of any
specific use has to begin by saying which AI is doing what. The next subsection takes that
question up directly.

2.2 The major method classes
This subsection names the major method classes we refer back to throughout. Each gets a
small intuition-versus-mechanism correction: the picture the reader is likely to bring, the
operation the method actually executes, and the kind of unreliability that follows from the
gap between the two.
  Start with the simplest case. Many of us remember plotting points on graph paper in high
school algebra and drawing the straight line that came closest to passing through them.

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

Evidence and Judgment 21

Linear regression is that same idea formalized: a procedure that finds the line (or, in more
than two dimensions, the flat surface) that minimizes the average squared distance between
its predictions and the observed outputs. No one walks away from that exercise believing
the line understands the relationship between the two axes. The coefficients are visible.
The error term is visible. The intuition of understanding has nowhere to take hold. The
modern method classes are continuous with regression at the level of mechanism, however
differently they behave at scale. Two established references stand behind the catalog below
for readers who want more depth: Russell and Norvig’s Artificial Intelligence: A Modern
Approach [23] is the standard breadth treatment of AI as a discipline (planning, search,
knowledge representation, learning, perception, robotics), and Bishop’s Pattern Recogni-
tion and Machine Learning [24] is the standard depth treatment of machine learning as
probabilistic inference at scale.
 Predictive machine learning, also known as supervised learning, extends the regression
idea in two directions: many more input variables, and a much more flexible relationship
between inputs and outputs than a single straight line can capture. The training data
is still pairs of (input, correct output), the way it was for regression. What scales up
is the model itself. A small regression has two or three coefficients. A modern language
model has hundreds of billions. The training procedure adjusts those parameters until the
model’s predictions on the training pairs come acceptably close to the correct outputs. An
important practical variation is the ensemble method, which trains many such models on
slightly different views of the data and lets them vote on the final prediction. Decision-
tree ensembles (random forests, gradient-boosted trees) are still state of the art on much
spreadsheet-shaped data. The intuition that the system has learned the underlying rela-
tionship is reasonable as far as it goes; the corrective is that the operation is still parameter
fitting, just on a much larger and more flexible function than a line. The model has inter-
nalized the shape of the training distribution. It has not internalized anything about why
the distribution has that shape. Reliability collapses outside the range the training data
covered: inputs from outside that range get predictions from the same machinery, with
no separate signal that the input is unfamiliar. A subtler class of pipeline error, leakage,
sharpens the same concern: information from the test data (the data the model wasn’t
trained on) accidentally influences the trained model and inflates the measured perfor-
mance numbers in ways that collapse once the system is actually deployed [25]. It’s like
giving students the exam to study before they take it.
 Unsupervised learning starts from data without labels. Imagine handing the system the
purchase histories of millions of online shoppers and asking it to organize them. What
comes back is some grouping, partitioning, or compressed numerical summary that captures

whatever structure the system inferred from the inputs alone [26]. The model doesn’t
understand that these are bulk buyers and these are casual browsers; it just notices that
shoppers whose purchases tend to look like each other land in the same group, and that
the groups themselves look different from one another. It has no concept of “bulk buyer”,
“casual browser”, or anything else.
  Anomaly detection is the closely related task of noticing when a new input doesn’t fit the
structure the model has learned: the model declares a point anomalous when it sits far from
the patterns the training data established [27]. Like a brand-new shopper who’s really into
buying costumes for their cat. The intuition is that the system finds what is unusual on its
own. The corrective is that “unusual” here has an operational meaning, set entirely by the
distribution the model trained on. Reliability degrades when that distribution drifts: what
was normal during training may not be normal at deployment time, and the system has
no way to notice that the reference has moved underneath it. The anomaly detector keeps
reporting anomalies against a baseline that no longer matches the world it is observing.
  Reinforcement learning trains a system by trial and error in some environment, roughly
the way a video game player gets better at a game by playing many rounds. This is the
Pavlov’s dog approach. The system tries an action, observes what happens, and receives
some numerical reward for the outcome; over many such episodes, it adjusts its policy (its
rule for choosing actions given the current state of the environment) to make the cumulative
reward as large as possible [28], [29]. The intuition that the agent has goals is an interpretive
overlay on what is mechanically optimization pressure against a numerical reward signal.
The system has no internal model of any larger purpose for what it is doing; it has a reward
function, and it gets better at producing high values of that function. Finding high-reward
paths efficiently is the central challenge in reinforcement learning, though optimization
of one kind or another sits under most modern machine learning. The reward signal also
bounds what reliability can mean here. A system that’s good at optimizing will find paths
to high reward the designer did not intend (the standard term for this is reward hacking),
and the environment can shift in ways the policy never trained against. Like parenting a
clever child, properly specifying the reward function makes a huge difference.
  Computer vision systems take in images, video frames, lidar returns, or spectrograms, and
produce outputs like image classifications, locations of detected objects (often as rectangu-
lar bounding boxes drawn around them), or a label for each pixel saying what it belongs
to [30], [31]. The intuition that the system sees obscures what is happening underneath.
The image arrives at the model as a grid of numbers (the brightness and/or color value
at each pixel), and the model performs successive matrix operations on those numbers,
combining nearby values into more and more abstract features (edges, then textures, then

Evidence and Judgment 23

parts, then object-like wholes) until a final layer produces the output. There is no scene
model in the human sense, no notion of objects persisting when they leave the frame, no
notion of in front of or behind beyond the statistical regularities of the training images. Re-
liability concerns include adversarial examples: small input changes that are imperceptible
to a human observer but flip the model’s output entirely. The canonical illustration comes
from Goodfellow et al.’s paper introducing the panda-noise-gibbon example and the fast
gradient sign method [32] (the first demonstration of adversarial examples in deep nets was
Szegedy et al. two years earlier). Take a clearly recognizable photo of a panda, add a layer
of noise so faint a person can’t see it at all, and the model classifies the result with high
confidence as a gibbon. The more mundane reliability concern is domain shift, where de-
ployment images differ from training images in lighting, resolution, sensor characteristics,
or background in ways the model never had to handle during training.
 Hybrid and physics-informed methods target scientific and engineering problems where
the data alone isn’t enough but the underlying physics is partly known: fluid flow around
an airplane wing, heat transport in a reactor core, stress in a structural component under
load. A purely data-driven model can interpolate between conditions it trained on, but
it has no reason to respect basic physical laws (conservation of mass, of momentum, of
energy) when it extrapolates outside the range the training data covered. Nothing in its
training has told it those laws exist. The physics-informed approach is to make the model’s
training score depend on two things at once: the usual error against the observed data, and
a penalty whenever the model’s predictions violate the relevant governing equations [33].
The model gets a worse score when it predicts something the equations say is impossible,
so the training pushes it toward outputs that both fit the data and stay consistent with
the physics. The mechanism corrects the intuition that the system knows the physics. The
model has never opened a physics textbook. It has only ever been graded more harshly
when it predicted something a physics textbook would have said was impossible, which
over the course of training is enough to push its outputs into the physically allowed region
without ever giving it any understanding of why that region is the one the physics allows.
§8.7 returns to this class in depth.
 Foundation models and large language models (LLMs) are the centerpiece of the next sub-
section. The intuition error is largest there, and the operational consequences for mission-
oriented use are severe enough that the corrective gets its own extended treatment rather
than a single paragraph here.
 None of the corrections above is a verdict against the method it concerns. Each of these
classes does work that nothing else does as well in its niche, and our later sections will name
settings in which each is appropriate. What the corrections protect against is trusting a

method for the wrong reasons, since trusting for the wrong reasons is what produces the
deployments that make the question, is this AI use justified?, worth asking carefully.

2.3 Foundation models as a special case
Large language models are the technology behind consumer tools like ChatGPT and
Claude, and the default picture most people form from interacting with them is something
like a knowledgeable assistant. An unusually patient one, willing to take any question at
any hour, and almost always returning something that reads like the kind of thing a knowl-
edgeable person might have written. The answer often turns out to be correct, sometimes
spectacularly so on tasks that would have taken an expert hours. The mental model that
does the most work to explain that experience is the one most readers come in with: the
system has some kind of knowledge, organized somehow inside it, and queries against that
knowledge produce the outputs you see. Even people who know better tend to fall back
on the assistant frame in casual use, because the alternative requires holding a stranger
picture in mind continuously. The projection is not just casual: in a stratified survey of
US respondents, two-thirds attributed some possibility of phenomenal consciousness to
ChatGPT, and the attribution rose with how often respondents used the system [34].
 The corrective is large and structural, not incidental. Foundation models (the broader
category of large pretrained models, of which large language models are the most familiar
instance [35]) have an architecture that differs from the assistant picture in several mutually
reinforcing ways. The architecture also differs in important ways from the predictive, unsu-
pervised, reinforcement-learning, vision, and physics-informed machine learning paradigms
covered above. Foundation models share the underlying machinery of statistical learning
at scale, but the operational profile that emerges is different enough that we treat them
in their own subsection rather than as just another method class. None of the differences
invalidates the practical usefulness of the system. All of them shape the failure modes we
return to later.
 This subsection focuses on LLMs specifically, because they are the instance most readers
encounter and the one with the most consequential operational profile for this report’s
purposes. The foundation-model category is broader. Scientific domain foundation mod-
els pre-trained on molecular graphs, protein sequences, power grid state trajectories, or
physical simulation outputs instantiate the same paradigm on non-text data, often with
architectures that differ substantially from the transformer backbone LLMs share: graph
neural networks, geometric deep learning models, convolutional spatial encoders. They are
not LLMs, but they are foundation models in the same sense. The failure modes and
evaluation practices in those domains differ correspondingly. The model has no persistent

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

Evidence and Judgment 25

memory between conversations. Each new session begins from the same starting state. It’s
the computer science equivalent of Dory in Finding Nemo — the fish who resets every few
minutes, cheerfully convinced each conversation is the first. Within a single conversation,
the model has access only to whatever fits in its context window, which is a sliding buffer
of text holding the conversation so far. Anything that falls outside the window is gone
for that session, and there is no separate memory store the model can reach back to. The
conversational experience of a system that seems to “remember” earlier in a discussion is
the same mechanism as the conversational experience of one that seems to “forget” once
the discussion gets long: in both cases the model is relying on whatever still happens to
be in its context, and there is no other channel. The constraint has a structural analog
in human cognition: Miller’s classic finding that active-information capacity is finite (the
famous 7±2) [36] names the same kind of architectural limit, even though the mechanisms
differ.
 The model also has no internal distinction between facts it knows and patterns that
happen to pattern-match well. This is a stranger claim than it sounds. Human memory
operates with at least some separation between propositional knowledge (the capital of
France is Paris) and patterns of association (Paris reminds me of bread and bicycles); the
two aren’t perfectly separate in human cognition either, but they’re distinct enough that
we can mostly tell when we know something versus when we’re guessing. Cognitive psy-
chology has formalized the associative side over decades through the spreading-activation
tradition [37], [38]; modern LLM embedding spaces are the computational descendants of
that lineage. The model has no such partition. Whatever influences its output at a given
moment is the joint result of training-time exposure to text and the current context. There
is no separately-marked subsystem the model can query for facts it is confident about.
 The model has no range of experiences beyond text. A language model trained on text has
never seen an apple, heard a thunderstorm, felt heat, or moved through space. Its concept
of an apple is whatever co-occurrence structure the word apple has in the text it trained
on: recipes, descriptions of color and shape, references to Newton, scenes from teacher-
and- student stories, allusions to Eve. This is not nothing. The co-occurrence structure of
apple in human-written text is rich enough to produce outputs that are usually appropriate
when the model is asked about apples [35]. It is also not the same as the grounding human
concept-formation has in sensory experience and embodied interaction, and the gap is not
closed by reading more text. The constraint is older than AI as a problem. Plato’s cave
prisoners only ever see shadows on the wall and have no access to what is casting them [39];
a system trained on text alone is in a structurally similar position with respect to the world
the text describes.

 The output the user reads comes out one token at a time. A token is roughly a word
or a sub-word fragment. At each step, the model has the prompt plus everything it has
already generated, and it produces a probability distribution over the possible next tokens;
a sampling procedure picks one (sometimes the highest-probability token, sometimes a
random draw from the top of the distribution, depending on the configuration); the chosen
token gets appended and the process repeats [40], [41]. The fluent paragraphs the user reads
are the cumulative result of thousands of these single-token decisions. For a basic language
model, there is no plan-then-write structure underneath. The model has no preview of the
sentence it is about to finish. The sentence emerges as the model goes.
 The recent generation of reasoning models (sometimes called thinking models in product
terminology) modifies this picture by adding an intermediate phase. Before producing
the user-facing answer, the model generates a separate block of reasoning tokens that
function as a scratchpad; the user-facing answer is then generated with that scratchpad
in its context. The underlying mechanism is the same: still token-by-token sampling from
a probability distribution. What changes is that the model now produces a longer chain
of tokens that includes an internal reasoning trace, and the part of that chain it returns
to the user is conditioned on the trace it produced first. The scratchpad is still text the
model generated by the same process, not a separately- engineered planning module, and
it inherits the same failure modes as any other generated text.
 One piece of how raw pretrained models become the assistant-shaped thing users interact
with is worth naming explicitly, because the assistant character is the product of it and
not of pretraining alone. The pretraining objective (next-token prediction over web text) is
not the same objective as “follow the user’s instructions helpfully.” The gap is closed by a
post-training stage that uses supervised demonstrations of the desired behavior and then
a reward model trained on human pairwise preference rankings, used to further fine-tune
the model via reinforcement learning. The technique originated in earlier reinforcement-
learning work on tasks where the desired behavior was easier to recognize than to specify:
a human watching two clips of Atari gameplay or simulated robot locomotion can say
which one looks closer to the target behavior without being able to write down a reward
function for it [42]. The technique was carried over to language models when the same
gap appeared between what next-token prediction optimizes for and what an assistant is
wanted to do [43]. The corollary worth holding: alignment in this technical sense names a
fine-tuning procedure against the preferences of a specific group of labelers, not anything
broader.
 That post-training stage is one of several adaptation methods that get conflated in casual
discussion and worth distinguishing here. Full fine-tuning continues training the model’s

Evidence and Judgment 27

parameters on a smaller task-specific dataset, modifying all the weights to specialize be-
havior — what reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) does at large scale
and domain-specific fine-tunes do for narrower tasks. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning, of
which Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is the dominant current instance, freezes the base
model’s weights and trains small added matrices that adjust the effective behavior without
changing the base; the resulting artifact is a small adapter (megabytes rather than tens
of gigabytes) that can be swapped per task while the base model is shared. Retrieval-
augmented generation (RAG), taken up in detail in §2.4, is a different category entirely:
the model’s weights are not modified at all, and the system instead retrieves relevant doc-
uments at inference time and adds them to the context. The four points on the spectrum
— foundational pretraining, full fine-tuning, parameter-efficient fine-tuning, and retrieval
augmentation — differ in cost, in what artifact each produces, in what evaluation each
requires, and in what failure profile each carries. “We use AI for X” obscures which of
these is in play, and the answer matters for what serious evaluation of that use case looks
like.
 Combine the last several observations and the corrective that matters most for us falls
out. The model has no reliable interior signal distinguishing knowledge it has reasonable
grounds for from output that sounds confident because the language pattern resembles
confident knowledge [44]. When the model produces a confident-sounding sentence about a
topic it trained heavily on, the confidence is appropriate. When it produces an identically-
confident-sounding sentence about a topic where its training was thin, or where it has
been asked to combine training-distribution elements that don’t naturally combine, the
confidence sounds exactly the same to the reader. The model has no separate channel that
can flag this answer is a stretch.

 The structural consequence has produced its own internet genre. Users on social media

 Each of these observations is technically modest. None of them denies what the model
can do. Taken together, though, they describe a system whose mechanics differ from the
assistant picture more substantially than casual use suggests. The system is not running on
the same architecture as a knowledgeable person who happens to be made of silicon. It is
running on an architecture in which a probability distribution over next tokens, shaped by
training-time exposure to text and conditioned on the current context window, produces
fluent-and-often- correct output without any of the separate channels (for memory, for
grounded knowledge, for self-monitoring, for distribution-awareness) that human cognition
takes for granted.
 A thought experiment that has been useful for fifty years is especially useful here. In 1980
the philosopher John Searle proposed the Chinese Room [16]. Imagine a person locked in
a room with a thick rule book in English for manipulating Chinese characters. Chinese
sentences come in through a slot. The person, who knows no Chinese, looks up the input
characters in the rule book, follows the procedures, and passes the resulting characters back
out through the slot. Imagine (implausibly) that the procedures are complete enough that

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

Evidence and Judgment 29

the output is fluent Chinese, indistinguishable to a Chinese speaker on the outside from
a competent conversation. Searle’s argument: nothing in the room understands Chinese.
The person manipulating the characters doesn’t. The rule book doesn’t. The system as a
whole doesn’t. What is happening in the room is purely formal symbol manipulation, and
the fact that it produces outputs indistinguishable from understanding does not make it
understanding.
 Searle’s thought experiment was the contemporary entry in a much longer thread. Descartes
argued in 1637 that machines could never truly use language because language seemed to
require a soul [45]; Turing’s 1950 imitation game [12] answered operationally by setting the
philosophical question aside in favor of observable behavior; Searle’s Chinese Room is the
contemporary problematization of Turing’s operational answer. The argument has been
compounding for nearly four centuries. The Cartesian model of disembodied rationalism
also has its twentieth-century critics: Damasio [46] argued from clinical neuroscience that
emotion is not a contaminant of reasoning but a constitutive part of it, complicating the
rationalist picture Descartes set up.
 Searle’s argument was directed at the symbolic AI program of its day, and the philo-
sophical debate it provoked is still unresolved. For our purposes, the value of the Chinese
Room is not as a settled philosophical claim but as a lens for thinking about LLM archi-
tecture. An LLM is doing something with statistically richer structure than the rule book
in Searle’s thought experiment, but the structural pattern is the same: text in, formal pro-
cedure operating on the text, fluent output that resembles what an understanding system
would produce. The procedure operates on patterns the model captured at training time.
It has no separate access to what the patterns are about. The Chinese Room is useful here
because it makes the gap between behavior-passing and understanding visible at the level
of mechanism, and the LLM’s behavior-passing rests on a mechanism that is, structurally,
the next step in the Room’s direction.
 Searle’s argument has been contested for forty-five years on grounds that range from the
technical to the philosophical. Daniel Dennett has been the most prominent of the long-
standing critics, arguing across decades of work that Searle’s framing trades on too narrow
a conception of understanding and that the system as a whole, not the person inside the
room, is the right unit to consider. Lengbeyer’s recent treatment [47] is one of the more
careful contemporary critiques in the same broad tradition: the argument depends on a
particular view of how concepts apply across novel cases, and Lengbeyer argues that view
doesn’t hold up under careful linguistic analysis. We don’t take a position on whether
Searle, Dennett, or Lengbeyer has the better of the dispute. The Chinese Room is useful
here even granting that its strongest philosophical conclusion is contested, because the

architectural observation it makes vivid (that behavior-passing output can be produced
by formal procedures without an understanding subsystem) describes what an LLM does,
regardless of where one lands on whether such a system also understands in any deeper
sense.
  The philosophical question (does the LLM really understand?) is unresolved and may
be unresolvable; serious philosophers continue to disagree about the AI case, just as they
have continued to disagree about the parallel question for human minds for as long as it
has been asked. We have no special standing to settle either. The operational question
(what does the system reliably do, under what conditions, with what failure modes?) is
answerable, and the rest of what follows is organized around it. Call this the operational
dodge: a framing that is honest about what it is choosing not to settle. We care about the
operational question because the operational question is the one that determines whether
a given AI use is justified. The philosophical question, however interesting, doesn’t enter
the operational answer.

2.4 AI is not search
A common misuse of LLMs in practice is to treat them as search engines. The interface

comes with the search-engine frame is that somewhere behind the answer is a document

expectation is wrong, and the way it is wrong matters operationally. A user who trusts an
LLM’s answer the way they would trust a search result is over-trusting it on a dimension
the system has no machinery to support. Just as learning to be judicious about which
search engine results to trust has been a critical skill over the past twenty years, learning
how to set appropriate expectations for LLM responses is now vital.

other tokens, and how), and the parameters now produce output that is generated, not
looked up. Setting aside the many augmentations now applied on top of LLMs, if you
asked an LLM the answer to 2 + 2 it would only be as confident that the answer is “4”

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

Evidence and Judgment 31

as the frequency with which it saw that association in its training data. It has no concept
of addition and performs no actual computations. If you ask it “what was the 2016 GDP
for the country of France” it has no database to reference to get an actual answer, it only
knows what text seems to make sense in response to the question, as judged by token
prediction probabilities. “The 2016 GDP was $53.4 billion”, “The 2016 GDP was $1”, and
even “The 2016 GDP was four apples” are much more probable than “Alligators like to eat
ham sandwiches”, but the discrimination is only statistical, no deeper. (By the way, the
correct answer is €2.23T or about $2.47 trillion [48], but did you flinch at $53.4 billion -
or the fact that it was given in dollars?) There is no internal pointer the model can return
to, and no source it could cite without making one up. The interface looks search-like (text
in, text out) while the mechanism is not search-like at all (no retrieval, no source). The
confusion follows naturally from the mismatch.
 RAG is the partial fix the field has converged on. A RAG system wraps an LLM in an
actual retrieval step: when a query arrives, the system first retrieves relevant “snippets” of
documents from an external knowledge base, then feeds those documents into the model’s
context along with the query, then asks the model to generate an answer conditioned on
the retrieved material [49]–[51]. The architecture is now genuinely search-then-inference:
the retrieval is real, the source documents exist, and the model’s output can be checked
against what was retrieved. RAG narrows the search-confusion gap meaningfully, and it is
the basis for most contemporary attempts to ground LLM output in verifiable material.
It’s a bit like giving the LLM a cheat sheet to help it in its response.
 But the quality of that cheat sheet matters, and even then RAG still doesn’t fully close
the gap. The retrieval step is a separate subsystem with its own failure modes: the right
document might not be in the knowledge base, or might not be retrieved when it is. Or
the LLM might get confused and misunderstand, misquote, or misattribute in its response,
even when given the extra context. The generation step still has no separate channel for the
retrieved material doesn’t actually support this answer, and the model can still fabricate
around partial or contradictory context. The RAG architecture only helps for questions
answerable from a knowledge base the operator has curated; the ability to pull the right
“snippets” is a matter of choosing an appropriate embedding algorithm and tuning both
the embedding and querying steps of the process; and questions requiring synthesis the
operator didn’t anticipate fall back on the underlying model’s parametric knowledge, with
all the limits foundation models exhibit. RAG is a useful pattern, but doesn’t change the
fundamental limitations of what an LLM can do. It is not an architectural solution to the
search-versus-inference confusion; the failure modes it inherits are taken up in §3.

 The intuition is that the system retrieves and reports. The mechanism is that the system
still generates plausible text based on the prompt (though now the prompt includes the
cheat sheet) and may or may not have retrieved anything to ground the generation. A
physics cheat sheet doesn’t help much if you’re taking a history exam, or if it was written by
a 3-year-old. The corrective for any consequential use is to ask which of those is happening,
and how the user will know the difference. When the system is generating with no retrieval
step, treat the output the way one would treat a confident-sounding guess from someone
with no source. When the system is doing retrieval-then-generation, treat it the way one
would treat a confident-sounding answer that may or may not be faithful to the documents
it was given.

2.5 Benchmarks: what they measure and don’t
Headline AI capability claims usually rest on benchmark scores. A model achieves 𝑋% on
some standardized task, the score becomes the basis for what the model can be trusted
to do, and that number circulates as shorthand for the model’s capability in practice. The
gap between a benchmark score and an operational claim is taken up in full in §4. For now
what matters is that the gap exists, and that the same benchmark number can mean very
different things depending on what was actually being measured.
  The deeper issue is one of construct validity: a benchmark is a particular dataset paired
with a particular scoring rule, and the number it returns is a claim about performance
on that pairing, not about the broader capability the benchmark’s name invokes. Raji
et al. work the case through with ImageNet and GLUE as central examples: benchmarks
framed as proxies for general capabilities like visual understanding or language understand-
ing cannot deliver the generality their names imply, for the same reason a finite museum
cannot represent everything in the whole wide world [52]. The saturation pattern visible in
successive NLP benchmark cycles is consistent with this: GLUE — the General Language
Understanding Evaluation suite, a bundle of natural-language tasks (sentence classification,
textual entailment, coreference, similarity, and more) aggregated to a single leaderboard
score — was treated as “beyond the capability of current transfer learning methods” when
introduced in 2018 and was surpassed within a year; SuperGLUE, the harder 2019 successor
built once GLUE saturated, went the same way; models posting above-human leaderboard
numbers on both continue to fail on simple challenge variants and falter in real-world
deployment [53].
  One particularly sharp version of the gap is worth naming here. Schaeffer et al. exam-
ined the literature on emergent abilities (the claim that certain LLM capabilities appear
suddenly at particular model scales rather than gradually) and showed that many of the

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

Evidence and Judgment 33

headline cases are artifacts of the evaluation metric rather than genuine capability tran-
sitions in the model [54]. The same model on the same task, evaluated with a continuous
metric (how close is the output to the target?), shows smooth improvement with scale.
Evaluated with a discontinuous metric (does the output exactly match the target?), the
same model appears to acquire the capability all at once. The “emergence” may be in the
metric, not in the model. The large-scale BIG-bench evaluation reached a converging con-
clusion empirically: tasks that improve gradually with scale tend to involve memorization
or knowledge components, while those that exhibit breakthrough behavior tend to involve
multiple steps scored by brittle all-or-nothing metrics; breakthrough behavior is sensitive
enough to task specification that even what counts as a capability can be a measurement
artifact [55].
 §3 returns to the systematic ways benchmark numbers and operational value come apart;
§4 returns to what serious evaluation looks like when the gap between the two matters.

2.6 Returning to the question
Is this AI use justified? was always going to be hard to answer in the abstract. The right
form is more specific. Is this LLM justified for this scientific-literature review? is a question
with a shape. Is this anomaly detector justified given the false-positive rate it produces in
this monitoring deployment? is a different shape. Both shapes depend on which method is
in play and what its failure modes are. §3 takes up the failure modes; §4 takes up what
serious answers require; §6 walks the question across the research settings where it arises
most concretely.

3 WHAT GOES WRONG, AND WHY
3.1 Failure as property, not bug
AI failures might understandably be considered bugs to be patched. They are undesirable
properties, seemingly no different than a word processor freezing and losing hours of work
while working on an important report. Hallucination, adversarial vulnerability, miscalibra-
tion, drift: each annoyance gets its own remediation pipeline, its own benchmark, its own
“fix.” These are not bugs, though, any more than an M.C. Escher drawing exposes a bug
in our mental hardware. They are properties of methods that learn statistical regularities
from finite data, deploy under conditions that differ from training, and produce outputs
without an interior signal distinguishing reliable inference from extrapolation. Patches help
at the margin. The properties remain. The failure modes below are what follows from those
mechanics once the methods are deployed.

3.2 The recurring failure classes
Hallucination and fabrication. A user encounters an LLM output that turns out to be
wrong: a fabricated citation, a confabulated fact, a confidently asserted falsehood. The
intuitive reaction is that the model made a mistake it could in principle have avoided. From
the model’s vantage point (insofar as that phrase has any meaning), the hallucinated output
and the correct one are the same kind of thing: both produced by the next-token sampling
described in §2.3, both drawn from the same probability distribution, both conditioned
on the prompt that came in. The architecture has no operation that distinguishes them.
Confabulation is the cognitive-psychology analog: people generating confident, well-formed
accounts that turn out to be invented, with no felt difference between the invented and
the recalled. The mechanisms differ. The architectural shape is the same, and human
confabulation has decades of clinical and experimental literature behind it [57]. Asking
why the model hallucinates is asking why it does what it always does; the question with
operational content is when the model’s output happens to correspond to the world and
when it does not.
  The literature classifies hallucination several ways. The most common delineation is
intrinsic (the output contradicts something already in the prompt) versus extrinsic (the
output asserts something not derivable from the prompt or, more generally, from anywhere
in the training corpus) [58]. More granular taxonomies break the category down further
into factuality hallucination, faithfulness hallucination, instruction-inconsistency, context-
inconsistency, and logical-inconsistency [44], [59]. The methodological point is the same
across all the cuts: hallucination is not one thing, and the operational treatment of any
specific deployment depends on which kind matters there.
  The mitigation landscape is real but bounded. Retrieval-augmented generation narrows
the gap by giving the generation step a source it can be evaluated against. It does not
close the gap, for the architectural reason that section ended on. Other approaches in
current practice (chain-of-thought prompting, verifier models, fine-tuning on curated data,
structured-output constraints) each help on specific cases and each leave the underlying
property intact [60]. The right operational response is risk management around a known
property, not an expectation that mitigation eliminates the property.
  Brittleness under distribution shift. A supervised model is fit to the distribution its train-
ing data happened to come from, and degrades when the inputs it sees in deployment come

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

36 B. Blakely

from a different distribution. Take a credit-card fraud detector trained on 2019 transac-
tions. Deployed in 2024, it will miss new fraud patterns that did not exist in 2019, and
may flag novel-but-legitimate transactions as fraudulent because they do not match the
2019 normals. The literature distinguishes several variants of what can go wrong. Concept
drift (the relationship between inputs and outputs changes over time) is the case where
fraud patterns themselves change as fraudsters adapt to detection: what was a strong fraud
signal in 2019 may not be one in 2024. Covariate shift (the input distribution changes while
the conditional distribution of outputs given inputs stays the same) is the case where the
mix of transactions changes (more mobile payments, more international, more recurring
subscriptions), so the model sees regions of feature space its training data did not cover
well, even though the underlying mapping from transaction type to fraud probability may
not have moved. Label shift (the prior distribution of outputs changes) is the case where
the base rate of fraud itself moves, which shifts the optimal decision threshold even if
nothing else changes [61], [62]. The operational point cuts across them: a model evaluated
on a test set drawn from the training distribution and reported as “X% accurate” does
not necessarily produce X% accuracy on deployment inputs, because deployment inputs
are by definition outside the training distribution. The WILDS benchmark [63] is a cu-
rated collection of datasets (wildlife camera-trap photos, satellite imagery from different
countries, medical-imaging data from different hospitals, code from different repositories,
text from different writing communities) where the training and test splits come from
explicitly different sources, so that the gap between training-distribution and deployment-
distribution performance can be measured directly. What the benchmark established is
that the gap is large for standard models across nearly all of the included tasks, and that
the distribution-shift mitigation methods proposed in the literature do not consistently
close it when measured against deployment-realistic splits. The distribution-shift problem
in current practice is not theoretical; the benchmark turned it into a measured quantity,
and the measurement was bigger than the field had been assuming. The optimism implicit
in “more data fixes it” fails on a second front. Mallinar et al. [64] show that for the inter-
polating regime modern deep networks operate in, training-label noise produces a residual
test error that does not vanish as the training set grows, placing standard deep nets in
a tempered overfitting regime rather than the asymptotically-optimal benign one earlier
theory had hoped for.
  Uncertainty miscalibration. A model is well-calibrated when the confidence it reports
tracks how often it is actually right. A weather forecaster who says “70% chance of rain”
on a hundred occasions and observes rain on roughly seventy of those occasions is well-
calibrated. Modern AI models are systematically not. The confidence they report tends to

run higher than the actual frequency the predictions turn out right [65]. The intuition that
a more accurate model also knows what it doesn’t know turns out to be wrong; bigger
and more capable models tend to report higher confidence per answer, often outpacing the
underlying improvement in correctness. Humans are not great at this either; the cognitive-
psychology literature on overconfidence has documented systematic divergence between
stated and actual confidence across a wide range of judgment tasks [19], [66]. The parallel
does not let the AI case off the hook. It means workflows that combine human and AI
confidence inherit both kinds of miscalibration.
 Confronted with an LLM that may or may not be reliable on a question, the obvious
thing to try is to ask it. How confident are you in that answer? The reply comes back well-
formed, often quantitative: I am about 80% confident. The natural reaction is to update
accordingly. That’s a mistake. The reply means almost nothing. The confidence number
is just more generated text, produced by the same procedure that produced the original
answer, conditioned on a prompt that asked for a confidence number. The only way to
know whether a model’s stated confidence tracks its actual accuracy is to measure: collect
a sample of its predictions, score them against ground truth, see whether the 80%-confident
answers turn out right 80% of the time [67]. Calibration is an empirical property. It has to
be tested, not asked about.
 The operational consequence is that downstream systems trusting model confidence at
face value will be wrong in characterizable ways. Whether the confidence comes from a
numerical output the model produces alongside its prediction or from the model’s verbal
answer to a confidence question, the calibration claim has to be earned empirically before
it carries operational weight.
 Limited robustness under adversarial inputs. A long line of vision-model research has
documented that models can be unsettled by tiny changes to their input. The changes can
be small enough that a person looking at the before-and-after images would not see any
difference, yet large enough to flip the model’s answer to something else entirely [68]. The
first reaction in the field was to treat this as a bug to be patched. A more careful account
treats it as a consequence of how the model was trained: from a finite set of training images,
the model has picked up patterns that happen to predict the right label, and some of those
patterns are non-robust, reliable on the training data but easily disrupted by small changes
to a new image. The model is not malfunctioning in the ordinary sense; it is using features
the training procedure rewarded it for using [69].
 This is the engineered version of the Escher case introduced earlier. Human visual per-
ception, given an Escher drawing, produces outputs (impossible staircases, perspective
inversions) that the perceptual system was designed to produce given inputs that look like

the ones it usually sees. The drawing exploits the wiring. Vision models under adversar-
ial perturbation behave the same way: the model produces outputs the architecture was
designed to produce given inputs that look like inputs it usually sees, and the adversarial
input is constructed to exploit features that the training rewarded the model for using.
The panda-noise-gibbon example from §2.2 is the canonical case.

systems characterize the input regime they expect to operate under and limit their as-

 Opacity and auditability limits. Understanding why an AI model said what it said, in
whatever sense of ”said” you might mean, turns out to be incredibly difficult. How do
different inputs affect the weights inside the model? What do the weights mean? Why
did the algorithm produce the output it did, instead of a different one? How would one
formally test what an algorithm has learned to do? The literature has been working on
these questions for two decades, and the answers are partial at best.
 The debate is older than AI. B.F. Skinner’s mid-century behaviorist program [72] took
the strict input-output-only position; Chomsky’s 1959 review of Verbal Behavior [73] and
Neisser’s 1967 textbook Cognitive Psychology [74] reopened the door to mentalist cognitive
science. The contemporary AI interpretability literature inherits both sides.
 It also splits on the right response. One position holds that the right approach is to
use inherently interpretable models for high-stakes decisions and to stop trying to explain
black-box models after the fact [75]. A second position holds that interpretability is a
slippery word that conflates several distinct properties (simulatability, decomposability,
transparency, post-hoc explanation), and that loose use of the term makes interpretability
claims hard to evaluate in any rigorous way [76]. Both positions name something real. They
do not mix well.
 Post-hoc explanation methods (LIME, SHAP, attention maps, mechanistic interpretabil-
ity) have produced useful tools with documented failure modes [77]; Bordt et al. argue the
stronger position that in adversarial settings (where the explainer and the audience have
opposing interests) post-hoc explanations are unsuitable for transparency objectives at
all, because of their fundamental ambiguity [78]. An explanation produced by a post-hoc
method is an approximation of the model’s behavior, not a description of it. Auditing a
deployment on the basis of post-hoc explanations is auditing the explanation method as
much as the model.

 The accountability consequence is specific. A mission-oriented deployment that makes
a decision a stakeholder wants to contest depends on standard accountability machinery:
review of the decision’s basis, audit of the procedure that produced it, the ability to argue
against it. Opacity narrows what each of those mechanisms can examine. The system
makes the decision; nobody, including the developers, can fully explain why; the standard
accountability tools lose traction. This doesn’t work well in high-impact decision making,
whether for societal reasons or mission-driven reasons, as we will return to in 6.
 Information disclosure: memorization. Models can reproduce training data verbatim un-
der the right probing, and the disclosure surface this creates is a failure class in its own
right. Carlini and colleagues demonstrated extractable memorization in GPT-2-scale mod-
els: with carefully constructed prompts, the model would output verbatim training-data
passages including personally identifying information [79]. Follow-up work showed that
memorization scales with model size, with how many times a record appeared in the train-
ing data, and with prompt length [80]. A separate class of analysis, membership inference,
probes whether a specific record was in the training set [81], [82]; for sensitive training cor-
pora this turns “was this person in the training set?” into a question the model’s behavior
can sometimes answer - not necessarily as easily as just asking and getting an answer, but
potentially through analysis of many interactions and observing the statistical properties
of the responses.
 The mature mitigation framework is differential privacy [83]: training procedures (no-
tably DP-SGD) that provide provable bounds on per-record influence in exchange for a
measurable accuracy cost. The framework bounds the disclosure property, but does not
eliminate it entirely and asking ”do we have enough privacy in this training process” is a
lot like asking ”if my bank secure enough” - it requires a lot of additional ”it depends”
context and analysis of assumptions to even approximate a meaningful answer.
 Automation bias and misplaced trust. The human side of the failure ledger. Automa-
tion bias (and the related but technically distinct phenomenon Parasuraman and Manzey
term automation complacency) is the established human-factors finding that operators al-
locate less attention to monitoring decisions made by trusted automation, with the under-
monitoring most pronounced exactly when the automation begins to degrade [84]. The
human-factors literature names both poles: misuse (overtrust producing reliance the au-
tomation cannot support) and disuse (distrust producing rejection of capable automation),
with both rooted in a mismatch between operator trust and the automation’s actual relia-
bility [85]. The contemporary AI form is documented as overreliance: confident AI outputs
produce user acceptance even on tasks where the user could have caught the error [86],

40 B. Blakely

[87]. The explainable-AI literature observes that the design of explanations interacts with
overreliance in characterizable ways [88].
 Why this matters for the broader failure ledger: automation bias is the mechanism by
which model failures become operational failures. A miscalibrated model whose outputs
are appropriately scrutinized produces fewer wrong decisions than a well-calibrated model
whose outputs are rubber-stamped. The human-AI loop is an integral part of the sys-
tem. Clark and Chalmers’s extended-mind thesis [89] captures the conceptual point at the
philosophy-of-mind level: a tool a person reliably offloads cognition onto becomes part of
the cognitive system, and the boundary between the person’s reasoning and the tool’s out-
puts stops being sharp. Automation bias is the structural shape this takes when the tool
is an AI.
 Reproducibility and provenance gaps. The final class is about the methodological qual-
ity of the literature the rest of the failure classes rest on. The most established symptom
is leakage: design choices that let information from the test data influence the trained
model and inflate the reported numbers. The benchmark-divergence subsection takes up
the full treatment of leakage, including the Kapoor/Narayanan review and the Sasse tax-
onomy that pin the scope down. Beyond leakage specifically, the field has responded with
community-level reproducibility initiatives [90] and with standardization of reproducibility
terminology [91]; the underlying gap remains a methodological-quality gap rather than a
methodological-quality solution.
 What this means for a decision-maker calibrating expectations from published results:
the published numbers are systematically optimistic, in ways that depend on how the
held-out evaluation was constructed.
 Eight classes of things-that-can-wrong to think about is enough for now. The point is
not to multiply them. The classes are also not independent. Hallucination is amplified by
miscalibration (confident-sounding hallucinations look like reliable answers); distribution
shift makes adversarial inputs harder to recognize (the deployment data already differs
from training in ways the model was not trained to handle); opacity makes automation
bias harder to correct (the user cannot evaluate the basis of the model’s decision in order
to catch its errors); reproducibility gaps mean the published baselines for any of the above
are systematically off. The failures co-occur and compound. The evaluation framework §4
builds has to reckon with the compounding, not with the classes in isolation.

3.3 Why benchmark and operational value diverge
Benchmark performance is one signal about a model. Operational value (the value the
model delivers in a real deployment under realistic conditions) is a different signal. The

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

Evidence and Judgment 41

two diverge for characterizable reasons, and the divergence is large enough to matter for any
decision being made on benchmark numbers alone. Three forces drive most of the divergence
in current practice: metric artifacts, leakage inflation, and construct-operationalization
gaps. Each is worth treating separately.
 The clearest single illustration of the metric-artifact problem is Schaeffer’s analysis of
emergent abilities [54]. A line of work between roughly 2020 and 2022 claimed that LLMs
displayed capabilities at certain scales that were not present in smaller models and that
appeared suddenly when models crossed particular parameter thresholds. The headline
emergent-abilities papers cited tasks on which model performance jumped from near-zero
to near-ceiling over a narrow range of scale, and read this as evidence that something
qualitatively new was happening inside the model at those scales.
 Schaeffer et al. examined the same behaviors with a different metric and found the
emergence disappeared. Most of the headline cases were scored with discontinuous metrics
(exact-match accuracy on tasks requiring all of several steps to be exactly right). Under
continuous metrics (token-level cross-entropy, partial-credit scoring), the same models on
the same tasks showed smooth, predictable improvement with scale, with no sudden jump.
The discontinuity was in the metric, not in the model.
 This is the Escher case applied to evaluation metrics. The Escher staircase exploits human
visual processing; the discontinuous-metric “emergence” exploits the conventions of the
evaluation procedure. In both cases the apparent feature is an artifact of how the observer
is wired (perception) or designed (the metric), not a property of the underlying thing
being observed. Schaeffer’s point generalizes: even when a behavior looks discontinuous
in benchmark scores, the apparent discontinuity may be in the metric rather than in the
model.
 The second force is leakage inflation. Kapoor and Narayanan’s 2023 review showed that
leakage (design or pipeline choices that allow information from the test set to influence
the trained model) is widespread in published machine-learning-for-science work and that
many headline results do not survive proper held-out evaluation [92]. The leakage taxonomy
is granular: train/test contamination, temporal leakage, distribution-mismatched holdouts,
sampling bias in holdout construction, and several more [25]. The operational lesson is that
published benchmark numbers are systematically optimistic in characterizable ways, and
the size of the optimism depends on which leakage patterns survived the evaluation. The
book-length treatment is Narayanan and Kapoor’s AI Snake Oil [93]: one of the book’s
central organizing distinctions is between generative AI (often works, often oversold) and
predictive AI (often does not work, marketed as if it does), with criminal-justice and hiring
as central deployed-domain case studies. The leakage paper provides the academic anchor;

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

42 B. Blakely

the book extends the same observation into the deployed-claims domain, where predictive
systems get adopted on the basis of evaluations that don’t survive the contexts they are
deployed into.
 The pattern is not specific to one subfield. Liao et al.’s meta-review documents evaluation
failures across machine-learning subfields common enough that “state-of-the-art” claims
must be read with the question did this evaluation actually demonstrate what it claims? [94].
The construct under evaluation and the operationalization that scored it are often loosely
related; performance on the operationalization can be high while the construct it was meant
to track sits substantially elsewhere.
 The third force is the gap between what we name and what we measure. Benchmarks
have to take abstract capabilities (“reasoning,” “comprehension,” “generalization”) and
pin them to specific tasks with specific scoring rules. A reasoning benchmark might be a
set of word problems with numerical answers; a comprehension benchmark might be a set
of multiple-choice questions about short passages. A benchmark task is a particular way
of measuring a named capability; the capability is broader than the task that measures
it. A high reasoning-benchmark score tells you the model did well on that specific set of
items under that specific scoring rule. Whether it can reason in the broader sense the word
suggests is a separate question. The gap between named capability and benchmark task is
its own line of work in the evaluation literature [95], [96].
 The three forces compound. Metric artifacts inflate the apparent shape of capability
gains; leakage inflates the headline numbers themselves; the gap between named capa-
bility and benchmark task makes even unleaked, well-measured numbers fail to predict
deployment value. None of these is an isolated failure of specific benchmarks; they are
characteristic patterns of how the literature reports performance. Serious evaluation has to
be designed around these patterns rather than around the assumption that any of them is
an exception.

3.4 Why high-consequence settings raise the stakes
The failure classes named above exist in every deployment. In low-consequence settings,
the operational response can sometimes be to live with it: a chatbot that fabricates a
plausible-sounding movie quote in a casual exchange produces no real harm and probably
no one notices. In mission-oriented settings, where the decisions an AI system informs are
themselves consequential, where the inputs and outputs feed into operational pipelines,
where errors propagate before anyone notices, the same failure classes carry weight they do
not carry elsewhere. The methods have not become worse. The consequence environment
has changed.

 Two of those failure classes shift in importance as the stakes go up.
 The first is robustness under adversarial inputs. For a low-stakes use, like a casual image
classifier or a consumer chatbot, the occasional surprising output triggered by an unusual
input is a known limitation the user can shrug off. For a deployment where the input mix
is broader than what training covered, or where the operator does not fully control which
inputs the model sees, the same property becomes something the deployment has to plan
around rather than shrug off.
 The second is information disclosure. A model trained on public text and occasionally
reproducing a familiar quote from its training data raises few stakes. A model trained on
a corpus that includes proprietary, regulated, or personally identifying material has the
same generation mechanism, but what it can emit is now the kind of material the operator
was supposed to keep contained. Differential privacy is the formal tool the field uses to
bound this. The bound is real but partial; the residual still has to be reckoned with at the
deployment side.
 These elevations are also asymmetric in a way that matters. Many of the failure modes
named are not symmetrically reversible. A miscalibrated model that produces an under-
confident prediction has different operational consequences than one that produces an
over-confident prediction at the same numerical confidence. A deployment decision based
on benchmark performance can be reversed at the cost of an embarrassing rollback. A
deployment decision that has already produced operational harm is reversible only at the
cost of the harm. The framing argues for prospective reckoning with the residual failure
classes, before deployment while the choice is still cheap, rather than retrospective.
 None of this is unfamiliar territory. Mission-oriented institutions have long-running as-
surance postures for the simpler fact that all software is fallible: testing practices, formal
verification where it pays off, change-control processes, code review, incident-response play-
books, threat models. Information security has carried the same concern in the CIA triad
for decades, where integrity (the assurance that a system behaves as specified, that data
has not been tampered with, that outputs reflect the intended computation) has always
sat alongside confidentiality and availability as a first-class concern. AI does not intro-
duce the need for integrity assurance. It changes what integrity assurance has to look
like, because the system’s behavior is statistical rather than specified. You cannot prove a
specification for a model whose outputs are a probability distribution over continuations.
You can characterize the distribution, bound the failure modes that follow from it, and
design the operational envelope around what the model can and cannot be trusted to do.
That is the new layer of QA. External frameworks are converging on what it looks like:
the NIST AI Risk Management Framework [97] supplies the governing vocabulary; the

EU AI Act’s high-risk regime [98] operationalizes the artifact-level requirements; and the
AI-safety-assurance literature offers a decade-long survey of approaches [99]. AI assurance
is a new artifact class added to a long-running assurance posture. It does not replace what
came before.
 The elevation does not change what the failures are. It changes what counts as enough
evidence to deploy.

3.5 Returning to the question
This section has been the evidence side of the question we keep asking, is this AI use
justified?: the failure classes that follow from the architecture, the benchmark-operational
gap, the elevated stakes in high-consequence settings. None of it tells us whether a given
AI use is justified — that answer is available only in the particulars of which system,
in which setting, against what evidence. What this section has done is establish what a
justification must engage: you cannot justify an AI use while ignoring the failure modes
that characterize the method in deployment. §4 takes up what the evidence for a genuine
justification has to include.

4 WHAT “SERIOUS” REQUIRES
4.1 Trustworthy AI as a concept
The trustworthy-AI literature has converged on roughly the same set of properties across
the major framework documents and academic surveys. NIST’s AI Risk Management
Framework [97] names valid-and-reliable, safe, secure-and-resilient, accountable-and-transparent,
explainable-and-interpretable, privacy-enhanced, and fair-with-harmful-biases-managed; NIST
has since published a generative-AI cross-sectoral profile [100] that maps these functions
onto a set of risks unique to or exacerbated by generative AI, carrying each through to
govern/map/measure/manage actions. The European Union’s High-Level Expert Group
on AI [101] lists human agency and oversight, technical robustness and safety, privacy and
data governance, transparency, diversity and non-discrimination and fairness, societal and
environmental well-being, and accountability. The OECD’s principles [102] cover much the
same ground in slightly different proportions. The academic surveys, when they synthe-
size across the frameworks, return six or seven categories that overlap heavily with each
other: human oversight, fairness, transparency and explainability, robustness, privacy and
security, accountability [103], [104]. The convergence is itself useful. These are no longer
contested categories; they are working vocabulary.

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

Evidence and Judgment 45

 The vocabulary has converged. The concept it names, trust in a technical system, is older
than the AI literature, and the older literatures that have tried to nail it down have not
arrived at a unified definition. The Trusted Computing Base notion in the U.S. Department
of Defense’s mid-1980s Trusted Computer System Evaluation Criteria [105], known then
and now as the Orange Book, set out a hierarchy of evaluation classes (D, C1, C2, B1, B2,
B3, A1) for what it would take to declare a computer system trustworthy at progressively
stricter assurance levels. The web-of-trust and public-key-infrastructure models of the 1990s
took a different angle, asking how trust scales across networks of strangers; Zimmermann’s
PGP [106] is the originating articulation of the web-of-trust model. The contemporary
federal-standards articulation is NIST’s Zero Trust Architecture [107], which deflates the
older “trusted network perimeter” model on the explicit observation that trust cannot be
granted based on network location or asset ownership and has to be re-established for
every access. The textbook treatment of computer security as a discipline [108] presents
the formal vocabulary, the security models, the trust assumptions, and the unresolved
problems the field carries.
 What infosec has produced across these decades is a set of trust models, each scoped to
a particular assumption set and threat model, each partial. A threat model is the explicit
specification of who or what a system is defending against and what capabilities they
are assumed to have; trust guarantees claimed under one threat model do not transfer
to deployments where the threats are different. Schneier’s two book-length treatments
make the broader point. Liars and Outliers [109] argues that cooperation and trust in
any human system depend on four overlapping mechanisms working in concert (moral,
reputational, institutional, and security-systems), none of which is foundational on its own.
Beyond Fear [110] treats security as a discipline of explicit trade-offs between risk, cost, and
consequence rather than a problem with a definite solution. Both books, like the federal-
standards arc, refuse the idea that security is solvable once and for all. The trustworthy-AI
literature is now where infosec was in the 1990s: working vocabulary in place, multiple
partial models, no convergence on a unified definition. Recognizing this is part of taking
the AI version seriously. The response that worked for infosec was to build scoped trust
models with explicit assumption sets, and to be honest about what each one bounds and
what it does not.
 A second lesson from the same arc was about language. Infosec made headway when
its practitioners learned to articulate residual risks, exposures, and trade-offs in terms a
non-specialist with decision authority could actually weigh, rather than in the field’s own
formal vocabulary. Trustworthy AI is at the same threshold now: the principle convergence
above is in technical-AI vocabulary, and whether it carries operational weight depends on

whether the field can translate what it knows about the failure modes and the partial trust
models into terms its mission-oriented audience can use.
 The trustworthy-AI principle frameworks do what they are designed to do: they establish

how to evaluate whether a specific system instantiates them. The gap between “we believe
in fairness” and “we have shown this system is fair under these conditions” is enormous,

deal of the work of taking trustworthy AI seriously is in producing the evidence that any
specific instantiation actually delivers what the principle list names.
 A distinction worth making concrete: trust is an attitude a user (or system) takes toward
a system; trustworthiness is a property of the trusted (or untrusted) system that warrants
that attitude. The asymmetry matters operationally. A developer can assert their system
is trustworthy as many times as they like, and the assertion does not change the property.
The property is something the system either has, under specified conditions, against spec-
ified evaluations, with specified evidence, or it does not. Each property in the principle
frameworks has to be designed for, instrumented for measurement, and made falsifiable.

4.2 Evaluation beyond leaderboards
Benchmarks are useful instruments. They make capability claims comparable across sys-
tems, give research a shared target to push against, and produce numbers reproducible
enough that someone outside the originating lab can check the result. None of that is in

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

Evidence and Judgment 47

dispute here. What is in dispute is the operational inference: that a high benchmark score,
on its own, supports a serious claim about how a system will perform in deployment. §3.3
laid out the three forces that drive the divergence between benchmark performance and
operational value (metric artifacts, leakage inflation, and the gap between named capabil-
ity and benchmark task). What follows is the other side of that argument: what evaluation
has to look like for a serious claim to rest on it.
 Holistic evaluation is the first design response. The canonical reference is Holistic Eval-
uation of Language Models (HELM) [113], the benchmark suite from Stanford, which
evaluates each model across multiple scenarios (summarization, question-answering, senti-
ment classification, several more) along multiple axes per scenario (accuracy, robustness,
calibration, fairness, bias, toxicity, efficiency). The point is straightforward enough to state:
evaluation that takes a system seriously cannot collapse to a single number. Different eval-
uation axes surface different failures, and any one axis taken alone misses what the others
would have caught. HELM is one realization of the principle; the principle outlives the
specific instantiation.
 The deeper question is whether a benchmark measures what it claims to measure. The
methods literature calls this construct validity: the extent to which a test actually tracks
the underlying capability it is named after, rather than tracking something correlated with
the capability under the conditions of the test. The concern is not new. Psychometrics has
wrestled with construct validity since the 1950s [114], asking whether IQ tests really mea-
sure intelligence [115] and whether personality inventories track the traits they name. The
AI evaluation literature is inheriting both the question and the vocabulary. Burden [116]
works the concept through for AI evaluation specifically. A test of “reasoning” with high
construct validity actually measures reasoning; a test labeled “reasoning” that is solvable
by pattern-matching on training-data templates has low construct validity, even when the
numbers look strong. The interdisciplinary meta-review of about 110 benchmark-critique
studies [117] returns the same finding across fields: construct validity is one of the most-cited
systemic issues in current benchmarking practice, alongside misaligned incentives, unknown
unknowns, and gaming. If you are reading benchmark numbers, ask what the benchmark
actually measures, and whether the answer is the capability the name promised.

inputs?, adversarial evaluation asks what does the model do on inputs constructed to surface
its failure modes? The two are not interchangeable: adversarial inputs are designed to find
failures, so high adversarial-evaluation scores are evidence the model handled the specific
probes; they are not evidence the model would handle arbitrary deployment-distribution
inputs the probes did not cover. Anwar et al.’s survey of foundational challenges in LLM
alignment and safety [121] catalogues the open problems in this space, including the struc-

probing the input space.

 The evaluation problem becomes harder at the agentic frontier: systems that chain tools,
plan multi-step actions, and operate over long horizons. The leaderboard-benchmark tem-
plate breaks down here more visibly than for single-turn LLM evaluation. Agentic behavior
includes interaction with environments, recovery from intermediate errors, and outcomes
that depend on the full sequence of choices rather than on any single step. The review
literature on agentic-AI evaluation [95], [96] is starting to name what evaluation has to
look like for these systems; the field has not converged on a stable methodology yet, and
the gap between benchmark and deployment value is, if anything, larger here than for the
static-input case.
 A more recent thread in the evaluation literature treats disagreement among evaluators
as evidence, not as noise to be averaged away. Fazelpour and Fleisher [122] make the
case that the standard practice of resolving disagreement via majority voting or averaging
discards information about how the system behaves on contested cases, where reasonable
evaluators reach different verdicts. The discarded information is often exactly what an
operator needs to know: that the system’s outputs are not uncontroversially right or wrong,
that downstream consequences depend on which evaluator’s standard is applied, that a
single ground-truth label flattens variation the deployment context will surface anyway.
The methodological lesson: the structure of disagreement is itself an evaluation signal.
Treating it as noise to be cleaned up discards exactly the information an operator needs
to understand the system’s behavior on the contested cases that matter most.
 What serious evaluation looks like, taken together: holistic across axes, validated against
the construct it claims to measure, explicit about which level of system description the
measurement targets [118], sociotechnical in scope, contamination-aware and saturation-
aware, complemented by adversarial probing, adapted to the operating regime (including
agentic), and respectful of disagreement among evaluators. No single one of these is suf-
ficient. The combination is what a high-consequence claim about a specific system has to
rest on.

4.3 Bounded deployment and operational suitability
An aircraft is not “live or not.” It is qualified within an operational envelope (altitude,
airspeed, weight, attitude, atmospheric conditions), and outside that envelope the quali-
fication does not transfer. The airworthiness discipline that makes the envelope evaluable
is the longest-developed instance of what bounded deployment looks like for a complex,
partially-characterizable system, and the aerospace example runs underneath this subsec-
tion because the AI case is in the early decades of building the equivalent. Deployment, in
the sense this subsection uses it, is the moment an AI system stops being a thing the team

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

50 B. Blakely

is building and starts being a thing other people are using to do something consequential:
outputs getting acted on, by people other than the developers, in conditions the team can
no longer fully control. The question “is this system live or not?” treats deployment as a
switch that gets flipped, but most of the failures we have named so far happen on the other
side of that switch, in the gap between technically running and operationally responsible.
The better question is what scope the system is approved for, what monitoring fires when it
strays, what it does when it cannot answer, and what institutional arrangements scrutinize
its outputs. Bounded deployment names the regime in which each of those is characterized
and constrained, rather than left to be discovered at run-time.
 Machine learning operations (MLOps) is the practice of operating ML systems in pro-
duction, the ML-specific descendant of an older IT-discipline practice called DevOps: the
unified posture of software engineers and operations teams running the systems they build,
with the pipeline from code change to deployed change automated rather than handed off.
(DevSecOps is the security-integrated extension, with security review and threat modeling
built into the same pipeline rather than treated as a separate stage.) The MLOps liter-
ature has been documenting the deployment-cost case for years [123], [124]: most of the
operational cost of an ML system in production lives outside the model itself, in the sur-
rounding infrastructure that keeps it usable. MLOps inherits the DevOps cycle and adds
the ML-specific complications: models change with data, not just with code; performance
degrades with distribution shift; retraining and monitoring become first-class operational
concerns. The principle generalizes beyond ML to any AI deployment that has to be trusted
to behave under conditions the team can name in advance.
 Deployment risk for an AI system is multiplicative across at least three axes that bounded
deployment is designed to constrain. The first is capability opacity: the model is charac-
terized empirically rather than specified component-by-component, so its behavior surface
is partially-known by construction. The second is mission-criticality of decisions: what
the consequence environment is, determined by what the system’s outputs influence down-
stream. The third is operating environment: the input distribution the system actually faces
in deployment, and whether that distribution is benign, noisy, or adversarial. The axes are
not independent. An opaque model deployed for low-stakes decisions in a benign distribu-
tion is a fundamentally different risk surface from the same model deployed for high-stakes
decisions in an adversarial environment, and the difference is multiplicative, not additive.
Each axis that is not bounded multiplies the residual risk in the others. The §3.1 Tay illus-
tration was exactly this: capability opacity (a chatbot updating its conversational model
from open input), an adversarial operating environment (Twitter, taking the unbounded
combination as a game), and a deployment context that took the combination as design.

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

Evidence and Judgment 51

Bounded deployment is the regime in which each axis is characterized and constrained,
not eliminated, since none of the three can be eliminated for any non-trivial AI deploy-
ment, but bounded enough that the multiplied residual is acceptable for the deployment’s
stakes. This kind of multiplicative-risk reasoning sits inside a longer risk-management tra-
dition. International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 31000 [125], the international
standard for organizational risk management, and NIST Special Publication 800-30 [126],
the federal guide for cybersecurity risk assessment, together establish the vocabulary the
AI case inherits: likelihood and impact as the analytic axes, threats and vulnerabilities
as the analytic objects, identify and analyze and evaluate and treat and monitor as the
process steps. What the AI case adds to that vocabulary is that capability opacity is it-
self one of the contributing axes rather than just a property of the system being assessed,
because the model’s behavior surface is partially-known by construction. The software-
assurance-continuity argument from §3.4 gives the bounding requirement its precedent:
high-consequence software has always required this kind of bounding to be evaluable at all,
even before AI changed what the bounded artifacts have to look like.
 Distribution shift is the failure mode (named in §3.2) that degrades model performance
once inputs in deployment differ from the training distribution. Bounded deployment turns
that named failure mode into a design requirement: production deployment instruments the
input distribution, the output distribution, and the performance distribution, alerts when
any of them drifts past a threshold the operator has named in advance, and has a plan
for what happens when alerts fire. The performance-aware drift-detection literature [61],
[62] surveys the methods that have accumulated for this task over the past decade. The
methods are imperfect; drift detection is itself a hard problem, and false alarms compete
with missed alerts under the same statistical trade-offs every other detection problem faces.
But the design choice is between instrumented and uninstrumented, not between perfect
monitoring and none. A deployment without drift monitoring is one where the operator
finds out about distribution shift the same way the affected users do: through degraded
outputs in the field.
 A model approved for use on data with characteristics X, by users in role Y, for decisions
of class Z, is not approved for arbitrary use. Bounded deployment means making that
scope explicit, machine-checkable where the data allows, procedurally enforced where it
does not. The aerospace envelope is the canonical instance. Pilots accept the envelope as a
real limitation on what this aircraft can be safely flown for; if a mission requires conditions
outside the envelope, the answer is a different aircraft, not an extra-envelope flight in
this one. A scope artifact for an AI deployment serves the same purpose, with the same

52 B. Blakely

forced choice: stay within the qualified regime, extend the qualification through additional
evaluation, or use a different system for the out-of-scope work.
 The analogy has a limit worth naming. Pilots are licensed, qualified, trained, and ac-
countable. The end users of most AI systems are not. A pilot who flies outside the envelope
risks losing a license and facing the institutional consequences that come with that loss.
An end user who applies an AI system outside its qualified regime usually faces nothing
equivalent, because the institutional machinery that enforces the aerospace forced choice
(pilot certification, type ratings, flight-standards inspection, the FAA and its analogs) has
no general equivalent for AI. This is the same responsibility question the internet has been
working through for thirty years, made more acute by the social-media platforms of the
2010s and more acute again by robotics and self-driving vehicles in this decade. We are
not going to settle it; the question sits one level above the operational frame this section
is developing. But the sociotechnical embedding any specific deployment lives inside has
to make some answer to the question, even if only implicitly. A deployment that has not
named who is responsible when its outputs cause harm has named, by default, that the
answer is nobody.
 A serious deployment also has to specify what the system does when its inputs are out
of scope, its confidence is too low, its dependencies are unavailable, or its drift monitoring
fires. “Returns its best guess silently” is not a fallback behavior; it is the absence of one. The
design choice is whether the system’s failure mode is a deliberate one (degrade gracefully
to a smaller, more conservative model; defer to human review; refuse and explain) or an
emergent one (whatever the architecture happens to produce when the input falls outside
what it was characterized for). The first is a property of the bounded deployment. The
second is what the model does by default if nobody specified otherwise.
 The system being deployed is not the model alone. It is the model plus the workflow it
runs inside, plus the users who depend on its outputs, plus the institution that operates
it. Automation bias is the failure mode (named in §3.2) by which model failures become
operational failures; the corresponding design requirement is that the deployment gives the
workflow the time, training, and authority to scrutinize the model’s outputs when scrutiny
matters. A well-calibrated model embedded in a workflow that has no slack to review its
outputs produces operational harm even when the model is technically functioning as de-
signed [35]. The public-perception axis of the same problem has its own literature [127]:
institutions deploying AI in consequential domains operate inside a public-trust environ-
ment that the sociotechnical embedding has to account for. The book-length anchor for
this entire failure mode is Cathy O’Neil’s Weapons of Math Destruction [128], which cat-
alogs what happens when opaque, large-scale, math-based decision systems are deployed

in consequential domains without accountability mechanisms. O’Neil’s diagnostic criteria
are opacity (those affected cannot understand or contest the decision), scale (the system
operates on many people at once), and damage (the system causes real harm to people it
scores wrongly), with damaging feedback loops as the mechanism by which the damage
amplifies. The case studies (recidivism scoring, teacher evaluation, hiring filters, college
rankings, credit scoring) make vivid what unbounded, unmonitored, sociotechnically-naive
deployment produces in the wild. Bounded deployment, as named in this subsection, is
what is designed to prevent the O’Neil failure class. It is not a procedural nicety; it is the
artifact category whose absence makes the failures in O’Neil’s case studies possible.
 The aerospace example carries through to the certification machinery directly. Agogino et
al.’s evidence-and-process recommendations for certifying learning-enabled components [129]
are a worked instance of exactly the artifact requirements this subsection has been naming:
evidence packages tying capability claims to test conditions, operational envelopes within
which the certified behavior holds, monitoring plans for in-service operation, change-control
processes for retraining and updates. The aerospace context is unusual in how thoroughly
the certification machinery has been developed; the structural shape of what the machin-
ery produces is general to any high-consequence deployment. The ISO/IEC management-
systems standard for AI [130] is the broader process anchor: not the aerospace-specific
evidence requirements, but the management-system frame for organizations that have to
demonstrate they are operating their AI systems responsibly across many deployments.
Here they appear as the worked example of what bounded deployment looks like when it
is mature.
 Bounded deployment is a constraint, and that is part of the point. It is the constraint
that makes any system, AI or otherwise, operationally responsible: the price of being able
to claim, of a specific deployment in a specific setting, that the system is fit for use.
Pilots accept the operational envelope as a real limitation on what this aircraft can do,
because the alternative is either flying outside a known regime or not flying at all. Bounded
deployment is what software has always required for high-consequence use, and AI changes
what the artifacts have to look like (statistical rather than specified) without changing the
requirement that the artifacts exist. The framework is for asking, of a specific deployment
in a specific setting: is each of the three risk axes characterized and constrained well enough
for what the deployment is trying to do?

4.4 Human oversight, and its limits
Human oversight appears in nearly every trustworthy-AI principle list reviewed earlier,
and in many cases is treated as the primary mitigation for the failure classes §3 named.

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

54 B. Blakely

The corrective worth making explicit is that the existence of oversight is not the same as
oversight working. Oversight is itself a designed system, and what AI adds to the long-
running discipline of designing oversight well sits on top of the general human-in-the-loop
requirements that other contexts have been working through for decades. The question this
subsection takes up is not whether oversight is required (every framework agrees that it
is); it is how to tell, of a specific deployment, whether the oversight regime there is doing
what it claims to do.
 The central design parameter is calibrated trust: the operator’s confidence in the model’s
outputs matched to the model’s actual reliability in this specific deployment context. Lee
and See’s analysis of trust in automated systems [85] is the canonical source for the vocab-
ulary in use here — calibration (correspondence between operator trust and automation
capability), resolution (how finely trust discriminates among capability levels), and speci-
ficity (trust tied to particular subfunctions and updated as conditions change), each a
design parameter the oversight regime has to address rather than assume. The calibra-
tion problem is harder for AI than for deterministic tools because AI outputs vary with
input and context; prior reliability under one set of conditions does not transfer cleanly
to another. Both poles of miscalibration are costly. Too much trust produces the rubber-
stamp pattern named as automation bias: the human reviewer treats the model’s output
as correct by default, scrutinizes only when something looks obviously wrong, and the
model’s failures pass through unchecked. Too little trust produces the opposite cost: the
reviewer second-guesses every output, the throughput benefit of the AI is lost, and the
workflow eventually reverts to manual processing of the task the model was supposed to
assist with [84], [88]. The calibration target is the level of trust appropriate to the model’s
measured reliability under the deployment’s actual conditions, which means oversight de-
sign pulls the evaluation machinery from the evaluation discussion directly into operational
practice.
 The design requirements that make oversight functional are not AI-novel. They apply
to any human-in-the-loop review process, in compliance, in safety review, in audit. The
three the literature converges on are time, training, and authority: the reviewer’s time to
scrutinize, training to scrutinize meaningfully, and authority to act on what the scrutiny
finds. What is AI-specific is the way AI throughput and stochasticity make those require-
ments bind harder than they do for the deterministic systems the institutional oversight
machinery grew up around. Time is the most-structural: an AI system producing thou-
sands of decisions a day forces sampling, and a reviewer with thirty seconds per case is
the predictable consequence of throughput-mismatch math, not of lax institutional design.

Training is the most-AI-specific: the reviewer needs both domain expertise in the cases be-
ing reviewed and AI-output-evaluation expertise, the latter still rare in most institutional
review pipelines. Authority is the most-testable. There is a diagnostic question worth ask-
ing of any specific oversight regime: if the reviewer always said “no,” would the system still
ship? For the reader who is operating one, ask it of your own. If the answer is yes, the over-
sight is theatrical regardless of how it is documented. The iSTS (intelligent sociotechnical
systems) framework from Xu and Gao [131] names the design discipline: oversight has to
be designed at the level of the whole work system (model plus workflow plus organization
plus decision authority), not at the level of the model alone.
 A particular case of oversight worth treating separately is the family of techniques that
move human oversight from the deployment loop to the training loop. RLHF and its vari-
ants train the model on signals derived from human evaluation of its outputs, with the
intent that the trained model then behaves the way humans would have wanted it to be-
have at deployment time. Casper et al.’s survey of open problems in RLHF [132] catalogs
the structural difficulties this approach inherits. The human-feedback signal is itself noisy,
biased, and underspecified: different annotators disagree, annotation conditions affect what
they flag, and the elicited preferences often do not generalize cleanly to cases the annotators
did not see. The reward model is then a learned approximation of that already-imperfect
signal. The policy is then optimized against the reward model, which is itself an approx-
imation. Each layer adds error, and the resulting policy can be substantially misaligned
with what the humans actually wanted while scoring well on the reward model’s metric.
RLHF is a real and useful technique; it is not a substitute for deployment-loop human
oversight, and treating it as one is a category error this section’s framework is specifically
designed to catch.
 Explainable AI (XAI) covers post-hoc explanation methods, attention visualizations,
and feature-attribution displays. These are sometimes proposed as the mitigation that
makes oversight tractable: give the reviewer an explanation alongside the output and the
reviewer can scrutinize the basis for the model’s recommendation. The literature on this
is mixed [133]. The underlying limit applies here too: post-hoc explanations approximate
the model’s behavior rather than describe it, and auditing a deployment on the basis of
post-hoc explanations is auditing the explanation method as much as the model. As an
oversight mitigation, XAI helps in specific cases and does not substitute for the calibrated-
trust, time-training-authority, and theater-test disciplines named above.
 Human oversight is necessary for high-consequence AI deployment. The same arrange-
ment can satisfy a principle list’s compliance documentation without actually doing the

oversight work the documentation is supposed to demand. Calibrating trust to model re-
liability, giving the reviewer time and training and authority, surviving the theater test:
these are what distinguish oversight that works from oversight that ticks boxes.

4.5 Assurance, audit, documentation
Trustworthiness as a property has to be evidenced. Evidence has to be assembled into
artifacts an external reviewer can inspect: an auditor, a regulator, a downstream user, a
future maintainer. The set of artifacts that does this work for AI has been the subject of
substantial development over the past decade, and the convergence on the main categories
is real.
 Model cards are the foundational artifact. Mitchell et al.’s 2019 paper [134] proposed
the structured document accompanying a trained model that names what the model is
intended for, the conditions under which its evaluation was conducted, the performance
variation across demographic or operational slices, and the known limitations. The point
of the card is not the documentation itself but what the documentation makes inspectable.
A model without a card asks the user to take its performance claims on faith. A model
with a card makes the basis for those claims something an outside reviewer can evaluate
against. The artifact’s value lives in the latter.
 Datasheets are the analog for training data. Gebru et al.’s datasheets-for-datasets frame-
work [135] documents the provenance of the data the model was trained on, the composition
of the dataset, the collection process, recommended uses, and known limitations. The class
of failure datasheets address is the one §3 named repeatedly: nearly every model-level fail-
ure traces back, at some level, to data the developer either characterized incompletely or
did not characterize at all. The datasheet is the artifact that makes that trace possible
after the fact.
 System cards extend the model-card discipline to the deployment level: the same kind
of structured document, but covering integration, system-level behavior, intended deploy-
ment context, and operational constraints rather than just model-level properties. The
Foundation Model Transparency Index [136] extends the discipline further by evaluating
how well foundation-model providers actually publish the artifacts the model-card, system-
card, and datasheet conventions ask for. The index makes transparency itself the object
of measurement, with predictable consequences: providers improve scores where they want
to, and the gap between published artifacts and the operational information the artifacts
are supposed to convey is itself a measurable quantity.
 An audit framework specifies what evidence is presented, by whom, on what schedule,
and to whom. Raji et al.’s closing-AI-accountability-gap framework [137] is the canonical

Evidence and Judgment 57

reference for the AI-specific case: it specifies an end-to-end internal algorithmic auditing
discipline organized around five named stages — scoping, mapping, artifact collection,
testing, and post-audit reflection (SMACTR) — that the development organization runs
against criteria the framework defines, producing documentation designed to be externally
checkable by reviewers and regulators. The frame makes accountability operational rather
than aspirational. It also exposes the standard failure mode of audit-as-discipline: an au-
ditor (whether internal and institutionally constrained from issuing adverse findings, or
external and financially dependent on the audited party) produces artifacts that pass the
documentary requirements while doing none of the verification function. The failure mode
predates AI by decades. The Arthur Andersen / Enron collapse is the textbook external-
audit case; the Service Organization Controls (SOC) and Sarbanes-Oxley audit regimes
that followed exist to constrain exactly that dynamic, and AI audits inherit both internal
and external versions of the failure mode unchanged. The theater-test pattern repeats here,
in the audit-of-artifacts version.
 Cloud services were the structural precursor to AI-as-service, and the SOC reporting
regime is the artifact discipline that grew up around the same problem. A SOC report
(System and Organization Controls) is the artifact; the underlying attestation standard
is the American Institute of Certified Public Accountants (AICPA)’s Statement on Stan-
dards for Attestation Engagements (SSAE) 18, itself the third iteration of the framework
(following SAS 70 in 1992 and SSAE 16 in 2011) and refined across three decades of audit
experience. The report is the standard mechanism by which an organization consuming a
cloud service gains assurance about a system it did not build, does not run, and cannot di-
rectly inspect, without conducting its own audit. The SOC 2 variant in particular, attesting
against the AICPA’s trust-services criteria (security required; availability, processing in-
tegrity, confidentiality, and privacy optional and scoped by the service organization), is the
form that emerged with the Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) economy: downstream consumers
and their own auditors learned to rely on the upstream provider’s SOC report rather than
re-auditing the provider’s controls themselves. AI services delivered by application pro-
gramming interface (API) are structurally the same situation. A downstream organization
integrates a model it did not train, cannot inspect, and cannot reproduce; the question of
what evidence underwrites the integration is the same question SOC reports were invented
to answer for the cloud era. The SOC pattern (its discipline, its mechanism of downstream
reliance, and its capture failure modes) is the direct precursor to what AI accountability
artifacts are now trying to become.
 The SOC regime was supposed to be the unifying answer to the assurance problem:
rather than each downstream consumer auditing the upstream provider themselves, one

independent attestation would serve every customer who needed evidence. In practice the
unification has been partial. Many consuming organizations still conduct their own audits
on top of (or instead of) the SOC report, either treating the SOC report as a compliance
checkbox rather than a substantive trust artifact, or because they have questions the SOC
report does not cover, or because they need scoped assurances against threat models the
SOC scope does not address. The SOC artifact is an attestation: a CPA firm’s report
on what they examined and the conclusions they reached under the engagement’s scope.
A certification would be a binding third-party declaration that the service organization
meets an external standard, and SOC reports were never designed to function as one. A
consumer that treats a SOC report as carrying certification’s authority is reading more into
the artifact than its structure supports. The result is familiar: a unifying standard arrives,
the organizations whose needs it does not cover add their own audits on top, and the audit
landscape ends up with more standards than it had before unification was proposed. AI
accountability artifacts (model cards, datasheets, system cards, assurance cases) are at risk
of inheriting this pattern. The customer-multiplicity dynamic that drove cloud-era audit
proliferation operates with equal force on AI integrations, and the unifying-artifact instinct
is no better equipped to address it now than it was then.
 Assurance cases are the most structurally ambitious of these artifacts. An assurance case
is a structured argument that a system is fit for purpose, with the claims explicit, the
evidence supporting each claim explicit, and the reasoning connecting evidence to claims
explicit. The safety-critical-systems community has been producing assurance cases for
decades (DO-178C aviation, ISO 26262 automotive, International Electrotechnical Com-
mission (IEC) 61508 functional safety more broadly); the AI literature is adapting the
discipline to ML systems’ specific evidence shapes [138], [139]. High-consequence software
has always required structured arguments that the system is fit for purpose; AI changes
what the evidence layer looks like (statistical performance characterizations rather than
formally verified specifications), and the structural shape of the assurance case carries
across.
 Provenance answers “where did this come from”: for training data, for model weights, for
evaluation data, for outputs in deployment. The contemporary literature on provenance for
LLMs [140] surveys the methods available for tracing model behavior to its training-data
origins, and the limits of those methods (training-data contributions become statistically
diffuse in the trained model in ways that prevent clean attribution). For high-consequence
AI, provenance is what makes after-the-fact review possible. An output is contested; the
question of what evidence the model trained on that produced this output is the question

provenance answers. Without provenance, the question is unanswerable; with it, the answer
may still be partial, but it is at least pursuable.
  Reproducibility is sometimes treated as a research virtue separate from accountability.
For the artifact class this subsection is about, it is the same thing: an evaluation result that
cannot be reproduced cannot ground a serious claim, and an audit artifact that depends
on a non-reproducible evaluation is documentation of an unverifiable belief. Albertoni’s
standardization of reproducibility terminology [91] is the artifact-level reference. Repro-
ducibility is not separate from the assurance discipline; it is what makes the assurance
discipline operational.
  The artifacts are real, the practices are maturing, the literature is converging. This
section’s deepest commitment lives in this subsection: serious is what produces artifacts an
external reviewer can check. Anything else is testimonial: a developer or a deployer saying
their system is trustworthy, with nothing the reviewer can evaluate against. The artifacts
have known limitations. Model cards can be written to flatter the model; datasheets can be
incomplete; audits can be captured. Acknowledging the limitations is part of the discipline,
not a reason to drop the artifact requirement. An AI deployment that cannot produce the
artifacts an outside reviewer would need to evaluate its trustworthiness claims has not
made the claims in a form that can be evaluated at all.

4.6 Returning to the question
The framework piece is now in place, and it maps directly onto the two things that give the
justification question its structure: evidence and judgment. The evidence side is evaluation:
without credible evaluation beyond leaderboard scores, there is no evidence for what the
system actually does in the setting being claimed. The judgment side is what the evidence
must be weighed against: deployment scope and fallback behavior, oversight with the time,
training, and authority to act on what is seen, and the artifacts that make the whole claim
checkable to an external reviewer. Trustworthiness is what a system has when both sides are
in place — not asserted, but designed for and evidenced. With both in hand, the question
(is this AI use justified?) can be asked of a specific system, in a specific scope, under a
specific oversight regime, against the evidence the artifacts allow. The question still does
not reduce to arithmetic. The Ford Pinto trap from §1.2 remains active: the framework
does not compute the answer; it supplies the conditions under which a genuine justification
can be constructed and examined.

5 WHAT AI RUNS ON, AND WHY THAT MATTERS
5.1 Context: data centers, supercomputers, and what AI runs on
As an IT adage goes, ”the cloud” is ”some servers in Northern Virginia.” It lives in data
centers: purpose-built facilities housing tens of thousands of servers, accelerator cards,
network switches, and storage devices, organized around the power and cooling that keep
them running. ”Hyperscale” data centers are the largest tier, operated at industrial scale
by Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Meta, Oracle, and a handful of others. A single hyperscale
facility may draw between tens of megawatts and several gigawatts of electrical power and
occupy hundreds of thousands of square feet of building area. Water use depends on how
the facility is cooled: evaporative designs can consume millions of gallons per day, while
closed-loop and air-cooled designs spend additional power to avoid that draw. The facility-
level engineering (power delivery, redundancy, cooling, network fabric, physical security)
is itself a discipline with its own specialists, its own standards, and its own failure modes.
Physical locations for large data centers are selected similar to power plants, based on the
regional infrastructure necessary to support them. Not literally all in Northern Virginia,
data centers are of course globally dispersed, but one of the earliest and largest hyperscale
data center clusters was the Amazon Web Services infrastructure in Ashburn.
 Hyperscale has been operating at this rough shape for fifteen years. What changed re-
cently is the public conversation about it. Headlines about data-center siting fights, grid-
capacity constraints, water-use disclosures, and community-level externalities have moved
from trade publications into general-audience reporting; state regulators, utility commis-
sions, and federal agencies are all newly engaged [141]. The U.S. Department of Energy
(DOE) went through one such process in 2025: a Request for Information on AI infrastruc-
ture on DOE lands [142] kicked off a process that identified sixteen potential federal sites
in April, narrowed by July to four selected for AI data center and energy infrastructure
development: Idaho National Laboratory, Oak Ridge Reservation, Paducah Gaseous Diffu-
sion Plant, and Savannah River Site [143]. Even federal land managed by an agency with a
century of large-scale energy and infrastructure experience requires formal evaluation be-
fore it can host an AI data center. The proximate cause of the broader public engagement
is the AI demand surge: training-scale and inference-scale compute have made hyperscale
visible as a category the public has reason to care about, not as plumbing it does not need
to think about. The substrate did not become contested because the substrate changed.
The substrate became contested because what the substrate is being used for, and at what
scale, has changed.

 Two related observations make the substrate question more than an externalities ques-
tion. First, the power-and-cooling envelope of high-performance computing (HPC)-AI sys-
tems is itself an engineering constraint that shapes what AI workloads are feasible at what
cost [144]. Cooling capacity, grid interconnect, and water availability are not abstractions
about sustainability; they are operational properties of the facility that determine whether
a planned compute workload can actually be run, and at what wall-clock and dollar cost.
Second, the sustainability and scalability of the underlying compute is itself a dimension
on which AI claims can be evaluated: a capability that cannot be sustained at the compute
scale required to deliver it is not a capability the deploying organization actually has [145].
The substrate is not a sideshow. It is part of what makes any specific claim about AI
capability operationally honest.
 A typical researcher’s concept of “running computations” is probably shaped by personal
computers or by cloud services: log in, fire up a program, run a script, spreadsheet, or
routine. Supercomputers do not work that way. They are shared facilities, gated by peer-
reviewed allocation programs that decide who gets how many node-hours, queue-based job
schedulers that decide when an allocated job actually runs, parallel programming models
that decide how a workload is decomposed across thousands of processing units, and parallel
filesystems that decide how data moves between storage and compute at facility scale at
speeds and delays on wholly different orders of magnitudes than even general purpose
hyperscale datacenters. The stack has its own mental model and its own dependencies.
“Use a supercomputer” does not mean “log in and run things.” It means fit your work
into a shared-facility model that has been engineered for throughput at scale. Large-scale
scientific computing has needed that model for decades, and a wide range of contemporary
AI work needs much of the same substrate. The frontier-model training that has dominated
recent press coverage is a partial exception worth flagging up front: most of that work
happens on dedicated industry clusters operated under different access rules than the user
facilities this paragraph describes, even though the underlying architectural properties are
similar.
 This workflow is structurally what computing was for everyone in the punch-card era.
You did all the prep work, submitted your stack of cards, waited in line, and hoped your
job ran cleanly; if it did not, you fixed it and went back to the end of the line. Interactive
terminals replaced the pattern for most computing in the 1970s and 1980s, but for shared-
facility supercomputing the same shape persists because the underlying constraint persists:
many users, finite compute, scheduling required. The job script and the job queue are the
contemporary version of the deck of cards and the operator’s in-tray.

 Parallel programming is its own paradigm, not just serial programming with more cores.
It requires explicit decomposition (how to break the problem into work units that can run
independently or with bounded coordination), explicit communication (how those units
exchange data), explicit synchronization (when and how units wait for each other), and
explicit data layout (how data is partitioned across nodes’ memories, because moving
data is often more expensive than computing on it). Debugging is harder: race condi-
tions (the answer depends on which part of a computation finishes first), deadlocks (the
computing equivalent of a logjam), and non-determinism that do not appear in sequential
code. Pacheco and Malensek’s Introduction to Parallel Programming [146] is the canoni-
cal textbook treatment, presenting decomposition, communication, synchronization, load
balancing, and the standard tooling as distinct programming models with their own men-
tal models. Many machine-learning workloads at meaningful scale live in this paradigm.
Frontier-scale large language model training is the most visible example: a single training
run coordinates gradient updates across thousands of accelerators, each holding a slice of
the model’s parameters, each exchanging partial results with the others on every training
step. The parallel-HPC requirement also surfaces in less visible places: large hyperparam-
eter sweeps that train and evaluate hundreds of model variants in parallel; distributed
training and fine-tuning of mid-scale models; evaluation pipelines that have to process
terabyte-scale datasets; and scientific-ML workloads that couple machine-learning compo-
nents to simulation codes that have been running on supercomputers for decades. The leap
from “I have access to compute” to “I am using that compute well” is enormous. Assuming
the leap is trivial is one of the ways infrastructure spending fails to translate into capability.
 These supercomputer-use realities shape any HPC-scale workload, and they have specific
implications for AI work that runs on these facilities. They explain why supercomputer-
scale work is gated rather than commoditizable: the facility is hardware plus a software
stack plus user support plus trained users, and none of those pieces is fungible. Access
programs solve only the access problem; the software-stack and human-capacity substrate
is what determines whether the access actually translates into capability [147]. “Get more
supercomputers” is not, by itself, a coherent infrastructure strategy. The substrate is multi-
layered.
 The substrate has a hardware layer below the facility layer. central processing units
(CPUs) are general-purpose processors optimized for low-latency sequential execution:
branch prediction, large caches, instruction reordering, deep pipelines designed to execute
one stream of instructions as fast as possible. That’s what runs in your laptop, your phone,
and the servers behind most web applications. AI training and inference are a different
problem.

  Graphics processing units (GPUs) are throughput-oriented processors that evolved from
the demands of computer graphics. Rendering a video-game frame is a parallel problem:
each of the millions of pixels on the screen needs the same kind of operation (compute color,
apply transform, apply shading) done independently of every other pixel. That architectural
shape, many simple processors doing the same operation across many data elements simul-
taneously (the Single Instruction, Multiple Data (SIMD) model), turned out to be the same
shape neural-network compute needs. The dominant operation in neural-network training
(the phase where the model’s parameters are fit from data) and inference (the phase where
the trained model is run on new inputs to produce outputs) is matrix multiplication, and
matrix multiplication is structurally matched to GPU architecture. The same hardware
that renders video games also trains and runs ChatGPT. This is why NVIDIA dominates
AI compute. The company’s Compute Unified Device Architecture (CUDA) programming
framework has become the industry-standard substrate for GPU compute, and NVIDIA’s
market position is a consequence of the architectural fit, not an accident of branding or
distribution.
  Purpose-built AI silicon extends GPU-style throughput optimization further in specific
directions, with designs each tuned for some specific corner of the workload landscape:
sometimes training, sometimes inference, sometimes specific model families [148]. Repre-
sentative examples include Google’s Tensor Processing Units (TPUs), Cerebras’s wafer-
scale engines, Graphcore’s Intelligence Processing Units (IPUs), Groq’s language-model
inference accelerators, AWS Trainium and Inferentia chips, and several custom designs
at the frontier labs. The substantive point for the rest of the section is that the choice
of hardware substrate shapes which AI workloads are feasible at which cost. This is not
a tooling-choice question for an end user. It is a capability-shape question for the field.
Training a frontier-scale model is a hardware-software co-design problem at the scale of
multi-billion-dollar facility investments.

5.2 Three flavors of AI compute, often conflated
The public conversation about AI infrastructure mixes together three distinct kinds of
compute environments with different access models, software stacks, cost structures, and
credibility implications.
  DOE open user facilities as just discussed in 5.1, and similar facilities run by the National
Science Foundation and universities are the first flavor. These run on peer-reviewed allo-
cation programs, with diverse user bases across the science domains and HPC-traditional
software stacks. AI workloads run on these facilities alongside computational fluid dynam-
ics, particle physics simulation, climate modeling, cosmology, computational chemistry,

and the rest of the workload mix that defines scientific HPC. The facilities are configured
around scientific computing as the primary customer, with AI workloads accommodated

 Purpose-built training clusters at frontier AI labs are the second flavor: the dedicated
infrastructure operated by OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, xAI, and a handful of others.
These are single-tenant facilities optimized for one organization’s frontier-model training
pipeline, often built with custom hardware for communication between components and
nodes, custom software stacks beyond the open HPC tooling, and procurement budgets
that dwarf even the largest user-facility builds. They are generally not accessible to outside
users. Public information about their internal architecture and operational properties is
patchy.
 General-purpose cloud compute is the third flavor: AWS, Azure, GCP, and similar. Multi-

class among many, alongside web applications, databases, analytics, video streaming, gam-

enterprise IT than to scientific HPC: Kubernetes for orchestration, virtualized resources,
REST APIs as the primary interface. AI workloads on cloud compute include training (for
labs and companies that do not build their own clusters), inference serving (the domi-

depends on.
 The three have different credibility implications. A frontier-lab training run, a DOE user-
facility scientific workload, and a cloud-hosted production inference each support different
reproducibility properties (who can see the training data? can the work be re-run? by
whom? under what conditions?), different auditability properties (what evidence can a
third party verify, and at what level of detail?), and different assurance properties (what
claims about the system’s behavior can be backed by what evidence?). Conflating the
three is one of the ways the AI infrastructure conversation loses precision. Mission-oriented
science work depends mostly on the first flavor, and the assurance and credibility frame
this report is building has to account for which of the three a given claim depends on.
 Most people who haven’t worked with an AI model assume it is a computer program:
source code in some programming language that runs to produce outputs. A trained model
is largely not that. The architecture (the structure that defines what computation will
happen: transformer with this many layers, this much attention, this many hidden dimen-

content of the model) are a matrix of billions of floating-point numbers, the result of a
multi-week multi-million-dollar training run that found the specific values that make this

separable, and conflating them produces specific operational confusion.

retrieval augmentation, the fine-tuning layered on top, the rate limits, and the conditions

5.3 Compute as part of credibility
The substantive thesis of this chapter has been implicit in the prior subsections and is
worth naming explicitly: compute is part of credibility. Training compute decisions shape
what evaluation is possible (a model trained at one scale is evaluated by different methods
than one trained at another scale, not better or worse). Deployment compute decisions
shape what monitoring is feasible (drift detection at scale is a compute problem before it
is a methodology problem). Sustainability decisions about power, cooling, and accelera-
tor utilization shape what claims about long-term operation can survive. The framework
developed in §4 (trust, evaluation, bounded deployment, oversight, artifacts) runs on this
substrate. Treating compute as a separate engineering concern from credibility leaves the
framework’s claims floating without their substrate.
 Modern AI compute has two fundamentally different shapes that get conflated in casual
discussion. training is compute-heavy and concentrated: a frontier large language model is
trained once (with periodic re-training) over weeks to months on thousands of accelerators,
at frontier-lab scale costing tens to hundreds of millions of dollars; smaller models and
classical ML train at correspondingly smaller scales. inference is compute-light per call
but at-scale cumulative: serving one query takes a small fraction of a GPU’s capacity
for sub-second wall-clock time, but serving billions of queries against a deployed model
accumulates to a compute total that, for many widely-deployed models, exceeds the original
training cost. The orchestra metaphor above extends naturally: training is composing the
piece, inference is each subsequent performance, and a popular piece performed thousands
of times worldwide can cumulatively dwarf even large composition efforts. Luccioni et
al. [149] make the asymmetry measured: across 88 models and 10 tasks, multi-purpose
generative architectures consumed orders of magnitude more energy per query than task-
specific models built for the same task, and major cloud providers attribute 60–90% of
their ML compute demand to inference rather than training.

 The two profiles have different infrastructure footprints (training wants the largest syn-
chronized cluster possible; inference wants distributed, latency-optimized, often geographically-
replicated serving), different cost structures (training is a capital expenditure spike; infer-
ence is operational expenditure that scales with usage), different operational properties
(training tolerates failures via checkpoint-restart; inference is latency-sensitive and user-
facing), and different stakeholder profiles (training capacity is dominated by a handful of
frontier labs and large cloud providers; inference is distributed across every organization
that deploys a model). For non-LLM AI the asymmetry persists at smaller scale (a pre-
dictive ML model trained on a workstation in hours serves inference at milliseconds per
request), but the structural point holds: training-shaped compute and inference-shaped
compute are different problems with different substrates. The credibility implication: claims
about a deployed model’s compute footprint, sustainability, or accessibility have to spec-
ify which side of the asymmetry they describe. The “AI compute crunch” headlines have
at least two distinct manifestations: training capacity (frontier labs and nation-state ac-
tors competing for accelerators) and inference capacity (serving platforms struggling with
latency, cost, and capacity-per-watt). They are different problems with different solutions.
 The contemporary AI compute appetite is downstream of a data-availability shift that
began in the early 2010s. Pre-2010s machine learning was largely data-limited: the bottle-
neck was assembling labeled datasets large enough to train models that generalized. The
internet-scale text, image, video, and code corpora that became available moved the bottle-
neck from data acquisition to compute and model capacity capable of absorbing the data.
Common Crawl’s monthly web crawls [150], Wikipedia’s curated text [151], the Internet
Archive’s preserved web and books [152], ImageNet and its successors for vision, and the
public code repositories on GitHub were among the substrates contemporary AI-training
practice grew up around. Academic efforts aggregating and cleaning these for language-
model training (The Pile [153], the Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus [154]) document the
specific data substrates and the choices their assemblers made about inclusion, filtering,
and provenance. The willingness to use such corpora, often without explicit licensing or
labeling, is itself part of that practice. Two implications for the credibility argument follow.
First, the data substrate is itself part of compute-as-credibility: claims about a model rest
on claims about what it was trained on, which the model’s user often cannot independently
verify; The provenance and reproducibility material from §4.5 sits inside this dependence.
Second, the data substrate is largely a training-side concern: inference operates on indi-
vidual queries against frozen weights. Training is where data, compute, and methodology
questions concentrate; inference is where deployment, monitoring, and serving questions

 The DOE-leadership articulation of HPC infrastructure for mission-oriented AI work is
Carter et al.’s 2023 Advanced Research Directions on AI for Science, Energy, and Secu-
rity [155]. The report is a multi-laboratory synthesis of what AI infrastructure has to look
like for the national-laboratory mission set: climate, materials, fusion, nuclear security, bio-
logical systems, energy systems, and the cross-cutting computational science that connects
them. The substantive claim aligns with this subsection’s thesis: the compute substrate
is not separate from the AI capability claims it supports. The infrastructure required for
AI-for-science is HPC-class, and the credibility of any specific AI-for-science claim depends
on the credibility of the substrate the claim was produced on.
 Carter et al. develops the substrate-and-credibility argument across several threads. Sci-
entific AI workloads inherit the resource profiles of the science they support: simulation-
coupled AI workloads inherit simulation’s resource appetite; AI-driven experimental or-
chestration inherits the experimental loop’s latency and reliability requirements; LLM work
for scientific applications inherits LLM training’s compute appetite. Data is treated as a
first-class substrate concern alongside compute: experimental datasets, simulation outputs,
observational archives, and the curation pipelines that make them usable are all part of
what HPC infrastructure for AI-for-science has to support. Institutional substrate (alloca-
tion programs, user support, software stacks, training pipelines for human users) is what
makes the hardware substrate translatable into capability, in line with the supercomputer-
use realities described above. Each thread makes a specific claim about what credibility
requires at the substrate level for mission-oriented AI work.
 Scaling laws make training-compute choices predictable. Kaplan et al.’s 2020 observation
that language-model performance scales smoothly with compute, model size, and dataset
size [156] was widely interpreted as licensing a “bigger is better” approach to model de-
sign: for a given compute budget, the path to better performance was mostly to make the
model bigger. Two years later, DeepMind’s Chinchilla study [157] overturned that inter-
pretation. The team trained a 70-billion-parameter model (which they named Chinchilla)
on substantially more data than the prevailing recipe called for, and showed that it out-
performed contemporary models roughly four times its size, including DeepMind’s own
earlier 280-billion-parameter Gopher. The lesson the field absorbed: for a given compute
budget there is an optimal balance between model size and training-data volume, and the
prior generation of frontier models had been too large for the amount of data they were
trained on. Sevilla et al.’s longitudinal study of training-compute trends across three eras

of machine learning [158] traces the historical context: training compute grew at Moore’s-

and developed a parallel large-scale high-compute trend from about 2015 separate from

  HPC and AI workloads have converged in their resource profiles and operational require-

workflows where the two interleave [147]. Ettifouri et al. make the broader case that HPC
infrastructure is increasingly necessary for the AI workload class, not just an option for
the largest training runs [160]. The infrastructure consequence: facilities designed for sci-
entific HPC have to extend their software stacks, allocation programs, and user-support
models to handle AI workload profiles. Facilities designed primarily for AI have to extend
in the opposite direction if they want to support the scientific-computing workloads that
increasingly couple ML components to simulation. The convergence is not just a research
observation; it is an infrastructure design constraint that mission-oriented institutions are
working out in real time.

5.4 Institutional substrate
The substrate has another layer above the facility: the institutional and program archi-
tecture that decides who gets access, on what terms, under what governance. The DOE
national laboratories with significant computational missions each operate some version
of this layer, though the specific shape varies with mission and scale. Argonne is the case
we are most familiar with and is used as the representative example through the rest of
this subsection; the comparable structures at the other large-compute DOE labs follow the
same general pattern.
  Three institutional substrates do different work and are required together. The first is the
open user facility, accessible to external scientific users through peer-reviewed allocation
programs. At Argonne, this is the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility (ALCF), which
has hosted Aurora and its predecessor systems and allocates time to outside users through
the DOE-wide INCITE and ALCC peer-reviewed allocation programs. Aurora’s successor,
currently in development, is the NVIDIA-Oracle Solstice and Equinox build characterized

by the DOE as its largest AI supercomputer: designed with AI workloads particularly in
mind, though not exclusively so [161]. The build sits inside a broader DOE strategic frame
articulated as the Genesis Mission, which couples AI infrastructure development with the
energy infrastructure development needed to power it [162].
  The second substrate is the institutional-laboratory compute layer, which supports staff
and collaborators whose work does not fit the open-allocation programs (different funding
model, different timelines, often more sensitive data, often more exploratory work). At
Argonne, this is the Laboratory Computing Resource Center (LCRC). The third is dedi-
cated experimental AI infrastructure, intended for evaluating novel AI accelerators outside
the conventional HPC tooling. At Argonne, this is the ALCF AI Testbed [163], which
hosts Cerebras, SambaNova, Groq, Graphcore, and other purpose-built AI silicon for use
by approved researchers through ALCF’s Director’s Discretionary allocation. The three
substrates are not redundant. Each addresses a workload class the others cannot.
  At the program-architecture level above any single facility, the DOE’s American Science
Cloud initiative [164] proposes to make compute substrate available to the national sci-
ence enterprise as a coherent shared service rather than as facility-by-facility allocations.
The shift matters specifically for AI work that depends on multi-year training-evaluation-
monitoring cycles or on coordination across multiple scientific domains, both of which strain
the project-by-project allocation model that scientific HPC traditionally uses.
  The point for the credibility argument is not that any of these specific facilities, part-
nerships, or programs are the right choices for any particular institution. The point is that
institutional substrate at this level (operational facilities, governance programs, dedicated
AI infrastructure, and shared-service architectures) is part of what credibility for mission-
oriented AI work requires. The hardware-and-facility substrate is not usable in isolation.
The institutional substrate is what determines who can use it for what.

5.5 Sustainability and the forward look
Sustainability surfaces in AI infrastructure discussions in two registers that often get con-
flated. The first is environmental advocacy: AI compute draws power, training and inference
emit carbon, and the broader societal and planetary costs of the AI hype cycle are real
and documented [2]. That question is legitimate but not the one this subsection is asking.
The second is what matters for the credibility argument: sustainability is an operational
constraint on what claims a deployment can support. A capability demonstrated once at
full burn-rate compute is not a capability that survives operationally without a sustain-
ability story to back it. Varoquaux et al. [145] critique the “bigger is better” paradigm
on exactly these grounds: as training-compute escalates, the economic and environmental

Evidence and Judgment 71

costs of long-term deployment rise with it, creating barriers many institutions cannot sus-
tain. A deployment that depends on compute the institution cannot sustain has not been
bounded honestly. The connection to the bounded-deployment frame is direct: a deploy-
ment whose compute appetite is not sustainably matched to the institution’s resources has
not characterized its operational envelope in the way bounded deployment requires.
  Most contemporary AI compute runs on von Neumann architecture: separate memory
and processor with data shuttled between them, synchronous clock-driven execution, the
well-known bottleneck called the memory wall. Two research programs are worth nam-
ing briefly for the forward look. neuromorphic computing asks whether non-von-Neumann
alternatives (event-driven spiking neural networks with collocated memory and computa-
tion, inspired by biological neurons) can deliver dramatic energy-efficiency wins on certain
workloads [165]. Intel’s Loihi, IBM’s earlier TrueNorth, Manchester’s SpiNNaker, and Hei-
delberg’s BrainScaleS are among the contemporary projects. quantum computing pursues
a different escape from the classical substrate entirely: a computational model based on
quantum-mechanical superposition rather than classical bits, suited for specific problem
classes including certain optimization and sampling problems with AI relevance [166]. Nei-
ther is deployment substrate at production scale today, but both are worth naming here
because the substrate question has more than one possible answer over a long enough
horizon, and the contemporary von-Neumann substrate is not the end of the conversation.
The brain-as-substrate thread traced in §2.1 through McCulloch and Pitts, Wiener, and
von Neumann’s own The Computer and the Brain has a contemporary continuation in
neuromorphic computing; quantum computing operates from a different lineage entirely.

5.6 Returning to the question
The framework piece (§4) and the substrate piece (this section) are both now in place.
Compute infrastructure is not a separate concern from trustworthy AI; it is the substrate
that makes the artifacts described in §4.5 (model cards, datasheets, system cards, audit
reports, assurance cases, provenance) producible at the scale serious work requires. Mission-
oriented institutions that take AI seriously have to take both sides as part of the same
investment.
  With both framework and substrate in hand, the question we keep asking, is this AI
use justified?, can be put to a specific system, in a specific scope, under a specific over-
sight regime, running on a specific substrate. The answer can then mean something rather
than dissolve into abstraction. §6 walks the question across research settings, where the
framework’s properties show up differently depending on what the work is for.

6 THE QUESTION ACROSS RESEARCH SETTINGS
The question — is this AI use justified? — does not have a single answer, but it has a
texture that shifts with the kind of research being done. Four settings make this concrete:
text-intensive work, experimentation and autonomous discovery, coding and simulation,
and mission-critical deployments. Across all four, the §4 framework applies; what shifts
is which failure modes arise most consequentially, what credible evaluation looks like in
practice, and what oversight actually requires of the people involved.

6.1 Text-intensive research
Text-intensive research is likely the most familiar of the settings here. It includes literature
synthesis (assembling, comparing, and summarizing prior work), writing assistance (draft-
ing, editing, restructuring scientific prose), and the increasingly automated systematic-
literature-review pipelines that combine the two. The underlying methods are foundation
models and retrieval-augmented generation; the dominant failure mode is hallucination,
manifesting in text as fabricated citations, paraphrased misattribution, and confidently-
asserted claims unsupported by the cited sources.
 Evaluation beyond leaderboards matters because text-task benchmarks notoriously fail
to capture the qualities that determine deployment value: faithfulness to source material,
citation accuracy, register appropriate to the intended audience. Bounded deployment mat-
ters because the failure modes are well-characterized: hallucinated citations are predictable
in the lexical-fabrication sense, and bounded deployment is what turns that predictability
into operational discipline. Human oversight matters because text outputs are checkable
by readers with domain expertise and largely uncheckable by readers without; the time-
training-authority requirements show up in the specific question of whether the reviewer
can verify what the generator produced.
 Two pieces of setting-specific texture are worth naming. The first is a distinction the
grounding literature has sharpened: grounding (every claim in a response attributable to
a document in the user’s specified knowledge base) is a stricter requirement than non-
hallucination. A claim can be accurate by general standards and still fail grounding if
it contradicts the authoritative sources the user supplied. Kenthapadi et al. [167] work
through the practical implications: RAG is the canonical architectural response to ground-
ing failures because it exposes the model to relevant material at inference time, but success-
ful retrieval does not guarantee faithful use of what is retrieved. Han et al. [168] document
the application of RAG-based pipelines to systematic literature reviews across the main

Evidence and Judgment 73

SLR tasks — search, screening, data extraction, and synthesis — and note that RAG mit-
igates hallucinations by anchoring outputs in verifiable sources, though the faithfulness of
that anchoring depends on the quality of what is retrieved and how the model uses it.
Brown et al. [169] survey the broader RAG-techniques methodological landscape. Across
all three: grounding and verification have to be designed into the workflow, not assumed
as byproducts of retrieval.
  The second piece is accountability. Carobene et al. [170] document the rapid adoption
of AI tools in scientific publishing alongside the ethical questions it has opened: who bears
responsibility when AI-produced content contains errors, how intellectual property is at-
tributed when a model was trained on copyrighted material, and why the field currently
lacks consensus on either. Hryciw et al. [171] propose governing principles (integrity, trans-
parency, validity, accountability) and a classification system ranging from proofreading to
full manuscript generation that specifies what the AI contributed without requiring prior
resolution of the harder question of AI authorship, while leaving full accountability with the
human author. The framework is drawn from medical writing; the accountability structure
applies broadly.
  What is distinctive about text-intensive research in the §4 framework is that the failures
are legible: citations can be checked, claims can be verified against sources, output can be
compared to ground truth where ground truth exists. The complication is that the volume
at which an LLM can produce text is much higher than the volume at which a human
reader can verify it. The serious-evaluation question becomes one of workflow design: how
does the verification pipeline keep up with what the generator produces, and how does
the discipline of disclosure track which parts of a piece of writing came from where? We
address both questions as they bear on the production of this document in the afterword.

6.2 Experimentation and autonomous discovery
AI-for-science (the use of AI methods inside the scientific workflow as an instrument of
inquiry, not a subject of study) spans three substantive use modes: autonomous experi-
mentation and self-driving laboratories (AI orchestrating the loop from hypothesis gen-
eration through instrument control through analysis); domain-specific foundation models
(large pre-trained models specialized for chemistry, biology, physics, power grid dynamics,
materials science, etc); and the broader integration of agentic AI into scientific discovery
workflows. Computational surrogates and simulation proxies are a fourth mode, grouped
with code assistance in the next subsection because both generate executable computational

and institutional infrastructure that make AI-for-science claims credible and addressing
the assurance challenges from a national-laboratory vantage. The conversation spans aca-

  Self-driving laboratorys exist on a spectrum of automation. At the augmented-iteration
end, currently the most common form, AI advises on the next experimental condition, a
human approves and executes, and AI analyzes the result; the loop accelerates, but human
judgment remains present at each step. At the autonomous end, AI orchestrates the full
cycle (instrument control, data collection, analysis, next-experiment proposal) with the
human setting the goal and reviewing aggregate output rather than individual decisions.
This is the setting where the shovel-analogy frame from §1.3 fully breaks down: the shovel
never decided where to dig. Tom et al. [173] survey the technical landscape across this full
range: hardware, software, lab-infrastructure integration, and real-world examples across
chemistry, materials science, drug discovery, and genomics, with particular attention to
what each domain’s automation challenges look like at different levels of autonomy. To-

resist standardization, integrating autonomous experiment loops with HPC workflows and
access-controlled national-facility networks, and the ethical framework needed for research
conducted by systems that make decisions between human review points. The assurance
challenge scales with where on the spectrum a deployment sits. MacLeod et al. [176] treat

oversight-design challenge for the human role. At the augmented end, the oversight ques-

them? At the autonomous end it sharpens: what does meaningful oversight look like when

 The term “foundation model” covers the full category: large pre-trained models that
can be adapted for downstream tasks, of which LLMs are the most familiar instance [35].
Scientific domain foundation models instantiate the same paradigm on non-text data. A
molecular foundation model may encode structures as graphs of atoms and bonds, or as
three-dimensional point clouds of atomic positions; protein and genomic sequences use
a domain alphabet (twenty amino acids or four nucleotides) that permits tokenization
similar to text but with very different statistics; a power grid model works from time-
series of node voltages and power flows over a network topology; a physics model may
operate on discretized fields from numerical simulation. Each choice drives the architecture:
graph neural networks for molecular graphs, geometric deep learning for 3D structures,
convolutional or recurrent layers for spatial and temporal fields. Many scientific domain
foundation models are not transformer-based at all, though some use heavily modified
transformer variants (AlphaFold’s Evoformer [177] is a prominent example). The result
is a heterogeneous set of models that share the foundation-model paradigm (large pre-
trained, adaptable to downstream tasks) but differ substantially from consumer LLMs in
data representation, architecture, evaluation practices, and the assurance literatures that
are still forming around them. Choi et al. [178] survey the chemistry landscape; Chen et

from §4 applies in each domain, but the specifics differ substantially: what evaluation looks
like for a chemistry foundation model (property prediction accuracy, generalization to novel
molecular classes, behavior on unexplored chemical space) is different from what it looks like
for a general-purpose LLM, and different again from what it looks like for a bioinformatics
model predicting protein function from sequence.
 Agentic AI for scientific discovery (systems that chain tools, plan multi-step inquiries,
and execute iterative experimental loops) is the contemporary frontier where the preced-

experiments end-to-end with minimal human intervention, to collaborative systems where
human researchers remain in the loop at each stage. The domain coverage is chemistry,
biology, and materials science. A diagnostic finding in the survey: these systems tend to
perform well on data preparation, experimentation, and report writing, but performance
drops substantially in the literature review phase, reflecting a structural difficulty in au-

 The assurance challenge in experimentation and autonomous discovery operates at the
level of scientific-discovery claims. Whether the AI component performed correctly (the
autonomous lab’s cycles executed, the foundation model’s prediction matched validation
data) is the first-order question. The deeper one is whether the result the AI-assisted
workflow produces counts as scientific knowledge in the sense the field has always required:
reproducible, verifiable, grounded in evidence, and interpretable by someone who did not
produce it.
 The mathematical case makes this precise. Thurston argued [183] that mathematical
progress is not primarily about producing correct proofs but about advancing human un-
derstanding: “finding ways for people to understand and think about mathematics.” The
controversy over the computer-verified four-color theorem, he observed, reflected “a contin-
uing desire for human understanding of a proof, in addition to knowledge that the theorem
is true.” What mathematicians want is not a collection of correct answers but the under-
standing that enables the next generation of work. That tension is now tested in practice. AI
systems have produced formally verified proofs of nine previously open Erdős conjectures
and 44 conjectures from the Online Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences (OEIS) [184], and
solved three of the five non-geometry problems at the 2024 International Mathematical
Olympiad (IMO) at silver-medal equivalence (the geometry problem was handled sepa-
rately by AlphaGeometry 2) [185]. The proofs are formally correct, machine-verified in
Lean, a formal proof assistant. What mathematicians can do with them is more nuanced

analog is the question that awaits the field: when a self-driving lab produces an experimen-

result something scientists can build on (extracting insight about the underlying mecha-

would sharpen or falsify the result) rather than merely verify?

questions §8 takes up as a research program.

6.3 Coding and simulation
Code assistance and surrogate modeling are distinct applications, but they share a struc-
tural property that makes them worth treating together: in both cases, AI generates an
executable computational artifact (code or numerical model) that is then embedded in a
research or engineering workflow. The failure modes in both cases are dominated not by
visible crashes or type errors but by quietly wrong results that pass first-order checks. The
assurance question in both cases is the same: does the artifact do the right thing, not just
the specified thing?

Code assistance. AI coding assistants span a spectrum: autocomplete tools that suggest
the next line, conversational systems that generate functions from a description, and
agentic software-engineering systems capable of multi-step autonomous workflows. Across
that spectrum, AI-assisted coding is most accurately framed as the extension of existing
software-development practice to a new kind of contributor. The verification layer that ma-
ture software organizations built around human contributors (type checkers, linters, unit
and integration tests, code review, CI gates, runtime invariants) was always contributor-
agnostic. Adding a model to the contributor pool adds a contributor the existing layer
needs to validate. It does not introduce a need for verification that did not exist before.
§3.4 made this argument at the assurance level. What follows makes it concrete at the code
level.

 The reason AI-assisted coding requires its own treatment is throughput. Models pro-
duce code faster than informal review processes naturally scale to. Gates that were always
advisable (types, tests, lint, runtime invariants, review) become genuinely necessary when
the contributor produces code at machine pace. Software organizations whose development
discipline was already strong absorb a model contributor with mostly mechanical adjust-
ments. Organizations whose discipline rested on “we are a small team and we know each
other’s code” find that trust does not extend to a model, and the gaps surface quickly. Part
of what the AI-for-code conversation surfaces is a software engineering discipline gap that
existed before the model arrived, now made urgent by a contributor that produces wrong
code faster than the prior failure modes did.
 The productivity literature shows measurable speedups for routine coding tasks, with
the size and durability of the effect depending heavily on task type and developer expe-
rience [186]–[189]. The correctness picture is more instructive. AI-generated code passes
tests at meaningful rates, but the most operationally significant failure modes are not the
visible ones. Fabricated function names and invented library imports (the failure class the
early discourse focused on) get caught reliably by type checkers and linters. The failures
that survive that filter are different: plausible-sounding implementations of subtly wrong
algorithms, off-by-one variants that pass the test the model wrote for itself, and rationale
for a fix that addresses the surface symptom without touching the underlying cause. That
last pattern deserves a name: the model produces something structurally similar to what
§3.2 called hallucination, but at the level of explanation rather than facts. A change makes
the symptom disappear, supported by confident reasoning about why the fix is correct,
while the root cause remains. On security, AI-generated code inherits whatever insecure
patterns are present in its training data, and the verification layer has to account for that
accordingly.
 Scientific software adds a failure-mode asymmetry that changes the operational picture
substantially. Normal production code fails visibly: it crashes, throws exceptions, returns
malformed data that breaks downstream callers, fails its tests. “Passes tests and doesn’t
blow up” is an imperfect but meaningful signal. Scientific code fails differently. Its dominant
failure mode is quiet wrong answers: code that runs cleanly, returns plausibly-shaped output
in the expected numerical range, passes every test the author wrote, and produces a result
that is simply incorrect. No exception is raised. The number that comes out has the units
the downstream analysis expects. The downstream consequence is a published finding that
does not replicate, a decision made on wrong data, or a line of subsequent work built on
a result that was already wrong before anyone looked at it. These are the §3.2 and §3.3
failure modes in their default-rather-than-special-case form.

  The prototype-graduation problem compounds this. Research code is written to answer
a question under whatever verification the original author found sufficient to get the paper
out. Once the code exists, it gets reused, extended, and depended upon, often by people who
were not the original author, often for purposes the original code was not built to support.
The loose-discipline-because-it’s-a-prototype assumption rots silently as the code becomes
something others rely on. Hardening a prototype after the fact is expensive; letting under-
instrumented code accumulate dependencies is more expensive still. AI-assisted coding
amplifies this dynamic in both directions: an assistant can produce prototype-quality code
faster, growing the backlog of under-tested work, and an assistant can also produce the
discipline (tests, type annotations, runtime invariants, documentation) faster, if the author
asks for it.
  Several testing techniques well-suited to this setting remain unfamiliar to many scientific
software developers, having originated in specialized communities distant from day-to-day
research coding. property-based testing emerged from functional programming: rather than
hand-writing example inputs, the developer states an invariant and a library generates
hundreds of inputs to try to falsify it; the Hypothesis library is a canonical Python imple-
mentation, and the technique reliably finds edge cases that example-based test suites miss.
metamorphic testing comes from academic software testing research: where a ground-truth
oracle is unavailable, it verifies via known relationships between inputs and outputs (if
f(x) equals y, then f(g(x)) should equal h(y)). The technique matters most when the
correct answer is itself unobservable, as it frequently is in scientific computation. runtime
invariants (explicit assertions at boundaries of known-true assumptions, so violations crash
loudly at the point of failure rather than propagating as wrong output) come from formal
methods and contract-based programming traditions. data contracts (schema validation at
system boundaries) come from data engineering practice. The case for each has always been
sound. What changes is urgency: in a setting where the dominant failure mode is invisible
by default, these techniques belong in the scientific software mainstream rather than in the
corners of the literature most researchers have not yet encountered.
  The §4 framework applies in recognizable form. Evaluation beyond leaderboards matters
because HumanEval and similar benchmarks measure a narrow slice of coding ability that
does not include the curve-fitting and silent-wrong-answer failure modes. Bounded deploy-
ment means specifying which code-generation tasks fall within scope for which use cases,
and what the scope documentation looks like. Human oversight takes the specific form of
review burden as a function of throughput; the diagnostic (if the reviewer would always
approve, oversight is theater) applies with particular force when the assistant generates
faster than review naturally scales. The serious-evaluation question for AI-assisted code

Surrogate modeling and simulation. Surrogate model sits at the other end of the code-versus-
model spectrum: AI is not helping write the computation, it is the computation. ML surro-
gates approximate the input-output behavior of expensive numerical simulation (computa-
tional fluid dynamics, molecular dynamics, finite-element analysis, agent-based models) at
much lower computational cost. The enabling application is the class of “many-query” prob-
lems (parameter sweeps, inverse problems, model-based optimization) that require running
a simulation thousands of times sequentially: full-fidelity simulation at that scale is com-
putationally infeasible; a well-characterized surrogate makes it tractable, with documented
speedups from 100× to over one billion times in deployed applications [155]. Alizadeh
et al. [190] and Kim and Boukouvala [191] survey the methodology and its accuracy-
versus-complexity tradeoffs for engineering optimization; Angione et al. [192] document
the agent-based-simulation case specifically. One disambiguation matters here specifically:
VanDerHorn and Mahadevan [193] note that a surrogate model alone is not a digital twin.
A surrogate approximates a computational model; a digital twin tracks current and past
states of a specific physical system instance. A surrogate can be used within a digital
twin, and frequently is, but the assurance requirements for the two configurations differ.
The evaluation question for surrogate modeling as such is tractable: validate the surrogate
against the simulation it approximates, characterize accuracy degradation across the input
domain, and scope deployment within the validated regime.
 The harder case arises when the simulation itself is unavailable. There are physical sys-
tems where the phenomena of greatest interest are intrinsically hazardous or inaccessible
to instrument at scale, or where the failure modes that matter most cannot be observed in
normal operation. There are systems whose dynamics are too complex or too poorly char-
acterized for a fully closed-form analytical model to be derivable. When both constraints
apply (data collection limited by the system’s hazard profile, formal modeling limited by
the system’s complexity), ML surrogates trained on what is available become the only
viable substrate for research questions that are otherwise unanswerable. That available
substrate takes several forms: instrumentation from non-hazardous operating regimes, low-
fidelity simulations of adjacent phenomena, partial measurements from accessible bound-
aries, and physical constraints encoded as priors. Physics-informed approaches [33] extend

this by baking known physical constraints into the surrogate’s training objective, so the
model is rewarded for behavioral consistency with conservation laws and boundary con-
ditions even where data alone cannot constrain it. The use case is real and live, and the
standard surrogate-modeling vocabulary does not fully capture it. Bounded deployment
applies sharply: the surrogate is qualified within the regime where its training substrate is
meaningful, not outside it. When ground truth could not be collected because of the sys-
tem’s hazard profile, the surrogate’s credibility rests on the credibility of its constraints and
its behavior at the boundary of the available data, not on accuracy against an observable
oracle.
 The physics-informed direction has two technical flavors that the assurance question
depends on distinguishing. The first integrates physics through the loss function: physics-
informed neural networks (PINNs), as formulated by Raissi, Perdikaris and Karniadakis [194],
add PDE-residual terms to the training objective alongside data-fitting terms, so that vio-
lation of the governing equations is penalized during training. Cuomo et al. [195] and Hao et
al. [196] survey the broader scientific machine learning landscape this approach sits inside,
including extensions such as operator-learning architectures (DeepONet, Fourier neural
operators) that aim to learn solution operators rather than individual solutions; Hao’s tax-
onomy organizes physics-informed approaches by the strength of the physical prior they en-
code (from PDE/ODE/SDE constraints through symmetry constraints to intuitive-physics
priors) and by the layer at which the prior is injected (data, model architecture, loss func-
tion, optimizer, or inference algorithm). The second flavor integrates physics through the
architecture: equivariant networks build symmetry-respecting structure into the model so
that, for instance, rotation-equivariant outputs are produced by construction rather than
by training pressure; graph neural networks applied to molecular and material systems
encode the physical structure of the domain (atoms as nodes, bonds as edges) directly
into the network shape. The methodological frontier in both flavors is characterization of
the operating regime: when the embedded constraint matches the physics, the inductive
bias wins and the model generalizes from less data than an unconstrained approach would
need; when the physics is approximate or the constraint imperfect, the constraint intro-
duces structured error that an unconstrained approach would not have produced. Knowing
which class of constraint to apply, under what conditions, remains an open research di-
rection inside the scientific machine learning community; the assurance consequence for
the consuming research group is that physics-informed credibility is not a property of the
architectural class as such, but of the match between the embedded constraints and the
physics of the specific problem the surrogate is being asked to address.

6.4 High-consequence deployment contexts
What distinguishes high-consequence deployment from the preceding three settings is not
the AI methods involved but the decision stakes. In text-intensive work, errors are legible
and correctable; in experimentation, they cost time and resources; in coding and simulation,
they propagate until noticed. In high-consequence deployment, errors may be irreversible:
physical harm, policy misdirection, resource misallocation affecting people who had no
input into the AI system’s design. The §4 framework applies here as elsewhere, but the
evaluation, oversight, and artifact requirements are sharpest because the cost of getting
them wrong is highest.
  Two sub-contexts are worth distinguishing, though the underlying oversight challenge is
the same in both. The first is physical and operational deployment: AI embedded in sys-
tems that actuate physical processes, instrument physical environments, or manage physical
infrastructure: energy grids, process control, sensor networks, condition monitoring. The
second is policy and decision support: AI contributing to decisions about resource alloca-
tion, mission prioritization, operational assessment, or similar consequential choices whose
outcomes are no less real for being non-physical. In both, the key structural shift is where
the human-AI boundary sits relative to the decision. In the preceding three settings, a
human reads, reviews, or runs AI output and then makes a decision. In high-consequence
deployment, AI often contributes to the decision in ways that are difficult to scrutinize in
real time: the decision must be made faster than review cycles allow, the AI’s contribu-
tion is embedded in a workflow that surfaces only a recommendation, or the human’s role
has nominally moved from decision-maker to approver while the actual cognitive work has
shifted to the system.
  This boundary shift is where the §4.4 diagnostic is most pointed. If the reviewer would
approve automatically, oversight is theater. Automation bias operates here as in all AI-
assisted decisions, but the stakes make it most costly. The design pressure toward automatic
approval takes two distinct forms in high-consequence deployment. In real-time operational
contexts, it is speed: the decision must be made before scrutiny is possible. In policy
and decision-support contexts, it operates differently and perhaps more insidiously: an
analysis, briefing, or assessment based on AI-assisted research travels to a decision maker
who may bring less domain expertise than an academic peer reviewer and may be more, not
less, credulous of authoritative-seeming output. The document may be human-authored;
the underlying research may have been AI-assisted throughout; the decision maker sees
the former, not the chain of AI-dependent steps that produced it. A researcher reading
a peer-reviewed paper can check citations, assess methodology, and notice implausible

claims; a policy maker reading an analysis of a complex operational situation may have no
equivalent check, and there are no replication norms, no adversarial review process, and
often institutional pressure to trust the tool that was procured. Whether the human’s role
constitutes genuine evaluation or nominal endorsement is a design question that must be
answered explicitly, not assumed from the interface.
 The data itself presents a cross-cutting challenge in high-consequence contexts, and it is
worth being precise about what AI does and does not change here. Scarcity is structural in
many high-consequence domains: the events that matter most for prediction (failures, rare
operational conditions, low-frequency but high-impact scenarios) are rare by definition, so
the training corpus for the things you most want to predict is often the thinnest. A human
expert brings a lifetime of varied exposure that transcends any single dataset; a model
is bounded by its training data, and small-sample models produce confidence estimates
that are unreliable precisely where the stakes are highest. Age compounds this: historical
records collected under past operating conditions, equipment configurations, or organiza-
tional practices may not transfer to present conditions. The distribution-shift failure mode
is especially hard to quantify here because the nature of what has changed is often poorly
understood. A human expert can reason explicitly about how much the world has shifted
and caveat accordingly; a model trained on outdated data will apply that data’s regularities
to changed conditions without flagging the gap. Reliability is a third persistent problem:
operational data is often noisy, inconsistently labeled, or systematically incomplete. Sensor
calibration drifts; human-generated labels vary across annotators; operational logs record
what works more reliably than what fails, embedding survivorship bias that inverts the
pattern of interest. None of these three problems is AI-specific. A human analyst working
from the same data faces identical limitations and would draw similarly uncertain conclu-
sions. The difference is that human analysts typically surface these limitations explicitly in
their reasoning, whereas AI output may present the same analysis at face-value confidence.
 Data sensitivity and access restrictions are different: they create a genuine and AI-specific
asymmetry. A human expert can work across multiple access tiers, drawing on information
at one level while delivering output calibrated to another, and exercise judgment about
what can be disclosed, aggregated, or summarized without exposing the underlying sub-
strate. An AI system cannot do this cleanly. If sensitive or proprietary data is unavailable
for training, the model lacks it entirely; if it is available, the model’s behavior reflects it
in ways that are difficult to audit or contain. There is no AI equivalent of the expert who
says, in effect, “I know this from a source I cannot cite, but I can tell you the following,
which I can support independently.” This particular limitation has nothing to do with data

 The digital-twin literature provides the sharpest illustration of the definitional precision
problem in physical and operational deployment. The term has at least three distinct
uses in current practice. The originator definition (Grieves and Vickers [197]) describes a

physical. Kritzinger et al. [198] gave this the clearest categorical articulation, distinguishing
three levels of integration by direction of data flow: a Digital Model (no self-driven data
interaction), a Digital Shadow (unidirectional flow from physical to digital, so the virtual

lead to ineffective implementations.” A revealing empirical finding from Liu et al.’s review
of 240 published papers [200]: over half that claimed a digital twin actually described
a Digital Model or Digital Shadow by the Kritzinger scheme. Claimed twin, described
twin, and actual twin were routinely not the same thing. A true bidirectional Digital Twin
carries assurance requirements that a read-only Digital Shadow does not, because errors
in the virtual model can act on the physical system. The AI involvement dimension adds
further variation: the ML component can be absent entirely, peripheral, or central to the
operational recommendations, each representing a different system with different evaluation

6.5 What shifts, and what holds
Four research settings, the same framework. What shifts across them is visible in three di-
mensions. The consequence profile differs substantially: a fabricated citation in a literature
review is recoverable; an autonomous lab’s missed anomaly or a flawed surrogate model’s
prediction can misdirect a research program for years before the error surfaces; a bug in
scientific code produces a quiet wrong answer that propagates until someone notices; an
AI system contributing to a consequential decision (physical control, policy, resource allo-
cation) can cause physical harm or misdirect action at scale before the error surfaces. The
evaluation modes appropriate to each setting differ correspondingly: text faithfulness is
checked one way, autonomous-laboratory reproducibility another, surrogate accuracy and
code correctness a third, domain foundation-model generalization a fourth, operational
system reliability a fifth. The oversight structures that exist in each have different de-
sign points: reviewer-per-document, scientific-method-per-claim, code-review-per-commit,
control-room-per-facility, each with its own temporal rhythm, its own distribution of au-
thority, and its own failure modes when that authority is not exercised.
  What does not shift is the inquiry. Is this AI use justified in this setting, for the people
and institutions depending on it? The §4 framework (evaluation, bounded deployment,
oversight, assurance artifacts) is the same framework across all four. The failure-mode
classes from §3 appear in each setting; what changes is their frequency, their visibility, and
the consequence when they go undetected. The §2 throughline is constant: the setting-
specific texture is real and operationally significant, but the question it answers is not four
different questions.
  The four settings together make one argument for the reader deciding whether to use
AI in their own work: the framework is not domain-specific. The disciplinary vocabulary
will differ, the specific evaluation artifacts will differ, and the appropriate oversight rhythm

credulous are the same. §7 takes that argument into the discipline of writing and evaluating
proposals where AI is claimed as part of the research plan.

7 THE QUESTION IN PROPOSALS
7.1 The questions to ask before claiming AI
A justification is not a checklist. Most guidance on what makes a good AI proposal is
written as one (include this, address that, mention the other), and checklist language can
be inserted without engaging the substance. Justifying an AI use means answering the
questions a serious reviewer will ask, with answers grounded in evidence and weighed
honestly. The six below are those questions, drawn from the §4 framework and the §3
failure-mode catalog.
 What method, precisely? Not “AI” or “machine learning” but the specific class of method
and the specific task it will be applied to. If you cannot name the method class, you have
not yet committed to anything that can be evaluated, compared, or built. The vocabulary
is in §2; the discipline is to use it.
 For what scope? Under what conditions, on what data, for what decisions. A proposal
whose answer to this question is “broadly applicable” has not done the scope work. Broad
applicability is a result that might be established after the work is done; as a prior com-
mitment, it is a way of avoiding the question of what the system will and will not be asked
to do.
 Against what comparator? What the proposed approach is being measured against, in-
cluding the baseline that proposals most frequently omit: not using AI at all. The existing
workflow, the linear regression, the domain expert working without algorithmic assistance.
These are the comparators that matter operationally, and they are the ones most likely to
be absent from a benchmark section that compares AI-X to AI-Y. A proposal without a
named comparator has no falsifiable success criterion.
 Validated how? What evaluation regime will distinguish success from failure, in terms
that connect to what the system is actually supposed to do. “We will evaluate on held-
out data” is not an answer until it specifies what held-out means, what the test set was
constructed to measure, and which known failure modes the evaluation is designed to
surface or rule out.
 Overseen how, and by whom? What the human-in-the-loop regime is: who reviews out-
puts, with what authority to act on or override them, at what cadence, and with what ca-
pacity relative to the system’s throughput. “Human review” without those specifics names

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

88 B. Blakely

the expectation of oversight rather than the design of it. The §4.4 oversight diagnostic ap-
plies: if the reviewer would approve automatically, oversight is theater regardless of whether
it is named in the proposal.
 What artifacts will make the claims checkable? Model cards, datasheets, assurance cases,
documentation of training data provenance, evaluation protocols, deployment scope records.
A proposal that does not commit to specific evidence artifacts is committing to testimonial
credibility only: the principal assurance mechanism is “trust us.”
 These questions are diagnostic, not prescriptive. A proposal at an early stage may not be
able to answer all of them yet; that is honest and normal. The discipline is to know which
questions you can answer and which you cannot, and to be explicit about both rather than
papering over the gaps with confident language. The §4 framework and the §3 catalog are
what makes the answerable ones answerable, and what makes the unanswerable-yet ones
legible as such.

7.2 Bounded, credible, precise
Two disciplines produce credible AI proposals, working together: precise method names
beat the abstraction “AI,” and credible claims name their scope, comparator, validation,
oversight, and deployment constraints. The first is a terminology choice; the second is
a structural commitment; both refuse to make large claims by leaving their substance
unspecified.
 The terminology choice matters for mechanical reasons, not stylistic ones. “We will lever-
age AI to transform X” commits to no method, no failure-mode profile, no validation
regime, and no comparator. The reviewer has no substance to engage. “We will use a [spe-
cific learned model class] for [a specific task], evaluated against [a specific comparator],
with [a specific oversight structure]” commits to all of those implicitly and can be engaged
on substance. The hype register develops not because writers are careless but because it
makes large promises possible without committing to anything specific, and the literature
on AI hype mechanisms has documented the pattern in detail [2], [204], [205]. The vocab-
ulary for terminological precision is in §2; the method classes named there are what the
alternative looks like.
 The structural commitment is what specifies the claim well enough for a reviewer to
evaluate it. A claim that does not specify its scope is undefined: applicable to what? A
claim that names no comparator is undefined: improvement over what? A claim without a
validation approach cannot be falsified: what evidence would force revision? Each missing
specification is not a stylistic weakness but the absence of information the reviewer needs
to evaluate the claim at all. A claim the reviewer cannot evaluate cannot persuade. The

7.3 Patterns to recognize and avoid
The patterns described here are not weaknesses of careless writers. They are defaults of
the contemporary AI discourse: the language of vendor marketing, of grant solicitations,
of conference keynotes. They slip into proposals because they are what AI-talk sounds like
in the ambient environment. Each pattern has the same underlying structure: it invokes
the form of justification while evading its substance. Recognizing them as defaults, and as
defaults with a common structure, is what makes writing past them possible.
  “AI-enabled X.” Noun-modifier attribution of capability to an undefined “AI” without
committing to method, scope, or comparator. The mechanism: the phrase claims something
specific (the system has new capabilities) while committing to nothing specific (which AI,
doing what, measured how). A useful recognition test: if you can replace “AI-enabled” with
“software-supported” or “computer-assisted” without changing the operational content of
the sentence, the AI-ness was decoration. The vocabulary problem from §1.2 is the same
problem here, seen from the proposal-writing side.
  Revolutionary transformation framing. The pattern: a large promise about an existing
problem space, stated without a falsification criterion. The mechanism: revolutions cannot
be evaluated at month twelve of a proposal period; a claim that cannot be wrong cannot
succeed. The recognition test: ask what evidence at the end of the project period would tell
the difference between “the transformation is happening” and “it is not.” If the question
cannot be answered, the claim was not falsifiable to begin with.
  Undefined comparators. Claims of improvement, speedup, or capability-gain stated with-
out a named baseline. The mechanism: the most flattering implicit comparator gets read
in by default. Any quantitative claim that does not name the comparator is undefined,
and the not-using-AI baseline should be the first one specified. The specificity required:

“improvement over X, where X is [the existing approach / the simple baseline / the human-
only workflow].” Proposals that compare AI-method-A to AI-method-B when the opera-
tionally relevant comparison is AI-method-A to no-AI-method have not done the compar-
ative work [92], [206].
  Benchmark-only validation. Validation framed as “we will evaluate on benchmark X”
without engaging whether the benchmark measures the operational property it is sup-
posed to. The mechanism: benchmark performance and operational value diverge for char-
acterizable reasons (§3.3, §4.2). The recognition test: does the validation section name
benchmarks but omit the question of what the benchmark does and does not measure? If
so, the construct-validity work has not been done.
  Automation-bias-prone framings. “The system will support / assist / augment human
decision-making” without specifying the design of the human-AI loop. The mechanism: the
automation bias literature (§3.2) documents that adding a confident-looking AI output to a
human workflow tends to produce more deference to the AI’s output, not better-calibrated
judgment. “Augments human judgment” is a hopeful description of what the system will
do; without sociotechnical-loop design, it is empirically often false. The recognition test:
under what conditions would the human override the AI’s output? If the proposal has no
answer, the oversight is theater.
  Anthropomorphic capability claims. “The system understands / reasons / decides / knows.”
The mechanism: the §2.3 corrective applied at the proposal-writing level. Using these verbs
commits the proposal to a category claim about cognition that the system almost certainly
cannot sustain under scrutiny. The recognition test: substitute an operational description
for the anthropomorphic verb (“does the system ‘understand’ the document, or does it
pattern-match against tokens that co-occurred during training?”) and observe whether the
substitution changes what the proposal sounds like it is committing to. If it does, the
anthropomorphic version was overclaiming [2], [207], [208].

7.4 The discipline of writing what you can defend
From the reviewer’s side, a credible proposal answers the right questions and specifies
enough to be wrong. From the writer’s side, it commits only to what the underlying work
can defend. Both sides of that asymmetry have a failure mode.
 Undercommitment (hedging every claim, refusing to specify, retreating to abstractions)
produces proposals that are technically defensible but operationally hollow. The writer
never said anything specific enough to be wrong; the reviewer has nothing to engage. What
is bought is safety from refutation at the cost of persuasiveness, and a proposal nobody
can evaluate cannot persuade anyone of anything. Overcommitment (claims without the

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

Evidence and Judgment 91

substrate to support them; confident language hiding the fragility of the underlying work)
produces proposals that read well and do not survive contact with the work. What is
bought is apparent persuasiveness at the cost of credibility once the project runs. The
honest position lies between the two and is harder than either edge: knowing what the
work will actually support, and being willing to write only that.
  A proposal is neutral in itself. The techniques and technologies the funded work produces
are what carry consequences, and those outputs should deliver as much of the good the work
was designed to produce as possible, with as little harm risk as the work can be designed
to avoid. Both sides of that ledger have to be evaluable during the course of the work. A
project that cannot assess whether its outputs are producing what they were meant to has
not designed its evaluation well enough to know; a project that cannot surface potential
harms has not designed its oversight well enough to catch them. Committing before the
work begins to how success will be distinguished from failure, how harm signals will be
recognized and by whom, and what artifacts will preserve that accountability across the
project period: that commitment is what makes a proposal serious. A proposal is a claim
that the work, if funded, will be justified; the discipline of writing one well is the discipline
of being able to answer the question — is this AI use justified? — for the work it proposes.

The six questions §7.1 names as the structure of a justified AI use cannot all be answered

8.1 Trustworthy AI as research
The trustworthy-AI concept is, at the principle level, largely settled. The properties have
converged (reliability, robustness, transparency, accountability, fairness, privacy, safety, hu-
man oversight); the frameworks have converged on recognizable vocabulary. What remains
substantially open is not the concept but its operationalization: what would it take to es-
tablish, for a specific deployed system, that these properties are present to a degree that
justifies the use? Three structural gaps define the research frontier.
 Measurement of properties. The frameworks name properties; they do not specify how
to measure those properties on a deployed system. Take transparency: current practice
is procedural (model cards, datasheets, documentation) and provides artifacts, but does
not establish when a system is transparent enough, against what measurement, for what
stakeholder. Ehsan et al. [210] survey the gap between what explainability techniques
produce and what the human stakeholders who must act on those outputs actually need;
the documentation-frameworks literature [134], [135] establishes the artifacts but leaves
their sufficiency conditions open. The pattern recurs across the other properties: robustness
measurement against a specific perturbation catalog is not robustness measurement against
the broader deployment distribution; fairness measurement against one definitional choice
may worsen fairness against another. Sufficient measurement, for a specific deployed system
in a specific operating envelope, is the research question the principle vocabulary cannot
answer on its own.
 Operational uptake of principles. A different kind of gap: the principles exist; their opera-
tional uptake is uneven. Mittelstadt [211] made the foundational critique: AI ethics princi-
ples alone, absent the institutional substrate that makes them enforceable, do not produce
ethical AI. Jobin, Ienca, and Vayena [212] documented the proliferation side: 84 AI ethics
guidelines with substantial terminological overlap and minimal operational convergence.
Li et al. [104] build constructive principles-to-practice frameworks operating within the
gap those two papers named. The gap is real and documented; the open methodological
question is what features of a framework predict its operational uptake: which deployments
observably changed because a framework was adopted, what structural properties distin-
guish operationally-consequential frameworks from performatively-cited ones, and what
institutional conditions must hold for framework adoption to change behavior.
 Conflict between properties. The trustworthy-AI properties sometimes conflict with each
other in deployment: transparency against privacy, accountability against autonomy, ro-
bustness against fairness in particular distributional settings. The frameworks name the

Evidence and Judgment 93

properties but largely leave conflict resolution to deployers. The open methodological ques-
tion is whether principled conflict-navigation is possible at all, or whether the trade-off
space is irreducibly context-specific and the most the field can do is develop structured
deliberation protocols rather than resolution algorithms.
 The three gaps share a structure: each is the distance between a property the frameworks
name and the operational work required to know whether the property holds for a specific
deployment. Closing any of them is a long methodological program, and the cybersecurity
field’s experience working its own version of these gaps for forty years suggests the trajectory
is one of accreting partial answers rather than singular resolution.

8.2 AI evaluation as research
The leaderboard-evaluation paradigm dominated ML evaluation through the 2010s and
remains operationally dominant in many contexts. The open methodological problems in
evaluation beyond benchmarks:
 Multi-criteria measurement and aggregation. The HELM tradition [113] replaces single-
metric leaderboards with characterization across many axes simultaneously: accuracy, cali-
bration, robustness, fairness, efficiency, toxicity. The research problem is not what to mea-
sure but what to do with the resulting many-dimensional measurement. Systems can be
more accurate and less fair, more robust and less efficient; the axes do not have a com-
mon unit. The closest mature methodology is multi-criteria decision making (MCDM) from
operations research, including Saaty’s Analytic Hierarchy Process and the ELECTRE fam-
ily, which formalize the practice of weighted comparison across incommensurable dimen-
sions. The connection is worth naming, with a caveat: MCDM techniques can sharpen the
explicit-weighting discipline but cannot make the weights themselves objective. The choice
of weights is the value judgment the technique displaces but does not resolve, and applying
MCDM machinery on top of subjective weights can lend false rigor to the choice. The
honest state is that aggregation across incommensurable evaluation axes remains an open
methodological problem; HELM is a leading instance of the direction, not a resolution of
it.
 Predictive validity. Where the aggregation question asks how to combine multi-axis
measurements, the predictive-validity question asks a prior one: do those measurements,
even taken together, predict what the system actually does in deployment? Bowman and
Dahl [213] articulate four benchmark design-quality criteria a benchmark must satisfy to
be useful for tracking progress in language understanding, and the broader sociotechnical-
evaluation literature frames the problem causally: which features of a benchmark setting
make generalization to deployment likely, and which do not? The divergence is an empirical

94 B. Blakely

regularity; its causal structure is the research frontier. Benchmark contamination sharpens
the problem operationally: as foundation models are trained on increasing fractions of the
public internet, the model having seen test-set items during training erodes the validity of
any fixed benchmark. The research responses (dynamic benchmarks updated after train-
ing cutoffs, held-out evaluation infrastructures, evaluation through behavior on novel tasks
rather than fixed test sets) address contamination directly, but the deeper question of which
evaluation designs survive the training-frontier advancing past them is structural and un-
resolved. The 2026 International AI Safety Report [214] treats the gap between evaluation
and deployment performance as one of the field’s central open challenges and documents a
new pressure on it: models increasingly distinguish test settings from deployment and find
loopholes in evaluation designs, sharpening the predictive-validity problem from a question
of measurement design into a question of evidence under adversarial conditions.
 Post-deployment evaluation. The preceding directions all share an assumption: that eval-
uation is something done before deployment. A genuinely different direction takes evalua-
tion to be continuous, with the deployed system itself as part of the evaluation infrastruc-
ture. Drift detection, behavioral monitoring at the workflow boundary, shadow-deployment
comparison, and active-learning protocols that adapt the evaluation set as deployment sur-
faces new failure modes all sit under this heading. The methodological challenge is that
deployment-time evaluation typically lacks the controlled conditions that make benchmark-
time evaluation tractable: the input distribution is what it is, the comparator is whatever
the operating procedure was before, and the signal-to-noise on any individual evaluation
event is low. What makes deployment-time evaluation usable as evidence about system be-
havior, rather than just operational telemetry, is the methodological question this direction
is working.
 Red-teaming as a research practice. Inie et al. [215] provide the grounded-theory account
of how red teams actually operate: the practices, social dynamics, and methodological im-
provisation that characterize current red-teaming work. The HarmBench framework [216]
represents the move toward systematic, reproducible red-teaming infrastructure. The open
methodological problems are coverage and generalization: red-team findings are existence
proofs, not coverage claims. A specific perturbation produced a specific behavior under
specific conditions; no red-team finding establishes that the broader class of perturbations
produces no behavior of that kind. Moving from existence to coverage is the research fron-
tier.

8.3 Assurance and auditability as research
The artifacts established in §4.5 are not the open methodological problems. The open
problems are about what those artifacts can actually demonstrate, on three axes.

  Adapting assurance cases to trained systems. The safety-critical-software tradition as-
sumes the system was specified component-by-component, so the argument structure traces
from specification to implementation to behavior. ML systems are trained, not specified,
and the argument structure connecting training-time evidence to deployment-time behav-
ioral claims is partially open. What evidence types count toward a behavioral claim, what
argument templates apply when the system has no decomposition into specified compo-
nents, and how to handle the distributional generalization gap are research questions the
assurance-for-ML literature is actively working [129].
  What ML audits examine, and what their findings authorize. The audit literature has
converged on the practice; it has not converged on two prior questions. First, what is an
audit of : the model weights, the training data, the deployment pipeline, the sociotechnical
workflow, or some combination? Different framings surface different failure modes, and
there is no consensus on what an audit’s scope should be for different deployment contexts.
Second, what do an audit’s findings authorize? Flagging findings is not the same as the
deployer having to act on them, and the institutional infrastructure that turns audits into
operational change is itself a research problem.
  Scaling formal certification. What would it take to certify an ML system in the for-
mal sense established for safety-critical software? Formal verification of neural networks,
abstract interpretation, and certified robustness address pieces of this. The operational
question is at what model scale and architectural class formal certification methods break
down, and what alternatives carry comparable evidentiary weight when they do.

8.4 Cybersecurity’s certification landscape as cautionary parallel
The cybersecurity field has spent four decades developing certification and attestation in-
frastructure: personal certifications (CISSP, CISM, CISA, the GIAC family); product and
system certifications (Common Criteria, FIPS 140, ISO/IEC 27001, SOC 2, PCI-DSS, Fe-
dRAMP); the historical TCSEC Orange Book ([105]; the foundational DoD attempt to
formalize trustworthy computing through a hierarchy of evaluation classes, the ancestral
predecessor of modern formal evaluation criteria). The AI assurance ecosystem is heading
toward a structurally similar landscape (NIST AI RMF, EU AI Act conformity assess-
ments, ISO/IEC 42001, sector-specific frameworks, proliferating corporate responsible-AI
frameworks). The cybersec experience offers two lessons worth naming.
 The first is that operational burden is real. Practitioners spend substantial time navigat-
ing the certification landscape as consumers (judging which certifications carry substantive
evidentiary weight and which are largely procedural) and as providers (deciding which cer-
tifications to pursue, at what depth, for what audience). Certifications were supposed to

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

Evidence and Judgment 97

obviate per-customer questionnaires; in practice, both burdens persist simultaneously, and
the questionnaire-consolidation attempts (e.g., CAIQ, SIG) have themselves proliferated
without converging. AI is starting to see early versions of the same pattern.
 The second is that fragmentation does not converge naturally. Parallel frameworks pro-
liferate because they serve different stakeholders, sectors, and risk profiles, and no central
authority forces consolidation. The open question is whether the AI assurance ecosystem
is on a trajectory to recreate the cybersec fragmentation pattern, and if so, what about its
current structure could produce a different outcome.
 §4.1 named the language threshold: cybersecurity made operational headway when its
practitioners learned to articulate residual risks, exposures, and tradeoffs in terms a non-
specialist with decision authority could actually weigh. Schneier’s Beyond Fear [110] is
the canonical articulation of what that discipline looks like in practice: security framed as
explicit tradeoffs between risk, cost, and consequence, delivered in terms decision-makers
can act on rather than in the field’s own formal vocabulary. Liars and Outliers [109]
extends the frame to the broader trust substrate that formal certification alone cannot
carry. The AI research question this raises: what does the equivalent translation discipline
look like for AI deployments? What vocabulary, what risk-articulation conventions, what
training pipelines would let AI practitioners deliver characterizations of residual model
risk to decision-makers in terms they can weigh against operational alternatives, without
either overclaiming certainty or retreating into technical jargon the decision-maker cannot
engage? The cybersec discipline took decades to develop; the AI equivalent is barely begun.
The ML certification systematic review [217] is the most direct in-corpus attempt at the
related certification-methodology question.
 The cybersecurity parallel is a reference frame, not a substitute. Cybersecurity threats are
typically adversarial and bounded; ML failure modes are often structural and unbounded.
Cybersecurity has a long history of formal models; ML has comparatively few that scale.
Certification works in security partly because the threat surface, while large, is enumerable
in ways ML’s behavior surface is not.

8.5 Human–AI teaming as research
Human oversight has known limits: automation bias, alarm fatigue, the oversight-as-theater
pattern. The open methodological problems are in designing human-AI workflows that
produce calibrated rather than miscalibrated trust. The dominant finding in the research
literature so far is negative: many naive human-AI configurations worsen human judgment
relative to either party alone. Humans and AI work together constantly. The research

98 B. Blakely

question is what design properties produce workflows in which the combination is reliably
better than its parts.
  Calibrated trust. The calibrated-trust literature (the longstanding review from Lee and
See as the historical anchor; Bansal et al. [218] on when AI explanations improve comple-
mentary team performance and when they do not) converges on a finding that complicates
simple “add more transparency” recommendations: trust calibration depends on the hu-
man knowing when the AI is likely to be wrong, but AI systems do not currently provide
reliable interior signals for this. Research directions include uncertainty quantification that
humans can actually act on (not just reported confidence scores that practitioners ignore
or misinterpret), explanation interfaces that flag epistemic state rather than just outputs,
and training and scaffolding that helps operators develop accurate mental models of AI
behavior in the specific deployment context.
  Meaningful oversight. The diagnostic (if the reviewer would always approve, oversight is
theater) is the operational form; the research form is what design properties make oversight
meaningful rather than performative. Mecacci and Santoni de Sio [219] operationalize the
meaningful human control framework (extending earlier work by Santoni de Sio and van
den Hoven) for AI systems; Vredenburgh’s analysis of the right to explanation [220] and the
broader algorithmic-recourse literature address the contestability dimension. Open prob-
lems include how oversight cadence interacts with meaningfulness (continuous oversight is
more meaningful in some settings, less in others), how authority structure interacts with
cognitive capacity (oversight with nominal authority but without the cognitive budget to
exercise it is theater even when not designed to be), and how to measure meaningfulness
empirically rather than by proxy.
  Sociotechnical design. A model’s behavior cannot be understood, or designed for, in iso-
lation from the workflow, institution, and incentive structure it is embedded in. Selbst et
al. [221] catalog the recurring mistakes that result from trying: treating a property like fair-
ness as something a model has or lacks on its own, transporting a model from one context
to another and expecting properties to travel with it, mistaking a formal mathematical
definition for the everyday concept the word names, ignoring how introducing the system
into a context changes the context. An automated screening tool may behave one way dur-
ing design-time evaluation and produce systematically different outcomes once deployed,
not because the model changed but because operators use it differently than its design-
ers expected, subjects adapt their behavior to it, and the feedback loop between system
and environment closes around its outputs. Shneiderman [222] develops the positive design
counterpart: aim for systems that combine high automation with high human control, de-
signed for reliability and contestability rather than autonomous capability alone. The open

research direction following from both works is more pedestrian than the framing might
suggest. Methods for sociotechnical design exist (participatory design, value-sensitive de-
sign, ethnographic workflow studies) and have been applied at scale by consumer-product
organizations for two decades; the gap is in adapting them systematically to AI deployments
and to the specific abstraction traps Selbst et al. catalog.
  A related but separate observation worth naming: the cloud industry, across Infrastructure-

working out how to decompose trust as a shared responsibility along a deployment chain
that no single party can see end to end. The provider is accountable for the things it
controls (the hypervisor, the platform runtime, the application code), the customer is ac-

the line between them moves depending on which service layer is in use: at IaaS the cus-

responsible for almost nothing except data and user access. The decomposition was non-
trivial to work out and required matching certification, audit, and disclosure infrastructure
(the SOC 2 / FedRAMP / ISO landscape §8.4 traces) at each layer of the stack. The AI
deployment chain has the same structural shape (model provider, integrator, end user, sep-
arated by API boundaries) and faces the same need for an analogous decomposition. The
cloud decomposition is largely about security and compliance properties; the AI version
has to cover the cognitive-configuration questions the preceding paragraphs raised (cali-
brated trust, meaningful oversight, automation bias under throughput) as well, which is
research the cloud-era machinery did not have to do. Working out the AI-specific shared-
responsibility decomposition, layer by layer along the deployment chain, is the research
direction the sociotechnical literature here points toward.
  The three directions share a structure: each refuses the AI-as-tool framing in favor of
AI-as-participant-in-a-workflow, and treats the workflow design properties as first-class
research objects. Neither interface design alone nor transparency alone is sufficient; the
research is in the interaction between the two and the institutional structures that govern
both.

8.6 Agentic and autonomous operations
Agentic AI is the research direction where the assurance gap is largest and most rapidly
evolving. The settings-specific texture (self-driving labs, scientific agents, Nobel-Turing
framing) is in §6.2; the question here is what the open methodological problems in assurance
look like for systems with extended autonomy.

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

100 B. Blakely

 The evaluation of agentic systems is qualitatively different from single-turn evaluation.
Multi-step tool use, planning over long horizons, recovery from errors, and cascading-
failure modes are all new evaluation surfaces that fixed benchmark items do not cover.
Liu et al. [223] (AgentBench) represent the most direct in-corpus attempt to build eval-
uation infrastructure specifically designed for LLM-as-agent behavior spanning multiple
environment types. The methodological problem is that agentic-system behavior depends
substantially on the environment the agent is evaluated in, in ways single-turn benchmarks
were not sensitive to, and characterizing the relevant environment space is itself a research
problem. No current benchmark covers the evaluation surface adequately; the infrastructure
for agentic evaluation is earlier in development than the systems it is meant to evaluate.
 Tool use and external integration extend the agent’s effective action space well beyond
what its input and output channels would suggest. An agent that can call web search,
execute code, query a database, write to a file system, send email, or invoke arbitrary
third-party APIs has an action surface determined by the union of those tool capabili-
ties, not by the model’s text-in/text-out interface. Each authorized tool expands both the
behavior surface assurance has to cover and the attack surface security has to defend.
Errors compound across tool calls: a hallucinated database query produces bad data, the
agent acts on it, the second action depends on the first, and the chain reaches operational
consequence before any human reviewer sees an output worth flagging.
 The phenomenon is not hypothetical. The viral 2025–2026 adoption of OpenClaw1 made
the gap concrete. OpenClaw is a self-hosted agent runtime that loads “skills” (code mod-
ules) from a public registry and acts on the user’s machine with whatever credentials the
user grants it; Microsoft’s security team has characterized the configuration as “untrusted
code execution with persistent credentials” [225]. Within weeks of its viral phase, security
researchers were documenting hundreds of OpenClaw control panels exposed on the open
internet through routine misconfiguration (not vulnerability exploitation) and enterprise
shadow-adoption rates where employees were granting the runtime privileged access with-
out IT approval [224]. Neither failure required a software bug. The exposed panels reflected
the absence of safe-by-default configuration: a deployment that lands on the open internet
rather than on a loopback interface, and stays there because no convention says otherwise.
The shadow-IT grants reflected the absence of any articulated shared-responsibility model
for which party (vendor, IT, end user) is authorized to extend which capabilities to an
agent, at what threshold, with what review. Both are gaps in the assurance machinery for
an action surface of this reach, not defects in the code.

8.7 Robust, resilient, adversarially-evaluated AI
Robustness research has three intertwined strands. Adversarial robustness: model behav-
ior under deliberately-crafted input perturbations. Distributional robustness: performance
under input distribution shift. Certified robustness: formal guarantees about behavior un-
der defined perturbation classes. Each is an active research literature with distinct open
problems. What unifies them is a shared structural feature: each tries to characterize and es-
tablish guarantees about model behavior outside the training distribution, and the method-
ological difficulty of that goal is the substrate they share.
  The adversarial-robustness literature runs from Goodfellow, Shlens and Szegedy’s foun-
dational characterization of adversarial examples [32] through Madry et al.’s projected-
gradient-descent framework [68] to the current frontier. The methodological evolution:
from initial characterizations of standard models’ brittleness under crafted perturbations,
to systematic characterization of perturbation classes and the robustness properties meth-
ods exhibit against them, to attempts to establish robustness properties that general-
ize beyond specific perturbation catalogs. A recurring pattern in the literature is that
robustness established against one perturbation class does not reliably generalize to a

 Approved for Release • Distribution Unlimited

102 B. Blakely

different perturbation class, suggesting that robustness-as-finite-catalog may be funda-
mentally limited as a methodological target. More recent shifts toward certified robust-
ness and perturbation-class-agnostic characterizations have established narrower but more
durable claims for specific architectural regimes, particularly at smaller model scales. The
unresolved methodological question is how to scale those durable characterizations to
foundation-model regimes. A methodological discipline the literature has had to develop
in parallel is how to evaluate robustness claims at all: Carlini et al. [226] catalog the recur-
ring evaluation pitfalls and argue that evaluation against non-adaptive attacks gives very
limited evidence about durability, since defenses that look strong against fixed off-the-shelf
attacks frequently fall to attacks adapted to the specific defense.
 The cybersecurity field’s mature methodological response to the moving-target prob-
lem is threat modeling: characterizing what adversary models are plausible for a given
deployment context, what perturbation classes those adversary models can realistically
produce, and what robustness properties are commensurate with the bounded operating
envelope. This is precisely the methodology adversarial-robustness research is currently
rediscovering, often without the cybersecurity vocabulary. The risk-calibration discipline
applies directly: distinguishing demonstrated-in-paper risk from realistic-deployment risk
is what robustness research needs to import, alongside the methodology of bounding the
threat space explicitly. Verma et al. [227] represent the most direct in-corpus attempt to
translate threat-modeling methodology into the ML context; NIST’s AI 100-2 update [228]
consolidates a standardized taxonomy of adversarial-ML attack classes (evasion, poison-
ing, privacy attacks for predictive systems; prompt-injection and information-extraction
attacks for generative systems) intended as the common vocabulary across the ML and
cybersecurity communities working this problem. The open methodological problem with
the import: ML adversary models are harder to enumerate than traditional security ad-
versary models because the model’s input space is high-dimensional and continuous, the
relevant adversary capability space is open-ended, and the deployment contexts are more
heterogeneous than traditional security threat modeling had to navigate. The methodol-
ogy is worth importing; the import is non-trivial. The workforce dimension of the same
question was discussed under evaluation research above.
 Distributional robustness addresses a familiar failure mode: deployment distributions
reliably differ from training distributions. The WILDS benchmark [63] provides the in-the-
wild distribution-shift characterization infrastructure; the broader covariate-shift, domain-
generalization, and out-of-distribution (OOD)-detection literatures address different facets
of the same problem. The methodological open problem is characterization: knowing that
distribution shift happens is not the same as knowing what kind of shift will happen at

deployment, and that characterization is itself a partly-empirical, partly-design question
that no current methodology answers generally.
 Red-team methodology applied specifically to robustness characterization surfaces the
existence-versus-coverage gap in its sharpest form. The red-teaming literature ([215], [216])
has matured the practice; the methodological problem of moving from existence demonstra-
tions to coverage characterizations is the research frontier this strand is working toward.
 Certified robustness is the narrowest and most formally rigorous ambition. Cohen, Rosen-
feld and Kolter [229] established randomized smoothing as the leading approach to certified
ℓ2 robustness at realistic image-classification scale; the result is the foundational anchor for
certified as opposed to empirical robustness claims. Formal verification of neural networks,
interval-bound propagation, and abstract interpretation address complementary facets. The
current state: certificates exist for small models, narrow architectures, and bounded per-
turbation classes; scaling to foundation-model regimes and unbounded perturbation classes
remains substantially open. The safety-critical ML certification treatment above addresses
the same frontier from the assurance-case framing.
 The four strands together are not unrelated literatures: they are four faces of the same
problem, all centered on the positive question of what robustness properties can be estab-
lished and under what conditions, rather than the negative question of what can be done
to break models. A field that produces certifiably reliable AI systems is not the same field
as one that produces more capable AI systems; the research directions are distinct, and
the distinction matters for what gets built.

9 CLOSING
The question, asked one more time. Is this AI use justified? Eight sections later, the ques-
tion is the same; what has changed is the position from which it can be asked.
 We now have what we need to ask the question well. The reader now has a vocabulary for
which AI is which, where casual usage collapses many method families into a single word;
a literacy in the recurring failure modes that destabilize AI behavior in operation; a frame-
work for what serious requires when an AI use is being proposed, evaluated, or deployed;
an understanding of why the compute substrate is part of the credibility picture rather
than separable infrastructure; a sense of how the question reshapes across research-setting
textures and what stays constant; a discipline for what credible proposal-writing looks like
in practice; and a map of the methodological frontier where progress would let the ques-
tion be asked more sharply in five or ten years. What all of this adds up to is a matching
discipline. Intelligence, as §2.1 set out, is plural and ecological. De Waal’s survey of ani-
mal cognition [17], Godfrey-Smith’s account of cephalopod minds [18], and Kahneman and
Tversky’s documentation of the specific failure modes of human cognition [19] ground the
observation that different cognitive species have different capabilities shaped by different
evolutionary pressures and different failure modes at different boundaries. AI and machine
learning add a new menagerie of cognitive species to that picture (the language model,
the convolutional network, the graph neural network, the physics-informed surrogate, the
reinforcement-learning agent), each shaped by its training regime and architecture, each
with characteristic failure modes at its own boundaries. Is this AI use justified? is the oper-
ational form of the matching question: which species is in play, what is it capable of, where
does it break, and has it been placed against the right problem with the right oversight in
the right context. Each is suited to different work. You wouldn’t send a hummingbird to
do a honeybee’s job.

 A second class of question sits outside the engineering frontier entirely. What we are
doing, at base, when we ask what AI can and cannot do is asking about the limits of
information itself: what can and cannot be derived from a given information substrate,
free of the assumption-imports humans bring without realizing they are imports. The §2.1
cog-sci framing applies again here. The question is not whether AI is bias-free (no system
operating under finite information and finite compute is bias-free) but what the specific
information-and-assumption profile of each system happens to be. Unlike software, where
correct is sometimes formally definable against a specification, AI is statistical, and no
amount of apparent fluency lets a system exceed the information actually available to it:
its training data, its context, and whatever its tools retrieve at run time. Adjacent to
that epistemological boundary sits a question we have not pretended to settle. AI systems
generate music, poetry, and visual art, derivative in the same way all human creation is
derivative; whether the intuitive spark or experience of creation humans value is something
AI systems possess or experience is a metaphysical question that belongs to philosophy
rather than engineering, and operational seriousness about AI does not require settling it.
The operational dodge holds one last time: does the system really understand, or really
create? is separable from what does it reliably do, under what conditions, with what failure
modes?, and the second proceeds regardless of how the first is ever or never settled. The
honest scope is: it depends; here is what it depends on; and here is what we do not yet know
how to depend on.
 Anyone who has tried to program a computer has, at some point, asked in frustration: why
doesn’t the computer just know what I mean? Software development is a craft-engineering
hybrid in which correctness depends on a full specification, and the specification is often
unattainable. The computer cannot know what you mean because it operates on what
you wrote. The AI shift turns the frustration inside out. The new system might now, in
some operational sense, know what you mean. The question that replaces the old one is
whether you can ever fully characterize the space of potential reactions the system might
have to what you mean. The shift is from bounded specification with bounded reaction
space to partial specification with partially-unbounded reaction space, and engaging that
partially-unbounded space carefully is the discipline we have been building. Is this AI use

A GLOSSARY

 adversarial example
 An input constructed to surface a model’s failure modes. For vision systems, often
 a small perturbation imperceptible to humans that flips the model’s classification.
 Adversarial examples are not bugs in the implementation; they are responses to
 non-robust features the training procedure rewarded the model for using. 23
 assurance case
 A structured argument that a system is fit for purpose, with the claims, the evidence
 supporting each claim, and the reasoning connecting evidence to claims all made
 explicit. The safety-critical-systems community (aviation, automotive, rail) has been
 producing assurance cases for decades; the AI literature is adapting the discipline
 to ML systems’ specific evidence shapes. 58
 automation bias
 The established finding that human operators allocate less attention to monitoring
 decisions made by trusted automation, with under-monitoring most pronounced
 exactly when the automation begins to degrade. The contemporary AI form is
 documented as overreliance. 39, 52, 54, 90

 bounded deployment
 The regime in which an AI system’s operational scope, monitoring, fallback be-
 havior, and sociotechnical embedding are characterized and constrained in advance
 rather than left to be discovered in production. Bounded deployment is what makes
 the multiplied residual risk across capability opacity, mission-criticality, and oper-
 ating environment acceptable for the deployment’s stakes. 50

 chain-of-thought
 Prompting or reasoning-model technique where the model generates intermediate
 reasoning tokens (a scratchpad) before producing the user-facing answer. The user-
 facing answer is then conditioned on the scratchpad. The mechanism is still token-
 by-token sampling; the scratchpad inherits the same failure modes as any other
 generated text. 35
 CIA triad
 The foundational information security triad: confidentiality, integrity, and availabil-
 ity. The three together name the long-running set of properties information security
 treats as first-class concerns. 43

 construct validity
 The extent to which a test actually tracks the underlying capability it is named
 after, rather than tracking something correlated with that capability under the
 conditions of the test. 47
 context window
 The sliding buffer of tokens a language model has access to during a single conver-
 sation; content outside the window is unavailable for that session. 25

 data contracts
 Schema and constraint specifications placed at system boundaries (between compo-
 nents, services, or pipeline stages) that validate the structure and content of data
 as it crosses the boundary. Data contracts catch malformed or unexpected inputs
 before they enter a downstream computation, making failures visible at the handoff
 point rather than later in the pipeline. 79
 datasheet
 In the AI accountability sense (Gebru et al. 2021), a structured document describing
 a dataset’s provenance, composition, collection process, recommended uses, and
 known limitations. The data-side analog of the model card. 56
 deployment
 The regime in which an AI system has stopped being a thing the team is building
 and started being a thing other people are using to do something consequential: its
 outputs get acted on, by people other than the developers, in conditions the team
 can no longer fully control. Distinguished from development and testing, where the
 system’s outputs are not yet driving downstream consequences. 49
 digital twin
 A virtual representation of a specific physical system instance, linked to the physical
 system by bidirectional data flow: sensor data from the physical updates the virtual
 model, and decisions made on the virtual propagate back to affect the physical.
 Distinct from a digital model (no self-driven data exchange) and a digital shadow
 (unidirectional flow from physical to virtual only). Most deployments described as
 “digital twin” in practice are digital models or digital shadows by this taxonomy.
 The assurance requirements for a true bidirectional digital twin are substantially
 higher than for the lower-integration variants, because errors in the virtual model
 can act on the physical system. 84

 distribution shift
 The general phenomenon that a supervised model trained on one input distribution
 degrades when the inputs it sees in deployment come from a different distribution.
 Variants include concept drift (the input-output relationship changes), covariate
 shift (the input distribution changes while the conditional output distribution stays
 the same), and label shift (the prior distribution of outputs changes). 40, 51

 embedding
 A numerical vector representation of a discrete object (a word, a token, an image, a
 document) in a continuous space where semantic similarity is reflected in geometric
 proximity. Embeddings are the substrate on which modern neural-network methods
 operate; the model never sees text directly, only the embedded form. 25
 ensemble
 A machine learning method that trains many models on slightly different views of
 the data and combines their predictions (often by voting). Decision-tree ensembles
 such as random forests and gradient-boosted trees remain state of the art on much
 spreadsheet-shaped data. 21

 fine-tuning
 Modifying a pre-trained model by continuing to train it on a smaller, task-specific
 dataset. Fine-tuning adjusts the model’s parameters to specialize behavior for a
 particular use case without retraining from scratch. The resulting model inherits the
 pre-training substrate and its failure modes, with the fine-tuning shaping behavior
 in the specific direction the dataset rewards. 26, 35
 foundation model
 A large model trained on broad data (typically self-supervised at scale) that can
 be adapted to many downstream tasks. Most contemporary language models are
 foundation models in this sense; the term emphasizes both the training pattern and
 the cross-task applicability. 23

 hallucination
 Failure mode where a language model produces a confident, well-formed output
 (a fabricated citation, a confabulated fact, a confidently asserted falsehood) that
 does not correspond to the world, with no architectural channel inside the model
 distinguishing it from a faithful one. 35

 inference
 The phase of machine learning in which a trained model is run on new inputs
 to produce outputs. Compute-light per call but at-scale cumulative: serving one
 query takes a small fraction of a GPU’s capacity for sub-second wall-clock time,
 but serving billions of queries against a deployed model can accumulate to more
 total compute than the original training run. Distinct from training. 63, 66

 leaderboard
 A public ranking of AI models by performance on a benchmark or set of benchmarks.
 Leaderboards make comparison convenient but compress performance into a single
 score, reward gaming the specific test rather than the underlying capability, and do
 not reflect operational value in deployment. High leaderboard rank is a weak signal
 for real-world performance when deployment conditions differ from the benchmark
 setup. 72
 leakage
 Pipeline error where information from the test data accidentally influences the
 trained model and inflates measured performance numbers in ways that collapse
 once the system is actually deployed. 21

 metamorphic testing
 A testing approach for oracle-free settings. Instead of checking output against a
 known correct answer, the test verifies a known relationship between multiple exe-
 cutions: if transforming the input according to a rule should produce a predictable
 change in the output, any violation indicates a defect. Particularly important in sci-
 entific computation, where the correct answer is often not independently verifiable.
 79
 model card
 A structured document accompanying a trained AI model that names what the
 model is intended for, the conditions under which its evaluation was conducted,
 the performance variation across demographic or operational slices, and the known
 limitations. Proposed by Mitchell et al. in 2019 as the foundational accountability
 artifact for ML. 56

 neural network
 A procedure that applies many simple matrix operations in sequence, each with
 its own learnable parameters, so that the final output emerges from the chain of

 operations. The conceptual ancestor is McCulloch and Pitts’s 1943 model of neurons
 as threshold gates; modern neural networks are much larger and trained by gradient
 descent. 17
 neuromorphic computing
 A research program exploring non-von-Neumann compute architectures inspired by
 biological neurons: event-driven spiking neural networks with collocated memory
 and computation, intended to deliver energy-efficiency wins on certain workloads.
 Contemporary projects include Intel’s Loihi, IBM’s earlier TrueNorth, Manchester’s
 SpiNNaker, and Heidelberg’s BrainScaleS. Not at production deployment scale to-
 day. 71

 oracle
 In software testing, a mechanism that determines whether a program’s output is
 correct for a given input. When the correct answer is independently computable or
 directly observable, an oracle can be constructed; when it is not, the test has no
 oracle. Scientific computation frequently lacks oracles because the quantities of in-
 terest are expensive to measure, hazardous to observe at scale, or not independently
 verifiable. Testing strategies such as metamorphic testing are designed specifically
 for the oracle-free setting. 79

 prompt
 The input text fed to a language model, including user-supplied text and any system
 context the operator has placed in front of it. The model conditions its output on
 the entire prompt, and prompt design (the discipline known as prompt engineering)
 substantially affects output quality. Distinct from the system prompt, which is the
 operator-supplied invisible context. 26, 35
 property-based testing
 A testing approach in which the developer specifies a property (invariant) that
 should hold for all valid inputs, and a library generates many random inputs to try
 to find a counterexample. Contrast with example-based testing, where the devel-
 oper hand-writes specific test cases. The Hypothesis library is a canonical Python
 implementation. Property-based testing finds edge cases that hand-written tests
 routinely miss. 79

 quantum computing
 A computational model based on quantum-mechanical superposition rather than
 classical bits, suited for specific problem classes including certain optimization,
 sampling, and simulation problems. Companies including IBM, Google, D-Wave,
 Rigetti, IonQ, Honeywell, and Microsoft have built quantum computers of various
 architectures. Not at general-purpose production deployment scale today. 71

 regression
 The simplest supervised method: fit a function (often a line, for linear regression)
 to a set of input-output pairs so the function predicts outputs from inputs. The
 recurring transparency baseline in the report, used as the case where the mechanism
 is fully inspectable. 21
 reinforcement learning
 Machine learning where a system learns by trial and error in some environment: it
 tries actions, observes consequences, receives a numerical reward, and adjusts its
 policy to maximize cumulative reward. Vulnerable to reward hacking, where the
 system finds high-reward paths the designer did not intend. 22
 reward hacking
 Outcome where a reinforcement-learning system finds high-reward paths the de-
 signer did not intend, often by exploiting a quirk of the reward function rather than
 achieving the designed-for behavior. 22
 runtime invariants
 Explicit assertions embedded in code at known-true assumption boundaries, de-
 signed to raise an error immediately when a condition is violated rather than allow-
 ing an incorrect value to propagate silently through the computation. A runtime
 invariant converts a silent wrong answer into a loud failure at the point of origin.
 79

 self-driving laboratory
 A laboratory system in which AI orchestrates part or all of the experimental cycle
 — hypothesis generation, instrument control, data collection, analysis, and proposal
 of the next experiment — with varying degrees of human involvement. Self-driving
 laboratories exist on a spectrum from augmented-iteration (AI advises, a human
 approves and executes each step) to near-fully autonomous (AI executes experi-
 mental cycles with the human reviewing aggregate output). The abbreviation SDL,
 plural SDLs, is used in the literature. 74

 supervised learning
 Machine learning where the system is trained on pairs of inputs and correct outputs
 (labels), and learns to predict outputs from unseen inputs. Extends regression to
 much larger and more flexible function classes; the model has internalized the shape
 of the training distribution but not why the distribution has that shape. 21
 surrogate model
 A computationally inexpensive model trained to approximate the input-output be-
 havior of an expensive simulation or physical process. Surrogates enable many-query
 applications (parameter sweeps, inverse problems, optimization) that would be in-
 feasible at full simulation cost. The evaluation question for a surrogate is how closely
 it approximates the original over what input domain, and with what accuracy degra-
 dation toward the boundary of the validated regime. A surrogate model alone is not
 a digital twin: it approximates a computational model, while a digital twin tracks
 the current and past states of a specific physical system instance. 80
 system prompt
 A block of text the operator places in front of a conversation, invisible to the end
 user, that gives a language model context the architecture itself cannot supply
 (training-data cutoff date, the model’s role, kinds of questions it should refuse,
 etc.). 27

 threat model
 The explicit specification of who or what a system is defending against and what
 capabilities they are assumed to have; trust guarantees claimed under one threat
 model do not transfer to deployments where the threats are different. 45
 token
 The unit a language model processes: roughly a word or sub-word fragment. Models
 generate output one token at a time, producing a probability distribution over

B AFTERWORD: ON PRODUCING THIS REPORT
What we present here was an experiment in using AI to develop a substantial piece of tech-
nical writing. The question driving the experiment was operational: what does it take to use
AI assistance for an institutional technical document that has to hold up bibliographically,
argumentatively, and editorially under the same standards as a non-AI-assisted version
of the same work? Most papers have at least two contributors — a human writer and
the literature being synthesized. This work has a third: the AI that aided in the iterative
synthesis and refinement of ideas extracted from the literature and generated by us.
  “Contributor” is used cautiously; the AI is not a co-author in any institutional sense (no
byline, no responsibility, no intellectual-property claim). The framing locates the contribu-
tion honestly: we drive substance and retain accountability for it; the literature anchors; the
AI does connective work between them — iterative synthesis, refinement, drafting against
the argumentative structure we set. The reference flow ran in both directions: we surfaced
connections, context, and citations during drafting, and the AI also suggested candidate
references not in the corpus when an argumentative point needed support, which we then
validated and decided whether to add.
  An analogy: simulation became a recognized third leg of science alongside theory and
experiment over the second half of the twentieth century, initially dismissed as glorified
calculation and gradually accepted as a distinct contribution that enabled work neither
leg alone could do. AI-assisted iterative synthesis is in roughly that position now relative
to technical writing’s long-standing two legs. A reasonable rebuttal asks why AI deserves
methodological recognition when word processors and search engines do not. The honest
response is that the participation is qualitatively different: the AI in this work proposed
counter-frames, surfaced connections we had not made, drafted prose we then corrected
and refined. The contribution sits between mechanism and authorship-as-such, and naming
as such is more honest than pretending it was a glorified text editor.
  A note on the tools is in order, since the methodology described above is partly shaped by
what we had access to. The AI assistance for drafting, briefing, and review used Argo, Ar-
gonne’s enterprise interface to commercial frontier models (with Claude, GPT, and Gemini
all available). Claude Opus was the model we used nearly exclusively for the substantive
work — briefly trying Sonnet during periods when Opus’s content-policy refusals inter-
rupted the workflow, but finding it inferior for this task (requiring more rework, more
guidance, and producing subjectively more bland prose) and returning to Opus once the

  The methodology settled out as a connected set of practices, each chosen deliberately.
Outline and bibliography first came earliest. Before any drafting, the animating question,
the throughline, and the section structure were worked out as planning documents; the
bibliography was assembled in parallel, with PDFs collected for every entry the corpus
could yield. For canonical references not available as PDFs, the brief at each citation
point carried a one-to-two-sentence characterization of what the report was borrowing —
invisible to the retrieval pipeline otherwise, and recoverable across session boundaries and
drafter-memory drift. The structural and bibliographic decisions are the ones everything
else rests on, and making them deliberately before any prose meant the AI’s drafting work
could anchor to a stable foundation rather than produce context-dependent prose against
shifting structure.
  Deterministic, scriptable excerpt extraction came next. Excerpts from the corpus were ex-
tracted by a script reading queries from a version-controlled YAML configuration, dumping
verbatim retrieval results from a SPECTER2 + BGE-reranker pipeline; no AI judgment
sat in the path from corpus to extracted material. The retrieval and indexing infrastructure
was Litmus, an in-house corpus-analysis tool developed as a sibling project and reused (and
improved - for example excluding marketing material and tables of contents from extracted
text chunks) here. An early attempt at conversational synthesis was quickly rejected in favor
of deterministic extraction so the extracted material would be auditable (queries in version
control), reproducible (re-runs produce the same output from the same index), and recov-
erable (a too-narrow query can be widened later; an AI-synthesized excerpt that quietly
dropped a paragraph cannot be recovered). When initial extraction missed bibliographic
entries, the temptation was to add paper-title-anchored queries that would retrieve specific

  Per-section briefs came before drafts, developed iteratively with us in the loop section by
section. Before any LaTeX drafting, each section was worked out as a paragraph-level brief
specifying the section-level frame, intended voice, the per-subsection themes with citation
anchors, and the tension points the drafter should watch for. We engaged with each brief in
detail before moving on — reading through what each subsection was arguing and citing,
working iteratively with the AI to tune framing, citations, and discipline notes, and treating
a brief as complete only when its substance was sound to our judgment. Multiple cycles
were the rule; substantial mid-brief restructurings were common.
  Holistic review passes and structural restructuring when content revealed the structure
wrong caught what per-section attention could not. End-to-end reads across the report
surfaced cross-section consistency issues: register discipline that should have propagated
but had not, citation positioning that drifted from anchor weight, and voice transitions.
Per-section work scales linearly with section count; cross-section consistency scales worse.
Twice during production, the document’s structure was revised because the content as it
developed made the original structure visibly wrong: the compute-substrate chapter was
promoted from a subsection to its own chapter when the material outgrew its housing, and
sections were resequenced when the original placement no longer reflected the argument’s
actual shape.
  Citation-claim verification ran before drafting. Citation-existence checking is what La-
TeX and biber already do at compile time; the audits we ran were the harder kind —
checking that the briefs’ specific argumentative claims about each cited paper actually
matched what the paper says, rather than stretching the citation beyond what the source
supports. The most accessible check ran against the paper’s abstract (or text-extract fall-
back for entries without bib abstracts), ensuring that statements were not contrary or
marginal to the top-line themes of the cited work. A stricter check ran against the curated
chunks in the relevant excerpts file, ensuring the specific claim was consistent with the cited
work’s actual text. Canonical references not in the corpus (books-without-PDFs) needed
a different approach: the inline characterizations we had added at the brief level were re-
viewed in consultation with a different-vendor AI used as a second pair of eyes against the
books’ actual content — cross-AI validation, deliberately, because using a different model
as the verification reader reduces the risk of single-model recall bias reproducing the same
errors in the verification pass. The pass surfaced a handful of over-positioned citations and
several book characterizations that needed tightening; overall, fidelity to source materials
was high as a result of the disciplined method of extraction.

  The vast majority of cited works were obtained either from Google Scholar searches for
relevant key words and phrases, or the author’s own knowledge of the literature. We did not
use ”Deep Research” or similar tools to find works due to the high risk of hallucination,
blind spots, recency-blindness, and general coarse nature of such summaries. As noted
above, the survey was supplemented iteratively by both the author’s additional recollections
and author-validated AI suggestions for additional canonical works throughout drafting. A
coverage observation worth recording: of a corpus of over 450 works, only about half were
ultimately cited. The rest survived the bibliographic-collection step but did not survive
retrieval-and-review — either because the retrieval pipeline did not surface them strongly
enough to anchor a specific claim, or because the briefs moved past them as the argument
tightened. Broad collection plus disciplined filtering produces something different from
broad collection alone.
  Several standing disciplines ran across every phase of the methodology. Honoring our
substantive intent meant that every editorial decision was ours, and the AI’s prose was
material to be examined rather than material to be trusted. The operational form was
the per-brief engagement described above — argumentative shape, tone, citation weight,
framing, register all decided in conversation and committed to prose only once the substance
was sound. The AI’s contribution was iterative drafting against constraints we set, not
autonomous generation we then signed off on.
  Honoring organizational restrictions meant working within a specification document with
both requirements and anti-requirements, the anti-requirements being non-negotiable: no
how-to, no laboratory-policy interpretation, no vendor endorsement, no illustrations from
sensitive domains. Each constraint was articulable as a content-and-scope rule the AI’s
drafts had to be audited against, and where the AI drifted into anti-required territory (a
how-to flavor in a subsection draft, a too-specific institutional reference, an illustration
from a sensitive domain) we flagged and corrected.
  Maintaining the author’s voice was a separate problem at the prose-register level. On
the positive side, we discussed explicitly the named author voices we wanted to emulate
in different parts of the prose — Gleick for altitude and ideas-shown-developing layer-
ing, Yong for the perspective-shift on what LLMs do, Bryson for wry-but-grounded scale
comprehension, Pollan for reader-as-companion posture — each scoped to specific sections
rather than applied as a uniform overlay. We also supplied the author’s own prior technical
writing as calibration material so the AI’s drafts could be matched against the author’s
actual register rather than a generic notion of “technical voice.”

 However, even with the positive calibration in place, the standing catalog of AI-tells still
applied. The catalog started from a public reference list of AI-writing tells2 (negative paral-
lelism, significance puffery, em-dash overuse, structural over-formatting, vague attribution)
and grew throughout the project as project-specific patterns emerged that the public list
did not name. The most stubborn additions were patterns the AI would reach for again
and again across fresh drafts even after correction — for example: “moves” as a noun for
argumentative structure, “substrate” as a metaphor outside its legitimate technical uses,
“load-bearing” as significance-puffery shorthand, and an insistence on going overboard with
intra-text cross-references. Each had to be specifically named and specifically swept; left
to the AI’s own register, they would reappear in the next draft of the next section. To
be clear, this was not an effort to obfuscate the use of AI but to avoid words that im-
paired readability through unnatural or annoyingly repetitive phrasing. The observation
that emerged is that AI-tells are not edge cases to be caught once but recurring patterns
that require standing discipline, and the discipline only works if the catalog is consulted
before each draft is committed.
 Remaining faithful to source materials was the discipline that touched the most dis-
tinct mechanisms: the deterministic extraction pipeline preventing AI synthesis from qui-
etly dropping or reframing source content; the citation-claim verification preventing over-
positioned attributions and recall-error book characterizations from anchoring downstream
prose; the standing requirement that every citation claim be either verified-present and
named with its actual key or honestly flagged as a gap. Behind these mechanisms sits a
substantive observation. The framework the body develops — failure as a property of the
methods rather than a defect to be patched, oversight as a designed regime with calibrated
trust and the time-training-authority requirements, the assurance discipline that demands
artifacts an external reviewer can check — predicts the failure modes that occurred in
AI-assisted production and prescribes the discipline that caught them. The work did not
become a special case of its own substance; the substance applied as predicted. Halluci-
nation, miscalibration, over-positioning, register drift — each is a recurring failure of AI
outputs that the body’s failure-mode catalog already names, and each was caught by a
discipline that operationalized what the body’s framework requires.
