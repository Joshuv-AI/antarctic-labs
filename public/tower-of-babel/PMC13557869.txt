Association between antibiotic exposure and outcomes of chemoimmunotherapy in extensive-stage small cell lung cancer: a single-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ying-Long Peng, Qian-Lin Huang, Hong-Yi Zhu, Zhi-Hong Chen, Yu Deng, Chang Lu, Chong-Rui Xu, Fa-Man Xiao, Qing Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer remains a leading cause of cancer incidence and mortality worldwide. Small cell lung cancer (SCLC), though only 15% of cases, shows aggressive biological behavior and poor prognosis. Chemoimmunotherapy is the standard first-line treatment for extensive-stage SCLC (ES-SCLC). Gut microbiota affects immunotherapy in other cancers, but its role in ES-SCLC is unclear. This study examined the association of antibiotics, cathartics, and probiotics with the efficacy of chemoimmunotherapy in ES-SCLC.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled patients with ES-SCLC receiving chemoimmunotherapy at the Guangdong Lung Cancer Institute (GLCI). Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox regression were used to evaluate associations of antibiotic, cathartic, and probiotic use with progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). Multivariable Cox regression models, including an Akaike information criterion (AIC)-based model and a clinically guided model with prespecified covariates, were used to evaluate the association between antibiotic exposure and survival outcomes. Propensity score-based weighting and matching, together with additional sensitivity analyses, were performed to assess the robustness of antibiotic-related associations.ResultsAntibiotic exposure was associated with shorter PFS in univariable Cox regression [hazard ratio (HR): 2.71, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.41–5.22; P=0.003] and multivariable Cox regression (AIC model: HR: 2.63, 95% CI: 1.28–5.41; P=0.008; clinical model: HR: 2.16, 95% CI: 1.06–4.39; P=0.03). Antibiotic exposure was also associated with shorter OS in univariable analysis (HR: 6.02, 95% CI: 3.06–11.84; P<0.001) and multivariable analysis (AIC model: HR: 6.88, 95% CI: 3.26–14.54; P<0.001; clinical model: HR: 5.34, 95% CI: 2.62–10.87; P<0.001). Propensity score-weighted and sensitivity analyses showed generally consistent directions of association, whereas propensity score-matched analyses showed a consistent adverse direction without reaching statistical significance. Cathartic and probiotic use were not significantly associated with PFS or OS in exploratory analyses.ConclusionsAntibiotic exposure was associated with shorter PFS in ES-SCLC patients receiving chemoimmunotherapy, with generally consistent findings across multiple analyses. The OS association was also observed. However, these results should be interpreted cautiously given the small exposed cohort, residual confounding, and possible proportional hazards violations. Prospective multicenter validation with detailed antibiotic, infection, and microbiome data is warranted.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Antibiotic exposure was associated with inferior clinical outcomes in patients with extensive-stage small cell lung cancer (ES-SCLC) receiving chemoimmunotherapy. This association remained evident in multivariable Cox and propensity score-weighted analyses, whereas propensity score-matched analyses showed a consistent adverse direction without reaching statistical significance.

What is known and what is new?

• Chemoimmunotherapy is the current standard first-line treatment for ES-SCLC; however, clinically relevant factors that influence treatment efficacy remain insufficiently defined. Although the gut microbiota has been reported to modulate responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors in several malignancies, evidence specific to ES-SCLC remains limited.

• This study provides clinical evidence that antibiotic exposure is associated with poorer outcomes in ES-SCLC patients receiving chemoimmunotherapy. These findings support further investigation into the potential role of antibiotic-related microbiota perturbation in modulating chemoimmunotherapy outcomes, although this mechanism remains hypothetical and requires prospective validation with integrated microbiome and immune profiling.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings support careful evaluation of antibiotic indications in ES-SCLC patients receiving chemoimmunotherapy, while antibiotics should not be withheld or delayed when bacterial infection is clinically indicated.

Introduction

Malignant tumors are a major cause of mortality worldwide, with lung cancer ranking first in both incidence and mortality among all cancers. According to global statistics, there were approximately 2.5 million new cases of lung cancer in 2022. More than 1.8 million deaths were attributable to lung cancer (1). This disease imposes a substantial burden on global health. Based on pathological classification, lung cancer can be divided into non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC). NSCLC accounts for approximately 85%, whereas SCLC accounts for about 15% (2). Although less common, SCLC exhibits a relatively higher degree of malignancy. SCLC can be classified according to the Veterans Administration Lung Study Group (VALG) into limited-stage SCLC and extensive-stage SCLC (ES-SCLC) (3). Although NSCLC has entered the era of precision medicine, with treatment strategies guided by genetic testing and related results, SCLC still remains distant from precision medicine (4). Many researchers have focused on subclassifying SCLC in the hope of developing appropriate treatment strategies based on these subtypes. Gazdar et al. proposed that SCLC can be divided into neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine subtypes (5). Gay et al. and Rudin et al. further classified SCLC into four transcriptional subtypes: SCLC-A, SCLC-N, SCLC-P, and SCLC-Y/I (6,7). Subsequent studies revealed that SCLC cannot be strictly categorized into four distinct subtypes. Inflammatory-related subtypes exist across different categories, and transitions between subtypes can occur under the selective pressure of treatment (8). These findings present significant challenges for both the classification and therapeutic targeting of SCLC. Given the limited clinical utility of these subtyping systems, chemoimmunotherapy remains the primary treatment approach for SCLC (9).

Currently, numerous studies indicate that the composition of the gut microbiota is associated with cancer development and treatment efficacy (10). Hamada et al. reported that antibiotic use was a significant risk factor for objective response rate (ORR), progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS) in NSCLC patients receiving immunotherapy (11). A prospective study evaluated the relationship between gut microbiota composition and immunotherapy efficacy, finding that greater microbial diversity was associated with better treatment outcomes (12). Nyein et al. showed similar findings (13). An animal study demonstrated that Bifidobacterium can enhance antitumor immunity and improve the efficacy of immunotherapy in a murine melanoma model (14). Tomita et al. evaluated the impact of Clostridium butyricum on immunotherapy in NSCLC and found that it significantly prolonged PFS and OS (15). For SCLC, Mendelian randomization analyses have suggested a potential genetically predicted association between gut microbiota composition and SCLC development (16,17). Sun et al. demonstrated that post-treatment gut microbiota composition is associated with the efficacy of immunotherapy in ES-SCLC (18). However, the current evidence regarding the impact of antibiotics on the efficacy of ES-SCLC chemoimmunotherapy treatment remains controversial (19-21). In addition, the effects of cathartics and probiotics on ES-SCLC outcomes are still not well characterized.

This study aimed to explore the association between antibiotic exposure and clinical outcomes in patients with ES-SCLC receiving chemoimmunotherapy. We further evaluated whether this association persisted after adjustment for baseline clinical characteristics, nutritional status, and systemic inflammatory markers, as well as in propensity score-based sensitivity analyses. The associations of cathartic and probiotic use with clinical outcomes were also explored. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-0571/rc).

Methods

Data sources and study population

This retrospective study enrolled patients from April 2018 to February 2025 at the Guangdong Lung Cancer Institute (GLCI), Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (No. KY2024-738-02, date of approval: Oct 15th, 2024) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. The inclusion criteria were: (I) pathologically diagnosed SCLC; (II) clinically staged ES-SCLC based on computed tomography (CT) or positron emission tomography/CT (PET/CT); and (III) treatment with chemoimmunotherapy, defined as systemic treatment combining chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. The exclusion criteria were: (I) pathologically diagnosed NSCLC; (II) clinically staged local stage SCLC; (III) ES-SCLC not treated with chemoimmunotherapy, including treatment with antibody-drug conjugates or chemotherapy alone; and (IV) missing data for variables required in the main analyses.

Cancer diagnosis and outcome

All enrolled patients underwent surgery or biopsy and received a pathological diagnosis of SCLC, and were classified as ES-SCLC based on CT or PET/CT according to VALG criteria. For ES-SCLC patients who received systemic therapy, treatment response was evaluated according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors 1.1 criteria (22). PFS was defined as the time from the initiation of chemoimmunotherapy to disease progression or the last follow-up. OS was defined as the time from the initiation of chemoimmunotherapy to death from any cause or the last follow-up. Patients without documented progression or death were censored at the date of last follow-up.

Definitions of antibiotic, cathartic, and probiotic use

Antibiotic, cathartic, and probiotic use were recorded from one month before treatment until tumor progression and were analyzed as binary variables. Antibiotic exposure was defined as any documented systemic antibiotic use during this period. Patients were categorized into the antibiotics group or antibiotic-free group accordingly. Among patients initially classified as antibiotic-exposed, exposure was further classified as confirmed or inferred. Confirmed antibiotic exposure was defined as antibiotic administration documented in the medical records of GLCI, whereas inferred exposure was based on external medical records without complete antibiotic details. To clarify the timing of exposure relative to chemoimmunotherapy initiation, antibiotic exposure was further categorized as pre-treatment exposure, defined as systemic antibiotic use within 30 days before chemoimmunotherapy initiation, and on-treatment exposure, defined as systemic antibiotic use after chemoimmunotherapy initiation. Antibiotic exposure characteristics, including timing, indication, infection severity, antibiotic class, route of administration, and duration, were summarized among antibiotic-exposed patients when available. Infection severity was operationally classified as mild, moderate, or severe with reference to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Mild infection was defined as infection managed with oral antibiotics only; moderate infection was defined as infection requiring parenteral or intravenous antibiotics without infection-related hospitalization; and severe infection was defined as infection requiring parenteral or intravenous antibiotics with infection-related hospitalization. For patients with multiple antibiotic exposures, infection severity was assigned according to the most severe episode, and antibiotic duration was calculated as the cumulative duration across all episodes. The primary analysis included only patients with confirmed antibiotic exposure, and patients with inferred exposure were included in sensitivity analyses. Cathartic and probiotic use were treated as exploratory exposures.

Covariates

Baseline clinical variables included age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS), smoking history, body mass index (BMI), chronic diseases, metastatic sites, and treatment line. ECOG PS was dichotomized as ECOG PS 2 versus ECOG PS 0–1. Smoking history was classified as current, former, or never smoker. Chronic diseases, including hypertension, diabetes, and coronary heart disease, were recorded as binary variables. Liver, bone, brain, pleural, adrenal, and pancreatic metastases were recorded as present or absent at baseline. Treatment line was categorized as first-line or non-first-line treatment. Pre-treatment laboratory variables included albumin, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, and platelet count. Composite inflammatory indices were calculated as follows: neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) = neutrophil count/lymphocyte count; platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) = platelet count/lymphocyte count; systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) = platelet count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count; neutrophil-to-platelet ratio (NPR) = neutrophil count/platelet count; and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) = neutrophil count × monocyte count/lymphocyte count. Albumin and ln-transformed NLR were evaluated as candidate covariates in Cox regression analyses. PLR, SII, NPR, and SIRI were evaluated in sensitivity analyses.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using R software (version 4.4.1) and Free Statistics software (version 2.1.1). A fixed random seed was used for propensity score-matched analyses. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) or range according to distribution, and categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test within each antibiotic exposure group. Continuous variables were compared using Welch’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Baseline balance was assessed using absolute standardized mean differences (SMDs).

Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to compare PFS and OS between the antibiotics and antibiotic-free groups. Median PFS and OS were reported, and differences were compared using the log-rank test. Kaplan-Meier analyses for cathartic and probiotic use were performed as exploratory analyses.

Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression was performed for candidate variables. For multivariable Cox regression, antibiotic exposure was forced into the model as the main exposure of interest, and other variables were selected using stepwise selection based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC). Separate AIC-selected multivariable Cox models were constructed for PFS and OS. Given the limited number of antibiotic-exposed patients, the AIC-selected models were interpreted cautiously. To reduce reliance on data-driven covariate selection, a complementary prespecified clinically guided multivariable Cox model was constructed, including antibiotic exposure, ECOG PS 2, treatment line, liver metastasis, and ln-transformed NLR (Figure S1).

Propensity score-based weighting analyses were performed as sensitivity analyses. Variables included in the propensity score model were selected based on baseline imbalance, clinical relevance, and model stability. Stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), truncated stabilized IPTW, and overlap weighting were performed, with covariate balance assessed using absolute SMDs. To further account for residual confounding, weighted Cox proportional hazards models with robust standard errors were additionally adjusted for covariates retained in the AIC-selected primary Cox models. Propensity score-matched analyses were also performed using 1:1 and 1:2 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement, with a caliper width of 0.2 SDs of the logit of the propensity score. Cox models with robust standard errors clustered by matched subclass were used in the matched cohorts. Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted according to antibiotic exposure timing and landmark definitions. Pre-treatment and on-treatment antibiotic exposures were analyzed separately. Landmark analyses were performed using 30- and 90-day landmark time points to reduce potential immortal time bias related to on-treatment antibiotic exposure.

Further sensitivity analyses were conducted separately in the primary cohort with confirmed antibiotic exposure and in the expanded cohort including inferred antibiotic exposure. These analyses included restriction to first-line chemoimmunotherapy, exclusion of patients with PFS ≤1 month, and additional adjustment for ln-transformed NLR, PLR, SII, NPR, or SIRI one at a time. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Associations were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Cathartic and probiotic use were evaluated as exploratory variables and covariates where appropriate.

Results

A total of 120 patients with ES-SCLC who received chemoimmunotherapy at GLCI between April 2018 and February 2025 were included in this study. Overall, disease progression was recorded in 74 patients, and death was recorded in 81 patients. The study flowchart for the overall cohort is shown in Figure 1. Baseline characteristics of all patients according to initial antibiotic exposure status are presented in Table 1. Among the 120 patients, 20 were initially classified as antibiotic-exposed and 100 were antibiotic-free. Patients initially classified as antibiotic-exposed had a higher frequency of cathartic use and lower baseline albumin levels than antibiotic-free patients. Although several variables did not reach statistical significance in conventional between-group comparisons, SMDs suggested baseline imbalance in ECOG PS, pleural metastasis, and several baseline inflammatory indices. Regarding treatment regimens, atezolizumab was the predominant immunotherapy agent, etoposide plus platinum-based chemotherapy was the most commonly used chemotherapy regimen, and the median number of treatment cycles in the overall cohort was 5.

Kaplan-Meier analysis in the primary analysis cohort showed that patients with confirmed antibiotic exposure had significantly shorter PFS than antibiotic-free patients. The median PFS was 7.5 months in the antibiotic-free group and 4.7 months in the antibiotics group (log-rank P=0.002; Figure 2A). Similarly, confirmed antibiotic exposure was associated with shorter OS. The median OS was 16.3 months in the antibiotic-free group and 5.1 months in the antibiotics group (log-rank P<0.001; Figure 2B). Exploratory Kaplan-Meier analyses were also performed for cathartic and probiotic use. Cathartic use was not significantly associated with PFS and OS (Figures S2,S3). Probiotic use was not significantly associated with either PFS or OS (Figures S4,S5).

Kaplan-Meier survival curves illustrating the association of antibiotic use with PFS (A) and OS (B) in ES-SCLC chemoimmunotherapy. ES-SCLC, extensive-stage small cell lung cancer; PFS, progression-free survival; OS, overall survival.

Univariable Cox regression analysis was performed in the primary analysis cohort to evaluate the association between each candidate variable and PFS or OS. In the univariable analysis for PFS, confirmed antibiotic exposure was significantly associated with shorter PFS (HR: 2.71; 95% CI: 1.41–5.22; P=0.003). For multivariable analysis, antibiotic exposure was forced into the model as the main exposure of interest, and other variables were selected using AIC-based stepwise selection. In the AIC-selected multivariable Cox model for PFS, antibiotic exposure remained associated with shorter PFS (HR: 2.63; 95% CI: 1.28–5.41; P=0.008; Figure 3A). To further assess the robustness of the findings, a prespecified clinically guided Cox model was constructed based on clinical relevance and the directed causal framework, including antibiotic exposure, ECOG PS 2, treatment line, liver metastasis, and ln(NLR). In this prespecified model, antibiotic exposure remained associated with shorter PFS, with results generally consistent with those of the AIC-selected model (HR: 2.16; 95% CI: 1.06–4.39; P=0.03; Figure 3A). In the univariable analysis for OS, confirmed antibiotic exposure was also significantly associated with shorter OS (HR: 6.02; 95% CI: 3.06–11.84; P<0.001). In the AIC-selected multivariable Cox model for OS, antibiotic exposure remained associated with shorter OS (HR: 6.88; 95% CI: 3.26–14.54; P<0.001; Figure 3B). Similar findings were observed in the prespecified clinically guided Cox model, in which antibiotic exposure remained associated with shorter OS after adjustment for ECOG PS 2, treatment line, liver metastasis, and ln(NLR) (HR: 5.34; 95% CI: 2.62–10.87; P<0.001; Figure 3B). The number of events, estimated parameters, events per variable, and event distribution according to antibiotic exposure status for the Cox models are summarized in Table S1.

Propensity score-based weighting analyses were performed to further assess the robustness of the association between antibiotic exposure and survival outcomes. The propensity score model included age, ECOG PS 2, cathartic use, pleural metastasis, albumin, and ln-transformed NLR. Overlap weighting achieved the most favorable covariate balance, whereas stabilized IPTW and truncated stabilized IPTW improved balance but residual imbalance remained for cathartic use and albumin (Figures S6-S8). In weighted Cox models additionally adjusted for covariates retained in the AIC-selected primary Cox models, antibiotic exposure remained significantly associated with shorter PFS and OS across all three weighting approaches (Figure 4A). The adjusted HRs for PFS were 2.20 (95% CI: 1.03–4.69; P=0.042) with stabilized IPTW, 2.22 (95% CI: 1.05–4.69; P=0.04) with truncated stabilized IPTW, and 4.70 (95% CI: 1.73–12.76; P=0.002) with overlap weighting. The corresponding adjusted HRs for OS were 5.42 (95% CI: 2.70–10.87; P<0.001), 5.48 (95% CI: 2.73–11.00; P<0.001), and 6.68 (95% CI: 2.62–17.02; P<0.001), respectively. Additional propensity score-matched analyses were conducted using 1:1 and 1:2 nearest-neighbor matching. In the 1:1 matched cohort, 12 antibiotic-exposed patients and 12 antibiotic-free patients were retained. In the 1:2 matched cohort, 12 antibiotic-exposed patients and 18 antibiotic-free patients were retained (Figures S9,S10). After matching, the associations between antibiotic exposure and shorter survival showed a consistent direction. In the 1:1 matched cohort, the HRs were 1.88 for PFS (95% CI: 0.58–6.15; P=0.29) and 2.01 for OS (95% CI: 0.89–4.54; P=0.10). In the 1:2 matched cohort, the HRs were 2.67 for PFS (95% CI: 0.93–7.70; P=0.07) and 2.10 for OS (95% CI: 0.95–4.60; P=0.07). Sensitivity analyses were further performed according to the timing of antibiotic exposure and landmark definitions (Figure 4B). For PFS, pre-treatment antibiotic exposure was not significantly associated with shorter PFS (HR: 1.32; 95% CI: 0.43–4.07; P=0.63), whereas on-treatment antibiotic exposure was significantly associated with shorter PFS (HR: 2.82; 95% CI: 1.33–5.98; P=0.007). In the 30- and 90-day landmark analyses, antibiotic exposure showed a consistent adverse direction for PFS, with HRs of 2.02 (95% CI: 0.70–5.83; P=0.20) and 3.19 (95% CI: 0.86–11.84; P=0.08), respectively. For OS, pre-treatment antibiotic exposure showed an unfavorable trend (HR: 3.04; 95% CI: 0.98–9.48; P=0.055), while on-treatment antibiotic exposure was significantly associated with shorter OS (HR: 6.64; 95% CI: 3.08–14.28; P<0.001). The 30- and 90-day landmark analyses also showed significant associations between antibiotic exposure and shorter OS, with HRs of 4.48 (95% CI: 1.52–13.19; P=0.007) and 6.66 (95% CI: 2.12–20.95; P=0.001), respectively.

Several sensitivity analyses were performed in both the primary cohort with confirmed antibiotic exposure and the expanded cohort including inferred exposure (Table 3). In the primary cohort, antibiotic exposure remained associated with shorter PFS after restriction to first-line chemoimmunotherapy (HR: 3.25; 95% CI: 1.46–7.27; P=0.004), exclusion of patients with PFS ≤1 month (HR: 2.87; 95% CI: 1.35–6.10; P=0.006), and additional adjustment for ln-transformed NLR, PLR, SII, NPR, or SIRI. Similar findings were observed for OS. In the expanded cohort, the association remained generally consistent for both PFS and OS, including after restriction to first-line treatment and exclusion of patients with PFS ≤1 month. These results provided additional support for the direction of the association, although the findings should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of antibiotic-exposed patients.

Discussion

At present, the association of antibiotics with the efficacy of chemoimmunotherapy in ES-SCLC remains unclear. In this study, antibiotic use was found to be associated with poorer prognosis in patients with ES-SCLC treated with chemoimmunotherapy, and this association was generally consistent across multiple analytical approaches. In contrast, cathartics and probiotics did not show significant associations with the efficacy of chemoimmunotherapy in ES-SCLC. These findings suggest that antibiotic exposure may be linked to less favorable outcomes of chemoimmunotherapy in ES-SCLC within exploratory analyses.

Antibiotics are commonly used drugs that can alter the composition of the gut microbiota. At present, studies investigating the impact of antibiotics on chemoimmunotherapy in ES-SCLC are limited, and the results are inconsistent. Takada et al. analyzed data from IMpower133 and found that antibiotics, proton pump inhibitors, and probiotics had no impact on the prognosis of ES-SCLC patients receiving chemotherapy or chemoimmunotherapy (21). In contrast, Deng et al. reported that antibiotic use appeared to show an unfavorable trend for PFS (HR: 1.250, 95% CI: 0.968–1.614, P=0.09) and OS (HR: 1.311, 95% CI: 0.988–1.740, P=0.06) (20). Zhong et al. reached different conclusions in ES-SCLC patients receiving subsequent-line regimens containing immunotherapy, suggesting that antibiotic use is a risk factor for immunotherapy outcomes (19). The discrepancies among these studies may be partly explained by differences in treatment setting, treatment line, antibiotic exposure definition, antibiotic exposure window, sample size, and adjustment for confounding factors. In summary, the association between antibiotic use and outcomes of chemoimmunotherapy for ES-SCLC remains controversial. In the present study, antibiotic exposure was significantly associated with shorter PFS and OS in the primary multivariable Cox models. A prespecified clinically guided Cox model based on clinical relevance showed generally consistent results with the AIC-selected model. Similar adverse associations were also observed in propensity score-weighted analyses and additional sensitivity analyses, while propensity score-matched analyses showed a consistent adverse direction but did not reach statistical significance, likely owing to the limited matched sample size. Nevertheless, because of the retrospective design, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

Previous research on gut microbiota in SCLC has been limited. Li et al. and Yang et al. analyzed gut microbiota data from MiBioGen to explore potential causal relationships with SCLC and identified certain taxa as risk or protective factors for its development (16,17). Gong et al., using data on 418 gut microbial taxa from the Dutch cohort, also reported potential associations with SCLC occurrence (23). These three studies were based on Mendelian randomization analyses. Sun et al. prospectively included more than forty ES-SCLC patients and investigated the relationships between gut microbiota, metabolites, and treatment response, confirming that microbiota composition and metabolites are associated with the efficacy of chemoimmunotherapy (18). Shi et al. established a cohort including patients with SCLC to investigate the relationship between the oral microbiome and the clinical characteristics and prognosis of lung cancer. They identified an association between oral microbial composition and lung cancer prognosis, and further showed that oral microbial diversity was correlated with ECOG PS score and ProGRP levels (24). Zhang et al. performed sequencing of the intratumoral gut microbiota in 71 patients with SCLC and found that Clostridium and Lactobacillus were associated with better prognosis, further supporting the potential value of the microbiome in small-cell lung cancer (25). Although studies in the field of SCLC are limited, the gut microbiota has been extensively investigated in other cancers. Lee et al. found that Bifidobacterium bifidum enhanced interferon-γ production and improved the efficacy of immunotherapy in NSCLC (26). Similarly, Bifidobacterium, Escherichia, and Sutterella were significantly enriched in NSCLC patients who responded well to immunotherapy (27). In colorectal cancer, Clostridium butyricum was shown to destabilize MYC and enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy (28). As an aggressive neuroendocrine malignancy, SCLC is characterized by an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, including relatively low cytotoxic T-cell infiltration and downregulated antigen-presenting machinery (7). Previous studies have suggested that the gut microbiota may influence systemic immune regulation and response to immune checkpoint blockade through microbial metabolites and immune-cell modulation (18,29-31). Therefore, antibiotic exposure could theoretically affect chemoimmunotherapy outcomes by perturbing gut microbial communities and related immune-regulatory pathways. However, because microbiome sequencing, metabolomic profiling, and immune profiling were not performed in the present study, this explanation remains hypothetical. Therefore, the observed association between antibiotic exposure and poorer outcomes should be interpreted as clinical and hypothesis-generating rather than mechanistically proven. Further prospective studies integrating antibiotic exposure data, gut microbiota composition, microbial metabolites, and immune profiling are needed to clarify whether gut microbiota mediates the association between antibiotic exposure and chemoimmunotherapy outcomes in ES-SCLC.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design may have introduced selection bias and residual confounding. Although multivariable Cox regression, overlap weighting, propensity score matching, and sensitivity analyses were performed, unmeasured confounding could not be fully excluded. In particular, antibiotic use may reflect infection, infection severity, hospitalization, or poor general condition rather than the direct effect of antibiotics themselves. Second, the antibiotics group included only 20 patients, which limited statistical power, especially in propensity score-matched and sensitivity analyses. Third, antibiotic exposure was analyzed primarily as a binary variable. Although information on antibiotic timing, indication, severity, class, route, and duration was available for patients with confirmed exposure, these data were incomplete for inferred exposure and the small number of antibiotic-exposed patients precluded reliable subgroup, dose-response, or class-specific analyses. Therefore, dose-response relationships and the effects of specific antibiotic types could not be evaluated. Validation in larger prospective multicenter cohorts with detailed antibiotic and infection data is warranted. In addition, antibiotic exposure was not a fixed baseline variable but a time-varying exposure. Treating on-treatment antibiotic use as a simple baseline binary variable may introduce immortal time bias. We therefore performed 30- and 90-day landmark analyses to mitigate this potential bias. However, given the limited sample size and the inability to perform reliable time-dependent Cox modeling, residual time-related bias could not be fully excluded.

Conclusions

This retrospective study found that antibiotic exposure was associated with shorter PFS in patients with ES-SCLC receiving chemoimmunotherapy. An association with inferior OS was also observed, but this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the small antibiotic-exposed cohort, possible residual confounding, and potential instability across sensitivity analyses. These findings support careful evaluation of antibiotic indications in clinical practice. Antibiotics should not be withheld or delayed when bacterial infection is clinically indicated. Prospective multicenter studies incorporating detailed antibiotic, infection, and microbiome data are warranted.
