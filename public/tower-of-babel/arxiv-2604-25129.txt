8DNA: 8D Neural Asset Light Transport by Distribution Learning 495
Liwen Wu, Haolin Lu, Bing Xu, Miloš Hašan, Ravi Ramamoorthi

Abstract
. High-fidelity 3D assets exhibit intriguing global illumination effects like subsurface scattering, glossy interreflections, and fine-scale fiber scatterings, which often involve long scattering paths that are expensive to simulate. We introduce 8D neural assets (8DNA) to pre-bake these light transport effects into neural representations. Unlike prior methods that assume far-field lighting and precompute light transport into 6D functions, 8DNA learns the full 8D light transport, enabling accurate rendering under near-field illumination. Our training leverages a distribution-learning formulation that learns light transport from forward path-traced samples, which produces less optimization variance with lower training budget than the prior regression-based approaches. Experiments show our 8DNA rendering closely matches path-traced results under various scene configurations, yet it achieves improved variance reduction and fast inference speeds on challenging assets.

1.
Introduction

Real-world 3D objects often exhibit interesting and complex light transport effects,
such as multiple scattering within media enclosed by refractive boundaries, glossy interreflections, fine-scale fiber scattering in hair, fur or fabrics, and more.
These effects are challenging to render in digital 3D assets: they often involve long light paths that are non-trivial to trace from either the camera or the light source, making them expensive to simulate with standard Monte Carlo methods. Furthermore, the appearance models responsible for the above effects can be non-trivial to consistently implement and precisely match between different rendering systems.

Recent work
(
Mullia et al. 2024
;
Tg et al. 2024a
)
has explored a promising alternative: precompute the light transport within such 3D assets into a neural representation, which can be rendered more efficiently, and transferred between renderers more easily. These methods propose to bake all light paths within a 3D asset into a relightable neural network, which can be evaluated for any point on the asset and any view and light direction.
However, such parameterizations are 6-dimensional: they fundamentally assume distant illumination. While they can still be approximately used under near-field lighting, we show that this can lead to inaccuracy.

The true light transport operator describing light paths through the 3D asset is an 8D function
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})
: it depends on both outgoing (camera) direction
\bm{\omega}_{o}
and position
\mathbf{x}_{o}
, as well as their incoming (light) counterparts
\bm{\omega}_{i}
and
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. This 8D function is not only challenging to compress, but its queries are non-trivial to estimate with low variance, especially in the most interesting cases: appearances that feature highly specular surface or fiber interactions, and/or forward-scattering phase functions.
Therefore, unlike the 6D functions in
Mullia et al. 2024
and
Tg et al. 2024a
, it is difficult to train the full 8D transport with a simple regression loss.

In this paper, we propose to recover the full 8D light transport in a distribution-learning framework.
From the forward path samples through the asset,
we estimate the global scattering distribution using a normalizing flow and predict the survival probability (albedo term), together giving a natural decomposition of the global light transport distribution
\mathbf{F}
.

In contrast to the challenging direct evaluations of
\mathbf{F}
,
sampling
\mathbf{F}
is as easy as standard path tracing through the asset without next-event estimation.
Such efficiency
allows our model to train faster than regression-based baselines while better capturing 8D light transport including accurate near-field illumination.
As a further benefit, the learned representation can be evaluated
and
importance-sampled, with the sampling distribution by construction precisely proportional to the transport.
Similar arguments are made in concurrent work on precomputing multiple scattering from microgeometry
(
Li et al. 2025
)
;
however, this work does not target 3D assets and still assumes 6D far-field light transport.

Figure
1
demonstrates that our 8D neural asset (8DNA) successfully reconstructs the global illumination of the jade seal lit by a nearby area emitter: a challenging configuration for the baselines.
Our representation is particularly effective for objects with long scattering paths,
such as translucent assets with complex interiors,
achieving noticeable variance reduction compared to standard path tracing (
Section

4
).
In summary, our contributions are:

•

An efficient and accurate 8D neural asset representation of arbitrary 3D assets with complex internal light transport.

•

A forward sampling training framework of our representation, whose implementation complexity and training cost are as low as standard forward path tracing without next-event estimation.

•

Results showing 8DNA assets rendered within a final scene at an accuracy closely matching ground truth, without tracing long light paths or needing to implement the original light scattering models in the deployment renderer.

2.
Preliminaries and Related Work

8D near-field light transport.

Recall our goal of precomputing global light transport within a 3D asset consisting of surfaces, volumes, fibers, etc.
We assume the asset is non-emissive and bounded by a 2D surface
\mathcal{M}
(support for volumes without boundary is discussed in
Section

4.3
).
For such assets,
light scattering can be encapsulated by a light transport function

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})
that maps incident radiance
\mathbf{L}_{i}
on any boundary point to outgoing radiance
\mathbf{L}_{o}
at another boundary point;
integrations are taken over each variable’s domain of definition (
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
,
\bm{\omega}_{i}\in
sphere) unless specified:

(1)

\mathbf{L}_{o}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o};\mathbf{L}_{i})=\iint\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathbf{L}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}.

Note that
\mathbf{L}_{i}
excludes the internal multiple-scattering within the asset,
and we will use the fact that
\mathbf{L}_{o}
is computable by forward path tracing given any
\mathbf{L}_{i}
.
Our objective is to learn the light transport
\mathbf{F}
for a given asset, in the form of a neural representation, to enable efficient re-rendering under arbitrary illumination
\mathbf{L}_{i}
.

The 8D transport
\mathbf{F}
, or its variants, are sometimes called a BSSRDF (bidirectional subsurface scattering reflectance distribution function) in the literature. We will avoid this term, as it unnecessarily constrains the concept to subsurface scattering, while our solution generalizes to any other kinds of scattering.

Global light transport with a far-field assumption

assumes incident light is sufficiently distant to depend only on
\bm{\omega}_{i}
.
In this case,

\mathbf{F}
can be further pre-integrated into a 6D transport
\mathbf{F}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i})
, invariant to the incident position:

(2)

\begin{split}\mathbf{L}_{o}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o};\mathbf{L}_{i})&=\iint\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathbf{L}_{i}(\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}\\
&=\int\left(\int\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\mathbf{L}_{i}(\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}\\
&=\int\!\mathbf{F}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathbf{L}_{i}(\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}.\end{split}

Many far-field neural representations of this kind have been proposed in previous work. On flat materials,
Kuznetsov et al. 2021
learn a spatially varying reflectance with a neural network
\mathbf{F}^{\prime}_{\theta}
by minimizing an L2 regression loss between
\mathbf{F}^{\prime}_{\theta}
and
\mathbf{F}^{\prime}
:

(3)

\begin{split}\iiint(\mathbf{F}^{\prime}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i})-\mathbf{F}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}))^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{o}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{o},\end{split}

where the integral is estimated by sampling random
(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i})
queries,
and the ground truth
\mathbf{F}^{\prime}
is obtained via standard path tracing under a directional delta light source
\mathbf{L}_{i}(\bm{\omega})=\delta(\bm{\omega}-\bm{\omega}_{i})
(using the fact
\mathbf{F}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i})\!=\!\int\!\mathbf{F}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\bm{\omega})\delta(\bm{\omega}\!-\!\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}
).
Similar ideas have been applied to layered materials
(
Zeltner et al. 2024
;
Sztrajman et al. 2021
;
Fan et al. 2022
)
, curved surfaces
(
Kuznetsov et al. 2022
)
, 3D assets with interreflection and scattering effects
(
Mullia et al. 2024
)
,
and to full-sphere incident directions for translucent objects
(
Tg et al. 2024a
)
.
Note, this paper uses near-field specifically to refer to multi-bounce light transport rather than local BSDF models.

While the far-field assumption leads to a simple and effective learning step,
the resulting 6D model cannot accurately capture the near-field illumination effects encoded in the full transport
\mathbf{F}
of
Eq.

1
.
A direct extension would be to train an 8D network
\mathbf{F}_{\theta}
with the same regression scheme, now additionally sampling
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and path tracing ground truth under a doubly delta illumination
\mathbf{L}_{i}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\omega})=\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i})\delta(\bm{\omega}-\bm{\omega}_{i})
,
but the combination of higher-dimensional sampling and strongly peaked illumination dramatically increases the difficulty of designing efficient Monte Carlo estimators.
While
Tg et al. 2024b
partially mitigates this variance by learning a light transport function over a collection of incident illumination samples,
it relies on an isotropic assumption for internal scattering (see supplementary),
and its rendering requires tracing multiple incident rays per outgoing ray
that does not easily fit into the path tracing pipeline.
These limitations motivate our shift to a distribution-learning-based training objective.

Distribution learning in rendering

is most commonly used for importance sampling.
These methods learn to sample a certain term of the rendering equation,
including BSDF sampling
(
Rainer et al. 2020
;
Xie et al. 2019
;
Xu et al. 2023
)
,
product sampling the BSDF times emission
(
Herholz et al. 2016
;
Hart et al. 2020
;
Clarberg and Akenine-Möllery 2008
;
Litalien et al. 2024
)
,
and path guiding from the approximated incident radiance
(
Vorba et al. 2014
;
Müller et al. 2017
;
Dodik et al. 2022
;
Diolatzis et al. 2020
)
.
The distributions are constructed from analytic mixtures or warps in classic approaches.
In neural methods,
normalizing flows
(
Dinh et al. 2014
;
Rezende and Mohamed 2015
;
Durkan et al. 2019
;
Müller et al. 2019
)
are widely used, yet still predict analytic mixtures for their efficiency
(
Sztrajman et al. 2021
;
Xu et al. 2023
;
Fan et al. 2022
;
Zeltner et al. 2024
)
.
Recent works further explore flow-based models
(
Lipman et al. 2022
;
Heitz et al. 2023
)
for BSDF sampling
(
Fu et al. 2024
)
and precomputing multiple scattering
(
Li et al. 2025
)
.
A reparameterization based neural sampling strategy is proposed in
(
Wu et al. 2025
)
without using generative neural networks.

Pre-computed radiance transfer (PRT)

also builds upon the far-field light transport formulation of
Eq.

2
but focuses on aggressively compressing both
L_{i}
and
\mathcal{F}

(
Ramamoorthi et al. 2009
)
.
This is typically achieved by basis function expansions using spherical harmonics
(
Sloan et al. 2002
;
Ramamoorthi and Hanrahan 2001
)
, wavelets
(
Ng et al. 2003
;
Ng et al. 2004
)
, polynomials
(
Ben-Artzi et al. 2008
)
, Gaussians
(
Tsai and Shih 2006
;
Xu et al. 2013
;
Lu et al. 2025
)
, or neural bases
(
Xu et al. 2022
;
Rainer et al. 2022
)
.
Extensions to near-field polygonal area lights have also been derived using spherical harmonics
(
Wang and Ramamoorthi 2018
;
Wu et al. 2020
)
.
In contrast to path tracing, PRT is primarily designed for deterministic, real-time evaluation under relatively simple illumination configurations,
rather than for the high-fidelity, scalable simulation of complex light transport that we target.

Multiple-scattering by BSSRDF.

BSSRDFs have been widely used to model specific multiple-scattering effects.

Jensen et al. 2001
first introduce an analytic BSSRDF for subsurface scattering in homogeneous media under flat surfaces.
Subsequent works refine this formulation
(
Habel et al. 2013
;
Donner and Jensen 2006
;
Donner et al. 2009
)
,
extend it to heterogeneous media
(
Donner et al. 2008
;
Peers et al. 2006
)
,
and employ neural representations to better capture shape-dependent behavior
(
Vicini et al. 2019
)
.
Similar BSSRDFs have also been used to model scattering inside translucent objects
(
Deng et al. 2022
)
and fur and hair
(
Yan et al. 2017
)
.
These methods typically adopt simplified BSSRDF forms (e.g., separable parameterizations) and focus on a single class of effects (e.g., subsurface scattering),
leaving other components of the light transport (e.g., surface interreflections) to be handled by other methods or ignored.
In contrast, our 8D light transport makes no simplifications and is designed to capture all scattering events within the asset—across both interior volumes and surfaces–up to the point where light exits the asset.

3.
8D Neural Asset

Because the light transport function
\mathbf{F}
is non-negative,
we can decompose it to a conditional distribution
\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})
and a normalizing factor
\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})
for each color channel independently:

(4)

\begin{gathered}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})=\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o}),\\
\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})\!=\!\!\iint\!\!\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i},\;\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})=\frac{\mathbf{F}}{\bm{\alpha}}.\end{gathered}

Conceptually,
\bm{\alpha}
corresponds to the directionally-varying albedo (survival probability) and
\mathbf{p}
describes the distribution of scattering events.
We learn the two components separately:
a normalizing flow
\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}
for the scattering distribution and a regular MLP
\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{\theta}}
for the albedo (
Section

3.1
),
which together formulate
\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}
.
Both
\mathbf{x}_{i}
defined on the asset boundary and
\bm{\omega}_{i}
on the sphere do not naturally fit into standard Euclidean normalizing flows.
We therefore apply a reparameterization of
\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}
in
Section

3.2
.
The training and inference of our model are described in
Section

3.3
,
and we include their pseudocode in the supplemental material.

Figure 2.

is performed by tracing random outgoing rays to the asset, encountering multiple events of surface/medium/null scattering and Russian Roulette (r.r.) until leaving the asset.
The exit ray configurations and throughputs are used to compute the negative log-likelihood loss and albedo regression loss (bottom right).

3.1.
Learning the scattering distribution and albedo

We learn both
\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}
and
\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{\theta}}
through stochastic gradient descent optimization.
We first discuss the training objectives under a fixed
(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})
then the combined loss for the full 8D domain.

Scattering distribution loss.

The common practice in statistical learning suggests
\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}
can be learned by minimizing the negative log-likelihood:

-\!\!\iint\!\mathbf{p}\log\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}=-\frac{1}{\bm{\alpha}}\!\!\iint\!\mathbf{F}\log\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}
.
We drop
\tfrac{1}{\bm{\alpha}}
as it is only a constant factor that does not affect the gradient-descent direction, which yields the loss:

(5)

\small\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}(\bm{\theta}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})\!=-\!\!\iint\!\!\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})\log\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\omega}_{i}.

Comparing the right-hand side of
Eq.

5
with
Eq.

1
,
we can interpret
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}
as rendering the asset with
-\log\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}
acting as the "incident light" (can be positive or negative):

\displaystyle-\!\iint\!\!\mathbf{F}\log\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\omega}_{i}\!=\!\!\iint\!\!\mathbf{F}(-\log\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\omega}_{i}=\mathbf{L}_{o}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o};-\log\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}).

Using the fact that
Eq.

1
can be unbiasedly estimated through path tracing,
we can compute
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}
in a similar path-tracing style (
Fig.

2
):
the ray
(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})
is traced (path-sampled) for multiple bounces until leaving the asset,
whose exit configuration
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})
and throughput
\bm{\beta}_{i}
is then used to form the Monte Carlo estimator (
\mathbb{E}^{n}
denotes empirical expectation over
n
samples):

(6)

\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}(\bm{\theta}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})=\!\!\mathop{\mathbb{E}^{n}}_{\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i},\bm{\beta}_{i}\sim\text{path sampling}}\!\!\left[-\bm{\beta}_{i}\log\mathbf{p}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})\right].

The path-sampling probability does not need to equal
\mathbf{p}
since
\bm{\beta}_{i}
gives the correction,
and the throughput-based Russian roulette also seamlessly fits here to terminate low-throughput paths early,
for which the terminated paths simply contribute
\bm{\beta}_{i}=0
.

Albedo loss.

Under the same intuition, the albedo can be interpreted as rendering the asset under constant unit illumination:

\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})=\iint\!\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})\times\mathbf{1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}=\mathbf{L}_{o}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o};\mathbf{1})

which can be estimated by averaging the throughput
\bm{\alpha_{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})=\mathbb{E}^{n}[\bm{\beta}_{i}]
,
reusing the same samples for training
\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}
.
We fit
\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{\theta}}
against the estimated
\bm{\alpha}
using a simple L2 loss:

(7)

\begin{split}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{\theta}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})&=(\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})-\bm{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o}))^{2}\\
&=(\bm{\alpha_{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})-\mathbb{E}^{n}[\bm{\beta}_{i}])^{2},\end{split}

whose gradient is linear in
\bm{\alpha}
,
therefore the empirical expectation gives an unbiased gradient estimator.

Loss variance analysis.

Figure
3
shows an example where the regression objective
Eq.

3
requires thousands of samples to estimate
\mathbf{F}^{\prime}
for convergence,
whereas our model trained with one sample per outgoing ray already recovers
\mathbf{F}
.
This is because the regression gradient
2\!\int\!(\mathbf{F}^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta}}-\mathbf{F}^{\prime})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{F}^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}
lacks an efficient way to both sample
\bm{\omega}_{i}
and estimate
\mathbf{F}^{\prime}
for arbitrary
\bm{\omega}_{i}
,
making the optimization especially hard for highly specular light transport induced by dielectric boundaries, fibers, etc. In contrast, our gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}\!\propto\!\iint\!\mathbf{F}\nabla_{\theta}\log\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}
and
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\alpha}}\!\propto\!(\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{\theta}}-\!\iint\!\mathbf{F}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{\theta}}
are simple integrals against
\mathbf{F}
.
This is what is importance-sampled by the path-sampling procedure,
therefore
Eqs.

7
and
6
admit empirically low-variance gradient estimators suitable for stochastic optimization.
This sampling efficiency ensures our model is trainable under the 8D configuration.

Overall loss.

To optimize
\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{\alpha}
jointly across all outgoing configurations,
we integrate
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}+\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\alpha}}
over
\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o}
,
estimated by sampling random outgoing rays that intersect the asset (
Section

3.3
),
and sum the contributions over color channels
c
(superscripts denote
c
-th component of vectors):

(8)

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})=\!\!\sum_{\forall c}\mathop{\mathbb{E}^{n}}_{\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o}}\left[\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{p}}^{c}(\bm{\theta}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})\!+\!\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\alpha}}^{c}(\bm{\theta}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})\right].

Separating the direct scattering.

Translucent objects often have simple but highly specular dielectric boundaries whose direct scattering is much sharper than the rest of the light transport (
Fig.

4
).
To better capture the remaining complexity,
we keep the (cosine-weighted) direct BSDF
\mathbf{f}
untouched and train the network only on the indirect transport
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})\!-\!V(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i})
,
where
V
denotes the asset’s self-visibility.
This is implemented by using the same loss
Eq.

8
while setting
\bm{\beta}_{i}=0
for path samples that exit the asset after one scattering event.
At inference time,
V\mathbf{f}
is still evaluated analytically, which is usually cheap,
yet this direct-indirect separation leads to more accurate matching of the ground truth appearance.

3.2.
Realizing the distribution network

Parameterization of network inputs.

To obtain a Euclidean parameterization of
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})
,
we trace the incident ray further to its intersection
\mathbf{u}_{i}
on the asset’s axis-aligned bounding box (assume centered at origin),
inducing the transformed pdf involving the surface normal
\mathbf{n}_{\square}
(the subscript indicates the query location):

(9)

\begin{split}\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})\!=\!\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})\left|\frac{\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\cdot\bm{\omega}_{i}}{\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}\cdot\bm{\omega}_{i}}\right|.\end{split}

This is a valid one-to-one reparameterization as explained in
Fig.

5
;
other proxies such as a bounding sphere could be used similarly.
The proxy uv-map gives an general Euclidean parameterization of the intersection,
but for a bounding box,
we can simply encode
\mathbf{u}_{i}
and
\bm{\omega}_{i}
in the asset’s local space to their cylindrical coordinates:

(10)

\begin{gathered}\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})=\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})\frac{|\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{i}|}{(\sqrt{\mathbf{u}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{i}})^{3}},\\
\mathbf{s}=\left(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{3}/\sqrt{\mathbf{u}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{i}},\arctan(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{1}/\mathbf{u}_{i}^{2}),\bm{\omega}_{i}^{3},\arctan(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{1}/\bm{\omega}_{i}^{2})\right).\end{gathered}

We do not use spherical coordinates as they have zero Jacobian determinants at the poles which introduces singularities.
Derivations of the projection factors above are described in the supplement.

Figure 5.
Bounding-box parameterization of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
.

We map
\mathbf{x}_{i}
along
\bm{\omega}_{i}
to its bounding-box intersection
\mathbf{u}_{i}
and reparameterize
Eq.

1
accordingly.
While tracing the ray back from
\mathbf{u}_{i}
may hit multiple points (e.g.,
\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}
),
only the outermost intersection (
\mathbf{x}_{i}
) is visible to the incident light and lies in the original integral domain;
other points contribute only to internal scattering.
Thus, the mapping from
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})
to
(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})
is injective, assuming incident illumination originates outside the asset convex hull (
Section

4.4
).

Vector-valued normalizing flow.

Given the Euclidean parameter
\mathbf{s}
,
we model the scattering distribution with an autoregressive normalizing flow

\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})=\prod_{j=1}^{4}\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}^{j}|\mathbf{s}^{k<j},\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})
.
As demonstrated in
Fig.

6
,
each conditional factor is realized by an MLP-predicted rational quadratic spline (rqs)
(
Durkan et al. 2019
)
of knots
\mathbf{g}_{j}=\text{MLP}_{j,\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}^{k<j},\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})
,
used for both pdf evaluation and sampling per color channel
c
:

(11)

\begin{split}\quad\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}^{j}|\mathbf{s}^{k<j},\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})=\frac{\mathrm{d}\text{rqs}^{-1}(\mathbf{s}^{j},\mathbf{g}_{j}^{c})}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}^{j}}\quad&\text{(pdf evaluation)}\\
\mathbf{s}^{j}=\text{rqs}(u,\mathbf{g}_{j}^{c})\quad u\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)\quad&\text{(pdf sampling)}.\end{split}

To sample
\mathbf{s}
, we draw
\mathbf{s}^{1}
then
\mathbf{s}^{2}
conditioned on
\mathbf{s}^{1}
and so on,
and the full pdf evaluation is the product of the four derivatives .
Unlike standard normalizing flows that output a single scalar density,
our
\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}
is vector-valued over RGB, so each
\mathbf{g}_{j}
encodes three splines accordingly.
The autoregressive structure further implies a factorization of the distribution
\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i}|\cdot)=\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i}|\cdot)\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\omega}_{i}|\mathbf{u}_{i},\cdot)
, which we later exploit for multiple importance sampling (MIS) in
Section

3.3
.

.

3.3.
Training and inference

Network architecture.

Each of the 4 MLPs in the distribution network uses ReLU activations,
4 hidden layers, and 128 hidden features.
The
j
-th MLP takes
(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\mathbf{s}^{1},\cdots,\mathbf{s}^{j-1})
as input and predicts the 2D knot positions and derivatives of a rational quadratic spline.
We use 32 spline knots per RGB channel, resulting in
3\times 32\times(2+1)=288
output size.
The albedo MLP takes
(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})
as input and outputs an RGB color.
It also uses ReLU activations and 128 hidden features but with 2 hidden layers.

Input encoding.

To capture high-frequency details,

\mathbf{x}_{o}
in all networks is encoded with a
3\times 64\times 64
triplane feature grid
(
Chan et al. 2022
)
,
and
\bm{\omega}_{o}
is encoded with a
6\times 32\times 32
cubemap feature grid
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
.
In the distribution network,

\mathbf{s}^{1},\mathbf{s}^{3}
in the first and third MLPs are encoded with a 1D feature grid of resolution 32.
For the third and fourth MLPs, the pair
(\mathbf{s}^{1},\mathbf{s}^{2})
is first mapped to a unit direction then encoded with a
6\times 32\times 32
cubemap grid.
All the encodings use feature vectors of size 8 per grid vertex.

Training data generation.

We optimize
Eq.

8
using a buffer of path-sampled tuples
(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i},\bm{\beta}_{i})
that are regenerated once being fully consumed.
Each tuple is obtained by first uniformly sampling a point on the asset’s bounding sphere then cosine-weighted sampling
\bm{\omega}_{o}
in the local tangent frame.
We trace this ray to find the first intersection
\mathbf{x}_{o}
and continue path tracing to obtain
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i},\bm{\beta}_{i})
.
Tuples whose initial ray misses the asset are excluded from the training.
Compared to precomputing the training data
(
Mullia et al. 2024
)
,
the online sampling prevents overfitting to a static set of paths.
The buffer stores
128^{4}
such tuples in 15GB RAM.

Optimization.

Our code is implemented using Pytorch
(
Paszke et al. 2019
)
and Mitsuba 3
(
Jakob et al. 2022
)
.
All networks are trained with the Adam optimizer
(
Kingma and Ba 2014
)
at a
0.0005
learning rate for 240K steps, using a batch size of 32768 samples per step.
This leads to
(240\text{K}\times 32768)/128^{4}\!=\!30
buffer refreshes.

Integrating with path tracing.

Path tracing with our model follows a standard emitter-path sampling scheme (
Fig.

7
):
each path-tracing bounce begins by sampling an incident location
\mathbf{u}_{i}\!\sim\!\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})
using the pdf factorization in
Section

3.2
over a uniformly sampled color channel
c
;
MIS is then performed at
\mathbf{u}_{i}
between the emitter-sampling technique and
\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}^{c}(\bm{\omega}_{i}|\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})
.
When
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{\theta}}
is trained on the indirect scattering,
we also emitter-sample the direct lobe
\mathbf{f}
at
\mathbf{x}_{o}
and stochastically choose between a direct path sample and a neural asset sample as the next-bounce ray.
Details of our path tracing is provided in the supplemental material.
We use a custom Mitsuba integrator for path tracing and a CUDA kernel for the pdf sampling and evaluation (
Eq.

11
).
We did not find a benefit of writing CUDA kernels for the MLPs given their relatively large sizes.

For each asset in

The rendering variance is estimated by the MSE between the rendering and a high-spp rendering of the same method,

We also measure the time required to prebake each asset.
All images are rendered in

Far-field.

We model the 6D
\mathbf{F}^{\prime}
with an MLP of 128 hidden units and 8 hidden layers trained over the regression loss
Eq.

3

and we learn the importance sampling of
\bm{\omega}_{i}

Xu et al. 2023

4.4.
Limitations

We pre-bake light transport for each asset in isolation,
so the internal scattering is essentially bounded by the asset’s convex hull.
When inserting the asset into a new scene,
our model remains accurate only if the internal scattering structure does not change.
This means no other objects can intersect with the asset’s convex hull (
Fig.

14
).
Applying convex decomposition to the asset then pre-baking each component separately can resolve this issue.
Meanwhile, we currently do not use MIS over
\mathbf{x}_{i}
,
which may lead to high rendering variance under small emitters like point lights.
Sampling incident locations visible to the emitters may help.

The usage of normalizing flows also introduces limitations.
On the one hand, the normalizing flow evaluation is less efficient than MLP,
so an MLP-based light transport model remains preferable if the far-field approximation is sufficiently accurate.
On the other hand,
normalizing flows have an inductive bias toward smooth distributions,
making it difficult to reproduce high-frequency phenomena,
such as specular interreflections between complex shapes or caustics and glints that lie in a sub-manifold of the incident domain.

5.
Conclusion and Future Work

We have demonstrated an 8D neural representation that learns global light transport inside 3D assets from forward path-traced samples.
This distribution-learning framework provides low-variance gradient estimates for optimizing our light transport model,
while the 8D parameterization accurately pre-bakes internal scattering even under near-field illumination.
Together, our method noticeably reduces rendering variance and time compared to standard path tracing while preserving high visual fidelity.
Looking forward, the model has the potential to deliver fast and accurate global illumination in real-time applications.

Appendix A
Additional Derivations

A.1.
Isotropic assumption of NeuralSSS

NeuralSSS
(
Tg et al. 2024b
)
utilizes an MLP
\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}

to predict the subsurface scattering at
(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})
given the input of
N
incident rays
(\mathbf{x}_{n},\bm{\omega}_{n})
with radiance
\mathbf{L}_{n}=\mathbf{L}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{n},\bm{\omega}_{n})
.
To train the network,

Tg et al. 2024b
first randomly sample
(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})
queries then path-sample
N
sub-rays for each outgoing ray,
recording their configuration
(\mathbf{x}_{n},\bm{\omega}_{n})
and throughput
\bm{\beta}_{n}
when leaving the subsurface media;
note this is different from our sampling that records the incident configuration when leaving the entire asset.
Each sub-ray is further paired with a random incident radiance value
\mathbf{L}_{n}
to compute the loss:

(12)

\left(\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\{\mathbf{x}_{n},\bm{\omega}_{n},\mathbf{L}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bm{\beta}_{n}\mathbf{L}_{n}\right)^{2}.

Unlike the far-field regression loss in Eq. (3) of the paper,

Eq.

12
here uses the same
(\mathbf{x}_{n},\bm{\omega}_{n})
for network queries and ground truth estimation.
This correlation deviates
\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}
from learning the true outgoing radiance.
Let
q(\mathbf{x}_{n},\bm{\omega}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})=q_{n}
denote the marginal distribution of path-sampled incident ray (note the path-sampling distribution may differ from the scattering distribution
\mathbf{p}
) and assume
N=1
,
the expectation of the loss gradient above is:

\displaystyle 2\iint q_{n}\left(\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\beta}_{n}\mathbf{L}_{n}\right)\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{d}\bm{\omega}_{n}

\displaystyle=2\iint q_{n}\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{d}\bm{\omega}_{n}-2\iint q_{n}\bm{\beta}_{n}\mathbf{L}_{n}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{d}\bm{\omega}_{n}.

The second integral equals
\mathbf{L}_{o}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o};\mathbf{L}_{i}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}})
as discussed in Sec. 3.1 of the paper;
writing it in the global light transport form (Eq. (1) of the paper) gives:

(13)

\begin{gathered}2\iint q_{n}\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{d}\bm{\omega}_{n}-2\iint\mathbf{F}_{n}\mathbf{L}_{n}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{d}\bm{\omega}_{n}\\
=2\iint q_{n}\left(\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}-\frac{\mathbf{F}_{n}\mathbf{L}_{n}}{q_{n}}\right)\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{d}\bm{\omega}_{n}\\
=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\iint q_{n}\left(\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}-\frac{\mathbf{F}_{n}\mathbf{L}_{n}}{q_{n}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{d}\bm{\omega}_{n},\;\mathbf{F}_{n}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i}).\end{gathered}

This suggest
\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}
regresses towards the estimator
\frac{\mathbf{F}_{n}\mathbf{L}_{n}}{q_{n}}
rather than the estimator expectation (outgoing radiance) at convergence.
A similar derivation can be made for arbitrary
N
,
where the network learns
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bm{\beta}_{n}\mathbf{L}_{n}
.
Therefore, it is the expectation of
\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}
under joint probability
q_{1}\cdots q_{n}
that estimates the outgoing radiance:

(14)

\begin{split}\int\!\!\cdots\!\!\int&\prod_{n=1}^{N}q_{n}\mathbf{S}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\{\mathbf{x}_{n},\bm{\omega}_{n},\mathbf{L}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{1}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{n}\\
&=\int\!\!\cdots\!\!\int\prod_{n=1}^{N}q_{n}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\mathbf{F}_{n}\mathbf{L}_{n}}{q_{n}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{1}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{n}\\
\\
&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\iint\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\mathbf{x}_{n},\bm{\omega}_{n})\mathbf{L}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{n},\bm{\omega}_{n})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{n}\\
&=\iint\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathbf{L}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}\end{split}

In order to estimate the integral of
\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{\theta}}
in
Eq.

14
,
a sampling strategy is needed to approximate the path sampling distribution
q
.

Tg et al. 2024b
learn to sample the marginal
q(\mathbf{x}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})=\int q(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}

using a normalizing flow
q_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})

but sample
\bm{\omega}_{i}
only by cosine-hemisphere sampling.
Such integral estimator is accurate only if the conditional distribution
q(\bm{\omega}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})
is cosine-hemisphere,
implying the subsurface scattering is nearly isotropic under an infinite slab.
Note
q(\bm{\omega}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})
also depends on the boundary shape of subsurface scattering,
so even if we use cosine-hemisphere path sampling for training,
the conditional distribution will still not be cosine-hemisphere as long as the boundary is not an infinite slab.
Constructing a cosine-hemisphere
q(\bm{\omega}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})
over arbitrary boundary shape is generally difficult.

A.2.
Projection factors of the reparameterizations

We use the fact that
p(\mathbf{x})\!=\!p(\mathbf{y})\frac{|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}|}{|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}|}
for a distribution
p(\mathbf{x})
and its reparameterization
\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})
.
The area of
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}_{i}
and
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{i}
projected along direction
\bm{\omega}_{i}
are equal, so we have:

(15)

\begin{split}|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}_{i}||\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}\cdot\bm{\omega}_{i}|=|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{i}||\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}\cdot\bm{\omega}_{i}|\Rightarrow\frac{|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}_{i}|}{|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{i}|}=\frac{|\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\cdot\bm{\omega}_{i}|}{|\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}\cdot\bm{\omega}_{i}|}.\end{split}

\mathbf{u}_{i}
on the zero-centered bounding box can be converted to solid angle domain by
\bm{\omega}=\frac{\mathbf{u}_{i}}{\sqrt{\mathbf{u}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{i}}}
with
\frac{|\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}|}{|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}_{i}|}=\frac{|\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}\cdot\bm{\omega}|}{\mathbf{u}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{i}}
,
and since projection factor between a solid angle and cylindrical coordinate is always 1, we have:

(16)

\frac{|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}|}{|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}|}=\frac{|\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}|}{|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}_{i}|}=\frac{|\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}\cdot\bm{\omega}|}{\mathbf{u}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{i}}=\frac{|\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{i}|}{\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{u}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{i}}\right)^{3}}.

A.3.
Path tracing details

We describe the algorithm in the context of a surface-based path tracer, though the same idea extends naturally to volumetric path tracing.
For each camera ray, we perform standard path tracing for multiple bounces,
accumulating pixel radiance
\mathbf{L}
and throughput
\bm{\beta}
.
When the ray hits a neural asset,
the total contribution to the pixel can be expressed as
\mathbf{L}+\bm{\beta}\mathbf{L}_{o}
, where
\mathbf{L}_{o}
is given by Eq. 1 of the paper.
We reparameterize
\mathbf{x}_{i}
to
\mathbf{u}_{i}
,
so that the resulting
\mathbf{u}_{i}
-integral can be estimated by sampling
\mathbf{u}_{i}\sim\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})=\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}
over a random color channel
c
(
\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o}
are omitted for simplicity):

(17)

\begin{split}\mathbf{L}_{o}&=\iint\!\!\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathbf{L}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}\sim\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}}\left[\int\frac{\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})}{\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{i})}\mathbf{L}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}\right]\\
&\quad\quad\quad\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})=\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})\left|\tfrac{\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}\cdot\bm{\omega}_{i}}{\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\cdot\bm{\omega}_{i}}\right|.\end{split}

The incident radiance
\mathbf{L}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})
is decomposed into surface emission
\mathbf{L}_{e}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime},-\bm{\omega}_{i})
and outgoing radiance
\mathbf{L}_{o}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i},-\bm{\omega}_{i})
,
where
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime}
is the surface intersection of ray
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})
:

(18)

\begin{split}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}\sim\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}}&\left[\int\frac{\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})}{\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{i})}\mathbf{L}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}\right]\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}\sim\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}}\left[\int\frac{\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})}{\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{i})}\mathbf{L}_{e}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i},-\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}\right]\\
&+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}\sim\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}}\left[\int\frac{\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})}{\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{i})}\mathbf{L}_{o}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i},-\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}\right].\end{split}

The first integral is estimated using MIS between an emitter-sampling distribution (in solid angle domain)
p_{e}(\bm{\omega}_{i})
and our path-sampling distribution
\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\theta}}^{c}(\bm{\omega}_{i}|\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o})=\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\bm{\omega}_{i}}
:

(19)

\begin{split}&\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}\sim\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}}\left[\int\frac{\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})}{\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{i})}\mathbf{L}_{e}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i},-\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}\right]\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}\sim\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}},\bm{\omega}_{i}\sim p_{e}}\left[w_{emit}(\bm{\omega}_{i})\frac{\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})}{\mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}^{c}(\mathbf{u}_{i})p_{e}(\bm{\omega}_{i})}\mathbf{L}_{e}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime},-\bm{\omega}_{i})\right]\\
&+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}\sim\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}},\bm{\omega}_{i}\sim\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\bm{\omega}_{i}}}\left[w_{path}(\bm{\omega}_{i})\frac{\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})}{\mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}^{c}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\bm{\omega}_{i}}(\bm{\omega}_{i})}\mathbf{L}_{e}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i},-\bm{\omega}_{i})\right]\\
&=\mathbf{L}_{emit}+\mathbf{L}_{path}.\end{split}

We include the emitter visibility to
\mathbf{L}_{e}
and use the power heuristic
(
Veach and Guibas 1995
)
with weights
w_{emit}=\frac{p_{e}^{2}}{p_{e}^{2}+(\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\bm{\omega}_{i}})^{2}}
,
w_{path}=1-w_{emit}
.
The remaining integral is estimated using the same path-sampled direction,
with
\mathbf{L}_{o}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime},\bm{\omega}_{i})
further traced in the next bounce:

(20)

\begin{split}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}\sim\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}}&\left[\int\frac{\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})}{\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{i})}\mathbf{L}_{o}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i},-\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}\right]=\mathbf{L}_{next}\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}\sim\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}},\bm{\omega}_{i}\sim\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\bm{\omega}_{i}}}\left[\frac{\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})}{\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\bm{\omega}_{i}}(\bm{\omega}_{i})}\mathbf{L}_{o}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i},-\bm{\omega}_{i})\right].\end{split}

dAs in standard path tracing,

\mathbf{L}_{emit}
is evaluated at the current bounce and accumulated as
\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{L}+\bm{\beta}\mathbf{L}_{emit}
.
The evaluation of
\mathbf{L}_{path}
is deferred:
we update the throughput
\bm{\beta}\leftarrow\bm{\beta}\times\frac{\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})}{\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\bm{\omega}_{i}}(\bm{\omega}_{i})}
,
project
\mathbf{u}_{i}
back to the asset boundary to obtain
\mathbf{x}_{i}
,
and continue tracing the ray
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})
;
at the next bounce, we update the radiance with the path contribution
\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{L}+w_{path}(\bm{\omega}_{i})\bm{\beta}\mathbf{L}_{e}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime},-\bm{\omega}_{i})
.

Modifications with direct-scattering separation.

When
\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}
only models indirect scattering,
the outgoing radiance at
\mathbf{x}_{o}
becomes:

(21)

\begin{split}\mathbf{L}_{o}&=\!\iint\!\!\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathbf{L}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}\\
&+\!\int\!\!\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})V(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})\mathbf{L}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime},\end{split}

where the second integral can be estimated in a similar way without needing to sample
\mathbf{u}_{i}
:

(22)

\begin{split}\int&\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})V(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})\mathbf{L}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime}\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime}\sim p_{e}}\left[w_{emit}(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})\frac{\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})}{p_{e}(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})}V(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})\mathbf{L}_{e}(\mathbf{x}_{o}^{\prime},-\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})\right]\\
&+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime}\sim p_{\mathbf{f}}}\left[w_{path}(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})\frac{\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})}{p_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})}V(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})\mathbf{L}_{e}(\mathbf{x}_{o}^{\prime},-\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})\right]\\
&+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime}\sim p_{\mathbf{f}}}\left[\frac{\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})}{p_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})}V(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})\mathbf{L}_{o}(\mathbf{x}_{o}^{\prime},-\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})\right]\\
&=\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{emit}+\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{path}+\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{next}.\end{split}

\mathbf{x}_{o}^{\prime}
is the surface intersection of ray
(\mathbf{x}_{o}^{\prime},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})
,
and
p_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})
denotes the path-sampling distribution of
\mathbf{f}
.
We use
\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime}
to emphasis this direction is sampled independently of the neural asset sample
\bm{\omega}_{i}
.

\mathbf{L}_{emit}^{\prime}
can be simply added to the radiance at the current bounce:
\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{L}+\bm{\beta}\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{emit}
.
However,
\mathbf{L}_{path}+\mathbf{L}_{next}
and
\mathbf{L}_{path}^{\prime}+\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{next}
cannot be evaluated simultaneously as only one path sample can be carried to the next bounce.
Instead, we draw a direct sample
(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})
and an indirect sample
(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})
then stochastically choose to continue tracing
\mathbf{L}_{path}^{\prime}+\mathbf{L}_{next}^{\prime}
with probability
m(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})
or with
\mathbf{L}_{path}+\mathbf{L}_{next}
otherwise.
This leads to a modified throughput update rule:

(23)

\bm{\beta}\leftarrow\bm{\beta}\times\begin{cases}\frac{1}{m(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})}\frac{\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})V(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})}{p_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})}&\text{if select }(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime})\\
\frac{1}{1-m(\mathbf{x}_{o},\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})}\frac{\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u}_{i},\bm{\omega}_{i})}{\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\mathbf{p}^{c}_{\bm{\omega}_{i}}(\bm{\omega}_{i})}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

where
\frac{1}{m}
and
\frac{1}{1-m}
are the correction factors of the stochastic selection.
We want to avoid choosing direct-path samples that are occluded and thus contribute nothing to
\mathbf{L}
.
Therefore,
m
is defined to be proportional to
V
by
m\!=\!\tfrac{V\mathbf{f}^{c}/p_{\mathbf{f}}}{V\mathbf{f}^{c}/p_{\mathbf{f}}+\mathbf{F}_{\bm{\theta}}^{c}/\mathbf{p}_{\bm{\omega}_{i}}^{c}}
,
so that samples with zero self-visibility are never selected.

GPU memory usage.

Since we generate training data online,
the GPU memory required during training depends on asset complexity,
which is around 6.8 GB for the volumetric assets, 5.3 GB for
Teaset
, and 7.2-11.4 GB for fiber-based assets (
CulrHair
,
Hair
,
Fabric
).
The network optimization uses around 4.5 GB GPU memory excluding the dataset generation.
Table
7
reports the GPU memory usage during inference.
Overall, all methods use similar GPU memory, but our method consumes slightly more memory due to the usage of more neural networks.
