Long-range interactions assisted shortcuts to adiabaticity and battery charging in open quantum critical systems
Shishira Mahunta, Victor Mukherjee

Abstract
In this work we show that long-range interactions can be significantly beneficial for implementing shortcuts to adiabaticity (STA) in many-body open quantum critical systems driven out of equilibrium, as well as for charging quantum batteries in the presence of dissipation. In sharp contrast to short range interactions where passage through criticality may demand STA control with non-zero interactions between infinitely distant spins, using the example of a Kitaev chain with long-range couplings, we find that the corresponding control may involve involve interaction strength with decays algebraically with distance. In case of non-unitary control, the advantage of long-range interactions manifest through reduction in the cost of STA. We further propose a modified STA technique aimed at charging a quantum battery in the presence of dissipation, in which case long-range interactions may enhance the resultant ergotropy. Our results establish long-range interactions as a valuable resource for quantum control, with direct implications for quantum technologies.



I
Introduction

Quantum systems driven out of equilibrium form a versatile area of research
Schaller 2014
, owing to their fundamental nature, as well as their for potential applications in quantum technologies
Bhattacharjee and Dutta 2020
;
Campbell et al. 2026
and condensed matter physics
Jacek 2010
. Recent progress in experimental research has resulted in significant advancements in our ability to control the dynamics of quantum systems for various tasks
Würkner et al. 2025
, including for preparation of desired quantum states
Müller et al. 2022
;
Ou et al. 2025
, for engineering novel phases of matter such as discrete
Sarkar et al. 2026
and continuous
Kongkhambut et al. 2022
time crystals, and for designing quantum technologies
Koch et al. 2023
. However, quantum systems driven out of equilibrium are in general associated with non-adiabatic excitations
Dutta et al. 2015
. This can be detrimental, for example, for quantum annealing
Rajak et al. 2022
, or for modeling high-performing quantum technologies, including quantum computers
Hegade et al. 2021
and quantum engines
Hartmann et al. 2020a
. Consequently, studies on control in quantum systems, such as through shortcuts to adiabaticity (STA)
Torrontegui et al. 2013
, in order to reduce non-adiabatic excitations in driven quantum systems, are crucial for designing high-performing quantum technologies
Hartmann et al. 2020a
.

STA has been studied rigorously in closed quantum systems, where this technique may involve applying a control counterdiabatic (CD) Hamiltonian in order to ensure that the system remains in the instantaneous ground state of a time-dependent bare Hamiltonian at all times
Berry 2009
. On the other hand, studies on STA in open quantum systems have received far less attention. Yet, their importance cannot be overstated, owing to the ubiquitous presence of dissipation in nature, and also due to the relevance of open quantum systems driven out of equilibrium in quantum condensed matter physics
Schnell et al. 2024
and quantum technologies
Cangemi et al. 2024
. STA in open quantum systems can entail designing a control protocol such that the system follows a particular target eigenstate of the Liouvillian
Vacanti et al. 2014
. Alternatively, control can be desgined to ensure that the system remains in it’s instantaneous thermal equilibrium corresponding to a particular temperature at all times, even in the presence of a time-dependent Hamiltonian
Alipour et al. 2020
. As recently reported in Ref.
Mahunta and Mukherjee 2025
, in contrast to STA in unitary dynamics where this protocol may involve many-body control terms only close to criticality
del Campo et al. 2012
, in the case of many-body open quantum systems control of entropy may result in the STA protocol involving many-body interacting control terms even away from criticality. Therefore rigorous studies on practically implementable STA protocols in many-body open quantum systems are crucial for designing near-term quantum technologies. To this end, in this work we study STA in a long-range Kitaev (LRK) model driven through quantum critical points, in the presence of a Fermionic bath.
Our main motivation is to study whether the complexity of control can be controlled by tuning the range of interaction present in the bare Hamilotnian. Notably, we show that in sharp contrast to STA in the presence of nearest neighbor interactions where the unitary control terms may involve infinite-range interactions at the critical point
del Campo et al. 2012
, here long-range interactions (LRIs) result in a CD Hamiltonian involving interaction strength which decays algebraically with distance. The same can contribute to reduction of the cost of STA in the case of non-unitary control.
We further use the protocol developed here to model a quantum battery in the presence of dissipation. Importantly, we show that depending on the inherent critical features of the model, LRIs may assist in enhancing the ergotropy of the quantum battery.

Notably, LRIs have attracted significant attention in recent years
Defenu et al. 2019
, and have been used for studying STA in closed quantum systems
Campbell et al. 2015
, for designing high-performing quantum engines
Solfanelli et al. 2023a
, quantum batteries
Puri et al. 2025
, and have also found to be highly relevant for designing discrete
Russomanno et al. 2017
and continuous
Kozin and Kyriienko 2019
time crystals in the absence of disorder.

II
Model and dynamics

II.1
STA in generic long-range interacting systems

We now consider a quench across a quantum critical point in the presence of LRIs, with the interaction range controllable by a tuning parameter
\alpha
. In general, one can expect the corresponding energy gap
E(\alpha)
to be a function of the parameter
\alpha
as well, such that
E(\alpha)\to 0
close to criticality
Sachdev 1999
. This in turn leads to non-adiabatic excitations in quantum critical systems driven out of equilibrium
Dutta et al. 2015
, thus necessitating the implementation of STA control protocols
del Campo et al. 2012
. In general, the STA control terms can be expected to be functions of
E(\alpha)
(see below, and also Refs.
Berry 2009
and
Alipour et al. 2020
), with the unitary control strength decreasing with increasing energy gaps
Takahashi 2013
. Consequently, we expect setups where one can tune
E(\alpha)
by varying
\alpha
can be ideal for implementation of practically realizable STA protocols, through appropriate choices of the parameter
\alpha
.

We exemplify the above using a detailed analysis of a LRK chain driven across quantum critical points. Importantly, a LRK chain is associated with two critical points with significantly different dispersion relations. In one case
E(\alpha)
increases with increasing range of interactions (i.e., decreasing
\alpha
). As we discuss below, this allows us to implement STA with less demanding unitary control fields, through suitable choices of
\alpha
. On the other hand, the other critical point is associated with
E(\alpha)
increasing with decreasing range of interactions (i.e., increasing
\alpha
), which can negate the advantages associated with LRIs.
In addition, we show that the above property of the LRK chain can also enable us to model a quantum battery with high ergotropy for longer ranges of interactions, through population inversion implemented by a modified STA protocol.

II.2
Long-range Kitaev chain

We consider a LRK chain comprising spinless fermions on a one–dimensional lattice with long-range coupling. The Hamiltonian is given by
Dutta and Dutta 2017
;
Defenu et al. 2019
;
Vodola et al. 2014

\displaystyle H_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathcal{J}\sum_{j}\left(c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j+1}+\text{h.c.}\right)-\mu(t)\sum_{j}\left(n_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\right)

(1)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{|i-j|^{\alpha}}\left(c_{i}c_{j}+c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}\right).

Here
c_{j}^{\dagger}
(
c_{j}
) are Fermionic creation (annihilation) operators at site
j
, and
n_{j}=c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j}
denotes the number operator. The parameter
\mathcal{J}
represents the nearest-neighbor hopping amplitude, while
\mu(t)
is the time-dependent chemical potential. We consider an interaction which decays algebraically with the distance between sites
i
and
j
, with an exponent
\alpha>0
;
\alpha\to\infty
limit corresponds to nearest neighbor interactions in which case LRK chain reduces to the transverse field Ising chain
Vodola et al. 2014
;
Vodola et al. 2016
, while smaller values of
\alpha
corresponding to enhancement in the range of interactions.
Introducing the momentum-space Fermionic operators

c_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{k}e^{-ikj}c_{k}

with antiperiodic boundary conditions
c_{j+L}=-c_{j}
, the Hamiltonian decouples into independent momentum sectors. In the Nambu basis:
\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}=(c_{k}^{\dagger},c_{-k})
, the Hamiltonian assumes the Bogoliubov–de Gennes form,

\displaystyle H_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}H_{0k}\Psi_{k},

(2)

with
H_{0k}(t)=\left[(\mu+2\mathcal{J}\cos k)\sigma^{z}+i\,\,f_{\alpha}(k)\,\sigma^{x}\right]
, and
f_{\alpha}(k)
denotes the Fourier transform of the long-range pairing function:

f_{\alpha}(k)=\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ilk}-e^{-ilk}}{l^{\alpha}}=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(lk)}{l^{\alpha}}.

(3)

In the occupation-number basis

\{|0_{k},0_{-k}\rangle,|0_{k},1_{-k}\rangle,|1_{k},0_{-k}\rangle,|1_{k},1_{-k}\rangle\}
one can write

\displaystyle H_{0k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}(2\mathcal{J}\cos k+\mu)&0&0&if_{\alpha}(k)\\
0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0\\
-if_{\alpha}(k)&0&0&-(2\mathcal{J}\cos k+\mu)\end{bmatrix}.

(4)

We note that while the unitary dynamics couples only the even-parity subspace, the inclusion of dissipation necessitates working in the full four-dimensional basis
Keck et al. 2017
;
Bandyopadhyay et al. 2018
.
Diagonalizing Eq. (
4
) via a Bogoliubov transformation yields the quasiparticle spectrum

\tilde{E}_{k}^{(1,4)}=\pm\sqrt{(2\mathcal{J}\cos k+\mu)^{2}+(f_{\alpha}(k))^{2}}=\pm E_{k}(\alpha),

(5)

while the remaining two eigenvalues satisfy
\tilde{E}_{k}^{(2)}=\tilde{E}_{k}^{(3)}=0
.
In order to have non diverging energy spectrum in k-space, we only consider
\alpha>1

Dutta and Dutta 2017
;
Defenu et al. 2019
.
As seen from Eq. (
5
), the energy gap vanishes for the modes
k=\pi
and
k=0
at the critical points
\mu=2\mathcal{J}
and
\mu=-2\mathcal{J}
, respectively

.
Throughout this work we set
\mathcal{J}=1/2
, the Planck constant
\hbar
and the Boltzmann constant
k_{B}
are set to unity, and the chemical potential
\mu(t)=\frac{t}{\tau}
is varied linearly in time
with a quench rate
\tau
.

We first focus on the long-wavelength behavior of the quasiparticle dispersion
E_{k}(\alpha)
near the critical points
\mu=\pm 1
. Close to
\mu=-1
, the Taylor expansion of
E_{k}(\alpha)
around
k\to 0
leads to,

E_{k}(\alpha,\mu\to-1)\approx\begin{cases}\sqrt{\epsilon_{-}^{2}+A_{\alpha}^{2}k^{2(\alpha-1)}},&1<\alpha<2,\\[5.69046pt]
\sqrt{\epsilon_{-}^{2}+B_{\alpha}^{2}k^{2}},&\alpha>2,\end{cases}

(6)

where
\epsilon_{-}=\mu+1
denotes the distance from the criticality,

A_{\alpha}=2\left|\cos\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)\Gamma(1-\alpha)\right|
and

B_{\alpha}=\zeta(\alpha-1)
. Here,
\Gamma(x)
and
\zeta(x)
denote the Gamma and Riemann zeta functions, respectively.
Owing to the change in the form of
E_{k}(\alpha)
at
\alpha=2
(see Eq. (
6
)), and following Refs.
Dutta and Dutta 2017
;
Defenu et al. 2019
, below we refer to

as the long-range regime, while

A
for details).

Close to
\mu=+1
, we get

E_{k}(\alpha,\mu\to+1)\approx\sqrt{\epsilon_{+}^{2}+\tilde{B}_{\alpha}^{2}q^{2}},

(7)

for both long-range and short-range regimes. Here
\epsilon_{+}=\mu-1
,
q=\pi-k
and
\tilde{B}_{\alpha}=\eta(\alpha-1)
, with
\eta(s)
denoting the Dirichlet eta function. Further, in sharp contrast to the case of
\mu\to-1
where

increases with decreasing
\alpha
, here
\tilde{B}_{\alpha}
, and hence

, monotonically increases with increasing

for

and approaches

as

. This suggests qualitative differences between the dynamics of system close to the two critical points, as we verify below through rigorous analysis.

We assume that the setup is coupled to a Fermionic bath

, such that an adiabatic dynamics with

results in each momentum mode remaining in its instantaneous thermal state

\displaystyle\rho_{k}^{\mathrm{th}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{k}(t)|n^{k}_{t}\rangle\langle n^{k}_{t}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\frac{e^{-\beta E_{k}(\alpha)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}&0&0&0\\

0&0&\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}&0\\
0&0&0&\frac{e^{\beta E_{k}(\alpha)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}\end{bmatrix}

.
Here

are the instantaneous eigenstates of

,

\lambda_{n}^{k}=e^{-\beta\tilde{E}_{n}(k)}/\mathcal{Z}_{k}
, and
\mathcal{Z}_{k}=2+e^{-\beta E_{k}}+e^{\beta E_{k}}

4
) and (

)).

In general a quench with a finite

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{k}[\rho(t)]=-i\left[H^{k}_{\rm STA},\rho_{k}(t)\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n}\gamma^{(k)}_{mn}\left(A^{(k)}_{mn}\rho_{k}A_{mn}^{(k)\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{A_{mn}^{(k)\dagger}A^{(k)}_{mn},\rho_{k}\}\right)

\displaystyle=

(9)

Here

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\phi_{t}|1_{k},1_{-k}\rangle+|0_{k},0_{-k}\rangle}{\sqrt{|\phi_{t}|^{2}+1}},

\displaystyle|\xi^{k}_{2}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|1_{k},0_{-k}\rangle,

\displaystyle|\xi^{k}_{3}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|0_{k},1_{-k}\rangle,

Further, the free Fermionic nature of the system allow us to write

\displaystyle\rho(t)=\bigotimes_{k}\rho_{k}(t),

such that

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{k1}\bigotimes_{k\neq k_{1}}\rho_{k}+\rho_{k1}\otimes\dot{\rho}_{k2}\bigotimes_{k\neq k_{1},k_{2}}\rho_{k}+\ldots

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\mathcal{L}_{k}\left[\rho\right]=\mathcal{L}\left[\rho\right].

III
CD drive across a QCP with LRIs

Now we investigate the effect of LRIs on
H_{CD}^{k}
(Cf. Eq. (
10
)).
Following the standard construction
of transitionless driving for free-fermionic systems exhibiting quantum
phase transitions
Berry 2009
;
del Campo et al. 2012
, the corresponding

H_{CD}^{k}
in the momentum space can be written as (see Appendix
B
)

H_{\rm CD}^{k}(t)=-\dot{\mu}(t)\sum_{k}\frac{1}{2}\frac{f_{\alpha}(k)}{(\mu+\cos k)^{2}+f_{\alpha}^{2}(k)}\,\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}\sigma^{y}\Psi_{k}.

(17)

Fourier transforming Eq. (
17
) to real space yields

\displaystyle H_{\rm CD}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{\mu}(t)\sum_{m\geq 1}h_{m}(\mu,\alpha)\,\hat{O}_{m},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{where}

\displaystyle\quad\hat{O}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}i\left(c_{j}c_{j+m}-c_{j+m}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle h_{m}(\mu,\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}dk\;\frac{f_{\alpha}(k)\sin(mk)}{(\mu+\cos k)^{2}+f_{\alpha}^{2}(k)}.

(18)

Here
\hat{O}_{m}
denotes the operators acting on sites separated by a distance
m
, while
h_{m}(\mu,\alpha)
denotes the strength of the associated CD field.
In the short-range limit
\alpha\to\infty
, the pairing function reduces to

and Eq. (
17
) reproduces the nearest-neighbor transverse-field Ising model results derived in Ref.

. In that limit, the CD coefficients decay exponentially with distance,

h_{m}(\mu)\sim e^{-m/\xi(\mu)}

with
\xi(\mu)
being the correlation length of transverse-field Ising model. However, as we show below, the long-range structure of

may lead to a qualitatively different spatial structure of the CD field, especially near criticality.

III.1
CD drive across the critical point
\mu=-1

III.1.1
Long-range regime (

We first analyze the behavior of the CD coupling coefficient

near the critical point

Using Eqs. (

h_{m}(\epsilon_{-},\alpha)\approx\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{A_{\alpha}k^{\alpha-1}}{\epsilon_{-}^{2}+A_{\alpha}^{2}k^{2(\alpha-1)}}\,\sin(mk)\,dk.

(19)

(i) Away from criticality
(\epsilon_{-}\neq 0)
:
Introducing a scaling variable

and performing the
change of variable

, Eq. (

\displaystyle h_{m}(\epsilon_{-},\alpha)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{4\pi}\,A_{\alpha}^{-1/(\alpha-1)}|\epsilon_{-}|^{(2-\alpha)/(\alpha-1)}\,\Phi_{\alpha}\!\left(\frac{m}{m_{\epsilon}}\right),

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad\Phi_{\alpha}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\;\frac{x^{\alpha-1}}{1+x^{2(\alpha-1)}}\sin(yx).

(20)

The asymptotic behavior of
h_{m}
follows from the limits of the scaling function
\Phi_{\alpha}(y)
.
For distances much longer than the crossover scale (
y\gg 1
or
m\gg m_{\epsilon}
), the factor

becomes rapidly

and the integral is dominated by the small-

\Phi_{\alpha}(y)\approx\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,\,\,x^{\alpha-1}\sin(yx)

Therefore we finally get

\lim_{m\gg m_{\epsilon}}h_{m}(\epsilon_{-},\alpha)\approx\frac{A_{\alpha}\,\Gamma(\alpha)\sin(\pi\alpha/2)}{4\pi}\;\frac{1}{\epsilon_{-}^{2}}\ \frac{1}{m^{\alpha}}.

On the other hand, for the distance much smaller than the crossover scale (

\displaystyle\lim_{m\ll m_{\epsilon}}h_{m}(\epsilon_{-},\alpha)

(ii) At the criticality

Charging a quantum battery through controlled dissipation

Till now we have focussed on application of STA to drive a quantum critical system along its instantaneous thermal states. In contrast, now we show that the same technique, with some modifications, can be applied to charge a quantum battery

through controlled dissipation, thereby emphasizing the versatile nature of the STA protocol studied here. In particular, we consider an evolution such that each mode is driven along the following trajectory:

\tilde{\rho}_{k}(t)=\sum_{n}\tilde{\lambda}^{k}_{n}(t)|n_{t}^{k}\rangle\langle n_{t}^{k}|,\quad\text{with}\quad\tilde{\lambda}^{k}_{n}(t)=\frac{e^{\beta\tilde{E}^{(n)}_{k}}}{\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}},

VII
Conclusions

We studied shortcuts to adiabaticity (STA) in an open long-range quantum critical system, through suitable engineered unitary and non-unitary operators. We show that for suitable choices of parameters, LRIs can be significantly beneficial for controlling excitations in open quantum critical systems driven out of equilibrium, as well as for charging a quantum battery through a STA inspired quantum control technique.

In this work we have considered the LRK chain, which shows two different dispersion relations close to the two quantum critical points
\mu=\pm 1
;
the energy gap
E_{k}(\alpha)
increases with increasing range of interactions for
\mu=-1
, which in turn results in the long range advantages discussed above. In particular, for LRIs quantified by
1<\alpha<2
, the CD Hamiltonian strength
h_{m}
denoting the strength of interactions between particles separated by a distance
m
decays algebraically with
m
both away from criticality, as well as at the criticality. This is in sharp contrast with the behavior seen for short range interactions obtained for
\alpha>2
, in which case
h_{m}
assumes a
m
-independent constant value at criticality, analogous to that seen earlier for the transverse Ising chain in Ref.
del Campo et al. 2012
. This result provides a clear way of harnessing LRIs for engineering practically realizable exact STA protocols in quantum critical systems, which otherwise can be expected to be impossible to implement, owing to the requirement of infinite-range interactions at criticality.
In case of control in the presence of dissipation, LRIs assist in STA through reduction in the costs of control, quantified by decrease of magnitude of heat current with decreasing values of
\alpha
. Finally we propose a modified STA protocol, which aims at using the setup as a quantum battery, through population inversion. We show that longer ranges of interactions are beneficial in this case as well, through enhancement of ergotropy for lower values of
\alpha
, for
\mu\approx-1
.

In sharp contrast to the beneficial effects of LRIs discussed above, range of interactions have distinctly different effects close to
\mu=1
. Here
E_{k}(\alpha)
assumes linear dispersion relation for all
\alpha>1
, with
E_{k}(\alpha)
decreasing with decreasing
\alpha
. This in turn results in vanishing of the long-range advantages observed earlier for
\mu\to-1
.

The above discussed effects of LRIs and criticality become more pronounced for lower values of temperatures. On the other hand, thermal fluctuations dominate at high temperatures, thereby washing away all effects of
\alpha
and criticality. Further, as discussed in Appendix
E
, inspite of the local form of the control Lindblad operators in the momentum space, the real space representation of the same involves multi-body interaction terms even away from criticality, for all values of
\alpha
. This can be attributed to the requirement for control of entropy, which in general can be expected to involve non-local control terms.

Finally, we note that the STA protocol presented in this work can be expected to be implementable in currently existing experimental platforms, including in ion traps
Roßnagel et al. 2016
and quantum simulators
Bernien et al. 2017
;
King et al. 2022
, and can be highly relevant for quantum control in many-body open quantum systems
Claeys et al. 2019
, as well as for applications in quantum technologies
Mukherjee and Divakaran 2024
, including finite-time quantum engines
Cangemi et al. 2024
and quantum batteries
Campaioli et al. 2019

Appendix A
Low-momentum expansion of the pairing function and quasiparticle spectrum

In this appendix, we derive the low-momentum behavior of the function
f_{\alpha}(k)
[Eq. (
3
)] and the quasiparticle spectrum
E_{k}
[Eq. (
6
)] near the critical modes
k=0
and
k=\pi
.

A.1
Expansion around
\mu=-1

Near the critical point
\mu=-1
, where the gap closes at
k=0
, we have
Vodola et al. 2016
;
Dutta and Dutta 2017
;
Olver et al. 2010
;
Abramowitz and Stegun 1964

f_{\alpha}(k)\sim\begin{cases}A_{\alpha}k^{\alpha-1},&1<\alpha<2,\\[4.0pt]
k\ln(1/k),&\alpha=2,\\[4.0pt]
B_{\alpha}k,&\alpha>2,\end{cases}

(45)

where

A_{\alpha}=\left|\Gamma(1-\alpha)\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)\right|,\qquad B_{\alpha}=\zeta(\alpha-1).

Defining
\epsilon_{-}=\mu+1
and expanding
\mu+\cos k\simeq\epsilon_{-}-k^{2}/2
, the quasiparticle spectrum reduces to (see Fig.
6
)

E_{k}^{2}\sim\begin{cases}\epsilon_{-}^{2}+A_{\alpha}^{2}k^{2(\alpha-1)}&1<\alpha<2,\\[4.0pt]
\epsilon_{-}^{2}+k^{2}\ln^{2}(1/k),&\alpha=2\\[4.0pt]
\epsilon_{-}^{2}+B_{\alpha}^{2}k^{2},&\alpha>2\end{cases}

(46)

Figure 6:
Quasiparticle spectrum
E_{k}
of the LRK model for different
\alpha
at
\mu=-1
. The inset shows the dispersion near
k=\pi
, and at
\mu=+1
where the spectrum remains linear for all
\alpha
.

A.2
Expansion around
\mu=+1

Near the second critical point
\mu=+1
, the gap closes at
k=\pi
. Writing
q=\pi-k
, the pairing function expands as
Solfanelli et al. 2023b

f_{\alpha}(\pi-q)\sim\tilde{B}_{\alpha}q,\qquad\tilde{B}_{\alpha}=\eta(\alpha-1),

(47)

where
\eta(s)=(1-2^{1-s})\zeta(s)
is the Dirichlet eta function. Defining
\epsilon_{+}=\mu-1
and expanding
\mu+\cos(\pi-q)\simeq\epsilon_{+}+q^{2}/2
, the spectrum becomes

E_{k}^{2}\sim\epsilon_{+}^{2}+\tilde{B}_{\alpha}^{2}q^{2}.

(48)

Hence, unlike the case
\mu=-1
, the quasiparticle spectrum at
\mu=+1
remains linear for all
\alpha>1
.

Appendix B
CD drive for the LRK chain

In this section we briefly derive the CD Hamiltonian
for the LRK chain in Fermionic representation, and its corresponding real space representation

B.1
Momentum-space representation

The momentum-space representation of the
long-range Kitaev chain (Eq.
1
) reads

H_{0}(t)=\sum_{k}\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mu)\Psi_{k},\qquad\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}=(c_{k}^{\dagger},c_{-k}),

(49)

with
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mu)=(\mu+\cos k)\sigma^{z}+f_{\alpha}(k)\sigma^{x}
and the quasiparticle spectrum is
E_{k}(\mu,\alpha)=\sqrt{(\mu+\cos k)^{2}+f_{\alpha}^{2}(k)}.
Each momentum sector therefore corresponds to an effective
two-level Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}_{k}=\vec{d}_{k}\cdot\vec{\sigma}

with
\vec{d}_{k}=\left(f_{\alpha}(k),\,0,\,\mu+\cos k\right).

For a generic two-level Hamiltonian the adiabatic gauge potential
is given by
del Campo et al. 2012
;
Kolodrubetz et al. 2017

A_{\mu}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\vec{d}_{k}\times\partial_{\mu}\vec{d}_{k}}{|\vec{d}_{k}|^{2}}\cdot\vec{\sigma}.

(50)

Since

\partial_{\mu}(\mu+\cos k)=1

and

\partial_{\mu}f_{\alpha}(k)=0
,
we obtain

\vec{d}_{k}\times\partial_{\mu}\vec{d}_{k}=(f_{\alpha},0,\mu+\cos k)\times(0,0,1)=(0,-f_{\alpha},0).

(51)

Thus the adiabatic gauge potential becomes

A_{\mu}^{(k)}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{f_{\alpha}(k)}{E_{k}^{2}}\sigma^{y},

(52)

which results in the counterdiabatic Hamiltonian Eq. (
17
):

\displaystyle H_{\rm CD}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{\mu}(t)\sum_{k}A_{\mu}^{(k)}

(53)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\dot{\mu}(t)}{2}\sum_{k}\frac{f_{\alpha}(k)}{E_{k}^{2}}\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}\sigma^{y}\Psi_{k}.

B.2
Real-space representation of
H_{CD}

To obtain the real-space form of the CD Hamiltonian we first note the
identity

\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}\sigma^{y}\Psi_{k}=i\left(c_{-k}c_{k}-c_{k}^{\dagger}c_{-k}^{\dagger}\right).

(54)

Substituting this into Eq. (
53
) gives

H_{\rm CD}(t)=-\frac{\dot{\mu}(t)}{2}\sum_{k}\frac{f_{\alpha}(k)}{(\mu+\cos k)^{2}+f_{\alpha}^{2}(k)}\,i\left(c_{-k}c_{k}-c_{k}^{\dagger}c_{-k}^{\dagger}\right).

(55)

We now express the Fermionic operators in real space using the inverse
Fourier transform

c_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j}e^{-ikj}c_{j},\qquad c_{-k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{l}e^{ikl}c_{l}.

(56)

This yields

c_{-k}c_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j,l}e^{ik(l-j)}c_{l}c_{j}.

(57)

Introducing the separation
m=j-l
and reorganizing the sums, the above
expression can be written as

c_{-k}c_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\sum_{m}e^{-ikm}\,c_{j}c_{j+m}.

(58)

Substituting this result back into the CD Hamiltonian(Eq.
55
) and performing the
summation over momentum, one obtains Eq. (
18
).

B.3
Derivation for the CD coupling
h_{m}

Here, we derive the form of CD coupling
h_{m}(\mu,\alpha)
across the criticality
\mu=\pm 1
, discussed in
Sec.
III
.

(a)
\bm{\mu=-1,\penalty\ 1<\alpha<2:}
Putting
\epsilon_{-}=0
in Eq. (
19
) one gets

h_{m}(\epsilon_{-},1<\alpha<2)\approx\frac{1}{4\pi A_{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\pi}k^{(1-\alpha)}\sin(mk)\,dk

Making the scaling substitution
q=mk
we get

h_{m}\approx\frac{m^{\alpha-2}}{4\pi A_{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{m\pi}q^{1-\alpha}\sin q\,dq.

The above integral converges for large
m
and using the standard identity
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2007

\int_{0}^{\infty}q^{\beta-1}\sin q\,dq=\Gamma(\beta)\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi\beta}{2}\right),\qquad 0<\beta<1,

(59)

with
\beta=2-\alpha
, we obtain

h_{m}\approx\frac{m^{\alpha-2}}{4\pi A_{\alpha}}\Gamma(2-\alpha)\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right).

(60)

Using
\Gamma(2-\alpha)=(1-\alpha)\Gamma(1-\alpha)
,
one finally obtains Eq. (
23
) in the main text.

(b)
\bm{\mu\to-1,\alpha>2:}

The integral (Eq.(
24
))

h_{m}(\epsilon_{-},\alpha>2)\approx\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{B_{\alpha}k\,\sin(mk)}{\epsilon^{2}+B_{\alpha}^{2}k^{2}}\,dk

(61)

is dominated by the momentum region
near
k=0
. Therefore the upper limit of integration can be extended to infinity.
Factoring out
B_{\alpha}^{2}
from the denominator gives

h_{m}(\epsilon_{-},\alpha>2)\approx\frac{1}{4\pi B_{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{k\,\sin(mk)}{k^{2}+\kappa^{2}}\,dk,\quad\kappa=\frac{|\epsilon|}{B_{\alpha}}.

(62)

Using the standard integral
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2007

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{k\sin(rk)}{k^{2}+\kappa^{2}}\,dk=\frac{\pi}{2}e^{-\kappa r},

(63)

we obtain Eq. (
25
).

(c)
\bm{\mu\to+1,\alpha:}
Now, we derive the CD coupling
h_{m}(\mu,\alpha)
near second critical point
\mu\to+1
, where the gap closes
at
k=\pi
. Small
q=\pi-k
expansions of pairing function and quasi particle spectrum are given in Eqs. (
47
)-(
48
) and using them in Eq.(
18
), one finds

h_{m}(\epsilon_{+},\alpha)\simeq\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}dq\;\frac{\widetilde{B}_{\alpha}q\sin(mq)}{\epsilon_{+}^{2}+\widetilde{B}_{\alpha}^{2}q^{2}}.

(64)

Where we have used the sine factor as,
\sin(mk)=\sin[m(\pi-q)]=(-1)^{m+1}\sin(mq)
.
Proceeding exactly as in the
\mu=-1
,
\alpha>2
case and defining:

\kappa_{+}=\frac{|\epsilon_{+}|}{\widetilde{B}_{\alpha}}.
we get

h_{m}(\epsilon_{+},\alpha)\simeq\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{4\pi\widetilde{B}_{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\;\frac{q\sin(mq)}{q^{2}+\kappa_{+}^{2}}.

(65)

Using the same standard integral(see Eq.(
63
) we get

h_{m}(\epsilon_{+},\alpha)\simeq(-1)^{m+1}\frac{1}{8\widetilde{B}_{\alpha}}\exp\left[-\frac{|\epsilon_{+}|}{\widetilde{B}_{\alpha}}m\right].

(66)

Finally, substituting
\widetilde{B}_{\alpha}=\eta(\alpha-1)
gives us Eq. (
27
).

Appendix D
Heat currents and Power

Heat Current:

As defined in the Eq.(
29
), the CD contribution to heat dissipation is given as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\rm CD}=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\mathcal{L}(\rho)H_{\rm CD}\right];\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathcal{L}(\rho)=\sum_{m}\dot{\lambda}_{m}|m_{t}\rangle\langle m_{t}|

(74)

Using the CD Hamiltonian
H_{\rm CD}
(C.f Eq.(
10
)) one gets,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{CD}=i\,\mathrm{Tr}\Bigg[\sum_{m}\dot{\lambda}_{m}|m_{t}\rangle\langle m_{t}|\sum_{n}\Big(|\partial_{t}n_{t}\rangle\langle n_{t}|\Big)\Bigg]

\displaystyle-i\,\mathrm{Tr}\Bigg[\sum_{m}\dot{\lambda}_{m}|m_{t}\rangle\langle m_{t}|\sum_{n}\Big(\langle n_{t}|\partial_{t}n_{t}\rangle|n_{t}\rangle\langle n_{t}|\Big)\Bigg].

One can further simplify
\mathcal{I}_{\rm CD}
as
Mahunta and Mukherjee 2025
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{CD}

\displaystyle=i\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\sum_{m,n}\dot{\lambda}_{m}|m_{t}\rangle\langle m_{t}|\partial_{t}n_{t}\rangle\langle n_{t}|\right]

\displaystyle\quad-i\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\sum_{m,n}\dot{\lambda}_{m}\langle n_{t}|\partial_{t}n_{t}\rangle|m_{t}\rangle\langle n_{t}|\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\dot{\lambda}_{m}\Phi_{mm}-\sum_{l}\dot{\lambda}_{l}\Phi_{ll}=0.

where we have used,
\Phi_{mm}=\langle m_{t}|\partial_{t}m_{t}\rangle
.

Power:
Next, we evaluate the power dissipated
\mathcal{P}_{\rm CD}=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho(t)\dot{H}_{\mathrm{CD}}(t)]
due to the CD Hamiltonian
H_{\rm CD}
in presence of a non-unitary evolution by considering the desired path for the system to be the instantaneous thermal state
\rho(t)=\sum_{l}\lambda_{l}(t)|l_{t}\rangle\langle l_{t}|
. One can use the completeness relation,
\sum_{l}|l_{t}\rangle\langle l_{t}|=1
to get

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\left(\sum_{l}|l\rangle\langle l|\right)=\sum_{l}|\ddot{l}\rangle\langle l|+2|\dot{l}\rangle\langle\dot{l}|+|l\rangle\langle\ddot{l}|=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{l}|\dot{l}\rangle\langle\dot{l}|=-\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{l}\left(|l\rangle\langle\ddot{l}|+|\ddot{l}\rangle\langle l|\right).

(75)

Further, the normalization condition
\langle l|l\rangle=1
leads to

\displaystyle\langle\dot{l}|l\rangle=-\langle l|\dot{l}\rangle\penalty\ \penalty\ \Rightarrow\penalty\ \penalty\ 2\langle l|\dot{l}\rangle=\langle l|\dot{l}\rangle-\langle\dot{l}|l\rangle.

(76)

Now we use Eq.(
75
) and Eq.(
76
) to evaluate the time derivative of the
H_{\mathrm{CD}}(t)
(C.f Eq.(
10

\displaystyle\dot{H}_{\mathrm{CD}}(t)

\displaystyle=i\hbar\sum_{l}|\ddot{l}\rangle\langle l|+|\dot{l}\rangle\langle\dot{l}|-\partial_{t}(\langle l|\dot{l}\rangle|l\rangle\langle l|)

\displaystyle=\,i\hbar\sum_{l}\dfrac{1}{2}(|\ddot{l}\rangle\langle l|-|l\rangle\langle\ddot{l}|)-\partial_{t}(\langle l|\dot{l}\rangle)|l\rangle\langle l|

\displaystyle-\langle l|\dot{l}\rangle(|\dot{l}\rangle\langle l|+|l\rangle\langle\dot{l}|).

(77)

Finally, we can use Eq.(
77
) to evaluate
\mathcal{P}_{CD}
as follows

,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\rm CD}

\displaystyle=i\sum_{l}\lambda_{l}\left[\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\langle l\ddot{l}\rangle-\langle\ddot{l}|l\rangle\right)-\partial_{t}\Phi_{ll}-\Phi_{ll}\partial_{t}(\langle l|l\rangle)\right]

\displaystyle=\;\dfrac{i}{2}\sum_{l}\lambda_{l}\left[\langle l|\ddot{l}\rangle-\langle\ddot{l}|l\rangle-2\partial_{t}\Phi_{ll}\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{i}{2}\sum_{l}\lambda_{l}\left[\langle l|\ddot{l}\rangle-\langle\ddot{l}|l\rangle-\langle\dot{l}|\dot{l}\rangle-\langle l|\ddot{l}\rangle+\langle\ddot{l}|l\rangle+\langle\dot{l}|\dot{l}\rangle\right]

Appendix E
Real-space structure of the non-unitary CD terms

In this appendix we analyze the real-space structure of the dissipative control terms and examine how LRIs affect their spatial profile. Starting from the momentum-space master equation [Eq. (
II.2
)], we focus on a representative contribution of the form
\sum_{k}\gamma_{12}^{(k)}(A_{12}^{(k)})^{\dagger}A_{12}^{(k)}\rho_{k}

\mathcal{K}^{(12)}_{1k}

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{(12)}_{1k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{12}^{(k)}|\phi^{(k)}_{t}|^{2}}{|\phi_{t}^{(k)}|^{2}+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{12}^{(k)}\frac{f^{2}_{\alpha}(k)}{(\mu+\cos(k)+E_{k})^{2}+f^{2}_{\alpha}(k)}.

T_{1}^{(12)}=\frac{1}{L^{3}}\sum_{k}\mathcal{K}^{(12)}_{1k}\sum_{n,m,p,q,r,s}c_{n}^{\dagger}c_{m}c_{p}c_{q}^{\dagger}c_{r}^{\dagger}c_{s}\,e^{i\mathcal{D}k}\,\rho,

where

(81)

with the real-space control strength given by
