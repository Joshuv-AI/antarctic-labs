Act now: strengthening critical care readiness in fragile health systems during war and disasters
Authors: Abdullahi Hassan Elmi
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

War and disasters do not only increase the number of critically ill patients; they also weaken the systems needed to keep those patients alive. In fragile health systems, a single crisis can rapidly disrupt oxygen supply, blood availability, referral pathways, essential medicines, electricity, transport, communication, and staffing. As Bielka and colleagues argued during the Ukraine conflict, critical care professionals have an immediate obligation to organize practical support when urban war damages health services and displaces communities [1]. This message remains highly relevant for conflict-affected and disaster-prone settings where intensive care capacity is limited even before a crisis begins.

Critical care readiness should therefore be understood as more than the presence of ventilators or intensive care beds. In many fragile settings, critically ill patients first present to emergency rooms, maternity units, operating theatres, district hospitals, or temporary field facilities rather than fully equipped ICUs. During war or disaster, preventable mortality may occur because patients arrive late, oxygen is unavailable, blood cannot be obtained quickly, monitoring is weak, or referral to a higher-level facility is delayed. These gaps are not only clinical problems; they are system failures that require preparedness before the next crisis [1, 2].

A practical critical care package for war and disaster areas should include reliable oxygen systems, pulse oximetry, essential airway equipment, emergency drugs, blood-transfusion pathways, infection prevention, and simple protocols for trauma, sepsis, obstetric emergencies, and postoperative deterioration. Triage systems must be clear enough to guide staff during patient surges but flexible enough to protect vulnerable groups, including women, children, older persons, and displaced populations. Such preparation is especially important where the same small workforce must manage trauma, severe infection, respiratory failure, obstetric complications, and routine emergencies at the same time [1–5].

Telemedicine and remote expert consultation may also have an important role when local teams are isolated or specialist support is unavailable. Earlier disaster-response literature described how telemedicine can connect remote teams with expert clinical decision support in austere environments [2–4]. However, telemedicine should not be viewed as a replacement for local capacity. It works best when paired with trained frontline staff, dependable communication, backup power, standard operating procedures, and clear referral links. Without these foundations, digital support may fail at the exact moment when clinicians need it most [3].

Nurses, midwives, emergency clinicians, anaesthesia providers, and critical care teams must be placed at the center of preparedness planning. They are often the first to detect deterioration, start oxygen, monitor vital signs, communicate with families, and coordinate transfer [4, 5]. Strengthening incident reporting, safety culture, and practical training can help teams learn from near misses and improve response during periods of pressure. Evidence from Mogadishu has shown that nurses may face barriers to incident reporting, highlighting the need to build supportive systems that encourage learning rather than blame [5].

For fragile health systems, the most realistic strategy is not to wait for a perfect ICU model. Instead, hospitals and health authorities should build scalable emergency critical care capacity that can function across wards, emergency departments, maternity units, operating rooms, and temporary treatment areas [1, 5]. This includes short competency-based training, supervised task-sharing, checklists for early warning signs, oxygen stewardship, safe blood pathways, and routine drills for mass-casualty and referral scenarios [1, 5].

Critical care in war and disaster areas should be treated as emergency infrastructure. Roads, electricity, water, oxygen, communication, transport, and trained staff are as lifesaving as any ICU machine. The lesson for fragile systems is clear: act now, before the next crisis overwhelms the bedside. Preparedness does not remove the suffering caused by war or disaster, but it can reduce avoidable deaths among patients who still have a chance to survive.
