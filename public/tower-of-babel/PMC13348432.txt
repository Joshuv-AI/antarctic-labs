Sex-stratified polygenic risk scores for coronary artery disease incidence: Insights from a 20-year cohort study
Authors: Leila Najd-Hassan-Bonab, Maryam Moazzam-Jazi, Davood Khalili, Maryam Mahdavi, Mitra Khalili, Maryam S. Daneshpour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolygenic risk scores (PRS) utilize genetic variants to evaluate susceptibility to diseases. While the performance of PRS has been extensively studied in European populations, its accuracy and validation in the Middle East, particularly in Iran, remain underexplored. We aimed to assess whether PRSs improve coronary artery disease (CAD) prediction beyond clinical risk factors using sex-stratified analyses.MethodsThis longitudinal study leveraged data from the Tehran cardiometabolic genetic study (TCGS) cohort (N = 16,226), a long-term population-based study in Iran, including participants free of baseline CAD. We evaluated three established CAD-PRSs (PRS241, PRS175, PRS161) derived from GWAS of European cohorts, alongside a meta-PRS and multi-ancestry data. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to assess the association between each PRS and CAD incidence, adjusting for established clinical covariates. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially adjusting for clinical risk factors to evaluate the independent effect of the PRS. Incremental predictive value beyond clinical factors was evaluated using net reclassification improvement (NRI), integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and decision curve analysis (DCA).The association of clinical risk factors associated with PRS was also investigated.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 15 years in 4,594 individuals, 991 CAD events occured. PRS241 demonstrated consistent sex-stratified associations, with a 70% increased risk in females (HR = 1.70, 95% CI: 1.28–2.25) and 54% in males (HR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.22–1.93) comparing highest versus lowest quartiles. In sensitivity analyses, adjustment for HDL, LDL, hypertension, and diabetes resulted in only modest attenuation of these associations, with PRS241 maintaining significant independent risk. PRS161 was significantly associated with CAD only in males (38% increased risk), while PRS175 showed no significant associations in either sex. Integrating PRS241 into clinical risk models yielded small improvements in CAD prediction, indicated by enhanced discrimination (female: ΔC = 0.004; male: ΔC = 0.004) and reclassification (males: relative IDI = 0.0255). DCA further confirmed small net benefit for PRS241. Also, PRS241 and PRS161 were linked to adverse HDL levels in both sexes (PRS161: β = − 0.0068, FDR P-value = 0.002 in females ; β = − 0.006, FDR P-value = 0.010 in males. PRS241: β = − 0.005, FDR P-value = 0.007 in females ; β = − 0.005, FDR P-value = 0.012 in males). Notably, PRS241 also showed sex-stratified associations with hypertension (OR = 1.11, FDR P-value = 0.04) and diabetes (OR = 1.66, FDR P-value = 0.008) exclusively in females.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that incorporation of PRS241 into established clinical risk factors provides small but complementary value for CAD risk prediction. This may facilitate improved risk stratification and support more targeted primary prevention strategies in both sexes.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06049-4.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the leading cause of death worldwide [1], with incidence rates increasing rapidly in Iran among both males and females [2, 3]. Despite improvements in prevention and treatment, substantial sex-based differences continue to exist in CAD incidence, age at onset, and clinical presentation [4, 5]. Traditional risk factors do not fully account for these disparities, indicating that differences in genetic architecture and sex-specific biological mechanisms play a significant role in disease susceptibility. CAD is a multifactorial disorder with a pronounced genetic component [6]. In the past decade, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified numerous genetic loci strongly associated with CAD and related traits [7]. However, most genetic variants have a negligible effect size and account for only a small proportion of CAD heritability; they are attractive biomarkers because they can be quantified at birth, well before the onset of clinical risk factors. Recent data suggest that aggregating these common variants, referred to as a polygenic risk score (PRS), can be used to predict CAD risk in the general population [8, 9]. Two recently published genome-wide PRSs for CAD by Khera et al. [10] and Inouye et al. [11] demonstrate improved early prediction when combined with clinical risk factors and may benefit risk stratification at an earlier age [12–16].

Despite the growing utility of PRSs, most current implementations rely on GWAS that pool data from both sexes. This approach can mask biologically meaningful sex differences in genetic architecture. To address sex differences of genetic effects, researchers have developed and evaluated sex-specific PRSs for lipid profiles [17–19]. Flynn et al. conducted a sex-stratified GWAS meta-analysis on lipid levels and found that three to five% of autosomal lipid-associated loci show sex-biased effects. Many of these loci are also linked to sex hormones, underscoring the importance of accounting for sex differences in genetic research [14, 17]. We previously found that the rs2048327-G allele, which maps to the SLC22A3 gene, is associated with higher Lp(a) levels and lower HDL concentration in males [20]. Our recent data also suggest that the rs10857064-G allele in the FABP2 gene may increase the risk of obesity, especially in females, a known CAD risk factor [21]. More importantly, CAD incidence also varies among ethnic groups. Most GWAS focus on European populations, despite the substantial genetic diversity that exists across populations with different ancestries. Thus, generalizing PRS across diverse ethnic populations requires caution due to differences in allele frequencies, effect sizes, and linkage disequilibrium [22–24]. It is essential to evaluate PRS performance in non-European populations and consider PRS in combination with clinical risk scores for CAD. Few studies use PRS as a CAD risk factor in sex-stratified designs, and the results have been inconsistent [25, 26].

In this study, we evaluated three recently published CAD scores in the Iranian population using data from the TCGS cohort. Our research focused on three key questions: (1) whether sex influences the predictive value of CAD-PRS in this population; (2) whether combining which PRS with clinical risk factors improves the risk prediction; and (3) whether the CAD-PRS can also influence CAD-related parameters, including body mass index (BMI), diabetes, cholesterol levels, hypertension, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and smoking.

Methods

Study design and population

This study utilized genetic data from the Tehran cardiometabolic genetic study (TCGS) cohort, comprising 16,226 members of Iranian ancestry, which is part of the longitudinal Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS) [27]. TLGS is a population-based cohort study launched in 1999 in Tehran, the capital of Iran [28]. Participants in the cohort are monitored annually for hospitalizations or deaths and are followed up every 3 years to assess cardiometabolic risk factors. The collected data were then evaluated by an adjudication committee comprising a principal investigator, a cardiologist, an internist, an endocrinologist, an epidemiologist, and the physician responsible for collecting the outcome data, with additional specialists as needed. At each visit, written consent was obtained from each subject. The ethics committee approved all procedures conducted in this human research study at the Research Institute for Endocrine Sciences, Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (code: IR.SBMU.ENDOCRINE.REC.1402.107), which adhered to the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments, or comparable ethical standards. The exclusion criteria were individuals (1) younger than 30 or older than 75 years, (2) Individuals with a prior diagnosis of CAD, and (3) Individuals who had missing data for one or more covariates. It is worth noting that this resulted in a final analytical cohort comprising unrelated individuals. The flowchart of individuals of the TCGS cohort is presented in Fig. 1.

Outcomes of interest

The primary outcome of interest was the incidence of CAD from 1999 to 2021. Incident CAD was ascertained using the following criteria: (1) definite myocardial infarction (MI) diagnosed by electrocardiogram (ECG) and biomarkers (including CK, CK-MB, CK-MBm, troponin (ctn), and myoglobin), (2) probable MI characterized by positive ECG findings plus cardiac symptoms or signs and biomarkers showing negative or equivocal results, (3) Unstable angina patients, who experience new cardiac symptoms or showed changing symptom patterns and have positive ECG findings with standard biomarkers and admitted to coronary care unit (CCU), (4) Individuals with angiographic-proven coronary heart disease with a result of ≥ 50% stenosis in at least one major coronary vessel and history of angioplasty or bypass surgery [29]. The International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) codes were used for event ascertainment. The validity of these codes has been demonstrated in prior studies conducted within the Iranian population [27].

Confounding variables

Information was also collected on potential risk factors, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels, cholesterol levels, current smoking, and diabetes (DM) status.

Genotyping and SNP selection for PRS calculation

Genomic DNA was extracted using the Proteinase K (salting out) standard method from a blood sample. DNA samples from 16,226 TCGS participants were analyzed using the Illumina Human OmniExpress-24-v1-0 bead chip, which contains 652,919 SNP loci, at the deCODE genetics/Amgen company in Iceland, according to the manufacturer’s specifications (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) [30]. Of these, 1462 samples were chosen for genome sequencing using Illumina’s TruSeq PCR-Free preparation kits. The samples had an average coverage of at least 30×. Furthermore, imputation based on the available whole-genome sequencing data of the human reference genome (hg38) was performed to fill the SNP array’s missing variants using the IMPUTE hidden Markov model (HMM) [31]. Relevant quality control steps were implemented to produce high-quality variants at every stage. For the current study, genotype data from individuals were used in PLINK format to calculate polygenic risk scores and examine their association with CAD incidence in the Iranian population.

The SNP selection and polygenic risk score calculation were based on a review of studies over the past decade [32–35]. Three recently published CAD PRS scores were chosen for their notable differences in ancestry representation and their clinical relevance to CAD prediction. First, PRS241 was derived from GWAS meta-analyses of CARDIoGRAMplusC4D, making it broadly applicable, including 241 SNPs [14]. Second, PRS175 provides insights from a multi-ancestry perspective, which is crucial for understanding its impact across diverse populations and includes 175 SNPs [36]. Third, PRS161 uses CAD-related traits and leverages data from a large-scale biobank to enhance precision in risk stratification and comprises 161 SNPs (See supplementary Table 1). Such an approach ensures that our study addresses multiple factors of genetic risk and offers a comprehensive understanding of CAD susceptibility. We assessed these three CAD PRS in the TCGS cohort, hereafter denoted as PRS241, PRS175, and PRS161, respectively. Each PRS was standardized by calculating the z-score for further analysis.

Prior to PRS calculations in the TCGS population, markers missing in our imputed data were replaced with proxy SNPs in high linkage disequilibrium [LD > 0.8], and markers without proxies were excluded. Subsequently, the local (TCGS data) PRS was calculated by summing, for each individual, the weighted SNP values multiplied by the number of risk alleles, following an additive model. The three CAD-PRS SNPs used in this study are listed in Supplemental Table 1. Statistical analyses

Cox proportional hazard models were run separately for males and females without a baseline history of CAD. This allowed us to evaluate the sex-stratified effects of the PRS on CAD incidence. To illustrate the genetic risk of CAD predicted by the PRS model, we divided participants into quartiles based on PRS scores. The first quartile (Q1) included individuals with PRS values below the 25th percentile and served as the reference group. We compared this group to each of the other three quartiles. Time-to-event analysis measured the duration from the baseline examination date to the first follow-up examination meeting CAD criteria. For censored participants, the last examination date was used as the endpoint. We used three models for genetic risk prediction: Model 1 included only the polygenic risk score. Model 2 incorporated the PRS alongside age and BMI. Finally, Model 3 comprised a fully adjusted model, integrating all conventional clinical risk factors (age, BMI, hypertension, HDL, LDL, cholesterol, smoking, and diabetes) with the PRS. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were conducted to examine the association between genetic risk categories and cumulative CAD incidence, with statistical comparisons performed using the log-rank test. Model discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index), with 95% CIs derived via bootstrap resampling (1,000 replicates) were conducted in Stata (v17). Given the time-to-event nature of the outcome, the C-index was used as the primary metric of predictive performance [37, 38]. Model improvement was quantified using the continuous Net Reclassification Improvement (cNRI) [39] and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI), with risk categories defined by 10-year CAD risk thresholds: low (< 5%), borderline (5–7.5%), intermediate (7.5–20%), and high (≥ 20%) [40]. Comparing the baseline model to each PRS model quantified the extent to which including PRS improves the prediction of disease status beyond that provided by clinical factors alone. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially adjusting for risk factors such as HDL, LDL, hypertension, and diabetes separately. Moreover, Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical net benefit of adding each PRS to the baseline clinical mode in male and females separately.

Finally, linear regression models were used to evaluate the associations between PRS and cardiometabolic traits, including HDL, LDL, cholesterol, BMI, hypertension, smoking status, and diabetes, among individuals who were free from prevalent CAD. All analyses were conducted in Stata (v17), and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. For power calculations, we performed a power assessment using Stata 14.

Results

Study population

Among the 16,226 TCGS cohort members, 4,560 were selected based on quality-controlled genotype data and non-missing clinical risk factors. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the chosen participants are shown in Table 1. Males comprised 46% of the participants, with a mean age of 43.6 years. This reflects regional CAD incidence patterns, as middle-aged males are more susceptible to CAD, as previously reported. Diabetes was more common in females (57% of all diabetic patients were female). Females also had a higher mean BMI than males in both the CAD and non‑CAD groups (Table 1). Two-thirds of females and half of males used lipid-lowering drugs. Over a 15-year median follow-up, 991 individuals developed CAD (male, n = 575; female, n = 416).

Among 2,127 females, 416 developed CAD during follow-up. Only PRS241 showed a significant association with CAD risk in the fully adjusted model (Model 3). Relative to the lowest genetic risk quartile (Q1), females in the highest quartile (Q4) had a 70% higher hazard of incident CAD (HRadj = 1.70; 95% CI: 1.28–2.25; P = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴). A significant positive trend was observed across quartiles (P-trend < 0.001). For PRS175, hazard ratios for CAD were not significantly elevated across quartiles in any of the models. For PRS161, compared to the lowest quartile (Q1), the adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for CAD incidence in Model 1 showed a significant increase across PRS quartiles. Q4 showed an HRadj of 1.47 (95% CI, 1.10–1.95). However, after full adjustment in Model 3, the association was attenuated and became non-significant; Q4 HRadj was 1.23 (95% CI 0.92–1.64). Kaplan-Meier curves show cumulative CAD event rates by genetic risk group. Over a median of 15 years, a higher genetic risk was associated with more CAD events for PRS241 (log-rank P < 0.001) (Fig. 2) and PRS161 (log-rank P = 0.044). No significant difference in events was observed across quartiles for PRS175 (log-rank p = 0.41) (see Supplementary Fig. 1).

Among 2,467 males (n = 575 CAD events) with a median 15-year follow-up, two polygenic risk scores (PRS161 and PRS241) showed significant increases in CAD risk in fully adjusted models. For PRS161, males in the highest quartile (Q4) had a 38% higher hazard than those in Q1 (HRadj = 1.38; 95% CI: 1.08–1.75; P-trend < 0.01). PRS241 showed a dose–response pattern: Q4 had a 54% higher hazard than Q1 (HRadj = 1.54; 95% CI: 1.22–1.93; P = 2.1 × 10⁻⁴; P-trend < 0.001) in model 3. PRS175 showed no significant association in all models. Additionally, Kaplan–Meier analysis in males showed significantly different CAD-free survival rates across PRS241 (log-rank p = 0.001) and PRS161 (log-rank p = 0.001) quartiles, with higher risk scores associated with a greater incidence of CAD over time. PRS175 showed no significant survival difference across quartiles (log-rank p = 0.67); curves largely overlapped (See Supplementary Fig. 1).

According to the power calculation, and assuming a two-sided significance level of 0.05, the observed number of events (575 in males and 416 in females) provided 80% power to detect a hazard ratio of 1.4 per 1-SD increase in PRS in males and 78% power in females. In the total population (991 events), 80% power was achieved for a hazard ratio of 1.3.

Sex-stratified incremental predictive value of three PRS for CAD incidence and decision curve analysis

To evaluate the incremental predictive performance of each PRS beyond a clinical risk model, we assessed changes in discrimination using Harrell’s C-index and reclassification using the category-based Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI), with analyses stratified by sex (Supplementary Table 2).

In females, the clinical model alone, comprising age, BMI, HDL, LDL, cholesterol, smoking status, diabetes, and hypertension, achieved a C-index of 0.858 (95% CI: 0.850–0.866). Of the three polygenic risk scores assessed, adding PRS241 yielded a small improvement in discrimination (C-index = 0.862; ΔC = 0.004), whereas PRS161 and PRS175 showed minimal benefit (C-index = 0.859 for both; ΔC = 0.001). Subsequently, reclassification analyses further elucidated the incremental value of each score. Adding PRS241 to the general CAD model yielded a small but statistically significant improvement in discrimination (absolute IDI = 0.0049, 95% CI 0.0020–0.0077), indicating better differentiation of risk levels, while the relative IDI, cutpoint-based NRI, and cutpoint-free NRI had confidence intervals crossing zero, suggesting no meaningful reclassification. For PRS161, no evidence of improvement was observed in IDI or cutpoint-based NRI (CIs included zero), but the positive and significant cutpoint-free NRI (0.1421, 95% CI 0.0621–0.2222) points to improved risk ordering without significant shifts across predefined thresholds. Incorporation of PRS175 resulted in small but statistically significant gains in IDI metrics, reflecting improved risk discrimination, while NRI measures showed no significant change.

In males, the clinical risk model yielded a C-index of 0.799. Among the three PRS evaluated, PRS241 and PRS161 showed marginal improvement in discrimination, with C-indices of 0.803 and 0.804, respectively. In contrast, PRS175 showed no improvement (C-index = 0.799). The enhanced model, which includes PRS241, demonstrated the most consistent improvements across several metrics, with increases in absolute IDI (0.0050, 95% CI 0.0020–0.0080), relative IDI (0.0255, 95% CI 0.0088–0.0422), and cutpoint-free NRI (0.2020, 95% CI 0.1302–0.2738), whereas the cutpoint-based NRI remained close to null. For PRS161, improvements in discrimination were small and not statistically significant for IDI measures, but the cutpoint-free NRI was again clearly positive (0.1782, 95% CI 0.0863–0.2700), indicating better ranking of males according to future CAD risk without strong evidence of category shifts. Conversely, for PRS175, neither absolute nor relative IDI provided evidence of improved discrimination, and the cutpoint-based NRI was slightly negative with a confidence interval entirely below zero (–0.0090, 95% CI − 0.0178 to − 0.0001), pointing to a slight deterioration in category-based reclassification despite a positive cutpoint-free NRI (0.1151, 95% CI 0.1086–0.1217). Overall, the enhancements were generally small in magnitude, with PRS241 exhibiting the most consistent gains in discrimination, especially among males, while PRS161 primarily contributed to improvements in continuous reclassification metrics across both sexes.

Finally, DCA indicated that among three PRSs, PRS241 provided slight improvement in net benefit over the baseline clinical model, particularly within threshold probabilities of about 20–30% in both females and males. In contrast, PRS161 and PRS175 did not demonstrate consistent net benefit across these thresholds. These findings are consistent with the small incremental improvements in discrimination for PRS241 (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Association of 3 PRS with known clinical CAD risk factors

In the next section, we studied participants without a baseline history of coronary artery disease. We assessed associations between polygenic risk scores (PRS) and clinical risk factors using linear regression. Both PRS241 and PRS161 were significantly associated with lipid profiles in females and males. Genetic risk score was linked to lower HDL (PRS161: β = − 0.0068, P-valueadj = 0.002 in females ; β = − 0.006, P-valueadj = 0.010 in males. PRS241: β = − 0.005, P-valueadj = 0.007 in females ; β = − 0.005, P-valueadj = 0.012 in males). In addition, PRS241 was associated with LDL in both sexes, PRS241: β = 0.002, P-valueadj < 0.001 in females; β = 0.003, P-valueadj < 0.001 in males. In addition, PRS161 was also associated with higher total cholesterol levels (PRS161: β = 0.001, P-valueadj = 0.028 in females; β = 0.002, P-valueadj < 0.001 in males). Notably, in females, PRS241 was also associated with hypertension (β = 0.105, P-valueadj = 0.049) and diabetes (β = 0.027, P-valueadj < 0.001), but not in males. PRS175 showed no significant association with clinical risk factors in either sex (see supplementary Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses

To further assess the independence of the genetic associations, sensitivity analyses were performed for PRS241 and PRS161. As shown in Supplementary Table 5, adjustment for HDL, LDL, hypertension, and diabetes resulted in only modest attenuation of the hazard ratios comparing the highest and lowest genetic risk quartiles (Q4 vs. Q1). The overall pattern of association remained largely unchanged.

Discussion

In this analysis of the TCGS, a large community-based prospective cohort from the MENA region, we systematically evaluated three recently developed PRS, namely PRS241, PRS175, and PRS161, and identified three principal findings.

First, we observed pronounced sex differences in PRS-CAD associations. Among the three PRS evaluated, PRS241 demonstrated a significant association with CAD in both sexes; however, the magnitude of risk was greater in females (70% increased risk) compared to males (54% increased risk) within the highest risk group, after comprehensive adjustment for age, BMI, HDL, LDL, cholesterol, hypertension, diabetes, and smoking status. PRS161 was associated with CAD exclusively in males (38% increased risk), with no significant effect observed in females, underscoring the importance of sex-stratified genetic susceptibility in genomic risk assessment.

PRS175, despite its derivation from multiple ancestries, exhibited no significant association with CAD risk in either sex. This may reflect population-specific differences in allele frequencies and linkage disequilibrium patterns. These findings highlight that the inclusion of diverse populations in GWAS does not inherently ensure the transferability of polygenic risk scores across different ethnic groups [22, 23, 41].

By contrast, PRS241, developed based on European GWAS data, demonstrated robust performance for sex-stratified CAD prevention strategies within our Iranian cohort. These findings align with recent genome-wide evidence of sex-specific genetic architectures in blood pressure (BP), a major CAD risk factor, that exhibit female-biased effects on CAD [42]. This study identified 412 female-specific BP loci (vs. 142 male-specific), with strong CAD association (OR = 1.73, 95%CI 1.67–1.79, P = 2.9 × 10⁻²¹¹). Notably, most European studies adjust for a limited set of variables, typically age, sex, BMI, and genetic ancestry components, in CAD-PRS analyses. These less stringent adjustment models typically yield hazard ratios for the highest versus lowest PRS deciles in the range of 1.5 to 2.0 [10, 11]. Our study employed a more comprehensive adjustment strategy, incorporating BMI, HDL, LDL, cholesterol, hypertension, diabetes, and smoking status. Notably, the hazard ratio (HR) for females decreased from 1.94 in the minimally adjusted model to 1.70 in the fully adjusted model. Sensitivity analyses for each of risk factors further showed that adjustment for HDL, LDL, hypertension, and diabetes resulted in only modest attenuation of the association between PRS and CAD, while the overall pattern remained stable. This finding is consistent with a degree of partial mediation through these risk factors.More broadly, cardiovascular PRS provide predictive information beyond conventional clinical risk scores (Framingham Risk Score, Pooled Cohort Equations), capturing genetic effects not fully explained by traditional risk factors. GWAS studies also point to additional biological pathways not captured by these risk factors. Therefore, PRS derived from genome-wide data may reflect both pathways operating through known risk factors (partial mediation) and independent mechanisms, supporting their inclusion in CAD risk models [43].

These findings in the Iranian population are further supported by data from Biobank Japan (BBJ), an independent Asian cohort. In BBJ, PRS241 was associated with incident CAD with a hazard ratio of 3.06 (p-value = 0.007) after adjustment for clinical risk factors. Moreover, age-stratified analyses revealed a statistically significant increased risk in the middle-aged group (HR = 1.63, p-value = 0.002) and older individuals (HR = 1.20, p-value = 0.04). BBJ also demonstrated significant sex differences in PRS association (p-value < 0.001), which is consistent with our findings. Collectively, these data support the external validity of PRS241 and its potential role in sex-stratified CAD risk prediction.

Second, the incorporation of PRS241 into established clinical risk models resulted in modest yet statistically significant improvements in both risk discrimination and reclassification.The observed benefit was more pronounced in females, as evidenced by a net reclassification improvement (NRI) of 3.05% for females and 0.60% for males. Although C-index increases were small (approximately 0.004–0.005), they were directionally consistent with gains observed in European cohorts and may still be meaningful at the population level [44]. Previous studies have similarly shown that CAD-PRSs can improve prediction beyond individual traditional risk factors, with reported increases in the C-index in the UK Biobank and the Malmö Diet and Cancer Cardiovascular Cohort [43]. Importantly, this slight discrimination gain was supported by decision curve analysis, in which only PRS241 showed a minimal net benefit (Supplementary Fig. 2), suggesting limited incremental clinical value beyond traditional risk factors. Nevertheless, PRSs may provide a complementary approach to early risk stratification because genetic risk is available from birth, before the development of clinical risk factors. Therefore, PRS241 may be useful for identifying individuals at elevated risk earlier in life, although this possibility requires validation in longitudinal studies before clinical application.

Third, both PRS241 and PRS161 were significantly associated with adverse lipid profiles, specifically lower HDL levels and higher total cholesterol levels, in both females and males. Notably, PRS241 also associated with hypertension and diabetes only in females, further supporting sex-stratified differences in CAD-related biology [45]. Importantly, the lack of association with BMI suggests that the observed PRS effects on CAD risk are unlikely to be mediated primarily through obesity. Instead, genetic variation may influence disease risk through multiple biological pathways, many of which are reflected in established metabolic and cardiovascular risk factors such as blood pressure [46], dyslipidemia, and cholesterol [47, 48], consistent with findings from several prior studies [49].

These findings are consistent with evidence for early genetic risk manifestation. For instance, the Biobank Japan (BBJ) cohorts, which included both children and adults, demonstrated that systolic blood pressure differences are evident early in life between individuals with high versus low PRS, and these differences become more pronounced with age [46, 50]. Early and sustained exposure to elevated blood pressure in genetically predisposed individuals emphasizes the need for timely intervention to mitigate future disease risk [51, 52]. Because PRSs capture genetic risk from early life, they can facilitate the identification of high-risk individuals before the clinical onset of diseases such as hypertension, thereby supporting primordial prevention [46]. Incorporating PRS into CAD risk assessments from an early age could allow individuals at elevated genetic risk to benefit from customized blood lipid management guidelines.

The influence of genetic factors on lifetime fluctuations of lipid levels remains unclear. Previous research suggests that PRS may predict both average and variable lipid levels across the lifespan, indicating a substantial genetic contribution to lipid variation from early life onward [53]. Similarly, Yixuan et al. reported that people with high PRS but an ideal lifestyle still had undesirably high mean TC, indicating ongoing risk [54]. Data from the National Cholesterol Education Program [55, 56] further demonstrate that individuals with the lowest genetic risk achieved desirable average total cholesterol levels, whereas those with high genetic risk consistently exhibited elevated total cholesterol levels. Together, these findings suggest that PRSs may capture not only static risk but also long-term trajectories of cardiometabolic risk factors.

Conclusion

This study examined sex-stratified associations between three previously published polygenic risk scores (PRS241, PRS161, and PRS175) and CAD incidence, addressing the generalizability of European-derived PRSs in the TCGS cohort. Our findings indicated that PRS241 is associated with CAD in both sexes after adjustment for clinical risk factors, whereas PRS161 showed association only in males and PRS175 showed no significant association. Although the incremental improvement of PRS241 beyond clinical models was small, decision curve analysis suggested a small but consistent net benefit. The associations of PRS241 and PRS161 with adverse lipid profiles, together with the female-specific links of PRS241 with hypertension and diabetes, further support sex-related heterogeneity in PRS performance and CAD biology. Overall, these results support further evaluation of PRS241 in personalized CAD risk assessment, while prospective studies are needed to determine whether PRS strategies improve clinical outcomes in both females and males.

Main strengths and limitations

This study represents the first validation of CAD-PRS within the Iranian population, showcasing its superior predictive performance compared to analyzing individual SNPs. One of the key strengths of our study lies in its extensive follow-up period (20 years), which surpasses the durations (4.6–12.0 years) used in previous studies [16, 57–59]. These results align with recent discoveries, thus adding further credibility to the findings. However, this study had several limitations that should be noted. The PRSs were derived from European ancestry data, limiting their applicability to our Iranian population due to differences in genetic architecture. Moreover, although the overall sample size and event counts provided adequate power for detecting associations between PRS-CAD and incident CAD, power to assess incremental discrimination and reclassification after sex stratification was more limited. Because statistically significant improvements in discrimination and reclassification generally require larger event counts than association analyses, the modest gains observed in these secondary metrics should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, an independent external cohort for replication was not available. Because large-scale genomic datasets from Middle Eastern or Iranian populations remain scarce, we were unable to validate our findings in an ancestrally matched cohort. Future replication in independent datasets is needed to confirm the robustness, generalizability, and clinical utility of PRS241.
