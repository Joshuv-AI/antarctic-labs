Nitsche methods for constrained problems in mechanics \tnotemark [1] Journal: International Journal of Engineering Science Note: This work was supported by the Research Council of Finland (Flagship of Advanced Mathematics for Sensing Imaging and Modelling grant 359181) and the Portuguese government through FCT (Fundação para a Ciência e a Tecnologia), I.P., under the project UIDB/04459/2025.
Tom Gustafsson, Antti Hannukainen, Vili Kohonen, Juha Videman

Abstract
We present guidelines for deriving new Nitsche Finite Element Methods to enforce equality and inequality constraints that act on the value of the unknown mechanical quality. We first formulate the problem as a stabilized finite element method for the saddle point formulation where a Lagrange multiplier enforces the underlying constraint. The Nitsche method is then presented in a general minimization form, suitable for nonlinear finite element methods and allowing straightforward computational implementation with automatic differentation. To validate these ideas, we present Nitsche formulations for a range of problems in solid mechanics and give numerical evidence of the convergence rates of the Nitsche method.

Introduction

In the 1970s, Nitsche
[
33
]
introduced a method for enforcing the Dirichlet boundary conditions in the weak formulation of the finite element method.
The benefits of the Nitsche method over its alternatives, the penalty method
[
19
]
and the mixed Lagrange multiplier method
[
1
]
, include consistency, symmetry and positive definiteness, stability, and conditioning; cf.
[
27
]
.
Hence, the ideas of Nitsche have later found applications also in domain decomposition
[
3
]
, discontinuous Galerkin methods
[
29
]
,
unfitted finite element and fictitious domain methods
[
11
]
,
Robin boundary conditions
[
30
]
, Kirchhoff plates
[
26
]
,
elastic contact problems
[
17
]
, friction problems
[
18
]
,
obstacle problems
[
22
]
, elastoplastic torsion problems
[
14
]
, and
plate contact problems
[
23
]
, to name a few.

In modern literature,
the Nitsche method
is often presented
as a consistency correction to the penalty method, i.e.,
to make sure that the exact solution
satisfies the discrete weak form, by
including additional terms; cf., e.g.,
[
7
,
27
]
.
This
fails to answer to
the obvious follow-up question:

how to generalize the
method to other
problems and constraints?

Consequently, we take
a different viewpoint
based on the saddle point
formulation and present guidelines to derive
optimal Nitsche methods for both equality and inequality
constraints.
These guiding principles apply
to arbitrary constrained
minimization problems, where the constraint acts
on the values of the unknown.
The resulting energy minimization form
is particularly well-suited for solving
nonlinear problems numerically.
We follow
the guidelines in practice
by deriving and implementing novel
methods for problems such as
inequality
boundary condition for
plates, membrane-solid contact problem,
and plate-plate contact problem.

Our formulation is based on
a reinterpretation of the Nitsche method
by Stenberg
[
34
]
.
More precisely,
on the idea that
the Nitsche method corresponds to
a conforming stabilized Lagrange multiplier
method
(in the sense of residual Barbosa–Hughes stabilization
[
2
]
).
For such stabilized methods,
an element-wise elimination of
the Lagrange multiplier will
lead to the equivalent Nitsche method;
see also
[
17
,
12
,
16
,
13
]
.
This connection was exploited in the
error analysis in Gustafsson
[
28
]
and
consequently it became evident that the
stabilized formulation leads to
a family of methods with
constraints of rather general form.

Let us summarize the prior theoretical findings for existing Nitsche methods:
(1) stability becomes automatic if correct
residuals of the Lagrange multiplier are included in the formulation; (2)
optimal convergence rates are obtained
if the scaling of the residual
with respect to the
mesh and the material parameters
correctly mimics the continuous
Sobolev norm of the Lagrange multiplier.

In this paper, built on these observations, we explain how
to define and use general Nitsche methods,
present examples of their adaptation
to novel physical problems,
and
show numerical evidence of their
convergence.
A more careful numerical analysis, i.e.,
proofs of stability and error estimates, is
beyond the scope of this work.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review
the original Nitsche method for the Dirichlet boundary condition
u=g
. In Section 3, we
demonstrate how the method is
modified to satisfy
the inequality constraint

u\geq g
.
In Section 4, we describe a
general form of the Nitsche method
and how it can be used to derive
new methods.
In Section 5, we present some
methods that exist in the literature
and are of this general form.
In Section 6, we derive novel
Nitsche methods from the general form and show numerical
evidence of their performance.

2
Background: Dirichlet boundary conditions

For better context, we briefly recall the original method of Nitsche
[
33
]
.
Consider the model problem

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\kappa\nabla u

\displaystyle=f,\quad\text{in $\Omega$},

(1)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=g,\quad\text{on $\partial\Omega$},

where
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is the problem-specific load,

g\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
is the Dirichlet boundary condition
and
\kappa>0
is a given parameter.
Note that Nitsche
[
33
]
considered
only the case
\kappa=1
, i.e., normalized units.

Problem (
1
) is solved in a finite element space

V_{h}\subset H^{1}(\Omega)

where
h
is the mesh parameter.
The classical Nitsche method for the Dirichlet boundary condition
u=g
reads: find
u_{h}\in V_{h}

such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\kappa\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla v_{h}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{1}{\gamma}u_{h}v_{h}-\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}v_{h}-\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{h}\,\kappa\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial n}

(2)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}fv_{h}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{1}{\gamma}gv_{h}-\int_{\partial\Omega}g\,\kappa\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial n}\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}.

Here
\gamma
is an
L^{2}(\Omega)
function whose restriction to each element
K
is the product of a dimensionless stabilization parameter
\alpha>0

and the local mesh parameter
h_{K}
, divided by
\kappa
, i.e.

\gamma|_{K}=\frac{\alpha h_{K}}{\kappa}.

According to Stenberg
[
34
]
, the Nitsche method corresponds to using stabilized finite element method to discretize
the saddle point problem: find
(u,\lambda)\in H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
such that

\mathcal{L}(u,\lambda)=\inf_{v\in H^{1}(\Omega)}\sup_{\mu\in H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\mathcal{L}(v,\mu).

(3)

The Lagrangian in (
3
) is defined as

\mathcal{L}(u,\lambda)=J(u)-\langle u-g,\lambda\rangle,

(4)

where

J(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\kappa\nabla u\cdot\nabla u-\int_{\Omega}fu

(5)

is the energy functional of the original problem (
1
)
[
10
]
,

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the duality pairing between
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
and
H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, and
\lambda

is a Lagrange multiplier.
We are able to derive the strong formulation of problem (
3
) to reveal that

\lambda=\kappa\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}
.

The Nitsche method (
2
) corresponds to
the stabilized discrete saddle point problem: find
(u_{h},\lambda_{h})\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}
such that

\mathcal{L}_{h}(u_{h},\lambda_{h})=\inf_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\sup_{\mu_{h}\in Q_{h}}\mathcal{L}_{h}(v_{h},\mu_{h})

(6)

where
the stabilized Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{h}
is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{h}(v_{h},\mu_{h})=\Big\{\mathcal{L}(v_{h},\mu_{h})-\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big(\mu_{h}-\kappa\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial n}\Big)^{2}\Big\}.

(7)

The space
Q_{h}\subset H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
denotes a set of
elementwise polynomials that are globally discontinuous.
The variational formulation of problem (
6
) reads: find
(u_{h},\lambda_{h})\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\kappa\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla v_{h}-\int_{\partial\Omega}\lambda_{h}v_{h}-\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{h}\mu_{h}

(8)

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial\Omega}\gamma\Big(\lambda_{h}-\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}\Big)\Big(\mu_{h}-\kappa\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial n}\Big)=\int_{\Omega}fv_{h}-\int_{\partial\Omega}g\mu_{h}

for every
(v_{h},\mu_{h})\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}
.
This method is stable for any pair of finite element spaces
V_{h}
and
Q_{h}
if
0<\alpha<C_{I}
where
C_{I}
is a constant of an inverse estimate, cf.
[
34
]
.

Following the steps laid out in Stenberg
[
34
]
, we can show that an elimination of the Lagrange multiplier element-by-element leads to the formulation (
2
). Assuming that
\kappa
is an element-wise constant and
choosing
v_{h}=0
leads to

-\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{h}\mu_{h}-\int_{\partial\Omega}\gamma\Big(\lambda_{h}-\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}\Big)\mu_{h}=-\int_{\partial\Omega}g\mu_{h}\quad\forall\mu_{h}\in Q_{h}.

(9)

Denoting by
\pi_{h}
the
L^{2}
projection onto
Q_{h}
, this reads

-\pi_{h}u_{h}-\gamma\Big(\lambda_{h}-\kappa\pi_{h}\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}\Big)=-\pi_{h}g

(10)

which gives

\lambda_{h}=\kappa\pi_{h}\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}-\frac{1}{\gamma}(\pi_{h}u_{h}-\pi_{h}g).

(11)

Let
V_{h}
correspond to the space of continuous piecewise polynomial functions of degree
p\geq 1
. If
Q_{h}
is chosen as discontinuous piecewise polynomials of degree
p
or greater, then
\pi_{h}
reduces to the identity mapping, i.e.

\lambda_{h}=\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}-\frac{1}{\gamma}(u_{h}-g).

(12)

Now we can eliminate the Lagrange multiplier
from the discrete saddle point problem.
After substituting
\lambda_{h}
to the stabilized Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{h}
we obtain

\displaystyle J(u_{h})

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial\Omega}\Big(\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}-\frac{1}{\gamma}(u_{h}-g)\Big)(u_{h}-g)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big(\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}-\frac{1}{\gamma}(u_{h}-g)-\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}\Big)^{2}

which simplifies to

\displaystyle J(u_{h})

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}(u_{h}-g)+\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{1}{\gamma}(u_{h}-g)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{1}{2\gamma}(u_{h}-g)^{2}

and finally to

\displaystyle J_{h}(u_{h})=J(u_{h})-\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}(u_{h}-g)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{1}{\gamma}(u_{h}-g)^{2}.

(13)

The classical Nitsche method (
2
) corresponds to
the following minimization problem
[
33
]
: find
u_{h}\in V_{h}
such that

J_{h}(u_{h})=\inf_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}J_{h}(v_{h}).

(14)

Note that by
adding and subtracting
\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\gamma}{2}(\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n})^{2}
to complete the square we can rewrite
J_{h}
in (
13
) as

\displaystyle J(u_{h})+\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big(\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}-\frac{1}{\gamma}(u_{h}-g)\Big)^{2}-\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big(\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}\Big)^{2}.

This form can be compared to the penalty method
which simply minimizes the penalized functional:

J(u_{h})+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{\partial\Omega}(u_{h}-g)^{2}.

The penalty method approximates the Robin boundary condition

\kappa\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(u-g),

(15)

which satisfies
u-g\rightarrow 0
when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. Unfortunately, small enough values of
\varepsilon
lead to ill-conditioned linear systems
[
27
]
.

3
Inequality constraints: Signorini’s problem

For inequality constraints, we first consider problem (
1
) with the inequality constraint
u\geq g
on the boundary
\partial\Omega
.
The resulting problem

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\kappa\nabla u

\displaystyle=f,\quad\text{in $\Omega$},

(16)

\displaystyle u\geq g,~\kappa\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\geq 0,~(u-g)\kappa\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{on $\partial\Omega$},

is often referred to as the scalar Signorini’s or Poisson–Signorini problem
[
21
]
.
The corresponding continuous saddle point formulation of (
16
) is: find
(u,\lambda)\in H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
,
\lambda\geq 0
, such that

\mathcal{L}(u,\lambda)=\inf_{v\in H^{1}(\Omega)}\sup_{\mu\in H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega),\,\mu\geq 0}\mathcal{L}(v,\mu),

(17)

where the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}
was defined in (
4
). The connection between the two formulations is again given by
\lambda=\kappa\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}
.
After residual stabilization, the finite element formulation reads: find
(u_{h},\lambda_{h})\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}
,
\lambda_{h}\geq 0
, such that

\mathcal{L}_{h}(u_{h},\lambda_{h})=\inf_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\sup_{\mu_{h}\in Q_{h},\,\mu_{h}\geq 0}\mathcal{L}_{h}(v_{h},\mu_{h}),

(18)

where the stabilized Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{h}
was defined in (
7
).
The error analysis of formulation (
18
) was given in
[
21
]

where it is concluded, among other things,
that the formulation
is stable for any conforming
Q_{h}
.

The corresponding variational inequality can be written as: find
\lambda_{h}\in Q_{h}
,
\lambda_{h}\geq 0
, so that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\kappa\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla v_{h}-\int_{\partial\Omega}\lambda_{h}v_{h}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\gamma\Big(\lambda_{h}-\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}\Big)\,\kappa\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial n}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}fv_{h},

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\Big(\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}-\frac{1}{\gamma}(u_{h}-g)-\lambda_{h}\Big)(\mu_{h}-\lambda_{h})

\displaystyle\leq 0,

for every
(v_{h},\mu_{h})\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}
,
\mu_{h}\geq 0
.
The second inequality implies that
\lambda_{h}
is the orthogonal
L^{2}
projection of

\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}-\frac{1}{\gamma}(u_{h}-g)
onto the set of nonnegative functions in
Q_{h}

[
10
, Theorem 5.2]
.
Since
Q_{h}
is arbitrary,
we may choose
Q_{h}=L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
and then the orthogonal projection is given explicitly by the maximum operator, i.e.,

\lambda_{h}=\max(\kappa\tfrac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}-\tfrac{1}{\gamma}(u_{h}-g),0)=:(\kappa\tfrac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}-\tfrac{1}{\gamma}(u_{h}-g))_{+}.

(19)

In order to obtain the Nitsche method, we again substitute the discrete Lagrange multiplier
\lambda_{h}
from (
19
) to (
18
) and rearrange the terms to obtain: find
u_{h}\in V_{h}
which minimizes

J(u_{h})+\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big(\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}-\frac{1}{\gamma}(u_{h}-g)\Big)_{+}^{2}-\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big(\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}\Big)^{2}.

(20)

Note that the corresponding method for the equality constraint
can be obtained by dropping the maximum operator (subscript
+
).

4
Structure of the method

We now generalize the method to arbitrary inequality constraints of the form
\beta(u_{h})\geq 0
.
The method (
20
) has the following main ingredients: the energy of the source problem, i.e.,
J
; the constraint to impose, i.e.,
\beta(u_{h})=u_{h}-g\geq 0
; the corresponding definition of the continuous Lagrange multiplier, i.e.,
\lambda(u_{h})=\kappa\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}
; and the correct scaling of the stabilization parameter
\gamma
with respect to the mesh parameter
h
and any material parameters, i.e.,
\gamma(h,\kappa)|_{K}=\tfrac{\alpha h_{K}}{\kappa}
.
In conclusion, the general form of the Nitsche method can be written as follows: find
u_{h}\in V_{h}
that minimizes the functional

J(u_{h})+\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\gamma(h,\kappa)}{2}\Big(\lambda(u_{h})-\frac{1}{\gamma(h,\kappa)}\beta(u_{h})\Big)_{+}^{2}-\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\gamma(h,\kappa)}{2}\lambda(u_{h})^{2}.

(21)

The final method is formulated as a minimization problem instead of a variational one as it is the starting point for our implementation of the method; see Section
6
and Appendix
A
.
In particular, the calculation of the second functional derivative of (
21
)
is performed automatically in our computational implementation of the Nitsche method.

Remark 1

We emphasize that the general form (
21
)
was obtained
following the derivation of (
20
).
Equivalently, it can be deduced starting
from a similar minimization form existing in
the literature for other physical problems;
see, e.g.,
[
17
, p. 135]
for elastic
contact problems and
[
12
, p. 364]
for the obstacle problem.
The form (
21
) is also present in
[
13
]

where the authors discuss an alternative approach using
augmented Langrangian methods.

Hence, for new problems written in the general form (
21
), with energy
J(u_{h})
and finite element space
V_{h}
, it is necessary to define:

1.

A constraint
\beta=\beta(u_{h})\geq 0
and the subset
\Gamma\subset\Omega
where the constraint
can become active.

2.

An expression for the continuous Lagrange multiplier (i.e., the contact force) as
\lambda=\lambda(u_{h})
.

3.

An expression for the parameter
\gamma=\gamma(h,\kappa)
.

In order to find an expression for
\lambda=\lambda(u_{h})
, it is necessary to derive the strong formulation of the continuous saddle point problem.
This is usually obtained by first deriving the
variational formulation and then integrating by parts
to find the corresponding differential equations and boundary conditions,
some of which will depend directly on
\lambda
.
For contact-type constraints between two domains,
prior research suggests that the parameter
\gamma
and, hence,
the expression for
\lambda
should be defined on the
less stiff side; cf., e.g.,
[
25
]
.

Physically, equation (
21
) shows that an important role of
\gamma
is to scale the units of displacement in
\beta
to the units of force in
\lambda
.
More precisely,
\lambda
should be proportional to
\frac{\beta}{\gamma}
with the proportionality constant independent of
h
and
\kappa
.
Therefore, the material parameters
of the problem must be included in
\gamma
.
Mathematically speaking,
a correct scaling of
\gamma

is necessary to
prove the uniform stability of the
discrete saddle point formulation
using
an
inverse inequality
; cf., e.g.,
[
22
,
23
,
25
]
.

Finally, we wish to remark that the penalty method
can be recovered from this formulation by removing
the Lagrange multiplier terms (formally,
\lambda=0
),
and that the Nitsche method for equality constraints
is obtained simply by dropping the maximum operator (subscript
+
).
The solution of the minimization problem (
21
) using Newton’s method
is explained in Appendix A.

5
Some existing methods

5.1
Membrane obstacle problem

Let
\Omega
be a polygonal/polyhedral domain
representing a Poisson membrane in its
undeformed state.
Minimization of the energy

J(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\kappa\nabla u\cdot\nabla u-\int_{\Omega}fu

(22)

corresponds to solving the Poisson equation
-\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla u)=f
with
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.
Let now the deflection of the membrane
u

be constrained by the rigid obstacle
g

below the membrane, i.e.,
u\geq g
.
The Lagrange multiplier corresponding
to the constraint
u-g\geq 0
(in
\Omega
) is given by

\lambda=-\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla u)-f=:-Lu-f
, cf.
[
22
,
12
]
.
The scaling is given by
\gamma|_{K}=\frac{\alpha h_{K}^{2}}{\kappa}
.
Hence, the Nitsche functional reads

J(u_{h})+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma}{2}\left(-L_{h}u_{h}-f-\frac{1}{\gamma}(u_{h}-g)\right)_{+}^{2}-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma}{2}\left(-L_{h}u_{h}-f\right)^{2},

(23)

where
L_{h}
denotes an element-wise evaluation of
L
.

5.2
Two-body linearized elastic contact

Let
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
be two polygonal/polyhedral domains that are initially at
contact on
\Gamma=\partial\Omega_{1}\cap\partial\Omega_{2}
.
The energy of the two-body linear elastic source problem reads

J((\bm{w}_{1},\bm{w}_{2}))=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{w}_{i}):\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{w}_{i})-\int_{\Omega_{i}}\bm{f}\cdot\bm{w}_{i}\right\},

(24)

where

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}(\bm{w})

\displaystyle=2\mu_{L}\,\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{w})+\lambda_{L}\mathrm{tr}\,\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{w})\bm{I},

(25)

\displaystyle\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{w})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\bm{w}+\nabla\bm{w}^{T}),

(26)

and
\bm{f}
is a given body load
and
(\mu_{L},\lambda_{L})
are the Lamé material parameters.

The physical non-penetration constraint on
\Gamma

reads
\bm{u}_{1}\cdot\bm{n}_{1}+\bm{u}_{2}\cdot\bm{n}_{2}\leq 0

where
\bm{n}_{i}
is the outward normal vector on
\partial\Omega_{i}
,
i=1,2
.
The Lagrange multiplier corresponding
to the constraint
(\bm{u}_{2}-\bm{u}_{1})\cdot\bm{n}_{1}\geq 0

is given, e.g., by

\lambda=-\bm{\sigma}(\bm{u}_{1})\bm{n}_{1}\cdot\bm{n}_{1}
.
Note that the choice to use the
normal traction on
\partial\Omega_{1}
instead of
\partial\Omega_{2}

is now arbitrary. In general, the stabilization
should be on the less stiff body or the body with smaller elements; cf.
[
25
]
.
The scaling is given by
\gamma|_{K}=\frac{\alpha h_{K}}{\mu_{L}}
.
The Nitsche functional
[
15
]
reads

\displaystyle J((\bm{u}_{1,h},\bm{u}_{2,h}))

\displaystyle+\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\gamma}{2}\left(-\bm{\sigma}(\bm{u}_{1,h})\bm{n}_{1}\cdot\bm{n}_{1}-\frac{1}{\gamma}(\bm{u}_{2,h}\cdot\bm{n}_{1}-\bm{u}_{1,h}\cdot\bm{n}_{1})\right)_{+}^{2}

(27)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\gamma}{2}\left(-\bm{\sigma}(\bm{u}_{1,h})\bm{n}_{1}\cdot\bm{n}_{1}\right)^{2}.

6
Novel methods

We now present some novel methods based on
formulation (
20
). More precisely, we consider the following problems:

1.

Two-membrane contact problem,

2.

Membrane against solid contact problem,

3.

Plate against plate contact problem,

4.

Kirchhoff plate with inequality boundary constraint.

For each problem, we present the Nitsche formulation
and numerically investigate its convergence rate.
The numerical results are computed using scikit-fem
[
20
]
which is used to solve the nonlinear variational problems using Newton’s method.
For completeness, we describe how to manipulate the energy minimization problems for Newton’s method in Appendix
A
. The programming implementation utilizes automatic differentiation with JAX
[
9
]
so that it is not necessary to manually calculate the functional derivatives involved in Newton’s method. The source code is available in
[
31
]
for reproducing the numerical results.

Convergence rates are estimated via uniform mesh refinement.
In the absence of an analytical solution, we estimate
the convergence rate by evaluating the error between
two subsequent discrete solutions corresponding to mesh parameters

2h
and
h
.
Assuming that the error satisfies
\|u-u_{h}\|\leq Ch^{p}
in the energy norm for some
C>0
and a given polynomial degree
p
of the finite element basis,
we can show that
the difference between two subsequent discrete solutions should
be bounded by the same asymptotic rate:

\|u_{h}-u_{2h}\|\leq\|u-u_{h}\|+\|u-u_{2h}\|\leq C_{1}h^{p}+C_{2}(2h)^{p}\leq C_{3}h^{p},\quad C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}>0.

6.1
Two-membrane contact problem

Figure 1
:
Numerical solution for two membranes in contact using the Nitsche method.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a polygonal domain describing the
shape of two structurally similar membranes
modeled by the Poisson equation that are initially

g
(units of distance) apart.
We denote the vertical displacements of the membranes by
u_{1},u_{2}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and their tensions by
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}>0
, respectively. The membranes are initially separated by a gap
g>0
.
The external loading
f_{1},f_{2}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
can cause the membranes to
come in contact.
Therefore, we introduce a contact constraint
u_{1}-u_{2}\leq g
in
\Omega
and a corresponding contact pressure
\lambda:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
to prevent penetration.

The strong form of the problem reads as: find the displacements
u=(u_{1},u_{2})
and the contact pressure
\lambda
between the membranes such that

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(\kappa_{1}\nabla u_{1})+\lambda

\displaystyle=f_{1}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(28)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(\kappa_{2}\nabla u_{2})-\lambda

\displaystyle=f_{2}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle u_{1}=0,\quad u_{2}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle u_{1}-u_{2}\leq g,\quad\lambda

\displaystyle\geq 0,\quad\lambda(u_{2}-u_{1}+g)=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega.

Remark 2

We define the problem such that the unknowns are the displacements of the membranes. Other authors, e.g.
[
5
,
6
]
, solve for the displaced coordinates of the membranes.

Explicitly, the second membrane is on top, initially
g
units higher than the first membrane. The constraint
u_{1}-u_{2}\leq g
prevents penetration by stating that the relative displacement of the membranes does not exceed the initial gap
g
. The contact pressure
\lambda
appears with opposite signs in the equilibrium equations to represent the opposing forces in contact. The displacements
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are zero on the boundary and we shall numerically approximate them in
\Omega
. We analytically solve and substitute the discrete
\lambda
in the Nitsche method.

To derive the Nitsche method, we first identify the total potential energy of the unconstrained system, i.e. ignoring the contact force
\lambda
. Multiplying the first equilibrium equation with a test function
v_{1}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and integrating by parts yields the weak form

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}-\nabla\cdot(\kappa_{1}\nabla u_{1})v_{1}=\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\nabla v_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f_{1}v_{1},

\displaystyle\implies\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\nabla v_{1}-\int_{\Omega}f_{1}v_{1}

\displaystyle=0.

It is clear that the first term is the variation (Gateaux derivative) of
\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\nabla u_{1}
. The same steps hold for the second equation. Hence, summing the two equations results in the total energy for the two-membrane source problem

J((u_{1},u_{2}))=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\nabla u_{1}-\int_{\Omega}f_{1}u_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{2}\nabla u_{2}\cdot\nabla u_{2}-\int_{\Omega}f_{2}u_{2}.

(29)

Next, we analyze the continuous saddle point problem and find the corresponding Lagrange multiplier as we move towards the general Nitsche formulation (
21
).
The constraint is given by
\beta((u_{1},u_{2}))=u_{2}-u_{1}+g\geq 0
.
Hence, for the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda((u_{1},u_{2}))
,
consider the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}((u_{1},u_{2}),\lambda)=J((u_{1},u_{2}))-\int_{\Omega}\lambda((u_{1},u_{2}))\beta((u_{1},u_{2})).

(30)

Let
\kappa_{1}<\kappa_{2}
such that the first membrane is less stiff. We can find the strong form of the Lagrange multiplier by setting the Gateaux derivative with respect to
v=(v_{1},0)
to zero, and finally applying integration by parts:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u,\lambda;v)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{1}\nabla(u_{1}+\epsilon v_{1})\cdot\nabla(u_{1}+\epsilon v_{1})-\int_{\Omega}f_{1}(u_{1}+\epsilon v_{1})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{2}\nabla u_{2}\cdot\nabla u_{2}-\int_{\Omega}f_{2}u_{2}-\int_{\Omega}\lambda(u_{2}-(u_{1}+\epsilon v_{1})+g)\bigg)\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{1}(\nabla u_{1}\cdot\nabla u_{1}+2\epsilon\nabla u_{1}\cdot\nabla v_{1}+\epsilon^{2}\nabla v_{1}\cdot\nabla v_{1})-\int_{\Omega}f_{1}u_{1}+\epsilon f_{1}v_{1}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{2}\nabla u_{2}\cdot\nabla u_{2}-\int_{\Omega}f_{2}u_{2}-\int_{\Omega}\lambda(u_{2}-u_{1}-\epsilon v_{1}+g)\bigg)\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\nabla v_{1}-f_{1}v_{1}+\lambda v_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}-\kappa_{1}\Delta u_{1}v_{1}-f_{1}v_{1}+\lambda v_{1}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\implies\lambda

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}\Delta u_{1}+f_{1}.

We now have the constraint and an expression for the Lagrange multiplier, but must still ensure that the stabilized discrete problem has the correct units. As described in Section
4
,
\gamma
scales the discrete problem such that
\lambda\propto\frac{\beta}{\gamma}
. Because the discrete solution
u_{h}
is differentiated twice in the definition
\lambda(u_{h})
and
\beta(u_{h})
includes the solution
u_{h}
in its original units (i.e., in meters),
\gamma
must here include a square of the mesh parameter. Further, as we derived
\lambda
with respect to the less stiff side per
[
25
]
, the tension of the first membrane is included in
\lambda
and correspondingly
\gamma
must have its reciprocal. Hence, it must be that
\gamma(h,\kappa)=\frac{\alpha h^{2}}{\kappa_{1}}
for the units to coincide.

To collect, the Nitsche terms are

\displaystyle\lambda(u_{h})=\Delta_{h}u_{h,1}+f_{1},\quad\beta(u_{h})=u_{h,2}-u_{h,1}+g,\quad\gamma(h)=\frac{\alpha h^{2}}{\kappa_{1}},

where
\Delta_{h}
denotes the element-wise Laplacian operator.
Substituting these to (
21
) produces the Nitsche problem: find
(u_{h,1},u_{h,2})\in V_{h}\times V_{h}
which minimizes

\displaystyle J((u_{h,1},u_{h,2}))

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha h^{2}}{2\kappa_{1}}\left(\Delta_{h}u_{h,1}+f_{1}-\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\alpha h^{2}}(u_{h,2}-u_{h,1}+g)\right)_{+}^{2}

(31)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha h^{2}}{2\kappa_{1}}\left(\Delta_{h}u_{h,1}+f_{1}\right)^{2}.

Remark 3

The Nitsche terms and formulation are derived similarly for other problems in Section
6
.

The problem was solved numerically over
\Omega=(0,1)^{2}
with the parameterization
g=0.05

f_{1}=1,f_{2}=0,\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=1
and
\alpha={1}\mathrm{e}{-2}
using linear triangular elements. Note that the element-wise Laplacian for linear elements is equal to zero which simplifies the formulation. The numerical solution is displayed in Figure

Plate against plate

The plate against plate problem follows very closely the two membrance contact problem with the difference of higher order derivatives in the strain energy. The energy of the source problem is

J((u_{1},u_{2}))=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial x_{i}x_{j}}\right)^{2}-\int_{\Omega}f_{1}u_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial x_{i}x_{j}}\right)^{2}-\int_{\Omega}f_{2}u_{2}.

7
Conclusions

While the Nitsche method is commonly viewed as a consistency correction to the penalty method, we demonstrate how new methods can be obtained starting from a general minimization form. This allows for developing novel Nitsche methods for problems with both equality and inequality constraints in a simple manner. We derived and implemented several new problems using the approach. Our numerical experiments suggest that the methods converge optimally while the more careful numerical analysis remains a topic of future work.

Appendix A
Newton’s method

Consider a problem-dependent Sobolev space
U
and its corresponding subspace
V
of functions with zero boundary values so that
V\subset U
and
u+v\in U\>\>\>\forall v\in V
. We want to find a solution to the energy minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{u\in U}J(u)

(40)

where
J:U\to\mathbb{R}
represents the energy of the system.
Let
v\in V
denote an arbitrary perturbation to
u
. A solution
u\in U
to (
40
) satisfies

\displaystyle J^{\prime}(u;v)=0\quad\forall v\in V,

(41)

where
J^{\prime}:U\times V\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the directional (Gateaux) derivative defined by

J^{\prime}(u;v)=\frac{\partial J(u+\epsilon v)}{\partial\epsilon}\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}.

The directional derivative is linear in
v
, but, in general, nonlinear in
u
.

Let
f=J^{\prime}
.
We assume that the problem

f(u;v)=0\quad\forall v\in V

is nonlinear in
u
and, hence, the standard Ritz–Galerkin method cannot be directly applied to obtain a linear system of equations. Consequently, we approach the problem as a root-finding problem and linearize
f
to use the Newton’s method. More precisely,
let
u_{0}\in U
be an initial value such that the solution is written as
u=u_{0}+w
for some
w\in V
. The perturbation
w
is unknown, but can be approximated by discarding the higher order terms of the Taylor series of
f
:

0=f(u;v)=f(u_{0}+w;v)\approx f(u_{0};v)+f^{\prime}(u_{0};v,w),

where
f^{\prime}:U\times V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
is the directional derivative of
f
.

A single step of the Newton’s method then reads: find
w\in V
such that

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(u_{0};v,w)=-f(u_{0};v)\quad\forall v\in V.

(42)

Note that if
u_{0}\in U
is fixed, then the problem is linear with respect to
w\in V
and the standard Ritz–Galerkin method can be applied iteratively.

For the numerical scheme, we use the finite element method. Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a mesh of elements with maximum edge length
h>0
. The finite dimensional subspaces
U_{h}\subset U
and
V_{h}\subset V
contain element-wise polynomial functions of a given degree. The discrete problem then reads: given
u_{0,h}\in U_{h}
, find
w_{h}\in V_{h}
such that

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(u_{0,h};v_{h},w_{h})=-f(u_{0,h};v_{h})\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}.

(43)

The directional derivative of
f
is computable as
u_{0,h}
is given.

The equation (
43
) can be posed as a linear system of equations. Let
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
n=\dim(V_{h})
, be a finite element basis that span
V_{h}
. Then

\displaystyle w_{h}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}\phi_{j},

(44)

where
\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
are the unknown degrees-of-freedom. Choosing
v_{h}=\phi_{i}
, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}f^{\prime}(u_{0,h};\phi_{i},\phi_{j})=-f(u_{0,h};\phi_{i}),

and collecting all
i=1,\dots,n
results in a linear system

\displaystyle\bm{J}\bm{\beta}=\bm{f}

(45)

where

\bm{J}_{ij}=f^{\prime}(u_{0,h};\phi_{i},\phi_{j}),\quad\bm{f}_{i}=-f(u_{0,h};\phi_{i}),

or, if the problem is given directly in the minimization form,

\bm{J}_{ij}=J^{\prime\prime}(u_{0,h};\phi_{i},\phi_{j}),\quad\bm{f}_{i}=-J^{\prime}(u_{0,h};\phi_{i}).

The direction (
44
) is then found from the solution
\bm{\beta}
.

To conclude, starting from an initial value
u_{0,h}\in U_{h}
, we find an approximation
u_{h}\in U_{h}
such that
J^{\prime}(u_{h};v_{h})=0
for all
v_{h}\in V_{h}
through the iterative scheme
u_{k+1,h}=u_{k,h}+\alpha_{k}w_{k,h}
, where
0<\alpha_{k}\leq 1
is a step size parameter. Under standard assumptions the method converges quadratically.
