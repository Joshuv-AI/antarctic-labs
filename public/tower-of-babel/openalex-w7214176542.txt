Abstract
Objective: To propose a critique of the logical-ontological theory of the early Wittgenstein, in the Tractatus LogicoPhilosophicus, which establishes a structural correspondence between world and language (the world as the totality of
facts, facts as combinations of simple objects, and complex propositions as truth-functions of elementary propositions),
from a processual, genetic and cognitive standpoint situated within Cognitive Psychiatry. Method: Critical-conceptual
analysis of the Tractarian architecture, combined with a narrative review of the literature published between 2021 and
2026. Results: The article examines the problem of the simplicity of the object, the transcendentalization and reification
of the subject, the status of falsity and truth, the limits of the distinction between simple and complex propositions, and
the progressive abstraction that leads from concrete episodes to the formal category of "relation". The central critique
distinguishes three levels that are frequently conflated: the real connection between processes in the world, the cognitive
connection through which an organism apprehends or reconstructs processes, and the logical-formal connection between
propositions, in which symbols can be combined independently of any causal or processual link between their contents,
material implication being the extreme case of this autonomization. The literature review shows convergence with this
thesis. Conclusion: Logical form is a late achievement of cognitive processuality rather than a condition prior to the
lived world, which repositions the architecture of the Tractatus as a product of a cognitive genesis still to be explained,
rather than as the founding structure that explains that genesis.
Keywords: Philosophy; Logic; Cognition; Cognitive Science; Knowledge; Psychiatry.

1. Introduction
Published in 1922, the Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus is recognized as one of the most influential works of twentiethcentury philosophy. It shaped the logical positivism of the Vienna Circle, directly influenced Russell, and remains, a century
later, the object of ongoing reassessment, as attested by the centenary volume that brings together contemporary specialists on
the work (Wittgenstein, 1922; Cahill, 2023). This influence stems in large part from the economy of its architecture: in a few
hundred numbered propositions, Wittgenstein seeks to establish a complete structural correspondence between world and
language. The world is conceived as the totality of facts; facts consist in the existence of states of affairs; states of affairs result
from the combination of simple objects; elementary propositions represent these states of affairs; and complex propositions can
be constructed as truth-functions of elementary propositions. It is precisely this economy, the capacity to derive the entire
representational complexity from a handful of simple elements and truth-functional operations, that constitutes, at the same time,
the strength and the blind spot of the work.
Over recent decades, and particularly intensely over the last five years, a heterogeneous set of research programs
(naturalized philosophy of logic, the empirical psychology of conditional reasoning, the cognitive neuroscience of mathematical
abstraction, the linguistics of object individuation, revisited genetic epistemology, and the philosophy of cognitive science itself)
has converged, from independent vocabularies and methods, on a single direction: that logical forms, concepts of object, and
operations of abstraction are not originary data of mind or world, but late products of cognitive, sensorimotor, linguistic, and
cultural processes that are empirically traceable (Santos, 2021a, 2021b, 2025; Sixtus, Krause, Lindemann, & Fischer, 2023; Lee
& Papafragou, 2026; Carpendale & Lewis, 2021; Chirimuuta, 2024; Weinstein, Sakcak, & LaValle, 2022). This body of findings
stands in direct tension with philosophical architectures, such as that of the Tractatus, which take simple objects, states of affairs,
and truth-functions as starting points not derived from anything prior.
It is in this context that this article proposes a critique of Tractarian ontology from a processual, genetic, naturalist, and
cognitive conception, situated in the field of Cognitive Psychiatry. The central thesis is that Wittgenstein converts into originary
logical conditions structures that, in fact, constitute late products of a long history of processualization, cognition, language,

not the inverse path (logical form, conditions of possibility, world) suggested by the Tractarian architecture.

2. Methodology
A qualitative study was conducted (Risemberg et al., 2026a; Pereira et al., 2018) within a logical-ontological framework
(Lauer, 2024), supported by a non-systematic narrative review (Ogassawara et al., 2025). This article combines two
complementary procedures: a critical-conceptual analysis of the logical-ontological architecture of the Tractatus LogicoPhilosophicus, and a narrative review of recent literature that engages with this analysis. The first procedure follows the method
proper to philosophical critique of systems: an exegetical reading of Wittgenstein's text (1922) and of the classical secondary
literature on it, followed by making explicit the ontological and cognitive presuppositions implicit at each stage of its architecture
(simple object, state of affairs, elementary proposition, truth-function), and by the systematic application, at each stage, of a
three-level distinction (real connection, cognitive connection, and logical-formal connection) formulated from the framework of
processual Cognitive Psychiatry. This three-level distinction functions as the article's central analytical instrument and is applied
uniformly across the nine subsections that make up the Results (Section 3).
The second procedure is a narrative, non-systematic review of the literature published between 2021 and 2026 in the
philosophy of logic, the psychology of reasoning, cognitive neuroscience, the linguistics of individuation, and genetic

3.1 The problem of the simple object
The ontology of the Tractatus begins with a decisive thesis: the world is not simply a collection of things, but a totality
of facts. A state of affairs consists in a combination of objects, and objects constitute the substance of the world. In the Tractarian

architecture, the simplicity of objects plays a fundamental role: they constitute the ultimate elements whose possibilities of
combination delimit the possible states of affairs. It is here that the first difficulty appears. Wittgenstein needs to arrive at
something that can no longer be decomposed, since otherwise any analysis could proceed indefinitely, from an object to its parts,
from the parts to the parts of the parts, and so on. The simple object interrupts this regress.
There is, however, a decisive difference between stating that, for a given system of analysis, we will treat X as a unit,
and stating that X is ontologically simple. A chair can function as a unit in the proposition "the chair is in the room," but this
does not make it simple. It has parts, materials, a history, spatial relations, and functions. The same problem remains when the
analysis is taken to more elementary levels: a unit considered simple at a certain descriptive level need not be simple in physical,
ontological, genetic, or processual terms. Hence a first thesis, that logical simplicity does not imply ontological simplicity, and
a second, that linguistic simplicity does not imply cognitive, genetic, or processual simplicity. In the processual conception
defended here, not even what we call "object" needs to be originarily simple: the object may already constitute a stabilization or
objectification within prior processes.
An even more problematic formulation then arises. Objects do not appear simply as particular facts among other facts;
their existence functions as a condition for facts to be possible and for propositions to have sense. A philosophical operation
already present, in another form, in Kant reappears here: that whose genesis should be investigated is instead posited as a
condition of possibility. The genetic question, how is the object constituted, the processual question, what processes make its
individuation, stabilization, recognition, and objectification possible, and the cognitive question, how does an organism come to
discriminate something as an object and subsequently to conceptualize and name it, are all suspended when the object is posited
as a logical condition for facts and propositions to be possible. The structure is presupposed, not explained, and it is precisely
here that one can recognize a methodological affinity with transcendentalism: instead of explaining how a given structure is
formed, one asks what structures must be given for something to be possible.
There is also an important linguistic peculiarity in this philosophical procedure. One does not simply say "we give sense
to propositions"; it is formulated impersonally as "the conditions for propositions to have sense." This shift seems small, but
philosophically it is enormous, since the subject disappears from the sentence. Along with it disappear learning, development,
action, memory, perception, history, acquired language, social interaction, and cerebral and psychological processuality. In their
place appears the "condition of sense." What could be studied as the activity of real organisms is transformed into a property of
a logical domain. The philosophical tradition becomes progressively adept at substituting "the subject performs X" with "X
constitutes a condition of possibility for Y," and this impersonalization contributes to a kind of reification of rationality. Reason
ceases to appear as the result of operations performed by concrete beings and begins to appear as an autonomous domain of
forms, possibilities, and necessities.
The difficulty increases when Wittgenstein maintains that each object possesses determinate possibilities of
combination. An object cannot combine arbitrarily with any other in any way whatsoever: "the chair is beside the table" is a
possible combination, whereas "sadness is thirty centimeters to the left of courage" does not work in the same way, since sadness
and courage are not spatial objects of that kind. Certain entities admit certain relations and not others. But a problem immediately
arises: if the object is absolutely simple, how can it possess within its own structure a multiplicity of relational possibilities? We
supposedly have a simple object, but simultaneously an object endowed with logical structure, with possibilities of combination
with B, with impossibility of combination with C, with possibility of combination with D, and so on. Simplicity begins to become
problematic. If the possibility of relating already belongs to the object, the object seems to carry with it a relational structure,
and relation already implies some differentiation. How can something absolutely simple contain determinations regarding the
relations it may or may not establish with other objects?

One might answer that these possibilities belong to the "logical structure" of the object, but this does not explain the
problem, it merely renames it. We ask why A can combine with B, and the answer is that this belongs to the logical structure of
A. We then ask how this logical structure was constituted, and we arrive once again at the starting point. Saying that something
belongs to "logical structure" may be a formal description of the combinatorial constraint, but it is not necessarily a genetic
explanation of its origin. It remains necessary to ask whether the relation lies in the object, in the subject, in the interaction
between the two, or whether both what we will later call "object" and what we will call "relation" are differentiations stabilized
within a prior processuality. This fourth possibility is particularly important for the processual Cognitive Psychiatry defended
here.
If we reject a psychological explanation, a naturalist explanation, and an explanation through the real processuality of
the cosmos, we risk ending up attributing to the object itself an originary combinatory capacity: the object simply can combine
this way and cannot combine that way, and the answer reverts to saying that this is its logical form. In this specific critical sense,
logical form risks playing the role of a power accepted without explanation, not because Wittgenstein explicitly excludes the
mystical from the domain of what can be meaningfully stated. However, a related difficulty arises because the expression “logical
structure” appears to close the investigation precisely where a genetic explanation would need to begin. Metaphysics therefore
seems to re-emerge at the very point at which it was intended to be excluded, insofar as the ultimate objects of analysis are treated
as possessing a combinatory capacity that must simply be accepted as constitutive rather than explained.
A related problem becomes apparent when considering the distinction between logical type and numerical identity. Two
objects may belong to the same logical type and possess exactly the same possibilities of combination while nevertheless
remaining numerically distinct objects. Consider two absolutely identical chairs, A and B, with the same shape, color, size, and
relational possibilities. Even under these conditions, A is not B; there are still two distinct objects. At the level of simple objects,
however, it is difficult to identify any further property that could account for this difference. One ultimately arrives at something
resembling the statement that “A is different from B because A and B are different objects.” From a processual perspective,
however, this formulation leaves unresolved the more fundamental question of how individuation itself is constituted. Formal
analysis reaches an irreducible element and terminates the inquiry, whereas a genetic perspective asks how the differentiation
between A and B came to exist in the first place.
A further difficulty concerns the order in which elements and their relations are conceptually organized. The assumption
that elements exist first and that their possible combinations can subsequently be investigated appears formally natural: first A
and B, and then A-B. Yet this sequence may represent only the analytical order adopted by the observer rather than the genetic
order through which the phenomena themselves were constituted. It is conceivable that, both ontogenetically and cosmologically,
what we retrospectively isolate as A and B emerged from prior relational and processual configurations. Formal analysis may
therefore treat objects as prior to their relations, whereas a processual ontology can instead propose that processes and relations
precede differentiation, that differentiation precedes stabilization, and that relatively stable objects emerge only as a consequence
of these processes. The distinction is important because it prevents the analytical order imposed by philosophical description
from being conflated with the ontogenetic order through which the phenomena themselves came into being.
This problem ultimately extends to the conception of the world as a whole. Wittgenstein treats elementary states of
affairs as logically independent, allowing the world to be conceived as the totality of existing states of affairs. This yields an
aggregative ontology in which the world is constituted by a collection of states of affairs that are, at the fundamental level,
independent of one another. A processual perspective challenges precisely this assumption of fundamental independence. The
cosmos need not be understood as a receptacle containing autonomous facts; it may instead be conceived as an extensive
historical network of causal processes, reciprocal causal influences, transformations, and dependencies. A tree, for example, is
not adequately characterized as the aggregate of trunk, leaves, roots, and fruit considered as independent components. It is also

3.2 Elementary proposition, complex proposition, and the three levels of connection
Wittgenstein distinguishes elementary propositions, which represent states of affairs, from complex propositions, which
are constructed as truth-functions of elementary propositions. If P means “the ball is on the table” and Q means “John is seated,”
we can construct propositions such as “P and Q,” “P or Q,” “not P,” and “if P, then Q.” At first glance, this appears to involve
nothing more than an increase in linguistic complexity. However, a more fundamental distinction may be at stake: combining
elements or processes in the world is not equivalent to combining propositions according to formal logical rules. The two
operations belong to different levels of organization and should not be treated as interchangeable.
Consider the sentence “the dog ran and reached the ball.” The conjunction “and” does not itself produce the relation
represented by the sentence. Prior to its linguistic formulation, there is already an underlying sequence of events: the dog moves,
approaches the ball, and reaches it. Language can represent this articulation because a processual organization capable of being
signified is already present. The connective therefore does not create the relation; rather, it expresses a relation whose possibility
of signification has been constituted through the underlying process. Evidence from cognitive development is consistent with
this distinction. Infants develop sensitivity to causal relations early in life through sensorimotor, social, and statistical
mechanisms, well before they acquire formal logical language (Basch, 2024). Similarly, children's acquisition of the conditional
construction “if... then” remains for an extended period closely associated with concrete pragmatic and causal relations before
being generalized to more abstract and decontextualized contexts (Lemen, Lieven, & Theakston, 2024). These findings do not
imply that linguistic reasoning is simply derived from prelinguistic causality, but they do suggest that formal logical operators
develop within, and remain initially grounded in, broader cognitive and relational structures.
The distinction becomes clearer when we consider the proposition “2 plus 2 equals 4 and Saturn has rings.” Formally,
this has the structure P ∧ Q. The conjunction is grammatically and logically well formed, and, assuming that both propositions
are true, the compound proposition is also true. Yet the connective “and” does not establish any processual or causal unity
between the arithmetical fact and the astronomical fact. It merely combines two propositions within a common logical structure.
What has been produced, therefore, is a formal concatenation rather than a processual unity in the world. Truth-functional
composition can determine how propositions combine in terms of their truth conditions, but it does not, by itself, establish the
existence of a corresponding processual relation among the phenomena represented. Formal concatenation should therefore not
be conflated with real processualization.
In mathematics and formal logic, abstraction can reach an extreme degree. At the level of ordinary cognition, numerical
concepts can be grounded in concrete experiences of plurality: one apple, another apple, and eventually the abstraction of two
units. In forming the concept of number, features such as color, weight, smell, position, history, ripeness, and provenance can be
disregarded, leaving only the numerical relation between units. Mathematics can then idealize these units and operate formally
upon them, and, as formalization progresses, symbols can be manipulated according to transformation rules that become
increasingly independent of the concrete richness of the processes from which such concepts were historically and cognitively
abstracted. Mathematical formalism, in its various forms, explores precisely this possibility of operating with systems defined
by symbols, axioms, and rules. The philosophical difficulty arises when the autonomy achieved by formal systems is
subsequently projected back onto the structure of reality itself.

This intuition, however, must be preserved without introducing an inaccurate historical genealogy. Material implication
was not introduced by Hilbert. The truth-functional treatment of the conditional is already present in Frege (1879) and was
subsequently developed by Russell (1903), who employed the terminology of “material implication” and incorporated the
concept, together with Whitehead, into the logicist framework of the Principia Mathematica (Russell & Whitehead, 1910–1913).
The truth-functional conception was subsequently incorporated by Wittgenstein into the Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus. Hilbert
is relevant to this discussion in a different respect. His work exemplifies, with particular clarity, the broader movement toward
axiomatic formalization, in which mathematical operations can be investigated through their internal rules, independence
relations, and consistency conditions without requiring every formal step to reproduce a concrete intuition or construction
(Hilbert, 1899, 1926). It would therefore be historically inaccurate to claim that Hilbert invented material implication and that
Wittgenstein simply adopted it. The more relevant thesis is that material implication and axiomatic formalism belong to a broader
historical movement toward the increasing autonomy of logical and mathematical operations, while Wittgenstein places truthfunctional analysis at the center of his general conception of the proposition. It is this philosophical transposition, rather than
formal implication itself, that warrants critical examination.
Consider again P as “2 plus 2 equals 4” and Q as “Paris is the capital of France.” We can then construct the conditional
“if P, then Q.” Under classical material implication, P → Q is false only when P is true and Q is false; in every other combination
of truth values, the conditional is true. Thus, because Q is true, the implication is true regardless of whether P is true or false.
Nothing in this formal relation requires us to claim that the truth of “2 plus 2 equals 4” causes Paris to be the capital of France,
nor does it require the existence of any causal, physical, historical, or genetic process connecting the two contents. The formal
system abstracts these considerations away: what remains are the propositions P and Q, together with a rule governing their
truth-functional combination. In this sense, the relation has undergone an extreme degree of abstraction. It is neither a causal nor
a physical relation, and it need not correspond to any particular mental process. Rather, it is a formal relation defined by the
positions and truth values of propositions within a logical system.
This critique does not seek to eliminate material implication, which is coherent and useful within the formal register for
which it is defined. Once the rules of the calculus have been specified, certain transformations are licensed and others are not,
allowing us to operate with P, Q, true, and false without reconstructing the concrete reality represented by the propositions at
every step. This capacity for abstraction is precisely one of the strengths of formalization. The philosophical problem arises only
when the legitimacy of an operation within a formal system is taken to entail that the operation discloses the structure of human
signification or of reality itself. The proposition that “this operation is valid within this formal system” does not, by itself, entail
the stronger proposition that “this operation reveals how relations are constituted in reality.” The latter requires an additional
ontological argument that cannot be obtained merely from the formal validity of the calculus.
We thus arrive at the central point of this critique: at least three levels must be rigorously distinguished. At Level 1, that
of real or processual connection, the statement “I push the cart, it rolls, and it falls off the table” describes an effective sequence
involving action, movement, loss of support, and falling. P and Q are connected through real processes, such that P is followed
by an intervening processual sequence that culminates in Q. At Level 2, that of mental processual connection, a subject may
think, “If I push that cart, it might fall,” even when nothing is occurring at that moment. The mind can recall, anticipate, simulate,
or reconstruct a processual sequence. There remains a meaningful relation connecting P and Q, but causality is now represented,
anticipated, imagined, or remembered rather than directly enacted in the external environment. At Level 3, that of formal
connection, we have simply P → Q, where P and Q may be abstract variables. The cart, the push, the movement, temporal
sequence, physical cause, fall, and subjective experience can all be omitted. In the case of material implication, even a causal
dependency between P and Q is unnecessary. This constitutes a level of analysis fundamentally different from the previous two.

The central claim of this critique can therefore be formulated more precisely. Wittgenstein operates with extraordinary
clarity at the third level and uses truth-functional analysis to clarify the general structure of complex propositions. However,
what is legitimate at Level 3 cannot automatically be transferred to Levels 1 and 2. Formal combinability does not entail
processual connectivity; formal validity does not by itself establish ontological validity; truth value does not entail processual or
semantic unity; and the formal possibility of combining P and Q does not demonstrate that P and Q jointly constitute a process
in the world. The central difficulty, from the perspective developed here, is therefore a conflation of analytical registers: a formal
relation is treated as though its properties could be directly generalized to the processual and cognitive domains from which
formal relations may have been abstracted.
Consider, once again, the statement “the hammer strikes the nail and the nail enters the wood,” compared with a simple
juxtaposition of words such as “hammer,” “head,” “galaxy,” “Earth,” and “run.” In the first case, there is a recognizable
processual articulation: the hammer moves, produces an impact, transmits force, displaces the nail, and contributes to its
penetration of the wood. The sentence possesses a semantic organization that can be interpreted against this underlying
configuration of events. The conjunction “and” does not itself generate this intelligibility; rather, it participates in the linguistic
representation of an articulation that can be understood independently of the connective. By contrast, the mere aggregation of
words does not necessarily establish an intelligible process. Grammar can generate indefinitely many well-formed sequences
without thereby guaranteeing a corresponding processual unity. From the perspective proposed here, therefore, signification is
not adequately explained by formal combination alone; it must also be understood in relation to the processes and relational
structures that make particular combinations intelligible.
This point can be developed further. A logical-formal framework may describe a complex proposition as the result of
combining propositions through logical connectives. The present theory proposes a different order of explanation: there may
first be a process P, followed by a relation or intermediate process, and then a process Q; subsequently, a cognitive system may
apprehend or reconstruct this articulation; linguistic expression may then encode it; and only at a later stage can the resulting
relation be subjected to formal operations such as “P and Q” or “if P, then Q.” On this account, the relation is not generated by
its formalization. Rather, the connective represents a linguistic and formal stabilization of relational operations that have a prior
processual and historical trajectory. This is a proposed genetic ordering, not a claim that every instance of formal reasoning must
empirically reproduce the same developmental sequence.
The question that remains largely outside Wittgenstein’s formal analysis is precisely the question that a genetic approach
to Cognitive Psychiatry would place at the center: how do the contents designated by P and Q come to be constituted as
distinguishable and meaningful units in the first place? One possible reconstruction begins with organisms embedded in broader
physical and biological processes and proceeds through perception and action, sensitivity to regularities, memory and
anticipation, cognitive reconstruction of processes, communication and language, the objectification of relations,
conceptualization, and abstraction. From this perspective, increasingly abstract representations can eventually become
formalized as P, Q, “and,” “or,” “not,” and “if... then.” Formal logic would therefore be situated downstream of a much longer
processual and cognitive history. The precise developmental and evolutionary pathways underlying this sequence remain an
empirical question rather than something that can be established by formal analysis alone.
By the time we arrive at “P implies Q,” almost all of this concrete content has been deliberately removed. We no longer
know whether P means “I pushed the cart” or “2 plus 2 equals 4,” nor whether Q means “the cart fell” or “Paris is the capital of
France.” The formalism becomes highly general precisely because it abstracts away from most of the particular content of the
propositions to which it is applied. This abstraction constitutes both its strength and its limitation. The greater the abstraction,
the greater the formal portability of the resulting structure; but formal generality does not establish ontological priority. A formal
relation may become operationally independent of the concrete processes from which its content was abstracted without thereby

becoming genetically independent of those processes. The distinction is therefore fundamental: operational autonomy does not

3.3 The philosophical inversion and the parallel with Kant
The genetic sequence proposed in this thesis begins with the processual cosmos and proceeds through organismic
experience, cognition, and language, eventually reaching abstraction and formal logic. The movement identified here in
Wittgenstein proceeds in the opposite analytical direction: it begins with logical form and moves through the space of possibilities
and the proposition toward the conditions under which meaningful representation of the world is possible. From the genetic
perspective, this reversal produces a fundamental inversion of explanatory order. What emerges at the end of a developmental
and historical process comes to function philosophically as the condition that determines the structure of what precedes it. The
product becomes a foundation, an abstraction becomes a condition of possibility, and a derived formal structure is treated as
though it were an originary structure.
The parallel with Kant arises at this point, although it should not be taken to imply that Kant and Wittgenstein defend
equivalent philosophical theories. Kant investigates the transcendental conditions that make possible experience and cognition,
whereas Wittgenstein, in the Tractatus, investigates the logical conditions that make meaningful representation possible. Their
projects therefore differ substantially in both scope and method. Nevertheless, from the genetic perspective developed here, both
approaches can be examined in relation to a common methodological tension: structures whose natural, historical, or cognitive
genesis could in principle be investigated may subsequently be reformulated as formal conditions of possibility. The question
“How did this structure arise?” is thereby displaced by the question “What must be presupposed for this structure to be possible?”
The latter is a legitimate philosophical question, but answering it does not, by itself, resolve the former. A transcendental or
formal analysis can identify conditions of possibility without thereby providing a genetic account of how those conditions
themselves emerged. The central criticism, therefore, is not directed against transcendental or formal analysis as such, but against
treating an analysis of conditions of possibility as though it exhausted the question of genesis.
3.4 The status of truth and falsity
Wittgenstein needs to explain something important: a proposition can have sense and be false. "Paris is the capital of
Spain" is a sentence we understand perfectly, even though it is false, and this constitutes a real strength of the representational
theory, since sense cannot simply be equated with the actual existence of the represented state of affairs. But it does not follow
from this that the false exists ontologically in the world. In the world there is no additional thing called "the falsity of Paris being
the capital of Spain"; there is a factual organization, and there is a subject capable of constructing a proposition that does not
adequately correspond to that organization. The false belongs to the evaluation of a cognitive-propositional construction relative
to the real; it need not be turned into an ontological component of the cosmos.
This distinction matters scientifically. In scientific work, a false hypothesis can be extremely useful, but "false" means
that the hypothesis was constructed, was confronted with certain processes or observations, and did not find the correspondence
required. Falsity is not a causal entity acting on the phenomenon under study: a bacterium does not change its metabolism because
a scientific proposition about it has the value false. The process occurs, and only afterward can our description be adequate,
inadequate, true, false, partial, or approximate. Truth value thus belongs to an epistemological-propositional level and should
not be confused with ontological processuality itself.
Truth-functions are extraordinarily powerful tools, but a truth table organizes only true and false; it does not
automatically represent causality, temporality, force, history, development, genetic dependency, or process. If "P implies Q" is
true according to a given combination of values, this does not mean that P caused Q, nor that P and Q belong to the same process,

Truth, in turn, also needs to be repositioned at its own level. The cosmic process occurs, the proposition is constructed,
and only afterward is a certain epistemological relation established between proposition and process. Saying that "P is true" does
not mean adding an entity called "truth" to the cosmos; it means evaluating P relative to some reality, criterion, or system. This
allows the objectivity of truth to be preserved without turning true and false into things existing at the same level as the
phenomena described.
3.5 The limits of the distinction between simple and complex propositions
Wittgenstein’s framework requires a distinction between elementary and complex propositions. Yet consider the
proposition “the hammer fell.” At the level of linguistic form, it may appear relatively simple. However, understanding the term
“hammer” already presupposes a substantial degree of cognitive organization involving object recognition, form, function,
persistence, individuation, memory, and categorization. Similarly, understanding “fell” requires the apprehension of movement,
spatial position, temporal sequence, change, and the relation between prior and subsequent states. The proposition is therefore
formally simple only relative to a particular analytical decomposition that treats “hammer” and “fell” as already constituted units.
From psychogenetic, semantic, ontological, and processual perspectives, however, neither term is simple in any comparable
sense. The distinction between simple and complex may consequently represent a useful feature of formal analysis without
corresponding to a natural division within the world or within cognition itself.
A related difficulty can be identified in the treatment of Moorean certainties, such as “here is a hand.” When expressions
such as “this hand,” “this chair,” or “this sign” are treated as immediately given or epistemically basic, the analysis may
presuppose objects that have already been individuated and stabilized as recognizable units. Yet the world need not present itself
to cognition in the form of objects already separated, categorized, and linguistically identified. Individuation can instead be
understood as emerging through the interaction of perceptual history, sensorimotor activity, practical engagement, memory,
language, and prior relational structures. What appears retrospectively as a self-evident unit may therefore be the outcome of
processes through which distinctions have been established and maintained.
This consideration also bears directly on the formal ordering of objects and relations. To posit A, B, and C as already
constituted entities and only afterward investigate the relations among them may reverse the order of constitution. From a
processual perspective, A and B may become distinguishable as A and B precisely through their participation in prior networks
of interaction and differentiation. The relation would therefore not merely connect two independently constituted entities; it
would participate in the processes through which those entities acquire their relative identity in the first place. The distinction
between objects and relations consequently becomes more complex than a simple opposition between independently existing
units and the connections subsequently established among them.
3.6 From processuality to objectification: the increasing levels of theorization
The alternative offered by Cognitive Psychiatry can be expressed as follows: we do not necessarily begin with simple
objects, we begin with processuality. Within it, relatively stable differentiations arise, which can be apprehended by organisms.
Recurrence allows recognition; recognition allows cognitive stabilization; stabilization allows objectification; objectification can
be linguistically shared; and only much later can we treat that unit as an "object." The path thus runs from process to
differentiation, from differentiation to stabilization, from stabilization to objectification, and from objectification to conceptual
object, rather than from simple object to relation and from there to world, as the Tractatus proposes.

This conception can be expressed through a scheme of increasing abstraction. At the first level we have a concrete
processual experience, such as "I push a cart, it rolls, it falls off the table." At the second level, we replace concrete events with
variables and obtain "if P, then Q." At the third, we strip away even the content of P and Q and retain only the conditional form,
"if... then...." At the fourth, this form is thought of as a generic structure of relation, P in correspondence with Q. At the fifth and
final level, we finally arrive at the abstract category "relation." Philosophy frequently begins at this last level and tries to explain
the first; the present theory proposes the reverse path, that of explaining how concrete experience could produce the intermediate
levels up to reaching the abstract category. This is the naturalization of form.
3.7 Mathematical abstraction, language, and the four levels of sense
Mathematics demonstrates just how far this abstraction can go. It manages to progressively strip away the particularities
of a red apple, a green apple, a stone, or a finger, and retain only the unit "1," then "1 plus 1 equals 2." This capacity is
extraordinary, but it does not mean that the concrete universe is constituted in the same way as the formal system used to represent
it. Mathematics can manipulate formalized units precisely because it has carried out an enormous prior labor of abstraction; the
efficiency of abstraction does not prove that the real is originally abstract.
It is at this point that the attempt to bring language and calculus together meets its limit. Mathematics can, in certain
regions, operate with highly desemanticized symbols or with rigidly controlled contents. Natural language, by contrast, has to
deal with context, action, time, intention, causality, praxis, history, the body, interlocution, ambiguity, and processes. Treating
language as a whole as though it could be reconstructed from the same kind of combinatorics that operates in a logical calculus
means reducing an extremely rich processual reality to an impoverished formal structure. It does not follow from the fact that
mathematics is a formal language that all language is essentially mathematical, much less that the world is structured like a
calculus.
We can thus distinguish four levels that a sentence may satisfy. The first is syntactic organization, in which words obey
grammatical rules. The second is formal manipulability, in which we can represent parts of the sentence as P and Q and apply
logical operations. The third is semantic intelligibility, in which we manage to construct some signification. The fourth is
processual anchoring, in which the articulation expressed adequately corresponds to or refers to processes, relations, or
possibilities constituted in the real. These four levels are not identical, and the fact that an expression satisfies the first two does
not automatically guarantee the last two. This is precisely one of the limits of the logical-formal reduction of language.
3.8 The sense of the false proposition and the return of psychologism
This distinction of levels finally allows us to explain false propositions without granting the formal the primacy
Wittgenstein gives it. How can we understand "Paris is the capital of Spain"? Because we separately possess cognitive processes
and already constituted concepts: Paris, capital, Spain, the relations between cities and countries, and the very structure "X is the
capital of Y." The mind can recombine previously constituted elements, and this recombination can produce an intelligible
proposition that does not correspond to the current factual state. The possibility of falsity can thus be explained by a path that
runs from experience to memory, from memory to concepts, from concepts to recombination, from recombination to proposition,
and from proposition to confrontation with the real. We need not turn falsity into an ontological component, nor posit a
transcendental logical space independent of cognitive genesis.
Logical-transcendental philosophy often seeks to distance itself from psychologism, but expressions appear such as
"conditions for us to be able to speak of facts" or "conditions for a proposition to have sense." Who speaks? Who understands?
Who represents? Who assigns sense? Who distinguishes P from Q? Who applies "and"? Who recognizes a rule? These operations
do not exist in a vacuum; they are performed by someone. By impersonalizing them, we do not eliminate the subject, we merely

This thesis need not, even so, defend classical psychologism, according to which a logical law would simply be an
empirical generalization about how people actually think, since that would be another reduction. The position defended here is
more sophisticated: a formal structure, once constituted, can possess its own rules, can be studied independently of its genesis,
and can achieve enormous operational autonomy. However, synchronic autonomy does not eliminate diachronic genesis. A rule
can function today independently of the history that produced it, but its current autonomy does not prove the absence of history.
This allows the value of logic to be fully recognized. Once a formal system has been created, we can ask whether a
derivation is valid, whether the system is consistent, whether the formula follows from the premises; these are legitimate
questions. A mental operation, insofar as it is actually performed, is a real process; insofar as it is a formal derivation, it can be
valid or invalid; insofar as it is a system, it can be consistent or inconsistent; insofar as it is a claim about the cosmos, it can be
true or false. The error begins when these levels are fused. A formally valid derivation may start from premises that do not
correctly describe the world; a system may be consistent without being an adequate description of the cosmos; a mental operation
may actually occur and still produce a false conclusion. The reality of the operation, the validity of the inference, the consistency
of the system, and the truth about the world are, therefore, different properties.
3.9 Critical synthesis of the Tractarian architecture
We can now bring together the problems traversed. Wittgenstein starts from simple objects, from combinatory
possibilities internal to objects, from states of affairs, from the independence of states of affairs, from elementary propositions,
from truth-functions, from complex propositions, and from a logical space of possibilities. The processual critique asks, at each
stage, where this came from: where did the object come from, where did its individuation come from, where did its relational
possibilities come from, where did the distinction between simple and complex come from, where did the capacity to represent
come from, where did the connectives come from, where did signification come from, where did logical form itself come from.
Answering that "this belongs to logical structure" does not resolve these questions; it merely transfers all of them to a formal
domain taken as originary.

4. Discussion
4.1 Dialogue with the contemporary literature (2021 to 2026)
Over the last five years, several independent lines of research have converged, even while starting from distinct
vocabularies, on the same central points of this critique: that logical form is a late achievement of cognitive processes, rather
than a structure prior to them.
In the philosophy of logic, the naturalization program continues to be developed. Santos (2021a, 2021b) argues that
enculturation and the choice among alternative logical theories have a historical-cognitive character, and the most recent volume
gathering this line of work explicitly describes logics as "cognitive tools," human creations designed to facilitate cognitive
operations, which brings logic closer to a technology than to a science of eternal objects (Santos, 2025). This reformulation is
directly compatible with the thesis defended here that the operational autonomy of the formal does not demonstrate its genetic
priority.
The psychology of conditional reasoning offers the most direct empirical support for the distinction between level 2 and
level 3 presented in subsection 3.2. Recent studies systematically show that ordinary speakers do not evaluate indicative
conditionals according to the semantics of material implication, but rather according to measures of inferential strength or

conditional probability (Douven, Elqayam, & Mirabile, 2022; Sikorski, 2023; Over & Cruz, 2023). Ordinary human cognition
itself thus resists treating "if P, then Q" as a purely extensional relation between truth values, preferring instead to mentally
reconstruct a relation of relevance or dependency between contents, which empirically corroborates the distinction between
cognitive connection and formal connection proposed in this article.
Regarding the cognitive origin of mathematical abstraction, recent reviews in cognitive neuroscience show that
numerical and arithmetical representations remain associated with concrete sensorimotor experiences even in their most abstract
uses, supporting a continuum between embodied cognition and abstract concept, rather than a rupture between them (Sixtus,
Krause, Lindemann, & Fischer, 2023). Studies in mathematics education show, in the same direction, that geometric abstraction
emerges from concrete sensorimotor activities before consolidating into independent symbolic operation (Boonstra, Kool,
Shvarts, & Drijvers, 2023). These findings corroborate the sequence process, differentiation, stabilization, objectification, and
abstraction proposed in subsection 3.6, as opposed to the inverse sequence simple object, relation, world, of the Tractatus.
The ontogeny of the distinction among the three levels presented in subsection 3.2 finds direct support in recent
developmental psychology research. Studies on causal learning in infants and young children show that the ability to recognize
real causal chains emerges early, supported by sensorimotor, social, and statistical mechanisms, and long precedes any domain
of formal logical language (Basch, 2024). In the same direction, research on the acquisition of conditional connectives shows
that young children understand "if" statements first in a pragmatic function, tied to real cause-and-effect relations or to concrete
speech acts, and only gradually extend this use to more abstract and decontextualized contexts (Lemen, Lieven, & Theakston,
2024). These findings offer direct ontogenetic evidence for the thesis that the connective "if... then" does not create the relation,
but expresses a relation whose significant possibility was previously constituted processually.
As for the individuation of objects, a problem central to subsection 3.1, recent research in cognition and language shows
that the distinction between object and substance, or between bounded and unbounded event, is not supplied a priori by the
physical world, but results from principles of construal, or cognitive elaboration, shared across the spatial and temporal domains
and, to a large extent, universal across languages (Lee & Papafragou, 2026). The very subject-object pair that the Tractatus takes
as its starting point is, according to this literature, the product of a cognitive operation of individuation, rather than a primitive
ontological datum.
In contemporary ontology, the tension between process and object remains an open and actively debated problem.
Recent reviews discuss how processual ontologies, by privileging becoming and continuity, risk reducing objects to mere
epiphenomena, whereas object-based ontologies, by privileging individuality, assume a universe of separate substances (Massie,
2025). This debate confirms that the choice between process-first and object-first, the very core of this critique of the Tractatus,
remains a live philosophical question, not one already resolved in favor of the object.
On the side of genetic epistemology, recent reassessments of Piaget's work highlight precisely its process-relational and
action-based character as a point of contact with contemporary embodied, enactive, and interactive approaches to cognition,
suggesting that the processual approach to cognitive genesis, of which the Cognitive Psychiatry proposed here is an heir, remains
an active and empirically fruitful research program (Carpendale & Lewis, 2021).
The connection between this processual framework and psychiatric practice has found direct support in the most recent
clinical literature. Within Wittgensteinian epistemology itself, a current debate explicitly distinguishes ordinary beliefs,
psychiatric delusions, and the so-called hinge commitments discussed in subsection 3.5: although both delusions and Moorean
certainties function as beliefs in the everyday sense of the term, neither constitutes a knowledge-apt belief in the strict
epistemological sense, and delusions cannot be equated with hinge commitments (Pritchard, 2024). This distinction, formulated
from within the Wittgensteinian tradition itself, reinforces that background certainties and pathological beliefs do not share the
same cognitive origin, countering any reading that treats both as products of a single, given logical structure. In computational

Also relevant is the discussion of the limits of abstraction within the cognitive sciences themselves. Analyzing
historically how neuroscience builds its models, Chirimuuta (2024) argues that every theoretical simplification, including logical

direct and neutral mirroring of reality. This argument reinforces, from the philosophy of science, the thesis defended in subsection
3.2 that the efficiency of abstraction does not prove that the real is originally abstract.
Finally, it is worth noting that even the most radically anti-computationalist and processual programs of contemporary
cognitive science, such as enactivism, which rejects mental representations of fixed content and conceives cognition as dynamic
coupling between organism and environment, have recently been formalized mathematically without this implying that form is
given priority over process: the model is built from enactivist principles, not the reverse (Weinstein, Sakcak, & LaValle, 2022).
This result concretely illustrates the central thesis of this article, that formalization can be obtained downstream of a processual
theory of cognition, preserving the operational autonomy of form without inverting its genesis.
4.2 Implications for Cognitive Psychiatry
The alternative proposed here can be condensed into a single genetic sequence: cosmos, processuality, causal and cocausal relations, organisms embedded within these processes, praxis, perception and memory, cognitive re-enactment and
anticipation of processes, stabilization of regularities, objectification, communication, language, concepts, abstraction, abstract
relations, formalization, and, ultimately, logic. Within this framework, logic is not denied or reduced to an arbitrary construction;
rather, it is naturalized and assigned a genetic position within a broader processual history. Logical form is consequently
understood as an emergent achievement of increasingly abstract cognitive and linguistic processes, rather than as an autonomous
or mysterious structure posited as prior to the lived world. Its formal validity remains intact within the domain of logic, but its
ontological and genetic status is no longer assumed to precede the processes from which formal abstraction may have emerged.

5. Conclusion
The critique developed here does not claim that formal logic is useless, that material implication is mathematically
erroneous, or that Wittgenstein failed to recognize the distinction between language and world. Such claims would constitute an
overly simplistic and historically imprecise criticism. The issue is more fundamental. The Tractatus provides a remarkably
systematic formal reconstruction of the conditions under which propositions can represent possible states of affairs and of the
manner in which complex propositions can be analyzed as truth-functions. Yet precisely because of the power and generality of
this formalization, it can obscure the processes, relations, and cognitive operations that have been bracketed out in order to obtain
the formal structure. The question, therefore, is not whether the formal system is valid on its own terms, but whether its formal
autonomy should be taken to disclose the genetic or ontological structure of the phenomena from which its abstractions are
derived.
Before P and Q, processes existed. Before “if... then...,” relations existed. Before concepts, there were operations of
differentiation, comparison, and stabilization. Before the logical object, there was a processual field within which something

could progressively become objectified. Before truth-functional representation, there were organisms capable of acting,
perceiving, comparing, remembering, anticipating, differentiating, affirming, denying, and recombining representations. The
difficulty arises when philosophy takes the products of this long developmental and historical trajectory, such as P, Q, true, false,
“and,” “or,” “not,” and “if... then...,” and treats them as explanatory keys for the conditions that made their own emergence
possible. The resulting inversion is that the derivative is made to explain the originary, the formal is treated as though it governed
the real, logical conditions displace genetic questions, possibility displaces process, formal concatenation is conflated with causal
organization, and truth value comes to occupy the place of effective relations with the world.
Processual Cognitive Psychiatry proposes what may be described as an inversion of this inversion. Rather than
beginning with formal structures and asking how the world can be represented within them, it begins with process and asks how
processes could give rise to organisms capable of objectifying, conceptualizing, signifying, abstracting, and eventually
formalizing relations. Along this trajectory, material implication occupies a comparatively late position. It represents an
extraordinarily refined form of formal connection, one in which P need not cause Q, explain Q, or share any processual history
with Q. This independence is precisely part of its formal power, but it is also why material implication cannot simply be projected
back onto the world as a general model of how relations are constituted.
The convergence between this philosophical critique and findings from the psychology of reasoning, cognitive
neuroscience, research on linguistic individuation, genetic epistemology, and naturalized approaches to the philosophy of logic,
reviewed in Section 4.1, suggests that the proposed inversion is not merely a product of philosophical interpretation and may
instead constitute a hypothesis amenable to empirical investigation. The central distinctions can be summarized as follows. The
possibility of formally linking P and Q does not demonstrate that P and Q are processually connected. The possibility of assigning
a truth value does not demonstrate that truth and falsity are ontological constituents of the cosmos. The possibility of treating
something as simple does not establish its ontological or cognitive simplicity. The possibility of formalizing a relation does not
demonstrate that formal structure is prior to the relation itself. Above all, the operational autonomy achieved by logic at the
culmination of a process of abstraction does not demonstrate that logic was genetically autonomous at the beginning of that
process.
This may constitute the fundamental inversion that a genetic, processual Cognitive Psychiatry introduces into the
interpretation of early Wittgenstein: form does not found process; rather, form is one of the possibilities that process itself makes
available.
