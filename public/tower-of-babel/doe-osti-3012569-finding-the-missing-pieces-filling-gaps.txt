ABSTRACT
High-throughput omics technologies such as DNA sequencing have made the sequencing and
computational assembly of microbial genomes recovered from the environment relatively
routine. Computational inference of the protein products encoded by these genomes, and the
associated biochemical functions, should enable the accurate prediction and modeling of
microbial metabolism, organismal interactions, and ecosystem processes. However, a lack of
scalable, probabilistic protein annotation tools limits the full potential of modeling for
understanding the metabolism and biogeochemical cycles of microbial communities. Our
approach to improve inference of protein annotations and metabolic models relied on learning
from and emulating expert manual curation, leveraging software engineering and data science
best practices to scale up the throughput and accuracy of annotations and metabolic model
construction, building software to objectively evaluate different annotation strategies, and more
closely linking the protein annotation and metabolic model inference process. Outcomes of this
research include several improved or new computational tools, including DRAM (Distilled and
Refined Annotation of Metabolism) for annotating microbial genomes with protein function and
metabolic traits, CAMPER (Curated Annotations for Microbial Polyphenol Enzymes and
Reactions) for annotating key polyphenol metabolisms, EC-Bench for comprehensive and
unbiased benchmarking of annotation tools, and several apps available via the DOE Systems
Biology Knowledgebase (KBase) for building genome-scale metabolic models. We demonstrate
that these tools allow us to scalably annotate and understand thousands of genomes for
microbial communities from a variety of systems and test cases, including rivers, thawing
permafrost, and gut microbiomes. All of these computational tools are available as open-source
software, with most broadly and easily accessible to the scientific community via KBase apps.

Brief technical description of most relevant projects/publications.
Introduction
The objective of this research was to innovate upon and scale up manual expert curation
approaches in order to create and improve annotation methods for microbial genomes and
metagenomes. Our goal was to create easy-to-use and metabolism-centric computational tools
for high-throughput protein annotation, to link improvements in protein annotation to metabolic
modelling improvements, and to disseminate these tools for use by the larger scientific
community. We reasoned that improved protein annotation tools would ultimately enable
improved prediction of microbial phenotypic traits and inter-organism interactions in the context
of of community metabolic models for a wide variety of ecosystems important to biogeochemical
cycling and bioenergy.
The results of this project have been shared via publications (all of which are publicly available /
open-access), and presentations to the scientific community, including at professional meetings
and to KBase users. All datasets and software developed during this project is publicly
available at either KBase (https://www.kbase.us), as datasets and new or improved KBase
applications, and via software with open-source licences shared at public repositories. We
demonstrated via several real-world applications the utility of these improved computational
tools in numerous publications with a broad range of scientific questions.
Approach
To complete this research, we leveraged project expertise in genome-centric microbial
metagenomics, microbial physiology and expert genome curation, metabolic modeling, machine
learning / artificial intelligence, and dissemination of software and data science tools via the
Department of Energy Systems Biology Knowledgebase (KBase). Our strategy was to 1) build
annotation tools that modeled how biologists integrate synergistic protein annotations into
pathways, traits, and phenotypes, 2) integrate these expert annotations with insights from
metabolic models, 3) develop and characterize newer annotation approaches, and 4) implement
our tools on real-world data from complex microbial communities of DOE relevance.
Most relevant projects, publications, and findings
  I.​ Improvements and extensions to the annotation tool DRAM.
One of the major outcomes of this research was the significant improvement and expansion of
the annotation tool DRAM (Distilled and Refined Annotation of Metabolism), along with
improvements to usability. DRAM development is led by the team at Colorado State University
(https://github.com/WrightonLabCSU/DRAM).
DRAM has been implemented as an App in KBase (as kb_DRAM). We demonstrate in our
publication that this makes it easy for a broad user base to benefit from high-quality annotations
that integrate with other KBase apps and outputs (Shaffer et al., 2023). The code base for the
DRAM KBase app is open-source and publicly available
(https://github.com/WrightonLabCSU/kb_DRAM). DRAM-v, a viral-specific version of DRAM,
was also made available to KBase users.

 2

kb_DRAM builds on the core DRAM code base, for which we also implemented a number of
significant improvements. To increase annotation speed, we improved the profile-based
homology searches. Combined with other refactoring, this significantly increased annotation
throughput. These software engineering improvements allowed us to use DRAM for annotation
of very large shotgun genomics datasets such as the Genome Resolved Open Watersheds
database (GROWdb; see Borton et al., 2025, and IV. below).
DRAM also now includes a rich trait classifier and other specialized modules for integrating
multiple protein-level annotations into broader microbial traits. For example, genomes can be
annotated with larger metabolic cycles, including methanogenic, respiratory, and photosynthetic
roles. The incorporation of the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) as a
DRAM data source allows DRAM to now also annotate antimicrobial resistance traits. All of
these traits can be assigned at scale, and help complement and improve inferences based on
metabolic modeling, by oﬀering validation or alternative hypotheses to modeling-based calls.
Polyphenol metabolism is one significant example of a trait which previously required significant
expert manual curation, but can now be curated in a high-throughput automated fashion. We
developed the Curated Annotations for Microbial Polyphenol Enzymes and Reactions
(CAMPER). CAMPER is available as open-source software
(https://github.com/WrightonLabCSU/CAMPER). Development of CAMPER is described in a
preprint (https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.09.24.559193v3) and in a publication that
used CAMPER and DRAM to understand polyphenol metabolism in the context of the thawing
permafrost carbon cycle via annotation of genome-resolved metatranscriptomes (McGivern et
al., 2024). CAMPER can be run as a standalone tool, but we and others now more frequently
run it as an integrated database in DRAM, where outputs and visualizations are seamlessly
integrated with other annotations and trait visualization and analysis tools.
 II.​ Improvements and new tools in KBase for improved metabolic modeling.
One of the core functionalities in KBase is the ability to build genome-scale metabolic models.
At their core, genome-scale metabolic models rely on accurate and complete protein
annotations. This project resulted in multiple advancements to KBase apps that improved the
ability of users to integrate multiple annotation sources (including from DRAM) to build metabolic
models, and to use these multiple annotation sources for improved gapfilling of models.
Specifically, we improved the MS2 - Build Prokaryotic Metabolic Models app in KBase to now
permit the use of alternative annotations when building a model. Users can select exactly which
combinations of annotations are used when building a model, and the order in which they
should be prioritized. We have also created a new
MS2 - Improved Gapfill Metabolic Models app which enables users to use these alternative
annotations sources when gapfilling a model to grow in a new condition or represent a new
phenotype.
A key limitation on the value of these advancements is the limited number of genomes for which
measured phenotype data exists. To overcome this limitation, we developed apps in KBase for
training and applying ML classifiers to predict phenotypes based on annotated genome
functions: Build Genome Classifier, and Predict Phenotype. There is a preprint manuscript
describing these apps and advances in bioRxiv: Machine Learning-Driven Phenotype

 3

Predictions based on Genome Annotations. An updated and improved version of this
manuscript will soon be submitted. We also further extended our phenotype prediction work
with greatly improved training sets and ML algorithms, and a manuscript on this work will also
soon be submitted.
Next, we combined our improved model gapfilling methods with these ML methods for
phenotype prediction by automatically fitting our models to positively predicted phenotypes. This
improved model accuracy in phenotype prediction from 56% to 72% when evaluated on 300
genomes with available biolog phenotype data. We applied these methods to improve models
constructed for 2093 surface water genomes described in the initial GROWdb publication (see
section IV below).
 III.​ Systematic benchmarking of Enzyme Commission number prediction in the context of
 newer annotation approaches.
Most protein annotation tools, including DRAM, primarily rely on sequence homology to infer
function. This has served the community well for decades because of the relationship between
sequence, structure, and function, but also has the risk of false positive and false negative
functional assignments. More recently, several innovative approaches to enzyme function
prediction have been presented, including those that rely on advances in data science, machine
learning, neural networks, and large language models. One popular ontology for describing
enzyme function in particular has been Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers. However, after we
implemented and released a new KBase app for EC number inference based on training
Generalized Language Models (GLMs; also known as Large Language Models) on protein
sequences (https://github.com/kbaseapps/KB-GLM4EC), we noticed there was a surprising
lack of systematic comparison of the performance of EC prediction (annotation) tools. This
makes it difficult to evaluate the various strengths and weaknesses of traditional and new
approaches to functional inference. In addition, because most annotations in existing
databases have been assigned via homology, it is difficult to develop a broad "gold standard" for
evaluating different prediction tools.
Given these observations, we implemented a unified platform to systematically benchmark
existing and future EC number prediction methods under consistent assumptions (Davoudi et
al., 2026). The benchmark, EC-Bench, is flexible and open-source
(https://github.com/dsaeedeh/EC-Bench), which will facilitate the future development and
evaluation of new methods for computational enzyme annotation. The initial publication and
release includes a representative collection of state-of-the-art EC number prediction models.
Importantly, we compared these models via curated standardized datasets (with train and test
splits), and multiple EC prediction tasks including exact EC number prediction, EC number
completion, and EC number recommendation. The metrics we used to evaluate the different
models cover accuracy, robustness, model agreement, and efficiency. This includes novel
evaluation metrics that rely not just on exact EC number matches, but that are
metabolic-pathway-aware and potentially more useful for evaluating how "close" an inferred
function is to the truth.
Importantly, our unbiased evaluation of EC number prediction models shows that there are stark
differences in the performance of existing models. However, models also vary in their

 4

performance depending on the evaluation task, and vary in their performance depending on the
specific functions being predicted (e.g. the location in the EC hierarchy). Collectively, this
means that ensemble approaches might have an important role in state-of-the field EC number
prediction, and we demonstrate this with initial simple ensemble models in our publication.
In summary, we anticipate that a robust benchmarking framework will help the field of EC
number prediction advance more quickly. This in turn should help efforts to use these
predictions for microbial trait inference, and metabolic modeling.
 IV.​ Application of new annotation and metabolic modeling tools to a wide variety of complex
 microbial communities of DOE relevance.
In addition to developing new tools for metabolism-centric annotation at scale, another major
objectives of this research was to demonstrate the real-world applicability of these tools with
microbial communities of varying complexity. We have used multiple scientific questions and
use cases to iterate through testing and refinement of the annotation and metabolic modeling
tools described above.
Our most extensive test case for applying and refining our annotation tools was the Genome
Resolved Open Watersheds database (GROWdb) (Borton et al., 2025). The initial GROWdb
publication reported 2,093 new dereplicated metagenome assembles genomes (MAGs)
sampled from 106 sites across US surface waters. We used our annotation and metabolic
modeling tools to annotate these genomes and functionally describe the expression of inferred
metabolic traits via metatranscriptomics. This allowed for description and prediction of microbial
community structure and biogeochemistry in US Rivers.
Annotation and analysis of the GROWdb MAGs relied heavily on DRAM and KBase metabolic
modeling tools. For example, the DRAM-inferred microbial traits were used to assign carbon
metabolism, photometabolism, and, broadly, respiratory capacity. Subsequently, genome-scale
metabolic models were built for all 2093 MAGs in KBase. The binned genomes, annotations,
and metabolic models are all available as public narratives and DOI-containing datasets in
KBase, available to the entire scientific community for further analysis in KBase or externally
(https://doi.org/10.25982/109073.30/1895615).
In addition to GROWdb, we also used the annotation tools developed as part of this project to
annotate 3634 high and medium-quality, dereplicated MAGs derived from methane-emitting
freshwater wetlands, refining the DRAM trait classifier in the process. This project further
demonstrated that the tools scale appropriately for a project where phenotypic trait
characterization simply could not be done with manual expert curation for all genomes. The
resulting genome resource is available as the Multi-omics for Understanding Climate Change
(MUCC) v2.0.0 database.
We also applied these tools to understand gut microbiomes (Kokkinias et al., 2024; Leleiwi et
al., 2023), the biogeochemistry and community interactions (including via metaproteomics) in
other river studies (Rodríguez-Ramos et al., 2023, 2022), and polyphenol metabolism in thawing
permafrost (McGivern et al., 2024). This last study required developing new software and a
trait-specific database (CAMPER, described above) that again scaled expert curation.
