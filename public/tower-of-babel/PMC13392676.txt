Structural basis of opioid receptor activation by PCP and ketamine
Authors: Qianru Jiang, Jianming Han, Eve J Fine, Nokomis Ramos-Gonzalez, Vipin Ashok Rangari, Micaela V Ruiz, Madalyn L Critz, Carl-Mikael Suomivuori, Jing Wang, Talia L Albert, Kyle Whiddon, Kunpeng Li, Michael J Robertson, Xi-Ping Huang, Benjamin B Land, Susruta Majumdar, Jonathan F Fay, Ron O Dror, Tao Che
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKetamine offers rapid relief for treatment-resistant depression and severe pain in the clinic, providing immediate benefits that traditional medications often fail to deliver. While its antagonistic action at the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) is a key mechanism, ketamine’s dual nature as both a promising treatment and a drug with abuse potential suggests its therapeutic effects extend beyond NMDAR inhibition. Here we provide structural evidence of human opioid receptors bound to ketamine and its parent analog phencyclidine (PCP), supporting that both ligands can directly bind and activate opioid receptors. The structures, together with site-directed mutagenesis and structure–activity relationship studies, identify key motifs involved in ketamine and PCP recognition and efficacy modulation. Furthermore, we determine the structure of the ligand-free state of human κ opioid receptor, revealing molecular details before ligand engagement. Compared to PCP, ketamine displays more notable binding dynamics in the orthosteric site that may contribute to its unique pharmacology at opioid receptors. Our findings highlight the importance of including opioid receptors to fully understand ketamine’s versatility in clinical settings.Subject terms: Electron microscopy, Drug discovery

Main

Ketamine and phencyclidine (PCP) are small-molecule drugs with diverse pharmacological profiles and mixed effects on human physiology1. PCP, the first arylcyclohexylamine dissociative hallucinogen, is known for inducing prolonged highs and substantial cognitive and emotional alterations, including psychosis and violence in humans2–4. In contrast, ketamine was initially found as a dissociative anesthetic for induction and maintenance of anesthesia and has recently gained increasing attention for its rapid and lasting antidepressant effects in treatment-resistant depression5,6. In addition, ketamine exhibits a much lower abuse potential compared to PCP. Since the 1970s, racemic ketamine has been used as a safe and effective anesthetic7, and (S)-ketamine was approved for depression treatment in 2019 (ref. 8). Beyond the anesthetic and antidepressant effects, ketamine has also been found to be an effective analgesic in both acute and chronic pain states9. The working dose for analgesia in the clinic is lower than that used for anesthesia10, indicating that analgesic and anesthetic properties may have different mechanisms.

Both ketamine and PCP primarily act as noncompetitive antagonists at the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR)11. Their antagonism at NMDAR has long been considered the molecular mechanism underlying ketamine’s desirable and undesirable effects12,13. However, not all NMDAR antagonists exhibit antidepressant or analgesic activity as ketamine does14–16. For example, the more selective NMDAR antagonist, MK-801, fails to demonstrate antidepressant activity2 and does not directly elicit analgesia17. This discrepancy suggests the existence of functional crosstalk between NMDAR and other endogenous systems. Indeed, the actions of ketamine and PCP are multifaceted, with their effects in humans likely involving targets beyond the NMDAR, including opioid receptors18. Ketamine exhibits low-micromolar affinity for the NMDAR, with about 10–100-fold higher affinity than opioid receptor18–20. Recent reports have also shown that the opioid antagonist naltrexone blocks the antidepressant effects of ketamine in humans, indicating that the antidepressant effects of ketamine require opioid receptor activation21–24. A long-standing debate centers on whether ketamine shares pharmacological profiles with opioids. Several studies support ketamine’s direct action on opioid receptors, albeit through different mechanisms. For example, some studies have reported that ketamine acts as a full agonist at the κ opioid receptor (KOR) and a partial agonist at the μ opioid receptor (MOR) but exhibits little activity at the δ opioid receptor (DOR), with MOR activation contributing to ketamine’s reinforcing effects and abuse potential19,25. Another study suggests that ketamine and its major metabolites function as positive allosteric modulators of opioid receptors26. Microinjection of ketamine into the periaqueductal gray region has been observed to potentiate morphine analgesia in mice27,28, yet whether this involves a direct or indirect effect on opioid receptors is unknown. Although ketamine has been suggested to interact with KOR19,29, whether this interaction contributes to its physiological effects in vivo remains to be elucidated. It is worth pointing out that KOR agonists, such as the naturally occurring hallucinogen salvinorin A (SalA)30,31, produce similar disruptive effects in mice and humans as ketamine, including reduced motivation and cognitive deficits32. These parallels raise the possibility that KOR signaling may contribute to the cognitive abnormalities associated with ketamine exposure, although further studies are needed to determine the extent and mechanism of this involvement.

Despite accumulating pharmacological evidence supporting interactions and functional crosstalk between ketamine or PCP and opioid receptors, the mechanisms by which these atypical drugs, characterized by low affinity and small molecular size, interact with opioid receptors remain to be fully understood. To better characterize how ketamine and PCP interact with opioid receptors, we used pharmacological assays demonstrating that ketamine and PCP can bind to opioid receptors and function as partial agonists. Our mouse analgesic models show that ketamine-induced antinociception was blocked by either the KOR-selective antagonist aticaprant or the nonselective opioid antagonist naloxone, supporting the functional relevance of ketamine’s engagement of opioid receptors in vivo. We further determined six cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures, including ligand-free KOR, KOR–PCP, KOR–(S)-ketamine, MOR–PCP, MOR–3-OH-PCP and MOR–(S)-ketamine. These structures provide atomic-level evidence that (S)-ketamine and PCP act as orthosteric ligands at opioid receptors. Combining structural analyses with molecular dynamics (MD) simulations reveals dynamic interactions between ketamine and opioid receptors. Complementary mutagenesis and structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies further identify structural and molecular determinants in both receptors and ligands that are important for the actions of ketamine or PCP at opioid receptors. These findings offer valuable insights into the complex actions of ketamine in humans.

Results

Pharmacological characterization of ketamine and PCP at opioid receptors

The NMDAR has been generally accepted as the molecular target responsible for ketamine’s therapeutic benefits. However, recent findings suggest that the efficacy of ketamine is also modulated by the endogenous opioid system, pointing to a multifaceted mechanism of action19,25. To better understand the mode of action of ketamine and PCP on opioid receptors, we characterized their binding profiles and functional activities on KOR, MOR and DOR. In our radioligand-binding and cAMP inhibition assays, all ketamine enantiomers and PCP exhibited higher binding affinity or greater potency at KOR (PCP: pKi = 5.12 ± 0.06, pEC50 = 4.95 ± 0.08; (S)-ketamine: pKi = 5.01 ± 0.05, pEC50 = 4.72 ± 0.05; (R,S)-ketamine: pKi = 4.80 ± 0.06, pEC50 = 4.47 ± 0.09; (R)-ketamine: pKi = 4.39 ± 0.07, pEC50 = 3.92 ± 0.06) and MOR (PCP: pKi = 5.32 ± 0.04, pEC50 = 5.37 ± 0.07; (S)-ketamine: pKi = 5.08 ± 0.04, pEC50 = 5.08 ± 0.05; (R,S)-ketamine: pKi = 4.94 ± 0.05, pEC50 = 4.93 ± 0.12; (R)-ketamine: pKi = 4.61 ± 0.05, pEC50 = 4.65 ± 0.05) compared to DOR (PCP: pKi = 4.22 ± 0.10, pEC50 = 4.42 ± 0.08; (S)-ketamine: pKi = 3.84 ± 0.10, pEC50 = 3.94 ± 0.06; (R,S)-ketamine: pKi = 3.88 ± 0.11, pEC50 = 3.64 ± 0.29; (R)-ketamine: pKi = 3.71 ± 0.15, pEC50 = 3.69 ± 0.14) (Fig. 1a,b, Extended Data Fig. 1a,b and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). The activation of KOR and MOR mediated by PCP and ketamine can be efficiently blocked by their highly selective antagonists, JDTic and CTOP, respectively (Extended Data Fig. 1c). Our results also revealed that ketamine exhibits stereoselective interactions, with (S)-ketamine showing greater affinity and potency than (R)-ketamine (Fig. 1a,b), which is consistent with previous findings19. Unlike previous reports identifying (S)-ketamine as a full agonist of KOR and a partial agonist of MOR19, our data showed that all ketamine enantiomers and PCP function as partial agonists at KOR, MOR and DOR when referenced against SalA, DAMGO and DPDPE, respectively (Fig. 1b, Extended Data Fig. 1b and Supplementary Table 2). (S)-Ketamine has been reported to exhibit positive allosteric activity at opioid receptors; however, we did not observe measurable potentiation by either PCP or (S)-ketamine in the cAMP inhibition or β-arrestin 2 recruitment assays (Extended Data Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 3 and 4).

To compare how ketamine and PCP bind to opioid receptors versus NMDAR in native tissue, we measured their binding affinities in rat brain membranes. Both PCP and (S)-ketamine had higher affinity for NMDAR than for opioid receptors (Extended Data Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 5). PCP bound NMDAR with a pKi of 7.12 ± 0.04, compared to 5.06 ± 0.15 at MOR and 4.60 ± 0.02 at DOR. (S)-Ketamine showed a similar pattern, with a pKi of 6.19 ± 0.05 at NMDAR versus 4.86 ± 0.22 at MOR and 4.56 ± 0.14 at DOR. However, using rat brain tissues with KOR radioligand did not produce detectable specific binding (Extended Data Fig. 3), likely because of KOR’s relatively low abundance and/or high nonspecific binding of 3H-U69,593 in the brain. These data support that ketamine can engage opioid receptors, at least at MOR, in the presence of abundant NMDARs. To further demonstrate the functional activity of ketamine at the MOR and KOR in vivo, we used the warm water tail immersion assay to measure antinociception. A single, subanesthetic dose of ketamine (30 mg kg−1, i.p.) produced a significant increase in tail withdraw latency time 40 min after injection (Fig. 1c). Strikingly, pretreatment with either the KOR-selective antagonist aticaprant (5 mg kg−1) or the nonselective opioid antagonist naloxone (2 mg kg−1) completely blocked the analgesic effect (Fig. 1c). This result corroborates previous data supporting a critical role for opioid receptors in ketamine antinociception and reinforces the in vivo efficacy of ketamine at both MOR and KOR.

Structural determination of ketamine and PCP at opioid receptors

To elucidate the structural basis of PCP and ketamine interactions with opioid receptors, we determined cryo-EM structures of KOR bound to PCP, MOR bound to PCP, KOR bound to (S)-ketamine and MOR bound to (S)-ketamine in complex with the Gi1 heterotrimer at nominal resolutions of 2.6 Å, 2.9 Å, 2.5 Å and 2.9 Å, respectively (Fig. 1d,e, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2 and Supplementary Table 6). Although the high-resolution maps enabled confident modeling of the receptors and G proteins, modeling the ligands proved challenging and required computational guidance. Specifically, determining the stereochemistry of the ligands was difficult for some maps because of the ligands’ small size and extremely low affinity (Fig. 1a). To validate the binding of ketamine and PCP at opioid receptors, we determined two additional G-protein-coupled structures: MOR bound to 3-OH-PCP (3.1 Å) and ligand-free state of KOR (3.4 Å) (Fig. 1d,e, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2 and Supplementary Table 6). The overall structures of KOR in the apo state and in complexes with (S)-ketamine or PCP all adopt a fully active conformation, as KOR is coupled to nucleotide-free G proteins in each structure. These structures closely resemble previously reported active-state KOR structures bound to other agonists (root-mean-square deviation (r.m.s.d.) of less than 0.9 Å for Cα atoms of KOR), featuring a pronounced outward displacement (13.3 Å) of transmembrane helix (TM) 6, a 2.7-Å shift in TM5 and an 8.8-Å outward movement of intracellular loop (ICL) 2, relative to the inactive KOR structure bound to the antagonist JDTic (Extended Data Fig. 4a–c). A comparison of the apo and agonist-bound complexes reveals that Gi1 adopts a similar conformation across these states (Extended Data Fig. 4b). In addition, the overall structures of PCP-bound, 3-OH-PCP-bound and (S)-ketamine-bound MOR are also comparable to previously reported agonist-bound MOR–Gi1 complex structures (r.m.s.d. of less than 0.8 Å)33 (Extended Data Fig. 5d–f).

PCP recognition by KOR and MOR

While PCP has a high potential for abuse, its interactions with opioid receptors have not been thoroughly characterized34. Compared to classic opioid agonists, PCP is a relatively small molecule that includes a piperidine ring group, a phenyl ring group and a cyclohexane ring group (Fig. 1e). Although continuous ligand-like density consistent with PCP was observed in the orthosteric sites of KOR and MOR, unambiguous assignment of these groups within the density maps was challenging because of the similarity of the three six-membered rings. To clarify this ambiguity, MD simulations were used to explore stable PCP poses compatible with the cryo-EM density. We selected the KOR–PCP structure given its better ligand density in the orthosteric site compared to MOR–PCP (Fig. 1d). An initial pose of PCP in the KOR-binding pocket was built (Fig. 2a, left) and this KOR–PCP model was used to start MD simulations. In all simulations, PCP moved away from the initial pose (Extended Data Fig. 5), which prompted us to examine the simulations for poses that were stably adopted. In three of six simulations, PCP stably adopted a different pose, termed pose 1 (Fig. 2a, middle, and Extended Data Fig. 5a), which was compatible with the ligand-density envelope in KOR (Fig. 1d). PCP also occasionally adopted another, less frequently observed pose in our KOR–PCP simulations that also fit within the ligand-density envelope, termed pose 2 (Extended Data Fig. 5b). To gain additional evidence for confident assignment of the PCP-binding pose in the structures, we solved the structure of MOR bound to an asymmetrical PCP analog that can be unambiguously modeled in the binding pocket (Fig. 1d). In this solved structure, the PCP analog clearly adopted a pose similar to pose 1, the most frequently observed pose in the PCP-bound KOR MD simulations (Fig. 1e and Fig. 2a, middle). Because (1) this analog is structurally very similar to PCP; (2) the pose adopted by the analog matches well with one of the two candidate PCP poses from MD simulations; and (3) the orthosteric binding pockets of MOR and KOR in the PCP-bound structures are highly similar (Fig. 2b,c), we adopted this analog-supported and MD-supported pose as our working model for PCP in the KOR–PCP and MOR–PCP cryo-EM structures (Fig. 2a, right). Comparison of the PCP pose to previously reported KOR and MOR structures shows that PCP occupies the orthosteric binding pocket in a region broadly similar to KOR agonists, such as dynorphin A, momSalB and GR89,696, as well as MOR agonists such as β-endorphin, morphine and fentanyl33,35,36 (Extended Data Fig. 6a).

The refined PCP models show broadly similar binding poses in KOR and MOR (Fig. 2b–d). The models suggest that these poses are primarily stabilized by hydrophobic interactions, a unique feature also observed in the binding of the nonnitrogenous ligand momSalB to KOR35. Specifically, the piperidine ring of PCP is oriented toward the extracellular vestibule and is positioned near a hydrophobic pocket formed by Y3.33 and Y/W7.35 (Fig. 2b–d). A salt-bridge interaction between the ligand nitrogen and D3.32 residue is a canonical interaction for many opioid ligands and is often critical for ligand binding and signaling37–39. In our refined PCP models, the piperidine nitrogen is separated from D3.32 by ~4.4 Å in KOR and ~5.2 Å in MOR (Fig. 2b–d), suggesting that a tight, canonical salt bridge is unlikely in these poses. This spatial separation may account for the observation that the D3.32N substitution, which disrupts the potential salt bridge, only reduced PCP binding at KOR (~3.4-fold) and had no effect at MOR (Extended Data Fig. 6b and Supplementary Table 7). However, the D3.32N substitution still reduced the potency of PCP in MOR activation (Fig. 2e, Extended Data Fig. 6c,d, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Tables 8–11), likely because of disruption of the polar interaction network formed by D3.32 and Q2.60, a conserved motif among opioid receptors critical for receptor activation36. Consistent with this, the Q2.60L substitution produced a similar reduction in the potency of PCP-induced KOR and MOR activation (Extended Data Fig. 6d, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Tables 8–11). In the model, the cyclohexane ring of PCP faces the crevice between TM3, TM6 and TM7 and is positioned to engage in hydrophobic interactions with M3.36 and I6.51 (Fig. 2b–d). Meanwhile, the phenyl ring points toward TM5 and TM6, interacting with M3.36, V5.42, I6.51 and H6.52 (Fig. 2b–d). Accordingly, most substitutions expected to weaken PCP–receptor interactions reduced PCP agonist activity at KOR and MOR, except for the M1423.36A substitution in KOR, which increased PCP’s potency and receptor’s basal activity (Fig. 2e, Extended Data Fig. 6c,d, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Tables 8–11) and will be further examined below when comparing apo KOR to ketamine-bound or PCP-bound KOR.

Although PCP adopts similar poses in KOR and MOR, the refined models indicate an approximate 1–2-Å shift of the ligand toward TM3 in KOR compared to its position in MOR, likely to avoid steric clashes between the piperidine ring and the nonconserved residues at Y/W7.35 (Fig. 2f). This displacement results in additional hydrophobic interactions between PCP and L1353.29 in KOR (I1353.29L substitution introduced to increase KOR expression during structural determination; Methods), which are not present in the MOR–PCP structure (Fig. 2f). Consistent with this, PCP activity was significantly reduced by the I1353.29A substitution in KOR, whereas the I1463.29A substitution had no effect on PCP’s potency or efficacy at MOR (Fig. 2g, Extended Data Fig. 6c,d, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Tables 8–11).

3-OH-PCP as a KOR antagonist and MOR agonist

To help confirm the receptor–PCP interactions, we tested a series of PCP analogs (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 7a). SAR analysis revealed that both 3-chloro-PCP (3-Cl-PCP) and 3-methoxy-PCP (3-MeO-PCP) exhibited comparable potency to PCP in activating KOR and MOR (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 12). PCP metabolites hydroxylated on either the piperidine ring (1-(1-phenylcyclohexyl)-4-hydroxypiperidine, PCHP)40 or the cyclohexane ring (trans-4-phenyl-4-piperidinocyclohexanol, t-PPC)41,42 exhibited reduced potency compared to PCP at both KOR and MOR (Extended Data Fig. 7a and Supplementary Table 12). Unexpectedly, compared to PCP, 3-OH-PCP was almost inactive in activating KOR, while exhibiting an approximately 50-fold increase in potency at MOR (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 12). Additionally, 3-OH-PCP exhibited enhanced binding affinity at both KOR and MOR compared to PCP (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Table 13), suggesting a potential antagonist profile at KOR. To test this, we performed cAMP inhibition assays in antagonist mode and confirmed that 3-OH-PCP effectively antagonized U50,488-induced KOR signaling (pIC50 = 5.46 ± 0.33) (Fig. 3d, Extended Data Fig. 7b and Supplementary Table 14). These results confirm that 3-OH-PCP functions as a KOR antagonist but as an MOR partial agonist, a pharmacological profile also observed with some other opioids (for example, buprenorphine, an MOR partial agonist and KOR antagonist43). Specific motifs in opioid receptors have been implicated with different roles in receptor activation. For example, direct interactions with a hydrophobic pocket (formed by the G7.42Y7.43T7.44 motif) are important for MOR antagonism37 but KOR agonism38. In addition, the PCP analogs PCPr and PCsBu, bearing N-propyl and N-sec-butyl substituents, respectively, retained similar activity to PCP at KOR but showed modestly reduced potency (~3.7-fold for PCPr and ~2.2-fold for PCsBu) at MOR (Extended Data Fig. 7a and Supplementary Table 12).

Given its markedly enhanced binding affinity and potency at MOR, we hypothesized that 3-OH-PCP should adopt a stable binding pose in MOR and, thus, determined the cryo-EM structure of MOR bound to 3-OH-PCP and G-protein heterotrimer (Fig. 1d). The solid ligand density enables confident modeling of the ligand with accurate geometry (Fig. 3e). Compared to the density of PCP in MOR, 3-OH-PCP exhibits enhanced ligand density and resolution that suggests the formation of a more stable complex resulting from the increased ligand affinity and potency (Fig. 3f). 3-OH-PCP adopts a binding orientation overall similar to PCP in the orthosteric pocket of MOR (Extended Data Fig. 7c). However, some differences were observed that could account for the enhanced activity of 3-OH-PCP. First, the map suggests an approximate 1–2-Å shift of 3-OH-PCP toward TM5, positioning its 3-OH group to form a hydrogen bond with the backbone carbonyl of K2355.39 (Extended Data Fig. 7c). Second, the phenolic ring of 3-OH-PCP adopts an orientation resembling the first tyrosine residue in endogenous opioid peptides, such as enkephalins, endorphins and dynorphins36 (Fig. 3g). This first tyrosine is known to be essential for the binding and signaling of endogenous peptides at opioid receptors through interactions with TM5 and TM6 (ref. 36). Third, the overall chemical structure and pose of 3-OH-PCP resemble the morphinan-scaffolded opioids such as morphine33 and BU72 (ref. 44) and, in the latter, the phenolic rings can form water-mediated hydrogen-bond interaction networks with Y1503.33 and H2996.52, which are critical for their high affinity and potency at MOR33,36,37 (Extended Data Fig. 7c). Consistent with this, the Y1503.33F and H2996.52A substitutions caused a significant reduction in the agonist activity of 3-OH-PCP at MOR (Extended Data Fig. 7d,e, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 15). A mutant cycle analysis45,46 indicated that Y1503.33F caused a greater reduction in potency for 3-OH-PCP than for PCP (ΔpEC50 = 1.15 versus 0.64), resulting in a ΔΔpEC50 of 0.51, which corresponds to an apparent coupling energy of approximately 0.69 kcal mol−1. These findings support functional coupling between the 3-hydroxyl group of 3-OH-PCP and Y1503.33, aligning with the proposed binding pose. Beyond polar interactions, 3-OH-PCP is further stabilized by hydrophobic contacts with residues from TM3, TM5, TM6 and TM7, as observed for PCP (Fig. 3e). Substitutions of these residues resulted in a reduction in 3-OH-PCP-mediated MOR signaling (Extended Data Fig. 7d,e, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 15).

(S)-ketamine recognition by KOR and MOR

Although ketamine has been reported to interact with opioid receptors, whether these interactions are orthosteric19 or allosteric26 remains unresolved. Our cryo-EM structures reveal (S)-ketamine density in the orthosteric pockets of both KOR and MOR, providing direct structural evidence for orthosteric engagement. While the ligand density in KOR is sufficient to build a binding pose for (S)-ketamine, the ligand density in MOR is not well resolved enough to identify its binding geometry (Fig. 1d). Considering the high structural similarity between KOR and MOR in the orthosteric sites, we propose a similar orientation for (S)-ketamine at MOR to that at KOR (Fig. 4a,b). To gain support for these binding poses, we implemented GemSpot47, which incorporates weighted contributions from computational docking and cryo-EM density restraints, and identified the (S)-ketamine configuration closely aligned with the cryo-EM density and resembling its modeled poses in both KOR and MOR (Extended Data Fig. 8a). The identified pose of (S)-ketamine in KOR or MOR is comparable to that of PCP and its conformation resembles that observed in the NMDAR-bound state48 (Extended Data Fig. 8b,c). In KOR–(S)-ketamine simulations, (S)-ketamine adopted several distinct binding poses (Extended Data Fig. 8d,e), including one that somewhat resembled the cryo-EM pose of (S)-ketamine (Extended Data Fig. 8d, pose S3). The ability of (S)-ketamine to adopt multiple binding poses indicates that (S)-ketamine may have a more flexible binding mode than PCP at opioid receptors, as the latter mainly showed two poses in the MD simulations (Extended Data Fig. 5).

Although chemically similar to PCP, (S)-ketamine is smaller, consisting of a cyclohexanone ring, a chlorophenyl ring and an aminomethyl substituent. (S)-Ketamine primarily engages in hydrophobic interactions within the binding pockets of KOR and MOR. The cyclohexanone ring occupies a subpocket formed by TM3 and TM6, where it forms extensive hydrophobic interactions with residues Y3.33, M3.36 and I6.51 (Fig. 4a–c). In KOR, the cyclohexanone ring additionally interacts with residue L1353.29 (Fig. 4a). The M1423.36A substitution increased the basal activity of KOR and enhanced the potency of (S)-ketamine by approximately 2.4-fold (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Tables 16 and 17), a phenomenon also observed for PCP, which suggests a potential role in receptor constitutive activity.

To further investigate the SAR of (S)-ketamine, we tested a series of analogs with modifications on the amine group and chlorophenyl ring. Compared to (S)-ketamine (-NH-CH3), the desmethyl analog (S)-norketamine (-NH2) exhibited 6.2-fold and 9.5-fold reductions in potency for KOR and MOR activation, respectively (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Table 18). This loss of potency is likely attributable to weakened hydrophobic interactions with I6.51 and Y/W7.35, consistent with the observed reduction in the potency of (S)-ketamine upon alanine or leucine substitutions at these positions (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Tables 16 and 17). Modifications to the chlorophenyl ring revealed that removal of the chlorine atom (DCK) or its substitution with fluorine (2-F-DCK), bromine (2-Br-DCK) or a methoxy group (2-MeO-DCK) had minimal effect on the activity at either receptor (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Table 19), suggesting that the halogen bond with Y3.33 is not essential for ligand engagement at opioid receptors. Previous in vivo studies reported that DCK and 2-F-DCK produce ketamine-like behavioral effects and side-effect profiles in rodents, including rewarding and reinforcing effects in conditioned place preference and self-administration paradigms49–51. Norketamine, a primary metabolite of ketamine, has been reported to produce antinociception in established rodent pain models52,53. Our cAMP data indicate that (S)-norketamine has lower efficacy at MOR than (S)-ketamine, which may also lessen its MOR-mediated adverse effects.

Prebinding conformation revealed by ligand-free KOR

Although G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) have been known to exhibit certain constitutive activity in the absence of a ligand54, capturing their apo or ligand-free states remains challenging because of the intrinsic conformational dynamics and heterogeneity of these receptors. Here, we determined the apo-state structure of KOR in complex with the dominant-negative55 Gi1 protein. The use of dominant-negative G protein appears to be critical, as a recent study using wild-type (WT) G protein failed to resolve the apo-state KOR–G protein complex56. In the apo KOR structure, the orthosteric pocket exhibited no ligand-like density, serving as a negative control that supports the specificity of PCP and (S)-ketamine binding observed in the ligand-bound structures (Fig. 5a).

This apo-state structure also provides insights into the receptor conformation before ligand engagement, which is a state that remains poorly characterized among class A GPCRs57,58. Despite the absence of an orthosteric ligand, the apo-state KOR–Gi1 complex adopts an active conformation (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b). The conformational arrangements of key microswitch motifs, including the P5.50I3.40F6.44 motif, N7.49P7.50xxY7.53 motif, D3.49R3.50Y3.51 motif and the sodium-binding pocket, all exhibit canonical features characteristic of the active state (Extended Data Fig. 4c). We propose that this active-like conformation is primarily enabled by G-protein coupling, as GPCRs intrinsically sample an equilibrium between active and inactive states that can be shifted toward an active-like state by both agonist binding and G-protein coupling59,60.

While the apo structure overall adopts a state similar to the liganded structures presented here, major structural differences are observed within the ligand-binding pocket, where several residues undergo positional and orientational shifts upon ligand engagement (Fig. 5b). In the apo state, residues Y1393.33 and I2906.51 adopt conformations that would sterically clash with ligands such as PCP and (S)-ketamine (Extended Data Fig. 10a). These observations indicate that ligand-specific conformational rearrangements within the binding pocket are required to accommodate and stabilize ligand engagement (Fig. 5c). As shown earlier, the M1423.36A substitution enhanced the potency of both PCP and ketamine at KOR (Figs. 2e and 4d and Supplementary Tables 8 and 16) and increased the basal activity of the receptor (Fig. 5d), indicating enhanced constitutive activation. In MD simulations of apo KOR, the side chain of M1423.36 adopts two distinct conformations (pose 1 and pose 2; Fig. 5e,f), which differs from liganded KOR simulations, in which M1423.36 adopts only one conformation resembling pose 1 (Fig. 5e and Extended Data Fig. 10b). Notably, the pose 2 conformation of M1423.36 seen exclusively in ligand-free KOR simulations occupies the central orthosteric binding pocket, which would sterically interfere with the binding poses of PCP and (S)-ketamine (Fig. 5g). The increased potency observed in the M1423.36A KOR mutant could, thus, be because of the removal of the steric hindrance for ketamine and PCP. However, this effect appears to be specific for ketamine or PCP because the same substitution reduced the potency of high-affinity opioid agonists such as SalA (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 10), momSalB, GR89,696 and MP1104 at KOR35,36,38. This observation suggests that these larger ligands can accommodate or overcome the steric constraint imposed by M1423.36 but rely on interactions provided by the M1423.36 side chain for efficient signal transduction. In contrast, the corresponding M1533.36A substitution in MOR did not increase basal signaling (Fig. 5d) and completely abolished ketamine- and PCP-mediated activity (Figs. 2e and 4d, Extended Data Figs. 6c,d and 9b,c and Supplementary Tables 9 and 17), suggesting that M1533.36 in MOR likely contributes to ligand engagement and activation61, rather than primarily acting as an apo-state steric gate as in KOR. We also analyzed other residues within the binding pocket and observed no substantial differences between apo and liganded simulations (Extended Data Fig. 10c).

Discussion

Ketamine is best known for its role as an NMDAR antagonist, which is believed to have a crucial role in its rapid antidepressant effects and its use as an anesthetic and analgesic. Its mechanisms of action on opioid receptors remain incompletely understood. In this study, high-resolution cryo-EM structures of (S)-ketamine and PCP bound to KOR and MOR were determined, providing molecular insights into their interactions with opioid receptors and the structural basis for their pharmacological activities. Considering the overlapping activities of ketamine with opioids on MOR and KOR, a hypothesis has been proposed that the physiological interactions of ketamine with opioid receptors may contribute to its complex activities observed in the clinic, including therapeutic analgesia, as well as adverse effects such as abuse liability and hallucinations (Fig. 5h).

Our cryo-EM structures provide direct structural evidence that (S)-ketamine and PCP bind to the orthosteric site of KOR and MOR. Interestingly, multiple binding poses of ketamine have been reported in NMDAR structures48,62. Our MD simulations of ketamine also suggest that (S)-ketamine can adopt multiple, potentially transient binding poses at opioid receptors, which is consistent with its weak binding affinity (~10 µM) at KOR and MOR. The relatively weak binding affinity of both (S)-ketamine and PCP could be attributed to their small molecular size and the predominance of hydrophobic interactions within the orthosteric site. Indeed, the salt bridge between the ligand’s amine and D3.32 that is frequently observed in endogenous peptides and many synthetic small-molecule ligands36,63,64 is weak, if not absent, in ketamine and PCP binding. All these together make the accurate modeling of PCP or ketamine challenging. In addition to MD simulations, further support for the binding mode of these ligands comes from the structure of MOR bound to 3-OH-PCP, which provides enhanced ligand density because of its improved binding affinity and potency at MOR. This improved activity in 3-OH-PCP is achieved by mimicking the phenolic ring commonly observed in high-affinity opioid agonists. It is worth noting that we believe that PCP-binding pose 1 (Fig. 2a, middle), which resembles that of 3-OH-PCP, is more likely. However, the current cryo-EM density, MD simulations and mutagenesis data do not exclude the alternative orientation where the benzene and cyclohexane rings are swapped (Extended Data Fig. 5b). Although we identified solid ligand density in the orthosteric site and (S)-ketamine and PCP showed limited positive allosteric modulatory activity in cAMP or β-arrestin recruitment under our assay conditions, we cannot rule out the possibility that (S)-ketamine may engage alternative binding sites as reported by other studies26.

Some physiological effects of ketamine have been associated with endogenous opioid systems25,65,66. Indeed, the opioid system also has a critical role in physiological responses (for example, analgesia67, abuse potential68, antidepressant effects24,69 and hallucinations70) that overlap with ketamine’s multifaceted effects in the clinic. Notably, not all NMDAR antagonists (for example, MK-801) share ketamine’s therapeutic profile15,71, indicating that NMDAR blockade alone may be insufficient to explain ketamine’s unique pharmacological actions. Ketamine’s spinal analgesia can be blocked by the opioid antagonist naloxone, which also fully abolishes its potentiation of morphine-induced analgesia65. Consistent with these reports, we observed that ketamine-induced antinociception in the warm water tail immersion assay, at a subanesthetic dose, was completely abolished by either naloxone or the KOR-selective antagonist aticaprant (Fig. 1c), suggesting opioid receptor involvement, including a KOR-dependent component, in this response. Interestingly, the degree of antinociception observed is similar in magnitude to that of other KOR-selective agonists such as U50,488 (refs. 72,73), supporting the aticaprant effect. It is possible that, at this dose in mice, ketamine favors KOR agonism over MOR agonism or our assay lacks the dynamic range to capture partial antinociceptive effects. Similarly, ketamine’s antidepressant effects are partially attenuated by the opioid antagonist naltrexone21,74, further implicating opioid receptor involvement. A recent study also demonstrated that activation of the opioid system in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) contributes to ketamine’s antidepressant actions75. Consistent with the findings, mechanism-guided evidence further identified MOR-enriched mPFC somatostatin-expressing interneurons as a central circuit mechanism underlying ketamine’s antidepressant effects76. Using positron emission tomography imaging, a reinforcing intravenous infusion of (S)-ketamine (20 mg kg−1 h−1) was shown to occupy brain MORs in rats, supporting direct MOR engagement in vivo25,77. These findings are consistent with the possibility that ketamine’s therapeutic efficacy may involve the endogenous opioid system. However, the extent and generality of this contribution remain an area of ongoing clinical debate, with reports of preserved antidepressant responses despite naltrexone coadministration78 and of ketamine’s antihyperalgesic effects in humans not being antagonized by naloxone78,79. In our binding assay using the rat brain membrane system, (S)-ketamine showed ~21-fold higher affinity for NMDAR than for MOR and ~43-fold higher affinity for NMDAR than for DOR (Extended Data Fig. 3), consistent with prior measurements18–20. These affinity differences suggest that, in native rat brain membranes, opioid receptors are engaged at substantially higher concentrations than NMDAR. For the commonly used subanesthetic antidepressant regimen (for example, 0.5 mg kg−1 intravenous infusion over 40 min), the reported maximum total plasma concentration (Cmax) is ~185 ng ml−1 (~0.78 µM)80,81. At anesthetic doses or during rapid intravenous induction, total plasma levels can be substantially higher, reaching ~9 µM under anesthetic maintenance and peaking at ~38–108 µM immediately after bolus injection82,83, potentially overlapping with the potency and affinity range we observe at opioid receptors. Therefore, opioid receptor binding is more likely to have a role under high-exposure conditions, such as during anesthesia or intoxication or at transient peak brain levels. Additional research is necessary to identify the specific opioid receptor subtypes, circuits and signaling pathways that connect ketamine to its in vivo effects, especially considering its low potency in activating opioid receptors and the potential that relevant engagement is limited, depending on region and context, by local unbound exposure and receptor occupancy levels.

In summary, this work provides structural evidence for the engagement between ketamine and opioid receptors that may contribute to ketamine’s diverse pharmacological effects. Our study reveals a more dynamic binding profile of ketamine at opioid receptors compared to PCP, suggesting that this behavior may be common among other low-affinity, small-molecule drugs, enabling polypharmacological engagement with various protein targets. These findings highlight the importance of taking opioid receptors into consideration during the mechanistic study of ketamine. An important question is whether ketamine’s therapeutic benefits can be pharmacologically dissociated from its abuse potential by appropriately balanced activation of NMDAR and opioid receptors and the structural insights gained here could guide the optimization of ketamine with reduced side effects.

Methods

Ethics statement

All research complied with all relevant ethical regulations. Animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Washington and conform to the guidelines on the care and use of animals implemented by the National Institutes of Health.

Chemical compounds

Ketamine, PCP, their structural analogs and DPDPE were purchased from Cayman Chemical, DAMGO was purchased from Fisher Scientific and CTOP was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. 3H-U69,593 and 3H-DAMGO were purchased from PerkinElmer and 3H-DPDPE was purchased from Revvity. SalA, BMS 986187, 3H-naltrexone and JDTic were provided as described in the Acknowledgements.

Generation of constructs for cryo-EM

Truncated human KOR (residues 54–358) containing the I1353.29L and S3247.47C substitutions38 and truncated human MOR (residues 54–361) carrying a thermostabilizing substitution (F1583.41W)33,84 were individually subcloned into the pFastBac vector (Thermo Fisher) containing an N-terminal FLAG tag, His tag, C3 protease cleavage site and thermostabilized apocytochrome b562 RIL (BRIL) fusion. Dominant-negative human Gαi1 (S47N, E245A, G203A and A326S)55 was fused to WT human Gγ2 by a flexible 9-aa linker and coexpressed with WT human Gβ1 containing a His tag and C3 protease cleavage site from a single pFastBac vector. The single-chain variable fragment of mAb16 (scFv16)85 was cloned into pFastBac vector with an N-terminal GP67 signal peptide, C-terminal His tag and C3 protease cleavage site.

Expression and purification of opioid receptor–Gi–scFv16 complexes

KOR, MOR, G-protein heterotrimer and scFv16 were expressed in Spodoptera frugiperda Sf9 cells (Expression Systems, 94-001 S) using the Bac-to-Bac baculovirus expression system. Sf9 cells were cultured in ESF 921 medium (Expression Systems) and infected with recombinant baculovirus at a density of 2.5 × 106 cells per ml.

For scFv16 purification, culture medium containing secreted protein was collected by centrifugation 96 h after infection. The supernatant was adjusted to pH 7.5, supplemented with 1 mM NiCl2 and 5 mM CaCl2 to quench chelating agents, stirred for 1 h at room temperature, clarified by centrifugation and filtered through a 0.45-μm (pore size) membrane filter. The supernatant was incubated overnight at 4 °C with Ni-NTA resin (Takara) and 10 mM imidazole. Resin was collected and washed with 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl and 20 mM imidazole and protein was eluted with the same buffer containing 300 mM imidazole. Eluted protein was concentrated and subjected to a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 column (GE Healthcare) in 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl and 100 μM TCEP. Fractions were collected, concentrated, flash-frozen and stored at −80 °C.

The Gi protein heterotrimer was coexpressed with KOR or MOR in Sf9 cells and cells were harvested 48 h after infection. For purification of the receptor–Gi–scFv16 complexes, cell pellets were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 2.5 U of apyrase (New England Biolabs), 10 μM agonist and a protease inhibitor cocktail (500 mM AEBSF, 1 mM E-64, 1 mM leupeptin and 150 nM aprotinin). Membranes were collected by centrifugation at 64,300g for 30 min at 4 °C and solubilized for 5 h at 4 °C in a buffer containing 40 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 5% (w/v) glycerol, 0.6% (w/v) lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG), 0.06% (w/v) cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS), 10 μM agonist, protease inhibitor cocktail and 200 μg of scFv16. After clarification at 92,600g for 30 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was incubated overnight at 4 °C with TALON IMAC resin (Takara) and 20 mM imidazole. Resin was collected and washed with 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 30 mM imidazole, 0.01% (w/v) LMNG, 0.001% (w/v) CHS, 5% (w/v) glycerol and 10 μM agonist and complexes were eluted with the same buffer containing 300 mM imidazole. For KOR–Gi–scFv16, TALON eluates were concentrated and subjected to a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 column equilibrated in size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) buffer containing 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 100 μM TCEP, 0.00075% (w/v) LMNG, 0.00025% (w/v) glyco-diosgenin, 0.00075% (w/v) CHS and 5 μM agonist. For MOR–Gi–scFv16, TALON eluates were further purified by batch incubation with anti-FLAG resin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 2.5 h at 4 °C, washed with the TALON wash buffer lacking imidazole and eluted with the same buffer containing 200 mg ml−1 FLAG peptide. The KOR–Gi–scFv16 peak fractions and MOR–Gi–scFv16 eluates were concentrated and incubated overnight at 4 °C with PNGase F (New England Biolabs), 3C protease (GenScript) and 100 μg of scFv16. Both complexes were then loaded onto a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 column in SEC buffer. Peak fractions were collected and concentrated to 1–3 mg ml−1 for cryo-EM experiments.

Cryo-EM grid preparation, data collection and three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction

For cryo-EM grid preparation, 3 μl of agonist-bound KOR–Gi–scFv16 or MOR–Gi–scFv16 sample was applied to glow-discharged 300-mesh Au grids (Quantifoil, R1.2/1.3). Grids were blotted for 3 s using Whatman no. 1 qualitative filter paper in a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 4 °C and 100% humidity, followed by plunge-freezing in liquid ethane, and stored in liquid nitrogen for screening and data collection.

Cryo-EM data were collected on a Titan Krios G3i transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 300 kV and equipped with a field emission gun (X-FEG), a Volta phase plate, a BioQuantum energy filter and a K3 direct electron detector (Gatan). The energy filter was operated with a slit width of 20 eV. Videos were recorded in super-resolution mode at a nominal magnification of ×105,000, corresponding to a pixel size of 0.414 or 0.424 Å, with a total accumulated electron dose of 60 e− per Å2. Automated data collection was carried out using SerialEM.

Dose-fractioned image stacks were processed using patch-based motion correction, followed by contrast transfer function estimation. Particles were picked using the blob picker, extracted from the micrographs and subjected to multiple rounds of two-dimensional (2D) classification, repicking, 3D classification and refinement. All image processing steps were carried out in cryoSPARC86,87. Additional details are provided in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2 and Supplementary Table 6.

Model building and refinement

Active-state human KOR and MOR cryo-EM models (PDB 8DZP and 8EF5) were used as initial models and rigid-body fitted into the cryo-EM density maps in ChimeraX88. Models were manually adjusted and rebuilt in Coot89. Ligands were generated using phenix.elbow90 and manually refined in Coot on the basis of the electron density maps, with guidance from MD simulations or GemSpot predictions. The ligand–receptor complexes were then corefined in PHENIX. The model statistics were validated using MolProbity91. Structural figures were prepared using ChimeraX and PyMol (https://pymol.org/2/).

Radioligand-binding assays

Unless otherwise specified, binding experiments were performed using membranes from transfected human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells (American Type Culture Collection, CRL11268); rat brain membrane experiments are indicated where applicable. HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM (Corning) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. HEK293T membranes were prepared from cells transiently transfected with 7 μg receptor plasmid DNA per 15-cm dish using TransIT reagent (Mirus Biosciences). After 24 h, cells were switched to medium containing 1% (v/v) dialyzed FBS for another 24 h. Cells were lysed in ice-cold lysis buffer (1 mM HEPES and 2 mM EDTA, pH 7.4, supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail) for 15 min at 4 °C. Lysates were collected and centrifuged at 17,000g for 30 min at 4 °C and pellets were resuspended in ice-cold standard binding buffer (50 mM Tris, 10 mM MgCl2 and 0.1 mM EDTA, pH 7.4), homogenized with a Dounce homogenizer, aliquoted and centrifuged at 16,000g for 15 min at 4 °C. Membrane pellets were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Rat brain membranes were prepared from frozen whole brains of male Sprague-Dawley rats. Brains (three per tube) were homogenized in 35 ml of ice-cold lysis buffer (10 mM Tris and 5% sucrose, pH 7.4) using a tissue tearer at low speed (four 15-s bursts) and centrifuged at 1,000g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove debris. Pellets were reextracted once by repeating homogenization and centrifugation and supernatants from both extractions were combined and ultracentrifuged at 200,000g for 30 min at 4 °C. Pellets were washed by repeated resuspension in lysis buffer and ultracentrifugation at 200,000g for 30 min at 4 °C for five cycles and then stored at −80 °C until use.

To determine radioligand affinity, saturation binding assays were performed by incubating membrane preparations with increasing concentrations of radioligand. Nonspecific binding was defined by adding 10 µM unlabeled ligand (naltrindole for DOR, JDTic for KOR, morphine for MOR or MK-801 for NMDAR). Binding assays used standard binding buffer for opioid receptors (50 mM Tris, 10 mM MgCl2 and 0.1 mM EDTA, pH 7.4) and NMDAR buffer for NMDAR (20 mM HEPES, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM glycine and 0.1 mM glutamate, pH 7.4). Reactions were incubated for 1 h at room temperature and terminated by rapid vacuum filtration using a 96-well FilterMate harvester (PerkinElmer). HEK293T-derived samples were filtered onto Filtermat A (PerkinElmer) presoaked in 0.1% polyethyleneimine (PEI), washed three times with ice-cold washing buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4), dried and treated with MultiLex melt-on scintillator sheets (Revvity). Radioactivity was quantified on a MicroBeta counter (PerkinElmer). Results were analyzed in GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software) using the ‘one site, specific binding’ model (Y=Bmax×X/(Kd+X)), where X is the radioligand concentration and Y is specific binding signal. Rat brain membrane samples were filtered onto UniFilter-96 GF/C plates (Revvity) presoaked in 0.3% (w/v) PEI, washed, dried and treated with Microscint-O (Revvity); then, radioactivity was quantified on a Wallac MicroBeta TriLux counter (PerkinElmer). Results were analyzed using in GraphPad Prism 9.5 using the ‘one site, total and nonspecific binding’ model (Ytotal=(Bmax×X)/(Kd+X)+NS×X+background and Ynonspecific=NS×X+background). Here, X is the radioligand concentration and Ytotal and Ynonspecific are the total and nonspecific binding signals. NS is the slope of nonspecific binding and background is the amount of nonspecific binding without added radioligand.

Competition binding assays were performed by incubating membranes with radioligand and increasing concentrations of competitor ligands. Radioligand concentrations were as follows: for HEK293T membranes, 3H-U69,593 (~1.3 nM), 3H-DAMGO (~2.2 nM), 3H-DPDPE (~1.5 nM) and 3H-naltrexone (1.6–2.0 nM for WT and 4.5–5.0 nM for D138N and D149N); for rat brain membranes, 3H-MK-801 (~2.3 nM), 3H-DAMGO (~3.2–3.5 nM), 3H-DADLE (3.1–4.0 nM) and 3H-U69,593 (2.5–3.7 nM). The following procedures were the same as those for saturation binding assays. Competition binding data were analyzed and plotted in GraphPad Prism 9.5. Data from HEK293T membranes were fitted and plotted using the ‘one site, fit Ki’ model (logEC50 = log(10logKi × (1 + RadioligandNM/HotKdNM)) and Y = bottom + (top − bottom)/(1 + 10(X − logEC50))), where RadioligandNM is the concentration of labeled ligand in nM and HotKdNM is the equilibrium dissociation constant of the labeled ligand in nM). For rat brain membranes, curves for NMDAR and KOR were plotted using the ‘one site, fit logIC50’ model (Y = bottom + (top − bottom)/(1 + 10(X − logIC50))), curves for MOR and DOR were plotted using the ‘two sites, fit logIC50’ model (logEC50Lo = log(10logKiLo × (1 + HotNM/HotKdNMLo)), logEC50Hi = log(10logKiHi × (1 + HotNM/HotKdNMHi)), span = top − bottom, Part1 = FractionHi × Span/(1 + 10(X − logEC50Hi)), Part2 = (1 − FractionHi) × Span/(1 + 10(X − logEC50Lo)), Y = bottom + Part1 + Part2) and Ki values were obtained using the ‘one site, fit Ki’ model. In all equations, X is the logarithm of the concentration of the unlabeled compound, Y is the specific radioligand binding, top and bottom are plateaus in the units of the y axis, FractionHi is the fraction of all the sites that have high affinity for the competitor and logIC50Hi and logIC50Lo are the logarithms of the two IC50 values.

cAMP inhibition assay

For cAMP inhibition assay, HEK293T cells were cotransfected with plasmids encoding human opioid receptor or mutants and the CAMYEL biosensor92(YFP–Epac–RLuc) at a 3:5 DNA ratio. After 16 h, cells were plated in poly(L-lysine)-coated 96-well plates at 40,000–50,000 cells per well in 200 μl of DMEM containing 1% dialyzed FBS. The following day, medium was removed and cells were washed once with 60 μl of drug buffer (20 mM HEPES and 1× HBSS, pH 7.4). For the agonist mode, cells were incubated with 40 μl of drug buffer containing 5 μM coelenterazine h (Nanolight Technology) and 10 μM forskolin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 5 min, followed by 20 μl of 3× concentrated test compounds for another 5 min. For the antagonist mode, cells were incubated with 40 μl of drug buffer containing coelenterazine h and forskolin and 20 μl of 3× concentrated test compounds for 15 min. Then, 20 μl of U50,488 at 4× the EC50 concentration was added, followed by a 5-min incubation. For allosteric modulation assays, cells were similarly incubated with coelenterazine h, forskolin and 3× test compounds for 15 min, followed by 20 μl of 4× agonist at varying concentrations for 5 min. Bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) signals were measured using a Mithras LB940 multimode microplate reader (Berthold Technologies), with RLuc luminescence at 485 nm and YFP fluorescent emission at 530 nm for 1 s per well. Net BRET ratios (eYFP/RLuc) were calculated by subtracting background BRET signal from control wells cotransfected with empty vector and CAMYEL biosensor. Net BRET ratios were plotted against drug concentration and normalized to WT receptor or reference ligand responses, as indicated. Drug potency and efficacy were determined in GraphPad Prism 9.2 using the ‘log (agonist) versus response’ model (Y = bottom + (top − bottom)/(1 + 10(logEC50 − X))) or ‘log (inhibitor) versus response’ model (Y = bottom + (top − bottom)/(1 + 10(X − logIC50))), where X is the logarithm of agonist or inhibitor concentration, Y is the response and top and bottom are plateaus in the units of the y axis.

Double-mutant cycle analysis

A mutant cycle analysis was used to evaluate functional coupling between the 3-hydroxyl substituent of 3-OH-PCP and Y1503.33. Substitution-induced changes in potency were calculated from pEC50 values derived from cAMP inhibition assay. The coupling effect was defined as ΔΔpEC50=[pEC50(WT,3−OH−PCP)−pEC50(Y1503.33F,3−OH−PCP)]−[pEC50(WT,PCP)−pEC50(Y1503.33F,PCP)]. The apparent coupling energy was calculated as ΔΔGint=RTln10×ΔΔpEC50, which simplifies to ΔΔGint≈1.36×ΔΔpEC50 kcal mol−1 at 298 K.

β-arrestin 2 recruitment assay

Allosteric modulation of β-arrestin 2 recruitment at opioid receptors was evaluated in HEK293T cells using a BRET assay. Cells in 10-cm dishes were transiently transfected with receptor–RLuc8 constructs (500 ng) and mVenus–β-arrestin 2 (2.5 μg) using TransIT reagent, then plated and washed with drug buffer as described for the cAMP inhibition assay. Cells were incubated with 60 μl of drug buffer containing coelenterazine h and 30 μl of 3× test compounds for 15 min, followed by 30 μl of 4× agonist at varying concentrations for another 5 min. BRET signals were measured as described for the cAMP inhibition assay. The mVenus/RLuc8 emission ratio was plotted against drug concentration, normalized to control responses and fitted in GraphPad Prism 9.2 using the ‘log(agonist) versus response’ model.

Animals

Male C57BL/6 mice (n = 23, Jackson Laboratories) were 8–12 weeks of age at the start of the study. Mice were group housed (2–4 per cage) and maintained on a 14-h light, 10-h dark cycle with food and water ad libitum. The housing temperature was maintained at 20–22 °C, with humidity at approximately 50%. All animals were drug naive with no prior procedures performed.

Warm water tail withdrawal

Ketamine HCl (Dechra, 100 mg ml−1) was diluted in sterile saline to 30 mg kg−1 (10 ml kg−1). Aticaprant (MedChemExpress, 1174130-61-0) was diluted in 5% Cremophor, 5% ethanol and 90% sterile saline at 5 mg kg−1. Naloxone (Sigma-Aldrich, 51481-60-8) was diluted in sterile saline at 2 mg kg−1.

Antinociception was measured using the warm water tail withdrawal assay. Mice were preinjected i.p. with saline, aticaprant (5 mg kg−1) or naloxone (2 mg kg−1) 15 min before baseline tail withdrawal. The response latency (s) for an animal to withdraw its tail after being immersed in 52.5 ± 0.3 °C water was measured immediately before and 40 min after ketamine treatment. A 15-s maximal immersion duration was used as a cutoff to prevent tissue damage.

Cell surface expression of receptors

Cell surface expression of WT and mutant human KOR and MOR was measured by ELISA. HEK293T cells were transiently transfected with equivalent amounts of WT or mutant receptor DNA and plated as described for the cAMP inhibition assay. The following day, cells were washed once with PBS pH 7.4 and fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature. After two washes with PBS, cells were blocked with 0.5% (w/v) BSA in PBS for 30 min and incubated with an anti-Flag (M2) horseradish-peroxidase-conjugated antibody (Sigma-Aldrich; 1:20,000) for 1 h at room temperature. After three PBS washes, one-step Ultra-TMB ELISA substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added and incubated for 15–30 min at 37 °C and reactions were stopped with 1 M sulfuric acid. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a BioTek microplate reader. Data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism 9.2 and normalized to WT receptor expression.

MD simulation and analysis

The KOR structures (presented in this manuscript) were aligned to the Orientations of Proteins in Membranes93 entry for PDB 6B73 (nanobody-stabilized active-state KOR38). We performed four sets of simulations: KOR apo, KOR–(S)-ketamine and two sets of KOR–PCP simulations (one with PCP starting from the cryo-EM pose and one with PCP starting from an alternate pose shown in Fig. 2a, left). For each of these four simulation conditions, we performed six independent simulations in which initial atom velocities were assigned randomly and independently. All parts of the Gi protein were removed from all KOR structures.

Prime (Schrödinger)94 was used to model missing side chains and add capping groups to protein chain termini. Protonation states of all titratable residues were assigned at pH 7.4, except for D1553.49 and D1052.50 of KOR, which were protonated (neutral) in all simulations, as these conserved residues are reported to be protonated in active-state GPCRs95,96.

Using Dabble97, the prepared protein structures were inserted into a preequilibrated POPC bilayer and the system was solvated. Sodium and chloride ions were added to neutralize each system at a concentration of 150 mM. The final system of the KOR simulations comprised roughly 54,000 atoms and the system dimensions were approximately 76 Å × 76 Å × 96 Å. We used the CHARMM36m force field for proteins, the CHARMM36 force field for lipids and ions and the TIP3P model for waters98,99. All simulations were performed using the CUDA version of PMEMD in AMBER 20 (University of California, San Francisco) on GPUs.

Systems were first minimized using three rounds of minimization, each consisting of 500 cycles of steepest descent followed by 500 cycles of conjugate gradient optimization. Then, harmonic restraints (10.0 and 5.0 kcal mol−1 Å−2) were applied to protein, lipids and ligand for the first and second rounds of minimization, respectively. Harmonic restraints (1 kcal mol−1 Å−2) were applied to protein and ligand for the third round of minimization. Systems were then heated from 0 K to 100 K in the NVT ensemble over 12.5 ps and then from 100 K to 310 K in the NPT ensemble over 125 ps, using harmonic restraints (10.0 kcal mol−1 Å−2) applied to protein and ligand heavy atoms. Subsequently, systems were equilibrated at 310 K and 1 bar in the NPT ensemble, with harmonic restraints on the protein and ligand nonhydrogen atoms tapered off by 1.0 kcal mol−1 Å−2 starting at 5.0 kcal mol−1 Å−2 in a stepwise fashion every 2 ns for 10 ns and then by 0.1 kcal mol−1 Å−2 every 2 ns for 20 ns. Production simulations were performed at 310 K and 1 bar in the NPT ensemble using the Langevin thermostat and the Berendsen barostat and using a timestep of 4.0 fs with hydrogen mass repartitioning100. Harmonic restraints (0.5 kcal mol−1 Å−2) were applied to residues that contained an atom within 4 Å of the partner G protein in the cryo-EM structure. Bond lengths were constrained using the SHAKE algorithm101. Nonbonded interactions were cut off at 9.0 Å and long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method with an Ewald coefficient of approximately 0.31 Å−1 and fourth-order B splines. The PME grid size was chosen such that the width of a grid cell was approximately 1 Å. Trajectory frames were saved every 200 ps during the production simulations and included equilibration steps.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times with similar results unless otherwise noted. Statistical significance was calculated using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test, two-way ANOVA followed by Šídák’s multiple-comparison test or unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, as indicated in the figure legends and supplementary tables. In figure legends, n represents the number of independent replicates. Sample sizes were chosen on the basis of reproducibility in independent experiments and prior experience with these assays. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. All quantitative data were presented as the mean ± s.e.m. Exact P values are provided in the respective Supplementary Tables. No animals or data points were excluded from the analyses. Randomization and blinding methods were not used.
