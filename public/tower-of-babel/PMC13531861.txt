Impact and implementation evaluation of Stress First Aid for the Substance Use Outreach Workforce (SFA/SUOW): protocol of a hybrid type I cluster randomized control trial
Authors: Anmol Desai, Katie A. McCormick, Juliette Rau, Haresh Rochani, Patrick Chang, Richard J. Westphal, Patricia J. Watson, Kasey R. Claborn, Justin K. Benzer, Suzannah K. Creech
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStress First Aid (SFA), an evidence-informed peer support intervention to address occupational stress, has been adapted to the needs of the substance use outreach workforce (SUOW) (SFA/SUOW) and pilot tested with initial positive outcomes. The goal of this study is to test and evaluate the effectiveness, implementation and sustainability of SFA/SUOW compared to a no treatment control condition in a hybrid-type I cluster-randomized controlled trial (RCT).MethodsApproximately 500 substance use outreach workers (SUOWs) and leaders from approximately 90 substance use outreach (SUO) organizations, Community Health Worker programs, and peer support programs arranged in 10 regions based on geographic co-location across Texas, Louisiana, Arkansas, Oklahoma, New Mexico, and tribal areas will be 50:50 randomized to the intervention group or the control group. Treatment will consist of a 2-hour virtual SFA training and up to six virtual 30-minute monthly learning collaboratives. All participants will be asked to complete a baseline assessment prior to randomization, and 2 follow-up assessments at 2-month and 6-month to assess occupational stress primary outcomes: 1) supervisor and coworker social support and 2) burnout. We will also assess use of SFA, job-related affective well-being, secondary traumatic stress, use of coping strategies, general self-efficacy, work engagement, turnover intention, and moral injury symptoms. Additionally, we will conduct interviews and focus groups with RCT participants, RCT non-participants, and addiction professional staff to evaluate the reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, and maintenance of SFA/SUOW.DiscussionThis RCT provides a robust trial of SFA/SUOW that has not been done before. The aim of the study will result in a national implementation plan to improve SUOWs well-being and subsequently improve quality care for persons who use drugs.Trial registrationThe study NCT07177014 is registered on clinicaltrials.gov https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07177014. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

The substance use outreach workforce (SUOW) is on the frontlines of the opioid and overdose crises in the U.S., providing life-saving services to marginalized people who use drugs (PWUD). Substance use outreach workers (SUOWs) and their community health and peer support counterparts are helping paraprofessionals, often with lived/living experience with substance use, and varying amounts of training in harm reduction philosophy, peer support, and outreach. SUOWs are employed in various organizational settings (e.g., grassroots mutual aid collectives, recovery community organizations) and have diverse job titles, such as outreach worker and recovery coach. Though extent and scope of practice vary considerably, SUOWs regularly engage in frontline work, including prevention, direct service provision, and psychosocial support [1].

Due to the nature of the role and responsibilities, SUOWs regularly grapple with an array of occupational stressors that can adversely impact their occupational well-being. Such stressors span socio-ecological levels and include interpersonal conflicts, overdose response and exposure to client death and trauma, outreach setting-based environmental stressors, and cultural and systemic barriers to substance use outreach and harm reduction services, as well as frustrations with outside organizations and a lack of internal organizational support [2, 3]. To bolster occupational resilience, SUOWs have called for colleague support, team building and collaborations with a network of SUOWs, non-judgmental guidance and support [2].

Stress First Aid (SFA) is an evidence-informed self-care and coworker support model developed to mitigate and enhance recovery from stress reactions for those in high-stress occupations [4]. The central goal of SFA is to help identify and address early signs of stress reactions within oneself and one’s coworkers to reduce the likelihood that stress outcomes develop into more severe, long-term problems. SFA is based on research related to five essential elements that have been found to be universally protective for ongoing stress and adversity including a) promoting a psychological sense of safety, b) promoting calming, which can reduce the anxiety that may generalize to other situations, interfere with engagement in life tasks, and increase risk for mental health disorders; c) promoting a sense of self-efficacy by increasing a person’s belief in their ability to endure through adverse events; d) promoting social connectedness, one of the strongest research-based protective factors for better emotional well-being and recovery following adverse experiences; and e) fostering hope, which is related to more favorable outcomes in a variety of adverse circumstances, via positive expectancy, a feeling of confidence in resources, faith, making meaning, or other hopeful beliefs [5]. SFA is therefore comprised of five “core actions” which map onto these five essential elements, and two additional core actions of checking in and coordinating with resources. Each core action begins with the letter “C” to make them easier to remember: check, coordinate, cover (safety), calm, connect, competence (self-efficacy), confidence (hope). By creating a common language for addressing stress and stress reactions, SFA reduces stigma in the workplace and changes organizational culture—ultimately fostering worker longevity. SFA is a highly flexible model that has been widely adapted for service-oriented professions particularly susceptible to stress and trauma [6–10].

Recognizing the critical gap in research on occupational stress interventions for the SUOW, we adapted and pilot tested SFA for the SUOW. The adaptation was conducted using feedback gathered through a series of focus groups with SUOWs and organizational leaders to understand occupational stressors and resilience strategies in the unique setting of frontline work in the opioid and overdose crisis. Based on these findings and guidance from a Harm Reduction Community Advisory Board, content-level adaptations included 1) incorporating examples of safety protocols utilized on the frontlines, 2) discussing existing resources for competence on the frontlines and existing well-being initiatives, and 3) creating space for sharing stressors experiences on the frontlines and discussing existing resilience strategies [2].

We then conducted a pilot test of the adapted SFA for the SUOW (SFA/SUOW) with a nonprofit recovery community organization. The pilot test consisted of a 2-hour in-person training followed by four 30-minute monthly virtual learning collaboratives with follow-up assessments over time. We assessed preliminary effectiveness on SFA and well-being outcomes and preliminary implementation outcomes at baseline, 2-month, and 4-month after the initial training. Results indicated that the adapted SFA model for the SUOW demonstrated preliminary effectiveness on improving SFA outcomes and is acceptable, appropriate, and feasible for the SUOW. Results regarding the impact of SFA/SUOW on well-being, coping, secondary traumatic stress, burnout, and turnover intention remained stable over time. Results from this study are reported elsewhere [11]. These results suggested further study against a control group with an RCT was warranted.

Despite the critical and growing role of the SUOW in the overdose continuum of care, almost no research has addressed their need for an occupational stress intervention. If effective, widespread adoption of SFA could improve workforce capacity for high quality care (i.e., developing relationships with PWUD, initiating conversations about overdose risk and mitigation strategies). This is an urgent public health need considering the important role the SUOW plays in reducing morbidity and mortality rates among PWUD.

Objectives {7}

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness and impact of SFA adapted for the substance use outreach workforce (SFA/SUOW) on SUOWs well-being, primarily social support and burnout, across Texas (TX), Louisiana (LA), Arkansas (AR), Oklahoma (OK), New Mexico (NM), and tribal areas. The secondary objective of this study is to evaluate the implementation and sustainability of SFA/SUOW.

Trial design {8}

This will be a hybrid-type I, parallel, two-group cluster-randomized controlled trial testing for superiority over a no treatment control condition. The unit of randomization will be substance use outreach (SUO) organizations at a 50:50 allocation ratio. This protocol is guided by the 2013 SPIRIT reporting guidelines (Additional File 1) [12].

Methods: participants, interventions and outcomes

Study setting {9}

Participants for the RCT will be recruited from an anticipated 90 community-based SUO organizations, Community Health Worker (CHW), or peer support programs within the United States Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) Region 6 which includes TX, LA, AR, OK, NM, and tribal areas.

Eligibility criteria {10}

At least 3 paid SUOWs above the age of 18 within a given SUO organization are available and interested in participating; then, the organization and those SUOWs are eligible to be enrolled in the study.

For inclusion in the RCT, SUOW participants must be (a) at least 18 years of age, (b) able to read and speak English, (c) employed in a paid role as a substance use outreach, harm reduction, peer support or Community Health/Outreach Worker (whose work includes substance use outreach or harm reduction) with people who use drugs within TX, LA, AR, OK, NM and tribal areas, and (d) able and willing to provide informed consent.

For inclusion in the implementation evaluation component of the study, focus groups and interviews, participants must be (a) at least 18 years of age, (b) able to read and speak English, (c) participants of the RCT who received the SFA intervention, non-participants of the RCT, or addiction professional staff, and (d) ability and willing to provide informed consent.

Who will take informed consent? {26a}

Among organizations that agree to participate in the study, any eligible SUOWs within the given SUO organization who is available and interested in participating will be formally enrolled, provided a Research Information Sheet (RIS) and verbally consented by the research team.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {26b}

The RIS will include a statement that participants’ de-identified and anonymized data will be shared on the Helping to End Addiction Long-term (HEAL) Initiative Data Platform and be stored and be made available for public use and future research on the National Addiction & HIV Data Archive Program (NAHDAP) Data Repository, accessible on https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/web/ICPSR/studies/39277.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

The comparator will be a no treatment control condition to test the impact of the intervention on well-being outcomes among SUOWs. There is no alternative treatment, and efficacy testing of SFA has not been completed; therefore, the appropriate control condition at this stage is a low formidability, no treatment comparison.

Intervention description {11a}

The intervention, SFA/SUOW, consists of 1) a 2-hour virtual SFA training. Booster sessions for each aspect of the content covered at the initial training will also occur at six virtual 30-minute monthly learning collaboratives. All trainings will be co-facilitated by at least two study team members with specialized training in the SFA/SUOW curriculum. New study team members will be trained on the SFA/SUOW curriculum by observing trainings, practicing facilitating trainings with the study team, and then co-facilitating trainings with trained study team members. Intervention components are focused on the peer support aspects of the SFA model. First, the training starts with an overview of the stress continuum and psychoeducation about occupational stress and how to identify and talk about it. Next, participants are led through each of the core principles of SFA (e.g. check, coordinate, cover, calm, connect, competence, confidence) using didactic psychoeducation, video examples, and an interactive case study/example that carries through the entirety of the training. Throughout the training, participants will be exposed to a common language to discuss occupational stress in the substance use outreach workforce and trained on actions they can utilize to apply the core skills when addressing stress in real time with a coworker, as a supervisor, or for self-care. For example, utilizing Observe, State Observation, Clarify Role, Ask Why, and Respond (OSCAR) communication to check on a coworker. The focus of the training will primarily be on coworker support with self-care and leadership resources to be provided after the training. A fidelity checklist will be used to audit all 2-hour trainings to ensure all core components of the SFA/SUOW curriculum are addressed.

The learning collaboratives are designed as boosters for the core content, covering each of the skills and using the same approach of psychoeducation, video content and case study/example, with opportunity for interaction around applying the model in the work setting. The total intervention time spans six months. In addition, organizational leaders are invited to a 30-minute training on the role of leaders in supporting and sustaining SFA. Leaders can attend themselves or designate a site champion to attend. The leader training will start with a brief overview of what and why SFA, the stress continuum, and stress indicators. Next, the leaders will be provided with examples of potential actions that those in leader roles can apply and ways they can model the core principles of SFA within their organization. The training will end with leaders and champions having an understanding of their role in embedding SFA in daily work and building buy-in from staff, and despite common leadership challenges, how SFA can be sustained at their organization over time.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

Organizations and/or participants may withdraw from the study at any time. Participants will be notified that any data collected prior to withdrawal will be retained in the final dataset.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

Strategies to improve participant adherence to interventions include reminder calls, texts, and emails about upcoming trainings and surveys. Trainings will be scheduled at accessible times. Participant adherence is also likely to be enhanced by the fact that the training is delivered in one 2-hour session, with shorter 30-minute boosters in the form of learning collaboratives and access to materials online.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

No concomitant care or interventions will be explicitly permitted or prohibited during the trial.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

SFA/SUOW curriculum and materials will be made available to all participants upon completion of the study via password protection on the study website.

Implementation outcomes

Guided by the RE-AIM framework we will measure reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, and maintenance of SFA/SUOW [22]. We will facilitate focus groups and interviews to understand (a) how to better reach intended participants of SFA, (b) adoption of SFA, (c) potential factors that may impact the effectiveness of SFA, (d) any necessary adaptations, and (e) participant perspectives about maintaining the program after the study ends. The findings will help inform the creation of an Implementation Strategy for a potential national rollout of SFA/SUOW.

Sample size {14}

Sample size determination was based on clinically meaningful effect sizes, pilot data, and standard operating characteristics for cluster-randomized trials. Design parameters and assumptions were selected to ensure adequate statistical power while preserving control of the family-wise Type I error rate.

This study includes two co-primary outcomes: perceived occupational social support and overall burnout. To control the family-wise Type I error rate, we applied a Bonferroni correction, allocating the alpha level equally across the two outcomes. Each outcome was tested at a two-sided significance level of α = 0.025, maintaining the overall Type I error rate at 0.05.

A parallel, two-group cluster-randomized design will be used to test differences in mean outcomes between the control group (μ1) and the intervention group (μ2), using a two-sided test of the null hypothesis (H0: δ = 0 vs. H1: δ ≠ 0, where δ = μ1 – μ2). The comparison will be performed using a two-sided t-test, with degrees of freedom based on the total number of clusters [24].

For the perceived occupational social support outcome, which will be measured using the Job Content Questionnaire (JCQ) Social Support Scale, there is no formally established clinically meaningful difference in literature. However, based on a review of relevant studies [25–27], and consultation with collaborators, we adopted an assumed medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) for our power calculation. This corresponds to a mean difference of 1.7 points and a standard deviation of 3.4, both of which fall within the ranges reported across prior studies (SD ranging from 2.5 to 3.4). Using this information and an alpha of 0.025, the estimated power to detect the hypothesized difference exceeds 0.9 with a total sample size of 450 participants.

For the overall burnout outcome, the subject-to-subject standard deviation (SD) is assumed to be 12, based on pilot data from the initial implementation of the intervention. The intracluster correlation coefficient is assumed to be 0.10, which aligns with values observed in similar cluster-randomized trials evaluating behavioral interventions. The coefficient of variation (COV) in cluster sizes is assumed to be 0.65. Campbell and Walters [28] note that as average cluster size increases, the COV tends to increase and approach 0.65; as larger COV values degrade power, we conservatively set this at 0.65. Based on the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory, a 5-point difference is considered a meaningful change in burnout levels [14]. To detect this difference between groups, with 45 clusters of an average of 5 participants per cluster (totaling 225 participants per arm, or 450 in total), the power is estimated to be 0.90.

To allow for an anticipated 10% attrition rate, the planned enrollment target is approximately 500 participants. All power calculations were performed using PASS software [29] and are based on established methods described in Ahn et al. [30], Chow et al. [31], and Donner and Klar [32].

Recruitment {15}

We have compiled an initial list of potentially eligible organizations as primary recruitment and outreach targets. We will arrange the anticipated 90 SUO organizations, CHW programs, and peer support programs across the five states and tribal areas into 10 regions based on geographic co-location such that all the SUO organizations (and the SUOWs within those organizations) within a given region could participate in a single SFA training program. We will hold 2-hour trainings at least every two months by region to reduce wait time, based on interest and enrollment. The recruitment, enrollment, randomization, and delivery of the SFA intervention will be stratified by region, and each region will participate in the trial on its own timeline based on the research team’s availability to provide the training. We anticipate recruitment and enrollment to start December 1, 2025.

Targeted emails with study flyers will be sent from the research team to the leaders of all SUO organizations, CHW programs, and peer support programs on a rolling regional basis. The research team will email leaders a brief enrollment form and follow up by phone call up to three times. SUO organization leadership agreement to enroll in the study does not mean their workers will be required to enroll in the study. Once the organization is enrolled, leaders will be asked to inform their SUOWs that study participation is available and they may volunteer if they desire to participate. Organizational leaders will be asked to provide contact information for their SUOWs to the research team or may choose to pass along study information with a scannable QR code for SUOWs self-enrollment. Once a potential participant is identified, research staff will contact them, introduce the study, confirm eligibility, and they will be invited to enroll in the study. Similar recruitment methods will be applied to recruit participants into the focus groups and individual interviews.

The leadership of each SUO organization will be approached and invited to participate in the study. If leadership agrees and at least three SUOWs within that organization are available and interested in participating, then the organization and those SUOWs will be formally enrolled, consented, and baseline measures (Table 1) will be collected. Treating SUO organizations as the clusters, all participants within each organization will then be randomized at a 50:50 allocation ratio within that region to either the SFA/SUOW training arm or the control condition arm. SUOWs will receive the assigned condition according to their organization’s group. SUOWs will be allowed to attend the training even if they do not wish to participate in the research. To keep recruitment contained, recruitment will be done region to region. A step-wise approach will be used to enroll and schedule SUO organizations until we reach saturation of target organizations and there are no new organizations in a region, before moving on to the next region.

The SFA/SUOW training will occur at the region level and thus is anticipated to involve participants from all of the study SUO organizations within that region that are randomized to SFA. We expect to offer multiple trainings per region to accommodate capacity and variation in enrollment timing, though each participant will only attend one training. Training will occur as soon as possible after randomization, but no later than two months. Following the training, there will be virtual 30-minute monthly learning collaboratives for the 6-month study period. We will collect the same measures from baseline at 2-month (time of primary endpoint) and 6-month post-training.

If randomly assigned to the control group, organizations will be offered the opportunity to be trained in SFA/SUOW at the conclusion of the study. We will also offer trainings for organizational leaders at pre-enrollment for study recruitment and post-enrollment for sustainability and maintenance of SFA/SUOW.

Participants of the implementation evaluation component of the study will be recruited across three populations including from the list of SUO organizations, CHW programs, and peer support programs recruited for the RCT, both those who 1) enrolled and 2) those who did not, as well as from the 3) addiction professional staff.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

The study statisticians will generate the randomization sequence, stratified by region based on geographic co-location. The allocation sequence will be generated separately within each of the 10 geographic regions (strata) using computer-generated random numbers. Within each region, clusters (SUO organizations) will be randomized 1:1 to the SFA training arm or the control arm. To reduce predictability, randomization will use permuted blocks of varying sizes (e.g., block sizes of 2, 4, and 6) with the order of block sizes randomized. Full details of block sizes and the random seed will be maintained in a separate document accessible only to the independent statistician and will not be available to study personnel involved in enrollment or assignment.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

Allocation will be concealed until the moment of assignment using a central randomization mechanism. The allocation list will be implemented through this secure, auditable central system, the randomization module on REDCap, a secure web-based data collection and management tool. Research staff seeking to randomize a cluster will request allocation via the central system, which will reveal the assigned arm only after the site confirms that baseline data collection for that cluster is complete.

Implementation {16c}

The allocation sequence will be generated by an independent statistician who is not involved in recruitment, enrollment, or outcome assessment. The research team, including the project coordinator, outreach coordinator and research assistants, will identify and enroll eligible SUO organizations and confirm that SUOWs baseline measures are complete. The research team will then randomize and assign enrolled SUO organizations to the intervention or control group via REDCap.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

Study personnel responsible for following up with participants to ensure completion of assessments will be blinded to the condition they are in. Study staff responsible for following up with participants to ensure completion of assessments will remain blinded to the allocation of SUO organizations to intervention or control. During trainings, the research team may know the names of participants and their organizations, and thus trainers will have a sense of which participants were in each condition; however, this will have minimal impact on outcome assessment because the data are self-reported. The REDCap central randomization module ensures allocation concealment until the point of assignment, supporting blinding of staff involved in data management and analysis. Any deviations from blinded procedures will be documented.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

N/A. Blinding is limited in this study.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

Demographic data will be collected along with baseline data for primary and secondary outcomes prior to randomization via REDCap surveys, with follow-up surveys at 2-months and 6-months. See Table 1 for additional details of scales we will use to measure outcomes. Participants of the RCT will receive $25 gift cards for their time completing each study assessment (baseline, 2-months, 6-months), for up to a total of $75.

Implementation outcomes will be measured through 15–20-minute interviews with RCT non-participants and up to 2-hour focus groups with RCT participants and addiction professional staff. Participants of the implementation evaluation component will participate in a focus group or interview and will likewise receive a $25 gift card for their time.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

Retention methods will include 1) regularly updating participant email addresses and phone numbers, 2) sending mid-month reminder emails or text messages via REDCap’s Mosio platform to leadership to talk to their teams about SFA concepts, 3) raffles for participation incentives (i.e., participants will get a raffle entry for each survey completed, with a raffle pull every 3 months for a $100 gift card), and 4) easy access to updated study phone numbers. For ease of survey completion, participants will receive emails and text messages via REDCap’s Mosio platform with embedded links. Participants who are about to miss their follow-up window can complete abbreviated primary outcome measures over the phone or by text which research staff will then input into REDCap, leaving secondary outcomes blank for quality assurance. As with recruitment, the research team will monitor participant retention closely in order to address problems early on.

Data management {19}

Data will be merged, edited, and cleaned using REDCap data files, a secure web-based application designed for field data collection that ensures accessibility for all key study personnel. A REDCap project will be set up, including variables (fields) along with range and consistency checks, in line with the study protocol. The statistical team will independently review the project to ensure all key variables are included. All post-randomization data will be collected, curated, and managed by the study team.

As part of the quality control/quality assurance process any discrepancies will be reviewed and resolved by the study team, as well as the resolution of any identified systemic or procedural irregularities. At regular intervals, the study team will download the data and audit all key variables for missingness, ranges, and consistency with other data elements.

At the end of the study, data collection instruments may be found on the NAHDAP Data Repository, accessible on https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/web/ICPSR/studies/39277.

Confidentiality {27}

Participants will complete surveys virtually, allowing participants to choose their own location for privacy of data collection. To maintain participant privacy, participant data will be de-identified for analysis, data sharing, publications, and presentations. Participants will be described by aggregate demographics including age, race, ethnicity, organizational role, and organization type.

To help ensure confidentiality during qualitative data collection, use of participant names will be minimized. Only audio recordings of conversations will be saved (not video). These audio recordings will be identified by participant numbers only and stored on a secured server, with access restricted only to staff for this specific research study. As soon as the audio recordings are transcribed, they will be deleted. Transcripts will be reviewed and de-identified prior to analyses. Transcription will occur using an encrypted platform.

All participants will be assigned a unique ID. These IDs will be used to identify all participants on all research materials, including surveys, tracking forms, transcripts, and in the study database. An identifier key will be created that will link the participant ID to participant names and contact information in order to facilitate follow-up with participants; contact information will have participant ID numbers but will not have any data. The identified key will be stored in a password-protected folder separate from study data. Consent will be linked to participants’ REDCap surveys. All electronic data will be maintained on the university’s secure servers. Participant information will not be released to any party outside the research team at any time. Participants’ names will never appear in any report or presentation resulting from the project.

Due to the extensive follow-up process for the RCT, an email field will be linked to survey responses on REDCap, a secure HIPAA-compliant online survey platform, to facilitate follow-ups and pairing participant baseline, 2-month, and 6-month follow-up survey data. REDCap and secure file storage will be the only platform through which emails are stored with participant data during the RCT. All participant data will be maintained on the university’s secure servers behind dual authentication processes. Participant information will not be released to any party outside the research team at any time. At the conclusion of the RCT and once follow-up surveys are completed, email records will be removed from REDCap and stored only with unique IDs in a separate password-protected folder.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Following data cleaning and the construction of variables for primary outcomes and covariates, analyses will begin with descriptive statistics to summarize baseline characteristics, group-specific (arm-) distributions, and outcomes. These summaries will include means, standard deviations, frequencies, and proportions, and will be presented by intervention arm and further stratified by key sociodemographic characteristics such as age, gender, race, ethnicity, and organizational role (e.g., worker versus leader).

To evaluate the effectiveness of the SFA/SUOW intervention, we will assess two co-primary outcomes: (1) overall burnout, as measured by the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory [14], and (2) perceived occupational social support measured using the Job Content Questionnaire Social Support Scale [13]. The primary statistical approach will involve linear mixed-effects models to account for the cluster-randomized design, with SUO organizations serving as the clustering units [32]. Each model will include a fixed effect for the intervention group and a random intercept for SUO organization, while adjusting for the baseline value of the corresponding outcome for endpoints burnout and social support. Group effects will be reported as estimated mean differences with 95% confidence intervals and p-values. Model assumptions, including the distribution and variance of residuals, will be examined and, if necessary, alternative approaches such as robust standard errors will be applied [33].

Interim analyses {21b}

N/A. As a minimal risk study, we will not be conducting an interim look at outcomes.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g. subgroup analyses) {20b}

For the RCT, we will also explore whether the intervention effects differ by participant subgroups. This will be done by incorporating interaction terms into the mixed-effects models for sociodemographic characteristics, including occupational role (i.e., worker versus leader) [34]. These analyses will help identify whether the intervention has varying effects across different segments of the population.

We will test whether the SFA intervention alters between-organization heterogeneity in SUOWs outcomes, relative to control. Specifically, we will compare the SUO organization-level random-intercept variances between arms to assess whether SFA compresses or amplifies organizational differences. Regions will be modeled as fixed effects.

We will evaluate whether the association between social support and burnout accounts for differences in burnout outcomes. The social support measure will be group-mean centered, and we will include the SUO organization-level mean as a predictor to capture between-organization effects. Models will be estimated using REML for parameter estimation. To formally test equality of the SUO organization-level variances, we will compare a constrained model with a common organization variance to an unconstrained model with arm-specific organization variances, using a likelihood ratio test fit under maximum likelihood. If convergence or small-sample concerns arise, we will perform a parametric bootstrap LRT. We will visually compare the dispersion of SUO organization effects by arm using a caterpillar plot of the model-based empirical Bayes estimates of each organization's random intercept.

Interviews and focus groups in the implementation evaluation component will be recorded, transcribed verbatim, and scrubbed of identifying information, and note takers will be present for all data collection events. However, we will use Rapid Qualitative Analysis [35] in the form of debriefings between interviewers/facilitators and note takers and a framework matrix to summarize information from participants for immediate synthesis. The interviewer/facilitator will create debriefing summaries for each area of questioning, summarizing key knowledge learned. The project coordinator will compile the debriefing summaries into a framework matrix display where interview/focus group protocol questions are the rows and debriefs and key participant statements are the columns. Matrices will be reviewed and discussed at team debriefing meetings. Recommendations for future adaptations will be developed through consensus of the scientific team. Any failure to reach consensus will be determined by majority vote with Principal Investigator (PI) as the deciding vote in the case of a tie.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

Missing data will be carefully assessed, and the extent and patterns of missingness will be reported. If missing data are non-negligible, multiple imputation will be employed under the assumption of data missing at random [36]. Sensitivity analyses will be performed to evaluate the robustness of the findings to this assumption [37].

Discussion

We adapted Stress First Aid, an evidence-informed self-care and coworker support model, to bolster occupational resilience among SUOWs. This RCT provides a robust trial of SFA that has not been done before. We expect to observe stability and/or improvement in well-being outcomes of SUOWs who participated in the SFA/SUOW intervention compared to the control group.

However, it is important to consider that the impact on well-being outcomes can take a longer time to be sustained. Additionally, the wide range of roles and responsibilities held by workers that will be enrolled in this trial increases heterogeneity of the sample, which can decrease power to detect an effect.

This study is the first test of the adapted SFA curriculum to the unique occupational context and needs of SUOWs. The results of the study will determine whether scaling up to a national implementation of the SFA for the SUOW intervention is warranted. This timeliness of that scale up will be enhanced because the present study will create an Implementation Plan as a blueprint for nationwide rollout of the SFA/SUOW. Further, because this is an intervention that directly affects the well-being of SUOWs, we anticipate that SFA will lead to opportunities for further improvements in the quality of substance use outreach care. For example, although we do not measure this directly due to the difficulty of doing so, we expect that enhancing workforce well-being will enhance SUD treatment outcomes for the population served. Considering the widespread scope and devastation of the overdose crisis in the U.S., effective interventions to support the well-being of the SUOWs, CHW and Peer Workers who are on the frontlines of SUD response are critical to sustaining the SUD continuum of care. This study will inform policymakers and other key stakeholders on whether SFA can effectively address the needs of the workforce.
