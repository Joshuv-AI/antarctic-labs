Ghost noise in single-fiber bidirectional transmission links and its suppression approaches
Authors: Wujie Wang, Qihao Hu, Lina Ma, Jiali Gao, Hongze Leng, Junqiang Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle-fiber bidirectional transmission (Bidi transmission) enables efficient reuse of existing global communication cables for sensing, yet environmental disturbances induce link noise that fundamentally limits long-distance sensing performance. Despite extensive research efforts dedicated to Bidi transmission link noise suppression, existing methods fail to eliminate link noise, forcing the abandonment of the Bidi transmission scheme in large-scale deployments. Here, we uncover that the origin of this residual noise is the “ghost noise,” which can be decomposed into polarization cross-coupling noise (PCN) and calculation cross-coupling noise (CCN). To address this ghost noise, we develop a polarization-traversal interrogation (PTI) technique integrated with pseudo-random binary sequence (PRBS) phase modulation. This hybrid approach achieves zero noise increase over a 7 km Bidi transmission link and realizes 94.8% background noise reduction in a 10 km Bidi transmission link. This breakthrough extends the low-noise Bidi analog transmission distance by six times, holding significant implications for the construction of sensing networks through global communication infrastructure.Subject terms: Fibre optics and optical communications, Optical sensors

Introduction

Large-scale fiber-optic sensing networks are increasingly imperative in the context of accelerating globalization and intelligentization1–4. The single-fiber bidirectional (Bidi) transmission scheme, which utilizes a single optical fiber to simultaneously transmit optical signals in both directions, offers high system integration and efficient resource utilization, providing a more cost-effective and higher-efficiency alternative to conventional dual-fiber unidirectional transmission for constructing ultra-large-scale sensing networks5–7. Representative applications include distributed acoustic sensing (DAS)8,9 and fiber-optic hydrophones10,11 for underwater acoustic monitoring12,13, oil-gas leakage detection in terrestrial pipelines14, earthquake and ocean dynamic detection15,16, and structural health monitoring in power grids17.

The Bidi transmission scheme has been widely adopted in optical communications due to its intrinsic advantages18–20. Specifically, environmental disturbances around Bidi transmission links have no impact on transmission quality as digital signals in communication systems inherently resist such disturbances, and wavelength division multiplexing further eliminates conflicts between uplink and downlink signals21–24. However, the deployment of Bidi transmission links in sensing systems presents a significant challenge: the analog nature of the signal transmission leaves the system highly susceptible to environmental disturbances, which couple into the link and generate high-intensity noise25–27. For example, submarine optical sensing systems exhibit heightened vulnerability to two dominant Bidi transmission link noise sources28,29, shown in Fig. 1: (I) pressure fluctuations from ocean currents, internal waves, and hydrodynamic processes perturbing the Bidi transmission link state30–32; (II) false signals induced by distant vessels or marine life intruding via the Bidi transmission link33. These noise types accumulate spatially across the Bidi transmission link, integrating disturbances over several kilometers into the sensing signal11,34–36. This effect causes performance to degrade progressively with link length, rendering the sensing system unusable long before optical attenuation becomes the limiting factor37,38.

Simultaneous uplink and downlink of sensing and communication signals is achieved through the Bidi transmission link. The collected sensing signals are demodulated to extract the target signal.

The Bidi transmission scheme enables efficient global sensing systems, but it inherently introduces severe noise accumulation. This challenge has prompted in-depth studies to identify the primary noise generation mechanisms, which typically include: (I) environmentally-induced birefringence39–41 and phase drift42,43 in the transmission link; (II) Doppler shift induced by longitudinal stretching of the link fiber44,45; (III) parasitic interference from Rayleigh back scattering (RBS)46–48. Extensive research indicates that, given the temporal constraints of interrogation pulses49,50 and the protection of armored optical cables44,51, the parasitic interference caused by RBS is universally regarded as the dominant noise source in Bidi transmission links.

Thus, current link noise suppression strategies primarily focus on mitigating parasitic interference by addressing its underlying RBS mechanism52–54, as summarized in Fig. 2. While the dual fiber architecture physically isolates uplink and downlink signals to achieve superior noise performance35,55–57, the Bidi transmission scheme remains of exceptional application value owing to its high resource utilization efficiency. Crucially, the Bidi transmission scheme enables the direct reuse of existing global optical communication infrastructure58–60, avoiding the economically and logistically prohibitive costs of laying additional dedicated sensing fibers and optical separation devices. Constrained by the strict necessity to preserve this minimalist, single-fiber architecture, however, effective noise suppression is currently only achievable for a 1.1 km Bidi transmission link56. Increasing the link length to 3.3 km leaves 6.8 dB residual noise, although it achieves 21.3 dB noise suppression11. Beyond this point, the system persistently exhibits residual noise, failing to achieve the zero-baseline noise level observed without the transmission link34,35,61. The current maximum Bidi transmission link distance is now confirmed at 4 km, with residual noise measuring 3.98 dB62. Crucially, even when the RBS coefficient is reduced to its absolute minimum using existing techniques, a highly resilient residual noise persists. The presence of residual noise indicates that previously unidentified noise mechanisms persist in the Bidi transmission sensing link, which not only hinders the complete suppression of noise but also remains within the system in a ghost-like manner, exhibiting strong resilience against elimination. Consequently, we define the residual noise—characterized by unknown underlying mechanisms and high resistance to suppression—as “ghost noise.”

In this study, we reveal for the first time the intrinsic physical mechanism of the “ghost noise” in Bidi transmission links. This noise originates from two distinct sources: polarization cross-coupling noise (PCN), induced by birefringence perturbations coupled with parasitic interference within the link, and calculation cross-coupling noise (CCN), which arises during the sensing signal demodulation process. Both PCN and CCN emerge along with the parasitic interference effect caused by RBS. This “ghost noise” phenomenon commonly exists in and severely limits the performance of various sensing architectures that rely on Bidi transmission, including DAS, as well as FBG and FRM sensor arrays. Based on these new understandings, we develop a polarization-traversal interrogation (PTI) technique integrated with pseudo-random binary sequence (PRBS) phase modulation. This hybrid strategy achieves complete ghost noise suppression while preserving the minimalist link architecture of Bidi transmission. The zero-noise-growth distance in Bidi transmission links extends to 7 km, representing a 6-fold improvement over current research findings, and the noise suppression ratio reaches 94.8% in 10 km Bidi transmission links. Because this underlying physical mechanism is inherent to all single-fiber bidirectional transmission schemes, the integrated PTI and PRBS modulation technique demonstrates broad universal applicability across these architectures. While the core hardware remains identical, the technique can be seamlessly adapted to any specific sensing type by simply adjusting the PRBS modulation bandwidth to match the unique optical path imbalance of the target sensors. These results demonstrate that by employing only a narrow-linewidth laser, optical fiber sensing systems can achieve performance levels comparable to those of high-performance optical frequency combs63.

Results

Polarization cross-coupling noise

As a critical component of the ghost noise, PCN arises from the synergistic effect of parasitic interference and birefringence perturbations of the Bidi transmission link. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the downlink interrogation pulse pairs (E11, E12, E21, and E22, …) and the uplink signal coexist within the transmission fiber. The uplink signal comprises two distinct components: the sensing signal (EF1,n2 and EF2,n1) reflected from the sensing unit, and the RBS signal continuously generated along the entire transmission link by subsequent interrogation pulses. Crucially, this continuous generation of RBS dictates that specific portions of the backward propagating scattered light (Erb,(n+1)1 and Erb,(n+1)2) will inevitably overlap in the time domain with the returning sensing signals. These precise spatial-temporal collision segments are defined as “RBS sensitive points,” such as points A and B in Fig. 3, which act as the direct triggers of the parasitic interference. Consequently, as the Bidi transmission link length increases, the increased RBS sensitive points inherently escalate the accumulated noise level within the system.

It is crucial to distinguish the physical timescales governing these interactions. The internal time interval between pulses within a single interrogation pair, τg, is orders of magnitude shorter than the period of even extreme high-frequency environmental mechanical vibrations. This extreme timescale disparity ensures that the fiber birefringence remains effectively quasi-static during the ultra-short pulse pair transit. This “frozen state” guarantees identical polarization evolution for the interfering pulses, thereby rendering the primary sensing signal entirely immune to intra-pair disturbances. In stark contrast, the inter-pair interval TAOM and the total transmission time over the long link fiber are significantly longer. Over the extended timescales, the dynamic accumulation of environmental birefringence perturbations becomes highly nonnegligible. It is the continuous coupling of these long-term birefringence drifts with the spatially distributed parasitic interference that fundamentally generates the PCN.

These simulation results indicate that in the single fiber bidi transmission-based sensing systems, the individual presence of either birefringence drift or the RBS effect does not affect the performance of the sensing system. A notable increase in background noise occurs only when both factors coexist and interact within the link fiber. Furthermore, if an RBS sensitive point exists in the link in conjunction with birefringence perturbations, simply reducing the RBS coefficient is insufficient to completely suppress PCN and CCN. This observation aligns with the physical mechanisms of PCN and CCN, providing further evidence that the incomplete suppression of these two noise components ultimately gives rise to the ghost noise in fiber-optic sensing systems.

Strategy for complete ghost noise elimination

Simulation results confirm that merely reducing the RBS coefficient fails to fully suppress the Bidi transmission link noise, resulting in the aforementioned ghost noise. Based on the generation mechanisms of PCN and CCN, we develop an integrated suppression strategy for ghost noise.

We have achieved PCN suppression by forcing the polarization-dependent coefficients of parasitic interference terms to zero, which is defined as the PTI technique. For CCN suppression, we note that its generation mechanism involves: (1) J1(C) and J2(C); (2) the RBS coefficient; (3) the interference degree of parasitic interference and main interference B2/B1; and (4) phase drift ϕn1 and sensing information ϕs. Among them, only the RBS coefficient can be actively suppressed, while others are inherent to system physics.

Therefore, to fundamentally suppress the CCN at its physical source, we employ PRBS phase modulation. The rationale for selecting PRBS over conventional analog schemes (e.g., sinusoidal or triangular waves) lies in its unparalleled ability to shape the optical autocorrelation function. Physically, the intensity of RBS-induced parasitic interference is directly proportional to the cumulative autocorrelation of the interrogation pulse over the spatial overlap region. While analog modulations can reduce the coherence length to suppress the RBS coefficient, their autocorrelation functions inevitably exhibit residual periodic oscillations (sidelobes). This leads to strictly limited suppression capabilities (a normalized cumulative autocorrelation of ~0.56 for optimal cosine modulation). Furthermore, the continuous phase variations inherent to these analog schemes inadvertently introduce severe spectral instability. In stark contrast, PRBS features statistical properties mathematically akin to Gaussian white noise. Applying a 0/π PRBS phase modulation forces the optical autocorrelation curve to collapse into a sharp, nanosecond-scale quasi-delta peak while maintaining the spectral stability. This unique characteristic plunges the normalized parasitic coefficient to near zero (~0.027) without introducing the spectral instability typically associated with analog modulations, making PRBS the optimal choice for eradicating CCN.

Figure 7c presents the frequency-domain result obtained by applying a fast Fourier transform to the time-domain signal acquired with a 3.3 km Bidi transmission link. The spectrum exhibits distinct line components at the polarization modulation frequency and its harmonics, along with the overlaid PGC frequency. The spectrum indicates that the maximum demodulation frequency of the current experimental system is bounded at 6.25 kHz. It is crucial to clarify that this 6.25 kHz threshold is merely a strict mathematical constraint of the specific PGC algorithm configured in our setup, rather than a fundamental physical limitation of the proposed PTI-PRBS ghost noise suppression method. According to the Bessel function expansion inherent to PGC demodulation, the target signal generates sidebands around the baseband and the carrier harmonics. To prevent spectral aliasing—wherein high-frequency sidebands fold back into the baseband, causing severe harmonic distortion and irreversible deformation of the demodulated waveform—the maximum measurable acoustic frequency must be strictly less than half of the PGC carrier frequency (12.5 kHz in our system). However, this bandwidth limitation can be easily overcome in practical engineering by utilizing a high-speed EOM (instead of a PZT) to significantly increase the PGC carrier frequency, accompanied by a proportionally higher DAQ sampling rate. Furthermore, since the PTI-PRBS modulation operates in the interrogation pulse frequency regime, it imposes no physical bottleneck on the expansion of the acoustic bandwidth, making the scheme fully compatible with ultra-wideband sensing applications.

Figure 7d demonstrates the demodulation results under the integrated PTI-PRBS-PGC scheme. The background noise level with a 3.3 km Bidi transmission link reaches −90.08 dB, closely matching that of the zero Bidi transmission link system. These findings demonstrate that ghost noise has been efficiently suppressed, restoring the noise performance of the 3.3 km Bidi transmission link to the zero-baseline noise level. We further analyze the average background noise levels across 31 sets of data collected by the system when the link fiber length ranges from 1 to 10 km. The results are summarized in Fig. 7e. For transmission link lengths of 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 km, the corresponding average noise levels are measured as −90.23, −90.03, −89.98, −89.50, and −88.82 dB, respectively. These findings confirm that the Bidi transmission link noise within a 10 km range has been suppressed. As illustrated in Fig. 7f, it is evident that as the link length increases, the median noise values remain consistently around −90 dB—matching the overall average—and the noise distribution closely resembles that of the zero-link baseline. This observation further supports the robustness of the algorithm in suppressing Bidi transmission link noise.

Moreover, due to system disturbances, the background noise of the system exhibits fluctuations even under identical link conditions, shown in Fig. 7f. The mean noise value calculated from the 31 data sets also demonstrates the corresponding variability. To rigorously quantify the uncertainty, the background noise floor was characterized under zero-link conditions through 124 independent measurement trials. As depicted in the statistical analysis in Fig. 7g, the noise level yields an average value of −90.3 dB, with peak-to-peak fluctuations strictly contained within the range of −89.8 to −90.8 dB. Based on this empirical distribution, an experimental measurement error margin of ±0.5 dB is established. According to Fig. 7h, with a 7 km Bidi transmission link, the residual noise of 0.41 dB remains within the tolerance range. It can be concluded that the method achieves absolutely “zero noise growth” over distances up to 7 km. At a link length of 10 km, the system background noise is significantly reduced from −60.42 dB (without modulation) down to −88.82 dB. Compared to the zero-link baseline of approximately −90.34 dB, the ghost noise after suppression is reduced to only 1.52 dB, corresponding to a noise suppression ratio of up to 94.8%. Further analysis was conducted to determine the physical origin of this 1.52 dB residual noise after PTI-PRBS modulation. We first rigorously excluded the contribution of unmodeled nonlinear optical effects, which could potentially arise in the 10 km link. Theoretical analysis confirms that this raises the stimulated Brillouin scattering (SBS) threshold to approximately 167 mW; alongside a calculated stimulated Raman scattering (SRS) threshold of ~1.6 W, both limits exceed the 10 mW operating power by one and two orders of magnitude, respectively, thereby ensuring the system operates strictly in the linear regime. Additionally, the theoretical Kerr-induced phase noise is found to be insignificant. These are experimentally substantiated by the optical spectra in Fig. 7i, which present the optical spectra of the interrogation signal before and after transmission through the 10 km fiber link. The central wavelength remains highly stable at 1549.96 nm, and the complete absence of wavelength drift or Stokes sidebands confirms that nonlinear phenomena, such as SBS, SRS, four-wave mixing and Kerr-induced phase noise had a negligible impact on the system.

With nonlinearities ruled out, characterizations reveal that this residual component stems fundamentally from “visibility-induced noise.” As detailed in the subsequent “Materials and Methods” section regarding PRBS parameter optimization, successfully suppressing the severe 10 km ghost noise required setting the PRBS chip width to 1.6 ns. While this optimally compresses the laser’s coherence length to decorrelate parasitic interference, it inevitably causes a slight degradation in the interference fringe visibility for the unbalanced interferometer (Fig. 7b). As illustrated by the “U-shaped” noise dependence curve (Fig. 8b), the theoretical noise penalty corresponding to this specific visibility drop perfectly matches the experimentally observed 1.52 dB residual noise after PTI-PRBS modulation. Consequently, this residual noise is not an artifact of incomplete ghost noise suppression, but rather the fundamental physical limit of the system, where parasitic interference suppression is optimally balanced against visibility maintenance.

Discussion

This study provides the first systematic elucidation of the link noise generation mechanism within Bidi transmission links. By establishing a theoretical Jones matrix model, we demonstrate that the link noise fundamentally arises from the superposition of PCN and CCN, driven by the interplay between RBS and birefringence perturbations. This mechanistic understanding provides a critical theoretical foundation for addressing external disturbances in Bidi systems. Building on these insights, we proposed an innovative noise suppression strategy integrating PTI and PRBS modulation.

Experimental validation over a 10 km Bidi link reveals that this integrated approach achieves an unprecedented background noise level of −88.82 dB, representing a 94.8% suppression ratio. This performance significantly outperforms conventional narrow-linewidth laser systems and even surpasses recent state-of-the-art benchmarks—such as the −85 dB noise floor achieved in a similar 10 km setup using a highly complex, dual-cavity-driven optical frequency comb63. Achieving superior noise reduction with a standard, cost-effective semiconductor laser underscores the exceptional efficacy of our strategy. Moreover, we achieve zero noise increase over a 7 km Bidi transmission link. To the best of our knowledge, this sets a record for the lowest background noise in comparable Bidi systems, extending the “zero noise growth” link length by over 6-fold.

While this study experimentally demonstrates exceptional noise suppression up to 10 km, it is important to contextualize this capability within fundamental physical limits and practical applications. Fundamentally, the proposed PTI-PRBS scheme possesses no hard functional distance limit and will continue to strongly suppress parasitic interference over longer links. At 10 km, the suppression remains highly significant (94.8%). This low-noise range thoroughly addresses major real-world deployment scenarios. For vertical deployments, this range covers virtually all oceanographic profiling needs, extending to the deepest regions of the global ocean, such as the Mariana Trench. For horizontal seafloor deployments, 10 km perfectly aligns with the typical sensor node spacing in established cabled seafloor observatory networks. Thus, the scheme readily fulfills the practical requirements for both deep-sea vertical profiling and distributed node-based seafloor monitoring.

Given the growing interest in integrating sensing networks with global communication infrastructure, our findings demonstrate that high-performance, low-noise Bidi transmission sensing can be achieved with simplified hardware, offering significant implications for large-scale deployment. However, we acknowledge that in ultra-long-haul applications significantly exceeding the current scope, in addition to the cumulative enhancement of RBS, making it difficult to completely suppress the ghost noise, nonlinear phase accumulation will also become a limiting factor, which will be the subject of our subsequent research.

Methods

Experimental system

The experimental configuration is strictly designed to ensure high stability and precision for weak signal detection. The laser source (RIO) operates at a central wavelength of 1549.96 nm with an ultra-narrow linewidth of approximately 1.1 kHz. It is characterized by an ultra-low intensity noise level of −120dBHz−1@1kHz and a phase noise level of −110dBHz−1@1kHz. This choice of a highly coherent source is driven by the theoretical relationship between source coherence and detection sensitivity: the intrinsic phase noise floor of an unbalanced interferometer is linearly proportional to the laser linewidth. The continuous-wave output is then modulated into pulses by an AOM (G&H) with an extinction ratio exceeding 50 dB. To maintain strict polarization stability, all components prior to the sensing link are interconnected using polarization-maintaining fibers. The Bidi transmission link itself utilizes standard G.652D single-mode fiber (FiberHome).

Two distinct sensing configurations are evaluated. In System A, the sensing unit integrates an optical circulator and a 5% coupler (FiberHome) with a high-reflectivity FRM (>99%, Lightpromotech). In System B, a femtosecond-laser-inscribed FBG (YOFC) with a 1550 nm central wavelength, 3 nm bandwidth, and 5% reflectivity is employed. PC in System B is facilitated by a high-speed polarization switch (PSW-LN-0.1, iXblue). For the generation of the PRBS modulation signal, a high-precision (AWG) (M4i.6631, Spectrum Instrumentation) is utilized. This signal is subsequently amplified by a dedicated RF amplifier (DR-AN-10-MO, iXblue) to drive the EOM (iXblue).

The detection and acquisition systems are critical for accurately quantifying the system’s low noise floor. An avalanche photodetector (APD, Lightpromotech) is used for optoelectronic conversion, preceded by a manually adjustable optical attenuator to ensure the incident optical power remains strictly within the detector’s optimal linear dynamic range. DAQ is performed using an NI-6131 DAQ card (National Instruments). The combination of the ultra-low-noise RIO laser, the high-sensitivity APD, and the high-resolution acquisition system ensures a highly stable measurement environment. As detailed in the previous statistical analysis (Fig. 7g), this robust hardware configuration successfully underpins the reliable experimental measurement error margin of ±0.5 dB established under zero-link conditions.

PRBS modulation parameters

The PRBS sequence exhibits autocorrelation and cross-correlation properties analogous to those of Gaussian white noise, making it highly effective for coherence control. Being deterministic, the PRBS sequence can be repeatedly generated and replicated using specific algorithms. Although numerous types of PRBS have been proposed, the most fundamental and widely adopted ones remain the m-sequence and the gold sequence. The latter is adopted in this study due to its superior cross-correlation characteristics, which effectively resolve the limitations of m-sequence scarcity. As illustrated in Fig. 8a, the generation mechanism of the gold sequence is constructed by performing a modulo-2 sum operation on two preferred pairs of m sequences generated via linear-feedback shift registers.

The effectiveness of PRBS phase modulation hinges on the selection of an appropriate chip width, which is dictated by a strict physical trade-off between parasitic noise and interference visibility. While reducing the chip width theoretically decreases the residual RBS coefficient and further suppresses the CCN, it simultaneously compresses the laser’s coherence length (Lc). For an unbalanced interferometer, excessive reduction in coherence length inevitably degrades the fringe visibility of the main sensing interference. Since the visibility-induced noise is inversely proportional to interference visibility, any further reduction in chip width would cause visibility to plummet, triggering a drastic exponential rise in the fundamental white noise floor that far outweighs the benefits of eliminating the residual RBS. Experimental evaluations rigorously confirm this “U-shaped” noise dependence, as depicted in Fig. 8b. For our 10 km bidirectional transmission link, the background noise reaches its global minimum at a coherence length of Lc = 0.93 m. Consequently, we set the optimal gold-sequence chip width to 1.6 ns. This specific value represents the theoretical optimum threshold where parasitic ghost noise is maximally suppressed without causing severe visibility-induced noise.

Having established the optimal chip width of 1.6 ns, the order of the PRBS sequence must be configured to ensure full coverage of the interrogation pulse duration. Given that the interrogation pulse width is set to 480 ns, the sequence must contain at least 300 symbol elements. To satisfy this requirement, we select a 9th-order gold sequence, which comprises 29−1 = 511 symbol elements. This configuration yields a total PRBS duration of 817.6 ns (511 × 1.6 ns). As illustrated in the modulation timing diagram in Fig. 8c, each gold sequence is precisely synchronized with the rising edge of the interrogation pulse. This ensures that the 817.6 ns modulation window completely covers the 480-ns optical pulse, maintaining uniform coherence reduction and optimal ghost noise suppression across the entire pulse profile.

Optical signal demodulation procedure

The link noise suppression scheme integrates simultaneous PTI and PGC modulation, necessitating demodulation of the DAQ-acquired signals. As detailed in Fig. 9, the demodulation procedure involves three sequential operations: (I) independent phase extraction from each polarization channel via PGC demodulation; (II) integration of phase data from all four channels to construct the polarization matrix; (III) determinant calculation of the matrix for sensing information derivation.
