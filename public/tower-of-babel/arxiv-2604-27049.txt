Non-Local Magic Resources for Fermionic Gaussian States
Daniele Iannotti, Beatrice Magni, Riccardo Cioli, Alioscia Hamma, Xhek Turkeshi

Abstract
Entanglement and magic are fundamental resources that capture the complexity of quantum many-body systems. Non-local magic isolates the irreducible nonstabilizerness intrinsically tied to entanglement. However, evaluating this quantity generally requires a prohibitive minimization over the full Hilbert space, making it computationally inaccessible beyond a few qubits. Here, we overcome this bottleneck by suggesting a closed-form expression for the non-local stabilizer entropies of fermionic Gaussian states over local Gaussian unitaries, which can be evaluated in polynomial time directly from the eigenvalues of the reduced Majorana covariance matrix. We apply this framework to characterize fermionic non-local magic across diverse physical regimes: we derive an exact Page-like curve for typical random states, reveal logarithmic scaling at the quantum critical point of the XY model, and establish a quasiparticle picture for magic generation during out-of-equilibrium quantum quenches. Crucially, because our result relies solely on two-point correlation functions, it provides a scalable route for the experimental estimation of fermionic non-local magic in large-scale quantum processors via fermionic shadow tomography.

II
Methods

We begin by introducing the notation and key definitions; we refer to Refs.
68
;
90
;
85
;
86
for pedagogical introductions.
We consider fermionic Gaussian systems on
N
qubits in a lattice
\Lambda=\{1,\dots,N\}
, described by a Hilbert space of dimension
d=2^{N}
.
The natural degrees of freedom are the
2N
Majorana operators
\gamma_{j}
, obtained via the Jordan–Wigner transformation
\gamma_{2j-1}=\bigl(\prod_{m=1}^{j-1}Z_{m}\bigr)X_{j}
and
\gamma_{2j}=\bigl(\prod_{m=1}^{j-1}Z_{m}\bigr)Y_{j}
, where
X_{j}
,
Y_{j}
,
Z_{j}
are the Pauli operators on qubit
j
.
These operators are Hermitian,
\gamma_{j}=\gamma_{j}^{\dagger}
, and satisfy the Clifford algebra
\{\gamma_{j},\gamma_{k}\}=2\delta_{jk}\mathbb{I}
.

Fermionic Gaussian unitaries, or matchgates, are the operators that act adjointly on Majorana as rotations
U_{O}\gamma_{j}U_{O}^{\dagger}=\sum_{m}O_{mj}\gamma_{m}
, with
O\in\mathrm{O}(2N)
.
Orthogonal matrices can be decomposed as
O=QR
with
Q\in\mathrm{SO}(2N)
and
R=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,\dots,1)
a reflection.
This implies that
U_{O}=X_{1}^{(1-\det(O))/2}\exp[H]
is split into a part induced by special orthogonal transformations, captured by the so-called fermionic Gaussian Hamiltonians

H=\sum_{m,n=1}^{2N}h_{m,n}\gamma_{m}\gamma_{n}\;,

(1)

where
h=-h^{T}
is the
2N\times 2N
antisymmetric matrix such that
Q=\exp[h]
, and the implementation of the reflection
R
via the Pauli operator
X_{1}
.

A pure fermionic Gaussian state (FGS), or free fermionic state, is defined by
|\Psi_{O}\rangle=U_{O}|\mathbf{0}\rangle
for the fermionic Gaussian unitary induced by
O\in\mathrm{O}(2N)
, and
|\mathbf{0}\rangle\langle\mathbf{0}|=d^{-1}\prod_{m=1}^{N}(\mathbb{I}-i\gamma_{2m-1}\gamma_{2m})
is the fermionic vacuum state.
Denoting for any pure state
|\Psi\rangle
the covariance matrix
\Gamma_{mn}=i\langle\Psi|[\gamma_{m},\gamma_{n}]|\Psi\rangle/2
, fermionic Gaussian states are fully characterized by the correlation matrix. In fact, given the vacuum correlation matrix

\Gamma_{0}={\bigoplus_{m=1}^{N}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}}\;,

(2)

using the adjoint transformation, one obtains
|\Psi_{O}\rangle\langle\Psi_{O}|=\exp\left[i\sum_{mn}\Gamma_{mn}\gamma_{m}\gamma_{n}/2\right]
with
\Gamma=O\Gamma_{0}O^{T}
.
For pure fermionic Gaussian states, Ref.
15
showed that local FGU,
U_{O_{A}}\otimes U_{O_{B}}\in\mathfrak{M}_{N}
, are sufficient to bring the state into a canonical modewise form of independent two-mode BCS-like pairs

\begin{split}|\Psi_{\mathrm{can}}\rangle:=(T_{\sigma_{A}}\otimes T_{\sigma_{B}})\bigotimes_{i=1}^{\ell}[|\mathrm{B}(\theta_{i})\rangle]\otimes|0\rangle^{\otimes(N-2\ell)}\;,\end{split}

(3)

where
|\mathrm{B}(\theta_{i})\rangle=\cos\theta_{i}\,|00\rangle_{i}+\sin\theta_{i}\,|11\rangle_{i}
and
T_{\sigma_{X}}
are permutations that act separately on qubits in
X\in\{A,B\}
. This form is analogous to the Schmidt decomposition for pure fermionic Gaussian states, and it is fundamental for the main result.

II.1
Non-local magic and its fermionic counterpart

We now present our main quantity: non-local magic.
We recall that the stabilizer entropy of order
\alpha
is defined for a pure state
|\Psi\rangle
as
M_{\alpha}(|\Psi\rangle)=(1-\alpha)^{-1}\log_{2}\zeta_{\alpha}(|\Psi\rangle)

62
, where
\zeta_{\alpha}=d^{-1}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{N}}\langle\Psi|P|\Psi\rangle^{2\alpha}
is the stabilizer purity and
\mathcal{P}_{N}=\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes N}
is the set of
N
-qubit Pauli strings. This quantity is a magic monotone for integer
\alpha\geq 2

40
;
61
.

The non-local magic (NLM) is defined for the bipartition
\Lambda=A\sqcup B
and a pure state as

M_{\alpha}^{\textup{NL}}:=\min_{U_{A},U_{B}}M_{\alpha}\left[T_{\pi_{AB}}(U_{A}\otimes U_{B})T_{\pi_{AB}}^{\dagger}|\Psi\rangle\right]\;,

(4)

where
A=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}\}\subset\Lambda
contains
\ell
qubits and
B=\Lambda\setminus A
the remaining
N-\ell
ones,
\pi_{AB}\in\mathrm{S}_{N}
is any permutation such that
\pi_{AB}(A)=\{1,\dots,\ell\}
and
\pi_{AB}(B)=\{\ell+1,\dots,N\}
, and the minimization is over
\mathrm{U}(d_{A})\otimes\mathrm{U}(d_{B})
with
d_{A}=2^{\ell}
and
d_{B}=2^{N-\ell}
. This is the magic that cannot be erased or extracted by local unitary operations. This notion is cognate (but distinct from) to that of
long-range magic
, that is, the non-stabilizerness that cannot be removed by finite-depth local unitary circuits
56
. One salient distinction is that maximally entangled states have vanishing non-local magic as they are locally equivalent to stabilizer states.
This minimization is superexponentially costly, rendering non-local magic intractable beyond a handful of qubits.
In Ref.
20
, it was shown that for
\alpha=2
a reliable proxy for non-local magic can be obtained from the Schmidt spectrum of the state over the same bipartition.

To study fermionic Gaussian states and their non-local magic, we introduce the fermionic non-local magic

M_{\alpha}^{\textup{FNL}}:=\!\!\min_{U_{O_{A}},U_{O_{B}}}\!\!M_{\alpha}\left[T_{\pi_{AB}}(U_{O_{A}}\otimes U_{O_{B}})T_{\pi_{AB}}^{\dagger}|\Psi\rangle\right],

(5)

where now the minimization is over the Gaussian unitaries induced by
\mathrm{O}(2\ell)\oplus\mathrm{O}(2(N-\ell))
, hence
M_{\alpha}^{\textup{NL}}\leq M_{\alpha}^{\textup{FNL}}
. Up to a non-Gaussian permutation, this quantity corresponds to the minimization over the full unitary group for Gaussian systems.
Our main result is that, for fermionic Gaussian states, the fermionic non-local magic can be evaluated exactly in
\mathrm{poly}(N)
resources.

III
Results

Consider a pure fermionic Gaussian state
|\Psi_{O}\rangle
with correlation matrix
\Gamma=O\Gamma_{0}O^{T}
and the bipartition
\Lambda=A\sqcup B
, where we denote without loss of generality
A
the smallest partition with
|A|=\ell\leq N/2
.
Then, its FNL magic with
\alpha\geq 2
integer is given by

\begin{split}M_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(|\Psi_{O}\rangle)\!&=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(\lambda_{i}^{2})\;,\end{split}

(6)

where
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(x)
is the weight function

\begin{split}\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(x)&:=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log_{2}\!\left[\frac{\left(1-x\right)^{\alpha}\!\!+1+x^{\alpha}}{2}\right]\;,\end{split}

(7)

and
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1,\dots,\ell}
are the positive eigenvalues of the
2\ell\times 2\ell
reduced correlation matrix
\Gamma_{A}:=\Gamma|_{\mathcal{I}_{A}}
of the Majorana mode restricted to
A
,
\mathcal{I}_{A}:=\{2m-1,2m\;|\;m\in A\}
.

We now present the idea behind this result discussed in Appendix
A
. The key point is that local Gaussian unitaries over a bipartition of the system bring any pure fermionic Gaussian state into a canonical modewise form
\ket{\Psi_{\rm can}}
in Eq. (
3
), consisting of independent two-mode Bell-like pairs
15
, a normal form that underlies several equivalent descriptions of fermionic Gaussian entanglement
89
;
71
;
59
.
Extensive simulations lead us to state that

M_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(|\Psi_{O}\rangle)=M_{\alpha}(|\Psi_{\rm can}\rangle)
,
from which Eq. (
6
) follows by direct computation of the two-mode stabilizer entropy
20
;
77
.

Several remarks are in order.
First, even though restricted to a subgroup of the unitary group, our result (
6
) works as a full minimization over unitary matrices for random states of a few sites (up to
4
) and Hamiltonian models studied in the following. This is due to a reordering operation that must be performed to reach the actual minimum. As the number of sites increases, the permutation that performs this reordering does not appear to be Clifford nor Gaussian, leading to a difference between the minimum obtainable with Gaussian minimization and the one accessible with generic unitaries.
Second, our formula is considerably simpler even than computing the
total
stabilizer entropy
M_{\alpha}
for fermionic Gaussian states, which requires either Majorana Monte Carlo
12
or perfect sampling
25
, both requiring an exponential number of samples for convergence in the worst case. In contrast, evaluating
M_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{FNL}}
via Eq. (
6
) requires only
\mathrm{poly}(\ell)
operations.

In the following, we will consider the simplified and relevant case
\alpha=2
, where the stabilizer entropy is a magic monotone. (Nevertheless, we have checked that the same phenomenology holds for
\alpha>2
).
In this case, the weight function simplifies and becomes
\mathfrak{m}_{2}(x)=-\log_{2}(1-x+x^{2})
.
We will show that the structure highlighted in Eq. (
6
) enables several analytical results, both at equilibrium and out-of-equilibrium.

III.1
Simple example: entanglement without non-local magic

Before presenting applications, we illustrate the physical content of the non-local magic with a simple but instructive example that clarifies the distinction between entanglement and non-local magic.
Consider
N
qubits with
\ell=N/2
and the rainbow state

|\Psi_{\mathrm{rb}}\rangle=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|0_{j}0_{\bar{\jmath}}\rangle+|1_{j}1_{\bar{\jmath}}\rangle\bigr)\;,

(8)

where
\bar{\jmath}=N+1-j
is the mirror site in
B=\{\ell+1,\dots,N\}
, for the bipartition
A=\{1,\dots,\ell\}
. This is a fermionic Gaussian state: it is the ground state of a free-fermion hopping Hamiltonian with suitable long-range pairing. Each pair
(j,\bar{\jmath})
forms a maximally entangled Bell pair, so every eigenvalue of
i\Gamma_{A}
vanishes,
\lambda_{n}=0
, and the entanglement entropy is maximal:
S=\ell
bits, a volume law. Yet, this state is a stabilizer state, it belongs to the Clifford orbit of the computational basis, and consequently its magic, and therefore its non-local magic, vanishes identically:
M_{2}^{\mathrm{NL}}=0
.

This result is transparent from our formula:
\mathfrak{m}_{2}(\lambda_{n}^{2})
vanishes both at
\lambda_{n}=0
(maximally entangled mode) and at
\lambda_{n}=1
(unentangled mode). Non-local magic requires modes at
intermediate
entanglement,
0<\lambda_{n}<1
, where the bipartite correlations are neither trivial nor of the Bell-pair type.

One might attempt to introduce magic into the rainbow state by applying local unitaries, e.g. a non-Clifford single-qubit rotation
R(\phi)
on site
j\in A
. This produces a state
(R(\phi)\otimes\mathbb{I})|\mathrm{Bell}\rangle_{j\bar{\jmath}}
that carries nonzero total magic. However, by construction, this magic is entirely
local
: it can be removed by the inverse rotation
R(-\phi)\otimes\mathbb{I}
acting on
A
alone, and therefore does not contribute to
M_{2}^{\mathrm{NL}}
. The only way to create genuine non-local magic is to entangle the two qubits of each Bell pair in a non-stabilizer fashion, for instance, by applying a joint non-Clifford rotation
V_{j\bar{\jmath}}

44
that acts simultaneously on sites in
A
and
B
. Such a rotation shifts
\lambda_{n}
away from
0
and
1
, placing it in the interior of
[0,1]
where
\mathfrak{m}_{2}(\lambda_{n}^{2})>0
. In other words, entanglement is necessary but not sufficient for non-local magic: a state can be maximally entangled yet carry zero nonstabilizerness across the bipartition.

III.2
Experimental observability

A key practical advantage of Eq. (
6
) is that the fermionic non-local magic of Gaussian states is entirely determined by the reduced covariance matrix
\Gamma_{A}
, a polynomial number of two-point correlation functions.
While a proof-of-principle demonstration of non-local magic has recently been reported on a superconducting processor in a two-qubit setting
4
, our result opens a scalable route applicable to large fermionic Gaussian systems.

A natural experimental protocol is
fermionic shadow tomography

104
: random matchgate circuits are applied, followed by computational-basis measurements, and
\Gamma_{A}
is inferred from the resulting classical-shadow data.
A conservative sample-complexity estimate yields, for failure probability at most
\delta
,

N_{\mathrm{shots}}=O\!\left(\frac{\ell^{2}\log(\ell/\delta)}{\epsilon_{\Gamma}^{2}}\right),

to reconstruct
\Gamma_{A}
with accuracy
\epsilon_{\Gamma}
, while its error propagation to
M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}
is discussed in the Methods.

III.3
Fermionic Non-local magic of typical Gaussian states

Having established the exact formula, we first investigate the fermionic non-local magic of
typical
Gaussian states, i.e., states
|\Psi_{O}\rangle=U_{O}|\mathbf{0}\rangle
generated by Haar-random
O\in\mathrm{O}(2N)
.
Our goal is to compute the average
\overline{M}_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}\equiv\mathbb{E}_{O\sim\mathrm{O}(2N)}[M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(|\Psi_{O}\rangle)]
, which for our closed-form expression reduces to a random matrix theory problem
69
;
64
;
102
.
For a fixed subsystem
A
of size
\ell\leq N/2
, let us define the stochastic variables
x_{k}:=\lambda_{k}^{2}
, whose joint probability distribution is a Jacobi ensemble
69

\mathbb{P}(x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell})=\frac{\Delta(x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell})}{Z_{\ell}}\prod_{m=1}^{\ell}\frac{(1-x_{m})^{N-2\ell}}{\sqrt{x_{m}(1-x_{m})}}\;,

(9)

with
\Delta(x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell})=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq\ell}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}
a Vandermonde determinant contribution and
Z_{\ell}
the normalization ensuring
\int\mathbb{P}(x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell})\prod_{m=1}^{\ell}(dx_{m})=1
.
Since
M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}=\sum_{m=1}^{\ell}\mathfrak{m}_{2}(x_{m})
is a sum over separate
x_{m}
contributions, the only ingredient required is the one-point density function

\begin{split}\varrho^{(\ell)}(x):&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\sum_{m=1}^{\ell}\delta(x-x_{m})\right]\\
&=\ell\int\mathbb{P}(x,x_{2},\dots,x_{\ell})\prod_{m=2}^{\ell}(dx_{m})\>,\end{split}

(10)

where we used relabelling in the second step. Notice that
\int_{0}^{1}\varrho^{(\ell)}(x)dx=\ell
.
Thus, the average fermionc non-local magic is

\overline{M}_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathfrak{m}_{2}(x)\varrho^{(\ell)}(x)dx\;.

(11)

For the Jacobi ensemble, the one-point density is given by the standard orthogonal-polynomial kernel.
Given
w(x)=(1-x)^{N-2\ell}/\sqrt{x(1-x)}
, we have
86
;
85

\rho^{(\ell)}(x)=w(x)\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}[p_{n}(x)]^{2}\;,

(12)

where
p_{n}
are polynomials in the domain
[0,1]
, that are orthonormal with respect to the weight
w(x)
, namely
p_{n}(x)=\mathrm{P}_{n}^{(-1/2,N-2\ell-1/2)}(1-2x)/\sqrt{h_{n}}
in terms of the Jacobi polynomials
\mathrm{P}_{n}^{(a,b)}
and with normalization

h_{n}=\frac{\Gamma(n+1/2)\Gamma(n+N-2\ell+1/2)}{(2n+N-2\ell)\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(n+N-2\ell)}.

(13)

Eq. (
12
) yields the exact average non-local magic at finite size
N
for a given
\ell
, which we benchmark against exact numerics in Fig.
1
(a), finding perfect agreement. We emphasize that this is a
quenched
average, the average is performed outside the logarithm, which is typically harder to evaluate than its annealed counterpart.

We now turn to the thermodynamic limit
\ell,N\to\infty
at fixed ratio
r=\ell/N\in[0,1/2]
, defining the fermionic non-local magic density
\overline{\mathsf{m}}_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}:=\overline{M}_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}/N
(the final result is symmetric under
r\to 1-r
).
In this regime, the one-point density
\varrho^{(\ell)}
converges to the free Jacobi law with support
x\in[0,c]
,
c=4r(1-r)
,

\varrho_{r}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{(c-x)x}}{2\pi x(1-x)}\mathbf{1}_{[0,c]}(x)\;,

(14)

where
\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)
is the characteristic function of
A
,
\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)=1
if and only if
x\in A
.
The total mass of the density is
\int_{0}^{c}\varrho_{r}(x)dx=r
, which is precisely the fraction of the number of eigenvalues over the system size.
Thus, in the scaling limit, we have

\overline{\mathsf{m}}_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(r)\simeq\int_{0}^{c}\mathfrak{m}_{2}(x)\varrho_{r}(x)dx\;.

(15)

A more convenient parametrization is obtained by setting
x=c\sin^{2}\theta
with
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
. Then the integral is

\overline{\mathsf{m}}_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(r)\simeq\frac{c}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{\cos^{2}\theta}{1-c\sin^{2}\theta}\mathfrak{m}_{2}(c\sin^{2}\theta)d\theta\;.

(16)

At exactly half bipartition,
r=1/2
, this integral can be performed exactly and the formula reduces to

\overline{\mathsf{m}}_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(r)\simeq 2-\log_{2}(2+\sqrt{3})
.
On the other hand, for small
r\simeq 0
(and by symmetry
r\simeq 1
), we have
c\simeq 4r
and the integral yields
\overline{\mathsf{m}}_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(r\simeq 0)\simeq\frac{r^{2}}{\ln(2)}+\mathcal{O}(r^{3})
.
This
fermionic non-local magic Page curve
shows a quadratic onset, for every
\alpha
(see Appendix Sec.
A.4
), with a transparent physical origin: when
\ell
is of order one while
N\to\infty
, the
\ell
modes of
A
are almost, or exactly, maximally entangled and thus carry no non-local magic.
This is in sharp contrast with the entanglement Page curve, which vanishes only at
|A|=0
and grows linearly for small
r
.

To test the universality of this result beyond Haar-random states, we consider the Sachdev–Ye–Kitaev model at quadratic order,
\mathrm{SYK}_{2}
.
This model is specified by the Hamiltonian Eq. (
1
), with
h
a random antisymmetric matrix whose elements are drawn from the normal distribution
h_{m,n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{J^{2}}/{N})
and set
J=1
.
The single-body Hamiltonian
ih
is Hermitian with eigenvalues in
\pm\varepsilon_{k}
pairs (
k=1,\dots,N
), each pair defining an effective fermionic mode. A many-body eigenstate is specified by a filling
\mathcal{F}\subset\{1,\dots,2N\}
of
N
single-particle modes with eigenvectors
|v_{k}\rangle
, yielding the one-body density matrix
\Xi=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{F}}|v_{k}\rangle\langle v_{k}|
and the covariance matrix
\Gamma=i(2\Xi-I)
.
The ground state fills the
N
lowest-energy modes; to probe typicality, we construct mid-spectrum eigenstates
H|E_{n}\rangle=E_{n}|E_{n}\rangle
by flipping a random subset of
N/4
to
N/2
modes from the ground-state configuration, replacing occupied negative-energy modes with their positive-energy partners. For each of
N_{\mathrm{dis}}=1000
disorder realizations, we compute
M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}
from
\Gamma
and average over 200 excited eigenstates
|E_{n}\rangle
realizations to obtain
\overline{M}_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}=\mathbb{E}_{h}[\overline{M}_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(|E_{n}\rangle)]
.
In Fig.
1
(b), we plot the resulting non-local magic density
\overline{\mathsf{m}}_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}
versus
r=\ell/N
for
N=40,80,160
.
The data converge to the analytical Haar prediction Eq. (
16
) as
N
increases, confirming that mid-spectrum eigenstates of SYK
2
reproduce the typicality of fermionic non-local magic expected from random fermionic Gaussian states.

We now apply Eq. (

H=-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\!\left[\frac{1+\eta}{2}X_{j}X_{j+1}+\frac{1-\eta}{2}Y_{j}Y_{j+1}+\mu Z_{j}\right],

(17)

Using the Jordan–Wigner transformation, Eq. (

h_{2m-1,2m}=2\mu
,
h_{2m,2m+1}=1+\eta
,
h_{2m+2,2m-1}=1-\eta
, and all remaining elements fixed by antisymmetry

.
We restrict to system sizes
N
divisible by
4
, for which the ground state lies in the
Neveu–Schwarz sector with antiperiodic fermionic boundary
conditions
\gamma_{N+1}=-\gamma_{1}

68
.
The allowed momenta are

k=k(n)=\pi(2n+1)/N

(0,\pi)
with
N/2

in the thermodynamic limit

[0,\pi]

Then, the single-body Hamiltonian

is diagonalized by a Fourier transform to momentum space followed by a
2\times 2
Bogoliubov rotation at each wave-vector
k

68
.
The Bogoliubov angle
\theta_{k}
is defined through

\cos\theta_{k}=\frac{\mu-\cos k}{\varepsilon_{k}}\;,\qquad\sin\theta_{k}=\frac{\eta\sin k}{\varepsilon_{k}}\;,

(18)

yielding the single-particle dispersion

\varepsilon_{k}=\sqrt{(\mu-\cos k)^{2}+\eta^{2}\sin^{2}k}\;.

(19)

In the diagonal basis, the Hamiltonian reads
H=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{k}\varepsilon_{k}\,\tilde{\gamma}_{2k-1}\tilde{\gamma}_{2k}
, where
\tilde{\gamma}_{m}=\sum_{n}G_{mn}\gamma_{n}
are the rotated Majorana operators.
The ground state is the vacuum of all Bogoliubov modes, with covariance matrix
\Gamma=G\,\Gamma_{0}\,G^{T}
.

From now on, we consider the bipartition fixed by the contiguous interval
A=\{1,\dots,\ell\}
and its complement
B=\Lambda\setminus A
.
In the thermodynamic limit

2\ell\times 2\ell

\begin{split}(\Gamma_{A})_{mn}&=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\,e^{ik(m-n)}\,g(k)\;,\\
g(k)&=\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{i\theta_{k}}\\
-e^{-i\theta_{k}}&0\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(20)

The fermionic non-local magic
M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}=\sum_{n}\mathfrak{m}_{2}(\lambda_{n}^{2})
is therefore a spectral function of
\Gamma_{A}
with symbol
g(k)
, providing a direct route to efficient numerical evaluation.
For a given Hamiltonian and subsystem size
\ell

\Gamma_{A}
by evaluating the integral in Eq. (

6

\pm\lambda_{n}
(

i\Gamma_{A}

This procedure reaches large subsystem sizes

\mathcal{O}(\ell^{3})

In the scaling limit
N\gg\ell\gg 1

;
50

Since the symbol (

for all

When

Logarithmic growth can arise only from singularities in the symbol, which for the Ising model (

III.4.1
Exact results off criticality via corner transfer matrices

The reduced density matrix of subsystem
A
is a fermionic Gaussian state, and can be written as
76

\rho_{A}=\frac{\exp[-H_{\mathrm{ent}}]}{Z_{\mathrm{ent}}}\;,\quad H_{\mathrm{ent}}=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}\varepsilon_{n}^{\mathrm{ent}}\,\tilde{\gamma}_{2n-1}\tilde{\gamma}_{2n}\;,

(21)

where
H_{\mathrm{ent}}
is the entanglement Hamiltonian,
Z_{\mathrm{ent}}=\mathrm{Tr}(\exp[-H_{\mathrm{ent}}])
is the normalization and
\tilde{\gamma}_{m}
are the Majorana operators that diagonalize
\rho_{A}
, and
\varepsilon_{n}^{\mathrm{ent}}\geq 0
are the single-particle entanglement energies.
The covariance matrix
\Gamma_{A}
and the single-particle entanglement Hamiltonian
h_{\mathrm{ent}}
implicitly defined in Eq. (
21
) are related by

\Gamma_{A}=\tanh\!\left(\frac{h_{\mathrm{ent}}}{2}\right),

(22)

so that the eigenvalues of
i\Gamma_{A}
are
\pm\lambda_{n}
with
\lambda_{n}=\tanh\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{n}^{\mathrm{ent}}}{2}\right)
.
Here
\lambda_{n}=1
corresponds to a pure (unentangled) mode and
\lambda_{n}<1
to an entangled one.

For the XY model away from criticality, the entanglement spectrum of a semi-infinite bipartition fixed by
A=\mathbb{Z}_{+}=\{1,2,\dots,\}
is known in closed form through the corner transfer matrix approach
75
;
29
.
As we show below, this leads to the exact computation of
M_{2}^{\mathrm{NL,\mathbb{Z}_{+}}}
in this regime.
This regime approximates the scaling limit
N\gg\ell\gg 1
.
The single-particle entanglement energies are equally spaced,

\varepsilon_{n}^{\mathrm{ent}}=\begin{cases}(2n+1)\,\varepsilon&\mu>1\;,\\
2n\,\varepsilon&\mu<1\;,\end{cases}

(23)

where
\varepsilon=\pi K(\kappa^{\prime})/K(\kappa)
is the level spacing,
K(x)
denotes the complete elliptic integral of the first kind,
\kappa^{\prime}=\sqrt{1-\kappa^{2}}
, and
\kappa
is conditioned on the phase
75

\kappa=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\eta^{2}+\mu^{2}-1}}&\mu>1\;,\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\eta^{2}+\mu^{2}-1}}{\eta}&\mu<1,\;\eta^{2}+\mu^{2}>1\;,\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1-\eta^{2}-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}}&\mu<1,\;\eta^{2}+\mu^{2}<1\;.\end{cases}

(24)

This leads to the exact expression for the off-critical phases

M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL},\mathbb{Z}_{+}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{m}_{2}\!\left[\tanh^{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{n}^{\mathrm{ent}}}{2}\right)\right].

(25)

For a block of
\ell
sites in the infinite chain with periodic boundary conditions, two separate boundaries contribute, so that the fermionic non-local magic is twice the single-cut result

M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(\ell)\simeq 2M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL},\mathbb{Z}_{+}}=2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{m}_{2}\!\left[\tanh^{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{n}^{\mathrm{ent}}}{2}\right)\right],

(26)

up to corrections exponentially small in
\ell/\xi
, where
\xi
is the correlation length of the problem.
Since
\mathfrak{m}_{2}(x)\to 0
exponentially as
x\to 1
, and
\lambda_{n}^{2}\to 1
for large
n
, the series converges rapidly: for
\mu
away from criticality, only a few terms contribute, consistently with the Szegő prediction of a finite
M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}
.

At the critical point

Quench dynamics and quasiparticle picture

We now turn from equilibrium to the non-equilibrium dynamics of fermionic non-local magic, focusing on quantum quenches in the Ising chain.
The system is prepared in the ground state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
of
H(\mu_{0})
and, at time
t=0
, the transverse field is suddenly quenched to
\mu\neq\mu_{0}
; the state then evolves unitarily as
|\Psi(t)\rangle=e^{-iH(\mu)t}|\Psi_{0}\rangle
.
Since both the initial state and the time evolution preserve the Gaussian structure,
|\Psi(t)\rangle
remains a fermionic Gaussian state at all times, fully characterized by its time-dependent covariance matrix
\Gamma(t)
.
In particular, the time-dependent block-Toeplitz symbol of
\Gamma_{A}(t)
takes the form
18

(\Gamma_{A})_{mn}(t)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\frac{dk}{2\pi}\,e^{ik(m-n)}\,g(k,t)\;,

(29)

with time-dependent symbol

g(k,t)=\begin{pmatrix}-q(k,t)&p(k,t)\\
-p(-k,t)&q(k,t)\end{pmatrix},

(30)

where we defined the functions

\begin{split}p(k,t)&=e^{-i\theta_{k}}\left[\cos(\Delta\theta_k)+i\sin(\Delta\theta_k)\cos(2\varepsilon_k t)\right],\\
q(k,t)&=i\,\sin(\Delta\theta_k)\,\sin(2\varepsilon_k t)\,.\end{split}

(31)

Here
\varepsilon_{k}
is the post-quench dispersion,
\theta_{k}
is the post-quench Bogoliubov angle, and
\Delta\theta_{k}=\theta_{k}-\theta_{k}^{(0)}
is the mismatch between the post-quench and pre-quench Bogoliubov angles, with

\cos(\Delta\theta_k)=\frac{(\mu-\cos(k))(\mu_{0}-\cos(k))+\sin^{2}(k)}{\varepsilon_{k}\,\varepsilon_{k}^{(0)}}\,.

(32)

At
t=0
,
q(k,0)=0
and
p(k,0)=e^{-i\theta_{k}^{(0)}}
, recovering the ground-state symbol of
H(\mu_{0})
.
The diagonal component,
q(k,t)
, is generated dynamically by the quench and oscillates as
\sin(2\varepsilon_k t)
.

III.5.1
Stationary state via dephasing

At long times
t\to\infty
, the oscillating terms
\cos(2\varepsilon_k t)
and
\sin(2\varepsilon_k t)
in
p(k,t)
and
q(k,t)
average to zero due to dephasing across modes with different group velocities
18
.
Only the time-independent part of
p
survives,
p(k,\infty)=e^{-i\theta_{k}}\cos(\Delta\theta_k)
, while
q(k,\infty)=0
.
The resulting stationary covariance matrix is that of a generalized Gibbs ensemble (GGE)
18
;
102
and is again a block-Toeplitz matrix, now with a
time-independent
symbol.
The eigenvalues
\pm\lambda_{k}^{\infty}
of the stationary
i\Gamma_{A}^{\infty}
are determined by the Bogoliubov angle mismatch alone,
and the stationary fermionic non-local magic density is obtained via the Szegő theorem
18

M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(\infty)=\frac{\ell}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\!dk\;\mathfrak{m}_{2}\!\left(\sin^{4}\!\Delta\theta_{k}\right)\;.

(33)

This result reveals that for any
\mu\neq\mu_{0}
, the quench generically produces extensive (volume-law) fermionic non-local magic; the expression is symmetric under
\mu\leftrightarrow\mu_{0}
.

III.5.2
Quasiparticle picture

Eq. (
33
) bears a striking structural similarity to the entanglement entropy of the same quench
18
,
S=\frac{\ell}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\!dk\;\mathfrak{f}\!\left(\sin^{2}\!\Delta\theta_{k}\right)
, with the binary entropy
\mathfrak{f}(x):=-(1-x)\log_{2}(1-x)-x\log_{2}(x)
replaced by
\mathfrak{m}_{2}(x^{2})
.
This parallel extends beyond the stationary state and allows us to derive, from first principles, a quasiparticle picture for the spreading of fermionic non-local magic.

The fermionic non-local magic can be written as a contour integral over the characteristic polynomial
D_{\ell}(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I - i\Gamma_A)
,

M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}d\lambda\;\mathfrak{m}_{2}(\lambda^{2})\;\frac{d}{d\lambda}\ln D_{\ell}(\lambda)\;,

(34)

where
\mathcal{C}
encircles all eigenvalues.
Expanding
\ln D_{\ell}(\lambda)
in powers of
\lambda^{-1}
expresses
M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}
as a series in the even moments
\mathrm{Tr}(i\Gamma_{A})^{2n}
.
The
2\ell\times 2\ell
matrix
i\Gamma_{A}
has a block-Toeplitz structure inherited from the symbol
g(k,t)
.
Exploiting this structure, one can show that its
2\ell
eigenvalues coincide with those of two Hermitian matrices
W_{\pm}=\mathcal{H}\pm i\mathcal{T}
, where
\mathcal{T}_{jl}=\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\,e^{-ik(j-l)}\,q(k,t)
is Toeplitz and
\mathcal{H}_{jl}=\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\,e^{-ik(j+l-\ell-1)}\,p(k,t)
is Hankel. This enables the exact time dependence of
M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(t)
following the strategy in Ref.
32
.
The moments
\mathrm{Tr}(i\Gamma_{A})^{2n}=\mathrm{Tr}\,W_{+}^{2n}+\mathrm{Tr}\,W_{-}^{2n}
are then computed by expanding the matrix products into
2n
-fold momentum integrals.
As shown in Ref.
32
, these integrals can be evaluated exactly in the limit
\ell\gg 1
via a multidimensional stationary phase approximation.
This procedure leads to the asymptotic expression

\begin{split}\!\!\!\mathrm{Tr}\,(i\Gamma_{A})^{2n}&\simeq\frac{\ell}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\!dk\cos^{2n}(\Delta\theta_{k})\\
+\frac{\ell}{\pi}&\int_{0}^{\pi}\!\!\!dk\bigl(1-\cos^{2n}(\Delta\theta_{k})\bigr)\min\!\left(1,\frac{2|v_{k}|t}{\ell}\right),\end{split}

(35)

where
v_{k}=d\varepsilon_{k}/dk
is the group velocity.

The first term is the stationary (GGE) contribution, while the second captures the ballistic propagation of quasiparticle pairs.
After integration in Eq. (
34
) to the stationary value Eq. (
33
) yields the quasiparticle formula

M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(\ell,t)\simeq\int_{0}^{\pi}\!\frac{dk}{\pi}\,\min\!\bigl(2|v_{k}|\,t,\;\ell\bigr)\;\mathfrak{m}_{2}\!\bigl(\sin^{4}(\Delta\theta_{k})\bigr),

(36)

valid in the scaling limit
\ell,t\gg 1
with
t/\ell
arbitrary.
Eq. (
36
) predicts linear growth
\mathrm{M}_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}\propto t
for
t<t^{*}=\ell/(2v_{\max})
and saturation to the extensive value
\mathrm{M}_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(\infty)/\ell=\pi^{-1}\int_{0}^{\pi}\mathfrak{m}_{2}(\sin^{4}\!\Delta\theta_{k})\,dk
for
t\gg t^{*}
.
Eq. (
36
) has a transparent physical interpretation in terms of the quasiparticle picture
18
;
32
: the quench creates pairs of entangled quasiparticles at each point, which propagate ballistically with mode-dependent velocities
\pm v_{k}
.
A pair at momentum
k
contributes to
M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}
of the block
A
only when one partner has entered
A
while the other remains outside, hence the
\min(2|v_{k}|t,\ell)
factor.
The weight
\mathfrak{m}_{2}(\sin^{4}\Delta\theta_{k})
quantifies the fermionic non-local magic carried by each entangled pair: it vanishes both when
\Delta\theta_{k}=0
(no quench, no entanglement production) and when
\Delta\theta_{k}=\pi/2
(maximally entangled but stabilizer-like mode), and is maximal at intermediate values.

We benchmark Eq. (
36
) at
\eta=1
(Ising chain) evaluating numerically Eq. (
29
).
Our results are reported in Fig.
3
for the quench

(panel (b)) at subsystem sizes

.
The exact time evolution (markers), obtained by constructing the full block-Toeplitz
\Gamma_{A}(t)
via Eqs. (

) and diagonalizing, is in excellent agreement with the semiclassical prediction (solid lines) from Eq. (

).

III.6
Random Gaussian circuit dynamics

Finally, we consider the dynamics of fermionic non-local magic under random Gaussian circuits
95
.
The circuit is composed of nearest-neighbor fermionic Gaussian unitaries arranged in a brick-wall geometry on
N
sites.
A single layer
O_{\mathrm{layer}}=O_{\mathrm{odd}}\,O_{\mathrm{even}}
consists of an even and an odd half-step, namely
O_{\mathrm{even}}=\prod_{j=1}^{N/2}R_{2j-1,2j}
and
O_{\mathrm{odd}}=\prod_{j=1}^{N/2-1}R_{2j,2j+1}
, where each
R_{j,j+1}\in\mathrm{SO}(4)
is a random orthogonal matrix drawn independently from the Haar measure, acting on the four Majorana operators
(\gamma_{2j-1},\gamma_{2j},\gamma_{2j+1},\gamma_{2j+2})
of sites
j
and
j+1
.
The full circuit after
t
layers is
O(t)=\prod_{s=1}^{t}O_{\mathrm{layer}}^{(s)}\in\mathrm{SO}(2N)
.
The initial state is the product state
|\mathbf{0}\rangle
(vacuum of all fermionic modes), with covariance matrix
\Gamma_{0}
.

The time-evolved state remains Gaussian at all times, with covariance matrix
\Gamma(t)=O(t)\,\Gamma(0)\,O(t)^{T}
,
O(t)\in\mathrm{SO}(2N)
.
The fermionic non-local magic is therefore entirely determined by the restricted covariance matrix
\Gamma_{A}(t)
and its eigenvalues
\pm\lambda_{j}(t)
.

In contrast to the ballistic quasiparticle spreading found in the integrable quench setting, operator spreading in local random free-fermion circuits is
diffusive

27
;
91
.
Since the fermionic non-local magic is a smooth spectral functional of
\Gamma_{A}(t)
, it inherits this diffusive transport.
At early times, expanding the single-mode contribution for small
\lambda
,
\mathfrak{m}_{2}(\lambda^{2})\simeq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\ln 2}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4})
, so that
M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(t)\simeq\frac{1}{2\ln 2}\mathrm{Tr}[\Gamma_{A}(t)^{2}]
, which is governed by the diffusive spreading of two-point correlations across the entanglement cut
27
.
This argument yields

M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(t)\simeq\sqrt{t}\;,\qquad 1\ll t\ll N^{2}\;.

(37)

At late times,
M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}
saturates to the Haar-random fermionic Gaussian value, and the natural scaling variable is
\sqrt{t}/N
.
In Fig.
3
(d), we plot
M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(t)/N
versus
\sqrt{t}/N
for
N=20,40,80
, finding an excellent data collapse onto a single scaling function that interpolates between
\sqrt{t}
growth and saturation at the Page-curve value
\mathsf{m}_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}(r=1/2)=2-\log_{2}(2+\sqrt{3})
(dashed line).
The saturation timescale
t\sim\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
is consistent with diffusive dynamics.

IV
Discussion

IV.1
Summary

We have derived an exact, closed-form expression for the fermionic non-local stabilizer entropies of Gaussian states.
By reducing the minimization over Gaussian unitaries, the computation simplifies to a single eigenvalue decomposition of the reduced covariance matrix, completely bypassing the prohibitive variational minimization over the full Hilbert space. This establishes fermionic Gaussian states as a broad class of highly entangled many-body systems where the related non-local magic is exactly tractable, providing a useful proxy for the non-local magic over the full Unitary group.

We applied this framework across three key physical settings.
For typical fermionic Gaussian states, we derived an exact Page-like curve via random matrix theory, a prediction independently confirmed by SYK
2
mid-spectrum eigenstates.
At equilibrium, we characterized the fermionic non-local magic of the XY spin chain ground state, obtaining exact results in both gapped and critical phases: a finite saturation value off criticality via corner transfer matrices, and logarithmic scaling at the critical point via the Fisher–Hartwig formula.
Out of equilibrium, we established a quasiparticle picture for the spreading of fermionic non-local magic after quantum quenches, finding linear growth up to a crossing time
t^{\star}\sim\ell/v_{\mathrm{max}}
followed by saturation to a volume-law value.
In contrast, random Gaussian circuits exhibit diffusive growth, with non-local magic scaling as
\sqrt{t}
before saturating at the Haar-random value on a timescale
t\sim N^{2}
.

IV.2
Outlook

The analytical tractability established here opens several natural directions for future investigation.

Higher dimensional systems.

While our results are formulated for arbitrary bipartitions of one-dimensional chains, fermionic Gaussian states in higher dimensions
D>1
offer a rich arena where entanglement and magic may exhibit qualitatively different behavior. In two and three dimensions, a richer structure tied to the geometry of the Fermi surface is expected, and the entanglement entropy obeys an area law with multiplicative logarithmic corrections
\mathcal{O}(\ell^{D-1}\ln\ell)

92
.
Investigating how the scaling rate
\beta_{\mathrm{FNL}}
emerges in such systems is a natural generalization.

Conformal field theory and integrable systems.

A key question is to determine whether
\beta_{\mathrm{FNL}}
captures universal features of the underlying conformal field theory, much as the central charge governs the scaling of entanglement entropy
17
.
Responding to this question requires extending our analysis to interacting integrable Hamiltonians, where Bethe-ansatz methods provide access to the entanglement spectrum.
On the dynamical side, it remains an open question whether the quasiparticle picture that successfully describes the spreading of entanglement after a quantum quench
5
;
6
;
57
can be adapted to account for the growth of non-local magic in these models.

Disordered systems.

Our formula provides a natural framework to study non-local magic in the presence of quenched disorder.
For example, in Anderson localization models
31
;
83
, we expect deeply localized eigenstates to have a trivial structure, for which the fermionic non-local magic density
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(x)
vanishes.
Similarly, free fermionic models of strongly disordered systems
49
;
70
;
79
, where rare Griffiths regions and anomalous entanglement scaling emerge, are another avenue of further study.

Topological systems.

One and two-dimensional topological phases, such as the Kitaev chain and model
54
;
55
, host boundary modes whose non-local magic content is expected to reflect the topological invariant.
In such systems, the interplay of non-local magic and quantum coherent errors in fermionic systems is another fascinating direction to pursue
101
;
11
.

Interplay with symmetry.

When the system possesses a continuous symmetry, such as
\mathcal{U}(1)
charge conservation, the covariance matrix acquires a block structure that could enable a symmetry-resolved decomposition of
M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}
into contributions from different charge sectors
36
.
This connects to the broader program of symmetry-resolved entanglement
13
, coherence
2
and stabilizer entropies, and may reveal sector-dependent magic resources
48
;
22
;
30
.
An intriguing related question is whether fermionic non-local magic exhibits quantum Mpemba-like effects
9
;
8
;
1
, where symmetry-broken initial conditions lead to anomalously fast relaxation of specific magic sectors.

Monitored systems and non-Hermitian dynamics.

Hybrid systems combining unitary evolution with local measurements give rise to measurement-induced phase transitions in entanglement
34
;
63
;
74
.
For free-fermionic setups, the dynamics remain within the Gaussian manifold, and our formula applies at each quantum trajectory.
Whether fermionic non-local magic exhibits its own measurement-induced transition, and how it relates to the entanglement transition, is an open question.
Similarly, non-Hermitian free fermionic Hamiltonians
10
;
53
generate effective Gaussian dynamics with complex spectra.
Our framework makes amenable to analytic treatment the behavior of fermionic non-local magic in these settings
98
;
35
;
38
;
60
.

A.1
Discussion of the main result

We now justify Eq. (
6
).
First, we note that minimizing over
M_{\alpha}(|\Psi\rangle)=(1-\alpha)^{-1}\log_{2}\zeta_{\alpha}(|\Psi\rangle)
is equivalent to maximizing over
\zeta_{\alpha}
. We will focus on the latter problem.
We consider a generic bipartition
A=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}\}\subset\Lambda
containing
\ell\leq N/2
qubits and
B=\Lambda\setminus A
the remaining
N-\ell
ones, with Hilbert space dimension respectively
d_{A}=2^{\ell}
and
d_{B}=2^{N-\ell}
.
We will show that the BCS-like canonical representative is a strict local maximizer of
\zeta_{2}
, hence a strict local minimizer of
M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}
, for arbitrary
\ell
, and that for
\ell\leq 3
the same argument can be sharpened to a global statement. For larger subsystems, the same conclusion is strongly supported by numerics and is fully consistent with Eq. (
6
).

The convenient starting point is to reorganize the sum over Pauli strings that defines
\zeta_{2}
into a sum over Majorana correlators classified by how many Majorana operators act on each side of the bipartition.
For every ordered subset
I=\{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\}
of Majorana indices in
subsystem
X\in\{A,B\}
we denote the corresponding Majorana monomial

M_{I}^{X}=\gamma^{X}_{i_{1}}\gamma^{X}_{i_{2}}\cdots\gamma^{X}_{i_{k}}
and introduce the

bi-graded Majorana correlator tensor
entries

T_{I_{A},I_{B}}:=\langle\Psi|M^{A}_{I_{A}}M^{B}_{I_{B}}|\Psi\rangle.

(38)

The index
(I_{A},I_{B})
runs over all pairs of ordered subsets; the two
integers
(r,s)=(|I_{A}|,|I_{B}|)
are the bi-degrees.
Intuitively,
T_{I_{A},I_{B}}
packs the expectation values of all Majorana
monomials with
r
operators on
A
and
s
on
B
into a single
rectangular matrix of size
\binom{2\ell}{r}\!\times\!\binom{2(N-\ell)}{s}
. A direct computation in the Jordan–Wigner representation gives

\zeta_{2}(|\Psi\rangle)=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{I_{A},I_{B}}|T_{I_{A},I_{B}}|^{4}.

(39)

For each pair of integers
(r,s)
with
0\leq r,s\leq 2\ell
, we define the
bi-graded block

T^{(r,s)}:=(T_{I_{A},I_{B}})_{|I_{A}|=r,|I_{B}|=s}
, such that

\zeta_{2}(|\Psi\rangle)=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{r,s}\|T^{(r,s)}\|_{4}^{4},\qquad\|X\|_{p}^{p}:=\sum_{i,j}|X_{ij}|^{p}.

(40)

The key property of the bi-graded encoding is that local fermionic
Gaussian unitaries act
independently on each block
, by means of a real orthogonal matrix built from minors of
O_{A}
or
O_{B}
.
Concretely, if
U_{A}^{G}
sends Majorana operators to their rotated
versions
U_{A}^{G}\gamma_{a}^{A}(U_{A}^{G})^{\dagger}=\sum_{b}(O_{A})_{ba}\gamma_{b}^{A}
, then
a product of
r
Majoranas transforms by the
r
-th exterior power

C_{r}(O_{A}):=\wedge^{r}O_{A}
, whose entries are the
r\times r
minors

[C_{r}(O_{A})]_{I,I^{\prime}}=\det\!\bigl((O_{A})_{I,I^{\prime}}\bigr)
.
Because
O_{A}
is orthogonal, so is
C_{r}(O_{A})
.
The consequence is that each bi-graded block transforms
only among entries of the same bi-degree:

T^{(r,s)}\;\longmapsto\;C_{r}(O_{A})\,T^{(r,s)}\,C_{s}(O_{B})^{\mathsf{T}}.

(41)

The maximization of
\zeta_{2}
under local FGU is thus equivalent to
maximizing
\zeta_{2}=2^{-N}\sum_{r,s}\|T^{(r,s)}\|_{4}^{4}

on the level set
\{I_{r,s}=\text{const}\}
.

We now evaluate these blocks on the Botero canonical form,

|\Psi_{\mathrm{can}}\rangle=\bigotimes_{m=1}^{\ell}\bigl(\cos\theta_{m}|00\rangle_{m}+\sin\theta_{m}|11\rangle_{m}\bigr)\otimes|0\rangle^{\otimes(N-2\ell)}.

(42)

Because the state factorizes pair by pair, the correlators inherit a
simple structure: each canonical pair contributes independently, and
many entries vanish identically by parity. More precisely, a correlator
can be nonzero only if, for every pair
m
, the numbers of Majorana
operators acting on the two sides have the same parity. This makes the
tensor
T
extremely sparse at the canonical point.
This sparsity is the basic reason why the canonical form is a natural
candidate for the minimum of
M_{2}
under local Gaussian unitaries.
Indeed, an infinitesimal local Gaussian rotation mixes entries of

T^{(r,s)}
inside the same block through an orthogonal transformation.
Around the canonical point, because of the parity selection rule, such a
mixing typically takes a single nonzero entry and spreads it over two
entries.

We can make this more precise in the following way.
Any subset
I_{X}\subseteq[2\ell]
can be split into its per-pair
restrictions
I_{X,m}:=I_{X}\cap\{2m-1,2m\}\subseteq\{2m-1,2m\}
; thus each

I_{X,m}\in\{\emptyset,\{2m-1\},\{2m\},\{2m-1,2m\}\}
has cardinality

0,1,1,
or
2
respectively, and the profile

(|I_{X,m}|)_{m=1}^{\ell}\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}
records how many Majoranas
of the monomial
M^{X}_{I_{X}}
act on each canonical pair. The tensor product of
\ket{0}
does not impact the magic computation so we neglect it in the following.
The canonical state is a tensor product
|\Psi_{\mathrm{can}}\rangle=\bigotimes_{m}|\psi_{m}\rangle
with

|\psi_{m}\rangle=\cos\theta_{m}|00\rangle_{m}+\sin\theta_{m}|11\rangle_{m}
.
We can write each ordered subset as a disjoint union of its per-pair
restrictions,
I_{X}=\bigsqcup_{m}I_{X,m}
with

I_{X,m}\subseteq\{2m-1,2m\}
.
For each pair
m
the operator
M^{A}_{I_{A,m}}M^{B}_{I_{B,m}}

acts on
|\psi_{m}\rangle
and commutes with operators supported on
different pairs (up to a global sign). Therefore

|\langle\Psi_{\mathrm{can}}|M^{A}_{I_{A}}M^{B}_{I_{B}}|\Psi_{\mathrm{can}}\rangle|=\prod_{m=1}^{\ell}|\langle\psi_{m}|M^{A}_{I_{A,m}}M^{B}_{I_{B,m}}|\psi_{m}\rangle|,

(43)

and the right-hand side equals
\prod_{m}K_{m}(I_{A,m},I_{B,m})
with

the single-pair correlator. The single-pair structure

can be read off directly from the two-qubit Jordan–Wigner realization

\begin{split}\gamma^{A}_{1}&=\sigma_{x}\otimes\mathbbm{1},\qquad\gamma^{A}_{2}=\sigma_{y}\otimes\mathbbm{1},\\
\gamma^{B}_{1}&=\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{x},\qquad\gamma^{B}_{2}=\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{y}.\end{split}

(44)

Introducing

p_{m}:=\sin 2\theta_{m}=\sqrt{1-\lambda_{m}^{2}},\qquad z_{m}:=\cos 2\theta_{m}=\lambda_{m},

(45)

where
\lambda_{m}
are the positive eigenvalues of
i\Gamma_{A}
, the nonvanishing single-pair correlators are

\displaystyle K_{m}(\emptyset,\emptyset)

\displaystyle=1,\qquad K_{m}(\{1,2\},\{1,2\})=-1,

(46)

\displaystyle K_{m}(\emptyset,\{1,2\})

\displaystyle=iz_{m},\qquad K_{m}(\{1,2\},\emptyset)=iz_{m},

(47)

\displaystyle K_{m}(1,1)

\displaystyle=ip_{m}\,J_{2},\qquad J_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},

(48)

and all parity-mismatched entries vanish.

To show that
|\Psi_{\mathrm{can}}\rangle

\zeta_{2}

f(O_{A},O_{B}):=\zeta_{2}\!\left((U^{G}_{O_{A}}\otimes U^{G}_{O_{B}})|\Psi_{\mathrm{can}}\rangle\right),

(49)

with
O_{A},O_{B}\in O(2\ell)

f
along every
generator of
\mathfrak{so}(2\ell)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(2\ell)

(\mathbb{I},\mathbb{I})

By symmetry we restrict to
A

E_{ab}^{A}
(the
B

O_{A}(\epsilon)=e^{\epsilon E_{ab}^{A}}
.

When
a
and
b

C_{r}(e^{\epsilon E_{ab}^{A}})
acts on each basis vector

e_{I}
of
\wedge^{r}\mathbb{R}^{2\ell}

The two indices

At most one of

Writing

-|T|^{4}\cdot\sin 2\epsilon\cos 2\epsilon|_{\epsilon=0}=0

Summing over all row-orbits and over

When

where the inner sum runs over all Givens row-orbits

For a mixed perturbation with

Hence, the full Hessian of

\zeta_{2}

The same mechanism can be pushed further in small subsystems.
For
\ell\leq 3
, the bi-graded blocks are simple enough that the above
quartic contraction can be checked exhaustively in all cases, which
yields a genuine global statement.
First of all, one can see that the cross-pair discussion for infinitesimal rotation can be directly extended to finite rotation or product of rotations over disjoint pairs. Secondly, given the structure of the
K_{m}
matrices, many bi-graded blocks of the canonical tensor have 1-sparse columns: each column carries all of its contribution on a single entry. Indeed, for a
1

and any orthogonal

Applying this column by column to the
1
-sparse structure of

and using orthogonality of

on the

The only blocks not covered by the previous cases are those
for which a column contains more than one admissible entry. For

these are precisely the degree-

and,

Each column of

coordinates. At the canonical point this matrix is block-diagonal

\sum_{i,j}|M_{ij}|^{4}\leq\sum_{k}\sigma_{k}(M)^{4}

\sigma_{k}(M)
are the singular values.
Under the

are preserved, hence the

norm of each column cannot increase.
By Hodge duality

for the degree-
(2\ell-2)

\ell=3

and in each summand the canonical columns reduce to a

Therefore, for
\ell\leq 3
the canonical form gives the global minimum
of the fermionic non-local magic over local Gaussian unitaries. For larger subsystems,

supported by numerics, and all the evidence we have is fully

\ell

and

Concretely, at the canonical point, the stabilizer purity factorizes over the

,

(51)

and therefore

This is precisely Eq. (

We discuss here the numerical methods behind the results in Fig.

We adopt a standard variational optimization approach: given a Gaussian state
|\Psi\rangle
on
N

N

\mathcal{C}(U_{A},U_{B}):=M_{2}\left[U_{A}\otimes U_{B}|\Psi\rangle\right]

(53)

with
U_{A},U_{B}
unitary operators acting on regions
A=\{1,\ldots,{N/2}\}
and
B=\{{N/2+1},\ldots,N\}
respectively. The state is represented by its
2^{N}
complex coefficients on the computational basis, and the unitaries as
d_{I}\times d_{I}
complex matrices, with
d_{I}=2^{|I|},\ I\in\{A,B\}
. The unitaries are then parametrized by a set of real numbers (or angles)
\vec{\theta}
, depending on their constraints. For generic unitaries, we use the exponential map
U(\vec{\theta})=\exp(-i\vec{\theta} \cdot\vec{\tau})

\vec{\tau}
the generators of the
\mathfrak{u}(d_{I})
algebra. For fermionic Gaussian unitaries, we write them as
U(\vec{\theta})=\exp(H(\vec{\theta}))

1

are given by the angles
\vec{\theta}
. With this parametrization, the cost function becomes a function of the angles
\mathcal{C}(\vec{\theta})
, and its gradient at a given point

in an arbitrary direction
\vec{e_{j}}=(0,\ldots,1_{j},\ldots,0)
can be approximated via the finite-difference method:

\partial_{\theta_{j}}\mathcal{C}(\vec{\theta})\approx\frac{C(\vec{\theta}+\epsilon\vec{e_{j}})-C(\vec{\theta})}{\epsilon},

(54)

where we set
\epsilon
to be of order
10^{-8}
.

The cost function and its gradient are then fed into a gradient-based optimization algorithm such as L-BFGS, which updates the parameters until a minimum of the cost function is reached. To help with convergence to a global minimum, the whole optimization routine is repeated multiple times, with each initial angle
\theta_{0}
chosen uniformly at random in
[0,2\pi)
.

, and can also be parallelized for substantial speedup. Finally, the finite-difference method can be replaced with backpropagation, which allows the full
N_{p}
-dimensional gradient to be computed at a cost comparable to a small constant number of evaluations of the cost function, rather than
O(N_{p})
. Note that the number of parameters
N_{p}
grows exponentially in
N
in the case of generic unitaries, and quadratically in
N
for fermionic Gaussian ones.

A.3
Error analysis for experimental accessibility

We provide the error propagation analysis connecting the accuracy on
\Gamma_{A}
to the accuracy on
M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}
.
Since
\mathfrak{m}_{2}(\lambda^{2})=-\log_{2}(1-\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{4})
is smooth on
[0,1]
, its derivative

\mathfrak{m}_{2}^{\prime}(\lambda^{2})=\frac{2\lambda(1-2\lambda^{2})}{(1-\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{4})\ln 2}

(55)

is bounded, so
\mathfrak{m}_{2}
is uniformly Lipschitz on
[0,1]
with constant
L=\max_{\lambda\in[0,1]}|\mathfrak{m}_{2}^{\prime}(\lambda^{2})|=O(1)
.
Combining with the Hoffman–Wielandt stability bound for eigenvalues of
i\Gamma_{A}
,

|\delta M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}|\lesssim L\,\sqrt{\ell}\,\|\delta\Gamma_{A}\|_{F}\;.

(56)

A uniform entrywise error
\epsilon_{\Gamma}
implies
\|\delta\Gamma_{A}\|_{F}=O(\ell\epsilon_{\Gamma})
, giving
|\delta M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}|=\mathcal{O}(\ell^{3/2}\epsilon_{\Gamma})
.
To estimate the non-local magic density
m_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}=M_{2}^{\mathrm{FNL}}/\ell
with additive accuracy
\varepsilon
, one needs
\epsilon_{\Gamma}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon/\sqrt{\ell})
, yielding

N_{\mathrm{shots}}=O\!\left(\frac{\ell^{3}\log(\ell/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

(57)

This estimate is pessimistic for gapped phases, where
\Gamma_{A}
is approximately banded and only
\mathcal{O}(1)
entanglement modes near the boundary contribute appreciably; near criticality the bound is closer to the true cost.

A.4
\alpha
-Generalization of Results

In the Main Text, several results were discussed exclusively for the Rényi index
\alpha=2
. For completeness, we now present their extensions to arbitrary integer
\alpha\geq 2
.

We begin with the Page curve. From Eq. (
16
), replacing
\mathfrak{m}_{2}
with
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}
, we find that the behavior for
r\simeq 0
remains the same for every
\alpha
up to a constant:

\overline{\mathsf{m}}_{\alpha}(r\simeq 0)=-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{r^{2}}{2\ln 2}+O(r^{3}).

(58)

Next, we consider the critical behavior in the Ising model. From Eq. (
28
), substituting
\mathfrak{m}_{2}\mapsto\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}
, a change of variables
\lambda=\frac{1-t}{1+t}
leads to

\beta_{\mathrm{FNL}}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}(1-\alpha)\ln 2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\hat{\mathfrak{m}}_{\alpha}(t)}{t},dt,

(59)

where

\hat{\mathfrak{m}}_{\alpha}(t)=\ln\left[\frac{(1+t)^{2\alpha}+(1-t)^{2\alpha}+4^{\alpha}t^{\alpha}}{2(1+t)^{2\alpha}}\right].

(60)

Noting that
\hat{\mathfrak{m}}_{\alpha}(t)=\hat{\mathfrak{m}}_{\alpha}(1/t)
, the integral can equivalently be written as

\beta_{\mathrm{FNL}}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}(1-\alpha)\ln 2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\hat{\mathfrak{m}}_{\alpha}(t)}{t},dt,

(61)

which corresponds to the Mellin transform
\mathcal{M}(s)
of
\hat{\mathfrak{m}}_{\alpha}(t)
evaluated as
s\to 0
.
The result of the integral is given by

\beta_{\mathrm{FNL}}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2(1-\alpha)\pi^{2}\ln(2)}\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{arccosh}^{2}(-r_{j})\;,

(62)

where
r_{j}
are the complex root of the polynomial
R_{\alpha}(x)=(x-2)^{\alpha}+(x+2)^{\alpha}+4^{\alpha}
.
