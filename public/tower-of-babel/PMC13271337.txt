Membrane vesicles associated antimicrobial peptides from PGPR Priestia megaterium VIT-2021 confer in silico efficacy against Pseudomonas syringae virulence protein
Authors: Huldah Pearlin Sarah Lazarus, Nalini Easwaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlants encounter multiple physiological and environmental constraints that hinder increased productivity. Among the protective factors, plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) plays a critical role in enhancing plant resilience through diverse mechanisms. Bacterial vesicles have been demonstrated to elicit a broad spectrum of immune responses, however, their application in plants has been relatively underexplored, and most research has focused predominantly on their immune modulatory properties. Vesicles derived from PGPR are particularly promising, as these vesicles carry immune stimulants and other bioactive molecules reflective of the parent bacterium. This research demonstrated that antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) present within the membrane vesicles (MV) of Priestia megaterium VIT-2021 contribute to resistance against the phytopathogen Pseudomonas syringae through an in silico approach. Molecular docking analysis revealed that these AMPs exhibit strong interactions with virulence-associated proteins of P. syringae, including avirulence protein (avrE), hrp outer protein (hopA1), hypersensitive response and pathogenicity protein (hrpS), ice nucleation protein (ina) and syringomycin. The highest binding affinities observed in the docking simulations were further validated through molecular dynamics simulations, which confirmed stable interactions between the AMPs and virulence-associated proteins. Building upon earlier findings demonstrating the inhibitory effect of P. megaterium VIT-2021 MVs against P. syringae, this in silico approach further establishes that AMPs derived from P. megaterium MVs possess significant antimicrobial potential against virulence proteins of P. syringae.

Introduction

Plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria elicit robust immunity and growth promotion in plants, which are necessary for maintaining plant health. 1 Priestia megaterium is one such PGPR, with potent growth-promotion and protection against environmental stressors. Studies have identified P. megaterium as a promising biological control agent because of its antimicrobial properties and ability to suppress various plant diseases. 2 P. megaterium is a Gram-positive rhizobacterium that exhibits various beneficial properties related to plant health protection, and research reports suggest that it can be utilized to enhance plant growth as well as to modulate host immunity. 3 For instance, P. megaterium UMCV1 strain enhanced plant growth through cytokinin-signaling pathway, whereas P. megaterium CDK25 revamped the quality of Capsicum annuum L. by enhancing proximate and nutrient constituents. 4 , 5 P. megaterium strain plays a vital role in phosphate solubilization and exhibits nematicidal activity. 6 P. megaterium RmBm31 demonstrated root hair and lateral root development through the production of volatile compounds, which were induced by auxin-signaling pathway. 7

Interestingly, membrane vesicles derived from PGPRs demonstrated similar potency towards plants by resisting pathogen infections and by modulating host immunity, leading to sustainable agriculture. 8-10 Membrane vesicles are nano-sized spherical vesicles ranging from 10–500 nm that naturally bud off from Gram-positive bacteria, packed with diverse proteins, lipids, genetic materials and various small molecules. 11 , 12

Research have shown that OMVs isolated from Xanthomonas campestris elicited an innate immune response in Arabidopsis thaliana. 8 Another study by McMillian et al., showed that P. fluorescens outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) protected plant health by modulating the A. thaliana immune system, similar to that of the parent bacterium. Further, the OMVs obtained from P. fluorescens inhibited various pathogens under in vivo conditions: pretreatment with P. fluorescens OMVs reduced the chlorosis caused by P. syringae in Arabidopsis and a reduced sporangial count of the oomycete Phytophthora infestans in tomato plants. 10 Our previous work illustrated the antimicrobial activity against P. syringae and the immunomodulatory properties of MVs derived from P. megaterium VIT-2021 in Oryza sativa. 9 Thus, a foundation was laid and anticipated to identify the key responsible agent of MVs for their potential role, which led to the identification of four novel antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) within isolated MVs. In recent decades, AMPs have gained significant attention as efficient alternatives to conventional antibiotics because of their broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against bacteria, fungi and viruses. 13 Furthermore, AMPs have also been explored for their potential therapeutic applications, especially in cancer treatment. 14 With the increase in the prevalence of antimicrobial resistance among microbial pathogens, AMPs play a vital role in combating resistant infections. Recent technological advances aim to enhance the stability and shelf-life of AMPs while minimizing their less toxicity. 15 Notably, AMPs naturally packed within MVs represent biologically safe and provide protection and stability. In summary, this study explores the AMPs derived from P. megaterium MVs in exerting antimicrobial activity against P. syringae using an in silico approach.

Methodology

Antimicrobial peptides identification from P. megaterium derived MVs, structure prediction, and validation

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) were predicted using the DBAASP and CAMP servers, and the common AMPs identified by both servers were compiled and chosen for further analysis. 16-18

The structures of four novel potential AMPs were predicted using the PEP-FOLD v3 server. To ensure the biological relevance and structural integrity of the predicted peptides, the positioning of amino acid residues within the allowed and disallowed regions was assessed through Ramachandran plot analysis using Discovery Studio software suite. 19 Jelleine-Ic was employed as a positive control, and its structure was similarly predicted and validated following the same protocol. 20

Structure prediction and validation of virulence associated proteins of P. syringae

P. syringae virulence associated protein sequences for avirulence protein (avrE), hrp outer protein (hopA1), hypersensitive response and pathogenicity protein (hrpS), ice nucleation protein (ina) and syringomycin proteins were obtained from NCBI in FASTA format. Subsequent 3D structure prediction of these proteins was performed using SWISS-MODEL server and evaluated via Ramachandran plot analysis. 17

Docking simulation of AMPs derived from P. megaterium VIT-2021 MVs against P. syringae virulence proteins

Molecular docking simulations between the AMPs and P. syringae's virulence proteins were conducted using HADDOCK webserver under optimized parameters. The resulting docked complexes were further validated through PRODIGY server to estimate binding affinities, and the interaction networks were visualized using LigPlot+. 17

Molecular dynamics study of AMPs derived from P. megaterium VIT-2021 MVs against P. syringae virulence proteins

Finally, molecular dynamics simulation of the docked complex was carried out through Gromacs software v2023.1 using CHARMM force field. Ligand topology and parameters were generated using the CHARMM-GUI Ligand Reader & Modeler. 21 Solvation of the complex was achieved with the TIP3 water model, and the system's charge was balanced with Na+ or Cl− ions through the Monte-Carlo ion replacement technique. Energy minimization involved the steepest descent approach, succeeded by a 1 ns equilibration phase at 310.15 K. The temperature and pressure were regulated utilizing velocity-rescale and Berendsen barostat algorithms, respectively, throughout the run. Subsequently, a 100 ns MD simulation was conducted in the NPT ensemble, maintaining a constant temperature of 310.15 K and pressure of 1 atm. The particle mesh Ewald method and the LINCS algorithm were employed to define the boundaries of the solvated system and to enforce geometric constraints on hydrogen-bonded covalent bonds. Upon simulation completion, water molecules, as well as translational and rotational movements, were eliminated to facilitate the analysis of internal interactions between the AMPs and proteins. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) and root mean square fluctuations (RMSF) were calculated to assess the conformational changes and the local flexibility or rigidity induced by the ligand, respectively. 22 The projection of the molecular dynamics trajectory onto the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) was performed using the gmx anaeig tool of GROMACS, considering Cα atoms of the system. A two-dimensional projection of the trajectory was generated using the −2d option, and the resulting principal component analysis (PCA) plot (PC1 vs PC2) was visualized using XMGRACE. 23 Furthermore, molecular mechanics Poisson‒Boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) analysis was carried out to calculate the binding free energy to reconfirm the stability of the complexes and explore residue-specific energetic contributions to AMP‒protein binding. 17

Results

The AMPs consistently identified by both databases were named P1, P2, P3, and P4, as summarized in Table 1. Current research focuses on investigating the interactions between the identified AMPs from MVs and key virulence proteins of P. syringae.

Further the docked complexes were subjected to molecular dynamics to validate the predicted strong interactions between the AMPs and virulence proteins. The AMPs from P. megaterium VIT-2021 MVs demonstrated superior binding affinities compared to the positive control, as confirmed by docking scores; the positive control was not advanced for subsequent dynamics simulations.

The structural stability and conformational changes in the AMP P1 and AvrE protein complex were evaluated using root mean square deviation (RMSD). The RMSD curve initially raised in the equilibrium phase, followed by fluctuations within 0.2 to 0.4 nm, indicating structural stability throughout the simulation (Figure 3A). Second, the variations in individual atoms and residues of the complex were studied using RMS fluctuations. The interaction between P1 and AvrE demonstrated fluctuations remained below 1 nm (Figure 3B). Third, radius of gyration analysis was used to evaluate the structural compactness of the complexes over 100 ns in the molecular dynamics simulations. For the P1-AvrE complex, Rg remained relatively stable at approximately between 3.44 and 3.5 nm, suggesting that the complex maintained a compact and conformationally stable structure throughout the simulation (Figure 3C). Finally, solvent accessible surface area (SASA) analysis supported these observations by providing insights into the extent of surface exposure to the solvent. For the P1-AvrE complex, the SASA profile remained relatively stable throughout the simulation, indicating minimal conformational changes and suggesting that the complex retained its structural integrity. A slight fluctuation between 580 and 610 nm2 was noted, likely attributable to transient solvent interactions (Figure 3D). Approximately four hydrogen bonds remained throughout the simulation (Figure 3E).

(C) The Rg reflected the high structural compactness. (D) SASA further confirmed the structural integrity by exhibiting minimal fluctuations representing reduced solvent exposure and finally (E) H-bonds reaffirmed the rigidity of the complex through the presence of consistent H-bonds throughout the simulation.

The interaction between P1 and HopA1 virulence protein was investigated, as P1 showed the highest binding affinity in the molecular docking analysis. Simulations revealed that the P1-HopA1 complex maintained a consistent RMSD, ranging between 1.0 and 1.5 nm, with minor deviations observed after 50 ns (Figure 4A). This was further supported by RMSF analysis, which showed a structurally stable complex exhibiting relatively low fluctuations across most residues (Figure 4B). Rg remained consistently stable across the entire 100 ns simulation, with minimal fluctuations between 2.3 and 2.6 nm, reflecting a uniformly compact and stable conformation (Figure 4C). Conversely, the complex exhibited an initial decrease in SASA during the equilibration phase, followed by minor fluctuations within the range of 210–240 nm2, indicative of dynamic solvent interactions and side chain rearrangements without significant loss of structural integrity (Figure 4D). Additionally, the complex was stabilized by an average of five hydrogen bonds, and intermittent disruption was observed between 60 and 70 ns, indicative of conformational rearrangements and stability (Figure 4E).

Following the MD simulation, principal component analysis was performed to investigate the structural motions and dynamic behaviors. The complexes AvrE-P1, HrpS-P1, Syr-P2, and Ina-P3 displayed dispersed distributions, reflecting the presence of stable conformational states and transitional intermediates (Figure 8A,C–E). While HopA1-P1 complex showed two distinct and significant conformational clusters, highlighting the enhanced structural stability throughout the simulations (Figure 8B).

PCA analysis of (A) AvrE-P1, (B) HopA1-P1 (C) HrpS-P1, (D) Syringomycin-P2, and (E) Ina-P3 complexes, which depicts the protein movements and structurally flexible and rigid areas.

Based on the molecular dynamics simulation trajectories, the MM/PBSA binding free energy was calculated to investigate the energetic stability of the complexes. Overall, the complexes demonstrated stable association by exhibiting overall favorable total binding free energies throughout 100 ns. The most favorable binding energy was observed through electrostatic interactions, subsequently van der Waals interactions. Further, polar solvation energy components, including EPB and GSOLV, reflect their involvement in complexes stabilization. Despite the unfavorable contributions from GASS in the Ina-P3 complex and ENPOLAR in almost all the complexes, the total binding free energy remained negative, reflecting thermodynamically favorable interactions between the AMP and protein complexes (Figure 9).

Residues contributing to AMP stability were identified using Per-residue decomposition analysis. For instance, in the AvrE-P1 complex, PRO1065, GLU1063, and ARG989 demonstrated the strongest favorable energy contributions (Figure 10A), while for the HopA1-P1 and HrpS-P1 complexes, the residues LYS181, LEU182, and LEU305 (Figure 10B); PRO177 and PRO17 (Figure 10C) were involved in the most favorable energy contributions respectively. Residues PHE138, TRP230, and ASN166 showed strongest favorable energy contribution within the Syringomycin-P2 complex (Figure 10D), while Ina-P3 complex demonstrated ASP401 and GLU383 strongest favorable energy contributions (Figure 10E). Collectively, these favorable energy contribution suggesting that these residues would be the major hotspots leading to overall peptide‒protein recognition and stabilization. Furthermore, few of the residues exhibited comparatively positive energy contributions, reflecting unfavorable interactions within the complexes.

Discussion

Our previous work focused on identifying the active agent responsible for the inhibitory effect of MVs derived from P. megaterium VIT-2021 against P. syringae. 9 Total 128 peptides were obtained, among which four were consistently predicted to have antimicrobial activity by both the DBAASP and CAMP servers. 9 , 17 Current study builds upon these findings and focuses on the in silico-based validation of the identified AMPs with their potential interactions with key virulence proteins of P. syringae. Further, this study aims to explore the potential future application of these AMPs in enhancing plant resistance against bacterial infections.

Initially, the structures of all four novel AMPs were predicted using PEP-FOLD server and evaluated via Ramachandran plot analysis. 17 The key virulence proteins selected for this study included avirulence protein E (AvrE), a type III effector protein known to induce water-soaked lesions in leaves and promote bacterial progression, ultimately resulting in the suppression of plant immunity. 24 Ice nucleation protein, an outer membrane protein that facilitates frost injury in plant tissues, resulting in the release of nutrients for bacterial proliferation. 25 , 26 In addition, the hypersensitive response and pathogenicity protein S (HrpS), a transcriptional regulator that activates and controls the T3SS. Hrp outer protein A (HopA) is another type III effector protein. 9 , 27 Lastly, syringomycin, a phytotoxin, was also considered owing to its ability to form pores in plant membranes, causing ion leakage and resulting in necrosis. 28 , 29 These major virulence proteins were selected for analysis and modelled using SWISS-MODEL and subsequently validated through Ramachandran plot.

The Ramachandran plot indicated that all residues were located in the allowed regions, confirming the stereochemical reliability of the generated models and enable subsequent docking and dynamic analysis. 22 The interactions between P. syringae virulence proteins and AMPs derived from P. megaterium MVs were assessed using HADDOCK through blind docking. This method allows the AMP molecules to explore the entire protein surface freely and identify the most energetically favorable binding pockets, facilitating an unbiased prediction of interaction hotspots. 30 The resulting complexes were further examined with PRODIGY to estimate binding affinities, and their interaction networks were visualized using LigPlot+. 17

Among the evaluated AMPs, P1 exhibited the strongest interactions with key virulence proteins, including AvrE (−9.0), HopA1 (−7.6), and HrpS (−5.9), often surpassing the binding affinities of PC, which shown −7.7 and −5.2 for AvrE and HrpS, respectively. The only exception was HopA1, for which the PC demonstrated a slightly higher affinity −8.0 than AMPs. Additionally, P2 and P3 displayed enhanced specificity toward syringomycin (−7.9) and Ina (−8.7), respectively. Across most analyses, the AMPs exhibited stronger interaction when compared to the employed positive control, indicating their potency in counteracting pathogen ingress. These interaction patterns were consistent with the physicochemical properties predicted by the DBAASP and CAMP servers, supporting the structural and functional attributes of the peptides. 9 , 31 Nevertheless, comprehensive experimental validation is required to confirm their antimicrobial efficacy and mechanism of action. These observations were further supported by molecular dynamics simulation analyses, which demonstrated potential and specific interactions between these AMPs derived from MVs and key virulence proteins of P. syringae. Notably, P1 exhibited significant interaction stability in the complexes AvrE, HopA1, and HrpS. In contrast, the AMP P2 showed a comparatively stronger interaction with syringomycin, whereas P3 displayed preferential binding affinity toward the Ina virulence protein. Among all, the most stable interactions were observed for P1-AvrE and P2-syringomycin complexes.

Furthermore, PCA was carried out on the MD simulation trajectories to study protein movements and dynamics, which provided insights into areas of structural flexibility and rigidity. 23 In the AvrE-P1 complex, the presence of multiple distinct clusters suggests several stable conformational states, where densely populated areas correspond to energetically favorable and frequently sampled states. While scattered points represent less stable and transient configurations for predicting flexibility with multiple energy minima and conformational transitions. 32 In the case of the HopA1-P1 complex, the PCA plot demonstrated two distinct clusters located in the left and right regions, representing major structural conformation states with intermediate and scatter points. This reflects internal flexibility and transitional conformations, highlighting notable conformational heterogeneity. Similarly, the HrpS-P1 and Ina-P3 complexes demonstrated dispersed distributions, representing predominantly transient and flexible conformational behavior. In contrast, Syringomycin-P2 revealed distinct clustering, indicating relatively higher conformational stability throughout the MD simulation.

Through MM/PBSA, strong negative electrostatic interactions followed by van der Waals interactions were observed to predominantly contributing toward complex stability and to maintaining AMP binding within the proteins interface. Additionally, the presence of stabilizing residues indicates the formation of a stable binding environment throughout the molecular dynamic simulations. Collectively, despite solvent rearrangements and unfavorable energy contributions during complex formation, the overall negative total binding free energy confirmed the thermodynamic stability of the complexes throughout the 100 ns dynamic simulations. 17

Building upon earlier findings, AMPs derived from P. megaterium MVs plays a significant role in conferring resistance against P. syringae infection, in silico. The interactions between these AMPs and virulence-associated proteins demonstrated highly stable binding throughout 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations. This study provides a foundation demonstrating that MVs associated AMPs strongly interact with key virulence proteins. However, these findings require further validation through in vitro and in planta trials. The next phase of this research will focus on investigating the mode of action by which these AMPs inhibit the virulence mechanisms of P. syringae.

Conclusion

Collectively, these in silico findings suggest that MVs associated with AMPs derived from P. megaterium may contribute to bio control activity against P. syringae under in planta conditions. Nonetheless, based on the collective physicochemical, functional and interaction properties, AMPs from MVs represent promising candidates for further evaluation of plant growth promotion and immunomodulation potential. Further, our results support the hypothesis that AMPs constitute a major determinant of the antimicrobial efficacy of MVs, which is consistent with our previous reports. While AMPs have been extensively investigated for their antimicrobial properties across diverse biological systems, their application in plant systems highlights a promising avenue toward sustainable agriculture.
