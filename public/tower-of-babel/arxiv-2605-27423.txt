Quantum Speed Limit under Calibration Uncertainty
Salman Sajad Wani, Saif Al-Kuwari

Abstract
Standard quantum speed limits presuppose exactly known parameters, overestimating operational speed under calibration uncertainty. We introduce a projected speed limit based on the quantum Fisher information that profiles out these nuisance parameters on a quotient manifold. We derive constructive bounds for general Markovian evolution using sensitivity equations. Applying this to Jaynes–Cummings sensors, we obtain explicit detuning tolerances and quantify speed limits arising from field-dependent Purcell loss. This framework turns geometric bounds into concrete design rules for calibration and interrogation time.



I
Introduction

Quantum speed limit (QSL) quantify the minimal time for a quantum state to evolve into a distinguishable state. The earliest bounds, due to Mandelstam–Tamm and Margolus–Levitin, relate the orthogonalization time to the energy variance and, respectively, to the mean energy above the ground state
28
;
2
;
29
. Geometric treatments recast these results in the Bures/Uhlmann geometry and use the symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD) quantum Fisher information (QFI) metric to fix the line element
8
;
45
;
6
;
34
. Subsequent work extended this framework to general open-system dynamics, yielding QSLs expressed directly in terms of the instantaneous statistical speed for completely positive trace-preserving evolutions
44
;
14
;
12
. Related developments also exploited the nonuniqueness of contractive distinguishability metrics to construct generalized geometric QSL families for unitary and nonunitary dynamics, including bounds that can be tighter than the conventional QFI/Bures choice
35
. For open dynamics, studies have emphasized complementary desiderata such as tightness, robustness under composition and mixing, and computational or experimental feasibility
9
. Related work also sharpened the operational interpretation of quantum speed limits, including in open-system settings
30
;
40
. More recent Liouville-space formulations have derived exact and inexact QSL families for completely positive trace-preserving dynamics and clarified attainability for time-optimal CPTP evolutions
42
.
These developments have deepened the link between QSLs, quantum estimation theory, and monotone information metrics
22
;
34
;
33
. They also underpin many analyses of quantum metrology, control, and dissipation
19
;
27
;
20
.

A common feature across the standard Bures/QFI setting, generalized geometric families, and more recent open-system and CPTP formulations is the assumption of a fixed, known generator
44
;
14
;
12
;
35
;
9
;
42
. Operationally, distinguishability along laboratory time is therefore confounded by nuisance directions in parameter space: an experimenter can partially compensate for changes in time by co-varying these parameters within their estimation uncertainty. Consequently, the physical Bures speed computed at a fixed generator generally overestimates the operational speed relevant for distinguishing states modulo nuisance reparametrizations.

In this paper we formulate a nuisance-aware projected quantum speed limit. We treat time and nuisance parameters jointly within the multiparameter SLD-QFI and project the Bures metric onto the time direction via a Schur complement
11
;
26
;
46
. This construction yields an effective information
F_{\mathrm{eff}}
and an associated projected speed
v_{\mathrm{quo}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{eff}}}
. This speed never exceeds the standard Bures speed and remains invariant under reparametrizations of the nuisance manifold. Crucially,
F_{\mathrm{eff}}
coincides with the partial SLD Fisher information, which sets the ultimate precision limit when nuisance parameters are profiled out
43
;
41
. In contrast to standard formulations
44
;
14
;
12
, which bound evolution for a frozen generator, our framework quantifies operational distinguishability modulo nuisances. We implement this for general Markovian dynamics using GKSL sensitivity equations
27
;
20
;
32
;
16
and analyze two paradigmatic sensors: a unitary Jaynes-Cummings (JC) model and a dispersive JC cavity with loss. For the examples studied here, the nuisance penalty is more pronounced in the dispersive open JC sensor, since the field controls both the coherent cavity-frequency shift and the effective decay rate. In both regimes, the projected QSL converts abstract geometric limits into concrete design rules. One example is the tolerance
|\Delta|t\lesssim 0.3
for retaining
99\%
of the physical speed.

II
Formalism

We work in the Bures–SLD geometry of quantum states. The
Bures metric, induced by the Uhlmann fidelity, provides the local statistical
distance on the space of density operators

8
;
45
;
6
;
34
. The corresponding
symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD) operators
L_{\mu}
are defined by

\partial_{\mu}\rho=\tfrac{1}{2}(\rho L_{\mu}+L_{\mu}\rho),

(1)

and the associated SLD quantum Fisher information matrix (QFIM)

F_{\mu\nu}
determines the local line element

dL^{2}=\tfrac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}\,dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}.

(2)

For any parameter direction, this quadratic form gives the local sensitivity of
the state to infinitesimal displacements; for time evolution, its square root is
the instantaneous statistical speed that underlies geometric quantum speed
limits
6
;
44
;
13
. We take the coordinate vector
x^{\mu}=(t,\bm{\lambda})
to include both laboratory time and the nuisance parameters
\bm{\lambda}
(e.g., detunings, loss rates). In our setting,
\bm{\lambda}
are not targets of joint estimation but sources of uncertainty. Throughout this work, the nuisance parameters
\bm{\lambda}
are treated as
run-wise fixed but imperfectly known calibration parameters that label the
generator of the dynamics. They are therefore distinct from explicitly
time-dependent stochastic fluctuations, which belong to a different modeling
layer and are not profiled by the present Schur-complement construction. In this multiparameter setting, the QFIM takes the block form

F=\begin{pmatrix}F_{tt}&\bm{f}^{\top}\\
\bm{f}&F_{\lambda\lambda}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\bm{f}:=(F_{t\alpha})_{\alpha},\;F_{\lambda\lambda}:=(F_{\alpha\beta})_{\alpha,\beta}.

(3)

Standard “physical” QSLs are obtained by fixing
\bm{\lambda}
and using
F_{tt}
alone. Operationally, however, an experimenter can re-tune
\bm{\lambda}
to reduce distinguishability along nuisance directions. Thus the relevant speed is set not by
F_{tt}
, but by the information component that is orthogonal to the nuisance manifold.

To isolate this component, we minimize
dL^{2}
over
d\bm{\lambda}
at fixed
dt
, profiling the Bures quadratic form in analogy with profile likelihood
11
;
26
;
1
. Solving the normal equations yields the Schur complement

F_{\mathrm{eff}}:=F_{tt}-\bm{f}^{\top}F_{\lambda\lambda}^{-1}\bm{f},

(4)

where
F_{\lambda\lambda}^{-1}
denotes the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse on the estimable nuisance subspace
3
;
46
.
Geometrically, this Schur complement is the squared norm of the time tangent
after orthogonal projection away from the nuisance directions in the Bures–SLD geometry. It therefore removes the distinguishability attributable to nuisance
reparametrizations. Equivalently, the profiled quadratic form induces the local
quotient metric obtained by identifying tangent variations that differ only by
nuisance components:

dL_{\mathrm{quo}}^{2}=1/4F_{\mathrm{eff}}\,dt^{2}.

F_{\mathrm{eff}}
satisfies
0\leq F_{\mathrm{eff}}\leq F_{tt}
and is invariant under smooth reparametrizations of
\bm{\lambda}
. When
F
is invertible,
F_{\mathrm{eff}}=((F^{-1})_{tt})^{-1}
, recovering the standard profiled information. Rank-deficient states (including pure states) are handled by restricting all constructions to
\operatorname{supp}(\rho)

22
;
37
.

In the SLD formalism,
F_{\mathrm{eff}}
coincides with the partial quantum Fisher information for time. Projecting
L_{t}
orthogonally to the span of nuisance scores
\{L_{\alpha}\}
via the inner product
\langle\!\langle A,B\rangle\!\rangle_{\rho}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\Tr[\rho\{A,B\}]
yields the efficient score
\widetilde{L}_{t}
, whose norm is

\langle\!\langle\widetilde{L}_{t},\widetilde{L}_{t}\rangle\!\rangle_{\rho}=F_{tt}-\bm{f}^{\top}F_{\lambda\lambda}^{-1}\bm{f}=F_{\mathrm{eff}}.

(5)

Thus, the projected metric coefficient sets the ultimate precision limit
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{t})\geq 1/(NF_{\mathrm{eff}})
attainable when nuisance parameters are profiled out
43
;
41
.

To embed this geometry into Markovian dynamics, we consider trajectories generated by the GKSL master equation

\partial_{t}\rho(t,\bm{\lambda})=\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}}[\rho]:=-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}[H(\bm{\lambda}),\rho]+\sum_{k}\mathcal{D}[F_{k}(\bm{\lambda})]\rho.

(6)

The time derivative entering the QFIM is
\partial_{t}\rho=\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}}[\rho]
, while the nuisance derivatives
\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}:=\partial_{\lambda^{\alpha}}\rho
satisfy the linear sensitivity equation

\partial_{t}\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)=(\partial_{\lambda^{\alpha}}\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}})[\rho(t)]+\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}}[\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)].

(7)

This equation allows
F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
to be evaluated efficiently along any trajectory using the Duhamel formula
32
;
16
.
At each fixed time, the profiling step remains explicit: one solves the normal
equations

F_{\lambda\lambda}\,d\bm{\lambda}_{*}=-\,\bm{f}\,dt

and substitutes the result into the quadratic form, recovering the same Schur
complement as above. The effective information defines the instantaneous projected speed

v_{\mathrm{quo}}(t):=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)}
.
Its time integral is the quotient length of the realized path, while the global
minimization is already contained in the quotient geodesic distance

L_{\mathrm{quo}}([\rho_{0}],[\rho_{\tau}])
.
Integrating this speed yields the projected quantum speed limit

\tau\;\geq\;\frac{L_{\mathrm{quo}}\big([\rho_{0}],[\rho_{\tau}]\big)}{\bar{v}_{\mathrm{quo}}},\qquad\bar{v}_{\mathrm{quo}}:=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)}\,dt,

(8)

where
L_{\mathrm{quo}}
is the geodesic distance between equivalence classes
[\rho]
on the quotient manifold. In the absence of nuisances (
F_{t\alpha}=0
), this reduces to standard QFI-based bounds
44
;
14
;
12
. For unitary dynamics, it yields a Mandelstam-Tamm–type bound governed by an effective variance
\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}

28
;
2
(see Appendix
Appendix A: Detailed formalism — Bures geometry, Schur complement, and GKSL dynamics
).
The projected QSL is saturated when the realized evolution,
viewed in the quotient geometry obtained after profiling out nuisance
directions, is a minimizing geodesic between the endpoint equivalence classes.
Equivalently,

L_{\rm quo}\big([\rho_{0}],[\rho_{\tau}]\big)=\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{\rm eff}(t)}\,dt.

(9)

When
F_{t\alpha}=0
, one has
F_{\rm eff}=F_{tt}
, so the projected-QSL
denominator coincides with the standard geometric denominator. If the realized
endpoint also attains the quotient optimization over the admissible nuisance
values, then the quotient endpoint distance coincides with the physical Bures
distance for that run, and the saturation question reduces to the usual one for
the underlying dynamics.

III
Unitary example: Jaynes–Cummings sensor.

We first illustrate the projected QSL in a setting where estimation and dynamics are statistically correlated: a Jaynes–Cummings (JC) qubit–cavity sensor for a magnetic field
B

25
;
18
;
10
. Within the rotating–wave approximation and restricted to the single–excitation manifold
\{\ket{e,0},\ket{g,1}\}
, the dynamics is generated by the traceless Hamiltonian

H^{\prime}(B)=g\,\tau_{x}+\frac{\Delta(B)}{2}\,\tau_{z},\qquad\Delta(B)=\omega_{q}+\gamma B-\omega_{c},

(10)

with associated Rabi frequency
\Omega=\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+4g^{2}}
. We initialize in
\ket{\psi_0}=\ket{e,0}
and treat the field
B
, through the detuning
\Delta(B)
, as a nuisance parameter.

For a pure state
\ket{\psi(x)}=U(x)\ket{\psi_0}
, the QFIM is given by the covariance of the local generators,
F_{\mu\nu}=4\,\mathrm{Cov}_{\psi_{0}}(G_{\mu},G_{\nu})

6
. Here the time generator is
G_{t}=H^{\prime}(B)
, while the field generator
G_{B}(t)
is dynamical because
[H^{\prime},\partial_{B}H^{\prime}]\neq 0
. An explicit evaluation of the Heisenberg equations (see Appendix
Appendix B: Closed Jaynes–Cummings sensor — unitary projected QSL
) yields

\displaystyle F_{tt}

\displaystyle=4g^{2},

(11a)

\displaystyle F_{tB}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{4g^{2}\gamma\,\Delta}{\Omega^{2}}\left(t-\frac{\sin\Omega t}{\Omega}\right),

(11b)

so that the diagonal element
F_{tt}
reproduces the standard Mandelstam–Tamm speed
v_{\rm phys}=2g
, while the nonzero off–diagonal term
F_{tB}
captures the correlation between time evolution and field uncertainty.

Eliminating the field uncertainty via the Schur complement
F_{\mathrm{eff}}=F_{tt}-F_{tB}^{2}/F_{BB}
yields the effective information

F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)=4g^{2}\,\frac{4\sin^{4}\!\bigl(\tfrac{\Omega t}{2}\bigr)}{\displaystyle\Delta^{2}\!\left(t-\tfrac{\sin\Omega t}{\Omega}\right)^{\!2}+4\sin^{4}\!\bigl(\tfrac{\Omega t}{2}\bigr)}.

(12)

Two regimes are particularly instructive. (i) At exact resonance (
\Delta=0
), the correlation
F_{tB}
vanishes and the projected speed coincides with the physical speed. (ii) For small detunings and short times (
|\Omega t|\ll 1
), a Taylor expansion gives a quadratic penalty:

\frac{F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)}{F_{tt}}\approx\frac{1}{1+(\Delta t/3)^{2}}\simeq 1-\frac{(\Delta t)^{2}}{9}.

(13)

Thus, even a modest residual detuning reduces the operational speed
v_{\rm quo}
below the naive Mandelstam–Tamm value.

This reduction leads directly to quantitative design rules. If we require the projected speed to retain a fraction
R
of the physical speed, we obtain the tolerance

|\Delta|\,t<3\sqrt{\frac{1}{R}-1}.

(14)

For example, keeping
R=0.99
requires
|\Delta|t<0.30
. This inequality shows how tightly the JC sensor must be kept near resonance for the operational QSL to remain close to the QSL computed at fixed generator. Profiling nuisance parameters therefore turns the geometric construction into concrete constraints on calibration effort and on the admissible
(\Delta,\tau)
working window (see Fig.
1
).

IV
Open example: Dispersive JC sensor with loss.

We next consider a realistic sensor with noisy, parameter-dependent dynamics: a Jaynes–Cummings cavity coupled to a two-level atom, operated in the dispersive regime (
\Delta\gg g
)
25
;
5
. Here the dissipation is modeled by a Markovian master equation for fixed
B
,
while the uncertainty in
B
itself is treated as static calibration uncertainty
during a given run.
The atom acts as a probe for the field
B
, shifting the cavity frequency via the AC Stark effect and at the same time introducing dissipation.

By applying a Schrieffer–Wolff transformation
5
;
39
and tracing out the fast-relaxing atom (see Appendix
Appendix C: Open Jaynes–Cummings sensor — dispersive lossy case
), we obtain an effective master equation for the cavity mode
\rho_{\rm cav}
:

\dot{\rho}_{\rm cav}=-i\big[\omega_{c}^{\prime}(B)\,\tilde{a}^{\dagger}\tilde{a},\rho_{\rm cav}\big]+\kappa_{\rm eff}(B)\,\mathcal{D}[\tilde{a}]\,\rho_{\rm cav}.

(15)

In the dispersive regime, the Schrieffer–Wolff transformation removes the
off-resonant atom–cavity exchange term order by order in
g/\Delta(B)
and
leaves a dressed cavity mode whose resonance is shifted by virtual atomic
excitations. The same dressing admixes a small atomic component into the
effective cavity excitation, so atomic relaxation is inherited by the cavity as
an additional decay channel, yielding the field-dependent Purcell contribution
to
\kappa_{\rm eff}(B)

36
. Hence the same parameter
B

controls both the coherent signal, carried by the field-pulled frequency

\omega_{c}^{\prime}(B)
, and the dissipative envelope, through the effective decay rate

\kappa_{\rm eff}(B)=\kappa+\delta\kappa(B)
. This joint dependence is the
physical origin of the trade-off in Fig.
2
: increasing the
dispersive response simultaneously strengthens the loss channel, which makes the
passage of time more easily mimicked by a change in the field-dependent decay
rate and therefore increases the nuisance penalty in the projected speed.

For an initial superposition state, the evolved density matrix takes the Bloch form
\rho_{\rm cav}(t)=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbb{I}+\vec{S}\cdot\bm{\sigma})
.
The Bloch components then evolve as

\displaystyle S_{x}

\displaystyle=\sin(2\theta)\cos[\omega_{c}^{\prime}(B)t]\,e^{-\kappa_{\rm eff}(B)t/2},

(16)

\displaystyle S_{y}

\displaystyle=-\sin(2\theta)\sin[\omega_{c}^{\prime}(B)t]\,e^{-\kappa_{\rm eff}(B)t/2},

\displaystyle S_{z}

\displaystyle=2\sin^{2}\theta\,e^{-\kappa_{\rm eff}(B)t}-1.

Accordingly, the parameter
B
enters both the oscillation frequency and the exponential decay envelope.
The effective information

F_{\rm eff}(t)=F_{tt}-F_{tB}^{2}/F_{BB}

quantifies the balance between coherent phase accumulation and irreversible amplitude decay.

v_{\mathrm{quo}}

.

These effects are summarized in Fig.

.
The physical speed
v_{\rm phys}
simply tracks the continuous decay of the state, whereas the projected speed
v_{\rm quo}
falls off rapidly and reveals a growing nuisance penalty.
This divergence arises because photon loss is statistically confounded with uncertainty in
B
: an experimenter cannot tell whether a weaker signal is caused by the passage of time or by a change in the Purcell rate.

To bound the evolution, we compute the quotient fidelity by maximizing over the calibration window
\mathcal{B}
:

\Theta_{\rm quo}(\tau):=\arccos\!\Big(\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\sqrt{F(\rho_{0},\rho_{\rm cav}(\tau;B))}\Big).

(17)

Combining this with the averaged projected speed gives the open-system bound
\tau\geq\Theta_{\rm quo}/\bar{v}_{\rm quo}
.
This result identifies a finite optimal interrogation time, before the nuisance penalty dominates.
In this way, the geometric limit becomes a concrete design rule for dispersive readout
5
;
17
.

V
Conclusion

We introduced a nuisance-aware quantum speed limit on the quotient manifold of physical states, which identifies trajectories that differ only by unobservable reparametrizations of the generator.
Projecting the multiparameter Bures metric via the Schur complement
11
;
26
yields an operational speed
v_{\mathrm{quo}}
, which provides a lower bound on distinguishability in the presence of calibration uncertainty.
Unlike standard QSLs that assume a fixed generator
44
;
14
, this framework captures the statistical confounding between time evolution and nuisance parameters.

By embedding Markovian dynamics into this geometry, we turn abstract information-geometric limits into concrete design rules.
As demonstrated for the unitary and dissipative Jaynes–Cummings sensors, the projected QSL sets explicit experimental tolerances—such as the detuning limit
|\Delta|t\lesssim 0.3
and the Purcell-penalty cutoff—needed to approach fundamental physical bounds.
This shift from kinematic constraints to operational benchmarks enables nuisance-profiled metrology in complex architectures and ties idealized geometric theorems to the noisy, uncertain behavior of real quantum hardware.

Appendix A: Detailed formalism — Bures geometry, Schur complement, and GKSL dynamics

In this section we derive, in full detail, the geometric quantities and constructions used in the main text:
the Bures metric and SLD quantum Fisher information matrix (QFIM), the Schur-complement projection that
profiles out nuisance parameters, and the embedding of Markovian dynamics via GKSL sensitivity equations.
This provides the rigorous backbone for the projected (quotient) quantum speed limit used in Sec. II of
the main text.

Throughout we work on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
Parameters are real and collected as

x^{\mu}=(t,\lambda^{1},\dots,\lambda^{m-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

(A1)

with
C^{1}
dependence of the state family
\rho(x)
and of the GKSL data

H(\bm{\lambda})
,
\{F_{k}(\bm{\lambda})\}
.

A.1
Setting and GKSL dynamics

For each fixed
\bm{\lambda}
, the laboratory time evolution is generated by a
time-independent GKSL (Lindblad) generator

\partial_{t}\rho(t,\bm{\lambda})=\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}}[\rho]:=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[H(\bm{\lambda}),\rho]+\sum_{k}\left(F_{k}\rho F_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{F_{k}^{\dagger}F_{k},\rho\}\right).

(A2)

See Refs.
21
;
27
;
7
.
Normalization
\Tr\rho(t,\bm{\lambda})=1
implies that for any tangent
\delta\rho
one
has
\Tr(\delta\rho)=0
.

Our goal in this section is to start from the Uhlmann fidelity, derive the Bures
metric and the SLD–QFIM, project away nuisance directions by a Schur complement
to obtain an effective (profiled) information
F_{\rm eff}
, and then combine this
with the GKSL dynamics (
A2
) to obtain a projected (quotient) quantum

A.2
From Uhlmann fidelity to the Bures metric

The Uhlmann fidelity between two density operators
\rho
and
\sigma
is
defined as

F_{U}(\rho,\sigma):=\left(\Tr\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho}\,\sigma\,\sqrt{\rho}}\right)^{2}.

(A3)

For nearby states
\sigma=\rho+\delta\rho
, the fidelity admits the expansion

F_{U}(\rho,\rho+\delta\rho)=1-g_{\rho}(\delta\rho,\delta\rho)+o(\|\delta\rho\|^{2}),

(A4)

which defines the Bures metric tensor
g_{\rho}

45
;
8
;
24
;
15
.

To make this explicit, let
\rho
be full rank (the rank-deficient case is handled below in the subsection “Rank-deficient states and singular nuisance
blocks”), with spectral decompositionequation A

\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\ket{i}\bra{i},\qquad p_{i}>0.

(A5)

Set

\sigma=\rho+\delta\rho,\qquad\mathcal{A}:=\sqrt{\rho}\,\sigma\,\sqrt{\rho}=\rho^{2}+\sqrt{\rho}\,\delta\rho\,\sqrt{\rho},

(A6)

and define the positive square root

\mathcal{M}:=\sqrt{\mathcal{A}}.

(A7)

We expand
\mathcal{M}
in powers of
\delta\rho
:

\mathcal{M}=\rho+X+Y+O(\|\delta\rho\|^{3}),

(A8)

where
X=O(\delta\rho)
collects linear terms and
Y=O(\delta\rho^{2})
collects
quadratic terms. The defining identity
\mathcal{M}^{2}=\mathcal{A}
then yields
two Sylvester equations:

\displaystyle\rho X+X\rho

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\rho}\,\delta\rho\,\sqrt{\rho},

(A9)

\displaystyle\rho Y+Y\rho

\displaystyle=-X^{2}.

(A10)

See, e.g.,
24
;
15
;
23
.

In the eigenbasis of
\rho
, the solution of (
A9
) is

X_{ij}=\frac{\sqrt{p_{i}p_{j}}}{p_{i}+p_{j}}(\delta\rho)_{ij},\qquad\Tr X=\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\delta\rho)=0,

(A11)

where we used
\Tr(\delta\rho)=0
. To obtain
\Tr Y
, we work with
(
A10
). Taking the trace in the eigenbasis,

\displaystyle\Tr Y

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\rho^{-1}X^2)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{p_{j}}{(p_{i}+p_{j})^{2}}\,|(\delta\rho)_{ij}|^{2}.

(A12)

Using symmetry under

,

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\frac{p_{j}}{(p_{i}+p_{j})^{2}}\,|(\delta\rho)_{ij}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{p_{i}+p_{j}}{(p_{i}+p_{j})^{2}}\,|(\delta\rho)_{ij}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{|(\delta\rho)_{ij}|^{2}}{p_{i}+p_{j}},

(A13)

so that

\Tr Y=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}\frac{|(\delta\rho)_{ij}|^{2}}{p_{i}+p_{j}}.

(A14)

Next, expand the square root of the fidelity.
By definition,

\sqrt{F_{U}(\rho,\sigma)}=\Tr\sqrt{\mathcal{A}}=\Tr\mathcal{M}.

(A15)

Using
\Tr\rho=1
and
\Tr X=0
, we find

\sqrt{F_{U}(\rho,\rho+\delta\rho)}=1+\Tr Y+O(\|\delta\rho\|^{3})=1-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}\frac{|(\delta\rho)_{ij}|^{2}}{p_{i}+p_{j}}+o(\|\delta\rho\|^{2}),

(A16)

where we used (
A14
). Since
F_{U}=(\sqrt{F_{U}})^{2}

F_{U}(\rho,\rho+\delta\rho)=1-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{|(\delta\rho)_{ij}|^{2}}{p_{i}+p_{j}}+o(\|\delta\rho\|^{2}).

(A17)

Comparing with the defining expansion (
A4
), we obtain

g_{\rho}(\delta\rho,\delta\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{|(\delta\rho)_{ij}|^{2}}{p_{i}+p_{j}},

(A18)

A.3
SLD representation and the SLD–QFIM

We now rewrite the metric (
A18
) in terms of the symmetric
logarithmic derivative (SLD). For any tangent
\delta\rho
, the SLD

L(\delta\rho)
is defined by the Sylvester equation

\delta\rho=\frac{1}{2}(\rho L+L\rho)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L=J_{\rho}^{-1}(\delta\rho),\qquad J_{\rho}(X):=\frac{1}{2}(\rho X+X\rho).

(A19)

In the eigenbasis of
\rho

L_{ij}=\frac{2(\delta\rho)_{ij}}{p_{i}+p_{j}}.

(A20)

Substituting (
A20
) into (
A18
) and simplifying,
one obtains

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\delta\rho,\delta\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{|(\delta\rho)_{ij}|^{2}}{p_{i}+p_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{p_{i}+p_{j}}\left|\frac{p_{i}+p_{j}}{2}L_{ij}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{i,j}(p_{i}+p_{j})|L_{ij}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}p_{i}|L_{ij}|^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\Tr(\rho L^2).

(A21)

Here we used symmetry under
i\leftrightarrow j
and the Hermiticity of
L
.
Equation (
A21
) is the standard SLD representation of the Bures
metric
22
;
6
;
34
.

We now introduce coordinates
x^{\mu}=(t,\lambda^{1},\dots,\lambda^{m-1})
on the
parameter manifold and expand

\delta\rho=\partial_{\mu}\rho\,dx^{\mu}.

(A22)

Define the coordinate SLD operators
L_{\mu}
by

\partial_{\mu}\rho=\frac{1}{2}(\rho L_{\mu}+L_{\mu}\rho),\qquad L_{\mu}:=J_{\rho}^{-1}(\partial_{\mu}\rho).

(A23)

By bilinearity of
g_{\rho}
, the Bures line element can be written as

\displaystyle dL^{2}

\displaystyle=g_{\rho}(\delta\rho,\delta\rho)=\frac{1}{4}\Tr[\rho(\delta L)^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Tr[\rho(L_{\mu}L_{\nu})\big]\,dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu},

(A24)

where we used
\delta L=L_{\mu}dx^{\mu}
. This identifies the SLD QFIM

F_{\mu\nu}
via

dL^{2}=\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}\,dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu},\qquad F_{\mu\nu}:=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\!\big[\rho(L_{\mu}L_{\nu}+L_{\nu}L_{\mu})\big].

(A25)

Since the SLDs are Hermitian, the SLD–QFIM can equivalently be written as the
real part of the unsymmetrized trace:

F_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\!\big[\rho(L_{\mu}L_{\nu}+L_{\nu}L_{\mu})\big]=\real\,\Tr(\rho L_\mu L_\nu).

(A26)

In particular, when
\Tr(\rho L_\mu L_\nu)
is real for the parameter pair
under consideration, the real-part symbol may be omitted.

Using (
A19
) and (
A23
), one also obtains a
computational expression that is convenient for open-system dynamics:

F_{\mu\nu}=2\,\Tr\!\big[(\partial_{\mu}\rho)\,J_{\rho}^{-1}(\partial_{\nu}\rho)\big].

(A27)

Equations (
A25
) and (
A27
) are the standard
SLD–QFIM formulas
22
;
6
;
34
;
4
.

A.4
Projecting nuisance directions: Schur complement

We now treat time
t
and the nuisance parameters
\bm{\lambda}
on equal
footing as coordinates of the parameter manifold, and then project out the
nuisance directions.

Split the indices
\mu=(t,\alpha)
and block the QFIM as

F=\begin{pmatrix}F_{tt}&\bm{f}^{\top}\\
\bm{f}&F_{\lambda\lambda}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\bm{f}:=(F_{t\alpha})_{\alpha},\quad F_{\lambda\lambda}:=(F_{\alpha\beta})_{\alpha,\beta}.

(A28)

The Bures line element (
A25
) can then be written as the
quadratic form

dL^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\Big(F_{tt}\,dt^{2}+2\,\bm{f}^{\top}d\bm{\lambda}\,dt+d\bm{\lambda}^{\top}F_{\lambda\lambda}\,d\bm{\lambda}\Big).

(A29)

For fixed
dt
, the physically relevant distance along
t
modulo nuisance
reparametrizations is obtained by minimizing (
A29
) over

d\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m-1}
. The normal equations are

\frac{\partial}{\partial(d\bm{\lambda})}(dL^{2})=\frac{1}{2}\big(\bm{f}\,dt+F_{\lambda\lambda}d\bm{\lambda}\big)=0,

(A30)

which gives

F_{\lambda\lambda}\,d\bm{\lambda}_{*}=-\,\bm{f}\,dt,\qquad d\bm{\lambda}_{*}=-F_{\lambda\lambda}^{-1}\bm{f}\,dt.

(A31)

Here
F_{\lambda\lambda}^{-1}
denotes the inverse of
F_{\lambda\lambda}
on
its range; when
F_{\lambda\lambda}
is singular, it is understood as the
Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse
3
;
23
.

Substituting
d\bm{\lambda}_{*}
back into (
A29
) yields the
minimal squared distance:

\displaystyle dL_{\min}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Big(F_{tt}-2\,\bm{f}^{\top}F_{\lambda\lambda}^{-1}\bm{f}+\bm{f}^{\top}F_{\lambda\lambda}^{-1}F_{\lambda\lambda}F_{\lambda\lambda}^{-1}\bm{f}\Big)\,dt^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Big(F_{tt}-\bm{f}^{\top}F_{\lambda\lambda}^{-1}\bm{f}\Big)\,dt^{2}.

(A32)

This identifies the effective (profiled) Fisher information for time as
the Schur complement of the nuisance block:

F_{\rm eff}:=F_{tt}-\bm{f}^{\top}F_{\lambda\lambda}^{-1}\bm{f},\qquad dL_{\min}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}F_{\rm eff}\,dt^{2}.

(A33)

Since the full QFIM
F
is positive semidefinite, its Schur complements are
positive semidefinite as well
23
;
46
, so

F_{\rm eff}\geq 0
.

Invariance and relation to profiled information.

Consider a smooth reparametrization of the nuisance manifold

\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}=\phi(\bm{\lambda})
with Jacobian
J
. The QFIM transforms as

F^{\prime}=J^{-\top}FJ^{-1}
, whence

\displaystyle F^{\prime}_{\lambda\lambda}

\displaystyle=J^{\top}F_{\lambda\lambda}J,

(A34)

\displaystyle\bm{f}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=J^{\top}\bm{f}.

(A35)

It follows that

\displaystyle\bm{f}^{\prime\!\top}(F^{\prime}_{\lambda\lambda})^{-1}\bm{f}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\bm{f}^{\top}J(J^{\top}F_{\lambda\lambda}J)^{-1}J^{\top}\bm{f}

(A36)

\displaystyle=\bm{f}^{\top}F_{\lambda\lambda}^{-1}\bm{f},

(A37)

and hence
F_{\rm eff}
is a scalar under nuisance reparametrizations.

If the full QFIM
F
is invertible, one may also use the block inversion
formula
23
:

F_{\rm eff}=\big((F^{-1})_{tt}\big)^{-1},

(A38)

which is the usual profiled Fisher information for
t

1
. Thus
F_{\rm eff}
coincides with the information for

t
after profiling (maximizing the likelihood) over the nuisance coordinates.

A.5
Embedding GKSL dynamics: sensitivities and QFIM blocks

We now bring the GKSL dynamics (
A2
) inside the geometric
construction. Treat
(t,\bm{\lambda})
as independent coordinates, and write

d\rho=\partial_{t}\rho\,dt+\sum_{\alpha}\partial_{\lambda^{\alpha}}\rho\,d\lambda^{\alpha}\equiv\dot{\rho}\,dt+\sum_{\alpha}\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}\,d\lambda^{\alpha},

(A39)

where
\dot{\rho}:=\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}}[\rho]
is determined by
(
A2
) and

\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}:=\partial_{\lambda^{\alpha}}\rho

(A40)

is the sensitivity of the state to a change in the nuisance parameter

\lambda^{\alpha}
.

Differentiating the GKSL equation (
A2
) with respect to

\lambda^{\alpha}
and commuting derivatives (under the assumed smoothness) gives

\partial_{t}\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)=(\partial_{\lambda^{\alpha}}\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}})[\rho(t)]+\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}}[\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)].

(A41)

This is a linear, inhomogeneous ODE on the operator space. Its solution is
given by the Duhamel formula
31
;
16
:

\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)=e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}}}\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}}}\,(\partial_{\lambda^{\alpha}}\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}})[\rho(s,\bm{\lambda})]\,ds.

(A42)

Trace preservation of
\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}}
and of

\partial_{\lambda^{\alpha}}\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}}
implies that

\Tr\,\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)=\Tr\,\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}(0)
; taking

\Tr\,\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}(0)=0
ensures that
\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)
remains a valid
tangent (trace-zero) operator for all
t

7
;
31
.

Using the QFIM expression (
A27
) with

\partial_{t}\rho=\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}}[\rho]
and

\partial_{\lambda^{\alpha}}\rho=\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}
, we obtain the matrix blocks along
a dynamical trajectory:

\displaystyle F_{tt}(t)

\displaystyle=2\,\Tr\!\big[(\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}}\rho)\,J_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}}\rho)\big],

(A43)

\displaystyle F_{t\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=2\,\Tr\!\big[(\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\lambda}}\rho)\,J_{\rho}^{-1}(\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha})\big],

(A44)

\displaystyle F_{\alpha\beta}(t)

\displaystyle=2\,\Tr\!\big[\rho^{\prime}_{\alpha}\,J_{\rho}^{-1}(\rho^{\prime}_{\beta})\big].

(A45)

Substituting these blocks into the Schur complement
(
A33
) yields the effective information
F_{\rm eff}(t)

along the laboratory trajectory. This is the quantity that enters the
projected quantum speed limit.

A.6
Quotient metric and local-to-global projected QSL

We now make explicit the quotient-geometric meaning of the profiled quantity

F_{\rm eff}
and its relation to the global projected quantum speed limit.

At each state
\rho
, the nuisance coordinates
\lambda^{\alpha}
generate tangent
vectors
\partial_{\lambda^{\alpha}}\rho
. Their span defines the vertical
nuisance subspace

\mathcal{V}_{\rho}:=\mathrm{span}\!\left\{\partial_{\lambda^{\alpha}}\rho\right\}_{\alpha}.

(A46)

On a regular patch where this nuisance distribution has constant rank, we define
the induced quadratic form on a quotient tangent class
[X]
by minimizing the
ambient Bures metric over vertical shifts:

g_{\rm quo}([X],[X]):=\inf_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{\rho}}g_{\rho}(X+V,X+V).

(A47)

Take now
X=\partial_{t}\rho
and write

V=\xi^{\alpha}\,\partial_{\lambda^{\alpha}}\rho.

(A48)

Using the block form of the QFIM, the corresponding Bures quadratic form is

4\,g_{\rho}(X+V,X+V)=F_{tt}+2\,\xi^{\alpha}F_{t\alpha}+\xi^{\alpha}\xi^{\beta}F_{\alpha\beta}.

(A49)

Minimizing (
A49
) over
\xi^{\alpha}
gives the normal
equations

F_{\alpha\beta}\,\xi_{*}^{\beta}=-\,F_{t\alpha},

(A50)

and therefore

4\,g_{\rm quo}\!\big([\partial_{t}\rho],[\partial_{t}\rho]\big)=F_{tt}-\bm{f}^{\top}F_{\lambda\lambda}^{-1}\bm{f}=F_{\rm eff}.

(A51)

Thus the Schur complement
F_{\rm eff}
is precisely the induced quotient metric
coefficient along the time direction.

When
F_{\lambda\lambda}
is singular, the inverse in
(
A51
) is understood in the Moore–Penrose sense on the
estimable nuisance subspace, exactly as in (
A33
). Because the
full QFIM block matrix is positive semidefinite, one has

\bm{f}\in\operatorname{Ran}(F_{\lambda\lambda})
, equivalently

\bm{f}\perp\ker(F_{\lambda\lambda})
. Hence the Moore–Penrose
pseudoinverse yields the correct profiled minimum on the estimable nuisance
subspace. Likewise, for rank-deficient states all constructions are understood
on
\operatorname{supp}(\rho)
, as discussed in Sec.
A.8
.

In the SLD language, the same result can be expressed through orthogonal
projection of the time score away from the nuisance-score span. If

\widetilde{L}_{t}
denotes the orthogonal projection of
L_{t}
onto the complement
of
\mathrm{span}\{L_{\alpha}\}
with respect to the inner product

\langle\!\langle A,B\rangle\!\rangle_{\rho}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\Tr[\rho\{A,B\}]
, then, when the nuisance-score Gram matrix is
singular, this projection is understood on the estimable nuisance-score
subspace, with coefficients determined by the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of

F_{\lambda\lambda}
,

\langle\!\langle\widetilde{L}_{t},\widetilde{L}_{t}\rangle\!\rangle_{\rho}=F_{\rm eff}.

(A52)

Hence the profiled Fisher information and the quotient-metric coefficient
coincide.

Once this local quotient metric is fixed, the instantaneous projected speed is
the norm of the tangent to the realized evolution in the quotient geometry:

v_{\rm quo}(t)=\sqrt{g_{\rm quo}\!\big([\dot{\rho}(t)],[\dot{\rho}(t)]\big)}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{\rm eff}(t)}.

(A53)

Therefore, along the realized trajectory
t\mapsto\rho(t)
, the quotient path
length is

\ell_{\rm quo}[\rho(\cdot)]=\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{\rm eff}(t)}\,dt.

(A54)

By definition of geodesic distance in the quotient geometry,

L_{\rm quo}\big([\rho_{0}],[\rho_{\tau}]\big)\leq\ell_{\rm quo}[\rho(\cdot)].

(A55)

Hence

L_{\rm quo}\big([\rho_{0}],[\rho_{\tau}]\big)\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{\rm eff}(t)}\,dt.

(A56)

This shows that no further optimization of
v_{\rm quo}
along the realized path
is needed. The only additional global minimization is the one already contained
in the definition of the quotient geodesic distance
L_{\rm quo}
itself, namely
as the infimum of quotient-path lengths over all curves connecting the endpoint
equivalence classes.

A.7
Projected speed and quotient quantum speed limit

As established in Sec.
A.6
, the instantaneous
projected (quotient) speed is

v_{\rm quo}(t):=\frac{dL_{\rm quo}}{dt}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{\rm eff}(t)}.

(A57)

and the corresponding integral quotient-QSL is

L_{\rm quo}\big([\rho_{0}],[\rho_{\tau}]\big)\;\leq\;\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{\rm eff}(t)}\,dt.

(A58)

Rearranging (
A58
) gives the time-to-target form used in the main text,

\tau\;\geq\;\frac{L_{\rm quo}\big([\rho_{0}],[\rho_{\tau}]\big)}{\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\rm quo}},\qquad\bar{v}_{\rm quo}:=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{\rm eff}(t)}\,dt,

(A59)

where
\bar{v}_{\rm quo}
is the time-averaged projected speed. This is the form
used in the main text.

Remark 1

(Physical vs projected QSLs)
.

The physical QSL (without profiling) pairs the physical Bures distance

L(\rho_{0},\rho_{\tau})
with the speed
\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{tt}}
, where
F_{tt}
is
the
tt
-element of the full QFIM. The projected QSL pairs the quotient
distance
L_{\rm quo}
with the projected speed

\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{\rm eff}}
. Since
F_{\rm eff}\leq F_{tt}
and

L_{\rm quo}\leq L(\rho_{0},\rho_{\tau})
(projection never increases distances),
one must not mix distances and speeds from different manifolds, as this can
invalidate the bound.

A.8
Rank-deficient states and singular nuisance blocks

The derivations above assumed full rank for
\rho
and invertibility of

F_{\lambda\lambda}
. We now state the precise conditions under which the

Lemma 1

(Support restriction and continuous extension)
.

Let
\rho
be a density matrix (possibly rank-deficient), and let

\mathrm{supp}(\rho)
denote its support. Restrict all constructions to

\mathrm{supp}(\rho)
, i.e., regard
\rho
as a strictly positive operator on

\mathrm{supp}(\rho)
and define
J_{\rho}
and
J_{\rho}^{-1}
accordingly. Then
the formulas (
A18
)–(
A27
),
(
A28
)–(
A38
),
(
A41
)–(
A45
), and
(
A57
)–(
A59
) all hold with this
understanding. Moreover, continuity of the fidelity in the trace norm implies
that the resulting Bures metric is the continuous extension from

\mathrm{supp}(\rho)
to
\mathcal{H}
.

A detailed discussion of these support issues for the Bures metric and the
SLD–QFIM can be found in Ref.
38
.

Remark 2

(Singular
F_{\lambda\lambda}
)
.

When the nuisance block
F_{\lambda\lambda}
is singular, the Schur complement
(
A33
) is defined using the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse

. This yields the unique minimizer on the estimable
nuisance subspace and preserves invariance under reparametrizations of the
nuisance manifold

;

.

Appendix B: Closed Jaynes–Cummings sensor — unitary projected QSL

In this section we specialize the general formalism of Appendix
Appendix A: Detailed formalism — Bures geometry, Schur complement, and GKSL dynamics
to a closed
Jaynes–Cummings (JC) sensor driven by a static magnetic field. We (i) reduce
the model to a two-level single-excitation manifold; (ii) compute the local
generators for laboratory time
t
and field
B
; (iii) evaluate the
two-parameter QFIM
\{F_{tt},F_{tB},F_{BB}\}
exactly; (iv) form the
Schur-complement effective information
F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
; and (v) relate
these quantities to the fidelity and the projected (quotient) quantum speed
limit. Finally, we derive a short-time expansion that yields explicit detuning
tolerances for retaining a given fraction of the physical speed.

B.1
Model and reduction to the single-excitation manifold

We consider a single-mode cavity of frequency
\omega_{c}
coupled to a
two-level system (atom or qubit) of bare transition frequency
\omega_{q}
and a
magnetic-field-dependent Zeeman shift
\gamma B
. In the rotating-wave
approximation, the laboratory-frame Hamiltonian reads

H(B)=\omega_{c}\,a^{\dagger}a+\frac{\omega_{q}}{2}\sigma_{z}+g\,(a\sigma_{+}+a^{\dagger}\sigma_{-})+\frac{\gamma B}{2}\,\sigma_{z},

(B1)

with
\hbar=1
and the usual Pauli and ladder operators

\sigma_{z}=\ket{e}\bra{e}-\ket{g}\bra{g}
,

\sigma_{+}=\ket{e}\bra{g}
,
\sigma_{-}=\ket{g}\bra{e}
.

We prepare the initial state

\ket{\psi(0)}=\ket{e,0},

(B2)

and assume that throughout the relevant evolution time the dynamics remains in
the single-excitation manifold spanned by
\{\ket{e,0},\ket{g,1}\}
. In this
manifold the total excitation number

N=a^{\dagger}a+\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-}

(B3)

is conserved and equals
1
.

In the ordered basis
\{\ket{e,0},\ket{g,1}\}
we have the matrix
representations

\displaystyle a^{\dagger}a

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{z}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},

(B4)

\displaystyle a\sigma_{+}+a^{\dagger}\sigma_{-}

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

Define the detuning

\Delta(B):=\omega_{q}+\gamma B-\omega_{c},

(B5)

and subtract the scalar term
(\omega_{c}/2)\,\mathbb{I}
from
H(B)
: this does
not affect any QFI element or variance, since adding
c\,\mathbb{I}
to a
Hamiltonian leaves commutators and variances invariant. We thus work with the
traceless effective two-level Hamiltonian

H^{\prime}(B)=g\,\tau_{x}+\frac{\Delta(B)}{2}\,\tau_{z},

(B6)

where
\bm{\tau}=(\tau_{x},\tau_{y},\tau_{z})
are Pauli matrices acting on

\{\ket{e,0},\ket{g,1}\}
. Introducing the generalized Rabi frequency

\Omega(B):=\sqrt{\Delta(B)^{2}+4g^{2}},

(B7)

we can write

H^{\prime}(B)=\frac{\Omega(B)}{2}\,\bm{n}(B)\!\cdot\!\bm{\tau},\qquad\bm{n}(B)=\frac{1}{\Omega(B)}\,(2g,\,0,\,\Delta(B)).

(B8)

The unitary time evolution in this manifold is

U(t;B)=e^{-iH^{\prime}(B)t}.

(B9)

The initial state
\ket{\psi(0)}=\ket{e,0}
is the
+1
eigenstate of
\tau_{z}
,
which we denote by
\ket{+z}
in what follows.

B.2
Local generators and QFI for
(t,B)

For a pure state family
\ket{\psi(x)}=U(x)\ket{\psi_0}
with parameters

x^{\mu}
, the SLD QFIM is given by the covariance of the local generators

G_{\mu}
defined by

i\,\partial_{\mu}U(x)=G_{\mu}(x)\,U(x).

(B10)

Explicitly
6
,

F_{\mu\nu}=4\,\mathrm{Cov}_{\psi_{0}}(G_{\mu},G_{\nu}):=4\,\real\Bigl(\innerproduct{\psi_0|G_\mu G_\nu|\psi_0}{\psi_0|G_\mu G_\nu|\psi_0}-\innerproduct{\psi_0|G_\mu|\psi_0}{\psi_0|G_\mu|\psi_0}\innerproduct{\psi_0|G_\nu|\psi_0}{\psi_0|G_\nu|\psi_0}\Bigr),

(B11)

where all expectations are taken in the initial state

\ket{\psi_0}=\ket{+z}
.

In our case the parameters are
(t,B)
and

U(t,B)=e^{-iH^{\prime}(B)t}.

(B12)

The time generator is simply the Hamiltonian,

G_{t}=H^{\prime}(B).

(B13)

The field generator
G_{B}(t)
is obtained by differentiating
U(t;B)
with
respect to
B
:

\displaystyle\partial_{B}U(t;B)

\displaystyle=-i\int_{0}^{t}U(t;B)U^{\dagger}(s;B)\,\partial_{B}H^{\prime}(B)\,U(s;B)\,ds

\displaystyle=-i\,U(t;B)\int_{0}^{t}U^{\dagger}(s;B)\,\partial_{B}H^{\prime}(B)\,U(s;B)\,ds.

(B14)

Thus the local generator for
B
is

G_{B}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}U^{\dagger}(s;B)\,\partial_{B}H^{\prime}(B)\,U(s;B)\,ds.

(B15)

From (
B6
), the
B
-dependence enters only through the
detuning, so that

\partial_{B}H^{\prime}(B)=\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\tau_{z}.

(B16)

Substituting (
B16
) into (
B15
) gives

G_{B}(t)=\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\tau_{z}(s)\,ds,\qquad\tau_{z}(s):=U^{\dagger}(s;B)\,\tau_{z}\,U(s;B).

(B17)

We therefore need the exact Heisenberg evolution of
\tau_{z}
under
H^{\prime}
.

The relevant moments in the initial state
\ket{+z}
are

\displaystyle\langle\tau_{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\langle\tau_{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\tau_{y}\rangle=0,

(B18)

\displaystyle\langle\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij},

\displaystyle\real\langle\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=0\quad(i\neq j),

which we will use repeatedly below.

B.3
Heisenberg evolution of
\tau_{z}
and closed-form
G_{B}(t)

The effective Hamiltonian (
B8
) shows that the dynamics is a
rotation on the Bloch sphere about the axis
\bm{n}(B)
with angular frequency

\Omega(B)
. For any vector
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

e^{+i(\Omega/2)\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\tau}\,s}\,(\bm{v}\!\cdot\!\bm{\tau})\,e^{-i(\Omega/2)\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\tau}\,s}=\bigl(R_{\bm{n}}(\Omega s)\bm{v}\bigr)\!\cdot\!\bm{\tau},

(B19)

where
R_{\bm{n}}(\theta)
is the rotation matrix about
\bm{n}
by angle

\theta
. Applying (
B19
) to
\bm{v}=\hat{\bm{z}}
yields

\tau_{z}(s)=v_{x}(s)\,\tau_{x}+v_{y}(s)\,\tau_{y}+v_{z}(s)\,\tau_{z},

(B20)

with coefficients determined by Rodrigues’ formula

R_{\bm{n}}(\theta)\bm{v}=\bm{n}(\bm{n}\!\cdot\!\bm{v})+(\bm{v}-\bm{n}\,\bm{n}\!\cdot\!\bm{v})\cos\theta+(\bm{n}\times\bm{v})\sin\theta.

(B21)

Using
\bm{n}=(2g/\Omega,\,0,\,\Delta/\Omega)
and
\bm{v}=\hat{\bm{z}}
,
a straightforward calculation gives

\displaystyle v_{x}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{2g\Delta}{\Omega^{2}}\Bigl(1-\cos\Omega s\Bigr),

\displaystyle v_{y}(s)

\displaystyle=-\,\frac{2g}{\Omega}\sin\Omega s,

\displaystyle v_{z}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}+\frac{4g^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}\cos\Omega s,

(B22)

where we suppress the explicit
B
-dependence of
\Delta
and
\Omega
for
readability.

Inserting (
B20
) and (
B22
) into
(
B17
), we obtain

G_{B}(t)=\frac{\gamma}{2}\Bigl(A_{x}(t)\,\tau_{x}+A_{y}(t)\,\tau_{y}+A_{z}(t)\,\tau_{z}\Bigr),

(B23)

with the time-dependent coefficients

\displaystyle A_{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}v_{x}(s)\,ds=\frac{2g\Delta}{\Omega^{2}}\Bigl(t-\frac{\sin\Omega t}{\Omega}\Bigr),

(B24)

\displaystyle A_{y}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}v_{y}(s)\,ds=-\,\frac{2g}{\Omega^{2}}\Bigl(1-\cos\Omega t\Bigr),

(B25)

\displaystyle A_{z}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}v_{z}(s)\,ds=\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}\,t+\frac{4g^{2}}{\Omega^{3}}\sin\Omega t.

(B26)

No approximations have been made: the only step between
(
B20
) and (
B24
)–(
B26
) is the
evaluation of elementary trigonometric integrals.

B.4
Exact QFIM entries
F_{tt}
,
F_{BB}
, and
F_{tB}

We now evaluate the QFIM elements for the parameter pair
(t,B)
using
(
B11
) and the local generators
(
B13
) and (

), with expectations taken in the
initial state
\ket{+z}
.

(i) The time-time element
F_{tt}
.

From (

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(H^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\bigl(g\tau_{x}+\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\tau_{z}\bigr)^{2}\right\rangle-\left\langle g\tau_{x}+\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\tau_{z}\right\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=g^{2}\langle\tau_{x}^{2}\rangle+\frac{\Delta^{2}}{4}\langle\tau_{z}^{2}\rangle+g\Delta\,\real\langle\tau_{x}\tau_{z}\rangle-\Bigl(0+\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\cdot 1\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=g^{2}+\frac{\Delta^{2}}{4}-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{4}=g^{2},

(B27)

so that

F_{tt}=4\,\mathrm{Var}(H^{\prime})=4g^{2}.

(B28)

Equivalently, one may note that
(H^{\prime})^{2}=(\Delta^{2}/4+g^{2})\,\mathbb{I}
, so the
variance in a pure state reduces to
g^{2}
.

(ii) The field-field element
F_{BB}
.

For any vector
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

(\bm{b}\!\cdot\!\bm{\tau})^{2}=\|\bm{b}\|^{2}\,\mathbb{I}
, as the antisymmetric
part drops out, and hence

\displaystyle\langle G_{B}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\frac{\gamma}{2}\Bigr)^{2}\langle(A_{x}\tau_{x}+A_{y}\tau_{y}+A_{z}\tau_{z})^{2}\rangle=\Bigl(\frac{\gamma}{2}\Bigr)^{2}\bigl(A_{x}^{2}+A_{y}^{2}+A_{z}^{2}\bigr),

(B29)

\displaystyle\langle G_{B}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{2}\bigl(A_{x}\langle\tau_{x}\rangle+A_{y}\langle\tau_{y}\rangle+A_{z}\langle\tau_{z}\rangle\bigr)=\frac{\gamma}{2}A_{z},

(B30)

where we used (
B18
). Therefore

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(G_{B})

\displaystyle=\langle G_{B}^{2}\rangle-\langle G_{B}\rangle^{2}=\Bigl(\frac{\gamma}{2}\Bigr)^{2}\bigl(A_{x}^{2}+A_{y}^{2}\bigr),

(B31)

and

F_{BB}(t)=4\,\mathrm{Var}(G_{B})=\gamma^{2}\bigl[A_{x}(t)^{2}+A_{y}(t)^{2}\bigr].

(B32)

Substituting (
B24
) and (
B25
), we obtain the explicit
closed form

\displaystyle F_{BB}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{4g^{2}\gamma^{2}}{\Omega^{4}}\left[\Delta^{2}\!\left(t-\frac{\sin\Omega t}{\Omega}\right)^{\!2}+\Bigl(1-\cos\Omega t\Bigr)^{\!2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{4g^{2}\gamma^{2}}{\Omega^{4}}\left[\Delta^{2}\!\left(t-\frac{\sin\Omega t}{\Omega}\right)^{\!2}+4\sin^{4}\!\Bigl(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\Bigr)\right].

(B33)

(iii) The mixed element
F_{tB}
.

By (
B11
), the mixed QFI element is

F_{tB}=4\,\mathrm{Cov}(H^{\prime},G_{B})=4\,\real\bigl(\langle H^{\prime}G_{B}\rangle-\langle H^{\prime}\rangle\langle G_{B}\rangle\bigr).

(B34)

Using
H^{\prime}=g\tau_{x}+(\Delta/2)\tau_{z}
and
G_{B}
from
(
B23
), with expectations in
\ket{+z}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(H^{\prime},G_{B})

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\real\left\langle\bigl(g\tau_{x}+\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\tau_{z}\bigr)\bigl(A_{x}\tau_{x}+A_{y}\tau_{y}+A_{z}\tau_{z}\bigr)\right\rangle-\frac{\gamma}{2}\Bigl(\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\Bigr)A_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\real\bigl(gA_{x}\langle\tau_{x}\tau_{x}\rangle+gA_{y}\langle\tau_{x}\tau_{y}\rangle+\tfrac{\Delta}{2}A_{z}\langle\tau_{z}\tau_{z}\rangle\bigr)-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\frac{\Delta}{2}A_{z}.

(B35)

Using (
B18
),

\langle\tau_{x}\tau_{x}\rangle=\langle\tau_{z}\tau_{z}\rangle=1
and

\real\langle\tau_{x}\tau_{y}\rangle=0
, we get

\mathrm{Cov}(H^{\prime},G_{B})=\frac{\gamma}{2}\,gA_{x}.

(B36)

Therefore

F_{tB}(t)=4\,\mathrm{Cov}(H^{\prime},G_{B})=2\gamma g\,A_{x}(t)=\frac{4g^{2}\gamma\,\Delta}{\Omega^{2}}\left(t-\frac{\sin\Omega t}{\Omega}\right).

(B37)

Collecting the results (
B28
), (
B33
), and
(
B37
), the full
2\times 2
QFIM for
(t,B)
is

\displaystyle F_{tt}

\displaystyle=4g^{2},

(B38)

\displaystyle F_{BB}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{4g^{2}\gamma^{2}}{\Omega^{4}}\left[\Delta^{2}\left(t-\frac{\sin\Omega t}{\Omega}\right)^{\!2}+4\sin^{4}\!\left(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\right)\right],

(B39)

\displaystyle F_{tB}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{4g^{2}\gamma\,\Delta}{\Omega^{2}}\left(t-\frac{\sin\Omega t}{\Omega}\right),

(B40)

with
\Omega=\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+4g^{2}}
and
\Delta=\omega_{q}+\gamma B-\omega_{c}
.

B.5
Schur-complement effective information
F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)

We now treat
t
as the parameter of interest and
B
as a nuisance, and apply
the Schur complement of Appendix
Appendix A: Detailed formalism — Bures geometry, Schur complement, and GKSL dynamics
to obtain the effective information for time.
With a single nuisance parameter, the Schur complement reduces to

F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)=F_{tt}-\frac{F_{tB}(t)^{2}}{F_{BB}(t)},

(B41)

understanding
F_{BB}^{-1}
as the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse when

F_{BB}=0
(in which case necessarily
F_{tB}=0
as well).

Substituting (
B38
)–(
B40
) and using

A_{x}
and
A_{y}
defined in (
B24
)–(
B25
), it is
convenient to write
F_{BB}=\gamma^{2}(A_{x}^{2}+A_{y}^{2})
and

F_{tB}=2\gamma gA_{x}
. Then

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)

\displaystyle=4g^{2}-\frac{4\gamma^{2}g^{2}A_{x}(t)^{2}}{\gamma^{2}\bigl(A_{x}(t)^{2}+A_{y}(t)^{2}\bigr)}

\displaystyle=4g^{2}\,\frac{A_{y}(t)^{2}}{A_{x}(t)^{2}+A_{y}(t)^{2}}.

(B42)

Using the explicit expressions for
A_{x}
and
A_{y}
,

\displaystyle A_{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2g\Delta}{\Omega^{2}}\left(t-\frac{\sin\Omega t}{\Omega}\right),

(B43)

\displaystyle A_{y}(t)

\displaystyle=-\,\frac{2g}{\Omega^{2}}\left(1-\cos\Omega t\right),

(B44)

we can write
F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
in closed form:

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)

\displaystyle=4g^{2}\,\frac{\bigl(1-\cos(\Omega t)\bigr)^{2}}{\Delta^{2}\left(t-\tfrac{\sin(\Omega t)}{\Omega}\right)^{2}+\bigl(1-\cos(\Omega t)\bigr)^{2}}

\displaystyle=4g^{2}\,\frac{4\sin^{4}\!\bigl(\tfrac{\Omega t}{2}\bigr)}{\displaystyle\Delta^{2}\left(t-\frac{\sin(\Omega t)}{\Omega}\right)^{\!2}+4\sin^{4}\!\bigl(\tfrac{\Omega t}{2}\bigr)}.

(B45)

By construction,
0\leq F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)\leq F_{tt}=4g^{2}
, and equality

F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)=4g^{2}
holds whenever
A_{x}(t)=0
, i.e., when the mixed
block
F_{tB}
vanishes.

The instantaneous projected speed is then

v_{\mathrm{quo}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)}=g\,\sqrt{\frac{A_{y}(t)^{2}}{A_{x}(t)^{2}+A_{y}(t)^{2}}},

(B46)

and the time-averaged projected speed is

\bar{v}_{\mathrm{quo}}=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)}\,dt.

(B47)

B.6
Fidelity, Bures angle, and quotient distance

To connect the effective information to an operational distance, we compute the
fidelity between the initial state
\ket{\psi(0)}=\ket{+z}
and the evolved
state
\ket{\psi(t,B)}=U(t;B)\ket{+z}
. Using

H^{\prime}=\frac{\Omega}{2}\bm{n}\!\cdot\!\bm{\tau}
and (
B8
), the
propagator is

U(t;B)=e^{-i\frac{\Omega t}{2}\,\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\tau}}=\cos\!\frac{\Omega t}{2}\,\mathbb{I}-i\sin\!\frac{\Omega t}{2}\,\bm{n}\!\cdot\!\bm{\tau},

(B48)

with
\bm{n}=(2g,0,\Delta)/\Omega
. The overlap with
\ket{+z}
is

\displaystyle\innerproduct{\psi(0)|\psi(t,B)}{\psi(0)|\psi(t,B)}

\displaystyle=\innerproduct{+z|U(t;B)|+z}{+z|U(t;B)|+z}

\displaystyle=\cos\!\frac{\Omega t}{2}-i\,\frac{\Delta}{\Omega}\,\sin\!\frac{\Omega t}{2},

(B49)

where we used
\innerproduct{+z|\bm n\!\cdot\!\bm\tau|+z}{+z|\bm n\!\cdot\!\bm\tau|+z}=n_{z}=\Delta/\Omega
. The
fidelity is therefore

\displaystyle F(t,B)

\displaystyle=\bigl|\innerproduct{\psi(0)|\psi(t,B)}{\psi(0)|\psi(t,B)}\bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}\!\frac{\Omega t}{2}+\Bigl(\frac{\Delta}{\Omega}\Bigr)^{2}\sin^{2}\!\frac{\Omega t}{2}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{4g^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}\sin^{2}\!\Bigl(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\Bigr).

(B50)

This is the closed-system survival fidelity for the JC sensor at detuning

\Delta(B)
.

When
B
is treated as a nuisance, the relevant distance on the quotient
manifold is obtained by optimizing the fidelity over admissible
B
values at
fixed interrogation time
\tau
. The quotient Bures angle is

\Theta_{\rm quo}^{\rm(uni)}(\tau):=\arccos\!\Big(\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\sqrt{F(\tau,B)}\Big),

(B51)

where
\mathcal{B}
is the experimentally admissible set of fields (set by
calibration and hardware constraints). Using (
B50
), this can
be written as

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm quo}^{\rm(uni)}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\arccos\!\left(\sqrt{\,1-\inf_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{4g^{2}}{\Omega(B)^{2}}\sin^{2}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{\Omega(B)\tau}{2}\Bigr)}\right).

(B52)

If some
B\in\mathcal{B}
satisfies
\Omega(B)\tau\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
, the
infimum evaluates to
0
and
\Theta_{\rm quo}^{\rm(uni)}(\tau)=0
, reflecting
the fact that the field may be chosen to bring the system back to its initial
state at time
\tau
.

The projected QSL then reads

\tau\;\geq\;\frac{\Theta_{\rm quo}^{\rm(uni)}(\tau)}{\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\mathrm{quo}}},

(B53)

with
\bar{v}_{\mathrm{quo}}
given by (
B47
). This is the
closed-system counterpart of the general projected QSL of Appendix
Appendix A: Detailed formalism — Bures geometry, Schur complement, and GKSL dynamics
.

B.7
Short-time expansion and detuning tolerances

For short evolution times where
|\Omega t|\ll 1
and
|\Delta t|\ll 1
, the
ratio of the effective to the physical Fisher information admits a simple
approximate expression that quantifies the impact of detuning on the projected
speed. From (
B42
),

\frac{F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)}{F_{tt}}=\frac{A_{y}(t)^{2}}{A_{x}(t)^{2}+A_{y}(t)^{2}},

(B54)

with
A_{x}
and
A_{y}
given by (
B24
)–(
B25
). Expanding

\sin(\Omega t)\approx\Omega t-\frac{(\Omega t)^{3}}{6},\qquad\cos(\Omega t)\approx 1-\frac{(\Omega t)^{2}}{2},

(B55)

we obtain to leading nontrivial orders

\displaystyle A_{y}(t)

\displaystyle=-\,\frac{2g}{\Omega^{2}}\bigl(1-\cos\Omega t\bigr)\approx-\,\frac{2g}{\Omega^{2}}\,\frac{(\Omega t)^{2}}{2}=-\,g\,t^{2}+O(t^{4}),

(B56)

\displaystyle A_{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2g\Delta}{\Omega^{2}}\left(t-\frac{\sin\Omega t}{\Omega}\right)\approx\frac{2g\Delta}{\Omega^{2}}\,\frac{(\Omega t)^{3}}{6}=\frac{g\Delta}{3}\,t^{3}+O(t^{5}).

(B57)

Therefore

\frac{A_{x}(t)^{2}}{A_{y}(t)^{2}}\approx\frac{\bigl((g\Delta/3)t^{3}\bigr)^{2}}{(gt^{2})^{2}}=\left(\frac{\Delta t}{3}\right)^{2},

(B58)

and substituting into (
B54
) gives

\frac{F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)}{F_{tt}}=\frac{1}{1+A_{x}^{2}/A_{y}^{2}}\approx\frac{1}{1+(\Delta t/3)^{2}}.

(B59)

Thus, at short times the projected (effective) speed satisfies

\frac{v_{\mathrm{quo}}(t)}{\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{tt}}}=\sqrt{\frac{F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)}{F_{tt}}}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\Delta t/3)^{2}}},

(B60)

showing that any nonzero detuning reduces the operational speed, with a
quadratic penalty in the product
|\Delta|t
.

To maintain the effective information at a fraction
R\in(0,1]
of the physical
one (i.e.,
F_{\mathrm{eff}}/F_{tt}\geq R
), the short-time approximation
(
B59
) implies the tolerance condition

\frac{1}{1+(\Delta t/3)^{2}}\;\geq\;R\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|\Delta|\,t<3\sqrt{\frac{1}{R}-1}.

(B61)

This sets a quantitative requirement on how close to resonance the system must
be kept for a given interrogation time
t
in order to retain a target fraction

R
of the physical QSL speed.

For example, using (
B61
), one finds

Retention

Tolerance condition on
|\Delta|\,t

|\Delta|\,t<1.00

valid in the regime
|\Delta|\,t\lesssim 1
. These relations can be used to
translate metrological design targets (e.g., “retain at least

of the

Appendix C: Open Jaynes–Cummings sensor — dispersive lossy case

In this section, we embed the projected speed-limit formalism of Appendix
Appendix A: Detailed formalism — Bures geometry, Schur complement, and GKSL dynamics
into a
realistic open Jaynes–Cummings (JC) sensor operating in the dispersive regime.
We (i) specify the full atom–cavity Hamiltonian with Markovian dissipation;
(ii) carry out a Schrieffer–Wolff (dispersive) transformation of both the
Hamiltonian and the Lindblad operators, making all approximations explicit;
(iii) derive an effective master equation for a truncated cavity qubit with a
field-dependent frequency and Purcell-enhanced decay rate; (iv) solve this
reduced dynamics exactly in Bloch form; (v) compute the quantum Fisher
information (QFI) for laboratory time
t
and field
B
, together with the
mixed element
F_{tB}
; and (vi) build the Schur-complement effective
information
F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
and the corresponding projected
(quotient) QSL.

Throughout this Appendix we work in units
\hbar=1
.

C.1
Full JC model with Markovian dissipation

We consider a two-level atom (or qubit) with ground and excited states

\ket{g}
and
\ket{e}
, coupled to a single cavity mode truncated to the
lowest two Fock states
\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
. The total Hilbert space is

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\text{cav}}^{(2)}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\text{atom}}=\operatorname{span}\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}\otimes\operatorname{span}\{\ket{g},\ket{e}\}.

(C1)

On the truncated cavity space we use

\tilde{a}=\ket{0}\!\bra{1},\qquad\tilde{a}^{\dagger}=\ket{1}\!\bra{0},\qquad\tilde{a}^{\dagger}\tilde{a}=\ket{1}\!\bra{1}.

(C2)

The atomic operators are the usual Pauli matrices

\sigma_{z}=\ket{e}\bra{e}-\ket{g}\bra{g}
and

\sigma_{+}=\ket{e}\bra{g}
,
\sigma_{-}=\ket{g}\bra{e}
.

The laboratory-frame Hamiltonian in the rotating-wave approximation is

H=\omega_{c}\,\tilde{a}^{\dagger}\tilde{a}+\frac{\omega^{\prime}_{a}(B)}{2}\sigma_{z}+g\bigl(\sigma_{+}\tilde{a}+\sigma_{-}\tilde{a}^{\dagger}\bigr),

(C3)

with cavity frequency
\omega_{c}
, atom–cavity coupling
g
, and a
field-dependent atomic transition frequency

\omega^{\prime}_{a}(B)=\omega_{a}+\eta B,

(C4)

where
\eta
is a Zeeman coefficient (e.g.
\eta=2\mu_{B}
in suitable
units). The magnetic field
B
is the sensing parameter.

Dissipation is described by a GKSL generator with three Markovian channels:

\displaystyle L_{\kappa}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\kappa}\,\tilde{a}

\displaystyle\text{(cavity loss)},

\displaystyle L_{\gamma_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma_{1}}\,\sigma_{-}

\displaystyle\text{(atomic relaxation)},

\displaystyle L_{\gamma_{\varphi}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma_{\varphi}/2}\,\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle\text{(atomic dephasing)}.

(C5)

The full master equation is

\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]+\kappa\,\mathcal{D}[\tilde{a}]\rho+\gamma_{1}\,\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{-}]\rho+\gamma_{\varphi}\,\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{z}]\rho,

(C6)

where the dissipator is

\mathcal{D}[L]\rho:=L\rho L^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,\rho\}.

(C7)

This is of the GKSL form

\dot{\rho}=\mathcal{L}_{B}[\rho]
used in Appendix
Appendix A: Detailed formalism — Bures geometry, Schur complement, and GKSL dynamics
, with
B
the nuisance parameter
and
t
the laboratory time.

We work in the dispersive regime

|\Delta(B)|\gg g,\qquad\Delta(B):=\omega_{c}-\omega^{\prime}_{a}(B),

(C8)

and keep terms up to second order in the small parameter

\lambda:=g/\Delta(B)
when performing the dispersive transformation.

C.2
Dispersive (Schrieffer–Wolff) transformation

To eliminate the transverse coupling and expose the dispersive shift and
Purcell effect, we implement a Schrieffer–Wolff transformation generated by

S=\lambda\bigl(\sigma_{+}\tilde{a}-\sigma_{-}\tilde{a}^{\dagger}\bigr),\qquad\lambda=\frac{g}{\Delta(B)}.

(C9)

The unitary transformation is
T=e^{S}
. The transformed Hamiltonian is

H^{\prime}=e^{-S}He^{S}\approx H+[H,S]+\frac{1}{2}[[H,S],S],

(C10)

where we keep terms up to
O(\lambda^{2})
.

A straightforward but standard calculation (see e.g.
10
;
18
)
gives the dispersive Hamiltonian

H^{\prime}=\omega_{c}\,\tilde{a}^{\dagger}\tilde{a}+\frac{1}{2}\bigl[\omega^{\prime}_{a}(B)+\chi(B)\bigr]\sigma_{z}+\chi(B)\,\sigma_{z}\tilde{a}^{\dagger}\tilde{a},

(C11)

with the AC Stark (dispersive) shift

\chi(B)=\frac{g^{2}}{\Delta(B)}.

(C12)

Equation (
C11
) removes the transverse coupling to
leading order, replacing it with a state-dependent cavity frequency and a
state-dependent atomic frequency.

C.3
Polaron-transformed dissipators and Purcell channel

The dispersive transformation must also be applied to the Lindblad operators.
For any system operator
L
, we define

L^{\prime}:=T^{\dagger}LT\approx L+[L,S]+O(\lambda^{2}).

(C13)

Retaining terms to first order in
\lambda
gives the leading corrections
generated by the atom–cavity coupling inside the dissipators.

Cavity loss.

For
L_{\kappa}=\sqrt{\kappa}\,\tilde{a}
,

\displaystyle[L_{\kappa},S]

\displaystyle=\bigl[\sqrt{\kappa}\,\tilde{a},\,\lambda(\sigma_{+}\tilde{a}-\sigma_{-}\tilde{a}^{\dagger})\bigr]

\displaystyle=-\,\lambda\sqrt{\kappa}\,\sigma_{-}[\tilde{a},\tilde{a}^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=-\,\lambda\sqrt{\kappa}\,\sigma_{-}.

(C14)

Thus

L_{\kappa}^{\prime}\approx\sqrt{\kappa}\bigl(\tilde{a}+\lambda\sigma_{-}\bigr).

(C15)

Physically, cavity loss now induces an additional decay path for the atom.

Atomic relaxation.

For
L_{\gamma_{1}}=\sqrt{\gamma_{1}}\,\sigma_{-}
,

\displaystyle[L_{\gamma_{1}},S]

\displaystyle=\bigl[\sqrt{\gamma_{1}}\,\sigma_{-},\,\lambda(\sigma_{+}\tilde{a}-\sigma_{-}\tilde{a}^{\dagger})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma_{1}}\lambda\,[\sigma_{-},\sigma_{+}]\,\tilde{a}

\displaystyle=-\,\sqrt{\gamma_{1}}\lambda\,\sigma_{z}\tilde{a}.

(C16)

Hence

L_{\gamma_{1}}^{\prime}\approx\sqrt{\gamma_{1}}\bigl(\sigma_{-}-\lambda\sigma_{z}\tilde{a}\bigr),

(C17)

which couples the atomic relaxation channel to the cavity field.

Atomic dephasing.

For
L_{\gamma_{\varphi}}=\sqrt{\gamma_{\varphi}/2}\,\sigma_{z}
,

\displaystyle[L_{\gamma_{\varphi}},S]

\displaystyle=\Bigl[\sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma_{\varphi}}{2}}\,\sigma_{z},\,\lambda(\sigma_{+}\tilde{a}-\sigma_{-}\tilde{a}^{\dagger})\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma_{\varphi}}{2}}\,\lambda\Bigl([\sigma_{z},\sigma_{+}]\,\tilde{a}-[\sigma_{z},\sigma_{-}]\,\tilde{a}^{\dagger}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma_{\varphi}}{2}}\,\lambda\Bigl(2\sigma_{+}\tilde{a}+2\sigma_{-}\tilde{a}^{\dagger}\Bigr).

(C18)

Thus

L_{\gamma_{\varphi}}^{\prime}\approx\sqrt{\gamma_{\varphi}/2}\Bigl(\sigma_{z}-2\lambda(\sigma_{+}\tilde{a}+\sigma_{-}\tilde{a}^{\dagger})\Bigr),

(C19)

which generates dephasing-induced excitation exchange between atom and cavity.

Approximation hierarchy.

A fully rigorous treatment would keep all first-order corrections in
(
C15
), (
C17
),
(
C19
) inside the dissipators

\mathcal{D}[L_{\kappa}^{\prime}]
,
\mathcal{D}[L_{\gamma_{1}}^{\prime}]
,

\mathcal{D}[L_{\gamma_{\varphi}}^{\prime}]
. In many dispersive experiments, however,
one operates in a parameter regime where

\kappa\lambda^{2}\ll\gamma_{1},\qquad\gamma_{\varphi}\lambda^{2}\ll\gamma_{1},

(C20)

so that the cavity-induced corrections to the
\kappa
and
\gamma_{\varphi}

channels are negligible compared to the dominant atomic relaxation. In this
case one can safely neglect the
\lambda
corrections in
L_{\kappa}^{\prime}
and

L_{\gamma_{\varphi}}^{\prime}
, while keeping the leading
O(\lambda^{2})
Purcell term
generated by
L_{\gamma_{1}}^{\prime}
.

Concretely, inserting
L_{\gamma_{1}}^{\prime}
into
\mathcal{D}[L_{\gamma_{1}}^{\prime}]
gives

\mathcal{D}[L_{\gamma_{1}}^{\prime}]\rho^{\prime}=\gamma_{1}\,\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{-}]\rho^{\prime}-\gamma_{1}\lambda\,\mathcal{K}[\sigma_{-},\sigma_{z}\tilde{a}]\,\rho^{\prime}+\gamma_{1}\lambda^{2}\,\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{z}\tilde{a}]\,\rho^{\prime},

(C21)

where
\mathcal{K}
denotes the interference superoperator. Under the usual
assumption that the interference term averages out in observables (e.g. on
timescales relevant for cavity dynamics), the dominant correction is the
second-order term, which yields an additional decay channel for the cavity via
atomic relaxation: the Purcell effect.

Within these approximations, the transformed master equation reads

\begin{split}\dot{\rho}^{\prime}&\approx-i[H^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}]+\kappa\,\mathcal{D}[\tilde{a}]\rho^{\prime}+\gamma_{1}\,\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{-}]\rho^{\prime}\\
&\quad\;\;+\gamma_{1}\lambda^{2}\,\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{z}\tilde{a}]\rho^{\prime}+\gamma_{\varphi}\,\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{z}]\rho^{\prime},\end{split}

(C22)

with
H^{\prime}
as in (
C11
).

C.4
Reduced cavity master equation and effective parameters

We now trace out the atomic degree of freedom under the assumption that the
atom relaxes rapidly to a diagonal state with inversion

\langle\sigma_{z}\rangle=p_{e}-p_{g},\qquad p_{e}+p_{g}=1,

(C23)

on the timescale of interest for the cavity dynamics. This is justified when

\gamma_{1}
is large compared to the effective cavity decay rate.

The coherent part of (
C22
) gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{atom}}\bigl(-i[H^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}]\bigr)

\displaystyle=-i\bigl[(\omega_{c}+\chi(B)\langle\sigma_{z}\rangle)\tilde{a}^{\dagger}\tilde{a},\rho_{\text{cav}}\bigr],

(C24)

where
\rho_{\text{cav}}:=\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{atom}}\rho^{\prime}
is the reduced cavity
state. Thus the cavity resonance frequency is shifted to

\omega_{c}^{\prime}(B):=\omega_{c}+\chi(B)\,\langle\sigma_{z}\rangle.

(C25)

The cavity loss channel
\kappa\,\mathcal{D}[\tilde{a}]\rho^{\prime}
reduces to the
same form on the cavity. The Purcell term
\gamma_{1}\lambda^{2}\,\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{z}\tilde{a}]\rho^{\prime}

contributes an additional cavity dissipator
\propto\mathcal{D}[\tilde{a}]

after tracing out the atom and inserting the inversion. Collecting these
contributions, one arrives at the reduced cavity master equation

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\text{cav}}

\displaystyle=-i\bigl[\omega_{c}^{\prime}(B)\,\tilde{a}^{\dagger}\tilde{a},\rho_{\text{cav}}\bigr]

(C26)

\displaystyle+\kappa_{\text{eff}}(B)\,\mathcal{D}[\tilde{a}]\rho_{\text{cav}},

with the field-dependent effective decay rate

\kappa_{\text{eff}}(B)=\kappa+\gamma_{1}\left(\frac{g}{\Delta(B)}\right)^{2}.

(C27)

The first term is the bare cavity loss, while the second is the Purcell
contribution arising from atomic relaxation in the dispersive regime.

Equations (
C25
) and (
C27
) provide
the
B
-dependence of the GKSL data
H(B)
and
F_{k}(B)
required to compute
the QFIM blocks along the trajectory, in the notation of Appendix
Appendix A: Detailed formalism — Bures geometry, Schur complement, and GKSL dynamics
.

C.5
Cavity qubit dynamics and Bloch-vector representation

We restrict the cavity to the
\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
subspace and prepare the
initial pure state

\ket{\psi_0}=\cos\theta\,\ket{0}+\sin\theta\,\ket{1},

(C28)

with
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
. After tracing out the atom, the initial cavity
density matrix is

\rho_{\text{cav}}(0)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos^{2}\theta&\cos\theta\sin\theta\\
\cos\theta\sin\theta&\sin^{2}\theta\end{pmatrix},

(C29)

in the ordered basis
\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
.

The master equation (
C26
) is that of a qubit subject to
a phase rotation at frequency
\omega_{c}^{\prime}(B)
and amplitude damping at rate

\kappa_{\text{eff}}(B)
. Its exact solution is well-known and reads

\rho_{\text{cav}}(t;B)=\begin{pmatrix}1-\sin^{2}\theta\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}t}&\cos\theta\,\sin\theta\,e^{i\omega_{c}^{\prime}t}\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}t/2}\\
\cos\theta\,\sin\theta\,e^{-i\omega_{c}^{\prime}t}\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}t/2}&\sin^{2}\theta\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}t}\end{pmatrix},

(C30)

where, for brevity, we write
\omega_{c}^{\prime}=\omega_{c}^{\prime}(B)
and

\kappa_{\text{eff}}=\kappa_{\text{eff}}(B)
.

Any qubit state can be written as

\rho_{\text{cav}}(t;B)=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\mathbb{I}+\vec{S}(t;B)\cdot\vec{\sigma}\Bigr],

(C31)

with Pauli vector
\vec{\sigma}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
and Bloch vector

\vec{S}(t;B)=(S_{x},S_{y},S_{z})
. From (
C30
) we obtain

\displaystyle S_{x}(t;B)

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\rho_{01}(t;B)=\sin 2\theta\,\cos(\omega_c' t)\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}t/2},

(C32)

\displaystyle S_{y}(t;B)

\displaystyle=-2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\rho_{01}(t;B)=-\sin 2\theta\,\sin(\omega_c' t)\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}t/2},

\displaystyle S_{z}(t;B)

\displaystyle=\rho_{11}(t;B)-\rho_{00}(t;B)=2\sin^{2}\theta\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}t}-1.

The time derivatives are

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{x}(t;B)

\displaystyle=-\sin 2\theta\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}t/2}\Bigl[\omega_{c}^{\prime}\sin(\omega_c' t)+\tfrac{\kappa_{\text{eff}}}{2}\cos(\omega_c' t)\Bigr],

(C33)

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{y}(t;B)

\displaystyle=-\sin 2\theta\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}t/2}\Bigl[\omega_{c}^{\prime}\cos(\omega_c' t)-\tfrac{\kappa_{\text{eff}}}{2}\sin(\omega_c' t)\Bigr],

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{z}(t;B)

\displaystyle=-2\kappa_{\text{eff}}\sin^{2}\theta\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}t}.

We will also need the squared norm and dot product

|\dot{\vec{S}}(t;B)|^{2}=\dot{S}_{x}^{2}+\dot{S}_{y}^{2}+\dot{S}_{z}^{2},\qquad\vec{S}\cdot\dot{\vec{S}}=S_{x}\dot{S}_{x}+S_{y}\dot{S}_{y}+S_{z}\dot{S}_{z},

(C34)

and

|\vec{S}(t;B)|^{2}=S_{x}^{2}+S_{y}^{2}+S_{z}^{2}.

(C35)

C.6
Fidelity between initial and time-evolved cavity states

The initial cavity state is pure,
\rho_{0}=\ket{\psi_0}\bra{\psi_0}
with

\ket{\psi_0}
given by (
C28
). The fidelity between
\rho_{0}

and
\rho_{\text{cav}}(t;B)
is therefore

F(\rho_{0},\rho_{\text{cav}}(t;B))=\bra{\psi_0}\rho_{\text{cav}}(t;B)\ket{\psi_0}.

(C36)

Writing
\ket{\psi_0}=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^{\top}
in the

\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
basis and inserting (
C30
), one finds

\displaystyle F(\rho_{0},\rho_{\text{cav}}(t;B))

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}\theta\,\rho_{00}(t;B)+\sin^{2}\theta\,\rho_{11}(t;B)+2\cos\theta\sin\theta\,\mathrm{Re}\,\rho_{01}(t;B)

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}\theta\Bigl[1-\sin^{2}\theta\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}t}\Bigr]+\sin^{4}\theta\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}t}

\displaystyle\quad+2\cos^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\theta\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}t/2}\cos(\omega_c' t).

(C37)

Collecting terms,

\displaystyle F(\rho_{0},\rho_{\text{cav}}(t;B))

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{4}\theta\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}t}-\cos^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\theta\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}t}

(C38)

\displaystyle+2\cos^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\theta\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}t/2}\cos(\omega_c' t).

This expression will enter the numerator of the projected QSL.

C.7
QFI for time and field; mixed element

For a general mixed qubit state with Bloch vector
\vec{S}(x)
depending on a
real parameter
x
, the SLD QFI is given by the standard formula

F_{Q}(x)=\bigl|\partial_{x}\vec{S}\bigr|^{2}+\frac{\bigl(\vec{S}\cdot\partial_{x}\vec{S}\bigr)^{2}}{1-|\vec{S}|^{2}},\qquad|\vec{S}|<1,

(C39)

with the pure-state limit obtained by continuity as
|\vec{S}|\to 1
.

Time QFI.

Taking
x=t
with
B
fixed, we write

F_{tt}(t;B):=F_{Q}(t)=|\dot{\vec{S}}(t;B)|^{2}+\frac{\bigl[\vec{S}(t;B)\cdot\dot{\vec{S}}(t;B)\bigr]^{2}}{1-|\vec{S}(t;B)|^{2}},

(C40)

with
\vec{S}
and
\dot{\vec{S}}
given by
(
C32
)–(
C33
). This is the
physical Bures speed squared (up to the factor
1/4
) along the open-system
trajectory for fixed field.

Field QFI.

The field enters through the effective frequency
\omega_{c}^{\prime}(B)
and decay
rate
\kappa_{\text{eff}}(B)
. It is convenient to denote

\Omega(B):=\omega_{c}^{\prime}(B),\qquad\Gamma(B):=\kappa_{\text{eff}}(B),

(C41)

and their derivatives

\Omega^{\prime}(B):=\partial_{B}\Omega(B),\qquad\Gamma^{\prime}(B):=\partial_{B}\Gamma(B).

(C42)

From (
C25
) and (
C27
),

\displaystyle\Omega^{\prime}(B)

\displaystyle=\chi^{\prime}(B)\,\langle\sigma_{z}\rangle=\langle\sigma_{z}\rangle\,\partial_{B}\Bigl(\frac{g^{2}}{\Delta(B)}\Bigr)=-\,\langle\sigma_{z}\rangle\,\frac{g^{2}\Delta^{\prime}(B)}{\Delta(B)^{2}},

(C43)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\prime}(B)

\displaystyle=\partial_{B}\Bigl[\kappa+\gamma_{1}\Bigl(\frac{g}{\Delta(B)}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr]=-\,2\gamma_{1}\,\frac{g^{2}\Delta^{\prime}(B)}{\Delta(B)^{3}},

(C44)

with
\Delta^{\prime}(B)=-\omega^{\prime}_{a}{}^{\prime}(B)
from (
C4
).

Differentiating (
C32
) with respect to
B
yields

\displaystyle\partial_{B}S_{x}

\displaystyle=-\sin 2\theta\,e^{-\Gamma t/2}\Bigl[t\,\Omega^{\prime}\sin(\Omega t)+\tfrac{t}{2}\Gamma^{\prime}\cos(\Omega t)\Bigr],

(C45)

\displaystyle\partial_{B}S_{y}

\displaystyle=-\sin 2\theta\,e^{-\Gamma t/2}\Bigl[t\,\Omega^{\prime}\cos(\Omega t)-\tfrac{t}{2}\Gamma^{\prime}\sin(\Omega t)\Bigr],

\displaystyle\partial_{B}S_{z}

\displaystyle=-2\sin^{2}\theta\,t\,\Gamma^{\prime}\,e^{-\Gamma t},

where again
\Omega=\Omega(B)
and
\Gamma=\Gamma(B)
.

The field QFI is then

F_{BB}(t;B):=F_{Q}(B;t)=|\partial_{B}\vec{S}(t;B)|^{2}+\frac{\bigl[\vec{S}(t;B)\cdot\partial_{B}\vec{S}(t;B)\bigr]^{2}}{1-|\vec{S}(t;B)|^{2}},

(C46)

with
|\partial_{B}\vec{S}|^{2}
and
\vec{S}\cdot\partial_{B}\vec{S}
evaluated using
(
C32
) and (
C45
). This is the
nuisance information needed in the Schur complement.

Mixed element.

The mixed QFI element
F_{tB}
can be written in Bloch form as

F_{tB}(t;B)=\dot{\vec{S}}(t;B)\cdot\partial_{B}\vec{S}(t;B)+\frac{\bigl[\vec{S}(t;B)\cdot\dot{\vec{S}}(t;B)\bigr]\bigl[\vec{S}(t;B)\cdot\partial_{B}\vec{S}(t;B)\bigr]}{1-|\vec{S}(t;B)|^{2}}.

(C47)

Equations (
C40
), (
C46
), and
(
C47
) provide the full
2\times 2
QFIM in the parameter pair

(t,B)
for the open, dispersive JC sensor.

C.8
Schur-complement information and projected QSL

Treating
t
as the parameter of interest and
B
as a nuisance, the effective
information for time is given by the one-dimensional Schur complement

F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t;B)=F_{tt}(t;B)-\frac{F_{tB}(t;B)^{2}}{F_{BB}(t;B)},

(C48)

with the understanding that
F_{BB}^{-1}
is interpreted as the Moore–Penrose
pseudoinverse (and
F_{\mathrm{eff}}=F_{tt}
) whenever
F_{BB}=0
.
By positive semidefiniteness of the QFIM,
F_{\mathrm{eff}}\geq 0
, and

F_{\mathrm{eff}}\leq F_{tt}
with equality if and only if
F_{tB}=0
.

The instantaneous projected (quotient) speed is

v_{\mathrm{quo}}(t;B)=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t;B)},

(C49)

and its time average over the interrogation interval
[0,\tau]
is

\bar{v}_{\mathrm{quo}}(B)=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{eff}}(t;B)}\,dt.

(C50)

For a fixed field
B
, this reduces to the standard QSL denominator, but with
the profiled speed rather than the physical one.

Because
B
is a nuisance parameter that may be re-tuned between experimental
runs within an admissible set
\mathcal{B}
, the numerator must live on the
quotient manifold in which states that differ only by a change in
B
are
identified. The relevant Bures angle is therefore

\Theta_{\mathrm{quo}}(\tau):=\arccos\!\Bigl(\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\sqrt{F(\rho_{0},\rho_{\text{cav}}(\tau;B))}\Bigr),

(C51)

where
F(\rho_{0},\rho_{\text{cav}}(\tau;B))
is given explicitly in
(
C38
).

The quotient angle is obtained by optimizing the explicit fidelity
(
C38
) over the admissible calibration window

\mathcal{B}
. Define

\displaystyle F(B;\tau):=

\displaystyle F\!\bigl(\rho_{0},\rho_{\mathrm{cav}}(\tau;B)\bigr)

(C52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{4}\theta\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}(B)\tau}-\cos^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\theta\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}(B)\tau}

\displaystyle+2\cos^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\theta\,e^{-\kappa_{\text{eff}}(B)\tau/2}\cos\!\bigl(\omega_{c}^{\prime}(B)\tau\bigr).

Then Eq. (
C51
) becomes

\Theta_{\mathrm{quo}}(\tau)=\arccos\!\left(\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\sqrt{F(B;\tau)}\right).

(C53)

Combining the quotient distance and the projected speed, the nuisance-profiled
QSL for a fixed realized field value
B
in the open JC sensor reads

\tau\;\geq\;\frac{\Theta_{\mathrm{quo}}(\tau)}{\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{F_{tt}(t;B)-\frac{F_{tB}(t;B)^{2}}{F_{BB}(t;B)}}\,dt},

(C54)

with
F_{tt}
,
F_{BB}
, and
F_{tB}
given by
(
C40
), (
C46
), and
(
C47
), and
\Theta_{\mathrm{quo}}(\tau)
given by
(
C53
).

If the dynamics is insensitive to
B
over
the admissible range (e.g.
\Omega^{\prime}(B)\equiv 0
and
\Gamma^{\prime}(B)\equiv 0
so
that
\partial_{B}\vec{S}\equiv 0
), then
F_{BB}=F_{tB}=0
and

F_{\mathrm{eff}}=F_{tt}
. The quotient angle reduces to the physical Bures
angle
L(\rho_{0},\rho_{\text{cav}}(\tau;B))
for any
B
, and
(
C54
) collapses to the standard open-system QSL. If
F_{tB}\equiv 0
while
F_{BB}>0
, then
F_{\mathrm{eff}}=F_{tt}
, so the
projected and physical QSL denominators coincide for the same realized field
value
B_{0}
. The numerator remains profiled via

\Theta_{\mathrm{quo}}(\tau)\leq\arccos\sqrt{F(\rho_{0},\rho_{\text{cav}}(\tau;B_{0}))}
, because the quotient angle
is obtained by maximizing the fidelity over the admissible calibration window
before applying the decreasing function
\arccos
. The projected QSL lower bound
is therefore weaker than or equal to the corresponding physical QSL lower bound. In the limit
\kappa_{\text{eff}}\tau\ll 1
,
\Gamma^{\prime}(B)\tau\ll 1
, and with detuning tolerance chosen such that the coherent dynamics dominates over decay, the open-system QFIM and projected QSL smoothly approach their closed-system counterparts of Appendix
Appendix B: Closed Jaynes–Cummings sensor — unitary projected QSL
)
