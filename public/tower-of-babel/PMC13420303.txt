Acute Exacerbation of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease Immediately After Extubation Following Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy: A Case Report
Authors: Mengyue Gu, Sen Yan, Ruiliang Wang, Fujun Zhang, Tianyao Zhang, Hui Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn patients with moderate‐to‐severe COPD, short‐term (1‐day) preoperative respiratory optimization may be insufficient to prevent acute exacerbation after extubation. Prolonged preoperative preparation, meticulous intraoperative lung‐protective ventilation, and a post‐extubation rapid‐response protocol are essential.Keywords: anesthesia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, extubation complication, postoperative acute exacerbation, respiratory medicine

Introduction

Extubation during recovery from general anesthesia is a critical phase of perioperative airway management and poses significant challenges for patients with comorbid chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). These patients are at a significantly increased risk of postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) [1], a risk positively correlated with the severity of airflow limitation [2]. Notably, even asymptomatic COPD patients face a higher PPC risk compared to the general population [3]. In clinical practice, the perioperative risk of patients with objectively identified moderate‐to‐severe lung impairment is often underestimated due to symptom tolerance or inadequate intervention [4]. This article reports a case of a patient with newly diagnosed, untreated moderate COPD who developed an acute exacerbation immediately after extubation following prolonged percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL). The aim is to discuss the necessity of implementing predictive and intensified perioperative management strategies for such high‐risk populations.

History and Examination

A 70‐year‐old male was admitted with a 4‐day history of bilateral flank pain. His height was 160 cm, and weight was 66 kg. He had a 54‐pack‐year smoking history and had quit smoking 1 week prior to admission. He reported dyspnea on exertion but had never received a formal diagnosis or treatment. Past medical history included right hip arthroplasty, with no history of hypertension or diabetes.

Preoperative arterial blood gas analysis (room air) revealed pH 7.403, PaCO2 34.8 mmHg, and PaO2 68.1 mmHg, indicating hypoxemia. Pulmonary function tests showed: FEV1 1.64 L (69.3% predicted), FVC 2.25 L, FEV1/FVC ratio 72.59%. The conclusion was moderate obstructive ventilatory dysfunction, moderate‐to‐severe small airway disease, severe emphysema, and mildly reduced diffusing capacity. A bronchial dilation test was negative. Chest CT indicated mild emphysema with minor streaky opacities and micronodules in both lungs. Urinary tract CT confirmed multiple kidney stones with hydronephrosis. In light of the pulmonary function results, preoperative respiratory optimization was initiated 1 day before surgery, consisting of intravenous bromhexine 4 mg twice daily, combined with salbutamol 5 mg and beclomethasone dipropionate 0.8 mg nebulization twice daily for 1 day.

Management

The patient underwent PCNL under general anesthesia in the prone position, a position that may influence respiratory mechanics during urologic surgery [5]. Induction was achieved with ciprofol (a novel intravenous anesthetic) 15 mg, sufentanil 20 μg, rocuronium 50 mg, and midazolam 2 mg. Maintenance was with sevoflurane inhalation combined with remifentanil and propofol infusion. A lung‐protective ventilation strategy was employed, with a tidal volume of 450 mL, PEEP of 5 cm H2O, and manual lung recruitment maneuvers performed every 2 h. The surgery lasted 4 h and 25 min, with stable intraoperative vital signs. Intraoperative blood gas monitoring at 1 h and 25 min after surgery start showed: pH 7.314, PaCO2 42.9 mmHg, PaO2 156.1 mmHg (FiO2 50%). Five minutes before the end of surgery, values were: pH 7.270, PaCO2 42.9 mmHg, PaO2 169.1 mmHg (FiO2 50%), suggesting a trend toward respiratory acidosis, although hypercapnia remained stable.

After completion of surgery, airway secretions were suctioned repeatedly. The patient was observed until consciousness and spontaneous respiration had recovered adequately, with tidal volume greater than 300 mL. After a 10‐min observation period, the endotracheal tube was removed. Two minutes after extubation, the patient complained of thick sputum that was difficult to expectorate. Auscultation revealed diffuse wheezing in both lungs. Monitoring showed a rapid decrease in SpO2 to 87%, heart rate increased to 131 bpm, blood pressure surged to 222/108 mmHg, and respiratory rate was 22 breaths per minute.

Emergency management was initiated immediately: high‐flow oxygen via face mask and repeated airway suction, which yielded a significant amount of thick, white sputum. Concurrently, intravenous methylprednisolone 40 mg and doxofylline 0.2 g were administered slowly; esmolol 10 mg and urapidil 10 mg were given intravenously to control the elevated heart rate and blood pressure. Approximately 30 min after this combined intervention, the patient's clinical condition improved markedly: SpO2 increased to 97%, wheezing significantly diminished, and heart rate and blood pressure gradually decreased and stabilized.

The patient was transferred to the general ward for continued treatment, receiving intravenous bromhexine 4 mg twice daily, combined with salbutamol 5 mg and beclomethasone dipropionate 0.8 mg nebulization twice daily. Respiratory symptoms resolved completely within 4 h postoperatively. Repeat arterial blood gas analysis on room air at 18 h postoperatively showed pH 7.364, PaCO2 31.4 mmHg, and PaO2 69.9 mmHg (Table 1).

Outcome

The patient's condition was rapidly reversed through timely airway clearance, targeted bronchodilation and anti‐inflammatory therapy, and swift cardiovascular intervention without reintubation or escalation to noninvasive ventilation. This case suggests that for patients with established moderate‐to‐severe COPD, preoperative preparation with mucolytics alone may be insufficient to cope with major surgical stress, necessitating an individualized management plan involving more aggressive pharmacotherapy, refined ventilation management, and close post‐extubation monitoring.

Discussion

The main novelty of this case is not the occurrence of respiratory difficulty after extubation itself, which is a recognized risk in COPD, but the combination of three features: acute deterioration occurred immediately after extubation despite preoperative recognition of COPD risk and initiation of therapy; the episode was accompanied by a marked hypertensive and tachycardic response; and the patient improved after a rapid, stepwise multimodal intervention rather than a single airway maneuver. These features make the case clinically useful for perioperative teams caring for surgical patients with newly identified COPD.

One important lesson is that starting respiratory treatment 1 day before surgery should not be considered equivalent to achieving preoperative optimization. This patient had several risk factors that could not be reversed quickly, including advanced age, a long smoking history, emphysema, impaired small‐airway function, and room‐air hypoxemia. Smoking increases perioperative respiratory risk and impairs mucociliary clearance, which may promote postoperative secretion retention [4, 6]. In such patients, a short course of mucolytic, bronchodilator, and inhaled corticosteroid therapy may reduce secretions and bronchial reactivity, but GOLD‐based COPD management principles emphasize sustained disease control rather than pharmacologic intervention immediately before a major surgery [7].

The postoperative event was considered more consistent with acute exacerbation of COPD than with isolated bronchospasm or simple secretion obstruction. An AECOPD is generally defined as an acute worsening of respiratory symptoms that require additional therapy [8, 9]. Thick sputum and airway narrowing contributed to the presentation, but the patient also had established COPD pathology, preoperative hypoxemia, intraoperative respiratory acidosis, diffuse bilateral wheezing, and a need for systemic corticosteroid and bronchodilator therapy with continued postoperative respiratory treatment. Systemic corticosteroids and bronchodilators are standard components of AECOPD treatment, and inhaled corticosteroid‐containing combination therapy has been used to reduce exacerbation risk in selected COPD patients [9, 10, 11]. These findings suggest broader COPD decompensation triggered by surgery, anesthesia, airway stimulation, and secretion retention.

Severe hypertension and tachycardia can be understood as a stress response to acute respiratory compromise, driven by hypoxemia‐ and hypercapnia‐induced sympathetic stimulation, catecholamine surge from extubation, and increased work of breathing and anxiety due to respiratory distress. In addition, beta2‐agonist therapy may contribute to tachycardia in susceptible patients. Short‐acting agents such as esmolol and urapidil were therefore selected to control heart rate and blood pressure while the airway and pulmonary causes were being treated.

For similar high‐risk patients, perioperative planning should include earlier pulmonary optimization when surgery can be delayed, careful intraoperative monitoring of ventilation and blood gas trends, active secretion management, and a structured extubation plan [7, 12, 13]. After extubation, close observation in a monitored setting is essential, and teams should be prepared to escalate from oxygen therapy and medications to high‐flow nasal cannula, noninvasive ventilation, or reintubation if respiratory failure progresses; preventive oxygen‐support strategies after planned extubation may reduce extubation failure in high‐risk patients [14].

Conclusion

This case exemplifies a perioperative crisis resulting from the convergence of multiple risk factors: advanced age, prolonged surgery, preoperative hypoxemia, and insufficient duration of preoperative optimization. It demonstrates that for elderly surgical patients with COPD, even preoperative preparation cannot completely prevent serious complications. Therefore, a reinforced management pathway is essential: preoperatively, early and thorough assessment followed by initiating standardized optimization (e.g., combined bronchodilators and anti‐inflammatory drugs) sufficiently in advance of surgery, rather than immediate preoperative initiation; intraoperatively, implementing strictly individualized lung‐protective ventilation for patients with underlying conditions like emphysema [12, 13]; post‐extubation, preparing a rapid‐response protocol ranging from medications to respiratory support (e.g., NIV/HFNC) for high‐risk patients [14]. Collaboration among anesthesiologists, surgeons, and pulmonologists is the cornerstone for building this safety net and ensuring patient recovery.
