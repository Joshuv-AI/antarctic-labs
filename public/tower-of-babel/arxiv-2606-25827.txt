Paths and Intersections: Recognizing Outerplanar Metrics
Yu Chen, Zihan Tan

Abstract
We study the following distance realization problem: given a metric D D on a set T T of terminals, does there exist an (edge-weighted) outerplanar graph G G , such that T ⊆ V ⁡ ( G ) T\subseteq V(G) , and for every pair t , t ′ ∈ T t,t^{\prime}\in T , dist G ​ ( t , t ′ ) = D ⁡ ( t , t ′ ) \textnormal{dist}_{G}(t,t^{\prime})=D(t,t^{\prime}) ? We first prove that there is no “local characterization”, forming a contrast with trees and Okamura-Seymour instances. Our main result is an efficient algorithm for this problem whose running time is polynomial in | T | |T| . Both our proof and our algorithm utilize a recent new approach of analyzing graph structures, by viewing graphs as paths and their intersections, which we believe is of independent interest.

We study the following distance realization problem: given a metric

As a fundamental problem in metric graph theory, the distance realization problems have found numerous applications in computational biology

For trees and Okamura-Seymour instances (both undirected and directed), their distance realizability can be characterized by a

From the algorithmic side, the central question for the distance realization problem is: Given a family

In this paper, we study the distance realization problem for a family that “interpolates” the previously studied families: outerplanar graphs. It generalizes trees and cactus graphs, and falls within the scope of Okamura-Seymour instances. We give answers on both characterization and algorithm questions.

1.1
Our Results

Our first result shows that for the family of outerplanar graphs, there is no
O(1)
-point condition for characterizing its distance realizability.

Theorem 1
.

For every integer
k\geq 20
, there exists a metric
D
on
k
terminals, such that
(i)
D
is not outerplanar (that is,
D
is not realizable by any outerplanar graph); and
(ii) the restriction of
D
onto any proper subset of terminals is outerplanar.

Although there is no clean and simple characterization, our next (main) result shows that, fortunately, there is still an efficient algorithm for the distance realization problem on outerplanar graphs.

Theorem 2
.

There is an algorithm that, given a metric
D
on
k
terminals, correctly decides if there is an outerplanar graph realizing
D
, and constructs one if the answer is yes. Its running time is
O(k^{5})
.

Although this is an algorithmic result, along the way we have also proved some graph-theoretic results, which we believe are of independent interest. At a high level, the algorithm has two steps: constructing the graph and the shortest path structure, and setting the edge weights. For Step 2, the existence of proper edge weights can be characterized by an LP. We show that the feasibility of the LP, a seemingly numerical issue, is guaranteed by a simple structural condition satisfied by the shortest path structure in Step 1, which can be summarized as: “every pair of shortest paths required to be disjoint are indeed disjoint”. Essentially, both our proof and our algorithm follow a new way of analyzing graph structures introduced by recent work
[
9
,
20
]
, by viewing graphs as paths and their intersections, and we believe this approach will prove useful for more graph problems.

2
High-Level Overview

To refute the
O(1)
-point condition for the family of outerplanar graphs, we consider the shortest-path distance metric of the wheel graph: a graph consisting of a cycle and a center connecting to each cycle vertex by a distinct edge. This graph is only one-vertex (the center) away from being an outerplanar graph, but we show that to realize such a metric, such an obstacle cannot be cleared by outerplanarity.
The proof focuses on analyzing the intersecting pattern of shortest paths, and is presented in
Appendix

A
.

In the remainder of this section, we provide an overview of our algorithm in
Theorem

2
.

Step 1. Compute the circular ordering or terminals

Outerplanar graphs with terminals are also Okamura-Seymour instances by definition. From previous work
[
9
]
, there is an efficient algorithm that, given any Okamura-Seymour metric, determines the circular ordering in which the terminals lie on the boundary. We can also apply it here and obtain the circular ordering. As we do not know for certain whether or not the input metric is outerplanar (even if it is verified to be an Okamura-Seymour metric), such a step needs to be carried out cautiously. We present the details in
Section

3
.

Step 2. the “Repelling paths condition”

Now we have the circular ordering in hand, the next step is to construct the graph structure, namely (i) the outerplanar graph
G
, (ii) its edge weights; and (iii) for every pair
t,t^{\prime}
of terminals, a path designated as the
t
-
t^{\prime}
shortest path.
When we handle a shortest-path distance problem on graph, it is sometimes useful to view the graph as consisting of shortest paths and their intersections
[
9
,
20
]
. In this work, we adopt this approach, and therefore among the above three types of information in the graph structure, we focus on (iii): the shortest path structure.

Consider now four distinct terminals
a,c,b,d
that appear on the boundary in this order. An immediate observation is that, no matter how we arrange the
a
-
b
shortest path and the
c
-
d
shortest path, they must intersect in the graph. And for a pair of intersecting shortest paths, we have the following simple yet useful lemma, whose proof is straightforward and is deferred to
Appendix

B
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
G
be a graph and let
a,b,c,d
be vertices of
G
. If the
a
-
b
shortest path in
G
intersects the
c
-
d
shortest path in
G
, then

\textnormal{dist}_{G}(a,b)+\textnormal{dist}_{G}(c,d)\geq\textnormal{dist}_{G}(a,c)+\textnormal{dist}_{G}(b,d);\text{ and}

\textnormal{dist}_{G}(a,b)+\textnormal{dist}_{G}(c,d)\geq\textnormal{dist}_{G}(a,d)+\textnormal{dist}_{G}(b,c).

As graph
G
is expected to realize
D(\cdot,\cdot)
as its shortest-path distance metric
\textnormal{dist}_{G}(\cdot,\cdot)
, together with
Lemma

3
, they impose some constraints on the shortest path structure of
G
.
Specifically,
let
t_{1},t_{2},t^{\prime}_{1},t^{\prime}_{2}
be terminals in
T
, where
(t_{1},t_{2})
form a pair and
(t^{\prime}_{1},t^{\prime}_{2})
form a pair.
We say that these pairs are
repelling
iff
either
D(t_{1},t_{2})+D(t^{\prime}_{1},t^{\prime}_{2})<D(t_{1},t^{\prime}_{1})+D(t_{2},t^{\prime}_{2})
or
D(t_{1},t_{2})+D(t^{\prime}_{1},t^{\prime}_{2})<D(t_{1},t^{\prime}_{2})+D(t^{\prime}_{1},t_{2})
holds. From
Lemma

3
, we obtain the following necessary condition for the shortest path structure:

If
(t_{1},t_{2})
and
(t^{\prime}_{1},t^{\prime}_{2})
are repelling, then the
t_{1}
-
t_{2}
and the
t^{\prime}_{1}
-
t^{\prime}_{2}
shortest paths are disjoint.

We call this the “repelling paths condition”. Our main result in this step is that, this simple necessary condition is, surprisingly, also sufficient! Specifically, if our constructed shortest path structure satisfies that for all repelling pairs
(t_{1},t_{2}),(t^{\prime}_{1},t^{\prime}_{2})
, the
t_{1}
-
t_{2}
and the
t^{\prime}_{1}
-
t^{\prime}_{2}
shortest paths are disjoint, then there must exist edge weights realizing the designated shortest paths (as indeed the shortest paths between certain pairs of terminals in
G
, and such that their lengths match the values in
D
).
Therefore, instead of an
O(1)
-point condition, which certifies/refutes the metric realizability by checking small groups of values in
D
, we manage to prove an
O(1)
-path condition (actually a
2
-path condition), which validates a shortest path structure by checking the intersecting patterns of small groups of paths.

The proof characterizes the existence of edge weights by a distance LP, and then analyzes its feasibility via its dual, which is a flow LP. The existence of proper edge weights is proved to be equivalent to the non-existence of certain flows, which is then confirmed via graph-theoretic techniques. Along the way, we develop a technique of
path switching
for manipulating flows and prove a graph-theoretic result on uncrossing chords in a disc, which could be of independent interest.
We present this part in
Section

4
.

Step 3. Dynamic programming for achieving outerplanarity

Via standard techniques, we can show that every outerplanar graph can be massaged into a canonical one, where the boundary is a simple cycle partitioned by terminals into segments, each remaining edge connects a pair of distinct segments, and they do not share endpoints (see
Figure

1
).
Hence, the task is to find a canonical outerplanar graph together with a shortest path structure satisfying the repelling paths condition (or certify that none exists).

Figure 1:
A canonical outerplanar graph: terminals
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{k}
and segments
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
.

The advantage of a canonical graph is that
the shortest path between a pair of terminals is characterized by the sequence of segments it visits
.
As the shortest path between consecutive terminals
t_{r},t_{r+1}
must be their boundary segment
S_{r}
, for the
t_{i}
-
t_{j}
shortest path to be allowed to visit
S_{r}
, the pairs
(t_{r},t_{r+1})
and
(t_{i},t_{j})
may not repel each other. So an intermediate goal is to find an outerplanar graph together with a “sequence of segments” for each terminal pair such that all the segment-related repelling paths conditions hold. This can be done with a delicate dynamic programming algorithm, which is presented in
Section

5
.

It turns out that, if some “segment sequence structure” achieves the intermediate goal, then it can be easily converted into a real shortest path structure satisfying all repelling paths conditions. Intuitively, this is because among all repelling paths conditions, the strongest ones (in that they imply all others) are those segment-related ones.

3
Computing the Circular Ordering of Terminals

As shown in the previous work
[
8
,
18
]
, for a metric
D
on a set
T=\left\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\right\}
of terminals, there exists an Okamura-Seymour instance
(G,T)
that realizes
D
as the shortest-path distance metric on
T
, such that terminals
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}
appearing on the boundary in this order, iff the following
four-point condition
(called
Monge property
) holds for all tuples
1\leq a<b<c<d\leq k
:

\emph{D(t_{a},t_{c})+D(t_{b},t_{d})\geq\max\left\{D(t_{a},t_{b})+D(t_{c},t_{d}),D(t_{a},t_{d})+D(t_{b},t_{c})\right\}.}

Since outerplanar graphs with terminals are Okamura-Seymour instances, we can apply the algorithm in
[
9
]
to compute a circular ordering of terminals appearing on the boundary, such that the input metric
D
satisfies all four-point conditions or certify there are no such orderings. If the answer is
No
, then this means that
D
is not realizable by any Okamura-Seymour instances, and we report that
D
is not realizable by any outerplanar graphs.
However, if there are more than one circular orderings that satisfy all four-point conditions, then we are uncertain about which one we should choose to proceed with further steps.
In this section, we address this question by showing that any one will do.

One crucial feature in the algorithm of
[
9
]
is that, if there are more than one orderings that satisfy all four-point conditions, then the algorithm will produce a bi-partition of the terminals into two sets
S,T
(abusing the notation
T
here for a subset of terminals), such that the terminals of the same set must appear together on the boundary, and for any
s_{1},s_{2}\in S
and
t_{1},t_{2}\in T
,

D(s_{1},t_{1})+D(s_{2},t_{2})=D(s_{1},t_{2})+D(s_{2},t_{1}).

Denote
S=\left\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{p}\right\}
and
T=\left\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{q}\right\}
. We prove the following strengthening of this fact.

Claim 4
.

There exist non-negative real numbers
\left\{\alpha_{i}\right\}_{1\leq i\leq p}
and
\left\{\beta_{j}\right\}_{1\leq j\leq q}
, such that

•

for all
1\leq i\leq p
and
1\leq j\leq q
,
D(s_{i},t_{j})=\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}
;

•

for all
1\leq i,j\leq p
,
\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}\geq D(s_{i},s_{j})
; and

•

for all
1\leq i,j\leq q
,
\beta_{i}+\beta_{j}\geq D(t_{i},t_{j})
.

Proof.

Since for all
s_{i},s_{j}\in S
and all
t_{\ell},t_{r}\in T
,

D(s_{i},t_{\ell})+D(s_{j},t_{r})=D(s_{i},t_{r})+D(s_{j},t_{\ell})
.
This means that for all
s_{i},s_{j}\in S
, the value
D(s_{i},t_{\ell})-D(s_{j},t_{\ell})
is the same for all
t_{\ell}\in T
, which we denote by
\Delta_{i,j}
. Moreover,
\Delta_{i,j}+\Delta_{j,m}=\Delta_{i,m}
holds for all
i,j,m
, which means that there are real numbers
\left\{\alpha_{i}\right\}_{1\leq i\leq p}
and
\left\{\beta_{j}\right\}_{1\leq j\leq q}
such that for all
s_{i}\in S
and
t_{j}\in T
,
D(s_{i},t_{j})=\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}
, and they are unique up to
+{\varepsilon}
for all
\alpha_{i}
and
-{\varepsilon}
for all
\beta_{j}
simultaneously.

Let
\alpha_{i}
’s be the smallest values such that for all
s_{i},s_{j}\in S
,
\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}\geq D(s_{i},s_{j})
. This implies that there are
s_{i^{*}},s_{j^{*}}\in S
with
\alpha_{i^{*}}+\alpha_{j^{*}}=D(s_{i^{*}},s_{j^{*}})
. For every
t_{j}\in T
,
\beta_{j}=D(s_{1},t_{j})-\alpha_{1}
. We now show that for any
t_{i},t_{j}\in T
,
\beta_{i}+\beta_{j}\geq D(t_{i},t_{j})
, which completes the proof.

Assume for contradiction that
\beta_{i}+\beta_{j}<D(t_{i},t_{j})
. Consider terminals
s_{i^{*}},s_{j^{*}},t_{j},t_{i}
, and assume without loss of generality that they appear on the boundary in this order. By Monge property,

D(s_{i^{*}},s_{j^{*}})+D(t_{i},t_{j})\leq D(s_{i^{*}},t_{j})+D(s_{j^{*}},t_{j})=\alpha_{i^{*}}+\alpha_{j^{*}}+\beta_{i}+\beta_{j}.

However,
\alpha_{i^{*}}+\alpha_{j^{*}}=D(s_{i^{*}},s_{j^{*}})
and
\beta_{i}+\beta_{j}<D(t_{i},t_{j})
, a contradiction.
∎

With
4
, we can design a natural divide and conquer algorithm: add a new terminal
o
, augment
D
to a metric on

, where

•

for every
s_{i}\in S
,

;

•

for every

; and

•

for all

We then proceed with the induced metrics

and

. If both induced metrics are realizable by outerplanar graphs, then we simply stick these graphs together at

, disregarding

as a terminal. From the above discussion, the resulting graph is clearly an outerplanar graph realizing

on

.
The correctness of this divide and conquer algorithm is confirmed by the following claim.

Claim 5
.

If

is outerplanar, then both

Proof.

Let
G
be the outerplanar graph realizing

G_{S}
realizing
D_{\mid S\cup\{o\}}
and
G_{T}
realizing
D_{\mid T\cup\{o\}}
.
For all

and

, let

\textnormal{dist}(o_{i,j},s_{i})=\alpha_{i}

\textnormal{dist}(o_{i,j},t_{j})=\beta_{j}

We contract all points

into a supernode

.

]

Graph Structure: the Repelling Paths Condition

The construction of an outerplanar graph realizing the input metric
D
consists of two steps:

1.

constructing the graph and the shortest path structure, which requires specifying

•

the graph
G
;

•

for each pair
t,t^{\prime}
of terminals, a
t
-
t^{\prime}
path
P_{t,t^{\prime}}
in
G
, designated as their shortest path, such that the intersection between every pair of shortest paths is empty or a subpath of both;

2.

setting the edge weights of
G
, such that for every pair
t,t^{\prime}
of terminals,

•

P_{t,t^{\prime}}
is indeed the shortest
t
-
t^{\prime}
path in
G
; and

•

the length of

equals

.

Now imagine that we are given the graph

and the shortest path structure
{\mathcal{P}}=\left\{P_{t,t^{\prime}}\right\}_{t,t^{\prime}\in T}
produced by Step 1. In this section, we will show that
Step 2 can be accomplished iff the repelling paths condition holds for every pair of terminal pairs
. In fact, our result in this section holds for all Okamura-Seymour instances, and can be summarized as the following lemma.

.

For a metric
D
on
T
, an Okamura-Seymour instance
(G,T)
and a shortest path structure
{\mathcal{P}}=\left\{P_{t,t^{\prime}}\right\}_{t,t^{\prime}\in T}
, the following are equivalent:

•

There exist nonnegative edge lengths
\left\{\ell_{e}\right\}_{e\in E(G)}
, such that
for every pair
t,t^{\prime}
of terminals,

–

P_{t,t^{\prime}}
is indeed the shortest
t
-
t^{\prime}
path in
G
; and

–

•
