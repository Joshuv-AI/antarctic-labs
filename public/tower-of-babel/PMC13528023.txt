Low Panton–Valentine leukocidin prevalence in circulating community MRSA: a genomic surveillance study in Qatar
Authors: Ristha Kamar, Khaloud J. Alobaidli, Alyaa N. Al Nuaimi, Rasha Abu-El-Ruz, Mutassim A. Salih, Susu M. Zughaier, Asmaa A. Al Thani, Merin Alex, Khalid H. Elawad, Nahla O. Eltai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStaphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), particularly methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), is a major human pathogen causing severe infections. While well studied in hospitals, limited data exist on the transmission and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) profiles of community-associated MRSA (CA-MRSA) in the Gulf region. This study investigated the AMR patterns of CA-MRSA.MethodsNasal and axillary swabs from 576 asymptomatic residents of Qatar without a history of hospitalization, surgery, dialysis, or indwelling medical devices in the preceding 12 months, and those who have not used antibiotics within the previous 3 months were analyzed. S. aureus was isolated on mannitol salt agar, confirmed by VITEK® 2 Compact, and methicillin resistance was determined by cefoxitin disc diffusion. AMR profiling followed CLSI guidelines. PCR and whole-genome sequencing using the GridION platform were performed for molecular confirmation and genomic characterization, including gene annotation and phylogenetic analysis. All sequences were deposited in NCBI.ResultsOf 60 confirmed S. aureus isolates (10.4% prevalence), 35% (n = 21) were MRSA, yielding an overall MRSA carriage prevalence of 3.6% (21/576). Most MRSA isolates came from young asymptomatic individuals aged 18 to 45 (80%, 17/21) and from men (58%, 12/21). Among the 21 MRSA isolates identified in this cohort, resistance rates were 29% for erythromycin and ciprofloxacin, 24% for tetracycline, 14% for gentamicin, and 10% for sulfonamides. A total of 71% of the isolates exhibited multidrug resistance. All MRSA isolates carried the methicillin resistance gene (mecA), while 4.7% harbored the Panton–Valentine leukocidin (PVL) gene. Phylogenetic analysis revealed four distinct clusters, indicating a polyclonal community rather than a single spreading strain.ConclusionsThe prevalence of multidrug-resistant MRSA with key virulence factors may represent a continuing public health concern, underscoring the need for strengthened community surveillance, antimicrobial stewardship, and infection control to monitor and limit the spread of multiple circulating strains.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13813-6.

Introduction

The emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), driven by genetic adaptation and the overuse of antibiotics, represents a growing global health threat that increases morbidity, mortality, and economic burden. In particular, Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) poses a significant challenge as it becomes increasingly resistant to commonly available therapeutic options [1], ]. In 2019, AMR was associated with 4.95 million deaths, including 1.27 million directly due to resistant infections [2]. Among key bacterial pathogens, Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is the most clinically significant of the 52 Staphylococcus species. Commonly colonizing human skin and mucosal surfaces, it is part of the normal flora in 20–30% of the population [3, 4]. As an opportunistic pathogen, it can cause diverse infections through virulence factors, such as toxins, immune-evasive surface proteins, and tissue-invasive enzymes. Its production of coagulase, which promotes clot formation, distinguishes it from other staphylococci. Its resistance to host defenses and antibiotics makes infections difficult to treat. Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), classified as a “serious threat” by the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), exemplifies this challenge [5]. MRSA causes an estimated 323,700 cases and 10,600 deaths annually in the USA [6]. First identified in the 1960s, shortly after methicillin’s introduction, MRSA has since become a major cause of global healthcare-associated infections [7]. Its resistance is mediated by the Staphylococcal Chromosomal Cassette mec (SCCmec), which carries the mecA gene encoding the alternative penicillin-binding protein PBP2A. Unlike native PBPs, PBP2A has low affinity for β-lactam antibiotics, allowing cell wall synthesis to continue despite inhibition of normal PBPs. While PBP2A retains only transpeptidase activity, it relies on native PBPs for transglycosylation to incorporate new peptidoglycan strands, enabling robust β-lactam resistance [8].

MRSA is classified epidemiologically into hospital-associated (HA-MRSA) and community-associated (CA-MRSA) strains. HA-MRSA, primarily spreading among hospitalized patients, is a major cause of nosocomial bacteremia, surgical site infections, and ventilator-associated pneumonia [9]. HA-MRSA pathogenicity is driven by virulence factors, including surface adhesins (e.g., fibronectin-binding proteins) that promote biofilm formation on medical devices, capsular polysaccharides (CP5/CP8) for immune evasion, and toxins and cytolytic peptides that cause tissue damage and support persistence. From the 1990s onward, CA-MRSA emerged outside hospital settings, typically causing mild skin and soft tissue infections such as furuncles, but occasionally progressing to severe conditions including necrotizing fasciitis, pyomyositis, osteoarticular infections, and community-acquired pneumonia. [10]. Infected individuals often remain asymptomatic and unrecognized carriers for months to years until the clinical symptoms appear [11]. CA-MRSA is characterized by specific molecular markers, including the SCCmec IV (type 4) element, and often carries Panton-Valentine Leukocidin (PVL) genes, which significantly contribute to its pathogenicity [12]. Beyond PVL, CA-MRSA strains may produce a diverse array of other cytolytic toxins (e.g., α-hemolysin, phenol-soluble modulins) that contribute to tissue destruction and immune evasion [13]. The prevalence of MRSA in both hospital and community is constantly changing, with novel clones of MRSA appearing across the different regions [14]. The incidence of MRSA ranges from 13% to 74% in various geographic areas [15]. The highest prevalence of HA-MRSA was found in Portugal (58.4% in 2013), Iran (51% between 2000 and 2019), and China (45% in 2015–2017) [16–18]. In contrast, the prevalence of CA-MRSA is highest in Australia (84.9%) in 2020 and Japan (79%) in 2018–2019 [18]. Data on MRSA in the Middle East, particularly in Qatar, remains limited. Between 2009 and 2010, a two-year hospital-based study found a significant reservoir of CA-MRSA among 61 MRSA isolates, with 52% classified as CA-MRSA and only 5% as HA-MRSA [19]. This high prevalence of CA-MRSA in a hospital setting indicates that standard infection control measures targeting HA-MRSA may be insufficient to contain the spread of community strains. Herein, we report the first comprehensive assessment of CA-MRSA in Qatar, integrating prevalence data with in-depth molecular characterization of circulating strains

Materials and methods

Ethical clearance

Ethical approvals for this study were obtained from the Institutional Biosafety Committee (IBC) at Qatar University and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Primary Health Corporation under approvals QU-IBC-2022/074 and QU-IRB 1812-E/23, respectively.

Study design and sample collection

This cross-sectional study involved 576 samples collected from 28 primary health care centers (PHCC) across Qatar (Fig. 1) to assess the prevalence of CA-MRSA. Participants were recruited from individuals attending the outpatient care centers. Sample collection was performed over 12 months from January 2023 to December 2023. Sterile cotton swabs (MWE, Wiltshire, UK) were used to collect specimens from the nasal and axillary regions, a painless procedure that takes only a few seconds. Immediately after collection, the swabs were placed in transport tubes and kept in a cool box with ice packs for safe transference to the Biomedical Research Centre (BRC) Microbiology Laboratory at Qatar University. Cultures were processed on the same day, and isolated bacteria were stored at −80 °C for further analysis.Fig. 1Map showing the 28 Primary Health care Corporation (PHCC) health centers’ locations in Qatar

The CDC-defined criteria were used to classify participants as having community-associated MRSA (CA-MRSA) [20]. Age ≥ 18 years, written informed consent, and asymptomatic carrying status at the time of sampling were the inclusion criteria. The following exclusion criteria were used to rule out healthcare-associated or healthcare-onset community MRSA: (i) hospitalization or surgery within the previous 12 months; (ii) living in a nursing home or long-term care facility; (iii) having indwelling catheters, percutaneous devices, or central lines; (iv) receiving dialysis therapy within the previous 12 months; or (v) using antibiotics within the previous three months. The CA-MRSA analytic cohort excluded participants who met any exclusion criteria.

Data collection

A structured questionnaire was used to collect demographic and clinical data, including sampling date, age, sex, nationality, recent antibiotic use (past three months), ongoing decolonization therapy, and recent hospitalization history. All samples were coded with arbitrary ID numbers to maintain confidentiality, and no personally identifiable information was collected.

Microbiological techniques

Isolation of Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus)

Nasal and axillary swabs were cultured on Mannitol Salt Agar to isolate S. aureus. Presumptive colonies were identified based on their morphology and ability to ferment mannitol, followed by biochemical confirmation using catalase and coagulase tests. Final identification was performed using the VITEK® 2 Compact system (bioMérieux, France).

Identification of MRSA and MSSA

Confirmed S. aureus isolates were tested for methicillin resistance using cefoxitin disc diffusion and classified as MRSA or MSSA according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institution (CLSI) guidelines. A 0.5 McFarland suspension (equivalent to 1.5 × 108 CFU/ml) was prepared from a fresh S. aureus culture and measured by the DensiCHEK Plus instrument (bioMérieux, France). Bacterial suspensions were evenly inoculated onto Mueller–Hinton agar, and a 30 µg cefoxitin disc was placed. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h, and inhibition zones were measured and interpreted according to the CLSI M100 (2024) guideline [21]. Isolates with a zone diameter of ≤21 mm were considered MRSA, whereas those with a diameter of ≥ 22mm were considered MSSA. Following this step, MRSA-confirmed isolates were used for the subsequent phases of the study.

Detection of multiple resistance strains

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method, and Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined using the Epsilometer test (E-test). MRSA isolates were standardized to 0.5 McFarland turbidity using DensiCHEK Plus (bioMérieux, France) and inoculated onto Mueller–Hinton agar. A panel of clinically relevant antibiotics (Liofilchem, Italy) was used for susceptibility screening, as detailed in Table 1. The resistance profile of each isolate was recorded individually. Additionally, all erythromycin-resistant but clindamycin-susceptible isolates were further tested using the inducible clindamycin resistance (D-test) to evaluate the presence of Inducible Macrolide-Lincosamide-Streptogramin B resistance phenotype. Following incubation, zones of inhibition were carefully measured and analyzed. The Minimum Inhibitory Concentrations (MICs) were determined according to CLSI, 2024 [21]. Throughout all steps, AST was validated using S. aureus ATCC 25,923 (methicillin-sensitive) and ATCC 33,591 (methicillin-resistant) as quality control strains in accordance with CLSI guidelines. The multiple antibiotic resistance index (MARI) was calculated for each MRSA isolate. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) of MRSA

MRSA isolates underwent whole-genome sequencing (WGS) following standard Oxford Nanopore Technology (ONT) protocols https://nanoporetech.com/document/ligation-sequencing-amplicons-native-barcoding-v14-sqk-nbd114-24. Initially, high-molecular-weight genomic DNA was quantified using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US). The DNA was then repaired and prepared for adapter ligation using the NEBNext FFPE Repair MIX (NEB, M6630) and Ultra II End Repair/dA-tailing Module (NEB, E7546) to address nicks, gaps, or deaminated bases. Native barcoding was performed by ligating barcodes (NB01-24) from the Native Barcoding Kit 24 V14 (SQK-NBD114.24) to the DNA ends using NEB Blunt/TA Ligase Master Mix (NEB, M0367), which was followed by the addition of EDTA to terminate ligation. Barcoded samples were pooled, and excess adapters were removed using 0.4X AMPure XP beads. Sequencing adapters were subsequently ligated to the pooled barcoded DNA through the Quick Ligation Module (NEB, E6056), and a final clean-up step was performed in a Long Fragment Buffer (LFB) to retain fragments > 3 kb. The R10.4.1 flow cell (FLO-MIN114) was primed to stabilize the nanopore array. The priming mix was loaded into the priming port, followed by a 5-minute incubation. The S. aureus library was diluted in Sequencing Buffer (SB) and Library Beads (LIB). A total of 75 µL of the library-bead mix was added dropwise to the SpotON sample port to ensure uniform distribution. Sequencing was initiated using the MinKNOW software (version 23.11.7) on the GridION 5X GXB01496 platform (ONT, Oxford Science Park, Oxford, UK).

Bioinformatic analysis

Bioinformatic analyses of the sequences were performed using Linux-based pipelines. Post-base-calling, quality control was performed using FastQC (version 0.12.1), a bioinformatics tool that generates per-base sequence quality scores. It also provides information on GC content distribution, sequence length distribution, and the presence of overrepresented sequences, such as adapters or contaminants. A specialized tool, Porechop, was used to trim adapter sequences and handle chimeric reads by splitting them at the junctions. Additionally, de novo assembly was performed using Flye. The assembly process involves multiple stages: error correction, repeat graph construction, contig generation, and final polishing. The final assemblies (n = 21) are available on the NCBI website under the genome accessions listed in Table 3. Gene annotation was performed with Prokka (v1.14.6). AMR genes were identified by screening the annotated genomes against the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) using the Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) tool (https://card.mcmaster.ca/analyze/rgi), accessed on 12 December 2025. For molecular characterization of methicillin resistance, the SCCmec type for each isolate was determined using SCCmecFinder (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/SCCmecFinder/). The assembled genomes were submitted to the online platform, which reports the SCCmec type (I–XI) and the ccr gene complex type. Plasmid replicon clusters were detected using PlasmidFinder v2.1 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/PlasmidFinder/). Acquired virulence genes, including haemolysins, enterotoxins, exoenzymes, immune evasion factors, and superantigens, were identified from the assembled MRSA genomes using the VirulenceFinder 2.0 web tool available at the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (CGE) platform (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/VirulenceFinder/). Spa typing was performed in silico using the spaTyper-1.0 web server available at the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/spaTyper-1.0/). The analysis was conducted using the integrated S. aureus database with default parameters (≥95% identity, ≥60% coverage). MLST was performed using the PubMLST S. aureus database. The assembled genome sequence (FASTA format) was submitted to the PubMLST BIGSdb platform (https://pubmlst.org/software/bigsdb). Within the S. aureus organism database, the typing section was selected, and the single-sequence query option was used. The draft genome assembly was uploaded and queried against all loci of the S. aureus MLST scheme. To investigate genomic epidemiology and trace potential transmission pathways, a core-genome WGS–based phylogenetic analysis was performed for 21 MRSA isolates, using MRSA-USA300 (ST8) as the reference strain. Sequence analysis was performed using the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (CSI) Phylogeny pipeline, which generated a maximum-likelihood tree based on high-confidence single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), with branch lengths representing substitutions per site and node support assessed via bootstrap resampling [25]. The final tree was visualized and annotated using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) platform to delineate genetic relationships among the isolates (https://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Fisher’s exact test was used to compare MRSA carriage prevalence across demographic groups. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic data

Among 576 healthy participants, 60 (10.4%) carried S. aureus; of these, 21 (35%) were identified as MRSA, giving an overall MRSA carriage prevalence of 3.6% (21/576). Most MRSA isolates (95.2%, n = 20) were recovered from the nares, while only one (4.7%) was recovered from the axilla. Additionally, the prevalence of MRSA was higher among males (58%) than among females (42%).

MRSA prevalence varied across nationality groups (Fig. 2), and the proportion of individuals who tested positive for MRSA was calculated for each group. The highest prevalence was observed among Southeast Asian participants (4.2%, 4/95), followed by MENA region participants (3.8%, 7/182), and South Asian participants (3.1%, 8/260). No MRSA cases were detected among Sub-Saharan participants in this sample (0/20). Single cases were identified among European/other Western and unknown demographic groups. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) and Bioinformatic analysis of MRSA

All 21 MRSA isolates subjected to WGS have been deposited in the NCBI database. Their corresponding accession numbers, MLST, SCCmec types, spa types, phenotypic resistance profiles, and resistance genes/mechanisms are provided in Table 3. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) revealed considerable genetic diversity, with isolates distributed across eight sequence types (STs): ST1, ST5, ST6, ST8, ST22, ST80, ST672, and ST1535. ST5 and ST6 were the most observed lineages, each accounting for 28.6% (6/21), followed by ST8 at 19.0% (4/21).

SCCmec typing identified type IV as the predominant methicillin resistance cassette (71.4%, n = 15), followed by type VI (19.0%, n = 4) and type V (9.5%, n = 2). spa typing further highlighted genetic heterogeneity, with t304 and t688 as the most common types, each identified in 4 isolates (19.0%), followed by t334 identified in 2 isolates (9.5%) alongside unique types including t5, t8, t84, t311, t315, t1198, t4562, t9736, and t10633.

Phenotypic resistance correlated consistently with genotypic profiles: all isolates carried the mecA gene, conferring resistance to penicillins and cephalosporins. The most common resistance profile (Type 1, 23.8%) involved β-lactam resistance exclusively via mecA. Efflux pump-associated genes (norB, sdrM, mepA, and mdtL) were widely distributed and associated with a high rate of multidrug resistance (71.4%), primarily affecting quinolones, aminoglycosides, and macrolides. To further characterize the pathogenic potential of the isolates, virulence-associated genes and mobile genetic elements were analyzed across all 21 MRSA genomes (Table 4). All isolates uniformly harbored gamma haemolysin genes (hlgA, hlgB, and hlgC), indicating a conserved capacity for host cell lysis across all phylogenetic lineages. Similarly, a core set of exoenzyme genes, particularly aur (aureolysin), was widely distributed, and exoenzymes such as splA, splB, and splE were present in most isolates, suggesting enhanced proteolytic activity and immune evasion capabilities. Leukocidin genes lukD and lukE were identified in most isolates, indicating widespread cytotoxic potential. However, the Panton–Valentine leukocidin (PVL) genes (lukF-PV and lukS-PV) were detected in only one isolate (UMG-11), highlighting their limited distribution within this cohort. A high degree of variability was observed in enterotoxin gene profiles. Seven isolates (33.3%) carried classical enterotoxins such as sea and seb, while others possessed combinations of enterotoxin gene clusters including seg, sei, sem, sen, seo, and seu. Some isolates (e.g., UMG-11, GHR-21, WBC-02, and RAK-09A) exhibited a broader range of enterotoxin gene clusters, whereas others (e.g., OBK-19 and MUA-16) carried fewer or no detectable enterotoxins, indicating differential virulence potential among the isolates. Immune evasion genes scn and sak were widely distributed across isolates, representing a common mechanism for modulating host immunity. Additional genes, such as ssl (staphylococcal superantigen-like proteins), icaR, edinB, and sepA, were detected in a subset of isolates, suggesting variability in immune interaction and biofilm regulation. Analysis of the cassette chromosome recombinase (ccr) gene complex showed that ccrA2B2 (57.1% of the total cohort) was the most prevalent type among the isolates. Other ccr types included ccrC1, ccrA2, ccrA4B4, ccrA4, and ccrB2, indicating diversity in SCCmec-associated recombinase elements. Core-genome phylogenetic analysis and SNP-based relatedness of MRSA isolates

Core genome Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) alignment-based phylogenetic analysis was conducted on 21 MRSA clinical isolates, with MRSA-USA300 (ST8) included as a reference strain (Fig. 4). The analysis separated the MRSA isolates into 4 well-supported clades, with bootstrap values ranging from 85% to 100%, indicating strong confidence in the inferred relationships among isolates. Branch lengths were generally short within clades, reflecting low SNP divergence and close core-genome relatedness among isolates within the same lineage, whereas longer branch lengths separating clades indicated the presence of genetically distinct MRSA lineages in the dataset. Discussion

This study provides a genomic and phenotypic characterization of CA-MRSA isolates from asymptomatic carriers in Qatar, revealing a high prevalence of MDR strains with substantial genetic diversity. These findings identify CA-MRSA as a significant, yet often overlooked, community reservoir of AMR, with critical implications for public health surveillance, infection control, and strategies in the Gulf region [26]. When compared to earlier clinical data from Hamad General Hospital (2009–2010), which indicated that CA-MRSA genotypes comprised 52% of MRSA in the hospital and these results suggest a genomic basis for potential community-to-hospital transmission [19]. Nevertheless, a direct and up-to-date comparison of resistance and virulence profiles between current community and hospital isolates remains unfeasible due to the absence of recent, accessible genomic data from healthcare settings in Qatar. To understand transmission dynamics, parallel, systematic genomic surveillance of MRSA in both community and hospital settings across Qatar and the wider GCC region should be initiated.

The overall S. aureus colonization rate in this cohort was 10.4%, lower than global community estimates of 20–30% [3], which may reflect differences in host, environmental, or behavioral factors in this population. While global estimates provide a useful benchmark, the lower rate observed here underscores the importance of considering regional and population-specific dynamics when interpreting colonization and AMR data. Of the 21 MRSA isolates recovered, 95.2% (n = 20) were from nasal swabs, while only one was from the axilla. The predominance of S. aureus in nasal swabs is expected, as the anterior nares represent the primary natural reservoir of S. aureus in humans, where the organism is evolutionarily adapted to the nasal epithelium, enabling efficient adherence and long-term persistence, although colonization can also occur at other skin sites, such as the axilla [27]. In comparison to the proportion of MRSA among S. aureus carriers found in various community-based asymptomatic studies from other regions, such as Europe and North America, where carriage rates often fall below 5% [28]. The prevalence of MRSA in this cohort is notably high at 35% (21 out of 60). However, the overall MRSA carriage prevalence among the 576 asymptomatic participants was 3.6%. This suggests that Qatar’s unique demographics, characterized by a highly mobile and multicultural population, may contribute to the introduction and spread of various MRSA strains [29]. Higher colonization rates have been observed in males (58%) and young people (aged 18- 45, 80% of MRSA carriers). These findings are consistent with several epidemiological studies on community-associated MRSA (CA-MRSA). Factors such as socioeconomic status, job exposure, and certain behaviors may increase the risk of transmission. A critical finding of this study is the high rate of MDR among the CA-MRSA isolates (71.4%). While resistance to β-lactams was expected due to the universal presence of the mecA gene, substantial resistance was also observed to erythromycin (29%), ciprofloxacin (29%), tetracycline (24%), and gentamicin (14%). This pattern aligns with CA-MRSA trends reported in the Middle East and Asia, where antibiotic use and over-the-counter access contribute significantly to resistance [30, 31]. The high rate of MDR along with the identification of inducible clindamycin resistance in 23% of strains, further restricts effective oral treatment options and complicates the selection of empirical therapies in community settings [32]. Notably, all isolates remained susceptible to last-line agents, including vancomycin, linezolid, and daptomycin, underscoring the importance of sustained antimicrobial stewardship to preserve their clinical utility [33].

MLST revealed a polyclonal structure with seven STs. ST5 and ST6 predominated (28.6% each, n = 6), followed by ST8 (n = 4), while ST22, ST80, ST672, and ST1535 were singletons. Their prevalence exceeds European reports (8–12%) but aligns with regional data from Saudi Arabia, where ST5 and ST6 are dominant, accounting for nearly half of MRSA infections [34, 35]. Similarly, in Oman, ST6 was reported as the predominant genotype in a tertiary hospital, although the isolates were notably PVL-negative [36]. The identification of ST8 (19%), a clone that includes the pandemic CA-MRSA strain USA300 in our community sample, demonstrates the widespread distribution of this successful lineage and aligns with previous findings in Qatari hospital settings (7%). Its established circulation in the region is further supported by the discovery of ST80, a classic CA-MRSA clone common in Europe, the Middle East, and North Africa [37]. However, compared to the only previous molecular study of MRSA in Qatar, which found USA1100/ST30 to be the most prevalent clone (28%) in a hospital setting (2009–2010), our community-based cohort (2023) was dominated by ST5 and ST6, with no ST30 detected, suggesting possible shifts in circulating lineages over time [19]. The sporadic detection of ST672, an emerging clone commonly reported in the Indian subcontinent [38], highlights the genetic diversity observed in this cohort, consistent with Qatar’s multicultural population. The rare ST1535, represented by a single isolate, belongs to clonal complex 15 (CC15) and has been previously described in Saudi Arabia as a CC15-MRSA strain carrying a novel SCCmecV/SCCfus composite element, underscoring its distinctive genetic architecture [39]. The identification of a single ST22 isolate in our CA-MRSA collection from Qatar warrants careful consideration, given the complex epidemiology of this lineage across the MENA region, where the previous Qatari study identified ST22 as a prominent healthcare-associated clone EMRSA-15 comprising 8 of 61 MRSA isolates (13.1%) [19]. In the Gulf region, ST22-MRSA-IV has been documented in both hospital and community settings, with Kuwait reporting that ST22 comprised 85.5% of CC22 isolates and included both PVL-positive and toxin-producing variants [40]. ST1 (USA400) was a dominant community-acquired MRSA lineage in North America, and it continues to cause community and hospital infections across diverse regions, including Australia, Brazil, and the Gulf [41]. The lack of a single dominant clone and the existence of strains with diverse genetic backgrounds (e.g., ST5, ST6, ST8, ST80) highlight the dynamic nature of MRSA colonizing asymptomatic community individuals in Qatar and the increasingly blurred line between traditional healthcare-associated and community-associated classifications. The predominance of SCCmec type IV (71.4%) reflects its smaller genetic size and fitness advantage in community settings and is a recognized hallmark of CA-MRSA globally. Similar high prevalence has been reported in community studies from the United States, Australia, and Europe [42]. The genetic heterogeneity of the MRSA population in Qatar is further reflected by the co-circulation of SCCmec types V (9.5%) and VI (19%), which are also commonly associated with community settings [43].

The high diversity of spa types observed in this study (n = 12) further supports the polyclonal nature of CA-MRSA carriage. The most common spa types were t304 (associated with ST6) and t688 (associated with ST5), which are also prevalent in neighbouring countries; a large-scale Kuwaiti study identified t688 and t304 as the dominant spa types, constituting approximately 23 and 18% of MRSA isolates, respectively [44]. The molecular study in a Qatari hospital setting reported 11 various spa types among 61 MRSA isolates from multinational patients, including t008 (USA300), t044, and t688 [19]. The detection of rare spa types such as t4562 and t10633, not previously reported in the Gulf region, alongside known epidemic types (t304, t688, t311), suggests ongoing introduction of novel MRSA lineages. This spa-type diversity, coupled with the polyclonal ST distribution, supports a model of multiple independent international introductions rather than clonal expansion of a single endemic strain.

A striking finding was the universal presence of gamma-hemolysin genes (hlgA, hlgB, hlgC) across all isolates, coupled with the complete absence of the alpha-hemolysin gene (hla), typically considered a core virulence factor in invasive S. aureus. Gamma-hemolysin is a bicomponent leukotoxin that targets and lyses neutrophils, providing a mechanism for immune evasion that may be sufficient for successful colonization without the tissue-destructive potential of alpha-hemolysin [45]. This finding aligns with the paradigm that colonizing strains often possess a distinct virulence repertoire focused on persistence rather than acute tissue damage. The widespread presence of lukD and lukE (in most isolates) further supports this, as these leukocidins are more commonly associated with colonizing strains and target neutrophils and monocytes [46].

A high degree of variability was observed in enterotoxin gene profiles. ST5 isolates uniformly carried sea and seb, while ST6 isolates harbored the enterotoxin gene cluster (egc), including seg, sei, sem, sen, seo, and seu. The egc cluster is typically associated with asymptomatic carriage rather than food-poisoning outbreaks, consistent with our study population of healthy carriers [47]. In contrast, isolates that carried sek and seq have been linked to more severe clinical outcomes [48]. The detection of tst (toxic shock syndrome toxin) in one ST22 isolate (UMG-11) is concerning, as TSST-1 is a superantigen associated with menstrual and non-menstrual toxic shock syndrome, highlighting that asymptomatic carriers can serve as reservoirs for toxin-producing strains [49].

The widespread distribution of scn (staphylococcal complement inhibitor) and sak (staphylokinase) reflects the importance of immune evasion for successful colonization. SCIN blocks complement activation at the C3 convertase stage, preventing opsonization and phagocytosis, while SAK dissolves fibrin clots and degrades antimicrobial peptides, facilitating persistence on host tissues [45]. The presence of scn and sak across all major genotypes suggests that these factors constitute part of the core immune evasion machinery in MRSA colonizing strains. Additionally, the detection of ssl (superantigen-like proteins), icaR (biofilm regulator), edinB (epidermal cell differentiation inhibitor), and sepA (serine protease) in subsets of isolates suggests variability in immune interaction and biofilm regulation, which may influence long-term carriage [50–52]. Despite its low prevalence (4.7%) in this study, the PVL gene is commonly associated with CA-MRSA, causing severe skin and soft tissue infections in North America and Europe [53]. In contrast to our findings, the study conducted in the hospital setting in Qatar reported PVL-positive isolates across multiple clones, including USA300 (100%, 4/4), ST80 (100%, 8/8), EMRSA-15/ST22 (62.5%, 5/8), and USA1100/ST30 (100%, 17/17) [19]. The substantially higher PVL prevalence in hospital clinical isolates compared to our community carrier cohort is consistent with previous reports showing that PVL is more frequently associated with clinical infection isolates than with community-associated isolates. [19]. The low PVL prevalence observed in this study is consistent with reports from several Asian and Middle Eastern countries, including Saudi Arabia, Jordan, and Egypt, suggesting regional differences in the virulence repertoire of CA-MRSA [34, 54, 55]. This finding suggests that PVL may not be a major contributor to CA-MRSA virulence in this region. Instead, other factors such as gamma-hemolysin, lukD/E, and enterotoxins may play a compensatory role in virulence. The low PVL prevalence is also consistent with a recent GCC scoping review, which reported variable distribution of PVL-positive strains across the Gulf region [56] The relatively low prevalence may further be influenced by the study design, as isolates were mainly obtained from nasal swabs rather than clinical infection sites, where PVL-positive strains are more commonly reported.

Core-genome phylogenetic analysis grouped the 21 MRSA isolates into four well-supported clades (bootstrap 75–100%). Isolates showed limited clustering by nationality, with strains from South Asian, Middle Eastern, and European participants interspersed across clades. Foremost community-associated lineages. ST5, ST6, and ST8 were distributed across multiple nationalities and AMR profiles, reflecting extensive strain mixing in a highly mobile population. Accordingly, higher MRSA prevalence in certain groups likely reflects cohort demographics rather than inherent biological susceptibility. This polyclonal structure, with multiple STs coexisting within clades and recurring across clades, contrasts with the clonal outbreak patterns usually seen in hospital settings. The lack of clear association between lineage, sequence type, participant nationality, or AMR profile suggests that the MRSA burden in this community is not driven by the expansion of a single dominant clone [20]. Rather, it suggests several independent introductions and a dynamic ecosystem of circulating strains shaped by frequent horizontal gene transfer, a phenomenon gaining widespread recognition in the epidemiology of CA-MRSA worldwide. [57]. Multiple plasmid replicons (e.g., rep cluster_1733, rep cluster_2214) and a transposon (Tn522) were detected in a subset of MRSA isolates. Additionally, plasmid replicon clusters were identified; some harbored a single replicon, while others carried two or more replicon clusters, suggesting the presence of single or multiple plasmid elements; however, complete plasmid circularization was not achieved, and plasmid architecture could not be definitively resolved. Collectively, these findings underscore the complexity of CA-MRSA transmission dynamics and reinforce the need for community-based genomic surveillance strategies that extend beyond traditional hospital-focused models.

Conclusion

This study reveals a reservoir of MRSA colonising asymptomatic residents of Qatar without a history of hospitalization, surgery, dialysis, or indwelling medical devices in the preceding 12 months and antibiotic usage within the previous 3 months, characterized by high MDR, diverse virulence determinants, and a polyclonal population structure, suggesting multiple introductions or diverse circulating lineages. These carriers represent an active, hidden reservoir from which resistant infections and resistance genes can spread, contributing to the persistence and possible dissemination of AMR in the community. Our findings highlight that community-associated MRSA may not be adequately controlled by hospital-focused surveillance alone. They underscore the urgent need for sustained, community-engaged genomic surveillance, expanded antimicrobial stewardship into outpatient and community settings, and targeted public education on hygiene and antibiotic use. Integrating these measures is likely essential to curb the silent spread of AMR and to align with both regional and global containment strategies.
