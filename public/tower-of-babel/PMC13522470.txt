Genetic profiling enhances cystic fibrosis prenatal diagnosis
Authors: Amin Hosseini Nami, Mahboubeh Kabiri, Fatemeh Zafarghandi Motlagh, Razie Zeinali, Ali Karimi, Saba Aslani, Sirous Zeinali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrenatal testing is a standard approach for detecting genetic diseases in expectant mothers, with more testing options becoming available. Homozygosity mapping is invaluable for identifying genes causing disease and improving molecular diagnostics. Here, we present the prenatal diagnosis outcomes of three families affected by cystic fibrosis (CF). A combination of phenotypic analysis, haplotype analysis, and CFTR gene sequencing was used to identify the causative mutations. This allowed for the effective detection of individuals misdiagnosed with routine screening procedures. A method that is potentially useful for similar autosomal recessive disorders. Our findings indicate that enhanced diagnostic accuracy enables earlier clinical interventions that lead to better management of affected pregnancies and improved neonatal care. We show the significance of comprehensive genetic testing to boost diagnostic accuracy for recessive diseases and minimize the risk of diagnostic errors in prenatal settings.Keywords: Prenatal diagnosis, Cystic fibrosis, Respiratory diseases, Short tandem repeat profiling, Autosomal recessive disorder, Pediatric respiratory geneticsSubject terms: Clinical genetics, Genetic linkage study, Genetic markers, Haplotypes, Medical genetics, Genetic techniques

Introduction

Hereditary diseases are the primary cause of newborn fatalities in the United States1 and can lead to significant disabilities2. Such congenital impairments complicate 3–5% of pregnancies3. The prenatal diagnostics include various methods to accurately detect these impairments in a suspected fetus4. Consequently, parents may opt to terminate the pregnancy or seek professional care for a child with such impairments5. Even with advances in prenatal testing, a study of 42 U.S. laboratories found issues in 0.33% of 4,234 cases. This included a specific error rate of 0.24% among the 1,270 CF cases analyzed6. Prenatal diagnostic testing (PND) mistakes occur due to a variety of reasons, such as sample mix-ups, non-paternity, and maternal cell contamination (MCC)7. It is crucial to address these issues seriously since misdiagnosis leads to a significant financial and emotional burden on parents. The worst outcome is the termination of a healthy pregnancy. Fetal samples get contaminated with maternal cells with an estimated 1–2% occurrence during chorionic villus sampling (CVS) and 0.5% during amniotic fluid sampling8. Furthermore, sample mix-ups might happen during prenatal twin sampling. Even a small contamination leads to a false positive misdiagnosis using Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR). Therefore, MMC testing is recommended as standard practice in most genetic labs8,9. Short tandem repeat (STR) markers minimize errors in DNA typing procedures that have improved the ability to solve forensic cases and familial relationships10,11. These markers are polymorphic variations formed by repeats of 2–6 nucleotides. The combination of different STR markers creates a unique profile of each individual12,13. The amplification is done using fluorescent-labeled primers, and the identification with fragment analysis12.

CF is one of the most common genetic disorders among the Caucasian population. It is caused by mutations in the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene14. The CFTR1 database15 has reported 2,121 CFTR variants as of September 2024, with 1,085 variants causing CF based on clinical data in the CFTR2 database16.

Here, we show that genetic profiling improves diagnostic accuracy in prenatal CF cases in ambiguous and high-risk cases. Technical issues like maternal cell contamination or sampling errors—particularly in twin pregnancies—can lead to the detection of two CFTR mutations in a fetus who is a carrier. Such errors cause false-positive diagnoses and unnecessary pregnancy terminations. The use of STR-based haplotyping revealed more than two alleles, indicating sample contamination or incompatible haplotypes between the fetus and father, indicating non-paternity. These results highlight the effectiveness of short tandem repeat (STR) methods in resolving diagnostic uncertainties and suggest a useful approach for improving routine prenatal screening. Notably, current best practice guidelines recommend STR marker analysis in all prenatal diagnostic samples to detect maternal cell contamination and sample mistakes17. By increasing the accuracy of CFTR genotyping, we enhance genetic counseling, support informed decision-making, and enable early respiratory care planning for neonates with CF. The case-based insight provides a foundation for future studies to validate STR profiling in broader clinical settings.

Materials and methods

Study participants, sample collection, and consent ethics

Three couples were referred to our center for prenatal genetic testing. A clinical geneticist examined the couples, identified their reproductive and genetic backgrounds, and explained the identification methods. We followed the appropriate guidelines for diagnostic procedures and investigated a total of 15 individuals.

A specialist collected CVS samples, which were then examined under a stereomicroscope to identify maternal cell contamination and ensure sample purity. Next, Fetal DNA was extracted directly from chorionic villi, while DNA from other pedigree members was isolated from parental peripheral blood using the salting-out method.

The written informed consent obtained from participants included explicit information that STR/haplotype analysis may be undertaken to confirm diagnostic results and ensure sample authenticity. Where paternal haplotyping was considered necessary for diagnostic clarification, parents were informed and provided written consent that explained the purpose of the test, the small possibility of incidental findings (including non-paternity), and our policy that incidental non-paternity findings are not disclosed to families and are used only for internal diagnostic quality control. All study procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Kawsar Human Genetic Research Center (Approval Number: [1400–6328]). All experiments involving human participants were performed following the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians.

Genotyping

Nanodrop spectrophotometry was used to determine the concentration of the extracted DNA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Next, we utilized direct sequencing for the genetic analysis of the DNA samples. Sequencing primers targeting all exons and 200 flanking intron bases were designed using a previously described approach18. The amplicons were sequenced using the BigDye Terminator Cycle sequencing kit and then analyzed on the 3130/XL Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Foster City, CA, USA, TF).

In addition, we employed the GT Hapscreen CFTR kit (Genetek Biopharma, Berlin, Germany, GT) to assess the inheritance pattern of CFTR-linked short tandem repeat (STR) markers following the manufacturer’s instructions (Fig. 1). The haplotype of each individual was determined and assessed using the user manual of the GT Hapscreen CFTR kit.

Result

Case 1: prenatal diagnosis of delF508 homozygosity with haplotype confirmation

A couple was referred to our center for prenatal diagnosis because they had a history of affected family members with CF. The male, 36 years old, and the female, 30 years old, were from the Khuzestan province in the southwest of Iran. They were referred at 8 weeks of gestation, and CVS sampling was conducted at 12 weeks. Ultrasound findings at 8 weeks revealed a single viable fetus in variable presentation with a crown-rump length (CRL) of 18 mm, corresponding to 8 weeks and 1 day. Heart rate and amniotic fluid were normal, and no abnormalities were noted in either adnexa. Parents were found to have only one copy of the delF508 mutation and did not demonstrate any other mutations. The fetus was homozygous for the delF508 mutation and shared the same haplotype with the mother but not the father (Fig. 1). Genetic counseling was provided to the family following the diagnosis, informing them that the fetus was affected with CF, and the decision regarding continuation of the pregnancy was left entirely to the parents.

Case 2: Maternal cell contamination detected and corrected in CFTR prenatal diagnosis

A pregnant woman and her partner (both 42 years old, unrelated) were found to be heterozygous for delF508 and c.2128 A > T mutation, respectively (Fig. 2A). Both parents are from the Kermanshah province, Iran. They did not carry any other mutations in the CFTR allele. Their first child (II-1) was diagnosed with CF at eight months, exhibiting respiratory issues, salty-tasting skin, greasy stools, and a positive sweat test in our center. Genetic analysis revealed a c.2128 mutation and a delF508 mutation on both alleles, confirming a compound heterozygous genotype. The couple was referred at approximately 8 weeks of gestation. Ultrasound findings at that time confirmed a single viable fetus with a normal heart rate and CRL of 16 mm. Genetic analysis of the fetus (Fig. 2A, II-2) confirmed the same genotype as II-1. During haplotype analysis of II-2, we observed more than three peaks, most likely due to maternal cell contamination with other family members having the expected haplotypes (Fig. 2A). Therefore, we decided to sample the fetus for a second analysis and found the fetus to be unaffected with one mutated allele carrying c.2128 A > T (Fig. 2B).

Correction of CF misdiagnosis in a fetus due to maternal cell contamination, using haplotype analysis. (A) Linkage analysis revealed matching haplotypes with STR markers in corresponding colors. Numbers indicate the base pair sizes, with the last digit of each marker size shown. Initial analysis of fetus II-2 shows three haplotypes, two shared with the mother (I-2) and one with the father (I-1), suggesting maternal contamination. (B) After resampling and linkage analysis, the fetus was correctly identified as a carrier of the c.2128 A > T mutation, not homozygous or affected by CF.

Case 3: sample mix-up errors in twin pregnancy resolved through CFTR haplotype analysis

An unrelated couple from Golestan Province, Iran, applied for a prenatal diagnosis due to a history of a child with cystic fibrosis. Their 6-year-old son was diagnosed with CF at the age of one, carrying the c.254 G > A and c.1397 C > G mutations in a compound heterozygous form (Fig. 3A). He also displayed CF phenotypes, including malabsorption, low weight gain, greasy stool, salty-tasting skin, and a positive sweat test. The mother, 27 years old and 14 weeks pregnant with twins, was found to be heterozygous for the c.1397 C > G mutation, while the father, 37 years old, was a carrier of the c.254 G > A mutation.

Initial misdiagnosis of CF in a twin pregnancy due to sample mix-up, corrected through haplotype and linkage analysis. (A) STR marker-based linkage analysis displayed matching haplotypes, with numbers representing the base pair sizes, each ending with the respective digit.The father (I-1) carries the c. 254 G > A mutation, and the mother (I-2) carries the c. 1397 C > G mutation. Initial analysis indicated both fetuses (II-2 and II-3) carried both mutations and were affected, with more than two haplotypes per locus suggesting a sample mix-up. (B) After additional sampling, linkage analysis revealed that only one fetus (II-2) is affected, while the other (II-3) is a carrier of the c. 1397 C > G mutation.

Ultrasound findings confirmed a dichorionic diamniotic twin pregnancy with two separate gestational sacs and placentas, with one placenta located in the upper right uterine segment and the other in the lower left uterine segment. The fetal growth and amniotic fluid volumes were normal. Placental samples were collected for fetal testing under ultrasound guidance, and the samples were processed independently.

Initial testing indicated that both fetuses (II-2 and II-3) were compound heterozygotes and were similarly affected (Fig. 3A). However, our analysis revealed identical haplotypes with more than two markers per locus, indicating a sample mix-up possibility. As a result, we conducted additional sampling and genotype analysis. One fetus had the same mutated alleles (c.254 G > A and c.1397 C > G), while the other twin demonstrated a heterozygous genotype carrying only the c.1397 C > G mutation (Fig. 3B). Inclusion of paternal haplotypes in the analysis provided an additional control for sample authenticity. The remaining family member haplotypes are displayed in Fig. 3B.

Discussion

We investigated the effectiveness of linkage analysis in enhancing the accuracy of prenatal diagnostics (PND) for cystic fibrosis (CF) in complex diagnostic settings. Our work highlights the critical role of linkage analysis in PND to prevent the delivery of an affected fetus or fatal loss due to errors. Our results also show that using multiple markers across different chromosomes improves detection accuracy and reduces errors. By incorporating these advanced diagnostic methods into routine prenatal care, we can enhance overall clinical workflows and minimize diagnostic missteps, making these techniques a valuable addition to standard practices.

We identified four known CFTR mutations: c.2128 A > T, c.254 G > A, c.1397 C > G, and c.1521–1523 del CTT in 12 (out of 15) individuals across three families. Notably, c.1521–1523 del CTT19 is the most common CFTR mutation and was found in two (out of three) families. The cases presented here demonstrate the advantage of using STR markers for conducting PND under various scenarios. In addition to diagnostic utility, these markers, when both parental haplotypes are analyzed, serve as robust internal controls to verify sample authenticity and minimize mix-up risks in high-throughput laboratory workflows.

In Case 1, the linkage analysis confirmed that family member I-1 is not the biological father of the affected individual despite sharing a similar mutation. Haplotype analysis ruled out sample contamination, confirming that the fetus inherited maternal haplotypes but not paternal ones. Notably, the true biological father shared the same haplotype on chromosome 7, which carries the CFTR mutation. This may be due to a blood relationship with the mother or the family’s origins in a small town in Khuzestan province, Iran, where genetic relatedness within the population is common. The ethical implications of identifying non-paternity were discussed with the family during the consent process. We clearly explained that our analysis was conducted solely to ensure sample authenticity and enhance diagnostic precision, not to investigate paternity. While non-paternity may be discovered as an incidental finding, we assured patients that this sensitive information would remain confidential. Additionally, we clarified that the anonymized results could be used in future research publications.

Presence of maternal DNA in Case 2 initially resulted in an incorrect diagnosis of CF in the fetus. Investigation of maternal cell contamination revealed that the fetus was a carrier of one CFTR mutation and prevented possible misdiagnosis. This case underscores the importance of haplotype analysis in detecting maternal cell contamination and preventing misdiagnoses in prenatal settings where sample integrity is crucial. In Case 3, a sample mix-up error in a twin pregnancy resulted in both fetuses being misdiagnosed with CF. After re-sampling and analysis, one fetus was confirmed, while the other was a carrier of only one mutation. In this case, the most plausible explanation was a wet-lab handling error during processing, as inter-twin contamination at biopsy is highly unlikely given the extensive experience of our physicians with CVS sampling. This emphasizes the importance of careful sample handling and the utility of haplotype analysis in detecting errors of twin pregnancies.

In all three cases, STR-based haplotype analysis ensured the accuracy of CFTR prenatal diagnoses, reducing the risk and the subsequent burden for families. Fragment analysis of the QF PCR product of STR markers is the most effective method for studying STR markers. In some instances, PCR20 and NGS21 have been employed to identify maternal cell contamination (MCC) using STRs. While QF PCR-based detection is easy, fast, and cost-effective, NGS can analyze an entire set of chromosomes22. Achieving the same outcome is possible with effective primer design in multiplex STR analysis. These fundamental techniques offer a scalable model for wider adoption in clinical diagnostic laboratories. Our work lays the groundwork for larger prospective studies to validate the effectiveness of these techniques across diverse populations.

During PND testing, a small fraction of maternal cells (~ 0.1%) could affect the outcome since an optimized PCR reaction is very sensitive23. There is a higher risk of maternal cell contamination in CVS sampling, particularly in cultured CVS samples. However, meticulous sample dissection can help mitigate MCC9,24. Linkage analysis relies on selecting an adequate number of STR markers where random similarity is extremely unlikely. Additionally, when contamination is below 1%, it may be challenging to distinguish maternal markers9. The Hapscreen kit includes six markers linked to the CFTR gene and nine on other chromosomes. This decreases the likelihood of two individuals having the same haplotype by chance, leading to a conclusive decision on MCC and ruling out other sampling contaminations. Linkage analysis using multiplex-PCR also offers several advantages by saving time, reagents, and labor where resources are limited for detecting sampling issues25.

In Case 3, both twin fetuses had the c.254 G > A / c.1397 C > G genotype based on only sequencing. The fragment analysis results showed the same set of markers for each locus, which was unexpected and indicated the presence of co-fetal contamination. Following a second round of sampling and analysis, one affected fetus was identified, and the other was only a carrier. Prenatal diagnosis in twin pregnancies is more complex as compared to singleton pregnancies. Errors can occur at any stage of CVS or amniocentesis26, with the likelihood of cross-contamination of one fetus in twin gestations being 1% in CVS24, and the probability of cross-contamination of both fetuses is exceedingly low (~ 1:1000). These statistics reinforce the possibility of laboratory handling error27.

Linkage analysis, though valuable, has limitations. It relies on having multiple family members available for analysis, and genetic markers may be uninformative within a family28. The heterozygosity of markers significantly impacts the effectiveness of linkage analysis. Moreover, it cannot be employed if the involvement of the causative gene is not certain29,30.

There are instances where incorrect judgments result from PND process flaws despite strong DNA evidence. This is particularly important when solid clinical evidence shows that a sibling is affected by an autosomal recessive disorder such as CF, even when no mutation can be detected using MLPA or whole-gene sequencing9. Moreover, haplotype analysis can efficiently identify a mutation and confirm the results of a direct mutation study31. Multiple markers from different chromosomes (e.g., chromosomes 6, 21, 18, 13, X, and Y) should be utilized to minimize the likelihood of detecting the same haplotype in different individuals. These markers are crucial in distinguishing between fetuses in multiple pregnancies or between samples that have undergone PND testing (parental, siblings, and fetuses), thereby reducing the error rate. These additional markers would be highly valuable in ruling out maternal contamination, sampling errors, sample authenticity, and differentiating between fetal samples (in multiple pregnancies). They can also determine whether a person is a triploid or has uniparental disomy.

Genetic diagnosis is an established practice in clinical settings31. It is crucial in disease prognosis and detection, as well as control policies. Considering the severe consequences of errors, we should constantly evaluate and improve the test choice, sampling method, and presentation of the test’s outcome.

We presented the results of a study on prenatal diagnostics that utilized linkage analysis in three cases of CF. This research aimed to emphasize the significance of haplotyping in detecting errors, particularly in cases involving paternal ambiguity, maternal cell contamination, and twin pregnancies. This approach is crucial for preventing unnecessary fetal loss. In general, paternal haplotyping is not routinely required for prenatal diagnosis. In this study, paternal haplotyping was performed only to confirm sample authenticity in a high-throughput setting and to provide indirect confirmation of sequencing results. When performed, paternal haplotyping must be accompanied by explicit informed consent, pre-test counselling that explains the purpose and the low risk of incidental findings (including non-paternity), and a clear policy that non-paternity will not be disclosed as a clinical result but handled only for internal diagnostic quality control and interpretation. We have added this clarification to avoid suggesting routine paternal testing while preserving its role as a limited, ethically governed diagnostic safeguard. Although the scale of this study is limited, it establishes a foundation for larger prospective studies that can validate these findings within more diverse populations. Our results highlight the effective use of specific marker combinations to reduce errors, ultimately leading to accurate and cost-effective diagnoses.

Our results emphasize the crucial role of advanced genetic techniques in improving the accuracy of prenatal diagnosis for CF, especially in complex cases. By addressing diagnostic challenges such as unclear paternal relationships, contamination issues, and twin pregnancies, our approach allows for more reliable clinical decisions, ultimately benefiting both families and healthcare providers. These findings suggest that incorporating a variety of genetic markers into prenatal testing protocols could further reduce errors, underscoring the continuous advancement of genetic testing as a valuable tool for informed prenatal care. By explicitly connecting these findings to clinical applications, particularly in respiratory medicine and pediatric care, the study highlights the broader implications of precise prenatal diagnostics for the management of CF.
