Proanthocyanidins inhibit methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus by disrupting membrane integrity and reprogramming transcriptional pathways
Authors: Jingge Wang, Xiaohui Chen, Junpeng Huang, Xiaoqiang He, Jiexiao Guan, Pengyan Li, Guiqin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proanthocyanidins (PACs) are promising antimicrobial agents due to their broad-spectrum biological activities and confirmed safety profile. However, their mechanisms of action against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to systematically investigate the antibacterial effects and potential molecular mechanisms of PACs against MRSA using microbiological assays, membrane integrity and oxidative stress analyses, transcriptomic profiling, RT-qPCR, molecular docking, surface plasmon resonance (SPR) validation, and protein expression analysis. PACs inhibited MRSA growth (MIC 156-312 μg/mL), disrupted membrane integrity, and altered the expression of genes involved in fatty-acid biosynthesis and oxidative stress responses. The involvement of membrane damage and oxidative stress was further supported by rescue experiments using vitamin E and N-acetyl-L-cysteine. Transcriptomic and RT-qPCR analyses revealed significant changes in genes associated with fatty-acid biosynthesis, oxidative stress responses, antibiotic resistance, and virulence regulation. Moreover, PACs enhanced the susceptibility of MRSA to β-lactam antibiotics, reduced mecA expression, and decreased the protein levels of PBP2a, SarA, and MgrA. SPR analysis further supported a direct interaction between PACs and SarA. Overall, these findings suggest that PACs exert anti-MRSA activity through multiple mechanisms involving membrane disruption, oxidative stress induction, and modulation of resistance- and virulence-related pathways. PACs may therefore represent a promising natural antimicrobial agent or antibiotic adjuvant for the control of MRSA infections.

Introduction

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a significant foodborne pathogen that colonizes and infects a broad range of human and animal hosts. It is also a primary causative agent of bovine mastitis, a disease that compromises animal health, reduces milk quality, and imposes substantial economic losses on the livestock and dairy industries (Khasapane et al., 2024). As a facultative intracellular pathogen, MRSA readily forms biofilms, which facilitate evasion of antimicrobial therapies and host immune responses, thereby promoting persistent infections (Niggli et al., 2023; Peyrusson et al., 2020). Moreover, MRSA is frequently characterized by multidrug resistance (MDR) and extensive drug resistance (XDR), contributing substantially to the global antimicrobial resistance crisis (Elshebrawy et al., 2025; Gonzalez-Machado et al., 2024). Therefore, the development of effective strategies to mitigate MRSA transmission and infection remains an urgent public health priority.

Natural compounds exhibit a wide range of biological activities, including antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory effects, and are therefore considered promising candidates for drug development, with potential applications in disease prevention and therapy (Li et al., 2024c; Liang et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2025). Proanthocyanidins (PACs), a class of plant-derived polyphenols abundant in blueberries, mulberries, grapes, and cinnamon, exhibit diverse biological activities, including antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties (Gong et al., 2025; Navarro-Masip et al., 2023). PACs from fresh black chokeberry have been identified as one of the major antimicrobial constituents of fruits, exhibiting activity against Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and nine other bacterial species and modulating gastrointestinal-resident phagocytes to confer dual antioxidant and antibacterial effects in vivo (Denev et al., 2019). In addition, PACs exert antimicrobial effects by interfering with microbial metabolic processes, including inhibition of extracellular enzyme activity and disruption of oxidative phosphorylation (Chen et al., 2024; Denev et al., 2019). Owing to their broad-spectrum bioactivities and well-established safety profile (Bai et al., 2019; Colom-Pellicer et al., 2022; Sierra-Cruz et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2021), PACs are regarded as attractive candidates for antimicrobial development.

Previous studies have demonstrated that PACs exhibit antibacterial activity against foodborne pathogens and enhance the efficacy of conventional antibiotics (Yang et al., 2023). This potentiation is primarily mediated by the repression of multidrug efflux pumps and decreased membrane permeability, accompanied by the inhibition of biofilm formation and persister cell viability (Maisuria et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2022). The abundant hydroxyl groups in PACs confer potent free-radical scavenging activity and may also interfere with bacterial adhesion and aggregation, thereby suppressing biofilm formation and reducing pathogenicity (Chen et al., 2024; Luca et al., 2020). The antibacterial mechanism of PACs against Escherichia coli (E. coli) primarily involves disruption of cell-envelope integrity and induction of oxidative stress (Tang et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2019, 2021). Although PACs derived from various botanical sources have been reported to disrupt membrane integrity, inhibit biofilm formation, and induce oxidative stress in bacteria (Ajiboye et al., 2016; Maisuria et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022), evidence regarding their antibacterial activity against S. aureus, particularly MRSA, remains limited and fragmented. Moreover, a systematic evaluation integrating phenotypic characterization with global transcriptional responses is still lacking. This knowledge gap limits a comprehensive understanding of their antibacterial potential and hinders their rational application against MRSA.

We hypothesized that PACs exert anti-MRSA activity through coordinated disruption of membrane integrity, induction of oxidative stress, and modulation of transcriptional networks associated with antimicrobial resistance and virulence. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the antibacterial activity and molecular mechanisms of PACs against MRSA using microbiological assays, analyses of membrane integrity and oxidative stress, transcriptomic profiling, RT-qPCR, molecular docking, surface plasmon resonance (SPR), and protein expression analysis.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains

The MRSA reference strain S. aureus ATCC 33591 was obtained from the Laboratory of Veterinary Pharmacology and Toxicology, China Agricultural University. Clinical MRSA isolates (XF6, W19, W10, and SCL) were isolated from mastitic milk samples collected from dairy cows in Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, China.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) assays

PACs (analytical standard, UV purity ≥95%) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of PACs against MRSA was determined using the broth microdilution method in 96-well plates according to the CLSI guidelines. Briefly, 100 μL of Mueller-Hinton broth (MHB) was dispensed into wells 1 to 10 of each row. Subsequently, 100 μL of PACs stock solution was added to well 1, yielding an initial concentration of 2.496 mg/mL in a final volume of 200 μL. Two-fold serial dilutions were then performed by transferring 100 μL from well 1 to well 2, mixing thoroughly, and continuing this stepwise transfer through well 10; the final 100 μL taken from well 10 was discarded. The MICs of vancomycin, oxacillin, and flucloxacillin against MRSA were determined using the same procedure. Thereafter, 100 μL of MRSA suspension (106 Colony-Forming Unit (CFU)/mL in MHB) was inoculated into wells 1–10. Wells 11 and 12 served as the growth control (bacterial suspension without PACs) and sterility control (MHB only), respectively. After incubation at 37°C for 18 h, the MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of PACs that completely inhibited visible bacterial growth. For minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) determination, 50 μL aliquots from wells showing no visible growth were spread onto Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA) plates and incubated at 37°C for 18 h. The lowest concentration yielding no colonies was recorded as the MBC. Bacterial suspensions (106 CFU/mL) were serially diluted 10-fold (10−1 to 10−6), and 10 μL aliquots of each dilution were spotted onto LB agar plates supplemented with different concentrations of PACs. Colony growth was recorded after incubation at 37°C for 18 h. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Time–growth curve analysis

Time–growth kinetics were assessed by exposing MRSA to PACs at concentrations of 1/4×, 1/2×, 1×, 2×, and 4× MIC, with untreated cultures serving as controls. 100 μL of bacterial suspension adjusted to 106 CFU/mL was inoculated into 9.9 mL of Tryptic Soy Broth containing different concentrations of PACs, resulting in an initial bacterial density of approximately 104 CFU/mL. Each condition was tested in triplicate. At 2-h intervals, 100 μL aliquots were collected for measurement of optical density at 600 nm (OD600) using a microplate reader. In parallel, aliquots were serially diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), plated onto MHA, and incubated at 37°C for 18 h before colony counting. Growth curves were generated by plotting OD600 values against incubation time.

Ultrastructural analysis by electron microscopy

MRSA cultures at mid-log phase were harvested, washed three times with PBS, and resuspended in MHB supplemented with PACs (1× and 2× MIC). Following incubation at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm for 4 h, bacteria were fixed with 3% glutaraldehyde at 4°C for 12 h. After PBS washes, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) samples were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 1–2 h, rinsed with ultrapure water, and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%). Dehydrated samples were critical-point dried, mounted on silicon wafers, sputter-coated with gold (Smart Coater, Japan), and imaged using SEM (JSM-IT700HR, Japan). For transmission electron microscopy (TEM), osmium tetroxide-fixed samples were dehydrated in graded acetone (30%–100%) and embedded in Epon-812 resin via acetone:resin infiltration (3:1, 1:1, and 1:3). Ultrathin sections (60 nm) were prepared and double-stained with uranyl acetate (15 min) and lead citrate (2 min). Ultrastructural alterations were examined by TEM (JEM-1400FLASH, Japan).

Biofilm inhibition and eradication assays

To assess biofilm formation, 100 μL of logarithmic-phase MRSA suspension (106 CFU/mL) was mixed with an equal volume of PACs solution in 96-well plates to achieve final PACs concentrations of 0, 2.44, 4.88, 9.75, 19.5, 39, 78, 156, and 312 μg/mL in a total volume of 200 μL per well. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 h, and each treatment was performed in triplicate. The culture medium was then aspirated, and non-adherent cells were gently washed away three times with PBS. Biofilms were stained with 200 μL of 1% crystal violet for 30 min at 37°C, followed by three PBS washes. Untreated bacterial cultures and MHB alone served as the positive and blank controls, respectively. The bound crystal violet was solubilized in 75% ethanol, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. To assess polysaccharide intercellular adhesin (PIA/PNAG) production, 10 μL aliquots of bacterial suspensions were spotted onto Congo-red agar (Table S1) and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Colony pigmentation intensity was recorded as a semi-quantitative indicator of PIA/PNAG production.

Effects of PACs on membrane disruption and oxidative stress in MRSA

Membrane permeabilization assessment

Bacterial cultures were diluted 1:100 in LB medium and grown to an OD600 of approximately 0.5. After centrifugation, the pellet was washed three times and resuspended in medium containing PACs at final concentrations of 0, 1/16×, 1/8×, 1/4×, 1/2×, and 1× MIC, followed by incubation at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm for 2 h. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation, and absorbance at 260 nm and 280 nm was measured to assess nucleic acid and protein leakage, respectively. N-phenyl-1-naphthylamine (NPN: 10 μM), propidium iodide (PI: 10 nM), and 3,3′-dipropylthiadicarbocyanine iodide [DiSC3(5): 0.5 μM] were added to bacterial suspensions treated as described above. After incubation at 37°C for 2 h in the dark, the cells were washed three times with PBS to remove unbound probes. Fluorescence intensity was subsequently measured using a multimode microplate reader (SuPerMax 3100, China) at excitation/emission wavelengths of 350/420 nm for NPN, 535/615 nm for PI, and 622/670 nm for DiSC3(5). Unstained bacterial suspensions and probe-only controls were included as autofluorescence and background controls, respectively. All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates, each containing three technical replicates. To validate the involvement of membrane damage in the antibacterial activity of PACs, bacterial suspensions were pre-incubated with the membrane stabilizer vitamin E (VE) for 30 min prior to PACs treatment. Membrane permeability and membrane potential parameters were then measured as described above.

Measurement of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels

Following PACs treatment (Section 2.6.1), cells were washed and resuspended in MHB containing 10 μM 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). The suspension was incubated at 37°C for 30 min in the dark with gentle mixing every 5 min, followed by three washes with PBS to remove unbound probe. Subsequently, 200 μL of each sample was transferred into black 96-well plates, and fluorescence intensity was measured using a multimode microplate reader at excitation/emission wavelengths of 488/525 nm. Unstained bacterial suspensions and probe-only controls were included to correct for cellular autofluorescence and background fluorescence, respectively. All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates, each containing three technical replicates.

Determination of intracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels

Bacterial cultures were grown to mid-log phase (OD600 = 0.5) in LB medium, washed, and exposed to PACs at concentrations of 0, 1/16×, 1/8×, 1/4×, 1/2×, and 1× MIC for 4 h at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm. Cells were then collected and lysed using the lysis buffer from an Enhanced ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. An ATP standard curve was prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions. For measurement, 100 μL of ATP detection working solution was added to each well of a 96-well plate and incubated for 5 min to eliminate background luminescence. Then, 20 μL of sample or ATP standard was added, rapidly mixed and immediately measured using a luminometer. ATP levels were calculated based on the standard curve and expressed relative to the untreated control.

NAD+/NADH assay

Following PACs treatment (Section 2.6.1), MRSA cultures were washed thrice and lysed with 200 μL of NAD+/NADH extraction buffer (UElandy, China). The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000× g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. The NAD+ and NADH were determined using an enzymatic cycling assay. Briefly, 20 μL of sample or NADH standard was mixed with 90 μL of freshly prepared alcohol dehydrogenase working solution in a 96-well plate to measure total NAD(H). To quantify NADH specifically, a separate 50 μL aliquot of the sample was heated at 60°C for 30 min to decompose NAD+. Following the addition of 10 μL chromogenic solution and incubation at 25°C for 30 min in the dark, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. NAD+ levels were calculated by subtraction.

Intracellular H2O2 quantification

MRSA cells treated with PACs were washed and lysed in 200 μL of ice-cold lysis buffer (Hydrogen Peroxide Assay Kit, Beyotime, China). After centrifugation at 12,000× g for 5 min at 4°C, the supernatant was collected and assayed immediately. H2O2 standard curve (0–100 μM) was prepared concurrently. In a 96-well plate, 50 μL of supernatant or standard was mixed with 100 μL of detection reagent. Following 30 min of incubation at 25°C, the absorbance was measured at 560 nm.

N-acetyl-L-Cysteine (NAC) validation of PACs-induced oxidative stress

To further verify the involvement of oxidative stress in the antibacterial activity of PACs, a ROS-scavenging assay was performed using N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC). MRSA suspensions were pretreated with NAC for 30 min and subsequently exposed to PACs under the same conditions described above. Intracellular ROS levels, H2O2 accumulation, NAD+/NADH ratio, and ATP levels were then determined as described in Sections 2.6.2–2.6.5. The protective effect of NAC against PACs-induced oxidative damage was evaluated by comparing these parameters between NAC-pretreated and PACs-only treated groups.

Transcriptomic analysis

MRSA was cultured to OD600 = 0.5; 36 mL aliquots (n = 6) were withdrawn. Three aliquots were treated with PACs at 1/8 MIC, whereas the remaining three received vehicle only (negative controls). Cultures were incubated at 37°C with shaking (200 rpm) for 4 h in the dark. Cells were harvested, washed three times with PBS, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 10 min. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and RNA integrity was assessed prior to library construction and sequencing.

RNA libraries were prepared and sequenced by Genedenovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with a paired-end 150 bp (PE150) sequencing strategy. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using a threshold of false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 2. Principal component analysis was performed in R software (https://www.r-project.org/) to visualize sample-related variance. Shared and unique genes across groups were identified based on expression abundance and visualized using the VennDiagram and UpSetR packages. DEGs were z-score-transformed, hierarchically clustered, and displayed as heatmaps. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment was tested by hypergeometric test against the genome background, and significantly enriched GO terms (FDR < 0.05) were identified. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment was analogously performed at the KEGG Orthology (KO) level. Pathway-level gene counts were summarized at KEGG level 2, and significant pathways were visualized as bubble plots (FDR < 0.05). All raw sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject accession number PRJNA1467155.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was reverse-transcribed with the M5 SuperFast qPCR RT kit (Mei5bio, China) per the manufacturer's instructions (Table S2). Complementary DNA (1 ng) was amplified in 20 μL reactions using the M5 HiPer One-Step qRT-PCR kit (Mei5bio) with gene-specific primers (Tables S3 and S4). The gyrB served as the internal reference gene. RT-qPCR amplification was performed using a CFX96 Touch™ (Bio-Rad, USA) with initial denaturation (94°C 2 min) and 40 cycles (95°C, 5 s; 55°C, 34 s; 72°C, 30 s). Relative expression was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Molecular docking

Penicillin-binding protein 2a (PBP2a) and MgrA sequences from S. aureus were retrieved from NCBI Nucleotide and homology-modelled in SWISS-MODEL Interactive Workspace (templates 1VQQ and 2bv6.1.A). The resulting structural models were downloaded in Protein Data Bank (PDB) format. SarA crystal structures (PDB IDs: 2FNP) were obtained from the RCSB PDB. The two-dimensional structure of PACs was downloaded from PubChem (CID: 107876) and converted into PDB format with PyMOL. Protein structures of PBP2a, MgrA, and SarA were prepared in AutoDockTools-1.5.6 by removing water molecules, adding polar hydrogens, and saving as PDBQT. The PACs ligand was protonated, assigned rotatable bonds, and converted into PDBQT format. Semi-flexible docking (10 runs) was performed with AutoDock Vina 1.1.2. The docking conformation exhibiting the lowest predicted binding energy and optimal structural complementarity was selected for subsequent visualization and interaction analysis using Discovery Studio 2020 (Dassault Systemes BIOVIA, San Diego, USA).

Western blot

Bacterial pellets from PACs-treated MRSA cultures (Section 2.6.1) were lysed in 1× Laemmli buffer (100°C, 5 min). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE at 200 V for 1 h and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes at 300 mA for 2 h. After blocking with 5% skim milk in TBST for 2 h, membranes were incubated with rabbit polyclonal antibodies against PBP2a, SarA, and MgrA for 2 h, washed three times with TBST, and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (2 h). Following three further washes with TBST, protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system, and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Antibiotic potentiation assay

MHA plates containing 1/2 MIC of PACs or PACs-free controls were prepared at temperatures below 50°C. After solidification, plates were uniformly spread with MRSA suspensions (OD600 = 0.1). Antibiotic discs were applied, and plates were incubated at 37°C for 20 h to assess the potentiation effect of PACs on antibiotic susceptibility.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

Recombinant SarA protein was expressed in E. coli following isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) induction and purified by Ni2+ affinity chromatography. SPR experiments were performed by SpecAlly Life Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). SarA protein was immobilized onto a CM5 sensor chip by standard amine-coupling chemistry. Serial concentrations of PACs prepared in running buffer were injected over the sensor surface (30 μL/min). Binding kinetics were analyzed using Biacore Insight Evaluation Software (Version 6.0) with a 1:1 Langmuir binding model to determine the association rate constant (Ka), dissociation rate constant (Kd), and equilibrium dissociation constant (KD).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation from at least three independent biological experiments. Statistical significance was analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by appropriate multiple-comparison tests. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Assessment of antibacterial activity of PACs

All MRSA isolates remained susceptible to vancomycin, with MIC values ranging from 0.25 to 2 μg/mL. In contrast, high MIC values were observed for oxacillin (8–64 μg/mL) and flucloxacillin (8–128 μg/mL), confirming the methicillin-resistant phenotype of the tested isolates (Table S5). PACs exhibited MIC/MBC values of 156/312 μg/mL against ATCC 33591, W10, and SCL, whereas XF6 and W19 showed relatively higher values of 312/624 μg/mL (Fig. 1A). These MIC values were substantially lower than those reported for cyanidin (5 mg/mL) (Dong et al., 2022; Gong et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022), consistent with earlier studies demonstrating the antimicrobial potential of PACs (Denev et al., 2019). Notably, these MIC values against MRSA were lower than those observed against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Streptococcus agalactiae (Fig. S1), indicating a relatively higher susceptibility of MRSA to PACs. At 156 μg/mL, no colonies were recovered at 10−3 and 10−4 dilutions of ATCC 33591 or XF6 cultures, and complete growth inhibition was achieved at 312 μg/mL across 10−1–10−6 dilutions (Fig. 1B). Growth curve analysis demonstrated time-dependent inhibition of MRSA strains W19, XF6, and ATCC 33591 following PACs treatment, with the most pronounced suppression occurring within the first 4 h of exposure (Fig. 1C). As shown in Fig. 1D, PACs treatment (39–156 μg/mL) significantly inhibited biofilm formation in all tested MRSA isolates (P < 0.01). SEM revealed that untreated XF6 cells exhibited dense distribution and intact morphology, whereas PACs-treated cells appeared sparse and morphologically altered, accompanied by surface-associated PACs aggregates and apparent membrane damage (Fig. 1E). TEM further revealed substantial ultrastructural alterations following PACs treatment, including disruption of cell-envelope integrity, vesicle formation, and leakage of intracellular contents (Fig. 1F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PACs exhibit moderate antibacterial activity against MRSA, inhibit biofilm formation at subinhibitory concentrations, and induce marked morphological and ultrastructural damage. Moreover, PACs exhibited stronger antibacterial activity against MRSA compared to Gram-negative pathogens (Maisuria et al., 2022). Li et al. (2022) reported that acylated cyanidin-3-glucoside caused S. aureus membrane damage, with MIC and MBC values of 312.5 μg/mL and 625 μg/mL, respectively. Notably, comparable antibacterial activity was achieved in the present study using native PACs without chemical modification, highlighting their potential as naturally derived antimicrobial agents against MRSA.

Assessment of PACs-induced membrane damage and oxidative stress in MRSA

To investigate the antibacterial mechanism of PACs, membrane integrity and oxidative stress-related parameters were evaluated in MRSA. PACs treatment resulted in a concentration-dependent increase in nucleotide leakage (Fig. 2A) and a significant elevation in protein leakage (P < 0.05; Fig. 2B). In addition, PI fluorescence, an indicator of compromised membrane integrity, increased significantly at concentrations ≥ 1/8 × MIC (P < 0.01; Fig. 2C). NPN and DiSC3(5) fluorescence intensities also increased markedly following PACs treatment (P < 0.001), indicating enhanced membrane permeability and membrane depolarization in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 2D and E). PACs treatment resulted in a dose-dependent increase in ROS levels (P < 0.001, Fig. 2F). Concurrently, intracellular H2O2 levels increased significantly with increasing PACs concentrations and became statistically significant at concentrations ≥1/8 × MIC (P < 0.001; Fig. 2G). Moreover, exposure to PACs caused a significant reduction in the intracellular NAD+/NADH ratio (P < 0.01; Fig. 2H), suggesting alterations in cellular redox homeostasis. In bacteria, elevated levels of ROS, particularly O2− and H2O2, inactivate critical enzymes, damage macromolecules (proteins, lipids, DNA), and compromise membrane integrity (Li et al., 2024b; Yadav et al., 2020), collectively triggering cell death (Ezraty et al., 2017; Qi et al., 2022; Sheng et al., 2022). Superoxide dismutase (SodA) catalyzes the dismutation O2− to H2O2, whereas AhpC reduces H2O2 and organic peroxides, thereby maintaining cellular redox balance (Martini et al., 2025). Consequently, PACs-mediated repression of sigB, ahpC and sodA elevates intracellular ROS levels, a mechanism analogous to the anti-staphylococcal action of naringenin and bisdemethoxycurcumin (Bloise et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2024). Membrane disruption results in envelope depolarization, facilitating ATP leakage and impairing energy synthesis (Li et al., 2022; Weng et al., 2024); this collapse is further exacerbated by ROS-mediated inhibition of the F0F1-ATPase complex (Zhang et al., 2025). Accumulated H2O2 drives Fenton chemistry, generating hydroxyl radicals that propagate lipid peroxidation, enlarge membrane pores, and promote efflux of NAD+/NADH, establishing a self-amplifying cytotoxic cycle. Collectively, PACs exert lethal membrane–stress synergy by simultaneous blocking fatty-acid synthesis and collapsing antioxidant defense, causing envelope rupture and osmotic lysis of MRSA.

Regulation of fatty-acid biosynthesis- and oxidative-stress genes by PACs in MRSA

S. aureus synthesizes phosphatidic acid (PA) through the essential type II fatty-acid synthesis (FASII) pathway, which consists of distinct initiation and elongation stages (Pang et al., 2019).The acyl-transferase fabD and the β-ketoacyl-ACP synthase III fabH drive initiation, whereas the elongation cycle is sustained by fabF (synthase II), fabG (β-ketoacyl-ACP reductase) and fabI (enoyl-ACP reductase)(Pang et al., 2019). Following PACs treatment, transcriptional reprogramming of the FASII operon was observed. fabD and fabH were consistently downregulated at all concentrations tested (P < 0.05; Fig. 4A and B). The fabG expression was significantly suppressed across the entire concentration range (P < 0.01; Fig. 4D). In contrast, fabF and fabI exhibited a biphasic response: transient upregulation at 1/16 MIC (P < 0.05) followed by pronounced downregulation at ≥ 1/4 MIC (P < 0.05; Fig. 4C and E). These findings suggest that PACs interfere with both the initiation and elongation phases of fatty-acid biosynthesis in MRSA. Fatty acid composition governs membrane fluidity, making fab expression a proxy for antimicrobial-induced membrane perturbation (Li et al., 2024a). Sigma factor B (sigB), a global regulator involved in environmental adaptation and stress tolerance in S. aureus (Qi et al., 2022), was significantly downregulated following 4 h of PACs exposure, with repression ranging from 3.3- to 9.2-fold across the tested concentrations (P < 0.001; Fig. 4F). Consistent with this trend, the antioxidant defense-related genes katE (catalase) and g6pd (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase) were significantly reduced by 2.8- to 50.6-fold and 1.5- to 10.8-fold, respectively (P < 0.05; Fig. 4G and H). The organic-hydroperoxide reductase gene ahpC was significantly downregulated at all tested concentrations (P < 0.05 to P < 0.001), whereas sodA (superoxide dismutase) displayed a transient increase in expression at 1/16 × and 1/8 × MIC, followed by significant repression at concentrations ≥1/2 × MIC (P < 0.05; Fig. 4I and J). These transcriptional changes indicate that PACs modulate oxidative stress responses in MRSA. The observed downregulation of multiple antioxidant defense-related genes is consistent with the increased intracellular ROS and H2O2 levels detected following PACs treatment, suggesting a potential association between oxidative stress accumulation and impaired bacterial stress adaptation. Simultaneously, PACs suppressed the global regulator sigB, which coordinates biofilm formation, oxidative stress responses, and FASII gene transcription (Qi et al., 2022). This dual inhibition parallels the effect of naringenin reported by He et al. (2019), wherein sigB inhibition resulted in decreased fab expression.

Mechanism of PACs-mediated potentiation of β-lactams against MRSA

Plant-derived compounds can act synergistically with conventional antibiotics to resensitize drug-resistant pathogens (Liang et al., 2022). In MRSA, resistance to β-lactam antibiotics is primarily mediated by the acquisition of PBP2a, a low-affinity penicillin-binding protein encoded by mecA, enabling peptidoglycan cross-linking and cell-wall biosynthesis even under antibiotic pressure (Liang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020). Disk diffusion assays revealed that 156 μg/ml PACs significantly potentiated the activity of β-lactam antibiotics, increasing inhibition zones for cefoxitin (FOX, +8 mm), ampicillin (AMP, +9 mm), penicillin (P, +10 mm), meropenem (MEM, +11 mm), amoxicillin (AMC, +11 mm), methicillin (MET, +12 mm), and cefotaxime (CTX, +23 mm) (Fig. 7A). Similar synergy has been reported for cranberry-derived PACs, which restores susceptibility to oxacillin and carbenicillin (Gallique et al., 2021). Additionally, Yadav et al. Furthermore, Western blot analysis further showed that PACs (1/8–1 MIC) significantly suppressed production of PBP2a, SarA, and MgrA (P < 0.001; Fig. 7F, Fig. S5). SarA downregulation was clearly dose-dependent, whereas MgrA suppression was most pronounced at lower PACs concentrations. These results suggest that PACs enhance the susceptibility of MRSA to β-lactam antibiotics and are associated with reduced expression of resistance- and virulence-related regulators. Collectively, these findings indicate that PACs enhance β-lactam activity against MRSA via dual targeting of PBP2a expression and oxidative-stress modulation, offering a translational route for treating bovine mastitis caused by antibiotic-resistant staphylococci.

Regulatory network analysis of the antibacterial mechanism of PACs against MRSA

The environmental resilience and escalating multidrug resistance of MRSA pose a formidable public health threat (Cheng et al., 2021; Refai et al., 2023). Plant-derived natural products offer a promising therapeutic approach to overcome antibiotic resistance while mitigating ecological toxicity (Li et al., 2024b; Keskey et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2021). PACs are widely available natural polyphenols with documented efficacy against diverse bacterial pathogens and a favorable safety profile (Chen et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021; Zhu and Oteiza, 2024). Based on these properties, we systematically investigated their anti-MRSA activity and underlying mechanism.

To define the mechanism by which PACs impair MRSA fitness, we built a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network using transcriptomic and qPCR-validated DEGs. After hiding disconnected nodes, the network comprised 61 nodes and 82 edges with average node degree of 3.37 (Fig. 9A). Among the top-five hub proteins (degree ≥9) down-regulated by 1/8 MIC PACs are PolA (DNA repair and oxidative-stress response), RecG (oxidative/DNA-damage response and β-lactam-induced upregulation), RpsQ (ribosome biogenesis), RsmA (antibiotic sensitivity and ribosome maturation) and MutL (DNA mismatch repair protein and antibiotic resistance) (Fig. 9B). Global regulators MgrA and SarA, themselves PACs targets, in turn down-regulate blaZ, sarV, clfA and mecA, reducing BlaZ, SarV and PBP2A levels and consequently β-lactam resistance. Transcriptomic profiling pinpointed three key pathways targeted by PACs, namely peptidoglycan biosynthesis (ko00550), teichoic acid biosynthesis (ko00552), and ABC transporters (ko02010)—all critical for maintaining cell wall integrity in MRSA (Fig. 9D). Inhibition of Mur enzyme-catalyzed assembly of muramic acid-peptide chains—where PBPs mediate final transpeptidation—compromised formation of the highly crosslinked Lys-type peptidoglycan scaffold characteristic of Gram-positive bacteria. Disruption of poly-glycerolphosphate assembly initiated by TagO impaired the membrane-anchored synthesis pathway (TarO-TarA-TarB-TarF-TarL). ABC transporter TarGH failed to flip the lipid-linked precursor, and LCP enzymes could not transfer GroP chains to MurNAc, preventing covalent “PolyGroP-WTA-peptidoglycan” assembly. Loss of PolyGroP charge neutralization reduced resistance to cationic antimicrobial peptides, impairing cell wall stability and initial biofilm adhesion. Moreover, by inhibiting ABC-dependent efflux pumps such as NorA (Nishino et al., 2021), PACs increase intracellular drug accumulation, leading to metabolic toxicity and cell death (Fig. 9D). Collectively, PACs simultaneously disrupt wall synthesis, biofilm formation, oxidative stress adaptation and multidrug transport, compromising MRSA morphology and fitness.

Conclusion

PACs exhibited moderate antibacterial activity against MRSA and inhibited biofilm formation. Mechanistic studies indicated that PACs induced membrane damage and oxidative stress, resulting in increased ROS and H2O2 accumulation, disruption of redox homeostasis, and depletion of intracellular ATP. Transcriptomic, RT-qPCR, and protein expression analyses revealed alterations in genes and proteins associated with fatty-acid biosynthesis, oxidative stress responses, antibiotic resistance, and virulence regulation. PACs also enhanced the susceptibility of MRSA to β-lactam antibiotics by reducing mecA expression and decreasing the protein levels of PBP2a, SarA, and MgrA. Furthermore, SPR analysis supported a direct interaction between PACs and SarA. Collectively, these findings suggest that PACs exert anti-MRSA activity through multiple complementary mechanisms and may represent a promising natural antimicrobial agent or antibiotic adjuvant for controlling MRSA infections.
