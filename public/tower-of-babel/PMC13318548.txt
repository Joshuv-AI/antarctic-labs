Necrotizing fasciitis in an immunocompetent child with a recent history of varicella-zoster virus infection: A case report from a resource-limited setting
Authors: Abdullahi Hassan Elmi, Fahma Hussein Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNecrotizing fasciitis is a rare but life-threatening soft-tissue infection characterized by rapid progression, extensive tissue destruction, and high morbidity and mortality. Secondary bacterial infections may occur after varicella-zoster virus infection; however, the timing and clinical context are essential when interpreting causality. We report the case of an 8-year-old previously healthy girl who presented with high-grade fever, severe pain, and rapidly progressive necrotic soft-tissue infection involving the right leg and buttock. She had a documented history of chickenpox two months before presentation, with residual healed scars noted on examination. Given the prolonged interval and absence of active varicella lesions, a direct causal relationship between varicella and necrotizing fasciitis could not be confirmed. Laboratory investigations showed leukocytosis, severe anemia, hyponatremia, markedly elevated C-reactive protein, and a Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis score of 10, supporting a high-risk diagnosis. Wound culture grew Proteus mirabilis, an uncommon organism in necrotizing fasciitis, and treatment was guided by culture sensitivity. The patient improved following prompt supportive care, intravenous antibiotics, urgent surgical debridement, and continued wound management. This case highlights the importance of cautious interpretation of remote varicella history, early recognition of necrotizing fasciitis, timely surgical intervention, culture-guided antimicrobial therapy, and multidisciplinary care, particularly in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Necrotizing fasciitis, Varicella-zoster virus, Proteus mirabilis, Pediatric infection, Resource-limited setting

Introduction

Chickenpox is generally a mild viral infection in childhood, yet its clinical course can be considerably more severe in adults and in people with compromised immunity. Approximately 47% of reported cases occur in adults, with males representing the predominant group affected [1]. Necrotizing fasciitis is an uncommon yet life-threatening soft-tissue infection involving the fascia, the connective layer encasing muscles, nerves, and blood vessels. The condition progresses swiftly, causing extensive tissue destruction and potentially leading to systemic organ failure if not treated promptly [1]. In uncommon instances, severe varicella infection can predispose patients to secondary bacterial complications, including necrotizing fasciitis.

This risk is particularly elevated in individuals with impaired immunity, such as those living with HIV, undergoing cancer treatment, or receiving immunosuppressive medications. Necrotizing fasciitis, although rare, is a rapidly progressive and potentially fatal soft-tissue infection that constitutes a true surgical and medical emergency [2]. Early vaccinations against varicella and prompt management of any ensuing bacterial infection are essential to minimizing the likelihood of serious outcomes such as necrotizing fasciitis [2], [3].

The diagnosis of necrotizing fasciitis relies on clinical findings supported by laboratory indicators, particularly through the use of scoring systems that incorporate C-reactive protein levels, total leukocyte count, serum sodium, serum creatinine, and blood glucose values [2]. A LRINEC score of ≥ 6 has been shown to yield positive and negative predictive values of approximately 92% and 96%, respectively. Here, we describe a case of necrotizing fasciitis in an immunocompetent pediatric patient with a recent history of varicella infection. Because the varicella episode occurred two months before presentation, the association should be interpreted cautiously as possible or remote rather than confirmed direct post-varicella necrotizing fasciitis.

Case presentation

An 8-year-old girl presented to the emergency department with a high-grade fever, severe pain, and a rapidly enlarging abscess involving the right leg and buttock region. The abscess began insidiously in the right leg and buttock area and progressively extended into the posterior and lateral compartments of the buttock over six days Two months earlier, she had a documented episode of chickenpox, and residual healed scars remained visible across her body. There were no active varicella lesions at the time of admission. Given the two-month interval, these healed scars may represent a previous varicella infection rather than a definite direct portal of entry for the current necrotizing soft-tissue infection. She had no documented vaccination history. On examination, she was conscious, alert, febrile, and visibly dehydrated, with widespread healed varicella scars. There was no jaundice, no sunken eyes, and her oral mucosa appeared dry. She appeared cachectic and markedly thin. Her vital signs were as follows: temperature 39 °C, blood pressure 109/61 mm Hg, heart rate 156 beats/min, and respiratory rate 31 breaths/min.

The affected areas showed zones of necrosis with foul-smelling discharge involving the leg and buttock. The infection extended deeply, with both the subcutaneous tissue and underlying skeletal muscle visibly compromised by the abscess. Following stabilization, transfusion with packed red blood cells was administered. Once the patient was stabilized, the general surgery team was consulted. They prepared the operating room and performed surgical debridement, removing the abscesses and all necrotic or blackened tissue. Intraoperatively, extensive necrotic and blackened soft tissue was observed in the affected leg and buttock region, with foul-smelling purulent discharge and deep extension into the subcutaneous tissue and adjacent muscle planes. All visibly nonviable tissue was removed during debridement until healthier tissue margins were reached. Specific hallmark operative findings such as dishwater fluid, easy separation of fascial planes, and bleeding characteristics were not fully documented, which represents a limitation of this case report. Postoperatively, the patient was monitored in the recovery unit. She remained hemodynamically stable and was able to tolerate small amounts of oral fluids. The patient was initially managed with broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics, analgesia, fluid resuscitation, and supportive care. Following culture and sensitivity results, the abscess culture grew Proteus mirabilis sensitive to meropenem, and antimicrobial therapy was adjusted to intravenous meropenem 500 mg three times daily. The patient continued combined antibiotic therapy and wound care for four weeks. She showed progressive clinical improvement and was discharged with scheduled outpatient follow-up

Discussion

Necrotizing fasciitis (NF) is an uncommon but life-threatening soft-tissue infection characterized by rapid progression and extensive tissue destruction. While typically associated with immunocompromised states, NF can rarely develop in otherwise healthy, immunocompetent children, often as a secondary complication of varicella-zoster virus (VZV) infection. Our patient was an 8-year-old immunocompetent child who developed necrotizing fasciitis in the context of a recent history of varicella infection. However, because the chickenpox episode occurred two months before presentation and no active varicella lesions were present, a direct causal relationship cannot be confirmed. The association is therefore best interpreted as possible or remote rather than definite post-varicella necrotizing fasciitis. Post-varicella invasive bacterial complications are usually reported shortly after the onset of skin lesions, commonly within days to a few weeks. In the present case, the two-month interval weakens causal inference. Therefore, alternative explanations should be considered, including an unrelated bacterial soft-tissue infection, delayed presentation, local skin trauma, malnutrition or poor nutritional status, and limited wound care access in a resource-limited setting. Varicella infections in children are usually mild, yet complications, including bacterial superinfections, are well-documented. Group A Streptococcus and Staphylococcus aureus are more commonly reported in necrotizing fasciitis associated with varicella. In contrast, Proteus mirabilis is an uncommon isolate in necrotizing fasciitis. In this case, Proteus mirabilis was isolated from the wound culture and was sensitive to meropenem. This may represent monomicrobial Gram-negative necrotizing fasciitis; however, polymicrobial infection cannot be fully excluded because microbiological testing may not have captured all organisms, particularly in a resource-limited setting or after prior antibiotic exposure [4], [5], [6]. The high LRINEC score (10) in our patient corroborated the severity of infection and served as a valuable tool in early recognition, consistent with previous studies demonstrating the utility of the LRINEC score in predicting NF risk [2].

Prompt recognition and management of NF are critical due to its rapid progression and high mortality rates. Early surgical intervention remains the cornerstone of treatment, combined with broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics and supportive care. In our case, emergent surgical debridement, along with intravenous meropenem tailored to culture sensitivity, led to marked clinical improvement and eventual discharge. This aligns with current literature emphasizing that aggressive early surgical management and targeted antimicrobial therapy significantly reduce morbidity and mortality in pediatric NF [2], [7], [8], [9]. This case also underscores the importance of preventive strategies. Varicella vaccination remains the most effective measure to reduce the incidence of primary infection and subsequent severe bacterial complications, including NF [1], [3]. Additionally, heightened clinical vigilance for signs of secondary bacterial infection following varicella is essential, even in immunocompetent children, particularly in settings with limited access to healthcare resources. Delayed presentation, malnutrition, and inadequate initial wound care may exacerbate disease severity and complicate management [10]. This case has several limitations. First, the two-month interval between chickenpox and necrotizing fasciitis limits the ability to establish direct causation. Second, clinical photographs were not available for inclusion. Third, some classic intraoperative features of necrotizing fasciitis were not fully documented. Finally, microbiological evaluation was limited to the organisms recovered on wound culture, so polymicrobial infection cannot be completely excluded [10].

Finally, this case highlights the unique challenges of managing complex infections in resource-limited environments. Access to prompt surgical care, advanced laboratory diagnostics, and appropriate antibiotics is often constrained, which can delay diagnosis and worsen outcomes. Multidisciplinary coordination among pediatricians, surgeons, and infectious disease specialists is essential to optimize recovery in such settings [11]. Post-varicella necrotizing fasciitis, although rare, can occur in immunocompetent children and demands rapid recognition and aggressive multidisciplinary management. Early surgical intervention, targeted antibiotic therapy guided by culture results, and supportive care are critical to favorable outcomes. This case further reinforces the value of preventive vaccination and heightened clinical vigilance for secondary bacterial infections following varicella, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Conclusion

Necrotizing fasciitis is a surgical and medical emergency that can occur in immunocompetent children and requires early recognition, prompt surgical debridement, broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy, and culture-guided treatment. In this case, the child had a documented history of varicella infection two months before presentation; however, the prolonged interval means that a direct causal relationship cannot be confirmed. The case should therefore be interpreted as necrotizing fasciitis with a possible remote association with prior varicella infection rather than definite post-varicella necrotizing fasciitis. This report also highlights the importance of careful clinical documentation, microbiological interpretation, and timely multidisciplinary management in resource-limited settings.
