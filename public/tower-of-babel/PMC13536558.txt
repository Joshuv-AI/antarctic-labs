The relationship between erythrocyte transfusion in very preterm infants and inflammatory cytokines and morbidities: a prospective observational study
Authors: Fatmakhanım Osmanova, Ali Bulbul, Evrim Kıray Bas, Hasan Avsar, Alper Divarcı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt is essential to assess not only the hematologic outcomes but also the immunologic and inflammatory consequences of RBC transfusion in preterm infants.AimsThis study aimed to evaluate changes in serum cytokine levels following red blood cell (RBC) transfusion in preterm infants born at ≤ 32 weeks of gestation and to assess their potential proinflammatory effects and associations with neonatal morbidities.Materials and methodsThis prospective, single-center observational study included preterm infants born at ≤ 32 weeks of gestation, aged > 7 days, who received RBC transfusion. Serum interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and melatonin were measured before and at 2 and 24 h after transfusion. Insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) and IGF-binding protein-3 (IGFBP-3) were analyzed at 1, 2, and 3 weeks post-transfusion.ResultsThirty-three infants met the inclusion criteria. The mean gestational age was 27.4 ± 2.7 weeks, and the mean birth weight was 1016 ± 356 g. Following transfusion, the hematocrit increased from 28.3 ± 3.1% to 35.0 ± 3.8%. No significant post-transfusion changes were observed in IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, melatonin, or IGF-1 levels. However, VEGF increased (p = 0.045) and IGFBP-3 decreased (p = 0.022) significantly. During follow-up, clinical sepsis was observed in 7 (21.2%), 4 (12.1%), and 1 (3.0%) infants at weeks 1, 2, and 3, respectively (p = 0.007). Respiratory support requirements decreased over time, particularly for non-invasive synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation (p = 0.029).ConclusionsIn this single-arm observational cohort, RBC transfusion was followed by increased VEGF and decreased IGFBP-3 levels, without significant changes in major proinflammatory cytokines. During follow-up, respiratory support requirements decreased and clinical sepsis was observed in a subset of infants. However, because no non-transfused control group was included, these clinical findings should be interpreted as descriptive and exploratory temporal observations rather than evidence of causality.

Introduction

Anemia develops rapidly in preterm infants as a result of frequent phlebotomy and immature hematopoietic function. Consequently, the majority of very low birth weight (VLBW) infants receive at least one erythrocyte transfusion during the early postnatal period [1–4]. In preterm infants, particularly those born at ≤ 32 weeks of gestation, red blood cell (RBC) transfusion represents not only a hematologic intervention but also a biological event capable of modifying inflammatory responses and influencing neonatal immune homeostasis [5, 6].

The relationship between RBC transfusion and neonatal morbidities—such as necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), and bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD)-remains controversial [7–10]. Evidence suggests that both severe anemia and the transfusions themselves may contribute to the pathogenesis of these conditions [11–13]. However, due to the inherent complexity and vulnerability of premature infants’ pathophysiological processes, the individual contributions of anemia and transfusion exposure cannot yet be clearly delineated.

Although the association between anemia and the development of sepsis remains controversial [13–15], recent studies have reported an increased incidence of sepsis in preterm infants following erythrocyte transfusion, even in the absence of preceding infection [16, 17]. These findings suggest that RBC transfusion may directly influence immune and inflammatory pathways beyond its role in restoring hematologic stability. Alterations in proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), along with vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), insulin-like growth factor-binding protein-3 (IGFBP-3), and melatonin levels measured before and after transfusion, support the biological basis for this relationship [10, 18–21].

Melatonin was included in the present study because of its well-established antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties and its potential role in modulating oxidative stress-related injury in preterm infants [20]. Although a direct mechanistic relationship between RBC transfusion and melatonin regulation has not been clearly established, transfusion-related oxidative stress and inflammatory activation have been proposed as potential contributors to neonatal morbidity. Therefore, evaluating melatonin alongside inflammatory cytokines and growth-related biomarkers may provide additional insight into the biological response to RBC transfusion in this vulnerable population.

Accordingly, it is essential to assess not only the hematologic outcomes but also the immunologic and inflammatory consequences of RBC transfusion in preterm infants. While existing research emphasizes the potential link between transfusion and sepsis, the underlying mechanisms and their clinical implications remain insufficiently understood.

This study aimed to investigate the relationship between RBC transfusion, proinflammatory cytokine responses, and preterm morbidities in VLBW infants ≤ 32 weeks’ gestation.

Methods

Study design and population

This prospective, single-center observational cohort study was conducted between July 1, 2020, and October 31, 2021. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of University of Health Sciences Türkiye, Şişli Hamidiye Etfal Training and Research Hospital (Approval No: 1569; Date: June 16, 2020) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participating infants. The study received financial support.

Infants were excluded if they had major congenital systemic anomalies (e.g., meningomyelocele, congenital heart disease), hereditary metabolic or genetic syndromes (e.g., Russell–Silver syndrome), sepsis within seven days prior to enrollment, a history of transfusion within the previous 48 h, a gestational age > 32 weeks, or if they required transfusion within the first seven days of life.

The required sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1.6 based on the primary biomarker outcomes of the study, namely changes in inflammatory and growth-related biomarkers following RBC transfusion. Assuming a large effect size (0.40), an alpha level of 0.05, and a power of 90%, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 34 infants [5]. The study was not specifically powered to detect differences in secondary clinical outcomes, including sepsis, BPD classification, respiratory support requirements, NEC, ROP, IVH, or PDA.

Collection of laboratory data

Transfusion indications were beyond the scope of this study; all transfusion decisions were made by the attending neonatologist according to standard clinical protocols. When an erythrocyte transfusion was indicated during follow-up, blood samples were obtained before and after the procedure. RBC units were supplied as pre-prepared packs from the Turkish Red Crescent blood bank, stored for less than five days. All units were leukocyte-depleted before storage, preserved in citrate–phosphate–dextrose (CPD) solution, irradiated, and confirmed cytomegalovirus (CMV) negative. Transfusions were administered at a dose of 15 mL/kg over 2–4 h following clinical guidelines.

For laboratory analysis, 1 mL of whole blood was collected into gel-free biochemistry tubes, centrifuged, and serum aliquots were stored at − 80 °C. Samples were obtained immediately before transfusion and at 2 and 24 h post-transfusion for measurement of melatonin, IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, and VEGF. Additional samples were collected before transfusion and at 1, 2, and 3 weeks post-transfusion to assess IGF-1 and IGFBP-3. For each infant, biomarker measurements and follow-up assessments were anchored to a single index RBC transfusion event. The study was not designed to evaluate the effects of cumulative transfusion exposure or repeated transfusions. When the target sample size was achieved, serum concentrations of all biomarkers were quantified using the ELISA method on a Riader Biotek EL800 analyzer. Commercial Elabscience® (USA) kits were used, catalog numbers E0080Hu, E0391Hu, E0103Hu, E1013Hu, E0077Hu, E0090Hu, and E0082Hu.

Collection of clinical data

Prenatal, perinatal, and postnatal information, including demographic and anthropometric characteristics (gestational age, birth weight, birth length, and head circumference), were recorded for all infants included in the study.

Diagnoses during follow-up, including sepsis, bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), and patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), were documented. The study aimed to evaluate the relationship between cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α) and growth factors (VEGF, IGF-1, and IGFBP-3) measured before and after transfusion and clinical outcomes observed during follow-up in very preterm infants receiving RBC transfusions. RBC transfusions were prescribed according to institutional neonatal transfusion guidelines based on postnatal age, hemoglobin level, respiratory support status, recurrent apnea, tachycardia, tachypnea, impaired weight gain, and overall clinical condition. Because transfusion decisions were based on clinical need rather than study allocation, infants requiring transfusion may have differed in baseline illness severity and respiratory status. Infants with transfusion exposure within the preceding 48 h were excluded. The interval between the last transfusion and study enrollment was therefore at least 48 h for all participants.

Clinical sepsis was defined as the presence of clinical signs and symptoms suggestive of infection requiring antibiotic treatment for at least 5 days, with or without microbiological confirmation, according to the institutional neonatal sepsis protocol. For morbidity analyses, sepsis status at each follow-up assessment represented point prevalence rather than cumulative incidence. Culture-proven sepsis was defined as the isolation of a pathogenic microorganism from blood or cerebrospinal fluid cultures.

BPD was diagnosed and classified according to established international criteria based on oxygen and/or respiratory support requirements at 36 weeks’ postmenstrual age or at discharge, whichever occurred first. Because transfusions were administered at varying postmenstrual ages, respiratory status and respiratory support requirements were also recorded during the first, second, and third weeks after transfusion. These serial assessments were collected to describe the infants’ clinical respiratory course during follow-up and should not be interpreted as repeated new diagnoses of BPD [22, 23]. For infants who received transfusions after 36 weeks’ postmenstrual age, BPD classification had already been established according to standard criteria. Therefore, subsequent follow-up assessments reflected respiratory status and support requirements rather than changes in the original BPD diagnosis. ROP were classified by international criteria [24], IVH according to the Papile classification [25], and NEC according to the modified Bell criteria [26].

Statistical analysis

Sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1.6. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 21.0 for Windows. Descriptive variables were expressed as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (min–max), depending on distribution. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and histogram inspection. Because continuous variables were not normally distributed, comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. For categorical data in dependent groups, the McNemar test was applied for two related samples, and the Cochran Q test for more than three related samples; post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the McNemar test. For paired multicategorical variables in dependent groups, including BPD classification and respiratory support categories, the marginal homogeneity test was applied. For continuous variables in dependent groups, repeated measures ANOVA (one-way) was applied when data were normally distributed, and the Friedman test when non-normal, followed by post hoc paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, respectively. When two categorical factors were present, two-way repeated measures ANOVA was used. Because multiple biomarkers and clinical outcomes were evaluated, the analyses should be considered exploratory. Formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was not performed due to the hypothesis-generating nature of the study; therefore, p-values should be interpreted cautiously, particularly for findings close to the threshold of statistical significance. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The planned sample size was 34 infants. Although 34 participants were initially enrolled, one infant was excluded after enrollment because a previously unrecognized metabolic disease was diagnosed during follow-up. Consequently, the final analytical cohort consisted of 33 infants. Among them, 57.6% were male, with a mean birth weight of 1016 ± 356 g and a mean gestational age of 27.4 ± 2.7 weeks. Discussion

This study investigated changes in inflammatory and growth-related biomarkers following RBC transfusion in very preterm infants. The principal findings were a significant increase in VEGF levels and a decrease in IGFBP-3 levels, whereas IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, melatonin, and IGF-1 remained unchanged. These findings suggest selective modulation of angiogenic and growth-regulatory pathways in temporal association with RBC transfusion. Clinical observations, including sepsis, BPD classification, and respiratory support requirements, were considered secondary exploratory outcomes and should be interpreted cautiously given the absence of a non-transfused control group. Notably, the observed reduction in respiratory support requirements may indicate potential short-term physiological benefits of transfusion in some infants, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously.

Prematurity remains one of the leading causes of neonatal morbidity worldwide [27, 28]. The immaturity of the immune system and incomplete organ development in these infants result in heightened sensitivity to inflammatory stimuli and a fragile balance of biochemical markers [5]. Antenatal infection, perinatal hypoxia, oxidative stress, and postnatal interventions may induce abrupt fluctuations in proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukins and TNF-α [9, 14]. These alterations in cytokines and growth factors can adversely influence vascular regulation and neurodevelopmental processes [5, 9]. Therefore, elucidating the impact of erythrocyte transfusion—a common intervention in preterm infants—on biomarker profiles is essential.

The reported effects of erythrocyte transfusion on proinflammatory cytokines in preterm infants remain inconsistent, even with the use of leukocyte-depleted products [5, 8, 9, 21]. Locke et al. [9] observed no post-transfusion changes in TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, or IL-10, whereas Keir et al. [8] found significant increases in IL-1, IL-8, TNF-α, and MCP-1. Similarly, Dani et al. [4] reported time-dependent elevations in IL-1β, IL-8, interferon (IFN)-γ, and IL-17 in recipient infants, despite an absence of cytokine load in transfused blood units [5]. In our study, no significant differences were observed in IL-1, IL-6, or TNF-α levels at 2 and 24 h after transfusion. Discrepancies across studies likely reflect methodological variations affecting cytokine measurements. Factors such as blood product storage duration, leukoreduction technique, sampling time, and postnatal age may account for these inconsistencies. Keir et al. [8] linked longer storage duration with increased TNF-α levels, while Dani et al. demonstrated cytokine elevations persisting up to 48 h [5]. Moreover, the wide range of postnatal ages (18–50 days) reported across studies may further contribute to variability in cytokine responses [5, 8, 9]. Standardized protocols addressing these factors are required to clarify the specific impact of transfusion on proinflammatory cytokine dynamics.

The association between biomarkers and clinical morbidities in preterm infants has become an increasing focus of research. Green et al. reported that elevated IL-1 plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of BPD, while Locke et al. suggested that leukocyte-depleted RBCs may attenuate cytokine responses [9, 16]. Ghirardello et al. observed a higher risk of BPD in infants receiving three or more transfusions, and Zhang et al. reported increased incidence and severity, particularly with early transfusions [12, 13]. Although mild BPD classification was observed more frequently during follow-up, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Infants requiring RBC transfusion often represent a clinically more vulnerable population with greater prematurity-related morbidity and prolonged respiratory support needs. Therefore, the present study cannot determine whether the observed BPD-related findings were associated with transfusion exposure itself or reflected the underlying clinical characteristics of infants requiring transfusion.

At first glance, the observation of more frequent mild BPD classification alongside declining respiratory support requirements may appear contradictory. However, these variables reflect different clinical constructs. Respiratory support status was assessed serially during follow-up and may improve with postnatal maturation, clinical stabilization, and recovery from anemia-related symptoms. In contrast, BPD classification reflects a chronic respiratory outcome determined according to standardized criteria at a fixed postmenstrual age. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as representing parallel or directly comparable physiological processes.

Biologically active compounds in stored blood, such as non-transferrin-bound iron (NTBI) and free heme, have been proposed to alter biochemical profiles by promoting oxidative stress [13]. Fluctuations in VEGF, IGF-1, and angiotensin (Ang)-1 have been linked to increased risks of BPD, ROP, and IVH through disruption of vascular integrity [10, 18, 29]. However, the reported effects of transfusion on these biomarkers remain inconsistent, likely due to variations in measurement techniques, sampling schedules, and follow-up durations.

Hübler et al. found reduced IGF-1 levels in severe ROP and suggested that unwashed erythrocytes might contribute to pathological vascularization, given the presence of IGF-1/IGF-2 in standard blood units [10]. In contrast, Bozkaya et al. reported no significant post-transfusion changes in IGF-1 or IGFBP-3 levels but identified an increased risk of ROP [30]. In the present study, IGF-1 levels remained stable after transfusion, whereas IGFBP-3 decreased significantly. Despite this decline, no increase in ROP incidence was observed. These findings suggest that IGF-1 remained relatively stable during follow-up, whereas IGFBP-3 levels decreased over time. However, because IGFBP-3 was evaluated over a three-week period, the observed decline cannot be attributed specifically to transfusion and may have been influenced by multiple factors, including nutritional status, infection, medication exposure, and the overall clinical course of these very preterm infants. Therefore, the biological and clinical significance of the observed IGFBP-3 reduction should be considered exploratory.

The timing of complications is as critical as their incidence. Christensen et al. reported that over one-third of NEC cases occurred within 48 h after transfusion, while Teiserskas et al. found that half developed during the same period [1, 11]. In contrast, Dani et al. observed no new NEC cases within 48 h post-transfusion, and Ghirardello et al. noted that infants who developed NEC had not received transfusions in the preceding 48 h [5, 7]. In the present study, NEC incidence remained unchanged throughout the three-week follow-up.

Regarding IVH, Christensen et al. [1–3] and Baer et al. reported that early transfusions might increase the risk of severe hemorrhage, whereas Roberts et al., using multivariate analysis, found transfusion was not an independent risk factor for IVH [31–33]. Similarly, Skubisz et al. emphasized that most IVH diagnoses are made before transfusion [15]. In our study, although IVH incidence showed a non-significant upward trend, this was attributed to the cohort’s later postnatal age and the absence of severe sepsis. No significant difference in PDA incidence was found, consistent with previous reports [12, 33].

Findings on the impact of transfusion on VEGF levels are highly inconsistent. Tschirch et al. reported that elevated pre-transfusion VEGF reflected hypoxia and declined significantly after transfusion, whereas Ergenekon et al. observed no change 30 min post-transfusion [18, 34]. In contrast, our study observed higher VEGF levels at 2 and 24 h during post-transfusion follow-up. The limited number of studies and their divergent results likely stem from methodological differences, including patient selection and follow-up duration.

Notably, the cohort in Tschirch et al. [18] included both term and preterm infants, whereas our study, similar to that of Ergenekon et al. [34], focused exclusively on very preterm infants but incorporated a longer observation period. The high incidence of BPD and ROP in our population may also have contributed to VEGF elevation. These findings suggest that increased VEGF levels may be associated with endothelial stress and inflammatory activation observed during post-transfusion follow-up; however, the underlying biological mechanisms remain uncertain.

Melatonin, another biomarker evaluated in this study, is recognized for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Previous studies have shown that melatonin administration reduces proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α and confers protection against oxidative injury [20, 35]. Endogenous melatonin levels are known to be lower in preterm than in term infants [36]. In our study, no significant changes in melatonin levels were observed at 2–24 h after transfusion. Given the limited understanding of the relationship between RBC transfusion and melatonin regulation, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, the absence of substantial changes suggests that melatonin may not be among the most responsive biomarkers following transfusion in this cohort. Interpretation of the melatonin findings should be further tempered by the fact that circadian influences, environmental light exposure, and postnatal maturation were not specifically standardized or recorded during sample collection.

Another observation was a decline in respiratory support requirements during follow-up after transfusion. This should not be interpreted as a direct beneficial effect of transfusion, because improvement may also reflect postnatal maturation, recovery from acute illness, clinical stabilization, or changes in respiratory management. Following transfusion, a general decrease in respiratory support parameters was observed, with a significant reduction particularly in the use of n-SIMV and n-CPAP. This finding contrasts with previous studies reporting an increased need for mechanical ventilation after transfusion [7, 12, 15]. The discrepancy may be explained by the greater clinical stability of our cohort, with a mean postnatal age of 40 days, and by the improvement of anemia-related symptoms such as apnea, tachypnea, and tachycardia after transfusion. At the same time, these findings may suggest that RBC transfusion provides short-term physiological benefits in selected preterm infants with clinically significant anemia. Improved oxygen-carrying capacity following transfusion may contribute to stabilization of cardiorespiratory status and reduced respiratory support requirements. However, given the observational design and absence of a control group, this interpretation remains speculative and should be confirmed in larger controlled studies.

A clinically notable observation was the presence of clinical sepsis during the post-transfusion follow-up period. However, because transfusion requirement itself may reflect greater illness severity, prolonged hospitalization, respiratory instability, and the overall vulnerability of very preterm infants, the present data cannot distinguish whether these observations were related to transfusion exposure or to the infants’ underlying baseline risk. Accordingly, the observed temporal association should not be interpreted as evidence that transfusion contributed directly to sepsis. Postnatal maturation, evolving clinical status, antibiotic exposure, and confounding by indication may all have influenced these findings. Although similar associations have been reported previously [13, 21], larger controlled studies are required to clarify the independent contribution of transfusion to infectious morbidity. It should also be emphasized that the definition of clinical sepsis used in this study included infants treated with antibiotics for at least five days based on clinical suspicion of infection, regardless of microbiological confirmation. Therefore, clinical sepsis does not necessarily represent culture-proven infection and may partly reflect the challenges of diagnosing infection in very preterm infants, where non-specific clinical signs frequently prompt empirical antibiotic treatment.

The present study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center and included a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Another limitation relates to gestational age stratification. The ≤ 26-week gestational age category included infants with potentially substantial differences in biological maturity, inflammatory responses, transfusion exposure, and respiratory morbidity. Consequently, residual confounding related to gestational maturity may have influenced both biomarker profiles and clinical outcomes. Because of the relatively small sample size, further subdivision of gestational age groups was not feasible. Future studies with larger cohorts should evaluate extremely preterm infants using more granular gestational age categories. Second, biomarker measurements were obtained at predefined time points and may not fully capture the dynamic inflammatory response following transfusion. Third, multiple biomarkers and clinical outcomes were evaluated without formal adjustment for multiple comparisons; therefore, the possibility of type I error cannot be excluded, and statistically significant findings, particularly those with borderline p-values, should be considered exploratory. Information regarding erythropoietin use, iron supplementation, phlebotomy reduction strategies, and other anemia management practices was not systematically collected and therefore could not be evaluated in the present study. These factors may have influenced transfusion requirements and clinical outcomes.

In addition, the observational design introduces the possibility of residual confounding, particularly confounding by indication, as infants requiring transfusion may differ systematically from those who do not. The absence of a non-transfused control group further precludes causal inference regarding both biomarker changes and clinical outcomes. Accordingly, the observed findings should be interpreted as temporal associations rather than effects directly attributable to RBC transfusion. Furthermore, the final analytical cohort (n = 33) was slightly below the planned sample size (n = 34), which may have reduced statistical power, particularly for secondary clinical outcomes and non-significant biomarker comparisons.

Finally, the interpretation of BPD-related findings requires caution. Because BPD is conventionally diagnosed at a fixed postmenstrual age, weekly post-transfusion assessments should not be interpreted as incident or fluctuating BPD. Instead, these observations are better regarded as descriptive indicators of the infants’ respiratory status and support requirements during follow-up. Larger multicenter studies with appropriate control groups are needed to validate these findings and clarify the relationship between RBC transfusion, inflammatory biomarkers, and neonatal morbidities.

In conclusion, this prospective single-arm observational study showed that RBC transfusion was followed by increased VEGF and decreased IGFBP-3 levels, while IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, melatonin, and IGF-1 remained unchanged. Clinical findings, including sepsis, BPD classification, and respiratory support requirements, should be interpreted as descriptive and exploratory temporal observations because the study lacked a non-transfused control group. Larger controlled studies are needed to clarify whether these biomarker and clinical changes are related to transfusion itself or to prematurity-related disease progression and confounding by indication.
