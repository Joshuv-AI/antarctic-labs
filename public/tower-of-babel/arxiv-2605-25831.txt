Clarify, Abstain or Answer? Strategising in Conversation with Belief-Augmented Generation
Joris Baan, Wilker Aziz, Barbara Plank, Raquel Fernández University of Amsterdam, MCML Munich, LMU Munich {j.s.baan,w.aziz,raquel.fernandez}@uva.nl , b.plank@lmu.de

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) define a distribution over text, which can be viewed as a probabilistic representation of uncertainty: sampling K K responses yields a belief state —responses a model deems plausible. Existing work exploits this representation for narrow tasks like either decoding or selective prediction, and often requires manual interventions, not controlling generation directly. We propose Belief-Augmented Generation (BAG): grounding LLMs in their own belief state via the prompt and letting them reason over these K K samples to decide on a conversational strategy: answer, clarify, or abstain. In a multi-turn ambiguous QA setting, we find that LLMs by default rarely clarify or abstain, ignoring uncertainty about the input or facts. BAG improves QA accuracy across six models and yields strategy decisions more faithful to the belief state than prompt-only baselines. Disentangling when to clarify from when to abstain, however, remains challenging.



1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are statistical models that autoregressively define a distribution over text. This can be viewed as a probabilistic representation of uncertainty or belief
Baan et al. 2023
. By drawing multiple samples we obtain a textual representation,
i.e.,

belief state
: responses a model deems plausible under a given prompt.

Such representations are routinely and effectively used in decoding by selecting the highest expected utility or majority generation
Eikema and Aziz 2020
;
Wang et al. 2023
; in selective prediction when entropy across semantic clusters of generations is high
Kuhn et al. 2023b
; or to communicate uncertainty reflective of the relative frequency of a claim in the belief state
Yona et al. 2024
;
Eikema et al. 2025
.

However, these applications require manual interventions (
e.g.,
decoders, uncertainty quantifiers, selective prediction heuristics, or finetuning) and focus on single, narrow decision-making tasks.
Furthermore, we argue that it is unlikely that LLMs can ‘introspect’ or exploit their own probabilistic representation of uncertainty, since there is no mechanism in training or inference supporting this. Indeed, initial empirical evidence indicates that LLMs do not communicate uncertainty faithful to their distribution
Yona et al. 2024
;
Kirchhof et al. 2025
. In this paper, we propose to give LLMs access to their own probabilistic belief state to power richer conversational strategies and improve responses in a multi-turn ambiguous question answering (QA) environment; see Figure
1
.

The key idea is simple: let LLMs reason over their own belief state, obtained via sampling, guiding them to clarify, abstain or answer directly—harnessing their strong ability to reason about available textual evidence as demonstrated in retrieval-augmented generation (
Lewis et al. 2020
, RAG;
). We call this
Belief-Augmented Generation
(BAG), grounding responses in the belief state (rather than external documents in RAG) via the prompt, letting the LLM decide when to answer, what strategy to follow, and how to formulate the answer all at once. BAG requires no training, no external data, and can be applied to any model, including closed models.

We find that the six LLMs in our experiments by default rarely clarify or abstain and ignore uncertainty in the input (
e.g.,
ambiguity) and about facts. Belief-augmented generation improves QA performance, leading to strategies more faithful to probabilistic uncertainty. However, recognising when to clarify vs abstain remains challenging. Upon publication, we will release our code and an online tool to explore BAG’s output and conversations (see Appendix Figure
15
for a screenshot).

Related Work

Decoding.

One of the earliest works that exploit a textual representation of probabilistic belief (
i.e.,

K
sampled generations) is Minimum Bayes Risk decoding (MBR), which selects the generation that best represents the entire set, demonstrating improved response quality
Eikema and Aziz 2020
;
Suzgun et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2025
.
Similarly, self-consistency samples multiple reasoning chains and pick the majority answer
Wang et al. 2023
;
Bertsch et al. 2023
. Both can be viewed as test-time compute scaling
Snell et al. 2025
.

Selective prediction.

Kuhn et al. 2023b
;
Farquhar et al. 2024
instead capitalise on the idea that semantically distinct claims in the belief state may be indicative of error (
i.e.,
model or epistemic uncertainty)—an intuition widely used to detect hallucinations
Geng et al. 2024
;
Vashurin et al. 2025
. Others view variation in belief states as uncertainty about the input (
Giulianelli et al. 2023
;
Baan et al. 2024
,
i.e.,
data or aleatoric uncertainty;
), and there have been efforts to disentangle these sources
Cole et al. 2023
;
Hou et al. 2024
. However, both decoding and selective prediction methods rely on hand-crafted features and their end goal is to predict error or select a generation rather than directly controlling generation.

Faithful uncertainty.

Another line of research guides models to communicate their uncertainty faithfully,
i.e.,
hedge or strengthen a claim such that it reflects the relative frequency of that claim in the belief state
Yona et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2024
;
Eikema et al. 2025
. Most similar to our work is
Kirchhof et al. 2025
, who propose to communicate the full internal distribution over possible answers rather than hedging a single answer, and find directly conditioning on
K
samples in the prompt most effective. We go beyond hedging and let models reason over their own belief state to decide on more complex conversational strategies. Also similar is
Nan et al. 2025
who condition a bigger, auxiliary model on another model’s belief state to analyse the source of its uncertainty. We instead let the same model reason about its own uncertainty to directly inform its generation process.

Conversational strategies.

LLMs use a limited range of conversational strategies and often answer directly, failing to recognize ambiguity or ask clarification questions, instead implicitly assuming an interpretation
Deng et al. 2023
;
Shaikh et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Testoni et al. 2025
. While there have been attempts to remedy this via prompting
Kuhn et al. 2023a
;
Zhang et al. 2024
and fine-tuning
Andukuri et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
, the belief state has been under-explored.

Testoni and Fernández 2024
suggest that human clarification questions as supervision for deciding when and how to clarify may not be ideal to resolve model uncertainty, which is echoed by
Kim et al. 2024
who finetune on questions ‘perceived’ by a model as ambiguous, and
Zhang et al. 2025
who construct clarification questions via an external model prompted to separate
K
sampled generations.
Zhang and Choi 2025
predict ambiguity via entropy over multiple simulated user responses without exploiting them for how to clarify, and do not allow abstaining.

We focus on clarification to address ambiguity, but other valid strategies (
e.g.,
long form answers) are fully compatible with BAG
Berant et al. 2025
;
Saparina and Lapata 2025
. Crucially, unlike classical selective prediction, BAG guides models to abstain with a direct response to the user based on the belief state (see the example in Figure
2
), and the abstain strategy can easily be enhanced with faithfully communicating uncertainty, or fetching external information,
i.e.,
deciding when to RAG. We are not aware of published work combining direct answer, clarification and abstention strategies.

3
Belief-Augmented Generation (BAG)

The goal of BAG is to feed a textual representation of probabilistic belief directly into the prompt, guiding models to improve their responses by making belief-informed decisions. We capitalise on the observation that sample diversity is often characteristic of different sources of uncertainty and, as such, is informative of when and how to clarify, abstain, or answer.

3.1
Constructing the Belief State

Suppose a model
\text{LLM}_{\theta}
with parameters
\theta
defines a distribution
P_{\theta}(Y|X=x)
over responses
Y\in\mathcal{X}
given input
X=x\in\mathcal{X}
, where
\mathcal{X}
is the space of strings. We take the stance that, for any input
X=x
, the probability measure
P_{\theta}
under a given sampling algorithm is a representation of the LLM’s uncertainty about the response
Y
, specifying the space of responses plausible under the model
Baan et al. 2023
.

To summarise the predictive distribution for a given input
X=x
one can draw multiple samples using Monte-Carlo (MC) simulation. Practitioners often reduce this set of strings to a single number to power a decision making tool, like what generation to pick
Eikema and Aziz 2020
;
Wu et al. 2025
or when to abstain
Farquhar et al. 2024
;
Vashurin et al. 2025
.

With BAG we retain the full set of
K
samples as the
belief state

B_{\theta}(x):=(Y^{(1)},\ldots,Y^{(K)})
, where
Y^{(k)}\sim P_{\theta}
.
Interestingly, this representation not only captures diversity across generations that a model deems plausible, but also linguistic cues within generations, like hedging, refusals, clarifications, knowledge cut-offs, or nuanced acknowledgments of ambiguity.

3.2
Prompting for Conversational Strategies

BAG is a two-stage approach. After a model is shown a question to obtain multiple responses, they are included in a new prompt to decide on and execute a conversational strategy. This prompt includes the user question
x
and belief state
B
and defines the allowed strategies, output format and how to analyse the belief state to arrive at a strategy.

A powerful property of BAG is the ability to capture multiple intuitions behind popular uncertainty quantification methods in text directly in this prompt. We explore four intuitions: (1) inconsistent facts among generations point to hallucinations and indicate a form of epistemic uncertainty that should lead to abstaining
(
Kuhn et al. 2023b
, akin to semantic entropy;)
, (2) generations that address different interpretations of the question should lead to a clarification question, (3) generations that are largely consistent should lead to a direct answer, and (4), direct answers should represent the full belief state
(
Eikema and Aziz 2020
, akin to MBR;)
. We discuss the exact prompts in Section
4.2
.

Instantiating BAG

Conversational Strategies.

We experiment with strategies that address the two major sources of uncertainty
Baan et al. 2023
: from data (
e.g.,
the input:
clarify
) and the model (
e.g.,
internal facts:
abstain
). With little uncertainty, models can
answer directly
.
We leave other strategies—like retrieving external information instead of abstaining (using BAG to decide when to RAG), or long-form answers instead of clarifying—to future work.

BAG prompts.

We test three prompts with slightly different instructions for how to analyse the belief state (BAG1-3). BAG1 in Appendix Figure
6

If BAG decides to clarify, we can augment the final response again—sampling
K
new generations given the three-turn history—and instruct to abstain or answer. We call this version BAG+, see the prompt in Appendix Figure

5
Evaluation

To evaluate the potential for clarification and conversational strategies with and without belief states we introduce four generation settings. Appendix Figure
6
provides an overview of the prompts.

Direct generation.

The
standard baseline
generates a response given the original question and the
disambiguation oracle
given an (annotated) disambiguated question, if one exists, or the original question otherwise (collapsing to the standard baseline). We consider this an oracle for when and how to clarify, taking ambiguity out of the equation and evaluating if models have the relevant factual knowledge to answer a particular intent.

Augmentation strategies.

We guide models towards conversational strategies and simulate interactions with a user. Initial responses and responses following clarification are evaluated against one specific user intent. Abstentions are excluded.
Strategy-Augmented Generation (SAG)
is a prompt-only variant and
Belief-Augmented Generation (BAG)
extends it by conditioning on the belief state. Like
BAG+
,
SAG+
also augments the final response after clarifications with strategies, but via prompting alone.

Simulating Interaction.

When a model asks a clarification question, we prompt Gemini-2.5-flash to role-play a user and provide it the user intent (unseen by the model under evaluation): a disambiguated version of the question if the annotation exists, and the reference answer if the question is not ambiguous. We design a conservative prompt (Figure
10
) that responds with “I don’t know” if the clarification question is unanswerable or off topic to avoid rewarding bad clarification questions. Appendix
B
provides an error analysis, but we generally find high-quality user answers. We limit the conversation to a single interaction,
i.e.,
four turns: user question, model question, user answer, final model answer, see Figure
1
for an example.

5.1
Metrics

We measure QA accuracy with a
reference-based
LLM judge: Gemini-Flash-2.5; see Appendix Figure
11
for the prompt. A small manual analysis detailed in Appendix
C
reveals a 97% correctness rate, though, like most QA datasets, the references can be time-sensitive
Pletenev et al. 2025
.

For the direct generation settings, we evaluate ambiguous questions with multiple intents against
one
, randomly chosen intent and
any
intent.

One intent.

This is the core metric evaluating whether models give the correct answer to a specific (randomly sampled) user intent. This metric is strict and may be unfair without the potential for interaction,
i.e.,
it may reject generations that answer another plausible intent, even though a knowledgable user might judge the answer’s relevance themselves, potentially following up by making their intent more explicit.

Any intent.

Do models give a correct answer w.r.t. any of the possible intents? We consider this an upper bound that does not care about ambiguity.

Results

6.1
Direct Generation

We first analyse direct generations results with no possibility to clarify or abstain; see Table

Disambiguations cannot fix lack of knowledge.

The disambiguation oracle yields relatively small improvements over the standard single intent baseline for some models (
e.g.,
Qwen3-8B), and much larger improvements for others (
e.g.,
Gemini-2.5-Flash). This demonstrates that some models are unable to exploit disambiguations due to a lack of factual knowledge, rather than an inability to handle ambiguity, and indicates varying and sometimes limited potential for clarification, further motivating why BAG supports both clarification and abstention.

Specific intents are difficult.

The gap between the standard one vs. any intent metric reflects the inherent difficulty of recovering a specific user intent without interaction or disambiguation—a model may give a perfectly reasonable answer while missing the target interpretation. Still, the one intent metric is valuable when considering that users may ambiguously inquire with a specific intent in mind, especially if they cannot foresee which interpretation is salient to the model.

Models presuppose a “salient” interpretation.

The disambiguation oracle’s improvement from the one intent to any intent metric indicates that models may presuppose a salient interpretation so strongly that they ignore the disambiguation, perhaps not having the relevant factual knowledge to support other intents. This aligns with
Sedova et al. 2024
who find biases toward preferred readings of entities that correlate with the frequency of those readings in training data. This may also limit the potential of clarification interactions for changing such salient interpretations.

6.3
Effect of Clarification

The ideal conditions to clarify are rather specific:

(1)

a direct answer would have been wrong,

(2)

the clarification question is relevant and a user would be able to answer it,

(3)

without the user’s answer, a model could not give the correct answer (
e.g.,
RAG would not help since a model does not know what information to fetch or fetches conflicting information), and finally

(4)

the model has the factual knowledge to answer the disambiguated question.

From Section
6.1
we know that condition (1) is often met (direct generation accuracy is low) and condition (4) is not (factual knowledge rather than ambiguity is often the problem, for which abstain is a better strategy). This severely limits the potential for clarification, which we confirm in Figure
3
. We will investigate condition (2) and (3) next.

Qualitative analysis.

Belief states display both
interpretation variation
due to multiple plausible interpretations of the question, and
hallucination variation
due to inconsistent facts, falling neatly on opposite ends of the data-model uncertainty spectrum
Baan et al. 2023
. Interpretation variation is addressed by interacting with the user, and hallucination variation by abstaining. However, we find that detecting this difference is challenging.

By design, BAG guides models to formulate a clarification question based on their belief state. When the belief state contains multiple plausible answers, each contextualised (
e.g.,
,
"in the
US
, …"
vs
"in
Canada
, …"
, or
"the
FIFA
world cup …"
vs
"The
rugby
world cup…"
), the clarifications are generally useful, like the examples in Figures
1
-
2
.

However, models sometimes come up with non-existent ambiguity to explain hallucination variation, especially if generations are not meaningfully contextualised. For example, conjuring non-existing adaptations of a movie in which different actors play the same character to explain mentions of those actors in the belief state. Put differently: clarification questions reflect what
Kim et al. 2024
call
perceived ambiguity
that does not necessarily align with real-world human ambiguity. While such clarification questions are truthful reflections of what models deem plausible—and arguably a better strategy than responding with one of the incorrect responses—they may still confuse a user.

6.4
Brevity-inducing Prompt

BAG’s computational cost is
K
samples plus the prompt to reason over them.
3
3

3

Interestingly, Qwen3 thinking mode consumes 7x more output tokens than direct generation, for just 2 more accuracy points. This cost is not much higher than sampling 10 generations, which, unlike thinking, is parallelisable. This puts BAG’s costs in perspective.

To reduce the size of belief states, and therefore token cost, we study the effect of two brevity-inducing prompts:
"Please provide a concise answer to the following question:"
, and
"Please provide a short answer of at most 1 sentence to the following question"
.

They reduce average generation length by up to a factor 10 without severely impacting accuracy (Appendix Figure
6
). However, the verbal cues for how models interpret a question (
e.g.,

"the FIFA world cup was in …"
) are often lost (examples in Appendix Table
4
), reducing the means by which users can judge an answer, and BAG a belief state.

7
Faithfulness

We evaluated BAG in terms of QA performance, but equally important is: are its strategic decisions faithful to its internal distribution?

We cluster generations on the main answer they assert and compute semantic entropy
Kuhn et al. 2023b
with Gemini-2.5-Flash: We find that belief states range from just a single answer to ten totally distinct answers. For smaller models, belief states are almost uniformly distributed across this range, while Gemini-2.5-Flash has many more single-answer belief states; about 50%; see Appendix Figure
16
for more details.

BAG’s strategies are more faithful.

Our hypothesis is that models will choose the direct answer strategy for lower entropy, and clarify or abstain for higher entropy belief states. Indeed, Figure
5
confirms this for Qwen3-14B and Gemini-2.5-Flash with BAG. Interestingly, the prompt-only SAG is not aligned with belief state entropy, providing further evidence that prompt-based uncertainty methods do not exploit the predictive distribution. We observe similar patterns for Olmo3 and Qwen3-8B, but not for Olmo2, which we explain by its limited ability to follow instructions.

Figure 5:
Routing decisions vs belief state entropy: BAG is more faithful to the predictive distribution than SAG, answering directly when entropy is low, and increasingly clarifying and abstaining as entropy increases.

Baselines rarely clarify or abstain.

Direct generation without any augmentation strategy rarely clarifies or abstains at all: classifying generations with Gemini-2.5-Flash we find they only do so on 3% of the questions. A small manual analysis (Appendix Figure
14
) confirms this, aligning with findings from previous work
Deng et al. 2023
;
Shaikh et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Testoni et al. 2025
. This confirms that direct generation is even less faithful to the internal distribution.

Clarification reduces entropy.

We sample
K
responses after clarification interactions (similar to BAG+), cluster them, and compute the difference between their semantic answer entropy and entropy of the turn 2 responses reported earlier. We find that clarification reduces entropy for all models. Averaged across BAG1-3, Gemini has the largest reduction of 54%, followed by Olmo2-13B at 20%, Qwen3-14B at 18%, Qwen3-8B at 16%, Olmo2-7B at 14%, and Olmo3-7B at 1.7%. This aligns with the disambiguation oracle results, which indicated that some models do not have the factual knowledge to exploit even annotated disambiguations.

8
Conclusion

We introduced Belief-Augmented Generation (BAG), a training-free approach that grounds LLMs in their own
belief state
—-
K
sampled responses—and lets them reason over it to answer directly, clarify, or abstain. In a multi-turn AmbigQA environment, BAG improves QA accuracy across six models and produces strategy decisions more faithful to the belief state than prompt-only baselines, while standard direct generation rarely clarifies or abstains at all. All three strategies contribute to BAG’s gains, though their relative weight varies across models and prompts. Distinguishing interpretation variation from hallucination variation in the belief state—and thus when to clarify versus abstain—remains challenging.

BAG combines a probabilistic view of LLMs with their modern instruction and reasoning capabilities. Promising extensions include richer strategies (
e.g.,
when to retrieve external information or call tools) and belief states (
e.g.,
with thinking traces), and prompts that more explicitly exploit verbal uncertainty cues.

9
Limitations

While AmbigQA is the perfect choice to simulate different user intents because it provides multiple disambiguation annotations—and is similarly used by many related studies because there are virtually no alternatives providing such ground truths—its reference answers can be outdated and the disambiguations slightly artificial. Rather than reflecting the actual intent of the user who asked her question to Google Search, which is impossible to reconstruct after the fact, AmbigQA annotators instead collect all the possible answers that can be found when searching Wikipedia and retroactively create a (minimally edited) disambiguated version of the original question that would lead to each answer. An important avenue of research is therefore to collect new datasets with real world questions and intents that correspond to actual user intents, ideally from users with diverse cultural backgrounds (
e.g.,
AmbigQA is very US-centered), which also allows comparison between models’ suppositions and whether they align with dominant user intents.

Another limitation is the quality of the belief states,
i.e.,
the model’s representation of uncertainty. While belief states provide richer insight into the range of responses that a model deems plausible, and are successfully exploited for decision making tasks like decoding or selective prediction, they still reflect the responses plausible under a model, and those are in no way guaranteed to be coherent with the external world (
e.g.,
highly consistent belief states with a single main claim can still be factually incorrect). A complementary avenue of research is therefore to improve the quality of belief states, which can then be more effectively exploited with methods such as BAG.

Dataset Statistics

Each AmbigQA question is annotated with a single answer (

i.e.,

The dataset contains 10,036 train and 2,002 development questions, with 10,251 and 3,312 annotations respectively (multiple annotators may annotate the same question). The development set has a higher ambiguity rate (58.5% vs. 47.3% for train) and annotation clash rate (

The two prompts for the user simulator LLM. The two prompts differ in the secret context provided: for ambiguous questions (left), we give a disambiguated version of the question; for non-ambiguous questions (right) we give the reference answer itself with an explicit instruction not to reveal it.

The user answer simulation prompt for Gemini-2.5-flash is shown in Figure
10
. We find that simulated user answers are generally of high quality. The user simulator is explicitly instructed not to reveal the reference answer and only to answer the clarification question. Nevertheless, references occasionally leak through. On average, across all assistant models and prompts, the user answer contains the reference answer for 1.7% of clarification questions.

However, some clarification questions already contain the reference answer themselves, which is not a leak on the user simulator’s part. Excluding those cases, only 0.7% of user answers constitute true leaks. To assess their impact on our results, we count how often leaks co-occur with a positive clarification flip (direct answer incorrect → final answer correct), finding this in only 1.9% of such flips. We therefore conclude that reference leaks do not meaningfully inflate the reported clarification gains.

The prompt instructs the user simulator not to answer clarification questions that are irrelevant to its intent or off-topic, to avoid rewarding bad CQs with useful additional information about the users intent. On average, about 5% of the user responses are an exact match with "I don’t know". This simulation strategy is very conservative, and we suspect real users to be much more helpful.

For example, consider the AmbigQA question
"What is the dog called in midsomer murders?"
with intent
"What is the dog’s breed called in midsomer murders?"
and the model’s clarification question
"There isn’t a single dog that consistently appears throughout all of Midsomer Murders with one name, as there have been different dogs belonging to the various DCI Barnabys. . The user simulator answers
"I don’t know"
, with the following reasoning
"Internal reasoning: The user is asking about the dog’s breed, not its name. The AI’s clarification question is about the DCI Barnaby to identify the dog’s name. . A real user might just respond with the much more helpful

We generally find the judge verdicts of Gemini-2.5-Flash about whether a generation is correct with respect to a reference of high quality, and manually evaluate 30 direct generation answers from Olmo2-13B against the same single reference answer that the judge is evaluating against (the 1 intent setting,

i.e.,
a success rate of 97%.

This error is actually an interesting case where the judge ignores the reference and uses its own knowledge to judge the generation as correct for the question "The cat in the hat boy and girl?". In fact, the generation does correctly answer another (much more obvious) interpretation of the question (the boy and the girl characters are unnamed), while being explicit about doing so, which makes it hard to fault the generation. This is a limitation of single-intent evaluation setting, because a real user might be able to judge that the generation is answering a different interpretation of the question than what they actually intended (
e.g.,
the annotated disambiguation "Who plays the girl in the 2003 Cat in the Hat film?"), and would potentially follow up to clarify this to the model.

C.1
Reference Quality

A more important limitation of the LLM judge is not its ability to judge whether a generation contains a reference, but the quality of the AmbigQA reference itself. In Table
5
we characterise several types of questions and their annotated disambiguation-reference pairs. One notable category is questions with answers that change over time.

Outdated references.

This problem is universal across QA datasets and discussed by
Pletenev et al. 2025
. We apply their pretrained classifier to filter out time-sensitive questions, and find it discards about 30%. When we filter those out, QA accuracy increases with a few points across all models and configurations, but the relative differences between generation settings remain the same. We hypothesise that the effect is so small because all methods are impacted equally by the filtering. We choose not to include these results in the paper because we manually observed that many subtly non-mutable questions are also being filtered out (
e.g.,
, "Who did Samuel L. Jackson play in Star Wars?", or "Who played James Bond in Licence to Kill?").

Artificial ambiguity.

One issue with the dataset is that the disambiguation annotations reflect what annotators could find on Wikipedia rather than what interpretation users genuinely meant when they asked their question to Google Search.
