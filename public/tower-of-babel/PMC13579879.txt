Alterations in glutamatergic and GABAergic signaling in ketamine-induced neurotoxicity: mangiferin mitigates neurochemical, oxidative, and astrocytic dysregulation in the rat temporal–frontal cortex
Authors: Godson Emeka Anyanwu, Victoria Onyemachi Chukwu, Nto Johnson Nto, Vivian Onyinye Ojiakor, Chinyere Nkemjika Anyanwu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKetamine is an antagonist of the N-Methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor with the effect of inducing aberrant excitatory-inhibitory neurotransmission and oxidative neurotoxicity, which mimic the cognitive impairments found in schizophrenia. This study explored the potential of mangiferin, a strong antioxidant xanthone polyphenol, to normalize neurotransmitter levels and mitigate oxidative and glial changes in rats subjected to ketamine treatment.MethodsMale Wistar rats were anaesthetised with ketamine (50 mg/kg, i.p., for 7 days) to induce excitotoxicity and subsequently treated for 14 days with mangiferin (25, 50 or 75 mg/kg, p.o.) or risperidone (2 mg/kg, i.p.). Behavioral performance was assessed using the Morris Water Maze, Y-Maze, Open Field, and Novel Object Recognition tests. Neurochemical assays in the prefrontal, hippocampal and temporal cortices measured glutamate, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), dopamine, and acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activities. Cellular pathology was evaluated through histopathology (H&E) and immunohistochemistry for glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2).ResultsKetamine induced severe cognitive deficits, hyperlocomotion, anxiety-like behavior, reduced cortical GABA and glutamate levels, increased dopamine, and elevated AChE activity—confirming excitatory-inhibitory imbalance and cholinergic disruption. Mangiferin enhanced spatial learning, working memory, recognition memory, and normalized locomotor activity in a dose-dependent manner, with higher doses restoring performance to near control levels. Neurochemically, mangiferin increased GABA and glutamate to baseline levels while decreasing dopamine and AChE hyperactivity. Histology showed preserved cortical cytoarchitecture and reduced neuronal loss and vacuolation. Notably, mangiferin reversed ketamine-induced astrogliosis (decreased GFAP immunoreactivity) and increased nuclear Nrf2 expression, indicating activated endogenous antioxidant defenses.ConclusionMangiferin exhibited significant neuroprotection against ketamine-induced excitotoxicity through restoration of neurotransmitter homeostasis, establishment of redox resilience, and regulation of astrocytic reactivity. These multifaceted actions underscore its therapeutic potential for neuropsychiatric diseases associated with oxidative stress and glial dysfunction.Keywords: Ketamine, Mangiferin, Glutamate, GABA, Oxidative stress, Nrf2, GFAP, Astrocytes, Cognitive impairment, Neurotransmitters, Wistar rats

Introduction

Excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitter balance is an important determinant of healthy cognitive functioning, and its dysfunction is linked to a broad spectrum of neurological and psychiatric pathology, including schizophrenia and neurotoxicity caused by substance abuse [1, 2]. Ketamine is a dissociative anesthetic that is a noncompetitive antagonist to NMDA receptors which preferentially bind to receptors found on GABAergic interneurons. This blockage interferes with the GABAergic inhibitory regulation leading to disinhibition of glutamatergic pyramidal neurons and temporary hyperglutamatergic state [3–5]. Chronic or high dose exposure to ketamine may induce glutamate-related excitotoxicity and oxidative stress and ultimately neuronal apoptosis of corticolimbic brain regions [4, 6, 7]. In fact, the daily administration of ketamine increases extracellular glutamate, which causes Ca2 + overload, mitochondrial dysfunction, formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and neurodegeneration [8, 9]. At the same time, the inhibitory tone blocked by ketamine results in GABAergic hypofunction [10] and excitation-inhibition imbalance, which is a pathophysiological marker of cognitive impairments and psychotomimetic effects [4]. This excitatory-inhibitory imbalance which is accompanied by oxidative damage is the root cause of the cognitive dysfunction identified in ketamine models [11], including learning, memory, and executive impairments [4]. Indicatively, ketamine-treated rodents exhibit longer escape latencies in spatial memory experiments and impaired novel object recognition and these effects resemble human brain cognitive impairment [11]. Oxidative stress induced by ketamine can be seen at the cellular level, with elevated lipid peroxidation and lost antioxidant defenses (e.g., reduced superoxide dismutase, SOD) in cortex and hippocampus [12]. Ketamine also triggers neuroinflammatory responses, and it has been reported that the brain responds to ketamine by activating microglia and abnormal astrocytic response to the excitotoxic injury [13].

Astrocytes are actively involved in neurotransmitter homeostasis and redox homeostasis of the CNS [14, 15]. They absorb surplus glutamate, discharge antioxidants and become reactive (astrogliosis) during stress, characterized by an increase in GFAP (glial fibrillary acidic protein) [16]. Reactive astrocytes may be either protective or pathogenic; increased expression of GFAP is frequently a response to neurological injury [17–20]. The astrocytic perturbation has been shown in the case of ketamine models [21]. Others have reported enhanced astrogliosis as GFAP immunoreactivity with neurotoxic ketamine regimens [22], whereas some have found region-specific astrocyte responses to ketamine rapid antidepressant action [23, 24]. In any case, the astrocytic reactivity is closely related to excitotoxic and oxidative signaling: the overload of astrocytes with excessive glutamate can trigger neurotoxicity, and oxidative stress can excite astrocytes, providing a feed-forward loop of neurotoxicity [25]. Nrf2 (nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2) is a transcription factor which coordinates antioxidant responses in glia and neurons. During oxidative stress, Nrf2 is dissociated with its inhibitor Keap1 and translocates to the nucleus to cause the expression of cytoprotective enzymes (e.g., heme oxygenase-1, glutathione S-transferases) [26]. The Nrf 2 pathway is neuro-protective in multiple models of brain injury upon activation [27, 28]. It is noteworthy that the previous studies demonstrate that the improvement of Nrf2 signaling can be used to overcome the effect of ketamine-induced oxidative apoptosis. In such a manner, ketamine neurotoxic sequelae can be countered by compounds that increase the Nrf2 activity and suppress astrocytic overactivation [8].

Mangiferin (MGF) is a polyphenolic nutraceutical (c-glucosyl xanthone) occurring in abundance in mango (Mangifera indica) and has good potential neuropharmacological effects [29]. It has a strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory action, partly through the activation of the Nrf2 pathway [27]. Mangiferin is able to scavenge free radicals and induce enzymes of antioxidant activity, which is evidenced in the models of neurotoxin intoxication and metabolic insults [27, 30, 31]. Notably, mangiferin has neuro-protective effects in excitotoxic and oxidative brain injury models: it decreases neurotoxicity caused by glutamate and inhibits the formation of ROS. Recent research has shown that mangiferin has the capacity to reduce excessive glutamate release through the regulation of synaptic vesicle dynamics [32], and it can potentially have a direct effect on excitatory neurotransmission. At the same time, mangiferin can strengthen inhibitory signaling; in particular, it was reported to increase the levels of GABA and promote GABAergic neurotransmission in models of seizures [33]. Mangiferin has cognitive effects in dementia and neurodegeneration models in animals along with neurotransmitter modulation [34]. The mangiferin treatment enhanced learning and memory of scopolamine-induced amnesic and aging models, which is related to the decreased brain AChE activity and lipid peroxidation [35]. Mangiferin increases cholinergic transmission by inhibiting AChE, which is essential in memory formation [34]. It also reduces the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative markers in the brain thus making the brain more conducive to synaptic plasticity [36]. Mangiferin improved memory impairment and reduced malondialdehyde and tumor necrosis factor-a in the hippocampal of a rat with cognitive dysfunction due to aluminum, and restored AChE level [27, 37]. These multimodal activities place mangiferin in the spotlight to reverse ketamine with its multi-faceted neurotoxic profile - exciting/inhibitory imbalance, oxidative and inflammatory damage [38].

Nevertheless, it is yet not clear whether mangiferin has the ability to salvage ketamine-impaired glutamatergic-GABAergic imbalance and its related neuropathology. As ketamine has shown the ability to cause a schizophrenia-like condition in rodents (cognitive impairment, hyperlocomotion, and social withdrawal) and no useful treatments of the glutamate system have been found so far, the research of mangiferin is quite timely. Our hypothesis is that the antioxidant and neuromodulatory activities of mangiferin will restore neurotransmitter homeostasis (a rise in GABA and normal in glutamate release) [39], decrease oxidative stress levels by stimulating Nrf2, and decrease astrocyte-mediated neuroinflammation (a reduction in GFAP) in rats exposed to ketamine, which will improve behavior.

This study will provide an in-depth analysis of the effect of mangiferin on (1) behavioral phenotypes in the Morris Water Maze (spatial learning/memory), Y-Maze (working memory), and Novel Object Recognition (recognition memory); (2) glutamate, GABA, dopamine, and AChE neurotransmitter profiles in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and temporal cortex (regions involved in cognition and schizophrenia). Our results highlight the intersection of oxidative stress regulation, astrocyte and excitatory-inhibitory neurotransmission in ketamine-induced neuropathology, and demonstrate how the multitarget actions of mangiferin can effectively modulate these interconnected mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Animals and ethical approval

Adult male Wistar rats (8–10 weeks old, 170–200 g) were acquired in the animal facility of the Faculty of Basic Medical Sciences (University of Nigeria, Enugu), and kept at the standard laboratory conditions (12 h light/12 h dark, approximately 230 C, 50% humidity, 5 rats per cage). Rats were given free access to normal chow and water ad libitum. The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University (Approval #UNEC-2025-012) reviewed and approved all experimental procedures and performed them in compliance with the U.S. National Research Council guide to the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines on transparent animal research reporting [40]. Animal suffering was minimized by all means and the minimum number of animals was used to obtain statistical significance. Male animals were selected to minimize variability associated with hormonal fluctuations linked to the estrous cycle, which can influence stress responsiveness, neuroinflammatory signaling, and behavioral outcomes.

Experimental timeline

The experimental timeline is presented in Fig. 1A, illustrating the sequence of procedures. Rats underwent acclimatization (Day − 14 to Day 0), followed by assessment of basal neurobehavioral parameters (BANP) on Day 1. Ketamine induction (50 mg/kg, i.p.) was administered from Day 1 to Day 7. Post-induction neurobehavioral parameters (PINP) were assessed on Day 8 prior to initiation of treatment. From Day 8 to Day 21, animals received mangiferin (25, 50, or 75 mg/kg, p.o.) or risperidone (2 mg/kg, i.p.) depending on group allocation. Post-treatment neurobehavioral parameters (PTNP) were evaluated on Day 22, after which animals were sacrificed for biochemical and histological analyses.

Experimental design and treatment schedule in the ketamine-induced neurotoxicity model

Experimental design and treatment program

A randomization program created a computer-generated randomization schedule in seven groups (n = 5) where each rat was randomly assigned to one of the groups (Fig. 1B). The following were the group assignments: Group A (Control): administered 0.9% saline (vehicle) only; Group B (Ketamine only): administered ketamine to induce neurotoxicity; Group C (Mangiferin only): administered mangiferin without ketamine; Groups D, E, F (Ketamine + Mangiferin 25, 50, 75): administered ketamine induction followed by low-, mid-, and high-dose mangiferin treatment, respectively; Group G (Ketamine Tail-markings were used to identify each rat and all treatments were performed in a double-blind manner (the experimenters conducted a behavioral test and analysis blinded to which group they were in).

Ketamine (50 mg/kg body weight, i.p., 1 time daily, 7 days) was administered to induce a glutamatergic/GABAergic imbalance akin to schizophrenia (Groups B, D-G). The rationale behind this sub-anesthetic and repeated dosage was supported by previous research that showed chronic ketamine resulted in persistent cognitive impairment and cortical oxidative stress [4]. Twenty-four hours following the last injection of ketamine, the treatment phase was initiated: Groups D-F were orally given Mangiferin (MGF) 14 days at 25, 50, and 75 mg/kg body weight, respectively. Mangiferin (98% pure, Fuma Natural Science, USA) was prepared in distilled water and was given orally (5 mL/kg of volume via gavage). The dosing was chosen based on literature that showed the efficacy of the substance in a rodent model [37]. A pilot study was performed to assess tolerability. Group C was given 50 mg/kg mangiferin (top dose vehicle control) without pre-treatment of ketamine. Risperidone (2 mg/kg i.p.) was administered to group G after 24 h post-ketamine and daily at the rate of 14 days. Risperidone is an atypical antipsychotic that was selected as a positive control because it has been demonstrated to reduce hyperactivity caused by ketamine and certain cognitive impairments. The entire injections and gavage activities were conducted in the morning (09:00–10:00) to reduce circadian effects. During the experiment, the health of animals was observed on a daily basis.

Behavioral testing

A series of behavioral tests were administered to test the effects of ketamine and mangiferin on cognitive and behavioral performance. The behavioral tests were conducted in the light phase (10:00 to 16:00) in a special behavioral room, and the rats were familiarized with the testing conditions at least 1 h before [41, 42]. The behavioral apparatuses were washed with 70% ethanol between trials to remove olfactory cues. The experiments were conducted at two time-points (1) Post-ketamine induction (baseline): at the end of 7 days of ketamine or vehicle (Group A) to determine impairment and (2) Post-treatment: at the end of 14 days of mangiferin/risperidone or vehicle, to determine recovery. The tests and protocols were the following:

Morris Water Maze (MWM) of spatial learning and memory

The MWM evaluated the hippocampal-dependent spatial navigation skill. We took a circle pool (150 cm diameter, 50 cm height) and filled it with water (25 ± 1 °C) to which we added non-toxic white tempera that had rendered the water opaque. In the NE quadrant a crystal plexiglass platform (10 cm diameter) was placed beneath the water surface 1–2 cm below. There were specific visual cues that were located on walls around the room and on the pool walls. The training program modified after [43] was comprised of standardization session and then acquisition trials within 4 days. On Day 1, the rats were conditioned to the pool in the absence of the platform in a 60 s time-period. On Day 2–5, the rats were permitted to run a 4-trial/day-session (commencing with semi-randomized quadrants, facing the wall). When the rat found the position of the hidden platform in 60 s, it could stay there during 15s; otherwise it was carefully directed to the platform and could have a 15 s rest there. A video tracking system was used to record the escape latency (time to reach platform) in every trial. A probe trial was established 24 h after the last training trial (Day 6): the platform was eliminated and each of the rats was released in the pool at the other quadrant. The rats swam 60 s; the time in the target quadrant (where the platform had been located) and the number of crossings the platform location recorded as signs of memory retention were measured. Extended escape latencies in acquisition and decreased target quadrant preference in the probe are evidence of defective spatial learning/memory.

Y-Maze spontaneous alternation test of working memory

The Y-maze (wooden, 3 arms at angle of 1200, each arm 40 cm x 10 cm x 15 cm walls) was utilized in the assessment of spatial working memory through the observation of spontaneous alternation behavior. The rats were put at the end of one arm and they were given the opportunity to explore freely all the three arms at the same time, 8 min. The arm entries (the four paws in an arm) were noted. A sequence of entries was recorded to compute the Alternation Percentage, which is a ratio of the number of sets of consecutive entries, which comprised entries to all three different arms (i.e. ABC, CAB, BCA, etc. including overlapping triplets) divided by the number of possible alternations (total entries-2) x 100, providing a measure of spatial working memory performance, where higher percentages indicate better memory function and cognitive flexibility. The percentage of alternation is high (≥ 70%) in the state of intact working memory, and is lower in degree of impaired memory or stereotyped behavior. Between trials, the maze was turned to prevent the possibility of odor or visual cues.

Novel object recognition test (NORT) for recognition memory

The NORT takes advantage of the natural predilection of rodents towards novel objects to test the recognition memory. The test was conducted in a box (50 cm x 50 cm x 40 cm) other than the OFT arena. On day 1, rats were used to be acclimatized to the empty arena in 5 min. During the second day (training): Two objects of similar shape (small plastic toys, 5 cm in height) were located in opposite parts of the arena with the distance to the walls of about 10 cm. The duration of exploration was left to the rat (5 min) and the time spent on the exploration of each object was registered (object exploration was considered to be a nose pointing to or touching the object in a radius of less than 1 cm; sitting on the object or bumping it was not recorded). The rat was then put back to the home cage. The rat was then introduced in the arena (testing stage) after a period of 1 h retention in the arena when one of the two objects would be substituted by a new object of different shape and color (however comparable size). The rat was allowed to experiment 5 min; it was allowed to spend time on the Novel object vs. Familiar object.

An observer blinded to treatment gathered all behavioral data and analyzed them using relevant software or manually where necessary. The statistical analysis involved the mean behavioral endpoints (latencies, distances, times, alternation percentage, etc.) per group.

Neurotransmitter and enzymatic assays

On day 15 of the treatment phase (i.e., 24 h after the last drug administration), rats were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg i.p.) and decapitated. Brain removal was done quickly and the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and temporal cortex (including hippocampal region of the temporal lobe) were excised on ice based on a rat brain atlas [44]. Biochemical analyses were done by immediately freezing the tissues at -80 °C. In each rat, neurotransmitter assays were performed using the PFC and temporal cortex of one hemisphere, and oxidative stress assays and immunohistochemistry were performed using the other hemisphere (see below). Neurotransmitter measurements in tissues were done as follows: Sample (approximately 50 mg) was homogenized in 0.1 M ice-cold perchloric acid (10% w/v) containing 0.02% EDTA to precipitate proteins and conserve monoamines. Homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 x g during 15 min at 4 °C and supernatants were harvested to be analyzed. For consistency purposes, biochemical assays (neurotransmitter and enzymatic analyses) were performed on the left hemisphere, while the opposite hemisphere was reserved for histological processing.

Glutamate and GABA levels

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with fluorescence detection following derivatization with o-phthalaldehyde (OPA) was used to determine Glutamate and GABA concentrations in cortical tissue. In brief, supernatant aliquots were reacted with OPA/b-mercaptoethanol and injected on a reversed-phase C18 column (Agilent Eclipse XDB). Amino acid derivatives were separated on the mobile phase (phosphate buffer with acetonitrile gradient) and then identified using a fluorescence detector (ex 340 nm, em 450 nm). GABA and glutamate were measured by comparing the peaks with the external standards and dividing the results with the tissue protein content (determined by Bradford assay). The findings are reported as mmol/gram of protein. This technique is nanomolar sensitive and appropriate in detecting alterations caused by ketamine (which is expected to alter the levels of glutamate/GABA). As an alternative, GABA was quantified with an enzyme-linked assay kit (e.g., Abcam), which showed consistent results.

Dopamine levels

Dopamine was determined in the same extracts by the HPLC technique with electrochemical detection. A C18 column was pumped at 0.7 mL/min with the mobile phase (citrate-acetate buffer with 10% methanol, 1 mM octanesulfonic acid and 0.1 mM EDTA, pH 3.5). The retention time (7–8 min) was used to identify dopamine and measured by peak height against a standard curve.

Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity

AChE activity at cortical homogenates was assessed using the Ellman colorimetric technique with minor modifications. In a 96-well plate, 1 mM acetylthiocholine iodide (substrate) and 0.5 mM 5,5’-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) were incubated in supernatants (in Tris-HCl buffer pH 7.5) at 25o C. Acetylthiocholine reacts with AChE to hydrolyze into thiocholine which reacts with DTNB to form a yellow 5-thio-2-nitrobenzoate anion, 412 nm per minute of 5 min. AChE activity was expressed as the linear change in absorbance per minute and calculated as micromoles of substrate hydrolyzed per minute per milligram of protein (ε₄₁₂ = 13,600 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹). An elevated AChE activity indicates more acetylcholine degradation, which is usually associated with memory impairment [45].

Oxidative stress markers

Although not the main outcome of the results presented in this paper, we also quantified the major oxidative stress indices, as they serve to support the results of Nrf2. Protease inhibitors were added to cortical tissue that was homogenized in ice-cold Tris-KCl buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 1.15% KCl). The homogenate was centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C) and the supernatants were measured on the following; Malondialdehyde (MDA), a lipid peroxidation product, by thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay (absorbance at 532 nm); Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) activity by inhibition rate assay (at 550 nm); Catalase (CAT) activity by hydro Such data were used to confirm our immunohistochemical evidence of oxidative stress (Nrf2 activation) [46].

Histology: hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Two rats from each group were selected for histopathological and immunohistochemical. These rats were anesthetized (pentobarbital 60 mg/kg i.p.) and transcardially perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS 24 h after the final treatment (Wu et al., 2021). Brains were removed and left overnight to post-fix in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C. The tissues were then subjected to a graded alcohols and xylene and paraffin embedded. At the levels of the prefrontal cortex (bregma + 2.7 mm to + 2.2 mm) and the temporal cortex, including the hippocampus (approximately bregma − 3.3 mm to − 4.0 mm), coronal Sect. (5 μm thick) were cut using a rotary microtome and mounted on silane-coated slides. For hippocampal analysis, sections were obtained from comparable rostro-caudal levels based on established anatomical landmarks to ensure consistent sampling of the dentate gyrus across experimental groups.

In case of general morphology, deparaffinized sections were stained using H&E. Slides were rehydrated and stained with Harris hematoxylin (5 min) and rinsed after staining followed by differentiation in acid alcohol and blued in ammonia water and eosin Y counterstaining (2 min). The sections were dehydrated and cleared, followed by the addition of covers lipped with DPX mountant. With H&E, one can examine the neuronal architecture and identify pathologic alterations, including neuronal death (pyknosis, karyorrhexis), vacuolation (spongiosis or edema), and gliosis. We targeted the layer-specific neuronal integrity of the PFC and the hippocampal pyramidal layer (CA subfields). Slides were examined by a Leica DM2500 brightfield light microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Images were taken at total magnifications of x100 and x400 with 10x and 40x objective lenses respectively. The 40x objective had a numerical aperture (NA) of around 0.65 which is sufficient for histological assessments. Each section was graded by an observer who was not aware of treatment on a qualitative scale on features: neuronal density, cell body morphology (normal vs. shrunken eosinophilic red neurons), presence of neuronophagia (microglial clusters around dying neurons), and general cytoarchitecture preservation. The digital microscope camera was used to capture the representative photomicrographs. Neuronal damage was semi-quantitatively analyzed by counting pyknotic neurons within a defined area (0.25 mm²) of layer V of the cortex or the hippocampal pyramidal cell layer (CA subfields Counts were made from 3 non-overlapping fields from each section and averaged for each animal. These quantitative data were used to support the visual scoring of neurodegeneration between experimental groups [45].

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) of GFAP and Nrf2

PFC and temporal paraffin sections that were adjacent to one another were IHC-processed to identify GFAP (astrocytes) and Nrf2 (oxidative stress response). Xylene was used to deparaffinize and rehydrate sections with graded ethanols. Antigen retrieval was done by heating the slides in citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) at 95–100 °C during 20 min (in a microwave or water bath) followed by cooling at room temperature during 20 min. The activity of endogenous peroxidase was inhibited by adding 0.3% of H 2 O 2 in PBS after 10 min. To prevent non-specific binding, sections were blocked in a blocking solution, 5% normal goat serum (or animal free blocking agent, Vector Labs) in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 1 h. Primary antibodies were then incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber on sections. The following were the main antibodies: rabbit anti-GFAP (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology #12389) and rabbit anti-Nrf2 (1:200, Proteintech #16396-1-AP). These are antibodies against GFAP (an intermediate filament in astrocytes) and Nrf2 (transcription factor) respectively and were tested in rat tissue. Following primary incubation, the slides were washed in PBS and incubated with a polymer of HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (ImmPRESS HRP anti-rabbit IgG polymer kit, Vector Labs) 30 min at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was once more seen and with the aid of 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (Vector DAB Peroxidase kit). The time of development was regulated (~ 2 min) under microscopic observation to accomplish certain brown staining. Parts were then counterstained with Harris hematoxylin (30 s), stained, dehydrated, cleared, and covered.

In negative controls, no primary antibodies were added on parallel sections (instead, blocking buffer was used); these did not have any DAB stain, which indicated specificity. We used rat hippocampal sections that have been known to express high levels of GFAP (with an unrelated brain injury model) and liver sections that express Nrf2 (with centrilobular inducible Nrf2) as positive controls so that the IHC protocol successfully labels the targets [15, 47, 48].

Image analysis

ImageJ (NIH) with relevant plugins was used to analyze the Image of GFAP and Nrf2 IHC. In the case of GFAP, the density of the astrocytes and the immunoreactivity in the PFC and temporal cortex were quantified. The use of high-magnification images (400x) was performed in the same region (e.g. in PFC layers II-IV and hippocampal CA subfields). GFAP-positive astrocytes (identified by a cell body surrounded by processes that were stained clearly) were counted manually in three fields of each Sect. (0.1 mm 2 each) using the Cell Counter plug-in. The averages of values were used to provide the number of astrocytes per mm 2. The percentage area density of the staining of GFAP in each field was also measured as a measure of astrogliosis (via a thresholding technique to separate DAB stain). In the case of Nrf2, we determined nuclear Nrf2 expression. The percentage of the number of hematoxylin-stained nuclei that were positive to Nrf2-DAB staining (nuclear Nrf2 index) was calculated using the ImmunoRatio (separating DAB and hematoxylin channels) to determine the percentage. It is basically a calculation of the ratio of brown (DAB) area in nuclei/total nuclear area to provide the proportion of cells with Nrf2 activation. The technique is appropriate with transcription factors such as Nrf2 which translocate to the nucleus when activated. For each animal, three anatomically matched coronal sections were analyzed from the prefrontal cortex, temporal cortex, hippocampal CA subfields (CA1–CA3), and dentate gyrus (DG). The DG was included because of its established role in learning, memory, and hippocampal neuroplasticity, and because it exhibited treatment-related histological and immunohistochemical alterations. Quantification within the DG was performed using anatomically defined boundaries, and measurements were obtained from standardized, non-overlapping high-power fields in a manner consistent with the procedures used for other hippocampal regions. Sections were obtained at standardized stereotaxic coordinates and spaced by at least 100 μm to reduce the likelihood of oversampling a given cellular population. Within each section, three non-overlapping, high magnification fields were quantified per region resulting in a total of nine fields per region per animal. Quantitative values were averaged for each animal before group-level statistical analysis, Results are expressed as the mean number of cells per high-power field (cells/HPF) that had positive immunoreactivity. This sampling strategy was deployed to minimize intra-section bias and increase representation of regional variability. The study adhered to ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. No adverse events were observed, apart from transient mild sedation following ketamine administration, and there was no mortality. Figure 1A presents a summary of the experimental timeline.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented in form of mean +- standard error of the mean (SEM) per group. First, behavioral data (escape latencies, times, distances, alternations, discrimination indices) and biochemical data (neurotransmitter levels, enzyme activities, cell counts, percentage area, etc.) were verified regarding normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene test). One-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied depending on the data set. As an example, data on two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (treatment group x training day) were used to analyze the data on MWM escape latency acquisition, whereas the data on probe trial performance and other one-time measures were analyzed using one-way ANOVA (factor: treatment group). In cases where ANOVAs showed a significant effect (p < 0.05), post hoc comparisons were done using the multiple comparison test by Tukey in order to compare all the groups. Baseline equivalence among ketamine-exposed groups was confirmed prior to treatment initiation In particular, the effect of ketamine was confirmed by comparing Group B (ketamine only) to Group A (control) and the effectiveness of drugs was compared by comparing treatment groups (C-G) to B. The dose-dependence of mangiferin was analyzed using the linear trend analysis among Groups D-F, AChE, and oxidative markers were also analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Statistical annotations in figures indicate comparisons versus vehicle (asterisks) or ketamine (hash symbols), as defined in each legend. The statistical tests were conducted using SPSS (v27) and GraphPad Prism 9.

Effects of mangiferin on spatial learning and memory (Morris Water Maze)

Following ketamine administration, escape latency was significantly increased when compared with vehicle treated group (p < 0.01) indicating successful induction of cognitive impairment (Fig. 2). No significant differences were found between ketamine-exposed groups at the post-induction time point which confirms uniform impairment prior to treatment initiation.

After the treatment phase, administration of mangiferin showed significant reduction in escape latency in a dose-dependent manner. Post hoc analysis revealed that 50 mg/kg and 75 mg/kg mangiferin significantly improved performance compared with the ketamine group (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively). Risperidone (2 mg/kg) produced moderate but significant improvement relative to ketamine (p < 0.05), whereas ketamine-only animals remained significantly impaired compared with vehicle controls (p < 0.01).

Number of platform crossings (Fig. 3)

No significant differences between groups were found at baseline in the number of platform crossings. Following ketamine induction, ketamine-treated animals showed significant decrease in the platform crossings as compared with the vehicle group animals (p < 0.01), suggesting impaired spatial memory. No significant differences were found between ketamine-experienced groups at the post-induction time point. After the treatment phase, the performance of platform crossing significantly improved with the treatment of mangiferin in a dose-dependent manner (p < 0.001). In the prefrontal and temporal cortices, the control groups had low GFAP immunoreactivity with fine astrocytic processes, suggesting maintenance of astroglial homeostasis. Ketamine administration significantly increased GFAP-positive hypertrophied astrocytes consistent with marked astrogliosis. Treatment with mangiferin led to a dose-dependent decrease in the level of astrocyte activation with slight reductions at lower doses and more marked suppression of GFAP expression at higher dosages. Quantitative analysis confirmed these observations and showed significantly increased GFAP levels after ketamine exposure that were gradually decreased by the treatment of mangiferin.

Similar trends were seen in the hippocampal regions where the exposure to ketamine had strong astrocytic activation, and the mangiferin treatment had a dose-dependent reduction of GFAP immunoreactivity, which suggests mitigation of ketamine-induced astroglial reactivity.

In parallel, Nrf2 immunohistochemistry showed decreased nuclear immunoreactivity of Nrf2 after ketamine administration in the cortex and hippocampus tissues. Control animals had basal Nrf2 staining with scattered positive nuclei while ketamine animals had drastically reduced Nrf2 nuclear staining. Mangiferin treatment increased Nrf2 immunoreactivity in a dose-dependent manner, and the most intense treatment resulted in the greatest recovery of Nrf2 positive nuclei. The quantitative analysis supported these findings and showed that Nrf2 expression was suppressed following ketamine exposure and there was significant recovery after mangiferin treatment.

Collectively, these results indicate that this neurotoxicity from ketamine is linked to an increased activation of astrocytes and a suppression of the Nrf2 mediated antioxidant signaling, both of which were diminished after treatment with mangiferin.

Discussion

We examined in this research the protective action of mangiferin in a model of ketamine-induced excitatory-inhibitory imbalance and neurotoxicity in rats. We have shown that, simultaneously, mangiferin can regulate neurotransmitter systems, mitigate oxidative stress, and restrain glial reactivity, which results in a remarkable neuroprotective effect and cognitive enhancement [49]. Such multimodal activity of mangiferin responds to the interconnected pathogenic mechanisms caused by ketamine: overreacting glutamatergic excitation, GABAergic inhibition, oxidative injury, and neuroinflammation [50].

In our model, the neurotoxic profile of ketamine was obviously reproduced. Sub-chronic administration of ketamine led to spatial memory impairment (MWM), working memory deficits (Y-maze), recognition memory deficits (NORT) and hyperlocomotion and anxiety behaviour changes of different types. These are consistent with established impacts of NMDA receptor impairment on cognition [8]; and are consistent with elements of schizophrenia and dissociative states observed in humans when ketamine is administered. The identified locomotor activity increase and decreased alternation are consistent with the capacity of ketamine to induce a schizophrenia-like phenotype (positive symptom analogs such as hyperactivity, and cognitive deficits) in rodents, which validates our model [51]. Ketamine resulted in a significant reduction of both GABA and glutamate tissue concentrations, neurochemically. Although acute ketamine causes synaptic glutamate release [52], the observed overall decrease in tissue content following days of exposure indicates potential injury of glutamatergic neurons or downregulation, which is in line with previously reported ketamine-induced neurotoxicity and loss of glutamatergic terminals in the cortex and hippocampus [4]. At the same time, the fact that ketamine affects GABA interneurons probably decreased the level of GABA production or release, which also contributes to the GABA deficit [53, 54]. Also, our study found that ketamine had a relatively small but significant increase in cortical dopamine, which is consistent with the disinhibition of excitatory drive to ventral tegmental area (VTA) dopamine neurons; these dopamine surges are involved in the locomotor stimulation of ketamine, as well as its psychosis like effects [55].

Importantly, oxidative stress and astroglial activation were induced by ketamine. We observed more lipid peroxidation (evidenced by high MDA and low SOD/CAT in ketamine group) and histological proof of cell damage (neuronophagia, pyknotic neurons) [55]. The excess production of ROS through NMDA-antagonism-induced hypermetabolism and the dysfunction of the mitochondrion has been associated with ketamine neurotoxicity. Interestingly, our model apparently did not elicit a sufficient Nrf2 antioxidant response (Nrf2 nuclear levels decreased) to ketamine, which could either be due to excessive oxidative stress preventing Nrf2 translocation or Nrf2 might be suppressed by anesthesia (as some studies suggest) [56]. The secondary response is the concomitant astrogliosis (increased GFAP) in ketamine-only rats: astrocytes become reactive in the conditions of neuronal injury and ROS, and they aim to contain the damage but may contribute to the increase in the inflammatory process in case of overactivation [57]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines can be released by reactive astrocytes, and some of its homeostatic functions (such as efficient glutamate uptake) are lost, exacerbating excitotoxic states [27]. In fact, the dysfunction of the astrocytes has gained more popularity in the models of NMDA antagonists and the pathology of schizophrenia (e.g., reduced astrocyte markers in patient brains) [58]. The glial alterations of our ketamine group (greater number of GFAP, microglial clusters) are compatible with the initial neuroinflammatory mechanism caused by excitotoxic injury.

It is on this backdrop that mangiferin was a spectacular protective agent. The results of mangiferin-treated rats were significantly better in all behavioral domains, which demonstrates the preservation of neural networks [34]. The mechanism of the cognitive effects of mangiferin can be attributed to regaining of neurotransmitter balance. It avoided the GABA decrease and glutamate imbalance caused by ketamine and was able to restore the excitatory-inhibitory balance. A potential process is that mangiferin decreased glutamate release overload and neuron loss (so tissue glutamate was still more), but facilitated GABAergic interneurons survival or activity. Literature is in favor of mangiferin being able to regulate presynaptic release machinery: Hsu et al. [32] reported that mangiferin can inhibit excessive vesicular glutamate release in cortical synaptosomes through inhibiting synapsin phosphorylation. This might inhibit excitotoxic damage in a hyperglutamatergic condition (e.g. acute ketamine). At the same time, mangiferin would have saved the GABAergic interneurons that are highly exposed to oxidative damage by reducing the oxidative stress. The overall impact was GABA homeostasis and restoration of E/I ratio, which directly transferred into the improvement of memory-related performance since balanced network activity is essential to synchronous firing and plasticity that underlie learning [59]. Our findings are consistent with those that mangiferin enhances memory in various models (scopolamine, aging, aluminum toxicity) through the modulation of neurotransmitters and antioxidant effects. As an example, it was found that in Biradar et al. [37], mangiferin improved learning, and decreased AChE and oxidative products, which is a very similar result as ours.

The antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of mangiferin were supported by immunohistochemical findings demonstrating increased Nrf2 immunoreactivity. Nrf2 is a key regulator of cytoprotective gene expression, including enzymes involved in cellular defense against oxidative stress [8, 27]. While the present study did not directly assess downstream antioxidant enzymes or oxidative stress markers, the observed increase in Nrf2 immunoreactivity suggests a potential engagement of endogenous protective pathways.

This increase in Nrf2 expression was observed alongside preservation of neuronal morphology, indicating a possible association between enhanced cellular defense signaling and structural neuronal integrity. Similar observations have been reported in previous studies, where polyphenolic compounds such as curcumin were shown to upregulate Nrf2 expression in models of ketamine-induced neurotoxicity [8].

Importantly, in the current study, interpretations are limited to the observed increase in Nrf2 immunoreactivity and its association with histological preservation, without implying direct confirmation of downstream oxidative modulation. The observed elevation of Nrf2 beyond baseline levels may reflect a pharmacological preconditioning effect, whereby cellular defense mechanisms are primed under treatment conditions. Given the established role of impaired Nrf2 signaling in neuropsychiatric disorders, including depression and schizophrenia [60], these findings support the relevance of Nrf2-associated pathways as potential targets of mangiferin’s neuroprotective effects.

At the same time, mangiferin inhibited the overactivation of astrocytes. Lower levels of GFAP in treated groups are signs of less reactive astrocytosis, with several possible positive implications [9, 61, 62]. Although reacting astrocytes may initially be protective (e.g., walling off injury), they may inhibit synaptic activity by releasing inflammatory mediators and by decreasing glutamate uptake. Maintaining the astrocytes in a less activated state could guarantee a greater clearance of extracellular glutamate (avoiding excitotoxic accumulation) and support of neuronal metabolism by mangiferin. This may be an indirect effect of less neuronal destruction (less debris and cytokines to react to) or a direct anti-inflammatory action of mangiferin on astrocytes. In fact, mangiferin has been shown to inhibit NF-kB and pro-inflammatory cytokines in the brain, which is likely to suppress the stimuli of astrogliosis. Furthermore, the activation of Nrf2 in the cells (the astrocytes themselves) may promote a positive phenotype that promotes the survival of neurons and suppresses inflammation [30]. Our data fits into this paradigm: the dual activity of mangiferin facilitates a condition in which astrocytes are protective, not pathogenic, and leads to regained homeostasis.

The other area of interest is acetylcholinesterase inhibition by mangiferin. The action of ketamine to increase AChE could worsen the cognitive impairment by decreasing acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter essential in attention in memory. Mangiferin had a significant inhibitory effect on AChE activity, probably extending the acetylcholine levels in synapses (some studies indicate that mangiferin directly or indirectly inhibits AChE [63]. Enhanced cholinergic transmission would complement the glutamate/GABA rebalancing to promote cognitive functioning. Mangiferin, therefore, treats cognitive impairment on several neurotransmitter levels: GABA, glutamate, and acetylcholine (through dopamine, as well). Such a wide-ranging neuromodulation is specifically useful with the intricate neurotransmitter dysregulation associated with such disorders as schizophrenia or dementia.

Comparison of mangiferin with risperidone

Risperidone was used as a reference drug that targets majorly dopamine D2 (and 5-HT2A) receptors [64]. In our findings, risperidone not only suppressed hyperactivity (through dopamine blockage) but also marginally improved memory tasks, but was otherwise not very significant in restoring the glutamate/GABA or the oxidative/inflammatory alterations. This brings to the fore a major aspect, namely, standard antipsychotics may mitigate certain symptoms (positive symptoms, agitation) through the modulation of dopamine, but it does not protect the brain directly or restore cognitive impairment to a significant degree. The existing antipsychotics pay little attention to cognitive deficits in schizophrenia models. On the contrary, mangiferin attacked the root causes of neuronal dysfunction (excitotoxicity and oxidative stress), providing more comprehensive recovery of neuronal function [30]. This highlights the possibilities of therapies exceeding neurotransmitter receptor antagonism to disease-modifying effects (antioxidant, anti-excitotoxic, anti-inflammatory). Mangiferin or analogs may be a new category of adjunctive therapies that seek to treat cognitive restoration in disorders that have NMDA receptor hypofunction or glutamate regulatory defects.

Integrated systems biology viewpoint

The results of this study are representative of the way in which various systems - neurotransmitters, redox regulation, glial support, etc. - intersect in the definition of neural health and functionality. Ketamine disturbed this network on numerous levels, but mangiferin having pleiotropic properties could intervene at numerous important nodes: it reduced oxidative stress (Nrf2 |), cellularly defended neurons and preserved the activity of inhibitory interneurons, and systemically at the cognitive circuit level (behavior improved). The integration is especially pertinent, since in neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric disease a single target drug may fail to be effective when it is unable to deal with the multifactorial pathology. The multi-target properties of mangiferin - antioxidant, anti-excitotoxic, anti-inflammatory, and neuromodulator - could be useful in complex CNS disorders where, e.g. chronic oxidative stress is augmented by glutamate dysregulation and vice versa. Our results are consistent with other studies that have reported the benefits of mangiferin using stroke [27] and Parkinson disease [64] and heavy metal neurotoxicity models [30]. Mangiferin decreased infarct size and neurologic impairment in ischemic stroke models via Nrf2 activation and ferroptosis inhibition [27], which is analogous to Nrf2-mediated neuroprotection in our study. This cross-condition efficacy highlights that the broad neuroprotective effects could be achieved due to improvement of intrinsic protective pathways (such as Nrf2) and neurotransmitter balance [65–68].

Limitations and future directions

Although our report contains some strong evidence of the protective effect of mangiferin, it is more of an acute treatment/preventive model (mangiferin administered immediately after ketamine insult). The question on whether mangiferin can also reverse the already existing deficits (therapeutic vs. prophylactic effect) and the effect it has on plasticity changes caused by ketamine in the long term, remains to be answered. Repeated exposure to ketamine induces synaptic remodelling and impairments in interneuron integration [69], which mangiferin might prevent under any circumstances (e.g. through electrophysiology or dendritic spine measurement) need to be investigated. Also, our neurotransmitter measures might be endpoint tissue levels - dynamic functional measures (microdialysis or electrophysiological recordings) could also better establish how mangiferin acutely regulates neurotransmission in vivo (e.g. does it suppress ketamine-induced glutamate spikes in real time? ). The other area of inquiry would be whether Nrf2 is required in the neuroprotection of mangiferin: the effects of Nrf2 inhibitors or knockout animals would establish the degree to which the effects are Nrf2-dependent [27]. Since mangiferin exhibits several bioactivities, the input of other pathways (e.g., SIRT1/PGC-1a or anti-apoptotic signaling [70–73]may also be involved; this was out of scope in our current study.

Translationally, mangiferin is a natural compound that has an excellent safety profile, and the doses applied to the rats (25–75 mg/kg) fall within a range that might be reproducible to human levels of supplementation (where metabolic differences are taken into consideration). Its penetrability of the blood-brain barrier and achievement of effective concentrations in the brain is evidenced by the research where oral mangiferin increased cognition and decreased brain oxidative markers [51]. As such, mangiferin or its derivatives could be viewed as nutritional or adjunctive interventions in diseases such as cognitive impairment in schizophrenia, or even to counteract the cognitive adverse effects of therapeutic ketamine (since this now has clinical uses in treatment-resistant depression). Indeed, co-treatment approaches that involve the use of an NMDA modulator and an antioxidant/inflammatory modulator such as mangiferin may have superior effects when used together than when used individually.

Finally, our study demonstrates the therapeutic potential of mangiferin in reinstating neural homeostasis following ketamine-derived derailment. It confirms the idea that a combination of the effects on excitatory-inhibitory balance and oxidative stress can be effective in rescuing cognitive performance and neuronal wellness. The multimodal activities of mangiferin - between synapses and gene expression - are an example of applications of systems biology to neuroprotection. Such findings prompt additional investigation into the use of mangiferin in complex brain diseases with similar pathogenic triad of glutamatergic overdrive, GABAergic dysfunction and oxidative neuroinflammation. Clinical research in the future would be able to assess whether dietary or supplemental mangiferin (e.g. mango extracts or isolated compound) can offer cognitive or neuroprotective benefit or be neuroprotective in at-risk populations to provide a safe and natural complement to current therapies. The present study was done with male rats. Sex differences in glutamatergic and GABAergic signaling, oxidative stress responses and ketamine sensitivity have been documented in the literature. Therefore, the findings may not be directly generalizable to female animals and future studies including both sexes would be of value to identify possible sex-dependent effects of ketamine and Mangifera treatment.
