Quantum state texture of dynamical criticality
Lucas C. Céleri, Krissia Zawadzki, Ivan Medina, Diogo O. Soares-Pinto

Abstract
We investigate the role of quantum state texture in dynamical quantum phase transitions by establishing a direct connection between critical nonequilibrium dynamics and the recently introduced notion of rugosity, a measure of the quantum state texture. Considering a generic quench protocol, we analyze both standard formulations of the dynamical quantum phase transition. For type-I transitions, defined through the long-time behavior of an order parameter, we show that the time averaged rugosity, evaluated in the eigenbasis of the pre-quench Hamiltonian, acts itself as an order parameter, sharply distinguishing the dynamical phases. In the Lipkin-Meshkov-Glick model, this behavior is traced back to the underlying semiclassical structure, where the crossing of the excited-state quantum phase transition separatrix controls the redistribution of the state over the pre-quench energy basis. For type-II transitions, characterized by nonanalyticities in the Loschmidt rate function, we demonstrate that rugosity acquires a universal interpretation. For a suitable choice of basis, the rate function is exactly given by the density of rugosity, establishing a model-independent equivalence. Moreover, we show that even in physically motivated bases, such as the pre-quench energy eigenbasis, rugosity provides clear signatures of dynamical criticality. Our results place rugosity within a broader class of quantities diagnosing dynamical quantum phase transitions, including complexity and entropy production, while highlighting its distinct role as a measure of a basis-dependent quantum resource. This work provides an information-theoretic perspective on dynamical critical phenomena and opens new directions for exploring quantum texture in nonequilibrium many-body systems.



I
Introduction

Studying the properties of quantum resources is a fundamental task not only for deepening our understanding of the structure of quantum theory, but also for enabling technological applications that exploit genuinely quantum features. In this direction, resource theories have emerged as a unifying and systematic framework to identify, quantify, and manipulate such resources under physically motivated constraints
Chitambar2019
. Among the various proposals, some resource theories have reached a particularly mature stage, including those of entanglement
Horodecki2009
, stabilizerness
Veitch2014
, quantum coherence
Streltsov2017
and quantum thermodynamics
Lostaglio2019
. These frameworks have led to significant progress on both the theoretical side, by clarifying the operational meaning of nonclassical features, and the experimental side, by guiding the design and certification of quantum protocols. Their impact spans a wide range of fields, from quantum computation and communication
Hahn2025
;
Son2025
to many-body physics
Garcia2025
and thermodynamics
Brandao2013
, thus explicitly demonstrating the central role of resource-theoretic thinking in modern quantum science.

More recently, a new resource theory has been introduced to characterize the structural irregularities of quantum states, establishing a direct connection between the geometry of density matrices and an operationally meaningful resource, the so-called rugosity
Parisio2024
. This quantity is intrinsically basis dependent, in close analogy with quantum coherence, but it captures a finer notion of structure by being sensitive not only to the magnitude of off-diagonal elements but also to their relative phases. In this sense, rugosity provides a measure of quantum state texture, quantifying how far a given state is from a completely uniform (flat) superposition in a chosen basis. Beyond its original formulation, several recent works have proposed alternative measures of quantum texture, explored their geometric and information-theoretic interpretations, and connected them to broader notions of complexity and structure in quantum states
Cao2026
;
Liu2025
;
Cui2026
;
Chen2026
;
Muthuganesan2026
. Applications have already begun to emerge in different contexts, including equilibrium quantum phase transitions
Patra2026
and relativistic open quantum systems
Huang2025
, suggesting that texture-based quantities may provide a new lens through which to analyze quantum phenomena.

In this work, we investigate the texture of the quantum state in the context of dynamical quantum phase transitions (DQPTs)
Heyl2013
;
Heyl2018
;
Heyl2019
. Unlike equilibrium phase transitions, which are governed by thermal ensembles and free-energy landscapes, DQPTs arise in the unitary time evolution of isolated quantum many-body systems following a quench. In such situations, the system typically explores a broad set of excited eigenstates with a highly nonthermal distribution, and its dynamics cannot be described within the standard framework of equilibrium statistical mechanics. This makes nonequilibrium dynamics a particularly rich setting for a probe of fundamental questions about ergodicity, thermalization, and the emergence of macroscopic behavior from microscopic quantum dynamics. In particular, the study of DQPTs has revealed that sharp, nonanalytic behavior, reminiscent of equilibrium critical phenomena, can emerge purely in time, providing a dynamical notion of criticality.

Recent developments have highlighted deep connections between DQPTs and concepts from quantum information and quantum thermodynamics. For example, within a phase space description, it has been shown that the Wehrl entropy exhibits quasi-monotonic growth and reaches maximal production rates near the dynamical critical point
Goes2020
, signaling enhanced mixing and spreading in phase space. Similarly, Krylov complexity has been identified as an effective order parameter for dynamical criticality in certain models
Bento2024
, reflecting the rapid growth of operator complexity and the exploration of Hilbert space. In addition, dynamical criticality has been associated with an acceleration of thermodynamic entropy production
Nascimento2024
, as well as with the buildup of symmetry-related coherences, as captured by asymmetry measures
Nascimento2025
. Taken together, these results point toward a unified picture in which DQPTs act as highly efficient mechanisms for redistributing quantum information, linking entropy production, complexity growth, and symmetry properties in nonequilibrium dynamics.

Within this broader context, our goal is to investigate the behavior of quantum state texture under nonequilibrium evolution. By considering both types of DQPTs, we first show that the density of rugosity exactly coincides with the Loschmidt rate function, thus characterizing type-II DQPT
Heyl2014
;
Heyl2015
;
Vajna2015
;
Heyl2017
;
Flaschner2018
, when evaluated on a suitably chosen basis, thus establishing a direct and model-independent connection between texture and dynamical criticality. For type-I DQPTs
Sciolla2013
;
Zhang2017
;
Smacchia2015
;
Halimeh2017
;
Chen2020
, defined through the long-time behavior of an order parameter, we demonstrate that the time-averaged rugosity, computed in the eigenbasis of the pre-quench Hamiltonian, plays the role of an order parameter in the Lipkin-Meshkov-Glick model. This behavior can be understood from the underlying semiclassical structure of the model, where the presence of a double-well energy landscape and the associated excited-state quantum phase transition govern the redistribution of the state over the relevant basis. Based on this mechanism, we conjecture that similar behavior should arise in other systems that admit a well-defined classical limit with a separatrix structure, suggesting that rugosity may provide a general and physically transparent probe of dynamical critical phenomena.

II
DQPT and rugosity

Consider a quantum system described by a Hamiltonian
H(h)
, where
h
denotes an externally controlled parameter or, more generally, a set of control parameters. A dynamical quantum phase transition is typically investigated through a sudden-quench protocol. The system is initially prepared in the ground state
\ket{\psi_0}
of the Hamiltonian
H_{0}\equiv H(h_{0})
. At time
t=0
, the control parameter abruptly changes from
h_{0}
to
h_{f}
, and the subsequent evolution is governed by the post-quench Hamiltonian
H_{f}\equiv H(h_{f})
. The state at time
t
is therefore

\ket{\psi_t}=e^{-iH_{f}t}\ket{\psi_0}.

(1)

Two complementary notions of dynamical quantum phase transitions have been developed in the literature. The first, commonly referred to as type-I DQPT (DQPT-I), is formulated in terms of the long-term behavior of an order parameter
Sciolla2013
;
Zhang2017
;
Smacchia2015
;
Halimeh2017
;
Chen2020
. Given an observable
O
, one defines its infinite-time average as

\overline{\langle O\rangle}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\differential t\,\langle O\rangle,

(2)

where
\langle O\rangle=\bra{\psi_t}O\ket{\psi_t}
. A DQPT-I occurs when
\overline{\langle O\rangle}
changes qualitatively as a function of the quench parameter. In many systems, including the Lipkin-Meshkov-Glick model considered here, the long-time order parameter is finite on one side of the transition and vanishes on the other. This behavior distinguishes a regime in which the dynamics retain memory of the initially symmetry-broken state from a regime in which the symmetry is dynamically restored.

The second characterization, known as type-II DQPT (DQPT-II), is based on the probability that the system returns to its initial state
Heyl2014
;
Heyl2015
;
Vajna2015
;
Heyl2017
;
Flaschner2018
. The latter is associated with the Loschmidt amplitude

\mathcal{G}_{t}=\bra{\psi_0}e^{-iH_{f}t}\ket{\psi_0},

(3)

whose squared modulus

\mathcal{L}_{t}=|\mathcal{G}_{t}|^{2}

(4)

is the Loschmidt echo, or return probability. In analogy with the free-energy density of equilibrium statistical mechanics, one defines the rate function

\lambda_{t}=-\frac{1}{N}\ln\mathcal{L}_{t},

(5)

where
N
denotes the number of subsystems (for example, the number of spins in a spin chain). In the thermodynamic limit, a DQPT-II is signaled by nonanalytic behavior of
\lambda_{t}
, which typically appears as cusplike singularities at a sequence of critical times
Heyl2018
;
Heyl2019
. These singularities arise when Fisher zeros of the analytically continued Loschmidt amplitude cross the real-time axis, in close analogy with the role played by Lee-Yang and Fisher zeros in equilibrium phase transitions
Heyl2014
;
Heyl2015
;
Vajna2015
;
Heyl2017
;
Flaschner2018
.

The aim of this work is to relate this nonequilibrium critical behavior to a recently introduced quantum resource theory for the quantum state texture, which we describe now
Parisio2024
. Consider a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{d}
of dimension
d
, along with an orthonormal basis
\{\ket{\epsilon_m}\}_{m=1}^{d}
. Associated with this basis one defines the state of minimum texture, or flat state

\ket{\omega}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{m=1}^{d}\ket{\epsilon_m}.

(6)

This is a unique pure state whose amplitudes are completely uniform in the chosen basis. The texture of a general quantum state
\rho
can be quantified by rugosity, which is defined as
Parisio2024

R_{\epsilon}(\rho)=-\ln\left[\bra{\omega}\rho\ket{\omega}\right],

(7)

with the subindex
\epsilon
denoting the basis.

Rugosity quantifies how distinguishable
\rho
is from the flat reference state. By construction,
R_{\epsilon}(\rho)\geq 0
, with equality if and only if
\rho=\ket{\omega}\bra{\omega}
. In contrast,
R_{\epsilon}(\rho)\rightarrow\infty
whenever
\rho
becomes orthogonal to
\ket{\omega}
. Therefore, large values of rugosity indicate that the state develops a highly nonuniform structure on the chosen basis
Parisio2024
.

The basis dependence of rugosity is essential for its physical interpretation. In the context of a DQPT (quench protocol), a natural choice is the eigenbasis of the pre-quench Hamiltonian,

H_{0}\ket{\epsilon_m}=\epsilon_{m}\ket{\epsilon_m},

(8)

for which the flat state corresponds to an equal superposition of all initial energy eigenstates. Rugosity then measures the extent to which the time-evolved state departs from a uniform distribution over the pre-quench spectrum. In particular, it quantifies how unevenly the quench dynamics populates the eigenstates of
H_{0}
.

However, at first sight, this choice appears unrelated to the return probability in this context. The initial state
\ket{\psi_0}
is the ground state of
H_{0}
, and is therefore highly localized on the basis of pre-quench eigenstates rather than flat. Consequently, the return probability defining the DQPT-II is not directly the overlap with the flat state defined above. However, as shown in the following, this mismatch can be removed by a suitable choice of basis. For any pure initial state, one can always construct a basis in which
\ket{\psi_0}
itself is the flat state. In that basis, the rugosity of the evolved state reduces exactly to minus the logarithm of the Loschmidt echo and therefore coincides, up to a constant, with the DQPT-II rate function. This establishes a direct and model-independent connection between rugosity and dynamical quantum criticality. Moreover, we also show that the physically motivated energy eigenbasis also marks dynamical criticality.

In the following section, we introduce the specific model employed in this work in order to make this connection explicit.

III
The model

Let us consider the Lipkin-Meshkov-Glick Hamiltonian
Lipkin1965
;
Meshkov1965
;
Glick1965
restricted to the fully symmetric sector,

H_{\mathrm{LMG}}=-hJ_{x}-\frac{\delta}{2j}J_{z}^{2},

(9)

where
h,\delta\geq 0
,
J_{\alpha}=(1/2)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{\alpha}^{(i)}
are collective spin operators, and
N=2j
. Restricting the dynamics to the symmetric subspace fixes the total spin to its maximum value
j=N/2
, reducing the many-body problem to the dynamics of a single collective spin of length
j
. Despite its simplicity, the model exhibits equilibrium
Lipkin1965
;
Meshkov1965
;
Glick1965
, excited-state
Corps2022
, and dynamical
Goes2020
quantum phase transitions.

In the ferromagnetic regime (
\delta>0
) the transverse field term tends to align the collective spin along the
x
-direction, whereas the interaction term favors a finite magnetization along
z
. In the thermodynamic limit (
j\to\infty
) this competition gives rise to an equilibrium quantum phase transition at the critical field

h_{c}^{\mathrm{QPT}}=\delta.

(10)

For
h>\delta
, the transverse field dominates and the ground state belongs to the symmetric phase, characterized by
\langle J_{z}\rangle=0
. By contrast, for
h<\delta
, the interaction term prevails, and the system enters a symmetry-broken phase with two degenerate minima carrying opposite magnetization,

\frac{\langle J_{z}\rangle}{j}=\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{h^{2}}{\delta^{2}}}.

(11)

The equilibrium transition therefore corresponds to the bifurcation of the ground-state minimum into two symmetry-related minima.

With regard to the excited-state quantum phase transition (ESQPT), its physical origin becomes particularly clear in the semiclassical limit. Introducing the canonical variables
(z,\phi)
, defined by
z=J_{z}/j
and
J_{x}=j\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\cos\phi
, the Hamiltonian density takes the form of

\mathcal{H}(z,\phi)=\frac{H_{\mathrm{LMG}}}{j}=-h\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\cos\phi-\frac{\delta}{2}z^{2}.

(12)

For
h<\delta
, the classical energy landscape has the structure of a double well. The two minima are located at

z=\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{h^{2}}{\delta^{2}}},\qquad\phi=0,

(13)

whereas the point
(z=0,\phi=0)
is no longer stable. Instead, it becomes a saddle point that separates the two wells. The energy of this saddle point plays a central role. Evaluating Eq. (
12
) at
(z,\phi)=(0,0)
, one obtains the critical energy

E_{c}^{\mathrm{ESQPT}}/j=-h.

(14)

Classically, this energy defines the separatrix that divides two qualitatively distinct families of trajectories. For energies below
E_{c}^{\mathrm{ESQPT}}
, the motion remains confined to one of the two wells, so the sign of
J_{z}
is preserved during evolution. For energies above the separatrix, the trajectories can cross between the two wells, dynamically restoring the symmetry. In the quantum problem, this change in the classical phase-space structure manifests itself as an excited-state quantum phase transition, signaled by singular behavior in the density of states and by a marked change in the structure of the excited eigenstates.

Let us now consider a sudden quench of the transverse field,
h_{0}\to h_{f}
, with the system initially prepared in the ground state of the ordered phase,
h_{0}<\delta
. After quenching, the state evolves under the final Hamiltonian
H(h_{f})
, but its energy with respect to this Hamiltonian is fixed by the initial condition. In the semiclassical limit, the injected energy is

E_{\mathrm{inj}}=-\frac{\delta}{2}-h_{f}\frac{h_{0}}{\delta}.

(15)

A dynamical quantum phase transition occurs when the injected energy coincides with the ESQPT separatrix energy of the post-quench Hamiltonian,

E_{\mathrm{inj}}=E_{c}^{\mathrm{ESQPT}}(h_{f})=-h_{f}.

(16)

Solving this condition yields the dynamical critical field

h_{f}^{\mathrm{DQPT}}=\frac{\delta+h_{0}}{2}.

(17)

The dynamical phase transition therefore admits a simple semiclassical interpretation: it occurs precisely when the quench injects enough energy for the system to reach the saddle point separating the two wells. Below this threshold, the evolution remains confined to a single symmetry-broken sector, and the long-time magnetization stays finite. Above it, the dynamics explore both wells, leading to dynamical symmetry restoration and a vanishing long-time magnetization.

These considerations are directly relevant for the interpretation of rugosity. As discussed above, a natural choice of basis in the context of a quench is the eigenbasis of the pre-quench Hamiltonian. In the LMG model, this basis is particularly meaningful because the DQPT is controlled by competition between the two semiclassical sectors associated with the double-well structure. The pre-quench basis resolves these sectors, while the ESQPT separatrix determines whether the post-quench dynamics remain localized in one well or explore both.

Consequently, the rugosity evaluated in the pre-quench basis is expected to be highly sensitive to the crossing of the ESQPT separatrix and, therefore, to the DQPT itself. Nevertheless, the state that initiates the dynamics is not the flat state
\ket{\omega}
, but rather the ground state
\ket{\psi_0}
of
H_{0}
. In general,
\ket{\psi_0}
is strongly localized in the pre-quench energy basis and already possesses a finite rugosity at
t=0
. The subsequent evolution under
H_{f}
redistributes both the populations and the phases on this basis, thereby modifying the rugosity over time.

The central question addressed in this work is whether this change in rugosity encodes dynamical critical behavior. In the following section, we answer this question in two complementary ways. First, within the framework of DQPT-I, we show that the time-averaged rugosity behaves as an order parameter for the transition. Second, within the framework of DQPT-II, we demonstrate that the rate function can be written directly in terms of rugosity when one adopts the basis in which the initial state is flat. Together, these results establish rugosity, which is a resource for quantum computation, as a fundamental quantity in the description of dynamical critical phenomena.

IV
Results

We first consider the type-I dynamical quantum phase transition, which is identified through the long-time behavior of the order parameter. The upper panel of Fig.
1
displays the infinite-time average of the magnetization along the
z
-direction. As expected, this quantity sharply distinguishes the two dynamical phases. For
h_{f}<h_{f}^{\mathrm{DQPT}}=1/2
, the long-term magnetization remains finite, indicating that the dynamics preserve the memory of the initial symmetry-broken state. By contrast, for
h_{f}>1/2
, the time-averaged magnetization vanishes, signaling that the evolution explores both sectors related to symmetry and dynamically restores the broken symmetry.

The lower panel of Fig.
1
shows the corresponding time average of the rugosity evaluated in the eigenbasis of the pre-quench Hamiltonian. A clear qualitative change is observed across the same critical field. As the quench approaches
h_{f}=1/2
from below, the average rugosity decreases, indicating that the time-evolved state spends an increasing fraction of its evolution closer to the flat state in the pre-quench basis. Physically, this reflects the fact that, near the dynamical critical point, the post-quench dynamics begin to explore a broader region of the pre-quench energy basis, leading to a more homogeneous redistribution of the wavefunction amplitudes.

However, once the critical field is crossed, the behavior changes abruptly. The time-averaged rugosity rapidly saturates to an approximately constant value. Although the instantaneous rugosity remains strongly oscillatory, the system now spends most of its time in highly textured states, that is, states whose support in the pre-quench basis is strongly nonuniform. This is consistent with the semiclassical picture discussed previously: above the critical quench, the injected energy exceeds the ESQPT separatrix, allowing the dynamics to explore both symmetry-broken sectors. The resulting interference between these sectors produces a persistent and highly structured distribution in the pre-quench basis. Therefore, the time-averaged rugosity in the pre-quench energy basis behaves as an order parameter for the type-I dynamical quantum phase transition.

It is worth emphasizing that the dynamical critical point does not induce rugosity. Rather, it controls the rate at which rugosity is generated and accumulated during evolution. Near the transition, the dynamics become especially efficient at redistributing the state over the pre-quench basis, thereby enhancing the sensitivity of rugosity to the underlying critical behavior. After the critical field is crossed, the dynamics rapidly drives the system toward highly textured states, thereby accelerating the generation of rugosity. In this regime, the state spends most of its evolution far from the flat reference state, so the creation of this quantum resource becomes faster and more efficient.

Let us now move to the case of DQPT-II. We first note that, in principle, there is no apparent relation between rugosity and the rate function, since they measure distinct things. Importantly, the rate function is independent of the basis, whereas rugosity is not: rugosity quantifies the distance between the instantaneous state and a flat state defined in a given basis. It is not always the case that the ground state of the initial Hamiltonian coincides with the reference uniform state on an arbitrary basis.

However, in the case of pure states, it is always possible to find a basis for which the initial ground state is the flat state. To this end, one needs to find a unitary transformation such that
U\ket{\omega}=\ket{\psi_0}
, which implies that

\ket{\psi_0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i}\ket{\phi_i},\hskip 5.69046pt\ket{\phi_k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{m}e^{-im\phi_{k}}\ket{\epsilon_m},

(18)

with
\phi_{k}=2k\pi/d
. On this basis, we have the following

\lambda_{t}=\frac{1}{N}R_{\phi}(\rho_{t}),

(19)

which implies that the rate function is the density of rugosity. Therefore, we conclude that there is always a choice of a basis for which the DQPT-II is captured by the rugosity. The basis is defined by the initial state of the dynamics. In the case of the LMG model with
h_{0}=0
, this is just the eigenbasis of the conjugate observable to
J_{z}
.

We emphasize that the equivalence between rugosity and the rate function on this special basis is exact in the thermodynamic limit
N\rightarrow\infty
. For finite systems, in general, the non-analytic behavior of the rate function reveals itself in the ground state manifold, and therefore involves multiple degenerate ground-states.

The system starts with
R_{\phi}(\rho_{0})=0
, since the initial state is the flat state. After the quench, the system evolves, and rugosity can be created. When the system is quenched at the dynamical critical point, the rate function will diverge at the critical times, where the evolved state is orthogonal to the ground state manifold and thus
R_{\phi}\rightarrow\infty
at the critical times. Consider the case of the LMG model with a quench
h_{0}=0\rightarrow h_{f}
. The initial state of the system can be chosen arbitrarily among all the states in the doubly degenerate ground state manifold. We then choose the state
\ket{\psi_0}=\ket{\epsilon_m = j}
, which means that the system is fully polarized in the
z
direction. The
\{\ket{\phi_k}\}
is the eigenbasis of the conjugate operator to
J_{z}
.

Therefore, given the pre-quench Hamiltonian, its associated ground state will be flat in the Fourier-transformed basis, independent of the model and the details of the quench. The rugosity density computed with respect to this basis is the rate function associated with the DQPT.

As we saw, the flat basis perfectly marks the DQPT. However, we are particularly interested in the dynamics of rugosity with respect to the eigenbasis of the pre-quench Hamiltonian, since this is deeply linked to the ESQPT and, as a consequence, to the DQPT, as discussed before. Therefore, we start by computing the rugosity with respect to this basis for two distinct quenches, one crossing, and the other one not crossing the critical point. The result, along with the comparison with the rate function, is shown in Fig.
3
. Although these quantities are not equal, there is a clear signature of the dynamical quantum phase transition in the rugosity density with respect to the pre-quench Hamilonian eigenbasis. This is reflected in the fast decay of the rugosity amplitude over time, the same behavior as presented by the rate function. This is in agreement with our intuition, as discussed earlier. Therefore, we can safely say that the energy-based rugosity can sign dynamical criticality, even if the initial state is not flat in this basis, and therefore the rate function does not need to be equal to the rugosity density.

V
Discussion

The results presented here place rugosity within a broader family of quantities that have recently been shown to diagnose dynamical quantum phase transitions. Previous studies of the LMG model have established that critical quench is accompanied by an increased spread of the state over Hilbert space, reflected in the behavior of Krylov complexity, inverse participation ratios, Shannon entropy
Bento2024
, thermodynamic entropy
Goes2020
;
Nascimento2024
, and asymmetry measures
Nascimento2025
. In all cases, the DQPT separates a regime in which the dynamics remain partially localized around the initial symmetry-broken sector from a regime in which the post-quench evolution explores both symmetry-related sectors and therefore a much larger portion of the available Hilbert space. The present work shows that rugosity captures the same transition, but from a fundamentally different perspective: rather than quantifying only spreading or delocalization, rugosity measures the generation of a basis-dependent quantum resource.

The connection with the recent study of the Krylov complexity is particularly interesting. In that case, the time-averaged Krylov complexity and the associated inverse participation ratio become sharply enhanced at the DQPT, signaling that the state populates an increasingly large number of basis states
Bento2024
. The same physical mechanism is responsible for the behavior of rugosity in the pre-quench energy basis. Near the critical field, the post-quench dynamics broadens the support of the state over the eigenbasis of the initial Hamiltonian, reducing the distance to the flat state and, therefore, lowering the average rugosity. Above the transition, however, the dynamics no longer merely broadens the distribution: the interference between the two semiclassical sectors generates a highly structured and strongly nonuniform pattern in the pre-quench basis, leading to the rapid saturation of a large rugosity. In this sense, the DQPT does not simply create a larger support in the Hilbert space but organizes this support into a highly textured structure. Rugosity therefore refines the information provided by complexity measures: while complexity quantifies how much of Hilbert space is explored, rugosity quantifies how far the explored state is from the flat reference configuration.

A similar interpretation emerges when comparing our results with the studies of Wehrl
Goes2020
and thermodynamic
Nascimento2024
entropy production in the DQPT. These works showed that the critical quench is accompanied by a strong increase in both the thermodynamic entropy and the entropy of the Husimi distribution, which reflects enhanced spreading and mixing in the semiclassical phase space. In particular, the entropy production rate is maximal near the dynamical critical point, indicating that the DQPT acts as an especially efficient mechanism to generate disorder. The present results reveal the complementary side of this process. The same critical dynamics that maximizes entropy production also maximizes the rate at which rugosity is generated. Therefore, DQPT simultaneously produces two apparently opposite effects: it increases disorder, as quantified by entropy, while at the same time creating a highly structured quantum resource. From this perspective, rugosity may be regarded as the useful byproduct of the strong redistribution of amplitudes induced by the critical quench.

An asymmetry measure that quantifies the amount of coherence between different symmetry sectors was shown to clearly signs of DQPT
Nascimento2025
. In the LMG model, the dynamical transition corresponds to the point at which the evolution begins to connect the two symmetry-broken wells separated by the ESQPT separatrix. Consequently, the post-quench state acquires a much larger coherence between the sectors associated with opposite magnetizations. Since rugosity is also basis dependent, it is naturally sensitive to this redistribution of coherence. However, while asymmetry measures the coherence itself, rugosity measures the distance from the flat state on the basis selected by the relevant observable. The two quantities therefore provide complementary descriptions of the same physical phenomenon: asymmetry characterizes the coherence created by the crossing of the separatrix, whereas rugosity quantifies how this coherence reorganizes the probability distribution in the chosen basis.

Together, these results suggest a unified physical picture. Dynamical criticality acts as an efficient converter of coherent energy injected by the quench into several distinct manifestations: enhanced complexity, larger entropy production, stronger asymmetry, and, as shown here, the generation of rugosity. Complexity and entropy quantify the cost of the critical dynamics, namely the increased spreading and apparent irreversibility of the evolution. Rugosity, on the contrary, quantifies the useful structure produced by this same process. In this sense, the DQPT may be viewed not only as a singular nonequilibrium phenomenon but also as a mechanism for the dynamical generation of a quantum computational resource, at the cost of entropy and complexity.

VI
Conclusions

In this work, we established a direct connection between dynamical quantum phase transitions and the notion of rugosity as a measure of quantum state texture. Two complementary types of DQPTs were analyzed. For type-I DQPTs, defined through the long-time behavior of an order parameter, we demonstrated that the time-averaged rugosity evaluated in the eigenbasis of the pre-quench Hamiltonian behaves itself as an order parameter, sharply distinguishing the dynamical phases. This behavior is rooted in the semiclassical structure of the Lipkin-Meshkov-Glick (LMG) model, where the crossing of the ESQPT separatrix controls the redistribution of amplitudes in the pre-quench basis. We conjecture that this should also be the case for any model with the underlying semiclassical structure. For type-II DQPTs, characterized by nonanalyticities in the rate function, we showed that rugosity acquires an even more fundamental role. For a suitable choice of basis, the rate function becomes exactly the density of rugosity, establishing a model-independent equivalence in the thermodynamic limit. Together, these results show that rugosity not only signals dynamical criticality but also provides a unifying framework connecting both notions of DQPT .

Beyond the specific model analyzed, our results place rugosity within a broader landscape of quantities used to characterize nonequilibrium quantum dynamics. In particular, the Krylov complexity, inverse participation ratios, and entropy production, all of which capture different aspects of Hilbert-space exploration during the dynamics following a critical quench. While complexity and entropy quantify the spreading and apparent irreversibility of the dynamics, rugosity provides a complementary perspective by quantifying the emergence of structured, basis-dependent quantum resources. In this sense, DQPTs appear as efficient mechanisms for converting the coherent energy injected by a quench into disorder (as captured by entropy production) and useful structure (as captured by rugosity). This dual role suggests that rugosity may serve as a bridge between quantum information-theoretic approaches and thermodynamic descriptions of nonequilibrium dynamics.

Several open questions naturally arise from this work. A central issue concerns the extension of these results to systems that do not possess all-to-all interactions or a clear semiclassical limit. In the LMG model, the existence of a well-defined classical phase space with a double-well structure provides an intuitive understanding of the ESQPT and its role as the trigger for the DQPT. In contrast, models such as the transverse-field Ising chain lack this simple semiclassical picture, raising the question of what replaces the geometric mechanism underlying the transition. In such systems, although DQPT-II can still be identified and the equality between the rate function and the rugosity density can be established for an appropriate basis, it remains unclear whether there exists a physically privileged basis, analogous to the pre-quench energy basis in the LMG model, that captures the transition in a similarly transparent way. This also raises the question of whether rugosity, in these more general settings, can still be interpreted as an order parameter for DQPT-I, or whether its role becomes more subtle.

The strong basis dependence of rugosity suggests exploring a broader class of connections between different choices of observables and their associated notions of texture. Although we have shown that there always exists a basis in which rugosity exactly reproduces the rate function, other physically motivated bases may encode distinct aspects of the dynamics, potentially revealing new aspects of the critical behavior. This opens the possibility of defining families of rugosity landscapes associated with different operator algebras or measurement schemes, and of investigating how these are related to concepts such as quantum chaos, information scrambling, eigenstate thermalization and changes of symmetries. Another important direction concerns the role of rugosity in mixed states, open quantum systems, and finite-temperature settings, where the interplay between decoherence, entropy production, and resource generation may lead to new phenomena. Addressing these questions will be essential for establishing rugosity as a general tool in the study of nonequilibrium quantum systems.
