Subdural empyema in a 14-year-old female initially diagnosed as meningitis: a case report of a rare Streptococcus constellatus infection
Authors: Mohammad Abu Thiab, Ahmad Rjoub, Belal Mohamed, Shailendra Magdum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Subdural empyema (SDE) is an uncommon, potentially life-threatening intracranial infection that can present with meningitis-like features, causing a diagnostic challenge. Streptococcus constellatus, a member of the Streptococcus anginosus group, is an unusual cause of central nervous system infections in adolescents, but is well known for its propensity to form abscesses.Case presentation:We report a previously healthy 14-year-old female who presented with acute severe headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, and vomiting following an upper respiratory illness with foul-smelling nasal discharge. Laboratory testing showed marked systemic inflammation (C-reactive protein >320 mg/L) and neutrophilia. Head CT demonstrated a right-sided 6-mm subdural collection with midline shift; magnetic resonance imaging confirmed a SDE with leptomeningeal enhancement and restricted diffusion. She underwent endoscopic sinus surgery and bilateral craniotomy for evacuation of the empyema with intracranial pressure monitoring; intraoperative cultures from the purulent collection grew S. constellatus. Management included targeted intravenous antibiotics and early neurorehabilitation. The patient exhibited steady improvement, as evidenced by clinical and laboratory findings.Conclusion:This case highlights that SDE can mimic meningitis in adolescents and underscores the importance of suspecting SDE in the presence of focal neurological deficits in addition to meningeal signs. Early neuroimaging, prompt combined surgical source control (including sinus drainage), and culture-directed antimicrobial therapy are essential for favorable outcomes. Clinicians should consider S. constellatus as a possible pathogen in sinus-associated intracranial empyema.

Introduction

Subdural empyema (SDE) is an uncommon intracranial pyogenic infection characterized by a purulent collection between the dura mater and arachnoid mater. It is a rare condition, with sparse data suggesting an annual incidence of subdural and extradural empyema of 5.3–8.79 per million children[1,2]. In children and adolescents, SDE often arises as a complication of sinusitis or otitis media[3,4]. Clinically, its presentation can overlap with that of meningitis, including symptoms such as fever, headache, vomiting, neck stiffness, and altered mental status, making early diagnosis particularly challenging[5–7]. It constitutes a neurosurgical emergency due to its rapid progression and the potential for severe neurological sequelae[3] Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is crucial for diagnosing SDE and distinguishing it from other intracranial infections[6]

The most common causative organisms in SDE include Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, and members of the Streptococcus milleri (anginosus) group[3–7]. Within this group, Streptococcus constellatus is an unusual pathogen in intracranial infections, typically associated with abscess formation in other anatomical sites, such as the oral cavity, lungs, and abdomen[6,7]. The current literature does not provide specific numerical data on pediatric SDE attributable to this species. In most large case series, S. constellatus is included within the S. milleri (anginosus) group and is not reported separately[1,8]. We present a rare case of SDE caused by S. constellatus in a 14-year-old female who was initially misdiagnosed with meningitis. This case underscores the importance of early neuroimaging in pediatric patients with neurological symptoms and a suspected central nervous system infection, especially when clinical signs evolve rapidly or do not correspond to the initial working diagnosis.

Case presentation

A 14-year-old female patient presented to the emergency department (ED) with a one-day history of severe headache, associated with a low-grade fever measured at home that responded to over-the-counter paracetamol (500 mg). The pain began in the right jaw 4 days before the ED visit and felt as if her jaw was locking, limiting her ability to eat and preventing her from sleeping. Then, on the day of presentation to the ED, she felt the pain all over her head, but more severe behind her eyes, and it was progressively worsening. Her pain was associated with neck stiffness and photophobia without phonophobia. She reported no history of abdominal pain, diarrhea, or urinary symptoms, but she had vomited twice 2 hours before her presentation to the ED.

Additionally, she reported numbness and a sensation of heaviness in her left leg. However, no history of recent trauma was reported. She reported having a sore throat, a feverish sensation, and a runny nose, with a clear but foul-smelling discharge that started a week before her visit to the ED, which occurred upon her return from a 10-day family trip to Turkey. Her past medical, surgical, family, and social histories were unremarkable. Her vaccinations were up to date, and she had no known food or drug allergies.

On examination in the ED, the patient appeared ill but conscious, alert, and oriented. She was hemodynamically stable, with normal oxygen saturation. Neurologically, she had a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 15/15, but she was unable to hold a conversation due to pain. She had mild neck rigidity and could not tolerate the room light. Neurological examination demonstrated positive Kernig’s and Brudzinski’s signs, mild neck rigidity, and subtle left-sided hyperreflexia (+2). Otolaryngological (ENT) examination demonstrated foul-smelling nasal discharge and restricted jaw opening due to pain.

Investigations

Given the patient’s history and clinical presentation, blood samples were collected for a full blood count, serum electrolytes (mainly sodium and potassium), liver enzymes, liver function tests, kidney function tests, C-reactive protein (CRP), venous blood gases (VBGs), and serum lactate. Laboratory investigations demonstrated marked systemic inflammation with a CRP >320 mg/L, neutrophilia (9.7 × 109/L), and elevated lactate (2.8 mmol/L). Blood cultures were obtained. Respiratory viral testing and meningococcal/pneumococcal polymerase chain reaction assays were negative. Diagnostic assessment and initial management

During reassessment and while awaiting laboratory results, the patient vomited three times and complained of worsening headache, severe photophobia, and severe neck stiffness. The initial clinical presentation was highly suggestive of meningitis, given the presence of headache, photophobia, neck stiffness, vomiting, and positive meningeal signs. Nevertheless, the rapid progression of symptoms, development of focal left-leg weakness, and significant systemic inflammation prompted consideration of an intracranial suppurative complication, warranting urgent neuroimaging. Other differential diagnoses included migraine, meningitis, meningoencephalitis, non-traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), brain abscess, and brain tumor. Initial management started with intravenous (IV) paracetamol (1 g) every 6 hours, ibuprofen PO (400 mg STAT) for analgesia, intravenous fluids (IVFs) with 0.9% normal saline (83 mL/h), ondansetron IV (4 mg) for vomiting, and ceftriaxone IV (2 g) as empirical antibiotic therapy. A chest X-ray was done and showed bilateral streaky changes, suggesting an atypical chest infection (Fig. 1). Consultations were held with the pediatric team, which recommended a head computed tomography (CT) scan with contrast due to the rapid worsening of the patient’s symptoms and a high suspicion of intracranial lesions. Following the imaging, the patient was admitted to the pediatric ward at the same hospital. The next morning, following admission to the pediatric ward, a head CT scan with IV contrast was performed, which showed a 6-mm subdural collection on the right lateral aspect of the right frontal lobe along the right falx cerebri. Additionally, a mild midline shift toward the left was noted (Fig. 2). Complete opacification of the right maxillary sinus and the right side of the frontal sinus was noted, without bony hyperostosis or evidence of intracranial involvement, and the posterior part of the frontal sinus was intact, findings suggestive of bacterial sinusitis. Given the clinical presentation, which was highly suggestive of meningitis, the CT scan results raised suspicion for SDE. Upon reassessment by a pediatric consultant following the CT scan, the patient was doing well, with modest improvement. The pediatric consultant incorporated the following medications into the management plan: azithromycin PO 500 mg once daily, administered empirically for a potential atypical respiratory infection, as indicated by bilateral streaky infiltrates on chest radiography; IV acyclovir 550 mg (10 mg/kg) once daily, to provide empirical antiviral coverage for possible viral encephalitis during the ongoing diagnostic evaluation; and dexamethasone IV 50 mg as a loading dose, followed by 12.5 mg every 6 hours, due to the continued consideration of bacterial meningitis as a major differential diagnosis and the presence of significant cerebral mass effect with midline shift on neuroimaging[3]. Following treatment initiation, the patient showed modest symptomatic improvement but continued to experience headache, photophobia, and neck stiffness. Additional consultations were held with the pediatric neurosurgery team, who recommended performing an urgent head magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan with contrast and a diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) sequence, and transferring the patient to the pediatric neurosurgery department. Given the cortical involvement and elevated risk of acute symptomatic seizures in patients with SDE, a 40 mg/kg loading dose of prophylactic levetiracetam (2200 mg) was administered[3].

A head MRI with contrast was performed the same day and showed a 6-mm subdural collection surrounding the right cerebral hemisphere, with slightly increased T2 signal and restricted diffusion. The midline shift to the left (approximately 8 mm) was again demonstrated. Leptomeningeal enhancement and thickening were noted overlying the right frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes. These MRI findings were highly suggestive of intracranial infection with SDE and associated leptomeningeal enhancement and midline shift, indicating raised intracranial pressure, possibly due to mass effect (Fig. 3). The patient and her parents were updated on her clinical situation and the proposed management plan. The patient was then transferred to the paediatric neurosurgery department at the John Radcliffe Hospital, Oxford University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust. Admission to the pediatric neurosurgery department and operation

The day after admission to the pediatric neurosurgery department, the patient underwent functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS) for right-sided sinusitis involving the maxillary, anterior ethmoid, and frontal sinuses. Intraoperative findings showed an obstructed ostiomeatal complex and pus filling the right maxillary sinus. The anterior ethmoid sinuses were clear of disease, and there was no pus from the frontal recess. Within the same operation, the patient also underwent a bilateral fronto-temporo-parietal craniotomy for surgical evacuation of the SDE, draining the pus collection from the convexity over the right hemisphere and extending to the interhemispheric fissure. Pus was suctioned, and swabs were sent for culture. A copious saline washout was performed. Moreover, an intracranial pressure (ICP) monitor was placed to monitor intracranial pressure, given the significant midline shift and concern for postoperative intracranial hypertension.

Postoperative course

The patient was then transferred to the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) postoperatively and remained intubated. Samples were taken from the evacuated fluid and sent for cultures. She was sedated with midazolam and morphine infusions. She required a 0.03% noradrenaline infusion for blood pressure support. Postoperative head CT with IV contrast was performed to assess for empyema recurrence and guide further management. The CT scan showed no evidence of a new enhancing empyema, with a very shallow, low-attenuation collection along the right tentorium and posterior falx. Mild bilateral extra-axial hemorrhage with postoperative SAH in the left cingulate gyrus was also noted. The right maxillary sinus, ethmoid air cells, and frontal sinuses were obscured by dense secretions (Fig. 4). The results of the blood cultures obtained on the day of presentation were negative at 2 and 5 days of incubation. Three cultures were obtained from the evacuated fluid sample. The results showed growth of S. constellatus in all cultures; the isolates were sensitive to all tested antibiotics, particularly penicillins and cephalosporins. One culture showed mixed growth of S. constellatus and methicillin-sensitive S. aureus (MSSA), likely due to skin contamination. Finally, bacterial sinusitis was strongly suspected as the source of the meningitis and subsequent SDE. Management with ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg (up to 4 g) IV OD and metronidazole 7.5 mg/kg (up to 500 mg) TDS IV was continued to cover S. constellatus and other anaerobes[3]. The patient tolerated the treatment without signs or symptoms of an allergic reaction.

The patient was managed in the PICU with gradual clinical improvement, allowing discontinuation of noradrenaline by postoperative day 2 as inflammatory markers declined. By postoperative day 3, she developed a fever with an elevated white blood cell count, and left-sided weakness became apparent as sedation was weaned, prompting a request for a follow-up brain MRI. Antibiotic therapy was continued throughout this period.

Four days postoperatively, the ICP monitor was removed under aseptic conditions, with a final reading of 11 mmHg, to permit follow-up MRI of the brain. MRI (including MRA and MRV) showed no significant change compared with the postoperative CT, with a shallow residual right occipital empyema, diffuse dural thickening, and a focal area of T2/FLAIR hyperintensity with restricted diffusion in the right precentral gyrus, suggestive of focal ischemia; no large vessel occlusion or venous sinus thrombosis was identified (Fig. 5). The patient was re-sedated and remained intubated overnight after imaging to allow intermittent neurological assessment and was successfully extubated on postoperative day 5. Following surgical evacuation, the patient demonstrated steady neurological and clinical recovery. She was successfully extubated with a GCS score of 11/15 and stepped down from PICU care. However, left hemiparesis was noted, prompting initiation of neurorehabilitation, which she engaged in and with which she gradually improved. She required a unit of packed red blood cells for postoperative anemia but remained hemodynamically stable and afebrile, with progressive reduction of inflammatory markers. Serial MRI scans showed stable postoperative changes, a small residual empyema, leptomeningeal enhancement consistent with meningitis, and a tiny superficial abscess, all of which were managed conservatively with continued IV antibiotics. By discharge, her neurological examination had significantly improved, with full limb power (mild left-sided fatigue only), and she was stable on ceftriaxone and levetiracetam. Dalteparin prophylaxis was prescribed because of recent neurosurgery, transient hemiparesis, and reduced mobility during recovery. Subsequent outpatient follow-up with weekly blood tests and a repeat MRI demonstrated continued clinical and radiological improvement, culminating in a normal MRI and complete neurological recovery by mid-January. Discussion

SDE is a rapidly progressive intracranial infection that remains a neurosurgical emergency, particularly in the pediatric population[3,6]. Its clinical presentation often overlaps with that of meningitis, leading to potential delays in diagnosis, as occurred in our patient, who initially presented with classical meningeal signs, including headache, photophobia, neck stiffness, and positive meningeal examination findings. The rapid evolution of symptoms, focal neurological complaint (left leg heaviness), and a severe systemic inflammatory response prompted early neuroimaging, which proved to be essential in differentiating SDE from meningitis and guiding management. An instructive aspect of this case was the presence of focal neurological symptoms at presentation, despite an otherwise typical meningitic syndrome. The report of unilateral leg heaviness served as an early indicator of intracranial focal pathology and led to prompt neuroimaging. These findings indicate that focal neurological symptoms in children with suspected meningitis should prompt early assessment for intracranial suppurative complications, even when classical meningeal signs are predominant[4,6]. Early recognition and intervention are crucial for favorable outcomes[3].

S. constellatus, part of the S. anginosus group (SAG), is an opportunistic organism increasingly recognized for its pyogenic potential and tendency to form deep-seated abscesses[9]. Although the exact mechanism remains undefined, many virulence factors of this group have been reported in the literature, including adhesins, a polysaccharide capsule, hydrolytic enzymes, cytolysins, and superantigen genes[9]. This biological behavior may explain its association with sinus-related intracranial suppuration and supports early microbiological confirmation when cultures are available. It was identified as the causative organism from intraoperative cultures. Although SAG organisms are known for their propensity to form abscesses, S. constellatus remains an uncommon cause of intracranial empyema in adolescents.

To contextualize the rarity and clinical significance of this case, previously reported pediatric cases of SDE associated with S. constellatus were reviewed (Table 3). The available literature is limited and consists mainly of isolated case reports[5,7,10–13]. Most infections originated from sinogenic sources, although odontogenic spread has also been documented. Clinical presentations often included orbital manifestations, focal neurological deficits, seizures, altered consciousness, or hemiparesis, indicating advanced intracranial involvement at diagnosis[3,5,7,10–13]. In contrast, the present patient initially exhibited a syndrome highly suggestive of acute meningitis, with headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, vomiting, and positive meningeal signs but only subtle focal neurological symptoms, specifically, left leg heaviness. As in previously reported cases, effective management required prompt neurosurgical drainage and prolonged IV antimicrobial therapy, with some patients necessitating repeat surgical intervention[7,10–13]. While outcomes were generally favorable with timely diagnosis and source control, severe complications and mortality have been reported[5,10]. These findings highlight the aggressive suppurative potential of S. constellatus and emphasize the need for a high index of suspicion for intracranial empyema in children presenting with meningitis-like symptoms, especially when accompanied by focal neurological findings or underlying sinonasal infection. The suspected source in this case was acute bacterial sinusitis, supported by imaging demonstrating opacification of the right maxillary and frontal sinuses and intraoperative findings of pus obstructing the ostiomeatal complex. Sinus-originated SDE is a well-recognized pathophysiological pathway, particularly in older children and adolescents[3,4,6,7]. However, infections due to S. constellatus are less commonly reported in this context, underscoring the importance of microbiological confirmation to optimize targeted antimicrobial therapy.

The progression to mass effect and midline shift, as evidenced on MRI, underscored the urgency of combined neurosurgical and ENT intervention. Prompt surgical evacuation via craniotomy, along with endoscopic sinus drainage, resulted in immediate decompression and source control, consistent with best practice recommendations for SDE[4,14–16]. Postoperatively, the patient showed transient neurological deficits, including left hemiparesis and radiological evidence of focal ischemia, which likely arose from a combination of inflammatory involvement of the cerebral convexity, raised intracranial pressure, and local venous congestion. These complications are well-documented sequelae of SDE and highlight the need for close neurological monitoring and repeated neuroimaging[4,16].

Blood cultures remained negative, which is not uncommon in intracranial infections due to prior antibiotic administration or localized infection; however, operative cultures were positive, enabling tailored antimicrobial therapy. The organism’s sensitivity profile allowed continuation of ceftriaxone, supplemented with metronidazole to provide anaerobic coverage. The patient demonstrated steady clinical and radiological improvement with prolonged IV antibiotic therapy and early neurorehabilitation, culminating in full neurological recovery – a favorable outcome despite the severity of the initial presentation[4].

The single-case design of this report limits the generalizability of its findings. However, the case offers clinically relevant insights into the early recognition and management of pediatric sinogenic SDE caused by a rare pathogen. In conclusion, this case illustrates that SDE may initially manifest as apparent meningitis, with early focal neurological symptoms potentially serving as the first indication of an underlying intracranial suppurative process. Prompt recognition of these warning signs, together with timely neuroimaging, urgent surgical intervention, and culture-directed antimicrobial therapy, can lead to favorable neurological outcomes. Clinicians should maintain vigilance for S. constellatus and other S. anginosus group organisms, given their high propensity for abscess formation.
