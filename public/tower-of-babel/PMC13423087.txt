Cancer-associated mesothelial cells drive immune escape and therapy resistance in ovarian cancer
Authors: Maeva Chauvin, Julien Roche-Prellezo, Virginie Lafont, Henri-Alexandre Michaud, Estelle Tromelin, Robin Michel, Clara Freixinos, Marie-Charlotte Meinsohn, Pierre-Emmanuel Colombo, Nathalie Bonnefoy, Laurent Gros, David Pépin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer-associated mesothelial cells (CAMCs) are key modulators of the ovarian tumor microenvironment, contributing to tumor growth and immune evasion. Mesothelial cells (MCs) maintain peritoneal homeostasis and immune surveillance and represent the first point of contact during abdominal dissemination of ovarian cancers. Yet, their role in ovarian tumor immunity remains poorly understood.MethodsLineage tracing, 3D models, and spatial transcriptomic profiling were used to characterize CAMC origin, localization, and phenotypic transitions during ovarian cancer progression. Multiplex cytokine panels were used to define the cytokine profiles associated with MC transformation into CAMCs. Functional studies were conducted in syngeneic ovarian cancer mouse models to assess the impact of CAMCs on tumor growth and response to immunotherapy. In parallel, CAMC-driven changes in immune cell phenotype and functional state within the tumor microenvironment were characterized.ResultsWe demonstrate that CAMCs originate from peritoneal MCs, populate the tumor surface, and progressively infiltrate the tumor core while undergoing a phenotypic transition toward a fibroblast-like phenotype. We characterize the function of an unrecognized CAMC signature marked by SERPINB2+ expression and a combination of markers absent in normal MCs. CAMCSerpinb2+ cells have reduced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-2, IL-7, IL-12, IL-15) and increased expression of IL-10, TGFβ1, and CCL17 compared with normal MCs. Functionally, the presence of CAMCSerpinb2+ cells correlates with accelerated tumor growth, reduced CD4+ T and B cell infiltration, an expanded Treg population, and ultimately resistance to combination immunotherapy in a syngeneic mouse model of ovarian cancer.ConclusionsThese findings identify CAMCs as central regulators of immune suppression in ovarian cancer and reveal a distinct SERPINB2+ immunosuppressive CAMC state associated with tumor progression and immunotherapy resistance. Targeting CAMCs may represent a promising therapeutic strategy to restore antitumor immunity and improve responses to current ovarian cancer treatments and immunotherapies.

Introduction

High-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) remains one of the most lethal of the gynecological malignancies, which is characterized by late-stage diagnosis, rapid progression, and results in 70% recurrence within 2 years.1–3 Despite advancements in targeted therapies and immunotherapies, the intricate interplay between cancer cells and the tumor microenvironment (TME) continues to pose significant challenges in the effective treatment of HGSOC. Among the various components of the TME, cancer-associated mesothelial cells (CAMCs) have emerged as pivotal players, influencing tumor behavior and immune responses.4 5

Mesothelial cells (MCs), which line the peritoneal cavity, have long been recognized for their role in maintaining serosal integrity and facilitating tissue homeostasis.6–8 MCs are highly plastic cells capable of undergoing mesothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (MMT) and differentiating into fibroblast-like cells during physiological processes such as wound healing and tissue repair.7 In pathological settings, including fibrosis and cancer, this plasticity is further exploited, enabling MCs to acquire stromal and cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF)-like phenotypes.9–12 During metastasis to the omentum, cancer cells are thought to attach to the mesothelium and invade the omental tissue and in that process, reprogram MCs into CAMCs that integrate into the tumor stroma and promote cancer growth.5 10 13–16 As tumor progression advances, MCs undergo significant alterations, acquiring characteristics associated with tumor promotion and immune evasion.4 CAMCs have been described as regulating many cell types of the TME, including macrophages and CAFs, by secreting growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines.10 17 Their immunomodulatory function is reminiscent of the role of normal peritoneal mesothelium in protecting peritoneal organs by producing serosal fluids and surveying the cavity for pathogens to activate innate and adaptive immune responses at the serosal surface in case of infection.6 18 MCs express multiple cytokines, antimicrobial peptides, and growth factors that, in turn, regulate the proliferation and differentiation of immune cells, including T cells.19 Similarly, in tumors, the secretion of TGFβ by ovarian cancer cells induces the production of cytokines by CAMCs, which, in turn, regulate cytotoxic T lymphocytes.20 This paracrine effect can modulate response to anti-PD-1 therapy and contribute to resistance to immunotherapies, particularly in ovarian cancer.6 21 22 Yet the role of CAMCs in regulating growth and immunosuppression in ovarian cancer remains poorly understood.

In this study, we characterized CAMCs subtypes and cell states present in TME of ovarian cancer and began to explore their underlying function, including a potential immunosuppressive phenotype that may contribute to immune evasion. We speculate that therapeutic strategies eliminating or reprogramming CAMCs could restore a more immunogenic TME and a better response to immunotherapy.

Results

Tumor stroma contains different subtypes of CAMCs in a syngeneic mouse model of ovarian cancer

To define the gene signature of CAMCs in the TME, we analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets from omental tumors of a syngeneic ovarian cancer mouse model (KPCA, GSE233423).23 Briefly, this transcriptome was derived from applying the 10X scRNA-seq pipeline to dissociated omental metastases generated by grafting the KPCA cell line (containing KRAS, PTEN, CCNE1, AKT2 alterations to mimic human CCNE1-amplified HGSOC) into the peritoneal cavity of syngeneic C57BL/6 mice as previously described16 23 (figure 2A). CAMCs were identified by expression of the classical mesothelial markers Upk3b and Lrrn4,16 24 25 and were distinguishable from CAFs, which expressed classical fibroblast markers such as Acta2 and Tgln26 (online supplemental figure 1A). Subclustering analysis of the CAMCs in the mouse tumors revealed three subsets of CAMCs: (1) a proliferative CAMC cluster marked by mitotic genes such as Top2a, Ccne2, Prc1, and mKi67,27 and two clusters of CAMC with distinct transcriptional signatures, defined in part by Serpinb2 marker expression as (2) CAMCserpinb2−, and (3) CAMCserpinb2+ (figure 1A,B). Indeed, when we examined the top 10 highly expressed genes of CAMC Serpinb2+ cells (Serpinb2, Ero1l, Asns, Adm2, Aldh1l2, Mthfd2, Phgdh, Vegfa, Amhr2) across all cell populations in the TME, we found that Serpinb2 was mostly absent in other cell types, and enriched in this subpopulation of CAMCs (figure 2E). The CAMCserpinb2+ gene expression profile revealed by scRNA-seq was significantly enriched for gene ontology terms related to metabolism, such as the L-serine biosynthetic process (online supplemental figure 1C). The CAMCSerpinb2− cluster was significantly enriched for gene ontology terms related to positive regulation of type 2 immune response and antigen presentation via MHCII and associated with a high level of MHC-II subunit expression (H2-Aa, H2-Eb1, H2-Ab1, Cd74) (figure 2B, online supplemental figure 1A). We hypothesize that all three CAMC populations arose from normal peritoneal MCs (Upk3b+/Lrrn4+) responding to the cancer microenvironment. These populations and their differential gene expression are highlighted in a heatmap (online supplemental figure 1A) and dot plot (figure 2B).

The CAMCSerpinb2+ transcriptional signature is unique to the tumor context

To characterize the pathogenic states of CAMC signatures within the TME, we aimed to contrast their transcriptomic profiles with those of normal MCs from mouse omenta. We took advantage of scRNA-seq datasets (GSE134355)28 that included cell populations from healthy mice omenta (n=4) (figure 2C). Clustering of MCs (Upk3b+/Lrrn4+) revealed a distinct subset enriched in CXCL/CCL chemokines (Cytokine-MCs: Ccl2, Cxcl1/2) (figure 1C,D), potentially indicative of an activated immune state.6 We then examined the proportion of cells in normal omental MCs expressing genes specifically associated with the CAMCSerpinb2− and CAMCSerpinb2+ (Log2FC>0.5) pathogenic states found in the TME (figure 2F). Upk3b, Lrrn4, Gpm6a, Nkain4, Msln, and MHC-II subunits (H2-Ab, H2-Ab1, H2-Eb1, Cd74) were detected in both normal MCs and pathogenic CAMCSerpinb2− and CAMCSerpinb2+ populations. However, MHC-II subunit expression was significantly enriched in all CAMC states, suggesting a heightened antigen-presenting function (figure 2F). Interestingly, markers of CAMCSerpinb2+ cells (Serpinb2, Adm2, Ndufa4l2, Asns, Aldh1l2, Mthfd2, Phgdh, Vegfa, Amhr2) were unique to the tumor context. Based on this gene expression data, we decided to further investigate the immune functions of CAMCs, and particularly the role CAMCSerpinb2+ on tumor immunity.

We next investigated whether CAMC subtype signatures were conserved in human HGSOC tumors. Analysis of the untreated patient with HGSOC dataset (GSE147082, n=6)29 revealed a similar CAMC cluster in the human TME. In contrast to KPCA tumors, HGSOC samples lacked well-defined SERPINB2+and SERPINB2− populations, perhaps due to the limited amount of CAMCs represented in this dataset. SERPINB2 and UPK3B were largely expressed within the same unique CAMC cluster (figure 1G,I), although SERPINB2 showed broader expression, including in the pre-CAF cluster. The proportion of double-positive cells (UPK3B+/SERPINB2+) was very low (0.9%), with limited colocalization (figure 2J I), suggesting these phenotypes were not overlapping. Furthermore, the CAF cluster appeared transcriptionally distinct from CAMCs, and distinguishable by the expression of fibroblast markers such as ACTA2, COL4A1, FAP, and TAGLN (online supplemental figure 1A,B), as observed in the mouse.

To better understand the conservation of the mouse CAMC gene signature in human tumors, we computed a module score based on the murine CAMC markers (“UPK3B”, “LRRN4”, “MSLN”, “SERPINB2”, “NDUFA4L2”, ADM2”, “ASNS”, “MTHFD2”, “PHGDH”, “ERO1A”, “ALDH1L2”, “AMHR2”) in human single-cell RNA-seq data. CAMC scores were computed using Seurat’s AddModuleScore function, which calculates the average expression of CAMC signature genes per cell and subtracts the expression of matched control gene sets to control for technical and expression-level biases.

This analysis revealed a significant enrichment of the module in the CAMCSERPINB2+ cluster (mean score=0.125), but not in CAFs (mean score=−0.0168). A low positive score was detected in the Pre-CAF population, suggesting a potential continuum between mesothelial and CAF cells. Overall, these data suggest a conservation of CAMC markers between mouse and human (online supplemental figure 1C).

Normal MCs acquire a CAMCSerpinb2+ signature in a time-dependent manner following stimulation by cancer-secreted factors

To understand the dynamic changes occurring during the differentiation of MCs into CAMC subtypes, as defined by expression of cluster-specific markers such as Upk3b (CAMCprolif, CAMCserpinb2−), H2-Aa (MHCII subunit, CAMCprolif, CAMCserpinb2−, CAF), Serpinb2 (CAMCserpinb2+), and Col4a1 (CAF) (figure 3A), we predicted their developmental trajectories based on gene expression dynamics over pseudotime (figure 3B). Pseudotime analysis suggested a linear transition from the CAMC Serpinb2− to the CAMCSerpinb2+ state, observed both in murine CAMCs (top panel) and in human HGSOC (bottom panel) (figure 3B). This trajectory is supported by dynamic gene expression changes, including a progressive decrease in UPK3B alongside a concomitant increase in SERPINB2 (online supplemental figure 3C).

To better understand how normal MCs differentiate into the CAMCSerpinb2+ state, we examined transcriptional changes in primary MCs, isolated from normal omenta, as they are differentiated in vitro into CAMCs by stimulating with media conditioned by KPCA cancer cells (24 hours conditioning). We followed the expression of CAMCSerpinb2+ markers in a time-dependent manner (0–96 hours) (figure 3C), assessing Upk3b, MHCII sub-units, and Serpinb2 expression by qPCR (figure 3D). We observed a significant decrease in Upk3b expression after 24 hours of cancer media stimulation, while MHCII subunits and Serpinb2 expression was increased significantly at 72 and 96 hours, respectively. To decipher the full transcriptomic changes associated with MC differentiation into CAMCs we performed bulk RNA-seq following 72 hours of stimulation with KPCA cancer-conditioned media. This analysis consistently identified an increase in Serpinb2, Ndufa4l2, Ero1a, and other markers of CAMCSerpinb2+ cells found in KPCA tumors, as well as an increase in MHC-II subunits (H2-Ab1) generally observed in CAMCs (online supplemental figure 2A,B). Notably, geneset enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (online supplemental figure 2B) revealed an enrichment of pathways related to T-cells, cytokines, and metabolism (online supplemental figure 2C). These data suggest that exposure to cancer cell-derived factors promotes a stepwise differentiation of MCs into the CAMC Serpinb2− and CAMCSerpinb2+ states.

To test this hypothesis, we examined the transcriptional changes in Upk3b, MHCII subunits, and Serpinb2 at the protein level in primary MCs stimulated by KPCA media after 24 and 96 hours of stimulation by Western blot (figure 3E). Consistent with the qPCR data, we observed a reduction in UPK3B and an induction of MHCII and SERPINB2 when compared with unstimulated normal MCs (figure 3E). Similar findings were confirmed by immunofluorescence analysis at 72 hours (figure 3F). Immunofluorescence analysis of MHCII and SERPINB2 staining showed extensive co-localization in primary MCs following stimulation with KPCA-conditioned media, suggesting that the CAMCSerpinb2+ arise through a linear transition from MCs MHCII+CAMCSerpinb2− cells, to MHCII+CAMCSerpinb2+ (figure 3F).

To reduce batch-to-batch variability of primary MCs, we established an immortalized MC line, iMC (SV40T-transformed mouse primary omental MCs). We confirmed that iMCs respond similarly to primary MCs when stimulated with cancer cell-conditioned media by recapitulating the downregulation of Upk3b expression and upregulation of Serpinb2 expression by qPCR, Western blot, and immunofluorescence (online supplemental figure 3). This pattern was also observed in a human MC line (Met5a) following stimulation with media conditionned by a primary cancer cells derived from a patient with HGSOC (HGSOC pt-D).30 However, stimulation of Met5a with media conditioned by Ov90 cells, another ovarian cancer cell line, had a more blunted response, only modestly inducing SERPINB2 at the 96-hour timepoint (online supplemental figure 3A–C). These results suggest that cancer cells are heterogeneous in their ability to reprogram MCs into the CAMCSerpinb2+ state, which might lead to heterogeneity of CAMC populations across patients. The differences in cancer cell secretions that underlie this heterogeneity remain unresolved.

The CAMCSerpinb2+ state is specifically induced by cancer-secreted factors but not other inflammatory stimuli such as bacterial lipopolysaccharide

We next sought to determine whether the CAMCSerpinb2+ gene signature was specifically induced by tumor-derived factors or can also be triggered by other external inflammatory stimuli. We used lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a component of bacterial cell walls, to induce MC activation in vitro. As expected, MCs responded to LPS stimulation (72 hours incubation) by upregulating Ccl2 and Cxcl1, two chemokines typically induced during inflammatory responses31 (figure 3G). Notably, these cytokines were also identified in the cytokine-MC cluster, supporting the idea that this subset reflects endogenous immune activation of MCs (figure 1C,D). However, LPS did not modulate the expression of CAMCSerpinb2+ markers (Serpinb2, Ndufa4l4, Ero1l, and Aldh1l2) or MHCII sub-units (Cd74, H2-Ab1, H2-Eb1) (figure 3G). In contrast, conditioned media from KPCA tumor cells (72 hours incubation) significantly increased the expression of CAMCserpinb2+ genes, suggesting that KPCA-derived factors reprogram MCs through distinct, cancer-specific mechanisms (figure 3G).

The CAMC expression signature at the tumor periphery changes upon infiltration into the tumor core

Given the scRNAseq pseudotime and in vitro data suggesting a linear progression from MCs to CAMC and CAF states, we hypothesized that CAMCs change their identity during tumor progression and undergo a spatiotemporal differentiation within the TME and contribute to stromal development. We investigated this spatiotemporal evolution of CAMC subtypes in vitro using a 3D spheroid model composed of KPCA cancer cells (2×105 cells/well) cultured for 3 days before addition of freshly isolated MCs from tdTomato reporter mice (1×105 cells/well) (figure 4A). The tdTomato enabled real-time tracking of MC-derived cells during spheroid development. After 1 week in culture, the spheres were cryosectionned and stained by immunofluorescence. The tdTomato-CAMCs were initially observed forming a layer at the spheroid surface, while after 2 weeks they were actively infiltrating the tumor core (figure 4B). Notably, CAMCs located at the spheroid periphery surface expressed UPK3B, MHCII, and SERPINB2, as determined by immunofluorescence staining on spheroid sections (figure 4C). The surface expression was quantified by contouring the spheroid periphery (approximately 50 µm thickness) and measuring the intensity of the stained area (figure 4D). In contrast, infiltrating CAMCs lacked these markers and instead exhibited COL4A1 expression, suggesting a transition toward a stromal fibroblast-like phenotype within the tumor (figure 3C,D), recapitulating the pseudotime expression progression in the scRNAseq of KPCA tumors (figure 2A,B). To better understand if the CAF state represented a terminal differentiation we further investigated the expression of Acta2 and Col4a1, two markers enriched in CAF subpopulations identified in both our murine and human scRNA-seq datasets (online supplemental figure 1). on stimulation with cancer cell-conditioned media, primary and immortalized MCs exhibited increased protein levels of ACTA2 and COL4A1 after 96 hours, as determined by immunofluorescence staining (online supplemental figure 3C), suggesting their terminal differentiation into stromal CAFs promotes tumor infiltration and contributes to stroma formation.

Anti-Müllerian hormone receptor type 2 (AMHR2) has been previously described as a specific surface marker of CAMCs.16 We took advantage of this marker to trace the fate of Amhr2+CAMC cells as they progress from the CAMCSerpinb2− state to the CAMCserpinb2+, and ultimately differentiate into CAFs (figure 5A). We utilized the AMHR2-CreERT2/mTmG inducible reporter mouse model,16 which allowed lineage tracing via tamoxifen pulse-chase induction of GFP (and loss of Tomato) in Amhr2+CAMCs and their descendants in vivo. KPCA cancer cells were engrafted intra-peritoneally into this reporter mouse, and tamoxifen (20 mg/kg) was administered 48 hours later to induce conversion of the floxed mTmG allele in AMHR2-CreERT2+ cells (figure 5B). GFP+ cells in tumors were assessed after 10 days of growth by cryosection and immunofluorescence analysis (figure 5D). We found GFP+ cells at the tumor surface that co-expressed UPK3B, MHCII, and SERPINB2, with a co-expression average of 77%, 83%, and 78%, respectively (n=3). Furthermore, GFP+ cells infiltrating deeper into the tumor mass were also observed, which lacked expression of UPK3B and SERPINB2, but showed robust expression of COL4A1, a marker of CAFs (intra-tumoral GFP-co-expression mean: 81%). These results suggest the AMHR2+CAMCSerpinb2− lineage is the progenitor of the CAMCSerpinb2+ and CAF cells (figure 4C,D), a finding that parallels our results obtained with the 3D spheroid model (figure 3C,D).

Spatial analysis of metastases finds an association between CAMCs and CD4+ T at the tumor periphery

We next sought to understand how CAMCs and their states might interact with immunocytes and shape the tumor immune microenvironment. We have previously described AMHR2+CAMCs located at the tumor surface in human high-grade serous ovarian tumors.16 We sought to further refine the spatial distribution of CAMC subtypes and their proximity to immunocytes using spatial transcriptomics on HGSOC tumors from untreated patients with HGSOC using the Xenium platform. This analysis confirmed that UPK3B+ and SERPINB2+CAMCs were predominantly at the tumor surface and within tumor invaginations (figure 5E). Given their location at the tumor periphery and their MHCII expression, we investigated the spatial correlation between CAMCs and lymphocyte populations. Specifically, we correlated total CAMC abundance (via UPK3B expression) and CAMCSERPINB2+ state (via SERPINB2+ expression) with proximity to T-cell subtypes, focusing on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. Regions of interest (ROI) with high expression of the mesothelial marker UPK3B were associated with a higher proportion of CD4+ T cells (UPK3B vs CD4: r=0.72, p<0.0001; SERPINB2 vs CD4: r=0.64, p<0.0001) compared with areas lacking in CAMCs (figure 5F). In addition, ROI devoid of CAMCs exhibited increased CD8+ T cell infiltration, which could imply a more cytotoxic immune landscape in areas without CAMC influence (figure 4E,F). These data suggest that CAMCs may contribute to the spatial heterogeneity of the T lymphocyte landscape within ovarian tumors.

CAMCSerpinb2+ cells inhibit lymphocyte infiltration and promote an immunosuppressive T cell

To further investigate the effect of CAMCSerpinb2+ cells on immune infiltration, we used a 3D heterotypic spheroid model composed of KPCA ovarian cancer cells mixed with either immortalized (iMCs) or iMCs pre-differentiated into CAMCs (iCAMCSerpinb2+) at a 1:1 ratio (2×10⁵ cells each). iCAMCSerpinb2+ were generated by stimulating iMCs during 9 days with three batches of cancer cell–conditioned media (72 hours each) from KPCA cells (figure 6A), thereby efficiently promoting their reprogramming into iCAMCSerpinb2+ cells before their introduction to the cancer cell spheroids (figure 6B). To compare the effect of iMCs and iCAMCSerpinb2+ on immunocyte infiltration, we performed short-term experiments, as longer co-cultures would be expected to lead to the reprogramming of iMCs into CAMCs from contact with KPCA cells within the spheroid. Freshly isolated murine splenocytes (1×105 cells) were added to the culture 1 day after spheroid formation. Spheroids were harvested after 6 days of culture, cryosectioned, and subjected to immunofluorescent staining (figure 6C). iCAMCSerpinb2+ labeled in red (tdTomato+), were distributed throughout the spheroid when co-cultured with cancer cells (figure 6C). Immune cells identified by immunofluorescent stain of CD45+ (shown in yellow), infiltrated more extensively in spheroids containing iMCs than those containing iCAMCSerpinb2+, where they were largely excluded and remained confined to the periphery (figure 6C). Additionally, spheroids formed with iCAMCSerpinb2+ had a trend of larger diameter compared with those formed with iMCs (p<0.057) (figure 6D), suggesting the former may promote proliferation and spheroid growth.

To evaluate the impact of iCAMCSerpinb2+ on the TME in vivo, we grafted KPCA cancer cells subcutaneously (2×106), either alone or mixed (ratio 1:1) with iMCs, iCAMCSerpinb2+, or iMCs pretreated with LPS overnight (12 hours) prior to grafting (figure 6E). The subcutaneous model was chosen to exclude endogenous mouse peritoneal MCs, which are absent in this location. Tumors formed by KPCA cells mixed with iMCs did not show a significant increase in mass compared with KPCA cells alone, whereas co-grafting with iCAMCSerpinb2+ resulted in significantly larger tumor masses (1.7-fold increase compared with iMCs; Figure 6F). Interestingly, iMCs pre-stimulated with LPS before grafting led to a marked reduction in tumor growth (1.9-fold decrease compared with iMCs; Figure 6F). Together, these results suggest that cancer-derived factors reprogram MCs toward a pro-tumorigenic iCAMCSerpinb2+ phenotype, which cannot be recapitulated by MC activation with LPS treatment.

To better understand the influence of iCAMCSerpinb2+ state on the immune cell phenotype, we performed immunostaining and compared the KPCA tumor mixed either iMC or CAMCSerpinb2+ (figure 6F). We observed a reduction of CD45+ cells infiltration in tumors with iCAMCSerpinb2+ compared to iMCs (figure 5G,H). We next analyzed the immune subpopulations by flow cytometry in tumors derived from KPCA cells mixed with either iCAMCSerpinb2+ (n=4) or iMCs (n=4). Immunophenotyping of each tumor was performed using a validated panel of antibodies to identify representative immune cell subtypes. The gating strategy is detailed in supplementary data (online supplemental figure 4A,B). We observed a trend of reduced total CD45+ cell count, mostly reflecting a significant reduction of CD4+ T cells and B cells, and a trend for reduced CD8+ T cells, in KPCA tumors mixed with iCAMCSerpinb2+ compared to iMCs. Furthermore, we observed a trend of increased T regulatory (Treg) cell abundance in the iCAMCSerpinb2+ condition compared with iMCs. However, myeloid cell infiltration was not impacted by iCAMCSerpinb2+, translating into a significant increase in the lymphoid to myeloid ratio (figure 6). Finally, we observed that CD4+ and CD8+ T cells exhibited an ‘exhausted’ phenotype marked by the increase of PD-1 and TIM3 exhaustion markers32 (figure 6). This was accompanied by a trend toward decreased IFNγ expression within the CD4+ T cell population (online supplemental figure 4C) as well as a decrease of CD107a expression at the cell surface in the CD8+ T cell population, indicating a lower degranulation and a potential reduction in effector and cytotoxic T cell activity (online supplemental figure 4D).32 Such a shift could reflect an immunosuppressive reprogramming of the CD4+ compartment by CAMCSerpinb2+ cells, favoring local tolerance over anti-tumor immunity.

To better understand how CAMCs may regulate immunosuppression, we investigated the chemokine secretion profiles of iMCs and iCAMCSerpinb2+. iMCs and iCAMCSerpinb2+ were cultured in 6-well plates at a density of 5×10⁵ cells per well in serum-free medium, and supernatants were collected after 24 hours to characterize their secretome using a multiplex ELISA assay (figure 6J). This analysis revealed a significant increase in CSF1, VEGFA, and CCL17 levels in iCAMCSerpinb2+ compared to iMCs. Additionally, we observed a trend toward elevated levels of GM-CSF (CSF2), CXCL1, and CCL11. Conversely, secretion of G-CSF (CSF3) was reduced in iCAMCSerpinb2+ (figure 6J). Altogether, the cytokine profile of iCAMCSerpinb2+ is consistent with a pro-tolerogenic secretome, which in turn could lead to the immune exclusion of T cells.33–35

CAMCSerpinb2+ cells present antigens via MHC II sub-optimally and promote a tolerogenic phenotype in CD4+ T cells

To better understand the role of CAMCs in the regulation of T cells, we analyzed the communication axes across the TME using ligand-receptor inference analysis of single-cell transcriptomic data from healthy omentum and KPCA tumor (figure 7A). Global interaction networks revealed that in the healthy omentum, MCs exhibited no detectable interactions with T cells, suggesting a limited role in T cell modulation under non-pathogenic conditions despite their MHC-II expression (figure 7A). In contrast, within the tumor context, CAMCs emerged as active signaling participants, engaging specific interactions with T cells as well as cancer cells and stromal cells (figure 7B). The scale and specificity of these CAMC-T cell connections contrast starkly with the negligible interaction landscape observed for MCs. To evaluate the ability of iCAMCSerpinb2+ to modulate T cell activity compared with iMCs, we co-cultured them with naïve T cells isolated from spleen for 72hours. Expression of Upk3b and Serpinb2 was evaluated in iMC and iCAMCSerpinb2+ by qPCR during co-culture with naïve T cells (figure 7C). In this co-culture setting, the presence of splenocytes further enhanced the iCAMCSerpinb2+ signature, marked by reduced Upk3b expression and increased Serpinb2 expression at 24 hours, and 72 hours (figure 7C). We next evaluated T-cell markers associated with activation and regulation (CD69+, IL-2+, IFNγ+, GZMB+, FOXP3+) after co-culture with iMCs or iCAMCSerpinb2+ using flow cytometry (figure 7D). No differential effects were observed, suggesting that neither iMCs nor iCAMCSerpinb2+ can activate naïve T cells in the manner of professional antigen-presenting cells.

To evaluate whether iCAMCSerpinb2+ can modulate CD4+ T cells through MHCII interactions following stimulation with cancer-secreted factors, we performed an antigen-specific presentation assay. iMCs or iCAMCSerpinb2+ were pulsed with the Ova2 peptide, washed to remove excess of antigen, and incubated with splenocytes from OT2 mice, whose CD4+ T cells express a TCR specific for the Ova2 epitope (figure 7E).36 After 24 hours and 72 hours, splenocytes were harvested, and the CD4+ T-cell population was analyzed by flow cytometry to assess activation and polarization using an established immuno-phenotyping panel (online supplemental figure 4). As an experimental control, splenocytes incubated with Ova peptide showed increased expression of the activation marker CD69 at both 24 hours and 72 hours (figure 7F). compared with iMC, the iCAMCSerpinb2+ induced a marked upregulation of CD69 on CD4+ T cells at both time points (24 hours and 72 hours), which was correlated with increased MHCII expression (figure 6G–I). In parallel, we also observed an increased proportion of CD4+ T cells expressing the transcription factor Foxp3, suggesting that iCAMCSerpinb2+ may promote the differentiation or expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) (figure 7G). We further analyzed MHCII expression along with co-stimulatory receptors (CD80/CD86 and CD40) to determine whether iCAMCSerpinb2+ were capable of modulating CD4+ T cells (figure 6H,I). While MHC II enables antigen presentation, co-stimulatory receptors and cytokines are required to deliver the second and third activation signals, respectively. The scRNA-seq analysis revealed MHCII expression in both iMCs and iCAMCSerpinb2+ (figure 7H), which was confirmed by qPCR and Western blot (figure 7). However, expression of co-stimulatory receptors was barely detectable, suggesting suboptimal activation signals delivered by iCAMCSerpinb2+ to T cells (figure 7H). Together, these data suggest that CAMCs can display antigens on MHC-II but likely lack the co-stimulatory signals necessary for full T cell activation.

To investigate whether CAMCs can mediate CD4+ T cell polarization through their cytokine secretion, we performed a cytokine array assay comparing supernatants from iMCs and iCAMCSerpinb2+ cultured under serum-free conditions (24-hour incubation). The multiplex analysis revealed a significant reduction of several pro-inflammatory cytokines in iCAMCSerpinb2+ supernatants, including IL-1α (fold change: 2.6), IL-2 (fold change: 1.5), IL-12p40 (fold change: 3.4), IL-15 (fold change: 1.6), and IL-7 (fold change: 1.7), all of which are commonly associated with promoting Th1 polarization. Conversely, IL-10, a key immunosuppressive cytokine, showed a tendency to increase in iCAMCSerpinb2+ supernatants (fold change: 1.4), along with a significant increase of Tgfb1 (fold change: 2.6), suggesting a pro-tolerogenic cytokine profile (figure 7K). Together with the induction of Foxp3 expression, these results support the hypothesis that iCAMCSerpinb2+ may contribute to immune evasion in the TME by skewing CD4+ T cell differentiation away from effector Th1 responses and toward a regulatory phenotype via pro-tolerogenic cytokines.

To understand if this association between CAMCSERPINB2+ and T regs was also present in human HGSOC, we analyzed the proportion of Tregs (CD4+/FOXP3+) in the TME of 20 untreated patients with HGSOC using available scRNA-seq datasets (GSE147082, GSE235931, GSE241221). Patients were stratified into two groups based on TMEs enrichment with CAMC markers (as defined by UPK3B+ median expression: CAMCLow (n=10), CAMCHigh (n=10)). We observed a significant enrichment of Tregs in the CAMCHigh group (figure 7L), suggesting CAMCs may contribute to Treg accumulation in human HGSOC tumors.

The CAMCSerpinb2+ signature is associated with poor prognosis, therapy resistance, and impaired immunotherapy response

Based on the impact of CAMCs on reducing immune infiltration and promoting immunosuppressive T cell phenotypes, we assessed whether the presence of CAMCSerpinb2+ cells could promote resistance to immunotherapy. First, we compared the proportion of CAMC subtypes in previously described isogenic clones of KPCA cells classified as sensitive or resistant to the triple combination of Prexasertib, anti-PD1, and anti-CTLA4 (PPC), as shown by Iyer et al, 2021. We observed an enrichment in CAMCSerpinb2+ and CAFs in tumors derived from the PPC-resistant KPCA by scRNAseq (figure 8A). Similarly, we found an increased abundance of CAMCSERPINB2+ and CAF cells in patients with HGSOC resistant to platinum-taxane treatment by scRNAseq (GSE201047)37 (figure 8B).

To assess the prognostic utility of CAMC-associated, we analyzed the association between SERPINB2 and UPK3B expression levels and survival outcomes in patients with ovarian cancer from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset. Kaplan–Meier analysis of the TCGA OV-cohort revealed that patients with high SERPINB2 expression had significantly worse disease-free survival compared with those with low expression (HR=1.4, p=0.016; Figure 8C). Consistent with our single-cell RNA-seq analyses, SERPINB2 emerges as a correlative marker of immunosuppressive CAMCSERPINB2+ abundance that is linked to poor prognosis. In contrast, UPK3B expression, which in our models marked less differentiated MCs and CAMCs, was not associated with patient outcome in HGSOC (figure 8C). These data suggest that the CAMC state, rather than MC abundance, may drive prognostic value.

We next sought to understand if CAMC states were associated with a particular ovarian cancer molecular histotype38 (figure 8D). To do so, we compared UPK3B and SERPINB2 transcript levels from an RNAseq dataset of the TCGA ovarian cancer UNC hub (n=308) annotated with the molecular subtypes as proliferative (Prolif), immunoreactive (Imm), differentiated (Diff), and mesenchymal (Mes), and found that UPK3B was significantly higher in the differentiated subtype compared with proliferative and immunoreactive (figure 8D). In contrast, SERPINB2 was significantly higher in the mesenchymal subtype compared with others (figure 7M), suggesting SERPINB2 overexpression may be associated with mesenchymal status and worse survival outcome in human tumors.38 It remains unclear if differences in CAMC differentiation directly contribute to the histotype signature or are driven by the unique TME.

To directly assess the impact of CAMCs on therapy response, KPCA cancer cells were grafted subcutaneously either alone (4×10⁶ cells) or mixed 1:1 with iCAMCs (2×10⁶ each) and treated weekly with the PPC combination (prexasertib, 10 mg/kg; anti-PD-1, 50 µg/mouse; anti-CTLA-4, 50 µg/mouse). In KPCA-only grafts, PPC treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth, and resulted in significantly smaller tumor masses at 4 weeks compared with untreated controls (p<0.01; Figure 8E). In contrast, PPC treatment in KPCA+iCAMC co-grafts did not significantly affect tumor growth, suggesting that the presence of iCAMCs abrogates the therapeutic effect of the PPC combination immunotherapy. Consistently, PPC treatment resulted in a complete response in 75% of mice bearing KPCA tumors (6/8 mice survived), whereas no durable responses were observed in mice with KPCA+iCAMC co-grafts (all 8 mice succumbed at 6 weeks; Figure 8F). These findings suggest that CAMCs may contribute to resistance to PPC therapy.

Collectively, these data support a model in which CAMCs actively drive immune evasion by suppressing pro-inflammatory signals while promoting tolerogenic cues. This environment may facilitate the expansion or stabilization of Tregs within the TME, dampening anti-tumor immunity and ultimately contributing to tumor progression and poor response to immunotherapy.

Discussion

In this study, we describe CAMCs and their subtypes as a functionally distinct and dynamic components of ovarian peritoneal carcinomatosis. Our findings support a model in which cancer cell-secreted factors act as primary initiators of CAMC differentiation in the TME. Through combined spatial, single-cell, and functional approaches, we demonstrate that CAMC differentiation states are tightly linked to their immunoregulatory functions and critically influence the composition and activity of immune cell populations, ultimately impacting prognosis and immunotherapy responses.

One of the key findings of our study was the identification of a unique ‘metabolic’ signature of CAMC observed in the KPCA tumors from a syngeneic mouse model and recapitulated in human HGSOC tumors, which was defined based on SERPINB2+ expression (CAMCSerpinb2+). This CAMC state was characterized by a transcriptional signature marked by increased expression of metabolic and stress-response genes such as Serpinb2, Ndufa4l2, Adm2, Asns, Aldh1l2, Ero1l, Phgdh, and Mthfd2. Importantly, the CAMCSerpinb2+ gene expression signature was tumor-specific and not present in normal MCs, or MCs activated with a pro-inflammatory stimulus (LPS), and could be induced in vitro by cancer cell-secreted factors, suggesting this differentiation program is not a general stress or inflammatory response,31 39 but a state actively induced by cancer cells. We propose that CAMCSerpinb2+ cells represent a tumor-adapted pathogenic MC state.

Although the CAMCSerpinb2+ cluster is distinct in its molecular profiles in scRNAseq datasets, in vitro and in vivo data suggest that it represents a transient state of differentiation of CAMCs, rather than a static subpopulation, as CAMCSerpinb2− cells progress toward a CAF-like phenotype. Indeed, in vitro experiments showed that MCs acquire CAMCSerpinb2+ signature upon stimulation with cancer-conditioned media in a time-dependent manner. The temporal changes in gene expression likely also coincide with spatial changes in the localization of these cells, as CAMCs infiltrating tumor spheroids in vitro eventually acquire CAF markers. Lineage tracing of CAMCs in vivo and spatial transcriptomics in human tumors confirmed the localization of UPK3B, SERPINB2, and MHCII enriched CAMCs at the tumor periphery. As CAMCs migrated and infiltrated the tumor core, they progressively lost expression of these CAMC-specific markers. This phenotypic shift highlights CAMC plasticity and transition toward a mesenchymal-like state, as evidenced by the upregulation of COL4A1 and ACTA2, markers of CAFs. Such a transition is consistent with the well-documented MMT, whereby MCs differentiate into fibroblast-like cells in both fibrosis and peritoneal metastasis contexts.10 12 25 40–42

Motivated by the expression of MHC II in CAMCs and their strategic localization at the tumor periphery, we next investigated the contribution of CAMCs as a potential gatekeeper of the tumor immune landscape. Immunophenotyping of tumors derived by grafting KPCA cancer cells alongside immortalized iCAMCserpinb2+ revealed a significant reduction in CD4+ T cells, which displayed a significantly exhausted phenotype (PD1+TIM3+), along with a trend toward increased Treg infiltration compared with tumors containing normal iMCs. This immunosuppressive profile may be explained, at least in part, by the cytokine secretome of CAMCSerpinb2+ relative to normal MC. Notably, iCAMCSerpinb2+ produced high levels of CCL17, a CCR4 ligand that selectively attracts Tregs, potentially creating a juxtacrine regulatory niche within the TME.43 In line with this, analysis of patient with HGSOC revealed that tumors with higher inferred CAMCSerpinb2+ abundance based on gene expression exhibited significantly increased Treg infiltration, a feature associated with poor prognosis.44 45 In addition to these chemokines and growth factors, CAMCSerpinb2+ had a significant reduction in IL-2, IL-15, and IL-12p40, cytokines normally required for effector CD4 T cell proliferation, survival, and Th1 polarization, while concomitantly increasing IL-10 and TGFβ1, two potent immunosuppressive cytokines. Taken together, these results suggest CAMCSerpinb2+ cells may contribute to an immune-suppressive, tumor-permissive microenvironment by promoting Treg recruitment, impairing effector T cell function, and excluding adaptive immune cells from tumor sites via their secretome. Strikingly, VEGF was also enriched in CAMCSerpinb2+ conditioned media, suggesting an additional pro-tumoral mechanism of action by driving angiogenesis while simultaneously restricting effector T cell infiltration, thereby contributing to immune exclusion.34 Beyond potential effects on T cell populations, CAMCSerpinb2+ cells also influence the myeloid compartment. While myeloid cell abundance was not specifically altered, CAMCSerpinb2+ secreted elevated levels of M-CSF (CSF1) and G-CSF (CSF3), two cytokines that promote the functional polarization of tumor-associated macrophages and neutrophils, respectively, which could in turn influence the immunosuppressive environment of HGSOC.17 46

Beyond their secretory activity, CAMCSerpinb2+ may exert a direct influence on CD4+ T cell responses directly through MHC II antigen presentation. Although CAMCs initially express MHC II they lack canonical co-stimulatory molecules such as CD80, CD86, and CD40, suggesting they may constitute non-professional antigen-presenting cells. In functional assays, CAMCs were able to present antigen and induce CD69+ marker on CD4+ T cells, but this was accompanied by an increased frequency of FOXP3+ T cells, indicating a tolerogenic state. These results suggest that CAMCSerpinb2+ may act as an atypical antigen-presenting cell that divert CD4+ T cell responses toward regulatory fates, thereby dampening anti-tumor immunity. This parallels findings from pancreatic cancer, where antigen-presenting CAFs have been shown to promote T cell tolerance.11 25

Spatial transcriptomic analysis of HGSOC tumors from four patients revealed that regions enriched in surface CAMCs (UPK3B+, SERPINB2+) strongly correlated with CD4+ T cells proximity but appeared to restrict their infiltration into the tumor core. In contrast, regions lacking UPK3B and SERPINB2 expression exhibited higher CD8+ T cell infiltration, suggesting that CAMC presence influences both the composition and localization of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. Extending these findings, we observed that tumors enriched in CAMCs displayed reduced infiltration of CD4+ T cells and B cells, while favoring the accumulation of regulatory T cells (Tregs). Together, these data establish CAMCs as active contributors to the remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment (TME), whereby their acquisition of a distinct SERPINB2+ immunomodulatory phenotype may support tumor immune evasion.

Futhermore, this study suggests that acquiring a CAMCSerpinb2+ signature may correlate with both immune tolerance and increased tumor growth. Indeed, co-grafting iCAMCSerpinb2+ cells along with cancer cells increased tumor burden by 1.7-fold compared with normal iMCs. In addition, we found increased abundance of CAMCSerpinb2+ cells in immunotherapy-resistant KPCA isogeneic clone-derived tumors. Indeed, co-grafting iCAMCSerpinb2+ cells with KPCA cells subcutaneously was sufficient to induce combined immunotherapy (Prexacertib, anti-PD1, anti-CTLA4) resistance in a syngeneic mouse model of ovarian cancer, suggesting CAMC immunomodulatory function may be an important driver of immunotherapy resistance. Similarly, CAMCSERPINB2+ cells were more abundant in taxane-resistant tumors of patients with HGSOC, suggesting they may also contribute to resistance to conventional chemotherapy, although the mechanism remains uncertain. This SERPINB2+ signature was also associated with worse disease-free survival, and the mesenchymal molecular phenotype in HGSOC, suggesting biomarkers reflecting CAMC cell abundance or state may have clinical utility.

Collectively, our findings identify CAMCs as key regulators of ovarian peritoneal carcinomatosis that promote immune evasion through complementary mechanisms. Cancer cells appear as the first drivers of MC reprogramming, which in turn differentiate and contribute to the tumor stroma both through the formation of distinct subtypes of CAMCs, and potentially serving as progenitors of CAFs. Beyond their strategic localization at the tumor periphery as gatekeepers modulating immune cell access, CAMCs contribute to the tumor immune microenvironment, with important implications for immune evasion and resistance to immunotherapy. This study further supports CAMCs as promising targets for strategies aimed at restoring immune responsiveness and improving patient outcomes in HGSOC.

Methods

Experimental model and subject details

Cell models

The KPCA cell lines were developed and described in Iyer et al23 and the OV90 cell lines of malignant papillary serous adenocarcinoma were purchased from ATCC. These cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 1% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin and incubated at 37°C/5% CO2. The primary human ovarian cancer cell (ptD) was collected from ascites of a patient after informed consent under Institutional Review Board-approved protocols (2007P001918/Massachusetts General Hospital) as part of a previous study.30 The adherent cells were cultured in DMEM:F12, supplemented with 5% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The Met5a cell line was purchased from ATCC and cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 5% FBS. Primary MCs were obtained from the omenta of female mice by mechanical disruption and enzymatic digestion using collagenase at 37°C for 45 min. The pieces of tissue were placed in a collector tube in a Gentlemacs dissociator (Miltenyi) for 1 min of processing. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70 µm filter and washed two times with PBS (Phosphate Buffer Saline). The adherent cells were cultured in DMEM:F12 medium, supplemented with 5% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

Generation of immortalized MCs

Immortalized MCs (iMCs) were generated by transducing primary MCs with a lentiviral vector encoding the SV40 large T antigen. After 72 hours, cells were treated with puromycin (2 µg/mL) for 2 weeks to select a homogeneous, stably transduced population. The resulting iMCs were maintained in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 5% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

Generation of CAMC by conditioned media treatment

CAMCs and their immortalized counterparts (iCAMCs) were generated by stimulating primary MCs or iMCs, respectively, with standardized KPCA cancer cell-conditioned medium. This medium was prepared by culturing KPCA cells to 80% confluence, replacing the medium with DMEM/F12 containing 1% FBS, and collecting the supernatant after 24 hours. The conditioned medium was centrifuged to remove cellular debris and applied to MC or iMC cultures for a minimum of 72 hours to induce a CAMCSerpinb2+ phenotype. For in vivo experiments, iCAMCs were subjected to three consecutive rounds of KPCA-conditioned media stimulation prior to the subcutaneous implantation.

Mouse models

This study was performed according to experimental protocols 2009N000033 and 2024000039, approved by the Massachusetts General Hospital Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee with C57BL6/J mice purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. All experiments used 12–16 week-old female C57BL6/J mice. In each experiment, we used 4 to 5 mice per group.

Amhr2-CRE-ERT2 x Rosa26-mTmG mice were developed in collaboration with the Jackson Laboratory (Strain #:037056 RRID:IMSR_JAX:037056). Briefly, they are the result of a knock-in of a CRE-ER fusion transgene in frame with the Amhr2 gene using a T2 exon skipping mechanism to ensure Amhr2 expression is maintained. These mice were then crossed with the Rosa26 CRE-inducible mTmG reporter as previously described.16 47 48 Ear genotyping of Amhr2-ERT2 and mTmG reporter mice was done using the REDExtract-N-Amp tissue PCR kit (Sigma, #SLBT8193) with sets of primers designed by IDT. Tamoxifen (4-OHT, Sigma-Aldrich) treatment was used to induce CRE-ER nuclear translocation, at a final concentration of 2 µM in vitro in female MCs isolated from omenta as described above. Only female mice carrying the Amhr2-CRE-ERT2/Rosa26-mTmG alleles were used in this study.

Human samples

Spatial transcriptomics analysis from tumor sections were donated by the Biological Resources Center of Montpellier Cancer Institute (ICM), France, and collected following the French regulations under the supervision of an investigator. The collection was declared to the French Ministry of Higher Education and Research (declaration number DC-2008-695). The tumor sections originate from HGSOC chemonaïve patients.

Cancer cell graft

Female mice were injected subcutaneously with 4×10⁶ KPCA cells, either alone or in combination with iMCs, iMC previously treated 24 hours with 1 µg/mL LPS (ThermoFisher # 00-4976-93) (iMC+LPS), or iCAMCSerpinb2+ at a 1:1 ratio. Mice were sacrificed 17 days post-injection, or when tumor volume exceeded 1500 mm³, to evaluate tumor burden.

Prexasertib, anti-PD1, anti-CTLA4 treatment combination: Treatments began when subcutaneous tumor reached 100 mm³ and were administered intraperitoneally once a week with the indicated doses during 6 weeks. For immunotherapy, anti-CTLA4 (50 µg/mouse; Bio X Cell, BE0164) and anti-PD1 (50 µg/mouse; Bio X Cell, BP0273) antibodies were used. Prexasertib (Selleckchem, S7178; 10 mg/kg) was administered in combination with the immunotherapies and was resuspended according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Control mice were injected with 10% DMSO (vehicle) and isotype antibodies.

Bulk-RNAseq

RNA was extracted from primary MCs and CAMCs using the Qiagen RNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Library construction, sequencing, and analysis were performed by Novogene Corporation. Genes with adjusted p value <0.05 and |log2(FoldChange)| >1 were considered as differentially expressed. RNA sequencing was performed on primary MCs and MCs stimulated with KPCA media from two clones (CAMCC and CAMCB) during 72 hours. Available in GEO NCBI deposit under identification number: GSE330590.

Immunofluorescence

The immunofluorescence staining was performed on fixed cells permeabilized with Triton X-100 (0.1%). The cells were incubated for 1 hour in blocking buffer at room temperature with PBS-BSA (2%), followed by three washes with PBS-Tween (0.01%). Then, the cells were incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4°C, with the antibody concentration as recommended by the manufacturer. The cells were washed and incubated for 1 hour with the specific secondary antibody. To visualize the stained proteins, the cells were counterstained with the DAPI nuclear stain for 5 min and a final wash step with PBS was performed. The coverslips were mounted onto glass slides using Vectashield mounting medium to preserve the fluorescent signal.

qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from MCs using the Qiagen RNA extraction kit, and cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis System for RT-PCR (Invitrogen). Expression levels relative to the geometric mean housekeeping genes were calculated by using cycle threshold (Ct) values logarithmically transformed using the 2−ΔCt function.

Western blot

MCs or iMCs were stimulated with cancer-conditioned media collected from KPCA cultures. Cells were incubated and sampled at different time points (0, 24, 48, and 72 hours). After stimulation, cells were washed with cold PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (20–30 µg) were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST for 1 hour at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against (specified target proteins, eg, UPK3B, MHC II, SERPINB2, and B-ACTIN as loading control). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Cytokine array

The cytokine profile was quantified in supernatants collected from in vitro cultures of iMCs and iCAMCs. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 hours under serum-free conditions. Supernatants were harvested, centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min to remove debris, and stored at −80°C until analysis. Cytokine concentrations were measured using a multiplex ELISA panel (Eve Technologies, Calgary, Canada) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were run in triplicate in three independent experiments.

Spheroid growth

Spheroids were generated by seeding KPCA cancer cells (2×105) either alone or mixed at a 1:1 ratio with iMCs or iCAMCs in ultra-low attachment 96-well plates (Corning). To investigate the contribution of MCs within the tumor microenvironment (TME), spheroids of KPCA cancer cells were first cultured and subsequently supplemented with iMCs (1×105 cells) for 1 or 2 weeks. At the indicated time points, spheroids were collected, embedded in OCT compound, cryosectioned, and subjected to immunofluorescence staining to evaluate MC persistence and immune cell infiltration.

To assess immune cell infiltration, KPCA cells were co-cultured with either iMCs or iCAMCSerpinb2+ at a 1:1 ratio in ultra-low attachment plates. Spheres were supplemented with 1×10⁵ freshly isolated splenocytes in complete RPMI medium and maintained for 6 days at 37°C with 5% CO₂. At the endpoint, spheres were harvested, cryo-embedded in OCT, sectioned at 8 µm, and subjected to immunofluorescence staining with anti-CD45 antibodies to evaluate immune cell infiltration.

Flow cytometry

KPCA tumors (n=4) were dissociated into single-cell suspensions on day 15 post-graft. A total of 1×105 cells per well were plated in 96-well V-bottom plates. Cells were first immunostained for extracellular markers, and the Live/Dead reagent (Viakrom808) was used, then permeabilized using nuclear Foxp3 permeabilization buffer (according to Biolegend’s instructions) for intracellular staining. After permeabilization, cells were washed and stained with antibodies targeting intracellular markers as recommended by the manufacturer, followed by three additional washes. Cells were then fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde. Immunostained cells were processed on a Cytoflex flow cytometer (BD), and data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (V.10).

OVA/OT-II T cell co-culture

iMCs or iCAMCSerpinb2+ were treated with 100 µg/mL Ovalbumin II (OVA II) peptide for 1 hour, washed three times, and then co-cultured at a 1:3 ratio with splenocytes from OT-II transgenic mice, which express the mouse α- and β-chains of the T cell receptor specific for OVA II and pair with the CD4 co-receptor. Co-cultures were performed in low-attachment 96-well plates for 24 or 72 hours. At the end of the culture period, cells were harvested, washed, and stained for extracellular and intracellular markers. Live/dead discrimination was performed using the Viakrom reagent. Flow cytometry acquisition was performed on a Cytoflex (BD), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis software

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.6 software (GraphPad Software, RRID:SCR_002798), using an independent sample t-test unless otherwise indicated or ANOVA test. p≤0.05 was considered as significant. Data are represented as mean±SE of the mean (SEM).

Image analysis

ImageJ software was used to quantify signal intensity following the pipeline described below. Each individual fluorescence channel corresponding to the proteins of interest (UPK3B, MHCII, SERPINB2, COL4A1, or ACTA2) was quantified and normalized to the DAPI channel. Tumor tissue was manually delineated, and a peripheral region extending 50 µm from the tumor contour was defined. The mean fluorescence intensity of each marker was then quantified within the selected regions.

Spatial transcriptomic analysis

Gene transcripts were conducted using the Xenium platform (10 x Genomics) on three untreated HGSOC tumor samples. The expression levels of UPK3B, SERPINB2, ACTA2, CD4, and CD8 transcripts were quantified across>10 representative ROI per tumor to capture the spatial heterogeneity. The data was processed and analyzed using Xenium software (10 x Genomics), enabling precise transcript quantification and spatial mapping of gene expression patterns. The correlation was performed between UPK3B and CD4 or CD8 transcripts.

Co-immunofluorescence image analysis

Quantification of colocalization was performed using the Coloc2 plugin in ImageJ and corresponds to the percentage of area overlap between purple staining (UPK3B or MHCII or SERPINB2 or COL4A1) and GFP staining.

Single-cell RNA and TCGA analysis

Transcriptomic data were extracted from the following datasets: human HGSOC (GSE235931, GSE14082, GSE241221), KPCA model (GSE233423), and healthy murine omentum (GSE134355), and analyzed in RStudio using the Seurat package. Patients were stratified into two groups based on UPK3B expression: CAMC-High (average expression above the population median) and CAMC-Low (average expression below the population median). The tumor microenvironments of CAMC-High and CAMC-Low patients were compared using specific markers indicative of cell phenotypes of interest, including CD4+FOXP3+ cells. To further assess differences between groups, the percentage of cells expressing these markers was calculated using the WhichCells command in Seurat.

To assess UPK3B and SERPINB2 expression across molecular histotypes of HGSOC, we analyzed transcript levels from the TCGA ovarian cancer (OV) UNC hub RNA-seq dataset (n=308), which includes metadata categorizing samples into proliferative, immunoreactive, differentiated, and mesenchymal subtypes.49 Level three data were obtained from the UCSC Xena repository. Transcript counts generated by Illumina HiSeqv2 were log-transformed using the formula log₂(norm_count+1) for downstream analysis.

Module score calculation

CAMC scores were computed in HGSOC single-cell RNA-seq data using Seurat’s AddModuleScore function, which calculates the average expression of CAMC signature genes per cell (“UPK3B”, “LRRN4”, “MSLN”, “SERPINB2”, “NDUFA4L2”, “ADM2”, “ASNS”, “MTHFD2”, “PHGDH”, “ERO1A”, “ALDH1L2”, “AMHR2”) identified from mouse-derived CAMC. Signature genes expression is subtracting of matched control gene sets for technical and expression-level biases. Gene set enrichment of the CAMC signature was assessed using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test comparing CAMCSERPINB2+ cells against all other clusters combined (Pre-CAF and CAF).

Survival analysis

Disease-free survival (DFS) analyses for UPK3B and SERPINB2 were performed using the GEPIA3 (Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis, version 3) online tool (https://gepia3.bioinfoliu.com). This platform integrates RNA-sequencing expression data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) projects.

For each gene of interest, patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups according to the median expression value. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to evaluate differences in DFS between groups. The log-rank test (Mantel-Cox) was used to determine statistical significance, and HRs with 95% CIs were reported. Graphical outputs were exported directly from the GEPIA3 interface.
