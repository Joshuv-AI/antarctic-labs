Executive Summary

One of the main goals of the Department of Energy (DOE) Atmospheric Radiation Measurement
(ARM) Tracking Aerosol Convection Interactions Experiment (TRACER) field campaign near
Houston, TX was to improve understanding of meteorology and aerosol effects on deep
convective storms. Houston summertime storms are often initiated and organized by the local
sea- and bay breeze boundaries, which move throughout the day. The meteorological and aerosol
environments may be very different across the airmass boundary. Depending on where the ARM
fixed sites are relative to the sea/bay breeze fronts and the storm’s inflow layer of air that
participates in the convective updraft, the ARM sites may be sampling very different air than is
being ingested by the storm, making it more difficult to achieve TRACER’s goal of disentangling
aerosol-cloud interactions from meteorology without additional measurements.
To help TRACER achieve its goals, the Texas A&M University (TAMU) team deployed the new
fully mobile Rapid Onsite Atmospheric Measurement Van (ROAM-V) carrying instruments
capable of measuring aerosol and atmospheric properties that was moved to multiple locations
on a deployment day. The main scientific goal of this project was to use these measurements
along with DOE ARM measurements to understand how the distribution of meteorology and
aerosols that serve as nuclei on which cloud drops or ice particles can form varies in different
types of air masses, and how these variations influence storm properties.
Our mobile platform and operations plan proved to be very successful, with over 30 deployments
where we sampled continental, maritime, convective outflow, outflow recovery, and post frontal
airmasses on the opposite side of the sea- and bay-breeze fronts from the ARM sites. The
measurements collected during our field campaign and aerosol profiles that we retrieved from
these measurements significantly enhanced the scientific impact of ARM’s TRACER campaign.
Through these measurements we have answered questions about the small-scale variations in
meteorology and cloud-forming properties of aerosols, and the relative importance of these
variations in both shallow and deep convective storms. The dataset we collected is already being
used by our team as well as the broader scientific community to answer important questions on
the influence of aerosols on deep convective storm properties, such as precipitation, intensity,
and lightning. The vertical aerosol profile retrieval that was developed by this team now serves
as initial aerosol profile conditions in a community-wide modeling effort.
Ultimately the measurements collected and research performed in this project addresses gaps in
our understanding of processes important for high impact weather events. The publicly available
data, model simulations, and peer-reviewed publications from this project contribute to
improving model representation of aerosol-convection interactions and their effects on weather
events that impact U.S. energy systems. This project also contributed significantly to workforce
development by providing high impact learning experiences and hands-training. We developed a
university course related to the project and trained 24 undergraduate students who participated in
the forecasting and deployment activities of both our field campaign and the broader DOE
TRACER community. Some also participated in research activities for the project. In addition to
the two graduate students who were integral to the project research and performed much of the
analysis, we also offered 11 other graduate students forecasting and field work experience. Three
postdoctoral researchers were also mentored and trained – all are or will be university faculty
members who will train the next generation of atmospheric scientists.

Comparison of Accomplishments to Project Goals

The overarching goal of this proposed project was to better understand how the vertical distributions
of both CCN and INP correspond to the inflow layer of deep convection (i.e., the layer of air that
participates in the updraft) in maritime, background continental, and polluted continental airmasses,
and how these variations influence properties of deep convection. To achieve this goal, we deployed
the Texas A&M University (TAMU) Rapid Onsite Atmospheric Measurement Van (ROAM-V) in
conjunction with ARM TRACER facilities toward four objectives described below. Underneath each
objective you will find a brief description of the publications documenting the accomplishments for
each objective.
 1. Quantify the ability of the complex aerosol populations in the Houston area to form cloud
 condensation nuclei (CCN) and ice nucleating particles (INP)

 Accomplishments toward this objective can be found in three published peer-reviewed
 manuscripts. The first manuscript, Rapp et al. (2024) describes the field campaign and
 measurements needed to address proposed objectives and enhance the overall scientific
 impact of TRACER campaign measurements. These measurements have already been
 used in a number of published studies from our group and the broader community.
 Thompson et al. (2025a) and Thompson et al. (2025b) describe CCN and the INP
 populations, respectively, as a function of the maritime or continental airmasses based on
 their location relative to the sea breeze front. Results show significant differences in the
 aerosol populations and their CCN/INP activity across airmass boundaries and large
 spatial heterogeneity in the aerosol number concentrations, size distributions, and cloud
 forming properties. Both findings have important implications for observational and
 modeling aerosol-convection interactions studies.
 During the field campaign, we also fortuitously observed the passage of multiple outflow
 boundaries which often generated secondary convection. Large shifts in the
 thermodynamic environments, aerosol populations and their CCN/INP activity were
 observed with the passage of these boundaries. This suggests that secondary convection
 initiated along the boundaries may experience a significantly different environment than
 the parent storm that generated the outflow. The results of this analysis were just accepted
 for publication in Thompson et al. (2025c).
 2. Develop vertical profiles of CCN and INP in different airmasses across sea/bay breeze fronts
 The vertical profile retrieval was developed, and the methodology and results were published
 in Chen et al. (2025a). These retrieved profiles are being used as the input aerosol profiles
 for TRACER ‘golden day’ simulations selected by the broader community for the TRACER
 Model Intercomparison Project (MIP; Fan et al. 2024). Beyond the profile retrievals in
 different airmasses, our team has also worked toward parameterizing the vertical profiles
 based on boundary layer characteristics as a first step toward using more widely available
 thermodynamic profile measurements as a predictor for aerosol profiles. A second manuscript
 (Chen et al. 2026) describing this work is in final editing stages and is planned for
 submission in early 2026.
 3. Determine how aerosol concentrations and inflow layers relate to changes in observed
 convection updraft size, intensity, and precipitation

 3

 The relationship between the environment and convection parameters using our
 measurements combined with ARM and KHGX radar observations was published in Sharma
 et al. (2024). We found significant thermodynamic and aerosol differences across the sea
 breeze front in the maritime and continental airmass. Radar reflectivity was significantly
 different across shallow cells in the two airmasses, with higher reflectivity in the cleaner and
 moister maritime airmass. For deep convection, cells initiated in the maritime airmass were
 wider than the continental, with significantly different boundary layer thermodynamic
 properties and weaker impacts from lateral entrainment. For deep cells, only those initiated at
 the sea breeze front had higher reflectivity. This suggests the importance of the sea breeze
 boundary itself for determining the intensity of convection and laid the foundation for our
 team’s future work.
 4. Use measured vertical CCN/INP, thermodynamic, and kinematic profiles in numerical
 simulations to disentangle the effects of meteorological conditions from ACI on deep
 convective updraft and precipitation characteristics.
 The observed aerosol, thermodynamic, and kinematic profiles collected during our project
 were used to drive numerical simulations aimed at disentangling ACI in deep convection.
 Accomplishments toward this objective are presented in a recently accepted Geophysical
 Research Letters paper Sharma et al. (2025), where we showed that ensemble simulations are
 necessary to disentangle aerosol from meteorological effects on updraft and precipitation
 characteristics. The simulations have been performed and drafts of two manuscripts for
 submission detailing the impacts of different aspects of the meteorological environment and
 aerosol characteristics on deep convection are expected for completion in early 2026. Results
 from these simulations are described in more detail in the Modeling Activities section below.

In summary, each of the objectives was met and goals were exceeded during the project.
Accomplishments related to each objective have been documented in numerous presentations
and peer-reviewed publications from our team and are listed in the Products Developed under the
Award section below.

Project Activity Summary

The project activities are briefly summarized below by their respective reporting periods.

September 2020-August 2021: Because the TRACER intensive operation period was delayed
due to COVID-19, the team spent this reporting period actively involved in TRACER planning
activities for forecasting and radiosonde operations. We also participated in practice forecasting
exercises with the broader TRACER community. In preparation for project objective 2, Co-PI
Brooks and two graduate students deployed the lidar along with instruments on a tethered
balloon to measure profiles of aerosol size distribution for refining scattering calculations and
preliminary work on the retrievals of vertical CCN/INP profiles planned for application to
measurements collected during the intensive operations period (IOP). The team also began
identifying potential deployment sites for operations during the intensive operation period
planned for 2022. Team members gave virtual oral and poster presentations and participated as
an invited precipitation workshop panelist.

 4

September 2021- August 2022: During this reporting period, the team focused on preparation
activities for the field deployment. We worked to coordinate radiosonde operations with the
broader TRACER and NSF ESCAPE teams. Team members continued to participate in joint
observation-modeling efforts, as well as forecast planning meetings and forecasting efforts for
the TRACER-Air Quality (AQ) campaign. We also developed a course and trained
undergraduate and graduate students in aerosol-cloud interactions, relevant meteorological and
aerosol instrumentation, sea breeze forecasting, and radiosonde launch procedures at no cost to
project. Many of these students ultimately participated in forecasting and deployment activities
for the main IOP. Deployment schedules, TAMU TRACER radiosonde deployment standard
operating procedures, templates for TAMU TRACER forecasting, daily operations plans, log
sheets, and deployment summaries were developed. In preparation for the IOP, Co-I Brooks’
team designed, developed, integrated and tested laboratory-grade instruments for the new Rapid
Onsite Atmospheric Measurement Van (ROAM-V) and installed samplers for INP measurements
at the ARM sites.

The main TRACER IOP occurred during June-September 2022, during which the TAMU team
had 22 joint radiosonde/aerosol deployments, 7 radiosonde-only, and 10 aerosol-only where we
sampled continental, maritime, convective outflow, outflow recovery, and post frontal airmasses
on the opposite side of the sea- and bay-breeze fronts from the ARM sites. This was a large effort
that involved coordinating 18 undergraduates (paid, course credit, and volunteers), 13 graduate
students (paid and volunteers), and 2 postdocs participating in forecasting, nowcasting, and
deployment teams. Members of the TAMU team also contributed heavily to the broader
TRACER community forecasting efforts.

September 2022-August 2023: The TAMU team continued field deployments in the early part of
the reporting period through September 2022. After the IOP, the team focused on data quality
control and processing for submission to the ARM archive. A postdoctoral researcher focused on
radiosonde quality control and identification of the airmass in which the TAMU soundings were
launched. The Brooks research group quality-controlled the aerosol number concentrations, size
distributions, CCN data, MPL backscatter and depolarization measurements for submission to
the ARM archive. All datasets submitted to the ARM Archive are listed under Table 1 in the
Data Products section. PI Rapp led the drafting and submission of a peer-reviewed manuscript
that gave an overview of our project, the deployment strategy we used to help better understand
aerosol-convection interactions, and that highlighted the datasets available from project.

Under the supervision of co-investigator Brooks, the graduate students on this project analyzed
samples for ice nucleation experiments to measure the ice nucleating temperatures from the
TAMU site and ARM sites. They also began analysis to quantify the ability of aerosols to serve
as CCN and INP in the different airmasses observed during TRACER and to compare our mobile
measurements to those at the ARM sites for Objective 1. A preliminary version of the vertical
aerosol profile retrieval was tested by combining surface CCN measurements with the lidar
profile measurements for Objective 2.

Working with Rapp and Nowotarski, a postdoctoral researcher implemented convective cell
tracking using the PyFLEXTRKR algorithm for both the ARM radar and National Weather
Service radar data. We identified the sea breeze evolution, assigned convective cells to their

 5

respective airmass based on the location relative to the sea breeze front, and matched their
corresponding environment (maritime, continental, at sea breeze front). We analyzed these data
to understand differences in cell properties such as size, echo top height, max reflectivity,
reflectivity and differential reflectivity profiles, and precipitation to address Objective 3. The
postdoctoral researcher began drafting the manuscript with results from this analysis which was
ultimately submitted during the next reporting period.

September 2023-August 2024: During this reporting year, project participants focused on
finalizing analysis of the TAMU and ARM site in situ and remotely-sensed observational data. A
graduate student in the Brooks group completed analysis of aerosols and their cloud forming
properties and performed comparisons between the sites for different airmasses. The team began
reparation of draft manuscripts documenting these results. Another graduate student performed
sensitivity studies to determine the effects of hygroscopic growth on aerosol profile retrievals
and ultimately completed implementation of a correction to the lidar aerosol profile retrieval to
account for the increases in lidar backscatter for aerosol water uptake in ambient humidity
conditions. Case studies comparing the ARM lidar to the TAMU lidar and between
measurements in two different locations with different emission sources were performed. A
manuscript draft documenting these results was prepared and ultimately submitted in the
following reporting period.

Mentored by Rapp and Nowotarski, the postdoctoral researcher submitted, revised, and
published the manuscript describing the differences in isolated convection properties for cells
initiating in different airmass regimes (Sharma et al. 2024). Based on our early findings on the
differences between the maritime and continental environments, the postdoc began testing use of
our observations as input to WRF model simulations of isolated convection. Multiple
thermodynamic, kinematic, and aerosol profiles were tested as initial conditions to determine
which of the TAMU TRACER days were candidates for simulation. Once cases were identified,
preliminary simulations were performed to identify best practices, tests sensitivities to model
assumptions, and refine analysis techniques. This work laid the foundation for the postdoctoral
researcher’s work in the following report period.

The TAMU team also submitted, revised, and published a paper led by PI Rapp describing the
TAMU TRACER campaign in the Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society (Rapp et al.
2024). During this period, group members also contributed datasets and prepared and edited text
for collaborative peer-reviewed manuscript submissions and community-led model simulation
efforts (Lamer et al. 2024, Fan et al. 2024, Jensen et al. 2025)

September 2024-August 2025: During the extension year of this project, the team focused on
completing all proposed data analysis, using the collected dataset for unexpected applications,
and completing all model simulations. Team members also drafted, submitted, revised, and
ultimately published seven papers during the final reporting period. A graduate student in the
Brooks research group finalized the vertical CCN/INP profile retrieval and published manuscript
describing the technique and demonstrating its application TRACER cases (Chen et al. 2025a).
This student also analyzed the relationship between boundary layer characteristics and aerosol
vertical profiles in attempts to develop parameterizations that could be used when only
atmospheric profile measurements, but no aerosol measurements available. This work is in final

 6

stages of editing for submission (Chen et al. 2026). Another graduate student in the Brooks lab
also led two papers with findings on the analysis of TRACER CCN (Thompson et al. 2025b) and
INP (Thompson et al. 2025a) measurements, as well as aerosol variations associated with the
passage of convective outflow boundaries (Thompson et al. 2025c). The graduate students
performing this work both successfully defended their dissertations for their Ph.D. degree and
obtained employment at a federal lab and another university.

The postdoctoral researcher working with Rapp and Nowotarski on this project used the
observed aerosol, thermodynamic, and kinematic profile measurements from our project to drive
an extensive suite of numerical simulations aimed at disentangling aerosol-convection
interactions in deep convection. Sensitivity experiments were performed to determine how large
of an ensemble is necessary to distinguish aerosol effects on convective updraft intensity from
random initial condition perturbations. This work was recently accepted for publication (Sharma
et al. 2025). Knowing the required ensemble size, model experiments were performed pairing the
different maritime and continental airmass thermodynamic environments with different aerosol
distributions to determine how they influence intensity, microphysics, and precipitation.
Additional simulations were run to test the sensitivity to different aerosol parameters and model
configurations. All simulations are completed, and two peer-reviewed journal submissions
(Sharma et al. 2026a, b) are currently in final phases of preparation but were not submitted
before this report. The details of these simulations are described in the Modeling Activities
section. The postdoc also performed additional simulations for the TRACER ‘golden days’ and
submitted the output files to the TRACER MIP.

Modeling activities

During the TAMU TRACER field campaign, Texas A&M University collected in situ aerosol
measurements that included aerosol size distribution, number concentration, and hygroscopicity.
Chen et al. (2025) describe how these measurements were merged across multiple instruments
sampling different size ranges, then combined with mini micropulse lidar-derived normalized
relative backscatter and relative humidity profiles to produce vertically resolved aerosol
concentration profiles. These aerosol vertical profiles, together with observed temperature and
moisture profiles from radiosondes, formed the initial conditions for the modeling work
summarized here.

We carried out idealized simulations of an isolated deep convective cell using Weather Research
and Forecasting (WRF V4.6.0) model at 500 m horizontal grid spacing. These simulations used
the fast spectral bin microphysics scheme that allows time-integrated growth of hydrometeor size
distribution across 33 bins and aerosol size distribution across 43 bins. The overall goal of these
simulations was to separate the influence of background thermodynamic environment from
aerosol characteristics on the evolution of convective cell microphysics and dynamics.
Convection was initiated using a warm-bubble method with a 3 K potential temperature
perturbation. The model configuration used for these simulations is described in Table 2.
Because small perturbations in initial conditions can lead to diverging solutions, we first
evaluated the ensemble size needed to capture a stable mean solution. Sharma et al. (2025)
shows that a 10-member ensemble adequately represents the convergent evolution of the
convective cell, so all subsequent analyses use this ensemble size. The key findings of this study
are as follows:
 (a) Random initial temperature perturbations can rival aerosol effects on updrafts, risking
 misattribution in deterministic simulations.
 (b) For TRACER-like environments, at least 10 ensemble members are needed to limit
 stochastic variability in updraft speed and supersaturation.
 (c) Future studies should move beyond deterministic runs and adopt ensemble frameworks to
 robustly quantify aerosol-induced updraft invigoration.
To quantify the relative influence of thermodynamic environment and aerosols, we selected two
observed soundings representing maritime and continental airmasses across the sea and bay
breezes. We paired each sounding with three aerosol profiles: a maritime aerosol population
(maritime control run), a continental aerosol population (continental control run), and a cleaner
maritime profile (lowest total aerosol concentration). This experimental design allowed us to test
how thermodynamic variability and aerosol characteristics influence updraft strength, updraft
supersaturation, cloud and rainwater content, ice hydrometeor mixing ratios, and accumulated
precipitation. All simulations shared the same initial horizontal wind profile to limit differences

We then performed a second group of sensitivity experiments focused on individual aerosol and
model parameters:
 (i) Aerosol hygroscopicity – varied from the minimum to maximum observed values (four
 scenarios, including the control).
 (ii) Model grid spacing – compared the baseline 500 m configuration with a 100 m grid.
 (iii) Microphysics parameterization – compared bin microphysics (control) with the NSSL
 three-moment bulk scheme.
 (iv) Total aerosol concentration – scaled total aerosol concentrations to 0.3x and 3x,
 compared to the observed values.
 (v) Aerosol vertical profile – replaced the observed vertically varying profile with uniform
 aerosol concentration profiles.
These tests were run as 10-member ensembles for both the maritime and continental control
environments. Preliminary results suggest that the choice of model configuration strongly impacts
the strength and range of aerosol-convection interaction effects. We are still evaluating the
qualitative behavior of these sensitivities and will identify which factor exerts the strongest
influence on the convective cell characteristics. These results will guide the design of future
aerosol-convection interaction studies and highlight which elements of model setup and
configuration must be controlled to ensure interpretable and consistent findings from numerical
simulations. A second manuscript detailing these findings is also in preparation for submission to
the Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres.
