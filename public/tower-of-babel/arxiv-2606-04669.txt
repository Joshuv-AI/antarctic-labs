SoK: Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) Implementation in Software Systems
R.D.N. Shakya, C.P. Wijesiriwardana, S.M. Vidanagamachchi, Nalin A.G. Arachchilage

Abstract
The transition to Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) is essential to protect software systems from emerging quantum-enabled threats. Although standardised PQC algorithms are now available, developers and organisations continue to face significant challenges in integrating them into real-world software systems due to integration complexity, limited developer expertise, and insufficient organisational readiness. While existing studies primarily focus on cryptographic performance and algorithmic security, it provides limited understanding of the broader socio-technological factors that influence successful PQC implementation. This Systematisation of Knowledge (SoK) investigates PQC implementation approaches and challenges through the Human, Organisation, and Technology (HOT) dimensions. By systematically synthesising and categorising existing approaches across these dimensions, we reveal a notable imbalance in the current body of knowledge, where technological solutions dominate, while human and organisational considerations remain underexplored. Our analysis further shows that PQC implementation challenges are not isolated to individual dimensions; rather, they emerge as interconnected socio-technological constraints that span HOT contexts, collectively shaping implementation outcomes. These findings indicate that PQC implementation extends beyond cryptographic replacement and represents a broader socio-technological transformation requiring coordinated approaches across all HOT dimensions. To address this gap, we propose the PQC-HOT model, a conceptual framework that explains how interactions among HOT dimensions collectively influence PQC implementation in software. The model synthesises the implementation interventions and challenges identified in the SoK into an integrated structure that supports systematic decision-making, planning, and organisational transition strategies. Based on these insights, we outline future research directions and design implications for scalable and sustainable PQC implementation in software systems.

Introduction

Quantum computing (QC) represents a transformative advance in computational paradigms, utilising principles of quantum mechanics to surpass the limitations of classical computing
[
15
]
. Its ability to solve computationally intractable problems at exceptional speeds has significant implications in computer science
[
15
,
25
]
. In particular, QC introduces a fundamental disruption to existing cryptographic systems, as quantum algorithms can efficiently solve mathematical problems that underpin widely deployed encryption schemes
[
22
]
. For example, when a user logs into an online banking system, the connection is commonly protected by encryption, using cryptographic schemes such as RSA (Rivest-Shamir-Adleman). These schemes rely on mathematical problems that are extremely difficult for today’s classical computers to solve within a practical time-frame. A classical computer might take several thousand years to break such encryption. However, quantum algorithms such as Shor’s algorithm could dramatically reduce this time, allowing a sufficiently powerful quantum computer to recover the secret encryption key and potentially expose sensitive information such as passwords or financial transactions
[
15
]
. Similarly, Grover’s algorithm can significantly speed up attacks against symmetric encryption by reducing the effort required to search for encryption keys
[
15
,
22
]
. Collectively, these developments indicate that emerging quantum capabilities pose a credible threat to widely adopted cryptographic standards, including RSA-2048, highlighting the urgent need to transition to quantum-resistant cryptographic solutions
[
15
,
22
]
. In response, Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) has emerged as a solution aimed at developing cryptographic schemes that remain secure against both classical and quantum adversaries
[
15
,
22
]
.

Although PQC algorithms provide strong theoretical security guarantees, their secure implementation in real-world software systems remains a significant challenge
[
15
]
. In practice, cryptographic security depends not only on the strength of the underlying algorithm, but also on how well it is implemented, configured, and integrated into software
[
34
]
. Prior studies have consistently demonstrated that secure cryptographic primitives can become vulnerable due to implementation flaws, insecure parametrisation, side-channel leakage, or incorrect usage by developers
[
34
,
10
,
49
]
. For example, weaknesses in key management or incorrect integration of cryptographic libraries can expose sensitive systems despite the use of theoretically secure algorithms
[
34
]
. Therefore, achieving robust post-quantum security requires both sound cryptographic design and rigorously engineered implementation practices within software systems
[
52
]
.

These challenges are further intensified in the PQC context. Compared to classical cryptographic primitives such as RSA, PQC schemes often introduce significantly larger key sizes, increased computational overhead, and higher memory requirements, which can stress existing software architectures and system constraints
[
28
,
46
]
. Furthermore, PQC integration must occur within legacy systems that were not designed to accommodate such computational characteristics, creating compatibility and performance trade-offs that developers must carefully manage
[
9
,
26
]
. At the same time, tooling ecosystems, implementation standards, and developer expertise remain immature, limiting the availability of reliable guidance for secure implementation
[
9
,
32
]
. Consequently, software developers play a critical role in ensuring the security of PQC deployments, as incorrect configurations, insecure coding practices, or misunderstandings of PQC mechanisms can introduce vulnerabilities into otherwise quantum-resistant systems
[
10
,
9
,
26
]
.

Despite these practical concerns, existing research on PQC has focused primarily on algorithm design, security proofs, and performance benchmarking
[
28
]
. While these contributions are essential, they provide limited insight for software developers into how PQC is implemented, deployed, and maintained within real-world systems. Consequently, current practice is often characterised by fragmented implementation, experimental integrations, and ad-hoc usage of cryptographic libraries, which increase the risk of misconfiguration and insecure deployments
[
26
]
. In response, prior studies have proposed implementation frameworks, guidelines, tooling support, and educational interventions to facilitate implementation
[
28
,
32
]
. However, these efforts remain isolated, addressing narrow aspects of the problem without establishing a unified perspective on how technological, human, and organisational dimensions collectively shape PQC implementation outcomes.

This fragmentation suggests that PQC is not solely a cryptographic transition, but a socio-technological transformation problem. The successful implementation of PQC depends on the interaction between software architectures, developer practices, organisational governance, and operational requirements. However, existing studies typically examine these dimensions in isolation, resulting in a limited understanding of their interdependencies and combined impact on secure implementation. Consequently, the current body of knowledge lacks an integrated synthesis of PQC implementation approaches, their relationships, limitations, and the broader socio-technological barriers that hinder secure and scalable deployment. This gap restricts both researchers and practitioners in developing a comprehensive understanding of the current implementation landscape and in identifying effective strategies for robust PQC implementation into real-world systems.

Therefore, a Systematisation of Knowledge (SoK) study is necessary to synthesise existing approaches, critically examine their limitations, and provide a structured foundation for advancing secure and practical PQC implementation in software engineering contexts. To systematically examine the current state of PQC implementation research, this study addresses the following research questions (RQs):

•

RQ1: What methodologies, frameworks, guidelines, tools, and educational interventions have been proposed or developed to facilitate the implementation of Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) in software?

•

RQ2: What challenges and limitations arise when applying these methodologies, frameworks, guidelines, tools, and educational interventions to implement PQC in software?

To address these RQs, this paper presents a SoK on PQC implementation in software systems. Unlike traditional surveys that primarily summarise prior studies, this work provides a structured and critical synthesis of existing implementation approaches and their associated challenges. The study is grounded in the observation that many barriers to effective PQC implementation arise from misalignments across human, organisational, and technological dimensions. Accordingly, this work adopts the Human-Organisation-Technology (HOT) perspective
[
13
]
to systematically analyse the implementation approaches and their challenges and introduces the PQC-HOT model. The proposed model conceptualises PQC implementation as a socio-technological system in which cryptographic mechanisms, developer capabilities, and organisational processes jointly determine implementation security outcomes. Building on this perspective, the study identifies key implementation challenges, synthesises cross-cutting limitations across existing approaches, and derives a structured research agenda to guide future investigations. Overall, this work positions PQC implementation not as a collection of isolated technological solutions, but as an integrated socio-technological engineering problem requiring coordinated advances across cryptography, software engineering, and organisational practice.

Based on this foundation, this study makes the following contributions:

•

We develop a socio-technological synthesis of PQC implementation approaches by mapping existing frameworks, tools, guidelines, and educational interventions onto the Human, Organisation, and Technology (HOT) perspective, thus exposing structural imbalances in current research and practice.

•

We introduce the PQC-HOT model, a conceptual framework that explains the interdependencies between human, organisational, and technological factors in the implementation of PQC within software systems.

•

We construct a layered classification of PQC implementation challenges, revealing their distribution across technological, organisational, and human dimensions, thereby extending prior fragmented categorisations.

•

We derive a consolidated research agenda grounded in identified gaps, providing actionable directions to advance effective and practice-oriented PQC implementation in software ecosystems.

3.3
Human, Organisational, and Technological (HOT) Analysis

To systematically synthesise the findings, we adopt the Human, Organisational, and Technological (HOT) framework
[
13
]
. The transition from classical cryptography to PC extends beyond the replacement of cryptographic algorithms. It represents a socio-technical transformation that affects software engineering practices, developer competencies, organisational processes, and governance mechanisms
[
34
,
10
,
36
]
.

The HOT framework provides a suitable lens for analysing this multifaceted transition by categorising implementation factors into three interrelated dimensions. The
Human (H)
dimension focuses on developer knowledge, skills, training, and support mechanisms that influence the secure implementation of PQC. The
Organisational (O)
dimension includes governance, policies, planning, resource allocation, and organisational readiness that facilitate or constrain PQC implementation. The
Technological (T)
dimension covers PQC algorithms, software libraries, implementation frameworks, engineering methodologies, testing approaches, and supporting tools required for integrating PQC into software.

Using this framework, the identified approaches and challenges reported in the literature were mapped to the corresponding HOT dimensions. This classification enables a structured synthesis of the current state of PQC implementation in software and provides a comprehensive perspective for addressing the RQs.

3.4
Threats to Validity

Following the guidelines of Kitchenham and Charters
[
33
]
, we acknowledge potential threats to validity across standard SLR dimensions.

•

Search and Selection Validity:
We employ a systematic search strategy with explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria; however, we cannot eliminate the risk of omitting relevant studies. This limitation arises from database coverage constraints and indexing inconsistencies. Selection bias may also occur during screening. To mitigate these risks, we apply forward and backward snowballing, iteratively refine search queries, and document the selection process transparently.

•

Data Extraction and Researcher Bias:
Subjective interpretation may introduce bias during study selection, data extraction, and thematic synthesis. To reduce this risk, we follow a structured data extraction protocol, apply reflexive thematic analysis, and perform iterative cross-checking and peer validation to improve consistency and reliability.

•

Publication Bias:
By restricting the review to peer-reviewed, English-language publications, we introduce a bias toward well-established or positive results while excluding grey literature and non-English studies. This constraint may limit coverage and under-represent emerging or industry practices.

•

External Validity and Practical Applicability:
We synthesise findings from existing literature without independently validating them through expert or practitioner engagement. Although such validation could strengthen practical relevance, it falls outside the scope defined (refer Table
2
). Therefore, we present the findings as a structured synthesis of current knowledge rather than an empirical validation of practice.

4
Results

This section presents a structured synthesis of the reviewed literature and addresses the RQs through a systematic analysis of PQC implementation in software development. First, it examines and categorises existing approaches relevant to PQC implementation (RQ1). Next, it analyses the key challenges and limitations associated with these approaches (RQ2). The identified approaches and challenges are subsequently mapped to the Human, Organisational, and Technological (HOT) dimensions to provide a socio-technological perspective of PQC implementation. Overall, the findings reveal a fragmented and weakly integrated ecosystem in which current approaches offer limited support for secure, scalable, and practically deployable PQC implementation in real-world software systems.

4.1
RQ1: PQC Implementation Approaches in Software Systems

This section synthesises existing approaches supporting PQC implementation in software, addressing RQ1 as illustrated in Figure
3
. The literature identifies four categories: guidelines (G1), frameworks (F1–F3), tools and libraries (T1–T4), and educational interventions (E1–E2).

Figure 3:
Overview of PQC implementation approaches identified in the literature (RQ1)

4.1.1
Guidelines (G1)

Guidelines are structured recommendations that define principles, practices, and procedures to support the secure design, implementation, and deployment of software systems
[
42
]
.

Existing guidelines for PQC design and implementation (G1) focus mainly on the mathematical and cryptographic foundations of PQC algorithms
[
29
]
, highlighting formal security proofs, correctness guarantees, and hardness assumptions. For example, lattice-based problems such as Learning with Errors (LWE) and Short Integer Solution (SIS) are frequently recommended as foundational design principles for PQC algorithms if the algorithm is lattice-based
[
29
]
. Integrating these paradigms into the PQC design and development process enables practitioners to reduce vulnerabilities and establish robust, quantum-resistant cryptographic systems. These guidelines are primarily situated within the technological dimension of the HOT framework.

4.1.2
Frameworks (F1–F3)

Frameworks are structured architectural or methodological systems that enable the integration, deployment, and management of cryptographic mechanisms within software and security infrastructures
[
48
]
. PQC frameworks structure the integration and deployment of PQC mechanisms within software systems. These frameworks include integration frameworks (F1), certification authority extensions (F2), and testing and evaluation frameworks (F3), all of which operate mainly within the technological dimension.

Integration frameworks (F1) provide a structured model for the systematic implementation and deployment of PQC. These frameworks either adopt hybrid cryptographic strategies that combine classical and PQC algorithms to support transitional security during migration phases
[
27
,
20
]
, or propose novel architectures designed specifically for fully quantum-resilient systems
[
38
]
. By facilitating interoperability between classical and post-quantum mechanisms, integration frameworks support a gradual and secure transition toward quantum-safe infrastructures. Certification authority frameworks (F2) focus on extending existing public key infrastructure (PKI) models to accommodate PQC algorithms within certificate generation, validation, and revocation processes. These frameworks preserve trust relationships and authentication mechanisms in quantum-resilient environments while ensuring compatibility with evolving cryptographic standards
[
50
]
. Finally, testing and evaluation frameworks (F3) emphasise the verification and assessment of PQC implementations before deployment. They provide mechanisms to evaluate correctness, robustness, and performance, thereby enabling developers and organisations to identify implementation weaknesses and operational limitations before integrating PQC solutions into production systems
[
32
,
53
,
51
]
.

4.1.3
Tools and Libraries (T1–T4)

Tools and libraries are software artefacts that provide practical capabilities for implementing, testing, evaluating, migrating, and operationalising different functions within software systems
[
7
,
44
]
. Tools and libraries for PQC enable developers to implement, evaluate, test, migrate, and enhance PQC algorithms and their implementations within software systems. These tools represent the most operationally relevant category of PQC approaches because they provide mechanisms for implementation activities. They include implementation and enhancement libraries (T1), embedded libraries and protocol plugins (T2), testing and validation tools (T3), and migration and transition tools (T4). These tools and libraries are primarily positioned within the technological dimension of the HOT analysis.

Implementation and enhancement libraries (T1) provide foundational support for developing and integrating PQC algorithms into software applications. These include hardness estimator libraries
[
24
]
, which assess the computational difficulty and resilience of PQC schemes against both classical and quantum adversaries, as well as general-purpose PQC libraries that either introduce standalone PQC implementations
[
45
]
or extend existing cryptographic libraries with quantum-resistant capabilities
[
28
,
4
]
. Collectively, these libraries enable developers to experiment with, evaluate, and deploy PQC mechanisms within software environments. Embedded libraries and protocol plugins (T2) further extend PQC implementation by integrating quantum-resistant functionality into privacy-preserving systems and secure communication protocols. Embedded privacy libraries
[
35
]
employ PQC algorithms to support mechanisms such as zero-knowledge proofs and succinct proofs, thus strengthening data confidentiality and integrity against quantum-enabled threats. In parallel, protocol plugins
[
14
,
31
,
3
]
incorporate PQC algorithms into secure communication infrastructures, including VPNs and tunnelling protocols, to enhance end-to-end security and facilitate compatibility with existing network architectures. Testing and validation tools (T3) focus on assessing the correctness, robustness, and performance of PQC implementations before deployment. These tools play a critical role in ensuring the reliability and trustworthiness of PQC-enabled systems by systematically evaluating security properties, interoperability, and computational efficiency
[
32
]
. For example, PQClean
[
32
]
provides a structured framework for validating and benchmarking PQC implementations before their integration into production environments. Finally, migration and transition tools (T4) are designed to support the secure implementation of PQC within existing software ecosystems. These tools assist developers in identifying quantum-related vulnerabilities in legacy systems and deployed applications. For instance, the QED tool
[
43
]
detects quantum-vulnerable cryptographic components in executables and binary files.

4.1.4
Educational Interventions (E1–E2)

Educational interventions are structured learning and training approaches designed to develop the knowledge, skills, and competencies required for understanding and application
[
5
]

5.1
Fragmentation of the Ecosystem of PQC Implementation Approaches

identified the dominant categories of approaches proposed in the literature to support the implementation of PQC in software, including guidelines, frameworks, tools and libraries, and educational interventions. While these approaches represent current efforts to facilitate PQC implementation, they remain fragmented and insufficiently integrated to support secure and scalable implementation in practice.

The synthesis reveals that the current PQC implementation ecosystem is constrained by several systemic limitations that extend beyond individual approaches. These limitations collectively hinder the secure, scalable, and maintainable implementation of PQC in software systems.

First, existing interventions provide limited end-to-end support across the Software Development Lifecycle. No single approach comprehensively addresses the full implementation process, spanning design, development, integration, testing, deployment, and long-term maintenance. Consequently, developers are often required to combine fragmented resources and independently bridge implementation gaps, increasing cognitive complexity, integration overhead, and the likelihood of inconsistent or insecure implementations.

Second, many proposed approaches assume ideal implementation environments and fail to sufficiently account for practical deployment constraints. Existing studies rarely consider legacy system dependencies, organisational limitations, performance overheads, resource constraints, or variations in developer expertise. As a result, the applicability of many proposed solutions within production-scale software environments remains limited.

Third, the lack of practical and developer-oriented implementation guidance. Widely available resources, including standards documents, implementation handbooks, and technical guidelines, are mostly theoretical and frequently written at a highly abstract level using dense technical terminology
[

]
. These resources often provide limited executable examples, implementation patterns, or empirically validated case studies. Consequently, developers face difficulties translating theoretical guidance into secure, reliable, and maintainable implementations.

The analysis further reveals the absence of standardised software engineering practices tailored specifically for PQC implementation. Current literature provides limited guidance on critical engineering concerns such as algorithm selection, secure API design, abstraction mechanisms, coding standards, implementation validation, and migration planning. Similarly, implementation requirements, such as constant-time execution, memory-safe programming, and leakage-resistant coding practices, remain insufficiently operationalised within software engineering workflows. In the absence of structured development practices, developers frequently rely on ad hoc implementation strategies, increasing the risk of misuse, misconfiguration, and implementation vulnerabilities.

These technological limitations are further intensified by the lack of mature governance frameworks and organisational methodologies for PQC implementation. Existing research provides minimal discussion of PQC-specific Software Development Lifecycle (SDLC) models, threat modelling approaches, vulnerability assessment procedures, or risk management frameworks. Moreover, no widely implemented maturity models comparable to Capability Maturity Model Integration (CMMI)

The review also identified limited attention to usability and developer experience. Existing research mainly prioritises mathematical soundness and cryptographic strength, while comparatively less emphasis is placed on usability, ease of integration, developer workflows, and adoption. There is little empirical investigation into how developers interact with PQC tools or implementation guidance in real-world settings

Similarly, the role of Artificial Intelligence in facilitating PQC implementation remains largely underexplored. AI-driven approaches could potentially support activities such as algorithm selection, automated configuration analysis, vulnerability detection, performance optimisation, and implementation validation. However, research in this area remains limited, despite its potential to improve the efficiency and reliability of the PQC implementation.

Finally, although considerable progress has been made in PQC algorithm development and standardisation, significantly less attention has been given to practical adoption strategies and organisational readiness. As a result, PQC implementation within real-world software systems remains fragmented, uncertain, and largely experimental.

Therefore, RQ1 derives a crucial insight:
PQC implementation is constrained not by the absence of individual solutions, but by the absence of integration among existing solutions.

Addressing this limitation requires a paradigm shift from isolated, component-level innovations toward integrated, lifecycle-aware, and developer-centric ecosystems that integrate technological capabilities (T), organisational readiness (O), and human capability development (H) to enable sustainable PQC implementation in practice.

5.2
Systemic Nature of PQC Implementation Challenges

The findings of RQ2 demonstrate that the challenges associated with the implementation of PQC extend far beyond isolated technological difficulties. Instead, they emerge from structural misalignments across the Human, Organisational, and Technological (HOT) dimensions, where weaknesses in one dimension frequently propagate into others and generate compounded socio-technological challenges. Examining these challenges through the HOT framework reveals that PQC implementation is not simply a cryptographic transition, but a broader socio-technological transformation involving software engineering practices, tooling ecosystems, organisational governance, and human capability development. This perspective extends beyond prior research that mostly frames PQC implementation as a cryptographic or performance-oriented problem and instead positions the implementation challenge as a systemic and interconnected phenomenon.

A key insight from the analysis is that the main security challenges arise from implementation rather than from the algorithm design itself. Although PQC algorithms are grounded in mathematically secure foundations, their real-world security depends heavily on how they are implemented, integrated, and maintained within operational software systems. The challenges identified in Section
4.2.1
demonstrate that even minor weaknesses in coding practices, memory handling, optimisation strategies, parameter management, or cryptographic transformations can undermine formal security guarantees. In this regard, the findings reinforce a fundamental principle of applied cryptography: secure algorithms do not inherently produce secure systems
[
34
]
. Within PQC environments, this challenge is amplified by increased algorithmic complexity, larger parameter sizes, and heightened exposure to side-channel and fault-based attacks. Consequently, implementation quality becomes a critical determinant of overall system security.

Beyond implementation-level concerns, the findings indicate that PQC implementation fundamentally challenges existing software engineering and architectural practices. The system-level and lifecycle constraints discussed in Section
4.2.2
reveal that PQC implementation cannot be treated as a discrete cryptographic upgrade, but rather as a long-term lifecycle transformation involving migration planning, interoperability management, hybrid deployments, and continuous maintenance. However, many existing software systems lack cryptographic agility, while tightly coupled architectures complicate incremental migration and algorithm replacement. Furthermore, evolving standards and uncertain long-term cryptographic requirements introduce additional complexity into architectural decision-making. These findings suggest that standardisation alone is insufficient to ensure sustainable PQC deployment. Instead, effective implementation requires modular system architectures, flexible cryptographic interfaces, lifecycle-aware engineering practices, and forward-compatible software designs capable of adapting to future algorithmic and operational changes.

The analysis further demonstrates that tooling ecosystems significantly shape implementation security and reliability. The tooling and ecosystem risks identified in Section
4.2.3
show that fragmented libraries, inconsistent APIs, weak interoperability, immature validation mechanisms, and insufficient benchmarking support collectively increase implementation complexity and the possibility of deployment errors. In this context, tooling functions not merely as supporting infrastructure, but as a socio-technological risk amplifier capable of influencing both secure and insecure development practices. Poorly designed tools, insecure defaults, and low-level abstractions transfer much of the implementation burden onto developers, thereby increasing the probability of misconfiguration, unsafe integration practices, and incorrect API usage. Consequently, tooling maturity directly affects the trustworthiness, scalability, and operational resilience of PQC-enabled systems.

From an organisational perspective, the findings reveal that governance and institutional readiness represent critical prerequisites for successful PQC implementation. As discussed in Section
4.2.4
, many organisations remain insufficiently prepared to manage PQC migration as a large-scale technological and operational transformation. Existing research provides limited guidance regarding PQC-specific governance frameworks, organisational maturity models, or lifecycle-oriented risk management practices. At the same time, evolving standards, regulatory fragmentation, infrastructure constraints, and uncertainty surrounding the progression of quantum computing complicate long-term planning and investment decisions. The findings therefore suggest that PQC implementation requires not only technological reworking, but also structured governance mechanisms capable of supporting strategic planning, compliance management, resource allocation, workforce development, and continuous organisational adaptation.

The human dimension further demonstrates that developer capability and ecosystem support directly influence implementation security. The human-centric vulnerabilities identified in Section
4.2.5
reveal an important disconnect between theoretical cryptographic understanding and practical implementation expertise. Developers frequently encounter difficulties arising from unclear documentation, insufficient implementation examples, weak community support, and limited opportunities for practice-oriented training. These limitations increase cognitive burden and contribute to implementation errors, insecure coding practices, and unsafe deployment decisions. Importantly, the findings suggest that developers should not be viewed merely as users of cryptographic systems, but rather as integral components of the security boundary itself. Insufficient human capability, therefore, becomes a direct source of implementation risk.

Another significant insight is that PQC implementation risks rarely remain restricted to individual layers. As discussed in Section
4.2.6
, vulnerabilities frequently emerge through dynamic interactions across technological, organisational, and human dimensions. For example, weak tooling abstractions and poor API design may increase developer error rates, while insufficient organisational governance can further amplify inconsistent implementation practices. Similarly, uncertainty surrounding evolving standards can generate organisational hesitation, delayed migration planning, and reduced developer confidence. These interdependencies create cascading socio-technological risks that cannot be effectively mitigated through isolated interventions focused solely on cryptographic performance, optimisation, or standalone educational initiatives. Instead, the findings demonstrate that implementation failures often emerge through accumulated interactions across multiple layers of the ecosystem.

The analysis also introduces a forward-looking perspective on PQC implementation risk. The identified challenges should not be viewed as fixed vulnerabilities, but as evolving risks influenced by new cryptanalytic findings, changing standards, advances in quantum technologies, and evolving software ecosystems. In particular, the presence of latent and emerging vulnerabilities highlights the long-term uncertainty surrounding PQC deployment. This uncertainty extends across all HOT dimensions, affecting technological resilience, organisational planning, and human decision-making processes. Consequently, secure PQC implementation requires continuous monitoring, adaptive governance, iterative validation, and sustained capability development rather than one-time deployment activities.

Overall, the findings of RQ2 demonstrate that PQC implementation is fundamentally a socio-technological challenge shaped by deeply interconnected technological, organisational, and human factors. Secure implementation therefore cannot be achieved through isolated technological innovation alone. Instead, effective PQC implementation requires holistic, system-level strategies that integrate secure software engineering practices, organisational governance, ecosystem maturity, lifecycle-aware migration planning, and human capability development.

Therefore, RQ2 derives a central insight:
PQC implementation is fundamentally a socio-technological challenge in which interconnected risks across human, organisational, and technological dimensions collectively shape security outcomes.

5.3
The PQC-HOT Model: A Socio-Technological Framework for Secure PQC Implementation

Building on the synthesis of existing approaches (Section
4.1
) and the challenges identified in RQ2 (Section
4.2
), this study introduces the PQC-HOT model as a socio-technological framework for analysing secure PQC implementation. The model extends the Human-Organisational-Technological (HOT) perspective
[
13
]
by conceptualising PQC implementation as an interaction-driven process rather than a set of isolated activities.

The model comprises three interdependent dimensions. The
Technological
dimension includes PQC algorithms, implementations, system integration, and supporting toolchains. The
Human
dimension captures developer knowledge, behaviour, cognitive effort, and secure coding practices. The
Organisational
dimension includes governance structures, policies, resource allocation, and regulatory compliance mechanisms. Effective and secure PQC implementation depends on the alignment of these three dimensions.

A key idea of the model is that implementation risks emerge primarily from misalignments among these dimensions. For instance, complex or poorly designed tools (Technological) can increase the possibility of developer misuse (Human), while weak governance structures (Organisational) may fail to detect or mitigate such issues. Over time, these misalignments amplify system-level risk and create cascading vulnerabilities across the software ecosystem.

By capturing these interactions, the PQC-HOT model provides a foundation for analysing PQC implementation as a coordinated socio-technological process. It provides a structured basis for understanding how risks propagate across dimensions and supports the development of integrated interventions that jointly address technological robustness, human capability, and organisational readiness for secure and scalable PQC implementation.

5.3.1
Conceptual Overview of the PQC-HOT Model

The PQC-HOT model represents PQC implementation as a tightly coupled triad of Human (H), Organisational (O), and Technological (T) dimensions, as illustrated in Figure
5
. These dimensions are mutually dependent, and weaknesses in one dimension can directly affect the others.

Figure 5:
PQC-HOT socio-technological model for analysing Human, Organisational, and Technological interactions in PQC implementation in software systems

Each dimension is defined as follows:

•

Human (H):
Represents developer expertise, cognitive effort, usability considerations, decision-making processes, and secure coding practices. This dimension influences how effectively PQC mechanisms are understood, implemented, and maintained in practice.

•

Organisational (O):
Involves governance structures, policies, compliance requirements, resource allocation, and strategic decision-making. This dimension defines the institutional and operational context in which PQC implementation occurs.

•

Technological (T):
Includes technological approaches such as PQC algorithms, cryptographic libraries, implementation frameworks, system architectures, and tooling ecosystems. This dimension determines algorithmic correctness, interoperability, and system-level integration within software systems.

5.3.2
Interaction Dynamics

The Model defines the following primary interactions:

•

Technological
\leftrightarrow
Human (T–H):
This interaction captures the relationship between technological system complexity and developer capability. High algorithmic complexity, low-level APIs, or poorly designed tools increase cognitive burden and the probability of implementation errors. In contrast, improved usability, abstraction, and developer support reduce misconfiguration risks and enhance secure usage.

•

Technological
\leftrightarrow
Organisational (T–O):
This interaction reflects how technological constraints influence organisational decisions. Performance overheads, migration complexity, and interoperability limitations shape strategic planning, often resulting in ineffective deployments or delayed migration strategies.

•

Human
\leftrightarrow
Organisational (H–O):
This interaction captures the relationship between workforce capability and institutional readiness. Limited expertise and insufficient training reduce the effectiveness of organisational PQC strategies, while weak governance structures increase the likelihood of human error, inconsistent implementation, and insecure practices.

The PQC-HOT model emphasises that PQC implementation failures rarely originate from a single dimension. Instead, they emerge through interacting and reinforcing effects across dimensions, where local weaknesses escalate into system-wide risks. Accordingly, the model frames PQC implementation as a socio-technological alignment problem and provides a foundation for analysing risk propagation and designing integrated, cross-dimensional solutions.

5.4
Implications for Research and Practice

5.4.1
Research Agenda

The findings of this study demonstrate that current PQC implementation efforts remain fragmented, technologically isolated, and insufficiently aligned with practical software engineering processes. Advancing the field, therefore, requires a shift toward integrated, lifecycle-aware, and developer-centric research grounded in real-world deployment environments. Based on the identified gaps and socio-technological challenges, this study outlines the following research agenda across the Human, Organisational, and Technological dimensions of the PQC-HOT model.

•

Technological Dimension:
Future research must move beyond the design and evaluation of cryptographic primitives and address the broader engineering realities of PQC integration within operational software systems. Researchers should develop PQC-aware software architectures that support modularity, interoperability, and cryptographic agility, enabling systems to adapt to evolving standards and future algorithmic changes. Greater attention should also be given to secure-by-design abstractions and misuse-resistant APIs that reduce developer error and encapsulate implementation complexity. In parallel, research is also needed on formal verification, side-channel resistance analysis, performance benchmarking, and runtime assurance techniques capable of evaluating PQC implementations under realistic operational constraints.

•

Human Dimension:
The findings highlight developers as a critical component of the PQC security boundary. Consequently, future research should treat developer experience and human capability as primary design considerations rather than secondary usability concerns. Researchers should conduct empirical studies investigating cognitive load, implementation behaviour, usability barriers, and common error patterns associated with PQC libraries, APIs, and development workflows. These insights should inform the development of developer-centric and misuse-resistant PQC interfaces that provide clear abstractions, safe defaults, and implementation guidance. In addition, research should focus on practice-oriented educational environments, including hands-on laboratories, secure coding exercises, integration simulations, and adaptive learning environments tailored to PQC implementation. The effectiveness of such interventions should be evaluated using measurable outcomes such as secure coding behaviour, implementation quality, and long-term knowledge retention.

•

Organisational Dimension:
The organisational dimension remains significantly underexplored despite its critical role in enabling large-scale PQC implementation. Future research should develop governance frameworks that support decision-making throughout the software lifecycle, including algorithm selection, migration planning, implementation validation, compliance management, and long-term maintenance. Researchers should also design structured migration methodologies that support phased transitions, hybrid deployments, and interoperability with legacy systems. In addition, there is a need for organisational maturity models and readiness assessment frameworks that evaluate an organisation’s technological, operational, and human capability to adopt PQC effectively. Further investigation is also required into how regulatory requirements, industry standards, and compliance obligations influence PQC implementation strategies across different operational contexts.

•

Cross-Cutting Integration:
The findings demonstrate that PQC implementation is inherently socio-technological; therefore, future research must explicitly address interactions across the Human, Organisational, and Technological dimensions. Researchers should develop integrated, end-to-end frameworks that support the entire software lifecycle and operationalise the PQC-HOT model into measurable and deployable constructs. Future work should also focus on ecosystem-level solutions that integrate tooling, governance, training, and validation into interconnected environments that support both developers and organisations. Longitudinal empirical studies are needed to examine real-world adoption dynamics, including migration behaviour, ecosystem evolution, implementation sustainability, and cross-cutting risk propagation. At the same time, continuous cryptanalysis and implementation security research remain essential for identifying emerging vulnerabilities and informing adaptive implementation strategies.

•

AI-Augmented PQC Engineering:
AI represents an emerging opportunity for improving the security, scalability, and usability of PQC implementation. Future research should explore the use of AI-driven approaches for vulnerability detection, side-channel analysis, automated testing, performance optimisation, configuration validation, and anomaly detection. From a human-centric perspective, AI-based assistants may support secure coding practices, reduce developer cognitive burden, and provide adaptive implementation guidance. At the organisational level, AI techniques may further support migration planning, risk assessment, compliance monitoring, and operational decision-making. However, future research must also critically examine the trustworthiness, transparency, and security implications of integrating AI into PQC engineering workflows.

5.4.2
Design Implications

The findings of this SoK highlight several important implications for the design and deployment of PQC-enabled software systems.

First, PQC implementation should be approached as a system-level software engineering challenge rather than a standalone cryptographic upgrade. Second, future PQC systems should prioritize developer-centric design. Clear abstractions, secure defaults, usable APIs, and accessible implementation guidance are essential for reducing cognitive complexity and minimizing implementation errors. Third, cryptographic agility should be treated as a core architectural requirement. Systems must support adaptability, modular integration, and long-term maintainability in response to evolving standards and future cryptanalytic developments. Fourth, PQC mechanisms should be integrated into existing software engineering and security workflows, including automated testing, deployment validation, and continuous monitoring processes. Fifth, successful PQC implementation requires a stronger ecosystem and organisational support. This includes shared tooling resources, implementation standards, workforce training, governance structures, and long-term maintenance strategies. Finally, organisations should incorporate workforce capability development into PQC implementation strategies. Effective implementation requires continuous training, hands-on learning opportunities, and interdisciplinary skill development across cryptography, software engineering, and cybersecurity governance to ensure long-term operational readiness. A standardized certification pathway and structured professional training program, similar to the Certified Ethical Hacker
[
23
]
, should be established to enhance organizational readiness and improve the quality and consistency of implementation.

Overall, these findings suggest that sustainable PQC implementation depends on maintaining alignment across the Human, Organisational, and Technological dimensions of the PQC-HOT model.

6
Conclusion

This study analyses PQC implementation approaches and associated challenges through the Human, Organisational, and Technological (HOT) dimensions. It identifies key misalignments and interdependencies that shape the current state of PQC implementation in software systems.

The analysis of RQ1 (Section
4.1
) shows that existing PQC implementation efforts are mostly concentrated within the technological dimension, focusing on algorithmic robustness, tooling, and formal security properties. In contrast, comparatively limited attention is given to human and organisational dimensions. This imbalance suggests that PQC implementation is often treated as a purely technological enhancement rather than a socio-technological transformation. Consequently, technologically sound solutions may still fail in practice due to insufficient developer support, weak integration pathways, and limited organisational readiness.

The findings of RQ2 (Section
4.2
) demonstrate that PQC implementation challenges are distributed across all HOT dimensions and are inherently interdependent. Technological constraints such as algorithmic complexity and performance overhead influence developer behaviour and increase the possibility of implementation errors. At the same time, organisational limitations, including the absence of structured migration strategies, governance mechanisms, and resource allocation frameworks, constrain both technological deployment and human capability development. Furthermore, limited developer expertise and insufficient practical guidance amplify these difficulties. These interacting factors create reinforcing feedback loops in which weaknesses in one dimension propagate across others, ultimately undermining secure and scalable deployment.

To address these issues, this study proposes the PQC-HOT model, which conceptualises PQC implementation as a tightly coupled socio-technological system comprising Human, Organisational, and Technological dimensions. The model integrates the fragmentation identified in RQ1 with the challenges observed in RQ2, providing a structured lens for analysing dependencies and risk propagation in PQC implementation. The PQC-HOT model highlights that these dimensions are not independent layers but mutually dependent components of a single system. Effective PQC implementation therefore requires balanced progress across all dimensions. Technological advancements must be accompanied by usable tooling and developer support, while organisational readiness must be strengthened through clear governance structures and migration planning. Similarly, human capability development must be supported through training and practical exposure. This integrated perspective explains why isolated interventions, such as improvements in standards or cryptographic libraries alone, are insufficient to ensure successful real-world deployment.

From a research perspective, these findings highlight the need to move beyond technology-centric approaches toward socio-technological solutions that explicitly integrate human and organisational considerations. This includes the development of usable cryptographic abstractions, empirically validated developer support mechanisms, and frameworks for assessing organisational readiness and migration maturity. From a practice perspective, organisations should align technological design, workforce capability, and governance processes by embedding PQC into secure software development lifecycles, investing in developer training, and establishing structured migration strategies.

In conclusion, PQC implementation in software systems represents a socio-technological transformation rather than a purely cryptographic upgrade. The PQC-HOT model provides a structured foundation for understanding this complexity and guiding future research and practice. These findings emphasise the importance of integrating cryptographic innovation with software engineering and organisational strategy to enable sustainable and secure PQC implementation.
