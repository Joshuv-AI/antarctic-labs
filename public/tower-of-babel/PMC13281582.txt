Targeting the mevalonate pathway with statins overcomes acquired resistance to KRASG12C inhibitors in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Weihao Lin, Qi-an Chen, Yannan Yang, Yibo Gao, Jie He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe emergence of covalent KRASG12C inhibitors has revolutionized the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), yet acquired resistance remains a major clinical challenge. Here, we systematically identified statins as potent agents capable of overcoming acquired resistance to KRASG12C inhibitors through high-throughput screening of 1,971 FDA-approved compounds. Statin treatment preferentially induced cell death in resistant cells, markedly impairing tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo. Proteomic profiling and pathway analysis revealed upregulation of the mevalonate (MVA) pathway in resistant cells. Genetic silencing of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and pharmacologic inhibition of the MVA-geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGPP) branch recapitulated the effects of statins. Mechanistically, MVA-GGPP signaling promoted Yes-associated protein (YAP) activation, whereas disruption of this axis by statins or geranylgeranyl transferase I (GGTase-I) inhibitors impaired YAP-driven transcription and cell survival. Combination therapy with simvastatin and KRASG12C inhibitors delayed resistance onset and enhanced antitumor efficacy across multiple in vivo models, with acceptable tolerability. These findings identify the MVA-GGPP-YAP pathway as a therapeutic vulnerability in acquired KRASG12C inhibitor resistance and support repurposing statins to improve KRASG12C-targeted therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40164-026-00797-x.Keywords: KRASG12C inhibitor, Acquired resistance, Mevalonate pathway, Statin, Non-small cell lung cancer

To the Editor,

Covalent KRASG12C inhibitors, including sotorasib and adagrasib, have marked a major advance in the treatment of KRASG12C-mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), establishing KRAS as a clinically actionable oncogenic driver [1, 2]. However, durable clinical benefit remains limited by the emergence of acquired resistance, reflecting incomplete pathway suppression and context-specific cellular programs [3–5]. Defining new resistance mechanisms and strategies to circumvent acquired resistance is therefore essential for improving the durability of KRASG12C-targeted therapy.

To address this challenge, we first assessed a panel of KRASG12C-mutant NSCLC cell lines for sensitivity to adagrasib and sotorasib (Fig. S1A; Table S1), selecting NCI-H358, the most sensitive cell line, for the generation of isogenic acquired-resistant derivatives. Through prolonged drug exposure, we established H358-AR (adagrasib-resistant) and H358-SR (sotorasib-resistant) models, both exhibiting pronounced resistance to KRASG12C inhibition in vitro (Fig. 1A; Fig. S1B) and maintaining resistance in vivo (Fig. 1B; Fig. S1C-E).

To systematically identify therapeutic vulnerabilities associated with acquired resistance to KRASG12C inhibitors, we performed high-throughput screening across 1,971 FDA-approved compounds in H358 parental and resistant cell models (Fig. 1C; Fig. S2A). Intriguingly, multiple statins emerged as the top hits that preferentially suppressed drug-resistant cells (Fig. 1D; Fig. S2B). Follow-up assays confirmed that fluvastatin and simvastatin reduced viability, induced caspase-3/7 activation, and triggered apoptosis predominantly in resistant cells, with limited effects on parental cells (Fig. 1E-H; Fig. S2C-K). Extending these findings in vivo, simvastatin, either as monotherapy or combined with sotorasib, markedly suppressed the growth of resistant tumors (Fig. 1I-J; Fig. S3).

Statins, a class of FDA-approved cholesterol-lowering agents, have been implicated in anticancer activity [6, 7]. To gain mechanistic insight into this statin sensitivity, we next performed quantitative proteomic profiling. Resistant cells displayed a distinct cluster of upregulated proteins (Cluster 4) enriched in terpenoid backbone biosynthesis and the mevalonate (MVA) pathway (Fig. S4A-B). Consistent with this enrichment, resistant cells expressed elevated levels of multiple MVA pathway components, including the rate-limiting enzyme HMGCR (Fig. 1K; Fig. S4C). Supporting the functional relevance of this pathway, HMGCR silencing phenocopied the cytotoxicity induced by fluvastatin and simvastatin (Fig. 1L; Fig. S4D). Additionally, supplementation with MVA or GGPP (but not FPP, squalene, or cholesterol) completely rescued fluvastatin- or simvastatin-induced cell death (Fig. 1M; Fig. S5), suggesting that the MVA-GGPP branch represents the dominant survival-relevant arm in this setting, whereas cholesterol-branch upregulation may accompany broader MVA pathway remodeling. Complementing these findings, geranylgeranyl transferase I (GGTase-I) inhibition by GGTI-298 or BAY-593 [8] recapitulated this statin-associated vulnerability (Fig. 1N-O), further validating this metabolic axis as a critical mediator of resistance.

Given the reported link between the MVA-GGPP pathway and YAP activation [9, 10], we next examined YAP signaling in KRASG12C inhibitor resistance. Transcriptomic profiling of resistant cells revealed significant enrichment of Hippo-YAP pathway signatures and upregulation of canonical YAP target genes, supported by target-gene validation and enhanced nuclear YAP staining in resistant tumors (Fig. 2A-D; Fig. S6A-F). YAP silencing selectively suppressed resistant-cell proliferation and reduced CTGF and CYR61 expression, whereas constitutive YAP activation increased YAP target-gene expression and conferred resistance to KRASG12C inhibition (Fig. S6G-L). Importantly, treatment with fluvastatin or simvastatin reduced nuclear YAP localization and downregulated its transcriptional targets, effects that were reversed by MVA or GGPP supplementation (Fig. 2E-F; Fig. S7A-J). In vivo, simvastatin-containing treatment reduced nuclear YAP staining in H358-SR xenograft tumors (Fig. S7K-L). Similarly, GGTase-I inhibition by GGTI-298 or BAY-593, as well as pharmacological Rho inhibition, reduced nuclear YAP localization and downstream signaling in resistant cells, supporting an MVA-GGPP-Rho-YAP signaling link (Fig. 2G-H; Fig. S8).

To validate these findings in vivo, we employed NCI-H358 xenografts and two NSCLC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models harboring KRASG12C mutations (Fig. S9A; Table S2). Combination therapy with simvastatin and KRASG12C inhibitors significantly suppressed tumor growth and delayed resistance onset relative to monotherapy (Fig. 2I-K). Comparable results were observed with BAY-593 plus KRASG12C inhibitors (Fig. 2L-M; Fig. S9B-E). Furthermore, in murine models bearing KRASG12C mutation (LLC-ΔNras and CT26-KRASG12C), combination treatments enhanced antitumor effects compared with monotherapy (Fig. 2N-O; Fig. S10; Fig. S11A-F). Notably, no significant weight loss was observed, indicating an acceptable safety profile (Fig. S11G-H).

In summary, our study identifies statin-mediated inhibition of the MVA-GGPP-YAP axis as a potential strategy to overcome acquired resistance to KRASG12C inhibitors in NSCLC. These findings provide a mechanistic rationale for repurposing statins in combination with KRASG12C inhibitors to delay resistance and extend therapeutic benefit in patients with KRASG12C-mutant NSCLC. However, the preclinical statin dosing used in this study may not be directly translatable to humans, and future clinical application will require careful pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and safety evaluation. Given the widespread clinical use and well-characterized safety profile of statins, well-annotated retrospective analyses and prospective studies are warranted to determine whether concomitant statin exposure can improve outcomes in patients receiving KRASG12C-targeted therapy.
