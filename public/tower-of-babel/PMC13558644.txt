Prognostic value of on-admission IL-6, lactate, base deficit, ESR, and CRP in a prospective study of multiple trauma patients
Authors: Behrad Eftekhari, Anita Khalili, Mohammad Taghi Ashoobi, Iraj Baghi, Nooshin Zaresharifi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interleukin-6 (IL-6), serum lactate, base deficit (BD), C-reactive protein (CRP), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) were assessed for their prognostic value in predicting mortality and clinical outcomes in trauma patients. A prospective, observational study was conducted at Poursina Educational Trauma Center between May and August 2023, enrolling 114 patients with moderate to severe injuries (Injury Severity Score > 12). Admission data, including demographic, clinical, and laboratory parameters, were collected, with in-hospital mortality and length of stay as primary outcomes. The overall mortality rate was 11.4%, with non-survivors presenting with higher Injury Severity Scores, lower Glasgow Coma Scale scores, and elevated IL-6 and lactate levels. ROC analysis identified IL-6 as the most accurate predictor of mortality (AUC = 0.948), followed by BD and lactate, while CRP and ESR had limited predictive value. IL-6 demonstrated superior prognostic accuracy for early mortality, although BD and lactate remain crucial in resource-limited settings where IL-6 testing may not be feasible. These findings highlight the potential of integrating inflammatory and perfusion biomarkers to improve early risk stratification in trauma care.

Introduction

Multiple Trauma, a condition characterized by multiple severe injuries, poses a significant global health challenge, ranking as the fifth leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide1. Despite medical advancements, the mortality rate for major trauma is still significant, with a reported 98 deaths per 10,000 affected individuals1. The economic burden imposed by traumatic injuries is staggering, amounting to hundreds of billions of dollars annually2. In Iran, road injuries are particularly concerning, ranking as the third leading cause of mortality in 2021 and resulting in 30.65 fatalities per 100,000 individuals3. The pattern of mortality following trauma exhibits a distinct bimodal distribution, encompassing both early and delayed phases4,5. The first peak involves immediate, inevitable deaths resulting from severe head injuries, major organ damage, or significant blood loss due to vascular injuries, while the second peak, occurring days to weeks after the trauma, is often linked to sepsis and Multiple Organ Dysfunction Syndrome (MODS)4,5. This latter pattern highlights the need to search for a biomarker to predict early morbidity and mortality in these cases.

The immune response to significant injury involves a range of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines, notably interleukin-6 (IL-6), a key biomarker in predicting morbidity and mortality in multiple trauma cases due to its rapid elevation post-injury6. Likewise, lactic acid, a byproduct of anaerobic metabolism due to inadequate perfusion of vital organs, is a proven indicator of morbidity and mortality7.

The development of MODS follows the initial inflammatory response to extensive trauma. This syndrome is recognized by reversible physiological abnormalities and simultaneous dysfunction in two or more organs, often extending ICU stays and elevating mortality rates in severe cases8. Proinflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), play a central role in the pathophysiology of MODS, rapidly entering the bloodstream after significant trauma, thereby signaling the extent of the injury. Initiated by tissue damage, IL-6 production is stimulated, triggering cascades that enhance cytokine production, leading to the acute phase reaction (APR)9. C-reactive protein (CRP), synthesized in APR, peaks within 48 h of inflammation and reflects disease progression or resolution10. Conversely, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), influenced by fibrinogen and immunoglobulin levels, has a delayed response and susceptibility to physiological variables, which are widely used as a pragmatic option for longitudinal monitoring where advanced tests are unavailable11. Although its routine clinical use is limited, IL-6 has been reported as an independent predictor of outcomes in critically ill patients, underscoring its prognostic importance.

Lactate, an indicator of cellular metabolism and energy production, serves as an indirect measure of tissue hypoxia. Typically, glucose is metabolized aerobically to produce adenosine triphosphate (ATP), but under reduced oxygen supply, as in respiratory or circulatory failure, anaerobic pathways convert pyruvate to lactate12. Tissue lactate concentrations serve as an indirect index of oxygen debt and a diagnostic marker for shock states, and when its measurement is unavailable, base deficit (BD) from arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis serves as an alternative13,14. BD quantifies the base required to normalize blood pH. Severe BD is strongly associated with hemorrhagic shock and mortality15,16.

Earlier studies using inflammatory markers such as mean platelet volume, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and C-reactive protein (CRP) have shown limited ability to predict outcomes accurately, even when paired with anatomical and physiological scoring systems17,18. Consequently, this study examines the efficacy of IL-6 and serum lactate levels, as well as CRP, ESR, and BD in predicting morbidity and mortality among patients suffering from Multiple Trauma. An explanatory schematic of the proposed biomarker-mediated pathways is provided in Fig. 1. Additionally, it aims to determine a predictive cutoff for these factors, which have not been extensively studied in the Iranian population. By investigating these biomarkers regarding traumatic injuries in northern Iran, we aim to enhance our understanding of their roles in managing these critical conditions and compare their prognostic value in affected patients.

Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective, observational, cross-sectional study was conducted from May 2023 to August 2023 at the Department of Emergency Medicine, Poursina Educational Trauma Center, Rasht, Iran, following approval by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Guilan University of Medical Sciences (reference number: IR.GUMS.REC.1402.015). The sample size was determined using logistic regression methodology. According to previous studies examining trauma factors and patient outcomes, with an odds ratio of 3.9, a 30% success probability, a 95% confidence level, and 90% test power, the required sample size was 104. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, the minimum sample size was 114 participants, obtained via convenience sampling.

Selection of participants

Patients were deemed eligible for the study if they met the following criteria: (1) aged between 16 and 85 years, (2) hospitalized for at least 24 h post-trauma, and (3) had an Injury Severity Score (ISS) above 12, signifying moderate to severe trauma. Exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) incomplete medical records, which could impede accurate data collection and analysis; (2) concomitant trauma and burn injuries, as burns could confound trauma-specific outcomes; (3) discharge against medical advice, as brief stays and unauthorized discharge could distort severity and outcome data; or (4) seeking medical attention more than 24 h post-trauma, as delayed presentation could alter the trajectory and prognosis of injuries. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant or their legal representative prior to their involvement in the study.

Measurements

Demographical and clinical data of the patient including age, gender, underlying disease (hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, pulmonary diseases, kidney disease, and malignancies), types of trauma (car-car accident, pedestrian-car accident, motorcycle-car accident, fall from height, car rollover accident, motorcycle rollover accident, pedestrian-motorcycle accident), anatomical site of a trauma injury (head, face, and soft tissues of the neck, lower limbs, upper limbs, chest, abdomen and pelvis, multiple trauma), numbers of surgery, level of consciousness upon admission, ISS, outcome (discharged, death), and laboratory findings including BD, ESR, and CRP.

The hospital information system (HIS) systematically collected demographic details, clinical data, and outcome metrics. Recorded variables included patient age, gender, injury mechanism, trauma location, surgeries required, Glasgow Coma Scale score upon admission, hospital and ICU stay duration, and final patient status (survived or deceased). This information was recorded using a researcher-designed checklist within the HIS.

Outcomes

The level of consciousness was assessed using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), which evaluates three primary aspects: eye opening (E), verbal response (V), and motor response (M). The scale assigned a score from 3 to 15, with higher scores indicating better neurological function. The eye response was scored between 1 and 4, the verbal response from 1 to 5, and the motor response from 1 to 6. A GCS score between 3 and 8 indicated severe brain injury or coma, a score of 9 to 12 reflected moderate brain injury, and a score of 13 to 15 represented mild injury or full consciousness19.

To calculate the ISS, the abbreviated injury scale (AIS) score for each injured body part (general, head and neck, chest, abdomen, and extremities) in each region was first determined. Then, the three injuries with the highest AIS scores were selected, and the sum of their squares was calculated. The minimum possible ISS score was 3, and the maximum was 75. If a patient’s AIS score was 6 (non-survivable), the ISS score was automatically 75. Based on the ISS, mild trauma was classified as an ISS less than 7, moderate trauma between 7 and 12, and severe trauma as an ISS greater than 1220.

All biomarker samples were drawn in the Emergency Department at the time of admission/initial evaluation. This included using a heparinized green-top tube to assess plasma lactate levels and a gold-top serum separator tube for interleukin-6 (IL-6). Both samples were centrifuged at 2,500 rpm for 15 min to separate plasma and serum, which were stored at -28 °C until analyzed. Lactate levels were determined using the Biorex Lactate Enzymatic Kit (Serial number: 420736, Biorex, Antrim, UK). IL-6 concentrations were quantified with an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using the LDN kit (Serial Number: 220111, LDN, Nordhorn, Germany). Other assessments included the measurement of CRP, which was evaluated using a biochemistry kit (Delta Darman Part, Tehran, Iran) based on the turbidimetry method on a Hitachi 717 autoanalyzer (Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The level of ESR was assessed with an automated ESR analyzer (LENA, Tehran, Iran) employing the Westergren method, and BD was evaluated via ABG using the GASTAT-700 system (Technomedica, Tokyo, Japan) on day zero. The normal range for CRP was less than 10 mg/L, with higher levels indicating inflammation or infection. The reference range for ESR was less than 15 mm/hr for men and less than 20 mm/hr for women, although values may vary with age and underlying conditions. For BD, the normal range was between − 2 to + 2 mEq/L, with negative values suggesting metabolic acidosis and positive values indicating metabolic alkalosis.

Analysis

Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations (SD), and frequency distributions, were computed to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Data were reported as numbers (percentages) and mean ± SD, as appropriate. For group comparisons, normally distributed variables were analyzed using the independent t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Relationships between continuous variables were examined using Pearson’s linear correlation coefficient, while associations between categorical variables were assessed with the Chi-square test.

Multivariate analyses were performed to explore the associations between laboratory markers, clinical factors, and outcomes. Multivariable analyses were performed for continuous outcomes (length of hospital stay and ICU stay) using multiple linear regression. For mortality, analyses were limited to univariable comparisons and ROC curve analysis of individual biomarkers. Because only 13 deaths occurred, multivariable logistic regression for mortality was not performed due to a high risk of overfitting and an insufficient events-per-variable (EPV) ratio. To further evaluate the predictive performance of laboratory and clinical markers, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted, and the area under the curve (AUC), along with positive and negative likelihood ratios (PLR and NLR), were calculated. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and a two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This research project did not involve any interventions, and all trauma patients received necessary and appropriate treatments regardless of their participation in the study. All patient information and names were kept confidential, and the research results will be published collectively without revealing individual identities. Instead of using patient names, medical record numbers, and study-specific serial numbers were used to ensure anonymity. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Guilan University of Medical Sciences (IR.GUMS.REC.1402.015) and conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Among the 114 patients, 101 (88.6%) were discharged, and 13 (11.4%) were deceased. Approximately 92.1% of the patients (n = 105) were male, and the mean age of the participants was 42.78 ± 18.34 years (range, 16–84 years). The frequency of underlying disease was significantly higher among deceased patients than discharged patients, at 100% (n = 13) vs. 55% (n = 64), respectively (P = 0.008). Hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, and malignancies were more common among the deceased group, with a prevalence of 85%, 69.2%, and 38.5%, respectively (Table 1).

The trauma mechanism was another important factor, with car-car accidents and pedestrian-car accidents leading to higher mortality rates compared to other trauma mechanisms (P = 0.038). The anatomical location of injuries did not have a significant impact on the outcome, although multiple injuries were more common among deceased patients (P = 0.082). Patients with a GCS score of less than 9 had a significantly higher mortality rate (69.2%) compared to those with higher GCS scores. Notably, only 7.7% of patients in the discharged group had a GCS score in this range (P < 0.001). The frequency of greater ISS (≥ 54) was higher among deceased patients (P < 0.001) (Table 1). Deceased patients were significantly older compared to discharged patients (58.92 ± 13.53 vs. 40.72 ± 17.89 years, P = 0.001). Neurological impairment was more severe in the deceased group, as indicated by a markedly lower GCS score (6.53 ± 1.08 vs. 14.2 ± 0.14, P < 0.001). Injury severity was also significantly higher among deceased patients, with an ISS of 48.2 ± 4.79 compared to 22.5 ± 0.89 in the discharged group (P < 0.001). Metabolic derangements reflected by BD were more pronounced in deceased patients (-1.11 ± 5.16 vs. -0.224 ± 0.51, P < 0.001), suggesting a greater degree of physiological compromise. Additionally, IL-6 and lactate levels were markedly elevated among deceased patients compared to discharged patients (213.3 ± 11.88 vs. 94.9 ± 5.8 pg/ml for IL-6, and 52.07 ± 7.96 vs. 25.1 ± 1.2 mg/dL for lactate, both P < 0.001), indicating a strong association between systemic inflammation, metabolic stress, and mortality (Table 2). ESR was significantly lower in deceased patients (P = 0.027), whereas CRP showed no significant difference between groups (P > 0.05).

ROC curve analysis demonstrated that IL-6 had the highest predictive value for mortality, with an AUC of 0.948 (p < 0.001). An IL-6 cut-off of 140.75 pg/ml provided 85.1% sensitivity and 92.0% specificity, with a PLR of 10.60 and an NLR of 0.16, highlighting its robust discriminatory ability. BD also showed strong predictive value, with an AUC of 0.887 (P < 0.001), a cut-off value of -2.65 yielding 87.1% sensitivity and 92.0% specificity, and a PLR of 10.88 and an NLR of 0.14. Lactate also demonstrated good predictive performance (AUC = 0.849, P < 0.001), with a cut-off of 39.5 mg/dL yielding 89.1% sensitivity and 76.9% specificity. In contrast, ESR (AUC = 0.312, P = 0.011) and CRP (AUC = 0.568, P = 0.357) demonstrated poor predictive performance, indicating limited clinical utility (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Trauma-induced shock injury is initially reversible; however, if tissue hypoperfusion is severe and prolonged, irreversible cellular damage occurs. Due to oxygen deficiency, anaerobic metabolism takes place within cells, leading to hypoxic cellular injury following trauma21. This process, occurring parallel to the inflammatory response, results in the production or elevation of specific biomarkers in the body, which may serve as predictors of patient outcomes and mortality. Our study demonstrates that among the biomarkers assessed on admission in multiple trauma patients, IL-6 has the most substantial prognostic value for in-hospital mortality, followed by base deficit (BD) and lactate. In contrast, traditional inflammatory markers (ESR and CRP) showed minimal predictive utility for early mortality. IL-6 yielded the highest discriminative performance, indicating excellent accuracy in identifying patients at risk of death. BD also emerged as a robust predictor, closely followed by lactate, underscoring the significance of early perfusion deficits in trauma outcomes. Meanwhile, CRP displayed only poor discrimination, and ESR was essentially uninformative.

IL-6 was consistent with its role as an early “first-hit” cytokine in trauma pathophysiology22. Prior work links admission IL-6 to injury severity, multiorgan dysfunction syndrome (MODS), and mortality6, and a meta-analysis highlights IL-6 within 24 h post-injury as predictive of immunologic complications and death23. Our mortality-associated IL-6 threshold (~ 141 pg/mL) was lower than Frink et al.’s 2,176 pg/mL6, likely reflecting sampling time and assay differences; nonetheless, IL-6’s superior AUC in our cohort underscores the prognostic importance of the early inflammatory surge.

BD aligned with its status as a surrogate of shock and tissue hypoperfusion. Non-survivors presented with larger base deficits, echoing evidence that BD could be a mortality predictor across resuscitation eras24. BD reflects “oxygen debt,” correlating with hemorrhage severity and organ failure risk, and it is rapidly obtainable by arterial blood gas (ABG), making it valuable where cytokine assays are unavailable. In resource-limited settings, BD and lactate are pragmatic substitutes for early risk assessment; a prospective Indian study revealed that a single ABG-derived BD and lactate independently predicted 48-hour mortality14. In our data, BD showed high sensitivity (~ 87%) and specificity (~ 92%) at a cutoff around − 2.6 mmol/L, supporting its use as an immediate triage indicator.

Lactate, another hypoperfusion marker, was also predictive (AUC ~ 0.85). Non-survivors had higher admission lactate (mean ~ 4.4 mmol/L) than survivors (~ 2.8 mmol/L), in line with trauma/critical-care literature. Although studies differ on whether lactate or BD is superior, both are correlated and reflect similar pathophysiology14. Elevated lactate and BD predict mortality even in normotensive patients; in a large cohort, lactate independently predicted in-hospital death, while BD better signaled coagulopathy25. In our cohort, BD marginally outperformed lactate, which nevertheless showed ~ 89% sensitivity near a ~ 4 mmol/L cutoff; serial lactate trends guide resuscitation, though we analyzed admission values14.

CRP and ESR were not valid for acute mortality prediction. Early CRP did not differ between survivors and non-survivors, and ESR was paradoxically lower in non-survivors, yielding an AUC < 0.5. These results align with biomarker kinetics: CRP peaks 24–48 h after injury, and ESR changes more slowly and is confounded by non-specific factors. Reviews note ESR’s delayed, low-specificity signal, and early CRP often fails to track acute injury severity17. Indeed, CRP (and markers such as IL-1β or mean platelet volume) appear unhelpful for early severity assessment, while IL-6 and TNF-α better reflect injury burden17. CRP’s short half-life is still useful for tracking postoperative infection after day 110, and ESR can aid longer-term inflammatory monitoring, but neither improved immediate prognostication in our model11.

Additionally, we found a higher prevalence of trauma among males and individuals aged 21–44 years. Car-car and pedestrian-car accidents were the most frequently reported ones, primarily causing head, neck, and soft tissue injuries. Young individuals often do not strictly adhere to traffic regulations. Additionally, a large number of young people in Iran rely on unsafe public transportation, such as motorcycles, further increasing their risk of trauma26. A study by Rostam et al. similarly reported that car crashes were the most common type of accident, with men and individuals aged 20–34 years being the most frequent trauma victims27.

Our findings demonstrated a prevalence of 11.4% deaths among accident trauma patients. The risk of mortality was higher among trauma patients who were admitted to the ICU. Studies reported various rates of mortality among trauma patients admitted to the ICU, from 5.6% to 46.1%. The high mortality may be due to a lack of pre-hospital care, limited ventilators, ICU setup differences, and delayed patient presentation, with nearly one-third taking over 12 h to reach medical care28–30. A rather high mortality rate observed in our study may be attributed to lower hospital standards in affiliated centers and an inadequate referral system to the trauma center.

The current study demonstrated that the highest mortality rate was observed in patients with lower GCS scores. The mean GCS score among deceased patients was significantly lower than that of survivors. Evidence illustrated the highest mortality rates in patients with a GCS score below 11. Patients with a GCS score < 9 not only suffered from more severe brain injuries but also required more aggressive neurosurgical interventions, suggesting a potential correlation between neurosurgical procedures and mortality31,32. We also found that ISS demonstrated a significant association with mortality rate. A study by Colnaric et al. demonstrated that ISS showed a moderate to high ability to identify deceased patients as a significant predictor of mortality33. Another study by Elgin et al. showed that higher ISS correlated with higher shock indices, and significant differences were observed in the means of shock index between deceased and alive patients34.

Our findings integrate with prior evidence: shock markers (lactate, BD) remain critical early predictors—including in older adults when combined at admission—while IL-6 can exceed conventional measures in predictive power. The novelty here is a head-to-head comparison in one cohort: IL-6 had the highest AUC, followed by BD and lactate, suggesting that integrating inflammatory and perfusion markers may optimize early prognostication. Multimarker models are increasingly advocated; IL-6 was among the top predictors of early MODS when combined with clinical indices35. Because IL-6 testing is not yet routine due to turnaround and cost in developing countries, the practical importance of BD (and lactate) is underscored, especially where rapid cytokine assays are unavailable; aggressive resuscitation guided by BD/lactate remains standard14. As trauma care systems globally face resource constraints, BD presents an invaluable tool that can significantly enhance the management of critically ill trauma patients, ultimately reducing mortality rates and improving clinical outcomes. In retrospect, conducting this study with a larger, multicenter cohort would have enhanced the external validity and statistical robustness of our findings. Furthermore, integrating additional clinical parameters such as shock index, transfusion requirements, and detailed pre-hospital data might have reduced residual confounding. If repeated, we would also prioritize a standardized protocol for blood sampling timing and explore the feasibility of rapid IL-6 assays to better reflect real-time clinical decision-making. In practical terms, measuring IL-6 may be most feasible as a targeted supplement in tertiary trauma centers, especially for patients with high ISS, severe traumatic brain injury, or ambiguous physiological parameters at admission. In such settings, IL-6 could assist with early in-hospital risk stratification and subsequent escalation of care (e.g., increased monitoring or enrollment in high-risk care pathways) once laboratory results are available, rather than serving as a universal screening test. This tiered approach may enable trauma systems to harness IL-6’s prognostic value while keeping costs low and workflow efficient.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, its single-center design may limit the generalizability of the results to other trauma systems with differing pre-hospital care protocols, referral patterns, and resource availability. Second, the relatively small sample size (n = 114) and the low mortality rate (11.4%) limited the statistical power for extensive subgroup analyses and might affect the generalizability of the proposed biomarker cut-off values. Importantly, only 13 deaths occurred, which precluded reliable multivariable logistic regression for mortality (EPV would be substantially below accepted thresholds), increasing the risk of overfitting. Therefore, mortality findings should be interpreted as based on univariable associations and ROC discrimination rather than independent effects. Larger, multicenter studies involving diverse trauma populations are necessary to validate these findings and improve external applicability. Third, the observational cross-sectional nature of the study precludes establishing causal relationships between biomarker levels and patient outcomes. Additionally, IL-6 measurement is not routinely performed in many trauma centers, and the laboratory turnaround time may limit its practical applicability in urgent settings. Future studies should evaluate rapid or point-of-care IL-6 testing in trauma workflows to determine whether IL-6 can be returned within the resuscitation window and meaningfully improve real-time triage decisions, compared with standard laboratory turnaround times. Furthermore, biomarkers were only measured at admission. Serial measurements of IL-6, lactate, and BD could better track dynamic physiological and inflammatory changes and might improve prognostic accuracy. While samples were drawn at the time of admission to the Emergency Department, we did not have precise data on the time from injury to hospital arrival. This missing data precluded comparison of time-to-admission between survivors and non-survivors, and residual confounding due to delayed presentation cannot be excluded. Future studies should ensure accurate timestamps for injury and admission, as well as serial IL-6 measurements to account for cytokine kinetics and better separate the effects of injury severity from timing-related variables. Finally, potential confounders such as pre-existing comorbidities, variations in resuscitation strategies, and delays in hospital arrival were not fully controlled, which may have influenced biomarker levels and outcomes.

Conclusions

In summary, IL-6, base deficit (BD), and lactate showed the strongest association with mortality and the highest discriminatory performance on ROC analysis, with IL-6 demonstrating the greatest prognostic discrimination. Conventional markers such as ESR and CRP showed minimal value for early outcome prediction. Routine measurement of BD and lactate should be prioritized for rapid risk stratification, especially in resource-constrained settings, while IL-6 may enhance prognostic precision where available. Integrating perfusion biomarkers (BD/lactate) into early trauma protocols may support immediate bedside assessment, while IL-6 may enhance early in-hospital risk stratification and inform subsequent escalation of care once results are available. Future multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings and explore multimarker approaches for optimizing early trauma care.
