ABSTRACT
Building an advanced economy using biomanufacturing requires robust sources of carbon for
converting into chemicals and products. Macroalgae hold significant potential due to their fast
growth rates, lack of freshwater usage, and lack of land usage. To be used as a common feedstock,
there are two primary challenges that need to be addressed: 1) unusual chemical composition and 2)
high salinity. A solution to both issues is to use a resilient, salt-loving organism with a broad
consumption profile for the conversion of macroalgae. Here we evaluate the ability of the
oleaginous yeast Rhodosporidium toruloides to grow in wild macroalgae-derived feedstocks and
identify a conversion process that is functional for two species of Sargassum collected from the
Caribbean using R. toruloides that does not require washing of the input seaweed material. Together
this demonstrates a future avenue of work that minimizes freshwater usage for incorporating
macroalgae as a biomanufacturing carbon source.

1. INTRODUCTION
Building an advanced economy using biomanufacturing requires robust sources of carbon for
converting into biochemicals and bioproducts. Macroalgae (seaweeds) hold significant potential for
acting as a carbon source due to their fast growth rates, lack of pesticides usage, low usage of
freshwater, and no competition with staple food crops for terrestrial land (Zheng et al. 2022; Sasaki
and Yoshikuni 2022) usage. Farming macroalgae can also have the added benefit of cleaning
contaminated water and preventing undesirable algal blooms (Sarma et al. 2021). Additionally, many
wild macroalgae are considered invasive pests that are actively removed and discarded to maintain
the natural or desired aquatic environment. Utilizing macroalgae for bioproduction would not only
increase the available carbon for biomanufacturing but also provide a value to these costly waste
streams. Despite the great potential of macroalgae, the current cost of obtaining and preparing it is
higher than the value derived from the macroalgae itself (van den Burg et al. 2016). A primary
avenue to remedy this challenge is to generate higher value products from the harvested biomass.
Bioconversion is of particular interest for macroalgae valorization as it greatly broadens the potential
products derived from macroalgae beyond what is natively present. As a feedstock for
bioconversion, however, macroalgae presents several challenges. Significantly, it is not a singular
biomass with consistent forms of carbon. The three major groups of macroalgae (brown, green, and
red) each contain different hexose and pentose sugar compositions, along with an array of additional
carbon molecules. Even within one group or species of macroalgae, the carbon and chemical
composition of macroalgae biomass can be highly variable based on the growth environment. While
some of these sugars have been used by individual industrial microbes to produce a small selection
of bioproducts (Laurens and Nelson 2020; Nagarajan et al. 2022), this strategy greatly limits the
sugars being used for production, leading to wasted carbon and a loss of potential value.
A second limiting barrier to the use of macroalgae as a biomanufacturing feedstock is salinity, which
can also vary dramatically based on growth and harvesting conditions. While colloquially referred to
as seaweed, macroalgae grow in a huge range of aquatic environments from freshwater to oceans
(Jung et al. 2013). Even if most conventional bioconversion hosts were engineered to consume all
the available sugars, they are not resilient enough to grow under such a wide range of saline
conditions. The current processes being utilized to bioconvert macroalgae negate one of the main
benefits of macroalgae by using freshwater to wash away salt prior to processing it into a feedstock.
To fully realize macroalgae’s potential within the bioeconomy, there needs to be a resilient and
flexible microbial host to turn minimally pre-processed macroalgae into valuable commodities.
Oleaginous yeasts are promising bioconversion organisms for many available feedstocks as they can
catabolize a wide array of carbon substrates in harsh environments and naturally generate large
quantities of interesting bioproducts, including lipids similar to those used to make sustainable
aviation fuels. Rhodosporidium toruloides is genetically tractable and has been engineered to greatly
broaden the range of products it can make. Promisingly, when evaluated on washed (and therefore
reduced salt) acid treated macroalgae biomass, R. toruloides has been demonstrated to grow (Shaigani
et al. 2021) by catabolizing some of the wide array of substrates. Additionally, R. toruloides has been
demonstrated to grow and produce chemicals in land biomass that has been deconstructed using salt
water (Das et al. 2020), indicating the high salt content of unwashed macroalgae should not present
an obstacle to R. toruloides growth. In this work, we sought to demonstrate that these two traits can
be combined to grow and produce lipids on treated un-washed macroalgae biomass. Through doing
so, we identify different deconstruction methods for sugar release from macroalgae that support R.
toruloides growth, develop methodologies to identify the sugars released, quantify the lipid

accumulation from this biomass, and start to build and evaluate a yeast strain that could potentially
consume a broader range of macroalgae derived sugars.

2. CONDUCTED WORK

2.1. Macroalgae Acquisition and Species Identification
The term “macroalgae” encompasses three subsets of large multicellular algae: brown, red, and
green. Here we focus on utilizing Sargassum biomass from the Caribbean Ocean where annual
blooms cause ecological and economic strife when tens of thousands of cubic metric tons per
kilometer wash ashore annually (Rodríguez-Martínez et al. 2025). From collaborators at the
University of Puerto Rico and at Texas A&M University, we were able to acquire two wild-collected
Sargassum spp. specimens. Visually, the two samples appeared to represent the two distinct species
(Figure 1), likely one each from the most abundant species of Sargassum in the Caribbean: S. fluitans
and S. natans. However, visual species identification of macroalgaes can be challenging as the
varying growth conditions lead to a large variability in appearance. As such, we genetically verified
the two species by extracting genomic DNA from both to amplify and sequence two marker genes
used for Sargassum speciation: COX3 and MTPS (Álvarez-Canali et al. 2024). DNA was extracted
using a Qiagen DNAeasy Plant kit and a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using
previously published primers (Table 1) and Q5 DNA polymerase. The PCR sequencing results
(Table 1) were analyzed using NCBI BLAST and confirmed we had acquired a specimen of both S.
fluitans and S. natans.

2.2. Sargassum Deconstruction Evaluation
The next phase of the project involved the evaluation of deconstruction methods to generate
hydrolysates compatible with the cultivation of R. toruloides. One of the primary compounds from
macroalgae biomass we wanted to assess for breakdown and consumption was alginate, which can
make up to 40% of the total biomass in brown algae (Bojorges et al. 2023). Pure sodium alginate
(4% in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 6) was also deconstructed in parallel to the two Sargassum
samples to directly evaluate the effect of the enzymes on the release and use of the alginate derived
monomer 4-deoxy-L-erythro-5-hexoseulose uronic acid (DEH). A combination of thermochemical
pretreatment and enzymatic hydrolysis reactions is likely to be beneficial and synergistic to solubilize

 12

the sargassum, disrupt the macromolecular structure, and convert polymeric sugars into monomers
that are more accessible to be consumed by yeasts, like R. toruloides.
 Three different pretreatment solvents and two enzymatic treatments were selected for testing
based on literature reports (Maneein et al. 2018) (Kooren et al. 2023) that indicate some degree of
success at deconstructing many types of seaweed and the availability of enzymes from commercial
vendors. These include using either 1% sulfuric acid, 1% sodium hydroxide, or water as solvents,
and enzymatic cocktails containing cellulase, hemicellulase, and alginate lyase activities.
In preparation for deconstruction experiments, the Sargassum biomass was first dried in an oven at
50 ºC for 16 h, without any pre-processing or washing. A mortar and pestle was then used to grind it
into a fine powder and the dry biomass was stored at room temperature until needed. Cellulase
(Cellic CTec3) and hemicellulase (HTec3) enzymatic cocktails were obtained from Novonesis, USA
and mixed at a ratio of 9:1 v/v of Ctec3:HTec3. Alginate lyase (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was
resuspended at a concentration of 100 Units/mL (10 mg enzyme in 1 mL of 0.1 M phosphate
buffer, pH 6). 10 µL of each enzyme or enzyme mixture was added per mL of pretreated biomass.
The pretreatment reactions were performed at two different scales (Figure 2). Small scale reactions
were carried out in either 2- or 10-mL plastic vials to facilitate screening of the different reagents and
reaction conditions.

  Figure 2. Scheme of the steps employed to deconstruct and characterize Sargassum biomass.
To prepare enough hydrolysate for bioconversion experiments, larger reactions were carried out in
250 mL glass bottles containing 100 g of biomass. In all cases, the solids loading was 10 wt.% and
the solvent and biomass mixtures were autoclaved at 121 ºC for 1 h. The resulting slurry was
allowed to cool down and the pH was adjusted to 6 with concentrated acid or base, before enzyme
addition. Enzyme hydrolysis was performed at 40 ºC for 48 h using an incubator with a shaking
platform set at 200 rpm. For small scale pretreatment, hydrolysis was carried out in the same tubes
and for the larger reaction, the pretreated biomass was transferred to a round bottom flask for better
mixing. The resulting hydrolysates were centrifuged and filtered (0.45 µm filters) before analysis and
microbial conversion experiments.
Base pretreatment proved to be the most effective at solubilizing the Sargassum samples and
promoted higher growth of R. toruloides (see next section). The larger scale reactions resulted in
biomass solubilization percentages of 77% for the sample of S. fluitans and 76% for the sample of S.

 13

natans after pretreatment and enzymatic hydrolysis. The release of DEH as a result of the different
treatments was tracked using LC-MS (Waters SYNAPT G2-Si QTOF) and the results are shown in
Figure 3.

 Figure 3. Detection of DEH and oligomers in pure alginate or pretreated Sargassum after
 enzymatic hydrolysis.
DEH was detected (m/z 175) in alginate hydrolysates only when alginate lyase was used, confirming
that the enzyme is active under the employed conditions and no other compounds of similar mass
were present in non-hydrolyzed alginate or the CTec3/HTec3 cocktails. DEH was also detected in
NaOH-pretreated sargassum without the addition of any enzymes, which suggests that a low level of
base-catalyzed hydrolysis of alginate is occurring during pretreatment. The DEH signal from
pretreated sargassum was much higher in presence of alginate lyase, as observed with pure alginate.
We also detected the release of a disaccharide (m/z 351) when all enzymes were present (Figure 3),
which aligns with previously published reports that have studied the effect of alginate lyases (Shibata
et al. 2017).

2.3. R. toruloides Growth Analysis on Screened Pretreatments
The initial screening described above compared acid, base (alkaline), and water pretreatment
methods, both with and without the addition of enzymes ("+E"). Base pretreatment with and
without enzymes resulted in the highest overall growth compared to the other conditions (Figure 4)
indicating the alkaline treatment was more compatible for yeast cultivation either by releasing higher
concentrations of fermentable compounds or by generating a more hospitable environment.

 14

 Figure 4. Growth of Rhodosporidium toruloides on different pretreated hydrolysates. Growth
  represents the change in the optical density at 600 nm with time zero subtracted from the final
 measurement on day 3.
Building on the success of the base pretreatment, further experiments were conducted to optimize
the process. When evaluating the amount of solid loading, 10% solid loading outperformed a 20%
solid loading. Additionally, 1% NaOH was more effective than 2% NaOH. Fermentations were
performed at 30C and to quantify growth cultures were centrifuged, the pellets were washed three
times with water, and then the OD was measured.
 The culture pretreated with 1% NaOH achieved a maximum optical density of 13.32 on day 2,
whereas the 2% NaOH had the highest growth on day 1 (Figure 5). This difference suggests that
while some alkalinity is necessary for effective pretreatment, an overly high concentration of NaOH
can create an environment that is detrimental or even toxic to the growth of R. toruloides. Of the
methods evaluated, this work indicated that an alkaline pretreatment using 1% NaOH is a
productive route for deconstructing Sargassum to support the growth of R. toruloides.

 concentrations and solid loadings.

2.4. Metabolic Engineering of R. toruloides for DEH Catabolism
As DEH is not a common substrate used by many organisms, we sought to engineer a strain of R.
toruloides that outperformed wildtype in consuming DEH. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the successful
introduction of the four genes (DHT1, DehR, KdgK, and KdgpA) allowed for DEH catabolism. Here,
we introduced those 4 genes using Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation (ATMT), a well-
established methodology for engineering yeast (Bundock et al. 1995). The process yielded 28
isolated clones.
Next, we tested the engineered strain along with wildtype R. toruloides on the two different species of
Sargassum. Here hydrolysates were diluted 1:1 with YNB to mitigate the effect of any inhibitory
compounds. The sodium alginate control was assessed along with a Yeast Nitrogen Base (YNB)
medium that matches the supplementation used for the hydrolysates to evaluate any growth derived
from the supplementation (Figure 6).
Superior growth of the engineered yeast over wildtype was observed using S. natans. In contrast, on
S. fluitans, both the engineered and wildtype yeast exhibited more moderate growth. Importantly,
growth of both wildtype and the engineered strain was also observed on the pure hydrolyzed sodium
alginate, suggesting that compounds specifically released from alginate can be substrates for R.
toruloides. Additional work should be performed to optimize the hydrolysis reaction and evaluate
consumption of DEH and other molecules derived from alginate by both wildtype and engineered
R. toruloides strains.

2.5. Lipid Accumulation in R. toruloides from Sargassum Biomass
For the lipid analysis, the lyophilized cell pellets were processed by adding 100 μL of internal
standard (10 mg/mL C13 methyl ester in methanol), 750 μL of 3N methanolic HCl, and 50 μL of
chloroform. The samples were homogenized using a bead beater at speed 20 for 15 minutes,
followed by incubation at 80°C for 1 hour. This bead beating and incubation cycle was repeated
once. Subsequently, 500 μL of n-hexane was added to each sample in a fume hood and thoroughly
vortexed. After centrifugation at 10,000 rcf for 1 minute, 100 μL of the hexane layer was transferred
to a GC vial and diluted with 900 μL of hexane. Fatty acid methyl ester analysis was performed using
Gas chromatography-flame ionization detection. The column temperature program consisted of
equilibration at 40°C for 1 minute, followed by a ramp to 200°C at 25°C/min, and a final hold at
230°C for 2.6 minute.
The day 2 results showed both the engineered and wild-type yeast produced comparable lipid
amounts when grown on the different seaweed hydrolysates. While the engineered yeast grown on S.
natans reached a lipid content of about 0.13 mg lipid/mg dry cell weight, the difference relative to
wildtype did not appear to be substantial (Figure 7). A similar trend was observed on S. fluitans,
where lipid levels were comparable between the two yeast strains.

3. CONCLUSIONS
Cumulatively this work demonstrates that a viable bioconversion process exists for macroalgae that
does not require washing of the input material. By using R. toruloides for the cultivations, we can take
wild collected macroalgae and generate lipids. This work is just the starting point, and more work
should be done to more directly evaluate what carbon sources R. toruloides is able to consume.
Additionally, it will be interesting to see how different strains of R. toruloides engineered for the
bioproduction of various chemicals grow and produce using macroalgae feedstocks.
