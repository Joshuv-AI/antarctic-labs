Rearing systems shape the successional dynamics of the gut microbiota, resistome, and mobilome in Lueyang Black-boned chickens
Authors: Tao Li, Tong Guo, Mengmeng Cui, Yungen Cao, Zhuo Zhi, Peng Wang, Qinli Li, Jianqin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the ecological factors shaping antimicrobial resistance (AMR) dissemination in agricultural environments is critical for global “One Health”. Here, we performed metagenomic sequencing to investigate the impact of intensive cage-reared (CR) and free-range (FR) systems on the gut microbiota, resistome, and mobilome dynamics of Lueyang Black-boned chickens across different production stages. Our analyses revealed that distinct rearing systems drove resistome alterations by reshaping microbial community assembly and horizontal gene transfer (HGT) pathways. Specifically, the CR system imposed strong deterministic stress, thereby enriching opportunistic taxa (such as Desulfovibrio) and promoting a highly connected but topologically fragile microbial network. Conversely, the FR system exhibited a higher total abundance of commensal resistance genes, a process mainly driven by diverse transposon-mediated integrations including tnpA and ISBf10. In contrast, the CR system was associated with high-risk, clinically relevant resistance determinants. These included extended-spectrum beta-lactamases and multidrug resistance cassettes. Targeted network tracking unmasked highly divergent potential host-vector-ARG associations. Resistance expansion under confined CR conditions showed strong vector-dependency, being fundamentally linked to the broad-host-range plasmid IncQ1 alongside clinically relevant mobilization elements, including Class 1 integrons. Longitudinally, the FR resistome achieved ecological stabilization. In contrast, the CR microbiota exhibited continued genetic flux, continuously acquiring transient resistance elements during the observed production period. These findings demonstrate that welfare-friendly rearing management serves as a critical ecological intervention to limit the proliferation of mobile, high-risk resistance traits. Ultimately, future agricultural surveillance must transition beyond quantifying total resistance gene abundance to prioritize functional risk assessments and mobilization potential.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as a severe global public health challenge. AMR directly causes approximately 1.27 million deaths annually, and this figure is projected to rise continuously without effective intervention strategies (Antimicrobial Resistance Collaborators, 2022). Under the “One Health” framework, agricultural ecosystems, particularly livestock and poultry farming environments, are widely recognized as critical environmental reservoirs for the emergence, enrichment, and evolution of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) (Zhu et al., 2013; Hernando-Amado et al., 2019). As the global poultry industry advances towards high-quality development, addressing this expanding resistome reservoir has become particularly urgent. Currently, poultry farming practices are undergoing a systemic transition from intensive cage-reared (CR) systems to cage-free or free-range (FR) systems, a shift primarily driven by growing demands for animal welfare and agricultural sustainability (Riber et al., 2018). However, this transition in production systems has also triggered a profound industrial and ecological dilemma. Although FR systems accommodate natural animal behaviors and effectively alleviate confinement stress, their expanded spatial range and highly dynamic environmental exposure introduce unpredictable biosecurity risks (Ocejo et al., 2019). Although intensive CR systems still maintain significant advantages in production efficiency and cost control, determining how to effectively disrupt or balance the exacerbated risks of ARG dissemination caused by FR environmental exposure, while simultaneously ensuring economic viability and animal welfare, has emerged as a core challenge in modern poultry science (Pal et al., 2015).

Within the poultry gut microecology, the intestinal microbiome serves not only as a crucial hub regulating host nutritional metabolism, immune homeostasis, and pathogen defense (Borda-Molina et al., 2018), but also functions as the core node for the maintenance and evolution of the resistome (Pehrsson et al., 2016). Notably, different rearing systems impose distinctly different ecological selection pressures, fundamentally reshaping the structure and function of this microbial community (Cui et al., 2017). Intensive CR systems are typically characterized by high stocking densities, production stress, and homogenized dietary structures, all of which readily induce the proliferation of opportunistic pathogens (Rubio et al., 2015). Beyond driving taxonomic alterations, routine environmental exposure exerts continuous and robust co-selection pressure on the gut microbiota. In CR environments, this is driven by heavy metal feed additives, such as copper and zinc, and disinfectant residues, thereby driving the enrichment of resistance determinants (Baker-Austin et al., 2006; Pal et al., 2015). In contrast, the complex soil and vegetation exposure in FR systems confers a higher level of microbial diversity to the host. However, such exposed environments also pave the way for the colonization of soil- and vegetation-associated environmental microbes, which act as potential vectors for the influx of ARGs into the host's internal network (Cui et al., 2017; Hubert et al., 2019). Consequently, even in the absence of direct antibiotic intervention, the rearing management paradigm itself implicitly determines the evolutionary trajectory of the gut resistome.

Although numerous previous studies have documented the taxonomic succession of gut microbiomes across different rearing environments, the micro-mechanisms by which rearing systems deeply shape the evolutionary dynamics of the resistome remain largely unknown. Existing literature predominantly relies on 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. However, this technique has inherent resolution limitations in deciphering deep functional genomic dynamics and capturing strain-level variations (Quince et al., 2017; Boolchandani et al., 2019). More critically, the environmental dissemination of ARGs is highly dependent on horizontal gene transfer (HGT) mechanisms mediated by mobile genetic elements (MGEs), such as plasmids, transposons, and integrons. Thus, microbial taxonomic profiles alone are far from sufficient to unravel these complex horizontal transmission networks (von Wintersdorff et al., 2016; Partridge et al., 2018). To date, the tripartite transmission network connecting “host phylogeny–mobilome–resistome” under different rearing models has not been fully elucidated (Zhang et al., 2021). Deciphering whether CR and FR systems select for differentiated genetic vehicles to perpetuate ARGs is of decisive significance for comprehensively evaluating the ecological trade-offs inherent in the transition of modern animal husbandry.

To bridge these methodological and microecological knowledge gaps, the present study systematically investigated the longitudinal dynamics of the poultry gut microbiome, mobilome, and resistome driven by different rearing systems. The Lueyang Black-boned chicken was selected as the biological model for this study. As a typical Chinese indigenous poultry breed, it retains a highly diverse genetic and microbial background compared to commercial broilers. This makes it an ideal carrier for exploring the ecological effects of industrial paradigm shifts (Li et al., 2025b; a). Relying on shotgun metagenomic sequencing technology, coupled with the latest ARGs-OAP v3.0 pipeline and the Structured ARG (SARG) database (Yin et al., 2023), we performed deep annotation of the resistome and mobilome. Building upon this, we integrated variance partitioning analysis (VPA) with a targeted tripartite cross-domain correlation network model. This approach allowed us to quantify the relative contributions of the gut microbiome and MGEs to ARG variations. This study hypothesizes that the differentiated ecological microenvironments and co-selection pressures inherent to distinct rearing environments drive starkly different evolutionary trajectories of the host gut communities. Consequently, these distinct pathways shape unique horizontal transmission networks that dictate the direction of ARG dissemination.

The ultimate objective of this study is to elucidate the mechanisms and pathways of ARG dissemination under the implicit selection of different rearing environments. The establishment of this microecological framework provides crucial theoretical support for understanding the dynamic evolution of the livestock and poultry resistome. Furthermore, it bears profound practical significance for scientifically guiding the sustainable health management of low-resistance-risk, welfare-friendly poultry farming under the global “One Health” initiative.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Ethical Committee of the Experimental Animal Center at Northwest Agriculture and Forestry University (Protocol number: WAFAC1008). All animals were handled in strict accordance with established animal welfare standards and in full compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines (Percie du Sert et al., 2020a, 2020b).

Experimental design and animal management

A total of 560 healthy Lueyang Black-boned (LYB) hens were randomly allocated into two distinct rearing systems consisting of the cage-reared (CR) group and the free-range (FR) group (7 replicates per group, 40 birds per replicate). The CR birds were housed in an environmentally controlled facility using a conventional three-tiered cage system (Cui et al., 2024). In contrast, the FR birds were managed under a forest enclosure model with access to natural grassland at a stocking density of 0.3 birds/m², relying on natural ventilation and light supplemented at night to maintain a 16L:8D photoperiod. The specific housing facilities, environmental controls, and daily management protocols for both the CR and FR systems were identical to those detailed in our previous study (Li et al., 2025b). Both groups were provided ad libitum access to water and the exact same basal commercial diet throughout the entire experimental period, completely free from any supplementary or therapeutic administration of antibiotics, medicinal growth promoters, anticoccidials, probiotics, prebiotics, or therapeutic mineral supplements.

Sample collection

To capture the successional dynamics of the gut microbiome and resistome, longitudinal sampling was conducted at four distinct developmental stages (120, 150, 180, and 360 days of age). At each designated time point, following a 12-hour fasting period, one bird was randomly selected from each replicate (n = 7 per group per time point, yielding a total of 56 samples). The selection was evenly distributed across different physical cages and free-range zones to guarantee population representativeness and eliminate potential microenvironmental pen-effects. In strict accordance with the recognized guidelines for the euthanasia of animals, the selected birds were euthanized by electrical stunning followed by exsanguination. Following euthanasia, the cecal contents were aseptically collected from each bird. All collected samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently transferred to a −80°C freezer, where they were stored until DNA extraction and shotgun metagenomic sequencing.

DNA extraction, fragmentation, and quality control

Total genomic DNA was extracted from the cecal samples using the Magnetic Bead Fecal Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (AU46111-96, BioTeke, China) according to the manufacturer's protocols. DNA concentration was quantified using the Qubit 1X dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Q33230, USA). For sequence library preparation, approximately 200 ng of qualified DNA from each sample was diluted to a final volume of 52 μL. The DNA was subsequently sheared into fragments ranging from 200 to 500 bp using a Bioruptor Pico sonication device (Diagenode, Belgium). The fragmented DNA was purified using TruSeq magnetic beads, and the fragment size distribution was validated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer equipped with High Sensitivity DNA Reagents (5067-4627, Agilent, USA). Samples falling outside the target fragment size range were subjected to repeated fragmentation.

Genomic library construction and shotgun metagenomic sequencing

Genomic sequencing libraries were constructed using the TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Preparation Kit (FC-121-4001, Illumina, USA). The construction process included end repair, adapter ligation, index PCR amplification, and final purification, strictly following the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration of the final libraries was re-quantified using the Qubit 1X dsDNA HS Assay Kit. Shotgun metagenomic sequencing was subsequently performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform operating in paired-end 150 bp (PE150) mode utilizing the NovaSeq 6000 XP 4-Lane Kit v1.5 (300 cycles, 20043131, Illumina) at LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China).

Data preprocessing and host depletion

The raw metagenomic sequencing data in fastq format were initially evaluated for quality using FastQC (v0.10.1). To obtain high-quality clean reads, adapter sequences and low-quality bases were trimmed using Cutadapt (v1.9) and fqtrim (v0.94) under default parameters. To eliminate host-derived genetic contamination, the quality-filtered reads were mapped against the chicken reference genome (GRCg6a) using Bowtie2 (v2.2.0), with all host-aligned reads strictly discarded to ensure subsequent analyses exclusively reflected the bacterial community.

Metagenomic assembly and non-redundant gene catalog construction

The host-depleted clean sequences from each sample were de novo assembled into contigs using MEGAHIT (v1.2.9). To ensure assembly reliability, only contigs longer than 500 bp were retained. Coding sequences (CDSs) within the contigs were predicted utilizing MetaGeneMark (v3.2.6), with sequences shorter than 100 nt routinely filtered out. Subsequently, a non-redundant unigene catalog was constructed by clustering the predicted CDSs via CD-HIT (v4.6.1) at a threshold of 95% sequence identity and 90% alignment coverage, with the longest sequence in each cluster chosen as the representative unigene. To determine the relative abundance of the unigenes, the clean reads from each sample were mapped back to the non-redundant unigene catalog using Bowtie2 (v2.2.0). Unigenes supported by ≤ 2 mapped reads across all samples were removed to minimize technical noise. To rigorously account for calculation biases introduced by varying gene lengths and library sequencing depths, the relative abundance of each unigene (Gk, expressed as a percentage) was quantified using the following length-normalized equation:Gk=rkLk×1∑i=1nriLi×100 where rk represents the number of mapped reads aligned to unigene k, Lk is the sequence length of unigene k, and n denotes the total number of non-redundant unigenes in the catalog.

Microbiota composition and diversity analysis

To characterize the structural shifts in the gut microbiota driven by different rearing systems, alpha diversity indices and beta diversity parameters based on Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) using Bray-Curtis dissimilarities were calculated utilizing the Vegan R package (v2.7.2). Taxonomic compositional profiles were visualized using stacked bar charts at both the phylum and genus levels to delineate the macroscopic structural reconfigurations.

Co-occurrence network construction and ecological assembly mechanisms

To explore the complex deterministic interactions and topological architecture of the gut microbiota, robust meta-community networks were constructed. Microbial taxa with a mean relative abundance < 0.01% or a prevalence < 20% across all samples were initially filtered out to minimize technical noise and spurious correlations. Co-occurrence networks were constructed based on Spearman's rank correlations utilizing the rcorr function in the Hmisc R package (v5.2.4). To ensure the scale-free property of the networks, a data-driven thresholding approach systematically scanned correlation coefficients from 0.60 to 0.95. The optimal correlation threshold was determined when the coefficient of determination (R2) for the power-law degree distribution first exceeded 0.8. Edges representing strong and significant correlations (Spearman's |R| optimal threshold, P < 0.01) were retained, and isolated nodes were excluded.

Sample-specific sub-networks were subsequently extracted by preserving taxa with a relative abundance > 0.01% within each respective sample. Global topological properties including average degree, clustering coefficient, and network density were computed using the Igraph package (v2.2.1). A composite Network Complexity index was generated for each sample by extracting the first principal component (PC1) via Principal Component Analysis (PCA) based on these topological metrics, wherein average path length and network diameter were inversely weighted.

To elucidate the underlying ecological assembly mechanisms driving these observed topological differences, the Sloan neutral community model was implemented to estimate the relative contribution of stochastic processes versus deterministic processes. Subsequently, to identify keystone taxa and evaluate advanced network stability, fast greedy modularity optimization was applied. Nodes were classified into four topological roles based on their within-module connectivity (Zi) and among-module connectivity (Pi), encompassing peripherals, connectors, module hubs, and network hubs. Network robustness was evaluated by measuring the decay of natural connectivity under two simulated perturbation scenarios comprising random node removal (averaged over 20 iterations) and targeted attacks based on node degree. Furthermore, NetShift analysis was performed to identify critical driver taxa associated with network rewiring between rearing systems. Key drivers were statistically defined as nodes exhibiting an absolute change in betweenness centrality (|Δbetweenness|) exceeding the mean plus 2.5 standard deviations of all centrality shifts.

Resistome and mobilome annotation and profiling

The annotation and quantification of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) were executed utilizing the ARGs-OAP v3.0 pipeline against the Structured ARG reference database (SARG). Simultaneously, mobile genetic elements (MGEs) were annotated against a comprehensive custom-curated MGE reference database (Pärnänen et al., 2018). Briefly, this database integrates the PlasmidFinder database with targeted mobile determinants extracted from the NCBI nucleotide database, specifically encompassing coding sequences (CDSs) for insertion sequences (e.g., IS and ISCR families), integrases (e.g., intI1, intI2), transposases (e.g., tnpA, tniA, istA), the Class 1 integron marker qacEΔ1, and Tn916 transposon open reading frames (ORFs). The reference database was strictly pre-filtered using VSEARCH to remove redundancy at a 99% identity threshold, and sequences outside the length range of 100 to 4,000 bp were excluded.

Both ARG and MGE annotations were subjected to rigorous alignment thresholds (E-value ≤ 1e-7, sequence identity ≥ 80%, and alignment coverage ≥ 75%) to ensure high precision. To account for variations in sequencing depth and bacterial biomass, the absolute read counts were normalized against the 16S rRNA gene copy numbers extracted from the corresponding metagenomes. Differential abundance analyses of ARG and MGE types/subtypes were performed and visualized utilizing heatmaps coupled with lollipop charts, representing Z-score normalized relative abundances and Log2 fold changes, respectively. Furthermore, identified ARGs were systematically stratified into distinct functional resistance mechanisms and public health risk ranks (Rank I-IV) based on their mobility and host pathogenicity.

Longitudinal dynamics and successional trajectories

To elucidate the temporal dynamics across the four developmental stages, longitudinal successional trajectories of the resistome and mobilome were characterized utilizing PCoA based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities. Trajectory paths were visually delineated by sequentially connecting the community centroids over time. The main and interactive effects of the rearing system and developmental stage on the structural dissimilarities were statistically evaluated using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA, with 999 permutations) via the Vegan R package. Furthermore, to ascertain the mechanistic coupling and structural concordance between the ARG and MGE profiles, symmetric Procrustes rotation and Mantel tests based on Spearman's rank correlations were conducted with 999 permutations to evaluate the goodness-of-fit (M2) and Mantel correlation (r) metrics.

Quantitative temporal dynamics of total ARG and MGE abundances were plotted representing the mean ± standard error (SE). To deeply profile the temporal persistence and intersection patterns of these determinants, advanced UpSet analyses were performed utilizing the UpSetR package (v1.4.0). To rigorously execute this, the core resistome and mobilome were algorithmically defined based on strict intra-group prevalence thresholds (> 40% for ARGs and > 50% for MGEs within each specific sampling cohort). This dynamic thresholding strategy enabled the precise categorization of distinct features into shared core elements, rearing-specific (CR or FR) persistent modules, and environmentally driven transient signatures.

Integrative network analysis and potential transmission tracking of the resistome

To evaluate the overall structural concordance among the gut microbiota, mobilome, and resistome, Procrustes analysis and Mantel tests were performed based on Bray-Curtis distance matrices. The significance of the Procrustes rotation was assessed using 999 permutations. Subsequently, to explicitly quantify the independent and shared explanatory power of the microbiota, mobilome, and rearing systems on the resistome variation, variance partitioning analysis (VPA) was executed using the Vegan R package. In the VPA model, the first three principal coordinates (PC1-PC3) derived from the PCoA of Hellinger-transformed microbiota and MGE abundance matrices, alongside a dummy matrix representing the rearing systems, were utilized as explanatory variables.

To infer potential horizontal gene transfer (HGT) routes, a comprehensive tripartite co-occurrence network (microbiota-MGE-ARG) was constructed. Prior to network inference, features present in less than 20% of the samples were filtered to reduce spurious correlations. Pairwise Spearman's rank correlations were calculated, and robust inter-domain edges were retained based on strict thresholds (Spearman's |R| > 0.50 and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P < 0.01). To evaluate the effect of rearing systems on overall network connectivity, the node degrees were calculated using the Igraph package and compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. To profile specific modular niche affinities, chord diagrams visualizing the top 30 strongest synergistic connections for both the microbiota-MGE and MGE-ARG dimensions were generated using the Circlize package. The globally dominant transposase gene tnpA was deliberately excluded in parallel visualizations to unmask specific plasmid-mediated and targeted MGE-driven interactions.

Finaly, an ARG-anchored targeted potential transmission tracking analysis was performed to delineate environment-specific putative dissemination mechanisms. The networks were explicitly anchored to the distinct ARGs significantly enriched in the FR and CR groups, respectively. The holistic network-inferred transmission routes, from microbial hosts to mobile vectors and subsequently to the targeted ARGs, were visualized as Sankey diagrams using the Ggalluvial R package. To elucidate the primary backbone of these routes, only the top 60 strongest network-inferred tripartite transmission paths, ranked by the average correlation intensity of their sequential linkages, were extracted and visualized.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and graphical visualizations were executed within the R programming environment (v4.3.0). Differences between the two independent rearing systems were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Where applicable, P values were strictly adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR procedure. A threshold of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Results

Rearing systems alter gut microbial diversity and community architecture

We initially assessed alpha diversity to evaluate the impact of different rearing systems on the gut microbiota. The analysis revealed pronounced and divergent differences between the two rearing systems (Fig. 1A). Specifically, the free-range (FR) group exhibited a significantly higher observed richness compared to the cage-reared (CR) group (P < 0.05). Conversely, the CR group displayed significantly elevated Shannon and Simpson indices (P < 0.001), indicating a greater degree of community evenness within the confined CR rearing system. This divergence in within-sample diversity extended to the overall community structure. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities demonstrated that the microbial communities of the CR and FR groups segregated into discrete and non-overlapping clusters (Fig. 1B). Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) confirmed that the rearing system significantly affected gut microbiota composition, accounting for 15.8% of the total variation (R2 = 0.158, P < 0.001).

Taxonomic profiling was subsequently conducted to identify the specific microbial taxa driving this structural segregation. At the phylum level, Bacteroidota and Bacillota were the dominant taxa across all samples, constituting the core microbiota (Fig. 1C). Despite this shared core, significant compositional shifts were evident between the rearing systems. The CR gut microbiota showed a marked expansion of Pseudomonadota (P < 0.001) and Bacillota (P < 0.01) relative to the FR group, alongside a significant upregulation of Thermodesulfobacteriota (P < 0.01). In contrast, the FR group had a significantly higher relative abundance of Actinomycetota (P < 0.01) and Spirochaetota (P < 0.001).

At the genus level, core taxa exhibited distinct colonization patterns that contributed to these phylum-level differences (Fig. 1D). Although Bacteroides remained the predominant genus across all samples with no significant disparity between the groups (P > 0.05), other core members demonstrated highly rearing system-specific enrichment. The genus Alistipes emerged as a strong discriminatory taxon for the CR group, exhibiting a significantly increased abundance compared to the FR group (P < 0.0001). Consistently, the genera Desulfovibrio (P < 0.05) and Faecalibacterium (P < 0.05) were also significantly enriched under cage-reared conditions. Conversely, Prevotella (P < 0.01) and Mordavella (P < 0.05) were significantly enriched in the FR group, which also contained a notably larger proportion of unclassified bacterial genera (P < 0.05).

Topological architectures of gut microbial co-occurrence networks

In addition to compositional changes, the rearing systems affected the interaction patterns of the gut microbiota. Co-occurrence networks constructed from the metacommunity data revealed stark structural reconfigurations between the two groups (Fig. 2A). Compared to the FR rearing system, the CR group presented a more complex and densely connected network. Specifically, the CR network exhibited significantly higher edge numbers, average degree, graph density, network centralization, and overall complexity metrics (P < 0.05; Fig. 2B). Conversely, the network diameter of the FR group was significantly higher than that of the CR group (P = 0.02). Topological role analysis based on within-module connectivity (Zi) and among-module connectivity (Pi) was used to evaluate the distribution of keystone taxa. Notably, no module hubs (Zi > 2.5, Pi ≤ 0.6) or network hubs (Zi > 2.5, Pi > 0.6) were identified in either rearing system. Instead, the keystone taxa consisted entirely of connectors (Pi > 0.6) (Fig. 3C). This analysis identified more than 10 connectors in the CR group, whereas only four connectors were identified in the FR group. These connectors belonged primarily to the phyla Bacillota and Bacteroidota. Prominent connectors in the CR network included Anaerobutyricum hallii, Christensenella sp., Enterocloster citroniae, and Bacteroides xylanisolvens, along with Firmicutes bacterium CAG:475 and Pseudoflavonifractor sp. An85. The four connectors in the FR network were Anaerobutyricum hallii, Luxibacter massiliensis, an unclassified Bacteroidota, and an unclassified Bacteroidia bacterium.

NetShift analysis was subsequently applied to identify the microbial drivers of this structural rewiring between the two rearing systems (Fig. 3D). The analysis identified Bacteroides sp. CAG:1076, Clostridium sp. CAG:508, and Sporobacter termitidis as CR-exclusive drivers. The FR network was driven by a different set of taxa, including Catenibacillus scindens, Alistipes onderdonkii, Bacteroides sp. GM023, Tannerella forsythia, Clostridium sp., and Hoylesella timonensis. Notably, Pseudoflavonifractor sp. An85, which was identified as a connector in the CR network, emerged as a key structural driver in the FR network. This indicates that this specific taxon underwent substantial topological rewiring and played a critical role in mediating the community structural shift between the two rearing systems.

These clear variations in network topology and keystone taxa significantly affected the overall network robustness. The natural connectivity of the CR group was consistently lower than that of the FR group under both random node removal and targeted attacks. Under targeted attack, the stability of the two networks converged only when the node removal proportion exceeded 40%. Under random removal, the difference in natural connectivity persisted until 80% of the nodes were lost (Fig. 3E). Taken together, these mathematical and topological configurations collectively indicate that the intensive cage system alters the ecological balance of the avian gut microbiota, shifting the community from a stochastically governed equilibrium toward a more deterministic, highly connected, yet topologically fragile architecture characterized by altered niche selection and lower network resilience.

Metagenomic data were analyzed using the ARGs-OAP pipeline to comprehensively characterize the ARG profiles. A total of 23 ARG types were identified across all samples (Fig. 4A). Discussion

As the poultry industry shifts toward sustainable and welfare-friendly production models, understanding the impact of rearing systems on the host gut microbiota and resistome is critical for advancing healthy farming practices and addressing global "One Health" challenges (Hernando-Amado et al., 2019; Larsson and Flach, 2022). Using the Lueyang Black-boned chicken, an indigenous breed characterized by high genetic and microecological diversity, this study investigated the dynamics of antimicrobial resistance under intensive cage-reared (CR) and free-range (FR) systems. Rather than viewing resistance merely as a product of direct antibiotic selection, our findings indicate that distinct rearing systems may shape the horizontal gene transfer (HGT) pathways of high-risk antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) by altering community assembly and the topological resilience of the gut microbiota. This process selectively favors specific mobile genetic elements (MGEs), offering a mechanistic framework of how distinct agricultural practices may indirectly influence resistome expansion.

The rearing system fundamentally altered the community assembly mechanisms and topological structure of the gut microbiota. Microbial community assembly is governed by a balance of deterministic processes and stochastic ecological drift (Nemergut et al., 2013). In the FR system, constant exposure to soil and vegetation provided a diverse exogenous microbial seed bank. This stochastic dispersal fostered a gut ecological niche characterized by high species redundancy (Cui et al., 2017; Ocejo et al., 2019). In contrast, the high stocking density, uniform diets, and intensive management of the CR system exerted strong deterministic selection, significantly enriching taxa such as Desulfovibrio and members of the Pseudomonadota phylum. This aligns with extensive observations in commercial poultry flocks, where environmental stress consistently correlates with the expansion of sulfate-reducing bacteria and opportunistic pathogens (Kers et al., 2018). In poultry, the overrepresentation of these taxa is frequently linked to altered sulfur metabolism and gut mucosal inflammation (Rubio et al., 2015).

Crucially, this reinforcement of deterministic selection in the intensive CR environment is mathematically supported by our neutral model configurations. Although high coefficients of determination (R2 > 0.80) and equivalent migration rates (m ≈ 1) indicate that stochastic drift governed the baseline assembly of the broader background biosphere in both systems, the true footprint of deterministic stress is reflected by the specific taxa drifting away from neutral expectations. Within the CR system, key stress-associated and opportunistic clades, most notably Desulfovibrio, exhibited severe deviations, falling entirely outside the 95% confidence intervals of the neutral curve. This selective deviation demonstrates that while neutral drift accommodates the transient or non-interactive microbiota, the chronic confinement and microenvironmental homogeneity of the cage system exert a highly localized, intense deterministic selection that forces adapted core mutualists and opportunistic pathogens out of stochastic equilibrium. Furthermore, this fundamental difference in assembly dynamics logically explains the divergent alpha-diversity patterns. The higher observed richness in FR chickens is likely driven by continuous foraging in open environments, which constantly introduces low-abundance environmental microorganisms that skew the community distribution. Conversely, the standardized CR system effectively blocks external microbial influx but exerts a uniform selective pressure, allowing adapted core mutualists to proliferate in a balanced manner, ultimately translating into elevated community evenness and higher Shannon and Simpson indices.

This taxonomic shift corresponded with a distinct architecture of microbial interactions. Network analysis showed that the CR system promoted a highly dense and connected co-occurrence network. Ecological theory suggests that such high connectivity often leads to topological fragility (Coyte et al., 2015). In the competitive CR rearing system, microbes likely form cross-feeding networks heavily reliant on a few specific hub nodes. Our robustness simulations demonstrated that the loss of these keystone taxa renders the CR network highly susceptible to structural disruption. Thus, while intensive cage-rearing may maximize short-term production efficiency, it appears to compromise the overall resilience of the host gut microbiota, potentially creating vulnerabilities for the colonization of exogenous ARGs and impeding effective disease prevention.

This altered microecological stability directly influenced the gut resistome profile, presenting a distinct contrast in ARG abundance, functional survival strategies, and actual clinical risk. Set intersection analysis revealed a robust core resistome comprising exactly 118 ARG subtypes shared across all sampling time points. This core architecture represents the basal genetic reservoir inherent to the avian host, persisting independently of external rearing pressures (Lu et al., 2014). Beyond this conserved core, the rearing systems drove highly divergent resistance trajectories.

Based on our quantitative assessments, Rank II ARGs dominated the overall resistome landscape, accounting for over 50% of the average relative abundance. The FR group exhibited a significantly higher absolute abundance of total ARGs, primarily driven by the enrichment of these Rank II determinants. Mechanistically, the FR resistome was characterized by an upregulation of antibiotic target protection and target alteration strategies. Rank II genes, typically executing these specific mechanisms, represent a vast commensal resistance pool sustained by the profound microbial richness and continuous exogenous microbial influx characteristic of the free-range system (Zhang et al., 2021). Furthermore, the FR-specific resistome exclusively incorporated diverse vancomycin resistance clusters (vanXD, vanF, and vanYM) alongside targeted tetracycline determinants (tet(K), tet(36)). The acquisition of these specific traits strongly reflects the continuous influx of soil-borne bacteria, as intact van and tet gene clusters are highly prevalent in natural soil reservoirs and environmental Firmicutes (D’Costa et al., 2006; Forsberg et al., 2012). Within this broad pool, intrinsic tet (often functioning via ribosomal protection) and erm (functioning via target methylation) classes are universally documented in global poultry and swine microbiomes (Zhu et al., 2013; Xiong et al., 2018).

Despite a lower overall ARG abundance and a reduced proportion of Rank II genes, the CR group enriched for critical high-risk (Rank I) clinical resistance subtypes. This structural partition underscores that a higher total resistance volume does not necessarily equate to elevated biosecurity threats; while the free-range system expands the background commensal resistome pool, the intensive cage environment tends to actively concentrate high-threat determinants. Set intersection analysis demonstrated that the CR-specific resistome exclusively harbored an extensive repertoire of beta-lactamases, specifically encompassing OXA-85, OXA-449, OXA-451, OXA-576, TEM-7, and TEM-192. The intensive confinement and dense housing conditions likely act as a strong selective environment, correlating with the enrichment of these highly mobile and clinically relevant extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) (Wellington et al., 2013). Furthermore, differential abundance analysis revealed that the CR system favored a multidrug-resistant profile encompassing sulfonamide, chloramphenicol, and critically, polymyxin and vancomycin resistance genes, beyond specific aminoglycoside determinants such as APH(3′)-IIIa. The enrichment of resistance against these last-resort antibiotics raises significant biosecurity concerns (Shen et al., 2020). Functionally, this high-risk proliferation in the CR group was accompanied by a significant enrichment in antibiotic target replacement mechanisms. Target replacement strategies, such as alternative dihydropteroate synthases encoding sulfonamide resistance, are fundamentally associated with highly mobile multidrug-resistant cassettes carried by opportunistic pathogens (Mazel, 2006). This mechanistic and risk-level shift is likely driven by the implicit co-selection pressure inherent to intensive agricultural management. Even without direct antibiotic supplementation, commercial poultry diets formulated with trace minerals, such as copper and zinc, and routine facility disinfectants exert persistent stress-related selective pressures. Continuous exposure to environmental heavy metals effectively co-selects for diverse highly mobile resistance mechanisms through cross-resistance (Pal et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2018). These findings highlight the necessity for global antimicrobial resistance surveillance in poultry farms to incorporate multidimensional risk assessments integrating functional mechanisms and mobility, rather than relying solely on total ARG abundance (Zhang et al., 2021).

The proliferation of these high-risk ARGs requires specific mobile vectors to navigate the microbial network. Variance partitioning analysis (VPA) indicated that the mobilome independently explained 10.41% of ARG variation, suggesting that HGT partially uncouples resistome dynamics from host phylogenetic constraints (Yin et al., 2023). Detailed profiling of the mobilome revealed distinctly divergent mobilization strategies. The FR-specific profile was associated with diverse broad-host-range plasmids, evidenced by the exclusive presence of IncFII, IncHI2A, and ColRNAI replicons, coupled with specific environmental insertion elements (IS26, ISBf10). Conversely, despite a lower total MGE abundance, differential abundance analysis demonstrated that the CR resistome heavily relied on clinically relevant mobilization elements. Class 1 and Class 2 integrase genes (intI1, intI2), the structural marker qacEΔ1, active insertion sequences (IS91), and targeted transposase variants (tnpA4, tnpA1133) co-occurred exclusively in the CR group. Functionally, qacEΔ1 acts as a critical antiseptic resistance determinant closely embedded within the 3′ conserved segment of Class 1 integrons, thereby mediating resistance to routine facility disinfectants. Concurrently, insertion sequences and transposases, such as IS91, ISBf10, and tnpA, function as active intracellular recombinases capable of flanking, excising, and capturing discrete resistance gene cassettes, effectively decoupling ARGs from host chromosomal constraints and driving their transposition into diverse conjugative vectors. The exclusive co-presence of intI1 alongside its classic structural marker qacEΔ1 indicates the intense selection of intact Class 1 integrons. This underscores that integron-mediated recombination plays a dominant role in capturing and accumulating multidrug resistance cassettes under intensive rearing stress (Gillings et al., 2015).

To precisely map these dissemination routes, we constructed targeted tripartite networks. While the global microbial networks were highly interconnected, targeted tracking revealed a strong vector-dependency in the confined rearing system, where the broad-host-range plasmid IncQ1 emerged as a dominant bridging hub capturing diverse high-risk ARGs. The selection for highly promiscuous vectors like IncQ1 is consistent with the fitness cost theory (San Millan, 2018). While plasmid replication and conjugative transfer impose a considerable metabolic burden on the host, the high animal density, high-frequency contact, and continuous co-selection pressures in the CR system likely provide survival benefits that offset these metabolic costs. Under such crowded conditions, utilizing promiscuous plasmids for rapid cross-species conjugation appears to be an efficient survival strategy (Partridge et al., 2018). Additionally, the absence of extensive ARG-MGE linkages for the remaining CR-enriched ARGs indicates that their persistence relies heavily on vertical clonal expansion. Conversely, the broader mobilome in the FR group was characterized by an upregulation of transposases (such as tnpA) and specific insertion sequences like ISBf10, forming robust multilateral correlations. In the fluctuating and highly heterogeneous FR rearing system, bacteria carrying large conjugative plasmids may face severe fitness disadvantages (Carroll and Wong, 2018). Therefore, transposition-driven gene movement, which facilitates the integration of resistance genes into the host chromosome, provides a mechanism for stable vertical transmission with lower maintenance costs (von Wintersdorff et al., 2016). This indicates that rearing management selects for optimal MGE vectors, thereby dictating specific potential network-inferred routes of ARG dissemination.

Beyond static cross-sectional profiles, longitudinal tracking provides critical insights into the successional trajectories of the gut resistome. The highly significant Procrustes concordance between ARG and MGE configurations across time points mathematically confirms that the temporal evolution of the resistome is deterministically tethered to mobilome flux (Xiong et al., 2018). Notably, the period between days 120 and 150 emerged as a critical developmental window for resistome maturation in both rearing systems. This trajectory aligns with extensive longitudinal studies in egg-laying hens, which demonstrate profound successional restructuring of the caecal microbiota during the transition into early adulthood and peak production periods (Videnska et al., 2014).

Following this initial maturation phase, the longitudinal trajectories of the two rearing systems diverged completely, highlighting that the free-range resistome reached an ecological equilibrium while the cage-reared microbiota remained in a continued genetic flux during the observed production period. The FR group achieved ecological stabilization, evidenced by a progressive decline in the acquisition of transient ARGs, culminating in a complete absence of novel stage-specific ARGs by day 360. This temporal resilience aligns with the observation that resistance in the FR system is primarily governed by stable chromosomal integration via transposons and dominated by commensal background genes (von Wintersdorff et al., 2016; Carroll and Wong, 2018). Unlike this stabilizing FR cohort, the CR group failed to reach a structural equilibrium. Stage-specific turnover rates indicated the continuous acquisition of transient ARGs and MGEs throughout the entire production cycle, terminating in a major mobilome reconfiguration at day 360. This temporal volatility directly mirrors the topological fragility observed in the CR microbial network. This continuous structural instability under chronic environmental stress exemplifies the “Anna Karenina principle” in host-associated microbial communities (Zaneveld et al., 2017). The confined cage system, coupled with continuous exposure to co-selection factors, imposes a chronic and non-resolving stress on the host. Adapting to this persistent pressure correlates with the continued genetic flux observed in the CR microbiota during the monitored production period. Rather than establishing a stable climax community, these microbes continuously acquire and shuffle high-risk mobile elements, including broad-host-range plasmids and integrons (San Millan, 2018). Such longitudinal instability further exacerbates the biosecurity risks associated with intensive rearing, highlighting the critical need for management interventions that alleviate environmental stress to maintain gut health.

Several limitations of the present investigation should be acknowledged. First, our pipeline was restricted to a single Chinese indigenous chicken breed within a single geographic production setting, meaning that the generalizability of these ecological patterns to other fast-growing commercial high-performance broiler strains requires further validation. Second, the host-vector-ARG dissemination pathways were mathematically inferred via correlation-based tripartite networks rather than through direct, experimental confirmations of in vivo horizontal gene transfer events. Future work utilizing single-cell metagenomics or epicPCR will be highly valuable to empirically validate these network-inferred transmission modules.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides comprehensive metagenomic evidence that rearing systems dictate the evolutionary trajectories and dissemination pathways of the avian gut resistome. Rather than exerting direct antibiotic selective pressure, distinct rearing practices indirectly reshape the resistome by altering microbial community assembly and the topological resilience of the gut microbiota. The intensive cage-reared (CR) system imposes a chronic deterministic stress that fosters a fragile, highly connected microbial network. This environment is associated with the enrichment of high-risk, clinically relevant resistance determinants, including extended-spectrum beta-lactamases and multidrug resistance cassettes. Crucially, the dissemination of these high-risk ARGs under CR conditions is heavily linked to broad-host-range plasmids, such as IncQ1, which drives the gut microbiota into continued genetic flux during the observed production period. In contrast, the free-range (FR) system promotes stochastic microbial assembly and long-term ecological stability. The FR resistome is predominantly characterized by commensal background resistance traits, whose dissemination is largely mediated by transposon-driven chromosomal integration, thereby conferring robust temporal resilience. Ultimately, our findings underscore that mitigating environmental stress through welfare-friendly rearing management serves as a critical ecological intervention to limit the proliferation of mobile, high-risk resistance elements. From a global “One Health,” perspective, these insights highlight the urgent necessity to upgrade current agricultural antimicrobial resistance surveillance frameworks by systematically prioritizing functional risk assessments and the tracking of clinically relevant mobilization elements.
