Oral Health Service Utilisation Behaviour and Its Influencing Factors Among Older Adults: An Empirical Study Based on the Composite Health Behaviour Model

Xiaoyuan Pan, Shuwen Su, Jianli Li, Leyi Han, Xinyue Chen

Abstract

AbstractBackgroundChina’s aging population faces significant unmet oral health care needs, with delayed service utilisation exacerbating disease burden. Currently, there is a lack of empirical research applying composite health models and analysing pathway relationships among influencing factors in this population.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from July to September 2023 among 356 older adults in Foshan and Guangzhou using a theory-driven questionnaire. Group comparisons used independent t tests/analysis of variance for sociodemographic variables. Structural equation modelling (SEM) with maximum likelihood estimation was employed to validate pathways.ResultsSignificant differences were observed: sex and age affected individual model variables; education, residence location, and economic status influenced multiple variables; and living arrangement showed no significant effects. Only 34.04% of older adults proactively sought oral health knowledge, while 65.03% did not receive regular dental checkups. Just 51.84% sought treatment (scaling, fillings, extractions, dentures, implants). When acute oral pain disrupted daily life, 25.15% still delayed medical care. SEM revealed that behavioural intention/control had the strongest total effect on utilisation behaviour (β = 0.593) followed by perceived benefits (β = 0.404), subjective norms exerted only indirect effects (β = 0.216), and both subjective norms (β = 0.365) and perceived benefits (β = 0.282) directly influenced behavioural intention and control.ConclusionOral health service utilisation among older adults remains passive. The present authors’ model identifies three key modifiable factors: behavioural intention and control, perceived benefits, and subjective norms. Strategies to enhance service utilisation should focus on strengthening social support and modifying unrealistic health perceptions.Keywords: geriatric oral health, health belief model, health services utilisation, structural equation modelling, theory of planned behaviour

Oral health service utilisation is defined as ‘the actual amount of dental services received by residents, reflecting both service provision and institutional efficiency’. Regular utilisation enables early detection and management of oral diseases through preventive care, effectively maintaining oral health while reducing long-term treatment frequency and costs. As China’s population ages rapidly, with the number of adults aged ≥ 60 years increasing by 5.44% from 2010 to 2020 and reaching 264.02 million (18.7% of the population) in 2020, older adults face disproportionately poor oral health. The Fourth National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey of China revealed alarming statistics among those aged 65 to 74 years: 98% had decayed/missing/filled teeth (DMFT), 90.3% exhibited dental calculus, and only 9.3% maintained periodontal health. This underscores an urgent need to address oral health disparities in China’s aging population.

These findings indicate suboptimal oral health behaviours among older Chinese adults, where behavioural factors represent critical modifiable targets. Influences on older adults’ behaviour are multifaceted, yet few domestic studies apply established behavioural theories to investigate oral health service utilisation. Existing research inadequately explores psychosocial determinants and lacks theoretical foundations for interventions. Behavioural theories, beyond categorising and predicting oral health habits, guide interventions from individual to population levels. As emphasised by the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), ‘theory-based behaviour change interventions demonstrate greater efficacy than atheoretical approaches’. Globally, preventive dentistry increasingly employs behavioural theories to enhance self-care and treatment adherence, evidenced by the 11th European Workshop on Periodontology recommending psychological methods for plaque control in periodontal management. Consequently, theoretically grounded identification of influencing factors and pathways represents the vanguard of oral health interventions.

Among behavioural frameworks, the health belief model (HBM) and theory of planned behaviour (TPB) are most established. This study therefore integrates the HBM and TPB to develop a composite model specific to older adults’ oral health service utilisation, aiming to elucidate behavioural mechanisms and inform targeted interventions.

Materials and methods

Theoretical Framework and Composite Model Construction

There is a growing consensus that theory-based behavioural research offers greater explanatory and predictive reliability. However, existing theoretical models remain insufficient to fully account for the variance observed in oral health behaviours. Consequently, future model development may need to consider further refinements to existing frameworks to enhance their predictive capacity. Some scholars have suggested that integrating two or more theoretical models to provide novel analytical perspectives on behaviour change may represent an important direction for the evolution of health behaviour theory.,
 Nevertheless, the combined application of models remains context-specific: given the substantial heterogeneity across different health behaviours, even identical constructs may exert differential effects depending on the behavioural domain. To date, integrated theoretical models have been developed primarily for specific behaviours rather than as universal frameworks.

Drawing upon the classic HBM and TPB frameworks, this study developed an integrated and optimised composite model tailored to the characteristics of older adults, with the aim of better explaining oral health service utilisation behaviour in this population. The HBM and TPB are conceptually complementary: the former emphasises perceived threats, benefits, and barriers as drivers of health behaviour, whereas the latter highlights the roles of subjective norms and behavioural intention. Given the conceptual overlap between the two models, the present authors streamlined similar dimensions and proposed a hypothesised composite model. The detailed model integration process, including construct merging and questionnaire development, is described in the methodological preprint. In brief, the composite model comprises six latent variables: perceived benefit (PBE), perceived barrier (PBA), perceived threat (PT), subjective norm (SN), behavioural intention and control (BIC), and behaviour (B). Their definitions are presented in Table 1, and the hypothesised pathways are illustrated in Fig 1.

Research Methods

Based on the hypothesised composite model described above, a structured questionnaire was developed through a systematic process of construct merging, item generation, expert review, and pilot testing, as detailed in the methodological preprint. In brief, the final questionnaire comprised 26 items distributed across six dimensions corresponding to the model variables: PBE, PBA, PT, SN, BIC, and B. All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ (1) to ‘strongly agree’ (5), with higher scores indicating more positive perceptions or behaviours. The questionnaire demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties: exploratory factor analysis extracted six principal components accounting for 62.616% of the cumulative variance, with factor loadings ranging from 0.517 to 0.817, and the overall Cronbach α was 0.864.

Sociodemographic characteristics were assessed using a structured set of single-choice questions. Participants were asked to select their sex (male/female), age (60 to 69, 70 to 79, ≥ 80 years), education level (primary school, junior high school, high school/secondary specialised school, college/associate degree, master’ s degree or above), place of residence (rural/urban), economic status (very affluent, relatively comfortable, financially sufficient, some financial difficulty, severe financial difficulty), and living arrangements (living alone, living with spouse, living with children, other).

Questionnaire design

A questionnaire was self-developed by integrating the HBM and the TPB. The present study referenced established scales and drew upon previous research findings to formulate items for each dimension. Items for B, PBE, PBA, and PT were developed based on the research by Nakazono et al and Xiang et al, items for SN were adapted from the work of Liu, and items for BIC were developed referencing studies by Ju and Sun.

The questionnaire was specifically designed considering the behavioural and psychological characteristics of the older adults. It underwent refinement through expert review and a pilot pre-survey before finalisation. The final questionnaire comprised 26 items across six dimensions: B, PBE, PBA, PT, SN, and BIC. Given the potentially limited comprehension abilities of older adult respondents, all items utilised a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ (scored 1) to ‘strongly agree’ (scored 5), with higher scores indicating more positive responses. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) extracted six principal components, accounting for a cumulative variance contribution rate of 62.616%. Factor loadings for all items ranged from 0.517 to 0.817. The overall Cronbach α coefficient for the scale was 0.864, indicating good reliability and validity.

Data collection

This cross-sectional study was conducted from July to September 2023. Participants were older adults recruited by convenience sampling from five residential communities in Foshan and Guangzhou, China. Recruitment was facilitated through community bulletin boards, local neighbourhood committee announcements, and on-site promotion during free oral health talk events. Eligible individuals were those aged ≥ 60 years, cognitively able to complete the questionnaire (either independently or with assistance), and willing to provide written informed consent.

Questionnaires were administered using paper forms and electronic surveys (through the Wenjuanxing platform), with the mode of completion determined by participant preference and mobility. Participants completed the questionnaire either in community health service centres or in their own homes, while trained researchers provided standardised verbal guidance. All research assistants were oral health care professionals who used a standardised script to explain the study purpose without leading responses, and performed real-time checks on questionnaire completeness. Before participation, all individuals were informed of the study purpose, the voluntary nature of their participation, and the confidentiality of their responses. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Foshan University (ethics approval no. 2022001), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Two independent researchers applied predefined exclusion criteria: patterned responses (e.g., straight-lining), contradictory answers within the same dimension, and logical inconsistencies (e.g., reporting no dental visits but detailing specific treatments). A total of 356 older adults were recruited and 352 questionnaires were returned. After excluding 26 incomplete or invalid responses, 326 valid questionnaires were retained for analysis. Given the 26 measurement items, the final sample size (n = 326) satisfied the common ‘10 times rule’ (minimum 10 cases per measured item) for structural equation modelling (SEM).,,

Statistical analysis

Data processing and analysis were performed using SPSS (version 19.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and AMOS software (version 24.0; IBM). Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages) were used to characterise the sociodemographic profile of the surveyed older adult participants and describe the current status of their oral health service utilisation behavior. SEM was employed to assess the goodness-of-fit of the integrated model of older adults’ oral health service utilisation behaviour. Effect analysis and path analysis were conducted on the integrated model within the SEM framework.

Results

Analysis of Sociodemographic Characteristics

This study identified the basic characteristics of the surveyed participants across dimensions including sex, age, education level, place of residence, economic status, and living arrangements. Questionnaire results showed that participants were aged ≥60 years, and there were 132 men and 194 women. The proportion of participants with education levels above high school or secondary specialised education was relatively low. Only 11% of the older adults respondents self-reported experiencing family economic difficulties. A majority (82.3%) of the older adults lived with their spouse or children. The sample covered younger-old (60 to 69 years), middle-old (70 to 79 years), and older-old (≥ 80 years) age groups. Specific details are presented in Table 2.

Univariate analysis was employed to examine the variables of sex, age, education level, etc., within the personal background of the older adult participants, aiming to explore differences in psychological and behavioural manifestations of oral health service utilisation across different group characteristics. As these variables could not be incorporated into the SEM, univariate analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 19.0).

Each of the four background variables, namely age, education level, economic status, and place of residence, was divided into at least three groups. Therefore, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to test for between-group differences in each variable across the questionnaire dimensions. Sex and place of residence were divided into only two groups, so independent samples t tests were used for analysis.

The results indicated that significant differences were found for sex and age in individual aspects of the model variables and also across multiple aspects for participants grouped by education level, place of residence, and economic status, but no significant differences were found among participants grouped by living arrangements (P > 0.05). Detailed data are presented in Table 3.

Descriptive Analysis of Oral Health Service Utilisation Behaviour

In the behavioural questionnaire, only 34.04% of older adult participants reported proactively learning about oral health knowledge. A substantial majority (65.03%) indicated they did not proactively seek regular oral checkups, and only 51.84% would proactively seek dental treatment (e.g., cleaning, fillings, extractions, dentures, or implants). When acute oral pain affected daily life, 25.15% still reported not seeking timely medical care (Table 4). These empirical findings indicate low oral health awareness among older adults; both the awareness and behaviour of proactively and regularly seeking oral health services were at alarmingly low levels, representing a key intervention point. They also show inefficient health service utilisation; current oral health service utilisation among older adults is predominantly passive, with low efficiency. Pain-driven utilisation (seeking care only when experiencing discomfort) is widespread.

Validation and Analysis of SEM for Older Adults’ Oral Health Service Utilisation Behaviour

Model fit verification

Based on the theoretically hypothesised model of older adults’ oral health service utilisation behaviour established previously, SEM was constructed using AMOS (version 24.0). In this hypothesised model, four variables, namely PBE, PBA, PT, and SN, were specified as exogenous latent variables, while BIC and B were specified as endogenous latent variables. This framework constituted the SEM for older adults’ oral health service utilisation behaviour, schematically represented in Fig 2.

Prior to conducting path analysis, the reliability and validity of the hypothesised model must be established through model fit assessment; that is, by importing data into the constructed model for estimation. Prevailing methodological consensus holds that overall Goodness-of-Fit Indices provide a feasible means to evaluate the model’s fit, verifying the extent to which the hypothesised model aligns with the observed data. Consequently, this study employed a comprehensive set of overall fit indices to assess model adequacy. The global fit of the structural equation model was evaluated across three categories: Absolute Fit Indices, Incremental Fit Indices, and Parsimonious Fit Indices. The formal questionnaire data were imported into AMOS 24.0 for processing. Absolute Fit Indices are presented in Table 5, Incremental Fit Indices in Table 6, and Parsimonious Fit Indices in Table 7. Synthesising these results, the model demonstrated reasonable fit, indicating that the hypothesised model for this study is acceptable.

Analysis of research path hypothesis verification

A core function of constructing SEM is to conduct an in-depth analysis of the path dependency relationships among multiple complex variables, aiming to reveal potential patterns of interaction between them. Therefore, following the model fit assessment, it is necessary to further test the original research hypotheses. Path analysis, as an effective tool within SEM suitable for handling continuous variable data, builds upon the analysis of correlations between variables to explore and infer potential causal chains.

This study employed the maximum likelihood (ML) method for hypothesis testing of path causal relationships. In testing path coefficients, a research hypothesis is accepted only if the standardised coefficient is significantly different from zero and P < 0.05. Otherwise, the null hypothesis is retained.

Analysis of Table 8 reveals that PBE and SN had significant effects on BIC (P < 0.001), PT and PBA did not have significant effects on BIC (P > 0.05), BIC and PBE had significant effects on B (P < 0.001), and PT, PBA, and SN did not have significant direct effects on B (P > 0.05). Consequently, the hypothesis validation results are presented in Table 9. As shown, hypotheses H1, H2, H7, and H9 were supported in this study, whereas the remaining hypotheses were not.

The magnitude of the path relationships, i.e., the influence between latent variables, is expressed through the path coefficients. Extracting the standardised path coefficients and reconstructing the model diagram based on the supported hypotheses (H1, H2, H7, and H9), the overall path relationships influencing older adults’ oral health service utilisation behaviour are depicted in Fig 3.

As illustrated in Fig 3, the influence of the four latent variables (PBE, SN, BIC, and B) on older adults’ oral health service utilisation behaviour manifests through three primary pathways. The variable BIC has a direct positive effect on B, and this path exhibits the largest total effect (β = 0.593); PBE can both directly positively influence B and indirectly positively influence it through its effect on the BIC variable; and SN can only indirectly positively influence B through the mediating role of the BIC variable.

Effect analysis

The effects within the model can be categorised into direct, indirect, and composite total effects. The direct effect is represented by the standardised path coefficient between variables, the indirect effect is calculated as the product of the standardised path coefficients along a mediated pathway, and the total effect is the sum of the direct and indirect effects. Based on the magnitude of total effects, the primary factors influencing older adults’ oral health service utilisation behaviour were ranked in descending order as BIC, PBE, and SN. Specific values are presented in Table 10.

The empirical data demonstrate that BIC exerted the strongest influence on B (total effect 0.593), PBE had the second strongest influence (total effect 0.404), and SN had the smallest influence, with no direct effect and only an indirect effect (indirect effect 0.216; total effect 0.216). Furthermore, regarding the factors influencing BIC, both SN and PBE exerted only direct effects (0.365 and 0.282, respectively), with no indirect pathways.

Discussion

Principal Findings: Low Utilisation Despite High Need

The Fourth National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey revealed concerning oral health status among the group aged 65 to 74 years, suggesting a ‘high need’ for oral health services. However, the present authors’ study on the current status of oral health service utilisation among older adults in China identified a ‘low utilisation’ phenomenon. This contradiction between high need and low utilisation underscores the critical necessity for interventions targeting older adults’ oral health service utilisation behaviours. Thus, there is an urgent need to intervene on influencing factors to improve the adoption rate of oral health utilisation behaviours and promote older adults’ oral health.

This ‘high need–low utilisation’ paradox is not unique to China, however. A multi-country analysis using World Health Organization Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization (WHO SAGE) data reported that the prevalence of self-reported unmet needs for oral health services among adults aged ≥ 50 years was 60% in China, 80% in Ghana, and 62% in India, with lower education levels consistently associated with higher unmet needs across all three countries. Even in high-income settings with relatively high overall attendance, significant intranational disparities persist. Kovács et al, analysing longitudinal data from 20,803 older adults across 14 European countries, reported that while annual dental care attendance was highest in Scandinavian countries (85.7%), the difference in service utilisation between socioeconomic groups was expanding over time regarding income level and residential area. This suggests that high national average utilisation rates may mask growing internal inequalities, implying that improving overall access alone is insufficient without targeted efforts to reach disadvantaged subpopulations.

These international comparisons collectively underscore that the ‘high need–low utilisation’ contradiction is a global geriatric oral health challenge, transcending national boundaries and health care systems. Therefore, interventions targeting modifiable behavioural and psychosocial determinants, rather than merely expanding service supply, are urgently needed to improve oral health service utilisation among older populations worldwide.

Key Influencing Pathways and Their Implications

The structural equation model revealed three significant pathways (P < 0.001) through which the composite model variables influenced oral health service utilisation behaviour among older adults. The first of these was a direct positive path from BIC to B. When older adults possess stronger behavioural intention and perceived control, their capability and autonomy to perform the behaviour are enhanced. These individuals are also more likely to believe in the effectiveness and benefits of utilising oral health services, thereby increasing the likelihood of performing the behaviour. This finding not only aligns with the fundamental assumptions of TPB but is also supported by multiple empirical studies.,,

The influence path of PBE resembles findings by Kiyak et al. PBE can both directly positively influence B and indirectly influence it through BIC acting as a mediating variable. This conclusion bridges constructs from distinct models, namely PBE from classic HBM and BIC from traditional TPB, establishing a novel influence pathway for older adults’ oral health service utilisation behaviour.

SN did not establish a direct link with B in the composite model. Rather, it exerted an indirect positive effect on older adults’ oral health service utilisation behaviour through the mediating role of BIC. This pathway of SN influencing behaviour solely through indirect means is consistent with the classical TPB.

Practical Implications for Intervention

The newly conceptualised BIC demonstrated the most significant influence on B among all factors (total effect 0.593). Research by Gamma et al confirms the high correlation between behavioural intention and behaviour. Furthermore, studies indicate that perceived behavioural control is a major predictor of both oral health behavioural intention and actual behaviour, explaining 34% of behavioural variance. This supports the role of BIC as a crucial predictor of older adults’ oral health service utilisation behaviour. PBE ranked second in influence (total effect 0.404), followed by SN (no direct effect, only indirect effect = 0.216). In social life, perceived benefit is a powerful driver of individual behaviour. Compared to external directives, elderly individuals are more inclined to perform behaviours they perceive as personally beneficial. Furthermore, regarding the factors influencing BIC, both SN and PBE exerted only direct effects (0.365 and 0.282, respectively). As a vulnerable group, older adults often consult family, friends, or health care professionals when encountering unfamiliar matters. These opinions significantly impact their behavioural intention and control (SN had a larger direct effect on BIC [0.365] than PBE [0.282]). Concurrently with this social influence, older adults often receive tangible assistance from these sources (e.g., family members accompanying them to dental visits). This assistance not only boosts behavioural intention but also directly enhances the resources and capability required to perform oral health service utilisation behaviours.

Based on the identified key dimensions, PBE, SN, and BIC, the present authors propose the following multi-level intervention strategies:

- 1.Enhancing PBE: On an individual/family level, this would involve recognising the decline in self-directed learning capacity among older adults. Family members should actively accompany elders, share relevant oral health knowledge, watch educational television programmes together, consult oral health care books with them, and answer their questions. On a societal level, mainstream media should prioritise oral health promotion. Health care institutions could establish specialised medical teams for community outreach programmes, enhancing interactivity during educational sessions and bridging the gap with the public through community-based initiatives. Government bodies should also optimise the allocation of oral health human resources and reasonably distribute oral health care resources to improve service experiences, thereby enhancing PBE.
- 2.Leveraging SN: On an individual level, it is essential for older adults to actively listen to and consider advice from relatives, friends, or health care personnel regarding oral health. On a family level, families can foster a home environment that values oral health, encourage elders to develop good oral hygiene habits, and regularly remind and accompany them to utilise oral health services. On a societal level, similar to the family approach, society-wide oral health education should be promoted to cultivate a broader societal atmosphere that prioritises oral health.
- 3.Strengthening BIC: This dimension, encompassing classical concepts of behavioural intention and perceived behavioural control, is the most influential in the model and serves as a proximal determinant of behaviour. Recommendations under PBE and SN will inherently contribute to improving BIC levels. Additionally, as BIC includes the capability to perform the behaviour, this can be bolstered by external support. Practical support would include family members accompanying elders to dental appointments and hospitals improving service accessibility and convenience. Genuine care and companionship for elders may be the most effective catalyst for promoting health care–seeking behavior.

Conclusion

Under the analytical framework integrating the HBM and TPB, this study employed empirical data to investigate the key influencing factors and underlying mechanisms of the ‘low utilisation’ phenomenon in oral health service utilisation behaviour among older adults.

Within the composite model, the identified influencing factors operate through distinct pathways. The variable BIC exerts a direct positive effect on B, demonstrating the largest total effect; PBE positively influences older adults’ oral health service utilisation behaviour both directly and indirectly by affecting BIC; and SN can only positively influence older adults’ oral health service utilisation behaviour indirectly, mediated through the BIC variable. Thus, it is recommended to implement strategies that focus on enhancing social support, addressing cognitive misconceptions, and strengthening the expectations and support from family, friends, and health care professionals. These interventions can effectively target SN, BIC, and PBE, ultimately promoting proactive utilisation of oral health services among older adults.

Collectively, this study provides a scientific foundation and effective strategies for improving oral health status among older adults, contributing novel perspectives and insights to advance the process of healthy aging.
