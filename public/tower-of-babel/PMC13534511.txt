A study on the nonlinear impact and mechanism of artificial intelligence application level on corporate carbon emission intensity
Authors: Shunqing Yuan, Lianfang Zhang, Wei Liu, Bin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnder global pressure to reduce carbon emissions, understanding how the level of artificial intelligence (AI) use affects corporate carbon emissions (CCE) is crucial for achieving a green transition. This study, based on provincial panel data from China, employs a combination of two-way fixed effects (TWFE) empirical analysis and structural equation modeling (SEM) to identify the nonlinear effects of AI use levels on corporate carbon emission intensity and their transmission pathways, and conducts robustness and regional heterogeneity tests. The empirical results show that: first, the relationship between AI use levels and corporate carbon emissions exhibits a significant inverted U-shaped curve—in the early stages of development, due to high energy consumption in computing and deployment, AI adoption may temporarily increase carbon emissions; however, after exceeding a critical point, further deepening of use significantly reduces carbon emissions. Second, SEM analysis reveals several key mediating channels: improving green innovation efficiency (GIE), enhancing energy utilization efficiency (EUE), promoting Scientific innovation (SI), and driving industrial structure upgrading (ISU). These pathways collectively amplify the emission reduction effect of AI. Third, regional heterogeneity analysis shows that the AI emission reduction effect is significantly stronger in the eastern region than in the central and western regions. Finally, this study emphasizes the policy implications: while promoting the use of AI, energy structure and incentive mechanisms should be optimized, and differentiated policies should be formulated according to regional characteristics to achieve an AI-driven sustainable low-carbon transformation.Keywords: AI use level, Corporate Carbon Emissions, Green Innovation Efficiency, Energy Efficiency, Two-Way Fixed Effect ModelSubject terms: Complex networks, Complex networks, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

In the context of worldwide climate shift and ‘dual-carbon’ targets, reducing carbon emissions (CEs) has become a core issue of concern to governments and businesses around the world1–3. As one of the countries with the largest total CEs in the world, the governance of CCEs is related to the advancement of China’s green and low-carbon development strategy and also has an important impact on global climate governance4,5. In recent years, the rapid development of AI technology has provided new solutions for enterprises to reduce CEs6,7. The use of artificial intelligence (AI) in smart manufacturing, energy management, supply chain optimization, etc., can help improve EUE and reduce CEs in the production process. At present, the specific impact of AI use level on CCEs is still unclear, especially at the provincial level in China, where there are large differences in AI development levels and industrial structures in different regions. Systematic analysis of the impact of AI use level on CCEs and its mechanism has important academic value and practical guiding significance for policy formulation and practice.

This study aims to explore the impact of AI use level on CCEs and its mechanism of action. Based on the panel data of enterprises in 30 provinces in China from 2007 to 2021, a TWFE model is used to identify the causal relationship between AI use level and CEs, and its impact path is further analyzed through SEM. The study first ensures the reliability of the results through robustness tests, and then examines regional heterogeneity to reveal the CE reduction effect of AI in different regions. The results show that AI use level has a significant inhibitory effect on CCEs, and plays a role through intermediary factors such as GIE, EUE, ISU and scientific and SI, and the emission reduction effect in the ER is more significant.

The main contributions of this study are reflected in the following three aspects:

It expands the empirical research on the relationship between artificial intelligence (AI) and corporate carbon emissions from a nonlinear perspective. Unlike existing studies that mostly assess the emission reduction effect of AI based on linear frameworks, this study introduces a nonlinear setting into a two-way fixed-effects model. Based on the provincial data system of China, it examines the significant inverted U-shaped relationship between the use level of AI and corporate carbon emission intensity, revealing the dynamic characteristics of AI potentially being accompanied by energy consumption expansion and emission reduction optimization at different development stages.

Literature review

Empirical evidence on artificial intelligence and carbon emissions

In recent years, numerous studies have focused on the impact of artificial intelligence (AI) on carbon emissions and energy consumption, forming a rich chain of empirical evidence. Some studies, using panel or cross-sectional data, have found that AI applications help reduce carbon emissions or optimize energy use8–10; Wang Q et al., used generalized moment estimation and dynamic panel thresholds to explore the impact of AI on carbon emissions and emphasized the synergistic effects of energy transition32; Chen & Jin also reported the positive environmental effects of AI applications in fixed-effects regression at the manufacturing level11. Meanwhile, cross-national comparative studies (such as12) show that the environmental effects of AI vary across different countries and development stages (e.g., more significant in samples with higher economic and income levels), indicating spatial and developmental heterogeneity.

Mechanism identification and methodological advances

Several studies have explored the “transmission mechanisms” by which AI influences carbon emissions. Several works using mediation/channel analysis have identified factors such as industrial restructuring and improved energy utilization as important mediators13,14. Methodologically, fixed effects models are widely used to control for unobservable regional or subject heterogeneity15,16, while two-way fixed effects (TWFE) are increasingly being adopted to simultaneously address individual and time-dependent fixed effects and improve causal identification17–19. On the other hand, structural equation modeling (SEM) has shown advantages in understanding direct and indirect paths due to its ability to simultaneously model multiple mediators and latent variables20,21. Overall, existing methods each have their own focus: fixed effects and dynamic panel methods are suitable for identifying overall correlations and temporal evolution, while SEM is beneficial for deconstructing complex mechanisms. The complementarity between the two deserves further development.

Research on regional heterogeneity and stage differences

Several studies suggest that the AI-environment relationship exhibits significant regional and stage differences10,12. In the Chinese context, the eastern and central-western regions differ in their AI infrastructure, industrial structure, and energy structure, which may lead to different environmental performances of the same technology in different regions (the descriptions of regional differences and empirical results are not entirely consistent). These studies emphasize the necessity of considering spatial adaptability in policy design and technology promotion.

Recently, the academic community has also increasingly focused on the regional/stage-specific impact mechanisms of artificial intelligence on energy consumption and carbon emissions. From an international comparative perspective, Işık et al.22 analyzed the dynamic relationship between AI and energy transition within the OECD (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development) national framework, pointing out that AI may have heterogeneous impacts on energy efficiency, environmental policy, and social governance (ESG), providing cross-national evidence for understanding regional differences under different institutional backgrounds. Regarding the coupling mechanism between energy and innovation, Ested et al.23 confirmed the moderating role of knowledge production and financial development on the environmental effects of AI in BRICS countries, further supporting the view that machine intelligence may have different carbon emission impacts at different stages of economic/energy development. Furthermore, research at the enterprise level also shows that the emission reduction effect of AI is more significant in downstream industries and high-polluting sectors, reflecting the importance of internal factors such as regional industrial structure on heterogeneous impacts (Lu and Liao33). Finally, factors such as spatial spillovers and the interaction effects of environmental policies have also been emphasized by Qiu et al.24, supplementing the complexity of regional AI environmental effects. In summary, these studies echo the findings of this study, all indicating that the relationship between artificial intelligence and carbon emissions/energy transition is not a single homogeneous effect, but exhibits significant differences across different spaces, policies, and development stages.

Comments

Existing literature has provided several useful clues in terms of methodology and themes: first, there is relatively abundant empirical evidence regarding the overall directionality of AI and carbon emissions; second, several studies have begun to reveal mediating pathways and regional differences; and third, in terms of causal identification and multi-mediation modeling, TWFE and SEM each have their advantages and show a complementary trend. Meanwhile, several issues warrant further systematic discussion:

Theoretical background and research hypothesis

Direct impact of AI use level on CCE

The improvement of AI use level can help companies reduce CE intensity. AI technology improves corporate production efficiency and reduces unnecessary energy consumption through intelligent scheduling, precise control and automatic optimization. Intelligent manufacturing systems based on machine learning can optimize production processes, reduce the operating time of high-energy-consuming equipment, and reduce CEs per unit of product. AI’s deep learning and big data analysis ability help companies accurately monitor energy consumption data, adjust production parameters in real time, and avoid energy waste. In supply chain management, the use of AI can optimize logistics routes and reduce CEs during transportation. In terms of energy management, the widespread use of AI can help enterprises achieve more refined energy management and reduce CE intensity.

This paper focuses on the dynamic evolution of the relationship between artificial intelligence (AI) technology applications and corporate carbon emission intensity. It abandons the assumption of a unidirectional linear path and instead constructs a nonlinear analysis framework based on the lifecycle characteristics of technology diffusion. In the early stages of AI applications, the deployment of computing infrastructure, large-scale model training, and the networking of intelligent devices are often accompanied by a significant expansion in energy consumption, which may drive up corporate carbon emission intensity in the short term. As the technology matures beyond a critical threshold, process reshaping, precise scheduling, and energy collaborative management resulting from algorithm optimization can become dominant, thereby achieving substantial suppression of carbon emissions.

H1: In the early stages of artificial intelligence applications, the high energy consumption of computing power and the scale expansion effect of system deployment may exacerbate the carbon emission intensity of enterprises in the short term due to the improvement of application level.

The relationship between AI use level and CCEs is not a simple linear relationship. In the early stages of AI use, enterprises mainly used AI for automation upgrades and intelligent production, which can effectively improve EUE and reduce CEs25. As AI technology is used more deeply, its high computing requirements, large-scale data storage, and deployment of smart devices can lead to additional energy consumption. For example, high-performance computing clusters, cloud computing centers, and AI training models often consume a lot of electricity, which may even offset its advantages in energy conservation and emission reduction. When companies widely apply AI, they can expand their production scale or enter new high-energy consumption fields, thereby increasing overall CEs. When AI use level is low, CCE intensity decreases with the development of AI, but when AI use level reaches a certain level, CEs may rebound, showing an inverted U-shaped feature.

From a theoretical perspective, the application of artificial intelligence (AI) may be simultaneously affected by both the “efficiency effect” and the “rebound effect”, a logic traceable to the Jevons Paradox in environmental economics. This theory posits that technological advancements improving resource utilization efficiency can lead to decreased unit costs, potentially stimulating demand expansion and thus increasing overall resource consumption. AI, by optimizing production processes and energy allocation, initially demonstrates a significant efficiency improvement effect; however, as application scale expands, factors such as computing power demands, data center energy consumption, and production scale expansion may lead to an increase in total energy consumption. Furthermore, discussions based on the “Green Paradox” suggest that when technology or policy is expected to alter the cost structure of resource use, corporate behavior may undergo intertemporal or scale-based adjustments, thereby weakening or even partially offsetting the environmental improvement effects. Therefore, under the combined influence of efficiency improvement and demand expansion mechanisms, the relationship between the level of AI application and carbon emission intensity exhibits a phased variation characteristic, theoretically possessing the basis for a non-linear inverted U-shaped relationship.

The Jevons Paradox and the Green Paradox have clear technological mapping paths in the context of AI-driven emissions reduction. From the perspective of the Jevons Paradox, AI algorithms significantly reduce the marginal energy cost per unit of output by optimizing equipment operating parameters and supply chain coordination. However, this efficiency improvement triggers companies’ motivation to expand their scale, leading to the outward extension of production boundaries or the overload operation of data center computing clusters, ultimately resulting in an increase in total energy consumption and carbon emissions instead of a decrease. From the perspective of the Green Paradox, when companies anticipate tighter carbon quotas or stricter environmental regulations in the future, they tend to accelerate the full-load operation of existing high-carbon production capacity during the policy window to lock in short-term profits. AI’s high-frequency monitoring and predictive maintenance capabilities provide technical support for this intertemporal production scheduling, thereby amplifying emissions pressure in the short term. The superposition of these two effects results in a typical phased reversal in the relationship between AI applications and carbon emissions, that is, the initial efficiency dividend is offset by scale expansion and anticipated behavior. Only when the technology penetration rate exceeds a critical value and the coupling degree of green energy reaches a certain level can the net emissions reduction effect of AI be fully released.

H2: There is a significant inverted U-shaped nonlinear relationship between the level of artificial intelligence application and corporate carbon emission intensity. In other words, as the depth of application increases, carbon emission intensity experiences an inflection point evolution of first rising and then falling.

Impact mechanism of AI use level on CCE

The in-depth use of AI technology in the industrial field can optimize energy usage patterns. For example, through smart sensors and data analysis technology, companies can monitor energy consumption in real time and use AI algorithms for prediction and optimization to reduce energy waste and improve EUE. Green innovation driven by AI helps companies develop low-carbon technologies and clean energy solutions, such as AI-driven material research and development, optimization of smart energy-saving equipment, etc., which can reduce CEs in the production process. Enterprises use AI technology to improve environmental management and formulate energy conservation and emission reduction strategies more accurately, thereby forming sustainable CE reduction ability in the long term. AI use level can indirectly reduce CCE intensity by optimizing energy use and promoting green technology innovation26.

H3: AI use level can reduce CCE intensity by improving EUE and GIE.

The widespread use of AI technology can accelerate the SI process of enterprises, optimize product development processes through machine learning and big data analysis, promote the breakthrough and use of low-carbon technologies, and thus reduce CEs. At the same time, the development of AI has also promoted the intelligent transformation of traditional industries, enabling high-pollution, high-energy-consuming industries to gradually transform into low-carbon, digital industries. In the manufacturing industry, intelligent manufacturing systems and automated control technologies can reduce raw material consumption and CEs, and promote the development of the environmental protection industry27. AI use level can indirectly reduce CCEs by promoting SI and ISU.

The “Scientific Innovation (SI)” used in this article does not refer to innovation activities in a general, abstract sense, nor is it the same as a single “R&D intensity” indicator. Instead, it is used to characterize a region’s knowledge creation and technology generation capabilities, centered on basic and applied research. At the operational level, SI focuses on reflecting a region’s comprehensive innovation level in terms of R&D input and technological output. Its connotation is closer to “technological innovation capability” than simply an input-based indicator measuring the scale of R&D investment. Unlike Green Innovation Efficiency (GIE), which focuses on the environmental orientation of innovation activities, SI emphasizes the general improvement in technological innovation capabilities stimulated by the application of artificial intelligence. By promoting the generation of new technologies, processes, and knowledge, it indirectly impacts energy utilization and production processes.

H4: AI use level can reduce CCE intensity by improving SI and promoting ISU.

Heterogeneous impact of AI use level on CCE Effects

There are significant differences in economic development levels, industrial structures, energy structures, and SI ability across the country, and the impact of AI use level on CCEs may show obvious regional heterogeneity. The ER has a relatively superior geographical location, relatively complete policies and systems, and relatively mature AI technology development. Enterprises have a high degree of digitalization and stronger green technology innovation ability, and AI’s effect on reducing CEs may be more significant. Due to the relatively traditional industrial base and lagging technology use in the CR and WR, enterprises are relatively weak in AI-enabled green transformation, which can result in their CE reduction effect being less than that in the ER28. The energy structure of each region also affects the effect of AI on CEs. For example, in regions where energy consumption mainly relies on fossil fuels, the energy conservation and emission reduction effect of AI can be limited, while in regions where new energy accounts for a higher proportion, AI technology can better play a role in optimizing the energy structure. Local government environmental policies and incentives can also affect the role of AI in carbon reduction. The impact of AI use level on CCEs may vary from region to region.

As a core area for receiving industrial transfers from the eastern region and ensuring national energy security, the central region’s industrial structure remains characterized by a high proportion of heavy industry and a coal-dependent energy consumption structure. Furthermore, its digital infrastructure and green technology collaborative network are not yet fully formed. Against this backdrop, the introduction of artificial intelligence (AI) technology faces high adaptation costs and transformation frictions. Enterprises often prioritize the automation of traditional production capacity rather than green process innovation, leading to the dilution of technological dividends by high-carbon path dependence. Simultaneously, the pace of clean energy substitution in the central region is relatively slow, and the efficiency improvements driven by AI are easily offset by the scale expansion of fossil fuel consumption. This results in a delayed inflection point and weakened statistical significance in the inverted U-shaped energy curve in this region, exhibiting a phased blunting characteristic.

H5: The carbon emission reduction effect of artificial intelligence shows significant regional heterogeneity. The emission reduction effect is most prominent in the eastern region, followed by the western region. However, the central region is affected by the rigid constraints of industrial structure and the lagging energy transition. The statistical evidence of its inverted U-shaped relationship is relatively weak, and the inflection point is significantly shifted to the right.

Variable selection

The AI referred to in this paper is not a general abstract technical concept, but rather defined from the perspective of “technology application capability”, specifically measuring the degree of actual application and transformation of artificial intelligence technology in enterprises at the regional level. In variable construction, AI primarily uses the number of times AI-related patents are used in each province as the core measurement indicator. This indicator reflects the strength of the transformation of AI technology from R&D results to actual production applications, and compared to the number of patent applications or authorizations alone, it better reflects the technology’s implementation and application capability. In the empirical model, AIit represents the level of AI application in province i in period t. In the empirical regressions, AIit is logarithmically transformed to reduce the impact of dimensional differences and heteroscedasticity. Through the above operational definitions, this paper ensures that the AI variable has a clear economic meaning and a measurable basis.

To ensure the scientific rigor and reproducibility of core indicators, data on the level of artificial intelligence application is extracted from the Patentthub patent database. IPC classification numbers, combined with NLP semantic screening technology, are used to identify actual implementation and commercial authorization records of AI-related patents, eliminating patent applications that remain only at the theoretical conceptual stage to accurately reflect the effectiveness of technology transfer. To address the issue of missing annual data for some provinces, this study employs linear interpolation based on trends from adjacent years, and validates the reliability of this method through tail reduction and subsample regression in robustness checks. The denominator for the Science Innovation (SI) indicator is chosen to be the size of the resident population, aiming to eliminate the interference of absolute amounts of R&D investment and instead characterize the region’s per capita knowledge creation and technology diffusion capabilities, better aligning with the policy orientation of universalizing innovative achievements and promoting regional collaborative development. To mitigate the measurement bias of a single patent indicator, this study introduces the provincial industrial robot installation density published by the International Federation of Robotics (IFR) as a proxy variable in robustness checks, further validating the robustness of the core conclusions.

Explained variable

This study uses CE intensity as the explained variable, and its calculation methods include three methods: the ratio of CEs to GDP (Gross Domestic Product), the ratio of CEs to regional population, and night light data. The study uses the ratio of each province’s carbon dioxide emissions to GDP to represent CE intensity.

Explanatory variables

The evaluation methods of AI use level include the installation rate of industrial robots and the number of AI patent uses. This study selects the number of AI patent uses that comprehensively consider the CE effect of SI to measure AI use level.

Data source

The study uses panel data from 30 provinces in China from 2007 to 2021 as samples, excluding Tibet, Hong Kong, Macao and Taiwan. The CE data of enterprises in each province is derived from the annual reports of the corresponding enterprises in 30 provinces in China and the carbon trading data platform of the State Council Research Center. The data on AI patent uses comes from the Patenthub patent database, and other variables are derived from the National Statistical Yearbook and the statistical yearbooks of each province. In the experiment, the collected data is interpolated to complete the missing data and standardized.

The carbon emission data at the enterprise level mainly comes from the annual disclosure information of key emitting enterprises included in the national and local carbon emission trading pilot systems. These enterprises are mainly concentrated in high-energy-consuming industries such as power, steel, cement, petrochemicals, chemicals, non-ferrous metals, and building materials, covering approximately several thousand large-scale enterprises during the sample period. The relevant emission data is monitored, calculated, and verified by third parties in accordance with the National Greenhouse Gas Emission Accounting Guidelines, and is therefore verified emission data. This study first compiles the annual carbon dioxide emission data of each enterprise at the enterprise level, and then aggregates them according to the enterprise’s registered location to the corresponding province. Based on this, the data is summed to form the provincial annual carbon emission total index; subsequently, carbon emission intensity is calculated by combining the GDP data of each province. The data platform of the State Council Carbon Trading Research Center is a public database platform that mainly integrates the verified emission information of national carbon market pilots and key emitting units (relevant information can be found on the official information release platform and its annual report related to national carbon emission trading). For enterprise emission records missing in certain years, this study performs linear interpolation based on the trend of adjacent years and conducts robustness checks to ensure that the results are not affected by the data processing method.

Although this study uses provincial panel data as the empirical unit, the construction of the explained variable “corporate carbon emission intensity” strictly follows a bottom-up micro-aggregation logic. The original data comes from the annual third-party verified emission lists of key emitting enterprises included in the national and local carbon emission trading systems in each province, comprehensively covering the actual production and energy consumption records of thousands of enterprises in high-energy-consuming industries such as power, steel, building materials, and chemicals. The provincial-level aggregation aims to control for high-frequency fluctuations, statistical differences, and disclosure incompleteness in the micro-enterprise sample over long periods. Regional aggregation smooths individual noise to capture the average processing effect of artificial intelligence technology diffusion on the carbon emission intensity of regional enterprise groups. Although this macro-proximity indicator masks micro-heterogeneities in enterprise size, ownership structure, and industry segmentation to some extent, combined with macro-proximity variables such as industrial structure, energy consumption structure, foreign direct investment, and urbanization rate already controlled in the model, it can still effectively identify the regional impact trends and phased characteristics of artificial intelligence application levels on enterprise carbon emission intensity. Future research can rely on micro-databases of listed companies or industrial enterprises to further conduct enterprise-level heterogeneity decomposition and dynamic tracking.

While this study effectively identifies the macro-level emission reduction effects of AI applications at the regional level using provincial panel data, the difficulty in matching enterprise-level carbon emissions with patent application data limits the ability to verify the linkage between micro and macro data. Provincial aggregation inevitably masks the differentiated impacts of enterprise size, ownership structure, and industry heterogeneity on the emission reduction effectiveness of technology, and also makes it difficult to directly characterize the micro-level transmission pathways of AI within enterprises. Future research will further integrate micro-level data from listed companies and large-scale industrial enterprises, using an enterprise-province matching method to deeply analyze the micro-mechanisms by which AI affects carbon emissions.

To verify whether the nonlinear relationship between the level of artificial intelligence application and the CE effect truly exhibits an inverted U-shaped characteristic, this study employs the “U-shaped Relationship Test” proposed by Lind and Mehlum. This test determines whether the coefficient of the quadratic term in the regression model is significant, and, combined with the sign of the first derivative at both ends of the sample interval, examines whether the curve has an inflection point within the sample range, i.e., whether it presents a statistically significant inverted U-shaped or U-shaped relationship. An inverted U-shaped inflection point is determined when the first derivative is positive at the left end and negative at the right end of the interval, and the coefficient of the quadratic term is significant. The “U-Test” column in Tables 1 and 2 reports the statistic and significance level of this test, ensuring the robustness and verifiability of the inverted U-shaped relationship between the level of artificial intelligence application and the CE effect. From an economic perspective, the estimated inflection point of the inverted U-shaped curve is approximately 4.09 (logarithmic scale), corresponding to the marginal emission reduction effect turning from positive to negative after the number of artificial intelligence patent applications exceeds a critical scale. To the left of the inflection point, for every standard deviation increase in the level of artificial intelligence application, the average carbon emission intensity of enterprises increases by about 0.82%, mainly reflecting the energy spillover during the computing power infrastructure and system debugging period. After crossing the inflection point, the same level of technological advancement can reduce carbon emission intensity by 1.15%, indicating that the economies of scale of algorithm optimization and energy synergy management have been fully realized. Among the control variables, the energy consumption structure coefficient is significantly positive, verifying that every 10% point decrease in coal dependence can release about 0.63% of carbon intensity compression space, highlighting the necessity of synergy between clean energy and digitalization.

Robustness test

To ensure the reliability of the results, the experiment was carried out to test the soundness of the regression outcomes and the findings are illustrated in Table 2. In Table 2, column (a) uses PCSE (Panel-Corrected Standard Error) instead of the panel data analysis method and re-estimates. Column (b) uses CEs instead of CE intensity as the explained variable, and column (c) shrinks the explained variable by 5% up and down. In Table 2, it is further proved that AI use level has a significant inverted U-shaped relationship with CE effect.

To mitigate potential reverse causality and omitted variable bias, this study, building upon existing methods such as variable substitution, tailing, and panel-corrected standard errors, further introduced the instrumental variable method (2SLS) for endogeneity testing. The interaction term between the 1984 fixed-line telephone penetration rate and topographic relief in each province was selected as the instrumental variable for the level of artificial intelligence application. This indicator satisfies both the constraints of correlation (historical communication infrastructure determines the diffusion path of digital technology) and exogeneity (early geographical and communication conditions do not directly affect contemporary corporate carbon emissions). The first-stage regression showed a high correlation between the instrumental variable and the core explanatory variable (F-value > 10, excluding the problem of weak instrumental variables). The second-stage results were consistent with the baseline regression direction and maintained stable significance, further confirming the reliability of the causal inference between artificial intelligence application and carbon emission intensity.

To further verify the robustness of the inverted U-shaped relationship, this study retested it using a Hansen panel threshold model. Using the level of artificial intelligence application as the threshold variable, after 500 bootstrap samplings, the F-statistic was 28.37 (p < 0.01), supporting the existence of a single threshold. The threshold estimate was 4.12, with a 95% confidence interval of [3.87, 4.36], highly consistent with the inflection point of the TWFE model, indicating that the inverted U-shaped relationship is not a product of the model specification. For the central region, the study further introduced the interaction term between artificial intelligence and dummy variables for testing. The results showed that the coefficient of the interaction term was significantly positive (β = 0.018, p < 0.05), indicating that the inflection point of the inverted U-shaped curve in the central region shifted to the right by approximately 0.75 units compared to the national sample, empirically supporting the inflection point delay effect caused by rigid industrial structure and energy dependence.

To overcome the limitations of quadratic term fitting, such as sensitivity to inflection points and potential neglect of multiple discontinuities, this study introduces the Hansen panel threshold model for cross-validation. Using the level of artificial intelligence application as the threshold variable, and after 500 bootstrap sampling tests, the results show that the single threshold effect is significant at the 1% level, while the double threshold effect is insignificant. This indicates that only a single structural inflection point exists within the sample period, and the threshold value is highly consistent with the benchmark regression, effectively eliminating model specification bias. Regarding the insignificant inverted U-shaped relationship in the central region, the reason lies in the high proportion of heavy industry and strong reliance on coal in this region. Enterprises in this region tend to focus on automated capacity replacement rather than low-carbon process reconstruction when introducing artificial intelligence. Coupled with insufficient coordination between environmental regulations and green finance, the scale rebound effect caused by efficiency improvements partially offsets the direct emission reduction potential, shifting the theoretical inflection point to the right outside the sample period.

The region has long maintained a high proportion of traditional heavy industries such as steel, building materials, and chemicals. Enterprises’ digital transformation has largely focused on capacity expansion and automation substitution, rather than process carbon reduction and energy efficiency optimization, resulting in the absorption of AI technology dividends by a high-carbon lock-in effect. Simultaneously, the penetration rate of clean energy in the central power grid is relatively low, and the operation of AI computing centers is highly dependent on fossil fuel power. The reduction in unit energy consumption brought about by efficiency improvements is insufficient to offset the overall rebound caused by scale expansion. Furthermore, insufficient coordination by local governments in supporting digital infrastructure and green finance means that enterprises lack the incentives and constraints to guide AI towards emission reduction scenarios. Ultimately, this manifests as a break in the transmission chain between technology diffusion and carbon intensity reduction, with the inflection point delayed beyond the sample period.

To overcome the limitations of traditional geographical divisions and enhance policy targeting, this study further expands the heterogeneity analysis framework by introducing three key dimensions: carbon trading pilot status, resource endowment type, and digital economy development level. In the carbon trading pilot dimension, provinces included in the market-based emission reduction mechanism, relying on carbon quota constraints and carbon asset pricing signals, are compelled to prioritize the deployment of artificial intelligence technologies in scenarios such as real-time energy consumption monitoring, carbon footprint tracking, and process optimization, resulting in a significantly higher technology-based emission reduction conversion rate than non-pilot areas. Regarding the distinction between resource-based and non-resource-based provinces, resource-based provinces, affected by the lock-in effect of high-energy-consuming industries, are prone to triggering scale expansion and energy consumption rebound in the early stages of AI application, with the inverted U-shaped inflection point shifting significantly to the right. In contrast, non-resource-based provinces, with their lightweight industrial structure and service-oriented characteristics, are more likely to cross the technology adoption threshold and achieve net emission reductions. Furthermore, based on the provincial digital economy comprehensive development index, the results show that the AI emission reduction effect is stronger in areas with high digital infrastructure. This indicates that computing infrastructure, data element circulation networks, and new communication base stations constitute the necessary preconditions for AI to exert positive environmental externalities. The above multidimensional grouping test further confirms the conditional dependence and institutional sensitivity of the AI emission reduction effect, suggesting that policy design needs to go beyond single geographical boundaries and shift to precise policies based on market mechanisms, factor endowments, and the synergy of digital infrastructure.

Structural equation model.

It should be noted that “mediation effects reaching 0.022 and 0.021 respectively” represent the absolute values of the indirect effects of AI application levels on CE through GIE and EUE, calculated as path products: 0.022 = (AI → GIE coefficient) × (GIE → CE coefficient), 0.021 = (AI → EUE coefficient) × (EUE → CE coefficient). The 0.022 and 0.021 reported here only represent the magnitude (absolute value) of the mediating effects, therefore the values are positive and do not contradict the negative signs of the path coefficients. This method of expression helps to intuitively demonstrate the contribution of each mediating path while maintaining consistency in directional interpretation.

The proportion of each intermediate path’s effect has been clearly quantified through Bootstrap sampling calculations: the direct effect of artificial intelligence on carbon emissions accounts for 18.2%, while the indirect effects through energy efficiency, green innovation efficiency, and industrial structure upgrading and scientific innovation account for 12.6%, 10.5%, 9.3%, and 8.7%, respectively. The cumulative contribution rate of the four mechanisms reaches 41.1%, with the remaining portion explained by control variables and unobserved factors.

Discussions

Based on panel data from 30 provinces in China (2007–2021), this study, within the joint framework of TWFE and SEM, yields the following main findings: a significant inverted U-shaped relationship exists between the level of artificial intelligence (AI) application and corporate carbon emission intensity; the emission reduction effect of AI includes both direct effects and multiple mediating pathways such as green innovation efficiency (GIE), energy utilization efficiency (EUE), scientific/technological innovation capability (SI), and industrial structure upgrading (ISU); and significant regional heterogeneity exists, with the emission reduction effect being most significant in the eastern region, while statistical evidence for the inverted U-shape is relatively weak in the central region. The following sections discuss in depth (1) the mechanism and theoretical explanation of the results; (2) comparison with existing research; (3) the implications of the inflection point in policy practice; and (4) research limitations and future directions.

Theoretical and industrial explanations of the results

The formation mechanism of the inverted U-shaped relationship: This study observes that the level of AI application may be accompanied by an increase in carbon emissions in the low-to-medium stage, but after exceeding a certain critical point, it generally shifts towards suppressing carbon emissions. On the one hand, the efficiency effect brought by AI—reducing energy consumption per unit of output through intelligent scheduling, process optimization, and improvements in supply chain and operations—provides a mechanism for long-term emission reduction (see Chap. 2, Assumptions and SEM Path). On the other hand, the initial promotion of AI is accompanied by a large amount of high-energy-consuming investment (servers, training computing power, data centers, deployment of intelligent devices) and possible scale/rebound effects: the decrease in unit cost may stimulate output expansion or entry into new high-energy-consuming businesses (such as large-scale data services and cloud computing business expansion), thereby increasing total energy consumption (Jevons paradox). In addition, policy expectations and market behavior may undergo intertemporal adjustments (Green paradox): if enterprises expect stricter carbon constraints in the future, they expand output or accelerate resource utilization in advance, thereby increasing emissions in the short term. The coexistence of these two mechanisms (efficiency improvement vs. scale/rebound and expectation effects) can naturally explain the inverted U-shaped phased performance. The results are consistent with this theoretical framework: initially, the first-order coefficient is positive (AI is positively correlated with CE), but the quadratic term is significantly negative, indicating that as the application of AI deepens, the marginal effect shifts towards emission reduction.

Economic Explanation of the Mediation Path: SEM shows that GIE, EUE, SI, and ISU are all significant mediators:

GIE (Green Innovation Efficiency) reflects the ability of a unit of R&D input to generate green technology output. AI accelerates the implementation of low-carbon technologies through assisted material discovery, process optimization, and energy-saving control, thereby indirectly reducing carbon emissions; this is consistent with the mediation conclusions of Liu et al.,14 and Dong et al.,13.

Dialogue with existing literature: points of consistency and differences

Conformity with Chen and Jin11, Wang et al.,10, etc. Chen and Jin,11 reported the positive emission reduction effect of AI in a manufacturing sample. The results, obtained after AI application levels reach a high level or exceed the inflection point, are consistent with this direction: when AI capabilities reach a certain maturity, they can significantly promote a decrease in carbon intensity. Wang et al.,10 emphasized the function of AI in promoting energy transition through dynamic panel threshold analysis. The findings also support the coupling mechanism between AI and energy transition (especially EUE improvement). Zhong et al.,12’s conclusion regarding cross-national differences (the effect varies with development stage and income level) corroborated the regional heterogeneity found in our study: regions with stronger technological foundations are more likely to realize the emission reduction potential of AI.

The discrepancies in existing research conclusions regarding the emission reduction effects of artificial intelligence (AI) essentially stem from a mismatch between sample periods, technology measurements, and development stages. Some literature, based on early cross-sectional data or single-industry samples, often captures the scale expansion effect in the early stages of AI application, thus concluding that it linearly promotes or weakly inhibits emissions. This study, however, using provincial long-term panel data and an inverted U-shaped test, reveals the net emission reduction turning point after the technology penetration rate crosses a critical threshold, overcoming the limitations of the static linear framework. Consistent with existing research emphasizing the synergistic effects of energy transition, this paper further quantifies the indirect contribution of AI through energy efficiency optimization and green innovation, and echoes cross-national heterogeneous research, confirming the boundary constraints of institutional environment, energy endowment, and industrial base on AI’s environmental performance. This provides more refined Chinese evidence for understanding the complex coupling between the digital economy and low-carbon transition.

Differences and Possible Reasons: Some literature directly reports linear or overall negative effects without examining nonlinear (inverted U) or stage-specific effects. This may be related to differences in sample scope (industry/country/province), AI measurement (enterprise level vs. patent/robot installation rate), and consideration of the high-energy-consumption investment period. This study, by introducing an AI² term and employing the Lind–Mehlum U test, reveals stage-specific characteristics: studies without significant nonlinearity may be near the sample mean or cover a mixture of effects from different development stages, thus masking nonlinearity. Furthermore, the failure of the U test in the central region suggests that factors such as regional energy structure (high coal share), policy support, and industrial base may alter the environmental return path of AI, explaining why the same AI promotion produces different net effects in different regions.

Practical and policy implications of the inflection point

Within the inverted U-shaped framework, the inflection point represents the “critical intensity” of AI application levels: below this intensity, the net effect of AI may not be an immediate emission reduction (requiring matching with green energy or efficiency constraints); above this intensity, the marginal contribution of AI transforms into net emission reduction. The policy implications include:

Phased policy design: For regions that have not yet reached the inflection point, the focus should be on reducing the “high energy consumption side effects” of AI promotion—for example, energy efficiency standards for data centers, encouraging data centers to use renewable electricity, and implementing temporary energy consumption regulations or assessments for high-energy-consuming AI projects. For regions that have exceeded the inflection point, the emission reduction benefits of AI can be amplified through subsidies, green procurement, and other means.

Limitations and future research directions

Despite the progress made in mechanism identification and regional heterogeneity assessment, three limitations remain that require further breakthroughs. First, provincial-level aggregated data masks the heterogeneity of technology adoption and industry-specific differences at the micro-level of enterprises. Future research should rely on micro-level enterprise panel data or IoT energy consumption monitoring data to improve measurement accuracy. Second, while historical instrumental variables are introduced to mitigate endogeneity, quasi-natural experimental designs for AI policy impacts (such as the “East-to-West Data” node layout and green computing power subsidy pilot programs) could further strengthen causal identification. Third, this study does not fully consider the spatial correlation effects of cross-provincial AI technology spillover and supply chain-based collaborative emission reduction. Future research could introduce a spatial Durbin model (SDM) to characterize the network externalities of technology diffusion and regional carbon leakage risks, thereby constructing a more comprehensive policy assessment framework.

Summary

This study provides systematic evidence at the provincial level in China: the level of AI application has an inverted U-shaped effect on corporate carbon emissions, and it plays a role through multiple technological and structural pathways (GIE, EUE, SI, ISU); the net emission reduction effect is most significant in the eastern region. Theoretically, this finding can be explained by the combined effect of efficiency and rebound/expectation effects (Jevons and Green paradox perspective); practically, it points out that promoting AI to achieve green transformation requires a three-pronged approach: “technology advancement + energy structure + differentiated policies”. The findings provide actionable empirical evidence for developing phased and regionally coordinated AI and low-carbon policies, while also suggesting that future research should enhance data granularity and causal identification to further clarify the optimal allocation of policy levers.

Conclusion and countermeasures

Conclusion

Based on provincial panel data from China, this study confirms a significant inverted U-shaped nonlinear relationship between the level of artificial intelligence (AI) application and corporate carbon emission intensity. The underlying mechanism involves the synergistic transmission through four pathways: improved energy efficiency, activated green innovation, enhanced scientific innovation capabilities, and industrial structure upgrading. Regional heterogeneity testing indicates that the eastern region, leveraging its synergistic advantages in digital infrastructure and clean energy, has already crossed the emission reduction inflection point, while the central and western regions, constrained by industrial lock-in and energy structure, are experiencing a phased lag in the release of technological dividends. Theoretically, this study introduces the Jevons paradox and the green paradox into the analysis of AI environmental effects, revealing the dynamic game mechanism between efficiency improvement and scale expansion, and expanding the theoretical boundaries of the intersection of the digital economy and low-carbon transformation. In practice, the research results provide quantitative evidence for the synergistic governance of “technology-energy-institutions,” emphasizing that policy design must consider differences in development stages and the matching of stakeholders’ capabilities to achieve a sustainable low-carbon transformation driven by AI.

Countermeasures and suggestions

In response to the phased characteristics of the inverted U-shaped curve, the central government should accelerate the formulation of energy efficiency standards for computing infrastructure and a mandatory green electricity quota system. For regions that have not yet crossed the inflection point, dynamic coordination of carbon pricing and energy consumption control should be implemented to prevent technological rebound effects. Local governments need to implement differentiated layouts based on regional endowments. Eastern regions can establish special funds for AI green transformation based on digital industry clusters to promote smart microgrids and carbon footprint tracking systems. Central regions should prioritize pilot projects that couple “intelligent transformation and digital conversion” with clean energy substitution in traditional high-energy-consuming industries, avoiding low-level automation expansion. Western regions can leverage their renewable energy advantages to build zero-carbon computing hubs, attracting AI companies to consume green electricity locally. At the enterprise level, a dual-track assessment mechanism of “AI-energy efficiency” should be established, incorporating algorithm optimization into the ESG disclosure system, prioritizing the deployment of predictive maintenance and process carbon reduction scenarios, and achieving a smooth transition from energy consumption expansion to net emission reduction through phased technological iterations.
