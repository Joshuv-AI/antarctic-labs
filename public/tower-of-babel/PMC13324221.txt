Intracardiac Thrombi in Behçet’s Disease: Systematic Review of 218 Patients’ Clinical Characteristics and Treatments
Authors: Anas M Barabrah, Anas R Tuqan, Sara M Darawish, Ameer Awashra, Mohammad H Shehadeh, Basel A Zaben, Mohammed Ayyad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Behçet’s disease (BD) is a rare autoimmune disorder with significant cardiovascular complications, including intracardiac thrombosis (ICT), which can lead to high morbidity and mortality.Methods:We conducted a comprehensive literature search across multiple databases up to December 2024, identifying articles related to BD and ICT. Data were extracted from case reports and cohort studies, focusing on demographics, clinical presentations and treatment outcomes.Results:A total of 218 cases of ICT in BD were analysed, predominantly affecting young males from regions along the historical Silk Road. ICT often presented as the initial manifestation of BD (53.21% of cases), with fever, dyspnoea and haemoptysis as common symptoms. The right heart chambers were most frequently involved. Treatment primarily involved corticosteroids, immunosuppressants and anticoagulants, with a complete resolution rate of 67.22% (n=160). Surgical interventions were associated with high recurrence and mortality rates of 60% and 34.78%, respectively.Conclusion:ICT in BD is a serious complication that requires prompt recognition and management. A multimodal treatment approach combining corticosteroids, immunosuppressants and anticoagulants is recommended to improve outcomes. Surgical intervention carries a higher risk of mortality and morbidity, making it a less preferred treatment option. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for ICT in BD patients and initiate timely medical therapy to mitigate complications. Further studies are needed to optimise treatment strategies and explore long-term outcomes.

Behçet’s disease (BD) is an autoimmune, multisystemic inflammatory disorder characterised by recurrent relapsing–remitting episodes of inflammation, affecting various organ systems. First described in 1937 by the Turkish dermatologist Hulusi Behçet, this vasculitic syndrome primarily affects blood vessels of all sizes on both the arterial and venous sides of the circulation and presents with a wide range of clinical manifestations.1–3

From an epidemiological perspective, BD exhibits a distinct geographical distribution, with the highest prevalence observed in the population along the historical Silk Road. Turkey reports the highest incidence of BD, with 420 cases per 100,000 population, whereas regions such as Europe and North America report significantly lower rates, ranging from 0.12 to 5.2 per 100,000 population.4 This geographical heterogeneity is thought to arise from complex interactions between genetic factors, particularly the HLA-B51 allele, which increases susceptibility, and environmental influences.5

Cardiac involvement in BD, although rare, encompasses a spectrum of manifestations, including pericarditis, myocarditis, valvular lesions, coronary artery disease and intracardiac thrombosis (ICT).3 Of these, ICT is of particular note due to its association with poor prognosis and high mortality if untreated.

Based on recent studies, cardiac involvement occurs in 1–6% of BD patients and, as far as our search could determine from the global literature up to 2024, 218 cases of ICT have been reported.6 This contrasts with more frequent vascular complications, such as venous thrombosis (up to 40% of BD patients) and arterial involvement (1–7% of BD patients).7,8 The lower prevalence of ICT may be attributed to underdiagnosis or asymptomatic presentations, although its clinical significance warrants careful evaluation in high-risk patients.

The pathophysiology of thrombus formation in BD remains incompletely understood, but endothelial dysfunction is recognised as a primary factor. Immune dysregulation and chronic vascular inflammation lead to endothelial cell activation, disrupting the balance between procoagulant and anticoagulant mechanisms. This autoinflammatory state leads to vascular insult manifesting as endothelial damage, whereas inflammation itself promotes stasis and hypercoagulability, all of which together create a prothrombotic state consistent with Virchow’s triad.9,10

Despite advances in understanding BD, significant gaps persist in the literature regarding the epidemiology, optimal treatment strategies, long-term outcomes and predictive biomarkers for ICT. Current evidence is largely derived from case reports and small case series, with only two systematic reviews available to date.11,12 This limited and fragmented evidence base highlights the need for more robust, high-quality studies to inform clinical decision-making and guide the development of standardised diagnostic and therapeutic protocols.

To address these critical gaps, this systematic review aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of ICT in BD. Specifically, it seeks to evaluate the prevalence, assess the clinical outcomes and examine the efficacy of current therapeutic interventions. By exploring the complex interplay between BD and ICT, this study aims to systematically compile data from the literature to showcase diagnostic and prognostic factors used to assess ICT in BD, as well as to unravel treatment strategies to improve patient outcomes.

Methods

Protocol Registration

We adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses and followed the recommendations of the PRISMA statement.13 In addition, we followed established guidance for conducting and reporting systematic reviews, with particular attention to methodological rigour, transparency and comprehensive reporting. This study was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the identifying number CRD42024623467.

Literature Search Strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was designed to identify relevant studies published from database inception until December 2024. The CINAHL, PubMed, Google Scholar and Scopus databases were systematically searched. Search terms were carefully chosen to ensure retrieval of pertinent articles. Various combinations of keywords, including ‘Behçet’s disease’, ‘intracardiac thrombus’, ‘thrombosis’, ‘cardiac thrombus’, ‘cardiac thrombi’, ‘cardiac involvement’, ‘cardiac lesion’ and ‘vascular complications’, were used. Both free-text terms and controlled vocabulary (e.g. MeSH terms in PubMed) were used. Boolean operators ‘AND’ and ‘OR’ were used to refine and expand the search, ensuring the inclusion of all relevant articles.

Eligibility Criteria

We included articles published in English that investigated the relationship between BD and ICT or described clinical features and management approaches for this condition. Exclusion criteria were studies not related to BD or ICT, as well as editorials, commentaries, studies with insufficient data or conference abstracts without full data.

Diagnostic Criteria

The diagnosis of BD in the included cases was confirmed using the validated International Study Group (ISG) diagnostic criteria.14 According to the ISG criteria, a diagnosis requires the presence of recurrent oral ulcerations along with at least two of the following four additional criteria: recurrent genital ulceration, ocular manifestations, cutaneous lesions or a positive pathergy test observed 24–48 hours after skin puncture. In addition to these primary criteria, other less frequently observed manifestations may also raise suspicion and aid in the diagnosis of individual cases. These include gastrointestinal symptoms, vascular manifestations and neurological involvement.

Most of the patients in this study were diagnosed retrospectively. The diagnosis of BD poses significant challenges because it primarily depends on clinical manifestations and typically follows a pattern of attacks and remissions. Consequently, patients may not exhibit sufficient symptoms at various stages of the disease, or they may present with less frequently observed manifestations, aside from the mandatory criterion of oral ulcers.15 This variability contrasts with the criteria established by the ISG for BD diagnosis. As a result, only 56% (n=122) of the patients fulfilled the validated ISG diagnostic criteria, with 44% (n=96) not meeting these criteria but displaying other less common manifestations that, according to existing literature, support a diagnosis of BD.

Study Selection

Six investigators (AT, AB, BZ, SD, AA, MH) independently conducted the literature search using the specified terms. Findings were cross-referenced to address technical discrepancies. Articles identified were imported into Rayyan, where duplicates were detected and removed systematically.16 Screening was conducted in two phases: evaluation of titles and abstracts, followed by full-text article review. Screening processes were performed independently by four researchers (BZ, SD, AA, MH), with conflicts resolved through consensus after review by two additional investigators (AB and AT).

Data Extraction

Data were extracted by six researchers (AT, AB, BZ, SD, AA, MH) using a standardised data extraction form in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Extracted information included study, participant and clinical characteristics, management strategies and outcomes. The data were double-checked by a senior author (MA) to ensure accuracy, with any discrepancies resolved by consensus discussion. Descriptive analysis was conducted on the extracted data.

Strategy for Data Synthesis

This review primarily used a narrative synthesis approach. Due to the predominance of case reports and small case series, as well as heterogeneity in clinical presentation, management strategies and outcome reporting, a quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible. Instead, the data were synthesised to examine the relationship between BD and ICT, as well as effective management strategies. Where possible, descriptive analysis was used to summarise findings.

Assessment of Risk of Bias

The risk of bias in case reports, case series and cohort studies was independently assessed by six reviewers (AT, AB, BZ, SD, AA, MH) using the Joanna Briggs Institute’s critical appraisal tools.17,18 In cases of disagreement, a consensus was reached with the involvement of the senior author (MA).

Ethical Considerations

This study is a systematic review of previously published literature and does not involve any new data collection from human subjects. All data analysed were derived from case reports and cohort studies available in the public domain, containing deidentified patient information. As such, institutional review board or ethics committee approval was not required. This complies with guidelines for systematic reviews using secondary data sources and presents no risk to human subjects. It was not appropriate or possible to involve patients or the public in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Our search identified 1,200 articles, of which only 157 advanced to the data extraction phase based on the eligibility criteria, as illustrated in the PRISMA flowchart (Figure 1). Interestingly, all extracted articles consisted exclusively of 145 case reports, 10 case series and two retrospective cohort studies. Data were extracted from a total of 218 BD patients diagnosed with ICT. Supplementary Table 1 provides a summary of all 218 patients included in our analysis.

Demographics and Characteristics of Patients With BD and ICT

The systematic review included a comprehensive analysis of 218 BD patients with ICT from diverse geographical regions, with a notable concentration of participants from the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. Specifically, the majority of the sample came from Turkey (n=80), Morocco (n=24), Tunisia (n=17) and Israel (n=6). Additional patients came from East Asian countries, such as China (n=22), Japan (n=7) and South Korea (n=4). Demographic characteristics revealed a pronounced sex imbalance in the study, with male participants significantly outnumbering females: of the total sample, 84.9% were male (n=185) and only 15.1% were female (n=33). The mean (± SD) age of participants was 28.53 ± 10.49 years, with 33 patients falling within the paediatric age group (≤18 years), indicating a relatively young and diverse sample population.

Clinical Presentation and Disease Course

ICT was the presenting feature leading to the diagnosis of BD in 53.2% (n=116) of patients. The time from the onset of BD symptoms to the development of ICT ranged from 1 month to 30 years. The most commonly reported clinical feature was fever in 61.93% (n=135) of patients (Figure 2). Pulmonary thromboembolism (PE) was the most frequent complication, present in 61.93% (n=135) of patients, followed by pulmonary artery aneurysm (PAA) in 26.15% (n=57) of patients. The presence of the HLA-B51 genetic marker, associated with BD, was identified in 18.35% (n=40) of patients. Inflammatory markers were elevated, with a mean (± SD) C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 16.79 ± 22.25 mg/dl and a mean erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 66.98 ± 34.78 mm/h, indicating a significant systemic inflammatory response.

Treatment Patterns Before Identification of ICT

Among the 70 patients who had a previous diagnosis of BD and before to the identification of ICT, the most common treatments administered included corticosteroids (68.57%; n=48), colchicine (38.57%; n=27) and immunosuppressive agents such as cyclophosphamide (22.86%; n=16) and azathioprine (15.71%; n=11). Anticoagulant therapies, including heparin (7.14%; n=5) and vitamin K antagonists (8.57%; n=6), were also used, as were interventions with biologic agents, particularly anti-tumour necrosis factor agents such as infliximab (8.57%; n=6).

Location of Intracardiac Thrombosis

The right ventricle was the most common site for ICT, occurring in 71.10% of first-time events (n=155/218) and in 70% of recurrent events (n=14/20). The right atrium was the second most frequent site, involved in 33.49% of first-time events (n=73/218) and in 45% (n=9/20) of recurrent events. Left-sided heart involvement was rare for both first-time events and recurrent events (Figure 3).

Valvular heart disease was observed in 42 patients, with the tricuspid valve being the most commonly affected (73.81%; n=31). Cardiogenic shock was reported in 10.55% (n=23) of the study population (Figure 3).

Diagnostic Modalities for Detecting Intracardiac Thrombosis

Transthoracic echocardiography was the primary diagnostic tool, used in 90.83% (n=198) of patients. Other diagnostic modalities included CT scans (33.49%; n=73), MRI/cardiac MRI (23.39%; n=51) and transoesophageal echocardiography (14.22%; n=31). Thrombosis was confirmed through surgery or autopsy in 7.80% (n=17) of patients and catheterisation in 2.75% (n=6) of patients.

Presumed Diagnosis Based on Imaging Features

ICT was the most common diagnosis based on imaging features, accounting for 50.92% (n=111) of intracardiac masses in BD patients (Figure 4). Other suspected diagnoses included cardiac tumours (14.22%; n=31), PE (6.88%; n=15) and infective endocarditis (3.67%; n=8). Unspecified masses and other conditions were diagnosed in 24.31% of patients (n=53).

Management Strategies and Outcomes

Among the 238 management plans, which included patients who were newly diagnosed as well as those experiencing their first or second recurrence, corticosteroids were the most frequently used treatment, administered in 76.47% of cases (n=182; Figure 5).

Overall, across the 238 management plans, treatment outcomes were favourable, with complete resolution of thrombosis achieved in 67.22% of cases (n=160) and partial resolution (incomplete disappearance of ICT) in 18.06% of cases (n=43) over a mean (± SD) duration of 8.78 ± 11.72 months. However, certain challenges persisted, including persistent thrombosis in 5.04% of cases (n=12), a mortality rate of 9.66% (n=23) and recurrence in 8.40% of cases (n=20) after a mean (± SD) duration of 11.72 ± 22.78 months (Figure 4).

Upon further analysis of the various drug combinations used in treatment, the most widely used regimen for achieving complete resolution of thrombosis was a combination of corticosteroids, immunosuppressive agents and anticoagulants, resulting in success in 33.13% (n=53) of the 160 cases with complete resolution. When colchicine was added to this regimen, complete resolution was recorded in 15.63% (n=25) of 160 cases. The mean (± SD) time from the initiation of treatment to complete resolution was 8.90 ± 10.94 months (Figure 4).

For the 43 cases with incomplete resolution, the most common treatment regimen was a combination of corticosteroids, immunosuppressive agents and anticoagulants (32.56%; n=14). The mean (± SD) duration from treatment initiation to incomplete resolution was 8.42 ± 13.85 months (Figure 4).

Among the 23 patients who died, the highest mortality rate was seen for those who had undergone surgical intervention (34.78%; n=8). In addition, among the 20 patients with recurrence, 60% (n=12) had undergone surgery to remove thrombi (Figure 4). Finally, among the 12 patients who had persistent ICT, 25% (n=3) received corticosteroids, anticoagulants, immunosuppressive agents and colchicine (Figure 4).

Outcomes Stratified by International Study Group Diagnostic Criteria

When analyses were restricted to patients fulfilling ISG diagnostic criteria, treatment patterns and clinical outcomes remained broadly consistent with those observed in the overall cohort.

Among those who achieved complete resolution of ICT (n=81), the most frequently used regimen was a combination of corticosteroids, immunosuppressive agents and anticoagulants (33.33%; n=27), followed by the same regimen with the addition of colchicine (14.8%; n=12). Surgical intervention was performed in 22.2% of patients (n=18). Smaller proportions of patients were managed with corticosteroids and anticoagulants alone (6%; n=5) or corticosteroids and immunosuppressive agents (6%; n=5), whereas the remaining 17.67% (n=14) received less commonly used regimens, most of which included corticosteroids. The mean (± SD) time to complete resolution was 7.66 ± 15.97 months.

Among patients with incomplete resolution (n=19), 36.8% (n=7) were treated with corticosteroids, immunosuppressive agents and anticoagulants, whereas 21.05% (n=4) received the same regimen with the addition of colchicine. The mean (± SD) duration from treatment initiation to incomplete resolution was 7.24 ± 15.62 months.

Among the 14 patients who died, surgical intervention was the most frequently reported management strategy (42.85%; n=6), followed by corticosteroids and immunosuppressive agents (21.4%; n=3).

Quality Assessment of Included Articles

Study quality varied across the included articles. Most studies clearly reported patient demographics, clinical histories, diagnostic methods, treatment interventions and post-treatment outcomes, allowing comprehensive data extraction and characterisation of ICT in BD. However, because the majority of included articles were retrospective case reports or small case series, the overall level of evidence is limited, and the findings should be interpreted with caution. For more details on the quality assessment of the included articles, refer to Supplementary Tables 2–4.

Discussion

Prevalence, Demographics and Patient Characteristics

ICT represents one of the rare but severe complications of BD, with an estimated prevalence of 1–7%.3,19 ICT predominantly affects young males and, despite its rarity, it carries significant morbidity and mortality risks.12

The present analysis of 218 cases of ICT in patients with BD provided a detailed understanding of its epidemiology. A distinct geographic and demographic pattern emerged, with most cases originating from the MENA region, particularly Turkey, Morocco and Tunisia, reflecting the high prevalence of BD in these areas. This is likely influenced by significant genetic factors, such as the presence of HLA-B51 among these populations.20,21

Furthermore, a pronounced sex imbalance was evident, with 82.96% (n=185) of the 218 patients being male, with a male:female ratio of 5.60:1. This indicates that male BD patients are approximately sixfold more likely to develop ICT than female BD patients. This finding aligns with a retrospective study conducted by Fei et al. involving 102 BD patients with vasculopathy, in which the male-to-female ratio was 3.86:1, indicating higher vascular involvement in males.22 This disparity may be attributed to significantly elevated genetic risks for BD in males, linked to distinct sex-specific factors that contribute to the increased prevalence of ICT among them. In contrast, females exhibit a distinct risk profile that offers protective effects.23

Interestingly, Fei et al. reported a mean (± SD) age of 29.47 ± 11.33 years.22 This closely aligns with the mean age in the present study of 28.53 years, suggesting that ICT primarily affects young adults in this population.

Elevated inflammatory markers were observed, with a mean (± SD) CRP level of 16.79 ± 22.25 mg/dl and an ESR of 66.98 ± 34.78 mm/h, indicating a significant systemic inflammatory response. These elevations are commonly seen in BD patients experiencing acute exacerbations or complications, such as ICT. Notably, a study on general BD patients with active vascular signs reported lower mean ESR and CRP values (60.4 ± 20.2 mm/h and 0.26 ± 0.11 mg/dl, respectively), suggesting that ICT may be associated with a more severe inflammatory response than other vascular manifestations of BD.24 In addition, that study article identified an optimal ESR cut-off of ≥42.5 mm/h for predicting active vascular manifestations.24

The mean ESR in the present study of 66.98 mm/h (range 10–140 mm/h) significantly exceeds this threshold, further highlighting the strong inflammatory component in BD patients with ICT. The markedly higher inflammatory markers in our patient group indicate that ICT in BD represents a severe manifestation linked to a particularly robust inflammatory drive. However, the presence of a minimum ESR of 10 mm/h (within the normal range) indicates that ICT can occur even in the absence of pronounced systemic inflammation, suggesting that non-inflammatory thrombogenic mechanisms (e.g. endothelial dysfunction, hypercoagulability) may contribute to thrombus formation in certain patients. This variability highlights the limitations of the ESR as a standalone diagnostic or prognostic marker for ICT, emphasising the need for imaging for thrombus confirmation and monitoring.

The HLA-B51 allele demonstrates a higher prevalence in BD populations, with endemic regions such as Turkey reporting frequencies between 54% and 82%.25 In contrast, the observed HLA-B51 positivity rate in our cohort with ICT was markedly lower (18.35%; n=40), suggesting a potential divergence in genetic associations between ICT and other systemic manifestations of BD. However, the specific role of the HLA-B51 allele in the development of ICT remains unclear.26 Notably, Fei et al. found that 22 of 102 patients with vascular BD tested positive for HLA-B5, with 21 of these patients being male.22 This finding highlights the increased prevalence of BD and, consequently, ICT, among males.

Clinical Characteristics and Associated Complications

The clinical presentation and disease course of ICT in BD are complex, significantly impacting patient outcomes. As a rare but serious complication, ICT is often difficult to diagnose due to its non-specific symptoms, which can result in delayed identification and treatment. The most frequently observed clinical manifestations associated with ICT (in descending order) were fever, dyspnoea, haemoptysis and cough. Consequently, diagnosis is made more difficult by the fact that these constitutional symptoms are generic and can resemble other illnesses, such as infectious endocarditis or malignancies. Prior to ICT diagnosis, corticosteroids (68.57%; n=48/70) and colchicine (38.57%; n=27/70) dominated therapeutic regimens, consistent with first-line BD management for mucocutaneous and articular symptoms.27 The limited pre-emptive use of immunosuppressants, such as cyclophosphamide (22.86%; n=16/70) and azathioprine (15.71%; n=11/70), suggests inadequate immunosuppression in non-severe cases, despite evidence that early immunosuppression reduces vascular complications.28 The delayed diagnosis of ICT, presenting as the initial BD manifestation in 53.21% (n=116/218) of patients, may further explain low prior immunosuppression rates, because vascular involvement often precedes classic BD symptoms.29

ICT Characteristics, Consequences and Diagnostic Techniques

Intracardiac thrombi in BD are frequently associated with major vascular complications, including thrombosis of the vena cava, PE and PAA.3 In our review of 218 patients, PE was the most common complication (61.93%; n=135, followed by PAA (26.15%; n=57). This highlights the fact that these ICT could potentially embolise, leading to PE. In addition, the systemic nature of BD-related vasculitis and the hypercoagulable state it induces necessitate prompt recognition and management to mitigate morbidity and mortality.30,31

Our study revealed that the most common site for ICT is the right heart, with the right ventricle being the predominant location (70.10%; n=155), followed by the right atrium (33.49%; n=73). This observation can be attributed to the reduced velocity of blood flow in the right heart, which facilitates thrombus formation, and the increased prevalence of venous involvement commonly seen in patients with BD.

Interestingly, in our study, cardiogenic shock was reported in 10.55% (n=23) of 218 patients. One major contributor to cardiogenic shock is valvular dysfunction, which arises from thrombus involvement in the valvular structures, leading to regurgitation and flow abnormalities in some cases. In addition, thrombi within the heart chambers can obstruct blood flow, leading to increased intracardiac pressures and impaired ventricular function.32,33 Furthermore, BD and chronic inflammation exacerbate the condition by causing recurrent myocardial injury, fibrotic remodelling and reduced contractility.34

The presence of ICT, along with constitutional symptoms, raises several differential diagnoses, including cardiac tumours, infective endocarditis and non-bacterial vegetations, making the diagnostic process more challenging.35 To address this, transthoracic echocardiography serves as the first-line imaging modality, favoured for its wide availability, safety and cost-effectiveness, and was used in 90.83% (n=198) of 218 patients in this study. When a more detailed evaluation is required, transoesophageal echocardiography helps refine the assessment by providing clearer visualisation of the mass’s size, attachment and its relationship with surrounding structures. In the case of complex or ambiguous findings, cardiac MRI provides a non-invasive evaluation with detailed insight into the mass’s composition, dimensions, vascularisation and infiltration of adjacent structures. It also aids in presurgical planning and accurately differentiates cardiac thrombi from tumours, as well as benign from malignant neoplasms.36

Comprehensive Management Strategies and Outcomes

The management of ICT in BD reflects the dual challenge of addressing vasculitis-driven inflammation and thrombotic complications. In this review, corticosteroids formed the therapeutic cornerstone (76.47%; n=182), aligning with guidelines prioritising immunosuppression to attenuate endothelial injury, the primary driver of thrombosis in BD.27 Cyclophosphamide, a potent immunosuppressant, was frequently used (45.80%; n=109/238), consistent with its established role in severe vascular BD.37

Given the limited data on optimal therapies for ICT in patients with BD, we aim to highlight the benefits of non-invasive or medical therapies over invasive treatments in achieving favourable outcomes. Specifically, a regimen that combines corticosteroids, immunosuppressants and anticoagulants showed the highest complete resolution rate (33.13%; n=53/160), emphasising the synergy between anti-inflammatory and antithrombotic strategies. Importantly, adding colchicine to this combination achieved a complete resolution rate of 15.63% (n=25/160), likely through the inhibition of neutrophil extracellular traps, which are significant contributors to thrombosis and inflammation associated with BD.38

A systematic review of 34 studies (2,516 vascular BD patients) found that anticoagulation was used in 74.6% of studies (mostly warfarin), and combined with immunosuppressants in 66.9% of studies, with positive efficacy outcomes in 62.5% of studies overall and all four cardiac-specific studies.39–41 In that systematic review, favourable thrombus resolution and low recurrence were noted in the ICT-focused studies (e.g. 22 patients in the study by Emmungil et al., 12 patients in the study by Wang et al. and 20 patients in the study by Zhu et al.).6,39,40 Although surgical intervention was performed in 23.12% (n=37/160) of patients in that systematic review, often in instances of thrombi causing haemodynamic compromise, it carried risks such as anastomotic dehiscence and recurrent thrombosis and was associated with high mortality compared with medical intervention.42,43 This agrees substantially with our results, whereby 60% (n=12/20) of patients who underwent surgical intervention experienced recurrence of ICT, and 34.78% (n=8) of the 23 patients who died had undergone surgery. These findings suggest that surgery alone may not be sufficient to prevent recurrence or have a mortality benefit. Therefore, multifaceted medical therapy should be prioritised as a first-line treatment to minimise complications associated with invasive approaches, improve long-term outcomes and reduce the likelihood of recurrence.

Among the 43 patients with incomplete resolution, 32.56% (n=14) were treated with the corticosteroid–immunosuppressant–anticoagulant combination; the potential efficacy of this regimen is worth noting, given its high complete resolution rate. The fact that combining immunosuppressive therapy with anticoagulants led to a considerably reduced thrombosis relapse rate (17.7%; 95% CI [9.7–30.1%]) than either treatment alone further supports that this could be an effective option for the treatment of ICT.44 We propose that optimising the duration and dosages of this combined treatment regimen could lead to even more favourable outcomes, and we recommend further studies to explore this issue.

Management of vascular disease in BD relies heavily on immunosuppressive therapy, which has been shown to reduce relapse rates and improve survival.28,45–49 Although the role of anticoagulation alone remains debated, particularly regarding its limited effectiveness in preventing relapses, we believe that combination treatment regimens may provide a synergistic and adjunctive effect in the management of ICT.29 This is supported by a recent systematic review and meta-analysis, which found significantly lower pooled thrombosis relapse rates with combined immunosuppressive and anticoagulant therapy (17.7%) compared with immunosuppressants alone (43.7%) or anticoagulants alone (54.9%).44 Notably, a retrospective study on 102 BD patients with vascular disease found that a combination of corticosteroids, immunosuppressants and anticoagulants/aspirin (primarily using cyclophosphamide as the immunosuppressive agent) yielded a recovery rate of nearly 92%, emphasising the significant effectiveness of this treatment regimen.22 This aligns with evidence from multiple studies demonstrating that the addition of anticoagulation to immunosuppressive therapy is associated with reduced relapse rates, including a decrease in postoperative thrombosis from 75% to 40% and lower venous relapse rates in cohorts with near-universal anticoagulation use.41

The high rate of ICT persistence in 25% (n=3/12) of patients who received a combination of corticosteroids, anticoagulants, immunosuppressants and colchicine is concerning. This finding appears to contrast with the findings of a recent meta-analysis, which demonstrated that combination therapy had the lowest incidence of relapse (0.5 events per 100 patient-months) compared with immunosuppressive (0.9 events) or anticoagulant therapy (1.8 events) alone.44 Although high-resolution rates were observed among those on this regimen in the present study, the persistence of ICT may be attributed to inadequate patient follow-up, because we could not determine the exact duration of treatment for these patients at the time when persistent ICT was noted. In addition, poor adherence to medication could play a role in these outcomes.

Study Strengths and Limitations

One of the key strengths of this review lies in its comprehensive systematic methodology, which involved the analysis of 157 articles published across diverse geographic regions. The study included a substantial number of patients, with extensive and detailed extraction of data encompassing demographic information, clinical characteristics, diagnostic modalities and, most importantly, treatment strategies and their outcomes. This approach provides significant clinical value to the literature, particularly in advancing the understanding of the diagnosis and management of ICT in the context of BD. A notable contribution of this review is its focus on the efficacy of non-invasive treatment strategies, which adds substantial depth to the existing body of knowledge, potentially enhancing patient safety and reducing the risk of complications.

The study has limitations. The reliance on case reports and case series introduces the possibility of publication bias and limits the generalisability of the findings, thereby constraining the ability to draw definitive conclusions applicable to the broader population. We attempted to mitigate this limitation by conducting a quality assessment of each included article, which informed our decisions on inclusion and exclusion.

Furthermore, the heterogeneous follow-up duration of patients between included studies could potentially overestimate or underestimate clinical outcomes after treatment initiation. In addition, the lack of precise reporting regarding disease duration in patient histories and the specific medications used in certain management strategies posed challenges. These gaps made it difficult to determine the mean disease duration at which ICT is most likely to occur and to further refine treatment recommendations. Our review is subject to selection, reporting, geographic and language biases, because the included studies were predominantly case reports or series published in English from select regions. These factors may limit the generalisability of our findings.

Conclusion

ICT in BD predominantly affects young males, particularly in regions with high BD prevalence, such as the Mediterranean basin and Middle East. ICT frequently serves as the initial clinical manifestation of BD, often presenting with non-specific symptoms such as fever, dyspnoea, haemoptysis and cough. PE and PAA are the most commonly associated complications. Transthoracic echocardiography appears to be the preferred initial diagnostic modality. Therapeutic management generally favours a multimodal approach combining corticosteroids, immunosuppressive agents and anticoagulants. This non-invasive combination therapy was frequently associated with higher rates of thrombus resolution and lower recurrence and mortality rates compared with invasive interventions. Surgical intervention was associated with high morbidity and mortality. An ESR cut-off of ≥42.5 mm/h may predict ICT presence; however, this needs to be validated by further assessment. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for ICT in BD patients and initiate prompt medical therapy to mitigate serious complications. Importantly, randomised controlled trials are needed to clarify optimal therapeutic strategies and establish evidence-based management guidelines for these patients.
