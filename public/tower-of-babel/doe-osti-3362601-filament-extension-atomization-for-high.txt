Executive Summary
The Palo Alto Research Center, Inc. (PARC) collaborated with Utah State University, Darigold,
Leprino Foods, and Tetra Pak to advance PARC’s Filament Extension Atomization (FEA)
technology and demonstrate spray drying of high concentration whey products to produce high
quality powders for the U.S. dairy industry with substantially less energy. Current industrial
spray drying relies upon conventional systems that are reliable, mature processes, yet are limited
to concentrations between 33-50%, which leads to substantial energy consumption to remove the
remaining solvent fractions (water). Almost one billion pounds of dry whey and approximately
500 millions pounds of whey protein concentrate was produced in the U.S. in 2024 according to
USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service reports, and this production consumes an
estimated 4.5 trillion Btu according based on our TEA analysis. By reducing the amount of
energy needed to spray dry product, the spray drying process can be made more efficient as
additional water removal can be conducted by more efficient processes. This will help the U.S.
dairy industry reduce the overall carbon emissions from producing dairy powders, reduce costs
of operating their facilities and producing powders, and help keep the industry competitive.

FEA is a novel spray technology that expands the range of high-performance spray feedstocks in
dairy product lines and across the entire spray drying industry, by enabling formulations that are
otherwise unsprayable by current methods. At its core, FEA involves two rollers that pull the
fluid into a contact point or nip. After emerging from the nip, filaments form between the two
rollers. Since the rollers are spinning, the filaments continue to be pulled and thinned until they
are thin enough that surface tension causes the filament to break into small droplets. The
development of FEA technology and subsequent integration into spray drying systems would
enable the dairy industry to increase the processable concentration of whey in spray drying
formulations, thereby reducing the fraction of water that must be removed during the drying
process, while simultaneously increasing whey output.

We set out in this work to demonstrate that we could scale FEA to reach outputs needed by
industrial production, while increasing solids loading of the sprayed product by at least 30% and
maintaining equal or better spray powder. After testing a wide range of products, in collaboration
with industry partners we decided on our primary spray products of dry whey and WPC-80, two
common materials processed and sold by US manufacturers with different parameters. We
sprayed these with FEA at solids loadings of 70% for dry whey and 45% for WPC-80 with a
spray output with particle sizes similar to industrial particles sizes and reduced variation in
particle size. We simultaneously scaled up FEA first with a multi-nip with 6 nips surrounding a
central roller with parallel axis of rotation and eventually with a tapered design that solved
problems we encountered with our initial design. We were able to achieve output from a single
array from our first design of up to 4.7 liters per minute (L/min) and from an array of our second
multi-nip of 8 L/min exceeding expectations. This demonstrates that FEA technology can indeed
be scaled up to meet the needs of industrial production. More arrays can be added as necessary to
meet a wide range of spray dryer designs. We also tested FEA to create dried powders from a
small scale (10 L/hour of water removal) spray dryer. Though we were not able to produce large
quantities of powder from FEA due to challenges in integration, the powder we produced was
higher quality and produced from higher solids loading materials. From our technoeconomic
analysis we for a typically sized spray dryer, we estimate a 27% cost reduction and 41% energy
and carbon reduction for WPC-80 and a 39-57% cost reduction and 52-76% energy and carbon
reduction for sweet dry whey (depending on the exact product).

 Page 4 of 25

From the work on this project we also made some significant learnings that will impact the
overall arc of the technology. Although our initial testing of the multi-nip tapered array met our
metrics for success with additional harvesting airflow, these challenges with harvesting of the
spray were exacerbated when we tried to integrate the system into a small scale spray device. It
will be important for future FEA development efforts to be integrated into spray drying systems
with modifications to support FEA. While we still believe retrofits will be possible, they will be
not be as simple as replacing a nozzle. More effort will be required to optimize the design of the
spray dryer and FEA system. This work also focused entirely on the FEA system and spray
drying, when in fact any powder production line will be a series of process steps. It will be
important to create demonstration systems that take into account the entire process and as
necessary adapts those processes for the high viscosity materials that FEA enables.

FEA is well positioned to be further developed and commercialized as a spray drying
technology. With both our existing partners and new partners further discussions are warranted
to discuss what kinds of testing and demonstrations will be required to fully adopt the technology
into production plants and realize the energy and carbon reductions. In addition to applications
within the dairy and broader food industry, there may be potential applications for FEA spray
drying technology in pharmaceuticals and chemical production. Although these industries are not
as large in terms of energy usage as food and beverage there may be opportunities to use FEA in
these applications as a demonstration before full commercialization in food and beverage.

1. Introduction
The Palo Alto Research Center, Inc. (PARC) collaborated with Utah State University, Darigold,
Leprino Foods, and Tetra Pak to advance PARC’s Filament Extension Atomization (FEA)
technology and demonstrate spray drying of high concentration whey products to produce high
quality powders for the U.S. dairy industry with substantially less energy. Current industrial
spray drying relies upon conventional systems that are reliable, mature processes, yet are unable
to handle intermediate whey concentrations (50-60% solids content), which leads to substantial
energy consumption to remove the remaining solvent fractions (water). Almost one billion
pounds of dry whey and approximately 500 millions pounds of whey protein concentrate was
produced in the U.S. in 2024 according to USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service reports,
and this production consumes an estimated 4.5 trillion Btu according based on our TEA analysis.
The development of FEA technology and subsequent integration into spray drying systems
would enable the dairy industry to increase the processable concentration of whey in spray
drying formulations, thereby reducing the fraction of water that must be removed during the
drying process, while simultaneously increasing whey output. By the end of the project, the team
aimed to demonstrate a small-scale pilot system that yields a spray-dried product of equal or
better quality than current systems with increased solids content of at least 70%, a flow rate of
7.5 L/min, and a drop size distribution tighter than existing spray technology. The combination
of decreasing solvent (water) content while creating a tighter drop distribution was expected to
yield an energy savings of at least 40% within the spray drying system and 15% in the overall
whey powder production process.
Budget period 1 focused on developing the core atomization physics, ideal materials of
construction, and process settings in order to spray at least two different whey products.
Concentrated whey formulations with 30% higher solids loading were created and characterized
for relevant rheology. Utilizing existing infrastructure, spray experiments were conducted on
whey products and particle sizes between 50 and 200 microns were produced. The results of this
testing was used to develop a design for a multi-nip system, resulting in a critical design review.
In budget period 2, a multi-nip system was developed for intended use in a 10 L/hour spray
drying system. Initial spray testing with the multi-nip system was conducted with the aim of
demonstrating 2.5L/min output from the multi-nip array. Initial spray drying tests in this budget
period allowed for the development of a second generation FEA unit in budget period 3.
Budget period 3 focused on fully optimizing the FEA spray system for both output and spray
dried product quality. An array of multi-nip spray systems were intended to demonstrate a 7.5
L/min total output and 10kg of material will be collected from the spray drying system with
quality equal to or better than material sprayed with standard atomizers. Finally, a
technoeconomic analysis and system level design exercise were conducted to position FEA
technology for pre-production trial runs and commercial adoption.

2. Background
FEA is a novel spray technology that expands the range of high-performance spray feedstocks in
dairy product lines and across the entire spray drying industry, by enabling formulations that are
otherwise unsprayable by current methods. At its core, FEA involves two rollers that pull the
fluid into a contact point or nip. After emerging from the nip, filaments form between the two
rollers. Since the rollers are spinning, the filaments continue to be pulled and thinned until they
are thin enough that surface tension causes the filament to break into small droplets. A harvesting
 Page 6 of 25

element then receives these droplets and directs them for further processing. While FEA
technology has been proven to be effective at spraying a wide range of materials including
extremely high viscosity sealants and coatings, shown as a feasible approach to polymer particle
production, and miniaturized for drug delivery applications, the technology has not yet been
extended to be applicable for high volume production.

3. Results and Discussion
 a) Budget Period 1
Summary: We conducted initial tests with a variety of whey products from our partners
(Leprino and Darigold) to downselect to two materials for testing. As a result, whey protein
concentrate (WPC) 80 and sweet whey/dry whey were selected as our primary materials.
Candidate rollers materials were obtained for testing, initial spray testing was conducted on
candidate materials, a quantitative mixer was designed, and our spray system was modified with
a slot die feed system. We optimized spray for each material, and while we had good results
spraying large quantities of dry whey, there was some difficulty in getting good quality spray
from WPC-80 and optimization took longer than required, especially at higher target
concentrations. We revised our target concentration of WPC-80 to 45% or more and this has
allowed us to produce product that can be consistently mixed and we can spray. Using this
material, we have been able to demonstrate spray from both materials that meets the target
particle size target with a 30% increase in solids loading over current state-of-the-art (SOA)
levels. We used a parametric model to analyze a wide range of different possible combinations of
roller layout, airflow speed, and operating parameters to develop some insight into how these
impact harvest efficiency and particle size distribution. We completed the design of the multi-nip
design with selected sizes and roller materials and textures for the multi-nip system based on
testing with our single nip test platforms.

Task 1: Reformulation of whey products
By evaluating multiple candidate materials and based on discussions with our dairy partners
(Darigold, Leprino, Tetrapak), we selected dry whey and WPC-80 as our primary candidate
materials for spraying. These materials represent a good cross section of materials used in
industry and are substantial products that would have an immediate impact on energy usage in
the industry if FEA was adopted.
After selecting our primary materials, we used both existing PARC infrastructure and built a new
overhead mixer with active and more accurate torque feedback control to investigate a possible
mixing procedure to achieve a high solids formulation from dried powder. We were successful in
concentrating dry whey at up to 80% concentration at scale. We were able to achieve this
consistently with different mixers and at a wide range of concentrations above 70%.
Concentrating WPC-80 proved to be much more difficult. While we were able to create limited
formulations with concentrations above 70%, we would observe inconsistent behavior of these
samples. The mixing process would also produce a product with a consistency closer to dough
and some evidence of damage to the proteins in the form of discoloration. Based on
conversations with our partners, we decided to pivot trying to achieve concentrations of 45%+.
This met our goal/no-goal for the project since starting concentrations of WPC-80 are typically
33% vs. closer to the 50% for dry whey.
We studied the differences between liquid product taken directly from the production feedstock
and reconstituted product at standard production concentrations. We also studied the differences

in rheology at different holding times and different temperatures. Rheology sweeps were
conducted across different concentrations of the whey products and the effect of mixing process
on the rheology was also investigated. These results have been summarized in peer reviewed
publications (see Section 6: Products).

Task 2: Single nip spray testing
From preliminary testing, we discovered that dry whey, which behaves as a shear thinning
concentrated suspension at large solids loadings, can be sprayed easily using existing rollers
within roller speeds on our existing setup – between 1000 and 5000 revolutions per minute
(RPM) – at high spray conversion efficiency leading to large amounts of droplets generated. We
encountered greater difficulty in spraying WPC-80 at extreme concentrations of 70%, given its
significant increase in elasticity, which manifests in the formation of highly stable liquid films
and filaments when stretched shown in Figure 1; though we were successful with concentrations
of WPC-80 at 45%.

Task 4: Multi-nip spray system and dryer design
We developed a comprehensive set of design options for multiple subsystems for the multi-nip
array. The system can be divided into several important sub-systems, the individual rollers, the
drive system, airflow for harvesting, fluid pumping, the layout and interactions of the rollers, the
fluid dispensing, and overall containment and cleaning of the system. For each subsystem, we
developed a list of possible solutions and weighed the pros and cons of each individual design.
This has allowed us to select a primary and backup solution for each subsystem. The overall
design is detailed in Figure 7 and Figure 8. Of these sub-systems, the fluid dispensing has proven
to be the most challenging given the space constraints in the system and the desire to maximize
space between the rollers to optimize harvesting based on modeling efforts. Some novel concepts
were developed that will be tested in parallel with the preliminary design. The initial analysis of
the system indicated ability to meet the required 2.5L/min using an array of 6 rollers.

Summary: With the completion of the detail design of the multi-nip array we ordered,

drop size distribution with a tight span that mirrors our previously presented results. Our span
measurements for the best performing conditions from extensive testing showed that our drop
size distribution is tighter than those found in commercial powders. Our FEA spray has a span of
0.9-1.2, compared to commercial powders with a span of 2.3-2.4. We built and tested an

Task 11: Next generation multi-nip FEA and array design
Our multi-nip testing showed that we were able to generate volumes of spray that far exceeded
our targets (4.7L/min vs. 2.5 L/min). Our biggest challenges were in harvesting the resultant
spray. Based on the learnings from this initial testing we developed a list of potential
improvements and more drastic improvements to the system that would improve harvesting
efficiency. These included both geometric changes to the arrangement of the rollers, additional
methods of harvesting, and introducing new systems aside from airflow that would allow us to
harvest. We completed testing of several new ideas including alternative geometric layouts. The
alternative layout that showed the most promise was to alter the direction of the nip so that it was
angled downwards. This would give the spray significantly more downward direction instead of
purely being directed outward and will allow for additional installation freedom and eliminate
the need for additional airflow. This went through several iterations to verify that we could
harvest materials as required and determine the optimal roller diameters and arrangements. Our
initial calculations show that within the same footprint as the existing multi-nip array we can
meet the same output performance, and given the variable roller speeds introduced by this design
testing at both the high and low end of those speeds shows we continue to meet spray quality and
a Dv50 between 50-200 microns.
Based on this initial design, we completed a design as shown in Figure 21 for a single multi-nip
and array and proceeded to manufacture the system. The new multi-nip is similar to the previous
version with some significant changes. The new arrangement only has 5 rollers per large roller
and the drive mechanism has changed. This version places all bearings in the rollers themselves
and uses stationary shafts to support the tapered rollers on the outside of the system. All parts
were purchased with a strong preference for U.S. manufacturers. Most machined parts were
made with either our in-house machine shop or local machine shops in the San Francisco Bay
area. The rollers were manufactured through dual vendors, a U.S. compression molding vendor
created a mold to over mold cores and a rubber grinding shop was also tasked with producing
these. Molded rollers give us better scalability but is riskier given the need for rigorous process
development to meet our runout specifications. In the end, parts from grinding proved to be
better tolerance and higher quality. More iteration will be needed with molded rollers to meet our
targets for dimensional quality.

Task 12: Cleanliness, contamination, and food safe viability
We pursued two cleaning strategies that can be used synergistically. First, we pressure wash the
rollers, this can be achieved in the field easily as it is common for spray dryers to undergo short
period of cleaning every 20 hours or so. Second, we have found an enzymatic cleaner that has
been shown in other applications to work well as a cleaner and disinfectant. This can be pumped
through the fluid feed system to ensure the entire system is clean.
In order to test our cleaning strategy, we have tested two bacterial environments. We tested
rollers that were coated with whey and allowed to dry, and another set of rollers which were left
in a wet state after being coated in whey. Two roller cleaning procedures were tested: 1) pressure
washing, and 2) pressure washing + immersion in enzymatic cleaner for an hour. Roller surfaces
were swabbed pre- and post-cleaning and outsourced to an analytical laboratory for CFU counts.
The dry rollers did not show significant bacterial growth, and had very low initial bacterial
counts. The wet rollers contained significant bacterial accumulation pre-wash, and showed ~105
reduction in colony-forming units (CFUs) when pressure washed followed by immersion in
enzymatic cleaner. Our testing indicates our cleaning procedure results in between a 5log10 and
6log10 reduction in bacterial loading with two successive cleaning steps.

spray quality from rollers at different diameters running and different speeds. This matrix will

impacts on the filament formation due to roller size and speed and droplet breakup due to

spray dryer, the capital cost of an FEA device is estimated to be recovered in approximately 1
year due to lower operating costs, using 2022 grid mix values for energy costs.

4. Conclusions
We largely met the technical goals set forth in the beginning of our project and demonstrated that
FEA technology can be used to spray dry whey products with a significant increase in solids
                                                    Page 22 of 25

loading and that it can be scaled to higher outputs that will be required for industrial production.
FEA was able to spray dry whey and WPC-80 at concentrations of 70% and 45% respectively, a
30%+ increase in solids loading representing a 41% and between a 52-76% energy and carbon
reduction (calculated). By multi-plexing the FEA array we were able to demonstrate that we could
spray material at a rate of 8 L/min. Furthermore, the spray particles produced by FEA are higher
quality, with a tighter span ((Dv90-Dv10)/Dv50), representing the width of the size distribution,
of between 0.9-1.1 as compared to industrial benchmarks of commercially obtained powders of
between 1.5-1.8. When mated with a small-scale spray dryer, we were able to produce better
quality spray with better flowability. While limitations in this spray dryer size prevented us from
producing our target quantity of powder (10 kg) our initial demonstrations provide us with valuable
experience for future scale up work.

Funding provided by the Department of Energy under this award has been instrumental in
advancing this technology and advancing it towards commercialization. With our development
work on a system that can be scaled and small spray drying, the technology has been significantly
de-risked and will help to jump start further commercial interest. Our demonstrations and
technoeconomic calculations also further underpinned the potential value of the technology to the
U.S. food and beverage industry. In addition to the substantial energy and carbon reductions
realized by increasing the solids loading, our financial models show that payback periods are <1
year for a retrofit application and would reduce costs of spray drying operations of between 27-
57%. Cost, energy, and carbon reductions will further increase competitiveness of the U.S.
manufacturing sector if the technology is fully adopted.

5. Future Work/Recommendations
At the end of this work we were able to demonstrate an FEA system capable of approximately 8
L/min of spray output. However, our drying tests to produce powder were limited by the scale of
the spray dryer we had access to. A new competitively-selected DOE award (DE-EE0010851
from the Industrial Efficiency and Decarbonization Office has been received to further scale-up
and demonstrate this technology. Our follow-on work will build on the work conducted during
this research with the overall goal of demonstrating a pre-production plant capable of drying at
food and beverage products at a rate of at least 100 L/hour of water removal. We expect this
work to provide a bridge to future commercialization and demonstrate that the technology can be
scaled to industrial scales. The scope of this work will also include expanding the material set
beyond whey products to demonstrate that the technology can be used on a diverse set of
materials and will tackle drying challenges that occur upstream of the spray dryer in the pre-
concentration steps that occur before the material is sprayed.

Beyond this work, FEA is well positioned to be commercialized as a spray drying technology.
With both our existing partners and new partners further discussions are warranted to discuss
what kinds of testing and demonstrations will be required to fully adopt the technology into
production plants and realize the energy and carbon reductions. In addition to applications within
the dairy and broader food industry, there may be potential applications for FEA spray drying
technology in pharmaceuticals and chemical production. Although spray drying applications in
these industries may not be as large in terms of energy usage as food and beverage, there may be
opportunities to use FEA in these applications as a demonstration before full commercialization
in food and beverage.
