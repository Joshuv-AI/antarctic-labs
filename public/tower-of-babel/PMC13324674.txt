Phytochemical investigation of Phyllanthus emblica (Amla) for therapeutic application in urinary tract infection (UTI) management and development of functional tea

Published: 2026

Introduction

Urinary tract infection (UTI) is among the most prevalent bacterial infections worldwide, affecting nearly 40 % of women and 12 % of men during their lifetime, with recurrent infections frequently reported among women [1], [2]. UTIs are primarily caused by Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Citrobacter, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus bovis, Candida albicans, and Candida glabrata [3]. The growing prevalence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) among these pathogens has become a major global healthcare concern, particularly in developing regions where empirical antibiotic usage and limited antimicrobial stewardship contribute to the emergence of multidrug-resistant strains [4], [5], [6]. In South Asian countries, including Pakistan and Bangladesh, increasing resistance of uropathogenic E. coli against commonly prescribed antibiotics such as cephalosporins and quinolones has further complicated clinical management of UTIs [7], [8]. Persistent infection and urothelial inflammation may also contribute to recurrent UTIs, discomfort, and long-term alterations in urinary tract function [9]. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are commonly prescribed to treat inflammation, but their long-term use may cause serious side effects [10]. Collectively, these findings underscore the growing limitations of conventional antibiotics in managing urinary tract infections and highlight the need for alternative strategies. In this context, plant-derived bioactive compounds have gained considerable attention as potential alternatives due to their diverse pharmacological properties [11], [12].

Phyllanthus emblica (Indian Gooseberry or Amla) is a deciduous tree that originates from rain-fed areas and falls under the family Euphorbiaceae and subfamily Phyllanthoidae [13]. It is mainly found in China, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, Pakistan, India, and Indonesia [14]. Nearly every part of this plant has pharmacological potential, including antimicrobial activity against clinically relevant uropathogens, especially Escherichia coli, Klebsiella oxytoca, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Candida spp. [15], [16], antiviral [17], anti-inflammatory, antioxidant [18], anti-aging [19], anti-diabetic [20], hypolipidemic [21], hypoglycaemic [22], neurologically protective [23], anti-cancer [24], immunomodulatory, and hepatoprotective activities [25]. Collectively, these studies indicate that P. emblica has promising biological potential, supported by the presence of bioactive metabolites. However, the efficiency of recovering these bioactive compounds largely depends on the extraction technique employed. Conventional extraction using solvents such as hexane, methanol, chloroform, ethanol, and water has drawbacks, including high solvent use, long processing times, low selectivity, and toxic concerns, especially for food and nutraceutical applications [26]. This has driven the development of faster, more efficient, and eco-friendly extraction techniques. Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) is an emerging green extraction technology based on sonochemical phenomena generated through acoustic cavitation. During ultrasonication, alternating compression and rarefaction cycles induce the formation, growth, and implosive collapse of cavitation bubbles, producing localized high-temperature and high-pressure microenvironments, shear forces, shock waves, and microjets [27]. These mechanochemical effects enhance solvent penetration, disrupt plant cell walls, reduce particle size, and improve mass transfer between the plant matrix and extraction solvent. In polar solvents such as methanol and ethanol, cavitation may also promote the generation of reactive radical species that contribute to structural disruption and enhanced release of intracellular phytochemicals [27], [28]. Conclusively, comparing with conventional methods, UAE enables better recovery of phenolic and flavonoid compounds associated with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, while its mild conditions help preserve thermolabile constituents [28].

Beyond the extraction efficiency, the delivery of these bioactive metabolites into viable consumable forms is also crucial. It is well known that functional beverages, especially herbal teas, are the most effective delivery systems because they are easy to prepare and highly acceptable to consumers [29], [30]. Aqueous infusion facilitates the extraction of water-soluble phytochemicals, such as phenolics and flavonoids, thereby contributing to antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects [31]. Functional beverage formulations may improve the gastrointestinal bioaccessibility of phenolic compounds and enhance their physiological effectiveness [32]. P. emblica has been previously explored in food and beverage systems, including fermented products and kombucha-based formulations [33]. However, previous studies have primarily emphasized nutritional characteristics, fermentation, or general functional attributes rather than antimicrobial activity against clinically relevant uropathogens and inflammation-associated pathways. Furthermore, comparative evaluation of conventional and ultrasound-assisted extraction for enhanced phytochemical recovery and bioactivity in P. emblica remains limited. So, it was hypothesized that sonochemical extraction would enhance the recovery of bioactive metabolites and improve the biological potential of P. emblica extracts. Therefore, the present study objectives were to: (i) compare conventional and sonochemical extraction, (ii) evaluate antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial activities adopting a bioassay-guided approach, and (iii) develop and assess a P. emblica-based functional tea targeting uropathogenic microorganisms.

Material and methodology

Sample preparation

Fresh amla (Phyllanthus emblica) was purchased from Multan, Pakistan, in March 2025. The Botany Department of Bahauddin Zakariya University performed the taxonomic identification. The sample was pitted for sun drying (40–45 °C for 72 h) and freeze drying (5 Pa pressure for 12 h at –50 °C) to grind it into a fine powder (< 200 µm). Prior to freeze drying, samples were pre-frozen at –20 °C for 24 h to facilitate ice crystal formation and structural stabilization. Two different methods were adopted to prepare the sample: 1st method involved sun-drying followed by conventional extraction (orbital shaker at 120 rpm for 24 h at 40 °C), and 2nd method involved freeze-drying followed by sonication-assisted extraction using ethanol and methanol with a 1:10 solid-solvent ratio in pulse mode with 50 sec ON and 50 sec OFF cycle. Samples underwent sonication for 10 min at 25 °C using an ultrasonic processor (VCX130, Sonics & Materials Inc., Newtown, USA) operating at 20 kHz. The titanium sonotrode, with a tip diameter of 18 mm and a length of 70 mm, was immersed approximately 3 cm into each sample. Sonication was performed at an ultrasonic power density of 0.41 ± 0.02 W/g. The resulting extracts were concentrated using a rotary evaporator (Heidolph, Hei-Vap, Schwabach, Germany) under reduced pressure to a semi-solid state for further analysis.

Phytochemical analysis of P. emblica extracts

Total phenolic content (TPC) was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method as described by Singleton and Rossi [34], with gallic acid as the standard and ethanol as the blank. Absorbance was measured at 765 nm using a spectrophotometer (V-3000; VWR, Darmstadt, Germany), and results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE)/g. In contrast, the total flavonoid content (TFC) was quantified using the AlCl3 colorimetric method as described by Pękal and Pyrzynska [35]. Absorbance was recorded at 510 nm using a spectrophotometer (V-3000; VWR, Darmstadt, Germany), and results were expressed as mg quercetin equivalents (QE)/g. Total tannin content (TTC) was assessed using the Folin-Denis method as prescribed by Ojha et al. [36], with tannic acid as a standard. Absorbance was measured at 720 nm using a spectrophotometer (V-3000; VWR, Darmstadt, Germany). Total tannin was calculated from the tannic acid calibration curve (1.5 to 20 µg/mL; r2 = 0.999) and expressed as mg tannic acid equivalent (TAE) per g of amla sample.

Antioxidant activity

The DPPH radical scavenging activity was evaluated using the method of Alara et al. [37]. Stock solution of each sample was prepared as 1 g/ 20 mL (50 mg/mL), and that was further diluted to working concentration from (1, 0.5, 0.25 to 0.125 mg/mL) to perform the analysis. In detail, for each concentration, the reaction mixture was prepared by adding 2 mL of DPPH (0.1 mM), 2 mL of methanol, and 0.2 mL of the extract. After that, the reaction mixture was incubated for 30 min in the dark at room temperature to prevent possible photodegradation of the DPPH radical. A spectrophotometer was used to measure absorbance at 517 nm using ascorbic acid as the standard. The percent inhibition was calculated using the following formula:

% inhibition = (Abs Control − Abs Sample) / (Abs Control) × 100.

Moreover, IC50 was also calculated using nonlinear regression analysis of a dose–response curve in GraphPad Prism. Ferric reducing antioxidant potential (FRAP) was determined by a modified method of Zahin et al. [38]. In brief, 100 µL of extract (1 mg/ mL) was added to 300 µL of FRAP reagent, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 593 nm using a spectrophotometer, and the findings were expressed as Fe2+ µmol equivalents/g of dried extract.

In vitro anti-inflammatory

Egg albumin denaturation assay

The inhibition of protein denaturation was tested using a modified method of Mizushima and Kobayashi [39]. The reaction mixture (5 mL of egg albumin, 0.2 mL of phosphate buffer at pH 6.5, and 2 mL of extract (400 µg/mL)) was incubated after heating at 70 °C for 5 min, and the absorbance was measured at 660 nm. Diclofenac sodium was the standard, and phosphate buffer was the control.

Bovine serum albumin (BSA) denaturation assay

The assay was performed according to the procedure outlined by Sakat and coworkers [40] with slight modifications. The turbidity of the reaction mixture (0.5 mL) prepared by adding 0.45 mL BSA (0.4 %) and 0.05 mL extract (400 µg/mL) was measured at 660 nm after incubation at 40 °C for 25 min. Diclofenac sodium was used as a standard, and phosphate buffer as a control. For both assays, percentage inhibition was calculated using the following equation:

% inhibition = (Abs Control – Abs treated)/ Abs Control × 100.

COX-1 and COX-2 inhibition potential

The in vitro inhibitory effects of P. emblica ethanol and methanol extracts (20, 40, 60, and 80 µg/mL) on COX-1 (ovine) and COX-2 (human recombinant) enzymes were evaluated using a COX Inhibitor Screening Assay Kit (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The reaction mixture in each well contained 150 µL assay buffer (0.1 M Tris-HCl, pH 8.0), 10 µL heme (for enzyme stabilization), 10 µL COX-1 or COX-2 enzyme, 10 µL test extract (20–80 µg/mL in DMSO), and 10 µL DMSO (vehicle control). After incubation at 25 °C for 5 min, the reaction was initiated by adding 20 µL of N,N,N′,N′-tetramethyl-p-phenylenediamine (TMPD) (colorimetric substrate) and 20 µL arachidonic acid (enzyme substrate). The plate was incubated for 2 min at 25 °C, and absorbance was measured at 590 nm. The percentage inhibition of COX activity was calculated using the equation below [41].

% Inhibition = (1 − Activity (control)/Activity (sample)) × 100.

Antimicrobial activity of P. emblica sequential extracts

The well diffusion technique [42] was used to evaluate the antimicrobial activity of P. emblica extracts against bacterial (Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, Citrobacter spp. ATCC 51116, and Klebsiella oxytoca ATCC 13182) and fungal (Candida albicans ATCC 90028 and Candida glabrata ATCC 15128) strains. The bacterial strains were revived on nutrient agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. The fungal strains were activated on Sabouraud dextrose agar and incubated at 28–30 °C for 24–48 h. The inoculum for each microbial strain was prepared by suspending freshly grown colonies in sterile normal saline (0.85 % NaCl) and adjusting the turbidity to 0.5 McFarland standard, i.e., ∼1 × 108 CFU/mL for bacterial and ∼ 1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL for fungal strains. The turbidity was adjusted spectrophotometrically (OD600 ≈ 0.08–0.1 for bacteria). In this method, 25 µL (25 µg/mL) of each extract and 5 µg/mL of standard drugs (Ciprofloxacin for bacteria and fluconazole for Fungi) were used, and results were expressed as zones of inhibition (ZOI) in millimeters (mm).

LC-ESI-MS/MS analysis

A Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer (TSQ Quantis, Thermo-Electron Scientific, USA) equipped with a heated electrospray ionization (H-ESI) source was used to perform the analysis by the direct insertion method. The parameters included were: ionization modes (positive, negative), flow rate (9 µL/min), capillary voltage (4.2 kV), capillary temperature (278 °C), sheath gas flow rate (14 units), auxiliary gas flow rate (4 units), scanning mass range (30–1500 m/z), and collision energy (10–40 V) [43]. Structural elucidation was performed using ChemDraw (ChemDraw Ultra 8.0) and then compared with previously published data.

Evaluation of functional tea development potential

To assess the development potential of functional tea, a freeze-dried powder of P. emblica (2 g per 150 mL) was brewed for 1–2 min at 95–100 °C. The resulting infusion was cooled to room temperature and processed using a rotary evaporator to yield a semisolid mass. The P. emblica tea extract was then evaluated for the retention of phenolics, flavonoids, and tannin contents. Antioxidant (DPPH) and antibacterial activities were assessed as described in 2.3, 2.6. Mass spectrometry analysis was also performed to check the signal intensity.

Development of functional tea formulations and sensory analysis

Three functional tea formulations were developed as tea bags (approximately 2 g) by combining various concentrations of freeze-dried P. emblica powder with green tea. T0 was considered the control and consisted of 100 % P. emblica; T1 was 25 % green tea and 75 % P. emblica; T2 was 50 % green tea and 50 % P. emblica; and T3 was 75 % green tea and 25 % P. emblica. To brew the tea, each tea bag was dipped into hot water (95–100 °C) and stirred for 1 min. Sensory evaluation was performed using a semi-trained panel of 12 judges (4 women and 8 men aged between 35 and 60 and 32 and 45, respectively), recruited from the Faculty of Food Science and Nutrition, BZU, Multan, and all were very familiar with the sensory attributes and evaluation procedure prior to analysis. Panelists were regular tea consumers and screened for known allergies to P. emblica and green tea. Tea samples were coded and presented in a randomized order to avoid bias. Each sample was evaluated for sensory attributes, including color (visual intensity), taste/bitterness (taste balance), aroma, mouthfeel (astringency and tactile sensation), and overall acceptability using a 9-point hedonic scale (1 = dislike extremely, 9 = like extremely). Although the panel size was relatively small, it is consistent with preliminary sensory screening studies, and the results provide indicative trends for formulation optimization.

Statistical analysis

All determinations were conducted in triplicate, and results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD). Statistical significance was assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's test for antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial assays in which extract activities were compared against corresponding standard compounds or drugs using GraphPad Prism-6 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA, http://www.graphpad.com). Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used for pairwise comparisons, i.e., in sensory analysis. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Phytochemical contents

Among solvents, methanolic extraction showed greater affinity for extracting phytochemicals than ethanol extraction, as shown in Table 1 for both methods: sun drying with an orbital shaker (method 1) and freeze drying with sonication (method 2). Our findings showed that method 2 consistently afforded higher total phenolic (170.18 ± 1.2 mg GAE/g), flavonoid (12.6 ± 1.3 mg QE/g), and tannin (49.4 ± 0.8 mg TAE/g) contents than those of the ethanol and method 1 counterparts. Research findings indicated that freeze-drying of M. chamomilla retains more phytochemical content than other drying methods, including sun-drying [44]. In addition, an investigation of purslane showed that freeze-drying with sonication yielded higher phenolic content than sun, tunnel, or vacuum drying [45]. The preservation of bioactive metabolites during freeze drying is achieved by subliming water at low temperature and pressure, which minimizes the degradation of heat-sensitive compounds such as flavonoids, phenolic acids, and vitamins [46]. Moreover, sonication breaks plant cell walls through acoustic cavitation, enhancing solvent penetration and facilitating the release of intracellular compounds [47]. Our findings are consistent with previous reports showing that ultrasound-assisted extraction with freeze-drying improves both extraction yield and phytochemical stability [48], [49]. Therefore, integrating freeze-drying and sonication represents a more effective and scientifically justified approach for maximizing the recovery of bioactive compounds from P. emblica. Antioxidant activity

Antioxidant potential of P. emblica was strongly correlated with its phytochemical content, indicating that polyphenols are the principal contributors to the downregulation of reactive oxygen species (Table 2). The methanol extract of the freeze-dried sample showed the highest FRAP value (35.7 ± 0.9 µmol Fe2+/g) and inhibition of stable free radical, i.e., DPPH, with IC50 of 208.2 µg/mL. The increase in activity is attributed to the preservation of heat-labile phytochemicals by freeze-drying, followed by ultrasonic extraction at 20 °C, which are otherwise prone to degradation during conventional sun-drying. The findings of the current study are consistent with previous reports that have outlined the superiority of freeze-drying for preserving bioactive integrity. In this context, Chen et al. [50] found that freeze-dried P. emblica extracts prepared by sonication exhibited notable antioxidant activity in DPPH, FRAP, and H2O2 assays. Similarly, Chauhan et al. [51] observed that the freeze-dried slurry of Daucus carota, Beta vulgaris, P. emblica, and Lycopersicon esculentum exhibits more phytochemical contents and antioxidant activity in the DPPH assay (77 % inhibition) than the sun-dried sample, which exhibited 40 % inhibition. In another study, freeze-dried juice powders of Psidium guajava, P. emblica, and Syzygium cumini were reported to have higher vitamin C, phenolic, and antioxidant content and to be more acceptable in sensory evaluation [52]. Moreover, published research indicated that freeze-dried P. emblica powder exhibited antioxidant activity in the DPPH assay, comparable to that of standard ascorbic acid [53]. Moreover, sonication-assisted extraction also enhanced activity by promoting efficient cell disruption and solvent penetration. Irfan et al. [54] demonstrated that the 70 % ethanol extract of Cymbopogon citratus obtained via sonication exhibits greater radical-scavenging activity against DPPH (73 % inhibition) than that obtained via maceration (65 % inhibition). Likewise, a sesame cake extract obtained with methanol using sonication exhibited greater antioxidant activity in DPPH and FRAP assays than the methanol extract obtained via conventional maceration [55].

Anti-inflammatory activity

In vitro anti-inflammatory potential of P. emblica extracts was evaluated using protein denaturation (egg and bovine serum albumin) and cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibition assays. Protein denaturation contributes to inflammation; numerous studies have validated this relationship [56], [57]. Our findings showed that the methanol extract of the freeze-dried powder inhibited the denaturation of egg albumin (62.6 ± 1.01 %) and bovine serum albumin (59.5 ± 0.8 %), outperforming diclofenac sodium (91.2–93 %) and the ethanol extract (54.2–57.6 %). In this regard, previous studies have also reported that P. emblica extracts protect against protein denaturation due to their phytochemical content [[58], [59], [60]]. The ability of commercial drugs/plant extracts to prevent or manage inflammation arises from their potential to modify the cell-surface charges of erythrocyte membranes and to exert antioxidant effects that prevent red blood cell aggregation [56], [57], [58]. On the other hand, sun-dried extracts showed very low activity (≤31.3 %), likely due to the possible degradation of activity-bearing compounds during thermal drying and high-temperature extraction, thereby indicating a direct relationship between phytochemical content and antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity.

Cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibition assays provided further insight into the mechanism of action. COX-1 is believed to be important in maintaining normal physiological functions, whereas COX-2 is inducible and is activated in inflammatory conditions. COX-1 inhibition is generally avoided, whereas COX-2 inhibition can help treat inflammation and neurological disorders such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases [61], [62]. Our findings indicate that the methanol extract of freeze-dried P. emblica is more effective at inhibiting COX-2 (IC50 46.7 µg/mL) than COX-1 (IC50 68.2 µg/mL) and is believed to be associated with fewer gastrointestinal side effects. In addition to ethanol extract, it also substantially inhibited both COX isoforms (IC50 values of 90.3 µg/mL for COX-1 and 74.2 µg/mL for COX-2). These results imply that the anti-inflammatory activity of P. emblica is mediated by COX-2 downregulation. In contrast, the sun-dried P. emblica extracts showed limited COX-2 inhibition, highlighting the effects of extraction and drying on bioactive compound retention. Previous in vitro and cellular studies further support these results, demonstrating that P. emblica extracts downregulate COX-2 expression and associated inflammatory mediators, such as NF-κB and iNOS [63]. Antimicrobial effects

The antimicrobial activity of P. emblica extracts (25 µg/mL) obtained by methods 1 and 2 was assessed against selected fungal and bacterial strains using the well-diffusion method (Table 4). Results revealed substantial variation in activity, relying on both drying method and extraction techniques. Our findings indicated that the methanol extract (ultrasound-assisted extraction) of freeze-dried sample produced larger zones of inhibition (ZOIs) against Klebsiella oxytoca (9.2 mm), Citrobacter (8.3 mm), and Escherichia coli (9.8 mm) than sun-dried counterparts (orbital shaker extraction), but lower than the standard ciprofloxacin (5 µg/mL). This suggests that the combination of low-temperature drying and ultrasound-assisted extraction more effectively preserves and retains antimicrobial compounds. The potent antimicrobial activity observed in our study is consistent with previous work. In this respect, Chaunde and colleagues [64] tested P. emblica fruit juice against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli and observed notable activity with ZOIs of 19.7 mm and 18.81 mm, respectively. Likewise, Tiwana et al. (2025) reported large ZOIs (up to 15 mm) for P. emblica methanol and aqueous extracts against Staphylococcus aureus and its methicillin-resistant variant (MRSA) [65]. The susceptibility of Staphylococcus aureus to P. emblica is a consistent observation, as Javale and Sabnis (2010) also found to be the most susceptible bacterium among the respiratory pathogens tested [66]. Moreover, our results with the methanol extract further strengthen this notion, suggesting a particularly potent effect against Gram-positive bacteria. On the other hand, Tiwana et al. (2025) found that P. emblica extracts are less active against Gram-negative bacteria such as E. coli, which is a crucial point of comparison [65]. Similarly, Chaunde and coworkers reported a lower ZOI (18.81 mm) against E. coli than against S. aureus (19.7 mm) [64]. The resistance may be due to the complex outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, which acts as a permeability barrier. On the other hand, the methanol extract of freeze-dried P. emblica at 25 µg/mL exhibited strong antifungal activity against Candida albicans (13.1 mm ZOI) and Candida glabrata (11.7 mm ZOI), greater than that of the standard drug, fluconazole, at 5 µg/mL. Published research found that the methanol extract of P. emblica fruit exhibits a similar activity of 14.3 mm against C. albicans [67], whereas the methanol extract of P. emblica seed tested against C. albicans resulted in a ZOI of 13 mm, followed by a C. glabrata ZOI of 10.7 mm [68].

Bioassay-guided fractionation and ESI-MS/MS analysis

Our findings indicated that the methanol extract of freeze-dried P. emblica was the most effective among the extracts and the sun-dried counterparts. This extract was subjected to liquid–liquid partitioning (LLP) using ethyl acetate and chloroform. LLP led to 3 fractions based on polarity: chloroform (A), ethyl acetate (B), and water (C). All fractions were reevaluated; only fraction B exhibited slightly greater antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial activity than the parent methanol extract, with a slight decline in antifungal activity. A decline in biological activity was recorded for the other two fractions, A and C (Supplementary Table S1). When fraction B was further subjected to reversed-phase HPLC (RP-HPLC), it afforded 4 more sub-fractions that exhibited reduced activity as compared to the LLP fraction B and the parent methanol extract. As a result, the methanol extract of LLP fraction B was subjected to ESI-MS/MS analysis to identify activity-bearing compounds, as shown in Table 5. Identification of the compounds was considered tentative based on their fragmentation patterns and retention behavior, compared with published literature, in the absence of a reference standard.

Research findings indicate that these phytochemical constituents exhibit notable biological activities. For example, ellagic acid mitigates oxidative stress by stimulating the body's endogenous antioxidant system [73] and reducing DNA damage induced by both H2O2 and bleomycin [74]. It also exerts anti-inflammatory effects by reducing IL-1β/IL-6 and boosting fibroblast function [75]. It also exhibits antimicrobial potential by protecting against gastric epithelial damage caused by H. pylori infection [76]. Similarly, gallic acid, present in a wide range of plants [77], is a well-known antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial agent [78], [79]. Gallic acid significantly mitigates the inflammatory response in myometrial cells by reducing IL1B-induced PTGS2 mRNA expression and PGF2α release [80].

Quinic acid exhibits anti-inflammatory activity in addition to its role as a transition metal chelator [81]. It hyperpolarizes the S. aureus cell membrane, as evidenced by reduced fluorescent intensity [82]. In contrast, chlorogenic acid downregulates carrageenan-induced oxidative stress and inflammatory reactions by inhibiting pro-inflammatory mediators [83]. Likewise, the antibacterial activity of chlorogenic acid is mediated by its membrane-disrupting properties [84]. Moreover, caffeic acid was reported to exert antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects in a rat model [85] and to inhibit Candida albicans biofilm formation [86]. Published reports on myricetin highlight its biological activities, including antioxidant, antiviral, anticancer, and antibacterial [87], [88]. Wang and coworkers [89] showed that myricetin exhibits significant anti-inflammatory activity via reducing ear edema induced by xylene and hind paw edema induced by carrageenan. Importantly, the freeze-dried methanol extract fraction B showed greater biological activity than the other fractions and is consistent with commercial drugs, suggesting the presence of synergistically active phytoconstituents. However, upon further RP-HPLC partitioning, the activity declined, suggesting that the combined effects of multiple compounds were responsible for the enhanced bioactivity observed in the crude fraction.

Previous research has reported similar synergistic interactions among phenolics, flavonoids, alkaloids, and tannins. Crude or semi-purified fractions demonstrated enhanced biological efficacy compared to individual isolated compounds [88], [89], [90]. However, the observed effects are preliminary evidence of synergy at the extract level rather than definitive confirmation. and future studies involving fraction recombination, compound isolation, and mechanistic evaluations are warranted to further characterize these interactions.

Functional tea potential of P. emblica

A freeze-dried P. emblica powder sachet (2 g) was brewed for 1 min at 95 °C to make tea, then concentrated using a rotary evaporator to assess the stability of the phytochemical content and the retention of biological potential. Results revealed that P. emblica tea extract exhibits higher TPC and TTC but lower TFC than the freeze-dried methanol extract, indicating that flavonoids are thermally unstable during brewing. The comparison between concentrated extracts and direct tea infusion revealed a marked reduction in both antioxidant and antimicrobial activities in the functional tea (Table 6). For instance, DPPH inhibition decreased from 53.3 % in the concentrated extract to 29.4 % in the infusion, indicating dilution of bioactive compounds under typical consumption conditions. Similarly, antimicrobial activity was reduced across most tested strains, with no inhibition observed against E. coli and Candida species, which showed smaller inhibition zones. Notably, Citrobacter spp. retained comparable inhibition (ZOI of 7.3 mm), suggesting that certain bioactive components remain effective even at lower concentrations. The results suggest that although the functional tea exhibits moderate activity when consumed, its effects may be cumulative or supportive rather than therapeutic. Further mass spectrometry analysis revealed the presence of all phenolic acids listed in Table 5, but flavonoids were not detected or exhibited only weak relative signal intensity. However, analysis suggests that P. emblica powder can be brewed into tea, but further studies comparing these results with commercially available teas are recommended to better contextualize the functional efficacy.

Sensory evaluation of functional tea formulations

Table 7 describes how three distinct P. emblica tea formulations were prepared. Each formulation (2 g sachet) was brewed for 1 min at 95 °C to assess the impact of P. emblica concentration on sensory acceptability and flavor attributes. T0 (control; 100 % P. emblica tea) was noted for its notable phytochemical potential but very low acceptability due to a strong astringent flavor, bitterness, and dark color. The blend of 75 % P. emblica and 25 % green tea (T1) indicated a slight improvement in visual appearance; however, residual bitterness limited its acceptability. The P. emblica /green tea equimolar mixture (T2) provided a relatively balanced flavor profile with moderate bitterness and intermediate sensory scores, but it did not achieve the highest acceptability. In contrast, a blend of 25 % P. emblica and 75 % green tea (T3) exhibited the most acceptable sensory profile, including mild astringency, acceptable color, and a pleasant aroma, resulting in the overall acceptability score comparable to T2 but higher than T0 and T1 on the 9-point hedonic scale. These findings suggest that blending P. emblica with green tea may effectively reduce the dark color and bitterness while maintaining a notable level of bioactive compounds, thereby enabling formulation optimization to align with consumer sensory preferences. However, the findings should be interpreted with caution due to the limited panel size and demographic imbalance, and further validation with a larger, more diverse consumer panel is recommended.

Conclusions

In summary, the present study establishes that P. emblica is a multifunctional botanical resource with significant potential for managing inflammatory conditions, such as urinary tract infections. Sample preparation and processing strategies were found to critically influence extract quality, phytochemical integrity, and biological efficacy, with freeze drying combined with ultrasound-assisted extraction emerging as an effective approach to preserve heat-labile bioactive compounds and enhance extract performance. The observed antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties are attributed to the synergistic action of the polyphenolic matrix, rather than to isolated compounds. These results highlight the importance of phytochemical synergy in clinical contexts involving simultaneous oxidative stress, infection, and inflammation. Furthermore, higher COX-2 inhibition than COX1, in combination with antimicrobial activity, may help mitigate symptom severity and potentially reduce dependence on conventional antibiotic therapies. Moreover, the development of P. emblica-based functional tea, particularly when blended with green tea, highlights its translational feasibility as a consumer-acceptable functional beverage with retained bioactivity. Future research should prioritize in vivo validation, mechanistic elucidation, and standardization of extraction and formulation protocols to facilitate clinical translation and nutraceutical development.
