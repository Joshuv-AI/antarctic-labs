Knowledge and awareness of epilepsy management, etiologies, and comorbidities among pediatricians in Palestine
Authors: Azzam Zrineh, Adham Shalalfeh, Renad Demah, Bilal Aqel, Kaser Abbas, Jaser Amro, Hamzah Sowan, Sherien Alsaid, Walaa Altamimi, Shahd Qadous, Nizar Tarawah, Abdallah Alwawi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpilepsy is one of the most prevalent neurological conditions in pediatric populations, and its management extends beyond seizure control to encompass comorbidities, psychosocial well-being, and long-term care. In the West Bank, Palestine, limited availability of pediatric neurologists means that general pediatricians often serve as the primary point of contact for children with epilepsy. Despite this critical role, no study has comprehensively assessed epilepsy knowledge among pediatricians in this setting. This study aimed to evaluate the knowledge and awareness of epilepsy management, etiologies, and comorbidities among pediatricians practicing in the West Bank.MethodsA descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted among 246 pediatric residents, general pediatricians, and pediatric subspecialists from November 2025 to March 2026. A validated questionnaire covering five knowledge domains (etiology, long-term management, comorbidities, quality of life, and first-aid management) was administered. Responses were classified as correct or incorrect, and composite scores were calculated for each domain. Group comparisons were performed using Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests, with post-hoc pairwise comparisons where applicable.ResultsThe overall aggregate correct response rate was 71.3%, with a median total score of 18 out of 25. Respondents performed best in first-aid management (81.6%) and long-term management (76.5%), while the lowest scores were observed in quality of life (55.2%) and comorbidities (67.7%). Notable gaps included low recognition of social difficulties (33.7%), increased aggression (34.1%), and comorbid hyperactivity (46.3%) in children with epilepsy, as well as incomplete awareness of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy, recognized by only 62.6% of respondents. Kruskal-Wallis tests revealed significant differences by professional role for etiology (p < 0.001), long-term management (p = 0.019), and total score (p = 0.021). Age and medical school location showed no significant associations with knowledge scores, while gender was associated with a significant difference only in the quality of life domain (p = 0.020).ConclusionsPalestinian pediatricians demonstrate moderate overall epilepsy knowledge but exhibit clinically meaningful gaps in recognizing psychosocial consequences and comorbidities. These findings highlight the need for targeted continuing medical education programs and curricular reforms in pediatric residency training, with emphasis on the psychosocial dimensions and comorbidity burden of childhood epilepsy.

Background

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by an enduring predisposition to seizures, affecting approximately 50 million people worldwide [1, 2]. It is one of the most prevalent neurological conditions in pediatric populations. A recent systematic analysis of the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 reported 18.15 million prevalent cases of epilepsy in children and adolescents globally, with a prevalence rate of 688.43 per 100,000 population, underscoring the substantial burden this condition places on pediatric healthcare systems worldwide [3]. Across Arab countries, epidemiological research on epilepsy remains limited. Nevertheless, available evidence indicates that the prevalence of epilepsy in the Arab region, including Palestine, is approximately 6.9 per 1,000 individuals [4].

Epilepsy arises from multiple possible causes, including metabolic, genetic, structural, infectious, immune, and unknown origins, and individuals may have more than one contributing etiology [5]. Beyond its neurological manifestations, epilepsy is frequently accompanied by a range of psychiatric and behavioral comorbidities that compound its clinical burden, particularly in pediatric populations [6–8]. These include attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, autism spectrum disorders, depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbances [9–12]. These comorbidities are frequently underrecognized in clinical practice, especially in resource-limited settings where access to neuropsychiatric services is constrained.

Moreover, a large proportion of people with epilepsy live in low- and middle-income countries, where restricted availability of diagnostic and therapeutic services leads to a substantial treatment gap. Many individuals in such settings remain undertreated or untreated due to shortages in healthcare infrastructure, limited availability of neurology and epilepsy-specific services, economic constraints, cultural beliefs, social stigma, and reliance on traditional treatments [1, 13].

The Palestinian healthcare system faces unique challenges, including limited resources, geographical fragmentation, restricted access to specialized care, and ongoing political instability [14–16]. Healthcare facilities are distributed unevenly across the West Bank, with specialized pediatric neurology services concentrated in major cities. To our knowledge, there are currently no fellowship-trained pediatric epileptologists practicing in the West Bank. Pediatric neurology services are provided by a small number of general pediatric neurologists, and there are no dedicated pediatric epilepsy centers or epilepsy monitoring units in the territory. Consequently, children with epilepsy often receive initial care from general pediatricians rather than specialists. This makes it essential that pediatricians possess adequate knowledge about epilepsy to provide appropriate initial management, recognize complications, educate families, and make timely referrals when necessary.

Several studies have examined epilepsy knowledge among healthcare providers globally [17–20], all highlighting variable levels of knowledge gaps in specific domains. A recent study by Abu Libdeh et al. [21] assessed epilepsy knowledge among 168 pediatricians in Jordan. The study revealed important knowledge gaps, especially regarding comorbidities and long-term management. Given the similarities in healthcare systems, cultural contexts, and training backgrounds between Jordan and Palestine, adapting their validated questionnaire provided an excellent opportunity to assess Palestinian pediatricians’ knowledge and compare findings.

To the best of our knowledge, no study has comprehensively assessed epilepsy knowledge among pediatricians in Palestine. This study, therefore, aimed to evaluate the overall knowledge and awareness of epilepsy management, etiologies, and comorbidities among pediatric residents and pediatricians practicing in the West Bank, Palestine, and to identify specific knowledge gaps that may inform targeted educational interventions.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a descriptive, cross-sectional, questionnaire-based study conducted among pediatricians and pediatric residents practicing across the West Bank, Palestine, from 14 November 2025 to 30 March 2026. Ethical approval was obtained from the Al-Quds University Ethical Committee prior to data collection (Ref No: 631/REC/2025). Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and informed consent was obtained before accessing the questionnaire.

Study population and sampling

The target population included all pediatric residents across all training years, general pediatricians, and pediatric subspecialists (excluding pediatric neurologists) currently practicing in the West Bank. A convenience sampling technique was mainly employed, which, while limiting generalizability to the broader population, is widely utilized in clinical settings where comprehensive sampling frames are unavailable and probability-based approaches are impractical [22]. To enhance representativeness, multiple recruitment strategies were used, including direct visits to hospitals and clinics across diverse geographic regions of the West Bank, digital recruitment through professional social media groups, and snowball sampling through participant referrals.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated using the OpenEpi online calculator [23] for a cross-sectional prevalence study with a finite population. The following parameters were specified: an anticipated proportion of adequate knowledge of 50% (conservative estimate), a margin of error of 5%, a confidence level of 95%, and an estimated population size of 500 practicing pediatricians in the West Bank based on expert consultation. The minimum required sample size was calculated as 218 participants. The final sample of 246 respondents exceeded this requirement, providing adequate statistical power for this study.

Instrument

This study used a questionnaire developed and validated by Abu Libdeh et al. [21] in the Jordanian context. Permission was obtained from the original authors for adaptation to the Palestinian setting. Only minor contextual modifications were made, including adapting geographic references and practice settings to reflect our Palestinian healthcare structure. The questionnaire was administered in English via Google Forms.

The instrument comprised six categories: demographics, causes of epilepsy (3 items), long-term management of epilepsy (5 items), comorbidities (6 items), quality of life (4 items), and first-aid management of seizures (7 items). Knowledge items (25 total) were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree”. Prior to data collection, a pilot study was conducted among 15 pediatricians (residents, general pediatricians, and subspecialists) not included in the final sample, who provided feedback on item clarity, relevance to Palestinian practice, and feasibility of completion. In our sample, the adapted questionnaire demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.707 for the full scale.

Statistical analysis

Consistent with the methodology of Abu Libdeh et al. [21], questionnaire responses were classified as correct if the response was one of the two Likert-scale choices in agreement with the correct answer, or incorrect if the respondent chose “neutral” or one of the two choices not in agreement with the correct answer. A binary variable (1 = correct, 0 = incorrect) was computed for each of the 25 knowledge items, and composite scores were calculated for each domain and for the total questionnaire.

The normality of knowledge score distributions was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. All scores significantly deviated from normality (p < 0.05), confirmed by visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Therefore, non-parametric tests were used for all group comparisons. Descriptive statistics were computed using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, while medians and interquartile ranges were computed for continuous variables. For inferential analysis, Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to compare knowledge scores across three or more groups, with Dwass-Steel-Critchlow-Fligner (DSCF) post-hoc pairwise comparisons where appropriate. Mann-Whitney U tests were used for two-group comparisons. All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. Analyses were performed using Jamovi Software (version 2.3).

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 246 pediatricians completed the questionnaire. The median age was 31 years (IQR: 28–40), with the majority (62.6%, n = 154) aged 25–35 years. Males constituted 58.9% (n = 145) of respondents. Most participants studied medicine in Palestine (57.7%, n = 142) and were trained or currently training in pediatrics within the country (89%, n = 219). Regarding practice setting, 43.1% (n = 106) worked in public Ministry of Health facilities and 41.9% (n = 103) in private settings. Nearly half (47.6%, n = 117) were general pediatricians, 40.2% (n = 99) were pediatric residents, and 12.2% (n = 30) were pediatric subspecialists. The vast majority (91.5%, n = 225) reported encountering patients with epilepsy in their practice. Table 1 summarizes the demographic and professional characteristics of all participants.

Overall knowledge scores

The overall aggregate correct response rate was 71.3%. The median total knowledge score was 18 out of 25 (IQR = 4). Performance varied across the five knowledge domains. Respondents scored highest in first-aid management (81.6% correct, median 6, IQR = 2), followed by long-term management (76.5%, median 4, IQR = 2), comorbidities (67.7%, median 4, IQR = 2), etiology (67.3%, median 2, IQR = 2), and quality of life (55.2%, median 2, IQR = 1). Individual question analysis

Table 3 presents the correct response rate for each of the 25 individual knowledge items. Within the etiology domain, 87% of respondents correctly identified that epilepsy is not a contagious disease, but only 52% correctly disagreed that epilepsy is caused by stress. In the long-term management domain, 80.5% appropriately rejected the notion that anti-seizure medications can be immediately withdrawn upon achieving seizure freedom, and 80.1% recognized that some children with epilepsy benefit from special diets.

The comorbidities domain revealed some gaps, as only 46.3% of respondents recognized that hyperactivity is more common in children with epilepsy (CWE), and awareness of the risk of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) was present in only 62.6%. The quality of life domain had the most pronounced deficiencies, as merely 33.7% agreed that CWE may have difficulty making friends and maintaining relationships, and only 34.1% recognized increased aggression in patients with epilepsy. First-aid management items had uniformly high correct response rates, with the notable exceptions of “do not stand away from the patient” (50.8%) and “do not put a metallic object in their hand” (78.5%).

Knowledge scores comparisons

Pediatric subspecialists demonstrated higher knowledge scores compared to general pediatricians and residents. Table 4 presents the domain-level descriptive statistics and results of Kruskal–Wallis comparisons by current role. Significant differences were found for etiology (p < 0.001), long-term management (p = 0.019), and total score (p = 0.021), while no significant differences were observed for comorbidities (p = 0.293), quality of life (p = 0.849), or first-aid management (p = 0.735).

Post-hoc pairwise comparisons using the Dwass-Steel-Critchlow-Fligner (DSCF) test revealed that for the etiology domain, subspecialists scored significantly higher than both residents (p < 0.001) and general pediatricians (p = 0.002), while the latter two groups did not differ from each other (p = 0.577). For long-term management, subspecialists scored significantly higher than residents (p = 0.013). For total score, subspecialists scored significantly higher than residents (p = 0.015), with a non-significant difference when compared to general pediatricians (p = 0.111). In all three domains, residents and general pediatricians performed comparably (all p > 0.46).

Furthermore, age group showed no significant association with knowledge scores across any domain or in total (all p > 0.2). Gender was associated with a significant difference only in the quality of life domain, where males scored higher than females (p = 0.020), but no gender differences were found in any other domain or in total scores (p = 0.646). Medical school location (Palestine vs. abroad) was not significantly associated with knowledge in any domain (all p > 0.14).

Discussion

This study comprehensively assessed the knowledge and awareness of epilepsy management, etiologies, and comorbidities among pediatricians in the West Bank, Palestine. Our findings revealed that Palestinian pediatricians possess a moderate overall aggregate correct response rate (71.3%), comparable to scores reported in a methodologically similar study among Jordanian pediatricians using the same instrument [21], with significant and clinically meaningful gaps concentrated in the quality of life and comorbidities domains. Our findings carry important implications for medical education, clinical practice, and healthcare policy in the Palestinian context, where pediatricians often serve as the primary and sometimes only point of medical contact for children with epilepsy due to the scarcity of pediatric neurologists.

Our results are broadly consistent with, yet show some notable differences from, the findings of Abu Libdeh et al. [21] from neighboring Jordan. The Jordanian study reported overall correct response rates of 73% for etiology, 64.5% for long-term management, 58.2% for comorbidities, 62.5% for quality of life, and 88% for first-aid management. Palestinian pediatricians performed comparably or better in long-term management (76.5% vs. 64.5%) and comorbidities (67.7% vs. 58.2%), while performing slightly lower in first-aid management (81.6% vs. 88%) and etiology (67.3% vs. 73%). Both populations demonstrated the strongest performance in first-aid management and the weakest in quality of life and comorbidities, suggesting shared regional patterns in medical education and similar curriculum deficiencies regarding the psychosocial dimensions of epilepsy. These parallel findings across two countries with comparable healthcare structures strengthen the argument for a coordinated regional approach to addressing these knowledge gaps. It should be noted, however, that the two samples differed in their professional composition. The Jordanian sample comprised 41.7% residents, 29.8% general pediatricians, and 28.6% subspecialists, whereas our sample included 40.2% residents, 47.6% general pediatricians, and 12.2% subspecialists. The higher proportion of subspecialists in the Jordanian sample may partly account for some of the observed differences in domain scores between the two studies, and direct comparisons should therefore be interpreted with caution.

The relatively strong performance in first-aid management (81.6%) is reassuring and suggests that Palestinian pediatricians are generally well prepared to manage acute seizure presentations, a clinical scenario they frequently encounter in practice, as confirmed by the 91.5% who reported seeing epilepsy patients. However, persistent misconceptions were identified, as 49.2% of respondents incorrectly agreed that one should stand away from a seizing patient, and 21.5% endorsed placing a metallic object in the patient’s hand during a seizure. These misconceptions may reflect cultural beliefs prevalent in the region, though this was not directly assessed in the present study. Regardless of their origin, they warrant targeted correction through continuing education programs and clinical training workshops.

The most concerning knowledge gaps were identified in the quality of life domain, which had the lowest correct response rate of all five domains (55.2%). Only 33.7% of respondents recognized that children with epilepsy may have difficulty making friends and maintaining relationships, and only 34.1% acknowledged increased aggression in patients with epilepsy. These figures closely mirror the Jordanian findings [21] and suggest a widespread underappreciation of the psychosocial impact of epilepsy among pediatricians in the region.

Epilepsy can markedly affect children’s psychological and social development because of persistent stigma and long-standing cultural misconceptions, which contribute to bullying, discrimination, and social exclusion in school environments [24, 25]. In many low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), the stigma surrounding epilepsy is reflected in labelled stereotypes, such as assumptions that children with epilepsy are intellectually disabled or infectious, and in the devaluation or discrimination of these children as different from their peers [26]. Caregivers can also become targets of stigma when seeking care at recognized epilepsy centers or when blamed for their child’s condition [26]. Given that pediatricians serve as trusted sources of information for families, this knowledge deficit may limit their ability to provide comprehensive counseling and appropriate referrals for psychosocial support.

Importantly, psychosocial support should be regarded as an integral component of comprehensive epilepsy care rather than a secondary consideration. The recognition of behavioral, cognitive, educational, and social challenges in children with epilepsy is essential not only for holistic patient management but also for empowering families with realistic expectations and appropriate coping strategies.

The comorbidities domain also revealed important gaps. Awareness of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) was present in only 62.6% of respondents, meaning that more than one-third of pediatricians were unaware of this critical risk. While this figure is somewhat better than the 58.9% reported in Jordan [21], it remains inadequate given the clinical importance of SUDEP counseling. A survey by Berl et al. [27] found that 75.3% of pediatric care providers were unaware of SUDEP in children with epilepsy, often assuming that neurologists bear sole responsibility for educating families. Therefore, our findings reinforce the need for integrating SUDEP awareness into general pediatric education rather than treating it as an exclusively subspecialty concern. Additionally, only 46.3% of respondents recognized that hyperactivity is more common in children with epilepsy, despite the well-established association between epilepsy and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder reported in the literature [28]. This gap in awareness may contribute to underdiagnosis and undertreatment of comorbid ADHD in children with epilepsy, potentially compounding the negative impact on academic performance and social functioning. This underscores the importance of incorporating routine screening for ADHD and other neurobehavioral comorbidities into standard epilepsy care, even in settings where pediatric neuropsychiatric resources are limited.

Within the etiology domain, while most respondents correctly identified that epilepsy is neither contagious (87%) nor purely psychological (63%), a striking 48% of respondents did not correctly disagree that stress causes epilepsy. Of these, our data showed that 22% actively endorsed the misconception (agreed or strongly agreed), while 26% selected a neutral response, indicating uncertainty rather than firm endorsement. Although these represent meaningfully different clinical risk profiles, the former reflecting a held misconception and the latter reflecting a knowledge gap, both warrant educational attention, as uncertainty may lead to inconsistent or inaccurate counseling of families. This misconception, which was also prevalent (48.8%) in the Jordanian sample [21], is noteworthy because it may lead pediatricians to provide inaccurate explanations to families regarding disease etiology, potentially reinforcing popular misconceptions and contributing to stigma. While stress may serve as a seizure trigger in some patients [29, 30], it is not an established cause of epilepsy as a disease entity, and this distinction must be clearly communicated in clinical education.

The findings of our study carry several practical implications. First, continuing medical education programs in Palestine should specifically target the identified knowledge gaps, especially in comorbidities and quality of life. Evidence from other settings is encouraging; a study from Bolivia demonstrated that even brief training modules of two days each can significantly improve knowledge and confidence in managing epilepsy [31]. Second, pediatric residency curricula across Palestinian training programs should be revised to incorporate structured, progressive epilepsy education throughout all training years, with emphasis on the psychosocial impact and comorbidity burden of epilepsy rather than focusing exclusively on acute seizure management. Third, given the parallel findings between Palestine and Jordan, collaborative regional educational initiatives may offer an efficient and culturally appropriate approach to addressing these shared knowledge gaps. Fourth, partnerships with established international epilepsy centers could offer practical and cost-effective strategies to reduce knowledge gaps and strengthen epilepsy care capacity. Virtual case conferences, tele-education programs, online continuing medical education activities, and collaborative training initiatives represent feasible approaches that can transcend the geographic and political barriers that limit traditional educational exchanges in the Palestinian context. Such initiatives could benefit pediatricians practicing not only in the West Bank but also in Gaza, where access to specialized neurological services is even more constrained.

A statistically significant gender difference was observed in the quality of life domain, where male pediatricians scored higher than females (p = 0.020). However, this was an isolated finding that was not replicated in any other domain or in total scores (p = 0.646). Given that multiple comparisons were performed across demographic variables and knowledge domains without formal correction for multiplicity, this isolated result is most likely attributable to chance. We therefore consider it of uncertain clinical significance, and future studies with a priori hypotheses regarding gender differences in epilepsy knowledge would be needed to determine whether this finding is reproducible.

This study has some notable strengths. It is the first to comprehensively assess epilepsy knowledge among pediatricians in Palestine, addressing an important gap in the regional literature. The use of a validated instrument adapted from a neighboring country with a comparable healthcare context enables direct cross-national comparisons. Furthermore, participants were recruited from multiple regions, enhancing geographical representativeness. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the convenience sampling approach may have introduced selection bias. Pediatricians who volunteered for an epilepsy knowledge survey are plausibly those with greater interest in or exposure to epilepsy, which would systematically inflate the observed scores relative to the true population level. Consequently, the knowledge gaps identified in this study may be underestimates of the actual deficiencies among the broader pediatrician population in the West Bank. Second, the Likert-scale questionnaire format with a neutral midpoint leaves room for guessing and social desirability bias. Additionally, the classification of neutral responses as incorrect, while consistent with the methodology of the reference study [21], does not distinguish between respondents who actively endorsed an incorrect belief and those who were merely uncertain. This distinction is clinically meaningful, as uncertainty may be more amenable to educational correction than firmly held misconceptions. The quality of life domain, which had the lowest correct response rate (55.2%) and the narrowest IQR (IQR = 1), may have been particularly affected by a high proportion of neutral responses, and the observed deficit in this domain may partly reflect uncertainty rather than outright misinformation. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences.

Conclusions

This study assessed epilepsy knowledge among pediatricians in Palestine, revealing a moderate overall level of knowledge but with clinically significant gaps concentrated in the quality of life and comorbidities domains. While Palestinian pediatricians demonstrated reassuring competence in first-aid seizure management, the low correct response rates observed for items related to psychosocial impact, comorbid behavioral conditions such as ADHD, and sudden unexpected death in epilepsy warrant attention in the context of continuing medical education and clinical training, particularly given that pediatric neurologists are scarce in the West Bank. Persistent misconceptions regarding epilepsy etiology, notably the attribution of epilepsy to stress, further underscore the need for corrective educational efforts. These findings, which closely parallel those reported in neighboring Jordan, call for the development of targeted continuing medical education programs and the integration of structured epilepsy education into pediatric residency curricula across Palestine. Collaborative regional educational initiatives between Palestine and neighboring countries with comparable healthcare structures may also offer an efficient strategy to address these knowledge deficiencies.
