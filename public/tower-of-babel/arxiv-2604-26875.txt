Dynamics of East Atlantic seed vortex populations in global km–scale models


Abstract
Africa is the primary source of cyclonic vortices over the tropical Atlantic. Over both land and sea, these vortices are entwined with deep convective activity, with the majority being African Easterly Wave troughs. Their convective interactions have downstream impacts, since the same vortices provide the seed population for Atlantic basin tropical cyclone (TC) genesis. Understanding the dynamics of East Atlantic seed populations, particularly the processes that distinguish vortices which undergo cyclogenesis, is crucial for understanding the formation of Atlantic hurricanes and model representations of their populations. Here we investigate these questions in three one–year, atmosphere–only global km–scale Met Office Unified Model simulations. We use objective tracking algorithms to independently identify seed vortices, easterly waves, TCs, and Mesoscale Convective Systems (MCSs), benchmarking against reanalysis and satellite–derived climatologies. Despite the simulations displaying comparable continental vortex populations, we show that the highest–resolution simulation with explicit convection produces fewer, weaker hurricanes than coarser, parameterised counterparts due to a failure to amplify vortices crossing the West African coastline. We identify a failure to maintain strong top–heavy mass flux profiles experienced by seeds as the primary cause, demonstrating profiles’ roles in low–level circulation development through vortex stretching. Using MCS tracks, we show that systematic differences in convective organisation between the simulations can explain the differences in mass flux profiles, and thus vortex evolution. Deficiencies in the explicit simulation stem from underestimation of MCS stratiform components, a bias shared with other explicit convection models; and a latitudinal offset between offshore seed vortex and MCS trains.

keypoints

A global km–scale, explicit convection model generates fewer, weaker Atlantic tropical cyclones than coarser, parameterised counterparts.

Plain Language Summary

Tropical cyclones typically form from pre-existing atmospheric disturbances named seed vortices. These seeds are mesoscale regions of relatively weak rotation; tropical cyclones form from a seed when environmental factors or local interactions act to develop a strong, near–surface circulation with rapidly rotating winds. For North Atlantic hurricanes, most seed vortices are troughs of African Easterly Waves, mid-level vortices generated within the West African Monsoon which propagate offshore from continental Africa. The evolution of East Atlantic seed vortices, and their propensity to develop into hurricanes, is strongly influenced by atmospheric moisture and interactions with large, but sub–vortex scale, organised deep convective systems. In this paper we study the processes which control seeds’ evolution in new, year–long global models run with a km–scale grid representing the atmosphere. In a simulation where deep convection is explicitly represented, we find that the offshore growth of seed vortex circulations is much weaker than in reanalysis climatology and simulations which parameterise (approximate) convective motion. There are subsequently fewer, weaker Atlantic hurricanes in the explicit simulation. We show that offshore biases in column moisture, and the size, intensity and relative location of organised convective systems, are a key cause of the differences in seed vortex evolution between simulations.

1
Introduction

Tropical cyclogenesis depends on large–scale environmental conditions and the availability of precursor seed vortices. Seeds are pre–existing compact regions of atmospheric vorticity, typically without the canonical full depth warm–core, symmetric structure of a tropical cyclone (TC;
Wang et al. (2025)
). In the North Atlantic (NATL) and East North Pacific (ENP) basins, the majority of available seeds originate over or near Africa: approximately 60% of NATL TCs, and 50% of ENP TCs, are associated with African Easterly Waves (AEWs;
Russell et al. (2017)
;
Du et al. (2025)
).

AEWs are synoptic–scale waves that propagate in the mid/lower troposphere across central and western North Africa. The waves exist as barotropic–baroclinic disturbances of the African Easterly Jet (AEJ) associated with a reversal in the meridional potential vorticity (PV) gradient
Pytharoulis & Thorncroft (1999)
;
J-S. Hsieh & Cook (2007)
. The AEJ is a fundamental component of the West African Monsoon (WAM) circulation generated through a thermal wind response to the large–scale meridional temperature gradient between the dry, hot Sahara and moist Guinea coast
Cook (1999)
. AEWs propagate in a northern and southern track relative to the AEJ, at roughly 850 and 650 hPa respectively
Thorncroft & Hodges (2001)
. The southern track AEWs in particular are intimately coupled to organised moist, deep convection
Mekonnen et al. (2006)
;
Semunegus et al. (2017)
. Mesoscale Convective Systems (MCSs) act as significant mid–level diabatic heating and potential vorticity (PV) sources, thereby acting as the primary source for AEW growth
Russell & Aiyyer (2020)
and playing an intrinsic role in AEW maintenance
Berry & Thorncroft (2012)
;
Tomassini et al. (2017)
;
Russell et al. (2020)
. The feedback is two–way, with AEWs providing favourable environmental conditions for convection
Janiga & Thorncroft (2016)
;
Sakaeda et al. (2025)
, thereby acting as a source of predictability for regional MCS activity
Bain et al. (2014)
.

Offshore propagation of AEWs then acts as a major contributor towards the East Atlantic seed vortex population
Thorncroft & Hodges (2001)
. However, there is uncertainty in their direct connection to TCG, with no correlation between annual hurricane counts and AEW numbers, despite AEWs acting as TC precursors
Russell et al. (2017)
. Sensitivity experiments suppressing AEW entry into the Atlantic basin show no significant decrease in NATL TC activity
Patricola et al. (2018)
;
Danso et al. (2022)
;
Kouski Jr et al. (2026)
.
Russell et al. (2017)
have instead shown a significant correlation with lower–tropospheric eddy kinetic energy around the west coast of Africa, pointing to the importance of vortex development and convective–interactions, rather than population size. The likelihood of AEWs later undergoing TCG is increased by coupling and collocation with large MCS anvils
Schwendike & Jones (2010)
;
Leppert et al. (2013)
;
Núñez Ocasio et al. (2020)
. Because AEW and MCS activity reflects monsoon activity, the mean WAM state can affect NATL TCG variability: case–study experiments show moistening the monsoon suppresses TC development from MCSs
Núñez Ocasio et al. (2024)
, while both intra- and inter–seasonal AEJ variability explain aspects of observed trends in NATL TC activity
Klotzbach et al. (2025)
;
Bercos-Hickey & Patricola-DiRosario (2024)
.

It is the southern track AEWs which primarily develop into NATL TCs in the Atlantic Main Development Region (MDR;
Hopsch et al. (2006)
). The development of such seeds into TCs can be directly facilitated by MCSs: convective mass fluxes amplify mid–level vortices through vortex stretching and if maintained, further develop low–level circulations which interact with oceanic latent heat fluxes
Bister & Emanuel (1997)
;
Raymond et al. (2007)
;
Raymond & Sessions (2007)
. This process chain is complementary to the frequently invoked “marsupial pouch” paradigm of TCG
Dunkerton et al. (2009)
;
Asaadi et al. (2016)
;
Wang & Hankes (2014)
, but emphasises the interactions between convection and mid–level precursor vortices
Raymond et al. (2014)
. For AEWs,
Schwendike & Jones (2010)
explicitly showed the role of MCS convection in causing vortex stretching in a southern track seed and subsequent genesis of Hurricane
Helene
(see also
Arnault & Roux (2010)
). In contrast, northern track waves suffer unfavourable offshore environmental conditions, and are associated with TCG around the Gulf of Mexico
T-C. Chen et al. (2008)
. Vortex merger events with southern track vortices off the Guinea coast provide a further mechanism for vortex development which contributes to a significant minority of TCG events stemming from AEWs
C-H. Chen & Liu (2014)
;
Duvel (2021)
;
Jonville et al. (2025)
, since northern track waves offer a precursor low–level circulation. Other known NATL seed vortex sources are frontal low–pressure systems, equatorial waves
Yang et al. (2018)
;
X. Feng et al. (2023)
;
Du et al. (2025)
, and inter tropical convergence zone (ITCZ) vortex breaking
Kouski Jr et al. (2026)
. NATL TCs without AEW origins experience less favourable environmental conditions, make landfall further north, and develop weaker maximum winds and thus lower economic impacts than AEW–origin systems
Bercos-Hickey & Patricola-DiRosario (2024)
.

Tropical cyclones, AEWs and MCSs are all phenomena characterised by convective scale–interactions, and thus are generally poorly represented by coarse–grid global climate models, which rely on parameterisations of convection
Martin & Thorncroft (2015)
;
Roberts et al. (2020)
;
Slingo et al. (2022)
. Even reanalyses struggle to accurately capture TC intensity, and thereby frequency, for the same reason
Hodges et al. (2017)
. The mutual interaction of these systems is therefore a prime opportunity for nascent large–domain km–scale, convection permitting (CP) simulations. Multiple year–long and even decadal global CP simulations now exist
Segura et al. (2025)
and are the subject of intense global co–ordinated assessment, under auspices such as DYAMOND (DYnamics of the Atmospheric general circulation Modeled On Non-hydrostatic Domains;
Stevens et al. (2019)
;
Takasuka et al. (2024)
). CP simulation of the WAM has remote influences across the entire Atlantic basin
Pante & Knippertz (2019)
, making global CP models some of the few suitable candidates for studying the full lifecycle of NATL TCs and their precursor vortices at kilometre scale.

Existing results indicate that CP models improve the representation and intensity of TCs
Judt et al. (2021)
, particularly with regard to rapid intensification
Baker et al. (2024)
. While not convection permitting, analysis of TCs in the HighResMIP ensemble shows decreasing model grid spacing improves TC intensity and frequency
Roberts et al. (2020)
. Over Africa, regional CP modelling of AEWs improves propagation and intensity versus observations
Tomassini et al. (2017)
. Diurnal cycles of convection are significantly improved versus parameterised models, which in West Africa leads to an upscale improvement in the full WAM circulation
Marsham et al. (2013)
;
Birch et al. (2014)
. MCS lifecycles are realistically simulated
Z. Feng et al. (2025)
, with storms showing improved responses to environmental controls
Maybee et al. (2024)
. However, CP models are also afflicted by biases, for example a systematic bias towards convective rainfall in MCSs
Z. Feng et al. (2025)
; model–formulation dependent biases in TC structure
Judt et al. (2021)
; insufficient TC counts versus reanalysis in specific models
Emlaw et al. (2026)
; and very high relative costs and computational requirements
Schär et al. (2020)
.

This article therefore serves two primary purposes: firstly, to use newly available global km–scale models to comprehensively investigate the characteristics of East Atlantic seed vortex populations, and the physical processes which distinguish the vortices which develop into TCs; and secondly, to use this process–analysis to evaluate the same models. Surprisingly, we find that a global CP model does not offer the improvements over coarser–grid parameterised counterparts in NATL TC numbers and intensity that the reader may reasonably expect based on the previous discussion, and despite previous improvements in NATL basin TC variability with decreasing grid spacing
Roberts et al. (2015)
;
Roberts et al. (2020)
. We explore inter–model NATL TC variability (Sec.
3.2
) and highlight the role of WAM mean state and vortex amplification around the Guinea coast (Sec.
3.1
). In Sec.
3.3
we explore the mechanisms which cause this amplification, tracing the role of mass flux profiles in driving vortex development. We then interpret the different mean profiles shown between models, and between developing and non–developing vortices, through changes in coastal MCS characteristics (Sec.
3.4
), before discussing the role of cascading biases in Sec.
4
and presenting our conclusions (Sec.
5
).

2.1
Data

Our study focuses on three global, year–long km–scale simulations performed with the non–hydrostatic, semi–Lagrangian Met Office Unified Model (MetUM,
Brown et al. (2012)

Donlon et al. (2012)

Best et al. (2011)
. All models use the same greenhouse gas forcings, with aerosol and dust climatologies generated with a full Earth system model.

The three simulations differ in their grid spacing and treatment of deep convection.

;

;

;

CoMorph, in contrast, is a more experimental science configuration which deploys, and is named after, a new mass flux convection scheme built and tested by the Met Office. The CoMorph–A package includes the new convection scheme alongside modifications to the cloud microphysics, large-scale cloud and PBL schemes that enable smoother timestep behaviour and improved coupling to the dynamics

2.2
Seed vortex, AEW and TC tracking

Objective identification of vortices is achieved using the TRACK algorithm
Hodges (1994)
;
Hodges (1995)
. For seed vortex and TC tracking we use the implementation of the algorithm described in detail by
Hodges et al. (2017)
. In brief, tracking is conducted globally between 60
∘
S and 60
∘
N from T63 spectrally filtered 6–hourly relative vorticity vertically averaged between 850 and 600 hPa. We label this field
\langle\zeta\rangle
. The spectral truncation excludes the first 0–5 total wavenumbers, and acts to both remove the large–scale background and suppress noise associated with small spatial scales. In the Northern Hemisphere, tracks are generated by first identifying off–grid maxima in
\langle\zeta\rangle
which exceed 5
\times
10
-6
s
-1
in each time frame. Maxima are then joined into temporal tracks by making a first join using a nearest–neighbour approach, and then minimising a cost function for track smoothness subject to adaptive constraints on displacement distance and track smoothness
Hodges (1995)
.

The seed vortex population comprises all such tracks which last for at least two days, or eight time steps. No further criteria is applied in terms of surface fields; the vortices are identified solely from lower–tropospheric relative vorticity. To identify those seeds which develop into TCs, additional criteria are applied following an objective detection method
Hodges et al. (2017)
. Additional fields are added to the base tracks by identifying extrema within a 5
∘
search radius, typically using a gradient descent method with B–spline interpolated fields. The relevant added fields are the maximum T63 vorticity at levels 850 and 700—200 hPa, minimum mean sea level pressure, and maximum 10 m winds using a direct grid point search within a 6
∘
radius.

TC tracks are restricted to beginning within 30
∘
S—30
∘
N. For a minimum of four consecutive time steps over ocean, both the T63 850 hPa relative vorticity
\zeta
, and the difference in T63 850 and 200 hPa
\zeta
, must be larger than 6
\times
10
-5
s
-1
. This ensures the system is appropriately intense and has a warm core structure. A T63 vorticity centre must also exist at each level between 850 and 200 hPa for the same four timesteps to ensure a coherent vertical structure
Hodges et al. (2017)
. We categorise TC tracks following
Klotzbach et al. (2020)
by using the additional mean sea–level pressure minima. A unique facet of using TRACK for TC identification is that the full tracks extend far beyond the period during which the system is categorised as a tropical depression or stronger, enabling communication between seed vortex and mature TC characteristics. For all tracked vortices, we calculate the Hart phase space parameters at each timestep:

-|V_{T}|^{L}=\frac{\partial\Phi(p)^{\prime}}{\partial\log p}\bigg|_{p=900\textrm{hPa}}^{p=600\textrm{hPa}}\,,\qquad-|V_{T}|^{U}=\frac{\partial\Phi(p)^{\prime}}{\partial\log p}\bigg|_{p=600\textrm{hPa}}^{p=300\textrm{hPa}}\,,

(1)

where
\Phi(p)^{\prime}
is the difference between the maximum and minimum geopotential values within a 5
∘
search radius of the vortex centre at pressure
p

Hart (2003)
. The Hart parameters encode the thermal wind response of a vortex, with positive values indicating a warm core in which
\Phi^{\prime}
decreases with height.

Populating the seed tracks with additional fields and applying further criteria allows identification of the TC subset. To identify those vortices which are AEWs, we use a distinct application of TRACK, following the method outlined in
Thorncroft & Hodges (2001)
and
Yang et al. (2018)
. The same T63 spectral resolution and vorticity thresholds are used, however tracking is conducted using relative vorticity on a single pressure level field, rather than a vertical average. AEWs are then such tracks which travel west for over 10,000 km after genesis over Africa. Following
Thorncroft & Hodges (2001)
, inland we consider tracks from 600 hPa
\zeta
to represent southern–track AEWs, and tracks at 850 hPa to represent northern–track AEWs. Note all composites and analyses are conducted on the seed vortex tracks: the AEW tracks are used only to interrogate the identity of the seeds.

Throughout the paper, we will refer to seed, developer, and non–developer vortices. Seed vortices refers to any vortex identified from
\langle\zeta\rangle
that lasts for two days, while developer vortices (DVs) refers to those seeds which later go on to meet the additional TC criteria. Non–developer vortices (NDVs) never evolve into TCs. A developer is considered a TC at the first instance when all TC criteria are met. Since our focus is on seeds, genesis events always refer to the start of a seed track, not the point of intensification of a developer into a TC. The acronym TCG continues to refer to standard tropical cyclogenesis, i.e. the point of development of a seed into a TC. It is important to note that the T63 spectral filtering underpinning TRACK ensures track characteristics for all MetUM simulations and ERA5 are coherent and sampled at a common horizontal resolution.

2.2.1
Vortex contours and circulation

We will be interested in the detailed vertical profiles and circulation evolution of vortices identified by TRACK. Vorticity tendencies are taken as (6–hourly) time derivatives of T63 characteristics. However, circulation requires a measure of vortex geometry, since circulation
\Gamma
is defined as

\Gamma=\int\eta\,\textrm{d}S=\oint_{C}\mathbf{v}\cdot\textrm{d}\mathbf{\ell}\,,

(2)

where
\mathbf{v}
is the horizontal velocity vector,
\eta=f+\zeta
is the absolute vertical vorticity, and
C
is a contour with unit element
\textrm{d}\mathbf{\ell}
that bounds a vertically–oriented surface
S
. To calculate
\Gamma
, one must make an arbitrary choice of
C
.

Typically, studies of vortex circulation focus on case studies or regional domains and can thus sensibly subjectively choose a fixed contour over which
\Gamma
is calculated
Schwendike & Jones (2010)
;
Raymond & López Carrillo (2011)
;
Morris et al. (2024)
. In this study we must account for large seed vortex populations, and the potential for vortex interactions. We therefore identify contours objectively by applying the TRACK vorticity maxima–detection threshold (5
\times
10
-6
s
-1
) to mask the underlying T63 vertically–averaged vorticity fields. A unique vorticity footprint is then assigned to each vortex centre; where footprints merge between vortex centres, a geodesic nearest–neighbour criteria is used to divide them uniquely. The contour
C
for a vortex is then taken as the Cartesian bounding box around the vortex
\langle\zeta\rangle
footprint to ease integration. This procedure is repeated at all timesteps for all East Atlantic vortices. For vortex–relative thermodynamic profiles we use area means over vortex contours. In all simulations, vertical profiles for the grid–scale mass flux
\rho w
, where
\rho
is density, are obtained from explicit vertical velocities
w
(only), calculated as the area integral over a vortex contour, and normalised by contour area.

To study the time evolution of
\Gamma
for a vortex, we make use of the horizontal flux form of the vertical relative vorticity evolution equation
Haynes & McIntyre (1987)
, which when integrated around contour
C
with normal vector
\hat{n}
gives the following form for the circulation tendency
Davis & Galarneau Jr (2009)
;
Schwendike & Jones (2010)
:

\frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial t}=-\bar{\eta}\,\tilde{\delta}A-\oint\,\eta^{\prime}\,\mathbf{v}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,\textrm{d}\ell+\oint\,\omega\left(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\times\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial p}\right)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,\textrm{d}\ell-\oint\left(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\times\mathbf{F}\right)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,\textrm{d}\ell\,.

(3)

The first three terms on the right hand side (RHS) are contributions from vortex stretching, eddy–fluxes and tilting, respectively. The stretching and eddy–flux terms are often considered as a combined convergence term,
\oint\eta\mathbf{v}\cdot\hat{n}\textrm{d}\ell

Raymond et al. (2014)
. They arise from splitting the vorticity
\eta
on the bounding contour into a contour mean (bars) and contour perturbations (primes).
\tilde{\delta}
is then the mean divergence over the area
A
within the the contour; the decomposed form is more informative for our analysis.
\omega
is the vertical velocity in pressure coordinates, and
\hat{\mathbf{k}}
is the vertical unit vector. The final integral is the contribution from turbulent fluxes
\mathbf{F}
. These cannot be quantified from the available model diagnostics, however explicit evaluation in the MetUM has shown their contribution is only significant close to the surface
Morris et al. (2025)
.

A necessary artefact of our objective contour identification method is that each vortex contour
C
can, and will, change for that vortex between timesteps, since the size of the region above the T63 threshold will fluctuate. For circulation contributions (and mass fluxes) we achieve group profile composites for vortices of different contour sizes by first normalising by contour area. However, the circulation theorem from which Eqn.
3
derives applies to circulation about a fixed contour
C
. Composite profiles for vortex circulation and its tendencies plotted in Section
3.3
must thus be interpreted as physical diagnostics, rather than absolute statements about circulation conservation — which we anticipate could not be verified in any case due to the difficulty of closing the budget
Morris et al. (2025)
. Our application of the fluid dynamics of circulation (Sec.
3.3.1
) is instead intended as a case for its utility as an informative process diagnostic.

3
Results

Our results are structured by scale, gradually zooming in to relevant local processes. We begin with East Atlantic seasonal mean state, then identify the basic seed vortex and TC populations within the three simulated seasons. We proceed to study the composite evolution and dynamics of seed vortices, isolating those which develop into TCs, before considering the role of convective organisation in driving the evolution of seeds.

3.1
East Atlantic mean state

TC genesis rates are controlled by both seed frequency and large–scale environmental factors
Gray (1968)
;
Agudelo et al. (2011)
;
T-L. Hsieh et al. (2020)
;
Emanuel (2022)
. The primary environmental control of TC variability is the sea surface temperature (SST); in this regard the simulations remain loosely coupled to reality, since all are driven by observed SSTs. Anomalously warm summer Atlantic SSTs play a pivotal role in making 2020 the most active hurricane season on record
Klotzbach et al. (2020)
. A critical mean–state factor for TC development is thus favourable, and identical, in all three simulations and reanalysis.

Further key favourable environmental conditions for TCG are weak vertical wind shear throughout the troposphere, high mid–level humidity, low saturation deficits, and high potential intensity
Emanuel (2022)
. The seasonal mean for these variables meets these criteria over the tropical Atlantic in all MetUM simulations: the parameterised models have moister mid–levels and marginally lower saturation deficits (Fig. S1), but all models show weak shear through the MDR, and potential intensities are higher in Explicit. Over the Caribbean Sea, the mean net surface shortwave radiation flux is reduced in Explicit due to an increase in stratocumulus deck coverage (Fig. S1d). Such biases in low–lying cloud cover are common in CP models, with areal coverage remaining dependent on the behaviour of sub–grid mixing schemes
Petch (2006)
;
Tomassini et al. (2023)
. Overall though, the basin–wide, large–scale means are comparable.

These are the North Indian (NI), West North Pacific (WNP), East North Pacific (ENP) and NATL basins. For reference, we show both the TRACK count from ERA5 and observed best–track count (IBTrACS). In the NI and WNP basins, all three models are overactive compared to observations, especially in WNP. This is partly due to large numbers of TCs identified by TRACK that do not reach Category 1 (light shading), reflecting the fact our TC identification uses vorticity criteria; the NI tracks, for example, capture many monsoon depressions. This detail aside, all MetUM simulations remain overactive both for Category 1+ and severe Category 3+ systems (Fig.

2

In benchmarking the simulations, it is crucial to bear in mind they are free–running (from February 2020) and thus expected to diverge from reality: the purpose of the comparison is rather to highlight the regional variability in inter–model spread. The CP model is capable of generating numerous, intense TCs, as expected, in the WNP and NI basins. This suggests its apparent failure in NATL and ENP must be due to regional processes. Note that this MetUM result differs to recent findings for the DYAMOND3 X–SHiELD simulation, where TC counts are below ERA5 in all basins
Emlaw et al. (2026)
. Figures
2

The canonical AEW pathway, with recurvature west of the MDR, dominates. Systems follow physically realistic tracks, especially when compared with JRA–3Q reanalysis (Fig. S3), which is closer to observations for both tracks and total accumulated cyclonic energy (ACE) than ERA5. The seasonal ACE values for Param and CoMorph (161.5 kt
2
and 168.5 kt
2

The significant inter–model differences in WAM mean state correlate with the stark differences in their NATL TC tracks. In all datasets except Explicit at least one track reaches a vorticity of 10
-4
s
-1
before leaving the East Atlantic region. Indications of TCG occur much further west in Explicit. The absence of strong systems in the overlapping part of the MDR, relatively low numbers of developer vortices originating from the canonical zonal AEW train, and continued prevalence of TCG in the western Atlantic, further suggest that deficiencies in Explicit TCs can be traced back to the East Atlantic seed vortex population. The particularly low TC count in the ENP also points to this, with no ENP TC seeds emanating from West Africa in Explicit, in contrast to all other models and the established role of AEWs in ENP cyclogenesis
T-C. Chen et al. (2008)
;
Serra et al. (2010)
. The surprise here is that the model with the most realistic WAM state, Explicit, shows the weakest TC population, in contrast to previous work showing that improved WAM state at finer grid spacing in parameterised models leads to improved NATL TC variability
Roberts et al. (2015)
;
Roberts et al. (2020)
.

Our task is thus to understand how East Atlantic processes cause the differences between the km–scale simulations. The dominant steering flow for the seed vortices is the AEJ. Examining daily variability of the AEJ (Figs.
3
b and c) through JJASO shows that in Param, the jet strength peaks in late June, when it also transitions north, reflecting a later monsoon onset date. Such behaviour is weakly shown by CoMorph, but overall CoMorph and Explicit hew closer to the ERA5 climatology. While nearly always further south than climatology, it is important to note that for Param the seasonal means (Fig.
1
) hide long periods where the AEJ is close to the observed baseline (Fig.
3
b). The weaker seasonal mean winds in CoMorph meanwhile reflect an early decline and southern shift of the jet, while in Explicit the jet is often too far north. Intriguingly, in 2020 the ERA5 AEJ was significantly weaker and further north than climatology, highlighting the pronounced role of SSTs in driving the active observed TC season
Klotzbach et al. (2022)
.

As to the classification of the seeds within the vortex populations, the peaks in the vortex number and dissipation distributions correspond closely to the southern AEW track for all datasets (Fig. S5), with stronger activity north of 15
∘
N also reflecting an active northern track. We find most identified vortices are indeed AEWs: over 60% of vortex tracks overlap with a tracked 850 hPa or 600 hPa AEW trough. These vortices are all southern track AEWs: there are no systems which only overlap with a 850 hPa (i.e. northern track) wave trough (Fig. S5f). The propensity of AEWs to later undergo TCG is known to be affected by interactions with deep convection over land and around the Coastal subdomain
Schwendike & Jones (2010)
;
Russell & Aiyyer (2020)
;
Núñez Ocasio et al. (2020)
. Indeed, the AEW tracks show waves deepening offshore (Fisg. S5a–d), with
\sim
35% of seeds overlapping simultaneously with 850 hPa and 600 hPa troughs during their lifetime (Fig. S5f). The Guinea coast marks the primary transition in the behaviour of our different vortex populations, and also the transition to a primarily thermodynamically–controlled convective regime which is more favourable in the parameterised models that exhibit more DVs. We therefore turn our focus to the evolution of seed vortices as they transition from the land to the ocean.

The Figure confirms that processes around the West African coast modulate the selection of DVs from the full seed vortex population. Results here are consistent with similar findings presented by
Duvel (2021)
using an alternative tracking method in ERA–Interim reanalysis. In all models, the DV mean vorticity diverges away from the NDV population around
T
=0. However, in Explicit (Fig.
4

4

T
=0 their vorticity is beyond the NDV spread. DVs then show the strongest vorticity amplification in the vortex’s first 2 days over the ocean — far prior to eventual TCG. NDVs meanwhile are characterised by little or no vortex amplification over the ocean, remaining at the strength set by their land–based evolution. In all models there is a subtle decline in mean NDV vorticity in the days before the vortex reaches the coast. This in part reflects vortex genesis events near the coast (e.g. over the Guinea highlands, Fig. S4a), but also points to the limited influence of deep–inland AEW activity on DV propensity: it is behaviour around the coast which is critical.

The thermodynamic structure of DVs also already distinguishes them from NDVs when leaving the African continent. Figures
4
e–h show that in the days following coastal transition, DVs typically have a stronger low–level cold core structure than NDVs, as measured by the Hart phase space parameter
-|V_{T}|^{L}
. This evolution appears delayed in Param and CoMorph, versus Explicit and ERA5, highlighting the pronounced role of coastal, rather than land–based, processes in the parameterised models. Previous work by
Hopsch et al. (2010)
further supports the hypothesis that DVs in the MetUM simulations are delayed in generating warm cores. TCs themselves are axisymmetric full–depth warm core structures, and indeed DVs are seen to develop low–level warm cores after roughly four days in ERA5, consistent with NATL TCG typically being in the western reaches of the MDR (Fig.
2
). Around the coast, the colder DV cores suggest they have a more pronounced mid–level vortex structure than NDVs: through PV inversion, a strong mid–level circulation causes lower temperatures near the surface, and higher upper–level temperatures, with a low–level warm core developing as the circulation deepens from the mid–troposphere
Bister & Emanuel (1997)
;
Raymond et al. (2014)
. All vortices show warm upper cores (positive
-|V_{T}|^{U}
) at the coastal transition (not shown), with higher values for DVs, consistent with
Brammer & Thorncroft (2015)
.

Turning to results for Explicit, Fig.

Param DVs thus undergo amplification via low–level circulation development, consistent with the maintenance of strong balanced mass flux profiles. For NDVs the profiles are generally more top heavy in shape, but weaker than for DVs. The NDV spread includes vortices with stronger upper–level mass fluxes (and mid–level circulations) than the DV mean, showing that vortex amplification near the African coast is no guarantee of later TCG: downstream conditions must also be favourable. The Explicit results offer the opposite corollary: TCG can be determined by those downstream conditions alone, since in this model DV dynamics appear indistinguishable from NDVs around the coast. Our results so far provide a dynamically consistent explanation for eventual seed vortex evolution centred around the West African coastline. Top–heavy and balanced mass flux profiles in Param reinforce mid–level circulations and cause low–level circulation development in DVs that is absent from NDVs. The lack of vorticity amplification in Explicit is due to the failure of DVs to maintain such mass flux profiles. So why then are strong balanced profiles maintained for Param (and CoMorph) DVs? One suggestion is given by comparing absolute relative humidity (RH) values (Fig.
6

T
for DVs, whereas in Explicit DVs have higher 800 hPa values, and RH declines significantly at both levels as the vortices move further offshore. Examination of full vertical profiles around
T
=0 (Fig.

Such peaks are a feature of the large–scale environment in Param (Fig. S7a). Thermodynamic profiles in CoMorph are consistent with those in Param, both in vortex composites (Fig. S6b) and the regional mean. The steep decline in RH after
T

After all, the convective environment in Explicit remains extremely favourable for convection. Within a common vortex population, however, our analysis confirms ambient moisture plays a crucial role in selecting DVs, consistent with the work of multiple authors identifying environmental moisture around the Guinea coastline as a key factor in distinguishing those AEWs which develop into TCs

3.3.1
Circulation tendencies

DVs are distinguished from the wider East Atlantic vortex population by stronger vorticity growth when transitioning offshore. From analysis of Param, the simulation with the most DVs, we have argued that this amplification can be explained by interaction of a precursor mid–level vortex with strong top and bottom–heavy vertical mass fluxes that are maintained offshore and cause the development of a low–level circulation. Supplementary results from CoMorph support this hypothesis (Fig. S6). In Explicit, mass fluxes are weak and not maintained, explaining the very limited offshore vorticity growth in the seed population.

To formally show that the differences in mass flux profiles can cause the changes in circulation shown in Fig.

In the circulation budget, only the stretching term captures internal processes within the vortex: the eddy–flux and tilting contributions are both limited to processes at the contour boundary (see discussion in
Davis & Galarneau Jr (2009)
). For Land vortices, such external contributions drive the positive circulation tendency above 700 hPa. External terms are stronger in Explicit (Fig.

Morris et al. (2024)

Tilting contributions are also stronger for Land in Explicit, and positive below 700 hPa. This term quantifies the deformation of local vortex tubes by updrafts lying on the contour (Fig. 1b in
Morris et al. (2025)
), and thus here we infer reflects the stronger deep convective activity occurring around inland vortices in Explicit.

The direct influence of convection on the vortex circulation is quantified by the stretching term. By mass continuity, the strong positive land values below 700 hPa in both simulations are consistent with the action of strong low–level convergence into deep convective cores. Similarly, strong negative values (in all subdomains) above 400 hPa reflect upper–level divergent outflows. Below 850 hPa the stretching term’s total influence on the net circulation tendency is very likely curtailed by the absent friction term, leading to the limited low–level amplification found in Fig.
6
a. While no robust Land DV (dashed pink) signal can be quantified in Explicit due to the small sample size (n=2), in Param the DVs already show positive vortex stretching at 700 hPa. This pattern continues for Coastal vortices, where in both simulations vortex stretching is the only driver of mid–level circulation growth. DVs are distinguished from the broader seed population mean by strong, positive Coastal stretching contributions, correlating with the distinct differences in DV mass flux profiles. For DVs in Param, vortex stretching extends from the surface to 600 hPa, consistent with mid–level vortex amplification and low–level development (Fig.
6
a) driven by deep–inflow mass flux profiles (Fig.
5
a). Coastal DV vortex stretching is similar in CoMorph throughout the lower troposphere (Fig. S6c), but weaker in Explicit and limited to around 700 hPa only.

In all simulations, background absolute vorticity advection (eddy flux) has a near–uniform net negative contribution to total circulation for both the Coastal and Ocean means, with stretching remaining the primary positive tendency in the Ocean subdomain in Param and CoMorph. In both cases, the seed vortex mean mid–level stretching is equivalent to that for DVs, demonstrating earlier mid–level circulation development over land or at the coast is key for later TCG. Indeed Param and CoMorph Ocean DVs primarily show low–level vortex stretching, consistent with the continued low–level vortex development documented in Fig.
6
a and maintenance of deep–convective mass fluxes. Oceanic vortices in Explicit, in contrast, show no clear source of positive circulation tendency, yielding the observed lack of offshore vorticity growth. Large residuals between the total circulation tendency profiles at low–levels implicitly confirm the role of frictional dissipation.

In summary, the coastal amplification of vortices is primarily caused by vortex stretching below 600 hPa. At mid–levels, the stretching reinforces and amplifies the existing circulation of southern track AEWs, which over land are supported by advection of background vorticity (eddy–fluxes). Vortex stretching is responsible for the majority of low–level DV circulation spin–up. The importance of the stretching, which depends on the vortex–average convergence
\tilde{\delta}
, also shows that the evolution of vortex mass flux profiles in Figs.
5
and
6
b are instrumental in causing the coincident changes in the circulation profile. Since the vortices we study are cold–cored seeds rather than active TCs, such mass fluxes are primarily driven by external convective activity which mutually interacts with the vortex circulation. This interaction between convection and seeds near the West African coast is then critical in determining the developer vortices which later undergo TCG.

3.4
Role of Mesoscale Convective Systems

The role of cloud populations in East Atlantic seed vortex evolution is already apparent from Fig.
6
d, which shows high–cloud cover declining significantly for DVs in Explicit as they move offshore. Deep convective activity in this region is primarily organised into MCSs
Wu et al. (2024)
, which have been shown to amplify AEWs through diabatic PV generation
Tomassini et al. (2017)
;
Russell et al. (2020)
. Interactions between AEWs and MCSs are then associated with a higher likelihood of downstream TCG
Núñez Ocasio et al. (2020)
, with MCS areal coverage within wave troughs a particularly important controlling factor
Leppert et al. (2013)
. The key physical aspect of MCSs in our context is that these organised systems exhibit both convective and stratiform regions
Janiga & Thorncroft (2014)
, and thus balanced deep–inflow mass flux profiles and large anvil cloud decks. In particular, the large stratiform rainfall regions lead to significant top–heavy profiles
Mapes & Houze Jr (1995)
;
Raymond et al. (2014)
, which we have highlighted as a key explanation for how vortices evolve, while radiative forcing from large anvil decks promotes vortex amplification by suppressing stratiform downdrafts
Luschen et al. (2026)
. Finally, regional mean mass flux profiles (Fig. S7c) show that mean Explicit upper–level mass fluxes are stronger in all subdomains than Param and CoMorph, and stronger at low–levels offshore, suggesting that the weak offshore flux profiles in Fig.
5
b are local to vortices rather than a reflection of the domain mean. This distinction motivates an examination of the behaviour and details of convective organisation in the MetUM simulations to explain the vortex evolution.

There are far more Coastal MCSs in Explicit than the parameterised models, with 317 unique systems versus 104 in Param in total — this is to be expected for a higher resolution CP model. Tracking outputs however are derived from a common 0.1

The interquartile ranges for rainfall maxima in Param and CoMorph are more realistic but biased too low, and their storms underestimate the high–rainfall skew of the observed distribution.

For MCS mean rainfall, which combines storm snapshot structure and intensity, Explicit exhibits by far the most realistic distribution. The distributions of much smaller values for storms in Param and CoMorph reflects these models’ systematic structural biases of too–large MCSs with too–low convective intensities. Yet storms in Explicit also suffer a structural bias, of too–small MCSs with too–high convective intensities. In Param and CoMorph, although extreme mass fluxes are weaker, the significantly larger MCS stratiform decks lead to the smoother, more uniform fluxes experienced by seeds, perhaps due to the deep convection scheme providing a more linear response to environmental variables than the explicit convection in Explicit. We thus arrive at an explanation for the reduced mass flux profiles available to seeds in Explicit versus Param, and thereby the reduced number of East Atlantic seeds which develop into TCs. The two models show systematic differences in MCS structure: systems in Explicit suffer a small–anvil bias, with upper–level mass fluxes concentrated into more vigorous, distinct updrafts, compared to unrealistically large storms in Param that have a weaker, more homogeneous updraft structure covering a much broader area, which aggregates to give a strong top–heavy mean mass flux. While the probability of a vortex lying near MCS convection is higher in Explicit, that of a vortex lying under an MCS anvil, and thus being influenced by a consistent deep–inflow mass flux profile, is higher in Param due to systems’ larger extent. Moreover, the primary vortex and MCS trains are latitudinally aligned, and the large MCSs in these trains persist longer than Explicit equivalents, enabling longer–lasting influences on vortex development. 4
Discussion

Our results show that a high–resolution global CP simulation is arguably outperformed by coarser–resolution, parameterised counterparts in the development of TCs from comparable convectively–coupled seed vortex populations, despite significant mean–state biases in the parameterised models. We say “arguably” as the nature of the year–long, free–running DYAMOND3 protocol to which these models adhere makes direct evaluation against observations and reanalysis difficult, hence the strategy adopted in this paper of comparison against climatology. The MetUM simulations considered are not intended as faithful reflections of the extremely active observed 2020 NATL TC season, and cannot be evaluated as such. However, all models are driven by that year’s observed SSTs, and even including ERA5, only Param and CoMorph generate bulk metrics for seasonal activity close to observations.

In drawing conclusions from our results, it is critically important to consider the cascading role of compound biases. The very small numbers of Explicit TC developers originating from West Africa is not a result of the model being unable to simulate the physical mechanisms through which vortex development occurs in this pathway: Figs.
5
–
8
show that DVs do have different characteristics to NDVs, with stronger top–heavy mass flux profiles, more pronounced mid–level PV anomalies, moister mid–levels and enhanced vortex stretching; all akin to DVs in Param and CoMorph. The issue is that on average, far fewer seeds receive the essential ingredients for undergoing development, particularly in the East Atlantic region that this study focuses on. Offshore seeds in Explicit suffer from a drier, cooler atmosphere than those in climatology and the parameterised models (Figs.
11
a,b), which is an essential ingredient for developing the convective mass flux signatures required for TCG (Fig.
11
c). Moreover, while Explicit simulates perhaps the most realistic Coastal MCS population, and the organised convection associated with MCSs is key to enhancing circulation via vortex stretching, the storms cannot contribute to vortex development as they are not collocated with seed vortex trains (Fig.
9
). Furthermore, Explicit MCSs have more realistic heterogeneous updraft structure, but significantly underdeveloped anvil extents, both of which act to produce less coherent top–heavy fluxes near vortices (Fig.
10
). Over land, all results indicate more active convection and vortex amplification in Explicit, consistent with previous findings regarding the benefit of convection permitting simulations for AEWs and the WAM
Marsham et al. (2013)
;
Tomassini et al. (2017)
;
Russell et al. (2020)
. While results from reanalysis affirm the importance of such interactions for DVs (e.g. Fig. S9), they also highlight the even stronger role of coastal processes.

The far more active vortex–development pathway, and thus NATL TC population, found here in Param does not show a “superior” model in the sense of physical realism. Param may better reflect documented 2020 NATL hurricanes, but it does so for several wrong reasons which together interfere constructively when it comes to the East Atlantic seed vortex population (Fig.
11
a). Firstly, the mean WAM state is far from the climatology, with an AEJ that is too strong, too far south, and extends too far offshore. Atmospheric columns are moister and warmer than climatology, and onshore, rain is confined too far south. These mean state biases affect vortex development two–fold: the AEJ characteristics push the vortex trains leaving West Africa further south than climatology, and provide an uncommonly favourable background environment for coastal MCSs and vortices. The shift in the vortex train causes direct spatial alignment with peak MCS activity, thereby leading to an increased propensity for the mechanisms outlined in Sec.
3.3
to occur. We know from Fig.
10
that the simulated MCSs are themselves misrepresentative of real systems, yet the extremely broad, homogeneous mean upper–level mass fluxes they generate remain favourable for vortex development
Leppert et al. (2013)
;
Luschen et al. (2026)
. The critical nature of offshore vorticity growth for TC development ensures that more destructive onshore biases in Param play a far more minor role.

In contrast, for Explicit the model biases interfere destructively (Fig.
11
b). The slightly–northwards AEJ in Fig.
1
a likely contributes towards pulling the coastal vortex trains away from peak offshore MCS activity, which itself lies at a similar latitude to the other models (Fig.
9
). We note that the daily jet metric in Fig.
3
c does not show a pronounced northwards bias versus the ERA5 2020 mean or CoMorph, and combined ERA5 and OBS–MCS climatology also shows a latitudinal displacement between offshore vortex and MCS trains. Existing understanding can well explain underlying biases in Param, with thermal wind response from an overly strong meridional temperature gradient explaining the AEJ bias (Fig.
3
a), and the lack of Saharan ventilation from onshore convective cold pools caused by deficiencies in the parameterised diurnal cycle of Sahel convection explaining the origins of that gradient
Marsham et al. (2013)
;
Birch et al. (2014)
.
For Explicit no such understanding exists, and further exploration is required, for example of whether the configuration’s boundary layer scheme is driving the oceanic dry–column bias versus climatology. It also remains unknown why MCS stratiform structure is systematically underestimated, despite afflicting most km–scale CP models
Z. Feng et al. (2025)
. Overestimated convective updraft widths caused by underestimated entrainment, which then bias key microphysics processes, are a candidate explanation
Lebo & Morrison (2015)
;
Prein et al. (2021)
;
Kukulies et al. (2024)
; however, the configuration used in Explicit has demonstrated significant improvement in the handling of entrainment processes in continental West African MCSs
Maybee et al. (2024)
.

Results from CoMorph show that a modern experimental convective parameterisation is able to reduce parameterisation biases in the continental WAM without introducing the same offshore biases as Explicit. Offshore, CoMorph continues to show a strong TCW bias, and unrealistic MCS structures (albeit with improved rainfall characteristics vs Param), but these are favourable for vortex development. Analysis of CoMorph corroborates the offshore vortex development mechanisms we have traced in Param (Fig. S6 and Fig.
11
c), while improvements over Param in continental Africa demonstrate that the new convection scheme, with some scale–aware capability, shows great promise for the representation of regions with intricate convective–scale interactions.

Turning from a model–development to a physical perspective, it is interesting to consider the role of fundamental vortex dynamics in influencing the different simulated seed vortex populations. All our results pertain to vortices identified by the TRACK algorithm from T63 spectrally–truncated vorticity fields. This spectral smoothing, while enabling fair model comparison and filtering out small–scale vorticity noise, also hides the native–grid eddies which ultimately make up the mesoscale seed vortices of interest. At the convective storm scale, diabatic heating generates horizontal dipoles which can extend through the troposphere
Lilly (1986)
;
Chagnon & Gray (2009)
. While individual PV anomalies are strong, the dipole structure averages to a relatively weaker composite vortex. The detailed structure of sub–MCS–scale dipoles is part–resolved in Explicit (
\sim
5 km/n2560 resolution), but unresolved in Param and CoMorph (
\sim
10 km/n1280), which instead generate larger, smoother coherent vorticity fields (Fig. S12). In all models, there is a significant correlation (
p<
0.05) between TRACK vortex maximum and mean area, and system lifetime (distributions in Fig. S2b). The smoother vorticity dynamics in the parameterised models may thus artificially extend the persistence of the larger mesoscale vortices, potentially providing another compounding factor that favours DVs in Param and CoMorph. The complex, highly non–linear dynamics of vortex–vortex interactions can lead to upscale energy transfer and thus perturbation growth in (midlatitude) circulations
Oertel & Schemm (2021)
. Large–domain CP models, particularly at lower grid spacings than Explicit, offer an unprecedented opportunity to study the details of such interactions in realistic settings, and should certainly be pursued in the context of TC development.

Our results also offer insights for ongoing research surrounding the relative role of seed populations and large–scale environmental conditions in controlling TCG
Emanuel (2022)
;
Wang et al. (2025)
. Many authors have used statistical genesis indices based on background environmental fields to explain trends in TC variability
Camargo et al. (2007)
;
Tippett et al. (2011)
, while spontaneous TCG has been described in model experiments
Nolan et al. (2007)
;
Wing et al. (2016)
. The potentially limited role of the base–state East Atlantic seed population in NATL TCG has also been demonstrated through numerous experiments suppressing AEW activity and finding limited changes, or even increases, in TC populations
Patricola et al. (2018)
;
Danso et al. (2022)
;
Bercos-Hickey & Patricola (2023)
;
Kouski Jr et al. (2026)
. In our study, mean state fields alone are not enough to explain inter–model TC variability: all show favourable Atlantic basin conditions for TCG (Fig. S1). The seed frequency also offers little explanatory power. In the framework of seed–based genesis indices
Vecchi et al. (2019)
;
T-L. Hsieh et al. (2020)
;
Emanuel (2022)
, we find the key factor is the transition probability of a seed vortex to a TC, which is far lower in Explicit. Issues with this factor have been identified in other global CP models
Emlaw et al. (2026)
. This perspective also perhaps offers a connection to AEW–suppression experiments, in that suppressing the seed frequency will have limited effect on TCG rates if the transition probabilities are high. Ensembles of such experiments have found increased East Atlantic (our Ocean subdomain) humidity and rainfall
Kouski Jr et al. (2026)
, implying increased convective activity and thereby increased seed–TC transition probabilities in a similar manner to the constructive action of biases in Param. We leave detailed investigation of the intersection with such experimental frameworks for future work.

5
Conclusions

Interactions between organised deep convection and mesoscale seed vortices in the Eastern Atlantic play a fundamental role in the lifecycle of Atlantic TCs. Here we have explored these interactions and their relationship with TC variability in three global, year–long Met Office Unified Model simulations, reanalysis data, and satellite observations. All simulations are high resolution and driven by observed (2020) SSTs, but differ significantly in their representation of deep convection and treatment of other atmospheric physics. We find that a
\sim
5 km grid, convection permitting model (Explicit) produces almost half of the number of NATL and ENP basin TCs as an equivalent
\sim
10 km configuration with parameterised convection (Param), with the canonical AEW–pathway to NATL TCG being most strongly affected. The discrepancy cannot be explained by regional environmental differences in the central and western Atlantic, which are favourable for TCG in both models. A
\sim
10 km configuration (CoMorph) using a different convection scheme, with a novel implementation of mass–flux principles, behaves closer to the Explicit simulation over continental Africa, but shows a TC population closer to Param and shares the same bias towards too–moist atmospheric columns over the Eastern Atlantic.

We show that different rates of NATL TC development from the East Atlantic seed vortex population stem from the dynamics of vortices as they transition from continental Africa to the Atlantic ocean. In Param, CoMorph, and ERA5 climatology, such developer vortices (DVs) show strong vorticity tendencies at the Guinea coastline that are absent in Explicit. This difference can be explained by the relative interaction of MCSs with seed vortices, which we and many previous authors argue act as a key catalyst for vortex development (e.g.
Schwendike & Jones (2010)
;
Russell et al. (2020)
;
Núñez Ocasio et al. (2020)
). Mean state biases in the West African Monsoon in Param act favourably to latitudinally align the primary vortex train with strong MCS activity and high atmospheric column moisture (Fig.
11
a). Systematic differences in MCS structure stemming from the representation of deep convection lead to unrealistically large, long–lived anvils accompanied by broader regions of positive upper–level mass flux in Param and CoMorph versus Explicit (Fig.
10
). Parameterised storms are characterised by very large regions of uniform weak intensity, in contrast to those in Explicit and observations which show heterogeneous distributions of smaller storms with more intense convection (Figs.
10
a,b). MCSs in Explicit exhibit a small anvil bias and overestimated convective rainfall, in common with other km–scale CP models
Z. Feng et al. (2025)
.

Through analysis of composite vortex profiles we demonstrate the mechanisms which distinguish DVs from non–developers, identifying a key chain (Fig.
11
c) whereby offshore top–heavy or deep–inflow mass flux profiles cause mid–level vortex stretching that reinforces an existing mid–level circulation. The maintenance of convection and bottom–heavy fluxes then causes low–level circulation growth, placing DVs on a recognised route to TCG
Bister & Emanuel (1997)
;
Raymond et al. (2007)
;
Raymond et al. (2014)
. The key MCS feedback here is the essential provision of the offshore deep–inflow, stratiform dominated mass flux. In this light, the unrealistic structures of parameterised MCSs are favourable for increasing the exposure of seed vortices to uniform regions in which this condition is met (Fig.
10
c). Our results reinforce the finding of
Leppert et al. (2013)
that convective area coverage is more important for vortex development than convective intensity. In this work we have analysed the role of local convective processes in driving vortex evolution in the simulations. An important extension would be to consider the role of known synoptic drivers of AEWs and TCG, for example equatorial waves
Lawton et al. (2022)
;
X. Feng et al. (2023)
;
Rios-Berrios et al. (2024)
, which can themselves suffer from large–scale biases in CP models
Takasuka et al. (2026)
.

In tracing the fundamental difference in East Atlantic vortex populations between different model configurations, our work serves to explicitly show the importance of convective–scale interactions in the development of NATL TC seeds, and highlights the crucial physics whereby MCS mass fluxes can engender mid and low–level circulation growth through vortex stretching (Figs.
8
and
11
c). Results from previous work
Schwendike & Jones (2010)
;
Leppert et al. (2013)
and our observational climatology are consistent with this mechanism, with the vortex climatology showing that DV characteristics are already distinguishable over the African continent (Fig.
4
). Here we have deliberately focussed on seed vortices, identified from vertically–averaged vorticity
\langle\zeta\rangle
using the TRACK algorithm, rather than AEWs. Independent application of TRACK on single pressure levels confirms that in all models, the majority of seeds are indeed AEWs (Fig. S5). However, the wider perspective allows us to focus on the physics of TC developers, rather than the physics of AEWs, which naturally emphasises convective interactions over the African continent. Our work builds on a similarly oriented study of vortices in reanalysis
Duvel (2021)
, and has enabled broader interrogation of physical drivers and interactions by using km–scale simulations. We indeed find DVs are associated with signatures of continental convective interactions, such as increased continental vorticity tendencies; however, the signature and the magnitude of coastal interactions is far greater
Hopsch et al. (2010)
;
Agudelo et al. (2011)
.

While West African coastal processes arguably remain under–explored versus those over e.g. the continental Sahel, extensive field campaigns and modelling efforts are rapidly improving our knowledge of this key region
Nowottnick et al. (2024)
;
Flamant et al. (2024)
;
Sakaeda et al. (2025)
;
Martinez et al. (2025)
;
Colón-Burgos & Bell (2026)
. Our results fully support such efforts from the context of NATL TC variability. A major limitation of this study is the lack of observations for investigating the vertical structures of real MCS and seed vortex populations. ERA5 cannot be considered observational truth for mesoscale dynamics, as exemplified by its own small 2020 NATL TC count (Fig.
2
f). We have relied on inference from ERA5 vortices and satellite–observation MCS tracks to make comments about which MetUM model offers the most realistic seed population dynamics and vortex structures, but cannot confirm the precise mechanisms for real systems beyond known case studies (e.g.
Bister & Emanuel (1997)
;
Schwendike & Jones (2010)
;
Raymond & López Carrillo (2011)
). In this context we look forward to investigating the potential step–change offered by new satellite missions measuring vertical soundings (EarthCARE,
Illingworth et al. (2015)
; INCUS,
Dolan et al. (2023)
), and their ability to test the mechanisms outlined here in the real atmosphere. Initial evaluations of convective vertical structure in km–scale models using EarthCARE data have already been achieved by
Roh et al. (2025)
.

Finally, our results raise crucial questions for the ongoing development of global CP models. West Africa is a natural testbed for such simulations due to the critical upscale role of deep convection in regional circulation, and indeed previous studies highlight the benefits of explicit convection in this region
Marsham et al. (2013)
;
Pante & Knippertz (2019)
. Simulation of TCs, TCG, AEWs and MCSs all benefit significantly from explicit convection. Yet here, with the reins and freedom to simulate all such systems’ interactions, the explicit model arguably performs poorly. The present simulations are too short to isolate whether this is simply natural variability, or a more systematic bias. Analysis by
Emlaw et al. (2026)
of a comparable simulation run with the X–SHiELD model demonstrates a deficiency in TC numbers across all basins, highlighting the issues facing global CP models. Robust intercomparison of MCS tracks from existing DYAMOND models shows systematic underestimation of their stratiform component
Z. Feng et al. (2025)
, while results from TC tracking activities at the 2025 WCRP Global km–scale hackathon suggest that the NATL–only regional low–TC count bias is not unique to the MetUM, and afflicts many available DYAMOND3 simulations. Are the two problems related? Our future work will explore this crucial question.

From this paper only, we can still offer a perhaps obvious, but important, message for global km–scale model development. Increasing the fidelity of convective interactions does not guarantee downstream or upscale improvements in key metrics, of which the Atlantic hurricane population is justifiably an example. Existing global parameterised configurations, such as GAL9 in Param, benefit from decades of tuning and operational testing. The next generation of global models will have to follow a similar path before achieving their potential benefits.
