Rethinking Layer Redundancy in Large Language Models: Calibration Objectives and Search for Depth Pruning
Minkyu Kim, Vincent-Daniel Yun, Youngrae Kim, YoungJin Heo, Suin Cho, Seong-hun Kim, Woosang Lim, Gaeul Kwon

Abstract
Depth pruning improves the inference efficiency of large language models by removing Transformer blocks. Prior work has focused on importance criteria and search algorithms, often treating layer redundancy as an inherent structural property of pretrained networks. In contrast, we adopt a functional perspective , where redundancy is jointly influenced by the model and the evaluation objective, suggesting that a universal ranking may not be sufficient. Through an empirical study across three LLM families, two calibration objectives, and seven search algorithms, we observe that different objectives yield qualitatively different redundant layers, and that perplexity and downstream accuracy rankings do not consistently align. Under a fixed objective, however, search algorithms tend to produce similar solutions. Overall, our results suggest that the calibration objective may play a more influential role than the choice of search algorithm, indicating that further attention to objective design could be beneficial.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved strong capabilities, but their scale incurs substantial deployment cost
[
5
,
21
]
. Among structured compression approaches,
depth pruning
removes entire Transformer blocks, reducing inference cost proportionally to the number of pruned layers
[
10
,
17
]
. Prior work advances depth pruning along two axes, importance criteria
[
10
,
17
,
24
,
20
]
and search algorithms
[
17
,
2
,
19
,
16
,
9
]
. Several of these works treat layer importance as an intrinsic property of the pretrained network: ShortGPT
[
10
]
ranks layers by cosine similarity independent of downstream tasks, and related similarity- and magnitude-based criteria
[
17
,
2
]
apply a single ranking across uses. We refer to this as the
structural view
, where layer redundancy is assumed to be a property of the pretrained model, recoverable by an appropriate criterion or search.

We examine whether this structural view is consistent with the empirical behavior of LLMs under pruning. Our central observation is that layer redundancy may be better understood as a
functional property
jointly influenced by the model and the evaluation objective: a layer identified as redundant under language modeling perplexity may remain important for downstream reasoning. This suggests that the appropriate object of study is not a single universal ranking, but an
objective-conditioned
notion of redundancy.

To provide empirical evidence, we disentangle the two design axes. Prior work typically varies the importance metric and search algorithm jointly, making it difficult to attribute observed gains to either component
[
24
,
9
]
. We instead hold one axis fixed while varying the other, casting depth pruning as the subset selection problem

S^{*}=\arg\min_{|S|=k}\ \mathcal{L}\big(\mathcal{D};\,f_{\theta\odot m_{S}}\big),

(1)

and comparing seven search algorithms under two distinct calibration objectives on three LLM families: language modeling perplexity on C4, and a task likelihood margin
[
20
]
on commonsense reasoning
[
6
]
.

Three empirical findings support this perspective. First, the same model exhibits different pruning patterns under different objectives: under perplexity, removals concentrate in contiguous mid-to-late layers and are largely consistent across search algorithms, whereas under the task likelihood margin, removals are more distributed and vary across algorithms. Second, for perplexity-pruned models, perplexity rank and downstream accuracy rank do not consistently align: Spearman correlations range from
\rho=-0.78
to near zero, with most conditions negative. Third, within a fixed objective, search algorithms converge to similar solutions, with average accuracy differences of
1
–
3
percentage points. These results indicate that the calibration objective may play a more influential role than the choice of search algorithm.

Contributions.
We articulate and empirically examine a
functional view
of layer redundancy in LLM depth pruning. We disentangle search and objective across three LLM families, two calibration objectives, and seven search algorithms in a controlled setting. Finally, we identify a systematic misalignment between calibration perplexity and downstream reasoning accuracy as an empirical consequence of objective-dependent redundancy.

Related Works

Pruning paradigms.

Unstructured pruning
[
18
,
4
,
22
]
can achieve high sparsity, but often relies on specialized hardware to realize practical speedups. In contrast, structured pruning removes architectural components, enabling immediate efficiency gains on standard hardware. Among structured approaches,
depth pruning
removes entire Transformer blocks deemed redundant
[
17
]
, with inference cost scaling approximately linearly with the number of retained layers.

Importance criteria.

A substantial line of work focuses on designing importance criteria to identify redundant layers, including cosine similarity
[
10
]
, inter-layer output similarity
[
17
]
, mutual information
[
24
]
, and prompt-conditioned routing
[
20
]
. These approaches share a common premise: that each layer admits an intrinsic notion of importance that can be recovered by an appropriate criterion. Under this view, once a reliable ranking is obtained, it can be applied across tasks and settings. We revisit this assumption and examine whether redundancy is better understood as a property of the layer itself or as dependent on the evaluation objective.

Search strategies and the metric-search confound.

Most pruning frameworks combine an importance metric with a search procedure. One-shot
[
10
]
and greedy iterative
[
17
]
methods make sequential and often irreversible decisions, which has motivated the use of more global approaches such as evolutionary search
[
16
]
and constrained binary optimization
[
9
]
for the underlying NP-hard subset selection problem
[
12
]
. However, these global methods typically introduce both a new metric and a new search strategy simultaneously, making it difficult to isolate the source of observed improvements. In this work, we explicitly disentangle these two axes by holding one component fixed while varying the other. The prior-guided GA and BO variants we employ are used as controlled search procedures rather than as standalone contributions.

3
Depth Pruning as Subset Selection

3.1
Notation and problem formulation

Let
f_{\theta_{0}}
denote a pretrained LLM with
N
transformer blocks, indexed by
I\triangleq\{1,\dots,N\}
. We assume access to a small calibration dataset
\mathcal{D}
and an evaluation loss
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D};\theta)
; throughout this work,
\mathcal{L}
is instantiated as either the negative log-likelihood or a task likelihood margin loss
[
20
]
. A pruning decision is represented by a subset
S\subseteq I
of removed layers, with induced mask
(m_{S})_{i}\triangleq\mathbb{1}[i\notin S]
; we write
f_{\theta_{0}\odot m_{S}}
for the resulting pruned model. Given a pruning budget
k
, depth pruning seeks a subset
S\subseteq I
with
|S|=k
that minimizes degradation on
\mathcal{D}
:

S^{\star}\;=\;\arg\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq I\\
|S|=k\end{subarray}}\;\mathcal{L}\big(\mathcal{D};\,\theta_{0}\odot m_{S}\big).

(2)

This formulation makes explicit that depth pruning is a combinatorial subset selection problem over
\binom{N}{k}
configurations. For modern LLMs with
N\approx 32
–
40
and
k\in\{7,9,11\}
, the search space already contains
10^{7}
to
10^{9}
candidates, well beyond exhaustive evaluation.

3.2
Non-additivity of layer removal

A central challenge in solving equation (
2
) is that the effect of removing multiple layers is non-additive. Define
\Delta(S)\triangleq\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D};\theta_{0}\odot m_{S})-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D};\theta_{0})
. For disjoint subsets
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq I
, in general

\Delta(S_{1}\cup S_{2})\;\neq\;\Delta(S_{1})+\Delta(S_{2}),

(3)

because removing layer
i
alters the hidden-state distribution seen by all subsequent layers, a phenomenon known as
reconstruction error explosion

[
8
]
. A methodological consequence is that layer selection is not an independent ranking problem: a per-layer score
s_{i}
that approximates
\Delta(\{i\})
cannot, in general, predict the joint effect
\Delta(S)
for
|S|>1
. Top-
k
ranking over such scores therefore implicitly assumes an additivity that the architecture does not provide.

3.3
Redundancy as a property of
(\theta_{0},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{D})

Equation (
2
) further makes explicit that the optimal subset
S^{\star}
is a property of the triple
(\theta_{0},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{D})
, not of
\theta_{0}
alone. Fixing
\theta_{0}
and varying
\mathcal{L}
can, in principle, yield entirely different minimizers, since two losses may assign different importance to the same hidden-state distortions. We therefore adopt a relational definition: a layer
i\in I
is
functionally redundant with respect to
\mathcal{L}
if
i\in S^{\star}
for some optimal or near-optimal subset of equation (
2
). Under this definition, the
structural view
implicit in prior work corresponds to the assumption that the functionally redundant set is approximately invariant to
\mathcal{L}
, so that a single universal ranking suffices. The
functional view
investigated here is its negation. Equation (
2
) allows us to test between the two by fixing
\theta_{0},\mathcal{D}
and separately varying the search procedure and the loss
\mathcal{L}
: small variation under the former combined with large variation under the latter constitutes direct evidence for the functional view, and this decomposition organizes Section
5
.

Experimental Setup

For each pruned model, we independently vary the search algorithm while fixing the calibration objective, and vice versa, isolating the two sources of variation in
S^{\star}
. All datasets used are publicly available NLP benchmarks; no human-subject data was collected. We evaluate three open-weight LLMs of comparable scale but differing training pipelines: LLaMA3 8B, LLaMA3.1 8B, and Qwen3 8B, to check whether observed phenomena are specific to a single pretraining recipe or recur across model families.

All experiments were conducted on 4 NVIDIA A40 GPUs.

Calibration objectives.

We consider two qualitatively different losses
\mathcal{L}
in equation (
2
): language modeling
perplexity
on C4
[
14
]
(64 samples, 2048 tokens each), and a

[
20
]
(the average log-likelihood gap between correct and incorrect answers; 128 samples from Commonsense 170k
[
7

). The two represent distinct styles of supervision: perplexity is a task-agnostic fluency signal, whereas the task margin is a discriminative, task-centric signal. If redundancy were structural, both should reward similar subsets.

Under each objective, we compare seven algorithms spanning the locality spectrum: one-shot top-

[
17
]
, beam search (

16
, elitism
0.2
, mutation
0.15
,

[
9
]
, and fast-block-select
[
24
]
. These serve as a controlled panel of search behaviors rather than competing method proposals; all share the same
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{D}
within each cell.

Pruning budgets.

We examine two budgets
k\in{7,9}
, spanning moderate to high compression of the roughly

–
40

) to regimes where reconstruction error becomes more pronounced (

).

5
Evidence for a Functional View of Redundancy

We present three empirical findings supporting the functional view of Section
3
: (i) the calibration objective qualitatively reshapes which layers are selected as redundant (§
5.1
); (ii) perplexity rank and downstream accuracy rank are not positively correlated (§
5.2
); and (iii) within a single objective, different search algorithms converge to similar solutions (§
5.3
).

5.1
Objectives reshape patterns

Figure
1
visualizes which layers are removed by each search algorithm under each calibration objective. Under the structural view, the two halves should look broadly similar: if a layer is intrinsically redundant in
\theta_{0}
, both objectives should select it.

The observed patterns are inconsistent with this prediction. Under
perplexity
calibration, pruning concentrates in two contiguous architectural clusters (indices
9
–
12
and
22
–
26
in LLaMA3 8B; similar mid-and-late clusters appear in LLaMA3.1 8B and Qwen3 8B), and this clustering is largely shared across search algorithms. Under the
task likelihood margin
on the same model, removals are scattered across the network and vary more strongly between search algorithms. The two objectives therefore produce structurally different redundant sets, not merely different rankings of the same structure: the geometry of
S^{\star}
is shaped by the loss used to define it. The contrast is qualitatively robust across all three model families.

5.2
Perplexity vs. accuracy

Do the two objectives at least agree on which pruning decisions preserve model quality at the outcome level? For each
(\text{model},M)
combination, we rank the seven perplexity-pruned models by average calibration perplexity and by average downstream accuracy, and compare the two rankings.

Under the structural view, both rankings should agree. The observed Spearman correlations (Figure
2
) instead range from
\rho=-0.78
to values near zero, with no condition showing a strong positive correlation and five of six being negative. The pruned model that best preserves perplexity is therefore not the one that best preserves reasoning. Under the functional view, this is expected: perplexity and task margin define distinct redundancy landscapes, and subsets optimal for one are not, in general, optimal for the other.

6
Conclusion

We examine whether layer redundancy in LLM depth pruning is structural or jointly determined by the evaluation objective. By disentangling search and objective across models, objectives, and algorithms, we find evidence consistent with the
functional view
: objectives induce different pruning patterns, perplexity and accuracy rankings do not align, and search algorithms yield similar solutions under a fixed objective. These results suggest that the calibration objective may be more influential than the choice of search algorithm, and that more complex search provides limited additional benefit once the objective is fixed.
