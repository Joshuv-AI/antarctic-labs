Clinical outcomes of teduglutide therapy in children with short bowel syndrome–associated intestinal failure: A single-center experience
Authors: Mariah Mukasa, Hadar Moran-Lev, Lindsey Hornung, Samuel A Kocoshis, Stephanie B Oliveira, Michael A Helmrath, Paul W Wales, Conrad R Cole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundShort bowel syndrome-associated intestinal failure (SBS-IF) requires long-term parenteral nutrition (PN), increasing the risk of complications such as central line associated bloodstream infections, sepsis and liver disease. Teduglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-2 (GLP-2) analog, has been shown to enhance intestinal adaptation and reduce PN dependence, but long-term real-world data in children are limited.ObjectivesTo describe the medium-term clinical outcomes and safety profile of teduglutide treatment in children with short bowel syndrome–associated intestinal failure (SBS-IF).MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted at a single tertiary center. There were 41 children with SBS-IF treated with Teduglutide among the 161children who were managed at the center between 2018 and 2022. Data on PN requirements, growth parameters, and treatment duration were analyzed. The time to PN weaning was assessed using a Cox proportional hazards model.ResultsAt a median follow-up of 29.2 months, 21 patients (51.2%) were successfully weaned off PN, and 24 patients (65%) achieved at least a 20% reduction in PN requirements. The median time to complete PN weaning was 7.3 months. Baseline variables associated with time to PN discontinuation included the percentage of PN-derived calories at treatment initiation and residual small bowel length. No treatment-related adverse events were reported, although 12% of patients required increased PN support over time.ConclusionsTeduglutide reduced PN dependence in children with SBS-IF, with over half achieving enteral autonomy. These findings support its role in intestinal rehabilitation and emphasize the need for individualized treatment strategies based on baseline clinical characteristics associated with response.Keywords: TPN, Intestinal failure, Pediatrics, Teduglutide

Introduction

Intestinal failure (IF) is produced by a variety of disorders that result in the reduction of the functional intestinal mass below that which can sustain life, leading to dependence on parenteral support for a minimum of 60 days within a 74 consecutive day interval [1]. Short Bowel Syndrome (SBS) is the leading cause of intestinal failure and defined as the consequence of any natural loss or surgical resection of small bowel (SB), below a critical value for nutritional supply for adequate growth [2]. Patients with IF often need to be supported with parenteral nutrition (PN) until maximal adaptation of the residual small bowel is achieved [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. Certain patients may require lifelong PN support and prolonged exposure to PN can lead to detrimental health outcomes such as Central Line-Associated Bloodstream Infections (CLABSI) and liver failure [1], [2], [3], [5], [6], [7], [8]. A relatively new therapeutic approach involves the use of Glucagon-Like Peptide−2 (GLP−2), an intestinotrophic growth factor secreted by L-cells in the ileum and proximal colon. GLP−2 plays a crucial role in intestinal adaptation by promoting enterocyte proliferation, enhancing mucosal growth, increasing nutrient absorption, and reducing gastric motility. Teduglutide, a long-acting GLP−2 analog, was developed through a single amino acid substitution that confers resistance to enzymatic degradation, thereby extending its half-life and enhancing its therapeutic potential [9].

The efficacy and safety of Teduglutide in pediatric patients with IF secondary to SBS have been demonstrated in Phase 3 clinical trials of 12- and 24-week durations [10], [11], and further supported in safety studies and post-hoc extension analyses [12], [13]. In 2019, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved Teduglutide for use in pediatric patients aged one year and older, leading to its integration into the clinical management of children with SBS-IF.

This study aims to describe our institutional experience with the use of Teduglutide in children with SBS, focusing on its safety, effectiveness, and median-term outcomes.

Methods

Patient population and study design

A cohort study of children (aged 0–18 years) with Intestinal Failure due to SBS that were treated with Teduglutide was carried out during July -August 2024. We included children that were treated with Teduglutide and managed by the Cincinnati Center of Excellence in Intestinal Rehabilitation (CinCEIR) between January 1st, 2018 to January 31st, 2022. The center team includes pediatric gastroenterologists and pediatric surgeons specialized in intestinal rehabilitation, nurse practitioners and dietitians. Speech therapists and a social worker were available as needed.

Patients who started Teduglutide at an outside hospital or initiated Teduglutide after receiving an intestinal transplant were excluded from the study.

Initiation of Teduglutide therapy was consistent with the FDA-approved indications, specifically in pediatric patients with short bowel syndrome older than one year who exhibited sustained dependence on parenteral nutrition for a minimum duration of 12 months with no significant reduction in parenteral support requirements over at least 2 months before initiation.

The Institutional Review Board of the Medical Center approved this study. As per institutional policy, approval numbers are not assigned for this study. Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of this study. The data retrieved from the medical records were anonymized for research purposes and the researchers were blind to the names of the patients and their parents.

Data collection

The data was the information retrieved from medical files included:

Sociodemographic characteristics: age, gender, race, and ethnicity.

Medical history: Perinatal characteristics (birth weight, gestational age), background diseases, remaining small bowel length (as measured at the time of first surgery), presence of the ileocecal valve, presence and type of ostomy, mucus fistula and prior lengthening procedures.

•Nutritional data - Duration of PN dependency, volumes, and calories from PN, weight and BMI z-score according to the CDC growth charts [14]

Nutritional data - Duration of PN dependency, volumes, and calories from PN, weight and BMI z-score according to the CDC growth charts [14]

Safety outcomes -were defined as any adverse events or treatment-related complications documented in the electronic medical record during follow-up after teduglutide initiation.

Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed using SAS®, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Statistical analyses were primarily descriptive, in keeping with the objective of the study. Variables measured at teduglutide initiation and at the last follow-up visit were summarized using medians and quartiles for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Changes over time were described using paired summaries. To evaluate the time to complete parenteral nutrition (PN) weaning, a Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed.

A Cox proportional hazards model was used as an exploratory analysis to examine associations between selected baseline variables and time to PN weaning. Covariates, based on expert opinion, tested in the model included percentage of total calories derived from PN at baseline, small bowel length, age at Teduglutide start, Ostomy at Teduglutide start, BMI Z-score at Teduglutide start, and diagnosis.

Results

During the study period, 161 children with PN were treated in our intestinal rehabilitation center. Among them, 48 patients received Teduglutide. Five patients were excluded due to initiation of Teduglutide at an external institution, and two additional patients were excluded as they began Teduglutide therapy following an intestinal transplant. Consequently, the final study cohort comprised 41 patients. The median follow-up duration was 29.2 months (IQR: 9.7–44.1, maximum follow-up in the cohort extended to approximately 80 months), and the median duration of PN dependency prior to teduglutide initiation was 3.6 years (IQR: 2.1–5.8).

Thirty-eight patients presented with neonatal short bowel syndrome and the median age when Teduglutide started was 4.1 (IQR: 2.8–5.8) years. Three patients were treated during childhood, and Teduglutide were initiated at a mean of 14 years (IQR: 13–17). Twenty-four of the patients (59%) were male. The most common diagnoses were volvulus (29%), gastroschisis (27%), and necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC; 20%). Before Teduglutide initiation all the patients were PN dependent and received TPN supplementation daily.

Discussion

The current study, which compiles retrospective data from a tertiary multidisciplinary center specializing in the treatment of children with intestinal failure with a relatively long follow up, demonstrates that treatment with Teduglutide resulted in a reduction in PN dependence supporting its role in the management of these patients. More than half of patients achieved enteral autonomy and 65% experienced a meaningful reduction of more than 20% in PN volume. PN weaning occurred progressively, with a median time of 7.3 months to complete discontinuation in those who achieved enteral autonomy. Importantly, although the majority of patients demonstrate an early therapeutic response, a subset exhibits delayed improvement. While most patients demonstrated improvement, a small subset (12%) required an increase in PN support.

Our study aligns with other real-world experiences that have demonstrated the efficacy and safety of Teduglutide treatment. A multicenter study from Spain evaluated the safety and efficacy of Teduglutide in 17 pediatric patients with SBS. Over 12 months, 12 patients (71%) achieved complete parenteral independence, while the remaining patients reduced their intravenous support by 50% [14]. A nationwide retrospective study in Israel assessed 13 children with SBS treated with Teduglutide for a median duration of 18 months. The findings revealed that 62% of the patients responded to the treatment, with two achieving complete weaning from parenteral nutrition (PN) and others experiencing significant reductions in PN requirements [15]. A single-center study from France evaluated 25 children with SBS-IF treated with Teduglutide for 48 weeks and 96% experienced a > 20% reduction in PN requirements by week 24, and 32% achieved complete PN weaning by week 48. Interestingly, this study also showed a progressive increase in plasma citrulline and total energy absorption rate, accompanied by a rise in fasting and postprandial endogenous GLP−2 levels over time, rather than the expected downregulation due to exogenous GLP−2 analog administration. [16].

Several studies have investigated potential adverse effects of Teduglutide treatment. A pooled analysis of four clinical trials reported that the most common adverse events were vomiting (51.7%), pyrexia (43.8%), upper respiratory tract infection (41.6%), and cough (33.7%). Additionally, 39.3% of patients experienced adverse events considered related to Teduglutide, with abdominal pain and vomiting being the most frequent (5.6% each). Serious adverse events related to the treatment, such as ileus, D-lactic acidosis, and gastrointestinal obstruction due to hard stools, were observed in 3.4% of patients, all of which resolved with appropriate management [12]. Wang et al. analyzed real-world adverse events associated with Teduglutide using the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System data. The findings confirm previously known adverse events while also identifying new potential safety signals, including nephrolithiasis, vascular device infection, dehydration, and weight loss [17]. In premarketing studies, there were cases of colorectal polyps described, although the relationship with the use of the drug was unclear [18], [19]. Given the discomfort associated with daily injections, as well as potential short- and long-term side effects, it is important to identify patients who are most likely to benefit from initiating treatment [20], [21].

Baseline variables associated with time to PN discontinuation in our cohort included the percentage of total calories from PN at Teduglutide initiation and bowel length. Both variables are established indicators of underlying disease severity in pediatric short bowel syndrome, reflecting the degree of intestinal insufficiency and the remaining absorptive capacity [1], [6], [7], [8]. In addition, the presence of the colon has been considered an important biological determinant of intestinal rehabilitation outcomes, given its contribution to fluid and electrolyte absorption, energy salvage through fermentation of malabsorbed carbohydrates, and its role as a major site of endogenous GLP−2 activity [3], [5], [13]. Prior studies have suggested that colon-in-continuity may improve the probability of achieving enteral autonomy, including during Teduglutide therapy [13]. Although this association was not demonstrated in our cohort, likely due to sample size limitations, the presence of the colon remains an important factor to consider when interpreting treatment response and in future prospective studies.

This study provides real world data on long-term outcomes in children with SBS treated with Teduglutide. It examines a relatively large and contemporary database that reflects recent approaches toward the treatment of children with Intestinal Failure. The children were diagnosed and monitored in a large tertiary medical center with a relatively long follow-up. Some limitations to our study should be acknowledged. As this is an observational study rather than a controlled clinical trial, no causality between treatment and outcomes could be claimed and hence only associations and clinical observations are reported. Moreover, the reduction in PN requirements could in part be attributed to the natural adaptation of the gut, although most of the patients in our cohort had long-lasting SBS beyond the expected time of spontaneous adaptation.

In conclusion, this analysis of the long-term effect of Teduglutide on intestinal failure patients highlighted the successful reduction of PN from the start of Teduglutide till the last visit. The time to wean off PN was associated with PN percentage of total calories and the bowel length. Teduglutide may be considered for use in patients who meet criteria for therapy. The duration of subsidized treatment should be extended enough to enable thorough assessment and complete realization of the therapeutic benefit.
