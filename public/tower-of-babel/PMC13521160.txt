The hybrid approach as a viable limb salvage strategy for subacute femoropopliteal thrombosis after knee arthroplasty
Authors: Hosaam Nasr, Faisal A. Almudaiheem, Bader S. Alhamdan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute limb ischemia (ALI) is defined as the sudden and rapid decrease in blood flow in the lower limb because of acute occlusion of a peripheral artery or bypass graft. The diagnosis of ALI is challenging and complex, as symptoms are mimicked by postoperative pain. This article presents a case of early symptoms of ALI after total knee arthroplasty, successfully managed by a hybrid open and endovascular procedure. The given case discusses a complex level of revascularization as a limb-saving procedure after total knee arthroplasty. This case highlights a combination of open surgical techniques, such as localized popliteal endarterectomy, and an endovascular approach to achieve blood flow to the limb.

Vascular complications that occur after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) do not occur frequently, but they carry a high risk of limb loss with acute limb ischemia (ALI), which occurs in 0.03% to 0.17% of cases1,2 However, the popliteal artery is prone to injury because of different reasons, including thrombosis, traction, and direct trauma, which are precipitated through peripheral artery diseases (PADs).3 Due to the attribution of ischemic pain to postoperative pain, the diagnosis of ALI is delayed after TKA.4 However, with the changes in health care and improved technology, the management of ALI has evolved significantly over time, with the use of endovascular therapy and open surgery. The recent results from randomized controlled trials, including the Surgery and Thrombolysis for Ischemia of the Lower Extremity and Thrombolysis of Peripheral Arterial Surgery trials, resulted in comparable rates of limb salvage but highlighted a major risk profile, including cases of thrombolysis having a high-risk proportion of bleeding complications.5,6 These randomized trials resulted in the finding that revascularization strategies and interventions depend on the overall duration of ischemia and surgery.5 Particularly in complex cases of acute chronic ischemia with thrombus, a hybrid procedure is sufficient instead of open surgery or endovascular surgery. Therefore, hybrid procedure modalities are used for limb salvage in complex disease patterns.7 This article presents a case of acute popliteal and infrapopliteal thrombosis after TKA, which was successfully treated with a complex hybrid approach involving retrograde endovascular revascularization and thromboendarterectomy. In this context, consent has been obtained for the publication.

Case presentation

This case is of a 67-year-old female patient who was referred to the vascular team with a 1-week history of sunset foot, which is right foot ischemic rest pain and small-sized necrotic patches over the second toe and hallux. Her symptoms began a month ago and were present immediately after her right knee replacement for osteoarthritis. Her medical history included prolonged hypertension and a reduced level of mobility after the knee surgery. However, her symptoms started after the knee replacement. She also had chronic kidney disease, having a baseline serum creatinine of 200 μmol/L and an estimated glomerular filtration rate of 40 mL/min/1.73 m2. The patient was not on any antiplatelet therapy. In her case examination, it was also assessed that her right foot was cool to the touch in comparison with the left foot and showed characteristics of an ischemic foot. The examination also resulted in the detection of palpable pulses in the right common femoral and popliteal arteries; however, there was no palpability of pedal pulses. The contralateral left leg of the patient was asymptomatic, and examination showed biphasic Doppler signals and an unrecordable ankle-brachial index, which is evident of subclinical PAD. According to the Rutherford classification, it is category 5. Considering her history of recent TKA, suspicion of ALI was raised, and a diagnostic angiography was performed. Preoperative venous mapping showed no suitable vein for harvesting.

A lower limb digital subtraction angiogram was performed, which showed a patent common femoral artery, profunda femoris artery, superficial femoral artery, and the proximal (P1) segment of the popliteal artery. In contrast, the midpopliteal (P2) artery was occluded at the level of the knee prosthesis with distal reconstitution. The findings of angiography revealed a subacute thrombosis of the popliteal and infrapopliteal vessels (Fig 1), likely initiated during the knee replacement of the patient and not identified during the postoperative period. The anterior tibial artery (ATA) was cannulated using 0.018", which confirmed a patent dorsalis pedis (DP) artery. A hybrid approach was made, and the popliteal and infrageniculate vessels were exposed using the standard medial approach. A well-organized chronic thrombus was noted inside the P2 segment (Fig 2). A Fogarty catheter was utilized for retrieving the thrombus from the P2 segment for antegrade inflow. A thromboendarterectomy of the popliteal artery was performed with extension of the P3 segment to the peroneal artery. In the patient examination, the origin of ATA was not visualized, and thrombus was retrieved from the patient's peroneal artery; however, the patient's posterior tibial artery remained occluded. A long polytetrafluoroethylene patch was used for repairing the arteriotomy, which extended from the popliteal artery to the peroneal artery (Fig 3). To address the ATA and pedal vessel disease, an endovascular approach by puncturing the dorsalis artery under ultrasound examination and gaining retrograde access into the ATA. Furthermore, buddy wires were used for recanalizing and treating the ATA and the peroneal artery with balloon angioplasty, 3 mm for the ATA and 2.5 mm for the peroneal artery (Fig 4). A 2-mm balloon was used for the plantar arch (Fig 5). The patient had a palpable DP pulse postoperatively; Doppler examination confirmed biphasic signals in the peroneal artery and the use of the plantar arch for retrograde filling of the posterior tibial artery. Postoperatively, the patient was started on aspirin 81 mg and clopidogrel 75 mg. In her 1-month follow-up in the clinic, it was assessed that necrotic patches on the patient's toes were healed, and she had a palpable DP pulse. Discussion

The overall case discussion and assessment of the patient resulted in key learning aspects in the management of vascular complications for the patient following orthopedic surgery. It was found that misattribution of the ischemic symptom results in major delays in the treatment and diagnosis, which increases the risk of amputation for the patient.4 The case discussed above highlights the significance of routine vascular examination, including Doppler assessment and pulse check in the postoperative period, especially in patients with PAD risk factors, including hypertension and diabetes.8 Therefore, in preoperative assessments, it is recommended to carry out ankle-brachial index measurements for patients undergoing TKA for identification and treatment of PAD before elective surgery.9 In the treatment of this patient, it was found that management of acute chronic limb ischemia was challenging, as inefficiencies resulted because of chronic components of thrombus. The benchmark Surgery and Thrombolysis for Ischemia of the Lower Extremity trial resulted in the finding that surgery is a suitable intervention in ischemia of long duration (more than 14 days) as compared with thrombolysis.5 The case presented that an open surgical approach is necessary for the management of thrombus and atherosclerotic plaque. For focal disease, it is recommended to adopt localized popliteal endarterectomy, particularly when endovascular options of treatment cannot be used.10 In the undertaken case, endarterectomy was necessitated by the presence of significant atherosclerotic disease. The use of a hybrid approach demonstrated a key and viable intention in such situations where the endovascular strategy is suboptimal. In this context, the use of a stent to stent a heavily thrombosed and diseased P3 segment presents a high level of risk for distal embolization and early failure. The availability of a vascular team proficient in both complex open and advanced endovascular techniques allowed for a tailored solution that addressed the unique challenges of this patient's pathology. In this instance, a hybrid procedure was the only viable option to achieve complete limb revascularization and avoid a major amputation.

Conclusions

Acute-on-chronic limb ischemia followed by TKA is a limb-threatening emergency, which requires an immediate diagnosis and a tailored intervention. A hybrid approach, combining popliteal thromboendarterectomy with retrograde endovascular reconstruction, is a viable and effective limb-salvage strategy. It demonstrated a crucial aspect and a reminder for orthopedic and vascular surgeons regarding the importance of postoperative vascular surveillance and the underlying need for a versatile, multidisciplinary skill set for the management of complex patterns of peripheral arterial disease and achieving optimal limb salvage.
