 Final Closing Report

What are the major goals of the project:

The goals of the proposed program are
1) to foster a conducive environment which nurtures in students a sense of belonging and being welcomed into
the field of physics. A field in which they can perceive themselves as future scientists, and where they are
perceived by others as physicists who would equally contribute to the advancement of science. A roadmap to
this approach needs a new structure of engagement between the established members in the field and minority
students and their mentors;
2) to provide a nuclear physics research experience with focus on development of knowledge base and technical
skills;
3) to provide professional development opportunities for career development;
4) removal of barriers which have persistently resisted the success of African American students, such as
financial hardships; and
5) to engage the students in community outreach to introduce a wider audience to the career opportunities in
nuclear physics and related field.

What was accomplished?

 1 ) Recruitment and integration into the research community: A total of two cohorts of research
 experience were conducted under this grant. The first year of the program in which a cohort of six
 students were recruited from three Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs). These
 students were Myra Saunders, Maxine Saunders, Kiara Archer (Florida Memorial University),
 Christian Heffner, Michael Henderson (Fisk University), and Erem Ujah (North Carolina Central
 University). These students travelled to Durham, NC in summer of 2022 to participate in this program
 which lasted from May 22 to July 30. Prior to their arrival, the students participated in an orientation
 in which they selected from a choice of topics for summer research and were paired up with mentors
 from Triangle Universities Nuclear Laboratory (TUNL). The participants of the program were
 integrated with the larger cohort of summer students who had arrived at TUNL as part of other
 programs, such as the Research Experience for Undergraduates (REU). All students were housed
 together as part of the community building. Special attention was paid to provide equitable resources
 to the entire community of students to foster an environment which nurtures in students a sense of
 belonging and being welcomed as part of larger community of scientists. The second cohort was
 similarly selected and had the same experiences as the first cohort. The complete list of both cohorts

Opportunity for Training and Professional Development:

Fifteen students participated in the training program. These are the training projects which were assigned
to them:

 1) GEANT4 Simulation to Model Neutron Radiation Dosage The Radiation Countermeasures
 Centers of Research Excellence (RadCCORE) is a network of academic medical centers (Duke
 University, University of North Carolina, and Wake Forest University). The consortium supports
 basic, translational and applied research leading to new medical countermeasures against
 radiological and nuclear exposures due to terrorist attacks. Their driving goal is to increase
 possible agents to detect, mitigate and treat people exposed to deterministic doses of radiation.
 Their research includes studying radiation effects on small animals as part of their development
 of radiation exposure diagnostic tools and treatments. Most radiation effects research is currented
 conducted using x-ray exposures. Because the kinetics of energy and momentum transfer differs
 for neutron interactions from that of photons, it is important to assess the effectiveness of
 diagnostics and treatment for both types of radiation exposure. We are collaborating with
 RadCCORE scientists to expand these studies to neutron irradiation using the neutron beam
 capabilities in the tandem accelerator laboratory at TUNL. The student working on this project
 will develop a GEANT-4 simulation that will be used to determine dosage from neutron
 irradiation on a phantom model of a mouse. The output of the simulation will be used to
 calculate factors for converting beam flux and beam energy to dosage for planning irradiations
 and estimating dosage in real-time during beam irradiations.
 2) Radioisotope Labeling of Small Plants: The DOE Office of Science program in Biological and
 Environmental Research (BER) is interested in supporting research aimed at advancing
 fundamental research or use-inspired technologies of new bioimaging or sensing approaches. The
 research should demonstrate a comparative advantage over state-of-the-art techniques or identify
 biological characteristics that cannot currently be measured. The BER encourages innovative
 “high-risk/high-reward” research that address new imaging and characterization challenges with
 potential for high impact. We are collaborating with groups from Jefferson Laboratory, Duke
 Biomedical Engineering, Duke Biology, and West Virginia University to develop new imaging
 systems for real-time tracking of radioisotope-labeled substances in plants. The student working
 on this project will assist with restoring the capabilities at TUNL for production of 1 IC and the
 safety systems at the Phytotron facility for radioisotope labeling measurements. If time permits,
 the student will participate in an experiment to demonstrate plant labeling capabilities at the
 Phytotron.
 3) Cosmogenic Exposure Tracking with CosmicWatch: High energy cosmic ray muons create
 radioactive isotopes in many materials, including the Germanium used to make detectors

 for LEGEND, a search for neutrinoless double-beta decay. To meet its ultra-low
 background goals, LEGEND-1000 must limit the cosmic ray exposure of this material as
 much as possible and carefully track the muon rate as the detectors are shipped around
 the world. For this project, a student will build multiple CosmicWatch pocket-sized muon
 detectors (http://www.cosmicwatch.lns.mit.edu), take measurements, develop automated
 analysis tools that integrate the results into an exposure tracking database, and use the
 results from a test-run to simulate cosmogenic backgrounds in LEGEND’S Germanium
 detectors. Students undertaking this project will learn hardware skills (including working
 with scintillators, Silicon Photomultipliers, and Arduinos), data analysis in Python, and
 particle physics simulations with Geant4.
 4) RadWatch: DoseNet Summer 2022: For the past several years, TUNL has participated in the
 DOSENET and Radwatch programs that are run by collaborators out of Berkeley. These are
 community driven, DIY radiation and environmental monitoring systems for which the data is
 recorded and posted in a world-wide online database. Past experiments has prepared initial tests
 of this equipment, and monitored naturally occurring radiation backgrounds across Duke’s
 campus, in Durham, Chapel Hill, Greensboro and the beeches of Michigan. This project will
 build several new modules, that include air VOC and particulate sensors as well, and incorporate
 the sensors into the DOSENET network.
 5) Quenching factors and 3D Imaging : At TUNL, our group regularly hosts guest collaborations
 from the Dark Matter and Neutrino communities (3-5 times per year) at the Tandem Van de Graaf
 accelerator so that we may calibrate their detector systems to low-energy nuclear recoils. These
 precision calibrations must be assembled and reassembled regularly, with a mind for the careful
 placement of the geometry and placement of the detectors and collimators. The measurement of
 these positions is often one of the most time-consuming tasks for each deployment. With this
 project, we aim to assess the precision with which the positions of these components can be
 determined using an X-box Kinect 3D imaging camera.

Impact on Development of the Field

Entering the realm of science and mathematics for students from diverse ethnic backgrounds often involves
crossing a cultural boarder. It is argued that when crossing this boarder, minority students often confront
societal barriers that are difficult to navigate. Furthermore, negative stereotypes discourage minority
students from choosing STEM careers, and STEM fields are perceived as subjects reserved for certain
people. This project addresses the consciousness of learning preferences in African American students. It
is established that African American students have a preference towards a relational learning style. Their
relational learning preference is characterized as freedom of movement, variation, creativity, divergent

thinking, inductive reasoning, and focus on people. Moreover, they take cues from the environment, are
people oriented, and prefer harmonious interaction with others and the environment. This project is tunned
to these fiundings and has created a program to employ best take-aways from science education research to
launch a pilot traineeship with the intent to broaden persistence and success of undergraduate minority
students, especially at the Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs), in nuclear sciences and
associated fields. This project will foster a conducive environment which nurtures in students a sense of
belonging and being welcomed into STEM fields. A field in which they can perceive themselves as future
scientists, and where they are perceived by others as physicists who would equally contribute to the
advancement of science.

What is the impact on other disciplines? None reported
What is the impact on the development of human resources:
In addition to the generation of important knowledge for the scientific community, the proposed
collaborative environment at the Triangle Universities Nuclear Laboratory (TUNL) will enable
recruitment and retention of African American students to graduate physics education in TUNL
consortium universities and others. We trained 15 African American students, some of whom are
pursing higher education in STEM fields. The skills acquired during the proposed training will
prepare these students to serve at national laboratories to mitigate the scarcity of
underrepresented minorities in STEM professions. Support for undergraduate students will
address the academic retention shortfalls by providing paid opportunities in mentored research
experiences. The proposed research program arranges for NCCU undergraduate and graduate
students to train in-house and at TUNL, and collaborate with the broader nuclear physics
community by attending conferences and workshops. This program helped contribute positively
to the production of a diverse and competitive scientific workforce by educating and training
underrepresented minorities in STEM.

What is the impact on physical, institutional, and information resources that form infrastructure?

Sub-awards to institutions such as Florida Memorial University helped them boost their STEM
facilities infra-structure. For example, new computation resources were acquired at Florida
Memorial university to support the continued training and education of students when they
returned to their respective institutions from the summer program. Likewise, control hardware
and software were added to training tools available for the students from Fisk university.

What is the impact on technology transfer?

Nothing to Report

What is the impact on society beyond science and technology?

The success of this program resides in providing the training to under-represented minority students
(URM), in skill sets relevant to job placement in areas of national needs. These trainings are tuned
towards placing graduating students in the workforce related to sciences at national laboratories,
national security, information systems, and data sciences. The goal of skills acquired during the
training mitigate the scarcity of underrepresented minorities in workforce. The support for
undergraduate students will address the academic retention shortfalls by providing opportunities in
mentored research experiences. This project contributes positively to the production of a diverse and
competitive scientific workforce by educating and training of underrepresented minorities.
