Arc search in graphs via Szegedy walks
Sho Kubota, Kiyoto Yoshino

Abstract
This paper studies the search for a single arc in a graph using the Szegedy walk. Arc search can be interpreted as finding a quantum particle not only in its position but also with a specific internal state. The quantum walk employed in this study is essentially the model proposed by Segawa and Yoshie for the purpose of edge search. First, we investigate how the symmetry of a graph is reflected in its time evolution matrix, and provide a sufficient condition under which the success probability of the search is independent of the marked arc. In particular, we prove that if a graph is arc-transitive, the success probability is independent of the choice of the marked arc. Next, we analyze path and cycle graphs and show that the quantum search is ineffective for these graphs, whereas it performs well for complete bipartite graphs K n , n K_{n,n} . These results provide a theoretical foundation for studying arc and edge searches on various graphs, while also suggesting new problems concerning the eigenvalue analysis of edge-signed graphs in spectral graph theory. Keywords: quantum search, signed graph, automorphism MSC 2020 subject classifications: 05C50; 81Q99

Introduction

Quantum search has been widely studied as one of the representative applications of quantum computing and is known to provide dramatic speedups over classical search algorithms.
In particular, Grover’s algorithm
[
7
]
demonstrated that a marked item can be found in a database in
O(\sqrt{N})
steps (more precisely,
\Theta(\sqrt{N})
steps), which has attracted significant attention.
Subsequently, it has been shown that Grover’s algorithm can be generalized to efficiently search for multiple marked items
[
3
]
.
These developments have established quantum search as a versatile tool for a variety of algorithms.
This is discussed, for example, in the textbook by Nielsen and Chuang
[
12
]
.

In parallel with this trend,
search algorithms based on quantum walks began to be studied,
such as
[
16
,
17
]
.
Furthermore, subsequent studies have established new frameworks for acceleration that exploit graph structures,
including applications to element distinctness
[
2
]
and triangle detection
[
11
]
.

This paper formulates the search problem from a perspective different from conventional quantum search.
The framework for defining quantum walks on finite-dimensional vector spaces indexed by symmetric arc sets has been extensively studied, but most existing search algorithms mark vertices in a graph as the search targets
[
4
,
9
]
.
Search algorithms for finding edges in graphs have also been discussed recently in
[
14
,
18
]
.
In contrast, this study considers the setting in which an arc is marked as the search target.
In this framework, the search can be interpreted as not only identifying the vertex where the walker is found but also simultaneously detecting internal degrees of freedom, such as the direction of motion or the spin state.

First, we study the relationship between search behavior in discovering a marked arc and symmetry of graphs.
We refer to the probability of finding a marked item, whether a vertex or an arc, as the
success probability
.
In the case of vertex search on vertex-transitive graphs, it is widely understood that the probability of finding a marked vertex is independent of which vertex is marked.
Similarly, in arc search on arc-transitive graphs, the success probability is likewise expected to be independent of the choice of marked arc.
However, this fact is not necessarily obvious and requires a rigorous mathematical justification.
One of our main results is to clarify conditions for symmetry of graphs that guarantee equal probabilities of discovering marked arcs.
In particular, we prove that if a graph is arc-transitive, the success probability is independent of the choice of the marked arc.
See Corollary
3.5
for details.

Next, we analyze the search behavior and computational complexity on path graphs, cycle graphs, and complete bipartite graphs.
We show that our search algorithm is not effective for path or cycle graphs, whereas it performs well on complete bipartite graphs
K_{n,n}
.
More specifically, the success probabilities on the path graphs
P_{n}
and the cycle graphs
C_{n}
are
\frac{1}{2n-2}
and
\frac{1}{2n}
, respectively, and are independent of the measurement time.
On the other hand, for the success probability
p^{*}
at an appropriately chosen measurement time in complete bipartite graphs
K_{n,n}
, it is shown that

p^{*}\geq\frac{1}{2}-\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

holds and the corresponding measurement time is
\Theta(n)
.
Since
K_{n,n}
has
2n^{2}
arcs, this yields a quadratic speedup for this graph.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)

|V|<\infty

E\subset\{\{x,y\}\subset V\mid x\neq y\}

Define
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(\Gamma)=\{(x,y),(y,x)\mid\{x,y\}\in E\}

\Gamma

The origin

We write the inverse arc of

Before defining the time evolution matrix used in the search,

Note that Grover walks are also referred to as arc-reversal walks or arc-reversal Grover walks,

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)

The

.
Clearly,

.
The time evolution matrix

for the Grover walk is defined by

U:=S(2d^{*}d-I),

but we do not use
U
itself.
Instead, we use a modified form of

,
which is introduced in the next section, to perform the search.

Furthermore, we describe relationships between symmetry of graphs and Grover walks.
For the convenience of a broad readership,

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a graph.
A mapping

if and only if

.
We denote the set of all automorphisms of

by

.

Let

be an automorphism of a graph
\Gamma
.
Since

it defines the permutation matrix

given by
(M_{g})_{x,y}=\delta_{x,g(y)}
.
Basic properties of permutation matrices are

M_{g}^{\top}=M_{g}^{-1}=M_{g^{-1}},

(2.1)

and

M_{g}e_{x}=e_{g(x)}

.

The action of automorphisms of a graph is naturally extended to the set of symmetric arcs.
Let

be an automorphism of a graph

,
and let

Define

Clearly,

and it holds that

Moreover, we have

3
Quantum search and symmetry of graphs

In this section, we begin by presenting the quantum search model introduced by Segawa and Yoshie
[
14
]
.
Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a graph, and let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(\Gamma)
denote the set of symmetric arcs.
A mapping
\sigma:\mathcal{A}\to\{\pm 1\}
is said to be a
sign function
if
\sigma(a)=-1
implies
\sigma(a^{-1})=1
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
In other words,
it is not allowed for both
a
and
a^{-1}
to be assigned negative signs.
An arc
a
with
\sigma(a)=-1
is called a
marked arc
.
Our objective is to perform a quantum search for marked arcs.
To this end, we use a time evolution matrix that is a slight modification of the boundary matrix introduced in the previous section.
For a sign function
\sigma
, we define the boundary matrix
d_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{C}^{V\times\mathcal{A}}
associated with
\sigma
by

(d_{\sigma})_{x,a}:=\frac{\sigma(a)}{\sqrt{\deg x}}\delta_{x,t(a)}.

In addition, we define the time evolution matrix
U_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}}
associated with
\sigma
by

U_{\sigma}:=S(2d_{\sigma}^{*}d_{\sigma}-I).

This quantum walk is a special case of the Szegedy walk, and therefore the unitarity of
U_{\sigma}
is guaranteed.
For the proof, see
[
15
, Proposition 1]
.
Yoshie and Yoshino calculate the
(a,b)
-entry of
U_{\sigma}
for
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
as follows
[
18
]
:

(U_{\sigma})_{a,b}=\frac{2\sigma(a^{-1})\sigma(b)}{\deg t(b)}\delta_{o(a),t(b)}-\delta_{a,b^{-1}}.

(3.1)

Using this time evolution matrix, we describe the quantum search for marked arcs.
Throughout this paper, we take the initial state to be the normalized all-ones vector

\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{A}|}}[1,1,\dots,1]^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{A}}.

Starting from
\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}
, the state after
\tau
steps is
U_{\sigma}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}
.
If we measure
U_{\sigma}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}
in the standard basis
\{e_{b}\mid b\in\mathcal{A}\}
, then the probability of observing an arc
b\in\mathcal{A}
is

|(U_{\sigma}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{b}|^{2}.

Accordingly, the success probability at time
\tau
is

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in\mathcal{A}\\
\sigma(b)=-1\end{subarray}}|(U_{\sigma}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{b}|^{2}.

In the remainder of this section, we study the relationship between graph symmetries and the success probability of the quantum search.
We define the
oracle matrix

\mathcal{O}_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}}
by

(\mathcal{O}_{\sigma})_{a,b}:=\sigma(a)\delta_{a,b}.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\sigma
be a sign function.
Then we have
d_{\sigma}=d\mathcal{O}_{\sigma}
.

Proof.

Indeed, for a vertex
x
and an arc
a
, we have

(d\mathcal{O}_{\sigma})_{x,a}=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{A}}d_{x,z}(\mathcal{O}_{\sigma})_{z,a}=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\deg x}}\delta_{x,t(z)}\cdot\sigma(z)\delta_{z,a}=\frac{\sigma(a)}{\sqrt{\deg x}}\delta_{x,t(a)}=(d_{\sigma})_{x,a},

as claimed.
∎

In this paper,
we primarily focus on sign functions that mark only a single arc.
For an arc
z\in\mathcal{A}
, we define the specific sign function
\sigma_{z}:\mathcal{A}\to\{\pm 1\}
by

\sigma_{z}(w):=\begin{cases}1\quad&\text{if $w\neq z$},\\
-1\quad&\text{if $w=z$}.\end{cases}

In this case, the success probability at time
\tau
is

|(U_{\sigma_{z}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{z}|^{2}.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph, and let
a
and
b
be arcs of
\Gamma
.
If an automorphism
g
of
\Gamma
satisfies
\tilde{g}(a)=b
,
then we have

N_{\tilde{g}}^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{b}}N_{\tilde{g}}=\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{a}}.

Proof.

For arcs
z
and
w
, we have

\displaystyle(N_{\tilde{g}}^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{b}}N_{\tilde{g}})_{z,w}

\displaystyle=e_{z}^{*}(N_{\tilde{g}}^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{b}}N_{\tilde{g}})e_{w}=(N_{\tilde{g}}e_{z})^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{b}}(N_{\tilde{g}}e_{w})

\displaystyle=e_{\tilde{g}(z)}^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{b}}e_{\tilde{g}(w)}=(\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{b}})_{\tilde{g}(z),\tilde{g}(w)}=\sigma_{b}(\tilde{g}(z))\delta_{z,w}.

In addition,

\displaystyle\sigma_{b}(\tilde{g}(z))=-1

\displaystyle\iff\tilde{g}(z)=b=\tilde{g}(a)

\displaystyle\iff z=a

\displaystyle\iff\sigma_{a}(z)=-1,

which implies

\sigma_{b}(\tilde{g}(z))\delta_{z,w}=\sigma_{a}(z)\delta_{z,w}=(\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{a}})_{z,w},

as claimed.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph, and let
a
and
b
be arcs of
\Gamma
.
If an automorphism
g
of
\Gamma
satisfies
\tilde{g}(a)=b
,
then we have

N_{\tilde{g}}^{*}U_{\sigma_{b}}N_{\tilde{g}}=U_{\sigma_{a}}.

Proof.

We will show that
U_{\sigma_{b}}N_{\tilde{g}}=N_{\tilde{g}}U_{\sigma_{a}}
.
We have

\displaystyle U_{\sigma_{b}}N_{\tilde{g}}

\displaystyle=S(2d^{*}_{\sigma_{b}}d_{\sigma_{b}}-I)N_{\tilde{g}}

\displaystyle=2S\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{b}}d^{*}d\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{b}}N_{\tilde{g}}-SN_{\tilde{g}}

(by Lemma
3.1
)

\displaystyle=2S\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{b}}d^{*}dN_{\tilde{g}}\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{a}}-N_{\tilde{g}}S

(by Lemma
3.2
and (
2.3
))

\displaystyle=2S\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{b}}d^{*}M_{g}d\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{a}}-N_{\tilde{g}}S

(by (
2.2
))

\displaystyle=2S\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{b}}N_{\tilde{g}}d^{*}d\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{a}}-N_{\tilde{g}}S

(by (
2.2
))

\displaystyle=2SN_{\tilde{g}}\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{a}}d^{*}d\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{a}}-N_{\tilde{g}}S

(by Lemma
3.2
)

\displaystyle=2N_{\tilde{g}}S\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{a}}d^{*}d\mathcal{O}_{\sigma_{a}}-N_{\tilde{g}}S

(by (
2.3
))

\displaystyle=N_{\tilde{g}}S(2d^{*}_{\sigma_{a}}d_{\sigma_{a}}-I)

(by Lemma
3.1
)

\displaystyle=N_{\tilde{g}}U_{\sigma_{a}},

as claimed.
∎

By applying Theorem
3.3
,
we can show that if there exists an automorphism that maps an arc
a
to an arc
b
, then the probabilities of finding
a
and
b
are equal.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph, and let
a
and
b
be arcs of
\Gamma
.
If an automorphism
g
of
\Gamma
satisfies
\tilde{g}(a)=b
,
then for any positive integer
\tau
, we have

(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{a}=(U_{\sigma_{b}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{b}.

In particular,

|(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{a}|^{2}=|(U_{\sigma_{b}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{b}|^{2}.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.3
, we have

(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{a}=e_{a}^{*}(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})=e_{a}^{*}(N_{\tilde{g}}^{*}U_{\sigma_{b}}^{\tau}N_{\tilde{g}}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})=(N_{\tilde{g}}e_{a})^{*}U_{\sigma_{b}}^{\tau}(N_{\tilde{g}}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}).

Since
N_{\tilde{g}}
is a permutation matrix,
it follows that
N_{\tilde{g}}\,\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}=\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}
.
Hence,

(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{a}=e_{\tilde{g}(a)}^{*}U_{\sigma_{b}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}=e_{b}^{*}(U_{\sigma_{b}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})=(U_{\sigma_{b}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{b},

as claimed.
∎

For a graph
\Gamma
,
the automorphism group
\Aut(\Gamma)
acts on the symmetric arc set
\mathcal{A}
via the rule
(g,a)\mapsto\tilde{g}(a)
,
where
g\in\Aut(\Gamma)
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
If this action has only one orbit,
the graph
\Gamma
is said to be
arc-transitive
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph,
and let
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{t}
be the orbits of
\mathcal{A}
under the action of
\Aut(\Gamma)
.
If
a,b\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
for some
i
,
then

|(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{a}|^{2}=|(U_{\sigma_{b}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{b}|^{2}.

In particular, if
\Gamma
is arc-transitive,
then for any arcs
a
and
b
, we have

|(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{a}|^{2}=|(U_{\sigma_{b}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{b}|^{2}.

Of course, the probabilities of discovering arcs from different orbits are generally not equal.

4
Arc search on cycle graphs and path graphs

In this section, we show that the quantum search model defined in the previous section does not perform well on path graphs and cycle graphs.
An intuitive reason for this is that each vertex in these graphs has degree at most
2
, so no interference of probability amplitudes arises from superposition, and the probability of finding a marked arc remains constant over time.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
a
be an arc of
\mathcal{A}:=\mathcal{A}(C_{n})
,
and let
U_{\sigma_{a}}:=U_{\sigma_{a}}(C_{n})
.
Then for any non-negative integer
\tau
and any arc
w
,
we have

(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{w}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}.

In particular,

|(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{a}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2n}.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on
\tau
.
The case
\tau=0
is obvious.
Assume that the statement holds for
\tau-1
.
Then we have

\displaystyle(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{w}

\displaystyle=(U_{\sigma_{a}}U_{\sigma_{a}}^{\tau-1}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{w}=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{A}}(U_{\sigma_{a}})_{w,z}(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{\tau-1}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{A}}\varepsilon(z)\left\{\frac{2\sigma_{a}(w^{-1})\sigma_{a}(z)}{2}\delta_{o(w),t(z)}-\delta_{w,z^{-1}}\right\},

(by (
3.1
))

where
\varepsilon(z)\in\{\pm 1\}
.
Since
\deg o(w)=2
,
there are exactly two arcs whose terminus is
o(w)
.
Let
w^{\prime}
denote the one that is not
w^{-1}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\mathcal{A}}\varepsilon(z)\left\{\frac{2\sigma_{a}(w^{-1})\sigma_{a}(z)}{2}\delta_{o(w),t(z)}-\delta_{w,z^{-1}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\,\,

\displaystyle\varepsilon(w^{-1})\left(\sigma_{a}(w^{-1})\sigma_{a}(w^{-1})-1\right)+\varepsilon(w^{\prime})\sigma_{a}(w^{-1})\sigma_{a}(w^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\,\,

\displaystyle\varepsilon(w^{\prime})\sigma_{a}(w^{-1})\sigma_{a}(w^{\prime}),

and hence

(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{w}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}\cdot\varepsilon(w^{\prime})\sigma_{a}(w^{-1})\sigma_{a}(w^{\prime})=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}.

This completes the induction.
∎

A similar statement can be proved for path graphs, with minor modifications.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
a
be an arc of
\mathcal{A}:=\mathcal{A}(P_{n})
,
and let
U_{\sigma_{a}}:=U_{\sigma_{a}}(P_{n})
.
Then for any non-negative integer
\tau
and any arc
w
,
we have

(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{w}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n-2}}.

In particular,

|(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{a}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2n-2}.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on
\tau
.
The case
\tau=0
is obvious.
Assume that the statement holds for
\tau-1
.
Then we have

(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{\tau}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{w}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n-2}}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{A}}\varepsilon(z)\left\{\frac{2\sigma_{a}(w^{-1})\sigma_{a}(z)}{\deg o(w)}\delta_{o(w),t(z)}-\delta_{w,z^{-1}}\right\},

(4.1)

where
\varepsilon(z)\in\{\pm 1\}
.
We consider path graphs, so
\deg o(w)\in\{1,2\}
.
If
\deg o(w)=2
, the claim can be proved by the same calculation as in the case of cycle graphs.
If
\deg o(w)=1
, the only arc whose terminus is
o(w)
is
w^{-1}
.
Thus, the right-hand side of Equality (
4.1
) becomes

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n-2}}\varepsilon(w^{-1})\left(\frac{2\sigma_{a}(w^{-1})\sigma_{a}(w^{-1})}{1}-1\right)=\frac{\varepsilon(w^{-1})}{\sqrt{2n-2}},

which completes the induction.
∎

5
Arc search on complete bipartite graphs

In this section, we consider the problem of finding a single arc in the complete bipartite graph
K_{n,n}
.
This study does not focus on the complete graphs, since their analysis has already been thoroughly carried out in the work of Segawa and Yoshie
[
14
]
on edge search.
Therefore, the behavior of arc search on complete graphs can be regarded as essentially understood, because it is expected to show the same dynamics as the edge search analyzed in
[
14
]
.
A complete bipartite graph is arc-transitive when the sizes of its partite sets are equal, so by Corollary
3.5
we may, without loss of generality, assume that an arbitrary arc is marked.
In contrast to the previous section, we will see that quantum search is effective on complete bipartite graphs.

To evaluate the success probability, it is necessary to obtain the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the time evolution matrix.
However, as we will see below, this task can be reduced to finding the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of edge-signed graphs.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a graph with a sign function
\sigma
.
We define the
discriminant matrix

T_{\sigma}=T_{\sigma}(\Gamma)\in\mathbb{C}^{V\times V}
by

T_{\sigma}:=d_{\sigma}Sd_{\sigma}^{*}.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
8
, Proposition 1]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph with a sign function
\sigma
,
and let
U_{\sigma}:=U_{\sigma}(\Gamma)
and
T_{\sigma}:=T_{\sigma}(\Gamma)
.
Then it holds that

\{e^{\pm i\theta_{\lambda}}\mid\lambda\in\Spec(T_{\sigma})\}\subset\Spec(U_{\sigma}),

where
\theta_{\lambda}=\cos^{-1}\lambda
.
In addition, the normalized eigenvector of
U_{\sigma}
associated with
e^{\pm i\theta_{\lambda}}
is given by

\varphi_{\pm\lambda}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}|\sin\theta_{\lambda}|}(d_{\sigma}^{*}-e^{\pm i\theta_{\lambda}}Sd_{\sigma}^{*}){\bm{f}},

where
{\bm{f}}
is the normalized eigenvector of
T_{\sigma}
associated with
\lambda
.

In fact, the discriminant matrix
T_{\sigma}
appearing in the above theorem can be interpreted as the normalized adjacency matrix of an edge-signed graph:

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a graph with a sign function
\sigma
,
and let
T_{\sigma}:=T_{\sigma}(\Gamma)
.
Then we have

(T_{\sigma})_{x,y}=\begin{cases}\frac{\sigma(x,y)\sigma(y,x)}{\sqrt{\deg x}\sqrt{\deg y}}\quad&\text{if $\{x,y\}\in E$,}\\
0,\quad&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

for
x,y\in V
.

Proof.

The same statement appears in
[
14
,
18
]
, where no proof was provided.
For completeness, we present a proof here.
For
x,y\in V
, we have

\displaystyle(T_{\sigma})_{x,y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}(d_{\sigma})_{x,a}S_{a,b}(d_{\sigma}^{*})_{y,b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{\sigma(a)}{\sqrt{\deg x}}\delta_{x,t(a)}\cdot\delta_{a,b^{-1}}\cdot\frac{\sigma(b)}{\sqrt{\deg y}}\delta_{y,t(b)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\deg x}\sqrt{\deg y}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sigma(a)\sigma(a^{-1})\delta_{x,t(a)}\delta_{y,t(a^{-1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\deg x}\sqrt{\deg y}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sigma(a)\sigma(a^{-1})\delta_{x,t(a)}\delta_{y,o(a)}.

If
\{x,y\}\in E
,
then we can take
a=(y,x)
as the unique arc satisfying
x=t(a)
and
y=o(a)
.
Hence

(T_{\sigma})_{x,y}=\frac{\sigma(x,y)\sigma(y,x)}{\sqrt{\deg x}\sqrt{\deg y}}.

If
\{x,y\}\not\in E
,
then no arc satisfies
x=t(a)
and
y=o(a)
,
and thus
(T_{\sigma})_{x,y}=0
.
∎

We search for a marked arc in the complete bipartite graph
K_{n,n}
. Since
K_{n,n}
is arc-transitive,
we may assume that an arbitrary arc, say
a=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}(K_{n,n})
, is marked without loss of generality.
Considering the quantum search model determined by the sign function
\sigma_{a}
,
Lemma
5.2
implies that the corresponding discriminant matrix
T_{\sigma_{a}}
is the normalized adjacency matrix of an edge-signed graph with precisely one negative edge.
That is,
T_{\sigma_{a}}
coincides with the normalized adjacency matrix of
K_{n,n}
,
except that the two entries
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
and
(y^{\prime},x^{\prime})
are replaced by
-1/n
instead of
+1/n
.

The eigenvalues and eigenvectors of such matrices have been studied in spectral graph theory.
We present the statement in a form adapted to our setting,
while the essential result is due to Akbari et al.
[
1
]
.

Theorem 5.3

(
[
1
, Theorem 4.1]
)
.

Let
a=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
be an arc of
\mathcal{A}(K_{n,n})
.
The spectrum of
T_{\sigma_{a}}
is

\Spec(T_{\sigma_{a}})=\{[\pm\lambda]^{1},[\pm\mu]^{1},[0]^{2n-4}\},

where
\lambda
and
\mu
are as follows:

\lambda=\frac{n-2+\sqrt{\Delta}}{2n},\quad\mu=\frac{-(n-2)+\sqrt{\Delta}}{2n},\quad\Delta=n^{2}+4n-4.

The normalized eigenvector
{\bm{f}}
associated with the largest eigenvalue
\lambda
is

{\bm{f}}_{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}\cdot\begin{cases}\frac{-n+\sqrt{\Delta}}{2}\quad&\text{if $x\in\{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\}$,}\\
1\quad&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(5.1)

Note that Akbari et al.
[
1
]
determined both the eigenvalues and the eigenvectors of edge-signed complete bipartite graphs in which the negative edges form a matching.
Among their results, we are concerned only with the largest eigenvalue
\lambda
and its corresponding eigenvector
{\bm{f}}
.
Further research on the eigenvalue analysis of edge-signed complete bipartite graphs has been conducted by Pirzada et al.
[
13
]
.

We remark that if
n\geq 3
,
then
(n+1)^{2}<\Delta<(n+2)^{2}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

For all sufficiently large
n
, we have

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}>\frac{\sqrt{2}(n+1)}{2n(n+2)}.

Proof.

Indeed,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\Delta+n\sqrt{\Delta}}}{\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-n^{2}\Delta}}

\displaystyle>\frac{\sqrt{(n^{2}+4n-4)+n(n+1)}}{\sqrt{n}\sqrt{(n^{2}+4n-4)^{2}-n^{2}(n^{2}+4n-4)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{n^{2}+\frac{5}{2}n-2}}{2\sqrt{n^{4}+3n^{3}-8n^{2}+4n}}

\displaystyle>\frac{\sqrt{2}(n+1)}{2\sqrt{n^{4}+4n^{3}+4n^{2}}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}(n+1)}{2n(n+2)},

as claimed.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
{\bm{f}}\in\mathbb{C}^{V(K_{n,n})}
be the vector defined by Equality (
5.1
).
For an arc
z\in\mathcal{A}(K_{n,n})
, we have

(d_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}})_{z}=\frac{\sigma_{a}(z)}{\sqrt{n}}{\bm{f}}_{t(z)},

(5.2)

and

(Sd_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}})_{z}=\frac{\sigma_{a}(z^{-1})}{\sqrt{n}}{\bm{f}}_{o(z)}.

(5.3)

Proof.

For convenience, let
V:=V(K_{n,n})
and
\mathcal{A}:=\mathcal{A}(K_{n,n})
.
Then we have

(d_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}})_{z}=\sum_{x\in V}(d_{\sigma_{a}}^{*})_{z,x}{\bm{f}}_{x}=\sum_{x\in V}\frac{\sigma_{a}(z)}{\sqrt{n}}\delta_{x,t(z)}{\bm{f}}_{x}=\frac{\sigma_{a}(z)}{\sqrt{n}}{\bm{f}}_{t(z)}.

This in turn yields the second relation:

\displaystyle(Sd_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}})_{z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{A}}S_{z,w}(d_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}})_{w}=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{A}}\delta_{z,w^{-1}}(d_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}})_{w}

\displaystyle=(d_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}})_{z^{-1}}=\frac{\sigma_{a}(z^{-1})}{\sqrt{n}}{\bm{f}}_{t(z^{-1})}=\frac{\sigma_{a}(z^{-1})}{\sqrt{n}}{\bm{f}}_{o(z)},

as claimed.
∎

Associated with the largest eigenvalue
\lambda
of
T_{\sigma_{a}}

and its normalized eigenvector
{\bm{f}}
,
we define and shall use the following notation throughout this section:

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle:=\arccos\lambda,

\displaystyle{\bm{\varphi}}_{\pm}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sin\theta}(d_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}-e^{\pm i\theta}Sd_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}){\bm{f}},

\displaystyle{\bm{\beta}}_{\pm}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}({\bm{\varphi}}_{+}\pm{\bm{\varphi}}_{-}),

\displaystyle t^{*}

\displaystyle:=\left\lfloor\frac{\pi}{2\theta}\right\rfloor,

\displaystyle p^{*}

\displaystyle:=|(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{a}|^{2}.

Here,
t^{*}
is the measurement time,
and
p^{*}
is the probability of finding the marked arc
a
at time
t^{*}
.
The justification for taking
t^{*}
as an appropriate measurement time is provided in
[
18
, Remark 4.3]
.
Roughly speaking, if the largest eigenvalue
\lambda
of
T_{\sigma_{a}}
is sufficiently close to
1
,
then we have
p^{*}=|(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{a}|^{2}\approx|{\bm{\beta}}_{+}|^{2}
.
In fact,
{\bm{\beta}}_{+}
is a vector whose probability amplitude is concentrated on
a
and
a^{-1}
,
and hence we will see that
|(U^{t^{*}}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{a}|^{2}
is approximately
1/2
.
In what follows, we provide mathematically rigorous arguments to justify the above rough discussion.

5.1
Technical estimates for the success probability
p^{*}

In this subsection, we present technical estimates needed to evaluate the success probability
p^{*}
.
We prove that the quantity
\sqrt{p^{*}}
is decomposed into three terms,
and estimate these three terms separately.

Lemma 5.6
.

With the above notation, we have

\sqrt{p^{*}}=|(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{a}|\geq|({\bm{\beta}}_{+})_{a}|-\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}(i{\bm{\beta}}_{-})+{\bm{\beta}}_{+}\|-\|\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}-i{\bm{\beta}}_{-}\|.

(5.4)

Proof.

The proof essentially reduces to an application of the triangle inequality.
First,

\displaystyle|(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{a}|

\displaystyle=|-({\bm{\beta}}_{+})_{a}+(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{a}+({\bm{\beta}}_{+})_{a}|

\displaystyle\geq|-({\bm{\beta}}_{+})_{a}|-|(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{a}+({\bm{\beta}}_{+})_{a}|

\displaystyle\geq|({\bm{\beta}}_{+})_{a}|-\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}+{\bm{\beta}}_{+}\|.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}+{\bm{\beta}}_{+}\|

\displaystyle=\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}-U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}(i{\bm{\beta}}_{-})+U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}(i{\bm{\beta}}_{-})+{\bm{\beta}}_{+}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}-U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}(i{\bm{\beta}}_{-})\|+\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}(i{\bm{\beta}}_{-})+{\bm{\beta}}_{+}\|

\displaystyle=\|\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}-i{\bm{\beta}}_{-}\|+\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}(i{\bm{\beta}}_{-})+{\bm{\beta}}_{+}\|.

Therefore,

\displaystyle|(U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})_{a}|

\displaystyle\geq|({\bm{\beta}}_{+})_{a}|-\left(\|\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}-i{\bm{\beta}}_{-}\|+\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}(i{\bm{\beta}}_{-})+{\bm{\beta}}_{+}\|\right)

\displaystyle=|({\bm{\beta}}_{+})_{a}|-\|\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}-i{\bm{\beta}}_{-}\|-\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}(i{\bm{\beta}}_{-})+{\bm{\beta}}_{+}\|,

as claimed.
∎

Hence, evaluating the success probability
p^{*}
reduces to evaluating the three terms on the right-hand side of Inequality (
5.4
).

Proposition 5.7
.

We have

({\bm{\beta}}_{+})_{a}=\frac{(n-\sqrt{\Delta})(3n-2+\sqrt{\Delta})}{2\sqrt{2n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}\sqrt{n^{2}-(n-2)\sqrt{\Delta}}}.

(5.5)

In particular, for all sufficiently large
n
,

|({\bm{\beta}}_{+})_{a}|>\frac{(n-1)(4n-1)}{4\sqrt{2}n(n+2)}.

(5.6)

Proof.

By Lemma
5.5
, we have

(d_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}})_{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{n-\sqrt{\Delta}}{2\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}},\qquad(Sd_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}})_{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{-n+\sqrt{\Delta}}{2\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle({\bm{\varphi}}_{\pm})_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sin\theta}\{(d_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}})_{a}-e^{\pm i\theta}(Sd_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}})_{a}\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sin\theta}\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}\{n-\sqrt{\Delta}-e^{\pm i\theta}(-n+\sqrt{\Delta})\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sin\theta}\cdot\frac{n-\sqrt{\Delta}}{2\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}(1+e^{\pm i\theta}).

Hence,

\displaystyle({\bm{\beta}_{+}})_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left\{({\bm{\varphi}}_{+})_{a}+({\bm{\varphi}}_{-})_{a}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sin\theta}\cdot\frac{n-\sqrt{\Delta}}{2\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}(1+e^{i\theta}+1+e^{-i\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sin\theta}\cdot\frac{n-\sqrt{\Delta}}{2\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}(2+2\cos\theta)

(5.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sin\theta}\cdot\frac{n-\sqrt{\Delta}}{\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}(1+\lambda).

By Theorem
5.3
,

\sin\theta=\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}\theta}=\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{n^{2}-(n-2)\sqrt{\Delta}}}{\sqrt{2}\cdot n}.

From this and Theorem
5.3
again, we have

\displaystyle({\bm{\beta}_{+}})_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}\cdot n}{2\sqrt{n^{2}-(n-2)\sqrt{\Delta}}}\cdot\frac{n-\sqrt{\Delta}}{\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}\left(1+\frac{n-2+\sqrt{\Delta}}{2n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-\sqrt{\Delta})(3n-2+\sqrt{\Delta})}{2\sqrt{2n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}\sqrt{n^{2}-(n-2)\sqrt{\Delta}}}.

Next, we show Inequality (
5.6
).
We have

\displaystyle\left|n-\sqrt{\Delta}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{n^{2}-\Delta}{n+\sqrt{\Delta}}\right|=\frac{4n-4}{n+\sqrt{\Delta}}>\frac{4n-4}{n+(n+2)}=\frac{2n-2}{n+1},

\displaystyle 3n-2+\sqrt{\Delta}

\displaystyle>3n-2+n+1=4n-1,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n^{2}-(n-2)\sqrt{\Delta}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+(n-2)\sqrt{\Delta}}}{\sqrt{n^{4}-(n-2)^{2}\Delta}}

\displaystyle>\frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+(n-2)(n+1)}}{\sqrt{n^{4}-(n-2)^{2}(n^{2}+4n-4)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2n^{2}-n-2}}{4n-4}

\displaystyle>\frac{\sqrt{2}(n-1)}{4(n-1)}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}.

These and Lemma
5.4
yield

\displaystyle|({\bm{\beta}}_{+})_{a}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\cdot\frac{\left|n-\sqrt{\Delta}\right|\cdot(3n-2+\sqrt{\Delta})}{\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}\sqrt{n^{2}-(n-2)\sqrt{\Delta}}}

(by (
5.5
))

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\cdot\frac{2n-2}{n+1}\cdot(4n-1)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}(n+1)}{2n(n+2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-1)(4n-1)}{4\sqrt{2}n(n+2)},

as claimed.
∎

Next, we estimate an upper bound for the second term appearing on the right-hand side of Inequality (
5.4
).
For later use, we state the following elementary identity:

|ie^{i\phi}+1|^{2}=2-2\sin\phi

(5.8)

for a real number
\phi
.

Proposition 5.8
.

We have

\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}(i{\bm{\beta}}_{-})+{\bm{\beta}}_{+}\|\leq\sqrt{\frac{n+2-\sqrt{\Delta}}{n}}.

(5.9)

In particular, for sufficiently large
n
,

\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}(i{\bm{\beta}}_{-})+{\bm{\beta}}_{+}\|<\sqrt{\frac{8}{n(2n+3)}}.

(5.10)

Proof.

First, we have

\displaystyle\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}(i{\bm{\beta}}_{-})+{\bm{\beta}}_{+}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}\left\{\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}({\bm{\varphi}}_{+}-{\bm{\varphi}}_{-})\right\}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}({\bm{\varphi}}_{+}+{\bm{\varphi}}_{-})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(e^{it^{*}\theta}{\bm{\varphi}}_{+}-e^{-it^{*}\theta}{\bm{\varphi}}_{-})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}({\bm{\varphi}}_{+}+{\bm{\varphi}}_{-})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}e^{it^{*}\theta}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right){\bm{\varphi}}_{+}+\left(-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}e^{-it^{*}\theta}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right){\bm{\varphi}}_{-}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(ie^{it^{*}\theta}+1\right){\bm{\varphi}}_{+}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(-ie^{-it^{*}\theta}+1\right){\bm{\varphi}}_{-}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left|ie^{it^{*}\theta}+1\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left|-ie^{-it^{*}\theta}+1\right|^{2}.

Since
-ie^{-it^{*}\theta}+1
is the complex conjugate of
ie^{it^{*}\theta}+1
, their absolute values are equal.
Thus,

\frac{1}{2}\left|ie^{it^{*}\theta}+1\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left|-ie^{-it^{*}\theta}+1\right|^{2}=|ie^{it^{*}\theta}+1|^{2}.

By Equality (
5.8
), we obtain

|ie^{it^{*}\theta}+1|^{2}=2(1-\sin t^{*}\theta)=2\left\{1-\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-t^{*}\theta\right)\right\}=2\left\{1-\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\left\lfloor\frac{\pi}{2\theta}\right\rfloor\theta\right)\right\}.

Here,
0<\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}
and
\frac{\pi}{2\theta}-1\leq\lfloor\frac{\pi}{2\theta}\rfloor\leq\frac{\pi}{2\theta}
imply

0\leq\frac{\pi}{2}-\left\lfloor\frac{\pi}{2\theta}\right\rfloor\theta\leq\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}.

Hence,

\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}(i{\bm{\beta}}_{-})+{\bm{\beta}}_{+}\|^{2}\leq 2(1-\cos\theta)=2(1-\lambda)=\frac{n+2-\sqrt{\Delta}}{n},

where the last equality follows from Theorem
5.3
.

We proceed to show Inequality (
5.10
).
We have

\displaystyle\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}(i{\bm{\beta}}_{-})+{\bm{\beta}}_{+}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{n+2-\sqrt{\Delta}}{n}}

(by (
5.9
))

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(n+2)^{2}-\Delta}{n(n+2+\sqrt{\Delta})}}

\displaystyle<\sqrt{\frac{8}{n(n+2+(n+1))}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{8}{n(2n+3)}},

as claimed.
∎

Finally, we estimate an upper bound for the third term.

Proposition 5.9
.

We have

\|\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}-i{\bm{\beta}_{-}}\|=\sqrt{2-\frac{\sqrt{2}(n-1)(\sqrt{\Delta}+n)}{n\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}}.

In particular, for sufficiently large
n
,

\|\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}-i{\bm{\beta}_{-}}\|<\sqrt{2-\frac{(n+1)(n-1)(2n+1)}{n^{2}(n+2)}}.

(5.11)

Proof.

First, we confirm that

i{\bm{\beta}_{-}}=Sd_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}}

(5.12)

and that
i{\bm{\beta}_{-}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}
.
Indeed,

\displaystyle{\bm{\beta}_{-}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}({\bm{\varphi}}_{+}-{\bm{\varphi}}_{-})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sin\theta}\left\{(d_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}-e^{i\theta}Sd_{\sigma_{a}}^{*})-(d_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}-e^{-i\theta}Sd_{\sigma_{a}}^{*})\right\}{\bm{f}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\cdot\frac{-2i\sin\theta}{\sqrt{2}\sin\theta}Sd_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}}

\displaystyle=-iSd_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\|\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}-i{\bm{\beta}_{-}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}-i{\bm{\beta}_{-}},\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}-i{\bm{\beta}_{-}})

\displaystyle=\|\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}\|^{2}+\|i{\bm{\beta}_{-}}\|^{2}-(\,\bm{\mathrm{j}},i{\bm{\beta}_{-}})-(i{\bm{\beta}_{-}},\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})

\displaystyle=2-2(i{\bm{\beta}_{-}},\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})

\displaystyle=2-2(Sd_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}},\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}).

(by (
5.12
))

By Lemma
5.5
, we obtain

\displaystyle(Sd_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}})_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{a}(z^{-1})}{\sqrt{n}}{\bm{f}}_{o(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}\cdot\begin{cases}-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta}-n}{2}\quad\quad&\text{if $z=a^{-1}$,}\\
\frac{\sqrt{\Delta}-n}{2}\quad&\text{if $z\neq a^{-1}$ and $o(z)\in\{o(a),t(a)\}$,}\\
1\quad&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The number of arcs
z
satisfying each of the above conditions is, from top to bottom,
1
,
2n-1
, and
2n(n-1)
, respectively.
Thus,

\displaystyle(Sd_{\sigma_{a}}^{*}{\bm{f}},\,\bm{\mathrm{j}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n^{2}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}\left(-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta}-n}{2}+(2n-1)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\Delta}-n}{2}+2n(n-1)\cdot 1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-1)(\sqrt{\Delta}+n)}{\sqrt{2}\cdot n\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}.

Therefore, we have

\|\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}-i{\bm{\beta}_{-}}\|^{2}=2-2\cdot\frac{(n-1)(\sqrt{\Delta}+n)}{\sqrt{2}\cdot n\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}=2-\frac{\sqrt{2}(n-1)(\sqrt{\Delta}+n)}{n\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}.

).
It is sufficient to show that

\frac{\sqrt{2}(n-1)(\sqrt{\Delta}+n)}{n\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}>\frac{(n+1)(n-1)(2n+1)}{n^{2}(n+2)}.

, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}(n-1)(\sqrt{\Delta}+n)}{n\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}

\displaystyle>\frac{\sqrt{2}(n-1)\{(n+1)+n\}}{n}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}(n+1)}{2n(n+2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+1)(n-1)(2n+1)}{n^{2}(n+2)},

as claimed.
∎

5.2
A brief review of Big-Theta notation

This subsection briefly reviews the definition and basic properties of Big-Theta notation,
which is used to describe the asymptotic order of functions.
Throughout this paper,
we denote the set of positive integers by
\mathbb{N}
.
Let
f,g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be functions with
f(n),g(n)>0
for all sufficiently large
n
.
We say that
f
and
g
are
of the same order
,
written as
f(n)=\Theta(g(n))
,
if there exist a positive integer
N
and positive constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that the implication

n\geq N\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_{1}g(n)\leq f(n)\leq c_{2}g(n)

holds.
If the quotient
f/g
tends to a positive finite limit,
then the two functions are of the same order:

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
f,g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be functions with
f(n),g(n)>0
for all sufficiently large
n
.
If

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{g(n)}=c\,(>0)

then
f(n)=\Theta(g(n))
.

Proof.

The limit
c
exists and is positive,
and hence let
\varepsilon:=c/2>0
.
Then there exists a positive integer
N
such that for all
n\geq N
,

\left|\frac{f(n)}{g(n)}-c\right|<\varepsilon=\frac{c}{2}.

This inequality implies

\frac{c}{2}g(n)<f(n)<\frac{3c}{2}g(n),

and therefore the claim follows.
∎

5.3
The order of the measurement time
t^{*}
and an asymptotic estimate of the success probability
p^{*}

In this subsection, we derive an asymptotic estimate of the measurement time
t^{*}
and the success probability
p^{*}
.

Lemma 5.11
.

For
x\in(0,1)
, we have

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-x)}}-1\leq\frac{1}{\arccos x}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-x)}}.

Theorem 5.12
.

We have

\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n(2n+3)}{8}}-2\right)\leq t^{*}\leq\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{n(n+2)}.

In particular,
t^{*}=\Theta(n)
.

Proof.

First, we establish the upper bound.
We have

\displaystyle t^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\pi}{2\arccos\lambda}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-\lambda)}}

(by Lemma
5.11
)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{n}{n+2-\sqrt{\Delta}}}

(by Theorem
5.3
)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{n(n+2+\sqrt{\Delta})}{(n+2-\sqrt{\Delta})(n+2+\sqrt{\Delta})}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{n(n+2+n+2)}{8}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{n(n+2)}.

The lower bound can be proven as follows.

\displaystyle t^{*}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\pi}{2\arccos\lambda}-1

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-\lambda)}}-1\right)-\frac{\pi}{2}

(by Lemma
5.11
)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{n+2-\sqrt{\Delta}}}-2\right)

(by Theorem
5.3
)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n(n+2+\sqrt{\Delta})}{(n+2-\sqrt{\Delta})(n+2+\sqrt{\Delta})}}-2\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n(n+2+n+1)}{8}}-2\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n(2n+3)}{8}}-2\right).

Furthermore, dividing every term of the obtained inequality by
n
yields

\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}+\frac{3}{8n}}-\frac{2}{n}\right)\leq\frac{t^{*}}{n}\leq\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{1+\frac{2}{n}}.

Since the leftmost and rightmost terms converge to
\frac{\pi}{4}
as
n\to\infty
,
Lemma
5.10
implies that
t^{*}=\Theta(n)
.
∎

Note that the number of arcs is
|\mathcal{A}(K_{n,n})|=2n^{2}=\Theta(n^{2})
, and thus a quadratic speedup is achieved.
Next, we derive an asymptotic estimate of
p^{*}
.

Lemma 5.13
.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function with
f(n)>0
for all sufficiently large
n
.
If

f(n)\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}-\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

then

f(n)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}-\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Proof.

From our assumption, there exists a function
g(n)=\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})
such that

f(n)\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}-g(n),

(5.13)

and hence there exist a positive integer
N
and positive constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that if
n>N
, then

\frac{c_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\leq g(n)\leq\frac{c_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}

(5.14)

holds.
If
n>\max\{N,2c_{2}^{2}\}
, then

g(n)\leq\frac{c_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}<\frac{c_{2}}{\sqrt{2c_{2}^{2}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

Thus, squaring both sides of Inequality (
5.13
) preserves the inequality, and we obtain

f(n)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}-\left(\sqrt{2}g(n)-g(n)^{2}\right).

Our remaining task is to show that
\sqrt{2}g(n)-g(n)^{2}=\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})
.
We claim that if
n>\max\{N,2c_{2}^{2},c_{2}^{4}/c_{1}^{2}\}
, then

\frac{(\sqrt{2}-1)c_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\leq\sqrt{2}g(n)-g(n)^{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}c_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}.

For the upper bound, Inequality (
5.14
) immediately gives

\sqrt{2}g(n)-g(n)^{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}c_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}-\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{n}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}c_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}.

For the lower bound, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}g(n)-g(n)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}c_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}-\frac{c_{2}^{2}}{n}

(by (
5.14
))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\sqrt{2}c_{1}-\frac{c_{2}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\sqrt{2}c_{1}-c_{2}^{2}\cdot\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}^{2}}\right)

(since
n>c_{2}^{4}/c_{1}^{2}
)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sqrt{2}-1)c_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Therefore,
\sqrt{2}g(n)-g(n)^{2}=\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})
,
which completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 5.14
.

We have

p^{*}\geq\frac{1}{2}-\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Proof.

First,

\displaystyle\sqrt{p^{*}}

\displaystyle\geq|({\bm{\beta}}_{+})_{a}|-\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}(i{\bm{\beta}}_{-})+{\bm{\beta}}_{+}\|-\|\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}-i{\bm{\beta}}_{-}\|

(by Lemma
5.6
)

By Propositions
5.7
,
5.8
, and
5.9
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{p^{*}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(n-1)(4n-1)}{4\sqrt{2}n(n+2)}-\sqrt{\frac{8}{n(2n+3)}}-\sqrt{2-\frac{(n+1)(n-1)(2n+1)}{n^{2}(n+2)}}.

Since

\displaystyle\frac{(n-1)(4n-1)}{4\sqrt{2}n(n+2)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}-\frac{13\sqrt{2}n-\sqrt{2}}{8n(n+2)},

\displaystyle\sqrt{2-\frac{(n+1)(n-1)(2n+1)}{n^{2}(n+2)}}=\sqrt{\frac{3n^{2}+2n+1}{n^{2}(n+2)}},

we have

\sqrt{p^{*}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}-\left\{\frac{13\sqrt{2}n-\sqrt{2}}{8n(n+2)}+\sqrt{\frac{8}{n(2n+3)}}+\sqrt{\frac{3n^{2}+2n+1}{n^{2}(n+2)}}\right\}.

Here, define

f(n):=\frac{13\sqrt{2}n-\sqrt{2}}{8n(n+2)}+\sqrt{\frac{8}{n(2n+3)}}+\sqrt{\frac{3n^{2}+2n+1}{n^{2}(n+2)}}.

Clearly,
f(n)>0
, and

\frac{f(n)}{1/\sqrt{n}}=\frac{\frac{13\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{n}}-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{n\sqrt{n}}}{8(1+\frac{2}{n})}+\sqrt{\frac{\frac{8}{n}}{2+\frac{3}{n}}}+\sqrt{\frac{3+\frac{2}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}}{1+\frac{2}{n}}}\to\sqrt{3}

as
n\to\infty
.
Thus, Lemma
5.10
yields
f(n)=\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})
,
that is,
\sqrt{p^{*}}\geq 1/\sqrt{2}-\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})
.
Therefore, Lemma
5.13
completes the proof.
∎

From this theorem,
we see that the lower bound of the success probability
p^{*}
converges to
1/2
at a rate of
1/\sqrt{n}
.

In previous studies
[
14
,
18
]
on edge search using the same model, the corresponding classical search problems can also be formulated in a natural way, and comparisons with quantum search are therefore discussed.
On the other hand, in the arc search treated in this paper, the coordinate of the quantum state corresponding to a marked arc is interpreted as a pair consisting of the position of the walker and its internal state.
Since no classical search problem directly corresponds to such a setting, this paper focuses solely on the analysis of quantum walks.

6
Remarks and discussions

In this paper, we prove that the limiting value of the lower bound on the success probability
p^{*}
for the search on the complete bipartite graphs
K_{n,n}
is
1/2
in time
\Theta(n)
.
In the remainder of this paper, we discuss the reason why the value
1/2
appears in the lower bound.
It can be shown that the second and third terms on the right-hand side of Inequality (
5.4
) satisfy

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|U_{\sigma_{a}}^{t^{*}}(i{\bm{\beta}}_{-})+{\bm{\beta}}_{+}\|=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\,\bm{\mathrm{j}}-i{\bm{\beta}}_{-}\|=0

as a consequence of Propositions
5.8
and
5.9
.
Therefore, the lower bound can be regarded as being primarily determined by the vector
{\bm{\beta}_{+}}
.
Furthermore, it can be seen that the amplitude is concentrated at the arcs
a
and
a^{-1}
when
n
is sufficiently large, as shown below:

Lemma 6.1
.

We have

({\bm{\beta}_{+}})_{a^{-1}}=-({\bm{\beta}_{+}})_{a}.

Proof.

Indeed, Lemma
5.5
and Theorem
5.3
yield

\displaystyle(d^{*}_{\sigma_{a}}{\bm{f}})_{a^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{a}(a^{-1})}{\sqrt{n}}{\bm{f}}_{t(a^{-1})}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\Delta}-n}{2},

\displaystyle(Sd^{*}_{\sigma_{a}}{\bm{f}})_{a^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{a}(a)}{\sqrt{n}}{\bm{f}}_{o(a^{-1})}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\Delta}-n}{2},

and hence

\displaystyle({\bm{\varphi}}_{\pm})_{a^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sin\theta}\left\{(d^{*}_{\sigma_{a}}{\bm{f}})_{a^{-1}}-e^{\pm i\theta}(Sd^{*}_{\sigma_{a}}{\bm{f}})_{a^{-1}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sin\theta}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\Delta}-n}{2\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}(1+e^{\pm i\theta}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle({\bm{\beta}_{+}})_{a^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(({\bm{\varphi}}_{+})_{a^{-1}}+({\bm{\varphi}}_{-})_{a^{-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\Delta}-n}{2\sin\theta\cdot 2\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}(2+2\cos\theta)

\displaystyle=-({\bm{\beta}_{+}})_{a},

(by (
5.7
))

as claimed.
∎

Proposition 6.2
.

We have

\lim_{n\to\infty}|({\bm{\beta}}_{+})_{z}|^{2}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\quad&\text{$z\in\{a,a^{-1}\}$,}\\
0\quad&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

First, we show that

|({\bm{\beta}_{+}})_{a}|<\frac{\sqrt{2n^{2}+6n-4}}{2n+1}

for all sufficiently large
n
.
Indeed, since

\displaystyle|n-\sqrt{\Delta}|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{n^{2}-\Delta}{n+\sqrt{\Delta}}\right|=\frac{4n-4}{n+\sqrt{\Delta}}<\frac{4(n-1)}{n+(n+1)}=\frac{4(n-1)}{2n+1},

\displaystyle 3n-2+\sqrt{\Delta}

\displaystyle<3n-2+(n+2)=4n,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\Delta+n\sqrt{\Delta}}}{\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-n^{2}\Delta}}<\frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+4n-4+n(n+2)}}{\sqrt{4n^{3}+12n^{2}-32n+16}}

\displaystyle<\frac{\sqrt{2n^{2}+6n-4}}{\sqrt{4n^{3}}}=\frac{\sqrt{2n^{2}+6n-4}}{2n\sqrt{n}},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n^{2}-(n-2)\sqrt{\Delta}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+(n-2)\sqrt{\Delta}}}{\sqrt{n^{4}-(n-2)^{2}\Delta}}<\frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+(n-2)(n+2)}}{4(n-1)}<\frac{\sqrt{2}\cdot n}{4(n-1)},

we have

\displaystyle|({\bm{\beta}_{+}})_{a}|

\displaystyle=\frac{|n-\sqrt{\Delta}|(3n-2+\sqrt{\Delta})}{2\sqrt{2n}\sqrt{\Delta-n\sqrt{\Delta}}\sqrt{n^{2}-(n-2)\sqrt{\Delta}}}

(by Proposition
5.7
)

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2n}}\cdot\frac{4(n-1)}{2n+1}\cdot 4n\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2n^{2}+6n-4}}{2n\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}\cdot n}{4(n-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2n^{2}+6n-4}}{2n+1}.

From this and Inequality (
5.6
), we have

\frac{(n-1)(4n-1)}{4\sqrt{2}n(n+2)}<|({\bm{\beta}_{+}})_{a}|<\frac{\sqrt{2n^{2}+6n-4}}{2n+1}.

In particular,

\lim_{n\to\infty}|({\bm{\beta}_{+}})_{a}|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

In addition, by Lemma
6.1
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}|({\bm{\beta}_{+}})_{a}|^{2}=\lim_{n\to\infty}|({\bm{\beta}_{+}})_{a^{-1}}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}.

On the other hand, since
\|{\bm{\beta}_{+}}\|=1
, we have

0\leq\sum_{z\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{a,a^{-1}\}}|({\bm{\beta}_{+}})_{z}|^{2}=1-|({\bm{\beta}_{+}})_{a}|^{2}-|({\bm{\beta}_{+}})_{a^{-1}}|^{2}\to 0

as
n\to\infty
.
Therefore, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}|({\bm{\beta}}_{+})_{z}|^{2}=0

for
z\not\in\{a,a^{-1}\}
, which completes the proof.
∎

One of the key factors that made this research possible, particularly the computations presented in Section
5
, was the work of Akbari et al.
[
1
]
on the eigenvalue analysis of edge-signed graphs.
Their study is also utilized in the work of Segawa and Yoshie
[

.
In future studies on quantum search for edges or arcs in various graphs,
it will be essential to begin with the eigenvalue analysis of edge-signed graphs.
Therefore, we believe that it is important to investigate in advance the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of various edge-signed graphs, also from the perspective of spectral graph theory.
