Rare case of quadricuspid aortic valve: A case report
Authors: Anika Chowdhury, Md Deluwar Hussen, Md Mahfuzur Rahman Zeehad, Jannatul Ferdous, Maruf Mustakim Fahim, Mst Jabunnahar Jaba, Shanzida Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuadricuspid aortic valve (QAV) is a rare congenital cardiac anomaly that typically presents in any age with progressive aortic regurgitation. A 55-year-old Bangladeshi woman came with a 3 weeks history of progressive dyspnea, intermittent chest tightness, and palpitations. Trans-esophageal echocardiography confirmed a QAV with central leaflet malcoaptation and mild aortic root dilatation. Further assessment with cardiac computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging enabled detailed anatomical characterization, severity assessment, and operative planning. Multimodality imaging is crucial for accurate diagnosis, risk stratification and surgical decision-making. This case highlights the value of comprehensive imaging and individualized surgical planning in achieving favorable outcomes in patients with QAV.Keywords: Quadricuspid aortic valve (QAV), Congenital anomaly, Aortic valve anomaly, Case report

Introduction

A quadricuspid aortic valve (QAV) is indisputably a rare anomaly, with an incidence of less than 0.05% [1], and the first-ever case was announced by 1862 by Balinton and to date there have been marginally over 200 reported cases worldwide alluding to its rarity [2]. Detailed preoperative assessment is critical for optimal surgical planning and favorable outcomes, and minimally invasive valve replacement represents a viable option in selected patients (Fig. 1).

Trans-esophageal echocardiography (mid-esophageal aortic valve short-axis view) demonstrating a quadricuspid aortic valve with 4 distinct cusps visible during systole, forming a characteristic 4-leaf clover appearance.

Here we present a 55-year-old South Asian woman with a prior history of an incidentally detected cardiac murmur presented with a 3-weeks breathlessness, chest tightness, palpitations and later on who was found to have QAV during a routine check-up, highlighting the importance of routine physical examination, especially on cardiac auscultation. That’s a clinical art that is progressively being replaced by modern investigations, yet remains critical for diagnosis. The case has been reported in line with the CARE 2017 guidelines [3].

Case presentation

A 55-year-old Bangladeshi woman presented with a 3-weeksprogression of exertional breathlessness, intermittent chest tightness and palpitations. She had no past history of disease, surgery, vaccination, blood transfusion, allergy or any drug history. On examination, her body temperature was normal and her blood pressure was 118/56 mm Hg with a stable heart rate. On cardiac auscultation with a regular rhythm and a grade 3/4 early diastolic murmur on the right parasternal border.

After the finding of the murmur, echocardiogram was performed because of increasing dyspnea. It demonstrated normal left ventricular systolic performance, severe left ventricular dilatation. At first we did ECG then followed by an echocardiogram in local hospitals then she was referred for regional cardiac specialized hospital. Trans-esophageal echocardiography (TEE) demonstrated a dilated left ventricle with preserved systolic function. The left ventricular end diastolic measurement 6.4 cm and the left ventricular end systolic diameter measured 4.2 cm. The calculated left ventricular ejection fraction was approximately 60% using Simpson biplane method. Left ventricular end diastolic volume was estimated 166 mL and end systolic volume 67 mL. Color dropper imaging revealed severe aortic regurgitation with central jet. The vena contracta measured 0.65 cm and pressure half time was approximately 280 ms, consistent with severe regurgitation. There was mild dilatation of the aortic root measuring 3.9 cm. TEE demonstrated a quadricuspid aortic valve with 4 distinct cusps and central malcoaptation of the leaflets resulting in severe regurgitation (Fig. 1).

To more clearly define aortic root anatomy and exclude congenital or acquired associated structural abnormalities, contrast cardiac CT angiography (CCTA) and cardiac MRI were performed. In CTA, Diastolic malcoaptation with abnormal central commissural configuration was consistent with aortic regurgitation. Aortic root was mildly dilated with no significant calcification was seen. Coronary artery was normal in origin and course. CT has shown a QAV without calcific burden (Fig. 2), as well as the accurate measurement of the ascending aorta measuring 3.9 cm (Fig. 3). Cardiac CT & MRI was also consistent with QAV of Type A morphology showing 4 relatively equal cusp. Prominent early diastolic central regurgitant jet flow from aorta to LVOT was seen on cine imaging. Phase contrast imaging confirmed severe aortic regurgitation with regurgitant fraction of 52% & regurgitant volume of 67 mL/beat. Left ventricle was significantly dilated with preserved ejection fraction (EF = 60%). Mild aortic root dilatation was measured about 3.9 cm. There was also not enough late gadolinium enhancement to suggest any fibrosis (Fig. 4).

Computed tomography (CT) confirmed the presence of a QAV with no significant calcific burden, aortic cusps are similar in size & Hurwitz and Roberts Type A QAV.

Contrast-enhanced cardiac CT angiography shows accurate measurements of the ascending aorta is 3.9 cm.

Hurwitz and Roberts classification of quadricuspid aortic valve.

Intraoperative TEE again confirmed the diagnosis of QAV with severe regurgitation. The patient proceeded to minimally invasive aortic valve replacement, receiving a 23-mm pericardial bio-prosthetic valve. Her postoperative recovery was largely uncomplicated, except for a brief, self-limiting episode of atrial fibrillation, nothing was observed in further follow-up. Her dyspnea improved significantly and murmur disappeared.

Discussion

Quadricuspid aortic valve (QAV) is an uncommon defect of the aortic valve, initially reported by Balington in 1862 during an autopsy examination [4]. QAV can appear as solitary abnormality, but it may also occur with other congenital cardiac abnormalities, such as coronary artery anomalies, atrial or ventricular septal defects, patent ductus arteriosus, mitral valve prolapse, tetralogy of Fallot, partial atrioventricular canal defects, or even an associated ascending aortic aneurysm [5]. Over the years, several attempts have been made to categorize its anatomical variations. Hurwitz and Roberts later proposed a classification system that outlines 7 anatomical subtypes of QAV, labeled A through G, based on how the cusps compare in size [6]. On this case, the valve was classified as Hurwitz and Roberts Type A QAV.

QAVs are clinically silent in younger people, but as patients get older, they tend to develop aortic insufficiency owing to progressive leaflet fibrosis and poor coaptation. According to Tutarel and Westhoff-Bleck, aortic regurgitation (AR) is found in nearly three-quarters of patients with QAV, while isolated stenosis is rare (0.7%); mixed stenosis and regurgitation is seen in 8.4%, and only 16.2% of valves are functionally intact [7]. Despite these observations, no recent studies have clarified whether specific anatomic subtypes carry a higher risk of AR and it is still uncertain whether the existing classification systems have meaningful prognostic value.

Our patient’s presentation is consistent with this pattern of progressive valve dysfunction observed by her worsening breathing difficulty, a transthoracic echocardiogram showed preserved left ventricular systolic function but marked ventricular dilatation and severe aortic regurgitation with a vena contracta of 0.65 cm. Because the valve morphology was not clearly defined on TTE, a trans-esophageal echocardiogram (TTE) was performed, which confirmed a QAV with central leaflet malcoaptation and mild aortic root dilatation measuring 3.9 cm.

Depending on how well the valve functions and whether other cardiac anomalies are present, patients with QAV may remain completely asymptomatic or develop symptoms such as chest discomfort, syncope, shortness of breath, palpitations, fatigue, or even peripheral edema [8]. Progressive aortic regurgitation is the most frequently encountered clinical outcome, although other complications including aortic stenosis, infective endocarditis and thoracic aortic dilation or aneurysm have also been described.

Since many people with QAV never develop symptoms, regular follow-up becomes especially important to understand how the condition evolves over time. This is particularly true for younger patients, who are more likely to develop significant aortic regurgitation and may eventually need surgery [9]. Surgical intervention is typically considered when the valve becomes significantly dysfunctional usually in cases of severe stenosis or regurgitation and this accounts for roughly one-fifth of all patients with QAV. In 1 cohort of 186 patients, almost half underwent surgery in their 50s or early 60s, with an average of just over 4 years between the initial diagnosis and the procedure [7]. Interestingly, long-term mortality did not differ much between those who had surgery and those who did not [10]. Still, because QAV is so uncommon, we have limited data on its long-term course, and much of its natural history remains uncertain, highlighting the need for further research.

Trans-esophageal echocardiography revealed preserved left ventricular systolic function with marked ventricular dilatation and severe aortic regurgitation. TEE confirmed a quadricuspid aortic valve with central leaflet malcoaptation and mild aortic root dilatation. Further assessment with cardiac CT and MRI also confirms the QAV & enabled detailed anatomical characterization, severity assessment, and operative planning. Those are very accurate and useful tools for diagnosis & intervention for such a rare case. In lower economic countries such as Bangladesh, similar cases often remain undiagnosed due to limited resources and restricted access to advanced diagnostic facilities. Consequently, patients may be misdiagnosed and treated as having respiratory distress. This case underscores the importance of clinical awareness, timely referral and appropriate investigation in resource limited area.

Conclusion

The QAV is evidently a rare cardiac malformation. However, the development of this valvular defect occurred in the embryonic period, patients often remain clinically silent until adulthood due to its insidious disease trajectory. Progressive valvular dysfunction in QAV is demonstrated by the patient’s delayed development of exertional dyspnea. This case illustrates the importance of a complete physical assessment. In this case, the detection of a murmur led to a diagnosis of a structural heart defect via trans-esophageal echocardiogram. In resource-limited areas, a keen clinical eye is important for physicians to ensure prompt diagnosis, ongoing follow-up, and surgery when needed.
