Effect of climate change on milk production and yield of buffalo, indigenous cattle and crossbred cattle in Haryana, India
Authors: Ruchi Yadav, Sanjit Maiti, Sanchita Garai, Mukesh Bhakat, Anil Kumar Dixit, Anjali Aggarwal, Goutam Mondal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate change significantly undermines livestock productivity worldwide, with profound implications for milk production. Unprecedented climatic changes indiscriminately affect the dairy production across the global south with profound effects on the Trans-Gangetic plains, especially the high milk production tract of Haryana. Hence, this study employs panel data analysis to assess the impact of climatic variables, including annual minimum, maximum, and mean temperatures, heavy rainfall incidence, temperature-humidity index (THI), and potential evapotranspiration (PET) on milk yield and production in buffalo (Bubalus bubalis), indigenous cattle (Bos indicus), and crossbred cattle (Bos taurus × Bos indicus) in Haryana, India, from 2004 to 2019. The study was based on data spanning 16 years of Integrated Sample Survey in Haryana, adopting a stratified three-stage sampling design (districts → villages/households → animals) with complete livestock population enumeration. Annually, 1,148 villages were surveyed for recording milk yield and milk production. Crossbred cattle, indigenous cattle and buffalo had daily milk yields of 8.08, 5.23 and 7.64 kg/day with annual production of 788.24, 338.81 and 6045.02 MT, respectively. The yearly population estimates based on complete enumeration, viz., indigenous cattle (n₁ = 0.179 million), crossbred cattle (n₂ = 0.291 million), and buffalo (n₃ = 2.22 million). The findings reveal that high temperatures (> 38 °C) combined with elevated humidity (> 70%) during July and August significantly reduce milk production, whereas winter temperatures exhibit negligible effects. Notably, PET (p < 0.01 for May and June across all species) emerges as a critical climatic indicator alongside THI and heatwaves, necessitating its integration with solar radiation, ambient temperature, and vapour pressure in climate impact assessments. These results underscore PET’s role in shaping adaptive strategies for sustainable livestock production amid global warming.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58067-z.

Introduction

Unprecedented climatic variability and anthropogenic activities in interaction lead to a greater rate of climate change across the globe1. These changes, though manifested in the form of escalated surface temperatures, in turn induce a greater number of climatic hazards2. To worsen the matter, the Indian subcontinent in general and India in particular suffer from even greater rates of climate change. In addition, the predictions suggest that global temperature might rise by 1.5 °C by 2050 and 2–4 °C by 21003. Taking these facts into account, it is well established that India is essentially a home to an agrarian community which is also dependent on dairy for sustenance. Evidently, India stands as the world’s largest milk producer with production of 239.30 million tonnes in 2023-24, with an annual growth rate of 6% in the last five yearsand contributes to gross value added of agriculture of 30.23% in 2023-244. In the light of ongoing climate changes, the sustenance of the dairy production system and thereby the agrarian livelihood is likely to be affected profoundly5. Evidently, the higher ranges of temperature-humidity index (THI) emanating from global warming severely affect the livestock physiology and productivity6. These effects are translated in the form of declining food intake, triggering of the homeostatic responses in the form of panting, sweating and reduced activity7–9. Stress hormones interfere with the cortisol levels, leading to stress, which in turn ultimately impairs milk ejection and thereby causesa drop in milk yield and, in severe cases, livestock mortality6,10. On the other hand, the rise in surface temperature affects the growth of fodder, reducing its productivity and production, bringing an indirect effect on livestock production system through alteration in quantity, quality and seasonal availability of feed and fodder resources. To add to the complexity, the aggravated hazard scenario in India disrupts the agricultural and livestock production through losses in the form of livestock mortality, losses to landholdings, and disruption of pasture growth11. Evidence in support of these facts reveal an average decline of 174+−7 kg per decade owed to rising temperatures12. Such stressors in India alone lead to 3.2 million tonnes of losses in milk, which is worth 2661 crore losses and might rise to 15 million tonnes by 2050s13. As far as Haryana is concerned, despite highest milk availability of 1087 g/day against national average; with 15 out of 22 district under moderate to high vulnerability with a mean annual temperature of the higher than India by 3.16 per cent, drop in humidity by 30 per cent and annual rainfall of 536.5 mm stands prone to effects of climate change on the Murrah as well as crossbred cattle breeds. These factors, in general, expose the livestock to thermal shocks for several months a year. In unison, jeopardising the milk production in Haryana14–16.

Results

Secondary data of the past sixteen years were collected from the Department of Animal Husbandry, Panchkula, Haryana, India, through the ‘Integrated Sample Survey’ of 2004–2019, covering nearly 1148 villages in a year. During this period of survey total of 4.66 million crossbred cattle, 2.86 million indigenous cattle and 35.56 million buffaloes were covered. The recoded milk yield of these species was recorded as 8.08, 5.23 and 7.64 kg/day, whereasthe annual total milk production of these species was 7.88.24, 338.81 and 6045.02 MT, respectively (Table 1).

The β-coefficient (Table 2) represents the expected change in milk yield (litres/animal/day) for a one-unit increase in the climatic parameter, holding other factors constant. Negative and positive signs indicate the decrease and increase in milk yield, respectively. Further, PET reflects atmospheric demand for water and is closely linked to heat stress. Results depicted that a unit increase in PET (mm/day) reduces milk yield by ~ 1.4 L per buffalo per day. Biologically, this suggests buffaloes, as well as crossbred cattle, are highly sensitive to evaporative demand in July, likely due to poor heat dissipation capacity (dark skin, fewer sweat glands). While no meaningful association was found in indigenous cattle. Their adaptive traits (loose skin, efficient sweating, lower metabolic heat) buffer against PET fluctuations. Likewise, a unit increase in THI reduces yield by 1.3 L/day in July and a massive 8.4 L/day in August. This highlights the extreme vulnerability of buffaloes to combined heat and humidity stress, especially in August when humidity peaks due to the monsoon.

Discussion

In the present study, the findings revealed that seasonal differences in milk production are caused by periodic changes in the environment over the year, which has a direct effect on animal milk yield and milk production through maintaining homeostasis by reducing dry matter intake and an indirect effect through fluctuations in the quantity and quality of feed and fodder.

The thermoneutral zone for buffalo is 13–25 °C, whereas for cattle it is 25 °C; andbeyond these temperatures, animals experience stress conditions15.In the present research, high temperatures and high humidity values inJuly and August (that is summer season) induced significant negative climatic effects on milk production. However, the incidence of cold waves (temperature remaining below 3 °C than normal, consecutively for 3 or more days) negatively affected milk production, as animals were not able to maintain body metabolism properly, but climatic parameters, however, did not inflict any changes.

As far as the dairy farmers of the study area were concerned, they exhibited not only awareness towards climate change but alsoadopted climate-resilient livestock technologies. The awareness of climate change, particularly heat stress, affecting animals andthe adoption of different practices to combat climate change, has beenvery well documented17–20.Views of respondentsaligned with the aforementioned studies implying that the farmers of the Haryana region were not only well aware of these technologies and practices but implemented them in the summer season (provided wallowing ponds to buffalo, agroforestry, keeping livestock in shed, alteration of microclimate via sprinklers, foggers, mist, etc., feeding management), and reduced the negative effects of the extreme THI in summer months. In the study area, milk and milk products are the main parts of the daily diet (second highest per capita milk consumption in India, i.e., 1147 gm per capita per day) (Integrated Sample Survey, 2019–2020), and farmers adopt the majority of the practices which aid in alteration of microclimate thereby directly reducing the effect of high temperature on animal productivity.

The THI in the summer months did not affect milk production significantly due to the adoption of technologies for the management of livestock in heat stress conditions. Adding further, soil evapotranspiration, water stress andthe PET of summer and rainy months weresignificantly inversely associated with the milk production of livestock. In the study area, buffalo mostly calved in winter months21, and in the months of April, May, or June, buffalo entered dry periods, leading to evasion of the detrimental effects of heat stress during the wet period. Therefore, the milk production of buffalowas not significantly affected by heat stress, although their reproductive efficiency declined.A significant effect of heat stress was observed in the crossbred cattle, whichmight be dueto the exotic genes along with calving in the months of March and April.Further, during the peak production period, they were exposed to high temperatures. Hence, high-producing animals were more susceptible to heat stress than low-producing animals. The exotic genes to adapt under tropical conditions are limited, and research studies have concluded that local or indigenous breeds survive and perform better in tropical environments than exotic breeds and their hybrids22–24.

Furthermore, it is well known that breeds of Indian origin, i.e., indigenous livestock, are resistant to the adverse effects of climate in their region; however, the incidence of heat waves and cold waves has significant negative effects on production. This might be due to the rearing of indigenous breeds by very few and mostly resource-poor small farmers, whogive less attention tothe scientific rearing of the livestock.In general, dairy Cattle experience heat stress when the environmental temperature risesabove their thermoneutral zone leading to increased energy requirement for homeostasis, leading to less energy availability for milk production25,26. Adding further, buffaloes in their lactating phase are more susceptible to heat stress and are unable to keep their body temperatures within the range of the thermo-neutral zone due to their unique species characteristics such as black body hue and a greater reliance on water for normal physiology.

On the other hand, indigenous cattle (e.g., Sahiwal, Hariana) are noted as more tolerant to heat stress, and their resilience is linked to loose skin, lighter coat colour, higher density of sweat glands, and efficient panting mechanisms, which allow better evaporative cooling compared to buffaloes. Further, Indigenous breeds generally produce less milk than crossbreds, which means their metabolic heat production is lower. This reduces the risk of heat stress and helps maintain homeostasis under high THI conditions. Apart from that, Indigenous breeds show stronger immunity against local parasites and pathogens. This resilience indirectly supports climate tolerance, since heat stress often compromises immune function. Therefore, Indigenous breeds should be conserved as reservoirs of climate-resilient traits. Policies must prioritise in-situ conservation and farmer-led breed improvement programs. Current crossbreeding programs often emphasize yield over resilience. Incorporating indigenous germplasm into crossbred lines could balance productivity with climate tolerance, reducing vulnerability to THI and PET stress. Further, selection indices should include heat tolerance, feed efficiency, and disease resistance alongside milk yield. Moreover, shifting of breeding policy from purely production-oriented to resilience-oriented is needed now. Since indigenous breeds are mostly reared by resource-poor farmers, breeding policies must integrate extension support, training, and incentives to encourage scientific management of indigenous herds. In short, indigenous cattle are more tolerant because of morpho-physiological traits, lower metabolic demands, and adaptive behaviours. Breeding policy should leverage these traits to design climate-resilient livestock systems, rather than marginalizing indigenous breeds in favour of high-yield but climate-sensitive crossbreds.

High temperatures lead to reproductive failure and embryo mortality, particularly in buffaloes27, as buffalo possess a thick black skin colour leading to absorption of more sunlight. Buffalo skin contains nearly one-sixth fewer sweat glands28 than Zebu skin, compromising evaporative heat loss by dissipation. These peculiar characteristics make buffalo a poor thermoregulator, and thereby tend to increase internal body heat and consequently reduce productive and reproductive performance. Despite these facts, a large population of buffaloes in such harsh, hot climates survive with productivity due to some of the special anatomical and morphological features of the skin. For instance, oily secretions from well-developed sebaceous glands make the skin slippery for water and mud and provide safety against harmful germs present in mud and water during wallowing. Furthermore, the sebum contributes to enhanced cutaneous radiance during the summer season by increasing specular reflectance of solar radiation, which may serve a thermoregulatory function through improved reflection of infrared and visible wavelengths, thereby reducing absorptive heat load.Consequently, due to heat stress, nutrient intake and nutrient uptake are both decreased in buffalo, leading to a reduction in overall milk production. During summer months, the dry matter intake of buffalo decreases, along with a decrease in the ratio of forage to concentrate intake, leading to a greater decline in milk production for high-producing buffaloes. Thus, the significant connection between the level of milk yield and the reduction in milk yield with an increase in the daily mean temperature can be observed.

As far as cattle are concerned, in a previous study, cowsexperienced heat stress when their rectal temperature exceeded 39 °C and respiration rate exceeded 60/min, indicating that they were experiencing heat stress and that this heat stress was severe enough to impact milk yield and fertility29. Conversely, livestock, being homeotherms, can withstand heat stress to varying degrees depending on breed, species, and production26,30.The sensitivity of different animal species to ambient temperature and the amount of moisture in the air varies. In general, cattle can endure even higher temperatures and a lower relative humidity. Cattle can dissipate excessive heat more efficiently than other livestock by sweating.

Although buffaloes are classified as country animals, it is important to take into account their morpho-physiology and thermoregulation mechanisms to prevent thermal stress and the resulting poor welfare and reduced productivity. However, if appropriate thermoregulation facilities are provided (i.e., ponds, pools, potholes, or swampy areas), buffaloes can properly thermoregulate and tolerate high ambient temperatures. Therefore, they may represent a good option and an appropriate animal-based enterprise under climate change and global warming conditions31.

The combined effects of air temperature and humidityareexpressed as a single value in the form ofthetemperature–humidity index (THI), which is associated with the level of thermal stress. The measurement of heat stress on livestock through THIis often done by numerous animal scientists32 and shows a significant negative correlation with the intake of dry matter; consequently, a negative correlation exists between the THI and milk production and milk yield. Also, heat waves significantly affect animal productivity. Furthermore, the trends of increasing frequency and intensity of heat waves vary annually, and may escalate even furtheras per future predictions33,34. Hence, as an optimised solution, forecasting heat wave incidence can enable livestock owners to apply mitigation strategies against adverse climatic eventspromptly.

Apart from the THI and heat waves, the PET was also one of the imperative indicators to correlate the effect of climate change with livestock productivity. As water and energy balances are indicated by evapotranspiration, in changing climatic scenarios, greater evapotranspiration is likely to imply that more severe drought conditions will exacerbate plant water stress35. Potential evapotranspiration occurs only when the availability of water for evaporation from soils and transpiration by plants is unlimited. Under global warming conditions with an enhanced atmospheric evaporative demand36, the surface water budget is altered37, and surface evapotranspiration changes accordingly38. Evapotranspiration also follows seasonal trends, such as air temperature, i.e., minimum rates in winter months and maximum rates in summer months, when water may be in limited supply.In addition, the rate of evaporative loss through the skin and lungs depends on the moisture content of the atmospheric air. The quantity of moisture in the environment becomes a critical factor when the animal’s daily temperature falls outside of its comfort zone to maintain animal homeostasis39. Hence, the presence of moisture at the commencement of a drought, vis-à-visthe severity and extent of a drought, will change the evaporation and transpiration pattern for the livestock. From an agricultural perspective, drought is determined by conditions that cause stunted growth with plant wilting. In this case, the relative humidity present in the air responds in lockstep with the temperature. A significant increase in the relative humidity of the air in India was also noticed, which consequently causes less evaporative demand and, hence, lower evapotranspiration40.

The present research findings show that seasonal changes in the production of milk are caused by periodic changes in the environmental parameters year by year, which have a direct effect on livestock productivity through the animal’s efforts to maintain homeostasis by reducing dry matter intake and an indirect effect via fluctuations in the quantity and quality of feed and fodder. Milk production in June, July, August, and January was negatively associated with the corresponding THI, which may be due to a maximum temperature coupled with a higher relative humidity (Fig. 3), thereby inducing heat stress.Seasonal variation in milk yield in Haryana was significantly influenced by climatic stressors, with both the Temperature–Humidity Index (THI) and Potential Evapotranspiration (PET) identified as key predictors of production decline. Milk yield during June, July, August, and January was negatively correlated with THI, reflecting the combined effects of high ambient temperatures and elevated relative humidity (> 70%) during the monsoon and late summer, which impaired evaporative cooling and intensified heat stress. In contrast, high temperatures in May–June were less detrimental due to low humidity (22–28%), enabling efficient cooling41.PET, integrating temperature, humidity, solar radiation, wind speed, and precipitation, significantly affected both water availability and livestock performance, with fluctuations in PET likely to exacerbate climatic stress. Incorporating PET, along with extraterrestrial solar radiation and atmospheric water vapour pressure, into predictive models is essential for climate adaptation planning in livestock systems42–47. Cold stress was also evident, with winter temperatures dropping to > 3 °C below normal48, leading to a reduction in milk yield by up to 30% during extreme events, attributable to greater metabolic energy demands for thermoregulation. Conversely, a 4 °C rise in minimum temperature was associated with a 1.4% yield increase, highlighting the benefits of improved thermal comfort.

While the temperature declined by more than 3 °C during winter months compared with the normal temperature, milk production was adversely impactedby up to 30% during the days after extreme events49. The production performance of dairy animals was also affected by cold waves because a larger proportion of energy was shifted towards the maintenance of body temperature; thus, the productivity of livestock depends on the ability to maintaina normal and stable body temperature. An increase in the minimum temperature of 4 °C causes milk production to increase by 1.4%50. Hence, improvement in terms of the climatic comfort of livestock has a positive impact on yield per cow.Even if global mean surface temperatures are constrained to 1.5 °C above thepreindustrial levels by the end of the century, the findings of this study imply that management alternatives should be implemented or scaled up in the face of increased levels of heat stress produced by a higher THI and higher PET. Early warning systems for detecting heat stress, soil moisture content, and regional water level are critically important to trigger appropriate management responses.

The majority of farmers in Haryana maintained animals in stall feeding areas. Yet in places where grazing was provided to animals to combat heat stress and they were allowed to leave pastures at night, the physiological responses of the animalswere decreased. In summary, there is no additional way to ameliorate the adverse effects of a changing climate except for proactively adopting good management practices, such as alterations in microclimate, nutritional management, and infrastructure facilities for providing comfort to livestock, as found in part of the research in the present study.The study identified THI and PET as the climate stress indicators that had a significant negative impact on milk yield and production. Therefore, the livestock farmers and extension staff may be trained and sensitized to better understand heat stress mitigation, including how to interpret indicators such as the THI and PET and how to prioritize different adaptive practices depending on those indices. In hot, humid areas, shades, sprinklers, and fans are particularly effective techniques for enhancing the thermal environment of dairy animals51,52. Green fodder additives and higher concentrations in ruminant rations in India could help to minimise the effects of climate change by increasing feeding efficiency53.To enhance climate resilience in Haryana’s dairy sector, the following concrete actions are recommended based on these findings:

Both indices, THI and PET, should be incorporated into localized early warning systems for farmers. Real-time THI and PET forecasts (delivered via mobile SMS, apps, or radio) would enable timely interventions (e.g., activating fans/sprinklers when THI > 72 or PET exceeds critical thresholds). Combining these with soil moisture and groundwater-level alerts would provide a comprehensive stress-forecasting platform.Training modules for farmers, veterinary officers, and extension workers should focus on interpreting daily/weekly THI and PET values and linking them to observable animal signs of stress. Accordingly, rank and implement cost-effective mitigation practices based on stress severity (e.g., shade + fans at THI 72–79; sprinklers + forced ventilation at THI ≥ 80).The pronounced negative impact of high THI and PET underscores the need to accelerate breeding programs for thermo-tolerant animals. Priority should be given to the use of crossbreds with higher proportions of Bos indicus (e.g., Hariana, Sahiwal, Tharparkar) or selected high-yielding crosses proven to maintain production above THI 80. Also, establishing heat-tolerance indices (based on milk yield persistence under elevated THI/PET) as selection criteria in future breeding value estimations.Without scaling these practices and embedding THI and PET into routine decision-making tools, training curricula, and breeding policy, climate change will continue to erode dairy productivity and livelihoods in the region.

Study limitations

The reliance on secondary data from 2004 to 05 to 2019–20 precludes the analysis of post-2020 climatic extremes and COVID-19-related disruptions, while the use of monthly TerraClimate and IMD aggregates lacks the granular daily precision necessary to capture microclimatic shifts or attribute shocks to specific extreme weather events. Methodologically, although fixed-effects models account for time-invariant heterogeneity, they remain susceptible to unobserved time-varying factors such as evolving veterinary access or breed-specific genetic improvements. Furthermore, despite applying the Deschenes-Greenstone framework to isolate climate signals, potential endogeneity from autonomous adaptation—such as shed cooling or reactive herd reductions—may persist in the absence of instrumental variables. Consequently, these results are specific to Haryana’s semi-arid context and require further validation before being generalized beyond the Indo-Gangetic Plains.

Conclusion

This study utilized a fixed-effects panel analysis of 19 Haryana districts (2004–2019, N = 304) to quantify the impact of climatic stressors on dairy productivity. The results confirm that climate change significantly impairs milk output, with THI and PET identified as the primary drivers of thermal stress. The main key findings are:

Seasonal Vulnerability: Elevated THI during the rainy months (July–August) and high PET during the summer (May–July) result in significant reductions in milk production. Specifically, a one-unit increase in THI is associated with a decline in buffalo milk yield of approximately 0.08–0.43 kg/day (p < 0.01).Breed-Specific Sensitivity: Buffaloes and crossbred cattle exhibit the highest vulnerability to thermal stress due to physiological constraints in heat dissipation. In contrast, indigenous breeds, such as the Sahiwal, demonstrate superior resilience to both heat and cold waves.Extreme Events: Both heatwaves and winter cold waves exacerbate productivity losses, with cold stress impacting buffalo populations most severely.

Policy and practical implications

The findings underscore the need for a strategic shift toward resilience-oriented livestock management. We recommend the integration of PET and THI metrics into regional early warning systems to allow for proactive management. Furthermore, long-term climate resilience should prioritize thermo-tolerant breeding programs that leverage Bos indicus traits. Immediate adaptive interventions, including the provision of improved housing shades, mist-cooling systems, and adequate wallowing facilities for buffaloes, are essential to sustain Haryana’s dairy output amidst a warming climate.

The study is entirely based on secondary data. The survey was conducted every year in the entire rural/urban areas of the state. As a general principle, the survey was targeted to cover around 5% villages in every season (summer, rainy and winter), taking into account a total of 15% villages, nearly 1148 villages covered in a year. The stratified three-stage sampling design was used with our district as a stratum. The first stage units were villages or urban wards, the second stage units were households, and third and ultimate stage units were animals. The list of villages as per the latest Livestock Census constituted the sampling frame. The survey was conducted in two stages, viz. (i) Complete Enumeration and (ii) Detailed Survey. The estimation of livestock population was derived from an enumeration of all animals and the calculation of the average from a detailed survey of the animals. Under the detailed survey, animals were classified species-wise (indigenous cattle, crossbred cattle and buffalo) and age-wise. Milk yield data of cows and buffalo were collected in the morning and evening, with lactation order and month of calving. Overall, 1,148 villages were surveyed for recording milk yield and milk production annually. The milk yield of the crossbred cattle, indigenous cattle and buffalo was 8.08, 5.23 and 7.64 kg/day. While yearly total milk production of these species was 7.88.24, 338.81 and 6045.02 MT, respectively. The yearly population estimates were based on complete enumeration, viz., indigenous cattle (n₁ = 0.179 million), crossbred cattle (n₂ = 0.291 million), and buffalo (n₃ = 2.22 million).

To establish the empirical relationship between milk production and milk yield and climatic parameters, annual minimum, maximum, and mean temperature data and rainfall data were extracted (grid scale of 0.25 × 0.25) from the Indian Meteorological Department, Pune. The monthly THI and PET were extracted from TerraClimate55. All the concerned climatic variables were taken as independent variables, whereas the average milk production and milk yield of indigenous cattle, crossbred cattle, and buffalo were taken as dependent variables.

Panel data with fixed effects model (FEM)

The Deschenes and Greenstone panel data approach58 was followed. All the district-level data used for analysis were adjusted to the district boundaries of 2004to account for changes in the administrative boundaries of districts due to the formulation of new districts. The effect of climate change was studied in terms of changes in production and productivity trends in the Haryana region. The panel consisted of 16 years of climatic parameters and production data for livestock in the Haryana region from 2004 to 2019 for 19 districts.

Pre and post diagnostic tests

In this study we describe the climate dataset and the econometric diagnostics used to ensure robust estimation. For the panel estimation, we perform pre- and post-estimation diagnostic tests to validate model assumptions and select the appropriate estimator: the Hausman test to choose between fixed and random effects with Mundlak test, panel unit-root tests to assess stationarity, variance inflation factors (VIF) to detect multicollinearity, and autocorrelation tests to detect serial dependence. Test statistics and p-values for each diagnostic are reported in the results.

Model selection and justification

Model selection was initially guided by the Hausman specification test, which is commonly used to determine whether the Fixed Effects or Random Effects estimator is more appropriate. Under the null hypothesis, the Random Effects model is preferred, while the alternative hypothesis supports the Fixed Effects model. In the present study, the Hausman test results for ICMP, CBCMP, and BMP yielded p-values greater than 0.05, thereby statistically favoring the Random Effects specification. However, despite this statistical indication, the Fixed Effects estimator was retained as the principal model for analysis. This decision is justified by the nature of the dataset and the substantive objective of the study. The panel does not constitute a random sample drawn from a broader population of herds or districts; rather, it represents a defined and complete set of livestock production units, namely indigenous cattle, crossbred cattle, and buffalo, observed in the Trans-Gangetic Plains of Haryana over a 16-year period. Because the analysis is focused on these specific units and seeks to control for unobserved, time-invariant heterogeneity across them, the Fixed Effects framework is more consistent with the study design.

Accordingly, the Fixed Effects model provides a more conservative and contextually appropriate basis for inference, as it controls for all unit-specific characteristics that do not vary over time, even when such characteristics are unobserved. This is particularly important in livestock and regional panel datasets, where omitted factors such as management practices, ecological conditions, breed characteristics, and location-specific structural differences may be correlated with the explanatory variables. The word “fixed effect” is used because over time, the intercept does not vary, i.e.,time is invariant, but across the subjects (districts), the intercept may differ59. Intercepts vary across districts due to their heterogeneous socioeconomic conditions, geography, etc.,unlike pooled OLS, which assumes the same intercept for all regions. The FEM approach does not measure the impact of time-invariant variables58. Multicollinearity (VIF value)

Multicollinearity among the independent variables was examined using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) to ensure the reliability and stability of the regression estimates. The VIF values for the explanatory variables, namely Heavy Rainfall Days (HRF), Cold Wave, Heat Wave, THI and PET, ranged from 1.07 to 5.77. According to standard econometric thresholds, VIF values below 5 indicate low multicollinearity, while values between 5 and 10 indicate moderate multicollinearity. The results showed that Cold Wave (3.09), Heat Wave (2.27), HRF (1.07), and THI (4.08) exhibited low multicollinearity, whereas PET (5.77) showed moderate multicollinearity. However, since all VIF values were below the critical threshold of 10, no severe multicollinearity problem was detected among the explanatory variables. Therefore, all selected climate variables were retained in the regression model for further analysis (Table 10).

The Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test results indicate that several climate variables and milk production variables exhibit non-stationarity at levels (p > 0.05), which is commonly observed in climate-related panel time series data due to long-term trends and structural variations. However, the presence of non-stationarity does not necessarily invalidate the panel estimation framework used in this study. The Fixed Effects (FE) panel model was intentionally employed because it focuses on “within-district” variation over time and controls for unobserved time-invariant district-specific heterogeneity (Table 11).
