Fracture resistance and failure modes in 4- vs. 6-implant supported zirconia full-arch prostheses with and without cantilevers: an in vitro study
Authors: Dong Zhou, Zhicheng Cai, Zejian Xu, Xi Jiang, Shuxin Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSETo evaluate the fracture resistance and failure modes of IFCDPs supported by 4 versus 6 implants, with and without cantilever loading.MATERIALS AND METHODSForty monolithic zirconia IFCDP specimens (20 with 4 implants, 20 with 6 implants) were fabricated using CAD-CAM technology and bonded to titanium copings on titanium alloy casts. Specimens were divided into four groups (n = 10 each): Group A (4 implants, non-cantilever load at second premolar), Group B (4 implants, cantilever load at second molar), Group C (6 implants, non-cantilever load at second premolar), and Group D (6 implants, cantilever load at second molar). Mechanical testing was performed using a universal testing machine with a three-point bending fixture loading bilaterally. Failure modes (fracture or cement debonding) and maximum failure loads were recorded. Statistical analysis included Fisher’s exact test for failure mode distribution and two-way ANOVA for failure load.RESULTSNon-cantilever loading caused framework fractures at high mean loads (Group A: 25,105 ± 5744 N; Group C: 27,189 ± 5753 N), while cantilever loading led to cement debonding at significantly lower failure loads (Group B:1,070 ± 404 N; Group D: 828 ± 432 N). Loading site significantly influenced failure load (P < .001), but implant number and its interaction with loading site had no significant effect (P > .05).CONCLUSIONBoth All-on-4 and All-on-6 zirconia full-arch prostheses show high static fracture resistance, but cantilever loading greatly reduces strength and induces debonding, underscoring the need to minimize cantilever length or improve bonding strategies.Keywords: Implant-supported fixed complete denture prostheses (IFCDPs), Fracture resistance, Cantilever loading, All-on-4 vs All-on-6

INTRODUCTION

Implant-supported fixed complete denture prostheses (IFCDPs), which have emerged as a cornerstone in modern prosthodontics, can effectively restore the oral health, function, and esthetics of edentulous patients. Monolithic zirconia frameworks have gained popularity over veneered or hybrid designs owing to their high strength, fracture resistance, esthetic appearance, and biocompatibility.1,2,3,4,5,6,7 Clinical studies demonstrated that monolithic zirconia IFCDPs exhibited a high survival rate over 4 – 6 years, ranging from 98.6% to 99.3%.1,8,9

Despite their advantages, monolithic zirconia IFCDPs remain susceptible to mechanical complications, such as debonding, and restoration fracture.1,3,8,9,10,11 In a large clinical series, failures have been rare but notable—for example, among 2,039 prostheses followed for five years, 6 prostheses fractured, while 6 experienced debonding of titanium copings and 3 suffered fractures of titanium copings.8 Similar low rates have been reported by other groups, with isolated cases of fracture, debonding, or screw failure.1,9 Beyond material properties, fracture resistance is influenced by prosthesis geometry—particularly implant number, cantilever length, distal screw channel position, and buccolingual thickness—as well as factors such as opposing dentition.9,10,11,12 Among these factors, the number of implants and the cantilever length are the most frequently discussed and concerned ones.

The number of implants can influence the fracture resistance of IFCDPs. Increasing implant number improves occlusal force distribution, reduces stress on individual implants, and shortens cantilever length. While retrospective studies report similar survival rates for All-on-4 and All-on-6 prostheses (94.7 – 95.4% vs. 96 – 99.3%), All-on-6 designs may offer greater stability in high-risk cases—such as elderly patients, those with low bone density, or patients with bruxism—and reduce mechanical complications when cantilevers exceed 10 mm.13,14 However, placing more than six implants may compromise fracture resistance due to reduced prosthesis dimensions and difficulties in achieving passive fit, with closely spaced implants linked to higher fracture incidence.1,9 Clinical survival also depends on multiple patient- and practitioner-related factors, making it difficult for retrospective studies to isolate the mechanical effect of implant number.1,5,12,13,14,15 To date, no laboratory study has directly compared the mechanical performance of full-arch frameworks supported by four versus six implants.

Cantilever length also significantly influences the fracture resistance of IFCDPs. The greater the cantilever length is, the more prone the restoration is to fracture.5,7,12,16,17,18 Clinical studies have linked cantilever length to restoration fracture,12 and in vitro work shows that shorter cantilevers with larger connector areas achieve higher fracture loads.16,19 Most existing laboratory studies, however, use simplified models and do not replicate the geometry or loading conditions of full-arch prostheses.

To date, no laboratory study has systematically compared the fracture resistance and failure modes of full-arch monolithic zirconia IFCDPs supported by four versus six implants under both cantilever and non-cantilever loading. This in vitro study addresses this gap, aiming to provide evidence-based guidance for prosthetic design. We hypothesized that: (1) increasing implant number would increase fracture resistance, and (2) cantilever loading would significantly reduce fracture resistance and alter failure modes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Casts preparation

A cast with standard dental arch dimensions was designed using the CAD software (DentalCAD, exocad GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany). Four multi-unit abutment analogs (φ5.0, WEGO, Weihai, China) were placed on the lateral canines and first molars areas to obtain an All-on-4 cast, and six multiunit abutment analogs were placed on its lateral incisors, premolars, and first molars areas to obtain an All-on-6 cast within the CAD software. The implant angulation in both casts was maintained below 2°. The two abutment level casts were milled from titanium alloy block for the following fracture strength experiments as shown in Figure 1A.

(A) Two abutment-level titanium alloy casts, one with 4 implants and the other with 6 implants. (B) Forty specimens were included and divided into four groups: Group A (4 implants, non-cantilever load at second premolar), Group B (4 implants, cantilever load at second molar), Group C (6 implants, non-cantilever load at second premolar), and Group D (6 implants, cantilever load at second molar).

2. Specimen preparation

The two casts were scanned using a 3D scanner (E4, 3Shape A/S, Denmark). After scanning, an All-on-4 and an All-on-6 monolithic zirconia restorations were designed using the dental CAD software (DentalCAD, exocad GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany). The copings (φ5.0, WEGO, Weihai, China) with a 0.15-mm bonding gap were selected from the database. The full-arch mandible restoration consisted of 14 units from tooth 37 to tooth 47. In the All-on-4 restoration, the screw accesses were located at the cingulum of lateral canines and the central fossae of lateral first molars. In the All-on-6 restoration, the screw accesses were located at the cingulum of lateral incisors and the central fossae of lateral premolars and first molars. In both the All-on-4 and the All-on-6 monolithic zirconia restorations, the length and morphology of the dental arch were identical, and the cantilever length was uniformly 12 mm. The thickness of buccal and lingual side around screw access ranged from 1.89 to 3.80 mm at gingival level, 3.39 to 4.15 mm at middle level, and 2.8 to 3.5 mm at incisal level, all of which met the fundamental design criteria.

In this study, 20 IFCDP specimens with All-on-4 design and 20 IFCDP specimens with All-on-6 design were fabricated. All zirconia (ST-color, Shenzhen Upcera Dental Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) restorations were milled using a milling machine (Rhino A52D for zirconia, Shenzhen Cradle Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). Then they were sintered at a maximum temperature of 1510°C for 2 hours to achieve crystallization. Before being bonded, the copings (φ5.0, WEGO, Weihai, China) were screwed into the analogs of cast and tightened to a torque of 25. The zirconia restorations were then tried in to obtain passive fit and subsequently bonded to the copings using a MEYDENT self-adhesive resin cement (Shanghai Huge Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Based on the number of implants and loading sites, the specimens were categorized into four groups: Group A (4 implants, non-cantilever load at second premolar), Group B (4 implants, cantilever load at second molar), Group C (6 implants, non-cantilever load at second premolar), and Group D (6 implants, cantilever load at second molar), with 10 specimens per group, as shown in Figure 1B.

3. Loading process

The loading tests were conducted using a universal testing machine (INSTRON 5985), equipped with Bluehill Universal software to control the test procedure, acquire data, and analyze results. To ensure symmetric bilateral loading of the zirconia framework, a cylindrical loading end with a 5 mm radius was used. During testing, the specimen was seated on the titanium alloy cast, which was firmly secured in a compression fixture to prevent slippage, and a vertical load was applied according to a predetermined protocol. The overall loading setup is shown in Figure 2. The load was gradually increased at a constant crosshead speed of 1 mm/min until fracture or structural failure of the specimen occurred. Throughout the test, the software continuously recorded the applied load and specimen deformation in real time. From the load–displacement curve, the software automatically determined the failure point and recorded the maximum load value, defined as the fracture load. The failure mode and the corresponding tooth position were documented for each specimen. In addition to real-time data acquisition, the software provided comprehensive analytical tools for evaluating fracture loads, failure modes, and other key mechanical properties, yielding reliable data for assessing the performance of dental prosthetic materials and designs.

Maximum fracture resistance was tested under bilateral loading applied on the cantilever of the specimen in Group B.

The sample size (n = 10 per group) was determined based on variability reported in previous in vitro studies of zirconia prostheses. Using prior data as a reference,5 a power analysis (α = 0.05, power = 0.80) indicated that a minimum of two specimens per group would be sufficient to detect a 15% difference in fracture load between groups. To account for potential specimen loss or testing anomalies, the number was increased to ten per group.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 23.0 software (IBM SPSS, Armonk, NY, USA), with the significance level set at P < .05. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the proportion of failures by tooth position across the four experimental groups. Two-way analysis of variance (Two-way ANOVA) was conducted to assess the main effects of implant number and loading site, as well as their interaction effect on failure load.

RESULTS

1. Failure mode analysis

In this study, mechanical tests were conducted on zirconia frameworks supported by varying numbers of implants, with bilateral loading applied at different sites to simulate failure behavior under diverse clinical scenarios. The results identified two primary failure modes: fracture of the zirconia framework and cement debonding between the zirconia framework and the titanium copings, as shown in Figure 3. When loading was applied at the non-cantilever site, framework fractures occurred in all cases, whereas with cantilever loading, cement debonding consistently occurred before any framework fracture. In non-cantilever loading groups (group A and group C), fractures occurred near and anterior to the loading site, with the highest proportion of fracture sites at Tooth 6 (17 out of 35 total fracture sites in Group A and Group C, 48.6%) and Tooth 3 (10 out of 35 total fracture sites in Group A and Group C, 28.6%). This pattern suggests that stress concentrations occurred in both the loading area (posterior region) and anterior regions. Specifically, failure sites in Groups A and C showed statistically significant differences (P < .001), suggesting the number of implants on the edentulous jaw has major impact on fracture distribution within the zirconia framework.

In contrast, in cantilever loading groups (group B and group D), no fractures were observed. However, cement debonding was exclusively observed in anterior teeth, indicating elevated stress at the framework-coping interface in this region during cantilever loading. Group D (6 implants) showed debonding sites distributed between Tooth 2 (17 out of 33 total debonding sites in Group D, 51.5%) and Tooth 4 (16 out of 33 total debonding sites in Group D, 48.5%), while group B (4 implants) had 100% of debonding concentrated at Tooth 3 (20 out of 20 total debonding sites in Group B, 100%). This distribution difference was statistically significant (P < .001), demonstrating that increasing implant numbers alters stress distribution but does not prevent cement debonding in cantilever frameworks.

In summary, fractures were predominantly concentrated in the non-cantilever loading tests, while cement debonding was more frequently observed in the cantilever loading tests. This trend remained consistent across 4-implant and 6-implant configurations.

2. Failure load analysis

To facilitate analysis of the experimental results, maximum failure load was converted into a box plot (Fig. 4). It demonstrates that the maximum failure load was significantly higher when loading was applied at the non-cantilever site compared to the cantilever site. Specifically, with non-cantilever loading, the average maximum failure load for frameworks with four implants was 25,104.90 ± 5744.22 N, slightly lower than the 27,188.66 ± 5753.83 N observed for six implants; with cantilever loading, four-implant frameworks averaged 1,070.08 ± 403.94 N, slightly higher than the 827.67 ± 431.67 N for six implants. Two-way ANOVA analysis revealed that neither implant number (F = 0.510, P = .480) nor the interaction between implant number and loading site (F = 0.814, P = .373) was significant (P > .05). However, the main effect of loading site was highly significant (F = 382.191, P < .001), which may be attributed to differences in stress distribution at different loading sites. In conclusion, loading site is the primary factor influencing the failure load of zirconia frameworks, whereas changes in implant number have only a minor impact.

DISCUSSION

Using edentulous titanium casts with multi-unit abutments and bilateral loading that more closely simulates clinical occlusal loading, this in vitro study systematically evaluated full-arch monolithic zirconia frameworks and provided direct evidence addressing our hypotheses. First, increasing the implant number from four to six did not enhance static fracture resistance or alter failure modes: under non-cantilever loading, both configurations failed by framework fracture at similar mean loads, whereas under cantilever loading both failed by cement debonding. Second, cantilever loading significantly reduced failure load and shifted failure mode to cement debonding, confirming its detrimental effect. Accordingly, Hypothesis 1 is rejected and Hypothesis 2 is supported.

For cantilever loading, the maximum failure load observed was 827.67 N for frameworks supported by six implants and 1,070.08 N for those supported by four implants. These results are comparable to those reported in previous studies. For instance, Alshiddi et al. reported that the fracture load of cantilever zirconia frameworks supported by two implants ranged from 670.39 N to 1,137.86 N, which is within a similar range to the present findings.19 Another investigation using 3Y-TZP-Zirconia Cantilevers showed much lower fracture loads, from 215.28 N to 442.30 N, highlighting the influence of material type and cantilever design on mechanical performance.5 Another study on one implant-supported cantilever framework in the anterior region reported fracture load in the range of 455 N to 665 N.20 While previous studies reported similar or slightly lower fracture load than this study, it is important to note that all prior investigations were conducted on short-span frameworks supported by fewer than three implants.

The consistent observation of cement debonding under cantilever loading (Groups B and D) underscores a critical mechanical vulnerability at the zirconia-cement-titanium interface. This finding aligns with clinical reports of debonding in monolithic zirconia IFCDPs, particularly in restorations with extended cantilevers.1,3,9,10,18 The elevated stresses at the anterior cement interface suggest that cantilever forces amplify shear stress beyond the resin cement’s bond strength, approximately 25.6 MPa for MEYDENT. To mitigate cement debonding, enhanced bonding protocols could be explored, such as surface treatments (e.g., sandblasting or laser etching) to increase zirconia surface roughness and bond strength.21 Alternatively, using dual-cure cements with higher shear resistance or adhesive primers could improve interface stability.22 Minimizing cantilever length (e.g., below 10 mm) or reinforcing the cantilever cross-section could further enhance mechanical performance, as supported by prior studies.13

For non-cantilever loading, the fracture resistance measured 25,104.90 N for 4-implant configurations and 27,188.66 N for 6-implant configurations. These values substantially exceed the average human bite force range of 35 – 330 N,23 demonstrating that monolithic full-arch zirconia frameworks possess sufficient mechanical strength under non-cantilever loading conditions in vitro. Given that static loading on a rigid titanium base was employed, clinical extrapolation of these results should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Fractures predominantly occurred near the posterior loading sites in this experimental model, consistent with clinical reports of framework failures in molar regions. However, midline fractures occasionally observed in clinical scenarios were absent in this study, suggesting potential contributing factors beyond static loading conditions - such as jaw deformation during function or asymmetric occlusal forces - that warrant further investigation.1,3,8,9,11,18

In this study, the number of implants did not impact either the failure mode or the maximum failure load, regardless of loading position. These findings support the notion that a 4-implant-supported full-arch monolithic zirconia framework is mechanically sufficient to withstand static loading conditions. Several clinical studies have corroborated this, reporting similar fracture incidences for zirconia frameworks supported by either 4 or 6 implants.1,3,8,9,11,18 However, the results of this study only demonstrate that the number of implants does not affect the mechanical performance of the framework; additional clinical factors—such as available bone quality and quantity, implant distribution, and patient masticatory habits—should also be carefully considered in clinical decision-making.

There are several limitations in this in vitro study, Firstly the static loading protocol, while standardized, does not replicate the variable magnitude, direction, and frequency of occlusal forces encountered intraorally over time, potentially influencing failure modes and load values; cyclic fatigue testing would be essential to better understand long-term performance. Secondly, the use of an ideal, non-resilient titanium alloy cast base differs from the clinical reality where implants are embedded in bone with varying density and resilience, and the jaws deformation during loading may play a role. Finally, only one specific zirconia material, coping design, resin cement, and bonding protocol were evaluated; variations in these factors could significantly impact the results, including the observed prevalence of cement debonding under cantilever load.

CONCLUSION

Within the limits of this in vitro model, 4-implant and 6-implant zirconia arches showed comparably high fracture loads under static conditions, and both far exceeded typical occlusal forces. Cantilever loading, however, dramatically lowered failure load by inducing cement debonding. Thus, when static strength is the criterion, additional implants yielded little benefit, but minimizing cantilevers or improving bonding is advisable.
