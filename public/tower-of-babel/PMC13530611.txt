Poster 51. Anatomic Considerations for Lateral Extra-Articular Tenodesis (LET) in Skeletally Immature Knees: Can We Reconstruct the LET and Avoid Physeal Injury?
Authors: Hubert Tuyishime, Bryan W. Khoo, Katelin J. Isakoff, Luis Fernando Viegas de Moraes Leme, Alan D. Anaya Gallegos, Iris Cong, Paul J. Bergeron, Calvin Chan, Amishi Jobanputra, Stephanie S. Pearce, Yi-Meng D. Yen, Matthew Schmitz, Marc A. Tompkins, Philip L. Wilson, Molly C. Meadows, Charles M. Chan, Henry B. Ellis, Theodore J. Ganley, Kevin Shea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Pediatric anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tears are increasingly common.1 Lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) is performed during ACL reconstruction to reduce graft failure and rotational laxity, particularly in pediatric patients with the highest risk of recurrent ACL tears.1-3 The modified Lemaire technique, the most common LET approach, utilizes an iliotibial band (ITB) strip passed deep to the Lateral Collateral Ligament (LCL) and fixed posterior and proximal to the lateral femoral epicondyle, often using the anterolateral ligament (ALL) femoral origin as guiding landmark.6 However, prior anatomic research in pediatric knees has shown that the ALL and LCL origins are close to the distal femoral physis (DFP), with notable variability in the ALL and LCL origins, particularly with age.7,8 The objective of this study is to define the relationships of the DFP to the ALL and the LCL origins, to guide anatomic placement of the LET graft without causing significant femoral physeal injury. We hypothesized that the ALL femoral origin is closer to the DFP than the LCL femoral origin.Methods:Seven skeletally immature cadaveric knees (aged 8 to 16) were dissected (Table 1). The iliotibial band (ITB) was resected and reflected inferiorly to reveal the ALL and LCL. Internal rotation of the tibia was performed to confirm visualization of the ALL fibers and their insertions. Metallic pins were placed at the ALL and LCL femoral origins (Figure 1). Coronal and sagittal fluoroscopic images were captured with a measurement calibration ruler. The distances from the ALL and LCL origins to the DFP were measured in the coronal view using the ImageJ software.3 The anterior-posterior (AP) positional relationship between the ALL and LCL femoral origins was assessed using the sagittal fluoroscopy images and correlation with assessment of gross dissection images from each specimen to ensure accuracy.Results:In the coronal view, the ALL femoral origin was on average 1.4 ± 1.01 mm distal to the DFP (Figure 2). The ALL femoral origin was distal to the DFP in 6 out of 7 specimens. In one specimen, the ALL femoral origin and DFP were at the same position.The LCL femoral origin was on average 4.9 ± 3.18 mm distal to the DFP. The LCL femoral origin was distal to the DFP in 7 out of 7 specimens.In the sagittal view, the ALL origin was posterior to the LCL origin in 5 of 7 specimens and anterior to the LCL origin in the remaining 2 out of 7 specimens (Figure 3).Conclusions:Our findings suggest that the ALL femoral origin is consistently closer to the DFP than the LCL origin. This study also highlights that the ALL and LCL femoral origins are close to each other and to the DFP, with notable AP positional variability consistent with prior anatomic studies. Anatomic reconstruction of the LET using the ALL femoral origin as a landmark suggests that the graft origin position should be at least 1.4 mm distal to the physis, 3.5 mm proximal to the LCL origin, and posterior to the LCL origin. The LET tunnel should also be positioned to avoid physeal injury, closer to the superomedial aspect of the lateral femoral condyle and with no significant overlap with the ACL tunnel inferiorly. These findings can help surgeons place the all-epiphyseal LET femoral tunnel in a more anatomic position and help to reduce the risk of physeal injury in skeletally immature patients.
