Addressing Child Malnutrition in Somalia: Beyond Humanitarian Responses
Authors: Ibrahim Abubakar Abdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChild malnutrition in Somalia represents one of the most severe and protracted public health crises of the contemporary era, rooted not solely in acute food scarcity but in a complex architecture of structural failures, governance deficits, climate vulnerability, and inadequate health system capacity. An estimated 1.7 million children under five years of age in Somalia faced acute malnutrition in 2025, including 466,000 cases of severe acute malnutrition (SAM) and 1.2 million cases of moderate acute malnutrition (MAM). This commentary argues that meaningful progress against child malnutrition in Somalia demands a decisive shift away from perpetual emergency response cycles toward integrated, development-oriented, and sustainably funded national strategies. We examine the epidemiological burden, the structural determinants of malnutrition, the limitations of exclusively humanitarian approaches, and propose a multi-sectoral framework encompassing health system strengthening, food system transformation, nutrition-sensitive social protection, gender-responsive programming, and community-based management. Addressing child malnutrition in Somalia is not merely a humanitarian imperative but a foundational prerequisite for national development.Keywords: child malnutrition, Somalia, acute malnutrition, wasting, stunting, humanitarian response, health system strengthening, food security, community-based management

Introduction

Somalia confronts one of the world’s most severe and chronically underaddressed child nutrition crises. The convergence of prolonged armed conflict, recurrent climate shocks, systemic poverty, and weakened public institutions has produced conditions in which malnutrition among children has persisted at emergency levels for more than three decades. Despite the mobilization of considerable international humanitarian assistance, the cycle of crisis and emergency response has proved inadequate to reverse deeply entrenched structural determinants of nutritional deprivation. The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) estimated that approximately 1.7 million children under five years of age in Somalia would suffer acute malnutrition in 2025, including 466,000 cases of Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) and 1.2 million cases of Moderate Acute Malnutrition (MAM), with approximately 64% of cases concentrated in southern Somalia.1 These statistics are not merely epidemiological abstractions; they represent profound failures of governance, equitable development, and international solidarity.

The humanitarian response architecture in Somalia, while essential for immediate life-saving interventions, has paradoxically contributed to a form of institutional dependency that crowds out investment in sustainable domestic nutrition systems. International non-governmental organizations, United Nations agencies, and bilateral donors have provided critical support, but the programmatic emphasis on therapeutic feeding for acutely malnourished children, while necessary, addresses consequences rather than causes. This commentary argues that Somalia requires a fundamental reconceptualization of its anti-malnutrition strategy—one that locates child nutrition within broader frameworks of food systems reform, health infrastructure development, climate adaptation, and social protection. Without such a shift, Somalia risks perpetuating cycles of emergency response that absorb resources without producing durable reductions in child malnutrition prevalence. Although Somalia’s Federal Government has formally recognized nutrition as a development priority within its National Development Plan, translating this policy commitment into financed, multi-year programming has remained constrained by insecurity, weak domestic revenue generation, and the continued dominance of short-cycle humanitarian financing over systems-strengthening investment.2 This structural mismatch between policy intent and implementation capacity helps explain why successive humanitarian responses, however well-resourced, have not translated into sustained reductions in malnutrition prevalence. The present commentary is distinct from earlier reviews of malnutrition determinants in Somalia in that it does not simply catalogue risk factors; rather, it synthesizes epidemiological, health-systems, and governance evidence specifically to argue for a shift in financing and programming architecture, and it situates this argument within Somalia’s current policy discussions on the transition from humanitarian to development-oriented nutrition financing.

Epidemiological Burden and Trajectory

The nutritional indicators for children in Somalia are among the most alarming globally. National surveys have consistently documented high prevalences of wasting, stunting, and underweight across the country’s diverse livelihood zones, with particularly severe burdens in the Bay, Hiran, Lower Shabelle, and Banadir regions. Estimates derived from national surveys and IPC analyses indicate wasting prevalences exceeding 20% in some regions, far surpassing the WHO emergency threshold of 15%.3 Chronic malnutrition, measured as stunting, reflects the accumulated burden of inadequate nutrition over time and affects over 31% of Somali children under five, with serious implications for cognitive development, educational attainment, and long-term economic productivity.4

The national prevalence of wasting, stunting, and low mid-upper arm circumference in children aged 6–59 months has been estimated at 21%, 31%, and 36%, respectively.3 A review conducted by Renzaho et al evaluating the performance of Integrated Management of Acute Malnutrition (IMAM) programs across Somalia found that, while therapeutic feeding programs achieved reasonable cure rates, high relapse and default rates undermined sustained recovery, reflecting inadequate post-discharge community support and household food insecurity.5 This finding mirrors evidence from analogous fragile settings, where treatment without addressing household-level structural determinants produces high recurrence rates.6

The 2022 Horn of Africa drought compounded pre-existing vulnerabilities, with estimates suggesting that nearly 43,000 people in Somalia perished by late 2022, half of them children under five. Somalia’s dependence on wheat imports—previously sourced predominantly from Ukraine and Russia—further exposed the fragility of its food supply chains to geopolitical disruptions. Acute food insecurity and acute malnutrition are functionally co-constituted in the Somali context, with household food insecurity serving as the single strongest proximate determinant of poor nutritional outcomes across all anthropometric indicators.7

Structural Determinants Beyond Acute Hunger

While hunger and food scarcity are the most visible manifestations of the malnutrition crisis, a growing body of evidence emphasizes that malnutrition is fundamentally a product of structural and social determinants that intersect with individual and household-level vulnerabilities. The UNICEF Malnutrition Framework—articulated through immediate, underlying, and basic causes—provides an organizing logic for understanding that inadequate dietary intake and infection (immediate causes) are themselves products of household food insecurity, inadequate care practices, and limited access to health services (underlying causes), which in turn reflect social, economic, political, and structural conditions (basic causes).8

In Somalia, the basic causes of malnutrition operate with particular intensity. Three decades of armed conflict have destroyed agricultural infrastructure, displaced pastoral communities, and depopulated functional governance institutions. The destruction of primary health care networks has eliminated community-level platforms for nutrition counseling, growth monitoring, and disease prevention—all of which function as critical buffers against child malnutrition. Landry et al have documented that Fragile and Conflict-Affected States (FCAS) systematically fail to achieve Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) related to health and nutrition precisely because conflict undermines the health system foundations upon which nutrition interventions depend.9

Social determinants further compound biological vulnerability. Low maternal educational attainment, gender inequality in household decision-making, cultural practices that prioritize adult male nutrition over children and women, and limited access to antenatal and postnatal care collectively perpetuate inadequate infant and young child feeding (IYCF) practices. Studies examining the socioecological drivers of malnutrition across African contexts have demonstrated that maternal literacy, access to diverse diets, safe water and sanitation, and political stability are among the most powerful predictors of child nutritional status.10 In Somalia, all of these social determinants remain severely compromised.

Climate change adds a third structural layer to this already complex causation map. Somalia’s predominantly agropastoral economy is acutely sensitive to rainfall variability. The enhanced vegetation index—a proxy for rainfall and agricultural productivity—has been identified as one of the strongest predictors of acute malnutrition risk in Somalia, with a single unit increase in vegetation index associated with a 38–59% reduction in malnutrition indicators.3 As climate change accelerates drought frequency and intensity, the nutritional crisis in Somalia will intensify unless mitigation and adaptation strategies are built into the architecture of food and nutrition security programming.

Governance deserves emphasis as more than a background condition: it is the determinant that mediates whether progress is made on each of the others. Weak and fragmented federal-state institutional relationships shape whether agricultural investment reaches production areas, whether primary health care financing is predictable enough to sustain community nutrition platforms, and whether social protection programs can be targeted and delivered without capture or diversion. Conflict-affected and fragile states research demonstrates that health and nutrition outcomes track institutional capacity and stability more closely than they track aid volume, underscoring that governance strengthening is not a peripheral concern but a precondition for the other elements of a sustainable nutrition strategy to succeed.2,9 Consequently, any framework for durable change in Somalia must treat governance and institutional capacity-building as an explicit, resourced component rather than an implicit assumption.

Limitations of Purely Humanitarian Approaches

Humanitarian nutrition interventions in Somalia have saved countless lives and deserve recognition for their contribution under extraordinarily difficult conditions. Outpatient Therapeutic Programs (OTPs), Targeted Supplementary Feeding Programs (TSFPs), Community Management of Acute Malnutrition (CMAM) protocols, and emergency food distributions represent the operational backbone of the humanitarian response. However, these modalities share a fundamental design limitation: they are engineered for emergency response and time-limited programming cycles rather than for building the durable systems and structural conditions that prevent malnutrition from recurring at scale.

The humanitarian funding cycle itself creates perverse incentives. Annual emergency funding appeals prioritize acute life-saving interventions over multi-year investments in primary health care, agriculture, education, and social protection—the very sectors whose strengthening would reduce the future demand for emergency humanitarian response. A review of systematic reviews on health system strengthening in fragile and conflict-affected states found that while community-based interventions and CHW models demonstrate effectiveness, their impact is consistently undermined by weak links to functional health systems, insufficient training capacity, and funding discontinuity.11

The relapse evidence is particularly instructive in this regard. Studies of children treated for SAM in outpatient therapeutic programs reveal substantial rates of relapse and re-admission within 12 months of discharge, reflecting that therapeutic treatment in the absence of household food security improvement, sustained community follow-up, and behavioral change support produces only transient gains.6 Community health workers (CHWs), who serve as the operational link between therapeutic services and household-level support, face critical barriers in conflict-affected and fragile settings, including programme disruption due to insecurity, limited supervision, and inadequate referral systems.12

Nutrition interventions in Somalia also face the challenge of program coverage. In many rural and peri-urban areas, geographic inaccessibility, insecurity, and community distrust of formal institutions limit uptake. Families in internally displaced persons (IDP) camps demonstrate severe acute and global malnutrition rates of 12.1% and 19.9% respectively, indicating that even populations in relative proximity to humanitarian services experience high burdens.13,14 Programs must therefore confront not only resource constraints but fundamental obstacles of reach, trust, and relevance. It is worth noting that some of the evidence cited above on relapse and community health worker performance is drawn from other fragile and conflict-affected settings rather than from Somalia specifically; while these findings are broadly consistent with the patterns observed in Somali IMAM programs, Somalia-specific longitudinal data on relapse and program discontinuation remain limited, and this is itself a priority area for future in-country research rather than an established Somalia-specific finding.

A Framework for Sustainable Action

Strengthening the Primary Health Care Architecture

Durable reductions in child malnutrition in Somalia require primary health care (PHC) infrastructure capable of delivering preventive and promotive nutrition services—growth monitoring, infant and young child feeding counseling, micronutrient supplementation, and disease prevention—routinely and at scale. Currently, Somalia’s health system remains critically fragile, with significant gaps in health facility density, trained healthcare workers, supply chains, and quality of care. The Federal Government of Somalia’s National Development Plan 2020–2024 identifies health system strengthening as a priority, but implementation has been constrained by insecurity and financing shortfalls.2 International partners should align their health investments with the government’s Essential Package of Health Services framework, prioritizing community-level platforms over facility-only approaches.

Training and deploying a cadre of community-based nutrition volunteers and community health workers with the capacity to conduct screening using mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) measurements, provide IYCF counseling, and facilitate linkages with OTP and TSFP programs represents a cost-effective strategy that has demonstrated impact in analogous settings.12 The Somalia 2021 IMAM Guidelines already articulate MUAC-based community screening as the operational standard; the challenge lies in ensuring consistent implementation across all regions, including those under non-state control.

Transforming Food Systems for Nutritional Security

Food system transformation—rather than food distribution—must become the centerpiece of Somalia’s long-term anti-malnutrition strategy. Somalia’s agricultural and pastoral sectors have the capacity to produce diverse, nutritious foods, but decades of conflict, climate shocks, and underinvestment have severely reduced productive capacity. Investments in small-scale irrigation, climate-resilient seed varieties, postharvest storage, and market infrastructure can increase the availability and affordability of nutritious foods at the household level. Dietary diversity is a well-established protective factor against stunting and wasting; studies across Sub-Saharan African contexts confirm that a dietary diversity score of four or more food groups is independently associated with reduced odds of stunting and wasting.8

Nutrition-sensitive agriculture programs that specifically target smallholder farming households with young children and pregnant and lactating women represent a particularly high-impact investment. Linking agricultural support with IYCF education creates synergistic pathways through which food production gains translate into improved nutritional status. The FAO Regional Overview of Food Security and Nutrition in Africa documents that countries with stronger agricultural diversity indicators demonstrate substantially lower rates of childhood wasting and stunting.15

Scaling Nutrition-Sensitive Social Protection

Social protection programs—including conditional and unconditional cash transfers, food vouchers, and livelihood support—have accumulated substantial evidence of effectiveness in improving household food security and reducing child malnutrition across low-income and fragile settings. Cash-based interventions allow households to exercise choice in meeting their nutritional needs while stimulating local markets. Evidence from the Cash Plus for Nutrition program implemented in Somalia’s Bay and Hiran regions suggests that cash assistance combined with community nutrition support can produce measurable improvements in wasting among enrolled children.13

Scaling social protection in Somalia requires addressing governance and targeting challenges specific to the Somali context, including the management of programs in areas with limited civil registration systems and the need to ensure that benefits reach women—who bear primary responsibility for child feeding—rather than being captured by male household heads. Integrating nutrition-sensitive conditions into social protection programs, such as requirements for growth monitoring attendance and participation in IYCF education sessions, can amplify nutritional impacts beyond household food security gains.

Gender-Responsive Nutrition Programming

Women’s empowerment, education, and agency are among the most powerful structural determinants of child nutritional outcomes. Maternal education is consistently associated with improved feeding practices, increased uptake of health services, and better anthropometric outcomes in children across all low-income country settings.9 In Somalia, female literacy rates remain low, access to formal education is constrained by insecurity and cultural norms, and women’s participation in household decision-making—particularly regarding food allocation and health-seeking behavior—is limited in many communities.

Gender-responsive nutrition programming explicitly recognizes that improving child nutrition requires addressing the constraints on women’s time, resources, knowledge, and agency. This includes investing in girls’ secondary education, supporting women’s economic empowerment, engaging male household members as allies in IYCF promotion, and ensuring that nutrition services are designed to accommodate women’s practical barriers to access, including distance, time, and childcare responsibilities. Keats et al systematic review confirms that maternal nutritional education and counseling, particularly when delivered through community platforms, produces significant improvements in infant and child feeding practices and anthropometric status.16

Multi-Sectoral Coordination and Domestic Investment

Addressing child malnutrition in Somalia ultimately requires coherent multi-sectoral coordination between health, agriculture, education, water and sanitation, and social protection sectors at federal and state levels. The current governance architecture—characterized by fragmented federal-state relationships, parallel humanitarian and development programming streams, and limited domestic resource mobilization—undermines the coherence required for multi-sectoral nutrition programming. Building the institutional capacity of the federal and state ministries of health and agriculture to plan, finance, and implement nutrition-sensitive programs is as important as any individual intervention.

Domestic resource mobilization for nutrition represents both a governance challenge and a political economy question. Somalia’s public expenditure on health and nutrition remains among the lowest globally. International donors and partners can support the transition from humanitarian to development financing by providing multi-year budget support aligned with the government’s national plans, rather than project-specific emergency funding. This requires donor willingness to accept the risks associated with systems strengthening in fragile states—a form of financing that is inherently longer-term and less amenable to short-cycle accountability mechanisms.

Conclusion

Child malnutrition in Somalia is not an intractable natural condition; it is the product of identifiable structural failures that are, in principle, amenable to policy and programmatic intervention. An estimated 1.7 million acutely malnourished Somali children in 2025 represent not only a humanitarian emergency but a fundamental indictment of development trajectories that have failed to invest in the foundations of human health and dignity.1 Moving beyond humanitarian responses requires political will, sustained financing, gender-responsive programming, climate adaptation, and the coherent application of evidence across interconnected sectors. This transition will not be straightforward: donor financing instruments remain structured around annual humanitarian appeals rather than multi-year development budgets, domestic revenue mobilization is constrained by weak state capacity and insecurity in parts of the country, and coordination across federal and state institutions remains fragmented. These are not reasons to defer the shift toward development-oriented programming, but they define the near-term priorities against which progress should be measured: predictable multi-year financing instruments, measurable governance and public financial management benchmarks, and phased geographic scale-up that begins in areas of relative stability before extending to harder-to-reach regions.

The medical and public health communities in Somalia—including institutions such as Al Hayat Medical University and the growing cadre of Somali-trained health professionals—have a critical role to play in generating context-specific evidence, training the next generation of nutrition practitioners, and advocating for policy environments that prioritize structural solutions over episodic emergency relief. Somalia’s children deserve not merely to survive acute malnutrition but to thrive with adequate nutrition as a foundation for their full development. Achieving this will require the sustained commitment of the Somali state, its international partners, and its civil society to a long-term, development-oriented nutrition agenda grounded in equity, structural analysis, and evidence-based practice.
