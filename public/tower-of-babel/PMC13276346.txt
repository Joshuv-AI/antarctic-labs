Protocol for direct in vivo evaluation of the stretch reflex in adult mice
Authors: Travis M Rotterman, Caroline Casiano-Rivera, Paul Nardelli, T Richard Nichols, Francisco J Alvarez, Timothy C Cope
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySensory feedback from muscle afferents that recruit spinal pathways is essential for coordination and stability. Dysregulation of these neural circuits may result in impaired coordinated motor behavior. Here, we present a protocol for assessing the stretch reflex in the adult decerebrate mouse. We describe steps for leg dissection, recording setup, decerebration, and reflex recording. This protocol can capture changes in muscle mechanics, sensory encoding, and ultimately muscle force generation under varying conditions.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Rotterman et al.1Subject areas: Neuroscience, Systems biology, Biotechnology and bioengineering

Before you begin

Sensory feedback from muscles, joints, and skin drives an extensive neural network in the spinal cord that influences motor coordination.2,3 The receptors of origin of muscle feedback include Golgi tendon organs and primary and secondary receptors from muscle spindles. The combined feedback from these receptors reports the distribution of stresses and strains within the musculoskeletal system and regulates the mechanical properties of this system in the face of internal and external disturbances.2,4

The stretch reflex circuit, one component of this network, arises from muscle spindle receptors and provides local regulation of individual muscles. The reflex ensures that muscles present a spring-like property that contributes to coordination and stability.5,6,7 Group Ia afferents from spindle primary receptors and their monosynaptic connections to motoneurons are the main contributors to this reflex,8,9 although pathways from spindle secondary receptors10,11,12 and autogenic excitation from Golgi tendon organs during locomotion13,14 also contribute. When the monosynaptic pathway arising from Ia afferents is blocked after transection and repair of muscle nerves.15,16, leaving other pathways of the network less disturbed,17 joint stiffness (the ratio of stress to strain) is reduced and interjoint coordination is disrupted during behaviors that feature active lengthening (eccentric work) such as walking down stairs or ramps.7,18

Based on the demonstrated importance of the stretch reflex for motor coordination in the real world, measures of the efficacy of this response in animal models of motor disorders provide important insights into the basis of the disrupted motor coordination due to nerve injury or disease. Such measures have revealed the mechanisms by which the stretch reflex is blocked following peripheral nerve injury and repair.1,19,20,21 This work has provided critical new information about the interactions between neurons and glia in the spinal cord, including the role of microglia in maintaining synaptic integrity and in plasticity in the face of injury.1,22,23

While many studies of reflex action in health and disease rely on the use of the minimally invasive H-reflex in research on both human and nonhuman animal subjects24,25,26,27, this technique is limited in excluding properties of the originating receptors and in activating large, myelinated axons that may not arise from muscle spindle receptors.20 In contrast, the method described here allows evaluation of all components of the entire reflex circuit, including alterations in the mechanical properties of muscle, mechanical properties and integrity of the originating spindle receptors, and the monosynaptic connections from primary spindle afferents to motoneurons. Disorders of these components include demyelination of sensory pathways, loss of monosynaptic connections described above,15,16 changes in muscle mechanics following stroke,28 or changes in the strength of receptor signaling that occur in aging.29

Innovation

Prior studies of mouse spinal reflex pathways relied on anesthetized preparations or ex vivo approaches that obscure intact circuit level dynamics, for example masking propriosensory afferent encoding to naturalistic stretch. Here, we introduce an approach that allows direct quantification of sensorimotor connectivity in vivo in an unanesthetized but insensate preparation. Unlike prior approaches, this methodology enables investigators to engage the complete neural circuitry required for reflex generation in response to stretch. This protocol further provides a platform for interrogating neural circuit level dysfunction in varying neurodegenerative conditions, neuropathies, and injuries.

Note: If the experimenter wants to account for cross-species comparisons, then the precise amount of stretch can be calculated via the equation for muscle strain defined as (ΔL⁄Lo) × 100%, where ΔL is the change in muscle length and Lois the initial length of the muscle. Prior study in the cat32 and rat11 preferentially used a strain of 7% to closely match that of slow walking.33 To accurately measure Lo the muscle is first attached to the servomotor lever via the tendon and the length of the muscle is stretched to a distance that generates 10nM of force (proxy for appropriate resting muscle length). We refer to this as a resting state. A ruler can be used to measure from the tendon at the origin to the tie around the tendon that is attaching the muscle to the lever to obtain the entire muscle length. This distance in mm is Lo. For example, if your lateral gastrocnemius muscle is 12mm long (Figure S1), at a 7% strain your ΔL = 0.84mm.

Expected outcomes

Proper decerebration is critical to ensure successful transition of the animal to a reflexive state following removal of isoflurane. Common errors that can occur include inadvertent damage to the brainstem, excessive blood loss, or insufficient time for residual isoflurane to dissipate, each of which can result in failure to elicit stretch reflexes. A minimum recovery period of 30 min post-decerebration is typically required for peak reflex activity. At this time, animals should exhibit a consistent limb withdrawal in response to a mild contralateral toe pinch. Once this reflexive state is confirmed, experimental data collection can begin. No stimulus-evoked EMG activity should be observed while the animal remains under anesthesia though it is plausible to observe spontaneous activity while the animal expires any remaining isoflurane.

Ramp–hold–release stretch: This stretch paradigm recruits a combination of muscle afferents, including Ia, Ib, and group II afferents. During the ramp phase, a robust EMG response is typically observed, which largely reflects mechanically induced activation of muscle afferents associated with muscle lengthening. During the subsequent hold phase, EMG activity is generally reduced, although its presence and amplitude may vary depending on the excitability of spinal circuitry and preparation variability. Increased baseline muscle force typically results in larger EMG amplitudes and recruitment of additional motor units compared to stretches initiated from rest.

Vibration: Application of high-frequency vibration to the tendon predominantly activates Ia afferents at a ratio of 1:1, providing strong monosynaptic excitation to the corresponding motor pool. This stimulus typically elicits sustained EMG activity and force production throughout the duration of vibration, serving as a reliable indicator of intact and responsive reflex circuitry.

Limitations

In addition to the technical challenges associated with decerebration, several biological variables must be carefully controlled to maintain a viable and stable preparation. One of the most important among these is the maintenance of body temperature, which is essential for preserving physiological function throughout the experiment. Regulation of fluid balance is also critical to prevent dehydration; however, excessive fluid delivery can lead to edema. Stability of the preparation is monitored over time by ensuring consistent EMG activity in response to stretch and by providing an occasional pinch to the contralateral paw to ensure a withdrawal reflex is elicited. If neither of these pertubations elicit a response by the animal after multiple attempts, we would suggest terminating the experiment.

Another limitation of this preparation is the potential for fluctuations in neural excitability over the course of a recording session. Even following successful decerebration, intrinsic changes in spinal excitability may occur and are only partially under experimental control. These fluctuations can be particularly problematic during transient episodes of spasticity, which may introduce substantial artifacts into EMG and force recordings during stretch trials. In such cases, additional stretch trials may be required to ensure collection of a sufficient number of “artifact-free” trials (e.g., ≥100) for analysis. Respiration-related motion artifacts represent another common source of contamination in the EMG and force traces. When respiratory movements interfere with signal quality, additional trials, again, may be necessary to obtain clean and analyzable stretch responses.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Properly grounding the electrophysiology rig can vary wildly depending on, for example, the location, other electronics in the surrounding area, and the construction of the equipment.

Potential solution

Prior to experimentation on a live animal, we highly recommend optimizing this by placing the EMG recording wires into a cup of saline and running practice trials. Adjustments may need to be made to reduce the background noise level. Some setups may require a Faraday cage to minimize electronic contamination from the surrounding environment. If the noise is specific to 60 Hz (or 50 Hz in the EU), an A-M Systems Humbug noise eliminator may be linked between the differential amplifier and the CED board.

Problem 2

Drastic changes in body temperature when moving the animal (under or above 36.5–38 oC), particularly from the surgical platform to the stereotaxic frame.

Potential solution

Maintaining body temperature in the physiological range (36.5–38 oC) is paramount for the health and wellbeing of the animal. Therefore, it is critical that the rectal temperature probe is securely positioned, as small movements can dislodge it. Once the probe is inserted, take a piece of the Transpore™ tape and first wrap it all the way around the probe wire then wrap around the tail and back around the probe. Continuously check that this is not loose when moving the animal around.

Problem 3

As the muscle is being stretched, one of the EMG wires may extrude from the muscle. This will result in a large introduction of noise into your recording. The source of this noise is not always obvious given that the muscle is covered in cotton.

Potential solution

This problem can easily be overcome. If you suddenly notice a large amount of noise in your trace and nothing obvious has changed on the prep, lift the cotton and make sure that both EMG wires are still inserted into the muscle. If not, stop the recording, re-insert, and see if the noise artifact is now resolved.

Problem 4

Oscillations in the motor force trace during the ramp stretch.

Potential solution

If the motor is not completely secure on the table, oscillations can occur particularly in the length and force traces. If this occurs, you need to place some stabilizing post or clamps to prevent any movement of the motor during stretching. Make sure this is properly secure prior to performing the decerebration. Additionally, this could result from the tendon not being tied directly to the motor lever and excessive amount of slack can cause the motor to go into high frequency oscillations.

Problem 5

The decerebration step is a pivotal point that will either facilitate a successful experiment or result in immediate unwanted euthanasia.

Potential solution

While this step is not particularly challenging from a technical perspective, it is the step that requires the most practice to improve speed and accuracy of the transection. It is also important to keep in mind that the total blood volume of an adult mouse, depending on weight, is approximately 2ml. Therefore, any excessive bleeding cannot to be tolerated by the animal. It is critical to master this step prior to attempting data collection by aspirating pooling blood and getting the cranium packed with Hemosponge and cotton as quickly as possible. Our recommendation is to optimize this step, and keep the animal alive for at least one hour prior to attempting an experiment with data collection.

If excessive bleeding continues, then you may also tie off both carotid arteries at the start of the experiment, prior to the leg dissection. We conducted experiments in which we tied the carotids and in others we followed the steps under the decerebration section as stated above. In our experience this only provides a marginal improvement, while increasing the risk of vagal nerve damage.
