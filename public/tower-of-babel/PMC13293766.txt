Digital connectivity and social wellbeing in a polycrisis setting: longitudinal, nationwide, population-weighted evidence from Myanmar, 2021 to 2024
Authors: Hein Thu, Jumana Hashim, Yuxuan Gu, Shahmir H Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundDigital technologies increasingly mediate social life, yet their relationship with social wellbeing in polycrisis settings where armed conflict, economic collapse, and surveillance coexist remains poorly understood. Nationwide evidence on multiple dimensions of digital connectivity and social wellbeing in active conflict settings remains scarce. Myanmar offers a critical case: rapid pre-2021 digital expansion has given way to recurrent shutdowns and worsening affordability amid conflict since 2021. We aimed to assess associations between multiple dimensions of digital connectivity and social wellbeing in Myanmar's polycrisis context, and describe national levels and inequalities in each.MethodsWe used data from the Myanmar Household Welfare Survey (MHWS), a nationwide, population-weighted panel survey across eight rounds (December 2021 to December 2024) covering 310 of 330 townships. Across 34,783 unique respondents, 100,003 survey responses were completed. Longitudinal analyses drew on the full panel; cross-sectional analyses focused on 16,454 respondents in the final two rounds with social wellbeing data. Regression models assessed associations between four digital connectivity indicators and four social wellbeing outcomes, adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, and geographic confounders.FindingsDigital disconnectivity was pervasive: over half lacked regular internet access (54.9% [8033/16,454], SE 0.6), computer ownership was rare (10.5% [2228/16,454], SE 0.3), and most households had fewer than one device per adult. Internet access ranged fivefold from 13.6% [117/502] (95% CI: 9.4–19.2%) in Rakhine to 70.1% [1770/2405] (95% CI: 67.3–72.8%) in Yangon. Intermittent internet use was associated with greater loneliness (AOR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.15–1.58) and friend conflict (AOR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.36–2.73); higher device density was independently associated with greater loneliness. Consistent internet access was protective against inability to contact friends. No digital indicator was significantly associated with social trust.InterpretationProfound inequalities in digital connectivity persist across Myanmar's polycrisis, leaving conflict-affected populations most excluded. Where connectivity exists, its relationship with social wellbeing is nuanced: consistent internet access supports relational reach, but intermittent access may amplify loneliness and interpersonal conflict. Digital connectivity was not associated with social trust in this analysis; understanding what shapes trust will require measures of governance and community conditions beyond the scope of these data. Policies should prioritize service reliability; ongoing monitoring, broader crisis survey wellbeing measurement, and qualitative work are needed.FundingThis analysis received no specific funding; the implementation of MHWS survey was funded by the Livelihoods and Food Security Fund (LIFT).Keywords: Digital connectivity, Social wellbeing, Health equity, Polycrisis, Myanmar

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar from inception to March 2026 using multiple combinations of terms including “digital connectivity,” “digital divide,” “digital inclusion,” “internet access,” “mobile phone,” “social wellbeing,” “loneliness,” “social trust,” “conflict-affected,” and “low-income countries,” without language restrictions, supplemented by manual searching and reference list screening. A substantial body of digital divide and digital inclusion scholarship (spanning foundational frameworks on technology and social inclusion, work on digital inequalities among vulnerable and socially excluded populations, and research on mobile communication among displaced persons) has established that meaningful connectivity requires more than device access, encompassing skills, social support, and the tangible outcomes of digital engagement. However, this literature has focused primarily on access gaps, digital skills, and qualitative experiences of connectivity rather than on quantitative associations between multiple dimensions of connectivity and distinct social wellbeing outcomes; and research specifically examining active conflict or polycrisis settings remains largely qualitative, small-scale, or focused on mental health broadly. Systematic reviews document that digital technology can reduce social isolation and support connectedness, though this evidence is predominantly drawn from stable, high-income contexts. Nationwide, population-weighted evidence on associations between multiple dimensions of digital connectivity and distinct social wellbeing outcomes in conflict or polycrisis settings is scarce, and population-level data on digital connectivity patterns in Myanmar since 2021 are lacking.

Added value of this study

Drawing on 100,003 survey responses completed across 34,783 unique respondents over eight survey rounds (December 2021 to December 2024) and covering 310 of 330 townships, this study adds nationwide, longitudinal, population-weighted evidence on digital connectivity trends and their associations with social wellbeing during a polycrisis. Among 16,454 respondents with social wellbeing data, it reveals that associations between connectivity and wellbeing are heterogeneous and outcome-specific: consistent internet access supports the ability to maintain friend contact, while intermittent access is associated with greater loneliness and friend conflict, and higher device density is independently associated with greater loneliness after full adjustment. Social trust was unassociated with any digital indicator and was instead strongly patterned by perceived local security, suggesting limits to what digital connectivity alone can do for this dimension of social wellbeing under conditions of ongoing conflict.

Implications of all the available evidence

Across existing digital divide scholarship and our findings, digital connectivity in crisis settings cannot be assumed to uniformly improve social wellbeing; the quality and consistency of connectivity matters as much as access itself, consistent with longstanding evidence that first-level access gaps capture only part of the picture. Policies should prioritize service reliability and affordability alongside device distribution, and humanitarian programming should leverage phone-based channels where internet infrastructure remains disrupted. For social trust, digital connectivity was not associated with this outcome in our analysis; identifying what shapes trust in this setting will require data beyond the scope of this study, including direct measures of governance, institutional functioning, and community-level dynamics. Continued longitudinal monitoring of digital connectivity and social wellbeing in crisis settings, broader integration of social outcomes into crisis surveillance surveys, and qualitative research examining how populations navigate unstable connectivity are needed to strengthen this evidence base.

Introduction

Polycrises, defined as the causal entanglement of crises across multiple global systems where conjoined harms are different from, and generally worse than, those each crisis would produce in isolation, have direct implications for social wellbeing and cohesion.1,2 We use this term to describe Myanmar's current situation, in which armed conflict, political instability, economic collapse, and telecommunications disruption are concurrent and mutually reinforcing. Constraints on movement and communication disrupt everyday relationships, affecting contact with friends and family, loneliness, and trust within communities.3 Myanmar is a stark case: amid conflict since 2021, political instability has intersected with escalating armed conflict, economic contraction, and worsening humanitarian access, producing displacement and disrupted service delivery at scale.4 An estimated 16 million people require urgent humanitarian assistance,5 and Myanmar ranks second globally for conflict severity in the most recent ACLED Conflict Index.6 Evidence from conflict-affected settings suggests that violence erodes social and political trust and the broader “social fabric”.7 Yet contemporary evidence on social wellbeing in polycrisis contexts remains largely qualitative or small-scale,3 leaving a major gap in population-level data to quantify population-level patterns and inequalities across connectivity, loneliness, and trust.

These disruptions occur against a backdrop of increasingly precarious digital connectivity. Foundational scholarship on digital inclusion established that what matters is not simply the physical availability of devices and infrastructure, but people's capacity to use those technologies to engage in meaningful social and economic practices, a shift that reframed the field from a narrow “digital divide” framing toward a broader concern with digital inclusion and social equity.8 Van Dijk's three-level digital divide framework characterizes digital inequalities across first-level material access to devices and infrastructure; second-level patterns, frequency, and diversity of use; and third-level tangible outcomes that individuals derive from digital engagement.9, 10, 11, 12 Critically, these levels are not independent: social and digital inequalities are structurally interlinked, such that those already disadvantaged in economic, cultural, and social terms tend to be disproportionately excluded from translating digital engagement into tangible benefit, with access best understood as a gradation rather than a binary condition.13 In contexts of conflict and forced displacement, this complexity intensifies further. Research on mobile communication among displaced populations has demonstrated that connectivity cannot be assumed to be uniformly beneficial: while digital access can facilitate contact with social networks and access to information, it can simultaneously introduce surveillance risks, emotional burdens, and new pressures of social exclusion, particularly when access is unreliable, costly, or mediated by coercive state actors.14 Myanmar's conflict context illustrates these tensions acutely, and motivates a multi-indicator approach to digital connectivity that distinguishes between access, use, and the conditions under which connectivity is experienced.

How digital connectivity shapes social wellbeing in such settings is, however, poorly understood. Social wellbeing, broadly understood as the appraisal of one's circumstances and functioning in society, is a heterogeneous construct encompassing subjective emotional states such as loneliness, behavioral dimensions of social functioning such as network maintenance and interpersonal conflict, and community-level trust and cohesion, all of which have well-established links to mental and physical health outcomes.15, 16, 17 While a substantial literature links digital communication to maintained contact, lower loneliness, and strengthened social support when mobility is constrained,18 most evidence comes from stable contexts where access is reliable and participation carries fewer risks. In high-insecurity and high-surveillance environments, connectivity may operate differently: monitoring, censorship, and episodic shutdowns can induce self-censorship and weaken interpersonal trust, potentially transforming technologies of connection into sources of stress.19,20 Myanmar is a critical case because it illustrates both sides of this tension. Rapid digital expansion during the 2010s, driven by policy liberalization, falling costs, and smartphone diffusion, brought over 90% of the population within range of 3G/4G networks by 2018.21 Amid conflict since 2021, connectivity has deteriorated through recurrent internet shutdowns, conflict-related infrastructure damage, and worsening affordability,22,23 while widespread mobile-first communication now coexists with digital authoritarian practices and episodic disruption that pose further risks to everyday social engagement.24,25 The association between digital connectivity and social wellbeing should therefore not be assumed to be uniformly beneficial, and may vary in direction and magnitude by both the dimension of connectivity and the specific social wellbeing outcome considered.26 Nationwide, population-weighted estimates of digital connectivity across key domains for the post-2021 context are particularly lacking.27,28

This study addresses these evidence gaps using the Myanmar Household Welfare Survey (MHWS), a nationwide, longitudinal, population-weighted household dataset.29 Our aims are to: 1) estimate national levels and inequalities across key domains of digital connectivity and social wellbeing; 2) characterize the distribution and correlates of digital connectivity across geographic, demographic, and socioeconomic groups; and 3) assess whether differences in digital connectivity are associated with social wellbeing outcomes in Myanmar's fragile and high-surveillance context. By providing population-based evidence where routine monitoring is severely constrained, these analyses can help identify where digital exclusion and social disconnection are concentrated and inform more targeted policy and humanitarian responses.

Methods

This study used data from the Myanmar Household Welfare Survey (MHWS),29 a nationwide phone-based panel survey, in which households were re-contacted in successive rounds, implemented by the International Food Policy Research Institute (IFPRI) across eight rounds between December 2021 and December 2024, designed to monitor household welfare and socioeconomic conditions following the February 2021 military takeover. The implementation of the MHWS was in collaboration with Myanmar Survey Research (MSR), a private survey research company based in Myanmar. At the time of MHWS survey initiation, MSR already owned a database of 280,274 phone numbers of adults who consented to be contacted for future participation in phone survey data collection, including geographical information of the township of residence of the respondent. Also, for every round of survey, each phone call started with a short introduction of the study and informed consent.

Data were collected using computer-assisted telephone interviewing (CATI) by trained enumerators.30 Respondents were purposively selected from MSR's database, stratified by township (n = 330) and sampled proportional to population size, with oversampling to mitigate non-response. Quotas targeted sex parity, urban-rural distributions aligned with the 2014 Census, low educational attainment, and agricultural livelihoods to improve representativeness. Unlike many phone surveys, the MHWS surveyed any household member aged 18 to 74 who answered the phone, with up to five contact attempts before replacement. The survey reached respondents in 310 of 330 townships (93.9%), including all townships in conflict-affected states such as Rakhine, comparing favorably with prior in-person surveys that excluded townships due to security constraints.31 Although Lambrecht et al. demonstrated that the MHWS methodology produces estimates broadly comparable to those from face-to-face surveys after post-stratification weighting,31 the phone-based design inherently excludes households without mobile phone access. Survey weights incorporating post-stratification and entropy balancing support nationwide inference among the phone-accessible population, though estimates may not fully capture the experiences of those entirely outside mobile networks.

Across eight rounds, 100,003 survey responses were collected from 34,783 unique respondents; 16,454 participated in at least one of the final two rounds. The total number of survey responses exceeds unique respondents because the MHWS panel design re-contacted households across successive rounds using unique household identifiers, with approximately 12,000–13,000 respondents surveyed per round; households that could not be re-contacted were replaced with new respondents from the same townships, matched on urban/rural residence, sex, education, and livelihood quotas.31 Analyses focused on late rounds because smartphone ownership was first collected in Round 7 and most social outcomes were introduced in Round 8, with social trust available in both. In Round 8, the household assets module (Module E, which includes smartphone counts) was not re-administered to returning households that had participated in a prior round and had not relocated, as specified in the survey's CAPI skip logic; it was asked only of new respondents. As a result, Round 8 smartphone counts were unavailable for 65.0% [7834/12,058] of respondents. For total mobile phone counts, IFPRI pre-loaded values from prior rounds into the Round 8 dataset (confirmed by 100% exact match between Round 7 and Round 8 values for returning households). Smartphone counts were not pre-loaded; following IFPRI's precedent for mobile phones, we carried forward each household's Round 7 smartphone count to fill missing Round 8 values. Given that smartphone ownership is a relatively stable household asset and the interval between Rounds 7 and 8 was approximately three months, this carry-forward was considered unlikely to introduce substantial misclassification.

Digital connectivity was assessed using four indicators informed by the first and second levels of van Dijk's three-level digital divide framework.9 Three indicators capture first-level material access: computer-type device ownership (yes/no), and counts of working mobile phones and smartphones converted to density ratios per adult household member; for descriptive analyses these were dichotomized as fewer than one versus at least one device per adult. One indicator captures a dimension of second-level usage: internet use frequency, which captured how often any household member used the internet in the past four weeks, analyzed as a four-level ordered variable (never, few times, most of the time, any time wanted); regular internet access was defined as “most of the time” or “any time wanted” for descriptive analyses.

Social wellbeing, defined as the appraisal of one's circumstances and functioning in society, is a heterogeneous construct whose specific dimensions vary across disciplines and theoretical frameworks, with no universally agreed operationalization beyond this broad framing.15,16 In the absence of a validated instrument for this construct in the Myanmar context, and given the constraints of phone-based surveying in an active conflict setting that preclude full multi-item batteries, the MHWS (implemented as a general household welfare monitoring instrument rather than a clinical tool) is used here to operationalize social wellbeing as a broad umbrella across four indicators. We identified specific items within the survey that connect to theoretically distinct and empirically recognized dimensions in the literature; while three (inability to contact friends, friend conflict, and social trust) capture behavioural and relational dimensions more precisely described as social functioning, loneliness reflects the subjective emotional experience of social disconnection and is not reducible to a functioning construct, making social wellbeing a more appropriate overarching term. Loneliness frequency (five–level scale from never to very often) is a well-established indicator of poor social wellbeing in displaced and conflict-affected populations, and is conceptually distinct from the objective isolation captured by the remaining items.32 Inability to contact any friends in Myanmar (yes/no) reflects objective social isolation (specifically the disruption of transnational social networks) a documented consequence of conflict-related displacement and infrastructure collapse.32 Friend conflict frequency (same five–level scale) captures the negative or conflictive dimension of active social relationships, a theoretically distinct domain from network absence.33 Social trust in the local area (four-point scale from very low to very high) operationalizes generalized community trust, a core dimension of social capital and cohesion.34 While these items were drawn from a general welfare survey rather than a purpose-built psychosocial tool, their wording nonetheless aligns with key components of established conceptualizations of social wellbeing (most notably social functioning and socio-emotional wellbeing) and its related constructs, as detailed above.

Adjusted models included demographic, socioeconomic, and geographic covariates. Demographics comprised sex, age, education (grouped as lower secondary or below [grades 0–9], upper secondary or some tertiary [grades 10–15], or bachelor's degree or higher [grade 16+]), and marital status (single, married, widowed, or divorced/separated). Household socioeconomic position was measured using income quartiles derived from total monthly household income aggregated across multiple sources; “prefer not to say” and “don't know” responses were recoded as missing, sources not applicable to a household were treated as zero, and total income was set to missing only when all 13 sources lacked valid data. Income quartiles were derived using household weights and linked to respondents for individual-level models. Geographic covariates included residence type (rural village tract versus urban ward), perceived local security (very insecure to very secure, with non-response recoded as missing), and a binary state versus region indicator aligned with Myanmar's first-level administrative structure; Nay Pyi Taw, administered separately as a Union Territory, was grouped with regions for analytic parsimony.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R using design-based methods via the survey package,35 applying adult population weights for individual-reported measures and household weights for household-reported measures, with standard errors accounting for household-level clustering. Sample characteristics were summarized overall and stratified by regular internet access, reporting unweighted frequencies alongside survey-weighted proportions; group differences were assessed using Rao-Scott chi-squared tests for categorical variables and survey-weighted t-tests for continuous variables.36

Temporal trends were estimated by calculating survey-weighted prevalence of key binary indicators (regular internet access, computer ownership, and higher mobile phone ownership) across all eight rounds from December 2021 to December 2024, with 95% confidence intervals using logit-based transformation. As total household size was not assessed prior to Wave 7, longitudinal changes in mobile or smartphone density could not be calculated across all rounds; instead, we present the prevalence of households owning 2 or more phones, noting that the majority of households assessed in Waves 7 and 8 had at least 2 members (94.2%). Average annual change was quantified using survey-weighted linear probability models regressing each indicator on a continuous time variable,37 with the coefficient interpretable as percentage-point change per year. Spatial patterning was visualized as choropleth maps of survey-weighted prevalence by state and region using shapefiles from the Myanmar Information Management Unit, with Shan subregions retained as separate units where available.

Separate regression models were estimated for each of the twenty-four exposure-outcome combinations (four digital indicators by four outcomes), reporting unadjusted and fully adjusted estimates. Confounders were selected a priori based on prior literature,18,38 and supported by bivariable associations. Regression models were estimated without additional survey weighting, as the covariates included in the models (sex, age, education, residence type, state/region, and household income) correspond to the variables used in the survey's post-stratification and entropy balancing procedures, making further weighting largely redundant for covariate-adjusted association estimates.39 Outcome modeling was guided by proportional odds diagnostics. Social trust was analyzed as binary (low versus high) using logistic regression after failing to satisfy ordinal modeling assumptions. Friend conflict satisfied the proportional odds assumption and was analyzed using standard proportional odds regression.40 For loneliness, the assumption was broadly supported for digital exposure variables but showed non-proportionality for sex, marital status, and perceived area security; primary results are therefore from proportional odds models for comparability, with a partial proportional odds sensitivity analysis relaxing the parallel slopes assumption for those covariates. Sensitivity results were substantively consistent with no meaningful differences in effect estimates or statistical inference. Statistical significance was assessed at α = 0.05; while 95% confidence intervals were reported to reflect estimation uncertainty around each effect estimate, p-values were additionally corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure across the twenty-four exposure-outcome combinations to assess whether any findings changed in significance after adjustment for multiple comparisons.41 Little's MCAR test was applied to assess missing data patterns among covariates in the Round 8 analytic sample,42 indicating that data were not missing completely at random (χ2 = 86.79, df = 44, p < 0.001). To assess potential bias from differential attrition between Rounds 7 and 8, a logistic regression predicting Round 8 participation from Round 7 demographic characteristics was estimated; older age (p < 0.001), female sex (p = 0.005), bachelor's-level education (p = 0.005), regular internet access (p < 0.001), and residence in a region rather than an ethnic state (p < 0.001) were independently associated with retention. Stabilized inverse probability weights, trimmed at the 1st and 99th percentiles, were derived and applied to the retained cohort (respondents participating in both rounds; n = 8404) in a sensitivity analysis re-running all adjusted regression models.

Final regression models were restricted to Round 8, where key outcomes had 0% missingness and social trust had 0.5%. Two sensitivity analyses examined wave-specific compositional differences: late-round respondents were older, more female, more urban, and more educated than those seen only in Rounds 1 to 6; and within the late-round sample, Round 7-only respondents were younger, more rural, more likely to live in an ethnic minority state, less likely to have regular internet access, and reported lower social trust than Round 8 respondents, while sex did not differ. All analyses applied survey weights derived through multi-stage procedures including post-stratification and entropy balancing, using household-level weights for household characteristics and adult population weights for individual-level analyses; while these weights calibrated to national benchmarks partially offset the compositional differences across each wave, findings should nonetheless be interpreted with some caution regarding generalizability.

Ethical statement

Ethical approval was obtained from IFPRI's Institutional Review Board (DSGD-21-0932); all respondents were provided an incentive in phone credits equivalent of $2.20 per round in Round 1 & 2 and $3.50 in subsequent rounds via MSR. The implementation of MHWS survey was funded by the Livelihoods and Food Security Fund (LIFT).

Role of the funding source

The funders of the MHWS–the Livelihoods and Food Security Fund (LIFT)–had no role in the study design, data analysis, interpretation, or writing of the present manuscript.

Temporal trends in digital connectivity

As shown in Fig. 1, mobile phone ownership (≥2 phones) was the most prevalent and stable indicator, increasing modestly from 8428 (69.7%) of 12,100 at Round 1 to 8845 (73.4%) of 12,058 at Round 8. Regular internet access was more volatile, ranging from 4887 (40.2%) of 12,142 at Round 2 to 6645 (51.5%) of 12,898 at Round 6 (October 2023), then declining to 5586 (46.3%) of 12,058 by late 2024. Computer ownership remained low and essentially unchanged (∼10%). Linear probability models confirmed these patterns, with modest positive trends for phone ownership (+1.58 percentage points/year, 95% CI: −0.10 to 3.26%) and internet access (+2.02 percentage points/year, 95% CI: −0.88 to 4.92%) and no meaningful change in computer ownership (−0.01 percentage points/year, 95% CI: −0.30 to 0.29%); smartphone prevalence (Rounds 7–8 only) was stable at ∼68% of households (not displayed).

Geographic variation in digital connectivity

Fig. 2 maps survey-weighted prevalence by state and region (Rounds 7 and 8); geographic inequalities were stark across all indicators. Regular internet access ranged from 13.6% [117/502] (95% CI: 9.4–19.2%) in Rakhine to 70.1% [1770/2405] (95% CI: 67.3–72.8%) in Yangon, a fivefold difference, with Sagaing (18.9% [413/1822], 95% CI 16.9–21.2) and Kayah (19.4% [16/79], 95% CI 10.0–34.4) also markedly low. Computer ownership was highest in Yangon (22.2% [596/2405], 95% CI 20.0–24.5) and lowest in Kayah (3.6% [4/79], 95% CI 0.9–13.0). Device saturation (≥1 per adult) varied less geographically but remained far from universal: mobile phones ranged from 28.7% [205/502] (95% CI 22.5–35.8) in Rakhine to 58.1% [1679/2405] (55.1–60.9) in Yangon, and smartphones from 25.1% [182/502] (95% CI 19.3–31.9) in Rakhine to 53.7% [1580/2405] (95% CI 50.8–56.5) in Yangon.

Demographic and contextual variation

Fig. 3 shows the three stratifiers with the largest deviations from population prevalence: perceived area security, education, and urban/rural residence (additional stratifiers in Supplementary Material 1). Area security showed the largest gradients: lacking regular internet was 71.8% [470/695] (95% CI 66.3–77.4) in very insecure versus 51.8% [3295/7205] (95% CI 50.0–53.5) in secure areas, and low social trust reached 53.2% [379/691] (95% CI 46.5–59.8) versus 7.7% [463/6066] (95% CI 6.6–9.0), a spread of 45.5 percentage points; inability to contact friends rose to 39.2% [158/440] (95% CI 30.6–47.8) in very insecure areas. Education showed the second-largest gradient for connectivity, with computer ownership deficits of 95.0% [8388/8865] (95% CI 94.4–95.7) among those with lower secondary education versus 71.7% [1812/2666] (95% CI 69.3–74.1) among graduates, though education differences in social wellbeing were modest. Among supplementary stratifiers, marital status most sharply differentiated wellbeing, with divorced or separated respondents reporting higher frequent loneliness (19.6% [51/286], 95% CI 11.0–32.4) than married respondents (4.2% [321/7888], 95% CI 3.5–5.0); sex and state/region differences were small.

Association between digital connectivity and social wellbeing

Table 2 presents unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios for each connectivity-wellbeing combination. Associations varied across outcomes and were not uniformly beneficial. For inability to contact friends, internet use showed the strongest, most consistent association: those accessing the internet any time they wanted had lower odds than never-users (AOR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.44–0.58), with a graded relationship across frequency levels; device measures were not significant after adjustment. For loneliness, associations were more complex: intermediate internet use predicted higher odds, whether a few times (AOR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.15–1.58) or most of the time (AOR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.06–1.56) versus never, whereas the most frequent users did not differ (AOR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.82–1.13). Higher mobile phone density (AOR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.17–1.44) and smartphone density (AOR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.14–1.40) were also associated with greater loneliness; computer ownership was not. For friend conflict, internet use was again associated with higher odds across all frequency levels, strongest among the most frequent users (AOR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.36–2.73), with device measures non-significant. For social trust, no digital indicator was significantly associated with low trust after full adjustment, although unadjusted associations for computer ownership, internet use, and device density were attenuated.

Discussion

Drawing on nationwide, population-weighted data from a polycrisis setting, this study documents profound inequalities in digital connectivity, with conflict-affected populations most excluded. The divergence between relatively stable device ownership and the volatility of internet access over the observation period reflects the distinct infrastructure and market dynamics underpinning each: whereas devices can be maintained through decentralized markets, internet access depends on centralized telecommunications infrastructure and affordability.43 Rising data costs following the military's 2021 price increases and ongoing currency depreciation likely further constrained affordability even where infrastructure remained functional.22 The pronounced subnational disparities, with Yangon exhibiting far higher rates of regular connectivity than conflict-affected areas such as Rakhine and Sagaing, underscore that digital connectivity cannot be inferred from device penetration alone. These findings align with digital divide scholarship showing that material connectivity inequalities persist well after physical access gaps narrow,10 and have direct policy implications: investments in digital infrastructure in crisis settings should prioritize service continuity, affordability, and safe access rather than device distribution alone. Where internet remains limited, more pragmatic phone-based approaches such as SMS, interactive voice response hotlines, and call-back mechanisms remain viable channels for humanitarian programming.44,45

Associations between digital connectivity and social wellbeing were heterogeneous and outcome-specific, and the patterns differed across levels of the digital divide framework. The dominant correlate for three of the four social wellbeing outcomes (loneliness, friend conflict, and inability to contact friends) was internet use frequency, our single second-level indicator of how often household members engaged with the internet. First-level material access measures (computer ownership, mobile phone density, smartphone density) were largely null after adjustment, with the exception of device density, which was independently associated with loneliness. More frequent internet use was strongly and dose-dependently associated with reduced odds of being unable to contact friends, suggesting that connectivity may support relational reach when mobility is constrained. However, intermediate levels of internet use and greater mobile and smartphone density were associated with higher reported loneliness and greater friend conflict, patterns that persisted after full adjustment.

Several mechanisms may help explain this. Intermittent or partial internet use may generate exclusion stress through interrelated pathways: sporadic exposure to others' online activity without the sustained engagement to participate fully, and disrupted rhythms of routine relational micro-exchanges that position individuals as observers rather than participants in their networks.46,47 The same pattern may help explain the elevated friend conflict observed alongside loneliness in the same exposure group, as missed messages and unanswered calls under intermittent use can be misread as neglect rather than as absence of stable access.48 Greater device density, our first-level material access indicator most strongly associated with a wellbeing outcome, may reflect within-household displacement, with each adult engaging through their own device and less in shared, unmediated interaction, consistent with the internet paradox hypothesis.46,49 In Myanmar's high-surveillance environment, connectivity may also increase exposure to state monitoring, misinformation, and polarizing content, which can erode interpersonal relationships and heighten conflict.50,51 Facebook in particular has been extensively documented as a vector for misinformation and coordinated propaganda in Myanmar, with the UN Fact-Finding Mission and Amnesty International both identifying its role in amplifying harmful content.52,53 The cross-sectional design does not permit causal attribution between these mechanisms, and longitudinal analyses exploiting the MHWS panel structure would help distinguish whether the dominant pathways involve exclusion stress, within-household displacement of interaction, or other factors. These findings nonetheless suggest caution against assuming that expanding connectivity will automatically reduce loneliness or improve relational quality, and indicate that those with precarious or disrupted connectivity may be at particular risk.

Beyond access, our indicators may also speak indirectly to how digital connectivity is used in this setting. Very low computer ownership alongside substantially higher mobile and smartphone density may suggest that the majority of internet users in Myanmar connect via mobile devices rather than computers, a pattern reinforced by industry estimates that around 79% of internet traffic in Myanmar is consumed via mobile phones54 and that mobile access is the dominant mode of internet usage.31 This is structurally distinct from many high-income contexts where computer-based access plays a substantial role, and it carries implications for the nature of digital engagement, including heavy reliance on smartphone applications and social media platforms, with Facebook in particular long described in qualitative research in Myanmar as effectively synonymous with the internet for many users.55 Existing qualitative work in Myanmar has further documented limited population-level digital literacy, vulnerability to misinformation, and self-censorship under surveillance as features shaping how individuals engage with online content.55,56 These contextual features likely shape how the second-level use patterns we observe translate into the social wellbeing outcomes measured here. While our data still cannot speak directly to digital skills, the type and quality of online activity, or the capacity for meaningful participation,9,12 finer-grained measures of these dimensions would refine our associations, particularly for the relational outcomes where intermittent internet use appears to matter most.

Finally, generalized social trust was not significantly associated with any digital connectivity measure after adjustment, and was instead strongly patterned by perceived local security. The contrast with relational outcomes is consistent with the conceptual distance between the constructs: loneliness, network contact, and friend conflict are proximal to patterns of mediated interaction and may therefore be sensitive to how often and how stably individuals engage online, whereas generalized trust in one's local area is shaped at the level of perceived community safety and predictability, which lies further from the dimensions of digital engagement our data could capture. Trust may, however, be sensitive to digital dimensions our measures did not capture, particularly the quality and content of online engagement and the capacity to participate meaningfully in online discourse.57,58 This is broadly consistent with evidence that armed conflict undermines both interpersonal and institutional trust primarily through the state's failure to maintain its protective function,7 and that surveillance-induced chilling effects may suppress the open communication that characterizes trust-building interactions.51,59 What our analysis can show is that digital connectivity, as captured by the indicators available here, was not associated with social trust in this setting; what determines social trust lies beyond the scope of these data. The strong patterning by perceived local security is consistent with the broader literature linking armed conflict and state failure to erosion of trust,7 but our cross-sectional design and the absence of measured governance, institutional, or community-level exposures preclude any direct claim about what would improve trust.

This study has several notable strengths. It provides nationwide, population-weighted evidence on digital connectivity and social wellbeing in a polycrisis setting, where population-level data are scarce. The MHWS reached respondents in 310 of 330 townships (93.9%), including all townships in conflict-affected states such as Rakhine, offering geographic coverage that compares favorably with prior in-person surveys that excluded townships due to security constraints. Survey weights calibrated to national, regional, and urban-rural benchmarks support population-wide inference, and item-level completeness was high across key exposure and outcome variables. The multi-indicator approach to digital connectivity, distinguishing internet use frequency, device ownership, mobile phone density, and smartphone density, moves beyond binary access measures and captures the multi-layered nature of digital connectivity, while four distinct social wellbeing outcomes allow for a more nuanced understanding of how connectivity relates to different dimensions of social life.

Several limitations should be considered. As a phone-based survey, the MHWS by design excludes households without access to a phone, likely underestimating digital exclusion among those entirely outside mobile networks; quota-based sampling targeting rural residents, low educational attainment, and agricultural livelihoods, combined with survey weights calibrated to national benchmarks, partially offsets this bias and supports nationwide inference among the reachable population. This exclusion is, however, bounded in scope: mobile phone ownership in Myanmar was already estimated at approximately 70–80% of households in the 2017 Myanmar Living Conditions Survey (a similar nationally, population-weighted, face-to-face survey) and has followed an upward trajectory since60 and, by end 2025, cellular mobile connections were equivalent to 114% of the total population, indicating that phone-based survey findings are applicable to the substantial portion of the Myanmar population.31 Importantly, the inclusion of multiple indicators beyond mobile phone ownership, including internet access frequency, smartphone density, and computer ownership, means the study still captures meaningful variation across dimensions of digital connectivity that are not reducible to phone access alone.

A limitation of particular salience given this study's outcomes is that phone-based surveys additionally may not reach households subject to active telecommunications shutdowns. Nonetheless, the validated MHWS methodology demonstrated no systematic pattern of conflict-related geographic exclusion—in contrast to in-person surveys, where conflict incidence and intensity were substantially higher in non-enumerated than enumerated townships31; notably, while the 2019 Inter-Censal Survey failed was unable to reach approximately 74% of selected enumeration areas in Rakhine State, MHWS reached all 17 Rakhine townships. Of the analytic sample, 30.4% (weighted; n = 4548) resided in the seven ethnic states (Chin, Kachin, Kayah, Kayin, Mon, Rakhine, and Shan), which encompass the majority of active conflict zones; however, Round 7–8 sample sizes in some of these states were small, particularly Kayah (n = 79) and Chin (n = 265), reflecting connectivity disruptions in the most conflict-affected areas. Fig. 2 presents state-level digital connectivity estimates, confirming that the most conflict-affected areas (Rakhine, Sagaing, Kayah) recorded the lowest internet access and device ownership, consistent with expectations of conflict-driven digital exclusion. While phone-based surveys carry well-documented limitations including shorter interview duration and potential measurement error relative to in-person administration, no viable alternative currently exists in Myanmar's polycrisis setting, where in-person enumeration is largely infeasible and updated sampling frames are unavailable; the relevant comparison is not with a methodologically perfect instrument but with an absence of population-level data entirely. Within these constraints, analyses emphasize density-based indicators (such as devices per adult household member) rather than binary access measures alone, capturing meaningful variation in connectivity constraints among the reachable population; more comprehensive characterization of those fully outside digital networks will require in-person approaches as conditions allow.

Several measurement and analytical limitations also warrant consideration. The cross-sectional regression analyses do not permit causal inference; associations may reflect reverse causation or unmeasured confounding, though we specified a priori confounders and tested progressively adjusted models to assess robustness. Internet use frequency was measured on an ordinal scale without capturing type or quality of engagement, precluding distinctions between social media, messaging, or informational browsing that may relate differently to social outcomes. Household-level measurement of device indicators may introduce mismatch if intra-household connectivity varies, and the very low prevalence of friend conflict produced a floor effect that limits the stability of estimates for that outcome. Self-reported wellbeing measures may be subject to social desirability bias in a high-surveillance environment, though the willingness of respondents to report low security and low trust provides some reassurance against uniform suppression of sensitive responses. Both the digital and social wellbeing indicators were measured using single survey items rather than validated multi-item scales, which limits assessment of measurement reliability and construct validity; no psychometrically validated instruments for these constructs currently exist for the Myanmar context, and the constraints of phone-based surveying in a conflict setting preclude administration of full multi-item batteries. While the multi-indicator approach to digital connectivity (spanning material access and usage frequency across the first and second levels of the digital divide framework) moves beyond binary access measures, key second-level dimensions including digital skills, the type and quality of online activity, and meaningful participation were not assessed, and third-level tangible outcomes of digital engagement likewise remain unmeasured. Internet use frequency captures household-level patterns rather than individual engagement quality, which may attenuate the second-level associations observable in this analysis. Finally, differential attrition meant late-round respondents were older, more urban, and more educated than early-round-only respondents, and Round 7-only respondents were disproportionately from ethnic minority states, which should be considered when interpreting generalizability to the most conflict-affected populations.

These findings reinforce and extend existing digital divide scholarship across multiple levels of the framework. Profound inequalities in material access persist across Myanmar's polycrisis, confirming that first-level divides remain far from resolved in fragile settings. At the second level, the relationship between usage frequency and social wellbeing was heterogeneous and outcome-specific, suggesting that in high-insecurity and high-surveillance environments, intermittent internet use may generate exclusion stress rather than social gain, and coercive digital environments may suppress the open engagement through which connectivity typically confers benefit. These findings offer nuanced insights into how digital divide dynamics operate in polycrisis settings, where service reliability, affordability, and the capacity for safe use shape whether connectivity at any level translates into social benefit. Moreover, for policy and humanitarian practice, findings suggest the importance of prioritizing stable and affordable access over device distribution, while recognizing that mobile phones remain a viable platform for humanitarian programming even where internet infrastructure has collapsed, with SMS, interactive voice response systems, and call-back mechanisms offering practical alternatives. The mixed associations with social wellbeing further suggest that connectivity is neither uniformly beneficial nor harmful, but deeply contingent on the conditions under which it is experienced. For social trust, neither dimension of digital connectivity captured here was associated with the outcome, and identifying the determinants of trust in this context will require data we did not have, including direct measures of governance, institutional functioning, and community-level dynamics.

Generating this kind of evidence in crisis settings remains methodologically challenging, and the approach demonstrated here offers a viable model for population-level monitoring where in-person data collection is infeasible. As the MHWS continues with additional data collection waves, more rigorous causal analyses will become feasible, and major disruptions such as the March 2025 earthquake offer important natural variation for examining how connectivity shocks affect social wellbeing. Future work would also benefit from linking survey data with other available data sources (including administrative records, satellite-derived indicators, or georeferenced event data) to more directly capture how conflict exposure and its material consequences shape the social conditions within which digital connectivity operates. Qualitative work is needed to clarify how people navigate unstable connectivity and manage the tension between staying connected and staying safe under surveillance, and to examine the more complex directional questions that quantitative research alone cannot resolve, including whether and how adversity shapes social outcomes through isolation or, alternatively, through the kinds of solidarity and cohesion that shared hardship can sometimes produce. Although the current Myanmar polycrisis context makes comprehensive in-person or community-based data collection largely impractical, future research should look to incorporate such approaches as conditions improve, to extend coverage to those entirely outside digital networks whose experiences and social wellbeing remain largely invisible in phone-based data. As polycrises become an increasingly defining feature of the contemporary world, generating rigorous population-level evidence on how digital connectivity shapes social wellbeing will be essential to designing interventions that support genuine social benefit rather than assuming that connectivity alone is sufficient.
