Swin-DRNet: A robust transformer framework for diabetic retinopathy screening under heterogeneous imaging conditions
Authors: Damodharan Palaniappan, N R Ram Mohan, T Premavathi, Yonis Gulzar, Musrrat Ali, Mohammad Shuaib Mir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic retinopathy (DR) has been known as one of the leading preventable causes of vision impairment globally and requires automated screening systems that are reliable to heterogeneous imaging conditions and severely imbalanced classes. While recent deep learning techniques have proven their capabilities within datasets, their limitations regarding large-scale remote screening of ophthalmology are encountered because of their poor generalization from one dataset to another and low sensitivity to minority disease stages. In this publication, we introduce Swin-DRNet, a robust transformer-based framework for automated screening of DR that incorporates multi-stage contrast-adaptive preprocessing, class-aware representation learning, and focal-optimized loss design, all built into the hierarchical architecture of Swin Transformers. This method is optimally suited for improving visibility of lesions, stabilizing the learning process across imbalanced classes, and increasing the robustness of screening performance when using multiple sources of fundus photographs as input. Results from extensive testing of this new approach on three publicly available benchmark datasets APTOS 2019, IDRiD, and Messidor2 demonstrate that the Swin-DRNet achieves consistently high-quality results across all datasets, with an overall accuracy of 96.68%, an F1-score of 96.20%, and a ROC-AUC score of 99.83% on the combined dataset, while also maintaining high recall rates for clinically important advanced DR stages. These findings suggest that the Swin-DRNet provides a reliable and scalable solution for providing diabetic retinopathy screening in the real world, using tele ophthalmology practices that include heterogeneous imaging platforms. Introduction

Today, artificial intelligence is the primary driver of development for clinical decision-support systems in ophthalmology, where large-scale screening of images is crucial for the early detection of disease. Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is one of the most common microvascular complications of diabetes mellitus and, according to the International Diabetes Federation, affects over 100 million people around the globe. Due to the increasing number of individuals with diabetes and the growing need for accurate and accessible DR screening solutions, particularly in rural and under-resourced countries, the demand for reliable automated DR screening options continues to grow. In this regard, the application of artificial intelligence in the analysis of fundus imaging presents a scalable opportunity to minimise both delayed diagnosis and variability among different ocular specialists. There are several stages of progression for diabetic retinopathy, from early (microaneurysms) to advanced (neovascular proliferation), and thus, assessment of severity plays an important role in the prevention of vision loss due to irreversible changes caused by advanced diabetic retinopathy. Several publicly accessible databases/collections (e.g., APTOS 2019, IDRiD, Messidor2) have been instrumental in facilitating the development and benchmarking of automated diabetic retinopathy (DR) grading systems1–3. Many of the above resources possess extensive differences among their respective imaging protocol, illumination conditions, patient demographic characteristics, and the disease characteristics of patients. Specifically, a lack of representation within the advanced classifications of diabetic retinopathy is a significant contributor to the difficulties in establishing and maintaining a stable and clinically applicable model for training purposes. As a result, DR screening solutions will require more than just high levels of accuracy within a single dataset. It will also need to provide robust performance, be able to handle heterogeneous imaging conditions across multi-source datasets, and offer the ability to interpret results when used with a variety of imaging techniques.

Convolutional neural networks (CNN) have been the primary source of good performance for the first generation of Automated DR (Diabetic Retinopathy) grading methods4–6. CNN’s ability to extract spatial features allowed for a good level of success in developing automated systems but the local area or receptive field of CNNs created challenges in establishing the larger retinal context. Hybrid architectures with attention mechanism components allowed for some of these challenges to be addressed as these types of architectures established global structural relationships and introduced long-range dependencies via convolutional representations7–9. The recent evolution of transformer-based architectures for Medical Image Analysis represent another means of applying self-attention mechanisms to model global structural relationships and build long-range dependencies in the retinal fundus images10–13. Transformer architectures still suffer from some of the same limitations, including class imbalance, domain shift and poor interpretability when used in conjunction with a wide variety of DR datasets, making their direct application on a large scale difficult in real-world DR screening settings.

This paper presents Swin-DRNet, which is an innovative method of detecting diabetic retinopathy (DR) through the use of advanced neural networks called Swin Transformers. The structure consists of several layers or building blocks with shifted window attention, allowing for efficient modeling of different retinal features at various scales while minimizing the computational requirements of the Swin Network model. To improve visibility of lesions under different lighting conditions, a multi-stage preprocessing pipeline that includes contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE), illumination normalization, and denoising was utilized prior to training the deep learning model. A focal loss function was incorporated as an optimizer to minimize problems related to heavy class imbalance among DR stages and emphasize misclassified or difficult to classify DR stages. Each of these components was designed to increase generalization and stability in analyzing multi-sources of fundus images and improving interpretability.

The experimental evaluation utilized three publicly accessible datasets: APTOS 2019, IDRiD, and Messidor2. The overall accuracy of the results attained was 96.68%, with a precision of 94.72% and recall of 98.11%, F1 score of 96.20%, Cohen’s kappa score at 0.95 and ROC-AUC of 99.83%. In addition, through the application of attention-based techniques for visualizing the prediction results, it was possible to show that the model is capable of locating the pathological areas of importance from a clinical standpoint, which would allow the implementation of automated screening. Related work

Retinal disease recognition has moved a long way-from generic convolutional approaches-to highly intelligent transformer-based frameworks enabling global feature reasoning. Such development highlights growing demands for accuracy in grading diabetic retinopathy, interpretability, and robustness on different datasets. Thirty-five representative works are thereby discussed in this section; they are organized systematically under five major waves of research evolution: CNN-based learning, hybrid CNN-attention frameworks, transformer and vision-based methodologies, data augmentation techniques, and survey-level analyses.

CNN-based retinal disease detection

The first systems of classification regarding the retinal image were in fact CNNs. Mohanty et al.1, Ohsugi et al.6 proved that CNNs can speak out disease severity nuggets with the accuracy level equivalent to human experts. Vaiyapuri et al.2, presented a multi-retinal disease diagnostic scheme which has incorporated several CNN branches to absorb variations of lesion among images. Shoukat et al.3 focused on glaucoma detection and have presented convincing results on Messidor2 dataset. Upadhyay et al.8, brought forward a coherent CNN based framework which emphasized coherence of retinal texture. Saha et al.4; Nawaz et al.5, worked on multi-class diabetic retinopathy classification where accuracies ranged between 85% and 93%. Barros14; Tobin15; Liu16, discriminative power integrated with other layers from OCT. Though CNNs do quite well at the task of localized lesion detection, they do not help much in modeling global dependencies and perform rather poorly when generalized over different datasets. Kumar et al.17 proposed a multi-stage CNN-based framework for diabetic retinopathy detection, where progressive feature extraction improves classification performance across different severity levels. Their approach demonstrates the effectiveness of hierarchical CNN architectures in capturing complex retinal patterns.

Hybrid CNN-transformer architectures

Convolutional encoders with attention mechanisms were based on the original convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and introduced in several studies. CNN feature extraction joined residual attention as proposed by DiaNet in the work of Islam et al.7 which helps improve grading accuracy. Hybrid network merging convolution and transformer blocks for token-level fusion named Conv-ViT, introduced by Dutta et al.18 achieved 96.7% accuracy on the IDRiD dataset. Fundus image enhancement is applied by Abbood et al.9, using a hybrid enhancement algorithm, then classified using CNNs. Handcrafted and learned features are integrated into dual-stream architectures by Nazir et al.19, Choudhary et al.20 Reviews of such hybrid systems have been conducted by Elsharif et al.21, Rajyaguru et al.22, finding that performance improvements exist when attention mechanisms are merged with convolutional approaches in retinal lesion recognition.

Vision transformer and attention-based frameworks for robust retinal analysis

The transformer architecture is changing the face of retinal disease analysis. One such example, Nazih et al.10, introduced a Vision Transformer (ViT) for predicting diabetic retinopathy severity with near-optimal area under the curve AUC scores on the EyePACS dataset. Philippi et al.11 used hierarchical ViTs for multi-disease classification, and Al et al.12 shared Fundus-DeepNet-a multi-label transformer known by its generalization capability that works quite well. Jamshidiha et al.23 explainable model based on transformers illustrated how attention-based interpretability could be tuned within medical imaging jams to this application variability in detecting Alzheimer’s disease24. That traces successfully located optic disc locality quite keenly using a Swin-based transformer, besides training deep ensemble models consisting of 39 ocular diseases achieving state-of-the-art accuracy that ranked first place13. This included Dutta et al.18, Nunez et al.25, and Thanki26. further proved the better strength of transformers when put side by CNNs. All these studies join hands to support the fact that transformer architectures are good at capturing global spatial dependencies, which is very important in the advanced grading of diabetic retinopathy.

Large-scale clinical validation studies have also demonstrated the effectiveness of deep learning systems for diabetic retinopathy screening. For instance, Dai et al.27 proposed a clinically validated deep learning system trained on large-scale heterogeneous fundus datasets, achieving robust performance across the full spectrum of DR severity. Their work highlights the importance of large-scale data diversity and real-world deployment considerations in building reliable screening systems.

Data enhancement and preprocessing techniques

Data augmentation and image enhancement play a major role in the identification of retinal illnesses. Abbood et al.9 used hybrid image preprocessing techniques consisting of CLAHE (Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization) and adaptive contrast filters to make vessels more visible. Giancardo et al.28 designed the Radon Cliff operator particularly for microaneurysm detectors as well as vascular structure enhancers. Ejaz et al.29 combined residual convolutional neural networks (CNNs) with adaptive histogram equalization that helps identify diseases earlier on. Also, Fan et al.30 together with Kozak et al.31 looked at synthetic augmentation and feature balancing as ways through which data imbalance can be reduced in glaucoma datasets. Prananda et al.32 applied nerve fiber segmentation to generate auxiliary features for deep learning classifiers therefore proving once again how important at least some form of domain specific augmentation or even lesion-aware preprocessing alongside image level enhancement is for both generalizability and overfitting reduction when it comes down to medical imaging models.

Survey and meta-analytical studies

More than one hundred and twenty learning-based schemes for the identification of diabetic retinopathy were collected by Goutam et al.33 and segmented into convolutional neural networks, hybrid, and transformer-based frameworks. Multi-label classification networks applied to DR were reviewed by Rodriguez et al.34 and Elsharif et al.21 raising challenges in their interpretability. Reviews by Shinde et al.35 and Barros et al.14 covered systems for glaucoma and optic nerve analysis while Rajyaguru et al.22 discussed the need for proper augmentation and loss optimization techniques which lead to a generalization that attention regularization as well as focal loss-based optimization are parts of modern deep retinal analysis hence helping to fine-tune loss in Swin-DRNet. Recent advances in explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) have focused on ensuring that deep learning models rely on clinically meaningful features rather than spurious correlations. Quellec et al.36 proposed the ExplAIn framework to validate whether model decisions align with ophthalmological knowledge. Similarly, Herrero-Tudela et al.37 demonstrated the use of SHAP to identify clinically relevant retinal features such as vascular changes and peripheral lesions. Furthermore, DR-XAI38 emphasizes the importance of interpretability in assessing disease severity and improving clinical trust. These studies highlight the need for more robust and clinically grounded interpretability techniques beyond traditional visualization methods.

Dataset and preprocessing

The quality, diversity, and consistency of the data used for training and evaluation essentially govern the reliability of any medical image classification model. Retinal fundus images in the application of DR show great variation, differences in illumination, focus, and field of view among acquisition devices and clinical environments. There is a broad tendency to develop standardized data free from artifacts as well as preprocessing pipelines to consolidate generalization over models and diagnostic stability; hence an adequate dataset with a good preprocessing pipeline is highly advocated. These three benchmark datasets: APTOS 2019, IDRiD, and Messidor2 used in this study will be discussed here together with the multi-stage preprocessing framework proposed for improving image quality by normalizing its illumination while maintaining detailed pathological structures that are necessary for strong DR grading.

Preprocessing pipeline

Fund which is the primary imaging modality we use has the issue of noise, variation in light level, and difference in image contrast that is a result of different patient physical characteristics and also from the process of image collection. As for those issues we developed a standard preprocesing routine before we put the images into the machine learning models. We aimed with this to achieve even lighting, better display of abnormal areas in the images, and very similar color representation in all our sets of data which in turn improved the results of the features we extracted. For the entire workflow, summarized in 1, I have three datasets with the following five non-overlapping stages: image resizing, applying the mask, contrast enhancement, denoising, and normalization. I opted this standard workflow for all three datasets with the intention to mitigate bias associated with how the data was acquired.

(1) Image Resizing and Cropping: Each fundus image is resized to 512× 512 pixels while keeping the aspect ratio. Circular cropping is used to remove the peripheral black borders by cropping to the disc of the image to fit the model to the relevant anatomical structures.

(2) Illumination Correction: Normalizing brightness variations across the field of view using Gaussian-based background subtraction improves the visibility of the macular and vascular zones.

(3) Contrast Enhancement: To amplify local contrast of lesions such as microaneurysms and hemorrhages, while avoiding over-enhancement, Contrast-Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) is performed on the L-channel of the LAB color space.

(4) Noise Reduction: By employing a bilateral filtering approach with adaptive kernel size , low-frequency background noise reduction is done while preserving fine vessel contours.

(5) Normalization and Channel Balancing: The intensities within the pixel are clipped to the [0, 1] interval, where mean subtraction and per-channel variance normalization is applied, stabilizing the gradients during training.

Focusing on this method greatly enhances the inter-dataset coherence and visual clarity, allowing the Swin-DRNet model to accept inputs free of artifacts and intensity balanced for multi-stage data augmentation.

Preprocessing algorithm for retinal fundus images

We develop a data pre-processing pipeline that employs a standardisation approach across datasets instead of a task-specific feature amplification approach, and therefore enhances generalisation across datasets, regardless of varying acquisition conditions. The ablation experiments described in Section Ablation study confirm that the intensity of pre-processing has a direct relation to the level of classification stability and generalisation.

The Swin-DRNet Architecture is constructed for the main goal of providing a robust diabetic retinopathy screening solution under different imaging conditions rather than maximizing dataset’s highest accuracy. Evaluation metrics

Model performance was evaluated using six complementary quantitative indicators Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-Score, Cohen’s Kappa, and ROC-AUC to ensure comprehensive assessment across all diabetic retinopathy (DR) severity levels.

Accuracy represents the overall proportion of correctly classified samples and serves as a global measure of predictive reliability across all datasets. However, because accuracy alone may obscure performance under class imbalance, additional class-sensitive metrics were incorporated.

Precision quantifies the correctness of positive predictions, reflecting the model’s ability to avoid false detections, particularly critical in distinguishing mild and moderate DR cases from healthy samples.

Recall (or Sensitivity) evaluates the fraction of true disease cases that were successfully identified, providing a measure of diagnostic sensitivity that is essential for clinical screening reliability.

F1-Score integrates both precision and recall into a single harmonic mean, ensuring balanced performance between false positives and false negatives across all severity levels.

Cohen’s Kappa was used to measure inter-class agreement beyond random chance, offering a robust estimate of consistency between predicted and true labels in multi-class DR grading.

Finally, the ROC-AUC (Receiver Operating Characteristic - Area Under Curve) metric was employed to assess the discriminative capability of the model across class thresholds, representing its ability to distinguish between DR progression stages.

For fairness and interpretability, both macro-averaged (unweighted mean) and weighted-averaged (class-proportion-adjusted mean) variants of these metrics were reported. This combination of metrics ensures a clinically comprehensive evaluation capturing overall performance, class-specific reliability, and inter-class agreement critical for validating the real-world applicability of Swin-DRNet in automated retinal disease diagnosis.

Ablation study

To identify the individual architectural components, preprocessing strategies and optimisation choices that contributed to the performance of the model, an extensive ablation study was performed. The ablation study consisted of four control conditions, which were created by methodically adding enhancements in the order of enhancement strength, focal loss optimisation and backbone capacity. Each condition underwent 40 epochs of training, using identical data splits, preprocessing pipelines, augmentation methods and optimisation schedules, in order to facilitate a fair and unbiased comparison. The ablation study was designed to provide a rationale for each design decision from a real-life diabetes retinopathy (DR) screening point of view, rather than just a metric-based optimisation perspective.

Overall ablation comparison

A comparison of the dataset outcomes for the various ablation variants across the APTOS 2019, IDRiD, Messidor2 and Combined datasets is presented in Table 5 . Based on the data above, the ablation variant labelled Ablation-1 achieved an accuracy of just 81.38% with a corresponding F1-score of 77.49% on the Combined dataset. The results confirm that the high amount of natural variability present within the raw fundus images along with the very high levels of class imbalance the ratio difference in among the various classes negatively impacted performance in baseline CNN-Transformer hybrids by limiting their effectiveness when applied to provide classification performances on DR stage I and below.

Ablation-1, which excluded enhancement and focal optimization, achieved only 81.38% accuracy and 77.49% F1-Score, confirming that raw fundus variability and class imbalance severely limit baseline CNN-Transformer hybrids. Incorporating minimal enhancement and focal loss in Ablation-2 led to a significant performance gain (accuracy 95.41%), demonstrating the positive impact of contrast normalization and adaptive weighting on underrepresented DR classes. Ablation-3 employed moderate enhancement with a Swin-Base backbone and further optimized the focal parameter (γ = 2.5), reaching 92.35% accuracy and 99.70% ROC-AUC. Ablation-4, using a higher-capacity Swin-Large backbone at reduced 224 px input, maintained similar recall but showed a minor drop in precision due to resolution compression.

The final Swin-DRNet model integrating full multi-stage enhancement, focal optimization, and cross-attention refinement achieved the highest accuracy (96.68%), F1-Score (96.20%), and ROC-AUC (99.83%), clearly outperforming all ablation variants. The corresponding graphical comparison in Fig. 10 visually highlights the consistent upward trend across all six metrics, demonstrating the synergy between enhancement diversity, focal weighting, and transformer hierarchy.

Discussion

The findings of this study show that Swin-DRNet is a balanced and reliable automated diabetic retinopathy (DR) screening system that works effectively across all types of imaging conditions. While previous solutions were mainly focused on the achievement of superior performance of single benchmark datasets, this framework was designed to be robust across many datasets that have heterogeneous acquisition protocols, illumination conditions, and class distributions. These design decisions reflect actual imaging conditions found in clinical tele-ophthalmology, where there is inherent variability in both imaging devices and patient populations.

From the results of our study, we see that all three clinically significant diabetic retinopathy (DR) stages (Mild, Moderate, and Severe) have been consistently recognized by the algorithms as improving on both recall and F1-score especially when compared to their previous versions. The value of a high level of recall for DR being diagnosed and treated early is essential, because if there are delays in identifying DR and appropriately referring patients with DR for treatment, it could lead to severe damage to vision. The results from our study further demonstrate how the integration of focal loss and class-aware optimizations during the training process provided more precise and accurate results when predicting DR at higher levels. As demonstrated in the ablation analysis of our study, the models that were not trained using focal loss or class-aware optimization had less stable precision to recall ratios when predicting DR for minority classes. However, we note that certain classes achieved near-perfect scores due to distinctive lesion characteristics and limited sample sizes, which should be interpreted with caution.

Swin-DRNet’s multi-stage pre-processing pipeline is very instrumental in enhancing robust performance under heterogeneous multi-source datasets. The Swin-DRNet methodology helps to standardize illumination, increase contrast via CLAHE, and eliminate background noise, thus reducing the Swin-DRNet’s reliance on Data Set-specific visual characteristics. In Messidor2 results, the differences in the illumination conditions between Messidor2 and those of APTOS-2019 and IDRiD are extreme yet the Swin-DRNet continues to perform at a consistent level of performance. These results indicate that the process of Pre-processing Standardization is not only essential to developing a fully deployable screening system, but also to improve accuracy for Data Sets alone.

In terms of architecture, the hierarchical Swin Transformer backbone is designed to be able to represent local lesion patterns (vascular detail and texture) while still having access to provide global context about the entire retina. The early stages of the model are tuned to look for fine-level vascular detail and texture, while the later stages look for patterns of disease progression (macular structure) within an individual’s retina. Additional implementations, such as class-aware token embeddings and cross-attention fusion modules, make the model particularly useful because it focuses the learned attention to those regions specifically related to a diagnosis. The distribution of learned global visual attention shown in the attention visualizations aligns closely with the clinically relevant lesions identified by the model (microaneurysms, hemorrhages, and neovascular regions).

Compared to various novel state-of-the-art methods that have been recently developed, Swin-DRNet shows competitive, and consistently higher performance in terms of specific metrics, especially sensitivity and F1 score but also maintains extremely high specificity. Most importantly, these additions to the Swin-DRNet do not require any post-hoc threshold tuning or test-time augmentation due to the stability of the learned representations. The increased performance of Swin-DRNet does not depend on an extreme degree of architectural growth; rather, it is the result of the synergy between data preprocessing, the design of the loss function, and the utilization of a hierarchical attention structure.

The main takeaway from this discussion is that, in order for DR screening to be reliable, there is much more to it than simply being maximally accurate when tested on small datasets. Therefore, in order for reliable DR screening to be clinically adopted, DR screening must provide robust generalization, sensitivity toward patients with more advanced stages of their disease, and provide a means of interpretation. In doing so, Swin-DRNet meets all of these identified needs via its integrated design that balances performance, stability, and explainability, thereby providing a practical framework for developing automated systems for screening diabetic retinopathy on a large scale.

Conclusion

The purpose of this study was to introduce the innovation of Swin-DRNet, a method of using a transformer model for the detection of diabetic retinopathy (DR) as an alternative to the typical methods on diverse imaging conditions. Swin-DRNet was created to help enhance lesion visibility through multi-stage preprocessing, and contrast adaptive augmentation; as well as mitigate class imbalances and increase generalizability of cross-dataset results through loss-refined optimisation. The results achieved on the datasets APTOS 2019, IDRiD, and Messidor2 confirmed that Swin-DRNet demonstrated a high level of consistent performance, showing an overall accuracy of 96.68%, F1-score of 96.20%, and ROC-AUC of 99.83% when evaluated on the combined dataset. This study has illustrated that Swin-DRNet, through the use of an ablation study, as well as an emphasis on attention and interpretability, has provided a stable yet interpretable design suited for the screening of DR through ophthalmic modalities instead of optimising on a particular dataset. However, the current evaluation does not represent true cross-dataset generalization, as the model is trained on samples from all datasets. Future work will investigate leave-one-dataset-out validation to assess generalization to unseen domains.

There are still many potential areas for further research to build on the strong results so far. For example, including more types of clinical data such as OCT and fluorescein angiography, along with additional patient-related data, will help to provide even better disease stage determination and decision support for clinicians. Building upon the current level of interpretability of the Swin-DRNet through use of newer Explainable AI techniques such as Grad-CAM+, SHAP and transformer derived salience mapping, will also help to improve the confidence of clinicians and provide transparency around specific lesions. Several areas for further research will also focus on using Privacy-preserving Methods and Federated Learning Variants of Swin-DRNet to support the development of Cross-institutional Deployment models of Swin-DRNet without needing to share patient data with other institutions, as well as developing Real Time Optimization and Model Quantization strategies to support use with Next Generation Point Of Care Ophthalmic Devices. Together, these avenues of research will help to move Swin-DRNet into the areas of practical, trustworthy, and globally scalable DR Screening Systems.
