Physico-mechanical and chemical performance of Portland cement modified with eggshell powder for sustainable construction
Authors: Abdulganiyu Sanusi, Emmanuel Eberechukwu Ndububa, Adekunle Akanni Adeleke, Ifeyinwa Ijeoma Obianyo, Usman Ibrahim Wambai, Yusuf Muhammad Kudu, Idris Mohammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCement is one of the most used materials in the construction industry. Its production contributes about 8% to the global emissions. This study explores the use of eggshell powder (ESP) that contains over 80% calcium carbonate (CaCO3) as a partial substitute for ordinary Portland cement (OPC), such as Types B and D, to substitute limestone within the cement matrix. The physical (consistency and setting time), compressive strength, and chemical (Scanning Electron Microscope with Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy, (SEM-EDS), and X-ray Diffraction (XRD), X-ray fluorescence (XRF)) properties were evaluated against British, Indian, and established standards in the literature. The results showed that cement consistency (31–35%) and setting times were within acceptable limits according to BS EN 197-1 (initial ≥ 60 min) and IS 8112 (initial ≥ 30 min, final ≤ 600 min). The mechanical strength of the samples exceeded the required strength (42.5 N/mm2) recommended by the British Standards, except for samples B10N and D10N, which showed strength reductions of 8.4% and 12.3%, respectively. The SEM-EDS and XRD analyses confirmed a high CaCO3 content in the samples. The study suggests that incorporating ESP into OPC should not exceed 5% by weight, as higher proportions could negatively impact the cement’s physical and strength properties. This approach will promote environmental sustainability by using agro-waste while ensuring the cement remains suitable for construction.Keywords: Eggshell powder, OPC, Strength, Chemical characterization, Construction sustainabilitySubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Ordinary Portland cement (OPC) was discovered in 1924 as a binding agent for construction materials1. It is a material commonly used in construction projects (such as buildings, bridges, roads, and other infrastructural facilities) to bond two or more materials together to form an entity. For example, mixing of fine aggregate mix with OPC and water to produce sandcrete or mortar. Cement is also mixed with fine and coarse aggregate to produce structural concrete. However, the product, cement, is obtained by burning a mixture of limestone, also known as calcareous (calcium) and argillaceous (clay or shale) materials, in a kiln at a high temperature (1000–1450 °C) to form clinker, in which gypsum is added to grind the processing material into fine powder for construction purposes2,3.

Cement, as a binding material is a key constituent of concrete. Concrete has been reported to be the most used material after water due to its versatility4–6. Sanusi et al.6 reported that the demand for concrete keeps increasing year after year due to rapid urbanization emanating from fast global industrial and infrastructural development. Moreover, In 2022, Mambo et al.4 reported that concrete alone generates 4 billion tons of carbon dioxide annually. This suggests that the impact of cement on environmental sustainability is significant, since cement is a major binding agent used for concreting and construction applications in general. Some of the applications, include soil stabilization, block production, mortar production, and so on. In addition, cement production has been discovered to contributes about 8% of global CO2 emissions annually7,8.

Moreover, the exploration of natural resources is another environmental challenge attributed to OPC production, which plays a vital role, resulting in the depletion of natural resources9,10. Also, higher temperature of 1000–1450 °C is required in cement production, leading to the continuous melting of the ozone layer, thereby contributing to the current Global Warming (GW) the world is experiencing7,11. Moreover, the emission of CO2 during manufacturing processes contributes to air and water pollution resulting from cement transportation to various distributors down to the point of use, which also leads to habitat destruction, land degradation, and a range of social issues in local communities12.

Considering the environmental and social impacts of cement production and distribution on the society, researchers/scientists, policymakers, and industry stakeholders need to source innovative solutions and technologies to drive cement industry towards sustainable practices and an eco-friendly future. This can be achieved through sourcing alternative raw materials and fuels, thereby improving energy efficiency, reducing emissions, increasing waste recycling and reclamation efforts, and overall promote environmental and built environment sustainability.

In the quest to address these challenges emanating from cement production, several studies have been conducted on the use of alternative materials such as fly ash, rice husk ash, and eggshell powder, to mention a few. These materials were used as partial replacements for cement in concrete or mortar production to reduce construction costs and improve environmental sustainability13–15. De Medeiros et al.13 present the compressive strength of concrete produced from the 25% replacement of cement with fly ash to be the same as that of the produced concrete from 100% cement after one and twelve months. Filho et al.14 supported the argument, stating that fly ash as a pozzolanic material reduces the cementitious composite expansion when exposed to sodium sulfate attack. However, rice husk ash (RHA) as a partial replacement for cement in concrete has been studied and reported to be adequate up to 20% due to the high presence of silica content16,17. Edale et al.18 further confirmes the use of RHA up to 20% without compromising the mechanical properties and quality of concrete; rather, it is cost-effective and promotes environmental sustainability. Furthermore, Samadhiya et al.19, investigated the stone residue, arc furnace slag and pulverized ash in alkali-activated cementitious mortar. The findings suggest that 50% stone residues, 10% arc furnace slag, 10% pulverized ash in the mortar matrix achieving compliance between performance and ecological impacts.

Another eco-friendly material that can partially replace cement is eggshell powder (ESP). ESP is obtained from refined waste eggshell after consuming the nutrients in it. Ismail et al.20 stated that eggshell (ES) waste contributes about 8 million tons to global solid waste every year. This waste generation will continue to increase due to human activities. Therefore, there is a pressing need to convert the waste into wealth for environmental sustainability. However, studies conducted on the replacement of cement with ESP in concrete, proves ESP to be a promising alternative for cement replacement. Zang et al.21 also reported that the use of eggshell powder (ESP) obtained from waste eggshells, which constitute environmental problems, is suitable for cement replacement in concrete for construction purposes, but should not exceed 10% due to the high calcium oxide content present in its chemical composition12. This is in contrast with the argument of Paruthi et al.22, which states that a 20% cement replacement with eggshell can be achieved when an artificial neural network (ANN) is used. Therefore, further study is required to validate the argument.

Despite the negative effect of cement on the environment, which results in GW, one of the other problems threatening the world is landfills, to which a significant amount of chicken eggshells from households, restaurants and industries contribute. However, the ESP obtained from ES has been discovered to consist of approximately 94% of calcium carbonate, which is beneficial in generating calcium14. Although improper disposal leads to solid waste in landfills. As a result, eggshells become harmful due to organic content, worms and rats influencing it, resulting in public health concerns14, thereby threatening the ecosystem. Despite the growing body of research on the utilization of eggshell powder (ESP) in cementitious systems, existing studies have predominantly focused on its application at the concrete or mortar level, with limited attention to its role in modifying the cement matrix itself. There is a lack of systematic understanding of ESP as a partial substitute for limestone in cement and its influence on the physicochemical and strength properties of the resulting material. Therefore, this study addresses this gap by investigating ESP-blended Portland cement through an integrated evaluation of its physical characteristics, strength performance, and chemical composition of the produced mortar. This approach provides new insights into the relationship between ESP incorporation, cement chemistry, and material performance, thereby contributing to the development of more sustainable cementitious systems.

Materials and methods

Materials

The materials used for this study are presented in Fig. 1, which includes eggshells (ES) sourced from households, cafeterias, and hotels within Abuja city. Type B and D cement of grade 42.5 N used for this study were sourced from Dei-Dei market, Abuja, Nigeria and are classified as CEM I according to BS EN 196-123. The reason for choosing these products was that the two Types of dominate the Nigerian market and are widely used for construction activities across Nigeria and in some African countries. Incorporating ESP in the cement types, which substitute limestone within the cement matrix, could enhance the reduction of CO2 emissions and the depletion of natural resources (limestone), thereby promoting environmental sustainability. Methods

The following methods were adopted for the processing of eggshell powder and testing of cement blended with ESP.

Eggshell powder processing

The ESP collected from households, cafeterias, and hotels was processed following the procedure specified by Paruthi et al.22. The procedure involves a thorough washing of the ESP with water and detergent to remove any organic matter or residues. The washed eggshells were air-dried at room temperature for 48 h and oven-dried at 105 ± 5 °C for 2 h to remove moisture completely, and kept in an air-tight container. Then, the dried ES was ground into a fine eggshell powder (ESP) using a grinding machine. The fine-ground ESP was screened through a 75 μm BS sieve to remove materials with sizes above 75 μm and ensure the ESP material exists at the micro level. The ESP particle that passed through the sieve with a 75 μm was collected and used for this research work. See Fig. 1 for refined ESP.

ESP fineness

The ESP fineness test was carried out following the procedure described in the BS EN 196 Part 625 for cement. Although the procedure was modified by using a 75-micron BS sieve. The ESP fineness was estimated using Eq. 1.

ESP particle size distribution

The particle size distribution of eggshell powder was conducted in accordance with the procedure outlined in BS EN 196-625 using BS sieves 300, 150, 75, 45, and 25 μm.

ESP-blended cement physical properties testing

Consistency of ESP-blended cement

Cement consistency is the determination of the water quantity required to produce a standard cement paste when mixed with powdered cement. Vicat apparatus that conforms to BS EN 196-328 was used following the procedure outlined in BS EN 196-3. That is, a cement sample of 300 g was taken in a clean, dry tray and mixed with a known percentage of distilled water by cement weight, starting from 24%, thereby gradually increasing the water by 2% until the cement’s normal consistency was achieved. The cement paste was mixed for three to five minutes before any sign of setting occurred. The gauging time started counting immediately after water was added to the dry cement until the commencement of Vicat mould filling. The plunger was placed on the levelled and compacted cement paste in the mould, and the plunger was allowed to freely penetrate the cement paste to a depth of 6 ± 1 mm from the bottom of the mould, according to BS EN 196-3. The procedure was repeated three times, and an average value was recorded for ESP-blended cement normal consistency.

Setting time of ESP-blended cement

Cement setting time refers to the period it takes for cement to go from a workable paste to a hardened state. A modified procedure of normal consistency was used according to BS EN 106 Part 328, which involved using 85% of the required water to produce cement of normal consistency, and a 1 mm thick needle was used instead of a plunger. The needle was gently lowered to the cement paste surface and quickly released to sink into the paste to measure and record penetration at 5 ± 0.5 mm from the bottom of the mould for the initial setting time. After the initial setting was achieved, the needle was replaced with another 1 mm thick needle with an annular attachment at the tip for the final setting time. The needle with an annular attachment was lowered to the paste surface until no impact was felt on the paste surface. At this point, the final setting time was taken and recorded.

Mechanical testing of ESP-blended cement

ESP mortar production and compressive strength testing

Sample preparation of mortar 50 mm x 50 mm x 50 mm was carried out in line with the description presented in BS EN 196-123. The sand used was oven-dried at a temperature ranging between 105 °C and 110 °C to ensure the total moisture removal. Also, the ESP blended cement used (Type B and Type D) was mixed with water, complying with BS EN 100824 for concrete and mortar production. The sample was mixed for 90 s as described in BS EN 196-123. The mixed sample was filled into a mould and vibrated using a vibration table in the laboratory to dispel the void in the mortar. Three specimens were produced for each % ESP replacement, making a total of fifty-four specimens. The specimens were cured in clean water, complying with BS EN 100824 for 2, 7 and 28 days, respectively. Upon maturity age, the specimens were tested for compressive strength using a speed rate of 2400 ± 200) N/s recommended in BS EN 196-1, and compressive strength was calculated using Eq. 3 reported in the literature29,30.

The oxide composition of cement Types B and D presented in Table 5 are within the acceptable limits of the Nigerian Industrial Standards (NIS), American Standards for Testing and Measurement (ASTM), and British Standards Institution (BSI), except for Sulfur trioxide (SO3), which cements Type D exceeded the specification of the ASTM, while Type B also did not meet the specification for Na2O+K2O, considering BS standards. The observation of the cement Types B and D aligns with the findings of Odewale et al.34. The presence of SO₃ in cement is critical, as it regulates the hydration of tricalcium aluminate (C₃A) through controlled ettringite formation, thereby influencing setting time and early hydration behaviour. However, when SO₃ exceeds recommended limits (ASTM specifications), it may destabilize the hydration system and introduce long-term durability concerns. Excess sulphate can promote delayed ettringite formation (DEF), particularly under moist or elevated temperature conditions, leading to internal expansion, microcracking, and gradual deterioration of mechanical properties. In addition, excessive SO₃ may increase susceptibility to internal sulphate attack and long-term strength reduction, especially in aggressive service environments. Table 5 shows that Type D exceeds ASTM SO₃ limits, indicating a potential durability risk under prolonged exposure conditions, whereas Type B complies with ASTM requirements and is therefore less likely to experience sulphate-related deterioration. At the investigated 5% ESP replacement level, the associated risk is considered moderate, as the overall sulphate balance may remain within tolerable limits; however, higher replacement levels or adverse environmental conditions could intensify these effects. Furthermore, high-sulphate, low-alkali systems may exhibit slower early strength development but improved resistance to external sulphate attack22, indicating a trade-off between early-age performance and durability. Therefore, careful control of SO₃ content is essential to ensure the long-term stability and durability of ESP-modified cementitious materials.

ESP X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

XRD analysis is used to study the mineral content of materials. The XRD graph of ESP presented in Fig. 3 shows that the higher mineral content of ESP is calcite crystalline with 85%, followed by illite (10%) and quartz (4.5%). The 85% calcite reveals that ESP possesses the highest level of natural calcium carbonate (CaCO3) in its mineralogy, a major component of limestone, chalk and marble. The calcite chemical formulation shows that one calcium (Ca) atom, one carbon (C) atom, and three oxygen (O) atoms are present in the ESP content. However, the calcite recorded in this study was reduced by 5% to the lower limit of 90%, as reported by several studies that 90–95% calcite is present in ESP22,35. While illite and quartz make up 15%, as against 4.3–10% reported for other minerals. The other minerals are Manganese, Iron, Copper, Boron, Zinc, Silicon, Magnesium, and sulfur or organic matter, depending on the region where the ESP was sourced. The reduction in ESP calcium carbonate in the current study can be attributed to the source variability, cleaning and pre-treatment process. This could lead to the presence of residual organic matter or inner membrane components. In addition, the grinding process as part of pre-treatment could be responsible for incomplete phase transformation or retention of minor amorphous. Therefore, these factors collectively contribute to the observed reduction in calcite content seen in the XRD results.

XRD graph for eggshell powder.

Although a slight reduction in the calcite purity level of ESP can influence its performance in cementitious systems, since calcite essentially acts as filler. However, the calcite content (85%) is slightly lower and remains adequately higher in ESP to enhance effective performance as limestone in the cement matrix to promote environmental sustainability, thereby reducing the depletion of natural resources and global warming due to a reduction in energy efficiency.

ESP-blended cement loss of ignition (LOI)

Loss of ignition is a measure of a volatile substance and the presence of excess moisture at a high temperature of 975 ± 25 °C in materials. The test measures the weight loss of materials. The LOI result of the sample presented in Table 6 shows that ESP has a higher LOI value of 42.5%, while cement Types B and D have lower values of 1.12 and 2.15%, respectively. The 42.5% ESP LOI falls within the range of value (40.0-47.8%) reported by Paruthi et al.22 meaning that the sample aligns with findings reported in the literature. The higher LOI obtained for ESP is primarily attributed to the decomposition of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃), being the major eggshell component, into calcium oxide (CaO) and carbon dioxide (CO₂), leading to mass loss11,12. Likewise, the Type B and Type D cement LOI certified the requirement of < 3% established in the British standard BS EN 197-123. This suggests that the two cements are suitable for materials recommended for construction work.

Although high LOI in cement content shows excessive moisture, carbonated, and unburnt contents, which can hamper cement’s long-term performance. In terms of long-term dimensional stability, high LOI can be connected to an increase in pre-hydrated and carbonated phases, leading to a higher porosity, shrinkage or expansion delay, thus reducing the volumetric stability over time. In addition, cement with higher LOI has a lower and higher reactive clinker phases and amounts of carbonated material, such as CaCO3, respectively. This reduces the hydration cushioning of the cement matrix against CO2 infiltration, thereby accelerating the depth of carbonation. Hence, reducing the alkalinity more rapidly, which threatens the embedded steel reinforcement and increases its risk of corrosion exposure. This enhances the penetration of aggressive substances, such as sulfates, chlorides, and acidic solutions, which weakens the microstructure of the cement composite, making it vulnerable to chemical attack and strength degradation that threatens cement durability under aggressive environments, as mentioned in the literature22. However, despite the higher ESP LOI recorded, the material is susceptible to positively influencing the physical and mechanical properties of cement. This is due to the improvement that has been reported in the literature when cement was partially replaced with ESP in concrete production12. Although the positive result was achieved due to the lower percentage replacement level of ESP in cement, which substitutes limestone within the cement matrix.

The cement consistency is a measure of cement flowability when mixed with water. The consistency of the ESP-blended cement presented in Table 7 shows that both Type B (B0N) and Type D (D0N) cements with zero ESP replacement had penetration depths of 6 mm and 7 mm, respectively, at 31% consistency. While B5N, B10N, D5N, and D10N penetrate the paste to 7, 5, 5, and 5 mm, respectively, for the consistency of 32%, 33%, 33%, and 34%. This implies that as the ESP content increases, the quantity of water required to produce adequate cement paste flowability also increases. Although all the tested samples satisfy the 6 ± 1 mm penetration between the plunger and the base plate recommended in BS EN 196-328. In conclusion, the influence of ESP on cement is linked to its porous surface properties, which resulted in higher water absorption.

ESP-blended cement setting time

The effect of ESP on the hydration kinetics of cement was assessed by measuring the setting time of cement paste containing different proportions of eggshell powder. The result indicated that as the ESP percentage rises, the blended cement initial setting times exceeded 60 min recommended by BS EN 197-123, except for the D5N sample, which dropped by 2 min (3.3%) below the standards. Although all the samples exceeded 30 min specified in IS 811236 for the final setting time. The overall test results presented in Table 7 showed the influence of ESP on both Type B and Type D cements. A slight reduction was observed at 5% ESP replacement, but increased at 10%. Das et al.42 reported that the heat of hydration is responsible for concrete or cement-mortar setting when cement is used as a binding agent. This implies that the heat of hydration increased at 5% ESP replacement and reduced at 10% ESP, leading to the reduction and increase in the cement initial setting time of the two cement types. Moreover, a slight drop of 2 min (3.3%) below the 60 min recommended by BS EN 197-1 is marginal since the observed deviation is less than 5% defect, statistically recommended for considerable data. However, the defect can be attributed to the addition of Ca²⁺ supplied by the eggshell, which promotes faster formation of early hydration products (C–S–H and portlandite). Furthermore, the partial dilution of ESP with clinker phases may have altered the aluminate and sulfate reactions, leading to a slightly faster initial setting time. However, from a practical point of view, the deviation was very small, indicating a tendency of a slight reduction in workability time, even though the cement behaves comparably to the standard cement. Therefore, the result suggests that D5N would be more suitable where minor adjustments (such as water content or the use of set retarders) can be made to ensure adequate workability and compliance with standards.

However, all the samples satisfied the requirement of ≤ 600 min recommended by IS 811236 and are suitable for construction application, since the highest final setting time was recorded at 178 min for D10N, where Type D cement was replaced with 10% ESP. in addition, the change in the setting time of the ESP-blended cement can be attributed to the alteration of the Type B and D cements’ chemical composition, thereby affecting the hydration kinetics. Therefore, this study suggests that the addition of ESP had no appreciable effect on the cement setting time and strength properties.

Mechanical properties of ESP-blended cement

ESP-blended cement compressive strength

The compressive strength of the cement mortar is one of the tests used to measure the mechanical properties of cement at various curing periods. Figure 4 presents the results of the ESP-blended ordinary Portland cement. The figure shows that all the samples tested (0, 5, and 10% replacement for Types B and Type D cements) exceeded the minimum of ≥ 20 N/mm2 recommended in Table 2 of BS EN 197-123 for 2-day early strength. The lower compressive strengths observed early on the mortar produced from ESP-blended cement can be attributed to the reduced cement content. That is, the inert nature of ESP (which is primarily composed of calcium carbonate, CaCO3) act as filler at early ages, and increase the composite porosity, leading to a low reactivity with cement. This argument was supported by the literature, having reported that the hydration of cement determines its strength development at the early stage. In addition, the lower the cement content, the lower the hydration product (calcium-silicate-hydrate or C-S-H gel) formed, resulting in slow and low early strength development compared to the concrete produced with 100% cement22.

Compressive strength of ordinary Portland cement blended with ESP (N/mm2).

Moreover, it was also noted that as the curing age increases, the ESP-blended cement strength increases. Although, only the control (0%) and 5% ESP replacement for Type B and D cement exceeded the minimum requirement of ≥ 42.5 N/mm2 at 28 days standard strength by 7%, 14%, 0.5%, and 5% for the 0% Type B, 5% Type B, 0% Type D, and 5% Type D while 10% ESP replacement for the Type B and D cements failed the standard strength requirement by 8.4% and 12.3% to the lower limit of 42.5 N/mm2. The results trend aligns with the study of several authors when ESP was used as a cement replacement in concrete. That is, the compressive strength of concrete at 28 days for 5% ESP replacement in cement increased more than the control, but decreased at 10% ESP replacement11,21. The strength reduction experienced by samples produced from 10% replacement with ESP is a result of an increase in impurities in the ESP mixture, as discussed by Yerramala et al.37. This implies that the presence of impurities in ESP (such as sulfur trioxide and alkalis) can adversely affect the cement’s strengths, durability, and corrosion resistance according to literature11,21. Jaber et al.16 support the argument by stating that a 5% ESP replacement for cement improves the compressive strength of concrete, but further increase of ESP led to a reduction in strength. Additionally, a study conducted by Kaliyavarashan et al.38 showed an improvement in concrete flexural strength and durability under freeze-thaw cycles when 10% limestone in cement was replaced with ESP. This suggests that ESP contributed to the improved tensile properties of the sample, indicating that the inclusion of ESP in cement, which substitutes for limestone within the cement matrix, could enhance concrete properties and promote environmental sustainability.

Some authors also argued that ESP can replace cement in concrete up to 15 and 20%22, but the current study on the OPC blended with ESP proves otherwise. This may be a result of natural coarse aggregate (granite) included in the concrete materials, which might have contributed to the bonding and strength characteristics of concrete due to the high content of silica present in granite. Moreover, it has been reported that curing age influences some concrete mechanical strength, especially when supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) are used as cement partial replacement39,40. Kumar and Nasier41 argued that a mixture consisting of SCMs, such as ESP, fly ash, and so on, often continues to gain strength beyond 28 days conventional benchmark used for the compressive strength assessment, due to low reactivity at the early stage. However, continuous curing at 56–90 days allows slow progressive reactivity in the composite, thereby consuming C-H and forming additional C–S–H gel11. This densifies the composite microstructure and enhances long-term mechanical performance. Therefore, the extended curing period beyond 28 days in water may improve the composite matrix densification and microstructure, thus enhancing the strength and the overall performance of ESP-blended cement, especially for the 10% ESP mixtures.

Comparing the use of RHA as OPC partial replacement in concrete with the current study, RHA significantly enhances the mechanical strength of concrete up to 20% due to its pozzolanic activities. However, the ESP, which was used as filler in cement to assess the physical and strength properties of the modified cement, shows an optimal of 5% replacement. Moreover, a higher dosage of superplasticizer is required as the supplementary cementitious materials (SCM) increase in the concrete mixture42, whereas no superplasticizer was used in the current study, suggesting that 5% ESP as a partial replacement for cement will contribute to the cost reduction, while promoting environmental sustainability. Although the use of SCMs such as RHA and metakaolin (MK), along with manufactured sand (M-sand), shows a significant reduction in concrete weight loss, indicating an improved durability under acidic attack43, whereas, durability tests would be considered in further study on ESP-blended cement.

However, the increase in strength observed in 5% ESP-blended cement for both Types B and D can also be linked to the reduction in setting time and a higher level of fineness (9.75%) reported for ESP for this study compared to the control. This is due to the lower and higher content of ESP and cement, respectively, thereby reducing the mortar porosity and improving ESP reactivity with cement, which eventually enhanced compressive strength compared to the mortar produced from 10% ESP. Hence, this study suggests that a 5% replacement of ESP improves the mechanical properties of ordinary Portland cement (OPC); likewise, further study on the long-term curing age is recommended, especially for the 10% above ESP replacement in OPC.

ESP-blended cement mineralogy

XRD result of ESP-blended cement is presented in Fig. 5. In the Figures, quartz, anorthite, wollastonite, muscovite and portlandite are seen in the B0N, B5N and B10N samples, while quartz, calcite, portlandite, muscovite and orthoclase are seen in D0N, D5N and D10N samples. Orthoclase, a potassium-rich alkali feldspar used for the production of glass and ceramics, is seen in D0N, D5N, and D10N samples, while wollastonite, a natural calcium-silicate mineral (CaSiO3) that forms when impure limestone experiences high temperature and pressure, usually in the presence of silica-bearing fluids, which aligns with the findings of Raj et al.45. This means 10% illite seen in ESP content in Fig. 3, which is referred to as an impurity22, influenced the behaviour of cement Type D when reacting with water.

Generally, calcite is used as filler to improve packing density. The C-S-H hydration product is responsible for strength development in concrete. While portlandite (calcium hydroxide) enhances alkalinity in the concrete matrix, which has a limited influence on strength contribution compared to C–S–H (hydration product). Although it has been reported that excessive portlandite can result in strength reduction when it is not efficiently transformed to C–S–H. However, the advancement of these phases directly influences the concrete compressive strength 11,46.

The presence of calcite, being the highest mineral in ESP-blended cement, is connected to the higher content of calcite in ESP, which is responsible for improved density packing and compressive strength at the 28-day maturity period. This observation aligns with the study of Shiferaw et al.46, where higher calcite in concrete and enhanced compressive strength were observed when cement was partially replaced with ESP. Moreover, portlandite (calcium hydroxide, C-H) was observed in the entire mortar produced from ESP-blended cement, which also agrees with the findings of Shiferaw et al.46. Furthermore, the degree of cement hydration can be attributed to the calcium silicate hydrates (C-S-H) and calcium hydroxide (C-H) gel contents, which determine the cement paste’s strength and overall performance11. Nonetheless, Akanbi et al.39 stated that proper curing through satisfactory moisture and temperature influences cement paste hydration and long-term strength development. Therefore, this suggests that a long-term curing period could improve the strength development of the ESP-blended cement samples.

SEM-EDS of ESP-blended cement mortar

The scanning electron microscopic (SEM) images in Fig. 6 show the morphological structures of the ESP-blended cement samples. The images indicated that both Type B and D cement are similar, having calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) and portlandite (C-H) as the major compounds responsible for cement hydration and strength development. The portlandite generates high alkalinity in the mortar matrix, enhancing the bonding and strength properties of the composite, likewise improving steel protection against corrosion in reinforced concrete. This agrees with Shiferaw et al.46 discovery of a crystalline morphology of portlandite when 10% and 20% ESP replacement for cement was studied. Also, Raj et al.45 confirmed the findings by stating the strength development as a result of the heat of hydration generated from portlandite in their study. This study also aligns with the argument of Adekunle et al.47, which states that silica plays a vital role, along with oxygen, for strength improvement in concrete. The images also show that as the ESP replacement in cement increases, the calcium carbonate in the mixture increases. Moreover, the SEM study confirms the research outcome of XRD carried out in this study. Likewise, the EDS in Fig. 7 confirms the observations of the SEM, XRD, and oxide composition (presented in Table 10) results that a high level of calcium was present in the two types of cement blended with ESP.

Estimation of limestone reduction and CO2 savings

For every 1 ton of cement produced, approximately 1.25 tons of limestone are required48. Therefore, a 5% replacement of limestone with eggshell powder (ESP) corresponds to a reduction of approximately 0.0625–0.0635 tons (≈ 63 kg) of limestone per ton of cement produced, as expressed in Eq. (6). This reduction contributes directly to the conservation of natural resources and decreases reliance on limestone extraction activities, thereby supporting sustainable cement production practices. From an environmental standpoint, limestone decarbonization constitutes a significant source of CO₂ emissions during clinker production, accounting for approximately 44% of the total process-related emissions. Accordingly, the avoided calcination emissions associated with 5% ESP substitution are estimated to be approximately 0.0165 tons (16.5 kg) of CO₂ per ton of cement produced, as presented in Eq. (7). This provides a semiquantitative estimate of the direct process-related CO₂ mitigation achievable through ESP incorporation, highlighting its potential contribution toward emission reduction and circular economy implementation within the cement industry15.

Although ESP and limestone are calcium carbonate–based materials, they significantly differ in environmental profile in terms of sources, processing requirements and life cycle influence. Limestone is known to be a non-renewable natural material, which requires quarrying, crushing, and transportation. In addition, its processing is related to land degradation, ecosystem disturbance, and significant CO₂ emissions, leading to global warming, especially during calcination in cement production. Meanwhile, ESP is an agro-waste material generated from the consumption of a large quantity of eggs in food industries and households. The utilization of waste in the construction sector indicates a form of waste valorization to enhance environmental cleanliness and reduce the need for virgin raw material, thereby promoting the circular economy.

Conclusion

The use of eggshell powder (ESP) as a partial replacement for ordinary Portland cement (OPC) to promote environmental sustainability in the construction sector has been investigated. The XRF analyses confirm a higher content of calcium oxide (81.24%) in ESP. The consistency of ESP-blended cement shows an improvement, while the setting time meets BS, ASTM, and IS standards. The compressive strength of ESP-blended cement ranged from 20.28 to 48.47 N/mm² over 2 to 28 days. Furthermore, cement samples with 5% ESP replacement exceeded the standard strength limits, while higher replacements (10%) experienced a decrease in mechanical strength. The XRD and SEM analyses revealed a high presence of calcite in ESP-blended cement mortar, especially at 5% replacement, which enhanced calcium silicate hydrate and improved strength development. The ANOVA the suitability of 5% ESP replacement of limestone in cement matrix for mortar production. This study suggests that ESP can replace up to 5% OPC without compromising the cement strength, making it a viable and sustainable alternative for construction applications.

Future research direction

Further study on the long-term curing period, especially for 10% and beyond, would enhance the data bank of ESP inclusion in the cement matrix. In addition, the influence of water-cement ratio (w/c) variation on ESP-blended cement is also recommended for construction applications and literacy purposes. Durability tests (water absorption, sorptivity, sulphate resistance, carbonation, chloride permeability, etc.) and other types of cement globally need further study. This will provide robust structural information on the use of ESP as a partial replacement for cement to substitute limestone within the cement matrix during the production process.
