Self-powered dual-electrode hydrogen production using a composite ion exchange membrane
Authors: Haoyu Yin, Chenghan Xie, Yunbo Dai, Ziming Cao, Shun Ao, Wenjia Wu, Zongyao Zhou, Jun Ma, Bruce E. Logan, Zhongbiao Wu, Le Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The electro-oxidation of formaldehyde at low potential with anodic hydrogen evolution offers an energy-saving way for hydrogen production. Coupling this reaction in an acidic catholyte with an alkaline anolyte within an acid-alkali hybrid electrochemical system enables self-powered hydrogen production with electricity generation. However, the performance of such systems is constrained by the membrane: conventional forward-bias bipolar membranes suffer from high ohmic resistance due to the delamination, while monopolar ion exchange membranes lead to undesirable acid-alkali neutralization. To address these limitations, we design a composite ion exchange membrane, composed of a through-pore anion exchange membrane and a cation exchange membrane. Employing this membrane, the resulting system achieves a Faradaic efficiency for hydrogen of 198 ± 3 % and outputs a peak power density of 142 mW cm−2. A numerical model demonstrates that the through-pore anion exchange membrane effectively regulates charge carrier transport, yielding low resistance and improved acid-alkali utilization efficiency. Here, we provide a promising pathway for sustainable hydrogen production by enabling the efficient conversion of acid-alkali chemical energy into electricity.

Introduction

Hydrogen (H2) is increasingly recognized as a cornerstone of the future sustainable energy landscape due to its high energy density by weight, and potential for zero-carbon emissions1–6. As global efforts to mitigate climate change intensify, developing efficient and cost-effective hydrogen production technologies has become an important scientific and industrial pursuit. Among these technologies, renewable energy-driven water electrolysis (WE) is advantageous for its high-purity hydrogen production yields and low carbon emissions4. However, WE is hindered by high thermodynamic barriers and sluggish kinetics of the anodic oxygen evolution reaction (OER)7, which have led to electricity alone accounting for over 70% of the total operational cost of H2 produced using WE8.

The electro-oxidation of small molecules, such as urea, sulfides, hydrazine and aldehydes, is more thermodynamically favorable than the OER, thereby reducing the energy consumption and improving the economic feasibility of H2 production9. Additionally, many of them are common water pollutants, and their efficient oxidation can thus serve multiple purposes, including efficient hydrogen production, hazardous wastes detoxification, and even value-added products generation9. For example, formaldehyde (HCHO), a common pollutant in wastewaters from various industries, such as resins, organic chemical industry, textiles and wood processing10–15, can be oxidized via its oxidation reaction (FOR) at a low potential (Eθ = –0.22 V vs. RHE)16–19. The partial oxidation of formaldehyde to formate concurrently releases H2 at the anode (HCHO + 2OH– → HCOO– + ½H2 + H2O + e–)16,20,21, in addition to the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) at the cathode, enabling dual-electrode hydrogen production with competitive energy consumption. This process also upgrades HCHO into value-added chemicals like formate and later formic acid10,20.

Industrial processes producing HCHO wastes often generate co-existing acidic or alkaline waste streams22,23. Rather than simply mixing these wastes, electrochemical neutralization energy (ENE) could harness the free energy from the pH gradient (∆pH) between these streams to provide an additional voltage supply7,24–26. For instance, the acid-base neutralization reaction exhibits a Gibbs free energy of –79.9 kJ mol–1 with the catholyte at pH=0 and the anolyte at pH = 14, corresponding to a theoretical cell voltage of 0.828 V7,24–26. In an acid-alkaline hybrid electrochemical (AAHE) system, ENE supports pH-decoupled reactions, adding value to wastewater treatment while reducing the net energy required for hydrogen production. By coupling acidic-HER with alkaline-FOR, the electrochemical neutralization energy extends the thermodynamic potential difference to –1.05 V at ∆pH=14 (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Note 1). This indicates the system not only reduces energy consumption but can potentially generate electricity during dual-electrode H2 production (Fig. 1b)10,24. Although similar AAHE or self-powered hydrogen production systems have been reported10,27, current studies have mainly focused on the development of electrocatalysts. However, the membrane (separator) that isolates the electrolytes is equally important in AAHE systems, as it plays a critical role in determining the overall energy efficiency. While current separators like bipolar membranes (BPMs) and cation exchange membranes (CEMs) are typically employed in AAHE systems25, an ideal membrane should exhibit low membrane resistance to minimize voltage losses and enable high-current-density operation28, and effectively inhibit crossover of water ions (H+ and OH–) to ensure the pH gradient is converted into ENE at the electrodes rather than wasted as heat through bulk-phase neutralization25,28. However, conventional membranes like BPMs suffer from delamination and high intrinsic resistance under forward bias, while CEMs suffer from rapid bulk-phase chemical neutralization under a high ∆pH, severely dissipating the ENE25,29–31.

To address the limitations of conventional membranes in AAHE systems, we herein propose a composite ion exchange membrane (pACM), integrating a CEM and an anion exchange membrane with a through-pore structure (pAEM). As a proof of concept, the pACM is fabricated using established commercial CEM and AEM materials, selected for their maturity, availability, and scalability. The AAHE system with pACM (AAHE-pACM) couples alkaline-FOR with acidic-HER, achieving simultaneous dual-electrode hydrogen production, value-added chemical synthesis, and even net electricity generation. The performance of the pACM was systematically evaluated against commercial BPM and CEM benchmarks. A two-dimensional (2D) numerical model was developed to comprehensively elucidate the charge-carrier transport mechanism, revealing that the pAEM effectively regulates ion transport, thereby suppressing chemical neutralization and salt precipitation. To quantitatively assess the performance gains by the pACM-based system, we designed an in-situ three/four-electrode device for precise voltage losses analysis. Finally, a techno-economic analysis (TEA) and a carbon footprint assessment were conducted to confirm the system’s economic and environmental merits in terms of the levelized cost of hydrogen production (LOCH) and CO2 equivalent emissions compared to conventional hydrogen production processes.

Results and discussion

Design of pACM and assembling of AAHE-pACM systems

The reason for the high resistance of a forward-bias BPM can be attributed to the migration of salt ions from anolyte/catholyte into the interlayer of the BPM, where they accumulate locally and increase the osmotic pressure. The resulting salt layer increases ohmic resistance by blocking ion transport. This process ultimately causes delamination of the BPM layers (Fig. 2a), and a rise in overall membrane resistance29,32. Additionally, although a reverse-biased BPM can catalyze H2O dissociation to supply the consumed water ions and thereby maintain the pH gradient, the voltage required to drive this reaction (>0.83 V) precludes net electricity generation in an energy-harvesting system33. The CEM is a promising alternative to the BPM in AAHE systems due to its intrinsic stability and much lower ionic resistance25. However, AAHE systems employing CEMs are inherently prone to the loss of Nernstian potential due to H⁺ crossover under high pH gradients (Fig. 2b)34. When employing the pACM, the introduced pore structure on the AEM provides dedicated channels for the release of accumulated ions in the interlayer, while the intact (unperforated) regions of the AEM maintain their inherent functions of effectively blocking H+ crossover and enabling the selective exchange of counter-ions, simultaneously lowering the overall resistance of the composite membrane (Fig. 2c). Integrating the inhibitive function of CEM in OH– crossover from anolyte to catholyte, the entire pACM can thereby possess high efficiency in pH gradient maintenance (Fig. 2c).

The pACM was fabricated by integrating a commercial CEM with a perforated commercial anion exchange membrane (pAEM) (Fig. S1). The pores introduced on the pAEM exhibited an average diameter of ~150 μm, with the tangent-to-tangent spacings of ~800 μm vertically and ~1250 μm horizontally (Fig. S1c, d). The total pore area accounted for approximately 2.15% of the AEM surface area. To construct the multi-functional AAHE system, we developed an alkaline-FOR anode and an acidic-HER cathode. A series of bimetallic copper-silver catalysts (CuxAgy, where x and y indicate molar ratios) supported on copper foam (CuxAgy/CF) were synthesized for FOR. Among these, Cu3Ag7/CF demonstrated the best electrocatalytic activity (801 mA cm−2 at 0.26 V vs. RHE) with a high electrochemically active surface area (ECSA, 0.24 ± 0.02 m2ECSA cm−2geometry) and high stability (100 mA cm−2 for 60 h) in 1 M KOH + 0.5 M HCHO solution (Figs. S2–4, Supplementary Note 2), and was therefore selected as the anode in the AAHE system. The Cu3Ag7/CF exhibited a dendritic microstructure (Fig. S5a–d), with homogeneously distributed Cu and Ag elements at a molar ratio close to 3:7 as expected (Fig. S5e, f). The bimetallic composition of Cu3Ag7/CF catalysts was further confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses (Fig. S6, Supplementary Note 2). The full AAHE system was assembled in a two-electrode flow cell configuration, consisting of a Cu3Ag7/CF anode, a pACM as the separator, and a Pt/C loaded graphite felt cathode. For comparison, control systems were also constructed using a commercial BPM (AAHE-BPM) and CEM (AAHE-CEM) as separators.

Electrochemical performance of the AAHE-pACM

The design of pACM was highly effective in enhancing the electrochemical performance of the AAHE system. The open circuit voltage (OCV) is a key parameter for assessing the energy output potential of AAHE systems35,36. The AAHE-pACM demonstrated the potential for high power density, achieving an absolute OCV value of 1.64 V, which was comparable to systems using commercial BPM and CEM (Fig. 2d). The observed OCVs were considerably more negative than the theoretical value (−1.05 V), likely due to higher local concentrations of H+ at the cathode or OH− at the anode compared to the bulk solution, as well as the impact of trace dissolved oxygen (DO) in the catholyte. The AAHE-pACM achieved a current density of 661 mA cm−2 and a peak power density of 142 mW cm−2 (Fig. 2d, e), consistent with its low initial high frequency resistance (HFR, 0.38 Ω) (Fig. 2f). In contrast, the AAHE-BPM system showed much lower current (up to 188 mA cm−2) and power densities (up to 66 mW cm−2) across different cell voltages (Fig. 2d, e), and had a higher initial HFR (0.89 Ω at OCV, Fig. 2f).

The AAHE-pACM effectively minimized crossover of H+ as evidenced by a slow water ion loss rate, which was quantified using a static OCV decay test (non-operating conditions, without electrolyte circulating and applied current), where the decay rate directly correlates to the change of pH gradient37. The OCV decay rate of AAHE-pACM (27.8 mV min−1 within 1 h, ∆pH decay rate of 0.47 min−1) was only 38.8% of that of the AAHE-CEM (71.8 mV min−1, ∆pH decay rate of 1.22 min−1, Fig. 2g). Consequently, the time required for the OCV to drop to 0 V was three times longer for the AAHE-pACM (70.8 min) than for the AAHE-CEM (23.5 min). Although the AAHE-BPM showed a slightly slow initial decay rate (21.8 mV min−1 within 1 h, ∆pH decay rate of 0.37 min−1, Fig. 2g), a subsequent test revealed an accelerated decay, indicating eventual membrane failure (Fig. S7).

To further quantify the loss of water ions in the AAHE-pACM, we measured the consumption of H+ and OH- after 6 h of operation at the peak power density cell voltage (−0.4 V) (Supplementary Note 3). After accounting for OH− consumption from the Cannizzaro reaction and CO2 dissolution (Fig. S8)20,38,39, the loss ratio of H+ to OH− was close to 1:1 (Fig. 2h). This stoichiometry confirms chemical neutralization due to the crossover of H+ from the catholyte through the membrane. Among the three systems with different membranes, the AAHE-pACM exhibited the lowest overall ion loss, with depletions of 13.54 mmol H+ and 12.69 mmol OH−, only 35%−39% of the AAHE-CEM’s loss. Contrary to what its initial OCV decay (static condition) might suggest (Fig. 2g), the AAHE-BPM did not perform optimally under −0.4 V, losing 12.9%−21.2% more water ions than the AAHE-pACM (Fig. 2h).

The pACM also demonstrated better operational stability than the BPM. During operation at −0.4 V, a sharp decline in current density within the first half hour of the AAHE-BPM indicated failure of BPM in a short operation time (Fig. S9). High Intrinsic resistances and salt precipitation also led to an obviously lower current density for the AAHE-BPM compared to the AAHE-pACM and AAHE-CEM (Fig. S9). These results indicate the satisfactory stability of the AAHE-pACM compared to the AAHE-BPM. Post-operation characterization revealed minimal change in the morphology of the pACM, with no signs of salt precipitation or delamination (Fig. 2ileft, S10a–c). In contrast, the BPM suffered severe deformation, a loss of translucency, and had substantial salt precipitation within its interlayer (Fig. 2iright, S10d–f and S11), suggesting a high rate of ion migration and precipitation that ultimately culminated in delamination. This structural damage was irreversible, as the deformation persisted even after dissolving the salts (Fig. S12), and it directly correlated with a substantial increase in HFR (up to 1.34 Ω, Fig. 2f). Following this maximum, the sharp decrease in HFR suggests physical damage to the BPM. This failure can be attributed to surface rupture due to the generation of solid salt crystals, as evidenced by the appearance of several cracks on the BPM after testing (Fig. S11). Although such damage lowers the intrinsic resistance of BPM, it simultaneously compromises its ability to inhibit water and ion crossover. A second LSV test on the same BPM confirmed this permanent degradation, showing substantially different performance from the initial test (Fig. S13).

Functions of pACM for regulating charge carrier transport

The pACM effectively enhanced electrical output and hydrogen production by improving water-ion conservation. The AAHE-pACM achieved a high H2 yielding rate (175 ± 8 mol h−1 m−2) and corresponding high Faradaic efficiencies (FEs) for H2 (197 ± 1% at −0.4 V and 198 ± 3% at 0 V) without external electricity consumption (Fig. 3a). It also showed high FEs ( ~ 100%) and yielding rate (174 ± 13 mol h−1 m−2 at 0 V) for HCOO− production (Fig. S14). While both systems achieved similar H2 FEs and stable OCVs (Figs. S15, 16), the AAHE-pACM delivered a higher energy storage capacity. Although the AAHE-CEM had a slightly higher onset discharge voltage (0.60 V at 150 mA cm−2) due to its lower HFR, the AAHE-pACM achieved a 29% higher volumetric capacity (6.06 Ah L−1, Fig. 3b) and 7.9% more cumulative electricity output (0.41 Wh, Fig. S17), owing to its longer discharge time. The pACM outperformed the CEM in conserving water ions under various electric fields, as evidenced by the consistently lower water ion loss observed for the AAHE-pACM compared to the AAHE-CEM. At a constant current density of 150 mA cm−2, the water ion loss for the AAHE-pACM (14.82 mmol H+ and 12.68 mmol OH−) was only 26%–32% of that for the AAHE-CEM (Fig. 3d). When operating at OCV (without an applied electric field), the loss of H+ and OH− of the AAHE-pACM increased to ~40 mmol over 6 h, in contrast to conditions at a constant current or voltage (Figs. 2h, 3d and S18), verifying the restraining effect of the electric field on H+ crossover. In addition, the decline in H+ and OH− concentrations in the catholyte and anolyte due to Faradaic reactions under the applied electric field may also contribute to the lower water-ion loss. Notably, while water ion conservation in AAHE-CEM relied solely on the electric field, pACM benefits from a synergistic interaction between its pAEM component and the applied electric field, further enhancing water ion retention. This synergy enabled AAHE-pACM to improve water ion conservation capability even without an electric field, compared to AAHE-CEM (Figs. 3d, S18). Additionally, AAHE-pACM exhibited more consistent water ion conservation performance that its water ion loss at constant voltage (–0.4 V) was similar to that at constant current density (150 mA cm−2), whereas AAHE-CEM showed a 32%-34% higher loss at constant current density (150 mA cm−2) than at constant voltage (–0.4 V). The more positive cell voltages observed in AAHE-pACM at 150 mA cm−2 may also contribute to the suppression of H+ crossover (Fig. S19).

The regulation of H+ transport by pACM was also influenced by its concentration, as diffusion behavior is governed by concentration gradient6,40. Increasing the initial H+ concentration in the catholyte enhanced both the current density and power density of the AAHE-pACM. When the H+ concentration in the catholyte increased from 0.2 M to 5.0 M, the current density at 0 V rose from 346 to 862 mA cm−2 (Fig. S20), and the peak power density increased from 73 to 200 mW cm−2 (Fig. 3e). However, under a constant current density of 150 mA cm−2, water ion loss gradually rose with increasing H+ concentration in the catholyte. As H+ concentration increased from 0.2 M to 5 M, the H+ loss increased by 30.7 times and OH- loss by 35.5 times (Figs. 3f, S21a). Additionally, a larger concentration difference between H+ and OH− led to a more rapid voltage change over time (Fig. S21b). These findings suggest that practical applications of AAHE-pACM require a balance between device performance and the efficiency of water ion conversion.

The pACM facilitated the transmembrane transport of anions such as SO42− within the catholyte and pAEM to maintain a low membrane resistance (Fig. 2c). To clarify the role of SO42− transport through pAEM (the key component of pACM), a perforated polyethylene terephthalate (pPET) layer matching the thickness, effective area, and perforation pattern of pAEM was used to replace pAEM for pACM assembly as a control. Unlike pAEM, pPET lacks ion-exchange capacity and only permits limited migration/diffusion of ions through its pores. The mild conditions used here (dilute sulfuric acid, 0.5 M, at room temperature 25 °C) are far less harsh than those typically required for PET depolymerization (high temperature, concentrated acid, and catalysts)41, allowing PET to remain stable during operation. The LSV curves showed negligible current densities in the AAHE system with CEM+pPET as the separator compared to that with the pACM (Fig. 3g). In addition, the HFR of CEM+pPET was about 400 times higher than that of the AAHE-pACM (Fig. S22), underscoring the essential contribution of anions in the catholyte as charge carriers.

To further investigate the mechanisms of the charge carrier transport, we quantified the molar amounts of potential charge carriers (H+, OH−, K+, SO42−, HCOO− and hydroxy hydrated formaldehyde anion) over time at 150 mA cm−2. The loss ratio of H+ to OH− remained close to 1:1 across all measured intervals (Figs. 3h, S23a), and the consumption of H+ was similar to the increase in K+ within the catholyte (Fig. 3i). Notably, SO42− levels in the anolyte and HCHO/HCOO− in the catholyte were negligible, indicating minimal anionic charge carriers crossover between catholyte and anolyte due to the impedance of CEM42–44, and confirming that H+ and K+ served as the dominant transmembrane charge carriers across the pACM (Figs. 2c, S23b, c). Despite the contribution of SO42− transport to the current density (Fig. 3g), its mobility was largely confined to the catholyte and pAEM region, with limited migration into the anolyte, and this behavior was largely unaffected by the applied electric field. For example, at a higher current density of 300 mA cm−2, the accelerated consumption of water ions reduced their concentration gradient, thereby decreasing water ion loss over time compared to 150 mA cm−2 (Fig. S24a, b). Nevertheless, the charge carrier profiles at 300 mA cm−2 remained consistent with those at 150 mA cm−2 (Fig. S24c–e), confirming that the underlying ion transport mechanism is independent of the current density under our operational conditions.

Clarifying mechanisms of charge carrier transport in pACM through modeling

A two-dimensional (2D) numerical model was developed (Tables S1, 2) to further describe the impact of the pACM on the charge carrier transport within the AAHE-pACM. The geometry of the 2D model contained four distinct layers arranged sequentially from left to right (Fig. 4a): the diffusion boundary layer of the anolyte (DBLa), the CEM, the pAEM, and the diffusion boundary layer of catholyte (DBLc). The pAEM was further divided into the membrane matrix (pAEMmem) and the pore region (pAEMpore).

The fidelity of the model was first established by the strong agreement between simulated and experimental time-resolved bulk ion concentrations (Supplementary Note 4, Figs. S25, S26). To elucidate the mechanisms by which the pACM regulates transport, the subsequent analysis focuses on quasi-steady-state profiles of ion concentration, potential, and current density, calculated using fixed boundary concentrations. This approach is justified by our finding that the internal transport profiles remain qualitatively consistent across the relevant electrolysis timescales, even as bulk concentrations undergo minor shifts. Physically, the use of fixed-concentration boundary conditions corresponds to a system with a sufficiently large electrolyte volume or with frequent replenishment, ensuring that the driving forces for transport remain stable during the period of interest. The simulated concentration profiles of water ion demonstrated the crucial role of the pAEM in preserving a stable pH gradient by effectively reducing water ion diffusion crossover. At Interf-3, the H+ concentration within the membrane matrix (vertical position 15−115 μm) remained higher than the adjacent pore region (vertical position 0−15 μm), confirming that H+ diffusion was hindered from the catholyte towards the anolyte (Fig. 4b). A sharp OH− concentration gradient observed at Interf-1 illustrated the efficient blockage of OH− crossover from the anolyte to the catholyte by CEM (Fig. S27). In addition, the OH− concentration at Interf-1 in AAHE-pACM can be seen to be higher than that in AAHE-CEM, underscoring the additional role of the pAEM in limiting H+ crossover to restrict the unwanted chemical neutralization reaction, thereby maintaining a sufficient pH gradient (Figs. S28, S29a, b and Supplementary Note 5).

The simulated spatial distributions of salt ions revealed the origin of the reduced resistance in the pACM compared to the BPM. Within Interf-2, the concentrations of K+ and SO42− decreased progressively from the membrane matrix to the pore region (∆CK+ = 2.4 M and ∆CSO42− = 1.1 M). This concentration gradient provides direct evidence that the pores within the pAEM offered a release pathway for accumulated salt ions (Fig. 4c, d), thereby mitigating the risk of salt precipitation, which can otherwise lead to membrane delamination and increased ohmic resistance (Fig. 2c, i). In addition, the uniformly distributed pore structure offered sufficient pathways for salt ion release from the membrane matrix, despite the small proportion of pore area in the pAEM (Fig. S1b−f). Furthermore, the pAEMmem facilitated SO42− transport, supplying essential negative charge into Interf-2 (Fig. 4d). To maintain local electroneutrality, an equivalent positive charge, carried by K+, migrated into Interf-2 and was subsequently released through the pAEMpore. This process made K+ the dominant charge carrier across the pACM, consistent with experimental results (Figs. 3i, S24c). To elucidate the overall function of pACM on charge carrier transport, we analyzed the resulting current density and potential distributions within the AAHE-pACM system. Consistent with the predominance of salt ions as charge carriers (Fig. 3g, i), the spatial patterns of current and potential directly reflect how pACM coordinated ion fluxes (Fig. 4e, f). As the dominant charge carrier, K+ contributed the largest proportion of net current in the AAHE-pACM, while SO42− accounted for a smaller share. In contrast, H+ in the catholyte and OH− in the anolyte carried a large amount of negative current due to diffusion opposing the migrational direction (Fig. S30a). The pAEMpore served as the primary conduit for net current across the pACM, confirming that this structure facilitated K+ migration (Fig. 4c, S30b). Specifically, localized current density peaks observed at Junctions 1 and 2 (Figs. 4e, S31 and S32a) corresponded to the leakage of K+ from Interf-2, where diffusive rather than migrational flux contributed dominantly to the net current (Figs. 4e, S32b). The enhanced K+ release facilitated by the pAEMpore also brought a much larger fraction of net current in this region compared to pAEMmem, despite the pore region having a shorter profile height (15 μm vs. 100 μm) (Fig. S30b).

While SO42− transport across pAEMmem was essential for lowering overall membrane resistance (Figs. 3g, S22), it did not generate a correspondingly large net current through pAEMmem (Figs. 4e, S32a). This apparent discrepancy was explained by a large counter-directed diffusive flux of SO42− from pAEMmem toward the catholyte, offsetting its migration current (Figs. 4e and S32b and Supplementary Note 6). The resulting potential profile (electric field distribution) across the AAHE-pACM was governed by the spatial arrangement of both mobile ions and fixed membrane charges (Fig. 4f). This regulation was clearly visualized along the horizontal interfaces, where abrupt potential changes were due to Donnan partition (Fig. S33, Supplementary Note 6).

The main role of pAEM in regulating charge carrier transport was further highlighted by comparison with the simulation analysis of AAHE-CEM. In the absence of pAEM, salt ion concentrations, current density and potential distributions became nearly uniform within each layer and across interfaces, underscoring the loss of spatially differentiated ion regulation (Figs. S29c, d, S34, S35 and Supplementary Note 5). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that pACM integrates the selective suppression of water ion crossover (maintaining pH gradient) with the coordinated transport of salt ions (reducing overall resistance), ultimately enabling the spatially modulated current and potential distributions essential for efficient AAHE system operation.

Voltage loss within the AAHE-pACM

We further examined the voltage loss across the membrane and electrodes within the AAHE-pACM system using a four-electrode flow cell (Figs. 5a, S36a, b) and a three-electrode flow cell (Fig. S36c, d). The voltage plateau observed in the polarization curves of pACM and CEM within the current density range of 100–200 mA cm⁻2 may be attributed to the reconstruction of the CEM–catholyte interface. Initially, exchangeable cations (K+) in the CEM are replaced by H⁺ at the catholyte-CEM interface (Figs. 4b, c, S29a, b). Meanwhile, K⁺ migrating from the anolyte underwent relay transport via H⁺ at the CEM surface, which might occupy only a small transmembrane voltage drop, with minimal variation due to the higher mobility of H⁺. Additionally, as the current increased, the enhanced electric field and suppressed H⁺ diffusion, leading to K+ accumulation within the CEM (Figs. 4b, c, S29a, b). This accumulation contributed a positive diffusive current, which likely also helped maintain a nearly constant transmembrane voltage drop. As the current increased further, the ohmic voltage drop across the membrane and solution became dominant, resulting in a linear increase in the transmembrane voltage drop for both CEM and pACM with current. The open circuit potential (OCP) of the anode matched its theoretical value (−1.05 V vs. SHE) (Fig. 5c). In contrast, the OCP of the cathode was 0.58 V vs. SHE, much more positive than the theoretical value (0 V vs. SHE). The positive shift of cathodic OCP may be attributed to the initial accumulation of H+ at the cathode24,45, and possibly impacted by DO46. This potential sharply became more negative (dropping to −0.1 V vs. SHE) as the current density increased to ~10 mA cm−2 (Fig. 5c), suggesting that while trace DO or locally accumulated H+ initially contributed to a high OCP, they were quickly depleted and thus had negligible influence on the electrochemical tests.

To identify opportunities for improving AAHE systems, we performed a voltage loss analysis of the AAHE-pACM. At current density of 600 mA cm−2, the anode overpotential accounted for the largest share of the total voltage loss of 43.4%, followed by the transmembrane drop (27.0%) and the cathode overpotential (22.6%), and only 7.0% contributed to the output cell voltage (Fig. 5d). This distribution indicated that developing a more efficient anodic catalyst is the promising path to enhancing the overall performance of the AAHE-pACM.

Scalable application and analysis of technical advantages

Having demonstrated the advantages of applying pACM in the AAHE system and revealed the specific charge carrier transport mechanisms in the AAHE-pACM, we next explored its performance under conditions relevant to practical applications. To demonstrate the scalable potential of the AAHE-pACM, we constructed a stack of three series-connected units, which achieved a high OCV of 4.82 V and a peak power output of 620 mW (Fig. 6a), sufficient to charge a smartphone (Fig. 6b). The AAHE-pACM system achieved highly efficient dual-electrode hydrogen production while simultaneously generating electricity. This integrated capability outperforms reported electrolyzers and self-powered systems that couple HER with various anodic reactions, with the peak power density at least 51% higher than recently reported self-powered systems (Tables S3, 4)10. Furthermore, the AAHE-pACM demonstrated satisfactory stability over 120 h of continuous operation at −0.4 V, maintaining FEs for H2 ranging from 190%–200% (Fig. 6c). Post-operation characterization confirmed the structural and chemical stability of both the Cu3Ag7/CF catalyst and the pACM (Figs. A simplified TEA confirmed that the LCOH for AAHE-pACM can be sharply reduced, enabling simultaneous value-added conversion of environmental wastes and net energy output (Supplementary Note 7). Assuming a renewable electricity price of $ 0.03 kWh−147, the sale of output electricity ($ 0.46 kgH2−1) and value-added products ($ 5.89 kgH2−1) affords an opportunity for the system to achieve a net profit of $ 5.34 kgH2−1 (total investment of $ 1.01−kgH2−1) (Fig. 6d, Table S5 and Supplementary Note 7). Sensitivity analysis demonstrates that the net profit is heavily dependent on the market price of the value-added product (formate). However, even with a 50% reduction in formate price, the AAHE-pACM system can still achieve net benefits from the H2 production (Fig. S41, Table S6). From an economic standpoint, the LCOH of AAHE-pACM is obviously lower than that of commercial alkaline water electrolysis (AWE, $ 5.30 kgH2−1) and proton exchange membrane water electrolysis (PEMWE, $ 3.87 kgH2−1) (Fig. 6d, Table S5), underscoring its potential to improve the economics of green hydrogen production. In addition, the high capability of the AAHE-pACM in pH gradient maintenance also gained 19% more profit compared to the AAHE-CEM from waste conversion (Supplementary Note 7).

From an environmental perspective, the AAHE-pACM exhibits lower equivalent CO2 emissions compared to AWE, PEMWE (CO2 emissions resulted from the production and transport process of renewable electricity) and conventional hydrogen production technologies47, such as natural gas steam reforming47, highlighting its high environmental sustainability (Table S7). Additionally, the electrochemical FOR in our system offers an efficient route for formaldehyde wastes conversion. Unlike traditional degradation methods such as chemical oxidation and catalytical combustion22,23,48, this process results in much lower or even zero CO2 emissions while co-producing value-added products (Fig. 6e).

This work successfully demonstrated an AAHE-pACM system for integrated hydrogen and power co-production. By designing a pACM to integrate acidic-HER and alkali-FOR, this system concurrently enables dual-electrode hydrogen production, value-added chemical synthesis, and net electricity output. The pACM decreased the membrane resistance by 57%–67% compared to a conventional BPM, and also reduced water ion loss by 68%–74% compared to a CEM. These advantages contribute to AAHE-pACM with a hydrogen production rate of 175 ± 8 molH2 m–2 h–1 without external electricity consumption and a high peak power density of 142 mW cm−2 for electricity output. Furthermore, the pAEM component in pACM has a crucial role in regulating charge carrier transport, thereby effectively suppressing water ion diffusion and salt precipitation. The pACM-enabled co-production strategy ultimately transforms the economics of hydrogen production, yielding a profit of $ 5.34 kgH2–1 and positioning the AAHE-pACM as a promising technology for profitable and environmentally sustainable hydrogen production. In this work, the fabrication of pAEM adopted an easy approach (perforation) for the proof of concept for pACM, while the precise regulation of pore architecture (such as pore size and distribution density) is expected to further improve the performance of pACM in the AAHE system. Therefore, subsequent work will focus on developing more precise pore fabrication methods, optimizing the pore size and area fraction, and elucidating how variations in pore architecture affect both the performance of pACM and the associated charge carrier transport mechanisms.

Methods

Chemicals and materials

CuSO4·5H2O (99%), H2SO4 (98%), Sodium citrate anhydrate (C6H5O7Na3, 98%), HCl (37%), C2H5OH (99.5%), HCHO (37%), KOH (85%), KCl (99.5%), H3PO4 (85%), isopropanol (99.7%) and acetonitrile (99.8%) were purchased form Sinopharm Group Co., Ltd. Pt/C (5%, Thermo Scientific), Nafion (5%, Macklin), 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH, 98%, Aladdin), Ag2SO4 (99%, Aladdin), Cu foam (CF, Kunshan Guangjiayuan Electronic Materials Co., Ltd), graphite felt (GF, Suzhou Sinero Technology Co., Ltd), bipolar membrane (BPM, 130 μm, Fumasep-FBM-PK), anion exchange membrane (AEM, 50 μm, Fumasep FAA-3-50), cation exchange membrane (CEM, 30 μm, Fumasep FKS 30) were used as purchased. Deionized (DI) water (18.25 MΩ·cm) was used to prepare aqueous solutions for the experiments. All chemicals were used without further purification.

Preparation of CuxAgy/CF anode

The CuxAgy catalysts were electrodeposited on a pre-cleaned CF substrate (1 × 1 cm2). The CF was sonicated sequentially in 1 M HCl solution, ethanol and DI water (10 min each) to remove contaminants. Electrodeposition was performed at −3 A cm−2 for 60 s in a two-electrode configuration (graphite rod counter electrode) under N2 (200 mL min−1) using a DH7002 electrochemical workstation. The electrolyte (20 mL) contained H2SO4 (1.5 M), C6H5O7Na3 (0.1 M), CuSO4 (20, 14, 10, 6, 0 mM) and Ag2SO4 (0, 6, 14, 10, 20 mM) was used, yielding the serial CuxAgy catalysts named as Cu10, Cu3Ag7, Cu5Ag5, Cu7Ag3 and Ag10.

Preparation of Pt/GF cathode

The GF (1 × 1 cm2) substrate was first calcinated at 500 °C in air for 5 h to enhance its hydrophilicity. It was then sequentially sonicated (10 min each) in 1 M HCl, ethanol and DI water to remove impurities. After drying at 40 °C overnight, a catalyst ink (10 mg Pt/C powder, 100 μL Nafion solution, 1 mL isopropanol, 0.66 mL DI and 0.2 mL ethanol) was sonicated for 6 h and then dropped-cast onto the GF to achieve a Pt loading of 0.5 mg cm−2.

Preparation of separators

The BPM and CEM were activated by soaking sequentially in 1 M KCl and DI water for 36 h. Similarly, AEM was activated in 1 M KOH and DI water for 36 h. To fabricate the pAEM, the activated AEM was perforated using a commercial microneedle roller (Chuyanzhenxuan 540, Fig. S1a), creating a uniform grid pattern with one horizontal and one vertical pass. The pAEM was then laminated with the activated CEM to form the composite ion exchange membrane, named pACM. AEM and CEM were kept hydrated throughout the process. During flow cell assembly, the CEM faced the anode, and the pAEM faced the cathode.

Electrochemical formaldehyde oxidation reaction (FOR) test

The electrochemical performance of CuxAgy electrocatalysts was evaluated in a 100 mL three-electrode configuration (batch type) using a Bio-Logic SAS VSP-3e electrochemical workstation. A Pt mesh (1 × 1 cm2) served as the counter electrode, and a Hg/HgO electrode served as the reference electrode. The electrolyte was a 1 M KOH solution containing 0.5 M HCHO. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was conducted at a scan rate of 10 mV s−1. A galvanostatic test was conducted at 100 mA cm−2 for 60 h, with the electrolyte refreshed every 20 h. All measured potentials versus the Hg/HgO reference were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) according to the equation: E(RHE) = E(Hg/HgO) + 0.059 × pH + 0.098 V. Prior to experiments, the reference electrode was calibrated by measuring its OCP against a fresh Hg/HgO (1 M KOH) electrode in a two-electrode setup, ensuring a potential difference of ±5 mV. iR compensation was conducted for the catalyst test, where the resistances were recorded by potential electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (PEIS) at OCP with a frequency ranging from 0.1–5 × 105 Hz and an amplitude of 10 mV.

Measurement of electrochemical surface area (ECSA)

The electrochemical active surface area (ECSA) of the CuxAgy electrocatalysts was estimated from their electrochemical double layer capacitance (Cdl). Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed in a 100 mL three-electrode configuration with a Pt mesh (1 × 1 cm2) counter electrode and a Hg/HgO electrode as reference electrode. The CVs were collected with scan rates ranging from 10 to 50 mV s−1 at potentials within the range of open circuit potential (OCP) ± 0.03 V (vs. Hg/HgO). The values of Cdl were converted to ECSA using the following equation:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${ECSA}=\frac{{C}_{{dl}}}{{C}_{s}}$$\end{document}ECSA=CdlCswhere Cs is the standard specific capacitance values (Cs, ~0.04 mF cm−2 cm−2ECSA)19.

Electrochemical performance acid-alkali hybrid electrochemical (AAHE) system

AAHE system's performance was tested in a lab-made stainless steel flow cell (two-electrode configuration) with a Cu3Ag7/CF anode and Pt/C/GF cathode (1 cm2 active area) using different separators (BPM, CEM, pACM; 4 cm2). Electrolytes (1 M KOH + 0.5 M HCHO anolyte, and 0.5 M H2SO4 catholyte) were circulated at 200 mL min−1. The pH was 13.8 ± 0.1 for anolyte, and 0.5 ± 0.1 measured by a pH meter (PHS-3E, INESA Scientific Instrument Co., LTD.). LSV was conducted at a scan rate of 10 mV s−1. High frequency resistance (HFR) was measured by staircase potential electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (SPEIS) at a constant frequency (108 kHz) and an amplitude of 10 mV from OCV to 0 V. Potentiostatic and galvanostatic tests (200 mL electrolyte) determined Faradaic efficiency (FE), yield rates for H2 and HCOO−, ion crossover, and discharge capacity. Each electrochemical test was repeated at least twice, and the standard deviation was obtained from the data of two parallel experiments. A long-term test for the pACM system used 10 L electrolytes, refreshed every 12 h without component replacement. Electrochemical measurements were conducted in duplicate.

OCV decay test

The anolyte and catholyte were continuously circulated through the flow cell, separated with BPM, CEM or pACM until a stable OCV was achieved, with the anode serving as the working electrode. The peristaltic pump was then stopped, leaving ~0.36 mL of electrolyte in each flow channel, allowing the system under non-operating conditions (without electrolyte circulating and applied current), rather than active operation. The subsequent decay of the OCV was monitored for 2 h, with an upper voltage limit set at 0 V.

Material characterizations

The crystal structure of the synthesized catalysts was characterized by an X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku-SmartLab) using Cu, Kα radiation at 40 kV and 30 mA. Surface elemental composition and chemical states were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Axis Supra+) with an Al/Ag Kα source. All XPS spectra were calibrated using the adventitious C1s peak at 284.80 eV, and the Cu 2p and Ag 3d spectra were fitted using the Avantage software (Version 6.6.0). The surface morphology and elemental distribution of the catalyst and the perforated AEM were examined using a scanning electron microscope with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDX, Hitachi-SU8010).

Products analysis

The gaseous and liquid products were quantified using a suite of analytical techniques. H2 generated at the cathode and anode was measured by the water drainage method. The crossover of water ions was assessed via acid-base titration using 10 mmol L−1 HCl and 10 mmol L−1 KOH. The concentration of K+ in the anode and cathode chambers was analyzed by flame atomic absorption spectroscopy (FAAS, novAA 800). The concentration of SO42− was determined by ion chromatography (IC, Dionex ICS-1000) equipped with an AS 11 analytical column and an AG 11 guard column, using 15 mM KOH as the mobile phase (1 mL min−1). Liquid products from the anolyte were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Agilent 1260). Prior to analysis, each 0.5 mL sample was neutralized with 0.5 M H2SO4 solution. HCHO was determined as its DNPH derivative at 355 nm using a C18 column and a mobile phase of 60% acetonitrile/40% DI water (1 mL min−1). The derivatization was performed with a solution containing 0.4 g DNPH, 20 mL concentrated H3PO4 and 140 mL acetonitrile. Formate/formic acid was detected directly at 210 nm using an Aminex HPX-87H column with 5 mM H2SO4 solution as the mobile phase (0.4 mL min−1).

Theory of two-dimensional (2D) numerical model of the AAHE-pACM and AAHE-CEM

Modeling charge carrier transport and electric potential across the CEM and pACM

The transport of charge carriers and the distribution of electric potential across the CEM and the pACM were modeled by numerically solving the Nernst-Planck equation under the electroneutrality condition using COMSOL Multiphysics 6.0 software. For time-dependent analysis (to establish model reliability), the electrolyte volume (200 mL) was consistent with the experimental conditions (Figs. 2h–i, S23). For the charge carrier transport mechanism analysis, all simulations were performed under an ideal condition with constant bulk electrolyte concentrations (physically corresponding to infinite electrolyte volume or non-circulating electrolytes). The results are presented at the equilibrium state, with the initial concentration of HCOO− set to zero.

Model geometry

The 2D geometry for the pACM model consisted of four sequential layers (Fig. 3a): an anolyte diffusion boundary layer (DBLa), a cation exchange layer (CEM), a perforated anion exchange layer (pAEM), and a catholyte diffusion boundary layer (DBLc). The CEM model (Fig. S28) simplified this structure to a DBLa, the CEM and a DBLc. All other physical and electrochemical parameters were consistent between the two models. The electrodes and electrochemical reactions were not included in the model domain. A comprehensive list of all parameters used is provided in Tables S1, S2.

Donnan partition

To correctly resolve the Donnan equilibrium at the interface between the pAEM and CEM, which is a challenge for the standard COMSOL electrochemistry module, a 1-μm thick electrolyte layer was inserted between them. This numerical approach is well established in the modeling of ion exchange membranes49 and has been previously employed to capture the correct potential jump and ion partitioning at interfaces without materially affecting the broader transport behavior. In contrast to BPMs, where a catalyst-containing interlayer may be present52, the absence of such a layer means that the electrolyte gap introduced in the model cannot be ascribed a direct physical counterpart. It is therefore considered a numerical construct introduced to enable thermodynamically consistent representation of the interface. Meanwhile, within the operating flow-cell setup, the precise thickness of any interfacial region is not experimentally accessible. Accordingly, the 1 µm value used in our simulations is treated as a fitted parameter rather than a measured physical dimension. Simulated Data Processing

Profiles of ion concentration, electric potential and current density were extracted directly from the modeling results. The total diffusion or migration flux for a specific ion across a boundary was calculated by integrating the x-component of the respective flux over that boundary’s length.
