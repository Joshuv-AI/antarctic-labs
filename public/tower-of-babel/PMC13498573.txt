Macroevolutionary trends of avian ichnodisparity in Gondwana
Authors: Martin E Farina, M Gabriela Mángano, Luis A Buatois, Claudia A Marsicano, Verónica Krapovickas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe concept of ichnodisparity is herein introduced for the study of fossil vertebrates, using Gondwanan avian footprints. We define ichnodisparity for trackways as the qualitative variation in morphological patterns of autopodial impressions, most likely reflecting functional or ecological thresholds. Foot architectural designs are defined based on anatomical features, including digit number and orientation and interdigital webbing, and size. Ichnodisparity in fossil bird tracks is proposed here as a useful proxy for analyzing evolutionary radiations and characterizing macroevolutionary patterns. Twenty-three geological formations from Gondwana were analyzed, spanning from the Early Cretaceous to the Late Pleistocene. The earliest morphotypes were predominantly large-sized, while small morphotypes became dominant over time. No zygodactyl footprints are recorded from Gondwana. Prior to the K/Pg boundary, ichnodisparity was high, consistent with a Cretaceous avian radiation. During the Eocene, morphological variety remained comparable to that of the Late Cretaceous — except for the large tridactyl design — suggesting ecological turnover rather than functional expansion; elevated ichnodisparity may be associated with the Early Eocene Climatic Optimum. The decline recorded in the Oligocene is likely attributable to sampling bias. The Miocene concentrates the highest ichnodisparity in the fossil record, with the reappearance of large pedal architectures linked to cursorial birds, supporting an expansion into open spaces. Introduction

The concept of disparity, initially proposed by Gould1, has been adapted to the field of ichnology by Buatois and Mángano2,3. While ichnodiversity focuses on the taxonomic diversity of trace fossils, ichnodisparity examines the variability of body patterns represented by biogenic structures. This concept has been useful for assessing the range of morphologic plans in biogenic structures, revealing major innovations in body plan, locomotory system, and/or behavioral programs4. It has been successfully applied in studies of invertebrates across different geological periods5–13. Its utility has been particularly evident in addressing paleobiological issues, such as the apparent temporal mismatch between the Cambrian explosion and the diversification of body patterns5. Despite these advances, specific applications or proposals for its application in the study of vertebrates have not yet been developed. The analysis of foot architectural designs as indicators of disparity is herein proposed as an alternative to overcome some of the limitations of ichnotaxonomy. While ichnotaxonomy may be biased by temporal factors, subjective interpretations, or variable taxonomic criteria, architectural designs provide a complementary approach that captures functional, behavioral, and ecological signs which can be traced through time, independently of specific ichnotaxa.

It has been suggested that an integrated assessment of morphological traits allows for more precise inferences about avian trophic niches and feeding habits than using individual traits14. Functional traits, defined as morphological and ecological characteristics that influence an organism’s performance and fitness, provide a fundamental quantitative framework for developing and evaluating hypotheses in evolutionary biology and ecology15. The analysis of these traits enables inferences at macroevolutionary level16. In birds, integrative studies have been conducted to infer ecological niches based on multiple traits14, and efforts have been made to unify databases that facilitate the evolutionary, structural, and functional extrapolation of diversity on a global scale15. These studies suggest that trophic niches exhibit similar morphological characteristics worldwide, highlighting convergence across multiple evolutionary contexts14. Additionally, Pigot et al.14 proposed that at least four independent morphological axes are required to predict variation in avian trophic niches.

The foot plays a crucial role as a functional trait, enabling the exchange of forces between an animal and its environment17. Birds stand out for their global success, as they populate all continents and adapt to a wide range of habitats. Within vertebrates, they are particularly intriguing due to the development of unique morphological and behavioral characteristics. The disposition of the leg bones and digitigrade form enables the legs to be used for propulsion, feeding, or grooming. This combination of locomotion modes (walking, running, jumping, flying, and swimming) has allowed birds to colonize nearly all environments on Earth18. Recently, it has been proposed that the plantar surface plays a significant role in navigating diverse substrates, such as the ground, branches, and water19. Consequently, footprint morphology has strong ecological implications for birds, serving as a potential indicator of past environments and paleoecological conditions.

The evolution of birds has been widely studied, yet certain questions remain unresolved.

Regardless of their origin, these footprints may indicate ecological convergence between archosaur producers and bird ancestors. Uncontroversial evidence of the origin of birds is known from the Middle to Late Jurassic, followed by various episodes of diversification21–23. The first radiation of modern birds occurred during the Late Cretaceous22. In the Oligocene, most of the current orders were established24, leading to the modern composition of avian fauna by the Miocene. During these phases of evolutionary radiation, birds underwent diversification of habits and ecological types, adapting to environments and available niches. However, some controversy remains regarding the evolution of the group22,24,25.

This study analyzes the architectural designs of bird feet to assess ichnodisparity, examining both its advantages and limitations as a proxy for ecological and evolutionary interpretation. Accordingly, we applied our analysis on ichnodisparity to the Gondwanan avian ichnological record in order to analyze paleocommunity dynamics through time.

Materials and methods

For the analysis of ichnodisparity, all known avian footprint-bearing sites from Gondwana were considered, spanning a temporal interval from the Early Cretaceous to the Late Pleistocene. A total of 23 geological formations were evaluated, reflecting the current state of knowledge of the Gondwanan avian ichnological record. These include one stratigraphic unit from Antarctica, three from Oceania, one from Africa, and eighteen from South America, seventeen of which correspond to Argentina. Material is available in Supplementary Table 1.

The terminology and quantitative parameters for describing footprints were based on Leonardi26and Lallensack et al27.. For descriptive terminology, we follow Herrero et al.28, but we prefer to use “giant” instead of “enormous” because we consider it grammatically more appropriate. Analytical figures were generated using RStudio (version 4.4.1).

From the avian autopodium, several morphological traits identified and conceptualized by previous authors17,19,29,30 serve as a basis for exploring ichnodisparity. These characteristics may differ substantially from ichnotaxobases, as they focus on ecological and evolutionary aspects, such as number and orientation of digits, presence and shape of interdigital webbing, and size. Size categories are defined as ranges measured from the base of the heel to the tip of digit III: Small (< 75 mm) and large (> 76 mm). Results

A total of eight pedal architectural designs were identified across the analyzed bearing-units: small tridactyl, large tridactyl, small semipalmate, large semipalmate, small palmate, large palmate, small anisodactyl, and large anisodactyl. Their occurrence, relative proportion, and richness per temporal interval are summarized in Figs. 2 and 3, and 4.

Architectural richness varies markedly through time (Fig. 2). The Early Cretaceous yielded three designs, rising to six in the Late Cretaceous. A decline to four is recorded in the Eocene, followed by a minimum of one in the Oligocene. Richness returned to six in the Miocene, dropped to three in the Pliocene and the Pleistocene. The large anisodactyl design was restricted to the Early Cretaceous, where it represented the dominant architecture (Fig. 3). The small anisodactyl design first appeared in the Late Cretaceous and persists through the Miocene and Pleistocene. The large tridactyl design was recorded in the Early Cretaceous, reappeared in the Late Cretaceous, and was again present in the Miocene and Pleistocene. The small tridactyl design occurred from the Late Cretaceous through the Miocene and Pliocene but was absent in the Pleistocene. Small semipalmate footprints were recorded from the Late Cretaceous onward, with the exception of the Oligocene, and persisted through the Pleistocene. The large semipalmate design appeared in the Oligocene — where it represented the sole architecture recorded — and is also present in the Miocene. Small palmate footprints were recorded in the Late Cretaceous, Eocene, and Miocene through Pleistocene. The large palmate design appeared for the first time in the Pliocene and persisted in the Pleistocene.

In terms of relative proportions (Fig. 3), the large anisodactyl design dominated the Early Cretaceous assemblage. In the Late Cretaceous, proportions were more evenly distributed among the six recorded designs. The Oligocene was characterized by the exclusive dominance of the large semipalmate design. During the Miocene, the small semipalmate architecture showed the highest proportion, while remaining designs contributee more equally.

Discussion

Architectural designs

Ichnodisparity measures the variability of morphological plans in biogenic structures rather than ichnotaxonomic richness2. Architectural designs are independent of taxonomic resolution and the subjectivity inherent to ichnotaxonomy as it is dependable on the taxonomist criteria for election of a character type to define taxonomic levels31. As a consequence, ichnodisparity and ichnodiversity are not necessarily concordant. Ichnodisparity may be high where ichnodiversity is low, and vice versa — a decoupling that has proven particularly informative in detecting ecological radiations without taxonomic richness increase, or diversity collapses without loss of morphological plans2,3. This decoupling is illustrated by the Gondwanan avian record itself, as exemplifies the Puesto La Flecha Formation (late Eocene–Oligocene). Although six ichnotaxa and morphotypes are recognized in this unit, the resulting ichnodisparity is considerably lower, with only one architectural designs identified: small anisodactyl (Supplementary Table 1).

The morphological characters used to define pedal architectural designs coincide with some — but not all — traditional ichnotaxobases for avian footprints. This is not a weakness but rather reflects the internal consistency of the approach. Ichnodisparity does not challenge ichnodiversity, nor replace it, simply operates at a different level of abstraction, as ichnodiversity counts ichnotaxa whereas ichnodisparity counts morphological plans2. The fact that both share the same underlying characters does not generate conceptual overlap, just as taxonomic diversity and morphological disparity in body fossils share anatomical characters without being equivalent32,33.

The footprint length threshold used in the present analysis to distinguish large and small architectural designs follows the convention of Herrero et al.28 and is explicitly operational in nature. Unlike purely qualitative criteria, however, it provides a quantitative and reproducible basis for size discrimination.

Digital configuration

The orientation of the digits is a key parameter for interpreting avian locomotor habits. The three major configurations (i.e., anisodactyl, tridactyl, and zygodactyl) based on toe orientation are associated with distinct functional specializations in birds34. Although strongly linked to ecological habits, no configuration is restricted to a single habitat or locomotor context. For example, Psittaciformes and some Piciformes show a broad association between zygodactyly and arboreal locomotion35. In contrast, neomorphine cuckoos retain the zygodactyly in a predominantly cursorial context, revealing both the dominant ecological signature of zygodactyly and the capacity of some lineages to decouple inherited morphology from habitat use35,36.

The morphology of digit I (hallux) is variable in birds, as it may articulate distally or more proximally on the tarsometatarsus34, and its orientation ranges from anteromedial to fully reversed37. From an evolutionary perspective, hallux morphological variation is associated with shifts in grasping power, prey manipulation, and tree-climbing ability (zygodactyl and anisodactyl with a long and or strong digit I)38. In several lineages, the posteriorly directed hallux may become vestigial or only minimally developed (functionally tridactyl), as typically observed in ground birds17,19. In the present framework, only the presence or absence of the hallux is considered in the architectural designs, independent of its orientation. Thus, the presence, absence, and positional attributes of digit I, together with the overall digital configuration, constitute key ecological and functional parameters.

Interdigital web

The presence of an interdigital web is commonly associated with birds adapted to aquatic environments. This is particularly true for species with conspicuous interdigital web, such as flamingos, ducks, and geese35. In general, webbing varies in its degree of development, configuring the palmate foot, semipalmate foot, totipalmate foot, and lobate foot29, which may also indicate differences in habitats. Conversely, the absence of webbing does not preclude occupation of aquatic environments39. For example, some charadriiforms exhibit only incipient webbing despite their close association with aquatic habitats, whereas groups such as flamingos, ducks, geese, and coots display a markedly developed and functionally important web. Thus, while a well-developed interdigital web can serve as a robust environmental indicator, more subtle expressions of webbing require cautious evaluation.

Macroevolutionary trends

In general terms, small morphotypes almost double the number of large morphotypes. No zygodactyl footprints are recorded from Gondwana, which is remarkable given that a considerable diversity of birds with this pedal architecture is known from Gondwana since the Eocene40. The only ichnotaxon with this pedal architecture recorded globally is Shandongornipes muxiai from the Early Cretaceous of China41,42.

The oldest undisputed avian footprints from Gondwana are preserved in the Barremian–Aptian Wonthaggi Formation of Australia43. Four morphotypes were described from this unit, three corresponding to large pedal architectures and one to a small pedal architecture, all anisodactyl. Somewhat younger material, of Albian age, is known from the Eumeralla Formation, also in Australia44, where two morphotypes are recognized, both corresponding to large tridactyl architectures. These architectures are consistent with the proposal of an anisodactyl ancestor.

From the Campanian through the early Danian, small forms predominated in Gondwana (Fig. 3). Palmate and semipalmate footprints are also recorded in this interval. Small architectural designs include anisodactyl, tridactyl, semipalmate, and palmate, whereas large architectural designs are represented exclusively by the tridactyl. This elevated ichnodisparity suggests the ecological diversification that occurred toward the end of the Cretaceous (Fig. 4), prior to the K/Pg boundary, and is consistent with a Cretaceous avian radiation22,24. Some of these forms are known from the Maastrichtian–Danian of Argentina45, indicating elevated ichnodisparity immediately before the K/Pg boundary. This pattern is consistent with the interpretation that the fossil record provides no evidence of a sudden disappearance of birds at that boundary45. The proposal of an avian bottleneck with restricted taxonomic diversity from the Late Cretaceous through the Paleogene47 thus appears unlikely.

During the Eocene, most pedal architectures recorded in the Late Cretaceous are still present, with the exception of the large tridactyl and large semipalmate designs (Figs. 3 and 4). Taxonomic diversity and inferred trackmakers differ between both intervals, but the same pedal architectures are maintained. This indicates that the observed change corresponds to a taxonomic turnover rather than an ecological replacement: no expansion of ecospace is evidenced, but rather the occupation of the same functional niches by different taxa.

The morphologies recorded in the early–middle Eocene of Antarctica are particularly noteworthy. Although ichnotaxonomic diversity is low (only three ichnotaxa recognized), each corresponds to a single pedal architecture (Gruipeda fuenzalidae = small semipalmate, cf. Uhangrichnus = small palmate, Avipeda isp. = small tridactyl). This one-to-one correspondence between ichnodiversity and ichnodisparity may indicate a high degree of ecological specialization among the observed taxa. Ichnodisparity remains elevated during the Eocene (Figs. 2 and 4), consistent with the avian radiation associated with the Early Eocene Climatic Optimum48. In Gondwana, the record allows this trend to be traced from the early Eocene (e.g., Fossil Hill and Puesto La Flecha formations) through the Oligocene (Tremembé Formation). For the latter unit, however, available information is restricted to a single ichnotaxon (Ardeipeda49), whose pedal architecture corresponds to a large semipalmate design. This abrupt decline may be linked to the climatic deterioration initiated during the late Eocene–Oligocene or may reflect a preservational bias associated with the scarcity of footprint-bearing levels and/or limited prospection of rocks of this age in Gondwana. A thorough analysis of the record gap during the Eocene–Oligocene, however, falls beyond the scope of this work.

The highest ichnodisparity is recorded during the Miocene (Figs. 2, 3 and 4), when small anisodactyl, small tridactyl, large tridactyl, small semipalmate, large semipalmate, and small palmate architectures are all recognized — the same designs recorded during the Late Cretaceous. This elevated ichnodisparity is accompanied by high ichnodiversity in the Vinchina and Sijes formations and reflects ecological conditions that would have allowed an avian radiation in the region. Particularly noteworthy is the record of the large tridactyl pedal architecture, absent since the end of the Cretaceous. This design is associated almost exclusively with cursorial taxa, which may reflect the expansion of open spaces during this period50,51 and the colonization of these ecosystems by birds.

Also remarkable is the absence of the large anisodactyl and large palmate morphotypes, as well as the zygodactyl, which, as noted above, has not yet been recorded from Gondwana. The large palmate pedal architecture does not appear until well into the Pliocene.

The Pliocene showed a marked decrease in pedal architectures relative to the Miocene, recovering from the Pleistocene onward (Fig. 2), when a turnover in these architectures was recorded. Although a similar number of morphotypes was maintained, small tridactyl forms disappeared and the large palmate architecture was recorded for the first time (Figs. 3 and 4). This pattern is remarkable, given that the small tridactyl architecture was widespread during the Miocene (e.g., Sijes and Vinchina formations) and is no longer observed in Pleistocene strata. Since this architecture is broadly distributed among birds and is observed in taxa with wide ecological amplitude, a detailed analysis of the reasons for its absence requires a thorough comparison with the body fossil record that exceeds the scope of this study.

In summary, at the Gondwanan scale, the highest ichnodisparity is recorded during the Late Cretaceous and the Miocene (Fig. 2). Minimum values were apparent during the Oligocene; this pattern, however, is interpreted as a sampling bias rather than a genuine biological signal. In this context, the low ichnodisparity observed in the Anacleto and Puesto La Flecha formations does not necessarily reflect macroevolutionary trends, but rather differences in preservational modes, sedimentary environments, and prospection effort. By contrast, the ichnodisparity observed in the Vinchina and Toro Negro formations shows a notable concordance with the general trends of the Gondwanan record.

Conclusions

Architectural designs highlight the value of ichnodisparity as a framework that captures ecological variation beyond taxonomic constraints. Rather than providing direct paleoenvironmental signals, these designs offer a qualitative proxy for ecological structuring. The proposed foot architectural designs are small anisodactyl, large anisodactyl, zygodactyl, small tridactyl, large tridactyl, small semipalmate, large semipalmate, small palmate, and large palmate.

Early Cretaceous avian footprints are dominated by large morphotypes, but small forms prevail thereafter. No zygodactyl tracks are recorded in Gondwana. High ichnodisparity before the K/Pg boundary indicates substantial ecological diversification, consistent with a Cretaceous avian radiation. In the Eocene, pedal architectures largely mirror those of the Late Cretaceous — except for the absence of large tridactyl forms — suggesting ecological turnover without functional expansion; ichnodisparity nevertheless remains high, most likely linked to the Early Eocene Climatic Optimum. The Oligocene shows a decline in both ichnodisparity and ichnodiversity, interpreted as sampling bias rather than a biological signal. The Miocene records peak ichnodisparity, coincident with renewed avian radiation and the reappearance of large cursorial pedal architectures, indicating expansion into open space. This pattern continues into the Pliocene. In contrast, the Pleistocene marks a shift, with the disappearance of small tridactyl forms and the first appearance of large palmate architectures, signaling a new episode of ecological turnover. Future work should focus on extending the ichnodisparity framework to other vertebrate groups with distinctive autopodial morphologies — such as non-avian theropods, mammals, and turtles — and on integrating datasets to better constrain the ecological and evolutionary drivers of vertebrate trace fossil assemblages through time.
