Tridendriform algebras on hypergraph polytopes, the other way around
Pierre-Louis Curien, Bérénice Delcroix-Oger, Jovana Obradović

Abstract
Hypergraph polytopes (or nestohedra) form a broad class of polytopes obtained by truncating faces of a simplex according to a hypergraph. In earlier work, the authors constructed q q -tridendriform algebras on the set of faces of certain families of hypergraph polytopes, including associahedra and permutohedra. The well-definedness of these structures relied on a connectedness property on the hypergraphs involved, called strictness. Nevertheless, notable examples of hypergraph polytopes such as cyclohedra fell outside this setting. We introduce a new connectedness condition, called anti-strictness, which goes opposite to strictness and captures a different class of hypergraph polytopes, including associahedra, permutohedra and cyclohedra. Our main result produces natural ( − 1 ) (-1) -tridendriform algebras in the anti-strict framework, which match previously introduced tridendriform algebras in the overlap of the two frameworks, thereby extending the range of hypergraph polytopes admitting such algebraic structures.



Introduction

In the previous work
[
4
]
,
we introduced
q
-tridendriform algebras (cf.
[
1
]
) on the sets of faces of certain families of hypergraph polytopes (a.k.a. nestohedra
[
10
]
), encompassing the classical constructions on associahedra and permutohedra due to Loday-Ronco and Palacios-Ronco (namely, shuffles of trees and shuffles of surjections). Hypergraph potytopes are the generalisation of graph associahedra which allows (hyper)edges of arbitrary cardinality, not only of cardinality 2.
Just like graph associahedra, hypergraph polytopes admit a purely combinatorial description of their face posets. It is this description that underlies the algebraic structures that we study.

The general construction is the following. We consider a finite family of connected hypergraphs
\{{\bf H}_{a}\,|\,a\in A\}
with pairwise disjoint vertex sets
H_{a}
, together with a connected hypergraph
{\bf H}
whose vertex set
H
is the disjoint union of the sets
H_{a}
. These data form what we call a
preteam
. A preteam
serves as the arity
of an operation that takes as input an
A
-indexed family of faces
C_{a}
, where each
C_{a}
is a face of the hypergraph polytope associated with
{\bf H}_{a}
, and returns a face of the hypergraph polytope associated with
{\bf H}
. Conceptually, this operation is a kind of generalised shuffle: the input faces may be thought of as structured “sets of cards” that are interweaved according to the combinatorics of
{\bf H}
.

For our general theory to work, we had to fix some condition on preteams. This condition, that we called
strictness
, requires that whenever
K
is a connected subset of
H_{a}
(according to the hypergraph structure
{\bf H}_{a}
), then
K
remains connected when viewed inside
{\bf H}
. We also have designed
a slight weakening of the strict framework, in terms of the
quasi-strictness
condition, allowing us to encompass simplices and hypercubes.

However, these frameworks did not cover certain natural and important families of polytopes. In particular, cyclohedra
[
9
,
12
]
could not be accommodated, despite the fact that an
explicit tridendriform algebra structure could be constructed for them by hand. In our search for yet another “variant” of our condition that would encompass the case of cyclohedra,
we discovered instead a different general and
incomparable
framework. Interestingly, the new framework reverses the direction of connectivity preservation: it requires that, whenever
K
is a connected subset of
H
(according to the hypergraph structure
{\bf H}
), then
K\cap H_{a}
is also connected in
{\bf H}_{a}
, for all
a
such that
K\cap H_{a}
is non-empty.
We call this condition
anti-strictness
. The resulting framework allows us to treat cyclohedra, as well as other examples, such as associahedra assembled by insertion rather than by concatenation of the underlying linear graphs. It also turns out that many examples previously studied in
[
4
]
still fit in the anti-strict setting as well, in the sense that they are both strict and anti-strict. Moreover, the proof that we get a polydendriform structure (which is the varying-arity version of tridendriform structure) is nicer, and even the definition of the shuffle product is more elegant, and coincides with the one in
[
4
]
for families happening to be both strict and anti-strict.

This paper reports on these new findings. It can be read mostly independently from our previous work, but we refer to it (and to
[
5
]
, as well as to the abundant literature on graph associahedra) for a more leisurely introduction to hypergraph polytopes and their geometric realisation as truncations of simplices.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
1
, we recall basic facts about hypergraph polytopes and their combinatorial description in terms of tree-like objects called constructs. We also review and discuss the notion of restriction of a construct, which plays an essential role in the sequel. In Section
2
, we define anti-strict teams and clans, and
give examples that in particular reflect the incomparability of strict and anti-strict frameworks. In Section
3
, we introduce shuffle product for anti-strict clans, and prove our main associativity result. Section
4
compares our framework with related constructions in the literature, in particular those of Ronco, Chapoton, Forcey and Springfield. We conclude with an appendix presenting a non-inductive characterisation of restriction of a construct, which may be of independent interest.

In this section, we recall the basic combinatorial framework of hypergraph polytopes, fixing notation and terminology. Sections

Most of the material in these four sections follows closely the earlier work

1.1
Hypergraphs

A hypergraph consists of a set
H
of
vertices
and a subset

{\bf H}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}(H)\backslash\emptyset
such that
\bigcup{\bf H}=H
, whose elements are called
hyperedges
. In addition, we assume that

\{x\}\in{\bf H}
, for all
x\in H
(in terminology of
[
6
]
and
[
4
]
, our hypergraphs are
atomic
). Throughout the paper, we shall use the same bold symbol
{\bf H}
to denote both an entire hypergraph and its set of hyperedges. A hyperedge of cardinality 2 is called an
edge
. Note that any ordinary graph
(V,E)
can be viewed as the hypergraph

\{\{v\}\mid v\in V\}\cup\{e\mid e\in E\}
(with no hyperedge of cardinality
\geq 3
).

Given a hypergraph
{\bf H}
and a subset
X\subseteq H
, we define the restriction of
{\bf H}
to
X
as the hypergraph

{\bf H}_{X}:=\{Z\mid Z\in{\bf H}\;\mbox{and}\;Z\subseteq X\}
, and we introduce the abbreviation
{\bf H}\backslash X:={\bf H}_{H\backslash X}
. We say that
{\bf H}
is
connected
if there is no non-trivial partition
H=X_{1}\cup X_{2}
such that
{\bf H}={\bf H}_{X_{1}}\cup{\bf H}_{X_{2}}
, and that
X\subseteq H
is connected in
{\bf H}

if
{\bf H}_{X}
is connected.
For each finite hypergraph there exists a unique partition

H=X_{1}\cup\ldots\cup X_{m}
such that each
{\bf H}_{X_{i}}
is connected and
{\bf H}=\bigcup({\bf H}_{X_{i}})
. The
{\bf H}_{X_{i}}
are called the
connected components
of
{\bf H}
. If
{\bf H}_{1},\ldots,{\bf H}_{n}
are the
connected components of
{\bf H}\backslash X
, we write

{\bf H},X\leadsto{\bf H}_{1},\ldots,{\bf H}_{n}
.

Convention 1.1
.

If
H
and
Z
are subsets of a common subset and
H\cap Z\neq\emptyset
, we shall often simply write

{\bf H}_{Z}
for
{\bf H}_{Z\cap H}
.

1.2
Constructs

As shown by Došen and Petrić
[
6
]
, the non-trivial connected subsets of a finite connected hypergraph
{\bf H}

encode the truncation data for a simplex with
|H|
vertices.
The faces of the resulting polytope admit a combinatorial description in terms of certain
non-planar trees, called
constructs
, whose nodes are decorated by non-empty subsets of
H
. We now recall their recursive definition, using the syntax introduced in
[
5
]
.

Let
\emptyset\neq Y\subseteq H
. If
{\bf H},Y\leadsto{\bf H}_{1},\ldots,{\bf H}_{n}
, and if
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}
are constructs of
{\bf H}_{1},\ldots,{\bf H}_{n}
, respectively, then the tree obtained by grafting
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}
on the root node decorated by
Y
, denoted by
Y(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
(or sometimes
Y\{T_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
), is a construct of
{\bf H}
. We write
Y=\mbox{root}(Y(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}))
.
The base case is when
Y=H
(and hence
n=0
): then the one-node tree
H()
(written simply
H
) is a construct.
We write
T:{\bf H}
to denote that
T
is a construct of
{\bf H}
.

Convention 1.2
.

We identify the vertices of constructs with the sets decorating them. In addition, to simplify notation, we omit braces around singleton vertices. For example, instead of

\{x\}(\{u,v\},\{y\})
and
\{x\}(\{u\}(\{v\}),\{y\})
, we shall write
x(\{u,v\},y)
and
x(u(v),y)
, respectively.

In the geometric realisation, the dimension of the face encoded by a construct
T
is
\sum_{X}(|X|-1)
, where

X
ranges over all nodes of
T
. In particular, the vertices of the polytope realising
{\bf H}
are the constructs of
{\bf H}
whose nodes are all singletons. They are called
constructions
.

Constructs can be ordered by edge contractions. More precisely, given a construct
S
with two adjacent nodes

X,Y
, we can contract the edge connecting them into a single node labelled
X\cup Y
. This captures exactly the immediate subface relation in the geometric realisation.

The polytopes realised in this way are called
hypergraph polytopes
, after Došen and Petrić
[
6
]
, or
nestohedra
, after Postnikov
[
10
]
.
In the special case where
{\bf H}
is a graph, we recover the extensively investigated notion of graph associahedra.

1.3
Fundamental examples of hypergraph polytopes

The polytopes for the complete graphs

{\bf P}^{X}=\{\{x_{1}\},\ldots,\{x_{n}\},\{x_{1},\ldots x_{n}\}\}\cup\{\{x_{i},x_{j}\}\mid 1\leq i\neq j\leq n\},

with
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
, are the
(n-1)
-dimensional
permutohedra
. Since each non-empty subset of
X
is connected in
{\bf P}^{X}
, the constructs of
{\bf P}^{X}
correspond to ordered partitions of
X
, or, equivalently, to surjections
X\xrightarrow{}\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow\{1,\dots,k\}
.

For a totally ordered set
X=\{x_{1}<\dots<x_{n}\}
, the polytope for the hypergraph

{\bf K}^{X}=\{\{x_{1}\},\dots,\{x_{n}\},\{x_{1},x_{2}\},\dots,\{x_{n-1},x_{n}\}\}

is called the
(n-1)
-dimensional
associahedron
. The constructs of
{\bf K}^{X}
are in one-to-one correspondence with partially parenthesised words, or, equivalently, with Schröder trees with at least two leaves (the equivalence of these three characterisations of the faces of the associahedra is illustrated in
[
5
, Section 2.4]
).

For a finite cyclically ordered set
X=\{x_{1}<\ldots<x_{n}<x_{1}\}
, the polytope for the hypergraph

{\bf O}^{X}=\{\{x_{1}\},\ldots,\{x_{n}\},\{x_{1},x_{2}\},\ldots,\{x_{n-1},x_{n}\},\{x_{n},x_{1}\}\}

is called the
(n-1)
-dimensional
cyclohedron
.
The constructs of
{\bf O}^{X}
correspond to partially parenthesised cyclic words, through their equivalence with tubings or nested sets (see
[
5
, Proposition 2]
and
[
9
, Lemma 1.4]
).

All examples so far are graph associahedra. There are two fundamental examples of genuine hypergraph polytopes, with which we close our list for now (later, we shall encounter erosohedra, friezohedra, stellohedra and teleassociahedra).

For a finite set
X
, the
(|X|-1)
-dimensional
simplex
is encoded by the hypergraph

{\bf S}^{X}=\{\{x\}\mid x\in X\}\cup\{\{X\}\},

having only trivial hyperedges (i.e., the vertices and the maximal hyperedge ensuring that the hypergraph is connected). The constructs have the form
Y\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
, where
\emptyset\subsetneq Y\subseteq X
and
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}=X\backslash Y
.

For a finite ordered set
X=\{x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}\}
, the polytope for the hypergraph

{\bf C}^{X}=\{\{x_{1}\},\dots,\{x_{n}\}\}\cup\{\{x_{j}\mid 1\leq j\leq i\}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}.

is the
(n-1)
-dimensional
hypercube
. The constructs of
{\bf C}^{X}
are in one-to-one correspondence with the set of words of
length
n
over the alphabet
\{+,-,\bullet\}
, starting with
+
(see
[
4
, Example 3.7]
for details).

1.4
Restriction

We recall below the definition of the restriction
{C}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}
of a construct
C:{\bf H}
to a non-empty connected subset
K
of
H
.

Definition 1.3

(Restriction of a construct)
.

For a hypergraph
{\bf H}
, a non-empty subset
K\subseteq H
such that the hypergraph
{\bf K}:={\bf H}_{K}
is connected, and a construct
C:{\bf H}
,
the restriction of
C
to
K
is the construct
C_{\lceil K}:{\bf K}
defined as follows:

•

if
C=H
, then
C_{\lceil K}=K
,

•

if
C=X(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})
, where
\emptyset\neq X\subsetneq H
,
{\bf H},X\leadsto{\bf H}_{1},\dots,{\bf H}_{n}
and
C_{i}:{\bf H}_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
, and

–

if
X\cap K=\emptyset
, then there exists
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
{\bf K}
is connected in
{\bf H}_{i}
, and we set
C_{\lceil K}={(C_{i})}_{\lceil_{K}}
;

–

otherwise, supposing that
{\bf K},(X\cap K)\leadsto K_{1},\dots,K_{p}
, we have that for each
j\in\{1,\dots,p\}
there exists
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, such that
K_{j}
is connected in
H_{i}
; we denote by
\psi:\{1,\dots,p\}\rightarrow\{1,\dots,n\}
the induced index correspondence, and we set

C_{\lceil_{K}}=(X\cap K)((C_{\psi(1)})_{\lceil_{K_{1}}},\dots,(C_{\psi(p)})_{\lceil_{K_{p}}}).

A non-inductive characterization of the notion of restriction of a construct can be found in the Appendix.

Remark 1.4
.

For connected subsets
\emptyset\neq L\subseteq K
and
\emptyset\neq K\subseteq H
and a construct
C:{\bf H}
, it holds that
{(C_{\lceil_{K}})}_{\lceil_{L}}=C_{\lceil_{L}}
.

In the following example, we illustrate a situation in which the restriction of a construct “brakes” the ancestry relation.

Example 1.5
.

For the hypergraph

{{\bf H}}=\{\{1\},\{2\},\{3\},\{4\},\{1,2\},\{2,3\},\{3,4\},\{1,4\}\},

the construct
U=1(2(3(4)))
of
{\bf H}
and the connected subset
K=\{1,2,4\}
of
{\bf H}
, we have that
{U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}=1(2,4)
. Therefore, the node
2
used to be strictly below the node
4
in
U
, while, in
{U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}
, these two nodes became incomparable.

2
Anti-strict teams

In this section, we introduce the main notion of the paper:
anti-strict teams
. We also introduce the corresponding notion of anti-strict clans and present examples illustrating the incomparability between strict and anti-strict frameworks.

We begin by recalling from
[
4
]
that a preteam
\tau
consists of
a finite collection
\{{\bf H}_{a}\mid a\in A\}
of connected hypergraphs with disjoint sets of vertices, together with
a connected hypergraph
{\bf H}
, such that
H=\bigcup_{a\in A}H_{a}
.
We use the notation
\tau=(\{{\bf H}_{a}\mid a\in A\},{\bf H})
and we refer to the
{\bf H}_{a}
’s as the participating hypergraphs of
\tau
and to
{\bf H}
as the coordinating hypergraph of
\tau
.

Definition 2.1

(Anti-strict team)
.

A preteam
\tau=(\{{\bf H}_{a}\,|\,a\in A\},{\bf H})
is called an
anti-strict team
if it satisfies the following property:

(AS)

for every subset
\emptyset\neq K\subseteq H
, if
K
is connected in
{\bf H}
, then for all
a\in A
such that
H_{a}\cap K\neq\emptyset
,
H_{a}\cap K
is connected in
{\bf H}_{a}
.

The notion of strictness and anti-strictness can be illustrated in a cobordism-like manner. We do this in Figure
1
.

(S)

feature the participating and coordinating hypergraphs, respectively. The forbidden configuration in the anti-strict case (AS) is given by a connected subset

{\bf H}_{a}
. The forbidden configuration in the strict case (S) is given by a connected subset
K
in one of the upper disks which disconnects into more than one connected component in the coordinating hypergraph

.

The following lemma provides an alternative characterisation of anti-strict team.
Let
\tau=(\{{\bf H}_{a}\,|\,a\in A\},{\bf H})
be a preteam. Pick a subset
\emptyset\neq B\subseteq A
and for each
b\in B
a subset
\emptyset\neq X_{b}\subseteq H_{b}
, and
consider the decompositions

\begin{array}[]{l}{\bf H}_{b},X_{b}\leadsto{\bf H}_{(b,1)},\ldots,{\bf H}_{(b,n_{b})}\\
{\bf H},X_{B}\leadsto{\bf H}_{1}^{B},\ldots,{\bf H}_{n_{B}}^{B}\quad(\textrm{where}\;X_{B}=\bigcup_{b\in B}X_{b}).\end{array}

(1)

Lemma 2.2
.

A preteam
\tau=(\{{\bf H}_{a}\,|\,a\in A\},{\bf H})
is an anti-strict team if and only if the following property holds: for each choice of subsets
\emptyset\neq B\subseteq A
and
\emptyset\neq X_{b}\subseteq H_{b}
for which
n_{B}=1
in the decomposition (
1
), we have that, for each
b\in B
, either
n_{b}=0
or
n_{b}=1
.

Proof.

The statement of the lemma is a matter of mere repackaging of Definition
2.1
. More precisely, it follows from the following sequence of observations:

•

The data of
B
and of the sets
X_{b}
amount to the data of some non-empty
X\subseteq H
(and then
B=\{b\in A\mid H_{b}\cap X\neq\emptyset\}
).
The assumption
n_{B}=1
amounts to say that
X=H\setminus K
for a connected subset
K
in
{\bf H}
.

•

The assumption
n_{b}=0
amounts to say that
H_{b}\cap K
is empty, while the assumption
n_{b}=1
says that

H_{b}\cap K
is connected in
{\bf H}_{b}
.

∎

We next define the notion of universe and of anti-strict clan. Informally, it is a collection of anti-strict teams satisfying a closure condition that will ensure the correctness of our definition of shuffle product in the next section.
To proceed, we need a notation.
For an anti-strict team
\tau=(\{{\bf H}_{a}\,|\,a\in A\},{\bf H})
and a connected subset
\emptyset\neq K\subseteq H
, we define an induced preteam
\tau_{K}
as follows (using Convention
1.1
):

\tau_{K}=(\{{({\bf H}_{{a}})_{K}\,|\,a\in A\;\mbox{and}\;H_{{a}}\cap K\neq\emptyset\},{\bf K}}).

(2)

Definition 2.3
.

A
universe
is a collection
\mathfrak{U}
of connected hypergraphs. An
anti-strict clan
on some universe
\mathfrak{U}
is a set
\Xi
of anti-strict teams such that, for each
\tau\in\Xi
, the participating hypergraphs and the coordinating hypergraph of
\tau
belong to
\mathfrak{U}
, and which is closed under decomposition, in the sense that,
\tau_{K}\in\Xi
for each connected subset
\emptyset\neq K\subseteq H
.

We now present two fundamental examples of anti-strict clans.

Example 2.4

(The clan of trees of associahedra)
.

As universe, we fix the class of all associahedra. We are going to describe preteams in terms of planar trees with leaves, as follows. Let
T
be a rooted planar tree with set of nodes
A
. For
a\in A
, denote by
l(T,a)
the set of leaves of
T
attached to
a
, endowed with the total order induced by planarity. Our preteams have the form

(\{{\bf K}^{l(T,a)}\,|\,a\in A\},{\bf K}^{l(T)}),

where
l(T)
is the set of all leaves of
T
, again ordered by planarity. In order to see that such a preteam is an anti-strict team, we note that each connected subset
\emptyset\neq K\subseteq{K}^{l(T)}
is a subinterval of
l(T)
, which entails, by planarity, that for each
a\in A
,
l(T,a)\cap K
is an interval on
l(T,a)
(or empty), and hence connected in
{\bf K}^{l(T,a)}
. We define the clan of trees of associahedra as the set of all teams defined as above. It remains to verify that this set of teams is closed under restriction to connected subsets. Indeed, each connected
\emptyset\neq K\subseteq K^{l(T)}
is an interval on
l(T)
, which in turn determines a subtree
T^{\prime}
of
T
having precisely
K
as its set of leaves. It is easily seen that
\tau_{K}
is determined precisely by
T^{\prime}
, and hence belongs to the clan.

We note that this clan is not strict. Indeed, consider the tree
T
with two nodes
a
and
b
, with root
a
having as inputs leaf 1, the edge from
b
and leaf 4 (in this order), and
b
has no incoming internal edge and has 2,3 (in this order) as leaves. We can write
T
in term form as
a(1,b(2,3),4)
.

Then in the corresponding preteam we have that
\{1,4\}
is connected in
{\bf K}^{l(T,a)}={\bf K}^{\{1,4\}}
but is not connected in
{\bf K}^{l(T)}={\bf K}^{\{1,2,3,4\}}
.

Example 2.5

(The clan of trees of associahedra and cyclohedra)
.

Recall from Section
1.3
the definition of cyclohedra.
Notice that, for each connected subset
\emptyset\subsetneq H\subsetneq O^{X}
, we have that
({\bf O}^{X})_{H}={\bf K}^{H}
, where
H
is endowed with the total order induced from
X
. Our universe will be formed by all associahedra and cyclohedra. We shall describe preteams of two different forms: one in terms of planar rooted trees, for which the coordinating hypergraph is an associahedron, and the other one in terms of planar non-rooted trees, for which the coordinating hypergraph is a cyclohedron.

(A)

Let, like in Example
2.4
,
T
be a rooted planar tree with set of nodes
A
and with the set of leaves
l(T)
, ordered by planarity. In addition, denote by
l(T,a)
the set of leaves of
T
attached to
a\in A
. Our preteams will have the form

(\{{\bf H}^{l(T,a)}\,|\,a\in A\},{\bf K}^{l(T)}),

where each
{\bf H}^{l(T,a)}
is either
{\bf K}^{l(T,a)}
, with
{l(T,a)}
endowed with the total order induced by planarity, or
{\bf O}^{l(T,a)}
, in which case
l(T,a)
is cyclically ordered by extending in the obvious way the total order given by planarity.

(B)

In order to describe the preteams whose coordinating hypergraph is a cyclohedron, we start with an unrooted planar tree
U
with the set of nodes
A
and the set of leaves
l(A)
, cyclically ordered by planarity. The set
l(U,a)
of leaves of
U
attached to
a\in A
comes equipped with the induced cyclic order. The corresponding preteams now have the form

(\{{\bf O}^{l(U,a)}\,|\,a\in A\},{\bf O}^{l(T)}).

In order to show that preteams of both forms are indeed anti-strict teams, we use exactly the same argument as in Example
2.4
. We define the clan of trees of associahedra and cyclohedra as the set of all these teams. Our goal now is to show that both forms of teams are closed under restrictions to connected subsets.

For teams of form (A), as in Example
2.4
, the choice of a connected subset
\emptyset\neq K\subseteq K^{l(T)}
determines the subtree
T_{K}
of
T
such that
l(T_{K})=K
. Let us define

\tau_{T_{K}}:=(\{{\bf H}^{l(T_{K},a)}\,|\,a\in\mbox{nodes}(T_{K})\},{\bf K}),

where

{\bf H}^{l(T_{K},a)}=\begin{cases}{\bf O}^{l(T_{K},a)},&\mbox{if }{\bf H}^{l(T,a)}={\bf O}^{l(T,a)}\mbox{ and }l(T_{K},a)=l(T,a)\\
{\bf K}^{l(T_{K},a)},&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This definition is motivated as follows. If
{\bf H}^{l(T,a)}={\bf O}^{l(T,a)}
and
l(T_{K},a)\subsetneq l(T,a)
, then
{\bf O}^{l(T,a)}\lceil_{l(T_{K},a)}={\bf K}^{l(T_{K},a)}
. Then it is easy to verify that
\tau_{T_{K}}
is precisely
\tau_{K}
, and hence,
\tau_{K}\in\Xi
.

For teams of form (B), the choice of a connected subset
\emptyset\neq K=\{k_{1}<\dots<k_{p}\}\subseteq O^{l(T)}
determines the subtree
U_{K}
of
U
such that
l(U_{K})=K
. Let
T_{K}
be the rooted tree obtained from
U_{K}
by rooting it at the node
a\in A
for which
k_{1}\in l(U_{K},a)
, in such a way that the planar order of the leaves of
T_{K}
reads precisely
k_{1}<\cdots<k_{p}
. We define
\tau_{T_{K}}
in exactly the same way as above and conclude by the same argument.

We conclude by noting that this example is not strict either since it contains the clan of Example
2.4
.

We shall give more examples of anti-strict clans, as well as two examples of strict but not anti-strict clans, in Section
3.3
.

3
Polydendriform shuffle product

In Section
3.1
, we define our shuffle product for anti-strict clans and provide examples. Our definition uses a fixed real parameter
q
. In Section
3.2
, we prove that, by specialising to
q=-1
, our product becomes associative.
In Section
3.3
, we show that the present shuffle product coincides with the one defined in
[
4
]
for clans that are both strict and anti-strict.

3.1
Definition and examples

Let
\tau:=(\{{\bf H}_{a}\mid a\in A\},{\bf H})
be a preteam. Recall from
[
4
]
that a
delegation of support

\tau
is a family of constructs
\delta=\{C_{a}:{\bf H}_{a}\,|\,a\in A\}
, one for each participating hypergraph. We write
\delta=(\{C_{a}:{\bf H}_{a}\,|\,a\in A\},{\bf H})
to take note of the coordinating hypergraph
{\bf H}
as well.
We first define, for each connected subset
\emptyset\neq K\subseteq H
, the restriction of
\delta
to
K
as the delegation

\delta_{\lceil_{K}}\,:=\,(\{{C_{a}}_{\lceil_{H_{a}\cap K}}\,|\,a\in A,\,H_{a}\cap K\neq\emptyset\},{\bf K})

of
\tau_{K}
. Suppose that

\mbox{root}(C_{a})=X_{a}
, for all
a\in A
. For a non-empty subset
\emptyset\neq B\subseteq A
, we set
X_{B}:=\bigcup_{b\in B}X_{b}
and, supposing that
{\bf H},X_{B}\leadsto{\bf H}_{1},\dots,{\bf H}_{n}
, we define recursively

\ast_{B}(\delta)=X_{B}(\ast(\delta_{\lceil_{H_{1}}}),\ldots,\ast(\delta_{\lceil_{H_{n}}})).

Finally, the
shuffle product
(or product) of
\delta
is defined by

\ast(\delta)=\sum_{\emptyset\neq B\subseteq A}q^{{|B|}-1}\ast_{B}(\delta),

(3)

where
q
is a fixed real number. Note that
\ast(\delta)
is a linear combination of constructs, living in the vector space spanned by the constructs of
{\bf H}
. We shall write
\ast^{\tau}(\delta)
instead of
\ast(\delta)
whenever we wish to record explicitly the underlying support team

\tau
.

Forgetting about the coefficients (or setting
q=1
), this definition has the following algorithmic (and ludic) reading: a summand of
\ast(\delta)
is obtained by choosing a subset
B
of the set
A
of “trees of sets of cards”, and by picking simultaneously the set of cards at the root (or “top”) of each chosen tree, and then continuing this game recursively. Note that if at each stage one chooses
B
to be a singleton, and if each
C_{a}
is a construction, then we have really the “feeling” of shuffling trees of cards in a sense closer to the one familiar to card game players.

In the following two examples, we illustrate the shuffle product on the clans of Example
2.4
and Example
2.5
, respectively.

.

Let us take
{\bf H}={\bf K}^{\{1<2<1^{\prime}<2^{\prime}<3\}}

3.2
Polydendriform structure

We first prove a useful property of commutation of the shuffle product (
3
) with restriction. With this goal, we extend the notion of restriction from constructs to linear constructs in the obvious way. In addition, from now on, we suppose

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\delta=(\{C_{a}:{\bf H}_{a}\,|\,a\in A\},{\bf H})
be a delegation and let
\emptyset\neq K\subseteq H
be a connected subset of
H
. For a subset

, set
B_{1}:=\{b\in B\mid X_{b}\cap K\neq\emptyset\}
and

B_{2}:=\{b\in B\mid X_{b}\cap K=\emptyset\}
. Then the following properties hold.

(P
1
)

If

, then
{(\ast_{B}(\delta))}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}={(\ast_{B_{1}}(\delta))}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}
.

(P
2
)

If
B_{1}=\emptyset
, then
{(\ast_{B}(\delta))}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}=\ast({\delta}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}})
.

(P)

The shuffle product is compatible with restriction, i.e.,
{(\ast(\delta))}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}=\ast({\delta}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}})
.

Proof.

We shall prove the three properties together, by induction on the size of
{\bf H}
.

We prove (P
1
) first. We set
X_{B}=\bigcup_{b\in B}X_{b}
and
X_{B_{1}}=\bigcup_{b\in B_{1}}X_{b}
, inducing

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\bf H},X_{B}&\leadsto&{\bf H}_{1},\ldots,{\bf H}_{n}\\
{\bf H},X_{B_{1}}&\leadsto&{\bf H}^{1}_{1},\ldots,{\bf H}^{1}_{m}\\
{\bf H}_{K},X_{B}\cap K&\leadsto&{\bf H}_{1}^{K},\ldots,{\bf H}_{p}^{K},\end{array}

as well as (a unique)
\varphi:\{1,\ldots,p\}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,n\}
, such that
H_{k}^{K}\subseteq H_{\varphi(k)}
for all
1\leq k\leq p
. We observe that, by construction,
X_{B}\cap K=X_{B_{1}}\cap K
. Therefore, the third decomposition also induces (a unique)
\psi:\{1,\ldots,p\}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,m\}
, such that
H_{k}^{K}\subseteq H^{1}_{\psi(k)}
for all
1\leq k\leq p
. The left-hand side of (
P_{1}
) unfolds as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{(\ast_{B}(\delta))}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}&=&(X_{B}(\ast(\delta\lceil_{H_{1}}),\dots,\ast(\delta\lceil_{H_{n}})))\lceil_{K}\\[2.84544pt]
&=&(X_{B}\cap K)(\ast(\delta\lceil_{H_{\varphi(1)}})\lceil_{H^{K}_{1}},\dots,\ast(\delta\lceil_{H_{\varphi(p)}})\lceil_{H^{K}_{p}})\\[2.84544pt]
&=&(X_{B}\cap K)(\ast((\delta\lceil_{H_{\varphi(1)}})\lceil_{H^{K}_{1}}),\dots,\ast((\delta\lceil_{H_{\varphi(p)}})\lceil_{H^{K}_{p}}))\\[2.84544pt]
&=&(X_{B}\cap K)(\ast(\delta\lceil_{{H^{K}_{1}}}),\dots,\ast(\delta\lceil_{{H^{K}_{p}}})),\end{array}

where we have successively used the definition of shuffle product, the definition of restriction, the induction hypothesis, and Remark
1.4
. On the other hand, the right-hand side is computed in an analogous way, replacing
B
by
B_{1}
, and
\varphi
by
\psi
. Since
X_{B}\cap K=X_{B_{1}}\cap K
, we thus obtain

{(\ast_{B}(\delta))}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}=(X_{B_{1}}\cap K)(\ast(\delta\lceil_{{H^{K}_{1}}}),\dots,\ast(\delta\lceil_{{H^{K}_{p}}}))={(\ast_{B_{1}}(\delta))}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}.

For the proof of the property (P
2
) , we consider only the decomposition
{\bf H},X_{B}\leadsto{\bf H}_{1},\ldots,{\bf H}_{n}
. We notice that the assumption
B_{1}=\emptyset
amounts to
X_{b}\cap K=\emptyset
, which entails that there exists a unique
1\leq i\leq n
such that
K\subseteq H_{i}
. The left-hand side now calculates as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{(\ast_{B}(\delta))}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}&=&(X_{B}(\ast(\delta\lceil_{H_{1}}),\dots,\ast(\delta\lceil_{H_{n}})))\lceil_{K}\\[2.84544pt]
&=&(\ast(\delta\lceil_{H_{i}}))\lceil_{K}\\[2.84544pt]
&=&\ast((\delta\lceil_{H_{i}})\lceil_{K})\\[2.84544pt]
&=&\ast(\delta\lceil_{K}),\end{array}

where we followed exactly the same steps as above.

Finally,
using the properties (P
1
) and (P
2
), we calculate

\begin{array}[]{l}{(\ast(\delta))}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}\\
=\Sigma_{\emptyset\neq B\subseteq A}q^{|B|-1}{(\ast_{B}(\delta))}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}\\
=\Sigma_{\emptyset\neq B_{1}\subseteq A_{1},B_{2}\subseteq A_{2}}q^{|B_{1}|+|B_{2}|-1}{(\ast_{B}(\delta))}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}+\Sigma_{\emptyset\neq B_{2}\subseteq A_{2}}q^{|B_{2}|-1}{(\ast_{B}(\delta))}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}\\
=\Sigma_{\emptyset\neq B_{1}\subseteq A_{1}}q^{|B_{1}|-1}(\Sigma_{B_{2}\subseteq A_{2}}q^{|B_{2}|}){(\ast_{B_{1}}(\delta))}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}+(\Sigma_{\emptyset\neq B_{2}\subseteq A_{2}}q^{|B_{2}|-1})\ast({\delta}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}).\end{array}

Now we observe (using
q=-1
) that

\begin{array}[]{l}\Sigma_{B_{2}\subseteq A_{2}}q^{|B_{2}|}=(1+q)^{|A_{2}|}=0\\
\Sigma_{\emptyset\neq B_{2}\subseteq A_{2}}q^{|B_{2}|-1}=\frac{(1+q)^{|A_{2}|}-1}{q}=1,\end{array}

and we conclude that
{(\ast(\delta))}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}=\Sigma_{\emptyset\neq B_{1}\subseteq A_{1}}0\>{(\ast_{B_{1}}(\delta))}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}+\ast({\delta}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}})=\ast({\delta}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}})
.
∎

3.3
Compatibility with the strict setting

Our next goal is to show that, for clans that are both strict and anti-strict, the shuffle product defined in Section

coincides with the one defined in

4
]
.

The following technical lemma will serve us to connect the two settings.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\tau=(\{{\bf H}_{a}\,|\,a\in A\},{\bf H})
be an anti-strict team. Define, for each decomposition (
1
),

\tilde{A}:=(A\backslash B)\cup\{(b,i)\mid b\in B,1\leq i\leq n_{b}\}
. Then,
for each
\tilde{a}\in\tilde{A}
and each

such

, the hypergraph
({\bf H}_{\tilde{a}})_{H_{i}}
is connected.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{a}\in\tilde{A}
and

be such that
H_{\tilde{a}}\cap H_{i}\neq\emptyset
.

If
\tilde{a}\in A\backslash B
, then, by instantiating Definition
2.1
for
K:=H_{i}
, we have that
H_{\tilde{a}}\cap H_{i}
is connected in
{\bf H}_{\tilde{a}}
, hence the conclusion.

If
\tilde{a}=(b,j)
for some

and
1\leq j\leq n_{b}
, then we a fortiori have that

. By instantiating again Definition
2.1
for

, we get that
H_{b}\cap H_{i}
is connected in
{\bf H}_{b}
. By construction, we have that
H_{i}\cap X_{b}=\emptyset
, which implies that
H_{b}\cap H_{i}
is contained in a unique

, for some

. By the assumption that

, we conclude that

. Therefore,
H_{(b,j)}\cap H_{i}=H_{b}\cap H_{i}
, and hence the conclusion.

We now recall from
[
4
]
the definition of strict clan and of the associated shuffle product. Let
\tau=(\{{\bf H}_{a}\,|\,a\in A\},{\bf H})
be a strict team, i.e., a preteam satisfying the following property:

(S)

for every

and every non-empty connected subset

, we have that

is also connected in
{\bf H}
.

An equivalent definition is that, for each subset
\emptyset\neq B\subseteq A
, we have,
in the notation of Lemma

, that for all
\tilde{a}\in\tilde{A}
,
H_{\tilde{a}}
is included in some

, thus inducing preteams

\tau_{i}^{B}=(\{{\bf H}_{\tilde{a}}\,|\,\tilde{a}\in\tilde{A}\>\mathrm{and}\>H_{\tilde{a}}\subseteq H_{i}^{B}\},{\bf H}_{i}^{B})
,
for all

.
A strict clan is a set
\Xi
of strict teams which is closed under decomposition, i.e., if
\tau\in\Xi
, then all induced preteams
\tau_{i}^{B}

We shall now reformulate the construction of Ronco in the language of preteams. In our setting, Ronco’s condition (R1) translates as the following requirement on preteams. We shall say that a preteam

So it seems that the approaches of Chapoton and Forcey-Springfields on one hand and ours on the other hand have different “virtues”. In our case, we try to have a general framework that we can instantiate “individually” to various families of polytopes, but we know little about how they relate. In their case, most of their new structures are obtained by projecting a known one from one family to another family, which in fact turns the projection into an algebra morphism.

Appendix: A non-inductive definition of restriction

We give an alternative, non-inductive characterisation of the restriction of constructs. This characterisation is not used elsewhere in the paper, but it may be of independent interest. We first introduce some preliminary notations.

Given
{\bf H}
, a construct
S:{\bf H}
and
a,b\in H
, there are unique nodes
X^{S}_{a}
and
X^{S}_{b}
of
S
such that
a\in X^{S}_{a}
and
b\in X^{S}_{b}
, respectively. We write

•

P_{<}(a,b,S)
if
X^{S}_{a}
is strictly below
X^{S}_{b}
in
S
,

•

P_{=}(a,b,S)
if
X^{S}_{a}=X^{S}_{b}
,

•

P_{<^{\#}}(a,b,S)
if
X^{S}_{a}
is strictly below
X^{S}_{b}
, or if
X^{S}_{a}
and
X^{S}_{b}
are incomparable in
S
, meaning that neither node lies above the other in
S
,

•

P_{=^{\#}}(a,b,S)
if
X^{S}_{a}=X^{S}_{b}
, or if
X^{S}_{a}
and
X^{S}_{b}
are incomparable in
S
.

We note that if
T
is a subtree of
S
and if
X^{S}_{a}
and
X^{S}_{b}
are nodes of
T
, then
P_{\ast}(a,b,T)
holds if and only if

P_{\ast}(a,b,S)
holds, for any
\ast\in\{<,=,<^{\#},=^{\#}\}
.

Proposition 4.5
.

For a connected hypergraph
{\bf H}
, a construct
U:{\bf H}
, a connected subset
K
of
{\bf H}
and a construct
S:{\bf K}
(with
{\bf K}:={\bf H}_{K}
),
we have that
S={U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}
if and only the following properties hold for all
a,b\in K
:

\begin{array}[]{lll}(1)\quad P_{<}(a,b,S)\Rightarrow P_{<}(a,b,U)&&(2)\quad P_{<}(a,b,U)\Rightarrow P_{<^{\#}}(a,b,S)\\
(3)\quad P_{=}(a,b,S)\Rightarrow P_{=}(a,b,U)&&(4)\quad P_{=}(a,b,U)\Rightarrow P_{=^{\#}}(a,b,S).\end{array}

Proof.

We first prove that
{U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}
satisfies the properties (1)–(4), for all
a,b\in K
, by induction on
U
. If
U=H
, then
{U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}=K
, and, hence, the implications (1) and (3) are vacuously true (since the antecendants are false), while (2) and (4) hold trivially given the single-node form of
{U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}
and
U
. Suppose now that
{\bf H},X\leadsto{\bf H}_{1},\dots,{\bf H}_{n}
and that
U=X(U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
, where
U_{j}:{\bf H}_{j}
, for each
1\leq j\leq n
. If
X\cap K=\emptyset
, then
{U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}={(U_{j})}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}
for some
1\leq j\leq n
and the claim holds by induction on
U_{j}
(relatively to
K
). It remains to prove the claim for the case

X\cap K\neq\emptyset
. In this case, supposing that
{\bf K},(X\cap K)\leadsto K_{1},\dots,K_{p}
, the restriction has the form

{U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}=(X\cap K)((U_{\psi(1)})_{\lceil_{K_{1}}},\dots,(U_{\psi(p)})_{\lceil_{K_{p}}})

(
\dagger
)

with
\psi
being the appropriate index correspondence.

We first show (1). Supposing that
P_{<}(a,b,{U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}})
, we consider the following two possibilities.

•

If
a\in X\cap K=\text{root}({U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}})
, then
a\in X=\text{root}(U)
. By the assumption,
b
appears in a node of
{U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}
which is strictly above
\text{root}({U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}})
. So, there exists an index
1\leq i\leq p
, such that
b
appears in a node of
(U_{\psi(i)})_{\lceil_{K_{i}}}
, meaning that
b\in K_{i}
. But
{H}_{\psi(i)}\cap X=\emptyset
, implying that
{K}_{i}\cap X=\emptyset
, and hence
b\not\in X=\text{root}(U)
. Therefore,
P_{<}(a,b,U)
holds.

•

Otherwise, there exists an index
1\leq i\leq p
, such that
a,b\in K_{i}
. In this case, we conclude by applying induction to

U_{\psi(i)}
(with respect to the connected subset
K_{i}
of
{\bf H}_{\psi(i)}
).

In order to show (2), suppose that
a,b\in K
are such that
P_{<}(a,b,U)
holds. Once again, we distinguish two cases.

•

If
a\in X=\text{root}(U)
, then
a\in X\cap K=\text{root}({U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}})
. By the assumption,
b
appears in a node of
U
which is strictly above
\text{root}(U)
, so
b\not\in X
, and concequently
b\notin\text{root}({U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}})
, meaning that
P_{<}(a,b,{U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}})
holds.

•

Otherwise, there exists an index
1\leq j\leq n
such that
a,b\in H_{j}
. In this case, there are two possibilities:

–

there exist
i_{1}\neq i_{2}\in(\psi)^{-1}(j)
such that
a\in K_{i_{1}}
and
b\in K_{i_{2}}
; in this case,
a
and
b
lie in incomparable nodes of

{U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}
;

–

there exists
i\in(\psi)^{-1}(j)
such
a,b\in K_{i}
; in this case we conclude by induction to
U_{j}
(with respect to
K_{i}
).

The proofs of (3) and (4) are similar.

In the other direction, we show that if the properties (1)–(4) hold for a construct
S:{\bf K}
, then
S={U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}
.
We proceed again by induction on
U
.
U=H
, then
{U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}=K
. Since
S
satisfies (4) and
P_{=}(a,b,U)
holds for all
a,b\in K
,
P_{=^{\#}}(a,b,S)
must hold for all
a,b\in K
. Suppose that for some
a,b\in K
,
X^{S}_{a}
and
X^{S}_{b}
are incomparable in
S
, and let
Y
be the first common ancestor of
X^{S}_{a}
and
X^{S}_{b}
in
S
. For each
y\in Y
,
P_{<}(y,a,S)
holds, so, since
S
satisfies (1),
P_{<}(y,a,U)
must hold as well. This is in contradiction with the single-node form of
U
, so we conclude that for each
a,b\in K
,
X^{S}_{a}=X^{S}_{b}
, i.e., that
S
is the single-node construct
K={U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}
. Suppose now that
{\bf H},X\leadsto{\bf H}_{1},\dots,{\bf H}_{n}
and that
U=X(U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
, where
U_{j}:{\bf H}_{j}
, for each
1\leq j\leq n
. As in the proof of (1), if
X\cap K=\emptyset
, then
{U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}={(U_{j})}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}}
for some

1\leq j\leq n
and we can apply induction to
U_{j}
(with respect to
K
and
S
). If
X\cap K\neq\emptyset
, then
\text{root}({U}_{{}^{\lceil}{K}})=X\cap K
. Suppose that
\text{root}(S)=Y
. We show that
X\cap K=Y
. Suppose that there exists an
a\in K
, such that
a\in Y

a\not\in X\cap K
, i.e.,

a\not\in X
, and let
b\in X\cap K
. Then
P_{<}(b,a,U)
, and hence
P_{<^{\#}}(b,a,S)
by our assumption, but this is impossible since
b\in\text{root}(S)
.
Suppose now that
a

a\in X\cap K

a\not\in Y

b\in Y

P_{<}(b,a,S)

P_{<}(b,a,U)
, contradicting the fact that

. Therefore

.
Using the notation of (

†

we can then apply induction
