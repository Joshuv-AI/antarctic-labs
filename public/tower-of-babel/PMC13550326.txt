Transcriptomic identification of key genes influencing m7G modification in thyroid carcinoma and experimental validation
Authors: Bo Li, Liying Zhu, Jiewu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundN7-methylguanosine (m7G) modification plays a critical role in RNA metabolism and is increasingly recognized for its implications in cancer biology. It can influence RNA stability, translation efficiency, and gene expression regulation. However, the specific role of m7G modification and its downstream genes in thyroid carcinoma (THCA) is not well understood. To comprehensively explore the impact of m7G methylation modification and the m7G-related gene ZNF831 on THCA, this study aims to identify key genes influencing m7G modification in THCA, with a particular focus on clarifying the role of ZNF831. This study is expected to further elucidate the pathological mechanisms of THCA and fill the current research gap in this field.MethodsWeighted gene co-expression network (WGCNA) analysis was used to evaluate the expression of m7G-related genes in the THCA expression data from the GEO (Gene Expression Omnibus) datasets. Machine learning algorithms, including the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), gradient boosting decision tree (XGBoost), and random forest (RF), were used to identify the feature genes, including GPSM3 and ZNF831, in the TCGA-THCA dataset. Immunohistochemistry was used to identify the expression difference of ZNF831 in 3 THCA tissues and 3 normal tissues. Finally, the changes of proliferation and migration of THCA cells after overexpression of ZNF831 were investigated.ResultsThis study investigated m7G-related genes in THCA, focusing on ZNF831 as a key tumor suppressor. Differential expression analysis revealed significant dysregulation of m7G-related genes in THCA. Functional and bioinformatics analyses, including gene set enrichment analysis and protein-protein interaction network construction, identified ZNF831 as a candidate gene. Experimental validation demonstrated that ZNF831 overexpression significantly reduced the proliferation and migration of THCA cells. Additionally, tumor microenvironment analysis showed a positive correlation between ZNF831 expression and immune cell infiltration, indicating its potential role in enhancing anti-tumor immunity.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the importance of m7G modifications and m7G-related gene ZNF831 in THCA pathogenesis, highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets. Further research is needed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms and explore clinical applications of these findings.Keywords: Thyroid carcinoma, M7G, ZNF831, WGCNA, Bioinformatics

Introduction

Background

Thyroid carcinoma (THCA) is one of the most common malignancies of the endocrine system, with an increasing incidence rate worldwide [1]. Since 1970, the incidence of thyroid cancer has increased significantly worldwide, while the mortality rate has stabilized [2, 3]. Approximately 90% of all thyroid cancers are differentiated, although the prognosis for patients with differentiated THCA s is generally favorable, the molecular mechanisms underlying the development and progression of THCA remain poorly understood [4]. More and more epigenetic studies on thyroid cancer have been conducted, and researchers have found that gene mutations and methylation changes play an important role in the occurrence and development of thyroid cancer [5]. Recent advancements in cancer genomics and transcriptomics have highlighted the importance of post-transcriptional modifications, such as N7-methylguanosine (m7G) methylation, in regulating gene expression and influencing tumorigenesis [6, 7].

Recent years, RNA modification has become a new research hotspot, and more than 170 RNA modifications have been identified [8, 9]. There are 12 prevalent modification classes in eukaryotic transcriptome regulation (m6A, m1A, m5C, m5U, m6Am, m7G, Ψ, I, Am, Cm, Gm, and Um), N7-methylguanosine (m7G) is a recently reported mRNA 5′cap modification that plays an important role in mRNA export, translation and splicing, occurs by adding a methyl group to the N7 atom of the RNA guanine [10, 11]. Recent studies have highlighted the critical role of m7G methylation in tumor progression: QKI was the first molecule found to have m7G methylation modification, which was found to be able to regulate the stability and translation process of target mRNA in response to chemotherapy drugs under stress conditions [12]. A research identifies METTL1 as a key player in regulating RNA modifications that contribute to immune evasion in colorectal cancer. It sheds light on the potential of targeting METTL1 or the PKM-CD155 axis as a therapeutic strategy to enhance anti-tumor immunity [13]. Another study identified M7G-associated lncrnas in thyroid cancer and their association with immune cell infiltration [14, 15].

Rationale and knowledge gap

Dysregulation of m7G methylation has been implicated in multiple types of cancers, yet its role in THCA is still underexplored. Therefore, the utilisation of bioinformatics to develop a better understanding of the key genes involved in regulating m7G modification in THCA could provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms of thyroid carcinogenesis and potential therapeutic targets.

Objective

In this study, a transcriptomic approach was utilized to systematically identify genes that influence m7G modification in THCA. By analyzing gene expression data from THCA tissues and paired normal tissues, candidate genes involved in the regulation of m7G modification were potentially identified. To validate the findings, experimental verification was performed using molecular techniques, providing further evidence of the involvement of these genes in THCA progression.

Methods

Data sources

For this study, the TCGA-THCA dataset was obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, which comprises 513 THCA tissues and 59 normal tissue samples, serving as our training set. Additionally, the GSE33630 dataset was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) database (platform: GPL570), encompassing 60 THCA samples and 45 normal thyroid samples, to be used as our validation set. The genes associated with m7G modification were also retrieved from previously published articles, including METTL1, WDR4, and RNMT [16, 17].

Differential expression analysis

To identify genes that are differentially expressed between the THCA group and the control group, differential expression analysis was performed on the training set using the “limma” package. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) samples diagnosed as thyroid cancer; (2) samples with matched clinical information and gene expression matrix; (3) samples with complete clinical information, including survival time, survival status, age, and gender. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) normal tissue samples; (2) samples without complete clinical information; (3) samples without expression values; (4) samples with biased expression values. The THCA group has 573 samples, including 513 THCA samples from TCGA-THCA dataset and 60 THCA samples from GSE33630 dataset, and the control group has 63 samples, including 59 normal samples from TCGA-THCA dataset and 45 normal samples from GSE33630 dataset. This analysis yielded a list of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with a threshold of |log2Fold Change (FC)| > 1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05. Additionally, volcano plots and heatmaps were constructed to visualize the DEGs. For the heatmaps, the top 10 up-regulated and down-regulated genes were selected based on their adjusted P-values for display.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

This study focused on genes related to m7G modification as the gene set. For all disease samples in the training set, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed to obtain scores, which were then used as traits to construct a co-expression network with the R package “WGCNA” to identify modules with the highest correlation with ssGSEA scores. After obtaining the ssGSEA scores, hierarchical clustering was first performed on all samples using Euclidean distance based on expression levels to identify and exclude outliers. Next, an appropriate soft-thresholding power from 1 to 20 was selected to determine the threshold of gene-to-gene correlations. To ensure that the constructed network satisfied the scale-free topology criterion, the soft-thresholding power was selected based on the relationship between the soft-thresholding power (β) and the scale-free topology fit index (R²), as well as the mean connectivity. A scale-free topology fit index of approximately R² = 0.85 was chosen as a balance between achieving scale-free network properties and maintaining sufficient network connectivity, which is a commonly accepted criterion in WGCNA analysis. The corresponding scale-free topology fit index and mean connectivity plots were generated to justify the selection of the soft-thresholding power. Subsequently, a scale-free network was constructed based on the selected soft-thresholding power, dividing genes into several modules, each colored distinctly. An adjacency matrix was constructed and transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM), followed by hierarchical clustering to identify gene modules. Then associations between the ssGSEA scores and the modules were built to calculate the correlation coefficient matrix between the module eigengenes and the scores, and selected modules with high absolute correlation with ssGSEA scores (|cor| > 0.3, P < 0.05) as key modules. To select more significant genes, module genes were filtered based on Gene Significance (GS) and Module Membership (MM), where GS represents the correlation between gene expression and phenotypic data, and MM represents the correlation between gene expression and module membership. thresholds of GS > 0.15 and MM > 0.9 were set to filter the genes within the modules.

Candidate gene selection and functional enrichment analysis

The DEGs obtained from the differential expression analysis were intersected with the m7G-related genes identified through WGCNA to compile a list of candidate genes. Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed on these candidate genes to identify the biological processes and signaling pathways in which they are involved. The GO analysis encompasses three aspects: biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC). Following this, the candidate genes were uploaded to the STRING database (http://www.string-db.org/) for the construction of a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, with an interaction score threshold set at > 0.4. The results from STRING were then imported into Cytoscape, and the cytoHubba tool was utilized to identify the top 10 genes as hub genes.

Machine learning

To identify feature genes within the TCGA-THCA dataset, both least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), Gradient Boosting Decision tree (XGBoost), and random forest (RF) algorithms were employed. Initially, LASSO regression analysis was conducted on the hub genes obtained from the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network using the “glmnet” package in R. The LASSO model with the minimum error was selected as the outcome of the LASSO analysis to derive the feature genes. Subsequently, the “randomForest” R package was utilized to implement the random forest algorithm for feature gene selection, ranking the genes based on the importance scores derived from the random forest importance (RIF). Then, the XGBoost algorithm was employed, implemented through the “xgboost” R package, to select feature genes based on their relative importance. The final set of feature genes was determined by intersecting the genes identified by LASSO, XGBoost, and RF methods.

Expression validation

To further validate the feature genes, the expression levels between disease samples and normal samples in both the training and validation sets were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and the results were visualized with violin plots. Genes that showed consistent expression trends across both sets and exhibited significant differences between the disease and control groups (P < 0.05) were designated as key genes.

Molecular regulatory network analysis

To analyze the interaction between key genes and transcription factors (TFs), the NetworkAnalyst platform (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/) was utilized to predict the transcription factors for the key genes using the ChEA database and the mRNA-TF interactions were visualized. Subsequently, GeneMANIA (http://genemania.org/) was employed to construct and analyze the interaction networks of each key gene’s transcription factors and their co-expressed genes. The candidate genes were queried to identify interacting genes and the types of interactions and related functions within the networks were discovered.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

To identify the signaling pathways in which key genes are involved, the correlation coefficients between key genes and all other genes in the training set was calculated. Genes were then ranked based on these correlation coefficients. Subsequently, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the R package “clusterProfiler”. The “c2.all.v2024.1.Hs.symbols” and “c5.all.v2024.1.Hs.symbols” gene sets were selected as references and enrichment results were obtained with a threshold of p-value < 0.05 and False Discovery Rate (FDR) < 0.05.

Tumor microenvironment (TME) analysis

To investigate the immune cell infiltration in THCA patients, immune infiltration analysis was conducted on the TCGA-THCA dataset within the training set using the single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm. Initially, the enrichment scores were calculated for 28 immune cell types across all samples in the dataset and a heatmap of these scores was plotted. The “corrplot” R package was then used to perform Pearson correlation analysis between immune cells and was plotted a correlation heatmap. Following this, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to determine if there were significant differences in the 28 immune cell types between the 513 disease and 59 control groups in the training set samples and visualized the results with boxplots. Initially, clustering was performed on all samples and no significant outliers were observed, indicating that no samples needed to be excluded. Cells that showed significant differences between the disease and control groups were termed differential immune cells. Subsequently, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between immune cells and key genes. Immune cells with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.3 and a p-value less than 0.05 were considered significantly correlated with key genes.

Overexpression of ZNF831 in THCA cell line TPC-1

TPC-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well one day prior to transfection. The ZNF831-overexpressing plasmid (OE-ZNF831 group), OriGene Technologies (Rockville, MD, USA; Cat. No. RC217154; pCMV6-Entry-Myc-DDK-ZNF831) or empty vector (Control group) was transfected into TPC-1 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, Karlsruhe, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2 µg of plasmid DNA was mixed with 5 µL of Lipofectamine 3000 and 100 µL of Opti-MEM medium in a sterile tube, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min to form transfection complexes. The complexes were then added dropwise to the cells, followed by gentle swirling of the plate. After 6 h of incubation, the transfection medium was replaced with fresh complete growth medium.

THCA tissue section immunohistochemistry

Three THCA tissues and corresponding adjacent normal tissues were collected from the hospital, with approval from the hospital’s ethics committee and written informed consent from all patients (Approval NO. YD2024-13). Paraffin-embedded THCA samples were cut into 0.4-micron-thick slides. To extract the antigen, the sample was soaked in 0.01 M citric acid buffer (pH 6.0) in for 10 min. The samples were first incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight exposure to ZNF831 antibodies (Invitrogen, Karlsruhe, Germany) at 4 °C. Subsequently, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, Karlsruhe, Germany) were applied and allowed to react with the samples for 1 h at room temperature. Images were captured via laser scanning fluorescence microscopy (IX73, Olympus) and analyzed with ImageJ software (Fiji).

CCK-8 assay

TPC-1 cells (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) of logarithmic growth stage were inoculated on 96-well plates with 5000 cells/well and incubated at 37℃ and 5% CO2 for 6 h. After the cells were attached to the wall, ZNF831-overexpressing plasmid (OE-ZNF831 group) or empty vector (Control group) was transfected into TPC-1 cells, and six technical replicates per group. The cells of each group were incubated for 24 h, and 10 µL CCK8 solution (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) was added to each well 2 h before the end of incubation. After incubation, OD450 was measured using a microplate reader.

Transwell migration experiment

After digestion, the cells of logarithmic growth stage were re-suspended in serum-free medium, the cell density was adjusted to 2 × 105 cells/mL, and cell suspension (100 µl) was added to the upper chamber. 600 µl medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum was added to the lower chamber, and the 2 µg of ZNF831-overexpressing plasmid (OE-ZNF831 group) or empty vector (Control group) was added to the upper and lower chambers, and the culture was continued for 24 h. The upper chamber was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with 0.2% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 10 min. Finally, the cells were observed under a microscope (Nikon, Melville, NY, USA), photographed, and the cell migration rate was calculated.

Western blotting

Protein extracts were obtained from cells using an NP40 lysis buffer (85124, Invitrogen, USA) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (23225, Invitrogen). Equal amounts of total protein (15 µg per lane) were loaded and separated by SDS–PAGE using a 7.5% resolving gel with a 5% stacking gel, which is suitable for the detection of high-molecular-weight proteins such as ZNF831 (~ 180 kDa). Electrophoresis was performed at 80 V for 30 min during stacking, followed by 120 V for protein separation. Proteins were then transferred onto PVDF membranes using a wet transfer system in standard transfer buffer under constant current at 300 mA for 200 min, with the transfer apparatus maintained under ice-bath cooling conditions to prevent overheating. After transfer, membranes were blocked with StartingBlock™ (PBS) Blocking Buffer (37578, Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ZNF831 (Cloud-Clone, Wuhan, China; PAV436Hu01, 1:1000). For internal loading control, membranes were incubated with anti-GAPDH antibody (Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig, 1:10,000). ZNF831 and GAPDH were detected on independent gels to ensure optimal resolution and linear signal detection for proteins with substantially different molecular weights. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, Karlsruhe, Germany; 1:5000) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence and captured with a Tanon chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China). Exposure times ranged from 5 to 30 s, and images were acquired within the linear detection range to ensure quantitative reliability. Band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All Bioinformatics analyses were executed in R language (version 4.4.1). The P-values and R-values for correlation analysis were calculated using pearson correlation coefficient. Differences between groups were analyzed by Wilcoxon rank-sum test P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Other data were analyzed and plotted using Graphpad Prism 9 (Version 9.4.0). All data are in means ± SD table. As shown, the statistical difference between groups was evaluated using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA analysis of variance, and a P value less than 0.05 was considered to have a significant difference.

Results

Identification of m7G-related genes with differential expression in THCA

Through differential expression analysis, a total of 10,360 DEGs between the THCA group and the control group were identified, with 3,047 up-regulated genes and 7,313 down-regulated genes (Fig. 1A and B). Subsequently, the focus was on the m7G-related gene set, and WGCNA was employed to identify co-expressed genes associated with m7G. Initially, clustering was performed on all samples, and no significant outliers were observed, indicating that no samples needed to be excluded (Fig. 1C). Then, R2 was set at 0.85, and a soft-thresholding power β of 14 was selected, corresponding to a scale-free topology with a connectivity close to 0 (Fig. 1D). Based on the selected soft-thresholding power, WGCNA analysis was conducted, leading to the identification of 8 co-expression modules (Fig. 1E). Modules with high absolute correlation with m7G-related gene ssGSEA scores (|cor| > 0.2, P < 0.05), specifically the “yellow” and “brown” modules, were designated as key modules (Fig. 1F). Finally, through Gene Significance (GS) and Module Membership (MM) filtering, 77 genes were retained and considered as m7G-related genes.

In the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional enrichment analysis, 3 enriched pathways (P < 0.05) were identified, such as T cell receptor signaling pathway, primary immunodeficiency, PD-L1 expression and PD-1 checkpoint pathway in cancer (Fig. 2C). By uploading 77 candidate genes to the STRING database, a PPI network was generated with edges representing interactions with a score greater than 0.4. Identification of key genes through expression validation

Subsequently, the expression patterns of GPSM3 and ZNF831 were examined in both the training and validation sets. Only ZNF831 showed consistent expression trends across both sets and exhibited significant differences between the disease and control groups (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < 0.05), with significantly downregulated expression levels in the tumor group (Fig. 4A and B). ZNF831 was designated as a key gene. Furthermore, immunohistochemical staining was performed on tumor tissues obtained from thyroid cancer patients and their matched adjacent non-tumor tissues. The results demonstrated that ZNF831 expression in tumor tissues was significantly lower than in adjacent tissues, consistent with the transcriptomic findings. In contrast, no significant difference in GPSM3 expression was observed between tumor and matched adjacent tissues (Fig. 4C).

Molecular regulatory network and GSEA of ZNF831

The NetworkAnalyst platform was utilized to predict 17 TFs that interact with ZNF831, with the ChEA database selected, and they were visualized as an mRNA-TF network. This network comprised 18 nodes and 17 edges (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, a Gene-Gene Interaction (GGI) network was constructed for ZNF831 using GeneMANIA, which included 20 interacting genes. Within the interacting network, Physical Interactions had the highest proportion (52.92%), followed by Co-expression (24.07%). The functions related to the transcription factors and their interacting genes were associated with pathways such as antigen receptor-mediated signaling pathway, regulation of antigen receptor-mediated signaling pathway, regulation of T cell receptor signaling pathway, and T cell receptor signaling pathway (Fig. 5B).

To investigate the immune cell infiltration in THCA patients, the ssGSEA was utilized to calculate the infiltration levels of 28 immune cell types in both THCA and normal samples within our training set (Fig. 6A). There was a moderate to high correlation observed among these immune cells (Fig. 6B), indicating their interplay within the immune microenvironment. Additionally, significant differences were identified in the abundance of 19 immune cell types between THCA and normal samples (Fig. 6C), including activated dendritic cell, CD56 bright natural killer cell, CD56 dim natural killer cell, central memory CD4 T cell, central memory CD8 T cell, effector memory CD4 T cell, gamma delta T cell, immature dendritic cell, Macrophage, mast cell, MDSC, monocyte, natural killer cell, natural killer T cell, neutrophil, regulatory T cell, T follicular helper cell, type 17 T helper cell, and type 2 T helper cell. These cells were referred to as differential immune cells. The correlation between immune cells and GPSM3 and ZNF831 were also calculated (Fig. 6D). The results indicated that most immune cells showed a strong positive correlation with both GPSM3 and ZNF831, with the activated B cell exhibiting the strongest correlation with ZNF831 (R = 0.71, P = 5.73E-07).

Overexpression of ZNF831 significantly reduced the proliferation and migration of THCA cells

To clarify the potential functions of ZNF831 in THCA, ZNF831 was overexpressed in THCA cell lines (Fig. 7A-B), the results of CCK-8 showed that the cell proliferation activity of OE-ZNF831 group was significantly decreased compared with control group (Fig. 7C). Transwell results showed that the mobility of the OE-ZNF831 group significantly decreased compared with the Control group (Fig. 7D). Those data suggest that ZNF831 may be a protective factor for THCA.

Discussion

Key findings

This study underscores the value of integrating bioinformatics with experimental approaches to unravel the complexities of cancer biology. By identifying ZNF831 as a m7G-related gene with tumor-suppressive functions in THCA, we provide a foundation for future studies aimed at understanding how RNA modification–associated transcriptional programs contribute to thyroid carcinogenesis. It should be noted that ZNF831 was identified based on transcriptomic association rather than as a direct regulator of m7G modification, and further mechanistic investigations are required to clarify its precise role within m7G-related regulatory networks. Expanding this research to include other cancer types and additional experimental models could further enhance its translational relevance.

Strengths and limitations

Although our study provides compelling evidence for the role of m7G-related genes, including ZNF831, in THCA, there are still several questions that need to be addressed. First, ZNF831 was defined as an m7G-related gene based on co-expression and enrichment analyses, and we did not directly demonstrate that ZNF831 functions as an m7G “writer,” “reader,” or “eraser,” nor that its mRNA is directly modified by m7G. The precise mechanisms by which ZNF831 interacts with m7G-associated transcriptional programs therefore warrant further investigation using approaches such as MeRIP-qPCR or ribosome-level analyses. Additionally, functional validation was performed primarily in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) cells, which represent the most common and clinically curable subtype of THCA. Whether ZNF831 exerts similar tumor-suppressive effects in more aggressive subtypes, such as anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, remains to be determined and represents an important direction for future studies. Finally, the therapeutic potential of targeting the m7G-related regulatory axis in conjunction with ZNF831 modulation requires validation in preclinical and clinical settings.

Comparison with similar research

Previous studies have revealed that DNA methylation modifications like m6A and m5C are linked to the development of THCA. As the most popular DNA methylation modification, m6A has been studied in thyroid cancer in many aspects, such as immune cells [18, 19], cancer-associated fibroblasts [20], cell differentiation [21], tumor metastasis [22], drug resistance [23], etc. The tRNA m5C methyltransferase NSUN2 is upregulated in undifferentiated thyroid cancer, maintains homeostasis and rapidly transports amino acids, significantly improving the efficiency of cancer-promoting translation programs [24]. A recent bioinformatics findings have revealed the correlation between m5C regulatory network and prognosis in papillary THCA [25]. m5C related methylation genes have also been identified in breast cancer brain metastases [26]. But m7G is still in the early stage of exploration, and more evidence is needed to prove its biological effects.

In this context, the present study expands current knowledge by systematically investigating m7G-related transcriptional features in THCA and identifying ZNF831 as a functionally relevant gene. Our findings are consistent with accumulating evidence that dysregulation of RNA modification–associated pathways contributes to tumorigenesis across cancer types [27, 28]. Rather than proposing ZNF831 as a direct m7G regulatory enzyme, our data support its role as a downstream or associated effector within m7G-related transcriptional networks.

Transcription factor is the main regulator of gene expression in eukaryotic cells. It is estimated that close to 6% of protein-coding genes in the human genome encode transcription factor [29, 30]. Notably, ZNF831 emerged as a pivotal tumor suppressor in our investigation. Its downregulation in THCA tissues underscores its potential as a biomarker for diagnosis and prognosis (Fig. 7A). Functional experiments further revealed that overexpression of ZNF831 significantly impaired the proliferative and migratory capabilities of THCA cells (Fig. 7C, D), suggesting its direct involvement in attenuating tumor progression. This aligns with the concept that ZNF831 serve dual roles in tumor suppression and modulation of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [31, 32] (Figs. 6 and 7).

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a critical role in shaping cancer progression and therapeutic responses. In this study, TME analysis highlighted a positive correlation between ZNF831 expression and immune cell infiltration, including cytotoxic T cells and natural killer cells (Fig. 6D). Together with the enrichment of immune-related pathways, such as PD-1/PD-L1 signaling in KEGG analysis, these observations raise the possibility that ZNF831 may influence immune-related transcriptional programs or chemokine signaling, thereby modulating anti-tumor immunity. However, these interpretations remain speculative and require further experimental validation.

In exploring the molecular underpinnings of ZNF831, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed its association with pathways integral to immune regulation and cellular adhesion. These pathways are often disrupted in THCA, contributing to metastasis and immune evasion. The molecular network analysis further emphasized ZNF831’s interactions with key signaling molecules, suggesting its role as a node in regulatory circuits that govern tumor behavior (Fig. 2).

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated m7G-related transcriptional features in thyroid carcinoma and identified ZNF831 as a gene associated with tumor progression and immune-related characteristics. By integrating transcriptomic analyses with functional validation and tumor microenvironment assessment, our work provides additional insight into the involvement of m7G-related gene networks in THCA. The observed inhibitory effects of ZNF831 on thyroid cancer cell proliferation and migration, together with its association with immune cell infiltration, suggest a potential role for ZNF831 in linking tumor-intrinsic regulation and the immune microenvironment. These findings extend current understanding of RNA modification–associated pathways in thyroid cancer and offer a basis for further mechanistic and translational investigations.
