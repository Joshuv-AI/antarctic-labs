Finite-Precision Quantum Mechanics
Abbas Edalat

Abstract
Standard quantum mechanics is an idealisation based on infinite-precision objects: point states, exact probabilities, and sharp measurements. Yet every real experiment has finite resolution, and for macroscopic systems we never have access to the microscopic state; we only measure a few macroscopic observables such as total energy, magnetisation, or particle number. Following Heisenberg’s call for a theory built only on observable quantities, and von Neumann’s insight that a complete description of a macroscopic system is neither possible nor necessary, we elevate the macroscopic state to a fundamental concept. We introduce Interval Quantum Mechanics (IQM) , in which the state of a quantum system is never a point but a quantum parcel or simply a parcel – a convex weak open set of density matrices. The physically fundamental subclass is that of experimental parcels , defined by finitely many open expectation intervals on macroscopic observables, which form a basis for the weak topology on the space of density matrices and are precisely the states certifiable by finite-precision measurements. Such a parcel is the exact mathematical representation of the set of all microscopic states compatible with the measured values of a finite set of macroscopic observables. We show that unitary evolution lifts to a deterministic flow on parcels, and that a finite-precision (fuzzy) measurement process is represented by a volume-contracting update that refines the initial parcel into a more constrained open set, strictly increasing the geometric information defined as the inverse of the Hilbert–Schmidt volume of the parcel. By introducing a second impossible set, we obtain a double-parcel whose information increases monotonically – resolving the entropy stagnation problem. The framework reformulates foundational puzzles without additional interpretational assumptions: wave-particle duality becomes a smooth trade-off; a macroscopic cat is described by a finite-precision parcel containing many microscopically distinct states, including decohered mixed states compatible with the same macroscopic observables; and the “spooky action at a distance” of entanglement disappears, replaced by a purely epistemic geometric update. All empirical predictions of standard quantum mechanics are recovered exactly in the infinite-precision limit, which is never physically attained.



1
Introduction

Standard quantum mechanics is built on the idealisation of infinite precision: point
-
like wave functions, exact real
-
number probabilities, and projective measurements that yield sharp outcomes. Yet every real experiment has finite resolution, and fundamental physics imposes hard bounds on the precision we can ever achieve. The Planck length
\ell_{P}\approx 1.616\times 10^{-35}
m is
conjectured by some approaches to quantum gravity to set a fundamental
limit on spatial resolution, though this remains an open question. In any case, every measurement apparatus has a finite energy range,
finite temporal resolution, and finite sample size. Consequently, the notion of a point state (a single density matrix) is an idealisation that can never be realised in the laboratory.

In his seminal 1925 paper, Heisenberg already called on physicists “to try to construct a theory of quantum mechanics in which only relationships among observable quantities occur.”
[
15
]

Von Neumann, in his foundational work
[
27
]
, was acutely aware that infinite precision is unattainable. He introduced the density matrix formalism precisely to describe ensembles and partial knowledge, and he emphasised that a complete description of a macroscopic system is neither possible nor necessary. The work of Goldstein, Lebowitz, Tumulka, and Zanghì
[
13
]
clarified and extended von Neumann’s original argument, introducing the concept of
normal typicality
. Reimann
[
22
]
extended von Neumann’s approach to
thermalisation, showing that states with sufficiently large effective
dimension exhibit only small deviations from their equilibrium values
for the overwhelming majority of times. The effective dimension measures
the extent to which the state is delocalised across energy eigenstates,
and Reimann’s bound controls temporal fluctuations in terms of this
quantity. This suggests that the correct object for describing a
macroscopic system at thermal equilibrium is not a single density matrix
but an entire set of states satisfying the same macroscopic energy
constraint — precisely what we call a quantum parcel. A detailed
IQM-native formulation of thermalisation, combining Reimann’s spectral
bound with the double-parcel framework developed below, is deferred to a
companion paper.

Following Heisenberg’s call and von Neumann’s insight, we elevate the quantum parcel—the open set of density matrices consistent with macroscopic resolution—to the role of the fundamental physical state. Formally, an experimental parcel is a convex weak open set defined by

O=\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}):a_{j}<\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H_{j})<b_{j},\;j=1,\dots,m\},

(1)

where
H_{1},\dots,H_{m}
are the measured Hermitian observables (typically macroscopic quantities such as total energy, magnetisation, or particle number) and the intervals
(a_{j},b_{j})
reflect the finite precision of the measurements. Such a parcel is not an approximation to a “true” point state – the point state is the idealisation. In the language of imprecise probability, the parcel is the epistemic state: it represents what we can actually know.

For macroscopic systems – a cat, a measuring device, a thermal bath – we never have access to the microscopic state. Instead, we measure only a few macroscopic observables. These observables cannot distinguish between the astronomically many microscopic configurations that give the same macroscopic values. This is not a practical limitation; it is a deep physical fact: macroscopic observables define equivalence classes of microscopic states. In IQM, each such equivalence class is precisely a quantum parcel.

The quantum parcel is the exact mathematical representation of the epistemic knowledge obtained from a finite set of coarse
-
grained measurements. It incorporates both the uncertainty due to finite precision and the irreducible indistinguishability of macroscopic states. Crucially, the parcel is not a probability distribution over point states; it is a geometric object – a convex set in the space of density matrices – whose volume quantifies the “size” of our ignorance. The Hilbert–Schmidt volume
\text{Vol}(O)
of a parcel
O
quantifies the “size” of our ignorance. The
geometric information
of a single parcel is the inverse of its volume; a smaller volume means more information. Under a fuzzy measurement the possible set shrinks, so the information increases.

To capture negative information – states that have been definitively ruled out – we introduce a
double-parcel

(O_{1},O_{2})
, where
O_{1}
is the set of possible states and
O_{2}
the set of impossible states (disjoint, and
O_{2}
may be empty). The geometric information of a double-parcel is the ratio
\operatorname{Vol}(O_{2})/\operatorname{Vol}(O_{1})
. As a measurement refines our knowledge,
O_{1}
contracts and
O_{2}
expands, so the ratio strictly increases – resolving the entropy stagnation problem. The partial order
(O_{1},O_{2})\sqsubseteq(O_{1}^{\prime},O_{2}^{\prime})
iff
O_{1}^{\prime}\subseteq O_{1}
and
O_{2}\subseteq O_{2}^{\prime}
directly captures information gain (smaller possible set, larger impossible set).

Related work

Geometric logic and domain theory.

The idea that the observable properties of a system are the open sets of an appropriate topology (point
-
free topology) is the cornerstone of geometric logic
[
18
]
. In this perspective, points are idealised limits of open sets, and the logical propositions are the open sets themselves
[
26
,
2
]
. Domain theory, as the study of partial orders of information, is closely aligned with geometric logic
[
23
,
24
,
1
]
. A concrete illustration of a domain is the collection of non-empty compact intervals of the real line ordered with reverse inclusion. In this domain, a real number is approximated by a nested sequence of rational intervals: the exact value
\pi=3.14159265\ldots
is never known; we only know it through intervals such as
3.14<\pi<3.15
, then
3.141<\pi<3.142
, and so on. In the same way, a quantum state
\rho
is never known exactly; it is known only through a nested sequence of experimental parcels defined by finitely many expectation intervals.

Epistemic interpretations.

Quantum Bayesianism (QBism)
[
12
,
11
]
treats the quantum state as a personal belief of an agent, updated via Bayes’ rule. While both QBism and IQM assign an epistemic role to the state, IQM is objective: the parcel is determined by the actual finite
-
precision data, not by an agent’s subjectivity. Moreover, IQM provides a geometric measure of information (
\mathcal{I}=\operatorname{Vol}(O_{2})/\operatorname{Vol}(O_{1})
) that increases monotonically under measurement, which has no analogue in QBism.

Operational probabilistic theories (GPTs).

GPTs
[
6
,
20
]
provide a general framework for probabilistic theories, but they typically assume exact states and measurements. IQM may be viewed as a specific generalized probabilistic theory in which the state space is convex and measurement updates act monotonically on open sets. Its central novelty is the treatment of finite-precision knowledge as a primitive physical feature, leading naturally to a geometric information measure and an alternative finite-precision account of the measurement problem.

Topos
-
theoretic approaches.

Butterfield and Isham
[
5
]
, Döring and Isham
[
9
]
, and Heunen et al.
[
17
]
developed a topos
-
theoretic approach that replaces Hilbert space with presheaves on contexts, providing a realist interpretation with intuitionistic logic, which is related to geometric logic. Their focus is on the logical structure of propositions, not on finite
-
precision measurements or macroscopic observables. Our parcels are concrete subsets of the state space, not logical propositions.

Partial orders on quantum states.

Coecke and Martin
[
7
]
defined a partial order on density matrices by spectral majorization — ordering states by their degree of mixing, with purer states higher in the order — and showed that the resulting structure forms a domain in the sense of domain theory; our partial order, by contrast, acts on sets of states (parcels) rather than individual states, and is driven by information gain from finite-precision measurements rather than by purity.

Interval
-
valued probabilities.

Interval
-
valued probabilities appear in classical imprecise probability theory
[
28
]
and have been considered in quantum contexts
[
14
]
. However, previous work treats intervals as arising from statistical ensembles or measurement imperfections, not as replacements for exact quantum states. In IQM, the interval is the fundamental object, not an approximation.

Resolving quantum paradoxes.

Standard interpretations address measurement differently: Copenhagen
[
4
,
16
]
invokes collapse; Many
-
Worlds
[
10
,
25
]
eliminates it through branching; QBism treats states as subjective beliefs. Our approach reformulates infinite
-
precision idealisation, making the appearance of collapse a result of information gain about an objectively uncertain state. The double-parcel
(O_{1},O_{2})
allows explicit tracking of excluded states, yielding a monotonic geometric information measure that offers a geometric resolution of the entropy stagnation problem within the IQM framework.

In summary, IQM is distinguished by:

(i)

Treating finite-precision macroscopic observables as primitive, leading naturally to
quantum parcels—convex weak open subsets of
\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{H})
that are themselves the
exact and complete physical state, not approximations to an underlying point state—with
standard quantum mechanics recovered in the infinite-precision limit.

(ii)

Introducing a double-parcel formalism that explicitly tracks excluded states, together with a geometric information measure that evolves monotonically under the measurement-update conditions of Theorem
5
.

(iii)

Providing unified finite-precision geometric reformulations of wave-particle duality, Schrödinger’s cat, and entanglement without introducing additional collapse postulates or hidden-variable structures.

(iv)

Offering computationally tractable parcel representations (hyper-rectangle parcels) for finite-precision quantum computation and interval-valued observable propagation.

Background and notation

We assume familiarity with the standard formalism of quantum mechanics: Hilbert spaces, density matrices, observables, and positive operator
-
valued measures (POVMs) – see e.g.
[
3
,
8
,
29
]
for comprehensive introductions.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable complex Hilbert space. Denote by
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
the space of trace
-
class operators and by
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
the space of bounded operators. The duality pairing is
\langle T,B\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(TB)
for
T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
,
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. The set of density matrices is

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})=\{\rho\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}):\rho\geq 0,\;\operatorname{Tr}\rho=1\},

which is a convex subset of the unit ball of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
.

The natural topology on
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
for our purposes is the
weak topology
\sigma(\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}),\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
,
defined as the coarsest topology on
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
making all
maps
\rho\mapsto\mathrm{Tr}(\rho B)
continuous for
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. It is generated by the expectation cylinders

\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}):a<\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H)<b\}

for
H\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, which are
precisely the building blocks of experimental parcels. Throughout this paper, “weak topology” always refers to the weak topology

\sigma(\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}),\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))

on the trace-class space
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
, not the weak
∗

topology on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

In finite dimensions all topologies on
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
coincide with
the Euclidean topology, and
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
is compact. In infinite
dimensions,
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
is closed in the weak topology but is

not
weak compact in general. The Banach–Alaoglu theorem gives weak*
compactness of the unit ball of the
dual
of a Banach space; however

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
plays the role of a predual here rather than a dual,
and no analogous compactness holds without additional assumptions. Compactness statements in this paper are therefore finite-dimensional
statements. In the infinite-dimensional setting, no compactness
assumption is used. Instead, Theorems
2

and
3
rely on explicit trace-norm concentration
and moment bounds to control high-energy tails and recover the finite-dimensional
approximation needed for the reduction results.

Since
\mathcal{H}
is separable,
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
is separable,
and by
[
21
, Section IV.2]
the weak topology on the unit
ball of a separable Banach space is metrizable, hence second countable.
Since
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
is contained in the unit ball of

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
, the weak topology on
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})

is second countable.

An experimental parcel is a finite intersection of expectation cylinders

\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}):a<\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H)<b\},\quad a,b\in\mathbb{R},\quad H=H^{*}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}).

Such sets are convex. Their closures, which are defined by the corresponding non-strict inequalities
together with the positive semidefinite constraint
\rho\geq 0
, are called

spectrahedra
. In finite dimensions the weak open sets are open in the Euclidean
topology and their closures are compact. In infinite dimensions their closures
need not be compact without additional compactness or tightness assumptions.

The Hilbert–Schmidt inner product
\langle A,B\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\operatorname{Tr}(A^{\dagger}B)
induces a Euclidean metric on the affine subspace
\{\rho\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}):\operatorname{Tr}\rho=1\}

\operatorname{Vol}(\cdot)
. Note that any finite set of linearly independent observables can be orthonormalised via Gram–Schmidt, yielding an orthonormal basis for the same subspace. For a finite set of orthonormal observables
\{H_{1},\dots,H_{m}\}
(
\operatorname{Tr}(H_{i}H_{j})=\delta_{ij}
), the map

\Phi:\rho\mapsto(\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H_{1}),\dots,\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H_{m}))

is an isometry from the affine span of the
H_{j}
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

When the observables
\{H_{1},\dots,H_{m}\}
are orthonormal with respect to the
Hilbert–Schmidt inner product, i.e.
\operatorname{Tr}(H_{i}H_{j})=\delta_{ij}
,
the coordinate map

\Phi:\rho\mapsto(\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H_{1}),\dots,\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H_{m}))

is an isometry from the affine span of the
H_{j}
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and the
Hilbert–Schmidt volume of an exerimental parcel defined by intervals
(a_{j},b_{j})

equals
\prod_{j}(b_{j}-a_{j})
(see Section
12
). For non-orthonormal observables this formula does
not hold in general and the volume must be computed via the Gram matrix of the

H_{j}
.

Extreme points

Let
C\subseteq\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
be a convex set. A point
\rho\in\overline{C}
(the weak closure of
C
) is called an
extreme point
of
C
if it cannot be written as a convex combination
\rho=t\sigma_{1}+(1-t)\sigma_{2}
with
0<t<1
and
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\overline{C}
,
\sigma_{1}\neq\sigma_{2}
.

For a weak compact convex set, every point can be expressed as a convex
combination of extreme points (Krein–Milman theorem), which holds in any
locally convex Hausdorff topological vector space and in particular under
the weak topology on
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
. In finite dimensions,
examples include
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
itself and the closure of any
experimental parcel, both of which are compact. In the case of a bounded polyhedron (such as the outer
approximations used in IQM), the extreme points coincide with its vertices.
Linear functionals continuous in the weak topology attain their infimum and
supremum over
C
at extreme points of
\overline{C}
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space of dimension
\geq 2
, and let
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
be the space of density matrices endowed with the weak topology. No non-empty weak open set
O\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
can consist exclusively of pure states.

3
Quantum Parcels

We now build a mathematical structure designed to represent states under finite
-
precision knowledge. The state space is the set
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
of density matrices equipped with the weak topology. The fundamental objects are open subsets of this space, called
parcels
, because they arise naturally from coarse
-
grained measurements: any finite set of interval constraints on expectation values defines an experimental parcel.

3.1
Single-parcels: possible states and information

Weak open subsets of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
need not be convex in general. However,
the expectation cylinders
\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}):a<\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H)<b\}

are convex, since
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H)
is linear in
\rho
, and their finite intersections
— the basic weak open parcels — are therefore convex weak open sets. These convex weak open
sets form a basis for the weak topology on
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
, so no generality is
lost by restricting attention to them. Convexity is moreover physically natural: if two
density matrices are both consistent with the measured data, so is any mixture of them.
We therefore define quantum parcels as convex weak open sets. This choice is preserved
under the two fundamental operations of the theory: unitary evolution preserves convexity
and openness by Theorem
4
, and fuzzy measurement preserves both by
Proposition
3
and Theorem
5
,
so the class of quantum parcels is closed under the dynamics of IQM.

Definition 1

(Single-parcel)
.

A
single-parcel
or simply parcel is a non-empty weak open convex subset

O\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
. A single-parcel is called

experimental
if it is a finite intersection of expectation
cylinders

O=\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})\mid a_{j}<\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H_{j})<b_{j},\;j=1,\ldots,m\},

(2)

where each
H_{j}
is a bounded Hermitian operator and
(a_{j},b_{j})
reflects
the finite precision of a direct measurement. Experimental parcels are
convex weak open sets and form a basis for the weak topology on

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
; they are precisely the parcels that can be
directly certified by a finite set of coarse-grained measurements. General
parcels arise naturally after dynamical operations such as unitary evolution
and fuzzy measurement.

The physical and operational necessity of defining a quantum state as an open parcel, rather than a point or a closed subset, is fundamentally rooted in the topological architecture of the state space under finite precision. This choice is rigorously justified by Proposition
1
, which establishes that no non-empty quantum parcel can consist exclusively of pure states.

The operational meaning of this topological fact is profound. In standard quantum mechanics, a pure state
\rho_{0}=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
represents an idealized system with infinite information, where certain observables possess exact, sharp values. However, because a quantum parcel is defined by a finite number of open macroscopic expectation intervals—representing the realistic resolution limits of a physical apparatus—any open neighborhood surrounding a pure state must necessarily leak into the interior of the state space, inevitably capturing a dense continuum of mixed states.

Consequently, it is physically impossible for an observer utilizing finite-precision measurements to isolate or trap a quantum system inside a pure state, or even within a region containing only pure states. Any operational interval drawn around an extreme point will instantly be populated by mixed states. This mathematically demonstrates that pure states are
operational singularities
—mathematical idealizations reachable only in the unphysical limit of infinite precision. In the physical reality of macroscopic observation, we are topologically confined to the open interior of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
, reinforcing that the true operational state of a quantum system is inherently an open parcel of mixed states.

Geometric information content.

The Hilbert–Schmidt volume
\mathrm{Vol}(O)
is well-defined and finite
for any experimental parcel and for any parcel obtained from one by unitary
evolution or fuzzy measurement, since all such parcels are contained in a
finite-dimensional affine subspace spanned by finitely many observables.
The information content of a single parcel is then defined as

I(O)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(O)},

(3)

which is finite and positive in all physically relevant cases.

A smaller volume (corresponding to more precise knowledge) yields a larger
information. In particular, if
O
is a hyper-rectangular parcel defined
by Hilbert–Schmidt orthonormal observables with interval lengths

\Delta_{j}
, then

\text{Vol}(O)=\prod_{j}\Delta_{j},\qquad I(O)=\left(\prod_{j}\Delta_{j}\right)^{-1}.

Partial order.

We say that the weak open set
O^{\prime}
is
more informative
than
O
, written
O\sqsubseteq O^{\prime}
, if
O^{\prime}\subseteq O
. This captures the idea that a smaller possible set represents less uncertainty.

3.2
Double-parcels: tracking the impossible

Our framework enables us to keep a record of what states are not possible. A single-parcel records only what is possible. In many situations – especially after a measurement – we also possess negative information: states that have been definitively ruled out. To capture this we introduce a
double-parcel

(O_{1},O_{2})
, where

O_{1},O_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})\text{ are weak open, convex, with }O_{1}\neq\emptyset\text{ and }O_{1}\cap O_{2}=\varnothing.

Here
O_{1}
is the set of
possible
states (consistent with all data), and
O_{2}
is the set of
impossible
states (definitely excluded). Disjointness is required because a state cannot be both possible and impossible.

Information for double-parcels.

The geometric information now takes into account both the shrinking of the possible set and the growth of the impossible set. We define

\mathcal{I}(O_{1},O_{2})=\frac{\operatorname{Vol}(O_{2})}{\operatorname{Vol}(O_{1})}.

As a measurement (under Theorem
5
) refines our knowledge,
O_{1}
contracts and
O_{2}
expands, so
\mathcal{I}
strictly increases. In the infinite
-
precision limit
O_{1}
shrinks to a point (zero volume) and
O_{2}
occupies almost the whole space, giving
\mathcal{I}\to\infty
.

The initialization of the impossible set
O_{2}
in a double-parcel
(O_{1},O_{2})
is not a
subjective mathematical choice, but an operational consequence of the measurement
apparatus. Suppose the finite number of measurements for specifying
O_{1}
includes a
measurement of a macroscopic observable
A
(e.g., energy or position) with
resolution
\varepsilon>0
. If the detector registers a value
v
, then

\rho\in O_{1}
in particular satisfies:

\mathrm{Tr}(\rho A)\in(v-\varepsilon,\,v+\varepsilon).

(4)

If
(c,d)
is an interval representing a macroscopically distinct reading
(such as a different spatial bin or a non-triggered state) such that

(c,d)\cap(v-\varepsilon,v+\varepsilon)=\emptyset
, then any parcel

O_{2}
defined by a finite number of measurements that includes, for

\rho\in O_{2}
, the constraint

\mathrm{Tr}(\rho A)\in(c,d)

(5)

satisfies
O_{1}\cap O_{2}=\emptyset
. Any
O_{1}
and
O_{2}
obeying these incompatible constraints are automatically
disjoint, i.e.
O_{1}\cap O_{2}=\emptyset
.

The parcel
O_{2}
defined by the constraint
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho A)\in(c,d)

is non-empty provided the interval
(c,d)
intersects the numerical range of
A

on
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
. This is guaranteed whenever
(c,d)
contains a
point in
[\inf\sigma(A),\sup\sigma(A)]
, where
\sigma(A)
denotes the spectrum
of
A
. In finite dimensions
\inf\sigma(A)
and
\sup\sigma(A)
are simply the
smallest and largest eigenvalues of
A
respectively. Since
(c,d)
represents a
macroscopically distinct detector bin disjoint from
(v-\varepsilon,v+\varepsilon)
,
this condition is satisfied whenever the observable
A
has sufficient spectral
range to distinguish the two bins, which is always the case in the operational
setting we consider. The initial geometric information

I=\mathrm{Vol}(O_{2})/\mathrm{Vol}(O_{1})
is therefore grounded in the
physical geometry of the measurement device.

Partial order.

The natural order on double-parcels is

(O_{1},O_{2})\sqsubseteq(O_{1}^{\prime},O_{2}^{\prime})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad O_{1}^{\prime}\subseteq O_{1}\text{ and }O_{2}\subseteq O_{2}^{\prime}.

That is, the new parcel is more informative when the possible set is smaller and the impossible set is larger.

Why two components are necessary.

A single-parcel cannot express that a state is known to be impossible – it can only grow larger (by forgetting) or smaller (by gaining knowledge). The double-parcel allows us to explicitly track negative information. This becomes essential when we study the update of a parcel under a measurement: the outcome not only refines the possible region but also moves some states into the impossible region. The double-parcel gives a monotonic information measure that aligns with intuition, in contrast to von Neumann entropy, which does not increase when a pure state is measured.

In the following sections we first develop the theory for single-parcels (unitary evolution, expectation intervals, measurement) and then extend it to double-parcels. The double-parcel formulation also leads to Theorem
5
, which gives two natural sufficient conditions (uniform positivity and separation), together with examples showing that neither condition can generally be omitted.

Hyper
-
rectangle parcels: a computational basis.

For practical computation we often specialize to
hyper
-
rectangle parcels
, defined by choosing orthonormal observables
\{H_{1},\dots,H_{m}\}
and intervals
(a_{j},b_{j})
for
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H_{j})
.
These are
outer polyhedral approximations
(since they ignore the positivity condition) that form a natural basis for the weak topology on
\mathcal{T}(\mathscr{H})
.
All results proved for general parcels apply to hyper
-
rectangle parcels, and their simple structure allows efficient interval
-
valued predictions.

4
Reduction to Standard Quantum Mechanics

Our interval-based quantum mechanics reduces to standard quantum mechanics in the limit of perfect knowledge. The precise meaning of this limit depends on whether
\mathcal{H}
is finite or infinite-dimensional, reflecting the different topological structures.

Any experimental parcel
O
as in Equation (
1
) has a parent convex weak open set
O_{\mathrm{outer}}
in
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
, which is an outer approximation to
O
:

O_{\mathrm{outer}}=\{\rho\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}):\operatorname{Tr}(\rho)=1,\rho=\rho^{\dagger},a_{j}<\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H_{j})<b_{j},\;1\leq j\leq m\},

with
H_{j}\in{\cal B}({\cal H})
.

4.1
Interval-valued physical quantities

In standard quantum mechanics, an observable
H
yields a definite expectation value
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H)
for a given state
\rho
. When the state is a quantum parcel (a weak open convex set), this single value generalises to an interval of possible values.

Definition 2

(Expectation interval)
.

Let
O\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
be a quantum parcel and
H
a
Hermitian operator. The expectation interval for
H
over
O
is

E_{O}(H)=\left(\inf_{\rho\in O}\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H),\,\sup_{\rho\in O}\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H)\right)\subseteq\mathbb{R}.

Since
O
is convex and open, and
\rho\mapsto\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H)
is
linear in
\rho
, the infimum and supremum are attained only on the boundary

\partial O
, which is disjoint from
O
. Hence neither bound is attained
inside
O
, and
E_{O}(H)
is always an open interval. Every value strictly
between the infimum and supremum is attained by some
\rho\in O
, by
convexity and the intermediate value theorem. The same reasoning applies to
probability intervals in Definition
5
and standard
deviation intervals in Definition
8
, which are
therefore also open intervals.

Convention for degenerate intervals.

Throughout this paper, when we write an interval
(\alpha,\beta)
with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha<\beta
, it denotes the open interval. If it happens that
\alpha=\beta
, the notation
(\alpha,\alpha)
is understood to represent the singleton
\{\alpha\}
. This convention applies to all expectation, probability, and standard deviation intervals defined below.

For computational purposes, if we have an experimental parcel, one may use an outer polyhedral approximation. Let
P_{\mathrm{outer}}=\operatorname{Conv}\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}
be a polyhedron that contains
P
(e.g., obtained by dropping the positivity constraint). Then a conservative estimate is

E_{P_{\mathrm{outer}}}(H)=\bigl(\min_{i}\operatorname{Tr}(v_{i}H),\;\max_{i}\operatorname{Tr}(v_{i}H)\bigr)\supseteq E_{P}(H).

Proposition 2

(Characterisation of expectation intervals)
.

Let
O
be a convex weak open set and let
H
be Hermitian. Set

\alpha=\inf_{\rho\in O}\Tr(\rho H),\qquad\beta=\sup_{\rho\in O}\Tr(\rho H).

Then

\{\Tr(\rho H):\rho\in O\}=(\alpha,\beta)

if
\alpha<\beta
, and is the singleton
\{\alpha\}
if

\alpha=\beta
. In particular, for every

\alpha<r<\beta
, there exists
\rho\in O
such that

\Tr(\rho H)=r
.

Theorem 1

(Reduction to point states: finite dimensions)
.

Let
\dim\mathcal{H}=N<\infty
and let
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
.
Fix an orthonormal basis
\{|\psi_{i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{N}
of
\mathcal{H}
and
construct the following complete basis of
N^{2}
Hermitian operators:

(i)

The
N
diagonal projectors:

|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|
for
1\leq i\leq N
.

(ii)

The
\frac{N(N-1)}{2}
symmetric real parts:

\frac{1}{2}(|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{k}|+|\psi_{k}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|)

for
1\leq i<k\leq N
.

(iii)

The
\frac{N(N-1)}{2}
antisymmetric imaginary parts:

\frac{i}{2}(|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{k}|-|\psi_{k}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|)

for
1\leq i<k\leq N
.

Denote this basis by
\{A_{m}\}_{m=1}^{N^{2}}
. Define the nested sequence of parcels

O_{n}(\rho_{0})=\Bigl\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}):|\operatorname{Tr}(\rho A_{m})-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}A_{m})|<2^{-n},\;m=1,\dots,N^{2}\Bigr\},

and the corresponding outer polyhedra (dropping the positivity constraint)

P_{n}(\rho_{0})=\Bigl\{\rho\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})_{sa}:\operatorname{Tr}(\rho)=1,\;|\operatorname{Tr}(\rho A_{m})-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}A_{m})|<2^{-n},\;m=1,\dots,N^{2}\Bigr\}.

Then
O_{n}(\rho_{0})\subseteq P_{n}(\rho_{0})
,
\rho_{0}\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})
for all
n
,
and

\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}O_{n}(\rho_{0})=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}P_{n}(\rho_{0})=\{\rho_{0}\}.

Moreover, for every bounded Hermitian operator
H
on
\mathcal{H}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}H),

and the same limits hold with
O_{n}(\rho_{0})
replaced by
P_{n}(\rho_{0})
. Moreover,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{diam}\!\bigl(O_{n}(\rho_{0})\bigr)=0,\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{diam}\!\bigl(P_{n}(\rho_{0})\bigr)=0,

where the diameter is taken with respect to the trace norm.

Theorem 2

(Reduction to point states: bounded observables in infinite dimensions)
.

Let
\dim\mathcal{H}=\infty
. Let
\{T_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a family that separates points of
\cal{T}(\mathscr{H})
(e.g., all finite
-
rank operators with rational matrix entries in a fixed orthonormal basis). Define, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the cylinder sets:

O_{n}(\rho_{0})=\bigl\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}):|\operatorname{Tr}(\rho T_{j})-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}T_{j})|<2^{-n},\;1\leq j\leq n\bigr\},

and their outer polygons
P_{n}\supseteq O_{n}
:

P_{n}(\rho_{0})=\Bigl\{\rho\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}):\rho=\rho^{\dagger},\operatorname{Tr}(\rho)=1,\;|\operatorname{Tr}(\rho T_{j})-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}T_{j})|<2^{-n},\;1\leq j\leq n\Bigr\}.

(Note that
\rho_{0}\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})\subseteq P_{n}(\rho_{0})
for all
n
,
and all convergence results for
P_{n}
transfer immediately to
O_{n}
.)
Then
\bigcap_{n}P_{n}(\rho_{0})=\bigcap_{n}O_{n}(\rho_{0})=\{\rho_{0}\}
.
For any bounded Hermitian operator
H
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{P_{n}(\rho_{0})}(H)=\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{O_{n}(\rho_{0})}(H)=\{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}H)\}.

Theorem 3

(Reduction for unbounded observables under a uniform moment bound)
.

Let
\{O_{n}(\rho_{0})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the nested family of outer polyhedra from Theorem
2
, and let
H\geq 0
be a self-adjoint (possibly unbounded) operator such that

\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}H)<\infty.

Assume moreover that there exists
\delta>0
such that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\;\sup_{\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H^{1+\delta})<\infty.

Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})}\left|\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H)-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}H)\right|=0.

Equivalently,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}H).

Remark 1
.

The additional moment assumption in Theorem
3

is a uniform integrability condition controlling the high-energy tails of the
states in the parcels. It is satisfied for many physically important
unbounded observables on natural classes of states, including the harmonic
oscillator Hamiltonian

H=\frac{1}{2}(P^{2}+X^{2})=a^{\dagger}a+\frac{1}{2},

the number operator
N=a^{\dagger}a
, and more general Schrödinger
operators of the form

H=-\Delta+V(x)

with confining potentials
V(x)
. For thermal states, coherent states,
Gaussian states, and more generally for families of states with uniformly
bounded energy moments, one typically has

\sup_{n}\sup_{\rho\in O_{n}}\Tr(\rho H^{1+\delta})<\infty.

By contrast, weak convergence alone does not control expectations of
unbounded observables. For example, let
He_{k}=\lambda_{k}e_{k}
with

\lambda_{k}\to\infty
, and define

\rho_{k}=\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\rho_{0}+\frac{1}{k}|e_{k}\rangle\langle e_{k}|.

Then
\rho_{k}\to\rho_{0}
in the weak topology, while

\Tr(\rho_{k}H)\to\infty

whenever
\lambda_{k}
grows sufficiently rapidly. Thus an additional
uniform moment condition is necessary in general for convergence of
expectation intervals of unbounded observables.

The above three results have two important consequences:

Corollary 1

(Standard QM as the infinite-precision limit)
.

Standard quantum mechanics, with point states and exact real-number predictions, is the idealized limit of interval quantum mechanics when all informational constraints are removed. This limit is robust to the geometric representation of finite-precision knowledge and holds for parcels, for outer cylinder-set polyhedra (which ignore positivity).
For bounded observables, the convergence is unconditional. For unbounded observables, the same conclusion holds under suitable uniform moment bounds on the parcels, as in Theorem
3
.

Corollary 2

(Operational distinction)
.

For any finite experimental resources, the parcels constructed in
Theorems
1
,

2
, and

3

yield generically interval-valued predictions. The width of these
intervals quantifies the epistemic uncertainty arising from finite
precision and provides a measure of how far an experimental situation
is from the infinite-precision ideal.

5
Interval Unitary Dynamics

We now lift the standard quantum mechanical operations to our interval
-
based framework. This section demonstrates how unitary evolution, observables, and probabilities become naturally interval
-
valued when states are represented as parcels rather than points.

The use of point
-
state unitary equations to define the evolution of quantum parcels is a deliberate borrowing of an infinite
-
precision idealization to obtain a consistent rule for finite
-
precision objects. The standard Schrödinger equation serves as a mathematical scaffold:
it defines the unitary map
U(t)
used in Definition
3
below, but the evolved
parcel
U(t)(O)
is itself a finite-precision object that makes no
reference to point states.

Let
U(t)
be a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
representing time evolution (e.g.,
U(t)=\exp(-iHt/\hbar)
for Hamiltonian
H
). In standard quantum mechanics, this evolves a density matrix as
\rho\mapsto U(t)\rho U(t)^{\dagger}
. Here, we lift this evolution to quantum parcels.

Definition 3

(Unitary evolution of a parcel)
.

Assume a unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
. For a single quantum parcel
O\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
and a double quantum parcel
(O_{1},O_{2})\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
with
O_{1}\cap O_{2}=\emptyset
, define the evolved parcels, respectively, as

U(O)=\{U\rho U^{\dagger}\mid\rho\in O\},\qquad U(O_{1},O_{2})=(U(O_{1}),U(O_{2})).

Since unitary maps are weak homeomorphisms and preserve disjointness,
U(O)
and
U(O_{1},O_{2})
are, respectively, a valid single and a valid double quantum parcel.

For computational purposes, we often work with convex weak open polyhedra as an approximation to a weak open parcel.

Definition 4

(Vertex
-
based evolution)
.

Let
P
be an experimental parcel defined by finitely many linear constraints
a_{j}<\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H_{j})<b_{j}
. The vertex
-
based evolution of the parent polyhedron
P_{\mathrm{outer}}
under a unitary
U
is

U(P_{\mathrm{outer}})=\operatorname{Conv}\{Uv_{i}U^{\dagger}\mid i=1,\dots,k\}.

Because unitary evolution is linear, the image of the convex hull is the convex hull of the images; thus the vertex prescription is exact for the outer polyhedron. For the true parcel
P
, the set
U(P)
is generally not the convex hull of the images of a finite vertex set, but
U(P_{\mathrm{outer}})
provides a guaranteed outer approximation to it.

Theorem 4

(Properties of unitary evolution)
.

Unitary evolution on a single-parcel
O
and on a double-parcel
(O_{1},O_{2})
satisfies:

(i)

Reversibility:

U^{-1}\cdot(U\cdot(O))=O
and
U^{-1}\cdot(U\cdot(O_{1},O_{2}))=(O_{1},O_{2})
.

(ii)

Preservation of information order:
If
O_{1}\sqsubseteq O_{2}
(i.e.,
O_{1}\subseteq O_{2}
), then
U(O_{1})\sqsubseteq U(O_{2})
. If
(O_{1},O_{2})\sqsubseteq(O_{1}^{\prime},O_{2}^{\prime})
, then
U\cdot(O_{1},O_{2})\sqsubseteq U\cdot(O_{1}^{\prime},O_{2}^{\prime})
.

(iii)

Volume preservation:
For any weak open set
O
with finite volume,
\operatorname{Vol}(U(O))=\operatorname{Vol}(O)
, where the volume is the Hilbert
-
Schmidt volume. In finite dimensions this reduces to the Lebesgue measure.

Thus, unitary evolution in our framework is a deterministic, reversible, volume
-
preserving flow on the domain of quantum parcels—a direct generalization of the standard picture.

5.1
Interval-valued probabilities

A probability in quantum mechanics is a special case of an expectation value: the probability of outcome
i
in a measurement described by a POVM
\{E_{i}\}
is
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho E_{i})
, where each
E_{i}
is a positive operator (
0\leq E_{i}\leq I
) and
\sum_{i}E_{i}=I
. In IQM, this becomes an interval
-
valued probability.

Definition 5

(Probability interval)
.

Let
O\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
be a convex weak open set and
E
a POVM element (
0\leq E\leq I
). The exact probability interval for outcome
E
over
O
is

\Pr_{O}(E)=\bigl(\inf_{\rho\in O}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho E),\;\sup_{\rho\in O}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho E)\bigr)\subseteq[0,1].

For an experimental parcel
P
with an outer polyhedral approximation
P_{\mathrm{outer}}=\operatorname{Conv}\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}
, a conservative estimate is

\Pr_{P_{\mathrm{outer}}}(E)=\bigl(\min_{i}\operatorname{Tr}(v_{i}E),\;\max_{i}\operatorname{Tr}(v_{i}E)\bigr)\supseteq\Pr_{P}(E).

Example 1

(Qubit measurement)
.

Consider a qubit polyhedron
P
contained in the Bloch ball. For a projective measurement along the
z
-axis with
E=|0\rangle\langle 0|
, the probability interval is

\Pr_{P}(E)=\bigl((1+z_{\min})/2,\;(1+z_{\max})/2\bigr),

with
z_{\min},z_{\max}
the minimum and maximum
z
-coordinates of vertices of an outer polyhedral approximation of
P
. The width of this interval quantifies our uncertainty about the outcome probability given our incomplete knowledge of the state.

5.2
Interval-valued physical quantities

The interval
-
valued approach extends beyond expectations and probabilities to any physically meaningful quantity derived from the state.

Definition 6

(Interval
-
valued functional)
.

For any continuous real
-
valued functional
F:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{R}
and a weak open set
O
, define its exact interval value as

F_{I}(O)=\bigl(\inf_{\rho\in O}F(\rho),\;\sup_{\rho\in O}F(\rho)\bigr).

If
F
is linear, the infimum and supremum are attained at extreme points of the closure
\overline{O}
. If
F
is concave (e.g., von Neumann entropy), the minimum occurs at an extreme point but the maximum may lie in the interior. For an experimental parcel
P
with outer polyhedral approximation
P_{\mathrm{outer}}=\operatorname{Conv}\{v_{i}\}
, the outer interval approximation is

F_{I}(P_{\mathrm{outer}})=\bigl(\min_{i}F(v_{i}),\;\max_{i}F(v_{i})\bigr)\supseteq F_{I}(P),

with equality if
F
is linear and
P_{\mathrm{outer}}
coincides with the closed convex hull of
P
.

Important linear examples include:

•

Fidelity interval:
For a target pure state
|\phi\rangle
,

F_{I}(O)=\bigl(\inf_{\rho\in O}|\langle\phi|\rho|\phi\rangle|,\;\sup_{\rho\in O}|\langle\phi|\rho|\phi\rangle|\bigr).

•

Correlation interval:
For a composite system
AB
and observables
A\otimes B
,

C_{P^{AB}}(A,B)=\bigl(\inf_{\rho^{AB}\in P^{AB}}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^{AB}(A\otimes B)),\;\sup_{\rho^{AB}\in P^{AB}}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^{AB}(A\otimes B))\bigr).

•

Entropy interval:
For a weak open set
P
,

S_{I}(P)=\bigl(\inf_{\rho\in P}S_{\mathrm{vN}}(\rho),\;\sup_{\rho\in P}S_{\mathrm{vN}}(\rho)\bigr),

where the minimum occurs at a pure
-
state extreme point of
P
, but the maximum generally requires optimization over the entire set. For outer polyhedral approximations, the vertex
-
based interval may not contain the true entropy interval because entropy is concave; thus entropy intervals are computed directly via convex optimization over
P
.

6
Measurement in Interval Quantum Mechanics

In Interval Quantum Mechanics, measurements are modelled by fuzzy Positive Operator
-
Valued Measures (POVMs) with finite resolution. This approach ensures that measurement updates preserve the open
-
set structure of quantum parcels and align with the finite
-
information philosophy of IQM. Assume we have a finite dimensional Hilbert space in this section.

6.1
Fuzzy POVM construction

Let
\{\Pi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
be a set of orthogonal projectors representing an ideal sharp measurement. To account for finite precision, we define a fuzzy POVM
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
as:

E_{i}=\eta\Pi_{i}+\frac{1-\eta}{m}I,

where
0<\eta<1
is the fuzziness parameter, representing measurement imperfection. Each
E_{i}
is strictly positive (
E_{i}>0
), ensuring the associated Kraus operators are invertible on the relevant subspace.

The corresponding Kraus operators are
M_{i}=\sqrt{E_{i}}
(positive square root). These satisfy:

\sum_{i=1}^{m}M_{i}^{\dagger}M_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}E_{i}=I.

Note that for
\eta=1
, the projector
E_{i}=\Pi_{i}
is rank deficient while for
\eta\in(0,1)
, it has full rank and is thus invertible.

6.2
Measurement update on quantum parcels

Definition 7

(Measurement update)
.

Let
O
be a single parcel and
(O_{1},O_{2})
a double parcel, where

O
,
O_{1}
, and
O_{2}
are experimental parcels, defined by finitely
many observables and hence contained in finite-dimensional affine
subspaces of the space of trace-one Hermitian operators.

For a fuzzy POVM measurement with outcome
j
and Kraus operators

M_{i}
, the Kraus map

f_{i}^{(\eta)}(\rho)=\frac{M_{i}^{(\eta)}\rho(M_{i}^{(\eta)})^{\dagger}}{\Tr(\rho E_{i}^{(\eta)})}

maps each finite-dimensional affine subspace into a finite-dimensional
affine subspace spanned by the images of its defining observables.

We denote by

W:=\Aff\!\left(O_{2}\cup\bigcup_{i\neq j}f_{i}^{(\eta)}(O_{1})\cup f_{j}^{(\eta)}(O_{2})\right)

the common finite-dimensional affine span of

O_{2}
, the sets
f_{i}^{(\eta)}(O_{1})
for
i\neq j
, and

f_{j}^{(\eta)}(O_{2})
.

We define the updated parcels by

O^{\prime}=f_{j}^{(\eta)}(O),

for a single parcel, and

O_{1}^{\prime}=f_{j}^{(\eta)}(O_{1}),\qquad O_{2}^{\prime}=\operatorname{Conv}\!\left(O_{2}\cup\bigcup_{i\neq j}f_{i}^{(\eta)}(O_{1})\cup f_{j}^{(\eta)}(O_{2})\right),

for a double parcel, where the convex hull is taken in the affine space

W
.
Since
W
is finite-dimensional, the convex hull of an open subset of

W
is open. Hence
O_{2}^{\prime}
is open, and therefore both
O_{1}^{\prime}
and

O_{2}^{\prime}
are open.
The updated single parcel
O^{\prime}
is therefore well-defined. The pair

(O_{1}^{\prime},O_{2}^{\prime})
is likewise well-defined as a pair of open sets
associated with the measurement update.

Theorem
5
provides natural sufficient conditions for the updated double-parcel to be a double-parcel. If an experimental single-parcel
O
is approximated by the outer polyhedron
O_{\mathrm{outer}}\supseteq O
, the update for outcome
j
can be computed by applying the Kraus map
M_{j}
to each vertex of
O_{\mathrm{outer}}
and taking the convex hull. This yields an outer approximation of the updated physical parcel, guaranteeing conservative probability intervals.

Proposition 3

(Measurement update for a single-parcel)
.

Let
O\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
be a single-parcel. Then, for all
\eta\in(0,1)
, the updated single-parcel

O^{\prime}=f_{j}^{(\eta)}(O)=\left\{\frac{M_{j}^{(\eta)}\rho(M_{j}^{(\eta)})^{\dagger}}{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho E_{j}^{(\eta)})}:\rho\in O\right\}

is a convex weak open set.

Thus, single-parcels are preserved under measurement. Quantum physicists have generally only considered updates of possible states, which is formulated in finite precision quantum mechanics in the simple form given by Proposition
3
.

Next, we show that subject to a couple of necessary conditions, which ensure the two components of the double-parcel are well
-
separated with respect to the measurement, the update for a double-parcel is again a double-parcel.

Theorem 5

(Double
-
parcel measurement update for sufficiently sharp measurements)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite
-
dimensional Hilbert space and let
(O_{1},O_{2})
be a double-parcel. Then there exists
\eta_{0}<1
such that for all

\eta\in(\eta_{0},1)
,

O_{1}^{\prime}(\eta)=f_{j}^{(\eta)}(O_{1}),

and

O_{2}^{\prime}(\eta)=\operatorname{Conv}\!\left(O_{2}\cup\bigcup_{i\neq j}f_{i}^{(\eta)}(O_{1})\cup f_{j}^{(\eta)}(O_{2})\right)

is a double-parcel

(O_{1}^{\prime}(\eta),O_{2}^{\prime}(\eta))
satisfying

(O_{1},O_{2})\sqsubseteq(O_{1}^{\prime}(\eta),O_{2}^{\prime}(\eta)),

provided that
the following two conditions hold:

(i)

(Uniform positivity)

There exists
\delta>0
such that for all

\rho\in\overline{O_{1}}\cup\overline{O_{2}}
and all

i
(
1\leq i\leq m
),

\Tr(\rho\Pi_{i})\geq\delta.

(ii)

(Separation)
There exists a Hermitian operator
H
with
H=\Pi_{j}H\Pi_{j}
(i.e.,
H
acts only on the range of
\Pi_{j}
) and constants
c_{1}>c_{2}
such that for all
\rho_{1}\in\overline{O_{1}}
and
\rho_{2}\in\overline{O_{2}}
,

\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{1}H)>c_{1}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{1}\Pi_{j}),\qquad\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{2}H)<c_{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{2}\Pi_{j}).

Remark 2
.

Condition (i) ensures no state in the parcels is orthogonal to any measurement outcome. The uniform bound
\delta>0
guarantees the update maps
f_{i}^{(\eta)}
avoid singularities and behave regularly on the compact closures. Condition (ii) ensures that the two components
(O_{1},O_{2})
of the quantum parcel are well separated for the measurement by
f_{j}^{\eta}
.

Theorem
5
ensures that sufficiently sharp measurements preserve the parcel structure. If the original parcel is approximated by a polyhedron, the theorem guarantees that the updated polyhedral approximation also satisfies the disjointness condition, provided the approximation is tight.

Example 2

(Necessity of condition (i) (uniform positivity))
.

The following shows that condition (i) cannot be dropped even when condition (ii) holds. Consider a
two-qubit system
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
with

\Pi=|0\rangle\langle 0|_{A}\otimes I_{B}
. Let
O_{1}
be a small
neighbourhood of
\rho_{0}=|0\rangle\langle 0|_{A}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|_{B}

and
O_{2}
be defined by
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\Pi)\in(0,0.5)
, so that

\inf_{\rho\in O_{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\Pi)=0
and condition (i) fails.
Then condition (ii) holds but the updated sets
O_{1}^{\prime}(\eta)
and
O_{2}^{\prime}(\eta)

fail to be disjoint for any

. The detailed construction

6.3
Monotonicity of measurement update

The measurement update defined on quantum parcels is monotone with respect to the information order.

Proposition 4

(Monotonicity of measurement update)
.

For any measurement outcome
j
, the update map
M_{j}
is monotone: if
(O_{1},O_{2})\sqsubseteq(U_{1},U_{2})
, then
M_{j}(O_{1},O_{2})\sqsubseteq M_{j}(U_{1},U_{2})
.

6.4
Volume contraction under measurements

Consider a qubit with Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
and let
\Pi_{j}=|0\rangle\langle 0|
(the projector onto the state
|0\rangle
). For a finite
-
precision measurement with sharpness parameter
\eta\in(0,1]
, the POVM element is

E_{j}^{(\eta)}=\eta\Pi_{j}+\frac{1-\eta}{2}I.

The corresponding Kraus operator is
M_{j}^{(\eta)}=\sqrt{E_{j}^{(\eta)}}
and the update map for outcome
j
is

f_{j}^{(\eta)}(\rho)=\frac{M_{j}^{(\eta)}\rho M_{j}^{(\eta)\dagger}}{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho E_{j}^{(\eta)})}.

In the Bloch representation
\rho=\frac{1}{2}(I+x\sigma_{x}+y\sigma_{y}+z\sigma_{z})
the map reads

x^{\prime}=\frac{\sqrt{1-\eta^{2}}\,x}{1+\eta z},\qquad y^{\prime}=\frac{\sqrt{1-\eta^{2}}\,y}{1+\eta z},\qquad z^{\prime}=\frac{z+\eta}{1+\eta z}.

Its Jacobian determinant (with respect to the volume on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
) is

J^{(\eta)}(z)=\frac{(1-\eta^{2})^{2}}{(1+\eta z)^{4}}.

(6)

Theorem 6

(Volume contraction for a qubit)
.

Let
P\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
be an open set of qubit states and assume that

\min_{\rho\in P}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\Pi_{j})=c>0.

Then there exists
\eta_{0}\in(0,1)
such that for every
\eta\in(\eta_{0},1)
,

\operatorname{Vol}(f_{j}^{(\eta)}(P))<\operatorname{Vol}(P).

If, moreover,
c\geq\frac{1}{2}
, then the inequality holds for all
\eta\in(0,1)
.

Multi
-
qubit case

Now let
\mathcal{H}=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
be the Hilbert space of
n
qubits,
\dim\mathcal{H}=d=2^{n}
. Let
\Pi_{j}
be a projector with rank
r<d
(the case
r=d
is trivial because then the measurement does not change the state). For
\eta\in(0,1]
define the POVM element:

E_{j}^{(\eta)}=\eta\Pi_{j}+\frac{1-\eta}{d}I,

and the corresponding update map

f_{j}^{(\eta)}(\rho)=\frac{M_{j}^{(\eta)}\rho M_{j}^{(\eta)\dagger}}{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho E_{j}^{(\eta)})},\qquad M_{j}^{(\eta)}=\sqrt{E_{j}^{(\eta)}}.

Theorem 7

(Volume contraction for
n
qubits)
.

Let
P\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
be a weak open subset of density matrices and assume

\min_{\rho\in P}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\Pi_{j})=c>0.

Then there exists
\eta_{0}\in(0,1)
such that for every
\eta\in(\eta_{0},1)
,

\operatorname{Vol}(f_{j}^{(\eta)}(P))<\operatorname{Vol}(P),

where volume is taken with respect to the Hilbert
-
Schmidt measure.

Corollary 3

(Strict increase of geometric information)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
5

and either Theorem
6
or
Theorem
7
, the geometric information

I(O_{1},O_{2})=\frac{\text{Vol}(O_{2})}{\text{Vol}(O_{1})}

strictly increases under measurement:

I(O_{1}^{\prime}(\eta),O_{2}^{\prime}(\eta))>I(O_{1},O_{2}).

6.5
Entropy evolution under measurement

The interval
-
valued von Neumann entropy
S_{I}(P)=(\inf_{\rho\in P_{1}}S(\rho),\sup_{\rho\in P_{1}}S(\rho))
, for a single-parcel
P
, may increase, decrease, or widen under a finite
-
precision measurement, reflecting the dual nature of such observations: information gain (exclusion of possibilities) versus added noise (imperfect resolution). This contrasts with standard quantum mechanics, where measurement of a pure state leaves von Neumann entropy unchanged despite an apparent gain of knowledge.

More fundamentally, for a double-parcel
(P_{1},P_{2})
, the total geometric information
\mathcal{I}(P_{1},P_{2})=\operatorname{Vol}(P_{2})/\operatorname{Vol}(P_{1})
strictly increases under any measurement satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
5
that refines the parcel, because
\operatorname{Vol}(P_{1})
contracts and
\operatorname{Vol}(P_{2})
expands (Theorems
6
and
7
). Thus
\mathcal{I}
provides a monotonic measure of information gain aligned with intuition, while von Neumann entropy does not.

The results of this section establish the core measurement-theoretic
foundation of IQM. Proposition
3
guarantees that single-parcels are
preserved under fuzzy measurement. Theorem
5
gives two natural
sufficient conditions for the double parcel structure to survive
measurement. Theorems
6
and
7
establish volume contraction of the
possible set, and Section
6.5
shows that the geometric information

I(O_{1},O_{2})=\mathrm{Vol}(O_{2})/\mathrm{Vol}(O_{1})
increases
monotonically under any measurement satisfying these conditions –
resolving the entropy stagnation problem. These results are applied
to the three foundational paradoxes in Sections 8–10. A further
application, combining the measurement framework with Reimann’s
thermalisation theorem to derive IQM-native thermalisation results
for double parcels, is developed in a companion paper.

Uncertainty as Geometric Interval Constraint

In Interval Quantum Mechanics, when observables
A,B
are measured on states represented by a weak open set
P\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
, their standard deviations become interval
-
valued.

Definition 8

(Standard deviation interval)
.

For a weak open set
P
and an observable
A
, define

\Delta_{P}(A)=\left(\inf_{\rho\in P}\Delta_{\rho}A,\;\sup_{\rho\in P}\Delta_{\rho}A\right),

where
\Delta_{\rho}A=\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho A^{2})-[\operatorname{Tr}(\rho A)]^{2}}
.

In keeping with the convention for expectation and probability intervals, we define the standard deviation interval as open because the parcel
P
is open; the infimum and supremum of
\Delta_{\rho}A
over
P
are not necessarily attained, but every value inside the interval occurs for some
\rho\in P
.

In finite dimensions, because
\Delta_{\rho}A
is continuous on the compact closure
\overline{P}
, the infimum and supremum are actually attained on
\overline{P}
, but the interval is the same. For computational purposes, if
P
is a weak open set, one may use convex optimization techniques. Since
\rho\mapsto\Delta_{\rho}A
is concave, upper bounds on

\max_{\rho\in\overline{P}}\Delta_{\rho}A

may be obtained by maximizing over an outer polyhedral approximation

P_{\mathrm{outer}}\supseteq P
, or via Lipschitz continuity on compact

\overline{P}
when working in finite dimensions.

Theorem 8

(Uncertainty relation for weak open sets)
.

[Uncertainty relation for weak open sets]
Let
P\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
be a quantum parcel and
A,B

self-adjoint operators. Then

\min_{\rho\in P}\left(\Delta_{\rho}A\cdot\Delta_{\rho}B\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\min_{\rho\in P}|\mathrm{Tr}(\rho[A,B])|.

Thus the uncertainty bound for the whole parcel is at least as strong as
the weakest bound among its constituent states.

Example 4

(Qubit weak open set)
.

For a qubit weak open set
P
and Pauli observables
\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y}
,

\min_{\rho\in P}(\Delta_{\rho}\sigma_{x}\cdot\Delta_{\rho}\sigma_{y})\geq\min_{\rho\in P}|\langle\sigma_{z}\rangle_{\rho}|.

The right
-
hand side is a linear minimization over
P
, while the left requires concave optimisation.

In standard QM the uncertainty principle is often regarded as ontological; in IQM it becomes an epistemic geometric constraint. The widths of
\Delta_{P}(A)
and
\Delta_{P}(B)
are bounded by their commutator. As a parcel narrows in one observable’s direction, it must expand in complementary directions—a geometric form of complementarity.

8
The Double-slit experiment in IQM

We model the double-slit experiment with a qubit:
\ket{L}
(left slit) and
\ket{R}
(right slit).
The state on the screen is described by the projector

\Pi_{\phi}=\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi},\qquad\ket{\phi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{L}+e^{i\phi}\ket{R}),

where
\phi
is the relative phase determined by the position on the screen.

A realistic preparation does not produce a pure state; it produces an experimental parcel
O
defined by finitely many expectation intervals. For instance, take an orthonormal set of observables
\{\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z}\}
on the qubit. Let the measured expectation values be intervals around the values for a specific superposition, e.g., for
\ket{+}=(\ket{L}+\ket{R})/\sqrt{2}
we have
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})=(1,0,0)
. Define

O=\bigl\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{2}):|\Tr(\rho\sigma_{x})-1|<\varepsilon,\;|\Tr(\rho\sigma_{y})|<\varepsilon,\;|\Tr(\rho\sigma_{z})|<\varepsilon\bigr\},

with
\varepsilon>0
chosen small. This set contains the pure superposition
\ket{+}\bra{+}
and also mixed states close to it. Thus the parcel encodes both the possibility of interference (through states near the superposition) and the possibility of partial which
-
path knowledge – there is no sharp distinction between “wave” and “particle”.

Which
-
path measurement as a fuzzy POVM.

A which
-
path measurement is described by the projectors
\Pi_{L}=\ket{L}\bra{L}
,
\Pi_{R}=\ket{R}\bra{R}
. The fuzzy POVM is

E_{L}=\eta\Pi_{L}+\frac{1-\eta}{2}I,\qquad E_{R}=\eta\Pi_{R}+\frac{1-\eta}{2}I,\qquad\eta\in(0,1).

If the outcome is
L
, the updated parcel is
O^{\prime}=f_{L}^{(\eta)}(O)
with

f_{L}^{(\eta)}(\rho)=\frac{M_{L}\rho M_{L}^{\dagger}}{\Tr(\rho E_{L})},\quad M_{L}=\sqrt{E_{L}}.

Interference visibility.

After the which
-
path measurement, we measure the screen position by projecting onto
\Pi_{\phi}
. The probability interval for outcome
\phi
is

\Pr_{O^{\prime}}(\phi)=\left(\inf_{\rho^{\prime}\in O^{\prime}}\Tr(\rho^{\prime}\Pi_{\phi}),\;\sup_{\rho^{\prime}\in O^{\prime}}\Tr(\rho^{\prime}\Pi_{\phi})\right).

As
\eta
increases (more precise which
-
path information), the parcel
O^{\prime}
shrinks towards the state
\ket{L}\bra{L}
. Consequently, the probability interval narrows and its centre moves towards
1/2
(the value for a classical mixture). For
\eta=1
(sharp measurement),
O^{\prime}
is a neighbourhood of
\ket{L}\bra{L}
(or a single point if the initial parcel was a point), and the probability interval becomes a point at
1/2
– no interference. Thus the visibility fades smoothly as
\eta
grows, without any abrupt transition.

8.1
Double-parcel description

To track both what is possible and what has been ruled out, we introduce a double-parcel
(O_{1},O_{2})
.

Construction.

Let
O_{1}
be an experimental parcel and a neighbourhood of the superposition
\ket{+}
defined above.
Let
O_{2}
be an experimental parcel and a neighbourhood of the state
\ket{L}\bra{L}
(e.g., a small ball around the left slit state). Choose
O_{2}
small enough so that
O_{1}\cap O_{2}=\varnothing
. Both sets are open and convex.

Assumption (i): uniform positivity.

For the which
-
path measurement, the projector is
\Pi_{L}=\ket{L}\bra{L}
. On the compact closures
\overline{O_{1}}
and
\overline{O_{2}}
, the functions
\rho\mapsto\Tr(\rho\Pi_{L})
are continuous and strictly positive because
O_{1}
is near the superposition (so
\Tr(\rho\Pi_{L})
is near
1/2
) and
O_{2}
is near
\ket{L}
(so
\Tr(\rho\Pi_{L})
is near
1
). Hence there exists
\delta>0
such that
\Tr(\rho\Pi_{L})\geq\delta
for all
\rho\in\overline{O_{1}}\cup\overline{O_{2}}
. Thus Assumption (i) holds.

Assumption (ii): separation.

We need a Hermitian operator
H
commuting with
\Pi_{L}
(hence diagonal in the
\{\ket{L},\ket{R}\}
basis) and constants
c_{1}>c_{2}
such that

\forall\rho_{1}\in O_{1}:\;\Tr(\rho_{1}H)>c_{1}\Tr(\rho_{1}\Pi_{L}),\qquad\forall\rho_{2}\in O_{2}:\;\Tr(\rho_{2}H)<c_{2}\Tr(\rho_{2}\Pi_{L}).

Write
H=a\ket{L}\bra{L}+b\ket{R}\bra{R}
with real
a,b
. For any state, let
t=\Tr(\rho\Pi_{L})
. Then

\Tr(\rho H)=at+b(1-t),\qquad\frac{\Tr(\rho H)}{\Tr(\rho\Pi_{L})}=a+b\,\frac{1-t}{t}\equiv f(t).

Because
\overline{O_{1}}
and
\overline{O_{2}}
are compact and disjoint,
the possible values of
t
on
\overline{O_{1}}
and
\overline{O_{2}}

form two disjoint compact intervals
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
contained in

[\delta,1]
, with

\max I_{2}<\min I_{1}

(since
O_{2}
is near
\ket{L}
, so its
t
is close to
1
,
while
O_{1}
is near the superposition, so its
t
is close to

1/2
). The function

\phi(t)=\frac{1-t}{t}

is continuous and strictly decreasing on
(0,1)
. Choose
b<0
.
Then

f(t)=a+b\phi(t)

is strictly increasing in
t
. Consequently,

\max_{t\in I_{2}}f(t)=f(\max I_{2}),\qquad\min_{t\in I_{1}}f(t)=f(\min I_{1}).

Since
\max I_{2}<\min I_{1}
, the strict increase of
f
implies

\max_{t\in I_{2}}f(t)<\min_{t\in I_{1}}f(t).

Choose numbers
c_{1},c_{2}
such that

\max_{t\in I_{2}}f(t)<c_{2}<c_{1}<\min_{t\in I_{1}}f(t).

These exist because the extrema are distinct. Then for all

\rho_{1}\in O_{1}
,

f(t)\geq\min_{I_{1}}f>c_{1},

and for all
\rho_{2}\in O_{2}
,

f(t)\leq\max_{I_{2}}f<c_{2}.

Multiplying by
t>0
gives the required inequalities. Thus
Assumption (ii) holds (e.g. take
a=0
,
b=-1
, i.e.

H=-\ket{R}\bra{R}
).

8.2
Conclusion for wave-particle duality

In IQM, wave
-
particle duality is resolved because:

(i)

The state is represented by an experimental parcel that contains both coherent superpositions and mixed states, reflecting finite-precision knowledge.

(ii)

A which
-
path measurement is a fuzzy POVM whose precision
\eta
continuously interpolates between full interference and complete particle
-
like behaviour.

(iii)

The double-parcel explicitly tracks the impossible set, and the geometric information
\mathcal{I}=\operatorname{Vol}(O_{2})/\operatorname{Vol}(O_{1})
increases monotonically as the measurement sharpens.

(iv)

The infinite
-
precision limit recovers the standard complementarity, but that limit is never physically attained.

Thus there is no duality: a single geometric object (the parcel) encodes both wave
-
like and particle
-
like behaviours, and the apparent transition is simply a matter of how precisely we have measured the path.

9
The Schrödinger Cat Paradox

In standard quantum mechanics, Schrödinger’s cat is described by a superposition

\ket{\Psi}=\alpha\ket{\text{undecayed}}\ket{\text{alive}}+\beta\ket{\text{decayed}}\ket{\text{dead}},

with arbitrary complex coefficients
\alpha,\beta
satisfying
|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1
. This leads to the paradox that the cat is neither alive nor dead until observed. In Interval Quantum Mechanics (IQM), the state is never a point; it is a parcel representing the set of density matrices consistent with finite-precision observations. We show that for any such superposition, an experimental parcel containing the corresponding coherent state configuration evolves under measurement in a manner that provides a finite-precision reformulation of the Schrödinger-cat paradox, and that the treatment applies uniformly both to a single qubit (the minimal model) and to the extremely high-dimensional Hilbert spaces associated with macroscopic systems.

9.1
Arbitrary superposition in a qubit

For a single qubit, map the states
\ket{0}=
alive,
\ket{1}=
dead. An arbitrary superposition

\ket{\psi}=\alpha\ket{0}+\beta\ket{1}

has Bloch vector
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
given by

x_{0}=2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^{*}\beta),\quad y_{0}=2\operatorname{Im}(\alpha^{*}\beta),\quad z_{0}=|\alpha|^{2}-|\beta|^{2}.

Choose an orthonormal basis of observables
\{\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z}\}
. For any
\varepsilon>0
define

O_{\varepsilon}=\bigl\{\rho\in\textrm{\bf{D}}(\mathbb{C}^{2}):|\Tr(\rho\sigma_{x})-x_{0}|<\varepsilon,\;|\Tr(\rho\sigma_{y})-y_{0}|<\varepsilon,\;|\Tr(\rho\sigma_{z})-z_{0}|<\varepsilon\bigr\}.

where
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
is the Bloch vector of a pure state. This set is open in the subspace topology of the state space and contains the pure state. The parcel contains:

•

The pure superposition
\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}
;

•

Mixed states close to that superposition;

•

Decohered mixtures (e.g.,
\frac{1}{2}|0\rangle\langle 0|+\frac{1}{2}|1\rangle\langle 1|
) only if the intervals are large enough to include them – for small
\varepsilon
, they are excluded.

Thus the parcel reflects the fact that our finite
-
precision knowledge does not distinguish between the pure coherent superposition and a slightly decohered mixture.

9.2
Arbitrary superposition in a high
-
dimensional cat

For a realistic cat, the Hilbert space is enormous (
\dim\sim 10^{23}
). The two macroscopic states are

\ket{\text{alive}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\ket{0}_{i},\qquad\ket{\text{dead}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\ket{1}_{i}.

The superposition
\ket{\Psi}=\alpha\ket{\text{undecayed}}\ket{\text{alive}}+\beta\ket{\text{decayed}}\ket{\text{dead}}
is a pure state in the joint system. We never have access to all degrees of freedom; instead, we measure a finite set of coarse
-
grained observables
H_{1},\dots,H_{m}
(e.g., total energy, number of alive molecules, etc.). Their expectation values on
\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}
are fixed numbers
v_{j}=\Tr(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}H_{j})
. An experimental parcel containing
\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}
is then

O=\bigl\{\rho:a_{j}<\Tr(\rho H_{j})<b_{j},\;j=1,\dots,m\bigr\},

where the intervals are chosen so that
v_{j}\in(a_{j},b_{j})
and the intersection with the state space is non
-
empty. For sufficiently tight intervals,
O
is a small neighbourhood of
\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}
. Despite the huge dimension, the parcel lives in the finite
-
dimensional affine subspace spanned by
\{H_{1},\dots,H_{m},I\}
. Its volume in the Hilbert–Schmidt metric is well
-
defined (restricted to that subspace) and is simply the product of the interval lengths when the observables are orthonormal.

9.3
Measurement of the cat’s status

A realistic “which
-
cat” measurement is a fuzzy POVM derived from a projector
\Pi_{\text{alive}}
(which projects onto the subspace where the cat is alive). For the qubit,
\Pi_{\text{alive}}=|0\rangle\langle 0|
. For the real cat,
\Pi_{\text{alive}}
is a projector onto a huge subspace (all states where, say, more than 99% of molecules are in the alive configuration). The fuzzy POVM is

E_{\text{alive}}=\eta\Pi_{\text{alive}}+\frac{1-\eta}{2}I,\qquad E_{\text{dead}}=\eta(I-\Pi_{\text{alive}})+\frac{1-\eta}{2}I,

with
\eta\in(0,1)
. The update map for outcome “alive” is

f_{\text{alive}}^{(\eta)}(\rho)=\frac{M_{\text{alive}}\rho M_{\text{alive}}^{\dagger}}{\Tr(\rho E_{\text{alive}})},\quad M_{\text{alive}}=\sqrt{E_{\text{alive}}}.

When applied to the parcel
O
, the updated possible set is

O^{\prime}=f_{\text{alive}}^{(\eta)}(O)=\left\{f_{\text{alive}}^{(\eta)}(\rho):\rho\in O\right\}.

9.4
Behaviour for arbitrary superposition

For a qubit, we compute the image of a pure state

|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
under the alive update map directly in the
computational basis. The key result is that for sufficiently sharp
measurements, the update map drives any pure state with a nonzero alive
component towards the alive state
|0\rangle\langle 0|
, independently
of the superposition coefficients.

Lemma 1

(Sharp limit of the alive update)
.

Let
|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle
be a qubit pure
state with
\alpha\neq 0
. Then

f_{\mathrm{alive}}^{(\eta)}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)\xrightarrow{\eta\to 1^{-}}|0\rangle\langle 0|,

in trace norm, independently of the values of
\alpha
and
\beta
.
When
\alpha=0
, the outcome “alive” has probability

\mathrm{Tr}(\rho E_{\mathrm{alive}}^{(\eta)})=\frac{1-\eta}{2}\to 0

as
\eta\to 1^{-}
, so the cat is definitively dead and the “alive”
outcome is never obtained in the sharp limit.

For mixed states inside the parcel
O
, the image under

f_{\mathrm{alive}}^{(\eta)}
is a convex combination of the images of
pure states, all of which concentrate near
|0\rangle\langle 0|
for

\eta
close to
1
by Lemma
1
. Hence the updated
parcel
O^{\prime}=f_{\mathrm{alive}}^{(\eta)}(O)
will be concentrated around
the alive state, independently of the initial superposition coefficients

\alpha
and
\beta
.

For the high-dimensional cat, the same property holds: for any state in
a sufficiently small neighbourhood of
|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|
, the
image under
f_{\mathrm{alive}}^{(\eta)}
for
\eta
near
1
is close
to a state supported on the alive subspace. Thus the updated parcel
O^{\prime}

will be concentrated around the alive state.

9.5
Dependence on the size of the parcel

If the initial parcel
O
is a small neighbourhood of
\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}
(tight intervals), then
O^{\prime}
will be a neighbourhood of the pure alive state. The cat becomes known to be alive.

If the initial parcel is larger (coarser knowledge), it may also contain states that are nearly dead or highly mixed. Then
O^{\prime}
may still contain some dead component. This is consistent with the idea that a measurement only refines knowledge; it does not cause an instantaneous collapse.

9.6
Double-parcel description of Schrödinger’s cat

We model the cat as a qubit, with
\ket{0}
= alive,
\ket{1}
= dead.
Let the initial possible set
O_{1}
be an experimental parcel and a neighbourhood of the superposition

\ket{\psi}=\alpha\ket{0}+\beta\ket{1}
(with
|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1
and
\alpha,\beta\neq 0
).
For example, take a small ball of radius
r>0
in Bloch coordinates around the pure state’s Bloch vector
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
.
Let the impossible set
O_{2}
represent the macroscopically rejected outcome (e.g., the detector interval corresponding to the dead cat), defined by expectation intervals disjoint from
O_{1}
.
Both
O_{1}
and
O_{2}
are open and convex.

Assumption (i): uniform positivity.

For the “alive” measurement, the projector is
\Pi_{\text{alive}}=\ket{0}\bra{0}
.
On the compact closure
\overline{O_{1}}
the function
\rho\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\text{alive}})
attains a positive minimum because it is continuous and
\overline{O_{1}}
is compact; denote this minimum by
\delta_{1}>0
.
Similarly, on
\overline{O_{2}}
the minimum
\delta_{2}=\min_{\rho\in\overline{O_{2}}}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\text{alive}})
is also positive (the closure does not contain states with exactly zero probability).
Set
\delta=\min(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})>0
. Then for all
\rho\in O_{1}\cup O_{2}
we have
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\text{alive}})\geq\delta
, so Assumption (i) holds.

Assumption (ii): separation.

We need a Hermitian operator
H
commuting with
\Pi_{\text{alive}}
(hence diagonal in the
\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
basis) and constants
c_{1}>c_{2}
such that

\forall\rho_{1}\in O_{1}:\;\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{1}H)>c_{1}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{1}\Pi_{\text{alive}}),\qquad\forall\rho_{2}\in O_{2}:\;\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{2}H)<c_{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{2}\Pi_{\text{alive}}).

Write
H=a\ket{0}\bra{0}+b\ket{1}\bra{1}
with real
a,b
. For any state, let
t=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\text{alive}})
. Then

\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H)=at+b(1-t),\qquad\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H)}{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\text{alive}})}=a+b\,\frac{1-t}{t}\equiv f(t).

Because
\overline{O_{1}}
and
\overline{O_{2}}
are compact and disjoint,
their images under the continuous map

t(\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\mathrm{alive}})

are two disjoint compact intervals
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
contained in

(0,1]
, with

\max I_{2}<\min I_{1}

(since
O_{1}
is near a superposition with
\min I_{1}>0
and

O_{2}
is near the dead state). The function

\phi(t)=\frac{1-t}{t}

is continuous and strictly decreasing on
(0,1]
. Choose
b<0
.
Then

f(t)=a+b\,\phi(t)

is strictly increasing in
t
(because
b<0
multiplies a decreasing
function). Consequently,

\max_{t\in I_{2}}f(t)=f(\max I_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad\min_{t\in I_{1}}f(t)=f(\min I_{1}).

Such numbers exist because the two extrema are distinct. Then for all
\rho_{1}\in O_{1}
we have
f(t)\geq\min_{I_{1}}f>c_{1}
, and for all
\rho_{2}\in O_{2}
we have
f(t)\leq\max_{I_{2}}f<c_{2}
. Multiplying by the positive quantities
t=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\Pi_{\text{alive}})
yields the desired inequalities. Thus Assumption (ii) holds for any choice of
b<0
and any
a
(for concreteness, take
a=0,b=-1
, i.e.
H=-|1\rangle\langle 1|
).

With both assumptions verified, Theorem
5

guarantees that for a sufficiently sharp measurement (i.e. for

\eta
in some interval
(\eta_{0},1)
), the updated pair

(O_{1}^{\prime},O_{2}^{\prime})
is again a double-parcel (i.e. a pair of disjoint open
sets). The possible set
O_{1}^{\prime}
is a neighbourhood of the alive state,
and the impossible set
O_{2}^{\prime}
contains a neighbourhood of the dead
state. Hence the cat is known to be alive, and dead states are moved to
the impossible set—without any collapse.

9.7
Conclusion for the Cat Paradox

In IQM, the Schrödinger cat paradox is resolved for any superposition of alive and dead states because:

(i)

The state is represented by an experimental parcel defined by finitely many accessible observables – independent of the dimension.

(ii)

This set contains the pure entangled state and, depending on the interval sizes, may also contain mixed decohered states – all consistent with the same coarse
-
grained data.

(iii)

A fuzzy measurement updates this set to a new open set; for sufficiently sharp measurements and small initial parcels, the updated possible set concentrates on the outcome subspace.

(iv)

By introducing a second
impossible
set, we form a double-parcel
(O_{1},O_{2})
. The impossible set explicitly tracks what has been ruled out, and the geometric information
\mathcal{I}=\operatorname{Vol}(O_{2})/\operatorname{Vol}(O_{1})
increases monotonically with each measurement.

(v)

The infinite
-
precision limit recovers standard quantum mechanics, but that limit is never physically reached – the point state is an idealisation.

Thus, there is no paradox: the cat is always objectively alive or dead, and our knowledge evolves by refining the parcel. The treatment works uniformly for a single qubit (any coefficients
\alpha,\beta
) and for the astronomically high
-
dimensional Hilbert spaces of real cats.

10
Entanglement in Finite-Precision Quantum Mechanics

10.1
Single-parcel description

Consider two qubits,
A
(Alice) and
B
(Bob), in the Bell state
\ket{\Phi^{+}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{00}+\ket{11})
.
An experimental parcel containing this state can be defined using the 15 orthonormal Pauli products
\{\sigma_{i}\otimes\sigma_{j}\}
with
(i,j)\neq(0,0)
. Let
v_{ij}=\Tr(\ket{\Phi^{+}}\bra{\Phi^{+}}(\sigma_{i}\otimes\sigma_{j}))
be the exact expectation values (e.g.,
v_{xx}=1
,
v_{yy}=-1
,
v_{zz}=1
, all others
0
). Choose a small
\varepsilon>0
and define

O=\bigl\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}):|\Tr(\rho(\sigma_{i}\otimes\sigma_{j}))-v_{ij}|<\varepsilon,\;(i,j)\neq(0,0)\bigr\}.

Lemma 2
.

In a finite-dimensional bipartite system
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, the set of separable states

\mathcal{S}=\operatorname{Conv}\{\,\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}\mid\rho_{A}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A}),\ \rho_{B}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{B})\,\}

is closed (in the usual topology of the finite-dimensional space of Hermitian operators, which coincides with the weak topology).

For sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, this experimental parcel is a neighbourhood of
\ket{\Phi^{+}}\bra{\Phi^{+}}
and consists entirely of entangled states (the set of separable states is closed, so it does not intersect a small enough neighbourhood of a pure entangled state). The parcel reflects the fact that our finite-precision knowledge cannot distinguish the perfect Bell state from a slightly noisy version, but entanglement is still certified.

10.2
Geometric Bell violation for weak open parcels

We now prove a central result that shows how a small enough experimental parcel around the Bell state certifies entanglement via the CHSH inequality.

For two qubits, define the CHSH operator

S=A\otimes B+A\otimes B^{\prime}+A^{\prime}\otimes B-A^{\prime}\otimes B^{\prime},

with the standard choices
A=\sigma_{x}
,
A^{\prime}=\sigma_{z}
,
B=\frac{\sigma_{x}+\sigma_{z}}{\sqrt{2}}
,
B^{\prime}=\frac{\sigma_{x}-\sigma_{z}}{\sqrt{2}}
.

Theorem 9

(Geometric Bell violation for polyhedra)
.

Let
P\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2})
be an experimental parcel with compact closure
\overline{P}
, and suppose
P
contains the Bell state
\ket{\Phi^{+}}\bra{\Phi^{+}}
. Then by choosing the intervals that define
P
sufficiently small (i.e., taking
P
to be a sufficiently tight neighbourhood of the Bell state), we have

E_{P}(S)\subset(2,\;2\sqrt{2}\,].

Hence every state in
P
violates the CHSH inequality and is therefore entangled.

Corollary 4

(Entanglement witness)
.

The same continuity argument applies to any entanglement witness
W
:
if
\Tr(\rho_{0}W)<0
for some
\rho_{0}\in P
, then by taking
P

sufficiently small we obtain

E_{P}(W)\subset(-\infty,0).

Hence, Theorem
9
is a special case of a general
principle: the image of the compact closure
\overline{P}
under a
continuous function is a compact interval; if this entire interval lies
in a region characterising entanglement, the parcel is certified
entangled.

10.3
Double-parcel description

To track both what is possible and what has been ruled out, we introduce a double-parcel
(O_{1},O_{2})
.

Construction.

Let
O_{1}
be an experimental parcel that satisfies the conditions of Theorem
9
(i.e., a small neighbourhood of
\ket{\Phi^{+}}\bra{\Phi^{+}}
such that
E_{O_{1}}(S)\subset(2,2\sqrt{2}]
).
Let
O_{2}
be an experimental parcel and a neighbourhood of a product state, e.g.,
\ket{00}\bra{00}
, chosen small enough so that
O_{1}\cap O_{2}=\varnothing
. Both sets are open and convex.

Assumption (i): uniform positivity.

Consider a local measurement on Alice’s qubit in the computational basis, with projector
\Pi_{0}=|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes I_{B}
. On the compact closures
\overline{O_{1}}
and
\overline{O_{2}}
, the functions
\rho\mapsto\Tr(\rho\Pi_{0})
are continuous and strictly positive. For
O_{1}
, the expectation is near
\frac{1}{2}
(the Bell state gives probability
\frac{1}{2}
for outcome
0
); for
O_{2}
, it is near
1
(since
\ket{00}\bra{00}
always gives outcome
0
). Hence there exists
\delta>0
such that
\Tr(\rho\Pi_{0})\geq\delta
for all
\rho\in\overline{O_{1}}\cup\overline{O_{2}}
. Thus Assumption (i) holds.

Assumption (ii): separation.

We need a Hermitian operator
H
commuting with
\Pi_{0}
and constants
c_{1}>c_{2}
such that

\forall\rho_{1}\in O_{1}:\;\Tr(\rho_{1}H)>c_{1}\Tr(\rho_{1}\Pi_{0}),\qquad\forall\rho_{2}\in O_{2}:\;\Tr(\rho_{2}H)<c_{2}\Tr(\rho_{2}\Pi_{0}).

Choose
H=-\sigma_{z}\otimes I_{B}
. Then
\Tr(\rho H)=-\Tr(\rho(\sigma_{z}\otimes I_{B}))
. For
\rho
near
\ket{\Phi^{+}}
,
\Tr(\rho H)\approx 0
; for
\rho
near
\ket{00}
,
\Tr(\rho H)\approx-1
. Moreover,
\Tr(\rho\Pi_{0})
is near
1/2
on
O_{1}
and near
1
on
O_{2}
. Define the ratios

R(\rho)=\frac{\Tr(\rho H)}{\Tr(\rho\Pi_{0})}.

On
O_{1}
,
R(\rho)
is near
0
; on
O_{2}
,
R(\rho)
is near
-1
. Because
\overline{O_{1}}
and
\overline{O_{2}}
are compact and disjoint,
their images under the continuous map

R:\rho\mapsto R(\rho)

are compact intervals satisfying

\max_{\overline{O_{2}}}R<\min_{\overline{O_{1}}}R.

Choose constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that

\max_{\overline{O_{2}}}R<c_{2}<c_{1}<\min_{\overline{O_{1}}}R.

For instance,
c_{1}=-0.2
,
c_{2}=-0.5
works. Then
c_{1}>c_{2}
, and for all

\rho_{1}\in O_{1}
,

R(\rho_{1})\geq\min_{\overline{O_{1}}}R>c_{1},

while for all
\rho_{2}\in O_{2}
,

R(\rho_{2})\leq\max_{\overline{O_{2}}}R<c_{2}.

Multiplying by the positive quantity

t=\Tr(\rho\Pi_{0})

gives the required inequalities. Hence
Assumption (ii) holds.

Measurement update.

With both assumptions satisfied, Theorem
5
guarantees that for a sufficiently sharp measurement (i.e., for
\eta
in some interval
(\eta_{0},1)
), the updated double-parcel
(O_{1}^{\prime},O_{2}^{\prime})
is a valid parcel (disjoint open sets). After Alice obtains outcome
0
,
O_{1}^{\prime}
is a neighbourhood of
\ket{00}\bra{00}
, while
O_{2}^{\prime}
contains a neighbourhood of
\ket{11}\bra{11}
. This update is purely epistemic, reflecting the information gained by Alice, not a physical influence on Bob’s system.

10.4
Conclusion for entanglement

In IQM, entanglement is understood as:

(i)

The state is represented by an experimental parcel that, when small enough around a pure entangled state, consists entirely of entangled states (Theorem
9
).

(ii)

The CHSH expectation interval over the parcel provides a rigorous finite
-
precision certificate of entanglement: if the entire interval lies above
2
, then every state in the parcel violates the inequality.

(iii)

A local measurement updates the parcel to a new open set; the double-parcel
(O_{1},O_{2})
tracks the impossible states, and the geometric information
\mathcal{I}=\operatorname{Vol}(O_{2})/\operatorname{Vol}(O_{1})
increases monotonically.

(iv)

The apparent “spooky action at a distance” disappears: the update is purely epistemic, reflecting the refinement of our knowledge, and no physical signal is sent.

11
Information Gain and Entropy: IQM Interpretation

The asymmetry between von Neumann entropy and geometric information is not merely
conceptual—it is physical. Von Neumann entropy
S(\rho)=-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\log\rho)
is defined on a point state
\rho
—a single density matrix specified with
infinite precision. As established in Section
4
, such a point state
is the idealised limit of a sequence of nested parcels and is never physically
realisable: infinite precision is not merely impractical but is excluded by the finite
resolution of every measurement apparatus, the finite energy range of every detector,
and, conjecturally, by fundamental limits such as the Planck scale. Von Neumann entropy
is therefore a theoretical construct defined on an object—the point state—that no
experiment can certify, prepare, or verify.

Geometric information
I(O_{1},O_{2})=\mathrm{Vol}(O_{2})/\mathrm{Vol}(O_{1})
, by contrast, is defined on a
parcel—the exact mathematical representation of what a finite-precision experiment can
actually certify. Every quantity entering its definition (the observables
H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}
, the interval widths
(b_{j}-a_{j})
, and the disjointness of
O_{1}
and

O_{2}
) is directly grounded in laboratory operations. Geometric information is
therefore not merely a
better
information measure than
S
in some technical
sense—it is the
only
information measure that is fully grounded in what is
physically accessible.

The entropy stagnation problem—the observation that measuring a pure state leaves
S=0
before and after, despite a genuine gain of knowledge—is a consequence of applying
an idealised infinite-precision construct to a situation that is inherently
finite-precision. In IQM the problem cannot be stated in its original form, because
there are no point states for
S
to be evaluated on. It dissolves as a consequence of
taking finite precision seriously as a physical primitive.

Geometric information is measurement-monotone.

For a double-parcel
(P_{1},P_{2})
, any finite-precision measurement satisfying the
conditions of Theorem
5
strictly shrinks the possible set

P_{1}
and strictly expands the impossible set
P_{2}
. By
Theorems
6
and
7
,
\mathrm{Vol}(P_{1})

contracts under the measurement update, while
\mathrm{Vol}(P_{2})
grows as excluded
alternatives are transferred into the impossible set. Consequently,

I(P_{1},P_{2})\;=\;\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(P_{2})}{\mathrm{Vol}(P_{1})}

(7)

increases strictly under every such measurement. This monotonicity holds
uniformly—for qubits, for finite multi-qubit systems, and for any parcel obtained
from an experimental parcel by the operations of IQM. It is the geometric counterpart
of the intuitive principle that measurement always refines knowledge, and it is a
theorem about physically realisable operations on physically realisable objects.

Entropy interval behaviour under measurement.

Although
S
is not a foundational quantity in IQM, the interval-valued von Neumann
entropy

S_{I}(P)\;=\;\Bigl(\inf_{\rho\in P}S(\rho),\;\sup_{\rho\in P}S(\rho)\Bigr)

(8)

remains a well-defined functional on a parcel
P
, and its behaviour under measurement
carries physical meaning about the mixedness of the compatible states. For a
single-parcel
P
,
S_{I}(P)
may increase, decrease, or widen under a finite-precision
measurement. This reflects the dual character of such measurements: information gain
(exclusion of possibilities, which shrinks
P_{1}
and increases
I
) coexists with added
noise from imperfect resolution. A sharp measurement concentrates the possible set on
the outcome subspace, typically producing states of lower entropy; a fuzzy measurement
with small
\eta
injects noise and may widen the entropy interval even as
I

increases. The two quantities therefore capture complementary aspects of the measurement
process. That
S_{I}
is not monotone under measurement while
I
is reflects precisely
the distinction drawn above:
S_{I}
tracks the internal mixedness structure of the states
within the parcel, while
I
tracks the observer’s epistemic position relative to the
full state space. Neither reduces to the other, and the non-monotonicity of
S_{I}
is not
a defect but an expression of the fact that it is measuring something different.

Multi-observer consistency.

IQM provides an objective epistemic framework in which multiple observers’ knowledge
combines naturally via set operations on parcels. If two observers have measured
different sets of observables
\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}\}
and
\{H^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,H^{\prime}_{k}\}

with compatible results, their combined parcel is the intersection
O\cap O^{\prime}
of their respective experimental parcels. Since both
O
and

O^{\prime}
are convex weak open sets, their intersection is again a convex weak open
set, provided it is non-empty. Non-emptiness is guaranteed whenever the observers’
measurements are physically compatible—that is, whenever there exists at least one
density matrix consistent with both sets of interval constraints simultaneously; this is
always the case when the observers measure commuting observables or when their
measurement intervals are sufficiently wide. If the intersection is empty, the possible
region vanishes, indicating contradictory observations that require resolution via
communication between the observers.

The impossible set of the combined parcel is the union
O_{2}\cup O^{\prime}_{2}
of the individual impossible sets, which grows monotonically as more
observers contribute data. Spacelike-separated observers’ updates commute, since
intersection and union of sets are commutative operations, and time-ordered updates
compose monotonically in the information order. The quantum parcel thus serves as an
objective, shareable information state that multiple agents can consistently
refine—avoiding the subjective belief conflicts of QBism and the branching
inconsistencies of Many-Worlds, while respecting causal structure.

IQM reformulates the measurement problem by: (i) eliminating the collapse postulate
from the dynamical description; (ii) treating finite observational precision as
fundamental rather than incidental; and (iii) providing a geometric epistemic framework
for multi-observer consistency in which
I
is the physically grounded measure of
knowledge gain.

Four interpretations of quantum mechanics compared.

Interpretation

Description

The cat is in superposition until observed; collapse is a physical
event.

Many-Worlds

The cat is both alive and dead in different branches of the universal
wavefunction.

QBism

The quantum state represents an agent’s personal beliefs, updated by
Bayes’ rule; no objective state exists independently of the

IQM

The cat is alive or dead at all times; the double-parcel tracks both

increases

Hyper
-
rectangle parcels as a data type

For computation we often replace an experimental parcel by an
outer hyper
-
rectangle
in a basis of orthonormal observables. This sacrifices some precision but yields a data type that is easy to store and manipulate.

12.1
Orthonormal observables and hyper
-
rectangles

Choose a finite set of orthonormal Hermitian operators
\{H_{1},\dots,H_{m}\}
with
\Tr(H_{i}H_{j})=\delta_{ij}
. For a state
\rho
, the vector
(\Tr(\rho H_{1}),\dots,\Tr(\rho H_{m}))
lies in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. A
hyper
-
rectangle parcel
is defined by intervals
(a_{j},b_{j})
for these expectations:

\mathsf{Hyp}(a,b)=\bigl\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}):a_{j}<\Tr(\rho H_{j})<b_{j},\;1\leq j\leq m\bigr\}.

The closure of the image of
\mathsf{Hyp}(a,b)
under the map
\rho\mapsto(\Tr(\rho H_{j}))
is exactly the closed hyper
-
rectangle
\prod_{j}[a_{j},b_{j}]
. Because the
H_{j}
are orthonormal, the Hilbert–Schmidt volume of
\mathsf{Hyp}(a,b)
is simply

\operatorname{Vol}(\mathsf{Hyp}(a,b))=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(b_{j}-a_{j}).

12.2
Expectation intervals

For an observable
A
, write its projection onto the span of
\{I,H_{1},\dots,H_{m}\}
as

A_{\parallel}=\frac{\Tr(A)}{d}I+\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}H_{j},\qquad c_{j}=\Tr(AH_{j}),

where
d=\dim\mathcal{H}
. The remainder
A_{\perp}=A-A_{\parallel}

satisfies
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho A_{\perp})=0
for all
\rho
in the affine
span of the
H_{j}
. This formula applies to states
\rho
in the affine
span of
\{I,H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}\}
, which is precisely the subspace in
which hyper-rectangle parcels are defined, so that

\mathrm{Tr}(\rho A_{\perp})=0
is automatically satisfied. Hence

\mathrm{Tr}(\rho A)=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(A)}{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H_{j}).

Over the hyper
-
rectangle parcel
\mathsf{Hyp}(a,b)
, the expectation interval for
A
is

E_{\mathsf{Hyp}(a,b)}(A)=\left(\frac{\Tr(A)}{d}+\sum_{j}(c_{j}^{+}a_{j}+c_{j}^{-}b_{j}),\;\frac{\Tr(A)}{d}+\sum_{j}(c_{j}^{+}b_{j}+c_{j}^{-}a_{j})\right),

where
c_{j}^{+}=\max(c_{j},0)
and
c_{j}^{-}=\min(c_{j},0)
. This formula avoids vertex enumeration and costs
O(m)
time per observable.

12.3
Unitary evolution

Under a unitary
U
, the hyper
-
rectangle parcel evolves to
\rho\mapsto U\rho U^{\dagger}
. In terms of the orthonormal basis, the expectation values become

\Tr(U\rho U^{\dagger}H_{j})=\Tr(\rho(U^{\dagger}H_{j}U)).

Thus the same intervals
(a_{j},b_{j})
are now associated with the rotated observables
H_{j}^{\prime}=U^{\dagger}H_{j}U
. For local unitaries on
n
qubits, each
H_{j}^{\prime}
can be computed efficiently (e.g., by applying the unitary to the Pauli basis). The storage remains
O(m)
; the cost of updating the representation is also
O(m)
.

12.4
Measurement update via Lipschitz bounds

A fuzzy measurement with Kraus operator
M_{j}
induces the map
f_{j}(\rho)=M_{j}\rho M_{j}^{\dagger}/\Tr(\rho M_{j}^{\dagger}M_{j})
. The exact image of a hyper
-
rectangle is not a hyper
-
rectangle, but we can obtain an outer approximation using Lipschitz constants. Let
\delta=\min_{\rho\in\mathsf{Hyp}(a,b)}\Tr(\rho M_{j}^{\dagger}M_{j})
. For
\rho
in the parcel, we have
\Tr(\rho M_{j}^{\dagger}M_{j})\geq\delta>0
(for
\eta<1
, this holds globally).
The Lipschitz bound

\|f_{j}(\rho_{1})-f_{j}(\rho_{2})\|_{1}\leq L_{j}\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{1},\qquad L_{j}=\frac{2\|M_{j}\|^{2}}{\delta},

follows from the general Lipschitz estimate for quantum channels of the
form

\rho\mapsto M\rho M^{\dagger}/\mathrm{Tr}(\rho M^{\dagger}M)

proved
in
[
19
]
, where
\delta=\min_{\rho\in\mathrm{Hyp}(a,b)}\mathrm{Tr}(\rho M_{j}^{\dagger}M_{j})>0
is the uniform lower bound on the
probability of outcome
j
over the parcel, which is strictly positive
for
\eta\in(0,1)
.
Given the width of the original intervals
\Delta_{j}=b_{j}-a_{j}
, the image of
\mathsf{Hyp}(a,b)
is contained in a hyper
-
rectangle whose intervals are expanded by an amount proportional to
L_{j}
times the original diameter. More refined bounds can be obtained via semidefinite programming if needed.

12.5
Complexity and practical considerations

With
m=O(n)
local observables for an
n
-
qubit system, all operations (expectation intervals, unitary update, Lipschitz measurement bounds) run in polynomial time in
n
, independent of the Hilbert space dimension. For a fixed precision, the number
m
can be chosen to match the desired accuracy; increasing
m
allows a tighter outer approximation but increases storage and runtime. The hyper
-
rectangle data type thus provides a tunable balance between precision and computational cost. It is a proper data type for quantum computation, enabling resource
-
aware compilation and verification of quantum programs under finite
-
precision constraints.

Comparison with experimental parcels.

Experimental parcels (defined by the same linear constraints together with the positive semidefinite cone) are more precise but require semidefinite programming, which is polynomial in
m
but with a higher constant factor and less scalability. Hyper
-
rectangle parcels are a coarse outer approximation that is much faster and sufficient for many tasks, such as bounding expectation intervals or certifying entanglement via CHSH intervals. The user can choose between the two representations depending on the required accuracy and computational resources.

13
Conclusion and outlook

We have introduced
Interval Quantum Mechanics (IQM)
as a reformulation of quantum theory that takes finite precision as a fundamental physical necessity rather than as an approximation. Motivated by Heisenberg’s call for a theory based solely on observable quantities, we replace the idealised notion of a point state by a
quantum parcel
– a weak open set of density matrices defined by finitely many strict expectation intervals. Such a parcel is the exact mathematical representation of the epistemic knowledge obtained from a finite set of coarse
-
grained measurements.

The framework provides finite-precision geometric reformulations of several long-standing foundational puzzles:

•

Wave-particle duality
becomes a continuous information-theoretic trade-off: the precision (
\eta
) of a which-path measurement interpolates smoothly between interference behaviour and particle-like localisation.

•

Schrödinger’s cat
is reformulated in terms of finite-resolution parcels rather than exact ontic point states. A parcel compatible with macroscopic observations may simultaneously contain coherent and decohered microscopic configurations, and measurement acts by refining the parcel and transferring excluded alternatives into the impossible set.

•

Entanglement
acquires a geometric epistemic description. When Alice measures one subsystem of an entangled pair, the joint parcel updates instantaneously, changing Bob’s marginal description without introducing superluminal signalling or violating relativistic causality.

All empirical predictions of standard quantum mechanics are recovered exactly in the infinite
-
precision limit, which is never physically attained. The geometric information
\mathcal{I}=\operatorname{Vol}(O_{2})/\operatorname{Vol}(O_{1})
(or
I(O)=1/\operatorname{Vol}(O)
for a single-parcel) provides a rigorous, monotonic measure of knowledge gain under measurement, resolving the entropy stagnation problem.

From a computational perspective, IQM provides a natural data type for finite
-
precision quantum states. Hyper
-
rectangle parcels, defined by intervals on an orthonormal set of observables, require only
O(m)
storage and support polynomial
-
time operations (unitary evolution, expectation intervals, Lipschitz
-
bounded measurement updates). This enables resource
-
aware compilation and precision tracking for near
-
term quantum devices, bridging the gap between high
-
level quantum algorithms and the noisy, finite
-
resolution reality of NISQ hardware.

Future directions

Several avenues for further development are possible:

•

Quantum programming languages:
Extend existing systems (Qiskit, Cirq, Q#) with interval types that explicitly track parcel width and separation certificates, enabling verified hybrid quantum
-
classical programs.

•

Quantum Ergodic Theory, Thermalization, and Macroscopic Typicality:

While this work establishes the fundamental kinematics and measurement dynamics of IQM, a major physical application—to be explored in a forthcoming companion paper—is the resolution of the quantum thermalization problem via quantum ergodic theory. Traditional approaches, such as the Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis (ETH) and Reimann’s typicality frameworks, attempt to recover thermodynamic behavior from the chaotic dynamics of standard microscopic point states. In contrast, the IQM framework allows for a native, robust formulation of ergodic properties directly in terms of quantum parcels. By utilizing ergodic theory to demonstrate how the deterministic unitary flow drives arbitrary initial parcels toward the macro-cells of a microcanonical energy shell, we aim to provide an objective, geometric foundation for the Second Law of Thermodynamics that inherently incorporates the observer’s macroscopic resolution limits.

•

Algebraic Quantum Field Theory and Interacting Fields:
The weak geometric formulation suggests a natural connection with algebraic quantum field theory (AQFT). In AQFT, physical systems are described by nets of local von Neumann algebras, and states are positive normalized linear functionals equipped with the weak topology. Since quantum parcels are themselves weak open convex sets determined by finitely many expectation constraints, the present framework appears naturally compatible with the operational structure of AQFT.

In particular, finite-precision state descriptions may provide an alternative perspective on several conceptual difficulties of relativistic quantum theory, including ultraviolet singularities, strictly localized states, and the operational meaning of field measurements at arbitrarily short distances. It would also be interesting to investigate how parcel dynamics interacts with modular theory, local state restrictions, and the type III structure of local algebras.

Entanglement certification:
Develop efficient algorithms to compute CHSH intervals for experimental parcels (via semidefinite programming) and to certify entanglement of many
-
body systems under finite precision.

Interval Quantum Mechanics offers an observation
-
first, computationally practical foundation for quantum theory that eliminates the idealisation of infinite precision. It unifies the formal and conceptual strengths of domain theory, geometric logic, and convex geometry into a coherent framework that is both mathematically rigorous and empirically faithful. We believe it provides a solid basis for the next generation of quantum information science, from undergraduate teaching to the design of fault
-
tolerant quantum computers.

Proposition

1

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that there exists a non
-
empty weak open set
O\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
consisting only of pure states. Let
\rho_{0}=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\in O
be a pure state. By the definition of the weak topology, there exists an experimental parcel
U\subset O
of
\rho_{0}
of the form

U=\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}):|\operatorname{Tr}(\rho A_{i})-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}A_{i})|<\epsilon_{i},\;i=1,\dots,n\},

where each
A_{i}
is a bounded self
-
adjoint operator and
\epsilon_{i}>0
.

Since
\dim\mathcal{H}\geq 2
, choose a normalized vector
|\phi\rangle
orthogonal to
|\psi\rangle
. For any
\lambda\in(0,1)
define

\rho(\lambda)=(1-\lambda)|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|+\lambda|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|,

which is a mixed state (its purity is
(1-\lambda)^{2}+\lambda^{2}<1
).

For each
i
, compute

|\operatorname{Tr}(\rho(\lambda)A_{i})-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}A_{i})|=\lambda\,|\langle\phi|A_{i}|\phi\rangle-\langle\psi|A_{i}|\psi\rangle|.

Let
M_{i}=|\langle\phi|A_{i}|\phi\rangle-\langle\psi|A_{i}|\psi\rangle|
, which is finite because
A_{i}
is bounded. If
M_{i}=0
, the inequality holds for every
\lambda
. For those
i
with
M_{i}>0
, choose
\lambda
such that

0<\lambda<\min_{i:M_{i}>0}\frac{\epsilon_{i}}{M_{i}}.

Then for every
i
we have
|\operatorname{Tr}(\rho(\lambda)A_{i})-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}A_{i})|<\epsilon_{i}
, so
\rho(\lambda)\in U\subset O
. But
\rho(\lambda)
is mixed, contradicting the assumption that
O
contains only pure states. Hence no
such
O
exists, and every quantum parcel necessarily contains mixed
states alongside any pure state it may include. ∎

Proposition
2

Proof.

Let

\alpha=\inf_{\rho\in O}\Tr(\rho H),\qquad\beta=\sup_{\rho\in O}\Tr(\rho H).

If
\alpha=\beta
, then every
\rho\in O
satisfies

\Tr(\rho H)=\alpha
, so the image is the singleton
\{\alpha\}
.

Assume
\alpha<\beta
. By definition of infimum and supremum, every
value
\Tr(\rho H)
lies in
[\alpha,\beta]
. Since
O
is weak
open and
\rho\mapsto\Tr(\rho H)
is weak continuous, the values

\alpha
and
\beta
cannot be attained in
O
unless

\rho\mapsto\Tr(\rho H)
is constant on
O
, which is excluded by

\alpha<\beta
. Hence all values lie in
(\alpha,\beta)
.

Conversely, let
r\in(\alpha,\beta)
. By the definitions of infimum
and supremum, there exist
\rho_{-},\rho_{+}\in O
such that

\Tr(\rho_{-}H)<r<\Tr(\rho_{+}H).

Since
O
is convex,

\rho_{t}=t\rho_{-}+(1-t)\rho_{+}\in O

for all
t\in[0,1]
. The function

t\mapsto\Tr(\rho_{t}H)

is continuous and affine, so by the intermediate value theorem there
exists
t_{0}\in(0,1)
such that

\Tr(\rho_{t_{0}}H)=r.

Thus the image is exactly
(\alpha,\beta)
.
∎

Theorem

1

Proof.

We first note that although the
N
diagonal projectors satisfy

\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|=I,

so that their expectations sum to
\Tr(\rho)=1
for any trace-one
operator
\rho
, this linear dependence is trivial on the trace-one
affine subspace. Consequently, the evaluation map

\Phi:\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{sa}}^{\Tr=1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}},\qquad\Phi(\rho)=\bigl(\Tr(\rho A_{m})\bigr)_{m=1}^{N^{2}},

is injective. Indeed, if
\Phi(\rho)=\Phi(\sigma)
, then

\Tr\bigl((\rho-\sigma)A_{m}\bigr)=0\qquad(m=1,\dots,N^{2}).

Since the operators
A_{m}
span

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{sa}}
and

\Tr(\rho-\sigma)=0
, it follows that
\rho=\sigma
.

Consider the trace-zero self-adjoint subspace

\mathcal{T}_{0}(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{sa}}=\{X\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{sa}}:\Tr(X)=0\}.

The map

X\mapsto\bigl(\Tr(XA_{m})\bigr)_{m=1}^{N^{2}}

is injective on
\mathcal{T}_{0}(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{sa}}
.
Since
\mathcal{T}_{0}(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{sa}}
is finite-dimensional,
all norms are equivalent. Therefore there exists a constant
C>0

such that

\|X\|_{1}\leq C\max_{1\leq m\leq N^{2}}|\Tr(XA_{m})|

for all
X\in\mathcal{T}_{0}(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{sa}}
.

Now let
\rho,\sigma\in P_{n}(\rho_{0})
. By definition,

|\Tr((\rho-\rho_{0})A_{m})|<2^{-n},\qquad|\Tr((\sigma-\rho_{0})A_{m})|<2^{-n},

for every
m
. Hence

|\Tr((\rho-\sigma)A_{m})|\leq|\Tr((\rho-\rho_{0})A_{m})|+|\Tr((\sigma-\rho_{0})A_{m})|<2^{1-n}.

Since
\rho-\sigma\in\mathcal{T}_{0}(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{sa}}
,
the norm-equivalence estimate gives

\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}\leq C\,2^{1-n}.

Therefore

\operatorname{diam}_{1}(P_{n}(\rho_{0}))\leq C\,2^{1-n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Since
O_{n}(\rho_{0})\subseteq P_{n}(\rho_{0})
, it follows immediately that

\operatorname{diam}_{1}(O_{n}(\rho_{0}))\leq\operatorname{diam}_{1}(P_{n}(\rho_{0}))\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Moreover,
\rho_{0}\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})\subseteq P_{n}(\rho_{0})
for every
n
.
Hence any element of

\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}P_{n}(\rho_{0})
must lie within trace distance
at most
\operatorname{diam}_{1}(P_{n}(\rho_{0}))
of
\rho_{0}
for every

n
. Since the diameters tend to zero,

\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}P_{n}(\rho_{0})=\{\rho_{0}\}.

The same argument yields

\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}O_{n}(\rho_{0})=\{\rho_{0}\}.

Now let
H
be a bounded Hermitian operator on
\mathcal{H}
.
The expectation functional

\phi_{H}(\rho)=\Tr(\rho H)

is Lipschitz continuous with respect to the trace norm:

|\Tr(\rho H)-\Tr(\sigma H)|\leq\|H\|\,\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Choose
n_{0}
such that

\operatorname{diam}_{1}(P_{n}(\rho_{0}))<\frac{\varepsilon}{\|H\|}

for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Since
\rho_{0}\in P_{n}(\rho_{0})
,
for every
\rho\in P_{n}(\rho_{0})
we have

|\Tr(\rho H)-\Tr(\rho_{0}H)|\leq\|H\|\,\|\rho-\rho_{0}\|_{1}\leq\|H\|\operatorname{diam}_{1}(P_{n}(\rho_{0}))<\varepsilon.

Therefore

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{\rho\in P_{n}(\rho_{0})}\Tr(\rho H)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\rho\in P_{n}(\rho_{0})}\Tr(\rho H)=\Tr(\rho_{0}H).

Since
O_{n}(\rho_{0})\subseteq P_{n}(\rho_{0})
, the same conclusion holds
for the parcels:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})}\Tr(\rho H)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})}\Tr(\rho H)=\Tr(\rho_{0}H).

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem

2

Proof.

Let
\{T_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a family that separates
points of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
(e.g., all finite-rank operators with rational
matrix entries in a fixed orthonormal basis). Define the outer cylinder sets in

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
:

O_{n}(\rho_{0})=\Bigl\{\rho\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}):\operatorname{Tr}(\rho)=1,\;\bigl|\operatorname{Tr}(\rho T_{j})-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}T_{j})\bigr|<2^{-n},\;j=1,\dots,n\Bigr\}.

Step 1: Basic topology.
Each
O_{n}(\rho_{0})
is weak open because each

T_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and the map
\rho\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}(\rho T_{j})

is weak continuous. Clearly
O_{n+1}\subseteq O_{n}
and
\rho_{0}\in O_{n}
for all
n
.

Step 2: Singleton intersection.
Suppose
\rho\in\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}O_{n}(\rho_{0})
.
For any fixed
j
, whenever
n\geq j
we have

|\operatorname{Tr}(\rho T_{j})-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}T_{j})|<2^{-n}
.
Letting
n\to\infty
gives
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho T_{j})=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}T_{j})

for all
j
. Since
\{T_{j}\}
separates points of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
,
we conclude
\rho=\rho_{0}
. Thus
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}O_{n}(\rho_{0})=\{\rho_{0}\}
.

Step 3: Collapse of expectation intervals.

Let
H\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a bounded Hermitian operator and

\epsilon>0
. Since
\{T_{j}\}
separates points of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})

and
H\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, the map
\rho\mapsto\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H)

is continuous in the weak topology. By Step 2,
\bigcap_{n}O_{n}(\rho_{0})=\{\rho_{0}\}
. Since
\mathcal{H}
is separable, the weak topology on
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})

is second countable, hence first countable. The nested weak open sets

O_{n}(\rho_{0})
with
\bigcap_{n}O_{n}(\rho_{0})=\{\rho_{0}\}
therefore form a
neighbourhood base at
\rho_{0}
: every weak open neighbourhood
U
of
\rho_{0}

contains some
O_{n_{0}}(\rho_{0})
, and hence
O_{n}(\rho_{0})\subseteq U
for all

n\geq n_{0}
. Applying this to the weak open neighbourhood

U_{\epsilon}=\left\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}):|\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H)-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{0}H)|<\epsilon\right\}

of
\rho_{0}
, we conclude that for all
n\geq n_{0}
and all

\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})
,

|\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H)-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{0}H)|<\epsilon,

and therefore

\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{O_{n}(\rho_{0})}(H)=\{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{0}H)\}.

∎

Theorem

3

Proof.

Let
\{E_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\geq 0}
denote the spectral family of
H
, and define the bounded truncations

H_{R}:=H\,E_{[0,R]}(H)

for
R>0
. Then
H_{R}
is bounded and satisfies
0\leq H_{R}\leq H
.

We decompose

\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H)-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}H)=\bigl[\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H_{R})-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}H_{R})\bigr]+\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\rho(H-H_{R})\bigr)-\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\rho_{0}(H-H_{R})\bigr).

Hence

\displaystyle\left|\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H)-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}H)\right|\leq\;

\displaystyle\left|\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H_{R})-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}H_{R})\right|

\displaystyle+\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\rho(H-H_{R})\bigr)+\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\rho_{0}(H-H_{R})\bigr).

(9)

(The tail terms
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho(H-H_{R}))
and

\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}(H-H_{R}))
are non-negative since
H-H_{R}\geq 0
,
so the absolute values may be dropped.)

We now estimate the three terms separately.

Step 1: Convergence for the bounded truncations.

Since
H_{R}
is bounded, Theorem
2
implies that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})}\left|\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H_{R})-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}H_{R})\right|=0

for every fixed
R>0
.

Step 2: Uniform control of the tails.

Observe that

H-H_{R}=H\,E_{(R,\infty)}(H).

Since
\lambda\leq\lambda^{1+\delta}/R^{\delta}
for all
\lambda>R
, the functional calculus gives

H\,E_{(R,\infty)}(H)\leq R^{-\delta}H^{1+\delta}.

Therefore, for every
\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})
,

\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\rho(H-H_{R})\bigr)\leq R^{-\delta}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H^{1+\delta}).

By the uniform moment assumption, there exists a constant
C<\infty
such that

\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\rho(H-H_{R})\bigr)\leq CR^{-\delta}

uniformly in
n
and
\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})
. Hence

\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{n}\sup_{\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})}\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\rho(H-H_{R})\bigr)=0.

Similarly, because
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}H)<\infty
, monotone convergence implies

\lim_{R\to\infty}\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\rho_{0}(H-H_{R})\bigr)=0.

Step 3: Conclusion.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary.

Choose
R>0
sufficiently large such that

\sup_{n}\sup_{\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})}\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\rho(H-H_{R})\bigr)<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}

and

\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\rho_{0}(H-H_{R})\bigr)<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

For this fixed
R
, choose
n_{0}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\sup_{\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})}\left|\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H_{R})-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}H_{R})\right|<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Then Equation (
9
) gives, for all
n\geq n_{0}
and all
\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})
,

\left|\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H)-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}H)\right|<\varepsilon.

Therefore,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\rho\in O_{n}(\rho_{0})}\left|\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H)-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{0}H)\right|=0.

The convergence of the upper and lower expectation bounds follows immediately.
∎

Theorem

4

Proof.

(i) Reversibility follows immediately from
U^{-1}=U^{\dagger}
and the
definition of the evolved parcel.

(ii) Preservation of the information order follows from the fact that

\rho\mapsto U\rho U^{\dagger}
is a bijection: if
O_{1}\subseteq O_{2}
then

U(O_{1})\subseteq U(O_{2})
, and similarly for double parcels.

(iii) The map
\rho\mapsto U\rho U^{\dagger}
is an isometry of the
Hilbert–Schmidt inner product, since

\langle U\rho U^{\dagger},U\sigma U^{\dagger}\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\mathrm{Tr}(U\rho U^{\dagger}U\sigma U^{\dagger})=\mathrm{Tr}(U\rho\sigma U^{\dagger})=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\sigma)=\langle\rho,\sigma\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}},

where we used cyclicity of the trace and
U^{\dagger}U=I
. Since unitary
conjugation is a linear isometry of the Hilbert–Schmidt metric on the
affine subspace
\{\rho\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}):\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)=1\}
,
it preserves the Hilbert–Schmidt volume: for any weak open set
O
with
finite volume,

\mathrm{Vol}(U(O))=\mathrm{Vol}(O).

In finite dimensions this reduces to preservation of the Lebesgue measure
on the affine subspace of trace-one Hermitian matrices.

Proposition

3

Proof.

We need to show that
O(\eta)
is weak open.
The map
f_{j}^{(\eta)}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
, is a composition of the linear map
\rho\mapsto M_{j}^{(\eta)}\rho(M_{j}^{(\eta)})^{\dagger}
and the normalization by the positive scalar function
\rho\mapsto\Tr(\rho E_{j}^{(\eta)})
. Since
E_{j}^{(\eta)}
is positive definite (as
\eta>0
), the denominator is strictly positive. Moreover,
M_{j}^{(\eta)}
is invertible, so
f_{j}^{(\eta)}
is a homeomorphism: its inverse is given by

\tau\mapsto\frac{(M_{j}^{(\eta)})^{-1}\tau((M_{j}^{(\eta)})^{\dagger})^{-1}}{\Tr\!\left((M_{j}^{(\eta)})^{-1}\tau((M_{j}^{(\eta)})^{\dagger})^{-1}\right)}.

Therefore,
f_{j}^{(\eta)}
maps open sets to open sets, and
O(\eta)
is open. It remains to show that
f_{j}^{(\eta)}
preserves convexity.
Let
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in O
and
t\in[0,1]
, set

\rho_{t}=t\rho_{1}+(1-t)\rho_{2}
and
p_{k}=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{k}E_{j}^{(\eta)})

for
k=1,2
. A direct computation gives

f_{j}^{(\eta)}(\rho_{t})=\frac{tp_{1}}{tp_{1}+(1-t)p_{2}}\,f_{j}^{(\eta)}(\rho_{1})+\frac{(1-t)p_{2}}{tp_{1}+(1-t)p_{2}}\,f_{j}^{(\eta)}(\rho_{2}).

The coefficients are non-negative and sum to
1
, so
f_{j}^{(\eta)}(\rho_{t})

is a convex combination of
f_{j}^{(\eta)}(\rho_{1})
and
f_{j}^{(\eta)}(\rho_{2})
.
Hence
f_{j}^{(\eta)}
maps convex sets to convex sets, and
O^{\prime}
is convex.
Thus
O^{\prime}
is a valid quantum parcel.
∎

Theorem

5

Proof.

We prove that for
\eta
sufficiently close to
1
, the updated
sets

O_{1}^{\prime}(\eta)=f_{j}^{(\eta)}(O_{1})

and

O_{2}^{\prime}(\eta)=\operatorname{Conv}\!\left(O_{2}\cup\bigcup_{i\neq j}f_{i}^{(\eta)}(O_{1})\cup f_{j}^{(\eta)}(O_{2})\right)

are open and disjoint.

Step 1: Openness.

For
0<\eta<1
, each effect

E_{i}^{(\eta)}=\eta\Pi_{i}+\frac{1-\eta}{d}I

is strictly positive, hence
M_{i}^{(\eta)}=\sqrt{E_{i}^{(\eta)}}
is
invertible. Therefore

f_{i}^{(\eta)}(\rho)=\frac{M_{i}^{(\eta)}\rho M_{i}^{(\eta)\dagger}}{\Tr(\rho E_{i}^{(\eta)})}

is a homeomorphism of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
onto itself, with inverse
given by the same formula using
M_{i}^{(\eta)-1}
, followed by
normalisation. Hence
f_{i}^{(\eta)}(O_{1})
and
f_{i}^{(\eta)}(O_{2})
are
open whenever
O_{1},O_{2}
are open.

The set

O_{2}\cup\bigcup_{i\neq j}f_{i}^{(\eta)}(O_{1})\cup f_{j}^{(\eta)}(O_{2})

is therefore open. Since we are in finite dimension, the convex hull of
an open set is open. Hence
O_{2}^{\prime}(\eta)
is open. Similarly,

O_{1}^{\prime}(\eta)
is open.

Step 2: Sharp-measurement separation.

Let

\phi(\rho)=\Tr(\rho H),

where
H=\Pi_{j}H\Pi_{j}
is the separating observable in assumption (ii).

By replacing
H
with
H+\alpha\Pi_{j}
, if necessary, we may assume

c_{2}>0
. Indeed, this replacement preserves
H=\Pi_{j}H\Pi_{j}
, shifts
both constants
c_{1},c_{2}
by the same amount
\alpha
, and therefore
preserves the gap
c_{1}-c_{2}>0
.

Define

A:=f_{j}^{(1)}(\overline{O_{1}})

and

B:=\overline{O_{2}}\cup\bigcup_{i\neq j}f_{i}^{(1)}(\overline{O_{1}})\cup f_{j}^{(1)}(\overline{O_{2}}).

For
\sigma=f_{j}^{(1)}(\rho_{1})\in A
, with

\rho_{1}\in\overline{O_{1}}
, we have

\sigma=\frac{\Pi_{j}\rho_{1}\Pi_{j}}{\Tr(\rho_{1}\Pi_{j})}.

Using
H=\Pi_{j}H\Pi_{j}
and assumption (ii),

\phi(\sigma)=\frac{\Tr(\rho_{1}H)}{\Tr(\rho_{1}\Pi_{j})}\geq c_{1}.

Next, if
\sigma=f_{j}^{(1)}(\rho_{2})
with

\rho_{2}\in\overline{O_{2}}
, then

\phi(\sigma)=\frac{\Tr(\rho_{2}H)}{\Tr(\rho_{2}\Pi_{j})}\leq c_{2}.

If
\sigma=f_{i}^{(1)}(\rho_{1})
with
i\neq j
and

\rho_{1}\in\overline{O_{1}}
, then
f_{i}^{(1)}(\rho_{1})
is supported on
the range of
\Pi_{i}
. Since
\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}=0
and
H=\Pi_{j}H\Pi_{j}
, we
have

\phi(\sigma)=0<c_{2}.

Finally, if
\sigma\in\overline{O_{2}}
, then by assumption (ii),

\phi(\sigma)=\Tr(\sigma H)\leq c_{2}\Tr(\sigma\Pi_{j})\leq c_{2},

because
0\leq\Tr(\sigma\Pi_{j})\leq 1
and
c_{2}>0
.

Thus

\phi(\sigma)\leq c_{2}\qquad\text{for all }\sigma\in B.

By linearity of
\phi
, the same inequality holds for every

\tau\in\operatorname{Conv}(B)
:

\phi(\tau)\leq c_{2}.

On the other hand,

\phi(\sigma)\geq c_{1}\qquad\text{for all }\sigma\in A.

Hence
A
and
\operatorname{Conv}(B)
are separated by the continuous linear
functional
\phi
with a gap at least
c_{1}-c_{2}>0
. Since both sets
are compact in finite dimension, their distance is strictly positive:

\varepsilon:=d(A,\operatorname{Conv}(B))>0.

Step 3: Uniform convergence of fuzzy to sharp updates.

We claim that

f_{i}^{(\eta)}\longrightarrow f_{i}^{(1)}

uniformly on
\overline{O_{1}}\cup\overline{O_{2}}
as
\eta\to 1^{-}
.

By assumption (i), there exists
\delta>0
such that

\Tr(\rho\Pi_{i})\geq\delta

for all
\rho\in\overline{O_{1}}\cup\overline{O_{2}}
and all
i
. Hence
the denominators in the sharp update are uniformly bounded below.

Write

E_{i}^{(\eta)}=\left(\eta+\frac{1-\eta}{d}\right)\Pi_{i}+\frac{1-\eta}{d}(I-\Pi_{i}).

Thus

M_{i}^{(\eta)}=a_{\eta}\Pi_{i}+b_{\eta}(I-\Pi_{i}),

where

a_{\eta}=\left(\eta+\frac{1-\eta}{d}\right)^{1/2},\qquad b_{\eta}=\left(\frac{1-\eta}{d}\right)^{1/2}.

Therefore

\|M_{i}^{(\eta)}-\Pi_{i}\|_{\infty}=\max\{|a_{\eta}-1|,b_{\eta}\}\longrightarrow 0.

Also,

\Tr(\rho E_{i}^{(\eta)})\longrightarrow\Tr(\rho\Pi_{i})

uniformly on
\overline{O_{1}}\cup\overline{O_{2}}
. Since the denominators
are uniformly bounded below, it follows that

\sup_{\rho\in\overline{O_{1}}\cup\overline{O_{2}}}\|f_{i}^{(\eta)}(\rho)-f_{i}^{(1)}(\rho)\|_{1}\longrightarrow 0

for every
i
.

Consequently, after decreasing
\eta_{0}<1
if necessary, for all

\eta\in(\eta_{0},1)
,

\sup_{\rho\in\overline{O_{1}}\cup\overline{O_{2}}}\|f_{i}^{(\eta)}(\rho)-f_{i}^{(1)}(\rho)\|_{1}<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\qquad\text{for all }i.

Step 4: Stability of separation for
\eta<1
.

Define

B_{\eta}:=\overline{O_{2}}\cup\bigcup_{i\neq j}f_{i}^{(\eta)}(\overline{O_{1}})\cup f_{j}^{(\eta)}(\overline{O_{2}}).

The preceding uniform convergence implies

d_{H}\bigl(f_{j}^{(\eta)}(\overline{O_{1}}),A\bigr)<\frac{\varepsilon}{3},

and

d_{H}(B_{\eta},B)<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Since the convex-hull operation is continuous with respect to the
Hausdorff metric on compact subsets of a finite-dimensional space, after
possibly increasing
\eta_{0}
we also have

d_{H}\bigl(\operatorname{Conv}(B_{\eta}),\operatorname{Conv}(B)\bigr)<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Therefore

d\bigl(f_{j}^{(\eta)}(\overline{O_{1}}),\operatorname{Conv}(B_{\eta})\bigr)\geq\varepsilon-\frac{\varepsilon}{3}-\frac{\varepsilon}{3}=\frac{\varepsilon}{3}>0.

Now

O_{1}^{\prime}(\eta)=f_{j}^{(\eta)}(O_{1})\subseteq f_{j}^{(\eta)}(\overline{O_{1}}),

while

O_{2}^{\prime}(\eta)=\operatorname{Conv}\!\left(O_{2}\cup\bigcup_{i\neq j}f_{i}^{(\eta)}(O_{1})\cup f_{j}^{(\eta)}(O_{2})\right)\subseteq\operatorname{Conv}(B_{\eta}).

It follows that

O_{1}^{\prime}(\eta)\cap O_{2}^{\prime}(\eta)=\emptyset.

Thus for all
\eta\in(\eta_{0},1)
, the updated sets

O_{1}^{\prime}(\eta)
and
O_{2}^{\prime}(\eta)

Theorem

7

Proof.

The Jacobian determinant of
f_{j}^{(\eta)}
at a state
\rho

J^{(\eta)}(\rho)=\frac{\bigl(\det M_{j}^{(\eta)}\bigr)^{2d}}{\bigl(\Tr(\rho E_{j}^{(\eta)})\bigr)^{d^{2}-1}}.

(10)

Indeed, the linear map

multiplies the
Hilbert–Schmidt volume by
\lvert\det(M_{j}^{(\eta)}\otimes M_{j}^{(\eta)})\rvert=(\det M_{j}^{(\eta)})^{2d}

f_{j}^{(\eta)}
is the composition

f_{j}^{(\eta)}=N\circ L,

where

is a linear map on
the
(d^{2}-1)

\mathcal{A}=\{\rho\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{sa}}:\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)=1\}

is the normalisation map that
returns

to the trace-one subspace
\mathcal{A}

The linear map
L
maps
\mathcal{A}
into the affine subspace

\mathcal{A}_{c(\rho)}=\{\sigma:\mathrm{Tr}(\sigma)=c(\rho)\}
where

. The
Jacobian of
L
with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt volume on
\mathcal{A}

is
(\det M_{j}^{(\eta)})^{2d}
, as computed above.

The normalisation map
N
restricted to
\mathcal{A}_{c(\rho)}
is a
homothety of ratio
1/c(\rho)=1/\mathrm{Tr}(\rho E_{j}^{(\eta)})
mapping

\mathcal{A}_{c(\rho)}
back to
\mathcal{A}
. Since
\mathcal{A}
and

\mathcal{A}_{c(\rho)}
are parallel affine subspaces of dimension
d^{2}-1
,
the homothety

scales all
(d^{2}-1)
directions by the same factor

, so its Jacobian on this
(d^{2}-1)

L
and
N

f_{j}^{(\eta)}=N\circ L

\Pi_{j}

(11)

Using the assumption

\eta
