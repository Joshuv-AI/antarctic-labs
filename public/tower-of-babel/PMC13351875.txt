Tumor-specific outcomes in spinal metastases: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Vivek Sanker, Emil O. R. Nordin, Anam Sayed Mushir Ali, Gokulesh D. Gurumurthy, Alexander Thaller, Philip Heesen, Zhikai Li, Linda Liverani, Maria Jose Cavagnaro, John Ratliff, Atman Desai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpinal metastases (SMs) are common, debilitating, and often associated with severe pain and neurologic disability. Treatment decisions hinge on anticipated life expectancy, yet survival and recurrence outcomes remain poorly defined across primary tumor types. This study is to systematically review and meta-analyze outcomes of SM stratified by primary cancer, with focus on survival, recurrence, and treatment patterns.MethodsPubMed, Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, and Embase (Ovid) were searched from inception to April 16, 2025. Eligible studies reported outcomes in patients with SM from any primary tumor. Random-effects meta-analyses were performed for survival and recurrence. Study quality was assessed with ROBINS-I.ResultsOne hundred twenty-three studies (38,780 patients) across nine primary tumors were included; 61 studies (9,222 patients) contributed to meta-analysis. Lung cancer accounted for the largest cohort (n=26,918), followed by gynecologic (n=3,679), breast (n=1,961), and renal (n=1,732). Pooled 1-year survival ranged from 46% in lung SM to 74% in prostate SM. Median survival was shortest for liver (8.2 months) and lung (12.1 months), and longest for thyroid (59.9 months) and melanoma (57.9 months). Recurrence rates were consistently low (<5%), though thyroid SM carried a slightly higher long-term risk.ConclusionsOutcomes in SMs are strongly influenced by primary tumor biology. These data provide benchmarks for clinical decision-making and highlight the need for prospective studies integrating molecular and functional outcomes.

Highlight box

Key findings

• The primary tumor biology is strongly correlated with survival and recurrence outcomes for spinal metastatic disease. Spinal metastases (SMs) from lung and liver cancers show the poorest prognosis, while those from thyroid, melanoma, breast, and prostate cancers demonstrate more favorable survival.

• In a pooled analysis of 123 studies involving 38,780 patients, one-year survival rate ranged from 46% in SM from lung cancer to 74% in SM from prostate cancer. Recurrence rates remained consistently low (<5%) across most tumor types.

What is known and what is new?

• SM are a major source of cancer-related morbidity, and treatment decisions are strongly influenced by expected survival. Prior reviews have not comprehensively stratified outcomes in SM by primary tumor type.

• This study provides a large-scale systematic review and meta-analysis of major primary cancers causing SM and reports tumor-specific benchmarks for survival, recurrence, anatomical distribution, and treatment patterns.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Tumor-specific prognostication should be incorporated into multidisciplinary treatment decisions for SM to better guide surgical aggressiveness, radiotherapy planning, and palliative care integration.

• Future prospective registries should include molecular subtype, neurologic function, and quality-of-life outcomes to refine individualized treatment strategies in the modern era of targeted therapy and immunotherapy.

Introduction

Spinal metastases (SMs) are the most common site of osseous dissemination in cancer (1) and the third most frequent site of all metastatic spread (2). They occur in approximately 15.67% of patients with solid tumors (3) and are increasingly recognized as a major contributor to oncologic morbidity. The World Health Organization (WHO) projects a rise to 29.4 million newly diagnosed cancer cases by 2040 (4), and with improvements in first-line therapies prolonging survival, the incidence of SM is expected to escalate (5). A recent systematic review confirmed this trend, noting that all included studies reported increasing rates of newly diagnosed SM over time (1).

Clinically, SM often presents with pain, spinal instability, and neurologic compromise, including sensory disturbances, ataxia, and bladder dysfunction (1). These symptoms significantly impair function and quality of life. Moreover, SM signals advanced systemic disease (typically stage IV) (6) and is associated with limited survival, making it a terminal condition in many cases. Treatment is therefore often palliative, requiring multidisciplinary coordination (7) and careful integration of life expectancy, neurologic status, and patient goals. Surgical decompression is frequently indicated for spinal cord compression (8), and surgery followed by adjuvant radiotherapy has been shown to improve outcomes compared to radiotherapy alone (9).

Recent advances in systemic therapies, including immunotherapy, targeted agents, and next-generation hormonal treatments, have reshaped survival trajectories across several cancers (10). Likewise, stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) has emerged as a high-precision modality offering durable local control with reduced toxicity (11). These developments underscore the need to re-evaluate survival and recurrence outcomes in SM within the context of modern oncologic care (12).

Despite its prevalence and clinical burden, survival and recurrence outcomes in SM remain incompletely characterized across primary tumor types. Prior reviews have pooled outcomes across cancers, limiting individualized care. No prior synthesis has stratified survival and recurrence outcomes by primary tumor.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate survival, recurrence, and treatment patterns in SM across major primary cancers, providing updated, tumor-specific benchmarks for clinical care. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2025-aw-202/rc).

Methods

Information sources and search strategy

We searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, and Embase (Ovid) databases to identify relevant studies, using a search query with specific keywords including ‘Spinal metastases’, ‘primary tumor’, and ‘outcome’ (full search strategy in Table S1). Individuals of all ages were considered. The objective was to identify studies reporting the demographic and clinical details of patients with SM, along with associated outcomes. Studies that were either unrelated to SM or did not report outcomes were excluded. Animal studies, reviews, and non-original research articles were also excluded. The electronic search ranged from the earliest available date up to April 16, 2025.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they reported survival or recurrence outcomes in patients with SM, stratified by primary tumor type. Both prospective and retrospective cohort studies, registry analyses, and clinical trials were included. Studies were required to report extractable data on overall survival, progression-free survival, local recurrence, or retreatment rates. Only studies published in English were considered.

Exclusion criteria included case reports, conference abstracts, reviews without primary data, and studies that did not stratify outcomes by primary tumor. Studies focusing exclusively on intradural metastases, primary spinal tumors, or non-metastatic spinal disease were excluded. Functional and neurological outcomes were excluded due to incomplete reporting. Non-English full texts were excluded. Where duplicate cohorts were identified, the most comprehensive or recent publication was retained.

Study selection and data collection

Two independent reviewers (E.O.R.N., V.S.) conducted title and abstract screening of 5,866 identified articles in Rayyan, of which 264 were included for full-text screening by the same reviewers. Disagreement was resolved by discussion or inclusion of a third reviewer (P.H.). A total of 123 studies were included for the systematic review, and 61 studies for the meta-analysis. Data extraction was performed by multiple independent reviewers (E.O.R.N., V.S., A.S.M.A., G.D.G.) based on a predefined data extraction sheet.

We sought to systematically review and meta-analyze patient outcomes based on primary cancer type, with a specific focus on recurrence rate, 1-year survival, and 2-year survival. We identified a sufficient number of studies (minimum three) to conduct subgroup random-effects meta-analyses for at least one of these metrics for the following primary cancer types: breast, lung, prostate, and renal. Furthermore, the subgroup analyses were extended to a compiled meta-analysis with data from the six primary cancer types that most commonly metastasize to the spine (breast, lung, prostate, renal, GI, and thyroid) (1). Based on the available data, 2-year survival was inconsistently reported; thus meta-analysis was not possible.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using the ‘meta’ package in R statistical software. We meta-analyzed data from studies that reported outcomes for their entire patient cohort, and the pooled proportions were coded with the inverse variance method as part of the random effects model. Only subgroups with 3 or more studies were eligible for meta-analysis. Heterogeneity was assessed using tau squared and I2. Given the anticipated heterogeneity across studies (different eras, institutions, and treatment modalities), the random effects model was designated as the primary analysis to allow for between-study variation in true effect sizes.

Quality assessment

The quality assessment was performed using the ROBINS-I assessment tool for non-randomized studies. Each article was screened twice, and no article was excluded following the assessment (Table S2). The following bias domains were assessed: bias due to confounding (D1), bias due to selection of participants (D2), bias in classification of interventions (D3), bias due to deviation from intended interventions (D4), bias due to missing data (D5), bias in measurement of the outcome (D6), bias in selection of the reported result (D7).

One study was judged low risk of bias, 56 studies moderate risk, 68 studies serious risk, and 1 study critical risk. No study was excluded based on risk of bias. Results are reported in Table S2.

Results

Study characteristics

Our search resulted in inclusion of 123 studies (Figure 1). Publication years ranged from 2006 to 2025, encompassing 38,780 patients with SM from nine primary tumor types (Table 1). The largest cohorts arose from lung cancer (n=26,918), followed by gynecologic (n=3,679), breast (n=1,961), and renal (n=1,732). A complete list of included studies is provided in Table S3. Among 5,753 patients with reported treatment status, 66% underwent surgery, 63% received radiotherapy, and 55% chemotherapy (Figure 2; Table S4).

Sensitivity analysis by publication era

To evaluate the impact of temporal variation on our primary outcomes, we performed a sensitivity analysis stratifying 1-year survival by publication era (pre-2015 versus 2015 to 2025) for breast, lung, and prostate SM. For lung SM, 1-year survival was 27.0% (95% CI: 18–36%) in studies published from 2015 onwards compared with 17.7% (95% CI: 9–27%) in earlier studies. Breast and prostate SM showed less pronounced differences across eras. These results are reported in Table S5.

Meta-regression

Meta-regression was performed with publication year as a covariate for tumor types with sufficient studies. For lung SM, publication year was significantly associated with higher 1-year survival (beta =0.041 per year, P=0.012), consistent with improvements in systemic therapy over time. No significant association with publication year was found for breast or prostate SM.

Survival outcomes

Average median survival as well as 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates by primary tumor location are reported in Table 2. The average median survival of patients with SM secondary to melanoma and thyroid tumors was the highest, at 57.9±16.4 and 59.9±16.9 months, respectively. Patients with SM secondary to lung and liver tumors had the lowest average median survival, at 12.1±12.7 and 8.2±3.7 months, respectively.

Recurrence

Across tumor subtypes, recurrence rates remained low (<5%) at 1, 2, and 5 years, supporting the effectiveness of local control strategies. Thyroid SM was the exception, with slightly higher long-term recurrence, though still below 10%. It should be noted that these recurrence rates are pooled across varying surgical and radiotherapeutic techniques and follow-up durations; head-to-head comparisons between SBRT, surgery, and combined modality treatment were not possible in this analysis.

Anatomical distribution of SM

The anatomical location of SM varied considerably across primary tumor types (Figure 4; Table S6). The thoracic spine was the most frequently affected region across all tumor types, with involvement rates ranging from 40.2% in gynecological cancers to 60.8% in melanoma. Thyroid cancer demonstrated a unique predilection for the cervical spine (55.5%), substantially higher than other primary tumor types where cervical involvement typically ranged from 10% to 18%. Multiple region involvement was most common in breast cancer (54.4%), while sacral involvement remained relatively rare across all tumor types (typically <10%). Notably, the thoracolumbar junction was frequently affected in lung (23.1%), prostate (25.0%), and liver cancers (15.2%), likely reflecting the biomechanical stress at this transitional zone.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis highlights the profound impact of primary tumor biology on outcomes in SM. By synthesizing data from 61 studies and over 9,000 patients, we provide tumor-specific benchmarks for survival, recurrence, anatomical distribution, and treatment patterns, offering a foundation for more individualized care.

Survival and recurrence outcomes vary by tumor origin

Survival outcomes differed markedly across tumor types. Lung and liver SM patients consistently exhibited the poorest prognosis, with median survival under 12 months and pooled 1-year survival rates between 28% and 31% (13,14). These findings reflect aggressive disease biology, late-stage diagnosis, and limited responsiveness to systemic therapy (3,15). Lung cancer, particularly non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) subtypes, frequently presents with widespread metastases, and spinal involvement often signals advanced systemic burden (14).

In contrast, thyroid and melanoma SM demonstrated median survivals approaching five years (14-21). Thyroid tumors showed low recurrence rates (<10%) despite multilevel and thoracic involvement, while melanoma’s extended survival likely reflects the impact of immune checkpoint inhibitors. However, leptomeningeal progression remains a concern in melanoma, with reported rates between 6% and 18% (3).

Breast and prostate SM showed intermediate but favorable outcomes. Meta-analyses revealed pooled 1-year survival rates of 73% to 76.5%, supported by widespread use of radiotherapy and hormonal therapy. Up to 70% of women with advanced breast cancer develop skeletal metastases, with the thoracic spine being the most common site (3). Improved survival likely reflects advantages in targeted and systemic therapies such as HER2-targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors (22). Similarly, prostate cancer’s skeletal tropism and slower progression allow for palliative radiotherapy and surgical intervention, though neurologic compromise remains significant in patients with high Gleason scores or epidural extension (13,23).

Our pooled estimates complement, rather than duplicate, existing prognostic tools such as the Tomita and Revised Tokuhashi scores. Whereas those instruments assign ordinal categories, our meta-analysis supplies tumor-specific survival probabilities (for example, 46% 1-year survival for lung SM versus 74% for prostate SM) that can be used alongside or to validate these scores in clinical practice.

Recurrence patterns and local control

Across tumor types, recurrence rates were reassuringly low (<5%), suggesting that combined surgery and radiotherapy regimens achieve effective local control. Thyroid SM carried a slightly higher long-term recurrence risk, underscoring the need for vigilant follow-up in this subgroup (16-20,24). It should be noted that these recurrence rates are pooled across varying surgical and radiotherapeutic techniques and follow-up durations; head-to-head comparisons between SBRT, surgery, and combined modality treatment were not possible in this analysis.

Anatomical distribution patterns and surgical implications

Distinct anatomical patterns emerged. Thoracic spine involvement predominated across most tumors (40% to 61%), consistent with its vascular anatomy and proximity to Batson’s plexus. Thyroid SM showed a striking cervical spine predilection (55.5% versus 10% to 18% in other tumors), likely due to direct venous drainage from the thyroid gland. Breast cancer exhibited high rates of multilevel involvement (54.4%), suggesting disseminated spinal disease at presentation. The thoracolumbar junction was frequently affected in lung and prostate cancers, with biomechanical stress at this region often necessitating surgical stabilization.

These distribution patterns have important implications for surgical planning and radiotherapy targeting, particularly in cervical and junctional metastases where neurological compromise and mechanical instability are more likely.

Clinical implications and treatment stratification

Treatment decisions in SM must integrate life expectancy, neurologic status, spinal stability, and tumor biology. Patients with thyroid, melanoma, breast, or prostate SM may benefit from aggressive local therapy, while those with lung, liver, or gastrointestinal SM often require early palliative integration. Advances in systemic therapy and SBRT have reshaped the therapeutic landscape, improving outcomes in select subgroups.

Multidisciplinary input remains essential. Across the review, 64% of patients underwent surgery and 60% received radiotherapy, yet recurrence remained low, highlighting the effectiveness of combined modality treatment.

Sensitivity analysis and temporal trends

The sensitivity analysis by publication era revealed that 1-year survival for lung SM improved from 17.7% in pre-2015 studies to 27.0% in post-2015 studies, consistent with the introduction of targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors. Meta-regression confirmed a significant positive association between publication year and 1-year survival for lung SM (beta =0.041, P=0.012). These findings emphasize that pooled estimates reflect outcomes averaged across treatment eras and should be contextualized with contemporary systemic therapy. Clinicians counseling patients with lung SM in the modern era should anticipate outcomes at the more favorable end of the reported range.

Sources of heterogeneity

The high statistical heterogeneity (I2>80% in most analyses) reflects several identifiable sources. First, temporal variation is substantial: studies span 2006 to 2025, a period encompassing revolutionary changes in systemic oncology, including the introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors for melanoma and lung cancer after 2015 and the widespread adoption of SBRT for local control. Second, treatment modality varied across studies (surgery alone, radiotherapy alone, combined approaches). Third, follow-up duration ranged from months to decades. Fourth, geographic and institutional variation contributed to differences in patient selection and treatment access. Given this heterogeneity, pooled estimates should be interpreted as a range across treatment eras and practice settings rather than a precise contemporary prediction. The random effects model, used as our primary analysis, accounts for this between-study variation and provides more conservative confidence intervals.

Limitations

This review is limited by heterogeneity in study designs, incomplete reporting of neurologic and functional outcomes, and underrepresentation of certain tumor subtypes. Variability in follow-up durations and outcome definitions may have influenced pooled estimates. While ROBINS-I was used for bias assessment, non-randomized designs inherently carry confounding risk. Nonetheless, the inclusion of over 9,000 patients provides robust comparative data. Temporal heterogeneity is a significant limitation: studies span 2006 to 2025, encompassing a period of revolutionary changes in systemic oncology, and the resulting high I2 values (consistently >80%) limit the precision of pooled estimates. Stratification of survival by treatment modality (surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy) or anatomical location was not possible due to insufficient reporting in the included studies; fewer than 15 studies per tumor type provided treatment-stratified survival data. Neurologic and functional outcomes (ASIA/Frankel scores, ambulation status) were excluded due to inconsistent reporting across studies (available in fewer than 30% of included articles); future prospective studies should systematically capture these endpoints alongside survival. Stratification by neurologic deficit severity, molecular subtype, and systemic therapy responsiveness would further refine prognostication and is a key priority for future registries.

Future directions

Future research should focus on prospective registries that integrate molecular markers, imaging features, neurologic outcomes, and quality-of-life metrics. Understanding predictors of spinal dissemination, functional recovery, and treatment responsiveness will refine prognostication and guide individualized care. Prospective data collection should systematically include neurologic deficit severity, molecular subtype, and systemic therapy responsiveness to enable subgroup analyses not possible in the present review. Standardized reporting of treatment modalities and follow-up duration across institutions will also reduce heterogeneity in future meta-analyses. As cancer prevalence rises, with WHO projecting 29.4 million new cases by 2040, SM will become an increasingly common and complex challenge, demanding coordinated interdisciplinary solutions.

Conclusions

Primary tumor biology is the dominant determinant of survival and recurrence in SM. Lung and liver metastases confer the poorest prognosis, while thyroid, melanoma, breast, and prostate metastases are associated with more favorable outcomes and may justify more aggressive local therapy. Recurrence rates remain low overall, suggesting that multimodal treatment strategies are effective for local control.

Accurate, tumor-specific prognostication is essential to guide surgical and radiotherapeutic decision-making, align treatment intensity with expected survival, and optimize quality of life. As advances in systemic therapy and radiotherapy continue to extend survival in selected cancers, individualized management strategies for SM will become increasingly important.
