I. Executive Summary
 The nation’s electric grid is evolving to supply the electricity needed for economic growth, health,
 and quality of life. As the DOE has pointed out “Old Tools Won’t Solve New Problems”
 This project conceived, designed, built, and demonstrated a new tool that promises to make the
 emerging grid dependable at a lower cost. The tool is a smart sensor with the sensitivity and the
 bandwidth to measure actionable information about grid health that was unmeasurable.
 The traditional approach has been to combine routine preventative maintenance with sensors
 that can promptly turn off the appropriate parts of the grid as failure occurs. This innovation is a
 bridge technology. It measures the same quantities as the traditional sensors and much more.
 Due to its significantly improved bandwidth and sensitivity, it provides enhanced capability for
 fault identification and location, i.e., what went wrong and where. It also can detect and identify
 information that the legacy sensors cannot, including incipient fault information that enhances
 distribution grid reliability.
 The sensor integrates a computer, electric measurement instruments, and a communication
 system into a package that clamps on to an energized power line in the grid. The package can be
 installed by a lineman in less than ten minutes and it has the necessary components to scavenge
 the power it needs from the line on which it is mounted.
 This advance is also unique in that the current practice is to have a specialized sensor for each
 event that is to be measured. This is a sentinel observing a wide range of information on the grid
 and alerting the operators if action may be needed. This function is possible because of the small,
 rugged computing capability that is available today.
 The system has also demonstrated capabilities that were previously unavailable. Preliminary
 indications are that these include:
 • Detecting the moment of break of an energized power line.
 • Detecting the interaction between an energized line and vegetation. Recording
 the differences, I that interaction prior to any smoke being produced, after
 smoke but before flames and after flames are ignited.
 • Cracked or polluted insulators.

 More unique examples are likely, but require more study for definitive identification.

II. Objectives
 The objective is to advance technology which enhances the reliability of the electrical
 distribution grid via a smart sensor. The cost of outages is a well-studied topic. For example, a
 one-day outage in the Chicago area1 was estimated to reduce the three-month GDP by $1.8
 billion. Similarly, outages in Texas are estimated2 to cost the economy almost $36 million for
 every megawatt hour of unserved load.
 So, improving reliability provides a financial return as well as enhancing quality of life. A
 technical challenge to improving reliability is the high level that has been achieved3. The U.S.
 Department of Energy’s (DOE) report baseline is that reliability is achieved if the total amount
 of energy that cannot be supplied to customers does not exceed 0.002% of the total energy
 demanded each year.
 The approach that enabled meeting today’s reliability goals has been to employ sensors in
 substations that detected dangerous events and removed power in high-risk situations. As
 was emphasized in the DOE report in footnote 3, “Old Tools Won’t Solve New Problems”.
 Fortunately, technology is making possible a novel approach to sensing the grid that promises
 to be a key tool for increasing grid reliability. Progress is possible because laboratory research
 shows that precursors to serious failures are present on the grid, but at too high a frequency
 and too low of an amplitude to be detected with legacy substation instrumentation.
 These precursors are high impedance (HiZ) faults. HiZ fault detection has been the subject of
 extensive research, with hundreds of published papers proposing various methods. Most of
 these approaches focus on signal processing techniques applied to the steady-state behavior
 of HiZ faults.
 Although these methods show promise, none have become universally accepted or
 standardized methodology for HiZ fault detection. The field remains a topic of investigation
 due to the risks posed by HiZ faults, which can lead to fires or evolve into short circuits that
 cause system outages. The sensor developed in this project can serve as a platform for the
 various artificial intelligence, machine learning and signal processing techniques that have
 been proposed by researchers around the world.
 This project accelerates the adoption of intelligent powerline sensors for fault identification,
 fault location, and islanding detection. The focus is on distribution systems serving larger
 geographic areas and accommodating distributed energy resources. The project designed,

III.1.a Power harvester
The power harvester, the sensor’s primary power source, extracts power from the power line
through magnetic induction. Batteries provide temporary backup power in cases of low current
or power outage.

Our primary design goals were to provide sufficient power at lower currents, minimize noise &
vibrations, and achieve adequate environmental protection while providing adequate power for
reliable operation.
The design is modular because the distribution circuit current is smaller at locations further from
a substation. Consequently, a smaller transformer assembly is required to placements nearer a
substation
 III.1.a.1 Principle of operation
The power harvester operation is based on well-known transformer technology. Specifically, an
ac current flows in the power line that produces an alternating magnetic flux. This flux in the
ferromagnetic core induces an alternating voltage in the coil output terminals. This voltage is
rectified to power the sensor. Figure 6 summarizes this concept
The power harvester is a current transformer with two parasitic air gaps resulting from the need
to clamp the sensor to an operating power line.

 12

 Figure 6 Basic concept of the power harvester

 III.1.a.2 Requirements
The powerline-current range in an operating distribution system can vary, e.g., between 1-50 A
rms. It was decided that designing for operation at locations where the current is typically at least
15 A rms provides a good compromise among utility, size, weight, and cost. If lower current
operation is needed, more modules can be added. The minimum load power at the battery
charging threshold is 7 W.
The sensors are designed for continuous operation in all weather conditions and weeks of
operation in a distribution grid have been demonstrated. This is a design constraint. The modules
were designed so that the number needed in practical applications would not exceed these limits
Mechanical constraints include a max weight of 7 kg and length of 400 mm. The sensor must
clamp on to both 1/0 ACSR (0.4”) or #4 ACSR (0.26”) power cables to accommodate the diversity
in the field. The system must be capable of being installed and by a single lineman wearing
insulated gloves using a bucket truck. The installation should require less than ten minutes.
Additionally, the sensor is designed to operate over an ambient temperature range of -10C to
+40C while accommodating the higher internal temperature due to solar radiation. It should
operate at relative humidity levels up to 90%.
The weight of the entire sensor is limited by the breaking strength of the cable. The National
Electric Safety Code sets this limit at 60% of the cable’s rated breaking strength. Ergonomic
considerations. Figure 7 summarized weight constraints.

 III.1.a.3 Electromagnetic analysis
The harvester design parameters include inner and outer radius, core length, magnetic
material, and the number of turns in the coil. Preliminary analytical assessment provided
important guidance. One is that the while the core cross section should be maximized, its inner
diameter is limited by the wire diameter and the maximum outer dimeter is a compromise
because the magnetic field produced by the current in the powerline decreases as the
reciprocal of the distance from the wire but the weight increases as the square of that
distance.
In addition, the core material should have high permeability, high saturation, and be corrosion
resistant. These requirements suggest a grain-oriented core material, which dictates a tape
wound core.
The analysis also highlighted that there is an optimum number of turns for which the power
is maximized. I also showed the significant negative influence of the two air gaps. That
influence is minimized by excellent surface quality and good tightening of the two halves.
The derivation of the power threshold for a given core is based on power flow analysis. The
analysis also provided guidance on the core's cross-sectional area and weight per unit of
power. Figure 8 summarizes the analysis. The left side of the figure shows the nonlinear
relationship between the line current and the required core means. It highlights conductors
with really low currents would require very large cores. The right side of the figure shows the
load per current in our prototype. The results of the extracted power exceed the expectations
of the core manufacturer.

 IIII.2.a.1 Fault Identification
 Transient events in distribution systems such as animal contacts, vegetation contacts,
 equipment failures, and switching operations produce characteristic voltage and current
 waveforms with unique frequency content. These signals, captured by line-mounted sensors
 or substation data acquisition (DAQ) units, are typically recorded in COMTRADE format with
 multiple analog channels sampled at high rates. Manual analysis of such datasets is time-
 consuming, error-prone, and difficult to scale.
 To address this, the system developed in this investigation integrates field data collection with
 an automated MATLAB-based processing framework. Fault signatures were first recorded

A set of interconnected MATLAB functions was developed to automate the end-to-end
processing of COMTRADE files:
• Batch organization: Group .cfg and .dat pairs into event folders.

• Event isolation: Identify transient windows and split data into pre-event, event, and post-
 event segments.

 18

 • Spectral analysis: Compute FFTs of voltage and current channels to obtain frequency-
 domain signatures.

 • Fault classification: Match new events to a fault signature library using a least-squares
 comparison of spectra.

 • Export and archiving: Generate Excel workbooks, time-domain/FFT plots, and structured
 result folders for reproducibility.

The framework includes four key components:
 I. process_comtrade_files.m (driver script),

 II. classify_fault_fft.m (FFT-based classifier),

 III. export_comtrade_to_excel.m (Excel exporter), and

 IV. build_fft_library.m (library builder).

This pipeline ensures that new fault events can be automatically categorized and archived for
future analysis. With utility input and nine months of field deployment, seven distinct event types
were identified. Each exhibits unique characteristics in both the time and frequency domains,
enabling reliable separation via FFT-based classification.
Type 1 - Three-Phase Voltage Step Change
Rapid RMS voltage shifts (100–200 V) across all phases, occurring a few times per week. These
events are often linked to voltage regulation and do not necessarily indicate system failures.
Transients are modest, with FFT showing energy up to a few kilohertz (Figures 11(b) and 11(c)).
Type 2 - Spiking (Rain/Lightning-Induced Transients)
High-frequency bursts caused by lightning or partial discharges during precipitation. Most are
three-phase events, with spectral energy above 1 kHz. Typically, clear within 1–2 AC cycles.
Type 3 - One-Cycle Overcurrent
Brief (1–2 cycle) high-current faults that do not trip feeder-head reclosers. Impedance remains
constant during the fault. FFT reveals pronounced high-order harmonics due to low impedance.
Type 4 - Sub-Cycle Transient Failure
Transformer or equipment failures generating sub-cycle transients. High fault currents cause
protective devices to clear these events almost immediately. Spectra show broad high-
frequency content.
Type 5 - Tree Touch
Single-phase events caused by vegetation contact. Initially low-level currents persist for
seconds before evolving into a short-circuit. The initial transient is a key early indicator. FFT
reveals high-frequency content before significant current rise.
To confirm the accuracy of the developed sensor, signatures measured by others using other
means applied to the sensor using an Omicron CMC256 plus. The UT sensor accurately captured

 19

the signals, even with current levels below 1 amp (broken conductor). Over a one-second sliding
window, the error in harmonic content was less than 5%.

Type 6 - Load Pick-Up (Cold Load Reconnection)
Inrush currents following grid outages. The DAQ system captured both the initiating
overcurrent and the subsequent load pick-up. FFT shows strong 2nd and 3rd harmonics
decaying over 10–20 cycles, often with accompanying voltage dips.
Type 7 - Animal Contact
Short, high-current faults from animal contact. These may blow fuses at service transformers,
causing localized outages. FFT spectra show strong high-order harmonic peaks in both voltage
and current.

 III.2.a.2 Fault Location
The sensor not only identifies anomalous behavior but includes software to locate events. The
traveling wave fault location method is explained in figure 12. Once a fault occurs, the transient
signal propagates in both directions on a line. The speed of the traveling wave is estimated
using the line parameters. If two sensors capture the fault waveforms, the time arrival
difference of pulse signals can be used to estimate the location of the fault.

This method is extended to multiple laterals and loop configuration by integrating a sensor-
network with GPS synchronization. Multiple sensors with sub-microsecond level synchronized
waveforms could improve the fault location accuracy. The key challenge is that the sensor
needs high bandwidth. Local intelligence on sensor controller can detect

When fault 1 happens the line current increases from 75 A to 150 A. This increase may not be
sufficient to trigger an over-current relay at the substation. This type of event is hard to detect
using the current signal only with a conventional current transformer, especially when the fault
happens at a time of low instantaneous voltage during a cycle. For the capacitor insulation
breakdown, only a small oscillation online current is detected. This difficulty arises because the
large line inductance filters the high frequency content from the line current signal. Compared
to underground cables, overhead lines have smaller distributed capacitance. This allows the

high frequency voltage signal during transient faults to travel longer distances. Thus, voltage
signals are more desirable to detect high impedance and transient faults in a distribution feeder
with overhead lines.

To investigate the impact of voltage sensor frequency bandwidth, the cutoff frequency of
voltage sensor is varied from 10 kHz to 1 MHz. As expected, as the cutoff frequency decreases,
the high frequency components are attenuated. To achieve sufficient fidelity to identify
transient faults, it suggests that the cutoff frequency should not be less than 100 kHz.
Otherwise, signals could not be accurately captured. Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT) are effective
tools to provide additional insight for the sensor frequency bandwidth selection.

The field testing was augmented with CHIL testing. The CHIL simulation system for sensor
application test is shown in figure 15. The simulated model in Opal-RT simulator is developed
based on an operating circuit in a rural distribution system.

Two different simulation approaches are adopted. For the islanding detection test, circuit
model is simulated using electromagnetic transient simulation approach in a CPU-based
simulator with 200 ps time step. In the traveling wave fault location test, FPGA-based simulator
is used to model detailed traveling wave behavior during fault with less than 1 ps simulation
time step. A similar circuit was implemented in the FPGA simulator to evaluate the traveling
wave fault location method. The lengths of lines 1, 2, and 3 are reduced to 4 km, 1 km, and 1
km, respectively. All the other parameters are maintained the same. The reduced line length
makes FPGA real-time simulation more efficient with less than 1 ps simulation time step.

The controllers are compact RIO 9039 (1.91 GHz Quad-Core CPU, 2 GB DRAM, and Kintex-7
325T FPGA) and 9043 (1.30 GHz Dual-Core CPU, 2 GB DRAM, Kintex-7 160T FPGA). GPS module
is available for controllers. In the lab test, the GPS antenna receives signals from 11 satellites.
Two groups of analog input channels can be configured for different applications. The first
group channels have 50 kHz sampling rate to support regular data acquisition or functions (such
as fault event record and islanding detection) and the second group channels have 1 MHz
sampling rate to support traveling wave fault location. The data acquisition system and sensor
local intelligence are implemented on the sensor controller. Various basic fault detection
criteria are implemented including over-current, under-voltage, and under-frequency to detect
permanent or short-circuit faults.

FFT algorithm is implemented on FPGA to extract high frequency content from measured
signals with 1 MHz rate. The calculated frequency spectrum of a pre-selected time window of
data by FPGA is transferred to the processor through direct memory access (DMA) FIFO in
operational real-time to detect sub-cycle transient faults which usually include significant
amount of high frequency content (1 kHz - 100 kHz). Once a fault is detected, a fault waveform
including ten-cycle before and after the fault initiation is captured with 50 kHz and 1 MHz rates.
The fault event record function is evaluated in the CHIL system and the monitored peak CPU
consumption is less than 60% and the averaged value is less than 25%.

To detect the fast pulse signal for traveling wave fault location, a second-order high-pass
Butterworth filter is implemented on FPGA with 50 kHz frequency cut-off. This is an effective
way to remove low frequency content in the fault signal and a constant threshold (such as 100
V) is applied as a triggering criterion. In the test, two sensors are placed at feeder head and
end, respectively, with 6 km separation. A GPS module is used to synchronize fault waveforms
and the captured waveforms are transferred through a communication network to a central
controller for fault location identification. The tests are repeated 30 times and the estimation
error is always less than 344 m. Figure 12(c) shows the captured waveform when fault 2 occurs.
Thirty tests show that the fault location error is less than 416 m.

The error percentage of the test results is less than 7%. The length of the studied feeder. 6 km,

 III.2.a.3 Islanding Detection
Local measurement-based islanding detection method has been implemented on the sensor
controller. Local measured voltage and frequency are used to detect an islanding event. A 5%
deviation from nominal value is selected as an islanding criterion, and an islanding notification
is sent to the central controller once an island is detected. In the real-time simulator, the test
system is a rural distribution feeder. The simulated system includes over 30 aggregated loads,
over 30 aggregated line segments, and capacitor banks. When an island happens, the local
generation and load mismatch is larger. Thus, large frequency and voltage deviation can be
quickly detected. The total islanding detection time in 30 tests is always less than 50 ms on the
local sensor controller. The event is transmitted to the central controller through a local area
network in a second.

Preliminary synchrophasor-based islanding detection is also prototyped on the intelligent
sensor. Each sensor controller acquires local voltage and current signals with 50 kHz sampling
rate. Standard P class synchrophasor measurement approach is prototyped using single phase
voltage and current signals. All the synchrophasor computation is performed on the FPGA with
an update rate of 10-60 reports per second. Each report includes synchronized time stamp,
phase angle, power flow, ROCOF, etc. Each data packet is transferred to the processor through
DMA FIFO. When an island occurs, the phasor on the island quickly deviates from phasors in the
rest of the grid. Using the same rural test system, the synchrophasor-based approach can
detect island in less than 1 second for 30 tests using a local area network.

III.2.b Vegetation Interaction
The interaction between power lines and vegetation can and has led to major fires. This has
stimulated layers of defense against such events. This sensor has shown it can be a valuable
addition to that defense. It can provide earlier warning than other observation systems.
Measurements in the grid of the Wood County Electric Cooperative showed that the sensor did
detect the same contact that was measured by the substation equipment. Measurements taken
in cooperation with Eaton, however, documented that, when a line fell to the ground at a point
where the local ground had insufficient conductivity to trip breakers, the sensor detected both
the break and the continuing interaction between the line and the ground.

To generate supporting data, the project developed a test facility at UT. The implementation
followed the design guidance outlined in IEEE Std 51-1983 “IEEE Recommended Practices for
Safety in High-Voltage and High-Power Testing”. CEM’s facility is approximately 20’ by 20’. It is
under a covered awning approximately 12’ high and is enclosed by a gated 8’ chain link fence.
The gate to the area features an interlock switch and warning strobe. The fenced MV facility is
inside a second 8’ chain link fenced yard which surrounds the rear side of CEM and has limited
accessibility. For additional safety, the facility is run remotely from an operator console that is
inside the building. A video camera is used to monitor the experiments
An electrical circuit of the test facility is shown below in figure 16. Primary power is provided via
one of CEM’s 480 V ac 3 phase buss ducts. A 400 A circuit breaker provides power to a fused
remote operated contactor. Since the line transformer requires 277 V ac input, a Delta-Wye
transformer was used for conversion. A single 277 V ac phase powers the low voltage winding of
a single phase 167 kVA line transformer to step-up to 12.47 kV. An overhead loop connects the
test line to a step-down transformer. The step-down transformer powers a load resistance, which
represents the nominal load condition. A common ground reference is connected to the cases of
all components.
An integral feature of the facility is the grounded pneumatic lift, figure 17. The lift is used to raise
different potential fault-creating objects, such as a tree branch, into the overhead MV loop. The
lift is controlled remotely using air to make and break contact with the overhead line. Use of
plastic pneumatic tubing insulates the operator from the test voltage. A trough is attached to the
top of the lift to facilitate attaching test objects and to connect to electrical ground.

The sensor has also provided indication of the multiple events that can occur in a gird due to lightning.
For background, lightning is a special case in power system design and analysis. Experience has
shown that lightning strikes on a grid are inevitable. But the location, the size, and the
distributed effect throughout the grid are less well understood. This situation has led to:

 30

 • Standard lightning test waveforms for grid equipment. The highest test voltage
 equipment received to qualify for grid installation is during a standard lightning test. It is
 called “standard” because it is a 1.2 x 50 µ s unipolar pulse specified in standards
 promulgated by the IEEE and the International Electrotechnical Commission.
 • Computer codes optimized for the prediction of the propagation of transients, e.g.,
 lighting impulses through the grid.
 • Development of enhanced techniques, including supervised learning and machine
 learning to predict an imminent lightning strike in a particular location to enhance
 protection.

A missing part in this global effort to reduce lightning-induced outages has been reliable
measurements of the signals with appropriate bandwidth to record salient behavior. Data
provided by this novel sensor deserves special attention because it shows promise in helping to
fill this information gap.

During a thunderstorm on April 20th, 2025, between 5 and 8 a.m., overvoltage, arcing, and
overcurrent signatures were recorded by two sensors on the same phase, Sensor A and Sensor
B. Sensor A was 1 mile from the nearest substation while Sensor B was 2.4 miles from that
substation. These signatures, figures 19 and 20 captured the evolution of fault events from
initial lightning-induced overvoltage to subsequent short circuits that led to multiple outages.

IV. Accomplishments and Conclusions
  Enhancing reliability in our emerging grid requires using the information the grid provides
  about its health that was ignorable in the past. Emerging sensor technology is an emerging key
  part needed in the future. This project showed that the technology is available to support a
  meaningful improvement in grid measurements. Significantly, the sensor was designed, built,
  and demonstrated both in laboratory environments and in grids operated by the Wood County
  Electric Cooperative and by the Guadalupe Valley Electric Cooperative. The laboratory
  assessment consisted of both conventional experiments and assessment using hardware-in-the-
  loop and control-hardware-in-the-loop approaches.

 33

The project showed:

 • The advantage of higher sensitivity and broader bandwidth than are used in today’s
 grid. Those signals contain valuable information about grid health but are unavailable
 today
 • The feasibility of using edge computing to identify different events with a single sensor.
 This included a pathway via assisted learning to exploit artificial intelligence and
 machine learning to enhance grid reliability.
 • The technology readiness of a system that senses, analyzes, and communicates in a
 package appropriate to be mounted on a powerline and powered by that line.
 • This is bridge technology. It detects the events detected by legacy equipment and
 provides vital details of the event that the legacy sensors cannot. In addition, it provides
 valuable information that is not available from legacy sensors.
 • Sensors can economically be located where needed and not concentrated. Sensing,
 computation, and communication are possible with a clamp-on sensor that can be
 installed in ten minutes.

This project met all its SOPO requirements. More importantly, it met them in a way that met
the larger DOE objectives by demonstrating the feasibility of an improved concept for
monitoring the emerging grid. The legacy approach has been to detect failure and repair
quickly. This system opens the door to an innovative approach in which naturally occurring
precursors to failure are often detected in time for system repair or reconfiguration without an
outage.

A critical impediment to rapid commercialization is that this sensor’s major value is that it
measures indicators of grid performance that have not been measurable previously.
Consequently, the utilities have not established procedures to assimilate, process, and act upon
this new data. Industry feedback has led to the insight that the commercialization path is likely
through a few early applications and upgrading applications over time.

This is a cell phone model in which the product is made more valuable as more apps are
available. The initial sensor may include apps for fault location, detection of unintentional
islanding, vegetation interactions, downed lines, and other operational considerations. The
makers of power equipment or third-party vendors can then exploit the platform by developing
apps that enhance reliability by detecting indications of incipient failure in time to schedule
maintenance so failure is avoided.
