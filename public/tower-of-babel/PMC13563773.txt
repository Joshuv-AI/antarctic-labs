Prognostic outcomes of pancreaticoduodenectomy with lymph node dissection in pancreatic and periampullary adenocarcinoma; a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Aylin Taghipour, Seyed Yahya Zarghami, Alireza Fatemi, Yasmin Yazdooei, Fariba Zare
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) is a key surgical treatment for pancreatic and periampullary malignancies, but morbidity remains a challenge. Lymph node dissection improves outcomes by removing cancerous tissue and preventing metastasis. This study aims to assess survival rates, recurrence, and other postoperative outcomes.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on 390 patients who underwent PD with for pancreatic and periampullary adenocarcinoma between 2019 and 2023 at a single tertiary center. Non-adenocarcinoma histologies were excluded to ensure a homogeneous cohort. Follow-up data were available for 299 patients (76.7%), collected through hospital records and structured telephone interviews. Primary outcomes were overall survival, recurrence, and postoperative morbidity. Statistical analyses included Kaplan–Meier survival estimation, Cox proportional hazards modeling, and recurrence-free survival analysis. Fine–Gray competing risk regression and a prognostic nomogram were used for further risk stratification.ResultsAmong the 390 patients, the median overall survival was 3.5 years. The 1- and 3-year survival rates were 69.2% and 50.6%, respectively. The 5-year survival estimate (41.0%) should be considered preliminary, as it is based on extrapolation from immature follow-up data; mature 5-year outcomes require extended observation beyond the current study period. Multivariable analysis identified several key predictors of mortality: age ≥ 65 years (HR: 2.55, p < 0.001), pancreatic tumor origin (HR: 1.58, p = 0.009), positive lymph node status (HR: 2.37, p < 0.001), and fewer than 12 nodes removed (HR: 1.53, p = 0.018). Adjuvant chemotherapy reduced the risk of death (HR: 0.68, p = 0.033). Recurrence-free survival demonstrated the steepest decline during the first 36 months postoperatively.ConclusionLymph node status, chemotherapy, and tumor origin are crucial for predicting survival after PD. Proper lymph node evaluation and adjuvant chemotherapy are essential for improving outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12893-026-03980-3.

Introduction

Pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure) is the standard curative-intent operation for pancreatic and peri-ampullary neoplasms. Initially proposed in 1935, it became the preferred surgery for various cancers in the pancreatic head, ampulla, bile duct, and duodenum [1–3]. While early studies reported high mortality rates leading to surgery abandonment, recent reports show decreased morbidity and mortality rates, although morbidity remains high at around 50% [4, 5]. Common complications include pancreatic fistula, delayed gastric emptying, hemorrhage, anastomosis leakage, and infections, impacting mortality, hospital stays, and costs [6]. Pancreatic adenocarcinoma comprises 90% of pancreatic cancers, ranking seventh globally in cancer-related deaths, with late diagnoses due to asymptomatic progression [7, 8].

Lymph node dissection added to pancreaticoduodenectomy aims to remove occult metastatic deposits, improve staging, and potentially enhance survival. When augmented with lymph node dissection, Pancreaticoduodenectomy becomes a comprehensive approach aimed at excising cancerous tissue along with nearby lymph nodes to impede the dissemination of cancer cells and enhance patient outcomes [9]. The intricate nature of this surgical technique demands a multidisciplinary approach involving skilled surgeons, oncologists, radiologists, and pathologists to ensure optimal patient care and treatment efficacy [10].

Pancreaticoduodenectomy with lymph node dissection is a major advance in surgical oncology, offering a potentially curative option for pancreatic malignancies by removing the primary tumor and involved lymph nodes to eradicate cancer, prevent progression, and improve survival and quality of life [10–12]. Its success depends not only on therapeutic benefits but also on managing postoperative complications and adverse outcomes [10, 13, 14]. Evaluating survival, mortality, morbidity, and recurrence rates is essential for assessing the procedure’s efficacy and safety [15–17]. Postoperative mortality and morbidity reflect immediate outcomes and highlight the need for meticulous perioperative care [18, 19]. Cancer recurrence after surgery remains a significant challenge, requiring close surveillance, adjuvant therapies, and timely interventions to combat disease progression and enhance long-term survival [20, 21].

Against this backdrop, the aim of this study is to comprehensively evaluate survival rates, mortality, morbidity, postoperative complications, and cancer recurrence following pancreaticoduodenectomy with lymph node dissection in patients with pancreatic and periampullary malignancies. By elucidating these critical parameters, this research seeks to inform clinical decision-making, refine treatment protocols, and advance the understanding of surgical and oncologic outcomes in the context of pancreatic cancer management.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective longitudinal study initially identified 390 consecutive patients who underwent pancreaticoduodenectomy, with lymph node dissection for pancreatic and periampullary malignancies at Firouzgar Hospital in Tehran, Iran between 2019 and 2023 through the hospital’s IT database. All pancreaticoduodenectomies followed the institutional protocol, consistent with the ISGPS definition, and included a standard lymphadenectomy. This routine dissection covered peripancreatic nodes around the pancreatic head, nodes along the common bile duct and proper hepatic artery, and nodes along the portal vein and superior mesenteric vein/artery. Extended lymphadenectomy with para-aortic nodal dissection was not routinely performed in this cohort.

For these 390 patients, we first collected all available demographic, surgical, and pathological data from the Hospital Information System (HIS). We then conducted telephone follow-ups to obtain additional outcome data including survival status, recurrence information, and details of adjuvant therapies. From the initial cohort, we successfully completed follow-up data collection for 299 patients (76.7% response rate), which constituted our final study population for analysis.

Data collection process

Data collection was conducted in three sequential phases. In the first phase, baseline demographic characteristics, perioperative variables, and surgical details for all 390 eligible patients were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. The second phase consisted of a detailed review of pathology reports to confirm tumor characteristics, including tumor size recorded as an estimated volume (cm³) calculated from the three orthogonal dimensions regarding American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) guidelines, margin status, lymph node yield, and lymph node involvement.

In the third phase of data collection, follow-up information was obtained through structured telephone interviews with patients or their legally authorized relatives. This interview was specifically developed for the purposes of the present study and has not been previously published or validated elsewhere. The interview focused on objective clinical outcomes, including vital status, date and cause of death, disease recurrence, timing of recurrence, receipt of adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy, and last known follow-up date. Information regarding chemotherapy exposure included whether chemotherapy was received after surgery, as well as the number of chemotherapy sessions administered before and after surgery. Adjuvant chemotherapy consisted of gemcitabine-based regimens; radiotherapy was used selectively for high-risk features such as margin positivity. For analysis, treatment was coded as chemotherapy (yes/no) and radiotherapy (yes/no). To ensure consistency, all interviews followed a predefined structured format. An English-language version of the interview form is provided as “Supplementary file 1.”. This multi-source approach improved data completeness and reduced missing information.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Python (v3.11) and R (v4.3.2), applying appropriate survival and risk modeling packages. Kaplan–Meier methods were used to estimate overall survival and recurrence-free survival (RFS) probabilities. Survival curves were stratified by age group, sex, tumor origin, lymph node status, chemotherapy status, and other clinicopathologic features. The log-rank test was used to evaluate differences between survival distributions, and median survival times were reported along with 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities.

To identify independent predictors of overall survival, a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was fitted. Covariates included age category, tumor site (pancreatic vs. non-pancreatic), lymph node status, number of nodes removed (< 12 vs. ≥ 12), surgical margin status, receipt of adjuvant chemotherapy, and presence of recurrence. Hazard ratios (HRs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and Wald p-values were reported. The threshold of 12 lymph nodes was selected based on AJCC staging guidelines. Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed prior to modeling. Cox regression was also extended to recurrence-free survival as a separate outcome.

To account for competing risks in the estimation of disease-specific mortality, Fine–Gray sub-distribution hazard modeling was performed. Cumulative incidence function (CIF) curves were plotted to evaluate the probability of disease-specific death over time while accounting for non-cancer competing events. These were stratified by combinations of chemotherapy receipt and lymph node involvement, highlighting interaction effects.

A prognostic nomogram was constructed from the final multivariable Cox model to estimate individualized survival probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years. The nomogram integrated weighted contributions from age, tumor origin, lymph node status, lymph node yield, chemotherapy, and recurrence status. Bar plots were generated to illustrate recurrence rates across subgroups defined by tumor origin, lymph node status, and adjuvant chemotherapy. Interaction effects were visualized to highlight potential treatment modification by nodal burden.

All statistical analyses adhered to recommended reporting guidelines including Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) for observational studies and, for the presentation of the prognostic nomogram derived from the multivariable model, we considered relevant items from the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable Prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guideline. Plots and modeling outputs were generated using the lifelines, matplotlib, scikit-learn, and seaborn libraries in Python, and the cmprsk and rms packages in R.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Iran University of Medical Sciences (Approval No. IR.IUMS.FMD.REC.1403.433). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committees and with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). As this study involved retrospective analysis of anonymized clinical data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legally authorized representatives during telephone follow-up interviews. All collected data were anonymized and securely stored to ensure patient confidentiality throughout the study.

Limitations

The primary limitation of this study was the 23.3% loss to follow-up from our initial cohort. Also, immature long-term follow-up, patients operated between 2021 and 2023 have not yet reached 5-year endpoints, making 5-year survival estimates preliminary and subject to change with extended observation. However, our sensitivity analyses demonstrated no statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics between patients with complete versus incomplete follow-up data, suggesting that this attrition was unlikely to have substantially biased our results. Additionally, the single-center nature of this study may affect the generalizability of our findings to other clinical settings. Our pooled analyses across pancreatic and non-pancreatic tumors may obscure important site-specific differences. We have therefore emphasized stratified and adjusted results where possible. Furthermore, only 5 patients had positive resection margins, limiting statistical power; thus, findings on margin status should be interpreted cautiously. Recurrence information was extracted from two sources: follow-up visits to our institution’s outpatient clinic (using imaging reports, pathology records, and oncologic clinic notes whenever available) and structured telephone interviews with patients or their legally authorized relatives. This introduces a potential recall bias, as patients or families may misreport treatment history or time of recurrence when telephone interviews are used as the primary source for recurrence status and adjuvant therapy details, particularly when systematic imaging review or complete oncology records are unavailable.

Results

Patient enrollment and cohort characteristics

Between January 2019 and December 2023, a total of 390 patients undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy as Whipple procedure, with lymph node dissection for pancreatic or periampullary malignancies were identified at Firouzgar Hospital. Among these, 299 patients (76.7%) completed follow-up through hospital records and structured telephone interviews and were included in the final analytic cohort. A flowchart of patient inclusion is shown in Fig. 1. There were no statistically significant differences in baseline demographics or clinical characteristics between patients who completed follow-up and those who were lost (p > 0.05), minimizing selection bias and preserving external validity.

The median age of the cohort was 59 years, with 62.9% male and 37.1% female. Most tumors originated in the pancreas (44.5%), followed by ampullary, biliary, and duodenal origins. Adjuvant chemotherapy was administered in approximately two-thirds of patients, and more than half underwent resection of at least 12 lymph nodes.

Overall survival outcomes

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated a median overall survival of 3.5 years (Fig. 2A), with 1-, and 3- survival rates of 69.2%, 50.6%, respectively. The 5-year survival estimate (41.0%) is preliminary and based on extrapolation from immature follow-up data; mature 5-year outcomes require extended observation beyond the current study period. These survival probabilities are further detailed in Table 1, which presents time-specific survival rates across clinical subgroups. In addition, Table 2 shows pooled mean and median survival times stratified by age, sex, tumor characteristics, and nodal involvement. For instance, patients aged ≤ 53 years and those with non-pancreatic tumors showed the most favorable survival, whereas patients ≥ 65 years and those with involved margins had notably poorer outcomes.

Stratified survival analyses

Survival outcomes varied significantly across age groups (Fig. 2B). Patients aged ≥ 65 years had significantly lower survival at all time points (Table 2), with multivariable analysis confirming an increased mortality risk (HR: 2.55; 95% CI: 1.63–3.98; p < 0.001). Sex showed no significant survival difference (Fig. 2C; Table 2), and Cox regression analysis reinforced the lack of independent association (p = 0.204).

Tumor origin was a strong predictor of survival. Patients with non-pancreatic tumors experienced superior survival probabilities compared to those with pancreatic adenocarcinoma (Fig. 2D), with a 58% increased hazard of death for pancreatic tumors (HR: 1.58; p = 0.009; Table 3). Tumor size and local invasion did not significantly influence survival in the adjusted model (Table 3), though minor differences were observed in univariate estimates (Table 2).

Lymph node and chemotherapy effects

Lymph node-related variables had strong prognostic value. Node-positive patients had significantly worse outcomes (Fig. 2E), with more than double the hazard of death compared to node-negative counterparts (HR: 2.37; p < 0.001; Table 3). Patients with < 12 lymph nodes removed had lower survival (Table 2), and multivariable analysis confirmed increased mortality risk in this group (HR: 1.53; p = 0.018). These patterns are echoed in Table 1, where node-negative patients had markedly longer mean and median survival times.

Adjuvant chemotherapy was significantly protective, with treated patients showing improved long-term survival (Fig. 2F), reflected in both survival percentages (Table 2) and hazard ratios (HR: 0.68; p = 0.033; Table 3). In contrast, radiotherapy and comorbidity status did not emerge as independent predictors.

Recurrence-free survival patterns

RFS analysis revealed a steep decline during the first 24–36 months postoperatively (Fig. 3A), highlighting a critical window for recurrence detection. RFS was more favorable among patients with non-pancreatic tumors, node-negative status, and those who received adjuvant chemotherapy (Figs. 3B-D), mirroring patterns in overall survival.

Based on the Cox model, a nomogram was created to estimate 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival probabilities (Fig. 6). It was internally validated using bootstrap resampling to correct for potential overfitting. External validation in independent cohorts will be required before clinical application.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of survival and recurrence outcomes following Pancreaticoduodenectomy with lymph node dissection in patients with pancreatic and periampullary malignancies. Our findings reinforce the prognostic importance of several clinicopathologic features, particularly lymph node status, extent of lymphadenectomy, tumor origin, and the receipt of adjuvant chemotherapy.

Consistent with prior studies, lymph node involvement emerged as a robust predictor of poor overall survival and RFS in our cohort. Patients with positive lymph nodes had significantly lower survival rates, a trend also observed in large multicenter cohorts and surveillance, epidemiology, and end results-based studies, which demonstrate that increasing numbers of positive lymph nodes and lymph node ratio (LNR) are strongly associated with mortality and recurrence risk in pancreatic cancer [22–24]. In particular, Goess et al. [25] reported that examining fewer than 21 lymph nodes may lead to nodal understaging, while Malleo et al. [26] emphasized the prognostic impact of metastases to stations 13, 14, and the jejunal mesentery. These data align with our finding that removal of fewer than 12 lymph nodes conferred significantly worse outcomes, suggesting that a more thorough lymphadenectomy is crucial for accurate staging and improved prognosis.

The benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy was another key finding in our study. Patients who received chemotherapy demonstrated significantly improved long-term survival, which remained an independent protective factor in the multivariable model. This aligns with findings by Frakes et al. [27], who reported improved outcomes in elderly patients receiving adjuvant therapy after pancreatic cancer resection, and by Khedr [28] and Ma et al. [29], who similarly highlighted the value of chemotherapy in high-risk periampullary carcinomas. In a related but distinct aspect of nodal assessment, Aoyama et al. [30] identified lymph node ratio as an important prognostic indicator in patients undergoing adjuvant treatment. Our results are consistent with the notion that nodal burden and adjuvant therapy together influence long-term outcomes.

The role of adjuvant chemotherapy in node‑negative (early‑stage) pancreatic adenocarcinoma remains debated. One study found benefit only in patients with pathologic risk factors such as perineural or lymphovascular invasion {Zou, 2024 #70}, whereas another reported benefit for all stage I patients {Guenther, 2022 #71}. In our cohort, node‑negative patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy had lower recurrence and disease‑specific death (Figs. 5C and 7). Given the lack of further risk stratification in our data, we cannot resolve this discrepancy, but our findings suggest a potential benefit that warrants prospective validation with detailed pathologic subtyping.

Tumor origin also played a significant role in shaping outcomes. Patients with non-pancreatic tumors, especially ampullary and duodenal malignancies, had superior survival compared to those with pancreatic adenocarcinoma. Our data align with reports by Kim et al. [31] and Memon et al. [32], which have consistently shown that ampullary tumors are associated with better prognosis following resection. Zakaria et al. [33] further confirmed that ampullary cancers had higher 5-year survival (42.1%) compared to pancreatic tumors (24.3%), a pattern replicated in our subgroup analysis. However, grouping all non-pancreatic periampullary tumors together may obscure distinct prognostic differences among ampullary, distal bile duct, and duodenal subtypes, which could not be examined separately due to limited sample sizes.

Because pancreatic and non-pancreatic periampullary adenocarcinomas have inherently different prognoses, all survival and recurrence analyses were either stratified by tumor origin (Tables 1 and 2; Figs. 2D and 3B) or adjusted for tumor origin in multivariable models (Table 3; Fig. 4). Pooled estimates for the entire cohort are presented for descriptive purposes only and should be interpreted with caution, as outcomes vary substantially by primary tumor site. Clinically, risk stratification and treatment decisions should be guided by site-specific prognostic factors.

Recurrence dynamics were particularly noteworthy. We observed a steep decline in RFS during the first 36 months after surgery, consistent with findings from Chen et al. [34] and Aoyama et al. [30], who identified perineural and lymphovascular invasion as strong predictors of early recurrence. Notably, Klein et al. [35] reported long-term recurrence patterns in ampullary carcinoma, reinforcing the importance of extended follow-up even in patients with favorable initial pathology.

The extent and type of lymphadenectomy remain an area of ongoing debate. While some trials, such as the Cochrane meta-analysis by Staerkle et al. [16], found no survival benefit for extended lymphadenectomy, others such as Lei et al. [36], demonstrated that dissection based on the TRIANGLE concept can delay tumor progression without increasing morbidity. Our study contributes to this discourse by showing that while extended lymphadenectomy did not increase immediate complications, it was associated with improved survival when ≥ 12 nodes were removed. This supports prior conclusions that node retrieval is a quality metric for oncologic surgery [22, 25, 26]. Nevertheless, our use of the AJCC‑recommended 12‑node threshold, rather than higher thresholds (e.g., 15–21 nodes) advocated by some recent studies, may have led to under-staging in a subset of patients.

The role of LNR has gained traction as a nuanced prognostic marker. Several studies have validated LNR > 0.2 as an independent predictor of decreased overall survival and RFS [29, 30]. Our analysis supports this, as higher nodal burden directly correlated with recurrence and mortality. The use of LNR may enhance risk stratification beyond simple node positivity.

Additionally, emerging evidence suggests that other pathological features such as tumor size, vascular invasion, and perineural spread can further refine prognosis. For instance, Zakaria et al. [33] and Khedr [28] identified tumor size > 3 cm and lymphovascular invasion as significant predictors of poor long-term survival. These variables, although not independently significant in our multivariable model, exhibited consistent trends across univariate analyses.

Finally, the patient-level heterogeneity in outcomes, visualized through decision tree analysis, illustrates the complexity of post-Whipple trajectories. Some patients with adverse pathological features achieved long-term survival, while others with low-risk tumors experienced early recurrence. This highlights the potential value of integrating nomogram-based tools and individualized risk stratification strategies [26]. A substantial proportion of patients did not receive adjuvant chemotherapy, a factor influenced by older age, comorbidities, postoperative complications delaying recovery, patient preference, and limited access to oncology services in a subset of cases.

Our findings reinforce the central role of lymph node assessment, adjuvant chemotherapy, and tumor biology in shaping postoperative outcomes in pancreatic and periampullary malignancies. Adequate lymphadenectomy (≥ 12–21 nodes), vigilant follow-up for high-risk groups, and the strategic use of chemotherapy are key pillars of optimized care. Future prospective studies should aim to refine lymphadenectomy protocols, validate nomogram tools, and integrate molecular predictors to further personalize treatment.

As a single‑center study, the generalizability of our findings is limited. Institutional factors, including surgical volume, surgeon experience, perioperative pathways, extent of lymphadenectomy, and local adjuvant therapy protocols, may influence survival and recurrence patterns. These center‑specific characteristics may differ from multicenter or population‑based settings and should be considered when interpreting the applicability of our results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study provides valuable insights into the prognostic factors influencing survival after pancreaticoduodenectomy with lymph node dissection for pancreatic and periampullary malignancies. The findings underscore the significance of lymph node status, chemotherapy, and tumor origin as key factors affecting both overall survival and recurrence-free survival. Additionally, thorough lymph node evaluation and adjuvant chemotherapy should be considered crucial components of post-surgical management. Future research should continue to explore the optimal extent of lymphadenectomy and the role of emerging therapies in improving long-term survival and reducing recurrence in these patients.
