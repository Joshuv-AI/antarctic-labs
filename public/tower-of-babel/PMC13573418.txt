A multilevel analysis of the factors associated with the use of different types of modern contraceptive methods: permanent methods, LARCs, MARCs, and SARCs, among married or in-union Nigerian women with a demand for family planning
Authors: Emomine Odjesa, Friday Ebhodaghe Okonofua, Lorretta Favour Ntoimo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the factors associated with the use of different modern contraceptives (MCs) by Nigerian married or in-union women of reproductive age with a demand for family planning (DFP). These MCs are permanent methods and long-acting reversible contraceptives (PLARCs), medium-acting reversible contraceptives (MARCs), and short-acting reversible contraceptives (SARCs).MethodsAn unweighted sample size of 9,983 women nested in a total of 1,118 communities was extracted from the 2024 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey. A multilevel multivariable multinomial logistic regression analysis was carried out using Stata 17.0. Two levels of analysis were utilised: the individual and the community level, with the accompanying variables adjusted for.ResultsThe DFP satisfied by modern methods (DFPSM) was approximately 33.9%, with the DFP satisfied by PLARCs, MARCs, and SARCs estimated as approximately 17.1%, 9.1%, and 7.8%, respectively. We found large remaining significant between-community variances in the odds of women satisfying their DFP with the different MCs. This was shown by the various estimated median odd ratios for the DFP satisfied by PLARCs, MARCs, and SARCs: approximately 3.870, 4.830, and 4.532, respectively. These remaining between-community variances were of greater relevance than the associated effects of most of the adjusted individual and household-level variables. This was with the exception of only the regression effects on DFP satisfied with MARCs of women in households with 1 to 2 dejure children at most 5 years old (aOR = 5.514, 95%CI = 2.226 to 13.658, p < 0.001); and those in households with no children at most 5 years old (aOR = 9.359, 95%CI = 3.656 to 23.954, p < 0.001). However, other variables that significantly increased the use of the different types of MCs by women with a DFP were their formal education level and women being usual residents of communities with high age at first marriage/cohabitation, low fertility clusters, and low polygyny clusters.ConclusionTo increase the very low DFPSM in Nigeria using different types of MCs, government policies/interventions aimed at improving the educational level of girls beyond the primary level should be implemented. A greater concentration should be within communities with low age at first marriage/cohabitation, high polygyny, and high fertility clusters.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04595-9.Keywords: Nigeria, SDG indicator 3.7.1, Modern contraceptives, Demand for family planning, Demand for family planning satisfied by modern methods, Permanent methods, Long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs), Medium-acting reversible contraceptives (MARCs), Short-acting reversible contraceptives (SARCs), Multilevel multivariable multinomial logistic model

Background

The United Nations (UN) Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) has the percentage of women of reproductive age (WRA) with demand for family planning satisfied by modern methods (DFPSM) as the only indicator representing modern contraceptive use out of all the indicators listed [1]. The DFPSM is the first indicator used to represent the 7th target of the 3rd SDG: that is, the SDG indicator 3.7.1 [1]. This is thus used in monitoring progress towards the achievement of SDG target 3.7, which aims to eliminate unmet needs for family planning, and in turn, the achievement of the 3rd SDG [1, 2]. The latter aims to achieve health and well-being for all ages by 2030 [1, 2]. The demand for family planning (DFP), on the other hand, represents the total need for family planning (NFP), that is, the sum of the need for spacing births and the need for limiting births [3]. These needs could either be met with or satisfied by the use of contraceptives (either modern or non-modern methods), making up the met need for family planning, or not met at all with contraceptives, making up the unmet need for family planning [3].

Given the diverse health and socioeconomic benefits of family planning [4], together with the fact that modern contraceptives (MCs) are mostly relatively more effective than non-modern methods [5], the goal should therefore be to increase the DFPSM. A benchmark of at least 75% has been suggested to be reached by 2030 in all countries [6]. In the past decade, most regions of the world have experienced an increase in the DFPSM, with sub–Saharan Africa experiencing the greatest increase from 51.6% to 57.9% in 2015 and 2025, respectively [7]. However, data from previous and the current Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) show that the DFPSM has been consistently very low in Nigeria since its value has always been less than 50% [8–14]. For example, according to the 2024 NDHS, only 39.7% of WRA had their DFP met with the use of MCs compared to 35.7% of WRA, as estimated by the 2018 NDHS [13, 14]. Nigeria is thus not on track in terms of meeting the stated 75% benchmark by 2030, signifying the dire need for an increase in the DFPSM.

However, there are different types of MCs with different characteristics that are considered important to the users, clinicians, and policy makers [5]. These characteristics include the effectiveness level, length of contraceptive action, cost-effectiveness, ease of reversibility of the contraceptive action once the specific method is discontinued, and clients ability to discontinue the use without the need of a medical provider [5]. In recognition of this, Ewerling et al. [15] subdivided the contraceptive indicator ‘DFPSM’ into the three categories. These are: demand for family planning satisfied (DFPS) with permanent methods, DFPS with long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs), and DFPS with short-acting reversible contraceptives (SARCs). Additionally, Festin et al. [5], in their classification of modern contraceptive methods, included a subclass called the ‘medium-acting reversible contraceptives (MARCs)’.

No single modern contraceptive method can thus serve the family planning needs of each individual and every subgroup within the population [16]. Therefore, an increase in DFPSM can be achieved through an increase in the use of different types of MCs [16]. This can be done through understanding the possible factors that influence the DFPS with the different types of MCs. Furthermore, according to Merlo [17], health outcomes are often contextual in nature, with resulting between- and within-context/cluster differences/variations in the likelihood of the outcome variable occurring [18–20].

Against this backdrop, the aim of this study is to empirically investigate the individual, household, and community-level factors associated with the DFPS with permanent methods, the DFPS with LARCs, the DFPS with MARCs, and the DFPS with SARCs among married or in-union WRA in Nigeria. This study utilised data from the 2024 NDHS, which is the latest cycle of NDHS. Additionally, we aim to determine the extent of the between-community variances, and hence the within-community variances, in the odds/likelihood of the married or in-union WRA satisfying their DFP with the different types of MCs and how these between-community variances compare with the effects of the associated factors.

Similar empirical studies have been carried out, both inside and outside of Nigeria, among WRA with a DFP [3, 15, 21–25], including a recent publication by Odjesa and Okonofua [26] using data from the 2018 NDHS. However, none of these studies, including that by Odjesa and Okonofua [26], have empirically investigated the factors associated with the use of different types of MCs, including MARCs, among women with a DFP [3, 15, 21–26]. Research thus needs to move beyond the dichotomy of using (modern) contraceptives or not and instead consider women’s different family planning needs and different contraceptive methods. Therefore, in terms of the Nigerian setting, further inferential statistical analysis is needed, with this study aiming to fill these gaps, as clearly stated by its aims.

Findings from this study will provide empirical evidence that will help in identifying not only individual women but also the clusters (that is, the households and communities) that should be targeted by family planning policies and programs. This is particularly important in Nigeria, as she is considered an ethnically, culturally, legally, religiously, and economically pluralistic society [14, 27].

Methods

Conceptual framework

This study’s conceptual framework is based on the adaptation of Bronfenbrenner’s sociocultural framework by McLeroy et al. [28]. According to this adapted framework, health outcomes are affected by the following hierarchical levels of influence: (1) the intrapersonal level, which reflects the personal characteristics of the individual; (2) the interpersonal level, which reflects both informal and formal social interactions among individuals; (3) the institutional level, which reflects organised institutions operating with either formal or informal rules and regulations; (4) the community level, which reflects relationships among individuals, organisations, or institutions operating within the confines of defined geographical, administrative, or residential boundaries; and (5) the public policy level, which reflects national, state, and local laws and policies. Further details about the sociocultural framework can be found in the literature [28].

Source of data

This study utilised cross-sectional data obtained from the current NDHS, which is the 2024 NDHS [14]. Data for this study was got from merging the individual recode (IR) and the household member or persons recode (PR) files. The IR file contains information on the demographic, socioeconomic, and reproductive characteristics of WRA, including that of their husbands or partners for those married or in-union [14]. The PR file contains information on household members’ (including the head of households) and households’ characteristics [14].

Data for the 2024 NDHS were collected using a stratified two-stage sampling technique [14]. Stratification was done by separating the 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory (FCT) of Nigeria into rural and urban areas, thereby generating 74 sampling strata [14]. Using a two-stage sampling technique, samples were then independently selected from each stratum. For the first sampling stage, a total of 1,400 primary sampling units (PSUs) or clusters were selected from the 74 strata: 699 in rural areas and 701 in urban areas. These PSUs were the enumeration areas (EAs) used as demarcation areas for Nigeria’s upcoming census [14]. Within each sampling stratum, the PSUs were selected with probability proportional to their sizes, with their sizes represented by the number of households within them [14].

On the other hand, for the second sampling stage, households were selected, following listing of all the households within each selected cluster. Using an equal probability selection process, a fixed number of 30 households were selected from each EA, giving a total of 42,000 households [14]. The 2024 NDHS was however successfully done in 40,047 households and 1,380 EAs, with information collected from a total of 39,050 women of reproductive age [14]. Thus, the 2024 NDHS has a hierarchical data structure with lower level units nested within that of higher levels [14]. Additional details about the 2024 NDHS sampling structure can be got from the survey report [14]. In addition, the 2024 NDHS dataset is freely accessible online from the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) website upon a written approval given in response to a written request made by the authors [29]. No further ethical clearance was thus needed.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Based on the aims of this study, married or in-union WRA with a need to space or limit births and who were also usual residents of the communities and households in which they were interviewed were selected. This represents this study’s analytical sample. Note that the PSUs or clusters of the 2024 NDHS were used in this study to proxy communities, following other empirical studies [3, 15, 21–26]. Measures

Dependent or outcome variable

In this study, the outcome or dependent variable is the type of MCs (or lack thereof) used by the married or in-union WRA (or their husbands/partners) with a DFP. It is a nominal categorical variable with the following five possible subcategories: (a) DFP met with women’s current use (or their husbands’/partners’ current use) of non-modern methods or not met with any contraceptive method at all, be it modern or non-modern. This subcategory is referred to as ‘non-DFPSM’, coded as ‘0’; (b) DFP met with women’s current use (or their husbands’/partners’ current use) of permanent methods. This subcategory is referred to as the ‘DFPS with permanent methods’, coded as ‘1’. (c) DFP met with women’s current use of LARCs. This subcategory is referred to as the ‘DFPS with LARCs’, coded as ‘2’; (d) DFP met with women’s current use of MARCs. This subcategory is referred to as the ‘DFPS with MARCs’, coded as ‘3’; and (e) DFP met with women’s current use (or their husbands’/partners’ current use) of SARCs. This subcategory is referred to as the ‘DFPS with SARCs’, coded as ‘4’. The term ‘current’ refers to information reported at the time of data collection.

Furthermore, contraceptive methods were classified into modern and non-modern methods following Austad et al. [33] and Hubacher and Trussell [34]. According to these authors, MCs are listed as follows: female sterilisation, male sterilisation, implants, intrauterine devices (IUDs), injectables, emergency contraception, daily contraceptive pills, female condoms, male condoms, vaginal rings, caps, diaphragms, and spermicidal agents such as foam/sponge, cream, and jelly [33, 34]. Therefore, contraceptive methods outside of these methods are classified as ‘non-modern methods’ [33, 34]. These methods include the withdrawal method, lactational amenorrhoea method (LAM), and periodic abstinence, including all fertility awareness-based methods (FAMs), such as the standard days method (SDM), basal body temperature methods, the two-day method, and the symptom-thermal method [33, 34].

According to the 2024 NDHS, there are folkloric methods such as amulets, beads, herbs, and douching that are also used by women with an NFP [13]. These folkloric methods, however, do not have a known scientific effectiveness level [35]. Thus, the users of these methods were grouped in this study with those not using any contraceptive method at all. Additionally, women who used non-modern methods and those who did not use any contraceptive method at all were grouped together in this study as subcategory ‘0’ since the focus of this study is on increasing the DFPSM using different types of MCs. Furthermore, following Festin et al. [5], we subsequently subdivided these modern contraceptive methods into the following: permanent methods, LARCs, MARCs, and SARCs. The permanent methods are male and female sterilisation; LARCs are IUDs and implants; MARCs are injectables; and SARCs represent the other remaining modern methods.

Independent or explanatory variables

The independent variables used in this study were all categorical: demographic variables, households’ characteristics, economic variables, reproductive health variables, and cultural and religious variables. These variables were divided across different hierarchical levels. All the independent variables used in this study were obtained from both the empirical literature review and the conceptual framework for this study.

A variable representing communities within states with sociocultural and religious laws (CRLs) in operation or not was also included in this study as an independent variable. Two CRLs were considered in this study: Sharia law and the non-domestication of the Child Rights Act (CRA) by states within Nigeria [27, 36]. These factors have all been implicated in undermining the sexual and reproductive health (SRH) of women [27].

In Nigeria, under the civil law, the CRA, which was adopted in 2003, establishes the minimum age of marriage for both sexes as 18 years [27, 36]. However, considering Nigeria’s pluralistic legal structure, the individual states must incorporate this act into their legislation in order for it to be applicable within the states [27, 36]. According to Akintola [36], 11 states, all within the northern parts of Nigeria, are yet to pass the CRA: Kebbi, Jigawa, Kano, Borno, Bauchi, Yobe, Adamawa, Gombe, Sokoto, Zamfara, and Katsina states. In addition, the Nigerian states with Sharia law in operation are Kaduna state and all 11 states mentioned above that have not passed the CRA [27]. Therefore, a total of 12 states, all within the northern part of Nigeria, have the CRLs considered in this study in operation.

Type of empirical model

Since the dependent variable is a nominal categorical variable, while still considering the aims of this study together with the use of the NDHS data with its hierarchical/multilevel/nested data structure, the model of choice was the mixed effect multilevel (two-level) multivariable multinomial logistic regression model. This is referred to simply as the multilevel multivariable multinomial logistic model, hereafter. Further details about multilevel analysis and its importance can be obtained from the literature [17, 19, 20, 37].

The two levels of analysis utilised in this study were the community level (representing level 2) and the individual level (representing level 1). The level 2 units were the communities, and the level 1 units were the individual women, with the latter nested within the former. Equations for this model type can be found in the literature [38, 39]. Level 1 thus contained individual and household-level factors or characteristics, while level 2 contained community-level factors or characteristics. Note that household level was not included in this study as a distinct level of analysis, since from the stated inclusion criteria, most households contained only one eligible woman. This will thus hinder the estimation of within and between-household variation in the likelihood of the outcome variable occurring. In this study, household-level factors were thus also included in level 1.

Therefore, on adapting this study’s conceptual framework, level 1 contained factors at the interpersonal level such as the age of the WRA and at the intrapersonal level such as women’s decision-making power regarding their own health. In this study, factors at the household level were also classified as operating at the interpersonal level. On the other hand, level 2 contained factors at the institutional level with the variable ‘community level of visitation to health facility by women in the last 12 months’ used to proxy availability of a health facility within the community. Level 2 also contained factors at the community level such as community median age at first marriage/cohabitation for women and at the policy level such as presence of CRLs or not.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses, including data preprocessing and model estimation, were conducted using Stata version 17.0. A two-sided significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied unless stated otherwise. Fixed effects from the regression models are reported as odds ratios (ORs) and adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with corresponding standard errors (SEs), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p values. On the other hand, the random intercept variances (RIVs), which are random effects, were presented as coefficients with accompanying 95% CI. Other random effects estimated were the covariances, with results presented as coefficients with accompanying 95% CI and p values.

Data preprocessing

As part of a preliminary data preparation, the appropriate level weights, which are the level-2 weights for level-2 units and the level-1 weights for level-1 units, were approximated following the instructions of Elkasabi et al. [40]. The application of level weights helps adjust for the under- and oversampling performed by the DHS team at various sampling stages, together with the different nonresponse rates at various sampling stages [40]. Further details on the estimation of level weights and the need for its application in multilevel analysis can be obtained from the literature [40].

Furthermore, still as part of the preliminary data preparation stage, community-level variables, which were mostly not directly available from the 2024 NDHS dataset, were estimated by aggregating the available individual-level variables up to the community level. To estimate these community-level variables, the cut-off values used were estimated from all the women sampled, irrespective of their marital status, by the 2024 NDHS. These values were referred to in this study as the national percentage value (NPV) or the national median value (NMV). These NPVs or NMVs were then used as cut-off points to create binary community-level variables from this study’s analytical sample. In this study, following other researchers [24, 25], using binary community-level variables ensured that adequate sample size were present within each cluster, thereby generating reliable regression estimates. Additionally, it allowed for the avoidance of complexity in the interpretation of community-level variables, while still reflecting important contextual variation between communities.

For example, in estimating the community-level variable ‘community median age at first marriage for women’, the median age at first marriage was first estimated from all the women sampled by the 2024 NDHS, irrespective of their marital status. This gave the NMV. Afterwards, from this study’s analytical sample, the median age at first marriage for the women was then estimated for each community. Those communities with women having a median age at first marriage of less than the NMV were described as having a low median age at first marriage, whereas communities with a value at least greater than the NMV were described as having a high median age at first marriage.

Independent variable selection process

According to Bursac et al. [41], the inclusion of all of the possibly relevant independent variables in the model to control for cofounding can lead to the generation of large SEs, together with an associated increased risk of having a type II error, and numerically unstable regression estimates. A purposeful independent variable selection process is thus needed [41]. In this study, four different independent variable selection steps were used, including correlation testing, as adapted from the literature [41–43]. Following the fourth independent variable selection step, the final selected independent variables, both at the individual and community levels, were thus obtained. An additional file contains details about the independent variable selection process carried out in this study [see Supplementary Material 1].

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all the final selected independent variables across the five different subcategories of the dependent variable to summarise the characteristics of the women in this study’s analytical dataset. This was done using the number of observations and percentages.

Inferential statistical analysis: multilevel model-building process

Following Sommet and Morselli [19], first, the empty or null model (Model A) was built to test the needfulness of carrying out a multilevel analysis using the estimated variances of the community-level residuals, that is, the RIVs obtained from the null model. After confirming the necessity of carrying out a multilevel analysis from Model A, the other intermediate multivariable models (Models B and model C) were subsequently built until the final model, Model D, was reached. Model B adjusted for only the final selected individual-level variables, whereas Model C had only the final selected community-level variables. On the other hand, Model D adjusted for both the final selected individual and community-level variables. Building the subsequent models allowed for the estimation of the percentage of the total unexplained variance at the community level in Model A that was explained, and otherwise not also explained, by the adjusted independent variables in each subsequent model. The statistical measure used for this estimation is known as the proportional change in variance (PCV) at the community level [17].

To further explain the general contextual effect, that is, the between-community variability in the odds/likelihood of the outcome variable occurring, as opposed to not occurring, other estimated measures are also used. These are the intracluster correlation coefficient (ICC)/variance partition coefficient (VPC) and median odds ratio (MOR). Further details about ICC, VPC, and MOR, including statistical formulas and calculations, can be found in the literature [19, 44–47]. Other supplemental statistical measures, such as 80% interval odd ratios (IORs) and the proportion of opposed odd ratios (POORs), were used to explain in more detail the fixed regression effect of the community-level variables in only the final model, Model D. This concentration on only the final model was done since the policy implications were based on this model. Further details about 80% IORs and POORs, including the statistical formulas and calculations, can be found in the literature [37, 44, 45]. Finally, a goodness-of-fit test using the adjusted Wald test was performed [48]. The adjusted Wald test estimates the goodness-of-fit for a relatively fuller model compared to that for a constrained model [48].

As shown in Table 1, the DFPSM was estimated at approximately 33.9%, which is considered very low since it is less than 50%. However, LARCs were the most used MCs among women with a DFP (approximately 16.2%), while permanent methods were the least used methods (approximately 0.9%). Table 1 also shows the distribution of the individual, household, and community-level factors among the study sample. Clear gradient exists for the different independent variables across the total study sample and across the different types of MCs, as shown in Table 1. Inferential statistical analysis is however needed to establish whether all these possible associations are real effects or if they occurred by chance.

Inferential statistics: multilevel models

Note that before the inferential statistical analysis was performed, a decision was made to combine permanent method users with LARC users, hereafter referred to as ‘PLARCs’. This was done to avoid the problems of small cell frequencies, for example, values of zero or close to zero, when cross-tabulation of the independent variables across the various subcategories of the dependent variable was carried out. The combination of these modern contraceptive methods, which are permanent methods and LARCs, is possible because, according to Festin et al. [5], both contraceptive methods are within the same tier/level of effectiveness. This is the level 1 or the top-tier effectiveness level [5]. However, permanent methods are only used to limit births, whereas LARCs are used to limit and space births [49]. Therefore, the dependent variable for this study was made up of four subcategories: (1) non-DFPSM, coded as ‘0’; (2) DFPS with PLARCs, coded as ‘1’; (3) DFPS with MARCs, coded as ‘2’; and (4) DFPS with SARCs, coded as ‘3’.

Nevertheless, a sensitivity analysis was conducted with a sub dataset in which all the permanent users were eliminated. This sub dataset was made up of 9,873 WRA with a DFP. These women were usual residents of 1,113 communities. The minimum and maximum numbers of women within the communities were 5 and 24, respectively, with an average size of approximately 10. Therefore, with this sub dataset, the dependent variable was made up of the following four subcategories: (1) non-DFPSM, coded as ‘0’; (2) DFPS with LARCs, coded as ‘1’; (3) DFPS with MARCs, coded as ‘2’; and (4) DFPS with SARCs, coded as ‘3’. The same final selected independent variables were also adjusted for in the sensitivity analysis.

The Wald test results in Tables 3 and 4, and 5 indicate that the inclusion of the additional variables in each of the successive models, each in comparison to the corresponding previous model, significantly improved the fit of each successive model. Therefore, Model D was the final chosen model.

Using results presented in Table 5, comparisons can be made between the fixed regression effects of all the individual and household-levels independent variables adjusted for in Model D and the MOR estimates, since they are all measured on the OR scale. Regarding this comparison, the estimated MOR was 3.870 (the inverse of this quantity is 0.258) in relation to the odds of the WRA having their DFPS with PLARCs, as opposed to either not using any contraceptive method or using non-modern method. From Table 5, the aORs for the fixed regression effects of all the individual and household-level independent variables adjusted for in Model D in relation to the use of PLARCs were within the range of 0.258 to 3.870. This means that the remaining between-community variation in the odds of the WRA having their DFPS with PLARCs that was not accounted for by the independent variables adjusted for in Model D, as measured by the MOR = 3.870, was of greater relevance than the associated effects of all the adjusted individual and household-level variables in this model. The same finding, as seen from Model D in Table 5, was obtained following comparisons of the fixed regression effects of all the individual and household-levels independent variables in relation to the use of SARCs by women with a DFP.

However, in relation to the use of MARCs by women with a DFP, from the regression effects of all the individual and household-levels independent variables adjusted for in Model D, as seen from Table 5, only that of one household-level variable was of greater relevance than the large residual between-community variation. This was the variable representing women in households with 1 to 2 dejure children aged at most 5 years (aOR = 5.514, 95%CI = 2.226 to 13.658, p < 0.001); and those in households with no children 5 years and under (aOR = 9.359, 95%CI = 3.656 to 23.954, p < 0.001). This is each in comparison to households with at least 5 dejure children that are at most 5 years old. This fixed effect of this household-level variable is said to represent the average regression effect across all the communities, that is, irrespective of any specific community.

Nevertheless, from Model D, there were other individual-and household-level variables that significantly increased the likelihood of using the different types of MCs, as opposed to either not using any contraceptive method or using non-modern method, as shown in Table 5. For example, the odds of women using PLARCs to meet their DFP was significantly higher for those formally educated compared to those without formal education. The odds increased as the level of formal education improved from primary to secondary and then to tertiary education level, holding all other fixed effects and the random effects constant. Further details can be found in Table 5.

Fixed regression effects of community-level variables

From the fixed effect of the community-level variables in Model D, holding all other fixed effects and the random effects constant, it was observed that there was a significantly increased odds of the WRA with a DFP using PLARCs by a factor of 1.633 when they are resident in communities with high median age at first marriage for women compared with those in communities with low median age at first marriage/cohabitation (aOR = 1.633, 95%CI = 1.177 to 2.264, p = 0.003). This was in opposition to women either not using any contraceptive method or using non-modern methods. This regression effect is the overall average aOR for the community-level variable ‘community median age at first marriage/cohabitation’.

Note that in the interpretation of this community-level fixed regression effect, the opposing communities have been compared. That is, communities with high median age at first marriage/cohabitation and those with low median age at first marriage/cohabitation. However, for this interpretation, since the random effects are also held constant, both of these communities are said to have the same community-level or level-2 residual values [44, 45]. This implies having the same unobserved/unmeasured characteristics that make the different community-specific intercepts for the different communities differ from the overall intercept. It therefore follows that the interpretation of the community-level fixed regression effect in a multilevel analysis is for women within the same community so as to maintain the same unobserved/unmeasured characteristics [44, 45].

This is said to create difficulty in the interpretation of the fixed regression effect of the community-level variables in multilevel analysis [44, 45]. This is because women within the same community share the same community-level characteristics, as seen from the community-level variables adjusted for in the multilevel model [44, 45]. Supplemental statistical measures such as 80% IOR and POOR are thus needed to further interpret the fixed regression effect of community-level variables in multilevel analysis [44, 45]. These supplemental statistic measures also take into account the remaining unexplained between-community variability in the odds of WRA with a DFP using PLARCs, as represented by the RIV and MOR estimated from the multilevel analysis [44, 45].

From Model D, as shown in Table 5, it was observed from the result of the 80% IORs for the community-level variable ‘community median age at first marriage/cohabitation’ that, upon a pairwise comparison of two WRA with a DFP, the odds of using PLARCs will lie between 0.125 and 21.360 in the middle 80% of the distribution of the aORs when these comparisons are performed. This was in opposition to women either not using any contraceptive method or using non-modern methods. For this comparison, one of the women is a usual resident of a community with high median age at first marriage/cohabitation while the other is from a community with low median age at first marriage/cohabitation. However, they both have the same individual and household-level characteristics and the other community-level characteristics (that is, except for the community-level variable ‘community median age at first marriage/cohabitation’), as seen from adjusted independent variables in Model D. The 80% IORs can also be interpreted as an 80% probability that, in these pairwise comparisons, the aORs will lie between 0.125 and 21.360. This wide 80% IOR contains the null odds ratio value ‘1’. This means that the remaining between-community variability estimated in Model D in relation to the DFP satisfied by PLARCs, as measured by the MOR = 3.870, is large compared with the regression effect of the community-level variable ‘community median age at first marriage/cohabitation’. This finding thus implies that this community-level variable did not add meaningfully in explaining the between-community variation in the odds of women satisfying their DFP with the PLARCs.

Furthermore, it was observed from the POOR estimates in Model E that in approximately 40.5% of these pairwise comparisons between communities with high median age at first marriage/cohabitation and those with low median age at first marriage/cohabitation, the aOR was in the opposite direction to the overall average aOR for this community-level variable. This is in relation to the odds of women with DFP using PLARCs, as opposed to either not using any contraceptive method or using non-modern method. That is, in 40.5% of these pairwise comparisons, there would be significantly lower odds of women with DFP using PLARCs in communities with a high median age at first marriage/cohabitation compared to communities with low median age at first marriage/cohabitation. This implies that the association effect of this community-level variable ‘communities with low median age at first marriage/cohabitation’ is heterogeneous. Similar interpretations follow for the fixed regression effects of the other community-level variables in relation to the use of different types of MCs by women with a DFP. Further details about these fixed regression effects can be found in Table 5.

Discussion

Summary of findings

This study was an empirical analysis of DFPS with different types of MCs among married or in-union WRA in Nigeria using a multilevel multivariable multinomial logistic model, with data obtained from the 2024 NDHS. Concentrating on only the married or in-union women allowed for the inclusion of variables that access the associating effect of the power dynamics between the WRA with a DFP and their husbands/partners. These include variables assessing the decision-making power of women about their health and the use of their own income. This was considered important because Nigeria is a patriarchal society. A patriarchal society is characterised by women’s reduced significance in terms of their active participation in both formal and informal settings where decisions are made and where resources are owned [50].

From our study, the DFPSM among married or in-union women was approximately 33.9%, with the DFPS with PLARCs, MARCs, and SARCs estimated as approximately 17.1%, 9.1%, and 7.8%, respectively. All these estimates are considered very low since they are less than 50%. These very low estimates have negative implications for the health of women and children and the socioeconomic development of families, communities, and the nation at large, considering the diverse benefits of family planning [51, 52]. This is particularly important in Nigeria because of her very poor maternal and neonatal death statistics and her poor economic profile. For example, in 2023, the country was estimated to have a very high maternal mortality ratio (MMR) of 993 deaths per 100,000 live births [53]. Additionally, Nigeria’s neonatal mortality rate (NMR) in 2022 was approximately 34 deaths per 1000 live births [54]. This made Nigeria the country with the 6th highest NMR in the world [54]. On the other hand, in 2025, approximately 31.2% of Nigerians, accounting for approximately 72 million people from Nigeria’s total population count of approximately 231 million people, lived in extreme poverty [55].

In relation to increasing the use of PLARCs among the women with a DFP, our study’s findings revealed that the regression effects of all the adjusted individual and household-level variables were of less relevance than the impact of the large remaining between-community variance that was present because of the unadjusted variables. Similar findings were also obtained from this study in relation to the use of SARCs by women to satisfy their DFP. However, women with formal education, especially with higher levels of education such as tertiary level, were significantly more likely to use PLARCs and to use SARCs to satisfy their DFP compared to those with no formal education. This research finding of a statistically significant positive effect of women’s higher educational level on the use of PLARCs was similar to that obtained by other similar studies carried out among Nigerian women only, among women in high-fertility sub-Saharan African countries, including Nigeria, and among Zambian women [56–59].

Women with higher educational levels have increased access to and increased ability to obtain knowledge from several sources, thereby helping them make informed decisions about the use of MCs, together with the specific type needed to meet their different reproductive goals [60, 61]. These sources include health care workers, print media, and online sources [60, 61]. Information can be obtained from them about the importance of family planning, the different contraceptive methods available, and where to source them from [60, 61]. In relation to the use of PLARCs, this information can particularly help women to overcome various myths and misconceptions such as fear of side effects of infertility and cancer occurring with their use, despite them being highly effective [62]. In fact, our study findings also showed that women exposed to family planning messages through various media sources were significantly more likely to use PLARCs to satisfy their DFP compared to those with no exposure. In addition, from our findings, women who visited health facilities in the 12 months prior to the survey and were informed about family planning by the health workers were significantly more likely to use PLARCs to satisfy their DFP compared to those without any visitation. Similar findings of significant positive effects from exposure to family planning messages through various media sources and through discussion with health workers at health facilities were also obtained in this study in relation to use of MARCs.

Furthermore, women being educated increases the likelihood of them using PLARCs to satisfy their DFP possibly because higher educational levels increases the likelihood of them being empowered economically with cash-paying jobs outside their households [57]. Additionally, there is an increased likelihood of them being empowered through their involvement, either alone or jointly with their partners/husbands, in economic decision-making, such as in relation to the use of their income or household income [57]. All of these findings have also been confirmed by empirical research [63]. This increases women’s ability to pay for MCs, especially PLARCs, which have the relatively highest upfront costs compared to MARCs and SARCs [64, 65]. However, the former MCs are relatively more cost-effective methods in the long run than the latter methods are [64, 65]. In fact, our study findings showed that women being employed with cash-paying jobs were significantly more likely to use PLARCs and to use SARCs to satisfy their DFP, even when control of cash earnings were mainly done by their husbands/partners, compared to those not employed. Economically empowered women also have greater contact and interaction with the public and thus increased access to information [66], possibly including that in relation to family planning.

In relation to increasing the use of MARCs among the women with a DFP, our study’s findings revealed that only the regression effects of households with no children at most 5 years old and those with 1 to 2 children at most 5 years were of greater relevance than the impact of the large remaining between-community variance that was present as a result of the unadjusted variables. These regression effects were each in comparison to households with at least 5 children at most 5 years old. Similar findings of significant positive effects were also obtained in this study among women in households with zero children at most 5 years old, compared to those with at least 5 children at most 5 years old, in relation to the use of PLARCs to satisfy their DFP. A similar study by Fadeyibi et al. [67] however had opposite findings. From their study, Nigerian women in households with children at most 5 years old were more likely to use of MCs compared to those in households without children at most 5 years old [67].

The findings from our study could reflect the fertility intentions of short birth spacing and high fertility rate, thereby making women want to have another child soon after they just had one. This could be driven by Nigeria’s strong pronatalist culture, especially among married women, strong male-children preference culture, and an uncertainty about child survival status [68, 69]. It was therefore not surprising that at the individual level, women whose response to the question of their ideal number of children were non-numeric, such as ‘it depends on God’, ‘as many as I can support’, had a significantly lower likelihood of using MARCs to satisfy their DFP. It therefore implies that the women in households without children of at most 5 years old are those that have likely reached their ideal fertility preference, including the number of sons, and do not have uncertainty about the survival status of their children. There is thus an increased likelihood of them using the very effective long-term acting MCs such as PLARCs and MARCs. This is also reflected in this study’s findings of women with at least one son and one daughter alive being significantly more likely to use PLARCs and MARCs to satisfy their DFP.

At the community level, findings from our study showed that women in communities with high median age at first marriage, low fertility, and low polygyny status were each significantly more likely to use PLARCs to satisfy their DFP compared to the women in the various counterpart communities. Similar findings were also obtained in this study in relation to the use of SARCs by women in communities with high median age at first marriage compared to their counterparts. The occurrence of community social, legal, and religious norms of low age at first marriage of less than 18 years is associated with various factors that are known to undermine the sexual and reproductive health and rights of women, including the use of contraceptives [27, 70, 71]. These include: high polygyny rates, higher fertility rate, inadequate birth spacing, and low empowerment levels of women across various dimensions [27, 70, 71]. These dimensions include economic, socio-cultural (such as women not having freedom of movement and female illiteracy), familial/interpersonal (such as poor domestic decision-making power, including in women’s reproductive health care, occurrence of domestic violence, including sexual violence), and psychological (such as low self-esteem and self- efficacy) [27, 70, 71].

This study’s findings also showed that at the community level, women residing in communities classified has having high employment level for women were significantly more likely to use MARCs to satisfy their DFP compared to their counterparts. Similar findings were obtained by Sifora et al. [72] of a significant positive effect on the use of MCs by South African women in communities with high employment level for women. Community socio-cultural norms that support women participating in workforce, with cash-payment received, can have a positive impact on the sexual and reproductive health and rights of women, including increased modern contraceptive use. The women are also able to afford the relatively high upfront costs incurred with the use of MARCs compared to other methods such as SARCs, although MARCs are relatively more cost-effective in the long-run compared to SARCs [64, 65].

Additionally, at the community level, from this study’s findings, women in urban areas were significantly more likely to use SARCs compared to those in rural areas. This could possibly be due to increased demand and supply-side barriers to the use of MCs occurring relatively more in rural areas compared to urban areas [73–76]. The demand-side barriers occur at the individual, household, and community-levels. They include: cultural and even religious disapproval at the individual, household, and community level, and difficulty in transportation to health facilities as a result of bad roads and the long distances that need to be travelled [73–76]. The supply-side barriers include: poor spatial distribution or non-availability of health facilities, absence or scarcity of family planning commodities at the health facilities, the presence of inadequately trained medical personnel or shortages of medical personnels at the health facilities, and high cost of family planning methods at the health facilities [73–76].

Considering the supply-side barriers, SARCs possess characteristics that are advantageous in a resource-constrained context such as rural areas. These include ease of access (these methods can be easily found and hence easily obtained from patent drug stores), ease of application and discontinuation of use since skilled health personnel are not needed, and a relatively cheaper initial cost [5, 77]. Our study findings of a significant positive effect on the use of SARCs by women within urban areas compared to those in rural areas could possibly imply a relatively stronger influence of the demand-side barriers on the use of this modern family planning method.

However, at the community-level, from this study’s findings, women who resided in communities where there is high level of visitation to heath facilities by women in the last 12 months were significantly more likely to use SARCs and to use PLARCs compared to their counterparts. High level of visitation to health facilities by women in a community can be used to proxy physical availability of a health facility within the community and the availability of services, including human resources, within these facilities [78]. SARCs that are usually not obtained from patent drug stores can thus be got from them. These include vaginal rings, caps, and diaphragms [14]. These health facilities will also serve as additional sources for the other SARCs that are also readily got from the patent drug stores such as condoms, emergency contraception, and daily contraceptive pills [14]. All of these will possibly culminate in an increase in the use of SARCs. Furthermore, in order for clients to use PLARCs, medical personnels are needed and surgical procedures may be done such as for male and female sterilisation methods [5]. Medical personnels are also needed for clients to discontinue the use of PLARCs [5]. Thus, the availability of geographically accessible health facilities within the communities will possibly also culminate in an increase in the use of PLARCs.

Nevertheless, from this study’s findings, all the regression effects estimated in relation to the effect of all the community-level variables on the DFPS with different types of MCs were heterogeneous. The remaining between-cluster variances were also large in comparison with the different regression effects of these variables. Despite this, critical insights for policy formation can still be drawn from this study’s findings.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Although this study filled a major gap in terms of the empirical analysis of DFPS with different types of MCs among Nigerian women, it is not without limitations. A major limitation is that this study used cross-sectional data, which precludes cause‒effect relationships from being established. The possibility of using longitudinal data should thus be examined in future research. In addition, the PSUs, which was used as a proxy for communities in this study represent administratively defined contexts or boundaries as used for census. It may therefore misclassify the individual women inappropriately into communities, which can introduce information biases in the multilevel analysis. However, using the PSUs to proxy communities was also done by other Nigerian studies [79–81].

Furthermore, since community-level variables were created by aggregating individual-level data, this creates correlation by construction. However, the inclusion of correlation testing and other independent variable selection steps in this study will help to reduce this. Also, the generation of community-level variables in this study by aggregating individual data and dichotomising by national median or mean could reduce valuable information. This is inconsideration of the pluralistic nature of the Nigerian society. However, this is also commonly done by other Nigerian studies [79–81]. Future studies should explore the possibility of creating and using community-level variables with more than two subcategories.

Future research should also adjust for other socioeconomic, demographic, health system, and policy/government factors. In line with this study’s conceptual framework, this includes factors at the institutional level such as the accessibility and availability of health facilities and that of family planning services within these facilities. On the other hand, other factors at the policy level such as government health policies at the state, national, or international levels in the relation to the provision of family planning should be taken into consideration by future studies. All of these may help to further account for the large remaining between-community variances in relation to the odds of WRA with a DFP using the different types of MCs. Nevertheless, major insights for policy makers and other researchers can still be obtained from our findings in relation to the topic at hand.

Policy recommendations

Based on our findings, it is very important for the Nigerian government to emphasise the education of girls and women beyond the primary level up to tertiary level. Various media sources and community outreaches can be used by the Nigerian government to increase sensitisation regarding the importance of girls being formally educated beyond the primary level and even advancing to the tertiary level. There should be increased effort or concentration within the communities with low median age at first marriage, high fertility clusters, and high polygyny clusters. Furthermore, within these communities, there should be an increased effort or concentration within households with many children 5 years and under. In addition, the Nigerian government should institute free public education up to the secondary level, with diverse types of government aid made available to citizens for tertiary education.

Community-based projects that are geared towards making women economically empowered should be carried out by the Nigerian government. Again, there should be increased effort or concentration within the communities with low median age at first marriage, high fertility clusters, and high polygyny clusters. This will help women be empowered about health care decision making and enable them to pay the medical and non-medical costs associated with the use of MCs. These community-based projects should involve the community chiefs, elders, and leaders, including religious leaders, who are usually the custodian of culture and tradition, and play an important governance and advisory role within the communities.

Information about family planning and fertility control should also be passed through all the different media sources together. This includes the use of radios and the use of mobile vans moving throughout the communities or stationed at strategic public places within the communities such as the main market square. The information should be passed using the predominant local language spoken within that area.

Community health workers can also be extensively utilised to pass information about family planning from household to household, particularly within the communities with low median age at first marriage, high fertility clusters, and high polygyny clusters. There should be a major concentration on the households with women without any formal education or those with primary education as their highest educational level obtained and the households with many children 5 years and under. This will be particularly helpful in improving the use of MARCs and SARCs, which are the MCs that can be easily administered by community health workers. All the above will thus help address a demand-side barrier of misinformation in relation to the use of MCs and even cultural myths and practices in relation to fertility control and fertility preferences such as high fertility rates.

On the supply side, the Nigerian government should also address accessibility and availability barriers. There should be proper distribution of health facilities within communities to improve geographical accessibility of women with a need to space or limit births. Within these facilities, there should be steady availability of diverse modern contraceptive methods to address the different family planning needs of women. This is particularly important for PLARCs, since the administration and discontinuation of use of these family planning methods require the availability of a health facility, with a skilled health provider present.

Conclusion

Nigeria’s DFPSM among the WRA has been consistently very low since it has always been less than 50%, with diverse health and socioeconomic implications. Considering the diverse family planning needs of women, an increase in the DFPSM through an increase in the use of PLARCs, SARCs, MARCs by women is needed in Nigeria. This will also increase the possibility of her achieving the SDG indicator 3.7.1 benchmark of at least 75% by 2030. The findings of this study suggest that this can possibly be achieved through improving the formal education of girls beyond the primary level and increasing their exposure to family planning messages through various media sources. In relation to this, there should be an increased concentration within the communities with low median age at first marriage for women. Other communities to focus on in relation to increasing the use of specific MCs include for PLARCs, the high fertility clusters and the high polygyny clusters; for MARCs, the communities with low employment level for women; and for SARCs, the rural residents.

A written request to access the dataset was first made by the authors to the DHS programme website (https://dhsprogram.com/methodology/survey/survey-display-609.cfm). Following this, the authors obtained a written authorization from the DHS Programme to freely use the dataset needed for this study. Verbal informed consent was obtained by the DHS interviewers from all the respondents before their enrolment in the interview, as seen from the 2024 NDHS final report (https://dhsprogram.com/publications/publication-FR395-DHS-Final-Reports.cfm). All study methods were performed by the authors in compliance with the relevant guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration. Due to public availability of the DHS dataset no further ethical clearance for this study was sought by the authors.
