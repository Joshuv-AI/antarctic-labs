Changes in the properties of Mentha spicata L. essential oil under the influence of soil characteristics, available water, and altitude in the mountainous regions of Kashan
Authors: Seyyed Ruhollah Seyyediyan, Mansureh Ghavam, Ali Tavili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mentha spicata L. (Persian: Naana Dashti) is a medicinal plant traditionally used to treat respiratory and digestive disorders. Given its industrial importance and complex chemical profile, this study investigates how environmental factors are associated with the essential oil yield, chemical composition, and antibacterial activity of M. spicata in the mountainous regions of Kashan (Barzok, Hosnarood, Kamoo, and Viduj). Essential oils were extracted via a Clevenger-type apparatus and analyzed by GC–MS. Antibacterial efficacy was assessed using the agar well diffusion (D IZ), MIC, and MBC methods. Results indicated that the highest essential oil yield occurred in Hosnarood (5.04%) and the lowest in Kamoo (1.08%), correlating with the lowest and highest altitudes and Plant Available Water (P.A.W) levels, respectively. Carvone was the predominant constituent in Viduj (63.98%), Hosnarood (54.45%) Kamoo (47.01%), and Barzok (46.85%), which showed a relationship with altitude variations. The essential oil from Barzok showed the strongest inhibition against Bacillus subtilis (12 mm), while Kamoo's oil was most effective against Escherichia coli (9.5 mm), both exhibiting relatively strong activity compared to Rifampin. In conclusion, altitude, water availability, and soil properties are significantly associated with the essential oil profile and biological potency of M. spicata, highlighting the importance of habitat selection for optimizing high-quality production.

Introduction

The medicinal significance of plant secondary metabolites in drug development has been recognized for millennia [1]. Their accumulation is highly dependent on environmental variables, including light, temperature, soil fertility, and salinity; even a single factor can significantly alter metabolite content [2]. Essential oils, complex aromatic liquids containing over 200 compounds [3], are integral to traditional medicine [4]. Variations in their chemical profile are driven by genetic factors or environmental responses to habitat ecosystems, such as soil composition and altitude [5]. Identifying specific soil types is therefore crucial for optimizing essential oil yield and quality [6], as chemical variations directly influence antimicrobial efficacy [7].

The Lamiaceae family, comprising over 4,000 species, is renowned for volatile oils with potent biological activities against pathogens [8]. Within this family, Mentha species are widely used in both traditional medicine and modern pharmaceutical products [9] for their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antibacterial effects [10]. Mentha spicata L. is one of the most commercially significant cultivated mints [11]. Its essential oil is valued for its unique aroma and therapeutic use in treating respiratory and digestive disorders [12]. Distinctively, M. spicata essential oil lacks menthol and is characterized by a high percentage of carvone [13]. Research indicates that this essential oil exhibits inhibitory effects against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria [14].

Despite the industrial importance of carvone-rich M. spicata essential oil, the concurrent impact of environmental factors on its quantity, quality, and antimicrobial activity in high-altitude ecosystems remains under-researched. Therefore, this study was designed for the first time to identify the optimal environmental conditions in the mountainous regions of Kashan to achieve the highest yield and biological quality of M. spicata essential oil.

Four regions—Barzok, Kamoo, Hosnarood, and Viduj—were selected based on the absence of irrigation/cultivation treatments, lack of soil nutritional manipulation, geographical distance, and varying altitudes. Plant sampling

In August 2022, leaves and stems were collected from three random points in each region (Collection permit obtained from the Agricultural Jahad Office). Samples were transported to the laboratory and air-dried at 25°C. The species was identified by Dr. Mansureh Ghavam (Voucher specimen code: 1024) and deposited in the herbarium of the Faculty of Natural Resources and Earth Sciences, University of Kashan, Iran..

Preparation of essential oils

Essential oils were extracted via hydro-distillation using a Clevenger-type apparatus. Briefly, 70 g of dried leaves and stems were ground and placed in a 2-L flask with distilled water (two-thirds volume). Extraction was performed for 4 h. The resulting essential oils were dehydrated using anhydrous sodium sulfate and stored in light-protected glass vials at 4 °C until further analysis. All extractions were performed in triplicate, corresponding to samples collected from three random points in each region.

Essential oil chemical properties

GC–MS was used to determine the quality of essential oils. The chromatograph model 6890 coupled with a mass spectrometer model 5973 N manufactured by Agilent, USA, has an HP-5MS capillary column with a 5% methylphenylsiloxane stationary phase (length 30 m, internal diameter 0.25 mm and stationary layer thickness 0.25 μm) and an ionization energy of 70 eV. The temperature program for the analysis was 60 °C and then increased to 246 °C at a rate of 3°C/min. The injected sample volume was 1 μL with a 1.50 split, the injector and detector temperatures were 250 °C, and the carrier gas was helium at a flow rate of 1.5 mL/min.

The essential oil constituents were identified based on a multi-criteria approach to ensure accuracy. First, the mass spectra of each compound were matched against the NIST14 and Wiley7n libraries, considering only those with a match factor (SI) and reverse match factor (RSI) greater than 90%. Furthermore, the identification was confirmed by calculating the Linear Retention Indices (LRI) for each peak. This was achieved by injecting a homologous series of n-alkanes (C8-C40) under the same chromatographic conditions. The calculated LRI values were then compared with those reported in the literature (e.g., Adams, 2007) and the NIST online database for equivalent columns. Only compounds that met both mass spectral similarity and RI consistency were reported.

Antibacterial activity of essential oil

Selection of bacteria

In this test, to evaluate the antimicrobial activity of the extract, two strains of Gram-positive bacteria Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 29737) and Bacillus subtilis (ATCC 6633) and two strains of Gram-negative bacteria Escherichia coli (ATCC 25922) and Shigella dysenteriae (PTCC 1188), from the Iranian Organization for Scientific Research and Technology (IROST) were prepared.

Measurement of the diameter of the zone of inhibition (D IZ)

This method was performed according to CLSI standards. For this purpose, plates containing Mueller Hinton agar culture medium were prepared, wells with a diameter of 6 mm were created on the culture medium, 100 μL of bacterial suspensions with a turbidity equivalent to half McFarland were cultured under uniform conditions on the surface of the culture medium. The essential oil was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and reached a concentration of 300 μg/mL. The amount 10 μL, equivalent to 300 μg/mL, of the essential oil was poured into the wells, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The D IZ was measured in millimeters using an antibiogram ruler in millimeters.

Measurement of the minimum growth inhibitory concentration (MIC)

In this method, the minimum growth inhibitory concentration for microorganisms sensitive to essential oils was determined by microdilution. First, DMSO dilution of 8000 μg/mL of essential oils was prepared. Then concentrations of 4000, 2000, 1000, 500, 250, 125, and 62.5 μg/mL of the initial concentration were prepared. After that, suspensions of the test strains were added to the plates containing different concentrations of essential oils. For this purpose, 96-well microplates A sterile well was prepared. 95 μL of culture medium, 5 μL of bacterial suspension with a 0.5 McFarland dilution, and 100 μL of different extract dilutions were added to each plate, and then the plate was heated in an incubator at 37 °C for 24 h. According to the turbidity and the range of color change in each well of the microplate, the MIC or minimum growth inhibitory concentration was determined.

Measurement of the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

To determine the minimum bactericidal concentration test, after 24 h of incubation, 5 μL of each well of the microplate in which there was no growth was inoculated into nutrient agar medium and incubated for 24 h at 37°C.

Statistical analysis

Mean values from triplicate biological samples per site were compared using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD post-hoc test at α = 0.05, in SPSS 27.0.1 (IBM Corp., Armonk NY).

Results and discussion

Essential oil yield (quantitative)

Statistical analysis revealed significant regional variations in M. spicata essential oil yield (P ≤ 0.01) (Table 3). Similar diversity has been documented in previous studies; for instance, [15] reported yields as high as 5.83% in Morocco. In the present study, the highest yield was observed in the Hosnarood region (5.04%), while the lowest was recorded in Kamoo (1.08%). Soil and water analysis were also associated with these variations. The highest oil yield in Hosnarood coincided with sand content of 50.6%, intermediate between the sandy-loam textures of Viduj (63.7%) and Barzok (58.2%) and the clay loam of Kamoo (39.5%). Conversely, the lower yield in Kamoo was observed alongside the lowest sand content (39.5%) and highest P.A.W (12.5%). While [17] reported lower yield ranges (0.41%–0.76%) in Pakistan, [18] suggested that M. spicata essential oil production may be resilient to water deficit. Furthermore, findings by [19] and [22] indicated a positive association between lighter soil textures (such as sandy loam) and increased oil yields.

Environmental factors—including altitude, temperature extremes, moisture availability, and soil composition—are closely linked to secondary metabolite accumulation [20, 21]. In the Kashan mountains, higher M. spicata essential oil yields correlated with lower altitudes, reduced water availability, and increased soil sand content. Notably, these mountainous yields were significantly higher than those reported in the lowland areas of Kashan, such as Yazdel (0.366%) [23] and Fin (3.3%) [19].

Number of compounds

The chemical profiles of M. spicata essential oil across the studied regions (detailed in the consolidated Table 3) showed significant regional differences (P ≤ 0.01) in both the types and percentages of constituents (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5). These quantitative and qualitative variations are often associated with genetic and non-genetic adaptations to diverse environmental conditions [24]. In this study, the Barzok region exhibited the highest chemical diversity (46 compounds), while Kamoo and Viduj contained the fewest (21 compounds). Recent studies have reported varying levels of diversity, such as 4 compounds in Taiwan [26] and 8–13 in Morocco [27]. Soil analysis indicated an association between alkalinity and compound diversity; Barzok featured the highest pH (8.026), while Kamoo and Viduj recorded the lowest (pH ~ 7.5). These findings are consistent with [19], who observed the highest number of M. spicata constituents in regions with higher soil pH (8.028).

Group of compositions

Oxygenated monoterpenes were the dominant chemical group in all samples, peaking in Viduj (70.58%) and reaching their lowest levels in Kamoo (55.22%). These results align with [9], who identified oxygenated monoterpenes (67.2%) as the primary group in Algerian M. spicata. While the ratio of oxygenated monoterpenes decreased at higher altitudes, the relationship appeared non-linear, as previously reported by [28] for Stachys inflata Benth. and [29] for Origanum majorana L.

Soil characteristics provided a descriptive backdrop to these chemical variations, though the limited sample size (n = 4 locations) precludes formal statistical correlation testing, rendering any inferences purely exploratory. The highest levels of lime (27.85%) and sand (63.7%), along with the lowest clay content (17.6%), coincided with the highest oxygenated monoterpene levels (70.58%) in Viduj. Conversely, Kamoo featured the lowest lime and sand but the highest clay content (32.2%), corresponding to the lowest oxygenated monoterpene percentage (55.22%). However, this descriptive trend was not strictly linear across all studied sites; for instance, Hosnarood (loam, 50.6% sand) exhibited a higher oxygenated monoterpene content (67.44%) than Barzok (66.67%), despite Barzok having a lighter sandy loam texture (58.2% sand). While sandy soils may facilitate root growth, differences in nutrient and water uptake compared to clay soils are often associated with shifts in essential oil composition [31]. Nevertheless, as noted by [30], physicochemical soil properties are key factors related to secondary metabolite profiles in aromatic plants, but these non-linear, localized variations highlight that These findings align with [19], who reported carvone as the dominant constituent in lowland and plain areas of Kashan. Carvone has also been identified as a major component in samples from Oman (65.21%) [34] and Peru (57.93%) [35]. Optical isomers elicit distinct sensory responses; R-(−)-carvone is characterized by a sweet, minty odor, while -(+)-carvone (D-carvone) offers a pungent, rye-like aroma [36]. Furthermore, carvone shows significant pharmacological potential, including antimicrobial and antioxidant activities [37].

D-Limonene was the second most abundant compound in most regions, peaking in Kamoo (27.97%). This is consistent with [38], who identified D-limonene as a major secondary component. While some studies identify limonene as the primary constituent [34, 39–41], in Barzok, it coincided with the presence of p-Menth-8-en-2-one, trans- (17.35%), a compound absent in other regions. Despite Kamoo’s higher altitude, D-limonene levels did not follow a linear altitudinal trend. Instead, the highest D-limonene levels were recorded alongside the heaviest soil texture in Kamoo (32.2% clay), similar to the high linalool accumulation observed in clay soils for Ocimum basilicum L. [42]. D-Limonene, the primary natural isomer [43], is a bioactive monocyclic monoterpene with broad-spectrum clinical potential [44].

While this indicates measurable inhibitory activity, it remains notably lower than the 19 mm observed for Rifampin. To our knowledge, this is the first report documenting the efficacy of M. spicata essential oil against this specific bacterium. Consistent with existing literature, our results indicated a higher susceptibility in Gram-positive compared to Gram-negative bacteria, which is frequently attributed to structural differences in their cell walls [46]. The essential oil from Kamoo demonstrated a modest inhibitory effect against the Gram-negative Escherichia coli (9.5 mm), which was lower than the DIZ of Rifampin (11 mm). Similar findings were reported by [47]. The antimicrobial efficacy of essential oils is often associated with their hydrophobicity, which facilitates partitioning into the lipids of bacterial cell membranes and mitochondria. This process is linked to disrupted cell structures and increased permeability, potentially leading to the leakage of vital ions. Notably, M. spicata essential oil showed no inhibitory effect against Staphylococcus aureus or Shigella dysenteriae across all regions, which differs from some previous reports [19, 47] and underscores the limited antibacterial spectrum of the current samples.

MIC and MBC

Analysis of variance showed significant differences (P ≤ 0.01) between the MIC and MBC values of the essential oils and control antibiotics. The essential oils exhibited MIC values ranging from 1000 to 4000 μg/mL and MBC values between 1000 and 8000 μg/mL. These values are substantially higher than those of the control antibiotics, indicating a relatively weak antibacterial potency for the studied essential oils.

Previous research by [47] reported lower MIC values (e.g., 2.5–10 μL/mL), which contrasts with the higher concentrations required in our study. Such discrepancies may be associated with variations in preparation methods, differences in chemical profiles, or the diverse environmental conditions of the plant habitats. Ultimately, these results suggest that while M. spicata essential oil possesses certain bioactive constituents, its antibacterial efficacy at the tested concentrations is modest, reflecting the inherent variability in essential oil compositions across different habitats [48].

Limitations of the study

While this study provides valuable baseline data on M. spicata, several methodological limitations must be acknowledged. First, the field sampling design was restricted to four distinct geographical locations (n = 4). Although three distinct sampling points were utilized per region to ensure internal consistency, this scale represents a constrained framework for generalized ecological modeling, and the sample size is below the threshold required for definitive statistical inferences regarding soil–plant interactions. Second, the temporal scope of this investigation is limited to a single sampling period (August 2022). Consequently, potential inter-annual bioclimatic variations or seasonal fluctuations in essential oil yield and composition were not captured. Finally, the biological replication within each site was limited to these three pooled sampling points, which precludes a deeper evaluation of micro-environmental or intra-site genetic diversity. Given these constraints, the relationships observed here should be considered preliminary and exploratory rather than globally generalizable, highlighting the need for multi-year, multi-site investigations with larger sample sizes to fully validate these ecological trends.

Conclusion

The industrial and pharmaceutical significance of secondary metabolites necessitates a clear understanding of the factors related to their yield and chemical variability. This study indicates that the essential oil profile of M. spicata in the mountainous regions of Kashan is closely associated with environmental variables, particularly altitude and soil physicochemical properties. Higher essential oil yields coincided with lower altitudes. These chemical variations, including the presence of p-Menth-8-en-2-one in specific regions, were linked to the observed modest antibacterial effects against B. subtilis. Although the antibacterial potency was limited compared to standard antibiotics, the findings provide baseline data on how specific habitat characteristics are associated with the chemical and biological profiles of M. spicata. Given the localized scale and temporal constraints of this study, these insights offer a preliminary framework for local habitat monitoring and the exploratory management of secondary metabolites in regional aromatic plants, rather than a globally generalizable model for cultivation optimization.
