Impact of BRCA2 pathogenic variants on outcomes to first-line CDK4/6 inhibitors plus endocrine therapy in HR-positive/HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer
Authors: M Cruellas, A Rodriguez-Hernandez, L Carità, E Seguí, M Cejuela, L Lema, E García-Torralba, A Roqué-Lloveras, A Fernández-Ortega, M Melé, M Tapia, S Servitja, S González-Santiago, N Blaya-Boluda, M Bellet, O Martínez-Sáez, I Pimentel, I García-Fructuoso, T Pascual, L Joval-Ramentol, G Viñas, M Rey Aceituno, A Casas, E Castillo, F Brasó-Maristany, A Vivancos, V Serra, G Villacampa, M Oliveira, A Prat, C Saura, B Adamo, J Balmaña
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrently, three cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 inhibitors (CDK4/6i) are approved in combination with endocrine therapy (ET) as first-line treatment of patients with hormone receptor (HR)-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative metastatic breast cancer (MBC). The impact of homologous recombination repair (HRR) pathogenic variants (PV) on outcomes with first-line CDK4/6i plus ET in HR-positive/HER2-negative MBC remains uncertain.Patients and methodsWe conducted a multicenter, real-world, case–control study including 233 patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative MBC treated with first-line CDK4/6i and ET. Among them, 116 presented HRR PVs and 117 were matched controls with negative germline testing. The primary objective was to compare progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival among germline-BRCA2 PV carriers, other HRR PV carriers, and controls. To minimize baseline differences in prognostic factors between PV carriers and controls, inverse probability of treatment weighting was applied. Molecular analyses in pre-CDK4/6i samples among patients with BRCA2 PV were carried out, including RAD51-foci, PAM50 intrinsic subtype, and RB1 loss of heterozygosity (LOH).ResultsAmong the included 233 patients, median age at diagnosis was 45 years (interquartile range 39-56) and 33% had de novo metastatic disease. Primary resistance to adjuvant ET was present in 10% and secondary resistance in 27%. After a median follow-up of 44 months, patients with germline-BRCA2 PVs (n = 67) had significantly shorter PFS [11 versus 27 months; adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) 2.73, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.65-4.51, P < 0.001] compared with controls. Among patients with endocrine-sensitive disease, germline BRCA2 PV carriers had markedly shorter PFS (median PFS 12 versus 39 months; aHR 4.04; 95% CI 1.82-8.98, P < 0.001). Exploratory analyses revealed RB1 LOH before CDK4/6i-treatment in most evaluable BRCA2 tumors.ConclusionsBRCA2 PVs were independently associated with poorer outcomes to first-line CDK4/6i plus ET in HR-positive/HER2-negative MBC compared with controls, especially relevant among patients with endocrine-sensitive disease. These findings suggest that patients with a germline PV in BRCA2 may require alternative first-line strategies.Key words: BRCA, CDK4/6 inhibitors, metastatic breast cancer

Introduction

The complexity in metastatic breast cancer (MBC) therapeutic strategies has markedly increased in recent years.1,2 Concurrently, the criteria for germline genetic testing in patients with BC have expanded substantially, driven by their implications for both prevention and treatment, particularly given the growing availability of targeted therapies such as poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi).3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants (PV) in genes such as BRCA1, BRCA2, or PALB2 are identified in ∼4%-5% of patients with BC.3,8 The prognostic and predictive impact of germline PV in these genes for targeted therapies (other than PARPi) among patients with hormone receptor (HR)-positive and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative BC remains controversial, and data with the most recent therapies in the metastatic setting are scarce.

Currently, three cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 inhibitors (CDK4/6i), palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib, are approved by both the Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency in combination with endocrine therapy (ET) as first-line treatment of patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative MBC. All three agents have demonstrated clinical benefit compared with ET monotherapy.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 Nevertheless, the registration trials of CDK4/6i in the advanced setting did not assess the germline status of the enrolled population, precluding any subsequent evaluation of its potential influence on treatment outcomes.

Clinical outcomes of patients with MBC harboring germline or somatic PV in homologous recombination repair (HRR) genes and treated with first-line CDK4/6i plus ET have been evaluated in prior retrospective studies.16, 17, 18, 19, 20 These studies have reported shorter progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in patients with a germline or somatic PV in BRCA2 or other HRR genes treated with CDK4/6i in the metastatic setting.16, 17, 18, 19, 20 These findings might be partially explained by the frequent development of RB1 biallelic loss in BRCA2-mutated tumors, a known mechanism of resistance to CDK4/6i, due to the close chromosomal proximity of the two genes and the selective pressure of treatment.20 Conversely, a subanalysis of an invasive disease-free survival-events-enriched subpopulation within the monarchE trial demonstrated that patients with germline BRCA1/2 PVs (n = 41) derived greater benefit from adjuvant CDK4/6i plus ET compared with ET alone.21 Considering the availability of other targeted options, such as PARPi, which have demonstrated significant PFS benefit in advanced BC, further clarification of this issue is warranted.22, 23, 24, 25

Given that CDK4/6i in combination with ET remains the standard first-line treatment of HR-positive/HER2-negative advanced BC,26 this study aimed to evaluate, using real-world data, whether patients harboring a germline or tumoral PV in BRCA2 and/or in another HRR-related gene experience worse outcomes compared with those without a germline PV in any HRR gene. Additionally, we investigated the resistance mechanisms by tumor genomics and gene expression.

Patients and methods

This was a retrospective, multicenter, real-world case–control study that included patients diagnosed with advanced HR-positive/HER2-negative BC who received first-line CDK4/6i plus ET between January 2016 and July 2024. Patients who fulfilled the inclusion criteria outlined in Supplementary Table S1, (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335) were eligible. Cases were defined as patients with a germline PV in an HRR gene (ATM, BARD1, BRCA1, BRCA2, BRIP1, CHEK2, PALB2, RAD51C, or RAD51D) or a tumor PV in BRCA1, BRCA2 or PALB2 (germline testing results of these cases could be negative or unknown). Variant allele frequency (VAF) from circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) was used as a contextual parameter to explore the potential germline origin of variants in predefined genes, with higher VAF values (e.g. >45%) considered as a germline origin (five cases). Controls [wild-type (WT) group] were defined as patients with a negative germline genetic test, including the analysis of the predefined HRR genes. Cases were recruited from 11 participating centers and the SOLTI-1903 HOPE BC trial (NCT04497285),27 whereas controls were enrolled at Vall d’Hebron Hospital and Hospital Clinic of Barcelona. The study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) at the coordinating center (Vall d’Hebron Hospital, IRB study code: PR(AG)130/2023) and by each participating center IRB.

Objectives and endpoints

The primary objective of the analysis was to compare PFS and OS between patients with a germline BRCA2 PV to those with a tumor BRCA2 PV, PV in other HRR genes and to the WT group. Secondary objectives were to evaluate PFS and OS between patients with a PV in any HRR-related gene and the WT cohort. We also assessed the likelihood of identifying a germline BRCA2 PV among endocrine-sensitive patients with short PFS (≤12 months), using previously published prevalence data as the reference (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335). Exploratory outcomes included the assessment of median PFS in patients treated with second-line PARPi following prior CDK4/6i plus ET, and the characterization of before and after treatment resistance mechanisms to CDK4/6i plus ET through tumor sample analysis.

For the main analysis, PFS was defined as the time from initiation of CDK4/6i therapy to documented radiologic or clinical disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. OS was defined as the time from initiation of CDK4/6i therapy to death from any cause; patients alive at last follow-up were censored at that date. Of note, not all patients underwent genetic testing before initiation of CDK4/6 inhibitors, which in other settings could introduce immortal time bias. However, in this study, all patients (cases and controls) were required to have undergone testing for inclusion, and the timing of testing was similar between groups [Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Methods (Time zero), available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335].

Clinical and molecular data

Clinical data were obtained from electronic medical records. Germline testing criteria were based on current guidelines.6 Genetic testing and pathologic examination were carried out locally. In this study, pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants were collectively referred to as PV. Clinical variables and their definitions are detailed in the Supplementary Methods (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335).

Molecular analysis of biomarkers of sensitivity and resistance were focused on: HRR status by RAD51 foci assay,28,29 intrinsic subtype by PAM50 gene expression profiling,1,30,31 and loss of heterozygosity (LOH) of tumor RB1 by sequencing.32 These analyses were carried out on pre-CDK4/6i samples with a BRCA2 PV, with additional genomic sequencing and RAD51 foci carried out on after treatment tissue to assess mechanisms of resistance and potential second-hit alterations. Further details regarding molecular data collection and sample processing are provided in Supplementary Methods (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335).

Statistical analysis

To minimize baseline differences in prognostic factors between PV carriers and controls, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied using the Average Treatment effect on the Treated (ATT) approach, outcomes were estimated in the resulting weighted cohort.33 The following variables were included in the IPTW model: age, endocrine resistance, visceral metastases, bone metastases, number of metastatic sites, breast cancer subtype, menopausal status, and type of CDK4/6 inhibitor. All variables had a standardized mean difference (SMD) <0.1 after weighting (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5; Supplementary Methods, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335).

The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate PFS and OS among patients with germline BRCA2 PV carriers, tumoral BRCA2 PV, other HRR PV carriers and controls. Multivariable hazard ratios (HRs) were also calculated using a weighted Cox model to compare survival outcomes among the study groups together with other prognostic factors. Missing data were handled using multiple imputations using the Rubin’s rules pooling with the mice package in R (more details of the missing data strategy are presented in Supplementary Methods, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335).

The prevalence of missing data was relatively low in all variables (Supplementary Table S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335). A sensitivity analysis was carried out using only the complete-case datasets to estimate the multivariable Cox models. Additionally, further sensitivity analyses were carried out to assess the robustness of the results for patients who underwent testing after initiation of CDK4/6 inhibitors (Supplementary Methods, Supplementary Table S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, using two-sided P values. To estimate the probability that patients who experienced a PFS event carried a germline PV, an adjustment was applied to reflect the real-world prevalence of germline PV (Supplementary Methods, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335). The median follow-up was calculated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.1.

Results

Overall, 233 patients were included in the study [116 harboring a germline or tumor PV (cases) and 117 controls]. The main characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. Within the cases group, 104 (90%) harbored a germline PV in an HRR-related gene. The most frequently mutated gene was BRCA2 (n = 67), followed by BRCA1 (n = 12), PALB2 (n = 8), ATM (n = 7), CHEK2 (n = 7), RAD51C (n = 2), and BRIP1 (n = 1) (Table 1). Additionally, 12 patients were included based on the presence of a tumor PV (six with confirmed negative germline testing and six with unknown germline status). Of these 12 patients, 7 patients had a tumor BRCA2 PV, 2 had a tumor BRCA1 PV and, 3 had a tumor PALB2 PV. After applying IPTW, the populations of cases and controls, and specifically, carriers of germline BRCA2 PVs and controls were well balanced (Supplementary Tables S4 and 5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.1083358).

After a median follow-up of 44 months, the median PFS was 11.0 months for patients with a germline BRCA2 PV, 11 months for patients with a tumoral BRCA2 PV, 21.0 months for those with a PV in other HRR-related genes, and 27.1 months for controls. The HR for PFS was 2.73 [95% confidence interval (CI) 1.65-4.51, P < 0.001] for patients with a germline BRCA2 PV, 3.15 (95% CI 1.52-6.54, P < 0.001) for patients with a tumoral BRCA2 PV and 1.23 (95% CI 0.72-2.10, P = 0.45) for patients with a PV in other HRR-related genes compared with the control group (Figure 1A). Similarly, the median OS was shorter for patients with a germline BRCA2 PV (45.1 months), compared with 55.0 months for patients with a PV in other HRR-related genes and not reached (NR) for controls and patients with tumoral BRCA2 PV. The HR for OS was 2.65 (95% CI 1.23-5.68, P = 0.01) for patients with a germline BRCA2 PV, and 1.86 (95% CI 0.81-4.29, P = 0.14) for patients with a PV in other HRR-related genes compared with the control group (Figure 1B). Consistent results were observed using different time zero definitions for cases tested after starting CDK4/6 inhibitors (Supplementary Table S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335).

The univariable Cox analysis is provided in the Supplementary Figure S1A and S1B (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335). Similarly, the weighted multivariable Cox analysis showed significantly worse PFS in patients with a germline PV in BRCA2 [adjusted HR (aHR) 2.73, 95% CI 1.67-4.46, P < 0.001] and in those with endocrine-resistant disease to adjuvant therapy (aHR 2.09, 95% CI 1.26-3.46, P = 0.004) (Figure 2A). Additionally, a worse OS was observed in patients harboring a germline BRCA2 mutation (aHR 2.61, 95% CI 1.14-5.95, P = 0.022). Independent predictors of shorter OS were endocrine-resistant disease to adjuvant therapy (HR 2.41, 95% CI 1.23-4.73, P = 0.015) and the presence of three or more metastatic sites (HR 2.20, 95% CI 1.14–4.22, P = 0.032), whereas premenopausal status showed a trend toward a more favorable OS (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.27-1.04, P = 0.055) (Figure 2B). Similar results were obtained in the multivariable analysis without missing data imputation (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335). In the analysis comparing PFS and OS between patients with a HRR PV (cases) and the controls, patients with a HRR PV experienced shorter PFS (median PFS: 15.0 versus 27.1 months; HR 1.97, 95% CI 1.25-3.11, P = 0.003), and OS (median OS: NR versus 49.0 months; HR 2.24, 95% CI 1.28-4.65, P = 0.03) (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335).

We explored differences in PFS across the four main groups [germline BRCA2 PV, tumoral BRCA2 PV, germline/tumoral (g/t) PV in other HRR-related genes, and controls] stratifying by endocrine-sensitive disease to prior adjuvant therapy. Patients with endocrine-resistant disease showed poor PFS in all groups (Figure 3A). However, among endocrine-sensitive patients, those with a germline BRCA2 PV had significantly shorter PFS than controls (12.0 months versus 39.0 months; HR 4.04; 95% CI 1.82-8.98; P < 0.001) (Figure 3B).

We then focused on assessing the impact of endocrine sensitivity among patients with a germline BRCA2 PV. Interestingly, no differences in median PFS nor OS were observed according to endocrine sensitivity in this subgroup (Supplementary Figure S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335), whereas a statistically significant difference was observed among patients without a germline BRCA2 PV (Supplementary Figure S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335). PFS and OS analyses restricted to patients with secondary endocrine resistance showed consistent trends across groups (Supplementary Figure S5A and S5B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335).

To further explore treatment outcomes beyond first-line therapy, we analyzed the median PFS of patients who received PARPi as second-line treatment (n = 26). The median PFS was 11.1 months (95% CI 6.8-NR), and at 36 months of follow-up, 15% of the patients remained on PARPi (Supplementary Figure S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335).

The estimated probability of carrying a germline BRCA2 PV among endocrine-sensitive patients experiencing disease progression within ≤12 months was 2.32% (Supplementary Table S8, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335). The detection of a germline BRCA2 PV in endocrine-sensitive patients with disease progression within ≤6 months had a sensitivity of 16.7% and a specificity of 83.1% (Supplementary Table S9, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335). The receiver operating characteristic curve yielded an area under the curve of 0.586 (Supplementary Figure S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335).

We identified 22 patients with a BRCA2 PV (19 germline, three tumoral) and MBC with either a pre-CDK4/6i archival tumor sample (n = 19), a post-CDK4/6i (n = 8), or both (n = 5) (Supplementary Table S10, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335). Clinical characteristics are described in Supplementary Table S11, (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335). Due to the heterogeneity in tissue availability, not all preplanned analyses were carried out across all samples.

Among the 13 pretreatment samples from BRCA2 PV carriers with sufficient tissue for RAD51 assay, five were nonevaluable due to technical constraints. Of the eight evaluable cases, we identified five samples with HR deficiency (HRD) and three with HR proficiency (HRP). RAD51 foci values ranged from 0% to 7% in the HRD group and from 15% to 43% in the HRP group. Median PFS did not differ significantly between these groups: 10 months (95% CI 9.0-NR) in HRD and 9 months (95% CI 8.1-NR) in HRP (HR 1.85, 95% CI 0.37-9.28, P = 0.500). Only one patient had RAD51 data available at both before and after treatment timepoints; this patient was HRP at baseline and remained HRP post-CDK4/6i, with a PFS of 11 months (Supplementary Figure S8, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335).

PAM50 intrinsic subtype analysis from BRCA2 PV carriers was carried out on 15 available pre-CDK4/6i samples; one was nonevaluable. Among the other 14, the subtype distribution included one luminal A tumor (PFS 11 months, 95% CI 8-14), three luminal B (median PFS 13 months; 95% CI 10-NR), two HER2-negative enriched (PFS 20 months; 95% CI 9.0–NR), three basal-like (PFS 12 months; 95% CI 9.0-NR), and five normal (median PFS 22 months; 95% CI 13-NR). No statistically significant association was found between intrinsic subtype and PFS (P > 0.05) (Supplementary Figure S8, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335).

RB1 LOH was evaluable in pre-CDK4/6i samples from six patients with BRCA2 PV. Four patients had LOH in RB1 (median PFS 11 months, 95% CI 8-NR). One patient had an equivocal RB1 status (PFS 30 months), and one had no RB1 LOH (PFS 47 months). No significant association was observed between RB1 LOH status and PFS in this cohort (p>0.05) (Supplementary Figure S9, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108335); however, this result may be limited by the small sample size and lack of statistical power.

Post-CDK4/6i tumor sequencing was carried out in two samples. Both cases had a tumoral BRCA2 PV with a variant allele fraction >50% and concordant with their germline status, and both had a mutation in GATA3. No definite RB1 second-hit mutations were identified, but the small number of cases precludes definitive conclusions.

Discussion

We conducted a multicenter, real-world case–control study including 116 patients with MBC harboring HRR PVs and treated with first-line CDK4/6i and ET. Notably, we observed differences in survival outcomes, both PFS and OS, among carriers of a germline BRCA2 PV compared with controls. After a median follow-up of 44 months, patients with a germline BRCA2 PV had significantly shorter PFS in the multivariable analysis (HR 2.73; P < 0.001). No significant differences in outcomes were observed between patients with non-BRCA2 HRR PV and controls.

Similar results were observed in patients with tumoral BRCA2 PV, although germline and somatic BRCA2 PV differ in their origin, both may converge at the tumor level on a HRD phenotype, providing a plausible shared biological basis.20 However, given the small size of the BRCA2 PV tumor-detected subgroup (n = 7), these subgroup-specific estimates should be interpreted with caution. In addition, interpretation of the tumor-detected subgroup is further limited by incomplete germline testing in a subset of cases; consequently, not all tumor-detected BRCA2 PV could be definitively classified as confirmed somatic events. Among controls, 14 had available tumor testing, and all were negative for BRCA2 PV. Because somatic BRCA2 PV appear to be uncommon in germline negative BC, misclassification is unlikely and, if present, would likely attenuate rather than inflate the observed differences.

Our study reinforces previous findings reporting that patients with a BRCA2 PV and HR-positive/HER2-negative MBC have worse outcomes in first-line treatment with CDK4/6i plus ET. This observation aligns with several retrospective cohorts and two recent meta-analyses.16, 17, 18, 19, 20,34,35 To our knowledge, our study is one of the largest multicenter series restricted to the first-line setting, thereby minimizing confounding from prior therapies and treatment heterogeneity. We also incorporated a matched control group with confirmed negative germline testing, reducing the risk of misclassification bias that may have affected earlier reports. Unlike other previous studies that pooled HRR-related genes, our analysis isolates BRCA2, demonstrating that the adverse outcomes are specifically driven by germline BRCA2 PVs. Additional evidence supporting this finding has recently been reported. In a comprehensive analysis, Safonov et al. examined outcomes of 977 patients treated with first-line CDK4/6i and ET, including 51 BRCA2 germline PV carriers and 926 WTs. BRCA2 carriers experienced significantly shorter PFS (HR 2.17).20 These poorer outcomes were consistent across therapy lines and were not observed with other treatments, suggesting that BRCA2 germline PV may be specifically associated with resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors.20 Similarly, a recently published meta-analysis including data from 14 studies with a total of 618 patients with a germline BRCA1/2 PV reached similar conclusions to our findings, reporting a HR for PFS of 1.68 and a HR for OS of 1.73 comparing germline BRCA1/2 PV carriers with WT counterparts. Only one study within the meta-analysis evaluated BRCA1 and BRCA2 carriers separately; in that study, BRCA1 carriers did not show statistically significant differences in clinical outcomes.17 Additionally, Azim et al. reported similarly poor clinical outcomes in both the adjuvant and metastatic settings among patients with germline BRCA1/2 PVs treated with CDK4/6i.16

In our cohort, the presence of a germline BRCA2 PV and endocrine resistance to adjuvant therapy were both independently associated with shorter PFS, consistent with previous reports.36 All patients received CDK4/6i-based therapy in first-line metastatic setting, with no prior ET therapy for metastatic disease. Endocrine sensitivity was therefore defined according to standard clinical criteria based on adjuvant ET. Surprisingly, among BRCA2 carriers, PFS did not differ significantly between the endocrine-sensitive and endocrine-resistant subgroups. Importantly, within the endocrine-sensitive subset, patients with a germline BRCA2 PV experienced significantly shorter PFS compared with controls (12 versus 39 months; P < 0.001), reinforcing the adverse impact of germline BRCA2 PVs regardless of endocrine sensitivity. These results support the concept of an intrinsic resistance to CDK4/6i among germline BRCA2 PV carriers. Because this is an observational study, the association between germline BRCA2 PV and poorer outcomes cannot be interpreted as causal. BRCA2 status may reflect underlying adverse tumor biology rather than a direct mechanism of resistance to CDK4/6 inhibition. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and the distinction between predictive and prognostic effects cannot be fully disentangled in this dataset.

Of note, among cases, 26 patients received subsequent PARPi, with a median PFS of 11 months, which is comparable to the 10 months reported in the OlympiAD trial for HR-positive/HER2-negative tumors37,38 and the 11.8 months reported by Zattarin et al. following first-line of CDK4/6i therapy.39 This suggests that PARPi may remain effective after CDK4/6i, likely due to distinct resistance mechanisms.

Biallelic RB1 loss, a well-established mechanism of resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors, has been reported in tumors from BRCA2 carriers, potentially reflecting a shared biological vulnerability.20 This may be partly explained by the higher likelihood of RB1 LOH in BRCA2-mutated tumors, possibly due to genomic proximity, as well as the broader genomic instability characteristic of HR-deficient tumors, which may predispose to RB1 loss-of-function alterations. Importantly, RB1 LOH is not restricted to BRCA2-altered cases: in a large independent cohort of patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer treated with first-line CDK4/6 inhibitors, it was observed in 47.8% of tumors and it was associated with significantly shorter PFS and OS, supporting its broader role as a determinant of CDK4/6i resistance beyond the BRCA2 context.20 In our cohort, RB1 LOH was observed in four out of five evaluable samples. Although RB1 LOH was not statistically associated with PFS, likely due to the small sample size, the median PFS was 11 months in patients with LOH compared with 47 months in the single patient without RB1 LOH. Similarly, no statistically significant association was found between intrinsic subtypes and PFS. However, the small cohort size, heterogeneous tissue availability, and incomplete assay coverage across samples limit statistical power and the interpretability of these exploratory analyses, and therefore these findings in our study should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than robust evidence of a definitive biological mechanism.

Our findings suggest that early progression on first-line CDK4/6i therapy among patients classified as endocrine-sensitive may be associated with the presence of a germline BRCA2 PV. However, given the modest discriminative performance observed, this observation should not be interpreted as a routine screening indication for BRCA testing. Rather, these results highlight that unexpectedly rapid progression in an ostensibly endocrine-sensitive patient may reflect specific underlying biological features, including BRCA2 alterations, and reinforce the importance of proactive germline testing in these patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative MBC.

Despite the strengths of this study, including relatively large, clinically well-annotated cohorts, several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective design and selection bias, with patients referred for testing based on clinical criteria, may have influenced some of the observed associations. The young median age of cases and controls likely reflects referral and selection bias, as younger patients are more often tested for germline variants40,41; however, this approach was preferred over including individuals with unknown genetic status, which could have introduced misclassification bias. The limited number of PVs in genes other than germline BRCA2 restricted our ability to draw firm conclusions regarding their predictive impact; however, germline BRCA2 PVs represent the most frequent germline HRR alterations in HR-positive/HER2-negative tumors, which underscores the clinical relevance of the observed associations.42,43 Germline testing status were missing in six cases with tumoral PVs, precluding the origin of the PV. Similarly, tumoral testing excluding a somatic PV in BRCA1/2 was not required for control selection. Overall, although paired normal samples were not available and formal assessment of second hits was not carried out, the combination of high VAF in ctDNA and mutation context provides supportive but not definitive evidence for possible germline origin in a subset of cases (n = 5). Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis generating and not as a direct basis for germline-driven clinical decision making. Finally, mechanistic exploration of resistance was limited by the small number and high heterogeneity of tumor samples, precluding correlation analyses.

Currently, patients with ER-positive MBC are treated with first-line CDK4/6i and ET,2,26,44 but there are no data from phase III randomized trials specifically addressing outcomes in carriers of a HRR gene PV.

Our findings are in line with prior evidence16, 17, 18, 19, 20 and suggest that in BRCA2 carriers, the most appropriate treatment and sequencing strategy in the metastatic setting may need to be further evaluated. One potential approach would be initiating treatment with a PARPi and reserving ET+CDK4/6i for second line, particularly in patients who acquire reversion mutations and restore BRCA2 function, therefore turning into HRP tumors. One may hypothesize that in these cases, the presence of RB1 LOH might be less prevalent. Prospective studies evaluating the optimal sequencing of PARPi and CDK4/6i, particularly in patients with germline BRCA2 PVs and HR-positive/HER2-negative MBC, are essential to refine treatment strategies for this population. In this regard, an ongoing phase III clinical trial (NCT06380751) is assessing in first-line the combination of a second-generation SERD with CDK4/6i, versus SERD with PARPi, versus standard ET with CDK4/6i, in patients with a germline or somatic BRCA1/2, or PALB2 gene PV.

To conclude, our study provides real-world evidence that germline BRCA2 PV, are independently associated with poor clinical outcomes in patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative MBC treated with first-line CDK4/6i plus ET. These findings highlight the need to develop novel therapeutic approaches and to refine treatment sequencing for this subgroup and reinforce the importance of comprehensive genetic characterization to guide personalized treatment decisions.
