Federated continual learning for privacy-preserving chest radiograph classification
Authors: Anay Sinhal, Amit Sinhal, Arpana Sinhal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multi-site deep learning for chest radiographs runs into two problems that are usually treated separately: privacy regulations prevent hospitals from pooling raw images, and clinical workflows change over time in ways that cause sequential model updates to overwrite earlier knowledge. Federated learning addresses the first problem, but most FL systems are built for static data distributions. Federated continual learning (FCL) handles both, though the methods that perform best on benchmarks tend to require public surrogate datasets or stored image replay — neither of which is easy to justify in a hospital context. We propose DP-FedEPC (Differentially Private Federated Elastic Prototype Consolidation), which combines elastic weight consolidation (EWC), prototype-based rehearsal, and client-side DP-SGD within a standard FedAvg workflow. EWC keeps weight updates from drifting away from parameters learned on earlier tasks. A small bank of latent prototypes — not raw images — holds class geometry stable across task shifts. Introduction

Chest radiography sits at the centre of cardiothoracic screening and is one of the more thoroughly studied tasks in medical imaging. Convolutional networks have shown they can match or approach radiologist-level performance on specific findings, but only when trained on large, varied datasets1. A model trained at a single institution often fails when deployed at another because the two hospitals use different scanners, different reporting templates, and see different patient populations. That gap compounds when the destination site is smaller or underrepresented in public data2.

Federated learning is the natural response: let hospitals jointly train a shared model without shipping raw radiographs to a central server3,4. It works reasonably well when the task is fixed. The trouble is that hospital data is not fixed. Labeling conventions change, new pathologies get added to the annotation protocol, scanner hardware turns over. If a federation simply fine-tunes the global model on the newest data, the model forgets what it learned earlier. This is catastrophic forgetting, and it happens in a setting where the privacy constraints already make the standard remedies — like replaying old training images — impractical. If a hospital (or a federation) updates a model on the newest data only, it can forget earlier tasks. This is catastrophic forgetting, now in a distributed, privacy-constrained setting.

Federated continual learning (FCL) aims to learn sequential tasks across multiple sites while preserving earlier performance5. Many recent FCL methods rely on public surrogate data, generators, or data-free distillation to approximate replay6,7. Those assumptions can break in clinical deployments. At the same time, prototypes are already a well-established tool in standard FL to handle heterogeneity and domain shift by exchanging compact class-level representations instead of raw data or full replay buffers8,9. What is still missing is a practical way to bring prototype ideas into the FCL setting while also giving formal privacy guarantees.

We address this gap for chest radiograph classification through DP-FedEPC (Differentially Private Federated Elastic Prototype Consolidation). The method integrates three components suited to clinical constraints: EWC to limit drift on parameters tied to earlier tasks, prototype rehearsal via latent embeddings rather than stored images to preserve class structure, and client-side DP-SGD giving each hospital a formal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\varepsilon , \delta )$$\end{document} guarantee. We accompany the main evaluation with interpretability and failure analysis to identify which findings are forgotten and under what conditions.

Background and related work

Federated learning in medical imaging

FL has been applied to medical imaging across classification, segmentation, and detection tasks. In the case of chest imaging as an example of a radiology subdomain, work on FL showed that it is possible to lower site-specific changes and increase the generalization capabilities of an algorithm, and all this without the need to pool raw data11,12. FL has also been used in imaging domains other than radiology. These include segmentation and classification of images based on their pathology, primarily to address regulatory constraints on the sharing of data13.

Prototype-based federated learning (ProtoFL): The use of prototypes is established in FL. Some studies use class prototypes (feature centroids representing a class or embedding) to address the problems of non-IID distributed data, client heterogeneity, and domain shift. This involves the alignment of the representation space across the different sites8,9. There is great scope for chest radiography. In the case of differing acquisition protocols and population mixes, prototype exchange can also reduce the communication burden relative to sharing raw data. Prototype embedding offers flexibility for the researcher and can easily be interpreted, as prototypes can be visualized in embedding space. The use of prototypes as a baseline for traditional FL has been reiterated in recent works with domain shift and adaptive prototype alignment14–16. What has not been well covered is how to carry these benefits into federated continual learning, where tasks evolve over time and privacy constraints are strict.

Multimodal architectures are an active and complementary direction. PCa-Mamba, for instance, uses spatiotemporal state space models on multi-parametric MRI for prostate cancer detection, finding that capturing temporal and spectral dependencies across imaging sequences raises diagnostic sensitivity17. The two directions address different axes of variation. Multimodal methods exploit multiple signals within a single site, whereas federated continual learning is built around temporal and spatial heterogeneity across institutions for single-modality data. As modality availability varies between hospitals and changes over time, future FCL designs will likely need to handle multimodal inputs as a first-class concern.

Continual learning and catastrophic forgetting

Continual learning (CL) targets the problem of learning from a stream of tasks without losing performance on earlier ones. The main strategies split into three broad families, though the boundaries are blurry in practice. Regularization methods add penalties that discourage changes to weights that mattered for earlier tasks. EWC18 is the canonical example: it adds a quadratic term weighted by the diagonal Fisher information, so parameters the model relied on heavily are harder to move. Synaptic Intelligence and Memory Aware Synapses take a similar approach but estimate importance differently — through path integrals over training trajectories or running gradient statistics. All of them share the same tradeoff: the penalty is only as good as the importance estimate, and those estimates can be noisy when computed on small local datasets.

Rehearsal methods keep a subset of old examples and replay them during new-task training. iCaRL19 formalized this as class-specific exemplar sets combined with a nearest-mean-of-exemplars classifier. The obvious objection is that storing pixels from past cases is essentially holding onto the data you wanted to forget. Later work started replacing stored images with latent prototypes or synthetic approximations — reducing memory footprint and, at least partially, the exposure risk20. Architectural approaches such as dynamically expandable networks take a different path entirely, growing new capacity for each task rather than replaying old data.

In medical imaging, CL has been explored for both segmentation and classification. Lifelong nnU-Net21 builds a standardized benchmark for sequential segmentation, showing that multiple CL strategies can be integrated on top of nnU-Net with manageable forgetting. A broader comparison by Quarta et al.4 across classification settings found that both regularization and rehearsal methods can hold performance across incremental tasks, though the margins depend on available memory and the degree of task similarity.

Federated continual learning

Federated continual learning (FCL) extends FL and CL together, requiring a global model to retain knowledge across two dimensions of heterogeneity: spatial (across clients) and temporal (across tasks). Yang et al. formalized this as spatial-temporal catastrophic forgetting and proposed a knowledge fusion view of FCL, drawing a distinction between synchronous and asynchronous scenarios22. Contemporaneous surveys by Hamedi et al. and Birashk et al.5 offer a broader taxonomy, organizing methods by fusion mechanism and cataloguing challenges around client heterogeneity, communication cost, and privacy.

The range of concrete algorithms is fairly wide. FedWeIT23 introduces learnable masks and client-specific transfer vectors so each site can hold on to local knowledge without fully overwriting what was shared; that controls cross-task interference but ships more data per round. CFeD24 takes a distillation route, using a public surrogate dataset to pass knowledge from old tasks into the new round — which works when you have such a dataset and raises questions when you do not. TARGET6 trains a generator to synthesize a proxy for the global distribution, removing the need for stored real examples but requiring the generator weights to be trained and transmitted. Federated Orthogonal Training25 is probably the most principled approach to forgetting prevention: it projects new-task gradient updates onto the orthogonal complement of the subspace used by previous tasks, so interference is bounded without storing any data at all. More recent data-free variants push in a similar direction7.

The common thread is that most of these methods need something extra — public data, a generator, stored exemplars, or intermediate representations that carry leakage risk. The surveys in5 note this plainly: formal differential privacy guarantees are largely missing from FCL. In a hospital context, model inversion and membership inference are not hypothetical risks. That gap is a real barrier to deployment, not just a checklist item.

Differential privacy in federated medical imaging

Differential privacy provides a formal bound on how much any single training example can change the output distribution of a learning algorithm. For any two datasets differing in one record, the probability ratio of observing a given output is bounded by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e^\varepsilon$$\end{document}, with slack δ. DP-SGD implements this during gradient-based training by clipping per-example gradients to a fixed norm C and adding Gaussian noise scaled to σ C. Privacy accountants — moments-based or Rényi DP — accumulate the budget across all training steps.

How much this costs in medical imaging has been studied across a few settings. Adnan et al.26 showed that DP-SGD can be applied to federated histopathology classification at moderate budgets without catastrophic accuracy loss, though there is a real gap relative to non-private training. Ziller et al.13 found similar results for CT segmentation across multiple sites. Nampalle et al.27 compared architectures and showed the trade-off varies non-trivially with model depth and dataset size. Zheng et al.28 went further by adapting noise levels to estimated gradient sensitivity rather than using a fixed multiplier, narrowing the accuracy gap somewhat.

All of this work assumes a fixed task for the life of training. None of it addresses a model that must keep learning new tasks without forgetting earlier ones. And on the FCL side, privacy is almost never formalized — data locality is treated as sufficient protection. The combination of sequential task learning and rigorous privacy accounting has not been seriously addressed.

Data and problem formulation

Datasets

CheXpert: CheXpert is a large public dataset of chest radiographs from Stanford Hospital, consisting of 224,316 images from 65,240 patients labeled for the presence of 14 observations, such as cardiomegaly, edema, and pleural effusion, with uncertainty labels derived from radiology reports1. We follow common practice and focus on a subset of 10 clinically important findings: cardiomegaly, edema, consolidation, pneumonia, atelectasis, pleural effusion, pneumothorax, support devices, enlarged cardiomediastinum, and lung lesion.

MIMIC-CXR: MIMIC-CXR is a large de-identified database of chest radiographs from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, comprising 377,110 images corresponding to 227,835 studies, with associated free-text reports10. We use the publicly available structured labels from PhysioNet, which map reports into CheXpert-style observations, to construct an out-of-domain test set.

Both datasets contain adult patients from tertiary care hospitals in the United States and are subject to label noise and reporting biases.

Clinically, a “task” here represents a realistic change in chest radiography practice over time. In our setup this includes (i) label set expansion (new findings start being labeled consistently), and (ii) protocol or scanner drift that changes the appearance statistics even for the same finding. This matters because forgetting is not uniform across findings. Some labels (for example, subtle opacities) can degrade faster than high-signal labels (for example, cardiomegaly). In Results, we therefore report per-finding performance and per-finding forgetting, not only macro averages.

Simulated hospital sites and temporal tasks

CheXpert does not include hospital identifiers, so we constructed the site structure ourselves. We partitioned by patient ID and frontal/lateral projection, treating each resulting subset as a separate hospital. Site A received 40 percent of patients (mostly frontal), Site B 30 percent (mixed projections), Site C 20 percent (filtered toward ICU admissions by report keyword), and Site D the remaining 10 percent (filtered toward follow-up studies). Patient assignment is disjoint across sites with no overlap. Each site ends up with a different prevalence profile across the 10 target findings — not as heterogeneous as real hospitals, but a reasonable proxy.

The task sequence is shared across all sites. Task 1 covers six findings: cardiomegaly, edema, consolidation, pleural effusion, pneumothorax, and support devices. Task 2 adds pneumonia and atelectasis. Task 3 adds lung lesion and enlarged cardiomediastinum, motivated roughly by oncology annotation programs that began labeling those findings later in clinical practice.

At each task boundary, only the current task’s data is available for training. Earlier cases are treated as archived or governance-restricted. This is a simplification — real hospitals vary in retention policy — but it is consistent with the scenario DP-FedEPC is designed for.

Preprocessing and cohort definition

Images from both datasets are resized to 320 × 320 pixels and normalized to dataset-wide mean and standard deviation. During training we apply random horizontal flips and small rotations. Any image with no labels across all 10 target findings is dropped.

CheXpert uses “uncertain” labels where the radiologist could not make a clear call. We map these to negative (the “U-zeroes” strategy), which is the standard conservative choice and keeps the label space binary.

For each site and task, patients are split into training (70 percent), validation (10 percent), and held-out test (20 percent). The split is by patient rather than by image, so the same patient cannot appear in both train and test. External evaluation on MIMIC-CXR uses the provided train-test split with our model trained only on CheXpert.

Prototype-based rehearsal without raw images

Raw replay buffers are hard to justify for chest radiographs because storing and reusing prior images can create governance and privacy issues. DP-FedEPC instead uses a small memory of latent prototypes inspired by iCaRL and prototype-based continual learning19.

Prototype memory: Each client stores up to M prototypes per class (finding). Why M = 20 in the main setup. We chose M = 20 as a small, fixed budget that stays practical per hospital and per finding, while still capturing more than one mode per class under site heterogeneity. With a ResNet-50 embedding (2048-dim float32), storing M prototypes is on the order of a few MB per site, which is negligible next to model weights. We still treat this as a tunable tradeoff and report an M sweep in Sect. "Ablations and design choices".

Prototype selection (why k-means): At the end of each task, client k builds a candidate pool of correctly classified examples from the current task data, embeds them, and runs k-means with K = M per class in latent space. We then store the nearest real example to each cluster centroid (not the centroid itself) to avoid creating synthetic points. K-means gives coverage across diverse appearance patterns (for example, portable AP views versus PA views) instead of overfitting the memory to common cases.

We compare k-means against simpler choices in Sect. "Ablations and design choices": random selection, herding (closest to class mean), and storing only the class mean embedding.

Client-side differential privacy

We use DP-SGD at each client to provide formal privacy guarantees for individual chest radiographs. For each mini-batch, client k computes per-example gradients for the total loss (classification + EWC + prototype terms), clips them to norm C, and adds Gaussian noise:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{g} = \frac{1}{B}\left( \sum _{i=1}^{B} \textrm{clip}(g_i,\, C)\right) + \mathscr {N}\!\left( 0,\, \sigma ^2 C^2 I\right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where B is the batch size, C is the clipping norm, and σ is the noise multiplier.

We track privacy using a moments/RDP accountant26 and report the achieved privacy budgets as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\varepsilon , \delta )$$\end{document} for each experiment setting in Table 7.

DP settings used: In all experiments we fix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta = 10^{-5}$$\end{document} and vary the noise multiplier σ. We also report batch size, sampling rate, total local steps, accountant type, and δ. This makes the privacy-utility tradeoff concrete instead of describing it qualitatively.

Threat model (what DP covers): Our DP guarantee is at the client level for training participation: it bounds what an attacker can infer about any single radiograph in a client’s training stream from the shared outputs (model updates and any shared statistics), under the standard DP-SGD assumptions. It does not guarantee removal of all representation-level leakage in downstream use, so we treat it as a strong baseline guarantee, not a complete privacy solution.

Prototype leakage and threat model

Prototypes are safer than raw image replay, but they are not “free.” A prototype embedding is still a learned representation of real data. In principle, an attacker could try membership inference (was a specific patient image used) or inversion-style attacks (recover partial information) from embeddings if they had enough side information.

Storing raw pixels makes the risk concrete and direct. A leaked replay buffer contains actual patient radiographs. Membership inference against a model trained with raw replay is also more tractable because the model has likely memorized specific training images.

Prototype embeddings are a step removed. They are lower-dimensional, not visually interpretable, and we do not store pixel values. An embedding that encodes the structural appearance of a pleural effusion is harder to trace back to a specific patient than the radiograph itself. That said, “harder” is not “safe.” Embeddings still capture appearance statistics derived from real cases, and an adversary with model access and enough side information could attempt membership inference against the embedding space.

What we do now is conservative: prototypes stay local by default, and when we share any prototype-derived statistics, we clip and noise them under the same DP mechanism used for training. We do not claim prototypes eliminate leakage. We claim they reduce reliance on raw replay and, paired with DP-SGD, give a clear formal bound on training-data privacy. We include a short failure-oriented discussion in Sect. "Privacy considerations and practical deployment".

Ablations and design choices

Table 5 steps through the components. Removing EWC (FedProto row) drops AUROC by 1.9 points and raises forgetting to 4.1. Removing prototypes (FedEWC row) gives similar numbers in the other direction. Neither mechanism alone matches the combination, and the gap is not small. FedEPC without DP reaches 86.2% and 2.0 forgetting points — the best of any configuration we ran.

Adding DP at σ = 0.5 costs 0.3 AUROC points and 0.2 forgetting points. At σ = 1.0 the cost is 1.6 AUROC points, but still far better than naive fine-tuning. The two mechanisms do different work: EWC constrains parameter drift, prototypes keep the output geometry aligned to past distributions. Discussion

Addressing spatial-temporal catastrophic forgetting

The 7.5-point AUROC gap and the 7.5-point forgetting gap between FedAvg-Seq and DP-FedEPC (σ = 0.5) come from two compounding problems in naive sequential FL. The first is within-site forgetting: fine-tuning on new task data pushes weights toward the new distribution and away from what mattered earlier. The second is cross-site interference: clients with different case mixes pull the global model in different directions each round, amplifying forgetting beyond what any single site would see training locally. Local-CL reaches 4.9 forgetting points; FedAvg-Seq reaches 9.7. That gap is the federation tax on top of ordinary sequential forgetting.

EWC handles the first problem reasonably well. It is less effective on the second, because Fisher importance computed at one site does not capture what the other sites need. The penalty can end up too weak against cross-site interference, or strong in the wrong directions. Prototypes approach the problem differently — by holding the output space aligned with representative past cases, they act as anchors in feature space even when parameter values drift substantially. The model can still produce correct logits for the stored prototypes regardless of how much it has changed to fit new data.

These two mechanisms address forgetting at different levels, which is why stacking them helps. The gap between FedEPC and either FedEWC or FedProto alone in Table 5 is not marginal — it is consistent and worth the added complexity.

Privacy considerations and practical deployment

The privacy argument has two parts, and it is worth keeping them separate.

The first part is formal. These are the strongest claims we make, and they cover training-data participation only — the standard DP-SGD threat model.

The second part is practical. Prototype rehearsal replaces raw pixel storage, removing the most direct form of data exposure. A leaked prototype bank does not contain radiographs. But prototypes are not private by construction — they are embeddings computed from real cases, and an adversary who can query the model and knows something about the data distribution could attempt membership inference against the embedding space. We apply the same DP mechanism to any prototype statistics that leave a client’s local storage. What we do not claim is that prototypes eliminate leakage. The formal guarantee is the DP bound on training participation, and nothing more.

Clinical relevance and robustness

The forgetting problem has a direct clinical reading. When a hospital updates its annotation protocol to include oncology-relevant findings — lung lesion and enlarged cardiomediastinum — a model retrained only on those new cases will get better at the new task and worse at the old ones, including common findings like pleural effusion that the clinical team still depends on. DP-FedEPC’s 2.2-point forgetting versus FedAvg-Seq’s 9.7 is the difference between a model that stays clinically usable across all tasks and one that essentially needs retraining each time the protocol changes. In multi-site networks, strong forgetting of small or specialized sites would exacerbate disparities, as those hospitals could see degraded performance despite contributing data. These implications warrant validation in real multi-institutional deployments.

Our external validation on MIMIC-CXR suggests that FCL with consolidation can improve robustness to domain shift compared to naive fine-tuning, likely because federated collaboration exposes the model to a broader range of imaging characteristics even under continual constraints. Still, absolute performance remains lower on MIMIC-CXR than on CheXpert, highlighting the difficulty of fully bridging distribution gaps.

Comparison to existing FCL methods

The FCL methods that are most similar to DP-FedEPC differ not only in mechanism but also in what they require from the deployment environment. TARGET6 trains a generator to synthesize global data for exemplar-free distillation. This avoids storing real images, but it requires the generator weights to be trained and sent. FedWeIT23 uses learnable masks and inter-client transfer parameters to control the amount of knowledge that is shared, but at an expense of additional communication. Generator and data-free replay variants7 decrease dependency on the storage of data, but usually they do not provide formal privacy protections. Orthogonal subspace methods25 also limit forgetting but usually assume public surrogate data or synchronous participation patterns.

Future directions

Adaptive noise allocation is the most natural extension. We use a fixed σ across all clients, rounds, and parameter groups. Sensitivity-aware mechanisms that assign more noise to high-risk updates and less to stable, low-information ones could narrow the accuracy gap at equivalent privacy budgets. Zheng et al.28 showed this is feasible in static FL; extending it to the continual setting is non-trivial but tractable.

Robustness against adversarial clients is a separate concern. DP-SGD limits what honest clients reveal but does not protect against a site that deliberately poisons its updates. Combining DP-FedEPC with robust aggregation rules — median-based or geometric median approaches — would make the system more realistic for open or semi-trusted federations.

Two further directions seem promising:Evaluating FCL under real multi-institutional collaborations spanning different regions and equipment vendors, using federated infrastructures already piloted for other imaging tasks.Extending the framework to foundation models or self-supervised pretraining, where FCL and DP constraints interact with representation learning at scale.

Conclusion

We investigated federated continual learning for chest radiograph classification under realistic privacy constraints, using a simulated multi-hospital federated setting. Using CheXpert and MIMIC-CXR as benchmarks, we formalized an FCL setting where hospitals act as federated clients with temporally evolving tasks and limited access to past data.

To address catastrophic forgetting across sites and tasks while preserving privacy, we proposed DP-FedEPC, which combines elastic weight consolidation, prototype-based rehearsal, and client-side differential privacy within a standard FedAvg framework. DP-FedEPC reduces forgetting from 9.7 to 2.2 forgetting points under moderate noise, improves macro-AUROC across all sites and tasks compared to naive sequential fine-tuning, and maintains this advantage on MIMIC-CXR. These results hold under DP noise levels with achieved \varepsilon values consistent with health data applications in the literature.

The results support the view that combining regularization, prototype rehearsal, and client-side DP can make FCL workable for hospital imaging networks that must update models sequentially under data governance constraints. The evaluation is limited to a simulated setting, and extending these findings to real deployments will require testing under asynchronous participation, larger hospital counts, and the operational privacy accounting that production environments demand.
