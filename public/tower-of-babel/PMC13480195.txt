Bereaved informal caregivers’ experiences with a Canadian home hospice program: a qualitative study
Authors: Miao Li, Davina Banner, Aderonke Agboji, Christopher A. Klinger, Donna Flood, Shannon Freeman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMost individuals wish to die at home, yet in Canada this is not always possible. Informal caregivers play a vital role to support individuals who wish to receive home-based end-of-life care and die at their own homes. Community-based home hospice programs aim to support both individuals at the end of life and their informal caregivers, however, informal caregivers’ experiences within such programs, particularly in northern communities, remain underexplored. This study aimed to describe the experiences of bereaved informal caregivers who received support from a community-based home hospice program.MethodsA qualitative approached was used. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with bereaved informal caregivers (N = 15) approximately three months following the death of their care recipient. All participants had been involved as informal caregivers as part of a home hospice program in a geographically isolated, medium sized northern city in Western Canada. Interviews explored their experiences of the program, including perceived benefits and challenges. Data was analyzed using thematic analysis following Braun & Clarke’s approach.ResultsParticipants described both emotional and practical benefits of home hospice, as well as challenges related to timing of referral and aspects of service delivery. Five themes were identified that captured informal caregivers’ experiences with the home hospice program: a preference for home-based end-of-life care, the ability to fulfill the wishes of the person they cared for, relief associated with receiving hospice support, the importance of compassionate relationships with hospice staff, and the perceived need for early awareness and referrals to home hospice services.ConclusionsBereaved informal caregivers valued home hospice support for enabling home-based end-of-life care, honoring the wishes of the person they cared for, and reducing caregiver burden through compassionate and holistic support. Improving early awareness and timely referral to home hospice programs may further improve caregiver experiences and strengthen the delivery of home hospice services in Canadian northern communities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02158-1.

Background

Aging is a multifaceted process characterized by biological, physiological, and psychosocial changes that vary among individuals [31, 36]. Globally, the population is aging rapidly, with the number of adults aged 60 years and older projected to rise to 2.1 billion by 2050 [62]. In Canada, adults aged 65 years and older currently represent 17.5% of the population, a proportion expected to reach nearly 25% by 2040 [24]. This demographic shift is accompanied by increased prevalence of chronic, life-limiting diseases such as cancer, cardiovascular conditions, diabetes, and chronic respiratory disorders, which collectively account for the majority of global deaths annually [62].

The growing burden of chronic illness highlights the need for accessible palliative and end-of-life care. Palliative care is a holistic approach aimed at relieving suffering and enhancing quality of life through physical, emotional, psychosocial, and spiritual support for patients and their informal caregivers, regardless of whether curative treatments continue [28, 38]. Hospice care, a specialized form of palliative care typically provided when curative treatments are no longer pursued, focuses exclusively on comfort and dignity during the final stages of life [48, 49]. Hospice services may be delivered in hospitals, long-term care (LTC) facilities, hospice residences, or at home, depending on patient and family preference [38].

Home hospice care

Home hospice care offers a meaningful alternative to acute and LTC settings for individuals wishing to die in their home near loved ones [13, 53]. Home hospice programs provide symptom management, respite care, education on caregiving tasks, 24/7 phone support, and grief counselling [38, 42]. Individuals receiving hospice care at home report higher satisfaction, better pain management, and improved emotional well-being compared to institutional settings, alongside reduced healthcare costs and hospitalizations [11, 56]. Home-based end-of-life care, including hospice services, have been shown to increase the likelihood of dying at home [50]. In Canada, access to palliative care remains fragmented [21]. CIHI reported that in 2021–2022, 58% of those who died were identified as palliative and received some form of palliative care. Among these individuals, 61% received palliative care in hospital only, while 36% received palliative home care. The timing of access also varied, with a median of 22 days from identification to death overall, compared to 119 days among those receiving palliative home care [13]. Despite these benefits, misconceptions about hospice care persist, and delayed referrals remain common due to limited understanding among both the public and healthcare professionals [17, 34, 64].

Place of death is closely associated with perceptions of a “good death” and quality of end-of-life care [8, 41, 63]. In Canada, the proportion of palliative care recipients who die at home has increased in recent years, supported by expanded community-based services [13, 14]. However, barriers such as limited home care resources, inequitable access to palliative services, and caregiver burden continue to constrain many individuals from achieving preferred place of death at home [4, 13].

Although the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends early integration of palliative care across the illness trajectory [61], many Canadians receive it only near death, particularly in rural areas [13]. Rural residents face unique challenges, including limited access to specialist. Limited access to care, long travel distances, reduced availability of respite services, and increased social isolation contribute to higher rates of institutional death [5, 13, 19, 22, 32]. These structural inequities underscore the importance of community-based hospice programs in rural settings.

Within this context, informal caregivers play an important role in supporting individuals at the end of life [32]. Informal caregivers are unpaid family members or close friends who offer essential physical, emotional, and practical support [16, 25, 33]. They provide the majority of home-based end-of-life care and contribute substantially to patients’ ability to remain at home [32, 35]. While caregiving can enhance patients’ quality of life, it often places significant emotional, physical, and financial strain on informal caregivers [7, 51, 54].

Home hospice programs can substantially influence informal caregivers’ experiences during caregiving and into bereavement. Grief and bereavement support are recognized as part of the continuum of palliative and end-of-life care, reflecting the ongoing needs of informal caregivers beyond death of their care recipient. Bereavement, referring to the period of loss following death, is a phase during which caregivers continue to process and interpret their caregiving experiences [65]. Compassionate communication and practical support from hospice staff foster caregiver confidence, reduce burden, and promote relational continuity during end-of-life care [44]. For informal caregivers, achieving home death with hospice support can shape their bereavement and grief experiences. When a home death occurs where support is adequate to meet the care recipient and informal caregivers needs, informal caregivers typically report greater satisfaction and meaningfulness [51, 59]. Conversely, when preferred home death is not achieved and death occurs in a different loation, informal caregivers may experience increased risk for complicated grief, characterized by prolonged emotional distress, feelings of guilt, and dissatisfaction with care [6, 26, 59].

Despite these documented benefits, informal caregivers’ lived experiences within home hospice programs, especially in rural and underserved communities, remain underexplored [10]. To address this gap, this qualitative study sought to answer the research question: “What are the bereaved informal caregivers’ experiences with a home hospice program in a Canadian context?” Description of these experiences is essential for creating effective support systems that address informal caregivers' unique needs and improve the quality of home hospice services.

Methods

This study employed a qualitative approach to explore the lived experiences of bereaved informal caregivers participating in a home hospice program in a geographically isolated, mid-sized northern community in Western Canada. The study was conducted as part of a larger evaluation of the program. The hospice organization of focus offers on-site (residential in-patient) and at-home (community based) hospice programs to support individuals nearing end of life and those close to them (e.g., family and friend informal caregivers). Hospice care is delivered by an interdisciplinary team of palliative care nurses, care aides, family physicians, and trained volunteers. Nurses experienced in hospice palliative care offer regular and as-needed home visits, with 24/7 on-call and phone support, assisting with symptom relief, medication management and overall patient comfort. Care aides assist with personal care during the day and evening, while the patient’s family physician oversees medical care and prescribes medications as needed. Trained volunteers are assigned to families to provide emotional support before and after a patient’s passing. Volunteers received role-specific training, including education in caregiver communication and emotional support. The program offers comprehensive whole person support addressing the medical, emotional, practical and spiritual needs of patients and families to ensure they receive compassionate and professional care in their preferred setting.

Study participants

Participants in the study were informal caregivers, defined as unpaid family members who provided physical, emotional, or practical support to a person receiving care through the home program and who provided informed consent to participate. Informal caregivers were introduced to the study during program admission and provided consent for potential future contact. Hospice staff followed up with caregivers approximately three months after the death of their care recipient to confirm interest in participation. For those who agreed, contact information was shared with the research team, who then scheduled one-on-one interviews either in person or via Zoom (Zoom Communications, Inc., San Jose, CA) as was preference of the participant.

During this study, 15 interviews were conducted by an experienced qualitative researcher with clinical and research expertise in palliative and end-of-life care (MS, DB, and SF). Each interview was conducted at least three months following the death of the person they had cared for. Guided by a semi-structured interview format, the questions explored various dimensions of caregiving, including the benefits, challenges, and informal caregivers' overall perceptions of the support received from the program. Interviews were conducted either in-person or via Zoom as was the preference of the respective participant and lasted 30 min to one hour. The sample size was considered sufficient to provide rich and meaningful insights. Data saturation was achieved as no new themes were identified in the final three interviews. Ethical approval and informed consent procedures are reported in the Declarations section.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was conducted using Braun and Clarke's framework [9] to systematically explore participants’ experiences with the program. The process began with familiarization, where interview transcripts were reviewed multiple times to identify preliminary patterns and insights related to informal caregivers’ experiences, challenges, and perceptions of the home hospice program. Initial coding was conducted by tagging key segments that captured meaningful aspects of informal caregivers' experiences, such as emotional strain, practical challenges in end-of-life caregiving, and the support received from home hospice. These initial codes were then grouped into broader themes that aligned with the study’s objectives. For instance, “Challenges in Providing End-of-Life Care” and “Supports and Benefits with Home Hospice Program”. Themes were then reviewed by team members and through discussion were refined to ensure they accurately represented informal caregivers' perspectives and aligned with the study’s focus. Themes were named using direct participant quotes, such as “Home is much better”, reflecting informal caregivers’ strong preference for home hospice, and “It allowed me to fulfill my mother’s wishes”, highlighting the emotional significance of honouring the end-of-life preferences of the person they cared for. After final definition and naming, themes were synthesized into a comprehensive narrative, incorporating direct quotes to support findings and provide a rich, contextual understanding of bereaved informal caregivers’ experiences with the home hospice program. Findings were reported following the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (COREQ) guideline [57]. The COREQ checklist is provided in Appendix A for reference.

Ethical considerations

Ethics approval was provided by the University of Northern British Columbia (File No: 6009117). Informed consent to participate in the larger sized evaluation was obtained from the informal caregivers at time of entry into the home hospice program and then was reconfirmed at three months post-bereavement by hospice staff prior to invitation by the research team to participate in the interview and immediately prior to data collection. Participants had multiple opportunities to ask questions and engage in discussion about the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the start of the scheduled interview and reconfirmed prior to participation in the interview. Prior to analysis, all identifiable data were de-identified to protect the anonymity of participants. This study acknowledged the sensitive nature of bereaved informal caregivers’ experiences and aimed to analyze and report findings respectfully, ensuring that the dignity of participants and their narratives was preserved. The data was securely stored and accessible only to researcher team members in accordance with institutional data protection policies.

Trustworthiness

Rigor and trustworthiness were guided by Lincoln and Guba’s (1985) framework. Credibility and dependability were supported through systematic, iterative thematic analysis [9] and regular analytic discussions with the research supervisors. Transferability was facilitated through detailed contextual description, while reflexivity and supervisory feedback were used to support confirmability in the absence of member checking due to the use of secondary data.

Results

The experiences of 15 bereaved informal caregivers who supported clients enrolled in the home hospice program were explored to identify benefits, challenges, and areas for improvement in home hospice support within a geographically isolated, medium-sized northern city in Western Canada. The majority of participants were female (66.7%) and adult children of the deceased (53.3%), with a mean age of 61.7 years. Most patients were enrolled in the program for less than one week (73.4%) and were referred by healthcare professionals (66.7%), primarily physicians (40.0%). Data were organized thematically [9], five key themes were identified, including "home is much better", "it allowed me to fulfill my mother’s wishes", "it was a relief", "they were right there with a hug", and "earlier would have been better".

“Home is much better”

Participants described a strong preference for receiving home-based end-of-life care, where care recipient could remain in a familiar and comfortable environment surrounded by family and personal belongings. For some, home hospice enhanced care recipient’ comfort and quality of life in their final days by allowing them to stay with family, sleep in their own beds, or sit in their favorite chairs. Others valued how home hospice maintained care recipient’ dignity and autonomy by enabling them to choose daily activities, move around in a familiar environment with accessibility adjustments in the house, and make end-of-life decisions that aligned with their own values and preferences. One participant emphasized the significance of staying home for maintaining the autonomy of the care recipient, stating: “She knew where her bathroom was. She could tell if somebody was using something in the kitchen that, ‘Hey, that's not the way I would use it.’ She was still in control because it was her house, which made a big difference” (P17, Female, Aged 50–60, Adult Child).

For all participants, home hospice empowered them with greater control and convenience in caregiving. It allowed them to provide care at a familiar environment, continue daily routines, actively engage in the care process with guidance from hospice staff, and remain physically closed with the care recipient without the stress of constant travel to healthcare facilities. Home hospice was widely viewed as the most comfortable and preferable option, not only for the care recipient but for the entire family, with one participant reflecting on the collective comfort of staying home, stating: “Home is much better than going into a setting where there’s other people. You’re more familiar, you’re more comfortable… for everybody” (P07, Male, Aged 40–50, Adult Child). Reflecting on previous hospital experiences, hospitals were commonly described as lacking support, privacy and dignity. Participants described hospital environments as crowded and impersonal, with limited space for families to stay together, especially in emergency rooms with busy schedules and medical equipment. Aggressive medical interventions were often prioritized without sufficient communication about health conditions, end-of-life care plans, and available care options, depriving patients and participants of the opportunity to make preferred end-of-life decisions that aligned with their needs. One participant described their frustrating experience in trying to stop unnecessary treatments, saying: "I was like, ‘No, no, no, he's dying. We don't need all this aggressive.’ So, they were all being aggressive … I was like, ‘Can we pause for a minute here, please?’” (P16, Female, Aged 40–50, Adult Child). Additionally, hospital stays often resulted in emotional distress and loss of dignity. Some patients were reluctant to go to the hospital, and in some cases, even expressed fear at the idea of being admitted. The lack of space, privacy, and suitable medical support led participants to describe hospitals as places where people: “[L]ose all dignity” and a “horrible place to have to go” (P18, Female, Aged 60–70, Spouse).

“It allowed me to fulfill my mother’s wishes”

One of the most deeply meaningful aspects of participants’ experiences with the home hospice program was the ability the wishes of the care recipient to remain at home in their final days. Participants often recalled that the care recipient had explicitly expressed a desire to avoid hospitalization and spend their final moments in a familiar and comforting environment, surrounded by family. It brought a sense of responsibility and purpose to honor the wish of the care recipient, despite the physical and emotional demands of caregiving. One participant emphasized the significance of fulfilling this final wish, stating: "I think what is the biggest aspect, is that you can fill a person’s wish to be in their home” (P12, Male, Aged 70–80, Adult Child). The ability to fulfill these final wishes was often accompanied by feelings of peace and comfort, and some even described it as a “blessing”, knowing they had done everything possible to provide the care recipient with a familiar, dignified and loving environment with the support of home hospice. One participant reflected on honoring his wife’s desire to pass at home, sharing through tears: “That was nice. It was definitely one of her wishes, so. And it was nice to have the support of the home hospice to do that too” (P01, Male, Aged 60–70, Spouse).

For some, the decision to pursue home hospice was deeply personal, reflecting their commitment and sense of duty. Some participants expressed a strong sense of responsibility to personally provide care, ensuring the care recipient was not alone in an unfamiliar setting. One participant recalled how she insisted on keeping her husband at home despite his concerns about burdening her, stating: “He didn't want to die at home. He said, ‘I would feel bad.’ And I kept saying, ‘No this way you're here I can look after you through the night’” (P10, Female, Aged 60–70, Spouse). For others, home hospice was about maintaining a deep physical and emotional connection, ensuring they were fully present in final moments of the care recipient, with one participant reflecting: "I wanted to be with him for every second. And so, home hospice made that possible. Yeah. I would have missed out on so much" (P05, Female, Aged 50–60, Spouse). Beyond obligation and presence, some viewed home hospice as a way to honor personal commitment to a shared journey made earlier in the clients’ illness, allowing them to stay together through every stage of the journey.

Despite their intentions, not all participants were able to fulfill the wish of the person they cared for to remain home, which sometimes led to prolonged feelings of guilt and regret. In some cases, the overwhelming caregiving burdens or a rapid health deterioration made it impossible to sustain home care, ultimately leading to the decision to transition the person they were caring for to a healthcare facility. One participant reflected on her past experience of caring for her husband, expressing deep regret over not being able to fulfill his wish, sharing: “I felt bad for that… I still feel bad that you didn’t fulfill the thing [home death] that he wanted” (P11, Female, Aged 60–70, Adult Child). However, this unresolved guilt became a driving force in her later decision to pursue home hospice for other family members. As her mother’s condition progressed and the strain of caregiving became overwhelming, the family ultimately decided to transition her to the local hospice facility for additional support. Unlike her previous experience with her husband, this transition was seen as a relief rather than a regret, as her mother understood their burden and decision, and the family was satisfied with the care provided at the hospice.

“It was a relief”

Participants consistently described home hospice as a profound source of relief, offering holistic support that extended beyond patient medical care. Some expressed the immediate easing of caregiving stress once the hospice team became involved, with one participant recalling the emotional release upon the hospice team’s arrival, stating: “When they came in and took over. It was like a relief… It was really, really good” (P03, Female, Aged 60–70, Adult Child). The program offered a comprehensive approach, covering medication and symptom management, hands-on training in caregiving tasks, regular home visits, and respite care. These supports enhanced participants’ confidence and preparedness in their caregiving roles and reduced the emotional and physical strain of providing end-of-life care. Beyond medical duties, hospice staff also assisted with household tasks such as cleaning snow or managing the yard, which participants found unexpectedly helpful. Some assumed that home hospice would focus strictly on patients’ medical care and were pleasantly surprised to find that their own well-being was also prioritized, with staff regularly checking in on their emotional state. One participant recalling a moment when this unexpected support caught him off guard, stating:“That was surprising. I thought they were there for my mom, but they made sure I was okay… And I thought she was talking about Mom. She was, ‘No, how are YOU? How are you taking this? How are you?’ It took me off guard.” (P07, Male, Aged 50–60, Adult Child).

“That was surprising. I thought they were there for my mom, but they made sure I was okay… And I thought she was talking about Mom. She was, ‘No, how are YOU? How are you taking this? How are you?’ It took me off guard.” (P07, Male, Aged 50–60, Adult Child).

Despite the overall positive emotional impact of home hospice, some also experienced frustration with medication pickup procedures, where participants or other family members were responsible for picking up the medication from a pharmacy due to the compensation rules. Although it was not described as a major issue, some participants found it “strange”, “frustrating”, and “confusing”, especially for those without additional family support, those living in remote areas or those who lacked access to transportation. The process was further complicated by disorganization, with participants experiencing miscommunication between hospice staff and pharmacies, receiving incorrect medications, and having to make multiple trips to get correct medication. In addition to the inconvenience, some participants struggled with the emotional difficulty of leaving the person they cared for alone during critical moments to pick up the medication, with one participant reflecting on this challenge, stating: “The only thing that I find strange, and I don't know why, is that I had to go get the medicine; I couldn't… So, I had to leave her when she was hurting the most to go get the medication” (P07, Male, Aged 50–60, Adult Child). Some suggested that designating volunteers or trained personnel for medication delivery could alleviate the burden on the family.

Before enrolling in home hospice, participants described feeling exhausted, overwhelmed and unprepared for end-of-life caregiving. Some struggled with managing daily tasks alone and reported difficulties in accessing consistent community-based home care, with care providers reluctant to travel “too far” or “too long”. Hospitals, meanwhile, were commonly seen as unsupportive and unhelpful in meeting the needs of participants and the care recipient. One participant recalled a frustrating experience in which a simply attempt to seek assistance with symptom management led them to the emergency room. Ultimately, they had to leave the hospital feeling helpless, stating: "We left the hospital ourselves because there was no one else to assist us" (P16, Female, Aged 50–60, Adult Child). The lack of sufficient external support resulted in significant physical and emotional strain on participants, leaving them feeling helpless and frustrated during providing end-of-life care.

“They were right there with a hug”

Hospice staff were commonly described as “professional”, “supportive”, and “compassionate”, establishing trust and positive relationships through their consistent availability and open communication. Participants emphasized that hospice staff were reliable, responsive, and attentive, whether by phone or in person. Knowing that hospice staff was “always there to help” provided participants emotional security and strengthened trust in the relationship. With one participant sharing her confidence in staff, saying: “You know, if I had a meltdown, they [hospice nurses] were right there with a hug” (P05, Female, Aged 50–60, Spouse). Others noted that hospice staff worked around family schedules and assisted with any tasks, with one participant sharing how she felt about staff: “If there was anything I didn't want to do, they'd do it” (P18, Female, Aged 60–70, Adult Spouse).

Open and effective communication was also important for building trust and positive relationships between participants and hospice staff. Participants highly appreciated being kept informed and involved throughout the caregiving process, including regular updates on the care recipient’s conditions, detailed explanations of care procedures, and guidance on additional resources, such as funeral and grief services. Some noted the hospice staff’s transparency and willingness to provide information, with one participant sharing: “One of the things she [hospice nurse] did was she asked how much I wanted to know. When I told her that I wanted to know everything, she was very forthcoming with information about what I could expect” (P02, Female, Aged 60–70, Spouse). Participants felt confident and comfortable asking questions, knowing hospice staff would explain everything thoroughly, as one participant recalled: “If we had any questions or any concerns, we would ask, and they [hospice nurses] would tell us the answers. And they would go in depth, in detail to make sure that we understood” (P03, Female, Aged 60–70, Adult Child). Other participants added that hospice staff adjusted their communication style to match their needs, with one participant sharing: “She [hospice nurse] understood the situation. And she was trying to choose the wording to convey that message to me” (P04, Male, Aged 60–70, Spouse).

Beyond professionalism, participants and hospice staff also built personal connections, where they were seen as friends or even part of the family. The personal connections formed during the caregiving process were deeply valued by participants, as hospice staff made the effort to get to know and spend time with families while providing individualized and respectful care with compassion and empathy. One participant described the relationship with hospice staff, saying: “You can talk to them [hospice nurses]. They have time for you because that's what they're there to help you and spend time with you. We would have beautiful conversations, you know, we get to know each other” (P18, Female, Aged 60–70, Spouse). Some also noted feeling treated as people rather than a job through small but deeply personal gestures like hospice staff remembering participant’s birthday or sitting close and leaning in when talking with the care recipient. One participant recalled a touching gesture of the hospice staff when her dad passed, stating: “The nurse tucked a little teddy under my dad’s arm before he passed. It was really nice” (P03, Female, Aged 60–70, Adult Child).

By contrast, participants commonly described their relationships with hospital nurses as impersonal and distant, often noting that these nurses seemed “too busy” to get to know the family and form personal connections. Some felt they were not treated with compassion or respect, with one participant recalling the care recipient experiencing mistreatment, stating: “Some nurse [hospital nurses] called him a lazy slug. Someone else, another nurse, said that if he couldn't turn on his own, when she did it, it was going to hurt. I was mortified” (P05, Female, Aged 50–60, Spouse). Miscommunication further worsened the relationships between participants and hospital nurses, as participants reported not receiving essential information regarding the care of care recipient.

Despite many expressing positive experiences, not all participants felted fully supported or developed a trusting relationship with hospice staff, especially when the care recipient’s condition deteriorated rapidly. One participant, whose family member receiving care passed away within 24 h of enrollment, described feeling lost and unprepared due to a lack of information and practical assistance throughout the program. She recalled calling hospice staff by phone at the moment of the care recipient’s passing, only to be instructed to call the funeral home. She recalled the moment of feeling lost and left alone, stating: "You just kind of feel that little bit lost but I don't know. There was just no one to talk to… There was no one there to discuss" (P16, Female, Aged 50–60, Adult Child). This participant also struggled with unclear terminology used by hospice staff, such as “the kit” and “sign out”, which led to uncertainty when further explanations were not provided. Another participant expressed concerns regarding medication administration, when the care recipient became unresponsive after the first injection. She felt that hospice staff had not provided sufficient explanation regarding the effects of the medication, leading to doubt and mistrust (P02, Female, Aged 60–70, Spouse).

“Earlier would have been better”

While the majority of participants expressed overall satisfaction and appreciation with the support provided by the home hospice, most reflected on how earlier awareness and referral to home hospice could have improved both the care recipient’s and their own care experiences. Participants often became aware of home hospice late in the care recipient’s illness, after struggling with repeated hospital visits or inadequate home-based care. This delay led to unnecessary suffering and resulted in missed opportunities for earlier comfort-focused home support, with one participant reflecting: “He could have been home and been very well taken care of instead of going through all the pain that he went through being in the hospital” (P05, Female, Aged 50–60, Spouse). Others emphasized that earlier awareness and access to the home hospice program would have allowed more time for end-of-life decision-making and better preparation for the program. With one participant reflecting on earlier awareness for better organization, stating: “I would like to actually have it if I knew the time was coming. A little bit more time to get it organized. I would have had it in the house a little bit sooner” (P07, Male, Aged 50–60, Adult Child).

When discussing the potential reasons behind late awareness and referral, participants frequently pointed to a knowledge gap among healthcare professionals and social workers, as well as public misunderstandings about hospice care. Some felt that healthcare professionals and social workers lacked a full understanding the service, leading to missed opportunities for earlier discussions and referrals. Others acknowledged their own misunderstandings, assuming that hospice care was only for those near death. One participant shared initial understanding of the hospice care, saying: “I just thought you go there, you know, just before you pass away, and they take care of you, and y’know, they're amazing. But I didn't really know a lot of what they offered” (P05, Female, Aged 50–60, Spouse). To address these issues, participants advocated raising awareness through training for healthcare professionals and social workers, media campaigns, and community outreach. Some suggested assigning dedicated hospice liaisons in hospitals to identify eligible patients and initiate conversations about home hospice earlier in the care process. By raising awareness and enhancing understanding of home hospice, participants believed that future patients and families could access home hospice earlier and receive better support in end-of-life care.

Discussion

This study provides insights into bereaved informal caregivers’ experiences with a home hospice program in geographically isolated, medium-sized northern city in Western Canada, highlighting both the benefits and challenges of supporting end-of-life care at home. Overall, informal caregivers valued home hospice for preserving dignity, autonomy, and comfort, while reducing caregiver burden through holistic support and compassionate communication. At the same time, some bereaved informal caregivers also encountered challenges, including logistical barriers to medication access, insufficient crisis support during rapid patient decline, and delayed hospice referrals due to systemic barriers and a lack of public awareness. These findings highlight the need to improve access to timely hospice and palliative care services, strengthen healthcare system coordination, and enhance awareness of available supports to better enable home-based end-of-life care.

A key contribution of this study lies in its contextual focus on a northern home hospice program, an area that remains underrepresented in the literature. Home was strongly preferred as the place of care due to its familiarity and sense of control [3, 27]. Caregivers in this study also described additional burdens related to limited access to healthcare and social services, reflecting challenges commonly reported in rural and northern settings [19, 32]. This study extends existing knowledge by illustrating how bereaved informal caregivers experience these structural barriers in their day-to-day caregiving and how such barriers can challenge the sustainability of home-based end-of-life care in geographically isolated contexts. These findings highlight the importance of strengthening community-based supports and addressing logistical barriers to better support informal caregivers in rural and northern communities.

The findings also contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the emotional trajectory of informal caregiving at the end of life. Bereaved informal caregivers described a shift from exhaustion and overwhelm prior to hospice involvement toward relief, confidence, and emotional reassurance once hospice support was introduced. Hospice enrollment appeared to alleviate emotional burden by providing both medical assistance and emotional support, contributing to improved caregiver well-being, consistent with prior research [52, 55]. In addition, some bereaved informal caregivers were initially unaware of the full scope of hospice services and were surprised by the extent of support available to them, a finding consistent with previous research indicating that caregivers may underestimate available supports and prioritize caregiving responsibilities over their own needs [6, 55]. These findings highlight the importance of ensuring that informal caregivers are clearly informed about available services and are encouraged to access both practical and emotional supports throughout the caregiving process.

This study also highlights the emotional significance of fulfilling end-of-life wishes. Facilitating a home death was associated with fulfillment, peace, and emotional closure, whereas being unable to do so contributed to prolonged guilt and regret. These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that fulfilling care recipient help alleviate emotional distress and enhance informal caregivers' well-being [43, 45], while unmet preferences may lead to emotional distress, including feelings of guilt and regret [58]. In this study, the inability to support a home death was often linked to insufficient external resources and limited caregiving capacity. Prior research similarly identifies access to home-based palliative care services and caregiver support as key factors influencing place of death [1, 2, 30]. These findings emphasize the importance of ensuring timely access to adequate home-based palliative care resources to support home death preferences and promote healthier bereavement outcomes for informal caregivers [23]. These findings also suggest that bereavement experiences may shape how informal caregivers reflect on and evaluate end-of-life care.

Communication between bereaved informal caregivers and healthcare professionals shaped bereaved informal caregivers’ end-of-life experiences and influenced trust. In this study, bereaved informal caregivers highly valued and appreciated the open, compassionate, and effective communication provided by the hospice team, which allowed them to remain informed, supported, and actively engaged in caregiving. In contrast, hospital-based care was frequently characterized by inadequate communication, leaving caregivers unprepared and uncertain about care decisions and end-of-life options. These findings align closely with existing research, emphasizing the benefits of effective communication in home hospice care, and the challenges commonly observed in hospital settings [20, 39, 46, 60]. Given communication’s critical role in shaping informal caregivers’ experiences, building trust, understanding patients’ wishes, and supporting informed decision-making [46, 47], these findings highlight the need to improve communication practices within hospital settings. Implementing structure communication training or standardized communication protocols that explicitly emphasize compassion, empathy, and transparency may better equip healthcare professionals to engage in meaningful end-of-life conversations, ultimately improving care experiences for informal caregivers and patients [18].

Finally, this study contributes to existing knowledge by highlighting persistent systemic barriers to timely hospice referral, including healthcare provider knowledge gaps and public misconceptions about hospice care, which often delayed access until the later stages of illness. A key barrier to timely hospice referral was limited knowledge among healthcare professionals and social workers, alongside misconceptions among informal caregivers that hospice care is only appropriate at the very end of life [34, 64]. Inadequate education and training among healthcare providers may further contribute to missed referral opportunities and the perception of hospice care as “giving up” rather than supportive care [17, 34]. In addition, stigma surrounding hospice care may deter both healthcare professionals and families from initiating timely end-of-life conversations, further limiting access to essential support [34]. Addressing these systemic barriers requires targeted funding and stronger integration of hospice care within existing healthcare systems and referral pathways to promote earlier hospice involvement and improve coordination between hospital-based and hospice services, particularly in rural and northern communities [12]. Enhancing education and training for healthcare professionals and social workers, including targeted strategies such as hospice liaisons in hospital settings, may help address knowledge gaps and promote timely referral practices [17]. These approaches are consistent with national priorities outlined in the Palliative Care Coalition of Canada's Blueprint for Action 2020–2025 [40] and the Canadian Interdisciplinary Palliative Care Competency Framework, which emphasize workforce capacity, education, and coordinated palliative care delivery [15]. Public awareness campaigns through media and community outreach should be implemented to dispel misconceptions and encourage timely access to hospice services [64], along with providing educational resources such as What to Expect When Someone Close to You Is Dying (National Initiative for Care of the Elderly [NICE] [37] and Health Canada’s national palliative care campaign to help informal caregivers better understand the dying process and available supports [29].

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of the study is the focus on bereaved informal caregivers’ reflections after their loved ones’ passing, allowing for insights across the full trajectory of hospice care, from referral and support received to the challenges they faced, as well as the post-bereavement services that shaped their caregiving and bereavement experience. Additionally, the rich and comprehensive interview data further strengthened the study, allowing for an in-depth thematic analysis that revealed both systemic issues and personal experiences. Given that research on hospice utilization in rural and northern communities is limited, this study also contributed valuable context-specific knowledge that can inform service improvements in similar healthcare settings.

Despite its strengths, the study has limitations. This study was conducted within a single home hospice program in a northern community, and findings reflect experiences within this specific context. As such, broader socioeconomic and structural factors influencing caregiving experiences across diverse settings were not the focus of this study. Although data saturation was achieved, the focused sample size and context-specific nature of the study may limit the use of the findings in larger metropolitan and urban centres. Additionally, this study involved secondary analysis of data originally collected as part of a larger program evaluation, which may have constrained the scope of inquiry. To address this limitation, research team members who are also hospice employees carefully reviewed the interpretation of findings through their clinical and experiential knowledge and through discussion, consensus on interpretation of thematic analysis was achieved.

Conclusion

Home hospice programs can reduce informal caregiver burden and enhance the end-of-life experience for both informal caregivers and the care recipient. Systemic barriers, including limited home-based service, delayed referrals, insufficient public and professional awareness continue to hinder access to timely and effective hospice care support. Addressing these challenges through healthcare system integration, healthcare provider education and training, interdisciplinary collaboration, and public education can improve the accessibility, efficiency, and impact of hospice services, ultimately ensuring that more patients and families receive the support they need for a dignified and well-supported end-of-life journey. Future research should explore the effectiveness of interventions such as hospice liaison roles in hospitals, policy changes for earlier referrals, and tailored support programs for informal caregivers to enhance home hospice experiences in rural and northern communities and beyond.
