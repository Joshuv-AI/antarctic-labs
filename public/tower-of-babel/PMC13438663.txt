Cooperative chelation for high-performance Perovskite light-emitting diodes
Authors: Rui Li, Chunru Fan, Min Lu, Fujun Zhang, Po Lu, Yingguo Yang, Mingze Liu, Weinan Dong, Xin Zhi, Li Jun Lim, Junzhe Feng, Zhennan Wu, Yu Zhang, Xue Bai, Zhi-Kuang Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Molecular additives are frequently employed to enhance the performance of perovskite light-emitting diodes (PeLEDs), either through surface defect passivation or crystal growth control. In general, such passivation is attained through the chelation of the molecular additive to a defective Pb site along a lattice plane. Unfortunately, nano-crystallites often display multiple lattice planes and defects that a single additive is inadequate in passivating. Here, we report a dual additive system, comprising 5-aminopentanoic acid and (4-fluoropheny)thiourea to enhance the performance of FAPbI3 PeLEDs. Our results and calculations reveal that the dual additives chelate cooperatively and synergistically with defective sites along the (100), (110) and (111) lattice planes, leading to significantly improved performance compared to the use of a single additive. The strong binding of the dual additives towards multiple lattice planes further slows down the growth of the nucleated crystallites, leading to small and uniform crystal grains as well as smoother thin films that exhibit higher exciton binding energy and improved luminescence quantum yields. The resulting PeLEDs attained a peak external quantum efficiency (EQE) of 26.3% at a high current density of 298 mA·cm−2 and a peak radiance of 1843 W·sr−1·m−2 at 795 nm. Such high efficiency and radiance, together with enhanced stability, could allow PeLEDs to become viable for commercial use.

Introduction

Near-infrared (NIR) light-emitting diodes (LEDs) play a critical role in the fields of optical communications, medical imaging and sensors, driving the development of a wide range of frontier technologies1–5. Three-dimensional (3D) FAPbI3 perovskite has emerged as a promising semiconductor for the development of efficient and bright NIR LEDs due to their high charge mobility and low quantum efficiency droop6–10. However, lattice plane defects in FAPbI3 that arise from thin film processing lead to non-radiative losses and lower photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQY), thus reducing the efficiency and radiance of perovskite light emitting diodes (PeLEDs)11–13.

The introduction of additives during the preparation of FAPbI3 perovskite has been an important strategy for achieving less defective films as well as an improved film morphology3,14,15. To date, many additive molecular have been reported to enhance the device performance of FAPbI3 PeLEDs through defect passivation4,16,17; film-growth control18–21; and the reduction of Auger recombination16,22–24. In recent works, the chelation interactions between specific lattice planes on FAPbI3 and single additives, such as phenethylamine (PEA) or 4-(2-aminoethyl)benzenesulfonamide (AEBS), were investigated. The PEA molecule exhibits the strongest adsorption energy (Ea) on the (100) plane, but shows weak passivation effects on the (110) plane. In contrast, the AEBS molecule, which possesses both sulfonamide and amino functional groups, demonstrates the highest Ea on the (110) plane25. These results suggest that a single additive could be inadequate for passivating defects that are present on multiple lattice planes.

Here, we present a cooperative chelation approach by using two molecular additives, 5-aminopentanoic acid (5AVA) and (4-halopheny)thiourea (4RPA) where R = F, Cl, or Br, to target and passivate multiple defective sites on different lattice planes in FAPbI3 perovskite. The strong chelation effect on multiple crystal planes further promotes isotropic and uniform growth of the FAPbI3 perovskite film, thereby enhancing the PLQY from 69% to 91%. The resulting NIR PeLEDs display a high EQE of 26.3% and a high radiance of 1843 W·sr−1·m−2.

Results

Cooperative chelation between dual additives and perovskite

The molecular structures of the additives are shown in Fig. 1a–d. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed to investigate the molecular interactions between FAPbI3 and the 4RPA & 5AVA additives. The appearance of S 2p, F 1s, Cl 2p, and Br 3d peaks in the 4RPA-5AVA modified FAPbI3 films confirms the successful introduction of 4RPA additives (Supplementary Fig. 1a–d). As shown in Fig. 1e, the XPS peaks of Pb 4 f display a notable shift towards lower binding energy when 4RPA is introduced, with the shift increasing as the electronegativity of the halogen atom increases (Br <Cl <F). This negative shift in the Pb 4 f binding energy indicates a decreased electronic charge density on Pb, suggesting strong electronic interactions between the undercoordinated Pb2+ and the C = S, C-R functional groups. Similarly, the shift in the I 3 d peaks indicates strong electronic interactions between the I⁻ and the additive molecules (Fig. 1f). The N 1 s peaks of 4RPA-5AVA films also shifted towards lower binding energies (Supplementary Fig. 1e), which demonstrates that the electronic cloud around N in the ammonium group is displaced due to stronger hydrogen-bond interactions26,27. To elucidate the origin of the strong interactions shown in XPS and FTIR, we employed density functional theory (DFT) to analyze the electrostatic potential (ESP) of different molecular additives (Supplementary Fig. 3a–c) and calculated their Ea on the (100), (110) and (111) planes of FAPbI3 perovskites (Fig. 1g–i). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3d, the negative ESPs of the 4RPA molecule appear mainly near the C = S, C-R, and C-NH2 chelation groups, and these functional groups in (4-fluoropheny)thiourea (4FPA) exhibit higher ESP than those of 4BrPA and 4ClPA, thus accounting for the stronger chelation of 4FPA towards the perovskite surface.

In Fig. 1g–i, we systematically compared the Ea of single additive (5AVA and 4FPA), as well as that of the dual additives (4FPA-5AVA) on the (100), (110), and (111) perovskite lattice planes. Calculations reveal that 5AVA exhibits a relatively high adsorption energy on the (100) plane (Ea = −4.029 eV) compared to the (110) and (111) planes (Ea = −2.846 eV and −3.347 eV, respectively). In contrast, 4FPA exhibits lower adsorption energy (Ea = −3.295 eV) on the (100) plane compared to the (110) and (111) planes (Ea = −3.639 eV and −3.851 eV, respectively). These computational results suggest that 5AVA and 4FPA may exhibit different binding trends on distinct planes. When single additive is employed, the additives are likely to coordinate selectively to certain lattice planes, while leaving others exposed and unpassivated. Interestingly, when both additives are employed (4FPA-5AVA), they function synergistically and display stronger adsorptions all three surface planes (Ea = −4.657, −4.103 and −4.573 eV on (100), (110) and (111) planes, respectively). Cooperative chelation thus leads to stronger passivation on all exposed surfaces, and leads to enhanced luminescence performance. It is also worth noting that the 4FPA-5AVA sample exhibits the strongest Ea (−4.657 eV) compared to 4BrPA-5AVA (−4.227 eV) and 4ClPA-5AVA (−4.554 eV), thus confirming the crucial role of the highly electronegative fluorine in attaining strong and stable surface passivation (Supplementary Fig. 4). To ensure the reliability of our conclusions, we conducted comparative calculations using surface models for the tetragonal phase. The results indicate that the Ea of 4FPA in the tetragonal phase is higher than 5AVA (Supplementary Fig. 5), further demonstrating its excellent passivation ability.

To elucidate the effect of cooperative chelation on exciton dynamics, we performed time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) and femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) measurements. The 4RPA-5AVA films have notably longer PL lifetimes with a significant increase in the radiative recombination component from 48% (5AVA) to 65% (4FPA-5AVA) (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Note 2, Supplementary Fig. 10), implying that the non-radiative recombination pathways are suppressed. Furthermore, the TA spectra of the 5AVA and 4RPA-5AVA (R=Br, Cl, F) samples are taken at several respective probe delays ranging from 0 ps to 5 ns (Fig. 2e, f and Supplementary Fig. 11). The TA spectra of these four samples exhibit sharp negative ground state bleaching (GSB) signals at around 788 nm. However, the bleach recovery of the 4RPA-5AVA (R=Br, Cl, F) samples is slower than that of the 5AVA sample, indicating that 4RPA (R = F, Cl, Br) can eliminate the trap state in perovskites28. Carrier dynamics are extracted from the GSB data using a triple-exponential fit (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 12). Apparently, the τ1 of the 4RPA-5AVA (R=Br, Cl, F) samples is accelerated compared to the 5AVA sample, which demonstrates that 4RPA-5AVA (R=Br, Cl, F) facilitates the state coupling related to the relaxation process. The 4RPA-5AVA (R=Br, Cl, F) samples display a slower τ2, which is consistent with the reduced defect state of the FAPbI3 films through 4RPA-5AVA (R=Br, Cl, F) passivation. The longer component of τ3 of the 4RPA-5AVA (R=Br, Cl, F) samples reveals that the radiative recombination paths are enhanced, and the excited carriers are efficiently utilized. These results are in agreement with the recombination rate constants we derived from the carrier population rate equation (Supplementary Fig. 13, Supplementary Note 3, Supplementary Table 1). The radiative recombination rate constant of the 4FPA-5AVA film (k2 = 1.38 × 10−10 ± 3.13 × 10−12 cm3s−1) is higher than that of the 5AVA film (k2 = 1.05 × 10−10 ± 5.89 × 10−12 cm3s−1), further indicating that the introduction of the dual additives enhances the radiative recombination process. The Auger recombination rate constant of the 4FPA-5AVA film (k3 = 2.05 × 10−29 ± 3.33 × 10−31 cm6s−1) is lower than that of the 5AVA film (k3 = 2.96 × 10−29 ± 5.79 × 10−31 cm6s−1), confirming that Auger recombination has been effectively suppressed. The enhancements of radiative recombination and the reduction of Auger recombination together form the key kinetic basis for achieving high-performance optoelectronic devices.

Growth kinetics of perovskite films

To study the effects of dual additives on the perovskite crystal structure and morphology, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurement were performed. Figure 3a–d show SEM images and the size distribution histograms of the perovskite thin films, and Fig. 3e–h show the corresponding AFM images. With only 5AVA, the perovskite film is rough (RMS roughness = 8.35 nm), non-uniform and comprises large (133 nm ± 3.0 nm) and irregular crystal grains. With dual additives, the 4RPA-5AVA films exhibit noticeably lower roughness (3.65 nm, 2.88 nm and 2.37 nm for Br, Cl and F, respectively). In particular, the nanocrystal grains on the 4FPA-5AVA sample are significantly more uniform and smaller (59 nm ± 1.3 nm). Additionally, the full width at half maxima (FWHM) of the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns for the 4RPA-5AVA samples are broader than that of the 5AVA sample, further confirming that the 4RPA-5AVA samples have smaller particle size (Supplementary Fig. 14)29,30. We employed in-situ PL measurements to monitor the growth kinetics of perovskite films during the thermal annealing process (0-900 s) and its subsequent cooling stage (900–1500 s) (Supplementary Fig. 15). The optimized 4RPA-5AVA films exhibit relatively low emission intensity during the thermal annealing stage, indicating a slower crystallization process. This suggests that the introduction of 4RPA additives slows down the reaction rate between FAI and PbI2, thereby enabling finer control over grain growth and avoiding size inhomogeneity that could result from rapid growth. Following the 900 s thermal annealing, recrystallization persists during the subsequent cooling stage. Notably, the PL intensity of the 5AVA sample stabilized after 1190 s, whereas the PL intensity of the 4FPA-5AVA film exhibits a pronounced upward trend at 1500 s, further confirming its slower crystallization kinetics. Grazing incidence wide angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) measurements were made to investigate how the additives impact the crystallinity and orientation of the FAPbI3 perovskite films. The GIWAXS patterns of the 4RPA-5AVA films display sharper Debye-Scherrer diffraction peaks compared to the 5AVA film (Fig. 3i–l), indicating a significant improvement in their crystallinity33,34. We analyzed the 1D GIWAXS patterns along qz direction at various probing angles (Supplementary Fig. 16), which shows that the cubic and tetragonal phases are distributed throughout 5AVA, 4BrPA-5AVA and 4ClPA-5AVA samples, and that the tetragonal phase component increases in the 4BrPA-5AVA and 4ClPA-5AVA samples. Notably, the 4FPA-5AVA sample comprises mainly of a single tetragonal phase. In-situ GIWAXS measurements further reveal that the diffraction peaks of the 5AVA sample are dominated by cubic phase characteristics during crystal growth, whereas the 4RPA-5AVA samples exhibit the diffraction peaks dominated by tetragonal phase (Supplementary Fig. 17). The tetragonal phase with its slight octahedral tilting effectively relieves internal lattice stresses and suppresses the formation of deep-level defects, thereby exhibiting significant advantages in structural stability35,36. The increase of the tetragonal phase ratio favors higher exciton binding energies (Eb)37,38, which is consistent with our temperature-dependent PL spectra studies below. These results demonstrate that the dual additives offer favorable conditions for the controlled growth of FAPbI3 perovskite films and significantly improve their crystallinity, morphology, optical and electronic properties.

We further measured the PL maps of the 5AVA and 4RPA-5AVA samples as a function of temperature (Fig. 4e, f and Supplementary Fig. 20). It can be seen that the PL intensity decreases significantly with increasing temperature, which is attributed to thermal quenching related to non-radiative traps42. We then compared the Eb and electron-phonon interaction of the 5AVA and 4RPA-5AVA films by fitting the temperature-dependent integral intensity and FWHM using the Arrhenius equation and the boson model (Fig. 4g, h and Supplementary Fig. 21, Supplementary Note 4). From 5AVA to 4RPA-5AVA samples, the Eb increases from 52.2 ± 3.2 meV to 57.7 ± 1.4 meV, 59.3 ± 1.9 meV and 62.8 ± 0.9 meV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 22). The increase of Eb suppresses the formation of free carriers, promotes the radiative recombination of excitons, and reduces the probability of exciton quenching. In addition, the 4FPA-5AVA film exhibits the lowest longitudinal optical phonon energy, indicating an increased population of photogenerated electron-hole pairs in the emissive state and suppressed quenching through exciton-phonon scattering.

Based on the above results, we postulate that the large particle sizes in perovskite films using single 5AVA additive result in long LD and low Eb, leading to the dissociation of excitons and reduction in radiative recombination efficiency in FAPbI3. In comparison, the reduced grain sizes in 5AVA-RPA dual additives offer shorter LD and higher Eb, thus enhancing radiative recombination and improving PeLED performance (Fig. 4i, j).

Performances of PeLEDs with cooperative chelation

We employed the above cooperative chelation strategy in the preparation of NIR-emitting PeLEDs. Our devices comprise a structure of Ag /polyethylenimine ethoxylated (PEIE)-modified zinc oxide (ZnO)/perovskite/TFB/molybdenum oxide (MoO3)/Au (Supplementary Fig. 23). The flat-band energy level diagrams of the layers are illustrated in Fig. 5a. The energy levels of perovskites are obtained from UV-visible spectra and ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) (Supplementary Fig. 24 and Supplementary Fig. 25). The introduction of 4RPA additives cause the energy levels of perovskite to shift upwards, thus reducing the hole-injection barrier and offering a more balanced carrier transport. The PeLEDs emit at 795 nm with a narrow FWHM of 42 nm, and maintain a stable spectral shape between driving voltages of 0 and 6 V (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 26). Figure 5c-e and Supplementary Fig. 27 show the device characteristics of the PeLEDs. The 5AVA device attained a peak EQE of 17.8% at a current density of 57 mA cm-2 and a radiance 50 W·sr−1·m-2. Compared with the 5AVA device, the performances of 4FPA-5AVA devices are significantly improved, with the champion device attaining a peak EQE of 26.3% at a current density of 298 mA cm-2 and a radiance of 388 W·sr-1·m-2. We note that this EQE represents essentially unity internal quantum efficiency for a planar device. A peak radiance of 1843 W·sr-1·m-2 was attained at a driving voltage of 6.3 V. Figure 5f and Supplementary Fig. 28 show the histograms of peak EQE in 40 devices, with an average EQE of 24.3% of 4FPA-5AVA devices. The device stability was tested at a constant current density of 100 mA·cm-2, and shows that the 4FPA-5AVA device has a longer T50 lifetime of 108.6 hours (Supplementary Fig. 29, Supplementary Fig. 30). Discussion

In summary, we demonstrate that a cooperative chelation strategy using dual additives is highly effective in passivating multiple defective sites along multiple lattice planes in perovskite thin films. The strong chelation effects also enable slow and isotropic growth of perovskite crystals to give small and uniform nanocrystals. The smaller crystals possess stronger exciton binding energy and higher radiative recombination rates, leading to PeLEDs with high efficiency and high radiance that would be crucial for commercial optoelectronic applications.

Methods

Materials

N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF), chlorobenzene (CB), 5-aminovaleric (5AVA), (4-Fluoropheny)thiourea (4FPA), (4-Bromopheny)thiourea (4BrPA), polyethylenimine ethoxylated (PEIE) and (4-Chloropheny)thiourea (4ClPA) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Lead (II) iodide (PbI2, >99.99%), Formamidinium iodide (FAI, >99.99%), poly(9,9-dioctyl-fluorene-co-N-(4-butylphenyl)diphenylamine) (TFB, 99.5%) and molybdenum oxide (MoO3, 99.99%) were purchased from Xi’an Yuri Solar Co., Ltd.

Perovskite precursor solution

The 4RPA-5AVA (R = F, Cl, Br) perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving FAI, PbI2, 5AVA, and 4RPA with a molar ratio of 2.40:1.30:0.36:0.09 at in 1 mL DMF. For the 5AVA sample, FAI, PbI2 and 5AVA, were dissolved with a molar ratio of 2.40:1.30:0.70 in 1 mL DMF. The prepared solution was stirred in a N2 glovebox over 2 h before being used as precursor.

Synthesis of ZnO nanocrystals

Firstly, NaOH (0.2 g) was fully dissolved in the ethyl alcohol (10 mL) to obtain the NaOH solution. Then zinc acetate (0.4403 g) and ethyl alcohol (30 mL) were added into a 100 mL three-neck flask, and the air in the flask was discharged with the aid of flowing N2 (10 min). The solution was heated to boiling until the zinc acetate powder was completely dissolved (30 min), then the solution was cooled to room temperature naturally. At this time, the solution in the flask was turbid, and the solution became clear after injection of NaOH solution (10 mL). The reaction lasted for 4 h under the flowing N2. Finally, the ZnO NC solution was purified by centrifugation. The reactants were put into eight 50 mL centrifuge tubes on average, the tubes were filled with n-hexane, and centrifuged at 2795 g for 10 min. The precipitate was dissolved in the ethyl alcohol of 3 mL for each centrifuge tube, and n-hexane was filled again for the second centrifugation (2795 g for 10 min). Finally, the precipitate was dissolved in 3 mL ethyl alcohol and filtered with a 0.44 μm filter head for later use43,44.

Device fabrication

The substrate was washed with soap, deionized water, ethanol, chloroform, acetone, and isopropanol, followed by treatment in UV ozone cleaning machine for 10 min, a 200 nm Ag film was deposited onto the substrate via thermal evaporation. ZnO NC solution was spin-coated on the silicon substrate at 1000 rpm for 40 s and annealed in air at 200 °C for 10 min. The substrate was transferred to a N2 glove-box, and PEIE 2-methoxyethanol solution (0.2% mass fraction) was spin-coated at 5000 rpm for 50 s and annealed at 125 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, perovskite film was deposited onto the PEIE-treated ZnO film by spin coating and annealed at 105 °C for 15 min in a N2 glovebox. TFB layer was deposited onto the perovskite film by spin coating (3000 rpm for 45 s) from solution (15 mg mL-1 in m-xylene). Finally, 6 nm of MoO3 and 12 nm of Au layers were subsequently thermal evaporated as electrodes through a shadow mask at a pressure of 7 × 10-4 Pa. The evaporation rate for both layers was 0.1 Å s-1. The PeLEDs device area was defined by the overlap area of the Ag film and Au electrode, which was 6 mm2.

LED characterization

The current density-voltage characteristics were measured using a Keithley 2612B source-measure unit. The voltage was swept from 0 V to 8 V. The electroluminescence (EL) spectra were recorded using an Ocean Optics spectrometer. Simultaneously, the photon flux was measured using Newport 1936-R power meter and a 1 cm2 Silicon photodiode (918D-SL-0D3R) with NIST traceable calibration at a distance of 16 mm. External quantum efficiency (EQE) was calculated by taking a Lambertian emission profile. The device lifetime was measured using the same setup, but under a constant current condition, with the constant current set at 100 mA·cm-2.

Material characterizations

Absorption spectra of the samples were measured using a Shimadzu UV-1900 spectrophotometer. Room temperature photoluminescence (PL) and temperature-dependent PL spectra were measured on an Edinburgh FLS1000 spectrofluorometer. The temperature was initially decreased to 140 K at high vacuum by liquid helium and gradually increased to 300 K. The absolute photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQY) of the samples were obtained on a fluorescence spectrometer (FLS920P, Edinburgh Instruments) equipped with an integrating sphere, a 450 W Xenon lamp and a PMT-1700 detector. Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) lifetime measurements were carried out by using a time-correlated single-photon counting (TCSPC) system of the FLS920P Edinburgh spectrometer and a laser diode with a peak wavelength of 480 nm was used as the excitation source. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were acquired by using a Bruker D8 Advance X diffractometer (Cu Kα, λ = 1.5406 Å). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted on an ESCALAB250 spectrometer. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was collected by an IFS-66V/S FTIR spectrophotometer. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) spectra were collected using a PREVAC/system. Femtosecond transient absorption (Fs-TA) measurements were performed on a commercial Ti: sapphire laser system (Coherent) and Helios pump-probe system (Ultrafast System) were used for TA spectroscopy measurements. Higher-resolution scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were collected on a Regulus 8100 instrument. The roughness of film was characterized by atomic force microscopy (AFM; 5500, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA).
