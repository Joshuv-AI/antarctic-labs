Overlap‐Weight Estimators With Machine‐Learned Plug‐Ins and Overlap‐Weight Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimator
Authors: Myoung‐jae Lee, Sanghyeok Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn estimating the average treatment effect (ATE), the plug‐in estimator with the efficient influence function and the targeted maximum likelihood estimator (TMLE) are semiparametrically efficient. However, the estimators suffer from the fact that too small/large a propensity score (PS) in the denominators can make the estimators unstable. This is usually overcome by trimming or truncating the data, that is, discarding observations with the PS too small/large or replacing the PS with some lower or upper threshold, which amounts to using a non‐smooth weight to move the target parameter away from the ATE. This paper proposes a smooth weight, quadratic in PS instead, which leads to targeting the “overlap‐weight (OW) treatment effect” that has several advantages over the ATE. We compare various estimators through simulation and empirical studies, including a trimmed efficient influence‐function estimator, TMLE, and their OW versions. We find that the OW versions perform better than the trimmed ones in terms of the absolute bias and root mean squared error, when the ATE is a constant so that all estimators share the same estimand. We also find that “cross‐fit” analogous to cross‐validation improves the asymptotic variance estimators.

Introduction

Finding the effect of a binary treatment D=0,1 on an outcome Y is an important issue in science, and the basics of treatment effect analysis can be found in Imbens and Wooldridge (2009), Lee (2005), Lee (2016), Pearl (2009), Rosenbaum (2002), and Imbens and Rubin (2015), among others. In observational data, the treatment group D=1 tends to differ from the control group D=0 in observed covariates X and unobserved covariates ζ, which can confound the effect of D on Y. Since ζ cannot be controlled to avoid confounding, usually the “no unobserved confounder assumption” is made: (1.1) (Y0,Y1)⨿D|X, where (Y0,Y1) are the potential outcomes, and “⨿” stands for statistical independence.

Define the X‐conditional mean of D and Y given the two groups D=0,1: (1.2) PSπX≡E(D|X)andρXd≡E(Y|X,D=d)ford=0,1. Among various effects, we focus on the average treatment effect (ATE) and its “overlap‐weight (OW) version”: (1.3) τ=E(Y1−Y0)=E{E(Y1−Y0|X)}=E{E(Y1−Y0|πX)},τow=E{ωow(πX)·E(Y1−Y0|πX)},ωow(πX)≡πX(1−πX)E{πX(1−πX)}.

The name “OW” appears, as ωow(πX) reaches its maximum at πX=0.5 (units with πX≃0.5 overlap well with the opposite group) and its minimum at πX=0,1 (units with πX≃0,1 overlap poorly). OW accords several advantages as has been reviewed by Choi and Lee (2023), and the main one is solving the support‐overlap problem by weighting up those with πX≃0.5, and down those with πX≃0,1 in a built‐in fashion.

Consider a sample version of (1.4) EρX1−ρX0+DπX(Y−ρX1)−1−D1−πX(Y−ρX0)=EρX1−ρX0+DπX−1−D1−πX(Y−ρX,D),ρX,D≡ρX0+(ρX1−ρX0)D which is semiparametrically efficient in estimating the ATE τ, in view of the semiparametric efficiency bound for τ (Hahn 1998): E{E(Y1−Y0|X)−τ}2+Var(Y1|X)πX+Var(Y0|X)1−πX(withVarfor variance). The efficient influence function η for τ is the function in (1.4) minus τ: (1.5) η≡ρX1−ρX0−τ+DπX(Y−ρX1)−1−D1−πX(Y−ρX0){withE(η)=0}.

An efficient estimator for τ can be constructed by plugging estimators into the unknown nuisance functions (πX,ρX0,ρX1), which is “double‐debiasing (DDB),” as will be examined in detail below. However, the plug‐in estimator has the “inverse probability weighting (IPW) problem” that too small or large πX’s can make the estimator unstable. The IPW problem is commonly dealt with by trimming or truncating/“weight‐capping” (Cole and Hernán 2008; Liu et al. 2024, among others), where trimming means dropping the observations with extreme πX’s and truncating means replacing extreme πX’s with some low or high threshold values such as 0.05 or 0.95. For example, Crump et al. (2009) suggested trimming as much as 10% of the observations from each tail when πX follows a beta distribution.

Define 1[A]≡1 if A holds and 0 otherwise. Trimming amounts to using a uniform weight: For a chosen trimming constant c, (1.6) ωc(πX)≡1[c<πX<1−c]E(1[c<πX<1−c])with0<c<0.5. Instead of ωc(πX) or its truncating version, we may apply a tent‐shaped weight (Li and Greene 2013) that is one‐degree smoother than ωc(πX). In this paper, we propose using ωow(πX) in (1.3) that is further one‐degree smoother than the tent‐shaped weight to estimate τow in (1.3). One may object to targeting τow because “it moves the goalposts” (Crump et al. 2006), but moving the goalposts is done also in trimming/truncating. It is better to move the goalposts smoothly with ωow(πX) than non‐smoothly with ωc(πX), if one is to assess small‐sample impacts of errors in weighting.

We just motivated OW as a smooth way of trimming/truncating, but OW accords more advantages of its own as was mentioned above. For example, applying OW ωow(πX) to a PS‐matching estimator for E(Y1−Y0|πX) in τow (see, e.g., Choi and Lee 2023, section 3 “Matching and OW”) addresses the support‐overlap problem in a built‐in way, because those overlapping well (πX≃0.5) are given high weights in τow, and those overlapping poorly (πX≃0,1) low weights; viewed differently, those who have a near zero probability of receiving one of the treatments are given near zero weight in τow, whereas they are fully counted in τ. Also, “regression adjustment” becomes robust to misspecified outcome regressions, if OW is used with the correctly specified πX (Vansteelandt and Daniel 2014); hence it becomes singly, but not doubly, robust. Choi and Lee (2023) further showed that τow can be estimated simply by linear regression of Y on D−πX as long as πX is correctly specified.

OW and τow may look foreign, but they naturally appear in ordinary least squares (OLS) estimator for Y=βx′X+βdD+error when X is “partialled out” (e.g., Angrist and Pischke 2009) as follows. With the linear projection L(Y|X)≡E(YX′){E(XX′)}−1X of Y on X, take L(·|X) on the Y model and subtract the result from the Y model to obtain Y−L(Y|X)=βd{D−L(D|X)}+error. Then OLS of Y−L(Y|X) on D−L(D|X) is consistent for τow under L(D|X)=E(D|X) as the Appendix shows.

There are many studies with τow as the target effect: for example, Lee (2018), Lee (2021), Li and Li (2019), Li et al. (2018), Liu et al. (2024), Mao et al. (2019), Matsouaka and Zhou (2024), Zhou et al. (2020), and Kim and Lee (2025). OW is widely used in the recent epidemiological/medical literature as well: Aminian et al. (2021), Anderson et al. (2023), Chen and Park (2025), Cheng et al. (2022), Hu et al. (2024), Thomas et al. (2020), Wan et al. (2024), and Xu et al. (2025).

Other than the plug‐in estimator, another efficient estimator for τ is the “targeted maximum likelihood estimator (TMLE)” in van der Laan and Rose (2011). TMLE estimates (πX,ρX0,ρX1) with machine learning (ML) methods, and takes an extra “targeting” step to make an adjustment with the terms involving πX in (1.4). Similar to the plug‐in estimator, TMLE uses approaches such as trimming to handle πX’s close to 0 or 1; otherwise TMLE may perform poorly as shown in tables 2 and 5 of Petersen et al. (2012), which compares non‐truncating and truncating estimators via both simulation and empirical studies. Hence there is a scope to apply OW to TMLE as well.

The asymptotic standard deviation (SD) of TMLE can be estimated using the influence function or resampling‐based methods. For small samples, however, depending on the data configuration, the asymptotic variance estimator of TMLE could be inaccurate, which was the case in the empirical study of M. J. Lee and S. H. Lee (2022) with too large SDs despite its semiparametric efficiency. To alleviate this problem, we apply “cross‐fitting” to TMLE, as used in DDB (Chernozhukov et al. 2018;2022) to overcome the difficulty of accounting for the impact of the first‐stage ML estimation error on the asymptotic variance of the effect estimator.

In short, our goal is to compare the plug‐in DDB estimator and TMLE in estimating τ and τow through simulation and empirical studies, where the nuisance functions (πX,ρX0,ρX1) are estimated with ML methods. Although τ≠τow in general, τ=τow when E(Y1−Y0|πX) is a constant. Hence, when τ=τow, we can compare four estimators: the plug‐in estimator and TMLE for τ, and the plug‐in estimator and TMLE with OW for τow. DDB estimator and TMLE are two representative data‐adaptive approaches in the recent treatment effect literature (Diaz 2020).

Throughout this paper, we assume an independent and identically distributed sample of size N. We also assume 0<P(D|X)<1 for all X; although not needed for OW per se, it is necessary for estimators based on matching, IPW or regression adjustment, to which OW is applied. For ML, we use the Super Learner (van der Laan et al. 2007) weight‐averaging multiple ML predictors for both the outcome regression and propensity score (PS) models. Our Super Learner uses neural network, random forest, and LASSO, which have the convergence rate faster than N1/4, as this rate is required for DDB.

Although TMLE is supposed to work for continuous Y (as well as for binary Y) through the transformation (Y−miniYi)/(maxiYi−minYi) restricting the range of Y to [0,1], there were often large biases in TMLE when Y is continuous. Because of this, and also because Y is binary in our empirical analysis, we focus on binary Y in this paper. Such biases seems to occur, as the transformation relies on extreme values of Y.

OW‐effect is a good causal parameter to target for several reasons: (i) avoiding the user intervention to screen out poorly matched units in matching, (ii) overcoming the too small denominator problem in IPW, (iii) making regression adjustment robust to outcome model misspecifications, and (iv) the attractive weighting scheme of OW for heterogeneous effects: weighting down those with πX≃0,1 who have a near zero probability of receiving one of the treatments, and weighting up those with πX≃0.5 who have a near equal probability of receiving each treatment.

In the remainder of this paper, Section 2 introduces plug‐in estimators for τ and τow. Section 3 reviews TMLE for τ, which is then modified for τow. Sections 4 and 5 provide simulation and empirical studies. Finally, Section 6 concludes this paper.

Efficient Influence Function and OW Version

Consider the two terms in (1.4) involving ρX1 or D/πX: (2.1) EρX1+DπX(Y−ρX1)=EρX11−DπX+DπXY. If this is equal to E(Y1), then we can estimate E(Y1) using a sample version of (2.1) with the functional forms of ρX1 and πX specified. Although the specified functional forms can be false, as long as either ρX1 or πX, not necessarily both, is correctly specified, (2.1) indeed equals E(Y1) as follows.

First, suppose ρX1 is correctly specified, but πX is not (i.e., E(D|X)≠πX). In this case, (2.1) still equals E(Y1), which can be seen easily using the left‐hand side of (2.1) because E(ρX1)=E(Y1) and, recalling (1.1), EDπX(Y−ρX1)=EDπX(Y1−ρX1)=EE(D|X)πX{E(Y1|X)−ρX1}=0. Doing analogously, when πX is correctly specified but ρX1 is not (i.e., E(Y1|X)≠ρX1), (2.1) still equals E(Y1), which can be seen easily using the right‐hand side of (2.1) this time. This kind of property also holds for the two terms in (1.4) involving ρX0 or (1−D)/(1−πX). Estimators based on (1.4) are “doubly robust (DR)” (Bang and Robins 2005; M. J. Lee and S. H. Lee 2019; Robins et al. 1994;2007; Rotnitzky et al. 2012; Scharfstein et al. 1999; Vermeulen and Vansteelandt 2015, among many others).

Being DR is a global property, because a DR estimator is consistent if either nuisance function is correctly specified, no matter how grossly the other specification is wrong. However, this is unlikely in reality; rather, what is likely is that both specifications are imperfect approximations of the truth. A local robustness suitable for this situation is “Neyman orthogonality” (Chernozhukov et al. 2018;2022). With η in (1.5), considering the “estimating equation” E(η)=0 for τ, if the directional derivative of E(η) with respect to (wrt) the nuisance functions is zero, then η is “Neyman‐orthogonal”: A little change in the nuisance functions does not disturb E(η). (Chernozhukov et al. 2018, section 5.1) explains the Neyman orthogonality of η for τ, so that a sample version of E(η) is DDB.

Recalling ρX,D in (1.4), ωc(πX) in (1.6), and ωow(πX) in (1.3), define (2.2) τcη≡Eωc(πX)·ρX1−ρX0+DπX−1−D1−πX(Y−ρX,D),τowη≡Eωow(πX)·ρX1−ρX0+DπX−1−D1−πX(Y−ρX,D). Whereas ωc(πX) in τcη drops too small/large πX’s, πX(1−πX) of ωow(πX) in τowη removes the troublesome denominators πX and 1−πX. Note τow=τowη due to E[{DπX−1−(1−D)(1−πX)−1}(Y−ρX,D)|X]=0, but their sample versions differ; also note τ=τow=τowη when E(Y1−Y0|X) is a constant.

Overcoming the IPW problem with ωow(πX) in τowη does entail a cost: If πX (i.e., ωow(πX)) is misspecified, then the sample version of τowη is non‐DDB although it is still robust to misspecifications of ρX0 and ρX1, which is shown in the Appendix. This seems natural because the key component of DDB is using variables centered at the X ‐conditional means (i.e., “residuals”), which cannot be done for ωow(πX) because the weight has to be positive always. The Appendix also shows that the sample version of τowη is close to DDB if πX≃0.5 (i.e., D close to being randomized). The Appendix further shows that the sample version of τowη is not DR, unless E(Y1−Y0|X) is a constant.

Denote ML‐based nuisance function estimators as (π^X,ρ^X0,ρ^X1); let ρ^X,D≡ρ^X0+(ρ^X1−ρ^X0)D. Define ωc(π^Xi)≡ωc,i/(N−1∑iωc,i) with ωc,i≡1[c<π^Xi<1−c], and ωow(π^Xi)≡ωow,i/(N−1∑iωow,i), ωow,i≡π^Xi(1−π^Xi). Then, τcη and τowη have the following estimators: with c=0.01 or 0.1, (2.3) τ^cη≡1N∑iωc(π^Xi)ρ^Xi1−ρ^Xi0+Diπ^Xi−1−Di1−π^Xi(Yi−ρ^Xi,Di), (2.4) τ^owη≡1N∑iωow(π^Xi)ρ^Xi1−ρ^Xi0+Diπ^Xi−1−Di1−π^Xi(Yi−ρ^Xi,Di). Based on the squared influence functions, τ^cη and τ^owη have the following asymptotic variance estimators: 1N∑iωc(π^Xi)ρ^Xi1−ρ^Xi0+Diπ^Xi−1−Di1−π^Xi(Yi−ρ^Xi,Di)−τ^cη2;1N∑iωow(π^Xi)ρ^Xi1−ρ^Xi0+Diπ^Xi−1−Di1−π^Xi(Yi−ρ^Xi,Di)−τ^owη2.

The impact of the estimation error of the first‐stage ML on the final effect estimator is difficult to assess. In DDB estimators, this difficulty is overcome by “cross‐fitting.” In the remainder of this section, we explain cross‐fitting, while omitting the weights ωc(π^X) and ωow(π^X) to simplify the exposition.

Split the sample into subsamples of each size n, say four subsamples S1,…,S4, assuming N=4n. A number other than 4 may be used, but Remark 3.1 (Chernozhukov et al. 2018, p. C24) recommends 4 or 5. Denote the ML‐based estimators at X=x using only S2,S3,S4 as (π∼x,1,ρ∼x,10,ρ∼x,11) while defining ρ∼x,D,1≡ρ∼x,10+(ρ∼x,11−ρ∼x,10)D, and evaluate them at x=Xi for i∈S1. In (π∼x,1,ρ∼x,10,ρ∼x,11), the tilde (“”) denotes cross‐fitting, and the subscript 1 denotes that S1 is used for the evaluation points, but not for the nuisance function estimation. This yields the “(S2,S3,S4)‐based estimator for S1 ”: (2.5) τ∼1η≡1n∑i∈S1ρ∼Xi,11−ρ∼Xi,10+Diπ∼Xi,1−1−Di1−π∼Xi,1(Yi−ρ∼Xi,Di,1). Repeat this four times to obtain (τ∼1η,τ∼2η,τ∼3η,τ∼4η); for example, obtain τ∼2η by estimating the nuisance functions using (S1,S3,S4) and evaluating them at Xi with i∈S2.

The final cross‐fitted estimator for τ is the average of τ∼jη, j=1,2,3,4: as N=4n, (2.6) τ∼η≡14∑j=14τ∼jη=1N∑j=14∑i∈Sjρ∼Xi,j1−ρ∼Xi,j0+Diπ∼Xi,j−1−Di1−π∼Xi,j×(Yi−ρ∼Xi,Di,j)Diπ∼Xi,j−1−Di1−π∼Xi,j. N(τ∼η−τ) is asymptotically normal with the asymptotic variance estimated by (2.7) 1N∑j=14∑i∈Sjρ∼Xi,j1−ρ∼Xi,j0+Diπ∼Xi,j−1−Di1−π∼Xi,j×(Yi−ρ∼Xi,Di,j)−τ∼ηDiπ∼Xi,j−1−Di1−π∼Xi,j2. Essentially, a sample splitting may lower the estimator efficiency, but this problem is overcome by cross‐fitting where all subsamples are used for both model fitting and treatment effect estimation in separate rounds.

If the estimand is defined implicitly with a moment condition, then there arise two types of DDB estimators. In our case, however, the estimand itself is a moment, so the two types are the same. To get an idea, consider E{X(Y−X′β)}=0 where the estimand β is defined implicitly. Solving the moment condition yields β={E(XX′)}−1E(XY). Then two types of estimators arise: b^(1)=14∑j=14∑i∈SjXiXi′−1∑i∈SjXiYi(average of four cross-fitted estimators);b^(2)=1N∑j=14∑i∈SjXiXi′−11N∑j=14∑i∈SjXiYi=14∑j=14∑i∈SjXiXi′−114∑j=14∑i∈SjXiYi from the combined moment condition ∑j=14∑i∈SjXi(Yi−Xi′b^(2))=0. This kind of difference does not arise in (2.6), because the estimand itself is a moment.

Targeted Maximum Likelihood and OW Version

Often researchers imagine the data generating process with a regression model, and then point out which parameter there is the parameter of interest; for example, linear models in (D,X), with the slope of D as the parameter of interest. This approach is ill‐advised, however, because what should be done first is defining the parameter of interest and then showing how it can be identified and estimated. For example, set τ≡E(Y1−Y0) as the parameter of interest that can be identified with E(ρX1−ρX0) under (1.1), which in turn can be estimated by plugging in estimators for (ρX0,ρX1).

When an ML‐based estimator ρ^Xd is used for ρXd, d=0,1, typically ρ^Xd is chosen to minimize E{(ρ^Xd−ρXd)2} by optimally trading off the bias and variance. However, this plug‐in tends to result in too high a bias for τ, because ρ^Xd is chosen for ρXd, not for τ. Hence, TMLE proposes taking an extra “targeting/tilting” step from the plug‐in. In the following, we explain TMLE for binary Y, drawing on van der Laan and Rose (2011), differently from which, however, we make the use of ωc(πX) in (1.6) explicit.

The first step of TMLE is constructing the so‐called “smart covariate”: with the logistic regression π^X, Δ^(D,X)≡Dπ^X−1−D1−π^XforΔ(D,X)≡DπX−1−D1−πX; Δ(D,X) appeared in (1.4), which is no coincidence. This is to set up a “logistic parametric submodel indexed by ε” as below, where ωc(π^X)Δ^(D,X) is the sole regressor and ln{ρ^X,D/(1−ρ^X,D)} is a known intercept with ρ^X,D=ρ^X0+(ρ^X1−ρ^X0)D: (3.1) E^(Y|X,D,ε)≡exp[ln{ρ^X,D/(1−ρ^X,D)}+ε·ωc(π^X)Δ^(D,X)]1+exp[ln{ρ^X,D/(1−ρ^X,D)}+ε·ωc(π^X)Δ^(D,X)]. “0<ρ^X,D<1” is necessary for ln{ρ^X,D/(1−ρ^X,D)}. Here, ωc(π^X) is used to avoid the IPW problem due to extreme values of π^X. If ε=0, then (3.1) becomes: E^(Y|X,D,0)=(1−ρ^X,D)·{ρ^X,D/(1−ρ^X,D)}(1−ρ^X,D)·{1+ρ^X,D/(1−ρ^X,D)}=ρ^X,D: The parametric submodel passes through the initially estimated model ρ^X,D when ε=0.

E^(Y|X,D,ε) in (3.1) may look complicated, but it is not much so. Suppose ρX,D=exp(βx′X+βdD)/{1+exp(βx′X+βdD)}—the usual logistic regression model. Then, ρX,D1−ρX,D=exp(βx′X+βdD)⟹E^(Y|X,D,ε)=exp{β^x′X+β^dD+ε·ωc(π^X)Δ^(D,X)}1+exp{β^x′X+β^dD+ε·ωc(π^X)Δ^(D,X)}, which is the usual estimated logistic regression model with ε·ωc(π^X)Δ^(D,X) extra.

The second step of TMLE is estimating the parametric submodel with the logistic MLE, where ε is the single parameter to estimate; let ε^ be the resulting estimator. Specifically on getting ε^, with the logistic MLE (β^x,β^d) of Y on (X,D) already done to obtain ρ^Xd=exp(β^x′X+β^dd)/{1+exp(β^x′X+β^dd)}, d=0,1, and ρ^X,D=ρ^X0+(ρ^X1−ρ^X0)D, the logistic MLE of Y on ωc(π^X)Δ^(D,X) is done using (3.1), where the intercept is known (so not estimated) and only ε is estimated as the slope of the single regressor ωc(π^X)Δ^(D,X). That is, ε^ satisfies the following logistic MLE first‐order condition “the sum of the regressor times the residual is 0”: (3.2) ∑iωc(π^Xi)Δ^(D,X)·{Yi−E^(Y|Xi,Di,ε^)}=0. E^(Y|X,D,ε^) can be written also as ρˇX,D≡ρˇX0+(ρˇX1−ρˇX0)D, where ρˇXd is the “ε^‐adjusted versions” of ρ^Xd, d=0,1: ρˇXd≡exp[ln{ρ^Xd/(1−ρ^Xd)}+ε^·ωc(π^X){dπ^X−1−(1−d)(1−π^X)−1}]1+exp[ln{ρ^Xd/(1−ρ^Xd)}+ε^·ωc(π^X){dπ^X−1−(1−d)(1−π^X)−1}]. This is an “updating”: logit (ρˇXd)= logit (ρ^Xd)+ε^ωc(π^X){dπ^X−1−(1−d)(1−π^X)−1}.

The third step of TMLE is constructing an estimator for τcη using ρˇX0 and ρˇX1: (3.3) τ^ctmle≡1N∑iωc(π^Xi)(ρˇXi1−ρˇXi0),ωc(π^Xi)≡1[c<π^Xi<1−c]N−1∑i1[c<π^Xi<1−c]with1N∑i[ωc(π^Xi){ρˇXi1−ρˇXi0+Δ^(Di,Xi)(Yi−ρˇXi,Di)}−τ^ctmle]2 that is an asymptotic variance estimator. N−1∑iωc(π^Xi){ρˇXi1−ρˇXi0+Δ^(Di,Xi)(Yi−ρˇXi,Di)} is a sample version of τcη in (2.2), but its last part Δ^(Di,Xi)(Yi−ρˇXi,Di) drops out due to (3.2) to render τ^ctmle.

The R package for TMLE sets c=5/{N1/2ln(N)} (=0.042 and 0.026 for N=400 and 800, respectively), but we use c=0.01 or0.1 to make comparison with the other estimators easier. TMLE τ^owtmle for τow can be analogously constructed with ωow(π^X). Recall that the initial estimator ρ^X,D should be bounded away from 0 and 1, for which we use the TMLE default procedure of replacing ρ^X,D with 0.0005 or 0.9995 in the R‐package if ρ^X,D goes out of the bounds (Gruber and van der Laan 2012).

In M. J. Lee and S. H. Lee (2022), the TMLE's asymptotic variance estimator tends to be inaccurate: sometimes too “optimistic” in the simulation study by under‐estimating the actual variance, and too “pessimistic” in the empirical analysis with the asymptotic variance greater than other semiparametric estimators' asymptotic variance despite the claimed semiparametric efficiency of TMLE.

As a way to overcome this problem, we apply the cross‐fitting idea to DDB as follows; cross‐fitting has been also applied to ML in Zivichi and Breskin (2020) and Naimi et al. (2023), among others. Heuristically, fitting the model and estimating its variance on the same data overfits to the sample, which is avoided by cross‐fitting.

Denote the ML estimators at X=x using only S2,S3,S4 as (π∼x,1,ρ∼x,10,ρ∼x,11), and denote the corresponding weight and smart covariate as ωc(π∼x,1) and Δ∼1(D,x)=Dπ∼x−1−(1−D)(1−π∼x)−1. Further, let the logistic MLE for ε be ε∼1. Denoting the ε∼1‐adjusted version of ρ∼X,1d as ρ˘X,1d and defining ρ˘X,D,1≡ρ˘X,10+(ρ˘X,11−ρ˘X,10)D, the “(S2,S3,S4)‐based estimator for S1” is, analogously to (2.5), τ∼c,1tmle≡1n∑i∈S1wc(π∼Xi,1)(ρ˘Xi,11−ρ˘Xi,10).

As in (2.5), (2.6) for τcη with N=4n, the final cross‐fitted TMLE for τcη is τ∼ctmle=14∑j=14τ∼c,jtmle=1N∑j=14∑i∈Sjwc(π∼Xi,j)(ρ˘Xi,j1−ρ˘Xi,j0). The data include 688 patients admitted to a hospital with stable angina, the most common manifestation of IHD, between July 1, 2002, and 30 June, 2005. For non‐OW estimates, as was the case for IHD, the effects and SDs vary substantially, depending on the trimming constant c; for example, τ∼0.01tmle has the effect 0.030 with SD 0.077, but τ∼0.1tmle has the effect 0.006 with SD 0.031. Interpretation of the results in Table 2 is not necessarily straightforward because the estimands differ: The OW estimators target τow whereas the c‐trimmed estimators target τc: Differences in estimates arise not only from the estimation errors but also from the estimand differences. Nevertheless, recall that cross‐fitted OW estimators perform best in RMSE and asymptotic SD estimation of our simulation study, especially when effect heterogeneity and poor PS overlap are present. Hence, given that πX‐conditional effects are heterogeneous and PS overlap is poor in our empirical analysis as Figure 7 shows, we conclude that the most reliable estimators are τ∼owη and τ∼owtmle.

Overall, the findings in the empirical study are largely coherent with those in the simulation study. OW estimators deliver stable performances, part of which is that the cross‐fitted OW estimator τ∼owη and cross‐fitted OW TMLE τ∼owtmle are similar, whereas non‐OW estimators vary much, depending on the trimming constant. Also, cross‐fit seems to make the asymptotic SD estimates more reliable.

Conclusions

Whereas using the efficient influence function to estimate the ATE makes the estimator double‐debiasing (DDB), we proposed to multiply the efficient influence function by the “overlap weight (OW)” to modify the DDB estimator, where the OW is proportional to πX(1−πX) with πX being the PS. We also proposed to apply the OW and “cross‐fitting” to the TMLEs. These proposals are made, because both the DDB estimator and TMLE suffer from the “inverse probability weighting (IPW) problem” that πX close to 0 or 1 makes the estimators numerically unstable.

One downside of our proposals is that, since OW requires πX to be correctly specified, the resulting estimator ceases to be DDB, because it becomes non‐robust to misspecifications in πX while it remains robust to misspecifications in the outcome regression functions, which is shown in the Appendix. Nevertheless, a small distortion in weight is in general a less serious problem than that in the outcome regression functions. We further showed that the OW‐modified estimator is almost DDB if the treatment effect is nearly constant or if πX≃0.5. The concerns for misspecifications are much alleviated by employing ML methods to estimate πX and the outcome regressions.

We conducted simulation and empirical analyses, comparing our proposals with the existing estimators. In those studies, the OW‐modified DDB estimator and TMLE performed well, and the OW‐modification indeed rendered the desired numerical stability, with no need for “an optimally trimmed” estimand that plagues the original DDB and TMLE estimators. Also, applying cross‐fitting to TMLE seems to yield better asymptotic variance estimators for TMLE than otherwise.
