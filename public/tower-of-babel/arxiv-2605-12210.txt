A Moment-QSOS Hierarchy for a Class of Quaternion Polynomial Optimization Problems Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Yanqing Liu and Jie Wang

Abstract
This paper introduces a Moment-Quaternion-Sum-of-Squares (QSOS) hierarchy for solving a class of quaternion polynomial optimization problems. This hierarchy is formulated directly in the quaternion domain and consists of a sequence of semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations that provide monotonic lower bounds on the optimal value. To improve scalability, we incorporate correlative sparsity, which can significantly reduce the size of the resulting SDPs for large-scale sparse problems. Furthermore, we introduce a strengthened QSOS relaxation, which enhances the tightness of the standard relaxation by enlarging the monomial basis in a controlled manner. Our various Numerical experiments show that our approach provides comparable bounds to existing approaches, while significantly reducing computation time and memory usage. In particular, applications to the quaternion-based maximum margin criterion problem and the classical orientation synchronization problem illustrate the practical effectiveness of the framework.

Introduction

Quaternion optimization problems are optimization problems in which the decision variables take values in the quaternion algebra. Such problems arise naturally in a broad range of areas where quaternion representations provide algebraic or geometric advantages, including signal processing
[
21
]
, image processing
[
18
]
, computer vision
[
26
]
, robotics
[
3
,
9
]
, and 3D rotation estimation
[
19
,
20
]
, among others.

Over the past decades, several approaches have been proposed for quaternion optimization problems.
One common approach is to reformulate quaternion optimization problems as equivalent optimization problems over the real field so that the standard real optimization techniques can be used.
Another line of work seeks to solve optimization problems directly in quaternion variables by developing appropriate notions of convexity, inner products, and duality within the quaternion algebra, together with corresponding optimization algorithms and theoretical guarantees
[
5
]
.

Polynomial optimization problems (POPs) constitute a fundamental class of optimization problems.
Most existing theoretical and algorithmic developments focus on POPs defined over the real or complex field.
A landmark result in this direction is the Lasserre hierarchy of moment-sum of squares (SOS) relaxations
[
13
,
11
]
, which approximates a POP by a sequence of semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations with provable convergence to the global optimum.
This framework has been widely adopted in the literature and successfully applied in a variety of problems
[
17
,
10
]
.
More recently, SOS techniques have been further extended to handle noncommutative polynomial optimization problems
[
25
]
.

However, polynomial optimization over the quaternion algebra remains largely unexplored.
A natural approach is to reformulate quaternion polynomial optimization problems as an equivalent optimization problem over the real field
[
12
,
27
]
. While this enables the application of existing methods for real polynomial optimization problems, it often leads to a substantial increase in problem dimension and computational complexity. More importantly, such reformulations may obscure or eliminate the intrinsic algebraic and geometric structures unique to quaternion models.
An alternative approach modifies the underlying multiplication rules to obtain a commutative quaternion model
[
8
]
, which allows the use of classical polynomial optimization tools but departs from standard quaternion algebra.

Motivated by these limitations, we aim to develop a Moment-Quaternion-Sum-of-Squares (QSOS) hierarchy that directly handles quaternion polynomials, generalizing the Lasserre hierarchy to the quaternion domain.
A key challenge lies in the hybrid commutativity structure of quaternions. Although quaternion multiplication is generally noncommutative(products of different quaternion variables do not commute), the product of a quaternion variable and its conjugate is commutative and real.
This mixture of noncommutative and commutative behaviors breaks many of the algebraic assumptions underlying standard polynomial optimization theory and complicates the construction of moment and SOS relaxations in the quaternion domain.

In this paper, we study quaternion polynomial optimization problems (QPOPs) and propose a QSOS relaxation framework that operates directly on quaternion polynomials.
The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

•

We present an economical reformulation that converts quaternion semidefinite relaxations of QPOPs into equivalent real SDPs. This reformulation ensures that our framework can be directly implemented with existing SDP solvers.

•

We establish a Moment-QSOS hierarchy for QPOPs.
Specifically, we derive a sufficient certificate for the nonnegativity of quaternion polynomials and QSOS relaxation is obtained by restricting this certificate to a fixed degree, leading to an SDP.
Its dual counterpart is the moment relaxation, where moments are indexed by quaternionic words and feasibility is enforced by positive semidefiniteness of the associated moment and localizing matrices. Together, these two perspectives form the Moment-QSOS hierarchy, providing a systematic approach to approximate QPOPs from below.

•

To improve scalability, we adapt correlative sparsity pattern of quaternion polynomials within the QSOS framework, which can significantly reduce the
size of the resulting SDPs for large-scale sparse problems. Furthermore, we incorporate auxiliary PSD constraints on extended bases to strengthen the relaxation without significantly increasing computational overhead.

•

We validate the proposed QSOS framework through numerical experiments on both randomly generated QPOPs and two representative QPOPs arising in realistic applications.
Across all tested instances, QSOS achieves comparable relaxation bounds as the conventional RSOS approach while consistently requiring less computational time.

The outline of this paper is as follows.
Section 2 introduces notation and basic properties of quaternion algebra.
Section 3 discusses quaternion semidefinite programming and its reformulation as real SDP.
Section 4 develops the QSOS framework and provides a sufficient condition for the nonnegativity of quaternion polynomials.
Section 5 introduces the Moment-QSOS hierarchy and derives a sequence of SDP relaxations for QPOPs, including sparse and strengthened variants.
Section 6 reports numerical experiments and comparisons with existing methods.

2.1

Let

denote the skew field of quaternions. Each

\quad\hbox{\bf{i}}\hbox{\bf{j}}=-\hbox{\bf{j}}\hbox{\bf{i}},\quad\hbox{\bf{j}}\hbox{\bf{k}}=-\hbox{\bf{k}}\hbox{\bf{j}},\quad\hbox{\bf{k}}\hbox{\bf{i}}=-\hbox{\bf{i}}\hbox{\bf{k}}.

The conjugate of

\overline{q}=q_{R}-q_{I}\hbox{\bf{i}}-q_{J}\hbox{\bf{j}}-q_{K}\hbox{\bf{k}}

Let

.

Similarly, any quaternion matrix

A=A_{R}+A_{I}\hbox{\bf{i}}+A_{J}\hbox{\bf{j}}+A_{K}\hbox{\bf{k}},

where
A_{R},A_{I},A_{J},A_{K}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}

The transpose of a quaternion matrix

is denoted by
A^{\intercal}
and its conjugate transpose is denoted by
A^{*}
. A quaternion matrix
A\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n\times n}
is said to be Hermitian if
A=A^{*}
. For a positive integer

, the set of
n\times n
Hermitian quaternion matrices is denoted by
{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
.
A quaternion matrix
A\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n\times n}
is said to be positive semidefinite (PSD), written as
A\succeq 0
, if
\mathbf{v}^{*}A\mathbf{v}\geq 0
for all
\mathbf{v}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
.
For
A,B\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n\times n}
, we denote by
\langle A,B\rangle

\langle A,B\rangle=\hbox{\rm{Tr}}(A^{*}B)
.

For
q=q_{R}+q_{I}\hbox{\bf{i}}+q_{J}\hbox{\bf{j}}+q_{K}\hbox{\bf{k}}\in{\mathbb{H}}

{\mathcal{R}}(q)\coloneqq q_{R},{\mathcal{I}}(q)\coloneqq q_{I},{\mathcal{J}}(q)\coloneqq q_{J},{\mathcal{K}}(q)\coloneqq q_{K}.

These notations are extended componentwise to quaternion vectors and quaternion matrices.
We regard
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
as a
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space and endow it with the scalar product

which is defined by

\langle{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}}={\mathcal{R}}({\mathbf{u}}^{*}{\mathbf{v}})={\mathcal{R}}({\mathbf{u}})^{\intercal}{\mathcal{R}}({\mathbf{v}})+{\mathcal{I}}({\mathbf{u}})^{\intercal}{\mathcal{I}}({\mathbf{v}})+{\mathcal{J}}({\mathbf{u}})^{\intercal}{\mathcal{J}}({\mathbf{v}})+{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbf{u}})^{\intercal}{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbf{v}}),\quad{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}.

For real vectors

\langle{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\rangle\coloneqq{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{y}}
, where
{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}
denotes the transpose of

.
For any quaternion matrix

2.2
Quaternion polynomials

Let
{\mathbf{q}}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})
be a tuple of noncommuting quaternion variables, and let
\langle{\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}}\rangle
be the set of all possible words (monomials) generated by

and its conjugate
\overline{{\mathbf{q}}}
. Each word

takes the form

, where
w_{i}\in{\mathbf{q}}\,\text{or}\,\overline{{\mathbf{q}}}
for
i\in[n]
. The empty word is denoted by

With

\mathfrak{R}\;\coloneqq\;\{\,w\in\langle{\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}}\rangle\;|\;w\ \text{does not contain any }|q_{i}|^{2}\,\},

every monomial

can be written as

w\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{n}|q_{i}|^{2\alpha_{i}}\tilde{w},\quad(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\tilde{w}\in\mathfrak{R}.

The
involution
of a monomial
\tilde{w}=w_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{t}\in\mathfrak{R}
is defined as
\tilde{w}^{*}=\overline{w}_{t}\cdots\overline{w}_{2}\overline{w}_{1}
,
and for a monomial
w\in\langle{\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}}\rangle
in normal form
w=\prod_{i=1}^{n}|q_{i}|^{2\alpha_{i}}\tilde{w}
, the
involution
is defined as
w^{*}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}|q_{i}|^{2\alpha_{i}}\tilde{w}^{*}
, and its degree
|w|
is defined as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}2\alpha_{i}
plus the length of
\tilde{w}
. Let
{\mathbf{w}}_{d}
denote the vector of all monomials of degree at most
d
.

Due to the noncommutativity of quaternion multiplication, general quaternion polynomials can have complicated forms.
In this paper, we consider quaternion polynomials of the form

f({\mathbf{q}})={\mathbf{w}}^{*}Q{\mathbf{w}},

where
{\mathbf{w}}
is a vector of quaternion monomials,
Q\in\mathbb{H}^{|{\mathbf{w}}|\times|{\mathbf{w}}|}
.
The degree of
f
is defined by

\deg(f):=\max\left\{|w_{i}|+|w_{j}|:\,Q_{ij}\neq 0\right\}.

The involution of
f
is defined by

f^{*}({\mathbf{q}}):={\mathbf{w}}^{*}Q^{*}{\mathbf{w}}.

We shall say that a quaternion polynomial
f
is
symmetric
if
f=f^{*},\text{i.e.}Q=Q^{*}
.
It is clear that a symmetric quaternion polynomial necessarily takes real values.
We define the set of symmetric quaternion polynomials by

\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}:=\left\{{\mathbf{w}}^{*}Q{\mathbf{w}},\;Q\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}|}\right\}.

Moreover, the two objective functions coincide, yielding

4
Quaternion sums of squares

To extend the Moment–SOS hierarchy to quaternion polynomial optimization, one first needs to introduce a suitable notion of sums of squares in the quaternion setting. In this section, we introduce the concept of QSOS polynomials, which provide a constructive way to certify nonnegativity of quaternion polynomials.

As in the real polynomial case, our goal is to characterize the nonnegativity of symmetric polynomials through an appropriate sum-of-squares structure.

Definition 4
(QSOS polynomial).

Let
f\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}
with
\deg(f)\leq 2d
.
We say that
f
is a
QSOS polynomial
if it admits a representation

f({\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}})=\sum_{k=1}^{r}v_{k}({\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}})^{*}v_{k}({\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}}),

where each
v_{k}=\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{w}}_{d}
.

The following proposition shows that QSOS polynomials admit an equivalent Gram matrix characterization.

Proposition 5
.

Let
f\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}
with
\deg(f)\leq 2d
.

f
is a QSOS polynomial if and only if there exists a quaternion Hermitian matrix

G\succeq 0
such that

f={\mathbf{w}}_{d}^{*}G{\mathbf{w}}_{d},

Proof.

Suppose that

f=\sum_{k=1}^{r}(\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{w}}_{d})^{*}(\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{w}}_{d})
,
then

f=\sum_{k=1}^{r}{\mathbf{w}}_{d}^{*}\overline{\mathbf{c}}_{k}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{w}}_{d}={\mathbf{w}}_{d}^{*}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{r}\overline{\mathbf{c}}_{k}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\intercal}\right){\mathbf{w}}_{d}.

Define

G_{f}:=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\overline{\mathbf{c}}_{k}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\intercal}
,
then
G_{f}\succeq 0
, and

f={\mathbf{w}}_{d}^{*}G_{f}{\mathbf{w}}_{d}.

Conversely, suppose that

f={\mathbf{w}}_{d}^{*}G{\mathbf{w}}_{d},\,G\succeq 0.

Since
G\succeq 0
, there exists a quaternion matrix
C
such that

G_{f}=C^{*}C.

Let
\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\intercal}
denote the
k
-th row of
C
.
Then

f={\mathbf{w}}_{d}^{*}C^{*}C{\mathbf{w}}_{d}=\sum_{k}(\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{w}}_{d})^{*}(\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{w}}_{d}),

which gives a QSOS decomposition.

From the above discussion, every QSOS polynomial is nonnegative on
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
.

5
Quaternion polynomial optimization and the Moment-QSOS hierarchy

A class of quaternion polynomial optimization problems (QPOPs) considered in this paper takes the form

\begin{cases}\inf\limits_{{\mathbf{q}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}}&f({\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}})={\mathbf{w}}^{*}Q{\mathbf{w}}\\
\,\,\rm{s.t.}&g_{i}({\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}})\geq 0,\quad i\in[t],\\
&h_{j}({\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}})=0,\quad j\in[s],\end{cases}

(QPOP)

where
{\mathbf{q}}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})
is a tuple of quaternion decision variables,
{\mathbf{w}}
is a vector of quaternion monomials generated by
{\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}}
,
Q\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}|}
, and
g_{i},h_{j}\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}
.

The Moment-QSOS hierarchy presented below applies in either of the following cases:

Case 1.

f,g_{i},h_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}\langle{\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}}\rangle
;

Case 2.

g_{i},h_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}[|{\mathbf{q}}|^{2}]
.

One way to handle (
QPOP
) is to reformulate it as a real polynomial optimization problem. More concretely, for each quaternion variable
q_{i}
, we introduce four real variables
q_{R}^{i},q_{I}^{i},q_{J}^{i},q_{K}^{i}
such that

q_{i}=q_{R}^{i}+q_{I}^{i}\mathbf{i}+q_{J}^{i}\mathbf{j}+q_{K}^{i}\mathbf{k}.

(4)

Since
f,g_{i},h_{j}
are symmetric, performing the substitution (
4
) in (
QPOP
) yields a real polynomial optimization problem which could then be solved using the usual real Moment-SOS hierarchy
[
13
]
.
However, this reformulation not only quadruples the number of variables but also destroys the quaternion structure.
In the following, we present a Moment-QSOS hierarchy for (
QPOP
) which is directly defined on the quaternion domain.

5.1
The Moment-QSOS hierarchy

Let

K:=\{{\mathbf{q}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}:g_{i}({\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}})\geq 0,\ i\in[t],\ h_{j}({\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}})=0,\ j\in[s]\}

be the feasible set of problem (
QPOP
).
Similarly to the real case, we aim to characterize nonnegativity of quaternion polynomials on the feasible set by constructing suitable sum-of-squares type representations.
The following proposition provides a sufficient condition for nonnegativity of symmetric quaternion polynomials on the feasible set.

Proposition 6
.

Let
f\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}
.
If
f
admits a decomposition of the form

f={\mathbf{w}}_{0}^{*}G_{0}{\mathbf{w}}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}{\mathbf{w}}_{g,i}^{*}G_{i}\,g_{i}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{g,i}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}{\mathbf{w}}_{h,j}^{*}H_{j}\,h_{j}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{h,j},

(5)

where
{\mathbf{w}}_{0}
,
{\mathbf{w}}_{g,i}
, and
{\mathbf{w}}_{h,j}
are vectors of quaternion monomials generated by
{\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}}
,

G_{0}\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{0}|}\succeq 0
,
G_{i}\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{g,i}|}\succeq 0
, and
H_{j}\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{h,j}|}
,
then
f
is nonnegative on
K
.

Proof.

Take any
{\mathbf{q}}\in K
and evaluate (
5
) at
{\mathbf{q}}
.
The first term
{\mathbf{w}}_{0}^{*}G_{0}{\mathbf{w}}_{0}
is nonnegative.
Since
{\mathbf{q}}\in K
, we have
g_{i}({\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}})\geq 0
. Hence,

{\mathbf{w}}_{g,i}^{*}G_{i}\,g_{i}({\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}})\,{\mathbf{w}}_{g,i}={\mathbf{w}}_{g,i}^{*}G_{i}{\mathbf{w}}_{g,i}\,g_{i}({\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}})\geq 0.

On the other hand, when
h_{j}({\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}})=0
, the corresponding term

{\mathbf{w}}_{h,j}^{*}H_{j}\,h_{j}({\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}})\,{\mathbf{w}}_{h,j}

vanishes.
Therefore, it follows from (
5
) that

f({\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}})\geq 0.

Since
{\mathbf{q}}\in K
is arbitrary,
f
is nonnegative on
K
.

5.1.1
QSOS relaxation

A real scalar
\lambda
is a lower bound for the optimal value
f_{\min}
of (
QPOP
) if and only if the polynomial
f-\lambda
is nonnegative on
K
.
Define
d_{i}\coloneqq\lceil\deg(g_{i})/2\rceil
for
i\in[t]
,
e_{j}\coloneqq\lceil\deg(h_{j})/2\rceil
for
j\in[s]
and
d_{\min}\coloneqq\max\,\{\lceil\deg(f)/2\rceil,d_{1},\ldots,d_{t},\,e_{1},\ldots,e_{s}\}
.

For a fixed relaxation order
d\geq d_{\min}
, we replace the nonnegativity condition

by the sufficient condition in Proposition
6

f-\lambda={\mathbf{w}}_{d}^{*}G_{0}{\mathbf{w}}_{d}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}{\mathbf{w}}_{d-d_{i}}^{*}G_{i}\,g_{i}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{d-d_{i}}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}{\mathbf{w}}_{d-e_{j}}^{*}H_{j}\,h_{j}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{d-e_{j}},

(6)

This leads to the
QSOS relaxation
of (

\begin{cases}\sup\limits_{\lambda,G_{0},G_{i},H_{j}}&\lambda\\[3.00003pt]
\rm{s.t.}&f-\lambda={\mathbf{w}}_{d}^{*}G_{0}{\mathbf{w}}_{d}+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{t}{\mathbf{w}}_{d-d_{i}}^{*}G_{i}\,g_{i}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{d-d_{i}}+\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{s}{\mathbf{w}}_{d-e_{j}}^{*}H_{j}\,h_{j}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{d-e_{j}},\\[6.00006pt]
&G_{0}\succeq 0,\quad G_{i}\succeq 0\ (i=1,\ldots,t),\quad H_{j}\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{d-e_{j}}|}\ (j=1,\ldots,s).\end{cases}

(QSOS-
{\mathbb{H}}
)

Typical choices of the monomial vector
{\mathbf{w}}_{d}
include
[{\mathbf{q}}]_{d}
and
[{\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}}]_{d}
.

(
QSOS-

H

QSDP-

H

) by matching the coefficients of the monomials in the constraints.
Applying the real reformulation method described in Section
3
then yields an equivalent real SDP, which can be solved using existing SDP solvers.

Let
p\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}
with
\deg(p)\leq 2d
.
Then
p
admits a representation of the form

p=p_{1}+{\mathcal{R}}\!\left(\sum_{u\in{\mathbf{w}}_{2d}\setminus\{1\}}p_{u}\,u\right),\quad p_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}},\quad p_{u}\in{\mathbb{H}}.

We denote by

\hbox{\rm{coe}}(p):=(p_{u})_{u\in{\mathbf{w}}_{2d}}

the coefficient vector of
p
with respect to the monomial vector
{\mathbf{w}}_{2d}
, where coefficients of monomials not appearing in

are taken to be zero. And
\hbox{\rm{coe}}(p)_{1}=p_{1}
.
In particular, if
f={\mathbf{w}}_{r}^{*}Q{\mathbf{w}}_{r}
with
r=\lceil\deg(f)/2\rceil
, then

f={\mathcal{R}}\!\left(\sum_{i,j}Q_{ij}\,w_{j}w_{i}^{*}\right),

and we define

b:=\hbox{\rm{coe}}(f)\in{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{2d}|}.

Next, write

g_{i}=\sum_{t}g_{i,t}\,t,h_{j}=\sum_{t}h_{j,t}\,t,

where
g_{i,t},h_{j,t}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
For each
u\in{\mathbf{w}}_{2d}
, define linear operators

\mathcal{A}_{u}^{(0)}:{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{d}|}\to{\mathbb{H}},\mathcal{A}_{u}^{(i)}:{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{d-d_{i}}|}\to{\mathbb{H}},\mathcal{B}_{u}^{(j)}:{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{d-e_{j}}|}\to{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{u}^{(0)}(G_{0})

\displaystyle:=\hbox{\rm{coe}}\!\left({\mathcal{R}}\!\left(\sum_{k,\ell}(G_{0})_{k\ell}\,w_{\ell}w_{k}^{*}\right)\right)_{u},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{u}^{(i)}(G_{i})

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{u}^{(j)}(H_{j})

\displaystyle:=\hbox{\rm{coe}}\!\left({\mathcal{R}}\!\left(\sum_{k,\ell,t}(H_{j})_{k\ell}h_{j,t}\,t\,w_{\ell}w_{k}^{*}\right)\right)_{u},\quad j=1,\dots,s.

Then the polynomial identity in (
QSOS-

H

b_{u}-\delta_{u,1}\lambda=\mathcal{A}_{u}^{(0)}(G_{0})+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{A}_{u}^{(i)}(G_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\mathcal{B}_{u}^{(j)}(H_{j}),\quad\forall u\in{\mathbf{w}}_{2d},

(7)

where
\delta_{u,1}
is the Kronecker symbol.

To obtain a standard SDP form, we introduce

\lambda=\lambda^{+}-\lambda^{-},\lambda^{\pm}\geq 0,

and decompose

H_{j}=H_{j}^{+}-H_{j}^{-},H_{j}^{\pm}\succeq 0.

Define the block diagonal matrix

X:=\diag\bigl(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-},G_{0},G_{1},\dots,G_{t},H_{1}^{+},H_{1}^{-},\dots,H_{s}^{+},H_{s}^{-}\bigr),

and

C:=\diag(1,-1,0,\dots,0).

For each
u\in{\mathbf{w}}_{2d}

{\mathscr{A}}_{u}(X):=\delta_{u,1}\lambda^{+}-\delta_{u,1}\lambda^{-}+\mathcal{A}_{u}^{(0)}(G_{0})+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{A}_{u}^{(i)}(G_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\mathcal{B}_{u}^{(j)}(H_{j}^{+})-\sum_{j=1}^{s}\mathcal{B}_{u}^{(j)}(H_{j}^{-}),

and set

{\mathscr{A}}(X):=({\mathscr{A}}_{u}(X))_{u\in{\mathbf{w}}_{2d}}.

Thus, (
QSOS-

H

) can be written as the standard quaternion SDP

\begin{cases}\sup\limits_{X\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{N}}&\langle C,X\rangle\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&{\mathscr{A}}(X)=b,\\
&X\succeq 0,\end{cases}

(8)

where
N
is the dimension of the block diagonal matrix
X
.

Proposition 7
.

If
f,g_{i},h_{j}
all have real coefficients, then problem (
QSOS-

H

) admits an optimal solution in which
G_{0}
and
G_{i}
can be taken as real symmetric positive semidefinite matrices, and

can be taken as real symmetric matrices.

Proof.

Take any feasible solution
(\lambda,G_{0},G_{1},\ldots,G_{t},H_{1},\ldots,H_{s})
of (
QSOS-

H

). Then

f-\lambda={\mathbf{w}}_{d}^{*}G_{0}{\mathbf{w}}_{d}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}{\mathbf{w}}_{d-d_{i}}^{*}G_{i}\,g_{i}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{d-d_{i}}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}{\mathbf{w}}_{d-e_{j}}^{*}H_{j}\,h_{j}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{d-e_{j}},

(9)

where
G_{0}\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{d}|}\succeq 0
,
G_{i}\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{d-d_{i}}|}\succeq 0
, and
H_{j}\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{d-e_{j}}|}
.

For each
u\in\{1,\hbox{\bf{i}},\hbox{\bf{j}},\hbox{\bf{k}}\}
, define
\Phi_{u}:{\mathbb{H}}\langle{\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}}\rangle\to{\mathbb{H}}\langle{\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}}\rangle
by

\displaystyle\Phi_{u}(a)=u^{*}au,\quad\forall a\in{\mathbb{H}},\quad\Phi_{u}(q_{\ell})=q_{\ell},\quad\Phi_{u}(\overline{q}_{\ell})=\overline{q}_{\ell},\quad\ell=1,\dots,n,

and extend it multiplicatively and additively, namely,

\Phi_{u}(p_{1}+p_{2})=\Phi_{u}(p_{1})+\Phi_{u}(p_{2}),\quad\Phi_{u}(p_{1}p_{2})=\Phi_{u}(p_{1})\Phi_{u}(p_{2})

for all
p_{1},p_{2}\in{\mathbb{H}}\langle{\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}}\rangle
.

For any matrix
M=(m_{\alpha\beta})
, write
\Phi_{u}(M):=(\Phi_{u}(m_{\alpha\beta}))
. Applying
\Phi_{u}
to both sides of (
9
), we obtain

f-\lambda={\mathbf{w}}_{d}^{*}\Phi_{u}(G_{0}){\mathbf{w}}_{d}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}{\mathbf{w}}_{d-d_{i}}^{*}\Phi_{u}(G_{i})\,g_{i}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{d-d_{i}}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}{\mathbf{w}}_{d-e_{j}}^{*}\Phi_{u}(H_{j})\,h_{j}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{d-e_{j}}.

Since
G_{i}\succeq 0
, there exists a quaternion matrix
B_{i}
such that
G_{i}=B_{i}^{*}B_{i}
. Noting that
\Phi_{u}(a^{*})=\Phi_{u}(a)^{*}
for all
a\in{\mathbb{H}}
, we have

\Phi_{u}(G_{i})=\Phi_{u}(B_{i}^{*}B_{i})=\Phi_{u}(B_{i})^{*}\Phi_{u}(B_{i})\succeq 0.

Similarly,
\Phi_{u}(G_{0})\succeq 0
. If
H_{j}\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{d-e_{j}}|}
, then

\Phi_{u}(H_{j})^{*}=\Phi_{u}(H_{j}^{*})=\Phi_{u}(H_{j}),

so
\Phi_{u}(H_{j})
is still Hermitian.

Now define

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{0}:=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{u\in\{1,\hbox{\bf{i}},\hbox{\bf{j}},\hbox{\bf{k}}\}}\Phi_{u}(G_{0}),\qquad\widetilde{G}_{i}:=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{u\in\{1,\hbox{\bf{i}},\hbox{\bf{j}},\hbox{\bf{k}}\}}\Phi_{u}(G_{i}),

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{j}:=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{u\in\{1,\hbox{\bf{i}},\hbox{\bf{j}},\hbox{\bf{k}}\}}\Phi_{u}(H_{j}).

For any
a=a_{0}+a_{1}\hbox{\bf{i}}+a_{2}\hbox{\bf{j}}+a_{3}\hbox{\bf{k}}\in{\mathbb{H}}

Hence
\widetilde{G}_{0}

are real symmetric matrices. Moreover,

Therefore,

is still feasible for (
QSOS-

H

) and has the same objective value. This proves the result.

5.1.2
Moment relaxation

We now introduce the corresponding moment relaxation from the dual point of view.
The dual problem of (
8

\begin{cases}\inf\limits_{y\in{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{2d}|}}&\langle b,y\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&{\mathscr{A}}^{*}(y)\succeq C.\end{cases}

\displaystyle[A_{u}^{(0)}]_{k\ell}=\hbox{\rm{coe}}\!\left({\mathcal{R}}(w_{\ell}w_{k}^{*})\right)_{u},\qquad k,\ell=1,\dots,|{\mathbf{w}}_{d}|,

y=(y_{u})_{u\in{\mathbf{w}}_{2d}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{2d}|}

\displaystyle({\mathscr{A}}^{*}(y))_{G_{0}}

\displaystyle({\mathscr{A}}^{*}(y))_{H_{j}}

[{\mathbf{M}}_{d}(y)]_{k,\ell}=y_{w_{\ell}w_{k}^{*}},\quad k,\ell=1,\dots,|{\mathbf{w}}_{d}|.

Let

(11)

E_{d-e_{j}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{d-e_{j}}|\times|{\mathbf{w}}_{d+1-e_{j}}|}
satisfying

{\mathbf{w}}_{d-d_{i}}=E_{d-d_{i}}{\mathbf{w}}_{d+1-d_{i}},\quad{\mathbf{w}}_{d-e_{j}}=E_{d-e_{j}}{\mathbf{w}}_{d+1-e_{j}}.

Define

\widetilde{G}_{0}:=E_{d}^{\intercal}G_{0}E_{d},\quad\widetilde{G}_{i}:=E_{d-d_{i}}^{\intercal}G_{i}E_{d-d_{i}},\quad\widetilde{H}_{j}:=E_{d-e_{j}}^{\intercal}H_{j}E_{d-e_{j}}.

Then

{\mathbf{w}}_{d+1}^{*}\widetilde{G}_{0}{\mathbf{w}}_{d+1}={\mathbf{w}}_{d}^{*}G_{0}{\mathbf{w}}_{d},

{\mathbf{w}}_{d+1-d_{i}}^{*}\widetilde{G}_{i}\,g_{i}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{d+1-d_{i}}={\mathbf{w}}_{d-d_{i}}^{*}G_{i}\,g_{i}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{d-d_{i}},

{\mathbf{w}}_{d+1-e_{j}}^{*}\widetilde{H}_{j}\,h_{j}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{d+1-e_{j}}={\mathbf{w}}_{d-e_{j}}^{*}H_{j}\,h_{j}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{d-e_{j}}.

Therefore, (
11
) can be rewritten as

f-\lambda={\mathbf{w}}_{d+1}^{*}\widetilde{G}_{0}{\mathbf{w}}_{d+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}{\mathbf{w}}_{d+1-d_{i}}^{*}\widetilde{G}_{i}\,g_{i}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{d+1-d_{i}}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}{\mathbf{w}}_{d+1-e_{j}}^{*}\widetilde{H}_{j}\,h_{j}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{d+1-e_{j}}.

Moreover,
\widetilde{G}_{0}\succeq 0
,
\widetilde{G}_{i}\succeq 0
for
i\in[t]
, and
\widetilde{H}_{j}\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{d+1-e_{j}}|}
. Hence
(\lambda,\widetilde{G}_{0},\{\widetilde{G}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t},\{\widetilde{H}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{s})
is a feasible solution of the
(d+1)
-th order QSOS relaxation.

This shows that every feasible solution of the
d
-th order relaxation can be extended to a feasible solution of the
(d+1)
-th order relaxation with the same objective value. Let
\rho_{d}
denote the optimal value of the
d
-th QSOS relaxation (
QSOS-

H

). Then
\rho_{d}\leq\rho_{d+1}
. Therefore,
\{\rho_{d}\}_{d\geq d_{\min}}
forms a monotonically nondecreasing sequence of lower bounds. The same conclusion holds for the dual moment relaxations (
Mom-

H

). This completes the proof.

Although the above proposition establishes monotonicity,
it does not imply that the bounds necessarily converge to the global optimum at a finite relaxation order, or even asymptotically.

First order relaxations of quaternion QCQPs

In this subsection, we study quaternion quadratically constrained quadratic programs (QCQP). We find that for this class, the first-order QSOS relaxation yields exactly the same optimal value as the first-order SOS relaxation applied to the equivalent real reformulation.

Consider the following quaternion QCQP:

\begin{cases}\inf\limits_{{\mathbf{q}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}}&f({\mathbf{q}})=[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}^{*}Q[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}\\
\text{\rm s.t.}&g_{i}({\mathbf{q}})=[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}^{*}D_{i}[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}\geq 0,\quad i\in[t],\\
&h_{j}({\mathbf{q}})=[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}^{*}F_{j}[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}=0,\quad j\in[s],\end{cases}

(12)

where
[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}=[1,q_{1},\dots,q_{n}]^{\intercal}
.
We assume that one of the following conditions holds:

1.

Q,D_{i},F_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
;

2.

Q\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{n+1}
, while

D_{i}
and
F_{j}
are real diagonal matrices.

The first-order QSOS relaxation of (
12
) is given by:

\begin{cases}\sup\limits_{\lambda,\,G,\,m_{i},\ell_{j}}&\lambda\\
\text{s.t.}&[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}^{*}Q[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}-\lambda=[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}^{*}G[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}m_{i}g_{i}({\mathbf{q}})+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}h_{j}({\mathbf{q}}),\\
&G\succeq 0,\ m_{i}\geq 0,\ \ell_{j}\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

(13)

Write

{\mathbf{q}}={\mathbf{q}}_{R}+{\mathbf{q}}_{I}\hbox{\bf{i}}+{\mathbf{q}}_{J}\hbox{\bf{j}}+{\mathbf{q}}_{K}\hbox{\bf{k}},

where

{\mathbf{q}}_{R},{\mathbf{q}}_{I},{\mathbf{q}}_{J},{\mathbf{q}}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Accordingly,

[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}={\mathbf{z}}_{R}+{\mathbf{z}}_{I}\hbox{\bf{i}}+{\mathbf{z}}_{J}\hbox{\bf{j}}+{\mathbf{z}}_{K}\hbox{\bf{k}},

where

{\mathbf{z}}_{R}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
{\mathbf{q}}_{R}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\mathbf{z}}_{I}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
{\mathbf{q}}_{I}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\mathbf{z}}_{J}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
{\mathbf{q}}_{J}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\mathbf{z}}_{K}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
{\mathbf{q}}_{K}\end{bmatrix}.

Define the associated real vector

{\mathbf{x}}:=[{\mathbf{z}}_{R}^{\intercal},{\mathbf{z}}_{I}^{\intercal},{\mathbf{z}}_{J}^{\intercal},{\mathbf{z}}_{K}^{\intercal}]^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{4n+4}.

By Lemma (
1
),

[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}^{*}A[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}={\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\Lambda(A){\mathbf{x}}

holds for every

A\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{n+1}
.

Therefore, the first-order SOS relaxation of the equivalent real problem can be written as

\begin{cases}\sup\limits_{\lambda,X,a_{i},b_{j}}&\lambda\\
\text{s.t.}&{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\Lambda(Q){\mathbf{x}}-\lambda={\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}X{\mathbf{x}}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}a_{i}{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\Lambda(D_{i}){\mathbf{x}}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}b_{j}{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\Lambda(F_{j}){\mathbf{x}},\\
&X\succeq 0,\ a_{i}\geq 0,\ b_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}.\end{cases}

(14)

Theorem 10
.

(
13
) is equivalent to (
14
). Moreover, optimal solutions correspond via
X=\Lambda(G)
.

Proof.

Let
(\lambda,G,\{m_{i}\},\{\ell_{j}\})
be a feasible solution of (
13
).
Since
[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}^{*}A[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}={\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\Lambda(A){\mathbf{x}}
holds for any
A\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{n+1}
.
The equality constraint in (
13
) gives

{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\Lambda(Q){\mathbf{x}}-\lambda={\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\Lambda(G){\mathbf{x}}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}m_{i}{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\Lambda(D_{i}){\mathbf{x}}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\Lambda(F_{j}){\mathbf{x}}.

Hence
(\lambda,X\coloneqq\Lambda(G),\{a_{i}\coloneqq m_{i}\},\{b_{j}\coloneqq\ell_{j}\})
is a feasible solution of (
14
) with the same objective value.

Conversely, let
(\lambda,X,\{a_{i}\},\{b_{j}\})
be a feasible solution of (
14
). The equality constraint in (
14
) can be written as

{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\Bigl(\Lambda(Q)-\lambda\Lambda(E_{00})-X-\sum_{i=1}^{t}a_{i}\Lambda(D_{i})-\sum_{j=1}^{s}b_{j}\Lambda(F_{j})\Bigr){\mathbf{x}}=0,

where
E_{00}:=\diag(1,0,\dots,0)\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{n+1}
. Hence we have the matrix identity

\Lambda(Q)-\lambda\Lambda(E_{00})=X+\sum_{i=1}^{t}a_{i}\Lambda(D_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{s}b_{j}\Lambda(F_{j}).\quad(\star)

(15)

Note that
\Lambda(Q)
,
\Lambda(E_{00})
,
\Lambda(D_{i})
, and
\Lambda(F_{j})
all belong to
\mathrm{Im}(\Lambda)
. It follows that
X\in\mathrm{Im}(\Lambda)
. Hence there exists a Hermitian quaternion matrix
G
such that
X=\Lambda(G)
. By Lemma (
1
),
X\succeq 0
implies
G\succeq 0
. Therefore, the matrix identity
(\star)
can be rewritten as

\Lambda(Q)-\lambda\Lambda(E_{00})=\Lambda(G)+\sum_{i=1}^{t}a_{i}\Lambda(D_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{s}b_{j}\Lambda(F_{j}).

Since
\Lambda
is injective, we obtain

Q-\lambda E_{00}=G+\sum_{i=1}^{t}a_{i}D_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}b_{j}F_{j}.

Multiplying both sides on the left and right by
[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}^{*}
and
[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}
, respectively, yields

[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}^{*}Q[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}-\lambda=[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}^{*}G[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}a_{i}g_{i}({\mathbf{q}})+\sum_{j=1}^{s}b_{j}h_{j}({\mathbf{q}}).

Thus,
(\lambda,G,\{m_{i}\coloneqq a_{i}\},\{\ell_{j}\coloneqq b_{j}\})
is a feasible solution of (
13
), again with the same objective value.

Therefore, the feasible solutions of (
13
) and (
14
) correspond to each other via
X=\Lambda(G)
,
a_{i}=m_{i}
, and
b_{j}=\ell_{j}
, while preserving the objective value
\lambda
. Hence the two problems have the same optimal value.

Tightness under a rank-one moment matrix

We continue to consider the quaternion QCQPs above and its first-order moment relaxation. We next give a sufficient condition under which the first-order quaternion moment relaxation is tight and an optimal solution of the original problem can be extracted from the moment matrix.

Proposition 11
.

Consider the first-order quaternion moment relaxation associated with the problem (
12
)

\begin{cases}\inf\limits_{y}&\langle b,y\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}}\\[3.00003pt]
\text{\rm s.t.}&{\mathbf{M}}_{1}(y)\succeq 0,\\[3.00003pt]
&{\mathbf{M}}_{0}(g_{i}y)\succeq 0,\quad i\in[t],\\[3.00003pt]
&{\mathbf{M}}_{0}(h_{j}y)=0,\quad j\in[s],\\[3.00003pt]
&y_{1}=1.\end{cases}

(16)

Let
y^{\star}
be an optimal moment sequence with moment matrix
M^{\star}:={\mathbf{M}}_{1}(y^{\star})
. If
\hbox{\rm{rank}}(M^{\star})=1
, then there exists an optimal solution
{\mathbf{q}}^{\star}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
of the original problem.

Proof.

Since
M^{\star}={\mathbf{M}}_{1}(y^{\star})\succeq 0
and
\hbox{\rm{rank}}(M^{\star})=1
, the spectral theorem for Hermitian quaternion matrices
[
4
]
implies that there exists a nonzero vector

{\mathbf{v}}=[v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n}]^{\intercal}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n+1}

such that

M^{\star}={\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}}^{*}.

Moreover,
|v_{0}|^{2}=(M^{\star})_{00}=y^{\star}_{1}=1
.
Multiplying
{\mathbf{v}}
on the right by the unit quaternion
\overline{v}_{0}
, if necessary, we may assume without loss of generality that
v_{0}=1
.

Define
{\mathbf{q}}^{\star}:=(q_{1}^{\star},\dots,q_{n}^{\star})
, where
q_{k}^{\star}:=v_{k}
for
k=1,\dots,n
. Then

{\mathbf{v}}=[{\mathbf{q}}^{\star}]_{1},\quad M^{\star}={\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}}^{*}=[{\mathbf{q}}^{\star}]_{1}[{\mathbf{q}}^{\star}]_{1}^{*}.

For each inequality constraint

g_{i}({\mathbf{q}})=[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}^{*}A_{i}[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1},

we have

{\mathbf{M}}_{0}(g_{i}y^{\star})={\mathcal{R}}\langle D_{i},M^{\star}\rangle=[{\mathbf{q}}^{\star}]_{1}^{*}D_{i}[{\mathbf{q}}^{\star}]_{1}=g_{i}({\mathbf{q}}^{\star}).

Similarly, for each equality constraint

h_{j}({\mathbf{q}})=[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1}^{*}B_{j}[{\mathbf{q}}]_{1},

we obtain

{\mathbf{M}}_{0}(h_{j}y^{\star})={\mathcal{R}}\langle F_{j},M^{\star}\rangle=[{\mathbf{q}}^{\star}]_{1}^{*}F_{j}[{\mathbf{q}}^{\star}]_{1}=h_{j}({\mathbf{q}}^{\star}).

Hence, the feasibility of
y^{\star}
implies that
g_{i}({\mathbf{q}}^{\star})\geq 0
and
h_{j}({\mathbf{q}}^{\star})=0
. Therefore,
{\mathbf{q}}^{\star}
is feasible for the original problem.

Finally,

f({\mathbf{q}}^{\star})=[{\mathbf{q}}^{\star}]_{1}^{*}Q[{\mathbf{q}}^{\star}]_{1}={\mathcal{R}}\langle Q,M^{\star}\rangle=\langle b,y^{\star}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}.

Since moment relaxation provides a lower bound for the objective value of the original problem, and equality holds here, the relaxation is tight and
{\mathbf{q}}^{\star}
is an optimal solution of the original problem.

5.3
Exploiting sparsities

In the QSOS relaxations introduced above, the size of the resulting SDP can grow rapidly as the number of variables and the relaxation order increase, leading to high computational and memory costs.
For quaternion polynomial optimization problems with sparse structure, one can exploit sparsity to reduce the size of the relaxation.
The sparsity considered in this subsection is measured by the number of different kinds of variable interactions appearing in the objective and constraint polynomials.
This type of sparsity is referred to as
correlative sparsity
.

Let
f
be the objective function,
g_{1},\dots,g_{t}
the inequality constraints, and
h_{1},\dots,h_{s}
the equality constraints of the original problem. To describe the correlative sparsity structure, we introduce the
correlative sparsity pattern matrix
(CSP matrix)
R=(R_{ij})\in\{0,\ast\}^{n\times n}
, where

R_{ij}=\begin{cases}\ast,&i=j,\\
\ast,&\text{if the variables }q_{i}\text{ and }q_{j}\text{ appear together in some term of }f,\\
\ast,&\text{if there exists }r\in[t]\text{ such that }q_{i},q_{j}\in\hbox{\rm{var}}(g_{r}),\\
\ast,&\text{if there exists }\ell\in[s]\text{ such that }q_{i},q_{j}\in\hbox{\rm{var}}(h_{\ell}),\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\ast
denotes a nonzero entry, and
\hbox{\rm{var}}(g_{r})
and
\hbox{\rm{var}}(h_{\ell})
denote the sets of variables involved in
g_{r}
and
h_{\ell}
, respectively. If the CSP matrix
R
is sparse, then the corresponding quaternion polynomial optimization problem is said to have a correlative sparsity.

The CSP matrix
R
naturally defines an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, where the vertex set is
V=\{1,\dots,n\}
and the edge set is

E=\bigl\{\{i,j\}:1\leq i<j\leq n,\ R_{ij}=\ast\bigr\}.

This graph is called the
correlative sparsity pattern graph
(CSP graph).

Inspired by the sparse moment–SOS relaxation framework
[
22
,
14
]
, we construct a chordal extension of the CSP graph
G
, and denote the resulting chordal graph by
G^{\prime}
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k}
be the maximal cliques of
G^{\prime}
. For background on chordal graphs and maximal cliques, see, for instance,
[
1
,
6
]
.
Next, We partition the inequality constraints
g_{1},\dots,g_{t}
and the equality constraints
h_{1},\dots,h_{s}
into

\{g_{r}\mid r\in\mathcal{I}_{j}\},\{h_{\ell}\mid\ell\in\mathcal{J}_{j}\},j=1,\ldots,k,

such that

(i)

\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{k}\subseteq[t]
are pairwise disjoint and
\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\mathcal{I}_{j}=[t]
;

(ii)

\mathcal{J}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{J}_{k}\subseteq[s]
are pairwise disjoint and
\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\mathcal{J}_{j}=[s]
;

(iii)

for every
r\in\mathcal{I}_{j}
, one has
\hbox{\rm{var}}(g_{r})\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{j}
;

(iv)

for every
\ell\in\mathcal{J}_{j}
, one has
\hbox{\rm{var}}(h_{\ell})\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{j}
.

Fix
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
and a relaxation order
d
. Let
{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathcal{C}_{j},d}
denote the monomial vector of degree at most
d
generated by
\{q_{i},\overline{q}_{i}\mid i\in\mathcal{C}_{j}\}
. Let

d_{r}:=\left\lceil\frac{\deg(g_{r})}{2}\right\rceil,e_{\ell}:=\left\lceil\frac{\deg(h_{\ell})}{2}\right\rceil.

A sparse QSOS relaxation of order
d
takes the form:

\displaystyle f-\lambda=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathcal{C}_{j},d}^{*}G_{0,j}{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathcal{C}_{j},d}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathcal{C}_{j},d-d_{r}}^{*}G_{r,j}\,g_{r}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathcal{C}_{j},d-d_{r}}

(17)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{J}_{j}}{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathcal{C}_{j},d-e_{\ell}}^{*}H_{\ell,j}\,h_{\ell}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathcal{C}_{j},d-e_{\ell}},

with

G_{0,j}\succeq 0,\quad G_{r,j}\succeq 0,\quad H_{\ell,j}\in{\mathbb{H}}{\mathbb{H}}^{|{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathcal{C}_{j},d-e_{\ell}}|}.

Compared with constructing a full Gram matrix over all variables, the above sparse relaxation introduces only local Gram matrices on the maximal cliques. When the maximal cliques are small, this can substantially reduce both the dimension and the memory cost of the resulting SDP. The numerical experiments in the next section illustrate the computational advantage of this construction for problems with explicit correlative sparsity structure.

Beyond correlative sparsity, term sparsity has proven effective in real polynomial optimization for reducing the SDP size
[
14
,
2
]
. Extending such techniques to the QSOS framework is a promising direction for future research.

5.4
Strengthening the relaxations

In this subsection, we discuss a practical method to enhance the tightness of the QSOS relaxation for a specific class of QPOPs when increasing the relaxation order is too costly to implement.
Specifically, we consider QPOPs of the form

\begin{cases}\inf\limits_{{\mathbf{q}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{s}}&f=[{\mathbf{q}}]_{k}^{*}Q[{\mathbf{q}}]_{k},\\
\text{s.t.}&g_{i}\geq 0,\quad i\in[t],\end{cases}

(18)

where
Q
is a Hermitian quaternion matrix and each
g_{i}\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}
.
Let

d_{j}:=\left\lceil\frac{\deg(g_{j})}{2}\right\rceil,d\geq\max\{k,d_{1},\dots,d_{t}\}.

For such problems, the QSOS relaxation of order
d
constructs a Gram matrix indexed by the monomial basis
[{\mathbf{q}}]_{d}
, producing a matrix
M_{0}
constrained to be positive semidefinite.
In order to improve the tightness of the relaxation without significantly increasing the overall size of the SDP, we adopt a strengthening strategy inspired by related developments in complex polynomial optimization
[
24
]
.
Specifically, instead of extending the basis to the full monomial set
[{\mathbf{q}},\overline{{\mathbf{q}}}]_{d}
, we introduce, for each variable
q_{i}
, an additional PSD constraint
M_{i,d}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{r_{d}\times r_{d}}
indexed by the extended basis
\overline{q}_{i}[{\mathbf{q}}]_{d}
, where
r_{d}=|[{\mathbf{q}}]_{d}|
.

The strengthened QSOS relaxation of order
d
for (
18
) is then formulated as:

\begin{cases}\sup\limits_{\lambda,\,G_{0},\,G_{0,i},\,G_{j}}&\lambda\\[3.00003pt]
\text{s.t.}&\begin{aligned} {}_{k}^{*}Q[{\mathbf{q}}]_{k}-\lambda&=[{\mathbf{q}}]_{d}^{*}G_{0}[{\mathbf{q}}]_{d}+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\overline{q}_{i}[{\mathbf{q}}]_{d})^{*}G_{0,i}(\overline{q}_{i}[{\mathbf{q}}]_{d})\\
&\quad+\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{t}[{\mathbf{q}}]_{d-d_{j}}^{*}G_{j}\,g_{j}\,[{\mathbf{q}}]_{d-d_{j}},\end{aligned}\\[3.99994pt]
&G_{0}\succeq 0,\quad G_{0,i}\succeq 0,\quad G_{j}\succeq 0.\end{cases}

(19)

We have implemented this strengthened relaxation and evaluated its performance in Section
6
.

6.3
Clique-based objective functions

In this set of experiments, we exploit correlative sparsity in the problem structure to reduce computational complexity. Specifically, we consider QPOPs whose objective functions are decomposed over disjoint subsets
(cliques) of variables.

\begin{cases}\inf\limits_{{\mathbf{q}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}}&\sum_{i=1}^{k}[{\mathbf{q}}_{i}]_{1}^{*}Q_{i}[{\mathbf{q}}_{i}]_{1}\\

{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\subseteq{\mathbf{q}}

i

[{\mathbf{q}}_{i}]_{1}

{\mathbf{q}}_{i}

1
and

Applications

To demonstrate the practical relevance of the proposed QSOS framework, we apply it to two representative QPOPs arising in realistic applications. The first example comes from feature extraction in image recognition, while the second concerns a classical rotation synchronization problem.

7.1
Application to Quaternion-based maximum Margin Criterion

Maximum margin criterion (MMC) method is widely used feature extraction method for image recognition.
The Quaternion-based MMC (QMMC)
[
15
]
extends the classical formulation to quaternion-valued features, in which color images are represented as quaternion vectors.

Suppose that we have
C
classes of training samples and each class
c
includes
N_{c}
samples, indexed by
\mathcal{I}_{c}
. Each sample is a color image, represented by a quaternion feature vector
{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
, where
n
denotes the feature dimension.
Let
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}
denote the mean of samples in class
c
, and
\boldsymbol{\mu}
denote the global mean over all training samples.

QMMC seeks a projection vector
{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
by maximizing the between-class scatter matrix
S_{B}
while minimizing the within-class scatter matrix
S_{W}
:

\begin{cases}\sup\limits_{{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}}&J({\mathbf{v}})={\mathbf{v}}^{*}S_{B}{\mathbf{v}}-\lambda{\mathbf{v}}^{*}S_{W}{\mathbf{v}}\\
\,\,\rm{s.t.}&\|{\mathbf{v}}\|=1,\end{cases}

(25)

where
\lambda>0
is a chosen trade-off parameter and

S_{B}=\sum_{c=1}^{C}N_{c}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}-\boldsymbol{\mu})(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{*},\qquad S_{W}=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}({\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c})({\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c})^{*}.

By defining

Q\coloneqq-(S_{B}-\lambda S_{W})\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n\times n},

problem (
25
) can be equivalently written as

\begin{cases}\inf\limits_{{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}}&{\mathbf{v}}^{*}Q{\mathbf{v}}\\
\,\,\rm{s.t.}&\|{\mathbf{v}}\|=1.\\
\end{cases}

(26)

In the numerical experiments, we take
C=2
and set
N_{1}=N_{2}=5
. Each sample is represented by a randomly generated quaternion feature vector. We consider feature dimensions
n\in\{20,30,40\}
, and generate three random instances for each dimension. For each instance, we solve the problem using QSOS with
d=1
and RSOS with
d=1
. The results are reported in Table
8
.

7.2
Application to Quaternion Synchronization

The goal of the rotation synchronization problem is to estimate a collection of unknown orientations from noisy
pairwise relative orientation measurements
[
7
]
.
Using unit quaternions to represent absolute orientations and relative orientation measurements, the
problem reduces to recovering unit-norm quaternions

{\mathbf{q}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
.

Given noisy relative orientation measurements

Q_{ij}\in{\mathbb{H}}
for
(i,j)
in a measurement graph
E
, where

Q_{ij}\approx q_{i}\overline{q}_{j}
, the appropriate minimization problem is expressed as

\begin{cases}\inf\limits_{{\mathbf{q}}\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}}&\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\|q_{i}\overline{q}_{j}-Q_{ij}\|^{2}\\
\,\,\rm{s.t.}&\|q_{i}\|=1,\ i=1,\ldots,n.\\
\end{cases}

(27)

By using the unit-norm constraint
|q_{i}|^{2}=1
, the original objective function can be expanded as

\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\left(1-2{\mathcal{R}}(\overline{Q}_{ij}q_{i}\overline{q}_{j})+\|Q_{ij}\|^{2}\right).

Since the constant terms do not affect the minimizer, the problem is equivalent to minimizing

-\sum_{(i,j)\in E}{\mathcal{R}}(\overline{Q}_{ij}q_{i}\overline{q}_{j})=-(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\overline{q}_{i}Q_{ij}/2q_{j}+\overline{q}_{j}\overline{Q}_{ij}/2q_{i}).

This gives rise to a QCQP, subject to unit-norm constraints.

In our experiments, the relative measurements are generated as

Q_{ij}=q_{i}\overline{q}_{j}+\varepsilon_{ij},

where
\varepsilon_{ij}
is a random unit quaternion scaled by a fixed noise level
0.2
.
The measurement graph
E
follows a random graph model
G(n,p)
with
p=0.2
.
For each
n\in\{20,40,60\}
, we generate three random instances and solve them using QSOS with
d=1
and RSOS with
d=1
.
The results are presented in Table
9
. The column labeled by ’true value’ reports the objective value evaluated at the ground-truth rotations used to generate the measurements.

8
Conclusions

In this paper, we developed a Moment-QSOS hierarchy for QPOPs.
By an economical reformulation into real SDPs, the proposed approach enables the direct handling of quaternion variables while remaining compatible with existing SDP solvers.
We further incorporated correlative sparsity structures to improve scalability.

Numerical experiments on both synthetic and application-driven problems show that QSOS achieves competitive bounds with significantly reduced computational cost compared to the direct RSOS method.
Moreover, the strengthened QSOS relaxation improves bound quality without substantially increasing problem size.
Applications to maximum margin criterion learning and quaternion synchronization further validate the effectiveness of the proposed framework.

Future work includes extending the framework to exploit term sparsity and studying the convergence properties of the QSOS hierarchy.
