Active layer thickening enhances environmental adaptability of larch and birch in northeast China’s permafrost region
Authors: Biao Li, Xiaodong Wu, Dongyu Yang, Xingfeng Dong, Shuying Zang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClimate warming deepens active layer thickness (ALT), altering soil water availability and reshaping plant water-acquisition strategies. However, variations in water use and environmental adaptability remain poorly understood across tree species in permafrost regions.ResultsHere we selected larch (Larix gmelinii) and birch (Betula platyphylla) under different ALT as the study species. Using hydrogen and oxygen stable isotopes, we analyzed seasonal changes in uptake depth and quantified water uptake plasticity, and assessed the importance of species and soil properties in explaining water-source contributions. The results showed that during the growing season (May-September), both species exhibited a downward migration of their principal water uptake layer. Along the ALT gradient, both species accessed deeper soil water and exhibited greater water uptake plasticity under a thicker active layer. Under similar ALT conditions, B. platyphylla showed a more rapid shift toward deeper soil water, greater use of deeper soil water, and slightly higher water uptake plasticity than L. gmelinii. Species accounted for the greatest proportion of variation in water-source contributions at 30–60 cm, whereas species and soil property were the primary drivers for water uptake at 0–30 and 60–100 cm, particularly for B. platyphylla.ConclusionsTaken together, these results suggest that both species show environmental adaptability under deeper ALT, while B. platyphylla may have a greater potential to compete for deep soil water under permafrost degradation. These results enhance our understanding of water uptake of different tree species in permafrost regions in a warming climate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09354-4.

Introduction

Boreal forests are key components of land-atmosphere water and energy exchanges, and they play a critical role in regulating the hydrological processes and climate system stability [1–3]. Water acquisition and use are major ecological determinants of plant growth, health, and species distributions [4, 5]. Plants in boreal regions may access multiple water resources, including precipitation, permafrost meltwater, surface runoff, and groundwater, but the relative contributions and uptake pathways vary among species and habitats. The water uptake capacities are largely constrained by root system architecture and depth distribution [6, 7]. In permafrost regions, the active layer is the seasonally thawed near-surface soil layer overlying perennially frozen ground, within which most plant roots are concentrated [8]. In recent decades, climate warming has driven progressive permafrost degradation, characterized by deepening of the active layer and rising ground temperatures [9–11]. These changes may intensify interspecific competition for newly available thaw water and alter plant water uptake strategies [7, 12]. Therefore, understanding the water-use patterns of dominant tree species along active layer thickness (ALT) gradients in permafrost regions is essential for assessing their responses to permafrost degradation.

Stable hydrogen and oxygen isotopes, as natural tracers, offer key advantages including high efficiency, accuracy, and minimal disturbance, making them widely used in studying vegetation water-use patterns [5, 7, 13, 14]. Most studies suggest that, with the exception of certain coastal wetlands [15] and arid saline-alkali vegetation [16], the water absorbed by plant roots does not undergo isotopic fractionation as it is transported to the leaves [17]. Based on this principle, mixing models can be used to quantify the proportional contributions of multiple potential soil water sources to xylem water, thereby evaluating vegetation water uptake plasticity. Plants with dimorphic root systems can flexibly adjust their uptake depth between shallow and deep soil layers to cope with the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of water availability, a trait that reflects their water uptake plasticity [18–21]. Greater water uptake plasticity generally confers enhanced resilience to environmental change [22].

The northeast China occupies the high-latitude permafrost zone, which has experienced a reduction of 35–37% in permafrost area since the 1970s due to climate warming [3, 23, 24]. Such rapid permafrost loss is typically accompanied by active-layer deepening and ground-ice thaw, which reshape soil hydrological connectivity and storage, thereby modifying the availability of precipitation, snowmelt, and thaw-derived meltwater to roots [25–28]. In the spring, when the active layer is shallow and the upper soil remains partially frozen [29], plants predominantly rely on surface moisture replenished by snowmelt and early precipitation. From summer to early autumn, as the active layer deepens, plant roots are able to access deeper thawed active layer water and precipitation to meet increasing water demands [7, 8]. In late autumn and winter, the active layer undergoes rapid bidirectional freezing [29], and vegetation enters dormancy, with significantly reduced water demand. Additionally, soil variables (texture, temperature, and porosity) [30, 31] and vegetation traits (leaf dry matter content and diameter at breast height) [32–35] also affect vegetation water use.

The permafrost region of the Northeast China forms an important component of China’s permafrost landscape and hosts one of the country’s largest tracts of primary forest [29, 36]. Vegetation communities are structurally complex and species rich. Vegetation and permafrost are tightly coupled through water and energy mediated feedbacks, whereby vegetation regulates ground thermal regimes by modifying albedo, shading, and evapotranspiration, while permafrost shapes vegetation growth by governing water and nutrient availability [7]. As permafrost degradation deepens the active layer, soil water dynamics may potentially lead to changes in plant survival and subsequent community turnover [12]. However, most previous studies on vegetation water use have focused on arid and semi-arid regions [5, 37, 38], while relatively little is known about tree species’ water-use strategies in permafrost regions, especially their variation along ALT gradients and responses to active-layer deepening. This knowledge gap hampers a mechanistic understanding of vegetation responses to permafrost degradation.

In this study, we used stable hydrogen and oxygen isotope analysis to elucidate the water-use strategies of two dominant tree species along ALT gradients in the permafrost region of the Northeast China. We selected Larix gmelinii (Rupr.) Kuzen. and Betula platyphylla Sukaczev because they represent contrasting coniferous and broadleaved functional types. L. gmelinii is a dominant cold-temperate conifer in relatively mature permafrost forest communities, whereas B. platyphylla is an important broadleaved pioneer species associated with forest succession and post-disturbance regeneration. Permafrost generally constrains the root systems of both species within the active layer, and progressive active layer deepening may influence their water-use strategies. Comparing these two species therefore provides a useful basis for assessing how dominant boreal tree species respond to permafrost degradation. Sampling was conducted at sites where ALT was approximately 60, 100, and 130 cm for L. gmelinii, and approximately 70, 100, and 140 cm for B. platyphylla, with a non-permafrost site included for each species. The objectives of this study were to: (1) quantify the patterns and seasonal dynamics of water uptake from different soil depths by the two species across contrasting ALT; (2) assess water uptake plasticity along the ALT gradient; and (3) identify the main drivers controlling tree water uptake. The results can improve our understanding of plant water-use mechanisms and ecological adaptation in cold-region forests undergoing permafrost degradation, and provide a basis for evaluating shifts in vegetation growth and forest ecosystem under climate warming.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study area is located in the northern Greater Khingan Mountains, northeastern China (Fig. 1). The landscape is characterized by low mountain ranges and rolling hills, with elevations ranging from 400 to 1,400 m. The region has a cold temperate continental monsoon climate, with an average annual precipitation of 450–550 mm, most of which falls between June and August. Based on data from the local meteorological station, during the 2025 growing season (May-September), relative humidity varied between 60% and 90%. Air temperature and vapor pressure showed an initial increase followed by a decline, with air temperatures ranging from 1.2 °C to 23.9 °C (mean value 12.4 °C), and vapor pressure ranging from 0.3 kPa to 2.1 kPa (mean value 1.1 kPa) (Fig. S1a). Forests are dominated by broadleaf mixed stands, with the tree layer primarily composed of Larix gmelinii, Betula platyphylla and Pinus sylvestris var. mongolica, and the shrub layer dominated by Ledum palustre, Rhododendron spp. and Vaccinium uliginosum.

Sample collection

Sampling was conducted during the 2025 growing season (May-September) in the Kamalan River Basin of the Greater Khingan Mountains. Samples comprised leaves, xylem, soil, and precipitation collected from naturally growing Larix gmelinii and Betula platyphylla. Prior approval for sample collection was obtained from the Huzhong Forestry Bureau of the Greater Khingan Mountains. Species identification was verified by experienced forest ranger (Chen Zhiyu) and laboratory personnel (Li Biao and Dong xingfeng). All samples were archived in the Key Laboratory, School of Geographical Sciences, Harbin Normal University. All sampling procedures and subsequent analyses complied with relevant institutional and national guidelines [39]. Preliminary site selection was guided by the permafrost distribution map of Northeast China [40]. Frost probing was then conducted using a 2 m steel probe to detect the depth to the frozen layer, after which ALT was measured in the excavated soil pits using a soil profile ruler. Based on these results and the constraints of natural permafrost conditions, we selected L. gmelinii and B. platyphylla stands with the most comparable ALT values available at three depths. For L. gmelinii, ALT was approximately 60, 100, and 130 cm; for B. platyphylla, ALT was approximately 70, 100, and 140 cm. At each ALT depth for each species, one 20 m × 20 m plot was established, within which three replicate samples were collected for leaf, xylem, and soil analyses. Sampling was carried out once per month, resulting in five sampling campaigns over the growing season. In addition, in early September, we collected leaf, xylem, and soil samples down to 150 cm depth for both species at three non-permafrost (seasonally frozen) sites to characterize the water sources of vegetation growing on seasonally frozen ground.

In each plot, three healthy, morphologically representative trees were selected based on height and crown architecture to reflect typical site conditions. For each selected tree, mature, undamaged, and disease-free leaves were collected from the canopy in four cardinal directions (east, south, west, and north) using an extended pole pruner. To standardize the sampling unit, 20 g of fresh leaves was collected from each direction and pooled into one composite sample of 80 g of fresh leaves per tree. Immediately after collection, the leaf samples were sealed in clean, dry, airtight bags to preserve their physical and chemical integrity. A total of 36 leaf samples were collected, comprising three trees from each of six plots for each of the two species.

For each selected tree, branches of similar diameter and position, approximately 5–8 cm in length, were collected from the four cardinal directions. After removing the bark and phloem, the remaining xylem tissues were pooled to form one composite xylem sample per tree. The xylem samples were immediately placed in 10 mL glass vials and sealed with parafilm. A total of 36 xylem samples were collected, comprising three trees from each of six plots for each of the two species.

At each activity layer site, a 1 m × 1 m soil pit was excavated manually to the upper boundary of the permafrost layer. Soil samples were collected at 10 cm intervals (e.g., 0–10 cm, 10–20 cm) from the surface to the permafrost boundary. The number of sampled soil layers therefore varied among ALT sites and sampling dates according to the actual thaw depth. Additionally, samples from the permafrost layer were also collected. In non-permafrost plots, samples were collected to a depth of 150 cm at 10 cm intervals. For each soil layer, equal amounts of soil from the four sides of the pit wall were homogenized into one composite sample and stored in a clean, dry, sealed bag. Sampling depths and bulk density data were recorded. A total of 285 soil samples were collected (see Table S1 for details).

During the growing season, precipitation was collected after each rainfall event using collectors installed in each sampling plot. Each collector consisted of a polyethylene bottle fitted with a 100 mm diameter funnel, with a ping-pong ball placed at the funnel inlet to minimize evaporation [7, 41]. The collected water was immediately transferred into 100 mL airtight polyethylene vials and sealed for storage. A total of 55 precipitation samples were collected.

All samples were transported to the laboratory on the day of collection and stored at low temperatures prior to processing to maintain their physicochemical stability.

Sample measurements

Water was extracted from plant xylem and soil samples using a fully automated vacuum cryogenic extraction system (LI-2100, Beijing, China). The collected water was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane and transferred to 1.5 mL glass vials for measurement. The isotopic composition of hydrogen and oxygen was determined using a mass spectrometer for stable isotopes (MAT253, Bremen, Germany). Each sample was analyzed six times; to minimize the memory effects, the first two measurements were discarded, and the mean of the remaining four was used as the final isotope value. The isotopic compositions were expressed as follows:

δ = [(R Sample /R Standard) -1] ×1 000‰ (1).

The isotopic ratio R refers to the ratio of heavy to light isotopes in a given sample. Specifically, δ18O and δ2H represent the ratios of 18O/16O and 2H/1H, respectively. The reference standard for δ18O and δ2H is Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water (VSMOW), analytical precision is ± 0.2‰ for δ18O, ± 1.5‰ for δ2H.

We measured vegetation attributes and soil physicochemical properties following standard protocols. Vegetation attributes included diameter at breast height (DBH) and leaf dry matter content (LDMC). DBH was measured at 1.3 m aboveground with a diameter tape. Leaf samples were oven-dried at 60 °C to constant weight and weighed to determine LDMC. Soil properties included soil water content, temperature, total porosity, and texture. Soil water content was determined by the oven-dry method; soil temperature was recorded in situ during sampling with a field thermometer; and total porosity was measured using the undisturbed core (ring-cutter) method. Soil texture was measured using a Mastersizer 3000 (Malvern, United Kingdom) and expressed as USDA classes (clay < 2 μm, silt 2–50 μm, sand > 50 μm).

Soil water source partitioning

Soil water layers were stratified based on the degree of δ2H and δ18O enrichment in the soil profile [37]. To ensure a uniform soil-layer classification, cases in which active layer thaw depth was < 30 cm were excluded from the analysis. When 30 ≤ thaw depth < 60 cm, two layers were defined: 0–30 cm and 30-active layer or 60 cm. When 60 ≤ thaw depth < 100 cm, three layers were defined: 0–30, 30–60, and 60-active layer or 100 cm. When thaw depth ≥ 100 cm, four layers were defined: 0–30, 30–60, 60–100, and 100-active layer. At non-permafrost sites, four fixed layers were used: 0–30, 30–60, 60–100, and 100–150 cm. The 0–30 cm layer was classified as shallow soil water, the 30–60 cm layer as upper-middle soil water, the 60–100 cm layer as lower-middle soil water, and the layer below 100 cm as deep soil water.

We used the MixSIAR model to quantify the water uptake strategies of two tree species across contrasting ALT conditions. This model enables robust estimation of plant water uptake from three or more potential sources by incorporating fixed and random effects and accounting for process and residual errors [42]. It is widely regarded as a robust tool for plant water sourcing analysis [5, 17]. In our study, mean δ2H and δ18O values of xylem water from vegetation, together with the corresponding values from each potential soil water source and fractionation values (set to zero) [5], were used as inputs to the model. The Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation was performed using the “long” setting (chain length = 300,000; burn-in = 200,000; thin = 100; chains = 3), with the error structure specified as “Process only”. Model convergence was assessed prior to output interpretation using the Gelman-Rubin statistic and Geweke diagnostic.

To explore the factors influencing soil water utilization across different soil layers for two tree species, Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to assess their relationship with influencing factors. Additionally, variance partitioning analysis (VPA) was employed to identify the primary determinants of soil water utilization for each species. In VPA analysis, environmental factors are first categorized, and subsequent analyses are conducted by constraining other categories of factors to assess the contribution of each category. In this study, all variables were divided into two groups of explanatory variables. LDMC and DBH were classified as tree species attributes, whereas soil temperature, total porosity, and texture were classified as soil properties.

Water uptake plasticity

Water uptake plasticity quantifies a plant’s ability to dynamically regulate water uptake from different sources. Higher plasticity values indicate greater flexibility in water use and stronger environmental adaptability. This trait is quantified using the coefficient of variation, calculated as [22]:

Water uptake plasticity (%) = (SD/mean) ×100 (2).

where the mean represents the average proportion of water absorbed from a given source during the observation period, and the standard deviation reflects the fluctuation of this proportion.

Statistical analysis

In this study, ordinary least squares (OLS) regression analyses were conducted on the xylem water, soil water, and precipitation hydrogen and oxygen isotopes of the two tree species across different ALT. The corresponding water line equations were derived and subjected to significance testing, with a significance level set at 0.05. In addition, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and independent-samples t-tests were used to assess significant differences in water uptake proportions and water uptake plasticity, with the significance level also set at 0.05. Statistical analyses were carried out using R software (4.1.1), and all figures were generated with Origin 2018 (OriginLab). The MixSIAR model and VPA were executed using the “MixSIAR” and “vegan” packages in R software.

Results

Precipitation, xylem, and soil water stable isotopes

Precipitation δ2H and δ18O values exhibited considerable fluctuation and poor stability (Fig. S1b). The δ2H values ranged from − 188.50‰ to -49.38‰, with a mean of -120.34‰, while δ18O values ranged from − 26.27‰ to -6.07‰, with a mean of -14.51‰. Notably, isotopic values were enriched in July and more depleted in May and September. The local meteoric water line (LMWL) was derived from precipitation samples (δ2H = 6.9δ18O − 20.27, R2 = 0.94, p < 0.001). Both the slope and intercept of the LMWL were lower than those of the global meteoric water line (GMWL) (Fig. 2). The slopes and intercepts of the soil water lines for all sites were also lower than those of the LMWL. In shallower ALT (Fig. 2a and e) and non-permafrost soils (Fig. 2d and h), soil water isotopes were distributed around the LMWL. In contrast, in deeper active layers (Fig. 2c and g), soil water (at depths of 60–130 cm or 140 cm) gradually deviated from the LMWL. SWL slopes differed significantly among different ALT conditions (p < 0.05; Fig. S2). Under similar ALT conditions, the slope of the soil water line (SWL) for L. gmelinii was slightly higher than that for B. platyphylla. The slope and intercept of the plant water line (PWL) were both lower than those of the LMWL.

Xylem water δ2H and δ18O of the two tree species exhibited pronounced temporal variation across different ALT conditions during the growing season. For both species, isotopic values increased and then declined, with maximum enrichment in July (Fig. 3). When ALT was deeper, xylem water δ2H and δ18O were relatively enriched. At similar ALT conditions, xylem δ2H and δ18O in B. platyphylla were consistently more enriched than in L. gmelinii. Under non-permafrost conditions, xylem water of both species was further depleted (Fig. 3). Across different ALT, the SWC of L. gmelinii and B. platyphylla generally followed a pattern of decreasing and then stabilizing over the course of the growing season, with the lowest values observed in July (Fig. S3a, c). SWC generally remained relatively stable along the soil profile, with an increase near the permafrost boundary. Sites with shallower ALT exhibited higher SWC (ranging from 36.59% to 64.16%). However, under non-permafrost conditions, SWC for both species decreased significantly (Fig. S3b, d). Across all ALT conditions, soil water δ2H and δ18O in both species rose with month and then decreased. The most enriched values occurred in July and August. Soil water δ2H and δ18O generally became progressively more depleted with depth (Figs. 4 and 5). Both species exhibited more enriched soil water δ2H and δ18O under deeper than under shallower ALT. At similar ALT conditions, soil water isotopes in B. platyphylla stands were generally more enriched than those in L. gmelinii stands.

Vegetation water sources

The MixSIAR results showed that, during the growing season, both L. gmelinii and B. platyphylla exhibited a progressive downward shift in their principal water uptake zones over time (Fig. 6). Under shallow ALT conditions (60–70 cm), both species relied primarily on shallow soil water (0–30 cm), with no significant difference between them (p > 0.05; Fig. S4a). The contribution of shallow soil water ranged from 63.2% to 80.8% for L. gmelinii and from 58.1% to 84.5% for B. platyphylla. When ALT reached approximately 100 cm, the principal water uptake layers differed significantly between the two species (p < 0.05; Fig. S4b). L. gmelinii continued to rely mainly on shallow soil water, with contribution rates of 37.5%-57.8%, whereas B. platyphylla shifted its primary water uptake to upper-middle soil water (30–60 cm), with contribution rates of 41.9%-47.3%. Under deeper ALT conditions, the principal water uptake layers also differed between the two species: L. gmelinii mainly used soil water from the 0–60 cm layer, with an average contribution of approximately 58.2%, whereas B. platyphylla mainly used soil water from the 60–140 cm layer, with an average contribution of approximately 57.2%. Under non-permafrost conditions, both species accessed deeper soil water (60–150 cm), with similar contribution rates of 55.5%-68.8% (Fig. 6d, h). Collectively, these results indicate that both species adjusted their water uptake depth under deeper ALT conditions, while B. platyphylla showed a more rapid shift toward deeper soil water and a greater capacity to use deeper soil water.

Water uptake plasticity

Given the observed shifts in dominant water uptake depth across different ALT, we employed a water uptake plasticity index to quantify the ability of L. gmelinii and B. platyphylla to adjust their water sources vertically within the soil profile. Water uptake plasticity varied significantly under different ALT conditions (p < 0.05; Fig. 7). Both species exhibited higher water uptake plasticity under deeper ALT and non-permafrost conditions, indicating a stronger ability to adjust their water uptake strategies in response to soil moisture dynamics and an enhanced capacity to cope with environmental change. Notably, under similar ALT, B. platyphylla exhibited a slightly higher mean water uptake plasticity than L. gmelinii.

Factors affecting vegetation water uptake from soil layers

To identify the factors controlling the proportional use of soil water (0–100 cm) by the two tree species, we calculated Pearson correlations between the water-use proportion at each soil depth and both soil properties and tree attributes (Table 1). In the 0–30 cm layer, water-use proportions of both species were strongly and negatively correlated with soil temperature, sand content and DBH (p < 0.01), while showing positive correlations with the other variables, although the significance levels varied. In the 30–60 cm layer, water-use proportions exhibited stronger and more significant correlations with soil texture and were highly positively correlated with LDMC (p < 0.01). In the 60–100 cm layer, correlations with soil and tree attributes were more heterogeneous. These results indicate that both soil properties and species traits may influence plant water use, but the dominant set of controlling factors remains uncertain.

Discussion

Isotopic composition

Precipitation δ2H and δ18O exhibited large temporal fluctuations, with an overall pattern of enrichment followed by depletion. This pattern is most likely driven by evaporative effects during rainfall events [43, 44]. During the mid-growing season (June-August), higher air temperatures enhance sub-cloud evaporation, promoting evaporation of falling raindrops and consequently enriching precipitation in heavy isotopes. Toward the end of the growing season (August-September), declining temperatures weaken sub-cloud evaporation, leading to more isotopically depleted precipitation. Similar unstable and seasonally coherent isotope dynamics have been reported for precipitation in the permafrost region of northeastern China, where δ2H and δ18O exhibit a significant positive correlation with air temperature, increasing by 4.96‰ and 0.66‰, respectively, per 1 °C rise [45]. The LMWL integrates the cumulative effects of evaporation, atmospheric moisture transport, condensation, and precipitation, and thus provides a fundamental baseline for interpreting the spatiotemporal variability of stable isotopes in atmospheric inputs [46]. The lower slope and intercept of the LMWL relative to the GMWL suggest that precipitation in the study area experienced secondary evaporation during rainfall [5].

Across the ALT gradient, δ2H and δ18O in soil water beneath both species showed a consistent seasonal pattern, with enrichment from early to mid-growing season and depletion toward its end (Figs. 4 and 5). This seasonal trajectory primarily reflects the interplay between infiltration of isotopically variable precipitation and evaporative fractionation within the soil profile [44, 47]. The distribution of soil water isotopes around the LMWL further suggests that soil water was mainly derived from precipitation [37], which is generally the dominant control on soil water isotopic [48]. During the mid-growing season (July-August), isotopically enriched rainfall infiltrates into the surface soil, driving enrichment of soil water. Meanwhile, elevated air temperatures enhance evaporation of lighter water molecules (1H216O) from the soil surface, further amplifying this enrichment signal. These effects are most pronounced in the shallow and mid-depth layers, whereas deeper soil water is more strongly influenced by meltwater derived from thawing permafrost. Under similar ALT, soil water isotopes beneath B. platyphylla were more enriched than those beneath L. gmelinii. This pattern may be closely linked to differences in the thickness of the surface humus layer. In our study area, coniferous forests typically develop a thicker humus layer than broadleaved forests [23]. A thicker humus layer reduces direct exposure of the soil to the air, effectively suppresses soil water evaporation, and enhances soil water retention. Therefore, evaporative losses are stronger in B. platyphylla, resulting in relatively greater enrichment of soil water isotopes. The SWL provides an additional diagnostic of evaporative modification, as its slope is inversely related to the intensity of soil evaporation, with smaller slopes indicating stronger evaporative effects [49]. Along the ALT gradient, both species exhibited lower SWL slopes under deeper active layers than under shallower ones, indicating that soil evaporation is stronger where the active layer is deeper. Under similar ALT, SWL slopes were slightly higher in L. gmelinii stands than in B. platyphylla stands, further supporting the inference that evaporative enrichment is more pronounced in B. platyphylla. The isotopic composition of xylem water largely reflects that of source soil water, its seasonal variability reveals shifts in plant water uptake among different depths over the course of the growing season [50].

Water sources of the two tree species

The depth of water uptake by L. gmelinii and B. platyphylla varied markedly with both ALT and seasonal progression. At the beginning of the growing season, the thaw depth of the active layer was shallow and increased slowly, yet soil moisture remained relatively high, likely due to inputs from snowmelt and precipitation, combined with low transpiration demand during leaf-out. During this period, shallow soil water (0–30 cm) constituted the primary source for both species, with a contribution rate of 61.7–84.5%. As the active layer deepened, both the availability and vertical distribution of soil water changed substantially, leading to a downward shift in water uptake depth under all active-layer conditions. When the ALT was approximately 60 cm and 100 cm, L. gmelinii relies predominantly on shallow soil water. However, when ALT exceeded 100 cm, L. gmelinii displayed a different water-use strategy, shifting its main water sources from shallow soil water (0–30 cm) to mid-upper (30–60 cm). Studies in the Siberian permafrost region have shown that although approximately 80% of the fine roots of L. gmelinii are concentrated in the upper 0–30 cm, these fine roots have limited absorptive capacity, whereas roots at 30–70 cm function as the principal organs for water acquisition under drought conditions [51]. Consistently, Churakova-Sidorova, et al. [52] reported that L. gmelinii mainly extracts water from the 0–50 cm soil layer. These studies corroborate the patterns of water uptake depth observed in our study. In contrast, when the ALT reached roughly 100 cm, the primary uptake depth of B. platyphylla shifted, upper-middle soil water (30–60 cm) became the main source in August and September. When ALT exceeded 100 cm, B. platyphylla displayed a more flexible water-use strategy. Rao, et al. [53] demonstrated that B. platyphylla can access water over a wide depth range (down to 80–150 cm) during cold and dry periods, whereas under warm and wet conditions it relies predominantly on water from 60 to 80 cm, which can satisfy more than 70% of its water demand. This evidence indicates that B. platyphylla accesses deeper soil water earlier and responds more rapidly to the changes of soil moisture.

Our results showed that water uptake plasticity was higher for both species under deeper active layer and non-permafrost conditions, implying that they are better able to adjust their water-use patterns in response to changes in soil water availability. Higher values of this index indicate a greater capacity to shift the main uptake depth and thus stronger adaptive potential to environmental variation [19]. Under similar ALT, B. platyphylla exhibited a slightly higher mean water uptake plasticity than L. gmelinii, suggesting a stronger capacity for environmental adaptation.

Factors influencing tree water uptake from different soil depths

Both species attributes and soil properties significantly influence the proportional use of water from different soil layers by L. gmelinii and B. platyphylla. In the shallow (0–30 cm) layer, infiltration of rainfall, snowmelt, permafrost thaw, and surface runoff jointly drive high-frequency fluctuations in SWC, resulting in short residence times of liquid water and pronounced spatiotemporal heterogeneity. Under such conditions, soil texture and porosity regulate infiltration, water retention, and aeration, thereby determining whether shallow soil water acts as a short-lived pulse that is rapidly depleted or as a buffered reservoir with longer residence time [54]. Soil temperature modulates water phase and evaporative demand, large temperature fluctuations near the surface accelerate liquid water loss and alter root activity and distribution patterns [55, 56]. Therefore, although the roots of both species can readily penetrate the shallow soil, the extent to which water can be retained in the rhizosphere and sustain an effective hydraulic gradient is primarily constrained by soil properties, whereas species attributes regulate how efficiently this water resource is exploited. Consequently, the proportion of shallow soil water used by the two species is shaped by the interaction between species attributes and soil properties.

In the 30–60 cm layer, the proportions of water taken up by both species were mainly controlled by species attributes such as LDMC and DBH. LDMC can be considered an influential factor affecting vegetation water use because it is an important leaf functional trait reflecting leaf structural investment, resource-use strategy, and drought-adaptive capacity. LDMC is closely associated with plant adaptation to drought or water stress, as some tree species have been reported to cope with drought by increasing LDMC and leaf mass per area [34]. Under arid or water-limited conditions, plants often exhibit greater leaf thickness and higher LDMC, traits that may contribute to reduced water loss and enhanced drought tolerance [57]. Likewise, larger DBH is typically associated with greater transpiration demand and higher water requirements [37]. This upper-middle layer is situated in the central part of the active layer, where soil moisture and temperature are relatively stable and spatial heterogeneity in texture and porosity is weaker at the plot scale. Accordingly, species attributes become the dominant factors governing plant water uptake from this layer.

In the 60–100 cm layer, soil temperature and texture primarily determine whether water is present in a usable liquid, hydraulically connected form and whether deep roots can access it [28]. Consequently, species effects in this depth interval are highly related to soil context and are expressed mainly as a pronounced interaction between species attributes and soil properties. Notably, the interaction term was stronger for B. platyphylla than for L. gmelinii, indicating that B. platyphylla responds more sensitively and rapidly to changes in soil temperature and water availability.

Effects of permafrost degradation on vegetation growth

In recent years, climate warming has markedly increased atmospheric evaporative demand for transpiration, heightening the sensitivity of vegetation growth to water availability [58–60]. Blume-Werry, et al. [8] showed that permafrost degradation can strongly promote root growth and vertical redistribution. Our results revealed that, as the active layer thickened, roots of both L. gmelinii and B. platyphylla extended into deeper soil, accessing previously unavailable water and nutrient pools and exhibiting higher water-uptake plasticity. Deepening of the active layer can facilitate tree growth and enhance the capacity of both species to adjust to changing soil water availability [28]. In low-lying, poorly drained sites with relatively shallow active layers, permafrost degradation is often accompanied by extensive ground-ice melt and surface ponding, which can drive a transition from forest or shrubland to wetland and severely reduce aeration in the root zone [28, 61, 62]. Because most boreal tree species lack strong adaptations to prolonged anoxic conditions, such environments can lead to pronounced growth decline and even tree mortality [63]. In areas with a relatively deep active layer, forests are generally more resilient to thaw-related hydrological disturbances. B. platyphylla can rapidly utilize deeper soil water and flexibly adjust its uptake depth, and is widely recognized as a fast-growing pioneer species that responds rapidly to changes in soil temperature and water availability. By contrast, L. gmelinii maintains a more conservative resource-use strategy, relies heavily on shallow soil water, and shows a delayed response in developing and using deep roots. In mixed stands, the two species compete for water, and as soil moisture becomes increasingly limiting, B. platyphylla is likely to gain a competitive advantage. Bai, et al. [64] further highlighted that under severe permafrost degradation, L. gmelinii faces considerable environmental pressure due to warming, potentially leading to its local extinction in China. Qi, et al. [65] similarly demonstrated that B. platyphylla has a greater growth advantage under permafrost degradation. Taken together, these findings indicate that permafrost degradation, through its effects on ALT and associated hydrothermal processes, impacts water uptake strategies of trees in the permafrost regions.

This study has several limitations. First, ALT conditions were not perfectly matched between the two species, which may have slightly overestimated the reliance of B. platyphylla on deep soil water. Second, the plasticity results should be interpreted as preliminary and relative indicators of water uptake variation. Future studies should incorporate complementary metrics, such as Shannon diversity or the effective number of water sources, to better characterize plant water uptake plasticity. Third, interspecific competition was not directly measured, and the number of sampling sites was relatively limited. Thus, the inferred greater potential of B. platyphylla to compete for deep soil water under permafrost degradation should be validated through broader spatial sampling and direct measurements of interspecific competition.

Conclusion

We investigated the seasonal dynamics and drivers of water uptake depth in Larix gmelinii and Betula platyphylla in Northeast China. L. gmelinii exhibited a more conservative resource-use strategy, relying primarily on shallow soil water, whereas B. platyphylla exhibited greater flexibility in shifting its dominant water uptake layer and an earlier capacity to access deeper soil water. In addition, as the active layer deepened, both species showed enhanced adaptability to environmental change through greater plasticity in water uptake. Overall, our results suggest that, under climate warming and progressive permafrost degradation, B. platyphylla may gradually gain an advantage in water competition. However, given the limited number of vegetation sampling plots under each ALT condition, caution is needed when extrapolating these findings to broader permafrost regions. These findings provide valuable insights into the relationship between vegetation growth and climate change in permafrost regions.
