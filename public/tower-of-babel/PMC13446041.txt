Germinated indica rice flour modified using five anionic polysaccharides: Analysis of physicochemical properties and multiscale structural characterization
Authors: Shutong Liu, Xiuqi Wang, Hongyue Quan, Xinyi Bai, Yutang He, Youhong Liu, Ao Tang, Guangchen Zhang, He Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although germination improves the nutritional and functional potential of indica rice, germinated indica rice flour is susceptible to retrogradation and structural instability. This study compared five food-grade anionic polysaccharides with different structural and functional profiles—namely, soybean hull polysaccharide (SHP), soluble soybean polysaccharide, κ-carrageenan (CG), low-methoxyl pectin, and gum arabic—to identify suitable modifiers for retrogradation inhibition and texture regulation in this flour. SHP yielded the lowest setback viscosity (228.33 vs. 358.67 mPa·s), thus providing the strongest short-term retrogradation inhibition. Meanwhile, CG produced the greatest hardness (218.09 g) and a more ordered, interconnected framework. Analyses of water mobility, short-range order, crystallinity, thermal behavior, and microstructure indicated that SHP promoted water redistribution and disrupted ordered starch packing, whereas CG reinforced gel network formation. Among the five polysaccharides, SHP and CG showed potential for retrogradation control and gel strengthening, respectively. These findings may guide polysaccharide selection for germinated indica rice flour-based products.

Introduction

Indica rice (Oryza sativa L. subsp. indica), one of the most widely cultivated rice subspecies in the world, is recognized for its significant contribution to global rice production due to its high yield potential (Eragam et al., 2022). However, conventional processing strategies largely involve refining, which removes the bran and germ layers, resulting in the loss of dietary fiber, vitamins, and other functional components (Fei et al., 2023). With increasing awareness of health and nutrition among consumers, as well as growing concerns regarding nutrient depletion, germinated whole grains have garnered considerable interest among researchers. These products provide nutritional advantages owing to the preservation of the intrinsic structure of grains and germination-induced biochemical alterations such as enhanced endogenous enzymatic activity, reduced levels of anti-nutritional factors, and the accumulation of bioactive compounds such as γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) (Wang et al., 2025). During rice germination, endogenous enzymes, especially α-amylase, may partially hydrolyze starch and alter its structural and functional properties, thereby affecting the crystalline features, gelatinization behavior, and pasting properties of rice flour systems (Luo et al., 2022).

Indica rice varieties typically show a high amylose content, and even after germination, their flour may still exhibit thermodynamic instability, weak gel-forming capacity, and a pronounced tendency of retrogradation during storage (Corrado et al., 2023). These physicochemical limitations are closely associated with undesirable textural changes in final products, including hardening, syneresis, and microstructural deterioration (Denchai et al., 2019). The broader utilization of germinated indica rice flour in rice flour-based products is hindered by these unresolved structural and functional limitations, underscoring the need for additional food-grade modification strategies beyond germination. This is particularly relevant for flour-based products such as rice gels, rice noodles, and gluten-free rice-based foods, wherein texture stability, water retention, and storage quality are critical.

To address these limitations and mitigate quality deterioration, various modification approaches have been developed, including chemical modification (Lee et al., 2024), physical treatments (Peressini et al., 2021), enzymatic hydrolysis (Ohmoto et al., 2024), and composite modification via food hydrocolloids, particularly polysaccharides (Liu et al., 2023). Although chemical modifications are effective in altering starch functionality, their application is often limited by potential food safety and regulatory concerns. Furthermore, physical and enzymatic treatments, although effective, often require specialized equipment and increase production costs (Juarez-Arellano et al., 2021). In contrast, polysaccharides are recognized as food-grade, sustainable additives and are widely used in the food industry, owing to their ability to retard starch retrogradation, modulate texture, and extend shelf life through favorable physicochemical interactions.

Polysaccharides can be broadly classified into cationic, neutral, and anionic groups based on their charge properties (Kim et al., 2025). Among these, anionic polysaccharides have attracted special attention due to the presence of negatively charged functional groups, which confer pronounced hydrophilicity. These polysaccharides are capable of interacting with starch chains and coexisting protein components through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic forces, thereby modulating gelatinization and retrogradation behavior. In light of these molecular interactions, anionic polysaccharides have been extensively utilized as modifying agents in cereal-based systems, with emphasis on their ability to inhibit starch retrogradation and improve physicochemical properties (Gong et al., 2024). For instance, gum arabic (AG) has been reported to regulate the pasting, rheological, structural, and hydrolysis properties of kudzu starch systems (Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2023). Meanwhile, soluble soybean polysaccharide (SP) can influence the gelatinization and retrogradation behavior of corn starches with different amylose contents (Wang et al., 2024). In addition, κ-carrageenan (CG) is recognized for its strong gel-forming capacity (Cheng et al., 2025), while low-methoxyl pectin (LP) and soy hull polysaccharide (SHP) have also demonstrated considerable potential in the modification of starch-based systems (Qu et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2022). More recently, hydrocolloid modification has also been found to improve the pasting, rheological, and microstructural properties of germinated highland barley starch gels, further highlighting the potential value of hydrocolloids in germinated cereal starch systems (Pan et al., 2024). However, most existing studies have examined the effects of individual hydrocolloids in purified starch or relatively simplified starch-gel systems. However, germinated indica rice flour represents a more complex whole-flour matrix in which non-starch constituents may influence polysaccharide functionality. Therefore, the similarities and differences in the regulatory effects of different anionic polysaccharides in this system remain unclear.

Based on the reported characteristics and practical relevance of the abovementioned food-grade hydrocolloids, i.e., SHP, SP, CG, LP, and AG, their effects on germinated indica rice flour were systematically investigated and compared. A fixed addition level of 0.5% (w/v) was adopted based on preliminary screening assays. The polysaccharides were incorporated into germinated indica rice flour systems by preparing flour suspensions using the corresponding aqueous polysaccharide solutions prior to co-gelatinization treatment. Subsequently, to compare the regulatory effects of these five anionic polysaccharides on germinated indica rice flour, the pasting, rheological, and textural properties of the resulting flour–polysaccharide systems were comprehensively evaluated. We focused on changes in water mobility, starch chain rearrangement, and gel network formation during co-gelatinization and gel development. Specifically, low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were employed to elucidate polysaccharide-induced changes in water mobility, crystalline structure, thermal stability, and microstructure. The findings of this study are expected to provide a theoretical foundation for the industrial application of anionic polysaccharides in the texture modification of food products. Overall, these findings could provide practical guidance for the rational selection of polysaccharides and quality stabilization of indica rice flour-based products. Further, they could help in the development and industrial production of novel products of this kind, including rice gels, rice noodles, and gluten-free rice-based foods, with improved texture and storage stability.

Materials and methods

Materials

Indica rice was procured from local farmers in Yangjiang City, Guangdong Province, China, and subsequently stored in airtight containers at 4 °C prior to use. SHP was extracted and purified from soybean hulls using a microwave-assisted ammonium oxalate extraction method based on the protocol established in our previous study (Li et al., 2022). Briefly, soybean hull powder was treated with ammonium oxalate solution, followed by centrifugation, ethanol precipitation, dialysis, and freeze-drying, to obtain purified SHP. Characterization of the obtained SHP showed that it was a soybean hull-derived acidic polysaccharide mainly composed of galacturonic acid, arabinose, mannose, and galactose. SP, CG, LP, and AG were purchased from Henan Ren'an Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Henan, China). The specifications and physicochemical characteristics of the polysaccharides are provided in Supplementary Table S1. All other chemicals and reagents used in this study were of analytical grade.

Preparation of germinated indica rice flour–polysaccharide systems

Complete and visibly undamaged indica rice grains were selected and soaked in 1% H2O2 for 5 min to enable surface disinfection. The grains were then thoroughly rinsed several times with deionized water to remove residual H2O2 and soaked in deionized water at 4 °C for 4 h. The soaked grains were placed in Petri dishes, mixed with an appropriate amount of deionized water, and covered with moistened gauze to maintain a humid microenvironment during germination. The samples were germinated in an incubator at 30 °C for 24 h. During this period, the moisture status of the gauze and the incubator temperature were monitored to maintain stable germination conditions. After germination, the samples were dried in a forced-air oven at 50 °C for 24 h, ground, and passed through a 100-mesh sieve to obtain germinated indica rice flour. The basic composition of the obtained germinated indica rice flour, including its total starch content, apparent amylose content, crude protein content, crude lipid content, and moisture content, was determined (Supplementary Table S2).

Based on the preliminary concentration-screening results, a polysaccharide addition level of 0.5% (w/v) was selected for this study. Polysaccharide solutions of SHP, SP, CG, LP, and AG (0.5% [w/v]) were prepared using deionized water. These solutions were subsequently used as the liquid phase to prepare germinated indica rice flour–polysaccharide systems for physicochemical analyses. The control sample prepared using deionized water was coded as CK, whereas the samples prepared using SHP, SP, CG, LP, and AG solutions were coded as SHP, SP, CG, LP, and AG, respectively. The same sample codes are used consistently herein, including in figures and tables.

Evaluation of pasting properties

The pasting properties of the samples were determined using a Rapid Visco Analyzer (RVA-4500, Perten, Australia). A suspension was prepared by weighing 3.0 g of germinated indica rice flour into an RVA canister, followed by 27.0 g of deionized water (control group; CK) or the corresponding polysaccharide solution, which acted as the liquid phase. The mixtures were manually homogenized using a plastic paddle until no visible dry powder or aggregates remained. The RVA measurement was conducted under the following conditions: initial holding at 50 °C for 60 s with a paddle speed of 960 rpm for dispersion, followed by a constant speed of 160 rpm for the remainder of the test. The temperature program involved heating to 95 °C within 225 s, holding at 95 °C for 150 s, cooling to 50 °C within 225 s, and a final holding period at 50 °C for 34 s. The following parameters were examined based on the pasting curves: peak viscosity (PV), trough viscosity (TV), breakdown viscosity (BD), final viscosity (FV), and setback viscosity (SV).

The gelatinized pastes obtained after RVA treatment were used as the starting material for various analyses. Freshly gelatinized pastes were used immediately for rheological analysis and LF-NMR measurements. For texture analysis, the gelatinized pastes were stored at 4 °C for 24 h to allow gel formation. For FT-IR, XRD, DSC, TGA, and SEM analyses, freshly gelatinized pastes were immediately freeze-dried, ground into powders, and stored in sealed containers until analysis. Except for thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), all analytical measurements were conducted in triplicate (per treatment group). Meanwhile, TGA was conducted once for each treatment group to obtain representative thermal degradation profiles.

Rheological analysis

The rheological properties of the gelatinized samples were determined using a dynamic rheometer (DHR-1, TA Instruments Ltd., USA) with a parallel-plate geometry (35-mm diameter). The gap size was set to 1 mm, and all measurements were conducted at 25 °C. Dynamic frequency sweep tests were performed at a fixed strain of 0.1% (within the linear viscoelastic region) over an angular frequency range of 0.1–100 rad/s to obtain the storage modulus (G'), loss modulus (G"), and loss tangent (tan δ). Additionally, steady shear measurements were conducted over a shear rate range of 0.1–100 s−1 to determine the apparent viscosity.

Evaluation of texture properties

The gelatinized samples obtained after RVA analysis were stored at 4 °C for 24 h to allow gel formation. The gels were then removed from the canisters and placed directly on the platform of a texture analyzer (TA.XT Plus, Stable Micro Systems Ltd., Godalming, UK). Texture profile analysis was performed using a P/0.5 cylindrical probe. The measurements were conducted at a test speed of 1.0 mm/s, with a trigger force of 1.0 g and a compression strain of 50%. Textural parameters, including hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, gumminess, and chewiness, were calculated from the TPA force–time curves. Gel strength was evaluated separately using a puncture test with a TA/0.5 probe. The test conditions were as follows: a speed of 2.0 mm/s, a puncture distance of 10 mm, and an interval time of 5 s. The maximum force recorded during puncture was defined as the gel strength.

LF-NMR spectroscopy

Water distribution in the gelatinized samples was analyzed using LF-NMR. Measurements were performed using an LF-NMR analyzer (Suzhou Niumai Analytical Instruments Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) with the Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) pulse sequence. Gelatinized pastes were transferred into NMR tubes and analyzed immediately. The transverse relaxation time (T2) was recorded to evaluate water mobility and distribution. The pulse parameters were as follows: 60 mm probe, 1500 ms waiting time, 250 kHz sampling frequency, 0.002 ms RF delay, 4 accumulations, 1.0 ms echo time, 500 ms repetition time, and 256 scan repetitions.

FT-IR

FT-IR spectra were recorded using a spectrometer (Nicolet iS5, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Freeze-dried sample powders were mixed with potassium bromide (KBr) at a ratio of 1:50 (w/w), thoroughly ground, and pressed into pellets under a pressure of 10 MPa. Spectra were collected over the range of 400–4000 cm−1 via 32 scans at a resolution of 4 cm−1.

XRD analysis

The crystalline structures of the samples were analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku Ultima IV, Japan) equipped with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.54178 Å). The instrument was operated at 40 kV and 40 mA. Diffraction patterns were collected in continuous scanning mode over a 2θ range of 10°–60°, with a scanning speed of 10°/min and a step size of 0.033°. The relative crystallinity (RC) was calculated with MDI Jade 6.0 software following baseline correction and peak deconvolution using the following equation:RC%=AcAc+Aa×100Here, Ac and Aa represent the crystalline and amorphous regions, respectively.

DSC analysis

The thermal properties were analyzed using a differential scanning calorimeter (Q20, TA Instruments, USA). Freeze-dried samples were mixed with distilled water at a ratio of 1:3 (w/w) in hermetically sealed aluminum pans. The samples were heated from 30 °C to 120 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min, using an empty sealed pan as the reference. The onset (To), peak (Tp), and conclusion (Tc) temperatures, as well as the gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH), were calculated from the thermograms.

Thermal stability analysis

The thermal stability of the freeze-dried samples was evaluated using TGA (PerkinElmer STA8000, USA). Approximately 10 mg of each sample was weighed into an alumina crucible. Measurements were conducted over a temperature range of 30–600 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen flow of 20 mL/min. Weight loss and derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) curves were recorded to characterize the thermal degradation behavior.

SEM

The surface morphology of the samples was examined using a field emission scanning electron microscope (S-4800, Hitachi, Japan). Freeze-dried samples were mounted on aluminum stubs using double-sided conductive tape and sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold. Imaging was performed at an accelerating voltage of 1.0 kV under magnifications of 1000× and 3000×.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data obtained from triplicate measurements are expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD). No statistical comparisons were made for the TGA data because TGA was only used to obtain representative thermal degradation profiles. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan's multiple range test, was applied to evaluate significant differences among different groups. Before one-way ANOVA, the distribution of replicate values and variance dispersion among treatment groups were checked to evaluate the suitability of the data for parametric analysis. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Graphs were generated using Origin 2025 software (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA). A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Pasting properties

The analysis of pasting properties demonstrated that the incorporation of different anionic polysaccharides significantly altered the viscosity profiles of germinated indica rice flour and also affected other related parameters (Table 1; Fig. 1). Notably, these changes were interpreted in relation to germination-induced alterations in starch structure. Statistical comparisons of the extracted RVA parameters are provided in Table 1. PV represents the maximum viscosity attained by a starch paste during heating. Comparisons with control samples showed that CG and LP significantly increased the PV of germinated indica rice flour (P < 0.05), with CG providing the highest value (604.00 mPa·s), approximately 42% higher than the control. This enhancement may be associated with the thickening and network-forming properties of carrageenan, which could increase the viscosity of the continuous phase and contribute to apparent system viscosity (Krystyjan et al., 2013). A similar viscosity-promoting effect was observed with LP, suggesting that network-forming polysaccharides enhance paste thickening via polymer–starch interactions (Zhai et al., 2021). In contrast, SHP, SP, and AG all significantly reduced PV, with SHP yielding the lowest value (240.67 mPa·s). The BD values further highlighted the differences in shear stability among the various polysaccharide-treated systems. The BD of the CG group (253.33 mPa·s) was higher than that of the control group (239.67 mPa·s), likely due to the increased system viscosity, which enhanced shear stress during heating and rendered swollen granules more susceptible to mechanical disruption. Conversely, SHP, SP, LP, and AG provided significantly lower BD values than the control (P < 0.05), suggesting that restricted granule swelling reduced the susceptibility of the rice flour systems to shear-induced disintegration. In these systems, the hydration of polysaccharides likely limited water availability for starch granules, thereby moderating granule swelling and enhancing apparent shear stability (Zhang, Dou, et al., 2023).

The SV reflected differences in short-term viscosity recovery and retrogradation tendency among the treated germinated indica rice flour systems. Significantly lower SV values for the SHP, SP, LP, and AG groups (P < 0.05) indicated that these polysaccharides restricted amylose re-association during cooling. The strongest inhibition was observed in the SHP group, likely due to the high water-binding capacity of this polysaccharide, which limited starch chain mobility and suppressed the formation of ordered structures. Conversely, CG exhibited a higher SV (399.00 mPa·s), suggesting that CG network development and carrageenan–starch interactions may have mediated the reassociation of leached starch chains during cooling (Techawipharat et al., 2008). Collectively, the findings showed that anionic polysaccharides modulated the pasting behavior of germinated indica rice flour through two contrasting pathways. Network-forming polysaccharides such as CG increased apparent viscosity and may have favored chain reorganization during cooling, whereas highly hydrophilic polysaccharides such as SHP restricted starch hydration and molecular mobility, thereby suppressing short-term retrogradation.

Rheological properties

Dynamic rheological measurements were conducted to characterize the viscoelastic behavior of germinated indica rice pastes containing different anionic polysaccharides. As shown in Fig. 2a,b, both G′ and G″ exhibited clear frequency dependence across all samples. G′ remained higher than G″ throughout the frequency range, revealing the elastic-dominant behavior characteristic of gel-like systems. Compared with the control samples, those treated with polysaccharides generally showed higher dynamic moduli, which suggested enhanced network strength. The CG-treated sample exhibited the highest G′ and G″ values, reflecting the formation of a more rigid gel network. This behavior may be associated with the thickening and network-forming properties of CG, leading to the development of a reinforced composite network and improving the resistance of pastes to deformation (Lin et al., 2016). In contrast, although the SHP-treated system exhibited higher moduli than the control system, its values remained the lowest among all polysaccharide-treated groups. This milder reinforcement may be related to the hydration behavior of SHP, which potentially reduced water availability during gelatinization and limited the development of a highly interconnected gel network (Huang et al., 2024).Fig. 2Rheological properties of germinated indica rice pastes treated with different anionic polysaccharides: (a) Storage modulus (G′); (b) Loss modulus (G″); (c) Loss tangent (tan δ); and (d) Apparent viscosity.Fig. 2

Rheological properties of germinated indica rice pastes treated with different anionic polysaccharides: (a) Storage modulus (G′); (b) Loss modulus (G″); (c) Loss tangent (tan δ); and (d) Apparent viscosity.

The loss tangent (tan δ), defined as the ratio of G″ to G′, was used to evaluate the viscoelastic behavior of the different samples. Tan δ values (Fig. 2c) remained below 1.0 in all samples, confirming their weak gel characteristics. The lowest tan δ was observed in the CG-treated system, reflecting a greater elastic contribution and a more stable gel structure. The SHP-treated system exhibited a lower tan δ than the control, suggesting a shift toward elastic-dominant behavior, although the magnitude of network reinforcement remained lower than that of the CG-treated system. Given the RVA results, the high G′ and low tan δ of CG were consistent with its gel-strengthening behavior, whereas the lower SV and moderate modulus increase in SHP suggested the formation of a softer gel network due to retrogradation control.

The apparent viscosity of the systems decreased as the shear rate increased (Fig. 2d), reflecting the shear-thinning behavior typical of non-Newtonian fluids. Consistent with the dynamic moduli values, all polysaccharide-treated samples exhibited higher apparent viscosity than the control. The CG-treated system showed a pronounced increase in viscosity at low shear rates, indicating enhanced structural consistency and resistance to flow. Conversely, the SHP-treated system demonstrated a more moderate increase. This behavior suggested that SHP improved paste stability while maintaining better fluidity.

Texture properties

To complement the rheological findings, the texture properties of germinated indica rice pastes treated with different polysaccharides were also evaluated (Table 2). Polysaccharide addition induced significant changes in the hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, gumminess, chewiness, and gel strength of the pastes. The CG-treated system recorded the highest values for most texture parameters, corresponding to the higher G′ and lower tan δ observed during rheological analysis. These findings indicated the formation of a more compact and mechanically stable gel network. This reinforcement may be associated with the gel-forming ability of CG and its contribution to the formation of a composite starch–polysaccharide network (Cheng et al., 2022). LP-treated samples also showed significantly higher hardness (154.69 g) and chewiness (91.75 g) than control samples (P < 0.05), suggesting that pectin contributed to network formation through polymer–starch interactions (Xie et al., 2024). However, compared with CG, LP produced lower hardness, gumminess, chewiness, and gel strength, indicating the formation of a reinforced but less rigid network. Both SP and AG yielded improved texture parameters, particularly in terms of gumminess and gel strength. In comparison, the SHP group exhibited the lowest values across all texture parameters, with hardness (81.70 g) and chewiness (23.67 g) values significantly lower than those of the control (P < 0.05). Such reductions reflected the formation of a softer and less rigid gel structure, which could be related to reduced network density and the hydration-mediated dilution of the starch matrix (Chen et al., 2023). These findings can be explained by the role of SHP in retrogradation control and the reduction of excessive hardening instead of strong gel-network formation. Overall, the texture variations revealed two pathways of texture modification by anionic polysaccharides. Specifically, CG enhanced gel compactness and rigidity, whereas SHP induced network plasticization via hydration-mediated dilution of the starch matrix. Water distribution and mobility

LF-NMR was employed to characterize water distribution and mobility in germinated indica rice pastes. In LF-NMR analysis, shorter T2 relaxation times generally reflect the presence of water with restricted mobility, whereas longer T2 relaxation times represent the presence of less restricted and more mobile water populations. As shown in Fig. 3 and Table S3, variations in relaxation behavior were observed among the anionic polysaccharide-treated groups, reflecting the distinct modes of water regulation within the systems. Single-peak distributions were observed in the CK, SP, LP, and AG groups, whereas bimodal distributions appeared in the SHP and CG groups. This suggested that the latter two treatments generated a more complex water environment. Short-relaxation peaks were detected in both the SHP and CG groups. The T21 and P21 values were 19.73 ms and 0.70% for SHP, and 35.71 ms and 1.77% for CG, respectively, indicating that both treatments generated an additional restricted water fraction, albeit through different regulatory patterns.Fig. 3T2 relaxation time distribution curves of germinated indica rice pastes treated with different anionic polysaccharides. Among the different groups, CG had the highest dominant T2 value, followed by CK, SP, AG, SHP, and LP. Compared with CK, the dominant peaks of the SP, AG, SHP, and LP groups showed a shift toward shorter relaxation times, reflecting the reduced mobility of the dominant water population and the enhanced restriction of water molecules in these systems (Chen et al., 2017). Among these treatments, the LP group exhibited the lowest T22 value (166.38 ms), which indicated the strongest restriction of the dominant water population. This result was consistent with the higher hardness and chewiness of LP-treated samples, implying that LP enabled the formation of a denser network structure (Wu et al., 2024). The T22 values of the SP and AG groups were 230.04 and 212.15 ms, respectively, lower than that of the CK group (270.50 ms). Hence, these two polysaccharides also limited the mobility of the dominant water population to some extent, although less effectively than LP. The SHP group showed a T22 value of 180.42 ms along with a shorter T21 peak, indicating pronounced water redistribution and the formation of an additional restricted water fraction. This behavior was likely associated with the stronger hydrophilicity of SHP and its competition for available water, which weakened starch chain aggregation and, to some extent, limited the formation of ordered structures (Huang et al., 2024). In line with these findings, the SHP-treated system showed a lower setback viscosity and the lowest hardness (81.70 g), suggesting that SHP mainly contributed to retrogradation control and the reduction of excessive hardening by regulating water distribution rather than forming a high-strength gel network. In contrast, although the CG group also displayed a bimodal distribution, it showed the highest T22 value (293.32 ms), while its P22 reached 98.23%. This indicated that a large proportion of the relatively mobile dominant water population was retained. This phenomenon could be attributed to the network-forming ability of CG, which could physically entrap water within the gel matrix while simultaneously strengthening network structure (Qiao et al., 2024).

Short-range ordered structure

The FT-IR spectra of germinated indica rice pastes treated with different polysaccharides were similar to those of CK samples (Fig. 4a). Thus, no new characteristic absorption bands indicative of covalent bond formation were detected in the polysaccharide-treated samples. This suggested that the modifications were mainly governed by non-covalent interactions, particularly hydrogen bonding. In the high-frequency region of 3600–3200 cm−1, the characteristic O—H stretching vibration peak was centered at 3397 cm−1 in the CK group. In comparison, most polysaccharide-treated samples exhibited a distinct red-shift toward lower wavenumbers. Notably, the LP group displayed a unique blue-shift to 3404.4 cm−1. This shift may reflect changes in the hydrogen-bonding environment and the reorganization of intermolecular interactions within the starch–polysaccharide matrix (Qi et al., 2024). In this study, the peak shifted from 1654.9 cm−1 (CK) to 1651.1 cm−1 in all treated groups. These changes may be associated with interactions between polysaccharides and starch chains, which modified the local hydrogen-bond network. Moreover, within the fingerprint region of 900–1200 cm−1, no new absorption peaks were detected in any treatment group. This further confirmed that the modification process was dominated by physical entanglement, and the chemical integrity of starch molecules was preserved.

Crystallization characteristics

The crystalline structure of starch can be classified into four types: A, B, C, and V. Native indica rice starch exhibits an A-type crystalline structure. In this study, after gelatinization and freeze-drying, a distinct diffraction peak appeared at 2θ ≈ 20° (Fig. 4b), indicating the formation of V-type crystals. This feature is commonly attributed to the formation of amylose–lipid complexes following the disruption of the native crystalline arrangement during gelatinization. Meanwhile, freeze-drying can also preserve or promote dehydration-associated molecular ordering during sample preparation (Rockembach et al., 2019). The positions of the diffraction peaks were largely consistent across all treatment groups, with no new or interfering peaks detected. This indicated that polysaccharide addition did not alter the V-type crystalline structure of indica rice flour and instead primarily influenced its RC.

RC serves as a metric for the integrity and regularity of crystalline domains. As illustrated in Fig. 4b, the CG group exhibited significantly higher RC (12.55%) than the control group (11.91%) (P < 0.05). Similarly, the highest R1047/1022 ratio was also observed on FT-IR in the CG group. This enhancement could be attributed to the thermodynamic incompatibility between starch and rigid CG chains, which induced molecular crowding and increased local starch chain concentrations, thus facilitating the formation of ordered crystalline domains. Conversely, other polysaccharide-treated samples displayed lower RC values than the control (P < 0.05). Notably, the SHP group recorded the lowest RC value (9.31%), in line with the findings of FT-IR, which revealed the lowest R1047/1022 ratio in this group. This outcome suggested that the abundance of polar groups in SHP molecules facilitated their interaction with water molecules, thereby weakening the ordered packing of starch and reducing the tendency of recrystallization (Huang et al., 2024).

In summary, the XRD and FT-IR results showed consistent trends. Polysaccharide addition did not change the V-type crystalline structure of indica rice flour, but it altered the relative degree of molecular order, with CG promoting ordered structure formation and SHP providing the strongest inhibitory effect.

Thermal properties

The thermal parameters of the samples are summarized in Table 3. The onset (To), peak (Tₚ), and conclusion (Tc) temperatures reflect the thermal stability of crystalline domains, while the gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH) represents the energy required to disrupt ordered double-helical structures and can thus be used to evaluate double-helix stability in starch-based systems. In this study, the CG-treated group exhibited the highest Tp (73.23 °C) and ΔH (11.50 J·g−1), with both values being significantly higher than those in the control group (P < 0.05). This elevation was indicative of enhanced crystalline stability in the presence of CG, consistent with the higher structural order observed on FT-IR and XRD analyses. Thus, greater thermal energy was required to disrupt the ordered structures in CG-treated starch pastes. In contrast, the SHP group exhibited the lowest gelatinization temperatures and the smallest ΔH value. This was likely due to the strong water-binding capacity of SHP, which altered the hydration environment and reduced the stability of the double-helical structures in starch. This hydration barrier effectively hindered the formation of stable starch–starch interactions, rendering the granules more susceptible to thermal disruption and consequently lowering enthalpy requirements (Wang et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2021). The AG group exhibited a comparatively lower ΔH (8.36 J·g−1), reflecting the lower stability of double-helical domains in this system, which facilitated the partial dissociation of ordered structures. Meanwhile, SP and LP displayed intermediate behaviors, with ΔH values of approximately 9.50 and 9.53 J·g−1, respectively, indicating the modest disruption of double-helical structures and a slight reduction in crystalline stability (Marelli et al., 2025). Importantly, except for the CG group, all other polysaccharide-treated groups exhibited lower ΔH values than the control (P < 0.05), suggesting that most hydrophilic polysaccharides reduced the stability of ordered double-helical structures.

TGA

The thermal stability of germinated indica rice pastes treated with different polysaccharides was evaluated via TGA (Fig. 4c); the detailed mass loss values are summarized in Table S4. All samples underwent three characteristic stages of decomposition. The first stage (30–200 °C) mainly corresponded to water loss, including the evaporation of free and weakly bound water. Notably, SHP-treated samples exhibited the smallest initial mass loss, indicating that they had stronger water–matrix interactions, enabling the retention of bound water at low temperatures. This could be related to the stronger water-binding capacity of SHP, which modified the hydration environment of starch and potentially contributed to a more homogeneous microstructure.

The second stage (200–330 °C) corresponded to the major thermal decomposition of the starch–polysaccharide matrix and accounted for the largest mass loss within the system (Martínez et al., 2021). The DTG curves (Fig. 4d) revealed that the mass-loss rate peaked at approximately 303–308 °C for all treatment groups, in agreement with previously reported ranges for starch degradation (Liu et al., 2013). Comparisons with control samples revealed that the primary degradation peak temperatures showed an overall shift to higher values following polysaccharide addition, suggesting that polysaccharides could alter the thermal degradation behavior of the starch-based matrix. Among the different polysaccharide systems, the SHP system showed the lowest mass loss during this stage (56.98%), indicating greater residue retention during the major decomposition stage. The mass loss in the second stage ranged from 56.98% to 62.68%, indicating that this stage corresponded to the major thermal decomposition of the samples.

The third and final stage (330–600 °C) was mainly associated with carbonization and the further degradation of residual organic components. The residual mass after decomposition reflected the retention of the remaining components following thermal degradation. Its value may also have been affected by ash or the mineral residues originally present in the polysaccharides. The residual mass was the highest in the SHP group, followed by the CG, SP, LP, AG, and CK groups.

Taken together, the findings showed that SHP treatment resulted in the greatest residual mass during thermal decomposition, followed by CG and SP treatment. In contrast, control samples exhibited the lowest residual mass. These results indicated that different polysaccharides differentially modified the thermal degradation behavior of the system, with SHP providing greater residue retention during thermal degradation. This highlighted the potential of SHP in improving the thermal processing stability of germinated whole-grain products.

Morphological characterization

Water sublimation during freeze-drying can create voids within the starch-based matrix, and the observed pore morphology may thus partly reflect water distribution. The abundance and geometry of these pores are also influenced by polysaccharide–starch associations (Li et al., 2024). Using SEM observations, the starch pastes examined in this study could broadly be classified into two distinct microstructural patterns based on pore development and matrix compactness (Fig. 5 and Fig. S1). The CK, CG, and LP samples exhibited a more porous structural pattern. After freeze-drying, the CK sample presented a typical porous starch framework, which served as the baseline morphology for comparison with the treatment groups. Among them, the CG-treated sample exhibited thicker pore walls and a more interconnected porous framework, in line with its higher hardness and chewiness (Table 2), indicating the reinforcement of the gel structure (Dong et al., 2020). In contrast, the SHP, SP, and AG treatment groups exhibited a less porous and more homogeneous microstructure, corresponding to the second structural pattern. To corroborate the visual observations of morphology based on SEM, the apparent pore area fraction was also estimated (Supplementary Table S5). Among the samples, CG showed the highest apparent pore area fraction (13.12%), whereas SHP showed the lowest value (1.59%). SP- and AG-treated samples were characterized by irregular sheet-like or lamellar structures with relatively smooth surfaces. Notably, the SHP-treated sample displayed the smoothest surface with only minor micro-cracks. This morphological feature was consistent with the reduced hardness (Table 2) and lower setback viscosity (Table 1) observed in this system. The observed behavior was consistent with the LF-NMR results and could be attributed to the strong water-binding capacity of SHP, which limited water mobility and starch chain reassociation during gel formation (Zhou et al., 2022). Overall, the SEM observations supported the presence of two distinct modes of structural regulation induced by different anionic polysaccharides. Specifically, CG promoted the development of a more interconnected gel framework, which was consistent with its stronger gel network and the relatively dominant mobile water population observed on LF-NMR analysis. Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the incorporation of anionic polysaccharides effectively regulates the physicochemical properties and multiscale structural organization of germinated indica rice pastes. Distinct modification patterns were observed across different polysaccharide treatment groups, with differential influences on retrogradation behavior, gel texture, water mobility, crystalline structure, and thermal stability. From a physicochemical perspective, network-forming anionic polysaccharides, particularly CG, promoted the formation of a more continuous and rigid gel framework, thereby enhancing gel strength and structural stability. From a multiscale structural perspective, highly hydrophilic anionic polysaccharides such as SHP primarily modified the hydration environment, hindered starch-chain mobility, reduced structural order, and consequently suppressed retrogradation tendency. From an application perspective, CG may be more suitable for products requiring a firm texture and strong gel structure, whereas SHP may be more appropriate for products requiring reduced retrogradation, a softer texture, or delayed hardening. The findings of the present study provide a scientific basis for the rational selection of anionic polysaccharides to improve the quality and stability of germinated whole-grain products. However, this study was conducted using a single uniform polysaccharide concentration and a model paste system. Hence, further studies are required to evaluate the storage stability, digestibility, and real food applications of such germinated whole-grain systems and to further clarify the interactions between starch and polysaccharides in these systems.
