Biomimetic self-assembled copper nanoadjuvant potentiates hepatocellular carcinoma immunotherapy via cuproptosis-driven cGAS-STING activation
Authors: Linhao Jing, Shuaijun Zhao, Chenguang Sun, Fangfang Cui, Yaning Xia, Yupeng Shi, Weijie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmunotherapy has reshaped cancer treatment, yet its efficacy in many solid tumors remains limited by an immunosuppressive microenvironment and insufficient antigen presentation. As a central gatekeeper of antitumor immunity, the cGAS-STING pathway represents a key axis for converting tumor-intrinsic stress into productive immune priming. Here, we propose an integrated strategy that couples immunogenic tumor cell death with tumor-restricted cGAS-STING activation and adjuvant-amplified innate signaling. Cuproptosis, a recently identified copper-dependent cell-death program, can act as an endogenous trigger of tumor-intrinsic cGAS-STING activation, providing a promising avenue to link cytotoxicity with immune stimulation. We engineered a biomimetic, tumor-responsive nanoplatform (Cu-R837@CM) by co-assembling Cu2+ with the TLR7 agonist R837 and cloaking the core with homologous tumor cell membranes to enhance tumor targeting and intratumoral retention. In vitro, Cu-R837@CM released Cu2+ under acidic conditions, inducing glutathione depletion and ROS accumulation, promoting DLAT aggregation and mitochondrial dysfunction, increasing intracellular 2′3′-cGAMP, and activating cGAS-STING signaling. Concurrently, R837 enhanced dendritic-cell maturation and cytokine production, thereby improving antigen presentation. In vivo, Cu-R837@CM markedly inhibited tumor growth, increased CD4+/CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and remodeled the tumor immune microenvironment toward an antitumor phenotype. Notably, combining Cu-R837@CM with PD-L1 blockade achieved complete tumor regression and significantly prolonged survival. Collectively, Cu-R837@CM offers a clinically translatable nanoadjuvant paradigm that integrates cuproptosis-driven cGAS-STING activation with TLR7 co-stimulation for hepatocellular carcinoma immunotherapy.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Immunotherapy, Cuproptosis, cGAS-STING pathway, R837

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) ranks as the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with its incidence steadily increasing [1,2]. Current first-line therapies, including tyrosine kinase inhibitors (e.g., Sorafenib and Lenvatinib) and anti-VEGF/PD-L1 combination regimens such as atezolizumab-bevacizumab, have provided modest improvements in median overall survival. Nevertheless, these benefits are typically limited to less than 19.2 months due to the rapid development of drug resistance, dose-limiting toxicities, and an immunologically suppressed tumor microenvironment (TME) [1,3,4]. Moreover, even when initial tumor control is achieved, the persistently immunosuppressive TME can blunt immune priming and facilitate tumor relapse. Mechanistically, this immunosuppression is driven in part by abundant MDSCs and M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), actively suppresses cytotoxic T cell function [5,6]. Consequently, therapeutic strategies that reprogram the immunosuppressive TME and restore effective cytotoxic T cell responses may yield improved and more durable outcomes in HCC.

The cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway has recently emerged as a promising therapeutic target for reinvigorating antitumor immunity [7,8]. This innate immune sensing mechanism bridges cytosolic DNA detection with robust type I interferon (IFN-α/β) production, dendritic cell (DC) maturation, and CD8+ T cell priming, thereby facilitating the conversion of immunologically “cold' tumors into “hot' lesions [9,10]. However, the clinical translation of synthetic STING agonists (including cyclic dinucleotides, amidobenzimidazole derivatives, and MSA-2) in early-phase trials has been hampered by their poor membrane permeability, rapid systemic clearance, and dose-limiting cytokine release syndromes, collectively resulting in suboptimal intratumoral accumulation and transient therapeutic efficacy [11,12]. These drawbacks indicate that STING should be activated selectively and persistently within tumors while avoiding systemic inflammation, prompting exploration of tumor-intrinsic routes to innate immune activation. In this regard, recent advances in metal ion-mediated cell death have identified cuproptosis, a novel copper-dependent programmed cell death pathway, as an endogenous mechanism for tumor-intrinsic cGAS-STING activation [13,14]. Mechanistically, intracellular copper accumulation disrupts mitochondrial metabolism through interference with protein lipoylation, leading to respiratory chain collapse and subsequent release of mitochondrial DNA along with other damage-associated molecular patterns [13,15,16]. These danger signals potently engage the cGAS-STING pathway, establishing a self-amplifying loop of type I IFN production that synergistically integrates direct cytotoxicity with innate immune activation [7,8].

Building on this tumor-intrinsic, cuproptosis-driven cGAS-STING immunogenic program, the imidazoquinoline derivative R837 (imiquimod) can provide complementary extrinsic immune stimulation through potent Toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7) agonism. R837 exerts multifaceted immunostimulatory effects, including DC maturation, IFN-α/β and IL-12 induction, MDSC suppression, and M2-to-M1 TAM repolarization [17,18]. Nevertheless, the clinical utility of free R837 is limited by its poor aqueous solubility, nonspecific systemic distribution, and potential for inducing cytokine release syndrome (CRS) [19]. Accordingly, safe tumor-confined and endosome-directed delivery of R837 remains a key unmet need to localize TLR7 activation, minimize systemic inflammation, and support its integration with the tumor-intrinsic cGAS-STING program for cascaded immune amplification [[20], [21], [22]].

Nanotechnology platforms provide an ideal vehicle for translating this combinatorial approach into clinical practice. Advanced nanocarriers enable co-encapsulation of otherwise incompatible therapeutic cargos (metal ions, nucleic acid agonists, and hydrophobic drugs) while offering stimulus-responsive release profiles (pH-, redox-, or enzyme-triggered) that permit spatiotemporally controlled induction of cuproptosis and immune activation with minimized systemic exposure [[23], [24], [25]].These multifunctional systems not only enhance tumor-specific payload accumulation and protect labile agents from degradation but also exhibit excellent compatibility with immune checkpoint blockade therapies for improved therapeutic outcomes [26,27]. However, conventional nanoparticle designs relying solely on passive enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effects demonstrate suboptimal biodistribution patterns marked by heterogeneous tumor deposition and preferential reticuloendothelial system sequestration [[28], [29], [30]]. In contrast, cell membrane cloaking endows nanoparticles with a biomimetic “self” identity and homotypic affinity, thereby enhancing tumor tropism and circulation persistence while mitigating opsonization and reticuloendothelial clearance [31].

Here, we engineered a biomimetic nanoplatform (Cu-R837@CM) that couples copper-ion delivery with a built-in TLR7 adjuvant within a tumor-cell-membrane cloak (Scheme 1). This innovative design capitalizes on native membrane proteins (including CD47 ″don't eat me' signals and homotypic adhesion molecules) to achieve prolonged circulation, reduced macrophage uptake, and enhanced tumor-specific homing, while preserved tumor antigens facilitate in situ immune recognition [[32], [33], [34]]. Mechanistically, the platform is designed to connect copper overload to innate immune activation: copper-induced cuproptosis coincides with cytosolic mtDNA (ND1/ATP6) escape and 2′3′-cGAMP production; in parallel, R837 engages TLR7 to potentiate dendritic-cell maturation. We therefore hypothesize that Cu-R837@CM can convert copper-based treatment from primarily cytotoxic chemistry into an immune-stimulatory program that reshapes the tumor microenvironment and enhances antitumor T-cell responses, while maintaining a favorable safety window. This platform may provide a clinically translatable nanomedicine framework for HCC immunotherapy and offers potential compatibility with immune checkpoint blockade, such as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibition.

Schematic illustration of the proposed mechanism of action for the Cu-R837@CM. The self-assembled nanoplatform induces GSH depletion and mitochondrial stress in tumor cells, leading to ROS production, DLAT aggregation, mtDNA release, and activation of the cGAS-STING pathway. These effects trigger cuproptosis and the release of IFN-β. The resulting immunogenic tumor cell death promotes dendritic cell (DC) maturation and T cell activation, while R837 further enhances immune responses. The therapeutic effect is monitored by MR imaging.

Synthesis of the Cu-R837 NPs

Briefly, a mixture of BIX (0.01 mmol, 2.4 mg) and R837 (0.02 mmol, 4.8 mg) was dissolved in 1 mL of DMF. While stirring at a constant speed of 900 rpm, a water solution of CuCl2 (0.01 mmol, 1.4 mg in 1 mL of water) was added to the BIX solution. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 2 h, during which the suspension rapidly changed from colorless to yellow, indicating the formation of a fine precipitate. After 2 h, the precipitation was collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting pellet was resuspended and rinsed three times with ethanol to ensure thorough washing. The sample was divided into two portions. In the first portion, the solvent was removed, and the resulting solid was dried under vacuum before undergoing comprehensive characterization using UV-Vis, FT-IR, ICP-MS, XPS and XRD. The second portion was preserved in solution to prevent aggregation during the drying process and was subsequently analyzed using DLS, zeta potential, TEM.

Preparation of the cell membrane

HepG2 cells and H22 cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Typically, these cells were harvested and resuspended in cold PBS. They were then lysed in a hypotonic buffer containing PMSF (Beyotime, China) and a membrane protein extraction reagent. Following incubation on ice for 15 min, the cells underwent repeated freeze-thaw cycles for disruption. The lysate was centrifuged at 800 g for 10 min, and the resulting supernatant was further centrifuged at 15,000 g for 30 min to isolate cell membrane fragments. The membrane products were lyophilized and stored at −80 °C. Before use, the lyophilized membranes were rehydrated in ultrapure water or PBS.

Preparation of the Cu-R837@CM nanoparticles

Cu-R837@CM NPs were constructed using a sequential extrusion protocol. Cu-R837 NPs (1 mL, 1, 5, 10, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120 μg/mL), CM (1 mL, 1.0 mg/mL) were mixed. The mixture was serially extruded through 800, 400, and 200 nm aqueous membranes using a syringe-driven device. Unloaded Cu-R837 NPs were eliminated by centrifugation, yielding Cu-R837@CM with an encapsulated NP concentration of 80 μg/mL for downstream characterization and assays. HepG2-derived membranes (HepG2-CM) were used for in vitro assays performed in HepG2 cells. H22-derived membranes (H22-CM) were used for experiments involving H22 cells and for all in vivo studies based on the H22 tumor model. Unless specified, Cu-R837@CM refers to the formulation coated with H22-CM/HepG2-CM.

SDS-PAGE

To confirm the effective coating of CM, its membrane proteins were analyzed using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Cu-R837 NPs, CM, and Cu-R837@CM samples were incubated at 100 °C for 5 min in SDS sample buffer. The prepared samples, along with protein markers, were carefully introduced into the gel wells. Electrophoresis proceeded under a stable voltage of 90 V for a duration of 2 h. Subsequently, the gel underwent staining with Coomassie Brilliant Blue, followed by a 2-h incubation at room temperature. It was then subjected to shaking for 90 min before being destained overnight to facilitate photographic documentation.

MRI imaging

To evaluate the relaxation properties, Cu-R837 nanoparticles at varying concentrations (Cu: 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 2, and 4 mM) were loaded into 2 mL Eppendorf tubes and subjected to magnetic resonance (MR) imaging using a 3.0 T GE Architect scanner (USA). Imaging protocols included fast spin echo (FSE) T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) with parameters set as TR/TE = 500/15 ms, NSA = 3, field of view (FOV) = 80 × 80 mm, slice thickness = 1.0 mm, and matrix size = 228 × 290. In addition, T1-mapping was performed using a combined inversion-recovery and spin echo sequence. The inversion-recovery segment used TR/TE/TI = 4000/160/400 ms, followed by a spin echo segment with TR/TE = 3500/20 ms, NSA = 1, FOV = 80 × 80 mm, slice thickness = 1.0 mm, and a matrix of 512 × 512. Based on the longitudinal relaxation times (T1) measured at different nanoparticle concentrations, the longitudinal relaxivity (r1) was determined.

Cellular uptake

To evaluate the cellular uptake and targeting capability of Cu-R837@CM NPs, both Cu-R837 NPs and Cu-R837@CM NPs were labeled with FITC. HepG2 cells were seeded into laser confocal culture plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After removing the medium, the cells were treated with the designated nanoparticles for 0, 2, 4, and 6 h. At each point, the cells were washed three times with PBS and stained with Hoechst dye for 15 min to visualize nuclei. Fluorescence microscopy was subsequently performed to assess nanoparticle internalization over time.

Consumption of the GSH in vitro

To evaluate the influence of Cu-R837 NPs concentration on glutathione (GSH) consumption, various concentrations of Cu-R837 NPs (0, 20, 40, 80, and 100 μg/mL) were incubated with 1.0 mM GSH in 450 μL of BBS buffer (pH 6.5) at 25 °C for 30 min under constant agitation. After incubation, 15 μL of 100 mM DTNB solution and 785 μL of 5 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.0) were added. The mixtures were then centrifuged, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured.

To assess the time-dependent GSH consumption, 100 μg/mL of NPs was incubated with 1.0 mM GSH in 450 μL of BBS (pH 6.5) at 37 °C for various durations (0, 1, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 30 min). After incubation, the same volume of DTNB and Tris-HCl solution was added as described above. Following centrifugation, the absorbance of the supernatant was recorded.

In vitro cytotoxicity assays

HepG2 cells were cultured in 96-well plates using a complete growth medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum) at 37 °C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2. After 24 h, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were subjected to different treatments: (i) the PBS control group; (ii) the CuCl2 group; (iii) Cu-R837 NPs at varying concentrations (1, 5, 10, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, and 120 μg/mL); and (iv) Cu-R837@CM at the same concentration gradients.

Cell viability was assessed using a standard CCK-8 assay, and optical density (OD) values were measured at 450 nm using a SpectraMax® i3X microplate reader. For flow cytometry analysis, HepG2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight. Following different treatments, apoptosis rates in each group were determined. Additionally, cell viability was further examined through CAM/PI staining.

Total DNA and cytosolic DNA extraction

Cells were trypsinized, neutralized with complete medium, and the suspension was split into two 1.5-mL tubes (Tube 1, total DNA; Tube 2, cytosolic DNA). Pellets were collected at 200×g for 5 min. For the cytosolic fraction, the pellet was gently resuspended in 250 μL Buffer A (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES, 25 μg/mL digitonin, pH 7.4) and incubated at room temperature for 10 min, then centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 min; the supernatant was transferred to a new tube and clarified at 17,000×g for 10 min to obtain the cytosolic supernatant. DNA was purified using the FastPure Cell/Tissue DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Vazyme, DC102-01), applying the cultured-cell protocol (08-1B) to Tube 1 pellets and the liquid-sample protocol (08-1C) to Tube 2 cytosolic supernatants. Elution volumes were matched between paired fractions.

Quantitative PCR

Cytosolic DNA and total cellular DNA were isolated from cultured cells according to the manufacturer's guidelines and used as the template for quantitative PCR. Quantitative PCR was then carried out in technical triplicates using TB Green Premix (Takara, RR820A) on a real-time PCR system. Mitochondrial ND1 and ATP6 were amplified from the cytosolic DNA fraction, whereas the nuclear 18S rDNA was amplified from the matched total DNA sample and used as the internal reference. Relative abundance was calculated by the 2^−ΔΔCt method, defining ΔCt = Ct(ND1 or ATP6 from cytosolic DNA) − Ct(18S rDNA from total DNA), with the untreated group serving as the calibrator. Primer sequences, synthesized and purified by a commercial provider, are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Intracellular cGAMP measurement

Intracellular 2′3′-cGAMP was quantified with a commercial 2′3′-cGAMP ELISA kit following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h, then stimulated with PBS, CuCl2, Cu-R837, or Cu-R837@CM for 12 h. After treatment, cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer on ice. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm to remove insoluble material, and the resulting supernatants were analyzed by ELISA to determine intracellular 2′3′-cGAMP levels.

DCs maturation in vitro

DC2.4 cells were planted in 6-well plates containing 2 mL of culture medium. The medium was half-changed each 2 days. Meanwhile, 5 × 105 H22 cells were cultured in 6-well plates overnight. The cells were treated with CuCl2, Cu-R837 NPs and Cu-R837@CM for 12 h and then replaced with fresh medium to treat for 12 h. Subsequently, the supernatant of tumor cells was used to co-cultured with DC2.4 on day 6. One day later, the DC2.4 dyed with antibodies Including anti-Percp-Cy5.5-CD11c, anti-FITC-CD80 and anti- APC/Cy7-CD86 antibodies for flow cytometry analysis.

Western blotting

HepG2 cells treated with PBS, CuCl2, Cu-R837 NPs and Cu-R837@CM formulations for 24 h, then HepG2 cells were obtained and treated with the radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer to lyse the cells for 30 min. Subsequently, the cell lysate was centrifuged in the presence of phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktail at 12000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was then collected and subjected processed with the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit to quantify the concentration of proteins. The samples were separated from SDS-PAGE gel and transferred on the nitro cellulose membrane. After that, the samples were blocked and incubated with TBK1, STING, p-TBK, p-STING, IRF3, p-IRF3 and DLAT antibodies overnight at 4 °C. This was treated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody, then administrated with ECL Substrate. The results were processed using ImageJ (GE Amersham Imager 600, USA).

In vivo biological distribution

After tail vein injection of IR783-labeled Cu-R837and IR783-labeled Cu-R837@CM nanoparticles, fluorescence images were acquired at 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h using a live animal fluorescence imaging system. Following the final imaging time point, the mice were sacrificed, and major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and tumors were collected. These tissues were then imaged ex vivo using a fluorescence imaging system to evaluate the biodistribution and metabolic processing of the nanoparticles within internal organs.

In vivo MRI imaging

Tumor-bearing mouse models were established by subcutaneous injection of 100 μL H22 cell suspension (5 × 106 cells/mL) into Balb/c mice. After 7 days, in vivo magnetic resonance (MR) imaging was performed using a GE Achitect 3.0 T scanner to evaluate the MR imaging capability of Cu-R837@CM NPs. Prior to administration, baseline T1-weighted MR images of the tumor site were acquired. Subsequently, one mouse was intravenously injected with 100 μL of PBS, while the other received 100 μL of Cu-R837@CM NPs via the tail vein. T1-weighted MR images were collected at 0, 2, 6, 12 and 24 h post-injection.

Hemolysis assay

Fresh murine blood from male Balb/c mice was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min, and the erythrocytes were purified through 5 successive washes with PBS buffer. A 10 μL aliquot of the erythrocyte suspension was then mixed with 90 μL PBS, resulting in final concentrations of Cu-R837 NPs at varying levels (0, 20, 40, 80, 120, 240 μg/mL). Triton-x and PBS served as positive and negative controls, respectively. After a 2-h incubation at room temperature, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 3 min, and the absorbance of the supernatants at 540 nm was measured using a versatile microplate detection system (Synergy™ HT, BioTek). The percentage of hemolysis was calculated using the formula: (ODsample- ODPBS)/(ODDDW-ODPBS) × 100%. The experiment was independently repeated 3 times.

Determination of in vivo antitumor efficacy

1 × 106 H22 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of male Balb/c mice to establish a subcutaneous model (6 weeks old). These mice were divided randomly into 4 groups and administrated with CuCl2, Cu-R837 NPs, Cu-R837@CM (Cu = 2 mg/kg) and PBS (n = 6 per group) when tumors grew to ∼ 50 mm3. Tumor volume and mice's body weight were measured every two days and then calculated by following equation: volume = length × width2 × 0.5. On the 14th day, mice were sacrificed to collect tumors and main organs for flow cytometric analysis and H&E staining. The harvested major organs and tumors were steeped in 4% paraformaldehyde and imbedded in paraffin. Subsequently, the paraffins were sliced and stained with TdT-mediated Dutp Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL) fluorescence and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). In addition, sera were collected for blood biochemical testing.

Flow cytometric analysis

For flow cytometry analysis, the collected tumor tissues were stained with the following antibodies: FITC anti-mouse CD11c, APC anti-mouse CD86, PE anti-Mouse CD80, APC anti-mouse CD45.2 (1:100, 109813, Biolegend), FITC anti-mouse CD3ε (1:50, 100305, Biolegend), PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD4 (1:100, 100421, Biolegend), PE/Cyanine5 anti-mouse CD8a (1:100, 100709, Biolegend), FITC anti-mouse/human CD11b (1:50, 101205, Biolegend). Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a MACSQuant® Analyzer (Germany) to assess the maturation of DCs in the spleen, as well as the populations of intratumoral/intrasplenic T cells.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 16.0 software, with all data presented as mean ± standard deviation. The significance of differences between groups was determined using a two-tailed Statistical t-test, considering P < 0.05 as indicative of statistical significance.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of Cu-R837 NPs

The Cu-R837 nanoparticles (NPs) were synthesized following previously established protocol with modifications [35]. Briefly, an aqueous solution of copper (II) chloride (CuCl2) was combined with R837 in the presence of the bivalent bridging ligand 1,4-Bis [(1H- imidazole-1-yl) methyl] benzene (BIX) under continuous magnetic stirring (900 rpm) at ambient temperature for 2 h to facilitate nanoparticle formation. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to evaluate the morphology and dispersity of the synthesized nanoparticles. As shown in Fig. 1a&b, the nanoparticles displayed a uniform spherical morphology with good monodispersity and no evident aggregation, indicating successful formation and favorable colloidal stability. The corresponding size distribution analysis obtained by dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Fig. 1c) shows an average diameter of approximately 43.68 nm with a relatively narrow size distribution, indicating good homogeneity. These physicochemical features are particularly relevant for in vivo translation, as nanoscale uniformity and colloidal stability are prerequisites for reproducible biodistribution and cellular uptake [36]. Meanwhile, the element mapping (Fig. 1d) showed a homogeneous distribution of Cu throughout the particles, confirming the successful fabrication of Cu-R837 NPs. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Fig. 1e) was used to assess the Cu valence states in the sample revealed that the high-resolution Cu 2p XPS spectrum (Fig. 1f) exhibited two characteristic Cu peaks at Cu 2p3/2 (932 eV) and Cu 2p1/2 (952 eV), suggesting that copper is predominantly in an oxidized state. Furthermore, high-resolution C 1s, N 1s, and O 1s spectra (Fig. S1) confirmed the presence of chemical states corresponding to C-C, -NH-, N=C-, C=O, and C-O groups, indicating the successful incorporation of organic ligands and R837 within the nanostructure. In addition, no apparent crystal peak was noticed in the X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) spectrum (Fig. 1g), implying the poor crystallinity of Cu in Cu-R837 NPs.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Fig. 1h) was performed to confirm the successful synthesis of nanoparticles composed of R837, Cu, and a BIX ligand. The FTIR spectrum displayed characteristic absorption bands corresponding to key functional groups in the synthesized nanoparticles. The broad peak at 3445.16 cm−1 is attributed to -NH2 stretching, while the peak at 3271.14 cm−1 corresponds to N-H stretching, indicating the presence of amine groups. Absorption bands at 1681.93 cm−1 and 1523.74 cm−1 are assigned to C=N and C=C stretching vibrations, respectively, suggesting the incorporation of imidazole rings from BIX. Additionally, the peak at 1253.44 cm−1 corresponds to C-N stretching, further supporting the presence of imidazole-related structures. These characteristic peaks confirm the successful formation of the intended nanostructures, demonstrating the interaction between the precursor materials and the development of a stable nanoparticle system. UV-vis spectra showed characteristic absorption peak of R837 at ∼318 nm observed in the nanoparticle solution, confirming the successful encapsulation of R837 into the nanoparticles. To enable quantification, a calibration curve for R837 was constructed by measuring UV-vis absorbance at 318 nm over 0-1.0 mg ml−1 (Fig. S2). Using the R837 calibration curve, the R837 loading content in Cu-R837 was determined to be approximately 10.7%. In addition, the longitudinal relaxivity (r1) of Cu-R837 NPs, measured on a 3.0 T MRI scanner, increased linearly with Cu2+ concentration (Fig. S3). The accompanying T1-weighted phantom images reveal a colour shift from dark blue to bright red as Cu2+ levels rise, underscoring the nanoparticles’ strong T1-contrast capability.

Given that nanoparticles are typically trafficked into acidic endo/lysosomal compartments after cellular uptake and that the tumor microenvironment is mildly acidic, evaluating pH-responsive Cu2+ release is essential [37]. To investigate the release behavior of Cu2+ from the nanoparticles under different conditions, we compared copper ion release profiles in acidic (pH 5.5) and physiological (pH 7.4) environments (Fig. 1j). In parallel, R837 release was measured by dialysis with UV-vis quantification at 318 nm using the calibration curve (Fig. S2). The cumulative release reached ∼60% within 24 h at pH 5.5 but was negligible at pH 7.4 (Fig. 1k). Consistently, TEM imaging after incubation at pH 5.5 revealed progressive morphological changes from clustered particles at 0 h to dispersed/vanishing features by 12 h (Fig. 1i), and TEM-derived particle diameters decreased accordingly over time (Fig. S4), supporting acid-triggered nanoparticle disassembly and cargo liberation. The amorphous nature and acid-triggered disassembly together indicate that the coordination network is sufficiently stable in circulation yet labile in lysosomal-like conditions, enabling spatially confined Cu2+/R837 liberation.

In light of the high GSH levels in tumor cells and their role in counteracting oxidative stress [38], we next investigated the glutathione (GSH) depletion capability of Cu-R837 NPs in vitro. GSH consumption was evaluated using the 5,5′-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) assay, the absorbance at ∼412 nm decreased progressively with increasing concentrations of NPs, demonstrating a concentration-dependent GSH consumption (Fig. S5a). Quantitative analysis revealed a linear relationship between absorbance and NPs concentration (Fig. S5b), as well as with reaction time (Fig. S5c), confirming the sustained GSH-depleting activity of the nanoparticles. Moreover, GSH depletion not only weakens the tumor antioxidant barrier but may also increase the fraction of redox-active copper, thereby amplifying oxidative stress and downstream mitochondrial damage. Overall, Cu-R837 NPs remain stable under physiological conditions yet undergo acid-triggered disassembly, enabling concurrent Cu2+/R837 release and GSH depletion, which supports their responsiveness to the tumor microenvironment.

Verification of tumor cell membrane coating

Considering the homotypic adhesion capability and immune-evasive properties conferred by homologous tumor cell membranes [31], we further applied membrane cloaking to Cu-R837 NPs. TEM (Fig. 2a) showed membrane-wrapped particles with a thin peripheral shell surrounding the nanoparticle core. SDS-PAGE (Coomassie) (Fig. 2b) revealed that Cu-R837@CM displays a protein pattern closely matching that of the isolated cell membrane (CM). Western blotting for representative membrane proteins (Fig. 2c) further confirmed the coating: E-cadherin (124 kDa) and CD47 (47 kDa) were present in CM and on Cu-R837@CM. CD47, a key ‘don't eat me’ ligand for SIRPα on macrophages, is expected to reduce opsonization and phagocytosis, favoring prolonged systemic persistence. E-cadherin mediates homophilic cell-cell adhesion in epithelial tissues, whose preservation on Cu-R837@CM suggests homotypic recognition/adhesion to the source tumor cells, which may facilitate tumor-site accumulation. Although protein orientation and activity were not directly assessed, the preserved membrane markers, corroborated by TEM and SDS-PAGE, provide evidence for effective membrane cloaking. Consistent with membrane cloaking, the ζ-potential shifted from approximately +23.0 mV for Cu-R837 to +11.8 mV for Cu-R837@CM (Fig. S6). Moreover, DLS revealed a narrow size distribution centered at ∼180 nm (Fig. S7). Collectively, these orthogonal measurements confirm successful fusion of tumor cell-membrane components onto the nanoparticle surface.

Tumor cell internalization and cytotoxicity of Cu-R837@CM NPs

Since homologous tumor cell-membrane cloaking is expected to enhance tumor cell recognition while reducing nonspecific uptake by macrophages and normal cells [39], we next profiled the cellular internalization behavior of Cu-R837@CM. The FITC-labeled Cu-R837@CM was cultured with HepG2 liver cancer cells to investigate the interaction between the nanoparticles and tumor cells. Fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 2d) revealed a time-dependent increase in green fluorescence intensity, confirming efficient uptake of the nanoplatform. Fluorescence signals were predominantly localized in the cytoplasm and became more prominent over 8 h of incubation, indicating progressive cellular accumulation of Cu-R837@CM. Intracellular uptake was further validated by flow cytometry: overlaid histograms (Fig. 2e) and mean-fluorescence-intensity (MFI) quantification (Fig. 2f) both demonstrated a clear, time-dependent rise in FITC intensity, corroborating efficient internalization of Cu-R837@CM. To examine whether cell-membrane cloaking confers immune evasive and normal cell sparing behavior, FITC-labeled Cu-R837@CM was further incubated with THP-1 cells and normal human hepatocytes (THLE-2). In sharp contrast to the robust, time-dependent internalization observed in HepG2 cells, both THP-1 and THLE-2 displayed only faint and sporadic FITC signals even after 6 h of incubation (Fig. S8), suggesting minimal uptake by macrophages and non-malignant hepatic cells. The preferential uptake by HepG2 over THP-1/THLE-2 is consistent with a membrane-mediated selectivity mechanism, which may reduce off-target copper exposure and improve the therapeutic index.

Inspired by these findings, the cytotoxic potential of different formulations was evaluated in HepG2 cells. The CCK-8 assay (Fig. 2g) revealed a pronounced, concentration-dependent reduction in cell viability, with Cu-R837@CM displaying superior cytotoxicity compared to CuCl2 or NPs alone, particularly at concentrations exceeding 40 μg/mL. Consistently, Calcein-AM/PI live-dead staining (Fig. 2h) and its quantification (Fig. 2i) recapitulated that cells treated with PBS remained predominantly viable, while those treated with Cu-R837@CM showed extensive red PI staining indicative of cell death. Apoptosis analysis by Annexin V/PI flow cytometry (Fig. 2j–k) further confirmed the Cu-R837@CM treatment resulted in a substantial increase in apoptotic populations, exceeding 80%, while CuCl2 and Cu-R837 NPs induced only moderate apoptosis. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Cu-R837@CM are efficiently internalized by tumor cells, deliver intracellular copper, and exert potent cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic effects.

Given that BIX serves as a key coordination/assembly component of the nanoplatform, we also evaluated its intrinsic biocompatibility to exclude potential interference with the experimental outcomes. BIX alone showed negligible toxicity across a broad concentration range (1–120 μg mL−1), with cell viability remaining high (Fig. S9), indicating that the ligand itself does not contribute appreciably to cell damage. To evaluate off-target toxicity of the assembled formulations, we examined viability of normal human hepatocytes (THLE-2). Across 1–120 μg mL−1, both Cu-R837 and Cu-R837@CM maintained ≥90–95% viability (Fig. S10), indicating good cytocompatibility of the nanoplatform toward non-malignant liver cells. Together with the potent, concentration-dependent killing of HepG2 described above, these data suggest a favorable selectivity window for Cu-R837@CM. The observed tumor-normal selectivity suggests that membrane cloaking may decouple antitumor potency from systemic toxicity, a key requirement for copper-based therapeutics [40].

Oxidative stress and innate immune activation in HepG2 cells

The oxidative potential of the Cu-R837@CM formulation was initially assessed using a methylene blue (MB) degradation assay, which serves as an indirect indicator of hydroxyl radical (•OH) generation. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, MB degradation was significantly enhanced in the Cu-R837 group compared to PBS or CuCl2 alone, indicating a stronger Fenton-like catalytic activity. Further evidence was provided by Cell Copper (Cu2+) Colorimetric Assay Kit, which revealed that cells exposed to Cu-R837@CM accumulated nearly four times more intracellular copper than those in the PBS group (Fig. 3b). Since copper ions are known to catalyze Fenton-type reactions that elevate reactive oxygen species (ROS) [41], these results suggest that the formulation efficiently delivers copper intracellularly, leading to oxidative stress amplification. In parallel, glutathione (GSH) depletion was observed in the same group (Fig. 3c), highlighting disrupted redox homeostasis under treatment. Fluorescence imaging using a ROS-sensitive probe confirmed substantial ROS accumulation following Cu-R837@CM exposure, with markedly increased green signal intensities (Fig. 3d). Collectively, these results demonstrate that Cu-R837@CM induces copper accumulation, GSH depletion, and ROS amplification in tumor cells.

We next examined whether this redox disturbance was associated with cuproptosis-related mitochondrial injury. Western blot analysis showed that Cu-R837@CM downregulated the cuproptosis-related proteins FDX1 and LIAS and induced DLAT aggregation, indicating a typical cuproptosis-associated protein alteration pattern (Fig. 3e). In addition, JC-1 staining showed an obvious shift from red fluorescence aggregates to green fluorescence monomers after Cu-R837@CM treatment, suggesting loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 3f). To further confirm the copper-dependent nature of Cu-R837@CM-induced cell death, rescue experiments were performed using the copper chelator BCS. BCS markedly attenuated Cu-R837@CM-induced alterations in cuproptosis-related proteins and substantially restored cell viability (Fig. S11). In contrast, Fer-1 and z-VAD showed only partial protective effects (Fig. S12), suggesting that ferroptosis- and apoptosis-related processes may contribute to the overall cell-death response but are not the dominant mechanism. Together, these rescue experiments support the copper-dependent nature of Cu-R837@CM-induced cytotoxicity, suggesting that the observed cuproptosis-related phenotype is mainly driven by bioavailable intracellular copper rather than nonspecific nanoparticle-induced stress.

Because cuproptosis is centered on mitochondrial metabolic perturbation, we next asked whether this mitochondrial injury promotes the release of immunostimulatory damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), particularly mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), which can serve as a trigger for cGAS sensing. Mitochondrial injury of this type can release DAMPs, including oxidized lipids and mtDNA, which are potent activators of innate immune signaling [42]. Therefore, we use qPCR to examine the mtDNA leakage, cytosolic mtDNA quantification showed a significant increase in ND1 and ATP6 copies following Cu-R837@CM treatment. These results support enhanced mitochondrial DNA efflux into the cytosol, a canonical trigger for cGAS sensing (Fig. 3g). We next examined downstream second-messenger production. Intracellular cGAMP levels rose modestly with CuCl2, but increased markedly with Cu-R837 and were highest with Cu-R837@CM. These data indicate that the biomimetic formulation amplifies cytosolic DNA sensing and cGAS activation (Fig. 3h). Subsequent evaluation of the cGAS-STING pathway, which is activated by cytosolic DNA and cellular stress, revealed pronounced phosphorylation of STING, TBK1 and IRF3 upon Cu-R837@CM treatment (Fig. 3i). Quantitative analysis further demonstrated that the p-STING/STING, p-TBK1/TBK1 and p-IRF3/IRF3 ratios were highest in this group, suggesting strong immunostimulatory signaling activation (Fig. 3j). To provide direct visual evidence for mtDNA recognition by cGAS, immunofluorescence co-localization analysis was performed. Compared with PBS and CuCl2, Cu-R837@CM markedly enhanced the co-localization between mtDNA-associated signals and cGAS, as reflected by the increased Pearson's correlation coefficient (Fig. 3k and l). In addition, to further verify whether mtDNA release contributes to Cu-R837@CM-induced cGAS-STING activation, cells were co-treated with 2′,3′-dideoxycytidine (ddC), an inhibitor of mitochondrial DNA replication. ddC markedly attenuated Cu-R837@CM-induced phosphorylation of STING and TBK1, supporting that mtDNA leakage is involved in the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway (Fig. S13).

Having confirmed robust activation of the cGAS-STING axis in tumor cells, we further evaluated whether these stress signals could be translated into functional immune activation in antigen-presenting cells. Accordingly, conditioned media collected from Cu-R837@CM-treated H22 cells were used to stimulate DC2.4 dendritic cell. Flow-cytometric dot plots (Fig. 3m) and their quantitative summary (Fig. 3n) demonstrated clear upregulation of the co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86, indicative of effective dendritic-cell maturation. Functionally, the ability of tumor-conditioned media to mature DCs implies that Cu-R837@CM may promote an immunogenic tumor milieu, bridging tumor intrinsic stress with antigen-presenting-cell activation. Beyond stress-induced DAMPs and STING pathway activation, the inclusion of R837, likely plays a pivotal role in enhancing the immunostimulatory properties of the nanoparticle system. R837 is known to activate plasmacytoid dendritic cells and trigger robust production of type I interferons and proinflammatory cytokines. Therefore, the presence of R837 in Cu-R837@CM not only supports innate immune activation but may also bridge toward adaptive immune responses by priming antigen-presenting cells more effectively. Taken together, this evidence demonstrates that Cu-R837@CM promote oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cuproptosis, while simultaneously activating the cGas-STING axis and TLR7 signaling pathways. This multifaceted immune modulation culminates in dendritic cell maturation and enhanced immunogenicity, underscoring the therapeutic potential of Cu-R837@CM in cancer immunotherapy.

Cu-R837@CM exhibit tumor-targeted accumulation and enhanced in vivo imaging capability

Efficient systemic delivery requires preferential tumor accumulation and limited off-target distribution [31,43,44], prompting us to evaluate the in vivo biodistribution of Cu-R837@CM. NIR fluorescence imaging was performed to monitor the real-time distribution of IR783-labeled Cu-R837 and Cu-R837@CM after intravenous injection. Compared with uncoated Cu-R837, Cu-R837@CM showed stronger and more sustained fluorescence at the tumor site, particularly between 4 h and 12 h, and the signal remained detectable at 24 h (Fig. 4a). Ex vivo imaging and quantitative analysis of major organs collected at 24 h further confirmed significantly enhanced tumor accumulation of Cu-R837@CM compared with Cu-R837 (Fig. 4b). Although detectable fluorescence was also observed in the liver and kidney, consistent with the typical clearance routes of nanomaterials, Cu-R837@CM displayed the highest tumor-associated signal among the examined tissues. These results suggest that tumor-cell-membrane coating improves tumor accumulation and retention, likely through the combined contribution of passive EPR effects and homotypic membrane-mediated targeting.

Building on the NIR evidence of preferential tumor uptake, we investigated whether copper enrichment within tumors translates into appreciable T1-weighted MRI enhancement. T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) further assessed diagnostic potential (Fig. 4c). In contrast to the PBS group, Cu-R837@CM produced a progressive brightening at the tumor site, most pronounced from 2 h to 12 h. Quantitative analysis of the MRI signal over time (Fig. 4d) showed a 1.9-fold increase at 2 h and a peak enhancement at 12 h, mirroring the fluorescence kinetics. This contrast originates from paramagnetic Cu2+ ions embedded within the nanoplatform, which shorten T1 relaxation times of local protons.

Altogether, the data demonstrate that Cu-R837@CM combine efficient tumor targeting with copper-induced T1 signal enhancement, supporting their application in precise tumor localization and MRI-guided therapeutic strategies.

Transcriptomic analysis reveals the molecular mechanisms of Cu-R837@CM treatment

Mechanistic phenotypes observed in vivo suggested broad immune and metabolic remodeling; therefore, RNA-seq was conducted to capture global transcriptional changes in tumors. Tumor samples were collected from four experimental groups: PBS, CuCl2, Cu-R837, and Cu-R837@CM. Library-level quality control showed comparable expression distributions across groups, as indicated by the boxplot and density plot of log10(TPM+1), and the Pearson correlation heatmap further confirmed good inter-sample consistency (Fig. S14). Comparative analyses, including CuCl2 vs PBS, Cu-R837@CM vs PBS, and Cu-R837@CM vs CuCl2, were then performed to characterize the transcriptional landscape induced by different treatments. Venn diagram analysis identified 6085 commonly deregulated genes across these comparisons, suggesting a shared transcriptional response associated with copper-related cellular stress (Fig. 5a). Volcano plots further visualized the magnitude and direction of differential gene expression among the three comparisons (Fig. 5b). CuCl2 treatment mainly induced transcriptional changes related to metal stress and oxidative responses, whereas Cu-R837@CM produced broader alterations involving immune and inflammatory regulation. Notably, the Cu-R837@CM vs CuCl2 comparison revealed additional gene-expression changes, suggesting that the biomimetic formulation elicited transcriptional responses beyond those induced by free copper ions alone.

CuCl2 treatment was associated with pathways related to DNA repair, cell cycle regulation, and metabolic adaptation. To further focus the transcriptomic analysis on the proposed mechanism, we analyzed selected genes related to cuproptosis, copper handling, oxidative stress, and innate immune activation. Heatmap analysis showed distinct expression changes in genes associated with cuproptosis and mitochondrial metabolism, including DLAT, LIAS, LIPT1, PDHA1, PDHB, and PDHX, as well as oxidative-stress-related genes such as HMOX1, NQO1, TXNIP, GCLC, GCLM, GPX1, and GPX2 (Fig. 5d). In addition, immune-related genes such as TBK1, IRF3, CXCL11, PSMB8, and PSMB9 were also altered, supporting the involvement of innate immune and antigen-processing responses after Cu-R837@CM treatment. These pathways collectively indicate that Cu-R837@CM induces a coordinated transcriptional program involving metal-ion stress, redox imbalance, antioxidant defense, and inflammatory immune activation. Although canonical cGAS-STING and type I IFN gene sets did not reach statistical significance in the current bulk RNA-seq dataset, these transcriptomic findings, together with our experimental evidence of mtDNA leakage, cGAMP production, mtDNA/cGAS co-localization, STING/TBK1/IRF3 phosphorylation, and ddC rescue, support Cu-R837@CM-induced activation of cGAS-STING-related innate immune signaling.

Collectively, this focused transcriptomic analysis supports the dual mechanism of Cu-R837@CM, in which copper stress and oxidative damage are coupled with inflammatory immune activation, thereby contributing to tumor immune microenvironment remodeling and enhanced antitumor efficacy.

In vivo antitumor effect of Cu-R837@CM

Guided by our in-vitro data showing that Cu-R837@CM induces strong oxidative stress, mitochondrial depolarization, DLAT aggregation, and cGAS-STING activation, we next tested therapeutic efficacy in vivo. A BALB/c subcutaneous H22 model was established (workflow in Fig. 6a). Mice were randomized into four groups (PBS, CuCl2, Cu-R837, Cu-R837@CM) and received i.v. injections on days 1, 3, and 5 at the doses specified in Methods. Tumor volume was recorded every other day through day 15. Cu-R837@CM significantly restrained tumor growth relative to all controls (Fig. 6b), with the lowest end-point tumor weight (Fig. 6e) and representative ex vivo tumors confirming the macroscopic reduction (Fig. 6d). The tumor-growth inhibition (TGI) rate was highest for Cu-R837@CM (Fig. 6f). For transparency, individual tumor trajectories for each mouse are provided in Fig. S18 (one line per animal), further illustrating the consistent response within the Cu-R837@CM cohort.

In parallel, we evaluated systemic tolerability and potential off-target toxicity under the same dosing regimen. Treatment was well tolerated. Body weight curves remained stable and comparable among groups (Fig. 6c), and no treatment-related clinical signs were observed. Histopathology of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) revealed no detectable lesions or inflammatory infiltrates (Fig. S19). Hematology (WBC, RBC) and serum biochemistry (ALT, AST, creatinine, urea) remained within normal limits (Fig. S20), indicating preserved hepatic and renal function. A red-blood-cell hemolysis assay showed negligible hemolysis across the examined concentration range (Fig. S21), confirming good blood compatibility. Together these datasets substantiate the systemic safety of the platform under the tested regimen.

To connect the macroscopic antitumor efficacy with in vivo mechanism, we next examined innate immune activation and tumor pathology readouts. Mechanistically, Cu-R837@CM significantly elevated circulating IFN-β levels (Fig. 6g). As a canonical downstream output of cGAS-STING signaling, type I interferons are known to promote dendritic-cell maturation, enhance antigen presentation, and prime CD8+ T-cell responses, thereby linking innate sensing to adaptive antitumor immunity, this finding is consistent with in vivo innate immune activation and aligns with our in vitro observation of STING phosphorylation [[45], [46], [47]]. Tumor histology provided concordant evidence of efficacy (Fig. 6h): H&E sections showed reduced cellularity and increased necrotic areas; TUNEL staining revealed widespread DNA fragmentation; and the proliferation index Ki-67 was markedly decreased. Semi-quantitative analyses of Ki-67 and TUNEL (Fig. 6i and j) supported significant suppression of proliferation and amplification of tumor cell death. Notably, DLAT aggregation increased in Cu-R837@CM-treated tumors (bottom row in Fig. 6h), consistent with cuproptosis engaged by copper overload in vivo and mirroring the cellular findings. The concordance between tumor suppression, DLAT aggregation, and increased IFN-β provides a coherent in vivo mechanistic chain linking cuproptosis-like stress to STING-associated innate activation.

Type I interferon signalling downstream of cGAS-STING is a key link between innate sensing and T-cell priming, therefore, we further profiled intratumoral and systemic immune populations. Because effective antitumor immunity requires coordinated innate and adaptive responses, we further profiled leukocyte populations by flow cytometry. Within tumors, the frequencies of CD3+CD4+ and CD3+CD8+ T cells among CD45+ cells were significantly elevated after Cu-R837@CM treatment (Fig. 7a–d), indicating enhanced T-cell infiltration into the tumor microenvironment. Systemically, splenic immunophenotyping showed increased CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell pools (Fig. 7e–h), together with a higher proportion of CD80+CD86+ dendritic cells (Fig. 7i–j), suggestive of active antigen presentation and ongoing priming in secondary lymphoid organs. In addition to enhancing effector immune-cell infiltration, Cu-R837@CM markedly attenuated myeloid-derived immunosuppression within the tumor microenvironment. Flow-cytometric analysis showed that Cu-R837@CM treatment reduced the proportion of tumor-infiltrating MDSCs, including both PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs, with the lowest total MDSC frequency observed in the Cu-R837@CM group (Fig. 7k and l). Additional flow-cytometric and immunofluorescence analyses further confirmed the reduced accumulation of MDSC-associated populations after Cu-R837@CM treatment (Figs. S22& S23). These findings indicate that Cu-R837@CM not only promotes T-cell infiltration and DC maturation, but also alleviates MDSC-mediated immunosuppression, thereby remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment toward an antitumor state. Mechanistically, these immune readouts are consistent with the platform design: copper-driven mitochondrial damage and cytosolic DNA stress provide DAMPs to engage the cGAS-STING pathway, while R837, as a TLR7 agonist, augments endosomal innate immune sensing and type I IFN/NF-κB signaling, jointly promoting DC maturation and T-cell activation. Future studies examining Treg infiltration, T-cell exhaustion markers, and effector cytokine production would further clarify whether Cu-R837@CM primarily enhances immune-cell infiltration, reinvigorates antitumor function, or both.

Taken together, Cu-R837@CM delivers robust in-vivo antitumor efficacy without measurable systemic toxicity. The transcriptomic, biochemical, histological, and immunological evidence support a dual-action mechanism: copper-mediated oxidative stress/cuproptosis plus potent immunomodulation that culminates in enhanced DC activation and strong CD8+/CD4+ T-cell responses. These findings highlight the translational promise of Cu-R837@CM as an immunogenic nanoplatform for hepatocellular carcinoma therapy. Nevertheless, subcutaneous xenograft-like settings cannot fully recapitulate the hepatic immune niche, future validation in orthotopic or autochthonous HCC models would be valuable for clinical relevance.

Combination of Cu-R837@CM with anti-PD-L1 antibody achieves synergistic tumor suppression in vivo

Since Cu-R837@CM induced robust innate immune activation and T-cell infiltration, we next asked whether this immune activation was accompanied by adaptive immune resistance. Western blot analysis showed that Cu-R837 and Cu-R837@CM markedly upregulated PD-L1 expression, with the highest level observed in the Cu-R837@CM group (Fig. S24). This finding suggests that although Cu-R837@CM activates antitumor immunity, it may also trigger a compensatory PD-L1 inhibitory feedback, thereby providing a mechanistic rationale for combination with PD-L1 blockade.

Based on this rationale, we further evaluated whether Cu-R837@CM could sensitize tumors to anti-PD-L1 therapy. H22-bearing BALB/c mice were treated with Cu-R837@CM (i.v. on days 1, 3, and 5) and/or anti-PD-L1 antibody (i.v. on days 2 and 4, 5 mg/kg), as outlined in Fig. 8a. Tumor growth and survival were monitored to assess the therapeutic efficacy. Both Cu-R837@CM and anti-PD-L1 monotherapies delayed tumor progression compared with PBS; however, the combination treatment produced near-complete tumor growth arrest, with tumor volumes remaining close to baseline through day 18 (Fig. 8b). Consistently, ex vivo tumor images showed the smallest tumor burden in the combination group (Fig. 8c). Kaplan–Meier analysis further demonstrated a marked survival benefit (Fig. 8d): all mice receiving Cu-R837@CM plus anti-PD-L1 survived until day 50, whereas all animals in the PBS and anti-PD-L1 groups succumbed by approximately day 35; Cu-R837@CM alone provided a partial survival benefit, with approximately 60% of mice surviving beyond day 40. These results demonstrate that Cu-R837@CM effectively sensitizes tumors to PD-L1 blockade and achieves superior antitumor efficacy compared with either monotherapy.

We then examined tumor proliferation and immune infiltration to understand the basis of the improved therapeutic outcome. Mechanistically, the combination elicited pronounced intratumoral apoptosis and immune infiltration (Fig. 8e). TUNEL-positive nuclei occupied ∼48% of the tumor area (∼5-fold over PBS), whereas the proliferative Ki-67 index fell from 63 ± 6% (PBS) to 11 ± 3% (combination). The copper-responsive mitochondrial enzyme DLAT showed robust aggregation in the combo group, consistent with efficient cuproptosis in vivo. Immunofluorescence revealed dense CD8+ (red) and CD4+ (green) T-cell infiltrates throughout the tumor parenchyma; bar-graph quantification confirmed significant increases in CD8 (Fig. 8f) and CD4 (Fig. 8g) compared with either monotherapy or PBS.

Systemically, day-18 sera from the combination group exhibited strong cytokine elevation by ELISA (Fig. 8h), including IFN-β (∼3.3-fold), IFN-α (∼5.1-fold), TNF-α (∼4.0-fold), IL-1β (∼6.2-fold), and IL-6 (∼5.8-fold) versus PBS. The cytokine elevation indicates broad immune activation, but also highlights the importance of dose optimization to maintain efficacy while avoiding excessive systemic inflammation. Although the elevated serum cytokines indicate enhanced systemic antitumor immune activation, they also highlight the need to evaluate potential immune-related toxicity. Therefore, H&E staining of major organs was performed after combination treatment. No obvious histopathological abnormalities, including tissue necrosis, hemorrhage, or severe inflammatory infiltration, were observed in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, or kidney across the treatment groups (Fig. S25).

Collectively, these data show that Cu-R837@CM functions as a danger-signal amplifier that converts otherwise “cold” tumors into PD-L1-responsive lesions, achieving durable tumor control and prolonged survival. The marked improvement over either monotherapy underscores the translational promise of integrating Cu-R837@CM with anti-PD-L1 checkpoint blockade.

Compared with previously reported copper-based nanoplatforms, which mainly exploit copper for chemodynamic therapy, oxidative stress amplification, ferroptosis/cuproptosis induction, or photodynamic/photothermal therapy [13], Cu-R837@CM is designed to convert copper-induced mitochondrial damage into an endogenous innate immune trigger. Previous studies have shown that copper overload can induce cuproptosis by targeting lipoylated TCA cycle proteins and causing proteotoxic stress, and recent evidence further indicates that cuproptosis can activate innate immune responses through the cGAS-STING pathway [48]. Unlike conventional STING-activating nanotherapies that directly deliver exogenous STING agonists such as cGAMP/CDNs [49], MSA-2 [50], or SR-717 [51], Cu-R837@CM activates STING through tumor-intrinsic mtDNA leakage and cGAS recognition. In addition, the incorporation of R837 provides TLR7-mediated co-stimulation to enhance dendritic-cell maturation and T-cell priming[52], while homologous tumor-cell-membrane coating improves tumor accumulation and retention [31]. Therefore, Cu-R837@CM integrates biomimetic tumor targeting, copper-triggered cuproptosis-associated mitochondrial stress, endogenous mtDNA/cGAS-STING activation, and TLR7 co-stimulation into a single nanoplatform, distinguishing it from existing copper-based or STING agonist-loaded nanotherapies.

Despite these encouraging results, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although Cu-R837@CM showed favorable tumor accumulation and short-term biosafety under the current therapeutic regimen, comprehensive pharmacokinetic profiling, including blood circulation half-life, clearance kinetics, tissue accumulation dynamics, and long-term toxicity, remains to be systematically investigated. In addition, R837 bioavailability in tumors and major organs was not directly quantified by LC-MS/MS, and the relationship between local R837 exposure and TLR7 mediated immune activation requires further validation. Moreover, CD4+ and CD8+ T cell depletion experiments are needed to determine the relative contribution of adaptive immunity to the therapeutic outcome. Quantitative correlations between MRI signal intensity and pharmacodynamic indicators, together with validation in orthotopic or autochthonous HCC models, dose escalation studies, and long-term evaluation of immune related adverse events, will be required to better define the translational potential and therapeutic window of Cu-R837@CM based combination therapy.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a biomimetic copper nanoadjuvant, Cu-R837@CM, as an integrated immunotherapeutic platform for HCC. Through homologous tumor cell membrane mediated targeting, Cu-R837@CM enhanced tumor accumulation and cellular uptake, enabling efficient intratumoral delivery of copper and R837. Mechanistically, intracellular copper accumulation induced mitochondrial stress, DLAT aggregation, and cuproptosis associated tumor cell damage, which further promoted mtDNA leakage and endogenous activation of the cGAS-STING pathway. Meanwhile, R837 acted as a TLR7 agonist to amplify innate immune activation, promote dendritic cell maturation, and strengthen T cell priming. These coordinated effects remodeled the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by enhancing CD4+ and CD8+ T cell infiltration while reducing MDSCs and Foxp3+ Tregs. Moreover, Cu-R837@CM induced PD-L1 upregulation in tumor cells, revealing adaptive immune inhibitory feedback and providing a mechanistic rationale for combination with PD-L1 blockade. As a result, Cu-R837@CM combined with anti-PD-L1 achieved enhanced tumor suppression and prolonged survival. Overall, this study provides a rational strategy that couples copper induced tumor cell damage with TLR7 and cGAS-STING mediated innate immune activation, offering a promising nanoplatform to improve immune checkpoint blockade therapy for HCC.
