Integrative transcriptomic and machine learning analysis identifies key extracellular matrix-related genes in diabetic retinopathy
Authors: Wang Xin, Wang Ying, Feng Qiang, Chen Yan, Zhang Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling contributes to retinal vascular basement membrane thickening, an early structural hallmark of diabetic retinopathy (DR). This study aimed to identify key ECM-related genes (ECMGs) associated with DR. Transcriptomic data of DR and ECMGs from MatrixDB were integrated to identify differentially expressed ECMGs. Six machine learning (ML) models, including Extra Trees (ET), Logistic Regression, Adaptive Boosting, Random Forest, Extreme Gradient Boosting, and naive Bayes classifier, were employed to construct DR classification models, with SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) used to interpret feature contributions. Functional enrichment analysis using GSEA and immune infiltration analysis using CIBERSORT were conducted to explore the potential mechanisms by which key ECMGs regulate DR. Regulatory networks were constructed using predicted miRNAs, lncRNAs, and transcription factors (TFs) via the ENCORI, miRWalk, and miRNet databases. Drug-key ECMGs-DM-related diseases interactions were further explored using the DGIdb and CTD databases. Nine candidate ECMGs were identified by overlapping 356 DM-associated DEGs, 1,626 DR-associated DEGs, and 1,023 ECMGs, including CILP2, FN1, DEFA3, COL17A1, CRISP3, TPSAB1, SFRP1, GPHA2, and ECM2. Among the six ML algorithms, the ET classifier exhibited the best overall performance, and five ECMGs (SFRP1, CILP2, FN1, TPSAB1, and ECM2) with non-zero SHAP values were retained as key genes. These genes showed distinct expression patterns across the healthy, DM, and DR groups, and were enriched in neural-related pathways, such as axon guidance, glycosphingolipid biosynthesis ganglio series, and neuroactive ligand receptor interaction. Immune profiling and correlation analysis revealed that FN1, TPSAB1, and CILP2 were correlated with memory/naive B cells, CD8 + T cells, activated memory CD4 + T cells, Tregs, monocytes, and neutrophils. Additionally, the ceRNA network contained five miRNAs, 7 lncRNAs, and two ECMGs, and further regulatory and pharmacologic analysis further linked key ECMGs to specific TFs, drugs, and diabetes-related diseases. This study identified SFRP1, CILP2, FN1, TPSAB1, and ECM2 as key ECMGs in DR, revealing their coordinated involvement in ECM remodeling, neural signaling, and immune modulation. Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia and is a growing global health burden1. The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) estimates that over 500 million adults aged 20–79 years are currently living with DM, a figure projected to reach 853 million by 2050, with the greatest burden in low- and middle-income countries2. Diabetic retinopathy (DR), one of the most common microvascular complications of DM, is a leading cause of preventable blindness in working-age adults3. Approximately 30% of individuals with DM develop DR4, with 4–8% progressing to vision-threatening, proliferative stages5,6. The pathogenesis of DR is multifactorial, involving chronic hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress, inflammation, and activation of various signaling pathways, including the AGE-RAGE, polyol, hexosamine, and protein kinase C pathways7. These processes lead to pericyte loss, basement membrane thickening, endothelial dysfunction, and blood-retinal barrier breakdown, ultimately causing retinal ischemia, neovascularization, and macular edema8–10.

Emerging evidence highlights the importance of extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling as a pivotal factor in the vascular and tissue complications of DM11. Persistent hyperglycemia drives excessive deposition and abnormal cross-linking of ECM components, such as collagen, fibronectin, and laminin, resulting in fibrosis, vascular wall stiffening, and impaired cell-matrix communication in organs, including the kidney and retina12,13. In DR, ECM dysregulation manifests as early thickening of the retinal vascular basement membrane, mediated by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β). These alterations create an imbalance between ECM synthesis and degradation, promoting fibrosis and the formation of epiretinal membranes during the proliferative stages14. Furthermore, aberrant ECM remodeling amplifies the effects of inflammatory cytokines and growth factors, aggravating endothelial dysfunction, pericyte loss, vascular leakage, and neovascularization15,16. Proteomic analyses of the vitreous humor have also identified dysregulated ECM proteins linked to oxidative stress, immune activation, and neurodegeneration in DR17. Beyond diabetes, age-related changes in ECM composition, such as reduced elasticity and advanced glycosylation, further weaken neurovascular coupling and barrier integrity, accelerating DR progression18. Despite these advances, the precise identity of key ECM-related genes and their mechanistic contributions to DR pathogenesis remain poorly defined, representing a critical barrier to developing targeted diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Peripheral blood transcriptomes are an accessible source of systemic molecular information that can reveal the circulating pathways associated with tissue pathology19. Although blood does not substitute for retinal tissue, blood-derived signatures can identify candidate biomarkers and systemic mechanisms that merit tissue-based validation20.

In this study, we integrated a curated ECM gene panel with peripheral blood transcriptomic data, and applied multiple machine learning and explainable AI approaches to identify ECM-related genes associated with DR. These genes were further characterized by pathway enrichment, immune infiltration, and regulatory network analyses to elucidate their potential biological significance. Finally, the expression of key candidate genes was experimentally validated by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), supporting their potential role in DR pathogenesis.Meanwhile, using transcriptomic classification performance to construct a DR prediction model is an important supplement to the existing field of DR detection.

Methodology

Data collection

The transcriptomic profiles of blood samples from 50 healthy controls, 74 diabetic patients without diabetic retinopathy (DM), and 69 DR patients in the GSE221521 dataset were sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE221521 dataset was used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and construct a model for predicting the risk of DR. A total of 1,023 extracellular matrix (ECM)-related genes (ECMGs) were sourced from MatrixDB database (http://matrixdb.ibcp.fr) and selected H. sapiens species.

Identification of ECM related differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in DR

The DEGs between DM and controls (DEGs1), as well as between DM and DR (DEGs2) were analyzed using “DESeq2” package (version 1.46.0) in R software (version 4.4.0) with the following criteria: |log2FoldChange (FC)| > 0.5, p < 0.05 in the GSE221521 dataset, and the DEGs were visualized using a volcano plot. To identify the ECM-related DEGs in DR (DE-ECMGs), DEGs1, DEGs2, and ECMGs were overlapped and visualized using “ggvenn” package (version 0.1.10). The “ClusterProfiler” package (version 4.14.6) was used to perform functional enrichment analysis, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) annotations. GO terms were categorized into biological processes (BPs), molecular functions (MFs), and cellular components (CCs).

Machine learning algorithms and model interpretation

To infer the risk of DR development among individuals with DM, we constructed a classification model trained to distinguish between patients with and without DR. Patients from GSE221521 were randomly divided into training and testing cohorts at a 7:3 ratio, respectively. Six machine learning (ML) algorithms, including Extra Trees (ET, version 1.0.5), Logistic Regression (LR, caret version 7.0–1), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), Random Forest (RF, version 4.7–1.1), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost, version 1.7.8.1), and naive Bayes classifier (NBC, e1071 version 7.0–14), were trained to identify features predictive of DR status. Briefly, the ET algorithm builds an ensemble of decision trees by introducing randomness in both feature selection and split points at each node, thereby increasing inter-tree diversity and enhancing the generalization capability through variance reduction21. The LR algorithm is a classic linear prediction method that relieson regression coefficients22. AdaBoost, a boosting ensemble algorithm, generates weak classifiers (commonly decision trees) and combines their predictions using an additive model to obtain the final result23. The RF algorithm is a widely recognized multi-class tree method that achieves high accuracy and rapid independent learning across various datasets by combining decision trees through majority voting24. The XGBoost algorithm constructs K regression trees to minimize the difference between the predicted and actual values while maximizing the generalization capability25. The NBC algorithm is a supervised learning algorithm based on the Bayes theorem, with attributes that independently affect the classification results26. Through ten-fold cross-validation, we determined the optimal hyperparameters of the models (Table S1). The model performance was comprehensively evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) algorithm was applied to interpret the optimal machine learning model. Feature importance values were assessed based on SHAP values. Genes with non-zero importance values were retained as key ECMGs in the DR.

Characterization of key ECMGs in DR

To characterize these key ECMGs, the tissue and sub-cellular distribution of the key DR-ECMGs was first explored using the BioGPS database (http://biogps.org) and GeneCards (www.genecards.org), respectively. Specifically, we queried BioGPS for each key ECMG and selected tissues with a correlation cut-off value > 0.9. The top five correlated tissues for each gene were extracted. The resulting gene-tissue associations were visualized as a network using Cytoscape. For the sub-cellular location of key ECMGs, we queried the GeneCards database and extracted localization information with a confidence score of 5. The resulting gene-tissue and gene-organelle associations were visualized as networks using Cytoscape. We, then examined their expression patterns in blood tissues from healthy individuals, DM without DR, and DR individuals. Subsequently, to identify the mechanisms related to key ECMGs in the development of DR, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of each gene using “c2.cp.kegg.v7.5.1.symbols.gmt” as the reference gene set. Besides, GeneMANIA (http://genemania.org) was used to identify the interaction network among key ECMGs and predict the function of key DR-ECMGs. We also measured the functional similarity among key DR-ECMGs using the GOSemSim package (version 2.32.0). In addition, the proportion of the 22 immune cells in GSE221521 was calculated using the CIBERSORT algorithm (version 2.1.0). The correlation between key ECMGs and immune cells was analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation.

Construction of regulatory network of key DR-ECMGs

The ENCORI (https://rnasysu.com/encori/) and miRWalk (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/) database were used to screen key ECMGs-associated miRNAs. The intersection of the predicted miRNAs from the two databases was retained. Meanwhile, lncRNAs targeting overlapped miRNAs were predicted using ENCORI. Additionally, transcription factors (TFs) that regulate the expression of key ECMGs were predicted using the miRNet database (https://www.mirnet.ca/). Moreover, we utilized the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb) (https://dgidb.org/) to screen potential therapeutic drugs/compounds targeting key ECMGs in the treatment of DR. In addition, we explored whether key ECMGs were involved in other diseases related to DM using the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD, https://ctdbase.org/). Finally, the networks of lncRNA-miRNA-key ECMGs, TFs-key ECMGs, and drugs-key ECMGs, drugs-ECMGs-DM diseases were constructed and visualized using Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1).

Sample collection and qRT‒PCR validation

All methods involving human participants were performed in accordance with the relevant ethical guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Between July 2025 to August 2025, we collected whole blood samples from ten patients with DM and ten patients with DR at Tianjin hospital of ITCWM Nankai Hospital, which was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Tianjin hospital of ITCWM nankai Hospital, and the Ethics number was NKYY_YXKT_IRB_2025_083_01. Informed consent was obtained from all the individuals involved in the study. Total RNA was separated using TRIzol reagent (CAT#DP424). The thermocycling conditions were as follows: an initial denaturation at 95℃ for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94℃ for 5 s and annealing and extension at 60℃ for 30s. Gene expression changes were quantified using the comparative CT (2 − ΔΔCt) method with β-actin as the normalization control. The primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using the R software. Differential significance was assessed using the Wilcoxon signed rank test, with a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Result

Nine DE-ECMGs were identified in DR

First, based on re-analysis of the GSE221521 dataset, we obtained 356 upregulated and 401 downregulated DEGs between patients with DM and healthy controls (Fig. 1A). In parallel, a comparison between the DM and DR groups yielded 1,392 upregulated and 234 downregulated DEGs (Fig. 1B). To further screen for DE-ECMGs involved in DR progression, we intersected these DEGs with a curated list of ECMGs, resulting in nine overlapping candidates: cartilage intermediate layer protein-like protein 2(CILP2), fibronectin 1 (FN1), defensin alpha 3 (DEFA3), collagen type XVII alpha 1 chain (COL17A1), cysteine-rich secretory protein 3 (CRISP3), tryptase alpha/beta 1 (TPSAB1), secreted frizzled-related protein 1 (SFRP1), glycoprotein hormone subunit alpha 2 (GPHA2), and extracellular matrix protein 2 (ECM2) (Fig. 1C). GO enrichment analysis revealed that these candidate ECMGs were mainly involved in ECM organization, structure and function-related BPs, including cell-matrix adhesion and cell-substrate junction assembly, collagen-containing extracellular matrix and vesicle lumen CCs, and MFs of heparin binding and glycosaminoglycan binding (Fig. 1D). In addition, KEGG pathway analysis showed that these ECMs were associated with bacterial invasion of epithelial cells, ECM-receptor interaction, and the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications (Fig. 1E). These findings suggest that candidate ECMGs may serve as important mediators linking ECM remodelling to the onset of DR in diabetic patients, potentially by modulating tissue structure, epithelial barrier integrity, and inflammation-associated signaling pathways.

Five key DR-ECMGs were identified by machine learning

To further screen key ECMGs, we evaluated their predictive potential using six machine learning algorithms: ET, RF, LR, XGBoost, AdaBoost, and NBC. In the training cohort, ET, RF, XGBoost, and AdaBoost performed better than LR and NBC (Fig. 2A). The AUC values for ET, RF, XGBoost, AdaBoost, LR, and NBC were 1.000, 1.000, 1.000, 1.000, 0.763, and 0.731, respectively (Fig. 2B). In the testing cohort, LR and ET demonstrated superior performance compared with the other four machine learning models (Fig. 2C). The AUCs for LR, ET, RF, XGBoost, AdaBoost, and NBC were 0.766, 0.741, 0.686, 0.625, 0.620, and 0.600, respectively (Fig. 2D). Between LR and ET, ET had higher accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score. Thus, ET was selected as the optimal model to predict the risk of DR. To further interpret the contribution of each candidate ECMGs to the risk prediction of DR, SHAP analysis was performed. The radar plot illustrated the relative importance of the nine ECMGs based on the normalized SHAP values. Among them, SFRP1 showed the highest contribution, followed by CILP2, TPSAB1, FN1, and ECM2, indicating their stronger influence in distinguishing DR from non-DR patients. Other genes, including GPHA2, DEFA3, CRISP3, and COL17A1, showed negligible importance (Fig. 3A). Similar results were obtained in the SHAP summary plot, which ranked the absolute SHAP values of the candidate genes, including SFRP1 (SHAP values: 0.040), CILP2 (0.030), TPSAB1 (0.015), FN1 (0.010), and ECM2 (0.010) (Fig. 3B). The expression levels of TPSAB1, FN1, CILP2, and ECM2 were significantly decreased in patients with DM compared to healthy controls but markedly increased in patients with DR relative to patients with DM. In contrast, SFRP1 showed the opposite trend, with its expression markedly elevated in the DM groups compared to the controls, but downregulated in DR relative to DM (Fig. 3C-D). Furthermore, for preliminary exploratory validation, consistent findings were observed in an independent, small-scale clinical cohort. The expression level of SFRP1 was significantly lower in the DR group than in the DM group, whereas the expression levels of the other four genes were significantly higher in the DR group (Fig. 3E). These results suggested that SFRP1, CILP2, TPSAB1, FN1, and ECM2 may serve as key ECMGs in the transition from DM to DR, potentially representing early molecular indicators of retinopathy.

Key ECMGs may cooperatively regulate DR via interactional crosstalk

Next, we explored the potential subcellular and tissue distribution of key ECMGs and found that key ECMGs were localized to the extracellular compartment. Additionally, FN1 was enriched in the endoplasmic reticulum and plasma membrane, and SFRP1 was detected in the cytosol and plasma membrane (Fig. 4A). Meanwhile, both SFRP1 and TPSAB1 showed high expression in the retina, suggesting their direct relevance to DR pathogenesis. In addition, SFRP1 was expressed in barrier and secretory tissues, such as the skin, salivary gland, and uterus, whereas TPSAB1 was enriched in the lung, thymus, and prostate, indicating their potential involvement in inflammatory and immune responses. Furthermore, FN1 is expressed in smooth muscle cells, cardiac myocytes, and adipocytes, supporting its role in vascular remodeling and metabolic stress. CILP2 and ECM2, although not retina-enriched, were associated with tissues such as the adrenal gland, spinal cord, and atrioventricular node, suggesting additional roles in neuroendocrine regulation (Fig. 4B). To explore the potential cooperative mechanisms among key ECMGs, we assessed their functional similarities using GOSemSim analysis. As shown in Fig. 4C, these genes shared moderate to high functional similarity scores, with FN1 and SFRP1 displaying broader functional overlaps. We further constructed an interaction network using GeneMANIA. All key ECMGs exhibited dense interconnections with each other and additional ECM- or inflammation-related proteins, such as COL1A1, MMP1, WNT2, and CEACAM1 (Fig. 4D). These interactions spanned multiple layers, including physical binding, co-expression networks, and genetic binding, and were functionally enriched in serine-type peptidase activity, serine hydrolase activity, and extracellular matrix organization.

Key ECMGs may regulate DR via metabolism, neural-associated pathways and inflammatory responses

To further elucidate the mechanisms by which key ECMGs influence DR progression, we performed GSEA to identify the biological pathways associated with their expression levels (Fig. 5A-E). Interestingly, low expression of ECM2 and FN1 was significantly associated with enrichment of gene sets involved in oxidative phosphorylation, proteasomes, ribosomes, and spliceosomes, suggesting potential links to metabolic dysregulation. The remaining ECMGs were enriched in neural-related pathways, implicating a possible role in neurovascular regulation. Specifically, low expression of TPSAB1 and SFRP1 was associated with axon guidance and glycosphingolipid biosynthesis, respectively. High CILP2 expression corresponds to neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions, indicating its potential involvement in retinal neuronal signaling. Given the established role of immune dysregulation in DR pathogenesis27, we assessed immune cell infiltration in DR samples (Fig. 6A) and evaluated the relationship between the expression of key ECMGs and DR immune cell infiltration. We observed that FN1 expression was positively correlated with naive B cells and monocytes, but negatively correlated with memory B cells. TPSAB1 expression showed a positive association with memory B cells, CD8 + T cells, and regulatory T cells, while demonstrating negative correlations with neutrophils, activated memory CD4 + T cells, and gamma delta T cells. In addition, CILP2 expression was negatively associated with gamma delta T cells (Fig. 6B). These findings suggest that key ECMGs act in concert to regulate DR through metabolism, neural pathways, and immune microenvironment reprogramming.

Construction of the regulatory and pharmacological network of key ECMGs

To uncover the upstream regulators of key ECMG expression, we integrated predictions from the miRNet, ENCORI, and miRWalk databases to identify non-coding RNA and TF interactions (Fig. 7A and B). A total of five miRNA-mRNAs regulatory pairs were identified, including hsa-miR-200b-3p-FN1, hsa-miR-200c-3p-FN1, hsa-miR-328-3p-SFRP1, hsa-miR-744-5p-SFRP1, and hsa-miR-27a-3p-SFRP1. Further analysis revealed that three lincRNAs (XIST, OIP5-AS1, and AC005261.1) targeted FN1-related miRNAs and five lncRNAs (XIST, FOXD2-AS1, AC018521.1, RBPMS-AS1, and AC016629.3) targeting SFRP1-related miRNAs. We also predicted 14 TFs targeting FN1, 12 TFs targeting ECM2, 11 TFs targeting TPSAB1, 8 TFs targeting CILP2, and 6 TFs targeting SFRP1. Among these, 13 TFs (JUN, CREB1, FOXC1, YY1, NFIC, SRF, PPARG, HNF4A, NFKB1, RELA, STAT1, PRDM1, and ESR1) were shared across multiple key ECMGs, indicating possible coordinated transcriptional regulation of these genes. To evaluate the translational potential, we queried the DGIDB for drug-gene interactions. A total of 13 drugs targeting FN1, such as L19IL2, L19TNFA, and AS1409; 11 drugs targeting ECM2, such as linifanib, altiratinib, and angiopoietin-1; and two drugs (gm-csf and thrombin) targeting TPSAB1 were identified (Fig. 7C). Based on this, we constructed a disease-gene-drug network (Fig. 7D), highlighting six DM relevant diseases, including type 1/2 diabetes mellitus, diabetic nephropathies, diabetic angiopathies, gestational diabetes, and nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, that are potentially modulated via FN1, ECM2, and TPSAB1. This network indicates that targeting key ECMGs may provide therapeutic avenues for managing DR and related complications.

Discussion

DR is a leading cause of vision loss in working-age adults, largely driven by hyperglycemia-induced microvascular damage and ECM remodeling15,28. Thickened basement membranes, fibrosis, and altered vascular integrity are central features of this process; however, the contribution of ECMGs to DR pathogenesis has not been fully elucidated. In this study, we used an integrative, data-driven strategy to identify ECM-related genes specifically associated with the progression from diabetes to DR. By sequentially comparing DM versus healthy controls and DR versus DM, we aimed to distinguish diabetes-related transcriptomic alterations from those uniquely linked to retinopathy development, thereby prioritizing ECM genes involved in DR pathogenesis rather than generalized diabetic effects. We combined a curated ECM gene set with peripheral blood transcriptomic profiles and used machine learning–based feature prioritization. This approach identified a small set of robust candidate genes (SFRP1, CILP2, FN1, TPSAB1, and ECM2) associated with the progression from diabetes to DR. Among the evaluated models, the ET classifier exhibited the most favorable diagnostic performance for distinguishing DR from DM. It achieved an AUC of 1 in the training cohort, with superior accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score compared with other algorithms. In the testing cohort, Extra Trees achieved an AUC of 0.741. This was slightly lower than the LR (AUC = 0.766). However, Extra Trees still performed well in accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score. Preliminary qPCR validation in an independent sample set revealed expression trends consistent with transcriptomic analyses. This provides initial experimental support for these ECMGs and underscores the exploratory and hypothesis-generating nature of this validation.

The identified key ECMGs align with emerging evidence of their roles in diabetic complications, particularly in DR. SFRP1, a modulator of Wnt signaling, has been implicated in photoreceptor degeneration and apoptosis in retinal diseases, where its dysregulation exacerbates Wnt pathway activation, contributing to vascular leakage and neurodegeneration in DR29. In broader diabetic contexts, SFRP1 influences choroidal endothelial cell function via interactions with cochlin and CaMKII, potentially aggravating retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and vascular pathology30. Our GSEA findings of SFRP1 enrichment in glycosphingolipid biosynthesis and axon guidance pathways resonate with studies showing early distal axonopathy in the visual pathway under diabetic conditions, where hyperglycemia induces neuronal apoptosis and disrupts neurovascular coupling31,32. CILP2, associated with insulin resistance in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), shows elevated circulating levels in prediabetic and diabetic states, correlating with impaired glucose tolerance33,34. Although less studied in DR, its enrichment in neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathways suggests a role in retinal neuronal signaling, potentially linking metabolic stress to neurodegeneration observed in diabetic polyneuropathy and optic nerve damage35,36. TPSAB1 is genetically linked to elevated serum tryptase levels in multisystem disorders; however, its direct involvement in DR appears to be novel. However, tryptase’s role in inflammation and mast cell activation may contribute to microvascular instability, aligning with our GSEA results in axon guidance, which could exacerbate retinal axon degeneration in diabetes32,37,38. FN1 is a well-established mediator of ECM thickening and fibrosis11,39,40 with additional links to metabolic dysregulation through oxidative phosphorylation pathways41. ECM2 may contribute to fibrotic remodeling and disrupt tissue homeostasis, consistent with findings in other diabetic complications11,42,43. A recent large-scale plasma proteomic profiling of incident DR identified specific protein-level changes, including dysregulation of ECM organization, immune activation, and neurovascular dysfunction44. These results support the notion that transcriptomic alterations seen in ECMGs, such as FN1, SFRP1, and ECM2, may correspond to functionally relevant molecular changes during DR progression. Further biological and pathological research integrating the latest proteomics evidence will be crucial in improving our understanding of the identified ECM-related genes in the future.

Because this study used peripheral blood transcriptomic data, the identified ECM-related genes should be viewed primarily as systemic molecular signatures linked to DR rather than as direct markers of retinal-specific mechanisms. ECM remodeling, neurovascular signaling, and immune dysregulation are well-known contributors to retinal pathology in DR; however, blood-derived expression profiles are more likely to reflect circulating inflammation, metabolic stress, and systemic extracellular matrix remodeling, which are characteristic of disease progression7,45,46. Thus, the differential expression of key ECMGs may indicate broader disease severity, chronic hyperglycemia, systemic inflammation, or other metabolic disturbances, rather than effects limited to DR. Patients with DR generally have a longer diabetes duration, poorer glycemic control, and higher inflammatory burden, all of which can influence blood gene expression47,48. Therefore, the identified ECM signatures likely reflect an integrated systemic response to advanced diabetic complications, with retinal pathology emerging downstream of these systemic changes.

We propose a working hypothesis that integrates the expression directionality, pathway enrichment, and immune correlations. FN1, ECM2, TPSAB1, and CILP2 were predominantly upregulated in DR compared to DM. Together, these genes may cooperatively promote pathological ECM remodeling, immune activation, and neurovascular dysfunction through fibrosis-related, metabolic, and inflammatory pathways. In contrast, SFRP1 exhibited a distinct expression pattern. It is elevated in DM but relatively downregulated in DR, suggesting a potential stage-dependent role in the progression of the disease. These coordinated ECM alterations may amplify chronic inflammation, disrupt vascular stability and accelerate neuronal injury. Such changes can facilitate the transition from diabetes to retinopathy.

Consistent with this hypothesis, immune correlation analyses revealed close associations between key ECMGs and immune cell subsets that contribute to DR pathogenesis. FN1’s positive association with naive B cells and monocytes and its negative association with memory B cells mirrors the findings that monocytes and macrophages infiltrate the diabetic retina, promoting chronic low-grade inflammation and vascular damage49–51. TPSAB1’s links to memory B cells, CD8 + T cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs), alongside negative correlations with neutrophils and activated memory CD4 + T cells, align with the role of adaptive immunity in DR, where T cell activation exacerbates retinal barrier breakdown and neurodegeneration52,53. CILP2’s negative association between gamma delta T cells further suggests ECM-immune crosstalk, as gamma delta T cells contribute to early inflammatory responses in diabetic microangiopathy54,55.

Following biological feature prioritization, we compared multiple machine learning models by evaluating their accuracy, AUC, and sensitivity–specificity in distinguishing DR from DM. Although logistic regression achieved slightly higher accuracy on the test set, the ET model demonstrated a higher AUC and a more balanced sensitivity–specificity, reflecting a superior overall discriminative ability. Given the nonlinear interactions, feature redundancy, and biological complexity inherent in transcriptomic data, ET was selected as the final model because of its robustness to multicollinearity, ability to capture nonlinear relationships, and improved generalization performance. When compared horizontally with imaging-based DR diagnostic models derived from retinal fundus photography, which typically achieve AUCs of 0.70–0.8056, the absolute performance of our transcriptome-based model was comparable. Importantly, the focus of these approaches differs: imaging-based models excel at detecting established structural lesions characteristic of later-stage DR, whereas transcriptomic profiling may capture earlier systemic and molecular alterations preceding overt retinal damage. Thus, our findings provide preliminary evidence that peripheral blood transcriptomic data are feasible for DR stratification and may complement conventional imaging by revealing early disease dynamics, rather than replacing routine clinical diagnosis.

The predicted drugs offer multifaceted therapeutic potential for DR and related diseases. For ECM2, angiopoietin-1 suppresses DR by stabilizing the vasculature and reversing leakage, acting at the endothelial level to mitigate hyperglycemia-induced damage57. Linifanib and altiratinib, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, can target angiogenic pathways, potentially reducing neovascularization in DR and angiopathies at the molecular level, while also addressing metabolic dysregulation in T2DM58. For TPSAB1, thrombin elevation in DR promotes thrombosis and inflammation via PAR-1 signaling, suggesting that thrombin inhibitors might prevent microvascular occlusion in retinopathies and nephropathies59. FN1-targeting agents, such as L19IL2 (an immunocytokine), suppress tumor growth by targeting extra-domain B fibronectin, which is overexpressed in diabetic tissues. Extending this, it may inhibit fibrosis in DR and nephropathies at the cellular level, with L19TNFA and AS1409 enhancing the anti-inflammatory effects in angiopathies60,61. Collectively, these drugs can intervene at the genetic, cellular, and systemic levels to prevent or treat DM-related diseases.

This study used a single, well-phenotyped peripheral blood transcriptomic dataset, which is both a strength and limitation. In ROC analysis, several machine learning models showed lower performance in the test cohort than in the training cohort, indicating that the models may have learned patterns specific to the training data rather than generalizable features, which is a sign of potential overfitting. The deliberate use of a single dataset aimed to minimize technical heterogeneity, batch effects, and inconsistent clinical annotation, which are common challenges in multi-dataset integration studies. The key innovation of this study is the development of a robust and interpretable analysis pipeline specifically focused on the ECM in DR. This pipeline integrates differential expression analysis, functional enrichment, machine learning modeling, and SHAP-based interpretation within a single, reproducible framework, emphasizing methodological rigor and biological interpretability. By prioritizing interpretability over maximal predictive performance, this study identified a set of high-confidence ECM-related candidate genes and interaction networks that inform specific mechanistic hypotheses for DR. These findings establish a methodological foundation for future validation studies in independent cohorts, retinal tissues, and experimental models.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the analysis were based on a single cross-sectional peripheral blood transcriptome dataset. Although internal cross-validation was performed, external validation with independent cohorts was not feasible at this stage and may be possible when appropriate datasets become available. Second, gene expression profiles derived from blood may reflect systemic molecular changes and may not fully represent retina-specific or cell-type–specific mechanisms potentially underlying DR. Therefore, future research should incorporate retinal tissue, vitreous samples, or relevant animal models to clarify the biological significance of the identified ECM-related genes and validate the findings in more context-specific systems. Furthermore, the pathway enrichment analyses (GSEA) were based on only nine candidate genes, which may yield unstable results and should be interpreted with caution. Third, experimental validation was influenced by sample availability; in particular, healthy control samples were not included in the PCR analysis because of practical constraints in sample collection. Finally, while major clinical variables were considered, residual confounding related to diabetes duration, metabolic control, or comorbidities cannot be entirely excluded and may be addressed in larger, longitudinal studies.

Conclusion

By integrating blood transcriptomes with explainable machine learning, we identified a five-gene ECM signature (SFRP1, CILP2, FN1, TPSAB1, and ECM2) that links ECM remodeling, neurovascular signaling, metabolism, and immune pathways in DR, serving as validated candidate biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets.
