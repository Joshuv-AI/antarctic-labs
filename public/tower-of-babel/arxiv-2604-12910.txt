Infrared Spectral Gap in a Gluonic Dark Sector as the Origin of the Galactic Acceleration Scale

Abstract
The radial acceleration relation reveals a nearly universal acceleration scale of order 10 − 10 ​ m ​ s − 2 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} in galactic dynamics, whose origin remains unexplained within conventional cold dark matter scenarios. We explore the possibility that this scale is associated with an intrinsic infrared spectral property of the dark sector. Specifically, we hypothesize that a long-lived, color-neutral gluonic vacuum component surviving the post-inflationary expansion era organizes, at large distances, into a spectrally rigid lowest-weight structure. The microscopic seed for this infrared organization is provided by the QCD trace anomaly, which breaks classical scale invariance and, through dimensional transmutation, generates an intrinsic infrared scale in the gluonic sector. Within an effective representation-theoretic framework, Lorentz covariance together with a positive-energy lowest-weight unitary realization points naturally to the Anti-de Sitter algebra 𝔰 ​ 𝔬 ​ ( 2 , 3 ) \mathfrak{so}(2,3) as the simplest symmetry admitting a discrete tower of states with a representation-theoretically protected gap. The associated gap introduces a finite correlation length r c r_{\texttt{c}} that controls the large-scale coherence of the dark sector. A self-gravitating condensate dominated by the lowest-weight mode then leads to a characteristic acceleration g ⋆ = G ​ M h / r c 2 g_{\star}=GM_{h}/r_{\texttt{c}}^{2} , naturally of the same order as the observed galactic acceleration scale, within standard Newtonian gravity. In this framework, the galactic acceleration scale is interpreted as the gravitational imprint of a trace-anomaly-seeded infrared spectral gap in a coherent gluonic dark sector, rather than as a consequence of modified gravity or of galaxy-by-galaxy formation histories.

A parameter exhibiting this level of stability across galaxies could instead point to an intrinsic large-scale property of the dark sector itself. From this perspective, the RAR is naturally viewed as the macroscopic manifestation of an underlying infrared scale governing the dynamics of DM.

I.2
Infrared Rigidity and the Motivation for a Lowest-Weight Structure

The RAR acceleration scale emerges at kiloparsec distances and exhibits striking insensitivity to microscopic particle parameters and detailed halo assembly histories. Its origin is therefore naturally sought in the large-scale collective dynamics of the dark sector rather than in ultraviolet particle physics. Any successful explanation must account not only for the presence of a characteristic scale but also for its universality and small intrinsic scatter across systems with diverse formation histories.

If the dark sector were infrared scale-free, it would contain no dynamically selected macroscopic length. Halo structure would then be shaped primarily by environmental factors — non-linear collapse, mergers, and baryonic feedback — and significant system-to-system variability would generically arise. The empirical robustness of a single acceleration scale instead suggests the existence of an intrinsic infrared regulator that constrains macroscopic structure.

In quantum field theory and many-body systems, long-distance behavior is governed by the lowest excitations of the spectrum. A gapless infrared spectrum yields algebraic decay of correlations and selects no preferred length scale, whereas a spectrum bounded from below by a non-zero gap produces exponential decay and dynamically introduces a finite correlation length
Hastings2006
. The presence of such a gap, therefore, provides a natural mechanism for limiting large-distance coherence and selecting a characteristic structural scale.

Nevertheless, the mere existence of a gap does not uniquely determine the rigidity of that scale under non-linear evolution. If the spectrum above threshold is continuous, long-distance behavior is controlled by near-threshold states and can depend on details of the spectral density, leaving room for environmental sensitivity. By contrast, if the infrared dynamics organizes into a lowest-weight discrete structure, the ground state is isolated and spectrally protected, so that macroscopic coherence is governed by a single dominant mode intrinsic to the dark sector. While discreteness is not logically required, it offers a particularly natural framework for understanding the observed universality and small intrinsic scatter of the galactic acceleration scale.

In this view, self-gravitating equilibria inherit — rather than dynamically generate — a characteristic structural scale, and the RAR is interpreted as the large-distance gravitational imprint of this intrinsic infrared organization.

I.3
Cosmological Background; the
\beta\longrightarrow\psi

We focus on the cosmological interval extending from the onset of the standard post-inflationary expansion era (point

Any mechanism proposed to account for the galactic acceleration scale must therefore satisfy two basic requirements:

1.

It must persist dynamically throughout this cosmological window.

2.

It must remain stable under the environmental diversity and non-linear gravitational evolution characteristic of structure formation.

We hypothesize that a long-lived gluonic vacuum sector survives throughout the
\beta\longrightarrow\psi
epoch and becomes effectively decoupled from the thermal plasma after confinement. The relevant late-time degrees of freedom are taken to be color-singlet bosonic excitations, which we model as scalar di-gluonic bound states, as naturally expected in a confining non-Abelian gauge theory. This assumption is conservative at the level of field content; no new fundamental particles are introduced. Instead, we posit that a coherent component of the gluonic vacuum persists as a dynamically distinct infrared sector whose long-wavelength organization differs from that of ordinary hadronic matter. Concretely, “decoupled” means that number-changing processes and couplings to the Standard Model become inefficient after confinement, so that the comoving abundance (or coherent fraction) of the color-singlet sector is effectively conserved on cosmological timescales and not re-thermalized by the baryonic plasma.

The QCD trace anomaly provides an important microscopic input. Classical Yang-Mills theory is scale invariant and contains no intrinsic mass parameter. At the classical level, one has:

\displaystyle T^{\mu}{}_{\mu}=0\,.

Thus, no preferred intrinsic infrared scale is selected by the gauge dynamics. Quantum effects, however, break this scale invariance. Renormalization induces a non-vanishing vacuum expectation value of the energy-momentum trace, dominated by gluonic contributions
Pasechnik2017
:

\displaystyle\langle T^{\mu}{}_{\mu}\rangle\neq 0\,,

thereby implementing dimensional transmutation. The renormalization-group flow replaces the classically scale-free Yang-Mills sector by a quantum theory characterized by a renormalization-group-invariant scale, e.g.,
\Lambda_{\rm QCD}
. Even in the absence of explicit mass parameters in the Lagrangian, the quantum gluonic vacuum therefore contains a dynamically generated dimensionful infrared input; the long-wavelength effective dynamics of the gluonic sector cannot remain strictly scale-invariant.

It is useful to place this point in a broader field-theoretic context. In discussions of dynamical scale generation in non-Abelian gauge theories, it has long been emphasized that once a dynamically generated gauge-theory scale is present, non-perturbative condensates with the corresponding canonical dimensions arise naturally. In particular, by analogy with the superconducting gap and its associated Cooper-pair condensate, one expects in a strongly coupled gauge theory not only a fermionic condensate scaling as the third power of the dynamical scale, but also, plausibly, a gluonic condensate scaling as its fourth power. From this perspective, the hypothesis that a residual color-singlet gluonic component can survive as a dynamically distinct infrared sector is not an
ad hoc
addition, but a structurally natural possibility within non-perturbative gauge dynamics
Adler1982
. This is the level at which the gauge-theory/superconductor analogy is relevant here; it supports the plausibility of condensate formation once a dynamical scale is generated, without by itself determining the spectral mechanism responsible for macroscopic infrared rigidity.

What still requires explanation is how this dynamically generated infrared input is promoted to a spectrally protected macroscopic correlation length, stable against environmental perturbations and non-linear structure formation. It is therefore essential to distinguish the existence of a dimensionful scale from the existence of a spectral gap. Dimensional transmutation ensures that the theory is no longer scale-free, but it does not fix the structure of the infrared excitation spectrum. A theory can possess an intrinsic scale while still supporting gapless modes or continuous spectra in certain channels. The trace anomaly and the associated condensate picture thus provide a necessary microscopic seed for infrared organization, but they do not by themselves guarantee discreteness, boundedness from below, or spectral rigidity.

If the empirical universality of the RAR is to be attributed to a dynamically selected macroscopic length, an additional structural ingredient is required. The relevant color-singlet sector should contain an isolated lowest excitation above the vacuum, and this lowest scale must remain stable under environmental perturbations and non-linear gravitational evolution. Among possible infrared organizations, a lowest-weight unitary structure provides a particularly economical algebraic framework in which the ground state is isolated by construction and excitations are organized relative to it in a controlled, representation-theoretic manner.

Our central hypothesis, therefore, is that the late-time infrared gluonic sector admits, at the effective level, a spectrally rigid lowest-weight structure. Confinement already motivates the expectation of isolated color-singlet bound states; we elevate this expectation to a structural principle by postulating that the infrared sector admits an effective lowest-weight unitary realization. Representation theory then provides a natural mechanism for protecting the gap; the presence of a positive-energy generator and a lowest-weight state isolates the ground level and organizes excitations into a discrete tower anchored at that state. I.4
From Spectral Gap to Galactic Acceleration

Once the infrared gluonic sector is assumed to possess a lowest-weight discrete spectrum with gap scale
\varkappa
, the emergence of a macroscopic ground-state population becomes natural. Bose-Einstein condensation does not require strong interparticle binding; already in an ideal Bose gas, condensation is driven by Bose statistics together with the existence of a distinguished lowest state and a discrete spectrum. In laboratory systems, this discreteness is usually imposed by finite volume or by an external trapping potential. In the present framework, by contrast, no external trap is introduced. The relevant spectral discreteness is intrinsic and follows from the lowest-weight
\mathrm{SO}(2,3)
organization itself, through the compact
\mathfrak{so}(2)
generator that orders the infrared modes into a tower bounded from below. The decoupled color-singlet sector is naturally expected to develop a macroscopic occupation of its lowest mode as a statistical consequence of its representation-theoretic infrared structure. At the effective level, this ground-state configuration defines a self-gravitating condensate characterized by the correlation length:

\displaystyle r_{\texttt{c}}\sim\varkappa^{-1}\,.

Once coupled to ordinary Newtonian gravity, such a condensate of total halo mass
M_{h}
carries the characteristic acceleration scale:

\displaystyle g_{\star}=\frac{GM_{h}}{r_{\texttt{c}}^{2}}\,.

The central proposal of the present work is that the observed galactic acceleration scale is the macroscopic gravitational imprint of this infrared length. In this picture, the RAR is not the consequence of a modification of gravity, but rather the phenomenological manifestation of a coherent gluonic dark sector endowed with a spectrally protected correlation length that survives throughout the
\beta\longrightarrow\psi
cosmological epoch. The remainder of the paper develops this program explicitly. We construct an effective infrared description of the color-singlet gluonic sector, show how a lowest-weight
\mathrm{SO}(2,3)
structure yields a protected scale
r_{\texttt{c}}
, and derive, within this framework, the corresponding halo mass, rotation-curve, and acceleration profiles.

At the same time, the scope of the present work should be kept in view. We do not attempt here to derive from first principles the full cosmological history of the gluonic sector, nor the complete linear response of the condensate to baryonic perturbations. Rather, we aim to show that, once a long-lived color-singlet sector with a lowest-weight infrared organization is assumed, a finite-mass halo profile and an intrinsic acceleration scale of the observed galactic order can be obtained. A first-principles derivation of the full radial acceleration relation is left for future work.

Breakdown of the

It is important to emphasize that, with respect to the standard inner product (

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\leq\zeta\leq\tfrac{3}{2}

A consistent framework is nevertheless available. The singleton (Rac) case

At the level of the radial integration, this change is reflected in a modification of the effective measure; in the range
\tfrac{1}{2}<\zeta\leq\tfrac{3}{2}

\displaystyle x^{2}(1+x^{2})^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

\zeta

AdS/CFT interpretation and double quantization.

This structure is precisely reflected in the AdS/CFT correspondence. For scalar modes in four-dimensional AdS, the near-boundary behavior involves two independent fall-offs:

\displaystyle\phi(r)\sim r^{-\Delta_{-}}\,,

\displaystyle\quad\Delta_{-}=\zeta\,,

\displaystyle\phi(r)\sim r^{-\Delta_{+}}\,,

\displaystyle\quad\Delta_{+}=3-\zeta\,.

(35)

The interval
\tfrac{1}{2}<\zeta\leq\tfrac{3}{2}

The choice of Hilbert-space structure then selects a quantization; the standard

Conformal scalar and the special role of
\zeta=2

A particularly important situation arises for the conformally coupled scalar in four-dimensional AdS. The complementary values

\displaystyle\zeta(\zeta-3)=-2\,,

Their distinction lies in their boundary behavior; the

In the present work, we focus primarily on the

IV
Interface Between DM Bose-Einstein Condensation and Baryonic Matter

In the present framework, the CDM component is modeled as a Bose-Einstein condensate of color-singlet gluonic degrees of freedom whose long-wavelength modes are organized by an effective AdS spectral structure. The condensate ground state (
41
) corresponds to the lowest-energy configuration of this sector. It is isotropic and spherically symmetric, and its spatial extent is set by the curvature scale of the effective AdS environment. This introduces, within the model, a characteristic halo radius:

\displaystyle r_{\texttt{c}}\sim\varkappa^{-1}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{|\Lambda^{\mathrm{eff}}_{\mathrm{AdS}}|}}\,.

(44)

For definiteness, and to anchor the discussion in a phenomenologically relevant regime, we shall frequently use the fiducial benchmark:

\displaystyle r_{\texttt{c}}=1\;\mathrm{kpc}\,,\quad M_{\mathrm{h}}=10^{9}\,M_{\odot}\,,\quad\zeta=2\,,

(45)

which is representative of a typical dwarf-galaxy halo. As noted above, the choice
\zeta=2
corresponds to the smallest normalizable scalar lowest-weight representation and leads to a finite total halo mass in the present construction.

In the absence of baryons, the condensate is described by an approximately spherical halo dominated by the ground-state density profile. Baryonic structures such as stellar cores, bulges, or localized baryonic clumps generate additional gravitational potentials that act as perturbations on this background configuration. Within the present framework, these perturbations generally break the spherical symmetry of the condensate and induce transitions from the ground state to excited AdS modes. In a multipole decomposition of the baryonic potential, the dominant response is controlled by the lowest angular excitations, leading to multipolar distortions of the DM density in the baryon-halo interface region.

The occupation of these first angularly excited levels can underlie several physically important effects:

1.

The polarisation of the condensate around baryonic mass concentrations. In analogy with the polarisation of a dielectric medium placed in an external electric field, the dipolar modes can generate a spatial displacement of the condensate density toward the baryonic potential minimum.

2.

The directional response of the DM distribution to an external gravitational field. Because the

3.

The formation of asymmetric DM wakes generated by moving baryonic objects. The

Its radial structure is also particularly suited to mediating the baryon-halo coupling. While its asymptotic decay matches that of the ground state, the additional factor
\varkappa r

Consequently, these modes are naturally concentrated in the intermediate region where the halo profile changes from the inner core to the outer regime, which observationally corresponds to the radial domain where the baryonic and DM contributions to the gravitational potential become comparable. In this transition zone, the dipolar excitations can efficiently transmit anisotropic perturbations from the baryonic sector to the condensate, generating the leading multipolar deformations of the halo density.

V
DM Mass Profiles, Rotation Curves, and Accelerations

In this section, we examine the main properties of the corresponding DM halo structure, including the mass profile, circular velocity, and radial acceleration. For clarity, we first restrict attention to a purely spherically symmetric configuration dominated by the ground state. Within the AdS-Bose-Einstein condensate framework described above, the DM density profile associated with the ground state of the gluonic condensate is given by Eq. (
52

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}(x)=\rho_{\texttt{c}}(1+x^{2})^{-\zeta}\,,\quad x=\varkappa r=\frac{r}{r_{\texttt{c}}}\,.

(59)

We then illustrate the phenomenological implications of the model using a representative dwarf-galaxy halo and investigate how including the first radial excitation modifies the resulting profiles. The effective AdS curvature parameter is chosen so as to produce a core radius consistent with observationally inferred values for dwarf galaxies.

On the role of AdS coordinates and the physical measure.

It is important to emphasize that, in the present construction, the effective AdS geometry is not interpreted as the physical spacetime geometry of the galactic halo. Rather, it provides an auxiliary representation-theoretic framework that organizes the infrared spectrum and the associated normal modes of the dark sector. The resulting ground-state profile is then interpreted in physical space by identifying the dimensionless radial variable as
x=\varkappa r=r/r_{\texttt{c}}
, where

denotes the usual radial coordinate in the weak-field spatial geometry relevant for galactic dynamics. Accordingly, all physical quantities considered in this section — such as the enclosed mass, circular velocity, and radial acceleration — are computed using the standard three-dimensional Euclidean measure,
\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}=4\pi r^{2}\,\mathrm{d}r
, appropriate to the Newtonian regime. In this sense, the AdS structure encodes the infrared spectral organization of the condensate, whereas the halo phenomenology is formulated in the ordinary physical space relevant for galactic dynamics.

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{DM}}(<x,\zeta)=4\pi\rho_{\texttt{c}}r_{\texttt{c}}^{3}F(x,\zeta)\,,

(60)

where the dimensionless mass function
F(x,\zeta)
is defined by:

\displaystyle F(x,\zeta)=\int_{0}^{x}f(u,\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}u\,,\quad f(u,\zeta)=u^{2}(1+u^{2})^{-\zeta}\,.

(61)

Relation to the incomplete beta function.

The function
F(x,\zeta)
can be expressed in closed form by introducing the variable:

\displaystyle t=\frac{u^{2}}{1+u^{2}}\,,

(62)

for which:

\displaystyle u^{2}=\frac{t}{1-t}\,,\quad\mathrm{d}u=\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{2\,t^{1/2}(1-t)^{3/2}}\,.

(63)

Substituting into Eq. (
61
), one finds:

\displaystyle F(x,\zeta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}u^{2}(1+u^{2})^{-\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\frac{x^{2}}{1+x^{2}}}t^{1/2}(1-t)^{\zeta-\frac{5}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

(64)

This is precisely the incomplete beta function:

\displaystyle B_{z}(a,b)=\int_{0}^{z}t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

(65)

with:

\displaystyle a=\frac{3}{2}\,,\quad b=\zeta-\frac{3}{2}\,,\quad z=\frac{x^{2}}{1+x^{2}}\,.

(66)

Therefore:

\displaystyle F(x,\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,B_{\frac{x^{2}}{1+x^{2}}}\!\left(\frac{3}{2},\zeta-\frac{3}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\!\left(\frac{x^{2}}{1+x^{2}}\right)^{3/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\frac{3}{2},\,\frac{5}{2}-\zeta;\,\frac{5}{2};\,\frac{x^{2}}{1+x^{2}}\right)\,,

(67)

where in the second line we used the standard hypergeometric representation of the incomplete beta function:

\displaystyle B_{z}(a,b)=\frac{z^{a}}{a}\,{}_{2}F_{1}(a,\,1-b;\,a+1;\,z)\,.

(68)

For
\zeta>\frac{3}{2}
,
F(x,\zeta)
converges as
x\longrightarrow\infty
to the finite limit:

\displaystyle F(\infty,\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}\,B\!\left(\frac{3}{2},\zeta-\frac{3}{2}\right)\,.

(69)

Consequently, the total mass of the AdS-gluonic-condensate halo remains finite and is given by:

\displaystyle M_{h}=M_{\mathrm{DM}}(<\infty,\zeta)=4\pi\rho_{\texttt{c}}r_{\texttt{c}}^{3}\;\frac{1}{2}\,B\!\left(\frac{3}{2},\zeta-\frac{3}{2}\right)\,.

(70)

This property is particularly noteworthy: although the density distribution extends formally to infinite radius, the total halo mass remains finite. This contrasts with several commonly used phenomenological halo profiles (see, for instance, Refs.
Begeman1991
;
Burkert1995
;
Navarro1997
;
Read2016
;
Li2020
), whose enclosed mass diverges logarithmically or linearly at large radii unless an explicit truncation radius is imposed.

Regularized form.

It is convenient to express the mass profile in a normalized (regularized) form:

\displaystyle\frac{M_{\mathrm{DM}}(<x,\zeta)}{M_{h}}=\frac{F(x,\zeta)}{F(\infty,\zeta)}=\frac{B_{\frac{x^{2}}{1+x^{2}}}\!\left(\frac{3}{2},\zeta-\frac{3}{2}\right)}{B\!\left(\frac{3}{2},\zeta-\frac{3}{2}\right)}\,.

(71)

Central and global behavior.

Near the halo center (
x\ll 1
), using
F(x,\zeta)\sim x^{3}/3
, one recovers a constant-density core:

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{DM}}(<x,\zeta)\sim\frac{4}{3}\pi\rho_{\texttt{c}}r_{\texttt{c}}^{3}\,x^{3}\,.

(72)

Mass concentration and
\zeta
-dependence of the halo profile.

For
\zeta>1
, the function
f(x,\zeta)=x^{2}(1+x^{2})^{-\zeta}
reaches its maximum at:

\displaystyle x_{\max}=(\zeta-1)^{-1/2}\,,

(73)

as follows from the condition
\mathrm{d}(\ln{f})/\mathrm{d}x=0
, equivalent to
f^{\prime}(x,\zeta)=0
for
x>0
. Since
F^{\prime}(x,\zeta)=f(x,\zeta)
, this point coincides with the inflection point of
F(x,\zeta)
. The maximal value is:

\displaystyle f_{\max}=\frac{(\zeta-1)^{\zeta-1}}{\zeta^{\zeta}}\,.

(74)

Note that the limiting case
\zeta=1
, discussed in the literature, corresponds to non-spherical (disk-like) configurations rather than genuine spherical halos.

These properties show that increasing
\zeta
leads simultaneously to a smaller total halo mass and to a stronger central concentration. Indeed, for the density profile
(1+x^{2})^{-\zeta}
(see Eq. (
59
)), larger values of
\zeta
produce a progressively steeper fall-off at large radii:

\displaystyle(1+x^{2})^{-\zeta}\sim x^{-2\zeta}\,,\quad\mbox{for}\quad x\gg 1\,.

(75)

Consequently, the outer halo contributes less to the mass integral, and a larger fraction of the total mass becomes confined within the inner region
r\lesssim r_{\texttt{c}}
.

\zeta>\tfrac{3}{2}

V.2
Rotation Curves

The circular velocity induced by the DM halo in its ground state follows from the usual relation for a spherically symmetric mass distribution:

\displaystyle v^{2}(x,\zeta)=\frac{GM_{\mathrm{DM}}(<x,\zeta)}{r_{\texttt{c}}x}=4\pi G\rho_{\texttt{c}}r_{\texttt{c}}^{2}\,\frac{F(x,\zeta)}{x}\,,

(76)

where
x=r/r_{\texttt{c}}
and
F(x,\zeta)
is the dimensionless enclosed-mass function introduced in Sec.
V.1
. It is convenient to write the velocity profile explicitly as:

\displaystyle v(x,\zeta)=\sqrt{4\pi G\rho_{\texttt{c}}r_{\texttt{c}}^{2}\,\frac{F(x,\zeta)}{x}}\,.

(77)

Differentiating with respect to the dimensionless radius
x
yields:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}v(x,\zeta)}{\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{4\pi G\rho_{\texttt{c}}r_{\texttt{c}}^{2}}{2\,v(x,\zeta)}\left(-\frac{F(x,\zeta)}{x^{2}}+\frac{f(x,\zeta)}{x}\right)\,,

(78)

where
f(x,\zeta)=x^{2}(1+x^{2})^{-\zeta}
is the integrand appearing in the definition of
F(x,\zeta)
.

Central behavior.

Near the halo center (
x\ll 1
), using
F(x,\zeta)\sim x^{3}/3
, one finds:

\displaystyle v(x,\zeta)\sim\sqrt{\frac{4\pi G}{3}\rho_{\texttt{c}}}\;\underbrace{r_{\texttt{c}}x}_{r}\,.

(79)

Thus, the velocity grows linearly with radius,
v\propto r
, which is the characteristic behavior of a solid-body rotation curve.

Asymptotic behavior.

For
x\gg 1
and
\zeta>\tfrac{3}{2}
, the function
F(x,\zeta)
approaches its asymptotic value
F(\infty,\zeta)
(
69
). The circular velocity therefore becomes (see Eq. (
70
)):

\displaystyle v(x,\zeta)\sim\sqrt{\frac{GM_{h}(\zeta)}{r_{\texttt{c}}x}}\,,

(80)

which corresponds to a slowly declining Keplerian behavior
v\propto r^{-1/2}
. For fixed
(\rho_{\texttt{c}},r_{\texttt{c}})
, increasing
\zeta
leads to a smaller total halo mass
M_{h}(\zeta)
, and consequently to systematically lower asymptotic velocities.

Maximum of the rotation curve and comparisons with NFW
Navarro1997
and Burkert
Burkert1995
profiles.

As illustrated in Figs.
4
and
5
(see Tables
2
and
3
for the explicit formulas), the rotation velocity reaches a maximum at a dimensionless radius that we denote by
x_{v;\max}
. From Eq. (
78
), the extremum condition
\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}x=0
immediately yields:

\displaystyle\frac{F(x_{v;\max},\zeta)}{x_{v;\max}}=f(x_{v;\max},\zeta)\,.

(81)

This relation admits a simple geometric interpretation. The quantity
F(x,\zeta)/x
represents the slope of the straight line joining the origin to the point
(x,F(x,\zeta))
, while
f(x,\zeta)=F^{\prime}(x,\zeta)
is the slope of the tangent to the curve
F(x,\zeta)
. Equation (
81
) therefore states that, at
x=x_{v;\max}
, the tangent to the curve
F(x,\zeta)
passes through the origin.

The velocity maximum occurs at a radius
x_{v;\max}
larger than the inflection point
x_{\max}
(
73
) of
F(x,\zeta)
, which is defined by
f^{\prime}(x,\zeta)=0
. Indeed, for
0<x\leq x_{\max}
the function
f(x,\zeta)
is strictly increasing, so that for any
x_{\circ}<x
one has
f(x_{\circ},\zeta)<f(x,\zeta)
. It follows that:

\displaystyle F(x,\zeta)=\int_{0}^{x}f(x_{\circ},\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{\circ}\;<\;\int_{0}^{x}f(x,\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}x_{\circ}=xf(x,\zeta)\,,

(82)

and therefore:

\displaystyle\frac{F(x,\zeta)}{x}<f(x,\zeta)\,,\quad\mbox{for all}\quad 0<x\leq x_{\max}\,.

(83)

On the other hand, the maximum of the rotation curve is determined by the condition (
81
). Since this equality cannot hold for
x\leq x_{\max}
, it follows that:

\displaystyle x_{v;\max}>x_{\max}\,.

(84)

Because Eq. (
81
) has no closed-form solution, the ordering is verified numerically; for example:

\displaystyle\zeta=2:

\displaystyle\quad x_{\max}=1\,,\quad x_{v;\max}\approx 1.8253\,,

\displaystyle\zeta=\frac{5}{2}:

\displaystyle\quad x_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\,,\quad x_{v;\max}=\sqrt{2}\,,

\displaystyle\zeta=3:

\displaystyle\quad x_{\max}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\approx 0.7071\,,\quad x_{v;\max}\approx 1.1892\,,

\displaystyle\zeta=4:

\displaystyle\quad x_{\max}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\approx 0.5773\,,\quad x_{v;\max}\approx 0.9403\,.

(85)

In all cases one finds
x_{v;\max}>x_{\max}
.

Importantly, the dimensionless location
x_{v;\max}
depends only on the shape parameter
\zeta
and is therefore universal within the model, while the corresponding physical radius is simply:

\displaystyle r_{v;\max}=x_{v;\max}\,r_{\texttt{c}}\,.

(86)

A remark on asymptotic falloff and the choice of measure.

It is worth noting that both the NFW and Burkert profiles share the same large-radius decay,
\rho(x)\sim x^{-3}
. This is suggestive when compared with the gluonic-condensate profile
\rho(x)\propto(1+x^{2})^{-\zeta}
, whose asymptotic behavior is
\rho(x)\sim x^{-2\zeta}
. Formally, the exponent
-3
corresponds to
\zeta=\tfrac{3}{2}
with the standard measure
x^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x
(see below Eq. (
32
)). By contrast, in the low-
\zeta
sector discussed below Eq. (
32
), the limiting case
\zeta=\tfrac{1}{2}
is associated with the modified measure
x^{2}(1+x^{2})^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}x
, so that the same asymptotic behavior can also be viewed, in that sense, as suggestive of
\zeta=\tfrac{1}{2}
. This analogy should not be overinterpreted. In the present framework, the distinction between
\zeta=\tfrac{3}{2}
and
\zeta=\tfrac{1}{2}
is governed not by the density falloff alone, but by the functional setting, in particular by the measure entering the norm and normalization problem. For phenomenological profiles such as NFW and Burkert, it is therefore not obvious a priori which of these two measures would furnish the more relevant comparison. A careful clarification of this point would require a more systematic analysis, which we leave for future work.

V.3
Radial Accelerations

The radial gravitational acceleration generated by the DM halo in its ground state is defined as:

\displaystyle g(r,\zeta)=\frac{v^{2}(r,\zeta)}{r}=4\pi G\rho_{\texttt{c}}r_{\texttt{c}}^{3}\,\frac{F\!\left(\frac{r}{r_{\texttt{c}}},\zeta\right)}{r^{2}}\,.

(87)

In terms of the dimensionless radius
x=r/r_{\texttt{c}}
, this reads:

\displaystyle g(x,\zeta)=4\pi G\rho_{\texttt{c}}r_{\texttt{c}}\,\frac{F(x,\zeta)}{x^{2}}\,,

(88)

with radial derivative:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}g(x,\zeta)}{\mathrm{d}x}=4\pi G\rho_{\texttt{c}}r_{\texttt{c}}\left(-2\,\frac{F(x,\zeta)}{x^{3}}+\frac{f(x,\zeta)}{x^{2}}\right)\,,

(89)

where, again,
f(x,\zeta)=x^{2}(1+x^{2})^{-\zeta}
.

Central behavior.

As
x\ll 1
, using
F(x,\zeta)\sim x^{3}/3
, one finds:

\displaystyle g(x,\zeta)\sim\frac{4\pi G}{3}\,\rho_{\texttt{c}}\,\underbrace{r_{\texttt{c}}\,x}_{r}\,,

(90)

corresponding to a linear growth of the acceleration. This behavior is independent of
\zeta
and reflects once again the solid-body character of the constant-density core.

Asymptotic behavior.

For
x\gg 1
and
\zeta>\tfrac{3}{2}
, the enclosed mass approaches a constant value,
F(x,\zeta)\longrightarrow F(\infty,\zeta)
, so that the acceleration becomes:

\displaystyle g(x,\zeta)\sim\frac{GM_{h}(\zeta)}{r_{\texttt{c}}^{2}\,x^{2}}\,,

(91)

which corresponds to the standard Newtonian inverse-square fall-off produced by a finite total halo mass.

Maximum of the radial acceleration.

The radial acceleration reaches a maximum at the radius
x_{\mathrm{g};\max}
determined by the condition:

\displaystyle\frac{F(x_{\mathrm{g};\max},\zeta)}{x_{\mathrm{g};\max}}=\frac{1}{2}\,f(x_{\mathrm{g};\max},\zeta)\,.

(92)

This relation fixes the position of the maximum of the radial acceleration. For the values of
\zeta
considered in this work, one obtains:

\displaystyle\zeta=2:

\displaystyle\quad x_{\mathrm{g};\max}\approx 0.9682\,,

\displaystyle\zeta=\frac{5}{2}:

\displaystyle\quad x_{\mathrm{g};\max}=1\,,

\displaystyle\zeta=3:

\displaystyle\quad x_{\mathrm{g};\max}\approx 0.8260\,,

\displaystyle\zeta=4:

\displaystyle\quad x_{\mathrm{g};\max}\approx 0.6766\,,

(93)

which satisfy the ordering (see Eq. (
V.2
)):

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{g};\max}<x_{v;\max}\,,

(94)

showing that the maximum of the radial acceleration is reached at a smaller radius than the maximum of the circular velocity.

As in the case of the mass and velocity profiles, the dimensionless position
x_{\mathrm{g};\max}
depends only on the shape parameter
\zeta
and is therefore universal, i.e., independent of the particular properties of a given galaxy. The corresponding physical radius is simply:

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{g};\max}=x_{\mathrm{g};\max}\,r_{\texttt{c}}\,.

(95)

V.4
On the Core Radius

The previous analysis shows that the notion of a “core radius” is not unique and can be defined in several physically meaningful ways. Within the present framework, at least four natural definitions can be identified:

1.

The value of
x
beyond which the central approximation
M_{\mathrm{DM}}(<x,\zeta)\sim\frac{4}{3}\pi\rho_{\texttt{c}}r_{\texttt{c}}^{3}x^{3}
(
72
) is no longer valid, signaling the breakdown of the constant-density regime.

2.

The value of
x=x_{\max}
(
73
) corresponding to the inflection point of the enclosed-mass profile, where the curvature of
M_{\mathrm{DM}}(<x,\zeta)
changes sign and the mass growth begins to slow.

3.

The value of
x=x_{v;\max}
(
81
) (see also (
V.2
)) at which the circular velocity
v(x,\zeta)
reaches its maximum.

4.

The value of
x=x_{\mathrm{g};\max}
(
92
) at which the radial acceleration
g(x,\zeta)
attains its maximum.

Although these definitions are not identical, they all select radii of the same order, thereby identifying a characteristic scale of the halo set by
r_{\texttt{c}}
. This reflects the existence of an intermediate radial region separating the inner constant-density core from the outer domain in which the dynamics approaches the asymptotically Newtonian regime.

As will become clear in the following sections, this transition region plays a special physical role. It is in this radial range that the relative importance of baryonic and dark components typically becomes comparable, and where additional effects — such as the excitation of higher condensate modes or the response of the halo to baryonic perturbations — may be most significant.

V.5
A Representative Example;
\zeta=2
in the Context of Dwarf Galaxies

The choice
\zeta=2
is the smallest admissible integer or half-integer value of the AdS representation (conformal) parameter for which the scalar lowest-weight unitary irreducible representation is normalizable. Within the present family of halo profiles, and for fixed
(\rho_{\texttt{c}},r_{\texttt{c}})
, this case corresponds to the least centrally concentrated halo, since the density profile
(1+x^{2})^{-\zeta}
then has the slowest large-radius decay among the admissible values of
\zeta

5)

F(x,\zeta)

(97)

V.6
Role of the First Radial Excitation

Including a small admixture of the first radial excitation
(n,l)=(1,0)
:

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}(r)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(|\varphi_{000}(r)|^{2}+\epsilon\,|\varphi_{100}(r)|^{2}\right)\,,\quad\epsilon\ll 1\,,

(104)

provides a controlled mechanism for modifying the transition region between the inner solid-body rise and the quasi-flat portion of the rotation curve. Because the radial wave function
\varphi_{100}
has a node, its contribution can produce a mild redistribution of mass across the characteristic scale
r\sim r_{\texttt{c}}
(see Eq. (
50
)). The resulting profile can slightly flatten the rotation curve or generate a modest overshoot in the intermediate region, while leaving the constant-density core essentially unchanged at very small radii.

In a more detailed phenomenological treatment, the parameter
\epsilon
may be used to account for the weak curvature features often visible in high-quality dwarf-galaxy rotation curves. This benchmark therefore suggests that, even within a minimal parameterization based on
(r_{\texttt{c}},\rho_{\texttt{c}},\zeta)
, the inclusion of a small first-radial-excitation component can already yield rotation curves of realistic amplitude and shape, while preserving a direct connection with the underlying AdS-Bose-Einstein-condensate microphysics.

V.7
Superposition Near Baryons

In the absence of baryons, the condensate is well described by the spherically symmetric ground state (
41
) discussed above. In the vicinity of a baryonic structure, however, the gravitational potential generated by the baryons acts as a perturbation that couples the ground state to the lowest excited levels of the AdS spectrum. The DM wave-function can therefore be described, to leading order, as a superposition of the lowest-lying modes:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathrm{DM}}(t,\mathbf{x})\sim

\displaystyle\;a_{000}\,\varphi_{000}(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=-1}^{1}a_{01m}\,\varphi_{01m}(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle+a_{100}\,\varphi_{100}(t,\mathbf{x})+\cdots\,.

(105)

This superposition becomes particularly relevant in the intermediate radial region
r\sim r_{\texttt{c}}
, set by the intrinsic condensate scale. For the halo profiles considered here, this is the radius at which the ground-state density profile begins to depart from its inner nearly constant-density behavior and cross over to the outer decaying regime (see Sect.
V.1
). It therefore marks the onset of the region where the halo structure is no longer controlled solely by the unperturbed core, but becomes increasingly sensitive to external gravitational perturbations. At the same time, the lowest excited modes have their largest radial support precisely in this domain (see Sects.
IV.1
and
IV.2
), so that the response of the condensate to the baryonic potential is expected to be maximal there. In the present framework, it is therefore natural to identify
r\sim r_{\texttt{c}}
with the baryon-halo interface region, namely the radial domain in which baryonic gravity begins to produce an appreciable deformation of the condensate away from its spherically symmetric ground-state configuration. In particular, the condensate:

1.

can respond to the tidal gravitational field generated by the baryonic mass distribution.

2.

can develop multipolar deformations encoded by modes with
l\geq 1
; see Sect.
IV.1
.

3.

can undergo breathing and higher radial oscillations associated with modes with
n\geq 1
; see Sect.
IV.2
.

4.

can exert a gravitational backreaction on the baryonic component, potentially affecting rotation curves, dynamical friction, and the stability of stellar systems.

The first excited levels thus play an important physical role. They encode the leading deviations from spherical symmetry and quantify the dynamical susceptibility of the condensate to baryonic perturbations. In this picture, baryons do not simply move in a fixed DM potential; rather, they can locally deform a coherent medium whose response is controlled by the lowest modes of the AdS spectrum. This offers a natural framework for understanding how baryons can reshape the effective DM distribution in the interface region without destroying the global coherence of the condensate.

VI
Discussion: RAR and Emergent Acceleration Scale

Having established the structure of the AdS-gluonic-condensate halo and its associated mass, velocity, and acceleration profiles, we now confront the model with one of the most stringent empirical constraints on galactic dynamics, namely the RAR.

As already discussed in the Introduction, one of the central results of the SPARC analysis
lelli2016SPARC
;
McGaugh2016
;
Lelli2017
is the existence of a remarkably tight correlation between the observed radial acceleration:

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{obs}}(r)=\frac{v^{2}_{\mathrm{obs}}(r)}{r}\,,

(106)

and the Newtonian acceleration generated by the baryonic mass distribution:

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{bar}}(r)=\frac{G\,M_{\mathrm{bar}}(<r)}{r^{2}}\,.

(107)

This correlation takes the form of an approximately galaxy-independent functional relation:

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{obs}}=g_{\mathrm{obs}}\!\left(g_{\mathrm{bar}}\right)\,,

(108)

and exhibits an unexpectedly small intrinsic scatter across galaxies spanning a broad range of masses, morphologies, and evolutionary histories.

The relation is controlled by a single characteristic acceleration scale:

\displaystyle g_{\dagger}\sim 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\,,

(109)

which delineates the transition between the baryon-dominated and DM-dominated regimes. Its apparent universality, combined with the exceptional tightness of the observed correlation, makes the RAR one of the strongest observational constraints on models of galaxy dynamics.

Although the RAR is often discussed in the context of modified-gravity phenomenology, it can equally be interpreted as an empirical constraint on the coupling between baryons and DM within the framework of standard gravity
Navarro2017
;
Desmond2017
;
FamaeyMcGaugh2012
. In the present work, we adopt this latter viewpoint.

Within our framework, the law of gravity itself is not modified. The observed radial acceleration is assumed to follow from the standard superposition of baryonic and DM contributions, so that the decomposition:

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{obs}}(r)=g_{\mathrm{bar}}(r)+g_{\mathrm{DM}}(r)

(110)

remains valid at all radii. The novelty of the model, therefore, does not lie in a change of the gravitational force law, nor in the introduction of an
ad hoc
interpolation formula, but in the physical nature of the dark component and in its dynamical response to the baryonic gravitational field.

In the scenario developed above, the DM halo is described as a coherent condensate whose long-wavelength dynamics are governed by an effective AdS spectral structure, dominated by its lowest-weight state. The presence of baryons perturbs this ground-state configuration and excites the lowest modes of the spectrum, so that the dark component can respond to the baryonic potential in a controlled and approximately universal manner. We now investigate whether, and under which conditions, this response can naturally generate an effective acceleration scale of order
g_{\dagger}\sim 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}
without galaxy-by-galaxy fine tuning, thereby reproducing the observed RAR within an otherwise standard Newtonian framework.

It should be emphasized, however, that the empirical RAR is used here primarily as a phenomenological target. What the present framework provides directly is an intrinsic halo acceleration scale, together with a concrete setting in which baryonic perturbations can probe the lowest condensate modes. The aim of the present analysis is therefore to isolate, within the model, the structural origin of the galactic acceleration scale.

VI.1
Phenomenology of the RAR

As discussed above, the radial acceleration produced by the DM halo in its ground state can be written as (see Eqs. (
88
), (
60
), and (
71
)):

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{DM}}(x,\zeta)\;\left(=g(x,\zeta)\right)

\displaystyle=4\pi G\rho_{\texttt{c}}r_{\texttt{c}}\,\frac{F(x,\zeta)}{x^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{GM_{\mathrm{DM}}(<x,\zeta)}{r_{\texttt{c}}^{2}x^{2}}

\displaystyle=g_{\star}\,\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,\frac{F(x,\zeta)}{F(\infty,\zeta)}\,,

(111)

where:

\displaystyle g_{\star}=\frac{GM_{h}}{r_{\texttt{c}}^{2}}

(112)

defines an intrinsic acceleration scale associated with the halo mass
M_{h}
and its geometric core radius
r_{\texttt{c}}
.

For a fixed value of the shape parameter
\zeta
, the DM contribution thus factorizes into a universal dimensionless shape function and a single acceleration scale
g_{\star}
. In particular, for
\zeta=2
and
x=x_{\max}=1
, corresponding both to the geometric core radius and to the inflection point of the mass profile (see Sect.
V.5
), one finds:

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{DM}}(1,2)\;\left(=g(1,2)\right)\sim 0.18\,g_{\star}\,,

(113)

a value slightly below the maximal radial acceleration, because the latter is attained at a slightly smaller radius,
x_{\mathrm{g};\max}<x_{\max}=1
(see Eq. (
V.3
)). At large radii, the same intrinsic scale governs the asymptotic decay as:

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{DM}}(x)\;\left(=g(x,\zeta)\right)\sim\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,g_{\star}\,.

(114)

Observationally, the SPARC RAR, with both
g_{\mathrm{obs}}
and
g_{\mathrm{bar}}
evaluated at the same galactocentric radius, can be written in the convenient form
McGaugh2016
;
Lelli2017
:

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{obs}}=g_{\mathrm{bar}}\,\nu\!\left(\frac{g_{\mathrm{bar}}}{g_{\dagger}}\right)\,,\quad\nu(y)=\frac{1}{1-e^{-\sqrt{y}}}\,,

(115)

where
g_{\dagger}
is the characteristic acceleration scale entering the empirical relation. The interpolation function
\nu(y)
satisfies the asymptotic behaviors:

\displaystyle\nu(y)

\displaystyle\;\longrightarrow\;1\,,\quad\mbox{when}\quad y\gg 1\,,

\displaystyle\nu(y)

\displaystyle\;\longrightarrow\;y^{-1/2}\,,\quad\mbox{when}\quad y\ll 1\,.

(116)

On the other hand, using the exact decomposition (
110
), the RAR (
115
) can be rewritten in terms of the DM acceleration alone as:

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{DM}}=g_{\mathrm{bar}}\,\mu\!\left(\frac{g_{\mathrm{bar}}}{g_{\dagger}}\right)\,,\quad\mu(y)\left(=\nu(y)-1\right)=\frac{1}{e^{\sqrt{y}}-1}\,,

(117)

with the asymptotic behavior:

\displaystyle\mu(y)

\displaystyle\;\longrightarrow\;0\,,\quad\mbox{when}\quad y\gg 1\,,

\displaystyle\mu(y)

\displaystyle\;\longrightarrow\;y^{-1/2}\,,\quad\mbox{when}\quad y\ll 1\,.

(118)

Then, in the low-acceleration regime (
g_{\mathrm{bar}}\ll g_{\dagger}
, that is,
y\ll 1
), one obtains:

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{obs}}\sim g_{\mathrm{DM}}\sim\sqrt{g_{\dagger}\,g_{\mathrm{bar}}}\,,

(119)

so that the observed acceleration is dominated by the DM contribution and follows the characteristic square-root scaling of the RAR. In the opposite, high-acceleration regime (
g_{\mathrm{bar}}\gg g_{\dagger}
, that is,
y\gg 1
), one finds:

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{obs}}\sim g_{\mathrm{bar}}\,,

(120)

while the DM contribution becomes negligible:

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{DM}}\sim 0\,,

(121)

corresponding to a baryon-dominated dynamics.

Although the functions
g_{\mathrm{obs}}(g_{\mathrm{bar}})
and
g_{\mathrm{DM}}(g_{\mathrm{bar}})
are universal, individual galaxies populate only finite regions of the RAR plane. Each galaxy samples the relation over the range of radii accessible to its baryonic mass distribution, so that the allowed values of
g_{\mathrm{bar}}
are bounded from above by a galaxy-dependent maximum, typically reached at small galactocentric radii
lelli2016SPARC
;
McGaugh2016
;
Lelli2017
.

SPARC data show that more massive galaxies attain larger values of
g_{\mathrm{bar}}
, often exceeding
10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}
, whereas low-mass dwarf galaxies remain confined to the low-acceleration regime and never reach
g_{\mathrm{bar}}\gtrsim g_{\dagger}

Lelli2017
;
Lelli2018
.

A key structural property of the RAR is that the dimensionless function:

\displaystyle\frac{g_{\mathrm{DM}}}{g_{\dagger}}=\frac{g_{\mathrm{bar}}}{g_{\dagger}}\,\mu\!\left(\frac{g_{\mathrm{bar}}}{g_{\dagger}}\right)

(122)

is non-monotonic. As
g_{\mathrm{bar}}
increases, the DM acceleration
g_{\mathrm{DM}}
reaches a maximum value
g_{\mathrm{DM}}^{\max}
at a characteristic baryonic acceleration
g_{\mathrm{bar}}^{\star}
. Since both quantities are determined entirely by the single scale
g_{\dagger}
, the values of
g_{\mathrm{DM}}^{\max}
and
g_{\mathrm{bar}}^{\star}
are universal. This behavior is illustrated in Fig.

.

\mu(y)
entering the RAR decomposition

The existence of a universal upper bound on
g_{\mathrm{DM}}
has an immediate consequence: the intrinsic halo scale
g_{\star}=GM_{h}/r_{\texttt{c}}^{2}
must accordingly remain bounded. For a given core radius
r_{\texttt{c}}
, this translates into an upper bound on the halo mass.

Accordingly, galaxy profiles in the
[g_{\mathrm{obs}},g_{\mathrm{bar}}]
(or equivalently,
[g_{\mathrm{DM}},g_{\mathrm{bar}}]
) plane naturally separate into two broad categories, depending on whether the maximum baryonic acceleration reached within a given galaxy, denoted
g_{\mathrm{bar}}^{\max}
, is larger or smaller than the universal scale
g_{\mathrm{bar}}^{\star}
at which the RAR predicts the maximum DM acceleration.

In the first category, the DM acceleration decreases as the baryonic acceleration increases, whereas in the second, it grows with it. As an illustration, Ref.
Lelli2018
discusses two extreme members of the SPARC sample that belong to these two categories:

1.

The massive spiral galaxy NGC 2841, with flat rotation velocities of order
300\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
, reaches

within a few kiloparsecs of the center, where the DM contribution is negligible. At large radii (
\sim 70\mathrm{kpc}
),

decreases to

, while
g_{\mathrm{bar}}\lesssim 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}
, allowing the DM contribution to become significant.

2.

The gas-dominated dwarf galaxy IC 2574, with rotation velocities below
80\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
, reaches a maximal observed acceleration of
\sim 10^{-10.7}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}
at radii of
8
-
10\mathrm{kpc}
, while the baryonic acceleration there is an order of magnitude smaller, indicating a regime dominated by DM.

VI.2
From the Intrinsic DM Halo Scale to the RAR Scale

The AdS-Bose-Einstein condensation framework naturally suggests a line of reasoning that starts from the DM distribution itself. Within this perspective, the empirical correspondence:

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{DM}}(<r)\;\longrightarrow\;g_{\mathrm{DM}}(r)\;\xrightarrow{\;\mathrm{RAR}\;}g_{\mathrm{bar}}(r)\;\longrightarrow\;M_{\mathrm{bar}}(<r)

can be viewed as the manifestation of an effective dynamical chain. The gravitational field generated by the condensate — determined primarily by its ground state together with the lowest excited modes — can set the large-scale gravitational environment in which baryons evolve (see Sect.
V.7
). In this interpretation, the baryonic distribution is not statistically independent of the dark sector, but instead can adjust to the potential produced by a coherent halo whose structure is governed by a small number of intrinsic parameters.

From this viewpoint, the RAR is not regarded as a fundamental law of dynamics. Rather, it appears as an emergent phenomenological relation encoding the response of baryonic matter to a coherent DM background whose properties are fixed by the condensate spectrum. The observed one-parameter universality of the RAR then reflects the fact that the halo structure is largely controlled by a single intrinsic scale, so that different galaxies probe different regions of the same underlying gravitational configuration.

Within this picture, the emergence of a single effective acceleration scale can be understood as arising from the condensate structure and from its response to the presence of baryons. The physical content of the SPARC RAR may therefore be translated into a constraint on the allowed correlations between the intrinsic condensate parameters
(M_{h},r_{\texttt{c}})
and the baryonic mass distribution, once the halo shape parameter
\zeta
is fixed.

The halo is characterized by a distinguished length scale
r_{\texttt{c}}
, together with a discrete excitation spectrum separated from the ground state by a gap of order:

\displaystyle\Delta E\sim\frac{\hbar c}{r_{\texttt{c}}}\,.

(123)

In the regime where the baryonic gravitational field acts as a weak perturbation of the condensate, the response of the system is expected to be dominated by the lowest excitations of this spectrum. To leading order, the condensate wave-function can therefore be described by a truncated expansion over the lowest AdS modes, parametrized by a small set of amplitudes:
a_{000}
for the ground state, together with
a_{01m}
and
a_{100}
for the first angular and radial excitations (see again Sect.
V.7
). These coefficients encode the linear response of the condensate to the external baryonic potential and determine the corresponding modification of the halo density profile in the baryon-halo interface region.

A first-principles determination of these amplitudes would require a linear-response analysis formulated in the AdS mode basis, in which the baryonic gravitational potential induces mixing between the ground state and the lowest excited modes of the condensate. Such a calculation could in principle generate an effective acceleration scale
g_{\dagger}=g_{\dagger}(\varkappa,\zeta)
whose value is controlled primarily by the intrinsic condensate parameters and only weakly affected by galaxy-dependent details of the baryonic distribution. Carrying out this program would require a systematic treatment of the condensate response beyond the present phenomenological level, and is therefore left for future work.

Nevertheless, even at the phenomenological level, one can already extract a robust scaling relation. Evaluating the DM acceleration at radii of order the core scale,
r\sim r_{\texttt{c}}
, Eqs. (
VI.1
) and (
112
) give:

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{DM}}(r\sim r_{\texttt{c}})\sim\alpha(\zeta)\,g_{\star}\,,

(124)

where the coefficient
\alpha(\zeta)
is a dimensionless shape factor obtained by evaluating the dimensionless halo-acceleration profile at radii of order
r_{\texttt{c}}
. In the simplest spherical ground-state description, it is determined entirely by the halo profile. In particular, for the phenomenologically relevant case
\zeta=2
, one finds
\alpha(2)\sim 0.18
at
r=r_{\texttt{c}}
(see Eq. (
113
)); in this case
r_{\texttt{c}}
lies slightly outside the radius at which
g_{\mathrm{DM}}(r)
reaches its maximum (see Eq. (
V.3
)).

This estimate provides a natural bridge between the intrinsic halo scale
g_{\star}
and the empirical RAR scale
g_{\dagger}
. Observationally,
g_{\dagger}\sim 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}
characterizes the transition between baryon-dominated and DM-dominated dynamics. In the RAR, this transition corresponds to the regime in which the baryonic and dark contributions to the total acceleration become comparable. Indeed, from Eqs. (
115
) and (
117
), if:

\displaystyle\frac{g_{\mathrm{bar}}}{g_{\dagger}}=\mathcal{O}(1)\,,

(125)

then the interpolation functions are themselves of order unity, and we have:

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{bar}}\sim g_{\mathrm{DM}}\sim g_{\dagger}\,,

(126)

up to numerical coefficients of order one.

In the framework developed here, the halo-side radial domain most relevant for comparison with the RAR is the baryon-halo interface region, where the condensate is most sensitive to baryonic perturbations and where the lowest excited modes attain their largest radial support. As discussed in Sect.
V.7
, this region is expected to lie at radii of order
r\sim r_{\texttt{c}}
. It is therefore natural to regard
g_{\mathrm{DM}}(r\sim r_{\texttt{c}})
as the characteristic halo-side acceleration entering the phenomenological comparison. This should not, however, be interpreted as implying that
g_{\mathrm{DM}}(r_{\texttt{c}})
must coincide with
g_{\dagger}
in every galaxy. The condition that baryonic and dark contributions be comparable characterizes only the crossover portion of the universal RAR, and an individual galaxy need not probe that regime near
r\sim r_{\texttt{c}}
, or even within the observed radial range at all. The more robust statement is therefore that the intrinsic halo scale
g_{\star}
is naturally of the same order as the empirical scale
g_{\dagger}
, whereas the local interface value
g_{\mathrm{DM}}(r\sim r_{\texttt{c}})\sim\alpha(\zeta)\,g_{\star}
is determined by the halo profile and need not itself equal
g_{\dagger}
. Only in galaxies for which the baryon-DM transition occurs near
r\sim r_{\texttt{c}}
does one expect the DM acceleration in that radial domain to be of order
g_{\dagger}
. In systems sampling a different portion of the universal RAR curve, the local relation is less direct, even though the same intrinsic halo scale
g_{\star}
continues to control the overall order of magnitude.

Numerical consistency check.

For reference:

\displaystyle g_{\star}=\frac{GM_{h}}{r_{\texttt{c}}^{2}}\sim 1.4\times 10^{-10}\left(\frac{M_{h}}{10^{9}\,M_{\odot}}\right)\left(\frac{1\,\mathrm{kpc}}{r_{\texttt{c}}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\,,

(127)

which is naturally of the same order as the empirical SPARC value:

\displaystyle g_{\dagger}\sim(1\text{-}2)\times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\,.

(128)

This agreement should be read as an order-of-magnitude consistency check rather than as a first-principles prediction of the halo parameters. In particular, the locally sampled acceleration near
r\sim r_{\texttt{c}}
is smaller than
g_{\star}
by the profile factor
\alpha(\zeta)
, and may be further shifted by baryonic geometry and by small admixtures of excited condensate modes. The robust statement is therefore not that
g_{\mathrm{DM}}(r_{\texttt{c}})
universally equals
g_{\dagger}
, but rather that the intrinsic halo scale
g_{\star}
naturally falls in the observed galactic range.

Why dwarf galaxies can naturally yield
g_{\star}\sim g_{\dagger}
.

The near coincidence between
g_{\star}
and
g_{\dagger}
is especially natural in dwarf galaxies. In these systems, baryons remain dynamically subdominant over most of the radial range, including around
r\sim r_{\texttt{c}}
, where one typically has
g_{\mathrm{bar}}(r_{\texttt{c}})\ll g_{\dagger}
. The point
r\sim r_{\texttt{c}}
therefore already lies on the low-acceleration, DM-dominated branch of the RAR, rather than at the baryon-DM crossover itself. In this regime, Eq. (
119
) implies

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{DM}}(r_{\texttt{c}})\sim\sqrt{g_{\dagger}\,g_{\mathrm{bar}}(r_{\texttt{c}})}\,.

(129)

On the other hand, within the AdS-Bose-Einstein condensate halo model,
g_{\mathrm{DM}}(r_{\texttt{c}})\sim\alpha(\zeta)\,g_{\star}
, with
\alpha(\zeta)\sim 0.18
for
\zeta=2
. Equating the two estimates gives:

\displaystyle g_{\star}\sim\frac{1}{\alpha(\zeta)}\sqrt{g_{\dagger}\,g_{\mathrm{bar}}(r_{\texttt{c}})}\,.

(130)

This shows why dwarf galaxies can naturally satisfy
g_{\star}\sim g_{\dagger}
without requiring
g_{\mathrm{DM}}(r_{\texttt{c}})
itself to be equal to
g_{\dagger}
. If
g_{\mathrm{bar}}(r_{\texttt{c}})
lies one to two orders of magnitude below
g_{\dagger}
, then the geometric mean
\sqrt{g_{\dagger}\,g_{\mathrm{bar}}(r_{\texttt{c}})}
lies only a factor of a few below
g_{\dagger}
; dividing by
\alpha(\zeta)\sim 0.18
brings the inferred
g_{\star}
back to the same order of magnitude as
g_{\dagger}
. Thus, dwarf galaxies do not generally satisfy
g_{\mathrm{DM}}(r_{\texttt{c}})\sim g_{\dagger}
; rather, they support the weaker but more robust conclusion that the intrinsic halo scale
g_{\star}
is naturally of the same order as the universal empirical scale
g_{\dagger}
, without fine-tuning of the halo parameters.

In this sense, the robust identification is between the intrinsic halo scale
g_{\star}
and the empirical RAR scale
g_{\dagger}
, whereas the local quantity
g_{\mathrm{DM}}(r_{\texttt{c}})
depends on how a given galaxy samples the universal RAR curve.

VI.3
Scaling Condition for RAR Universality

This subsection emphasizes that the existence of a universal RAR places non-trivial constraints on any halo model with a finite total mass, i.e., such that
F(\infty,\zeta)<\infty
. The argument developed below, therefore, does not rely on the specific AdS-Bose-Einstein condensate realization, but follows more generally from the combination of a cored density profile, a finite halo mass, and the empirical universality of the RAR. As a consequence, the conclusions apply to a broad class of physically admissible halo models rather than to a particular microscopic construction.

For a given value of
\zeta
, the circular velocity generated by the condensate can be written as (see Eqs. (
76
) and (
71
)):

\displaystyle v^{2}(r)=\frac{GM_{h}}{r_{\texttt{c}}}\,\frac{F(x,\zeta)}{x\,F(\infty,\zeta)}\,,\quad x=\frac{r}{r_{\texttt{c}}}\,.

(131)

In the quasi-flat regime, typically corresponding to
x=\mathcal{O}(1
-
5)
, the velocity varies only weakly with radius, and one may write (see Eq. (
80
)):

\displaystyle v_{f}^{2}\sim\eta(\zeta)\,\frac{GM_{h}}{r_{\texttt{c}}}\,,

(132)

where
\eta(\zeta)
is a dimensionless coefficient determined solely by the halo shape.

Empirically, the SPARC RAR implies the scaling
lelli2016SPARC
;
McGaugh2016
:

\displaystyle g_{\dagger}\sim\frac{v_{f}^{4}}{GM_{\mathrm{bar}}}\,,

(133)

which is closely related to the baryonic Tully-Fisher relation. Combining Eqs. (
132
) and (
133
), we obtain:

\displaystyle g_{\dagger}\sim\eta(\zeta)^{2}\,G\,\frac{M_{h}^{\,2}}{r_{\texttt{c}}^{\,2}\,M_{\mathrm{bar}}}\,.

(134)

Equation (
134
) shows that reproducing a universal value of
g_{\dagger}
across galaxies requires, at least at the level of scaling, a correlated scaling between halo and baryonic parameters. In the present variables, the condition for RAR universality takes the form:

\displaystyle\frac{M_{h}^{\,2}}{r_{\texttt{c}}^{\,2}\,M_{\mathrm{bar}}}\approx\mathrm{const.}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad M_{h}\propto r_{\texttt{c}}\,\sqrt{M_{\mathrm{bar}}}\,.

(135)

This relation should not be interpreted as a fine-tuning requirement, but rather as a consistency condition expressing the fact that halo structure and baryonic content cannot remain statistically independent if a universal RAR is to emerge. Within the AdS-Bose-Einstein condensation framework, such a correlation can naturally arise from the response of the condensate to baryonic matter, mediated by the lowest excited modes and controlled by the intrinsic scale
r_{\texttt{c}}
.

VI.4
Synthesis and Perspective

The analysis developed in Sects.
V
and
VI
suggests a coherent link between the internal structure of a finite-mass DM halo and the empirical RAR observed in galaxies. Starting from a minimal AdS-Bose-Einstein condensation description of the halo, characterized by a core scale
r_{\texttt{c}}
, a total mass
M_{h}
, and a shape parameter
\zeta
, we have derived the corresponding mass, velocity, and acceleration profiles within a fully Newtonian framework. These profiles naturally introduce an intrinsic halo acceleration scale
g_{\star}={GM_{h}}/{r_{\texttt{c}}^{2}}
, which governs both the inner behavior of the halo and its asymptotic Newtonian regime.

When baryons are included, the condensate no longer remains in its spherically symmetric ground state but responds through the excitation of its lowest AdS modes. This response may induce an effective coupling between baryonic and DM accelerations, leading to the appearance of an emergent acceleration scale
g_{\dagger}
that controls the RAR. In dwarf galaxies, which predominantly probe the low-acceleration regime, the intrinsic halo scale
g_{\star}
can then be reflected in the observed relation, explaining why these systems naturally populate the DM-dominated branch of the RAR.

The empirical universality of the RAR further implies a correlated scaling between halo and baryonic parameters. In the present framework, this requirement takes the form
M_{h}\propto r_{\texttt{c}}\,\sqrt{M_{\mathrm{bar}}}
(see Eq. (
135
)), which expresses the fact that halo structure and baryonic content cannot be statistically independent if a galaxy-independent acceleration scale is to emerge. Within the AdS-Bose-Einstein condensation scenario, this relation is not interpreted as a fine-tuning, but rather as the possible consequence of the dynamical response of a coherent condensate to the gravitational field generated by baryonic matter.

Taken together, these results are consistent with the interpretation of the RAR as an emergent phenomenon arising from the interaction between a coherent dark-sector background and baryonic structure, rather than as an indication of a modification of the gravitational law. A first-principles derivation of the condensate’s linear response to baryonic perturbations, which lies beyond the scope of the present work, would provide an important test of this interpretation and could help determine the acceleration scale
g_{\dagger}
directly from the microscopic parameters of the model.

The discussion of Sects.
V
-
VI
has been carried out using a Newtonian description of galactic dynamics, while the microscopic organization of the dark sector was formulated in terms of an effective AdS geometry. It is therefore necessary to clarify the consistency between these two levels of description, and to show that the use of Newtonian dynamics for rotation curves remains justified despite the AdS-based spectral structure underlying the condensate.

VII
Is a Newtonian Treatment Legitimate, Although the Condensate Lives in an AdS Geometry?

At first sight, it may appear inconsistent to describe the condensate using Newtonian dynamics while its microscopic modes are defined in an effective AdS background. However, the two descriptions correspond to different levels of approximation, and their compatibility can be understood from the appropriate non-relativistic and weak-field limits of the underlying relativistic theory.

Non-relativistic/weak-field limit.

Galaxy rotation curves probe orbital velocities satisfying
v\ll c
and dynamical time scales that are much longer than the intrinsic microscopic time scales associated with the condensate modes. In this regime, the motion of baryonic tracers (stars, gas, and stellar clusters) is governed by the slow-motion, weak-field limit of the relativistic gravitational dynamics. In this limit, the equations of motion reduce to Newtonian mechanics, provided that the gravitational potential
\Phi
satisfies:

\displaystyle\frac{|\Phi|}{c^{2}}\ll 1\,.

(136)

This condition is well satisfied on galactic scales, where typical potentials are of order
|\Phi|/c^{2}\sim 10^{-6}
or smaller. Consequently, the use of Newtonian dynamics to relate the enclosed mass to the circular velocity remains well justified, even if the microscopic description of the dark sector involves a relativistic field theory defined in an effective AdS geometry.

AdS as an effective background for the dark sector.

In the present scenario, the AdS structure is introduced only to organize the normal-mode spectrum of the dark sector and to fix the condensate scale
r_{\texttt{c}}=\varkappa^{-1}
through the discrete Fronsdal spectrum. It does not imply that the galactic gravitational field itself is AdS. Rather, AdS acts as an effective kinematical background governing the long-wavelength excitations of the gluonic condensate, while the spacetime relevant for galactic dynamics remains in the weak-field regime.

The motion of baryonic matter is therefore determined by the usual Newtonian potential sourced by:

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm tot}=\rho_{\rm bar}+\rho_{\rm DM}\,,

(137)

for which
|\Phi|/c^{2}\ll 1
on galactic scales. In this sense, the AdS structure fixes the internal spectrum and correlation length of the dark component without modifying the external gravitational field experienced by baryonic tracers.

Newton-Hooke correspondence and the effective harmonic structure.

A useful consistency check is obtained by considering the non-relativistic limit of the static AdS metric. In the weak-field approximation, the timelike component of the metric may be written as:

\displaystyle g_{tt}\sim\left(1+\frac{2\Phi}{c^{2}}\right)c^{2}\,,

(138)

where
\Phi
is the effective Newtonian potential. Comparing this expression with the AdS metric component:

\displaystyle g_{tt}=(1+\varkappa^{2}r^{2})c^{2}\quad\text{(see Eq. \eqref{metCom})}\,,

(139)

one obtains — to leading order in the weak-field limit:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm AdS}(r)\sim\frac{c^{2}}{2}\,\varkappa^{2}r^{2}\,.

(140)

The corresponding non-relativistic equation of motion then reads:

\displaystyle\ddot{\bm{r}}=-\nabla\Phi_{\rm AdS}=-\varkappa^{2}c^{2}\,\bm{r}\,,

(141)

which corresponds to a harmonic-type contribution controlled by the AdS curvature scale. This behavior is consistent with the Newton-Hooke limit of AdS symmetry, in which the non-relativistic dynamics acquires a quadratic term reflecting the presence of a negative cosmological constant.

It is important to emphasize that this effective harmonic term should not be interpreted as a physical Newtonian trapping potential acting on baryonic matter. In the present framework, the discrete and oscillator-like structure of the spectrum discussed in Sects.
II
-
III
originates from the global geometry of AdS itself, in particular from the existence of a timelike boundary and the associated normal-mode quantization conditions. The Newton-Hooke correspondence, therefore, provides only a macroscopic analogy for the role of curvature in the non-relativistic limit. The actual spectral discreteness responsible for the condensate structure is a genuinely relativistic and geometric effect, and does not arise from a classical harmonic potential in ordinary Newtonian gravity.

Newtonian gravity for rotation curves as a macroscopic closure.

The comparison with observed rotation curves ultimately requires a relation between the enclosed mass and the circular velocity. In the weak-field, slow-motion regime, the orbital velocity of a test body in a quasi-spherical gravitational field satisfies:

\displaystyle v^{2}(r)\sim\frac{GM_{\rm tot}(r)}{r}\,,

(142)

which follows from the Newtonian limit of the relativistic equations of motion. Within the present framework, the AdS structure does not modify this macroscopic relation. Its role is to determine the internal organization of the dark sector, and in particular the DM density profile
\rho_{\rm DM}(r)
obtained from the lowest AdS modes and their baryon-induced admixtures. Once the mass distribution is specified, Eq. (
142
) provides a consistent closure for the dynamics of baryonic tracers at galactic scales.

This description remains valid as long as the system stays in the weak-field, non-relativistic regime, namely, provided that:

1.

The orbital velocities satisfy
v\ll c
.

2.

The gravitational potential obeys
|\Phi|/c^{2}\ll 1
.

3.

One does not attempt to describe genuinely relativistic phenomena such as strong lensing or strong-field dynamics.

These conditions are well satisfied in galaxies, supporting the use of the Newtonian relation (
142
) even though the microscopic description of the dark sector involves an effective AdS geometry.

A more systematic formulation.

A fully explicit derivation could be obtained by starting from a relativistic scalar field propagating on the effective curved background and performing a controlled non-relativistic expansion. Writing the field in the standard form

\displaystyle\varphi(t,\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m_{\rm eff}c^{2}t/\hbar}\psi(t,\mathbf{x})\,,

(143)

and assuming that the residual field
\psi
varies slowly compared with the Compton time scale, the relativistic field equation reduces, at leading order in
1/c
, to a Schrödinger- or Gross-Pitaevskii-type equation defined on the spatial slices, whose metric is inherited from the effective background geometry.

The weak gravitational field generated by baryons and by the condensate then enters through a Newtonian potential satisfying a Poisson equation sourced by the total mass density. In this formulation, the use of Newtonian dynamics for galactic rotation curves is not an
ad hoc
assumption, but can be viewed as the natural macroscopic limit of the underlying relativistic field theory describing the dark condensate coupled to gravity.

In summary, the use of Newtonian dynamics for the description of galactic rotation curves is consistent with an AdS-based microscopic description of the condensate. In the present framework, the role of the AdS geometry is to determine the mode structure of the dark sector and to fix the characteristic core scale of the halo, while the motion of baryonic tracers takes place in the weak-field, non-relativistic regime relevant for galactic dynamics.

Possible curvature-induced contributions may arise in the non-relativistic limit as quadratic corrections of the Newton-Hooke type. However, such terms are expected to remain parametrically small on kiloparsec scales and therefore should not affect, at leading order, the standard relation between enclosed mass and circular velocity. As a result, the usual Newtonian closure (
142
), which connects the density profile to the observed rotation curve, remains applicable even though the microscopic organization of the dark component is governed by an effective AdS geometry.

VIII
Conclusion and Outlook

The existence of a nearly universal acceleration scale in rotationally supported galaxies, encoded in the RAR, suggests that the structure of DM halos may reflect an intrinsic infrared property of the dark sector rather than being determined solely by galaxy-by-galaxy details of formation history. In this work, we have explored the possibility that this scale may originate from a coherent gluonic dark sector, in which the QCD trace anomaly provides the microscopic seed of an infrared scale, and the large-distance dynamics organizes into a positive-energy lowest-weight unitary structure. Requiring Lorentz covariance then points naturally to the group
\mathrm{SO}(2,3)
, whose discrete spectrum carries a representation-theoretically protected infrared gap and an associated correlation length. This correlation length defines, within the model, the macroscopic size of galactic halos and provides the basis for interpreting the observed acceleration scale as the gravitational imprint of an intrinsic infrared organization of the dark sector.

Within this framework, the infrared gluonic sector admits an effective AdS description whose lowest scalar modes form a Bose-Einstein condensate. The ground state yields a regular cored density profile with finite total mass, while the lowest excited modes provide the leading channels through which the condensate responds to baryonic perturbations. The corresponding halo mass, rotation curve, and radial acceleration can be derived analytically from the condensate structure and introduce the intrinsic acceleration scale
g_{\star}
defined in Eq. (
112
).

We have argued that this scale is naturally of the same order as the empirical acceleration entering the RAR,
g_{\star}\sim g_{\dagger}
. In this perspective, the RAR can be interpreted as the macroscopic manifestation of a coherent dark sector characterized by a spectrally protected infrared scale, rather than as evidence for a modification of gravity or as the outcome of statistical correlations between baryons and halos. The observed universality of the relation then reflects the fact that halo structure is controlled, to leading order, by a small number of intrinsic parameters fixed by the infrared spectrum.

The present work should be regarded as a structural and phenomenological proposal. A major next step will be a first-principles calculation of the condensate response to baryonic gravity, formulated as a linear-response problem in the AdS mode basis. Such a calculation could help clarify how the empirical scale
g_{\dagger}
emerges from the intrinsic parameters
(\varkappa,\zeta)
and from the mixing between the ground state and the lowest excited modes, and would therefore provide a quantitative test of the mechanism proposed here.

Further progress also requires clarifying the relation between the microscopic gluonic sector and its effective infrared description, as well as the cosmological conditions under which a coherent color-singlet component can survive from the confinement epoch to galactic times. On the phenomenological side, the model should be confronted systematically with high-quality rotation curves, the SPARC sample, and dwarf-galaxy observations, including possible signatures of dipolar response, breathing modes, and other condensate-specific environmental effects.

More generally, the present scenario suggests that the dark component behaves as a coherent medium whose large-scale properties are fixed by an underlying infrared spectral organization. In such a picture, cored halos, finite-mass profiles, and the emergence of a nearly universal acceleration scale are not independent empirical facts, but different manifestations of the same underlying structure. Whether this interpretation can be elevated to a predictive theory will depend on the success of the linear-response program outlined above, but the results obtained here suggest that the observed galactic acceleration scale traces a fundamental infrared property of the dark sector.

Viewed from this perspective, it is instructive to compare the present framework with other DM scenarios in which large-scale coherence also emerges as a fundamental ingredient.

Comparison with ultralight-boson scenarios.

Models based on axions or other ultralight bosons, often referred to as fuzzy or wave DM
Hu2000
;
Schive2014
;
hui2017
, provide a natural point of comparison with the present framework. In those approaches, coherence arises from an extremely small particle mass, leading to macroscopic de Broglie wavelengths and dynamics governed by the Schrödinger-Poisson system. In the present scenario, by contrast, coherence is not tied to the introduction of an ultralight elementary particle but to the existence of a spectrally protected infrared structure in a color-singlet gluonic sector. The characteristic halo scale is therefore set by the correlation length associated with the lowest-weight spectrum, rather than by a de Broglie wavelength.

Although both classes of models can produce cored density profiles and wave-like phenomena, they attribute these features to different physical mechanisms. In particular, the connection to the RAR is indirect in ultralight-boson models, whereas in the present framework, the appearance of a characteristic acceleration scale is tied to the intrinsic parameters of the condensate and to its response to baryonic gravity. Distinguishing between these possibilities will likely require going beyond static rotation curves and studying time-dependent, environmental, and strongly non-spherical effects in the dark component.

Large-radius regime and weak-lensing extensions of the RAR.

It is also useful to comment on recent weak-lensing analyses (see, for instance, Refs.
Brouwer2021
;
Mistele2024
) that extend the radial acceleration relation down to accelerations as low as
g\sim 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}
, corresponding to projected distances of order
\mathrm{Mpc}
from galaxy centres. These studies report a smooth continuation of the SPARC relation into the very low-acceleration regime and have been interpreted as favouring an effective outer behavior close to that produced by a density profile of the form
\rho(r)\propto r^{-2}
.

At first sight, this may seem in tension with the class of finite-mass halo profiles considered here. In particular, for the phenomenologically relevant case
\zeta=2
, the density falls off as
\rho(r)\sim r^{-4}
at large radii. However, the weak-lensing regime probes distances at which the notion of a strictly isolated halo becomes less clear. In the present framework, each halo formally extends to infinity while still carrying a finite total mass, so that at sufficiently large distances the local DM density should be viewed not as the asymptotic tail of a single halo, but as the superposition of contributions from many halos and from the surrounding large-scale environment.

From this viewpoint, the effective behavior inferred from weak lensing need not coincide with the intrinsic outer profile of an individual condensate halo. Rather, it may reflect a collective background generated by the overlap of many finite-mass halos. In particular, if halo centres are distributed throughout space, the cumulative contribution of distant halos can be substantially shallower than the
r^{-4}
tail of each individual profile, thereby producing an effective large-radius behavior closer to that inferred from weak-lensing analyses. The weak-lensing extension of the RAR would then probe the onset of this collective regime, rather than the genuine asymptotic profile of a single isolated halo.

In this sense, the apparent tension is not necessarily a contradiction, but may instead indicate that the very low-acceleration, megaparsec-scale regime is sensitive to environmental superposition effects that lie beyond the single-halo description developed in the present work. A more complete treatment of this regime would require embedding the finite-mass condensate halo in a population of neighbouring halos and in the corresponding large-scale dark-sector background.

Cosmological origin of the infrared scale.

Beyond these large-scale environmental effects, a further open question concerns the origin of the infrared scale itself. In the present analysis, the correlation length
r_{\texttt{c}}
(equivalently, the frequency scale
\omega_{\mathrm{AdS}}=c/r_{\texttt{c}}
) has been treated as an intrinsic property of the dark sector, inferred from galactic phenomenology and tied to the lowest-weight structure of the underlying
\mathrm{SO}(2,3)
representation. It is natural, however, to ask whether this scale may originate from an earlier cosmological epoch. In particular, one may envisage that it emerges from a primordial phase of the dark sector, possibly associated with a high-energy transition such as the QCD epoch, and is subsequently transported to late times through cosmic expansion. In such a picture, a microscopic frequency scale at formation could be redshifted into the present-day value of
\omega_{\mathrm{AdS}}
, thereby linking the macroscopic size of galactic halos to a primordial infrared imprint. While simple scaling arguments suggest that relations between the characteristic energy, temperature, and size of the condensate may arise along these lines, a consistent dynamical treatment of the formation and evolution of the condensate lies beyond the scope of the present work and will be addressed elsewhere.
