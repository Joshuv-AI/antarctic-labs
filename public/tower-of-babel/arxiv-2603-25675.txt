Atomic Gradient Flows: Gradient Flows on Sparse Representations 0 0 footnotetext: 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 47J35, 49J27, 49J52, 46A55, 28A33, 47A52
Christian Amend, Marcello Carioni, Konstantinos Zemas

Abstract
One of the most popular approaches for solving total variation-regularized optimization problems in the space of measures are Particle Gradient Flows (PGFs). These restrict the problem to linear combinations of Dirac deltas and then perform a Euclidean gradient flow in the weights and positions, significantly reducing the computational cost while still decreasing the energy. In this work, we generalize PGFs to convex optimization problems in arbitrary Banach spaces, which we call Atomic Gradient Flows (AGFs). To this end, the crucial ingredient turns out to be the right notion of particles , or atoms , chosen here as the extremal points of the unit ball of the regularizer. This choice is motivated by the Krein–Milman theorem , which ensures that minimizers can be approximated by linear combinations of extremal points or, as we call them, sparse representations . We investigate metric gradient flows of the optimization problem when restricted to such sparse representations, for which we define a suitable discretized functional that we show to be to be consistent with the original problem via the means of Γ \Gamma -convergence. We prove that the resulting evolution of the latter is well-defined using a minimizing movement scheme, and we establish conditions ensuring λ \lambda -convexity and uniqueness of the flow. These conditions crucially depend on the geometric properties of the set of extremal points as a metric space. Then, using Choquet’s theorem , we lift the problem into the Wasserstein space on weights and extremal points, and consider Wasserstein gradient flows in this lifted setting. As observed for PGFs, this lifted perspective is essential for understanding stability and convergence properties of AGFs. Our main result is that the lifting of the AGF evolution is again a metric gradient flow in the Wasserstein space, verifying the consistency of the approach with respect to a Wasserstein-type dynamic. Finally, we illustrate the applicability of AGFs to several relevant infinite-dimensional problems, including optimization of functions of bounded variation and curves of measures regularized by Optimal Transport-type penalties.



1
Introduction

Solving convex optimization problems posed in infinite-dimensional spaces has long been a central challenge in optimization. Over the years, a wide range of algorithms with rigorous convergence schemes have been devised in order to efficiently face this challenge, developing theory and techniques that are specifically adapted to infinite-dimensional settings. On top of that, infinite-dimensional modeling has surged in popularity in fields that are traditionally more application-driven, such as data-science, inverse problems and imaging.

In this context, variational problems posed over spaces of measures have attracted growing attention, with a particularly effective approach being the so-called
Particle Gradient Flows
(PGFs). Roughly speaking, given an ambient space
X
, PGFs solve a convex optimization problem in the space of measures
M(X)
of the form

\displaystyle\inf_{\mu\in M(X)}F\left(\int_{X}\phi\,d\mu\right)+\|\mu\|_{TV}\,,

(1.1)

by restricting the search to empirical (or, so called,
sparse
) measures of the form

\displaystyle\mu_{\rm sparse}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}\delta_{x^{j}}\,,\ \ \text{where }(c^{j},x^{j})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times X\,.

The resulting optimization problem is then solved by performing a gradient flow with respect to the particle weights and positions
(c^{j},x^{j})
driven by the energy (
1.1
). Empirically, PGFs exhibit strong performance provided that the number of particles
n
is big enough. Several works have established theoretical results such as convergence to global minimizers under suitable assumptions, by relating the particle dynamics to the Wasserstein gradient flow of the original functional
[
21
,
24
,
49
,
32
]
. Moreover, variants in the space of measures have been considered
[
48
,
23
,
22
,
7
]
and applications to machine learning have been explored
[
41
,
1
,
43
,
27
]
.

Generally, PGFs belong to the class of
sparse optimization methods
in infinite-dimensional spaces; one operates by directly optimizing over sparse measures, thus taking advantage of the sparse structure of the problem. Due to its simplicity, it is natural to ask whether approaches similar to PGFs can be applied to more general infinite-dimensional optimization problems, where it is even unclear what sparse objects should be, which is the guiding question of this work.

Our goal is to extend the PGF approach beyond measure spaces. In particular, in our setting we consider composite convex minimization problems posed on a Banach space
\mathcal{M}
of the form

\inf_{u\in\mathcal{M}}J(u)\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{where}\ \ J(u):=\mathcal{F}(Ku)+\mathcal{R}(u)\,,

(1.2)

where
\mathcal{F}
is a convex fidelity term,
K:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow Y
is a linear operator mapping to a Hilbert space and
\mathcal{R}
is a convex,
1
-homogeneous regularizer, cf.
Section

2
for details.
A key insight underlying our approach comes from recent developments in infinite-dimensional sparsity and representation theory. Sparsity is traditionally understood as the possibility to represent solutions using only a small number of simple building blocks (atoms). In the recent works
[
10
,
14
]
, it has been shown that for optimization problems of the form (
1.2
), the natural atoms are given by the extremal points of the unit ball of the regularizer

\displaystyle{\rm Ext}(B)\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\ B:=\{u\in\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{R}(u)\leqslant 1\}\,.

In particular, when the operator
K
maps into a finite-dimensional space, representer theorems guarantee the existence of solutions that can be expressed as finite linear combinations of such extremal points.
Motivated by this theory, we focus on
sparse ansatzes
of the form

u_{\rm sparse}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}u^{j}\,,\ \text{where }(c^{j},u^{j})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\,,

(1.3)

and
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}:=\overline{{\rm Ext}(B)}^{*}
is the weak*-closure of the set of extremal points.
This representation serves as the foundation for a generalized particle-based optimization framework, applicable to a wide class of convex problems.

Crucially, on the compact set
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
, the weak*-topology of
\mathcal{M}
can be metrized by a metric
d_{*}
. Thanks to this, we can take advantage of the well-developed theory of gradient flows in metric spaces
[
4
]
in order to construct the gradient flow of

(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u}):=(c^{j},u^{j})_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}}^{n}\,,

according to the energy in (
1.2
) restricted to sparse elements of the form (
1.3
), that is,

J_{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u}):=J\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}u^{j}\right)\,.

(1.4)

We name such general optimization schemes Atomic Gradient Flows (in short AGFs) to indicate that the gradient flow is performed at the level of the atoms. More precisely, we construct AGFs through a minimizing movement approach, whose well-posedness will be based on the assumptions for the optimization problem (
1.2
), cf. again
Section

2
for the precise setup.
As detailed also in Subsection
6.1
, our work directly generalizes PGFs, since for
\mathcal{R}:=\|\cdot\|_{TV}
, and
\mathcal{M}:=M(X)
, the extremal points will be
{\rm Ext}(B)=\{\pm\delta_{x}:x\in X\}
.

Importantly, the motivation for the definition of AGFs is that the discretized functional
J_{n}
provides a variational approximation of the target functional
J

in the sense of
\Gamma
-convergence. As a consequence, minimizers of
J_{n}
converge, as
n\rightarrow+\infty
, to minimizers of
J
, at least under suitable boundedness assumptions on the weights. This provides a theoretical justification for studying the gradient flow of
J_{n}
: for large
n
the sparse dynamics of
J_{n}
can be viewed as a tractable proxy for approximating minimizers of the infinite-dimensional problem (
1.2
) with respect to
J
.

After introducing the main setup and recalling standard convexity and derivative notions in metric spaces in
Section

2
, in
Section

3
we introduce the discretized functional and first justify the consistency of our approach using
\Gamma
-convergence, cf. Subsection
3.1
. In Subsection
3.2
we establish the well-posedness of AGFs via a minimizing movement scheme, and then turn to the investigation of qualitative properties of the flow. In particular, in Subsections
3.3
-
3.4
we identify conditions on the linear operator
K
and on the interaction between the regularizer and the geometry of extremal points which ensure that
J_{n}
is
\lambda
-convex for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. This property is fundamental in the theory of gradient flows in metric spaces, as it guarantees that minimizing movements are curves of maximal slope with respect to the local slope

|\partial J_{n}|
. We then address uniqueness of the minimizing movements defining AGFs, under the additional assumption that the set of extremal points is non-positively curved (NPC). The NPC condition is a classical geometric assumption in the theory of gradient flows in metric spaces and plays a central role in establishing contraction and regularity properties
[
37
]
.

In Section
4
we introduce and analyze a suitable lifting of the functional
J
to the space
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}})
of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
, with the aim of studying its dynamics as a
Wasserstein gradient flow
.
This construction is motivated by the observation that, as shown in
[
21
]
, interpreting PGFs as Wasserstein gradient flows in the space of probability measures over weights and positions is crucial for understanding both their dynamical behavior and their convergence properties.
In our more general setting however, such an interpretation is not immediate, since the underlying variational problem is formulated in a Banach space and is therefore not directly expressible in terms of probability measures.
Instead, however, here one can rely on
Choquét’s theorem

[
40
]
, which ensures that every
u\in B
can be represented as the weak barycenter of a positive measure
\mu\in M_{+}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})
supported on the set of extremal points,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle u=\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}v\,d\mu(v)\,.

This representation provides the bridge between the original Banach-space formulation and its lifted counterpart in Wasserstein space. Thanks to this identification, and further factorizing the mass of the measure
\mu\in M_{+}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})
in the domain, following
[
13
]
, one can define the following lifted problem as

\displaystyle\min_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}})}\mathcal{J}(\nu),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\ \ \mathcal{J}(\nu):=\mathcal{F}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}c^{2}Ku\,d\nu(c,u)\right)+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}c^{2}\,d\nu(c,u)\,.

(1.5)

We advocate that the convergence properties of AGFs can be understood by analyzing the metric gradient flow of the lifted functional
\mathcal{J}
. A first crucial observation supporting this perspective is that the minimization problems (
1.5
) and (
1.2
) are equivalent, thanks to the surjectivity of the Choquet representation. This equivalence ensures that the lifted formulation faithfully captures the original variational problem.
After establishing this equivalence and deriving the fundamental properties of the lifted functional in Subsection
4.1
, in the rest of the section we study its minimizing movement scheme. Our setting differs substantially from that of
[
21
]
: In contrast to problems where the extremal points possess additional properties, here we must consider a gradient flow on the metric space
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
, which in general lacks any differential structure. As a consequence, classical tools based on continuity equations and differential calculus are not available, which requires a fully metric approach and significantly complicates the analysis.
We prove well-posedness of the minimizing movement scheme for
\mathcal{J}
and establish its
\lambda
-convexity under the same assumptions that guarantee
\lambda
-convexity of
J_{n}
. In particular, under these conditions, every minimizing movement is a curve of maximal slope with respect to the metric slope
|\partial\mathcal{J}|
.

Finally, in
Section

5
, which contains the main result of this paper, we establish a precise link between the AGF of
J_{n}
and the corresponding metric gradient flow of
\mathcal{J}
. Specifically, we show that if
(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})
is a curve of maximal slope for
J_{n}
with respect to
|\partial J_{n}|
, then the curve of empirical measures

\displaystyle t\mapsto\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{(c_{t}^{j},u_{t}^{j})}

(1.6)

is a curve of maximal slope for
\mathcal{J}
with respect to
|\partial\mathcal{J}|
. The significance of this result is that it shows that the metric gradient flow of
\mathcal{J}
recovers (at a discrete level) the dynamics of the initial AGF and thus sets the necessary basis for analyzing convergence of the AGF to minimizers of
J
when
n\rightarrow+\infty
; this analysis is left to future works. The proof relies on a careful comparison between the metric slopes of
J_{n}
and
\mathcal{J}
, using techniques from semi-discrete optimal transport
[
5
,
38
]
generalized to metric spaces. These tools allow us to localize the slope estimates around each Dirac mass appearing in the empirical representation (
1.6
).

We conclude the paper by presenting in
Section

6
several natural examples illustrating the applicability of AGFs to different variational problems. For each example, we provide the characterization of
\mathrm{Ext}(B)
and analyze the metric induced by the weak*-topology on
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
. We investigate whether this metric space is NPC, which in turn would imply uniqueness of the AGFs, and we examine the structure of the lifting in each case.
More precisely, we cover the following examples: optimization problems in the space of measures, recovering the framework of
[
21
]
; one-dimensional BV functions regularized by their total variation seminorm
[
14
,
18
]
and dynamic problems in the space of time-dependent measures regularized by the Benamou–Brenier energy
[
11
,
12
,
31
,
20
]
. Moreover, we briefly mention how AGFs could be applied to optimization problems regularized with KR-norms
[
18
,
8
]
, scalar differential operators
[
14
,
45
]
and higher dimensional BV functions
[
3
]
.

2
Setting and Preliminaries

Throughout this paper we consider minimization problems of the form

\inf_{u\in\mathcal{M}}J(u)\,,\ \ \mathrm{where}\ J(u):=\mathcal{F}(Ku)+\mathcal{R}(u)\,,

(2.1)

where
\mathcal{M}
is the topological dual of a separable Banach space
\mathcal{C}
. The norm on
\mathcal{C}
will be denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{C}}
and the duality pairing between
u\in\mathcal{M}
and
p\in\mathcal{C}
by
\langle u,p\rangle
. The space
\mathcal{M}
is a Banach space when equipped with the canonical dual norm

\|u\|_{\mathcal{M}}:=\sup\{\langle u,p\rangle\colon\|p\|_{\mathcal{C}}\leqslant 1\}.

(2.2)

The forward operator
K:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow Y
is a linear operator mapping into a Hilbert space
Y
. The inner product and induced norm on
Y
will be denoted by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{Y}
and
\|\cdot\|_{Y}
respectively.
We make the following standard assumptions on
\mathcal{F}
,
K
and
\mathcal{R}
.

(A1)

The forward operator
K:\mathcal{M}\to Y
is linear, weak
∗
-to-weak continuous from
\mathcal{M}
into a Hilbert space
Y
.

(A2)

The fidelity term
\mathcal{F}:Y\to\mathbb{R}
is bounded from below, convex, and twice Fréchet differentiable on
Y
.

(A3)

The regularizer
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{M}\to[0,+\infty]
is convex and positively one-homogeneous,
i.e.
,

\mathcal{R}(\lambda u)=\lambda\mathcal{R}(u)\ \ \forall\lambda\geqslant 0,\ u\in\mathcal{M}\,,

(2.3)

and is also weak
∗
-lower semicontinuous.

(A4)

For every
\alpha\geqslant 0
, the sublevel set

S_{\alpha}^{-}(\mathcal{R}):=\{u\in\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{R}(u)\leqslant\alpha\}\,,

(2.4)

is weak
∗
-compact.

(A5)

The forward operator
K:\mathcal{M}\to Y
is sequentially weak
∗
-to-strong continuous in the domain of
\mathcal{R}
, defined by

\mathrm{Dom}(\mathcal{R}):=\{u\in\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{R}(u)<+\infty\}\,.

(2.5)

Note that, due to
(A1)
, there exists a linear and continuous operator

K^{\ast}:Y\to\mathcal{C}
, the
adjoint operator
of
K
, which satisfies

\langle u,K^{\ast}y\rangle=(Ku,y)_{Y}\ \hskip 10.00002pt\forall u\in\mathcal{M},\ y\in Y\,;

(2.6)

see, for example,
[
16
, Remark 3.2]
. Moreover, the existence of the pre-adjoint
K^{*}
implies the strong-to-strong continuity of
K
on
\mathcal{M}
. Note further that
(A4)
implies that the sublevel sets are weak*-closed and norm bounded.
It is immediate to verify (see also
[
13
, Proposition 2.3]
) that, under the above assumptions, the existence of a minimizer to (
2.1
) is guaranteed.
In what follows, we will denote the unit ball of
\mathcal{R}
by

B:=S^{-}_{1}(\mathcal{R})=\{u\in\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{R}(u)\leqslant 1\}\,.

(2.7)

Definition 2.1
.

An element
u\in B\subset\mathcal{M}
is called an
extremal point
of
B
if there exists no choice of
u_{1},u_{2}\in B
with
u_{1}\neq u_{2}
, and
s\in(0,1)
such that
u=(1-s)u_{1}+su_{2}
. The set of all extremal points of
B
is denoted by
\mathrm{Ext}(B)
. We also set

\tilde{\mathcal{B}}:=\overline{\textup{Ext}(B)}^{*}\,.

(2.8)

A consequence of the
Krein-Milman theorem

[
35
]
is that
B
is the weak*-closure of the convex envelope of
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that by
(A3)
and
(A4)
,
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
is weak
∗
-compact and non-empty.
Since the predual space
\mathcal{C}
is separable, there exists a metric
d_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}
metrizing the weak
∗
-convergence on
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
,
i.e.
, for all sequences
(u_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
and
u\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
we have:

u_{k}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}u\ \text{ as }k\to\infty\iff\lim_{k\to\infty}d_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}(u_{k},u)=0\,.

(2.9)

In particular, one has that
(\tilde{\mathcal{B}},d_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}})
is a compact metric space and thus separable. Moreover, compactness of
(\tilde{\mathcal{B}},d_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}})
guarantees the existence of
u_{1},u_{2}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
such that

d_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}(u_{1},u_{2})=\sup\big\{d_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}(u,v)\colon u,v\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\big\}<+\infty\,.

(2.10)

We next recall some of the necessary terminology for metric spaces. In what follows, given a metric space
(X,d)
, a curve
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to X
is always intended to be continuous with respect to the metric
d
. Of course, the interval of parametrization
[0,1]
can also be replaced with any subinterval of
[0,+\infty)
.

Definition 2.3

(
Geodesic Space
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. For
x,y\in X
, a curve
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow X
is called a
geodesic between
\gamma_{0}:=x
and
\gamma_{1}:=y
, if

d(\gamma_{s},\gamma_{t})=|s-t|\,d(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\hskip 10.00002pt\forall s,t\in[0,1]\,.

(2.11)

Then
(X,d)
is called
geodesically complete
iff for every
x,y\in X
there exists a geodesic
\gamma
between
x
and
y
. The space of all such constant-speed geodesics in
X
, will be denoted by
\mathrm{Geo}(X)
.

Note that for simplicity, we only use constant speed geodesics parametrized on the unit interval.

Definition 2.4

(
Convexity and
\lambda
-convexity along curves
)
.

A functional
F:X\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
is said to be
convex
along a curve
\gamma\colon[0,1]\rightarrow X
,
\gamma_{t}:=\gamma(t)
, iff

F(\gamma_{t})\leqslant(1-t)F(\gamma_{0})+tF(\gamma_{1})\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in[0,1]\,,

(2.12)

and given
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
F
is said to be
\lambda
-
convex
along
\gamma
, iff

F(\gamma_{t})\leqslant(1-t)F(\gamma_{0})+tF(\gamma_{1})-\frac{\lambda}{2}t(1-t)d^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in[0,1]\,.

(2.13)

For metric gradient flows, properties of the metric itself are crucial for the well-posedness of gradient flows, for instance in establishing uniqueness
as in Subsection
3.4
. Moreover, it is well known (cf.
[
37
]
) that
(X,d)
being a so-called
space of global non-positive curvature (NPC) in the sense of Alexandrov
implies regularity of the gradient flows. Recall that there are various equivalent conditions for a geodesic space
(X,d)
to be NPC (for more details see for instance
[
6
,
17
]
).

Definition 2.5

(
NPC space
)
.

A geodesic metric space
(X,d)
is a space of (global)
non-positive curvature
iff for every triplet of points
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},w\in X
and geodesics
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow X
, the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle d^{2}(\gamma_{t},w)\leqslant(1-t)d^{2}(\gamma_{0},w)+td^{2}(\gamma_{1},w)-t(1-t)d^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in[0,1]\,.

(2.14)

Next, we collect some basic definitions of differentiability of curves and functionals on metric spaces, which we will use throughout the sequel, and refer the reader to
[
4
, Chapter 1]
for more details.

Definition 2.6

(
Absolutely continuous curves
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space,
p\in[1,+\infty]
. We say that a curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
belongs to the space of
p
-absolutely continuous curves
AC^{p}([0,1];X)
iff there exists
m\in L^{p}(0,1)
such that

d(\gamma_{s},\gamma_{t})\leqslant\int_{s}^{t}m(r)\,\mathrm{d}r\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\ 0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant 1\,,

(2.15)

and analogously define
AC^{p}([a,b];X)
for all
a,b\in[0,+\infty)
with
a<b
.

In the case
p=1
the above definition reduces to the one of absolutely continuous curves and we will
denote the corresponding space simply with
AC([a,b];X)
. In addition, we set

AC([0,\infty);X):=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}AC([0,n];X)\,,\ \ AC_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,\infty);X):=\{\gamma\colon\gamma\in AC([a,b];X)\ \forall\ 0\leqslant a<b<\infty\}\,.

Definition 2.7

(
Metric derivative
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space. For any
\gamma\in AC([0,1];X)
, we define the metric derivative of
\gamma
at
t\in(0,1)
as

\left|\gamma^{\prime}\right|(t):=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{d(\gamma(t+h),\gamma(t))}{|h|}\,.

(2.16)

The above limit is defined for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in(0,1)
,
|\gamma^{\prime}|\in L^{1}(0,1)
, and
|\gamma^{\prime}|
is exactly the
minimal
function
m\in

L^{1}(0,1)
satisfying the inequality (
2.15
).
We next turn to the standard definitions of differentials for functionals defined on metric spaces.

Definition 2.8

(
Local slope
)
.

Let
F:X\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
with proper effective domain,
i.e.
,

\mathrm{Dom}(F)\colon=\{u\in X\colon F(u)<+\infty\}\neq\emptyset\,.

We define the local slope of
F
at a point
u\in\operatorname{Dom}(F)
as

|\partial F|(u):=\limsup_{w\rightarrow u}\frac{(F(u)-F(w))^{+}}{d(u,w)}\,.

(2.17)

Under suitable assumptions, the local slope is indeed a kind of metric gradient for the functional, in the following sense.

Definition 2.9

(
Strong upper gradient
)
.

A function
g:X\rightarrow[0,+\infty]
is a strong upper gradient for
F:X\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
iff for every
\gamma\in AC([0,1];X)
, the function
g\circ\gamma
is Borel and the following inequality holds:

|F(\gamma(t))-F(\gamma(s))|\leqslant\int_{s}^{t}g(\gamma(r))\left|\gamma^{\prime}\right|(r)\,dr\hskip 10.00002pt\forall 0<s\leqslant t<1\,.

(2.18)

In particular, if
g\circ\gamma\left|\gamma^{\prime}\right|\in L^{1}(0,1)
, then
F\circ\gamma\in AC([0,1];X)
and

\left|(F\circ\gamma)^{\prime}\right|(t)\leqslant g(\gamma(t))\left|\gamma^{\prime}\right|(t)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for }\mathcal{L}^{1}\text{-a.e. }t\in(0,1)\,.

(2.19)

We next recall the concept of curves of maximal slope, which is a suitable generalization of the standard concept of gradient flows in the metric setting.

Definition 2.10

(
Curves of maximal slope
)
.

A curve
\gamma\in AC_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,+\infty);X)
is said to be a curve of maximal slope for a functional
F\colon X\to(-\infty,+\infty]
with respect to its strong upper gradient
g
, iff the function
F\circ\gamma\colon[0,\infty)\to(-\infty,\infty]
is
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e. equal to a non-increasing map
\varphi
, and

\varphi^{\prime}(t)\leqslant-\frac{1}{2}\left|\gamma^{\prime}\right|^{2}(t)-\frac{1}{2}g^{2}(\gamma(t))\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for }\mathcal{L}^{1}\text{-a.e. }t>0\,.

(2.20)

3
Formulation of the Atomic Gradient Flow

In this section we present a discretization approach to problem (
2.1
), which we show to be consistent with the original problem by means of
\Gamma
-convergence. To approximate minimizers of (
2.1
), we then formalize the evolution of the discretized functional through
Atomic Gradient Flows
(in short AGFs) by using a minimizing movement approach.

3.1
Discretization of the functional
J
and consistency

We start by setting the necessary notations to define the restriction of the optimization functional to sparse representations. In what follows, we always follow the convention that components of vectors will be denoted as superscripts, while subscripts will typically denote a time-parameter.

Definition 3.1
.

Recalling the definitions of
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
in (
2.8
) and the functional
J
in (
2.1
), let us fix
L>0
, and a closed and geodesically complete subset
\mathcal{B}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
(cf.
2.3
), and a metric
d_{\mathcal{B}}
metrizing the weak
∗
-convergence on
\mathcal{B}
. Then, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we set

\Omega^{n}_{L}:=[0,L]^{n}\times\mathcal{B}^{n}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\tilde{\Omega}^{n}_{L}:=[0,L]^{n}\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}}^{n}.

(3.1)

We endow
\Omega^{n}_{L}
with the distance

{d_{n}}((\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u}),(\tilde{\mathbf{c}},\tilde{\mathbf{u}})):=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(|c^{j}-\tilde{c}^{j}|^{2}+d^{2}_{\mathcal{B}}(u^{j},\tilde{u}^{j})\big)\right)^{1/2}\,,

(3.2)

and analogously for
\tilde{\Omega}^{n}_{L}
with
d_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}
in place of
d_{\mathcal{B}}
in (
3.2
).
For
n=1
we write
\Omega_{L}:=\Omega_{L}^{1}
and
d_{\Omega}:=d_{1}
,
i.e.
,

d_{\Omega}((c,u),(\tilde{c},\tilde{u}))=\left(|c-\tilde{c}|^{2}+d^{2}_{\mathcal{B}}(u,\tilde{u})\right)^{1/2}\,.

(3.3)

It is well-known that the properties of
(\mathcal{B},d_{\mathcal{B}})
propagate to
(\Omega_{L}^{n},d_{n})
, as the following lemma suggests.

Lemma 3.2
.

The spaces

\Omega_{L}
and
\Omega_{L}^{n}
,
n>1
, are both compact, separable and geodesic metric spaces.
Moreover, a curve

(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})\colon[0,1]\to\Omega_{L}^{n}\,,\ \text{with}\ (\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})=\big((c_{t}^{1},u_{t}^{1}),\dots,(c_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\big)

is a geodesic in
(\Omega_{L}^{n},d_{n})
iff for every
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
the coordinate curve

(c_{t}^{j},u_{t}^{j}):[0,1]\to\Omega
is a geodesic in
(\Omega,d_{\Omega})
, or equivalently, iff

c_{t}^{j}:[0,1]\to[0,L]
and
u_{t}^{j}:[0,1]\to\mathcal{B}
are geodesics in their respective spaces.

Proof.

That
\Omega_{L}
and
\Omega_{L}^{n}
are compact and separable metric spaces is a simple consequence of equipping them with the
\ell^{2}
-metric, cf. (
3.2
).
The existence of geodesics and geodesic completeness follows from the product–geodesic property in,
e.g.
,
[
17
, Proposition 5.3]
. Since we endowed
\Omega_{L}^{n}
with the
\ell_{2}
-metric
d_{n}
,
(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})
is a unit
-speed geodesic in
\Omega_{L}^{n}
iff, for every
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, each coordinate
(c_{t}^{j},u_{t}^{j})
is a unit-speed geodesic in
\left(\Omega,d_{\Omega}\right)
, cf. (
3.3
). As
d_{\Omega}
is the
\ell_{2}
-metric on
[0,L]\times\mathcal{B}
and both spaces in the product are geodesic spaces,
(c_{t}^{j},u_{t}^{j})
is a unit-speed geodesic in
\Omega
iff
c_{t}^{j}
and
u_{t}^{j}
are unit-speed geodesics in
[0,L]
and
\mathcal{B}
, respectively.
∎

Note that above, and also in what follows, with a slight abuse of notation we have reordered the components of a vector
(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\in\Omega_{L}^{n}
into an
n
-component vector with the entries being pairs of the form
(c,u)\in\Omega_{L}
, with the initial enumeration being taken into account, hence identifying

(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u}):=\big((c^{1},\dots,c^{n}),(u^{1},\dots,u^{n})\big)=\big((c^{1},u^{1}),\dots,(c^{n},u^{n})\big)\in\Omega_{L}^{n}\,.

(3.4)

Adopting this notation throughout, we next introduce a
discretized version
of the initial functional
J
of (
2.1
), by restricting it to sparse representations.

Definition 3.3

(
Discretization by restriction to sparse representations
)
.

We define the functional
J_{n}:\Omega_{L}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
as

J_{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u}):=J\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}u^{j}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\in\Omega_{L}^{n}\,.

(3.5)

One defines
J_{n}
on
\tilde{\Omega}_{L}^{n}
in the same way.

In general, by
(A3)
and the fact that
u^{j}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}=\overline{\mathrm{Ext}\{\mathcal{R}(u)\leqslant 1\}}^{\ast}
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
(which in particular implies that either
\mathbb{R}(u^{j})=1
or
u^{j}=0
), one has

\mathcal{R}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{j}u^{j}\right)\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{j}\,,\ \text{where }\alpha^{j}\geqslant 0\hskip 10.00002pt\forall j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\,.

(3.6)

In addition to
(A3)
–

(A5)
, it is convenient to impose the following extra condition on the regularizer
\mathcal{R}
, called the no loss of mass condition.

(A6)

The regularizer
\mathcal{R}
has
no loss of mass
on
\mathcal{B}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
,
i.e.
, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
(\alpha^{j})_{j=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
(u^{j})_{j=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{B}
, it holds that

\mathcal{R}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{j}u^{j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{j}\mathcal{R}(u^{j})\,.

(3.7)

The following assumption will also be necessary for the some of the subsequent results, for which we will mention it explicitly.

(A7)

The closed, geodesically complete set
\mathcal{B}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
does not to contain
0
,
i.e.
,
0\notin\mathcal{B}\,.

Remark 3.4
.

We make the following remarks about the previous definitions and assumptions.

(i) The setting described above for the AGF-evolution
is natural, since firstly, one has that the canonical identification with elements in the lifted Wasserstein-2 space of probability measures (see
Section

4.1
for the respective definitions), given by

\displaystyle(\Omega_{L}^{n},d_{n})\ni(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\mapsto\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{(c^{j},u^{j})}\in(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega^{n}_{L}),\mathcal{W}_{2})

is
1
-Lipschitz, as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{(c^{j},u^{j})},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{(\tilde{c}^{j},\tilde{u}^{j})}\right)\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{\Omega}^{2}\big((c^{j},u^{j}),(\tilde{c}^{j},\tilde{u}^{j})\big)\right)^{1/2}.

Secondly, using the Krein-Milman theorem, we prove in
3.6
that on
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
,
J_{n}{\rightarrow}J
as
n\to\infty
, in the sense of
\Gamma
-convergence. Afterwards, we restrict to a geodesically connected subset
\mathcal{B}
of
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
, which is sensible in the context of gradient flows, and analogous to replacing
\{\pm\delta_{x}:x\in X\}
by
\{\delta_{x}:x\in X\}
for the optimization problem (
1.1
).

(ii) In particular, with the choice
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{TV}
, the latter being the total variation norm in the space of measures, the no loss of mass property (
3.7
) holds.

(iii) It is important to note that many of our results can be proved in the more general setting of unbounded weights in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and without assuming the no loss of mass of property. In those cases, we simply state the result for,
e.g.
,
\Omega_{\infty}:=\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{B}
instead of using
\Omega_{L}
. But for ease of presentation, unless specifically mentioned, we assume for simplicity both
(A6)
-
(A7)
throughout the manuscript.

Due to the compactness of
\Omega_{L}^{n}
and our constitutive assumptions, one can easily prove that
J_{n}
is lower-semicontinuous and thus admits a minimizer in
\Omega_{L}^{n}
by the direct method in the Calculus of Variations.

Lemma 3.5

(
Existence of minimizers for the discretized problem
)
.

Under assumptions
(A1)
-
(A5)
,
J_{n}
is lower-semicontinuous on
\tilde{\Omega}_{n}
, and
\inf_{\tilde{\Omega}_{L}^{n}}J_{n}
admits a minimizer. In addition, the same holds on
\Omega_{L}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
(\mathbf{c}_{\ell},\mathbf{u}_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\tilde{\Omega}_{L}^{n}
be a sequence such that
(\mathbf{c}_{\ell},\mathbf{u}_{\ell})\overset{d_{n}}{\longrightarrow}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\in\tilde{\Omega}_{L}^{n}
as
\ell\to\infty
.
In particular, since
d_{\mathcal{B}}
metrizes the weak*-convergence in
\mathcal{B}
, recalling (
2.9
) and
3.1
, we have that componentwise,

(c_{\ell}^{j})^{2}u_{\ell}^{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}(c^{j})^{2}u^{j}\ \ \text{as }\ell\to\infty\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\,.

Therefore, denoting

x_{\ell}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c_{\ell}^{j})^{2}u_{\ell}^{j}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\textup{and}\hskip 10.00002ptx:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}u^{j}\,,

(3.8)

it holds that
x_{\ell}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}x
as
\ell\to\infty
. In view of
(A1)
, (
3.8
) implies that
Kx_{\ell}\rightharpoonup Kx
in
Y
, and by
(A2)
,
\mathcal{F}(Kx)\leqslant\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}\mathcal{F}(Kx_{\ell})
.
Similarly,
(A3)
combined again with (
3.8
) and the super-additivity of the
\liminf
, yield in total

J_{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\leqslant\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{\ell},\mathbf{u}_{\ell})\,,

(3.9)

i.e.
,
J_{n}
is lower semicontinuous. The existence for minimizers for
J_{n}
follows by the compactness of
\tilde{\Omega}_{L}^{n}
(since
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
is weak
\ast
-compact) as a standard application of the direct method in the Calculus of Variations.

For
\Omega_{L}^{n}
, the same arguments can be repeated verbatim.
∎

The strategy of optimizing
J_{n}
as a substitute for
J
is based on the intuition that the variational problem associated with
J_{n}
provides a consistent approximation of the original problem. More precisely, as
n
increases, one anticipates that minimizers of
J_{n}
will converge, in an appropriate sense, to minimizers of
J
. We will formalize such a result using a
\Gamma
-convergence approach, cf.
[
26
]
for a detailed treatment.

Recalling the notation (
3.4
), we first note that one needs to work with unbounded weights and the full set
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
here in order to compare
J_{n}
with
J
on the domain of
\mathcal{R}
. Thus,
we extend
J_{n}
to
v\in{\rm Dom}(\mathcal{R})
by setting

\displaystyle J_{n}(v):=\begin{cases}J\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}u^{j}\right),&\text{ if }v=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}u^{j}\,,\text{ for some }(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}}^{n}\,,\\
+\infty,&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

(3.10)

Proposition 3.6

(
\Gamma
-Convergence result
)
.

Assume
(A1)
-
(A5)
. With respect to the weak
∗
-topology on
{\rm Dom}(\mathcal{R})
, we have that
J_{n}\overset{\Gamma}{\rightarrow}J
as
n\to\infty
, i.e.,

•

(
\Gamma
-liminf inequality):
For every
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset{\rm Dom}(\mathcal{R})
with
v_{n}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}v\in{\rm Dom}(\mathcal{R})
, it holds that

\displaystyle{J(v)\leqslant\liminf_{n\to\infty}J_{n}(v_{n})}\,.

•

(
\Gamma
-limsup inequality):
For each
v\in{\rm Dom}(\mathcal{R})
, there exists a recovery sequence
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset{\rm Dom}(\mathcal{R})
with
v_{n}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}v\in{\rm Dom}(\mathcal{R})
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}J_{n}(v_{n})\leqslant\displaystyle{J(v)}\,.

Actually, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
v_{n}
is given by some
(\tilde{c}^{j}_{n})_{j=1}^{n}\subset[0,\sqrt{n\mathcal{R}(v)}]
, and
(\tilde{u}_{n}^{j})_{j=1}^{n}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
such that

v_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\tilde{c}_{n}^{j})^{2}\tilde{u}_{n}^{j},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and }\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\tilde{c}_{n}^{j})^{2}\leqslant\mathcal{R}(v).

Proof.

(
\Gamma
-liminf
) Let
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset{\rm Dom}(\mathcal{R})
be such that
v_{n}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}v\in{\rm Dom}(\mathcal{R})
as
n\to\infty
.
If we suppose that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}J_{n}(v_{n})=+\infty
, then there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, we may without restriction assume that
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}J_{n}(v_{n})<\infty
. By the definition of
J_{n}
on
{\rm Dom}(\mathcal{R})
, this implies that each
v_{n}
admits a representation as in (
3.10
), and

J_{n}(v_{n})=J(v_{n}).

Since
J
is weak
∗
-lower semicontinuous and
v_{n}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}v
as
n\to\infty
, we get

\displaystyle J(v)\leqslant\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}J(v_{n})\leqslant\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}J_{n}(v_{n})\,,

which is the desired
\Gamma
-liminf inequality.

(
\Gamma
-limsup
) Take
v\in{\rm Dom}(\mathcal{R})
, for which we can suppose that
\mathcal{R}(v)\neq 0
. Indeed, otherwise,
v=0
, cf.
(A3)
, and thus the recovery sequence would be the trivial one.

We claim that for
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
(\beta^{j}_{n})_{j=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
(u^{j}_{n})_{j=1}^{n}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
such that

v_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\beta_{n}^{j})^{2}u_{n}^{j}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}v,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\beta_{n}^{j})^{2}=\mathcal{R}(v)\,.

(3.11)

Indeed, by the Krein-Milman theorem, there exists a sequence of convex combinations of extremal points
(u_{n}^{j})_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
, with
k_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
, so that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\tilde{\alpha}_{n}^{j}u_{n}^{j}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\frac{v}{\mathcal{R}(v)},\ \text{ with }\tilde{\alpha}_{n}^{j}\geqslant 0\ \text{ and }\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\tilde{\alpha}_{n}^{j}=1\,.

Note that by adding extremal points with weights equal to zero we can assume that
k_{n}
is strictly increasing in
n
and thus
k_{n}\rightarrow+\infty
.
Moreover, by a rescaling argument (and multiplying both sides of the above with
\mathcal{R}(v)
), we can find weights
\alpha_{n}^{j}\geqslant 0
such that

\frac{1}{k_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}(\alpha_{n}^{j})^{2}u_{n}^{j}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}v,\ \text{ with }\alpha_{n}^{j}\geqslant 0\,,\text{ and }\frac{1}{k_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}(\alpha_{n}^{j})^{2}=\mathcal{R}(v)\,,

in particular
\alpha_{n}^{j}:=\sqrt{k_{n}\mathcal{R}(v)\tilde{\alpha}_{n}^{j}}
.
Define now the following sequence. Set
k_{0}:=0
, and define

v_{n}:=\begin{cases}0\,,&\text{ if }n\leqslant k_{1}\,,\\[2.0pt]
\frac{1}{k_{\ell}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{\ell}}(\alpha_{\ell}^{j})^{2}u_{\ell}^{j}\,,&\text{ if }k_{\ell}<n\leqslant k_{\ell+1}\,.\end{cases}

Then clearly still
v_{n}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}v
as
n\to\infty
and for
n\geqslant k_{1}
it holds that for any extremal point
\bar{u}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
that

\displaystyle v_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{\ell}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{\ell}}(\alpha_{\ell}^{j})^{2}u_{\ell}^{j}=\frac{1}{k_{\ell}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{\ell}}(\alpha_{\ell}^{j})^{2}u_{\ell}^{j}+\sum^{n-k_{\ell}}_{j=k_{\ell}+1}0\cdot\bar{u}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{\ell}}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{n}{k_{\ell}}}\alpha_{\ell}^{j}\Big)^{2}u_{\ell}^{j}+\sum^{n-k_{\ell}}_{j=k_{\ell}+1}0\cdot\bar{u}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\beta^{j}_{n})^{2}\tilde{u}^{j}_{n}\,,

for a suitable choice of
\beta^{j}_{n}\geqslant 0
and
(\tilde{u}^{j}_{n})^{n}_{j=1}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
with
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\beta^{j}_{n})^{2}=\mathcal{R}(v)
, which proves (
3.11
).

It remains to show that the constructed sequence in (
3.11
) is a recovery sequence. By assumption
(A5)
, the weak
∗
-to-strong-continuity of
v\mapsto\mathcal{F}(Kv)
holds, implying that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(Kv)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{F}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\beta^{j}_{n})^{2}Ku_{n}^{j}\right).

By Jensen’s inequality and
(A3)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\beta^{j}_{n})^{2}u_{n}^{j}\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\beta^{j}_{n})^{2}\mathcal{R}(u_{n}^{j})\leqslant\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\beta^{j}_{n})^{2}=\mathcal{R}(v)\,.

Therefore, it follows by the weak
∗
-lower semicontinuity of
\mathcal{R}
that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(v)\leqslant\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{R}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\beta^{j}_{n})^{2}u_{n}^{j}\right)\leqslant\mathcal{R}(v)\,.

As a consequence, equality must hold and thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(v)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{R}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\beta^{j}_{n})^{2}u_{n}^{j}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{so also }\ J(v)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}J\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\beta^{j}_{n})^{2}u_{n}^{j}\right)\,,

as desired.
∎

By the coercivity of
J
and general properties of
\Gamma
-convergence, every sequence of minimizers of
J_{n}
of the form

v_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(c_{n}^{j}\right)^{2}u_{n}^{j}

admits a weak*-convergent subsequence, and every weak*-limit minimizes
J
.

Proposition 3.7

(Convergence of Minimizers)
.

Assume
(A1)
-
(A5)
. Then, there exists
L>0
such that for every sequence of minimizers

\displaystyle(\mathbf{c}_{n},\mathbf{u}_{n})\in\argmin_{(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\in\tilde{\Omega}_{\sqrt{nL}}^{n}}J_{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\,,

the linear combination
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c_{n}^{j})^{2}u_{n}^{j}
admits a convergent subsequence. Moreover,
for every such convergent subsequence it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}(c_{n_{k}}^{j})^{2}u_{n_{k}}^{j}=v_{0}\in\argmin_{u\in\mathcal{M}}J(u)\,.

Proof.

Let us consider a minimizer
v_{*}
of
J
in
\mathcal{M}
and choose
L>\mathcal{R}(v_{*})
.
Choosing
(\mathbf{c}_{n},\mathbf{u}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
as in the statement, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j}_{n})^{2}u^{j}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{n},\mathbf{u}_{n})-\mathcal{F}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j}_{n})^{2}Ku^{j}_{n}\right)\leqslant\min_{(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\in\Omega^{n}_{\sqrt{nL}}}J_{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})-\inf_{v\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{F}(v)\,.

The term in the right hand side above remains bounded, since it holds that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\min_{(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\in\tilde{\Omega}^{n+1}_{\sqrt{(n+1)L}}}J_{n+1}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\leqslant\min_{(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\in\tilde{\Omega}^{n}_{\sqrt{nL}}}J_{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\,,

as one can show that all elements in
\tilde{\Omega}_{\sqrt{nL}}^{n}
are valid competitors for the problem in
\tilde{\Omega}_{\sqrt{(n+1)L}}^{n+1}
, by adding an extremal point with zero weight, and rescaling.
Therefore, there exists a
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c_{n}^{j})^{2}u_{n}^{j}\in\{v:\mathcal{R}(v)\leqslant C\}\,,

which is weak*-compact by
(A4)
.
Hence, there exists a subsequence
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
v_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}(c_{n_{k}}^{j})^{2}u_{n_{k}}^{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}v_{0}\,.

It remains to prove that
v_{0}\in\argmin_{v\in\mathcal{M}}J(v)
.
Since
v_{*}\in{\rm Dom}(\mathcal{R})
, by the
\Gamma
-convergence result of Proposition
3.6
, there exists a recovery sequence
(\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{n},\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{n})\in\tilde{\Omega}^{n}_{\sqrt{nL}}

such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\tilde{c}_{n}^{j})^{2}\tilde{u}_{n}^{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}v_{*}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\tilde{c}_{n}^{j})^{2}\leqslant\mathcal{R}(v_{\ast})\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptJ_{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\tilde{c}_{n}^{j})^{2}\tilde{u}_{n}^{j}\right)\longrightarrow J(v_{*})\,\ \text{ as }n\to\infty\,.

In particular,
(\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{n},\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{n})
is an admissible competitor for
J_{n}
, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, so that

J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{n},\mathbf{u}_{n})\leqslant J_{n}(\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{n},\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{n})\,,

which, together with the
\Gamma
-liminf inequality implies that

\displaystyle J(v_{*})\geqslant\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}J_{n_{k}}\left(\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}(\tilde{c}_{n_{k}}^{j})^{2}\tilde{u}_{n_{k}}^{j}\right)\geqslant\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}J_{n_{k}}\left(\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}(c_{n_{k}}^{j})^{2}u_{n_{k}}^{j}\right)\geqslant J(v_{0})\,,

i.e.
,
v_{0}\in\argmin_{v\in\mathcal{M}}J(v)
, which completes the assertion.
∎

3.2
Definition of AGFs through minimizing movements

Atomic gradient flows (AGFs)
are defined as gradient flows in the metric space
\Omega_{L}^{n}
of the functional
J_{n}
. In this subsection, we show the existence of such gradient flows using a minimizing movements approach, also called JKO scheme, cf.
[
34
]
. The minimizing movements setup is also briefly recalled in
Section

A.1
. Here, considering again
\Omega_{n}^{L}
makes sense to enforce geodesical completeness and compactness, which we will both require in the proofs. We first define the approximation scheme.

Definition 3.8

(
Approximation scheme
)
.

Let
\tau>0
and
(\mathbf{c}^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0})\in\Omega_{L}^{n}
be a given initial datum. Set
(\mathbf{c}^{0}_{\tau},\mathbf{u}^{0}_{\tau}):=(\mathbf{c}^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0})
and for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
choose iteratively

(\mathbf{c}^{k+1}_{\tau},\mathbf{u}^{k+1}_{\tau})\in\argmin_{\Omega_{L}^{n}}G_{n,\tau}^{k}\,,

(3.12)

where the
\tau
-discretized energy

G^{k}_{n,\tau}\colon\Omega_{L}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

G_{n,\tau}^{k}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u}):=J_{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})+\frac{1}{2\tau}d^{2}_{n}\big((\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u}),(\mathbf{c}^{k}_{\tau},\mathbf{u}^{k}_{\tau})\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\in\Omega_{L}^{n}\,.

(3.13)

Note that the existence of minimizers of
G^{k}_{n,\tau}
follows directly from the compactess of
\Omega_{L}^{n}
and the lower semicontinuity of
J_{n}
stated in Lemma
3.5
.

The existence of minimizing movements in our setting can now be obtained by a direct application of
[
4
, Proposition 2.2.3]
. The necessary lower-semicontinuity, coercivity and compactness properties follow by
3.5
, the fact that
J_{n}
is bounded from below and the compactness of
\Omega^{L}_{n}
.

Theorem 3.9

(
Minimizing movement scheme as limit path for
\tau\to 0
)
.

Let
\tau>0
and an initial datum
(\mathbf{c}^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0})\in\textup{Dom}(J_{n})
. Define
(\mathbf{c}_{\tau},\mathbf{u}_{\tau}):[0,+\infty)\to\Omega_{L}^{n}
, by

(\mathbf{c}_{\tau},\mathbf{u}_{\tau})(0):=(\mathbf{c}^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0})\,,\ \text{ and }\ (\mathbf{c}_{\tau},\mathbf{u}_{\tau})(t):=(\mathbf{c}^{k}_{\tau},\mathbf{u}^{k}_{\tau})\ \ \text{ for }t\in(k\tau,(k+1)\tau]\ \text{and }k\in\mathbb{N}\,,

(3.14)

where
(\mathbf{c}_{\tau}^{k},\mathbf{u}_{\tau}^{k})
as in (
3.12
). There exists a sequence
(\tau_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,1)
with
\tau_{\ell}\to 0
as
\ell\to\infty
, and a curve
(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\in AC_{\textup{loc}}^{2}([0,+\infty);\Omega_{L}^{n})
such that for all
t\in[0,+\infty)

d_{n}((\mathbf{c}_{\tau_{\ell},t},\mathbf{u}_{\tau_{\ell},t}),(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t}))\to 0\ \ \textup{as}\ \ \ell\to+\infty\,.

(3.15)

In particular, we have
(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})(0^{+})=(\mathbf{c}^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0})
and we call
(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})
a minimizing movement, see also
A.3
.

Remark 3.10
.

Note that in the comprehensive treatment of the theory of minimizing movements, detailed in
[
4
, Chapter 2]
, what we simply called minimizing movements are called
Generalized Minimizing Movements
, denoted by
{\rm GMM}(G,u_{0})
therein. However, we have decided to simplify the treatment of minimizing movements here for brevity.

3.3
\lambda
-Convexity and curves of maximal slope

Next, we study the
\lambda
-convexity of
J_{n}
and, whenever this property holds, we derive finer properties of the minimizing movements associated with the AGF.

Remark 3.11

(
Scalar and geodesic
\lambda
-convexity
)
.

We first note the following connection between scalar and geodesic
\lambda
-convexity. Let
(X,d)
be a geodesic metric space,
f\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to X
a geodesic. Suppose that
f\circ\gamma\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is
\alpha(\gamma)
-convex
for some
\alpha(\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}
,
i.e.
, the map

t\mapsto f(\gamma_{t})-\frac{\alpha(\gamma)}{2}t^{2}

is convex. Equivalently, for every
t\in[0,1]
it holds that

\displaystyle f(\gamma_{t})\leqslant(1-t)f(\gamma_{0})+tf(\gamma_{1})-\frac{\alpha(\gamma)}{2}t(1-t)\,.

Comparing the last inequality with the definition of geodesic
\lambda
-convexity of
f
, cf. (
2.13
), one immediately sees that for scalar
\alpha(\gamma)
-convexity along every geodesic
\gamma
to imply
\lambda
-convexity of
f
,
\alpha(\gamma)
needs to be of the form

\alpha(\gamma)=\lambda d^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
.

To prove the
\lambda
-convexity of
J_{n}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we will need an important assumption that asks for a compatibility between the forward operator
K
and the geodesic structure of
\mathcal{B}
. A consequence of the
\lambda
-convexity will be then that the local slope is a strong upper gradient for
J_{n}
.

(A8)

There exists a constant
C:=C(K,\mathcal{B})>0
such that for
m\in\{1,2\}
,

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left\|\frac{d^{m}}{dt^{m}}K(u_{t})\right\|_{Y}\leqslant\ Cd^{m}_{\mathcal{B}}(u_{0},u_{1})\,

(3.16)

for all geodesics
u_{t}:[0,1]\to\mathcal{B}
.

Remark 3.12
.

Note that (
3.16
) with
m=1
implies the existence of
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\|Ku-Kv\|_{Y}\leqslant Cd_{\mathcal{B}}(u,v)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\hskip 10.00002ptu,v\in\mathcal{B}\,.

(3.17)

Such Lipschitz-like property of
K
on
\mathcal{B}
is inherently of metric nature and can replace
(A8)
in the subsequent Theorem
5.2
, if one were to consider curves of maximal slope with regard to so-called weak upper gradients (c.f.
[
4
, Definition 1.2.2]
). However, since (
3.17
) alone is not enough to ensure
e.g.
,
\lambda
-convexity of
J_{n}
, we stick to
(A8)
to simplify the presentation.

In the following, by
C>0
we denote a generic constant that depends only on the data (
e.g.
the forward operator
K
, the fidelity term
\mathcal{F}
and the regularizer
\mathcal{R}
) and whose value is allowed to vary from line to line. The dependence of a constant on a particular parameter will be denoted by a subscript. Moreover, we remark that all derivatives of the forward operator
K
are intended in a weak sense,
i.e.
, testing against elements in the Hilbert space
Y
. We also recall the definition of
\Omega_{L}^{n}
and
J_{n}
,
i.e.
, (
3.1
) and (
3.5
).

Proposition 3.13
.

Assume
(A1)
–
(A8)

Then
J_{n}
is
\lambda(L)
-convex on
\Omega_{L}^{n}
for some constant
\lambda(L)\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})\colon[0,1]\rightarrow\Omega_{L}^{n}
be a geodesic, cf. (
3.4
). By Lemma
3.2
, for every
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the components

c_{t}^{j}=(1-t)c_{0}^{j}+tc_{1}^{j}\,,

and
u_{t}^{j}
are also geodesics in their target, and by the continuity of
K
, (
3.16
) also holds for
m=0
. Recalling
(A6)
–
(A7)
, we denote

\mathcal{R}_{n}(t):=\mathcal{R}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j}_{t})^{2}u_{t}^{j}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c_{t}^{j})^{2}\,.

Then, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and (
3.2
), we get

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\mathcal{R}_{n}(t)=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|c_{1}^{j}-c_{0}^{j}|^{2}\leqslant 2d_{n}^{2}((c_{0},u_{0}),(c_{1},u_{1}))\,.

(3.18)

Next, we compute the derivatives of
K_{n}:[0,1]\rightarrow Y
, defined as

K_{n}(t):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}K((c_{t}^{j})^{2}u_{t}^{j})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c_{t}^{j})^{2}K(u_{t}^{j})\,,

(3.19)

namely

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}K_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{t}^{j}\Big(2(c_{1}^{j}-c_{0}^{j})K(u_{t}^{j})+c_{t}^{j}\frac{d}{dt}K(u_{t}^{j})\Big)\,,

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}K_{n}(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big[2(c_{1}^{j}-c_{0}^{j})^{2}K(u_{t}^{j})+4c_{t}^{j}(c_{1}^{j}-c_{0}^{j})\frac{d}{dt}K(u_{t}^{j})+(c_{t}^{j})^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}K(u_{t}^{j})\Big]\,.

Using Jensen’s inequality, that
0\leqslant c_{t}^{j}\leqslant L
for every
t\in[0,1]
and
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
(A8)
and the fact that
K
is even strong-to-strong continuous (cf.
(A1)
and
[
16
, Remark 3.2]
), we estimate

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\Big\|\frac{d}{dt}K_{n}(t)\Big\|_{Y}&\leqslant C_{n,L}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(|c_{1}^{j}-c_{0}^{j}|+d_{\mathcal{B}}(u_{0}^{j},u_{1}^{j})\big)\\
&\leqslant C_{{n,L}}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(|c_{1}^{j}-c_{0}^{j}|^{2}+d^{2}_{\mathcal{B}}(u_{0}^{j},u_{1}^{j})\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}=C_{{n,L}}\,d_{n}((c_{0},u_{0}),(c_{1},u_{1}))\,,\end{split}

(3.20)

and analogously,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left\|\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}K_{n}(t)\right\|_{Y}&\leqslant\ C_{n,L}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big((c_{1}^{j}-c_{0}^{j})^{2}+(c_{1}^{j}-c_{0}^{j})d_{\mathcal{B}}(u_{0}^{j},u_{1}^{j})+d^{2}_{\mathcal{B}}(u_{0}^{j},u_{1}^{j})\Big)\\
&\leqslant C_{n,L}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(|c_{1}^{j}-c_{0}^{j}|^{2}+d_{\mathcal{B}}^{2}(u_{0}^{j},u_{1}^{j})\right)=C_{n,L}d_{n}^{2}((c_{0},u_{0}),(c_{1},u_{1}))\,.\end{split}

(3.21)

Finally, by the chain rule, we compute

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\big(\nabla\mathcal{F}(K_{n}(t)),\frac{d}{dt}K_{n}(t)\big)_{Y}+\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\mathcal{R}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}(K_{n}(t))\Big[\frac{d}{dt}K_{n}(t),\frac{d}{dt}K_{n}(t)\Big]_{Y}+\Big(\nabla\mathcal{F}(K_{n}(t)),\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}K_{n}(t)\Big)_{Y}+\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\mathcal{R}_{n}(t)\,,

which together with (
3.18
), (
3.20
) and (
3.21
) yields

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})\right|\leqslant

\displaystyle\,2C_{n,L}\|\mathcal{F}\|_{C^{2}}\,d^{2}_{n}((c_{0},u_{0}),(c_{1},u_{1}))+2d_{n}^{2}((c_{0},u_{0}),(c_{1},u_{1}))

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\,C_{n,L,\mathcal{F}}\,d_{n}^{2}((c_{0},u_{0}),(c_{1},u_{1}))\,.

Hence, there exists a
\lambda:=\lambda(n,L,K,\mathcal{B},\mathcal{F})<0
such that

J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})\colon[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}\ \text{ is }\ \lambda\cdot d_{n}^{2}((c_{0},u_{0}),(c_{1},u_{1}))\text{-convex}\,,

implying that
J_{n}:\Omega_{L}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is
\lambda
-convex along all unit-speed geodesics
(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})
.
∎

Example 3.14

(
\lambda
-convexity for convolutions of measures)
.

In this example, we show that
(A8)
is fulfilled for standard convolution operators in the space of measures. Similar reasoning applies also in more general settings.
Let
\mathbb{T}
be the unit circle, which we implicitly identify with
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
,
and let us consider the linear operator
K:M(\mathbb{T})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
, where

K\mu(s):=\int_{\mathbb{T}}k(s-x)\,d\mu(x)\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\mu\in M(\mathbb{T})\,,

(3.22)

with a suitable convolution kernel
k\in C^{2}(\mathbb{T})
. Here,
M(\mathbb{T})
is the space of Radon measures on
\mathbb{T}
. Choosing as regularizer the total variation of measures
\mathcal{R}(\cdot):=\|\cdot\|_{TV}
, it holds that

\tilde{\mathcal{B}}=\{\pm\delta_{x}:x\in\mathbb{T}\}\,.

Then take as the weakly*-closed, geodesically complete set
\mathcal{B}:=\{\delta_{x}:x\in\mathbb{T}\}
, and as
d_{\mathcal{B}}
the standard
2
-Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}_{2}
, which metrizes the weak*-convergence on
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
.
Note that the unit-speed
\mathcal{W}_{2}
–geodesic joining
\delta_{x_{0}}
to
\delta_{x_{1}}
is
t\mapsto\delta_{x_{t}}
where
x_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1}
, since we implicitly identified
\mathbb{T}
with
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
.
Along this geodesic we have that for every
s\in\mathbb{T}
,

K\delta_{x_{t}}(s)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}k(s-x)\,d\delta_{x_{t}}(x)=k(s-x_{t})\,,

which is twice differentiable in
t
. In particular, by the chain rule, we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}K\delta_{x_{t}}(s)=k^{\prime}(s-x_{t})(x_{0}-x_{1})\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}K\delta_{x_{t}}(s)=k^{\prime\prime}(s-x_{t})(x_{0}-x_{1})^{2}\,.

Thus, the map
t\mapsto K\delta_{x_{t}}
satisfies (
3.16
).

Example 3.15

(Necessity of local convexity)
.

In this example we show that Proposition
3.13
cannot, in general, be improved to a global

\lambda
–convexity statement on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{B}
.
Let
\mathcal{M}:=M([0,1])
and
\mathcal{R}(\cdot):=\|\cdot\|_{TV}
, and set again
\mathcal{B}:=\{\delta_{x}:\ x\in[0,1]\}
. We define the linear map
K:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle K\mu:=\int_{0}^{1}y\,d\mu(y)\,,

and consider
\mathcal{F}(y):=y^{2}
and
n=1
.
If
(c,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{B}
, with
\mu=\delta_{x}
for some
x\in[0,1]
, then

\displaystyle J_{1}(c,\mu)=\mathcal{F}\!\big(K(c^{2}\delta_{x})\big)+c^{2}=\mathcal{F}(c^{2}x)+c^{2}=(c^{2}x)^{2}+c^{2}=c^{4}x^{2}+c^{2}.

(3.23)

With the
\ell_{2}
-product metric, geodesics in
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{B}
have the form

(c_{t},u_{t})=\left((1-t)c_{0}+tc_{1},\delta_{(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1}}\right)\,.

Along such geodesics we may view
J_{1}
as the function

f(c,x):=c^{4}x^{2}+c^{2}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times[0,1]
. Its Hessian at
(c,x)
becomes

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(c,x)=\begin{pmatrix}12c^{2}x^{2}+2\,\ &8c^{3}x\\[2.0pt]
8c^{3}x\,\ &2c^{4}\,,\end{pmatrix}

(3.24)

so that

\displaystyle\det\nabla^{2}f(c,x)=\bigl(12c^{2}x^{2}+2\bigr)\cdot 2c^{4}-(8c^{3}x)^{2}=4c^{4}\bigl(1-10c^{2}x^{2}\bigr)\,.

(3.25)

Hence, for every fixed
x\in(0,1]
and all sufficiently large
c
, one has
\det\nabla^{2}f(c,x)<0
,
so
\nabla^{2}f(c,x)
has a negative eigenvalue. In particular, if
\lambda_{\min}(c,x)
denotes the minimal eigenvalue of
\nabla^{2}f(c,x)
, then

\lambda_{\min}(c,x)\to-\infty
as
c\to\infty
. Therefore,
J_{1}
is
only locally
semiconvex
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{B}
,
i.e.
, semiconvex on
\Omega_{L}
for all
L>0
.

Proposition
3.13
yields as a first consequence that the local slope is a strong upper gradient according to Definition
2.9
, even with non-compact weights, so on

\Omega^{n}_{\infty}:=[0,\infty)^{n}\times\mathcal{B}^{n}\,.

(3.26)

Recall from
2.8
that the slope is a local concept, hence there is no need to write,
e.g.
,
\left|\partial J_{n}|_{\Omega_{L}^{n}}\right|
instead of just always
|\partial J_{n}|
.

Lemma 3.16
.

Assume
(A1)
-
(A5)
and that the forward operator
K:\mathcal{M}\to Y
fulfills
(A8)
. Then, the local slope
|\partial J_{n}|
is a strong upper gradient for
J_{n}
on
\Omega^{n}_{\infty}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.13
,
J_{n}
is
\lambda(L)
-geodesically convex, hence by
[
4
, Corollary 2.4.10]
the metric slope

|\partial J_{n}|
is a strong upper gradient for
J_{n}
on
\Omega_{L}^{n}
.
Now, let
\mathbf{v}\in AC([0,1];\Omega_{\infty}^{n})
and take
L>0
large enough so that
\mathbf{v}_{t}\in\Omega_{L}^{n}
for every
t\in[0,1]
.
Then, for all
0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant 1
, locality of the metric slope gives

\displaystyle|J_{n}(\mathbf{v}_{t})-J_{n}(\mathbf{v}_{s})|\leqslant\int_{s}^{t}|\partial J_{n}|(\mathbf{v}_{r})\,|\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{r}|\,\mathrm{d}r,

since
|\partial J_{n}|
is a strong upper gradient for
J_{n}
on
\Omega_{L}^{n}
. Thus
|\partial J_{n}|
is also a strong upper gradient for
J_{n}
on
\Omega_{\infty}^{n}
.
∎

This gives the existence of curves of maximal slope, again without the need for compactness, for which we recall
3.9
and (
3.26
) .

Theorem 3.17
.

Assume
(A1)
-
(A8)
. Then every minimizing movement
(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})
in
\Omega_{\infty}^{n}
is a curve of maximal slope for
J_{n}
with regard to
|\partial J_{n}|
.
In addition, minimizing movements in
\Omega^{n}_{L}
are also curves of maximal slope.

Proof.

Let
(\mathbf{c}^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0})\in\Omega_{\infty}^{n}
, and let
(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})
be a minimizing movement for
J_{n}
in
\Omega_{\infty}^{n}
. First, assume that there exists
L>0
such that
c_{t}^{j}\in[0,L]
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
t\geqslant 0
.

By
3.9
, there exist discrete solutions
(\mathbf{c}_{\tau_{\ell},t},\mathbf{u}_{\tau_{\ell},t})\in\Omega^{n}_{\infty}
such that

d_{n}((\mathbf{c}_{\tau_{\ell},t},\mathbf{u}_{\tau_{\ell},t}),(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t}))\to 0\ \ \textup{as}\ \ \ell\to+\infty\,.

(3.27)

Since
c_{t}^{j}\leqslant L
, for
\ell
big enough,
(\mathbf{c}_{\tau_{\ell},t},\mathbf{u}_{\tau_{\ell},t})\in\Omega_{\tilde{L}}^{n}
for
\tilde{L}>L
.
This implies that the limit is a minimizing movement in
\Omega_{\tilde{L}}^{n}.

Now using
[
4
, Corollary 2.4.11]
together with Proposition
3.13
gives that
(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})
is also a curve of maximal slope for
J_{n}|_{\Omega_{\tilde{L}}^{n}}
with regard to
|\partial J_{n}|_{\Omega_{\tilde{L}}^{n}}|
. Since all of the involved terms are local,
(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})
is a curve of maximal slope for
J_{n}
with regard to
|\partial J_{n}|
.

So the only thing left to prove is that for each minimizing movement
(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})
in
\Omega_{\infty}^{n}
, there exists some
L>0
such that
c_{t}^{j}\in[0,L]
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
t\geqslant 0
. By
(A6)
-
(A7)
, and as
\mathcal{F}
is bounded from below by
(A2)
, it holds that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c_{t}^{j})^{2}=\mathcal{R}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c_{t}^{j})^{2}u_{t}^{j}\Big)=J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})-\mathcal{F}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c_{t}^{j})^{2}Ku_{t}^{j}\right)\leqslant J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})+C(F)\,.

(3.28)

As for every
\tau>0
, (
3.12
)
and (
3.13
) yield

\displaystyle J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{\tau}^{1},\mathbf{u}_{\tau}^{1})\leqslant J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{\tau}^{1},\mathbf{u}_{\tau}^{1})+\frac{1}{2\tau}d_{n}^{2}((\mathbf{c}_{\tau}^{1},\mathbf{u}_{\tau}^{1})),(\mathbf{c}^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0}))\leqslant J_{n}(\mathbf{c}^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0})\,,

and thus iteratively for all
k\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{\tau}^{k},\mathbf{u}_{\tau}^{k})\leqslant J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{\tau}^{k-1},\mathbf{u}_{\tau}^{k-1})\leqslant J_{n}(\mathbf{c}^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0})\,.

(3.29)

In view of (
3.14
), (
3.27
) (taking the limit
\ell\rightarrow\infty
) and the lower semicontinuity of
J_{n}
with respect to
d_{n}
imply that
J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})\leqslant J_{n}(\mathbf{c}^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0})
, which together with (
3.28
) and the fact that
n
is fixed imply that the
c_{t}^{j}
stay indeed uniformly bounded for all
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
t\geqslant 0
.
∎

3.4
NPC of the extremal points and uniqueness of the flow

We now turn to the issue of uniqueness of the AGF with respect to the functional
J_{n}
introduced in (
3.5
). Here, we show that if the metric space
\mathcal{B}
is non-positively curved (NPC), cf. Definition
2.5
, we can ensure such a uniqueness property. To this end, in
Section

A.1
we recall a local uniqueness result, see
A.4
, which we extend below to a global one. We remind the reader that uniqueness of gradient flows on NPC metric spaces was extensively studied in
[
37
]
, and among other works, this analysis was further refined in
[
4
, Chapter 4]
.

First, it is well-known that
\mathcal{B}
being NPC implies that
\Omega_{L}^{n}
is as well. This is essentially a consequence of
3.2
, and of choosing the
\ell_{2}
-metric on the product space
\Omega_{L}^{n}
.

Lemma 3.18

(NPC of product space)
.

If
(\mathcal{B},d_{\mathcal{B}})
is a space of NPC according to
2.5
, then so is
(\Omega_{L}^{n},d_{n})
.

We are then ready to show uniqueness, provided that
(\mathcal{B},d_{\mathcal{B}})
is NPC. Uniqueness also comes with contraction estimates between minimizing movements with different initial points.

Theorem 3.19
.

Assume
(A1)
-
(A8)
and that
(\mathcal{B},d_{\mathcal{B}})
is NPC. Then, for every initial point
(\mathbf{c}^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0})\in\Omega_{\infty}^{n}
, there exists a unique minimizing movement for
J_{n}
. Moreover, for every other initial point
(\tilde{\mathbf{c}}^{0},\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{0})\in\Omega_{\infty}^{n}
, and corresponding minimizing movement
(\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{t},\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t})
the following contraction estimate holds:

d_{n}\big((\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t}),(\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{t},\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t})\big)\leqslant e^{-\lambda t}d_{n}\big((\mathbf{c}^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0}),(\tilde{\mathbf{c}}^{0},\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{0})\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }\mathcal{L}^{1}\text{-a.e. }t>0\,,

(3.30)

for a
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
depending only on
|\mathbf{c}^{0}|
and
|\tilde{\mathbf{c}}^{0}|
.

Proof.

Again by
(A6)
-
(A7)
, and as
\mathcal{F}
is bounded from below by a constant
-C(\mathcal{F})
, it holds that

J_{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})=\mathcal{F}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}K(u^{j})\right)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}-C(\mathcal{F})\,,

i.e.
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}\leqslant J_{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})+C(\mathcal{F})\,.

Assume that
(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})
is a minimizing movement with initial point
(\mathbf{c}^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0})
. Then by the previous estimate and (
3.29
), we have that
(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})\in\Omega_{L}^{n}
for some
L=L(n,(\mathbf{c}^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0}))
large enough. Since
J_{n}
is
\lambda
-convex on
\Omega_{n}^{L}
by Proposition
3.13
, minimizing movements in
\Omega_{L}^{n}
exist, and fulfill the contractivity property (
3.30
) by
A.4
, cf. (
A.7
).
∎

4
The lifted problem

To further our analysis, we follow a lifting approach to the Wasserstein space, detailed in
[
13
]
. This leads to a comparison of Atomic Gradient Flows in
\Omega_{L}^{n}
versus metric gradient flows in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L}^{n})
in
Section

5
, which can be thought in spirit similar to the investigation of Chizát and Bach in
[
21
]
. A key difference here is that lifted particles are Dirac deltas supported on elements of
\Omega_{L}^{n}
. In particular, for
TV
-regularized problems, where extremal points are Dirac deltas, lifted particles would be elements of the form
\delta_{\delta_{x}}
. However, it is clear that this representation of lifted particles is isometric to
\delta_{x}
, so that the lifted formulation remains fully consistent with the perspective of Chizát and Bach (more details will be given in Section
6
).

Note that to keep the lifted problem equivalent to the original problem (
2.1
) on
\mathcal{M}
, (only) in this section one has to work again with the full set of extremal points
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
of (
2.8
).

Definition 4.1

(
Lifting in the space of positive measures
)
.

We consider the lifting of (
2.1
) to the space of positive measures on
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
as follows:

\inf_{\mu\in M^{+}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})}j(\mu)\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\ \ j(\mu):=\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{K}\mu)+\|\mu\|_{TV}\,,

(4.1)

where
M^{+}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})
is the cone of positive (Radon) measures on
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
. The forward operator
\mathcal{K}:M^{+}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})\to Y
is set as

\mathcal{K}\mu:=K\mathcal{I}(\mu)\,,

(4.2)

where the representative
\mathcal{I}(\mu)\in\mathcal{M}
is defined via the following version of
Choquet’s theorem
.

Proposition 4.2

(
Version of Choquet’s theorem
,
[
13
, Proposition 5.2]
&
[
40
, page 14]
)
.

Every measure
\mu\in M^{+}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})
defines the linear action of some
\mathcal{I}(\mu)\in\textup{Dom}(\mathcal{R})
via duality, namely,

\langle\mathcal{I}(\mu),p\rangle=\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\langle v,p\rangle\,d\mu(v)\,\ \ \forall p\in\mathcal{C}\,.

(4.3)

Furthermore, the map
\mathcal{I}:M^{+}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})\to\textup{Dom}(\mathcal{R})\subset\mathcal{M}
is a linear surjection and
\mathcal{I}(\mu)
is called the (weak-) barycenter of
\mu
.

For
\alpha>0
, we also write
M^{+}_{\alpha}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}}):=\{\mu\in M^{+}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}}):\|\mu\|_{TV}\leqslant\alpha\}
. We can now state the equivalence between the optimization of the lifted problem and the original one.

Proposition 4.3
.

By
[
13
, page 4 and Theorem 5.4]
, we have the equivalence

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathcal{M}}J(u)=\min_{\mu\in M^{+}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})}j(\mu)\,.

(4.4)

In addition, there exists an
\alpha=\alpha(J)>0
such that

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathcal{M}}J(u)=\min_{\mu\in M^{+}_{\alpha}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})}j(\mu)\,.

(4.5)

Proof.

The statement in (
4.4
) is already proved in
[
13
]
, while for (
4.5
) it suffices to consider a minimizer
u_{*}\in\underset{u\in\mathcal{M}}{\argmin}J(u)
, and
\alpha>\mathcal{R}(u_{\ast})
.
∎

4.1
Lifting the problem in Wasserstein space

Recalling (
3.1
) and the subsequent notation, let
\tilde{\Omega}_{L}:=[0,L]\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}}

for a fixed
L>\sqrt{\alpha}
, with
\alpha>0
as in
4.3
. Denote by
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})
the space of probability measures in
\tilde{\Omega}_{L}
with finite second moment endowed with the Wasserstein-2 metric.
In particular, since the metric
d_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}
is bounded, we can write

\mathcal{P}_{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L}):=\Big\{\nu\in M^{+}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})\colon\nu(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})=1\Big\}\,.

(4.6)

For every
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})
, their Wasserstein 2-distance is defined as

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}):=\sqrt{\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}}\int_{\tilde{\Omega}_{L}\times\tilde{\Omega}_{L}}d^{2}_{\Omega}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\,d\gamma(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})}\,,

(4.7)

where

\Gamma_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}:=\{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L}\times\tilde{\Omega}_{L})\colon(\pi_{1})_{\#}(\gamma)=\nu_{1},(\pi_{2})_{\#}(\gamma)=\nu_{2}\}\,.

(4.8)

In (
4.8
), for
i=1,2
, we have denoted by
\pi_{i}:\tilde{\Omega}_{L}\times\tilde{\Omega}_{L}\to\tilde{\Omega}_{L}
the
i
-th coordinate projection. As a reminder, cf. (
3.3
), the distance
d_{\Omega}:\tilde{\Omega}_{L}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is defined as follows. For
\omega_{i}:=(c_{i},u_{i})\in\tilde{\Omega}_{L}
, we set

d_{\Omega}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}):=\left(|c_{1}-c_{2}|^{2}+d^{2}_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}(u_{1},u_{2})\right)^{1/2}\,.

(4.9)

The infimum in (
4.7
) is actually a minimum, cf.
[
4
, Section 7.1]
, and the set of all possible corresponding minimizers will be denoted by
\Gamma_{0}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
.
We define the homogeneous projection operator
\pi:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})\to M^{+}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})
via

\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\psi(u)\,d[\pi\nu](u):=\int_{\tilde{\Omega}_{L}}c^{2}\psi(u)\,d\nu(c,u)\,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})\,,\psi\in C(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})\,.

(4.10)

Note that the homogeneous projection is a typical tool for addressing the unbalanced formulation of Optimal Transport and is also one way to define the Hellinger-Kantorovich distance
[
36
]
.
Thanks to this homogeneous projection we can then transform the problem in the right hand side of (
4.1
) into a minimization problem in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})
in the following way.

Definition 4.4

(
Lifting in the space of probability measures
)
.

We consider the problem

\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})}\mathcal{J}(\nu)\,,\ \text{where}\ \mathcal{J}(\nu):=j(\pi\nu)\,.

(4.11)

Then, the functional
\mathcal{J}
can be written more explicitly as

\mathcal{J}(\nu)=\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{K}[\pi\nu])+\int_{{\tilde{\Omega}_{L}}}c^{2}\,d\nu(c,u)\,.

Using (
4.2
), (
4.3
), and (
4.10
), we can also check that

\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{K}[\pi\nu])=\mathcal{F}(K\mathcal{I}[\pi\nu])=\mathcal{F}\left(\int_{\tilde{\Omega}_{L}}c^{2}Ku\,d\nu(c,u)\right)\,,

so that in total

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\nu)=\mathcal{F}\left(\int_{\tilde{\Omega}_{L}}c^{2}Ku\,d\nu(c,u)\right)+\int_{\tilde{\Omega}_{L}}c^{2}\,d\nu(c,u)\,.

(4.12)

Remark 4.5
.

The definitions above can be made analogously with
\mathcal{B}
instead of
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
, but as mentioned before, the equivalence of the problems that we show in the following only holds with
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
in general.

Next, we show that problems (
4.1
) and (
4.11
) are equivalent. We first need to prove that the projection operator
\pi
is surjective.

Lemma 4.6
.

For every
\alpha>0
and
L>\sqrt{\alpha}
, we have that the homogeneous projection operator
\pi:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})\to M_{\alpha}^{+}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})
is surjective.

Proof.

Consider a measure
\mu\in M_{\alpha}^{+}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})
. Note that if
\mu=0
, then
\pi\nu_{0}=\mu
for every
\nu_{0}
of the form
\nu_{0}=\delta_{0}\otimes\delta_{u}
, and for any
u\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
. Therefore, in what follows we will suppose that
\mu(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})>0
.
Define now

\nu_{\mu}:=\delta_{\sqrt{\mu(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})}}\otimes\frac{\mu}{\mu(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})}\in M^{+}_{\alpha}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})\,,

(4.13)

since
L>\sqrt{\alpha}
. Note that for every test function
\varphi\in C(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})
, we have

\displaystyle\nu_{\mu}(\varphi):=\int_{\tilde{\Omega}_{L}}\varphi(c,u)\,d\delta_{\sqrt{\mu(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})}}(c)\,d\Big(\frac{\mu}{\mu(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})}\Big)(u)=\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\varphi(\sqrt{\mu(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})},u)\,d\big(\frac{\mu}{\mu(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})}\big)(u)\,.

Testing the above definition with the constant function
\varphi\equiv 1
, it is clear that the measure
\nu_{\mu}
as defined in (
4.13
) is a probability measure.
Finally, let us verify that
\pi\nu_{\mu}=\mu
. For every
\psi\in C(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})
it holds that

\displaystyle\pi\nu_{\mu}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\psi(u)\,d[\pi\nu_{\mu}](u)=\int_{\tilde{\Omega}_{L}}c^{2}\psi(u)\,d\nu_{\mu}(c,u)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,L]\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}c^{2}\psi(u)\,d\delta_{\sqrt{\mu(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})}}(c)\,d\big(\frac{\mu}{\mu(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})}\big)(u)=\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\psi(u)\,d\mu(u)=\mu(\psi)\,,

showing that
\pi\nu_{\mu}=\mu
.
∎

Proposition 4.7
.

For every
\alpha>0
and
L>\sqrt{\alpha}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\min_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})}\mathcal{J}(\nu)=\min_{\mu\in M_{\alpha}^{+}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})}j(\mu)\,.

(4.14)

Moreover, if
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})
minimizes
\mathcal{J}
, then
\pi\nu\in M^{+}_{\alpha}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})
minimizes
j
. Conversely, if
\mu\in M^{+}_{\alpha}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})
minimizes
j
, then every
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})
such that
\pi\nu=\mu
minimizes
\mathcal{J}
.

Proof.

The existence of minimizers in both problems follows by the structural assumptions
(A1)
-
(A6)
and a standard application of the direct method in the Calculus of Variations. The equivalence of the two minimization problems follows immediately from the surjectivity of
\pi
and the definition of
\mathcal{J}
in (
4.11
). Indeed, let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})
be a minimizer of
\mathcal{J}
.
Then, for every
\mu\in M^{+}_{\alpha}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})
, using Lemma
4.6
, there exists
\nu_{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})
such that
\pi\nu_{\mu}=\mu
, so that by (
4.11
), we have

j(\mu)=j(\pi\nu_{\mu})=\mathcal{J}(\nu_{\mu})\geqslant\mathcal{J}(\nu)=j(\pi\nu)\,,

i.e.
,
\pi\nu\in M^{+}_{\alpha}(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})
is a minimizer for
j
. The converse follows analogously, showing (
4.14
). ∎

4.2
Minimizing movements for the lifted problem

Following the previous discussion and the approach in Subsection
3.1
, we fix again a closed (so compact), geodesically complete subset
\mathcal{B}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
, and recalling (
3.1
)-(
3.3
), we consider

\Omega_{L}=[0,L]\times\mathcal{B}\,,

and the lifted functional
\mathcal{J}
on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})
, which itself a compact and geodesically connected subset of
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{L})
. We recall that, having restricted to
\Omega_{L}
, the functional
\mathcal{J}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\nu):=\mathcal{F}\left(\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}Ku\,d\nu(c,u)\right)+\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}\,d\nu(c,u)\,.

(4.15)

Again, we use the JKO scheme to show existence of mimizing movements. We note that similarly as in Subsection
3.1
, we can only hope to recover an approximate minimum of
J
with
\mathcal{J}
in
\mathrm{conv}\,\mathcal{B}
, but here one needs to restrict the support of our measures to
\mathcal{B}
to obtain existence of,
e.g.
, curves of maximal slope.

Definition 4.8

(
Approximation scheme
)
.

Let
\tau>0
and
\nu^{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})
be a given initialization. Set
\nu_{\tau}^{0}:=\nu^{0}
, and choose (iteratively) for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\nu^{k+1}_{\tau}\in\underset{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})}{\rm argmin}\left\{\mathcal{J}(\nu)+\frac{1}{2\tau}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu,\nu^{k}_{\tau})\right\}.

(4.16)

Define then the piecewise constant curve
\nu_{\tau}:[0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})
by
\nu^{0}_{\tau}=\nu^{0}
and for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\nu_{\tau,t}:=\nu^{k}_{\tau}\ \ \text{ for }t\in((k-1)\tau,k\tau]\,.

(4.17)

The next theorem shows the existence of minimizing movements for the lifted problem.

Theorem 4.9

(
Minimizing movements as limit path for
\tau\rightarrow 0
)
.

With
\nu_{\tau,t}
as in (
4.17
), there exists
(\tau_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,1)
with
\tau_{\ell}\searrow 0
, and a curve
\nu_{t}\in AC^{2}_{loc}([0,+\infty);\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L}))
such that for every
t\in[0,+\infty)
it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu_{\tau_{\ell},t},\nu_{t})\rightarrow 0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{as }\ell\to\infty\,.

In particular, we have that
\nu_{t}
is a minimizing movement in the sense of
A.3
.

Proof.

We first check that
\mathcal{J}:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is lower semicontinuous with respect to the narrow convergence. Indeed, if
(\nu^{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})
is narrowly converging to
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})
, and we assume without restriction that

\displaystyle\liminf_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{J}(\nu^{m})=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{J}(\nu^{m})=:M<+\infty\,,

(4.18)

then

\liminf_{m\to\infty}\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}d\nu^{m}(c,u)\geqslant\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}d\nu(c,u)\,,

thanks to
[
4
, Formula 5.1.15]
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is continuous and convex cf.
(A2)
, it is also weakly lower semicontinuous. Therefore, it is enough to prove that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}Ku\,d\nu^{m}(c,u)=\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}Ku\,d\nu(c,u)

with respect to the weak convergence in
Y
. To this end, given
y\in Y
it holds that

\Big(\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}Ku\,d\nu^{m}(c,u),y\Big)_{Y}=\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}(Ku,y)_{Y}\,d\nu^{m}(c,u)\,.

(4.19)

Note that
c^{2}|(Ku,y)_{Y}|
is uniformly integrable with respect to
\nu^{m}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}|(Ku,y)_{Y}|\,d\nu^{m}\leqslant\big(\|K\|\|y\|_{Y}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{B}}\|u\|_{\mathcal{M}}\big)\mathcal{J}(\nu^{m})\,,

and the right hand side of the last inequality is uniformly bounded in
m
thanks to (
4.18
). Therefore, applying
[
4
, Lemma 5.1.7]
to (
4.19
) we conclude the lower semicontinuity of
\mathcal{J}
with respect to the narrow convergence. Finally, since
\mathcal{W}_{2}
metrizes the narrow convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})
, we can apply

[
4
, Proposition 2.2.3]
to conclude the proof.
∎

4.3
\lambda
-Convexity for the lifted problem

Analogously to Subsection
3.3
, we turn to the study of convexity properties of the functional
\mathcal{J}
along geodesics in the Wasserstein space
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})
. In particular, we address the question whether for a geodesic
\gamma_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})
, the function

t\mapsto\mathcal{J}(\gamma_{t}):=\mathcal{F}\left(\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}Ku\,d\gamma_{t}(c,u)\right)+\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}\,d\gamma_{t}(c,u)

is
\lambda
-convex,
i.e.
, fulfills (
2.13
). In the following, we denote by
C([0,1];\Omega_{L})
the separable and complete metric space of continuous curves in
\Omega_{L}
, endowed with the metric of uniform convergence induced by
d_{\Omega}
, cf. (
3.3
). Define then the
evaluation map

e_{t}:C([0,1];\Omega_{L})\rightarrow\Omega_{L}
as

e_{t}(v):=v(t)\ \text{ for every }t\in[0,1]\,.

Note also that by
[
2
, Theorem 10.6]
, geodesics in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})
exist, as
\Omega_{L}
is a geodesic space. To prove the
\lambda
-convexity, we first recall the
metric dynamical formulation of optimal transport
, which is a major ingredient in the proof.
For every
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})
, consider the minimization problem

\min\Big\{\int_{C([0,1];\Omega_{L})}\int_{0}^{1}|\gamma^{\prime}_{t}|^{2}\,dt\,d\eta(\gamma)\colon\eta\in\mathcal{P}(C([0,1];\Omega_{L}))\,,(e_{0},e_{1})_{\#}\eta=(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\Big\}\,.

(4.20)

We call any such admissible
\eta
a
dynamic transport plan
, the collection of which is denoted by
\mathrm{DTP}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
, and any optimal one will be called an
optimal dynamic geodesic plan
. We denote by
\mathrm{OptGeo}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
the collection of all optimal geodesic plans from
\mu_{0}
to
\mu_{1}
, which is be supported on
\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})
(see
[
2
, Theorem 9.13]
).
Using
[
2
, Theorem 9.13]
and Lemma
3.2
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\min_{\eta\in\mathrm{DTP}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}\int_{0}^{1}\left|(c_{t},u_{t})^{\prime}\right|^{2}\,dt\,d\eta(c,u)\,.

(4.21)

In addition,
[
2
, Theorem 10.6]
guarantees that for any geodesic
\gamma_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})
, there exists an
\eta\in\mathcal{P}(C([0,1];\Omega_{L})
with
\eta\in\mathrm{OptGeo(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})}
and
\mathrm{spt}\,\eta\subset\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})
such that

\gamma_{t}=(e_{t})_{\#}\eta\,.

(4.22)

Thus, fixing any geodesic
\gamma_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})
, we can write
\mathcal{J}(\gamma_{t})
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{J}(\gamma_{t})&=\mathcal{F}\left(\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}Ku\,d[(e_{t})_{\#}\eta](c,u)\right)+\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}\,d[(e_{t})_{\#}\eta](c,u)\\
&=\mathcal{F}\left(\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}c^{2}_{t}Ku_{t}\,d\eta(c,u)\right)+\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}c_{t}^{2}\,d\eta(c,u)\,.\end{split}

(4.23)

Proposition 4.10
.

Assume
(A1)
-
(A8)
. Then for every
L>0
, there exists a
\lambda(L)\leqslant 0
such that
\mathcal{J}
is
\lambda
-convex along geodesics on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})
.

Proof.

Note that for every geodesic
(c_{t},u_{t})\in\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})
by
3.2
we have that
c_{t}\in\mathrm{Geo}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
u_{t}\in\mathrm{Geo}(\mathcal{B})
. Thus
c_{t}=(1-t)c_{0}+tc_{1}
.
Therefore,

\frac{d}{dt}c_{t}^{2}=\ 2t(c_{1}-c_{0})^{2}+2(c_{1}-c_{0})c_{0}\leqslant 2\big(|c_{1}-c_{0}|^{2}+|c_{1}-c_{0}|c_{0}\Big),\ \text{ and }\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}c_{t}^{2}=\ 2(c_{1}-c_{0})^{2}\,.

(4.24)

Let now
\gamma_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})
be a geodesic.
Thanks to
[
2
, Theorem 10.6]
there exists
\eta\in\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L}))
with
\gamma_{t}=(e_{t})_{\#}\eta
and
\eta\in\mathrm{OptGeo}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
.
Therefore, by the Dominated Convergence Theorem,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}d\gamma_{t}=

\displaystyle\ \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\left(\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}d\gamma_{t+h}-\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}d\gamma_{t}\right)/h=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\left(\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}d(e_{t+h})_{\#}\eta-\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}d(e_{t})_{\#}\eta\right)/h

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}\Big(\frac{c^{2}_{t+h}-c^{2}_{t}}{h}\Big)\,d\eta=\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}\frac{d}{dt}(c^{2}_{t})\,d\eta\,,

and similarly
\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}d\gamma_{t}=\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}(c^{2}_{t})\,d\eta
.
Recalling (
2.16
) for geodesics, (
4.21
) and (
4.24
), we can further estimate

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}d\gamma_{t}=&\ \int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}2(c_{1}-c_{0})^{2}\,d\eta\leqslant\ 2\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}\big(|c_{1}-c_{0}|^{2}+d_{\mathcal{B}}^{2}(u_{1},u_{0})\big)\,d\eta\\
=&\ 2\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}\int_{0}^{1}|(c_{t},u_{t})^{\prime}|^{2}\,dt\,d\eta=2\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\,.\end{split}

(4.25)

Moreover, by a similar application of the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we have

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}Ku\,d\gamma_{t}=\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}\Big(\frac{d}{dt}(c_{t}^{2})Ku_{t}+c_{t}^{2}\frac{d}{dt}Ku_{t}\,,\Big)\,d\eta\,

(4.26)

and

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}Ku\,d\gamma_{t}=\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}\Big(\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}(c_{t}^{2})Ku_{t}+2\frac{d}{dt}(c_{t}^{2})\frac{d}{dt}Ku_{t}+c_{t}^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}Ku_{t}\Big)d\eta\,.

(4.27)

Arguing as in the proof of
3.13
, using (
4.26
), Jensen’s inequality for the probability measure
\eta
on
\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})
, (
4.24
), (
3.16
), the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and for a constant
C:=C(K,\mathcal{B},L)>0
that is allowed to vary from line to line, we estimate

\displaystyle\Big\|\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}Ku\,d\gamma_{t}\Big\|_{Y}^{2}\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}\big|\frac{d}{dt}c_{t}^{2}\big|^{2}\left\|Ku_{t}\right\|_{Y}^{2}+2\big|\frac{d}{dt}c_{t}^{2}\big||c_{t}^{2}|\|Ku_{t}\|_{Y}\big\|\frac{d}{dt}Ku_{t}\big\|_{Y}+|c_{t}^{2}|^{2}\left\|\frac{d}{dt}Ku_{t}\right\|_{Y}^{2}d\eta

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C\Big(\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}|\frac{d}{dt}c_{t}^{2}|^{2}\left\|Ku_{t}\right\|_{Y}^{2}\,d\eta+\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}|c_{t}^{2}|^{2}\left\|\frac{d}{dt}Ku_{t}\right\|_{Y}^{2}\,d\eta\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\ C\Big(\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}\Big(\big(|c_{1}-c_{0}|^{2}+|c_{1}-c_{0}|c_{0}\big)^{2}+(|c_{0}|+|c_{1}-c_{0}|)^{4}d^{2}_{\mathcal{B}}(u_{0},u_{1})\Big)\,d\eta\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\ C\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}\big(|c_{1}-c_{0}|^{2}+d_{\mathcal{B}}^{2}(u_{0},u_{1})\big)\,d\eta=C\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\,,

(4.28)

where in the last equality we used again (
4.21
). Analogously, using this time (
4.27
) and again (
3.16
), we estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}c_{t}^{2}\left\|\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}Ku_{t}\right\|_{Y}d\eta\leqslant

\displaystyle\ C\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}(|c_{1}-c_{0}|+c_{0})^{2}d_{\mathcal{B}}^{2}(u_{0},u_{1})\,d\eta

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\ C\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}\big(|c_{1}-c_{0}|^{2}+d_{\mathcal{B}}(u_{0},u_{1})^{2}\big)d\eta=C\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\,,

which directly leads to

\Big\|\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}Ku\,d\gamma_{t}\Big\|_{Y}\leqslant C\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\,.

(4.29)

Finally, considering the
\mathbb{R}
-valued map
[0,1]\ni t\mapsto\mathcal{J}(\gamma_{t})
, and setting for brevity

\displaystyle\underline{K}(t):=\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}c_{t}^{2}Ku_{t}\,d\eta\,,

by (
4.23
), (
4.25
), (
4.10
), and (
4.29
),
we get

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\mathcal{J}(\gamma_{t})=

\displaystyle\ \frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\mathcal{F}\left(\underline{K}(t)\right)+\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\int_{\mathrm{Geo}(\Omega_{L})}c_{t}^{2}\,d\eta\,

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\underline{K}(t)},\frac{d}{dt}\underline{K}(t)\right)_{Y}+2\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}_{\underline{K}(t)}\left[\frac{d}{dt}\underline{K}(t),\frac{d}{dt}\underline{K}(t)\right]+\left(\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\underline{K}(t)},\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\underline{K}(t)\right)_{Y}+2\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\ ||\mathcal{F}||_{C^{2}}\left(\left\|\frac{d}{dt}\underline{K}(t)\right\|_{Y}^{2}+\left\|\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\underline{K}(t)\right\|_{Y}\right)+2\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\ C\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\,,

where the constant
C>0
in the last line depends also on
\mathcal{F}
. Thus, the map
t\mapsto\mathcal{J}(\gamma_{t})
is
\tilde{\lambda}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
-convex, for some
\tilde{\lambda}:=\tilde{\lambda}(\mathcal{F},K,\mathcal{B},L)\leqslant 0
. This exactly implies that
\mathcal{J}
is
\tilde{\lambda}
-convex along geodesics in
\Omega_{L}
.
∎

.

Assume
(A1)
-
(A8)
. Then

is a strong upper gradient for
\mathcal{J}
on

. In addition, every minimizing movement

\mathcal{J}
with regard to

.

Proof.

First,
[

implies that for all

and lower-semicontinuous.
Next, let

and
\mu_{t}
a minimizing movement for

Remark 4.12
.

Note that here one cannot obtain uniqueness of the curves of maximal slope via standard methods, contrary to the setting for the non-lifted functional
J_{n}
. This is because in general,
\mathcal{P}_{2}(X)
is not NPC, as geodesics can be non-unique. For

being a Hilbert space, the uniqueness in

still holds for lower semicontinuous functionals which are

[

), but follows from a variational characterization of the so-called Wasserstein subdifferential. If

is just a metric space such as in our setting, this characterization and even the Wasserstein subdifferential are not available.

5

In this section we aim to relate the minimizing movements defined in Subsections

In particular, we will show that gradient flows for

-
(A8)
,

, the standard assumptions, the no loss of mass condition on the regularizer (

For the next theorem, we again recall (

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that
(A1)
-
(A8)
hold, and let
(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})\in AC([0,1],\Omega^{n}_{L})
be a curve of maximal slope for
J_{n}
with respect to the strong upper gradient
|\partial J_{n}|
. Then,

\mu_{t}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{(c_{t}^{j},u_{t}^{j})}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})

(5.1)

is a curve of maximal slope for
\mathcal{J}

We first show that

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose that
(A1)
-
(A8)
hold and let

Proof of
5.1
.

Note that

and

are strong upper gradients for

and
\mathcal{J}
respectively by Theorem

and Lemma

Recalling Definitions

and

, we need to show that

2\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{J}(\mu_{t})\leqslant-|\mu_{t}^{\prime}|^{2}-|\partial\mathcal{J}|^{2}(\mu_{t})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }\mathcal{L}^{1}-\mathrm{a.e.}\ t\in[0,1]\,,

In the following, we prove

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose that
(A1)
-
(A8)
hold. Let
n=1
and
\mu_{t}=\delta_{(c_{t},u_{t})}
. For
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.

, it holds that

\lim_{\nu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\mu_{t}}\frac{\left|\int_{\Omega_{L}}\mathcal{F}(c^{2}Ku)\,d\nu-\mathcal{F}\left(\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}Ku\,d\nu\right)\right|}{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\mu_{t})}=0\,.

(5.5)

Then we can show the slope equality for

Proposition 5.4
.

In the setting of
5.1
and
5.3
, one has
|\partial\mathcal{J}|(\mu_{t})=|\partial J_{1}|(c_{t},u_{t})
.

Proof.

Note that by the definitions (
2.17
),
(
3.5
), and (
4.15
),

\displaystyle|\partial\mathcal{J}|(\mu_{t})=\limsup_{\tilde{\mu}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\mu_{t}}\frac{(\mathcal{J}(\mu_{t})-\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\mu}))^{+}}{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{t},\tilde{\mu})}

\displaystyle\geqslant\limsup_{(\tilde{c},\tilde{u})\to(c_{t},u_{t})}\frac{(\mathcal{J}(\delta_{(c_{t},u_{t})})-\mathcal{J}(\delta_{(\tilde{c},\tilde{u})}))^{+}}{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\delta_{(c_{t},u_{t})},\delta_{(\tilde{c},\tilde{u})})}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{(\tilde{c},\tilde{u})\to(c_{t},u_{t})}\frac{(J_{1}(c_{t},u_{t})-J_{1}(\tilde{c},\tilde{u}))^{+}}{d_{\Omega}((c_{t},u_{t}),(\tilde{c},\tilde{u}))}=|\partial J_{1}|(c_{t},u_{t})\,,

i.e.
, the inequality
|\partial\mathcal{J}|(\mu_{t})\geqslant|\partial J_{1}|(c_{t},u_{t})
is a direct consequence of the definitions.

For the reverse inequality, let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})
. By (
4.15
), and that
\mathcal{R}(u)=1
,
we can estimate

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\mu_{t})-\mathcal{J}(\nu)

\displaystyle=J_{1}(c_{t},u_{t})-\int_{\Omega_{L}}J_{1}(c,u)\,d\nu+\int_{\Omega_{L}}J_{1}(c,u)\,d\nu-\mathcal{F}\left(\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}Ku\,d\nu\right)-\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}\,d\nu

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\Omega_{L}}(J_{1}(c_{t},u_{t})-J_{1}(c,u))^{+}\,d\nu+\left|\int_{\Omega_{L}}\mathcal{F}(c^{2}Ku)\,d\nu-\mathcal{F}\left(\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}Ku\,d\nu\right)\right|\,.

(5.6)

Since

\displaystyle|\partial J_{1}|(c_{t},u_{t})=\limsup_{(c,u)\rightarrow(c_{t},u_{t})}\frac{\left(J_{1}(c_{t},u_{t})-J_{1}(c,u)\right)^{+}}{d_{\Omega}((c_{t},u_{t}),(c,u))}\,,

for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that if

, then

\displaystyle\frac{(J_{1}(c_{t},u_{t})-J_{1}(c,u))^{+}}{d_{\Omega}((c_{t},u_{t}),(c,u))}\leqslant|\partial J_{1}|(c_{t},u_{t})+\varepsilon\,.

Note also that, thanks to
[
4
, Theorem 5.3.1]
and Jensen’s inequality, for
\pi\in\Gamma_{0}(\nu,\mu_{t})
(cf. the notation after (
4.9
)),

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\mu_{t})=\left(\int_{\Omega_{L}}\int_{\Omega_{L}}d_{\Omega}^{2}((c,u),(\tilde{c},\tilde{u}))\,d\pi((c,u),(\tilde{c},\tilde{u}))\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\Omega_{L}}d_{\Omega}^{2}((c_{t},u_{t}),(c,u))\,d\nu(c,u)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{\Omega_{L}}d_{\Omega}((c_{t},u_{t}),(c,u))\,d\nu(c,u)\,.

Therefore, denoting for brevity

B_{\delta,t}:=B_{\delta}((c_{t},u_{t}))\,,

(5.7)

the latter intending the
\delta

\Omega_{L}
centered in
(c_{t},u_{t})
, we can easily estimate,

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{B_{\delta,t}}d_{\Omega}((c_{t},u_{t}),(c,u))\,d\nu}{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\mu_{t})}\leqslant\frac{\int_{\Omega_{L}}d_{\Omega}((c_{t},u_{t}),(c,u))\,d\nu}{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\mu_{t})}\leqslant 1\,.

(5.8)

By using (
5.8
) we can therefore decompose

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{L}}\frac{(J_{1}(c_{t},u_{t})-J_{1}(c,u))^{+}}{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\mu_{t})}\,d\nu

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{\delta,t}}\frac{(J_{1}(c_{t},u_{t})-J_{1}(c,u))^{+}}{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\mu_{t})}\,d\nu+\int_{\Omega_{L}\setminus B_{\delta,t}}\frac{(J_{1}(c_{t},u_{t})-J_{1}(c,u))^{+}}{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\mu_{t})}\,d\nu

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(|\partial J_{1}|(c_{t},u_{t})+\varepsilon)\int_{B_{\delta,t}}d_{\Omega}((c_{t},u_{t}),(c,u))\,d\nu}{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\mu_{t})}

\displaystyle+\hskip 20.00003pt\int_{\Omega_{L}\setminus B_{\delta,t}}\frac{(J_{1}(c_{t},u_{t})-J_{1}(c,u))^{+}}{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\mu_{t})}\,d\nu

(5.9)

In addition, for every

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\nu,\mu_{t})\geqslant\int_{\Omega_{L}\setminus B_{\delta,t}}d^{2}_{\Omega}((c_{t},u_{t}),(c,u))\,d\nu\geqslant\delta^{2}\nu(\Omega_{L}\setminus B_{\delta,t})\,\implies\nu(\Omega_{L}\setminus B_{\delta,t})\leqslant\frac{\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\nu,\mu_{t})}{\delta^{2}}\,,

and since

Lemma 5.5

Suppose that

\frac{k_{j}}{n}=\pi(A_{j}\times\{y^{j}\})\,,

allowing one to directly generalize the proofs.

Proof.

Lemma 5.7

(

.

Suppose that

\displaystyle\ \int_{B_{\delta}(\mathbf{y}_{t})}d_{n}^{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{t})\,d\overline{\nu}^{n}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\int_{\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\bigtimes}}B_{\delta}(y^{i}_{t})}d_{n}^{2}((x^{1},y_{t}^{1}),\ldots,(x^{n},y_{t}^{n}))\,d\nu_{1}(x^{1})\ldots d\nu_{n}(x^{n})

5.16
) and
\nu_{R}:=\nu-\tilde{\nu}
.
Recalling also the notation in (
5.23
),
5.5
in particular implies that,

\limsup_{\nu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\mu_{t}}\frac{(J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})-\mathcal{J}(\nu))^{+}}{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\mu_{t})}=\limsup_{\nu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\mu_{t}}\frac{(J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})-\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\nu}))^{+}}{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\mu_{t})},

(5.33)

so that it is enough to consider
\tilde{\nu}
instead of
\nu
. By the triangle inequality, we can simply estimate

\displaystyle\big|J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})-\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\nu})\big|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\Omega_{L}^{n}}J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})\,d\overline{\nu}^{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})-\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\nu})\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\int_{\Omega_{L}^{n}}\big(J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})-J_{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\big)\,d\overline{\nu}^{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\right|+\left|\int_{\Omega_{L}^{n}}J_{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\,d\overline{\nu}^{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})-\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\nu})\right|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{B_{\delta}(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})}\big(J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})-J_{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\big)\,d\overline{\nu}^{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\Big|+\left|\int_{\Omega_{L}^{n}}J_{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})\,d\overline{\nu}^{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})-\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\nu})\right|\,.

By
5.8
this time, it suffices to consider the first term in the right hand side of the above line for the slopes. Then, combining (
5.32
), (
5.33
), (
5.25
), and
5.7
, gives

\displaystyle|\partial\mathcal{J}|(\mu_{t})

\displaystyle=\limsup_{\nu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\mu_{t}}\frac{(\mathcal{J}(\mu_{t})-\mathcal{J}(\nu))^{+}}{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\mu_{t})}\leqslant\limsup_{\nu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\mu_{t}}\frac{\int_{B_{\delta}(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})}(J_{n}(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})-J_{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u}))^{+}\,d\overline{\nu}^{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})}{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\mu_{t})}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\,\limsup_{\nu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\mu_{t}}\left(|\partial J_{n}|(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})+\varepsilon\right)\int_{B_{\delta}(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})}\frac{d_{n}((\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t}),(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u}))}{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\mu_{t})}\,d\overline{\nu}^{n}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(|\partial J_{n}|(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})+\varepsilon\right)\limsup_{\nu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\mu_{t}}\alpha_{\nu}=|\partial J_{n}|(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})+\varepsilon\,,

and as this holds for all
\varepsilon>0
, also the inequality
|\partial\mathcal{J}|(\mu_{t})\leqslant|\partial J_{n}|(\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t})
follows.
∎

5.3
Semi-Discrete Optimal Transport on compact metric spaces

Here, we recall some standard facts about semi-discrete optimal transport that can be found in
[
38
]
and in the classical treatise
[
5
]
, or more recently in
[
30
]
. Afterwards, we use them to generalize the necessary statements to our setting of a general compact metric space.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space,
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(X)
and consider an atomic measure

\mu:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\delta_{y^{j}}\,,\text{ with }\alpha_{j}>0\,,\ \sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}=1,\text{ and }y^{i}\neq y^{j}\ \text{ for }i\neq j\,.

(5.34)

Set also
Y:=\{y^{1},\dots,y^{n}\}
and for every bounded Lipschitz function
\psi\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
, let us set
\psi^{j}:=\psi(y^{j})
. Then, the Kantorovich duality gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu,\mu)=\sup_{\phi(x)+\psi(y)\leqslant d^{2}(x,y)}\,\int_{X}\phi(x)\,d\nu(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\psi^{j}.

(5.35)

Thus, one can consider the above maximization problem to be posed between
X
and the discrete space
Y
. Then by,
e.g.

[
47
, Theorem 5.10]
, the supremum above is attained at a couple
(\psi^{c},\psi)
, where

\displaystyle\psi^{c}(x):=\min_{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}}(d^{2}(x,y^{j})-\psi^{j})\,.

(5.36)

Hence,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu,\mu)=\sup_{(\psi^{1},\ldots,\psi^{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{X}\psi^{c}(x)\,d\nu(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\psi^{j}\,.\end{split}

(5.37)

This duality motivates defining the
Laguerre cells

A_{j}
and the

tie sets

\Sigma_{j}
as follows.

Definition 5.9

(
Laguerre cells
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space,
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega_{L})
,
\mu
as in (
5.34
) and
\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a dual maximizer of (
5.37
). For every
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, one defines the
Laguerre cell

A_{j}
and
tie sets
or also
boundaries of the Laguerre cells

\Sigma_{j}
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{(i)}&\quad A_{j}:=\{x\in X:d^{2}(x,y^{j})-\psi^{j}\leqslant d^{2}(x,y^{i})-\psi^{i}\text{ for }i\neq j\}\,,\\[2.0pt]
\mathrm{(ii)}&\quad\Sigma_{j}:=\bigcup_{i\neq j}(A_{j}\cap A_{i})\,.\end{split}

(5.38)

Define also the
interior of the Laguerre cells
as
A_{j}\setminus\Sigma_{j}
, and the
tie set

\Sigma
as

\Sigma:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\Sigma_{j}\,.

(5.39)

A simple statement about the interior of the Laguerre cells follows directly from the definition.

Lemma 5.10
.

For every
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, it holds that

\displaystyle A_{j}\setminus\Sigma_{j}=\{x\in X:d^{2}(x,y^{j})-\psi^{j}<d^{2}(x,y^{i})-\psi^{i}\text{ for }i\neq j\}\,.

Thus, for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
x\in A_{i}
, it holds that

\psi^{c}(x)=\min_{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}}(d^{2}(x,y^{j})-\psi^{j})=d^{2}(x,y^{i})-\psi^{i}\,,

(5.40)

and in the interior, if
x\in A_{i}\setminus\Sigma_{i}
, by Lemma
5.10
,

\psi^{c}(x)<d^{2}(x,y^{j})-\psi^{j}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }j\neq i\,.

(5.41)

This can be combined with known facts on Kantorovitch duality to derive the desired properties.

Lemma 5.11

(
Properties of semi-discrete optimal couplings
)
.

In the above setting, consider an optimal coupling
\pi\in\Gamma_{0}(\nu,\mu)
. Then in fact
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(X\times Y)
, and moreover, for a choice of dual optimizer
\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and Laguerre cells
(A_{j})_{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathrm{(i)}&\quad\mathrm{spt}\,\pi\cap(A_{j}\times Y)\subset A_{j}\times\{y^{j}\},\\
\mathrm{(ii)}&\quad\mathrm{spt}\,\pi\cap(A_{j}^{c}\times Y)\subset A_{j}^{c}\times(Y\setminus\{y^{j}\})\,,\end{aligned}

(5.42)

and

\alpha_{j}=\pi(A_{j}\times\{y^{j}\})\,.

Proof.

First of all, note that cylindrical sets are generators of the Borel
\sigma-
algebra in
X\times X
, and for any such
A\times B\subset X\times X
, with
B\subset Y^{c}
, by (
4.8
) and (
5.34
), one has

0\leqslant\pi(A\times B)\leqslant\pi(X\times B)=\mu(B)=0\,.

Therefore, it is immediate that
\mathrm{spt}\,\pi\subset X\times Y
, and
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(X\times Y)
. For the second part,
[
47
, Theorem 5.10]
implies that

\mathrm{spt}\pi\subset\{(x,y)\in X\times Y:\psi^{c}(x)+\psi(y)=d^{2}(x,y)\}\,.

(5.43)

Now, by definition of the Laguerre cells, for
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
x\in A_{j}^{c}
, there must exist an
i_{0}\neq j
such that

d^{2}(x,y^{j})-\psi^{j}>d^{2}(x,y^{i_{0}})-\psi^{i_{0}}\,.

But then, by (
5.41
), also

\psi^{c}(x)<d^{2}(x,y^{j})-\psi^{j}\implies\psi^{c}(x)+\psi(y^{j})<d^{2}(x,y^{j})\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(x,y^{j})\in A^{c}_{j}\times\{y^{j}\}\,.

Therefore, by (
5.43
),
\pi(A^{c}_{j}\times\{y^{j}\})=0
, which implies (
5.42
), and moreover, by (
5.34
) we have

\alpha_{j}=\mu(\{y^{j}\})=\pi(X\times\{y^{j}\})=\pi(A_{j}\times\{y^{j}\})+\pi(A_{j}^{c}\times\{y^{j}\})=\pi(A_{j}\times\{y^{j}\})\,,

which concludes the proof.
∎

In the next lemma we discuss properties of optimal couplings when restricted on the interior of the Laguerre cells.

Lemma 5.12
.

For
\pi\in\Gamma_{0}(\nu,\mu)
,
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, and
L_{j}:=(A_{j}\setminus\Sigma_{j})\times X
, it holds that

\pi|_{L_{j}}=\nu|_{(A_{j}\setminus\Sigma_{j})}\otimes\delta_{y^{j}},

so that

\int_{L_{j}}d^{2}(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y)=\int_{A_{j}\setminus\Sigma_{j}}d^{2}(x,y^{j})\,d\nu(x)\,.

Proof.

For every
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, one has by Lemma
5.11
that

\displaystyle L_{j}\cap\mathrm{spt}\,\pi\subset(A_{j}\setminus\Sigma_{j})\times\{y^{j}\}\,.

(5.44)

By disintegrating
\pi|_{L_{j}}
with respect to
\nu|_{(A_{j}\setminus\Sigma_{j})}
, we have that

\pi|_{L_{j}}=\nu|_{(A_{j}\setminus\Sigma_{j})}\otimes\mu_{x}\,,

where
x\in A_{j}\setminus\Sigma_{j}
. From (
5.44
) it follows that

{\rm spt}\,\mu_{x}\subset\{y^{j}\}\ \mathrm{for}\ \nu\text{-a.e.}\ x\in A_{j}\setminus\Sigma_{j}\,.

(5.45)

Note now that for every Borel subset
D\subset A_{j}\setminus\Sigma_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\nu(D)=\pi(D\times X)=\int_{D}\mu_{x}(X)\,d\nu(x)\,,

implying that
\mu_{x}
is a probability measure for
\nu
-a.e.
x
in
A_{j}\setminus\Sigma_{j}
.
Therefore, in view also of (
5.45
), it holds that
\mu_{x}=\delta_{y_{j}}
concluding the proof. ∎

Thanks to the previous lemma we can give a structural statement about the Wasserstein distance between
\nu
and
\mu
using the semi-discrete theory, for which we also recall the notation in (
5.39
).

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space,
\mu
as in (
5.34
),
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(X)
and
\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
a corresponding dual maximizer. For an optimal coupling
\pi\in\Gamma_{0}(\nu,\mu)
, one has

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu,\mu)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{A_{j}\setminus\Sigma_{j}}d^{2}(x,y^{j})\,d\nu(x)+\int_{\Sigma\times Y}d^{2}(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y)\,.

Proof.

The statement follows from
5.12
, since, recalling (
5.39
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu,\mu)

\displaystyle=\int_{X\times Y}d^{2}(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{(A_{j}\setminus\Sigma_{j})\times Y}d^{2}(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y)+\int_{\Sigma\times Y}d^{2}(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{A_{j}\setminus\Sigma_{j}}d^{2}(x,y^{j})\,d\nu(x)+\int_{\Sigma\times Y}d^{2}(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y)\,.

∎

6
Examples

In this section we consider concrete examples that fall into our general framework.

Finite dimensional settings.
As a preliminary observation, we note that on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the sublevel sets of
\mathcal{R}(u):=\|u\|_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}(u):=\|u\|_{\infty}

possess isolated extremal points. Consequently, the only geodesically connected admissible sets
\mathcal{B}
reduce to singletons, and thus AGFs are not meaningful in this setting. In contrast, for
\mathcal{R}(u):=\|u\|_{p}
with
1<p<\infty
,
the set of extremal points coincides with the boundary of the
p
-balls. In this case, AGFs are essentially equivalent to Riemannian gradient flows on
\partial\{u:\|u\|_{p}\leqslant 1\}
.

Strictly convex Banach spaces.
In case
\mathcal{M}
is a strictly convex Banach space and
\mathcal{R}(u):=\|u\|_{\mathcal{M}}
, it is immediate to check that the extremal points of the unit ball of
\mathcal{R}
coincide with
\partial\{u:\|u\|_{\mathcal{M}}\leqslant 1\}
. Therefore, if
\mathcal{M}
admits a separable predual, then AGFs are metric gradient flows in the weak*-closure of
\partial\{u:\|u\|_{\mathcal{M}}\leqslant 1\}
endowed with any metric metrizing the weak*-topology.

6.1
Total variation regularization in the space of measures

As mentioned in the introduction, AGFs applied to optimization problems in the space of measures regularized with the total variation penalization recover the PGF-setting analyzed in
[
21
,
24
]
. Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex, compact set and set

\mathcal{C}:=C(X)
. In this case
\mathcal{M}
is the space of finite signed Radon measures on
X
. Choosing as regularizer
\mathcal{R}:=\|\cdot\|_{TV}
, one can prove that
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}=\{\pm\delta_{x}:\ x\in X\}
.
As weakly*-closed, geodesically connected subset of
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
one can choose for example

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{+}:=\{\delta_{x}:\ x\in X\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{or}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{B}_{-}:=\{-\delta_{x}:\ x\in X\}\,.

Note that the Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}_{2}
metrizes the narrow convergence on
\mathcal{B}_{\pm}
. Moreover, the spaces
(\mathcal{B}_{\pm},\mathcal{W}_{2})
are flat and in any case isometric to
(\mathcal{B}_{+},\mathcal{W}_{2})
.
As a consequence, uniqueness for the minimizing movement scheme and contraction estimates follow from the application of
3.19
.

Now, we can concretely examine how the AGF framework defined in
Section

3
looks like in this particular scenario for the choice of
\mathcal{B}_{+}
, which can be repeated verbatim for
\mathcal{B}_{-}
.
The discretized functional
J_{n}
can be written as

\displaystyle J_{n}\big(((c^{j}),(\delta_{x^{j}}))^{n}_{j=1}\big)=\mathcal{F}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}\,K\delta_{x^{j}}\right)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}\,,

(6.1)

recovering the discrete problem in
[
21
, Equation (2)]
.
Moreover, since
\mathcal{B}_{+}
is isometric to
X
, the AGF of
J_{n}
, defined as metric gradient flow in
\Omega_{L}^{n}
, is equivalent to the finite dimensional gradient flow of the functional
((c^{j}),(\delta_{x^{j}}))^{n}_{j=1}\mapsto J_{n}\big(((c^{j}),(\delta_{x^{j}}))^{n}_{j=1}\big)
, which is precisely how PGFs are defined.
The lifted functional defined as in (
4.15
), for
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{L})
, is written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\nu)=\mathcal{F}\left(\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}K\delta_{x}\,d\nu(c,\delta_{x})\right)+\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}d\nu(c,\delta_{x})\,.

(6.2)

Note that
\mathcal{P}([0,L]\times\mathcal{B}_{+})
is isometric to
\mathcal{P}([0,L]\times X)
through the maps
T(\nu):=\mathcal{T}_{\#}\nu
, where

\mathcal{T}(c,\delta_{x}):=(c,x)\,.

Indeed,
T
is an isometry since, due to the fact that
\mathcal{T}
is a bijective isometry, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}(T(\nu_{1}),T(\nu_{2}))=\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mathcal{T}_{\#}\nu_{1},\mathcal{T}_{\#}\nu_{2})=\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})\,.

Therefore, the metric gradient flow of the lifted functional (
6.2
) is equivalent to a Wasserstein gradient flow of

\displaystyle\nu\mapsto\mathcal{F}\left(\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}K\delta_{x}\,d\nu(c,x)\right)+\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}\,d\nu(c,x)\,,

(6.3)

i.e. precisely to the lifted dynamics of PGFs.

One-dimensional BV functions

Following
[
18
]

[
44

\mathcal{M}:=L^{\infty}((0,1))

0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}

\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{u\in L^{\infty}((0,1)):u(x)=0\text{ for a.e. }x\in(0,\varepsilon)\cup(1-\varepsilon,1)\right\}\,.

\displaystyle|Du|((0,1)):=\sup\left\{\int_{0}^{1}u(x)\operatorname{div}\varphi(x)\,dx:\varphi\in C_{c}^{1}((0,1)),\ \|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1\right\}

the
BV

u\in L^{\infty}((0,1))
, and we choose the regularizer

\mathcal{R}(u):=\begin{cases}|Du|((0,1))&\text{ if }u\in BV((0,1))\cap\mathcal{D_{\varepsilon}}\,,\\

It is straightforward that
\mathcal{R}

(A4)

(A5)

2

[

\tilde{\mathcal{B}}=\left\{\sigma\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}_{[a,b]}:a,b\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon],\ a\leqslant b\text{ and }\sigma\in\{-1,1\}\right\},

(6.4)

where
\mathds{1}_{A}(t)
denotes the indicator function of the set
A

\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
, we can choose either

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{+}=\left\{\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}_{[a,b]}:a,b\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon],a\leqslant b\right\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{or}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{B}_{-}=\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}_{[a,b]}:a,b\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon],a\leqslant b\right\}.

\mathcal{B}_{\pm}

,
\frac{1}{2}\sigma\mathds{1}_{\left[a_{k},b_{k}\right]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\frac{1}{2}\sigma\mathds{1}_{[a,b]}
for
\sigma\in\{-1,1\}

a_{k}\rightarrow a
and
b_{k}\rightarrow b

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sigma\mathds{1}_{[a,b]},\frac{1}{2}\sigma\mathds{1}_{[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]}\right):=\sqrt{|a-a^{\prime}|^{2}+|b-b^{\prime}|^{2}}\,,

d_{\mathcal{B}}

\mathcal{B}_{\pm}
. With this choice of the metric, both
\mathcal{B}_{+}

\mathcal{B}_{-}

Q_{\varepsilon}:=\{(a,b)\in[(0,\varepsilon)\cup(1-\varepsilon,1)]^{2}:a\leqslant b\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\,,

and thus are flat and NPC. As a consequence, uniqueness for the AGF and the contraction estimates follow again from Theorem
3.19
. The discretized functional
J_{n}
can be written via

\displaystyle J_{n}\big(((c^{j}),(a_{j},b_{j}))_{j=1}^{n}\big)=\mathcal{F}\!\left(\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}\,K\mathds{1}_{[a_{j},b_{j}]}\right)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}\,,

(6.5)

and the lifted functional is defined as in (
4.15

\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{L})=\mathcal{P}([0,L]\times\mathcal{B}_{+})

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\nu):=\mathcal{F}\left(\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}K\mathds{1}_{[a,b]}\,d\nu(c,\mathds{1}_{[a,b]})\right)+\int_{\Omega_{L}}c^{2}\,d\nu(c,\mathds{1}_{[a,b]})\,.

(6.6)

Reasoning similarly to Subsection
6.1
, the space
\mathcal{P}([0,L]\times\mathcal{B}_{+})
is isometric
\mathcal{P}([0,L]\times Q_{\varepsilon})
. Therefore, the gradient flow of the lifted functional is equivalent to a Wasserstein gradient flow in
\mathcal{P}([0,L]\times Q_{\varepsilon})
of

\displaystyle\nu\mapsto\mathcal{F}\left(\int_{[0,L]\times Q_{\varepsilon}}c^{2}K\mathds{1}_{[a,b]}\,d\nu(c,(a,b))\right)+\int_{[0,L]\times Q_{\varepsilon}}c^{2}d\nu(c,(a,b))\,.

(6.7)

6.3
Benamou-Brenier dynamical formulation of optimal transport

Introduced in
[
9
]
, the
Benamou-Brenier formula
allows to compute an optimal transport between two probability measures
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
on a closed, bounded domain
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
through the minimization of the
kinetic energy

(\rho,v)\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{X}\left|v_{t}(x)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)\,dt\,,

(6.8)

where
\rho_{t}\in M^{+}(X)
is a time-dependent probability measure interpolating between
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
, and
v_{t}:[0,1]\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a vector field, and the pair
\left(\rho_{t},v_{t}\right)
satisfies the
continuity equation

\partial_{t}\rho+\operatorname{div}\left(\rho v_{t}\right)=0

in the sense of distributions.
One can reformulate the Benamou-Brenier energy as a convex functional on the space of Borel measures
\mathcal{M}:=M^{+}([0,1]\times X)\times M\left([0,1]\times X;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
via

\mathbf{B}(\rho,m):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{X}\left|\frac{dm}{d\rho}\right|^{2}d\rho(t,x)\,,\ \text{if }\rho\geqslant 0,m\ll\rho\\[3.0pt]
+\infty\,,\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\text{ otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

(6.9)

In this case, the continuity equation becomes the linear constraint
\partial_{t}\rho+\operatorname{div}m=0
. In
[
42
,
12
,
15
]
the Benamou-Brenier energy was used as a convex regularizer to solve dynamic inverse problems, and its sparsity properties were subsequently investigated
[
11
,
12
,
31
,
20
]
. This was done by
considering the functional

\mathcal{R}_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho,m):=\beta\mathbf{B}(\rho,m)+\alpha\|\rho\|_{TV}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ subjected to }\hskip 10.00002pt\partial_{t}\rho+\operatorname{div}m=0

(6.10)

defined for all
(\rho,m)\in\mathcal{M}
and
\alpha>0,\beta>0
.
Here, AGFs apply directly to composite optimization problems regularized with
\mathcal{R}:=\mathcal{R}_{\alpha,\beta}
since one can show that
\mathcal{R}_{\alpha,\beta}
satisfies
(A4)
-
(A5)
.
Moreover, as proved in
[
11
]
, it is possible to characterize the extremal points of

B_{\alpha,\beta}:=\left\{(\rho,m)\in\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{R}_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho,m)\leqslant 1,\text{subjected to }\partial_{t}\rho+\operatorname{div}m=0\right\}.

Theorem 6.1

(Theorem 6,
[
11
]
)
.

Let
\alpha>0,\beta>0
. It holds that

\displaystyle{\rm Ext}(B_{\alpha,\beta})=\{(\rho,m):\rho=C_{\gamma}\,dt\otimes\delta_{\gamma(t)},\hskip 10.00002ptm=\dot{\gamma}(t)C_{\gamma}\,dt\otimes\delta_{\gamma(t)}\}\cup\{(0,0)\}\,,

(6.11)

where
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow X
is a curve in
H^{1}([0,1];X)
and
C_{\gamma}:=\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}dt+\alpha\right)^{-1}
.

To define AGFs, one can consider the weak*-closed set
\mathcal{B}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}:=\{(\rho,m):\rho=b\,dt\otimes\delta_{\gamma(t)},\hskip 10.00002ptm=\dot{\gamma}(t)b\,dt\otimes\delta_{\gamma(t)},\text{for }b\in[0,C_{\gamma}]\}\,.

(6.12)

Moreover, the metric
d((\rho_{1},m_{1}),(\rho_{2},m_{2}))=\|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}\|_{H^{1}([0,1];X)}

metrizes the weak* convergence on
\mathcal{B}
on curves of bounded speed (
b>0
).
Therefore since
H^{1}([0,1];X)
is an Hilbert space and any Hilbert space is NPC, uniqueness and contractivity estimates for AGFs again follow from the NPC framework.

Here, the functional
J_{n}
can be written (on curves of bounded speed) as

\displaystyle J_{n}\big(((c^{j}),(\gamma_{j}))_{j=1}^{n}\big)=\mathcal{F}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}\,K(C_{\gamma_{j}}dt\otimes\delta_{\gamma_{j}(t)},C_{\gamma_{j}}\dot{\gamma}_{j}(t)dt\otimes\delta_{\gamma_{j}(t)})\right)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c^{j})^{2}\,,

and AGFs correspond to the
([0,L]\times H^{1}([0,1];X))^{n}
gradient flow of such functional. The lifting can be written similarly as in the previous sections as a gradient flow in
\mathcal{P}_{2}([0,L]\times H^{1}([0,1];X))
. Interestingly such algorithm has been implemented (without mathematical investigation) as an acceleration step in
[
12
]
.

6.4
Further examples

In this section we list with less details several further examples that can be handled by AGFs. We refer the reader to the references for more insights about extremality properties of the given problems.

Kantorovich-Rubinstein-norms and Wasserstein distances on balanced signed measures.
As shown in the recent papers
[
19
,
8
,
18
]
Kantorovich-Rubinstein norms can be used as regularizers for inverse problems and mean-field neural networks training. The
{\rm KR}
-norm can be defined as follows, cf.
[
8
]
:

\displaystyle\|\mu\|_{\rm KR}:=|\mu(X)|+\sup\left\{\int_{X}f(z)\,d\mu(z):f(e)=0,L(f)\leqslant 1\right\}\,,

(6.13)

where
\mu\in M_{1}(X)
is a signed measure with finite first moment,
e\in X
is a base point and
L(f)
is the Lipschitz constant of
f
.
In case of balanced measures,
i.e.
, when
\mu_{+}(X)=\mu_{-}(X)
, the KR norm is simply the
\mathcal{W}_{1}
-distance between the positive and the negative part of
\mu
.
Alternatively, other possibilities to include unbalanced measures are possible through infimal convolution approaches, cf.
[
19
]
.
As shown in
[
8
]
(see also
[
18
]
and
[
19
]
for corresponding and similar results), the extremal points of the ball of the KR-regularizer can be characterized. In case
X
is a compact set, a simple consequence of the previously mentioned results yields that extremal points of

\{\mu\text{ balanced}:\|\mu\|_{\rm KR}+\|\mu\|_{TV}\leqslant 1\}\,,

are dipoles of the form
\mu_{\rm dip}=\frac{\delta_{x}-\delta_{y}}{d(x,y)+1}
.
Therefore, AGFs applied to optimization problems on balanced signed measures regularized with the
{\rm KR}
-norms are equivalent to Euclidean gradient flows in
([0,L]\times X\times X)^{n}
. Note that such algorithm have also been implemented (without mathematical investigation) in
[
8
, Section 8]
.

PDE-regularized optimization problems and splines.

Optimization problems regularized with the total variation norm of a scalar differential operator
L
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{R}(u):=\|Lu\|_{TV}
also fit the AGF framework. Indeed, the extremal points of the ball of
\mathcal{R}
can be characterized (up to elements in the null-space of
L
) as fundamental solutions translated by
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, cf.
[
14
,
45
,
46
]
. Therefore, depending on the regularity of the fundamental solution, AGFs applied to such problems are Euclidean gradient flows in
([0,L]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
.

BV functions in higher dimensions.
Optimization problems on BV functions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
regularized with the
BV
-seminorm can also be studied through AGFs. The extremal points of the unit ball of the
BV
-seminorm have been shown to be indicator functions of simple sets, cf.
[
14
,
3
,
33
,
25
]
. However, in this case, it is challenging to characterize the weak* distance on
\mathcal{B}
and the properties of the resulting metric space
[
29
,
28
]
.

A.1
Minimizing movements and curves of maximal slope

In order to formulate the concept of gradient flows in our setting, we have chosen (generalized) minimizing movements (see Section
3
and Section
4
). For completeness we recall the classical setup from
[
4
, Chapter 2]
.

Definition A.1

(
Discrete Scheme
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space and
F:X\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
. The discrete scheme is defined as follows.
Given a partition of the time interval
[0,+\infty)
by a sequence of time steps
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}:=(\tau_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,+\infty)
with
|{\boldsymbol{\tau}}|:=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\tau_{n}<+\infty
, let us set

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}&:=\{0=:t^{0}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}<t^{1}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}<\dots<t^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}<\dots\}\,,\quad I_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{n}:=(t_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{n-1},t_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{n}]\,,\\
\tau_{n}&=:t_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{n}-t_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{n-1}\,,\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}t_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}^{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\tau_{k}=+\infty\,,\end{split}

(A.1)

and consider the functionals

\displaystyle G(\tau_{n},u_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{n-1};v):=F(v)+\frac{1}{2\tau_{n}}d^{2}(v,u_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{n-1})\,.

(A.2)

Given an admissible initialization
u_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{0}\in\mathrm{Dom}(F)
, we define successively
(u^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset X
, with the property that

\displaystyle u^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\in\argmin_{v\in X}G(\tau_{n},u_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{n-1};v)\,\ \ \forall n\geqslant 1\,.

(A.3)

One then defines accordingly the piecewise constant interpolation

\overline{u}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(t):=\left\{\begin{IEEEeqnarraybox}[]{[}\IEEEeqnarraystrutmode\IEEEeqnarraystrutsizeadd{2pt}{2pt}][c]{rCl}&u_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{0}\,,&\quad\text{ if }t=0\,,\\
&u_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{n}\,,&\quad\text{ if }t\in(t^{n-1}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},t^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}]\,\ \ \forall n\geqslant 1\,.\end{IEEEeqnarraybox}\right.

(A.4)

We call
\overline{u}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
of (
A.4
) a
discrete solution
corresponding to the partition
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
of (
A.1
).

Remark A.2
.

The operator which provides all the solutions to the minimization problem (
A.3
) (given
u_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{n-1}
) is generically multi-valued, and is often called the
resolvent operator
. For a general
\tau>0
and
u\in X
it is defined via

J_{\tau}(u):=\argmin_{v\in X}G(\tau,u;v)\,,\ \textit{i.e.},\hskip 10.00002ptu_{\tau}\in J_{\tau}(u)\iff G(\tau,u;{u_{\tau}})\leqslant G(\tau,u;v)\ \ \forall v\in X\,.

Thus, Definition
A.1
can equivalently be phrased as follows:
\bar{u}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is a discrete solution iff
u_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{n}\in J_{\tau_{n}}(u_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{n-1})
for every
n\geqslant 1
.

Definition A.3

(
Minimizing Movements
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space,
F:X\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
and
G
be defined as in (
A.2
). We call a curve
x_{t}:[0,+\infty)\rightarrow X
a
minimizing movement
for
G
starting from
x_{0}\in\mathrm{Dom}(F)
iff there exists a sequence of partitions
({\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with
|{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{k}|\rightarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
, and a sequence of discrete solutions
(\overline{x}_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
as in (
A.4
) such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{(i)}&\quad\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}F(x^{0}_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{k}})=F(x_{0})\,,\quad\limsup_{k\to\infty}d(x^{0}_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{k}},x_{0})<+\infty\,,\\
\mathrm{(ii)}&\quad\overline{x}_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{k},t}\rightarrow x_{t}\text{ for all }t\geqslant 0\,,\ \text{as }k\to+\infty\,.\end{split}

(A.5)

More precisely, in
[
4
, Definition 2.0.6]
, such
x_{t}
are called
generalized minimizing movements
, and denoted by
x_{t}\in\mathrm{GMM}(G,x_{0})
. A related concept is the one of
minimizing movements
that can be defined as follows.
A curve
x_{t}:[0,+\infty)\rightarrow X
is a
minimizing movement
for
G
starting from
x_{0}\in\mathrm{Dom}(F)
, and one writes
x_{t}\in{\rm MM}(G,x_{0})
, iff for every partition
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}:=(\tau_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a discrete solution
\overline{x}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
as in (
A.3
)-(
A.4
), such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{(i)}&\quad\lim_{|{\boldsymbol{\tau}}|\downarrow 0}F(x^{0}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}})=F(x_{0})\,,\quad\limsup_{|{\boldsymbol{\tau}}|\downarrow 0}d(x^{0}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},x_{0})<+\infty\,,\\
\mathrm{(ii)}&\quad\overline{x}_{\boldsymbol{\tau},t}\rightarrow x_{t}\text{ for all }t\geqslant 0\,,\ \text{as }|{\boldsymbol{\tau}}|\rightarrow 0\,.\end{split}

(A.6)

We also recall and combine some standard results on uniqueness of gradient flows on metric spaces of non-positive curvature in the following, using as a reference
[
4
, Chapter 4]
.

Theorem A.4
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space of non-positive curvature cf.
2.5
, and
F:X\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
be
\lambda
-convex for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then for every initial point
x_{0}\in X
, there exists a unique
x_{t}\in\mathrm{GMM}(G,x_{0})
that is locally Lipschitz and fulfills the following contractivity property: for every
x_{0},\tilde{x}_{0}\in X
,

d\big(x_{t},\tilde{x}_{t}\big)\leqslant e^{-\lambda t}d\big(x_{0},\tilde{x}_{0}\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }\mathcal{L}^{1}\text{-a.e. }t>0\,.

(A.7)

Given
x_{0}\in X
, the unique corresponding
x_{t}\in\mathrm{GMM}(G,x_{0})
is also the unique solution to the following evolution variational inequality
(\mathrm{EVI})_{\lambda}
:

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}d^{2}(x_{t},y)+\frac{\lambda}{2}d^{2}(x_{t},y)+F(x_{t})\leqslant F(y)\,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall y\in\mathrm{Dom}(F)\,,

(A.8)

and is a curve of maximal slope from
x_{0}
for
F
, hence fulfills the inequality:

2\frac{d}{dt}F(x_{t})\leqslant-|x_{t}^{\prime}|^{2}-|\partial F|^{2}(x_{t})\,.

(A.9)

In addition, for all absolutely continuous curves
\tilde{x}_{t}
starting from
x_{0}
,
x_{t}
is the unique one that fulfills (
A.9
), and therefore it is also the unique one with this property among all curves of maximal slope from
x_{0}
with respect to
F
.

Proof.

Since
F
is
\lambda
-convex and
(X,d)
is NPC, by
[
4
, Remark 4.0.2]
it follows that
v\mapsto G(\tau,w;v)
is
(\tau^{-1}+\lambda)
-convex (see
[
4
, Assumption 4.0.1]
. Therefore, existence, uniqueness and regularity of
u\in{\rm MM}(G,x_{0})
as well as (
A.7
) follow from
[
4
, Theorem 4.0.4]
.

To prove that
{\rm MM}(G,x_{0})={\rm GMM}(G,x_{0})
, note that the solutions to the discrete scheme (
A.2
) are unique for
|\boldsymbol{\tau}|
small enough, as then
[
4
, Theorem 4.1.2]
is applicable because then
\lambda|\boldsymbol{\tau}|>-1
. By Definition
A.3
, one has that for all partitions
\boldsymbol{\tau}
the unique discrete solution
\overline{x}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
of (
A.4
) satisfies

\overline{x}_{\boldsymbol{\tau},t}\rightarrow x_{t}\ \text{ for }\ |{\boldsymbol{\tau}}|\rightarrow 0\,.

Now, if
\tilde{x}_{t}\in{\rm GMM}(G,x_{0})
, by the definition of generalized minimizing movements,
\tilde{x}_{t}
is obtained by a specific choice of partitions
({\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and discrete solutions
(\overline{x}_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset X
, for which

\overline{x}_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{k},t}\rightarrow\tilde{x}_{t}
as

.
But then it must also hold that
\overline{x}_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{k},t}\rightarrow x_{t}
as

, hence

, showing the uniqueness. The uniqueness among all curves of maximal slope follows from the existence of

solutions, cf.
[
39
, Theorem 4.2]
.
