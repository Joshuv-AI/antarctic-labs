A qualitative study of the reimplementation of a standardised paediatric early warning system pilot in England
Authors: Stephanie Stockwell, Sarah Ball, Damian Roland, Graham Martin, Jennifer Newbould
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEfforts to improve healthcare quality and safety and optimise resource use are increasingly focused on deimplementation—the reduction or cessation of existing practice. Reimplementation, a common form of deimplementation, involving the reintroduction of an existing intervention with some degree of modification, is under-researched, particularly in the context of standardising clinical tools. Recent initiatives, such as the national rollout of a standardised paediatric early warning system (PEWS) in England, highlight the need to understand the processes and challenges of substitution, especially from nursing and paediatric perspectives.MethodsThis qualitative study employed a comparative case study design across four diverse hospital sites. Data collection (June 2023-November 2024) included semi-structured interviews with staff (n = 29), site-specific workshops and a final stakeholder workshop. Thematic analysis, informed by the Theoretical Domains Framework, was used to analyse interview and workshop data, with cross-case comparisons to identify influences on the reimplementation of PEWS.ResultsBuy-in and firm commitment (purchase) was identified as a central influence on the reimplementation effort and was in turn influenced by factors falling into four key categories: product and process (characteristics of, familiarity with and confidence using the system being reimplemented – connected to specific training needs and gaps); people (the people involved in or providing support for reimplementation – having strong leadership across roles and seniority, ward champions and peer support); purpose and promise (belief in the value of the reimplementation); and practice (practical and contextual influences on reimplementation efforts – staffing pressures, removal of the previous system, and acknowledgement that the change would require an initial adjustment period).ConclusionsThe considerations identified in this study provide a simple ‘5P’ framework to help understand and support reimplementation, which maps to and is grounded in existing theory. Those considering reimplementation efforts should consider (1) the people, (2) the product and process, (3) the purpose and promise, and (4) the practical influences when reimplementing systems or initiatives. These factors greatly influence (5) purchase – buy-in and commitment – and, we suggest, ultimate success. This will be important in the wider roll-out of a national PEWS as well as other standardisation efforts in the NHS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-01013-x.Keywords: Implementation, Deimplementation, Reimplementation, Paediatric early warning system, Early warning system

Background

Efforts in healthcare to improve safety, reduce costs, and enhance outcomes involve constant attempts to implement new practices and approaches. However, a growing body of literature also emphasises the importance of the deimplementation of existing healthcare practices, to reduce risks of patient harm and improve quality and safety [1–6], and with the potential for cost savings [7]. Deimplementation, defined as “reducing or stopping the use of a health service or practice provided to patients by health care practitioners and health care delivery systems” (p93) [2], is an emerging topic in implementation science, with a particular focus on identifying influences on its success [1, 8, 9]. It has sometimes been conceptualised as an implicit part of implementation [10, 11].

One distinct type of deimplementation, and a very common yet under-researched strategy in clinical practice, is ‘substitution’ – whereby an existing practice is substituted with a related replacement [10–13]. Substitution takes various forms [13]. One scenario involves the reintroduction of an existing intervention within the same environment, but with some degree of modification, with the aim of addressing the limitations of the previous version and better achieving the desired outcomes – a process that has been termed ‘reimplementation’ [14].

Reasons for reimplementation are various and include issues with the intervention itself, failures in the previous implementation process or in sustaining implementation [14], and standardisation [15, 16]. The benefits of standardisation efforts are widely lauded [17–19], including, for example, their potential to reduce error [20, 21], to reduce uncertainty in clinical interactions [22] and to facilitate the training of staff who may move between settings [16, 23]. However, the challenges of reimplementing standardised versions of existing tools have not been explored in detail in the literature. While there is a growing interest in clinical practice deimplementation and substitution research, few real-life examples have been documented to date [24]. Moreover, there is an identified need for further research to explore the sociocultural and technical aspects of standardisation [25] and to understand how reimplementation happens in practice [14].

One recent example of a coordinated reimplementation effort is ongoing work to introduce a national standardised system for monitoring and responding to clinical deterioration in children (a Paediatric Early Warning System – PEWS) in hospitals across England [26, 27]. All hospitals in England that provide paediatric inpatient care have a locally implemented PEWS in place [28]. However, the variation in systems has been recognised as a threat to the quality and safety of care provided to children [29, 30]. With a view to addressing this variation, in 2019 NHS England, the Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health (RCPCH) and the Royal College of Nursing (RCN) launched a programme to introduce a single standardised National PEWS (NPEWS) in hospitals across England. This programme involved the iterative development of NPEWS over time, based on piloting. Replacement of early versions of the NPEWS with refined versions essentially resulted in multiple reimplementation efforts in succession.

In this paper we use a theory-based approach to understand the processes and experiences of these reimplementation efforts in selected sites in England that were part of the pilot programme. The study explored the influences on successful reimplementation (i.e., a smooth and complete transition from the old to the new system – with the new system becoming practice as usual, and the old system no longer in use), with a view to informing both the national rollout of the NPEWS and wider reimplementation efforts in other settings.

This study addresses several gaps in the existing literature. First, while research on deimplementation has been undertaken across a range of healthcare contexts, including in the paediatric setting, others have identified a particular need for research in this field, given the specific challenges for deimplementation associated with paediatric hospital care [31, 32]. These include, for example, the influence of parental pressure in relation to continuation of previous practice, and clinician fears associated with missing a diagnosis or sudden deterioration in paediatric patients – resulting in poor outcomes or legal ramifications. Secondly, much existing research on deimplementation in healthcare has focused on the medical rather than the nursing perspective [33]. Given their central role in the functioning of PEWS, nurses’ perspectives are foregrounded in this work. Finally, the largely atheoretical nature of deimplementation research has impeded the insights it offers and its generalisability, and the use of theory has been recommended to support the understanding of how and why efforts succeed or fail and influences on their success [3, 34]. By using the Theoretical Domains Framework [35], we seek to enhance the transferability of our findings.

Methods

This qualitative study used a comparative case study design [36, 37]. Reporting is guided by the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) [38] (Additional File). The study explored the reimplementation of PEWS across four case study sites. Following initial scoping and case site selection data collection involved (i) case study interviews; (ii) case study workshops; and (iii) a final workshop with wider stakeholders. Data were collected between 19th June 2023 and 7th November 2024. The researchers involved had varying levels of experience, from five to over 25 years in qualitative research, with backgrounds in applied social psychology, sociology, quality improvement and implementation science.

Ethics statement

The project was reviewed by the RAND Human Subjects Protection Committee (Ref. 2022-N0235, determination provided 19th August 2022). The governance and legal compliance of the study were assessed and approved by the Health Research Authority (HRA).

Initial scoping and case study site selection

Initial scoping activities informed the design of the study and helped to gather information used to select case study sites. These included desk research and stakeholder engagement activities. Desk research involved the collation and review of programme-related documentation (available on the NHS Futures platform [39]) and publicly available information on the characteristics of the sites being considered for selection. Stakeholder engagement involved scoping conversations with individuals involved in the development of the system (NPEWS) that was being rolled out (n = 5), and an initial online stakeholder workshop with staff from a range of roles within pilot sites (n = 8). Based on the information gathered through these scoping activities, four case study sites were purposefully sampled from 15 that had launched the pilot at the point of selection. When sampling, we sought to ensure diversity in geographic location, type of hospital (specialist, teaching, small or large district general hospital) and whether the previous PEWS and NPEWS were delivered through paper-based or digital modes (Table 1). The NPEWS was introduced in case study sites between November 2021 and June 2022.

Case study interviews

In collaboration with the central programme team, a lead contact (responsible for NPEWS roll-out locally) was identified within each of the selected sites. Following a briefing, this contact took responsibility for identifying team members with relevant and varied experience of the reimplementation effort and forwarded an email on behalf of the research team containing information about the study and contact details for the study team. We aimed to interview up to eight members of staff involved in the reimplementation of the existing PEWS in each case study site, from a range of professional roles (including consultant paediatricians, nurses, health care assistants, and other staff who may need to engage with the system such as emergency department and ambulance staff). Semi-structured online interviews (S1: Case Site Interview Topic Guide) explored the experiences of staff before deimplementation, during the preparations for reimplementation and as the system was reimplemented. The topic guides were informed by information gathered in the scoping stage and by the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF), developed by Michie et al. (2005) [35]. The TDF is a determinant framework (describing determinants believed to influence implementation outcomes) commonly used in research on deimplementation [2, 34]. Developed to increase the accessibility of behaviour change theories for implementation researchers, the TDF consists of a wide range of constructs across 14 domains. These can be used to identify determinants of behaviour and potential interventions.

All participants provided written informed consent to participate and for the interview to be audio recorded. Audio files were transcribed verbatim by a professional transcription service. Transcripts were checked for accuracy by a member of the research team before they were analysed.

Online workshops relating to each case study site

After the completion of interviews, an online workshop was held to explore emerging themes and reflections from case study sites on the process of reimplementation. Workshops served to feedback key learning from the interview phase and to refine the learning gained through discussion amongst staff within each individual site. All interview participants were invited to attend the workshop. The invitation was also shared by email with any additional staff from the site with relevant experience and/or interest by the by the lead contact. Each workshop involved an initial presentation by a member of the research team, summarising the interview findings for that site. This was followed by a facilitated discussion covering the following questions: What seems to have worked well? What have been the challenges? Do you have any top tips for other sites? Any other questions/reflections? Each workshop was recorded with permission of the attendees using Microsoft Teams’ recording facility. Notes taken in the workshop and the recordings were used to produce a slide deck for feedback and use in analysis.

Final analysis workshop with wider stakeholders

Once all data was collected and analysed, a final online workshop was convened, which included attendees from the research team, policy organisations such as NHS England and representatives from case study sites. Emerging findings from the cross-case analysis were shared through a presentation; the final workshop provided an opportunity to further refine the findings through discussion and to seek input around their interpretation and implications, including reasons for differences between sites and the influences on reimplementation.

Data analysis and synthesis

Our approach to analysis was guided by the TDF [35]; however consideration was also given to additional factors relating to the process of reimplementation, beyond those proposed in the TDF. The four cases were analysed independently in the first instance. Thematic analysis of interview transcripts was conducted for each case [40], using NVivo 12 to assist with data management. A coding frame was developed for the first case (based on the topic guides and an initial reading of interview/workshop transcripts and guided by the TDF). This was developed iteratively as analysis progressed. Coding was undertaken by two members of the research team (SS and SB), with transcripts divided between them. Regular discussions took place between members of the research team to resolve any instances of coding uncertainty and to highlight new emerging themes and required refinements to the coding frame. The analysis of subsequent cases used the initial coding frame, with further refinements where necessary (as established through team discussion). Particular attention was paid to examples which did not fit the identified themes.

The research team constructed case descriptions for each case in a structured template to facilitate comparison, which included the key characteristics of the site and findings relating to key themes. During a series of cross-case analysis workshops, the research team first created an analysis matrix (incorporating data from the four structured case descriptions) to inform systematic comparisons on each of the major themes identified. Details on the context of the reimplementation effort at each site were explicitly considered when exploring similarities and differences between cases with regard to how reimplementation played out. Then, during a further workshop, the research team mapped out relationships between the identified influences on reimplementation.

Findings from the cross-case analysis were shared with participants and other stakeholders (including policy makers) to explore these findings through further discussion and to aid interpretation before final synthesis and reporting.

A total of 29 staff members participated in an interview (average length 37 min; range: 23–86 min) and/or the case site workshops across 4 case study sites (Site A = 6; Site B = 8; Site C = 8; Site D = 7 participants). Results

Interviews and workshops across the case-study sites provided rich data on how reimplementation efforts unfolded and the perceived influences on their success. These influences fall into four key categories: product and process (characteristics of and familiarity with the system being reimplemented); people (the people involved in or providing support for reimplementation); purpose and promise (belief in the value of the reimplementation); and practice (practical and contextual influences on reimplementation efforts). Our findings suggest that the influences in the four categories described above often appear to exert their effects via their impact on purchase – the degree to which staff are committed to and invested in the endeavours described. Based on these findings, we propose a framework – the ‘5P’ framework – to help understand and support reimplementation efforts in practice, which maps to and is grounded in existing theory. In Fig. 1, we present a model setting out how the 5Ps appear to be linked to successful reimplementation, and mapping the influences identified in this study to the domains of the TDF (to facilitate comparison with existing and future theory driven research in the field and enhance the practical utility of the framework in supporting practice change). Detailed findings from the case study interviews and workshops relating to each of the five framework headings are presented in turn below.

5P framework for painless reimplementation

Purchase: the degree of buy-in for reimplementation

Buy-in (conceptualised as “a personal and professional commitment to actively engage in a process, task, or initiative” [42]) was highlighted by staff across case-study sites as central to getting individuals to engage in reimplementation, at an institutional and personal level – with firm commitment (or purchase) being dependent on anticipated returns. Purchase appeared to be strongly influenced by the product and its associated processes (i.e. the NPEWS itself), the people leading reimplementation efforts, the purpose and promise (i.e. rationale and anticipated impacts), and practice (practical and contextual factors) – outlined in more detail below. Purchase then influenced staff ‘intentions’ (as conceptualised in the TDF) and subsequent behavioural change to support and sustain reimplementation. More important than individual-level buy-in was the collective buy-in of all the ward staff. This was often driven by key individuals, and not necessarily those in leadership positions:

Once you got one or two people that were quite vocal on the ward on side, you were alright, um, but if you didn’t, then it could be quite a rocky ride to introduce change. (Site B, Experienced Paediatric Practice Development Nurse)

Product and process: Characteristics of and familiarity with using the new system

Familiarity with and confidence in using the product and process being reimplemented (encapsulating the TDF’s ‘Knowledge’, ‘Skills’ and ‘Beliefs about capabilities’ domains) emerged as an important contributor to the perceived success of reimplementation across the sites, through its influence on staff buy-in (purchase). However, differences were noted in the approaches used to support staff in gaining familiarity with NPEWS, associated with local variation in the degree of difference between the existing PEWS and the NPEWS replacement. In Site A, the NPEWS was very similar to the previous PEWS, with a few minor changes to the layout of the chart, meaning that staff were already familiar with and confident using the chart. Participants reported that no formal training was provided at Site A for existing staff: instead, differences between the charts were highlighted to staff via a poster and staff signed off that they had read the required documentation:

I don’t think we needed any additional in-depth training for this project […] You didn’t need two hours in a classroom to go through the new PEWS. Having the support on the ward was better… (Site A, Ward Sister)

Staff at Site A mentioned there were still some challenges around differing interpretations of aspects of the chart or wording used in written parts, which were often worked out at a ward level or between wards. An online training package, developed centrally by NHS England after the initial reimplementation, was made available to staff at all sites, and was the only formal training mentioned at site A.

Conversely, there was a greater difference between NPEWS and previous PEWS in Sites B, C and D (e.g. changes to the thresholds for action for individual scores). At these sites, formal training developed by the sites themselves took place, ranging from watching a PowerPoint presentation to completing practice scenarios. Specific training was mentioned as required for elements of NPEWS that differed more drastically from the previous PEWS. At two sites (C and D), additional training was provided regarding NPEWS’ parental concern element, which did not feature in their previous PEWS. Similarly, staff required additional support to understand the scores, thresholds and escalation processes if they differed from the previous PEWS. This was delivered in training and through informal peer support on the ward:

I think a lot of the time it is then just putting it into your practical job, your everyday real life, just seeing how it works. I think the training was sufficient. I don’t think we really needed anything else. (Site D, Paediatric Sister, experienced in day-to-day use of PEWS)

The opportunity to practise using the chart with case studies and/or dummy IT programmes was valued by staff as it gave them an opportunity to apply knowledge and problem solve in a training situation. However, one specific training need appeared to be missed in the reimplementation efforts. For Site B, whilst comprehensive training was delivered on the digital PEWS, no training was provided on the back-up paper version. When there were IT failures, staff resorted to the paper version of the previous PEWS rather than the paper version of NPEWS – meaning that the reimplementation process was incomplete.

Familiarity with and confidence in using the product and process being reimplemented was identified as an influence on purchase across sites. For example, at site C, one participant (Advanced Nurse Practitioner) described how the long lead-in time to the reimplementation, with lots of discussion and training provided in the run-up, meant that staff were keen to get on with the change by the time it was introduced:

I feel like they talked about it for ages before it actually started. So by the time it started, everyone was like, yeah, finally, we’ll start doing it. Rather, like, they’d already done all the training with everyone, and we’d been talking about it for like, ages, so. Then we just swapped them over, and that was that, really. (Site C, Advanced Nurse Practitioner)

The degree to which the NPEWS resembled the previous PEWS also appeared to influence confidence in making the switch, and in turn staff buy-in. For example, at Site B, one participant indicated that they were “happy to roll with the changes” because, while it took some time to adapt, “it wasn’t anything that was too tricky. It’s very similar.” (Site B, Deputy Ward Sister, experienced in the day-to-day use of PEWS).

People: Leadership, ward champions, peer support, and confidence in change

The type and characteristics of people involved (including the frontline staff for whom a change in behaviour was required and those supporting the endeavour at a variety of levels), and the associated confidence of teams when navigating change, were also identified across sites as an important influence on how the reimplementation unfolded (again, by influencing purchase). These influences map to the ‘Social and professional role’, ‘Social influences’ and ‘Emotions’ domains of the TDF – with clear communication between the involved parties also appearing to be key to success. Staff in all pilot sites expressed confidence in their ability to deal with change as they had been involved in pilot projects before; many staff members were not particularly worried about the reimplementation. Similarly, all sites had strong leadership involvement in the pilot, including nurses (Site A, B, C) and/or consultants (Site C, D). Endorsement by the RCPCH, and knowledge that the intention was for the NPEWS to be the national standard, encouraged sites to take part in the pilot.

Having dedicated staff (e.g. educators, safety teams) or ward champions to help with the operationalisation of the reimplementation (e.g. ordering charts, providing training, providing explicit instructions to staff around the changeover, and ensuring everything was in place) was extremely valuable, and where it was temporarily lacking the impact was felt:

We started doing the training for staff. That was another challenge because, obviously, at that time, we didn’t have any non-clinical staff. We didn’t have a practice educator. We’ve got full-time posts now, so if it was happening now, it might have been better. (Site C, Ward Sister - Nurse lead for PEWS)

The importance of a combination of practice educator support and having ward managers onboard with frontline experience was also emphasised with regard to securing staff buy-in / commitment to reimplementation (i.e. purchase):

I think people could connect to somebody that has worked on the shop floor with kids, done their obs[ervations], […] I think it’s a bonus, and having an educator on the ward as well, because not every ward at that time did, and I’m sure not every ward in the country has either. So I think having that support around, and a ward manager that was on board and around, I think that’s a really good, yeah, set up to start with. (Site B, Paediatric Practice Educator)

Similarly, at site C, involving frontline staff in developing and delivering training around the reimplementation was highlighted as an important influence on staff buy-in, ensuring that they feel a part of the effort and invested in its success:

Having a couple of healthcare assistants [involved in delivering the training] really helped because then they didn’t feel excluded. They felt part of it. I think quite often change does come either from above […] but, yeah, it was very inclusive, and well, I just asked for some volunteers to be part of the team, and to both camps, really, registered and unregistered nurses. So, yeah, that helped, I think. (Site C, Ward Sister - Nurse Lead for PEWS)

Alongside formal leadership, informal peer support from colleagues on the ward was also frequently mentioned as particularly helpful, as they were available to answer questions as things arose with real patients, rather than practice scenarios. This reassured some staff members who were initially apprehensive about the implementation. Across all pilot sites, most staff felt that information about the upcoming reimplementation was communicated clearly, early, via multiple channels (e.g. email, face-to-face on the wards, WhatsApp, posters, presentations) and at multiple time points. Face-to-face communication was generally valued above other forms.

Purpose and promise: Belief that the change is needed and an improvement

Most staff across pilot sites were aware of the rationale for reimplementation. Whilst many were optimistic or at least agnostic about the reimplementation, some staff were cynical about the change and the need for it, especially given the constant changes they perceived in the NHS:

There’s probably not a sensible reason that’s evidence based as to why you’re doing it. It’s probably just been decided that this is what we’re doing. So, you just think, okay, just let them do it as quickly as we can before the next thing changes. […] It’s a bit like… the milk that you get from Sainsbury’s is semi-skimmed. It used to have this green packaging around it. Now, they’ve changed the packaging to something else. It’s still the same milk. Still the same stuff inside it. How it looks like on the outside, you notice for a bit. Then you forget because it’s still the same on the inside, which is the heart rate and the resp[iratory] rate. Those observations don’t change. It’s just the format on which it sits changes. (Site B, Paediatric Consultant)

Staff beliefs about the consequences of the reimplementation were generally positive. Many staff members felt that NPEWS would have positive impacts on patient safety because of the increased number of observations and ability to detect deterioration earlier due to its enhanced sensitivity, even if it increased demands on staff:

When that patient would have gone downhill, would have seen that a lot earlier than what it would have been. So, probably would have been less emergencies on the ward. (Site D, Bank Nurse, with experience in the use of the incumbent and NPEWS)

Most staff believed that standardising PEWS across the country would be beneficial for staff and patients if they move across hospitals because of the shared understanding. A few staff commented that they hoped that the change would help families and patients feel more listened to.

Across sites, the degree to which staff understood the intended purpose and believed in the benefits of the reimplementation effort appeared to influence buy-in and resistance to change.

Yeah, I think everybody with change, you go through this denial [process], don’t you? […] ‘Oh, this is too much work, it takes longer’. But then eventually when you say - it’s like a safety thing actually… It’s there to flag up when a child’s unwell. It gives you that opportunity to escalate that and you’ve got that evidence. So, if you can get people to understand that it’s a patient safety [issue], then you find that people become… get on board with it. It’s not there to be tricky for the staff. It’s there to [ensure] protection for the child, isn’t it? (Site C, Paediatric Advanced Clinical Practitioner, experience in completing and reviewing PEWS)

Where the benefits were less clear to staff, such as in the case where the incumbent system was perceived to work well, resistance to change could result:

I think any change, especially when we had a system that seemingly wasn’t broken, it’s always received with some reluctance, especially as the format was changing. It was a bit of… I guess, it was a bit of getting used to. So, there was understandable anxiety and reluctance too. (Site D, Paediatric Consultant)

Practice: Practical and contextual influences on reimplementation

A range of practical and contextual influences on PEWS reimplementation were identified by study participants (covering the TDF’s ‘Environmental context/resources’ and ‘Memory, attention and decision making’ domains). These related to environmental context and resources, features of how the reimplementation process was managed in practice, and the impact of time and need for adjustment.

As highlighted in Fig. 1, these practical considerations appeared to have both a direct and indirect influence on reimplementation – in that the presence of practical challenges and overlapping demands not only contributed to the purchase of the reimplementation effort (the degree to which staff bought into it), but also directly impacted the substitution of old with new behaviours. In an example of this direct influence, not having access to the replacement chart (due to printing or supply issues) would prevent or delay the reimplementation process (regardless of whether staff were committed to reimplementation). Conversely, the complete removal of all copies of the previous chart was one way of preventing staff from reverting to it even if they wanted to:

We just got rid of all the old ones and we just put them all in confidential waste. We didn’t hold onto anything. That way, no-one’s going to pick up an old one and there was a stock. We had a stock of the new ones in my office which they knew, they were kept separate from the old ones till then. (Site A, Ward Sister)

Whilst removal of the previous PEWS was possible within most of the pilot wards (Sites A, B, C), the old system often remained in use in other areas of the hospital across all sites. It could be challenging for staff who worked across wards to remember which PEWS they were using and therefore what a score meant for the patient. It could also lead to patients/parents questioning staff about observations when the system was not the same across the hospital.

Of the two sites that had previously had digital systems, Site B was able to reimplement a digital NPEWS, but Site C was unable to, because its information systems contractor was unable to accommodate the iterative pilot process leading to many changes in a short period of time. This led to some staff commenting that going back to paper felt like a backward step.

Other contextual factors, such as severe staffing issues made reimplementation more challenging in some sites. Resident doctors’ strikes at the time of the reimplementation effort meant consultants were covering an increased workload. In addition, the paper NPEWS was perceived as large and cumbersome to use, especially as there were often limited work surfaces around patients’ beds to complete it.

Has to be filed and you have to open out the whole sheet to see the chart and you’ve not got very big desks or areas to write on so it’s hard to open it out fully in front of you. (Site D, Ward Sister, day-to-day experience of using paper- based PEWS)

The indirect influence of contextual and practical factors on the success of reimplementation, via their influence on purchase, includes the impact of frustration around practical challenges on willingness to engage with the process. In addition, the nature of the local context and prior staff experience with change initiatives was identified as a further influence on purchase:

They are pretty used to it by now. So, they’re like ‘Oh, okay. What do we need to do?’ […] honestly, we’ve had so many things that we’ve been used to be a pilot for and change. They’re just literally like, ‘What now? What have we got to do? Let’s do it’. (Site B, Paediatric Practice Educator)

A unique challenge for the pilot sites was the iterative development of NPEWS, requiring new versions to replace older versions of NPEWS, resulting in multiple reimplementation efforts in a short timeframe. With any change, staff acknowledged an initial period of time that required them to concentrate harder and meant that observations took longer with NPEWS than with the previous systems:

The first few times of using or the first few days or patients, it’s just a bit more clunky. […] I think we all like to be lazy and confident in what we know. And when that’s challenged or changed, it just can make you feel a little bit out of sorts. And that you need to do a bit of extra brain power but, obviously over time it was just, you know, as with anything once you get used to using it and you can see the benefits of it. (Site B, Clinical Lead Physiotherapist, day-to-day experience of using and reviewing PEWS)

Discussion

In this qualitative study, using a theory-driven approach and comparative case-study methodology, we identified a wide range of influences on the successful reimplementation of PEWS, in the context of pilot work to introduce a standardised system across hospitals in England. Central to the ease and success of reimplementation efforts the four sites studied appeared to be securing the buy-in and firm commitment – what we call purchase – of the staff who must engage with the PEWS on a day-to-day basis. In turn, the extent to which this occurred was found to be influenced by the product and process (characteristics of and familiarity with the system being reimplemented), the people (those involved in or providing support for reimplementation) and the purpose and promise of the efforts (belief in the value of the reimplementation). In addition, practical and contextual influences on reimplementation – practice – emerged as key contributors to the ease and success of the substitution. This was both by virtue of their influence on securing firm commitment from staff to engage with the initiative (purchase) and by facilitating or hindering behaviour substitution directly.

The identification of five key categories of influence on reimplementation provides a helpful framework for the analysis of the findings of this study. It also holds promise as a simple and practical framework to support the development of reimplementation strategies in practice and to further research on the topic. In particular, the identification of purchase as an apparent mediating mechanism through which other influences exert their effect warrants further exploration. The 5Ps of the model presented have been mapped to the domains of the TDF, facilitating comparison with existing and future theory-driven research in the field and enhancing the practical utility of the framework.

Despite its frequent occurrence, substitution and specifically reimplementation has received little research attention to date [10, 24]. Our findings align with and extend the small amount of prior work showing that substitution can be more complex than other deimplementation efforts because of entrenched practices, and the benefits from leveraging a wide range of facilitators to ease the process [13, 24] – the 5Ps.

Consistent with the principles set out by Patey et al. (2023) for selecting substitute behaviours, effective reimplementation is supported when the new behaviour has ‘a strong evidence-based or clinical rationale’, serves ‘both the clinical objective and practical objective’, and is ‘easily explainable to patients’ [13] – echoing our ‘purpose and promise’ of reimplementation. Similarly, based on the findings of a scoping review of literature reporting on cases of reimplementation, Moyal-Smith et al. (2023) highlighted the need to adapt implementation strategies for reimplementation [14] – for example by focusing training and messaging on the rationale for the change (rather that the rationale for the intervention per se). Our findings suggest that staff at the selected sites understood the rationale for the replacement of existing PEWS with NPEWS and generally believed that the substitution would be for the benefit of the safety of the patients.

Regarding the product and process, staff largely felt their existing skills transferred to the substitute behaviour (the use of NPEWS), limiting training burden, which is another principle suggested by Patey et al. (2023) [13]. Nonetheless, the literature [1] and our findings highlight the importance of skills training and audit, and feedback approaches as part of an effective strategy for smooth transition, particularly for elements that differed between the old and new measures. Where training was provided, opportunities to practise and comprehensive coverage (i.e. training on paper back up for digital outages) were valued.

In addition to training, audit and feedback, the people involved in reimplementation are critical. Building on Norton and Chambers (2020) [1] and Moyal-Smith et al. (2023) [14], our findings emphasise the importance of leadership support, ward champions and clear, timely, multi-modal communication as drivers of positive attitudes and engagement for reimplementation, reflecting common deimplementation strategies in health care [1, 3, 43]. In the present study, nursing staff were key to the reimplementation, but the involvement of other staff roles may have created a sense of trust that encouraged individual behaviour change [33, 44, 45], which might be more effective for sustaining reimplementation in paediatric settings [31]. Therefore, our findings support claims in the literature that fostering a positive and supportive environment, involving multiprofessional teams in deimplementation is likely to be key to success, beyond traditional knowledge and skills training alone [3, 9, 45]. Meaningful stakeholder engagement and two-way feedback during the modification process for reimplementation, incorporating ‘human-centred design principles’ [14], were appreciated by staff in the present study.

Practice – the practical and contextual environment – may have stronger influence in the case of reimplementation than for other types of deimplementation effort due to the need to consider factors related to the initial implementation [14]. Contrary to prior recommendations, that substitutes should be no more time-consuming or expensive than what they replace [13] and that there may be increased resistance to change routinised processes in the context of time pressures [14], some staff in the present study noted that using NPEWS took longer than previous systems because of an increase in the level of detail required. Yet this was largely accepted due to the potential for associated improvements in patient safety, which was of greater importance to staff. In addition, deliberate environmental changes (e.g. removing old PEWS charts) helped prevent staff from reverting, especially in the initial transition period. This means that practice is important, and whilst a substitution that creates more work or is more complicated will be challenging to implement, it is possible to obtain purchase if the purpose is deemed ultimately of value.

We highlight purchase as a key factor influencing reimplementation, which appears to be reliant not only on clear communication and stakeholder engagement [14] to convey purpose and promise but also on familiarity and confidence with the new system (product and process), supportive social environments (people) and practical enablers (practice). Consistent with the evidence that multifaceted deimplementation interventions in healthcare settings are more effective than singular ones [43], all sites used a multifaceted approach tailored to local needs and contexts [46], and all were successful in replacing the incumbent system with the standardised alternative. These multifaceted approaches effectively targeted each of the 5Ps of our proposed framework to bring about behaviour change.

Strengths and Limitations

This research explored a real-world example of reimplementation, from paediatric settings, underpinned by theory. A large proportion of the data comes from a nursing perspective, which is often lacking in deimplementation research. The data from this study comes from a broad sample, representing a range of hospitals, professionals across different levels, using both paper and digital PEWS.

A limitation of this research is that the sites participating were pilot sites and may therefore have specific motivations and resources for being involved in the pilot or the research, which may not be directly applicable to other sites if there was a national roll out. Pilot sites may also have faced unique challenges due to the iterative development of the system, which required multiple reimplementation efforts in short timeframes. The interval between the reimplementation start and the data collection at sites may have led to recall bias. In addition, it created challenges with recruiting resident doctors to the study as they had often rotated out of the site before data collection occurred. However, this time lag did allow for greater reflection on the process of embedding NPEWS, which may have provided useful insights. It should also be noted that staff of one case study site were unable to attend an online workshop, despite multiple follow-up attempts, due to clinical workload; therefore, refinements to the analysis of Site A were made only by the authors and during the national workshop.

Finally, the 5Ps framework proposed in this paper, while data- and theory-informed, drew on the subjective reflections and inferences of the research team in its development. Further testing of the model is warranted to establish its validity, particularly with regard to the centrality of purchase as a mediating mechanism.

Conclusions

This study used a theory-based approach to understand the processes, experiences and influences associated with reimplementing a paediatric early warning system. A supportive social environment, with buy-in from leadership and ward champions across multiprofessional teams promoted positive attitudes among individual staff members, underpinning behaviour change. Training was required to update staff, particularly on aspects of NPEWS that differed from what they were used to and whilst an initial period of transition required more cognitive effort from staff, they quickly became used to NPEWS as it fitted within their existing clinical practice.

The considerations identified in this study provide a simple ‘5P’ framework to help understand and support such endeavours, which maps to and is grounded in existing theory. Those considering reimplementation should consider (1) the people, (2) the product and process, (3) the purpose and promise, and (4) the practical influences on reimplementation as these factors greatly influence (5) purchase – and ultimately the success of reimplementation.
