Adaptive Experimentation for Censored Survival Outcomes
Yuxin Wang, Dennis Frauen, Jonas Schweisthal, Maresa Schröder, Emil Javurek

Abstract
Adaptive experimentation enables efficient estimation of causal effects, but existing methods are not designed for survival data with censoring, where event times are only partially observed (e.g., overall survival in cancer trials but with dropout). In this paper, we develop a novel framework for adaptive experimentation to estimate causal effects under right censoring. For this, we derive the semiparametric efficiency bound for the average survival effect curve as a function of the treatment allocation policy and thereby obtain a closed-form efficiency-optimal allocation policy. The policy generalizes classical Neyman allocation to survival settings by prioritizing patient strata where both event and censoring dynamics induce high uncertainty. Building on this, we propose the Adaptive Survival Estimator (ASE) , an adaptive framework that learns the allocation policy and estimates the average survival effect curve sequentially. Our framework has three main benefits: (i) it accommodates arbitrary machine learning models for nuisance estimation; (ii) it is guided by a closed-form efficiency-optimal allocation policy; and (iii) it admits strong theoretical guarantees, including asymptotic normality via a martingale central limit theorem. We demonstrate our framework across various numerical experiments to show consistent efficiency gains over uniform randomization and censoring-agnostic baselines.

Semiparametric efficiency bound and optimal treatment allocation

To guide optimal experiment design in survival settings, we first derive the semiparametric efficiency bound for ATE estimation. This then characterizes the variance-minimizing allocation strategy and motivates our ASE method later (see §

We consider a setting where the experimenter has direct control over

We now propose the
Adaptive Survival Estimator (ASE)
, a sequential framework for learning the optimal allocation policy and estimating the average survival effect curve. Our goal is to minimize the semiparametric efficiency bound from Theorem
4.1
. We proceed as follows: (1) We introduce the
adaptive treatment allocation policy
in Section
5.1
and the adaptive version of
average survival effect curve estimation
in Section
5.2
. We provide an extension to batch settings in Appendix

.

5.1
Adaptive treatment allocation policy

Our framework operates in two steps. In the first
R_{0}
rounds, units are assigned under a fixed policy
\pi_{\mathrm{init}}(X)
(e.g., Bernoulli(
1/2
) randomization) to accumulate sufficient history for stable estimation of the survival and censoring hazards
\lambda^{S}_{t},\lambda^{G}_{t}
. From round
R_{0}+1
onward, the policy switches to the data-driven policy
\pi_{r}(X\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1})
below, which sequentially approximates the A-optimal allocation of Proposition
4.3
. Concretely, we obtain a direct estimate
\widetilde{\pi}_{r}(X\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1})
by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\pi}_{r}(X\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{r-1}):=\frac{\sqrt{\hat{V}_{1,r-1}(X)}}{\sqrt{\hat{V}_{1,r-1}(X)}+\sqrt{\hat{V}_{0,r-1}(X)}},

(9)

where
V_{a,r-1}(X)
is the trace of the conditional covariance matrix based on data in
{\mathcal{H}}_{r-1}
. We then apply a truncation step to
\widetilde{\pi}_{r}(X\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{r-1})
to obtain the assignment policy
\pi_{r}(X\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{r-1})
at round
r
.

Closed-form
V_{a,r-1}(X)
estimation:
We now present the closed-form estimation of
V_{a,r-1}(X)
by combining Theorem
4.1
with A-optimal policy:

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{a,r-1}(X)=\sum_{t=0}^{t_{\max}}\hat{S}_{t,r-1}(x,a)^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{t}\frac{\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{i,r-1}(x,a)}{\hat{S}_{i,r-1}(x,a)\,\hat{G}_{i-1,r-1}(x,a)},

(10)

where
\hat{S}_{i,r-1}(x,a)
and
\hat{G}_{i-1,r-1}(x,a)
are the survival- and censoring-hazard nuisances already required by the EIF in Eq.
4
, but now estimated sequentially from the accumulated history
\mathcal{H}_{r-1}
. Specifically,
\widetilde{\pi}_{r}(x)
is a deterministic function of
\eta_{t,r-1}(X)
alone.

Remark 5.1
.

These conclusions highlight the difference to existing work
(
44
)
, which typically requires a second-stage regression to estimate the variance target governing the optimal allocation. In contrast, Eq.

expresses
\hat{V}_{a,r-1}(x)
as a closed-form function of the hazard nuisances
\hat{\eta}_{\,r-1}
, which are already used inside the EIF construction at round

. This yields two practical advantages: (i) no auxiliary second-stage learner for the variance is needed; (ii) the non-negativity of
\hat{V}_{a,r-1}
holds by construction.

Extreme censoring or small within-stratum sample sizes can drive

toward zero, thus causing the direct estimates of policy

to collapse to the boundary of

(
10
;
13
;
44
)
, we clip
\widetilde{\pi}_{r}
to a shrinking interval, defining the final assignment policy as

\displaystyle\pi_{r}(X\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{r-1}):=\min\bigg\{1-\frac{1}{k_{r}},\max\big\{\frac{1}{k_{r}},\widetilde{\pi}_{r}(X\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{r-1})\big\}\bigg\},

(11)

where
k_{r}\in[2,\infty)
is a non-decreasing sequence with
k_{r}\rightarrow\infty
as
r\rightarrow\infty
. This keeps all assignment probabilities strictly interior, controls the growth of the censoring-weighted scores in the EIF, and is necessary for the theoretical guarantees in Section
6
.

5.2
Sequentially cross-fitted adaptive ATE estimation

Why a custom cross-fitting approach is needed:
The reason is the sequential dependence: at round
r
, the estimated nuisances
\hat{\eta}_{t,r-1}
are functions of the same accumulated history
\mathcal{H}_{r-1}
. The standard central limit theorems (CLTs) therefore do
not
apply. Our approach resolves this via a near-martingale decomposition: we isolate an oracle term that satisfies a martingale CLT and a nuisance remainder whose bias vanishes at rate
o_{p}(R^{-1/2})
, which is guaranteed once each hazard estimator achieves the
o_{p}(r^{-1/4})
rate imposed in Theorem
6.3
.

Estimation via sequential cross-fitting:
To mitigate finite-sample bias and ensure the nuisance remainder is of order
o_{p}(R^{-1/2})
, we apply the sequential cross-fitting scheme of
60
with
K=2
folds. For each unit
r\in\mathbb{N}
, define the fold index
J_{r}:=r\bmod 2\in\{0,1\}
, and split
{\mathcal{H}}_{r-1}
into two temporal folds, i.e.,
{\mathcal{H}}_{r-1}={\mathcal{H}}_{r-1}^{(0)}\cup{\mathcal{H}}_{r-1}^{(1)}
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{r-1}^{(j)}:=\bigl\{(X_{\tilde{r}},A_{\tilde{r}},\widetilde{T}_{\tilde{r}},\Delta_{\tilde{r}}):{\tilde{r}}\in[1,2,...,r-1],\ J_{\tilde{r}}=j\bigr\},\qquad j\in\{0,1\}.

(12)

Given the cross-fitted nuisances
\hat{\eta}_{t,r-1}^{(-J_{r})}
and the adaptive policy
\pi_{r}(X\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{r-1})
, we define the cross-fitted EIF pseudo-outcome at horizon
t
by

\hat{\phi}_{t,r}^{\mathrm{cf}}:=\hat{S}_{t,r-1}^{(-J_{r})}(X_{r},1)-\hat{S}_{t,r-1}^{(-J_{r})}(X_{r},0)-\frac{\{A_{r}-\pi_{r}(X_{r})\}\hat{\xi}\bigl({\mathcal{H}}_{r},\hat{\eta}_{t,r-1}^{(-J_{r})}\bigr)}{\pi_{r}(X_{r})\{1-\pi_{r}(X_{r})\}}\hat{S}_{t,r-1}^{(-J_{r})}(X_{r},A_{r}),

(13)

where
\hat{\xi}({\mathcal{H}}_{r},\hat{\eta}_{t,r-1}^{(-J_{r})})
denotes the sequentially estimated version of
\xi({\mathcal{H}}_{r},\eta_{t})
in Eq.
4
, with all nuisance components replaced by
\hat{\eta}_{t,r-1}^{(-J_{r})}
. We now yield our ASE: our aim is to sequentially estimate the ATE from the non-centered efficient influence function in Eq.
4
with sequentially updated nuisance functions. In ASE, the estimator at horizon
t
is defined as

\hat{\tau}^{\mathrm{ASE}}_{t,R}=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\phi}^{\mathrm{cf}}({\mathcal{H}}_{r};\pi_{r},\hat{\eta}_{t,r}^{\mathrm{cf}}),

(14)

where
\hat{\eta}_{t,r-1}^{\mathrm{cf}}=\{\hat{\lambda}^{S,\mathrm{cf}}_{i,r-1}(X,0),\hat{\lambda}^{S,\mathrm{cf}}_{i,r-1}(X,1),\hat{\lambda}^{G,\mathrm{cf}}_{i,r-1}(X,0),\hat{\lambda}^{G,\mathrm{cf}}_{i,r-1}(X,1)\}_{i=0}^{t}
denotes the estimates of the nuisance functions at round
r
with sequential cross-fitting, which are constructed solely from the past data
{\mathcal{H}}_{r-1}
. The treatment assignment policy
\pi_{r}(X\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{r-1})
, defined by the experimenter based on the estimated optimal policy from Section
5.1
, is treated as known and does not require further estimation from data. The full procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

6
Theoretical guarantees

This section provides several theoretical guarantees for the ASE. We establish asymptotic normality and an A-optimal semiparametric efficiency bound in Section
6.1
, and robustness of the ASE under partial nuisance misspecification in Section
6.2
. Both results extend naturally to batch settings where policy updates occur at the end of each batch rather than every round, since the near-martingale decomposition depends only on
L_{2}
-consistency of the nuisance estimates and
not
on the update frequency. In Appendix
H
, we additionally derive asymptotically-valid time-uniform confidence sequences for the ASE, thereby enabling valid inference at arbitrary data-dependent stopping times.

6.1
Asymptotic normality of the ASE

To derive our asymptotic guarantees
7
7

7

Throughout, estimators use sequential cross-fitting of Section
5.2
; we suppress the superscript
cf
for brevity.
, we impose the following uniform overlap assumption on the survival and censoring hazards. This condition is common in semiparametric survival analysis
(
11
;
56
)
and ensures that the inverse probability of censoring weights in the Eq.
4
remain bounded.

Assumption 6.1

(Uniform overlap up to horizon
t
)
.

For each fixed
t\in\{0,\dots,t_{\max}\}
, there exist constants

\underline{c}^{S}_{t}
,
\underline{c}^{G}_{t}>0
such that, for all
a\in\{0,1\}
, all
x
, and all
i\leq t
, we have
1-\lambda^{S}_{i}(x,a)\geq\underline{c}^{S}_{t}
and
1-\lambda^{G}_{i}(x,a)/(1-\lambda^{S}_{i}(x,a))\geq\underline{c}^{G}_{t}
.

Remark 6.2

(Necessity of stepwise overlap)
.

Assumption
6.1
imposes bounds at every intermediate step
i\leq t
rather than only at the endpoint, since
S_{t}
and
G_{t-1}
are cumulative products: a single step with
\lambda^{S}_{i}\to 1
drives the
\xi({\mathcal{H}}_{r};\eta)
in the EIF to become unbounded. Hence, this constitutes the discrete-time survival analogue of the positivity condition standard in semiparametric survival analysis
(
11
;
56
;
61
)
. See Appendix
F.3
for details.

Theorem 6.3

(Asymptotic normality of the adaptive survival estimator)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
, and Assumption
6.1
hold, and let
k_{r}\in[2,\infty)
be a non-decreasing truncation sequence satisfying
k_{r}\to\infty
and
k_{r}=o(r^{1/4})
as
r\to\infty
. Suppose there exists a non-adaptive policy
\pi(X)\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
for some
\varepsilon>0
such that the adaptive assignment policy
\pi_{r}(X\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{r-1})
are
L_{2}
-consistent relative to truncation schedule, i.e.,
k_{r}\|\pi_{r}-\pi\|_{2}=o_{p}(1)
. Furthermore, assume that, for each fixed
t
and treatment
a
, we have
\max_{0\leq i\leq{t_{\mathrm{max}}}}\|\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{S}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}=o_{p}(r^{-1/4})
and
\max_{0\leq i\leq{t_{\mathrm{max}}}-1}\|\hat{\lambda}^{G}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{G}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}=o_{p}(r^{-1/4})
. Then, the adaptive estimator is asymptotically normal:

\displaystyle\sqrt{R}\left(\hat{\tau}^{\mathrm{ASE}}_{t,R}-\tau_{t}\right)\rightarrow^{d}\mathcal{N}(0,V_{\mathrm{eff},t}(\pi)),

(15)

where
V_{\mathrm{eff},t}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{v_{t,1}(X)}{\pi(X)}+\frac{v_{t,0}(X)}{1-\pi(X)}\right]+b_{t}
denotes the semiparametric efficiency bound for
\tau_{t}
under policy
\pi
and
b_{t}=\mathbb{E}[(S_{t}(X,1)-S_{t}(X,0)-\tau_{t})^{2}]
. In particular, if we have
\pi=\pi^{\star}
, then
\hat{\tau}^{\mathrm{ASE}}_{t,R}
attains the A-optimal semiparametric efficiency bound.

Proof.

See Appendix
F.4
.
∎

The proof decomposes
\sqrt{R}(\hat{\tau}_{t}^{\mathrm{ASE}}-\tau_{t})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}z_{t,r}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}m_{t,r}
into an oracle term and a nuisance remainder. The oracle term
z_{t,r}=\phi_{t,r}-\tau_{t}
forms a martingale difference sequence with respect to
\mathcal{H}_{r-1}
, since the adaptive policy
\pi_{r}
is determined by
\mathcal{H}_{r-1}
and the inverse censoring augmentation satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\xi({\mathcal{H}}_{r},\eta_{t})\mid A_{r},X_{r},\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]=0
. The martingale central limit theorem (CLT) then yields asymptotic normality. The remainder
m_{t,r}=\hat{\phi}_{t,r}-\phi_{t,r}
is controlled via the cause-specific structure of
G_{t-1}(X,a)
: the bias term vanishes at rate
o_{p}(r^{-1/2})
under
o_{p}(r^{-1/4})
hazard consistency, and the martingale fluctuation term is negligible by
L_{2}
-boundedness of the cross-fitted scores, thus requiring no Donsker conditions.

Importantly, the truncation does
not
interfere with the optimal allocation in the limit. The reason is that, since
\pi^{\star}(X)\in[\alpha,1-\alpha]
and
1/k_{r}\to 0
, the clipping interval eventually contains
\pi^{\star}(X)
.

Proposition 6.4

(Policy convergence under hazard consistency)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
, and
6.1
hold, and that the hazard estimators satisfy the rate conditions of Theorem
6.3
. Assume further that
\pi^{\star}(X)\in[\alpha,1-\alpha]
almost surely for some
\alpha>0
. Then, the direct variance estimates satisfy
\|\hat{V}_{a,r-1}-V_{a}\|_{2}=o_{p}(r^{-1/4})
for each
a\in\{0,1\}
. Consequently, for any non-decreasing truncation sequence
k_{r}=o(r^{1/4})
, we have
k_{r}\|\pi_{r}-\pi^{\star}\|_{2}=o_{p}(1)
.

Proof.

See Appendix
F.5
.
∎

6.2
Consistency guarantees under partial nuisance misspecification

As shown in Theorem
6.3
, the convergence rate of ASE is primarily governed by the estimation error of
\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{t}(X,\cdot)
and
\hat{\lambda}^{G}_{t}(X,\cdot)
. This reflects a
robustness
property formalized in the following corollary: misspecification of the censoring hazard
\hat{\lambda}^{G}_{t}(X,\cdot)
alone does
not
affect consistency, provided the event hazard
\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{t}(X,\cdot)
is estimated consistently.

Corollary 6.5

(Robustness to the censoring hazard)
.

Suppose that the event hazard estimators are consistent, i.e.,
\|\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{t,r-1}-\lambda^{S}_{t}\|_{2}=o_{p}(1)
for all
t
, and that the censoring hazard estimators satisfy
\|\hat{\lambda}^{G}_{t,r-1}-\lambda^{G}_{t}\|_{2}=o_{p}(1)
for all
t
. Then, for each
t\leq t_{\max}
,

\displaystyle|\hat{\tau}_{t,R}^{\mathrm{ASE}}-\tau_{t}|=O_{p}(R^{-1/2})

(16)

\displaystyle+O_{p}\!\left(\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{S}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\Bigr)^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{S}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\|\hat{\lambda}^{G}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{G}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right).\vskip-11.38092pt

In particular, there is no standalone first-order term
O_{p}(\|\hat{\lambda}^{G}_{t}-\lambda^{G}_{t}\|_{2})
; any perturbation in
\lambda^{G}
enters only through its interaction with the event nuisance, reflecting the structure of misspecification of censoring hazard.

Proof.

See Appendix
F.6
.
∎

For the no-ties case, we can even provide a double-robustness result (see Appendix
G
, where
\Delta=\mathbf{1}(T<C)
excludes simultaneous event and censoring times. In this setting, the censoring survival function simplifies to
G_{t-1}(x,a)=\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}(1-\lambda^{G}_{i}(x,a))
, which is independent of the event hazard
\lambda^{S}
. As a result, the ASE is doubly-robust (i.e., to misspecification of both
\lambda^{S}
and
\lambda^{G}
), and the remainder vanishes whenever either nuisance is consistently estimated, regardless of the other.

We estimate the nuisance functions using LightGBM, trained with standard gradient-boosted tree objectives for classification (see Appendix

Synthetic data:
We follow the common practice in adaptive experimentation

)
and evaluate the effectiveness of our framework on both synthetic and semi-synthetic datasets to benchmark against ground-truth results. We provide the estimated average survival effect curves for synthetic and semi-synthetic data in Appendix

. In our experiments, we aim to evaluate three research questions:

a

How much do we benefit from adaptive allocation?

\Rightarrow

Effectiveness of A-optimal treatment allocation
: For this, we report the relative MSE with respect to the Oracle estimator in Fig.
3
(a). Our ASE achieves a relative MSE of approximately
1.05\times
of the oracle at

, which nearly matches oracle performance despite using estimated nuisances. In contrast, the non-adaptive baselines (ASE-NA and plug-in-NA) stabilize at

b

Does ASE improve estimation accuracy over rounds?

\Rightarrow

Estimation accuracy
: We thus plot the MSE against
R
in Fig.
3
(b). First, ASE, ASE-MS, plug-in, and a2ipw-NAïve converge to the true
\tau
. Out of them, ASE achieves the lowest error and thus performs best. The results further highlight the importance of our robustness property: ASE-MS remains consistent and even achieves a similar speed of convergence as ASE, despite one nuisance component being misspecified.

c

Is ASE inferentially valid?

\Rightarrow

Inferential validity
: We here report empirical coverage of nominal 95% confidence intervals (CIs) in Fig.
3
(c). Consistent with our theoretical guarantee, our ASE and ASE-NA achieve nominal coverage. In contrast, the other methods fail to do so. For example, the plug-in, plug-in-NA, and a2ipw-NAïve perform poorly as

Semi-synthetic data:
We also evaluate ASE on a semi-synthetic dataset based on the Twins dataset as in
17
;
37
to demonstrate the applicability to medical data. The results are in Appendix
J.1
.

Results:
Our findings are consistent with the synthetic setting: (i) adaptive treatment allocation policy improves estimation efficiency, (ii) ASE achieves superior coverage and consistency, and (iii) robustness holds under partial nuisance misspecification.

Conclusion:
Our work highlights the potential broader impact of adaptive experimentation to improve clinical trials with survival data, especially in settings where efficient evidence generation is critical.
Limitations:
Future work could be extended to multi-arm trials and demonstrate its practical value in real clinical settings with limited and heterogeneous patient populations.

Appendix D
Additional theoretical results for optimal data acquisition

In the main text, we focus on
A-optimal
allocation, i.e., minimizing
\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi))
, which targets small marginal variances across horizons and admits the closed-form solution in Proposition
4.3
. Alternative classical criteria from optimal experimental design are
D-optimality
and
E-optimality
, which target the joint uncertainty of the full survival effect curve vector
\tau=(\tau_{0},\ldots,\tau_{t_{\max}})^{\top}

[
1
]
, or control the worst-case linear combination of survival effect curve contrasts. We extend our derivations to D-optimality and E-optimality below.

D.1
D-optimality

Definition D.1

(D-optimal allocation policy)
.

A feasible allocation policy
\pi^{D}
is
D-optimal
if

\displaystyle\pi^{D}\in\arg\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}}\det(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi))\quad\text{equivalently}\quad\pi^{D}\in\arg\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}}\log\det(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi)),

(24)

where
\Pi_{\alpha}=\{\pi:\alpha\leq\pi(x)\leq 1-\alpha\text{ for all }x\}
.

Interpretation:
The confidence ellipsoid for
\tau
has volume proportional to
\det(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi))^{1/2}
(up to constants independent of
\pi
). Hence, D-optimal allocation tends to yield small
joint
uncertainty across all time horizons simultaneously, which is desirable when the full survival effect curve is the object of interest rather than any individual
\tau_{t}
.

Theorem D.2

(D-optimal allocation policy)
.

Assume
\Pi_{\alpha}\neq\emptyset
and that Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
hold. Then, any D-optimal policy
\pi^{D}
satisfies, for a.e.
x
,

\displaystyle\pi^{D}(x)=\mathrm{clip}_{\alpha}\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi^{D})^{-1}\Sigma_{1}(x)\right)}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi^{D})^{-1}\Sigma_{1}(x)\right)}+\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi^{D})^{-1}\Sigma_{0}(x)\right)}}\right).

(25)

The characterization is a fixed-point equation since
\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi^{D})
depends on
\pi^{D}
.

Remark D.3
.

The form of
\pi^{D}
parallels the A-optimal rule in Proposition
4.3
, but with
V_{a}(X)=\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{a}(X))
replaced by
\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi^{D})^{-1}\Sigma_{a}(x))
. This difference has a clean calculus origin: differentiating
\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi))
w.r.t.
\pi(x)
yields
\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{a}(x))
, whereas differentiating
\log\det(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi))
via the identity
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}A}\log\det(A)=\mathrm{tr}(A^{-1}dA)
yields
\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}\Sigma_{a}(x))
. The
\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}
weighting introduces a global dependence on the full covariance structure: directions already well-estimated are upweighted, redirecting allocation toward reducing joint uncertainty across horizons. This is precisely what prevents
\pi^{D}
from admitting a closed-form expression, since
\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi^{D})
depends on
\pi^{D}
itself, making the characterization a fixed-point equation.

Computation via fixed-point iteration:
Unlike the A-optimal rule in Proposition
4.3
, the D-optimal policy depends on
\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi^{D})^{-1}
and has therefore no closed-form solution. A simple solver is a fixed-point iteration:

1.

Initialize
\pi^{(0)}
(e.g., the A-optimal policy from Proposition
4.3
).

2.

For
k=0,1,2,\ldots
:

(a) Estimate
\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi^{(k)})
using the accumulated data
\mathcal{H}_{r-1}
or the population formula if available.

(b) Update

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}^{(k+1)}(x)=\mathrm{clip}_{\alpha}\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi^{(k)})^{-1}\Sigma_{1}(x)\right)}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi^{(k)})^{-1}\Sigma_{1}(x)\right)}+\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi^{(k)})^{-1}\Sigma_{0}(x)\right)}}\right).

(26)

(c) Set
\pi^{(k+1)}\leftarrow\tilde{\pi}^{(k+1)}
and stop when the iterates stabilize.

D.2
E-optimality

E-optimality minimizes the largest eigenvalue of
\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi)
and thereby controls the worst-case variance over all unit-norm linear contrasts
v^{\top}\tau
.

Definition D.4

(E-optimal allocation policy)
.

A feasible allocation policy
\pi^{E}
is
E-optimal
if

\displaystyle\pi^{E}\in\arg\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}}\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi)),

(27)

where
\lambda_{\max}(\cdot)
denotes the largest eigenvalue.

Interpretation:
By the variational characterization
\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi))=\max_{\|v\|=1}v^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi)v
, the E-optimal design minimizes the variance of the worst-case linear combination of survival effect curve contrasts
v^{\top}\tau
. This is the natural criterion when the experimenter lacks a prior preference over individual horizons
\tau_{t}
and wishes to guard against the worst-case linear contrast.

Theorem D.5

(E-optimal allocation policy)
.

Under the same conditions as Theorem
D.2
, any E-optimal policy
\pi^{E}
satisfies, for a.e.
x
,

\displaystyle\pi^{E}(x)=\mathrm{clip}_{\alpha}\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{(v^{\star})^{\top}\Sigma_{1}(x)\,v^{\star}}}{\sqrt{(v^{\star})^{\top}\Sigma_{1}(x)\,v^{\star}}+\sqrt{(v^{\star})^{\top}\Sigma_{0}(x)\,v^{\star}}}\right),

(28)

where
v^{\star}=v^{\star}(\pi^{E})\in\mathbb{R}^{t_{\max}+1}
is the unit eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi^{E}))
. The characterization is a fixed-point equation since
v^{\star}
depends on
\pi^{E}
through
\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi^{E})
.

By the envelope theorem, at an interior optimum the first-order condition w.r.t.
\pi(x)
yields
(v^{\star})^{\top}[\partial\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi)/\partial\pi(x)]v^{\star}=0
, which gives
-(v^{\star})^{\top}\Sigma_{1}(x)v^{\star}/\pi(x)^{2}+(v^{\star})^{\top}\Sigma_{0}(x)v^{\star}/(1-\pi(x))^{2}=0
. Solving for
\pi(x)
and applying the overlap constraint yields the stated expression.

Remark D.6
.

The quantity
(v^{\star})^{\top}\Sigma_{a}(x)v^{\star}=\mathrm{Var}\bigl(\sum_{t}v^{\star}_{t}S_{t}(X,a)\,\xi_{t}(\mathcal{O},\eta_{t})\mid X=x,A=a\bigr)
is the conditional variance of the worst-case linear contrast
v^{\star\top}\phi(\mathcal{O};\tau,\eta)
in arm
a
at covariate value
x
. Thus, the E-optimal policy has the same Neyman form as the A- and D-optimal policies, but with the scalar
V_{a}(x)
replaced by the worst-case directional variance
(v^{\star})^{\top}\Sigma_{a}(x)v^{\star}
.

Computation via fixed-point iteration:
The E-optimal policy can be computed analogously to the D-optimal case:

1.

Initialize
\pi^{(0)}
(e.g., the A-optimal policy from Proposition
4.3
).

2.

For
k=0,1,2,\ldots
:

(a) Estimate
\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi^{(k)})
from accumulated data
\mathcal{H}_{r-1}
.

(b) Compute the leading eigenvector
v^{(k)}=\arg\max_{\|v\|=1}v^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi^{(k)})v
.

(c) Update

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}^{(k+1)}(x)=\mathrm{clip}_{\alpha}\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{(v^{(k)})^{\top}\Sigma_{1}(x)\,v^{(k)}}}{\sqrt{(v^{(k)})^{\top}\Sigma_{1}(x)\,v^{(k)}}+\sqrt{(v^{(k)})^{\top}\Sigma_{0}(x)\,v^{(k)}}}\right).

(29)

(d) Set
\pi^{(k+1)}\leftarrow\tilde{\pi}^{(k+1)}
and stop when the iterates stabilize.

D.3
Comparison of A-, D-, and E-optimality

All three criteria share the same Neyman allocation structure
\pi^{\star}(x)=\sqrt{Q_{1}(x)}/(\sqrt{Q_{1}(x)}+\sqrt{Q_{0}(x)})
for an arm-specific scalar
Q_{a}(x)
, but differ in how they aggregate the cross-horizon covariance matrix
\Sigma_{a}(x)
:

1.

A-optimality uses
Q_{a}(x)=\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{a}(x))=V_{a}(x)
(sum of marginal variances, closed-form);

2.

D-optimality uses
Q_{a}(x)=\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}\Sigma_{a}(x))
(precision-weighted trace, fixed-point);

3.

E-optimality uses
Q_{a}(x)=(v^{\star})^{\top}\Sigma_{a}(x)v^{\star}
(worst-case directional variance, fixed-point).

When
t_{\max}=0
,
v^{\star}=1
and, therefore, all three criteria coincide. The A-optimal criterion is the only one that separates additively over
t
, which is why it yields a closed-form solution; D- and E-optimal criteria couple all horizons jointly through
\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}
and its eigenvector and therefore require iterative computation.

which yields
p=\sqrt{V_{1}(x)}/(\sqrt{V_{1}(x)}+\sqrt{V_{0}(x)})
. Applying the
overlap constraint, we yield

\displaystyle\pi^{\star}(x)=\mathrm{clip}_{\alpha}\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{V_{1}(x)}}{\sqrt{V_{1}(x)}+\sqrt{V_{0}(x)}}\right).

(42)

F.3
Remark on necessity of stepwise overlap

Remark F.1

(Necessity of stepwise overlap)
.

Assumption
6.1
imposes lower bounds on
1-\lambda^{S}_{i}(x,a)
and
1-\lambda^{G}_{i}(x,a)/(1-\lambda^{S}_{i}(x,a))
at every intermediate step
i\leq t
, rather than only at the endpoint
t
. This is necessary: since
G_{t-1}(x,a)=\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\bigl(1-\lambda^{G}_{i}(x,a)/(1-\lambda^{S}_{i}(x,a))\bigr)
and
S_{i}(x,a)=\prod_{j=0}^{i}(1-\lambda^{S}_{j}(x,a))
are both cumulative products, a single intermediate step at which
\lambda^{S}_{i}(x,a)\to 1
drives
S_{i}(x,a)\to 0
and hence
G_{i-1}(x,a)^{-1}\to\infty
, which the inverse probability of censoring weights in the EIF to become unbounded regardless of the behavior at other time steps. Assumption
6.1
rules this out uniformly across
i\leq t
and thus ensures that
S_{i}(x,a)\geq(c^{S}_{t})^{t+1}
and
G_{i-1}(x,a)\geq(c^{G}_{t})^{t+1}
for all
i\leq t
. Hence, this constitutes the discrete-time survival analogue of the positivity condition standard in semiparametric survival analysis
[
11
,
56
,
61
]
.

F.4
Proof of Theorem
6.3

The asymptotic argument relies on the martingale central limit theorem under a Lindeberg-type condition. We use a streamlined version of the martingale difference sequence (MDS) central limit theorem originally due to
16
, as presented in Theorem 2 of
66
.

To begin our proof, we define the centered EIF score at horizon
t
as

\psi_{t,r}:=\phi(X_{r},A_{r},\tilde{T}_{r},\Delta_{r};\tau_{t},\pi_{r},\hat{\eta}_{t,r})
, where
\phi
is given in Eq.
4
. Let

\eta_{t}=\{\lambda^{S}_{i}(X,0),\lambda^{S}_{i}(X,1),\lambda^{G}_{i}(X,0),\lambda^{G}_{i}(X,1)\}_{i=0}^{t}
denote the oracle nuisance functions. We can decompose
\psi_{t,r}
as

\displaystyle\psi_{t,r}=

\displaystyle\phi_{t,r}-\tau_{t}+\hat{\phi}_{t,r}-\phi_{t,r}

(43)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\phi(X_{r},A_{r},\tilde{T}_{r},\Delta_{r};\tau_{t},\pi_{r},\eta_{t})-\tau_{t}}_{z_{t,r}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\phi(X_{r},A_{r},\tilde{T}_{r},\Delta_{r};\tau_{t},\pi_{r},\hat{\eta}_{t,r})-\phi(X_{r},A_{r},\tilde{T}_{r},\Delta_{r};\tau_{t},\pi_{r},\eta_{t})}_{m_{t,r}},

(44)

such that

\displaystyle\sqrt{R}\bigl(\hat{\tau}_{t,R}^{\mathrm{ASE}}-\tau_{t}\bigr)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}z_{t,r}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}m_{t,r}.

(45)

Our proof proceeds in three steps: (1) we prove that, for each horizon
t
,
\{z_{t,r}\}_{r=1}^{R}
forms a martingale difference sequence, (2) we show that
\{z_{t,r}\}_{r=1}^{R}
satisfy conditional variance convergence and Lindeberg condition, so we have the
\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}z_{t,r}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,V_{\textrm{eff},t}(\pi))
, and (3) we show that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}m_{t,r}=o_{p}(1)
.

F.4.1
Martingale difference sequence

Before we start to the first steps, we state the martingale central limit theorem first.

Theorem F.2

(Martingale CLT, adapted from Theorem 2 in
66
)
.

Let
\{z_{t,r},\mathcal{H}_{r}\}_{r=1}^{R}
be a real-valued sequence, and let
\bar{z}_{t,r}=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}z_{t,r}
such that:

1.

(Martingale difference sequence)
\{z_{t,r}\}_{r=1}^{R}
is a martingale difference sequence; that is
\mathbb{E}[z_{t,r}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]=0
for all
r\in[1,R]
.

2.

(Conditional variance convergence) There exists a constant

V_{\textrm{eff},t}(\pi)>0
such that

\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}[z_{t,r}^{2}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]\xrightarrow{p}V_{\textrm{eff},t}(\pi),

(46)

3.

(Lindeberg condition) For every
\varepsilon>0
,

\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}\left[z_{t,r}^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{|z_{t,r}|>\varepsilon\sqrt{R}\}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right]\xrightarrow{p}0.

(47)

Then,
\sqrt{R}\bar{z}_{t,r}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,V_{\textrm{eff},t}(\pi))
.

The first step is that we need to prove
\mathbb{E}[z_{t,r}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]=0
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[z_{t,r}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S_{t}(X_{r},1)-S_{t}(X_{r},0)-\frac{A_{r}-\pi_{r}(X_{r})}{\pi_{r}(X_{r})\{1-\pi_{r}(X_{r})\}}\xi({\mathcal{H}}_{r},\eta_{t})S_{t}(X_{r},A_{r})\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]-\tau_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau_{t}-\tau_{t}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{A_{r}-\pi_{r}(X_{r})}{\pi_{r}(X_{r})\{1-\pi_{r}(X_{r})\}}\xi({\mathcal{H}}_{r},\eta)S_{t}(X_{r},A_{r})\mid X_{r},\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right]\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{A_{r}-\pi_{r}(X_{r})}{\pi_{r}(X_{r})\{1-\pi_{r}(X_{r})\}}\xi({\mathcal{H}}_{r},\eta)S_{t}(X_{r},A_{r})\mid A_{r},X_{r},\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right]\mid X_{r},\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right]\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(48)

We further yield

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\xi({\mathcal{H}}_{r},\eta_{t})\mid A_{r},X_{r},\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right]

(49)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{t}\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\tilde{T}=i)\mathbbm{1}(\Delta=1)-\mathbbm{1}(\tilde{T}\geq i)\lambda^{s}_{i}(X_{r},A_{r})}{S_{i}(X_{r},A_{r})G_{i-1}(X_{r},A_{r})}\mid A_{r},X_{r},\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right]

(50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\tilde{T}=i)\mathbbm{1}(\Delta=1)-\mathbbm{1}(\tilde{T}\geq i)\lambda^{s}_{i}(X_{r},A_{r})}{S_{i}(X_{r},A_{r})G_{i-1}(X_{r},A_{r})}\mid A_{r},X_{r},\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right]

(51)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{t}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{T}=i,\Delta=1\mid X_{r},A_{r},\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{T}\geq i\mid X_{r},A_{r},\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{T}=i,\Delta=1\mid\tilde{T}>i,X_{r},A_{r}\right)}{S_{i}(X_{r},A_{r})G_{i-1}(X_{r},A_{r})}

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{t}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{T}=i,\Delta=1\mid X_{r},A_{r},\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{T}=i,\Delta=1\mid X_{r},A_{r},\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right)}{S_{i}(X_{r},A_{r})G_{i-1}(X_{r},A_{r})}

(53)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(54)

Thus, for each fixed
t
,
\{z_{t,r},\mathcal{H}_{r}\}_{r\geq 1}
is a martingale difference sequence, since the assignment probability
\pi_{r}
is
\mathcal{H}_{r-1}
-measurable and the oracle augmentation term satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\xi({\mathcal{H}}_{r},\eta_{t})\mid A_{r},X_{r},\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]=0
.

F.4.2
Conditional variance convergence and Lindeberg condition

We first need to show that
\mathbb{E}[z_{t,r}^{2}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]-V_{\mathrm{eff},t}(\pi)\xrightarrow{p}0
. Let
b_{t}(X):=S_{t}(X,1)-S_{t}(X,0)-\tau_{t}
. Using
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{t}({\mathcal{H}}_{r},\eta_{t})\mid X_{r},A_{r},\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]=0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[z_{t,r}^{2}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[b_{t}(X_{r})^{2}+\frac{v_{t,1}(X_{r})}{\pi_{r}(X_{r})}+\frac{v_{t,0}(X_{r})}{1-\pi_{r}(X_{r})}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right].

(55)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}[z_{t,r}^{2}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]-V_{\mathrm{eff},t}(\pi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[v_{t,1}(X_{r})\left|\frac{1}{\pi_{r}(X_{r})}-\frac{1}{\pi(X_{r})}\right|+v_{t,0}(X_{r})\left|\frac{1}{1-\pi_{r}(X_{r})}-\frac{1}{1-\pi(X_{r})}\right|\;\middle|\;\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\frac{k_{r}}{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{t+1}{\underline{s}_{t}\,\underline{g}_{t}}\right)^{2}\|\pi_{r}-\pi\|_{2}=o_{p}(1),

(56)

According to Assumption
6.1
, we have
1-\lambda^{S}_{i}(x,a)\geq\underline{c}^{S}_{t}

1-\lambda^{G}_{i}(x,a)/(1-\lambda^{S}_{i}(x,a))\geq\underline{c}^{G}_{t}
, so we have
S_{i}(x,a)=\prod_{j=0}^{i}1-\lambda^{S}_{j}(x,a)\geq(\underline{c}^{S}_{t})^{i+1}\geq(\underline{c}^{S}_{t})^{t+1}=\underline{s}_{t}
and
G_{i-1}(x,a)=\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}1-\lambda^{G}_{j}(x,a)/(1-\lambda^{S}_{j}(x,a))\geq(\underline{c}^{G}_{t})^{i}\geq(\underline{c}^{G}_{t})^{t+1}=\underline{g}_{t}
. We note
\big|\sum_{i=0}^{t}\frac{S_{i}(X,a)}{S_{i}(X,a)G_{i-1}(X,a)}(\mathbf{1}(\widetilde{T}=i,\Delta=1)-\mathbf{1}(\widetilde{T}\geq i)\,\lambda_{i}^{S}(X,A))\big|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{t}\frac{S_{i}(X,a)}{S_{i}(X,a)G_{i-1}(X,a)}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{t}\frac{1}{\underline{s}_{t}\underline{g}_{t}}
. Therefore,
v_{t,a}(X)=\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{t}\mid X,A=a)\leq\mathbb{E}[\phi_{t}^{2}\mid X,A=a]\leq\left(\frac{t+1}{\underline{s}_{t}\,\underline{g}_{t}}\right)^{2}
where the last line we use (i) the boundedness assumption from Assumption
6.1
and thus
\|v_{t,1}(X)\|_{\infty}\leq\left(\frac{t+1}{\underline{s}_{t}\,\underline{g}_{t}}\right)^{2}
, (ii)
\pi(X_{r})
,
1-\pi(X_{r})>\varepsilon
from Theorem
6.3
statement, (iii)
\pi(X_{r})
,
1-\pi(X_{r})>1/k_{r}
by construction, and the
L_{1}
-norm is bounded by the
L_{2}
norm. Thus, setting
\sigma^{2}:=V_{\textrm{eff},t}(\pi)
, we have that the term converges in probability to
\sigma^{2}
, i.e.,
E[z_{r}^{2}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]\xrightarrow{p}\sigma^{2}
, where
\sigma^{2}
is finite. And we further have that
\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}[z_{r}^{2}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]\xrightarrow{p}\sigma^{2}
.

Now, we prove the
Lindeberg condition
. For every
\varepsilon>0
, by the truncation rule,
1/k_{r}\leq\pi_{r}(X_{r})\leq 1-1/k_{r}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{A_{r}-\pi_{r}(X_{r})}{\pi_{r}(X_{r})\{1-\pi_{r}(X_{r})\}}\right|\leq k_{r}.

(57)

Moreover, by Assumption
6.1
, the survival and censoring weights in the oracle EIF are uniformly bounded for each fixed
t
. Therefore, there exists a constant
C_{t}<\infty
such that
|z_{t,r}|\leq C_{t}k_{r}
. Since
k_{r}=o(r^{1/4})
, we also have
k_{r}=o(\sqrt{r})
. If
k_{r}
is nondecreasing, then

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq r\leq R}|z_{t,r}|\leq C_{t}k_{R}=o(\sqrt{R}).

(58)

Thus, for every
\varepsilon>0
, eventually
|z_{t,r}|\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{R}
for all
r\leq R
, and hence
\mathbbm{1}\{|z_{t,r}|>\varepsilon\sqrt{R}\}=0
for all
r\leq R
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}\left[z_{t,r}^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{|z_{t,r}|>\varepsilon\sqrt{R}\}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right]=0,

(59)

which proves the Lindeberg condition.
Thus, by Theorem
F.2
we proved that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}z_{t,r}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,V_{\textrm{eff},t}(\pi))
.

F.4.3
The remainder term

For a fixed horizon
t
, recall that

m_{t,r}:=\phi_{t}({\mathcal{H}}_{r};\pi_{r},\hat{\eta}_{t,r-1})-\phi_{t}({\mathcal{H}}_{r};\pi_{r},\eta_{t}).

(60)

We aim to show

\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}m_{t,r}=o_{p}(1).

(61)

We decompose

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}m_{t,r}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{R}\left(\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}[m_{t,r}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\Bigl(m_{t,r}-\mathbb{E}[m_{t,r}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]\Bigr)

\displaystyle:=\Delta_{t}^{A}+\Delta_{t}^{B}.

(62)

We show separately that
\Delta_{t}^{A}=o_{p}(1)
and
\Delta_{t}^{B}=o_{p}(1)
.

Step 1: Bias term
\Delta_{t}^{A}
.

Let

\Delta_{t,r}^{A}:=\mathbb{E}[m_{t,r}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\phi_{t}({\mathcal{H}}_{r};\pi_{r},\hat{\eta}_{t,r-1})-\phi_{t}({\mathcal{H}}_{r};\pi_{r},\eta_{t})\;\middle|\;\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right].

(63)

Using the same expansion as in the proof of the robustness survival estimator, one obtains

\displaystyle\Delta_{t,r}^{A}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{R}_{t,r,1}(X_{r})-\mathcal{R}_{t,r,0}(X_{r})\;\middle|\;\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right],

(64)

where, for
a\in\{0,1\}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{t,r,a}(x)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{t}\frac{\hat{S}_{t,r-1}(x,a)}{\hat{S}_{i,r-1}(x,a)\hat{G}_{i-1,r-1}(x,a)}S_{i}(x,a)\,\bigl\{\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}(x,a)-\lambda_{i}^{S}(x,a)\bigr\}\bigl\{\hat{G}_{i-1,r-1}(x,a)-G_{i-1}(x,a)\bigr\}

According to assumptions in Theorem
6.3
, for each arm
a
, we have
\max_{0\leq i\leq{t_{\mathrm{max}}}}\|\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{S}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}=o_{p}(r^{-1/4})
and
\max_{0\leq i\leq{t_{\mathrm{max}}}-1}\|\hat{\lambda}^{G}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{G}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}=o_{p}(r^{-1/4})
. This implies

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{i}^{S}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)^{2}=o_{p}(r^{-1/2}),

(66)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{i}^{S}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\|\hat{\lambda}^{G}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{i}^{G}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)=o_{p}(r^{-1/2}).

(67)

Then, the prefactor in Eq.
is uniformly bounded by a constant
C_{t}<\infty
depending only on
t,\underline{s}_{t},\underline{g}_{t}
. Hence

\displaystyle|\Delta_{t,r}^{A}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{t}\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}}\left[\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{i}^{S}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{i}^{S}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{G}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{i}^{G}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)\right].

(68)

By the assumed product-rate condition, the right-hand side is
o_{p}(r^{-1/2})
. Therefore, we have

\Delta_{t,r}^{A}=o_{p}(r^{-1/2}),\qquad\text{and hence}\qquad\Delta_{t}^{A}=\sqrt{R}\left(\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\Delta_{t,r}^{A}\right)=o_{p}(1).

(69)

Step 2: Martingale fluctuation term
\Delta_{t}^{B}
.

Define
U_{t,r}:=m_{t,r}-\mathbb{E}[m_{t,r}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]
.
Then
\{U_{t,r},\mathcal{H}_{r}\}_{r\geq 1}
is a martingale difference sequence, and
\Delta_{t}^{B}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}U_{t,r}
.
It therefore suffices to show that

\mathrm{Var}(\Delta_{t}^{B})=o(1).

(70)

Since the cross-terms vanish for a martingale difference sequence, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\Delta_{t}^{B})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}[U_{t,r}^{2}]\leq\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}\!\left[m_{t,r}^{2}\right].

(71)

We now bound
m_{t,r}^{2}
. We write

\hat{\xi}_{t,r}:=\xi_{t}({\mathcal{H}}_{r},\hat{\eta}_{t,r-1}),\qquad\xi_{t,r}:=\xi_{t}({\mathcal{H}}_{r},\eta_{t}),\qquad W_{r}:=\frac{A_{r}-\pi_{r}(X_{r})}{\pi_{r}(X_{r})\{1-\pi_{r}(X_{r})\}},

(72)

and

\Delta_{t,r}^{S}(a):=\hat{S}_{t,r-1}(X_{r},a)-S_{t}(X_{r},a),\qquad a\in\{0,1\}.

(73)

Then, we yield

\displaystyle m_{t,r}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t,r}^{S}(1)-\Delta_{t,r}^{S}(0)-W_{r}\Bigl[(\hat{\xi}_{t,r}-\xi_{t,r})\hat{S}_{t,r-1}(X_{r},A_{r})+\xi_{t,r}\,\Delta_{t,r}^{S}(A_{r})\Bigr].

(74)

By
(u_{1}+\cdots+u_{4})^{2}\leq 4(u_{1}^{2}+\cdots+u_{4}^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle m_{t,r}^{2}\leq C_{t}k_{r}^{2}\Bigl(|\Delta_{t,r}^{S}(1)|^{2}+|\Delta_{t,r}^{S}(0)|^{2}+|\hat{\xi}_{t,r}-\xi_{t,r}|^{2}\Bigr),

(75)

where
C_{t}<\infty
depends only on
t
and the overlap constants
\underline{s}_{t},\underline{g}_{t}
.

It remains to bound the two ingredients on the right-hand side.

Step 2a: Control of
\Delta_{t,r}^{S}(a)
.

Since

S_{t}(x,a)=\prod_{i=0}^{t}(1-\lambda_{i}^{S}(x,a)),\qquad\hat{S}_{t,r-1}(x,a)=\prod_{i=0}^{t}(1-\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}(x,a)),

(76)

the product-difference identity gives

\displaystyle|\Delta_{t,r}^{S}(a)|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{t}|\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}(X_{r},a)-\lambda_{i}^{S}(X_{r},a)|.

(77)

Hence, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we yield

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[|\Delta_{t,r}^{S}(a)|^{2}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right]\leq(t+1)\sum_{i=0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\bigl(\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}(X_{r},a)-\lambda_{i}^{S}(X_{r},a)\bigr)^{2}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{H}_{r-1}\right].

(78)

Step 2b: Control of
\hat{\xi}_{t,r}-\xi_{t,r}
.

Using the definition of
\xi_{t}
and a first-order decomposition of the difference between

\frac{N_{t,i}-Y_{t,i}\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}}{\hat{S}_{i,r-1}\hat{G}_{i-1,r-1}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{N_{t,i}-Y_{t,i}\lambda_{i}^{S}}{S_{i}G_{i-1}},

(79)

together with the uniform lower bounds
\underline{s}_{t},\underline{g}_{t}
, we obtain

\displaystyle|\hat{\xi}_{t,r}-\xi_{t,r}|^{2}\leq C_{t}\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}}\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{t}\bigl(\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}(X_{r},a)-\lambda_{i}^{S}(X_{r},a)\bigr)^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\bigl(\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{G}(X_{r},a)-\lambda_{i}^{G}(X_{r},a)\bigr)^{2}\right\},

(80)

for some constant
C_{t}<\infty
.

Combining Eq.
75
, Eq.
78
, and Eq.
80
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[m_{t,r}^{2}\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]\leq C_{t}k_{r}^{2}\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}}\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}(\cdot,a)-\lambda_{i}^{S}(\cdot,a)\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{G}(\cdot,a)-\lambda_{i}^{G}(\cdot,a)\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(81)

Under the stated
L_{2}
rate conditions and the boundedness implied by Assumption
6.1
, the expectation of the right-hand side of Eq.
81
is
o(1)
.

Conclusion.

Since both
\Delta_{t}^{A}=o_{p}(1)
and
\Delta_{t}^{B}=o_{p}(1)
, we conclude that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}m_{t,r}=o_{p}(1)
.
This proves that the nuisance remainder is asymptotically negligible.

If the limiting policy satisfies
\pi=\pi^{\star}
, then the limiting variance in the above display becomes
V_{\mathrm{eff},t}(\pi^{\star})
. By Theorem
4.1
, this is the semiparametric efficiency bound under the A-optimal allocation.

Proof of Proposition

By Eq.

,

and
k_{r}\to\infty
, we have
1/k_{r}<\alpha
for all sufficiently large
r
, so
\pi^{\star}(X)\in[1/k_{r},1-1/k_{r}]
. Because clipping onto
[1/k_{r},1-1/k_{r}]
is the Euclidean projection and is non-expansive with respect to any point inside the interval, we have
|\pi_{r}(x)-\pi^{\star}(x)|\leq|\widetilde{\pi}_{r}(x)-\pi^{\star}(x)|
pointwise. Hence, we arrive at
\|\pi_{r}-\pi^{\star}\|_{2}\leq\|\widetilde{\pi}_{r}-\pi^{\star}\|_{2}=O_{p}(r^{-1/4})
. Since
k_{r}=o(r^{1/4})
, it follows that
k_{r}\|\pi_{r}-\pi^{\star}\|_{2}=o_{p}(1)
.
∎

F.6
Proof of Corollary
6.5

The claim is an immediate consequence of Theorem
6.3
and Eq.
66
, which yields

\displaystyle\bigl|\hat{\tau}_{t,R}^{\mathrm{ASE}}-\tau_{t}\bigr|=O_{p}(R^{-1/2})

(82)

\displaystyle+O_{p}\left((\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{S}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2})^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{S}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\|\hat{\lambda}^{G}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{G}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right).

Since
\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{S}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)^{2}=o_{p}(r^{-1/2})
and
\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{S}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\|\hat{\lambda}^{G}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{G}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}=o_{p}(r^{-1/2})
, so
\hat{\tau}_{t,R}^{\mathrm{ASE}}\xrightarrow{p}\tau_{t}
. The absence of any standalone term
O_{p}(\|\hat{\lambda}_{t}^{G}-\lambda_{t}^{G}\|_{2})
shows that perturbations in
\lambda^{G}
enter only through interaction terms, which is exactly the robustness condition we aimed to show.
∎

Appendix G
Extensions to no ties

In the no-ties setting, we refine the observed data structure by recording separate event and censoring indicators. Specifically, each observation is
{\mathcal{O}}=(X,A,\widetilde{T},\Delta^{S},\Delta^{G})
, where
\widetilde{T}=\min(T,C)
,
\Delta^{S}=\mathbf{1}(T=\widetilde{T})
,
\Delta^{G}=\mathbf{1}(C=\widetilde{T}
), so that
\Delta^{S}+\Delta^{G}=1
almost surely. In this case, the event and censoring hazards are defined separately by

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}^{S}(x,a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(T=t\mid T\geq t,X=x,A=a),

(83)

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}^{G}(x,a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(C=t\mid C\geq t,X=x,A=a),

(84)

with corresponding survival functions

\displaystyle S_{t}(x,a)=\prod_{i=0}^{t}(1-\lambda_{i}^{S}(x,a)),

\displaystyle G_{t-1}(x,a)=\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}(1-\lambda_{i}^{G}(x,a)).

(85)

For a possibly misspecified nuisance limit
\tilde{\eta}_{t}=(\tilde{\lambda}_{i}^{S}(\cdot,\cdot),\tilde{\lambda}_{i}^{G}(\cdot,\cdot))_{i=0}^{t}
,
we analogously define

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{t}(x,a)=\prod_{i=0}^{t}(1-\tilde{\lambda}_{i}^{S}(x,a)),

\displaystyle\tilde{G}_{t}(x,a)=\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}(1-\tilde{\lambda}_{i}^{G}(x,a)).

(86)

In the no-ties setting, the censoring survival function
G_{t}
depends only on the censoring hazard
\lambda^{G}
, so the event and censoring nuisances form two variation-independent blocks. As a result, the adaptive survival estimator satisfies a literal two-block double robustness property.

Theorem G.1

(Convergence rate of the adaptive survival estimator under no ties)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
hold, and that there exists a non-adaptive policy
\pi(X)\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
for some
\varepsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle k_{r}\|\pi_{r}-\pi\|_{2}=o_{p}(1).

(87)

Let
\tilde{\eta}_{t}
denote a possibly misspecified limit of the cross-fitted nuisance estimators, and suppose

\displaystyle k_{r}\|\hat{f}_{t,r-1}-\tilde{f}_{t}\|_{2}=o_{p}(1)

(88)

componentwise for
\tilde{f}_{t}\in\tilde{\eta}_{t}
. Then, for each horizon
t\leq t_{\max}
,

\displaystyle\bigl|\hat{\tau}_{t}^{\mathrm{ad\text{-}cf}}-\tau_{t}\bigr|

\displaystyle=O_{p}(R^{-1/2})+O_{p}\!\left(\|\tilde{S}_{t}-S_{t}\|_{2}\,\|\tilde{G}_{t}-G_{t}\|_{2}\right)+\sum_{i=0}^{t}O_{p}\!\left(\|\tilde{\lambda}^{S}_{i}-\lambda^{S}_{i}\|_{2}\,\|\tilde{G}_{i-1}-G_{i-1}\|_{2}\right).

(89)

Corollary G.2

(Double robustness under no ties)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
G.1
, if either

\displaystyle\|\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{t,r-1}-\lambda_{t}^{S}\|_{2}=o_{p}(1)\qquad\text{for all }t,

(90)

or

\displaystyle\|\hat{\lambda}^{G}_{t,r-1}-\lambda_{t}^{G}\|_{2}=o_{p}(1)\qquad\text{for all }t,

(91)

then

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{R,t}^{\mathrm{ad\text{-}cf}}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\tau_{t}\qquad\text{for each }t\leq t_{\max}.

(92)

Proof.

The claim follows directly from Theorem
G.1
. If either the event hazard estimators or the censoring hazard estimators are
L_{2}
-consistent, then the corresponding product terms in the remainder are
o_{p}(1)
, and hence
\bigl|\hat{\tau}_{t}^{\mathrm{ad\text{-}cf}}-\tau_{t}\bigr|=o_{p}(1)
.
Therefore,
\hat{\tau}_{R,t}^{\mathrm{ad\text{-}cf}}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\tau_{t}
for each,
t\leq t_{\max}
.
∎

Why the tied-case remainder is not of pure product form:

We explain the structural reason why the second-order remainder in the tied setting, stated in Theorem
6.3
, contains both a pure event-hazard quadratic term and an event–censoring cross-interaction term, in contrast to the clean two-block product structure of the no-ties case (Appendix
G
).

The source of changes:

In both settings, the key remainder after first-order cancellation involves, for each time horizon
t
and arm
a
, a sum of the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{Rem}_{t}:=\sum_{i=0}^{t}\bigl(\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{S}-\lambda_{i}^{S}\bigr)\cdot\bigl(\hat{G}_{i-1}-G_{i-1}\bigr).

(93)

This cross-product arises because the EIF augmentation term at each time
i
is weighted by
1/(S_{i}\cdot G_{i-1})
: when the event hazard estimator deviates from truth, the error in
\hat{G}_{i-1}
multiplies it. The two settings differ precisely in how
G_{i-1}
depends on the nuisance parameters.

Tied setting:

In the tied setting, the censoring survival function is constructed via a cause-specific factorization, i.e.,

\displaystyle G_{i-1}(x,a)=\prod_{k=0}^{i-1}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{k}^{G}(x,a)}{1-\lambda_{k}^{S}(x,a)}\right),

(94)

because at each discrete time
k
the conditional probability of censoring given survival to time
k
is
\lambda_{k}^{G}/(1-\lambda_{k}^{S})
, not
\lambda_{k}^{G}
alone. Let
\delta_{k}^{S}:=\hat{\lambda}_{k}^{S}-\lambda_{k}^{S}
and
\delta_{k}^{G}:=\hat{\lambda}_{k}^{G}-\lambda_{k}^{G}
. A first-order Taylor expansion of
\hat{G}_{i-1}-G_{i-1}
gives

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{i-1}-G_{i-1}\approx-G_{i-1}\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{k}^{G}/(1-\lambda_{k}^{S})}\left[\frac{\delta_{k}^{G}}{1-\lambda_{k}^{S}}+\frac{\lambda_{k}^{G}\,\delta_{k}^{S}}{(1-\lambda_{k}^{S})^{2}}\right].

(95)

The critical observation is the second term inside the brackets: because
\lambda_{k}^{G}
appears in the numerator of the cause-specific hazard, a perturbation
\delta_{k}^{S}
in the event hazard also perturbs
G_{i-1}
. Substituting into Eq.
93
and bounding in
L_{2}
, the remainder can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{Rem}_{t}\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\underbrace{\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\delta_{i}^{S}\|_{2}\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\frac{\lambda_{k}^{G}}{(1-\lambda_{k}^{S})^{2}}\|\delta_{k}^{S}\|_{2}}_{\text{pure }\lambda^{S}\text{ quadratic}}+\underbrace{\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\delta_{i}^{S}\|_{2}\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\frac{\|\delta_{k}^{G}\|_{2}}{1-\lambda_{k}^{S}}}_{\lambda^{S}\text{--}\lambda^{G}\text{ cross-interaction}}.

(96)

Since
t\leq t_{\max}
is fixed, summing over
i
and
k
contributes only a finite multiplicative constant, so, at the level of asymptotic rates, this gives

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{Rem}_{t}\|=O_{p}\!\left(\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{S}-\lambda_{i}^{S}\|_{2}\Bigr)^{2}+\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{S}-\lambda_{i}^{S}\|_{2}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{G}-\lambda_{i}^{G}\|_{2}\Bigr)\right).

(97)

This is precisely the remainder structure appearing in Theorem
6.3
. Note in particular that there is no standalone term of the form
O_{p}(\sum_{i}\|\delta_{i}^{G}\|_{2}^{2})
: a perturbation in
\lambda^{G}
alone, without any event-hazard error, does not appear in
\mathrm{Rem}_{t}
at all, which is the robustness of the estimating equation with respect to the censoring nuisance.

No-ties setting (contrast):

In the no-ties setting, the censoring survival function factorizes independently of the event hazard:

\displaystyle G_{i-1}(x,a)=\prod_{k=0}^{i-1}(1-\lambda_{k}^{G}(x,a)).

(98)

The first-order expansion then gives

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{i-1}-G_{i-1}\approx-G_{i-1}\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\frac{\delta_{k}^{G}}{1-\lambda_{k}^{G}},

(99)

which involves only
\delta^{G}
and contains no
\delta^{S}
term. Consequently,
\mathrm{Rem}_{t}
involves only the cross-product
\delta^{S}\cdot\delta^{G}
, yielding the clean remainder structure of Theorem
G.1
:

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{Rem}_{t}\|=O_{p}\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\tilde{\lambda}_{i}^{S}-\lambda_{i}^{S}\|_{2}\,\|\tilde{G}_{i-1}-G_{i-1}\|_{2}\right).

(100)

Because the two nuisance blocks are now variation-independent, the remainder vanishes whenever either block is
L_{2}
-consistent, giving the literal two-block double robustness of Corollary
G.2
. In the tied setting, this property fails: even if
\lambda^{G}
is estimated perfectly, a nonzero
\delta^{S}
still induces a nonzero
\hat{G}_{i-1}-G_{i-1}
through the cause-specific factorization, so the pure
\lambda^{S}
quadratic term remains.

Appendix H
Asymptotic confidence sequences

The fixed-time intervals in Section
6
guarantee
(1-\alpha)
coverage
solely
at pre-specified sample size
R
. In practice, however, analysts often
peek
at interim results and may stop the study early once a decision rule is met
[
45
]
; this behavior invalidates fixed-time intervals. To remain valid under such data-dependent stopping, one needs a
confidence sequence (CS)
, i.e., a collection of intervals
[L_{t,r},U_{t,r}]_{r\geq 1}
at horizon
t
satisfying the time-uniform guarantee,
\mathbb{P}(\forall r\in\mathbb{N}^{+}:\tau_{t}\in[L_{t,r},U_{t,r}])\geq 1-\alpha
.

Constructing
non-asymptotic
, anytime-valid CSs can be difficult when the target estimand contains estimated nuisance functions. Fortunately, for the adaptive survival estimator, the nuisance-induced remainder is asymptotically negligible under the rate conditions below, so an
asymptotic CS
(valid after a finite, burn-in phase) remains both tractable and practically useful.

Definition H.1

(Asymptotic time-uniform coverage
[
14
]
)
.

A sequence of random intervals
\widetilde{C}_{t,r}=[\widetilde{L}_{t,r},\widetilde{U}_{t,r}]_{t\geq 1}
is an asymptotic time uniform
(1-\alpha)
confidence sequence (AsympCS) for a parameter
\tau_{t}
at horizon
t
if the following two conditions hold:

1.

Asymptotic confidence sequence:
there exists an exact (potentially unknown)
(1-\alpha)
confidence sequence
C^{\star}_{t,r}=[L^{\star}_{t,r},U^{\star}_{t,r}]_{t\geq 1}
such that
\widetilde{L}_{t,r}/L^{\star}_{t,r}\rightarrow 1
and
\widetilde{U}_{t,r}/U^{\star}_{t,r}\rightarrow 1
almost surely.

2.

Asymptotic time-uniform coverage:

\lim_{R_{0}\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\forall r\geq R_{0}:\tau_{t}\in\widetilde{C}_{t,r})\geq 1-\alpha
.

Definition
H.1
can be read as follows: if one waits to “peek” until the sample size is sufficiently large (
R\geq R_{0}
for some burn-in
R_{0}
), the band then covers the true parameter
every
later time with probability approaching
1-\alpha
. In practice, rare coverage failures occur almost exclusively during this short initial window; later, the intervals tighten rapidly and deliver appreciable power gains over fully non-asymptotic sequences
[
10
,
60
]
.

Building on the methodologies of
60
and
10
, we now present the corresponding asymptotic confidence sequence (AsympCS) for our estimator:

Theorem H.2

(AsympCS for adaptive survival estimator)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
and
6.1
hold and that there exists a non-adaptive policy
\pi(X)\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
for some
\varepsilon>0
such that the adaptive assignment policy
\pi_{r}(X\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{r-1})
are
L_{2}
-consistent relative to truncation schedule, i.e.
k_{r}\|\pi_{r}-\pi\|_{2}=o_{a.s.}(1)
. Furthermore, assume that, for each fixed
t
and treatment
a
,
\max_{0\leq i\leq{t_{\mathrm{max}}}}\|\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{S}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}=o_{a.s.}\left(\left(\frac{\log r}{r}\right)^{1/4}\right)
, and
\max_{0\leq i\leq{t_{\mathrm{max}}}-1}\|\hat{\lambda}^{G}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{G}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}=o_{a.s.}\left(\left(\frac{\log r}{r}\right)^{1/4}\right)
. Let

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{t,R}:=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left(\hat{\phi}_{t,r}-\hat{\tau}_{t,R}^{\mathrm{ASE}}\right)^{2},

(101)

be the estimated variance of
\{\phi(O_{r};\pi_{r},\hat{\eta}_{t,r})\}
, and fix a user-specified
\rho>0
. Then, for all
R\geq 1
, the interval

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{R}^{\mathrm{AsympCS}}:=\left(\hat{\tau}_{t,R}^{\mathrm{ASE}}\;\pm\;\sqrt{\frac{2(R\hat{V}_{t,R}\rho^{2}+1)}{R^{2}\rho^{2}}\,\log\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{R\hat{V}_{t,R}\rho^{2}+1}}{\alpha}\right)}\right)

(102)

is an asymptotic
(1-\alpha)
-CS for
\tau_{t}
. The width is approximately minimized at

\rho^{\star}=\sqrt{\frac{-2\log\alpha+\log(-2\log\alpha+1)}{R}}.

(103)

Remark H.3

(Difference in convergence rates for fixed-time and anytime-valid inference.)
.

For fixed-time inference, the second-order nuisance remainder is required to be
o_{p}(r^{-1/2})
, which is implied by pointwise nuisance rates
o_{p}(r^{-1/4})
. For the AsympCS, the asymptotic linearity remainder only needs to be
o_{a.s.}(\sqrt{\log r/r})
, which is implied by pointwise nuisance rates
o_{a.s.}((\log r/r)^{1/4})
. Thus, the AsympCS condition allows a slightly slower rate up to a logarithmic factor, but requires almost-sure rather than in-probability convergence.

Proof.

For the proof, we rely on existing results for asymptotic confidence sequences
[
60
]
. For completeness, we provide this in Lemma
H.4

Lemma H.4

(Corollary 3.4 of
60
)
.

Suppose
\hat{\tau}_{R}
is an asymptotically linear estimator with influence function
\phi
satisfying

\hat{\tau}_{t,R}-\tau=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\phi(O_{r};\,\pi_{r},\eta_{t,r})+o_{a.s.}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log R}{R}}\right).

(104)

If
\mathrm{Var}(\phi)<\infty
, then the interval from Eq.
102
is a valid
(1-\alpha)
-AsympCS.

It suffices to verify: (i) the residual error is of smaller order than
\sqrt{\log R/R}
, almost surely; and (ii)
\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{v})<\infty
.

Step 1: Second-order remainder analysis

From the decomposition in Eq.
43
, we need to show
\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}m_{t,r}=o_{a.s.}\bigl(\sqrt{\log R/R}\bigr)
, i.e.,
\sum_{r=1}^{R}m_{t,r}=o_{a.s.}\bigl(\sqrt{R\log R}\bigr)
.

Structure of
m_{t,r}
.

For a single arm
a
and time
t
, the remainder at observation
r
is:

m_{t,r}^{a}=\hat{\phi}_{t}^{a}(O_{r};\,\hat{\eta}_{t,r-1})-\hat{\phi}_{t}^{a}(O_{r};\eta_{t}).

(105)

Expanding using the definition from Eq.
4
:

\displaystyle m_{t,r}^{a}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\bigl[\hat{S}_{t,r-1}(X_{r},a)-S_{t}(X_{r},a)\bigr]}_{\text{term 1}}

(106)

\displaystyle-\underbrace{\frac{\mathbf{1}(A_{r}=a)}{\pi_{a}(X_{r})}\sum_{i=0}^{t}\left[\frac{\hat{S}_{i,r-1}}{\hat{S}_{i}(X_{r-1},a)\cdot\hat{G}_{i-1}(X_{r-1},a)}d\hat{M}_{i}^{r}-\frac{S_{i}}{S_{j}\cdot G_{j-1}}\,dM_{i}^{r}\right]}_{\text{term 2}},

(107)

where we define
d\hat{M}_{i}^{r}:=\mathbf{1}(\tilde{T}_{r}=i,\,\Delta_{r}=1)-\mathbf{1}(\tilde{T}_{r}\geq i)\cdot\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}(X_{r},a)
.

Decomposing the summand in equation
106
.

For each
i
, define the weight function
W_{i}(\eta_{t}):=S_{t}(X,a)/[S_{i}(X,a)\cdot G_{i-1}(X,a)]
and write:

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{i}\cdot d\hat{M}_{i}^{r}-W_{i}\cdot dM_{i}^{r}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(\hat{W}_{i}-W_{i})\cdot dM_{i}^{r}}_{\text{(I): weight error $\times$ true martingale}}+\underbrace{\hat{W}_{i}\cdot(d\hat{M}_{i}^{r}-dM_{i}^{r})}_{\text{(II): estimated weight $\times$ hazard error}}

\displaystyle=(\hat{W}_{i}-W_{i})\cdot dM_{i}^{r}-\hat{W}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}(\tilde{T}_{r}\geq i)\cdot(\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}-\lambda_{i}^{S}).

(108)

Term (I): Weight error.

The cross term involves:

\bigl(\hat{S}_{t}/\hat{S}_{i}-S_{t}/S_{i}\bigr)\cdot\bigl(1/\hat{G}_{i-1}-1/G_{i-1}\bigr),

(109)

using
\hat{S}_{t}/\hat{S}_{i}=\prod_{k=j+1}^{t}(1-\hat{\lambda}_{k}^{S})
and
\hat{G}_{i-1}=\prod_{k=0}^{i-1}(1-\hat{\lambda}_{k}^{G}/(1-\hat{\lambda}_{k}^{S}))
.
Combining the bounds above yields

\displaystyle\|\hat{W}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-W_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{t}\sum_{k=i+1}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}_{k,r-1}^{S}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{k}^{S}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+C_{t}\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\|\hat{\lambda}_{k,r-1}^{S}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{k}^{S}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+C_{t}\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\|\hat{\lambda}_{k,r-1}^{G}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{k}^{G}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}.

(110)

Consequently, after summing over
i=0,\dots,t
, the resulting second-order remainder is bounded by

\displaystyle C_{t}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}_{k,r-1}^{S}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{k}^{S}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{t}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}_{k,r-1}^{S}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{k}^{S}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\|\hat{\lambda}_{k,r-1}^{G}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{k}^{G}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right).

(111)

where
C_{t_{\max}}
is a constant depending on
t_{\max}
,
\gamma
, and the boundedness of hazards.

Term (II): Hazard estimation error.

This term involves the event-hazard estimation error
\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{i}^{S}(a,\cdot)
directly. As in the standard augmented influence-function argument, the leading linear terms cancel after combining term (I) and term (II). In the present tied setting, however, the resulting remainder is not of pure product form in the event and censoring hazard errors, because
G_{i-1}(x,a)=\prod_{k=0}^{i-1}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{k}^{G}(x,a)}{1-\lambda_{k}^{S}(x,a)}\right)
, so that perturbations in
\lambda^{S}
also induce perturbations in
G_{i-1}
. Consequently, after first-order cancellation, the remaining bias is of second order and is bounded by the sum of a pure event-hazard quadratic term and an event-censoring interaction term.

Aggregating over
i
.

Using the bounds above for the weight error and the hazard error, and summing over
i=0,\ldots,t
, we obtain that for each
a\in\{0,1\}
,

\displaystyle|m_{t,r}^{a}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{t}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{i}^{S}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+C_{t}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{i}^{S}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{G}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{i}^{G}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right).

(112)

Since
t\leq t_{\max}
is fixed, the summation over
i
contributes only a finite constant and does not alter the rate.

Summing over
r
.

Suppose the nuisance convergence conditions hold almost surely and
\max_{0\leq i\leq{t_{\mathrm{max}}}}\|\hat{\lambda}^{S}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{S}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}=o_{a.s.}\left(\left(\frac{\log r}{r}\right)^{1/4}\right)
,
\max_{0\leq i\leq{t_{\mathrm{max}}}-1}\|\hat{\lambda}^{G}_{i,r-1}(a,\cdot)-\lambda^{G}_{i}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}=o_{a.s.}\left(\left(\frac{\log r}{r}\right)^{1/4}\right)
. We have

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{i}^{S}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)^{2}=o_{a.s.}(\sqrt{\log r/r}),

(113)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{i}^{S}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{G}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{i}^{G}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)=o_{a.s.}(\sqrt{\log r/r}),

then Eq.
112
implies
m_{t,r}=o_{a.s.}(\sqrt{\log r/r})
. Hence,

\sum_{r=1}^{R}m_{t,r}=o_{a.s.}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sqrt{\log r/r}\right)=o_{a.s.}(\sqrt{R\log R})
,
and therefore

\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}m_{t,r}=o_{a.s.}(\sqrt{\log R/R})
. This establishes the asymptotic linearity condition in Eq.
104
.

Remark H.5
.

In contrast to the no-ties setting, the second-order remainder in the tied setting is not purely of product form in the event and censoring hazard errors. This is because
G_{i-1}(x,a)=\prod_{k=0}^{i-1}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{k}^{G}(x,a)}{1-\lambda_{k}^{S}(x,a)}\right)

so the censoring survival function depends on both
\lambda^{G}
and
\lambda^{S}
. As a result, the remainder contains both
\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{i}^{S}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)^{2}
and
\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{S}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{i}^{S}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\|\hat{\lambda}_{i,r-1}^{G}(a,\cdot)-\lambda_{i}^{G}(a,\cdot)\|_{2}\right)

terms. Since
t\leq t_{\max}
is fixed, summing over
i
affects only the multiplicative constant and not the asymptotic rate.

Step 2: Finite variance

Under Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
, the influence function is uniformly bounded. Indeed, for arm
a\in\{0,1\}
, we have

\phi_{t}^{a}(O;\pi,\eta)=S_{t}(X,a)-\frac{\mathbf{1}(A=a)}{\pi_{a}(X)}\sum_{j=0}^{t}\frac{S_{t}(X,a)}{S_{j}(X,a)\,G_{j-1}(X,a)}\Bigl\{\mathbf{1}(\widetilde{T}=j,\Delta=1)-\mathbf{1}(\widetilde{T}\geq j)\lambda_{j}^{S}(X,a)\Bigr\},

(114)

where
\pi_{1}(X)=\pi(X)
and
\pi_{0}(X)=1-\pi(X)
. By positivity,
\pi_{a}(X)\geq\epsilon
.
Moreover, for each
j\leq t
,
\left|\mathbf{1}(\widetilde{T}=j,\Delta=1)-\mathbf{1}(\widetilde{T}\geq j)\lambda_{j}^{S}(X,a)\right|\leq 1
,
since
\mathbf{1}(\widetilde{T}=j,\Delta=1)\in\{0,1\}
,
\mathbf{1}(\widetilde{T}\geq j)\in\{0,1\}
, and
\lambda_{j}^{S}(X,a)\in[0,1]
. Also,
0\leq\frac{S_{t}(X,a)}{S_{j}(X,a)}\leq 1
for all
j\leq t
.
Hence,

\displaystyle|\phi_{t}^{a}(O;\pi,\eta)|

\displaystyle\leq|S_{t}(X,a)|+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\sum_{j=0}^{t}\frac{S_{t}(X,a)}{S_{j}(X,a)\,G_{j-1}(X,a)}\left|\mathbf{1}(\widetilde{T}=j,\Delta=1)-\mathbf{1}(\widetilde{T}\geq j)\lambda_{j}^{S}(X,a)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\sum_{j=0}^{t}\frac{1}{G_{j-1}(X,a)}.

(115)

Under Assumption
6.1
, for each
a\in\{0,1\}
and each
k\leq t_{\max}
, we have

1-\lambda_{k}^{S}(X,a)\geq\underline{c}_{t}^{S}

and

1-\dfrac{\lambda_{k}^{G}(X,a)}{1-\lambda_{k}^{S}(X,a)}\geq\underline{c}_{t}^{G}.

Hence, for every
j\leq t_{\max}
,

G_{j-1}(X,a)=\prod_{k=0}^{j-1}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{k}^{G}(X,a)}{1-\lambda_{k}^{S}(X,a)}\right)\geq(\underline{c}_{t}^{G})^{j}\geq(\underline{c}_{t}^{G})^{t_{\max}}.

Therefore,

\frac{1}{G_{j-1}(X,a)}\leq(\underline{c}_{t}^{G})^{-t_{\max}}
for all
j\leq{t_{\mathrm{max}}}
.
Combining this with Eq.
115
, we obtain

\displaystyle|\phi_{t}^{a}(O;\pi,\eta)|

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{1}{\epsilon}(t_{\max}+1)(\underline{c}_{t}^{G})^{-t_{\max}}

\displaystyle=:B(\epsilon,\underline{c}_{t}^{G},t_{\max})<\infty.

(116)

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\bigl(\phi_{t}^{a}(O;\pi,\eta)\bigr)^{2}\right]\leq B(\epsilon,\underline{c}_{t}^{G},t_{\max})^{2}<\infty.

If, more generally,

\phi_{v}(O;\pi,\eta):=\sum_{t=0}^{t_{\max}}v_{t}\,\phi_{t}(O;\pi,\eta),

then, by the triangle inequality, we yield

|\phi_{v}(O;\pi,\eta)|\leq\sum_{t=0}^{t_{\max}}|v_{t}|\,|\phi_{t}(O;\pi,\eta)|\leq\|v\|_{1}\,B(\epsilon,\underline{c}_{t}^{G},t_{\max}),

and, hence,

\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{v})\leq\mathbb{E}[\phi_{v}^{2}]\leq\|v\|_{1}^{2}\,B(\epsilon,\underline{c}_{t}^{G},t_{\max})^{2}<\infty.

∎

Appendix I
Extension to Batch Settings

The theoretical analysis in Section
6
is stated for the fully sequential setting in which the policy
\pi_{r}(X\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1})
and the nuisance estimates
\hat{\eta}_{t,r-1}
are updated after every individual observation. In practice, however, it is common to update both the policy and the nuisance estimates only at the end of each
batch
of observations, thereby reducing computational overhead and stabilizing estimation. We show here that ASE extends naturally to this setting without any modification to the asymptotic normality result.

Batch setting:
Partition the
R
rounds into
B
batches of size
m=R/B
each. Let batch
b
consist of rounds
r\in\mathcal{B}_{b}:=\{(b-1)m+1,\ldots,bm\}
. All units within batch
b
are assigned using the same policy
\pi_{b}(X):=\pi_{(b-1)m+1}(X\mid\mathcal{H}_{(b-1)m})
, computed once from the accumulated history at the end of batch
b-1
. Similarly, the nuisance estimators
\hat{\eta}_{b-1}
are refitted once per batch using
\mathcal{H}_{(b-1)m}
via the sequential cross-fitting scheme of Algorithm
1
. The ASE estimator is
\hat{\tau}_{R}^{\mathrm{ASE}}=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\phi}_{r}^{\mathrm{cf}}
, as before.

The martingale difference sequence (MDS):
The key structural requirement for asymptotic normality is that the oracle pseudo-outcome at horizon
t
,
\phi(X_{t,r},Z_{t,r},A_{t,r},Y_{t,r};\pi_{t,r},\eta)
forms a martingale difference sequence (MDS) with respect to the filtration
\{\mathcal{H}_{t,r}\}
. In the batch setting,
\pi_{b}
is computed once from
\mathcal{H}_{(b-1)m}
before any unit in batch
b
is observed. Since
(b-1)m\leq r-1
for all
r\in\mathcal{B}_{b}
, the policy
\pi_{b}
is already determined by time
r-1
. Hence, the MDS property
\mathbb{E}[\phi(X_{t,r},Z_{t,r},A_{t,r},Y_{t,r};\pi_{b},\eta)\mid\mathcal{H}_{r-1}]=\tau
continues to hold exactly. The martingale CLT
[
10
]
then applies without modification to the oracle term in the near-martingale decomposition

\displaystyle\sqrt{R}\left(\hat{\tau}_{t,R}^{\mathrm{ASE}}-\tau_{t}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}z_{t,r}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}m_{t,r},

(117)

where
z_{t,r}=\phi({\mathcal{H}}_{r};\pi_{b},\eta_{t,r})-\tau
is the oracle MDS term and
m_{t,r}=\hat{\phi}_{t,r}^{\mathrm{cf}}-\phi({\mathcal{H}}_{r};\pi_{b},\eta_{t,r})
is the nuisance remainder.

L_{2}
-consistency condition in the batch setting.
Theorem
6.3
requires
k_{r}\|\pi_{r}-\pi^{\star}\|_{2}=o_{p}(1)
. In the batch setting,
\pi_{r}\equiv\pi_{b}
for all
t\in\mathcal{B}_{b}
, so this reduces to
k_{b}\|\pi_{b}-\pi^{\star}\|_{2}=o_{p}(1)
as
b\to\infty
, where
k_{b}
is the truncation parameter for batch
b
. This is no harder to satisfy than in the sequential case; indeed, because each batch-level policy update uses
m
additional observations relative to the previous update, the plug-in estimate of
\pi^{\star}
is typically more stable, making the convergence condition easier to verify in practice.

Cross-fitting within batches.
Since all
m
units in batch
b
share the nuisance estimator
\hat{\eta}_{b-1}
fitted on
\mathcal{H}_{(b-1)m}
, the standard sequential cross-fitting scheme from Algorithm
1
applies directly: the history
\mathcal{H}_{(b-1)m}
is split into two temporal folds, nuisances are fitted on one fold and pseudo-outcomes are evaluated on the other. This ensures that the remainder term satisfies
\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}m_{r}=o_{p}(1)
under the same
L_{2}
-consistency conditions as in Theorem
6.3
, with no additional assumptions.

Asymptotic normality is unchanged.
Combining the above, the limiting distribution of the ASE estimator under the batch protocol is identical to the fully sequential case:

\displaystyle\sqrt{R}\left(\hat{\tau}_{t,R}^{\mathrm{ASE}}-\tau_{t}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\,V_{\mathrm{eff},t}(\pi^{\star})\right),

(118)

where
V_{\mathrm{eff}}(\pi^{\star})
is the semiparametric efficiency bound from Theorem
4.1
. In particular, if
\pi_{b}\to\pi^{\star}
as
b\to\infty
, the estimator remains semiparametrically efficient. The batch size
m
affects only the finite-sample rate at which
\pi_{b}
converges to
\pi^{\star}
and the computational cost of nuisance estimation, but does not alter the asymptotic variance or the form of valid confidence intervals.

Appendix K
Implementation details

K.1
Data generation

Synthetic data generation:
We sample a one-dimensional confounder
X\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)
and set
t_{\max}=4
. Survival and censoring hazards are generated via logistic link functions:

\displaystyle\lambda^{S}_{t}(x,a)

\displaystyle=\sigma\!\left(\alpha_{t}+\eta(x)+\tau(x)\cdot a\right)

(121)

\displaystyle\lambda^{G}_{t}(x,a)

\displaystyle=\sigma\!\left(\gamma_{t}+\phi(x)+\psi(x)\cdot a\right),

(122)

where
\{\alpha_{t}\}_{t=0}^{4}
is a time-varying intercept path constructed so that the marginal control survival
S_{t}(x,0)
decreases from
0.50
to
0.02
over the horizon, and
\{\gamma_{t}\}_{t=0}^{4}
is similarly constructed to decrease from
0.84
to
0.62
. The covariate functions are:

\displaystyle\eta(x)

\displaystyle=1.05+0.12(x-0.5),

(123)

\displaystyle\tau(x)

\displaystyle=-0.28+0.65\cdot\mathbf{1}(x\leq 0.35)-0.42\cdot\mathbf{1}(x\geq 0.75),

(124)

\displaystyle\phi(x)

\displaystyle=0.06(x-0.5),

(125)

\displaystyle\psi(x)

\displaystyle=0.14-0.26\cdot\mathbf{1}(x\leq 0.35)+0.20\cdot\mathbf{1}(x\geq 0.75).

(126)

The function
\tau(x)
captures heterogeneous treatment effect on the event hazard, with the treatment arm exhibiting higher survival probability than control across all
x
. The censoring functions
\phi(x)
and
\psi(x)
introduce arm-dependent censoring (
\lambda^{G}_{t}(x,0)\neq\lambda^{G}_{t}(x,1)
), consistent with the motivating setting of Section
5.1
. Ground-truth average potential outcomes
\mu_{a}=\mathbb{E}_{X}[S_{t}(X,a)]
are computed via Gauss–Legendre quadrature over
x\in[0,1]
.

Twins semi-synthetic data generation:
For our semi-synthetic case study, we employ the Twins dataset following
37
, which records birth outcomes for twin pairs in the United States. Following
37
, we use the gestational age variable
GESTAT10
as the single covariate, binarized as
X=\mathbf{1}(\texttt{GESTAT10}>3)
, which is highly correlated with survival outcome. The time horizon is
t\in\{0,\ldots,3\}
. Survival and censoring hazards are time-homogeneous and defined as:

\displaystyle\lambda^{S}_{t}(x,a)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0.50,&\text{if }a=0,\\
0.40\cdot\mathbf{1}(x=0)+0.01\cdot\mathbf{1}(x=1),&\text{if }a=1,\end{cases}

(127)

\displaystyle\lambda^{G}_{t}(x,a)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0.05,&\text{if }a=0,\\
0.108,&\text{if }a=1.\end{cases}

(128)

This design exhibits arm-dependent censoring (i.e.,
\lambda^{G}_{t}(x,0)\neq\lambda^{G}_{t}(x,1)
) and covariate-dependent treatment effect heterogeneity in the survival hazard, making it a realistic testbed for our adaptive allocation procedure.

K.2
Experiment setup

For both synthetic and semi-synthetic datasets, each estimator was evaluated over
50
seeds per dataset. Simulations were run over
R=20
rounds with a burn-in period of
N_{0}=1000
rounds, after which nuisance estimators were updated in mini-batches of
100
units over
20
successive rounds. For all adaptive methods, we applied the clipped optimal allocation policy from Proposition
4.3
with truncation parameter
\alpha=0.05
. Oracle methods used ground-truth nuisance functions computed analytically from the DGP parameters, while the misspecified variant
ASE-MS
was constructed by replacing
\lambda^{G}(X,A)
with a constant fit to the marginal average.

For each estimator, we report the mean squared error at round
r
, averaged over time horizons:

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}(r):=\frac{1}{{t_{\mathrm{max}}}+1}\sum_{t=0}^{{t_{\mathrm{max}}}}\left(\hat{\tau}_{t}(r)-\tau_{t}\right)^{2}.

(129)

The relative MSE is computed as
\mathrm{MSE}(r)/\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{Oracle}}(r)
, where
\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{Oracle}}(r)
is the MSE of the oracle estimator using ground-truth nuisance functions.

Inference coverage calculation:
For each horizon
t
, we construct a nominal
95\%
confidence interval for the average survival effect
\tau_{t}=\mathbb{E}\{S_{t}(X,1)-S_{t}(X,0)\}
. The reported coverage is averaged over horizons and simulation seeds. Hence, it measures marginal coverage of each
\tau_{t}
, consistent with our A-optimal trace criterion, rather than simultaneous coverage of the full survival-effect vector.

K.3
Implementation

All experiments were run on an Intel Xeon Silver 4316 CPU with 40 cores and 976GB RAM.

Model architecture and parameters:
We estimate the nuisance survival and censoring hazards using LightGBM classifiers, fit separately for each time point and treatment arm, with fixed default hyperparameters.
