 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 This report examines the benefits and challenges associated with recycling recovered uranium (RU)
from the used nuclear fuel (UNF) discharged from a once-through sodium-cooled fast reactor (SFR) with
high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU) which has a higher residual U-235 content in the RU.
However, there are three minor uranium isotopes that influence the value of the RU. There will be trace
quantities of U-232 in RU, which can increase dose rates and, in turn, increase the cost of fuel fabrication.
Additionally, the U-234 and U-236 isotopes are found in higher concentration in RU than in NU, and act
as neutron poisons, requiring higher U-235 concentrations to compensate for the neutron poisoning
effects of these isotopes.
 In commercial power reactors, minimizing refueling costs is an important objective. In thermal-
spectrum commercial power reactors, this results in the UNF having low residual fissile concentrations,
even if the fresh fuel initially is HALEU. In fast-spectrum commercial power reactors, it is equally
desirable to minimize refueling costs, but a higher fissile content is required for the reactor to remain
critical. As a result, the UNF will have a higher residual fissile content. This higher residual fissile content
in fast reactor UNF makes it more appealing for recycling. Consequently, this report focuses on recycling
RU from fast reactor UNF and attempts to quantify the value of recycling RU, taking into account the
impacts of the minor uranium isotopes in the RU.
 U-232 is present at the part-per-billion level in RU and is not important for reactor performance.
Therefore, accurate modeling of the production of U-232 has not been a priority. However, small amounts
of U-232 in the RU may require additional radiation protection measures, which can increase the unit
costs for processing the RU. A literature review was conducted on the production pathways and measured
concentration in different sources of RU. There is significant uncertainty on the U-232 concentration in
UNF because of the production pathways, including significant contributions of low-level concentration
of uranium daughters that may carry over as impurity in the fuel. Despite this significant uncertainty, the
RU from sodium-cooled fast reactor (SFR) HALEU UNF should have similar or lower U-232
concentrations than prior experience fabricating enriched RU for light-water reactors. At these
concentrations, there is likely just a small cost premium associated with the fabrication of RU fuels. All
concentrations should be well below the limits in the American Society for Testing and Materials
(ASTM) standards for RU-derived materials.
 To compensate for the neutron poisoning effects of U-234 and U-236, RU-derived fuels require
higher enrichment to achieve the same burnup and cycle length as enriched uranium from natural uranium
(ENU). In this report, the higher enrichment level of RU-derived fuels is called the equivalent enrichment.
For example, the equivalent enrichment of down-blended RU for a PWR having discharge burnup of 50
GWd/t is 4.4-4.7%, while it is 4.2% with a fresh ENU. The U-234 and the U-236 increase the U-235
content in the RU-derived fuels between 7%-13% relative to the actual concentration in the RU for
recycling in a PWR. Similarly, SFR requires about a 5% higher equivalent enrichment of re-enriched RU-
derived fuel due to the effects of U-234 and U-236.
 The levelized fuel cost at equilibrium (LFCAE), which is defined by the levelized cost from mining to
disposal except for reactor cost, was assessed for the recycling of RU from HALEU-fueled SFR UNF
with a residual U-235 concentration between 3-8% to account for potential variations in SFR fuel
technology. Two fuel cycle options were evaluated as alternatives for a once-through fuel cycle. Fuel
Cycle #1 (FC #1) was the use of the RU as PWR fuel, downblending the RU as needed. Fuel Cycle #2
(FC #2) was the re-enrichment of the RU for reuse in the same SFR. This analysis accounts for unit cost
changes from different U-232 concentrations and changes in equivalent enrichment from the U-234 and
U-236 concentrations. The U-232 only has the smallest impact on the fuel fabrication cost. This analysis
assumes RU recycling at a commercial scale and that commercial vendors are willing to accept RU.

 For both fuel cycles, the analysis estimated the breakeven point at which the net LFCAE of the RU-
derived fuel is equal to the LFCAE of once-through ENU fuel. The net LFCAE of RU-derived fuel
includes the avoided costs, such as UNF disposal, as a credit to the RU-derived fuel. This assessment
considered the large uncertainties in the future costs of all aspects of these nuclear fuel cycles, evaluating
the probability distribution of the breakeven points to show the mean and uncertainty. The use of the RU-
derived fuel as PWR fuel or SFR fuel (both fuel cycle options) has a breakeven point where the LFCAE
with the RU-derived fuel is equal to or cheaper than the LFCAE with the fresh ENU fuel. For a fully
deployed fuel cycle at commercial scale, the breakeven point is the residual U-235 content range of 6.0%
to 7.5%. The LFCAE for the RU-derived fuel is lower than fresh ENU fuel if the residual U-235 content
in the SFR UNF is above the breakeven point.
 While the U-235 content in the RU decreases with advances in SFR fuel, the plutonium content will
increase. A small scoping analysis for recycling of both uranium and transuranic (TRU) recovered from
the SFR UNF was performed in this study. The LFCAE for the recycling fuel with RU/TRU was lower
than the fresh HALEU fuel for all cases analyzed.
 This report conducted analyses at equilibrium conditions, which do not count the fuel cycle dynamic
effects from the current once-through cycle to the recycling of RU. The dynamic effects need to be
evaluated for the potentially valuable alternative to recycling the uranium recovered from the SFR UNF.

 SYSTEMS ANALYSIS AND INTEGRATION CAMPAIGN
 ASSESSMENT OF URANIUM ISOTOPE IMPACTS
1. Introduction
 The benefits of recycling uranium from used nuclear fuel (UNF) depend on many factors, including
the isotopic composition of the remaining uranium. Enriched uranium using natural uranium (ENU)
contains the isotopes uranium-234, -235, and -238 (U-234, U-235, and U-238), with U-234 only present
in minimal quantities. The recovered uranium (RU) from reprocessed UNF will also contain uranium-232
and -236 (U-232 and U-236), as well as larger concentrations of U-234 than ENU. U-232 may affect the
fuel fabrication process by increasing the dose rate to workers, increasing the fabrication costs to account
for additional radiation protection measures. U-234 and U-236 are neutron poisons in the fuel. To
overcome the additional neutron poison of the U-234 and U-236, the enriched uranium using RU (ERU)
fuel will need to have a higher U-235 enrichment than it would be for ENU to achieve the same burnup.
Variations in the isotopic concentration of RU are expected to impact the cost of recycling and producing
RU-derived fuel.
 It is not possible to consider the recycling of UNF and isotopic variations for the entire range of
reactor designs in which the use of HALEU can be envisioned. Designs range from specialized micro
reactors to large commercial power plants. Therefore, this report focuses on large commercial power
plants, which could range from many small modular reactors (e.g., NuScale standard 12-module plant at
924 MWe) to a small number of gigawatt-scale reactors (e.g., AP1000 at slightly more than 1000 MWe
each) at the same site.
 Table 1-1 shows a few examples of UNF that should be representative of commercial power reactors
of different types utilizing current and advanced fuel technologies [1]. The results show that pressurized
water reactor (PWR) or high temperature gas reactor (HTGR) UNF will have a residual U-235 content of
1% or less. However, sodium-cooled fast reactor (SFR) designs have a residual U-235 content of up to
about 8% in commercial reactors. Advancements in SFR fuel technology may increase the average
neutron fluence on the fuel, reducing the residual U-235 in the UNF. Theoretically, advanced SFR fuels
could be fueled with unenriched uranium (Breed and Burn concepts) and have negligible residual U-235,
but that is likely far in the future and is not captured in this analysis.
 All types of commercial reactors aim to minimize refueling costs. For thermal-spectrum commercial
reactors, the result is a small residual concentration of fissile material (U-235, Pu-239, and Pu-241)
regardless of the initial enrichment of the fresh fuel. The low residual U-235 content in thermal reactor
UNF potentially requires 10 tons of UNF to have enough RU to make 1 ton of fuel. This ratio of
reprocessed UNF to ERU fuel will likely make it more expensive to fabricate fuel from RU than to
fabricate fuel from ENU. The expected high cost of recycling light water reactor (LWR) UNF was shown
in the study of back-end alternatives [2] where a range of recycling alternatives (i.e., RU-recycle and
RU/Pu recycle) was evaluated. Consequently, this report does not further investigate the use of UNF
from LWRs.
 Fast-spectrum commercial reactors target minimizing fuel costs, but this cannot result in a small
residual concentration of fissile material: a higher residual enrichment is required for the fast-spectrum
reactor to remain critical. As a result, fast reactor UNF will have a higher residual enrichment than
thermal reactor UNF in commercial power reactors. This report is focused on recycling of RU, so the
residual U-235 content is the only fissile isotope being recycled with Pu-239 and Pu-241 being sent to the
waste stream. For HALEU-fueled fast reactors, the residual U-235 at discharge will be sensitive to the
design and will decline with increasing irradiation (advancements in fuel lifetime). This relationship
between irradiation time and residual U-235 means that the value of recycling only the RU depends on
how advanced the fuel and the reactor design are; the more advanced the fuel and reactor design (i.e., the

higher the fluence level), the less residual U-235 in the RU. The effect of fuel and reactor design on the
value of RU from fast reactors will be explored in this report.

 For current SFR technology, the residual enrichment in the RU is sufficiently large to allow for use in
an LWR without the need to re-enrich and would require down blending to produce fuel equivalent to the
current LWR fuel. About 1 ton or less of SFR UNF would need to be reprocessed to make 1 ton of LWR
fuel. Additionally, RU from SFR UNF could be re-enriched for use in SFRs (about 2 tons of SFR UNF
are needed to make 1 ton of SFR fuel).
 The viability, including the costs, of these fuel cycle options for the SFR UNF could be impacted by
the uranium isotopic composition of the UNF. This report explores the impact of U-232, -234, and -236
on the use of RU from SFR UNF. By estimating the impacts of these isotopes in this report, improved
estimates will be made of the material balances and resulting costs of the fuel cycle alternatives, including
recycling of the RU. From these results, the estimated value and system impacts will be assessed. To
inform on the potential value of recycling, the net cost of producing RU-derived fuel will be compared to
producing the equivalent fuel from ENU. However, cost is not the only factor of value that may be
considered when deciding to recycle. To inform on this, the impacts on the system, such as reduction in
NU requirements and spent nuclear fuel (SNF) disposed of, will be calculated.

1.1.1 Down Blending and Direct Use as PWR Fuel (FC #1)
 The first fuel cycle (FC #1) is the recovery of the uranium from the SFR UNF, down blending the RU
as necessary, and utilizing ERU fuel in an LWR. This fuel cycle is shown in Figure 1-1. If it is necessary

ENU. The LWR UNF produced from this RU-derived fuel will be stored temporarily in an independent
spent fuel storage installation (ISFSI), a consolidated storage facility (CSF), or then dispose in a
repository.

1.2 Experience with Recovered Uranium
 IAEA-TECDOC-152 “Management of Reprocessed Uranium Current Status and Future Prospects,”
provides a detailed summary of many of the issues and experience with the use of RU [3]. This
experience is from a range of reactors, with PWRs being a large part of this experience, but includes
MAGNOX and other reactor types. Nearly all the commercial experience is with enrichments well below
HALEU. This report focuses on the RU that will be generated by advanced fuels and reactors for which
there is very little to no experience.

1.4 Report Structure
 The report is organized to first evaluate the effects of the minor uranium isotopes in the RU and then
to utilize the understanding of the minor uranium isotopes to provide an assessment of the potential value
of RU recycle. There are additional sections to supplement and support these focuses of the report.
 Section 2 evaluates the quantity of U-232 (daughters increase dose) in the RU and the potential cost
impacts of that level of U-232. Section 3 evaluates U-234 and U-236 (neutron poison isotopes) and how
they will impact the required U-235 concentrations in RU-derived fuel. The estimates of U-232, U-234,
and U-236 and their impacts are incorporated into the material flows (required U-235 concentration in
RU-derived fuel) and recycling costs (increased costs associated with increased dose rates).
 Section 4 evaluates the cost for the equilibrium system for different fuel cycle alternatives in which
the RU from SFR UNF is recycled. Also evaluated in Section 4 are the impacts on the nuclear energy
system that result from using the RU-derived fuel instead of additional fuel produced from the enrichment
of additional NU. These impacts are potential sources of value not captured in the costs.
 The report focuses on RU recycling at equilibrium. Section 5 provides a short analysis of the
recycling of RU/TRU. Section 6 summarizes this report. Given the specific assumptions of this report,
there is a range of additional analyses to consider dynamic factors and other aspects not addressed in this
initial assessment of the potential value. Section 7 includes proposed future work. Appendix A-1 provides
the equilibrium unit costs utilized in the cost analysis. Appendix A-2 provides the material balances for
the equilibrium systems used to evaluate both the equilibrium costs and the impacts on the system from
incorporation of recycling.

 The U-232 concentration in UNF can be dependent on a variety of factors, such as the initial
concentration of impurities (e.g., thorium and neptunium) in the fuel, the initial enrichment, the neutron
energy spectrum, and the operation history [9].
 U-232 can pose challenges in the fuel fabrication process when using RU because of the 2.6 MeV
gamma ray emitted by one of its daughter products, thallium-208 (Tl-208). This high-energy gamma
poses a radiological hazard to workers. Therefore, fuel fabrication methods may need to be modified to
ensure worker safety. U-232 has a 69-year half-life, the longest half-life of its daughter products is 1.9
years (thorium-228, Th-228), and most daughter products have a half-life less than 4 days. Therefore, the
concentration of the Tl-208 that is produced as U-232 decays can be estimated based on the secular
equilibrium of the U-232 and the Th-228. Figure 2-1 shows the concentrations of U-232 and Th-228 as a
function of time, assuming an initial sample of 100 g of U-232. Using the Th-228 concentration as a
proxy for the Tl-208 concentration, the Tl-208 concentration will reach a maximum at about 10 years of
decay time. Therefore, one method to reduce the radiological hazard of the RU is to process the material
quickly, before Tl-208 can build up in the material. If the material cannot be processed in a short enough
time window, then additional radiological protection mechanisms must be added to the fabrication
facility, increasing the cost of the fabrication process.

3. U-234/U-236 in Recycle Uranium
 Uranium-234 is a naturally occurring isotope, produced from the decay of uranium-238:
 ) 𝑈→ 𝑇ℎ + 𝛼
                                       )       𝑇ℎ →    𝑃𝑎 + β + 𝜈̅
                                       )       𝑃𝑎 →    𝑈 + β + 𝜈̅
 This production method yields minimal quantities of U-234 because the half-life of U-238 is over 4
billion years. The U-234 from this production pathway is only present in NU, as the time scale for this
production pathway is far greater than the time scale of reactor operation.
 Uranium-234 is also produced in the reactor by production and decay of plutonium-238 (87.7 year
half-life):
 ) 𝑃𝑢 → 𝑈+ 𝛼
 Uranium-236 is not naturally occurring and is produced in a nuclear reactor through neutron capture
of U-235 or alpha decay of Pu-240.
 Both isotopes affect reactor physics by acting as neutron poisons in the fuel. Uranium-234 transmutes
into U-235, and increased concentrations of U-234 will flatten the reactivity curve of the fuel across an
operating cycle. Uranium-236 is a parasitic neutron absorber, however, and transmutes into U-237 which
decays to Np-237 with a 6.75 day half-life. To compensate for the additional neutron absorption in the U-
236, the U-235 enrichment of ERU is higher than that of ENU.
 Given that these isotopes impact on the reactor performance, there is confidence that the models used
to estimate these concentrations are accurate. Unlike U-232 that has no impact and is produced by
complex pathways, the concentration of these isotopes should be estimated accurately in current reactor
physics models.

3.2     Impact of U-234/U-236 in SFR
 The impact of the U-234 and U-236 in the ERU fuel requires estimates of the relative concentration
of the isotopes after they are re-enriched. When considering re-enrichment, U-234 is lighter than U-235
and U-236 is heavier. As a result, in the re-enriched fuel based on the gaseous diffusion technology, the
ratio of U-234/U-235 will increase while the ratio of U-236/U-235 will decrease. This requires more
complex enrichment models to solve for all the different isotopes in the RU. Given the high-level nature
of this study, a simple approximation was made that U-234/U-235/U-236 ratios would remain constant,
and the enrichment search would adjust the ratio of these three isotopes to U-238. This approach will
underestimate the U-234 and overestimate U-236 in the ERU. However, this approximation will have a
small impact on the overall uncertainty and should not affect the overall conclusion.
 Using this assumption, the REF_18months case was re-evaluated and the new enrichment calculated
for the same number of batches and cycle length as in [1]. The result was that the U-235 weight fraction
increased from 17.7% for ENU to 18.5% for the ERU, which is a 5% relative increase in U-235 to
account for the U-234 and U-236 neutron poisoning. This 5% relative increase was applied for all FC #2
cases. Given the large uncertainties in the unit costs, design assumptions, etc., a more accurate estimate
for all cases was deemed unnecessary.

4. Estimate the Potential Value of Recovered Uranium
 The value estimation performed in this report is primarily an analysis of cost at system equilibrium. It
does not consider any other sources of value that may be derived from using one alternative over the
other. For example, RU will likely be a purely domestic source of nuclear fuel whereas the natural
uranium derived fuel may require significant international sources in the supply chain which has
significant value not accounted for in this study.
 There will be value in recycling if the cost of producing the RU-derived fuel is less than the cost of
producing the equivalent fuel derived from natural uranium. The magnitude of the difference in cost
indicates the net value in using the lower cost alternatives.
 For this report, the focus is on the long-term system equilibrium costs of the various fuel cycles
considered. Some of the important assumptions of this analysis are:
  A relatively large fleet of fast-spectrum commercial power reactors producing sufficient UNF to
 supply the commercial scale recycle systems,
  Recycling facilities will be on a commercial scale,
  Sufficient demand will exist for RU-derived fuels such that capacities will be nearly fully
 utilized. This includes enrichment facilities that will accept the RU for re-enrichment, fabrication
 facilities to fabricate the RU fuel, and all other parts of the fuel cycle that will handle RU.
These are important assumptions that will not hold true when systems are initially deployed and grow to
scale. As a result, this approach does not account for the transition costs (underutilization, development,
etc.) associated with putting the system in place and growing to a highly utilized commercial scale
system. The assumptions in this work are likely to yield a near-theoretical minimum cost for the fuel
cycles, which identifies where potential benefits may exist. Promising alternatives will need to be
evaluated in more detail to capture the transition costs not considered here. The method can easily be
modified to add any other sources of value to adjust these costs.
 The first step is to identify all components in the fuel cycle which have significant contributions to the
cost of the fuel. A material balance was performed to provide the units required at equilibrium for each
step. The cost is estimated with the equilibrium unit cost multiplied by the number of units and summed
over the fuel cycle. These estimates can be normalized to the number of kg U of fuel ($/kg U) or the
energy generated ($/MWh).
 To cover a range of potential SFR UNF that would result from SFR deployments in the near term,
this analysis used fuel and reactor designs based on current technology through a series of advances in
fuel and reactor design. Six examples of SFR fuel and reactor designs were taken from a study on deep
burnup fuels [1], shown in Table 3-1.
 For this study, the primary figure of merit is the levelized fuel cost at equilibrium (LFCAE). For the
recycling alternatives the net LFCAE is calculated. Net LFCAE means that costs avoided by recycling
(e.g., the UNF disposal costs) are included as cost savings in the cost of the RU-derived fuel. The avoided
costs that reduce the LFCAE continue to be costs included in LFCAE of the fuel that is not being recycled
(e.g., the LWR UNF in FC #1).
 Accounting for the costs this way keeps the total system cost zero. The total system costs are the costs
of the HALEU fuel produced from the NU plus the costs of recycling SFR RU when it is recycled. These
costs that are avoided by recycling and included as a negative in the net cost of recycling, are still
included as a positive in the cost of HALEU fuel being recycled. This is the once-through fuel cycle cost
and is not changed by recycling. Then those avoided costs included as a negative value in the RU-derived
fuel and cancel out when the two are summed to calculate the total system cost for producing the HALEU

August 31, 2025                                                                                             29

fuel from NU and then recycling it once. Without accounting for the avoided costs, the value of recycling
will be underestimated.
 The major avoided cost from recycling is the disposal of the SNF. Another avoided cost is the cost for
deconversion and DU disposal when the RU is down blended with DU.
 The net LFCAE of the RU alternative when the UNF is recycled is then compared with LFCAE of
producing same fuel using NU and directly disposing of that UNF. The difference represents the value of
utilizing the lower cost option over the higher cost option.
 The LFCAE does not include the effect of discounting that results from costs occurring at different
times. Given the large uncertainties in unit costs and other assumptions, the small difference that would
result from discounting combined with the large number of assumptions needed does not provide any
improvement to this preliminary assessment of potential value of recycling. Cost discounting will be
important when actual facilities and the appropriate details are available to create realistic business
models. At this stage, it is an unnecessary complication.
 The unit costs at equilibrium come primarily from the Cost Basis Report (CBR) [17]. However, some
of the systems modeled in the CBR are not accurate representations, so additional assumptions and
approximations are made to provide a complete set of unit costs. All unit costs and assumptions are in
Appendix A-1.
4.1 Recovered Uranium Impact on Unit Costs
 There are two effects on cost from using RU: the increase of U-235 enrichment to account for the
neutron poisoning effect of U-234 and U-236 and additional radiation protection measures to protect
workers from larger doses if there is sufficient U-232 in the RU. The first item will impact on the material
balances, but it will not impact on the unit costs of the fuel cycle because these isotopes do not have
significant radiological impacts. The second item will impact unit costs of the fuel cycle because the
additional radiation protection measures may change facility designs, change process procedures, or
require more equipment. In this section, the unit fuel fabrication cost using the RU from SFR UNF was
calculated using the fuel fabrication cost using RU from LWR UNF. The estimated costs based on the
recycling of LWR UNF should represent an upper bound of the unit cost impacts of using SFR RU
because the U-232 content in the LWR UNF is higher than that in the FR UNF.
 Table 4-1 reports some of the fuel fabrication costs from Module D of the Cost Basis Report (CBR)
[17]. The reported unit costs in the CBR for LWR fuel fabrication from RU (referred to as R-LEU in the
CBR) are about 10% larger than the costs for fabrication with ENU (referred to as V-LEU in the CBR).
The values reported in CBR Module D1-1b were applied to the PWR fuel fabrication from SFR UNF
downblending (FC #1). In the CBR for the SFR HALEU metallic fuel using ERU, costs were scaled by
similar proportions as the fabrication of ERU PWR fuel relative to NU-derived PWR fuel (1.08-1.10).
This work does not assume any impact on the unit costs of other steps of the fuel cycle that handle RU,
which includes the conversion to UF6, enrichment, and transportation of the various materials. These
assumptions seem to be reasonable for the estimated levels of U-232 in the RU. For sensitivity, the
fabrication costs for NU-derived fuels are also used to provide the minimum impact that might occur if
the U-232 estimates are overly conservative and there are no unit cost impacts from the U-232
concentration.
 There may be some hesitance to accept RU at facilities that have not previously processed it. If a
facility accepts RU, the material will likely contaminate the system and carry over when handling NU-
derived materials. For fabricators who do not want to accept RU, if NU is enriched in a facility that has
also enriched RU, it may make meeting the standards for NU-derived fuels difficult. The standard for
UF6 enriched to less than 5% is 0.0001 μg/g U-235 for the NU-derived fuels versus 0.05 μg/g U for the
RU derived fuels (See Table 1-2), which is a factor of 500 difference.

4.2 Down Blending and Direct Use as PWR Fuel (FC #1)
 FC #1 in this work is an alternative fuel cycle to the use of ENU fuel in a PWR. Therefore, the cost of
FC #1 is compared with the cost of a once-through fuel cycle with ENU in a PWR. Three of the example
SFR UNFs have RU with high enough residual U-235 that do not require re-enrichment to produce fuel
equivalent to the 4.2% ENU PWR fuel for 50 MWd/kg burnup. The other three examples would require
re-enrichment to achieve a burnup of 50 MWd/kg. Re-enrichment to produce PWR fuel was not
considered in this report. The SFR UNF RU can be down blended with DU or NU, with DU and NU
having different U-235 weight fractions. The different weight fractions will impact the RU to down
blending material ratios and thus impact the costs of the fuel cycle.
4.2.1 Down Blend with DU

for the 50 MWd/kg PWR example). For those examples, the RU can be used by down blending to
produce 4.21% equivalent enrichment which allows irradiation to 50 MWd/kg. The ERU fuel should be
able to be used interchangeably with the 4.21% ENU. For these cases, DU (0.25% tails enrichment) was
blended with the RU recovered from the SFR UNF to produce the same equivalent enrichment using the
polynomial defined in Section 3.1. Table 4-2 shows the information about these different fuel options.

SFR RU decreases to produce the same equivalent enrichment in the PWR fuel. The increase in the RU to

4.2.3 Impacts of Using SFR RU by Down Blend to PWR Fuel
 The focus thus far is the impact of FC #1 on cost, but there are other impacts that result from
recycling the SFR RU. For example, reductions in NU will be needed because the use of the RU reduces
the amount of ENU fuel that will be required. This section quantifies some of these additional impacts,
based on the recycling of HALEU SFR UNF from one GWe of SFR capacity. Table 4-3 reports the SFR
RU characteristics from all six SFR UNF examples and the effects on material requirements relative to a
fuel cycle based on ENU.
 The net impact on the system results from two primary sources. The first is the avoidance of
requirements to produce ENU. For the assumed tails assay (0.25%), it takes 8.6 MT of NU to fabricate 1
MTU of ENU PWR. Producing PWR fuel from SFR RU avoids needing that amount of NU. It also
avoids the enrichment required and the DU that is produced in the enrichment process. The second impact
is the quantity of SFR UNF recycled, because recycling avoids the direct disposal of that amount of SFR
UNF. However, recovering only the uranium in the UNF means that all fission products and TRU
elements are disposed of in the HLW stream, so all these functions need to be added to the system.
 The overall impact on the system will depend on the size of the SFR fleet and the resulting amount of
UNF that is produced and the amount of that is recycled. If a large fleet of HALEU-fueled SFRs are
deployed there is the potential for a larger reduction in NU requirements, enrichment requirements, and
DU produced because there is more SFR UNF than can be recycled into PWR fuel. At equilibrium, the
amount of PWR electricity that can be supplied by SFR RU would be about 0.7 GW-yr for each GW-yr of

more detailed value of the cooling time will require detailed modeling of the recycle system and the heat
and dose limits, but this minimum cooling time is likely in the range of 5 to 10 years. The result of these
dynamic effects is a complex relationship as the SFR fleet grows because the amount of PWR fuel that
can be produced at a point in time is determined by the size of the SFR fleet in the past, when the SFR
fleet was smaller. This complexity requires the use of dynamic systems models which are utilized by the
Systems Analysis and Integration Campaign to address these important dynamic effects.

higher than that of ENU because of the additional neutron poison effects from U-234 and U-236 in the

5. RU/TRU Recycle for Use as SFR Fuel
 The focus of this report is on the reuse of the RU. However, the SFR UNF will have a large content of
TRU, primarily plutonium. As a result of the physics, Pu-239 and Pu-241 are more valuable in a fast
reactor than U-235. Additionally, as fuel technology and reactor design advances (i.e., fuel burnup and
cycle time increase), the amount of plutonium in the UNF will increase while the residual U-235
decreases. This trend can be seen in the example SFR UNF compositions in Table 1-1.
 The cost difference between recycling RU only and the RU with plutonium is not likely to be that
large. Much of the cost of reprocessing comes from processes outside of the separation chemistry: the
handling of the highly radioactive UNF, receiving the casks, unloading the assemblies, chopping them,
etc. Only some of the chemistry in the UNF reprocessing might have some cost saving when only
separating the uranium from the UNF. Also, if recovering only uranium the resulting HLW will have a
high content of plutonium which could affect HLW production and disposal costs. Also, recycling of the
plutonium in the form of RU/TRU recycling allows for the use of electrochemical reprocessing for reuse
in fast reactors. Recycling the RU and plutonium is assumed to use the integral electrochemical and
remote fuel fabrication system, which recycles all TRU isotopes and not just the plutonium with the RU.
A short study was conducted that considered this RU/TRU recycled back to the SFR as an alternative (FC
#3 in Section 1.1.3). The estimated ratio of RU/TRU to produce fuel equivalent to the original HALEU
was estimated from a previous study that looked at transition from HALEU fuel to equilibrium RU/TRU
recycle [16].
 All TRU is fabricated into the RU/TRU fuel. Only the amount of RU needed to get the equivalent
enrichment of the original HALEU is recycled with the TRU and fabricated into the RU/TRU fuel. To
achieve the same equivalent enrichment of the original HALEU fuel, some cases will produce some
excess of RU while other cases will require down blending. The excess RU is assumed to be disposed in
this work. The amount of plutonium that is equivalent to 1 kg of U-235 in the fuel is sensitive to the
reactor design and some other factors. For the high Pu-239/Pu ratio resulting from recycling of SFR UNF,
it is expected that the Pu/U-235 ratio is 0.7 [16].
 Table 5-1 summarizes the recycling of the RU/TRU from the six example SFR UNF cases. For the
RU recycle, as the irradiation of the SFR fuel increased, the residual U-235 decreases, reducing the value
of the recycling. However, the plutonium content increases with irradiation. Also, for the larger cores
with reduced enrichment, the breeding ratio increases eventually to the point where the recovered
RU/TRU needs to be down blended with TRU to produce TRU-derived fuel equivalent to the original
HALEU. In the UNF, about 95%-97% of the TRU is plutonium, with nearly the rest being neptunium and
a small fraction of americium. As a result, even full recycling of the TRU should be manageable in a
glove box if sufficiently low levels of fission product carryover [16], meaning that there are no large
impacts on fabrication unit costs from additional radiation protection measures.

values represent reductions in cost and that there is value in recycling the RU/TRU in terms of the

6. Summary
 This report examines the benefits and challenges associated with recycling the recovered uranium
(RU) from used nuclear fuel (UNF) discharged from a once-through sodium-cooled fast reactor (SFR)
with high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU). The benefits of recycling uranium in the UNF are
caused by the relatively high residual U-235 content, but the benefits are also influenced by U-232, -234,
and -236 concentrations. The U-232 concentration affects radiation protection measures required at
facilities because of the 2.6 MeV gamma released in the decay chain of U-232. The U-234 and U-236
isotopes act as neutron poisons, which require higher U-235 concentrations to have the same performance
(in particular, cycle length and burnup) as enriched uranium using natural uranium (ENU).
 In commercial power reactors, minimizing refueling costs is an important objective. Meeting this
objective results in thermal-spectrum commercial reactor UNF having low residual fissile concentrations
even if the fresh fuel was HALEU. Examples of advanced thermal-spectrum commercial reactor designs
of PWR with enrichments up to 8% and a pebble-bed HTGR with an enrichment of 16% have residual
enrichments of 0.7%-0.8%. A large cost component for recycling is the ratio of UNF reprocessed to the
fuel produced from the recycled material. Even before accounting for the effects of U-234/U-236 and the
U-235 that ends up in the depleted uranium stream, for HALEU fuel with an enrichment of 7%-16%, with
0.8% U-235 in the RU, it would take 9-20 kg of UNF to make one kg of RU-derived fuel.
 Minimizing refueling costs is also important for fast-spectrum commercial reactors. However, a
higher fissile content is required for the fast reactor to remain critical. This results in fast-spectrum
commercial reactor UNF having a higher residual fissile content than thermal reactor UNF. The higher
residual fissile content in fast-spectrum reactor UNF makes it more appealing for recycling. For recycling
RU only, the amount of residual U-235 will determine how much fuel can be made from the RU. A range
of examples of SFRs were considered that represent advancements in the fuel, from current state-of-the-
art technology to significant improvements in fuel lifetime, which will reduce the levelized cost of the
HALEU fuel. The residual U-235 in the UNF decreases as the fuel advances. The SFR UNF examples
have a range of 8.4% to 2.6% g U-235 per g of UNF. For the highest residual RU, down-blending the RU
from the SFR to make fuel for a PWR would only require 0.6 kg of SFR UNF to make one kg of RU-
derived fuel for a PWR. Re-enrichment of the RU-derived fuel to the equivalent enrichment of the
original HALEU ENU requires between 2.4 to 6.9 kg of UNF to make one kg of RU-derived fuel.
 This report focused on the recycling of the RU from SFR UNF. The fuel cycles considered in this
report include both down-blending to make PWR fuel (FC #1) and re-enrichment for reuse in the same
SFR (FC #2). Also, FC #3 for RU/TRU recycling. For FC #1 it takes between 0.6 to 1.0 kg of SFR UNF
reprocessed to make 1 kg of PWR fuel. For the re-enrichment fuel cycle, it takes reprocessing between
2.4 to 5.7 kg of UNF to make 1 kg of re-enriched HALEU SFR fuel.
 U-232 is present at the part-per-billion level and is not important for reactor performance. Therefore,
accurate modeling of the production of U-232 has not been a priority. However, the decay chain of U-232
contains a 2.6 MeV gamma emission, and an increased presence of U-232 affects the radiation protection
measures required. A literature review was conducted on the production pathways and measured
concentration in different sources of RU. There is significant uncertainty on the U-232 concentration in
UNF because of the production pathways, including significant contributions of low-level concentration
of uranium daughters that may carry over as impurity in the fuel. Despite the large uncertainty in the U-
232 concentrations, the RU from SFR UNF should have similar or lower U-232 concentrations than prior
experience fabricating ERU for LWRs. At this level, there is likely just a small cost premium associated
with the fabrication of RU fuels.
 U-234 and U-236 are neutron poisons that affect reactor performance. Accounting for larger
concentrations of these uranium isotopes in RU-derived fuels means that fresh fuel must be at a higher
enrichment level than ENU design for the same cycle length and burnup. The effect of U-234 and U-236
in the SFR was estimated for a single example SFR UNF composition by calculating the equivalent

42                                                                                   August 31, 2025

enrichment for the RU-derived fuel. The ratio between the RU-derived fuel enrichment and the ENU
enrichment was used to scale the enrichment of the other five SFR UNF compositions. The example using
ENU had an enrichment of 17.7% and the RU-derived equivalent enrichment was 18.5%. Then, the
equivalent enrichments of other cases were calculated by the same scaling up factor of 1.04 (=18.5/17.7).
If there is interest in re-enrichment, more analysis may be desirable, but the effect is a relatively small
fraction for the higher residual enrichment RU examples.
 The LFCAE was assessed for the recycling of the range of SFR UNF, accounting for the changes in
the equivalent enrichment and the higher fabrication cost of the RU fuel due to the presence of the minor
uranium isotopes. The net LFCAE of RU-derived fuel is included as a credit in the costs for those costs
which are avoided as a result of recycling. The primary avoided cost is the disposal of the UNF that is
required in a once-through fuel cycle. The LFCAE for FC #1 and FC #2 and a once-through fuel cycle for
PWRs and SFRs were determined as a function of residual U-235 concentration. Comparing the once-
through fuel cycles and the alternatives (i.e., FC #1 compared with ENU for PWRs, and FC #2 compared
with ENU for SFRs) yielded a breakeven point in the U-235 concentration at which the fuel cycle options
had equivalent LFCAE. This assessment considered the large uncertainties in the future costs of all
aspects of these nuclear fuel cycles. The results show that FC #1 and FC #2 had a net LFCAE equal to the
LFCAE for the once through options if the residual U-235 concentration was in the range of 6.0% to 7.5%
U-235 and will have a lower net LFCAE above that level, depending on the cost assumptions. Near-term
SFR UNF is expected to have a residual U-235 of just over 8%, which suggests that FC #1 and #2 are
likely to have lower LFCAE than the once-through options. Over time, fuel and core design advances are
expected to produce UNF with lower residual U-235, causing the value of the RU to decrease and
potentially fall below the breakeven point.
 In addition, this report briefly discusses other impacts to the recycling of the U/TRU from the SFR
UNF to make U/TRU SFR fuel. The entire range of advanced SFR fuel showed a lower net LFCAE than
the original HALEU ENU fuel. Recycling the U/TRU to make new SFR fuel required the recycling of
between 0.8 and 1.7 kg of UNF to be reprocessed per kg of U/TRU SFR fuel produced. All TRU is
recycled with only process losses of plutonium and minor actinides ending up in the HLW as opposed to
large quantities of plutonium in the HLW when only the RU is recycled. There may be additional benefits
from reprocessing the U/TRU material over only the RU, such as… that were not explored in this work.
 There are significant challenges associated with getting to the equilibrium state as assumed in this
report. This requires the application of dynamic tools and analysis of specific scenarios. While most of
these will increase the cost of recycling, once fully deployed and the capital has been recovered, the
marginal cost will likely be significantly reduced allowing for the economic recycle of lower quality UNF
and/or producing lower value RU-derived fuels because of the lower cost after the capital costs have been
recovered.

7. Future Work
 The results of this study show that under the conditions of a fully deployed commercial scale recycle
system there is potential value in the recycling of the UNF from HALEU-fueled SFRs. There are many
assumptions embedded in this analysis that need to be analyzed before any decisions can be made. Future
work is needed to analyze these assumptions and improve estimates accounting for the dynamic effects of
transitioning from a non-existing system to a large commercial system.
 This analysis assumes equilibrium fuel cycles and fuel cycle costs. All costs, material flows, fleet
size, etc., are assumed to be constant over time. Reality will be dynamic with changes in all important
aspects of the fuel cycle. This analysis will require the use of dynamic system modeling tools to evaluate
the evolution of the fast reactor fleet and resulting UNF discharged as a function of time. The fully
dynamic evaluation can help in evaluating optimal times to deploy reprocessing and fabrication facilities
based on UNF discharge rates and facility costs. This dynamic evaluation can also be used to evaluate
system expansion rates.
 The once-through HALEU ENU fuel cycle option will also have dynamic behavior which must be
accounted for in a complete comparison between the fuel cycle options. The facilities for the HALEU
ENU fuel cycle will be fully deployed and operating well in advance of the availability of sufficient UNF
from HALEU fuel to deploy commercial recycling. Once the capital is paid off, this HALEU ENU may
have a lower marginal cost. Estimating the market price for a dynamic system will be a factor in the use
of HALEU ENU and fuels produced from recycled materials. There are many dynamic factors that will
come into play such as the capital investment and timing of that investment which can be informed on by
dynamic system studies.
 The cost of disposal of UNF and HLW will be an important factor in the net cost of recycling. With
the wide range of initial enrichment and burnups, there is a wide range of UNF compositions. The
assumption was that they all cost the same to dispose, which is probably not accurate. For the HLW, the
recycling of RU only puts a large amount of plutonium into the HLW. This was assumed to have the
same impact on cost on a per kg basis as the fission products. A better understanding of the cost
dependencies of the UNF and HLW for both transportation and disposal (e.g., impacts of sodium
bonding, ferritic steel cladding, metal fuels, etc.) will improve the estimates. Improving the granularity of
the UNF and HLW transportation and disposal will improve the estimates of the value of recycling.
