From biomimicry to clinical actionability: rethinking high-shear thrombosis as a mechanobiological system
Authors: Marcus Vinicius Batista da Silva, Christopher A Bresette, Viviana Clavería
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of reviewArterial thrombosis remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, while its mechanistic understanding and clinical management remain limited. INTRODUCTION: THE HIGH-SHEAR CHALLENGE

Arterial thrombosis is increasingly recognized as a dynamic mechanobiological process where fluid forces dictate biochemical outcomes. Bridging the gap between lab-based models and clinical practice requires a fundamental shift toward systems that capture these high-shear dynamics.

The clinical burden of arterial thrombosis and the mechanosensitive role of von Willebrand factor

Arterial thrombosis is a leading cause of myocardial infarction and stroke [1]. Its mechanistic basis remains incompletely understood, particularly under conditions of elevated and dynamic shear stress that fundamentally differ from the stasis-driven framework of Virchow's triad [2▪▪,3▪▪]. In these environments, thrombus formation is dominated by platelet-rich aggregates and governed by von Willebrand factor (vWF) [2▪▪,4]. As a mechanosensitive glycoprotein, vWF transitions from a globular to an elongated conformation under high wall shear rate (from 3000/s up to 400 000/s), exposing binding domains A1 essential for platelet adhesion [4]. Recent evidence shows that shear-induced platelet aggregation (SIPA) produces thrombi with hierarchical vWF–platelet architectures that are mechanically stiffer and stronger than fibrin-dominated clots, mimicking the morphology of thrombi retrieved from stroke patients [2▪▪,5].

The ‘translational stagnation’: why impressive biomimetic models have yet to change clinical practice

Despite the sophistication of microfluidic platforms, a ‘translational stagnation’ persists [6,7▪▪]. Conventional platelet function tests (e.g. light transmission aggregometry, VerifyNow, platelet function analyzer), remain largely static or agonist-driven, failing to capture the shear-dependent mechanisms [3▪▪,7▪▪,8] and patient-specific variability [9] that govern clinical risk. Furthermore, nonphysiological shear stress (75–175 Pa) in medical devices can paradoxically trigger both thrombosis and bleeding via receptor shedding – phenomena poorly explained by reductionist models [10].

The increasing complexity of biomimetic systems also introduces hurdles in reproducibility and standardization [6]. To transform these models into actionable tools, the field must advance beyond descriptive biomimicry by: capturing the multidimensional and dynamic nature of shear stress, integrating mechanobiological complexity into standardized and reproducible assay frameworks, and facilitating targeted modulation of the thrombotic process through integrated computational modeling and robotic intervention [11]. These pillars form the framework for translating recent experimental innovations into precision medicine for vascular disease.

RECENT ADVANCES IN FLOW-BASED MODELS

Microfluidic thrombosis models have evolved from simplified straight channels into sophisticated ‘vessel-on-a-chip’ platforms that integrate three-dimensional geometries, endothelialization, and physiologically relevant injury models [7▪▪,12,13▪]. These architectures demonstrate that thrombus initiation is driven by local hemodynamic disturbances, specifically at bifurcations and stenotic regions, where spatial variations in flow velocity create intrinsic thrombotic hotspots [14,15]. Beyond geometry, current ‘artery-on-a-chip’ models increasingly incorporate multicellular constructs and mechanical cues such as cyclic stretch and matrix stiffness, offering a more integrative representation of the arterial microenvironment compared to acellular systems [13▪,16]. However, this increasing complexity necessitates a transition toward standardized testing protocols and scalable manufacturing to move beyond manual prototyping [6,17▪▪].

From prototyping to industrial scalability

The clinical translation of microfluidic research is fundamentally limited by the reliance on polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS). Despite its rapid prototyping advantages, PDMS suffers from small-molecule absorption, hydrophobic recovery, and poor compatibility with mass production [18,19]. Recent efforts to bridge this gap focus on industry-standard substrates, such as cyclic olefin copolymers (COC) and polystyrene, which support high-throughput manufacturing via injection molding and hot embossing [6,19]. These materials satisfy the rigorous material standards required for medical-grade devices, particularly regarding surface-induced hemocompatibility and controlled protein adsorption [20]. Transitioning to these scalable platforms has enabled the development of automated point-of-care (POC) devices that utilize small blood volumes to detect patient-specific thrombotic responses and antiplatelet therapy efficacy [21▪▪,22]. Strategic simplified approaches, such as coating-free glass assays, further reduce experimental variability by focusing on shear-driven aggregation as a standalone diagnostic metric [23].

The multidimensional shear landscape

Contemporary models have shifted from steady-state shear assumptions to capturing the dynamic, multidimensional shear environments found in stenotic vessels and mechanical circulatory support devices. Platelets and vWF are highly sensitive to temporal flow gradients; for instance, vWF exhibits a fast extension time constant (0.19 ± 0.04 s) but a significantly slower recoil (~1 s), a mismatch that promotes aggregation under high-frequency pulsatility [24]. Furthermore, transient exposure to peak shear (e.g. >5000/s) for as little as 68.8 ms is sufficient to ‘prime’ platelets for stable aggregation, even when followed by lower shear conditions [25,26]. High-vorticity conditions (expansion angles of 120o–150o with vorticity magnitudes reaching up to 5000/s), in particular, can produce thrombi up to four times larger than low-vorticity (expansion angles of 30°–60° and vorticity values around 10/s) environments, showing a strong correlation (r = 0.6698) between vorticity magnitude and thrombus volume [3▪▪]. Consequently, capturing these spatial and temporal shear gradients is now recognized as a requirement for accurate thrombosis modeling and the interpretation of flow-based assays [3▪▪].

BEYOND VON WILLEBRAND FACTOR: EXPANDING THE MECHANOBIOLOGICAL MAP AND RARE MECHANOSENSITIVE PATHWAYS

While advances in flow-based modeling have improved the replication of vascular hemodynamics, they also highlight that thrombosis cannot be fully explained by physical parameters alone. Beyond canonical vWF-centered mechanisms, recent studies have revealed additional mechanosensitive pathways that contribute to thrombus formation, reinforcing that thrombosis emerges from a broader and more complex mechanobiological network. Recent computational frameworks have expanded this network by implementing nonlinear, shear-dependent kinetic rates to model platelet adhesion and cohesion beyond critical thresholds (2000/s), capturing how extreme mechanical forces directly alter molecular bond lifetimes [27]. Furthermore, mesoscale hydrodynamic simulations incorporating explicitly resolved blood cells and mechanosensitive proteins demonstrate that the physical stability, structural growth, and thrombo-embolization behavior of early-stage clots are bounded by a strict balance of internal stresses and hydrodynamic drag forces, independent of initial biochemical stabilization [28].

A compelling example of this expansion is the identification of SIPA mechanisms that operate independently of conventional vWF-mediated adhesion. While it is well established that arterial blood flow can be occluded by transverse cables of vWF and platelets under high shear rates [2▪▪], recent evidence suggests that certain exogenous factors can bypass this classical pathway. Specifically, adenoviral vectors used in vaccination have been shown to directly induce platelet aggregation under arterial flow conditions through biomechanically activated integrin αIIbβ3 pathways [29]. In this novel biophysical mechanism, the shear-induced mechanical force facilitates direct binding between the viral particles and the activated integrin, independent of the GPIb-vWF axis. Parallel to these novel pathways, therapeutic modulation of vWF-mediated pathways is also being explored in thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and vWD, with targeted strategies capable of selectively modulating shear-dependent platelet aggregation without broadly affecting hemostasis [4,30,31]. Pathological and physiological variability further shapes mechanosensitive responses. Differences in vWF structure, multimer distribution, and biomechanical properties can alter platelet recruitment kinetics under flow, even when static assays appear similar [32]. Likewise, conditions such as hypertension and aging enhance mechanosensitive signaling, including the GPIbα–integrin αIIbβ3 axis, intensifying thrombus formation under shear stress [33▪].

Together, these findings demonstrate that shear-mediated thrombosis involves interacting mechanosensitive pathways rather than a single dominant mechanism. vWF–platelet aggregates can also undergo cycles of formation and dissociation under changing shear conditions [34], highlighting the dynamic and reversible nature of thrombus formation. This complexity underscores the need for models that integrate molecular, cellular, and hemodynamic factors to better capture thrombotic behavior and guide therapeutic targeting.

SOLVING THE STANDARDIZATION GAP

Despite major advances in microfluidic thrombosis models, their clinical translation remains limited by insufficient standardization of device geometries, surface coatings, and testing protocols [7▪▪,20]. In this context, standardization refers to the harmonization of critical parameters, such as channel dimensions, material hemocompatibility, and blood handling procedures, to ensure that results are comparable across different laboratories and platforms [20]. Current efforts focus on integrating automation in both hardware (e.g. precise fluid handling and automated sampling) and software (e.g. AI-driven image analysis powered by Deep Learning architectures for real-time feature extraction [36▪]) to transform complex flow-based assays into actionable tools [8,35▪▪,36▪].

These advancements aim to improve assay robustness, allowing clinicians to distinguish true pathological signatures from technical noise and procedural inconsistencies [7▪▪,23].

Automation in platelet function testing and artificial intelligence-assisted thrombus phenotyping

Importantly, this gap is not due to a lack of technological capability, but rather to the absence of commercially available assays that translate mechanistic insights into clinically interpretable metrics. Available platelet function tests often rely on static or agonist-driven conditions and fail to reproduce the complex shear-dependent environment of arterial thrombosis, leading to a lack of predictive value for thrombotic risk [7▪▪,8].

To address this, there is a growing shift toward automation in both hardware and software. Hardware automation, including integrated micro-robotic fluid handling and precise pressure control, enables the controlled replication of physiologically relevant flow conditions while reducing operator-dependent error [35▪▪,37▪▪]. Complementarily, software automation through machine learning and artificial intelligence (specifically leveraging convolutional neural networks for pixel-level image segmentation and random forest algorithms for classification) allows for the multiparametric characterization of thrombus formation. This approach enables the automated extraction of high-dimensional data such as growth kinetics, structural organization, and spatial heterogeneity [35▪▪,36▪,38].

These advances have also contributed to the concept of thrombus phenotyping, where formation is treated as a complex biological process rather than a binary outcome. By integrating computational modeling with patient-specific variables, artificial intelligence-assisted systems can identify disease-specific signatures, such as those associated with hypertension or aging, that are undetectable through conventional analysis [35▪▪]. Furthermore, the emergence of digital twins offers the potential to simulate patient-specific vascular responses, enabling personalized risk assessment and therapeutic optimization [38].

Addressing intra-patient variability and intra-assay reproducibility for point-of-care devices

A major challenge in thrombosis diagnostics is the substantial variability observed both between and within patients. While inter-patient variability reflects genetic and clinical diversity, intra-patient variability, driven by inflammation, metabolic shifts, or pharmacological treatments, represents a critical target for monitoring. For example, microfluidic assays have demonstrated increased sensitivity for detecting residual disease activity in patients with TTP who appear to be in clinical remission, providing a tool for more precise longitudinal assessment [39].

POC devices offer a promising strategy to address these challenges by enabling rapid and repeated testing. Recent platforms have successfully reproduced high-shear thrombosis using small blood volumes while maintaining sensitivity to antiplatelet therapies [20]. However, reproducibility across POC devices remains a hurdle, often complicated by differences in device materials. For instance, the use of PDMS or other polymers can significantly influence protein adsorption and platelet adhesion, necessitating standardized, material-agnostic protocols [20,23].

Furthermore, the material strength of the resulting thrombi, which can be up to seven-fold stronger in shear-induced conditions compared to standard coagulation clots, must be integrated into diagnostic endpoints to ensure the reproducibility of clinical readouts [40]. Future POC systems will likely move beyond isolated metrics, integrating indicators of platelet function, thrombus structure, and flow dynamics to provide a comprehensive and reproducible assessment of thrombotic risk.

EMERGING INTERVENTIONS: MICROROBOTS AS THE NEXT FRONTIER

Microrobots transition thrombosis research from passive observation to active intervention. Unlike passive microfluidic models, these active probes quantify thrombus resistance and cohesion, functional endpoints that complement the known tensile strength of vWF–platelet architectures or can navigate vascular geometries and apply localized mechanical forces [37▪▪,40,41,42▪]. Swarm-based systems further extend this capability, operating under high-shear conditions within complex networks without disrupting global flow dynamics [41,43].

Closed-loop systems and targeted therapy

Microrobots serve as the interventional arm of closed-loop management, linking microfluidic phenotyping and digital-twin predictions to adaptive therapy [35▪▪]. By providing superior spatial precision over systemic thrombolysis, these platforms enable localized mechanical disruption and targeted drug delivery [41,43]. Current strategies include tPA-anchored nanorobots [44], reconfigurable microswarms for ultrasound-guided thrombolysis [45], enzyme-magnetite swarms [46], platelet-derived nanomotors [47], and C-shaped systems for navigating interrupted flow [48]. This synergy of localized enzymatic fibrinolysis and mechanical interaction allows for patient-specific treatment optimization, where ex-vivo clot phenotypes guide robot selection, dosing, and actuation parameters.

Despite their promise, clinical translation requires overcoming challenges in tracking, clot penetration, and embolic safety within physiological flow environments. Near-term implementation is most feasible in catheter-accessible or locally confined vasculature, where magnetic actuation and ex-vivo screening can be integrated into adaptive therapeutic loops.

FUTURE PERSPECTIVES: ACHIEVING CLINICAL ACTIONABILITY

Clinical actionability will require combined thrombosis platforms to connect phenotype, prediction, and intervention outputs. Microfluidics can quantify patient-specific thrombus formation and lysis under defined shear; digital twins can predict treatment response; microrobots could provide localized thrombolysis or embolization when systemic therapy is insufficient or unsafe. The roadmap already identifies vascular microrobots for embolization and thrombolysis, including magnetic swarms, microfiberbots, microgels, tPA-loaded nanorobots, and platelet-derived nanomotors [44–51].

Regulatory and technical hurdles for microrobotic and microfluidic integration

Clinical translation remains constrained not only by device performance but also by the persistent mismatch between rapid experimental innovation and evolving regulatory frameworks, particularly regarding the lack of standardized biomarkers and validation criteria for platelet dysfunction and thrombotic risk assessment [22]. In microfluidics, increasing device complexity improves physiological fidelity by incorporating multicellular interactions, pathological geometries, and hemodynamic realism; however, this same complexity introduces fabrication variability and operational heterogeneity that complicate reproducibility, interlaboratory standardization, and large-scale validation [6,33▪].

For microrobotic systems, the central challenge extends beyond navigation itself toward achieving controlled and predictable action within blood under physiological flow conditions. Microrobotic thrombolysis platforms must demonstrate efficient clot targeting, thrombus penetration, lysis efficacy, real-time trackability, safe biodegradation or clearance, and minimization of off-target fibrinolysis or embolic fragmentation [44–48,51]. Conversely, microrobotic hemostatic or embolization systems must ensure stable and localized occlusion without migration, unintended vascular blockage, distal ischemia, or excessive procoagulant activation [49,50,52]. More broadly, ensuring safety against immune recognition, vascular obstruction, and adverse blood-material interactions remains a critical prerequisite for in-vivo implementation [41,43].

Microfluidic thrombosis assays face a complementary translational challenge: physiological relevance must be balanced against robustness and reproducibility. Accordingly, validation strategies should move beyond simple endpoint measurements and incorporate standardized functional metrics such as shear-dependent platelet adhesion, clot-growth dynamics, occlusion time, thrombus stability, fibrinolysis kinetics, and postlysis embolic debris generation. For microrobotic systems, these performance metrics must be coupled with comprehensive blood-compatibility assessments, including hemolysis, platelet activation, coagulation perturbation, immune recognition, protein corona formation, biodistribution, degradation behavior, and systemic clearance.

The integration of artificial intelligence and digital-twin frameworks introduces an additional regulatory layer [35▪▪,37▪▪]. If deep-learning algorithms and multilayer neural networks are used to guide thrombolytic dosing, magnetic actuation parameters, intervention timing, or patient stratification, then uncertainty quantification, model interpretability, data provenance, and decision traceability become integral components of the safety and regulatory evaluation process. Consequently, successful clinical translation will require not only advances in microengineering, robotics, and computational modeling but also the coordinated evolution of regulatory science, standardization protocols, and interdisciplinary validation frameworks capable of supporting adaptive and patient-specific therapeutic systems.

A vision for personalized, ‘actionable’ thrombosis assays

The convergence of microfluidics, artificial intelligence, digital twins, and microrobotic systems points toward a new paradigm for thrombosis management, shifting from population-based diagnostics toward dynamic and patient-specific therapeutic strategies. Rather than relying exclusively on generalized treatment protocols, future platforms will integrate patient-derived hemodynamic and thrombotic data into adaptive closed-loop systems capable of continuously predicting and modulating thrombotic processes in real time. A realistic near-term vision involves an ex-vivo-to-intervention pipeline where patient blood is first analyzed using high-shear microfluidic and POC platforms to quantify an individualized thrombotic phenotype. This phenotype can then be incorporated into artificial intelligence-assisted digital twin models powered by physics-informed neural networks to simulate therapeutic responses in silico, predicting the efficacy of antiplatelet, anticoagulant, or catheter-based interventions. Within this framework, microrobotic systems serve as the precision interventional arm, deployed only when localized actuation offers a clear advantage over systemic therapy [44–48]. To achieve this, the immediate translational objective must be the establishment of standardized benchmarking frameworks to evaluate microrobot performance, specifically transport efficiency, clot penetration, and embolic safety, within realistic vascular environments. Ultimately, this closed-loop integration of sensing, prediction, and targeted intervention represents a conceptual shift from passive diagnosis toward proactive and adaptive control of thrombotic disease.

CONCLUSION

Traditional biochemical frameworks fail to capture the dynamic mechanobiology of arterial thrombosis. This review proposes a transformative pathway through the convergence of microfluidics, artificial intelligence, digital twins, and microrobotics. While microfluidics provides mechanobiological insights, artificial intelligence and digital twins enable personalized in-silico predictions. Complementarily, microrobotics offer active, targeted intervention, such as localized thrombolysis, bridging the gap between diagnosis and therapy, though safety and regulatory hurdles remain. Achieving clinical actionability requires a closed-loop integration of these tools. This synergy will redefine thrombosis management, shifting from reactive treatment to proactive, patient-specific control within precision cardiovascular medicine.
