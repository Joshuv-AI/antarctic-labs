Cytomegalovirus congenital infection awareness, knowledge, and attitude towards prenatal screening among Jordanian women: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Fida Thekrallah, Mohammad Bushnaq, Omar Alshamali, Mohammad Almansi, Mohammad Nihad, Basel Asfan, Omar Alsalameen, Laila Tutunji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesCytomegalovirus (CMV) can be transmitted from the mother to the fetus during pregnancy, leading to a congenital CMV (cCMV), the most common congenital infection worldwide and may lead to severe neurological sequelae. Therefore, it is essential to evaluate the awareness of CMV infection and cCMV in childbearing women in high CMV prevalence countries, such as Jordan.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted to assess Jordanian women’s knowledge and awareness of CMV and cCMV infection, attitudes toward prenatal and neonatal screening for CMV, and the frequency of behaviors that may increase the risk of CMV transmission. Data were collected over two months using a structured questionnaire administered face-to-face in private settings at public community centers and the University of Jordan Hospital clinics. The questionnaire included demographic variables, CMV-related knowledge, attitudes toward screening, and behaviors potentially associated with CMV transmission risk. Data were recorded electronically by interviewers.ResultsOut of 589 eligible women, only 16.3% of women had heard of CMV, and among those, 67% were aware of cCMV. Never-pregnant women were more likely to report awareness of cCMV than recently-pregnant women. After receiving educational information about CMV, 91.7% and 94.9% of women believed that CMV screening for pregnant women and newborns, respectively, should be offered. Additionally, 71.3% indicated they would choose prenatal screening in a future pregnancy. Childcare‑related behaviors that may increase CMV transmission—such as kissing children on the lips or sharing cups and food—were commonly reported, highlighting important knowledge gaps.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates a substantial lack of awareness of CMV and cCMV among Jordanian women of childbearing age, regardless of pregnancy history. However, brief education markedly improved support for both prenatal and newborn CMV screening. These findings underscore the urgent need for comprehensive CMV education, increased public health messaging on CMV transmission, and consideration of policy measures to reduce cCMV risk in high‑prevalence populations.

Introduction

Cytomegalovirus (CMV) is a double-stranded DNA virus of the Herpesviridae family and is transmitted through infected body fluids such as saliva, tears, urine, blood, and genital secretions [1]. CMV infection is one of the most common infections worldwide [1, 2]. In immunocompetent individuals, primary infection typically triggers IgM and IgG antibody and T cell responses that control viral replication and lead to subclinical infection and virus clearance. However, CMV may establishes lifelong latency, particularly in myeloid lineage cells, where episomal viral genomes persist and reactivate and produce viral particles under certain conditions [2].

CMV can cross the placenta during pregnancy, resulting in congenital CMV (cCMV) infection—the most common congenital infection globally and may lead to severe neurological sequelae [3]. Although approximately 90% of infected neonates are asymptomatic at birth, up to 20% develop long-term sequelae, including sensorineural hearing loss, developmental delay, psychomotor deficits, vision loss, or death [4]. cCMV is the leading cause of congenital sensorineural hearing loss [5]. In utero transmission may occur during primary maternal infection, reinfection with a new CMV strain, or reactivation of latent virus in women with pre-existing immunity [2]. Given the high global seroprevalence of CMV, an estimated two‑thirds of infants with cCMV are born to mothers who were already seropositive [6].

Despite the substantial burden of cCMV and the absence of an effective vaccine, awareness of CMV and its transmission risks among women of reproductive age remains low. Studies from the United States, Europe, Canada, and Japan report that only 15–53% of women have heard of CMV, and just 10–32% are aware of cCMV [7–17]. In the Middle East and North Africa region, data are scarce; only one study from Saudi Arabia found that 8% of women had heard of CMV [18]. In Jordan, CMV seroprevalence among women of childbearing age is extremely high—approximately 93% in two independent studies [19, 20]—yet no data exist on awareness of CMV or cCMV.

Given the high prevalence of CMV infection in Jordan and the global evidence of limited public awareness, evaluating knowledge of CMV, cCMV, and associated risk behaviors among Jordanian women is essential. We therefore conducted a cross‑sectional study to assess awareness of CMV and cCMV among recently pregnant and never‑pregnant women, attitudes toward prenatal and neonatal CMV screening, and the frequency of behaviors that may increase CMV transmission risk. Comparing these two groups provides insight into how CMV‑related information is acquired and where targeted public health interventions may be most effective.

Methods

Study design

We employed a cross-sectional study design to assess CMV knowledge and awareness among Jordanian women of reproductive age. The primary variable of interest was CMV knowledge and awareness. Still, the study also assessed attitudes towards screening for CMV infection and the frequency of behaviors that could increase the risk of prenatal and neonatal CMV infection. These variables were assessed using a structured questionnaire adapted from Taslad et al. [15]. Demographic variables such as age, educational level, health care workers status, marital status and socioeconomic status were also collected.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (10/2023/3055) of the University of Jordan and conformed with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Jordan University Hospital Research Policy. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Measures were taken to ensure confidentiality of participant information, including collecting data in an anonymous way (so no identifying factors were used in the questionnaire), storing the data in a secure database, and conducting the interview in a private setting.

Participants and sample size

Participants were recruited from various healthcare facilities, primarily from patients attending University of Jordan Hospital clinics. Inclusion criteria included being a female aged 18–45 years, not currently pregnant, and either never been pregnant (never-pregnant) or been pregnant within the past 3 years (recently-pregnant), and willing to participate in the study. The 3 years were chosen to be reasonable for the women to recall their prenatal care. Exclusion criteria included currently pregnant women, and inability to communicate effectively whether in Arabic or English. Currently pregnant women were excluded to minimize potential anxiety related to questions about congenital infections and to avoid influencing clinical decision‑making during pregnancy. This approach also reduced the risk of biased responses, as currently pregnant women may have different levels of health information‑seeking behavior compared with non‑pregnant women.

where the z-score is 1.96 for 95% confidence, p is the expected proportion (0.5 for 50%), and e is the margin of error. The sample size target was estimated to be not less than 385 women.

Data collection

Data collection took place over two months at various public community centers across Jordan, but primarily among patients attending the University of Jordan Hospital clinics. Interviewers administered the questionnaire to participants face-to-face in a private setting to ensure the validity of the study and participants’ confidentiality. Participants were informed about the purpose of the study and provided written informed consent before completing the questionnaire. The survey took approximately 10–15 min to complete.

Instrument

The instrument was adopted from Teslad et al. [15], and the structured questionnaire included multiple-choice questions to assess participants’ knowledge of CMV, including transmission, prevention, potential consequences, and their sources of information about CMV and other similar diseases and conditions, if they have ever heard of them. The questionnaire was translated into Arabic and was checked by experts in medical language translation.

Based on a pilot study performed on 34 participants, only minor language editing was made to improve clarity. The questionnaire contained questions about demographics and awareness of CMV and other diseases and conditions. In the mentioned diseases, the study incorporated a fabricated disease (“Julio virus”) to evaluate the authenticity of responses, assessing whether some respondents checked all the choices or performed random checking to appear knowledgeable or to progress through the survey quickly [15]. After participants demonstrated their baseline understanding of CMV infection, we presented succinct educational content to each participant. This content included details on the prevalence and potential consequences of CMV.

Moreover, the questionnaire explored attitudes towards screening methods using the five-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree”. Furthermore, to assess behaviors potentially associated with CMV transmission risk, some questions asked about behaviors that could lead to CMV transmission, and, as a control, others asked about behaviors unlikely to increase CMV transmission risk for comparison [15].

The survey was also tailored for participants with and without children; participants with at least one biological child were directed to respond to the questions as if they had a child. Those without children were prompted to indicate how frequently they would engage in the described activities if they were to have a child, which encompasses a range of inquiries to elicit comprehensive insights. All responses were recorded on Google Survey forms by the interviewer using a mobile phone or a tablet.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS26. Age was described as a continuous variable as mean (± standard deviation (SD)) and all other variables were described as proportion variables. Responses between the two groups (recently-pregnant and never-pregnant) were compared using a chi-square test with 95% confidence interval (95% CI), whereas responses regarding knowledge, attitudes, and practices were compared using multivariable logistic regression. Furthermore, the dependent variable in the multiple-choice question was coded as 1 for correct and 2 for incorrect to identify the correct response. In the model, the potential confounding factors were adjusted for including age (18–24; 25–29; 30–34; 35–39; and 40–45 years), education (basic education, high school, diploma, bachelor’s degree, Master degree, PhD), income (monthly income Under 500JD; 500-1000JD or 1000JD+) and history of working in a daycare (binary: Yes; No) or in healthcare (binary: Yes; No).

Results

Demographics

Among the initial 613 women who completed the questionnaire, 24 women’s data were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria. The mean age of the 589 participants was 29.5 ± 8.5. Most of the participants (77%) had a bachelor’s degree or higher, 78% had never worked as a healthcare provider, and 93% had not worked in a daycare facility (Table 1). Among the participants, 273 were pregnant at least once. Recently-pregnant women were significantly older than never-pregnant women, less educated, had less household income, and were less keen to get pregnant again (Table 1).

Awareness of CMV, cCMV, other viruses, and fetal diseases

Among the participants, only 96 women (16.3%) heard about CMV, and 11% were aware of cCMV infection (Fig. 1). On the other hand, most women were familiar with autism (92.9%), influenza (89.1%), Down syndrome (88.6%), Rubella (73.2%), and toxoplasmosis (62.3%). The knowledge was less about β-Streptococcus (41.3%), sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) (34.8%), neural tube defect (22.8%), and fetal alcohol syndrome (13.4%). The least known virus of the list among Jordanian women was Zika (9.3%). As part of data quality check, 5.01% reported that the fabricated disease (“Julio virus”) cause birth defects, and 1.2% of participants had selected all selected 6 diseases and conditions. Among the women who did not identify the fabricated virus (“Julio virus”), regardless of their other responses, 10% (58/582) indicated they were aware of cCMV.

Never-pregnant women were more likely to have heard about CMV (p < 0.05), Down syndromes (p < 0.01), and fetal alcohol syndrome (p < 0.001), whereas recently-pregnant women were more aware of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) (p < 0.01) and β-Streptococcus (p < 0.0001) than never-pregnant women. However, after adjusting for demographic variables, never-pregnant women were more aware of fetal alcohol syndrome (p < 0.001, 1.18–3.40), but less aware of SIDS (p < 0.0001. 0.34–0.71) and β-Strep (< 0.0001, 0.29–0.59) than recently-pregnant women.

Among all women who indicated that they know some information about cCMV, the most commonly cited sources of information were from physicians (36.5%). Furthermore, 44.8% of whom heard of CMV correctly identified that hearing loss was the most common problem associated with cCMV; most of them were never-pregnant (Table 2). Over a third of women (35.5%) correctly responded that screening for CMV was not typically offered during pregnancy in Jordan, though 54.7% were not sure if it was, and 32.1% correctly responded that newborns are not usually screened for cCMV in Jordan (Table 2).

Perception after succinct education about CMV and cCMV

After presenting participants with succinct educational content and learning about the incidence and risks of CMV and cCMV, 91.7% and 94.9% of women, respectively, believed that CMV screening for pregnant women and newborn should be offered (Table 3). However, only 71.3% chose to be screened if they were to become pregnant in the future. Overall, recently-pregnant women reported different attitude for CMV screening if became pregnant than never-pregnant women (p = 0.02).

When asked about behavioral practices, a high proportion of women reported engaging in activities that may increase risk of CMV transmission, including kissing their child on the lips (45.5%), sharing a cup (61.6%), or food (73.7%) with their child either “rarely” or “often” (Table 4). As for differences in behavioral practices between the two groups, more women who had children reported kissing their young child on the lips and cleaning a pacifier with their own mouth than never-pregnant women would if they had a child (Table 4).

Discussion

The present study surveyed women of childbearing age across multiple healthcare facilities, primarily JUH clinics, to capture a diverse sample representing various socio‑economic backgrounds. This approach enabled a broad assessment of CMV awareness and CMV‑prevention behaviors among Jordanian women (Table 5). Overall, awareness of CMV and cCMV was low among both recently‑pregnant and never‑pregnant participants. These findings align with reports from several countries, including the United States [7, 15, 16], Spain [11], Portugal [17], Japan [12], Canada [13], Singapore [21], and Saudi Arabia [18], where awareness remains limited despite high CMV seroprevalence.

However, when compared with other Middle Eastern or high‑prevalence settings, the results reveal important regional contrasts. For example, the only published study from Saudi Arabia reported that just 8% of women had heard of CMV [18], a figure is slightly less than our findings. This suggests that low CMV awareness may be a broader regional pattern across the Middle East, despite the region’s consistently high CMV seroprevalence. In contrast, countries such as Italy [8], Germany [14], and France [10] report substantially higher awareness levels. These differences may reflect the presence of routine prenatal CMV screening and structured maternal‑health education programs in parts of Europe, systems that are not yet widely implemented in Middle Eastern countries. Although 77% of women in our study held a bachelor’s degree or higher, awareness remained low, reinforcing the idea that system‑level prenatal education and screening policies may be more influential than education alone.

In our study, only 11% of women were aware of cCMV, and among those, 36.5% cited physicians as their primary information source. Even among first‑year medical students, baseline cCMV knowledge has been reported to be low (34%) [22], suggesting that awareness in the general population is likely far lower than in healthcare‑related subgroups [18, 23]. Moreover, fewer than half of Obstetricians and Gynecologists report counseling pregnant women about CMV [11, 24], often citing time constraints and limited educational materials [24]. These findings highlight a critical gap in clinical practice and underscore the need for nationally standardized CMV education guidelines, integrated into routine antenatal care and supported by accessible patient‑facing materials.

When compared with the Minnesota cohort studied by Tastad et al. [15], Jordanian women demonstrated similar awareness of common conditions such as influenza, Down syndrome, autism, SIDS, and rubella, but lower awareness of Zika virus, fetal alcohol syndrome, and CMV. Lower awareness of Zika virus likely reflects its minimal presence in Jordan, while fetal alcohol syndrome is less recognized due to low alcohol consumption among Jordanian women. Conversely, Jordanian women showed higher awareness of toxoplasmosis, a more prevalent concern in Jordan due to widespread exposure to stray cats and therefore, physicians in Jordan look for toxoplasmosis as a common parasitic infection.

Regarding attitudes toward CMV screening, both recently‑pregnant and never‑pregnant women in our study agreed that prenatal CMV testing should be offered. This contrasts with findings from Minnesota, where never‑pregnant women were more supportive of prenatal testing than recently‑pregnant women [15]. However, when asked whether they would personally choose to undergo screening, recently‑pregnant women in our study expressed different preferences than never‑pregnant women, suggesting that recent pregnancy experience may influence personal decision‑making. Attitudes toward newborn screening were consistently positive across both groups, aligning with findings from other populations [15].

Young children with CMV infection shed high levels of virus in saliva and urine for prolonged periods, often exceeding 29 months [25, 26]. This prolonged shedding increases the risk of maternal infection, especially when behaviors such as cleaning pacifiers with the mouth, kissing children on the lips, or sharing toothbrushes are common. Such behaviors were reported in our study and others [15]. CMV shedding is also more common among mothers living with or caring for young children [27], and shedding rates increase as pregnancy progresses [28–30].

Given Jordan’s high CMV seroprevalence and the low awareness observed, the findings have clear implications for national maternal‑child health policy. Integrating CMV education into: prenatal counselling protocols, maternal‑child health guidelines, routine antenatal classes, and public health campaigns targeting high‑risk groups, could substantially improve awareness and reduce preventable exposures [31]. Furthermore, since cCMV screening within the first 21 days helps distinguish congenital from postnatal infection and guides follow-up care, newborn screening shortly after birth that are not clinically evident but require early intervention should be implemented [31]. Countries with higher awareness, such as Italy and Germany, demonstrate that systematic prenatal education and optional CMV screening programs can meaningfully increase public knowledge [8, 14]. Jordan and other Middle Eastern countries may benefit from adopting similar approaches, tailored to local cultural and healthcare contexts.

A notable limitation of this study is the potential for social desirability bias, particularly because data were collected through face‑to‑face interviews. Participants may have overstated their knowledge or provided responses they believed were expected. Future research should consider anonymous, self‑administered questionnaires or digital survey platforms, which may reduce this bias and yield more accurate estimates of CMV awareness. Additional limitations include the sample size, recruitment from healthcare facilities (which may exclude women with limited healthcare access), and the cross‑sectional design, which precludes causal inference. The use of an Arabic‑language questionnaire may also have limited participation among women with low literacy or those more comfortable in other languages.

In conclusion, this study reveals a substantial lack of awareness of CMV and cCMV among Jordanian women of childbearing age, consistent with findings from other Middle Eastern countries but lower than awareness levels reported in parts of Europe. Following a brief educational intervention, most participants supported offering prenatal CMV testing and newborn cCMV screening. These findings underscore the urgent need for comprehensive educational materials, structured prenatal counseling, and national policy initiatives aimed at reducing cCMV risk in high‑prevalence populations.
