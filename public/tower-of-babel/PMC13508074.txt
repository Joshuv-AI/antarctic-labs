Modulating the Reactivity of Amine-Based Electrolytes for Stable Rechargeable Magnesium Batteries
Authors: Seonmo Yang, Jeongheon Seok, Junyoung Choi, Jinyoung Kim, Hyo Chul Ahn, Hayoung Ahn, Jihoon Oh, Minkwan Kim, Dongmin Park, Taemin Kang, Ben Dlugatch, Doron Aurbach, Yousung Jung, Jang Wook Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite their advantages complementary to lithium- and sodium-ion batteries, rechargeable magnesium batteries (RMBs) still suffer from multiple limitations that impede their commercialization. Amine-based electrolytes have recently emerged as promising options to overcome the long-standing challenge of sluggish Mg2+ ion transport at the magnesium metal interface; however, their uncontrolled chemical and electrochemical reactivity impairs cycling and calendar life. After screening a range of bidentate methoxyethylamine derivatives, we herein identify aminoacetaldehyde dimethyl acetal (ADMA)functionalized with a methoxy substituentas a single electrolyte solvent that achieves an optimal balance between interfacial ionic transport and (electro)­chemical stability. These optimal properties are enabled by the concerted modulation of electron donicity and steric hindrance in ADMA. Consequently, the ADMA-based electrolyte delivers stable cycling for Mg||SS half-cellseven under operating protocols incorporating intermittent rest periodsas well as for full-cells paired with Mo6S8 and tellurium cathodes. This study demonstrates that longstanding interfacial issues at the Mg metal anode in RMBs can be resolved through the sophisticated molecular engineering of scalable, bidentate methoxyethylamine electrolyte solvents.

Introduction

The expanding utilization of energy storage technologies across various applications has sparked a growing interest in alternative battery systems − beyond the currently commercialized lithium-ion batteries (LIBs). , This trend reflects the inherent inability of any single type of battery system to achieve optimal performance in every respect. Particularly, geopolitical risk caused by the uneven distribution of lithium (Li) resources has prompted research investment into alternative battery systems that utilize more abundant elements for charge carriers.

In this context, rechargeable magnesium batteries (RMBs) have drawn considerable attention in the past decade as promising energy storage systems due to their utilization of low-cost raw materials from globally distributed reserves. − Furthermore, their high theoretical volumetric capacity originating from the bivalency of the Mg2+ carrier ions, enhanced safety features, and lower air sensitivity and dendritic growth on the metallic surface, jointly make RMBs appealing as cost-effective, high-performance battery options with fewer safety concerns. ,

Despite the expected advantages arising from using metallic Mg anodes, the realization of RMBs capable of reliable and sustainable operation has not been forthcoming. , In contrast to Li battery systems, where the formation of inorganic solid-electrolyte-interphase (SEI) enables selective ionic conduction while suppressing parasitic reactions, similar passivation layers on Mg surfaces severely restrict Mg2+ ions transport and render the electrode electrochemically inactive. − Consequently, conventional Li-based SEI design principles may not be directly extended to RMBs. This situation, related to Mg anodes’ interfaces in most relevant electrolyte solutions, is one of the major hurdles in the development of practical RMBs.

To overcome these problems, early research adopted chloride-based electrolytes that enable reversible Mg deposition–dissolution processes via the formation of nonpassivating interfaces at the Mg anodes. − However, during prolonged cycling, the absence of a protective SEI-type surface layer on the Mg anodes leads to continuous electrolyte decomposition and subsequent corrosion of the Mg anodes Moreover, chlorides-containing solutions usually corrode aluminum (Al) or stainless steel (SS) current collectors at the cathodes’ side. −

These limitations have shifted attention toward amine-based electrolytes, which exhibit stronger coordination with Mg2+ ions than conventional ethereal solvents. Such strong coordination can facilitate reversible Mg plating-stripping by suppressing anion participation in the solvation sheath and promoting the formation of ionically conductive organic passivation layers, − while maintaining higher oxidative stability than their chloride-based counterparts. As a result, amine-based electrolytes have emerged as one of the few electrolyte platforms capable of enabling highly reversible Mg electrochemistry. Nevertheless, recent reports have revealed that the amine-based systems are not completely immune to parasitic reactions. Wang et al. observed corrosion of Mg anodes in amine-based electrolytes, while Li et al. detected gas evolution associated with them. Such side reactions can lead to a rapid performance decay, which becomes particularly pronounced when amine-based solvents are solely employed without the presence of ethereal cosolvents. , These findings underscore that ensuring long-term performance and stability of the amine-based electrolytes requires careful consideration of these degradation pathways. However, studies aimed at mitigating such parasitic reactions remain limited, particularly with respect to understanding and controlling the inherent reactivity of electrolyte components. Amine-based solvents, with their high Mg2+ affinity, tend to increase the proportion of solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIP) configurations, , which is advantageous for constructing organic, ion-permeable interphasial layers. Nevertheless, their relatively low cathodic stability can also trigger undesired side reactions including corrosion. Therefore, precise regulation of SSIP with respect to abundance, cathodic stability, and surface accessibility is required within a range that does not compromise ion-conducting film formation or bulk ionic conduction.

In this study, we systematically investigated the parasitic reactions of amine-based electrolytes, with particular emphasis on corrosion, by tailoring solvent structures to modulate electron donicity and steric hindrance. After screening a range of methoxyethylamine bidentate molecular structures, aminoacetaldehyde dimethyl acetal (ADMA)with an additional methoxy substituent to the bidentate analogueswas identified as an optimal solvent that balances electron donor strength and steric hindrance. This subtle modification enabled a finely tuned formation and reactivity of SSIP species, thereby mitigating excessive electrolyte decomposition and corrosion. As a result, the ADMA-based electrolytes delivered the highest performance and cyclability among the tested electrolytes, achieving an average Coulombic efficiency (CE) of 97.3% under galvanostatic conditions with a current density of 0.5 mA cm–2 and an areal capacity of 0.5 mAh cm–2 in Mg||SS asymmetric cell configuration, sustaining over 500 h of operation. This electrolyte system also cycled stably in full-cells paired with Mo6S8 and tellurium (Te) cathodes, maintaining 77.4% and 99.2% capacities after 250 and 300 cycles, respectively. These findings demonstrate that interfacial degradation in amine-based electrolytes can be effectively mitigated through the molecular-level tuning of coordination behavior, establishing useful design principles for high-performance RMB electrolytes.

Results and Discussion

Solvent Design and Screening Strategies

Inorganic-rich interphasial layers are relatively resistant to swelling, thereby suppressing electrolyte penetration through the interphase. Moreover, the high electronic insulation of inorganic components effectively blocks electron leakage. Such characteristics render inorganic interphases particularly favorable for suppressing parasitic reactions at the Mg metal–electrolyte interface. However, the strong electrostatic interactions between inorganic components and Mg2+ ions severely impede ionic transport through the interphase. In addition, the native magnesium oxide layer that inevitably forms on the Mg metal surface exhibits similar characteristics, leading to increased overpotential during Mg (de)­plating processes (Figure a, left). Consequently, the formation of an ionically resistive, inorganic-rich surface layer in RMB systems typically leads to poor electrochemical performance and is thus generally unfavorable. , As a result, RMB systems have preferentially been designed to promote the formation of an organic-rich surface layer. However, most organic interphases are, in turn, more susceptible to swelling and electron leakage (Figure a, right), and therefore do not usually warrant robust interfacial stability. Hence, electrolyte design for RMBs often falls into a fundamental dilemma between inorganic-rich and organic-rich passivation, each with its own limitations.

In this context, the SSIP configuration in amine-based electrolytes can be seen as a double-edged sword (Figure b). A higher proportion of SSIPs facilitates the formation of a solvent-derived (thus organic-rich) interphase for facile ion transport therein, but simultaneously accelerates parasitic reactions due to the more permeable nature of the interphase matrix and the weakened reductive stability of amine-based species. Specifically, once the electron-rich donor sites in amine solvents coordinate with Mg2+ ions, the local electron density is substantially reduced, heightening the susceptibility of the solvents to reductive decomposition. Moreover, the more permeable interphase matrix allows corrosive species to access the Mg metal surface for severe corrosion. These considerations suggest that tuning the electron donicity and steric hindrance of solvent molecules toward a moderate SSIP fraction is crucial for achieving both the thermodynamic and kinetic stability in amine-based RMB electrolytes.

Guided by these insights, we devised two molecular design strategies based on the methoxyethylamine framework, which intrinsically exhibits high Mg2+ coordination strength and thus provides a suitable baseline for structural tuning (Figure c). The first involves alkylation, which slightly increases steric hindrance and thereby weakens coordinating power. However, the electron-donating nature of alkyl substituents can concurrently strengthen donor ability of solvents, offering only a limited relief. By contrast, incorporating electron-withdrawing groups provides a more effective route, as it lowers the electron density of donor atoms and moderates coordinating strength without introducing such trade-offs.

Following this rationale, we conducted an initial screening of four solvents (Figure d): 2-methoxyethylamine (MOEA), tetrahydrofurfurylamine (THFA), ADMA, and furfurylamine (FA). The calculated ESP surface area (SESP surface) follows the trend of ADMA (157.1 Å2) > THFA (153.7 Å2) > FA (143.3 Å2) > MOEA (132.2 Å2), suggesting a progressive decrease in steric hindrance along this sequence. In parallel, the calculated minimum ESP values and binding Gibbs free energies (Figures d and S1) indicate an electron donicity order of THFA > MOEA > ADMA > FA. Among these candidates, FA failed to dissociate Mg­(OTf)2 salt due to its insufficient electron donicity (Figure S2), underscoring the need for careful tuning to identify an appropriate coordination range. On the basis of these criteria, ADMA was expected to most effectively suppress parasitic reactions such as corrosion through its large size and moderate coordination.

Comparison of Electrochemical Performance

Because the influence of amine-based solvents is often veiled by ethereal cosolvents, we employed single-solvent electrolyte configurations to enable explicit evaluation. In galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) tests using the Mg||SS asymmetric cells, all three amine-based solvents exhibited relatively low overpotentials in Mg plating (below 500 mV, Figure a). Considering that the high overpotential of conventional 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME)-based electrolyte (Figures a and S3) arises from its inability to form an SEI that facilitates Mg ion transport, the significantly reduced overpotentials of the amine-based electrolytes were likely enabled by the formation of ion-conducting interphasial layers. Notably, the MOEA-based cell showed a substantially lower overpotential (∼100 mV) than the THFA- and ADMA-based cells (∼500 mV). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) consistently revealed lower interfacial resistance in the MOEA electrolyte (Figure S4). However, such overpotential and resistance behavior was not directly reflected in the cycling performance (Figure b). The ADMA-based cellsdespite exhibiting the large initial overpotentialachieved the best cycling stability, with an average CE of 97.3% over 250 cycles. Although there remains room for further improvement, this level of cycling efficiency represents a notable advancement compared to those reported to date, particularly under single solvent conditions where parasitic reactions are critical. ,− ,− In contrast, the MOEA-based cells, which had the lowest initial overpotential, exhibited limited reversibility of Mg (de)­plating with CE of 96.1% over 100 cycles, while the THFA performed the worst among the amine-based ones, achieving CE of only 93.4% over 25 cycles. In a similar line, the overpotentials of the MOEA- and THFA-based cells increased abruptly around 236 and 93 h, respectively, whereas the ADMA-based cell stably maintained its potential profile for over 500 h without a substantial increase in the overpotential (Figure c). Mg||Mg symmetric cells produced similar outcomes (Figures d and S5), with the ADMA-based cell exhibiting cycling stability comparable to those of previously reported amine-based Mg electrolytes (Table S1). The lack of correlation between initial overpotential and subsequent cycle life deviates from the conventional expectation that lower overpotential translates into superior cycling performance. Collectively, these unusual electrochemical features suggest that, beyond ion-conducting film formation alone, interfacial degradation phenomenasuch as corrosion and other peripheral reactionsplay a decisive role in governing the performance of amine-based electrolytes, as elucidated in the following sections.

Investigation of the Mg Metal Interface

The surfaces of cycled Mg metal anodes were systematically characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis to see the effect of the amine-based electrolytes on the interfacial composition (Figures a and S6). The N 1s spectra confirmed that amine decomposition primarily yielded organic species rather than inorganic Mg3N2. Together with the absence of MgF2 in the Mg 2p spectra and the lack of CF x peaks in the C 1s spectra, these results indicate the formation of organic-rich interphasial layers across all the electrolytes. The tendency of the methoxyethylamine-based electrolytes to form organic-rich interphases becomes more evident when compared with the conventional DME-based electrolyte, which produced appreciable amounts of inorganic species on the Mg surface (Figures S7 and S8). These observations were further supported by time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) analysis (Figures S9–S13) and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, Figures S14–S17), which showed similarly low levels of F- and S- containing species, confirming that salt decomposition was effectively suppressed in all the amine-based electrolytes. Therefore, variations in electron donicity and steric hindrance among the amine-based solvents appear to exert only a minor influence on the formation of an organic-rich interphasial layer.

Despite these shared features, pronounced differences emerged in the condition of the native oxide layer and the resulting interfacial morphology. In the Mg 2p spectra, the MgO peak intensity varied markedly across the MOEA, THFA, and ADMA treated samples. Given the strong MgO peak observed for pristine Mg foil (Figure S18), the MgO signals were primarily attributed to the inherent native oxide layer rather than electrochemically formed species. Notably, the significant decrease of the MgO signal in the MOEA case, and to a lesser extent for THFA, indicates that corrosion disrupted the native oxide layers in these samples. In contrast, the ADMA electrolyte preserved the oxide signal to a degree comparable to that of pristine Mg. In addition, the C 1s data revealed an intensified C–C peak for the THFA-treated sample, attributable to (electro)­chemical ring-opening reaction of its cyclic substituent. This reaction produced a progressively thicker polymeric interphase during rest periods and upon cycling (Figure S19), which likely contributed to the inferior electrochemical performance of the THFA electrolyte. These findings highlight that substituent-induced side reactions, in addition to coordination characteristics, must be considered when designing amine-based solvents.

The distinct interfacial behaviors were further reflected in the deposition morphologies observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Figure b). The deposits in the MOEA electrolyte displayed widely distributed and well-defined hexagonal features (Figure b, left), indicative of kinetically facile Mg2+ ions transport through the interphase. This behavior can be explained by corrosion-induced disruption of the native oxide layer, which in turn accounts for the diminished overpotential and low interfacial resistance observed with the MOEA-based cells (Figures a and S4). The THFA electrolyte produced hemispherical deposits with some aggregates, resulting from hindered ion transport likely due to the polymeric films on the Mg surface. Once again, this polymeric film formation is ascribed to the ring-opening reaction of the cyclic substituent of THFA. The ADMA electrolyte exhibited partially faceted morphologies with less pronounced crystallographic features than MOEA. Taken together with the compositional analyses (Figures a and S6–S17), these observations suggest that while all the amine-based electrolytes form organic-rich films on the Mg electrodes, their electrochemical performances diverge due to differences in the extent of side reactions.

Investigation of the Corrosion Behavior

Despite the low interfacial impedance and overpotential observed in the MOEA electrolyte, its electrochemical performance was markedly inferior to that of its ADMA counterpart. Interfacial characterizations revealed that this behavior is likely associated with substantial disruption of the native oxide layer in the MOEA electrolyte, implying its severe corrosivity. To elucidate the origin and extent of corrosion behavior, we conducted SEM analysis for the Mg foils after 72 h immersion in the solvents and electrolytes (Figure a,b). When the Mg foils immersed in the neat MOEA, THFA, and ADMA solvents were examined, they displayed no apparent signs of corrosion (Figure a). Interestingly, exposure to the electrolytes containing 0.3 M Mg­(OTf)2 resulted in markedly different levels of corrosion depending on the solvent. The Mg foils immersed in 0.3 M Mg­(OTf)2 MOEA electrolyte exhibited extensive pitting, whereas the corrosion of Mg foils in the THFA electrolyte was less pronounced, yet noticeable. In contrast, the foil immersed in the ADMA electrolyte maintained its integrity without significant corrosion (Figures b, S20 and S21). The pronounced difference in corrosion behavior between the MOEA and ADMA electrolytes was also reflected in the potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) analysis (Figure S22). These observations suggest that the observed degradation may originate from the electrolyte solutions rather than direct solvent reduction. The Mg foils that underwent electrochemical interfacial formation exhibited the same corrosion trends (Figure S23), indicating that the electrochemically formed interphasial layers provided only limited protection against corrosion. Furthermore, electrolyte-exchange experiments between different electrolytes (Figure S24) confirmed that the suppressed Mg corrosion observed in the ADMA electrolyte does not originate solely from the protection of its interphasial layer. In addition, the 1H NMR and 19F NMR spectra (Figure S25) revealed no new chemical species arising from chemical reactions between solvent and salt, ruling out corrosion driven by secondary byproducts. All in all, the series of results indicate that the origin and differences in corrosion behavior are mainly associated with the coordinated species in the electrolytes, rather than the reactivity of solvents alone, interphasial layer properties, or secondary reaction pathways.

Thus, we next examined the structural and electronic characteristics of the bare solvents and their solvated complexes in detail. The computed lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels of the bare solvents were highly positive (Figure S26), indicating that direct reduction of the amine-based solvents is unlikely to occur. In contrast, their solvated complexes exhibited substantially lower LUMO energy levels, shifting into the negative regime. In particular, the SSIP configurations without anion coordination showed even lower values compared to their contact ion pair (CIP) counterparts (Figure S27). This trend arises because the absence of anion coordination in SSIPs leaves the Mg2+ center less charge-stabilized than in CIPs, rendering the complexes more prone to reduction. Accordingly, the reduction propensity of SSIP species of Mg-complexes is most likely responsible for triggering Mg corrosion. Overall, these findings suggest that the distinct corrosion behaviors among the amine-based electrolytes are predominantly governed by the population and electronic distributions of their SSIP species.

In this context, Raman spectroscopy provides a key clue for understanding the anticorrosive behavior of the ADMA-based electrolyte (Figure c). The spectra of the ADMA-based electrolyte exhibited peaks slightly shifted from the SSIP region toward the CIP region compared to those of MOEA and THFA, reflecting a reduced SSIP population. Quantitative Raman fitting revealed SSIP/CIP ratios of 93/7, 91/9, and 78/22 for the MOEA-, THFA-, and ADMA-based electrolytes, respectively (Figure S28). Consistent trends were also observed in the MD simulation results (Figure S29). This decreased SSIP fraction of the ADMA-based electrolyte can be linked to its diminished Mg metal corrosion through the weakened coordination strength of donor atoms O and N, while still allowing the formation of an ion-permeable, organic-rich interphasial layer. Although ADMA as a bare solvent possesses the lowest intrinsic LUMO level (Figure S26), the trend reverses upon coordination: its SSIP complex, [Mg­(ADMA)3]2+, exhibits the highest LUMO levels among the three complexes under comparison (Figure d). This inversion arises from the relatively weaker coordination of Mg2+ with the N atom in ADMA, which alleviates the depletion of electron density around the N–H bond and thus renders the Mg-complex less susceptible to reduction (Figure e).

Galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) measurements using three-electrode cells (Figures f and S30) also support the enhanced tolerance of the ADMA-based electrolyte to Mg corrosion from the perspective of interphasial diffusion kinetics. During the relaxation periods following current pulses of incrementally increasing magnitude, the ADMA-based electrolyte exhibited slower relaxation, indicative of reduced diffusivity of solvation complexes across the interphasial layer compared to the MOEA-based counterpart. This trend is also consistent with the activation energy trend related to the interphasial transport kinetics of Mg2+ complexes (Figure S31). Such reduced mobility is likely associated with the larger solvation sheath in the ADMA system, arising from its weaker coordination and greater steric hindrance. The formation of a larger solvation sheath in the ADMA electrolyte was further corroborated by wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) analysis (Figure S32). All in all, these observations highlight the balanced coordination of ADMAthrough simultaneous modulation of electron donicity and steric hindranceto effectively suppress Mg corrosion.

The strong corrosive behavior can lead to breakdown of the surface oxide layer and further amplify the differences among the electrolytes. The highly corrosive MOEA electrolyte exhibited the most severe destruction of the native oxide layer, as reflected by the weakened Mg­(OH)−, MgO–, Mg­(OH)2 –, and MgO2 – signals shortly after sputtering in the ToF-SIMS analysis (Figure S33, arrows) as well as by the enhanced Mg0 signal relative to MgO and its derivatives in the XPS spectra (Figure S34). The THFA solution showed a less severe but still evident degradation. In contrast, the ADMA electrolyte induced negligible changes in the oxide layer, consistent with the XPS results in Figure a. Importantly, immersion tests using MgO powder revealed no changes in crystallinity (Figure S35) or crack formation (Figure S36), confirming that the amine-based electrolytes themselves do not perturb the structural integrity of the oxide layer. Instead, the electrolytes are presumed to infiltrate through microcracks in the native oxide layer that were formed during the polishing process. The infiltrated electrolyte species corrode the underlying Mg metal, forming voids that impose local mechanical stress, eventually rupturing the oxide layer (Figure S37). Accordingly, in more corrosive electrolytes such as MOEA, the oxide film is removed more extensively, which lowers interfacial resistance but simultaneously aggravates reactions between Mg metal and SSIP, ultimately impairing charge–discharge cyclability.

To quantitatively assess these parasitic processes, we performed an additional electrochemical analysis using GCD tests with an 8 h rest period inserted after every fourth plating and stripping (Figure S38). The CE values following these rest periods decreased notably compared to those during standard cycling (Figure g), reflecting the oxidation of the deposited Mg via corrosion or side reactions during the rest periods. Among the tested cells, the cells with the ADMA-based solution demonstrated superior cycling stability with minimal CE reduction between the regular and postrest cycles, followed by its MOEA- and THFA-based counterparts (Figures g and S39). Notably, this trend was also consistent with the SSIP/CIP distributions obtained from the Raman fitting and MD simulations, supporting a relationship between SSIP populations and the extent of parasitic reactions. The THFA-based cells, despite its lower corrosivity than that of MOEA, exhibited the largest CE drop, presumably owing to the aforementioned ring-opening polymerization process in addition to corrosion. This trend was mirrored in the open circuit voltage (OCV) measurements during the rest periods (Figure S40), where the ADMA-based cells exhibited the highest OCV, indicating minimal subsurface chemical reactions. A consistent trend was also observed in the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) tests (Figure S41), as the current magnitude reflects the extent of side reactions. These results align with the long-term cycling performance trends without rest periods (Figure b–d), and emphasize the importance of suppressing corrosion and side reactions for sustained RMB operation.

Full-Cell Demonstration

In addition to cathodic (reductive) stability, anodic (oxidative) stability is another critical requirement for practical cell operation, particularly for compatibility with high-voltage cathodes. The calculated highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) levels of the bare solvents were highest for THFA, followed by MOEA and ADMA, whereas the coordinated states showed the opposite trend between ADMA and MOEA, with [Mg­(ADMA)3]2+ exhibiting the highest HOMO level and [Mg­(MOEA)3]2+ the lowest one (Figure S42). This trend reflects electronic structure changes induced by the Mg2+–solvent coordination, similar to the behavior observed for the LUMO levels. Experimentally, however, LSV tests revealed that the oxidative stability of the electrolytes follows the sequence of THFA, ADMA, and MOEA (Figure S43). The inconsistent behavior of MOEA appears to involve additional side reactions, plausibly associated with the oxidation of hydrogen gas generated from its decomposition (Figure S44).

Full-cell performance was evaluated using Chevrel phase Mo6S8 cathodes (Figure a,b). The Mg||Mo6S8 full-cells employing the 0.3 M Mg­(OTf)2 MOEA electrolyte showed low polarization and a high initial capacity of 87.0 mAh gCATHODE –1, when operated in the voltage range of 0.2–2.2 V at 1C (1C = 128 mA g–1). However, its capacity retention rapidly declined after 30 cycles, with only 13.4% of its capacity retained after 50 cycles. This decay likely arose from severe corrosion-induced Mg loss together with the limited oxidative stability of the MOEA-based electrolyte. The THFA-based cells exhibited substantial polarization due to the high interfacial resistance at the anode side, which resulted in both a low initial capacity of 46.7 mAh gCATHODE –1 and an average capacity of 39.7 mAh gCATHODE –1 over 250 cycles. By contrast, the Mg||Mo6S8 full-cells with the 0.3 M Mg­(OTf)2 ADMA electrolyte showed an initial capacity of 72.6 mAh gCATHODE –1 and an average capacity of 62.6 mAh gCATHODE –1 over 250 cycles under the same conditions, highlighting its superior long-term stability among the tested amine-based electrolytes. Notably, reliable operation was maintained even when the Mo6S8 loading was increased (Figure S45). These full-cell performance trends were also reflected in the cyclic voltammetry (CV) results (Figure S46). The Mg||Mo6S8 cells employing the ADMA-based electrolyte exhibited more stable and reversible redox responses than their MOEA- and THFA-based counterparts, consistent with the improved cycling stability above. Notably, the electrochemical performance achieved in the present work is comparable to those reported for other amine-based Mg electrolytes despite the absence of any cosolvents or supporting components (Table S2). Furthermore, the ADMA-based electrolyte was evaluated with a Te cathode, which operates via a conversion mechanism. The Mg||Te configuration, selected to demonstrate the advantages of the rate capability over conventional intercalation-based cathodes, operated with remarkable stability at 300 mA g–1 using the same ADMA-based electrolyte (Figure c,d). Although the CE slightly exceeded 100% because of the shuttling behavior of chalcogen conversion cathode, the cell maintained an average capacity of 190 mAh gCATHODE –1 for 300 cycles, with a 99.2% retention, revealing the broad cathode compatibility of the ADMA-based electrolyte.

Conclusion

We established design strategies for amine-based solvent molecules in the RMB electrolytes, particularly focusing on the formation of ion-conducting interphasial layers while minimizing parasitic reactions. Although SSIP configuration is indispensable for forming an ion-conductive interphasial layer, it can in turn give rise to interfacial side reactions represented by Mg metal corrosion, underscoring the need for precise regulation of the Mg2+ solvation structure. ADMA achieves this balance by moderately reducing the SSIP fraction and enhancing reductive stability through the concerted tuning of electron donicity and steric hindrance. The resulting relatively larger solvation clusters further regulate the ion diffusivity through the interphasial layer, effectively mitigating Mg metal corrosion. These improvements in interfacial stability translated into markedly improved Mg plating–stripping cyclability, even under cycling protocols that included rest periods. The robustness of our design principle was validated by stable operation of Mg||SS asymmetric cells, delivering an average reversibility of 97.3% over 500 h, and by sustained capacities in Mg||Mo6S8 and Mg||Te full-cells over 250 and 300 cycles, respectively. Overall, this study conveys an important message that the longstanding trade-off at the Mg metal interphasebetween ionic transport and (electro)­chemical stabilitycan be overcome through sophisticated molecular design of electrolyte solvents.

Experimental Section

Materials Preparation

The electrolyte solutions were prepared using vacuum-dried (200 °C, 24 h) Mg­(OTf)2 (>98%, TCI chemicals) dissolved in various predried solvents: DME (anhydrous, 99.5%, free of inhibitors, Sigma-Aldrich), MOEA (99%, Sigma-Aldrich), THFA (>98%, TCI chemicals), FA (>98%, TCI chemicals), and ADMA (99%, Sigma-Aldrich). Before use, all solvents were dried overnight using activated 4 Å molecular sieves, and the solutions were stirred at 25 °C for 24 h. The Mo6S8 electrodes were fabricated via the slurry casting method by combining Mo6S8 powder (NEI Corporation), carbon black (Imerys, France), and poly­(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF, Kureha, Japan) in a 7:2:1 weight ratio on SS foils. The electrodes were vacuum-dried at 60 °C overnight, and Mo6S8 mass loading was 1.6 mg cm–2. The Te electrodes were prepared by casting a slurry comprising Te/porous carbon composite, Ketjen black (EC-600JD, Sigma-Aldrich), and PVDF (8:1:1 weight ratio) on Al foils followed by vacuum drying at 120 °C for 12 h, with a Te mass loading of 0.5 mg cm–2. The Te/porous carbon composite was synthesized following previous reports. , Te powder (−200 mesh, 99.8% trace metals basis, Sigma-Aldrich) and porous carbon (TOYO TANSO KOREA Co., Ltd., Korea) were mixed in a weight ratio of 7:3 through wet ball-milling at 600 rpm for 6 h, followed by heat treatment at 600 °C under Ar atmosphere.

Electrochemical Evaluations

All electrochemical measurements were conducted using CR 2032 coin-type cells assembled in an Ar-filled glovebox. The Mg||Mg symmetric cells, Mg||SS cells, and full-cells were constructed using a 12 pi Mg disk (0.1 mm thick, MTI Korea), 12 pi SS disk, or 10 pi prefabricated cathode, with two pieces of 18 pi glass microfiber separators (GF/F, Whatman). Prior to assembly, the Mg disks were polished with 2000 grit sandpaper and cleaned with hexane (anhydrous, 95%, Sigma-Aldrich). The Mg||Mo6S8 full-cells were operated in constant current (CC) mode during charge–discharge at the 1C rate (1C = 128 mAh g–1) within a voltage range of 0.2–2.2 V, without preactivation cycles. For the Mg||Te full-cells, a glass fiber separator coated with reduced graphene oxide (rGO) and carbon paper (Toray) were introduced as interlayers. The Te-based cells were operated in CC mode at 300 mA g–1 within 0.2–1.9 V with 10 activation cycles. For the GITT measurements, custom-made three-electrode cells were used. Each cell employed a 12 pi Mg disk as both the working (WE) and counter (CE) electrode, while a Cu mesh coated with electrodeposited Mg served as reference electrode (RE). All EIS measurements were conducted after 1 cycle (0.1 mA cm–2–0.5 mAh cm–2), using a potentiostat (VSP, Bio-Logic, France) over a frequency range from 1.000 MHz to 0.500 mHz.

Characterization Techniques

Surface morphology and elemental analysis were performed using SEM-EDS (JSM-7800F Prime microscope, JEOL, Japan) equipped with a Silicon Drift Detector (SDD, 80 mm2 active area). ToF-SIMS analysis was conducted using a ToF.SIMS-5 instrument (ION-TOF, Germany) with a 30 keV Bi+ primary beam and a 0.5 keV Cs+ beam for depth profiling (400 × 400 μm2 grid size). 1H and 19F NMR samples were prepared using coaxial inserts with benzene-d6 (99.9 atom % D, Sigma-Aldrich) and spectra were acquired using a BRUKER NMR spectrometer (BRUKER, ADVANCE III HD, 600 MHz, Cryoprobe). SEM-EDS, ToF-SIMS, and NMR analyses were performed at the National Centre for Interuniversity Research Facilities (NCIRF) at Seoul National University. Raman spectroscopy was carried out using a LabRAM HR Evolution spectrometer (HORIBA, Japan) at the Research Institute of Advanced Materials (RIAM), Seoul National University, employing a 633 nm helium neon laser source over a range of 700 to 800 cm–1. XPS measurements were conducted by employing an AXIS-His instrument (KRATOS, U.K.) under Ar conditions at the Korea Institute of Ceramic Engineering and Technology (KICET). WAXS analyses were performed at beamline 4C of the In-vacuum Undulator 20 (IVU 20; 1.4 m short; 20 mm period) source (Incident X-ray wavelength (λ) = 1.24 Å) at Pohang Accelerator Laboratory (PAL). Samples were transferred into cylindrical quartz capillary and analyzed by using transmission geometry. Sample-to-detector distances (SDD) of 0.20 m was applied to measure different q range.

Computational Details

Density functional theory calculations were performed using the Q-Chem 6.1 software package. Geometry optimization and energy calculations utilized M06-2X/6-311 + G­(d,p) with a polarized continuum model (ε = 7.19, DME-like).

The binding energies (Gb) of solvation structures were calculated as Gb=∑Gi−Gcluster where ∑G i is the sum of the free energies of all molecules and the Mg2+ ion that formed the solvation structure, and G cluster is the free energy of the corresponding solvation structure. Larger positive values indicate greater stability of the solvated structure. The calculated structures and the electrostatic potential (ESP) surface were visualized using Iqmol. Electron density changes in solvent molecules upon coordination were analyzed and visualized using Multiwfn based on the electron density distributions before and after coordination.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS). The 0.3 M Mg­(OTf)2 electrolyte systems for simulation contained 152 OTf– and 76 Mg2+ units, with varying numbers of solvent molecules: 2331 ADMA, 2921 MOEA, and 2461 THFA. Initial configurations were generated using the Packmol package with a minimum interatomic spacing of 2 Å. The Generalized Amber Force Field (GAFF) was employed, with parameters generated via ACPYPE. , The Lennard-Jones (LJ) parameters between different atom types were obtained according to the arithmetic rule, and the Coulombic interactions were computed by employing the particle–particle particle-mesh method. All systems were first equilibrated in an isothermal–isobaric (NPT) ensemble at 1 atm and 298 K for 2 ns, followed by canonical (NVT) ensemble equilibration for 1 ns. Production runs were performed in the NVT ensemble for 5 ns using a time step of 1 fs.
