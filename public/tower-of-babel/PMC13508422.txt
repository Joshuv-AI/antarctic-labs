Small-bore vs. large-bore chest tubes for pneumothorax: a retrospective study
Authors: Yaxi Li, Tingting Wen, Yang Liu, Min Tan, Xiyuan Cao, Xiaolian Song, Xuan Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare the clinical efficacy and safety of small-bore (≤ 14 Fr) versus large-bore (> 14 Fr) chest tubes in the treatment of pneumothorax.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted. Patients were categorized into small-bore and large-bore groups based on the initial drainage tube size. Outcomes, including treatment efficacy, drainage duration, lung re-expansion time, hospital stay, complication rates, and analgesic use, were compared between groups. Subgroup analyses were performed based on pneumothorax type as primary and secondary spontaneous pneumothorax.ResultsIn the overall population of 95 patients (55 large-bore, 40 small-bore), no statistically significant differences were found between the two groups regarding treatment efficacy (90.9% vs. 80.0%, P = 0.063), drainage time, lung re-expansion time, or hospital stay. The large-bore group required a significantly higher equivalent dose of analgesics (P = 0.049). Complication rates were similar between groups. In patients with secondary spontaneous pneumothorax (SSP), the small-bore tube group demonstrated significantly shorter drainage duration and lung re-expansion time compared to the large-bore group (both P = 0.007).ConclusionIn this retrospective analysis, small-bore and large-bore chest tubes demonstrated comparable treatment efficacy for pneumothorax management, with no statistically significant difference observed between groups. The large-bore group required a higher equivalent analgesic dosage, although this finding is exploratory given the borderline statistical significance and absence of validated pain assessment instruments. In the SSP subgroup, small-bore tubes were associated with significantly shorter drainage duration and lung re-expansion time, though these findings warrant further prospective validation.

Introduction

Pneumothorax is a respiratory emergency caused by abnormal gas accumulation in the pleural cavity, leading to symptoms including dyspnea, chest pain, and irritative cough. Without prompt and effective management, progressive pneumothorax may impair both respiratory and circulatory functions, thereby posing a serious threat to patient survival. According to its etiology, pneumothorax is primarily classified into three categories: primary spontaneous pneumothorax (PSP), secondary spontaneous pneumothorax (SSP), and traumatic pneumothorax.

The therapeutic landscape for pneumothorax has evolved substantially in recent years. British Thoracic Society Guideline for pleural disease [1] recommends conservative observation for minimally symptomatic PSP regardless of size and supports ambulatory management with one-way valve devices for selected patients. Joint ERS/EACTS/ESTS clinical practice guidelines on adults with spontaneous pneumothorax [2] make a strong recommendation for needle aspiration over chest tube drainage as initial treatment for PSP. These contemporary guidelines reflect a paradigm shift toward less invasive initial management strategies for stable patients.

Nevertheless, when non-drainage strategies are contraindicated, unsuccessful, or deemed inappropriate such as in cases of tension physiology, significant symptoms, large or bilateral pneumothorax, or SSP with poor pulmonary reserve thoracic drainage remains essential. Thoracic drainage serves as the cornerstone therapeutic intervention for pneumothorax. The underlying mechanism involves the insertion of a catheter into the pleural cavity to facilitate continuous gas evacuation, thereby restoring negative intrapleural pressure and enabling re-expansion of the collapsed lung tissue. In clinical practice, the selection of drainage catheter diameter remains diverse. A notable divergence exists in international guidelines concerning the optimal chest tube size for initial pneumothorax management. According to the American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP), large-bore chest tubes (16–28 Fr) are recommended for the treatment of larger or unstable primary and secondary spontaneous pneumothorax. Conversely, the British Thoracic Society (BTS) guideline advises that small-bore tubes (≤ 14 Fr) are adequate for most cases of pneumothorax and are associated with less pain [1]. The discrepancy in historical versus contemporary guidelines, combined with variations in real-world practice, continues to generate clinical uncertainty, particularly for SSP patients in whom evidence remains sparse.

To address this existing evidence gap, specifically, the comparative effectiveness of small-bore versus large-bore chest tubes when drainage is clinically indicated, with a focus on real-world outcomes in a Chinese tertiary care setting, this study aims to conduct a retrospective analysis to systematically compare the therapeutic efficacy and safety profiles of small-bore versus large-bore chest tubes in the management of pneumothorax. The findings are intended to provide more robust, real-world evidence to inform clinical decision-making within the broader framework of modern pneumothorax management.

Methods

Study design and patients

This single-center retrospective study was conducted at Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital from June 2020 to December 2022. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital (Approval No.SHSY-IEC-6.0/25K162/P01). As a retrospective study utilizing anonymized data, the requirement for informed consent from patients was waived.

Patients were included in the present study if they met all of the following criteria: (1) aged ≥ 16 years, (2) radiographically confirmed pneumothorax requiring closed thoracic drainage based on clinical assessment, and (3) availability of complete and traceable clinical medical records. Patients meeting any of the following criteria were excluded from the study: (1) tension pneumothorax; (2) concurrent active bleeding or severe coagulation dysfunction; (3) contraindications to thoracic drainage or inability to tolerate the procedure; (4) absence of key clinical data (e.g., drain size, removal time); or (5) initial chest tube placement as a postoperative surgical drain.

A total of 137 patients with pneumothorax were initially screened. According to the exclusion criteria, the following patients were excluded: 2 patients aged < 16 years, 13 patients who received observational management only, 2 patients with tension pneumothorax, 19 patients with missing key clinical data, and 6 patients who underwent surgical intervention as first treatment. The 19 patients excluded due to missing key clinical data lacked one or more of the following: documented drain size, exact date of tube removal, or complete analgesic administration records. Comparison of available demographic characteristics (age, sex, pneumothorax type) between excluded and included patients showed no statistically significant differences, suggesting limited attrition bias. Consequently, 95 patients were enrolled in the final analysis, with 55 patients allocated to the large-bore group and 40 patients to the small-bore group. Of these, 47 patients presented with PSP, 36 with SSP, and 12 with traumatic pneumothorax.

Patients were grouped based on the drain size (French gauge, Fr) explicitly documented in the record of the first closed thoracic drainage procedure. The threshold of ≤ 14 Fr to define small-bore tubes was selected based on the classification commonly used in international guidelines [1], which considers tubes ≤ 14 Fr as small-bore. According to conventional clinical classification, patients treated with tubes ≤ 14 Fr were assigned to the Small-Bore Group, while those treated with tubes > 14 Fr (typically 16–32 Fr) were assigned to the Large-Bore Group. The selection of tube size was based on material availability, the clinical judgment and preference of the treating physician at the time of intervention, rather than a standardized institutional protocol. This introduces a potential source of selection bias, as tube size may have been influenced by patient-specific clinical characteristics (e.g., perceived severity of air leak, suspicion of hemopneumothorax, or underlying pulmonary pathology). The grouping was entirely based on the initial treatment record.

Data collection

All required data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system (HIS) and Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). A standardized data collection form was designed. Data extraction and entry were performed independently by two trained researchers. For inconsistent or ambiguous data entries, the principal investigator reviewed the original records for arbitration to ensure data accuracy and consistency. All patient information was anonymized prior to analysis.

Study variables and measurement

The following demographic and clinical characteristics were collected for each patient: age, gender, height, weight, pneumothorax history, pneumothorax side (left/right), pneumothorax type (primary/secondary), and pneumothorax size.

Pneumothorax size was quantified using the Light Index on standard upright posteroanterior chest radiographs taken before treatment. The calculation formula was: Pneumothorax size (%) = [1 − (L³ / H³)] × 100%, where L is the lung diameter and H is the hemithorax diameter. The measurements for L and H were performed according to Light’s criteria [3]. For patients unable to undergo upright PA radiography (e.g., severe SSP or trauma patients), bedside anteroposterior or supine radiographs were used. In these instances, the inherent pleural magnification effect of supine/AP imaging is acknowledged, and pneumothorax size estimates should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Furthermore, the Light Index assumes concentric lung collapse and has been shown to systematically underestimate pneumothorax size compared with CT volumetric measurements. Pneumothorax size was determined through joint analysis by a radiologist and a pulmonologist. Both assessors were aware of the patient’s clinical condition; formal blinding to treatment group was not performed, which represents a potential source of measurement bias. Inter-rater reliability data were not formally collected.

Clinical efficacy was evaluated according to the following criteria: Markedly effective: Complete gas absorption and full lung re-expansion confirmed by imaging, accompanied by resolution of all clinical symptoms. Effective: Imaging demonstrated significant gas reduction (> 50%) with near-complete lung re-expansion and substantial alleviation of clinical symptoms. Ineffective: After two weeks of drainage, the lung remained unexpanded, symptoms showed no improvement, or persistent massive air leakage required further surgical intervention. This three-tier classification, while adapted from clinical practice conventions, is not a formally validated international outcome instrument. The use of clinician-defined efficacy categories represents a limitation, and future studies should incorporate validated outcome measures. The 14-day threshold for defining ineffective treatment was selected based on clinical convention, reflecting the point at which persistent air leak is generally considered to warrant surgical evaluation. Patients who required surgical intervention before day 14 were categorized as ineffective and were excluded from the drainage duration and re-expansion time analyses, as their drainage course was truncated by surgery rather than by clinical resolution of pneumothorax. The overall effective rate was calculated as: (number of markedly effective cases + number of effective cases) / total number of cases × 100.Lung re-expansion time was defined as the interval (in days) from chest-tube insertion until no air leakage was observed in the water-seal system during quiet breathing and coughing over 24 consecutive hours. Drainage duration was calculated as: (actual tube removal date − tube insertion date + 1) days. The decision to remove the chest tube was based on daily clinical evaluation and absence of air leak that no air bubbles were observed in the water seal chamber despite vigorous coughing, and chest radiography confirming full lung expansion. Hospital stay was calculated as: (discharge date − admission date) days. The complication rate included tube-related adverse events occurring during drainage, such as subcutaneous emphysema, pulmonary edema, local infection, and pleural effusion. Analgesic use was recorded as the total dose of non‑steroidal anti‑inflammatory drugs (primarily loxoprofen sodium or celecoxib) administered during drainage. Based on an equivalent‑dose conversion [4], all doses were converted to “loxoprofen sodium equivalent milligrams” for comparison. Patients who received no analgesics during the drainage period were included in the median calculation with a dosage of zero.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0. Most variables were found to be non-normally distributed. Hence, continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were expressed as frequency and proportion. The non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare quantitative variables between two groups. Chi-square test was used for categorical variables. Fisher’s exact test was applied if any expected cell count was < 5. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Subgroup analyses

The results of subgroup analyses based on pneumothorax type are detailed in Tables 6 and 7. Among patients with primary spontaneous pneumothorax, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups (all P > 0.05)(see Table 6). Notably, a baseline imbalance in initial pneumothorax size was identified in the PSP subgroup: patients in the large-bore group presented with a statistically significantly larger median pneumothorax size (50% [41–70] vs. 50% [40–50], P = 0.045). This discrepancy constitutes a potentially important confounder in the PSP subgroup analysis, as larger initial pneumothorax may inherently require longer drainage duration and exhibit lower spontaneous resolution rates.

Among patients with SSP, the distribution of underlying pulmonary diseases was as follows: COPD (n = 21), pulmonary infection (n = 3), lung malignancy (n = 3), bronchial asthma (n = 2), pulmonary tuberculosis (n = 2), asthma-COPD overlap (n = 1), asthma with lung tumor (n = 1), and COPD with pulmonary infection (n = 3). This shows that COPD was the main etiology among SSP patients in our center, accounting for 69.4% of SSP cases. This etiological heterogeneity is acknowledged as a limitation in the interpretation of SSP subgroup findings. In patients with SSP, both drainage duration and lung re-expansion time were significantly shorter in the small-bore tube group compared to the large-bore tube group, with statistically significant differences (both P = 0.007) (see Table 7). The findings, while statistically significant, are based on a modest sample size(n = 36) and should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Discussion

Before interpreting these findings, it is essential to contextualize them within the contemporary therapeutic landscape. The BTS 2023 and ERS/EACTS/ESTS 2024 guidelines emphasize conservative observation and needle aspiration as first-line strategies for stable PSP. Our study does not compare drainage against non-drainage management; rather, it addresses the clinical scenario where closed thoracic drainage has been deemed necessary by the treating clinician. In this context, the question of optimal tube caliber remains clinically relevant, particularly for SSP where evidence is most limited.

This study aimed to compare the clinical efficacy and safety of small-bore (≤ 14 Fr) versus large-bore (> 14 Fr) chest tubes in the treatment of pneumothorax through a retrospective analysis. The findings that no significant difference between the two groups align with the core conclusions of several studies [5, 6]. A meta-analysis including 11 studies with a total of 875 patients similarly reported that small-bore catheters and large-bore chest tubes had comparable success rates as initial treatment for pneumothorax [7]. While some earlier studies reported advantages for small-bore tubes in these metrics, a result similar to ours has also been documented in other literature. This suggests that such differences may be influenced by various factors, including patient heterogeneity, disease severity, and institutional practice patterns. Furthermore, although the rate of analgesic use was similar between the two groups, The small-bore group showed a statistical trend associated with reduced analgesic consumption (P = 0.049). It is critical to note that pain intensity was not measured using validated instruments (e.g., Visual Analog Scale or Numeric Rating Scale). Analgesic consumption serves as an indirect and imperfect proxy for patient comfort, and differences in analgesic prescribing practices, patient pain thresholds, and comorbidities may confound this association. Consequently, this finding is exploratory and should be interpreted with appropriate caution rather than as definitive evidence of superior pain management.

Given the heterogeneity in the etiology of pneumothorax, subgroup analyses based on the main types were conducted in this study to explore the potential impact of tube size selection. In patients with primary spontaneous pneumothorax (PSP), the findings from the subgroup analysis were consistent with those of the overall population analysis. Although there was a numerical difference in treatment success rates between the large‑bore group (96.0%) and the small‑bore group (86.4%), this difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of drainage duration, lung re‑expansion time, or hospital stay. These results align with existing study in some respects but also show subtle differences. One randomized controlled trial reported numerically better outcomes in the small‑bore group regarding success rate, re‑expansion time, and hospital stay, though the differences did not reach statistical significance [8]. Moreover, multiple studies [9, 10, 11] support the view that small‑bore tubes exhibit comparable treatment efficacy, and in some aspects such as patient comfort and complication risk superior to large‑bore tubes for the treatment of PSP.

However, it should be noted that in the baseline data of the PSP population, patients in the large-bore group had a statistically significantly larger initial pneumothorax size (p = 0.045). This, to some extent, reflects real-world clinical practice, where clinicians tend to prefer large-bore tubes for patients with larger pneumothorax. Even though evidence suggests that for PSP with a certain degree of lung collapse (e.g., < 92.5%) [12], small‑bore drainage tubes can ensure a reliable success rate. However, this baseline imbalance may confound the interpretation of outcomes within this subgroup, as larger initial collapse is known to potentially require longer treatment duration and potentially lower success rates [13]. Given the smaller pneumothorax size distribution in the small-bore tube group in the present study, it cannot be ruled out that the advantage in pneumothorax volume contributed to the lack of statistically significant difference in efficacy between the small-bore and large-bore tubes. Therefore, the conclusion that there is no difference in efficacy between the two types of tubes for PSP at our center should be interpreted with greater caution.

Based on current evidence, there is broad consensus in the academic community that small‑bore drainage tubes hold an important role in the initial management of PSP patients, including adults, adolescents [14], and children [15]. Therefore, although this study did not demonstrate the superior efficacy of small‑bore tubes in terms of drainage efficiency, their recognized advantages in patient comfort and analgesia management, along with the widespread recommendations in international guidelines, support the use of small‑bore drainage tubes as a reasonable and recommended initial treatment option for most patients with uncomplicated PSP undergoing closed thoracic drainage tube placement.

Secondary spontaneous pneumothorax (SSP) differs significantly from PSP in terms of etiology, patient characteristics, and clinical outcomes. SSP typically occurs secondary to underlying pulmonary diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), interstitial lung disease, pulmonary infection, or malignancy. Patients are generally older, have more comorbidities, and experience poorer overall prognosis, often with higher recurrence rates and longer hospital stays [16]. In the SSP cases COPD was the main etiology among SSP patients in our center accounting for 69.4% of SSP cases.

In the SSP subgroup analysis, this study found that the success rates of drainage were 80% for the large‑bore tube group and 68.8% for the small‑bore tube group, with no statistically significant difference between the two. However, both drainage duration and lung re‑expansion time were significantly shorter in the small‑bore tube group compared to the large‑bore tube group. Given the modest sample size of the SSP cohort (n = 36) and its etiological heterogeneity, these findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. This finding is supported by a randomized controlled trial [17] including 22 patients, which showed comparable efficacy between 14 Fr and 30 Fr catheters in SSP, with significantly shorter drainage time and hospital stay in the small‑bore group. Similar trends have been reported in other studies. Liu et al. [18] observed that small‑bore tubes might reduce drainage and hospital days, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. Vedam and Barnes [11] also suggested that small‑bore tubes may shorten the average hospital stay. Together with the present study, these findings indicate a potential advantage of small‑bore tubes in this population. The underlying mechanism may be related to the smaller tissue injury during placement of small‑bore tubes, leading to a milder local inflammatory response. This could explain the more pronounced pain and higher analgesic requirements observed after large‑bore tube insertion. Small‑bore drainage tubes may reduce pleural irritation and exudation, thereby creating more favorable conditions for lung re‑expansion and earlier tube removal. Furthermore, patients with small‑bore tubes experience less pain, which encourages earlier mobilization, effective coughing, and respiratory exercises [19, 20]. These activities actively promote lung re‑expansion and secretion clearance, which is particularly important for SSP patients with underlying lung diseases. The minimally invasive nature and better tolerability of small‑bore tubes may thus be key factors contributing to shorter drainage and re‑expansion times in SSP patients. Nevertheless, an apparent paradox warrants discussion, despite significantly shorter drainage duration and re-expansion time in the small-bore group, no significant difference in total hospital stay was observed between the two groups (8.5 days vs. 9 days, P = 0.904). This discrepancy may be explained by several factors that are particularly relevant to the SSP population.

First, the management of underlying comorbidities often dictates the length of hospitalization independently of pneomothorax resolution. In our SSP cohort, COPD accounted for 69.4% of cases. These patients frequently require extended treatment for acute exacerbations, including intravenous corticosteroids, antibiotics, and respiratory therapy, which cannot be safely administered on an outpatient basis even after complete lung re-expansion. Second, institutional discharge practices may contribute to this paradox at our center patients with significant underlying lung disease often undergo a mandatory observation period of 24–48 h after chest tube removal to monitor for early recurrence or respiratory deterioration, a precautionary measure that adds to hospital stay regardless of tube size. Third, while small-bore tubes facilitate earlier removal, the time saved may represent only a fraction of the total hospitalization. For example, if a large-bore tube is removed on day 8 and a small-bore tube on day 4, but both patients require an additional 5 days for comorbidity management and observation, the difference in total stay diminishes. Fourth, although our data did not capture re-intervention rates, some studies suggest that small-bore tubes may carry a higher risk of blockage or displacement, potentially necessitating tube manipulation or replacement [21]. Such events could offset the early time advantage gained from faster re-expansion. However, because relevant data on tube-related re-interventions were not systematically collected in this retrospective study, this potential explanation remains speculative. Future prospective studies should document tube patency, dislodgement events, and the need for repositioning or replacement to clarify whether these factors attentuate the hospital stay benefits suggested by shorter drainage times. This hospital stay paradox underscores the complexity of evaluating drainage efficacy in SSP, where the clinical course is often dominated by the underlying pulmonary disease rather than the pneumothorax itself.

It should be noted that due to the diverse etiologies of SSP and the limited sample size within each etiologic subgroup in this study, it is currently not possible to determine whether the advantage of small‑bore tubes is more pronounced in specific underlying conditions such as COPD or interstitial lung disease. This represents an important direction for future research.

In this study, 10 patients with traumatic pneumothorax received large-bore chest tubes, while 2 patients received small-bore tubes. Due to the limited number of cases and low statistical power, subgroup analysis for this category of pneumothorax was not performed. As a retrospective study, our data reflect real-world practice, showing that small-bore tubes were less frequently used than large-bore tubes in the treatment of traumatic pneumothorax. This suggests that clinicians tend to prefer large-bore tubes, likely to prevent potential complications such as pleural effusion or hemopneumothorax. No conclusions regarding optimal tube size for traumatic pneumothorax can be drawn from this dataset.

In summary, current evidence along with the results of this study suggest that small‑bore drainage tubes can be an effective initial treatment option for SSP patients, with potential advantages in shortening key treatment periods. However, clinical decision‑making should still take into account the specific etiology, baseline pulmonary function, and potential risks associated with drain management in individual patients.

The strengths of this study include the provision of real-world evidence from a Chinese tertiary care cohort, which adds geographic diversity to the predominantly Western literature. Additionally, the observation of shortened drainage time in the SSP subgroup, while requiring validation, represents a potentially valuable signal that warrants further investigation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective design with non-randomized treatment groups, which may introduce selection bias. Specifically, the choice of chest tube size was based on clinician preference, raising the risk of confounding by indication. Large-bore tubes may have been preferentially selected for patients presenting with a more severe clinical phenotype such as larger initial lung collapse, suspicion of hemopneumothorax, or poor pulmonary reserve, which inherently biases the comparison of outcomes. While most baseline characteristics were comparable, the influence of unmeasured confounding factors cannot be fully excluded. Due to the retrospective design and sample size, a multivariable regression analysis to adjust for confounders was not feasible. unmeasured confounding variables may influence the observed outcomes, and therefore, the results represent associations rather than causal relationships. Second, the limited sample size in this study represents another limitation, which may affect statistical power and restrict the comprehensiveness of the conclusions particularly for the SSP subgroup analyses. Within the PSP subgroup, the baseline imbalance in pneumothorax size further complicates the interpretation of comparative efficacy. these results should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Third, several process-level variables that may influence outcomes were not systematically documented, including suction pressure protocols, quantitative air leak assessments, tube insertion technique (Seldinger vs. blunt dissection), and tube blockage rates. Fourth, outcome assessment had methodological constraints. Pneumothorax size was measured using the Light Index on 2D radiography, which systematically underestimates size compared to CT volumetry, particularly in non-concentric collapse. The radiologist and pulmonologist were not blinded to treatment groups, and inter-rater reliability was not assessed. The three-tier efficacy classification is clinician-defined rather than a validated instrument. Pain intensity was not measured using validated scales; analgesic consumption serves only as an indirect proxy for comfort. Fifth, the exclusion of ineffective cases from time-to-event analyses (Table 5), while clinically justified, introduces potential immortal time bias. Sixth, the extremely small traumatic pneumothorax subgroup precludes any conclusions for this etiology. Finally, as a single-center study conducted in a tertiary care hospital in China, the generalizability of the findings to different healthcare systems or community settings may be limited.

Based on these findings and limitations, future research should focus on conducting prospective, multicenter randomized controlled trials targeting SSP patients to further validate the efficacy advantages of small-bore tubes and clarify their optimal target population. Alternatively, future studies may explore more refined subgroup analyses incorporating underlying lung disease types such as COPD, interstitial lung disease and include quality of life and physiological activity measures to comprehensively assess treatment benefits and influencing factors.

Conclusion

This retrospective study suggests that small-bore and large-bore chest tubes have comparable treatment effectiveness for the management of pneumothorax. The large-bore group required a higher equivalent dose of analgesics, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously because pain intensity was not assessed using validated instruments (e.g., Visual Analog Scale or Numeric Rating Scale). In the SSP subgroup, small-bore tubes were associated with shorter drainage duration and lung re-expansion time; therefore, small-bore tubes may be considered a reasonable option in selected SSP patients, although these observations require prospective validation. Clinical decision-making should be individualized, taking into account the type of pneumothorax, the patient’s underlying condition, and treatment tolerance.
