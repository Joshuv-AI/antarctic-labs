Efficacy and Safety of Somapacitan versus Daily Growth Hormone in Children with Growth Hormone Deficiency: A Pooled Analysis of Randomised Trial Data
Authors: Lars Sävendahl, Aristides K. Maniatis, Alberto Pietropoli, Nicky Kelepouris, Michael Højby, Jean-Marc Ferran, Junfen Fu, Jun Mori, Martin Wabitsch, Bradley S. Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSeveral studies have individually suggested that somapacitan, a once-weekly growth hormone (GH) treatment, has similar efficacy and safety to once-daily GH in children with GH deficiency. Here, we assessed the efficacy and safety of somapacitan versus daily GH after 52 and 156 weeks of treatment using pooled data from the REAL 3, REAL 4, and REAL 6 trials.MethodsParticipants receiving somapacitan 0.16 mg/kg/week or daily GH 0.034 mg/kg/day were included. Some participants switched from daily GH to somapacitan after 52 weeks of treatment, so data beyond week 52 were analysed separately. Outcomes, including height velocity (HV) and adverse events (AEs), were analysed by treatment group. An additional analysis was conducted in relation to puberty onset and Tanner stage.ResultsAt week 52, HV was similar in participants receiving somapacitan and those receiving daily GH. At week 156, mean (SD) HV (cm/year) was 7.5 (1.6) in participants who had always received somapacitan, 7.6 (2.0) in those who had always received daily GH, and 7.8 (1.4) in those who switched to somapacitan from daily GH. Most AEs were mild and unlikely to be treatment-related. HV remained stable for up to 130 weeks in pubertal children, and was similar across treatment groups and Tanner stages.ConclusionsIn this pooled analysis of clinical trial data, somapacitan demonstrated similar efficacy and safety to daily GH for up to 156 weeks of treatment. A dose of 0.16 mg/kg/week of somapacitan was able to maintain consistent growth in children and adolescents who reached puberty.

Introduction

Growth hormone deficiency (GHD) is one of the main causes of short stature in children starting treatment with recombinant human growth hormone (GH) [1]. Treatment with recombinant GH can promote growth and allow children with GHD to achieve their expected adult height [1]. Treatment can be administered as either once-daily injections of recombinant human GH product or once-weekly injections of products containing analogues of human GH that have a longer duration of action. Both options have demonstrated efficacy in increasing the height of children with GHD- and non-GHD-related short stature due to growth failure [2–7].

Somapacitan (Sogroya®, Novo Nordisk A/S, Bagsvaerd, Denmark) is a long-acting GH (LAGH) treatment that is designed to be administered once weekly for the treatment of GHD in children and adults [8, 9]. Several clinical trials have investigated the efficacy and safety of once-weekly somapacitan compared with daily GH (Norditropin®, Novo Nordisk A/S, Bagsvaerd, Denmark) [5, 10, 11]. The results of these trials have supported the approval of somapacitan in the European Union, the USA, and other regions [8, 9]. In addition to non-inferiority to once-daily GH, treatment with somapacitan is associated with a reduced treatment burden due to less frequent injections compared with daily GH [12], possibly leading to improved adherence to treatment [13].

REAL 3 (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02616562), REAL 4 (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03811535), and REAL 6 (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04970654) were three such randomised, controlled trials that were designed to compare the efficacy and safety of somapacitan versus Norditropin® in children with GHD. REAL 3 results at 26 weeks, 1 year, 2 years, 3 years, 4 years, and 7 years confirmed sustained increases in height outcomes and similarities in insulin-like growth factor-I (IGF-I) standard deviation score (SDS) for participants receiving somapacitan versus daily GH, alongside other benefits in terms of efficacy and safety [10, 12, 14–16]. Results from the 1-year and 3-year data from REAL 4 demonstrated noninferiority for height velocity (HV), as well as similarities in other height outcomes and IGF-I SDS, for somapacitan compared with daily GH [5, 17]. Similarly, for REAL 6, noninferiority for HV and similarities for other height outcomes and IGF-I SDS for somapacitan compared with daily GH were observed after 1 year of treatment [11].

Despite promising results in clinical trials, data regarding the long-term safety of LAGH formulations are relatively sparse, and their use continues to be closely monitored. Several clinical considerations, such as IGF-I monitoring, are of importance when considering treatment with an LAGH such as somapacitan [18]. Furthermore, few data exist about the use of GH in children of pubertal age and, indeed, whether GH dose should be changed in individuals with GH progressing through puberty [19].

To further assess the efficacy and safety of somapacitan versus daily GH in children with GHD, we conducted a pooled analysis, up to week 156 of treatment, of data from REAL 3, REAL 4, and REAL 6. This included a separate analysis of children who reached puberty during these trials to determine whether changes in dose are necessary for the attainment of optimal growth outcomes.

Methods

Trial Design

The REAL 3, REAL 4, and REAL 6 clinical trials were selected for inclusion in this pooled analysis, as they all included participants treated with somapacitan or daily GH and had similar trial designs and inclusion criteria. The full methodologies for all three trials have been previously published and are briefly described in Figure 1 [5, 10–12, 14].

Study design for REAL 3, REAL 4, and REAL 6 up to week 52 (a) and REAL 3 and REAL 4 up to week 156 (b) [5, 10, 11]. GH, growth hormone; GHD, growth hormone deficiency.

In summary, REAL 3 was a phase 2, open-label, active-controlled, double-blind trial conducted in 11 countries across Asia, Europe, and the Americas. The trial began in March 2016 and concluded in September 2024. The initial trial period comprised a 26-week main phase followed by a 26-week extension phase. A 104-week safety extension was then conducted, followed by a 208-week long-term safety extension and a 4-week follow-up period [10, 12, 14, 20]. REAL 4 was a phase 3, open-label, active-controlled trial being conducted in 20 countries across Asia, Europe, and the USA. The trial design consisted of a 52-week main phase followed by a 156-week safety extension. The trial began in May 2019 and concluded in September 2025 [5, 15, 17, 21]. Finally, REAL 6 was a phase 3, open-label, active-controlled, multicentre trial conducted in China consisting of a 52-week main phase. The trial began in July 2021 and concluded in December 2023 [11].

Participants

The full inclusion/exclusion criteria were similar for all three trials [5, 10, 11]. In brief, prepubertal children with a confirmed diagnosis of GHD and no prior exposure to GH treatment were enrolled and randomised to receive either once-weekly somapacitan or daily GH. In all three trials, pubertal status was determined using Tanner staging and age at screening: girls meeting Tanner stage 1 for breast development (i.e., no palpable glandular breast tissue) and pubic hair, age of ≥2 years and 26 weeks to ≤9 years; and boys with Tanner stage 1 for pubic hair and testes volume of <4 mL, age of ≥2 years and 26 weeks to ≤10 years were enrolled. Children with any clinically significant abnormality deemed likely to affect growth or the measurement or ability to evaluate growth were excluded.

Treatment

In REAL 3, participants were randomised 1:1:1:1 to receive once-weekly somapacitan (0.04, 0.08, or 0.16 mg/kg/week) or daily GH (0.034 mg/kg/day) until week 52. For the safety extension (weeks 52–156), all participants receiving somapacitan were switched to the 0.16 mg/kg/week dose, and those receiving daily GH continued at the same dose. In REAL 4, participants were randomised 2:1 to receive once-weekly somapacitan (0.16 mg/kg/week) or daily GH (0.034 mg/kg/day) until week 52. For the safety extension phase (weeks 52–156), all participants were treated with somapacitan 0.16 mg/kg/week. Those treated with somapacitan from baseline continued the same dose, and those treated with daily GH from baseline were switched to somapacitan. Finally, in REAL 6, participants were randomised 2:1 to receive once-weekly somapacitan (0.16 mg/kg/week) or daily GH (0.034 mg/kg/day) until week 52.

For the purposes of the current pooled analyses, only participants receiving somapacitan 0.16 mg/kg/week or daily GH 0.034 mg/kg/day from baseline were included. Thus, participants in cohorts 2 and 3 (receiving 0.04 and 0.08 mg/kg/week of somapacitan, respectively) from the REAL 3 trial were excluded. Dose adjustments per protocol were made by weight adjustment only, unless IGF-I levels were elevated to a point that required a dose reduction [5].

Efficacy

The efficacy outcomes assessed during this pooled analysis included HV, HV SDS, height SDS, IGF-I SDS, bone age to chronological age (BA/CA) ratio, and body mass index (BMI) SDS. For the analysis of weeks 0–52, efficacy outcomes were statistically compared for participants treated with somapacitan (0.16 mg/kg/week) and those treated with daily GH (0.034 mg/kg/day) across the three trials. For weeks 0–156, efficacy outcomes were descriptively compared once participants were categorised, depending on the treatment regimen they received from baseline to week 156. The groups were defined as follows: (1) participants who received treatment with somapacitan from baseline to week 156 (somapacitan/somapacitan group); (2) participants who received treatment with daily GH from baseline to week 156 (daily GH/daily GH group); (3) participants who began treatment with daily GH at baseline and then switched to receive treatment with somapacitan from weeks 52 to 156 (daily GH/somapacitan group). Only participants from REAL 3 and REAL 4 were included in the analysis of outcomes from week 0 to week 156, as REAL 6 was conducted for 52 weeks only. To account for any variation in results due to the inclusion of pubertal participants, a sensitivity analysis excluding participants reaching puberty before or at weeks 52 and 156 was conducted.

The analysis of IGF-I measurements and the calculation of a weekly average IGF-I SDS have been previously described as per the methodology of the REAL 4 trial [17]. Sampling of peak, average, and trough IGF-I levels was conducted every 13 weeks during each of the three trials and is outlined in Table 1. Differences in the types of IGF-I samples (peak, average, and trough) collected at weeks 52, 104, and 156 across the three trials were corrected based on timing after dose by adding or subtracting a given value from the measured IGF-I SDS according to the timepoint when the sample was obtained (specific details have been previously published [22]). Samples were only collected up to week 52 for the REAL 6 trial, as it did not continue beyond week 52.

Safety

Adverse events (AEs) were assessed using descriptive data and presented by treatment regimen, Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) system organ class (SOC), and MedDRA preferred term. The incidence of AEs from baseline to week 52 was compared for participants treated with somapacitan 0.16 mg/kg/week versus daily GH 0.034 mg/kg/day using data from all three trials.

AEs were also assessed from week 52 to week 156, with participants categorised according to their treatment regimen, as previously described. As with efficacy, REAL 6 participants were not included in any analyses of safety between week 52 and week 156, as REAL 6 was conducted for 52 weeks only.

Pubertal Data Analysis

Two sub-analyses of participants who were treated with somapacitan 0.16 mg/kg/week post week 52 and who reached puberty during the REAL 4 trial were conducted. Outcomes of these analyses included height SDS, HV, HV SDS, and IGF-I SDS; all outcomes were analysed according to (1) puberty onset (defined as Tanner stage II or menarche) and (2) Tanner stages (I, II, III, and IV/V). For the analysis by puberty onset, menarche was only used as a criterion in the case that Tanner stage data were not available. For the analysis by Tanner stages, only data collected beyond 52 weeks of treatment were used, to ensure that all participants had been exposed to GH therapy for enough time to see its effect on growth, as well as for IGF-I levels to be reflective of somapacitan. For each individual participant, the mean value across all measurements of a given endpoint at a given Tanner stage was computed and used in the analysis [23]. For example, reference data for HV based on age, sex, and country/region were summarised according to the Tanner stage a given participant was in. Participants could therefore contribute to more than one Tanner stage. Some participants in REAL 3 also reached puberty; however, these participants were excluded from the pubertal data analysis due to difficulties with determining the accurate timing of puberty onset. The exclusion of these participants was a deliberate choice to ensure accurate reporting of results and robust analyses.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical comparisons were conducted for efficacy outcomes at week 52 across REAL 3, REAL 4, and REAL 6. On-treatment datapoints were used for the efficacy analyses. Participants in each trial were analysed “as treated” according to their contribution to the full analysis set (FAS) and the safety analysis set (SAS). The FAS included all participants who were randomly assigned to either once-weekly somapacitan or daily GH; the SAS included all participants who received at least one dose of randomised treatment.

Mixed models of repeated measurements were used to compare HV, change in height SDS, change in HV SDS, and change in IGF-I SDS between participants treated with somapacitan versus those treated with daily GH. Each mixed model was nested by visit and treatment, study, sex, age group, region, GH peak group, and sex by age group by region interaction term as factors. IGF-I SDS at baseline and the baseline value for the respective outcome were used as the covariates. For the HV model, baseline height was used in place of baseline HV. For IGF-I SDS, in addition to peak and trough IGF-I levels, comparison between somapacitan average score and daily GH observed values was conducted and reported herein for weeks 52 and 156.

Changes in BA/CA ratio were compared via an analysis of covariance model, with treatment, study, sex, age group, region, GH peak group, and sex by age group by region interaction term as factors, and IGF-I SDS at baseline and BA/CA ratio at baseline as covariates.

Efficacy data are reported to one decimal place. Efficacy outcomes at week 156 and safety outcomes at week 52 and week 156 were to be given as descriptive data only. On-treatment risk period datapoints (from date of first administration to 14 days after last administration) were used for the safety analyses.

Safety

Weeks 0–52

At week 52, the rates of AEs were similar between the two treatment groups (294.6/100 patient-years in the somapacitan group and 289.0/100 patient-years in the daily GH group) (Table 3). Most AEs were mild in both the somapacitan (76.4%) and the daily GH (66.1%) groups, and the majority of AEs were judged unlikely to be related to treatment (77.7% for somapacitan vs. 67.8% for daily GH). The most common AEs occurring in ≥5% of participants in the somapacitan group were pyrexia (n = 29; 13.2%), bronchitis (n = 14; 6.4%), COVID-19 (n = 13; 5.9%), nasopharyngitis (n = 19; 8.6%), respiratory tract infection (n = 13; 5.9%), upper respiratory tract infection (n = 44; 20%), pain in extremity (n = 13; 5.9%), headache (n = 18; 8.2%), and cough (n = 18; 8.2%).

Two serious AEs (generalised oedema and vomiting) experienced by one participant in the somapacitan group were judged probably related to treatment; both were moderate in intensity and resolved over time. Injection-Site-Related AEs. There were a few reports of injection-site AEs (n = 8 [3.6%] and n = 8 [6.8%] in the somapacitan and daily GH groups, respectively). Two participants in the somapacitan group and one participant in the daily GH group reported mild and transient injection-site pain.

The participant in the somapacitan group, who was from the REAL 6 trial, discontinued treatment due to adenoidal hypertrophy (possibly related to treatment). The two participants in the daily GH group, both from the REAL 3 trial, discontinued treatment due to, in one case, drug hypersensitivity (probably related to treatment) and, in the other case, nephrotic syndrome (unlikely related to treatment), which was classed as a serious AE.

Weeks 52–156

From weeks 52 to 156, the rates of AEs were 188.8/100 patient-years in the somapacitan/somapacitan group, 198.5/100 patient-years in the daily GH/daily GH group, and 148.0/100 patient-years in the daily GH/somapacitan group (Table 3). Similar to the week 52 safety results, most AEs were mild (72.9% for the somapacitan/somapacitan group, 81.8% for the daily GH/daily GH group, and 75.0% for the daily GH/somapacitan group) and judged unlikely to be related to treatment (75.0% for the somapacitan/somapacitan group, 90.9% for the daily GH/daily GH group, and 77.9% for the daily GH/somapacitan group).

The type and frequency of AEs were similar between the somapacitan/somapacitan and daily GH/somapacitan groups. From weeks 52 to 156, the most common AEs occurring in ≥5% of participants in the somapacitan/somapacitan group were vomiting (n = 13; 9.0%), pyrexia (n = 13; 9.0%), bronchitis (n = 8; 5.6%), COVID-19 (n = 26; 18.1%), gastroenteritis (n = 9; 6.3%), influenza (n = 13; 9.0%), nasopharyngitis (n = 18; 12.5%), cough (n = 9, 6.3%), and rhinitis allergic (n = 8; 5.6%). The most common AEs occurring in ≥5% of participants in the daily GH/somapacitan group were pyrexia (n = 6; 8.8%), bronchitis (n = 4; 5.9%), COVID-19 (n = 16; 23.5%), nasopharyngitis (n = 9; 13.2%), upper respiratory tract infection (n = 6; 8.8%), viral infection (n = 4; 5.9%), and headache (n = 4; 5.9%).

Rates of serious AEs were 5.4/100 patient-years in the somapacitan/somapacitan group, 4.0/100 patient-years in the daily GH/daily GH group, and 1.5/100 patient-years in the daily GH/somapacitan group (Table 3). Across all groups, most serious AEs were deemed unlikely to be related to treatment. One event (lipoatrophy) in one participant in the somapacitan/somapacitan group was judged probably related to treatment; this was judged moderate in severity and had not resolved at the time of this analysis. Two events in one participant in this same group were judged possibly related to somapacitan treatment; these were pharyngotonsillitis (deemed severe) and lipoma (deemed moderate in severity), and had both resolved.

Injection-Site-Related AEs. There were no reports of injection-site AEs within the daily GH/daily GH group, and few were reported in the somapacitan/somapacitan and daily GH/somapacitan groups (n = 9 [6.3%] and n = 3 [4.4%], respectively). The most common injection-site AE in the somapacitan/somapacitan group was a nonspecific injection-site reaction and was reported by three participants (2.1%).

Treatment Discontinuations due to AEs. There were no treatment discontinuations in the week 52 to week 156 period.

Discussion

In this pooled analysis of data from REAL 3, REAL 4, and REAL 6, similar efficacy and safety profiles were observed for somapacitan and daily GH at week 52, and sustained efficacy and safety outcomes for somapacitan were observed at 156 weeks. This further supports the previously reported results from the three trials and further builds the well-established efficacy profile of somapacitan, emphasizing its status as an important alternative treatment option for children with GHD.

At week 52, no difference was observed between the two treatment groups for HV, HV SDS, change in HV SDS, height SDS, change in height SDS, BA/CA ratio, or change in BA/CA ratio. In particular, HV remained numerically similar between somapacitan and daily GH groups at week 52 and at week 156. Both HV and HV SDS decreased after the switch in treatment at week 52 in the daily GH/somapacitan group and the somapacitan/somapacitan group of the three REAL trials. However, HV and HV SDS were similar for participants in all three treatment regimen groups at week 156, indicating that growth response was not affected by switching from daily GH to somapacitan or continuing the same treatment regimen. Indeed, a decrease in HV and HV SDS is often seen in participants after 1 year of GH replacement treatment [24], which may partially explain the patterns in HV seen in this analysis. As baseline height SDS, HV, and HV SDS were similar between daily GH and somapacitan groups, it is possible that participants were following their growth potential based on their baseline characteristics rather than a difference in treatment regimen, resulting in similar growth patterns throughout 156 weeks. This is supported by the results of the REAL 4 trial, which, similarly to this pooled analysis, identified a continuous trend towards increased height SDS in each group regardless of treatment [15].

IGF-I levels are commonly used to monitor efficacy and adherence to GH treatment. It is recommended that IGF-I levels during GH replacement in children diagnosed with GHD should be maintained within the normal range of between −2 and +2 SDS [25]. LAGH formulations result in a different IGF-I profile to that seen with daily GH, with peaks and troughs over the weekly dosing interval [5]. Due to the relatively small fluctuations in IGF-I levels following treatment with daily GH, the sampling of IGF-I measurements during the dosing interval is not so strongly affected by the timing at which they are taken [26]. However, for LAGH formulations like somapacitan, it is important to consider the mean IGF-I levels over the dosing interval, as well as other potential factors such as time with elevated IGF-I levels (above 2 SDS) and other variables involving IGF-I concentrations. In this pooled analysis of REAL 3, REAL 4, and REAL 6, we saw a difference between the weekly average IGF-I SDS for somapacitan and observed IGF-I SDS for daily GH at week 52. Nevertheless, changes in IGF-I SDS did not move either of the treatment groups across the clinical threshold of +2 SDS. Indeed, both the observed and modelled average IGF-I SDS values for somapacitan and daily GH remained well within the normal range throughout the duration of the three trials, up to week 156. Given the known dose–response relationship between GH and IGF-I SDS [5], these results further corroborate the efficacy and safety profile of somapacitan reported by the three trials individually and in accordance with recently reported IGF-I SDS data from other somapacitan trials [27, 28]. Nevertheless, given the uncertainties pertaining to the long-term physiological effects of LAGH on IGF-I profiles, further studies investigating this and those focussing on adult outcomes are needed to determine whether any differing effects on IGF-I levels are exerted by LAGH and daily GH.

BMI SDS increased slightly from baseline to week 52 in the somapacitan group (from −0.2 to 0.3) and decreased slightly from baseline in the daily GH group (from −0.3 to −0.5). Similarly, from baseline to week 156, BMI SDS increased in the somapacitan/somapacitan group and after the switch in treatment regimen in the daily GH/somapacitan group. Importantly, the initial increase observed in the somapacitan group stabilised over time, and BMI SDS remained within the normal range for both the somapacitan and daily GH groups. In addition, the BMI SDS remains within the normal range now for up to 7 years of treatment with somapacitan, as recently reported [16].

A recent study by Levaillant et al. has approached the topic of BMI in the context of LAGH treatment [29]. BMI SDS significantly increased in participants treated with LAGH formulations after 1 year, yet remained stable for those treated with daily GH. This result was reciprocated in participants who switched from daily GH to LAGH; however, for those treated continuously with LAGH, BMI SDS appeared to stabilise after 1 year. While these results are pertinent, the authors acknowledge that the effect on BMI was limited in duration and magnitude, and that further studies are needed to assess any consequence of the transient increase in BMI SDS on body composition. As a surrogate measure, BMI does not provide information on body composition (e.g., lean mass and fat mass). Insights into the relative effects of somapacitan and once-daily GH treatment on body composition can be inferred from the REAL 1 phase 3 trial of somapacitan, daily GH, or placebo in adults with GHD, as well as a phase 3 switch study in adult GHD, both of which investigated body composition variables [30–32]. In summary, after 34 weeks of treatment, a similar reduction in visceral fat and similar increases in total lean body mass and appendicular skeletal muscle mass were observed for the somapacitan group and the daily GH group; a less pronounced reduction in truncal fat percentage was seen in the somapacitan group. Changes in body composition were maintained after 86 weeks of continuous treatment with somapacitan or daily GH, with no notable differences for percentage change in visceral fat, lean body mass, or appendicular skeletal muscle mass [30]. In the Japanese subpopulation, the change from baseline in truncal fat percentage was almost identical between somapacitan and daily GH groups [32]. This is likely to be a result of the Japanese subpopulation being more homogeneous and having more similar baseline characteristics than the global REAL 1 trial population. In addition, in this Japanese cohort, slightly more pronounced decreases in visceral fat and increases in lean body mass were observed for the somapacitan group compared to the daily GH group [32]. Long-term studies of the effect of LAGH administration on BMI SDS in children with GHD are now reported to be within the normal range for up to 7 years of treatment [16]. No long-term data on body composition in children with GHD treated with LAGH have been reported to date.

Somapacitan has been shown to have a similar safety profile to daily GH and sustained tolerability in children with GH deficiency in the previously published results from REAL 3, REAL 4, and REAL 6 [11, 12, 17]. In this pooled analysis, there were no new safety or tolerability issues identified at week 52 or at week 156, and most AEs were mild and judged as unlikely related to the study treatment. There were few injection-site reactions at either 52 weeks or 156 weeks. Treatment discontinuations due to AEs were also uncommon, with three participants discontinuing treatment in the initial 52 weeks (one within the somapacitan group and two in the daily GH group). Of these, two instances of treatment discontinuation due to AEs were deemed possibly or probably related to treatment – one in each of the treatment arms.

Information and guidance regarding the use of GH in individuals with GHD undergoing puberty is limited. While many studies focus on paediatric and adolescent participants, only a few older studies investigated the effect of GH and its dose on pubertal progression and total pubertal growth specifically [33, 34]. The present analysis of data from participants with GHD undergoing puberty during REAL 4 found that HV SDS remained consistently above 0 for up to 130 weeks after puberty onset, indicating that these children continued to grow more than those in the general population for the first 2.5 years of puberty. Prior to puberty, HV and IGF-I SDS followed a strong correlation, with peaks and troughs occurring at roughly the same timepoint and to an equivalent degree. Following puberty onset, HV remained constant at around 8 cm/year, whereas IGF-I SDS increased and followed an upward trajectory until 130 weeks but still remained within the normal range (−2 to +2 SDS). Analysis of pubertal data in relation to Tanner stage found no notable differences between participants who had always been treated with somapacitan (somapacitan/somapacitan group) and those who switched from daily GH to somapacitan (daily GH/somapacitan group). Similar to the analysis of data by puberty start, HV SDS remained above 0 across all Tanner stages, further supporting the notion that these children continue to grow faster than children in the general population across the different stages of puberty. A numerical increase in IGF-I SDS was seen at Tanner stage III (0.83 vs. 0.40 at stage II and 0.56 at stage I) and was maintained into Tanner stage IV/V (IGF-I SDS: 1.0). This is in line with the observations of the analysis based on puberty onset and could be explained by an increased variability of IGF-I levels during mid to late puberty, influenced by age and sex as well as the timing of the pubertal growth spurt [35, 36]. Given these results and the fact that the dose of somapacitan remained consistent at 0.16 mg/kg/week throughout the pubertal analyses, it may follow that changes in somapacitan dose during puberty are not necessary to obtain optimal height outcomes.

This pooled analysis of data from three large studies of somapacitan showed that it was possible to maintain consistent height SDS improvements in children without any change in somapacitan dose, including in participants who reached puberty during the treatment periods. A key strength of this analysis was that it investigated the efficacy and safety outcomes of somapacitan versus daily GH in a larger cohort of participants with GHD than for each trial individually, allowing for greater heterogeneity and better representation of this population. The long extension also adds credibility to the reported results, and comparisons of data side by side allowed for a greater overview of natural variability of growth, which is not always as easily distinguished in a controlled-trial setting.

Limitations of the analysis include the fact that there was a small sample size of participants in the daily GH/daily GH group (n = 11), and therefore no statistical comparisons could be made. In addition, no data were available from REAL 6 after 52 weeks, limiting the scope of the analyses at this timepoint. These two limitations combined highlight the need for larger and longer-term follow-up of participants to further strengthen the results of the present analysis. Different preferred terms were used to describe AEs between the three trials; thus, comparisons in terms of specific events were not possible. While the trial design and inclusion/exclusion criteria were largely aligned between trials, there were also minor differences that may have affected the analyses. Analyses of prepubertal data were subject to the possibility of participants starting puberty at or prior to 52 and 156 weeks and, in some cases, rested on menarche to determine pubertal progress. However, only a very small number of participants started puberty at or prior to 52 and 156 weeks, and the findings were not meaningfully impacted by this factor. Finally, the clinical significance of the differing BMI SDS patterns observed between the somapacitan and daily GH groups remains unclear. While the assessment of changes in body composition in young, growing children poses significant challenges, changes in BMI SDS that occur following the use of long-acting GH therapies should be explored not only in long-term follow-up studies but also in studies that specifically assess body composition by appropriate imaging measures, with the understanding that BMI is expected to change as children grow and age.

Despite these limitations, the large number of participants enrolled in these studies and consequently included in this analysis lends credibility to the study and its results. The study design allowed for increased statistical power, as well as the ability to focus on specific subgroups of participants who were going through puberty at the time of the study.

Conclusions

The results of this pooled analysis of data from three clinical trials show similar efficacy and safety for somapacitan and daily GH over 52 weeks of treatment in treatment-naïve children with GHD. These efficacy outcomes were sustained after 156 weeks and were also observed in participants switching from daily GH to somapacitan after 52 weeks. Furthermore, this analysis indicated that consistent improvements in height SDS could be sustained in children and adolescents with GHD, the latter of whom entered puberty, without the need to change recommended somapacitan dosing.
