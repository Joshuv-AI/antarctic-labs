Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell-derived TSG-6 regulates microglial pyroptosis and alleviates bone cancer pain by inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome
Authors: Danyang Xu, Mengna Jiang, Hao Ming, Jing Zhang, Yunqi Li, Ping Wu, Qingping Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe central sensitization mechanism of bone cancer pain is related to neuroinflammation, glial cell activation, and an acidic environment. Among them, the NLRP3 inflammasome is involved in the occurrence of bone cancer pain. Studies have shown that an intrathecal injection of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) alleviates bone cancer pain by inhibiting microglial cell activation. Upon inflammatory stimulation, BMSCs produce increased levels of Tumor necrosis factor-α-stimulated gene 6 (TSG-6), which regulates intercellular signaling and inflammatory responses; however, whether the secretion of TSG-6 from BMSCs inhibits NLRP3-mediated microglial pyroptosis to alleviate bone cancer pain is unknown. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the analgesic effect of TSG-6 released by BMSCs on bone cancer pain (BCP) and explore its potential mechanisms through in vitro and in vivo experiments.MethodsIn vivo, Walker 256 breast cancer cells were injected into the bone marrow cavity of the left tibia of rats to establish a BCP model. On days 7 and 14 after surgery, intrathecal injections of BMSCs or exogenous recombinant TSG-6 were administered, and the analgesic effects were observed at 2, 4, 8, 24, and 48 h after administration. Spinal cord tissues were collected for Western blotting and immunofluorescence staining to assess the activation of the NLRP3 signaling pathway. In vitro, BV2 microglia were cocultured with BMSCs or recombinant TSG-6, and the same detection methods were performed to evaluate changes in the levels of proteins involved in the NLRP3 signaling pathway. BMSCs transfected with TSG-6-targeting shRNA were intrathecally injected in vivo or cocultured with microglia in vitro to investigate whether TSG-6 is involved in the inhibition of microglial pyroptosis and the inflammatory response mediated by BMSCs to alleviate BCP.ResultsBMSC transplantation or treatment with exogenous recombinant TSG-6 significantly improved BCP-related behaviors, inhibited the expression of key proteins in the NLRP3 signaling pathway in spinal dorsal horn microglia in rats, and reduced the release of inflammatory factors. Experiments revealed that coculturing BMSCs with BV2 microglia or treating BV2 cells with exogenous recombinant TSG-6 inhibited the LPS-induced activation of NLRP3 in BV2 cells and regulated microglial pyroptosis. Transfection of BMSCs with TSG-6-targeting shRNA significantly weakened the inhibitory effect on microglial pyroptosis.ConclusionIn vivo and in vitro experiments revealed that bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells inhibit the NLRP3 signaling pathway to regulate the pyroptosis of microglia through the secretion of TSG-6, thereby alleviating bone cancer pain.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-05121-2.Keywords: Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), Tumor necrosis factor-α-stimulated gene 6 (TSG-6), NLRP3, Microglia, Pyroptosis, Bone cancer pain (BCP)

Introduction

Bone cancer pain (BCP) is among the most common complications in cancer patients with bone metastases or primary bone tumors and affects approximately 70%-80% of patients with advanced breast, prostate, and lung cancers [1]. BCP is characterized by a persistent dull ache accompanied by sudden, severe pain, which leads to severe sleep disorders, anxiety [2], and opioid dependence in patients and significantly decreases survival and reduces quality of life [3]. Currently, a variety of treatments are available for bone cancer pain, including bisphosphonates, opioids, radiotherapy and interventional therapy [4]. Although these modalities can relieve symptoms to a certain extent, their analgesic effects are poor because of drug resistance and related side effects. Therefore, exploring new therapeutic strategies has become a future direction for improving the management of bone cancer pain.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are a type of adult stem cell with self-renewal capacity and multipotent differentiation potential that can be isolated from various tissues, such as adipose tissue, the umbilical cord, and bone marrow. They have been widely used to treat diseases, including myocardial infarction, stroke, arthritis, and acute lung injury [5]. MSCs are believed to alleviate pain by repairing damaged tissues, modulating immune responses, and secreting bioactive factors [6, 7]. MSCs may also improve symptoms of chronic pain by improving the local microenvironment [8]. Therefore, an intrathecal injection of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) provides safe and effective analgesia [9]. Recent studies have also shown that intrathecal BMSC injection alleviates bone cancer pain by inhibiting microglial activation [10]. Research has indicated that the therapeutic benefits of MSC transplantation are primarily mediated by paracrine effects rather than through cell replacement, which is attributed to the various bioactive substances secreted by MSCs, such as IL-10, TGF-β, PGE₂, and TSG-6 [11]. Tumor necrosis factor-α-stimulated gene 6 (TSG-6), also known as TNFAIP6, is a secreted glycoprotein involved in inflammation and tissue remodeling [12] and has a molecular weight of approximately 35–38 kDa. It is constitutively expressed in a few tissues but is typically induced at inflammatory sites, where it regulates intercellular signaling and inflammatory responses [13]. Studies have shown that TSG-6 protects tissues from inflammatory damage in the context of certain conditions, such as acute myocardial infarction [12], atherosclerosis [14], liver fibrosis [15], acute pulmonary inflammation [16], and spinal cord injury [17]. Additionally, research has demonstrated that BMSCs suppress spinal microglial activation through the paracrine secretion of TSG-6 and that an intrathecal injection of exogenous TSG-6 can mitigate neuropathic pain [18]. In a traumatic brain injury (TBI) model, TSG-6 inhibited microglial pyroptosis, reduced the expression of inflammatory cytokines, and thereby improved neurological function [19]. However, whether BMSCs alleviate bone cancer pain via TSG-6 secretion remains unknown.

Although several studies have shown that BMSCs can alleviate bone cancer pain, their specific mechanism of action remains unclear [10, 20]. The occurrence of bone cancer pain involves complex neurobiological mechanisms. Studies have shown that inflammatory factors and acidic mediators in the tumor microenvironment stimulate peripheral sensory nerve endings, leading to the excitation and sensitization of sensory neurons, which constitute the peripheral mechanisms of bone cancer pain [21]. Among the central sensitization mechanisms in bone cancer pain are factors related to neuroinflammation, glial cell activation and acidic environments [22, 23]. During bone cancer pain, microglia shift from a resting phenotype to an activated phenotype. Activated microglia further exacerbate the sensitivity of spinal cord neurons through the release of a variety of proinflammatory cytokines [24, 25]. Microglia pyroptosis, a form of programmed cell death mediated by the NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, is characterized by sustained cell swelling until the cell membrane ruptures, at which time a large amount of the proinflammatory factor IL-1β is released, leading to intense neuroinflammation. Our previous findings suggest that microglial activation during the development of BCP promotes pain onset and maintenance through the NLRP3/IL-1β signaling pathway [26]. Studies have confirmed that NLRP3 not only drives mechanical nociceptive hypersensitivity in bone cancer pain models but also has a molecular crossover mechanism with various pain syndromes, such as neuropathic pain and migraine [27–29]. A deeper understanding of the role of cellular pyroptosis in bone cancer pain will aid in the development of new therapeutic strategies to alleviate patient suffering. Our preliminary research confirmed that BMSCs can effectively alleviate BCP, but whether BMSCs can treat bone cancer pain by regulating pyroptosis and neuroinflammation in spinal dorsal horn microglia remains unclear.

This study investigated the inhibitory effect of BMSCs on the pyroptosis of microglia in the dorsal spinal cord in a BCP model. Notably, RNA sequencing technology was used to identify potential cytokines secreted by BMSCs in the inflammatory microenvironment of microglia. The results indicate that TSG-6 may be a key factor in this process, and further research has explored the potential mechanisms through which TSG-6 exerts its anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects. These findings show that TSG-6 secreted by BMSCs can inhibit the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in microglia in the dorsal spinal cord and prevent their pyroptosis. These effects were observed both in vivo and in vitro, suggesting the therapeutic potential of TSG-6 for BCP. This study not only provides theoretical support for the therapeutic mechanisms of BMSCs in BCP but also highlights the critical role of TSG-6 in alleviating inflammation caused by bone cancer pain.

Materials and methods

The data from this study are reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Ethics statement

All experiments involving animals were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Dalian Medical University, with ethical approval number AEE24168, and were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for laboratory animal care and use. We implemented risk prevention measures to minimize any potential harm to both the animals and the environment. This study was conducted with a strong focus on ethical standards and animal welfare, ensuring that this research was both scientifically sound and ethically responsible.

BMSC cultivation and identification

Sprague–Dawley (SD) rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (SCSP-402) were purchased from the Cell Bank/Stem Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The cells were derived from SD rats aged 3–6 weeks and can differentiate into adipocytes, osteoblasts, and chondrocytes. The cells tested negative for mycoplasma, bacteria, and fungi, and the samples passed quality control tests and could be used directly for experiments. The cells were cultured in MEM Alpha (1 ×) + GlutaMAX™−1 medium (Gibco™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Fetal Bovine Serum (Prime) (FSP500), Excell, China) and three antibiotics (100 U/ml penicillin, 16 µg/ml streptomycin, and 10 µg/ml amphotericin B) in a constant temperature and humidity incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 (Sanyo Electric Co., Ltd., Japan). When the bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells reached 90% confluence, they were passaged at a ratio of 1:3. Cell morphology was observed under a microscope, flow cytometry was used to detect cell surface markers, and the ability of the BMSCs to undergo osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation (SSRCC, Shanghai, China) was assessed to identify the BMSCs after passage. Cells from the third to fifth generations were used for subsequent experiments.

Animal experiments

In this study, healthy, SPF-grade female SD rats, weighing 180–220 g, were acquired from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and the Liaoning Provincial Experimental Animal Resource Center in Dalian, China. The rats were placed in sterile plastic cages. They were housed at room temperature (23 ± 2 °C), with the relative humidity controlled at 40–60% on a 12-h light/dark cycle and given unlimited access to food and water.

Intrathecal catheter implantation

Three days before modeling, 1.25% tribromoethanol (Aladdin, Shanghai, China; H2409449) was injected intraperitoneally (IP) at a dose of 300 mg/kg to anesthetize all the rats. A 7-gauge needle was inserted closely along the L5 spinous process in the midline of the spine. When tail flicking or hind limb swinging was observed, the needle was confirmed to be in the subarachnoid space. A sterilized PE-10 catheter was placed at approximately 2 cm and fixed, with the external end of the catheter tunneled subcutaneously and exiting the skin at the neck, and was properly secured to prevent catheter dislodgment. Daily checks were conducted for spinal nerve injury, local infection, and catheter dislodgment, bleeding, or blockage. The day after the intrathecal catheterization, 20 µl of 2% lidocaine was injected intrathecally, followed by a slow flush of the catheter with saline. Rat hind limb paralysis was observed to determine if the catheter position was correct. If bilateral limb paralysis occurred 10–30 s after the injection, correct catheter placement was indicated; if no paralysis or unilateral paralysis was observed, incorrect catheter placement was indicated. Such cases were excluded from further intrathecal treatment.

Establishment of a rat model of bone cancer pain

The SD rats were randomly divided into a Sham group, BCP group, and the following BCP treatment groups: BCP + BMSC (BMSC group), BCP + shTSG-6 BMSC (shTSG-6 BMSC group), BCP + shNC BMSC (shNC BMSC group), BCP + 1 μg of rmTSG-6 (1 μg rmTSG-6 group), and BCP + 5 μg of rmTSG-6 (5 μg rmTSG-6 group). All rats in the BCP and BCP treatment groups received a direct injection of Walker 256 breast cancer cells into the bone marrow cavity of the left tibia to establish a bone cancer pain model. The rats were anesthetized with 1.25% tribromoethanol at 300 mg/kg via intraperitoneal (IP) injection. Then, the left tibial plateau was shaved, disinfected, and incised and the muscle was separated, avoiding the white patellar ligament, to expose the tibial plateau. A microinjection syringe was used to slowly inject Walker 256 cells (5 × 105 cells/10 μl) along the diameter of the tibial bone marrow cavity, avoiding penetration of the marrow cavity. After the injection was complete, the syringe remained in the marrow cavity for 2 min while the tibia was held in an upright position to prevent the cancer cells from leaking along the puncture track. After the syringe was removed, the hole was sealed with bone wax, and the wound was sutured. The Sham group underwent the same procedure with an injection of an equal volume of PBS. Aseptic techniques were strictly followed throughout the process.

BMSC or rmTSG-6 injection

On the 7th and 14th days after BCP surgery, the BMSC group was injected with BMSCs (5 × 106 cells/20 µl), the shTSG-6 BMSC group was injected with BMSCs transfected with TSG-6-targeting shRNA (5 × 106 cells/20 µl), the shNC BMSC group was injected with BMSCs transfected with NC shRNA (5 × 106 cells/20 µl), the 1 μg rmTSG-6 group was injected with 1 μg of recombinant human TSG-6 protein (R&D Systems, USA; 2104-TS-050) in 20 µl of PBS, and the 5 μg rmTSG-6 group was injected with 5 μg of recombinant human TSG-6 protein in 20 µl of PBS [18]. The rats in the Sham group and BCP group were injected with an equal amount of saline. After injection of the indicated materials, each group was flushed with 10 µl of saline, and the operation was completed via heat sealing of the tubes.

Behavioral tests

The animals’ status was observed daily after BCP surgery, and behavioral tests were performed 1 day (−1) before surgery and on Days 1, 4, 7, and 14 after surgery. The analgesic effect was analyzed at 2, 4, 8, 24, and 48 h after administration on Days 7 and 14. Before each behavioral test, the rats were placed in a cubic plastic container with a wire mesh floor for at least 30 min. Behavioral tests were performed by researchers blinded to the groups.

Assessment of the 50% paw withdrawal threshold (50% PWT)

During the measurement process, each group of rats was placed in a transparent glass box with a bottom made of wire mesh. A series of von Frey filaments (0.4, 0.6, 1.4, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 15 g) were used to stimulate the center of the plantar surface of the left hind paw of the rats, pressure was applied to bend the filaments for 5 s, the withdrawal response of the rats was observed, and the order and intensity of the stimuli were carefully recorded. If the rat exhibited a withdrawal or licking response during this period, stimulation was immediately terminated, and a positive response of “X” was recorded. If no response was observed after the rat’s paw was stimulated, it was recorded as a negative response “O”. If inaccurate measurements or rat movement were noted, the measurements were repeated until accurate determinations were made. One negative response was recorded prior to the first positive response, followed by five consecutive measurements, and the data were used to calculate the 50% PWT.

Paw withdrawal latency (PWL)

Thermal hyperalgesia during the latency period of harmful thermal stimulus-induced paw withdrawal was detected. Each group of rats was placed in a glass box, and after acclimatization, a radiant heat source was aimed at the center of the rats’ paw. If the rat exhibited a paw withdrawal response, the radiant heat source was turned off, and the duration of the response was recorded. A cutoff time of 20 s was set to prevent tissue damage. The same experiment was repeated three times with a 10-min interval between each measurement.

Gait analysis

In this study, a VisuGait small animal gait analysis system (XIN RUN, XR-FP101, Shanghai, China), which is similar to the CatWalk gait analysis system (Noldus Information Technology, Netherlands), was used to analyze the gait of rats and assess changes in gait caused by pain or motor deficits during natural walking. The system records the contact of the rat’s footprints with a glass plate while it is walking in a closed corridor and uses footprint brightness refraction technology to capture the actual footprint traces with a high-speed, high-definition camera placed beneath the walkway; these footprints were automatically classified with visual processing software. At least three consecutive gaits or complete passages of data were collected as valid samples. Two parameters were selected to evaluate the dynamic behavior of bone cancer pain: the maximum contact area (the imprint area at maximum contact of the hind paw) and the maximum contact intensity (the maximum intensity at hind paw contact). The data are presented as percentages of the surgical side (left)/contralateral side (right) of the hind paw [30].

Radiology

X-ray scans of the tibia on the surgical side of the rats in the Sham group and BCP group were performed on postoperative days 7 and 14 to determine the extent of bone destruction, which was assessed by two radiologists. The criteria for assessing the degree of bone destruction were as follows: 0, normal bone structure with no destruction; 1, 1–3 small punctate areas of radiolucency visible; 2, 4–6 small, visible punctate areas of bone destruction; 3, loss of medullary bone and cortical bone erosion; 4, full-thickness single cortical bone loss; and 5, full-thickness double cortical bone loss and displaced fracture [26].

Method of animal euthanasia

In a well-ventilated room, the animals were placed in a transparent euthanasia chamber that was not filled with carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide was introduced into the chamber at a rate of 10%–30% of the chamber volume per minute. The animal was exposed for 10 min, after which time it was confirmed that the animal was motionless, did not breathe, and had dilated pupils. The carbon dioxide supply was then turned off, and the animals were observed for an additional 2–3 min to confirm death.

HE staining

The left tibia of each rat was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for HE staining. Images were collected and analyzed using a microscope (Olympus, Japan) to observe tumor growth and bone destruction.

Transfection of BMSCs with shRNAs

Third-generation BMSCs were plated in 6-well dishes at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. In accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines, BMSCs were infected with a lentiviral shRNA targeting TSG-6 (shTSG-6) or its negative control (sh-NC) (GenePharma, Suzhou, China). The two shRNA candidate sequences used for the Tnfaip6-rat knockdown lentiviral vector were as follows:

sequence 1, GGCTGACTATGTAGAAATATA; and

sequence 2, GCAGCCAGAAAGATTGGATTC.

RNA and protein were extracted from the transfected cells and assessed by RT‒qPCR and Western blotting, respectively, to verify the knockdown of TSG-6 expression.

Transwell coculture of microglia with BMSCs and rmTSG-6

A 6-well Transwell system (0.4 μm pore membrane; Corning, USA) was used to study the specific mechanism by which TSG-6 secreted by BMSCs regulates pyroptosis in BV2 microglia. The mouse BV2 cell line was purchased from Cas9X™ Starbio Co., Ltd. (HyCyte™ TCM-C718, Suzhou, China). BV2 cells were cultured in DMEM-H + 10% FBS + 1% P/S medium with a gas environment of 95% air + 5% carbon dioxide, and the temperature was maintained at 37 °C.

BV2 microglia (2 × 105) were added to the lower chamber and cultured for 12 h to allow them to adhere. After confirming that the cell morphology was normal, the microglia were cocultured with one of the following types of cells in the upper chamber for 24 h: (1) Control group, BV2 cells in control medium; (2) LPS group, BV2 cells treated with 1 μg/ml LPS (Sigma‒Aldrich, USA, L2630); (3) BMSC group, BV2 cells treated with 1 μg/ml LPS and 2 × 105 BMSCs; (4) shTSG-6 BMSC group, BV2 cells treated with 1 μg/ml LPS and 2 × 105 shTSG-6 BMSCs; (5) shNC BMSC group, BV2 cells treated with 1 μg/ml LPS and 2 × 105 shNC BMSCs; and (6) rmTSG-6 group, BV2 cells treated with 1 μg/ml LPS plus 100 ng/ml recombinant TSG-6.

Immunofluorescence staining

Spinal cord immunofluorescence staining

Fourteen days after model establishment, the appropriate treatment was administered to each group and behavioral observations were recorded before the tissues were collected. The rats were anesthetized with 1.25% tribromoethanol via intraperitoneal (IP) injection at a dose of 300 mg/kg. PBS was injected into the left ventricle until the outflow liquid became clear and the liver turned white, after which the body was slowly injected with 4% paraformaldehyde until it became rigid, at which point perfusion was stopped. The L4‒6 spinal cord segments of the rats were removed at 4 °C and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C. The tissues were then sequentially transferred to 10%, 20%, and 30% sucrose for dehydration, with incubation with each sucrose concentration at 4 °C for 24 h; if the tissue did not settle, the time was extended to 48 h. Once the tissue had settled, the samples were embedded, and a cryostat (Leica, Germany) was used to cut the tissues into 20 μm thick sections. The sections were washed with PBS (5 min, 3 times), treated with 0.3% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 10 min, and then blocked with an immunofluorescence blocking solution (Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 1 h. The primary antibodies used for colocalization staining included a recombinant mouse anti-IBA-1 antibody (1:100 dilution; Abcam, USA; ab283319) with a rabbit anti-NLRP3 antibody (1:50 dilution; Abcam, USA; ab283819), a rabbit anti-GSDMD and cleaved N-terminal antibody (1:100 dilution; Abmart, China; PU 224937), and a rabbit anti-IL-1β antibody (1:500 dilution; Affinity, China; AF5103). A rabbit anti-IBA-1 antibody (1:500 dilution; Affinity, China; DF6442) was used for immunofluorescence staining of spinal dorsal horn microglia. The samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C, washed three times with PBS, and then incubated in the dark with the following fluorescent dye-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h: Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit antibody (1:500; Abways, AB0141) and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse antibody (1:500; Abways, AB0152). After three washes with PBS, DAPI (Abcam, USA; AB104139) was used for nuclear staining, and the sections were mounted. The spinal cord sections were observed and photographed under a fluorescence confocal microscope (Leica Biosystems). We quantified the expression of IBA-1, NLRP3, IL-1β, and GSDMD using ImageJ software (fiji-win64) to standardize the size of the region of interest (ROI) in the spinal dorsal horn and outlined the spinal cord images by quantifying the percentage of fluorescence-positive area [31, 32]. The total number of cells in each ROI was determined using ImageJ, and double-positive cells (yellow fluorescence) were identified and counted by a laboratory technician who was blinded to the experimental design and grouping [31].

Cellular immunofluorescence staining

After coculture, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 8 min, and then blocked with an immunofluorescence blocking solution (Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 1 h. The primary antibodies used included a recombinant mouse anti-IBA-1 antibody (1:100 dilution; Abcam, USA; ab283319) and a rabbit anti-NLRP3 antibody (1:50 dilution; Abcam, USA; ab283819), as well as a rabbit anti-GSDMD and cleaved N-terminal antibody (1:100 dilution; Abmart, China; PU 224937) to assess colocalization of the target proteins. The samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C, washed three times with PBS, and then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:500; Abways, AB0141) and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse (1:500; Abways, AB0152) secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. After three washes with PBS, DAPI (Abcam, USA; AB104139) was used for nuclear staining. The cells were observed under a fluorescence confocal microscope (Leica Biosystems) and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from L4‒L6 segmental ipsilateral spinal cord tissues and BV2 cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, China) supplemented with PMSF (Beyotime, China). Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Key GEN BioTECH, China), and equal amounts of protein were separated via SDS‒PAGE. The proteins were then transferred to methanol-activated PVDF membranes (Millipore, Merck KGaA, Germany) and blocked with Tris-buffered saline supplemented with 5% nonfat milk (Solarbio, China) for 1 h at room temperature. The primary antibodies used were as follows: anti-IBA-1 (1:1000, Abcam, USA, ab153696), anti-NLRP3 (1:1000, Abcam, USA, ab283819), anti-GSDMD and cleaved N-terminal (1:1000, Abmart, China, PU 224937), anti-total and cleaved Caspase-1 (1:1000, Abmart, China, P79884R2), anti-IL-1β (1:1000, Affinity, China, AF5103), anti-IL-18 (1:1000, Affinity, China, DF6252), anti-TSG-6 (1:200, Santa Cruz, USA, SC-65886), and anti-β-actin (1:3000, Servicebio, China, GB15003), which were incubated with the samples overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were subsequently washed six times with TBST for 5 min each and incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The immunoblots were developed using a chemiluminescence kit (Omni-ECL, Shanghai, China) and analyzed using ImageJ software.

RNA-seq

We seeded 2 × 105 BV2 microglia in the lower chamber for coculture with 2 × 105 BMSCs in the upper chamber. The cells were then split into two groups: the BB group (BV2 cells + BMSCs) and the BBL group, which included BV2 cells + 1 μg/ml LPS + BMSCs. Total RNA was extracted from the BMSCs using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. We enriched mRNA using oligo-dT primers for subsequent isolation by eluting the polyA-tailed mRNA, followed by fragmentation. The processed mRNA served as a template for reverse transcription to synthesize cDNA, which we then amplified by PCR using primers. We purified the PCR products using NEBNext to create a library, and once it passed quality control, we performed paired-end sequencing on the library using the Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform (LC Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Hangzhou, China) according to standard procedures in PE150 sequencing mode. We conducted differential expression analysis using DESeq2 software. After Cutadapt was used to filter out poor-quality sequences (such as sequencing adapters and low-quality sequences) from the raw data, we obtained valid data (clean data). We performed reference genome alignment using HISAT2. Using the alignment results from HISAT2, we reconstructed the transcripts with StringTie, and the expression levels of all the genes in each sample were calculated. We set the threshold for identifying differentially expressed genes at a fold change of ≥ 1.2 (i.e., the absolute value of log2(fc) ≥ 0.283) and a q value < 0.05 (where the q value is the adjusted p value).

RT‒qPCR

This section outlines the methods used for RNA extraction and analysis. Total RNA was extracted from the BMSCs and BV2 cells using the Steadypure Quick RNA Extraction Kit (AG, AG21023, China). cDNA was synthesized using the Evo M-MLVRT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR Ver. 2 (AG, AG11728, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A two-step method was used to first remove gDNA and then perform reverse transcription. RT‒qPCR was subsequently performed using the SYBR® Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit II (AG, AG11702, China) and a 7500 Fast quantitative PCR system (Thermo, USA) with CFX Manager™ software version 3.0 (Bio-Rad Hercules CA94547, USA). The following primer sequences (5’ to 3’) were designed using Primer 5.0 software:

Mus Tnfaip6 (F) AGGCTGTTTGGCTGACTATGT and

(R) TTTCCTGTGCTGATGATGTCTT;

Rattus Tnfaip6 (F) AAGCAGCCAGAAAGATTGGA and

(R) TTCGGGTTGTAGCAATAGGC;

Rattus β-actin (F) TCAGGTCATCACTATCGGCAAT and

(R) AAAGAAAGGGTGTAAAACGCA;

Mus Gapdh (F) TTCACCACCATGGAGAAGGC and

(R) GGCATGGACTGTGGTCATGA.

We present the data as relative Ct values. We used the 2−ΔΔCt method to calculate the relative expression levels.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The supernatants of BV2 cell culture medium and the ipsilateral dorsal horn of the L4‒L6 spinal cord were analyzed. BV2 cell culture medium was collected and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 20 min, after which the supernatant was collected, and an ELISA kit (Mouse IL-1β Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay Kit, Jianglai Biotechnology, JL18442) was used to measure the concentration of IL-1β according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The spinal cords were rinsed with precooled PBS (0.01 M, pH = 7.4), weighed, and cut into pieces. PBS was added to the samples at a weight-to-volume ratio of 1:9 in a glass homogenizer, and the samples were ground in the homogenizer in lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Finally, the homogenate was centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5‒10 min, the supernatant was retained, and the concentration of IL-1β was determined using an ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Rat IL-1β ELISA Kit; Jianglai Bio, JL20884).

Detection of LDH release

In this study, we used a detection kit (DOJINDO, Shanghai, China) to determine lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity in BV2 cells, as follows. First, 50 μl of the cell suspension diluted with culture medium was added to a flat-bottom 96-well plate, followed by the addition of 50 μl of drug-containing culture medium and incubation for an appropriate duration in a 37 °C incubator with CO2. After 10 μl of lysis buffer was added to the sample wells, the mixture was incubated for 30 min under the same conditions. Subsequently, 100 μl of working solution was added to each well for incubation at room temperature for 20 min in the dark. Immediately after 50 μl of Stop Solution was added to each well, the absorbance at 490 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). All the experiments were repeated at least three times, and the data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t test (two-tailed) was used for comparisons between two groups, while intergroup comparisons of behavioral data were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with post hoc multiple comparisons conducted using Tukey’s test. Other statistical analyses were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Identification of the characteristics of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs)

Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells were identified after passage. Microscopic observations revealed that the cells adhered to the surface, had a uniform morphology, and were spindle shaped, similar to fibroblasts (Additional file 1: A). Flow cytometry was performed to detect the surface antigen markers of third-generation BMSCs, and the results indicated that these cells were positive for the mesenchymal stem cell markers CD90.1, CD29, and CD44 but negative for the hematopoietic cell and monocyte/macrophage markers CD45 and CD11b/c (Additional file 1: B). In vitro differentiation models revealed that these BMSCs could successfully differentiate into osteoblasts, adipocytes, and chondrocytes (Additional file 1: C–E).

Establishment of the bone cancer pain model

Following tumor cell injection into the left tibia, the rats underwent behavioral, histological, and radiological analyses to confirm tumor formation in the bone cancer pain model. Compared with the Sham group, the BCP group exhibited progressive reductions in both the 50% paw withdrawal threshold (PWT) and paw withdrawal latency (PWL) at multiple time points after model establishment (Additional file 2A, B). CatWalk gait analysis is a recommended and valuable method for assessing chronic pain behavior in neuropathic inflammatory pain models [33]. The advantages of CatWalk in obtaining intensity and area data, along with its successful application in a bone cancer pain model, suggest that CatWalk gait analysis should be prioritized when evaluating bone cancer pain [30, 34]. Similar to CatWalk gait analysis, the VisuGait system revealed significant changes in the gait parameters of the maximum contact area, indicating that the imprint area of the rat’s hind paw at maximum contact was significantly reduced in the BCP group at 14 days postsurgery, likely because of bone destruction and pain from the tumor, which reduced their ability to bear weight. The maximum contact intensity indicates the intensity at maximum contact. The maximum contact intensity and mean intensity of the left hind limb were significantly reduced in the BCP group, which indicated reduced function of the affected limb (Additional file 2C–F). Histopathological analysis revealed extensive tumor cell infiltration in the tibial tissues of BCP rats, which exhibited cellular pleomorphism with enlarged hyperchromatic nuclei. Marked osteolysis, characterized by trabecular attenuation/disruption, cortical thinning with focal defects, and medullary cavity necrosis accompanied by dense inflammatory infiltrates, was observed in tumor-infiltrated regions. Prominent tumor-associated neovascularization was also evident. In contrast, specimens from the Sham group presented a preserved trabecular architecture with intact marrow constituents devoid of osteolytic lesions or neoplastic proliferation (Additional file 2G, H). Comparative radiography revealed initial osteolytic lesions with localized periosteal reactions at 7 days post-inoculation in the BCP group, indicating early tumor invasion. By day 14, the tibial structure was significantly damaged, featuring uneven local bone density, accompanied by the loss of bone structure, cortical bone defects, and swelling of the surrounding muscles and soft tissues (Additional file 2I).

BMSCs alleviate BCP behaviors and inhibit the activation of ipsilateral spinal dorsal horn microglia

BMSCs were intrathecally injected at 7 days (the beginning of BCP development) and 14 days (the peak of pain) after BCP model induction to verify the hypothesis that BMSCs can alleviate bone cancer pain (BCP). Compared with those in the BCP group, the mechanical pain threshold (PWT) (Fig. 1A) and thermal pain threshold (PWL) (Fig. 1B) were significantly greater in the BMSC group, indicating that BMSCs effectively relieved bone cancer pain. Tumor cells can secrete a variety of inflammatory factors, which can activate microglia in the central nervous system, causing them to shift from a resting state to an activated state and rapidly migrate to the site of injury. The activation of microglia is an important mechanism for the occurrence and development of bone cancer pain. Immunofluorescence staining was used to observe changes in the expression of the microglial marker IBA-1 in the ipsilateral spinal dorsal horn in each group. The results revealed that the expression of IBA-1 in the ipsilateral spinal dorsal horn was significantly increased in the BCP group, indicating the activation of microglia; however, after the intrathecal injection of BMSCs in BCP rats, the expression of IBA-1 decreased, and the degree of microglial activation was significantly reduced. These findings suggest that BMSCs may exert their analgesic effects by inhibiting the activation of microglia (Fig. 1C).

The TSG-6 gene is upregulated by BMSCs during the LPS-induced BV2 microglia inflammatory response

To clarify the mechanism by which BMSCs inhibit microglial activation, we used a coculture system of BMSCs and BV2 cells with or without LPS stimulation, and further performed transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis to screen potential key paracrine molecules (Fig. 2A). RNA-seq revealed a set of differentially expressed genes in BMSCs cocultured with LPS-stimulated BV2 cells. Among these genes, Tnfaip6 (encoding TSG-6) was notably upregulated (log2FC = 0.5, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B), and there was significant enrichment of genes in the TNF signaling pathway and the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway (FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). To identify the key paracrine factor responsible for the anti-inflammatory effects of BMSCs, we integrated these findings. We prioritized Tnfaip6/TSG-6 for further investigation on the basis of a threefold rationale: (1) its marked upregulation in the inflammatory microenvironment; (2) the enrichment of the TNF pathway, a major inflammatory regulator wherein TSG-6 is a TNF-α-inducible secreted protein; and (3) the concurrent enrichment of the NOD-like receptor pathway, particularly the NLRP3 inflammasome axis pivotal for pyroptosis, which the literature indicates can be directly inhibited by TSG-6 [16]. Therefore, TSG-6 emerged as the primary candidate linking the observed transcriptomic changes to a potential functional mechanism. We consequently hypothesized that the anti-inflammatory effect of BMSCs is mediated by their increased secretion of TSG-6 under inflammatory conditions.

To test this hypothesis, we performed Western blot and RT‒qPCR analyses. The results confirmed that coculture with LPS-stimulated BV2 microglia significantly increased both TSG-6 protein and Tnfaip6 mRNA levels in BMSCs (Fig. 2D, E, G). Interestingly, synchronous increases in TSG-6 and Tnfaip6 expression levels were also detected in the BV2 cells themselves (Fig. 2D, F, H). These observations strongly support the notion that TSG-6 functions as a crucial paracrine factor secreted by BMSCs to modulate microglial activity.

TSG-6 is secreted by BMSCs and exogenous rmTSG-6 alleviates BCP

Compared with the sham surgery group, all of the other groups presented worsening mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia (Fig. 3B, C). Previous studies have shown that paracrine TSG-6 secreted by BMSCs attenuates neuropathic pain [18] and that an intra-articular injection of BMSCs inhibits central sensitization to pain and increases the expression of the anti-inflammatory and joint-protective factor TSG-6 [35]. We tested the hypothesis that bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells alleviate bone cancer pain by intrathecally injecting BMSCs at the onset of bone cancer pain development at 7 days and at the peak of pain at 14 days after model induction. Notably, analgesia was first detected at 2 h after injection, was optimal at 24 h after injection, and weakened at 48 h after injection. rmTSG-6 (1 μg) and rmTSG-6 (5 μg) were administered intrathecally 7 and 14 days after BCP model establishment to confirm whether exogenous recombinant TSG-6 had the same analgesic effect. Surprisingly, the analgesic effect and onset of action of 5 μg of rmTSG-6 were similar to those of the BMSC group, both of which were superior to those of the 1 μg rmTSG-6 group (Fig. 3D–G). At the optimal treatment time, 14 days after BCP induction, gait analysis of the maximum contact areas and maximum intensities of the left and right hind limbs revealed that both BMSCs and rmTSG-6 improved the contact area and maximum intensity of the affected limb (Fig. 3H–J). Furthermore, intrathecal administration of the same dose of BMSCs or rmTSG-6 remained effective despite pain peaking on day 14 (Fig. 3F, G ). On the basis of the above data, we transfected Tnfaip6-targeting shRNA into BMSCs (TSG-6 shRNA-expressing BMSCs) to verify whether BMSCs induce analgesia by secreting TSG-6 and observed that TSG-6 protein and Tnfaip6 mRNA expression was significantly downregulated in the BMSCs, as determined by Western blotting and RT‒qPCR (Fig. 5B–D). At 7 and 14 days after the intrathecal administration of the NC shRNA- or TSG-6 shRNA-expressing BMSCs, the analgesic effect of the TSG-6 shRNA-treated BMSCs was significantly attenuated compared with that of BMSCs or NC shRNA-treated BMSCs (Fig. 5E–M).

The effects of TSG-6 secreted by BMSCs and exogenous rmTSG-6 on the pyroptosis of microglia in the ipsilateral spinal dorsal horn of rats with BCP were investigated, with a specific analysis of NLRP3 signaling pathway activation and associated inflammatory factor release

Previous studies have shown that during the development of bone cancer pain, spinal dorsal horn (SDH) microglia are activated, exacerbating neuroinflammation and pain sensitization through the release of proinflammatory cytokines (such as IL-1β). Additionally, BCP can induce the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in the SDH, indicating that microglial activation may mediate pathological processes by regulating NLRP3 inflammasome assembly [36]. On the basis of previous findings that BMSCs can inhibit the activation of microglia, this study further explored whether they exert analgesic effects by targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome‒pyroptotic pathway. Immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect the colocalization of SDH microglia (labeled with IBA-1) with the NLRP3 inflammasome, pyroptosis executioner protein GSDMD, and mature IL-1β to elucidate the role of microglia in inflammasome assembly (NLRP3), pyroptosis execution (GSDMD cleavage), and proinflammatory factor release (IL-1β). Compared with those in the Sham group, the activation of IBA-1-positive microglia in the ipsilateral spinal dorsal horn of BCP rats was significantly increased, and the colocalization ratios of NLRP3 (Fig. 4A–C), GSDMD (Fig. 4D–F), and IL-1β (Fig. 4G–I) were markedly elevated, suggesting that microglial activation may be involved in inflammasome assembly and pyroptosis execution, as well as the generation and release of IL-1β. Western blotting revealed that the protein expression of IBA-1 (Fig. 4J, K) in the SDH of the BCP group significantly increased and the expression of the inflammasome protein NLRP3 (Fig. 4J, L) increased, while its downstream effector molecule Caspase-1 (Fig. 4J, Q) was activated, as indicated by the detection of cleaved Caspase-1 (Fig. 4J, R), which in turn catalyzed the cleavage of the pyroptosis executioner protein GSDMD (Fig. 4J, N) to generate the membrane pore-forming active GSDMD-N (Fig. 4J, O) domain. This molecular cascade reaction is consistent with the release of proinflammatory factors: significantly elevated protein levels of mature IL-1β (Fig. 4J, M) and IL-18 (Fig. 4J, P) were detected, while ELISA confirmed a significant increase in the secretion of IL-1β (Fig. 4S), which is consistent with the classic mechanism of membrane pore formation and the release of cellular contents mediated by GSDMD-N during pyroptosis. Analysis of behavioral performance indicated that 5 μg of rmTSG-6 was superior to 1 μg; thus, we chose 5 μg of rmTSG-6 for subsequent experiments. After the intrathecal administration of BMSCs or rmTSG-6, the changes in the levels of the aforementioned pyroptosis-related molecules significantly reversed. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that BMSC or rmTSG-6 intervention reduced the number of IBA-1-positive microglia that colocalized with NLRP3 and GSDMD, suggesting that these treatments exerted regulatory effects by inhibiting the assembly of microglial inflammasomes and the recruitment of pyroptosis executioner proteins. Western blot analysis confirmed that the protein expression levels of NLRP3, cleaved Caspase-1, and GSDMD-N were significantly decreased and that the levels of the mature forms of IL-1β and IL-18 were also reduced. The ELISA results were consistent with these findings, indicating that the secretion of IL-1β in the spinal cord tissue of the BMSC and rmTSG-6 groups was significantly lower than that in the BCP group. These multilevel inhibitory effects indicate that BMSC or rmTSG-6 treatment may target key nodes of the NLRP3 inflammasome‒pyroptosis pathway, ultimately reducing the release of proinflammatory factors and alleviating the neuroinflammatory cascade associated with bone cancer pain.

We cocultured LPS-induced BV2 microglia with BMSCs, shTSG-6 BMSCs, or exogenous recombinant TSG-6 to determine whether the TSG-6 secreted by BMSCs regulates microglial pyroptosis by inhibiting the NLRP3/Caspase-1/GSDMD signaling pathway to exert analgesic effects. Discussion

Pain caused by bone cancer involves not only the abnormal excitation of neurons but also the activation of microglia and the release of inflammatory factors [37]. Microglia maintain homeostasis in the central nervous system and remove dead cells and cellular debris through phagocytosis, and their activation is closely related to neuroinflammation; however, excessive or sustained microglial activation can further aggravate neurotoxicity, leading to the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, subsequent microglial pyroptosis and the exacerbation of nerve injury [38, 39]. In the context of neuropathic pain and bone cancer pain, microglia are activated to release a variety of proinflammatory cytokines, triggering a neuroinflammatory response that increases neuronal sensitivity and promotes pain perception and maintenance [18, 40, 41]. Activated microglia can trigger pyroptosis, and the inflammatory factors released during pyroptosis can further activate microglia and exacerbate neuroinflammation, forming a vicious cycle that exacerbates pain symptoms [27]. Therefore, inhibiting microglial pyroptosis can alleviate the occurrence and development of neuroinflammation, making it a potential therapeutic strategy for relieving pain and reducing bone cancer pain. Previous studies have shown that BMSCs can inhibit microglial activation by secreting TSG-6, thereby alleviating neuroinflammation and pain [18], and the incidence of cancer-related pain was significantly reduced after injection of transgenic miR-9-5p-modified BMSCs into a mouse model of bone cancer, suggesting that stem cell therapy may alleviate pain by inhibiting inflammatory responses in the central nervous system [20]. However, the detailed mechanism underlying the analgesic effect of BMSCs in bone cancer pain has not been fully clarified. We used a Walker 256 tumor cell-induced rat BCP model in this study to evaluate whether BMSCs inhibit the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway to regulate microglial pyroptosis and alleviate bone cancer pain through the secretion of TSG-6. Both the intrathecal injection of BMSCs and the administration of exogenous recombinant TSG-6 significantly improved pain behavior indicators in rats with BCP and inhibited the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway and the release of inflammatory factors in the spinal dorsal horn. This study is the first to clarify the mechanism of action of TSG-6 secreted by BMSCs in the treatment of bone cancer pain, providing a new strategy for its clinical management.

An increasing number of studies have documented the significant role of paracrine effects in the therapeutic outcomes of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). In this study, through RNA sequencing of an in vitro model, we revealed that after BV2 cells were stimulated with LPS, the expression of the Tnfaip6 gene (which encodes TSG-6) significantly increased in BMSCs that were cocultured with these BV2 cells. KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment of differentially expressed genes in multiple signaling pathways, and in the present study, we focused on genes most relevant to inflammatory regulation and the functional phenotype of BMSCs, especially those associated with NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis. Among them, there was marked enrichment of the differentially expressed genes in the TNF signaling pathway and the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway. The TNF signaling pathway plays a crucial role in inflammatory responses. TNF-α, an important proinflammatory cytokine, can activate multiple downstream signaling pathways, leading to the release of inflammatory factors and the intensification of inflammatory responses. TSG-6 (Tumor necrosis factor -α-stimulated gene 6), an important protein whose secretion is induced by TNF-α [42], is upregulated in BMSCs in response to inflammatory stimuli through the TNF signaling pathway. Pathogen- and damage-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs and DAMPs) can be detected by the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, leading to the formation of inflammasomes upon activation, which then activates Caspase-1, promoting the maturation and release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β. In this study, KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment of the differentially expressed genes in the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, indicating that BMSCs may sense injury signals in the inflammatory environment through this pathway. Secretion of TSG-6 can inhibit the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and reduce the production of IL-1β [16]. When BMSCs are pretreated or stimulated by inflammatory factors (such as TNF-α or LPS), the expression of TSG-6 increases significantly. This upregulation not only increases the anti-inflammatory activity of BMSCs but also promotes their polarization to M2 macrophages, thus exerting a protective effect on the inflammatory microenvironment [43–45].

The TSG-6 protein has various anti-inflammatory functions, inhibits the expression of proinflammatory factors and promotes the release of anti-inflammatory factors [42]. TSG-6 is involved in cell adhesion, extracellular matrix remodeling, and the regulation of inflammatory responses and participates in immune regulation and tissue regeneration. Accumulating evidence indicates that the secretion of TSG-6 by mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) has a protective effect on various inflammatory diseases. For example, TSG-6 secreted by BMSCs can regulate surrounding cells and inhibit the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby alleviating acute pulmonary inflammatory responses [16]. Exosomes derived from LPS-pretreated bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (LPS pre-Exos) inhibit NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling through TSG-6, thus accelerating peripheral nerve regeneration [45]. In our study, compared with the use of untransfected or NC-shRNA-transfected BMSCs, the use of a TSG-6-targeting shRNA to specifically knockdown Tnfaip6 expression in BMSCs weakened the ability of BMSCs to alleviate bone cancer pain, their protective effect against microglial pyroptosis and the release of proinflammatory factors. Additionally, when TSG-6 shRNA-treated BMSCs were cocultured with LPS-treated BV2 microglia, an increase in the secretion of IL-1β was observed. We intrathecally injected exogenous rmTSG-6 to verify the role of TSG-6 in alleviating bone cancer pain, and the results indicated that exogenous TSG-6 could significantly alleviate bone cancer pain in rats and reduce the expression of IL-1β in the spinal dorsal horn. Previous studies have shown that the intrathecal injection of BMSCs can produce long-lasting analgesia that can be maintained for approximately 2–4 weeks in neuropathic pain models [18, 46], whereas in a bone cancer pain (BCP) model, the analgesic effect of BMSCs lasts only 48 h [10], which is consistent with our findings. Similarly, the effect of exogenous recombinant TSG-6 peaked at 24 h after injection in the BCP model, and analgesia was maintained for 48 h. However, in this study, the duration of the analgesic effects of the BMSCs coincided with that of exogenous TSG-6, confirming that TSG-6 is the main mediator of the anti-inflammatory mechanism mediated by BMSCs and their analgesic effect on bone cancer pain. In the BCP model, relying solely on the continuous secretion of TSG-6 from BMSCs or a single injection of exogenous TSG-6 cannot overcome the limitation of 48 h of analgesia, possibly because of persistent inflammatory stimulation, bone destruction in the tumor microenvironment, and the release of many proinflammatory factors, which may limit the sustained efficacy of BMSCs and their secreted factors. In contrast, the neuropathic pain model is characterized by inflammation triggered by nerve injury, in which a relatively stable nerve remodeling phase occurs after the initial inflammatory storm. This difference highlights the importance of matching pain types with treatment mechanisms: TSG-6 is an effective early intervention target for treating tumor-driven inflammatory pain, but the long-lasting analgesic effect of BMSCs on neuropathic pain may far exceed that of the single anti-inflammatory factor TSG-6, which is related to its multitarget regulatory effects on chronic neuropathic injury and central plasticity. Although BMSCs provided less sustained analgesia in the bone cancer pain (BCP) model than in the neuropathic pain model, they still exhibit clear and irreplaceable immediate analgesic value during the acute phase of BCP through the secretion of TSG-6 to inhibit microglial pyroptosis and reduce the levels of proinflammatory factors such as IL-1β. This study provides a critical mechanism for subsequent combined sustained release or immunomodulatory strategies.

We elucidated the mechanism of action of BMSCs in the BCP model and found that the number of activated microglia in the dorsal horn of the ipsilateral spinal cord of rats with BCP was significantly increased and that these cells exhibited an amoeboid morphology, increased cytosol and a reduced branching structure. Intrathecal injection of BMSCs reduced the number of activated microglia in the dorsal horns of rats with BCP and decreased the expression of the microglial marker IBA-1. Pyroptosis is a form of programmed cell death that microglia undergo in response to pathological stimuli and is triggered primarily by the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to the activation of Caspase-1 into cleaved Caspase-1 accompanied by the activation of GSDMD, resulting in cell membrane rupture and the release of the active forms of IL-1β and IL-18, thereby exacerbating neuroinflammation and cell damage [47, 48]. In our study, as shown in Fig. 8, TSG-6 secreted by BMSCs and exogenous recombinant TSG-6 inhibited the upregulation of NLRP3, cleaved Caspase-1, GSDMD, GSDMD-N, and the proinflammatory factors IL-1β and IL-18 both in vivo and in vitro, indicating that the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway in spinal dorsal horn microglia was suppressed after the intrathecal injection of BMSCs or exogenous TSG-6 in the rat model of bone cancer pain. However, when TSG-6 expression was knocked down, the inhibitory effect of BMSCs on these pyroptosis-related proteins was weakened. This outcome is in line with published reports, confirming that targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome effectively relieves bone cancer pain (Fig. 8). MCC950, a selective NLRP3 inhibitor, significantly relieves mechanical hypersensitivity associated with bone cancer pain [29]. Curcumin analogs inhibit central sensitization in rats with bone cancer pain by suppressing the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and reducing the production of IL-1β, thus alleviating bone cancer pain [49]. Similarly, AMPK activation has been shown to attenuate cancer-induced bone pain by mitigating mitochondrial dysfunction and subsequent NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated neuroinflammation [50]. Our research group’s earlier studies also revealed that in the context of bone cancer pain (BCP), activated microglia promote the maturation and release of IL-1β through the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, thereby exacerbating the transmission and maintenance of pain signals, leading to neural sensitization and resulting in persistent and aggravated pain [26]. These results further support the important roles of NLRP3 inflammasome activation and IL-1β release in bone cancer pain and provide important evidence for the potential application of TSG-6 secreted by BMSCs in the treatment of this condition. Although this study validated the occurrence of pyroptosis in microglia and the regulatory role of BMSCs by detecting core indicators such as NLRP3 inflammasome activation, Caspase-1 and GSDMD cleavage, LDH release, and IL-1β/IL-18 secretion, it is also important to note the distinctions between pyroptosis and other forms of inflammatory or lytic cell death. Apoptosis is a noninflammatory form of programmed cell death that does not cause cell membrane rupture, whereas necroptosis does not rely on Caspase or GSDMD, and lytic necrosis is a form of passive death without a clear regulatory mechanism [51]. Future research should focus on the characteristics of various forms of cell death and further explore their roles and regulatory relationships in bone cancer pain models.

Schematic of how TSG-6 secreted by BMSCs regulates the pyroptosis of dorsal horn microglia in BCP rats

Although primary microglia better recapitulate the in vivo physiological environment, we used BV2 cells (a mouse microglial cell line) because of their convenient culture, stable proliferation, and ability to effectively simulate microglial inflammation. Although BV2 cells cannot fully replicate all the characteristics of primary microglia in vivo, this model is widely accepted and extensively used in mechanistic studies of bone cancer pain and neuroinflammation. This constitutes a limitation that should be considered when evaluating the translational potential of our findings to clinical settings. Activating BV2 cells with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) simulates the activation state of microglia in the inflammatory environment of bone cancer pain. In this study, we found that TSG-6 secreted by BMSCs and exogenous rmTSG-6 significantly inhibited the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in LPS-induced BV2 microglia, thereby reducing the generation of cleaved Caspase-1 and GSDMD-N fragments and the release of active IL-1β and IL-18. This mechanistic characteristic has been reported earlier: TSG-6 suppresses proinflammatory cytokine production via binding CD44 and inhibiting the NF-κB pathway [43]. In addition, TSG-6 can form stable complexes with hyaluronic acid (HA), further stabilizing its interaction with CD44 and thereby amplifying its anti-inflammatory effect [52]. We found that treatment with BMSCs and rmTSG-6 significantly reduced the fluorescence intensity of NLRP3 and GSDMD in BV2 cells and alleviated microglial activation, confirming the anti-inflammatory effect of TSG-6. LDH release experiments and ELISA detection of IL-1β also revealed that TSG-6 can inhibit the destruction of cell membrane integrity caused by pyroptosis. Although we did not perform ELISA for IL-18, the results of the Western blot analysis were consistent with those of the LDH release experiment and the ELISA results for IL-1β, supporting the role of TSG-6 in inhibiting microglial pyroptosis and the inflammatory response. In the present study, 100 ng/mL rmTSG-6 was used for in vitro experiments in BV2 cells. This concentration was selected on the basis of both our preliminary dose-finding experiments and previous relevant studies [19]. In our preliminary tests, 50, 100, and 200 ng/mL rmTSG-6 were tested for their inhibitory effects on NLRP3 activation, and 100 ng/mL rmTSG-6 showed stable and remarkable inhibitory efficacy. These findings not only reveal the potential therapeutic mechanism of TSG-6 in a bone cancer pain model but also provide new directions for future research.

Although this study revealed the important role of TSG-6 secreted by BMSCs in inhibiting microglial pyroptosis in a rat model of bone cancer pain, several limitations remain. Future studies may further optimize the concentration gradient of rmTSG-6 to determine its EC50 and elucidate its pharmacokinetic characteristics in the central nervous system. The secretome of BMSCs, including IL-10, TGF-β, and PGE₂, can be analyzed to evaluate its effect on the specificity of TSG-6. The application of NLRP3 inhibitors (e.g., MCC950) will help identify the target of BMSC-secreted TSG-6 and verify whether it modulates NLRP3 inflammasome activation via TLR4 signaling, mitochondrial ROS production, or autophagy pathways. Dual staining of CD44 and microglia in the spinal dorsal horn to examine their colocalization may clarify the effect of TSG-6 on microglia–neuron interactions.

Despite these limitations, this study provides important evidence for the potential application of BMSCs in the treatment of bone cancer pain and offers new directions for future research.

Conclusions

In summary, our results indicate that the intrathecal injection of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) in a rat model of bone cancer pain can alleviate pain through the secretion of tumor necrosis factor-α-stimulated gene 6 (TSG-6). Further in vivo and in vitro experiments confirmed that the release of TSG-6 by BMSCs significantly inhibited the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway, thereby reducing the release of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 produced by microglia undergoing pyroptosis. Our research also demonstrated that the intrathecal injection of exogenous recombinant TSG-6 may be a potential therapeutic intervention for cancer-related pain, including BCP.
