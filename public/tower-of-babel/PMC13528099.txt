Biochar and melatonin alleviate microplastic–cadmium (MP-Cd) stress in rice by enhancing antioxidant activity and regulating Cd transport
Authors: Yingfen Yang, Chenghu Ye, Li Liu, Yanjun Wang, Kaixing Qu, Qaiser Shakeel, Changwei Cui, Jun Yang, Areej A Alkhalaf, Wael N Hozzein, Muhammad Zeeshan Ul Haq, Waqar Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoil contamination by microplastics (MPs) and heavy metals (HMs), particularly cadmium (Cd), poses an emerging threat to agricultural sustainability, food safety, and human health. Although the individual effects of MPs and Cd on crop performance have been widely investigated, their interactive impacts on rice remain poorly understood. Biochar (BC) and melatonin (MT) have recently attracted attention for their capacity to alleviate HM toxicity and abiotic stress in plants; however, their combined potential to mitigate MP–Cd co-stress has not yet been explored. This study aimed to evaluate the individual and enhanced effects of BC and MT on rice growth, physiological and molecular responses, Cd bioavailability, and soil properties under MP–Cd co-contamination.ResultsExposure to Cd (20 mg kg⁻¹) and MPs (1%) significantly inhibited rice growth and productivity by inducing oxidative stress, enhancing Cd uptake and accumulation, suppressing chlorophyll biosynthesis, and impairing water and nutrient acquisition. In contrast, the combined application of BC (2%) and MT (100 µM) markedly alleviated these adverse effects and outperformed individual amendments. Co-application substantially increased chlorophyll content (82%), leaf relative water content (48.47%), antioxidant enzyme activities (57.84–99.42%), proline accumulation (49.21%), and endogenous melatonin (EM) levels (48.35%). At the molecular level, BC + MT treatment upregulated antioxidant-related genes (OsAPx6, OsCAT, OsPOD, and OsSOD), the proline biosynthesis gene OsP5CS, and the MT biosynthesis gene OsCOMT, while significantly downregulating Cd transporter genes (OsNRAMP1 and OsHMA3). Furthermore, this combined treatment reduced soil Cd bioavailability and Cd accumulation in rice tissues, while improving soil fertility by increasing nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), and soil organic carbon (SOC).ConclusionsThis study provides the first evidence that the combined application of BC and MT effectively mitigates the detrimental effects of simultaneous MP and Cd contamination in rice. The enhanced physiological, molecular, and soil-level improvements induced by BC and MT collectively enhance rice growth and productivity under MP–Cd stress. These findings highlight a promising, integrated remediation strategy to manage co-pollution of MPs and HMs in agricultural soils, with important implications for sustainable crop production and food security.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09301-3.Keywords: Rice (Oryza sativa), Biochar and melatonin, Antioxidant enzymes, Microplastics and cadmium toxicity, Abiotic stress mitigation, Sustainable agriculture

Introduction

Soil contamination by toxic metals and microplastics (MPs) has become a significant global concern due to their detrimental effects on plants, human health, and ecosystems [1–3]. The surge in disposable plastic use, combined with a recycling rate of merely 9%, has resulted in substantial plastic accumulation in the environment, with approximately 80% eventually fragmenting into MPs that disperse through multiple pathways [3, 4]. MPs, defined as plastic particles smaller than 5 mm, are now ubiquitously detected in soils, water bodies, and food products [5]. In agricultural contexts, plastic mulch films, irrigation water, and organic fertilizers constitute major sources of MP contamination, promoting their entry into soils and subsequent transfer into the food chain [6, 7]. Beyond impairing soil quality and plant growth, MPs can act as vectors for toxic metals, thereby exacerbating environmental risks [8]. Their phytotoxicity manifests as inhibited root development, disrupted physiological processes, and suppressed chlorophyll biosynthesis, often mediated by elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [9, 10].

Heavy metals (HMs) constitute a major class of contaminants in agroecosystems, with their concentrations increasing sharply due to rapid industrialization and urban expansion [11]. Among these, cadmium (Cd) is of particular concern because of its high toxicity, persistence, and bioavailability, posing serious risks to both plants and human health [12]. Cd exposure disrupts photosynthesis, reduces chlorophyll synthesis, impairs nutrient uptake, weakens antioxidant defenses, and induces oxidative stress, collectively resulting in stunted growth, decreased yields, and compromised crop quality [13]. Recent studies indicate that MPs significantly influence the environmental behavior and toxicity of co-occurring contaminants, with effects dependent on particle characteristics such as size, shape, and exposure duration [14–16]. As vectors in the soil–plant continuum, MPs can adsorb HMs, thereby altering their speciation, mobility, and bioavailability [17]. Their high surface area enhances interactions with pollutants, generating complex toxicity patterns. For instance, MPs have been reported to reduce Cd adsorption capacity in soils, thereby increasing its mobility [18], while co-exposure to MPs and Cd has been shown to exacerbate toxicity by facilitating Cd uptake in plants [19]. Collectively, these findings highlight that MP–metal co-contamination often produces greater toxic effects than those of individual contaminants, underscoring the urgent need for effective mitigation strategies.

Biochar (BC), a stable carbon (C)-rich material, has gained considerable attention for its capacity to enhance soil quality [20, 21], improve crop productivity [22], and remediate contaminated environments [23, 24]. In the context of MPs, BC has been reported to mitigate toxicity and promote plant growth by enhancing antioxidant activity, nutrient uptake, root development, and microbial functions [25]. For example, the BC application significantly improved seedling growth, biomass accumulation, and root architecture in Amaranthus tricolor grown under combined Cd and MP stress [26]. In addition to soil amendments, phytohormones have emerged as effective regulators of plant responses to abiotic stress. Among these, melatonin (MT) is recognized as a master regulator of plant growth and stress tolerance [27]. Structurally analogous to auxin, MT is readily translocated within plant tissues, where it scavenges ROS, modulates hormonal balance, enhances photosynthetic efficiency, promotes osmolyte accumulation, improves nutrient uptake, and delays senescence [28]. Although MT’s role in alleviating HM toxicity is well documented, its potential to mitigate MP-induced stress remains largely unexplored. Notably, a recent study demonstrated that nano-TiO₂ treatment elevated endogenous MT levels in Zea mays, which in turn alleviated MP toxicity [29], underscoring the promising yet under-investigated role of MT in MP remediation strategies.

Oryza sativa (rice), one of the world’s most important staple crops, is highly susceptible to Cd contamination in paddy soils [30]. More recently, MPs have also been detected in rice fields, seedlings, and even commercial rice products [31]. While the individual effects of MPs or Cd have been investigated, the combined impact of MPs and Cd on rice growth and productivity remains poorly understood. Although BC has demonstrated effectiveness in mitigating HM toxicity, its role in alleviating Cd–MP co-contamination remains unclear. Similarly, MT is well recognized for its protective effects against Cd stress [32], but its potential to counteract MP-induced toxicity has not been established. Based on this context, we hypothesized that (1) combined exposure to Cd and MPs would produce enhanced toxicity exceeding the effects of each stressor alone, and (2) co-application of BC and MT would provide greater mitigation than individual treatments. To test these hypotheses, the present study aimed to (i) assess the physiological, biochemical, and molecular responses of rice under Cd–MP co-contamination, with particular emphasis on Cd bioavailability and soil properties, and (ii) elucidate the mechanisms by which BC–MT co-application reduces Cd uptake and enhances plant tolerance under combined Cd–MP stress.

Materials and methods

Experiment details

This study was conducted at Chuxiong Normal University, Yunnan Province, China, using soil samples collected from the experimental field (0–20 cm depth). The soil, characterized as silty loam with acidic pH (5.42), contained 119.12 mg kg⁻¹ available potassium (AK), 26.33 mg kg⁻¹ available phosphorus (AP), and 1.56 g kg⁻¹ total nitrogen (TN). Following collection, the soil was sieved (2 mm), and debris-free samples were prepared for pot filling (10 kg per pot). Cadmium (Cd) contamination was established by adding cadmium chloride (CdCl₂, analytical grade, ≥ 99% purity, Sigma-Aldrich, USA, Catalog No. 202908) to achieve a final concentration of 200 mg kg⁻¹ soil. The treated soil was stabilized at 70% field capacity for 2 months to allow Cd to equilibrate. Soil was spiked with 200 mg kg⁻¹ Cd, a concentration higher than typical environmental or industrial levels. This elevated dose was chosen to ensure observable physiological and molecular stress responses in a controlled experimental setting. While higher than field-relevant concentrations, the results provide mechanistic insights into plant responses and tolerance, which can inform predictions under realistic agricultural exposure scenarios [33, 34]. Subsequently, polyethylene microplastics (MPs) (10 μm in diameter) were uniformly incorporated at a concentration of 1% (w/w), based on previous research demonstrating significant growth inhibition at 0.5-2% MPs [35]. One week after the MP addition, Zea mays straw-BC (pyrolyzed at 500 °C) was mixed into designated pots at a 2% (w/w) concentration in designated pots. The BC exhibited alkaline properties (pH 9.71) with total C content of 84%, oxygen of 11%, phosphorus (P) of 1.7%, and magnesium (Mg) of 0.9%. Further details about the BC properties are presented in (Fig. 1). The experiment employed a completely randomized design with three replicates and five treatments: Control (uncontaminated soil), Cd (200 mg kg⁻¹) + MP (1%), Cd + MP + BC (2%), Cd + MP + MT (100 µM), and Cd + MP + BC + MT [34, 36]. Rice (Oryza sativa cv. ‘Nipponbare’) was transplanted at 5 seedlings per pot after soil stabilization. Melatonin (MT, ≥ 98% purity, Shanghai Macklin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China, CAS: 73-31-4) treatment was applied 20 days after transplantation via foliar spray at a rate of 15 mL per plant until run-off [37, 38]. Pots were maintained under natural sunlight (~ 450–500 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), with day/night temperatures of 25 ± 2 °C/20 ± 2 °C and a relative humidity of 60–70%. Water depth was kept at 2–3 cm to simulate paddy conditions. Plants were harvested at 90 days post-transplantation for comprehensive analyses.

Growth and photosynthetic measurements

Five plants per pot were harvested, with roots and shoots separated. Root length (RL) was measured, followed by fresh weight (FW) and dry weight (DW) after drying at 65 °C to constant weight. Aboveground measurements included tiller counting, total biomass (FW), grain yield, and 1000-grain weight. Oxidative markers, antioxidant activity, and osmolyte measurements

Leaf samples (0.5 g) were analyzed for membrane integrity and oxidative stress markers using established protocols. Electrolyte leakage (EL) was determined by incubating fresh leaves at 40 °C for 30 min to record initial electrical conductivity (EC₁), followed by an additional 10 min incubation to measure EC₂, with EL% calculated as (EC₁/EC₂) × 100. Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) concentration was measured by homogenizing 0.5 g of leaves in 0.1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA), centrifuging at 8,000 rpm for 15 min, and analyzing the supernatant mixed with potassium phosphate buffer (PPB) and potassium iodide (KI) at 600 nm. Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was assessed through leaf homogenization in 0.1% TCA, centrifugation (10,000 rpm) for 20 min, and spectrophotometric measurement at 532 nm and 600 nm.

Antioxidant enzyme activities were determined from leaf extracts prepared in 0.01 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min. Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity was measured by monitoring H₂O₂-dependent ascorbate oxidation at 290 nm in a reaction mixture containing 0.15 mL enzyme extract, 3 mM H₂O₂, 50 mM phosphate buffer, and 0.5 mM ascorbate [39]. Peroxidase (POD) activity was assayed according to the method of Chance and Maehly (1955), using 50 mM phosphate buffer, 30 mM H₂O₂, and 100 µL of enzyme extract, with absorbance measured at 240 nm. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was evaluated using the method of Zhang et al. [40], which involves the inhibition of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) reduction at 560 nm in a reaction mixture containing 100 µM EDTA-NA₂, 75 µM NBT, 20 µM riboflavin, and 130 mM methionine. Catalase (CAT) activity was determined as described by Aebi (1984) by measuring H₂O₂ decomposition at 240 nm in 50 mM phosphate buffer containing 10 mM H₂O₂ and 0.2 mL enzyme extract. Proline content was quantified for biochemical analyses following Bates et al. (1973) by homogenizing in 3% sulfosalicylic acid and centrifuging at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. Soluble protein was measured using the Bradford method [41], with absorbance measured at 595 nm after mixing the supernatant with the Bradford reagent.

Soil properties measurement

Following plant harvest, soil samples were collected from each treatment, with care taken to remove all root debris and plant material. Air-dried soil samples were analyzed for various properties. Soil pH was measured with a calibrated meter from a 1:5 (w/v) soil-water suspension. TN content was quantified by Kjeldahl digestion, while AP and AK concentrations were measured spectrophotometrically and by flame photometry, respectively. The sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) external-heating oxidation method was used to assess soil organic carbon (SOC) content [29].

Assessment of cadmium concentration

For the assessment of soil Cd concentration, samples were digested in a 1:2 (v/v) mixture of concentrated hydrochloric acid (HCl) and nitric acid (HNO₃), with Cd concentrations subsequently determined by atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS). Plant Cd accumulation patterns were evaluated using two key indices: the translocation factor (TF) and bioconcentration factor (BCF), which were calculated using the methods of Mahmood et al. [42]. Rice roots and shoots were oven-dried, finely ground into powder, and digested using a 1:2 (v/v) mixture of hydrochloric acid (HCl) and nitric acid (HNO₃), followed by Cd concentration measurement via atomic absorption spectrophotometry.

Seedling nutrient contents measurement

Nutrient concentrations in rice seedlings were quantified using established analytical methods. Nitrogen (N) content was determined via the Kjeldahl technique, and potassium (K) was measured by flame photometry. At the same time, calcium (Ca) and magnesium (Mg) were analyzed using AAS. The P concentration was measured spectrophotometrically using the molybdenum-blue method.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

Leaf samples were collected from rice plants and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen (N2) to preserve the RNA. Total RNA was extracted using the TIANGEN RNA extraction kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quality and concentration were verified via spectrophotometry (A260/A280 ratio) and agarose gel electrophoresis. For reverse transcription, 2.5 µL of high-quality RNA was converted to cDNA by HiScript® III RT SuperMix Kit (Vazyme). cDNA was reverse-transcribed from 1 µg of total RNA using the FastKing gDNA Dispelling RT SuperMix Kit. The SuperReal PreMix Plus (SYBR Green) Kit (Tiangen, FP205-2) was used to perform all quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) reactions. Gene expression levels were normalized to and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [43]. Three technical replicates were included for each biological sample. The specific primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS 25.0, with treatment means compared by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test at p < 0.05 significance level. Multivariate analyses, such as Pearson correlation and principal component analysis (PCA), were conducted in R Studio (R 4.3.0) using the “ggplot2” package. All graphical representations were generated using SigmaPlot (version 10.0), and data are presented as the mean ± standard error (SE; n = 3).

Results

Photosynthetic pigment responses to BC and MT under Cd–MP stress

Exposure to combined Cd and MP stress significantly reduced photosynthetic pigment concentrations relative to controls (Table 1). Individual applications of BC and MT partially alleviated these reductions, increasing chlorophyll a (Chl-a) by 73.64% and 56.06%, respectively. Notably, co-application of BC and MT demonstrated an enhanced effect, restoring Chl-a by 98.74%, chlorophyll b (Chl-b) by 82%, and carotenoids (Cart.) by 89.16% relative to stressed plants. These results indicate that BC and MT complement each other in protecting the photosynthetic apparatus, likely through metal immobilization BC and antioxidant enhancement MT (Table 1; p < 0.05).

Leaf water balance and oxidative stress responses

Combined Cd and MP stress significantly impaired leaf relative water content (RWC) and induced oxidative damage, while BC and MT treatments effectively mitigated these adverse effects (Table 1). Control plants maintained optimal RWC (75%), whereas Cd + MP exposure resulted in severe water loss (44% RWC). BC and MT treatments partially mitigated this stress, with the BC + MT combination achieving superior recovery (65% RWC) compared to BC (60%) or MT (53%) alone. Oxidative stress indicators, including electrolyte leakage (EL), malondialdehyde (MDA), and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), were markedly elevated under Cd + MP stress (EL + 153%, MDA + 319%, H₂O₂ +306%) (Fig. 2). Co-application of BC and MT effectively reduced these markers (EL: 14.51%; MDA: 110.59%; H₂O₂: 46.95%), highlighting their enhanced role in maintaining cellular water homeostasis and alleviating membrane lipid peroxidation.

Osmoprotectant accumulation and antioxidant enzyme responses

Combined Cd and MP stress significantly affected osmoprotectant levels and antioxidant enzyme activities in rice plants, while BC and MT treatments alleviated these effects (Figs. 2 and 3). In control plants, baseline levels of proline, total soluble proteins (TSP), and endogenous melatonin (ET) were 0.91 mg g⁻¹ FW, 5.40 mg g⁻¹ FW, and 16.73 ng g⁻¹ FW, respectively (Fig. 2). Cd + MP exposure triggered a stress response, leading to increased accumulation of these osmoprotectants as part of the plant’s defense mechanism. The combined BC + MT treatment resulted in the highest levels of proline (2.85 mg g⁻¹ FW; 213% increase), TSP (10.93 mg g⁻¹ FW; 102% increase), and endogenous MT (53.70 ng g⁻¹ FW; 221% increase) compared to Cd + MP-stressed plants (Fig. 2). Antioxidant enzyme activities showed a similar pattern. Cd + MP stress enhanced the activities of ascorbate peroxidase (APX; 24.01%), catalase (CAT; 41.18%), peroxidase (POD; 44.08%), and superoxide dismutase (SOD; 39.40%) relative to unstressed controls. Co-application of BC and MT further elevated enzyme activities, with increases of 99.42% (APX), 57.84% (CAT), 79.66% (POD), and 74.69% (SOD) compared to stressed plants (Fig. 3). These results indicated that BC and MT enhance osmoprotective and antioxidant defense systems, thereby improving rice tolerance to combined Cd and MP stress.

Growth, yield, and elemental accumulation

Combined Cd and MP stress significantly reduced rice growth and yield attributes, whereas BC and MT treatments alleviated these adverse effects (Table 2). Under Cd + MP stress, plants treated with the BC + MT combination exhibited improved root development, with a root length (RL) of 26.73 cm, fresh weight (RFW) of 9.37 g, and dry weight (RDW) of 3.73 g, compared to stressed controls. Cd + MP co-contamination markedly decreased key growth and yield parameters, including plant height (PH) (39.68%), tiller number per plant (TPP) (13.03%), thousand grain weight (TGW) (64.96%), total biomass (BYPP) (40.35%), grain yield (GYPP) (99.77%), and harvest index (HI) (42.75%). Application of BC + MT most effectively mitigated these reductions, restoring PH by 28.57%, TPP by 8.37%, TGW by 48.20%, BYPP by 24.56%, GYPP by 77.16%, and HI by 42.67% relative to Cd + MP-stressed plants (Table 2). These results indicated that BC and MT can enhance growth and yield under combined Cd and MP stress.

Nutrient analysis revealed that control plants maintained optimal concentrations of N, phosphorus (P), K, Iron (Fe), and zinc (Zn), whereas combined Cd and MP stress markedly reduced their accumulation (Fig. 4). Co-application of BC and MT significantly improved nutrient uptake, increasing N by 96.75%, P by 71.10%, K by 65.84%, Fe by 27.18%, and Zn by 81.30% relative to Cd + MP-stressed plants. Cd accumulation exhibited an inverse pattern. The highest Cd concentrations in roots and shoots were observed in untreated Cd + MP-stressed plants. The BC + MT treatment most effectively reduced Cd uptake, lowering root and shoot Cd concentrations by 65.89% and 71.14%, respectively, compared with stressed controls. Individual applications of BC or MT showed intermediate efficacy in reducing Cd accumulation (Fig. 4). These findings demonstrated that BC and MT enhance nutrient acquisition while limiting Cd translocation, thereby improving rice tolerance under combined Cd and MP stress.

Gene expression modulation

Combined Cd and MP stress significantly upregulated antioxidant defense genes in rice, with increases of 80.87% (OsAPX), 40.56% (OsCAT), 28.89% (OsPOD), and 40.54% (OsSOD) compared to unstressed controls. Cd + MP exposure also induced the expression of Cd transporter genes OsNRAMP1 and OsHMA3 (Fig. 5). Co-application of BC and MT further enhanced antioxidant gene expression, resulting in additional increases of 35.34% (OsAPX), 51% (OsCAT), 39.14% (OsPOD), and 51.44% (OsSOD) relative to Cd + MP-stressed plants. Simultaneously, BC + MT strongly suppressed the expression of metal uptake genes, with OsNRAMP1 and OsHMA3 reduced by 65% and 58%, respectively. Moreover, BC + MT co-application activated stress-mitigation pathways, elevating OsCOMT (melatonin biosynthesis) by 5.8-fold and OsP5CS (proline biosynthesis) by 4.5-fold compared to Cd + MP stress alone (Fig. 5). These coordinated molecular responses indicate that BC and MT enhance plant defense under combined contamination by (i) amplifying antioxidant capacity, (ii) limiting Cd uptake, and (iii) promoting stress-protectant biosynthesis (Fig. 5).

Soil property responses

Co-application of BC and MT most effectively reduced soil Cd availability, with a 73.60% decrease compared to Cd + MP-stressed soils, whereas individual applications of BC and MT reduced Cd availability by 46.84% and 21.69%, respectively (Fig. 6). Soil pH was slightly decreased under Cd + MP stress; however, BC alone and in combination with MT significantly increased pH, whereas MT alone had no significant effect. Cd + MP contamination also reduced soil nutrient levels, with decreases of 40.81% (N), 83.99% (P), 42.13% (K), and 25.69% (SOC) relative to controls. Remediation treatments improved soil fertility, with the BC + MT combination achieving the greatest enhancements: N (31.63%), P (54.95%), K (33.29%), and SOC (66.79%). Individual BC and MT treatments also increased nutrient availability, albeit to a lesser extent (BC alone: N 23.46%, P 43.80%, K 23.76%, SOC 44.02%; MT alone: N 12.24%, P 21.70%, K 12.90%, SOC 24.71%) (Fig. 6).

Multivariate analysis of treatment effects

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct treatment-specific response patterns. Growth traits accounted for 93.8% of the total variance, with clear separation between Cd + MP-stressed and control plants along PC1 (84.6%). Remediation treatments (T2–T4) formed a tight cluster, reflecting their consistent mitigation effects (Fig. 7). Physiological traits, representing 95.7% of the variance, showed that BC and MT treatments clustered closely with antioxidant and osmolyte markers along PC2 (41.6%), indicating their role in alleviating oxidative stress. Nutrient and gene expression analyses (97.9% variance) positioned controls negatively along PC1 (61.6%), reflecting optimal nutrient status, whereas BC/MT treatments clustered with improved nutrient profiles and reduced Cd accumulation (Fig. 7). Analysis of soil properties further corroborated these trends, with BC+MT applications consistently associating with enhanced soil quality and reduced Cd uptake in plant tissues. Collectively, PCA highlights the enhanced effect of BC and MT in improving plant performance, physiological resilience, nutrient homeostasis, and soil health under combined Cd and MP stress.

Correlation analysis of treatment effects

Correlation analysis revealed significant relationships among measured parameters under combined Cd and MP stress (Fig. 8). Yield components were strongly positively correlated (r > 0.75) with most growth and physiological traits, except total plant protein (TPP), which showed only a weak association (r < 0.3). Antioxidant enzymes and osmolytes were closely positively correlated (r = 0.82–0.91), reflecting their coordinated role in mitigating Cd + MP toxicity. In contrast, oxidative stress markers (MDA, EL, H₂O₂) exhibited strong negative correlations with both antioxidant capacity and EM levels (r = -0.68 to -0.79), indicating their responsiveness to remediation treatments. Nutrient analysis revealed competitive interactions with Cd accumulation, with N (r = -0.85) and K (r = -0.81) showing strong negative correlations. SOC was positively correlated with macronutrient availability (r = 0.78–0.86) and negatively correlated with Cd accumulation (r = -0.72), highlighting its dual role in improving soil fertility and immobilizing metals. The BC + MT-mediated increase in soil pH (ΔpH = 1.2–1.8 units) was strongly negatively correlated with Cd bioavailability (r = -0.88), confirming the importance of pH modulation in the remediation mechanism (Fig. 8).

Discussion

Soil is a vital growth medium for plants, yet various pollutants are increasingly accumulating, compromising agricultural productivity, soil health, and human food safety [35]. Among these contaminants, MPs are detrimental to plant growth and physiological processes [35]. MPs further exacerbate environmental risks by acting as vectors for toxic HMs, significantly amplifying their bioavailability and phytotoxicity [44]. Our results demonstrate that combined exposure to MP and Cd significantly reduces both aboveground (shoots) and belowground (roots) biomass in rice plants, with roots showing greater sensitivity (42% vs. 31% reduction in shoots and roots, respectively). This differential response likely reflects the roots’ direct contact with soil and higher exposure to contaminants. The observed growth inhibition resulted from three enhanced mechanisms: (1) elevated oxidative stress (MDA 153%, H2O2 +306%), (2) enhanced Cd uptake and translocation (root Cd accumulation: 319%), and (3) impaired physiological functions including nutrient acquisition (N 41%, K 42%) and chlorophyll synthesis (Chl a 65%). These findings align with established reports of MP-Cd co-contamination effects in crops [31, 45], confirming their interactive toxicity in agricultural systems. In the acidic, silty loam soil used in this study, MPs showed limited adsorption capacity, thereby increasing the bioavailable fraction of Cd in the soil solution (dilution effect) [18, 46]. At the same time, MPs can act as vectors facilitating Cd transport within the soil–plant system. Under our experimental conditions, the dominant effect appears to be the increased Cd availability due to low adsorption, while the vector effect may additionally contribute to enhanced uptake. This distinction clarifies that MPs can both occupy space and modestly transport Cd, depending on soil properties.

The BC and MT demonstrated superior efficacy compared to individual treatments, enhancing rice growth and biomass yield by 28–77% under Cd-MP co-contamination. Our data showed that the BC + MT treatment reduced Cd accumulation in rice tissues and decreased oxidative stress markers (MDA and H₂O₂). These effects are consistent with previous reports suggesting BC-mediated Cd immobilization and MT-induced ROS scavenging [47, 48], although the precise molecular mechanisms were not directly measured in this study. These findings confirm that BC and MT act through distinct but mutually reinforcing pathways: BC primarily modifies soil chemistry, while MT enhances plant stress tolerance, providing comprehensive protection against combined contamination. Photosynthetic pigment concentrations showed strong positive correlations with photosynthetic efficiency in this study. The combined Cd + MP stress significantly reduced chlorophyll synthesis (Chl a: 45% decrease; Chl b: 38% decrease), consistent with previous findings in rice [49]. This chlorophyll degradation likely resulted from multiple mechanisms: (1) ROS-induced damage to chloroplast ultrastructure [50], (2) disruption of pigment-protein-lipid complexes, and (3) activation of chlorophyll-degrading enzymes [48]. Carotenoids, which serve as crucial membrane-associated antioxidants under stress conditions, also declined by 32% under Cd + MP exposure, indicating compromised oxidative defense capacity in rice plants. Notably, the combined BC and MT treatment effectively counteracted these effects, increasing chlorophyll and carotenoid content by 82% through enhanced enhancement of both pigment biosynthesis and antioxidant protection.

The integrated BC and MT supply improved plant performance under Cd and MP stress through multiple mechanisms. First, the treatment enhanced the availability of essential nutrients, particularly Fe and Mg, while protecting the photosynthetic apparatus from oxidative damage. This dual action resulted in improved chlorophyll and carotenoid synthesis [42]. Furthermore, MT was found to upregulate key genes involved in chlorophyll biosynthesis, thereby enhancing photosynthetic pigment production even under metal stress [51]. Cd + MP co-exposure significantly reduced leaf RWC through several pathways. Cd stress impaired root elongation, disrupted aquaporin function, and triggered stomatal closure via increased ABA synthesis, collectively reducing water uptake. MPs exacerbated these effects by physically restricting water absorption and further promoting stomatal closure, ultimately decreasing transpiration rates. Reduced water content in Cd-stressed plants may be associated with disrupted aquaporin function and ABA-triggered stomatal closure, as suggested by previous studies [52, 53]. These are presented here as hypothesized mechanisms rather than directly measured responses, consistent with the scope of the present study.

The Cd + MP combination enhanced MDA production, consistent with earlier studies reporting that combined Cd + MP toxicity significantly increased MDA production [54]. The presence of MP and Cd increased ROS production, consistent with earlier studies [32]. Plants possess various antioxidants to counteract ROS [55]. We observed that MP presence increased Cd availability and accumulation in plant tissue, thereby increasing ROS production. The activities of CAT and SOD were significantly increased by BC and MT, indicating the neutralization of ROS to counter Cd- and MP-induced oxidative damage [56]. The BC and MT applications significantly enhanced the expression of OsAPX6, OsCAT, OsPOD, and OsSOD, consistent with improved antioxidant defense. While other transporters, such as OsABCC1, may also play a role, this was not measured in our study and is presented as a literature-supported hypothesis [57]. BC also enhances root ROS scavenging capacity by increasing antioxidant activity, thereby alleviating ROS-induced toxicity [29].

Osmo-protectants play a crucial role in plant growth under stress conditions. Soluble proteins are crucial osmo-regulatory compounds that maintain cellular balance and protect cellular membranes [58]. BC and MT significantly increased TSP synthesis by enhancing nutrient uptake, particularly N, which is crucial for protein synthesis. This also suggests that BC + MT may facilitate the conversion of amino acids into protein, increasing TSP [59]. Proline is also crucial in osmotic adjustment and counteracting the toxic effects of HMs. The BC and MT increased proline synthesis, thereby maintaining osmotic adjustment and counteracting the toxicity of Cd + MPs [60]. MT improves antioxidant activities, hormonal balance, and plant growth [61]. We observed that MT synthesis increased under Cd + MP, suggesting that rice plants upregulated MT synthesis to counter the toxicity of this combination. Furthermore, BC + MT significantly enhanced MT’s endogenous synthesis by increasing OsCOMT expression, which is involved in MT synthesis.

In the current study, we observed that MP increased the availability of Cd. Generally, MPs exhibit low adsorption capacity for pollutants, leading to a dilution effect that increases the availability and mobility of contaminants [62, 63]. Furthermore, metal availability varies with soil pH, microbial communities, SOC, and root exudates, and all these factors are altered by MP [64]. We found that MP increased Cd uptake, aligning with previous studies [65]. The MPs act as vectors for Cd and lower the soil pH (Fig. 6); they may also contribute to physical abrasion on root surfaces, making them more permeable to Cd. This, in turn, increased the Cd availability and its accumulation in plant organs. We also observed that Cd concentration was higher in roots than in shoots. The plant roots come into direct contact with Cd, which may be a crucial factor in the increased accumulation of Cd in the roots. However, BC and MT significantly reduced Cd availability. This may be linked to the fact that BC alters soil microbial activity, nutrient availability, and root exudates, which, in turn, affect root absorption and the subsequent translocation of toxic metals to aerial plant parts [64, 66]. Further, BC enhanced SOC, which immobilizes and fixes Cd by increasing soil pH, thereby reducing its availability and subsequent accumulation in plant tissues.

The results indicated that Cd + MP decreased the soil pH (Fig. 6). Microplastics reduce soil aeration, thereby indirectly altering soil pH [67]. Furthermore, the presence of toxic metals in soil also alters microbial communities, favoring the activity of acid-producing bacteria and thereby decreasing soil pH [68]. However, BC + MT significantly enhanced soil pH due to the alkaline nature of BC and the increase in base cations following BC and MT. MPs combined with Cd decreased nutrient availability and their accumulation in plant tissues. The Cd alters soil microbial N cycling and the genes involved in N cycling, thereby reducing N availability and plant N uptake [69, 70]. The presence of Cd, along with MP, inhibits N fixation and nitrification and suppresses the activities of microbes that secrete acids to solubilize P, thereby reducing the availability of both N and P. The Cd and MP might also reduce the release of K minerals and displace K from soil colloids and root exchange sites, thus decreasing K availability and its uptake by roots [71].

The BC + MT decreased Cd availability and increased nutrient availability. The BC and MT enhanced the soil pH, potentially increasing electrostatic attraction between the negatively charged BC surfaces and Cd, thereby decreasing Cd mobility and its accumulation in plant organs [72]. Furthermore, the negatively charged functional groups of BC form stable complexes with Cd, thereby decreasing its availability [73]. The EDS analysis of BC revealed that it contained significant amounts of C, Ca, Mg, Si, and K, which triggered cation exchange with Cd, thereby immobilizing Cd in the soil and reducing its accumulation [8]. Previous studies suggest BC may influence Cd transporters such as OsABCC1, potentially reducing Cd uptake; however, this was not directly measured in our experiments and remains a literature-supported hypothesis [74]. The BC and MT application significantly enhanced nutrient availability and accumulation under Cd + MP-polluted soil. BC is a crucial source of nutrients and modulates soil microbial activity, thereby improving the availability of soil nutrients. Furthermore, BC and MT increase root growth, which may help with nutrient and water uptake. The BC has a significant surface area that binds Cd, reducing its availability and reducing subsequent competition with essential nutrients. Both BC and MT increase soil pH, enhance nutrient retention, stimulate root growth, and chelate Cd, thereby decreasing its interference with nutrient uptake [75].

Conclusions

In summary, MPs and Cd co-contamination significantly reduced rice productivity by enhancing Cd bioavailability, inducing oxidative stress, and impairing photosynthetic activity, physiological performance, and soil nutrient status. In contrast, the combined application of BC and foliar MT spray markedly improved rice growth and yield under Cd–MP stress. These improvements were associated with reduced Cd bioavailability and tissue accumulation, enhanced physiological performance, increased soil nutrient and C availability, and the upregulation of genes involved in antioxidant defense, melatonin biosynthesis, and proline metabolism. Collectively, these findings highlight BC and MT as promising sustainable strategies to mitigate the adverse effects of Cd–MP co-contamination in rice production. Future research should investigate interactions among MP types and concentrations, their impacts on soil microbial diversity and plant transcriptomic responses, and the optimization of BC and MT application regimes to support the scalable implementation of agricultural practices.
