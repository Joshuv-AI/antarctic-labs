Extremal problems on the p p -Seidel energy of graphs
Alexander Guterman, Shib Sankar Saha

Abstract
Let G G be a graph with vertex set { v 1 , … , v n } \{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\} . The Seidel matrix of G G is an n × n n\times n matrix whose diagonal entries are zero, i ​ j ij -th entry is − 1 -1 if v i v_{i} and v j v_{j} are adjacent, and otherwise is 1 1 . The p p -Seidel energy of the graph G G is defined as the sum of the absolute values of the p p -th powers of all eigenvalues of the Seidel matrix of G G and introduced in [European Journal of Combinatorics, (86) (2020), 103078]. In this article, we characterize the graph that minimizes the p p -Seidel energy among all graphs with fixed order n n , for p > 2 p>2 . We also characterize the graph that maximizes the p p -Seidel energy among all graphs with fixed order n n , for 0 < p < 2 0<p<2 . In addition, for every p > 2 p>2 , we characterize the graph that minimizes the p p -Seidel energy among all r r -regular graphs with fixed order n n , where n n is a prime power with n ≡ 1 ( mod 4 ) n\equiv 1\pmod{4} , r = n − 1 2 r=\frac{n-1}{2} . For every p > 2 p>2 , we also characterize the graph that maximizes the p p -Seidel energy among all r r -regular graphs with fixed order n = 2 ​ r n=2r . Finally, we pose several open problems concerning the p p -Seidel energy for different values of p p .

Remark 2.3

The Seidel matrix

\operatorname{tr}\big(S(G)^{2}\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(S(G))_{ij}^{2}=n(n-1),

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}=n(n-1).

(1)

From equality (

\mathcal{E}_{2}(S(G))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}=n(n-1),

Remark 2.6
.

Conference matrices were introduced by Belevitch in connection with ideal telephone conference networks
[
3
]
. The subsequent combinatorial formulation linked these matrices with conference graphs
[

]
. Although the existence of conference graphs is unresolved for many orders

The following lemma is known as the classical power mean inequality (also called the generalized mean inequality).

Lemma 2.7
.

[
5
, Theorem 1]

Let
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
be nonnegative real numbers and let

. Then

\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}\geq\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{s}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}},

with equality if and only if
a_{1}=a_{2}=\cdots=a_{n}
.

In the following theorem, we prove that among all graphs, the conference graph achieves the minimum
p
-Seidel energy with fixed order
n
, where
p>2
, which also answers Question
2.4
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
G
be a simple graph on
n\geq 2
vertices. Then, for every
p>2
,

\mathcal{E}_{p}(S(G))\;\geq\;n\,(n-1)^{\frac{p}{2}},

with equality if and only if the Seidel matrix
S(G)
is a conference matrix, i.e.,

S(G)^{2}=(n-1)I.

In particular, for
n\geq 5
, equality holds if and only if
G
is a conference graph.

Proof.

Let
\lambda_{i}
be the Seidel eigenvalues of
G
, for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
.

Substituting
a_{i}=|\lambda_{i}|
into Lemma
2.7
and using equality (
1
) we obtain

\mathcal{E}_{p}(S(G))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|^{p}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{p}\;\geq\;n\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}=n\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}=n\,(n-1)^{\frac{p}{2}},

the equality holds if and only if
|\lambda_{1}|=\cdots=|\lambda_{n}|
.

Let us denote
|\lambda_{1}|=\cdots=|\lambda_{n}|=a
. Using equality (
1
), we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a^{2}=na^{2}=n(n-1),

hence
a^{2}=n-1
and therefore

|\lambda_{i}|=\sqrt{n-1}\quad\text{for all }i=1,\dots,n.

If every Seidel eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
of
G
satisfies
\lambda_{i}^{2}=n-1
, so all Seidel eigenvalues are roots of
x^{2}-(n-1)
. Since
S(G)
is real symmetric, it is diagonalizable, and its minimal polynomial has distinct roots. Hence the minimal polynomial of
S(G)
must divide
x^{2}-(n-1)
and also have the same roots, so

S(G)^{2}=(n-1)I.

Hence,
S(G)
is a conference matrix.

Conversely, if
S(G)^{2}=(n-1)I
, then every eigenvalue
\lambda
of
S(G)
satisfies
\lambda^{2}=n-1
, so

|\lambda_{i}|=\sqrt{n-1}\quad\text{for all }i=1,\dots,n,

and hence

\mathcal{E}_{p}(S(G))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|^{p}=n\,(n-1)^{\frac{p}{2}}.

This shows that equality holds if and only if
S(G)
is a conference matrix.

Hence, for
n\geq 5
, it is known that a simple graph
G
has a Seidel matrix
S(G)
that is a conference matrix if and only if
G
is a conference graph.

This completes the proof.
∎

For

and using the Lemma
2.7

\left(\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}z_{i}^{p}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{p}}\;\geq\;\left(\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}z_{i}^{2}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{2}},

\hskip 28.45274pt\implies\sum_{i=1}^{t}z_{i}^{p}\;\geq\;t^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t}z_{i}^{2}\right)^{\!\frac{p}{2}},

\hskip 28.45274pt\implies\sum_{i=2}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|^{p}\;\geq\;(n-1)^{1-\frac{p}{2}}\!\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}},

Now using equality (

G
is

\operatorname{Spec}\big(A(G)\big)=\Big\{\frac{n-1}{2},\ \underbrace{\theta_{1},\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{1}}_{\frac{n-1}{2}~\text{times}},\ \underbrace{\theta_{2},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{2}}_{\frac{n-1}{2}~\text{times}}\Big\}.

where
\theta_{1}=\frac{-1+\sqrt{n}}{2}
and
\theta_{2}=\frac{-1-\sqrt{n}}{2}
.

Since
G
is
r
-regular with
r=\frac{n-1}{2}
and from Lemma
3.2
,
(n-1-2r)=(n-1-(n-1))=0
is a Seidel eigenvalue of multiplicity
1
and the remaining Seidel eigenvalues are
-(1+2\theta)=\pm\sqrt{n}
, each with multiplicity
\frac{n-1}{2}
.

Hence, the Seidel spectrum of
G
is

\operatorname{Spec}(S(G))=\Big\{0,\ \underbrace{-\sqrt{n},\dots,-\sqrt{n}}_{\frac{n-1}{2}~\text{times}},\ \underbrace{\sqrt{n},\dots,\sqrt{n}}_{\frac{n-1}{2}~\text{times}}\Big\}.

Therefore

\mathcal{E}_{p}(S(G))=\Big(\frac{n-1}{2}\Big)~|\sqrt{n}|^{p}+\Big(\frac{n-1}{2}\Big)~|-\sqrt{n}|^{p}=(n-1)n^{\frac{p}{2}},

so
G
attained at the lower bound in inequality (
3
).

This completes the proof.
∎

4
Maximum
p
-Seidel energy of
r
-regular graphs,
p>2

In mathematical optimization, the method of Lagrange multipliers is a strategy for finding the local extrema (i.e., maxima and minima) of a function subject to constraint equations (i.e., subject to the condition that one or more equations have to be satisfied exactly by the chosen values of the variables)
[
10
]
. The following lemma summarizes the method of Lagrangian multipliers.

Lemma 4.1
.

[
12
, Theorem, Page 327]

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g_{1},\dots,g_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuously differentiable functions, and consider the constrained optimization problem

\text{maximize/minimize }F(x)\quad\text{subject to}\quad g_{1}(x)=0,\ \dots,\ g_{k}(x)=0.

Assume that
x^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a local extremum and that the gradients
\nabla g_{1}(x^{\ast}),\dots,\nabla g_{k}(x^{\ast})
are linearly independent.

Then there exist real numbers
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}
such that

\nabla F(x^{\ast})=\alpha_{1}\,\nabla g_{1}(x^{\ast})+\cdots+\alpha_{k}\,\nabla g_{k}(x^{\ast}),

(4)

together with the constraints

g_{1}(x^{\ast})=\cdots=g_{k}(x^{\ast})=0.

Thus, any interior constrained extremum must satisfy the system (
4
).

To determine the maximum
p
-Seidel energy among all
r
-regular graphs, we need to introduce a new summation function and optimize it under certain constraints. Thus, the optimization problem with constraints is stated in the following lemma, which is valid for
p>2
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
p>2
. Consider the following problem

F=F(t_{1},\dots,t_{n-1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|t_{i}|^{p}

subject to the constraints

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}=1,~~~~\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}^{2}=n(n-1)-1,~~~~~|t_{i}|\leq n-1\ \forall i=1,\ldots,n-1.

Then the maximum of
F
is attained by all vectors that are
\big\{n-1,~\underbrace{-1,-1,\dots,-1}_{(n-2)~\text{times}}\big\}
up to the ordering.

Proof.

Let us consider the feasible set to be

\mathcal{M}=\Bigl\{(t_{1},\dots,t_{\,n-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{\,n-1}:\ \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}=1,\ \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}^{\,2}=n(n-1)-1,\ |t_{i}|\leq n-1\ \forall i\Bigr\}.

Then
\mathcal{M}
is non-empty as
t^{(0)}=\{n-1,~\underbrace{-1,-1,\dots,-1}_{(n-2)~\text{times}}\}\in\mathcal{M}
. The set
\mathcal{M}
is closed and bounded, hence compact. Since
F
is continuous, the maximum of
F
over
\mathcal{M}
is attained at some

t^{\ast}=(t_{1}^{\ast},\dots,t_{n-1}^{\ast})\in\mathcal{M}.

Case (1):
Suppose that
t^{\ast}
has a coordinate on the boundary, i.e.,
|t_{j}^{\ast}|=n-1~~\text{for some }j
. Without loss of generality, assume that
j=z
,
1\leq z\leq(n-1)
.

Subcase (1.1):
Let
t_{z}^{\ast}=n-1
.

We have

\sum_{i\neq z,~i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}^{\ast}=1-t_{z}^{\ast}=1-(n-1)=2-n.

Thus the arithmetic mean of
t_{1}^{\ast},t_{2}^{\ast}\dots,t_{z-1}^{\ast},t_{z+1}^{\ast},\dots,t_{n-1}^{\ast}
is

\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{i\neq z,~i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}^{\ast}=\frac{2-n}{n-2}=-1.

We have

\sum_{i\neq z,~i=1}^{n-1}(t_{i}^{\ast})^{2}=\bigl(n(n-1)-1\bigr)-(t_{z}^{\ast})^{2}=n(n-1)-1-(n-1)^{2}=n-2.

Thus the arithmetic mean of the squares is

\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{i\neq z,~i=1}^{n-1}(t_{i}^{\ast})^{2}=\frac{n-2}{n-2}=1.

From Lemma
2.7
, for any non-negative real numbers
x_{1},\dots,x_{n-2}
,

\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}x_{i}^{2}\geq\left(\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}x_{i}\right)^{2},

(5)

with equality if and only if
x_{1}=x_{2}=\cdots=x_{n-2}
.

Substituting
x_{i}=t_{i}^{\ast}
into the inequality (
5
), we have

t_{1}^{\ast}=\cdots=t_{z-1}^{\ast}=t_{z+1}^{\ast}=\cdots=t_{n-1}^{\ast}=-1.

Therefore

t^{\ast}\ \text{is}\ \{n-1,~\underbrace{-1,-1,\dots,-1}_{(n-2)~\text{times}}\}~\text{up to the ordering}.

Subcase (1.2):
Let
t_{z}^{\ast}=-(n-1)
.

We have

\sum_{i\neq z,~i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}^{\ast}=1-t_{z}^{\ast}=1+(n-1)=n.

Thus the arithmetic mean of
t_{1}^{\ast},t_{2}^{\ast}\dots,t_{z-1}^{\ast},t_{z+1}^{\ast},\dots,t_{n-1}^{\ast}
is

\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{i\neq z,~i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}^{\ast}=\frac{n}{n-2}.

Again we have

\sum_{i\neq z,~i=1}^{n-1}(t_{i}^{\ast})^{2}=\bigl(n(n-1)-1\bigr)-(t_{z}^{\ast})^{2}=n(n-1)-1-(n-1)^{2}=n-2.

Thus, the arithmetic mean of the squares is

\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{i\neq z,~i=1}^{n-1}(t_{i}^{\ast})^{2}=\frac{n-2}{n-2}=1.

Substituting
x_{i}=t_{i}^{\ast}
into the inequality (
5
), we have

1\geq\Big(\frac{n}{n-2}\Big)^{2},

a contradiction as
n\geq 3
. Hence, this subcase is impossible for a feasible point (a point that satisfies all the given constraints).

Combining the two subcases, we conclude that

t^{\ast}\ \text{is}\ \big\{n-1,~\underbrace{-1,-1,\dots,-1}_{(n-2)~\text{times}}\big\}~\text{up to the ordering}.

Case (2):
Assume that the maximum is attained at a point
t^{\ast}
whose coordinates satisfy
|t_{i}^{\ast}|<n-1
for all
i
.

Let us define

g_{1}(t)=g_{1}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n-1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}-1,\quad g_{2}(t)=g_{2}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n-1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}^{2}-\bigl(n(n-1)-1\bigr).

The gradients of the constraints are

\nabla g_{1}(t)=\big(\underbrace{1,1,\dots,1}_{(n-1)~\text{times}}\big),\qquad\nabla g_{2}(t)=\big(\underbrace{2t_{1},2t_{2},\dots,2t_{n-1}}_{(n-1)~\text{times}}\big).

These are linearly independent for any
t
with not all
t_{i}
equal, which is certainly the case here because of the constraints.

Therefore, we apply Lemma
4.1
at
t^{\ast}
for some
\alpha_{1},~\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.

\nabla F(t^{\ast})=\alpha_{1}\,\nabla g_{1}(t^{\ast})+\alpha_{2}\,\nabla g_{2}(t^{\ast}),

(6)

together with the conditions,

g_{1}(t^{\ast})=0,\quad g_{2}(t^{\ast})=0.

Now computing
\nabla F
in each coordinate
t_{j}
we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{j}}|t_{j}|^{p}=p\,|t_{j}|^{p-1}\operatorname{sgn}(t_{j}).

Thus

\Big(\nabla F(t)\Big)_{j}=p\,t_{j}|t_{j}|^{p-2}.

Computing
\nabla g_{1},~\nabla g_{2}
in each coordinate
t_{j}
we have

\frac{\partial g_{1}}{\partial t_{j}}=1,~~\frac{\partial g_{2}}{\partial t_{j}}=2t_{j}.

Substituting into equality (
6
), the
j
-th coordinate of that vector equation we obtain

p\,|t^{\ast}_{j}|^{p-1}\operatorname{sgn}(t^{\ast}_{j})=\alpha_{1}\cdot 1+\alpha_{2}\cdot 2t_{j}^{\ast}=\alpha_{1}+2\alpha_{2}t_{j}^{\ast}.

Finally,

p\,|t^{\ast}_{j}|^{p-1}\operatorname{sgn}(t^{\ast}_{j})-\alpha_{1}-2\alpha_{2}\,t_{j}^{\ast}=0,~~~j=1,\dots,n-1.

(7)

We now prove that equality (
7
) can take at most two distinct values of the coordinate. Suppose by contradiction that equality (
7
) holds for at least three different values
a<b<c
. Then we can choose indices
i,j
such that
t_{i}^{\ast}=b
and
t_{j}^{\ast}\in\{a,c\}
.

We keep all coordinates except
(t_{i},t_{j})
fixed and vary the pair
(t_{i},t_{j})
under the two constraints

t_{i}+t_{j}=S,~~t_{i}^{2}+t_{j}^{2}=Q,

where
S=1-\sum\limits_{k\neq i,j}t_{k}^{\ast}
and
Q=n(n-1)-1-\sum\limits_{k\neq i,j}(t_{k}^{\ast})^{2}
.

Let the feasible set for
(t_{i},t_{j})
be
\mathcal{C}=\{(t_{i},t_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:t_{i}+t_{j}=S,\ t_{i}^{2}+t_{j}^{2}=Q\}
.

Let

\Phi(t_{i},t_{j})=|t_{i}|^{p}+|t_{j}|^{p},~~p>2.

Since the function
u\mapsto|u|^{p}
is strictly convex for
p>2
,
\Phi(t_{i},t_{j})
is strictly convex on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. A strictly convex function achieves its maximum on a compact one-dimensional set only at the endpoints of that set.

Eliminating
t_{j}
using
t_{j}=S-t_{i}
gives

t_{i}^{2}+(S-t_{i})^{2}=Q.

Expanding,

t_{i}^{2}+S^{2}-2St_{i}+t_{i}^{2}=Q,

so

2t_{i}^{2}-2St_{i}+(S^{2}-Q)=0.

This is a quadratic equation in
t_{i}
:

2t_{i}^{2}-2St_{i}+(S^{2}-Q)=0.

The solutions of the quadratic are,

t_{i,\pm}=\frac{2S\pm\sqrt{\,4S^{2}-8(S^{2}-Q)\,}}{4}=\frac{S\pm\sqrt{\,2Q-S^{2}\,}}{2}.

Thus, there are exactly two feasible values of
t_{i}
, which we denote by
t_{i,-}
and
t_{i,+}
with
t_{i,-}<t_{i,+}
. Then

t_{j,\pm}=S-t_{i,\pm}.

Hence, when
t_{i}=t_{i,+}
is maximum,
t_{j}=t_{j,-}=S-t_{i,+}
is minimum, and
when
t_{i}=t_{i,-}
is minimum,
t_{j}=t_{j,+}=S-t_{i,-}
is maximum.

Under the assumption
a<b<c
appears among the coordinates of
t^{\ast}
, choosing
t_{i}^{\ast}=b
and
t_{j}^{\ast}\in\{a,c\}
shows that
(t_{i}^{\ast},t_{j}^{\ast})
cannot be an endpoint, since
b
is neither minimum nor maximum. Hence,
(t_{i}^{\ast},t_{j}^{\ast})
is an interior point of the feasible set.

Because
\Phi(t_{i},t_{j})
is strictly convex for
p>2
, attains its maximum on
\mathcal{C}
. Therefore,
(t_{i}^{\ast},t_{j}^{\ast})
cannot maximize
\Phi
on the feasible set, a contradiction as
t^{\ast}
has three different values. Therefore, a maximizer
t^{\ast}
cannot have three distinct values of the coordinate.

Hence, a maximizer
t^{\ast}
can have at most two values of the coordinate. Let us call them
a
and
b
, and write the coordinates of the vector
t^{\ast}
as

t_{1}^{\ast}=\cdots=t_{k}^{\ast}=a,\qquad t_{k+1}^{\ast}=\cdots=t_{n-1}^{\ast}=b,

for some
1\leq k\leq n-2
, where
|a|,|b|\leq n-1
.

With
k
entries equal to
a
and
n-1-k
equal to
b
, the constraints become

\displaystyle ka+(n-1-k)b

\displaystyle=1,

(8)

\displaystyle ka^{2}+(n-1-k)b^{2}

\displaystyle=n(n-1)-1.

(9)

Since the vector
(t_{1}^{\ast},\dots,t_{n-1}^{\ast})
contains
k
components equal
to
a
and
n-1-k
components equal to
b
, the arithmetic mean is

\bar{t}=\frac{ka+(n-1-k)b}{n-1}.

Hence

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(t_{i}-\bar{t})^{2}=k(a-\bar{t})^{2}+(n-1-k)(b-\bar{t})^{2}.

(10)

We compute

a-\bar{t}=a-\frac{ka+(n-1-k)b}{n-1}=\frac{(n-1-k)(a-b)}{n-1},

b-\bar{t}=b-\frac{ka+(n-1-k)b}{n-1}=-\,\frac{k(a-b)}{n-1}.

Thus

(a-\bar{t})^{2}=\frac{(n-1-k)^{2}(a-b)^{2}}{(n-1)^{2}},\qquad(b-\bar{t})^{2}=\frac{k^{2}(a-b)^{2}}{(n-1)^{2}}.

Substituting these into equality (
10
), we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(t_{i}-\bar{t})^{2}=\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{(n-1)^{2}}\Big(k(n-1-k)^{2}+(n-1-k)k^{2}\Big)=\frac{k(n-1-k)}{n-1}(a-b)^{2}.

Therefore,

(a-b)^{2}=\frac{\big(n(n-1)-1-\tfrac{1}{n-1}\big)(n-1)}{k(n-1-k)}.

(11)

Thus,
|a-b|
attains its maximum when
k(n-1-k)
attains its minimum in equation (
11
). Since the function
k\mapsto k(n-1-k)
is minimized at
k=1
or
k=n-2
, the maximum of
|a-b|
occurs at these values of
k
. Therefore, any maximizer
t^{\ast}
must have either

(t_{1}^{\ast},\dots,t_{n-1}^{\ast})=\Big(a,~\underbrace{b,b,\dots,b}_{(n-2)~\text{times}}\Big)\quad\text{or}\quad(t_{1}^{\ast},\dots,t_{n-1}^{\ast})=\Big(b,~\underbrace{a,a,\dots,a}_{(n-2)~\text{times}}\Big),

with
k=1
or
k=n-2
.

For
k=1
, we assume that
t_{1}^{\ast}=a
and
t_{2}^{\ast}=\cdots=t_{n-1}^{\ast}=b
. Then equality (
8
) gives

a+(n-2)b=1\implies a=1-(n-2)b.

Substituting the value of
a
into equality (
9
) we have

(1-(n-2)b)^{2}+(n-2)b^{2}=n(n-1)-1\implies b=-1.

Then

a=1-(n-2)(-1)=n-1.

Thus, one feasible extremal solution is

(t_{1}^{\ast},\dots,t_{n-1}^{\ast})=\{n-1,~\underbrace{-1,-1,\dots,-1}_{(n-2)~\text{times}}\}.

For
k=n-2
, we obtain the same multiset of coordinates.

Hence, a maximizer is

(t_{1}^{\ast},\dots,t_{n-1}^{\ast})=\{n-1,~\underbrace{-1,-1,\dots,-1}_{(n-2)~\text{times}}\}\quad\text{up to the ordering}.

However, this contradicts our assumption in Case (2) that
|t_{i}^{\ast}|<n-1

for all
i
, since one coordinate satisfies
|t_{i}^{\ast}|=n-1
. Therefore, Case (2) cannot occur.

This completes the proof.
∎

In the following result, Berman, Shaked-Monderer, Singh, and Zhang give the complete list of the Seidel spectrum of the complete bipartite graph
K_{r,s}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

[
4
, Lemma 2.1(c)]

Let
K_{p,q}
be the complete bipartite graph with
n=p+q
vertices. Then the Seidel spectrum of
K_{p,q}
is

\{n-1,~\underbrace{-1,-1,\dots,-1}_{(n-1)~\text{times}}\}.

Lemma 4.4
.

[
11
, Theorem 2.5.6]

Every real symmetric matrix
M
admits the spectral decomposition

M\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{t}\lambda_{i}\,u_{i}u_{i}^{\top},

where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{t}
are the nonzero eigenvalues of
M
, and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{t}
are the corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors.

We now characterize the graph whose Seidel spectrum is attained in Lemma
4.3
, up to isomorphism.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
G
be an
r
-regular graph on
n=2r
vertices. Then the following statements are equivalent:

1.

\,\operatorname{Spec}(S(G))=\{n-1,~\underbrace{-1,-1,\dots,-1}_{(n-1)\text{ times}}\}
.

2.

\,G\cong K_{r,r}
.

Proof.

(1)\implies(2):
We now prove that if a graph
G
has the given Seidel spectrum
\operatorname{Spec}(S(G))
, then it is isomorphic to
K_{r,r}
.

Since
S(G)
is a real symmetric matrix with eigenvalues
n-1
(with multiplicity
1
) and
1
(with multiplicity
n-1
). The spectral decomposition (Lemma
4.4
) gives

S(G)=(n-1)\,u_{1}u_{1}^{\top}+(-1)\sum_{i=2}^{n}u_{i}u_{i}^{\top},

where
u_{1}
is a unit eigenvector corresponding to
n-1
, and
u_{2},\ldots,u_{n}
are orthonormal eigenvectors corresponding to
-1
.

Using
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}u_{i}^{\top}=I
, we obtain

S(G)=(n-1)u_{1}u_{1}^{\top}-\big(I-u_{1}u_{1}^{\top}\big)=-I+n\,u_{1}u_{1}^{\top}.

Taking
v=u_{1}
, we have

S(G)=-I+n\,vv^{\top}.

From
S(G)_{ii}=0
we obtain

0=-1+nv_{i}^{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad v_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{n},\qquad\forall\,i.

Hence
v_{i}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
. For
i\neq j
,

S(G)_{ij}=nv_{i}v_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}.

Let
z_{i}=\sqrt{n}\,v_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
. Then

S(G)_{ij}=z_{i}z_{j},\qquad i\neq j.

On the definition of the Seidel and adjacency matrices,
a_{ij}=\frac{1-z_{i}z_{j}}{2}
, so vertices with the same sign of
z_{i}
are non-adjacent, and vertices with opposite signs are adjacent. Thus,
G
is a complete bipartite with bipartition
\{\,i:z_{i}=1\,\}\ \cup\ \{\,i:z_{i}=-1\,\}
. If
G
is
r
-regular and
n=2r
, both parts have size
r
, and therefore
G\cong K_{r,r}
.

(2)\implies(1):
Suppose that
G\cong K_{r,r}
. Then, by Lemma
4.3
, the result follows.

This completes the proof.
∎

In the following theorem, we prove that among all
r
-regular graphs, the balanced complete bipartite graph (up to isomorphism) achieves the maximum
p
-Seidel energy with fixed order
n=2r
, where
p>2
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
G
be an
r
-regular graph on
n=2r
vertices. Then for every
p>2
,

\mathcal{E}_{p}(S(G))\leq(n-1)^{p}+(n-1),

with equality if and only if
G\cong K_{r,r}
.

Proof.

Let
G
be an
r
-regular graph on
n=2r
vertices. For every Seidel matrix
S(G)
of order,
n
we have the following:

(a)

The diagonal of
S(G)
is zero, so
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=0
.

(b)

From equality (
1
) we have
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}=n(n-1)
.

(c)

Since the sum of the absolute values in each row of
S(G)
is
n-1
, every eigenvalue of
S(G)
has absolute value at most
n-1
. Therefore,

|\lambda_{i}|\leq n-1\quad\text{for all }i=1,2,\ldots,n.

Since
G
is
r
-regular with
n=2r
, then from Lemma
3.2
,
\lambda_{1}=n-1-2r=2r-1-2r=-1
is a Seidel eigenvalue with multiplicity
1
.

Let us denote
t_{i}=\lambda_{i+1},~~i=1,\dots,n-1
. Then
\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|^{p}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}|t_{i}|^{p}
and the Seidel spectrum is
\{-1,t_{1},\dots,t_{n-1}\}
.

From (a), we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}=-\lambda_{1}=1.

(12)

From (b), we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}^{2}=n(n-1)-\lambda_{1}^{2}=n(n-1)-1.

(13)

From (c), we know

|t_{i}|\leq n-1~~\forall~~i=1,\dots,n-1.

(14)

The
p
-Seidel energy of
G
is

\mathcal{E}_{p}(S(G))=|\lambda_{1}|^{p}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|^{p}=|-1|^{p}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|t_{i}|^{p}=|-1|^{p}+F(t_{1},\dots,t_{n-1}).

(15)

Thus in order to solve the problem of maximizing
\mathcal{E}_{p}(S(G))
over all
r
-regular graphs with
n=2r
, it is sufficient to solve the following optimization problem:

\max~F(t_{1},\dots,t_{n-1})=\max~\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|t_{i}|^{p},

subject to the constraints (
12
), (
13
), and (
14
).

From Lemma
4.2
,
\max~F(t_{1},\dots,t_{n-1})
attained at the following feasible point:

(t_{1},\dots,t_{n-1})=\Big\{n-1,~~\underbrace{-1,-1,\dots,-1}_{(n-2)~\text{times}}\Big\},\text{up to the ordering.}

By Lemma
4.5
, a graph has Seidel spectrum
\{n-1,\underbrace{-1,-1,\dots,-1}_{(n-1)\text{ times}}\}
if and only if it is isomorphic to the balanced complete bipartite graph
K_{r,r}
. Therefore, this Seidel eigenvalue pattern is achieved by a graph, and that graph must be
K_{r,r}
.

Substituting this spectrum into (
15
) gives

\mathcal{E}_{p}(S(G))=|-1|^{p}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|t_{i}|^{p}\leq|-1|^{p}+\Big(|n-1|^{p}+(n-2)\cdot|-1|^{p}\Big),

with equality if and only if
G\cong K_{r,r}
.

Hence

\mathcal{E}_{p}(S(G))\leq(n-1)^{p}+(n-1),

with equality if and only if
G\cong K_{r,r}
.

This completes the proof.
∎
