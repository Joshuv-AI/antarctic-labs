Assessment of drug storage temperature compliance in veterinary clinics within Nigeria’s Federal Capital Territory
Authors: Jude Nduka Omeje, Jones Soladoye Akinbobola, Isaac Divine Uzochukwu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe efficacy and safety of pharmaceutical products are critically dependent on maintaining appropriate storage temperatures throughout the supply chain, from manufacturer to end-user. In veterinary medicine, this includes storage within clinics and during ambulatory services. The Federal Capital Territory (FCT) of Nigeria experiences high ambient temperatures, posing a significant risk to drug stability. This study aimed to investigate drug storage temperatures in veterinary clinics that also dispense and retail veterinary pharmaceuticals within the FCT and to assess their compliance with manufacturers’ recommended storage conditions. A cross-sectional study was conducted across 23 veterinary clinics in four Area Councils of the FCT (AMAC, Bwari, Gwagwalada, Kuje). A structured questionnaire was used to gather data on storage infrastructure, including refrigerator use, alternative power supply, and temperature monitoring devices. The temperature of drug storage areas was recorded over two weeks (March 1st-15th, 2025) using an HTC-2 thermometer (Guangdong, China). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics. While all 23 (100%) clinics possessed a refrigerator, only 60% had an alternative power supply. Critically, 60.9% (n = 14) of clinics lacked any form of temperature monitoring device in their storage areas. The mean ambient storage temperatures in all clinics exceeded the recommended maximum of 30 °C for pharmaceuticals stored in cabinets, with some clinics recording temperatures above 36 °C. During ambulatory services, 87% of Clinicians used drug storage boxes, but 30% parked their vehicles in areas without shade, potentially exposing drugs to high temperatures during transport. This study reveals a significant gap in compliance with manufacturer drug storage temperature recommendations among veterinary clinics in the FCT. Introduction

Veterinarians provide medical care not only within clinic settings but also during farm visits and ambulatory services [1]. In Nigeria, as in many low-resource settings, veterinary retail shops and practices are critical points in the pharmaceutical supply chain [2]. The rational use of medications is fundamentally determined by their quality at the point of administration, which is directly influenced by procurement, storage, and distribution practices [3]. Poorly stored medications can degrade, losing their potency, hence, treatment failure and potential toxicity [4].

The National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) provides guidelines for drug storage, emphasizing temperature as one of the most critical conditions to control. These guidelines stipulate that “room temperature” for pharmaceutical storage should be between 22 °C and 25 °C, and “ambient temperature” should not exceed 30 °C [5]. Despite these clear recommendations, there is a paucity of data on actual storage temperatures in veterinary facilities in Nigeria. The Federal Capital Territory (FCT), with its tropical climate and daytime temperatures that can soar above 40 °C in the dry season [6–8], presents a particularly challenging environment for maintaining drug integrity.This study was therefore designed to investigate drug storage temperatures in veterinary clinics that provide both clinical and pharmaceutical dispensing services across the FCT and to determine their level of compliance with manufacturers’ and NAFDAC’s recommended storage temperatures. The findings will provide crucial baseline data to inform regulatory oversight and improve clinical practices, ultimately safeguarding animal and public health.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted in the Federal Capital Territory (FCT), Abuja, Nigeria, located in the guinea savannah zone of North Central Nigeria. It is situated between latitude 8° 56’ N and longitude 7° 5’ E. The FCT has a tropical climate with distinct wet (April-October) and dry (November-March) seasons. Dry season daytime temperatures frequently exceed 40 °C, while nighttime lows can dip to 15 °C. The rainy season sees more moderate temperatures between 28 °C and 30 °C [6–8].

Study design and sampling technique

A cross-sectional study was carried out in four Area Councils of the FCT: Abuja Municipal (AMAC), Bwari, Gwagwalada, and Kuje. These areas were selected based on preliminary inquiries indicating they harbored the highest concentration of functional veterinary clinics. A purposive sampling technique was used to recruit 23 functional veterinary clinics. These clinics routinely provided clinical veterinary services and also maintained in-house veterinary drug dispensing and retail units. No standalone veterinary retail drug shops were included in the study. For the purpose of this study, the term ‘veterinary clinic’ refers to a veterinary facility that performs dual functions of clinical service delivery and retail dispensing of veterinary pharmaceuticals. Data were therefore collected from a single category of establishment rather than from separate clinic and retail-shop facilities.

Data collection

Data collection took place from March 1st to March 15th, 2025 during the dry season. Two primary methods were used: (i) a structured questionnaire survey administered to all respondents were personnel working within veterinary clinics that maintained on-site pharmaceutical retail and dispensing services and (ii) direct temperature measurement of drug storage areas using a digital thermometer.

Questionnaire survey

A pre-tested, structured questionnaire was administered to the clinic manager or lead veterinarian at each participating clinic. The questionnaire gathered information on availability of cold storage facilities (refrigerators, cold packs, nitrogen tanks), alternative power supply (generator, inverter, solar), use of temperature monitoring devices (e.g., thermometers, data loggers) and practices during ambulatory services, including vehicle parking conditions and type of temporary drug storage used.

Temperature measurement

Temperature readings were taken three times daily (at 9.00 am, 12.00 pm and 3.00 pm) between 9 am and 3 pm, as this period corresponds to peak ambient temperature fluctuations in the FCT, thereby capturing the most critical conditions affecting drug stability.

An HTC-2 digital thermometer was placed in the main drug storage area (where non-refrigerated drugs were kept) of each clinic. Temperature monitoring was manually conducted, while data loggers recorded temperature every 30 min continuously for 24 h.

The lowest, highest, and mean temperatures for each clinic were recorded. These were then compared against the NAFDAC recommended storage limit of 30 °C for ambient storage [5].

Data analysis

Data from the questionnaires and temperature readings were entered into Microsoft Excel 7 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, and means) were generated. The analysis was performed using OpenEpi version 2.1 [9].

Ambulatory drug storage practices

During ambulatory services, a variety of temporary storage methods were used. The majority of respondents (87%, n = 20) used dedicated drug storage boxes. However, 9% used handbags, and 4% used no defined storage space. Regarding vehicle parking during farm visits, 43.5% (n = 10) parked under shade, 30.4% (n = 7) parked in “no defined space,” and 26.1% (n = 6) parked in “any available space,” which could include direct sunlight.

Discussion

This study provides the first documented evidence of drug storage temperatures in veterinary clinics with integrated pharmaceutical dispensing and retail services within Nigeria’s FCT. The findings reveal a critical disconnect between regulatory guidelines and on-the-ground practices, raising serious concerns about the quality of pharmaceuticals administered to animals in this region.

The most significant finding is that none of the 23 clinics surveyed maintained ambient storage temperatures within the NAFDAC-recommended limit of 30 °C. The consistently high mean temperatures, often exceeding 33 °C, create an environment where the chemical stability of many pharmaceuticals is compromised. This aligns with global concerns about drug degradation in tropical climates. Studies have shown that exposure to such elevated temperatures can accelerate the degradation of active pharmaceutical ingredients, leading to reduced efficacy and the potential formation of toxic by-products [10, 11]. The stability of a pharmaceutical product is only guaranteed when it is stored within its predetermined range throughout its shelf life [12].

The lack of temperature monitoring infrastructure is a major contributing factor. With 60.9% of clinics lacking any monitoring device, clinicians are operating blindly, unaware of the suboptimal conditions to which their drugs are exposed. This is a worse finding compared to a study by Chukwu and Adibe [13], who reported a lower percentage (50.4%) of facilities with poor performance. Simply possessing a refrigerator does not guarantee drug integrity, especially if the surrounding ambient temperature is high and power failures are common. While 60.9% of clinics had an alternative power supply—an improvement over the 11.7% reported by Shah et al. [14] in Pakistan and the 43.6% reported by Chukwu and Adibe [13] in Abuja (Nigeria) —it is insufficient to mitigate the overall high ambient temperatures within the storage rooms themselves.

The ambulatory practices further compound the risk. Although 87% of veterinarians used drug storage boxes, the thermal protection these boxes offer is highly dependent on the ambient conditions they are exposed to. Parking vehicles in direct sunlight, as practiced by over a quarter of respondents, could turn a vehicle’s interior into a “hot box,” rapidly raising the temperature inside any uninsulated storage container far above the already high external ambient temperature. This is consistent with observations by Ondrak et al. [1], (United States of America), who highlighted the extreme temperature variations within large animal veterinary practice vehicles in the summer, finding that temperatures frequently exceeded the 25 °C and 30 °C limits common on drug labels.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small (n = 23) and limited to four Area Councils, which may affect the generalizability of the findings to the entire FCT and Nigeria as a whole. Second, the use of purposive sampling may introduce selection bias, as only accessible and willing clinics were included. Third, temperature measurements were conducted over a two-week period, which may not fully capture seasonal variations in storage conditions. Finally, the study assessed storage conditions but did not directly measure drug potency or degradation, which would provide stronger evidence of clinical impact.

The implications of these findings are revealing, as these practices can lead to therapeutic failure, prolonged animal suffering, increased costs for livestock owners, and the potential for unobserved toxicity. In the context of antimicrobials, sub-potent drugs could also contribute to the development of antimicrobial resistance, a significant One Health concern [15].

Conclusion and recommendations

This study concluded that 60.9% of veterinary clinics in the FCT are currently not compliant with manufacturers’ and NAFDAC’s recommended drug storage temperatures. The pervasive lack of temperature monitoring and the high ambient storage environment pose a significant threat to drug quality and, consequently, reduced treatment efficacy and safety.

Based on these findings, the following recommendations became relevant:

Mandatory Temperature Monitoring: The Veterinary Council of Nigeria (VCN) and NAFDAC should mandate the installation and monitoring of calibrated thermometers or data loggers in the drug storage areas of all registered veterinary premises, as recommended in the NAFDAC Good Distribution Practice guidelines [5], and should use such gathered data for decision making.

Mandatory Temperature Monitoring: The Veterinary Council of Nigeria (VCN) and NAFDAC should mandate the installation and monitoring of calibrated thermometers or data loggers in the drug storage areas of all registered veterinary premises, as recommended in the NAFDAC Good Distribution Practice guidelines [5], and should use such gathered data for decision making.

Infrastructure Improvement: Veterinary clinics should invest in low-cost climate control solutions (e.g. cross ventilation of practise premises, efficient air conditioning, use of passive cooling techniques like ceiling insulation and installation of shade structures outside the clinic) for their storage areas and ensure reliable backup power supply to protect the cold chain for vaccines and other sensitive biologics.

Development of Best Practice Guidelines for Ambulatory Care: Specific guidelines should be developed and monitored by NAFDAC and VCN for drug transport by animal health professionals, including the use of validated, insulated cool boxes with ice packs and mandatory shaded parking during farm visits.
