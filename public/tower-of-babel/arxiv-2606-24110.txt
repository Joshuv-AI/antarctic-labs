Asymptotics of the Average Stack-Sorting Depth
Jerry Zhang

Abstract
. Let 𝒟 n \mathcal{D}_{n} denote the average number of passes of the stack-sorting map s s required to sort a permutation in S n S_{n} . We use the recently introduced framework of stack-sorting diagrams and tableaux to prove that the limit lim n → ∞ 𝒟 n / n \lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{D}_{n}/n exists. This resolves a longstanding conjecture of West originally proposed in 1990 1990 . As a consequence, we also provide a monotonically increasing sequence that converges to lim n → ∞ 𝒟 n / n \lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{D}_{n}/n , improving upon Defant’s lower bound of λ ≈ 0.62433 \lambda\approx 0.62433 .

Introduction

West
[
2
]
introduced the stack-sorting map
s
in 1990 as a deterministic procedure on permutations. The input permutation
\pi
is read from left to right, and at each step, the next entry of
\pi
is pushed into the stack if either the stack is empty or the top element of the stack is greater than the next entry. If the next entry of
\pi
cannot be pushed into the stack, then the top element of the stack is popped out and appended to the output permutation. The procedure concludes when all entries have been appended to the output permutation. Figure
1
illustrates the map on
\pi=1324
. Note that each application of
s
sends the greatest unsorted entry to its correct position. Thus, at most
n-1
applications of
s
are required to sort a permutation with
n
elements. The
stack-sorting depth

\langle\pi\rangle
is the minimum number of passes required to sort
\pi
.

Define the average stack-sorting depth over
S_{n}
to be

\mathcal{D}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\langle\pi\rangle.

West
[
2
]
provided the first asymptotic lower bound for
\mathcal{D}_{n}
with a pattern avoidance argument, and he conjectured that
\mathcal{D}_{n}/n
converges as
n
approaches infinity.

Theorem 1.1

(West)
.

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{D}_{n}}{n}\geq 0.23.

In 2020, Defant
[
1
]
improved upon West’s lower bound and proved the first nontrivial upper bound.

Theorem 1.2

(Defant)
.

\lambda\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{D}_{n}}{n}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{D}_{n}}{n}\leq\frac{3}{5}(7-8\ln 2)

where
\lambda
is the Golomb–Dickman constant.

Our main result is a proof of the convergence of
\mathcal{D}_{n}/n
as
n
approaches infinity.

Theorem 1.3
.

The limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{D}_{n}/n
exists.

Let
S_{n}^{\prime}
be the set
\{\rho 0:\rho\in S_{n}\}
, where
\rho 0
denotes the concatenation of
\rho
and
0
, and define the quantity

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\rho\in S_{n}}\langle\rho 0\rangle.

Defant
[
1
]
showed that the difference between
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n}/n
and
\mathcal{D}_{n}/n
goes to
0
as
n
approaches infinity.

Proposition 1.4

(Defant
[
1
, Proposition 2.9]
)
.

We have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n}}{n}-\frac{\mathcal{D}_{n}}{n}\right)=0.

Thus, a proof of the convergence of
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n}/n
is sufficient for
Theorem

1.3
. As a natural consequence of the proof of
Theorem

1.3
, we also obtain a means to compute lower bounds arbitrarily close to
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{D}_{n}/n
.

Corollary 1.5
.

(\mathcal{D}_{n-1}^{\prime}/n)_{n=2}^{\infty}
is a monotonically increasing sequence, which converges to
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{D}_{n}/n
.

The current author has computed
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n-1}/n
up to
n=38
, which yields the bound

0.724182\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{D}_{n}}{n}\leq\frac{3}{5}(7-8\ln 2).

2.
Preliminaries

We follow the notation and conventions of
[
3
]
with some minor discrepancies.

Basic notation

A
composition
of
n
, written
\alpha\models n
, is a tuple
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})
of positive integers summing to
n
. The
length
and
width
of
\alpha
are denoted by
\ell(\alpha):=k
and
w(\alpha):=\max_{i\in[k]}\alpha_{i}
respectively. The
composition diagram
of
\alpha
is the set

D(\alpha):=\{(i,j)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}:i\leq\alpha_{j}\}

along with the partial order relation
\geq_{D}
satisfying

(i,j)\geq_{D}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\iff i\leq i^{\prime},\ j\leq j^{\prime}.

A
linear extension
of
(D(\alpha),\geq_{D})
is a bijection
f:[n]\to D(\alpha)
such that
f(x)\geq_{D}f(y)
implies
x\geq y
for all
x,y\in[n]
. The set of all linear extensions of
D(\alpha)
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}(D(\alpha))
.

Stack-sorting diagrams and tableaux

For every permutation
\pi=\rho 0\in S_{n}^{\prime}
, we assign two coordinates
\col_{\pi}(i)
and
\row_{\pi}(i)
to each entry
i\in[n]
. The column
\col_{\pi}(i)
is the unique integer for which
i
is sent to the right of
0
when
s
is applied to
s^{\col_{\pi}(i)-1}(\pi)
. If
\col_{\pi}(i)=1
, then the row
\row_{\pi}(i)
denotes the position of
i
amongst the right-to-left maxima of
\rho
. Otherwise,
\row_{\pi}(i)
is defined recursively by the equation

\row_{\pi}(i)=\row_{\pi}(\leftof_{\pi}(i)),

where
\leftof_{\pi}(i)
is the first element of
s^{\col_{\pi}(i)-2}(\pi)
to the right of
i
such that

\col_{\pi}(\leftof_{\pi}(i))=\col_{\pi}(i)-1.

The existence of
\col_{\pi}(i)
,
\row_{\pi}(i)
, and
\leftof_{\pi}(i)
are proven in
[
3
]
.

Remark 2.1
.

The general motivation for this framework is the observation that each element
i\in[n]
is sent to the right of
0
with iterative applications of
s
on
\pi
, and the permutation is sorted precisely when every element has been sent to the right of
0
. The column
\col_{\pi}(i)
tracks the iteration in which
i
is sent to the right of
0
, while
\row_{\pi}(i)
is responsible for tracking the relative position of
i
with respect to elements of the current and previous column.

We now introduce the stack-sorting diagram and the stack-sorting tableau of
\pi\in S_{n}^{\prime}
. Let the composition
\alpha_{\pi}\models n
be defined such that the
j
th part of
\alpha_{\pi}
is

(\alpha_{\pi})_{j}:=|\{i\in[n]:\row_{\pi}(i)=j\}|.

We refer to
D(\alpha_{\pi})
as the
stack-sorting diagram
of
\pi
, and the
stack-sorting tableau
of
\pi
is the map
T_{\pi}:[n]\to D(\alpha_{\pi})
satisfying

T_{\pi}(i):=(\col_{\pi}(i),\row_{\pi}(i))

for all
i\in[n]
.

Hook length formula for stack-sorting

For every composition
\alpha\models n
and cell
(i,j)\in D(\alpha)
, let
u_{\alpha}(i,j)
denote

\max(\{j^{\prime}\in[j-1]:\alpha_{j^{\prime}}\geq i-1\}\cup\{0\}),

and define the
hook length
function
h_{\alpha}:\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\mathbb{N}
such that

h_{\alpha}(i,j):=\bigl|\{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in D(\alpha):i^{\prime}\leq i-1,u_{\alpha}(i,j)<j^{\prime}\leq j\}\bigr|

if
i>1
and
h_{\alpha}(i,j):=1
if
i=1
for all
(i,j)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
. In words, if
i>1
, then
h_{\alpha}(i,j)
is the number of cells between columns
1
and
i-1
and rows
u_{\alpha}(i,j)+1
and
j
.

Theorem 2.5

(Zhang
[
3
, Corollary 4.11]
)
.

For all
\alpha\models n
, the number of permutations
\pi\in S_{n}^{\prime}
satisfying
\alpha_{\pi}=\alpha
is

N(\alpha):=|\mathcal{L}(D(\alpha))|\cdot\prod_{(i,j)\in D(\alpha)}h_{\alpha}(i,j).

Theorem

2.5
is arguably the most powerful property of stack-sorting diagrams. Two natural consequences are

\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\alpha\models n}N(\alpha)=1

and

\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\alpha\models n}N(\alpha)\,w(\alpha)=\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}.

Corners

For every composition
\alpha\models n
, an
inner corner
of
D(\alpha)
is a minimal element of
(D(\alpha),\geq_{D})
. Let
\inn(D(\alpha))
denote the set of all inner corners of
D(\alpha)
, and for all
c=(\alpha_{j},j)\in\inn(D(\alpha))
, define
\alpha^{-c}
to be the composition obtained by decrementing
\alpha_{j}
by one and removing part
j
if it becomes zero. Likewise, for every composition
\beta\models n-1
, an
outer corner
of
D(\beta)
is a cell
c\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\setminus D(\beta)
such that there exists a composition
\beta^{+c}\models n
for which
c
is an inner corner of
D(\beta^{+c})
and
\beta=(\beta^{+c})^{-c}
. Let
\out(D(\beta))
denote the set of all outer corners of
D(\beta)
.

3.
Proof of the Main Result

In this section, we prove
Theorem

1.3
and
Corollary

1.5
by understanding
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}
in the language of stack-sorting diagrams. We begin by proving some recursive identities about
N(\alpha)
and
h_{\alpha}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all
\alpha\models n
and
c\in\inn(D(\alpha))
, it holds that

\prod_{(i,j)\in D(\alpha)}h_{\alpha}(i,j)\;=\;h_{\alpha}(c)\,\prod_{(i,j)\in D(\alpha^{-c})}h_{\alpha^{-c}}(i,j).

Proof.

It suffices to prove
h_{\alpha}(i,j)=h_{\alpha^{-c}}(i,j)
for all
(i,j)\in D(\alpha^{-c})
. Now, note that

\{j^{\prime}\in[j-1]:\alpha_{j^{\prime}}\geq i-1\}=\{j^{\prime}\in[j-1]:\alpha^{-c}_{j^{\prime}}\geq i-1\}

and

u_{\alpha}(i,j)=u_{\alpha^{-c}}(i,j)

because
c
is a minimal element of
D(\alpha)
and a difference between the two sets would imply
(i,j)\leq_{D}c
. It subsequently follows that

c\notin\{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in D(\alpha):i^{\prime}\leq i-1,u_{\alpha}(i,j)<j^{\prime}\leq j\},

so
h_{\alpha}(i,j)=h_{\alpha^{-c}}(i,j)
as desired.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For all
\alpha\models n
, it holds that

N(\alpha)=\sum_{c\,\in\,\inn(D(\alpha))}N(\alpha^{-c})\,h_{\alpha}(c).

Proof.

By casework on the assignment of the least element, it is well known that the number of linear extensions of a poset
(D(\alpha),\geq_{D})
can be decomposed into

|\mathcal{L}(D(\alpha))|=\sum_{c\in\inn(D(\alpha))}|\mathcal{L}(D(\alpha^{-c}))|.

Multiplying both sides by
\prod_{(i,j)\in D(\alpha)}h_{\alpha}(i,j)
and applying
Lemma

3.1
, we obtain the identity

\displaystyle N(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\inn(D(\alpha))}\left(|\mathcal{L}(D(\alpha^{-c}))|\cdot\prod_{(i,j)\in D(\alpha^{-c})}h_{\alpha^{-c}}(i,j)\right)\cdot h_{\alpha}(c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\inn(D(\alpha))}N(\alpha^{-c})\,h_{\alpha}(c).\qed

Definition 3.3
.

For every composition
\beta\models n
, let
j_{\max}(\beta)
denote the greatest index

j
for which
\beta_{j}=w(\beta)
, and define

t(\beta):=\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\beta)}\beta_{j}=\bigl|\{(i,j)\in D(\beta)\mid j\leq j_{\max}(\beta)\}\bigr|.

Note that
t(\beta)
counts the cells of
D(\beta)
up to and including its last longest row.

Lemma 3.4
.

For all
\beta\models n-1
, it holds that

\sum_{c\,\in\,\out(D(\beta))}h_{\beta^{+c}}(c)=n

and

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\,\in\,\out(D(\beta))\\
w(\beta^{+c})=w(\beta)+1\end{subarray}}h_{\beta^{+c}}(c)=t(\beta).

Proof.

Let
(j_{t})_{t=1}^{m}
denote the sequence of weak right-to-left maxima of
\beta
, and set
j_{0}:=0
. The outer corners of
D(\beta)
are precisely
c_{t}:=(\beta_{j_{t}}+1,j_{t})
for
t\in[m]
and
c_{m+1}:=(1,\ell(\beta)+1)
. Now, note that for all
t\in[m]

u_{\beta^{+c_{t}}}(\beta_{j_{t}}+1,j_{t})=\max\left(\{j^{\prime}\in[j_{t}-1]:\beta_{j^{\prime}}\geq\beta_{j_{t}}\}\cup\{0\}\right)=j_{t-1}

by the very definition of
(j_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
, so it follows that

\displaystyle h_{\beta^{+c_{t}}}(c_{t})

\displaystyle=|\{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in D(\beta^{+c_{t}}):i^{\prime}\leq\beta_{j_{t}},\;j_{t-1}<j^{\prime}\leq j_{t}\}|

\displaystyle=|\{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in D(\beta):j_{t-1}<j^{\prime}\leq j_{t}\}|.

The intervals
\{(j_{t-1},j_{t}]\}_{t=1}^{m}
partition
[\ell(\beta)]
. Thus, summing
h_{\beta^{+c_{t}}}(c_{t})
over
t\in[m]
yields
n-1
, and adding
h_{\beta^{+c_{m+1}}}(c_{m+1})=1
gives the final sum of
n
.

For the second identity, observe that the condition
w(\beta^{+c_{t}})=w(\beta)+1
is equivalent to
\beta_{j_{t}}=w(\beta)
, so if we let
j_{p}:=j_{\max}(\beta)
, then the expression simplifies to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\,\in\,\out(D(\beta))\\
w(\beta^{+c})=w(\beta)+1\end{subarray}}h_{\beta^{+c}}(c)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\,\in\,\out(D(\beta))\\
t\in[p]\end{subarray}}h_{\beta^{+c_{t}}}(c_{t}).

The intervals
\{(j_{t-1},j_{t}]\}_{t=1}^{p}
partition
[j_{\max}(\beta)]
, and again, summing
h_{\beta^{+c_{t}}}(c_{t})
over
t\in[p]
yields
t(\beta)
.
∎

With
Lemma

3.2
and
Lemma

3.4
, we express the difference
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}-\mathcal{D}_{n-1}^{\prime}
cleanly in terms of
N(\beta)
and
t(\beta)
for
\beta\models n-1
.

Theorem 3.5
.

For all
n\geq 2
,

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n}=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n-1}+\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\beta\models n-1}N(\beta)\,t(\beta).

Proof.

First, by
Lemma

3.2
, we deduce

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\alpha\models n}N(\alpha)\,w(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\models n\\
c\,\in\,\inn(D(\alpha))\end{subarray}}N(\alpha^{-c})\,h_{\alpha}(c)\,w(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\models n-1\\
c\in\out(D(\beta))\end{subarray}}N(\beta)\,h_{\beta^{+c}}(c)\,w(\beta^{+c}).

Now observe that
w(\beta^{+c})
is either
w(\beta)
or
w(\beta)+1
, so by casework and invoking
Lemma

3.4
, the expression further reduces to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\beta\models n-1}N(\beta)\left(w(\beta)\sum_{c\in\out(D(\beta))}h_{\beta^{+c}}(c)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\,\in\,\out(D(\beta))\\
w(\beta^{+c})=w(\beta)+1\end{subarray}}h_{\beta^{+c}}(c)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\beta\models n-1}N(\beta)\bigl(w(\beta)\,n+t(\beta)\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n-1}+\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\beta\models n-1}N(\beta)\,t(\beta).

∎

Lemma 3.6
.

The sequence
(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n-1}/n)_{n=2}^{\infty}
is monotonically increasing.

Proof.

Since all parts of
\beta
are positive,
t(\beta)=\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\beta)}\beta_{j}\geq\beta_{j_{\max}(\beta)}=w(\beta)
for all
\beta\models n-1
. Substituting this inequality into
Theorem

3.5
yields

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n}\;\geq\;\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n-1}+\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\beta\models n-1}N(\beta)\,w(\beta)=\;\frac{n+1}{n}\,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n-1}

for all
n\geq 2
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

By
Lemma

3.6
,
(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n-1}/n)_{n=2}^{\infty}
is a monotonically increasing sequence bounded above by
1
, so the limit

L^{*}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{D}_{n-1}^{\prime}}{n}

exists. It shortly follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n}}{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n+1}{n}\cdot\frac{\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}}{n+1}=L^{*}

and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{D}_{n}}{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n}}{n}+\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{\mathcal{D}_{n}}{n}-\frac{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n}}{n}\right)=L^{*}.

∎

4.1
.
