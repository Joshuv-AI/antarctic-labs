Clapeyron-type theorems in nonlinear elasticity
Yury Grabovsky, Lev Truskinovsky

Abstract
Clapeyron’s Theorem of classical linear elasticity provides a way to explicitly express the energy stored in an equilibrium configuration in terms of the work of the forces applied on the boundary. We derive several new integral relations which can be viewed as nonlinear analogs of this classical result, reinterpreting them as rather general statements within Calculus of Variations. These relations reflect specific properties of Lagrangians, that we call “partial variational symmetries”, since they are more general than classical variational symmetries. In the framework of nonlinear elasticity, partial variational symmetries, such as scale invariance, or scaling homogeneity, lead, via Noether’s analysis to different nonlinear generalizations of Clapeyron’s Theorem that combine naturally the work of physical and configurational forces. We present a series of illuminating examples showing the effectiveness of the obtained general results in different problems of nonlinear elasticity.

W(\bm{x},\lambda\bm{y},\lambda\bm{F})=\lambda^{p}W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F}),\quad\forall\bm{x}\in\Omega,\ \bm{y}\not=0,\ \bm{F}\not=0,

(3.1)

for some
p\not=0
. Property (
3.1
) is more natural than
(
2.39
), and admits heterogeneous structures made of different

p
-homogeneous materials.
In this case the variation

\widetilde{\bm{x}}=\bm{x},\qquad\widetilde{\bm{y}}=e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}

(3.2)

results in
\widetilde{\bm{y}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})=e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}(\bm{x})=e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})
, so that

E[\widetilde{\bm{y}}](\epsilon)=\int_{\Omega}W(\widetilde{\bm{x}},e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}),e^{\epsilon}\nabla\bm{y}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}))d\widetilde{\bm{x}}=e^{p\epsilon}E[\bm{y}].

We have now
\delta\bm{x}=0
,
\delta\bm{y}=\bm{y}
which leads to our first
generalization of Clapeyron’s theorem.

Theorem
3.1
.

Suppose that
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is a Lipschitz extremal of
(
2.1
). If the energy density function satisfies
(
3.1
), then

E[\bm{y}]=\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}\,dS.

(3.3)

in the sense of traces.

We remark that in nonlinear elasticity the deformation
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is
an orientation preserving diffeomorphism mapping the reference
configuration
\Omega
into the deformed configuration
\Omega^{*}
. We
recall that in this context the Cauchy stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{y})
is
related to the Piola stress tensor
\bm{P}(\bm{x})
by

\bm{\sigma}(\bm{y}(\bm{x}))\bm{N}\,dS^{*}=\bm{P}(\bm{x})\bm{n}\,dS,

where
\bm{N}
is the outward unit normal to
\partial\Omega^{*}
at
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
,
while
\bm{n}
is the outward unit normal to
\partial\Omega
at

\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega
. Hence, in application to nonlinear elasticity, the

p
-homogeneous Clapeyron theorem can also be written
entirely in terms of Eulerian coordinates

E[\bm{y}]=\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\int_{\partial\Omega^{*}}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{y})\bm{N}\cdot\bm{y}\,dS^{*}.

(3.4)

This means that
p
-homogeneity of the energy density ensures static
determinacy, where the total elastic energy depends only on the current state
of stress and not on the underlying displacements or
material moduli.

The classical CT (
1.1
) is obtained from
Theorem
3.1
, applied to the linear elastic energy density
(
1.2
). The absense of inner variations
\delta\bm{x}=0
, according to (
3.2
), makes
formula (
3.3
) valid for all extremals, including the ones that
are not stationary in the sense of Definition
2.4
.
Also, since
\delta\bm{x}=0
the relation
(
3.3
) remains valid even when the Lagrangian
W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})
as a function of
\bm{x}
is only
measurable and bounded.

3.2
Generalized Clapeyron Theorem (GCT)

We are now in the position to state a simple but general result to which we can refer
as the Generalized Clapeyron Theorem (GCT) because it holds for any
Lipschitz stationary extremal of an arbitrary energy functional of the form
(
2.1
). The corresponding theorem will be the source of all subsequent
results.

Theorem
3.2
.

Suppose
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is a Lipschitz stationary extremal of the energy functional
(
2.1
). Then

E[\bm{y}]=-\frac{1}{n}\int_{\Omega}\{W_{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{x}+W_{\bm{y}}\cdot\bm{y}(\bm{x})\}d\bm{x}+\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}+\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS,

(3.5)

where
\bm{P}\bm{n}
and
\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}
in the surface integral are
understood in the sense of traces, as in the Noether formula
(
2.31
). The term
-W_{\bm{y}}
in the volume integral term has the meaning of externally applied bulk forces, while
-W_{\bm{x}}
describes external configurational bulk forces.

Proof.

Consider a class of scaling transformations of the form

\widetilde{\bm{x}}=e^{\epsilon}\bm{x},\quad\widetilde{\bm{y}}=e^{\epsilon}\bm{y},

(3.6)

where
\delta\bm{x}=\bm{x}
,
\delta\bm{y}=\bm{y}
, and

\widetilde{\bm{y}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})=e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}(\bm{x})=e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}(e^{-\epsilon}\widetilde{\bm{x}})
. For
our general class of functionals (
2.1
) we compute

\delta E=\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}W(e^{\epsilon}\bm{x},e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))e^{n\epsilon}d\bm{x}\right|_{\epsilon=0}=nE[\bm{y}]+\int_{\Omega}\{W_{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{x}+W_{\bm{y}}\cdot\bm{y}(\bm{x})\}d\bm{x}.

(3.7)

Since
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is a Lipschitz stationary extremal, all
assumptions of Theorem
2.5
are satisfied, and
therefore, the generalized Noether’s formula (
2.31
) is valid.
Then, the representation (
3.5
) of the elastic energy follows.
∎

3.3
Clapeyron-Eshelby theorem (CET)

Next we show how a particular partial variational symmetry,
representing scale invariance of an associated variational problem,
produces a simpler formula for the stored elastic energy of the body
in stable equilibrium in terms of physical and configurational
tractions on its boundary.
By scale invariance of the variational problem we mean that

W(\lambda\bm{x},\lambda\bm{y},\bm{F})=W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F}),\quad\forall\lambda>0,

(3.8)

which says that the simultaneous change of scale of Lagrangian and
Eulerian coordinates does not affect the energy density. We note that
if
W
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, property (
3.8
)
simply means that
W=W(\bm{F})
and does not depend on
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}

explicitly. If
W
is not required to be continuous at
\bm{x}=0
or

\bm{y}=0
, then the class of functions satisfying (
3.8
) is
much broader.

The assumption (
3.8
) says that
W
is homogeneous of
degree zero as a function of
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
, and therefore, by Euler’s
formula for
p
-homogeneous functions we have

W_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})\cdot\bm{x}+W_{\bm{y}}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})\cdot\bm{y}=0

identically. Combining this straightforward observation with GCT, Theorem
3.2
, we obtain

Theorem
3.3
.

Suppose
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is a Lipschitz stationary extremal of the functional (
2.1
).
If the energy density function satisfies (
3.8
), then

E[\bm{y}]=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}+\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS(\bm{x}),

(3.9)

where
\bm{P}\bm{n}\in L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\in L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
are understood in the sense of traces.

We refer to Theorem
3.3
as the
Clapeyron-Eshelby Theorem (CET) since formula
(
3.9
) is a generalization to
arbitrary
m
and
n
and not necessarily smooth extremals of
Eshelby’s formula in 3D elasticity theory

[
20
, Eq. (16)]
. Interestingly, this crucial result
was largely overlooked at the time, and was subsequently re-derived several times
in different forms, see for instance
[
38
, formula (2.10)]
,

[
52
, Eq. (2.5)]
,
[
45
, Eq. (38)]
). Moreover, it looks as if in the subsequent derivations the
important role of configurational tractions, so
prominent in the original Eshelby formulation, was obscured.

Observe that the first term on the right-hand side of
(
3.9
) can be rewritten as the classical work of applied forces. Indeed,
we can rewrite (
3.9
) as

E[\bm{y}]=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{u}+\bm{P}_{\rm disp}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS(\bm{x}),

(3.10)

where
\bm{P}_{\rm disp}^{*}=W\bm{I}_{n}-(\nabla\bm{u})^{T}\bm{P}
. Such rewriting makes the first
term on the right-hand side of (
3.10
) equal to the
work of tractions
\bm{P}\bm{n}
over displacements
\bm{u}=\bm{y}-\bm{x}
on the
boundary of the domain. However, the resulting Clapeyron-type relation (
3.9
) remains
fundamentally different from the classical CT
(
1.1
), where the additional term on the right-hand side of
(
3.9
) with the Eshelby’s energy-momentum tensor
\bm{P}^{*}
is absent.

In this respect we recall that while Piola stress
\bm{P}
acts on the virtual
displacements
\delta\bm{y}
of the points on the boundary of the domain in
the actual (Eulerian) space, the Eshelby stress
\bm{P}^{*}
acts on the
virtual changes of the shape of the domain
\Omega
in the reference
(Lagrangian) space. The former is usually interpreted as the action of
“physical forces”, and the latter – as the action of
“configurational forces” (see for instance,
[
41
]
). Therefore,
the two terms in the surface integral in (
2.31
) represent the
work of physical and configurational forces. While the meaning of the
work of physical forces does not require any clarifications, we show
in Section
3.8
that the work of configurational forces can
be associated with the acquisition of a pre-stress during the

formation
of the body.

Another major difference between CT and CET is that in the latter the
multiple in front of the surface integral is
1/n
instead of
1/2
, even in
3D space. As we have seen in Section
3.1
, the
coefficient 1/2 emerges in the classical CT due to the 2-homogeneity
of the quadratic energy density function of linear elasticity, while
no such homogeneity property is assumed in the more general nonlinear elastic
context of CET.

3.4
CET follows from a higher dimensional version of CT

Next we show that in some sense the
p
-homogeneous
Clapeyron-type Theorem
3.1
can be viewed as implying the CET
Theorem
3.3
. This becomes clear if we realize that at
their source, the transformations (
2.4
) treat the

(\bm{x},\bm{y})
-space
\mathfrak{X}
as a single entity.
Specifically, to see
the implied relation between CT and CET we need to go back to the description of the
problem in the extended space introduced in Section
2
.
We recall that in this space the original Lagrangian
W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})
is replaced by the

graph
-Lagrangian
{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{z},\bm{{\mathcal{F}}})
, given by (
2.25
).
Now, if
{\mathcal{W}}
does not depend explicitly on
\bm{z}
, i.e.,
W=W(\bm{F})
, then

{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{{\mathcal{F}}})
satisfies (
3.1
), with
p=n
. Therefore,
Theorem
3.1
gives

E[\bm{y}]={\mathcal{E}}[\bm{z},D]=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial D}\bm{{\mathcal{P}}}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{z}(\bm{t})dS(\bm{t}).

Using formula (
2.29
) for
\bm{{\mathcal{P}}}
we obtain

E[\bm{y}]=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial D}\{\bm{P}^{*}(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{y}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{t})))\mathrm{cof}(\nabla_{\bm{t}}\bm{\eta}(\bm{t}))\bm{\nu}(\bm{t})\cdot\bm{\eta}(\bm{t})+\\
\bm{P}(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{y}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{t})))\mathrm{cof}(\nabla_{\bm{t}}\bm{\eta}(\bm{t}))\bm{\nu}(\bm{t})\cdot\bm{y}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{t}))\}dS(\bm{t}),

where
\bm{\nu}(\bm{t})
is the outward unit normal to
\partial D
. Changing variables in
the above surface integral, using the relation

\mathrm{cof}(\nabla\bm{\eta}(\bm{t}))\bm{\nu}(\bm{t})dS(\bm{t})=\bm{n}(\bm{x})dS(\bm{x}),

we obtain the CET represented by formula (
3.9
). We
emphasize that the defining commonality of all versions of Clapeyron’s Theorem is that they express the elastic energy of a stable configuration in terms of boundary tractions, meaning both physical and configurational tractions.

3.5
CET for incompressible materials

We now show that the relation (
3.9
) can be generalized to cover the case of scale-free energy with scale-free pointwise constraints. A typical example is incompressible elasticity, where every deformation
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
must satisfy the incompressibility constraint

\det\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})=1

(3.11)

for almost all
\bm{x}\in\Omega
. Specifically, one can prove the following theorem:

Theorem
3.4
.

Suppose that the Lipschitz deformation
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
minimizes the
energy (
2.1
) and satisfies the
k
pointwise constraints

C^{i}(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))=0,\quad i=1,\ldots,k,

(3.12)

where both functions
W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})
and
C^{i}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})
are
scale-free in the sense of (
3.8
). Then,

n\int_{\Omega}W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\nabla\bm{y})d\bm{x}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{P}_{L}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}+\bm{P}^{*}_{L}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS,

(3.13)

where the right-hand side is understood in the sense of traces, and where

\bm{P}_{L}
and
\bm{P}^{*}_{L}
denote the Piola and Eshelby tensors corresponding to the Lagrangian

L(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})=W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})-\bm{\Lambda}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F}).

Proof.

From the general theory of constrained variational problems we know
that the energy minimizers must be stationary extremals of the
augmented energy functional

\widetilde{E}[\widehat{\bm{y}}]=\int_{\Omega}\{W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\nabla\bm{y})-\bm{\Lambda}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\nabla\bm{y})\}d\bm{x},

where
\hat{\bm{y}}=[\bm{y};\bm{\Lambda}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m+k}
. While both the energy
density
W
and the constraints
\bm{C}
are scale-free in the sense of
(
3.8
), the augmented Lagrangian

\widetilde{L}(\bm{x},\widehat{\bm{y}},\widehat{\bm{F}})=W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})-\bm{\Lambda}\cdot\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F}),\quad\widehat{\bm{F}}=[\bm{F};\bm{G}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+k)\times n}

is not, as a function of
\bm{x},\widehat{\bm{y}},\widehat{\bm{F}}
, because it is affine in
\bm{\Lambda}
.
Therefore we can apply Theorem
2.5
to the functional

\widetilde{E}[\widehat{\bm{y}}]
and the variation

\bm{X}=e^{\epsilon}\bm{x},\quad\widehat{\bm{Y}}=[e^{\epsilon}\bm{y};\bm{\Lambda}].

We compute

\widetilde{E}_{\epsilon}[\widehat{\bm{Y}}]=\int_{e^{\epsilon}\Omega}\widetilde{L}(\bm{X},\widehat{\bm{Y}}_{\epsilon}(\bm{X}),\nabla\widehat{\bm{Y}}_{\epsilon}(\bm{X}))d\bm{X},

which, after the change of variables
\widetilde{\bm{x}}=e^{\epsilon}\bm{x}
, becomes

\widetilde{E}_{\epsilon}=\epsilon^{n\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}\{W(e^{\epsilon}\bm{x},e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))-\bm{\Lambda}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{C}(e^{\epsilon}\bm{x},e^{\epsilon}\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))\}d\bm{x}.

and since both
W
and
\bm{C}
satisfy (
3.8
), we have

\widetilde{E}_{\epsilon}=\epsilon^{n\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}\{W(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))-\bm{\Lambda}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))\}d\bm{x}=\epsilon^{n\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}Wd\bm{x},

where we took into account that
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
satisfies the constraints

\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))=0
, as an extremal for
\widetilde{E}[\widehat{\bm{y}}]
.
Therefore,

\delta\widetilde{E}=n\int_{\Omega}W(\bm{x},\bm{y}(\bm{x}),\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))d\bm{x},

and Noether’s formula (
2.31
) becomes (
3.13
),
since

\delta\bm{x}=\bm{x}
,
\delta\widehat{\bm{y}}=[\bm{y};0]
, while

\bm{P}_{\widetilde{L}}=[\bm{P}_{L};0],\quad\bm{P}_{\widetilde{L}}^{*}=\bm{P}^{*}_{L}.

∎

Applying this theorem to the incompressible elasticity constraint
(
3.11
) and energy density
W(\bm{F})
that does not depend on

\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
explicitly, we obtain, noting that both the energy
density and the constraint are scale-free,

\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{(\bm{P}-p(\bm{x})\mathrm{cof}\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}(\bm{x})+(\bm{P}^{*}+p(\bm{x})\bm{I}_{n})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS,

(3.14)

where, following convention, we have denoted the Lagrange multiplier
\Lambda(\bm{x})
by
p(\bm{x})
to indicate its physical meaning as pressure, and took into account that

\bm{P}^{*}_{L}=\bm{P}^{*}-p(\bm{x})\left[(\det\bm{F}-1)\bm{I}_{n}-\bm{F}^{T}\mathrm{cof}(\bm{F})\right]=\bm{P}^{*}+p(\bm{x})\bm{I}_{n}.

Note that when
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is an orientation preserving diffeomorphism, we can change variables
\bm{y}=\bm{y}(\bm{x})
in the first term on the right-hand side of (
3.14
) and obtain

\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\bm{y})d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega^{*}}(\bm{\sigma}-p(\bm{y})\bm{I}_{n})\bm{N}\cdot\bm{y}\,dS^{*}(\bm{y})+\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{P}^{*}+p(\bm{x})\bm{I}_{n})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\,dS(\bm{x}),

(3.15)

where
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{y})
is the Cauchy stress tensor,
\bm{N}(\bm{y})
is the outward unit normal to
\partial\Omega^{*}=y(\partial\Omega)
, and
p(\bm{y})=p(\bm{x})
, with
\bm{y}=\bm{y}(\bm{x})
.

3.6
Shifting of the origin

Next we mention a straightforward result that will prove useful in what follows.

Remark 3.5
.

When
W
does not depend explicitly on
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
, the
stationary extremals satisfy

\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}\bm{n}\,dS(\bm{x})=\bm{0},\qquad\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\,dS(\bm{x})=\bm{0}.

These relations allow one to re-write (
3.9
) as

\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\bm{y})d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{(\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n},\bm{x}-\bm{a})+(\bm{P}\bm{n},\bm{y}(\bm{x})-\bm{b})\}dS(\bm{x}),

(3.16)

where
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are arbitrary constant
vectors.

Formula (
3.16
) expresses a physically obvious fact
that when the energy density function does not depend on
\bm{x}
and

\bm{y}
explicitly, the choice of the coordinate system origins in both the reference and
the deformed configurations can be made arbitrarily and independently.

3.7
Various facets of CET in linear elasticity

To illustrate the obtained general formulas it is instructive to
present the classical Clapeyron’s Theorem (
1.1
) in relation to
CET (
3.9
).
Consider first a general linear elastic energy density (
1.2
),
where
\bm{\varepsilon}=(\bm{F}+\bm{F}^{T})/2
.
When the body is also homogeneous, meaning that the tensor of elastic
moduli
\mathsf{C}
is constant, the energy density function (
1.2
)
is both 2-homogeneous and scale-free in the sense of (
3.1
),
with
p=2
, and (
3.8
), respectively. In this case one can
also show that all equilibrium solutions are smooth and therefore, by
the Noether relation (
2.16
), also stationary. Hence, in
addition to (
3.3
) that has the form (
1.1
) in the
linearly elastic context, formula
(
3.9
) is also applicable. Combining (
3.3
) and
(
3.9
) permits us to give an alternative expression for the
energy in terms of the configurational stresses

E[\bm{y}]=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n-p}\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\,dS,

(3.17)

if
n\not=p
, and

\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\,dS=0,

(3.18)

if
n=p
.
In relation to three-dimensional linear elasticity, where
p=2
, and

n=3
formula (
3.17
) is very different from the classical
CT (
1.1
), in contrast to which formula (
3.17
) involves the skew-symmetric part
w(\bm{u})
of
\nabla\bm{u}
,

w(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\bm{u}-(\nabla\bm{u})^{T}),

representing the infinitesimal rotations.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left\langle\mathsf{C}e(\bm{u}),e(\bm{u})\right\rangle d\bm{x}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\bm{\sigma},e(\bm{u})\right\rangle\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}-\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\cdot(e(\bm{u})+w(\bm{u}))\bm{x}\right\}dS(\bm{x}).

(3.19)

When
n=2
we don’t have a new alternative formula for the
energy. Instead, formula (
3.18
) gives an (apparently new)
integral relation satisfied on the boundary by the antisymmetric part
of the gradient of an equilibrium solution:

\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\cdot w(\bm{u})\bm{x}\,dS=\int_{\partial\Omega}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\bm{\sigma},e(\bm{u})\right\rangle(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})-\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\cdot e(\bm{u})\bm{x}\right\}dS.

(3.20)

In general, one can eliminate the elastic energy from formulas
(
1.1
) and (
3.9
) and obtain the analogue of
(
3.20
) in any number of dimensions:

\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\cdot w(\bm{u})\bm{x}\,dS=\int_{\partial\Omega}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\bm{\sigma},e(\bm{u})\right\rangle(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})-\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\cdot e(\bm{u})\bm{x}-\frac{n-2}{2}\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{u}\right\}dS.

(3.21)

Since (
3.21
) holds in any subdomain occupied by the elastic
body, we can rewrite (
3.21
) in differential form

\nabla\cdot(\bm{\sigma}w(\bm{u})\bm{x})=\nabla\cdot\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\bm{\sigma},e(\bm{u})\right\rangle\bm{x}-\bm{\sigma}e(\bm{u})\bm{x}-\frac{n-2}{2}\bm{\sigma}\bm{u}\right),

(3.22)

giving the differential equation for
w(\bm{u})
in terms of
e(\bm{u})
. In
two space dimension equation (
3.22
) simplifies with the right-hand side depending only on
e(\bm{u})
:

\nabla\cdot(\bm{\sigma}\nabla\bm{u}\bm{x})=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot(\left\langle\bm{\sigma},e(\bm{u})\right\rangle\bm{x}).

To the best of our knowledge, formulas
(
3.19
)–(
3.22
) are new. It is likely that they
did not appear in the literature on linear elasticity due to the
rather striking appearance in them of the skew-symmetric part

w(\bm{u})
of the gradient
\nabla\bm{u}
.

3.8
Physical meaning of
\bm{P}^{*}
loading

In our main Theorem
3.3
we encounter two important tensor fields
\bm{P}
and
\bm{P}^{*}
. As we have already mentioned, while the physical meaning of Piola stress
\bm{P}
is well known, the interpretation of the Eshelby stress
\bm{P}^{*}
is more subtle. To elucidate the fundamental difference between the work done by
\bm{P}
and
\bm{P}^{*}
we present in this section
two illustrative examples focusing on the loading effected exclusively by configurational forces.

Consider first a one-dimensional body, given in Lagrangian coordinates as an interval
[0,L]
.
The stored energy is

Our second example illustrates the fact that an elastic body may
have a nonzero energy even if it is not loaded externally by
“physical” surface tractions in the sense that the boundary condition

\bm{P}\bm{n}=0,\quad\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega

is imposed. According to GCT, this would imply
that the body is still loaded by the “configurational” surface
tractions
\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}
. The idea is to construct
a nontrivial stationary extremal of the functional

E[\bm{u}]=\int_{\Omega}W(\bm{x},e(\bm{u}))d\bm{x},

defined on the domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
; the notation for linear elastic strain
e(\bm{u})
was defined in (
1.3
). To this end it will sufficient to consider the simplest linearly elastic quadratic energy density of the form

W(\bm{x},\bm{\varepsilon})=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\bm{\varepsilon}-\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\bm{x})|^{2},

(3.28)

where
\bm{\varepsilon}
is a symmetric
n\times n
matrix, and the inhomogeneity
\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\bm{x})
is incompatible in the sense that

{\rm Ink}[\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\bm{x})]\neq 0,

(3.29)

where

{\rm Ink}[\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})]_{ijkl}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\varepsilon_{ij}}{\partial x^{k}\partial x^{l}}-\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\varepsilon_{ik}}{\partial x^{j}\partial x^{l}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\varepsilon_{lk}}{\partial x^{i}\partial x^{j}}-\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\varepsilon_{lj}}{\partial x^{i}\partial x^{k}}.

It is clear that (
3.29
) eliminates the trivial solution

\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})=\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}
with zero energy. In fact, as we show below,
the configurational prestress can be attributed exclusively to the
incompatibility of the embedded inhomogeneity
\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\bm{x})
.

We begin with recalling the orthogonal Beltrami-Donati decomposition
(e.g.
[
23
]
) of the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}=L^{2}(\Omega;\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))

{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{E}}\oplus{\mathcal{J}},\quad{\mathcal{E}}=\{e(\bm{u}):\bm{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\},\ {\mathcal{J}}=\{\bm{\sigma}\in{\mathcal{H}}:\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}=0,\ \bm{\sigma}|_{\partial\Omega}\bm{n}=0\}.

(3.30)

In view of (
3.30
) we can write any field in
{\mathcal{H}}
as a sum of a field in
{\mathcal{E}}
and a field in
{\mathcal{J}}
. Specifically, for a generic prestrain
\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}\in{\mathcal{H}}
we can find
\bm{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\bm{\sigma}\in{\mathcal{J}}
, such that

\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\bm{x})=e(\bm{u})-\bm{\sigma}.

(3.31)

Then, it is straightforward to show that the energy minimizer in the elastic problem involving physically unloaded body is
\bm{u}(\bm{x})
, the associated residual stress is
\bm{\sigma}=e(\bm{u})-\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\bm{x})
, and the nontrivial minimum value of the elastic energy is

E[\bm{u}]=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\sigma}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

The incompatible component
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{x})
of pre-strain
\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\bm{x})
can be found by solving the system

\begin{cases}{\rm Ink}[\bm{\sigma}]=-{\rm Ink}[\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}],&\bm{x}\in\Omega\\
\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}=0,&\bm{x}\in\Omega,\\
\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}=0,&\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(3.32)

The decomposition (
3.30
) implies that the boundary value problem (
3.32
) has a unique solution on simply connected
domains, since Ink
[\bm{\varepsilon}]=0
implies
\bm{\varepsilon}=e(\bm{v})
for some
\bm{v}\in H^{1}
. Thus,
\bm{\sigma}
, and therefore the energy
E[\bm{u}]
depends only on
{\rm Ink}[\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}]
. In fact, it is straightforward to show that

\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}=W\bm{n}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\bm{\sigma}|^{2}\bm{n},\text{ on }\partial\Omega,

which suggests that our “physically unloaded” body was in fact “configurationally loaded.”

To illustrate the solutions we consider the problem with spherical symmetry. Specifically,
the goal will be to construct a nontrivial radial stationary extremal of the functional

E[\bm{u}]=\int_{B(0,R)}W(\bm{x},e(\bm{u}))d\bm{x},

(3.33)

3.28

We assume further that

\bm{P}\bm{n}=0,\quad|\bm{x}|=R.

To ensure both spherical symmetry and scale invariance we choose

in the form

\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\bm{x})=a\widehat{\bm{x}}\otimes\widehat{\bm{x}},\quad\widehat{\bm{x}}=\frac{\bm{x}}{|\bm{x}|}.

(3.35)

It is then straightforward to check that

{\rm Ink}[\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}]_{ijkl}(\bm{x})=\frac{2a}{r^{2}}(\hat{x}^{l}\hat{x}^{k}\delta_{ij}-\hat{x}^{i}\hat{x}^{k}\delta_{lj}-\hat{x}^{l}\hat{x}^{j}\delta_{ik}+\hat{x}^{i}\hat{x}^{j}\delta_{lk}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}-\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}),

(3.36)

except when

{\rm Ink}[\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}](\bm{x})
is a
multiple of a Dirac delta-function; the latter condition means
that there is a local displacement field in

\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}

3.35

({\rm Ink}[\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}]\bm{\xi})_{ij}={\rm Ink}[\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}]_{ijkl}\xi^{kl},

{\rm Ink}[\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}]\bm{\xi}=\frac{2a}{r^{2}}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}-\mathrm{Tr}\,(\widetilde{\bm{\xi}})\widetilde{\bm{I}}\right),\quad\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}=\widetilde{\bm{I}}\bm{\xi}\widetilde{\bm{I}},\quad\widetilde{\bm{I}}=\bm{I}-\widehat{\bm{x}}\otimes\widehat{\bm{x}},\quad n>2,

\begin{cases}\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{u})+\Delta\bm{u}=2\nabla\cdot\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\bm{x}),&\bm{x}\in\Omega,\\

To obtain such a minimizer explicitly, when

We will look for a radial solution

Since

Such loading can be thought of as the factor ensuring the embedding of the incompatibility Ink

compute the energy by applying
Theorem

E[\bm{u}]=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\,dS=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}W(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{F})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\,dS.

(3.46)

Here we used the fact that in our example “physical” tractions were absent and therefore

Note also that Theorem

E[\bm{u}]=\frac{R}{n}\int_{\partial B(0,R)}W(\bm{x},\nabla\bm{y})dS=\frac{R|\partial B(0,R)|}{2n}\left((\eta^{\prime}(R)-a)^{2}+(n-1)\frac{\eta(R)^{2}}{R^{2}}\right).

\eta(r)=\frac{a}{n}\left(r+(n-1)r\ln\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)\right),

we then obtain the expression for the energy

E[\bm{u}]=\frac{a^{2}(n-1)}{2n^{2}}|B(0,R)|,

which naturally agrees with the direct
computation of the stored elastic energy in (

reiterate that the classical Clapeyron Theorem does not account for
such “work of creation” of a residually stressed elastic body.
The reason for the failure of the classical Clapeyron’s Theorem in the presence of incompatibility is that it breaks the homogeneity of degree 2 required for this classical theorem to hold.

Using Theorem
3.3
we can generalize our example to illustrate additional aspects of the configurational loading by the generalized tractions
\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}
.
We now only assume that there exists a general “inelastic strain”
\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}(\widehat{\bm{x}})
, such that the energy density

satisfies, for any

for some

Note next that if there exists a Lipschitz stationary equilibrium

4
Applications of CET in nonlinear elasticity

In this section we present a few examples of the
effectiveness of CET in various challenging problems of nonlinear
elasticity rooted in the Calculus of Variations. We
remark that while formula (
2.31
) holds for any Lipschitz
stationary configuration, the quantities
\bm{P}\bm{n}
and
\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}
,
understood in the sense of traces cannot be understood pointwise,
i.e., as smooth functions of
\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})
, which is not well-defined on

\partial\Omega
, in general. In practice, the discontinuities of

\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})
are confined to closed nowhere dense sets, like phase
boundaries or shock waves, that have either no intersection with

\partial\Omega
or intersect
\partial\Omega
over a closed, subset of
\partial\Omega
of zero
surface measure. For these configurations
\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))
and

\bm{P}^{*}(\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))
can be interpreted pointwise on
\partial\Omega
.
Hence, throughout this section we will assume that
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is a
Lipschitz stationary extremal in
\Omega
that is sufficiently regular on
a neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
, so that
\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is well-defined on
\partial\Omega
,
at least as an
L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
function.

4.1
Necessary conditions of metastability

The problem of metastability is central for nonlinear elasticity
theory and, more generally, for vectorial variational problems with
nonconvex energy densities. In our special setting, it concerns the
possibility of existence of strong local minima that are not automatically global minima.
To analyze this problem we can take advantage of the fact that CET
gives a formula for the energy in terms of the boundary values of
the solution of the Euler-Lagrange equations and its gradient. The
idea is to consider the difference of CET representations of energies of two
competing stationary states and rearrange the terms advantageously.

As our subsequent analysis shows, a helpful hint is to isolate in the boundary integral an expression involving a Weierstrass excess function

{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F},\bm{G})=W(\bm{G})-W(\bm{F})-\left\langle W_{\bm{F}}(\bm{F}),\bm{G}-\bm{F}\right\rangle.

(4.1)

This function has proven useful in the Calculus of Variations for
comparing the energies of two different stationary configurations with
the same Dirchlet data
[
44
,
90
,
27
]
. The result of the proposed rearrangement can be formulated in the form of a theorem which relies essentially on the CET.

Theorem
4.1
.

Assume that the Lipschitz deformations
\bm{y}_{1}(\bm{x})
and
\bm{y}_{2}(\bm{x})
are
stationary extremals for the energy functional

E[\bm{y}]=\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x}))d\bm{x}.

(4.2)

Suppose that
\bm{y}_{1}(\bm{x})
and
\bm{y}_{2}(\bm{x})
are of class
C^{1}
near
\partial\Omega
and that

\bm{y}_{1}(\bm{x})=\bm{y}_{2}(\bm{x})=\bm{y}_{0}(\bm{x}),\quad\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega.

(4.3)

Then

\int_{\Omega}\{W(\bm{F}_{2})-W(\bm{F}_{1})\}d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{1},\bm{F}_{2})(\bm{x},\bm{n})+(\bm{P}_{1}-\bm{P}_{2})\bm{n}\cdot(\bm{F}_{2}\bm{x}-\bm{y}_{0})\}dS(\bm{x}),

(4.4)

where

\bm{F}_{j}=\nabla\bm{y}_{j}(\bm{x}),\qquad\bm{P}_{j}=W_{\bm{F}}(\nabla\bm{y}_{j}(\bm{x})),\qquad j=1,2.

Proof.

Since the energy density is scale-free in the sense of
(
3.8
), we can use the GCT Theorem
3.3
, and write

\int_{\Omega}W(\bm{F}_{i})d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{P}_{i}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}_{0}+\bm{P}_{i}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}dS,

Subtracting these two equations and expanding
\bm{P}^{*}_{j}
via (
2.11
) we obtain

\int_{\Omega}\{W(\bm{F}_{2})-W(\bm{F}_{1})\}d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{F}_{1},\bm{F}_{2})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}+(\bm{P}_{1}-\bm{P}_{2})\bm{n}\cdot(\bm{F}_{2}\bm{x}-\bm{y}_{0})+\\
\left\langle\bm{P}_{1},\bm{F}_{2}-\bm{F}_{1}\right\rangle\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}+\bm{P}_{1}\bm{n}\cdot(\bm{F}_{1}-\bm{F}_{2})\bm{x}\}dS.

Since
\bm{y}_{1}
and
\bm{y}_{2}
are of class
C^{1}
near
\partial\Omega
and

\bm{y}_{1}-\bm{y}_{2}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, we conclude that there exists a
continuous vector field
\bm{a}:\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, such that

\bm{F}_{1}-\bm{F}_{2}=\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n}.

(4.5)

Therefore,

4.2
Quasiconvex envelope

We recall that the quasiconvex envelope of the energy density
W(\bm{F})
is the largest quasiconvex function that does not exceed

. It is denoted

and is often referred to as quasiconvexification of

]

QW(\bm{F})=\inf_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\By|_{\Md D}=\BF\Bx}{\By\in W^{1,\infty}(D;\bb{R}^{m})}}\displaystyle\frac{1}{|D|}\int_{D}W(\nabla\bm{y})d\bm{x},

(4.12)

where

can be any open and bounded set, with
|\partial D|=0
.
The rank-one convex envelope

is the largest rank-one convex
function
5
5

5

i.e.,function convex along all straight lines connecting two
points that differ by a rank-one matrix.
that does not exceed
W
. It was shown in
[
65
]
that every quasiconvex function is rank-one convex and therefore,
RW(\bm{F})\geq QW(\bm{F})
. The question whether
QW(\bm{F})=RW(\bm{F})
has been answered in the negative in
[
88
]
, except in the case

. Nonetheless nontrivial examples where

are extremely rare, see for instance
[
26
]
.

While, in many cases the variational problem (
4.12
) has no solutions,
there could be particular domains

, where Lipschitz minimizers do
exist
[
36
]
.
Or next theorem shows how the CET can
furnish somewhat minimal additional conditions that stationary
extremals of (
4.2
) should satisfy to be minimizers in
(
4.12
). Furthermore, the CET permits us to
transfer the knowledge about quasiconvexity from a single point to the
entire range of
\nabla\bm{y}
, provided that rank-one convexity at the values of
\nabla\bm{y}
on the boundary can be verified.

Theorem
4.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a star-shaped domain with Lipschitz boundary. Assume that

(BVP)

For a given
\bm{F}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
the function
\overline{\bm{y}}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
solves

\nabla\cdot\bm{P}(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}})=\bm{0},\quad\nabla\cdot\bm{P}^{*}(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}})=\bm{0},

(4.13)

in the sense of distributions, and satisfies
\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})=\bm{F}_{0}\bm{x}
for all
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega
;

(REG)

\overline{\bm{y}}
is of class

near
\partial\Omega
;

(RCX)

W(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}))=RW(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}))
for all
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega

(Q=R)

Then

(i)

\displaystyle RW(\bm{F}_{0})=QW(\bm{F}_{0})=\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}})d\bm{x}
, i.e.
\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})
is the global minimizer in (
4.12
).

(ii)

QW(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}))=W(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}))
for a.e.
\bm{x}\in\Omega
.

Proof.

Assumption (

) implies that the GCT is applicable to the
stationary equilibrium
\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})
of (
4.12
), and we obtain, by

for some

, and therefore,

\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}})d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}(W(\bm{F}_{0}+\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n})-\bm{P}(\bm{F}_{0}+\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{a})(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})dS.

(4.14)

Next, we appeal to
[
32
, Lemma 4.2]
.
We quote the part of the lemma needed for the proof.

Lemma
4.4
.

Let

be a rank-one convex function such that

. Let

{\mathcal{A}}_{V}=\{\bm{F}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:W(\bm{F}_{0})=V(\bm{F}_{0})\}.

Then for every
\bm{F}_{*}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{V}
,
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\bm{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

V(\bm{F}_{*}+\bm{b}\otimes\bm{m})\geq W(\bm{F}_{*})+\bm{P}(\bm{F}_{*})\bm{m}\cdot\bm{b}.

(4.15)

We now apply Lemma
4.4
by choosing

,

\bm{F}_{*}=\nabla\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})=\bm{F}_{0}+\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n}
, and
\bm{b}\otimes\bm{m}=-\bm{a}(\bm{x})\otimes\bm{n}
,
for each
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega
. The
assumption (

) implies that Lemma

is applicable, and therefore,

RW(\bm{F}_{0})\geq W(\bm{F}_{0}+\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n})-\bm{P}(\bm{F}_{0}+\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{a}.

(4.16)

Finally, we use the assumption that
\Omega
is star-shaped. If we choose the

is always

. Therefore, inequality (

together with the identity (

) delivered by the GCT,

Since

is the minimal value of the functional in (

By the assumption (

is the global minimizer in (

Proof.

(4.25)

We complete the proof by the application of the Clapeyron theorem:

\bm{P}\bm{n}=w_{1}\widehat{\bm{x}},\quad\bm{P}^{*}=w\bm{I}_{n}-\eta^{\prime}w_{1}\widehat{\bm{x}}\otimes\widehat{\bm{x}}+w_{2}\frac{\eta}{r}(\bm{I}_{n}-\widehat{\bm{x}}\otimes\widehat{\bm{x}})

Thus, according to (
3.9
),

\int_{B(0,R)}W(\nabla\overline{\bm{y}})d\bm{x}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial B(0,R)}\left\{w_{1}\widehat{\bm{x}}\cdot\eta\widehat{\bm{x}}+(w-\eta^{\prime}w_{1})\widehat{\bm{x}}\cdot R\widehat{\bm{x}}\right\}dS.

We conclude that

QW\left(\frac{\eta(R)}{R}\bm{I}_{n}\right)=w_{1}(R)\frac{\eta(R)}{R}+w(R)-\eta^{\prime}(R)w_{1}(R).

The theorem is now proved.
∎

5
Conclusions

In this paper we revisited the classical Clapeyron’s Theorem (CT)
(
1.1
) of the linear elasticity theory
which expresses the energy of an equilibrium configuration in terms of
the work of physical forces on the boundary. We interpreted this
engineering result in the framework of Noether formula (
2.31
) for general
variation of the graph of a stationary extremal.
This new perspective allowed us to derive various nonlinear analogs
of the CT by revealing their links with partial
symmetries of the corresponding Lagrangians, such a
p
-homogeneity
and scale invariance. The resulting formulas express bulk energy in terms of
the generalized surface work that includes not only physical but also
configurational forces.

An important mechanical aspect of our approach is the possibility
to differentiate between the part of the elastic energy stored due to
the action of applied physical loads and the “cold work type” part
of the energy emerging due to accumulation of elastic
incompatibility. We showed further that when scale invariance is
combined with
p
-homogeneity, the equilibrium energy can be expressed
through the work of either only physical or only configurational
forces, except when
p=n
, in which case the work of configurational
forces is always zero.

Our paper reveals that while the classical CT
and the CET (Theorem
3.3
) are
independent relations, coming from different variational
symmetries, (
3.6
) and (
3.2
), both can be derived
from Theorem
3.1
. One of them directly, as a quadratic
function of the strain, the other, via
reformulating the nonparametric variational functional
(
2.1
) as a parametric one, which is always
n
-homogeneous.

More generally, the proposed broader reading of the CT allowed us to link it to various seemingly unrelated
results in nonlinear elasticity and the possibility to express the
minimal value of the equilibrium energy through the Weierstrass excess
function. Moreover, we provided compelling evidence that the
CET can open new ways of addressing important problems of
material stability, in particular, regarding the possibility to
rule out the presence of strong local minimizers that are not global
in in the case of hard device loading. Of significant interest are
the potential applications of the CET to elastodynamics where it
naturally accounts for the inevitable formation of shock waves. We
deal with some aspects of these particular applications in

[
28
]
, accounting for the fact that extremals are not
necessarily stationary extremals of the action functional, in which
case CET become a global inequality.

It is also important to mention that since the concept of a partial
variational symmetry is rather general, Noether’s formula used to
obtain various nonlinear versions of the classical CT
applies to a broad class of variational problems not at all related
to nonlinear elasticity theory. For instance, the proposed approach
can offer a unified interpretation to various Rellich and Pohozaev
type identities which have proved useful in several
reaction-diffusion type theories. These issues will be addressed in a
companion paper
[
30
]
, where the CET is applied to problems
involving semi-linear equations, including p-Laplacian and its various generalizations. Finally, we show in
[
29
]
how the CET can be used in important engineering problems related to fracture and cavitation.
