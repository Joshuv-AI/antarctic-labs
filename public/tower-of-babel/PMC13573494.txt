“A lifeline to belonging”: exploring caregiver’s perspectives on quality participation in Easter Seals’ adapted physical activity and parasport programs
Authors: Sofia J. So, Maggie Locke, Raven Ghazzawi, Amanda Higgins, Hannah Bowman, Shanon Phelan, Joanne Bernard, Sarah A. Moore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInclusive adapted physical activity and parasport programs support physical and psychosocial well-being of children experiencing disability. Easter Seals offers opportunities for children of all abilities to engage in physical activity through introductory parasports.PurposeOur evaluation aimed to understand caregiver’s perspectives on quality participation in Easter Seals programs.MethodsWe collected quantitative and qualitative data through surveys followed by semi-structured interviews to further explore survey data findings. Survey data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and interviews using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsSurveys highlighted caregiver-perceived participant satisfaction and a caregiver-perceived positive impact of programs on participants’ physical and psychosocial well-being. Five themes were generated: (1) caregiver support and dedication, (2) meaningful participation, (3) mastery of skills, (4) thriving alongside peers, and (5) hopes for the future.ConclusionFindings illustrate the importance of thoughtfully designed programs, focused on quality participation, for children experiencing disability and their families.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07191-w.

Background

Physical activity provides universal physical and psychosocial benefits for children of all ages and abilities [1–3]. According to the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health framework, participation in meaningful sport and recreation is an important contributor to overall childhood growth and development [4, 5]. Participation in out-of-school physical activities (e.g., sports, recreation) has been linked to positive outcomes for children, regardless of disability status [6, 7]. These benefits are often attributed to opportunities for children to build physical and social skills, form friendships, and boost self-esteem and confidence [8]. However, children experiencing disability often face challenges accessing inclusive sport and recreation programs. Limited adapted opportunities and facilitators’ lack of familiarity with useful adaptations further contribute to the exclusion of children experiencing disability from meaningful participation in sports and recreation [6, 7, 9, 10]. To address these barriers, there is a need to improve access to quality adapted physical activity and parasport programs, which support healthy development and lifelong well-being [11].

All children have the right to play and participate in quality sport and recreation [12, 13]. Quality participation is particularly important for children experiencing disability and cannot be assumed simply because a program is accessible or labeled “inclusive”. [14] The Canadian Disability Participation Project outlines six building blocks of quality participation: (1) belonging, (2) autonomy, (3) mastery, (4) challenge, (5) engagement, and (6) meaning [14]. These elements are the foundation for positive, supportive, and enjoyable adapted physical activity and parasport experiences. Quality participation is achieved by centering the values, goals, and needs of individuals and their families in programming decisions [14]. When prioritized, adapted physical activity and parasport has the potential to be meaningful and provide long-lasting benefits for participants [14]. Similarly, Therapeutic Recreation (TR) principles, such as person-centered and strengths-based approaches further support participants by fostering independence, skill-building, and a sense of belonging [15, 16]. When quality participation and TR principles are combined, these approaches offer a comprehensive framework enhancing participation for children experiencing disability [14, 16, 17].

Context

In Nova Scotia, Canada, one in five children experience disability, yet only few programs offer inclusive physical activity opportunities [18, 19]. Under the Nova Scotia Accessibility Act, the province aims to become barrier-free by 2030, with standards being developed for adapted and inclusive programming [20]. The overarching goal of these initiatives is to ensure equitable access and participation in physical activity, sport, and play for individuals of all abilities. Easter Seals Nova Scotia, a non-profit organization, offers services and programs to support individuals experiencing disability. Their Take PART (Physically Accessible Recreation Today) initiative offers a series of inclusive sport and recreation programs for children and youth ages 5–18. Learn to Wheel is an introductory wheelchair basketball program where participants learn fundamental and sport-specific skills. Learn to Dance is an introductory dance program that incorporates all movement forms including the use of any mobility aids (e.g., wheelchairs, walkers, etc.). This initiative also includes Learn to Sledge, an introductory sledge hockey program, and Learn to Ride which is a therapeutic horse-back riding program. Learn to Wheel, Dance, and Sledge run for 10 weekly sessions in the fall and winter months, while Learn to Ride is delivered over 10 weekly sessions in the summer. Designed to be inclusive for all individuals across all ability levels, these programs aim to foster skill development, peer connection, and enjoyment through sport and recreation.

Recently, The Take PART programs have shifted to intentionally incorporate quality participation and TR principles; however, these efforts have not yet been formally evaluated. This evaluation explored caregivers’ perspectives on quality participation in the programs. Findings of this evaluation may enhance future programming and support program participants and their families.

Methods

Program evaluations provide a means to assess strengths and limitations of programs [21]. Timely evaluation can support organizations to continually improve their programs through regular participant feedback and evaluating programs can assess the effectiveness of programs and approaches used [21]. Engaging families in program evaluations can foster collaboration and promote sense of ownership in families on program outcomes which can increase overall commitment [22]. Importantly, evaluation hears directly from the community and can help to understand whether programs are personally meaningful to the community, in this case, participants and their families [23]. This evaluation study aimed to understand perceived participant satisfaction with the four Take PART programs, explore program effectiveness, and obtain feedback for future improvement of the programs. This was done by engaging participants’ caregivers to gain their perspectives on their child’s participation and understand their children’s unique needs through a survey and follow-up interviews. For the purpose of this evaluation, a caregiver was defined as any parent, legal guardian, or family member providing primary care to the participant.

An exploratory sequential mixed methods design was used to address the following questions: (1) To what extent does Take PART’s mission, vision core values, and foundations align with participant and family experiences?, (2) What are some of the important features of Take PART programs?, (3) What are the perceived benefits and barriers of Take PART programs?, and (4) What are suggestions to improve quality experience and participation in Take PART programs? This design was selected because it allows for a comprehensive evaluation of the program; initial quantitative data provides a broad assessment of caregiver-perceived participant satisfaction and general outcomes, while the subsequent qualitative data offers in-depth context to explain why these outcomes occurred and how the programs are experienced [24]. By collecting and analyzing quantitative data initially, followed by qualitative interviews, we were able to directly expand on the initial quantitative results to generate practical, actionable insights for program improvement.

Surveys

In collaboration with Easter Seals Nova Scotia, a caregiver survey (see supplemental file 1) was developed to gather data on participant and program demographics, motivators and barriers to participation, perceived benefits and challenges, and general feedback. To ensure content validity, the survey questions were grounded in the quality participation framework developed by the Canadian Disability Participation Project (CDPP) [14]. Additionally, the questionnaire was pilot-tested internally by the research team to evaluation question clarity, flow, and estimated completion time. The survey (via Microsoft Forms or hard copies) was distributed to caregivers of all Take PART participants from the last two years. Descriptive statistics (e.g., count, mean, standard deviation, range) were calculated for quantitative questions. Open-ended responses were summarized to capture feedback and suggestions. Survey findings informed the development of the qualitative interview guide.

Interviews

Following surveys, caregivers were invited to participate in one-on-one semi-structured interviews (see supplemental file 2), regardless of survey completion. Interviews were conducted via secure Microsoft Teams, averaging 60 min. Guided by survey findings, the interview guide was developed using a constructivist lens and the QP framework [14]. Questions explored program foundations, features, benefits, barriers, and improvement suggestions.

Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and de-identified to ensure accuracy and support data familiarization. A second member reviewed transcripts for verification. The team engaged in reflexive thematic analysis reading transcripts, taking notes, and inductively coding data using NVivo (Release 1.7.1., QSR International) [25, 26]. Codes were developed without applying pre-existing frameworks, and themes were collaboratively identified, reviewed, and refined. Throughout data collection and analysis, the team maintained reflexive journals and engaged in ongoing dialogue to consider positionality and its influence on interpretation [26].

We invited all caregivers of children who participated in any Take PART program over the past two years to participate in survey and interviews. Easter Seals facilitated recruitment by circulating emails to families participating in programs. A total of 20 caregivers agreed to participate and complete the survey. Survey results

Motivators & barriers to participation

As detailed in Table 2, the survey results indicated that the primary motivators for enrolling children in programs were opportunities for physical activity (70.0%), socialization with peers (50.0%), and interest in sports (50.0%). Notably, nearly half of respondents (45.0%) indicated that Take PART was their only option for parasport, highlighting the critical role this programming plays in the community. As for barriers to participation, although weather (45%) and location (25%) were noted, caregivers described willingness to travel long distances, with one caregiver driving up to 170 km to attend sessions (range: 5–170 km; mean: 41.6 ± 48.8 km). Fatigue and weather also impacted attendance, but caregivers were committed to overcoming these challenges. For instance, one respondent shared in the survey that they “make the time and day work” and that they believe that the “programs are wonderful and worth any travel required”.

Perceived program benefits

Survey results also indicate the variety of physical, psychosocial, and cognitive benefits of the program for children. As detailed in Table 3, improvements in strength, coordination, and mobility were commonly observed. For example, one caregiver noted “an improvement in her mobility” through the Learn to Dance program. Caregivers also believed that the program increased mental stimulation and concentration and reduced development of adverse emotions. Among the benefits of the program, positive feelings about the psychosocial benefits that the program provided to participants was most notable. Some psychosocial benefits included providing participants with increased sense of community, feelings of belonging, self-esteem, and social skills. One caregiver highlighted how the Learn to Wheel program “helps [their] son interact with other children”, while others appreciated the relationships their children formed with peers and volunteers.

Program improvement suggestions

Caregivers provided recommendations through open-ended survey responses, emphasizing the importance of continuing one-on-one support, providing developmentally appropriate activities, and offering more accessible program times and locations. Additionally, 78% of respondents were interested in other parasports, with suggestions including swimming, curling, and hockey. Caregivers also proposed incorporating a wider range of activities such as hip hop or ballet and updating games to maintain engagement.

Interview findings

Theme 1: caregiver support & dedication

Interview data reinforced the survey findings, highlighting caregivers’ significant efforts to support participation. Several caregivers adjusted their work schedules or relocated to access inclusive programs. One caregiver shared that “Easter Seals is the reason why [they] moved to Halifax because in Cape Breton there’s limited inclusive programs” (Caregiver G). Caregivers went out of their way to ensure that their children could have access to inclusive programming as it was important to them and their child.

Caregivers addressed logistical barriers such as transportation and scheduling to ensure their child could regularly attend programs. For example, caregiver C mentioned that they “tend to trade taking a bit of time off work to try to get [their child] to the programs”, and transportation to programs “is a big commitment but [they] do it because [their child] loves it and [they] get so much out of the program”. Their willingness to make sacrifices was vital to enabling their children’s participation. Recognizing and valuing the contributions of caregivers is important to foster inclusive and accessible program environments that meet the diverse needs of participants.

Additionally, caregivers shared strategies to support involvement, including proactively tailoring activities with staff. Caregivers emphasized the importance of clear communication with staff and the ability to collaborate to support their child’s needs. Caregivers often collaborated with program staff to identify and implement effective strategies for addressing participants’ individual needs and challenges. Caregiver G explained “that [this] system seems to work really well” and helps their children “build on skills from one week to the next”.

There were several motivators that drove caregivers’ support and dedication to the Take PART programs. Among all, it was the enhancement of their children’s quality of life and personal development that encouraged caregivers the most. Caregiver B mentioned that “all [they] want is for [their] kids to be happy” and that “these programs provide so much happiness for [their child] specifically”. Caregivers felt fulfilled seeing their children benefit emotionally, socially, and physically. Caregiver L said that “it was so cool to see…exposure to sport” for their child. Additionally, caregivers also stated that the program served as an opportunity for their children to build meaningful connections with peers, influencing their decisions to continue registering their children in Take PART programs. For example, Caregiver I mentioned that “it was really good for [their child] to have more experience with peers and more experiences that [their child] wouldn’t have in school…”.

Theme 2: meaningful participation

This theme reflects how inclusive and dynamic program environments led to meaningful participation. One caregiver expressed the ease of having their child participate in these programs, which has not been their previous experience when accessing programs for their child. They stated that, “here with Easter Seals we just register, we show up, and everything works well” (Caregiver G). This theme also describes the various ways in which participants engaged with the programs, reflecting their individual values, preferences, and experiences.

Caregivers consistently expressed appreciation for the welcoming atmosphere and accessible facilities. One caregiver noted that “the environment that it’s in all of the facilities and locations have been, you know, inclusive, accessible to everyone” (Caregiver C). Adaptive equipment and modifications were also seamlessly integrated into program activities, allowing participants to fully engage regardless of their differences. For example, caregiver E noted that when their child is in dance programs, they notice that “other people in wheelchairs are also doing the same maneuvers as [their child] on [their] feet” and stated that “this is [their] most inclusive experience [they’ve] ever seen in all of [their] life”.

Additionally, caregivers described staff and volunteers as “providing a warm a loving environment” (Caregiver C), contributing to participants feeling accepted. Caregivers also noted that delivery and facilitation techniques helped to accommodate participants’ unique interests and abilities. Caregiver F expressed that the volunteer to participant ratio “is a huge piece in terms of being able to accommodate the needs” of program participants and that staff and volunteers “understand that the majority of the kids that would be in these programs, the goals are independence and confidence, and skill development”. Caregivers felt that participants were empowered to engage meaningfully in ways that reflected their individual preferences.

Theme 3: mastery of skills

This theme demonstrates caregiver’s perspectives of participants’ development of physical skills and abilities alongside their sense of being challenged within the Take PART programs. This theme emphasizes the impact of fostering skills acquisition, personal growth, and a sense of achievement among participants.

Caregivers described the noticeable gains in physical abilities and functional independence. Caregivers felt the significant value of program activities that are focused on building strength and coordination, while still catering to participants’ diverse needs and abilities. In fact, Caregiver F noted that their child “wouldn’t be where [they] are physically, because [they] wouldn’t be doing these activities that promote physical development”. Additionally, Caregiver H expressed that the program “helped with [their child’s] coordination and being able to manipulate [their] chair a little more”. Caregivers also noticed a sense of pride and accomplishment as they observed tangible improvements in their children’s physical abilities, which they noticed were also being transferred in other environments (e.g., school and home). Caregiver H mentioned that “[their child] did track at school. And of course, it was definitely a benefit to have [their child] have a little bit more control over the wheelchair after doing the [Learn to] Wheel program.” Similarly, Caregiver A stated that “dance has really strengthened [their child’s] legs and given [them] a lot of confidence to keep pushing [themselves] forward”. This highlights the transformative impact of the Take PART program on children’s functional independence and growth.

Caregivers also noted the important role of challenge in fostering motivation and resilience. Caregiver C stated that their child “thrives on being challenged” and when “[their child] works one on one and has that…challenge, he’s succeeding”. Similarly, Caregiver K stated that their child “feels success in something maybe that [they’re] working towards”, emphasizing the importance of thoughtful programming that supports achievement and self-efficacy.

Theme 4: thriving alongside peers

This theme illustrates the role of peer interaction and social development in participants’ program experiences. Caregivers reported improvements in communication, cooperation, and confidence. For example, Caregiver G observed that their child “speaks to a lot more people and [they’re] asking [people], ‘what’s your name?’, [their child] would never do that before”. Caregiver G also stated that the program has “opened [their child] up”. Another caregiver noted that their child learned to “[help] other children, the nonverbal children that were there” (Caregiver A). These observed improvements in social skills highlight the impact of the programs in fostering positive relationships and experiences for participants.

Additionally, caregivers expressed that program staff and volunteers facilitated many social interactions throughout programs, creating a supportive environment conducive to social skill development. Caregiver C noted that their child “bonded with a few kids” in programs and this has allowed their child to gain “confidence [which] has helped [their child] going into other activities”. Caregiver A also noted that at the “learning centre, they’ve noticed a tremendous difference” in their child’s social skills. For example, their child started talking to peers more, knowing their names, and telling them about experiences in the dance program. This highlights the importance of creating supportive environments where all children can thrive and cultivate meaningful relationships with their peers.

Caregivers also described how the programs promoted a sense of belonging. For example, Caregiver B noted that the program “promotes [their child’s] individuality” while Caregiver C expressed that it “been so good for [their child] to be able to see a variety of peers with different disabilities so that [their child] knows [they’re] not alone” which helped their child develop “a better sense of self-worth”. This emphasizes the importance of soliciting shared experiences through programs to allow children to develop a sense of belonging in their community. Caregiver H described the staff asking, “what do we think [child] would say?” to support autonomy and inclusion, adding that “it gives them all a chance to feel like they’re a part of something.” These reflections highlight how supportive environments contributed to participants’ confidence, self-esteem, and social identity.

Theme 5: hopes for the future

This theme reflects caregivers’ aspirations for continued growth and improvement of the Take PART programs. Caregivers offered suggestions to address barriers and implement recommendations to improve program outcomes (e.g., promote inclusivity, increase well-being for individuals with disabilities).

Transportation and scheduling challenges were commonly raised. Caregiver L noted the difficulty of “driv[ing] 25 minutes in around rush hour” and recommended that programs be offered “closer to the suburb areas”. Another caregiver suggested to offer programs on a “weekend or different like timing for people who can’t make those programs…when most people are at work” (Caregiver J). These changes were hoped to increase accessibility and engagement.

Moreover, caregivers also expressed a desire for increased awareness and understanding of disability-related challenges among the broader community. Caregivers suggested to offer programs that also include children without disabilities. As caregiver F said, “I think we need to look at in terms of having offerings whereby our kids that need adaptations are doing programs that also include kids or adults that don’t need adaptations”. Caregivers expressed hope that this may lead to greater inclusivity and increased social skill development for their children.

Additionally, there was strong interest in program expansion and community outreach. Caregivers offered valuable recommendations to enhance the impact of the program. Caregiver B noted that “perhaps one of the limitations may be that all of the…programming takes place in Halifax” which can be a barrier for some families that live in rural settings. Caregiver J supported this statement by expressing that it is challenging to access programs “depending on the weather to get to downtown Halifax”. Many hoped for increased offerings across Nova Scotia; as one caregiver powerfully noted that “these programs are [their child’s] lifeline to belonging” (Caregiver F). This emphasizes the importance of continuing to improve and expand these initiatives. Ultimately, recognizing and prioritizing the experiences of participants and their families is essential for guiding program development and ensuring that Take PART programs remain responsive to the diverse needs and preferences of their current and future participants.

Discussion

Children experiencing disability often face barriers to inclusive sport and recreation, which can negatively impact their health and well-being [11]. The Take PART programs aim to address these barriers by offering inclusive, adapted physical activity and parasport opportunities that emphasize quality participation. As demonstrated by caregiver feedback, these programs have fostered physical, emotional, and social growth among participants, while providing meaningful and enjoyable experiences. Caregivers’ observations support existing research, which highlights the importance of accessible programs for enhancing well-being and development in children experiencing disabilities [27–29]. Their feedback also reinforces the value of person-centered approaches and adapting programming to align with children’s interests and needs. Inclusive environments not only improve outcomes for children but foster a sense of community for families.

The incorporation of therapeutic recreation and quality participation principles appears to be an important contributor to the program’s success. Caregivers’ reflections on autonomy, belonging, challenge, and mastery align with the Canadian Disability Participation Project’s quality participation framework [14]. These findings can guide future adaptations to ensure programming remains responsive and impactful.

Essentially, the findings of this evaluation contribute to the growing body of evidence-based recommendations for inclusive programming for children experiencing disability in their communities. This is just the first step to increasing resources for developing more opportunities for children experiencing disability to be further involved in sports and recreation in their communities [28].

Recommendations and future directions

With the implementation of provincial accessibility standards, it is timely to also think about ways to design and implement quality participation in programs [14]. Easter Seals’ continued focus on one-on-one partnerships, goal setting, and therapeutic relationships will be essential. Expanding programming to reach more communities and scheduling flexibility can reduce barriers and increase reach. Moving forward, it is important to continue to involve families meaningfully in program design and evaluation to ensure that participant voices are heard. This way, we can understand how they envision inclusion in these programs which can increase a sense of support for families. As there is a lack of studies that present the experiences of children experiencing disability, evaluations like these can be done to create strategies for improving accessibility in programs that offer supports for participants and their families.

This program evaluation has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Because participation was voluntary, this inherently introduces selection bias; caregivers with greater availability or those who had particularly strong experiences, may have been more likely to participate. Furthermore, future evaluations should include the perspectives of the children themselves, which were not captured in this study. Engaging children directly through interviews or creative participatory methods could offer deeper insight into what makes programming meaningful for them. Ultimately, ongoing efforts to listen, adapt, and co-create programming will be critical for supporting lifelong participation and well-being.

Self-Reflecting Questions for Organizations & Program Facilitators to Improve Quality Participation in Programs

• In what ways are your programs co-created by participants and their families?

• In what ways are you adapting or modifying activities, tools, and environments based on individual participant needs?

• How have you created opportunities for participants to experience autonomy, choice, and direct their involvement in programs?

• How do you know that participants have engaged in meaningful participation in your programs?

• How have you supported children’s physical skill acquisition, while still using adaptations and modifications in programs?

• In what ways do your programs support children in building confidence as part of their physical skill development?

• How have you supported children in fostering personal growth and resilience in programs (e.g., sense of achievement, overcoming obstacles, testing limits)?

• How do you know that participants have experienced mastery and challenge in your programs?

• How are children in your programs experiencing a sense of identity?

• How have you created opportunities for children to thrive and cultivate meaningful relationships with peers in programs?

• In what ways do your programs support children in their confidence as part of their social skill development?

• How do the adaptations and modifications in your programs allow for children to interact with peers and develop social skills?

• In what ways do you assess whether participants experience a sense of belonging in your programs?

Conclusions

Caregivers of children in the Take PART programs reported physical and psychosocial benefits for their children, alongside tangible examples of their children experiencing quality participation through the programs (i.e., autonomy, belongingness, challenge, mastery, engagement, and meaning). While the results are intended to support quality improvement efforts at Easter Seals, the findings likely apply more broadly to organizations that offer adapted physical activity and parasport programs. Our study illustrated the importance of thoughtfully designed adapted physical activity and parasport programs, focused on quality participation, for children experiencing disability and their families.
