Abstract
. Transformers have revolutionized machine learning and deploying attention layers in the model is increasingly standard across a myriad of applications. Further, for large models, it is common to implement Low Rank Adaptation (LoRA), whereby a factorized parameterization of them is trained, to achieve a surprisingly beneficial accuracy-size trade-off. In this work, via a unified framework we rigorously establish trainability of such models under stochastic methods. We prove that for any mild regularization, the empirical regression loss on a attention layer and LoRA on a shallow neural net, both induce Poincaré inequality for the corresponding Gibbs’ measure. Then it follows via invoking recent results that a certain SDE, which mimics the SGD, minimizes the corresponding losses. In both the cases, our first-of-its-kind results of trainability on attention and nets, do not rely on any assumptions on the data or the size of the architecture.



1.
Introduction

The remarkable empirical success of attention mechanisms have fundamentally reshaped modern machine learning, most prominently through the transformer architecture – which is the backbone of Large Language Models (LLMs)
[
32
]
. By allowing models to dynamically weight interactions between “tokens”/data fragments, attention layers enable modeling of complicated distributions. Central to the attention mechanism are the query and key matrices,
{\bm{W}}_{Q}
and
{\bm{W}}_{K}
which occur in the model as the product
{\bm{W}}_{Q}{\bm{W}}_{K}
and one such pair are trained in each “attention head” of which there are many in each of the many attention layers in any commonly used transformer. Despite their central role in the practice of modern AI, our theoretical understanding remains limited of how these matrices evolve during successful training.

While modern attention mechanisms are now the standard, they originally emerged to address the bottleneck of fixed-length representations in early sequence modeling. Early sequence-to-sequence models were limited by fixed-length representations.
[
2
]
introduced attention to dynamically aggregate encoder states, allowing the decoder to focus on relevant input positions. Building on this,
[
28
]
proposed alternative attention variants, including global attention over all positions and local attention over a subset.

These developments ultimately led to the transformer architecture
[
35
]
, which removes recurrence entirely and instead models sequence interactions purely through stacked self-attention layers, where given an input
{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{t\times d}
each layer computes
\text{RowSoftMax}_{\beta}\left(\frac{{\bm{X}}{\bm{W}}_{Q}{\bm{W}}_{K}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right){\bm{X}}{\bm{W}}_{V}
, where
{\bm{W}}_{Q},{\bm{W}}_{K}
and
{\bm{W}}_{V}
are learned weight matrices where the RowSoftMax operator is defined as
[\text{RowSoftMax}_{\beta}({\bm{M}})]_{ij}\coloneqq\frac{e^{\beta M_{ij}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{t}e^{\beta M_{ik}}}
.

The transformer architecture
[
35
]
has become the dominant paradigm for sequence modeling, replacing recurrence with stacked self-attention and feed-forward layers. Numerous variants have since been proposed, namely Sparse Transformer
[
7
]
, Longformer
[
3
]
, Linformer
[
37
]
, Transformer-XL
[
10
]
, ALBERT
[
25
]
, and Vision Transformer
[
12
]
, among many others. A foundational reason explaining these successes is given in works like
[
38
]
that have show that transformers are universal approximators of sequence-to-sequence matrix functions.

The Rising Importance of Doing Regression on Transformers
The success of transformers has led to their widespread adoption in scientific machine learning, where many tasks can be naturally formulated as regression problems over high-dimensional discretizations of function spaces. In particular, applications in fluid dynamics and weather prediction — ranging from operator learning for PDEs to data-driven forecasting — often reduce to learning mappings between input and output fields by doing regression using a attention-based architecture, FourCastNet
[
31
]
, GraphCast
[
24
]
, Pangu-Weather
[
4
]
, Poseidon
[
17
]
and GenCFD
[
29
]
. This perspective motivates studying attention not only as a representation mechanism, but as a regression operator whose properties govern generalization and efficiency in continuous domains.

Q1:
With no assumptions on data or architecture, can a attention layer be trained by a stochastic algorithm?

On the other hand, the rise of large-scale pretraining has motivated parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), which constrains updates to lie in low-dimensional subspaces while preserving the pretrained backbone. More precisely, the idea is to freeze the pre-trained weight matrix
{\bm{W}}_{\rm pre}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}}
and only train a small update. This update is represented as the product of two small matrices,
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
. The modified weights
{\bm{W}}^{\prime}
are parameterized as,
{\bm{W}}^{\prime}={\bm{W}}_{\rm pre}+\frac{\alpha}{r}{\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}

where
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\text{in}}}
and
{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times r}
are the trainable factors and
\alpha
being a scaling constant. By training only
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
, we significantly reduce the number of parameters to update.

As showed in
[
34
]
, LoRA works best when applied to all weight matrices and small-to-medium datasets, and its optimal learning rate is largely independent of rank due to the
\frac{1}{r}
scaling. Overall, LoRA performs similar to full-finetuning in the “low-regret regime” making it a parameter-efficient alternative for post-training adaptation. However, the product
{\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}
introduces a specific scaling problem : we can multiply
{\bm{A}}
by a constant and divide
{\bm{B}}
by the same constant without changing the final output. This redundancy makes the training landscape “flat” in certain directions, which is seemingly a natural obstacle for gradient based algorithms to succeed. And yet LoRA has proven strikingly effective in practice, dramatically reducing memory and computational overhead while retaining performance. Thus we posit that the optimization dynamics of neural models under LoRA have remained unclear from a theoretical standpoint.

Q2:
With no assumptions on data or width, can a implementation of LoRA on a net be trained by a stochastic algorithm?

In this work,
firstly
we make progress towards uncovering hitherto unknown mathematical properties about the attention map and thus uncover a first-of-its-kind provably convergent training mechanism for the query and key matrices.
Secondly,
this work also initiates a theoretical study of training dynamics for standard neural networks under LoRA parameterization — which evidently shares a mathematical similarity of training a factorized weight parameterization as while training the key and query matrices in the attention, as introduced above.

Whether for training the key and query matrices of an attention head or for training standard nets under LoRA, we consider training through stochastic differential equations (SDEs), capturing the continuous-time limit of stochastic gradient methods commonly used in practice.
In both cases — for any number of parameters and for any data — we prove convergence of a risk function for the models under a SDE flow.

1.2.
Literature Review

A theoretical analysis of transformers in the infinite-width limit by deriving their Neural Network Gaussian Process (NNGP) and Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) equivalents was provided in
[
18
]
. They showed that self-attention layers admit well-defined kernel limit and in this regime, gradient descent training of a Transformer is equivalent to kernel regression with the corresponding NTK.

A global convergence framework for transformers by analyzing training dynamics in the mean-field limit was established in
[
13
]
. By treating model width and depth as approaching infinity, the authors demonstrate that discrete gradient descent converges to a Wasserstein gradient flow on the distribution of parameters. Albeit the use of an infinite-width limit it strictly requires the inclusion of a weight decay parameter
\lambda>0
, for the convergence proof to work.

A formal proof that transformers can converge to the functional behavior of near-optimal Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms through the optimization of a log-likelihood objective was given by
[
27
]
. By viewing the attention mechanism as an iterative optimizer, they demonstrate that supervised pre-training on offline trajectories allows the model to implement algorithms like LinUCB and Thompson Sampling directly. However, these convergence guarantees rely on non-standard architectures, most notably the use of ReLU-based attention to facilitate exact linear algebraic operations. Furthermore, the authors assume model realizability, implying that the Transformer’s capacity must be sufficient to encapsulate the expert’s decision-making logic.

A multi-layer transformer trained on
n
-gram data, where each token depends on the preceding
n
tokens, is analyzed in
[
6
]
, and it is shown that gradient flow converges to a model exhibiting induction head behavior. Specifically, induction here refers to the phenomenon whereby, if a token at position
i
matches a previous occurrence at position
j
, the model attends to the token following position
j
to predict the next token at position
i
. Their results provide a rigorous characterization of how attention layers, feed-forward networks, and normalization interact to learn features from context. This work advances prior studies that focused on linear or single-layer models by handling richer architectures and more realistic data distributions.

Fundamental algorithmic limits of Multi-Head Attention (MHA) restricted to a discrete Boolean input distribution
X\in\{\pm 1\}^{k\times d}
was established in
[
5
]
. Theorem 1.2 in
[
5
]
establishes that, under a non-degeneracy condition on the attention and projection matrices and realizability assumption of the samples, there exists an algorithm that for
m-
headed attention estimates the parameters in
(kd)^{O(m^{3})}
time, using
(kd)^{\Theta(m)}
samples, and achieves predictions that are
(kd)^{-\Omega(m)}
close to the true values in expectation. This result identifies the number of heads
m
as the dominant factor in computational scaling.

1.2.1.
Review of Existing Attempts at Provable Training of LoRA on Neural Networks — with Weight Regularization

LoRA was first introduced by
[
19
]
where it was asserted that the weight updates for task-specific adaptation in attention based models reside in a manifold of low intrinsic dimension
[
1
]
. By reparameterizing the update matrix
\Delta{\bm{W}}
as the product of two low-rank matrices
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
,
[
19
]
demonstrated that optimization can converge to high-performance solutions with significantly fewer trainable parameters. Crucially, their initialization strategy — setting one matrix to zero — ensures a stable starting point at the pre-trained state, effectively bridging the gap between training efficiency and the convergence stability typically observed in full fine-tuning.

A rigorous analysis of LoRA on neural networks in the generic non-linear regime was given by
[
21
]
, establishing a formal dichotomy between global convergence and parameter divergence. By characterizing the optimization as governed by a global Restricted Strong Convexity, they prove that the combination of zero-initialization and weight decay induces an implicit bias toward a low-rank global minimum. Crucially, they demonstrate that while the non-linear landscape may harbor spurious local minima, these points are spectrally isolated in high-rank regions and do not intersect with the stable optimization trajectory.

In the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) framework LoRA was analyzed by
[
20
]
, where the neural network’s optimization can be treated as a linearized system. In this regime, the training dynamics are governed by a quadratic objective subject to a low-rank structural constraint. Alongside a non-standard regularizer — derived from the Rademacher complexity of the low-rank bottleneck — the authors prove that the non-convex BA reparameterization does not introduce spurious local minima.
[
20
]
further demonstrates, if the rank is above a certain threshold, gradient-based methods like stochastic gradient descent (SGD) converge to a low-rank global minimizer.

Limitations of LoRA
It was demonstrated by
[
34
]
that the perceived equivalence between LoRA and full fine-tuning is an “illusion” maintained by surface-level metrics. Despite its parameter efficiency, LoRA can lead to catastrophic forgetting, thereby degrading performance in settings that require continual learning.

Mathematical Setup

In this section, we define the analytic conditions, architectures, and loss functions, alongside the core assumptions that underpin our subsequent analysis.

The Villani condition was introduced in
[
36
]
to guarantee that, when a function satisfies this condition, the associated Gibbs measure satisfies the Poincaré inequality. We recall that a distribution
\pi
is said to satisfy the Poincaré inequality for some constant
C_{PI}
, if for all smooth functions
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\mathrm{Var}_{\pi}(h)\leq C_{PI}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\norm{\grad h}^{2}]
.
[
33
]
leverage the Poincaré inequality induced by the Villani condition to establish convergence results for certain stochastic differential equations (SDEs). We now proceed to formally define the corresponding analytic conditions.

Definition 2.1

(
Confining Condition).

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is said to be confining if it satisfies the following conditions,

(1.)

f\in C^{\infty}
,

(2.)

\lim_{\|{\bm{x}}\|\to+\infty}f({\bm{x}})=+\infty
, and

(3.)

{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}e^{-\frac{2f({\bm{x}})}{s}}\differential{\vx}<\infty~\forall s>0
.

Definition 2.2

(
Villani Condition).

A confining function
f
is said to satisfy the Villani condition if for all
s>0
,
\frac{\|\nabla f({\bm{x}})\|^{2}}{s}-\Delta f({\bm{x}})\rightarrow\infty
as
\|{\bm{x}}\|\to\infty
:

In recent works
[
14
,
15
]
, it was shown that the Villani condition holds for depth-2 neural networks of arbitrary width and for both squared and logistic losses. Building on this,
[
23
]
proved that Langevin Monte Carlo consequently achieves population risk minimization. In contrast to
[
14
,
15
,
23
]
, our results do not require any lower bound on the regularization parameter.

Next we formally define the attention model and its regression loss that we choose to train.

Definition 2.3

(
Attention Layer).

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{t\times d}\ni{\bm{X}}\mapsto{\rm Attention}({\bm{X}})\coloneqq

A notable application of this framework is the Vision Transformer (ViT)

Remark 3.4

(
Necessity of Factor Regularization)
.

We note that the factorized loss function

{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{T}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}({\bm{U}}{\bm{V}}^{\top}),

where
{\bm{T}}=({\bm{U}},{\bm{V}})
, exhibits a scaling invariance under the transformation,

g_{\bm{A}}({\bm{U}},{\bm{V}})=({\bm{U}}{\bm{A}},{\bm{V}}{\bm{A}}^{-1}),\quad{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},\ \det(\mA)\neq 0,

since,

{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{U}}{\bm{A}},{\bm{V}}{\bm{A}}^{-1})={\mathcal{L}}({\bm{U}},{\bm{V}}).

So the potential
{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{T}})
is constant along the non-compact orbits generated by the general linear group.

As a consequence, the Gibbs’ measure

\mu_{\gamma}\propto e^{-\gamma{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{T}})}

is non-normalizable. Specifically, for any fixed rank-
r
matrix
{\bm{W}}_{0}\neq 0
,
{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{T}})
is constant along the orbit

\mathcal{O}=\{({\bm{U}}{\bm{A}},{\bm{V}}{\bm{A}}^{-1})\mid\det(\mA)\neq 0\}
, which extends infinitely far from the origin. Hence, the partition function

Z=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}e^{-\gamma{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{T}})}d{\bm{T}}

diverges because it includes an integral of a non-zero constant density over an infinite-volume set. Consequently, the confining condition is violated, and the Poincaré Inequality cannot hold for the unregularized factorized loss.

\text{diag}({\bm{S}}_{i})-{\bm{S}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{S}}_{i}
represents the exact covariance matrix of a categorical distribution. We have the bound

(5.3)

\displaystyle\|\text{diag}({\bm{S}}_{i})-{\bm{S}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{S}}_{i}\|_{2}\leq\|\text{diag}({\bm{S}}_{i})\|_{2}+\|{\bm{S}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{S}}_{i}\|_{2}\leq\max_{k}{\bm{S}}_{i,k}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}{\bm{S}}_{i,k}^{2}\leq 2.

In the above step, the first inequality applies the triangle inequality. The subsequent equality is exact for the spectral norm (
\|\cdot\|_{2}
): the norm of the diagonal matrix equals its maximum entry, and the norm of the rank-1 positive semi-definite matrix
{\bm{S}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{S}}_{i}
equals its trace. The final strict inequality holds because
{\bm{S}}_{i}
is a probability vector which dictates that
\max_{k}{\bm{S}}_{i,k}<1
and
\sum_{k=1}^{t}{\bm{S}}_{i,k}^{2}<1
.

We denote
\differential{\bm{M}}
and
\differential{\bm{S}}
as the first-order matrix differentials, representing an arbitrary infinitesimal perturbation in the input pre-activation matrix and the corresponding induced perturbation in the output probability matrix, respectively. For a single row
i
,
\differential{\bm{M}}_{i}
and
\differential{\bm{S}}_{i}
represent their respective row vector differentials. Since the full Jacobian is block-diagonal, the differential mapping from
\differential{\bm{M}}
to
\differential{\bm{S}}
operates independently on each row. For any individual row
i
, the Euclidean norm (2-norm) of the differential vector satisfies
\|\differential{\bm{S}}_{i}\|_{2}\leq 2\beta\|\differential{\bm{M}}_{i}\|_{2}
based on the spectral norm bound derived above. By definition, the squared Frobenius norm of a matrix is the sum of the squared 2-norms of its row vectors. Summing over all
t
rows, we obtain:

(5.4)

\displaystyle\|\differential\bm{S}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\|\differential{\bm{S}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}(2\beta)^{2}\|\differential{\bm{M}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=4\beta^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\|\differential{\bm{M}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=4\beta^{2}\|\differential\bm{M}\|_{F}^{2}

Taking the square root of both sides directly yields the global bound for the differential:

(5.5)

\displaystyle\|\differential\bm{S}\|_{F}\leq B_{s^{\prime}}\beta\|\differential\bm{M}\|_{F}

Therefore, the Jacobian norm is bounded by
\beta B_{s^{\prime}}
, where the constant is explicitly evaluated as
B_{s^{\prime}}=2
.

Hessian Bound
Differentiating the local Jacobian element
\frac{\partial{\bm{S}}_{i,j}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,k}}=\beta({\bm{S}}_{i,j}\delta_{j,k}-{\bm{S}}_{i,j}{\bm{S}}_{i,k})
with respect to another input
{\bm{M}}_{i,l}
yields an additional factor of
\beta
via the chain rule.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{S}}_{i,j}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,k}\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,l}}

\displaystyle=\beta\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{S}}_{i,j}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,l}}\delta_{j,k}-\frac{\partial{\bm{S}}_{i,j}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,l}}{\bm{S}}_{i,k}-{\bm{S}}_{i,j}\frac{\partial{\bm{S}}_{i,k}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,l}}\right)

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}\left(({\bm{S}}_{i,j}\delta_{j,l}-{\bm{S}}_{i,j}{\bm{S}}_{i,l})\delta_{j,k}-({\bm{S}}_{i,j}\delta_{j,l}-{\bm{S}}_{i,j}{\bm{S}}_{i,l}){\bm{S}}_{i,k}-{\bm{S}}_{i,j}({\bm{S}}_{i,k}\delta_{k,l}-{\bm{S}}_{i,k}{\bm{S}}_{i,l})\right)

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}{\bm{S}}_{i,j}\left(\delta_{j,k}\delta_{j,l}-{\bm{S}}_{i,l}\delta_{j,k}-\delta_{j,l}{\bm{S}}_{i,k}+{\bm{S}}_{i,l}{\bm{S}}_{i,k}-{\bm{S}}_{i,k}\delta_{k,l}+{\bm{S}}_{i,k}{\bm{S}}_{i,l}\right)

(5.6)

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}{\bm{S}}_{i,j}\left(2{\bm{S}}_{i,k}{\bm{S}}_{i,l}+\delta_{j,k}\delta_{j,l}-\delta_{k,l}{\bm{S}}_{i,k}-\delta_{j,k}{\bm{S}}_{i,l}-\delta_{j,l}{\bm{S}}_{i,k}\right)

This scalar element-wise formulation is the direct expansion of the matrix-level second derivative derived in Lemma C.1 of
[
30
]
. This expression exclusively comprises attention probability values
{\bm{S}}_{i,\cdot}\in(0,1)
and Kronecker deltas
\delta\in\{0,1\}
. By the triangle inequality, the absolute value of each individual entry in this local 3D Hessian tensor
\mathcal{H}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{t\times t\times t}
, defined as the local 3D Hessian tensor
\mathcal{H}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{t\times t\times t}
as the collection of all second-order partial derivatives of the
i
-th row of the output
{\bm{S}}
with respect to the
i
-th row of the input
{\bm{M}}
. For
i\in\{1,2,\dots,t\}
, its element at index
(j,k,l)
is defined as:

(\mathcal{H}_{i})_{j,k,l}:=\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{S}}_{i,j}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,k}\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,l}}\quad\text{for }j,k,l\in\{1,\dots,t\},

which is strictly bounded as,

(5.7)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{S}}_{i,j}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,k}\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,l}}\right|<\beta^{2}\cdot 1\cdot(2\cdot 1\cdot 1+1+1+1+1)=6\beta^{2}

For a single row
i
, the squared Frobenius norm of its local Hessian tensor
\mathcal{H}_{i}
is the sum of its
t^{3}
squared entries. We bound this local tensor norm as,

(5.8)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{H}_{i}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{j,k,l=1}^{t}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{S}}_{i,j}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,k}\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,l}}\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{j,k,l=1}^{t}(6\beta^{2})^{2}=t^{3}(36\beta^{4})\implies\|\mathcal{H}_{i}\|_{F}\leq 6\beta^{2}t^{1.5}

For mapping
\bm{S}=\text{RowSoftMax}_{\beta}(\bm{M})
, the complete global 6th-order Hessian tensor
H_{full}\in\mathbb{R}^{t\times t\times t\times t\times t\times t}
is defined as the derivative of any output entry matrix
\bm{S}_{i,j}
with respect to any two input entries
\bm{M}_{p,k}
and
\bm{M}_{q,l}
:

(5.9)

\displaystyle(H_{full})_{i,j,p,k,q,l}:=\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{S}_{i,j}}{\partial\bm{M}_{p,k}\partial\bm{M}_{q,l}}\quad\text{for }i,j,p,k,q,l\in\{1,\dots,t\}.

As discussed in Appendix C.1, Lemma C.1 of
[
30
]
, the full Hessian tensor for the entire matrix-to-matrix mapping is block-diagonal, all cross-row second derivatives evaluate to zero. We have

(5.10)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{S}_{i,j}}{\partial\bm{M}_{p,k}\partial\bm{M}_{q,l}}=\begin{cases}(\mathcal{H}_{i})_{j,k,l}&\text{if }i=p=q,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This simplifies its total squared Frobenius norm being simply the sum of the squared norms of the
t
independent row blocks. Thus, the global Hessian norm
\|H_{full}\|_{F}
,satisfies,

\displaystyle\|H_{full}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\sum_{p=1}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\sum_{q=1}^{t}\sum_{l=1}^{t}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{S}_{i,j}}{\partial\bm{M}_{p,k}\partial\bm{M}_{q,l}}\right)^{2}

(5.11)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sum_{j,k,l=1}^{t}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{S}_{i,j}}{\partial\bm{M}_{i,k}\partial\bm{M}_{i,l}}\right)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\|\mathcal{H}_{i}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}(36\beta^{4}t^{3})=36\beta^{4}t^{4}.

Taking the square root of both sides, the global Hessian norm is bounded by
6\beta^{2}t^{2}
. Therefore, the bound can be denoted as
\beta^{2}B_{s^{\prime\prime}}
, that is

(5.12)

\displaystyle\|\differential^{2}\bm{S}_{i}\|_{F}\leq\beta^{2}B_{s^{\prime\prime}}\|\differential\bm{M}_{i}\|_{F}^{2},

where the constant is explicitly evaluated as
B_{s^{\prime\prime}}=6t^{2}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

We begin by bounding the norm of the gradient
\nabla_{{\bm{T}}}\hat{R}_{A}
, where
{\bm{T}}=({\bm{W}}_{Q},{\bm{W}}_{K})
. Recall from Definitions
2.4
and
2.5
that
\hat{R}_{A}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}({\bm{T}})
. Taking gradients and applying the triangle inequality yields
\|\nabla_{{\bm{T}}}\hat{R}_{A}\|\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{{\bm{T}}}\ell_{i}({\bm{T}})\|
.
Thus, it suffices to bound
\|\nabla_{{\bm{T}}}\ell_{i}({\bm{T}})\|
, where
\|\nabla_{{\bm{T}}}\ell_{i}({\bm{T}})\|^{2}=\|\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{K}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}^{2}
.

From Definition
4.1
, recall that
{\bm{M}}_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}{\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{Q}{\bm{W}}_{K}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}
.
In equation (
5.26
), we will show that the parameter gradients
\gradient_{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}\ell_{i}
and
\gradient_{{\bm{W}}_{K}}\ell_{i}
can be expressed in terms of the intermediate gradient
\nabla_{{\bm{M}}_{i}}\ell_{i}
as

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}\ell_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}(\nabla_{{\bm{M}}_{i}}\ell_{i})\,{\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{K},

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{K}}\ell_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}(\nabla_{{\bm{M}}_{i}}\ell_{i})\,{\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{Q}.

Therefore, it suffices to bound
\|\nabla_{{\bm{M}}_{i}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}
. Next, we will express the intermediate gradient in terms of the upstream gradient
(\gradient_{{\bm{S}}_{i}}\ell_{i})
in equation (
5.20
),
\nabla_{{\bm{M}}_{i}}\ell_{i}=\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{S}}_{i}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}}\right)^{\top}\nabla_{{\bm{S}}_{i}}\ell_{i}
, where
{\bm{S}}_{i}
is defined in Definition
4.1
. In equation (
5.16
) we will show that the upstream gradient admits the form
\nabla_{{\bm{S}}_{i}}\ell_{i}={\bm{E}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{v}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}
, where
{\bm{E}}_{i}=\hat{{\bm{Y}}}_{i}-{\bm{Y}}_{i}
is the error matrix.

Thus, we proceed in four steps,

(Step 1)

Bounding the Error Matrix
(i.e. bounding
\|{\bm{E}}_{i}\|_{F}
), which in turn allows us to

(Step 2)

Bounding the Upstream Gradient
(i.e. control
\|\nabla_{{\bm{S}}_{i}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}
), then

(Step 3)

Bounding the Intermediate Gradient
(i.e.
\|\nabla_{{\bm{M}}_{i}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}
) and finally

(Step 4)

Bounding the Parameter Gradients
.

\bullet
Bounding the Error Matrix:
First using the triangle inequality and the Softmax output bound
\|{\bm{S}}_{i}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{t}
to bound
{\bm{E}}_{i}=\hat{{\bm{Y}}}_{i}-{\bm{Y}}_{i}
to obtain
\|{\bm{E}}_{i}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{t}B_{x}B_{w}+B_{y}
(see Equation
5.13
).

Recalling
{\bm{E}}_{i}=\hat{{\bm{Y}}}_{i}(T)-{\bm{Y}}_{i}
we have,

(5.13)

\displaystyle\|{\bm{E}}_{i}\|_{F}\leq\|\hat{{\bm{Y}}}_{i}\|_{F}+\|{\bm{Y}}_{i}\|_{F}\leq\|{\bm{S}}_{i}\|_{F}\|{\bm{X}}_{i}\|_{F}\|{\bm{W}}_{v}\|_{F}+B_{y}\leq\sqrt{t}B_{x}B_{w}+B_{y},

where we have used the bound of
\|{\bm{S}}_{i}\|_{F}
proved in Lemma
4.2
. Recall the other constants used in the RHS come from definitions
2.3
,
2.8
and
2.9
.

\bullet
Bounding the Upstream Gradient:
Based on the explicit expression for the loss gradient with respect to the output probability matrix,
\nabla_{{\bm{S}}_{i}}\ell_{i}={\bm{E}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{v}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}
, we apply sub-multiplicativity to bound the upstream gradient:
\|\nabla_{{\bm{S}}_{i}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}\leq(\sqrt{t}B_{x}B_{w}+B_{y})B_{w}B_{x}
(see Equation
5.17
).

Recalling
\hat{{\bm{Y}}}_{i}({\bm{T}})={\bm{S}}_{i}({\bm{T}}){\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{v}
, for the loss
\ell_{i}({\bm{T}})=\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{Y}}_{i}-\hat{{\bm{Y}}}_{i}({\bm{T}})\|_{F}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\mE_i^\top\mE_i)
, take the differential

(5.14)

\displaystyle\text{d}\ell_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}((\text{d}\bm{E}_{i})^{\top}\bm{E}_{i}+\bm{E}_{i}^{\top}\text{d}\bm{E}_{i})=\text{Tr}(\bm{E}_{i}^{\top}\text{d}\bm{E}_{i}).

Since the target
{\bm{Y}}_{i}
is a constant, the is simply
\text{d}{\bm{E}}_{i}=\text{d}\hat{{\bm{Y}}_{i}}
. Recalling
\hat{\bm{Y}}_{i}(\bm{T})=\bm{S}_{i}(\bm{T})\bm{X}_{i}\bm{W}_{v}
,
\bm{X}_{i}
and
\bm{W}_{v}
are treated as constants with respect to the pre-activations, we have
\text{d}\hat{\bm{Y}}_{i}=(\text{d}\bm{S}_{i})\bm{X}_{i}\bm{W}_{v}
.
So the differential of the loss
\ell_{i}
with respect to
{\bm{S}}_{i}
is given by:

(5.15)

\displaystyle\differential\ell_{i}=\text{Tr}({\bm{E}}_{i}^{\top}\differential{\hat{\mY}_i})=\text{Tr}({\bm{E}}_{i}^{\top}(\differential{\mS_i}){\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{v}).

Using the cyclic property of the trace, we can rearrange this as
\differential\ell_{i}=\text{Tr}({\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{v}{\bm{E}}_{i}^{\top}\differential{\mS_i})=\text{Tr}(({\bm{E}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{v}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top})^{\top}\differential{\mS_i})
. By identifying this with the standard Frobenius inner product
\differential\ell_{i}=\text{Tr}((\nabla_{{\bm{S}}_{i}}\ell_{i})^{\top}\differential{\mS_i})
, we extract the exact gradient,

(5.16)

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{S}}_{i}}\ell_{i}={\bm{E}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{v}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}.

So we have,

(5.17)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{{\bm{S}}_{i}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}=\|{\bm{E}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{v}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}\|_{F}\leq\|{\bm{E}}_{i}\|_{F}\|{\bm{W}}_{v}\|_{F}\|{\bm{X}}_{i}\|_{F}\leq(\sqrt{t}B_{x}B_{w}+B_{y})B_{w}B_{x}.

\bullet
Bounding the Intermediate Gradient:
Utilizing the exact chain rule
\nabla_{{\bm{M}}_{i}}\ell_{i}=(\frac{\partial{\bm{S}}_{i}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}})^{\top}\nabla_{{\bm{S}}_{i}}\ell_{i}
and substituting the Softmax Jacobian bound
\|(\frac{\partial{\bm{S}}_{i}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}})\|_{F}\leq\beta B_{s^{\prime}}
, we obtain the intermediate gradient bound:
\|\nabla_{{\bm{M}}_{i}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}\leq(\sqrt{t}B_{x}B_{w}+B_{y})B_{w}B_{x}\beta B_{s^{\prime}}
(see Equation
5.21
). Recall that the local Jacobian
(\frac{\partial{\bm{S}}_{i}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}})\in\mathbb{R}^{t\times t}
and its bound are defined in Lemma
4.2
.

Since the loss differential can be equivalently expressed in terms of either
\bm{M}_{i}
or
\bm{S}_{i}

(5.18)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\ell_{i}=\langle\nabla_{\bm{M}_{i}}\ell_{i},\mathrm{d}\bm{M}_{i}\rangle=\langle\nabla_{\bm{S}_{i}}\ell_{i},\mathrm{d}\bm{S}_{i}\rangle,

express this in trace form, we have

(5.19)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left((\nabla_{\bm{M}_{i}}\ell_{i})^{\top}\mathrm{d}\bm{M}_{i}\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left((\nabla_{\bm{S}_{i}}\ell_{i})^{\top}\mathrm{d}\bm{S}_{i}\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left((\nabla_{\bm{S}_{i}}\ell_{i})^{\top}(\frac{\partial{\bm{S}}_{i}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}})\mathrm{d}\bm{M}_{i}\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(((\frac{\partial{\bm{S}}_{i}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}})^{\top}\nabla_{\bm{S}_{i}}\ell_{i})^{\top}\mathrm{d}\bm{M}_{i}\right),

we can extract

(5.20)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{M}_{i}}\ell_{i}=(\frac{\partial{\bm{S}}_{i}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}})^{\top}\nabla_{\bm{S}_{i}}\ell_{i}.

Take Frobenius norm of both sides and apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

(5.21)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{{\bm{M}}_{i}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}\leq(\sqrt{t}B_{x}B_{w}+B_{y})B_{w}B_{x}\beta B_{s^{\prime}}.

\bullet
Bounding the Parameter Gradients:
By extracting the gradient with respect to the query and key weight matrices,
{\bm{W}}_{Q}
and
{\bm{W}}_{K}
, we obtain the bound of
\|\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}
and
\|\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{K}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}
using the bound of
\|\nabla_{{\bm{M}}_{i}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}
, then we substitute the intermediate bound to achieve the final bound
\|\nabla_{{\bm{T}}}\ell_{i}\|^{2}=\|\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{K}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}^{2}
, and hence the gradient squared bound (see Equation
5.30
).

Next we fix
{\bm{W}}_{k}
to analyze the partial derivative with respect to
{\bm{W}}_{Q}
. To see that first consider the change in
{\bm{M}}_{i}
due to variation in
{\bm{W}}_{Q}
,

(5.22)

\displaystyle\differential_{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}{\bm{M}}_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}{\bm{X}}_{i}(\differential{\bm{W}}_{Q}){\bm{W}}_{K}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top},

Since,

(5.23)

\displaystyle\differential\ell_{i}=\langle\nabla_{{\bm{M}}_{i}}\ell_{i},\differential_{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}{\bm{M}}_{i}\rangle=\text{Tr}((\nabla_{{\bm{M}}_{i}}\ell_{i})^{\top}\differential_{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}{\bm{M}}_{i})=\text{Tr}((\nabla_{{\bm{M}}_{i}}\ell_{i})^{\top}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}{\bm{X}}_{i}(\differential{\bm{W}}_{Q}){\bm{W}}_{K}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}),

apply the cyclic property of trace, we have

(5.24)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\ell_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\text{Tr}\left(\bm{W}_{K}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}(\nabla_{\bm{M}_{i}}\ell_{i})^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}(\mathrm{d}\bm{W}_{Q})\right).

Compare this with the differential directly expressed in terms of the gradient with respect to
{\bm{W}}_{Q}
as

(5.25)

\displaystyle\differential\ell_{i}=\langle\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}\ell_{i},\differential{\bm{W}}_{Q}\rangle=\text{Tr}\left((\nabla_{\bm{W}_{Q}}\ell_{i})^{\top}\differential{\bm{W}}_{Q}\right),

we have
(\nabla_{\bm{W}_{Q}}\ell_{i})^{\top}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\bm{W}_{K}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}(\nabla_{\bm{M}_{i}}\ell_{i})^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}
, or

(5.26)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}_{Q}}\ell_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}(\nabla_{\bm{M}_{i}}\ell_{i})\bm{X}_{i}\bm{W}_{K}.

By taking Frobenius norm of both sides and applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have:

(5.27)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\bm{W}_{Q}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\|\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\|_{F}\cdot\|\nabla_{\bm{M}_{i}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}\cdot\|\bm{X}_{i}\|_{F}\cdot\|\bm{W}_{K}\|_{F}=\|\nabla_{{\bm{M}}_{i}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}\frac{B_{x}^{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\|{\bm{W}}_{K}\|_{F}.

Finally, substituting the upper bound
\|\nabla_{{\bm{M}}_{i}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}
derived from equation
5.21
, we have:

(5.28)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}\leq(\sqrt{t}B_{x}B_{w}+B_{y})B_{w}B_{x}\beta B_{s^{\prime}}\frac{B_{x}^{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\|{\bm{W}}_{K}\|_{F}.

By symmetry,

(5.29)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{K}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}\leq(\sqrt{t}B_{x}B_{w}+B_{y})B_{w}B_{x}\beta B_{s^{\prime}}\frac{B_{x}^{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\|{\bm{W}}_{Q}\|_{F}.

Combining equations
5.28
and
5.29
, we have,

(5.30)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{{\bm{T}}}\ell_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{K}}\ell_{i}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\left[(\sqrt{t}B_{x}B_{w}+B_{y})B_{w}B_{x}\beta B_{s^{\prime}}\frac{B_{x}^{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\right]^{2}(\|{\bm{W}}_{K}\|_{F}^{2}+\|{\bm{W}}_{Q}\|_{F}^{2})

(5.31)

\displaystyle=\left[(\sqrt{t}B_{x}B_{w}+B_{y})B_{w}B_{x}\beta B_{s^{\prime}}\frac{B_{x}^{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\right]^{2}\|{\bm{T}}\|^{2}.

So for
\|\nabla_{{\bm{T}}}\hat{R}_{A}\|
, we have,

(5.32)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{{\bm{T}}}\hat{R}_{A}\|\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{{\bm{T}}}\ell_{i}({\bm{T}})\|\leq\left((\sqrt{t}B_{x}B_{w}+B_{y})B_{w}B_{x}\beta B_{s^{\prime}}\frac{B_{x}^{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\|{\bm{T}}\|.

Thus we have proven equation
4.3
and next we prove equation
4.4
.

\bullet
Bounding the Laplacian of the Loss

For the second-order variations, we expand the Laplacian using the chain rule. By substituting both the Jacobian bound (
\beta B_{s^{\prime}}
) and the Softmax Hessian bound (
\beta^{2}B_{s^{\prime\prime}}
) from Lemma
4.2
into
|\Delta_{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}\ell_{i}|
and
|\Delta_{{\bm{W}}_{K}}\ell_{i}|
, we obtain the Laplacian bound.

Recalling,
\ell_{i}({\bm{T}})=\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{Y}}_{i}-\hat{{\bm{Y}}}_{i}({\bm{T}})\|_{F}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\mE_i^\top\mE_i)
, firstly we observe that,

(5.33)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b}2{\bm{E}}_{i,ab}\cdot\frac{\partial{\bm{E}}_{i,ab}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}=\sum_{a,b}{\bm{E}}_{i,ab}\cdot\frac{\partial(\hat{{\bm{Y}}}_{i,ab}-{\bm{Y}}_{i,ab})}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}},

where
{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}
is the
(j,k)-
th element of the matrix
{\bm{W}}_{Q}
, which is a scalar.

Note that
{\bm{Y}}_{i,ab}
is a constant and hence,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\ell_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b}\left(\left[\frac{\partial{\bm{E}}_{i,ab}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}\right]\cdot\frac{\partial\hat{{\bm{Y}}}_{i,ab}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}+{\bm{E}}_{i,ab}\cdot\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{{\bm{Y}}}_{i,ab}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}^{2}}\right]\right)

(5.34)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{{\bm{Y}}}_{i,ab}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}\right)^{2}+\sum_{a,b}\left({\bm{E}}_{i,ab}\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{{\bm{Y}}}_{i,ab}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}^{2}}\right)=\left\|\frac{\partial\hat{{\bm{Y}}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\text{Tr}\left({\bm{E}}_{i}^{\top}\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{{\bm{Y}}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}^{2}}\right).

Towards analyzing the second term in the RHS above, consider the following derivative of
{\bm{S}}_{i}=\text{RowSoftMax}({\bm{M}}_{i})
.

(5.35)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bm{S}}_{i,ab}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}=\sum_{c,d}\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{S}}_{i,ab}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,cd}}\cdot\frac{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,cd}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}\right)\eqqcolon\nabla\text{Softmax}\left[\frac{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}\right].

We have denoted the RHS to be
\nabla\text{Softmax}\left[\frac{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}\right]
. This means the perturbation of the underlying weight
{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}
induces a directional change in the intermediate pre-activation matrix
{\bm{M}}_{i}
. By passing this direction through the Jacobian tensor of the Softmax operator and performing a tensor contraction, we compute the exact resulting variation in the output probability matrix
{\bm{S}}_{i}
.

To compute the second derivative
\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{S}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}^{2}}
, we differentiate the above result with respect to
{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}
again to get,

(5.36)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{S}}_{i,ab}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{c,d}\sum_{e,f}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{S}}_{i,ab}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,cd}\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,ef}}\cdot\frac{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,cd}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}\cdot\frac{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,ef}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}\right)+\sum_{c,d}\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{S}}_{i,ab}}{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i,cd}}\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{M}}_{i,cd}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}^{2}}\right).

As before, we denote the RHS in tensor notation as “
\nabla^{2}\text{Softmax}\left[\frac{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}},\frac{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}\right]+\nabla\text{Softmax}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{M}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}^{2}}\right]
”— the first term represents the second-order sensitivity of the Softmax operator (a 6th-order Hessian tensor) acting simultaneously as a bilinear map on two identical first-order directional perturbations and the second term represents the first-order sensitivity (Jacobian tensor) acting on the second-order perturbation of the intermediate matrix
{\bm{M}}_{i}
.
Recalling
\hat{{\bm{Y}}}_{i}({\bm{T}})={\bm{S}}_{i}({\bm{T}}){\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{v}
, and using the above notation we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{{\bm{Y}}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{{\bm{S}}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}^{2}}{\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{v}=\frac{\partial}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}\frac{\partial{\bm{S}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}{\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{v}=\frac{\partial}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}\left(\nabla\text{Softmax}\left[\frac{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}\right]\right){\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{v}

(5.37)

\displaystyle=\left(\nabla^{2}\text{Softmax}\left[\frac{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}},\frac{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}\right]+\nabla\text{Softmax}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{M}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}^{2}}\right]\right){\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{v}.

Note that
{\bm{M}}_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}{\bm{X}}_{i}{\bm{W}}_{Q}{\bm{W}}_{K}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}
is linear in
{\bm{W}}_{Q}
, so
\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{M}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}^{2}}\equiv 0
.
From equation
5.36
, using equation
5.22
and the Hessian bound from Lemma
4.2
, we have

(5.38)

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k}\left\|\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{S}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}^{2}}\right\|_{F}\leq\sum_{j,k}\|\nabla^{2}\text{Softmax}({\bm{M}}_{i})\|_{F}\cdot\left\|\frac{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}\right\|_{F}\cdot\left\|\frac{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}\right\|_{F}\leq\beta^{2}B_{s^{\prime\prime}}\sum_{j,k}\left\|\frac{\partial{\bm{M}}_{i}}{\partial{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}_{jk}}\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq\beta^{2}B_{s^{\prime\prime}}\frac{B_{x}^{4}}{d}\|{\bm{W}}_{K}\|_{F}^{2}.

So

(5.39)

\displaystyle|\Delta_{{\bm{W}}_{Q}}\ell_{i}|\leq(\beta B_{s^{\prime}}B_{x}B_{w})^{2}\left[\frac{B_{x}^{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\right]^{2}\|{\bm{W}}_{K}\|_{F}^{2}+(\sqrt{t}B_{x}B_{w}+B_{y})B_{x}B_{w}\beta^{2}B_{s^{\prime\prime}}\frac{B_{x}^{4}}{d}\|{\bm{W}}_{K}\|_{F}^{2}.

By symmetry,

(5.40)

\displaystyle|\Delta_{{\bm{W}}_{K}}\ell_{i}|\leq(\beta B_{s^{\prime}}B_{x}B_{w})^{2}\left[\frac{B_{x}^{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\right]^{2}\|{\bm{W}}_{Q}\|_{F}^{2}+(\sqrt{t}B_{x}B_{w}+B_{y})B_{x}B_{w}\beta^{2}B_{s^{\prime\prime}}\frac{B_{x}^{4}}{d}\|{\bm{W}}_{Q}\|_{F}^{2}.

We have,

(5.41)

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{{\bm{T}}}\hat{R}_{A}\|\leq\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{{\bm{T}}}\ell_{i}(T)\|\leq\|\Delta_{T}\ell_{i}(T)\|\leq\frac{B_{x}^{4}}{d}\left((\beta B_{s^{\prime}}B_{x}B_{w})^{2}+(\sqrt{t}B_{x}B_{w}+B_{y})B_{x}B_{w}\beta^{2}B_{s^{\prime\prime}}\right)\|{\bm{T}}\|^{2}.

∎

6.1.
Results analysis

In the very small regularization regime
(\epsilon=10^{-6}
and
\lambda=10^{-4}
or
10^{-5}
),
all methods, no regularization, “log” regularization, and “power” regularisation, exhibit nearly identical convergence behaviour in both normalised test/train RMSE and test relative L2 error.
As shown in Figure
1
, all configurations rapidly decay from their initial error and stabilise at approximately the same performance level (
0.78
), indicating that at this scale of regularization,
there is no meaningful difference in predictive accuracy.

Despite the similarity in error metrics,
substantial differences emerge in the internal dynamics of the model.
In particular, the Frobenius norm (
||{\bm{W}}_{Q}||^{2}_{F}+||{\bm{W}}_{K}||^{2}_{F}
) grows steadily in the absence of regularization, reaching significantly larger values over training.
In contrast, both “log” and “power” regularization effectively constrain this growth, with power regularization enforcing the strongest suppression and log regularization yielding a slightly higher but still stable norm plateau.
Firstly,
these results demonstrate that even weak regularization of the kind studied here can meaningfully alter the scaling of the attention mechanism without impacting predictive performance.

In the generalization gap plot in Figure
1
, all methods initially exhibit a rapid reduction in gap but the unregularized model shows a gradual increase over time, suggesting mild overfitting. Both log and power regularization mitigate this effect, maintaining a consistently lower and more stable gap throughout training, with power again providing the strongest control.
Secondly,
this result indicates that despite comparable test error improved generalization stability is induced by the regularizers being studied.

Overall, these findings suggest that the regularization studied in the presented theory primarily influences model stability.
In low-
\lambda
regimes, log regularisation provides a robust default by stabilising the key and query weight norms with minimal intervention, while power regularisation is preferable when stronger suppression of attention weight growth is desired.

7.
Conclusion

This work establishes that the SDE, that mimics the SGD, converges for both attention layers and depth-2 neural networks trained with LoRA, for arbitrary data and network sizes, even under arbitrarily low regularization, and we further provides non-asymptotic convergence rates.

A natural next question is whether the loss function on the attention layer with it’s key-query-value matrices using LoRA, satisfy the Villani conditions in the corresponding space of factor matrices. More generally, it remains open to prove convergence guarantees for a full transformer layer in which all three matrices per head (
{\bm{W}}_{Q}
,
{\bm{W}}_{K}
,
{\bm{W}}_{V}
) and the feedforward network weights are trained jointly, with one or more of these components potentially using LoRA.

An interesting direction for future work is to extend our guarantees to more advanced sequence models such as FlashAttention
[
11
]
, Performers
[
9
]
, Mamba
[
16
]
. In contrast to vanilla attention, which computes softmax attention, these methods modify the computation in different ways. FlashAttention
[
11
]
computes the same softmax attention but using a memory-efficient tiled algorithm, which retains the same mathematical structure as vanilla attention and is thus likely amenable to a similar analysis. Performers
[
8
]
approximates the softmax kernel
\exp(\mQ\mK^\top)
using positive random feature maps
\phi({\bm{Q}})
and
\phi({\bm{K}})
, resulting in an approximate attention computation of the form

\text{Attn}({\bm{Q}},{\bm{K}},{\bm{V}})\approx\frac{\phi({\bm{Q}})(\phi({\bm{K}})^{\top}{\bm{V}})}{\phi({\bm{Q}})(\phi({\bm{K}})^{\top}\mathbf{1})},
which reduces the computational complexity. Mamba
[
16
]
replaces attention entirely with an input-dependent state-space model (SSM), yielding a linear-time recurrence. Extending convergence guarantees to these alternative attention mechanisms remains an open problem.

Proof.

Proof.
