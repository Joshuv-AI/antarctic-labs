Right bundle branch block with ST-segment elevation – myocardial infarction or not?
Authors: Adriana Vazão, Rita Ribeiro Carvalho, Hélia Martins
Published: 2026

Clinical vignette

An out-of-hospital urgent care team was called to assist an 81-year-old female patient who had attempted suicide by ingesting multiple tablets (alprazolam, lorazepam, and dosulepin). Upon arrival, they performed nasogastric intubation and gastric lavage. The patient was monitored, and an abnormal electrocardiographic trace was observed, prompting the performance of a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) (Figure 1A). The patient denied chest pain. She was then transported to the nearest hospital. During transport, another electrocardiographic change was noted on the monitor, and a new ECG was recorded (Figure 1B) ∼6 min after the initial one. In the emergency room, her blood pressure was 210/169 mmHg, heart rate 89 b.p.m., and temperature 36.4°C. Physical examination revealed a systolic murmur (grade II/VI) but was otherwise unremarkable. Initial laboratory evaluation showed normal creatine kinase at 93 U/L, mild elevation of high-sensitivity troponin I at 15 pg/mL (RV < 11.6 pg/mL), and elevated N-terminal prohormone of brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) at 3000 pg/mL. Point-of-care cardiac ultrasound revealed severe left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy with preserved ejection fraction, no wall motion abnormalities, and a calcified aortic valve with mild aortic stenosis.

Discussion and explanation:

The initial ECG shows sinus rhythm at 97 b.p.m., first degree atrioventricular (AV) block (PQ 240 ms), left axis deviation, and right bundle branch block morphology with ST-segment elevation in V1–V3, I, and aVL. As this ECG was obtained out of hospital, electrode misplacement is likely, specifically right and left arm lead reversal. This explains the isoelectric qR pattern in lead I, the RS morphology in aVR with a predominantly positive QRS (aVR resembling aVL and vice versa), and the inversion of leads II and III.1

The correct answer is A.

Discussion and explanation:

In a patient with acute chest pain, the initial ECG would suggest ongoing myocardial ischaemia, prompting emergent coronary angiography. However, the patient was asymptomatic, and ST-segment changes resolved within 6 min, with normalization on repeat ECG, supporting the need for continuous monitoring. The main abnormality on admission was severe hypertension, requiring immediate treatment. Despite markedly elevated NT-proBNP, there was no clinical or echocardiographic evidence of decompensated heart failure. In the context of a suicide attempt in an elderly patient, psychiatric evaluation is essential but not an immediate priority, as initial management should focus on haemodynamic stabilization and monitoring for cardiac complications.

Question 3

ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction

ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction

The correct answer is C.

Discussion and explanation:

The most likely cause is dosulepin intoxication. Tricyclic antidepressant (TCA) overdose blocks cardiac sodium channels,2 causing PR, QRS, and QT prolongation, AV block, and repolarization abnormalities that may mimic ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).3 Benzodiazepines (alprazolam, lorazepam) may cause tachycardia or AV block but do not explain the repolarization changes. Coronary vasospasm and STEMI are unlikely given the absence of chest pain, rapid normalization of ST-segment, and modest troponin elevation. Pulmonary embolism is also unlikely due to the lack of respiratory symptoms. Importantly, limb lead abnormalities in the initial ECG were due to electrode misplacement, while the transient ST elevation and conduction changes were attributable to TCA toxicity (see Supplementary material online, Figures S1 and S2).
