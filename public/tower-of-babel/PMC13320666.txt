Enhanced rock weathering has greater promise as a sustainable farming practice than a CO2 removal technology
Authors: Ian M. Power, Jonathan Spence, Minger Guo, Sasha Wilson, Shaun Watmough
Published: 2026

Carbon dioxide removal (CDR) is likely to be part of any credible plan to meaningfully address climate change. To be effective, such strategies must maximize net greenhouse gas reductions while efficiently using energy, land, materials, and financial resources. One relatively inexpensive way to remove CO2 from the atmosphere is enhanced rock weathering (ERW)—the spreading of crushed alkaline silicate rocks on agricultural land to accelerate natural weathering. Compared with many other CDR strategies, ERW offers lower capital requirements and a clearer path to large-scale deployment. Crucially, it can be readily integrated into existing agricultural practices by replacing the agricultural lime (aglime) that farmers worldwide use to reduce soil acidity (Fig. 1). As a result, ERW has attracted considerable attention and investment.

Scaling up presents big challenges, however. While ERW has a global potential to sequester 0.5–2 gigatons of CO2 per year by 2050 (4), achieving this scale will require gigatons of alkaline silicate rock powder from new mines or mineral waste stockpiles (5), as well as widespread adoption by farmers to apply these powders to vast agricultural lands at dosages beyond those currently used for liming (6). We argue that this level of adoption is unlikely under the current deployment model, which is largely controlled by the carbon market, including profit-seeking carbon registries and ERW companies. A more effective approach would be to incentivize farmers directly, enabling them to drive ERW deployment as a sustainable agricultural practice.

Wise Deployment

The current deployment model, structured around the processes of those who generate, certify, and sell carbon credits, limits the speed and scale of ERW as a climate mitigation strategy. Demonstrating in-field CO2 removal currently requires expensive, labor-intensive, and highly specialized monitoring, reporting, and verification (MRV) protocols that are executed and overseen by these organizations, creating a substantial financial barrier to widespread adoption. Estimates suggest that MRV alone can cost up to $275 USD per ton of CO2 removed, accounting for a large portion of total ERW costs (7).

These costs do more than slow deployment—they determine who can participate. The current system acts as a gatekeeper, limiting participation by farmers and smaller actors while concentrating control in organizations with the financial resources and technical expertise to navigate MRV requirements established by carbon registries. Despite ERW being a low-technology practice applied on farms, its implementation is effectively outsourced to a narrow set of private actors.

One study (8) estimates that more than 20 ERW companies have raised over a quarter of a billion dollars. These companies are advancing ERW through field trials and MRV development, but they also act as intermediaries between feedstock suppliers, farmers, and carbon credit buyers—positioning themselves at the center of ERW deployment. Under this model, there is a financial motivation for ERW companies and carbon registries to certify credits, in a system with little to no regulatory oversight from governments. Even though companies provide rock powders to farmers at low or no cost, they clearly aim to obtain carbon credits, establish a business model, and eventually generate profits.

This creates a fundamental misalignment: decision-making is driven by carbon credit generation rather than soil health. ERW is framed as a CDR technology by start-up companies, rather than as a farming practice that sequesters CO2 as a cobenefit.

The structure of carbon markets compounds these problems. Demand is presently concentrated among a small number of corporate buyers, making the system vulnerable and reinforcing centralized control. The voluntary CDR market grew by 78% in 2024, but over 75% of purchases were by Microsoft, Google, and Frontier (9).

At the same time, farmers are likely to find the ERW process difficult to access and often unrewarding. Asked about ERW, farmers and other participants expressed concerns about costs, environmental impacts, and who would ultimately profit from deployment (10). Trust depended heavily on the perceived motivations of those involved, with greater confidence placed in universities and public institutions than profit-driven carbon companies.

Farming Rocks

ERW is fundamentally a farming practice. As such, application rates should be guided by agronomic principles and soil properties, particularly pH (Fig. 1). Adjusting soil pH provides a practical basis for assessing the application of ERW rock powders and comparing with liming—a widely adopted practice with clear benefits and well-established guidelines. While some researchers advocate increased use of aglime as an ERW strategy (11), others propose substituting aglime with silicate-based amendments that sequester CO2 without the risk of releasing it (12). Standard methods to assess the neutralization ability of liming agents are readily adapted to silicate-based ERW feedstocks (1).

Testing shows that greater doses of silicate rock powder are required to achieve the same soil pH change as aglime, but their slower dissolution rates may sustain optimal soil pH over longer periods, potentially reducing application frequency (13). Furthermore, replacing aglime with alkaline silicate rock powders supplies additional nutrients, including phosphorus, potassium, trace elements, and dissolved silica, which can enhance plant growth and improve pest resistance (14).

The central policy question is not whether ERW can contribute to climate change mitigation, but rather who is best positioned to scale the practice up to a level that will have a real impact.

Critically, soil health should not be compromised in pursuit of carbon credits through arbitrarily high application rates of silicate rock powders (e.g., 50 ton/hectare/year) that could cause adverse impacts, including metal accumulation (e.g., nickel; ref. 15).

ERW has extraordinary potential to be readily and widely deployed at relatively low cost; yet, it also has one of the most uncertain and expensive MRV requirements among CDR strategies (7, 16). Currently, stringent ERW protocols set by carbon registries attempt to ensure quality carbon credits. However, serious complications remain, including cation sorption that can promote CO2 release due to liberation of exchangeable soil acidity (17); secondary mineral precipitation that reduces CDR efficiency (18); and downstream CO2 losses caused by processes such as carbonate precipitation, river degassing, and biological cycling during transport from soils to the ocean (19). Many of these processes have yet to be accounted for by carbon registries. Conservative MRV is necessary to avoid overestimating CDR rates and overcrediting, which would undermine confidence in ERW as a durable climate mitigation strategy (16).

The Right Incentives

Shifting control to farmers and increasing adoption will take new incentives, such as direct government subsidies, tax credits, or cost-sharing programs for silicate rock application, alongside a streamlined MRV framework developed by governments, researchers, and carbon registries. Scaling ERW may depend on accepting less precise, but more cost-effective and conservative, approaches to quantifying CDR.

For example, ERW companies and commercial laboratories could test feedstocks supplied directly from quarries to farmers to determine their neutralizing ability and most labile and CO2-reactive fraction, which is generally less than 10% of their total theoretical CDR capacity (20). In this framework, ERW companies could play a supporting role in testing amendments, consulting farmers, and providing guidance. Governments could use this conservative estimate of CDR potential to justify subsidizing ERW, thereby motivating farmers to use silicate rock powders instead of aglime. Because governments or certification bodies would credit only the most reactive fraction of the feedstock, these conservative accounting approaches would generate fewer carbon credits, even though the actual long-term CDR achieved in the field may be substantially greater.

Alongside simplified MRV, another viable route for expanding ERW is to focus on soil benefits and agronomic parameters, including pH, which are already routinely monitored by farmers. Successful scaling depends on farmers adopting ERW as a sustainable farming practice, motivated by demonstrable improvements in soil quality and crop yields. Farmer-led systems play a central role in how agricultural practices scale. For example, the adoption of cover cropping—planting non-cash crops such as legumes, grasses, or brassicas during off-seasons to protect and improve soil health—illustrates how farmer-to-farmer learning, often supported by extension programs and incentives, can serve as a model to expand ERW. When introducing cover crops, farmers incur costs for seed, field preparation, and crop termination (21), and yields can decline in the initial years. These trade-offs highlight the importance of sustained incentives and support during early adoption.

Although farmers are motivated to improve the health and productivity of their soils, switching from aglime to silicate-based ERW amendments comes with additional costs, mainly the sourcing, transport, and application of larger quantities of silicate rock powder needed to achieve optimal pH (Fig. 1). One approach to offset these costs is an action- or hybrid-based system (22), in which farmers receive financial incentives from governments, agricultural programs, or carbon markets for applying silicate rock powders (i.e., an action) and/or achieving optimal soil pH using these amendments (i.e., a result). In the United States, incentive programs have helped increase cover crop adoption by 50% from 2012 to 2017 (23). Funds from these programs ranged from $1.5 billion to $2.1 billion per year from 2014 to 2021, with obligations totaling $1.8 billion in 2021 for 21.6 million acres, or 2.4% of the total acres farmed in the United States in 2021 (895 million acres; ref. 24). While overall adoption of cover cropping remains low, these levels would be game-changing for ERW.

In addition to being ERW practitioners, farmers can provide the social license for expanding ERW. In the United States, nearly 9 in 10 adults trust farmers (25), yet their participation in soil carbon research is low (26). Reframing ERW as a sustainable farming practice will require greater farmer participation in ERW research, demonstration, and deployment. Fortunately, public support for subsidizing farmers—including for adopting sustainable production practices—is strong (27).

The central policy question is not whether ERW can contribute to climate change mitigation, but rather who is best positioned to scale the practice up to a level that will have a real impact. By incentivizing farmers directly, simplifying certification, and reducing reliance on for-profit carbon markets, ERW can be placed under the control of those who manage the land.
