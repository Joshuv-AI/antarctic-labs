The BDNF/TrkB/CREB signaling improves chronic stress-induced decreased ovarian reserve by inhibiting pyroptosis of granulosa cells and disorders of sex hormone secretion
Authors: Yuanyuan Chen, Jun Tang, Yi Wen, Zhaohua Liu, Yi Zhang, Man Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDecreased ovarian reserve (DOR) seriously affects female reproductive health. However, the mechanism of DOR remains unknown. This study aimed to explore the mechanism of BDNF/TrkB/CREB signaling in granulosa cell pyroptosis and sex hormone secretion in DOR.MethodsRecombinant BDNF (rBDNF) and TrkB antagonist ANA-12 were used to investigate the effects of the BDNF-TrkB signaling pathway on chronic unpredictable stress (CUS)-induced DOR in vivo. The mechanism of the BDNF-TrkB signaling pathway in NE-induced pyroptosis and sex hormone secretion was further investigated in vitro by the combined application of the CREB antagonist KG-501.ResultsThe CUS model reduced BDNF expression in brain tissue, peripheral blood, and ovarian tissues and inhibited p-TrkB/TrkB, p-PI3K/PI3K, and p-AKT/AKT expression. rBDNF enhanced the ovarian index and improved histological structure in CUS mice, restored the disordered estrus cycle, reduced the serum corticosterone level, and increased the pregnancy rate and embryo number. rBDNF inhibited serum FSH, LH, and NE levels and apoptosis in CUS mice while increasing AMH, E2, P, and T levels, accompanied by downregulated NLRP3, ASC, GSDMD-N, Cleaved Caspase-1, IL-18, and IL-1β levels. However, the ANA-12 effect on the above factors was opposite to that of rBDNF. Mechanistically, the BDNF-TrkB signaling pathway inhibited NE-induced pyroptosis of granulosa cells and sex hormone secretion disorders by promoting CREB phosphorylation.ConclusionOur findings indicate that the BDNF-TrkB signaling pathway inhibits pyroptosis of granulosa cells and sex hormone secretion disorders by promoting CREB phosphorylation, thereby improving DOR. These results provide a new theoretical basis in DOR for targeting the BDNF-TrkB-CREB axis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-026-02111-z.

Introduction

Ovarian reserve is defined as the number of oocytes stored or available for recruitment in the ovaries, reflecting a woman’s reproductive potential [1]. Decreased ovarian reserve (DOR) indicates a decline in the quantity and/or quality of oocytes, leading to insufficient ovarian function and reduced fertility [2]. Chronic psychological stress is regarded as one of the main factors related to DOR and impaired female reproductive function [3]. Women who experience excessive stress are more likely to exhibit negative emotions such as depression and anxiety, as well as reproductive endocrine disorders, which lead to a decrease in luteinizing hormone (LH) levels during the luteal phase, an increase in follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels, a reduction in serum estradiol (E2) and progesterone (P) levels, and an increase in anovulatory cycles [4]. With the trend of delaying pregnancy in modern society, DOR has become one of the most common challenges in current clinical reproductive medicine [5]. Therefore, exploring the potential mechanisms of DOR is crucial for safeguarding women’s reproductive health.

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a pivotal neurotrophin, is critically implicated in many stress-related mental disorders [6]. Chronic stress exposure has been shown to dysregulate BDNF expression in both central neural circuits and systemic circulation [7]. Furthermore, BDNF has been found to possibly be a physiological regulatory factor that promotes follicular development, granulosa cell proliferation, and oocyte maturation [8]. Granulosa cells convert androgens into estrogens, synthesize progesterone, and secrete insulin-like growth factors. Dysfunction or apoptosis of these cells may lead to follicular atresia [9]. BDNF directly modulates ovarian somatic cells (e.g., granulosa cells) via binding to its receptors, thereby facilitating the transition from primary to secondary follicles [10]. Tyrosine kinase receptor B (TrkB) is a functional receptor of BDNF and mediates BDNF signal transduction during follicular development [11]. Cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) response element-binding protein (CREB) is a leucine zipper transcription factor activated by phosphorylation, and its phosphorylation is crucial for the expression of BDNF [12]. Studies have shown that activating CREB increases sex hormone levels, reduces the number of atretic follicles, and protects ovarian reserve [13]. The BDNF-TrkB signaling pathway activation ameliorates depression-like behaviors in perimenopausal mice by enhancing CREB phosphorylation [14]. However, it remains unclear whether the BDNF-TrkB signaling pathway affects sex hormone secretion in chronic unpredictable stress (CUS)-induced DOR by regulating CREB phosphorylation.

Pyroptosis is a form of programmed necrotic cell death mediated by gasdermins, a family of pore-forming proteins [15]. Once activated, NLRP3 facilitates the cleavage of pro-caspase-1 into activated caspase-1, thereby driving the maturation of gasdermin D (GSDMD) and pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-18 [16]. A large amount of evidence indicates that pyroptosis is associated with various diseases, such as ovarian cancer, polycystic ovary syndrome, and several other inflammatory diseases [17, 18]. Inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome activation mitigates pyroptosis and inflammation in granulosa cells, which in turn ameliorates polycystic ovary syndrome [19]. In rats, cyclophosphamide-induced premature ovarian failure can be opposed by suppressing NLRP3/Caspase-1/GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis [20]. Cyclophosphamide-induced DOR can be alleviated through the regulation of gonadal hormones along the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis and the protection of ovarian granulosa cells from undergoing pyroptosis [21]. These pieces of evidence highlight the importance of pyroptosis of granulosa cells in the pathogenesis of DOR. It has been reported that upregulation of BDNF improves depression by inhibiting pyroptosis mediated by the P2X7/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway [22]. However, whether BDNF improves CUS-induced DOR by inhibiting pyroptosis of granulosa cells remains to be further clarified.

This study aims to explore whether the BDNF/TrkB/CREB signaling improves CUS-induced DOR by inhibiting pyroptosis of granulosa cells and disorders of sex hormone secretion. Targeted regulation of the BDNF/TrkB/CREB signaling pathway may be a novel therapeutic strategy for clinical DOR.

Materials and methods

Animal experiment

Female C57BL/6 mice aged 6–8 weeks were purchased from Hunan Slake Jingda Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. The mice were kept on a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle at 23 ± 1℃ with 50 ± 10% relative humidity. The mice were given free access to water and conventional rodent food. After one week of adaptive feeding, the mice were randomly and evenly divided into six groups (n = 10), including the Control, CUS, CUS + BDNF Vehicle, CUS + BDNF, CUS + ANA-12 Vehicle, and the CUS + ANA-12 group. Except for the Control group, the CUS model was induced in the remaining groups as described previously [23, 24]. Each model mouse was housed individually and received a 28-day stress program. Excluding any 24 h continuous stressors, stressors were applied daily between 09:00 and 11:00. Specifically, the sources of stress are as follows: (1) 24 h fasting, (2) 24 h water deprivation, (3) 24 h cage tilt (45°), (4) 24 h wet cage, (5) Overnight light exposure, (6) 2 h physical restraint, (7) 5 min cold swimming at 4℃ followed by hair removal and drying, (8) 5 min shaker stress. (9) 24 h placement in an empty cage and (10) 1 min tail clamping (1 cm from the tail end). When animals are repeatedly exposed to predictable stressors, such as the chronic restraint stress model, habituation can occur. Therefore, all stressors were randomly arranged during the 1-week experimental period and repeated in the 4-week experiment (Table 1) to ensure the unpredictability of the stressors. The mice in Control group were grouped and raised without stress. Based on findings that intraperitoneal injection of 100 ng/rat recombinant BDNF could effectively induce a decrease in the colonic response threshold [25], an excess of BDNF (1 µg /200 µL) was used in this study. Starting from the 21st day, mice in the CUS+BDNF group were intraperitoneally injected with 1 µg/200 µL of recombinant BDNF (APG02257G, ANNORON, China) every day for treatment. The treatment lasted until the day when the mice were sacrificed. Mice in the CUS + BDNF Vehicle group were injected with the vehicle (0.9% NaCl). Starting from the 26th day, mice in the CUS + ANA-12 group were intraperitoneally injected with 0.5 mg/kg ANA-12 (SML0209, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) every day. The treatment lasted until the day when the mice were sacrificed. The CUS + ANA-12 Vehicle group mice were injected with the vehicle (17% DMSO). After the stress test, all groups of mice received 5 IU of pregnant mares’ serum gonadotropin intraperitoneally for 48 h, and then received 10 IU of human chorionic gonadotropin treatment. After the intervention, ovarian tissue and serum samples were collected. The ovarian index is calculated using the following formula: ovarian index = ovarian weight (mg) / body weight (g). The Animal Ethics Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (No. 20250776) approved all animal experiment procedures.

Estrus cycle determination

To monitor the estrus cycle of mice, we performed vaginal smears every morning for two weeks. The morphology of epithelial cells collected on the smear was analyzed under an optical microscope. The estrus cycle of normal adult mice consists of four stages: Proestrus, Estrus, Metestrus, and Diestrus. The vaginal smears collected during proestrus contain a large number of oval nucleated epithelial cells and a small amount of white blood cells, while during Estrus, sheet-like anucleated keratinized (squamous) cells were dominant. In the Metestrus, three types of cells, namely white blood cells, nucleated epithelial cells, and anucleated cornified flakes, were seen. Finally, during the Diestrus, the cells were mainly white blood cells. If the estrus cycle of mice is prolonged (≥ 6 days), incomplete, or lasts for more than three days in a single stage, it is considered that the estrus cycle is disordered.

Mating experiment

Five female mice were selected from each group for breeding to evaluate their reproductive capacity. Female mice were mated with male C57BL/6 mice of the same age for one week at a ratio of 2: 1 per cage. After the mating period ended, the females were continuously weighed and checked for signs of pregnancy. The number of pregnancies and embryos in each group was recorded.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

The tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Following fixation, specimens underwent sequential dehydration through a graded ethanol series and xylene-mediated paraffin infiltration. Paraffin-embedded blocks were sectioned at 3 μm thickness using a microtome. Dewaxing was performed via xylene and graded ethanol rehydration, followed by routine H&E staining. Histological structures were visualized using a light microscope (BA210T, Motic, Singapore).

Ovarian follicle quantification

Ovarian pathological changes were assessed using H&E staining. We made continuous sections of the ovarian tissue and sorted them, then extracted one slice every 10 slices in order, and selected 10 slices of each ovarian tissue in each group for follicle counting. The number of follicles at different stages was counted using an optical microscope, including primordial follicles, primary follicles, secondary follicles, antral follicles, atretic follicles, and corpus luteum. Primordial follicles were identified by an oocyte surrounded by a single layer of flattened pre-granulosa cells. Primary follicles were defined by an oocyte enclosed within a single layer of cuboidal or columnar granulosa cells. Secondary follicles featured an oocyte surrounded by two or more layers of granulosa cells, without an antrum. Antral follicles were characterized by the presence of a fluid-filled cavity, the antrum. Atretic follicles exhibited signs of degeneration, including oocyte nuclear pyknosis or fragmentation, dissolution of chromatin and cytoplasm, and a reduction in the granulosa cell layer. Corpora lutea were recognized by the presence of luteal cells displaying eosinophilic cytoplasm and basophilic nuclei. Two independent researchers calculated the number of follicles in each category.

Isolation and culture of granulosa cells

The granulosa cells were released and isolated by immersing the ovaries in 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS)-Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) and puncturing them with a No. 26 needle. The cells were inoculated in 3.5 cm culture dishes and exposed to DMEM in the presence of 10% FBS at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator for 48 h. The cells were exposed to 1 mM norepinephrine (NE) for 24 h [26]. The cells were treated for 48 h without or with gradient concentrations (10, 100, 1000 ng/mL) of BDNF, respectively. 5 µM ANA-12 or 25 µM was added to the cells for intervention for 48 h.

The terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT)-mediated dUTP nick-end labelling (TUNEL)

The TUNEL assay Kit (40306ES50, YEASEN, China) was used to assess apoptosis in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Briefly, tissue sections were permeated with Proteinase K for 30 min. After washing with PBS buffer 3 times, sections were incubated in 1 × equilibrium buffer for 30 min, and then stained with TdT enzyme, FITC-dUTP, and reaction buffer at 37℃ for 60 min in the dark. The nucleus was stained with DAPI at 37℃ for 10 min. The images were taken with the fluorescence microscope (BA210T, Motic). The apoptotic index was calculated as a ratio of (number of apoptotic cells) to (total number of cells) in each field.

Western blot

Following the extraction of proteins using RIPA (P0013B, Beyotime, China), the total protein concentration within the cells was precisely determined via the BCA method. Following quantification, proteins were resolved by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). After successful separation, these proteins were carefully transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) skim milk powder for 90 min. Primary antibodies (details in Table 2) were applied overnight at 4℃. After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated Goat anti-mouse IgG (SA00001-1, 1:5000, Proteintech) or Goat anti-rabbit IgG (SA00001-2, 1:6000, Proteintech) for 90 min. β-actin served as the loading control. For visualization, the membrane was incubated with ECL chemiluminescence solution for 1 min. Subsequently, a gel imaging system (ChemiScope6100, CLINX) was utilized for imaging. The exposed image strips were analyzed with Quantity One professional grayscale analysis software (v4.6.8, BIO-RAD).

Immunofluorescence

Sections were prepared using H&E staining methods. After heating for antigen retrieval, the sections were placed in a sodium borohydride solution at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, the sections were blocked with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min, followed by incubation in 5% BSA at 37℃ for 90 min. Next, the sections were incubated overnight with FSHR (22665-1-AP, 1:100, Proteintech) at 4℃, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L). Sections were incubated with TYP-520 fluorescent dye at 37℃ in the dark for 10 min. The antigen retrieval and blocking steps (with hydrogen peroxide and BSA) were repeated. Sections were incubated overnight with Caspase-1 (ab207802, 1:100, Abcam) at 4℃. After that, they were incubated with HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) at 37℃ for 60 min. Another round of incubation with the TYP-520 fluorescent dye at 37 °C in the dark for 10 min was performed. The nucleus was stained with DAPI at 37℃ for 10 min. The sections were preserved in buffered glycerin and observed under a fluorescence microscope (BA210T, Motic). Positive expression was quantified by measuring the integrated optical density in positively stained regions per field using Image Pro Plus software 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Corticosterone (CORT), NE, anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH), FSH, E2, LH, testosterone (T), P, IL-1β, and IL-18 levels in serum and cell supernatant were determined using commercial ELISA kits (CUSABIO, China). The absorbance at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader (MB-530, HEALES, China). The CORT (CSB-E07969m), NE (CSB-E07870m), AMH (CSB-E13156m), FSH (CSB-E06871m), E2 (CSB-E05109m), LH (CSB-E12770m), T (CSB-E05101m), P (CSB-E05104m), IL-1β (CSB-E08054m), IL-18 (CSB-E04609m), and BDNF (CSB-E04505m) ELISA kits were operated according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunohistochemistry

Sections were obtained by referring to H&E staining methods. After antigen retrieval and blocking with 5% BSA at 37℃ for 90 min, Sections were incubated overnight with primary antibody BDNF (28205-1-AP, 1:100, Proteintech) at 4℃. Sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L). Sections were visualized using a light microscope (BA210T, Motic). Positive expression was quantified by calculating the ratio of the integrated optical density in positively stained regions to the total tissue area per field using Image Pro Plus software 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, USA).

Cell Counting Kit 8 (CCK-8) assay

Following the manufacturer’s protocol, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (NU679, Dojindo Laboratories, Japan) was added for 3 h. Cell viability was analyzed at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.).

Flow cytometry

The cells treated as mentioned above were digested with trypsin without EDTA, followed by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 5 min. The cell pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of binding buffer and then mixed with Caspase-1 and propidium iodide (PI) from the pyroptosis detection kit (ab219935, Abcam). After incubation for 30 min at 37℃ without light, pyroptosis was detected by flow cytometry (A00-1-1102, Beckman, China).

Statistics and analysis

All data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc., USA) was used for statistical analysis. Student’s t-test was used for comparison between the two groups. One‐way analysis of variance with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for multi‐group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05.

Results

Ovarian reserve function was reduced in CUS-induced mice

Firstly, the effect of CUS on the ovarian reserve function of mice was evaluated. Under CUS induction, the ovarian index was decreased (Fig. 1A). In the control group, normal distribution of follicles (including primordial follicles, primary follicles, secondary follicles, antral follicles ) and corpus luteum could be observed, while in the CUS group, the proportion of atretic follicles was increased, accompanied by abnormal structure of the granulosa cell layer (disordered arrangement, reduced number of cell layers, and blurred basement membrane structure) (Fig. 1B and C). Through 14-day consecutive vaginal smear tests, it was found that the estrus cycle in the CUS group was disordered, manifested as prolonged estrus and shortened proestrus (Fig. 1D), suggesting dysfunction of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. CUS induced an increase in serum CORT levels (Fig. 1E), suggesting that the continuous activation of the HPA axis might be a key pathway mediating reproductive injury. Mating experiment data showed that the pregnancy rate and embryo number in the CUS group were lower than those in the control group (Fig. 1F). Our findings indicate that ovarian reserve function is reduced in CUS-induced mice.

CUS induced reproductive hormone secretion disorders and pyroptosis of granulosa cells in mice

Then, CUS effects on reproductive hormone secretion and pyroptosis of granulosa cells in the ovaries of mice were evaluated. Gonadotropins (FSH, LH) levels in the serum of the CUS group were increased, and at the same time, the release of the sympathetic nervous system activity marker NE was increased, while the concentrations of AMH, E2, P, and T were lower than those of the control group (Fig. 2A). The apoptosis of ovarian granulosa cells in the CUS group was higher than that in the control group (Fig. 2B), accompanied by an increase in the levels of key components of the NLRP3 inflammasome (NLRP3, ASC, GSDMD-N) and inflammatory executive molecules (Cleaved-Caspase-1) and decrease in the pro-Caspase-1 level (Fig. 2C and D). IF detection showed that the number of FSHR-positive granulosa cells and Caspase-1 expression in the ovarian tissues of the CUS group were increased compared with the control group (Fig. 2E and F). Further cytokine testing confirmed that IL-18 and IL-1β levels were elevated in the CUS group (Fig. 2G). Our findings suggest that CUS induces reproductive hormone secretion disorders and pyroptosis of granulosa cells in the ovaries of mice.

CUS inhibited the activation of the BDNF/TrkB signaling pathway

Next, CUS influences on the BDNF/TrkB signaling pathway were discussed. The CUS model inhibited BDNF expression in brain tissue and peripheral blood (Fig. 3A). BDNF level in the ovarian tissue of the CUS group was lower than that of the control group (Fig. 3B and C). Furthermore, after CUS intervention, p-TrkB/TrkB, p-PI3K/PI3K, and p-AKT/AKT expression in ovarian tissue were inhibited (Fig. 3D). The above findings indicate that CUS induces the inactivation of the BDNF/TrkB signaling pathway.

Upregulating the BDNF/TrkB signaling pathway improved DOR in mice

To explore BDNF/TrkB signal effect on the progression of DOR in mice, we conducted relevant experiments using recombinant BDNF (rBDNF) and the TrkB antagonist ANA-12. rBDNF enhanced BDNF and p-TrkB/TrkB expression in the ovarian tissues of the CUS model. On the contrary, ANA-12 inhibited p-TrkB/TrkB expression in the ovarian tissues of the CUS model, but had no significant effect on BDNF expression (Fig. 4A). rBDNF treatment increased the ovarian index in CUS model mice, whereas ANA-12 administration reduced this index (Fig. 4B). In terms of the morphology of ovarian tissue, rBDNF increased the number of normal follicles (including primordial follicles, primary follicles, secondary follicles, antral follicles) and corpus luteum in CUS model mice and decreased the number of atretic follicles. Conversely, ANA-12 exacerbated the morphological alterations by further diminishing normal follicles and increasing atretic follicles in the CUS model (Fig. 4C and D). Vaginal smear tests for 14 consecutive days revealed that rBDNF restored the estrus cycle disorder induced by CUS, while ANA-12 induced estrus cycle disorder in CUS model mice (Fig. 4E). rBDNF reduced the elevated serum CORT level induced by CUS, while ANA-12 increased the serum CORT level in CUS model mice (Fig. 4F). Mating experiment data show that rBDNF increased the pregnancy rate and embryo number of CUS model mice, while ANA-12 reduced these two indicators (Fig. 4G). These results indicated that rBDNF can effectively restore the ovarian reserve function in CUS-induced mice, while TrkB antagonism further reduced the ovarian reserve function of CUS-induced mice.

In terms of serum hormone levels, rBDNF reduced FSH, LH, and NE levels in the serum of CUS model mice, while increasing AMH, E2, P, and T levels. The effect of ANA-12 was opposite to that of rBDNF, which promoted FSH, LH, and NE levels in the serum of CUS model mice, while inhibiting AMH, E2, P, and T levels (Fig. 4H). rBDNF inhibited ovarian granulosa cell apoptosis in CUS model mice (Fig. 4I), accompanied by a decrease in key components of the NLRP3 inflammasome (NLRP3, ASC, GSDMD-N) and the inflammatory executive molecule (Cleaved-Caspase-1) levels and an increase in the pro-Caspase-1 level (Fig. 4J and K). ANA-12 promoted ovarian granulosa cell apoptosis in CUS model mice (Fig. 4I), and simultaneously increased the above-mentioned inflammation-related indicators levels (Fig. 4J and K). IF detection showed that rBDNF could reduce the number of FSHR-positive granulosa cells and Caspase-1 expression in the ovarian tissues of CUS model mice. ANA-12 increased these indicators (Fig. 4L). Further cytokine testing confirmed that rBDNF downregulated IL-18 and IL-1β levels in the ovarian tissues of CUS model mice. ANA-12 increased these two cytokine levels (Fig. 4M). Our findings suggest that rBDNF has the effect of improving CUS-induced DOR in mice, reproductive hormone secretion disorders in the ovaries, and pyroptosis of granulosa cells, while ANA-12 promotes the occurrence and development of these pathological processes.

Upregulation of the BDNF-TrkB signaling pathway improved NE-induced pyroptosis of granulosa cells and disorders of sex hormone secretion

Then, the BDNF-TrkB signaling pathway effects on NE-induced pyroptosis of granulosa cells and sex hormone secretion were further investigated in vitro. Granulosa cells were pretreated with rBDNF at gradient concentrations (10, 100, 1000 ng/mL). It was found that when the concentration of rBDNF reached 1000 ng/mL, the cell viability decreased. However, 10 and 100 ng/mL rBDNF had no significant effect on cell viability (Fig. 5A). Based on this, 100 ng/mL rBDNF was selected as the intervention concentration in the subsequent experiments. In the NE-induced granulosa cell injury model, rBDNF enhanced the NE-decreased cell viability, while ANA-12 further intensified the inhibitory effect of NE on cell viability (Fig. 5B). rBDNF can promote NE-induced BDNF, p-TrkB/TrkB, p-PI3K/PI3K, and p-AKT/AKT expression. The intervention of ANA-12 would further inhibit the activation of the above-mentioned pathways (Fig. 5C and D). rBDNF inhibited NE-induced FSH and LH levels upregulation, while promoting the secretion of AMH, E2, and T. ANA-12 will further promote the NE-upregulated FSH and LH levels, while inhibiting the downregulated AMH, E2, and T levels (Fig. 5E). rBDNF inhibited NE-upregulated key components of the NLRP3 inflammasome (NLRP3, ASC, GSDMD-N) and the inflammatory executive molecule (Cleaved-Caspase-1) levels, and increased pro-Caspase-1 level, and reduced pyroptosis. ANA-12 enhanced the above-mentioned inflammatory factors expression and the degree of pyroptosis (Fig. 5F and I). Further cytokine testing confirmed that rBDNF downregulated IL-18 and IL-1β levels upregulated by NE. ANA-12 will further promote the release of these two pro-inflammatory factors (Fig. 5J). Our findings reveal that rBDNF improves NE-induced pyroptosis of granulosa cells and disorders of sex hormone secretion, while ANA-12 promotes the occurrence and development of these pathological processes.

The BDNF-TrkB signal inhibited NE-induced pyroptosis of granulosa cells and sex hormone secretion disorders by promoting CREB phosphorylation

CREB is a ubiquitous transcription factor that plays a key role in cell survival, proliferation, and differentiation, and it is known that the activation of the BDNF/TrkB signal leads to the phosphorylation of the transcription factor CREB [27]. Notably, when the ovaries of aged mice were treated with rBDNF, a significant increase in the protein levels of p-CREB and cyclin D1 was observed [10], which suggests the possible positive role of BDNF-TrkB-CREB signaling in maintaining ovarian function. Here, we found that NE treatment reduced p-CREB/CREB expression in granulosa cells (Fig. 6A). rBDNF intervention alleviated NE-caused cell viability decline, and this protective effect was blocked by the CREB antagonist KG-501 (Fig. 6B), suggesting the core position of CREB phosphorylation in this process. rBDNF can effectively reverse the inhibitory effect of NE on the BDNF/TrkB signaling axis through the activation of the TrkB receptor, which was specifically manifested as: increased phosphorylation of TrkB (p-TrkB/TrkB) level, the downstream PI3K/AKT pathway (p-PI3K/PI3K, p-AKT/AKT) activation, and enhanced phosphorylation of CREB (p-CREB/CREB). The introduction of KG-501 can eliminate the activation effect of the above-mentioned pathway (Fig. 6C and D), confirming that CREB phosphorylation is the key node of this signal transduction. rBDNF inhibited the upregulation of FSH and LH levels by NE, while promoting the secretion of AMH, E2, and T. This regulatory mode was revoked by KG-501 (Fig. 6E), suggesting the important role of the CREB signaling pathway in maintaining reproductive endocrine homeostasis. rBDNF inhibited key components of the NLRP3 inflammasome (NLRP3, ASC, GSDMD-N) and the inflammatory executive molecule (Cleaved-Caspase-1) levels upregulated by NE, and reduced pyroptosis. The introduction of KG-501 can eliminate these protective effects (Fig. 6F and I), further verifying the regulatory role of the CREB signaling pathway. Further cytokine detection confirmed that rBDNF inhibited the excessive secretion of IL-18 and IL-1β induced by NE. KG-501 eliminated these protective effects (Fig. 6J). Our findings indicate that the BDNF-TrkB signaling inhibits NE-induced pyroptosis of granulosa cells and sex hormone secretion disorders by promoting CREB phosphorylation.

Discussion

DOR is a multifactorial gynecological endocrine disorder that elevates the risk of poor ovarian response, diminishes the embryo implantation rate, and compromises the success rate of assisted reproductive technology [28]. Therefore, for patients seeking pregnancy, improving ovarian reserve is of vital importance. Here, we found that BDNF expression was decreased in the brain tissue, peripheral blood, and ovarian tissue of the CUS model, accompanied by a simultaneous decrease in the phosphorylation level of the TrkB receptor (p-TrkB/TrkB) and the phosphorylation level of key molecules of the downstream PI3K/Akt signaling pathway (p-PI3K/PI3K, p-AKT/AKT). Our results indicated that the BDNF-TrkB signal axis shows systemic functional deactivation under CUS. BDNF improved CUS-induced reproductive hormone secretion disorders in mice by activating TrkB, inhibiting pyroptosis of granulosa cells, and restoring ovarian reserve function. Mechanistically, the BDNF-TrkB signaling pathway inhibited NE-induced pyroptosis of granulosa cells and sex hormone secretion disorders by promoting CREB phosphorylation. Our findings suggest that TrkB agonists are a promising candidate drug for the prevention and treatment of DOR.

Under normal circumstances, gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) neurons in the hypothalamus can secrete GnRH in a pulsatile manner, regulating the secretion of FSH and LH cells in the pituitary gland, leading to the periodic secretion of estrogen and progesterone and affecting ovarian function [29]. Chronic stress increases stress mediators such as NE and adrenaline through the HPA axis, damaging the functions of the hypothalamus and ovaries [30]. BDNF plays a key role in stress-induced depression and Alzheimer’s disease [31]. However, its role in CUS-induced DOR is unknown. Here, we found that BDNF expression was decreased in the brain tissue, peripheral blood, and ovarian tissue of the CUS model, accompanied by a simultaneous decrease in the phosphorylation level of the TrkB receptor (p-TrkB/TrkB) and the phosphorylation level of the downstream PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, suggesting that the BDNF-TrkB signal axis plays an important role in CUS-induced DOR.

DOR reduces the number of available follicles remaining in the ovaries or causes a decline in the quality of oocytes, resulting in decreased reproductive potential and abnormal reproductive endocrine functions [32]. Excessive CORT can damage the growth and development of follicles and reduce oocyte quality [33]. Follicle number determines the onset of menstrual cycle disorders [34]. Adolescent cetylpyridinium chloride exposure decreased primordial follicle numbers, increased atretic follicles, induces granulosa cell apoptosis, potentially increasing the risk for the development of DOR [35]. Zihuai recipe administration elevated ovarian index and serum levels of AMH and E2, while lowering those of FSH and LH, increased the number of primordial, primary, secondary, and antral follicles as well as corpora lutea, but decreased atretic follicles, thereby alleviating DOR [36]. Under normal circumstances, the estrus cycle of female mice lasts for 4 to 5 days. Estrus cycle disorder is considered to be shorter than 4 days, longer than 6 days, or a single cycle lasting for more than 3 consecutive days. We found that rBDNF increased the ovarian index of CUS model mice, increased the number of normal follicles (including primordial follicles, primary follicles, secondary follicles, antral follicles), decreased the number of atretic follicles, restored the disordered estrus cycle, reduced the serum CORT level, and increased the pregnancy rate and embryo number. The TrkB antagonist ANA-12 showed a regulatory trend opposite to that of rBDNF. Our results suggest that upregulating the BDNF-TrkB signaling pathway regulates ovarian endocrine function and maintains ovarian reserve function, which is consistent with previous studies [37].

In clinical practice, ovarian reserve is routinely assessed through a combination of antral follicle count and serum sex hormone levels, including AMH, FSH, and E2 [38]. When DOR occurs, a notable feature is a sharp decrease in the number of antral follicles, which leads to a decline in E2 secretion and is accompanied by a compensatory increase in FSH level [39]. Granulosa cells are central to follicular development. On one hand, AMH, secreted by developing granulosa cells, exhibits relative stability and has been established as a crucial predictor of female fertility [40]. On the other hand, the proliferation and apoptosis status of granulosa cells directly determine the fate of the follicle [41]. Notably, FSH activates downstream signaling pathways by specifically binding to the FSHR on the granulosa cell surface, thereby finely regulating key mammalian reproductive processes such as oocyte maturation and follicular development [42–45]. Importantly, the research into DOR therapies has revealed new pathological mechanisms and potential intervention targets. In the DOR mouse model, inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated, Caspase-independent pyroptosis pathway within granulosa cells could effectively increase the number of surviving follicles, restore normal hormone levels, and ultimately improve ovarian function [46]. Here, we found that rBDNF increased AMH, E2, P, and T levels in the serum of CUS model mice, while reducing FSH, LH, and NE levels and apoptosis, accompanied by decreased key components of the NLRP3 inflammasome (NLRP3, ASC, GSDMD-N), inflammatory executive molecules (Cleaved-Caspase-1), IL-18, and IL-1β levels. In addition, rBDNF decreased the number of FSHR-positive granulosa cells and Caspase-1 expression in the ovarian tissues of CUS model mice. However, the ANA-12 effect on the above factors was opposite to that of rBDNF. Our findings suggest that upregulating the rBDNF-TrkB pathway improves the effects of CUS-induced reproductive hormone secretion disorders in the ovaries, pyroptosis of granulosa cells, and DOR. Notably, in the CUS-induced DOR mouse model employed in this study, we observed elevated FSHR expression in ovarian granulosa cells. This finding contrasts with some prior literature. For instance, Gao et al. reported reduced FSHR protein expression in a comparable CUS model [23]. However, FSHR expression changes in DOR are not unidirectional and are regulated by multiple factors. First, compensatory feedback mechanisms may contribute to this phenomenon. DOR reduces estrogen synthesis, potentially triggering increased pituitary FSH secretion as a compensatory response [47]. Since FSH could upregulate FSHR expression in granulosa cells, the elevated FSHR levels observed here may reflect ovarian adaptation to CUS. Second, FSHR expression exhibits significant time-dependency and estrous cycle-dependency. Regan et al. demonstrated dynamic fluctuations in murine ovarian FSHR protein levels throughout the estrous cycle, peaking during estrus and declining markedly in diestrus [48]. In this study, ovarian samples were collected without estrous cycle synchronization. Consequently, inter-individual variations in cycle stages at sampling may partially account for discrepancies between our results and previous reports. Additionally, etiological heterogeneity across DOR models is a critical factor. For example, Wang et al. found that dandelion extract improved ovarian function by upregulating granulosa cell FSHR expression in ovarian dysfunction patients [49], while Guner et al. reported significantly lower FSHR membrane localization in granulosa cells of advanced reproductive-age patients compared to controls [50]. These seemingly contradictory findings collectively suggest that FSHR expression and localization are influenced by multiple variables, including DOR etiology type, disease stage, therapeutic interventions, and detection methods (e.g., total protein, mRNA, or membrane-specific quantification).

CREB is essential in the proliferation and activation of ovarian granulosa cells [51]. Inhibiting CREB phosphorylation enhances the apoptosis of granulosa cells [52]. Activation of CREB phosphorylation promotes progesterone synthesis in granulosa cells [53]. Activation of cAMP/PKA/CREB improves ovarian function in rats with early-onset ovarian insufficiency by promoting steroid production in granulosa cells [13]. CREB accelerates the seasonal reproduction of dairy goats by regulating the synthesis of steroid hormones in ovarian granulosa cells [54]. Our results indicate that the BDNF-TrkB signaling pathway inhibits NE-induced pyroptosis of granulosa cells and sex hormone secretion disorders by promoting CREB phosphorylation.

There are still some limitations. We only focused on the BDNF-TrkB-CREB signaling pathway to improve DOR by inhibiting pyroptosis of granulosa cells and restoring disordered sex hormone secretion. Future research should explore other approaches to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms of pyroptosis and sex hormone secretion in DOR. In addition, developing TrkB selective agonists and conducting clinical trials in combination with hormone replacement therapy for stress-related reproductive disorders is the future research direction. In this study, single-cage housing was employed to isolate the interference of social interactions on the CUS model. However, single-cage housing itself might trigger mild stress responses (such as reduced activity levels and fluctuations in cortisol levels). While this practice represents a standard methodological approach in CUS paradigms, the potential confounding influence of isolation stress on neuroendocrine indicators warrants careful consideration. To more precisely delineate the specific impact of CUS from that of isolation, future investigations could benefit from incorporating a single-cage housing control group.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that the BDNF-TrkB signaling pathway inhibits NE-induced pyroptosis of granulosa cells and sex hormone secretion disorders by promoting CREB phosphorylation, thereby improving DOR in CUS mice. This discovery provides a new molecular target for the treatment of ovarian dysfunction related to neuroendocrine disorders. In particular, the intervention strategy targeting the regulation of the BDNF-TrkB-CREB axis has significant clinical translational value.
