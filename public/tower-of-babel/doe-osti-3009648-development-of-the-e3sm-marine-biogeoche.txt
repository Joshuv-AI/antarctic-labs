DE-SC0022177, "Development of the E3SM Marine Biogeochemistry for Studying Biogeochemistry-

9/1/2021-8/31/2025

 The overarching goal of this work was to improve the representation of marine biogeochemical
cycles and associated marine ecosystem dynamics in the E3SM to allow fuller representation of
potential Biogeochemistry-Climate feedbacks and interactions. We accomplished our major goals,
resulting in several high-impact, publications and a number of significant improvements to the
MARBL/E3SM codebase. Our first major goal was to incorporate dynamically varying nitrogen to
carbon ratios in the phytoplankton and to complete the development of a dynamic, variable elemental
stoichiometry in the phytoplankton and sinking particulate export fluxes. This builds on our
previous work optimizing the stoichiometry and mean lifetimes of the dissolved organic carbon, nitrogen,
and phosphorus pools (both semi-labile and refractory pools, Letscher et al., 2015; Moore and Letscher,
2015), and incorporating dynamic phosphorus to carbon (P/C) ratios in the plankton and sinking export
fluxes (Wang et al., 2019, funded by our previous DOE grant). A key aspect of this work is developing
phytoplankton group-specific parameter values that allow the model to reproduce the often unique
biogeochemical impacts of different functional groups. Along these lines we have developed a next
generation marine ecosystem model, with eight phytoplankton and four zooplankton groups. The
additional ecological complexity allows the model to represent organism-specific export efficiencies and
unique BGC impacts (Yu et al., 2025). Another part of this work is the continued development and
application of offline inverse models to help constrain key fluxes and optimize model parameters
(Wang et al., 2023). Another goal is to incorporate marine methane and nitrous oxide cycling into
the E3SM-MARBL framework, allowing for air-sea exchange. In our original proposal, we had also
hoped to begin exploring nutrient inputs from glacial processes in the E3SM. However, time and
resource limitations as well as the ongoing development of the ice-sheet interactions, prevented us from
working on this task.

 We accomplished the primary goals of this project. Below I describe our recent
resulting papers that nicely summarize our progress. The papers are divided into three groups
based on which PI was leading the efforts, though we all collaborated across these lines as well.

Global patterns of nutrient limitation governed by phytoplankton stoichiometry
Nicola A. Wiseman,1,3* J. Keith Moore,2 Robert T. Letcher4, Patrick Rafter5, Adam Fagan, Adam C. Martiny1

Abstract

Phytoplankton acclimate to increasing nutrient stress by decreasing their cellular quotas
(nutrient/carbon ratios). Reducing cellular quotas reduces the export efficiency of the limiting nutrient,
helping sustain biological productivity. Surface nutrient concentrations and global patterns of nutrient
limitation are a function of both the nutrient supply ratios and the elemental ratios in the exported organic
matter (which often differ, driven by variable phytoplankton stoichiometry). The weaker ability of
phytoplankton to acclimate to N stress by lowering their cellular quotas relative to other nutrients,
pushes marine ecosystems towards nitrogen limitation. Only when the nutrient supply ratios are highly
skewed, exceeding the ability of the phytoplankton to acclimate, do other nutrients become growth-
limiting, as with iron in the High Nitrate, Low Chlorophyll (HNLC) regions. Variable plankton
stoichiometry, given sufficient time, will push marine ecosystems towards nutrient co-limitation, as non-
limiting nutrient are more efficiently drawn down and exported (higher cellular quotas), relative to the
growth-limiting nutrient (lower quotas).

In this work, we present a new paradigm for the global patterns of nutrient limitation that control
phytoplankton growth rates in the oceans. The scientific literature widely attributes the observed
patterns of nutrient limitation to the stoichiometry of the inputs of key nutrients to surface waters. We
demonstrate that the stoichiometry of the biologically exported material is equally important in driving
the nutrient limitation patterns, which in turn determine the spatial patterns and magnitude of
carbon export by the ocean's biological pump. This work is the culmination of more than a decade of
model development in collaboration with field researchers to be able to accurately capture the
stoichiometric responses of phytoplankton as they acclimate to available resources (i.e., Martiny et al.,
2013; Wang et al., 2019; Wiseman et al., 2025). Below we list a number of the key findings and "firsts"
associated with this manuscript.

- Our model is the first to include fully dynamic, phytoplankton stoichiometry (N/C, P/C, Fe/C, and
Si/C) that accounts for phytoplankton acclimation to available nutrients, with realistic stoichiometric
ranges and mean quotas for all nutrients based on field observations.

- We demonstrate that the stoichiometry of the biologically exported material has first order impacts
on the patterns and degree of nutrient stress in the oceans. While previous work focused on the
stoichiometric ratios of nutrient inputs to surface waters. We show that both processes drive the
observed nutrient limitation patterns, this is a new paradigm for marine nutrient limitation.

- We show that dynamic stoichiometry will promote nutrient co-limitation given time, as the non-
growth limiting nutrients are exported more efficiently than the limiting nutrient. This helps explain the
expanding observations of nutrient co-limitation (Browning et al., 2023).

limited by nitrogen or phosphorus (Tyrell, 1999). We show that much of the ocean is nitrogen-limited

 Our second long-term goal was to develop a model that better represents plankton
community structure (for phytoplankton and their zooplankton grazers), focusing on key groups
with significant or unique impacts on marine biogeochemical cycling. We believe better
representing complex marine ecosystems will lead to improved simulation of marine biogeochemical
cycling, including key carbon and nitrogen fluxes with the potential to directly impact climate. Plankton
community structure will change in most regions with the warming climate.
 Beginning with the three phytoplankton group, one zooplankton group (3p1z) version of the
ecosystem model implemented in the E3SMv1.0 and CESM2.0, we have now developed two
expanded versions of the ecosystem model in collaboration with NCAR researchers (Matt Long,
Kristen Krumhardt, and Keith Lindsay). The first version includes four phytoplankton and two
zooplankton groups (4p2z), replacing the implicit calcifiers in the current model with an explicit
coccolithophore group (Krumhardt et al., 2019), and dividing the single zooplankton group into
microzooplankton and mesozooplankton groups. The second version (led by Moore and PhD graduate
student Jun Yu at UCI) includes eight phytoplankton and four zooplankton groups (8p4z). This is a next
generation ecosystem model, with more realistic biology, ecology, and biogeochemistry, than found
within any existing Earth System Models (ESMs). We published a paper that described the results
from an intermediate version of the model that had six phytoplankton and one zooplankton
group (Letscher et al., 2023). This included the initial development of three explicit picophytoplankton
groups discussed in the next section. We showed that despite their small size, these picophytoplankton
contribute significantly to export within the subtropical gyres.

 Our plan is to maintain both 4p2z and 8p4z ecosystem model versions and to furnish users with
recommended parameter settings for each. A major advantage of MARBL is that the code to run these
would be identical, only the input file user_nl_marbl is modified to switch between the 4p2z and 8p4z
configurations. The number of phytoplankton and zooplankton groups and their parameter values can
be set at run time by modifying the input files. The 8p4z is a next generation ecosystem model,
ideally suited to complement the high-resolution capabilities within MPAS (Yu et al., 2025).

Abstract
Current representations of marine ecosystems in Earth System Models are greatly simplified, neglecting
key interactions between dynamic food webs, biogeochemistry, and climate change. We use the Marine
Biogeochemistry Library code base within the Community Earth System Model 2.2.2 to create an
expanded ecosystem model with eight phytoplankton groups and four zooplankton size classes
(MARBL-8P4Z). Incorporating more specific plankton types and size classes has the potential to
capture a wider range of possible behaviors of the ecosystem, its complex interactions with
biogeochemistry, and its feedback to climate change. It also permits stronger observational constraints,
including in situ group-specific biomass and various observational estimates of plankton community
composition. MARBL-8P4Z broadly captures observed global scale patterns in biomass and community
composition for both phytoplankton and zooplankton, with a good performance in simulating broad
biogeochemistry fields. The model shows comparable spatial patterns and magnitudes to the observed
picophytoplankton biomass (Prochlorococcus, Synechococcus, picoeukaryotes), and captures the
seasonal cycle of mesozooplankton biomass. Picophytoplankton groups and microzooplankton
dominate biomass and production in oligotrophic, subtropical regions, while nano-phytoplankton,
diatoms and the larger zooplankton groups prevail at higher latitudes and within upwelling zones. The
model simulates reasonable energy transfer efficiency through the food web, with tight linkages
between the phytoplankton community composition, zooplankton grazing, and carbon export, with the
potential to link to fisheries models. Thus, MARBL-8P4Z has the potential to account for key climate-
driven ecological shifts in the plankton that will modify ocean biogeochemistry in the future.

Plain Language Summary
Earth System Models are essential tools for understanding marine ecosystems and how they might be
impacted by climate change. Current model representations of marine ecosystems are too simple to
reliably predict what might happen in the future. To address this, we develop a
more complex marine ecosystem model (MARBL-8P4Z), with eight types of phytoplankton and four size
classes of zooplankton. By incorporating these specific plankton types and sizes, the model can better
represent important ecological and biogeochemical processes. It also aligns more closely with real-
world observations of plankton biomass distributions. MARBL-8P4Z captures global patterns in biomass
and community composition for both phytoplankton and zooplankton, under current climate conditions.
This suggests the model can predict how plankton community composition and ocean biogeochemistry
will change in a warming world.

 In the paper we further show that the model captures high-latitude phytoplankton bloom
dynamics well, with diatoms dominating but also contributions by multiple nanophytoplankton-

" Observed declines in upper ocean phosphate-to-nitrate availability"
Authors: Skylar D. Gerace, Jun Yu, J. Keith Moore, Adam C. Martin

Abstract
Climate warming is increasing ocean stratification, which in turn should decrease the flux of nutrients to
the upper ocean. This may slow marine primary productivity, causing cascading effects throughout food
webs. However, observing changes in upper ocean nutrients is challenging because surface
concentrations are often below detection limits. The nutricline depth, where nutrient concentrations
reach well-detected levels, is related with productivity and indicates upper ocean nutrient availability.
Here, we quantified nutricline depths from a global database of observed vertical nitrate and phosphate
profiles to assess contemporary trends in global nutrient availability (1972 - 2022). We found strong
evidence that the P-nutricline (phosphacline) is mostly deepening, especially throughout the southern
hemisphere, but the N-nutricline (nitracline) remains mostly stable. Earth System Model simulations
support the hypothesis that reduced iron stress and increased nitrogen fixation buffer the nitracline, but
not phosphacline, against increasing stratification. These contemporary trends are expected to continue
in the coming decades, leading to increasing phosphorus stress but not nitrogen stress for marine
phytoplankton, with important ramifications for ocean biogeochemistry and food web dynamics.

 Earth System Models predict that ocean nutrients are decreasing from ocean warming with
severe impacts on marine life globally. However, direct observations have yet to support predictions
due to the low detectability of nutrients throughout the ocean surface. Here, we quantified the depths
where nitrate and phosphate reached well-detected concentrations through time over five decades. The
temporal trends of these depths revealed that upper ocean phosphate is mostly declining, while nitrate
is mostly stable. Using Earth System Models, we demonstrate that this difference is likely due to
decreasing iron stress for nitrogen fixation, which replenishes nitrate with increasing stratification. These
findings suggest that phosphorus limitation is expanding throughout the ocean, consequently bringing
many important implications for marine ecosystems.

UCI-co-PI Francois Primeau led efforts to use inverse models to constrain current-era
biogeochemical fluxes as well as changes in circulation/ventilation

As part of the DOE-funded effort to improve representation of the marine biological carbon pump and
associated transport processes in Earth System Models (ESMs), Yi Liu and Francois Primeau advanced
both methodological and scientific understanding through two complementary studies: a data-
constrained global inverse model of ocean biogeochemistry (Wang et al. Nature, 2023) and a transient
ventilation/transport analysis using CESMv1 (Liu and Primeau, JPO, 2024). Together, these papers
demonstrate the feasibility and importance of rigorous parameter calibration frameworks—incorporating
observations, inverse methods, and numerical optimization—to reduce uncertainty in key ocean
biogeochemical and physical processes within prognostic ESMs. Yi Liu is a former UCI PhD student
who worked extensively with Moore and Primeau as co-advisors, and was funded by DOE. She is now
at a successful postdoctoral position at Princeton.

1. Global Biological Pump Constraints from Inverse Modeling (Wang et al., Nature, 2023)
This study constructs a global inverse model of the marine biological carbon pump by synthesizing
available hydrographic datasets (World Ocean Atlas and GLODAPv2). Using numerical optimization
and a data-constrained representation of the climatological mean circulation of the ocean, the work
estimates remineralization rates, export fluxes, oxygen utilization, and associated carbon sequestration
efficiencies.
Key outcomes include:
 • A baseline, observation-consistent estimate of the strength, spatial structure, and
 remineralization length scales of the global biological carbon pump.
 • Quantification of regional variations in biological export and oxygen utilization that ESMs
 frequently misrepresent.
 • A rigorous demonstration of how inverse models can constrain uncertain biogeochemical
 parameters—such as remineralization length scales, stoichiometric ratios, and export
 production profiles—in ways that directly inform ESM parameterization choices.
Relevance to the DOE mission:
The results provide an empirical benchmark for calibrating biological carbon pump parameterizations in
ESMs. Because the DOE grant specifically targets reducing uncertainty in biogeochemical controls on
carbon sequestration, this inverse-model baseline is essential: it supplies physically consistent,
observation-anchored targets that can be used to objectively evaluate and tune forward ESMs.
Furthermore, this work gave the team valuable experience applying advanced numerical optimization
methods to large-scale ocean biogeochemical inverse problems, building DOE-relevant capabilities for
future parameter calibration pipelines. We used the P/C ratios in sinking POM estimated from this work
to help develop the dynamic stoichiometry model. These estimated carbon fluxes are further

2. Ventilation Patterns and Timescales in a Warming Ocean (Liu and Primeau, JPO, 2024)
The paper addresses a complementary problem: how surface-to-interior and interior-to-surface
transport timescales evolve in a fully transient, climate-warming circulation. The authors develop an
offline tracer-transport framework using annually varying transport operators from CESMv1 (1850–
2300), enabling the first fully time-dependent computation of ocean transit-time distributions (TTDs)
without assuming stationarity.
Major findings:
 • Climate-driven weakening of NADW and AABW formation reduces the fraction of global ocean
 waters ventilated at high latitudes by ~2.1% by 2300, with compensating increases from
 subantarctic regions.
 • The globally averaged mean age (surface→interior) increases by ~110 years (∼17%) between
 1850 and 2300 due to stratification and MOC slowdown.
 • Mean first-passage times (interior→surface) depend strongly on post-2300 circulation
 trajectories, introducing an unavoidable uncertainty in long-term carbon sequestration durability
 and CDR interventions.
 • The framework clarifies the mathematical relationship between ideal age tracers and full TTD
 moments in time-dependent flows, identifying conditions under which age tracers in ESMs
 correctly reflect true ventilation timescales.
Relevance to the DOE mission:
Ventilation time scales govern the residence time of remineralized nutrients and respired carbon in the
deep ocean, and thus the efficiency of the biological pump under climate change. This study provides:
 • A quantitative basis for evaluating how changes in circulation alter sequestration efficiency—
 critical for DOE-supported carbon cycle and CDR assessments.
 • A methodological foundation for calibrating and interpreting ESM ideal-age and TTD-based
 diagnostics so that they can be linked to measurable ventilation rates.
 • A demonstration of adjoint-based diagnostic tools that can be extended toward formal parameter
 calibration and uncertainty quantification in DOE ESMs.

Integrated Significance for Parameter Calibration in ESMs
Together, the Nature and JPO papers advance DOE goals in three key ways:
 1. Establishing a data-constrained baseline for the biological pump.
 The inverse model quantifies global export production, remineralization profiles, and oxygen
 utilization from observations. These serve as ground-truth targets for tuning biogeochemical
 parameters that govern carbon sequestration in DOE ESMs.
 2. Developing and exercising computational tools for parameter optimization.
 Both studies rely on large-scale numerical optimization, forward–adjoint transport operators, and
 Green’s-function-based diagnostics. These tools are directly transferrable to formal calibration
 frameworks for uncertain ESM parameters.
 3. Clarifying how physical transport uncertainties propagate into biological pump behavior.
 The transient ventilation analysis shows how changing overturning circulation alters
 sequestration efficiency and interior-to-surface return times. This allows model developers to
 incorporate realistic bounds on transport-related uncertainties when tuning biogeochemical
 process parameters.

UNH-Co-PI Robert Letscher led efforts to incorporate methane and nitrous oxide cycling into the
MARBL framework

Subaward to University of New Hampshire, PI Robert Letscher. Project postdoc Riyanka Roy
Chowdhury.

Goal: Add two additional explicit tracers to the MARBL code base, N2O and CH4, which are known to
have water column and bottom boundary source fluxes, and gas disequilibria with the atmosphere,
leading to air-sea gas exchange. These additions contribute to the state of the art found within the
MARBL model, giving it novel functionality to cycle two additional greenhouse gases within the ocean
interior and exchange with the atmosphere.

Approach: The MEMENTO database provides global compilations of both marine N2O and CH4
concentration observations. Data density is sparse–machine learning approaches were used to map
these tracers onto 1º, 3D annual climatologies to be used as the initial conditions for simulations of
CESM-MARBL containing explicit representation of these two greenhouse gases (CESM-MARBL-
GHG), as well as for model validation.
 Governing equations for marine N2O and CH4 were encoded based on known source and sink
processes for these gases. For N2O, source fluxes arising from water column nitrification and
denitrification are represented. For CH4, source fluxes include dissolved organic phosphorus
remineralization as well as a bottom boundary (sediments) source flux modeled as a function of the rain
rate of sinking particulate organic carbon hitting the seafloor and the bottom water oxygen
concentration. Sinks for both gases include slow 1st order kinetic oxidation rates and air-sea exchange
at the sea surface.
 Model parameter calibration was achieved by building offline versions of the marine N2O and
CH4 model, utilizing the saved information on the tracer transport fluxes from the POP ocean model in
the form of a matrix, coupled with data assimilation of the MEMENTO database to optimize the uncertain
parameter values. These were then prescribed in the online CESM-MARBL-GHG with additional fine
parameter tuning performed to achieve simulated output that best matched the MEMENTO and
machine learning derived data product observational constraints.

Addition of macromolecular marine DOM cycling to the Marine Biogeochemistry Library
(MARBL)
by Robert T. Letscher and J. Keith Moore

Abstract
The differential cycling of marine macromolecules, such as dissolved carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids
play a fundamental role in marine microbial metabolisms, ultimately regulating the ocean’s capacity to
sequester carbon downwards via the biological pump or move carbon up the food chain, supporting
marine food webs. To date, their representation is global scale models of marine biogeochemistry and
ecosystems is lacking. Here we add explicit representation of marine macromolecular cycling for
dissolved polysaccharides, lipids, amino polysaccharides, and proteins, within the dissolved organic
matter pools of the Marine Biogeochemistry Library (MARBL) ecosystem model, implemented within
the ocean circulation model of the Community Earth System Model. The resulting dissolved
macromolecule distributions identifies polysaccharide and amino polysaccharide accumulation within
the upper ocean of the subtropics owing to longer lifetimes than dissolved lipids and proteins which are

the marine ecosystem.
