Decision making at the front door
Authors: Andrew Duncombe
Published: 2026

When you receive a letter in your inbox on a complex issue that you know requires your expert and experienced input, what do you do? Do you give it your undivided attention and deal with it straightaway? Or do you sometimes consign it to a digital limbo land in the vain hope you will one day return to it when you have more energy or hope that one of your less experienced team members on the Cc list eventually takes it on due to your inaction? I think we instinctively know that either of these last pathways will result in a slower response and poorer outcome. So it is crucial that we also design our healthcare delivery systems around early senior physician decision making at the front door. Nowhere is this more important than in urgent and emergency care (UEC). Step forward the acute medical unit (AMU).

I am delighted to welcome Anika Wijewardane as our guest editor for this themed issue on AMUs. Anika is a passionate visionary advocate for these units and for clinicians to lead them to deliver the high-quality urgent care that we want patients to receive. This themed issue highlights best practice, the importance of leadership, digital infrastructure, point-of-care testing and patient-centred care, and presents a vision for the future. I strongly recommend reading Anika’s guest editorial in which she discusses all these in more detail, signposting to our expert contributors1. Elsewhere in this issue, we demonstrate the breadth of content of Future Healthcare Journal with submitted articles on healthcare systems, clinical bioinformatics, postgraduate training programmes, quality improvement reports and correspondence.

The transition to digital is one of the UK government’s three pillars to transform the NHS. So there is clearly a need for expansion in training of skilled specialist bioinformaticians and this was the recommendation of the Topol Review. Pruski3 asks why this hasn’t happened, despite funding being provided. Worse, there is evidence of poor retention of those completing training, perhaps linked to underappreciation of these specialists. The author asks whether failure of uptake by NHS organisations is due to lack of knowledge or reservations about their value. Either way, this seems to me to represent a major organisational failure of a healthcare system, particularly in hospital-based medicine, which is desperately in need of better digital connectivity and innovation that needs urgent corrective action.

Staying with healthcare organisation, does it often seem to you that financial resources seem unfairly and arbitrarily divided among clinical services? Mohammed et al4 propose that the Nobel prize-winning Shapley value, derived from cooperative game theory, may offer a principled approach to cost allocation within healthcare systems. In this model, costs and benefits are allocated based on each party’s contribution, which encourages collaboration towards common goals. As global health systems move away from fragmentation and competition towards greater collaboration, the authors argue that the Shapley value shows promise and should be explored further in real-world settings.

How would you rate your training in adolescent and young adult health? If you answered that it was absent or minimal, you would be in the majority. Fernandes et al5 report a large UK national survey of resident doctors and supervisors, concluding that little progress has been made on this in the last 10 years. Over 30% had received no training, fewer than 20% had attended transition clinics and awareness of national guidance was very low at 5%. They suggest integration into curricula, structured clinic access and mandating core competencies as steps forward to address these deficits.

Future Healthcare Journal has a proud history of championing well-designed quality improvement (QI) projects in clinically relevant areas, especially by less experienced authors as an introduction to peer-reviewed academic publication. We are pleased to showcase three such examples in this issue.

How confident do you feel in assessing the frailty of one of your older patients you are referring for significant surgery? Geriatricians in Leicester have piloted a perioperative medicine for older people undergoing surgery (POPS) service for patients referred with lower limb vascular pathology and high Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) score.6 Their QI project demonstrated improvement in CFS accuracy by the referring doctors through a structured teaching programme and use of a mobile application, so enabling accurate identification of patients to the POPS team.

Do you do regular routine arterial blood gas (ABG) measurements on your sick patients? Previous studies have shown that unnecessary routine blood testing is associated with patient and environmental harm and increased costs. Anderson et al7 demonstrate that transition from routine ABG testing to indication-based testing in an intensive care unit in New Zealand led to significant reduction in ABG testing and improved sustainable value without causing harm to patients. This was achieved through a Sustainability in Quality Improvement (SusQI) project involving a guideline, education programme, visual cues and audit and feedback. Impressively, the impact has been sustained for 2 years post-intervention. This methodology can clearly be applied more widely to help us reduce unnecessary blood tests and so reduce patient harm and healthcare costs.

Speed of access to life-saving specialist services in tertiary centres should clearly not depend on initial patient location – yet it frequently does, with delays having potentially fatal consequences. Establishment of specialist clinics in non-specialist centres is clearly one reasonable approach to this problem. Chotalia et al8 demonstrate the efficacy of such a solution for transcatheter valve implantation (TAVI), where establishment of a local TAVI clinic in a non-TAVI centre significantly shortened the time to TAVI from over 7 months to less than 3 months; a wait similar to those patients already at the TAVI centre. Furthermore, the local clinic reduced aortic stenosis-related hospitalisations. As with the previous QI report, this model is potentially more widely applicable and could facilitate entry to many other specialist services that patients currently struggle to access effectively.

We welcome your correspondence relating to our published content and we feature a particularly relevant letter to our recent issue on symptom-based disorders. If we are to manage symptoms effectively, we must be able to describe and record them accurately. Boland et al9 describe a practical tool; the 5WH framework (Who, What, When, Where, Why, How), which enables a structured assessment for all clinical symptoms. Using a common example symptom of emesis, they demonstrate how this can provide a comprehensive, holistic picture that can inform management plans as well as avoiding harmful interventions.
