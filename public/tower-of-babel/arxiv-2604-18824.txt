Abstract
. Given n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 , we study the existence of a tree on n n vertices whose independence polynomial is symmetric and unimodal as well as the existence of a symmetric and unimodal independence polynomial of degree n n of a tree.

Introduction

Every graph considered in this paper is finite and simple. For a graph
G
, let

g_{i}(G)
denote the number of independent sets of
G
of cardinality
i
. The

independence polynomial
of
G
is

P_{G}(x)=\sum_{i\geq 0}g_{i}(G)x^{i}.

Equivalently, independence polynomials are the face polynomials of independence
complexes of graphs, and are therefore closely related to the study of face vectors of flag
complexes. They have been studied extensively from several points of view

[
11
,
6
,
7
,
4
,
8
]
in graph theory.

In 1987
[
1
]
, Alavi, Malde, Schwenk, and Erdős conjectured that the independence
polynomials of trees are unimodal. As of April 2026, the conjecture remains open in general, and has been computationally verified for trees on at most
29 vertices
[
15
]
. Motivated by this problem, we study
the existence of trees whose independence polynomials are symmetric and unimodal.

A finite sequence
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}
of nonnegative integers is called

symmetric
if
a_{i}=a_{d-i}
for each
0\leq i\leq d
. A symmetric sequence

a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}
is called
unimodal
if
a_{0}\leq a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}
. A polynomial
P(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+\cdots+a_{d}x^{d}

with
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
is called
symmetric
(resp.
unimodal
)
if its coefficient sequence is symmetric (resp. unimodal).

More precisely, we address two existence questions. First, for which integers
n
does
there exist a tree on
n
vertices whose independence polynomial is symmetric and
unimodal? Second, for which integers
d
does there exist a tree whose independence
polynomial is symmetric and unimodal of degree
d
?

Our method combines two ingredients. The first is a new gluing construction for rooted
graphs, introduced in the
Bridge Lemma
(Lemma
1
).
This lemma allows us to construct larger rooted trees from smaller ones while preserving
the structure needed in our arguments. The second is the
\gamma
-positivity viewpoint
for symmetric polynomials. If
h(x)
is symmetric with center of symmetry
d/2
, then
it admits a unique expansion

h(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\gamma_{i}x^{i}(1+x)^{d-2i}.

When all
\gamma_{i}
are nonnegative, the polynomial
h(x)
is said to be

\gamma
-positive. This viewpoint provides a convenient route to unimodality; see

[
2
]
. In the body of the paper, we adapt it to rooted trees through the
notion of
\gamma
-admissibility.

The notion of
\gamma
-positivity appeared first in the work of D. Foata and M. Schützenberger
[
9
]
and it is extremely useful as it directly implies symmetry and unimodality. This application of
\gamma
-positivity appears widely in combinatorial and geometric contexts (see
[
3
,
5
,
12
,
14
,
16
]
).

The main result of the present paper is the following.

Theorem 0.1
.

(1)
Given
n\geq 1
with
n\notin\{2,4,5,7,10\}
, there is a tree on

n
vertices whose independence polynomial is symmetric and unimodal.

(2)
Given
d\geq 1
with
d\neq 3
, there is a tree whose independence
polynomial is symmetric and unimodal of degree
d
.

1.
Bridge Lemma

In this section, we introduce a new technique to construct symmetric independence polynomials of trees. Given a graph
G
and a vertex
r\in V(G)
, the
rooted graph

(G,r)
is the graph
G
with vertex
r
viewed as a root. Each vertex of
G
can be chosen as a root.

Definition 1.1
.

Given two rooted graphs
(G,r)
and
(H,s)
, the
bridging
of
(G,r)
and
(H,s)
, denoted by
(G,r)\vee(H,s)
, is the rooted graph with
r
its root which is obtained from the disjoint union of
(G,r)
and
(H,s)
by adding the edge
\{r,s\}
.

.

Bridging is not necessarily associative. Let
(A,r),(B,s)
, and
(C,t)
be rooted graphs given in
Figure

.

(A,r),(B,s)
and

((A,r)\vee(B,s))\vee(C,t)\neq(A,r)\vee((B,s)\vee(C,t))
as shown in

To obtain symmetric and unimodal independence polynomials, we use
\gamma
-positivity (see
[
2
]
. for a survey on
\gamma
-positivity). Although
\gamma
-positivity is
usually introduced for symmetric polynomials, for our purposes it is convenient to adopt
the following equivalent formulation. Set

y=\frac{x}{(1+x)^{2}}.

Definition 1.3
.

A polynomial
h(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x]
is called
\gamma
-positive
if there exist an
integer
d\geq 0
and nonnegative real numbers

\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}
such that

h(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\gamma_{i}x^{i}(1+x)^{d-2i}.

Equivalently,
h(x)=(1+x)^{d}~\Gamma_{h}(y)

where
\Gamma_{h}(y)=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\gamma_{i}y^{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[y]
.

The following is well-known in the literature but we include it here for completeness.

Lemma 1.4
.

If
h(x)
is
\gamma
-positive, then
h(x)
is symmetric and unimodal. If, in
addition,
h(0)\neq 0
, then
\deg h(x)=d
.

Proof.

Since the substitution
x\mapsto 1/x
leaves
y
invariant, we have

x^{d}h(1/x)=x^{d}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{x}\Bigr)^{d}~\Gamma_{h}\left(\frac{1/x}{(1+1/x)^{2}}\right)=(1+x)^{d}~\Gamma_{h}(y)=h(x).

Thus
h(x)
is symmetric with center of symmetry
d/2
.

Moreover, each polynomial
x^{i}(1+x)^{d-2i}
has coefficient sequence obtained from
that of
(1+x)^{d-2i}
by padding with
i
zeros at the beginning and at the end.
Hence each
x^{i}(1+x)^{d-2i}
is symmetric and unimodal with center of symmetry
d/2
.
Since
h(x)
is a nonnegative linear combination of these polynomials, it follows that

h(x)
is unimodal.

Finally, if
h(0)\neq 0
, then
\gamma_{0}=h(0)>0
. Thus the term

\gamma_{0}(1+x)^{d}
occurs with positive coefficient. Therefore
\deg h(x)=d
.
∎

Remark 1.5
.

Let
G
be any finite simple graph and
H=G\circ 2K_{1}
be the graph obtained by joining each vertex of
G
to two new vertices. It is known that
H
has a symmetric independence polynomial
[
17
,
10
]
. Moreover, it was shown in
[
13
]
that the independence polynomial of
H
is unimodal. It can also be easily verified that
P_{H}(x)
is
\gamma
-positive. This follows from observing that

P_{H}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\alpha(G)}g_{i}x^{i}(1+x)^{2n-2i}

where
|V(G)|=n
and
g_{i}
is the number of independent sets of size
i
in
G
.

We now introduce the notion of
\gamma
-admissibility for rooted graphs.

Definition 1.6
.

A rooted graph
(G,r)
is called
\gamma
-admissible
if there exist an integer

d\geq 0
and polynomials
A(y),B(y)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[y]
such that

P_{G}(x)=(1+x)^{d}A(y),\qquad P_{G-r}(x)=(1+x)^{d}B(y).

Example 1.7
.

Let
R_{3}=P_{3}
be rooted at its middle vertex
r
. Then
(R_{3},r)
is
\gamma
-admissible since

P_{R_{3}}(x)=1+3x+x^{2}=(1+x)^{2}(1+y),\qquad P_{R_{3}-r}(x)=1+2x+x^{2}=(1+x)^{2}

where
A(y)=1+y
and
B(y)=1
such that

A(y)-B(y)=y\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[y]
.

Remark 1.8
.

If
(G,r)
is
\gamma
-admissible, one has

x^{d}P_{G}(1/x)=P_{G}(x),\qquad x^{d}P_{G-r}(1/x)=P_{G-r}(x).

This implies that both
P_{G}(x)
and
P_{G-r}(x)
have degree
d
since independence polynomials have constant term
1
. Since
P_{G}(x)
and
P_{G-r}(x)
are symmetric of degree
d
, by the standard theory of
\gamma
-positivity for symmetric polynomials (see
[
2
]
), there are unique coefficients
a_{0},\dots,a_{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
b_{0},\dots,b_{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

such that

A(y)=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}a_{i}y^{i},\qquad B(y)=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}b_{i}y^{i}.

Therefore,
P_{G}(x)
and
P_{G-r}(x)
are

\gamma
-positive. Hence, both are symmetric and unimodal by
Section

1
.

The following result, which we refer to as
Bridge Lemma
, is useful in creating symmetric and unimodal independence polynomials for larger graphs from smaller ones.

Lemma 1.9
.

(Bridge Lemma)

Let
(T,r)
and
(U,s)
be
\gamma
-admissible with

P_{T}(x)=(1+x)^{d}A(y),\qquad P_{T-r}(x)=(1+x)^{d}B(y),

P_{U}(x)=(1+x)^{e}C(y),\qquad P_{U-s}(x)=(1+x)^{e}D(y),

where
A(y),B(y),C(y),D(y)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[y]
. Suppose that
A(y)-B(y)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[y]
.

If
(W,r)=(T,r)\vee(U,s)
, then

(W,r)
is
\gamma
-admissible.

Proof.

By the standard recursion at the root
r
, we have

P_{W}(x)=P_{W-r}(x)+xP_{W-N[r]}(x).

Since
W-r=(T-r)\sqcup U
and
W-N[r]=(T-N[r])\sqcup(U-s)
,
we obtain

P_{W}(x)=P_{T-r}(x)P_{U}(x)+xP_{T-N[r]}(x)P_{U-s}(x).

Since
xP_{T-N[r]}(x)=P_{T}(x)-P_{T-r}(x)
,
we have

P_{W}(x)=P_{T-r}(x)P_{U}(x)+\bigl(P_{T}(x)-P_{T-r}(x)\bigr)P_{U-s}(x).

Substituting the
\gamma
-admissible expressions gives

P_{W}(x)=(1+x)^{d+e}\bigl(B(y)C(y)+(A(y)-B(y))D(y)\bigr),

and

P_{W-r}(x)=P_{T-r}(x)P_{U}(x)=(1+x)^{d+e}B(y)C(y).

Since
B(y),C(y),D(y)
, and
A(y)-B(y)
all belong to
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[y]
,
it follows that
(W,r)
is
\gamma
-admissible.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Given
d\geq 1
with
d\neq 3
, there is a tree
T
such that

is symmetric and unimodal with
\deg P_{T}(x)=d
.

Proof.

We first show that degree
3
is impossible. Suppose that
T
is a tree on

vertices such that

is symmetric and

. Then

P_{T}(x)=1+nx+g_{2}x^{2}+x^{3}.

Since
P_{T}(x)
is symmetric, we must have

. On the other hand, a tree
on
n

n-1

of cardinality
2
is

g_{2}=\binom{n}{2}-(n-1)=n,

that is,
n^{2}-5n+2=0,

which has no integer solution. Thus there is no tree
T
such that

P_{T}(x)
is symmetric and
\deg P_{T}(x)=3
.

For

, take

. Then

. Now let

with

.

If

is even, write

with

. Starting from

and

1
using

-copies of
(R_{3},r)
, we obtain a tree whose independence

.

If

, one may take
G_{8},G_{11},G_{14},G_{17}
, respectively.

Finally, let

. Starting from

(R_{19},r)

Closing Remarks

A supplementary catalogue accompanying this paper records the finite computations behind the small-order counts. It lists every nonisomorphic tree on at most
21
vertices whose independence polynomial is symmetric (and unimodal), together with the corresponding independence polynomial. It also records, for each
\gamma
-admissible tree, the factorization

P_{T}(x)=(1+x)^{d}A(y),\qquad P_{T-r}(x)=(1+x)^{d}B(y),\qquad y=\frac{x}{(1+x)^{2}},

for every admissible root.
