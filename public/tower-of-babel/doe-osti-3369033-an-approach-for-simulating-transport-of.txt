ABSTRACT
This paper extends the particle Green’s function approach of Gallis et al. [Phys. Fluids 13,
3482–3492 (2001)] to compute the force and heat transfer on nonspherical, sublimating particles
in rarefied gas flows. Green’s functions are derived for rods and plates, which are representative of
ice particle shapes, and a numerical procedure is developed for computing the Green’s functions
for a general discretized convex shape. Additionally, a sublimation model is developed to simulate
the evolution of ice particles in rarefied gas flows. These models are implemented in a direct
simulation Monte Carlo code and demonstrated for the case of ice-particle-laden hypersonic flow
over a ramp.

1. INTRODUCTION

The motion of low-earth-orbit satellites, Martian atmospheric entry, plumes on Io, and flight
through high-altitude clouds are all examples of particle-laden rarefied flows. Consequently,
accurately simulating these flows requires a model that accounts for both rarefied gas phenomena
and locally free molecular particulates. The goal of this work is to develop such a model. We focus
herein on the case of high-altitude clouds but expect that our approach will generalize to some of
the other aforementioned applications.
Hale et al. [1] noted that uncertainty in the state of the atmosphere at high altitudes created a
persistent difficulty for planning the descent trajectory of the space shuttle. In particular, flight
trajectories were purposefully adjusted to avoid noctilucent clouds since the potential damage to
the shuttle caused by traversing these clouds is not well understood. Noctilucent clouds are found at
altitudes between 75–85 km, where the gas-molecular mean free path is on the order of millimeters.
By contrast, the size of the ice particles in these clouds are on the order of 10–102 nm [2–5].
The flow around the ice particles is thus well into the free-molecular regime, indicating that the
drag forces on them are minuscule. This is problematic because it means that ice particles can
strike vehicles at high speeds, which may erode or roughen their surfaces and damage instruments
or optical windows. Therefore, accurate tools and analysis that enable quantifying risk posed by
noctilucent clouds is essential for high-speed flight at high altitudes.
Previous work in this area includes approximating the crater size due to hypervelocity particle
impact [6], estimating particle mass flux to thin-bodies in inviscid hypersonic flow [7], and extending
the analysis to include possible particle breakup [8]. In parallel, several experiments were carried out
to characterize vehicle surface erosion due to particle impacts [9, 10]. Later, several groups utilized
one-way coupled models (only gas-to-particle interactions are resolved) to compute the distribution
of impact velocities for a wide range of vehicle geometries and freestream conditions [11–15]. Two-
way coupled models (both gas-to-particle and particle-to-gas interactions are resolved) have been
used in cases where the particle number density is relatively high or the particle Knudsen number is
not large [16–19]. Several models have been developed that also consider particles undergoing phase
change. For example, Burt and Boyd [20] considered liquefaction and solidification of alumina
particles with constant mass, and Palmer et al. [18] proposed a decoupled model for vaporization
of silicon dioxide particles in which either the particle’s mass or temperature changes.
The purpose of this work is to extend the particle Green’s function approach developed by Gallis
et al. [21] to model nonspherical ice particles in hypersonic flows. The particle Green’s function
approach computes the gas-to-particle drag force and heat transfer by integrating over the local
molecular velocity distribution and is thus well suited to implementation using the direct simu-
lation Monte Carlo (DSMC) method [22]. The approach is valid so long as the flow around the
particle is locally free-molecular but has thus far been limited to spherical particles. However,
ice particles are often highly nonspherical [23], so force and heat transfer expressions developed

 11

for spherical particles may not be accurate for ice particles. Therefore, we first generalize the
Green’s function approach by providing explicit formulae for disk- and rod-shaped particles, which
are both common ice particle shapes [23], and by outlining a numerical procedure for arbitrary
convex particles. Second, we introduce a coupled approach for modeling particle sublimation. The
modified algorithm is implemented in SPARTA, Sandia’s massively parallel open-source DSMC
solver [24] and demonstrated by computing ice-particle-laden hypersonic flow over a wedge.

2. FORCE AND HEAT TRANSFER GREEN’S FUNCTIONS FOR
 NONSPHERICAL PARTICLES

The essential idea of the particle Green’s function approach introduced by Gallis et al. [21] is
that when the flow around the particle is locally free-molecular, collisions between incoming
molecules and molecules reflected from the particle surface are sufficiently rare that the particle
does not significantly alter the local molecular velocity distribution. In that case, one can consider
separately the force and heat transfer due to all molecules with velocity relative to the particle 𝒄, i.e.,
a delta-function velocity distribution. The resulting expressions are referred to as the force and heat
transfer Green’s functions. Integrating the Green’s functions over the local velocity distribution
then yields the total force on and heat transfer to the particle.

The principal advantage of the particle Green’s function approach is that it applies to any molec-
ular velocity distribution and thus does not require any assumptions about the flow being near
equilibrium. Because the DSMC method can provide molecular velocity distributions and is valid
arbitrarily far from equilibrium, implementing the particle Green’s function approach in a DSMC
code allows one to compute the force and heat transfer on particles in highly nonequilibrium
settings, such as those encountered in high-altitude hypervelocity flight.

To develop the Green’s functions for nonspherical particles, we first provide general formulae for an
infinitesimal element of the particle’s surface with area d𝑆. Then, we integrate the formulae over the
surfaces of a disk and a cylinder to obtain the Green’s functions for disk-like and rod-like particles,
which are common ice particle shapes and serve as analytical references for the numerical procedure
to evaluate the Green’s function for an arbitrary convex surface. Note that convexity of the particle
surface is an important restriction since it precludes reflected molecules from re-colliding with
the surface, which would violate the assumption that all incident molecules are drawn from the
background local velocity distribution. While many ice particles are essentially convex [23], some,
such as dendrite crystals, are not. Therefore, the error introduced by approximating such particles
as convex should be considered. Such an error analysis is beyond the scope of this work.

The present approach is similar to that by Dahneke [25], who also developed formulae for the
force on nonspherical convex bodies in free-molecular flow. However, that approach assumes
(i) the particle moves through a uniform gas and hence restricts the molecular velocities to being
the Maxwell–Boltzmann-distributed, and (ii) the particle velocity is small relative to the mean
molecular speed. By contrast, the present approach relaxes these two assumptions as is appropriate
for particle-laden hypersonic flows.

In practice, this limits the range of particle shapes that can feasibly be considered to those having at
most 𝑁 𝑠 = 𝑂 (102 ). Thus, to broaden the scope of particle shapes that can be practically considered,
we employ a shape simplification algorithm to reduce the number of particle surface elements. The
basic idea is to map the set of elements of the original surface, denoted 𝐸 = 𝐸 ( 𝒙ˆ 𝑛 , Δ𝑆𝑛 ), to a new
set 𝐸 ′ = 𝐸 ′ ( 𝒙ˆ ′𝑛 , Δ𝑆′𝑛 ) of representative elements such that the total surface area is conserved. 𝐸 ′
should be chosen such that for any element in 𝐸, there exists at least one element in 𝐸 ′ for which
𝒙ˆ 𝑛 · 𝒙ˆ ′𝑛 > 0.

A simple choice for 𝐸 ′ is the set of elements defined by distributing points on the unit sphere.
We consider two common approaches to doing so. The first is to sample uniformly spaced points
from a Fibonacci spiral [28, 29]. However, this approach is undesirable because it can break
symmetries of the original surface. The second approach, which we use herein, samples points
from a longitude-latitude grid [30, 31]. The grid consists of two orthogonal meridians and 𝑁di𝑣
parallels. The parallels are separated from each other and from the poles by an angle 𝜋/(𝑁di𝑣 + 1).
The vectors 𝒙ˆ ′𝑛 are the unit-normal vectors at all intersections on the grid. Evidently, 𝑁di𝑣 should
be chosen such that the total number of points 𝑃 = 4𝑁di𝑣 + 2 < 𝑁 𝑠 .

Although all realistic particles are three-dimensional, for illustrative purposes we describe the shape
simplification procedure in detail for the much simpler two-dimensional case. In two dimensions,
this amounts to placing 𝑃 = 4𝑁di𝑣 uniformly spaced points on the unit circle, as illustrated in
Fig. 2-1(a) for 𝑁di𝑣 = 2, 3. The area Δ𝑆′𝑛 of each element in 𝐸 ′ is initialized to zero. Each element
in 𝐸 is mapped to the element in 𝐸 ′ that maximizes the product 𝒙ˆ 𝑛 · 𝒙ˆ ′𝑛 . If several 𝒙ˆ ′𝑛 maximize
this product exist, then one of them is chosen at random. The area of the chosen element in
𝐸 ′ is then incremented by Δ𝑆𝑛 . Fig. 2-1(b) shows the mapping from a hexagonal particle to a
representative square one for 𝑁di𝑣 = 1. In Fig. 2-2, the final representative surfaces of a spherical
particle are shown for different values of 𝑁di𝑣 . Herein, we refer to surfaces resulting from the shape
simplification procedure as simplified surfaces, which will include a subscript to indicate the value
of 𝑁di𝑣 used.

3. PARTICLE SUBLIMATION

At altitudes relevant to noctilucent cloud formation, ice particles passing through a shock are
exposed to high temperatures but low pressures, suggesting that shock-induced sublimation may
occur. Models accounting for sublimation of silicon-dioxide particles have been proposed [6,
18]. These models assume that the particle temperature increases to the vapor temperature 𝑇𝑣 (the
wet-bulb temperature) [35, 36], after which excess heat causes the particle to vaporize. Because
the particle energy equation does not include an evaporative cooling term, these models tend to
overestimate the rate at which the particle temperature increases.

To more accurately couple the particle mass and energy equations, we begin with the evaporating
droplet model developed by Crowe [37]. We make the following assumptions:

(1) The background humidity is zero (no supersaturation or deposition).

(2) The particles only sublimate (no liquid phase).

(3) The particles do not agglomerate.

(4) The particle temperature and density are uniform.

We justify assumption (1) by noting that the ice-particle solid fraction in noctilucent clouds is
extremely small (≲ 10−12 ). Consequently, the amount of water vapor generated sublimating
particles is also small. Additionally, water vapor molecules also have short residence times in
high-speed flight, preventing the water volume fraction from increasing appreciably. Since the
humidity is nearly zero, there is no mechanism for condensation or deposition, meaning that we can
ignore the ice-particle mass accumulation. Likewise, we ignore water molecules created particle
sublimation.

We justify assumption (2) by noting that at altitudes relevant to noctilucent cloud formation, the
gas pressure tends to be much lower than the triple-point pressure. In cases where the gas pressure
is high enough for the liquid phase to exist, a more complex model should be implemented [38,
39].

We justify assumption (3) by noting that because the ice-particle number density in noctilucent
clouds is on the order of 1–103 cm−3 [2, 5, 40], the frequency of particle-particle collisions is also
low.

Assumption (4) is equivalent to assuming that heat conduction within the ice particles is infinitely
faster than the heat exchange rates (due to gas-molecule collisions and sublimation) at the particle
surface. Therefore, it is justifiable only when the thermal diffusion time 𝑑 2𝑝 /𝛼, where 𝛼 is the
particle thermal diffusivity, is much smaller than the particle temperature rise time.

4. RESULTS

4.1. Ice particle sublimating in a hot gas

To assess the present sublimation model, we simulate single spherical ice particles sublimating in a
hot gas. The gas number density is fixed at 𝑛 = 1019 m−3 , and the gas temperature and velocity are
set to the post-shock state using the Rankine–Hugoniot relations for Ma∞ ∈ {5, 10, 15, 20, 25} and a
freestream temperature of 𝑇∞ = 200 K. The initial particle temperature and velocity are 𝑇∞ and 𝑈∞ ,
respectively, such that the particle velocity relative to the freestream is initially 𝑣rel = 𝑣 𝑝 − 𝑈∞ = 0.
The particle diameter and mass density are 𝑑 𝑝 = 37.5 nm and 𝜌 𝑝 = 917 kg m−3 , respectively.
Fig. 4-1 shows the particle relative velocity, temperature, and mass as a function of time. The relative
velocity is normalized by 𝑈∞ , the temperature by 𝑇∞ , and the mass by the initial particle mass 𝑚 0 .
While the particle velocities do not change dramatically, the particle temperatures rise rapidly. The
particle temperatures reach a constant value once the energy lost due evaporative cooling is balanced
by the gas-to-particle heat transfer. Note that the steady-state particle temperature is not fixed in
these simulations. The plot of particle mass shows that particles do not experience significant mass
loss until they reach the steady-state temperature. As expected, the particle temperature reaches
its steady-state value more quickly and loses mass more rapidly with increasing the Mach number.
These results are consistent with those reported by Kersey et al. [45], who found that the momentum
transfer to the particles is small, but the energy transfer is significant.
The fastest particle temperature rise time shown in Fig. 4-1 is ∼10−7 s, whereas using a thermal
diffusivity for ice of 𝛼 ≈ 2 × 10−6 m2 s−1 [46] gives a thermal diffusion time of ∼10−9 s. This
justifies Assumption (4) described in Ch. 3 for the conditions of interest.

along with the particle streamlines, which are colored by the instantaneous particle mass. Unlike
previous studies, the particle properties are updated during the simulation. For Ma∞ = 5, the

deflection, which is expected since the drag forces are small. The rod trajectories exhibit slightly
more deflection. For Ma∞ = 25, all particle trajectories show even smaller deflections, and more
particles that enter the shock layer ultimately reach the vehicle surface. However, the particles’ mass
loss is significant in the shock layer. Near the ramp’s leading edge, the particles’ lose approximately
40% of their initial mass before reaching the surface, whereas they lose approximately 75% of their
initial mass near the trailing edge.
The particle speeds are evaluated along the line segment between the points (0 m, 0.04 m) and
(2 m, 0.39 m) and plotted in Fig. 4-3. This line is about 4 cm above the surface. As discussed
above, the particles retain high speeds since the drag forces are small. Near the leading edge,
the particles have not slowed noticeably, whereas the particle speeds are slightly reduced near the
trailing edge. While the impact speeds are large for the Ma∞ = 25 case, note that the particles have
also lost more mass. On the latter half of the surface, the total particle momentum is fairly similar.
At 𝑥 = 2 m, the particle momentum for Ma∞ = 25 is only ∼20% larger than that for Ma∞ = 5.
Comparing different particle shapes shows that the spheres have the lowest impact speeds, and the
cubes have the highest impact speeds. In particular, at 𝑥 = 2 m, the cube impact speed is 13.6%
percent higher that of the sphere, indicating that particle shape can have a significant influence on
particle impact behavior.

5. DISCUSSION

We have extended the particle Green’s function method introduced by Gallis et al. [21] to non-
spherical, sublimating particles. A shape simplification procedure was also proposed to make
simulations of complex, multi-faceted shapes more computationally feasible.
The approach was demonstrated by simulating Mach 5 and 25 flow with ice particles over a ramp.
Both spherical and nonspherical particles tend to retain high speeds, and their trajectories exhibit
minimal deflection. This suggests that nanoparticle hypervelocity impacts remain a possibility
even at Mach 25. While our simulations used argon as the working gas, it is likely that ice particles
would still reach the vehicle surface if a more realistic gas mixture was used.
While the present model captures relatively complex physics, there remains room for further devel-
opment. Notably, all simulations described herein were one-way coupled. While the momentum
flux to the gas phase will likely be small, ice particles may rapidly absorb heat from the gas. Kersey
et al. [45] showed that evaporative droplets absorb a non-negligible amount of heat from the shock
even for low mass loadings. Additionally, water molecules resulting from particle sublimation
were neglected. In a non-reacting gas, the additional water molecules would have a negligible
effect due to their low mole fraction. However, these water molecules could complicate gas phase
chemistry, particularly that relevant for ablating carbon-based thermal protection systems, which
form hydrocarbons. Therefore, including particle-to-gas interactions may be important [17, 33, 49].
Going further, one could develop a fully coupled model by including particle-particle interactions.
Since the ice particle number density is small for the conditions of interest, these interactions could
possibly be modeled using an approach similar to that for granular gases [50].
Note that the model described herein can be extended to model other aerosols in rarefied gases, such
as dust in the Martian atmosphere. However, the model becomes invalid when the particle Knudsen
number is small enough that the flow can no longer be considered locally free-molecular. In this
case, the flow around the particles must be resolved. One approach to study these hypervelocity
impacts for near-continuum particles is to use a moving-surface algorithm [51]. Both the fully
coupled model and moving surface simulations are the subject of ongoing work.
