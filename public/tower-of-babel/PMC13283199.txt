WNT4 reprograms dental pulp stem cells to resist PANoptosis and rebuild neurogenic potential for facial nerve injury repair
Authors: Wanqiu Xu, Hualei Xi, Song Lin, Lihong Yao, Jian Sun, Bing Xue, Guiyan Piao, Ying Liu, Wenjing Sun, Xiumei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective and designThis study investigated the protective role and molecular mechanism of WNT4 in preserving the viability and neurogenic potential of dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) within an inflammatory microenvironment following facial nerve injury (FNI).Material or subjectsHuman DPSCs (hDPSCs; authenticated by surface marker profiling and multi-lineage differentiation) and a rat model of facial nerve transection (n = 15 per group) were utilized.TreatmentIn vitro, hDPSCs were treated with TNF-α(10 ng/mL) to model inflammation. In vivo, WNT4-overexpressing or control DPSCs (1 × 106 cells) were injected into the tail vein immediately after nerve transection.MethodsPANoptosis and neurogenic differentiation were assessed by Western blot, flow cytometry, and immunofluorescence staining. Transcriptional regulation was analyzed by dual-luciferase reporter assay and ChIP-qPCR. Protein interaction was examined by LC–MS/MS and docking. In vivo recovery was monitored by functional assessments and histology.ResultsWNT4 overexpression significantly attenuated TNF-α-induced PANoptosis in hDPSCs and restored their neurogenic potential. Mechanistically, KLF7 transcriptionally activated WNT4. WNT4 interacted with NOTCH1, activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling and upregulating c-Jun, CYCD1, and VEGFA. In vivo, engineered hDPSCs reduced local inflammation and accelerated functional recovery and histological repair.ConclusionsWNT4 protects DPSCs against inflammatory PANoptosis and enhances neuroreparative potential, providing a basis for optimizing stem cell therapy in FNI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00011-026-02296-5.

Introduction

Facial nerve injury (FNI) is a common disease in stomatology and is often caused by trauma or improper maxillofacial surgery. Among them, idiopathic facial nerve palsy is the most common and is highly likely to cause functional and aesthetic abnormalities such as mastication difficulties and eyelid closure difficulties [5]. At present, surgery is still the main clinical treatment method [10, 14], and the prognosis of patients is often poor.

In recent years, with the continuous development of regenerative medicine, stem cell therapy has become an important means to overcome many medical problems, including nerve injury diseases [9]. However, although stem cells can nest at the site of injury [24], in pathological microenvironments such as inflammation, stem cells exhibit decreased multidirectional differentiation ability, high immunogenicity, aging and even mass death, resulting in unsatisfactory therapeutic effects [4, 12, 27, 35]. Previous studies have shown that there is a serious immune imbalance and inflammatory cascade in the local microenvironment after facial nerve injury. Therefore, the current focus and difficulty of using stem cells to treat FNI lies in how to resist the death of stem cells in the inflammatory microenvironment and how to improve the neurogenic potential of stem cells.

Studies have shown that dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) have great advantages in the treatment of neurological diseases [25, 40]. First, compared with mesenchymal stem cells from other sources, DPSCs originate from the neural crest and can express a variety of neural markers and have greater neurogenic potential. Second, DPSCs are generally obtained from pulp extracted from human third molar teeth and orthodontic teeth, which can address two major challenges in clinical translation: limited accessibility and insufficient cell numbers. In addition, DPSCs have increased safety and anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory abilities [19, 31], which can strongly guarantee the maintenance of a good neural regeneration microenvironment.

PANoptosis is an inflammatory form of programmed cell death with key characteristics of pyroptosis, apoptosis and necroptosis [37]. Previous studies have focused mainly on a small number of tumors and bacterial or viral infectious diseases [16, 29]. Recent studies have shown that PANoptosis also affects the occurrence and development of various neurological diseases [33, 34]. DPSCs are seed cells used for the treatment of nervous system diseases, and their viability strongly affects their therapeutic ability. Therefore, exploring whether DPSCs undergo PANoptosis in the inflammatory microenvironment and further identifying therapeutic means to prevent PANoptosis in DPSCs are expected to provide a new direction for overcoming nervous system diseases. However, at present, there are no relevant reports.

Wingless-type MMTV integration site family member 4 (WNT4) is a WNT ligand with context-dependent signaling capacity, capable of engaging both canonical and non-canonical pathways depending on cellular context [3, 7, 22]. WNT4 regulates stem cell multilineage differentiation and is implicated in neuroinflammatory processes [13]. The Wnt/β-catenin pathway critically regulates stem cell pluripotency and tissue repair. Whether WNT4 inhibits PANoptosis and restores neurogenic potential in DPSCs via Wnt/β-catenin activation remains elusive.

This study focused on changes in the local microenvironment after FNI and explored the ability of WNT4-modified DPSCs to promote the regeneration of facial nerve axons and myelin, improve the local inflammatory microenvironment, and thus accelerate the recovery of facial nerve tissue and function in the context of inflammation. Furthermore, we investigated the upstream transcriptional regulation of WNT4 by KLF7 and the downstream mechanism whereby WNT4-NOTCH1 interaction activates Wnt/β-catenin signaling thereby inhibiting PANoptosis and enhancing neurogenic potential in DPSCs. These results provide additional theoretical support for the clinical application of DPSCs in FNI treatment.

Results

WNT4 is associated with the neurogenic differentiation of DPSCs in an inflammatory microenvironment and the repair of injured facial nerves

First, human primary DPSCs were extracted and identified (Fig. 1A–D). We found that under TNF-α stimulation at 10 ng/ml, the neurotropic differentiation ability of hDPSCs was inhibited, and the neural markers NESTIN, neuron-specific enolase (NSE) and Tubulin beta 3 class III (TUBB3) were significantly decreased (Fig. 1E, F). Through data analysis, we found that WNT4 is related to the neural differentiation of hDPSCs (Fig. 1G) and further analyzed its functions (Fig. 1H). GO enrichment analysis revealed that WNT4 was widely localized in cellular structures such as the "neuron body" and "postsynaptic membrane". It also has the biological function of "regulating mesenchymal stem cell differentiation". KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that WNT4 was closely related to "axon guidance" and other signaling pathways. After neural differentiation of hDPSCs, WNT4 protein expression and mRNA levels were significantly increased (Fig. 1I, J). RT-qPCR analysis further revealed that the mRNA levels of neurotrophic factors NGF, BDNF, and NTF3 were significantly upregulated during neural differentiation of hDPSCs, providing additional evidence for the neurogenic potential of DPSCs (Fig. 1J). However, when hDPSCs were continuously stimulated with 10 ng/ml TNF-α, WNT4 protein expression levels continuously decreased at 24, 72 and 120 h (Fig. 1K, L). In addition, the protein expression level of WNT4 in injured facial nerve tissues was significantly lower than that in normal facial nerve tissues (Fig. 1M, N). Overexpression of WNT4 reshaped the neurogenic potential of DPSCs and inhibited their PANoptosis in the inflammatory microenvironment

Improving the neurotropic differentiation ability of DPSCs in an inflammatory microenvironment is crucial for enhancing their efficacy in the treatment of nervous system diseases. hDPSCs overexpressing WNT4 were constructed. Compared with those in the NC group, the protein expression levels of NESTIN, TUBB3 and NSE in the WNT4 group were significantly increased in response to neuron-induced differentiation medium and 10 ng/ml TNF-α (Fig. 2A,B). The immunofluorescence results were consistent with the WB results, and the fluorescence intensities of NESTIN and TUBB3 in the WNT4 group were significantly greater than those in the other groups, and the volume of neural spheres formed was greater (Fig. 2C). Moreover, overexpression of WNT4 increased the mRNA expression of neurotrophin 3 (NTF3), nerve growth factor (NGF) and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) in the inflammatory microenvironment of dental pulp stem cells (Fig. 2D). Overexpression of WNT4 increased the mRNA expression of the anti-inflammatory factors IL-4 and IL-10 in hDPSCs and inhibited the mRNA expression of the proinflammatory factors IL-6 and IL-1β (Fig. 2E). Inflammation can affect the viability of DPSCs. PANoptosis of DPSCs in the inflammatory microenvironment is an important reason for the decreased viability of DPSCs. After hDPSCs were continuously stimulated with 10 ng/ml TNF-α for 48 h, the overexpression of WNT4 significantly reduced the protein expression levels of the PANoptosis markers cleaved-GSDMD/GSDMD, cleaved-caspase 3/caspase 3, RIPK1, cleaved-caspase 1/caspase 1, p-MLKL/MLKL and RIPK3 (Fig. 3B, C). The results of the immunofluorescence experiments were consistent with the Western blot results. Overexpression of WNT4 reduced the fluorescence intensity of cleaved caspase-3 and RIPK3 in hDPSCs. Moreover, it disrupted the plasma membrane translocation of GSDMD and MLKL and inhibited the formation of cleaved GSDMD and p-MLKL (Fig. 3A). In addition, flow cytometry results revealed that overexpression of WNT4 inhibited early apoptosis, late apoptosis, and necrosis of hDPSCs in the inflammatory microenvironment (Fig. 3D, E). The results of TUNEL staining were consistent with those of flow cytometry (Fig. 3F, G). The results of the CCK8 assay revealed that the OD value of the OE group was significantly greater than that of the NC group on day 3 (Fig. 3H). WNT4 interaction with NOTCH1 activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and regulates downstream targets

In the context of inflammation, the nuclear translocation of the β-catenin protein decreased after the neural differentiation of hDPSCs. The overexpression of WNT4 restored the nuclear translocation of the β-catenin protein. Treatment with ICG-001 (20 μM, 12 h), a specific inhibitor of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, reduced the protein expression of β-catenin in the nucleus (Fig. 4A, B). The results of the immunofluorescence assay were consistent with those of the Western blot analysis (Fig. 4C). Moreover, WNT4 overexpression up-regulated mRNA levels of the Wnt/β-catenin target gene Jun proto-oncogene (c-Jun) and vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), while down-regulating G1/S-specific Cyclin D1 (CYCD1) transcription.Pretreatment of the cells with 20 μM ICG-001 significantly reversed these effects (Fig. 4D). Among these genes, VEGFA was identified as a key gene for the neural differentiation of DPSCs in our previous study. Activation of the classical Wnt signaling pathway is typically mediated by a series of cascading reactions initiated by the binding of WNT ligand proteins to membrane-bound receptors. To identify receptors that interact with the WNT4 ligand, we performed CoIP/MS experiments to identify and analyze proteins that interact with WNT4 via mass spectrometry. A Venn diagram revealed that in an inflammatory microenvironment, the OE group presented 437 unique WNT4-binding proteins compared with the NC group (Fig. 4E). Subsequent functional analysis, with a focus on proteins enriched in the GO categories of the biological processes (BPs) “regulation of stem cell differentiation”, “tissue regeneration”, and “peripheral nervous system neuron axon regeneration”, revealed that only NOTCH1 was concurrently enriched in these three BPs (Fig. 4F). NOTCH1, a well-characterized transmembrane protein, is localized to the cell membrane (Fig. 4G). Immunofluorescence colocalization experiments confirmed the colocalization of WNT4 and NOTCH1 within the cytoplasm and cell membrane (Fig. 4H). Molecular docking experiments indicated the possibility of a stable interaction between WNT4 and NOTCH1 (Fig. 4I).

Transcription factor KLF7 positively regulates WNT4 transcription and expression

We used the UCSC database to predict transcription factors that may bind to the WNT4 promoter region (Fig. 5A). Among these genes, KLF7 is highly expressed in the central and peripheral nervous systems and is closely related to the growth and regeneration of neuronal axons and dendrites. Accordingly, KLF7 protein rose markedly in neurogenically induced dentalpulp stem cells (Fig. 5B, C). We searched the JASPAR database for KLF7 binding site sequences (Fig. 5D) and further predicted potential KLF7 binding sites in the WNT4 promoter region (Fig. 5E). Dual-luciferase reporter assays revealed that the activity of WNT4 promoters 1-3 was elevated in the KLF7 group than in the NC group, and the activity of the WNT4-3 promoter was the highest (Fig. 5F). ChIP-qPCR quantitatively demonstrated that KLF7 bound to the WNT4 promoter region from -1032-1040 bp, -1425-1433 bp, and -1573-1581 bp (Fig. 5G‒I). Additionally, siRNA-mediated knockdown of KLF7 reduced WNT4 mRNA levels (Fig. 5J) and significantly decreased WNT4 protein expression (Fig. 5K, L). WNT4-modified hDPSCs promote facial nerve repair in rats

Research has shown that stem cells have a homing ability, with intravenous injection being more effective than local injection for treatment [24]. The exact transection site and segment length of the buccal branch of the facial nerve as well as its relationship to adjacent anatomical landmarks are shown in Fig. 6A. After facial nerve injury (FNI) models were induced, the whisker angle on the injured side markedly increased (Fig. 6B). hDPSCs overexpressing WNT4 and labeled with GFP were injected via the tail vein. Postoperatively, these cells homed to the injured facial nerve site. Eight weeks later, the facial nerve tissue structure of the Lv-WNT4 group was well preserved without tissue adhesions (Fig. 6C). H&E staining revealed better preserved nerve morphology and less inflammatory infiltration in the Lv-WNT4 and Lv-Ctrl groups than in the FNI group, with the Lv-WNT4 group performing best (Fig. 6D). Discussion

Compared with the central nervous system, facial nerves can regenerate after injury [1]. However, as they are in the face, patients expect higher recovery standards. Current treatments for facial nerve injuries are limited and do not meet patients’ expectations. Our previous studies revealed that DPSCs are effective for facial nerve repair [32]. However, DPSC functions, such as inflammation, ischemia, and hypoxia, are easily affected by the local pathological microenvironment [2, 20]. After facial nerve injury, the local microenvironment is flooded with inflammatory cells and factors, inhibiting the therapeutic effects of DPSCs and causing their mass death. To solve this problem, we transfected DPSCs with the WNT4 gene. WNT4 is implicated in regulating cell growth and differentiation and modulating cell death pathways. WNT4 reduces LPS-induced dental pulp apoptosis and inflammation [18]. Additionally, WNT4 protects mouse islet microvascular endothelial cells from oxidative stress-induced apoptosis [28, 30]. Our results demonstrated that WNT4 overexpression inhibited TNF-α-induced DPSC apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis. Additionally, WNT4 overexpression activates the JNK1 signaling pathway, promoting odontoblastic differentiation of DPSCs [39]. WNT4 also enhances the osteogenic potential of inflamed DPSCs for treating rat bone defects [38]. Our data indicated that WNT4 overexpression upregulated the neural markers Nestin, TUBB3, and NSE in hDPSCs under inflammatory conditions, suggesting a partial restoration of neurogenic differentiation potential.

A favorable neural regeneration microenvironment is critical for the directional regeneration of injured nerves. Neurotrophic factors provide essential support for the differentiation and growth of neural cells [11]. Our study revealed that WNT4 overexpression in hDPSCs upregulated the mRNA expression of neurotrophic factors. Moreover, WNT4 overexpression enhanced the expression of anti-inflammatory factors while inhibiting proinflammatory factors. This helps balance immunity and inflammation in the regeneration microenvironment, reducing tissue adhesion and fibrosis.

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is a key regulator of stem cell pluripotency and tissue repair [26]. We hypothesize that WNT4 exert its effects through this pathway. As a secreted glycoprotein, WNT4 requires binding to cell membrane receptors such as LRP5, LRP6, and the FZD family [23, 36]. Our results suggest that WNT4 interacts with the transmembrane protein NOTCH1, promoting β-catenin nuclear translocation and regulating key downstream genes such as VEGFA, c-Jun, and CYCD1. While these findings are consistent with a mechanistic link, direct binding remains to be validated by biophysical approaches such as surface plasmon resonance (SPR) or cryo-EM structural analysis. This finding is consistent with those of previous studies. Furthermore, NOTCH1 is abundant in neural tissue and serves as a key regulator of stem cell proliferation and differentiation [6]. Membrane-bound NOTCH1 undergoes two enzymatic cleavages, exposing the intracellular domain of NICD1. NICD1 induces β-catenin nuclear translocation, modulating Wnt/β-catenin signaling [8]. We further explored the upstream molecular mechanisms regulating WNT4 expression and revealed that the key transcription factor KLF7 binds to the WNT4 promoter region, promoting WNT4 transcription and thereby influencing its expression. KLF7 is highly expressed in the developing central and peripheral nervous systems and promotes the growth of neuronal axons and dendrites. Transplantation of SCs and BMSCs transfected with KLF7 can promote axon and myelin formation in injured sciatic nerves, accelerating sciatic nerve regeneration [28, 30].

To investigate the therapeutic effects of WNT4-modified DPSCs on FNI, we established a classic animal model of facial nerve injury-the rat facial nerve buccal branch transection model. Local administration delivers a high concentration of cells to the target area. However, simple cell suspension injection for FNI often results in significant stem cell leakage, poor retention at the lesion site, and short survival time, frequently necessitating scaffold materials that introduce additional experimental variables and increase tissue trauma. Intravenous administration, by contrast, leverages the intrinsic inflammatory homing capacity of DPSCs, enabling circulating cells to accumulate specifically at the injured nerve region via systemic circulation, with minimal invasiveness, sustained therapeutic effects, and no requirement for scaffold assistance [17, 24]. Our data confirmed that DPSCs can home to the injured facial nerve site. It has been reported that WNT4 is involved in the occurrence and development of various neurological diseases. WNT4 directly participates in axon guidance and axon growth [21]. Transplantation of WNT4-modified neural stem cells can significantly promote functional recovery after SCI [15]. After 8 weeks of treatment, the WNT4-overexpressing DPSCs accelerated facial nerve myelination and nerve fiber regeneration, increased the expression levels of nerve markers, and inhibited local inflammation in the facial nerve, thereby promoting the functional and tissue recovery of hDPSCs in the treatment of facial nerve injury.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that WNT4 protects hDPSCs against inflammatory PANoptosis and enhances their neuroreparative potential in FNI. We recognize that limitations remain, including the absence of a direct comparison between local and intravenous injection and the lack of long-term functional assessment. We look forward to exploring these questions in our ongoing and future investigations to further refine this therapeutic approach.
