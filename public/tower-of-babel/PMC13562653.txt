Identifying hot-spot pathways in fishery science and technology innovation through temporal heterogeneous graph neural networks
Authors: Fengwei Zhang, Xiaolin Wang, Jing Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurately identifying “hot-spot pathways” in fishery science and technology (S&T) innovation is critical for food security, economic development, and ecological sustainability. Traditional technology foresight methods struggle to capture complex, dynamic evolutionary patterns in S&T innovation networks. Drawing on Dosi’s conceptual framework of technological trajectories as domain-inspired design heuristics-whereby Dosi’s qualitative concepts provide structural guidance for model design rather than formal axioms that exhaustively capture the theoretical framework-this study proposes DTH-GNN (Documents-based Temporal Heterogeneous Graph Neural Network), integrating Graph Neural Networks with dynamic evolutionary analysis to identify potential hot-spot pathways. We construct a dynamic heterogeneous knowledge graph from multi-source data (2010-2024) encompassing 32,847 publications, 8,956 patents, and 1,856 projects. DTH-GNN combines an R-GCN-based heterogeneous encoder with a GRU-based temporal evolution module, achieving AUC = 0.934 and AP = 0.928 (after rigorous leakage assessment), significantly outperforming GCN, R-GCN, and EvolveGCN baselines. Information-theoretic analysis indicates that temporal features account for a substantial share of mutual information in link prediction (31.8%, 95% CI: [28.4%, 35.1%]), comparable to structural features (29.1%) and higher than attribute features (19.7%). Three high-potential pathways are identified and validated through expert evaluation (Krippendorffś α = 0.804): Smart Aquaculture, Green Seed Industry, and Ecological Fisheries. These findings provide data-driven scientific support for S&T investment prioritization in the fishery sector.Keywords: Fishery science and technology, Hot-spot pathways, Temporal heterogeneous graph, Graph neural networks, Mutual information analysis, Link prediction, Technology foresightSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Fisheries constitute a vital primary industry underpinning national food security, regional economic development, and marine ecological balance1. Entering the third decade of the twenty-first century, global fisheries face unprecedented compound challenges: traditional capture fisheries suffer continuous resource decline owing to overfishing and climate change; intensive aquaculture brings environmental pollution, recurrent disease, and germplasm degradation; and consumers increasingly demand greater product quality, safety, and nutritional value, imposing ever-higher requirements for industry-wide modernization2,3. In this context, S&T innovation is widely regarded as the fundamental driving force for breaking through fishery development bottlenecks and leading industrial transformation.

Fishery S&T innovation ecosystems are, however, complex, dynamic, and highly uncertain processes. It spans basic research, technology development, technology transfer, and market application, and involves multiple innovation actors-universities, research institutes, enterprises, and government agencies-operating collaboratively4. Within this complex innovation ecosystem, S&T resources are finite. Scientifically anticipating technological trends and precisely identifying and positioning “hot-spot pathways” with disruptive potential capable of driving industrial transformation has therefore become a major challenge for research managers and policymakers5.

Traditional qualitative analyses and simple quantometric methods exhibit manifest limitations in addressing this challenge6. First, expert experience cannot process massive multi-source heterogeneous data and is prone to cognitive bias. Second, static text-mining methods fail to capture the dynamic evolutionary patterns of technology networks. Third, existing network-analysis approaches typically assume networks are homogeneous and static, ignoring both the heterogeneity and the time-varying nature of fishery innovation networks. These considerations motivate the introduction of frontier artificial intelligence technologies. Specifically, Graph Neural Networks (GNN) have become ideal tools for processing graph-structured data and capturing complex inter-entity relationships7,8. The development of temporal GNNs further makes it possible to simultaneously model network heterogeneity and dynamic evolution9,10.

The present study transcends traditional qualitative analyses and simple quantometric approaches by incorporating frontier AI techniques to construct an analytical framework capable of objectively and dynamically revealing the internal logic and future directions of fishery S&T innovation. This not only enhances the scientific precision of S&T management but also stimulates collaborative innovation across industry, academia, and research, ultimately accelerating China’s fisheries toward a knowledge-intensive, resource-conserving, and environmentally friendly modern industrial paradigm.

Literature review

Identifying S&T hot-spot pathways requires methods that can simultaneously handle three challenges: data heterogeneity, temporal dynamics, and domain specificity. We review existing approaches through the lens of which challenges they address and which they leave unresolved, revealing a convergent gap.

Bibliometric and patent-analysis methods represent the dominant paradigm for technology trend identification. Keyword co-occurrence analysis, citation analysis, and patent mapping have been extensively applied to depict the evolutionary landscape of technology domains6,11,12. However, these approaches share a fundamental limitation: they treat technologies as isolated units connected by co-occurrence statistics, ignoring the social-technical networks-projects, institutions, researchers-that produce and mediate technological innovation4,13. This is not merely an omission of additional data sources; it is a theoretical blind spot. As innovation systems theory emphasizes, knowledge creation and diffusion are relational processes occurring through networks of collaboration and citation among heterogeneous actors4. Methods that cannot model these relational structures are, by construction, unable to capture the cross-boundary knowledge flows that drive technological convergence.

AI and machine learning approaches in the fishery domain have primarily addressed point-level technical problems-fish species recognition14, yield prediction15, and remote sensing classification16-rather than network-level strategic analysis. This gap is not coincidental but reflects a deeper methodological constraint: most AI models (CNNs, RNNs, ARIMA) operate on Euclidean data structures (images, time series), whereas innovation networks are inherently non-Euclidean graph structures. The recent emergence of GNNs-specifically designed for graph-structured data-opens a methodological pathway that was previously inaccessible7,8.

Graph neural networks and temporal network analysis have achieved success in recommender systems, knowledge graph reasoning, and bioinformatics8,17,18. However, existing applications share two limitations relevant to our study. First, most temporal GNN architectures (e.g., EvolveGCN9, TGN10,19) model homogeneous graphs, collapsing the heterogeneous actor types that are theoretically critical for understanding innovation dynamics. Second, existing GNN-based technology forecasting studies13,20,21 treat pathway identification as a byproduct of graph embedding rather than as a formally defined prediction task with explicit theoretical grounding-consequently, their identified “pathways” are descriptive patterns rather than predictive constructs.

Convergence gap

The three streams converge on a common unsolved problem: no existing method simultaneously (a) models the heterogeneous multi-actor structure of innovation networks, (b) captures their temporal evolutionary dynamics, and (c) formalizes pathway identification as a theoretically grounded, predictive task. Addressing this convergence gap motivates the present study.

Research gaps

Based on the convergent limitations identified above, we specify three precise research gaps that the present study addresses:

The analogy gap. Despite Dosi’s22 theoretical framework of technological trajectories being widely cited in innovation studies, no existing work has drawn inspiration from Dosi’s qualitative framework of technological trajectory to design a computationally tractable pathway extraction procedure that can be validated against real-world technology evolution data. Existing quantitative approaches either extract trajectories post-hoc from bibliometric data (losing predictive capacity) or apply GNNs as black-box predictors (losing theoretical grounding). Following Hesse23, we characterize our approach as an analogical mapping: Dosi’s concepts-technological paradigm, technological trajectory, and selection environment-provide structural guidance for model design, but do not constitute formal axioms that exhaustively capture the theoretical framework. Our Definition 1 addresses this gap by approximating Dosi’s qualitative intuition through four operational conditions that define a domain-inspired pathway extraction pipeline (Algorithm 1). We emphasize that this is an approximation-an analogical bridge between a rich sociological theory and a computationally tractable procedure-rather than an exhaustive formalization: Dosi’s constructs (e.g., “technological paradigm”) are complex socio-technical concepts that cannot be fully captured by graph-theoretic definitions.

Relation to technology foresight methods

We note that the present study is positioned within the computational technology forecasting tradition rather than the classical technology foresight tradition. Established foresight methodologies-including Delphi surveys25, technology roadmapping26, and patent trend analysis11-rely primarily on expert judgment or descriptive statistical patterns. These methods face two fundamental limitations when applied to pathway identification: (a) they cannot process the scale and heterogeneity of multi-source innovation data (publications, patents, projects, institutions), and (b) they do not provide predictive pathway construction as a formally defined computational task. DTH-GNN addresses these limitations by operationalizing pathway identification as a link prediction problem on a heterogeneous temporal graph, while acknowledging that its “predictions” are correlational rather than causal (see Limitations section). Classical foresight methods remain indispensable for qualitative context-setting, policy feasibility assessment, and stakeholder engagement-functions that fall outside the scope of our computational framework.

(3)The domain-specific evidence gap. No prior study provides large-scale longitudinal evidence on the structural properties (degree distribution, convergence patterns, temporal clustering) of fishery S&T innovation networks. While such evidence exists for general technology domains6,20, the domain-specific characteristics of fishery innovation-such as the regulatory-driven adoption cycles, the strong role of government-funded projects, and the cross-domain convergence of ICT/biotechnology/marine science-produce distinct network dynamics that cannot be assumed from general-domain studies. Our 15-year longitudinal dataset (2010-2024) provides the first such evidence for the fishery sector.

Related work

Theory of hot-spot pathways

Theoretical foundations: from technological trajectories to network-based pathway identification

Understanding the dynamics of technological evolution has been a central concern in innovation studies. Dosi formalized the concept of technological paradigms and technological trajectories within a paradigm as the “paths of ‘normal’ problem solving activity” guided by the paradigm’s “implicit or explicit ‘model’ and ‘exemplar’ ”22. Nelson and Winter further developed an evolutionary theory of economic change, emphasizing the routines, search behaviors, and selection environment that shape technological development over time27.

Building upon these foundational works, contemporary innovation systems theory4 extends the analytical lens from individual firms to the broader ecosystem of actors, institutions, and interactions that co-produce technological change. A critical insight from innovation systems theory is that knowledge creation and diffusion are fundamentally relational processes-they occur through networks of collaboration, citation, and co-invention among heterogeneous actors.

This relational nature suggests that technological evolution can be inspired through network structures. Specifically, we propose that:

The nodes of an innovation network represent technological concepts, actors, and artifacts, whose properties (centrality, connectivity patterns) encode their role in the technological ecosystem.

The edges of an innovation network represent relationships of various types (collaboration, citation, co-occurrence), whose weights and temporal dynamics capture the strength and evolution of technological connections.

Pathways-ordered sequences of technologically and economically related nodes-represent our domain-inspired approximation of Dosi’s technological trajectories within a modern, data-rich environment where network representations are computationally tractable. We use the term “domain-inspired approximation” to signal that this operational definition captures the sequential and directional aspects of Dosi’s trajectories while abstracting from the broader socio-institutional context (e.g., firm-level capabilities, regulatory environments) that Dosi’s original framework encompasses.

Limitations

This correlational framework has three methodological limitations: (1) Confounding: two technologies may co-occur frequently without direct causal influence; (2) Selection bias: our dataset prioritizes publicly funded research (CNKI, CNPD, NSFC), potentially underrepresenting private-sector innovations; (3) Causal identification: establishing causal effects would require exogenous shocks (e.g., policy mandates as instrumental variables). These constitute important directions for future work.

Empirically motivated hypotheses

Based on the domain-inspired design framework, we derive two empirically motivated hypotheses that guide our experimental design. We deliberately use the term “empirically motivated” rather than “theoretical” to signal that these hypotheses are engineering design choices informed by domain knowledge rather than logical deductions from Dosi’s formal theory. Dosi’s framework motivates why one might expect heterogeneity and temporality to matter for pathway prediction, but it does not logically entail that R-GCN + GRU is the optimal architecture or that AUC improvements necessarily confirm the theory. These hypotheses generate performance predictions testable against ablation experiments and data-splitting comparisons:

H1 (heterogeneity value-added hypothesis)[empirically motivated]. Predictions derived from heterogeneous graphs that explicitly distinguish actor types (technology, project, institution, researcher, literature) and relation types (collaboration, citation, funding, co-occurrence, containment, affiliation, undertaking) achieve significantly higher link prediction accuracy than predictions from homogeneous graphs that collapse these distinctions, with the improvement being primarily attributable to the preservation of cross-type information pathways (e.g., literature→technology, institution→project→technology) that mediate indirect technology-technology associations.

This hypothesis generates the following testable prediction: removing heterogeneous relation types from DTH-GNN (i.e., reducing to homogeneous GCN) will cause a disproportionate accuracy decline for technology pairs that are connected primarily through multi-hop heterogeneous paths rather than direct co-occurrence edges. We operationalize this prediction in the ablation experiment (Table 15): the +HGA module should contribute more to AUC for edge types with high heterogeneous-path dependency than for edges directly connected by co-occurrence.

H2 (temporal dynamics primacy hypothesis)[empirically motivated]. The temporal ordering of network evolution events provides predictive information that cannot be recovered from time-aggregated static features, because the directionality and recency of technology co-occurrence patterns encode path-dependent and regime-specific dynamics that static aggregation irreversibly destroys.

This hypothesis generates the following testable prediction: replacing the temporal split with a random split will inflate static model performance more than temporal model performance, because static models exploit training-set diversity in ways that do not reflect genuine temporal generalization capacity to compensate for their inability to model temporal dynamics. Equivalently, the performance gap between DTH-GNN and static baselines should be significantly larger under temporal split than under random split. We test this prediction in the data splitting strategy comparison (Table 14).

Graph neural networks (GNN)

A Graph Neural Network is a deep learning model specifically designed for processing and learning from graph-structured data. A graph G = (V, E) consists of a set of vertices V and a set of edges E. The core idea of GNN is message passing32: each node updates its representation (embedding) by aggregating information from its neighbors. This process typically iterates over multiple rounds (layers), enabling each node to capture multi-hop neighborhood structural information within the graph.

Popular models such as Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) and Graph Attention Networks (GAT) differ primarily in their specific implementations of AGGREGATE and UPDATE. The power of GNN lies in its end-to-end learning of node feature representations that encode rich topological structure information, making it well suited for downstream tasks such as node classification and link prediction33.

Temporal graph neural networks

Many real-world networks-social networks, knowledge graphs, and others-evolve dynamically over time. Fishery S&T innovation networks are no exception: new projects are continuously established, new publications and patents emerge, and research collaborations change constantly. To capture this dynamism, it is necessary to introduce the concept of dynamic or temporal graphs. Temporal GNNs (TGNs) extend GNNs to dynamic graphs, with the core objective of learning an evolution function f(v, t) whose output captures the state of node v at time t. Depending on the treatment of time, TGNs fall into two broad categories: (i) discrete-time models, which partition time into a series of snapshots and apply GNN to each snapshot while using RNN, LSTM, or Transformer-type sequence models to capture temporal dependencies across snapshots9,34; and (ii) continuous-time models, which treat graph evolution as an event stream and directly model timestamped events using temporal point processes or temporal encoding functions10,19. This study adopts the discrete-time model, as it aligns naturally with the annual-cycle release pattern of S&T outputs (publications, patents, and projects) and facilitates data processing and model construction.

Recent advances in temporal graph learning have introduced transformer-based architectures for dynamic graph representation. Among these, DyFormer35 represents the state-of-the-art temporal graph Transformer for link prediction, which we evaluate on a restricted subgraph due to its attention complexity. Notably, GraphGPS36 is designed for static graph representation learning and does not natively support temporal dynamics, making it fundamentally inapplicable to temporal link prediction tasks. The R-GCN + GRU architecture in DTH-GNN represents a favorable trade-off between heterogeneous temporal modeling and computational efficiency for S&T innovation network analysis.

Heterogeneous graph neural networks (HGN)

Traditional GNNs assume homogeneous graphs in which all nodes and edges share a single type. However, fishery S&T innovation networks are typical heterogeneous networks containing multiple node types-technology, project, institution, researcher, and literature-as well as multiple edge types, including collaboration, citation, and funding. Applying a homogeneous GNN to such a network ignores node and edge type information, leading to information loss.

Heterogeneous GNNs (HGNs) are designed precisely to address this issue. Their core idea is to aggregate information based on meta-paths or relation types: for an “institution” node, information from “collaborating” “institution” neighbors and from “funded” “project” neighbors is aggregated separately. Different types of neighbor information are processed through distinct transformation matrices, thereby preserving the heterogeneous semantic information of the network17.

This study adopts the R-GCN architecture8 for handling heterogeneous relations. R-GCN is a classical model for heterogeneous graphs that learns an independent weight matrix for each relation type and selects the corresponding matrix during message passing. R-GCN directly parameterizes relation types, which aligns with the seven explicitly defined edge relations in our heterogeneous graph (collaboration, affiliation, undertaking, funding, citation, co-occurrence, and containment), while maintaining a controllable parameter count suitable for large-scale training on data with millions of edges.

Alternative heterogeneous GNN architectures include HAN24 and HetGNN37, which employ attention mechanisms and random walk-based sampling, respectively. While these models achieve competitive performance on benchmark datasets, R-GCN’s explicit relation parameterization aligns more closely with the predefined edge typology (seven relation types in Table 6) and offers more interpretable attention weights for domain analysis.

Data preparation and preprocessing

Data sources

The multi-source heterogeneous data used in this study comprise three categories: (1) Academic publications: journal articles and graduate dissertations published between January 2010 and December 2024, retrieved from CNKI and Wanfang Database using the topic terms “fishery”, “aquatic”, “fishing”, and “aquaculture”, along with titles, abstracts, keywords, authors, institutions, publication year, and references. (2) Invention patents: fishery-related invention patents (IPC code A01K) retrieved from the Derwent Innovation Index, with patent title, abstract, inventors, applicant (institution), and application year. (3) Research projects: national fishery-related research project data obtained from the NSFC project retrieval system and the National S&T Management Information System Public Service Platform, including project title, principal investigator, host institution, abstract, keywords, start/end year, and funding amount.

Data collection

Data collection followed the PRISMA-Emerging38 protocol, adapted for observational database studies.

Adaptation rationale: while PRISMA was originally developed for systematic reviews in health sciences, its core principles-transparent reporting, explicit eligibility criteria, and reproducible selection procedures-are domain-agnostic and have been increasingly adopted in interdisciplinary research. The PRISMA extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR)39 demonstrates that the PRISMA framework can be validly extended beyond clinical medicine to broader evidence synthesis contexts. For our fishery S&T domain, we retain PRISMA-Emerging items applicable to multi-source data compilation (search strategy, deduplication protocol, inclusion/exclusion criteria) and omit health-specific items (e.g., clinical outcomes, risk of bias). Figure 3 illustrates our adapted flow diagram; detailed search strings and screening protocols are described in “Data collection protocol checklist”.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for data collection.

The screening proceeded as follows: For academic publications, we applied the same search query TS=(fishery OR aquatic OR fishing OR aquaculture OR marine fishery) AND (innovation OR technology OR science) in CNKI and Wanfang, covering 1 January 2010 to 31 December 2024; CNKI returned 48,692 records and Wanfang returned 41,611 records. For patents, IPC code A01K combined with relevant keywords in the Derwent Innovation Index produced 15,847 records. For projects, NSFC and the National S&T Management Platform were queried using “fishery” and “aquatic” keywords, yielding 3,256 national research projects. The total raw retrieval across all four sources was 109,406 records.

Records were deduplicated in three steps. Step 0 identified 30,265 CNKI-Wanfang overlapping records by DOI match and removed them as a batch. Step 1 matched remaining records by unique identifier (DOI for publications, patent number for patents, project approval number for NSFC projects), removing 12,452 duplicates. Step 2 applied normalized Levenshtein string similarity (threshold ≥ 0.90) on title fields to catch cross-database duplicates without standard identifiers, removing an additional 1,244 records. After all three deduplication steps (total removed: 43,961), 65,445 unique records remained for screening (Table 1).

Negative sample generation

In the link prediction task, constructing negative samples (non-existent edges) is critical for training a well-specified classifier. Following best practices in link prediction literature41, for each positive-sample edge , we generate negative samples as follows:

Negative sample strategy. For each positive-sample edge , five candidate node pairs are randomly selected from all node pairs that do not co-occur in the same time-step graph snapshot , ensuring temporal comparability. The 5-candidate filter reduces the probability of accidentally sampling weak-positive pairs (two technologies that co-occurred rarely but genuinely) as negative samples. From these five candidates, one negative edge is randomly retained to maintain a positive-to-negative ratio of 1:1. Importantly, no model-predicted probabilities are computed during this sampling step, and the retained negative is not selected by hardness; this procedure is therefore a 5-candidate filtered random negative sampling strategy rather than online hard negative mining. We verified that this strategy yields AUC = 0.934 ± 0.002, compared to AUC = 0.931 ± 0.003 for direct 1:1 sampling (a consistent +0.003 AUC advantage across 5 seeds). We note that this random selection from the 5-candidate pool differs from selecting the lowest-PMI candidate (which yields AUC = 0.932 ± 0.003, statistically indistinguishable from random selection), confirming that the 5-candidate advantage stems from the filtering effect rather than from a specific candidate selection criterion within the pool.

Hard negative mining analysis

To assess whether the 5-candidate strategy merely selects less informative negative samples (which could bias evaluation without improving genuine predictive power), we conducted a hard negative mining experiment.

Procedure

For each positive edge , instead of using the default 5-candidate filtered random sampling procedure, we selected the single negative candidate (u, v') with the highest PMI (Pointwise Mutual Information) value among all non-neighbor pairs. This “hard negative” represents a technology pair that nearly co-occurs but does not form an edge in the current snapshot-a more challenging negative sample that tests whether the model can distinguish genuinely absent connections from near-miss co-occurrences.

Edge weight calculation

Edge weights are computed separately by relation type to preserve the semantics of heterogeneous links. Collaboration edges are weighted by the annual number of co-authored publications, co-filed patents, or jointly participated projects between two researchers. Affiliation, undertaking, funding, and citation edges are binary within each annual snapshot (weight = 1 if the relation is observed, 0 otherwise). Technology-technology co-occurrence edges are weighted by annual co-occurrence frequency adjusted by Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) to reflect semantic association strength44:

where p(x) and p(y) denote the probabilities of keywords x and y in the network, respectively, and p(x, y) denotes the probability of their co-occurrence in the same document. A positive PMI value indicates a positive correlation between two nodes; a negative value indicates negative correlation. To mitigate noise from low-frequency co-occurrences, PMI is set to 0 when co-occurrence frequency is three or fewer.

For technology association edges, we distinguish direct text-derived edges from projected edges. A publication, patent, or project node is directly connected to a technology node when the normalized keyword appears in its title, abstract, or keyword field; the edge weight is the keyword’s TF-IDF value in that record. Institution-technology and researcher-technology edges are not literal “containment” edges. They are projected from document/project-level relations: an institution is associated with a technology through its affiliated publications, patents, or undertaken projects that contain the keyword, and a researcher is associated with a technology through authored publications, invented patents, or funded projects that contain the keyword. The projected edge weight is the sum of TF-IDF values over linked records within the same year, normalized by the number of linked records for that institution or researcher. All edge weights are computed independently within each annual snapshot before being passed to the heterogeneous graph encoder.

Hot-spot pathway identification via link prediction

(1) From edge predictions to pathways: a three-layer empirical justification The pathway identification problem presents a methodological challenge: DTH-GNN’s high edge-level prediction accuracy (AUC = 0.934) does not, a priori, guarantee that sequences of predicted edges form semantically coherent technological trajectories. The pathway construction problem is fundamentally combinatorial: even with perfect edge-level predictions, the number of possible paths grows exponentially with length, and not all high-probability edge sequences correspond to meaningful technological trajectories. We address this challenge through a three-layer empirical justification that combines algorithmic analysis, empirical validation, and domain expert evaluation. We note that none of these layers constitute formal mathematical guarantees; rather, they provide converging empirical evidence that the greedy pathway construction produces meaningful results on our specific graph structure:

Layer 1: empirical validation of pathway stability. We establish that the greedy pathway construction does not accumulate prediction errors. Validation 1 empirically demonstrates that under the calibrated confidence threshold (τ = 0.50), each edge extension preserves rather than degrades pathway quality, measured by the geometric mean score S(P). This property, which we term monotonic pathway coherence, ensures that the greedy algorithm’s sequential construction does not compound edge-level prediction errors into pathway-level degradation.

Layer 2: empirical validation of approximation quality. We establish that the greedy algorithm’s outputs are robust to algorithmic variation. Validation 2 empirically confirms that under the observed probability separation conditions. the greedy-optimal pathway converges with beam search (beam width k=3) for top-ranked candidates. This greedy-beam equivalence confirms that the greedy assumption does not sacrifice solution quality for computational efficiency.

Layer 3: domain validation. Theoretically grounded pathways are validated by domain experts using a modified Delphi method. Expert evaluation confirms high pathway coherence scores (Technical Feasibility: 5.87 ± 0.64; Application Value: 6.13 ± 0.35) and substantial inter-rater agreement (Krippendorff’s α = 0.804), providing external validation that the formally constructed pathways correspond to meaningful technological trajectories recognized by domain practitioners.

The residual gap between formal guarantees and empirical pathway quality reflects the inherent limitation of treating technological trajectories as optimization targets: the algorithm optimizes a proxy objective (geometric mean edge probability) rather than “meaningfulness” per se. This limitation is acknowledged and addressed through dual validation (theoretical + expert).

(2) Hypothesis-component correspondence

The two theoretical hypotheses proposed in the Theoretical Hypotheses Section provide intuition rather than formal guarantees for the pathway construction algorithm: H1 (Heterogeneity Value-Added Hypothesis) motivates the use of R-GCN for heterogeneous graph encoding, ensuring that predicted edges are informed by multi-actor relationships; H2 (Temporal Dynamics Primacy Hypothesis) motivates the sequential reinforcement logic in Algorithm 1, reflecting the path-dependent evolution patterns captured by the GRU module.

(2) Pathway construction logic: theoretical and algorithmic grounding

Experiments and analysis

Experimental setup

Environment setup

The DTH-GNN model is implemented using PyTorch52, with heterogeneous graph operations handled by the Deep Graph Library (DGL) and temporal modeling by PyTorch Geometric, on a server equipped with an Intel Xeon Gold 6248R CPU and an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU. Key hyperparameters are as follows: learning rate = 0.001 with the Adam optimizer; epochs = 200 with early stopping (patience = 20) to prevent overfitting; batch size = 64; three graph convolutional layers; hidden dimension = 256. The loss function is binary cross-entropy.

Computational cost: training DTH-GNN on the full temporal graph (15 snapshots, ∼1.1M edges in 2024) requires approximately 4.2 h on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU. Inference on the 2024 snapshot (computing all candidate link probabilities) requires approximately 12 min. For comparison, GCN training requires 1.8 h, R-GCN requires 2.5 h, and EvolveGCN requires 3.1 hours under identical hardware conditions.

Hyperparameter search space: key hyperparameters were selected via grid search on the validation set (2023 snapshot) within the following ranges: learning rate ; hidden dimension ; number of R-GCN layers ; GRU hidden dimension ; dropout rate . The selected configuration (lr = 1e-3, hidden_dim = 256, layers = 3, GRU_dim = 256, dropout = 0.0) achieved the highest validation AUC. Early stopping with patience = 20 epochs was applied to prevent overfitting.

Data splitting strategy

To prevent future information leakage, the full temporal range (2010–2024) is divided into three non-overlapping periods:

Training set: edges observed in 2010–2022 (inclusive, 13 time-step snapshots)

Validation set: edges observed in 2023 (1 time-step snapshot, used for hyperparameter tuning and early stopping)

Test set: edges observed in 2024 (1 time-step snapshot, used for final model evaluation)

This split ensures that the model never observes future edges during training, which is consistent with the real-world decision-making scenario where planners must predict future connections based on historical evidence only.

For each positive technology co-occurrence edge, five candidate negative edges are randomly selected from node pairs that do not co-occur in the same time-step snapshot. The 5-candidate filter reduces the probability of accidentally sampling weak-positive pairs as negative samples. From these five candidates, one negative edge is randomly retained to maintain a 1:1 positive-to-negative ratio across all datasets (training, validation, and test), ensuring temporal comparability while preventing class imbalance during both training and evaluation.

Sample statistics

Following PRISMA protocol screening of raw multi-source data retrieval, the final source corpus comprises 32,847 academic publications, 8,956 invention patents, and 1,856 research projects (totaling 43,659 records). These source records and the graph nodes reported in Table 7 use different accounting units. Literature nodes (A) represent graph-instantiated publications and patents only, while research projects are represented separately as project nodes (P). In the 2024 snapshot, the graph contains 33,862 literature nodes and 1,431 project nodes, together representing 35,293 document/project nodes. The remaining nodes are extracted entities: 29,876 technology nodes, 2,156 institution nodes, and 15,678 researcher nodes. Therefore, the total 2024 graph-node count (83,003) exceeds the source-record count because extracted technology, institution, and researcher entities are modeled as additional nodes, while the literature-node count alone is not expected to equal the total number of source records. The 2024 snapshot contains 1,132,547 edges, with technology-technology co-occurrence edges accounting for approximately 65% of all edges. Temporally partitioned: the training set spans 13 annual snapshots (2010-2022); the validation set comprises one snapshot (2023); and the test set comprises one snapshot (2024). All datasets maintain a 1:1 positive-to-negative ratio for the link prediction task, with negative samples generated through the strategy of “randomly selecting one from five candidate non-co-occurring node pairs per positive edge”. The exact edge counts for each partition are determined by the technology co-occurrence frequencies within the respective time steps.

Evaluation metrics

Quantitative evaluation of link prediction employs AUC (Area Under the ROC Curve) and AP (Average Precision). AUC measures overall ranking ability, with values closer to 1 indicating better performance; AP summarizes precision and recall jointly by ranking predicted positive samples. Qualitative evaluation assesses predicted pathways on two dimensions-rationality and novelty-with novelty focusing on whether predictions reveal cross-domain technology convergence opportunities undetectable by traditional methods, and rationality assessing technical feasibility and application value. Five senior fishery domain experts-including two researchers from the Chinese Academy of Fishery Sciences, two university professors, and one enterprise technical director-anonymously scored the Top-K predicted pathways using a modified Delphi method54 with a seven-point scale (1 = very low, 4 = moderate, 7 = very high) across three dimensions (technical feasibility, innovation novelty, application value). Ablation experiments were also conducted to evaluate each module’s contribution.

Validation of temporal modeling necessity via data splitting strategy comparison

To rigorously validate that the observed performance gains of DTH-GNN primarily stem from its capacity to model temporal dynamics rather than merely capturing static structural patterns, we conducted a comparative evaluation using two distinct data splitting strategies: (i) the temporal split (chronological 2010-2022/2023/2024 for training/validation/test) employed in the main experiments, and (ii) a random split (edges randomly shuffled and partitioned 70%/15%/15% across all 15 annual snapshots, i.e., 2024 edges may appear in training/validation). This random split relaxes the temporal boundary to test whether model performance gains stem from temporal generalization or from exploiting an enlarged training set. Results are presented in Table 14.

Critical findings: the results reveal three critical findings:

First, DTH-GNN achieves the highest absolute performance under the temporal split (AUC = 0.934), the evaluation scenario that most closely reflects real-world deployment where future data is unavailable. Under temporal split, DTH-GNN outperforms EvolveGCN by ΔAUC = +4.3% (0.934 vs. 0.891), astatistically significant margin (paired t-test, p < 0.001). This primary comparison establishes DTH-GNN’s superior temporal modeling capacity.

Second, the random-split comparison provides supporting evidence for this claim. All models improve under random splitting (where future edges are visible during training), but the magnitude of improvement differs markedly: DTH-GNN’s marginal gain is only +0.7%, whereas static baselines exhibit double-digit gains (GCN: +10.7%, R-GCN: +6.5%). This differential response confirms that DTH-GNN relies less on training-data diversity to achieve its performance, consistent with its explicit temporal encoding. Notably, EvolveGCN (+1.4%) falls between static models and DTH-GNN, confirming it captures some temporal dynamics but fewer than DTH-GNN.

Third, the performance hierarchy between models changes markedly across split strategies. Under random split, the gap between DTH-GNN and static baselines narrows considerably (e.g., DTH-GNN vs. R-GCN: absolute AUC gap = 0.040 under random split vs. 0.098 under temporal split; relative gap = 4.4% vs. 11.7%).Conversely, under temporal split-reflecting real-world deployment scenarios where future data is unavailable-DTH-GNN’s advantage becomes pronounced and statistically significant. This pattern confirms that temporal dynamics constitute significant discriminative information for accurate hot-spot pathway prediction in fishery S&T innovation networks, as further supported by the ablation experiment showing that removing the temporal encoding module (TEM) causes the largest single-module performance decline (+0.049 AUC).

Causal interpretation of performance differentials: beyond the quantitative performance comparison presented above, we provide a qualitative interpretation of why temporal dynamics contribute significantly to hot-spot pathway prediction in fishery S&T innovation.

Argument 1: domain-specific temporal clustering: fishery S&T innovation exhibits pronounced temporal clustering of related technologies. For example, gene editing applications in aquaculture clustered around 2019-2022 following the democratization of CRISPR tools; IoT and sensor network applications surged after 2016 driven by smart agriculture national policies; and blue carbon and carbon-sink fisheries research accelerated post-2020 aligned with China’s carbon neutrality commitments. Static models that pool observations across all time steps cannot capture these regime-specific patterns, leading to averaging artifacts that dilute predictive signal. DTH-GNN’s explicit temporal encoding enables the model to recognize that a gene editing-disease resistance link formed in 2021 carries different predictive weight than the same link formed in 2015.

Argument 2: irreversibility of technology trajectories: some technology transitions in fishery S&T are lumpy and partially irreversible. The shift from extensive to intensive aquaculture, the adoption of factory-based recirculating systems, and the transition from capture-based to culture-based marine ranching represent path-dependent transitions where early adoption creates infrastructure lock-in, skill accumulation, and institutional reinforcement. Models that ignore temporal ordering cannot distinguish between reversible co-occurrence patterns (e.g., two technologies that rise and fall together due to funding cycles) and path-dependent technological shifts (e.g., the irreversible substitution of manual feeding by automated precision feeding systems). DTH-GNN’s directional temporal modeling captures this asymmetry: the probability of observing edge at t depends not merely on whether existed at any prior time, but on the specific temporal trajectory of ’s relationship evolution.

Argument 3: information degradation in static aggregation. When edges from different time steps are pooled without temporal tags, the model receives a degraded signal where contemporaneous and historical relationships are confounded. Consider a technology pair that co-occurred frequently in 2010-2015 (establishment phase) but rarely in 2020-2024 (mature phase). A static model observes high aggregate co-occurrence and predicts high future linkage probability-yet this prediction is erroneous if the technology pair has entered a saturation or substitution phase. Conversely, a pair with low historical but rapidly accelerating recent co-occurrence signals emerging hot-spot formation that static models miss. DTH-GNN’s temporal encoding preserves the temporal ordering, enabling the model to distinguish “trending” connections (rising edge weights in recent time steps) from “established” ones (stable high weights) and “decaying” ones (falling weights).

To quantify the contribution of temporal dynamics, we computed the mutual information between temporal features (node age, edge recency, temporal clustering coefficient) and link existence in the test set. Temporal features account for a substantial share of mutual information (31.8%, 95% CI: [28.4%, 35.1%]), comparable to structural features (29.1%, [26.2%, 32.0%]) and higher than attribute features (19.7%, [17.1%, 22.3%]), using the Kraskov-Stögbauer-Grassberger (KSG) estimator with k=5 nearest neighbors after feature standardization and PCA reduction of high-dimensional BERT embeddings.

This evidence, combined with the substantial performance degradation of static models under temporal split, indicates that temporal dynamics are significantly correlated with link prediction accuracy in this domain. We note an important caveat: mutual information analysis establishes statistical association between feature categories and link existence, but does not establish causal primacy-that is, we cannot conclude from MI alone that temporal dynamics are the “primary cause” of accurate predictions. The 2.7-percentage-point gap between temporal (31.8%) and structural (29.1%) features is modest and falls within the overlapping 95% confidence intervals ([28.4%, 35.1%] vs. [26.2%, 32.0%]), indicating that the difference is not statistically distinguishable at conventional significance levels. The remaining 19.4% is reported as a non-additive residual due to overlap among feature groups and estimator limitations, not as a directly measured contribution of feature interactions or model nonlinearity. To more directly assess the causal contribution of temporal features, we rely on the ablation experiment (Table 15) and the data-splitting comparison (Table 14), which provide stronger evidence for the necessity of temporal modeling.

Hot-spot pathway identification results

The top-3 highest-probability technology links predicted for 2025 and beyond were visualized and analyzed. By connecting these high-probability links, multiple potential hot-spot pathways were identified.

Pathway 1: smart aquaculture and precision management:

IoT → Sensor Networks → Intelligent Net Cages → Online Water Quality Monitoring → Precision Feeding Models → Growth Behavior Analysis → Disease Early-Warning Systems → Blockchain-based Aquatic Product Traceability

This pathway exemplifies the deep empowerment of traditional aquaculture by information technology. Beginning with foundational data collection (IoT, sensors), it progresses to mid-level intelligent equipment (smart net cages) and real-time monitoring, advancing to algorithm-driven decision-making (precision feeding, disease early warning), and culminating in product value enhancement (blockchain traceability). The model successfully captures the technological deepening logic from “sensing” to “decision-making” to “trust.”

Pathway 2: biotechnology-driven green seed industry innovation:

Genomics → Molecular Marker-Assisted Breeding → Gene Editing (CRISPR) → Disease/Stress-Resistance Trait Improvement → High-Yield/Fast-Growing Variety Cultivation → Cell Factory/Cultured Aquatic Products → Synthetic Biology → Alternative Protein Sources

This pathway reveals that biotechnology-especially frontier gene editing-is reshaping aquatic seed industries. Beginning with basic genome analysis and proceeding through advanced breeding to improve key economic traits, the pathway extends to disruptive “cellular agriculture” and alternative protein domains, signaling that future fisheries may serve not only as traditional aquatic product providers but as important sources of biomanufactured and novel food products-carrying strategic significance for long-term food security57.

Pathway 3: ecological fisheries and resource restoration convergence:

Marine Ranching → Artificial Reefs → Habitat Restoration Technology → Environmental DNA (eDNA) Monitoring → Fishery Resource Acoustic Assessment → Ecosystem Modeling → Carbon-Sink Fisheries → Blue Carbon Economy.

This pathway reflects the convergence of production and ecology. Starting from engineered ecological restoration measures (marine ranching, artificial reefs) and combining advanced ecological monitoring techniques (eDNA, acoustic assessment), it advances to model-based systematic management (ecosystem modeling) and ultimately aligns with China’s carbon neutrality strategy, pointing toward the emerging “blue carbon economy” domain16. The model successfully identifies the value evolution pathway of fisheries expanding from single food production to ecosystem service functions.

Conventional fishery innovation literature assumes that aquaculture evolves along “capture → culture → intensive culture” trajectories58. Our finding suggests that future fishery R&D may pivot toward biomanufacturing platforms, implying that policy should explicitly include cellular aquaculture and synthetic biology as priority disciplines.

Pathway construction algorithm: ablation and comparison

To assess the robustness of hot-spot pathway to the construction algorithm, we compared three variants: (i) greedy sequential search (Algorithm 1), (ii) beam search with k=3, and (iii) beam search with k=5.

Key finding: The top-3 pathways are fully stable across all three construction algorithms, confirming that the primary policy recommendations are robust to algorithmic choice. The greedy algorithm is retained as the default due to its favorable trade-off between computational efficiency (12 min vs. 312 min for beam-5) and pathway quality.

Justification of greedy choice: The greedy sequential strategy is retained for the following reasons: (1) top-3 stability is confirmed; (2) deterministic outputs enhance interpretability for policy use; (3) the primary use case (R&D prioritization) prioritizes speed and clarity over exhaustive search of exponentially many pathways; and (4) the beam-5 expansion to all seed nodes is computationally prohibitive on the full graph.

Back-testing and external validation

To assess whether DTH-GNN’s link prediction accuracy generalizes to unseen future time steps (rather than being an artifact of the 2024 test distribution), we conducted a retrospective back-testing experiment.

Design: we trained DTH-GNN using data from 2010–2019 (inclusive, 10 annual snapshots) and used the trained model to predict technology co-occurrence links for the 2020 time step. Predictions were compared against actually observed links in 2020 publications, patents, and projects. Caveat: this design confounds two factors-reduced training data volume (10 vs. 13 snapshots in the primary experiment) and a longer prediction horizon (4 years vs. 1 year)-so the observed performance drop (ΔAUC = 0.087) should be interpreted as the joint effect of reduced training capacity and longer-term prediction uncertainty, and should not be attributed to temporal generalization limitations alone.

Seed specification: the back-test uses 10 random seeds (10, 20, 30, 42, 100, 123, 456, 2024, 7, 2025) to increase robustness of the retrospective evaluation. The main experiment uses 5 seeds (42, 123, 2024, 7, 2025) for computational efficiency, consistent with standard practice in the GNN literature.

Edge-level results: for the top-10% highest-probability predicted edges in 2020, DTH-GNN achieved mean AUC = 0.847 ± 0.003 and mean AP = 0.840 ± 0.004 across 10 independent training runs (Table 20), as well as precision = 0.734 and recall = 0.689 (Table 21). Dynamic evolution of “smart fisheries”: a case study

Focusing on the “smart fisheries” sub-domain, the dynamic evolution of internal technology themes is analyzed62 with attention to domain-specific factors unique to fishery science and technology.

Biological cycle impacts. The “Smart Aquaculture” pathway is validated against the aquaculture production cycle. IoT sensor deployment peaks align with disease susceptibility periods (summer high-temperature seasons, approximately June-September). The model captures how technology adoption accelerates during high-risk periods when monitoring and automated intervention offer the highest marginal productivity gain for aquaculture operations.

Resource constraint dynamics. The “Ecological Fisheries” pathway is contextualized against China’s fishery resource depletion timeline. The 2015-2020 marine fishing moratorium and the 2021 Yangtze River fishing ban created regulatory forcing that accelerated technology adoption in ecological restoration and resource monitoring. The model captures the technology response trajectory following these policy interventions, providing quantitative evidence of regulation-driven innovation patterns.

Counter-intuitive finding. The model identifies “synthetic biology” as a high-probability node in Pathway 2 (Green Seed Industry) with a predicted edge probability of 0.71. This technology has not yet received mainstream policy attention in China’s fishery sector but emerges through indirect heterogeneous pathways (seed breeding → molecular biology institution → synthetic biology research collaboration). This represents a genuine counter-intuitive prediction that merits further policy attention. Smart fisheries technology grew rapidly from 2016 onward, driven by the convergence of AI and IoT. Biotechnology showed steady growth, while deep-sea fishing technology accelerated after 2020 under policy and market drivers.

Discussion

Contributions and implications

The results of this study demonstrate that the proposed framework, which constructs multi-source heterogeneous data into a dynamic heterogeneous graph and employs DTH-GNN for learning and prediction, is both feasible and effective. DTH-GNN comprehensively outperforms all baseline models in link prediction, confirming its superiority in capturing and modeling complex innovation networks8,9. These findings align with and extend prior work on dynamic network analysis for emerging technology identification13,20, with the key advance being the simultaneous modeling of heterogeneity and dynamism in a domain-specific context.

The three identified high-potential pathways-smart aquaculture, green seed industry, and ecological fisheries-not only reveal internal technological deepening logic but, importantly, identify cross-domain technology convergence trends1,2. The smart aquaculture pathway, in particular, represents a comprehensive digital transformation trajectory that could substantially improve production efficiency and product traceability. The biotechnology pathway, culminating in synthetic biology and alternative proteins, signals a paradigm shift in how fisheries contribute to global food systems. The ecological fisheries pathway, connecting restoration technology with carbon-sink valuation, positions fisheries as active contributors to climate mitigation strategies.

The case analysis of “smart fisheries” further demonstrates that the framework clearly and intuitively reveals the dynamic evolutionary process of internal structure within specific technology domains-from shifts in technology hotspots to transfers of core technology paradigms-providing a powerful analytical tool for understanding technology life cycles and anticipating future breakthroughs.

From a practical standpoint,the hot-spot pathways provide indicative rather than prescriptive guidance for S&T planning and investment decisions. Specifically, the pathways highlight technology convergence directions that are statistically supported by historical innovation patterns, but they do not constitute causal predictions of inevitable technological futures. The translation from pathway identification to policy action requires additional considerations-including regulatory feasibility, market readiness, infrastructure requirements, and social acceptability-that lie beyond the scope of the current model. We therefore recommend that the framework be used as a screening and prioritization tool within a broader strategic planning process that incorporates qualitative expert judgment and contextual analysis, rather than as a standalone decision-making instrument.

Correlation and causation: the identified hot-spot pathways are probabilistic co-occurrence sequences that reflect statistical regularities in historical innovation patterns. They do not constitute causal claims about inevitable technological evolution. A high predicted link probability indicates that two technologies have historically co-occurred frequently within similar institutional and collaborative contexts, but it does not guarantee that future co-occurrence will follow the same pattern. Several factors beyond what the model captures - including policy shifts, sudden scientific breakthroughs, market disruptions, and resource constraints - can alter technological trajectories in ways that the model cannot anticipate. We therefore recommend interpreting the hot-spot pathways as statistically grounded hypotheses for further investigation, not as deterministic forecasts.

Algorithmic Limitations Of Greedy Pathway Construction. The greedy pathway construction algorithm (Algorithm 1) is an empirically validated heuristic, not a theoretically guaranteed optimization procedure. While three lines of empirical evidence support its practical effectiveness on our specific graph-(i) monotonic pathway coherence in 98.2% of extensions (Validation 1), (ii) greedy-beam equivalence for top-3 pathways (Validation 2), and (iii) pathway stability under edge perturbation (perturbation analysis)-these are data-dependent observations that hold for the Chinese fishery S&T co-occurrence network with its specific probability separation structure (). They do not constitute universal theoretical guarantees that would transfer to graphs with different topological or probability characteristics.

Specifically, for graphs where the probability separation between top-1 and top-2 candidates is small (Δ < 0.08, which accounts for 14.3% of technology pairs in our graph), the greedy algorithm may produce suboptimal pathways. This rank-sensitivity is confirmed by our observation that greedy-beam equivalence degrades for pathways ranked 4–10 (Table 16). We therefore recommend that practitioners: (a) use DTH-GNN for prioritization of top-3 technology pathways rather than comprehensive ranking of all candidates; and (b) validate predicted pathways with domain experts before incorporating them into policy decisions, as our expert evaluation (Krippendorff’s α = 0.804) provides independent confirmation of pathway meaningfulness.

Theory-method correspondence boundary: returning to the epistemological framework established in the theoretical section: our Dosi-inspired design heuristics provide structural guidance (what graph structure to build, what temporal encoding to use) but do not derive or prove the existence of the identified pathways. In other words, the Dosi framework motivates the architecture of our approach (heterogeneous nodes reflecting actor diversity, temporal modeling reflecting path-dependence), while the existence and content of specific pathways are empirical findings that stand on their own methodological merits (high link prediction accuracy, expert validation). This distinction is important for interpreting our theoretical contribution: we do not claim that Dosi’s theory “predicts” the Smart Aquaculture pathway, but rather that Dosi’s framework provides a principled basis for designing the computational infrastructure that discovers such pathways.

Limitations and boundary conditions

First, our empirical analysis focuses exclusively on China’s fishery S&T ecosystem, and the hot-spot pathways reflect domain-specific structural features (e.g., the dominant role of government-funded projects, the regulatory-driven adoption patterns characteristic of China’s central planning system) that may not transfer to market-driven innovation systems. The model’s architectural design (DTH-GNN) is domain-agnostic and transferable, but the learned parameters and hot-spot pathway are specific to the Chinese fishery context. Generalizability to other national contexts, other agricultural sub-sectors, or other technology domains requires systematic cross-contextual validation that we leave to future work. Accordingly, all policy recommendations should be understood as applicable to the Chinese fishery S&T planning context specifically, rather than as universal claims about global fisheries.

Second, the model relies on Chinese-language databases (CNKI, Wanfang, CNIPA), which may underrepresent international collaboration patterns.

Third, the five expert evaluators are all based in mainland China; incorporating diverse international perspectives could enhance the external validity of pathway validation. Future research should extend this framework to comparative cross-national studies and integrate multilingual data sources.

Fourth, A fundamental limitation of the proposed pathway identification framework is its reliance on high-centrality seed nodes. The degree threshold () ensures that hot-spot pathway originate from technologies with established co-occurrence patterns, but this design systematically excludes emerging technologies with currently low degree but potentially high future impact. This bias is a deliberate trade-off: it prioritizes pathway reliability (based on sufficient historical co-occurrence evidence) over novelty (detecting emerging-from-scratch trajectories). The most transformative innovations-those arising from radical recombination of previously uncorrelated technologies-may not be detectable through this framework. Future work could relax the seed selection criterion (e.g., using citation burst detection or PageRank growth rate) to include emerging technologies with high acceleration but low absolute degree.

Conclusions

This study proposes a comprehensive framework integrating GNN with dynamic evolutionary analysis for identifying hot-spot pathways in fishery S&T projects, making three principal contributions:

(1) Methodological innovation: This study is, to our knowledge, among the first to systematically apply temporal heterogeneous GNNs to fishery S&T hot-spot pathway identification, introducing the DTH-GNN model capable of simultaneously processing multi-type node relations and temporal dynamic evolution. This method provides a new pathway prediction and analysis approach for existing hot-spot pathway identification research, filling the gap in GNN applications to fishery S&T innovation analysis.

(2) Empirical contribution: The research findings-that the fishery S&T innovation network exhibits power-law degree distribution, prominent multi-actor co-evolution characteristics, and stage-wise leaping patterns in technology convergence-provide empirical evidence for applying innovation systems theory to specific industrial domains.

(3) Practical value: The framework successfully identifies several high-potential hot-spot pathways covering smart aquaculture, green seed industry, and ecological fisheries, providing data-driven reference evidence for S&T investment prioritization and policy planning within the Chinese fishery context.

Several limitations and future research directions merit attention: (1) Data breadth and depth-future work should incorporate additional data dimensions such as enterprise investment data, S&T policy texts, and market sales data. (2) Model interpretability-GNN “black-box” characteristics limit result interpretability; future work could combine graph attention visualization and counterfactual explanation methods to enhance transparency. (3) Causal inference-the hot-spot pathways are primarily correlation-based predictions rather than strict causal relationships; future work could introduce causal inference theory combined with quasi-experimental designs. (4) Human-machine interactive decision support systems-the ultimate goal is to develop an interactive human-machine collaborative decision support system integrating “data + algorithms + expert knowledge” for more efficient and scientific S&T innovation strategy management.

In summary, leveraging GNNs and dynamic evolutionary analysis to empower fishery S&T hot-spot pathway identification represents a challenging but broadly promising research direction. This study takes a solid step forward and aspires to contribute intelligence and insights toward advancing sustainable fishery development in China and globally.
