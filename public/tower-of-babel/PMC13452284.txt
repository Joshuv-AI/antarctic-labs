Ectopic anterior mediastinal parathyroid adenoma: A case report

Introduction

Hyperparathyroidism refers to excessive production of parathyroid hormone that leads to high levels of calcium in blood. Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is caused by autonomous parathyroid hormone secretion, resulting in hypercalcemia and systemic complications involving kidney, bone, and neurological functions.

The most common phenotype is asymptomatic and it is usually observed in patients with mild hypercalcemia. Hyperparathyroidism is usually caused by a single benign parathyroid adenoma (85%), less commonly by multiple parathyroid gland disease (hyperplasia and/or multiple adenomas, 15%) and only in less than 1% of cases by parathyroid carcinoma [1].

Most parathyroid adenomas are located posterior or inferior to the thyroid gland. Ectopic adenomas account for up to 5% of cases. The inferior parathyroid glands are more likely to be ectopic, secondary to their prolonged course of descent during development. Ectopic superior parathyroids are most common in the tracheoesophageal groove, retro-esophageal area, or posterior mediastinum. Ectopic inferior parathyroid glands most commonly present within the thymus, the anterior mediastinum, within the thyroid or within the thyrothymic ligament [2].

Parathyroid scintigraphy plays a crucial role in detecting ectopic parathyroid adenomas with a reported sensitivity of approximately 89% and a positive predictive value of 90% for localizing ectopic glands [3].

The definitive treatment of ectopic parathyroid adenoma is surgical resection. Minimally invasive method, such as video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), has emerged as effective technique, particularly for lesions located in the anterior mediastinum.

Case description

A 55-year-old male patient presented to our hospital with a three-month history of persistent lumbar pain, nephrolithiasis, fatigue, bone and joint pain and severe constipation. His medical history was significant only for hypertension, for which he was receiving antihypertensive therapy. There was no history of previous surgical interventions or other chronic disease.

The patient had initially been treated for renal stones and ureterohydronephrosis. During the previous three weeks, he reported worsening severe constipation, for which a colonoscopy was performed. The examination showed no pathological findings. Physical examination revealed no signs of neuromuscular irritability, including negative Chvostek and Trousseau signs. No electrocardiographic abnormalities were identified.

Laboratory investigations demonstrated PTH-dependent hypercalcemia, with markedly elevated intact parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels of 10.1 ng/L and serum calcium levels of 3.0 mmol/L. Additional laboratory findings included alkaline phosphatase elevation (126 U/L), low vitamin D levels (25[OH]D: 14.2 ng/mL) with serum creatinine of 0.8 mg/dL and mildly reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 67 mL/min/1.73 m².

A non-contrast CT scan of the chest and abdomen was initially performed as part of the diagnostic workup for persistent lumbar pain, nephrolithiasis and recurrent renal colic in the setting of hypercalcemia. It detected an ovoid well-circumscribed soft-tissue density lesion with homogenous attenuation in the prevascular compartment of mediastinum. The lesion measured approximately 25 × 23 × 17 mm and was in close proximity to ascending aorta and mediastinal vascular structures, without evidence of invasion.

Given the presence of PTH-dependent hypercalcemia and the identification of a well-circumscribed prevascular mediastinal lesion on CT, an ectopic parathyroid adenoma was suspected. Therefore, Tc-99m sestamibi scintigraphy with a dose of 20 mci was performed to functionally characterize the lesion and confirm the presence of hyperfunctioning ectopic parathyroid tissue. Planar scintigraphy demonstrated physiologic radiotracer uptake in the salivary glands and thyroid gland. In addition, a focal area of increased radiotracer uptake was identified inferior to the thyroid gland, in the upper anterior mediastinum, corresponding to the lesion seen on CT. This finding is characteristic of ectopic parathyroid tissue.

The combination of patient’s clinical symptoms, biochemical abnormalities and imaging findings strongly supported the diagnosis of primary hyperparathyroidism due to an ectopic adenoma. According to current management guidelines, our patient met the criteria for surgical intervention, given the presence of symptomatic hypercalcemia (serum calcium 3.0 mmol/L), an elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH) level of 10.1 ng/L and hypercalciuria (16.77 mmol/L per 24 h). Following surgical evaluation, the patient underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), for minimally invasive resection of the ectopic adenoma under general anesthesia, without intraoperative or postoperative complications (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3).

Discussion

Primary hyperparathyroidism is one of the most common causes of hypercalcemia and is usually caused by a single parathyroid adenoma. Whereas most parathyroid adenomas are located in the cervical region next to the thyroid gland, approximately 6-16% of the cases involve one or more hyperfunctioning parathyroid glands in ectopic location. This represents a significant diagnostic and surgical challenge [4].

In our case, the lesion was found in the prevascular compartment of the mediastinum, consistent with an ectopic inferior parathyroid gland. This anomaly arises due to abnormal embryological migration. Along its embryological migration pathway from the mandibular angle to the mediastinum, the ectopic glands can be located anywhere along this median line. The inferior parathyroid glands originate from the third pharyngeal pouch and descend together with the thymus, explaining their frequent ectopic localization within the thymus or anterior mediastinum. In contrast, the superior parathyroid glands arise from the fourth pharyngeal pouch and are more commonly located in the tracheoesophageal groove or posterior mediastinum [5].

Clinically, many patients experience no obvious symptoms; however, as serum calcium levels increase, clinical manifestations related to hypercalcemia may develop. Asymptomatic PHPT has been the dominant phenotype of PHPT in modern western populations with a prevalence of 72.7% to 95%. In western societies, renal manifestations represent approximately one-third of symptomatic presentations [6].

Our patient presented with a three-month history of persistent lumbar pain, nephrolithiasis, fatigue, bone and joint pain and severe constipation. Laboratory findings demonstrated marked hypercalcemia, hypophosphatemia and elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels confirming PTH-dependent hypercalcemia.

Accurate preoperative localization is essential, particularly in cases of ectopic glands, in order to guide the optimal surgical approach. In our case, a non-contrast CT revealed a well-circumscribed soft tissue lesion in the prevascular compartment of mediastinum. Although SPECT/CT has demonstrated superior localization accuracy for ectopic mediastinal parathyroid adenomas, it was not available at our institution at the time of evaluation. Nevertheless, the combination of non-contrast CT and Tc-99m sestamibi planar scintigraphy enabled accurate preoperative localization and successful surgical management. Functional imaging with technetium-99m sestamibi demonstrated focal radiotracer uptake in the same region, confirming the diagnosis [7].

The differential diagnosis of a mediastinal mass includes thymoma, thymic cyst, lymphadenopathy, germ cell tumors, ectopic thyroid tissue and other neuroendocrine lesions. In our case, non-contrast CT demonstrated a well-circumscribed, soft-tissue lesion in the prevascular compartment of mediastinum without evidence of local invasion, findings that may overlap with other mediastinal pathologies. However, in the setting of hypercalcemia and elevated parathyroid hormone levels, ectopic parathyroid adenoma should be strongly considered. The combination of anatomical and functional imaging was essential in narrowing the differential diagnosis and establishing the correct preoperative diagnosis. Similar imaging features and diagnostic challenges have been previously described in the literature [8].

Surgical excision remains the definitive treatment for PHPT in symptomatic patients and in those meeting guideline-based criteria. Minimally invasive video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery is the preferred technique for ectopic anterior mediastinal adenomas. VATS allows improved visualization of mediastinal structures, reduced operative time, shorter chest tube duration and decreased hospital stay. Additionally, it offers superior cosmetic results compared to traditional approaches such as sternotomy or thoracotomy. In our case, this method enabled safe and complete resection of the lesion without complications [9].

Although ectopic mediastinal parathyroid adenomas have been previously reported, this case highlights the diagnostic challenges associated with prevascular mediastinal lesions and emphasizes the value of combining anatomical and functional imaging for accurate localization in settings where SPECT/CT is not available.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we report the case of an ectopic mediastinal parathyroid adenoma in an adult patient, emphasizing the importance of appropriate diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. A multimodal imaging approach combining CT and 99mTc-sestamibi scintigraphy is crucial for accurate localization of ectopic parathyroid adenomas. Minimally invasive surgical resection enables effective treatment with favorable outcomes.
