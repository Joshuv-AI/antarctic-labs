Supernodes and Halos: Loss-Critical Hubs in LLM Feed-Forward Layers


Abstract
We study the organization of channel-level importance in transformer feed-forward networks (FFNs). Using a Fisher-style loss proxy (LP) based on activation-gradient second moments, we show that loss sensitivity is concentrated in a small set of channels within each layer. In Llama-3.1-8B, the top 1% of channels per layer accounts for a median of 58.7% of LP mass (range: 33.0–86.1%). We call these loss-critical channels supernodes . Although FFN layers also contain strong activation outliers, LP-defined supernodes overlap only weakly with activation-defined outliers and are not explained by activation power or weight norms alone. Around this core, we find a weaker but consistent halo structure: some non-supernode channels share the supernodes’ write support and show stronger redundancy with the protected core. We use one-shot structured FFN pruning as a diagnostic test of this organization. At 50% FFN sparsity, baselines that prune many supernodes degrade sharply, whereas our Scar variants explicitly protect the supernode core; the strongest variant, Scar -Prot, reaches perplexity 54.8 compared with 989.2 for Wanda-channel. The LP-concentration pattern appears across Mistral-7B, Llama-2-7B, and Qwen2-7B, remains visible in targeted Llama-3.1-70B experiments, and increases during OLMo-2-7B pretraining. These results suggest that LLM FFNs develop a small learned core of loss-critical channels, and that preserving this core is important for reliable structured pruning.

Introduction

Large language models contain rare components that appear to carry disproportionate functional importance. Prior work has found massive activation coordinates
(
Sun et al. 2024a
)
and individual “super weights”
(
Yu et al. 2024
)
, suggesting that importance in LLMs is not spread uniformly across parameters or features. We ask whether a similar concentration occurs at the level of FFN channels, which can be measured, ablated, and pruned as whole components.

Figure
1
gives a high-level view of the structure we study. A small set of FFN channels carries most of the loss-sensitive channel mass; these channels are only partially explained by activation outliers, and pruning performance can collapse when methods remove too many of them.

(B)

(C)

(D)

Structured pruning is both a route to deployable LLM compression
(
Ma et al. 2023
;
An et al. 2024
;
Yin et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2025
)
and a useful diagnostic: removing whole FFN channels reveals whether importance is diffuse or concentrated. The goal is straightforward: remove components that contribute little to model performance, but the practice is difficult. Unstructured pruning can reach high sparsity
(
Frantar & Alistarh 2023
;
Sun et al. 2024b
)
, while hardware gains usually require removing whole neurons or channels. That is exactly where quality often drops.

We study the intermediate channels of FFN layers, which account for a large share of parameters in modern decoder LLMs and are a natural target for structured compression
(
Touvron et al. 2023
;
Grattafiori et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2024
)
. Our starting observation is that loss sensitivity across FFN channels is extremely uneven. Using a Fisher-style proxy that combines activations with gradient information, we find that the top 1% of channels per layer captures between 33.0% and 86.1% of the total sensitivity mass (median 58.7%). We call these channels
supernodes
. Their sensitivity can exceed the layer mean by roughly three orders of magnitude, and often by more.

Supernodes are not merely statistical outliers: targeted ablations indicate that they are functionally critical. When we force a pruning mask to remove them, perplexity jumps from 6.2 to over
8\times 10^{5}
, even at only 50% sparsity (Appendix Table
A5
). Removing the same number of non-supernode channels is far less damaging. This points to one common failure mode of aggressive structured pruning: many methods eventually hit a very small set of channels that the model relies on heavily.

Because LP is estimated on calibration data, we also separate two notions of stability. The
phenomenon
is stable: extreme LP concentration appears across calibration domains. The
identities
are less stable: the specific top-LP channels are domain-conditioned. In practice, this means that
Scar
should be calibrated on data representative of the intended deployment domain.

After identifying the supernode core, we ask whether nearby non-core channels contain structure that can guide pruning. Within each layer, channels with overlapping high-magnitude coordinates in their
W_{\text{down}}
columns cluster around supernodes, forming what we call
write halos
. These halo channels show measurable redundancy with respect to the protected supernode core. Across layers, channels in the next layer that read heavily from the supernode output support form
read halos
. We use halo structure as a secondary refinement, not as the main explanation for pruning behavior.

We then use structured pruning as a functional test of this structure. At 50% FFN channel sparsity on Llama-3.1-8B, the baselines in Table
1
remove roughly 20–42% of supernodes and degrade to perplexities of several hundred to several thousand. Methods that protect all supernodes retain substantially lower perplexity: our best variant,
Scar
-Prot, reaches perplexity 54.8, compared with 989.2 for channel-adapted Wanda. Across methods, supernode hit-rate is strongly associated with perplexity, and controlled random-mask sweeps show a clear dose-response trend.

1.1
Contributions

Our contributions are fourfold. Together, they show that FFN importance is concentrated in a small loss-critical core and that preserving this core is often the difference between stable and unstable structured pruning.

First
, we define a Fisher-style channel loss proxy for FFN channels and use it to identify
supernodes
: the top 1% of channels by loss sensitivity. We show that these channels account for a large fraction of LP mass and that ablating them is far more damaging than ablating comparable non-supernode sets.

Second
, we show that supernode hit-rate explains a major failure mode of one-shot structured FFN pruning. Methods that remove more supernodes degrade more sharply, and controlled random-mask sweeps show a dose–response relationship between supernode removal and perplexity.

Third
, we characterize secondary halo structure around the supernode core. Write-halo channels share supernode write support and show higher redundancy with the protected core, while read-halo diagnostics suggest modest cross-layer dependence on the same support.

Fourth
, we validate LP concentration and core protection across Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B, Llama-2-7B, Qwen2-7B, targeted Llama-3.1-70B experiments, and OLMo-2-7B training checkpoints, where LP concentration increases during pretraining.

2
Background and Related Work

We first fix the channel-level notation used throughout the paper, then relate our work to three nearby lines of research: activation outliers, critical individual weights, and structured pruning. Supernodes sit between these prior phenomena. They are not individual weights, but they are also not generic activation outliers; they are structured FFN channels whose importance is defined through loss sensitivity.

2.1
Preliminaries: FFN Channels

We first review the FFN channel structure in which supernodes arise. Modern decoder LLMs such as Llama, Mistral, and Qwen commonly use SwiGLU-style feed-forward layers
(
Touvron et al. 2023
;
Grattafiori et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2024
)
.
For layer

:

\displaystyle=W_{\text{up}}h,\quad g=W_{\text{gate}}h,

(1)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\sigma(g)\odot z\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad y=W_{\text{down}}u\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where

A

W_{\text{down}}

The column

Equivalently, a channel has

2.2
Outlier Phenomena in LLMs

Several recent results suggest that rare components can have disproportionate influence in LLM computation. These components have been studied at different granularities, from activation coordinates to individual weights. We review them because they motivate the central question of this paper: whether an analogous concentration exists at the level of whole FFN channels.

Massive activations.

Sun et al. 2024a
show that a small number of activation coordinates exhibit unusually large values that behave like implicit bias terms and can create attention sinks.
We study FFN intermediate channels instead, defining supernodes by loss sensitivity rather than activation magnitude.

Outliers in quantization.

Activation outliers are also central to LLM quantization: LLM.int8() isolates outlier feature dimensions in higher precision
(
Dettmers et al. 2022
)
, and SmoothQuant reduces activation outlier difficulty by shifting scale from activations to weights
(
Xiao et al. 2023
)
.
These works show that outlier structure matters for compression. They do not, however, identify a channel-level loss-critical FFN core or test its role in structured pruning.

Super weights.

Yu et al. 2024
identify individual weight parameters whose removal destroys model output.
They show that as few as a single super weight can be critical, and that super weights induce corresponding super activations.
In contrast, we focus on structured FFN channels spanning rows and columns across projections, identify them by loss sensitivity, and characterize their halo neighborhoods.

Prior work shows that outlier activations and individual critical weights can strongly affect LLM behavior and compression.
We extend this line of evidence to structured FFN channels. Specifically, we show that second-order channel sensitivity is concentrated in a small loss-critical core, that ablating this core is highly damaging, and that protecting it substantially improves one-shot structured FFN pruning.
Halo structure provides an additional local signal for ranking non-core channels, but the primary contribution is the identification and validation of the supernode core.

2.3
Structured Pruning of LLMs

Pruning provides both a practical compression tool and a diagnostic for understanding which parts of a model are functionally important. In this paper, we use pruning in the second sense as well as the first: if a small set of channels is truly loss-critical, then pruning methods that remove those channels should fail more sharply than methods that protect them. Structured FFN pruning therefore gives a natural testbed for studying supernodes.

Unstructured pruning.

Wanda
(
Sun et al. 2024b
)
scores weights by
|w|\cdot|x|
and achieves strong unstructured sparsity.
SparseGPT
(
Frantar & Alistarh 2023
)
uses second-order information for one-shot weight removal.
These methods excel at fine-grained sparsity but do not yield hardware speedups without specialized sparse kernels.

Structured pruning.

Group Fisher Pruning
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
uses Fisher information to score parameter groups for removal in CNNs, establishing that group-level Fisher sensitivity is an effective structured pruning criterion.
LLM-Pruner
(
Ma et al. 2023
)
performs structural pruning with LoRA recovery.
OWL
(
Yin et al. 2024
)
extends Wanda with outlier-aware non-uniform layer-wise sparsity allocation.
OATS
(
Zhang & Papyan 2025
)
decomposes weights into sparse + low-rank terms.
The novelty here is not Fisher-based scoring itself, but the observation that such scoring reveals extreme concentration in a small channel subset (supernodes), and that protecting this subset accounts for much of the sharp pruning gap we observe.
However, aggressive
FFN channel removal
(our focus) remains challenging in one-shot settings: at 50% channel sparsity, standard channel-adapted methods can suffer severe perplexity degradation.

Why channel pruning is hard.

We argue that part of the difficulty comes from supernode structure.
The loss-critical core is so small that pruning rules not designed to protect it can remove many supernodes while still looking reasonable under aggregate saliency.
Once a method removes even a modest fraction of this core, model quality can degrade sharply.

3
Supernode Characterization

We now formalize channel importance and characterize the resulting structure.

3.1
Loss Proxy: Second-Order Channel Sensitivity

Consider masking channel
i
(setting
u_{i}=0
).
This induces the output perturbation
\Delta y=-u_{i}v_{i}
, where
v_{i}=W_{\text{down}}[:,i]
.
Under a Fisher/Gauss–Newton approximation, the expected loss increase from this perturbation is:

\mathbb{E}[\Delta\mathcal{L}]\approx\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[u_{i}^{2}\cdot(v_{i}^{\top}g_{y})^{2}\right],

(3)

where
g_{y}=\nabla_{y}\mathcal{L}
is the loss gradient at the FFN output.

Defining the
projected gradient signal
as
s_{i}=v_{i}^{\top}g_{y}=(\nabla_{u}\mathcal{L})_{i}
(the
i
-th component of the gradient backpropagated through
W_{\text{down}}
), we obtain the
loss proxy
:

\mathrm{LP}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[(u_{i}\cdot s_{i})^{2}\right].

(4)

Three aspects of this proxy matter for the experiments below.

Interpretation.

\mathrm{LP}_{i}
captures the joint second moment of activation and gradient.
A large
\mathrm{LP}_{i}
can arise from large activations, large projected gradients, or strong sample-level alignment between the two.
Thus, LP measures loss-sensitive channel importance rather than pure magnitude.

Magnitude controls.

Appendix Fig.
A1
provides a top-tail ablation check for
\mathrm{LP}
and shows that
\mathrm{LP}
is not explained away by simple magnitude metrics such as activation power or weight norms (additional diagnostics in Appendix Table
A4
).

Efficient computation.

\mathrm{LP}_{i}
requires only quantities already computed in standard backpropagation: we record the FFN intermediate activations
u_{i}
in the forward pass, record
s_{i}=(W_{\text{down}}^{\top}g_{y})_{i}
in the backward pass, and accumulate
\frac{1}{2}(u_{i}s_{i})^{2}
over calibration tokens.
This avoids per-channel backward passes.

3.2
Supernode Definition

We use this proxy to define supernodes within each layer rather than globally across the model. This makes the definition comparable across layers with different score scales and gives every layer a small candidate core.

Definition 1

(Supernodes)
.

For layer
\ell
with
m
intermediate channels and loss proxy scores
\{\mathrm{LP}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
(Eq.
4
), let
\operatorname{Top}_{\rho}(\{\mathrm{LP}_{i}\})
denote the
\lceil\rho m\rceil
channels with the largest LP values. The
supernode set
is

\mathcal{M}_{\ell}=\operatorname{Top}_{\rho}(\{\mathrm{LP}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}).

Unless stated otherwise, we use
\rho=0.01
, so supernodes are the top 1% of FFN channels by LP within each layer.

3.3
Empirical Characterization

We first ask how much of the loss-proxy mass is carried by the highest-scoring channels. The distribution is highly skewed: a very small fraction of channels accounts for a large share of the layer-level sensitivity.

Finding 1

(Loss Proxy Concentration)
.

Across all layers of Llama-3.1-8B, the top
\rho=1\%
of channels by
\mathrm{LP}_{i}
captures between 33.0% and 86.1% of the total proxy mass
\sum_{i}\mathrm{LP}_{i}
, with a median of 58.7%.
This pattern holds across Mistral-7B, Llama-2-7B, and Qwen2-7B.

Finding 2

(Extreme LP Outliers)
.

Supernodes are not merely high-scoring channels; they are extreme LP outliers.
Figure
1
C
compares the maximum LP value in each layer to the layer mean and shows about 2.5–4 orders of magnitude of separation, with a median max/mean ratio of about
2.6\times 10^{3}
.
This gap varies systematically with depth, with larger extremes in early and late layers.

Finding 3

(Concentration Across FFN Depth)
.

LP concentration is extreme throughout FFN depth, but the degree varies with layer position.
Figure
1
D
shows that loss-proxy mass accumulates within the top few percent of FFN channels far above a uniform-distribution reference, with the tightest concentration in early (L0–L3) and late (L28–L31) layers and a slightly more gradual rise in middle layers (L14–L17).
This suggests that LP concentration is a recurring FFN-level pattern, with stronger concentration near the network’s edges.

Why does concentration emerge?

LP concentration is not explained by activation magnitude or curvature alone.
In Appendix Fig.
A9
, we decompose LP into activation-only, curvature-only, factorized activation–curvature, and exact activation–gradient terms.
Exact LP is more concentrated than the simpler baselines, especially at 70B scale, suggesting that supernodes arise from joint activation–gradient structure rather than from activation outliers alone.
Because the per-layer summaries and the 8B/70B factor comparison use different aggregation protocols, percentages should only be compared within the same protocol.

Calibration-domain stability.

We distinguish two notions of stability:
phenomenon stability
(does extreme LP concentration occur?) and
identity stability
(are the same channels identified?).
Across the four calibration domains we test (WikiText-2, C4, code, and arXiv), extreme LP concentration appears consistently, with per-layer top-1% mass in the same broad range as the main WikiText-2 analysis.
However, the specific channel identities are domain-conditioned: mean pairwise Jaccard overlap is only
\approx
6% at the 1% supernode fraction.
This instability is not the same as activation-defined outliers: even within a single domain, the top-1% LP and activation-defined channel sets overlap only weakly (Fig.
2
D), although activation-defined supernodes can still serve as an effective protected-set surrogate when SCAR’s pruning score is held fixed (Appendix §
D.4
).
Activation-defined supernodes are more stable across domains, whereas LP-defined supernodes better reflect domain-specific loss sensitivity (Appendix §
D.4
).
This reinforces the practical recommendation that
Scar
should be calibrated on data representative of the intended deployment domain.

4
Write-Halo Structure

Having identified the loss-critical core, we next ask whether the remaining channels are locally structured around it. This analysis has a narrower role than the supernode result: it looks for signals that may help rank non-supernode channels once the core is protected. We call these neighborhoods halos, defined by write-pattern overlap (Eq.
6
) together with redundancy diagnostics.

We distinguish two related concepts. A
write halo
consists of channels within the same layer whose write vectors overlap with the supernode-defined write support. This is the halo object used in the pruning diagnostics. A
read coupling
effect occurs when channels in the next layer concentrate their read weights on the supernode output support, providing a mechanistic link across layers.

4.1
Write-Pattern Overlap

Channels in an FFN layer do not connect directly to each other; they interact through their contributions to the shared output
y=W_{\text{down}}u
.
We therefore define functional proximity via
downstream write-pattern overlap
.

Let
v_{i}=W_{\text{down}}[:,i]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the write direction for channel
i
.
For a supernode set
\mathcal{M}
in a layer, define the
aggregated supernode write pattern
:

a=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{M}}|v_{s}|\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(5)

where
|\cdot|
is elementwise absolute value.
This captures which output dimensions are heavily used by supernodes.
Although
a
is dense across the
d
output dimensions, its largest coordinates define the concentrated reference support used below.

For any non-supernode channel
j
, let
\mathcal{S}=\text{TopK}(a)
be the indices of the
K
largest coordinates of
a
(the “core write support”).
We define
connectivity to supernodes
:

\mathrm{Conn}_{j}=\frac{\sum_{h\in\mathcal{S}}|v_{j}[h]|}{\|v_{j}\|_{1}}.

(6)

\mathrm{Conn}_{j}\in[0,1]
measures what fraction of channel
j
’s write mass lands on output dimensions heavily used by supernodes.
We use
K=256
by default (roughly 6% of
d=4096
).

4.2
Halo Definition

The connectivity score is continuous, but for pruning and ablation experiments we need a concrete set of nearby non-supernode channels. We therefore define the write halo as the highest-connectivity subset of non-supernodes.

Definition 2

(Write halo)
.

For layer
\ell
, let
\mathcal{M}_{\ell}
be the supernode set and let
\mathrm{Conn}_{j}
be the write-connectivity score from Eq.
6
for each non-supernode channel
j
. The
write halo

\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
is the top
\eta
fraction of non-supernode channels by
\mathrm{Conn}_{j}
. Unless stated otherwise, we use
\eta=0.10
.

Thus, write-halo channels are non-supernodes whose write vectors place the most mass on output dimensions heavily used by supernodes.

Write-halo size.

By construction, write halos are the top
\eta{=}10\%
of non-supernode channels by connectivity
\mathrm{Conn}
(Definition
2
), i.e., roughly
0.1(1-\rho)\approx 9.9\%
of channels per layer when
\rho=1\%
.
The halo size is fixed by definition. The relevant question is whether membership in this fixed-size set is associated with redundancy in loss-relevant contribution space (Fig.
2
).

5
Directed Redundancy Within Write Halos

Write-pattern overlap defines which channels are geometrically close to supernodes, but geometric proximity does not guarantee functional redundancy. Some halo channels may write to similar output directions while carrying unique information; others may be largely replaceable once the supernode core is preserved. To distinguish these cases, we measure
directed redundancy
in loss-relevant contribution space.

5.1
Loss-Relevant Contributions

Raw activation correlation can be misleading: two channels may have correlated activations but write to orthogonal output directions.
We therefore measure redundancy using the
loss-relevant scalar contribution
:

q_{i}=u_{i}\cdot s_{i},

(7)

which captures channel
i
’s instantaneous contribution along the local loss gradient direction.

Interpretation.

q_{i}
is the first-order term in the loss perturbation from masking channel
i
:
\Delta\mathcal{L}\approx-q_{i}+O(q_{i}^{2})
.
If
q_{i}
and
q_{j}
are highly correlated, then channel
j
’s loss-relevant contribution may be less unique, because it is partially predictable from channel
i
’s contribution.

5.2
Gaussian-Inspired Redundancy Score

For two channels
i,j
, let
\rho_{ij}=\text{corr}(q_{i},q_{j})
estimated over calibration tokens.
For a bivariate Gaussian, mutual information is
-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\rho_{ij}^{2})

(
Cover & Thomas 2006
, Ch. 8)
.
We use this formula as motivation, but define a positive-correlation redundancy score:

\rho^{+}_{ij}=\max(\rho_{ij},0),\qquad\mathrm{Red}(i,j)=-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-(\rho^{+}_{ij})^{2}).

(8)

This treats positively correlated loss-relevant contributions as redundant, while not counting anti-correlations as substitutes.
Intuitively, anti-correlation can reflect canceling or competing contributions rather than substitutability, so a symmetric
\rho_{ij}^{2}
score can overestimate functional redundancy for pruning.

5.3
Directed Redundancy to Supernode Core

For a halo channel
j\in\mathcal{H}
, we define redundancy to the supernode set as:

\mathrm{Red}^{\rightarrow\mathcal{M}}_{j}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{s\in\mathrm{Top}\text{-}k(\mathcal{M};\,j)}\mathrm{Red}(j,s),

(9)

where
\mathrm{Top}\text{-}k(\mathcal{M};\,j)
returns the
k
supernodes with largest
\mathrm{Red}(j,s)
for channel
j
(we use
k=5
unless stated otherwise).

Finding 4

(Halo Redundancy)
.

Write-halo channels tend to have higher directed redundancy to the supernode core than a matched sample of non-halo channels, with substantial layer-to-layer variation (Fig.
2
).
The enrichment is modest in absolute scale, but consistent: aggregated redundancy to the protected core is about
2\times
higher for halo channels, and the per-layer effect varies substantially rather than being driven by a single layer (Fig.
2
A-B).
We report the per-layer redundancy profile and a matched non-halo comparison in Fig.
2
A-B, and use conditional ablations in Fig.
2
C to test the effect size directly.

5.4
Conditional Halo Ablation Control

Correlation-based redundancy suggests that some halo channels may be less unique once the supernode core is preserved. We test this with an ablation control: (i) a random subset of halo channels while preserving supernodes, and (ii) a matched subset of non-halo channels at the same count (matched by loss-proxy quantiles).
The control supports the same conclusion: the dominant effect comes from the supernode core itself. Halo-subset ablations are only modestly more damaging than matched non-halo ablations, and both are far smaller than removing supernodes (Fig.
2
C; enlarged in Appendix Fig.
A3
).

5.5
Cross-Layer Read Dependence on Supernode Write Support

The write-pattern analysis also suggests a cross-layer diagnostic: if supernodes emphasize a reference support
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[d]
in the residual stream (here
\mathcal{S}=\mathrm{TopK}(a)
), we can test whether some next-layer FFN channels preferentially read from
\mathcal{S}
.
We treat this read-side analysis as a diagnostic rather than as the main pruning signal, because the observed association is weaker than the within-layer write-halo effect.

Definition 3

(Read halo (cross-layer diagnostic))
.

Fix layer
\ell
with supernode-defined write support
\mathcal{S}=\mathrm{TopK}(a)\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
For channel
j
in layer
\ell{+}1
, define the
read-connectivity score

\mathrm{ReadConn}_{j}=\frac{\|W_{\mathrm{gate}}[j,\mathcal{S}]\|_{1}+\|W_{\mathrm{up}}[j,\mathcal{S}]\|_{1}}{\|W_{\mathrm{gate}}[j,:]\|_{1}+\|W_{\mathrm{up}}[j,:]\|_{1}}.

This measures the fraction of channel
j
’s input-weight mass concentrated on the supernode output support.
The
read halo
is the top
\eta_{r}
fraction by
\mathrm{ReadConn}
(default
\eta_{r}{=}10\%
); we primarily use
\mathrm{ReadConn}
as a continuous diagnostic rather than a hard set.

We then ablate the support subspace
\mathcal{S}
at the input to the next-layer FFN and measure each channel’s change in intermediate activation
|\Delta u_{j}|
.
We find a modest positive association, with elevated top/bottom disruption ratios in several layers, especially near the edges of the network (Appendix Fig.
A6
).
Because the raw association is modest, we report decile-binned effect sizes for interpretability.

6
Validation: Structured Pruning as a Probe

If supernodes capture genuine functional organization, pruning strategies that preserve them should be more robust than otherwise similar strategies that remove them.
We use structured FFN channel pruning to test this claim quantitatively, with halo features treated as a secondary refinement after the core is protected.

6.1
Pruning Framework:
Scar

We implement
Scar
(
S
upernode-
C
onstrained
A

The main comparison suggests that supernode hit-rate is a key variable. Here we test that more directly by changing the protected set and by exposing supernodes to pruning on purpose.

8
Limitations

Several limitations remain. Supernode identification depends on calibration data, so large distribution shifts may require re-estimating the scores even if the
phenomenon
of extreme concentration still appears. We study FFN channels because the effect is clearest there; we do not test whether analogous loss-critical cores exist in attention blocks or MoE experts. LP is also a Fisher/Gauss–Newton approximation and may miss nonlinear or non-Gaussian effects.

There are also scope limitations. Although we include targeted 70B validation, the most complete benchmark coverage is still at 7–8B scale. The OLMo checkpoint analysis is observational. It shows that LP concentration is acquired during pretraining, but it does not identify the training mechanism that creates supernodes. Finally, we do not directly evaluate quantization, fine-tuning, or downstream model-editing interventions. These are natural applications of the supernode lens, but they are beyond what the present experiments establish.

9
Conclusion

We identify a small loss-critical core within LLM FFN layers. Under a Fisher-style activation-gradient proxy, channel-level sensitivity is highly concentrated, and direct ablations show that removing this core is far more damaging than removing matched non-core channels. In one-shot structured FFN pruning, a major failure mode is direct supernode removal; protecting these channels explains much of the robustness gap between SCAR and several channel-pruning baselines. Halo structure provides a secondary ranking signal around the protected core, but it is not the main driver of pruning failure. The same LP-concentration pattern appears across several model families, remains visible at 70B scale, and emerges during OLMo-2 pretraining. These results suggest that LLM FFNs develop a highly skewed channel-importance geometry, placing activation outliers and super weights alongside a structured channel-level counterpart that can be measured, protected, and potentially shaped during training.

Impact Statement

This paper studies reliability in structured pruning for large language models. More reliable pruning can reduce memory use and computational cost, making models cheaper to run and easier to analyze. These efficiency gains may support beneficial uses, while also lowering barriers to misuse. Our contribution is analytical and methodological rather than application-specific; its broader impact depends on how improved compression methods are deployed.

For halo channel

\mathrm{Protect}_{j}=\alpha+(1-\alpha)(1-r_{j}^{\gamma}),

(10)

The three SCAR variants use the following channel scores for non-supernode channels:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Score}_{i}^{\mathrm{LP}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{LP}_{i},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Score}_{i}^{\mathrm{Prot}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{LP}_{i}\mathrm{Protect}_{i},

(12)

Because lower scores are pruned first, multiplying LP by a smaller protection value makes highly redundant halo channels more likely to be removed.
Channels with low redundancy (low

Appendix C
Derivations

This section provides the derivations behind the two main quantities used in the paper: the Fisher-style channel loss proxy and the Gaussian-inspired redundancy score. These derivations clarify the approximations and show where the resulting scores enter the pruning pipeline.

C.1
Loss Proxy Derivation

Our loss proxy builds on the classical second-order sensitivity framework introduced by
LeCun et al. 1989
(Optimal Brain Damage) and extended by
Molchanov et al. 2019
for modern neural networks.

Masking channel
i
induces
\Delta u=-u_{i}e_{i}
and
\Delta y=-u_{i}v_{i}
.
Under Fisher/Gauss–Newton approximation, the expected loss change is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta\mathcal{L}]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\Delta y^{\top}F_{y}\Delta y]

(14)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[u_{i}^{2}\cdot v_{i}^{\top}F_{y}v_{i}]

(15)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[u_{i}^{2}\cdot(v_{i}^{\top}g_{y})^{2}]

(16)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[(u_{i}s_{i})^{2}]=\mathrm{LP}_{i}.

(17)

The key approximation replaces the Fisher information
F_{y}
with its empirical estimate
g_{y}g_{y}^{\top}
, which is standard in scalable importance estimation
(
Molchanov et al. 2019
)
.

Relation to weight-level pruning.

Standard unstructured pruning methods like
Han et al. 2015
use magnitude-based criteria.
Our loss proxy extends this to structured channel removal by jointly considering activation magnitude (
u_{i}
) and gradient coupling (
s_{i}
), capturing loss-sensitive importance beyond pure magnitude.

C.2
Gaussian MI Motivation for Redundancy

For loss-relevant contributions
q_{i}=u_{i}\cdot s_{i}
and
q_{j}=u_{j}\cdot s_{j}
, the exact Gaussian mutual information provides a useful reference point.
For jointly Gaussian
(q_{i},q_{j})
with Pearson correlation
\rho_{ij}
:

I(q_{i};q_{j})=-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\rho_{ij}^{2}).

(18)

This is the analytic MI for a bivariate Gaussian, which depends only on the correlation coefficient.
Our main redundancy score is a positive-only variant motivated by this expression:

\displaystyle\rho^{+}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\max(\rho_{ij},0),

(19)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Red}(i,j)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1-(\rho^{+}_{ij})^{2}\right).

(20)

It measures how much positively correlated information about
q_{i}
’s contribution to loss is already captured by
q_{j}
, without treating anti-correlations as substitutable.
Channels with high directed redundancy to the supernode core are candidates for lower-risk removal because their loss-relevant contributions may be partially predictable from protected supernodes.

D.4
LP-Defined vs Activation-Defined Supernodes

A natural question is whether LP-defined supernodes differ from activation-defined ones, or whether they are mostly the same channels under another name. The main text already shows the core 8B result in Fig.
2
D-E; here we add the matched 70B control and the scale and stability analyses that help interpret it.
We find that the two definitions identify substantially different channel sets at the default 1% threshold (Jaccard
\approx
5.4% at 8B,
\approx
8.4% at 70B; Spearman correlation
\approx
0.26 at 8B,
\approx
0.31 at 70B).
This overlap is itself threshold-sensitive: when the protected core is made smaller, the LP–activation intersection drops further (e.g., to
\approx
2.8% at 0.1% on 8B), while larger fractions produce gradually higher overlap.
This is the sensitivity question most relevant to the paper’s main claims, because the supernode fraction determines which channels are treated as the protected core, whereas choices such as the connectivity top-
K
only affect the secondary halo construction.
Despite this low overlap, when the
pruning score
is held fixed as SCAR-LP and only the
protected set
definition is changed, pruning outcomes are very similar: on Llama-3.1-70B at 50% sparsity, SCAR-LP with LP-defined supernodes achieves perplexity 10.71 and SCAR-LP with activation-L2-defined supernodes achieves perplexity 10.71 (shared protected channels: 15.4% of each set).

Figure A7:
Threshold sensitivity of LP-vs-activation overlap and LP domain stability.

(A)
Mean LP-vs-activation Jaccard rises with the supernode fraction: on 8B it increases from 2.8% at 0.1% to 5.4% at 1% and 9.9% at 5%; on 70B it increases from 4.9% at 0.1% to 8.4% at 1% and 16.2% at 5%.
(B)
LP cross-domain stability is also fraction-sensitive: mean pairwise Jaccard across WikiText/C4/Code/arXiv rises from 4.6% at 0.1% to 6.1% at 1% and 12.3% at 5%. The default 1% setting therefore lies in a low-overlap regime, but the trend with fraction is smooth rather than discontinuous.

Thus, activation-defined supernodes can serve as an effective
surrogate protected set
within SCAR.
However, using activation magnitude as the
pruning score
(rather than LP) yields much worse results: plain activation-L2 pruning gives perplexity 26.84 at the same sparsity on 70B.
Our interpretation is that LP is the more direct loss-sensitive lens for
scoring
channels, while activation outliers identify structurally critical channels that are effective for
protection
.
Figure
A8

protection

Appendix E
Supernode Emergence During Pretraining (Extended)

The main text (§
7.2
) summarizes the OLMo-2-7B training-checkpoint trajectory. This appendix gives the setup and the extended replication of our structured pruning suite on OLMo-2-7B at the final checkpoint.

Setup.

We compute
\mathrm{LP}
on ten OLMo-2-7B checkpoints spanning
\sim
1B to
\sim
4T training tokens (nine stage-1 steps plus the final post-stage-2 release,
main
). All checkpoints are evaluated with the identical calibration protocol used for Llama-3.1-8B: 64 sequences of 512 tokens from the WikiText-2 training split.

Final-checkpoint concentration.

On OLMo-2-7B (
main
), the top 1% of FFN channels by LP captures a median of 71.7% of layer LP mass (range over 32 layers: 33.3–94.1%). The median LP max/mean ratio is 3690, and the mean activation-outlier ratio is
\approx
25
\times
. This matches the FFN-channel concentration pattern we document for Llama, Mistral, and Qwen; on OLMo the top-1% mass fraction is in fact
higher
than on Llama-3.1-8B.

Interpretation.

Figure
5
in the main text shows the three key trajectories. Taken together, they suggest an emergence-and-sorting account: the FFN progressively develops a small set of channels whose joint activation–gradient product dominates the loss proxy, but the specific channels filling that role are not fixed until fairly late in pretraining. We frame this as evidence rather than a formal theory; confirming the underlying mechanism (for instance whether supernodes are locked to specific massive-activation token positions, attention sinks, or residual-stream axes) is beyond this paper.

Pruning replication on OLMo-2-7B.

To check whether core protection matters in the pruning setting for OLMo as well, we run the full structured pruning suite (SCAR-LP, SCAR-Prot, SCAR-Conn, Wanda-channel, Magnitude, activation-L2, Random) on OLMo-2-7B at six sparsity levels (20–70%). Figure
A13
shows the sparsity–perplexity curves. The unpruned perplexity is 6.03; at 50% FFN channel sparsity SCAR-LP reaches 15.43 versus 15.20 for channel-adapted Wanda, essentially tied at this sparsity with Wanda marginally lower, while magnitude pruning degrades sharply (134.08) and random pruning yields extremely high perplexity (
\gg
10
3
). The qualitative pattern on OLMo differs from Llama-3.1-8B: SCAR-LP and Wanda remain close across the 20–70% sparsity range, with Wanda rising slightly faster only at the highest sparsity (70%). This is consistent with the broader observation that extreme Wanda degradation is most pronounced on Llama-3.1-8B, while other SwiGLU models show a much smaller SCAR vs. Wanda gap (see Fig.
A12
for the 70B analogue).

Figure A13:
OLMo-2-7B structured pruning curves.
Perplexity on WikiText-2 as a function of FFN channel sparsity for SCAR variants and baselines. SCAR-LP and Wanda-channel track each other closely across sparsities, with a small separation only at 70%; magnitude and random pruning are far worse throughout.
