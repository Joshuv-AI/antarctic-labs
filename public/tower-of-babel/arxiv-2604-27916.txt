Fixed-point-free automorphisms of solvable Lie algebras
Dietrich Burde, Karel Dekimpe

Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate the existence of fixed-point-free automorphisms for finite-dimensional Lie algebras. By a result of Jacobson, a Lie algebra admitting a fixed-point-free automorphism is solvable. We prove that such a Lie algebra must be even strongly unimodular. We find a necessary and sufficient criterion such that a complex almost abelian Lie algebra admits a fixed-point-free automorphism. For complex filiform Lie algebras we show that the existence of a fixed-point-free automorphism is equivalent to not being characteristically nilpotent.



2000 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 17B30, 17B08

1.
Introduction

Fixed-point-free automorphisms of groups and Lie rings have been studied by many authors.
An automorphism
\varphi
of a group
G
is called f.p.f. (fixed-point-free) if
C_{G}(\varphi)=1
,
that is, the only fixed point of
\varphi
is
1
. For Lie rings, an automorphism is called f.p.f., if

\varphi(x)=x
implies
x=0
for all
x
.
Higman showed in
1957
that a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field of characteristic zero
admitting an f.p.f. automorphism of prime order
p
is nilpotent. It was then asked for a function
h(p)
,
which is a good bound on the nilpotency class, depending on
p
.
In
1963
Kreknin showed that if
\mathfrak{g}
admits an an f.p.f. automorphism of
any finite order, then
\mathfrak{g}
is solvable. Jacobson proved the solvability also without the assumption
that
\varphi
has finite order. It is also known that a finite group admitting an f.p.f. automorphism is solvable.
There are many other results, and a detailed survey can be found in the paper
[
7
]

by Khukhro.

In this paper we present some new results on Lie algebras admitting an f.p.f. automorphism.
In section
3
, we show that any such Lie algebra is
strongly unimodular
. Here strongly unimodular Lie algebras
form a subclass of unimodular Lie algebras, arising in the context of lattices in Lie groups.
Garland showed in
1966
that the Lie algebra of a (connected and simply connected) solvable Lie group admitting
a lattice is strongly unimodular.

In section
4
we classify all complex Lie algebras of dimension
n\leq 4
admitting an f.p.f. automorphism.
For such Lie algebras we also determine all possible finite orders of f.p.f. automorphisms. In dimension
4

we find a one-parameter family of Lie algebras, where all Lie algebras are strongly unimodular, but only
a few of them admit an f.p.f. automorphism. This family consists of almost abelian Lie algebras.

In section
5
we determine, motivated by the family of Lie algebras in dimension
4
, a necessary and sufficient
criterion for the existence of an f.p.f. automorphism for complex almost abelian Lie algebras.
It is related to
n
-cyclotomic operators, see Theorem
5.4
.

Finally, in section
6
we prove that a complex filiform Lie algebra admits an f.p.f. automorphism if and only if it
is not characteristically nilpotent, if and only if it admits a nonsingular derivation, and if and only if it is a
derived Lie algebra, i.e., it equals
[\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{h}]
for some Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}
. Here we are using results from a recent
paper
[
9
]
by Siciliano and Towers.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field
K
of characteristic zero.
Denote by
\Aut(\mathfrak{g})
the group of Lie algebra automorphisms of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A Lie algebra automorphism
\varphi\in\Aut(\mathfrak{g})
is called
fixed-point-free
, or f.p.f.,
if
\varphi(x)=x
implies
x=0
for all
x\in\mathfrak{g}
.

Note that an automorphism
\varphi\in\Aut(\mathfrak{g})
is f.p.f., if and only if
\varphi-\id
is
nonsingular.

It is well known that the existence of an f.p.f. automorphism (of finite or infinite order) has
consequences for the algebraic structure of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. For a survey on some results see

[
10
]
. As we have mentioned in the introduction, Higman
[
5
]
showed in
1957
, that the
existence of an f.p.f. automorphism of prime order of
\mathfrak{g}
implies that
\mathfrak{g}
is nilpotent.

Proposition 2.2

(Higman)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field of characteristic zero admitting
an f.p.f. automorphism of order
p
with
p
prime. Then
\mathfrak{g}
is nilpotent.

For
p=2
we have the following result, which is much easier to prove, see
[
10
]
.

Proposition 2.3

(Zha)
.

Suppose that
\mathfrak{g}
admits an f.p.f. automorphism of order
2
. Then
\mathfrak{g}
is abelian.

Proof.

Let
\varphi
be an f.p.f. automorphism of order
2
. Then the linear transformation

\id-\varphi
is surjective, so that every element in
\mathfrak{g}
can be written as

x-\varphi(x)
for some
x
. Because of

\varphi(x-\varphi(x))=\varphi(x)-\varphi^{2}(x)=-(x-\varphi(x))

it follows that
\varphi(x)=-x
for all
x\in\mathfrak{g}
. This implies

\varphi([x,y])=[\varphi(x),\varphi(y)]=[-x,-y]=[x,y]

for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
. Thus
[x,y]=0
for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
.
∎

A much more general result is the following one by Kreknin
[
8
]
, published in
1963
.

Proposition 2.4

(Kreknin)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field of characteristic zero admitting
an f.p.f. automorphism of order
n
. Then
\mathfrak{g}
is solvable.

This result is still true if
\mathfrak{g}
admits any f.p.f. automorphism, not necessarily of finite order.
This has been shown by Jacobson
[
6
]
, see Theorem
9
.

Proposition 2.5

(Jacobson)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field of characteristic zero admitting
an f.p.f. automorphism. Then
\mathfrak{g}
is solvable.

The converse of this proposition is not true.

Example 2.6
.

The
2
-dimensional solvable non-abelian Lie algebra
\mathfrak{r}_{2}(K)
does not admit an f.p.f. automorphism.

Indeed, let
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
be a basis, with
[e_{1},e_{2}]=e_{1}
. Then any automorphism
has the form

\varphi=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\cr 0&1\end{pmatrix}

for some scalars
\alpha,\beta\in K
. But we have
\det(\varphi-\id)=0
, and
\varphi
has a nonzero fixed-point.

Denote by
\mathfrak{n}
the nilradical of
\mathfrak{g}
. Let
\mathfrak{n}^{k}=[\mathfrak{n},\mathfrak{n}^{k-1}]
be the
k
-th term in the descending central
series of
\mathfrak{n}
for all
k\geq 1
, with
\mathfrak{n}^{1}=\mathfrak{n}
.

Definition 2.7
.

A Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is called
strongly unimodular
, if it is solvable and if for all
x\in\mathfrak{g}
the
induced operator of
{\rm ad}(x)
on each subspace
\mathfrak{n}^{k}/\mathfrak{n}^{k+1}
has trace zero, for all
k\geq 1
.

A Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is called
unimodular
, if
\mathop{\rm tr}({\rm ad}(x))=0
for all
x\in\mathfrak{g}
.

Strongly unimodular Lie algebras arise in the study of lattices in solvable Lie groups.
The following result was proved by Garland
[
3
]
.

Theorem 2.8

(Garland)
.

Let
G
be a connected and simply connected solvable Lie group admitting a cocompact lattice.
Then its Lie algebra is strongly unimodular.

It is well known that a strongly unimodular Lie algebra is unimodular. For completeness we will
give a proof here.

Lemma 2.9
.

A strongly unimodular Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is unimodular.

Proof.

As vector space,
\mathfrak{g}
corresponds to the direct sum

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{n}\oplus\mathfrak{n}/\mathfrak{n}^{2}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{2}/\mathfrak{n}^{3}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{c}/\mathfrak{n}^{c+1},

where
\mathfrak{n}^{c+1}=0
. Since
\mathfrak{g}
is solvable,
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
is nilpotent, so that
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]\subseteq\mathfrak{n}
.
Hence the quotient Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{n}
is abelian, and we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm tr}({\rm ad}(x))

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm tr}({\rm ad}(x)_{0})+\mathop{\rm tr}({\rm ad}(x)_{1})+\cdots+\mathop{\rm tr}({\rm ad}(x)_{c})

\displaystyle=0

for all
x\in\mathfrak{g}
, where
{\rm ad}(x)_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq c
) is the induced operator on the quotient
\mathfrak{n}^{i}/\mathfrak{n}^{i+1}
and
{\rm ad}(x_{0})=0
(which is the induced operator on
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{n}
).
∎

The converse statement need not be true.

Example 2.10
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the solvable non-nilpotent Lie algebra with basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{5}\}
and
defining Lie brackets

\displaystyle[e_{1},e_{5}]

\displaystyle=2\alpha e_{1},

\displaystyle[e_{2},e_{5}]

\displaystyle=\alpha e_{2}+e_{3},

\displaystyle[e_{4},e_{5}]

\displaystyle=-4\alpha e_{4},

\displaystyle[e_{2},e_{3}]

\displaystyle=e_{1},

\displaystyle[e_{3},e_{5}]

\displaystyle=-e_{2}+\alpha e_{3},

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. Then
\mathfrak{g}
is unimodular but not strongly unimodular,
for all
\alpha\neq 0
.

Indeed, the trace of
{\rm ad}(e_{5})
, induced on
\mathfrak{n}/\mathfrak{n}^{2}={\rm span}\{\bar{e}_{2},\bar{e}_{3},\bar{e}_{4}\}
(with
\bar{e}_{i}=e_{i}+\mathfrak{n}^{2}
) equals
2\alpha\neq 0
.

3.
Fixed-point-free automorphisms and strongly unimodular Lie algebras

It turns out that there is a strong link between the existence of an f.p.f. automorphism
for a Lie algebra and strong unimodularity. We will show the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field of characteristic zero admitting
an f.p.f. automorphism. Then
\mathfrak{g}
is strongly unimodular.

Proof.

By Jacobson’s theorem in
[
6
]
,
\mathfrak{g}
is solvable. Let
\varphi
be an f.p.f. automorphism of
\mathfrak{g}
.
We may assume that the field
K
is algebraically closed, and that
\varphi
is semisimple. Indeed, the semisimple
part
\varphi_{s}
of
\varphi
is f.p.f. if and only if
\varphi
is f.p.f. We will prove the result by induction on
the nilpotency class
c
of the nilradical
\mathfrak{n}
of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Let
c=1
, i.e., let
\mathfrak{n}
be abelian. Since
\varphi
is semisimple and
K
is algebraically closed, we can
find a basis of eigenvectors
\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m},b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}\}
of
\mathfrak{g}
, such that

\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}\}
is a basis of
\mathfrak{n}
. So there exist constants
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}\in K
with

\varphi(a_{i})=\alpha_{i}a_{i}
and
\varphi(b_{j})=\beta_{j}b_{j}
.
As
\varphi
is f.p.f., we have that
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{j}
are neither equal to 0 nor to 1. We have to show
that for each
1\leq i\leq m
, the linear map

{\rm ad}(a_{i})_{|\mathfrak{n}}:\mathfrak{n}\to\mathfrak{n},\;x\mapsto[a_{i},x]

has trace zero. Let
c^{i}_{kj}\in K
be such that

(1)

[a_{i},b_{j}]=c^{i}_{1j}b_{1}+c^{i}_{2j}b_{2}+\cdots+c^{i}_{kj}b_{k}+\cdots+c^{i}_{nj}b_{n}.

Then we have to show that the matrix
\left(c^{i}_{kj}\right)_{1\leq k,j\leq n}
(which is the matrix representation
of
{\rm ad}(a_{i})_{|\mathfrak{n}}
) has trace zero for all
i
.
By applying
\varphi
to both sides of (
1
), we find that

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}(c^{i}_{1j}b_{1}+c^{i}_{2j}b_{2}+\cdots+c^{i}_{kj}b_{k}+\cdots+c^{i}_{nj}b_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}[a_{i},b_{j}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi([a_{i},b_{j}])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(c^{i}_{1j}b_{1}+c^{i}_{2j}b_{2}+\cdots+c^{i}_{kj}b_{k}+\cdots+c^{i}_{nj}b_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c^{i}_{1j}\beta_{1}b_{1}+c^{i}_{2j}\beta_{2}b_{2}+\cdots+c^{i}_{kj}\beta_{k}b_{k}+\cdots+c^{i}_{nj}\beta_{n}b_{n}.

By comparing the coefficient of
b_{j}
in the top and bottom expression above, we find that

c^{i}_{jj}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}=c^{i}_{jj}\beta_{j}.

Using the fact that
\beta_{j}\neq 0
and
\alpha_{i}\neq 1
, we find that
c^{i}_{jj}=0
. So the diagonal entries of

\left(c^{i}_{kj}\right)_{1\leq k,j\leq n}
are all zero and hence this matrix has trace zero.

We now assume that the nilpotency class of
\mathfrak{n}
is
c>1
and that the proposition is correct for
smaller nilpotency classes. Note that
\varphi
induces an f.p.f. automorphism on
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{n}^{c}
and hence by the
induction hypothesis
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{n}^{c}
is strongly unimodular. This shows that for all
x\in\mathfrak{g}
, the map
{\rm ad}(x)
induces a
linear map with trace zero on all
\mathfrak{n}^{k}/\mathfrak{n}^{k+1}
for
1\leq k<c
. So we still have to show that
{\rm ad}(x)
induces
a map with zero trace on
\mathfrak{n}^{c}/\mathfrak{n}^{c+1}=\mathfrak{n}^{c}
. We can now fix a basis

\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m},c_{1},c_{2},\ldots c_{l},b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}\}
of
\mathfrak{g}
consisting of eigenvectors of
\varphi

such that
\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots c_{l},b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}\}
is a basis of
\mathfrak{n}
and
\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}\}

is a basis of
\mathfrak{n}^{c}
. It now suffices to show that the maps

{\rm ad}(a_{i})_{|\mathfrak{n}^{c}}:\mathfrak{n}^{c}\to\mathfrak{n}^{c}:x\mapsto[a_{i},x]

have trace zero. This follows exactly in the same way as above in the abelian case, which finishes
the proof.
∎

The converse of this result does not hold. A strongly unimodular Lie algebra need not admit an
f.p.f. automorphism. For a family of such Lie algebras see Example
4.4
in the next section.

4.
A classification in low dimension

Since

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a non-abelian

-dimensional complex Lie algebra. Then

\mathfrak{g}
admits an f.p.f. automorphism if and only if
\mathfrak{g}
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{n}_{3}(\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathbb{C}
,
\mathfrak{n}_{4}(\mathbb{C})
,

\mathfrak{r}_{3,-1}(\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathbb{C}
,
\mathfrak{g}_{9}(\omega,\omega^{2})
, or
\mathfrak{g}_{10}(-1)
, where
\omega
is a primitive third root of unity.
All of these Lie algebras admit an f.p.f. automorphism of finite order, and
the possible orders are as follows.

(1)

\mathfrak{n}_{3}(\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathbb{C}
admits an f.p.f. automorphism of every order
n\geq 3
.

(2)

\mathfrak{n}_{4}(\mathbb{C})
admits an f.p.f. automorphism of every order
n\geq 4
.

(3)

\mathfrak{r}_{3,-1}(\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathbb{C}
admits an f.p.f. automorphism of every order
2m
, with
m\geq 2
.

(4)

\mathfrak{g}_{9}(\omega,\omega^{2})
admits an f.p.f. automorphism of every order
3m
, with
m\geq 2
.

(5)

\mathfrak{g}_{10}(-1)
admits an f.p.f. automorphism of every order order
2m
, with
m\geq 3
.

The
4
-dimensional strongly unimodular Lie algebras are given by
\mathbb{C}^{4}
,
\mathfrak{n}_{3}(\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathbb{C}
,

\mathfrak{n}_{4}(\mathbb{C})
,
\mathfrak{g}_{7}(-2)
,
\mathfrak{g}_{9}(\alpha,\beta)
with
\alpha+\beta+1=0
, and
\mathfrak{g}_{10}(-1)
. It is easy to see that

\mathfrak{g}_{7}(-2)
does not admit an f.p.f. automorphism. In fact, a short computation shows that

must be an eigenvalue.
The algebra
\mathfrak{n}_{3}(\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathbb{C}

\varphi=\mathop{\rm diag}(s,t,st,s^{2}t)
. The possible orders of such
\varphi
, which are f.p.f., are all
n\geq 4
. We may
take
s=t=\zeta_{n}
.

The Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}_{9}(\alpha,\beta)
with
\alpha+\beta+1=0
admit an f.p.f. automorphism if and only if
\alpha
is a primitive
third root of unity and
\beta=\alpha^{2}
, see also section
5
.
For
\mathfrak{g}_{9}(\omega,\omega^{2})
one can verify by a direct computation, that the f.p.f. automorphisms
\varphi

have entry
(1,1)
different from
1
for powers
m>1
, which are not a multiple of
3
. On the other hand,
consider the following automorphisms, with complex parameter
\lambda
,

\varphi=\begin{pmatrix}\omega&0&0&0\cr 0&\lambda&0&0\cr 0&(\omega^{2}-1)\lambda&\omega^{2}\lambda&0\cr 0&3\omega\lambda&(\omega-1)\lambda&\lambda\omega\end{pmatrix}

We have
\det(\varphi)=\omega\lambda^{3}
and
\det(\varphi-\id)=(\omega-1)(\lambda^{3}-1)
. Hence we need

\lambda\neq 0,\lambda^{3}\neq 1
. We have
\varphi^{3m}=\id
if and only if
\lambda^{3m}=1
. So we can take
\lambda=\zeta_{3m}
, where we need

m\geq 2
because of
\lambda^{3}\neq 1
.

Finally, For
\mathfrak{g}_{10}(-1)
, every f.p.f. automorphism is of the form

\varphi=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0&0\cr\alpha&\beta&\gamma&0\cr\delta&-2\beta&-\beta&0\cr\varepsilon&-\beta(2\alpha+\delta)&\gamma\delta-\beta(\alpha+\delta)&\beta(2\gamma-\beta)\end{pmatrix}

satisfying

\displaystyle\beta(2\gamma-\beta)

\displaystyle\neq 0,

\displaystyle(2\beta\gamma-\beta^{2}+1)(2\beta\gamma-\beta^{2}-1)

\displaystyle\neq 0.

Every odd power greater than
1
of
\varphi
has coefficient
-1
at the entry
(1,1)
. Hence
\varphi
cannot have odd order.
On the other hand,

\varphi=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0&0\cr 0&\zeta_{m}&0&0\cr 0&-2\zeta_{m}&-\zeta_{m}&0\cr 0&0&0&-\zeta_{m}^{2}\end{pmatrix}

is an f.p.f. automorphism of
\mathfrak{g}_{10}(-1)
of order
2m\geq 6
. For
m=2
, we have
\det(\varphi-\id)=0
, which is
a contradiction.
∎

At the end of section
3
we have mentioned that there are families of strongly nilpotent Lie algebras, which do
not admit an f.p.f. automorphism. We are able to present such a family now by considering the algebras

\mathfrak{g}_{9}(\alpha,\beta)
.

Example 4.4
.

The Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}_{9}(\alpha,-1-\alpha)
are strongly unimodular for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, but do not
always admit an f.p.f. automorphism.

Note that the Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}_{9}(\alpha,\beta)
are
almost abelian
. We will study f.p.f. automorphisms of such
Lie algebras in the next section.

5.
Automorphisms of almost abelian Lie algebras

For this section, we assume that all algebras are defined over the field of complex numbers.
However, let us recall the definition of an almost abelian Lie algebra over an arbitrary field
K
.

Definition 5.1
.

A finite-dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
over a field
K
is called
almost abelian
, if it is
non-abelian and has an abelian ideal of codimension
1
.

If
\mathfrak{g}
is almost abelian, with
\dim(\mathfrak{g})=n
, then
\mathfrak{g}\cong\mathfrak{a}\rtimes\langle v\rangle
,
where
\mathfrak{a}
is abelian of dimension
n-1
and
v\neq 0
in
\mathfrak{g}
. An almost abelian Lie algebra
is
2
-step solvable. Its nilradical is given by
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{a}
, so that
\mathfrak{n}^{2}=0
and

\mathop{\rm tr}({\rm ad}(x))=\mathop{\rm tr}({\rm ad}(x)_{\mid\mathfrak{n}})
. Hence an almost abelian Lie algebra is strongly unimodular if and only
if it is unimodular.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
A\in M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
and
n\geq 2
be an integer. We say that
A
is
n
-cyclotomic
if there exists a matrix

B\in M_{k}(\mathbb{C})
with
m=kn
such that
A
is similar to the block matrix

B_{\zeta_{n}}=\begin{pmatrix}B&0&0&\cdots&0\cr 0&\zeta_{n}B&0&\cdots&0\cr 0&0&\zeta_{n}^{2}B&\cdots&0\cr\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\cr 0&0&0&\cdots&\zeta_{n}^{n-1}B\end{pmatrix}

We assume that
n\geq 2
, because otherwise every matrix
A\in M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
is
1
-cyclotomic. Note that every

n
-cyclotomic matrix
A
has trace zero, because we have

\mathop{\rm tr}(A)=(1+\zeta_{n}+\zeta_{n}^{2}+\cdots+\zeta_{n}^{n-1})\mathop{\rm tr}(B)=0.

Furthermore
A
is invertible if and only if
B
is invertible, because we have

\det(A)=(1\cdot\zeta_{n}\cdot\zeta_{n}^{2}\cdots\zeta_{n}^{n-1})^{k}\det(B)^{n}=\pm\det(B)^{n}.

Denote by
I_{k}
the identity matrix of size
k
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
B\in GL_{m}(\mathbb{C})
and
n\geq 2
. Then
B
is
n
-cyclotomic if and only if there exists an
invertible matrix
A\in GL_{k}(\mathbb{C})
with
m=kn
, such that
B
is similar to

M_{A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&\cdots&0&A\cr I_{k}&0&0&\cdots&0&0\cr 0&I_{k}&0&\cdots&0&0\cr\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\cr 0&0&0&\cdots&I_{k}&0\end{pmatrix}

Proof.

Let
B
be
n
-cyclotomic and
\zeta=\zeta_{n}
. Then

B\sim C_{\zeta}=\mathop{\rm diag}(C,\zeta C,\ldots,\zeta^{n-1}C)

for some
C\in GL_{k}(\mathbb{C})
with
m=kn
. Define a matrix
P\in M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
by

P=\begin{pmatrix}C^{n-1}&(\zeta C)^{n-1}&(\zeta^{2}C)^{n-1}&\cdots&(\zeta^{n-1}C)^{n-1}\cr\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\cr C^{2}&(\zeta C)^{2}&(\zeta^{2}C)^{2}&\cdots&(\zeta^{n-1}C)^{2}\cr C&\zeta C&\zeta^{2}C&\cdots&\zeta^{n-1}C\cr I_{k}&I_{k}&I_{k}&\cdots&I_{k}\end{pmatrix}

Let
A=C^{n}
. A direct computation shows that

M_{A}P=PC_{\zeta}.

Indeed, both sides are equal to

\begin{pmatrix}C^{n}&C^{n}&\cdots&C^{n}&C^{n}\cr C^{n-1}&(\zeta C)^{n-1}&\cdots&(\zeta^{n-2}C)^{n-1}&(\zeta^{n-1}C)^{n-1}\cr\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\cr C^{2}&(\zeta C)^{2}&\cdots&(\zeta^{n-2}C)^{2}&(\zeta^{n-1}C)^{2}\cr C&\zeta C&\cdots&\zeta^{n-2}C&\zeta^{n-1}C\end{pmatrix}

It remains to show that
P
is invertible. Then
B\sim C_{\zeta}\sim M_{A}
as claimed. Note that
P
is invertible
if and only if
\mathop{\rm diag}(I_{k},C,C^{2},\ldots,C^{n-1})P
is invertible. But this is true, because
it is the Kronecker product of two invertible matrices, namely the matrix
\mathop{\rm diag}(I_{k},C,C^{2},\ldots,C^{n-1})P
equals

\begin{pmatrix}C^{n-1}&\zeta^{n-1}C^{n-1}&\cdots&(\zeta^{n-1})^{n-1}C^{n-1}\cr C^{n-1}&\zeta^{n-2}C^{n-1}&\cdots&(\zeta^{n-1})^{n-2}C^{n-1}\cr\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\cr C^{n-1}&\zeta C^{n-1}&\cdots&\zeta^{n-1}C^{n-1}\cr C^{n-1}&C^{n-1}&\cdots&C^{n-1}\cr\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\zeta^{n-1}&\cdots&(\zeta^{n-1})^{n-1}\cr 1&\zeta^{n-2}&\cdots&(\zeta^{n-1})^{n-2}\cr\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\cr 1&\zeta&\cdots&\zeta^{n-1}\cr 1&1&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}\otimes C^{n-1}

Here the first matrix on the right side is a Vandermonde matrix.

Conversely assume that
B\sim M_{A}
for some
A\in GL_{k}(\mathbb{C})
. Then there exists a matrix
C\in GL_{k}(\mathbb{C})

such that
A=C^{n}
. Then we obtain
B\sim M_{A}\sim C_{\zeta}
as before in the first part,
so that
B
is
n
-cyclotomic.
∎

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{a}\rtimes\langle v\rangle
be a complex almost abelian Lie algebra with
Z(\mathfrak{g})=0
. Then

\mathfrak{g}
admits an f.p.f. automorphism if and only if
{\rm ad}(v)_{\mid\mathfrak{a}}
is
n
-cyclotomic for some

n\geq 2
. In this case
\mathfrak{g}
admits an f.p.f. automorphism of order
kn
for all
k\geq 2
.

Proof.

Since we have
Z(\mathfrak{g})=0
, the linear map
{\rm ad}(v)_{\mid\mathfrak{a}}:\mathfrak{a}\rightarrow\mathfrak{a}
has no eigenvalue zero. Hence it is a
vector space isomorphism. Assume first that
\mathfrak{g}
admits an f.p.f. automorphism
\varphi
. We may assume that
\varphi

is semisimple. Because of
\varphi(\mathfrak{a})\subseteq\mathfrak{a}
we have
\varphi(v)=\alpha v+a_{v}
with
a_{v}\in\mathfrak{a}
.
It follows that
\alpha
is an eigenvalue of
\varphi
, so that
\alpha\neq 0,1
. Let

\mathfrak{a}=V_{\lambda_{1}}\oplus V_{\lambda_{2}}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{\lambda_{n}}

be the decomposition of
\mathfrak{a}
into a direct sum of eigenspaces
V_{\lambda_{i}}
for
\varphi
, with distinct eigenvalues

\lambda_{i}
of
\varphi
. Fix an eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
. Then we have
[v,V_{\lambda_{i}}]\subseteq V_{\alpha\lambda_{i}}
. Indeed, with

w\in V_{\lambda_{i}}
we have

\displaystyle\varphi([v,w])

\displaystyle=[\varphi(v),\varphi(w)]

\displaystyle=[\alpha v+a_{v},\lambda_{i}w]

\displaystyle=\alpha\lambda_{i}[v,w],

because
[a_{v},w]=0
. So we have
[v,w]\in V_{\alpha\lambda_{i}}
as required. As
{\rm ad}(v)_{\mid\mathfrak{a}}
is a vector space
isomorphism of
\mathfrak{a}
we have that

\dim([v,V_{\lambda_{i}}])=\dim(V_{\lambda_{i}})\leq\dim(V_{\alpha\lambda_{i}})

Hence we obtain a series of subspaces

V_{\lambda_{i}},V_{\alpha\lambda_{i}},V_{\alpha^{2}\lambda_{i}},\ldots,V_{\alpha^{k}\lambda_{i}},\ldots

of
\mathfrak{a}
of non-decreasing dimension. Since
\mathfrak{a}
is the direct sum of all eigenspaces, these
subspaces cannot be all different. So there exist
p>q
such that
\alpha^{q}\lambda_{i}=\alpha^{p}\lambda_{i}
, or

\alpha^{p-q}=1
. It follows that
\alpha
is an
n
-th primitive root of unity, where
n\geq 2
because of
\alpha\neq 1
.
We have

\dim(V_{\lambda_{1}})\leq\dim(V_{\alpha\lambda_{1}})\leq\cdots\leq\dim(V_{\alpha^{n}\lambda_{1}})=\dim(V_{\lambda_{1}}),

so that we have equality everywhere, and

[v,V_{\alpha^{k}\lambda_{1}}]=V_{\alpha^{k+1}\lambda_{1}}

for all
k\geq 0
. Let
V_{1}=V_{\lambda_{1}}\oplus V_{\alpha\lambda_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{\alpha^{n-1}\lambda_{1}}
. If
V_{1}\neq\mathfrak{a}
,
then there exists an eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}
of
\varphi
such that
\lambda_{2}\neq\alpha^{k}\lambda_{1}
for all
k
. Then

V_{2}=V_{\lambda_{2}}\oplus V_{\alpha\lambda_{2}}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{\alpha^{n-1}\lambda_{2}}
is another subspace of
\mathfrak{a}
with

V_{1}\cap V_{2}=0
,
V_{1}\oplus V_{2}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}
and
[v,V_{\alpha^{k}\lambda_{2}}]\subseteq V_{\alpha^{k+1}\lambda_{2}}
for all
k
.
Continuing this way, we find finitely many eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}
, each giving rise to a sequence of
subspaces
V_{\lambda_{i}},V_{\alpha\lambda_{i}},\ldots,V_{\alpha^{n-1}\lambda_{i}}
with
[v,V_{\alpha^{k}\lambda_{i}}]\subseteq V_{\alpha^{k+1}\lambda_{i}}
for all
k
.
Now let

\displaystyle U_{1}

\displaystyle=V_{\lambda_{1}}\oplus V_{\lambda_{2}}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{\lambda_{m}}

\displaystyle U_{2}

\displaystyle=V_{\alpha\lambda_{1}}\oplus V_{\alpha\lambda_{2}}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{\alpha\lambda_{m}}

\displaystyle\vdots\hskip 5.12128pt

\displaystyle=\hskip 5.69046pt\vdots

\displaystyle U_{n}

\displaystyle=V_{\alpha^{n-1}\lambda_{1}}\oplus V_{\alpha^{n-1}\lambda_{2}}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{\alpha^{n-1}\lambda_{m}}

Note that all spaces
U_{i}
have equal dimension, say
\dim(U_{i})=k
. Then we have

\displaystyle U_{1}\oplus U_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus U_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{a},

\displaystyle[v,U_{i}]

\displaystyle=U_{i+1},\;\text{for all}\;i<n,

\displaystyle[v,U_{n}]

\displaystyle=U_{1}.

Choose a basis
\{u_{11},\ldots,u_{1k}\}
of
U_{1}
, and let
u_{2i}=[v,u_{1i}]
for
i=1,\ldots,k
.
Then
\{u_{21},\ldots,u_{2k}\}
is a basis of
U_{2}
. Continuing this way, with
u_{j+1,i}=[v,u_{ji}]
for
j<n

we obtain a basis of each subspace
U_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq n
. With respect to the basis

\{u_{11},\ldots,u_{1k},u_{21},\ldots,u_{2k},u_{31},\ldots,u_{nk}\}

of
\mathfrak{a}
the matrix of
{\rm ad}(v)_{\mid\mathfrak{a}}
is given by

M_{A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&\cdots&0&A\cr I_{k}&0&0&\cdots&0&0\cr 0&I_{k}&0&\cdots&0&0\cr\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\cr 0&0&0&\cdots&I_{k}&0\end{pmatrix}

for some
A\in GL_{k}(\mathbb{C})
. By Lemma
5.3
it follows that
{\rm ad}(v)_{\mid\mathfrak{a}}
is
n
-cyclotomic.

Conversely, assume that
{\rm ad}(v)_{\mid\mathfrak{a}}
is
n
-cyclotomic. Then there is a basis
\{u_{11},u_{12},\ldots,u_{nk}\}

of
\mathfrak{a}
such that the matrix of
{\rm ad}(v)_{\mid\mathfrak{a}}
with respect to this basis is given by
M_{A}
as above. Now
choose a
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
and a primitive
n
-th root of unity
\zeta
such that

\zeta^{\ell}\lambda\neq 1\text{ for all }0\leq\ell\leq n-1.

According to the form of
M_{A}
, we can decompose
\mathfrak{a}
into subspaces
U_{i}
such that

\displaystyle U_{1}\oplus U_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus U_{m}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{a},

\displaystyle[v,U_{i}]

\displaystyle=U_{i+1},\;\text{for all}\;i<n,

\displaystyle[v,U_{n}]

\displaystyle=U_{1}.

We define a map
\varphi\colon\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}
for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{a}\rtimes\langle v\rangle
by
\varphi(v)=\zeta v
, and

\varphi_{\mid U_{i}}
as the multiplication with
\zeta^{i-1}\lambda
. So with respect to the above basis,
\varphi_{\mid\mathfrak{a}}

is represented by the block matrix

{\rm diag}(\lambda I_{k},\zeta\lambda I_{k},\ldots,\zeta^{n-1}\lambda I_{k}).

Since
\zeta^{\ell}\lambda\neq 1
, it follows that
\varphi
is fixed-point-free. We claim that

\varphi\in\Aut(\mathfrak{g})
. For this it is enough to show that for
each
1\leq i\leq n
and for each
u\in U_{i}

we have

\varphi([v,u])=[\varphi(v),\varphi(u)].

Since
[v,u]\in U_{i+1}
, with
U_{n+1}=U_{1}
, we have
\varphi([v,u])=\zeta^{i}\lambda[v,u]
, and

[\varphi(v),\varphi(u)]=[\zeta v,\zeta^{i-1}\lambda u]=\zeta^{i}\lambda[v,u],

which was to be shown.

Finally, if
{\rm ad}(v)_{\mid\mathfrak{a}}
is
n
-cyclotomic for some
n\geq 2
, we can choose

\lambda=\zeta_{kn}
in the definition of
\varphi
for every
k\geq 2
. Then

\varphi
is an f.p.f. automorphism of order
kn
.
∎

As an example, we consider the almost abelian unimodular Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{9}(\alpha,-1-\alpha)

for any
\alpha\neq 0,-1
, with Lie brackets

[e_{1},e_{2}]=e_{2},\,[e_{1},e_{3}]=e_{2}+\alpha e_{3},\,[e_{1},e_{4}]=e_{3}-(1+\alpha)e_{4}.

Then we have
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{a}\rtimes\langle e_{1}\rangle
, with
\mathfrak{a}=\langle e_{2},e_{3},e_{4}\rangle
and
Z(\mathfrak{g})=0
.
Furthermore,

A={\rm ad}(e_{1})_{\mid\mathfrak{a}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\cr 0&\alpha&1\cr 0&0&-1-\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

Example 5.5
.

The almost abelian Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{9}(\alpha,-1-\alpha)
, for
\alpha\neq 0,-1
, admits an f.p.f. automorphism if and only if

1+\alpha+\alpha^{2}=0
, i.e., if
\alpha
is a primitive
3
-rd root of unity.

Indeed, by Theorem
5.4
,
\mathfrak{g}
admits an f.p.f. automorphism if and only if
A
is
3
-cyclotomic, i.e.,
if and only
A
is similar to
\mathop{\rm diag}(c,\omega c,\omega^{2}c)
, for some
c\neq 0
and
\omega=\zeta_{3}
. The comparison of
the characteristic polynomials,

t^{3}-t(\alpha^{2}+\alpha+1)+\alpha(\alpha+1)=t^{3}-c^{3},

yields
\alpha^{2}+\alpha+1=0
. Then the matrices are also similar. This coincides with the result in Proposition
4.3
.
Note that the algebras
\mathfrak{g}_{7}(\alpha)
are also almost-abelian, and that we also can recover the result in Proposition
4.3

from Theorem
5.4
.

For the general case of an almost-abelian Lie algebra, we will need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{a}\rtimes\langle v\rangle
be an almost abelian Lie algebra. Suppose that
\mathfrak{a}=V\oplus W
, where
V
and
W

are ideals of
\mathfrak{g}
, i.e.
V
and
W
are invariant under the action of
{\rm ad}(v)
, and that there exists a basis

e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}
of
V
such that
{\rm ad}(v)_{|V}
is given by means of a Jordan block with zero’s on the diagonal:

{\rm ad}(v)_{|V}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
1&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&&\ddots&0&0\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1&0\\
\end{pmatrix}

Suppose that
\varphi^{\prime}
is an f.p.f. automorphism of the subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}=W\rtimes\langle v\rangle
with

\varphi^{\prime}(v)=\zeta v
for some
\zeta\neq 1
and
\varphi^{\prime}(W)=W
. Then
\varphi^{\prime}
can be extended to an
f.p.f. automorphism
\varphi
of
\mathfrak{g}
with
\varphi(V)=V
.

Proof.

The fact that
{\rm ad}(v)_{|V}
is a Jordan block as above means that

[v,e_{i}]=e_{i+1}\;\;(1\leq i\leq n-1)\mbox{ and }[v,e_{n}]=0.

Choose
0\neq\mu\in\mathbb{C}
in such a way that
\zeta^{k}\mu\neq 1
for all
0\leq k\leq n-1
. Extend
\varphi^{\prime}
to a linear map

\varphi
on
\mathfrak{g}
by defining
\varphi(e_{i})=\zeta^{i-1}\mu e_{i}
. It is obvious that
\varphi
is an f.p.f. invertible
linear map of
\mathfrak{g}
with
\varphi(V)=V
. We also have that, for
1\leq i\leq n-1
,

\varphi([v,e_{i}])=\varphi(e_{i+1})=\zeta^{i}\mu e_{i+1}=[\zeta v,\zeta^{i-1}\mu e_{i}]=[\varphi(v),\varphi(e_{i})]

and

\varphi([v,e_{n}])=\varphi(0)=0=[\zeta v,\zeta^{n-1}\mu e_{n}]=[\varphi(v),\varphi(e_{n})].

Moreover, for all
w\in W
and
1\leq i\leq n
we also have that
\varphi[e_{i},w]=0=[\varphi(e_{i}),\varphi(w)]
,
showing that
\varphi
is an automorphism of
\mathfrak{g}
.
∎

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite dimensional Lie algebra. The terms
Z_{i}(\mathfrak{g})
of the upper central series of
\mathfrak{g}
are
determined by
Z_{1}(\mathfrak{g})=Z(\mathfrak{g})
and
Z_{i+1}(\mathfrak{g})/Z_{i}(\mathfrak{g})=Z(\mathfrak{g}/Z_{i}(\mathfrak{g}))
for all
i\geq 1
.
We let
Z_{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})=\bigcup_{i}Z_{i}(\mathfrak{g})
. Note that
Z_{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})=Z_{i}(\mathfrak{g})
for some
i
big enough, since we assume
that
\mathfrak{g}
is finite dimensional and so
Z(\mathfrak{g}/Z_{\infty}(\mathfrak{g}))=0
.

We are now ready to treat the general case of
almost abelian Lie algebras by reducing it to centerless almost abelian Lie algebras.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{a}\rtimes\langle v\rangle
be an almost abelian Lie algebra. Then
\mathfrak{g}
admits an f.p.f. automorphism
if and only if
\mathfrak{g}/Z_{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})
admits an f.p.f. automorphism.

For the theorem above, when
\mathfrak{g}/Z_{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})=0
, we say that
\mathfrak{g}/Z_{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})=0
admits an f.p.f. automorphism (indeed, the
only fixed point of the only automorphism is
0
).

Proof.

Assume that
\mathfrak{g}
admits an f.p.f. automorphism
\varphi
. As
\varphi(Z_{\infty}(\mathfrak{g}))=Z_{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})
, it follows that

\varphi
induces an automorphism
\bar{\varphi}
on
\mathfrak{g}/Z_{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})
which is clearly also f.p.f.  i.e., it does not have eigenvalue
equal to
1
.

Conversely, assume now that
\mathfrak{g}/Z_{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})
admits an f.p.f. automorphism. We choose a basis of
\mathfrak{a}
with respect
to which
{\rm ad}(v)_{|\mathfrak{a}}
is represented by a matrix of the form

\begin{pmatrix}J_{n_{1}}&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&J_{n_{2}}&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&J_{n_{k}}&0\\
0&0&\cdots&0&B\end{pmatrix}

where each
J_{n_{i}}
is a Jordan block of size
n_{i}
with zero’s on the diagonal and
B
is a matrix which does
not contain
0
as an eigenvalue. It is possible that there is no
B
and we only have Jordan blocks with zero’s on
the diagonal. This is the case when
\mathfrak{g}
is nilpotent, and so
Z_{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})=\mathfrak{g}
.

Suppose first that
\mathfrak{g}
is not nilpotent (so there is a matrix
B
). Then
Z_{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})
is exactly the subspace of
\mathfrak{a}

corresponding to the first
n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{k}
basis vectors (so the generalised eigenspace of
{\rm ad}(v)_{|\mathfrak{a}}
corresponding
to eigenvalue
0
). If we let
W
denote the space generated by the other generalised eigenvectors, then
{\rm ad}(v)_{|W}
is given
by the matrix
B
. It follows that
\mathfrak{g}/Z_{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})
is then isomorphic to the subalgebra

W\rtimes\langle v\rangle
of
\mathfrak{g}
.
As we assume that
\mathfrak{g}/Z_{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})
admits an f.p.f. automorphism, by Theorem
5.4
we have that
B
is
n
-cyclotomic.
From the proof of that theorem we know that there exists an f.p.f. automorphism
\varphi^{\prime}
of

W\rtimes\langle v\rangle
with
\varphi^{\prime}(W)=W
and
\varphi(v)=\zeta v
, where
\zeta
is a primitive
n
-th root if unity.
By now iteratively applying Lemma
5.6
for each Jordan block
J_{n_{i}}
, we can extend
\varphi^{\prime}
to
an f.p.f. automorphism of
\mathfrak{g}
.

When
\mathfrak{g}
is nilpotent (this is the case where
\mathfrak{g}/Z_{\infty}(\mathfrak{g})=0
), we can start by taking
\varphi^{\prime}(z)=\zeta z
for
any choice of
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}
and then also extend
\varphi^{\prime}
iteratively to the whole of
\mathfrak{g}

(where in the first step we take
W=0
).
∎

6.
Automorphisms of filiform Lie algebras

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a complex filiform Lie algebra of dimension
n\geq 3
. It has an adapted basis, which is a basis

\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}
such that

\begin{array}[]{l}{[e_{1},e_{i}]}=e_{i+1}\mbox{ for }i=2,3,\ldots,n-1\\[2.84526pt]
{[e_{1},e_{n}]}=0\\[2.84526pt]
{[e_{2},e_{3}]}\in{\rm span}\{e_{5},e_{6},\ldots,e_{n}\}.\end{array}

For such a basis, it holds that there is an
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\begin{array}[]{l}{[e_{i},e_{j}]}\in\langle e_{i+j},e_{i+j+1},\ldots,e_{n}\rangle\mbox{ if }i+j\leq n\\[2.84526pt]
{[e_{i},e_{n+1-i}]}=(-1)^{i}\alpha e_{n}\mbox{ for }i=2,3,\ldots n-1\mbox{ with }\alpha=0\mbox{ if $n$ is odd.}\end{array}

If
n
is even, we may assume that
\alpha=0
or
\alpha=1
. For details see section
6
in
[
2
]
. Furthermore
the subspaces

\mathfrak{g}_{i}={\rm span}\{e_{i},e_{i+1},\ldots,e_{n}\}

are characteristic ideals of
\mathfrak{g}
. Let us recall the definitions of the filiform Lie algebras
L_{n}
and
Q_{n}
.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
L_{n}
be the standard graded filiform Lie algebra algebra of dimension
n
with adapted basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
, with
defining Lie brackets
[e_{1},e_{i}]=e_{i+1}
for
2\leq i\leq n-1
.
For
n
even, let
Q_{n}
the graded filiform Lie algebra with adapted basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
, and nonzero Lie brackets defined by

\displaystyle[e_{1},e_{i}]

\displaystyle=e_{i+1}\text{ for }2\leq i\leq n-1,

\displaystyle[e_{i},e_{n+1-i}]

\displaystyle=(-1)^{i}e_{n}\text{ for }2\leq i\leq\frac{n}{2}.

To each filiform Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
one can associate a graded Lie algebra by

{\rm gr}(\mathfrak{g})=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{g}^{i}/\mathfrak{g}^{i+1},

where

[x+\mathfrak{g}^{i+1},y+\mathfrak{g}^{j+1}]=[x,y]+\mathfrak{g}^{i+j+1}

for
x\in\mathfrak{g}^{i}
,
y\in\mathfrak{g}^{j}
. It follows that the associated graded algebra
{\rm gr}(\mathfrak{g})
is either isomorphic to
L_{n}
or
Q_{n}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a filiform Lie algebra satisfying
{\rm gr}(\mathfrak{g})\cong Q_{n}
and
[\mathfrak{g}_{2},\mathfrak{g}_{2}]\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{n}
.
Then
\mathfrak{g}
is isomorphic to
Q_{n}
.

Proof.

By assumption
\mathfrak{g}
has an adapted basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
, such that

\displaystyle[e_{1},e_{i}]

\displaystyle=e_{i+1},\;2\leq i\leq n-1,

\displaystyle[e_{i},e_{j}]

\displaystyle=c_{i,j}e_{n},\;2\leq i,j\leq n

for some
c_{i,j}\in\mathbb{C}
with
c_{i,n+1-i}=(-1)^{i}
. We have
c_{i+1,j-1}=-c_{i,j}
for all
i\geq 2,j\geq 3
because of

\displaystyle[e_{i+1},e_{j-1}]

\displaystyle=[[e_{1},e_{i}],e_{j-1}]

\displaystyle=-[[e_{i},e_{j-1}],e_{1}]-[[e_{j-1},e_{1}],e_{i}]

\displaystyle=-[e_{i},e_{j}]

Here we have used that
[e_{i},e_{j-1}]\in[\mathfrak{g}_{2},\mathfrak{g}_{2}]\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{n}
, and
[e_{n},e_{1}]=0
. The above identity implies

c_{i,j}=0
for
i+j\equiv 0\bmod 2
and
c_{i,j}=(-1)^{i}c_{2,j+i-2}
for all
i\geq 2,j\geq 3
.

Using these conditions it is straightforward to show that there are
\beta_{4},\beta_{6},\ldots,\beta_{n-2}\in\mathbb{C}

such that the linear map
D\colon\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}
defined by

\displaystyle D(e_{1})

\displaystyle=e_{2}+\beta_{4}e_{4}+\beta_{6}e_{6}+\cdots+\beta_{n-2}e_{n-2},

\displaystyle D(e_{i})

\displaystyle=e_{i},\;2\leq i\leq n-1,

\displaystyle D(e_{n})

\displaystyle=2e_{n}

is a derivation of
\mathfrak{g}
. Indeed, one can verify that we have

D([e_{i},e_{j}])=[D(e_{i}),e_{j}]+[e_{i},D(e_{j})]

for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
, where the
\beta_{4},\beta_{6},\ldots,\beta_{n-2}\in\mathbb{C}
are determined by the following system of
equations:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=c_{2,3}+\beta_{4}c_{4,3}+\beta_{6}c_{6,3}+\cdots+\beta_{n-6}c_{n-6,3}+\beta_{n-4}c_{n-4,3}+\beta_{n-2},

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=c_{2,5}+\beta_{4}c_{4,5}+\beta_{6}c_{6,5}+\cdots+\beta_{n-6}c_{n-6,5}+\beta_{n-4},

\displaystyle\hskip 5.69046pt\vdots

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=c_{2,n-5}+\beta_{4}c_{4,n-5}+\beta_{6},

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=c_{2,n-3}+\beta_{4}.

It follows that the rank of
\mathfrak{g}
must be equal to
2
. Indeed, the rank of a filiform Lie algebra can only be

0
,
1
or
2
, and
D
has one eigenvalue
1
, one eigenvalue
2
and
n-2
eigenvalues
0
.
Hence the rank cannot be zero or one. For details see
[
4
]
, Proposition
1
.
This implies that
\mathfrak{g}\cong Q_{n}
.
∎

Let us recall the following definition.

Definition 6.3
.

A Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is called
characteristically nilpotent
, or a
CNLA
, if all derivations

D\in{\rm Der}(\mathfrak{g})
are nilpotent.

In particular, a characteristically nilpotent Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
has only nilpotent inner derivations

D={\rm ad}(x)
for all
x\in\mathfrak{g}
, and hence is nilpotent by Engel’s theorem.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a complex filiform Lie algebra of dimension
n\geq 3
. Assume that
\mathfrak{g}
is a CLNA. Then
\mathfrak{g}
does
not admit an f.p.f. automorphism.

Proof.

Let
\varphi
be an automorphism of
\mathfrak{g}
. We need to show that
1
is an eigenvalue of
\varphi
. We may again assume
that
\varphi
is semisimple. Because of
\varphi(\mathfrak{g}_{i})=\mathfrak{g}_{i}
for all
i
, the matrix of
\varphi
with respect to
an adapted basis of
\mathfrak{g}
is lower-triangular, with eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n
on the diagonal.
We will show that every
\lambda_{i}
is a power of
\lambda_{1}
. For this, we can pass to an adapted basis

\{e_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,e_{n}^{\prime}\}
with
\varphi(e_{2}^{\prime})=\lambda_{2}e_{2}^{\prime}
as follows: there exists an eigenvector
e_{2}^{\prime}=e_{2}+x_{2}
of

\varphi
corresponding to
\lambda_{2}
, where
x_{2}\in\mathfrak{g}_{3}={\rm span}\{e_{3},\ldots,e_{n}\}
. Define
e_{1}^{\prime}=e_{1}
and

e_{i+1}^{\prime}=[e_{1},e_{i}^{\prime}]
inductively for all
i\geq 2
. Then we can write
e_{i}^{\prime}=e_{i}+x_{i}
with
x_{i}\in\mathfrak{g}_{i+1}
and
x_{n}=0
.
We have

[e_{2}^{\prime},e_{3}^{\prime}]=[e_{2},e_{3}]+[e_{2},x_{3}]+[x_{2},e_{3}]+[x_{2},x_{3}]\in\mathfrak{g}_{5},

so that the basis is adapted. For convenience, let us rewrite this basis again as
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}

with
\varphi(e_{2})=\lambda_{2}e_{2}
. We know that either
{\rm gr}(\mathfrak{g})\cong L_{n}
, or
{\rm gr}(\mathfrak{g})\cong Q_{n}
.

Case 1:
We have
{\rm gr}(\mathfrak{g})\cong L_{n}
. We have
\varphi(e_{3})=\lambda_{3}e_{3}+y_{3}
with
y_{3}\in\mathfrak{g}_{4}
, and also

\displaystyle\varphi(e_{3})

\displaystyle=\varphi([e_{1},e_{2}])=[\varphi(e_{1}),\varphi(e_{2})]

\displaystyle=[\lambda_{1}e_{1}+y_{1},\lambda_{2}e_{2}]

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}e_{3}+\lambda_{2}[y_{1},e_{2}]

with
y_{1}\in\mathfrak{g}_{2}
and
[y_{1},e_{2}]\in\mathfrak{g}_{4}
. This implies
\lambda_{3}=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}
. By induction it follows that

\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{1}^{i-2}\lambda_{2}

for all
i\geq 3
. Since
[\mathfrak{g}_{i},\mathfrak{g}_{j}]\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{i+j}
for the case
{\rm gr}(\mathfrak{g})\cong L_{n}
, we have

[e_{2},e_{n-1}]=0
. On the other hand, it is impossible that
[e_{2},e_{k}]=0
for all
k\geq 3
, because
otherwise one inductively obtains
[e_{i},e_{j}]=0
for all
i,j\geq 2
, so that
\mathfrak{g}\cong L_{n}
. This is a contradiction, since

L_{n}
is not a CNLA. Let
j
be the largest index such that

[e_{2},\mathfrak{g}_{2}]\subset\mathfrak{g}_{j}.

Then we have
[e_{2},e_{i}]=\alpha e_{j}+x
for some
i\geq 3
, with
\alpha\neq 0
,
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{j+1}
and
[e_{2},e_{k}]\in\mathfrak{g}_{j}

for all
k\geq 3
. We also can fix the largest
i
such that
[e_{2},e_{i}]=\alpha e_{j}+x
with
\alpha\neq 0
and
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{j+1}
.
It follows that
j-i\geq 2
, because we have
[\mathfrak{g}_{2},\mathfrak{g}_{i}]\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{i+2}
. Applying
\varphi
we obtain

\displaystyle[\varphi(e_{2}),\varphi(e_{i})]

\displaystyle=\varphi([e_{2},e_{i}])

\displaystyle=\alpha\varphi(e_{j})+\varphi(x)

\displaystyle=\alpha\lambda_{j}e_{j}+z,

with
z\in\mathfrak{g}_{j+1}
.
On the other hand, we have (using
[e_{2},\mathfrak{g}_{i+1}]\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{j+1}
)

\displaystyle[\varphi(e_{2}),\varphi(e_{i})]

\displaystyle=[\lambda_{2}e_{2},\lambda_{i}e_{i}+y]\mbox{ (for some $y\in\mathfrak{g}_{i+1}$)}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{2}\lambda_{i}[e_{2},e_{i}]+\lambda_{2}[e_{2},y]

\displaystyle=\alpha\lambda_{2}\lambda_{i}e_{j}+w

with
w\in\mathfrak{g}_{j+1}
. It follows that
\lambda_{2}\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}
. Together with
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{1}^{k-2}\lambda_{2}
for all
k\geq 2

this yields
\lambda_{2}^{2}\lambda_{1}^{i-2}=\lambda_{2}\lambda_{1}^{j-2}
, and hence
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{1}^{j-i}
.
So the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
are given by

(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1}^{j-i},\lambda_{1}^{j-i+1},\ldots,\lambda_{1}^{j-i+n-2}).

We claim that not all eigenvalues are different. To show this, assume that they are all different, and let

\mathfrak{h}_{k}
be the eigenspace corresponding to
\lambda_{1}^{k}
for
k=1,j-i,j-i+1,\ldots,j-i+n-2
.
Then we have

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{h}_{j-i}\oplus\mathfrak{h}_{j-i+1}\oplus\cdots\mathfrak{h}_{j-i+n-2}.

However, this is a positive grading of
\mathfrak{g}
. For
x\in\mathfrak{h}_{k}
and
y\in\mathfrak{h}_{\ell}
we have

\varphi([x,y])=[\varphi(x),\varphi(y)]=[\lambda_{1}^{k}x,\lambda_{1}^{\ell}y]=\lambda_{1}^{k+\ell}[x,y],

so that
[x,y]\in\mathfrak{h}_{k+\ell}
. It follows that
\mathfrak{g}
is a not a CNLA, which is a contradiction. Hence we have

\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{\ell}
for some
k<\ell
.

Assume first that
k\geq 2
. Then we have
\lambda_{1}^{j-i+k-2}=\lambda_{1}^{j-i+\ell-2}
,
so that
\lambda_{1}^{\ell-k}=1
. Let
r=\ell-k\leq n-2
.
It follows that the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
is an
r
-th root of unity, and the eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}^{j-i},\lambda_{1}^{j-i+1},\ldots,\lambda_{1}^{j-i+n-2}

form a sequence of
n-1
consecutive powers of
\lambda_{1}
, so that at least one of them must be equal to
1
.

Now assume that
k=1
, i.e.,
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{\ell}
for some
\ell>1
. Then
\lambda_{\ell}=\lambda_{1}^{j-i+\ell-2}=\lambda_{1}
and hence

\lambda_{1}^{j-i+\ell-3}=1
.
If
\ell>2
it follows that

\lambda_{\ell-1}=1
in which case we are done. If
\ell=2
, this shows that
\lambda_{1}^{j-i-1}=1
, but also that the sequence of eigenvalues becomes

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{1},\lambda_{3}=\lambda_{1}^{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{1}^{n-1})

As
2\leq j-i\leq n-3
, we have that
\lambda_{j-i}=\lambda_{1}^{j-i-1}=1
is one of the eigenvalues of
\varphi
.
This concludes the first case.

Case 2:
We have
{\rm gr}(\mathfrak{g})\cong Q_{n}
.
Then we have
[e_{2},e_{n-1}]=e_{n}
, and
\varphi
induces an automorphism on
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}_{n}
with lower-triangular matrix
having eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1}
with respect to the basis
\{e_{1}+\mathfrak{g}_{n},\ldots,e_{n-1}+\mathfrak{g}_{n}\}

of
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}_{n}
. Clearly we have that
{\rm gr}(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}_{n})\cong L_{n-1}
, where
n-1
is odd. On the other hand,
suppose that
[\mathfrak{g}_{2},\mathfrak{g}_{2}]\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{n}
. Then
\mathfrak{g}\cong Q_{n}
by Lemma
6.2
, which is impossible, since

Q_{n}
is not a CNLA. Hence
[\mathfrak{g}_{2},\mathfrak{g}_{2}]\not\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{n}
and
[(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}_{n})_{2},(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}_{n})_{2}]\neq 0
. This implies

\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}_{n}\not\cong L_{n-1}
. By the arguments of case
1
we obtain

(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1})=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1}^{k},\lambda_{1}^{k+1},\ldots,\lambda_{1}^{k+n-3})

for the first
n-1
eigenvalues of
\varphi
, with
2\leq k\leq n-4
. Applying
\varphi
to
[e_{2},e_{n-1}]=e_{n}

yields

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}e_{n}

\displaystyle=\varphi(e_{n})=[\varphi(e_{2}),\varphi(e_{n})]

\displaystyle=[\lambda_{2}e_{2},\lambda_{n-1}e_{n-1}]=\lambda_{2}\lambda_{n-1}e_{n},

so that
\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{1}^{2k+n-3}
for the last eigenvalue of
\varphi
. Now we can proceed as in case
1
.
It follows that not all eigenvalues are different, because
\mathfrak{g}
is not graded. Hence we obtain

\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}
for some
1\leq i<j\leq n
.

If
j<n
, then the induced automorphism
\overline{\varphi}
on

\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}_{n}
also has two equal eigenvalues. Again we can apply the arguments of case
1
to see that

\overline{\varphi}
has an eigenvalue equal to
1
. Hence
\varphi
has an eigenvalue
1
as well. So we are left with the case
that
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{n}
for some
1\leq i<n
(and
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
for
1\leq i<j\leq n-1
). Assume first that
i\geq 2
. Then
\lambda_{1}^{k+i-2}=\lambda_{1}^{2k+n-3}
, which implies

\lambda_{1}^{k+(n+1-i)-2}=1
, so that
\lambda_{n+1-i}=1
. Now let
i=1
, i.e., we have
\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{1}
. We will again show that
\mathfrak{g}
admits a positive grading, which is a contradiction.

e_{n}
is one of the eigenvectors of
\varphi
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{n}
. We can find another eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
which is of the form
e_{1}+x
with
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{2}
. Now let
\mathfrak{h}_{1}=\langle e_{1}+x\rangle
,
\mathfrak{h}_{2k+n-3}=\langle e_{n}\rangle
and
\mathfrak{h}_{i}
be the eigenspace corresponding to eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{1}^{i}
for
k\leq i\leq k+n-3
. One can now check that

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{h}_{k}\oplus\mathfrak{h}_{k+1}\oplus\cdots\mathfrak{h}_{k+n-3}\oplus\mathfrak{h}_{2k+n-3}

is a positive grading of
\mathfrak{g}
(using the fact that
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]\subseteq\mathfrak{h}_{k}\oplus\mathfrak{h}_{k+1}\oplus\cdots\mathfrak{h}_{k+n-3}\oplus\mathfrak{h}_{2k+n-3}
). But this implies again that
\mathfrak{g}
is not a CNLA, which concludes the proof.

∎

Finally we are ready to state our main result for complex filiform Lie algebras.
Recall that a Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}
is called a
derived algebra
, if
\mathfrak{g}=[\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{h}]
for some Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}
.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a complex filiform Lie algebra. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1)

\mathfrak{g}
admits an f.p.f. automorphism.

(2)

\mathfrak{g}
is not a CNLA.

(3)

\mathfrak{g}
admits a nonsingular derivation.

(4)

\mathfrak{g}
is a derived Lie algebra.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2):
This follows from Proposition
6.4
.

(2)\Rightarrow(3):
This follows from Proposition
1
in
[
4
]
.

(3)\Rightarrow(1):
Let
D\colon\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}
be a nonsingular derivation. Note that this implies that

\mathfrak{g}
is nilpotent, but we will not need this. Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
D
. They are
all non-zero by assumption. For
r\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
, also
rD
is a nonsingular derivation. By replacing
D

with
rD
for some
r>0
small enough, we can assume that
\exp(\lambda_{i})\neq 1
for all
i
. Then

\exp(D)
is an automorphism of
\mathfrak{g}
with eigenvalues different from
1
, and hence an f.p.f. automorphism
of
\mathfrak{g}
.

(4)\Leftrightarrow(2):
This follows from Proposition
2.14
in
[
9
]
.
∎
