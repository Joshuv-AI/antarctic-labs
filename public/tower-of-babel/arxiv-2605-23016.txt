Sample correlation adjustments for robust Multi–fidelity Monte Carlo under limited pilot sampling Department of Mechanical Engineering, University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, MI, 48109 NASA Langley Research Center, Hampton, VA, 23666, USA.
Michael Stanley

Abstract
Multi-fidelity Monte Carlo (MFMC) is a variance reduction method that leverages a multi-fidelity ensemble of models of varying cost and accuracy levels. Constructing an MFMC estimator with optimal variance requires knowledge of the correlation coefficients between the different fidelity models which are not usually known in practice. The correlations are typically estimated using offline pilot samples and the sample correlation formula, after which the MFMC method proceeds as if the estimated correlations are the true correlations. Computational cost often restricts the number of pilot samples used leading to poor correlation estimates and suboptimal estimators. Leveraging the MFMC problem setting and probabilistic information about the sample covariance matrix, we present a method to improve standard sample-based correlation estimates in the presence of limited pilot samples. We define a novel discrepancy function quantifying the estimator suboptimality which in turn facilitates selecting a correlation estimator minimizing the worst-case expected discrepancy, where the expectation is taken with respect to the pilot sampling variability. Through a simple bivariate Gaussian example and a multi-fidelity modeling application from a NASA Entry, Descent, and Landing (EDL) problem, we show that this method produces better MFMC estimators than the standard sample covariance under small pilot sample sizes and limited total budgets.

Introduction

Multi-fidelity (also called multi-model) Monte Carlo methods have revolutionized the Monte Carlo estimation of statistical functionals for forward model uncertainty quantification (UQ)
[
14
,
28
,
32
,
38
,
36
]
.
These methods use ideas from the control variates literature
[
23
]
to accelerate the estimation of model statistics via variance reduction, optimally constructing an estimator from multiple correlated models with different computational costs.
Constructing an estimator requires the computational cost of each model, the total computational budget, and the covariance of the model outputs (which, importantly, contains the inter–model correlations).
Although the first two are typically known ahead of time, the covariance matrix often must be estimated in a so-called pilot study.
The pilot study is an offline procedure where each model is run a finite number of times at the same model input values and a sample covariance matrix is computed.
The resulting sample covariance matrix is then treated as the ground truth, and an optimization problem is solved to find the optimal sample allocations and control variate weights.

Importantly, most of the multi-fidelity UQ literature relies on exact knowledge of the covariance matrix through large pilot sample sizes.
In practice, obtaining a large pilot sample size is intractable since the computationally intensive high-fidelity model must be run once per Monte Carlo sample.
As a result, many works
[
1
,
36
,
37
]
have proposed heuristics for pilot sampling termination and more recent works
[
11
,
12
]
have investigated methods for determining optimal pilot sample sizes that directly incorporate the covariance-matrix uncertainty and pilot sampling cost into the problem formulation.
Under limited budgets, the pilot sample sizes that one can practically afford are small, often just 5 to 20 pilot samples.
Since the sample covariance matrix is highly variable for these small sample sizes, the downstream estimator hyperparameters are generally suboptimal.

This paper provides a rigorous characterization of the suboptimality resulting from pilot sampling variability, as well as a strategy to address it.
In particular, we propose a framework for producing multi-fidelity estimators that are robust to pilot sampling variability, in turn broadening the practicality of these methods to cost-constrained settings.
To achieve this task, we turn our attention to the sample covariance formula.
While the sample covariance formula has desirable characteristics outside of the multi-fidelity setting, including unbiasedness, it is simply one possible choice for covariance estimation.
Our basic claim is that if one incorporates knowledge of the multi-fidelity estimation problem and the relevant sampling distributions, we can define an alternate covariance estimator that optimally navigates the bias-variance trade-off and can produce better downstream multi-fidelity estimators.

We consider the case where a user has some limited pilot sample data and wishes to produce a multi-fidelity estimator that mitigates the effects of the small pilot sample size.
To produce such an estimator, we first define a
discrepancy function
that quantifies the estimator suboptimality as a result of inexact covariance information.
Next, we define a parameterized
adjustment function
that maps from the sample covariance to a corrected covariance estimate.
Lastly, we formulate and solve an optimization problem to find the risk-minimizing adjustment parameters according to a carefully selected risk metric on the discrepancy function.
Specifically, we find a minimax adjustment on the expected discrepancy, with the expectation taken over the sampling distribution of the sample covariance matrix and the maximum taken over a
1-\alpha
confidence set on the true covariance matrix as calculated from the given data.
We call this process the “data-driven minimax” (DDMM) adjustment, and, to the best of our knowledge, it is the first such method for rigorously handling pilot sampling variability for multi-fidelity forward UQ.
We also introduce a data-free version of the minimax adjustment but focus our demonstrations on the DDMM procedure.

During our discussion, we intentionally introduce the DDMM adjustment generally — the framework can be used regardless of one’s choice of adjustment or discrepancy function.
The DDMM method can easily be extended in formulation to settings with multiple low-fidelity models, adjustments that include standard deviation estimates, or other estimators with more complex sample allocation schemes, at the expense of computational cost, which we leave to future work.
We make specific choices in our own implementation that make the problem tractable.
We focus on the bi-fidelity setting and aim to correct the single correlation coefficient between the two available models.
A global sensitivity analysis using Shapley values
[
27
,
35
,
26
]
finds that the correlation coefficient contributes significantly more to the variance of estimator variance than the standard deviations, justifying our choice to only adjust the former.
In addition, we limit our implementation to Multi-Fidelity Monte Carlo (MFMC)
[
28
]
estimators, which have analytical solutions for their variance-minimizing hyperparameter settings and which we show perform well under limited pilot sample sizes in comparison to more general sample allocation schemes.

The contributions of this work are as follows:

•

We introduce a novel log-ratio discrepancy function quantifying the suboptimality due to pilot sampling covariance variability by directly considering the unrealized reduction in estimator variance without access to exact covariance information.

•

We provide an empirical study into estimator robustness and the problem of pilot sampling. We perform a global sensitivity analysis on the MFMC estimator variance using Shapley values, highlighting that the correlation coefficient is the most important covariance component to estimate well. Together with an empirical investigation into the suboptimality of various multi-fidelity estimators beyond MFMC, this result justifies our practical focus on correlation adjustments for MFMC estimators.

•

Drawing inspiration from Statistical Decision Theory (SDT), we define the DDMM adjustment procedure, which adopts a risk-aware approach to finding an optimal covariance estimate (under our assumptions), and thus estimator hyperparameters, given a set of pilot samples. We then develop a practical methodology for solving the DDMM adjustment optimization problem under the bi-fidelity MFMC setting.

•

We develop a numerical procedure for determining the optimal DDMM confidence level
\alpha
such that the adjusted estimator variance is expected to be as small as possible, as quantified by the expected discrepancy function.

•

We demonstrate the overall method on two examples,
showing that the method can produce better estimator variances than unadjusted estimators across a variety of estimator budgets and pilot sampling sizes in real multi-fidelity UQ analyses.

2.1
Demonstration of suboptimality

The fundamental goal of multi-fidelity methods is to combine different model fidelity output to minimize estimator variance subject to finite computational constraints.
To introduce the sense in which using the sample covariance leads to estimator suboptimality, we use the following toy example.
Suppose we have two computational models,
\mathcal{M}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
for
i=0,1
, where
i=0
refers to the high-fidelity (hifi) model and
i=1
refers to the low-fidelity (lofi) model.
Each of these model outputs is random due to a random input sampled
{\bm{z}}\sim\mathcal{P}
.
Model
\mathcal{M}_{i}
is associated with cost
c_{i}
and we assume
c_{0}>c_{1}
.
Given a total compute budget,
C
, we must satisfy the constraint that
n_{0}c_{0}+n_{1}c_{1}\leq C
, where
n_{0}
is the number of hifi model evaluations and
n_{1}
is the number of lofi model evaluations.
In this example, we assume
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},{\bm{I}}_{2})

\bm{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\
\rho&1\end{pmatrix}={\bm{L}}{\bm{L}}^{T},

(1)

with
{\bm{L}}
denoting its Cholesky factor.
When we draw a sample
{\bm{z}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},{\bm{I}}_{2})
and define
{\bm{y}}={\bm{L}}{\bm{z}}
, we have
{\bm{y}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma})
.
We suppose that the first component of
{\bm{y}}
is the output of our hifi model and the second component the output from our lofi model.
We then have the following:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:toy_{d}gm}y_{0}=\mathcal{M}_{0}({\bm{z}}),\quad y_{1}=\mathcal{M}_{1}({\bm{z}}),\quad\text{Cov}[y_{0},y_{1}]=\rho.

(2)

To facilitate problem articulation, we further assume that we know each of the model output variances (without loss of generality these are taken to be
\text{Var}(\mathcal{M}_{i}({\bm{z}}))=1
), and thus we can simply explore the suboptimality of the pilot sample covariance through the sample correlation

.
We also assume that we know the model correlation is positive
\rho\in(0,1)
.
Following the notation of
[

, the general MFMC estimator of the expectation is defined as,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:estimator}\widehat{y}(\xi):=\widehat{y}_{0}+\xi\left(\widehat{y}_{1+}-\widehat{y}_{1-}\right),

(3)

where
\widehat{y}_{0}=n_{0}^{-1}\sum_{i}^{n_{0}}\mathcal{M}_{0}(z^{(i)})
is a hifi Monte Carlo (MC) estimator,
\widehat{y}_{1+}=n_{0}^{-1}\sum_{i}^{n_{0}}\mathcal{M}_{1}(z^{(i)})

\widehat{y}_{1-}=n_{1}^{-1}\sum_{i}^{n_{1}}\mathcal{M}_{1}(z^{(i)})
is a lofi MC estimator using an augmented input set with

The MFMC estimator has the following variance under the above model assumptions:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:est_{v}ariance}v(\bm{\beta};\rho):=\text{Var}\left[\widehat{y}(\xi)\right]=\frac{1}{n_{0}}+\left(\frac{1}{n_{0}}-\frac{1}{n_{1}}\right)(\xi^{2}-2\xi\rho).

(4)

We have condensed the left-hand side using
\bm{\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}n_{0}&n_{1}&\xi\end{pmatrix}^{T}
to indicate the quantities over which the variance is optimized, i.e., the MFMC estimator hyperparameters.
The optimal model evaluation numbers and estimator weight are then found by minimizing the estimator variance subject to the known computational constraints,

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{\beta}}{\min}

\displaystyle\quad v(\bm{\beta};\rho)

(5)

subject to

\displaystyle\quad n_{0}c_{0}+n_{1}c_{1}\leq C.

We refer to the solution to Optimization
5
by
\bm{\beta}^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}n_{0}^{*}&n_{1}^{*}&\xi^{*}\end{pmatrix}^{T}
.
If
c_{0}c_{1}^{-1}>\rho^{-2}-1
, there is a closed-form solution to the global minimizer of Optimization

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:optimal_{b}fmc}n_{0}^{*}=\frac{C}{c_{0}+c_{1}r},\quad n_{1}^{*}=n_{0}^{*}r,\quad\xi^{*}=\rho

Suppose we obtain a small number of pilot samples,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:sample_{c}orr_{f}ormula}\widehat{\rho}_{N}:=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y^{i}_{0}-\mu_{0})(y^{i}_{1}-\mu_{1})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y^{i}_{0}-\mu_{0})\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y^{i}_{1}-\mu_{1})}},

2.2
The discrepancy function quantifies suboptimality

Suppose now that we have
M\in\mathbb{N}
models and their associated costs.
For a fixed covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{++}^{M}
(i.e., the set of symmetric positive definite matrices of dimension
N\times N
), let
v(\cdot;\bm{\Sigma}):\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
be the multi-fidelity estimator variance we wish to optimize, where
p
denotes the estimator hyperparameter dimension, i.e., the total number of sample allocations and weights for the
M
models.
We generalize Optimization
5
as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\beta}}\quad

\displaystyle v(\bm{\beta};\bm{\Sigma})

(8)

subject to

\displaystyle{\bm{c}}^{T}\bm{\beta}\leq C,

where
\bm{\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{n}&\bm{\xi}\end{pmatrix}^{T}
such that
\mathbf{n}
is the sample allocation vector,
\bm{\xi}
is the weights vector,
C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the total computational budget, and
{\bm{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denotes the individual model costs for the first
M
components and zeros for the remaining
p-M
.
Let
\bm{\beta}^{*}
denote the global minimizer of Optimization
8
.
Suppose we obtain
N\in\mathbb{N}
pilot samples by generating
N
I.I.D. samples
{\bm{z}}_{i}\sim\mathcal{P}
(the distribution over the input vectors) for
i=1,2,\dots,N
, producing the model outputs
{\bm{y}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{2},\dots,{\bm{y}}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
(henceforth compactly referred to as
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
) with the associated sample covariance matrix
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}
.
Let
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
denote the global minimizer of Optimization
8
but with
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}
replacing the true covariance.

Intuitively, we wish to quantify the estimator variance suboptimality resulting from using the pilot samples to estimate the covariance matrix.
We propose the
discrepancy function
as one such option.

Definition 1

(Discrepancy Function)
.

Define the function
\delta:\mathcal{S}_{++}^{M}\times\mathcal{S}_{++}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
as follows,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:discrepancy_{f}unction}\delta(\bm{\Sigma}_{1},\bm{\Sigma}_{2}):=\log\left(\frac{v(\bm{\beta}_{1};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})}{v(\bm{\beta}_{2};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})}\right),

(9)

where
\bm{\beta}_{1}
minimizes
v(\bm{\beta};\bm{\Sigma}_{1})
and
\bm{\beta}_{2}
minimizes
v(\bm{\beta};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})
, both subject to the constraint
{\bm{c}}^{T}\bm{\beta}\leq C
.

In practice, to evaluate the performance of the sample covariance
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}
for a fixed true covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
, we consider
\delta(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N},\bm{\Sigma})
.
Clearly, if
\bm{\Sigma}_{1}=\bm{\Sigma}_{2}
, then
\delta(\bm{\Sigma}_{1},\bm{\Sigma}_{2})=0
, otherwise it is positive, reflecting a notion of distance between the obtained estimator variances.
We have refrained from referring to this function as a distance metric since it is not symmetric.
We have also refrained from referring to this function as a loss function despite our inspiration from SDT.
Although our discrepancy function could be characterized as a loss function, we primarily care about the discrepancy of the optimal estimator variances rather than the proximity of
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}
to
\bm{\Sigma}
, so we choose this different name to emphasize the distinction.

There are other valid ways to define a discrepancy function such that it quantifies estimator variance suboptimality due to covariance misspecification.
An obvious alternative is a function of the form,
\delta_{regret}(\bm{\Sigma}_{1},\bm{\Sigma}_{2})=v(\bm{\beta}_{1};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})-v(\bm{\beta}_{2};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})
, where we use the term “regret” to indicate its similarity to traditionally defined regret functions (see
[
4
,
9
]
, for example).
Interestingly for MFMC,
\delta_{regret}
can be seen as a Bregman divergence associated with a particular convex function
[
9
]
.
Through this connection, one can potentially make some interesting theoretical insights, but since the discrepancy as defined in
Equation

9
is invariant to budget (see the following paragraph and
Lemma

2.1
), we focus the results in this paper around this choice.
Furthermore, we found our discrepancy function to be more numerically stable than the potential “regret”-type option.

2.3
The discrepancy function is invariant to estimator budget

When using approximate control variate (ACV), which includes MFMC, estimators
[
14
]
, we can make some additional comments about the discrepancy function.
One particular quantity of interest (QoI) for a given multi-fidelity estimator is the
variance reduction ratio
(VRR), quantifying how much the estimator reduces variance relative to the corresponding hifi MC estimator of the same budget.
Defining the hifi MC estimator variance as
v_{0}(\bm{\Sigma})
, the VRR associated with an estimator with hyperparameters
\bm{\beta}_{1}
is defined as
\gamma(\bm{\beta}_{1};\bm{\Sigma})=\frac{v_{0}(\bm{\Sigma})}{v(\bm{\beta}_{1};\bm{\Sigma})}
.

To analyze the behavior of the discrepancy function with respect to the budget
C
, we assume a continuous relaxation of the sample allocations, ignoring nearest-integer rounding constraints.
Under this relaxation, the estimator variance is inversely proportional to the total budget.
We can decompose the hyperparameters into
\bm{\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}C\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}&\bm{\xi}\end{pmatrix}^{T}
, where
\bm{\xi}
contains control variate weights and
\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}
contains the budget-normalized sample allocations satisfying
{\bm{c}}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}\leq 1
.
The estimator variance then factors as
v(\bm{\beta};\bm{\Sigma})=\frac{1}{C}\widetilde{v}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}};\bm{\Sigma})
, where
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}&\bm{\xi}\end{pmatrix}^{T}
represents the budget-independent hyperparameters.

Because the budget acts as a universal scaling term on both the numerator and the denominator, the VRR is a budget-agnostic measure of the efficiency of a multi-fidelity estimator.
This scaling property naturally extends to our discrepancy function.

Lemma 2.1

(Discrepancy function budget invariance)
.

Under the continuous relaxation of sample allocations, the discrepancy function in Definition
9
is independent of the total budget
C
and is equivalent to the log-ratio of the VRRs associated with
\bm{\beta}_{1}
and
\bm{\beta}_{2}
:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:discrepancy_{v}rr}\delta(\bm{\Sigma}_{1},\bm{\Sigma}_{2})=\log\left(\frac{\gamma(\bm{\beta}_{2};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})}{\gamma(\bm{\beta}_{1};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})}\right),

(10)

where
\bm{\beta}_{1}
and
\bm{\beta}_{2}
minimize the budget-normalized variances
\widetilde{v}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}};\bm{\Sigma}_{1})
and
\widetilde{v}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})
respectively, subject to
{\bm{c}}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}\leq 1
.

Proof.

By the definition of the variance reduction ratio,
\gamma(\bm{\beta}_{2};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})/\gamma(\bm{\beta}_{1};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})=v(\bm{\beta}_{1};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})/v(\bm{\beta}_{2};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})
. Factoring the estimator variances into their budget-dependent and budget-independent components yields:

\frac{v(\bm{\beta}_{1};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})}{v(\bm{\beta}_{2};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})}=\frac{\frac{1}{C}\widetilde{v}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{1};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})}{\frac{1}{C}\widetilde{v}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{2};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})}=\frac{\widetilde{v}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{1};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})}{\widetilde{v}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{2};\bm{\Sigma}_{2})}.

The scale factor
1/C
cancels, demonstrating that the ratio—and consequently the discrepancy function
\delta(\bm{\Sigma}_{1},\bm{\Sigma}_{2})
—is invariant to the total budget constraint
C
.
∎

2.4
The expected discrepancy metric

As described in
Section

2.1
, the discrepancy function becomes a random variable through the sampling distribution of the sample covariance which depends on the distribution of the inputs (
\mathcal{P}
) and the available models.
We assume
{\bm{y}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
, i.e., that the model outputs follow a multivariate Gaussian distribution with both unknown mean and covariance.
This assumption is not necessary for the statement of our framework, but it does conveniently lead to the sampling distribution of the sample covariance,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:wishart_{s}ampling}(N-1)\cdot\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}\sim\mathcal{W}(N-1,\bm{\Sigma}),

(11)

where
\mathcal{W}(N-1,\Sigma)
is a Wishart distribution with
N-1
degrees of freedom and scatter matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
. The associated sample covariance is defined as usual,

\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left({\bm{y}}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{{\bm{y}}}\right)\left({\bm{y}}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{{\bm{y}}}\right)^{T},

(12)

where
\mu_{{\bm{y}}}
denotes the sample mean over the vectors
{\bm{y}}_{i}
.
It is well known in the statistics literature that the Wishart distribution characterizes the sampling distribution of the maximum likelihood estimator of the covariance of a multivariate Gaussian distribution (e.g.,
[
2
]
).
This assumption is particularly convenient since the Wishart distribution can be used to estimate statistical functionals of
Equation

9
; we primarily consider the expectation,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:expected_{d}iscrepancy}\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}}\left[\delta\left(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N},\bm{\Sigma}\right)\right].

(13)

As we show in
Section

4
, we use this notion of expected discrepancy to choose a different estimator of the covariance matrix, as it provides a quantification of a multi-fidelity estimator’s (e.g., MFMC) expected suboptimality with respect to pilot sample variability.

3
An empirical investigation into the robustness of multi-fidelity estimators

Now that we have introduced the problem of pilot sampling and defined a discrepancy metric to quantify the suboptimality associated with pilot sampling, we will empirically investigate how multi-fidelity estimators perform in terms of pilot sampling robustness.
Specifically, we will compare a variety of popular multi-fidelity sampling-based estimators across pilot sampling sizes and modeling scenarios to see if there are specific estimators that tend to be more or less susceptible to pilot-sampling variability.

The results in

further corroborate this finding. Since each estimator discrepancy is with respect to its own oracle (best-case) estimator variance, this plot shows the relative sensitivity of each estimator to pilot sampling variability.
While more general estimators may eventually overtake MFMC and WRDIFF in terms of absolute performance, they are unequivocally more sensitive to pilot sampling variability in terms of the expected estimator discrepancy.

We posit two possible explanations for these effects.
First, the more general estimators have a tendency to “overfit” their sample allocations to pilot sampling variability.
Since these estimators have fewer constraints on their sample-allocation optimization problem, they make use of every cross-correlation in the provided covariance matrix and are affected by spurious cross-correlation
pilot-sampling noise.
In contrast, the sample-allocation constraints of MFMC and WRDIFF regularize the associated optimization solutions and make the estimator variance a function of just
M-1
cross-correlations, leading to far less variability under the same pilot sampling variability.

Second, calculating the optimal weights can become highly sensitive under these more general estimators due to the linear algebra involved.
Generalized ACV methods require the inversion of a dense
(M-1)\times(M-1)
covariance matrix to find the variance-optimal weights while the corresponding MFMC and WRDIFF matrices are sparse and diagonal.
Since this matrix that must be inverted is a direct function of the provided sample covariance matrix, its inversion can further amplify pilot variability, making MFMC and WRDIFF more robust since inverting a diagonal matrix is more stable.
The advantage of diagonalizing this matrix is especially salient when considering that ACVIS, while it is further generalized by GISMR, does not seem to benefit from the same advantages as MFMC and WRDIFF since it also involves a dense matrix inversion.
Of note, the corresponding matrix inversion for MLBLUE can grow even larger, over all possible models in all possible model groupings, further exacerbating this issue and making it the most sensitive estimator to pilot sampling variability.

A related concern regarding robustness is the
projected
estimator variance, which refers to the estimator variance obtained when evaluating under the sample covariance,
v(\widehat{\bm{\beta}};\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}})
.
Since practitioners generally consider this projected variance when deciding how much to trust one’s estimator outputs, there can be significant overconfidence issues when it is significantly less than the true estimator error evaluated using the oracle covariance,
v(\widehat{\bm{\beta}};\bm{\Sigma})
.
Under the above test setup, we plot the ratio between the true estimator variance

3.1
A global sensitivity analysis of MFMC suboptimality

Global sensitivity analysis (GSA) is a statistical method for evaluating how the uncertainty in model inputs affects the model outputs.
These methods generally assign a sensitivity index to each input of the system, with a larger index indicating a larger impact on the output uncertainty.
In our case, we are interested in how the uncertainty in the sample covariance matrix
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}
affects the uncertainty in the estimator suboptimality
\delta(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}},\bm{\Sigma})
for some true covariance
\bm{\Sigma}
.
We use GSA to identify which components of the sample covariance matrix have the greatest impact on the uncertainty in estimator suboptimality, helping guide our proposed correction strategy in
Section

4
.

We adopt a variance-based approach using Shapley values
[
26
]
to properly account for the dependent inputs, namely the sample correlation
\widehat{\rho}
and sample standard deviations
\widehat{\sigma}_{0},\widehat{\sigma}_{1}
.
Rooted in game theory, the Shapley GSA method quantifies each input’s fair contribution to the discrepancy metric’s variability by averaging each input’s marginal contribution across all possible coalitions.

Appendix

D
shows the mathematical formulation and computational details of this approach.

Sweeping over 100 values of
\rho\in[0.01,0.99]
for the setup from
Section

2.1
, the results of the Shapley-based GSA are shown in
Figure

3.4
.
Clearly, the main contributor to the uncertainty in the true estimator variance from the sample covariance uncertainty is the correlation coefficient between the low- and high-fidelity model, with an average (over
\rho
) Shapley value of 0.74, while the low- and high-fidelity model standard deviations have an average Shapley value of just 0.09 and 0.17, respectively.
This may be explained by two phenomena.
First, the VRR, and thus the optimal sample allocation, under oracle covariance information is independent of the standard deviations.
This point is elucidated in
[
36
]
and therefore removes the standard deviations entirely from the numerator of the discrepancy function, as defined in
Equation

10
, for any ACV estimator.
Second, as highlighted in
Equation

6
, the MFMC weights vector is a function of the
ratio
between each lofi standard deviation and the hifi standard deviation, which may be easier to estimate and more statistically controlled than individual standard deviations when using shared pilot samples.
Motivated by these results, we focus our attention on the correlation coefficient when constructing a protocol to mitigate the effects of pilot sampling variability for MFMC.

4
Producing an improved covariance estimator from pilot samples

Using the discrepancy function defined in
Equation

9
, this section provides a procedure to select an improved estimate of the true covariance matrix under pilot sample uncertainty.
Intuitively, the sample covariance computed from the pilot samples provides some information about the true covariance.
Under the Gaussian model outputs assumption, one such piece of information can be probabilistically identified as a
(1-\alpha)
confidence set (for
\alpha\in(0,1)
) in the space of positive definite (PD) matrices.
Leveraging this information, our procedure looks to pick an estimator that minimizes the worst-case expected discrepancy within that
(1-\alpha)
confidence set.
The section is structured as follows.

Section

4.1
defines how we characterize estimator optimality using the language of SDT.

Section

4.2
then defines a data-driven minimax construction along with key mathematical intuition and theory.
Finally,
Section

4.3
describes the estimator implementation.
A concise description of the procedure is given by
Algorithm

1
.

4.1
Evaluating a covariance estimator

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the space of all possible covariance estimators that are a function of observed data such that for each
h\in\mathcal{H}
,
h(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)
produces a PD matrix estimating the true covariance (recall, we use
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
to compactly refer to the collection of
N
pilot samples).
The expected discrepancy function provides a way to compare the performance of different covariance estimators such that the structure of the multi-fidelity scenario is taken into account.
We ideally desire an estimator (
h^{\prime}
) that is better (i.e., lower expected discrepancy) than the sample covariance for all possible covariance matrices,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:dominating_{e}stimator_{s}ample_{c}ovariance}\mathbb{E}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\left[\delta\left(h^{\prime}\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\right),\bm{\Sigma}\right)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\left[\delta\left(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N},\bm{\Sigma}\right)\right],\;\;\forall\;\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{S}^{M}_{++}.

(14)

In the language of SDT, the existence of
h^{\prime}
would make
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}

inadmissible
or equivalently,
dominated
by
h^{\prime}
(see chapter one of
[
4
]
).
More specific to the multi-fidelity problem setting, we wish to find an estimator that is better than the sample covariance for known types of covariance matrices.
For instance, in the scenario explored in the
Section

2.1
, we wish to find an estimator that is better than the sample covariance when
\rho\in(l,u)\subset(0,1)
.

4.2
Data-driven minimax (DDMM) construction

With the expected discrepancy defined by
Equation

13
, we define a class of procedures producing a covariance estimator.
We restrict our attention to
adjustments
of the sample covariance matrix and define an adjustment as follows.

Definition 2

(Adjustment)
.

Let
\Theta
be a space in which we may define a decision (e.g., a Euclidean parameter space like
\mathbb{R}^{s}
).
A function
g:\Theta\times\mathcal{S}_{++}^{M}\to\mathcal{S}_{++}^{M}
is considered an adjustment function if it maps the sample covariance matrix to a new covariance matrix.

For example, let
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
and suppose we have an adjustment function
g
and have computed the sample covariance
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}
from the
N
pilot samples.
Then, we write the
adjusted
sample covariance as
g(\bm{\theta},\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N})
.
We have intentionally limited the scope of
Definition

2
to use the sample covariance matrix as an input, but this choice is not strictly necessary.
In principle, one could simply define a map from the observed data to a PD matrix.
For example, if we know
a priori
that
\rho\in(0,1)
, the adjustment function could take the form
\widehat{\rho}\mapsto\max\{0,\widehat{\rho}\}
.
SDT refers to
\Theta
as the
action space
and allows us to define a
minimax adjustment
(similar to Definition 1 in Chapter 5V in
[
4
]
).

Definition 3

(Minimax Adjustment)
.

Under an adjustment function
g
, we call
\bm{\theta}^{*}
a minimax adjustment if it satisfies the following:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:minimax_{a}djustment}\sup_{\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{++}^{M}}\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}}\left[\delta\left(g\left(\bm{\theta}^{*},\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}\right),\bm{\Sigma}\right)\right]=\inf_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\sup_{\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{++}^{M}}\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}}\left[\delta\left(g\left(\bm{\theta},\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}\right),\bm{\Sigma}\right)\right].

(15)

Unlike
[
4
]
, we have parameterized the action space through the function
g
to add structure.
This decision builds off our decision above to define adjustments with respect to the sample covariance.

If one were to compute
\bm{\theta}^{*}
according to
Definition

3
, it would be maximally robust with respect to the chosen adjustment function.
Furthermore, it would be computable ahead of any observed pilot samples, since its computation only relies upon the chosen variance estimator and statistical assumptions (i.e., Gaussian outputs).
Although this latter property may appear desirable, it could potentially be improved by incorporating the information from the pilot samples as indicated at the beginning of
Section

4
.
Said differently, this covariance estimator is likely
too
robust and would not render the sample covariance inadmissible as desired.

To address these points, we propose a similar minimax-style estimator that relies upon the pilot samples.
For each fixed adjustment parameter
\bm{\theta}
, we propose maximizing over a subset of all possible covariances matrices depending on the sample covariance.
Intuitively, we find a subset of
\mathcal{S}_{++}^{M}
containing the true covariance matrix with high probability (e.g., probability
1-\alpha
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
) resulting in a maximization over a smaller set.
More formally, let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}
be the sample covariance resulting from the pilot samples.
If a function
C_{\alpha}:\mathcal{S}_{++}^{M}\to\mathcal{S}_{++}^{M}
operates such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{\Sigma}\in C_{\alpha}\left(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}\right)\right)\geq 1-\alpha,\quad\forall\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{++}^{M},

(16)

then
C_{\alpha}(\cdot)
is referred to as a
1-\alpha

confidence set
of the true covariance matrix,
\bm{\Sigma}
.
Note, the output of
C_{\alpha}(\cdot)
is a random subset of
\mathcal{S}_{++}^{M}
because the pilot samples are random and thus the sample covariance matrix is random.
Additionally, since our empirical results focus on adjustments of the sample correlation rather than the sample covariance, after this subsection we use
C_{\alpha}(\cdot)
with the sample correlation (
\widehat{\rho}_{N}
) instead of the sample covariance matrix.
This confidence set allows the following modification of
Definition

3
.

Definition 4

(Data-Driven Minimax (DDMM) Adjustment)
.

In the context of an adjustment function
g
, we call
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)
a
data-driven
minimax adjustment if it satisfies the following,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:dd_{m}inimax_{a}djustment}\sup_{\bm{\Sigma}\in\widehat{C}_{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}}\left[\delta\left(g\left(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu),\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}\right),\bm{\Sigma}\right)\right]=\inf_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\sup_{\bm{\Sigma}\in\widehat{C}_{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}}\left[\delta\left(g\left(\bm{\theta},\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}\right),\bm{\Sigma}\right)\right],

(17)

where
\widehat{C}_{\alpha}:=C_{\alpha}\left(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}\right)
.

For all
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
, the worst-case expected discrepancy is probabilistically guaranteed to be lower for the data-driven formulation since the maximization is over a smaller set.
Finding such an adjustment carries the intuition that we do not want to protect against unfavorable covariance scenarios if they are sufficiently far from our sample covariance.
Alternatively, we still wish for our estimator to be probabilistically robust in the sense that the DDMM expected discrepancy is an upper bound on the expected discrepancy at the true covariance matrix.
The above intuition is formalized in
Proposition

4.1
.

Proposition 4.1

(Inspired by Proposition 1 in
[
10
]
)
.

Let
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)
denote the DDMM adjustment specified by
Definition

4
.
Let
\eta^{*}:=\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}}\left[\delta\left(g\left(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu),\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}\right),\bm{\Sigma}^{*}\right)\right]
for any true underlying covariance matrix,
\bm{\Sigma}^{*}
.
Let
\widehat{\eta}:=\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}}\left[\delta\left(g\left(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu),\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}\right),\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime}\right)\right]
where
\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime}
maximizes the expected discrepancy over all
\bm{\Sigma}\in C_{\alpha}\left(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}\right)
.
Then,

\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{\eta}\geq\eta^{*}\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

(18)

Proof.

By definition, the probability that
\bm{\Sigma}^{*}\in C_{\alpha}\left(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}\right)
is at least
1-\alpha
.
If
\bm{\Sigma}^{*}\in C_{\alpha}\left(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}\right)
, it follows that

\widehat{\eta}=\max_{\bm{\Sigma}\in C_{\alpha}\left(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}\right)}\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}}\left[\delta\left(g\left(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu),\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}\right),\bm{\Sigma}\right)\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}}\left[\delta\left(g\left(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu),\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}\right),\bm{\Sigma}^{*}\right)\right]=\eta^{*}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{\eta}\geq\eta^{*}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{\Sigma}^{*}\in C_{\alpha}\left(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}\right)\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

∎

Some intuition for
\alpha

Since
\alpha
sets the miscoverage level of the confidence set for the true covariance, it controls an intuitive tradeoff involving robustness and optimality.
When
\alpha
is small, the confidence set is large and thus DDMM produces an adjustment accounting for a large set of covariance matrices.
In turn, the smallest worst-case expected discrepancy is larger.
When
\alpha
is large, the confidence set is small and DDMM produces an adjustment acccounting for a relatively small set of possible covariance matrices.
Since there is a tradeoff, it is reasonable to think that there is a
best

\alpha
to choose when using DDMM.
Indeed,

E.3
describes our approach for selecting

that provides optimal performance relative to the sample covariance (correlation).

4.3
Implementing DDMM

We focus our implementation on the scenario outlined in
Section

2.1
.
Namely, we consider a single hifi model and single lofi model with known unit variances and thus the DDMM procedure only needs to adjust the sample correlation.

4.3.1
A sigmoid adjustment function

To fit our
a priori
knowledge that
\rho\in(0,1)
, we use a sigmoid adjustment function defined as follows:

g(\bm{\theta};\widehat{\rho}):=\frac{\exp\left(\theta_{0}\widehat{\rho}+\theta_{1}\right)}{1+\exp\left(\theta_{0}\widehat{\rho}+\theta_{1}\right)},

(19)

where
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
are the first and second components of
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, respectively.
By construction, the sigmoid adjustment function ensures that any sample correlation
\widehat{\rho}\in(-1,1)
gets mapped to
(0,1)
.

4.3.2
Computing the confidence interval

Once the
N
pilot samples have been observed, we compute
\widehat{\rho}
according to
Equation

7
and compute
C_{\alpha}(\widehat{\rho})
using the following method.

\alpha\in(0,1)
can be chosen either heuristically (as is often done in statistics and robust optimization) or computationally (see
Section

E.3
).
Unlike the general exposition in
Section

4.2
, we compute a confidence interval such that
C_{\alpha}(\widehat{\rho})\subset(-1,1)
.

We seek an interval
[\rho_{l}(\widehat{\rho}),\rho_{u}(\widehat{\rho})]
such that
\mathbb{P}(\rho\in[\rho_{l}(\widehat{\rho}),\rho_{u}(\widehat{\rho})])\geq 1-\alpha
for any
\rho\in(-1,1)
.
In the following exposition, we eliminate the
\widehat{\rho}
from the interval endpoint notation as it should be clear that these are random endpoints downstream of the random sample correlation.
Equivalently, we want endpoints such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\rho_{l}\leq\rho\leq\rho_{u}\right)\geq 1-\alpha,\;\;\rho\in(-1,1).

(20)

Since we know the sampling distribution of
\widehat{\rho}
both in closed-form (e.g.,
[
17
]
) or by sampling the appropriate Wishart distribution, finding these endpoints can be framed as a root-finding procedure.
Namely, let
r\in(-1,1)
denote the realized sample correlation of the random variable,
\widehat{\rho}
.
The lower interval endpoint is the smallest
\rho
such that the probability that
\widehat{\rho}>r
is equal to
\alpha/2
.
Formally,
\rho_{l}
is the correlation value such that,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:ci_{l}ep}\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{\rho}>r\mid\rho_{l},N\right)=\frac{\alpha}{2},

(21)

where the above probability notation is the probability of the event
\{\widehat{\rho}>r\}
under the sampling distribution defined by the true correlation set to
\rho_{l}
and the number of pilot samples set to
N
.
Similarly, the upper endpoint is the largest
\rho_{u}
such that,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:ci_{u}ep}\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{\rho}<r\mid\rho_{u},N\right)=\frac{\alpha}{2}.

(22)

The root-finding procedure to find these two endpoints simply adjust
\rho_{l}
and
\rho_{u}
until the desired probability is obtained.

Additional details on this procedure and others can be found in
[
2
,
22
]
.
Note, for the more general case in which a confidence set in
\mathcal{S}_{++}^{M}
is desired, inverting a likelihood ratio test provides a clear path forward.
The probabilities in
Equation

21
and
Equation

22
can be computed numerically or via Monte Carlo using the methods described in
Appendix

.
For the purpose of choosing an optimal

as described in
Section

E.3
, it is advantageous to learn the interval endpoint surfaces over the
(\alpha,r)
space, since solving the above root-finding problems can be computationally challenging.
Since the sample correlation density is smoothly varying as a function of both the confidence level (
\alpha
) and the true correlation (
\rho
), using a smoothing or interpolating spline to fit these surfaces produces accurate results.
Details of this approach can be found in
Section

E.2

4.3.3
Solving the minimax optimization

Since solving minimax problems is generally challenging
4
4

4

Solving this problem using gradient-based optimization falls in the category of stochastic optimization and can be approached using stochastic gradient descent (SGD)
[
25
]
. Using SGD-based approaches typically requires convex-concave assumptions on the objective to guarantee solution optimality, however there are more recent efforts to relax these assumptions
[
18
,
24
]
.
, we pursue a discretized solution.
Since the expected discrepancy is neither convex in
\bm{\theta}
nor concave in
\rho
, avoiding numerical (non)gradient-based optimization in favor of computational complexity is prudent.
Let
G_{\bm{\theta}}:=\{\bm{\theta}\}_{i=1}^{n_{\bm{\theta}}}
denote a grid of
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
G_{\rho}:=\{\rho_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{\rho}}
denote a grid of
\rho\in(0,1)
.
Once
C_{\alpha}(\widehat{\rho})=[\rho_{l},\rho_{u}]
has been computed, if given a matrix containing the expected discrepancy values corresponding to the
(\bm{\theta},\rho)
settings with rows indexed over
G_{\bm{\theta}}
and columns indexed over
G_{\rho}
, then we simply ignore the columns outside of
C_{\alpha}(\widehat{\rho})
. We can then produce the DDMM solution by first maximizing over all remaining rows then minimizing over the remaining one-dimensional array.
Numerically, the discretization error can be arbitrarily minimized via the grid sizes.
This approach relies upon an expected discrepancy array over
(\bm{\theta},\rho)
, which can be cumbersome to compute at fine resolutions of
\bm{\theta}
and
\rho
.
As such, in
Section

E.1
, we propose an efficient procedure for computing this high-resolution array which involves first computing a low-resolution array, performing a tensor decomposition, using splines to fit the orthogonal basis functions, and up-scaling the matrix by re-composing the tensor at the desired resolutions.

5
Computational and Empirical Results

We present computational and empirical results to build intuition for DDMM’s operation on a sample level, demonstrate its superior performance under small pilot sample regimes, and support the robustness of its superior performance to assumption violations.
Computationally, we use the toy scenario in
Section

2.1
for which all assumptions hold and show that the DDMM-adjusted sample correlation yields a lower expected discrepancy than the typical sample correlation.
Empirically, we use the NASA EDL multi-model Monte Carlo dataset
[
38
]
to show that the theoretical performance superiority holds under assumption violations arising from real data.
We further show that the DDMM adjustment improves the expected variance reduction across all QoIs relative to the unadjusted sample correlation.

In line with
Section

4.1
and
Equation

14
, we wish to show that the DDMM-adjusted sample covariance achieves a lower expected discrepancy than the unadjusted sample covariance across all possible true covariance matrices consistent with our knowledge.
To facilitate this exposition, we consider the
Expected Discrepancy Difference
(EDD) and
Expected Discrepancy Difference percentage
(%EDD) where the latter is computed by dividing EDD by the unadjusted expected discrepancy.

Definition 5

(Expected Discrepancy Difference (EDD))
.

Given a covariance estimator
h\in\mathcal{H}
,
N
pilot samples
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, and
\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{S}_{++}^{M}
, the EDD is defined as follows,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:edd}EDD:=\mathbb{E}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\left[\delta(h(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu),\bm{\Sigma})-\delta\left(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N},\bm{\Sigma}\right)\right].

(23)

If the DDMM estimator
h
produces
\Delta(h,\bm{\Sigma})\leq 0
across all
\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{S}
, then DDMM dominates the sample covariance.
In the following two sections, we consider using a single hifi and single lofi model and only consider covariance matrices via the correlation.
I.e., in
Section

5.1
we assume the individual model variances are known and equal to one, and in
Section

5.2
we only adjust the sample correlation but leave the sample variances untouched when evaluating the (%)EDD.
Both scenarios consider the (%)EDD over
\rho\in(0,1)
.

5.1
Bivariate Gaussian benchmark

Under the bivariate Gaussian assumption as described in

6
Summary

For multi-fidelity Monte Carlo procedures, methods are sensitive to pilot sample variability (especially at low sample sizes).
We have proposed a mathematical framework based on a novel discrepancy metric with which this sensitivity can be characterized and quantified, and we have investigated the robustness of multi-fidelity estimators through this lens.
Our empirical investigation into the robustness of these estimators elucidated that there is a clear trade-off between estimator expressivity and estimator robustness, with more generalized estimators suffering both in terms of performance and sensitivity under limited pilot samples while MFMC in particular performs well in these conditions.
We also showed, via a global sensitivity analysis of the MFMC estimator discrepancy, that the uncertainty in the sample correlation coefficient has the greatest impact on estimator variability.
To improve estimator robustness to pilot sample variability, we proposed the data-driven minimax procedure which minimizes the worst-case expected discrepancy over a set of plausible covariance matrices.
Computationally, we demonstrated that the DDMM estimator renders the standard sample covariance inadmissible, in that it produces lower expected discrepancy across all correlation settings
\rho\in(0,1)
in the described two-model scenario.
We also observed that the DDMM adjustment uniformly improves performance up to
N=15
pilot samples and shows potential gains even for larger pilot sample sizes.
Empirically, using the NASA EDL multi-fidelity Monte Carlo dataset, we showed that DDMM’s superior performance holds even when its theoretical assumptions are violated.
Furthermore, the theoretical expected discrepancy difference values were good approximations of the empirical ones obtained for from EDL data for nearly all QoIs in this dataset.
Finally, we showed that, across all QoIs, the variance reduction was improved when using the DDMM correction over just the pilot sample covariance.

We hope the tools, methods, and results in this paper enable the multi-fidelity Monte Carlo community to better quantify and handle variability due to pilot sampling.
With its current implementation, an optimal
\alpha
(see
Section

E.3
) and DDMM-adjusted sample correlation can be computed in less than 15 minutes using a personal computer (see
Section

E.3.2
for the details of the machine used to generate the results of this paper).
As such, adding this adjustment step to an MFMC pipeline has relatively little computational cost while substantially improving estimator robustness.
There are many possible generalizations of this work including different discrepancy functions, different adjustment functions, different statistical notions of estimator optimality, and extensions to multiple lofi model scenarios, which we leave for future work.
Additionally, even though the EDL results show that the improvements can be robust to the Gaussian model output assumption, it would be useful to find ways to avoid the Gaussian assumption altogether.

Appendix A
Sample correlation density and Wishart Distributions

The ideas in this paper are largely predicated upon our ability to compute expectations of the form,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:exp_{c}orr}\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\rho}}\left[f(\widehat{\rho})\right]=\int_{-1}^{1}f(r)dP(r),

(24)

for a univariate function
f
, where the expectation is taken with respect to the distribution of the sample correlation.
We also compute probabilities via integration for confidence interval computation as described in
Section

4.3.2
.
There are few scenarios in which this distribution is known in closed form, one of which is the bivariate Gaussian scenario used throughout this paper.
As such, we have two options for computing
Equation

24
: numerical integration of the product of the function of interest and the sample correlation density, or Monte Carlo sampling.

To use the former approach, we leverage the density as given in
[
17
]
,

f(r\mid\rho,N)=\frac{(1-\rho^{2})^{\nu/2}(1-r^{2})^{\frac{\nu-2}{2}}}{\mathcal{B}(1/2,\nu/2)}\;_{2}F_{1}(1/2,1/2;(\nu+1)/2;\rho r),

(25)

where
\mathcal{B}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the Beta function,
\;{}_{2}F_{1}(\cdot,\cdot;\cdot;\cdot)
denotes the Gauss hypergeometric function, and
\nu=N-2
.
When
f
is set to the discrepancy function with a fixed
\bm{\theta}
,
\delta(g(\bm{\theta};r),\rho)
, the expectation can be computed using numerical quadrature or Gauss-Legendre quadrature.
The latter happens to be an excellent option in this case since the integral is defined over
[-1,1]
and the quadrature can be written in a vectorized summation form for speed.

To use the latter approach, under the bivariate Gaussian assumption we can leverage the Wishart distribution as described by
Equation

11
or slightly more circuitously sample directly from a bivariate Gaussian distribution and compute the sample correlation.
There is a slight advantage to directly using the Wishart distribution, which is that one can sample a realization,
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}
, and compute the sample correlation using fewer steps,

\widehat{\rho}=\frac{\widehat{\Sigma}_{01}}{\sqrt{\widehat{\Sigma}_{00}\widehat{\Sigma}_{11}}}.

(26)

This shortcut relative to directly sampling from the bivariate Gaussian means that the operation can be easily vectorized, making large sample sizes easy to handle.

Of course,
Equation

24
is general and does not rely upon any Gaussian assumption.
Given data from some arbitrary distribution, it could be possible to use a subsampling procedure to estimate this expectation as we did in
Section

5.2
.
We emphasize that this expectation is on the sampling distribution of the sample correlation which adds a layer of complexity in computing such a value.

Appendix B
Exact MFMC solutions using Pool Adjacent Violators Algorithm (PAVA)

The analytical solutions to the MFMC estimator hyperparameter problem from
Section

2.1
are only applicable under certain model relationships
[
28
]
.
This can present a significant issue when using non-hierarchical model ensembles or noisy sample correlations since the conditions for these analytical solutions may be violated.
In these cases, users often eschew MFMC for other estimators or rely on gradient-based optimization algorithms to approximately solve the sample allocation problem, increasing the computational burden significantly.
While this computational burden may be small for single estimation tasks where the sample allocation problem only must be solved once, the cost can become prohibitive when evaluating the expected discrepancy metric since the sample allocation problem must be solved many times to numerically estimate the outer expectation.
Here, we introduce an isotonic optimization algorithm called Pool Adjacent Violators Algorithm (PAVA)
[
8
]
which provides exact solutions to the (relaxed) sample allocation problem with
\mathcal{O}(M-1)
complexity, alleviating the computational burden of using gradient-based optimization for the MFMC sample allocation problem.

Suppose we have many (
M
) computational models,
\mathcal{M}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
for
i=0,\dots,M-1
.
As before, each model
\mathcal{M}_{i}
is associated with a computational cost
c_{i}
, and we must satisfy the total compute budget constraint
\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}n_{i}c_{i}\leq C
.
As part of the MFMC sample allocation constraints, the sample sets must be strictly nested, enforcing the monotonicity constraint
n_{0}\leq n_{1}\leq\dots\leq n_{M-1}
.

The general MFMC estimator of the high-fidelity expectation is defined as:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:gen_{e}stimator}\widehat{y}(\bm{\xi}):=\widehat{y}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}\xi_{i}\left(\widehat{y}_{i+}-\widehat{y}_{i-}\right),

(27)

where
\widehat{y}_{0}=n_{0}^{-1}\sum_{j}^{n_{0}}\mathcal{M}_{0}(z^{(j)})
is the hifi MC estimator,
\widehat{y}_{i+}=n_{i-1}^{-1}\sum_{j}^{n_{i-1}}\mathcal{M}_{i}(z^{(j)})
is the lofi MC estimator using the nested
n_{i-1}
samples,
\widehat{y}_{i-}=n_{i}^{-1}\sum_{j}^{n_{i}}\mathcal{M}_{i}(z^{(j)})
is the lofi MC estimator using the augmented set of
n_{i}
samples, and
\bm{\xi}
are the control variate weights.

Defining the model-output covariance matrix as
\bm{\Sigma}
and its associated standard deviations as
\sigma_{i}
for
i=0,\ldots,M-1
and pairwise correlations as
\rho_{ij}
for
i\neq j
, the variance of the MFMC estimator is given by:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:gen_{e}st_{v}ariance}v(\bm{\beta};\bm{\Sigma})=\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}}{n_{0}}+\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}\left(\frac{1}{n_{i-1}}-\frac{1}{n_{i}}\right)(\xi_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}-2\xi_{i}\rho_{0,i}\sigma_{0}\sigma_{i}),

(28)

where
\sigma_{i}^{2}
is the variance of model
\mathcal{M}_{i}
,
\rho_{0,i}
is the correlation coefficient between the hifi model and the
i
-th lofi model, and
\bm{\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}n_{0}&\dots&n_{M-1}&\xi_{1}&\dots&\xi_{M-1}\end{pmatrix}^{T}
.
Setting the partial derivatives with respect to
\xi_{i}
to zero yields the optimal weights
\xi_{i}^{*}=\rho_{0,i}\sigma_{0}\sigma_{i}^{-1}
.
Substituting
\xi_{i}^{*}
back into
Equation

28
and regrouping the terms by
1/n_{i}
, the optimal variance simplifies to:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:s_{v}ariance}v(\bm{\beta}^{*};\Sigma)=\sigma_{0}^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\frac{S_{i}}{n_{i}},

(29)

where the variance reduction contributions
S_{i}
are defined as:

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle=1-\rho_{0,1}^{2}

\displaystyle S_{i}

\displaystyle=\rho_{0,i}^{2}-\rho_{0,i+1}^{2}\quad\text{for }0<i<M-1

(30)

\displaystyle S_{M-1}

\displaystyle=\rho_{0,M-1}^{2}.

The sample allocation problem is then defined as:

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{n}}{\min}

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\frac{S_{i}}{n_{i}}

(31)

subject to

\displaystyle\quad n_{i}-n_{i+1}\leq 0\quad\forall i\in\{0,\dots,M-1\}

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}c_{i}n_{i}=C.

Following
[
28
]
and applying the notation from
Equation

30
, if the constraints

\displaystyle\frac{c_{i-1}}{c_{i}}>\frac{S_{i-1}}{S_{i}}

(32)

are met, then the closed-form global minimum for the sample allocation is defined by a ratio vector
\mathbf{r}^{*}
, where its components for
i=0,\dots,M-1
are:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:optimal_{r}atios}r_{i}^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}S_{i}}{c_{i}S_{0}}}.

(33)

(Note that by definition,
r_{0}^{*}=1
).
The optimal number of high-fidelity model evaluations (
n_{0}^{*}
) is found by distributing the total computational budget
C
according to these ratios:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:optimal_{n}0}n_{0}^{*}=\frac{C}{\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}c_{j}r_{j}^{*}}.

(34)

Finally, the optimal sample allocations for the lower-fidelity models (
i=1,\dots,M-1
) are determined by scaling the high-fidelity sample size:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:optimal_{n}i}n_{i}^{*}=n_{0}^{*}r_{i}^{*}.

(35)

However, when the inequalities in
Equation

32
are not satisfied, no such closed-form solution is available.

B.1
Isotonic Optimization via PAVA

Due to the strict ordering constraints of the MFMC sample allocation,
n_{0}\leq n_{1}\leq\ldots\leq n_{M-1}
, Optimization
31
is a classical isotonic optimization problem.
To solve this problem when the original MFMC constraints from
Equation

32
are not satisifed, we utilize the Pool Adjacent Violators Algorithm (PAVA), which is a gold-standard algorithm for isotonic optimization that mathematically satisfies the KKT conditions exactly for convex functions.
PAVA partitions the models into contiguous blocks to enforce the monotonicity constraints.
By tracking the variance-to-cost ratio
S_{i}/c_{i}
, the unconstrained allocation dictates
n_{i}\propto\sqrt{S_{i}/c_{i}}
.
Whenever a monotonicity violation occurs (i.e.,
R_{i-1}>R_{i}
), PAVA resolves it by merging the adjacent models into a single pooled block, performing a weighted average of the variance reduction contribution and costs within that block, and continuing to do so until the monotonicity constraints are met.
For the bifidelity case where
M=2
, this procedure amounts to setting
n_{0}=n_{1}=\frac{C}{c_{0}+c_{1}}
.

The resulting algorithm is summarized via the following steps:

•

Initialization:
For each model
i=0,\dots,M-1
, compute the variance-to-cost ratio
R_{i}=S_{i}/c_{i}
. If not already done so, order the models according to their correlation to the hifi model. Initially, treat each model as its own independent block.

•

Violation Detection:
Evaluate the sequence of blocks for monotonicity violations in
R
. Because the unconstrained optimal sample allocation dictates
n_{i}\propto\sqrt{R_{i}}
, a nested sampling violation occurs if a higher-fidelity block requires more samples than its adjacent lower-fidelity block, i.e.,
R_{i-1}>R_{i}
.

•

Pooling:
If a violation is detected, merge the adjacent violating blocks into a single pooled block. The new block is assigned an aggregated variance reduction
S_{\text{pool}}=\sum S_{i}
and an aggregated cost
c_{\text{pool}}=\sum c_{i}
from its constituent models, yielding an updated pooled ratio
R_{\text{pool}}=S_{\text{pool}}/c_{\text{pool}}
.

•

Iteration:
Continue evaluating and merging adjacent blocks using a stack mechanism until the sequence of ratios across all remaining blocks is strictly monotonically non-decreasing.

•

Sample Allocation:
For each finalized block, determine the pooled sample size
n_{\text{block}}\propto\sqrt{R_{\text{block}}}
. Scale these values uniformly by a single Lagrange multiplier to exactly satisfy the total computational budget
C
, and assign
n_{i}=n_{\text{block}}
to all individual models contained within that block.

Since PAVA operates in linear time over a stack mechanism, it operates in strictly linear time
\mathcal{O}(M)
.
We refer the reader to
[
8
,
19
]
for further details.
In our tests, PAVA can provide orders-of-magnitude speed-ups over gradient-based optimization protocols for modest (
2-30
) model ensemble sizes and benefits from being free from any optimization parameters.

34
]
to density-based metrics such as the
\delta
-importance measure
[
6
]
.
In this work, we restrict ourselves to the bi-fidelity case using MFMC and adopt a variance-based approach based on Shapley values
[
26
]
that is amenable to dependent inputs — since we assume the sample covariance matrix follows a Wishart distribution,
(N-1)\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}\sim\mathcal{W}(\bm{\Sigma},N-1)
, it implies a joint distribution over the sample correlation
\widehat{\rho}
and sample standard deviations
\widehat{\sigma}_{0},\widehat{\sigma}_{1}
such that these inputs are not independent.
Many methods, such as Sobol’ indices, lose their interpretability when the independent inputs assumption is violated.
Many extensions, such as generalized Sobol’ indices, aim to disentangle the issue of mutual independence of the model inputs, each with their own interpretations and drawbacks.
The most popular method introduces
Shapley values
(or
Shapley effects
) as a sensitivity measure
[
27
,
35
,
26
]
.
As a quantity used in game theory, Shapley values provide a uniquely fair way to distribute the total “payoff” (in our case this is the variance of the MFMC estimator variance,
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}[\delta(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}},\bm{\Sigma})]
) among the “players” (in our case this is the input parameters,
\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{D}}=\{\widehat{\rho},\widehat{\sigma}_{0},\widehat{\sigma}_{1}\}
). Interestingly, the seminal work
[
33
]
proves that it is the only such measure that satisfies the four axioms of a fair game, namely efficiency, symmetry, dummy/null, and additivity.

The Shapley value
\phi_{d}
for an input
x_{d}
is its average marginal contribution to the variance, computed over all possible subsets
S
of the other inputs. Let
\mathcal{D}
be the index set of the inputs, with cardinality
D=|\mathcal{D}|
.
The general formula is:

\displaystyle\phi_{d}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{V}\text{ar}[\delta(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}},\bm{\Sigma})]}\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal{D}\setminus\{d\}}\frac{|S|!(D-|S|-1)!}{D!}\times\left(\mathbb{V}\text{ar}[\mathbb{E}[\delta(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}},\bm{\Sigma})|x_{S\cup\{d\}}]]-\mathbb{V}\text{ar}[\mathbb{E}[\delta(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}},\bm{\Sigma})|x_{S}]]\right)

In this formulation,
S
is a subset of inputs not containing
d
, and
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}[\mathbb{E}[\delta(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}},\bm{\Sigma})|x_{S}]]
represents the variance of the MFMC discrepancy that is explained by the subset of inputs
x_{S}
. This formula calculates the “fair” contribution by averaging the marginal increase in explained variance that
x_{d}
provides, weighted across all possible coalition sizes.
Computationally,
\phi_{d}
is estimated using MC or kernel methods — we do so using the nearest neighbor search method proposed in
[
7
,
3
]
in the
\mathtt{sensitivity}
package in
\mathtt{R}
using
10^{4}
MC samples at each tested oracle
\bm{\Sigma}
.

Appendix E
Technical details for solving DDMM

E.1
Computing the expected discrepancy array

As stated in
Section

4.3.3
, we trade the theoretical complexity of solving the minimax optimization of DDMM for computational complexity such that the solution can be accurately approximated using vectorized array operations.
Implementing this idea requires computing expected discrepancy values over a dense grid of
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
(the adjustment parameter space) and
\rho\in(0,1)
(the true correlation space) to ensure small approximation error.
While such a dense gridding can be achieved in a brute-force embarrassingly parallel approach, we concluded this approach would not realistically be feasible for implemention on a personal computer.
As such, we instead leverage smoothness assumptions about the expected discrepancy
surface
over
(\bm{\theta},\rho)
.

For each
(\bm{\theta},\rho)\in\Theta\times(0,1)
, we define the expected discrepancy surface as follows,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:M_{t}heta_{r}ho}M(\bm{\theta},\rho):=\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\rho}}\left[\delta\left(g(\bm{\theta};\widehat{\rho}),\rho\right)\right]=\int_{-1}^{1}\delta\left(g(\bm{\theta};r),\rho\right)f(r\mid\rho,N)dr,

(36)

where
f(r\mid\rho,N)
is the sample correlation density under the bivariate Gaussian assumption as defined in
Appendix

A
when the true correlation is
\rho
and the sample correlation is composed of
N
pilot samples.
As remarked in
Appendix

A
,
f(r\mid\rho,N)
changes smoothly as a function of
\rho
and since
g(\bm{\theta};r)
is a sigmoid function, it changes smoothly as a function of
\bm{\theta}
.
Thus, it is reasonable to assume the surface
M(\bm{\theta},\rho)
is smooth over its inputs.

Since
M
is smooth and defined over a compact domain, it is square integrable (
M\in L^{2}
).
Thus, the operator defined by
M
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator and implies the kernel has an infinite sum expansion,

M(\bm{\theta},\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{i}u_{i}(\bm{\theta})v_{i}(\rho),

(37)

where
\sigma_{i}\geq 0
and
\sigma_{i}\to 0
as
i\to\infty
, where the
\sigma_{i}
values rapidly decay
[
31
]
.
For each
i
,
u_{i}(\bm{\theta})
is also a bivariate function and if we use a similar infinite expansion,

u_{i}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{ij}b_{ij}(\theta_{0})c_{ij}(\theta_{1}),

(38)

hence, we get the following tensor decomposition,

M(\bm{\theta},\rho)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}v_{k}(\rho)b_{k}(\theta_{0})c_{k}(\theta_{1}),

(39)

where we reindex
(i,j)
to
k
and absorb the
\sigma_{i}
and
\lambda_{ij}
values into the basis functions.
Since the spectrum rapidly decays, we rely upon the following approximation,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:finite_{a}pprox}M(\bm{\theta},\rho)\approx\sum_{k=1}^{R}v_{k}(\rho)b_{k}(\theta_{0})c_{k}(\theta_{1}),

(40)

where
R<\infty
.

Motivated by
Equation

40
,
Algorithm

2
computes
M
at a small collection of
(\bm{\theta},\rho)
locations within its compact domain to form an array.
We then perform a canonical polyadic (CP) tensor decomposition using the tensorly package
[
21
]
, which provides us with a finite collection of evaluations of the orthonormal functions
v_{k}
,
b_{k}
, and
c_{k}
.
For each function, we use a cubic spline to approximate the function values at the
(\bm{\theta},\rho)
values not present in the grid.
Finally, using the fitted splines, any desired grid resolution of the function values can easily be obtained and recomposed by recomposing the tensor.

For the minimax optimizations in this paper, we achieved negligible minimax solution error using the following fine-dimension settings;
d^{\prime}_{\theta_{0}}=200
,
d^{\prime}_{\theta_{1}}=200
, and
d^{\prime}_{\rho}=1000
.
To provide a guideline around the choice of
R
in
Equation

40
and demonstrate the accuracy of this computational approach, we computed the output of
Algorithm

2
using a brute force approach (i.e., we parallelized computing each
M_{ijk}
component) and the approach in
Algorithm

2
using different
R
values.
Denote the brute-force output by
\bm{M}_{true}
and the output of
Algorithm

\bm{M}(R)
.
We consider relative error as a function of
R

e(R):=\frac{\lVert\bm{M}(R)-\bm{M}_{true}\rVert_{F}}{\lVert\bm{M}_{true}\rVert_{F}},

(41)

and show the result in the left panel of

The center and right panels of

Overall, both closely match, although we observe a slight deviation in the center panel on the boundary of the

E.2
Correlation confidence intervals and computing their surrogates

Once the expected discrepancy array detailed in
Section

E.1
is computed, the necessary root-finding algorithms to find the confidence interval to solve the DDMM optimization are the computational bottleneck.
This computational burden becomes significant when one wants to solve the DDMM optimization many times (either to look at the adjustment under a collection of
\alpha
settings or to implement our procedure to choose an optimal
\alpha
as described in
Section

E.3
).
As shown in
Section

4.3.2
, we care about equations of the form,

p(\alpha,r;\rho):=\frac{\alpha}{2}-\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{\rho}<r\mid\rho,N\right)=\frac{\alpha}{2}-\int_{-1}^{r}f(t\mid\rho,N)dt,

(42)

where for a particular
(\alpha,r)
, the root-finding algorithm finds
\rho^{\prime}
such that
p(\alpha,r;\rho^{\prime})=0
.
The function
p
is clearly linear in
\alpha
and smooth in
r
since the sample correlation density is smooth.
As such, similar to the intuition in
Section

E.1
, the confidence interval endpoints for a particular desired miscoverage level,
\alpha
, and observed sample correlation,
r
should be similar to the endpoints at a nearby setting, e.g.,
(\alpha+\epsilon,r+\epsilon)
, where
\epsilon
is small.
Using this smoothness intuition, we reduce the computational bottleneck by training confidence interval endpoint surrogate models over the space of possible miscoverage levels and sample correlations (i.e.,
(0,1)\times(-1,1)
).

To fit these surrogates, we fix a pilot sample size, and define a grid over the miscoverage and sample correlation space.
For each grid point, we solve the lower and upper endpoint root-finding problems.
For each collection of endpoint points (lower and upper), we fit a bivariate spline to generate the endpoint surrogates.
Once the surrogates are fit, we have a computationally efficient way to compute a confidence interval for arbitrary
(\alpha,r)
.
In speed tests performed on a personal computer, the spline computes intervals in the order of microseconds while the root-finding algorithm in the order of milliseconds (a speedup of three orders of magnitude)
This efficiency boost facilitates the optimal-
\alpha
algorithm in
Section

E.3
.
The full procedure is written in
Algorithm

3
.

E.3
Optimizing DDMM confidence level

This section provides the details of choosing
\alpha
as described in
Section

4.3.2
.
For a chosen
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there is a resulting expected discrepancy difference (EDD, see
Equation

23
) with respect to the unadjusted sample correlation.
We ultimately wish to make this difference as negative as possible over
\rho\in(0,1)
and since
\alpha
affects the balance between robustness (conservatism) and optimality, we claim that there are
\alpha
settings achieving this goal.
The following procedure is meant to be performed
before
observing the pilot samples.

Define the following surface over the space of possible miscoverage settings and true correlations,
(\alpha,\rho)\in(\alpha_{l},\alpha_{u})\times(\rho_{l},\rho_{u})\subset(0,1)^{2}
,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:alpha_{r}ho_{s}urface}f(\alpha,\rho):=\mathbb{E}_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\left[\delta(h_{\alpha}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu),\rho)-\delta(\widehat{\rho},\rho)\right],

(43)

where
h_{\alpha}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)
denotes the DDMM estimator defined by
\alpha
and the
N
samples,
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, while
\widehat{\rho}
denotes the usual sample correlation computed via the
N
samples.
Since we target an estimator
h
that dominates the sample correlation, we ideally want to pick
\alpha
such that
f(\alpha,\rho)<0
for all
\rho\in(\rho_{l},\rho_{u})
.
Then, of all the
\alpha
such that this condition holds, we want to pick the
best
ones, leading to the following minimax quantity,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:alpha_{s}tar}\alpha^{*}:=\underset{\alpha\in(\alpha_{l},\alpha_{u})}{\text{argmin}}\underset{\rho\in(\rho_{l},\rho_{u})}{\max}f(\alpha,\rho),

(44)

i.e., the
\alpha
minimizing the worst-case expected discrepancy difference.
For each
(\alpha,\rho)
, computing
Equation

43
is nontrivial since computing
h_{\alpha}(\widehat{{\bm{y}}})
involves solving a minimax problem.
However, under the bivariate Gaussian assumption and having computed the expected discrepancy array detailed in
Section

E.1
along with the confidence interval surrogates detailed in
Section

E.2
, we can easily sample realizations of the random process defined at any
(\alpha,\rho)
.
As such, we can use any regression (ideally nonparametric so we avoid structural assumptions) to estimate the conditional mean surface, i.e.,
f(\alpha,\rho)
.
By definition, regression minimizes squared-error loss and provides the optimal estimate of this conditional expectation surface.
Once this surface is estimated, we can numerically solve the minimax problem in
Equation

44
to obtain the
\alpha
produces the smallest worst-case expected discrepancy difference.
Clearly, any regression approach to estimate
f(\alpha,\rho)
will include sampling variability.
Although we do not explicitly characterize this variability for our resulting estimate, we do consider the bias-variance tradeoff resulting from our data-generating process, giving us training data that optimize expected squared error.

E.3.1
Training data generation, Gaussian Process (GP) regression, and optimizing miscoverage level

For a fixed
(\alpha,\rho)
, define the following random variable,

\Delta(\alpha,\rho):=\delta(h_{\alpha}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu),\rho)-\delta(\widehat{\rho},\rho),

(45)

where the randomness arises from the
N
samples,
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\bm{y}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
.
Since
\Delta(\alpha,\rho)
has a large variance relative to its mean for each
(\alpha,\rho)
, we increase the signal to noise ratio by constructing the training data using
D\in\mathbb{N}
design points, and
R\in\mathbb{N}
repetition per design point.
As such, for fixed
(D,R)
, we obtain data of the form,

\mathcal{D}:=\left\{(\alpha_{i},\rho_{i}),\left(\widehat{\mu}_{i},\widehat{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{D},

(46)

where

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:mean_{v}ar}\widehat{\mu}_{i}=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{j=1}^{R}\Delta_{ij},\quad\widehat{\sigma}_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{R(R-1)}\sum_{j=1}^{R}\left(\Delta_{ij}-\widehat{\mu}_{i}\right)^{2},\quad\Delta_{ij}=\Delta(\alpha_{i},\rho_{i}).

(47)

In
Section

E.3.2
we discuss our approach to allocating training data generating time between design points and repetitions.
Once the number of design points has been chosen, we generate them using Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) to ensure more uniform coverage of the desired region compared to sampling uniformly at random.

Given our smoothness assumption on
f(\alpha,\rho)
and our desire to optimize over the output of the regression, a Gaussian Process regressor is a reasonable model choice
[
16
,
30
]
.
We use a GP with a nugget term, i.e., we model a deterministic smooth surface that we observe with additive Gaussian noise.
This assumption on the noise is reasonable by the Central Limit Theorem since we use
R
repetitions to compute a mean for each output value and thus its sampling distribution is asymptotically Gaussian.
As discussed in Chapter 10 of
[
16
]
, we use the computed
\widehat{\sigma}_{i}^{2}
values to construct a multivariate nugget matrix essentially allowing for heteroskedatic GP regression, allowing the GP regressor to smooth the data and better respect the variance structure across the input space.
Note, the definition includes a
R(R-1)
term in the denominator since the standard error of the empirical mean contracts at rate
R^{-1/2}
, i.e., as we increase the number of repetitions, we further sharpen the signal to noise ratio since the sampling variance asymptotically vanishes.

When exploring the GP fitting process, we noticed that the argmax in the
\rho
space is consistently found in the region where
\rho\geq 0.5
.
As such, we perform LHS on the top half of the desired region as shown in
Figure

3(a)
, which further shows the mean surface of the fitted GP.

Figure

3(b)
then shows the pointwise mean surface over
\rho
for each
\alpha
which is then used to find
\alpha^{*}
(as indicated by the dashed line).
Note that for all
\alpha
in the computed range, we estimate that DDMM has a worst-case EDD less than zero, i.e., we can choose any
\alpha
and still out-perform the sample covariance.
But clearly there is a unique
\alpha
optimizing the performance.

E.3.2
Choosing number of design points and repetitions

To constrain the combinations of design points and repetitions to consider, we anchored our set of choices to those combinations that could be generated in

seconds on a personal computer.
All computational experiments were performed on a personal computer running Windows 11, equipped with an Intel Core i5-1245U processor (12 cores, 1.6 GHz) and 316 GB of RAM.
Some experimentation showed that each sample (where one sample is the combined generation of the sample correlation, its confidence interval, and the DDMM solution) requires approximately
r=0.035

Thus, we wish to consider all
(D,R)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:pareto_{f}ront}DRr=t.

(48)

Taking the logarithm of both sides yields a linear relationship shown in

To investigate different combinations along this line, we sampled a large dataset of design points (

These boundary values are indicated by the gray lines on

We generate a grid of
(D,R)

We refer to the index enumerating these settings along this Pareto front as the index on the Pareto front, as used in Figures (

Using all design points and repetitions, we estimate the conditional expectation surface in
Equation

\alpha

We refer to this optimal settings at

Although all

To locate the optimal setting, we propose the following procedure minimizing the

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:risk_{a}lpha}\mathcal{R}(K):=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{\alpha}_{K}-\alpha_{orcl}\right)^{2}\right]=\text{bias}\left(\widehat{\alpha}_{K}\right)^{2}+\text{Var}\left(\widehat{\alpha}_{K}\right),

(49)

where

using a GP mean surface fitted on

According to functional linear approximations in empirical process theory

on the estimated conditional expectation surface, the bias and variance can be approximated as follows,

\text{bias}\left(\widehat{\alpha}_{K}\right)\approx\frac{b_{1}}{R},\quad\text{Var}\left(\widehat{\alpha}_{K}\right)\approx\frac{c_{1}}{D}+\frac{c_{2}}{DR}.

(50)

\mathcal{R}(K)\approx\mathcal{R}_{appr}(K;b_{1},c_{1},c_{2}):=\left(\frac{b_{1}}{R}\right)^{2}+\frac{c_{1}}{D}+\frac{c_{2}}{DR},

(51)

and thus the risk curve along the Pareto front can be approximated by fitting the parameters

\left\{\left(K_{i},r_{i}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{I},\quad r_{i}=\frac{1}{100}\sum_{j=1}^{100}(\widehat{\alpha}_{K_{i}}-\alpha_{orcl})^{2}.

(52)

The pairs

\displaystyle\underset{b_{1},c_{1},c_{2}}{\min}

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{i=1}^{100}\left[\widehat{\sigma}_{i}^{-1}\left(r_{i}-\mathcal{R}_{appr}(K_{i};b_{1},c_{1},c_{2})\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\quad c_{1}\geq 0,\;c_{2}\geq 0,

The fitted function is shown by the blue solid curve in

We observe that risk again decreases for

Said differently, as we lose repetitions per design point, the regressor model class incurs more systematic bias.
This intuition can be seen by looking at the fitted bias and variance curves in
