Intravascular ultrasound-guided percutaneous coronary intervention for syphilitic coronary ostial lesions: a single-center case series
Authors: Yize Qi, Zhiyuan Wang, Junzhi Liu, Xiangdong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aims to preliminarily explore the feasibility and safety of intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)-guided percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for syphilitic coronary artery ostial lesions (SCAOL), providing hypothesis-generating insights for optimal stenting strategies in this distinct pathological entity.BackgroundCardiovascular syphilis, a severe manifestation of tertiary syphilis, frequently leads to coronary ostial stenosis. While coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is the traditional revascularization modality, PCI has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative. Given the “borderless” inflammatory nature of syphilitic aortitis, the efficacy of the conventional “flush-alignment” stenting strategy lacks specific evidence.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed seven consecutive patients with confirmed SCAOL who underwent PCI at our center between January 2019 and February 2026 (follow-up through March 2026). All cases were diagnosed via syphilis serology, and IVUS was utilized for pre-procedural assessment and post-stenting optimization. The IVUS characteristics of SCAOL and PCI-related outcomes were analyzed to explore the potential impact of stent positioning strategies. The study received institutional ethical approval and written informed consent from all patients.ResultsThe cohort included four males and three females (mean age: 54.3 ± 3.4 years). Pre-procedural IVUS consistently revealed concentric fibrous hyperplasia and significant negative remodeling, lacking typical atherosclerotic plaque features. Second-generation drug-eluting stents (DES) were successfully implanted in all seven patients. Six patients, whose stents protruded variably into the aortic sinus (ranging from approximately 2–4 mm in most cases), remained free of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) over a median follow-up of 4 years (IQR: 3–6 years). Conversely, one patient (Case 6), who received a “flush-alignment” LM stent (protrusion <1 mm) and lacked standardized anti-syphilitic therapy, developed NSTEMI due to severe in-stent restenosis (ISR) 8 months post-procedure. A bailout PCI utilizing a protrusion of approximately 4–5 mm was performed, and the stent remained patent at the 13-month follow-up.ConclusionsIVUS-guided PCI was technically feasible in this small retrospective series of patients with suspected syphilitic coronary ostial lesions. Moderate aortic protrusion may help ensure ostial coverage, but the optimal protrusion length and its effect on restenosis require confirmation in larger multicenter studies. Furthermore, mechanical revascularization must be coupled with standardized systemic anti-syphilitic therapy and careful control of residual inflammatory activity to optimize long-term prognosis.

Introduction

Syphilis is a systemic infection caused by Treponema pallidum. Despite the long-term application of penicillin effectively curbing its epidemic, a striking global resurgence has been observed in recent years. Cardiovascular syphilis, a fatal manifestation of tertiary syphilis, frequently involves the coronary ostia, leading to syphilitic coronary artery ostial lesions (SCAOL)— a rare but life-threatening cardiovascular manifestation of late tertiary syphilis. Although the abbreviation SCAOL is utilized herein for concise reporting of this specific cohort, it refers explicitly to isolated or bilateral ostial stenosis driven by syphilitic patch-like aortic endarteritis, distinct from conventional atherosclerotic ostial disease. These lesions often affect relatively young populations and are prone to inducing acute myocardial infarction or sudden cardiac death, posing a substantial clinical hazard (1).

The pathological characteristics of SCAOL are distinctly different from typical coronary atherosclerosis. The disease originates from obliterative endarteritis of the vasa vasorum in the aortic adventitia, leading to significant fibrosis and elastic recoil of the involved vessel wall. These lesions often extend diffusely without clear boundaries (‘borderless’) (1, 2). Historically, coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) was the primary revascularization modality. However, with the evolution of interventional techniques, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), leveraging its minimally invasive advantages, has emerged as a crucial alternative for patients who are elderly or surgically ineligible (3, 4).

Despite significant advancements, a critical clinical gap remains: the optimal stent positioning strategy for SCAOL has not yet been established. Unlike focal atherosclerotic plaques, the ‘borderless’ nature of syphilitic inflammation raises a clinical concern: is the traditional ‘flush-alignment’ technique sufficient to cover the inflamed ostial margins, or does it increase the risk of early postoperative restenosis?.

This study retrospectively analyzed seven SCAOL patients who underwent IVUS-guided PCI. By evaluating clinical outcomes and long-term imaging follow-up, particularly the clinical course of Case 6, we sought to describe procedural experience and generate hypotheses regarding stent positioning strategies in this rare disease entity.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective, single-center case series included seven consecutive patients diagnosed with syphilitic coronary artery ostial lesions (SCAOL) who underwent PCI at the Department of Cardiology, China-Japan Union Hospital of Jilin University, between January 2019 and February 2026 (follow-up cutoff: March 2026). All cases were discussed collectively by the Heart Team. To ensure diagnostic and procedural rigor, a standardized matrix of core evaluation metrics was applied throughout the study period (Table 1), with granular interventional and procedural parameters meticulously tracked across all treated segments (detailed in Supplementary Table S1). Exclusion criteria consisted of concomitant severe aortic regurgitation requiring surgical valve replacement, acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) necessitating emergency surgery, or clinical suspicion of other vasculitides, such as Takayasu arteritis (2).

Diagnostic criteria for enrollment were defined as follows: (1) positive syphilis serology, confirmed by reactive results on both the Treponema pallidum particle agglutination (TPPA) assay and the rapid plasma reagin (RPR) test; (2) coronary angiography demonstrating >70% stenosis in at least one coronary ostium, with intervention extended to the contralateral ostium when bilateral involvement was angiographically confirmed; and (3) IVUS-confirmed non-atherosclerotic features isolated to the coronary ostium, consistent with syphilitic involvement.

Diagnostic definition of suspected syphilitic coronary ostial lesions

The diagnosis of suspected syphilitic coronary ostial lesions was established through integration of clinical history, serological testing, coronary angiography, and IVUS findings. All included patients demonstrated positive treponemal and non-treponemal serological tests together with isolated coronary ostial stenosis and IVUS characteristics suggestive of non-atherosclerotic fibroproliferative disease.

Whenever available, echocardiography and aortic imaging were reviewed to assess associated cardiovascular syphilis manifestations. Comprehensive CTA, MRI, or PET-CT was not routinely available in all patients because of the retrospective design. All suspected cases were multi-disciplinarily confirmed by both senior interventional cardiologists and infectious disease specialists. Alternative causes of ostial coronary stenosis, including Takayasu arteritis, Behçet disease, IgG4-related disease, radiation-associated vasculopathy, and congenital coronary ostial abnormalities, were excluded based on clinical history, laboratory findings, and imaging evaluation.

Given the retrospective nature of this study and the absence of histopathological confirmation, diagnostic certainty should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

IVUS analysis

All available IVUS studies were retrospectively reviewed. Quantitative measurements included minimum lumen area, lesion length, reference vessel dimensions, and minimum stent area after implantation whenever image quality permitted reliable assessment.

Measurements were performed by experienced interventional cardiologists using standard IVUS analysis software. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, certain parameters were unavailable in some cases.

Assessment of aortic protrusion length

Aortic protrusion length was retrospectively estimated using a combination of angiographic orthogonal projections, stent-enhancement imaging, IVUS pullback assessment, and procedural records.

The aorto-coronary junction served as the anatomical reference point for estimation of stent protrusion. Because precise determination of the true ostial plane remains technically challenging, particularly in retrospective analysis, protrusion measurements should be regarded as semi-quantitative estimates rather than exact anatomical measurements.

All measurements were reviewed by experienced operators familiar with the index procedures.

Interventional procedure

Procedural details, including vascular access, guide catheter selection, lesion preparation strategy, stent implantation technique, post-dilation parameters, and final angiographic outcomes, were retrospectively collected from procedural reports and imaging archives. All patients received standard dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT; aspirin 100 mg/d plus clopidogrel 75 mg/d or ticagrelor 90 mg twice daily) prior to the procedure. Access was obtained via the radial or femoral artery. For cases of critical ostial stenosis, operators specifically selected guide catheters with side holes or lower backup support to prevent transient myocardial ischemia or ventricular fibrillation induced by complete hemodynamic occlusion of the stenotic ostium.

Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS; Boston Scientific iLab or Volcano) was utilized for procedural guidance throughout. The standardized interventional strategy comprised the following phases: Pre-treatment: Robust pre-dilation of the ostial fibrous annulus was performed using cutting balloons and high-pressure non-compliant (NC) balloons.Stent Implantation: Second-generation drug-eluting stents (DES) were utilized in all cases. Stents were initially deployed flush with the coronary ostium under tangential angiographic projection, followed by IVUS-guided positioning optimization.Post-dilation and Optimization: High-pressure post-dilation was performed under IVUS guidance to ensure optimal stent expansion, complete coverage of the coronary ostium, and full strut apposition.

Results

The group included four males and three females. Remarkably, traditional cardiovascular risk factors were largely absent: only two patients had hypertension, and none had diabetes mellitus or pathological dyslipidemia.

Clinical comparison of stent positioning strategies

In our cohort, all initial procedures followed the conventional ostial stenting technique, aiming for a “flush-alignment” with the inferior margin of the coronary ostium under a tangential angiographic view. However, IVUS revealed subtle variations in the final stent position relative to the aortic wall. Six patients, whose stents were intentionally or incidentally protruded ≥1 mm into the aortic sinus, remained free of ischemic symptoms or major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) over a median follow-up of 4 years (IQR: 3–6 years). Follow-up angiography in these cases confirmed persistent stent patency with favorable endothelialization.

Mechanistic analysis of a clinical failure: case 6

Conversely, Case 6 followed a distinct clinical course that provided critical observations relevant to both procedural and systemic disease management. During the index procedure, the left main (LM) stent was deployed flush with the ostial margin, exhibiting only minimal protrusion (<1 mm) beyond the ostial edge (Figure 2), whereas the right coronary artery (RCA) stent was deployed with a secure 4.0-mm aortic protrusion. Notably, the patient was non-compliant with standardized anti-syphilitic therapy post-procedure.

Bailout strategy and long-term management for case 6

A comprehensive, bilateral bailout strategy was successfully executed. The primary therapeutic focus was directed at the LM ostium, where a new drug-eluting stent (DES) was deployed overlapping the original stent, with an intentional protrusion (4–5 mm) into the aortic sinus to ensure complete mechanical scaling across the affected aorto-coronary junction. Concurrently, the atherosclerotic RCA ISR was systematically treated with an additional overlapping DES as an adjunctive intervention (detailed in Supplementary Table S1).

Following revascularization, a multidisciplinary consultation initiated a standardized course of benzathine penicillin G combined with adjunctive low-dose colchicine to stabilize the localized inflammatory milieu. During the subsequent 13-month follow-up, the patient remained completely asymptomatic, and repeat angiography confirmed optimal stent patency in both vessels (Figure 3).

Discussion

This case series describes the procedural feasibility of IVUS-guided PCI in patients with suspected syphilitic coronary ostial lesions and generates hypotheses regarding lesion coverage strategies in this unique clinical setting. The heterogeneous duration of follow-up reflects the retrospective nature of the study. Nevertheless, complete follow-up was achieved in all patients, thereby reducing uncertainty regarding major clinical outcomes.

Diagnostic considerations

We acknowledge that definitive attribution of coronary ostial disease to syphilis remains challenging in the absence of histopathological confirmation. Therefore, the diagnosis in the present study was based on a combination of serological evidence, characteristic lesion morphology, IVUS findings, and exclusion of alternative etiologies. Future multicenter studies incorporating advanced multimodality imaging and pathological correlation may further improve diagnostic certainty.

Imaging guidance: the indispensability of IVUS

Coronary angiography has significant limitations in managing SCAOL due to vessel overlap, rapid contrast dilution at the aortic root, and negative remodeling of inflammatory lesions, which often lead to underestimation of stenosis or anatomical misjudgment.

IVUS provides real-time tomographic imaging that not only quantifies stenosis but, more importantly, identifies the axial extent of the lesion. In selected cases, IVUS suggested that angiographically flush stent deployment might not fully cover the thickened aorto-coronary transition zone. IVUS-guided post-dilation and repositioning ensure that the proximal stent edge truly bridges the active inflammatory zone while achieving an optimal Minimum Stent Area (MSA) (7). This aligns with expert consensus recommending that adequate MSA and complete lesion coverage are paramount for improving long-term outcomes in complex ostial lesions (8). Although OCT provides higher resolution for detecting syphilis-specific vasa vasorum depletion and elastic fiber fragmentation, its procedural complexity in ostial positioning—driven by the need for voluminous contrast displacement and susceptibility to aortic wall interference—limits its utility in this context (9). The addition of quantitative IVUS measurements may facilitate future comparisons with larger studies and improve characterization of this uncommon lesion subtype.

Mechanical versus biological contributors to failure

The clinical course of Case 6 warrants cautious interpretation. Although minimal stent protrusion (<1 mm) may have resulted in incomplete coverage of the aorto-coronary transition zone, the patient simultaneously exhibited the highest RPR titer within the cohort and did not receive standardized anti-syphilitic therapy following the index procedure.

Notably, the RCA stent, which had a secure 4.0-mm aortic protrusion, also developed severe ISR, manifesting as in-stent neoatherosclerosis rather than periaortic fibrous proliferation. This indicates that adequate mechanical protrusion alone does not guarantee protection against restenosis when systemic inflammatory and lipid-related processes remain uncontrolled, and reinforces that protrusion length addresses only the mechanical dimension of this multifactorial disease.

Accordingly, two non-mutually exclusive mechanisms may have contributed to the subsequent development of ISR: (1) incomplete lesion coverage at the ostial margin and (2) persistent inflammatory or infectious activity associated with inadequately controlled cardiovascular syphilis.

Because of the small sample size and observational design of this study, the relative contribution of these factors cannot be determined in this study. Therefore, Case 6 should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal relationship between stent protrusion length and restenosis. It should be regarded solely as an observational case illustrating the coexistence of procedural and systemic factors.

Pathological dimension: From vasa vasorum to “borderless” lesions

The essence of syphilitic aortitis is the involvement of the vasa vasorum in the aortic adventitia (1). Treponema pallidum invades the perivascular lymphatics, triggering lymphoplasmacytic infiltration and subsequent obliterative endarteritis. This process is most severe at the aortic root due to the high density of vasa vasorum. Destruction of medial elastic fibers and subsequent fibrosis (the “bark-like” appearance) lead to diminished mechanical support and compensatory intimal thickening (4).

Two key features of this inflammatory process are critical: first, it involves the entire vessel wall rather than being limited to the intima; second, it is “borderless” (1). In treating SCAOL, the angiographic ostial margin often does not represent the inflammatory terminus. Histologically, collagen disarray and endarteritis have been reported to frequently extend into the seemingly ‘normal’ aortic sinus wall, based on autopsy and surgical pathology series (1). Consequently, a conventional “flush-alignment” strategy may not fully account for the histological extent of inflammatory involvement described in prior pathological studies. Residual inflammatory tissue at the stent margin may theoretically contribute to neointimal proliferation and adverse vascular remodeling. However, the present study cannot determine whether this mechanism played a dominant role in the development of ISR observed in Case 6.

PCI versus CABG in syphilitic coronary ostial disease

Although PCI offers a less invasive revascularization strategy and may be particularly attractive for isolated ostial lesions, CABG remains an important therapeutic option in selected patients,with prior comparative data suggesting lower restenosis rates and better mid-term outcomes with surgical revascularization in SCAOL (10).

Surgical treatment may be preferred in the presence of significant aortic regurgitation, ascending aortic aneurysm, diffuse aortitis requiring root reconstruction, complex multivessel disease, or when concomitant valvular surgery is anticipated.

Conversely, PCI may represent a reasonable alternative in patients with isolated ostial disease, elevated surgical risk, or anatomy suitable for percutaneous treatment.

Because excessive stent protrusion could theoretically complicate future coronary re-engagement or surgical manipulation of the aortic root — particularly in patients who may subsequently require aortic valve surgery or root reconstruction — individualized procedural planning and consideration of stent protrusion length are recommended in clinical decision-making (11). In such cases, early multidisciplinary discussion between interventional cardiologists and cardiac surgeons is strongly recommended before committing to a percutaneous strategy.

Systemic management: the bedrock of success

The restenosis observed in Case 6 involved both procedural and systemic factors and cannot be attributed to a single mechanism. The persistently elevated RPR titer (1:128), together with the absence of standardized anti-syphilitic treatment, was present in this case; however, its direct contribution to ISR cannot be determined (12). The severe restenosis in Case 6 may suggest that incomplete pathological coverage at the stent margin, combined with uncontrolled inflammation, deteriorates the mechanical environment and accelerates ISNA, which is consistent with observations reported in previous IVUS studies describing residual edge-related restenosis patterns, where residual lesions at the stent margins led to procedural failure (13).

Hemodynamic dimension: mechanical rationale for protrusion vs. Flush-Alignment

The coronary ostium resides in a complex hemodynamic environment characterized by high shear stress and flow instability. Computational fluid dynamics simulations in ostial lesions suggest that conventional flush-alignment stenting may induce localized flow disturbances at the aortic root. In the context of SCAOL, a modest protrusion (∼2–4 mm) has been hypothesized to potentially influence local flow patterns at the coronary ostium by exposing the stent inlet to more stable aortic flow, thereby minimizing localized low wall shear stress that drives neointimal hyperplasia. In this study, six patients with favorable clinical outcomes had stents protruding 2–4 mm into the aortic sinus. This observation may be useful for hypothesis generation but should not be interpreted as evidence supporting a specific protrusion target. However, as computational modeling was not performed in our specific cohort, this mechanical rationale remains hypothesis-generating. Conversely, excessive protrusion (>5 mm) must be strictly avoided, as it has been associated with aortic valvular injury, future thrombotic complications, and potential impedance to future surgical or percutaneous aortic root intervention (17, 18).

Conclusion

This case series suggests that IVUS-guided PCI is technically feasible in selected patients with suspected syphilitic coronary ostial lesions. Moderate aortic protrusion was observed in this case series and may help ensure ostial coverage in the setting of inflammatory ostial disease, although its clinical significance for restenosis prevention remains uncertain. However, the present study cannot determine the optimal protrusion length or establish its impact on restenosis risk. These observations should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger multicenter studies.

Mechanical intervention remains only one component of comprehensive management; clinicians must ensure that mechanical revascularization is synergistically coupled with standardized systemic anti-syphilitic therapy. Future large-scale, multicenter prospective studies and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations are warranted to further refine the optimal aortic protrusion depth and evaluate long-term safety and efficacy in this unique patient population.
