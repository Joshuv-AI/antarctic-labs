It is well known that the poorer classes in our large cities at
the best of times find it difficult to balance income and
expenditure and to obtain an adequate diet. This has been
shown by the series of dietary studies carried out in Glasgow
since 1911. It is obvious that the industrial depression from
which the country has been, and is, suffering must have made it
increasingly difficult for these people to live and to procure
a sufficient supply of food.
In a paper published in the Lancet of 2nd July, 1921, an
account was given of an investigation of the diet and the
condition of nutrition of the children in eleven typical labouring-
class families in Glasgow in April, 1921, with the object of

ascertaining the effect of the trade depression which was
then making itself manifest. A strike of coal miners was in
progress, and the fathers were either on short time or totally
unemployed.
The conclusion then arrived at was that these families were
unable to procure an adequate supply of food to afford the
necessary energy for even an entirely sedentary life or for the
proper growth and development of the children. The economic
difficulties had then not lasted for many months, and the
restricted diet had hardly had time to tell very markedly upon
the nutrition of the children.
Since then trade depression has become increasingly pro-
nounced, and unemployment has extended on all sides.
Accordingly, it was thought that it might be of interest to
investigate the effect of this prolonged period of unemployment,
and a study was therefore made of twelve typical families in
June and the beginning of July, 1922. These families were not
chosen as being specially necessitous, but were selected as
representative of the type constantly met with in connection
with Child Welfare work in the poorer districts of the city.
The study included 18 men, 14 women, 37 children over 5 years,
and 25 under 5 years?in all, 94 individuals. Eight of the
fathers had been unemployed for a considerable time, and of the
remaining four, two were dockers with a constantly varying
income, and two were working on short time. Fortunately, it
was possible to include in the present study three of the families
(Nos. 2, 3, 4) from those which were investigated in April, 1921
?No. 2 on two separate occasions.

Before analysing the results, some indication may be given of
the actual financial position of the families. With the exception
of families Nos. 2 and 3 (docker families), and 7 and 11 (on
short time), all have been unemployed for several months. At
first they had received an unemployment weekly allowance?
a sum varying according to size of family?from the Labour
Bureau, augmented by a fortnightly dole from the Parish. The
result of this, in some cases, was an ability to meet expenses for
the first week by using up the fortnightly Parish allowance,
and in the second week, when money was received from the
Bureau only, a contracting of debts. A change was made in the
administration of the Unemployment Insurance at the beginning
of April. By that time most of the men had exhausted their
unemployment payments, with the result that they were only
eligible for the five weeks' system. This meant that for five
weeks they obtained their money in the usual way from the
usual sources. Then for the next five weeks no money was
received from the Unemployment Exchange, the Parish paying
the full amount. Between the last Bureau payment and the
first Parish augmented payment there was, in many cases, a gap
of ten days when the people had no income at all. Food was
obtained by borrowing money from neighbours, and in some
cases credit was given by shopkeepers. Thus, the people were
in a continuous state of debt, and a large percentage of their
income when obtained had to go in liquidating debts contracted
the previous week. For this reason, in Table I the figures
relating to income give merely a very general idea. When
stated as " Income per man per week," one has to bear in mind
that on the day the money was paid probably several shillings
had at once to be expended in the payment of loans, and
accordingly the actual amount available per man for the
ensuing week may be less than that stated. The figures giving
the percentage of income spent on food are therefore also
misleading. For example, in No. 4, 71*6 per cent of the total
income is noted as spent on food. In reality, on the day the
money (?2, 2s. 6d.) was received, ?1, Is. 7d. had to be paid

as rent, thus leaving a little over 20s. for food, coal, gas, and
other expenses. Some of the food bought was obtained by
borrowing money long before the week was over, the repayment
being made the first charge on next week's allowance. This
condition is typical; pressing debts had to be met, coal and gas
and other necessities had to be obtained, and then by hook or
by crook a certain amount had to be got for food.
The rent was in arrears in almost all the families, as no rents

In considering this table, it is necessary to recollect that
an ordinary healthy man at rest in bed actually expends
energy at about 1,700 calories per day, leading a sedentary
life 2,500 calories, and when working hard it may be as great
as 4,000 calories?average probably between 3,000 and 3,500
calories. The energy supplied in food is not all available,
some 10 per cent being lost in digestion, so that to supply
2,500 calories net the food must contain 3,000 gross. In only
one family (No. 6) does the diet yield energy equivalent
to 3,000 calories, and this is largely due to the smallness
of the family and to the percentage of income spent on food.
The total expenditure of this family is really much in excess
of the income. In nine families an average of 2,416 calories
was secured, while two families averaged no more than 1,648
calories per man per day. (For the condition of these families

Infants.
In the families studied there were six infants under a year,
and it is of interest to note that the majority of these were large
healthy babies, the only one markedly under weight (family No. 5)
being noted as unhealthy. The number is too small to allow
for any generalisation, but it would seem that even in such
extreme poverty the child begins life without any marked
initial handicap.

Conclusions.
These families may be taken as a fair sample of hundreds
of others.
The results reveal a distressing picture of under-feeding and
under-nutrition of a condition approaching that existing in
Vienna in 1920-21, when, according to the investigations made
in a large number of families, the average energy supply in the
food amounted to about 2,000 calories with about 60 grms. of
protein, and from 40 to 55 grms. of fat per man per day.
Now, these Glasgow families with incomes of under 8s. per
man per week are getting about 2,200 calories, with 63 grms. of
protein and 61 grms. of fat. Existence on such a ration is
only possible if the normal rate of chemical change in the body
as measured by the basal metabolism is markedly reduced.
This Benedict has proved to occur in prolonged semi-starvation.
But there can be no doubt that the decreased activity must
lead not only to a lowered capacity for work, but also to a
lowered resistance to infection and disease.
The investigation was carried out at the instigation of
Professor Noel Paton, and we desire to thank him for the
assistance and advice he has given to us in the preparation of

our results. The work was done under the Child Life Com-

Appendix of Family Histories.
1. Father a labourer, unemployed several months; unemploy-
ment due to a strike. For five weeks after strike no income
at all. Two children for past month getting three free meals
daily in school; only meals at home were supper on Saturday
and all meals on Sunday. House untidy; rent in arrears.
Youngest child under-nourished, rickets. Income, 30s.
2. Father a docker. Short time for past fifteen months;
average income about 40s.; this week, 30s. House rather
untidy; rent, three months arrears. Children delicate; three
youngest rickets. Mother thin and unhealthy appearance.
3. Father a docker; until three months ago very slack?
two and a half days weekly. This week's income, 52s.; son,
message boy, 12s. (total income, 64s.). House and children
well looked after. Children quite healthy. Rent arrears, 24s. 4d.
4. Father a labourer. Worked only for short spells for
past two years; six months unemployed, then six months
on Road Scheme, and again unemployed. Children have been
getting three meals daily at school?stopped owing to school
holidays. House clean and tidy. Children pale and not
healthy appearance, especially boy, 4 years. Mother delicate?
rheumatism and anaemia. Income, 42s. 6d.
5. Father a painter's labourer. Seven months unemployed?
just started work (expects 44s.), but will not be paid till
following week; son (19) and girl (15) unemployed. House
dirty; care of children not very good. Three children get
meals at school?stopped owing to holidays. Child (4) not
walking yet, rickets; unhealthy baby. Rent, three months
arrears. Income, 41s.
6. Father a labourer. Seven months unemployed. Wife's
parents live next door?also unemployed, but probably help.
House clean and children quite healthy. Income, 30s.
7. Father a labourer. Working odd days for seven or
eight months, Income varying weekly?week of study worst
since short time started?only 8s. 6d. received; 7s. to factor
for rent?borrowed money to keep family for rest of week;
following week totally unemployed. House very clean, and

children well cared for. Mother suffering most from under-
nourishment.
8. Father a labourer. Lost work last May through lock-out,
and so not entitled to any Unemployment Insurance or Parish
allowance. For three weeks no income at all. Then charitable
society granted 10s. for a week, and again for second week.
Worked two days last week. Study started on 35s. granted
by Canteen Fund. Before May on short time for several months.
Children unhealthy, and have rickets, the three youngest (twins
of 2 years and baby) not walking, pale and delicate-looking.
Baby recovering from pneumonia. House clean and tidy.
Rent not badly in arrears.
9. Father a carter. Unemployed one year. Before that
ten months working at night in a music hall?21s. weekly.
Made no application to Parish Council till three months after
losing work. Parish granted 30s. for two months; now
increased to 32s. 6d. House clean; children well looked after.
Children quite healthy, except eldest. Boy (8), almost all meals
at grandparents. Mother pregnant?suffers from creeping
paralysis. Rent in arrears.
10. Father a miner. Unemployed through miners' strike.
House fairly clean, and children quite well looked after. Had
been receiving three pints free milk daily for nine months
previously. Boy (7), meals at school; only meals at house were
supper on Saturday, and all meals on Sunday. All healthy.
Rent in arrears. ?
11. Father a park labourer?relief work?works alternate

required, and only 26s. 5d. left over. Three sons (17, 15, and 15)
unemployed. Daughter (19) working, 19s. weekly. House
clean but untidy. All children undersized, and show signs of
rickets. Rent arrears 57s.
12. Father a labourer. Unemployed over one year. Heavy
drinker, and at present on year's probation for wife assault.
Pension 114s., paid quarterly?generally used for quarter's rent,
but last quarter husband spent it all on drink, &c. Four
children get all meals daily at school. This week only meals
at home on Sunday. They are not healthy. House clean
and well furnished. Income, 27s.
