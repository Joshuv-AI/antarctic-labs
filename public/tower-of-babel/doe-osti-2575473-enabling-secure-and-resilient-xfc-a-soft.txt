Executive Summary
Extremely fast charging (XFC) has the potential to reduce the charging time of
battery electric vehicles (BEV) to be equivalent to the filling time of internal
combustion engine vehicles (ICEV), thus eliminating one of the few advantages
ICEV still poses for light- and heavy-duty vehicles. Enabling XFC will, however,
require coordination and cooperation between the grid, charging stations, and the
vehicles themselves, which leads to an inevitable increase in the attack surface for
all systems combined. In securing the overall system, we must not only embrace
traditional cybersecurity, which is chiefly concerned with communications and the
operation of digital systems, but also cyber-physical systems security as the proper
operation of XFC is critically dependent on systems’ abilities to know about (sense)
and interact with (actuate) the physical world. The project team consists of academic
and industry researchers with backgrounds in cybersecurity, cyber-physical systems
security, learning in adversarial environments, transportation security, grid security
and resilience, wireless power transfer, converter design, and battery management
systems.

The goal of the proposed work is to enable the decrease in battery charge time in a
secure and efficient manner. Charging will occur under situations in which electric
vehicle service equipment (EVSE) and the BEV themselves are untrustworthy (or at
least not trusted by each other or the grid). While we will adopt strategies and
designs to produce secure EVSE and BEV, it is inevitable that successful
compromises of either BEV or EVSE will take place. It is thus necessary that a
resilient (and not just secure) system be put in place: one that is able to detect
compromises, minimize their effects, and prevent them from spreading. Given the
inherently, and necessarily, dispersed nature of charging infrastructure and the
possible disruption resulting from widespread inoperable EVSE for even a short
time, operators should also be able to remotely address compromises (e.g., through
over-the-air updates (OTAU)) in a secure manner. Finally, as XFC is expected to
lead to increased demands on the electrical grid, it will be necessary to securely
coordinate the actions of charging networks so as to prevent EVSE from being used
to undermine the operation of the grid. Fully preventing the latter will require
deployment of grid-side monitoring and response technologies to counter
compromised EVSE/BEV.

To achieve our goal of secure and efficient charging, we propose a
hardware/software-security (HW/SW- Sec) co-design approach that will: 1) provide

resilient operation of BEV/EVSE/grid through the use of security-hardened
controllers, converters, and monitoring systems that leverage secure
sensing/actuation techniques, moving-target based detection and mitigation
strategies, and 2) the ability to guarantee successful remediation of vulnerabilities in
EVSE/BEV through remote updates, all while 3) respecting end-user privacy.

2. Background
 While existing designs for EVSE/BEV provide efficient and safe charging, the
 designs implicitly assume a trusted environment (i.e., one in which all actors are
 nominally trustworthy and not attempting to undermine system operation). While the
 resulting designs are safe in the case of non-malicious failures, as research in
 automotive systems has shown [1–5], the design process used for safety critical
 systems does not produce inherently more secure systems. Some preliminary work
 does exist for securing EVSE/BEV [6, 7]; however, the conclusions and designs are
 either proprietary [8–11] or the work only provides high-level operational
 requirements for securing communications between systems [12–15]. Not only does
 existing work not provide workable implementation details but only standard
 communication channels have been examined. That is, threats to, and protections
 for, the systems and sensors/actuators that control/measure the power channel have
 not yet been examined.

 In developing our proposed work we were guided by three attack scenarios involving
 compromised EVSE and/or BEV [16]. In the first scenario (vehicle-to-grid) a network
 of compromised EVSE could be used to simultaneously discharge the batteries of
 BEV, leading to islanding. Scenario two (grid-to-vehicle) involves compromised BEV,
 with possible collusion from compromised EVSE, drawing from the grid in a
 coordinated manner so as to cause instability (under-frequency or under-voltage).
 The third attack scenario imagined malware being spread from a BEV to other BEV
 through the compromise of single or multiple EVSE (which would then infect other
 BEV). Thus, our work must encompass solutions for EVSE manufacturers and
 network operators, BEV manufacturers, and utility companies. Light-duty vehicles
 charging at power levels of 200 kW to 400 kW, via conductive and inductive
 interfaces, were considered.

3. Project Objectives
 The objective of this project was to develop security-hardened controllers,
 converters, and monitoring systems for a fast charging infrastructure that maintains
 user privacy by using secure sensing and actuation techniques and learning-enabled
 moving-target defense while providing successful remediation of vulnerabilities
 through remote updates. The feasibility of the technologies developed were
 demonstrated, in part, on a real-world testbed that includes an extreme fast charge
 (XFC) unit and Electric Vehicle (EV) situated in a microgrid.

3.1 Task Summaries
3.1.1 Task 1.0: Undertake a cyber-physical threat and vulnerability assessment
of EVSE/BEV

Outcome(s): Attack trees and attack graphs for electric vehicle supply equipment
(EVSE) and battery electric vehicle (BEV) based on output from automated tools;
risk analysis; trust models based on game theory.

3.1.2 Task 2.0: Perform experimentally-validated, grid-side modeling of XFC
loading on a microgrid and using a reachability analysis to determine the safety of a
given charge request

Outcome(s): Models of XFC loading behavior on microgrid based on simulated and
real-world data; deployment of ChargePoint XFC unit to a microgrid.

3.1.3 Task 3.0: Development of a moving-target defense for sensor and
actuator attacks against EVSE/BEV/grid controllers

Outcome(s): Model-based framework that allows for detecting sensor and actuator
attacks and switching to mitigating controller.

3.1.4 Task 4.0: Design AC-DC (for EVSE) and DC-DC (for BEV) converters and
battery management systems (for BEV) capable of resisting false data and false
actuation attacks by leveraging redundancy, diversity, and watermarking

Outcome(s): Cyber-physical security-hardened 60 kW AC-DC converter and 5 kW
DC-DC converter. The 60 kW module will be a fully functional building block with
integrated sensors and controls for the build of a 300 kW unit.

3.1.5 Task 5.0: Use device fingerprinting to determine whether an actual EV is
connected to the EVSE and, for the case of inductive charging, building a secure
ranging system with spoofing detection to ensure that a vehicle is properly and
safely aligned with the charging pad

Outcome(s): EVSE would be able to identify the type and charging capabilities of a
target BEV.

4.1.1.2 Spoofing Threats
Spoofing is pretending to be something or someone other than yourself and is a
violation of authenticity. Spoofing may allow an attacker to monitor, confuse,
substitute or disrupt communications and normal charging, inflict damage or perform
theft of electrical energy.

4.1.1.3 Tampering Threats
Tampering is the intentional modification of data, software and/or hardware of the
victim system. It may include physical damage or modifications to physical devices
and their properties. Tampering involves a violation of integrity. Possible objectives
include theft of electrical energy, harvesting of sensitive data, manipulation of control
data.

4.1.1.4 Repudiation Threats
Repudiation is claiming not to be responsible for an action; it is a violation of Non-
repudiation. An example of repudiation is an identity claiming not have charged a
vehicle when in fact he/she did.

  Information disclosure is exposing information to someone not authorized to see it, a

4.1.2 Vulnerability assessment of a BEV (Subtask Y1.1.2)
Outcome: An assessment of a BEV was carried out.

Using technical and maintenance documentation for a BEV provided by a project
partner, and guided by the TARA analysis, we identified 18 potential attacks focused
on the vehicle’s power electronics. We then selected four high impact attacks and
developed attack trees as a prerequisite to probing the vehicle itself to determine
viability of the attacks (e.g., whether failsafe mechanisms exist that would thwart the
                                       Page 12 of 51

attack and, if they do exist, whether they can be overcome).

The four attacks were: 1) cause the battery to reach a critical temperature to
damage cells/charging system via manipulation of the coolant system; 2) cause the
battery to reach a critical temperature to damage cells/charging system via
manipulation of charging control/monitoring sensors; 3) cause the battery to
discharge suddenly, and in excess of safety limits, to damage circuitry via
manipulation of load sensors; and 4) inducement of arcing at the charge point
terminal, leading to potential harm to operator, via pilot signal manipulation that
causes charging switches to remain closed upon removal of charger. An attack
graph for attacks one and two is presented below.
4.1.2.1 Attack Description and Attack Graph
In order to cause permanent damage to the vehicle, we attempted to overheat the
main battery pack by either under-cooling the pack or over-charging it. The internal
modules controlling cooling and charging were identified and the team worked to
understand their interactions and how fail-safes might be bypassed. An open-
source, failsafe hardware injector with a communication interface capable of
substituting arbitrary CAN messages in a timely fashion and impersonating
legitimate sensor readings was utilized. The procedure for accomplishing this is
detailed in Figure 2.

4.1.2.2 Attack Validation
The team was able to successfully spoof the temperature and voltage values of half
of the cells in the battery pack. Sensor values were acquired while the vehicle was
at 80% SoC and then replayed while charging with an actual SoC of 90%, with the
goal is to increase the amount of current seen at 90% SoC to be the same as that of
80% SoC, thus resulting in overcharging. The vehicle would display incorrect
information on the infotainment unit but charging would be otherwise unaffected. We
attributed this outcome to the BECM averaging readings across all cells; as we could
not spoof all of the sensors in the battery pack, due to accessibility issues, the attack
effect was minimized.

4.1.3 Trust Models of EVSE/BEV/grid (Subtask Y1.1.3)
Outcome: By defining a defender-attacker game we identified the most likely attack
vectors and attack paths. We also discovered the critical nodes that would affect the
most the system if not protected.

4.1.3.1 Automatic Threat Assessment
We focused on CPS-centric attacks (i.e., delay, jamming, false-data injection, false-
actuation injection) against the inputs and outputs of an AC/DC converter and
DC/DC converter in a BMS. As both approaches require a model of the system
under consideration, work focused on a linearized model of the converters (Figure
3). A cost-benefit analysis, based on a linear-quadratic differential game, was used
to examine the effects of false-data injection (FDI), sensor jamming, and false-data
actuation (FDA) against the team's proposed BMS design. The results show that
most vulnerable components of the system lie primarily with the current and voltage
sensor and that efforts should expended on hardening these attack points from
false-data injection and jamming, the latter of which was found to be the most cost
                                        Page 14 of 51

 effective. To mitigate these attacks our hardened converter designs will have to rely
 on sensor/actuator redundancy and sensor diversity and the moving target defense.

 4.1.3.2 Optimal Attack Graph Generation
 We completed a formulation that accommodates FDI and FDI in a computationally
 efficient and tractable manner, with feasible paths forward for jamming/delay of
 actuation/sensor signals (Figure 5). Given this formulation it is possible to state

 Based on this work, we were able to unify the two problems (i.e., solving the
 jamming problem for will solve the cost-effective problem, too, as they are both
 optimization problems using linear-time invariant state-space models).

 Fig. 7. (also referred to as storming), and grammar tests
 are standard test types utilized as part of communications robustness testing. In our
 work, communications robustness testing was collectively referred to as “fuzzing.”
 This type of testing can discover flaws that result in severe security vulnerabilities,
 such as Remote Code Execution (RCE) and Denial of Service (DoS). The fuzzing
 tests used for this project target the Transport (TCP/UDP), Session (V2GTP),
 Presentation (EXI), and Application (SDP, V2G Messages) layers of the OSI model.

 A fuzzing test, or “fuzzer,” sends invalid packets to the target by randomly modifying

                                                                                                                                         Page 17 of 51

data in specific fields of the request. A grammar test, is similar, but is more
systematic in its approach. For grammars, the fuzzed data is pre-selected based on
values at the beginning, end, and middle of the data field’s values, and the test
cases iterate over a combination of the targeted fields to provide systematic
coverage.

A flood test determines the ability of the target to handle a large number of requests
at a high data rate. Additional types of fuzzing tests were used in this project,
specifically scans and damage tests. A scan test determines the ability of the target
to handle typical port scan methods that are used to perform reconnaissance against
networked devices. A damage test determines the ability of the target to process
packets that have minor alterations, particularly focused on error and length
processing.

4.1.4.2 EVSE Target
RISE V2G provides Java-based EVSE and BEV simulators that can communicate
with each other: “RISE V2G is the Reference Implementation Supporting the
Evolution of the Vehicle-2-Grid communication interface ISO 15118. The
international standard ISO 15118, entitled "Road vehicles - Vehicle to grid
communication interface", defines a digital IP-based communication interface
between an electric vehicle (EV) and a charging station (named Electric Vehicle
Supply Equipment - EVSE). It allows for a user-friendly "Plug And Charge"
mechanism for authentication, authorisation, billing, and flexible load control based
on a wide set of information exchanged between the EV and EVSE.”

Research suggests that although RISE V2G is a reference implementation project, it
is used in proof-of-concept environments, and it is intended to be used in final
products as well. Testing the RISE V2G reference implementation gives a general
understanding of the risk associated with electric vehicle chargers. The simulated
target that was tested functions the same as a production EVSE, from a
communications standpoint, and is only limited by the simulated status and data that
can be returned.

Version 1.2.6 was obtained from the GitHub repository. For testing purposes, RISE
V2G was rebuilt with an updated version of Java, which required several build
changes. In addition, vulnerable dependencies were fixed.

4.1.4.3 Vulnerabilities Identified
For the RISE V2G SECC target that was tested, several findings were identified with
fuzz testing techniques. The findings were primarily related to failures in the EXI
codec processing routines, specifically the decoding functionality. Java stack errors
were observed with some of these fuzz cases generated during EXI Decoder
fuzzing. Identifying faults in the EXI codec libraries represent a common failure
source that can have impact beyond a single implementation of the ISO 15118
protocol.

Additionally, a finding was identified related to the schema processing of V2G
Messages. The RISE-V2G code assumes that all V2G messages after the
SupportedAppProtocolReq use the grammarMsgDef grammar. If a second
SupportedAppProtocolReq message is sent when the SECC is expecting a different
message type, the code crashes due to the wrong schema being used. Although
the target seemed to recover from this error, processing routines can be enhanced
to gracefully handle failure cases.

The RISE-V2G code managing receipt of UPD messages improperly assumes no
more than 20 bytes of data being sent. This flaw appears to have been reported, but
was unfixed in the version tested.

The RISE-V2G code processing V2G-TP messages incorrectly uses a signed value
instead of an unsigned value for payload length. This flaw appears to have been
reported, but was unfixed in the version tested.

From simulation results, as charging power increases/decreases, the voltage tends
to decrease/increase accordingly. The fluctuating charging pattern was found to
impact the feeder voltage profile most severely, resulting in the greatest voltage drop
compared to other charging curves. The ramping pattern imposes the least impact
on voltage profile. For grid-connected mode, all voltage magnitudes are within the
acceptable limit (0.95 p.u. to 1.05 p.u.).

In contrast, the overall voltage variations tend to become much larger and more
susceptible to the aggressive charging curves in the islanded mode. The voltage
level reaches and even exceeds the upper limit (1.05 p.u.), for both the step pattern
and fluctuating pattern, which are likely to trigger over voltage protection relay. This
is largely due to a lack of strong voltage regulation which can absorb excessive
reactive power supply (PVs are present in the network but they operate in the unit
power factor mode, Q=0).

Outcome: Load, voltage and frequency profiles for different attack/mitigation

4.2.2.1 Demand-side Attacks
We proposed the use of reachability methods to systematically estimate the effect
power demand changes have on the grid in the most general possible terms—i.e.,
when the attacker is given arbitrary control of power consumption. Our approach
allowed us to determine primary frequency response to load disturbances within the
grid and analyze their effects based on attacker resources, free of assumptions of
attacker behavior within the prescribed power injection bounds.

The purpose of reachability, from a security perspective, is to find the possible
output results (or the reachable set) given a decision space of one actor in the
scenario. If the entire reachable set is within safe operating parameters, then the
system is safe even in the case of cyber attacks, as long as the cyber attack lies
within the decision space.

                                    Page 24 of 51

The issue with applying reachability is computational complexity. We reduce the
complexity of the power system model in two ways; clustering (breaking the problem
into sub-problems) and model order reduction (allowing us to further reduce each
sub-problem).

4.2.2.2 Grid Impacts
We applied our framework to small and medium testbeds (39-118 bus systems), and
synthetic power versions of a state-level grid (the Polish power grid), allowing us to
predict the vulnerability of attacks given varying threat models and attack scenarios.
Across all scenarios we assumed that the attacker seeks to adjust the generator
speed by ±0.5Hz to cause ULFS shedding. Automatic-gain control (AGC)
parameters were set to allow the system to be stable, by separating the grid into
zones and stabilizing those zones, then adjusting further upwards until the entire grid
was stable. The grids were reduced to a differential power flow model, based on DC
input/output.

Across all testbeds, time-varying, attacker-induced demand patterns could be found
that induced load shedding. The 118 bus case is illustrative. It was assumed that
the attacker had control over 30 MW increase/decrease per load, which was 45.26%
of the 42.42 GW normal power demand across 64 loads. Figure 12 illustrates the
result of the reachability analysis, showing that a particular generator can be
manipulated to disconnect from the grid; Figure 13 depicts the behavior of the
generator under attack that leads to shedding.

4.3.1 Combined proactive and reactive defense mechanism (Subtasks Y1.3.1,
Y2.3.2)
Outcome: Proactive and reactive techniques with theoretical stability, optimality and
robustness guarantees that can be integrated into power cyber-physical systems.
4.3.1.1 Secure Control in Adversarial and Uncertain Environments
We utilized a stochastic switching structure to dynamically and continuously alter the
parameters of a control system, consisting of sensors and actuators, while hindering
an attacker’s ability to conduct successful reconnaissance to the system. The
unpredictability of the current actuator and sensor is optimized using an information
entropy measure, which is induced by probabilistic switching.

A reactive mechanism on the other side, detects potentially attacked components,
namely sensors and actuators, by leveraging online data to compute an integral
Bellman error. A rigorous mathematical framework was utilized to guarantee the
stability of the equilibrium point of the closed-loop system, and we provide a
quantified bound on the performance loss when utilizing both reactive and proactive
mechanisms.

In order to show the effectiveness of our approaches we used a linearized five-
dimensional model of the ADMIRE benchmark aircraft. The model has seven
redundant actuators and two redundant sensors. Figure 14 indicates the
performance of the combined the reactive and the proactive security system. The
adversary manages to completely shut down one of the actuators belonging to the
most optimal controller. However, it is clear that the system is stabilized. Having
identified and removed the compromised component, the system and MTD structure

4.4.1.2 Adversarial Evaluation of Hardening Options
Numerous countermeasures have proven capable of protecting sensitive 103 circuitry,
cabling, and their signals from the effects of electromagnetic interference (EMI).
             Authorized licensed use limited to: to IEEExplore provided by University Libraries | Virginia Tech. Downloaded on July 31,2025 at 19:32:38 UTC from

However, in the case of intentional electromagnetic interference, existing
countermeasures possess limited efficacy. IEMI-capable adversaries attack the
signal processing circuits and signal paths between sensors/actuators and the
controller, seeking to manipulate the signals and falsify data. On a printed circuit
board (PCB), the traces carrying these signals act as unintentional receiving
antennae to a time-varying electromagnetic field generated by an adversary.

We demonstrated IEMI attacks on the PCBs used in electric vehicle (EV) charging
systems, a highly safety-critical CPS. To mitigate these attacks, we implemented
passive PCB-level countermeasures (Figure 22), namely, differential signaling, via-
fencing, and optical fiber for interconnects. In addition, we proposed and implement
a multiplexer-based defense that dynamically modifies the route path and evades
the adversary. All four countermeasures were extensively evaluated against multiple
adversarial setups and ranked based on their impact (Table 1).

DC converter (Subtask Y1.4.1, Y1.4.2, Y1.4.3, Y2.4.2, Y2.4.3)
Outcome: Module that will be a fully functional building block with integrated sensors
and controls for the build of a 300 kW XFC EVSE and BMS.

We proposed a high-performance Unfolder-based single-stage ac-dc converter for
electric vehicle battery charging applications. The dc-dc converter utilized a T-type
bridge structure to process the soft dc-link voltage generated from an Unfolder. The
converter was designed to operate efficiently over a wide range of variations in the
output and input voltages. A leading-edge aligned modulation technique was used to
increase the soft switching range of the primary bridge switching devices. A
decoupling control strategy was introduced to achieve the two control objectives of
power factor control and output current control. The proposed 18 kW module serves
as a scalable building block for a modular extreme fast charging system. Hardware
results from an 18kW module are presented with 96.3% ac-dc and 97.3% dc-dc
efficiency for rated operating conditions.
4.4.2.1 Circuit Topology
The proposed ac-dc circuit topology comprises an ac-dc unfolding circuit and
isolated dc-dc converter as shown in Figure 23. Waveforms of the Unfolder circuit
and the switching pulses Sa , Sb , and Sc for the common source back-to-back
switches (Qa3+ , Qa3− , Qb3+ , Qb3− , Qc3+ , Qc3− ) are shown in Figure 24. The
same pulse is given to two back-to-back switches of a phase. The Unfolder diodes
can also be replaced by MOSFETs, which would be beneficial for restricting the
inrush current path to the capacitors present at the output of the Unfolder circuit. The
use of MOSFETs would also make the circuit bidirectional for vehicle-to-grid (V2G)
applications. The common source back-to-back switches of the Unfolder only
operate at twice the mains frequency, while the rectifier diodes operate at the mains
frequency.

The two output ports of the front-end Unfolder circuit across P − O and O − N nodes
are denoted as p-port and n-port, respectively. Voltages Vpo and Von are pulsating
dc voltages, which are termed soft dc-link voltages. The T-type primary bridge of the
dc-dc converter essentially operates as a 2-port converter with two input ports in
series processing different powers denoted by Pp and Pn. The total power Ptot
processed by the transformer and secondary bridge remains constant as observed
in Figure 24. The focus of this work was the design of the dc-dc converter with soft
dc-link input from a three-phase Unfolder. The topology is derived from a dual active
bridge series resonant converter by replacing the primary bridge with a 3-level T-
                                    Page 35 of 51

 type bridge as shown in Figure 23. To distinguish the topology from a 3-level bridge
 operating with equal input voltages, the term “asymmetric” is introduced due to two
 different time-varying soft dc-link voltages at two input ports of the dc-dc converter.

4.5 Device fingerprinting and Secure Ranging for BEV/EVSE Interactions (Task
 5)
4.5.1 Devising device fingerprinting methodologies for conductive and inductive
chargers (Subtask Y1.5.1)
Outcome: EVSE would be able to identify the type and charging capabilities of a
target BEV.

The lack of secure identification of vehicles poses multiple threats to a rapidly
growing intelligent transportation system. One notable threat involves battery electric
vehicles (BEVs), wherein an adversary that has compromised the BEV’s software
system deceives a charging station (plug-in or wireless) into overcharging the
vehicle to produce catastrophic failure of the battery pack, including combustion or
explosion. To address this threat we proposed to identify vehicles, and hence their
privileges and/or charging capabilities, using device fingerprinting.

In particular, we leverage inductive loop detectors (ILD) to determine the make,
model, and year of vehicles. A wide-band signal is coupled via electromagnetic
induction to a vehicle and its variations (field amplitude vs. frequency) used to
capture unique frequency-dependent features of a vehicle resulting from its size,
shape, metal structure, and content. An ILD-based approach is cost-effective to
implement as it utilizes an already widely deployed infrastructure, in contrast to
approaches that use surveillance cameras or other sensors. A circuit-level, low-
cost, drop-in replacement to enable existing ILD deployments to fingerprint vehicles
is proposed and realized as a custom, open-source PCB. A comprehensive
                                    Page 39 of 51

 evaluation against a dataset acquired from 32 vehicles of different make, model, and
 year, over nine months, shows that the proposed approach is effective even under
 temporal, environmental, and measurement variation without retraining. Overall
 multi-class classification accuracy of up to 93% is achieved.
 4.5.1.1 Architecture
 An ILD consists of a multiple-turn, insulated loop of wire installed underneath the
 road (usually less than 5 cm from the surface), an electronic unit housed in the close
 vicinity, and a lead-in cable connecting the loop and the electronic unit. A transmitter
 in the electronic unit excites the loop with a time-varying current. An associated
 receiver in the unit monitors (directly or indirectly) some implementation- specific
 electrical variation of the loop to detect the presence of a vehicle. While our
 approach (Figure 29) replaces traditional electronic units with a higher bandwidth
 driver and specialized signal processing hardware/software, and re-purposes the
 loop for fingerprinting, the basic mechanism of effect that we rely on is common to all
 ILD systems.

4.6 Formally Verified, Remote Firmware Update Procedure with Unreliable
 Primary Communications (Task 6)
4.6.1 Integration of EVSE update framework into UPTANE (Subtask Y1.6.1,
Y2.6.4)
Outcome: A formally-verified, TCB-based routine capable of initiating remote update
procedure on an EVSE, authenticating firmware, and installing it.

Over-the-air (OTA) software updates are an important feature to remotely analyze
and upgrade any section of currently running software on battery-operated electric
vehicles and its supply equipment. Even though a secure OTA framework can verify
and validate updates before installation, the integrity of the framework itself cannot
be guaranteed, and can easily introduce system and software vulnerability with
potential catastrophic consequences. Our work showed how a popular automotive
OTA secure update framework (Uptane) can be deployed entirely inside a trusted
execution environment (TEE)-enabled commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) embedded
device to extend its security considerations and improve its resilience against both
internal and external security breaches. We also leveraged a software analysis tool
that leverages SAWScript to verify our proposed solution against any functional and
logical inconsistency, while validating our approach on a real COTS hardware
(Raspberry Pi 3B). The Uptane implementation in C was formally verified using
Frama-C guided by the Uptane standard and the architectural analysis using
VERDICT.
4.6.1.1 Update Architecture
We designed a secure software update framework that ensures that secure OTA
updates, along with the entire OTA verification framework, can be deployed and
serviced inside a trusted secure environment fully protected from any external or
internal attacks. For proof of concept, we utilize the Uptane framework to model a
secure OTA update and verification framework in BEVs and EVSEs. Uptane is a
secure open-source industrial software update standard for automobiles. The
framework services protection against malicious attackers by signing and delivering
software updates through a secure communication channel in EVSEs and BEVs.
Our proposed solution isolates and deploys the entire OTA verification framework
inside TEE to protect its integrity and avert any security breaches.

The TrustZone is ARM’s open-source implementation of trusted execution
environment for COTS embedded systems. It provides a secure virtualization
platform where code that requires trusted execution can be executed in isolation
                                   Page 44 of 51

 from the entire system without loss of integrity.

 Figure 34 shows an example overview of our proposed system model inside
 BEV/EVSE. Since Uptane’s software update process is based on a publish-
 subscribe model, we divide our work into three parts; (1) an Uptane-compliant
 secure server which complies with the Uptane standards, (2) a TEE- specific client-
 side implementation (inside BEV/EVSE) that includes all functional components that
 perform software and data exchange between the client sub-modules and the
 secure server, and (3) a secure standard communication protocol that services the
 client requests, and implements a coordinated data transfer between the server and
 the client. The client- side modifications ensure a seamless integration of the Uptane
 standard policies within the TEE framework. Since, it is the programmer’s
 responsibility to ensure that the application code running inside TEE do not
 inadvertently introduce any software-related security vulnerabilities, we formally
 verify our client-side implementation using the SAW tool and Frama-C to analyse
 and check for security vulnerabilities.

 graphs, and an automatic cyber-physical security assessment tool for EVSE/BEV
 and their interactions with the grid; 2) hardware-in-the-loop simulations evaluating
 XFC EVSE impacts to a microgrid to establish the resulting load, voltage and
 frequency profiles, trade-offs between robustness, optimality, feasible response, and
 timely detection of attack scenarios for EVSE-grid iteractions; 3) a learning-capable
 moving-target defense that can be incorporated into EVSE/BEV/grid controllers to
 provide resilience in the face of sensor and actuator attacks; 4) designs and
 reference implementations for AC-DC and DC-DC converters and battery
 management system that are hardened against known attacks; 5) a device
