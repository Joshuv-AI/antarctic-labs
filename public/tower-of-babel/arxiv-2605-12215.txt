A Tighter Upper Bound for the Number of Distinct Squares in Circular Words
Shuo Li, Yuan Song

Abstract
A square is a word of the form u ​ u uu , where u u is a nonempty finite word. Given a finite word w w of length n n , let [ w ] [w] denote the corresponding circular word , i.e., the set of all cyclic rotations of w w . We study the number of distinct square factors of the elements of [ w ] [w] . Amit and Gawrychowski first showed that this number is upper bounded by 3.14 ​ n 3.14n in [ 2 ] . In a recent article [ 6 ] , Charalampopoulos et al. improved this upper bound to 1.8 ​ n 1.8n and conjectured that the sharp upper bound is 1.5 ​ n 1.5n . Introduction

Repetitions in words constitute a central topic in combinatorics on words, with numerous connections to algorithms on strings and to the structural theory of periodicity. Among the various kinds of repetitions, the simplest and most studied are
squares
, i.e., factors of the form
uu
with
u
a nonempty word. The earliest result concerning squares in words is obtained by Thue
[
19
]
, who proved the existence of square-free words of all lengths in three letters. The research concerning counting distinct squares in finite words was initiated by Fraenkel and Simpson. Let
w
be a word of length
n
and let
\mathrm{Sq}(w)
be the maximum number of distinct squares in
w
. In
[
8
]
, Fraenkel and Simpson proved that
\mathrm{Sq}(w)\leq 2n
and conjectured that
\mathrm{Sq}(w)\leq n
. After a series of improvements of the upper bound of
\mathrm{Sq}(w)
, see, for example,
\mathrm{Sq}(w)\leq 2n-O(\log n)
due to Ilie
[
9
]
;
\mathrm{Sq}(w)\leq\frac{95}{48}n
due to Lam
[
11
]
;
\mathrm{Sq}(w)\leq\frac{11}{6}n
due to Deza, Franek and Thierry
[
7
]
and
\mathrm{Sq}(w)\leq 1.5n
due to Thierry
[
18
]
, the square conjecture was confirmed by Brlek the first author in
[
5
]
.

The square-counting problem has also been studied in the context of
circular
words. Currie
[
10
]
first showed the existence of square-free circular words in three letters for all but a finite number of length exceptions using a computer-aided proof. After that, Shur
[
17
]
gave a computer-free proof for the existence of exponentially many ternary square-free words, in terms of length, for given length. Let
w
be a word of length
n
and let
\mathrm{Sq}([w])
denote the maximum number of distinct squares in the circular word
[w]
. The first linear upper bound for
\mathrm{Sq}([w])
was obtained by Amit and Gawrychowski
[
2
]
, who proved that
\mathrm{Sq}([w])\leq 3.14n
and for infinitely many
n
, there are words
w
of length
n
such that
\mathrm{Sq}([w])\geq 1.25n
. As a direct consequence of
[
5
]
and
[
12
]
, one has the number of distinct square factors of a circular word
w
of length
n
is not larger than the number of squares in
w^{2}
, which induces
\mathrm{Sq}([w])\leq 2n
. In a recent article‘
[
6
]
, Charalampopoulos et al. improved this upper bound to
\mathrm{Sq}([w])\leq 1.8n
and showed that, for infinitely many
n
, there are words
w
of length
n
such that
\mathrm{Sq}([w])\geq 1.5n
. Charalampopoulos et al. also conjectured that
\mathrm{Sq}([w])\leq 1.5n
is the sharp upper bound for all
w
of length
n

[
6
]
. The goal of the present work is to improve the upper bound of
\mathrm{Sq}([w])
. The main result of this note states the following.

Main Theorem

Let
w
be a finite word of length
n
. The number
\mathrm{Sq}([w])
of distinct square factors of the circular word
[w]
is at most
\frac{5}{3}n
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we recall some basic notions on words and graphs. In Section
3
we recall the definition of Rauzy graphs and small circuits and prove more properties of small circuits. In Section
4
we give a proof of our main theorem.

For any positive integer

. In particular, a

, the

.

For all integers

In particular, if

, then

if

is the set of all length-

factors of

.

A word

is said to be

of a word distinct from

. The set of primitive factors of

.

Let

and

. Let

.

Theorem 1
(
Fine and Wilf
[
13
]
)

If a finite word

has two different period

and

, then

.

Next, we recall some basic definitions and properties concerning graphs mainly from Berge
[
3
]
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a directed graph such that
V
is the set of its vertices and
E
is the set of its edges.
A
chain
of
length
k
is a sequence of edges
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k}
, such that for each
i
satisfying
1<i<k
,
e_{i}
has one vertex in common with the preceding edge
e_{i-1}
and another vertex in common with
e_{i+1}
. A
path
is a chain such that, for each
i
satisfying
1\leq i<k
, the terminal vertex of
e_{i}
coincides with the initial vertex of
e_{i+1}
. A chain or a path is
closed
if it begins and ends at the same vertex. A
cycle
is a closed chain and a
circuit
is a closed path. Obviously, any circuit is a cycle, but a cycle may not be a circuit.
A cycle
C=(V,E)
(or a circuit) is
elementary
if each vertex on the chain occurs exactly twice: in this case, the
length
of
C
is
|V|=|E|
.

A graph
G
is
weakly connected
, if for any pair of distinct vertices
(v_{1},v_{2})
, there exists a chain with edges
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k}
such that
v_{1}
is an endpoint of
e_{1}
and
v_{2}
is an endpoint of
e_{k}
. For a weakly connected graph
G
with
l
edges and
s
vertices, the number
\chi(G)=l-s+1
is the
cyclomatic number
of
G
.

Now fix an order of the edges
E=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{l}\}
and choose an orientation for each edge. Given a cycle
C
in
G
, its
vector-cycle
is the vector
\mu(C)=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{l})\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
defined as follows:
for each
1\leq i\leq l
, let
r_{i}
be the number of times
e_{i}
occurs in
C
with the chosen orientation, and let
s_{i}
be the number of times it occurs against that orientation; then set
c_{i}=r_{i}-s_{i}
.
A family of cycles
C_{1},\ldots,C_{t}
is
independent
if the vectors
\mu(C_{1}),\ldots,\mu(C_{t})
are linearly independent.
This notion does not depend on the initial choice of default orientations.

Let
\mathrm{Indep}(G)
be the maximum number of independent cycles in
G
. We call a set of
\mathrm{Indep}(G)
independent cycles in
G
a
basis
of
G
.

A graph
M=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
is called a
subgraph
of
G
if
V^{\prime}\subset V
and
E^{\prime}\subset E
. In this case, let us denote

.

Theorem 2
(
Th. 2, Chap. 4 in
[
3
]
)

The cyclomatic number of a graph is the maximum number of independent cycles in this graph.

Rauzy Graphs and small circuits

Let us first recall the standard construction of Rauzy graphs introduced in
[
15
]
. Let
w
be a word of length
n

i
such that
1\leq i\leq n
, the Rauzy graph
\Gamma_{i}(w)
is a directed graph whose set of vertices is
\hbox{\rm Fac}_{i}(w)
and the set of edges is
\hbox{\rm Fac}_{i+1}(w)

An edge

starts at the vertex
u
and ends at the vertex
v

u
is a prefix and
v
is a suffix of

are weakly connected for all

. A formal proof is given in
[
1
]
, and has already been mentioned in
[

]

Let

be a Rauzy graph of

. An elementary circuit
C
in

is called

is smaller than or equal to
i
.

For
1\leq i\leq n
, let

be the number of small circuits in

, and set

sc(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}sc_{i}(w).

We list and extend some useful propositions and notations from
[

In

]

, there exists a primitive word

such that

. This fact can be extended to all circuits that are not necessarily small. Thus, for any circuit

in

Proof of the main theorem

Let
w=w_{1}\ldots w_{n}
be a word of length

W=w^{2}=W_{1}\ldots W_{2n}
. Let

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sc}^{\prime}(W)=\{C(q,l)|\;|q|<\frac{n}{2},q\in\hbox{\rm Prim}(W),|q|\leq l\leq|\hbox{\rm Class}_{q}(W)|+|q|-1\};

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sc}(W)=\{C(q,l)|\;|q|<\frac{n}{2},q\in\hbox{\rm Prim}(W),|q|\leq l\}.

From the fact that
W
is a square word, for any integer

,
|\hbox{\rm Fac}_{m}(W)|\leq n
. Moreover, since all factors of

of length

appears at least once at some position

and the factor

appear at least twice in

is a (not necessarily primitive) circuit. Moreover, one has the following lemma from

]

Lemma 9

If

\mathrm{Sq}([w])\leq\frac{3n}{2}
.

From now on, let us suppose that

is primitive. In this case,

. Consequently,
\Gamma_{n}(W)
is an elementary circuit. Moreover, there is no circuit in
\Gamma_{l}(W)
for

.

Observing that if

is a square of

From Lemma

. Moreover, one has

(1)

Consequently, one only needs to consider the small circuits of size smaller than

w^{\prime}[1,i]=w^{\prime}[j,i]q
. If
|C|<f(i)
, we will prove that
w^{\prime}[1,l]=w^{\prime}[j,j+l]=p^{\prime}
and
j+l\leq kl
in two cases. If
i\leq kl
, one has
i-j+1=i-|q|>l
and hence
w^{\prime}[1,l]=w^{\prime}[j,j+l]=p^{\prime}
. Moreover,
j+l=|q|+l-1\leq i\leq kl
. Similarly, if
i>kl
, one has
i-j+1=i-|q|>i-(k-1)l=l+(i-kl)
and one also has
w^{\prime}[1,l]=w^{\prime}[j,j+l]=p^{\prime}
. Moreover,
j+l=|q|+l-1\leq(k-1)l+l\leq kl
. In all cases,
w^{\prime}[1,l]=w^{\prime}[j,j+l]=p^{\prime}
and
j+l\leq kl
. Consequently,
j-1=sl
for some positive integer
s\leq k-1
and
|q|=sl
. Since
w^{\prime}[j,n]=w^{\prime}[1,n-sl]
,
w^{\prime}[kl+1,n]=w^{\prime}[(k-s)l+1,n-sl]
is a prefix of
p
, which contradicts the hypothesis that
w^{\prime}[1]\neq w^{\prime}[kl+1]
.

Now, let us prove that there exists only one circuit
C
passing through the vertex
w^{\prime}[1,i]
in
\Gamma_{i}(W)
. Otherwise, the vertex
w^{\prime}[1,i]
must occur more than twice in
W^{\prime}=w^{\prime}w^{\prime}[1,i]
. Obviously, the first and the last occurrences of
w^{\prime}[1,i]
appear respectively at the positions
W^{\prime}[1]
and
W^{\prime}[n+1]
. If there exists a third occurrence of
w^{\prime}[1,i]
at some position
W^{\prime}[j]
, then both
j-1
and
n+1-j
must be larger than or equal to the size of an elementary circuit passing through
w^{\prime}[1,i]
. But all of these circuits (if there is more than one such circuit) are of size
|C|>\frac{n}{2}
, therefore,

n=n+1-j+j-1>2\frac{n}{2}=n,

which leads to a contradiction.

In conclusion, the circuit
C
must appear in all bases of
\Gamma_{i}(W)
and
|C|>\frac{n}{2}
. ∎

Lemma 12

If there exists an integer
m\geq\frac{n}{2}
such that

1

for all integers
m+1\leq i\leq n
, all bases of
\Gamma_{i}(W)
contain at least an elementary circuit
C
of size
|C|\geq\frac{n}{2}
,

2

\Gamma_{m}(W)
contains a circuit
C=C(p,m)
with some primitive word
p
such that
|p|\leq\frac{n}{4}
,

3

\Gamma_{i}(W)
does not contain the elementary circuit
C=C(p,i)
for all integers
m+1\leq i\leq n
,

then
\mathrm{Sq}([w])\leq\frac{13n}{8}
.

Proof

First, let
|\hbox{\rm Class}_{p}(W)|=t
. Since the first small circuit of the form
C(p,.)
appears in the graph
\Gamma_{|p|}(W)
,one has
t\leq m-|p|+1
.
From Corollary
5
,

|O_{p}(W)|\geq\frac{t-|p|}{2}.

Second, since

\{C(p,l)|\;|p|+t+1\leq l\leq m\}\subset\mathrm{Sc}(W)\setminus\mathrm{Sc}^{\prime}(W),

one has,

|\mathrm{Sc}(W)\setminus\mathrm{Sc}^{\prime}(W)|\geq m-|p|-t.

Third, let us count
NC_{large}
the number of Rauzy graphs containing circuits
C
such that
|C|\geq\frac{n}{2}
in all bases. Let
k,r
be integers such that
0\leq r=n-k|p|<|p|
, and from the hypotheses,
k\geq 4
. We first affirm that
t\leq(k-1)l
. Otherwise, from the definition of
\hbox{\rm Class}_{p}(W)
, there is
p^{\prime}
a conjugate
p
such that
p^{\prime k+1}\in\hbox{\rm Fac}([w])
, which contradicts the fact
n-k|p|<|p|
. Let us count
NC_{large}
in two cases.

Case I
If
n-2|p|-r<t\leq(k-1)|p|
, then
(k-2)|p|=n-2|p|<t\leq(k-1)|p|
, and thus, there exists
p^{\prime}
a conjugate of
p
such that
p^{\prime k}\in\hbox{\rm Fac}([w])
. From Lemma
11
,
NC_{large}\geq|p|
. In this case,
n-|p|\geq\frac{3n}{4}
and thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sq}([w])

\displaystyle\leq 2n-\frac{t-|p|}{2}-|p|

\displaystyle=2n-\frac{t+|p|}{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2n-\frac{n-|p|}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{13n}{8}.

Case II
If
t\leq n-2|p|
, then from the definition of
m
,
NC_{large}\geq n-m
. In this case,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sq}([w])

\displaystyle\leq 2n-\frac{t-|p|}{2}-(m-t-|p|)-(n-m)

\displaystyle=n+\frac{t+3|p|}{2}

\displaystyle\leq n+\frac{n+|p|}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{13n}{8}.

Thus, in all cases,
\mathrm{Sq}([w])\leq\frac{13n}{8}.
∎

Lemma 13

If there exists an integer
m\geq\frac{n}{2}
such that

1

for all integers
m+1\leq i\leq n
, all bases of
\Gamma_{i}(W)
contain at least an elementary circuit
C
of size
|C|\geq\frac{n}{2}
,

2

\Gamma_{m}(W)
contains a circuit
C=C(p,m)
with some primitive word
p
such that
\frac{n}{4}\leq|p|\leq\frac{n}{3}
,

3

\Gamma_{i}(W)
does not contain the circuit
C=C(p,i)
for all integers
m+1\leq i\leq n

then
\mathrm{Sq}([w])\leq\frac{5n}{3}
.

Proof

Let us do an analogous proof as in the previous case. Let
|\hbox{\rm Class}_{p}(W)|=t
, then
t\leq m-|p|
. From the same observation as above,
\{C(p,l)|\;|p|+t+1\leq l\leq m\}\subset\mathrm{Sc}(W)\setminus\mathrm{Sc}^{\prime}(W).

Thus,

\mathrm{Sq}([w])\leq|\mathrm{Sc}^{\prime}(W)|-|O_{p}(W)|\leq|\mathrm{Sc}(W)|-(m-t-|p|)-|O_{p}(W)|.

If
t>2|p|
, then there is a word
q
conjugate to
p
such that
q^{4}\in\hbox{\rm Fac}(w^{\prime})
for some
w^{\prime}\in[w]
. However, since
\frac{n}{4}\leq|q|\leq\frac{n}{3}
from the hypothesis, then
q^{4}
is a conjugate of
w
, contradicting the primitivity of
w
.
If
|p|\leq t\leq 2|p|
, then
|O_{p}(W)|=t-|p|
. Thus,

\mathrm{Sq}([w])\leq|\mathrm{Sc}(W)|-|O_{p}(W)|\leq|\mathrm{Sc}(W)|-(m-t-|p|)-(t-|p|)\leq\frac{5n}{3}.

If
t<|p|
, then

\mathrm{Sq}([w])\leq|\mathrm{Sc}(W)|-|O_{p}(W)|\leq|\mathrm{Sc}(W)|-(m-t-|p|)\leq n+2|p|\leq\frac{5n}{3}.

In all cases,

\mathrm{Sq}([w])\leq\frac{5n}{3}.

∎

Proof
(of the Main Theorem)

First, if
w
is non-primitive, the theorem has been proved in Lemma
9
.

Now, let us suppose that
w
is primitive. In this case,
C(w,n)\sqsubset\Gamma_{n}(W)
is an elementary circuit. If
C(w,.)
never splits, or splits at some
m<\frac{n}{2}
, then one can conclude using Lemma
10
.

If
C(w,.)
splits at some
m\geq\frac{n}{2}
into more than
2
elementary circuits, then, from Observation
7
, there exists a circuit
C\sqsubset\Gamma_{m}(W)
such that
|C|\leq\frac{n}{3}
. Then one can conclude using Lemma
12
, Lemma
13
.

If
C(w,.)
splits at some
m\geq\frac{n}{2}
into
2
elementary circuits, then, from Observation
7
, there exist
C_{1}=C(q_{1},m),C_{2}=C(q_{2},m)
such that
|C_{1}|\geq\frac{n}{2},\;|C_{2}|\leq\frac{n}{2}
.

If
C(q_{1},.)
never splits, or splits at some
m^{\prime}<\frac{n}{2}
, then, from Observation
8
,
C(q_{2},.)
cannot split before
\frac{n}{2}
, the two circuits
C(q_{1},i),C(q_{2},i)
form a basis of
\Gamma_{i}(W)
for
\frac{n}{2}\leq i\leq m
. Thus, one can conclude using Lemma
10
.

If
C(q_{1},.)
splits at some
m^{\prime}\geq\frac{n}{2}
, then, from the same argument as above,
C(q_{1},i),C(q_{2},i)
form a basis of
\Gamma_{i}(W)
for
m^{\prime}\leq i\leq m
with
|C(q_{1},i)|\geq\frac{n}{2}
. Moreover, from Observation
7
, there exists a circuit
C\sqsubset\Gamma_{m^{\prime}}(W)
such that
|C|\leq\frac{n}{3}
. Thus, one can conclude using Lemma
12
, Lemma
13
. ∎

5
Conclusion

In this note, we give a tighter upper bound of the number of distinct squares in a circular word of length
n
, which was previously
1.8n
. However, it is mentioned in
[
2
]
that, from computation, the sharp upper bound seems to be
1.5n
. Thus, the current upper bound may also be improved.
