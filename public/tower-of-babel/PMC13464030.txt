Successful application of the condom technique in closure of a large coronary aneurysm—a case report
Authors: Jakob Knold, Martin Kirk Christensen, Lisette Okkels Jensen, Krishnaraj Rathod, Youssef Mahmoud AbdElMoneim, Eslam Abbas, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConsensus regarding treatment of coronary aneurysms is lacking. The condom technique has been described as a simple technique to obtain coronary vascular occlusion in different clinical settings. We report a case of percutaneous occlusion of a large aneurysm by the condom technique.Case summaryAn 83-year-old male with prior ischaemic heart disease presented to the emergency room after a fall with a thoracic trauma. The patient underwent extensive clinical investigation. The use of cardiac computed tomography revealed a very large right coronary artery (RCA) aneurysm. Assessing prior computed tomography angiographies, the coronary aneurysm in the RCA had grown significantly to 60 mm. A heart team recommended intervention due to a potential rupture risk. Due to patient preferences to avoid open-heart surgery and the anatomy of the aneurysm, the percutaneous coronary intervention with the condom technique was chosen to close the aneurysm with closure of the proximal part of the RCA as the RCA was chronic total occluded distally to the aneurysm. A coronary angiogram after 4 weeks showed complete closure of the aneurysm in the RCA, and the patient was doing well without adverse outcomes after 4 months.ConclusionThis is the first case with closure of a coronary artery aneurysm by the percutaneous condom technique. The technique might be used to close aneurysms originating from chronic total occluded coronary vessels among elderly patients not eligible for open-heart surgery or other interventions. However, long-term evaluations of safety and durability are needed before recommendations can be drawn.Keywords: Case report, Coronary aneurysm, Condom technique, Vascular closure, Ischaemic heart disease, Percutaneous coronary intervention

Introduction

The condom technique has been described as a novel cheap and easy-to-use tool to obtain vascular closure.1-3 Previous reported applications of the technique consist of closing a venous graft due to competitive flow after a chronic total occlusion (CTO) procedure, closing a coronary artery perforation and sealing a coronary artery fistula.1-3 We present an alternative application of the condom technique in closing a large coronary aneurysm as an alternative to open-heart surgery. Coronary aneurysms are often identified by coincidence and may be associated with risk of rupture, compression of adjacent vessels resulting in ischaemia and a poor prognosis.4 There are only limited guideline recommendations regarding management of coronary aneurysms due to lack of randomized trials.4-9 American Heart Association guidelines describe management of Kawasaki’s disease and prevention of aneurysms.7,8 Reviews on aneurysms in a broader perspective recommend an individualized approach.4,9

Case presentation

An 83-year-old male with known ischaemic heart disease presented in 2025 to the emergency department due to a fall and muscular thoracic pain (Table 1). The patient had a history of ischaemic heart disease, hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, and non–insulin-dependent diabetes. A thoracic computed tomography (CT) was performed in 2016 as part of investigation for rheumatic polymyalgia identifying a coronary aneurysm at the right coronary artery (RCA) measuring 23 × 27 mm in 2016 (Figure 1A and B). In 2021, the patient presented with an acute inferior ST elevation myocardial infarction with dyspnoea being the primary symptom. The patient was referred to an immediate coronary angiography (CAG), now showing a relatively large saccular coronary aneurysm at the RCA with an occlusion at the middle of the artery distal to the aneurysm. A thoracic CT confirmed growth of the aneurysm now measuring 33 × 33 mm. During percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), it was not possible to wire the distal lumen of the RCA. Due to an ejection fraction of 50% and no remaining ischaemia, the patient was treated conservatively. The RCA was left with a total occlusion distal to the aneurysm.

At the current admission in 2025, the patient denied angina pectoris. Physical examination revealed a minor haematoma under the right eye from the fall, otherwise the patient was clinically stable. An electrocardiogram (ECG) showed no changes compared with previous ECG with a known right bundle branch block. Due to an elevated D-dimer at 1.33 mg/L at admission, a thoracic CT was performed. CT ruled out pulmonary embolism but showed development of the aneurysm to 60 mm in diameter (Figure 2A and B). Thus, the aneurysm had expanded over 9 years from approximately 20 to 60 mm with an expansion of 30 mm over the last 4 years. As the patient was in a high-performance status, closure of the aneurysm was recommended due to risk of further growth or rupture of the aneurysm. The patient refrained from open-heart surgery, and a minimal invasive procedure was chosen.

The use of a covered stent, embolization or coiling of the entire 60-mm-wide aneurysm was considered not to be optimal options. An alternative approach with the condom technique was chosen. The patient was informed and accepted the procedure.

CAG, with right radial artery access, confirmed that the aneurysm was supplied by the RCA (see Supplementary material online, Video S1). No significant ipsilateral collaterals were seen on highly selective angiography of the RCA. Occlusion of the proximal vessel was not considered likely to cause any adverse outcomes. As the PCI wire could not be advanced distal to the neck of the aneurysm in the native RCA, intracoronary imaging could not be performed. The distal part of the RCA was supplied from the left coronary artery and was not influenced by the treatment in the proximal RCA. The RCA and the aneurysm were wired with a Sion Blue through a 7Fr Amplatz Left 0.75 guiding catheter from the right radial artery, and PCI was performed according to elective PCI standards with unfractionated heparin (7500 IU) administered. The condom technique was applied by placing a 3.5 × 22 mm stent with a cut remnant of a 30-mm balloon mounted on the outside of the stent1-3 in the proximal RCA (see Supplementary material online, Video S2). The aneurysm was not closed sufficiently. The procedure was then repeated with a 4.0 × 16 mm stent and a 3.5 × 30 mm balloon with sufficient closure. Monotherapy with aspirin was selected as the artery was closed after the procedure without any risk or concerns of stent thrombosis.

A follow-up CAG after 4 weeks showed a complete occlusion of the proximal part of the RCA and no filling of the aneurysm (see Supplementary material online, Video S3). The patient had no adverse outcomes and was feeling completely fine 4 months after the procedure.

Discussion and conclusion

This case demonstrates the applicability of the condom technique to close an advanced asymptomatic coronary aneurysm as an alternative to open-heart surgery in an elderly patient with a distal CTO RCA.

Coronary aneurysms are mainly caused by atherosclerosis in adults as in this case.4 Other causes are vasculitis, genetic predisposition (Marfan syndrome and Ehlers–Danlos syndrome), and stent complications.4 Kawasaki’s disease is the most common cause in children.4 The mechanism of aneurysmal formation involves an inflammatory reaction leading to increased levels of nitric oxide promoting vasodilation and aneurysm development.4 Management of coronary aneurysms is described in reviews, but European guideline consensus is lacking.4,5,9,10 In this case, the proximal occlusion of the parent vessel feeding the aneurysms leads to cessation of inflow, reduced intra-aneurysmal pressure, blood stasis, and activation of the endothelia and the coagulation cascade according to Virchow’s triad, resulting in aneurysm thrombosis.11 The condom technique is limited by the inability to retrieve the system and the risk of embolization which we managed to avoid. This technique might be applied in elderly fragile patients not eligible for surgery. Specifically in cases where the distal part of the vessel is CTO. Methods to close a coronary aneurysm include covered stent, embolization with a coil, vascular plug, or surgical ligation. In this case, a PCI wire could not be advanced distally to the neck of the aneurysm due to CTO of the RCA distally to the aneurysm, making implantation of a covered stent over the neck of the aneurysm impossible. This would require CTO PCI of the RCA. Because the patient did not have angina and the CTO was old, there were no indications for CTO PCI. The huge size of the neck of the aneurysm and the aneurysm itself made coiling less attractive. A vascular plug could be used, but the curved anatomy of the RCA and a short landing zone may limit the suitability of vascular plugs.12 Additionally, these devices are expensive and not available in all sizes in our lab. As the patient was free from angina pectoris and the RCA was an old infarct artery, the surgical option with ligation and bypass grafting was rejected after heart team discussion due to surgical risk in an elderly gentleman and patient preference. Importantly, this single case with limited follow-up does not provide data on reproducibility, durability, and long-term safety aspects. Within the broader landscape of aneurysm management, the condom technique seems to provide a complementary role where other treatment options are not feasible.
