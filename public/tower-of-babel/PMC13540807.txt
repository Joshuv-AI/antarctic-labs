Hugo™ RAS versus da Vinci® for robot-assisted radical prostatectomy in localized prostate cancer: a GRADE-assessed systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective comparative studies
Authors: Wajahat Mirza, Muhammad Haroon Haider, Talal Habib, Rao Nouman Ali, Muhammad Bilal Moeen-Ud-Din, Abdalla M. Hadhoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe comparative adoption of the Hugo™ robotic-assisted surgery platform for robot-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) is increasing; however, the strength and consistency of prospective head-to-head evidence versus da Vinci® remain uncertain. We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective comparative studies comparing Hugo™ RAS with da Vinci® for localized prostate cancer, incorporating sensitivity analyses and GRADE certainty assessments.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, CENTRAL, and ClinicalTrials.gov databases from inception to January 2026. Prospective comparative studies enrolling patients with localized prostate cancer undergoing RARP with Hugo™ versus da Vinci® were included in this review. Screening and extraction were performed in duplicate with consensus resolution. Random-effects meta-analyses were performed, and risk of bias was assessed using ROBINS I and certainty using GRADE.ResultsThree prospective comparative studies comprising 145 Hugo RAS cases and 145 da Vinci cases were included. The primary outcome, intraoperative complications, showed no statistically significant difference between Hugo™ RAS and da Vinci®; however, the estimate was imprecise with a wide confidence interval, reflecting limited statistical power and uncertainty around the true effect size (RR 1.95, 95% CI 0.49–7.72; I² 0%). Secondary outcomes also showed no statistically significant differences, including positive surgical margin (RR 1.20, 95% CI 0.49–2.98), operative time (MD − 15.34 min, 95% CI − 41.11 to 10.43), estimated blood loss (MD − 39.09 mL, 95% CI − 143.51 to 65.32; I² 90%), length of stay (MD 0.10 days, 95% CI − 0.02 to 0.22), and catheter duration (MD 0.01 days, 95% CI − 3.97 to 3.99; I² 92%). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis reduced heterogeneity in blood loss after removing a single study and shifted the pooled estimate toward lower blood loss with Hugo™. The certainty of evidence ranged from moderate to very low, most often downgraded for risk of bias, imprecision, and inconsistency.ConclusionsIn the currently available prospective comparative evidence, Hugo™ RAS showed no statistically significant difference from da Vinci® RARP for perioperative safety and early postoperative outcomes in localized prostate cancer. However, the small sample size, sparse events, and wide confidence intervals, particularly for intraoperative complications, preclude conclusions of clinical equivalence or non-inferiority. Larger multicenter prospective comparative studies with standardized outcome definitions and longer oncologic and functional follow-up are required to generate higher-certainty evidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12893-026-03952-7.

Introduction

Prostate cancer remains a major global health burden, and radical prostatectomy is a key curative option for clinically localized disease. Robot-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) has been widely adopted because it improves visualization and instrument control in the deep pelvis, aiming to reduce morbidity while preserving continence and erectile function. The da Vinci® platform has dominated robotic surgery for decades, but the recent introduction of Hugo™ RAS has stimulated comparative evaluation across perioperative, functional, and oncologic endpoints [1–3].

Oncologic control and functional recovery after RARP depend on precise apical dissection, atraumatic neurovascular bundle handling, meticulous hemostasis, and watertight urethrovesical anastomosis. Prospective head-to-head comparisons have reported perioperative metrics, pathology, and short-term function [4, 5], while early high-volume series have defined safety signals during adoption [6]. Propensity score–matched studies add 1-year functional and oncologic context [7], although broader meta-analyses remain limited by heterogeneous observational designs [8].

Key controversies center on learning curves, technical malfunctions, and whether efficiency differences translate into clinically meaningful harms. Intraoperative performance studies document time differences concentrated in draping and docking and report malfunctions that may prolong cases without clear increases in major complications [9]. Technique standardization, particularly for nerve-sparing approaches, may modify outcomes and interact with platform ergonomics [10]. Matched comparative analyses reduce confounding in non-randomized settings [11], although systematic reviews emphasize that the evidence base remains preliminary [12, 13].

Platform effects on efficiency extend to other robotic prostate procedures, underscoring the broader relevance of setup and console ergonomics in urologic practice [14]. Comparative platform work highlights the importance of case-mix and institutional expertise [15]; however, prior quantitative summaries often combine mixed study designs, apply variable endpoint definitions, and rarely translate statistical findings into actionable certainty for clinicians and health systems.

Therefore, a synthesis restricted to prospective comparative evidence is timely to guide adoption, training, and procurement decisions. This GRADE-assessed systematic review and meta-analysis quantifies the perioperative, early functional, and early oncologic outcomes of Hugo™ RAS versus da Vinci® RARP in localized prostate cancer, strengthening inference with prespecified sensitivity and subgroup analyses to deliver clinically meaningful interpretation for patient counseling and resource allocation.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was designed and conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420261290011). We prespecified methods for study identification, selection, data extraction, risk of bias appraisal, quantitative synthesis, and certainty of evidence assessment, focusing on head-to-head comparative evidence evaluating Hugo™ RAS versus da Vinci® platforms for robot-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) in localized prostate cancer.

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive electronic search was conducted from the inception of databases to January 15, 2026, across major bibliographic databases and trial registry. Searches combined controlled vocabulary (when available) and free-text keywords related to the robotic platforms (Hugo, da Vinci) and the procedure (robot-assisted radical prostatectomy [RARP] ). The full search strategy and database-specific yields are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The reference lists of eligible full-text articles and relevant reviews were also hand-searched to identify any comparative studies that may have been missed.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included full-text, peer-reviewed, prospective comparative studies enrolling adults with localized prostate cancer undergoing RARP, in which outcomes were reported for both the Hugo™ RAS and da Vinci® platforms within the same study framework. Eligible designs included prospective parallel cohorts and prospectively collected comparative datasets using predefined matching (e.g., genetic matching), provided a head-to-head comparison, and available extractable quantitative outcome data.

We excluded noncomparative single-arm series, editorials, letters, narrative reviews, conference abstracts without full text, technical notes without patient-level comparative outcomes, studies focused on procedures other than radical prostatectomy (e.g., simple prostatectomy), and studies in which the comparator arm was not assembled under a clearly comparative framework (e.g., selected historical comparators without prospective comparative enrollment). In cases of overlapping cohorts, the most informative report with the most complete extractable data was prioritized.

Data extraction and outcome measures

Two reviewers (W.M. and T.H.) independently screened the titles and abstracts, assessed the full texts, and extracted the data using a standardized, pilot-tested form. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third author (M.R.N.). The extracted items included the study setting, design, eligibility criteria, sample size, baseline clinicopathological characteristics, surgical approach, and pre-specified outcomes. The primary outcome was intraoperative complications (any), extracted as events and totals for each platform. Secondary outcomes were limited to variables with extractable numerical data in at least two studies and included positive surgical margins (PSM) (events/total), operative or console time (minutes), estimated blood loss (mL), length of stay (days), and catheter duration or time to catheter removal (days). Outcomes reported in non-comparable formats across studies (for example, docking time reported in only some cohorts, platform-specific segmented timing metrics, technical malfunctions, and user satisfaction measures) were captured for qualitative context but were not pooled quantitatively. Because the included studies did not uniformly report or standardize Hugo™ RAS arm configuration, port placement, and access strategy, the platform was analyzed at the system level rather than by technical setup subtype. Technical malfunctions and platform-performance events were recorded separately for qualitative interpretation and were not pooled with patient-centered intraoperative complications.

Quality assessment

Given the non-randomized comparative nature of the eligible evidence, the risk of bias was assessed at the outcome level using ROBINS-I, evaluating confounding, participant selection, intervention classification, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Each domain was rated, and an overall risk-of-bias judgment (low, moderate, serious, or critical) was assigned, with disagreements resolved by consensus.

Statistical analysis

All meta-analyses used random-effects models to account for anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the comparative cohorts. For dichotomous outcomes, pooled risk ratios (RRs) were calculated using random-effects models, while continuous outcomes were summarized as mean differences (MDs) when measured on identical scales and standardized mean differences (SMDs) when measurement scales differed across studies.

When studies reported medians with interquartile ranges or other dispersion measures, values were transformed to mean ± SD using the standard methods. For sparse-event outcomes, particularly intraoperative complications, pooled estimates were interpreted cautiously because the small number of events and included studies limited the stability and precision of model-based estimates. Alternative rare-event approaches, including Peto odds ratio and generalized linear mixed models, were considered; however, given the very small number of included studies and sparse event counts, these methods were unlikely to provide more stable or clinically reliable estimates. Therefore, the primary interpretation emphasized the direction of effect, width of the confidence interval, and the certainty of evidence rather than statistical non-significance alone.

Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using I² and τ², with statistical uncertainty interpreted alongside clinical relevance. Small-study effects and publication bias were planned to be assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test when at least 10 studies contributed to a given comparison; however, these assessments were not performed because fewer than 10 studies were available for all pooled outcomes.

Sensitivity analysis, subgroup analysis, and GRADE certainty of evidence

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using a leave-one-out approach for each pooled outcome to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the overall effect estimates. The prespecified subgroup analysis was based on geography, comparing Europe (two studies: Italy and Spain) versus Asia (one study: Hong Kong SAR, China). The subgroup findings were interpreted cautiously, where subgroup estimates were informed by a small number of studies. The certainty of evidence for each outcome was rated using the GRADE framework (high, moderate, low, and very low), considering the risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Summary judgments were used to contextualize the strength and clinical applicability of pooled estimates.

Results

Database/register searches identified 217 records, and six additional records were retrieved through citation searching, with 118 duplicates removed. After screening 105 records, 88 were excluded at the title/abstract stage, leaving 17 full-text reports assessed, with no reports retrieved (Fig. 1). Fourteen full texts were excluded due to wrong comparator, wrong study design, or wrong outcomes, resulting in three prospective comparative studies included in the final systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

All included studies were prospective comparative cohorts from Europe and Asia, with sample sizes ranging from 40 to 150 patients. Endpoints were heterogeneous: Antonelli et al. focused on intraoperative platform performance (timing, malfunctioning, complications, satisfaction), Brime Menendez et al. prespecified co primary perioperative time and outcome metrics with functional follow up as secondary outcomes, while Ko et al. prioritized early oncologic and safety outcomes (3-month PSA and 30-day complications) with perioperative and early functional outcomes as secondary measures (Table 1).

Methodological quality and study integrity

The ROBINS-I assessment showed an overall moderate risk of bias across all three included prospective comparative studies, driven consistently by confounding (D1) and selection of participants (D2), reflecting the non-randomized nature of platform allocation (Fig. 2A–B). All remaining domains were judged to be at low risk, including classification of interventions (D3), deviations from intended interventions (D4), missing data (D5), measurement of outcomes (D6), and selection of the reported result (D7), indicating clear platform identification, largely complete reporting, and predominantly objective perioperative/early outcomes with no clear evidence of selective reporting (Fig. 2A–B).

Primary outcome

Intraoperative complications

The pooled analysis showed no statistically significant difference in intraoperative complications between the Hugo™ and da Vinci® systems (RR 1.95, 95% CI 0.49–7.72; p = 0.34). This pooled outcome reflects reported intraoperative clinical complications rather than standalone device malfunctions or workflow interruptions. However, the confidence interval was wide, indicating substantial imprecision and limited statistical power. Therefore, the absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical equivalence, non-inferiority, or proven comparable safety between platforms. Intraoperative adverse events were uncommon in both groups, and heterogeneity was minimal (I²=0%) (Fig. 3).

Secondary outcomes

Positive surgical margin (PSM)

No statistically significant difference was observed in the PSM rates between the platforms (RR 1.20, 95% CI 0.49–2.98; p = 0.69). The direction of effect was uncertain, with moderate heterogeneity (I²=58%), suggesting variability across studies. Overall, the oncologic margin outcomes appeared similar between the Hugo™ and da Vinci® systems (Fig. 4).

Operative time (min)

The pooled mean difference indicated no significant difference in operative time between Hugo™ and da Vinci® (MD − 15.34 min, 95% CI − 41.11 to 10.43; p = 0.24). The estimate favored shorter times with Hugo™; however, the CI crossed zero, indicating uncertainty. Heterogeneity was moderate (I²=50%) (Fig. 5).

Estimated blood loss (mL)

Estimated blood loss did not differ significantly between the platforms (MD − 39.09 mL, 95% CI − 143.51 to 65.32; p = 0.46). Although the point estimate favored lower blood loss with Hugo™, the CI crossed zero in this study. Heterogeneity was substantial (I²=90%), indicating marked between-study variability (Fig. 6).

Length of stay (days)

Length of stay was not significantly different between the groups (MD 0.10 days, 95% CI − 0.02 to 0.22; p = 0.11). The effect estimate was small and clinically marginal, with no heterogeneity (I²=0%). The overall hospitalization duration was comparable between the platforms (Fig. 7).

Catheter duration (days)

Catheter duration showed no statistically significant difference between Hugo™ and da Vinci® (MD 0.01 days, 95% CI − 3.97 to 3.99; p = 0.99). The CI was wide, indicating imprecision, and the heterogeneity was high (I²=92%). Overall, the postoperative catheter management duration appeared to be similar across the platforms (Fig. 8).

Across the primary and secondary analyses, none of the pooled outcomes reached statistical significance, including intraoperative complications, PSM, operative time, blood loss, length of stay, and catheter duration (Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8). Point estimates variably favored Hugo™ for operative time and blood loss; however, confidence intervals crossed the null in all cases (Figs. 5 and 6). Heterogeneity was low for intraoperative complications and LOS (Figs. 3 and 7), but substantial for blood loss and catheter duration, limiting the precision and consistency of inference (Figs. 6 and 8). Overall, the current comparative evidence suggests broadly similar perioperative and early oncologic performance between platforms within the available data (Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8).

Sensitivity analysis

As shown in Supplementary Table 2, estimated blood loss demonstrated substantial heterogeneity in the primary analysis (I²=90%) with no significant differences between platforms. After removing Antonelli 2024, heterogeneity was resolved completely (I²=0%), and the pooled effect became statistically significant, favoring Hugo™ with lower blood loss (MD − 78.51 mL). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was not feasible for positive surgical margin, operative time, and catheter duration because each outcome included only two studies, despite moderate-to-high heterogeneity for some of these outcomes. Overall, the blood loss findings indicate that the pooled estimate was sensitive to the exclusion of one study and should be interpreted cautiously, considering the small evidence base.

Subgroup analysis

Because only three studies were included, the pre-specified geographic subgroup analysis could not be performed. Two studies were clustered within one geographic subgroup (n = 2), while the remaining study comprised the other subgroup (n = 1); with only a single study in one subgroup, pooling within subgroups and formal between-subgroup interaction testing were not statistically meaningful. Therefore, we report the geographic findings descriptively: across regions, the direction of effect was broadly consistent for key perioperative and early oncologic endpoints, with no clear signal that one platform performed differently in one region versus another. Notably, between-study variability appeared to be driven more by individual study characteristics than by geography per se, as reflected by the sensitivity of the blood loss outcome to the exclusion of one study. Overall, the available evidence did not suggest geographically distinct effects; however, the small number of studies, particularly the single-study subgroup, precludes firm conclusions and underscores the need for additional prospective comparative cohorts across diverse healthcare settings.

GRADE certainty of evidence

In Supplementary Table 3, the certainty of evidence was very low for intraoperative complications and several secondary endpoints, largely due to non-randomized design-related confounding/selection concerns and imprecision from small sample sizes and wide confidence intervals. For the primary outcome, the confidence interval was particularly wide and compatible with both potential benefit and potential harm; therefore, intraoperative complications were downgraded for imprecision and interpreted cautiously. Outcomes with high heterogeneity, notably estimated blood loss and catheter duration, were downgraded for inconsistency, with very serious inconsistency applied when I² was extreme. Indirectness was rated as not serious because the populations, interventions, and outcome definitions aligned directly with the review question. Publication bias could not be formally assessed because fewer than 10 studies contributed to each pooled outcome. Overall, the evidence did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference between platforms, but the certainty remains limited and is insufficient to establish clinical equivalence, non-inferiority, or definitive comparable safety between Hugo™ RAS and da Vinci®.

Discussion

The current synthesis of prospective comparative evidence indicates that perioperative safety signals for robot-assisted radical prostatectomy performed with the Hugo™ RAS platform are broadly comparable to those of established robotic systems, aligning with early real-world feasibility reports and reinforcing the clinical relevance of platform-level evaluation as adoption expands [1]. In our pooled analysis, intraoperative complications were uncommon and not statistically different between platforms (RR 1.95, 95% CI 0.49–7.72), translating from 13.8 per 1,000 with da Vinci® to an estimated 26.9 additional events per 1,000 with Hugo™ RAS at the point estimate, but with wide uncertainty spanning potential benefit and harm [2]. These findings should be interpreted in the context of prior comparative syntheses that combined heterogeneous designs and broader evidence frameworks, where similar conclusions were often driven by non-randomized data and variable outcome definitions [3]. The prospective comparative study evidence base included in this review remains small and center-specific; however, it provides the most methodologically consistent lens currently available for head-to-head inference in localized disease [4]. Nonetheless, because this synthesis includes only three prospective comparative studies, the resulting evidence base remains limited in precision, generalizability, and certainty for platform-level conclusions.

Across secondary endpoints, we observed no statistically significant pooled difference in estimated blood loss (MD − 39.09 mL, 95% CI − 143.51 to 65.32) despite substantial between-study heterogeneity (I² 90%), emphasizing that the direction and magnitude varied meaningfully by setting and possibly by workflow maturity [5]. Large contemporary single-center series have demonstrated that Hugo™ RAS radical prostatectomy can be performed with acceptable perioperative, early functional, and oncological outcomes; however, these uncontrolled cohorts cannot resolve comparative effect sizes and may underestimate early implementation frictions [6]. Propensity score–matched comparative analyses with longer follow-up have generally suggested similar functional and early oncological outcomes across platforms, supporting the plausibility that patient-important endpoints are primarily driven by technique, case mix, and surgeon experience rather than the brand label alone [7]. Prior meta-analytic evaluations that pooled controlled studies have also tended to report broadly comparable perioperative and oncological outcomes; however, they frequently acknowledge residual confounding, inconsistent endpoint definitions, and limited granularity for resource use [8].

Mechanistically, the most plausible pathway for platform-associated differences lies in setup ergonomics, docking workflow, instrument set behavior, and failure modes rather than in oncologic extirpation itself, which is largely standardized across contemporary robotic radical prostatectomy techniques [9]. Technique-specific refinements, such as structured nerve-sparing approaches performed on newer platforms, highlight that functional recovery is modifiable through surgical strategy; however, comparative attribution requires standardized reporting of nerve-sparing intent, baseline function, and follow-up windows [10]. Our operative time estimate was not statistically different between platforms in the two-study pool (MD − 15.34 min, 95% CI − 41.11 to 10.43), and the absence of a consistent signal is compatible with the expectation that efficiency is tightly coupled to the learning curve and institutional choreography [11]. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses comparing Hugo™ RAS and da Vinci® RAS have similarly concluded that the overall surgical and clinical outcomes are comparable, while emphasizing the preliminary nature of the evidence and the need for higher-quality prospective datasets [12]. An additional source of heterogeneity is the technical variability within the Hugo™ platform itself. Three-arm and four-arm configurations, as well as differences in port strategy and access approach, are not operationally equivalent and may influence docking efficiency, instrument exchange, assistant workflow, and total operative time. Because the available studies did not permit meaningful stratified meta-analysis by configuration, the pooled estimates should be interpreted as platform-level rather than setup-specific comparisons. For emerging robotic platforms, device-related malfunctions and patient-centered intraoperative complications are conceptually distinct outcomes, as technical interruptions may affect workflow without necessarily translating into direct patient harm.

From a practical standpoint, a distinct feature of the Hugo™ RAS platform is its modular architecture with independently positioned arm carts, which may offer greater flexibility in operating room setup, port spacing, and adaptation to surgeon preference or patient-specific anatomy. This may be particularly relevant in centers seeking customizable workflow integration or alternative access strategies. At the same time, this modularity may introduce additional variability in docking choreography, bedside coordination, and instrument exchanges, especially during early adoption, which could influence perioperative efficiency endpoints even when patient-centered outcomes remain similar.

Positive surgical margins, pooled from two studies, were not statistically different (RR 1.20, 95% CI 0.49–2.98), corresponding to an estimated increase from 168 per 1,000 with da Vinci® to 202 per 1,000 with Hugo™ RAS at the point estimate, but again with wide imprecision that limits inference for oncologic equivalence [13]. Broader comparative evaluations of urologic platforms in adjacent procedures underscore that platform comparisons can be sensitive to institutional purchasing strategies, case selection, and perioperative pathways, factors that may obscure or exaggerate device-level effects if not explicitly modeled [14]. Early comparative evidence between the da Vinci and HUGO platforms has highlighted the importance of distinguishing implementation phase outcomes from steady-state performance, particularly for efficiency endpoints that tend to improve nonlinearly with cumulative experience [15].

Contemporary systematic reviews of worldwide experiences have reinforced that most published Hugo™ RAS radical prostatectomy data remain early adoption reports, creating an inherent risk of optimism bias and incomplete capture of rare adverse events [16]. The current findings align with emerging comparative meta-analyses that report broadly similar perioperative outcomes while noting that heterogeneity is often driven by differences in surgeon background, docking and port strategy, and whether extraperitoneal or transperitoneal access is used [17]. Regional pioneer experiences illustrate that programmatic adoption is occurring across diverse health systems, which will likely expand external validity but also increase variability in perioperative pathways and cost structures that complicate pooling [18, 19]. Comparative studies that include more than two robotic platforms suggest that measured efficiency metrics, such as docking and transition times, can differ between systems, underscoring why workflow endpoints should be prespecified and harmonized across future platform trials [20].

From a surgical systems standpoint, access strategy may influence efficiency and recovery, and the extraperitoneal Hugo™ RAS series demonstrates that alternative approaches are feasible in high-volume centers [21]. Pooled analyses of Hugo™ RAS radical prostatectomy outcomes have highlighted acceptable early oncologic and functional results; however, they cannot determine whether outcomes reflect platform capability or the concentration of cases in expert centers during early dissemination [22]. IDEAL framework evaluations of Hugo™-assisted radical prostatectomy reinforce that the evidence base is still progressing through early stage assessment, supporting cautious interpretation of equivalence claims until mature prospective comparative cohorts are accumulated [23]. Retzius-sparing approaches on the Hugo™ RAS platform highlight the expanding technical repertoire and provide a biologically plausible route for earlier continence recovery through the preservation of anterior support structures [24]. Modifications, such as three-arm configurations, are relevant to resource utilization and operating room footprint; however, they further diversify exposure definitions and may increase clinical heterogeneity unless explicitly stratified in future comparative studies [25]. Cost analyses directly comparing initial hospital stay costs between da Vinci and Hugo™ RAS for radical prostatectomy indicate that economic endpoints are measurable and clinically relevant; however, they are highly context-dependent and should be modeled using standardized costing frameworks alongside clinical effectiveness [26].

Our certainty of evidence is constrained chiefly by nonrandomized design features, small sample sizes, and imprecision, with additional downgrading for inconsistency in outcomes, such as estimated blood loss, where the direction differed across studies [27]. In practice, learning curve work with larger consecutive cohorts suggests that efficiency and outcomes improve with experience, supporting the pragmatic view that early comparative differences may attenuate as teams stabilize port strategy, docking choreography, and troubleshooting routines [28–30]. Clinically, the most defensible implication is that Hugo™ RAS can be implemented for localized prostate cancer radical prostatectomy with similar short-term safety and perioperative profiles to incumbent systems in experienced centers, while counseling patients that the evidence base remains early and that rare harms and long-term oncologic equivalence require further prospective follow-up [1]. A concrete research agenda should prioritize multicenter prospective comparative studies with standardized reporting of intraoperative complications, conversion and malfunction metrics, margin status definitions, continence and potency time points, and resource use endpoints, enabling higher certainty GRADE ratings and more precise absolute risk translation for shared decision-making [2].

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this review is that it restricted inclusion to prospective comparative studies, thereby reducing bias inherent to retrospective series and improving temporal clarity of exposure and outcome ascertainment. This was complemented by a comprehensive multi-database search, duplicate screening and extraction, random-effects meta-analysis, and structured assessment of internal validity using ROBINS-I and certainty of evidence using GRADE. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was also used to probe robustness for outcomes with substantial heterogeneity.

However, the evidence base remained small and largely nonrandomized; therefore, residual confounding and selection bias could not be eliminated. Outcome definitions and reporting formats were incompletely standardized across studies, requiring median-to-mean conversions for some continuous outcomes and limiting some prespecified analyses. The primary outcome was also limited by sparse event counts and wide confidence intervals, which reduced statistical power and made the pooled estimate imprecise. Although alternative rare-event models may be considered in such settings, the very small number of included studies made these approaches statistically unstable; therefore, the intraoperative complication estimate should be interpreted as exploratory and imprecise rather than confirmatory evidence of comparable safety.

Another limitation is that some continuous outcomes required the transformation of nonparametric summary statistics into mean ± SD for pooling. This is particularly relevant for small early-adoption cohorts, in which variables such as operative time and estimated blood loss may be skewed, making transformed estimates less stable and potentially contributing to heterogeneity. In addition, setup-level differences within the Hugo™ platform itself, including arm configuration, port placement, and access strategy, could not be analyzed separately and may have contributed to heterogeneity in perioperative efficiency outcomes.

Conclusions

Among patients with localized prostate cancer undergoing robot-assisted radical prostatectomy, the best available prospective comparative evidence did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference between Hugo™ RAS and da Vinci® for intraoperative complications, positive surgical margins, operative time, estimated blood loss, length of stay, or catheter duration. However, the primary outcome was limited by sparse events and wide confidence intervals, meaning that the current evidence does not establish clinical equivalence, non-inferiority, or definitive comparable safety between platforms. These findings support cautious short-term use of Hugo™ RAS in experienced settings, while highlighting the need for larger multicenter prospective comparative studies with standardized definitions for complications, margin status, functional recovery time points, and resource use, alongside longer follow-up for oncologic control.
