Historical roots of hospital-centred operational governance in China: a path dependence analysis and its implications for global health system reform
Authors: Jackie Zhanbiao Li, Duo Wang, Xin Li, Menglan Zhu, Yingying Tiong, Yingqian Lao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHospital-centred operational governance has been a persistent feature of China’s health system. While this model has supported administrative control, rapid service expansion and organisational standardisation, it has also contributed to the continued concentration of resources in hospitals, constrained local flexibility and the relative marginalisation of primary healthcare. This study examines, from the perspective of path dependence, how this governance model gradually emerged and persisted from 1835 to the present.MethodsDrawing on historical studies, policy documents, institutional historical data, official materials and comparative global health literature, the study identifies seven phases in the historical evolution of hospital-centred operational governance in China: missionary introduction and early administrative formation; early institutional formation; nationalist integration and public function expansion; wartime adaptation and emergency coordination; socialist consolidation and planned administration; market transition and managerial modernisation and optimisation, digitalisation and performance governance.ResultsAcross these stages, four self-reinforcing mechanisms collectively stabilised this pattern: institutional steering, functional expansion, managerial standardisation and market-oriented transformation. The evidence indicates that hospitals became dominant not simply because they appeared earlier but because they repeatedly accumulated administrative visibility, professional authority, technical capacity, fiscal channels and reform implementation functions.ConclusionsFor health system reform, the findings suggest that strengthening primary healthcare and improving equity require technical management reforms to proceed in parallel with changes in governance power, financing incentives, resource allocation, workforce status and data infrastructure. The global health implication is that countries pursuing universal health coverage should avoid allowing hospitals to become the default centre of finance, information, professional prestige and policy implementation.

Introduction

Hospital-centred governance systems occupy a central position in contemporary global health systems, particularly where governments rely on hospitals to relieve political pressure, provide visible service capacity and organise increasingly complex healthcare services.14 However, the centrality of hospitals is not merely a technological consequence of the development of modern medicine. Rather, it is also the product of institutional choices that shape the allocation of power, the distribution of resources and the parts of the health system that receive sustained policy attention.24 From the perspective of global health development, these long-term institutional choices are highly consequential because they influence health equity, the continuity of patient care and the extent to which primary healthcare can truly serve as the foundation of the health system.14

As a core actor in the global health system, China’s experience is of considerable significance. Since the introduction of Western-style hospitals into China in the nineteenth century, Chinese hospitals have, over nearly two centuries of development, gradually evolved from missionary institutions providing limited medical services into core organisational nodes that integrate resource allocation, performance evaluation, technical governance and policy implementation.510 This evolution has unfolded through major political transformations, the building of the socialist state, market-oriented reforms and more recent reforms aimed at improving quality, efficiency, digital capacity and provider payment.1121 Meanwhile, repeated research and policy practice concerning uneven resource distribution, overuse of high-level hospitals and weak primary healthcare suggest that reforms have more often adjusted the hospital-centred model than replaced it.56 11 2224

Existing research has revealed the historical roots of hospital centrism and hospital dominance in China and has shown that hospitals have long enjoyed structural, institutional and practical advantages over primary healthcare.5 6 Related nursing and hospital operations research further shows how professional hierarchy, workforce status and operational management tools can reproduce hospital-centred patterns within clinical organisations.2532 However, the formation of hospital-centred operational governance has received relatively limited academic attention. Hospital-centred operational governance refers to the process by which hospitals become the preferred organisational vehicle for budget management, performance monitoring, resource planning, management authority and information system development. Focusing on this operational level is important because many contemporary reforms are implemented through management tools that may strengthen hospital-centred capacity even when official policy language emphasises primary healthcare.

Based on this, the present study draws on path dependence theory to analyse the historical roots of hospital-centred operational governance in China from 1835 to the present. It makes four contributions. First, it reframes the issue from the general concept of ‘hospital dominance’ to the more specific concept of ‘operational governance’. Second, it develops a seven-stage historical framework (see table 1 and online supplemental appendix 1) to explain how this governance model emerged and why it has persisted. Third, it makes the evidentiary chain between documents, mechanisms and interpretation explicit through a timetable and a mechanism matrix. Fourth, it interprets the significance of systemic healthcare operational reform from a global health perspective, arguing that payment, budgeting and digitalisation reforms will remain incomplete if they are not aligned with changes in power allocation, incentive structures and resource allocation mechanisms.

Methods

Study design and analytical framework

This study adopts a historical institutionalist approach and uses path dependence theory as its primary analytical framework to examine the historical roots of hospital-centred operational governance in China.3344 Path dependence theory emphasises that early institutional arrangements exert a lasting influence on subsequent development and generate institutional lock-in through self-reinforcing mechanisms, making deviation from the existing path difficult even after the relevant problems have been widely recognised. In health system research, this perspective helps explain why provider-dominated structures, performance evaluation systems, resource allocation patterns and administrative authority can remain persistent across repeated reform cycles.

The analysis uses four concepts. Critical junctures refer to periods in which disruption or reform opens space for institutional choice. Institutional layering refers to the addition of new rules, functions or expectations to existing organisations rather than replacing them. Self-reinforcement refers to processes through which early advantages generate further advantages. Lock-in refers to the point at which alternative pathways become increasingly costly because resources, professional identities, administrative routines and public expectations have become organised around the dominant pathway.3343

Data sources

This study draws on five types of materials: historical studies of missionary hospitals, modern Chinese medicine, public health and early hospitals; government and policy documents from different periods; institutional historical materials and publications related to hospital management; peer-reviewed literature on hospital development, healthcare reform, primary healthcare and Chinese political economy; and comparative global health literature on primary care, universal health coverage and hospital-centred service delivery.13245

Sources were included when they met at least one of five criteria: they described changes in hospital organisation or management; addressed the relationship between hospitals and primary healthcare; analysed financing, professional authority, policy implementation, operational management or governance mechanisms; provided comparative context from other health systems; or discussed the global health relevance of primary healthcare, universal health coverage and hospital-centred service delivery. Purely clinical studies without institutional relevance, commentaries without empirical or historical content and materials unrelated to health system organisation were excluded. Evidence from historical accounts, policy documents, institutional histories and health policy studies was triangulated where possible.

Analytical strategy

The analysis proceeded in five steps. First, the historical record was divided into seven phases according to major changes in political authority, financing logic, administrative capacity and reform orientation. Second, documentary materials were coded for recurring institutional functions, including resource allocation, professional formation, public health responsibility, administrative reporting, payment incentives, technology adoption, patient-flow management and data governance. Third, each period was examined against plausible alternative pathways, including community-based, primary care-oriented or decentralised governance. Fourth, four mechanisms were derived iteratively by combining path dependence theory with evidence repeatedly observed across periods. Fifth, the mechanisms were compared with international cases to assess whether the Chinese experience represents a unique national trajectory, a wider global pattern or a combination of both.

The four mechanisms are analytically distinct but mutually reinforcing. Institutional steering placed hospitals at the centre of policy implementation. Functional expansion added clinical, teaching, emergency, public health, quality-control and managerial roles to hospitals. Managerial standardisation made hospitals increasingly visible and governable through budgets, accounting rules, reporting systems, quality indicators and digital platforms. Market-oriented transformation converted accumulated advantages into further financial, reputational and organisational expansion. Historical evolution of hospital-centred operational governance in China

The position of hospital-centred operational governance in China has gradually taken shape through the accumulation of institutional layers (see table 1 and online supplemental appendix 1). In the course of health system development, each major historical period introduced new organisational capacities, governance functions or policy expectations for hospitals, thereby further deepening their integration into the health system. Over time, hospitals evolved from simple providers of health services into hubs of operational governance through which the state and health administrative authorities organise budgets, personnel, quality supervision, technology adoption, data infrastructure and performance control.51012

The phase of missionary introduction and early administrative formation (1835–1911)

The establishment of the Canton Ophthalmic Hospital in 1835 marked the beginning of Western-style hospital organisation in China (see table 1 and online supplemental appendix 1).79 Early missionary hospitals were limited in scale, highly uneven in geographic distribution and dependent on missionaries and churches, but their historical significance lay in their organisational form.6468 They brought together bounded premises, patient reception, dispensaries, wards, staff hierarchy, records, admission rules, material supply and administrative order.7964 Compared with household-based healing, itinerant practitioners and local charitable care, hospitals made medicine administratively visible by coordinating resources, processes, personnel and patient movement.7964

This period did not make hospitals dominant in scale or system influence. Its importance was that it established a template for healthcare as a managed institution. Hospitals gradually became identifiable spaces in which medical services, material inputs, personnel labour and patient mobility could be organised in a relatively systematic manner. They also familiarised local actors with administrative practices associated with modern institutionalised medicine. This early linkage between healthcare delivery and institutionalised management created a foundation for later path dependence in subsequent reform processes.

The phase of early institutional formation (1911–1928)

Following the 1911 Revolution (Xinhai Revolution), hospitals assumed a more prominent role in China’s emerging modern health sector (see table 1 and online supplemental appendix 1).6971 Organisational structures began to differentiate, with increasing attention to staffing, procurement, financial management, service coordination and expenditure control.6972 As hospitals received public and private capital investment, they became increasingly reliant on formal budgeting and accounting systems and management activities expanded from routine administration to operational control.9 10

The significance of this period lies in the transformation of hospitals from clinical facilities into administrative organisations. Community-based alternatives existed, but they lacked comparable professional prestige and stable fiscal support. Hospitals therefore gained legitimacy not only as treatment facilities but also as institutions requiring efficiency, accountability and managerial capacity, thereby strengthening their position within the developing modern health system.

The phase of nationalist integration and public function expansion (1928–1937)

After the establishment of the Nanjing Nationalist Government in 1928, hospitals became more closely linked to national development and public health administration (see table 1 and online supplemental appendix 1).1045 46 7376 Their functions expanded beyond therapeutic medicine to include training, prevention, health education, public health demonstration and resource management.10 45 46 This functional expansion strengthened the institutional position of hospitals and increased their value to administrators and professionals.10 45 46 73

Community-oriented experiments, including rural reconstruction and village health work, offered alternative models of low-cost, community-based improvement, but they often depended on local initiatives, external support or special political conditions. Hospitals were more easily incorporated into national administrative and professional structures. As authorities promoted standardised procedures, professional staffing and organisational routines, hospitals gradually became key nodes linking medical services, administrative power and public health goals within the developing modern health system.

The phase of wartime adaptation and emergency coordination (1937–1949)

The outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War in 1937 led to intensify the need for triage, emergency care, logistics and disciplined resource allocation during the war years (see table 1 and online supplemental appendix 1).7779 Under conditions of scarcity, rising medical demand and administrative disruption, hospitals were required to manage scarce resources, coordinate emergency procurement and control the supply of materials under extreme pressure.7779 They concentrated scarce expertise, beds, supplies and command capacity, while providing visible centres for treatment, training and mobilisation.7779 Fragmented systems struggled to coordinate emergency response, whereas hospitals could integrate professional labour, logistical resources and administrative command.7779

Wartime adaptation did not by itself create hospital centrality, but it strengthened the perception that hospitals were indispensable in crisis. Emergency governance reinforced the expectation that medical capacity should be concentrated where command, expertise and materials could be coordinated. Post-war reconstruction carried forward some of these managerial expectations, especially in relation to budgetary, logistical, disciplined resource use and administrative control systems.

The phase of socialist consolidation and planned administration (1949–1978)

Following the establishment of the People’s Republic of China in 1949, the country built a hierarchical and planned health system in which hospitals occupied a central position (see table 1 and online supplemental appendix 1). Hospitals became key public institutions for personnel allocation, professional training, budgeting, reporting, procedural regulation and technical standard-setting.6 47 Embedded in the planning apparatus, they offered the state a convenient organisational form for allocating resources, monitoring activity, standardising services and implementing national priorities. The socialist period promoted rural cooperative medicine and barefoot doctors, which expanded basic access and became internationally influential in primary healthcare.22 46 47

However, the contrast with primary care was crucial: cooperative medicine was low-cost, locally organised, preventive and less professionally prestigious, whereas hospitals represented state capacity, technical medicine, organisational legitimacy and upward referral. This difference gave hospitals durable advantages in resource claims and professional status, entrenching them as the preferred vehicle for operational coordination even when prevention and mass health mobilisation were officially emphasised.

The phase of market transition and managerial modernisation (1978–2001)

China’s Reform and Opening-up in 1978 reshaped hospital governance by introducing market incentives, stronger financial accountability, reduced direct subsidies, fee-for-service payments, drug mark-ups, technology-based revenue opportunities and efficiency requirements (see table 1 and online supplemental appendix 1).1418 Hospitals were pushed toward revenue generation, cost control, performance monitoring and greater managerial self-accountability.141880 These reforms did not weaken hospitals; instead, they converted hospitals’ pre-existing organisational and professional advantages into market advantages.141880

Hospitals were better positioned than primary care facilities to attract patients, employ specialists, purchase equipment and generate revenue through outpatient and inpatient services. Management tools such as cost accounting, performance assessment and early information systems developed as hospitals became more autonomous organisations balancing social responsibility with financial pressure. The market transition produced a second cycle of self-reinforcement: hospitals became financially stronger because patients and resources flowed to them, and patients continued to flow to them because hospitals were financially and technically stronger.

The phase of optimisation, digitalisation and performance governance (2001-Present)

Since the early 2000s, following China’s World Trade Organization accession in 2001 and later healthcare reforms, China expanded insurance coverage, promoted public hospital reform, hierarchical care, medical alliances, smart hospitals, digital tools and payment reforms such as Diagnosis-Related Groups and Diagnosis-Intervention Packet (see table 1 and online supplemental appendix 1).1213 1921 48 Information systems, hospital resource planning tools and quality management systems increased the technological and managerial complexity of hospital operations.1213 1921 48 Public hospitals became key sites for integrated management of finance, data, quality and performance monitoring.1213 1921 48

These developments enhanced hospital management capabilities, but they did not fundamentally rebalance the system. Large hospitals led medical alliances, provided technical support to lower-level institutions, controlled specialist resources and dominated digital infrastructure. Their stronger data infrastructure, performance tools, political visibility and managerial authority reinforced hospital-centred operational governance. Meanwhile, primary care facilities remained weaker in staffing, technology, organisation and visibility. Recent reforms may modernise hospital dominance rather than replace it unless primary healthcare gains comparable governance capacity.

Comprehensive analysis: four self-reinforcing mechanisms

Across the seven phases outlined above, four self-reinforcing mechanisms underpinned hospital-centred operational governance (see table 2 and online supplemental appendix 1).198 They clarify the causal pathway between historical events and institutional outcomes and prevent the analysis from conflating chronological sequence with causal explanation.

First, institutional steering refers to recurring policy choices that placed hospitals at the centre of service provision, reform implementation and administrative oversight. Because hospitals were visible and administratively legible, they were easier to govern than dispersed community-based providers.

Second, functional expansion refers to the gradual accumulation of roles inside hospitals. Treatment, teaching, emergency response, public health support, quality control, technology adoption, performance reporting and payment management were repeatedly layered onto hospitals.

Third, managerial standardisation refers to the way accounting systems, reporting rules, performance measures, quality indicators and digital tools reinforced hospitals as governable units. The spread of operational management methods made hospitals more measurable and therefore more attractive to administrators.

Fourth, market-oriented transformation refers to the conversion of hospital advantages into financial and organisational expansion. After 1978, hospitals not only remained instruments for implementing national policy but also became active managerial entities operating in a context of competition, revenue incentives and service growth.

In summary, these mechanisms help explain why hospital centrality has persisted despite repeated reform efforts aimed at improving efficiency, equity and primary healthcare. The path dependence discussed here does not imply complete institutional stagnation. The Chinese hospital system itself has undergone substantial change. Crucially, however, these changes have repeatedly occurred within a framework that preserves and strengthens the hospital’s central role in operational governance.

Discussion

This study draws on path dependence theory to extend existing research on hospital-centred health system development in China by focusing on the historical construction of hospital-centred operational governance from 1835 to the present.3343 The theoretical claim is that hospital centrality should be understood not only as a service-delivery pattern, but also as a governance pathway produced through critical junctures, institutional layering, self-reinforcement and lock-in. In this pathway, hospitals became the most visible, measurable, professionalised and governable organisations in the health system.

The study finds that hospital-centred operational governance in China emerged through seven historical phases and was stabilised by four mechanisms: institutional steering, functional expansion, managerial standardisation and market-oriented transformation. The causal explanation is not that hospitals appeared first and therefore remained important. Rather, hospitals repeatedly solved problems that governments and professionals regarded as urgent: making medicine administratively visible, concentrating scarce expertise, standardising professional practice, demonstrating state capacity, generating revenue, producing data and implementing complex reforms. These accumulated advantages explain why reforms often modernised hospital governance instead of redistributing authority across the system.

These findings are consistent with earlier research showing that China’s hospital-centred system has deep historical roots and that public hospital reforms have not automatically strengthened primary healthcare.56 11 1418 24 The present study adds to this work by explaining why hospitals became the preferred organisational carrier for operational tools such as budgeting, reporting, performance appraisal, payment reform and digital governance. At the organisational level, studies of hospital nursing, workforce experience, job crafting, clinical work conditions and hospital operations further show how professional hierarchy, staffing arrangements, managerial expectations and everyday work routines can stabilise hospitals as privileged sites of authority and resource coordination.2532 This body of evidence complements macro-level analyses of financing and policy by showing that hospital-centred governance is also reproduced through the organisation of professional work inside hospitals.

The findings also have global health relevance. Hospital centrality is not unique to China. Japan and South Korea illustrate how hospitals and specialists can remain strong even under universal insurance, while Thailand shows how district health systems and primary care-oriented public financing can support universal coverage with stronger first-contact capacity.5052 Singapore demonstrates a compact model of state stewardship, public hospitals, polyclinics and regulated financing.53 54 Outside Asia, European systems with stronger gatekeeping show that primary care can become a real governing layer when payment, workforce status and patient pathways are aligned.49 60 61 The USA illustrates the risks of fragmented incentives and expensive specialist-centred care, whereas Brazil’s Family Health Strategy shows that community-based primary care can be scaled within a universal system when local teams, public financing and political commitment are sustained.5557 These comparisons clarify that hospital-centred development is common globally, but its consequences depend on whether financing, professional training, data systems and patient pathways give primary care real authority.

Recent evidence on hospital management, workforce distribution, payment reform, performance appraisal and resource allocation in China further supports this interpretation. Management score variation, maldistribution between hospitals and primary health centres, case-based payment reform, resource allocation efficiency studies and historical-institutionalist analyses of public hospital performance all indicate that managerial improvement can coexist with the continued concentration of authority inside hospitals.808691

Historical and institutional sources on Chinese hospitals also show that hospital-centred operational governance was supported by organisational memory, accounting practices, wartime institutional histories, missionary hospital records and national reconstruction projects. These sources help explain how the hospital became a durable administrative form rather than simply a clinical facility.647377 The wider global relevance is that hospital-centred governance should be analysed together with fiscal change, health-sector globalisation, socialist-era health system development and the contemporary agenda for high-quality health systems in the Sustainable Development Goals era.747697 98

The contribution of this study is fourfold. First, it reframes the debate from hospital dominance to hospital-centred operational governance. Second, it provides a seven-stage timetable that clarifies the institutional layering through which hospital centrality accumulated. Third, it develops a mechanism matrix linking documentary indicators to causal interpretation and global health implications. Fourth, it identifies a policy lesson for health systems pursuing universal health coverage: primary healthcare cannot become the foundation of the system if hospitals remain the main sites of finance, data, professional prestige and reform implementation.

This study has several limitations. Historical and documentary materials are unevenly distributed, particularly for wartime institutions and local practices. The seven-phase periodisation is an interpretive device rather than a definitive classification. The analysis focuses mainly on national-level institutional evolution and cannot fully capture variation across provinces, urban and rural areas or organisations. The comparative discussion is purposive and mechanism-oriented rather than a full comparative systematic review; it is used to test the global relevance of the explanatory framework, not to rank health systems.

Future research should combine archival work, subnational comparison, quantitative resource-flow analysis, systematic comparative reviews and cross-country studies. Such research could test how different governance arrangements affect primary care capacity, patient pathways, hospital utilisation, workforce distribution and health outcomes. It should also examine whether digital health, payment reform and integrated care networks empower primary healthcare or simply extend tertiary hospitals’ managerial reach.

Conclusions

Hospital-centred operational governance in China is not merely a recent managerial preference but the product of a long historical process. From the introduction of Western-style hospitals in 1835 to contemporary reforms in budgeting, digitalisation, performance appraisal and payment methods, hospitals have continuously accumulated organisational advantages that have been reinforced through national priorities, functional expansion, standardisation and market-oriented transformation. This path-dependent history helps explain why contemporary reforms have often focused on modernising hospital governance rather than fundamentally redistributing power and resources across the health system. For China, as well as for other countries facing similar hospital-centred structures, the core policy lesson is clear: lasting and effective reform requires not only better hospital management but also deliberate rebalancing of power, incentives, professional status, data capacity and resource allocation throughout the health system, particularly in primary healthcare.
