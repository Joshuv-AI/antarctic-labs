roadDB: An R package for exploring early human prehistory and environments with the ROCEEH Out of Africa Database

Christian Sommer, Jesper Borre Pedersen, Zara Kanaeva, Timo P Streicher, Andrew W Kandel, Nicholas J Conard, Volker Hochschild

ABSTRACT
Macroscale and interdisciplinary approaches are emerging as central paradigms in the study of human evolution, as demonstrated by a growing body of large-scale, data-driven research. This development is paralleled by an expanding ecosystem of R packages that provide access to paleoenvironmental and paleoclimatic reconstructions, as well as advanced tools for spatial and temporal analysis and modeling. However, within this computational framework, direct programmatic access to comprehensive archaeological and anthropological data remains limited. We present roadDB, an R package that enables streamlined access to the ROCEEH Out of Africa Database (ROAD), a major repository of archaeological, paleoanthropological, paleoenvironmental, geographical and chronological data relevant to human evolution. roadDB bridges the gap between the existing R ecosystem and the ROAD archaeological dataset that covers most of the last 3 million years of human evolution across Africa and Eurasia and thereby represents the only openly available archaeological dataset with such an extensive temporal and geographic coverage. In short, roadDB offers the research community unrestricted, transparent access to a richly curated collection of site-level and assemblage-level information, opening new possibilities for large-scale quantitative research. The package strengthens the integration of archaeological and paleoenvironmental evidence in macroscale analyses and supports robust, transparent and interdisciplinary research on the spatio-temporal dynamics of human evolution.

1. Introduction Large-scale, integrative databases are increasingly central to research on human evolution and deep prehistory [ 1 ]. Kintigh and colleagues [ 2 ] proclaimed, that the grand challenges in archaeology require systematically collected data, modeling and synthetic research. Addressing questions that span hundreds of millennia and entire continents requires data infrastructures that combine archaeological, paleoanthropological, paleoenvironmental, and chronological evidence within a unified spatio-temporal framework. The ROCEEH Out of Africa Database (ROAD [ 3 ]) was developed precisely to meet this challenge, providing a curated, relational repository of information on the deep human past across Africa and Eurasia between 3,000,000 and 20,000 years ago. This resource has been widely used, especially in recent years (see https://www.zotero.org/groups/5497463/roceeh/library ). While ROAD offers a powerful web-based interface for data exploration and manual downloads, many contemporary research questions require programmatic, reproducible, and extensible workflows. Increasingly, archaeological inference relies on computational pipelines that integrate multiple data sources, apply transparent transformations, and enable replication and reuse [ 4 , 5 ]. In this context, the lack of a native scripting interface to ROAD has represented a significant bottleneck. The roadDB R package was developed to bridge this gap. It provides structured, script-based access to ROAD data directly within R [ 6 ], allowing researchers to integrate ROAD seamlessly into reproducible analytical workflows. In doing so, roadDB lowers barriers to accessing the ROAD database; it paves the way for multi-scalar and interdisciplinary research about the early human past based on transparent, reproducible analytical workflows. 1.1 Human evolutionary studies at the macro scale In recent years, there has been a marked increase in scholarly contributions addressing human evolution and paleoenvironmental dynamics across extended spatial and temporal scales. This paradigm shift is driven by two mutually reinforcing factors. These include: 1) technological advances that have rendered computational methods increasingly cost-efficient as well as 2) a broader transformation toward open and FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) science [ 7 ]. Within this evolving research culture, data sharing has become not only standard practice but also an expected norm of good scientific conduct [ 8 ], paralleled by the growing institutionalization of code sharing as a core component of transparent and reproducible research [ 9 ]. These developments widened the scope of prehistoric research in terms of demography [ 10 – 13 ] and the evolution of material culture over hundreds of millennia and continental scales [ 14 – 17 ]. The growing reach of paleoenvironmental reconstruction allows the study of the early associations between cultural expression and environments [ 18 – 22 ], environmental resources [ 23 ] or the interaction of early humans with other taxa [ 24 ]. A variety of ecological niche models has been applied to estimate the potential distribution of diverse human species or cultures [ 25 – 43 ] and the niches of faunal taxa [ 44 , 45 ]. By combining paleoanthropological datasets, one can study the drivers of macroscale biological evolution of hominin species [ 46 ]. Geographical expansions are modeled by means of least cost path (LCP) and agent-based modeling (ABM) [ 47 – 50 ]. Increasingly, machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) permeate all fields related to the quantitative study of human prehistory [ 51 ]. These quantitative and computer-driven examples reflect the emergence of data-centric prehistoric research [ 52 ]. The scripting language R plays a central role in this development [ 53 ], having developed into a comprehensive ecosystem for human evolutionary research through an extensive array of domain-specific packages and reproducible workflows. It is therefore only logical to introduce roadDB, integrating the most comprehensive database of its kind, into this ecosystem as a native data source. 1.2 Background to the ROCEEH Out of Africa Database (ROAD) roadDB is an R package that provides simplified programmatic access to ROAD, a curated interdisciplinary database developed since 2008 by the research center “The Role of Culture in Early Expansions of Humans” (ROCEEH), funded by the Heidelberg Academy of Sciences and Humanities and located at the University of Tübingen, Germany, and the Senckenberg Research Center, Frankfurt, Germany. Unique in its spatial and temporal scope it currently encompasses more than 29,000 assemblages from over 2,700 localities, derived from more than 6,500 scientific publications in over 10 languages. ROAD is a geospatial data infrastructure designed to integrate disciplinary information from archaeology (material culture and traces of human modification), anthropology (human fossil remains), paleontology (faunal remains), and paleoenvironmental archives (botanical remains). Much of this information is structured at the assemblage level. Interdisciplinary integration is achieved by situating assemblages within localities and their stratigraphic context, implemented as geological and archaeological layers. Chronometric information derived from these layers, as well as from individual finds, extends the geospatial framework with a temporal dimension. Publications are linked to localities, ensuring that the provenance of stored information can be transparently traced back to its original sources. At the core of this infrastructure lies a PostgreSQL relational database comprising 68 tables and numerous attributes. This structure reflects the high degree of detail and formal organization of the disciplinary data. At the same time, it underscores ROAD’s status as an expert database, requiring both technical skills to formulate meaningful queries and domain-specific knowledge to interpret its contents. To facilitate access for different user groups, several user interfaces have been developed: ROAD Simple Search provides a dashboard offering a condensed overview of sites and broad find categories via an interactive web map and filterable table. Summary Data Sheets present site-specific information in reader-friendly, graphically structured PDF format. AskROAD enables customized downloads of predefined data tables in open formats such as CSV, JSON, and XML through a guided web interface. The ROAD Query Tool (registration required) allows users to formulate interactive SQL queries in flexible ways, supporting complex spatio-temporal analyses. Results can be downloaded as csv files or visualized in the Map Module, a WebGIS viewer. The SPARQL Endpoint provides semantic web access via the SPARQL Protocol and RDF Query Language, enabling interoperability with linked data environments. Among these interfaces, the ROAD Query Tool and AskROAD provide downloadable, machine-readable data in open formats. However, practical experience has shown that the ROAD Query Tool can be overwhelming for many users, as it requires technically correct SQL statements and a comprehensive understanding of the database structure. AskROAD reduces these technical barriers by offering predefined query templates, though its limit is quickly reached with complex multidisciplinary queries. It also requires subsequent formatting and data cleaning for integration into analytical software. These limitations highlight the need for direct programmatic integration within analytical environments. Such integration enhances reproducibility, eases interoperability with other analytical tools, and lowers barriers for large-scale or iterative analyses. 2. Methods The roadDB package provides wrapper functions that establish a connection to the online ROAD database, construct and execute structured query language (SQL) statements, and return query results in a standardized data frame format suitable for downstream analysis. A structural overview is presented in Fig 1 . 10.1371/journal.pone.0359952.g001 Fig 1 Workflow and system architecture. In a typical r oad_get_* function, the user provides inputs using standard R syntax. User-supplied information (such as function type, arguments, and values) is internally combined with a basic SQL query structure and predefined SQL snippets. This creates a custom SQL string, which is then queried against the online roadDB database. The roadDB database is a snapshot of the ROAD production system and updated regularly every three months. The query result received from the database are formatted as standard data frames and returned to the user. Note that query results may differ each time the roadDB database is updated. 2.1 Online database architecture The architectural backbone of the package is the roadDB PostgreSQL database called “snapshot”, which represents a regularly updated, exact replica of ROAD’s primary production system. While the main production database is continuously modified and extended, the snapshot is derived from it at quarterly intervals. This structural separation serves multiple purposes. First, it enhances security by preventing direct access to the production system and mitigating risks such as SQL injection and unauthorized data manipulation. Access via the roadDB package is restricted through read-only permissions. Second, the periodically updated snapshot can be versioned systematically, unlike the continuously evolving production system. This versioning ensures that identical queries yield consistent results, as long as the snapshot is active. The snapshot is updated every three months. Consequently, the approach supports reproducibility of analyses and enhances transparency in scientific reporting, e.g., by stating the snapshot version date. Upon loading the package, users are provided with a URL that provides information about the current snapshot version and online status ( https://github.com/sommergeo/roadDB?tab=readme-ov-file#roaddb-- ). 2.2 Data retrieval workflow The roadDB functions foster interoperability and reusability by replacing the manual formulation of SQL queries, which many users report as a significant barrier. The road_get_* functions assemble SQL statements following the general structure shown below. The specific expressions are determined by the function selected and the arguments supplied by the user. Depending on the function type, the result fields (columns) are specified using the SELECT clause, while the required tables and joins are defined via the FROM clause. Predefined SQL code snippets are dynamically assembled to ensure that all necessary tables are correctly linked. User-supplied arguments are incorporated through the WHERE clause, with conditions and filter predicates constructed dynamically from predefined SQL snippets and the supplied argument values. The function’s level of detail (see section 3.2 ) determines how rows are aggregated using the GROUP BY clause. Results are ordered alphabetically by locality name. Description 3.1 Package overview The R package roadDB is available on CRAN under https://doi.org/10.32614/cran.package.roaddb and the development version is on GitHub at https://github.com/sommergeo/roadDB . It allows users to obtain ROAD data in R through a clear and consistently named set of retrieval functions: the road_get_* functions ( Fig 2 ). These functions provide optional and flexible filtering arguments and return results as clean data frames that follow the tidy data schema [ 56 ]. In this format, each variable forms a column, each observation forms a row, and each value occupies a single cell. This design improves interoperability with the wider R ecosystem, particularly the ‘tidyverse’, whose packages share common data structures and a consistent grammar for data import, transformation, and visualization [ 57 ]. In practice, this means that roadDB outputs can be used immediately with auxiliary tools (see section 4.1 ) without manual cleaning and with minimal reshaping. A key design feature of roadDB is that these retrieval functions follow three Levels of Detail (LOD), which determine the granularity of the output: LOD 1 (Locality-level): Each row represents a locality (site), including attributes such as geographic coordinates, locality type, cultural attributions, and ROAD-derived age ranges. LOD 2 (Assemblage-level): Each row represents an assemblage, including details on contents such as lithic typology, symbolic artifacts, organic tools, or human, faunal, and botanical remains. LOD 3 (Object-level): Each row represents an individual object, such as chronometric dates and publication sources. 10.1371/journal.pone.0359952.t001 Table 1 Summary of all retrieval functions, their level of detail and a short description of contents. The arguments determine which subset of the data is retrieved by applying filters based on geography, time, cultural attribution, or content. Arguments are optional. If no arguments are supplied, a road_get_* function returns all available records of the corresponding domain from ROAD. In practice, however, most research questions require more targeted datasets. Queries can therefore be refined by specifying one or more arguments, for example, to restrict results to a particular continent or cultural period. Arguments can be combined freely, and users may supply either single values or vectors of values. For example: # Single argument road_get_localities(cultural_period = "MSA") # Multiple arguments road_get_localities(cultural_period = "MSA", country = "South Africa") # Lists of values road_get_localities(cultural_period = c("MSA", "LSA"), country = c("South Africa", "Namibia")) The package distinguishes between two classes of arguments ( Table 2 ). First, general arguments are available across all road_get_* functions and provide flexible, consistent filters for spatial, temporal, and cultural dimensions of the data. Second, function-specific arguments are implemented only for selected functions and enable refinements unique to particular domains (e.g., lithic typology, dating methods, or biological taxonomy). Here we demonstrate how a timeline of aoristic sums can be calculated from roadDB outputs with the package ‘aoristAAR’ (v. 0.1.0 [ 60 ],). This ensures reproducibility, even if a later snapshot yields different results. Furthermore, we recommend validating the data retrieved from roadDB, for example by consulting the original literature (available through road_get_publications() ) and searching for newer publications not yet included in ROAD. Note that ROAD is curated by experienced researchers and trained research assistants at ROCEEH, but may still contain errors. The information is recorded to closely follow the interpretations of the original literature and does not reflect the opinions of the curators. Nevertheless, some subjective choices had to be made, as the database is sourced from over 150 years of literature in diverse languages and research traditions, which naturally introduces a degree of ambiguity. This package provides simplified access to the most frequently used contents in ROAD. Users with more specific or complex requests can obtain full read access using road_get_sql() or via the browser interface ( https://www.roceeh.uni-tuebingen.de/roadweb/smarty_road_simple_search.php ). In either case, users should familiarize themselves with the database structure ( https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17720874 ) and the detailed table descriptions ( https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17660207 ). 4. Discussion The scientific value of roadDB lies not only in its disciplinary breadth, but also in its suitability for macroscale inference. As argued by Perreault [ 1 ], archaeology is particularly well positioned to identify large-scale patterns that emerge only through the aggregation of data across vast spans of time and space, a research strategy often described as macroarchaeology. roadDB directly supports this agenda by enabling the rapid assembly of continental and intercontinental-scale datasets, the systematic compilation of cultural, biological, and environmental variables, and the integration of ROAD data with external climatic, chronological, and spatial models. 4.1 roadDB in the R ecosystem roadDB functions as a structured data provider within a growing ecosystem of R packages dedicated to human evolutionary and paleoenvironmental research. It supplies synthesized archaeological, anthropological, paleoenvironmental and spatio-temporal information from archaeological sites. It thus complements specialized packages providing data from chronometric dating (‘p3k14c’ [ 63 ], ‘c14bazAAR’ [ 64 ]), paleontology (‘neotoma2’ [ 65 ], ‘rgbif’ [ 66 ], ‘paleobioDB’ [ 67 ]), paleoclimate reconstruction (‘pastclim’ [ 68 ], ‘rpaleoclim’ [ 69 ]) and geology (‘rmacrostrat’ [ 70 ]). By providing structured access to ROAD, it complements input to tools focused on specific analytical domains, including ecological niche modeling (‘ppgm’ [ 71 ], ‘tidysdm’ [ 72 ]), age modeling (‘BayLum’ [ 73 ], ‘rcarbon’ [ 74 ], ‘coffee’ [ 75 ], ‘oxcAAR’ [ 76 ]), time series analysis (‘baorista’ [ 77 ], ‘deeptime’ [ 78 ], ‘aion’ [ 79 ], ‘datplot’ [ 80 ], ‘ArchaeoPhases’ [ 81 ]), archaeological analysis (‘seriation’ [ 82 ], ‘tabula’ [ 83 ], ‘tesselle’ [ 84 ]), paleontological analysis (‘analogue’ [ 85 ], ‘paleotree’ [ 86 ], ‘zooarch’ [ 87 ]) paleoenvironmental reconstruction (’crestr’ [ 88 ],), and spatial analysis (e.g., [ 58 ]). Crucially, roadDB does not attempt to replace these tools. Rather, it acts as an infrastructure layer, ensuring that ROAD can be integrated directly into reproducible analytical workflows. In this sense, roadDB is best understood not as a standalone analytical tool, but as a connector package that embeds ROAD within a growing ecosystem of computational methods for research on human evolution. As archaeological and paleoenvironmental research increasingly shifts toward large-scale synthesis, comparative analysis, and explicit modeling, tools like roadDB become essential for transforming curated legacy datasets into engines of new discovery. 4.2 Openness, reproducibility, and FAIR principles Open science has become a central concern across archaeology, particularly as research increasingly relies on large datasets and computational methods. Marwick and colleagues [ 5 ] argue that traditional archaeological publications often prioritize final results over the analytical processes that produced them and thereby leave key decisions about data selection, transformation and modeling implicit or undocumented. In computational research, this lack of transparency substantially limits reproducibility as even minor undocumented choices can strongly influence results. From this perspective, reproducibility is not a binary property but a spectrum ranging from studies that present only narrative interpretations and figures to those that provide data, code, and fully executable analytical workflows. Moving archaeological research along this spectrum requires not only open access to publications and data, but also open methods: scripted, inspectable, and reusable analytical procedures implemented in transparent software environments. Programmatic access to data plays a key role in this transition. While many archaeological datasets are now openly available, they are often accessed through manual downloads, interactive web interfaces, or undocumented filtering steps. Such workflows hinder reproducibility by making it difficult to reconstruct exactly which data were used and how they were selected. Here a shift from data ownership to data stewardship, in which researchers treat datasets as shared community resources and document their analytical use in ways that facilitate verification, reuse, and extension is called for [ 4 , 5 ]. The roadDB package directly supports this approach by embedding access to ROAD within a scripted R environment. Queries to ROAD become explicit, shareable code, rather than undocumented actions carried out through a graphical interface. This makes data selection and filtering part of the analytical record and allows workflows to be rerun, modified, and audited. By operating within R, an open-source environment widely used for reproducible research, roadDB aligns with best practices for transparent computational analysis. In addition, roadDB supports the FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). ROAD records are retrieved through structured queries, improving findability and accessibility. Outputs are returned as tidy data frames, enhancing interoperability with other analytical tools in the R ecosystem. Reusability is supported by clear documentation of function behavior, argument values, and database versions, enabling researchers to specify precisely which data release underpins a given analysis. By lowering the barrier between a complex relational database and reproducible analytical workflows, roadDB provides another way of using ROAD as an active component of open archaeological science. In doing so it provides a concrete implementation of the open methods agenda, demonstrating how curated macroarchaeological databases can be integrated into transparent, repeatable, and extensible research pipelines. 5. Conclusion roadDB is a practical R package that enables the integration of open, structured, and synthesized data from the ROCEEH Out of Africa Database (ROAD) into the R ecosystem. Its optimal compatibility with auxiliary R packages makes it a valuable data provider for further scientific analysis in prehistoric and paleoenvironmental research. By facilitating transparent data access and reusable workflows, roadDB constitutes a cornerstone for open and reproducible science.
