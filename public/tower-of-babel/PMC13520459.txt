Anterior periosteal flap elevation to aid reduction and reduce retinacular tension in Pipkin I/II femoral head fractures
Authors: Matteo Giachino, Riccardo Giai Via, Andrea Audisio, Alessandro Aprato, Alessandro Massè
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPipkin I/II type femoral head fractures are rare injuries in which it can be difficult to achieve stable anatomical reduction of the inferomedial fragment. In some cases, the medial synovial fold and the medial retinaculum act as soft tissue tensor, limiting the mobilisation of the fragments and increasing the risk of vascular injury during reduction.ObjectivesThis technical note describes a periosteal flap manoeuvre designed to release tension on the medial retinaculum while preserving its vascular contribution, thereby facilitating controlled reduction of inferomedial fragments in Pipkin I/II fractures.ConclusionThe technique described allows for safe mobilisation of the retinaculum complex and effortless reduction of the fragment without aggressive dissection. This manoeuvre may be a useful adjunct when standard reduction methods fail.

Introduction

Fractures of the femoral head are uncommon: they usually result from high-energy traumas and are often accompanied by posterior hip dislocations or, more rarely, anterior dislocations. Type I and II fractures, according to Pipkin, specifically involve the area of the femoral head below the fovea capitis (I) or extended above the fovea (II) [1, 2]. Generally, surgery is considered when, after the hip has been reduced and imaging has been completed, the fragment involves roughly more than 15–20% of the femoral head and remains displaced by more than 3 mm [3].

The basis for a successful treatment of this type of fractures is achieving precise anatomical reduction and stable fixation; however, the ideal surgical method for achieving this remains a matter of debate.

Over time, several approaches have been outlined, each with its distinct advantages and disadvantages. The surgical hip dislocation (SHD) described by Ganz et al. provides complete access around the femoral head with no risk of accidental vascular damage, provided it is performed properly [4, 5]. Alternatively, anterior approaches, including the Smith-Petersen and modified Hueter intervals, allow direct visualization of anteromedial fragments and avoid disruption of posterior stabilizers, and have been associated with satisfactory outcomes in selected Pipkin I and II injuries [6, 7]. In recent years, hip arthroscopy has become increasingly popular. Although technically challenging, due to its advantages of being less invasive, and allowing for faster recovery, it has established itself as an option for selected cases and accessible fragments [3].

Despite these advances, intraoperative reduction of the inferior fragments of the femoral head remains technically challenging. The fragment placed in the inferomedial part of the femoral head is often bound and retracted by the medial synovial fold and the vessels of Weitbrecht’s retinaculum, constituting part of the vascular supply to the femoral head [8–10]. This may significantly limit the mobilisation of fragments, make reduction more difficult, and increase the risk of vascular compromise in the event of damage.

The most common strategies, such as gentle dissection of the retinacular attachments, may help facilitate fragment reduction, although this is a risky procedure. In contrast, excision of the round ligament, a routine manoeuvre in the surgical hip dislocation (SHD) approach, is generally of little use in Pipkin I fractures.

Therefore, the purpose of this technical note is to describe an additional manoeuvre to facilitate the reduction of the inferomedial fragment in Pipkin I fractures. Specifically, we describe in detail the creation of a periosteal flap of the medial synovial fold, which allows controlled mobilization of the fragment while preserving the retinacular vessels and minimizing vascular risk. In this technical note we provide detailed instructions to achieve safe anatomical reduction, and we discuss the potential advantages of this technique over retinacular dissection.

Surgical technique

For this surgery, a general anaesthesia or a spinal anaesthesia is required. The patient is positioned in lateral decubitus with the affected hip in neutral abduction. Sacral and anterior retractors are positioned to hold the pelvis in place during surgery. After antibiotic prophylaxis, a sterile field is prepared, the lower limb to be operated on is placed in a stockinet, and a Mayo bag is positioned anteriorly to allow hip dislocation. Both Kocher-Langenbeck and Gibson skin incisions can be developed depending on the patient’s habitus, fracture type (eg, associated posterior wall fracture), or the surgeon’s preference. The tensor fascia lata is incised, the trochanteric bursae is removed, and the abductors and extrarotators are exposed. As in standard SHD, the trochanter flip is developed, and the trigastric fragment is fully mobilised. Capsular exposition is performed by gentle dissection of the gluteus minimus and the iliocapsularis muscle. A standard Z-capsulotomy is performed, and the femoral head is visualized. At this point, the fracture haematoma may be identified and evacuated.

A 2 mm drill-hole may be performed on the femoral head to assess its vascularity (NOTE bleeding sign). Absence of bleeding from the drill hole should be interpreted as a warning sign of impaired femoral head perfusion rather than an absolute indication for immediate arthroplasty. In young patients with a fixable fracture and acceptable cartilage conditions, our preference is to proceed in any case with anatomical reduction and fixation, while informing the patient of the increased risk of avascular necrosis and possible secondary conversion to total hip arthroplasty. By careful flexion, adduction and extrarotation and the help of a bone hook placed under the femoral neck, the femoral head can be partially dislocated from the acetabular socket, until the round ligament is released with curved scissors placed inside the joint, taking care not to damage the articular cartilage; then the head can be totally dislocated (Fig. 1). This passage requires particular caution due to the risk of further displacement of the fracture and the potential worsening of vascular damage. A tip is to perform a temporary K-wire fixation of the inferior fragment before dislocation. The inferior fragment is now debrided from clots and should be reduced. At this point, the anatomical reduction is often limited by the tension of the inferior retinaculum (Weitbrecht’s ligament) that, if stretched, could impair the inferior vascularization (Fig. 2). The anterior periosteal flap can be used to reduce the tension of the inferior retinaculum (Fig. 3). An ‘L’ incision of the periosteum is performed, starting from the head-neck junction, pointing at the anterosuperior angle of the trochanteric osteotomy. The incision is brought down to the insertion of the vastus intermedius muscle. The incision should be guided by anatomical landmarks rather than fixed millimetric measurements, remaining extra-articular on the anterosuperior femoral neck and avoiding posterior or superior extension toward the posterosuperior retinaculum, where the terminal branches of the medial femoral circumflex artery are localized. With a sharp elevator, the periosteum can be peeled off the entire antero-inferior part of the femoral neck from the short branch of the L to the lesser trochanter. At this point, the periosteal flap will include the inferior retinaculum, now freed from any tension, and the inferior fragment could be reduced effortlessly. In the post-operative period, the hip is mobilised early, with avoidance of extreme flexion and rotation for the first 30 days. Prophylaxis for heterotopic ossification is administered with celecoxib 200 mg twice daily for 21 days. Toe touch weight is allowed immediately, progressing to full weight bearing from 6 weeks post-operatively. Clinical and radiographic evaluations are performed at 6 and 12 weeks.

Discussion

Achieving anatomical reduction of the inferomedial fragments of the femoral head in Pipkin I injuries can be technically challenging, even when using extensible approaches such as surgical hip dislocation. Although SHD provides complete access to the femoral head and it is safe when performed correctly [8, 9], it may have its pitfalls: the fractured fragment is often challenging to mobilise. The main limiting factor is the tension of the medial synovial fold and the inferior retinaculum, which together act as a soft tissue tie-back on the fragment. Since these structures contribute significantly to the vascularisation of the femoral head [4, 5, 9, 11], aggressive release or forced manipulation to achieve the desired reduction is not desirable [12, 13].

To our knowledge, there are no descriptions in the literature of this specific intraoperative problem in Pipkin I/II fractures. Although derived from the illuminating work of Prof.Ganz on intracapsular hip osteotomies and the modified Dunn procedure, the full development of the anterior retinacular flap has not been specifically described as an adjunct to facilitate reduction in Pipkin femoral head fractures. The present technique should therefore be considered as an adaptation of established principles to a specific intraoperative scenario, rather than a completely novel surgical concept.

Although several approaches have been proposed for the fixation of femoral head fractures, including anterior exposure [6, 7], SHD modifications [8, 9], arthroscopic techniques in selected cases [3] and excision of fragments when reduction is not possible [10], none of them specifically addresses the scenario in which an intact and tense retinaculum prevents reduction of the inferomedial fragment [14].

The periosteal flap technique was first described by Ganz and is currently commonly used in the treatment of intracapsular abnormalities of the femur, such as slipped capital femoral epiphysis or Perthes disease [15]. The posterior flap, which is technically more challenging, is designed to protect the superior retinaculum, while the anterior flap, which is simpler, allows the inferior retinaculum to be easily freed.

The periosteal flap manoeuvre described in this technical note aims precisely to address this difficulty and limitation. By lifting the periosteum on the anteroinferior femoral neck and thus incorporating the retinaculum within the flap, it is possible to mobilise the retinaculum tissue in a controlled manner without sacrificing its continuity. In practice, once the retinaculum is freed from its tension, the fragment often returns to its anatomical position with minimal reduction manoeuvres.

The main indication for this maneuver is the presence of an inferomedial fragment that cannot be anatomically reduced due to tension of the medial retinaculum, despite adequate exposure through surgical hip dislocation.

This situation is typically encountered when the fragment appears tethered and cannot be mobilised without excessive traction or risk of retinacular injury. More broadly, this technique may be considered in most cases of open reduction of inferomedial femoral head fragments, as retinacular tension is almost invariably present and may hinder controlled reduction.

Conversely, this technique may not be necessary when the fragment is freely mobile and can be reduced without resistance. Caution is also advised in cases with suspected severe vascular compromise, where additional soft tissue manipulation should be minimized.

The flap must be large enough to allow tension release at the level of the retinaculum, but not so aggressive as to risk devascularisation or periosteal stripping beyond what is necessary.

To our knowledge, this specific application of periosteal flap principles to facilitate reduction of an inferomedial fragment constrained by the medial retinaculum has not been widely described.

Initial experience suggests that this is a reproducible and anatomically valid technique; however, a larger series of cases with post-operative vascular assessment, perhaps using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), would be essential to confirm its safety and long-term value.

Conclusion

This technical note describes a periosteal flap manoeuvre designed to overcome a specific obstacle that has not been addressed to date in the management of Pipkin I/II-type femoral head fractures: the difficulty of reducing the inferomedial fragment constrained by tension in the medial retinaculum. By lifting the anterior periosteum and incorporating the medial retinaculum, a flap is created that reduces fragment retraction while preserving vascular supply. In our experience, this facilitates reduction manoeuvres of the fractured fragment without the need for forced reduction manoeuvres or dangerous dissection of the retinaculum. Although the anatomical and clinical reasons are compelling, this is, to our knowledge, the first description of such an approach; therefore, further validation in a prospective clinical series with careful vascular and functional follow-up is necessary.
