Human-inspired hyperparameter optimization for long-horizon forecasting of freshwater and desalination per-capita dynamics
Authors: Sarah M Alhammad, El-Sayed M El-kenawy, Ebrahim A Mattar, Marwa Radwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGrowing imbalance between freshwater availability and demand, driven by population growth, infrastructure constraints, and climate variability, has increased the need for reliable long-horizon forecasting of freshwater withdrawal and desalination dynamics on a per-capita basis. Although transformer-based models can capture complex temporal dependencies, their performance is highly sensitive to hyperparameter configuration, especially for multivariate and nonstationary environmental data. This study proposes a data-driven framework that integrates the Frequency Enhanced Decomposed Transformer (FEDformer) with the Improved Horse Herd Optimization algorithm (iHOW) for systematic hyperparameter optimization. The framework constructs policy-relevant per-capita indicators from country-level time-series data and evaluates the optimized model through controlled comparison with multiple metaheuristic optimizers under identical computational budgets. The iHOW-optimized FEDformer achieves the best overall performance, with a mean squared error of , root mean squared error of , Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.986, and Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency of 0.985. Convergence analysis further shows that iHOW reaches lower best-fitness values more rapidly and more stably than the competing optimizers. Repeated-run uncertainty summaries, omnibus statistical comparison, a lightweight hyperparameter-optimization budget sensitivity study, and a quantitative feature-importance analysis further strengthen the empirical characterization of model performance and stability under the evaluated design. Additional robustness experiments indicate that the framework preserves useful predictive structure across longer forecasting horizons, input-perturbation scenarios, temporal aggregation levels, and alternative missing-data treatments. Architecture ablation further shows that the gains depend materially on FEDformer’s frequency-aware, decomposition-based, and multivariate design. Overall, the proposed framework provides a robust, transparent, and computationally reasonable tool for long-term water-resource assessment and policy-oriented analysis of freshwater stress and desalination reliance under the evaluated data, assumptions, and computational budget.Keywords: Freshwater resources, Desalination analysis, Long-horizon time-series forecasting, Human-inspired optimization, Deep learning for water systems, Hyperparameter optimization, Policy-oriented forecastingSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Freshwater resources constitute a foundational element of socio-economic development, public health, food security, and ecosystem stability. At a systems level, the dependence is immediate: municipal supply and sanitation determine disease burdens; agriculture and industry anchor livelihoods and growth; and environmental flows maintain aquatic biodiversity and the regulating services that stabilize regional climates and landscapes. Yet over recent decades, the balance between freshwater availability and demand has been progressively destabilized by interacting forces—demographic expansion, accelerated urbanization, industrial growth, and climate-driven hydrological variability—whose combined effects are now evident in many national and transboundary basins1–3. The resulting picture is not simply a matter of “less water”: it is also about where water is located, when it is available, the infrastructure and governance needed to mobilize it, and the ecological constraints that limit how much can be withdrawn without triggering irreversible degradation4,3.

In regions where renewable freshwater is geographically uneven, seasonally unreliable, or already exploited near sustainable limits, the question of long-term water security becomes structurally difficult. Even when annual totals appear adequate, multi-year droughts, interannual variability, and shifting precipitation regimes can produce recurrent supply shocks, while rising temperatures increase evaporative losses and water demands in agriculture and cities1,5. These pressures have sharpened policy interest in water supply strategies that can reduce dependence on uncertain hydrological inflows and provide a more controllable production profile.

Within this context, desalination has emerged as a strategically significant supplement to conventional freshwater sources, particularly in arid, semi-arid, and water-stressed regions with access to saline water bodies. The technological trajectory—especially for reverse osmosis—has been characterized by improved membrane performance, better process integration, and energy recovery systems that collectively reduced specific energy consumption and expanded feasible deployment6,7. Economic and operational analyses nevertheless underline that desalination remains energy-intensive and capital-demanding, with feasibility and cost-effectiveness tightly coupled to electricity prices, financing conditions, and local environmental constraints8. Moreover, the environmental footprint is not limited to energy-related emissions; brine production and disposal introduce additional pressures, with large-scale global assessments highlighting the magnitude of brine volumes and the need for improved management strategies9. Consequently, desalination’s role within national and regional water portfolios varies substantially across countries and over time, reflecting not only hydroclimatic necessity but also infrastructure lock-in, energy system characteristics, and institutional capacity.

Assessing freshwater withdrawal and desalinated water production on a per-capita basis is especially important, because population growth directly mediates the effective availability of water resources and conditions how aggregate supply statistics translate into experienced scarcity. Per-capita indicators support comparability across countries with very different population sizes and enable long-term trend analyses in a normalized space that is more interpretable for sustainability and equity discussions10,11. However, modeling per-capita freshwater and desalination dynamics is challenging. The underlying processes are nonlinear and temporally dependent: capacity expansions occur in discrete steps; policy and pricing introduce delayed behavioral responses; and climate anomalies can introduce transient shocks that propagate over multiple years. Accordingly, the forecasting problem addressed in this study is not merely one of extrapolating annual trends, but of learning long-horizon, multivariate, country-scale water dynamics under nonstationary socio-hydrological conditions. Any empirical modeling strategy therefore needs to accommodate long-range dependencies, multivariate coupling, potential regime changes, and the possibility that the relative importance of freshwater withdrawal, desalination production, and population pressure evolves over time.

Recent progress in deep learning for time-series analysis has created new opportunities for addressing these challenges. Transformer-based architectures, originally introduced for sequence modeling, have shown a notable ability to represent long-range temporal structure through attention mechanisms and to integrate multivariate context without relying on purely recurrent propagation12. In time-series forecasting specifically, a growing body of work has adapted and extended transformers to long-horizon settings, emphasizing efficiency and long-range dependency modeling through architectural innovations such as sparse attention (e.g., Informer) and decomposition mechanisms (e.g., Autoformer)13,14. These developments make transformer-family models particularly relevant for national-scale water forecasting, where the objective is to anticipate the joint evolution of freshwater and desalination per-capita indicators over extended planning horizons rather than only to fit short-term fluctuations. This study is therefore motivated by the need for a forecasting framework that is simultaneously long-horizon, multivariate, and sufficiently robust to nonstationary water-resource dynamics.

Despite the appeal of transformer-based forecasting, their practical effectiveness is highly sensitive to hyperparameter configuration. Architectural parameters (e.g., model width, depth, attention heads), optimization settings (e.g., learning rate schedules, batch size), and regularization choices (e.g., dropout, weight decay) jointly shape convergence behavior, stability, and generalization—often in ways that are not predictable from theory alone15. The sensitivity becomes more consequential in long-horizon tasks, where error propagation, distribution shift across lead times, and subtle instabilities can produce large downstream performance gaps.

This sensitivity is particularly salient for FEDformer, a frequency-enhanced decomposed transformer designed for long-term series forecasting. By incorporating frequency-domain structure into the modeling pipeline, FEDformer aims to represent long-range temporal patterns more effectively than purely time-domain attention alone16. In practice, however, the benefits of this design can be attenuated by suboptimal hyperparameter settings: frequency-related components, decomposition choices, and training dynamics interact with standard transformer hyperparameters, creating a search landscape that is typically nonconvex, multimodal, and evaluation-expensive.

Conventional tuning strategies such as grid search and random search remain widely used because they are straightforward and embarrassingly parallel. Yet they scale poorly with the dimensionality of the hyperparameter space and often require prohibitive computational budgets for deep forecasting models, especially when each candidate configuration entails lengthy training and validation15. When evaluation budgets are constrained, naive sampling approaches also risk missing narrow basins of good performance, which is a practical concern for models whose generalization depends on delicate training stability.

Population-based metaheuristic optimization offers an alternative paradigm. Rather than exhaustively enumerating configurations, metaheuristics balance exploration and exploitation through adaptive sampling of the search space, making them attractive when gradients with respect to hyperparameters are unavailable and objective surfaces are rugged or noisy17,18. Motivated by these considerations, this study investigates the use of a previously published human-inspired metaheuristic—the Improved Horse Herd Optimization Wrapper (iHOW)—as a structured mechanism for optimizing FEDformer hyperparameters. The central problem addressed is whether integrating iHOW into the configuration process can yield systematic improvements in forecasting performance and training stability for freshwater and desalination per-capita time series, relative to baseline configurations and alternative optimization strategies, under controlled experimental conditions.

To sharpen the scope of the study, the manuscript is organized around three guiding research questions. RQ1: Can a frequency-aware transformer provide robust long-horizon forecasts for national freshwater and desalination indicators under temporal nonstationarity? RQ2: Does iHOW improve FEDformer hyperparameter optimization relative to established metaheuristics under a fixed and fair computational budget? RQ3: Are the resulting forecasts robust to horizon length, scenario perturbations, temporal aggregation, missing-data treatment, and architectural simplification?

The overarching objective of this study is to develop and analyze a data-driven forecasting framework capable of modeling the temporal evolution of freshwater and desalination per-capita indicators using country-level time-series data. Within this broader aim, the study focuses on the role of metaheuristic hyperparameter optimization in enhancing the performance and reliability of transformer-based long-horizon forecasting models.

More specifically, the work targets the construction of an end-to-end forecasting pipeline that transforms heterogeneous water production, withdrawal, and population data into coherent per-capita indicators suitable for learning and evaluation. A further objective is the incorporation of the iHOW optimization algorithm as a hyperparameter tuning mechanism for FEDformer, with the goal of identifying high-quality configurations under realistic computational budgets—without relying on exhaustive search. In addition, the study aims to conduct a controlled evaluation of iHOW-assisted tuning by benchmarking against baseline FEDformer configurations and several established metaheuristic optimizers, holding constant the experimental protocol and resource allocation. Finally, the study evaluates model behavior using complementary error-based and goodness-of-fit metrics to support a more hydrologically interpretable assessment of forecasting quality19.

In line with the research questions above, the study also aims to assess the robustness of the proposed framework beyond single-point predictive performance. Specifically, the objectives now include examining how forecast quality changes across alternative prediction horizons, under input perturbation scenarios, across temporal aggregation levels, and under different missing-data handling strategies. A further objective is to isolate the contribution of key architectural components of the FEDformer-based framework and to evaluate the computational efficiency of iHOW relative to competing optimizers under the same resource constraints.

This work makes several contributions at the intersection of water resource modeling, deep time-series forecasting, and metaheuristic optimization. First, it articulates a coherent preprocessing and feature-construction pathway that converts heterogeneous national-scale water and population measurements into per-capita indicators that are analytically comparable across countries and informative over time. The intent is not merely numerical normalization; rather, the per-capita formulation aligns the modeling target with a policy-relevant notion of availability and demand pressure, consistent with established perspectives on water scarcity assessment11.

Second, the study proposes a hybrid modeling approach coupling FEDformer with iHOW-based hyperparameter optimization, positioning the optimizer as an external mechanism that improves model configuration without changing the underlying architecture. Third, the work implements a controlled comparative analysis where iHOW is evaluated alongside multiple metaheuristic optimizers under identical training budgets and validation settings, allowing relative optimization behavior to be interpreted in a fair setting17,18. Finally, the study adopts a comprehensive evaluation protocol informed by hydrological model assessment practice, using complementary metrics to characterize accuracy, goodness-of-fit, and robustness across horizons19.

Building on these foundations, the manuscript makes the following contributions more explicitly. First, it presents a per-capita forecasting framework that converts heterogeneous freshwater, desalination, and population records into a multivariate representation suitable for long-horizon national-scale learning and evaluation. Second, it couples FEDformer with the iHOW optimization mechanism, thereby linking a frequency-aware transformer architecture with a dedicated human-inspired hyperparameter search strategy. Third, it conducts a controlled optimizer comparison in which iHOW is assessed against several established metaheuristic algorithms under matched budgets and validation conditions. Fourth, it supplements endpoint performance comparisons with convergence analysis to examine optimizer behavior in terms of search speed and stability. Fifth, it introduces robustness and sensitivity analyses covering forecasting horizon variation, scenario perturbations, and temporal aggregation effects. Sixth, it incorporates a targeted missing-data handling study to evaluate the implications of alternative treatments for incomplete desalination-related observations. Seventh, it adds a model architecture study to isolate the contributions of frequency-domain components, decomposition, and multivariate coupling within the proposed framework. Eighth, it reports a computational-efficiency analysis that complements predictive accuracy with resource-oriented evidence relevant to practical deployment. Ninth, it supplements the main predictive comparison with repeated-run uncertainty summaries, omnibus statistical evidence, lightweight budget sensitivity analysis, and quantitative feature-importance analysis. Although the optimization framework is conceptually portable to related forecasting settings, external generalization beyond the present task and dataset is not empirically validated in this study.

The remainder of this paper is organized to reflect a logical progression from background to methodology and analysis. “Related work” reviews related literature on data-driven water resource modeling, deep learning approaches for long-horizon time-series forecasting, and metaheuristic hyperparameter optimization. “Materials and methods” details the dataset, preprocessing methodology, forecasting model, and optimization framework adopted in this study. “Experimental setup” describes the experimental setup and validation strategy. “Results” presents the empirical findings. “Discussion” discusses the results, their implications, and limitations. Finally, “Conclusion” concludes the paper and outlines potential directions for future research.

More specifically, the empirical section extends beyond core predictive comparisons to include convergence behavior across optimizers, forecast-horizon sensitivity, scenario-based sensitivity, temporal-aggregation sensitivity, missing-data handling analysis, model architecture ablation, hyperparameter search-space clarification through the selected configuration analysis, computational-efficiency assessment, statistical uncertainty analysis, budget-sensitivity analysis, and quantitative feature-importance assessment. This expanded structure is intended to provide a more comprehensive and transparent account of how and why the proposed iHOW + FEDformer framework performs under varied modeling assumptions and decision-relevant conditions.

Related work

Water scarcity indicators and the empirics of desalination expansion

A central methodological premise of this paper is that per-capita freshwater availability and per-capita desalination production are not merely descriptive indicators, but dynamic signals reflecting interacting demographic, infrastructural, economic, and technological processes. Foundational global assessments established the importance of tracking freshwater resources with long-term, comparable indicators. Gleick’s early synthesis framed “water in crisis” as a systems problem where population pressure, governance, and consumption patterns jointly shape availability trajectories, implying that purely local or short-range analyses miss structurally important components of water stress 20. In a similar spirit, Shiklomanov’s appraisal of world water resources emphasized the heterogeneity of national and regional water endowments and highlighted why per-capita measures (rather than absolute totals) are essential for cross-country comparability and for interpreting scarcity in the context of population growth 21. While these works were not forecast-centric in the modern machine learning sense, they established the conceptual legitimacy of long-horizon analyses of per-capita water indicators, which is precisely the regime targeted by long-horizon sequence models.

As desalination scaled from a specialized option into a strategic supply component for many arid and semi-arid regions, the research focus broadened from feasibility toward system-level implications and constraints. Elimelech and Phillip’s review in Science made two points that remain methodologically consequential for forecasting: (i) energy use is a binding constraint that couples desalination trajectories to energy prices and technology improvements, and (ii) environmental externalities (especially brine disposal) can introduce policy-driven nonlinearities in deployment trends 6. Complementing this, Ghaffour et al. surveyed renewable-energy-driven desalination technologies, underscoring that technology pathways (e.g., coupling with solar/wind) can alter cost curves and thereby shift adoption dynamics over time 8. These analyses motivate the expectation that desalination series may exhibit regime changes and nonstationary behavior—features that challenge classical linear time-series models and strengthen the case for flexible nonlinear forecasters.

A further step toward global empirical grounding is provided by Jones et al., who quantified desalination and brine production at global scale and offered a structured view of where capacity is concentrated and how environmental burdens distribute across regions 9. For forecasting studies, the main implication is that desalination trends are not uniform; instead, trajectories likely reflect regional clusters of technological capacity, policy incentives, and water-stress severity. In practice, this heterogeneity suggests that forecasting models must generalize across diverse temporal patterns, including slow growth, rapid takeoff, and saturation behaviors. Such requirements align naturally with long-horizon architectures that can capture low-frequency trends while remaining robust to localized irregularities.

From the standpoint of decision support, this literature also clarifies that the forecasting problem addressed here differs from short-horizon operational control tasks such as daily distribution management, pump scheduling, or near-term urban demand balancing. The present study contributes instead to policy-relevant, long-horizon, national-scale forecasting, where the objective is to characterize the medium- to long-term evolution of per-capita freshwater and desalination indicators under demographic change, infrastructure expansion, and nonstationary hydroclimatic conditions. This distinction matters because the temporal scale, aggregation level, and planning context alter what constitutes an appropriate target variable, what forms of model robustness are required, and which forecast attributes are most useful for water-resource strategy.

From classical water-demand forecasting to long-horizon, country-scale learning

A large body of water-related forecasting research historically concentrated on short-term urban demand, where operational planning benefits from hourly-to-daily predictions. Donkor et al. reviewed methods for urban water demand forecasting and documented the progression from classical time-series/statistical approaches toward machine learning, emphasizing that forecast accuracy depends heavily on data granularity, exogenous covariates, and the time horizon of interest 22. This remains relevant because it clarifies a key mismatch: many water forecasting studies are optimized for operational horizons and local datasets, whereas national per-capita freshwater/desalination series demand models that remain stable over long temporal spans and can learn persistent structural components rather than only short-term fluctuations.

More recent empirical work has leveraged high-resolution sensing and smart meter deployments to improve demand modeling. Pesantez et al. demonstrated that user-level smart meter data can support data-driven forecasting frameworks that integrate past demand with exogenous drivers such as weather and household characteristics, showing that heterogeneous consumption profiles can be modeled more effectively when clustering and predictors are incorporated 23. Although the scale differs from country-level per-capita indicators, this line of work strengthens two broader methodological conclusions: (i) exogenous factors matter, and (ii) heterogeneity is a first-order feature, not a nuisance. Both points are consistent with adopting architectures that can represent complex nonlinear mappings across long contexts.

At the same time, review work has clarified the fragmentation of the water demand forecasting literature and the lack of consensus on “best” methods across settings. Niknam et al. provided a critical review of short-term water demand forecasting tools, systematically comparing classes of methods and highlighting the importance of selecting models that match the temporal structure of the target process and the decision context 24. For the present paper, the conceptual contribution of such reviews is less about specific algorithms and more about methodological discipline: model choice should be justified by horizon length, nonstationarity, and the nature of the target indicator (per-capita structural measures versus operational demand). This pushes the argument toward long-horizon forecasting methods developed explicitly for extended prediction windows.

Transformer-based long-horizon forecasting and frequency-aware modeling

Deep sequence modeling in time-series forecasting has historically been dominated by recurrent architectures, with LSTM offering a widely adopted mechanism for mitigating vanishing gradients and capturing medium-range temporal dependencies 25. Encoder–decoder variants (including GRU-based designs) further enabled sequence-to-sequence modeling, especially in contexts where inputs and outputs have different lengths or alignment properties 26. Despite their success, recurrent models remain limited by sequential computation and by challenges in learning very long dependencies when the horizon extends substantially.

Transformers addressed these constraints by replacing recurrence with attention mechanisms, enabling global receptive fields and improved parallelization 12. However, naïve self-attention introduces quadratic complexity in sequence length, which can be prohibitive for long contexts. This motivated specialized transformer designs for long-horizon time-series forecasting. Informer proposed probabilistic sparse attention to reduce complexity while maintaining long-range dependency modeling capacity, demonstrating strong performance on long sequence forecasting benchmarks 14. Autoformer introduced decomposition and an autocorrelation mechanism tailored to time series, explicitly treating seasonal and trend components as distinct modeling targets and improving performance for long-term forecasting settings 13. These contributions collectively established that transformer-based forecasting is not merely an architectural transplant from NLP; rather, successful time-series transformers require inductive biases that reflect temporal structure.

Within this trajectory, FEDformer advanced the design further by integrating frequency-domain representations into the decomposed transformer framework. By exploiting sparsity in Fourier/wavelet bases and coupling this with decomposition and mixture-of-experts ideas, FEDformer aims to better represent global low-frequency structure and periodicities that dominate long-horizon behavior, while maintaining computational efficiency 16. This is particularly aligned with per-capita water indicators, where long-term trends, regime shifts, and periodic patterns (e.g., policy cycles or multi-year variability) may be more consequential than high-frequency noise.

A complementary development is the “patching” paradigm for time series. PatchTST (Nie et al.) reframed long-horizon forecasting by segmenting time series into patches that serve as tokens, drastically reducing attention-map costs while preserving local semantics; the work also emphasized channel-independence as a practical inductive bias that supports multivariate generalization 27. The relevance of PatchTST to this paper is methodological rather than substitutive: it underscores that long-horizon forecasting performance depends strongly on architectural design choices that control the granularity at which temporal information is tokenized and aggregated. In turn, these design choices introduce nontrivial hyperparameters (patch length, embedding width, depth, dropout, learning schedules), amplifying the importance of principled hyperparameter optimization.

Finally, it is important to recognize that transformer-based forecasting performance is not solely a function of the architecture family; it is highly sensitive to how locality and memory constraints are handled. Li et al. (LogSparse Transformer for time series) diagnosed two weaknesses of canonical transformers in forecasting—locality agnosticism and memory bottlenecks—and proposed convolutional attention and sparse patterns to address them, establishing that careful attention design is often necessary for stable long-horizon performance 28. This sensitivity motivates the paper’s emphasis on optimizer-driven hyperparameter tuning: if performance depends strongly on interacting architectural and training hyperparameters, then manual tuning becomes unreliable and potentially irreproducible.

Relative to several benchmark families used in this study, FEDformer is especially relevant because its design directly targets three properties that are central to long-horizon water forecasting. First, compared with sequence-to-sequence recurrent models such as Seq2Seq and with state-space or graph-based variants such as NODE and STGCN, FEDformer offers a more direct mechanism for representing very long-range dependencies without relying on purely sequential hidden-state propagation. Second, compared with architectures whose main advantage lies in attention sparsification or efficiency alone, such as Informer, FEDformer explicitly embeds decomposition-based representation so that slowly varying trend structure and residual temporal components are handled in a more structured manner. Third, compared with models that remain primarily time-domain in their inductive bias, FEDformer introduces frequency-domain selectivity, which is particularly attractive when low-frequency structure, multi-year variation, and persistent periodic behavior are expected to influence forecast quality. These differences do not imply that alternative models are uninformative; rather, they clarify why FEDformer is an appropriate focal architecture for a study concerned with long-horizon, multivariate, and potentially nonstationary freshwater–desalination dynamics. They also motivate the later architecture analysis in this paper, where the contribution of frequency-domain processing, decomposition, and multivariate coupling is examined more explicitly.

Hyperparameter optimization: foundations, best practices, and metaheuristic perspectives

The empirical success of deep forecasting models has made hyperparameter optimization (HPO) a central methodological concern, not a peripheral engineering task. A robust literature establishes that the hyperparameter search space is typically high-dimensional, noisy, and nonconvex, especially when objectives reflect stochastic training dynamics. Bergstra and Bengio demonstrated that random search often outperforms grid search in high-dimensional spaces because it allocates trials more effectively across influential dimensions, challenging the then-common assumption that grid search is a reasonable default 15. Snoek et al. formalized practical Bayesian optimization for hyperparameter tuning, providing a principled approach that can be more sample-efficient than naive strategies when objective evaluations are expensive 29.

Subsequent work clarified that HPO methods should be selected not only by theoretical efficiency but also by compatibility with training budgets and early-stopping opportunities. Hyperband introduced a bandit-based allocation strategy that couples random sampling with aggressive early stopping, yielding strong empirical results across many model selection tasks and providing a pragmatic mechanism to trade off breadth and depth in the search 30. These foundations are particularly relevant for transformer tuning because training costs can be high, and poorly chosen hyperparameters may lead to non-convergence or wasted compute.

In parallel, the AutoML literature has consolidated best practices and taxonomies of HPO. Feurer and Hutter’s Springer chapter provides a structured overview of HPO approaches, clarifies typical hyperparameter types (continuous, discrete, conditional), and emphasizes reproducibility concerns such as fair evaluation and the risk of overfitting to validation sets 31. Bischl et al. further extended this perspective in a WIREs review that unifies foundations, algorithms, practical recommendations, and open challenges, including issues of parallelization, benchmarking, and robust performance estimation under limited budgets 32. For this paper, these sources justify two operational decisions: (i) HPO should be treated as a first-class methodological component with explicit reporting and (ii) evaluation protocols (splits, seeds, budgets) must be tightly controlled to avoid conflating optimization efficacy with evaluation noise.

A distinct but increasingly influential strand concerns the use of evolutionary and nature-inspired metaheuristics for optimization in complex landscapes. Claesen and De Moor framed hyperparameter search as a challenging optimization problem and highlighted why disciplined strategies are required given the stochasticity and cost of evaluations 33. Yu and Zhu provided a broad review of HPO algorithms and applications, covering Bayesian methods and evolutionary strategies, and explicitly discussed applicability to deep learning regimes where gradients are unavailable or uninformative for the outer-loop optimization problem 34. In this context, metaheuristic methods are often motivated by their ability to explore multimodal spaces without restrictive assumptions on smoothness.

At the same time, the metaheuristics literature includes cautionary discussions about the proliferation of metaphor-driven algorithms and the importance of grounding algorithmic novelty in clear mechanisms and fair comparisons. Lones argued that many nature-inspired proposals re-label established metaheuristic concepts with domain metaphors, complicating scientific communication and reproducibility; this critique strengthens the case for carefully describing optimizer mechanics, parameterization, and evaluation fairness 35. Similarly, Yang discussed open challenges in nature-inspired optimization, including benchmarking methodology, scalability, and parameter tuning—issues that are directly relevant when such algorithms are applied to HPO for deep models 36. Fister et al. provided an accessible review of nature-inspired optimization algorithms, serving as a stable reference point for algorithm families and their typical operating principles 37.

Recent hydrological forecasting research also reinforces the relevance of optimizer-assisted and hybrid modeling strategies in nonlinear and nonstationary environmental settings. In particular, recent studies such as38,39, further indicate that contemporary hydrology research is increasingly moving toward advanced deep architectures, decomposition-aware designs, and optimization-enhanced prediction pipelines for difficult runoff forecasting problems. The importance of these studies for the present paper is not that they replicate the same model structure, but that they strengthen the field-specific rationale for treating hyperparameter optimization as a substantive methodological layer in hydrological forecasting rather than a secondary implementation detail. Taken together with the broader HPO literature, this emerging hydrology-focused evidence supports the use of a dedicated bounded search strategy when the target process is long-horizon, nonlinear, evaluation-expensive, and potentially nonstationary. More generally, repeated-run comparisons in deep forecasting benefit from uncertainty summaries and inferential context, because stochastic training variation can otherwise blur the distinction between genuine optimization gains and seed-specific fluctuations.

Synthesis and positioning of the present study

The corrected and expanded literature base supports a coherent rationale for the methodological choices in this paper. Water resource indicators motivate the need for long-horizon, policy-relevant forecasting at national scale 20,21. Desalination’s coupling to technology, energy, and environmental constraints implies nonstationary trajectories where flexible nonlinear models are advantageous 6,8,9. Meanwhile, the time-series forecasting literature has converged on specialized transformer architectures (decomposition, sparse attention, frequency-domain blocks, patching) as state-of-the-art options for long contexts 13,14,16,28,27. Finally, the HPO literature establishes both the necessity and the methodological responsibility of systematic hyperparameter tuning—particularly when model performance is sensitive, training is expensive, and evaluation noise can mislead naive search 15,29–32,34.

This synthesis positions the present study as a principled integration: long-horizon transformer forecasting is applied to freshwater/desalination per-capita dynamics, and hyperparameter optimization is treated as an explicit methodological layer rather than an ad hoc afterthought.

More specifically, the present study is positioned at the intersection of frequency-aware long-horizon forecasting and human-inspired hyperparameter optimization in a hydrological application domain. Relative to prior work, its contribution is not limited to reporting predictive accuracy for a single model configuration. Instead, it combines a FEDformer-based forecasting framework with iHOW-driven hyperparameter search and evaluates the resulting system through multiple complementary lenses, including comparative optimizer behavior, convergence characteristics, robustness to forecasting horizon and scenario perturbations, sensitivity to missing-data treatment, dependence on architectural components, and computational-efficiency considerations. This broader evaluation perspective is important because, in water-resource forecasting, practical usefulness depends not only on how accurate a model is at one operating point, but also on how consistently and transparently it behaves under alternative assumptions and constrained computational budgets. Within this framing, model-internal interpretability and model-agnostic quantitative variable-importance analysis should be distinguished: the former concerns how the forecasting architecture arrives at its predictions, whereas the latter concerns how strongly the observed variables contribute to predictive performance at the system level.

The pipeline begins with structured preprocessing of country-level freshwater, desalination, and population data, followed by the construction of physically consistent per-capita indicators. These multivariate time series are modeled using the FEDformer architecture, which leverages frequency-enhanced decomposition and frequency-domain attention to capture long-horizon temporal dependencies. Dataset description

The empirical basis of this study is a publicly available dataset obtained from the Kaggle platform, which compiles longitudinal information on freshwater withdrawal and desalination activity at the country level. The dataset is designed to capture the evolution of water resource utilization across a broad temporal horizon, enabling the analysis of long-term trends and structural changes in national water supply portfolios. Its structure and scope make it suitable for data-driven time-series modeling, particularly in the context of per-capita water resource assessment.

From a temporal perspective, the dataset spans multiple decades, beginning in the mid-twentieth century and extending to recent years. This extended temporal coverage allows the modeling framework to observe both gradual transitions and more abrupt changes in desalination adoption and freshwater withdrawal patterns. Each observation corresponds to a specific country–year pair, resulting in a panel-style time-series dataset with annual resolution. The annual granularity reflects the reporting conventions of national water statistics and aligns with the long-term planning horizons typically associated with water resource management and infrastructure development.

Geographically, the dataset encompasses a diverse set of countries representing a wide range of climatic conditions, levels of economic development, and water scarcity profiles. This heterogeneity introduces substantial variability in both freshwater availability and desalination reliance, thereby increasing the complexity of the modeling task. At the same time, it provides a rich empirical foundation for learning generalized temporal relationships that are not confined to a single region or hydrological regime.

The raw dataset consists of eight primary variables. Two variables identify the spatial and temporal context of each record, namely the country (denoted as the reporting area) and the calendar year. The remaining variables quantify water production, withdrawal, and population characteristics using standardized units. Freshwater withdrawal is reported in terms of total annual volume, measured in billions of cubic meters per year, reflecting aggregate extraction from surface and groundwater sources. Desalinated water production is provided in two complementary forms: an annual volume expressed in billions of cubic meters per year and an average daily production expressed in billions of liters per day. This dual representation facilitates cross-validation of magnitudes and supports alternative formulations of per-capita indicators.

In addition to production and withdrawal metrics, the dataset includes estimates of total renewable water resources, reported in billions of cubic meters per year. This variable provides contextual information regarding the natural replenishment capacity of national water systems and serves as an important reference point for interpreting withdrawal and desalination levels. Population data are reported in thousands of inhabitants, corresponding to mid-year population estimates commonly used in national statistics.

All variables are numerical and continuous, with no categorical predictors beyond the country identifier. The dataset does not provide explicit uncertainty bounds or metadata regarding data collection methodologies, which is consistent with many large-scale aggregated environmental datasets. Consequently, careful preprocessing and validation are required prior to model development to ensure numerical consistency, physical plausibility, and compatibility with machine learning algorithms.

At the same time, the absence of formal uncertainty metadata implies several data-quality limitations that are important for interpretation. Because the dataset aggregates national statistics compiled across countries and years, reporting heterogeneity may arise from differences in measurement practices, administrative definitions, temporal updating procedures, and institutional reporting capacity. These differences can introduce measurement error or reporting bias that is not directly observable in the released records. In addition, country-level aggregation necessarily compresses within-country spatial variability and infrastructure heterogeneity into single annual observations, which improves comparability at the national scale but may obscure subnational disparities and localized anomalies. For this reason, the present study does not interpret the dataset as a noise-free representation of hydrological reality; rather, it treats the records as policy-relevant national indicators whose uncertainty must be considered when evaluating long-horizon forecasts. Since explicit probabilistic uncertainty bounds are unavailable, the manuscript addresses part of this issue through strict preprocessing checks and, later, through dedicated robustness analyses involving alternative missing-data treatments and sensitivity experiments rather than formal uncertainty quantification.

The annual temporal resolution and country-level aggregation imply that the dataset is best suited for modeling medium- to long-term trends rather than short-term operational fluctuations. Accordingly, the forecasting task addressed in this study focuses on capturing persistent temporal dependencies and structural patterns in freshwater and desalination per-capita indicators, rather than high-frequency variability. This alignment between data characteristics and modeling objectives underpins the methodological choices described in the subsequent sections.

Data preprocessing

Prior to model development, the raw dataset was subjected to a structured and carefully controlled preprocessing workflow aimed at improving clarity, numerical consistency, and physical validity. Given the heterogeneous nature of the original variables and their aggregation from multiple reporting conventions, preprocessing was treated as a critical methodological stage rather than a purely mechanical step.

The first preprocessing operation consisted of systematic column renaming and standardization. Original variable names, which included descriptive phrases and unit annotations, were reformulated into concise, machine-readable identifiers while preserving their physical meaning. This step was undertaken to improve interpretability, reduce ambiguity during downstream analysis, and ensure compatibility with machine learning pipelines. In particular, variables describing water volumes were renamed to explicitly encode both the quantity type and the temporal scale, while population-related variables were standardized to reflect their role as demographic denominators in per-capita calculations. No semantic transformation of the underlying data was performed at this stage; the operation was strictly nominal.

Handling of missing values focused specifically on desalination-related variables. In the dataset, missing entries for desalinated water production were interpreted as structural zeros rather than unobserved quantities, reflecting the absence of desalination activity in certain country–year combinations. Accordingly, missing values in both annual and daily desalination production fields were replaced with zeros. This treatment avoids artificially inflating desalination estimates while maintaining continuity in the temporal series. Other variables exhibited no systematic missingness that required imputation, and records with incomplete contextual information were retained only if their numerical values remained physically interpretable.

Because this assumption is consequential, it is important to distinguish between structural zero desalination and generic unobserved missingness. A structural zero refers to a country–year case in which desalination activity is assumed to be absent, so that a zero value is physically meaningful and consistent with the intended indicator definition. By contrast, generic missingness would imply that the underlying desalination quantity is unknown rather than absent, in which case zero substitution could bias both magnitude estimates and derived desalination-share dynamics. The preprocessing choice adopted here follows the interpretation that missing desalination entries predominantly reflect non-activity rather than latent positive production; however, this assumption is not treated as unquestionable. To assess its influence, the manuscript later includes a dedicated missing-data handling study that compares the baseline structural-zero treatment with alternative strategies, including missingness-aware encoding and model-based imputation. This issue is especially relevant for countries or periods with sparse reporting, where the distinction between true non-adoption and incomplete observation may be less clear and may therefore affect policy interpretation of desalination reliance.

Population data required explicit unit conversion to ensure numerical consistency across derived indicators. The original population variable was reported in thousands of inhabitants, which is a common convention in international statistical datasets. For all subsequent calculations, population figures were converted to absolute counts by applying a uniform scaling factor. This conversion was performed once at the preprocessing stage to prevent repeated scaling operations and to minimize the risk of arithmetic inconsistencies during feature construction.

Following unit harmonization, a series of numerical consistency checks was applied to all continuous variables. These checks included verification of non-negativity for water production and withdrawal volumes, inspection of extreme values relative to known physical and demographic constraints, and cross-validation of annual and daily desalination quantities to ensure order-of-magnitude coherence. While the dataset did not provide uncertainty estimates, these consistency checks served to identify implausible records that could distort model training if left unaddressed.

Finally, filtering based on physical plausibility was applied to derived indicators that represent ratios or normalized quantities. In particular, desalination share values, defined as the ratio between desalinated water production and total freshwater withdrawal, were constrained to lie within physically meaningful bounds. Values falling outside these bounds were excluded from subsequent analysis, as they likely reflected inconsistencies arising from reporting artifacts or aggregation mismatches rather than genuine hydrological behavior.

Collectively, this preprocessing workflow established a numerically stable and physically coherent dataset suitable for time-series modeling. By enforcing unit consistency, addressing structurally missing data, and filtering implausible values, the preprocessing stage reduced noise and ambiguity in the input data, thereby supporting more reliable learning dynamics in the forecasting models introduced later in this study.

At the same time, the preprocessing framework recognizes that physically motivated data cleaning does not eliminate all uncertainty associated with aggregated national records. Rather, it establishes a transparent baseline representation on top of which robustness analyses can be performed. This is particularly important for desalination-related variables, where sparse reporting, zero-dominant series, and cross-country heterogeneity may interact with model assumptions in nontrivial ways. The robustness analyses on missing-data handling therefore evaluate sensitivity to observational assumptions and preprocessing choices, but they do not provide formal measurement-error uncertainty bounds for the underlying national statistics.

Per-capita indicator construction

Following data preprocessing, a dedicated feature-construction stage was undertaken to derive per-capita indicators that more directly reflect the effective availability and utilization of water resources at the population level. The motivation for this transformation lies in the fact that absolute water volumes, while informative at an aggregate scale, do not adequately capture the societal dimension of water stress or resource dependence. Per-capita measures provide a normalized representation that facilitates meaningful temporal comparisons within countries as well as cross-country analyses across heterogeneous demographic contexts.

Annual per-capita freshwater consumption was derived by relating total annual freshwater withdrawal volumes to population size. Specifically, total freshwater withdrawal, originally reported in billions of cubic meters per year, was first converted to cubic meters by applying the appropriate scaling factor. The resulting annual volume was then divided by the absolute population count for the corresponding country and year, yielding an annual freshwater consumption indicator expressed in cubic meters per capita. An analogous procedure was applied to desalinated water production reported on an annual basis, producing an annual desalination per-capita indicator that quantifies the contribution of desalination to individual-level water availability.

In addition to annual indicators, daily per-capita measures were constructed to capture shorter-term average consumption rates and to exploit the complementary daily desalination and freshwater withdrawal variables provided in the dataset. Daily water production and withdrawal volumes, reported in billions of liters per day, were converted to liters and subsequently normalized by population size. This resulted in daily per-capita freshwater and desalination indicators expressed in liters per capita per day. The inclusion of both annual and daily per-capita measures allows the modeling framework to capture consistent temporal trends while retaining sensitivity to differences in reporting scales and aggregation conventions.

Beyond absolute per-capita quantities, a relative indicator was introduced to characterize the structural role of desalination within national water supply systems. This indicator, referred to as the desalination share, was computed as the ratio of annual desalinated water production to total annual freshwater withdrawal. By construction, this ratio provides a dimensionless measure of the extent to which desalination contributes to overall water supply, independent of population size. As such, it serves as a complementary descriptor that highlights strategic reliance on desalination rather than absolute consumption levels.

To ensure physical interpretability and numerical stability, bounding procedures were applied to the desalination share indicator. Since desalinated water production cannot exceed total freshwater withdrawal in physically meaningful scenarios, desalination share values were constrained to the closed interval between zero and one. Values outside this range were excluded from subsequent analysis, as they likely arose from reporting inconsistencies or aggregation artifacts rather than genuine system behavior.

Finally, normalization procedures were selectively applied where required by downstream learning algorithms. While per-capita indicators already mitigate scale disparities introduced by population differences, additional normalization was employed to reduce the influence of extreme magnitudes and to facilitate stable model training. These transformations were performed using statistics computed exclusively from the training data to avoid information leakage. The resulting set of per-capita indicators thus combines physical interpretability with numerical properties well suited for deep time-series modeling.

Through this structured feature-construction process, the original heterogeneous variables were transformed into a coherent set of per-capita indicators that explicitly encode both absolute consumption levels and relative desalination dependence. These indicators form the primary inputs to the forecasting models described in the subsequent sections.

Target scaling and metric scale

Although the constructed per-capita indicators already reduce part of the raw scale heterogeneity induced by cross-country population differences, additional feature scaling was applied where required to support stable optimization of the deep forecasting models. In the present study, this normalization was performed using statistics computed exclusively on the training portion of each forward-chaining split, thereby preventing information leakage from validation or test periods into model fitting. The same fitted scaling transformation was then applied to the corresponding validation and test subsets within each replication.

This distinction between physically meaningful indicator construction and model-oriented scaling is important for interpretation. Per-capita freshwater, per-capita desalination, and desalination-share variables retain their original physical definitions during feature engineering, while normalization is introduced only as a downstream numerical operation to stabilize training dynamics, reduce the influence of extreme magnitudes, and improve comparability across model inputs. Accordingly, the forecasting models are trained on scaled representations of the target series when required by the learning pipeline, but model outputs are mapped back to the original output space before final evaluation.

As a result, the reported performance measures are interpreted on the output scale of the constructed indicators rather than on an abstract normalized scale. In particular, error-based metrics such as MSE, RMSE, and MAE are reported after inverse transformation of the predictions and references to the corresponding target scale, so that the numerical values remain consistent with the physical meaning of the modeled indicators. Likewise, goodness-of-fit and correlation-based measures are computed on the same post-inverse-transformation series, ensuring that the reported performance reflects forecasting fidelity in the original indicator space rather than merely agreement in normalized coordinates. This clarification is especially important given the very small error values achieved by the optimized model, because their interpretation depends directly on whether evaluation is performed on scaled or original targets.

Forecasting models

The forecasting backbone adopted in this study is the Frequency Enhanced Decomposed Transformer (FEDformer)16, a transformer-family architecture designed explicitly for long-horizon time-series forecasting under the computational constraints that make vanilla full self-attention increasingly impractical as context length grows. In conventional Transformer formulations, the attention operator scales quadratically with the sequence length, which becomes prohibitive when the forecasting horizon and historical window are both expanded—precisely the regime required for long-run environmental and infrastructure dynamics. FEDformer16 addresses this bottleneck by combining (i) an explicit seasonal—trend decomposition pathway with (ii) frequency-enhanced representations that exploit the empirical sparsity of many time series in Fourier- or wavelet-like bases. This design is not merely an efficiency heuristic; rather, it encodes a modeling prior that long-horizon predictability is often dominated by low-frequency components (trend, regime drift, and persistent periodicity), while high-frequency components frequently correspond to transient fluctuations whose contribution to multi-year forecasts may be limited or unstable.

FEDformer16 is motivated by a recurring property of socio-environmental time series: the global shape of the signal—its slow drift, structural transitions, and persistent periodicities—often carries most of the predictive value when the horizon spans years or decades. When a model operates strictly in the time domain with generic token-to-token interactions, it can allocate substantial capacity to short-term irregularities while failing to consolidate the signal’s long-range structure. FEDformer16 reduces this risk by integrating decomposition and frequency-domain reasoning, exposing global temporal regularities directly within the representational pipeline. This emphasis aligns well with the present problem, where freshwater and desalination per-capita trajectories are shaped by slow-moving drivers (demography, investment cycles, policy shifts), and where the key forecasting challenge is to maintain coherent long-horizon dynamics rather than merely tracking short-term oscillations.

Architecturally, FEDformer16 begins with an embedding stage that maps multivariate inputs into a latent space via value embeddings augmented with temporal/positional information. From there, the model departs from canonical full attention by introducing frequency-enhanced operations that transform representations into spectral components. The central mechanism is that attention is not spread uniformly across all pairwise temporal interactions; instead, the model focuses on a reduced set of dominant frequency modes that compactly summarize long-range dependencies. This selective focus is principled in settings where the signal energy concentrates in a relatively small subset of spectral coefficients—an empirical pattern that is particularly common in environmental and economic series16. In effect, the architecture attempts to preserve the long-range structure while down-weighting distractors that tend to degrade horizon stability.

Within the encoder–decoder paradigm, FEDformer16 retains the structured sequence-to-sequence forecasting workflow while supporting horizon extension without the prohibitive cost of dense attention across long contexts. The encoder aggregates historical information into hierarchical latent representations, and the decoder generates future values conditional on encoder states and previously generated outputs. Critically, decomposition and frequency-domain processing are propagated through stacked layers rather than being confined to a single preprocessing step, so spectral cues remain available throughout representation refinement. This matters in long-horizon settings: multi-year forecasts can be sensitive to small representational mismatches early in the pipeline, and layered propagation of global structure can act as a stabilizer against cumulative drift.

A further reason FEDformer16 is appropriate here is its natural support for multivariate forecasting. Water-resource systems are intrinsically coupled: freshwater withdrawal, desalination capacity or production, and population-linked per-capita indicators co-evolve and constrain each other. Modeling these channels independently can yield forecasts that appear locally plausible yet violate system-level coherence. By jointly modeling correlated variables, FEDformer16 can learn cross-variable interactions within the latent dynamics rather than relying on post hoc reconciliation.

To ensure that conclusions are not an artifact of a single architectural family, we benchmark FEDformer16 against a set of competitors that represent distinct inductive biases for long-horizon forecasting. We include Informer14, an efficient Transformer variant that introduces ProbSparse attention and architectural distillation to reduce memory and computational load for long-sequence time-series forecasting. Informer14 is a particularly relevant comparator because it remains within the Transformer family while emphasizing sparsity and efficient decoding, providing a clean counterpoint to FEDformer’s decomposition- and frequency-centered approach.

We also evaluate N-HiTS40, a neural forecasting model that targets long-horizon stability by hierarchical interpolation and multi-rate sampling. Unlike attention-based architectures, N-HiTS40 decomposes forecasting into multiple resolutions and progressively reconstructs the output, an approach that is often effective when signals combine slow drift with mid-frequency variability. This is a meaningful baseline for freshwater and desalination dynamics, where decade-scale transitions and shorter fluctuations can coexist.

For an interpretable neural baseline, we consider a standard sequence-to-sequence (Seq2Seq) encoder–decoder formulation41. Although introduced in neural machine translation, Seq2Seq41 captures a general paradigm for mapping past sequences to future sequences using recurrent encoders and decoders. In long-horizon forecasting, Seq2Seq-style models can suffer from error accumulation and limited long-range dependency modeling, but that limitation is precisely why the baseline is diagnostically valuable: if specialized long-horizon architectures fail to improve upon Seq2Seq41, then either (i) the dataset offers limited exploitable structure at the intended horizon, or (ii) the experimental protocol requires scrutiny.

To test whether continuous-time latent dynamics offer advantages for slowly evolving processes, we include a Neural Ordinary Differential Equation (Neural ODE) formulation42. Neural ODEs42 parameterize hidden dynamics via differential equations and compute outputs through numerical integration, providing a conceptually distinct alternative to discrete-depth networks. While not a forecasting-specific architecture in the narrow sense, Neural ODEs42 offer a principled baseline for examining whether continuous-time modeling better matches decade-scale resource trajectories.

Finally, we incorporate two convolution-centric baselines. The first is the Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (STGCN)43, originally proposed for traffic forecasting with explicit graph-structured spatial dependencies. In our context, STGCN43 functions as a representative of graph-temporal convolutional modeling and a counterpoint to attention-based methods—useful for testing whether temporal convolutions (combined with a relational structure over variables or entities, when defined) can achieve competitive long-horizon performance. The second is the Convolutional LSTM (ConvLSTM)44, which augments LSTM recurrence with convolutional transitions and has a strong inductive bias toward local spatiotemporal structure. Including ConvLSTM44 helps distinguish gains that come from global long-range modeling (as in FEDformer16 and Informer14) versus gains that come from robust local feature extraction and recurrence.

Collectively, this suite spans frequency-aware Transformers (FEDformer16), sparse-attention efficient Transformers (Informer14), hierarchical multi-scale neural forecasting (N-HiTS40), classical neural encoder–decoder learning (Seq2Seq41), continuous-depth latent dynamics (Neural ODE42), and convolution-centric spatio-temporal modeling with and without graph structure (STGCN43; ConvLSTM44). This breadth is intentional: long-horizon forecasting performance is rarely explained by a single inductive bias, and credible evaluation requires demonstrating improvements relative to heterogeneous baselines rather than within a narrow architectural subset.

Benchmark model configuration philosophy

To ensure methodological transparency and prepare a fair comparison framework, all benchmark models were implemented within a common evaluation pipeline that standardizes data preprocessing, temporal splitting, repeat structure, and metric computation across architectures. In other words, differences in reported performance are intended to reflect differences in forecasting model behavior under a shared experimental protocol rather than differences in data handling or evaluation logic. The specific configuration protocol used for these benchmark models, including the principles governing initialization, tuning scope, and comparison conditions, is described in the “Experimental setup” section. This explicit separation between model description and configuration protocol is adopted to make the fairness assumptions of the comparative study transparent and to avoid conflating architectural differences with procedural inconsistencies. At the same time, the benchmark architectures were not all subjected to a fully symmetric secondary architecture-specific hyperparameter-optimization study. Instead, the optimizer-assisted comparison reported later in the manuscript is focused on comparing multiple metaheuristics over the same FEDformer hyperparameter search problem under a common preprocessing, split, repetition, and metric pipeline.

In the present study, the internal iHOW control settings were kept fixed across runs rather than adaptively retuned for different temporal splits or benchmark comparisons. This decision was made for methodological rather than merely procedural reasons. Using fixed internal coefficients preserves fairness across competing optimizers under the same evaluation budget, reduces the risk of over-calibrating iHOW to a specific validation partition, and keeps the comparison focused on the optimizer’s intrinsic search behavior rather than on additional optimizer-specific meta-tuning. Accordingly, the study does not claim that the chosen internal settings are universally optimal for all hydrological forecasting tasks; instead, they are treated as stable reference settings that allow the search dynamics of iHOW to be evaluated transparently under equal computational resources. The implications of this choice are revisited later through convergence analysis and in the limitations discussion, rather than being obscured by an additional layer of unsupported parameter re-optimization.

By combining a frequency-aware deep learning model with a mathematically structured, human-inspired optimization strategy, the proposed framework aims to systematically navigate the high-dimensional hyperparameter space of FEDformer. The explicit modeling of exploration, learning, and knowledge acquisition in iHOW provides a principled mechanism for balancing global search and local refinement, which is particularly important for complex, nonconvex optimization landscapes encountered in deep time-series forecasting.

For the main optimizer comparison, each metaheuristic was evaluated under a matched budget of 50 iterations and a population size of 20, corresponding to 1000 candidate evaluations per optimizer. In addition, a lightweight budget-sensitivity study is included using three budget settings—20 × 10, 50 × 20, and 80 × 30—to examine how predictive performance changes under reduced, baseline, and expanded search effort. Across these budget settings, the hyperparameter search space, objective function, and evaluation protocol were kept fixed so that the comparison isolates the effect of computational budget rather than changes in optimization target or validation design.

Comparator optimization algorithms

To contextualize the effectiveness of the proposed iHOW-based hyperparameter optimization strategy, a set of baseline and alternative metaheuristic optimizers was selected for comparative evaluation. The purpose of this comparator suite is methodological rather than merely enumerative: each selected optimizer corresponds to a distinct mechanism of information propagation and search control, which in turn exposes different vulnerabilities when the objective function is induced by full model training (stochastic gradients, early stopping, and validation noise) rather than by a deterministic analytic surface. In long-horizon forecasting, the hyperparameter landscape is often rugged and weakly informative locally—small changes in learning rate, dropout, or regularization can trigger qualitatively different training trajectories—so comparator diversity is not optional; it is the only way to separate genuine optimizer capability from incidental alignment with a particular region of the search space.

Within this framework, the primary optimizer is the iHow Optimization Algorithm (iHOW), a human-inspired metaheuristic that models cognitive processes such as incremental learning, knowledge acquisition, and experience-guided refinement to regulate population evolution and balance exploration against exploitation45. In this study, iHOW is treated as the main optimization mechanism driving hyperparameter selection for FEDformer, and its merit is evaluated relative to a carefully chosen set of widely used and well-established alternatives.

Evolutionary optimization is represented by the Genetic Algorithm (GA), a canonical population-based search paradigm grounded in formal analogies to selection and inheritance, and operationalized through selection pressure, crossover, and mutation46. In hyperparameter optimization, GA most commonly encodes candidate configurations as chromosomes and iteratively refines the population by preferentially reproducing higher-fitness individuals. Its inclusion is critical because it provides a classical evolutionary baseline whose behavior is extensively characterized; as a result, deviations in performance between iHOW and GA can be interpreted as evidence of substantive differences in the search trajectory rather than as artifacts of population-based sampling per se.

Swarm intelligence methods are included through Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) and the Bat Algorithm (BA). PSO updates each particle using a combination of its personal best position and the swarm’s global best position, thereby coupling individual memory with collective information sharing in a manner that is often effective in continuous search spaces47. BA, motivated by echolocation behavior, introduces frequency tuning and pulse-related control variables to modulate the exploration–exploitation balance as the search proceeds48. These swarm-based comparators are particularly informative in hyperparameter tuning because they tend to exploit smooth, correlated fitness gradients at the population level when such structure exists, but may also stagnate under deceptive or noisy loss surfaces; this makes them useful diagnostic references when the objective is generated by a stochastic training pipeline.

Physics-inspired and nature-inspired optimization paradigms are represented by Differential Evolution (DE), the Multi-Verse Optimizer (MVO), Biogeography-Based Optimization (BBO), and the Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA). DE is a widely adopted continuous optimizer whose core mechanism is vector-difference mutation coupled with recombination, yielding strong performance in real-valued search spaces and a natural ability to maintain population diversity through differential perturbations49. MVO models exploration and exploitation via cosmology-inspired operators (white holes, black holes, and wormholes), enabling probabilistic migration of candidate solutions and local intensification around promising regions50. BBO models the migration of species among habitats, operationalizing information exchange through immigration and emigration rates rather than through crossover (as in GA) or velocity dynamics (as in PSO)51. WOA simulates the bubble-net feeding strategy of humpback whales, alternating between encircling behaviors and spiral updating to intensify search around good candidates while preserving some capacity for exploration52. Together, these algorithms instantiate distinct population dynamics, which is essential for a credible comparator set because differences in performance can then be attributed to specific search mechanisms (e.g., differential variation versus migration exchange) rather than to the mere presence of a population.

In addition, the Stochastic Fractal Search (SFS) algorithm is included to represent stochastic diffusion-based search strategies. SFS employs fractal-inspired diffusion steps to explore the search space and has been demonstrated to perform effectively on optimization problems that exhibit irregular, noisy, or multimodal landscapes53. This inclusion is motivated by the empirical reality that hyperparameter tuning objectives in deep learning often inherit stochastic variability from minibatch sampling and initialization, so diffusion-style exploration offers a principled counterweight to overly exploitative dynamics.

Finally, the Artificial Protozoa Optimizer (APO) is incorporated as a modern bio-inspired optimizer that models protozoan survival mechanisms such as foraging, dormancy, and reproduction, thereby implementing adaptive regime switching across exploration- and exploitation-dominant phases54. APO is a meaningful comparator to iHOW because both methods motivate adaptivity through multi-mode behavioral metaphors, but instantiate it through different mathematical operators and population update rules; including APO therefore helps distinguish whether iHOW’s advantage is tied to its specific human-inspired operator design rather than to a generic “adaptive behavior” framing.

To ensure a fair and meaningful comparison, all optimization algorithms were subject to identical fairness constraints and computational budgets. Specifically, the population size, maximum number of iterations, and total number of objective function evaluations were held constant across all optimizers. Each candidate solution proposed by any optimizer corresponded to a complete FEDformer training and validation cycle, ensuring that fitness evaluations were directly comparable across methods. Hyperparameter search spaces were defined uniformly and bounded identically for all algorithms, preventing any optimizer from benefiting from an advantaged search domain.

Furthermore, all optimizers were initialized under the same random seed control policy to minimize stochastic bias and to strengthen reproducibility. No algorithm-specific heuristics or problem-dependent modifications were introduced beyond the standard formulations described in their original sources. Under these constraints, performance differences can be interpreted as consequences of intrinsic search behavior and information-sharing dynamics rather than as consequences of implementation asymmetries or experimental artifacts. This controlled design therefore provides a rigorous basis for evaluating iHOW as a hyperparameter optimization mechanism for FEDformer and for interpreting its performance relative to established metaheuristic alternatives.

LLM-assisted methodological support

In addition to conventional algorithmic procedures, this study made limited and carefully scoped use of an open-weight large language model to support methodological reasoning during the early stages of data preparation and feature construction. The role of the language model was explicitly restricted to high-level advisory functions, with the intent of improving clarity, consistency, and methodological soundness rather than automating analytical decisions or influencing model outcomes.

The language model employed for this purpose was microsoft/NextCoder-32B, an open-weight causal language model designed primarily for code understanding and structured reasoning tasks. Its use in this study was confined to conceptual guidance related to preprocessing diagnostics, unit consistency checks, and the logical formulation of derived indicators. In particular, the model was consulted to validate preprocessing logic, such as confirming the appropriateness of unit conversions, identifying potential numerical inconsistencies arising from mixed reporting scales, and reasoning about physically meaningful bounds for ratio-based indicators.

Crucially, no raw data, intermediate datasets, or model outputs were provided to the language model at any stage. All interactions were conducted using abstract descriptions of variables, units, and methodological steps, ensuring that the model did not have access to sensitive, proprietary, or empirical information. The language model did not perform any automated preprocessing, feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, or forecasting, nor did it generate executable code that was applied directly without human inspection.

The use of the language model can therefore be characterized as analogous to consulting a technical reference or an external methodological checklist. Its contributions were limited to highlighting potential pitfalls, suggesting alternative formulations for per-capita indicators, and encouraging consistency in notation and terminology across preprocessing stages. All final methodological decisions, including the choice of transformations, filtering criteria, and feature definitions, were made by the authors based on domain knowledge and explicit inspection of the data.

By constraining the role of the language model to non-decision-making, advisory support, this study avoids ethical concerns related to data leakage, automated inference, or opaque model-driven analysis. The forecasting models, optimization procedures, and evaluation metrics remain entirely deterministic functions of the dataset and the explicitly defined algorithms. The inclusion of LLM-assisted reasoning is thus positioned as a supplementary methodological aid that enhances rigor and transparency without altering the scientific control or reproducibility of the study.

Experimental setup

Implementation environment

All experiments conducted in this study were implemented within a controlled computational environment designed to ensure numerical stability, methodological transparency, and full reproducibility. Particular care was taken to document software dependencies, execution protocols, and randomness control mechanisms, in line with best practices recommended by Springer journals for computational research.

The experimental workflow was executed on a standard scientific computing workstation equipped with general-purpose processing capabilities suitable for deep learning experimentation. The hardware configuration consisted of an Intel-class multi-core CPU (8 logical cores), 32 GB of system memory, and a single consumer-grade GPU with at least 8 GB of VRAM. GPU acceleration was used for neural network training where available; however, all experiments were verified to be executable on CPU-only systems to ensure broad reproducibility.

From a software perspective, all experiments were implemented using the Python programming language (version 3.9). Core numerical operations were supported by NumPy (v1.24+) and SciPy (v1.10+). Data handling and preprocessing relied on pandas (v1.5+). Deep learning model development and training were performed using PyTorch (v2.0+), with CUDA support enabled when available. The FEDformer architecture was implemented using a reference implementation consistent with the original model specification, without architectural modifications beyond hyperparameter tuning.

Metaheuristic optimization algorithms, including the improved Horse Herd Optimization algorithm (iHOW) and all comparator optimizers, were implemented using a unified optimization interface. This interface standardized population initialization, fitness evaluation, constraint handling, and termination logic. Each optimizer interacted with the forecasting model through an identical wrapper-based protocol, whereby each candidate solution corresponded to a full FEDformer instantiation, training, and validation cycle.

To support reproducibility, strict randomness control measures were enforced. Fixed random seeds were applied to all stochastic components, including data splitting, neural network weight initialization, and optimizer population initialization. Deterministic execution modes were enabled in PyTorch where applicable, and non-deterministic CUDA operations were disabled. All source code, configuration files, and experimental scripts were version-controlled, enabling exact replication of results.

In addition to reporting predictive accuracy, the manuscript now also documents wall-clock training and testing time, CPU/GPU utilization, and memory usage through a dedicated computational-efficiency analysis presented later in “Results”.

Using the same 50 × 20 = 1000-evaluation budget across all optimizers also preserves fairness by ensuring that no method benefits from preferential search opportunity. As with other wrapper-based HPO designs for time series, there remains a risk that repeated use of the validation subset could encourage over-specialization to that partition; in the present study, this risk is mitigated through the forward-chaining protocol and repeated experimentation under controlled seeds rather than through ad hoc adjustment of optimizer budgets.

Baseline model configuration and tuning protocol

To improve transparency in the comparative analysis, all benchmark forecasting models were evaluated under the same temporal split, repeated-run structure, and metric suite used for FEDformer. Their implementations were integrated into the same experimental pipeline so that preprocessing, training logic, validation usage, and test evaluation remained procedurally consistent across models. Within this framework, the primary comparison in the paper is designed to isolate the effect of iHOW-guided hyperparameter optimization applied to FEDformer, rather than to claim that every competing baseline has been exhaustively retuned under an identical secondary search study. Accordingly, benchmark models were configured using stable reference implementations and controlled experimental settings, while the explicit wrapper-based optimization layer was applied to the FEDformer-centered comparisons that form the main methodological contribution of this work. The fairness implications of this design choice are acknowledged explicitly in the “Discussion” section so that the scope of the comparison remains clear.

Validation protocol and reproducibility measures

The validation protocol was explicitly designed to respect the temporal nature of freshwater and desalination time-series data. A forward-chaining temporal split was adopted, with approximately 70% of the earliest observations used for training and hyperparameter optimization, 15% for validation, and the remaining 15% reserved for testing. No random shuffling of temporal indices was performed.

To quantify stochastic variability, each experiment was repeated 10 independent times. Each repetition used a distinct but controlled random seed while preserving identical data splits and hyperparameter bounds. This repeated-run protocol enables statistically reliable performance estimation.

Performance metrics were aggregated across repetitions using mean values and standard deviations. These aggregated statistics form the basis of all reported results and visualizations.

The combination of controlled data partitioning, repeated experimentation, explicit parameter specification, and standardized execution environments ensures that all reported results are fully reproducible and extensible by independent researchers.

More precisely, the forward-chaining protocol preserves chronological ordering by assigning the earliest segment of the series to model fitting, the subsequent segment to validation, and the most recent segment to final testing. Hyperparameter optimization is performed only with access to the training and validation portions, so that candidate configurations are selected using temporally subsequent but still pre-test observations. The test segment remains strictly held out during both model fitting and HPO, and is used only once a configuration has been selected. For multi-step forecasting, rolling predictions are generated in a temporally consistent manner from the trained model, with each replication respecting the same historical ordering and forecast horizon definition. This design ensures that the evaluation mimics the practical forecasting condition in which future observations are unavailable at the time of model selection.

The repeated-run protocol is intended to reduce sensitivity to stochastic initialization and optimizer randomness rather than to support unsupported inferential claims. Accordingly, the manuscript reports mean values and standard deviations where available, but does not rely on unreported significance testing or bootstrap-based confidence intervals. This choice keeps the evaluation transparent while still acknowledging the role of stochastic variability in deep forecasting experiments.

To further support reproducibility, the experimental assets for this study consist of version-controlled source code, preprocessing scripts, configuration files, fixed-seed settings, and environment specifications sufficient to recreate the computational setup. These materials are organized to allow end-to-end reconstruction of the preprocessing, model-training, and evaluation workflow, and include a minimal environment definition together with a lightweight verification script for testing pipeline consistency.

Forecast horizon protocol

The horizon-sensitivity experiments reported later in the manuscript were conducted at four explicit lead times: 1-year, 5-year, 10-year, and 20-year ahead forecasting. For each horizon, the model is first trained on the training segment and selected using the validation segment under the same forward-chaining chronology described above. Forecasts are then generated over the held-out test interval in a rolling multi-step manner: starting from the earliest test position for which sufficient historical context is available, the model produces the forecast at the selected lead time, the temporal window is advanced chronologically, and the process is repeated until the test segment is exhausted. This protocol preserves temporal causality at every horizon and ensures that horizon-specific results reflect forecasting difficulty under the same split logic rather than under different data-partitioning schemes.

Statistical and interpretability supplements

Beyond the core accuracy and goodness-of-fit evaluation, the study now also reports additional statistical and interpretability summaries to provide a more informative characterization of optimizer-assisted forecasting behavior. These additions include repeated-run means and standard deviations, supplementary confidence intervals where reported, an omnibus analysis of variance across optimizer-assisted repeated-run outcomes, supplementary one-sample test summaries for run-level metric distributions, and a SHAP-based feature-importance analysis used as a model-agnostic interpretability supplement. Taken together, these analyses strengthen the treatment of uncertainty, comparative context, and variable-level interpretability in the empirical section. At the same time, they should be interpreted as complementary evidence rather than as a full replacement for richer paired per-seed pairwise testing or bootstrap-based pairwise superiority analysis.

Overall, the results demonstrate that while multiple metaheuristic algorithms are capable of improving FEDformer performance, the iHOW-assisted optimization strategy yields the most pronounced and consistent gains across all evaluation dimensions. This outcome highlights the effectiveness of iHOW’s exploration–learning–knowledge integration mechanism when applied to high-dimensional hyperparameter optimization problems in long-horizon time-series forecasting.

Optimization dynamics and search behavior

While the endpoint results reported in “Baseline forecasting performance” establish that iHOW yields the strongest final FEDformer configuration among the tested metaheuristic optimizers, endpoint performance alone does not reveal how those solutions are reached. For wrapper-based hyperparameter optimization, this distinction is important because practical usefulness depends not only on the final fitness value, but also on convergence speed, refinement behavior, and the stability with which the optimizer improves candidate configurations under a fixed evaluation budget. To address this point, the manuscript supplements the final-score comparison with an explicit convergence analysis across all considered optimizers.

Figure 26 shows the evolution of best fitness as a function of iteration for iHOW and the alternative optimization algorithms. The horizontal axis is reported on a logarithmic scale in iteration count, while the vertical axis shows the best fitness on a logarithmic scale as well, allowing large differences in optimization progress to be visualized clearly across early and late search stages. Under this representation, steeper downward trajectories indicate faster improvement in the best discovered solution, whereas flatter curves indicate either slower convergence or stagnation in comparatively weak regions of the search space.

Convergence plot comparing the best-fitness trajectories of iHOW and alternative metaheuristic optimizers during FEDformer hyperparameter search. Lower values indicate better fitness.

A clear distinction emerges between iHOW and the comparator optimizers in both convergence speed and late-stage refinement behavior. The iHOW trajectory decreases sharply from the earliest iterations and continues to improve over the course of the search, ultimately reaching the lowest fitness values among all methods shown. This pattern indicates that iHOW is able to identify promising regions of the FEDformer hyperparameter space rapidly, while also preserving sufficient refinement capacity to continue improving after the initial exploratory phase. In contrast, several competing algorithms exhibit either slower initial descent, earlier plateauing, or both, suggesting weaker balance between broad exploration and sustained exploitation under the same budget.

The comparison with GA is especially informative. GA also demonstrates substantial improvement over the search process and remains the closest competitor to iHOW in terms of overall trajectory quality; however, its convergence curve remains consistently above that of iHOW and reaches a higher final fitness level. This indicates that although GA is effective at navigating the bounded FEDformer search space, iHOW attains stronger best-solution improvement more rapidly and maintains this advantage into the later stages of optimization. The difference is particularly relevant in wrapper-based tuning, where each additional improvement requires a costly model training and validation cycle.

The remaining optimizers display progressively weaker convergence behavior. Methods such as BA, WOA, MVO, PSO, SFS, BBO, and APO show more gradual or limited reductions in fitness, with trajectories that tend to remain substantially above those of iHOW and GA over most of the optimization horizon. Some algorithms improve modestly during the early and middle stages but then flatten comparatively early, indicating reduced capacity for late-stage refinement once the easily accessible improvements have been exploited. Others descend more steadily but do so at a slower rate, which in practical terms means that they consume a larger portion of the fixed evaluation budget before reaching configurations that are still inferior to those obtained by iHOW.

From an early-versus-late search perspective, the figure also supports the interpretation that iHOW maintains a more effective transition between exploration and exploitation. In the initial phase, iHOW reduces the best fitness quickly, consistent with broad but productive search over the bounded hyperparameter space. In the later phase, the curve continues to descend rather than collapsing immediately into a flat plateau, implying that the algorithm retains enough guidance from its learning and knowledge components to keep refining the best candidate configuration. This behavior is consistent with the design rationale discussed in “Hyperparameter optimization using iHOW”, where the optimizer’s internal state structure is intended to support both wide exploration and progressively more confident refinement.

The convergence evidence therefore complements the final performance tables in an important way. It suggests that iHOW is not merely producing a better terminal configuration by chance, but is exhibiting a more favorable search trajectory under the same computational budget. In practical terms, this means that iHOW combines rapid early progress with stronger late-stage stability, yielding lower best-fitness values more consistently than the alternative optimizers considered here. This convergence profile provides direct empirical support for the claim that the exploration–learning–knowledge integration in iHOW is advantageous for high-dimensional FEDformer hyperparameter optimization, and it constitutes the primary dynamic evidence supporting the optimizer comparison in this study.

Missing-data handling analysis

The strong predictive performance of the proposed framework is meaningful only if it remains interpretable under alternative data assumptions and if the contribution of its architectural design choices can be isolated rather than treated as a black box. For this reason, the manuscript supplements the main results with additional diagnostic analysis of how forecast performance changes when missing desalination-related information is handled differently, thereby testing the robustness of the structural-zero preprocessing assumption introduced in “Data preprocessing”. The analyses below therefore examine robustness to alternative missing-data assumptions and representations, rather than serving as direct measurement-error quantification for the underlying source statistics.

Discussion

Effect of hyperparameter optimization

The results presented in “Results” provide clear evidence that hyperparameter optimization plays a decisive role in determining the practical effectiveness of deep time-series forecasting models in the context of freshwater and desalination per-capita analysis. The contrast between baseline model performance and the optimized configurations highlights how architectural capacity alone is insufficient to guarantee strong predictive behavior when hyperparameters are inadequately configured. At the same time, the conclusions of the manuscript now rest not only on endpoint accuracy and convergence behavior, but also on repeated-run uncertainty summaries, omnibus inferential evidence, lightweight budget sensitivity analysis, and quantitative feature-importance evidence. Taken together, these additions strengthen the interpretation that the proposed framework is useful because it remains comparatively strong, coherent, and interpretable under changing assumptions, not because it is invariant to them.

At the baseline level, FEDformer already demonstrates superior performance relative to the other comparison models, indicating that its frequency-aware architecture is well aligned with the long-horizon and multivariate characteristics of the dataset. However, the magnitude of improvement observed after hyperparameter optimization reveals that a substantial portion of FEDformer’s representational potential remains unrealized under default or manually selected configurations. This observation is consistent with the high sensitivity of transformer-based models to parameters governing learning dynamics, representation dimensionality, and attention-related mechanisms.

Hyperparameter optimization primarily affects model behavior through three interrelated mechanisms. First, it improves convergence properties during training. Optimized learning rates and regularization settings allow the training process to progress more smoothly toward well-generalizing minima, reducing both underfitting and unstable oscillatory behavior. This effect is reflected in the systematic reduction of error-based metrics such as MSE and RMSE across all optimized configurations. Second, optimization enhances the model’s ability to capture long-range temporal dependencies. By appropriately configuring frequency decomposition parameters and architectural depth, the model more effectively separates low-frequency trends from higher-frequency fluctuations, leading to improved alignment with observed temporal patterns. This improvement is directly manifested in higher correlation coefficients and coefficients of determination.

Third, hyperparameter optimization reduces systematic bias, as indicated by the consistent decrease in MBE across optimized models. This reduction suggests that optimized configurations better balance the influence of historical trends and recent observations, thereby avoiding persistent overestimation or underestimation. The simultaneous improvement of bias-sensitive and magnitude-sensitive metrics indicates that optimization refines the internal representation learned by the model rather than compensating for errors through superficial adjustments.

The relative performance differences among optimized configurations further illuminate the role of the optimization strategy itself. While all metaheuristic optimizers produce meaningful gains over the baseline, the extent and consistency of these gains vary considerably. This variation reflects differences in how effectively each optimizer explores the high-dimensional hyperparameter space and exploits promising configurations under fixed computational budgets. Algorithms with stronger mechanisms for balancing global exploration and local refinement tend to identify configurations that generalize more reliably, resulting in coherent improvements across all evaluation metrics.

In particular, the consistently superior performance of the iHOW-optimized FEDformer suggests that its human-inspired learning and knowledge-acquisition mechanisms are well suited to navigating complex, nonconvex hyperparameter landscapes. By progressively integrating exploration outcomes with accumulated experience, iHOW appears to avoid both premature convergence and excessive random search, leading to configurations that simultaneously reduce error, enhance correlation, and improve efficiency-based performance measures.

The results strengthen this interpretation by showing that the value of iHOW is visible not only in the terminal metric values but also in the search trajectory itself. The convergence analysis demonstrates that iHOW reaches lower best-fitness values more rapidly than the alternative optimizers and continues refining the solution during later iterations rather than plateauing prematurely. This is an important point, because in wrapper-based deep model optimization the usefulness of an optimizer depends not merely on whether it eventually finds a good solution, but also on how effectively it allocates a limited number of expensive training-and-validation evaluations. The fact that iHOW combines strong endpoint performance with a favorable convergence trajectory therefore makes the argument for its utility more persuasive than a comparison based solely on final scores.

More broadly, the convergence results align well with the mechanism-level rationale developed for iHOW in “Hyperparameter optimization using iHOW”. The optimizer’s explicit separation of exploration, learning, and knowledge states appears to support productive early search while preserving late-stage refinement capacity. Under the fixed evaluation budget used here, this balance is particularly advantageous because it allows the optimizer to exploit informative regions of the FEDformer hyperparameter space without collapsing too early into suboptimal local behavior. Accordingly, the role of hyperparameter optimization in this study is not only to improve FEDformer numerically, but also to provide a more reliable pathway through a high-dimensional and evaluation-expensive search landscape.

Overall, the observed performance differences between baseline and optimized models underscore the importance of systematic hyperparameter optimization in deep time-series forecasting. They also demonstrate that the choice of optimization algorithm is not merely a secondary implementation detail, but a critical component that can substantially influence the reliability and interpretability of forecasting outcomes in data-driven water resource analysis.

Robustness and sensitivity synthesis

The empirical analysis shows that the usefulness of the proposed framework cannot be judged solely by its best-case predictive accuracy. Instead, its value emerges from a broader pattern of robustness across forecasting horizons, input perturbations, temporal aggregation levels, missing-data treatments, architectural simplifications, and computational-efficiency trade-offs. This broader perspective is especially important in long-horizon water forecasting, where practical credibility depends on how performance behaves under changing assumptions rather than only on how well one optimized configuration performs under a single evaluation setting.

First, the forecast-horizon sensitivity analysis confirms the expected deterioration of predictive performance as the lead time increases, but also shows that the degradation is gradual rather than abrupt. The iHOW + FEDformer framework remains comparatively strong at 5- and 10-year horizons and retains meaningful correlation and goodness-of-fit even at the 20-year horizon. This finding is important because it suggests that the model is preserving long-range structural information rather than merely overfitting short-horizon behavior. In other words, the optimized framework does not eliminate the intrinsic difficulty of long-range prediction, but it does appear capable of maintaining useful forecast structure over planning-relevant timescales.

Second, the scenario-based sensitivity analysis reveals that the optimized model is robust to moderate perturbations, but not uniformly so. Changes in freshwater availability, desalination contribution, and population size all affect predictive performance, with the strongest deterioration appearing under certain desalination reductions and coupled perturbation scenarios. This indicates that the framework is learning a system in which water-source composition and demographic normalization interact meaningfully, rather than treating each variable independently. The scenario results therefore serve both as a robustness test and as a reminder that long-horizon forecasts become more fragile when multiple structural drivers shift simultaneously.

Third, the temporal-aggregation analysis clarifies the scale at which the framework is strongest. As the data are aggregated from annual observations into 3-year, 5-year, and 10-year blocks, forecast performance improves steadily across all metric families. This suggests that the proposed model is especially effective at recovering medium- to long-term structural trends once short-scale variability and reporting irregularities are attenuated. Rather than weakening the model’s practical relevance, this result sharpens it: the framework appears particularly well aligned with strategic planning tasks that are concerned with medium- and long-run trajectories rather than year-to-year noise.

Fourth, the missing-data diagnostics show that the main comparative conclusions are not highly fragile with respect to the treatment of incomplete desalination-related information. Although the absolute performance levels vary across the structural-zero baseline, missingness-as-indicator treatment, and model-based imputation, the overall ranking of model families remains stable, with FEDformer consistently retaining the strongest performance. This is a reassuring result because it indicates that the superiority of the proposed forecasting backbone is not merely a by-product of one particular missing-data convention. At the same time, the change in absolute scores across treatments underscores that desalination reporting assumptions do matter and should not be ignored in interpretation.

Fifth, the architecture ablation study confirms that the gains of the full iHOW + FEDformer framework are not reducible to a single isolated design choice. Removing frequency-domain processing, removing decomposition, or collapsing the framework to univariate or time-domain-only variants all degrades performance. The full model therefore appears to benefit from the coordinated interaction of multiple inductive biases: spectral selectivity for long-range structure, decomposition for organizing temporal components, and multivariate coupling for preserving system-level coherence. This strengthens the argument that the observed performance gains are structural rather than accidental.

Finally, the computational-efficiency analysis adds an important practical dimension to the robustness discussion. The best-performing iHOW + FEDformer configuration does not simply win by consuming the largest amount of compute. Instead, it combines strong predictive performance with moderate runtime and comparatively low CPU, GPU, and memory demand relative to the other optimizer-assisted pipelines. This means that the framework’s advantages are not only statistical but also operational: it delivers strong results without imposing the heaviest computational burden among the compared methods.

Taken together, these analyses suggest that the proposed framework is robust in a qualified and policy-relevant sense. It is not invariant to changes in horizon, perturbation scenario, data treatment, or temporal scale, nor should one expect it to be. Rather, its behavior remains coherent, interpretable, and comparatively strong across these changes, which is the more meaningful standard for long-horizon environmental forecasting.

Missing-data assumption and policy interpretation

The missing-data results have a direct implication for how desalination-related forecasts should be interpreted in policy contexts. The structural-zero preprocessing assumption used in the main analysis is physically plausible for many country–year combinations, especially where missing desalination records likely reflect absence of desalination activity. However, the alternative missing-data experiments show that performance changes measurably when missingness is treated as informative or when model-based imputation is applied, which implies that the observational process itself contains potentially relevant information.

This matters most in sparse-reporting countries or in periods where institutional data collection may be incomplete. In such settings, the absence of recorded desalination output may not always coincide perfectly with the true absence of desalination activity. As a result, forecasts of desalination share or desalination reliance should be interpreted with added caution where the reporting regime is weak, evolving, or inconsistent over time. The present results do not invalidate the structural-zero assumption, but they do show that it is an assumption with policy consequences rather than a purely technical preprocessing convenience. These robustness results therefore concern preprocessing assumptions and observational sparsity rather than explicit measurement-error intervals in the underlying data source.

Scenario-based planning relevance

The scenario results also support a more applied interpretation of the framework as a “what-if” planning tool. By examining how predictive performance changes under perturbations in freshwater availability, desalination contribution, and population, the analysis provides a first indication of how sensitive the modeled system is to different classes of structural change. In particular, the larger degradation observed in some desalination-reduction and coupled scenarios suggests that planners should expect greater forecasting difficulty when supply composition shifts simultaneously with demographic or freshwater-pressure changes.

Although the study does not present mechanistic policy simulations, the scenario analysis still has decision-support value. It shows which classes of perturbation are comparatively easy for the model to absorb and which are associated with larger reductions in predictive fidelity. This can support exploratory planning around desalination expansion, future freshwater pressure, and population growth by helping decision-makers understand not only the forecast trajectory itself but also the relative stability of that trajectory under alternative assumptions. In this sense, the scenario analyses have exploratory planning value, but they are not probabilistic decision-support outputs.

Benchmark fairness and comparison scope

The comparative design of this study is controlled at the level of preprocessing, temporal split, evaluation metrics, repeated-run protocol, and optimizer budget, and the revision has made the benchmark-configuration philosophy more explicit than in the original manuscript. This improves transparency and helps clarify that the reported differences are not due to inconsistent preprocessing or evaluation logic. Within the optimizer-assisted FEDformer comparison specifically, fairness is enforced by having each optimizer operate over the same FEDformer search space under matched budgets and the same wrapper-based validation protocol. At the same time, the study does not claim that every baseline architecture was exhaustively re-tuned under a fully symmetric secondary hyperparameter optimization procedure. A broader retuning study for all baselines would be a valuable future extension, but it is outside the scope of the present experimental design. Accordingly, the current comparison should be interpreted as a controlled evaluation centered on the effect of iHOW-guided FEDformer optimization rather than as a definitive ranking under all possible tuning budgets.

Interpretability: what is addressed and what remains open

Interpretability in the present study is addressed primarily at the system, robustness, and component levels rather than at the level of model-internal attribution. What is addressed includes scenario sensitivity, which clarifies how the forecasting framework responds to perturbations in freshwater, desalination, and population inputs; missing-data diagnostics, which reveal how sparse-reporting assumptions affect forecast quality and interpretation; architecture ablation, which isolates the contribution of frequency-domain processing, decomposition, and multivariate coupling; and the new quantitative feature-importance analysis, which provides a global variable-level view of which observed inputs contribute most strongly to predictive performance. Together, these analyses make the empirical behavior of the framework more transparent without requiring access to internal activations.

What remains open, however, is model-internal interpretability in the stricter sense. The present study does not provide attention maps, saliency analyses, attribution-style interaction diagnostics, horizon-specific internal attribution, or partial-dependence-like analyses that would expose how internal representations assign importance across variables and forecast horizons. These forms of analysis could further strengthen policy interpretability and model trust, but they are not part of the current empirical results. They should therefore be regarded as important future extensions rather than as achieved contributions of the present manuscript.

Regional heterogeneity and transferability

A further point that warrants discussion is regional heterogeneity. Because the dataset is aggregated at the country level and spans countries with very different climatic regimes, desalination pathways, energy systems, and institutional capacities, the forecasting task is intrinsically heterogeneous. This heterogeneity is useful for learning broad temporal relationships, but it also means that extrapolation risk may differ across hydrological and policy contexts. The present study does not include region-stratified experiments or climate-regime-specific analysis, so it cannot determine whether the same level of forecast quality would be maintained uniformly across all settings.

Several regimes are particularly likely to be underrepresented or harder to generalize across. These include arid countries with sparse or intermittent reporting, non-arid countries with negligible desalination dependence, countries where desalination is politically important but still low-volume in quantitative terms, and country-level records that compress strong subnational heterogeneity into a single annual observation. In such cases, the learned relationships may still be informative at the national trend level, but they should not be assumed to transfer uniformly to all local or regional planning contexts.

At the same time, the methodological design suggests a degree of transferability in principle. Because iHOW is implemented as a wrapper around a validation-based black-box objective, it is not intrinsically tied to FEDformer or to this specific water-resource dataset. The same optimizer-guided tuning logic is therefore conceptually portable to other nonstationary environmental time-series problems, provided that a suitable forecasting model and a coherent validation objective are defined. This does not establish cross-dataset or cross-domain effectiveness empirically, and such external generalization is not validated in the present study.

Budget sensitivity and compute trade-offs

The new budget-sensitivity analysis adds an important refinement to the interpretation of the optimizer comparison. The baseline budget of 50× 20 was originally selected as a fairness- and feasibility-oriented compromise, and the additional three-level sweep shows that this choice is empirically defensible. Smaller budgets reduce predictive performance modestly, while larger budgets improve the results only incrementally. This means that the main matched-budget design is not best understood as a weak or arbitrary budget cap, but rather as a practical operating point that captures most of the available performance benefit without incurring the full additional computational cost of a larger search.

When this result is considered together with the computational-efficiency analysis, a clearer picture emerges. The strongest-performing optimizer does not require a qualitatively different compute regime to demonstrate its advantage, and increasing the budget beyond the baseline does not radically alter the relative conclusions. Instead, the main gain of larger budgets is marginal refinement, whereas the cost increase is immediate and operationally relevant. For this reason, the matched baseline budget is best interpreted as a pragmatic compromise rather than as a tuning choice that artificially constrained the comparison.

What the new uncertainty analyses do and do not establish

The revised manuscript now provides repeated-run dispersion summaries, confidence-interval summaries where reported, and omnibus statistical evidence across optimizer-assisted configurations. These additions materially strengthen the empirical support for the optimizer comparison because they move the discussion beyond single endpoint values and make the stability of the ranking more transparent. In particular, they show that the comparative conclusions are supported not only by mean performance but also by run-level variability patterns and by statistically significant between-optimizer differences at the group level.

At the same time, these additions have clear limits. They do not provide formal source-data uncertainty bounds for the national water statistics used as inputs, and they do not fully replace richer paired or bootstrap-based pairwise inferential designs that could be constructed under a more explicitly matched repeated-run framework. Accordingly, the new uncertainty analyses strengthen the credibility of the optimizer comparison, but they should not be interpreted as exhaustive uncertainty quantification for either the forecasting task or the underlying observational record.

Climate covariates: planned extension rather than validated evidence

A further limitation and future extension concerns exogenous climate information. The present study does not include a pilot experiment incorporating precipitation anomalies, temperature anomalies, drought indices, or related hydroclimatic drivers. Such variables could plausibly improve long-horizon forecasting skill by helping the model distinguish infrastructure-driven changes from environmentally driven fluctuations, but that hypothesis is not empirically tested here.

A coherent future protocol would be to integrate such covariates as additional multivariate input channels within the same forecasting pipeline, while preserving the forward-chaining chronology, repeated-run design, and matched-budget comparison logic used in the present study. This would allow the incremental value of climate-informed modeling to be evaluated under the same methodological discipline applied here. At the same time, the addition of hydroclimatic covariates would expand input dimensionality and likely increase the burden of hyperparameter optimization, making the compute–accuracy trade-off more demanding. For this reason, climate-informed extension should be regarded as a planned protocol for future work rather than as evidence already established by the present results.

Implications for water resource analysis

The findings of this study carry several important implications for data-driven water resource analysis, particularly in the context of long-term assessment of freshwater availability and desalination reliance. By demonstrating that a carefully optimized deep time-series forecasting framework can reliably model per-capita water indicators over extended temporal horizons, this work highlights the potential of advanced machine learning techniques to complement traditional water resource planning and policy analysis tools.

One of the central implications concerns the ability to capture gradual structural transitions in national water supply systems. Freshwater withdrawal and desalination adoption are typically governed by slow-moving drivers such as demographic change, infrastructure investment, and policy reform rather than short-term fluctuations. The frequency-aware modeling capabilities of FEDformer, when combined with systematic hyperparameter optimization, are particularly well suited to representing such long-term dynamics. This enables the extraction of stable temporal patterns that may be difficult to identify using conventional statistical models or short-memory learning approaches.

The use of per-capita indicators further strengthens the policy relevance of the proposed framework. By normalizing water production and withdrawal volumes by population size, the model outputs align more directly with indicators commonly used in water stress assessment, sustainability reporting, and international benchmarking. This facilitates cross-country comparison and supports the evaluation of equity and efficiency in water resource allocation. In addition, the explicit modeling of desalination share provides a quantitative lens through which to assess strategic dependence on energy-intensive water supply alternatives.

From a policy-oriented perspective, the framework offers a means of exploring counterfactual and scenario-based analyses without requiring explicit mechanistic assumptions about future behavior. While the present study focuses on retrospective forecasting and validation, the same modeling approach can be extended to project future per-capita water trajectories under different demographic or infrastructural assumptions. Such projections may inform decisions related to desalination capacity expansion, investment prioritization, and long-term water security planning.

The integration of metaheuristic hyperparameter optimization has further implications for the reliability of data-driven insights. In policy contexts, model credibility and robustness are critical, as decisions based on unstable or poorly calibrated models can have significant economic and social consequences. The demonstrated consistency of performance improvements across multiple evaluation metrics suggests that optimized configurations are less sensitive to stochastic variability and better suited for repeated or operational use. This robustness enhances confidence in model-based assessments and supports their use as supplementary decision-support tools.

Finally, the methodological structure of the proposed framework emphasizes transparency and reproducibility, both of which are essential for policy engagement. By relying on publicly available data, open modeling architectures, and explicitly defined optimization protocols, the framework facilitates independent verification and adaptation by other researchers or institutions. This openness supports collaborative refinement and increases the likelihood that advanced forecasting methods can be responsibly integrated into broader water resource assessment and policy analysis workflows.

In summary, the proposed iHOW-optimized FEDformer framework provides a scalable and interpretable approach for analyzing long-term freshwater and desalination dynamics. Its alignment with per-capita indicators, combined with robust temporal modeling, positions it as a valuable analytical tool for researchers and policymakers concerned with sustainable water resource management in an increasingly water-constrained world.

Limitations

While the proposed framework demonstrates strong potential for modeling long-term freshwater and desalination dynamics, several limitations should be acknowledged to place the findings in an appropriate methodological and practical context. These limitations arise primarily from the scope and structure of the dataset, the effects of spatial and temporal aggregation, and the computational demands associated with deep learning and metaheuristic optimization.

A first limitation concerns the scope and granularity of the dataset. The analysis relies on country-level, annually aggregated data, which necessarily masks subnational heterogeneity in water availability, infrastructure development, and consumption behavior. In many countries, water stress and desalination deployment are highly localized phenomena influenced by regional climate, urbanization patterns, and sectoral demand. Aggregation at the national level may therefore obscure important intra-country dynamics and limit the applicability of the results for fine-grained regional planning. Additionally, the dataset does not explicitly encode uncertainty in reported values or methodological differences across data sources, which may introduce unquantified variability into the modeling process.

Temporal aggregation constitutes a second important limitation. Annual resolution is well suited for analyzing long-term trends and structural shifts, but it precludes the investigation of seasonal variability, short-term shocks, or rapid policy-induced changes. As a result, the forecasting framework is not intended to capture intra-annual dynamics or operational-scale decision processes. The conclusions drawn from the model should therefore be interpreted as reflecting long-term tendencies rather than short-term predictive precision.

The reliance on historical data also implies that the model’s predictive capability is conditioned on past patterns of behavior. Structural breaks driven by unprecedented technological innovation, abrupt regulatory changes, or extreme climate events may not be adequately represented in the training data. While the frequency-aware architecture of FEDformer enhances robustness to nonstationarity, purely data-driven approaches remain limited in their ability to extrapolate beyond the range of observed dynamics.

From a computational perspective, the integration of metaheuristic hyperparameter optimization introduces nontrivial resource requirements. Each candidate solution evaluated by an optimizer entails a complete training and validation cycle of a deep forecasting model. Although computational budgets were carefully controlled and shared across optimizers in this study, the overall cost may still be prohibitive for large-scale operational deployment or rapid iterative analysis. This limitation is particularly relevant for practitioners with constrained computational resources or those seeking real-time or near-real-time forecasting capabilities.

Finally, while the use of a large language model for methodological guidance was deliberately constrained to avoid automated decision-making, this component nonetheless introduces a dependency on external reasoning tools. Although no data were exposed and no direct analytical decisions were delegated, future users of the framework must exercise similar caution to ensure that such tools remain supplementary and do not compromise methodological transparency or reproducibility.

The analyses also make several additional limitations explicit. First, the study does not provide formal predictive uncertainty quantification, confidence intervals, or probabilistic forecast distributions; consequently, the reported results should be interpreted as point-forecast performance under controlled evaluation settings rather than as full uncertainty-aware risk estimates. Second, the dataset is subject to measurement and reporting heterogeneity across countries and years, and these inconsistencies cannot be fully resolved through preprocessing alone. Third, part of the modeling outcome remains dependent on assumptions about missing desalination-related values, and although the missing-data diagnostics strengthen transparency, they do not eliminate the underlying ambiguity in sparse-reporting contexts.

Fourth, wrapper-based hyperparameter optimization carries an inherent risk of over-specialization to the validation split, especially when model evaluations are repeated many times on the same bounded search space. The forward-chaining protocol and repeated runs mitigate this risk, but they do not remove it completely. Fifth, the current manuscript improves interpretability at the robustness and architectural level, but it does not include model-internal interpretability diagnostics such as attention inspection, saliency analysis, or attribution-based interaction tracing. Sixth, abrupt structural breaks caused by major policy shifts, energy-price shocks, technological disruptions, or extreme climate events may lie outside the range of patterns represented in the historical data and may therefore challenge extrapolation reliability.

Seventh, the present analysis does not incorporate exogenous climate covariates such as precipitation anomalies, temperature signals, drought indices, or energy-price variables that could materially influence long-horizon freshwater and desalination dynamics. Eighth, although the computational-efficiency analysis shows that the selected framework is comparatively favorable among the tested optimizers, the overall cost of repeated deep-model training remains nontrivial. These constraints mean that the framework should be interpreted as a strong data-driven forecasting tool for long-term national indicators, but not as a complete or uncertainty-exhaustive representation of future water-system behavior.

Future research directions

Several concrete extensions follow naturally from the present findings. A first priority is the incorporation of uncertainty-aware forecasting methods so that future versions of the framework can provide probabilistic envelopes, scenario-conditioned uncertainty summaries, or other risk-oriented outputs alongside point forecasts. A second direction is the integration of exogenous drivers, particularly climate-related variables such as precipitation indices, temperature anomalies, drought indicators, and potentially energy-price proxies that could improve realism for long-horizon desalination and freshwater forecasting.

A third direction concerns spatial structure. Regional stratification or climate-regime-specific analyses would make it possible to test whether the framework generalizes uniformly across heterogeneous hydrological settings or whether different regimes require different model structures or search spaces. A fourth direction is methodological: multi-objective hyperparameter optimization could be explored to balance predictive accuracy against model complexity, computational cost, or interpretability rather than optimizing only a single validation criterion.

A fifth direction is deeper interpretability. Future studies could augment the current robustness-oriented interpretation with model-internal analyses such as attention-pattern tracing, saliency mapping, variable-attribution diagnostics, or cross-variable interaction analysis across forecast horizons. Finally, an especially promising path would be to combine data-driven forecasting with mechanistic or process-informed water-resource models, thereby linking the predictive flexibility of deep learning with the physical structure and explanatory clarity of hydrological system modeling.

Ethical and governance note

Long-horizon forecasts of freshwater and desalination indicators can influence planning priorities, infrastructure investment, and perceptions of future water security. For that reason, they should be communicated and used as decision-support tools rather than as deterministic prescriptions. The assumptions embedded in the data, preprocessing choices, and model design should be stated transparently, and the absence of formal uncertainty bounds should be made clear when results are presented to non-technical audiences or decision-makers. Responsible deployment in water planning therefore requires expert interpretation, institutional oversight, and explicit attention to the governance implications of acting on data-driven forecasts in socially and environmentally consequential settings.

Conclusion

This study has presented a comprehensive data-driven framework for long-horizon forecasting of freshwater and desalination per-capita indicators, combining a frequency-aware deep learning architecture with a human-inspired metaheuristic hyperparameter optimization strategy. By systematically integrating FEDformer with the iHOW optimization algorithm, the work addresses two tightly coupled challenges in environmental time-series modeling: capturing complex, long-term temporal dependencies and configuring high-capacity models in high-dimensional, nonconvex hyperparameter spaces.

The empirical analysis confirms that FEDformer provides a strong baseline for modeling multivariate per-capita water indicators, reflecting its suitability for long-term temporal pattern extraction. More importantly, the results demonstrate that systematic hyperparameter optimization substantially enhances this baseline performance. Across all evaluation dimensions, iHOW-assisted FEDformer exhibits consistent and pronounced improvements over both the unoptimized configuration and alternative metaheuristic optimization strategies. These improvements are coherent across error-based, correlation-based, and efficiency-based metrics, indicating that the gains arise from a more faithful internal representation of the underlying water resource dynamics rather than from metric-specific artifacts.

The effectiveness of iHOW in this context highlights the value of its human-inspired design principles. By explicitly modeling learning, experience accumulation, and knowledge refinement, iHOW proves capable of navigating the complex hyperparameter landscape associated with deep transformer-based forecasting models. Its ability to balance exploration and exploitation under fixed computational budgets makes it particularly well suited for optimization tasks in which each fitness evaluation is computationally expensive. The consistent performance hierarchy observed across all metrics further reinforces the robustness of the optimization outcomes.

From an application standpoint, the proposed framework offers a scalable and interpretable approach for long-term water resource analysis. The use of per-capita indicators aligns model outputs with policy-relevant measures of water availability and desalination reliance, supporting cross-country comparison and sustainability assessment. The methodological transparency of the framework, grounded in publicly available data and explicitly defined optimization protocols, enhances its potential for adoption and extension in applied water resource research.

Several directions for future work naturally follow from the present study. One promising extension involves the formulation of multi-objective optimization problems in which predictive accuracy is balanced against additional criteria such as model complexity, training time, or interpretability. The iHOW algorithm is inherently well suited to such extensions, given its population-based structure and flexibility in defining composite fitness functions. Another avenue concerns scenario-based forecasting, where the trained models could be used to explore alternative population growth trajectories or infrastructure development pathways, providing decision-makers with comparative insights into potential futures.

Further research may also focus on integrating exogenous climate and environmental variables, such as precipitation indices, temperature anomalies, or drought indicators, to enhance the model’s responsiveness to climate-driven water availability changes. Such integration would move the framework closer to climate-informed water resource modeling while preserving its data-driven foundation. Finally, efforts to reduce computational overhead through surrogate-assisted optimization or parallelized training strategies could broaden the applicability of the framework in resource-constrained settings.

In conclusion, the evidence supports several closely connected takeaways. First, iHOW improves not only the final selected FEDformer configuration but also the search trajectory by achieving faster and more stable best-fitness improvement under a fixed evaluation budget. Second, the study’s conclusions are now supported not only by endpoint performance, convergence, and robustness analyses, but also by repeated-run uncertainty summaries, omnibus inferential comparison, lightweight budget sensitivity analysis, and quantitative feature-importance evidence. Third, the proposed framework remains comparatively robust in a qualified but meaningful sense across forecasting horizons, input-perturbation scenarios, temporal aggregation levels, and alternative missing-data treatments, indicating that its utility is not restricted to a single narrow evaluation setting. Fourth, the architecture ablation results show that the observed gains depend materially on the coordinated contribution of FEDformer’s frequency-domain processing, decomposition pathway, and multivariate coupling, rather than on any single isolated design element. Fifth, the combination of strong long-horizon performance, favorable efficiency characteristics, and scenario-oriented robustness gives the pipeline practical value for medium- and long-term water-planning applications, including desalination outlooks and freshwater-stress assessment under the evaluated assumptions and data conditions. Sixth, responsible use of such forecasts requires transparent communication of assumptions, missing-data conventions, uncertainty limitations, and data-aggregation constraints, especially when the outputs are interpreted in policy or governance settings.

Taken together, these findings show that the combination of iHOW-assisted hyperparameter optimization with FEDformer constitutes an effective, robust, and methodologically transparent approach for per-capita water forecasting under the matched-budget protocol evaluated in this study. The results support the conclusion that iHOW-guided FEDformer is the strongest configuration among the tested optimizer-assisted alternatives and remains comparatively coherent across the analyzed perturbations and horizons. At the same time, the present work does not establish validated cross-dataset or cross-domain generalization, does not provide probabilistic decision-support outputs, and does not resolve uncertainty in the underlying national statistics through robustness analysis alone. By connecting advances in deep time-series learning, optimizer-guided model configuration, and long-horizon water-resource analysis, the proposed framework contributes a practical methodological basis for future extensions in sustainable freshwater and desalination forecasting.
