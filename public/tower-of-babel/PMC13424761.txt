Comment on: “Combination of immunotherapy-based systemic therapy and radiotherapy achieves durable response and favorable survival in recurrent head and neck cancer”
Authors: Erkan Topkan, Efsun Somay, Ugur Selek
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the recent study by Wang et al., 1 addressing a key therapeutic question in recurrent head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). The study included 23 patients treated with an immune checkpoint inhibitor combined with locoregional radiotherapy, with a median follow-up of 16.4 months. Remarkably, 20 patients achieved an objective response, with a 1-year duration of response rate of 75.3% and a median response duration of 16.7 months. Median progression-free survival (PFS) and locoregional PFS (LRPFS) were 17.0 and 27.2 months, respectively, while median overall survival (OS) and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) were not reached; 1-year OS, PFS, LRPFS, and DMFS rates were 100%, 79.5%, 79.5%, and 100%. Although the study results are encouraging, we are concerned that the survival endpoint definitions differ from international standards, which may limit comparability and complicate interpretation.

Wang et al. 1 defined OS, LRPFS, and DMFS as the interval from diagnosis of recurrence to the relevant event, while PFS was calculated from the onset of recurrence to progression or cancer-related death. 1 By contrast, established methodological guidelines specify that OS should be measured from the initiation of study treatment (or randomization) to death from any cause, and PFS from treatment initiation to disease progression or death.2,3 This definitional departure has at least two essential implications (Table 1 of Wang et al. 1 ).

First, the use of recurrence as the point of reference, or “time zero,” rather than the initiation of treatment, introduces lead-time bias and may lead to an artificial inflation of survival outcomes, 4 especially when there are delays between the diagnosis of recurrence and the start of therapy. This artifact and selection of patients with better biology (locally advanced disease) may partly explain the exceptionally favorable 1-year OS rate of 100% and median PFS of 17.0 months, outcomes that appear disproportionately superior to those reported in prospective trials such as KEYNOTE-048, where median PFS was 5–6 months despite larger and less selected populations. 5 This fact may partly explain the unusually favorable 1-year OS rate of 100% and a median PFS of 17.0 months in a predominantly unresectable cohort.

And second, the adoption of non-standard endpoint definitions poses significant challenges to the comparability of studies in clinical research on recurrent/metastatic HNSCC. Such that, the observed remarkable efficacy of combined modality treatment in Wang et al.’ study may inadvertently be overstated compared to standard-of-care benchmarks.1,5 Therefore, utilizing established standard survival definitions is essential for persuasive benchmarking across studies and for enhancing the synthesis of evidence. Any deviation from these definitions may jeopardize the integration of findings into meta-analyses, the development of clinical guidelines, and informed clinical decision-making.

In conclusion, the precise definition of survival endpoints is crucial for fostering transparency and reproducibility, particularly within small retrospective cohorts. As underscored by the STROBE statement, accurately specifying outcomes significantly reduces the potential for misinterpretation. As a result, conducting re-analyses from the point of treatment initiation, or at a minimum, offering parallel reporting of both definitions, would substantially enhance the methodological rigor and clinical relevance of this study.
