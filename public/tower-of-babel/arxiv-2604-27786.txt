On the Expressive Power of GNNs to Solve Linear SDPs
Chendi Qian, Christopher Morris

Abstract
Semidefinite programs (SDPs) are a powerful framework for convex optimization and for constructing strong relaxations of hard combinatorial problems. However, solving large SDPs can be computationally expensive, motivating the use of machine learning models as fast computational surrogates. Graph neural networks (GNNs) are a natural candidate in this setting due to their sparsity-awareness and ability to model variable-constraint interactions. In this work, we study what expressive power is sufficient to recover optimal SDP solutions. We first prove negative results showing that standard GNN architectures fail on recovering linear SDP solutions. We then identify a more expressive architecture that captures the key structure of SDPs and can, in particular, emulate the updates of a standard first-order solver. Empirically, on both synthetic and SdpLib benchmarks of various classes of SDPs, this more expressive architecture achieves consistently lower prediction error and objective gap than theoretically weaker baselines. Finally, using the learned high-quality predictions to warm-start the first-order solver yields practical speedups of up to 80 % 80\% .

Introduction

Semidefinite programming
(SDP) is a cornerstone of modern optimization, serving as a fundamental tool in both combinatorial and convex optimization
(
Boyd & Vandenberghe 2004
;
Nocedal & Wright 1999
)
. In particular, it provides tight relaxations for
NP
-hard

(

)
, max clique
(

)
, and graph coloring
(

; and it subsumes important problem classes including

(SOCP),
quadratically constrained quadratic programming
(QCQP), and
linear programming
(LP)
(
Dattorro 2010
)
, see
Appendix

D
for more details. Despite their theoretical utility, solving large-scale SDPs remains computationally prohibitive; state-of-the-art
interior point method
(IPM) solvers typically scale super-cubically with the problem size, e.g.,
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
or even
\mathcal{O}(n^{6})
per-iteration, depending on the concrete implementation
(
Helmberg et al. 1996
;
Vandenberghe & Boyd 1996
;
Jiang et al. 2020
)
.

To address these scalability challenges,
learning-to-optimize
(L2O) has emerged as a promising approach that aims to replace computationally intensive optimization routines with lightweight machine learning proxies. In this context,
graph neural networks
(GNNs)
(
Scarselli et al. 2008
;
Gilmer et al. 2017
)
have shown strong potential, particularly for learning to solve
mixed-integer linear programs
(MILPs) by representing them as variable-constraint bipartite graphs
(
Gasse et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2023
)
. Recent works have further aligned GNNs with classical algorithms, such as the IPM or
primal-dual hybrid gradient
(PDHG) algorithm
(
Qian et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024a
)
.

However, extending this success to SDPs poses a fundamental challenge, and there is currently a lack of neural architectures capable of effectively representing even
linear SDPs
. Unlike LPs, where variables are vector entries, SDP variables are matrix entries, where the underlying matrix is constrained to the
positive semidefinite
(PSD) cone. This introduces unique structural symmetries, such as equivariance under simultaneous row and column permutations, that standard bipartite representations fail to capture. While the expressivity of GNNs and their connection to the Weisfeiler–Leman (
WL
) hierarchy of graph isomorphism tests
(
Cai et al. 1992
)
are well-studied
(
Xu et al. 2018
;
Morris et al. 2019
;
Morris et al. 2020
;
Maron et al. 2019a
)
, the specific expressive power required to represent the mapping from a linear SDP instance to its optimal matrix solution remains unclear.

Present work

In this work, we bridge GNN expressivity theory and an important class of SDP. That is, we formally characterize the inductive bias needed to represent linear SDPs. Our analysis reveals that
1
- and
2
-
\mathsf{WL}
-like methods, which we term
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
and
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
, are insufficient. In contrast, we show that a
folklore
2
-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman
(
2\text{-}\mathsf{FWL}
) equivalent architecture, which we denote as
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
, is capable of capturing linear SDPs.

Concretely, we contribute the following.

1.

Impossibility results
We prove that standard constraint-variable message passing (
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
) fails to represent SDPs. Furthermore, we show that the higher-order architecture
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
is also insufficient to capture SDPs.

2.

Theoretical sufficiency for solution
We establish that the expressivity of
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
is sufficient to represent SDP solutions. Specifically, we prove that the algorithmic operations of the PDHG solver
(
Chambolle & Pock 2016
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
are subsumed by the stable coloring of
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
. This implies that
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
possesses the expressivity to simulate the solver’s trajectory and approximate the solution.

3.

Empirical validation
We validate our theory on synthetic and real-world benchmarks, showing that
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
-expressive architectures consistently achieve the lowest loss and objective gap. Moreover, we demonstrate that utilizing these high-quality predictions to warm-start a standard solver significantly reduces convergence time.

In summary, we formally establish the expressivity level required to solve linear SDPs, offering the first rigorous theoretical framework to ground the design of neural SDP solvers. By validating that expressive power directly translates to solution quality, our work establishes a principled blueprint for future research in learning-based optimization.

Representing SDP instances

Here, we outline our graph representation for faithfully encoding SDP problems.

2.1
From LP to SDP

We first revisit the standard graph representation used for LP. Given an LP instance
I\colon\min_{\mathbold{x}\geq\bm{0}}\;\mathbold{c}^{\intercal}\mathbold{x}\text{ s.t. }\;\mathbold{A}\mathbold{x}=\mathbold{b}

)

\mathbold{X}
as a collection of

individual variables, initializing node features with coefficients from the objective matrix
\mathbold{C}

m

\mathbold{b}
. A variable node indexed by

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{init}_{\text{c}}\left(b_{k}\right),\text{ for }k\in[m],

(2)

\displaystyle\mathbold{v}^{t}_{ij}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathsf{hash}\left(\mathbold{v}^{t-1}_{ij},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{c}^{t-1}_{k})\mid k\in N(ij)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\right),

2.2
Leveraging higher-order information

The limitation of standard bipartite graph representation stems from treating SDP variables as independent entities, effectively flattening the problem and discarding the crucial intrinsic matrix geometry. Unlike standard LPs, the primal variables
X_{ij}
of an SDP form a PSD matrix, characterized by spectral properties and global correlations. Consequently, an effective representation must move beyond bipartite graph representation and instead explicitly model the global interactions among
X_{ij}
.

We seek a neural architecture
\mathsf{NN}\colon\mathbb{S}^{n}\times(\mathbb{S}^{n})^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{S}^{n}
that maps an SDP instance to a solution
\mathbold{X}
while strictly respecting the problem’s underlying symmetries. Specifically, we enforce three design principles.

1.

Symmetry
Since the solution resides in
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
, for any symmetric inputs
\mathbold{C}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
, the solution
\mathbold{X}\coloneq\mathsf{NN}\left(\mathbold{C},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}},\mathbold{b}\right)
must satisfy
\mathbold{X}=\mathbold{X}^{\intercal}
.

2.

Equivariance
For any permutation matrix
\mathbold{P}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
, transforming the inputs via
\mathbold{P}\mathbold{C}\mathbold{P}^{\intercal}
and
\mathbold{P}\mathbold{A}_{k}\mathbold{P}^{\intercal}
for all
k
must result in the equivalently permuted output
\mathbold{P}\mathbold{X}\mathbold{P}^{\intercal}
.

3.

Invariance
For any permutation matrix
\mathbold{Q}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}
applied to the constraints, the output must remain unchanged:
\mathsf{NN}\left(\mathbold{C},\mathbold{Q}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}},\mathbold{Q}\mathbold{b}\right)=\mathsf{NN}\left(\mathbold{C},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}},\mathbold{b}\right)
.

Since the primal variables are indexed by pairs
(i,j)
, they are structurally analogous to the colored
2

2\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
and
2\text{-}\mathsf{FWL}
emerge not merely as heuristics, but also as the natural inductive bias for this domain. Consequently, we can adapt the iterative color refinement to distinguish the variables of SDP instances. We formally introduce two variants, the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
and
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}

and

\mathbold{v}_{ji}
are not guaranteed to be identical. To strictly enforce symmetry in output, we explicitly symmetrize the states:
\mathbold{v}_{ji}^{t}\leftarrow\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{t}
for all
i<j
.

In contrast, the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
update couples the neighbor indices to capture joint interactions, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{t}\coloneq\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{t-1},

\displaystyle\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t-1},\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t-1}\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\},

\displaystyle\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{c}^{t-1}_{k})\mid k\in N(ij)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbold{c}^{t}_{k}\coloneq\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\mathbold{c}^{t-1}_{k},

\displaystyle\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\left(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{t-1}\right)\mid(i,j)\in N(k)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr).

Unlike standard
2\text{-}\mathsf{FWL}
, which aggregates a multiset of
ordered tuples
, our design aggregates a multiset of
unordered multisets

\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t},\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t}\}\kern-2.24998pt\}
. This modification ensures the updated
\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{t}=\mathbold{v}_{ji}^{t}
without the need for manual symmetrization. For theoretical comparison, we define a tuple-based version,
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL+}
, in
Section

E.1
.
We establish the following strict hierarchy of expressivity, see
Section

C.2
for the detailed proof, i.e.,

\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL+}\sqsubset\{\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}\not\equiv\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}\}\sqsubset\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}.

That is,
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL+}
strictly refines
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
and
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
, while
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
and
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
are incomparable, and they both strictly refine
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
.
Guided by this hierarchy, we first examine the limitations of the intermediate variant,
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
. Despite strictly refining the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
, we demonstrate that it remains insufficient.

Proposition 2.2
.

The
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
fails to represent linear SDP solutions. That is, there exist instances where the stable colors under
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
satisfy
\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{pq}
for distinct indices
(i,j)
and
(p,q)
, yet the unique optimal solution entries differ, i.e.,
X^{*}_{ij}\neq X^{*}_{pq}
.

However, we argue that
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL+}
is
overly expressive
by proving in
Section

C.4
that
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
is sufficiently expressive for this domain. Formally,

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\mathbold{X}^{*}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
be the primal optimal solution to a given SDP instance and given indices
(i,j),(p,q)
. If the stable colorings of
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
satisfy
\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{pq}
, then the solution values satisfy
X^{*}_{ij}=X^{*}_{pq}
.

In practice, excessive expressivity can introduce unnecessarily too many colors or features, potentially causing the neural network to overfit. On the contrary, by discarding unnecessary directional information, our design yields a smaller set of colors which facilitates faster convergence, and encourages the neural network to learn more robust and generalizable embeddings.

In summary, both
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
and
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
satisfy our three design principles: symmetric outputs, permutation equivariance regarding
\mathbold{C},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
, and invariance to constraint ordering. Crucially,
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
provides the expressivity level required to solve the problem.

2.3
Complexity

We analyze the computational complexity of
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
by mapping it to the standard
1\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
operating on an implicit auxiliary graph. By leveraging tight complexity bounds from
Berkholz et al. 2017
, we have the following proposition (see
Section

C.5
for proof):

Proposition 2.4
.

Given an SDP of
n\times n
variables and
m
constraints,
\text{nnz}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}})
denotes the number of non-zero entries in
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
, then
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
converges in time

\mathcal{O}\left((n^{3}+\text{nnz}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}))\log n\right)
.

The derived complexity is highly competitive for practical applications. In many applications, such as max-cut, the constraint tensor is
sparse
, rendering the
\text{nnz}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}})
term negligible. Notably, our total complexity is comparable to the fastest implementation of IPM for SDPs
(
Jiang et al. 2020
)
, which has complexity
\mathcal{O}(n^{3.5}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon})
. Besides, the per-iteration of our
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
is
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}+\text{nnz}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}))
, which is comparable to any SDP solver per iteration
(
Wen et al. 2010
;
Jiang et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
, as they all require spectral decomposition of complexity
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
. A critical advantage of our approach, however, is that the neuralized
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
being highly compatible for parallelization on GPUs.

2.4
Expressive neural architecture

Based on the initialization functions of
Section

2.1
and the update functions of
Section

2.2
, we can neuralize the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
using multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) and instantiate a neural network, which we name
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FMPNN}
.

The feature initialization is

\displaystyle\mathbold{h}_{k}^{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathsf{INIT}_{\text{c}}(b_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbold{h}_{ij}^{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathsf{INIT}_{\text{v}}(C_{ij},\mathbb{I}_{i=j})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

and the update function

\displaystyle\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{v}\rightarrow\text{v}}^{t}

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{u\in[n]}\mathsf{MSG}_{\text{v}\rightarrow\text{v}}^{t}\left(\mathsf{MAP}^{t}\left(\mathbold{h}_{uj}^{t-1}\right)+\mathsf{MAP}^{t}\left(\mathbold{h}_{iu}^{t-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{c}\rightarrow\text{v}}^{t}

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{k\in N(ij)}\mathsf{MSG}^{t}_{\text{c}\rightarrow\text{v}}\left(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{h}_{k}^{t-1}\right)

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbold{m}_{k,\text{v}\rightarrow\text{c}}^{t}

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{(i,j)\in N(k)}\mathsf{MSG}^{t}_{\text{v}\rightarrow\text{c}}\left(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{h}_{ij}^{t-1}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbold{h}_{ij}^{t}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{UPD}_{\text{v}}^{t}\left(\mathbold{h}_{ij}^{t-1},\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{v}\rightarrow\text{v}}^{t},\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{c}\rightarrow\text{v}}^{t}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbold{h}^{t}_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{UPD}_{\text{c}}^{t}\left(\mathbold{h}^{t-1}_{k},\mathbold{m}_{k,\text{v}\rightarrow\text{c}}^{t}\right)

where
\mathsf{INIT},\mathsf{MSG},\mathsf{MAP},\mathsf{UPD}
are MLPs. We now formally establish that this neural architecture possesses the capacity to fully simulate the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
algorithm, thereby inheriting its theoretical expressivity.

Proposition 2.5
.

There exists a set of parameters for the functions
\mathsf{INIT},\mathsf{UPD},\mathsf{MSG},\mathsf{MAP}
, such that
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FMPNN}
has maximal expressivity equal to the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
.

See to
Section

C.6
for a proof and how we also neuralize
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
and
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
to
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{MPNN}
and
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{MPNN}
.

Empirical results

In the following, we aim to assess the extent to which our theoretical results translate to practice. Concretely, we answer the following questions.

Q1 Expressivity

Do the theoretical hierarchies of expressivity translate into approximation performance on SDP?

Q2 Efficiency

Can the proposed neural architecture accelerate the solving process for state-of-the-art solvers?

https://github.com/chendiqian/GNN4SDP
.

Neural architecture

We evaluate the approximation performance of
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FMPNN}
for solving SDPs against theoretically weaker baselines, i.e.,
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{MPNN}
and
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{MPNN}
, to verify whether empirical results align with our expressivity hierarchy.
Besides, there are a few interesting neural architectures. The
\delta\text{-}\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{MPNN}
and
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{IGN}
, adapted from
\delta\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}

(
Morris et al. 2020
)
and
\text{2-}\mathsf{IGN}

(
Maron et al. 2019b
)
respectively, are similar in form to
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{MPNN}
, see
Section

E.2
and
Section

E.3
for their definition and expressivity analysis. Furthermore, we benchmark against the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{ET}
, a variant of Edge Transformer (ET) with expressivity equivalent to
2\text{-}\mathsf{FWL}

(
Müller et al. 2024
)
, see
Section

E.4
for details.

Datasets

We employ synthetic SDP problems as relaxations of classical CO problems, i.e., max-cut, max-clique, max independent set (MIS), vertex cover, and max 2-SAT, as well as linear matrix inequality (LMI) problems from control theory.
Detailed problem formulations are provided in
Appendix

D
.
For each problem class, we generate
10\,000
instances and partition them into training, validation, and test sets with
8{:}1{:}1
ratio.
For the graph-based tasks, i.e., max-cut, max-clique, MIS, and vertex cover, we construct instances from Erdős–Rényi graphs
(
Erdős & Rényi 1959
)
with 100 nodes and edge probability of 0.1, resulting in SDP problems with
10\,000
variables.
To specifically challenge the expressivity of weaker baselines and expose approximation gaps, we also curate a more difficult max-cut dataset comprising
10
-regular graphs with
100
nodes. Beyond the graph problems, the suite includes max 2-SAT instances with
100
variables and
1000
clauses, as well as LMI control problems with
2500
variables and
500
constraints. We emphasize that our experimental design prioritizes learnability and structural expressivity over scalability. While the instance sizes are moderate, they are sufficient to: (i) reveal the limitations of weaker architectures and (ii) demonstrate significant acceleration for classical SDP solvers.

To demonstrate real-world applicability beyond synthetic benchmarks, we also evaluate our method on real-world instances from the
SdpLib

(
Borchers 1999
)
. We select the class of max-cut problems, which consists of 13 instances that are homogeneous in problem structure but vary significantly in scale, as detailed in
Table

9
. Due to the limited number of available instances, the standard train-validation split is infeasible. Instead, we treat this as a direct test of
trainability
, i.e., assessing whether the architectures can overfit and effectively predict the near-optimal solution.

Training and evaluation

All models were trained using the Adam optimizer
(
Kingma & Ba 2015
)
with default hyperparameters and batch size of 256. The training objective is to minimize the supervised mean squared error (MSE) between the predicted and ground-truth solution matrices. Training for synthetic datasets were conducted on a compute cluster with four NVIDIA L40S GPUs, whereas
SdpLib
utilized a single GPU. We trained for at most
1000
epochs, using a learning rate scheduler that decays the learning rate after 100 consecutive epochs without improvement in the relative objective gap on the validation set. Additionally, early stopping was applied if this metric failed to improve for
200
consecutive epochs.

To quantify the computational efficiency of neural SDP solvers, we benchmarked inference time against classical solvers using the max-cut problem as a representative case study. We compared against SDPLR, a solver based on Burer-Monteiro method
(
Burer & Monteiro 2003
)
, MOSEK
(
ApS 2022
)
, an IPM solver, and SCS
(
O’Donoghue 2021
)
, a first-order solver based on the ADMM algorithm
(
Wen et al. 2010
)
.
SDPLR and MOSEK were executed on an Intel
®
Xeon
®
Silver 4510 CPU, as they lack native GPU support.
Both the SCS solver and all the neural architectures were executed on a single NVIDIA L40S GPU.
Additionally, we investigate the practical utility of our approach by using the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FMPNN}
prediction to warm-start the SCS solver.

For all experiments, including model training and solver timing, we compute evaluation metrics, specifically loss and objective gap, by averaging over the full dataset. We repeat this process using five different random seeds and report the resulting mean and standard deviation.

Conclusion

We studied the expressive power required of GNNs to predict optimal solutions of linear SDPs, using the unique minimum-Frobenius-norm solution as the learning target. We proved that
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
and higher-order
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
can fail on SDPs, whereas
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
-equivalent architectures are sufficient because they can emulate PDHG solver updates until convergence. Empirically, these more expressive architectures achieve the lowest prediction error and the smallest objective gaps across synthetic and real-world benchmarks. Moreover, their predictions can warm-start a first-order solver (SCS), yielding substantial speedups on
SdpLib
instances.
Overall, our results sharpen the understanding of the expressivity required to learn solutions for a large, practically important class of convex optimization problems.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work aimed at advancing the field of machine learning. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none of which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.

Appendix A
Limitations and future directions

A key limitation of our study is that the required
2\text{-}\mathsf{FWL}
-level expressivity comes at a nontrivial computational cost, as it requires considering every variable pair. In addition, our theoretical results demonstrate only that there exist parameter assignments that allow recovery of the optimal low-norm solution, and do not guarantee that gradient-descent-based methods will converge. In addition, our expressivity guarantees are non-uniform, in the sense that we only show the existence of a parameter for a given SDP problem instance size and do not shed light on the architectures’ size-generalization capabilities
(
Levin et al. 2025
)
.

Looking forward
, we identify three key directions. First, we propose designing efficient architectures for specific SDP subclasses where lower-order expressivity suffices. Second, the rigorous alignment between our architecture and the PDHG algorithm opens a promising avenue for self-supervised primal-dual learning
(
Park & Van Hentenryck 2023
;
Tanneau & Van Hentenryck 2024
)
. Rather than relying on supervised learning, future models could be trained by directly minimizing algorithmic residuals or duality gaps. Finally, we aim to extend this framework to broader conic programs and integrate these learned components into combinatorial optimization pipelines, such as branch-and-cut frameworks.

We introduce some standard notations first. For

to denote multisets, i.e., the generalization of sets allowing for multiple instances of each of its elements. A

G

(V(G),E(G))
with
finite
sets of

V(G)
and
edges

E(G)\subseteq\{\{u,v\}\subseteq V(G)\mid u\neq v\}

\{u,v\}
in

by
(u,v)
or
(v,u)
. A
labeled graph

G
is a triple
(V,E,l)
with
(node) coloring
or
label function

l\colon V(G)\to\mathbb{N}
. The
neighborhood
of
v

G
is denoted by
N(v)\coloneqq\{u\in V(G)\mid\{v,u\}\in E(G)\}
.

Two graphs
G
and
H
are
isomorphic
and we write
G\simeq H
if there exists a bijection
\varphi\colon V(G)\to V(H)
that preserves the adjacency relation, i.e.,
(u,v)\in E(G)\iff(\varphi(u),\varphi(v))\in E(H)
. Then
\varphi
is an
isomorphism
between
G
and
H

l(v)=l(\varphi(v))
for
v
in
V(G)
. We further define the atomic type
\mathsf{atp}\colon V(G)^{k}\to\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathsf{atp}(\mathbold{v})=\mathsf{atp}(\mathbold{w})
for
\mathbold{v},\mathbold{w}\in V(G)^{k}
if and only if the mapping
\varphi\colon V(G)^{k}\to V(G)^{k}
where
v_{i}\to w_{i}

v_{i}=v_{j}\iff w_{i}=w_{j}

The
1\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
or color refinement is a simple heuristic for the graph isomorphism problem, originally proposed by
Weisfeiler & Leman 1968
. Intuitively, the algorithm determines if two graphs are non-isomorphic by iteratively coloring or labeling vertices. Given an initial coloring or labeling of the vertices of both graphs, e.g., their degree or application-specific information, in each iteration, two vertices with the same label get different labels if the number of identically labeled neighbors is not equal. If, after some iteration, the number of vertices annotated with a specific label is different in both graphs, the algorithm terminates and a stable coloring (partition) is obtained. We can then conclude that the two graphs are not isomorphic. It is easy to see that the algorithm cannot distinguish all non-isomorphic graphs
(
Cai et al. 1992
)
.

Formally, let
G=(V,E,l)
be a labeled graph. In each iteration
t+1
, the
1\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
computes a node coloring for node
v
as
\mathbold{c}_{v}^{t}
, which depends on the coloring of the neighbors. That is,

\mathbold{c}^{t+1}_{v}\coloneqq\mathsf{hash}\left(\mathbold{c}^{t}_{v},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{c}^{t}_{u}\mid u\in N(v)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\right),

where
\mathsf{hash}
injectively maps the above pair to a unique natural number, which has not been used in previous iterations. In iteration
0
, the coloring
\mathbold{c}^{0}_{v}\coloneqq l(v)
.

To test if two graphs
G
and
H
are non-isomorphic, we run the above algorithm in “parallel” on both graphs. If the two graphs have a different number of vertices colored
c
in
\mathbb{N}
at some iteration, the
1\text{-}\mathsf{WL}

distinguishes
the graphs as non-isomorphic. Moreover, if

\mathbold{c}^{t}_{v}=\mathbold{c}^{t}_{w}\iff\mathbold{c}^{t+1}_{v}=\mathbold{c}^{t+1}_{w},

for all vertices
v
and
w
in
V(G)
, the algorithm terminates. For such
t
, we define the
stable coloring

\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{v}=\mathbold{c}^{t}_{v}
for
v
in
V(G)
.

Due to the shortcomings of the
1\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
or color refinement in distinguishing non-isomorphic graphs, several researchers
(
Babai 1979
;
Immerman & Lander 1990
;
Cai et al. 1992
;
Grohe 2017
;
Morris et al. 2019
;
Grohe 2021
)
devised a more powerful generalization of the former, today known as the
k

k\text{-}\mathsf{FWL}
) and

Intuitively, to surpass the limitations of the
1\text{-}\mathsf{WL}

algorithm colors subgraphs instead of a single node. More precisely, given a graph
G

V(G)^{k}
for
k\geq 2
instead of the vertices. By defining a neighborhood between these tuples, we can define a coloring similar to the
1\text{-}\mathsf{WL}

G

k\geq 2

t\geq 0

\mathbold{c}^{t}_{\mathbold{v}}
for a tuple
{\mathbold{v}}

t=0

\mathbold{v},\mathbold{w}\in V(G)^{k}

\mathbold{c}^{0}_{\mathbold{v}}\coloneqq\mathsf{atp}(\mathbold{v})
. The update of the coloring is defined by

\mathbold{c}^{t+1}_{\mathbold{v}}\coloneqq\mathsf{hash}\left(\mathbold{c}^{t}_{\mathbold{v}},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\left(\mathbold{c}^{t}_{\phi_{1}(\mathbold{v},w)},\dots,\mathbold{c}^{t}_{\phi_{k}(\mathbold{v},w)}\right)\mid w\in V(G)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\right),

where

\phi_{j}(\mathbold{v},w)\coloneqq(v_{1},\dots,v_{j-1},w,v_{j+1},\dots,v_{k}).

That is,
\phi_{j}(\mathbold{v},w)
replaces the
j
-th component of the tuple
\mathbold{v}
with the node
w
. Hence, two tuples are
adjacent
or
j
-neighbors
, with respect to a node
w
, if they are different in the
j
th component (or equal, in the case of self-loops).

k\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
differs from
k\text{-}\mathsf{FWL}
in the way the
j

k\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
is

\mathbold{c}^{t+1}_{\mathbold{v}}\coloneqq\mathsf{hash}\left(\mathbold{c}^{t}_{\mathbold{v}},\left(\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{c}^{t}_{\phi_{1}(\mathbold{v},w)}\mid w\in V(G)\}\kern-2.24998pt\},\cdots,\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{c}^{t}_{\phi_{k}(\mathbold{v},w)}\mid w\in V(G)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\right)\right).

Again, we run the
k\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
or
k\text{-}\mathsf{FWL}
algorithm until convergence, i.e.,

\mathbold{c}^{t}_{\mathbold{v}}=\mathbold{c}^{t}_{\mathbold{w}}\iff\mathbold{c}^{t+1}_{\mathbold{v}}=\mathbold{c}^{t+1}_{\mathbold{w}},

for all
\mathbold{v}
and
\mathbold{w}
in
V(G)^{k}
holds, and call the partition of
V(G)^{k}

induced by
\mathbold{c}^{t}
the stable partition. For such
t
, we define the stable coloring
\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{\mathbold{v}}=\mathbold{c}^{t}_{\mathbold{v}}
for
\mathbold{v}
in
V(G)^{k}
. Hence, two tuples
\mathbold{v}
and
\mathbold{w}
with the same color in iteration
(t-1)
get different colors in iteration

j
in
[k]
such that the number of
j

\mathbold{v}
and
\mathbold{w}

To test whether two graphs
G
and
H
are non-isomorphic, we run the
k\text{-}\mathsf{FWL}
or
k\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
in “parallel” on both graphs. Then, if the two graphs have a different number of vertices colored
c
in
\mathbb{N}
, the
k\text{-}\mathsf{WL}

Proof for
Proposition

1.1
.

We prove by contradiction. Suppose there exists another feasible solution
\mathbold{X}^{\prime}\neq\mathbold{X}^{*}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
, such that
\mathcal{A}(\mathbold{X}^{\prime})=\mathbold{b}
, and
\left\langle\mathbold{C},\,\mathbold{X}^{\prime}\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathbold{C},\,\mathbold{X}^{*}\right\rangle
, and
\lVert\mathbold{X}^{\prime}\rVert_{{F}}^{2}=\lVert\mathbold{X}^{*}\rVert_{{F}}^{2}
. Note that we are not assuming
\lVert\mathbold{X}^{\prime}\rVert_{{F}}^{2}<\lVert\mathbold{X}^{*}\rVert_{{F}}^{2}
or
\left\langle\mathbold{C},\,\mathbold{X}^{\prime}\right\rangle<\left\langle\mathbold{C},\,\mathbold{X}^{*}\right\rangle
, otherwise the
\mathbold{X}^{\prime}
will replace
\mathbold{X}^{*}
to be the optimal solution with least Frobenius norm. Assuming the existence of
\mathbold{X}^{\prime}
, we can find a third solution
\mathbold{X}^{\prime\prime}=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathbold{X}^{*}+\mathbold{X}^{\prime}\right)
, which is feasible and optimal, in that:

•

\mathbold{X}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
, as
\mathbold{X}^{*}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
and
\mathbold{X}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
, and the PSD cone is a convex cone.

•

\mathcal{A}(\mathbold{X}^{\prime\prime})=\dfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{A}(\mathbold{X}^{*})+\dfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{A}(\mathbold{X}^{\prime})=\mathbold{b}
, i.e.,
\mathbold{X}^{\prime\prime}
is feasible w.r.t. the constraints.

•

\left\langle\mathbold{C},\,\mathbold{X}^{\prime\prime}\right\rangle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left\langle\mathbold{C},\,\mathbold{X}^{*}\right\rangle+\dfrac{1}{2}\left\langle\mathbold{C},\,\mathbold{X}^{\prime}\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathbold{C},\,\mathbold{X}^{*}\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathbold{C},\,\mathbold{X}^{\prime}\right\rangle
, i.e.,
\mathbold{X}^{\prime\prime}
has the same objective value.

Besides,
\lVert\mathbold{X}^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{{F}}^{2}=\dfrac{1}{4}\lVert\mathbold{X}^{*}+\mathbold{X}^{\prime}\rVert_{{F}}^{2}\leq\dfrac{\lVert\mathbold{X}^{*}\rVert_{{F}}^{2}+\lVert\mathbold{X}^{\prime}\rVert_{{F}}^{2}}{2}=\lVert\mathbold{X}^{*}\rVert_{{F}}^{2}=\lVert\mathbold{X}^{\prime}\rVert_{{F}}^{2}
, and equality holds only when
\mathbold{X}^{*}=\mathbold{X}^{\prime}
.
Since we assume
\mathbold{X}^{*}\neq\mathbold{X}^{\prime}
, this implies
\mathbold{X}^{\prime\prime}
has a strictly lower Frobenius norm than
\mathbold{X}^{*}
and
\mathbold{X}^{\prime\prime}
, contradicting the assumption that
\mathbold{X}^{*}
and
\mathbold{X}^{\prime}
are minimum norm solutions. Thus, the minimum norm solution must be unique.
∎

Remark C.3
.

From a measure-theoretic perspective, one might argue that a specific counterexample could merely reside on a set of measure zero, thereby still allowing a model to maintain universal approximation
almost everywhere
. However, this defense is inadequate for SDP relaxations of CO problems. First, the input distributions of CO problems are not uniformly drawn from
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Rather, they are heavily concentrated on specific discrete topological structures. Second, our identified failure cases are not isolated points. As established in
Lemma

C.24
, scaling any failing instance by a scalar
\alpha>0
generates an uncountable infinite set (a ray) of failures. This specific construction represents just one of many systematic methods to expose these representational blind spots. Ultimately, the empirical collapse of the less expressive models observed in
Table

1
confirms that this theoretical failure translates into a severe, practical expressivity limitation, rather than a negligible mathematical anomaly.

Proposition C.4

(Restatement of
Proposition

2.2
)
.

The
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
fails to represent linear SDP solutions. That is, there exist instances where the stable colors under
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
satisfy
\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{pq}
for distinct indices
(i,j)
and
(p,q)
, yet the unique optimal solution entries differ

.

Proof for
Proposition

2.2
.

We prove this by construction, using a counterexample in which the objective matrix
\mathbold{C}
forms a Latin square. Consider the following SDP instance with
n=6
,

\mathbold{C}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3&4&5&6\\
2&1&4&5&6&3\\
3&4&1&6&2&5\\
4&5&6&1&3&2\\
5&6&2&3&1&4\\
6&3&5&2&4&1\\
\end{bmatrix},\hskip 11.49994pt\mathbold{A}_{1}=\mathbold{I}_{6},\hskip 11.49994pt\mathbold{b}=[1].

We focus on the entries
(1,5)
and
(2,4)
.
First, observe that their initial features are identical, i.e.,
\mathbold{C}_{15}=\mathbold{C}_{24}=5
, and both are non-diagonal entries. Secondly, observe that every row and column in

contains the set of values

Since
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
aggregates row and column multisets independently as in

from
(2,4)
at initialization or any subsequent iteration. Thus,
\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{15}=\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{24}
under
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}

C.2
Proof for expressivity hierarchy

We first compare
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
and
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
by showing the following result.

Proposition C.5
.

The
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
algorithm strictly refines
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
, denoted as

\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}\sqsubset\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}.

Proof.

Observing from the update functions of
Section

2.1
and
Equation

3
, we notice that the update of constraint nodes is exactly the same. Now, consider the variable nodes. Observe that the information aggregated by the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
hash function for a variable
\mathbold{v}_{ij}
consists solely of its constraint neighbors. By definition, the corresponding input for
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
includes this constraint information as a subset, strictly augmented by the structural row and column multisets.

We now formally prove
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}\sqsubseteq\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
, that is, for any iteration
t
, if
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
fails to distinguish
\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{t}
and
\mathbold{v}_{pq}^{t}
, so cannot
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
. First, for
t=0
, the color
\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{0}
is basically the encoding of the problem
C_{ij}
, therefore if for
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
:
\mathbold{v}^{0}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{0}_{pq}
, it also holds for
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
. Then, for all
t>0
, we have, by definition of
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
:

\displaystyle\mathbold{v}^{t}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{t}_{pq}

\displaystyle\implies\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{t-1},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t-1}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t-1}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{c}^{t-1}_{k})\mid k\in N(ij)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\mathbold{v}_{pq}^{t-1},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{uq}^{t-1}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{pu}^{t-1}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(A_{k,pq},\mathbold{c}^{t-1}_{k})\mid k\in N(pq)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}

\displaystyle\implies\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{t-1},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{c}^{t-1}_{k})\mid k\in N(ij)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\mathbold{v}_{pq}^{t-1},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(A_{k,pq},\mathbold{c}^{t-1}_{k})\mid k\in N(pq)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr).

Since this corresponds exactly to the update rule of
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
, it follows that
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
also cannot distinguish them. Thus,
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}\sqsubseteq\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
.

To show the strict refinement, we pick the example in the proof of
Proposition

2.1
where
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
fails to distinguish the variables
X^{*}_{11}
and
X^{*}_{33}
. In contrast,
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
converges to
\begin{bmatrix}a&b&c\\
b&e&d\\
c&d&f\\
\end{bmatrix}
, where
\{a,b,c,\cdots\}
are from an alphabet to represent colors.
Here, the diagonal entries are distinguished (
a\neq f
), allowing the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
algorithm to distinguish the solutions.

Therefore,
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}\sqsubset\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
.
∎

Similarly,
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}\sqsubset\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
can be proven in exactly the same way with the same example; we omit the proof.

Next, we show the hierarchy between
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL+}
and
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
.

Proposition C.6
.

The
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL+}
algorithm strictly refines
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
, denoted as

\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL+}\sqsubset\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}.

Proof.

The update of constraint nodes is exactly the same for
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
and
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL+}

We first prove
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL+}\sqsubseteq\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}
.
First, for
t=0
, the colors
\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{0}
is basically the encoding of the problem
C_{ij}

\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL+}

\mathbold{v}^{0}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{0}_{pq}

\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}

For any

\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL+}

\mathbold{v}^{t}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{t}_{pq}

\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{WL}

\displaystyle\mathbold{v}^{t}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{t}_{pq}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{t-1},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\left(\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t-1},\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t-1}\right)\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{c}^{t-1}_{k})\mid k\in N(ij)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\mathbold{v}_{pq}^{t-1},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\left(\mathbold{v}_{uq}^{t-1},\mathbold{v}_{pu}^{t-1}\right)\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(A_{k,pq},\mathbold{c}^{t-1}_{k})\mid k\in N(pq)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle{\mathbold\displaystyle v}^{t-1}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{t-1}_{pq}\land

\displaystyle\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\left(\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t-1},\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t-1}\right)\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\}=\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\left(\mathbold{v}_{uq}^{t-1},\mathbold{v}_{pu}^{t-1}\right)\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\land

\displaystyle\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{c}^{t-1}_{k})\mid k\in N(ij)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}=\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(A_{k,pq},\mathbold{c}^{t-1}_{k})\mid k\in N(ij)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}

\displaystyle{\mathbold\displaystyle v}^{t-1}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{t-1}_{pq}\land

\displaystyle\mathsf{hash}\Big(\mathbold{v}^{t-1}_{ij},

\displaystyle\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t-1}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t-1}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\}

\displaystyle\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{c}^{t-1}_{k})\mid k\in N(ij)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Big)

\displaystyle\mathsf{hash}\Big(\mathbold{v}^{t-1}_{pq}

\displaystyle\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{uq}^{t-1}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{pu}^{t-1}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\}

thus with

update they also have the same color. The implication holds because the tuples are ordered, which ensures that their marginal entries form multisets that are respectively identical.

We then prove strict refinement by giving an example in the proof of

\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL+}

Remark C.13

(Strong convergence in finite dimensions)
.

Standard convergence analyses for operator splitting methods, such as those in

Let

We begin by proving a critical lemma. While

refines the spectral decomposition of the graph adjacency matrix, we contribute a similar result specifically for a multiset

2\text{-}\mathsf{FWL}
retains this crucial spectral refinement property.

Lemma C.15
.

Let
\mathbold{M}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
be a symmetric matrix with spectral decomposition

\mathbold{M}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}\mathbold{P}_{k}

where
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}\cdots>\lambda_{m}
are the non repeating eigenvalues, and
\mathbold{P}_{k}
are Frobenius covariants
(
Horn & Johnson 1994
, p. 437)
, i.e., the symmetric matrices describing the projection onto the eigenspace of eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
.

The stable coloring
\mathbold{v}^{\infty}
are produced by the multiset
2\text{-}\mathsf{FWL}
algorithm:

\displaystyle\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{0}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{hash}\left(\mathbold{M}_{ij},\mathbb{I}_{i=j}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{t}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{t-1},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t-1},\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t-1}\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr),\;\forall t>0,

otherwise 0.
The following result holds:

\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{\infty}=\mathbold{v}_{pq}^{\infty}\implies(\mathbold{P}_{k})_{ij}=(\mathbold{P}_{k})_{pq},\hskip 11.49994pt\forall k\in[m].

Proof.

The proof is based on the fact that the spectral projectors
\mathbold{P}_{k}
are polynomials in
\mathbold{M}
via Sylvester’s formula. We say a coloring
\mathbold{v}
refines a matrix
\mathbold{A}

\mathbold{v}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}_{pq}\implies A_{ij}=A_{pq}.

We proceed by induction to demonstrate that the multiset aggregation effectively simulates matrix multiplication: if the

\mathbold{M}^{d}
, the next iteration refines
\mathbold{M}^{d+1}

\mathbold{M}

At initialization

\mathbold{v}^{0}

\mathbold{M}^{1}

\mathbold{M}
. Besides,
\mathbold{v}^{0}

\mathbold{M}^{0}=\mathbold{I}

\mathbb{I}_{i=j}
.

Assume that at iteration

\mathbold{v}^{t}
refines the matrix power
\mathbold{M}^{d}

\mathbold{v}^{t}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{t}_{pq}\implies\left(\mathbold{M}^{d}\right)_{ij}=\left(\mathbold{M}^{d}\right)_{pq}.

We show that the next iteration
\mathbold{v}^{t+1}
will refine
\mathbold{M}^{d+1}

\mathbold{v}^{t+1}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{t+1}_{pq}\implies\left(\mathbold{M}^{d+1}\right)_{ij}=\left(\mathbold{M}^{d+1}\right)_{pq}.

Specifically, by definition of the multiset

\displaystyle{\mathbold\displaystyle v}^{t+1}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{t+1}_{pq}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{t},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t},\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t}\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)=\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\mathbold{v}_{pq}^{t},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{uq}^{t},\mathbold{v}_{pu}^{t}\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t},\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t}\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)=\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{uq}^{t},\mathbold{v}_{pu}^{t}\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle f\Bigl(\{\kern-2.24998pt\{g\left(\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t},\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t}\right)\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)=f\Bigl(\{\kern-2.24998pt\{g\left(\mathbold{v}_{uq}^{t},\mathbold{v}_{pu}^{t}\right)\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)

The last implication holds for all choices of functions
f,g
due to the injectivity of hash function, as long as
g
is invariant to the order of inputs. In this specific case, we pick
f(\cdots)
to be sum over a multiset. The choice for
g
is trickier, we introduce extra functions
h_{1}\left(\mathbold{v}^{t}_{ij}\right)=\left(\mathbold{M}^{d}\right)_{ij}
and
h_{2}\left(\mathbold{v}^{t}_{ij}\right)=\left(\mathbold{M}^{1}\right)_{ij}
, and
g\left(a,b\right)=h_{1}(a)h_{2}(b)+h_{2}(a)h_{1}(b)
. The construction for function
h_{1}
is valid because of the induction assumption
\mathbold{v}^{t}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{t}_{pq}\implies\left(\mathbold{M}^{d}\right)_{ij}=\left(\mathbold{M}^{d}\right)_{pq}
, we can find such a function
h_{1}
that maps the colors to the matrix entries of
\mathbold{M}^{d}
. The construction for function
h_{2}
is also valid because
\mathbold{v}^{0}
already refines
\mathbold{M}
, and
\mathbold{v}^{t}
refines
\mathbold{v}^{0}
. Besides,
g
is invariant to the order of the inputs:
g\left(a,b\right)=g\left(b,a\right)
, compatible for the multiset.

Further, we have

\displaystyle f\Bigl(\{\kern-2.24998pt\{g\left(\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t},\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t}\right)\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in[n]}g\left(\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t},\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in[n]}h_{1}\left(\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t}\right)h_{2}\left(\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t}\right)+h_{2}\left(\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t}\right)h_{1}\left(\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in[n]}\left(\mathbold{M}^{d}\right)_{uj}\left(\mathbold{M}^{1}\right)_{iu}+\left(\mathbold{M}^{1}\right)_{uj}\left(\mathbold{M}^{d}\right)_{iu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in[n]}\left(\mathbold{M}^{1}\right)_{iu}\left(\mathbold{M}^{d}\right)_{uj}+\sum_{u\in[n]}\left(\mathbold{M}^{d}\right)_{iu}\left(\mathbold{M}^{1}\right)_{uj}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathbold{M}\mathbold{M}^{d}\right)_{ij}+\left(\mathbold{M}^{d}\mathbold{M}\right)_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(\mathbold{M}^{d+1}\right)_{ij}

Likewise, we have
f\Bigl(\{\kern-2.24998pt\{g\left(\mathbold{v}_{uq}^{t},\mathbold{v}_{pu}^{t}\right)\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)=2\left(\mathbold{M}^{d+1}\right)_{pq}
. Therefore,
\mathbold{v}^{t+1}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{t+1}_{pq}\implies\left(\mathbold{M}^{d+1}\right)_{ij}=\left(\mathbold{M}^{d+1}\right)_{pq}
.

By induction, the stable coloring
\mathbold{v}^{\infty}
refines
\mathbold{M}^{d}
for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Consequently, it also refines any matrix polynomial
p(\mathbold{M})=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}a_{d}\mathbold{M}^{d}
. This holds because the expressivity to distinguish the structural features of individual powers
\mathbold{M}^{d}
implies the ability to distinguish their linear combinations.
The real-valued matrix
\mathbold{M}
is symmetric and diagonalizable, therefore we apply spectral decomposition
\mathbold{M}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}\mathbold{P}_{k}
, where matrices
\mathbold{P}_{k}
are given by the Sylvester’s formula
(
Horn & Johnson 1994
, p. 437)
:

\mathbold{P}_{k}=\prod_{j\neq k}\frac{\mathbold{M}-\lambda_{j}\mathbold{I}}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}.

Because each
\mathbold{P}_{k}
is a polynomial in
\mathbold{M}
, the stable coloring
\mathbold{v}^{\infty}
refines each
\mathbold{P}_{k}
. Thus, it holds for all
k
that
\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{\infty}=\mathbold{v}_{pq}^{\infty}\implies(\mathbold{P}_{k})_{ij}=(\mathbold{P}_{k})_{pq}
.
∎

Remark C.16
.

The lemma above analyzes multiset
2\text{-}\mathsf{FWL}
, a simplified
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
algorithm acting solely on a symmetric matrix
\mathbold{M}
without constraint nodes. Note that our full
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
architecture
Section

2.2
subsumes this process. The
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
variable update aggregates the same multiset of variable neighbors
\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{uj},\mathbold{v}_{iu}\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\}
together with constraint information. Therefore,
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
is no less expressive than the simplified process, as it uses strictly more information for hashing, and any refinement made by the simplified process is guaranteed for
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
as well. Thus, the spectral refinement properties proven above apply directly to the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
variable colors. Now with the lemma, we formally prove
Theorem

2.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

2.3
.

We provide a constructive proof by simulating the primal-dual hybrid gradient (PDHG) algorithm
(
Chambolle & Pock 2016
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
. We show that the stable coloring of
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
refines every update of PDHG algorithm, which is proven to converge.

As established in
Section

C.3
, the PDHG updates for the Frobenius-regularized SDP are given by:

\displaystyle\mathbold{X}^{t+1}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}}\left[\dfrac{\mathbold{X}^{t}-\alpha_{t}\mathcal{A}^{\!*}(\mathbold{y}^{t})-\alpha_{t}\mathbold{C}}{1+\alpha_{t}\varepsilon}\right]

(26)

\displaystyle\mathbold{y}^{t+1}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathbold{y}^{t}+\beta_{t}\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbold{X}^{t+1}+\theta_{t}\left(\mathbold{X}^{t+1}-\mathbold{X}^{t}\right)\right)-\beta_{t}\mathbold{b}.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, the SDP problem has a unique primal solution
\mathbold{X}^{*}
. And if
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, it reduces to normal linear SDP. We proceed by induction on
t
, initializing with
\mathbold{X}^{0}=\bm{0}_{n\times n}
and
\mathbold{y}^{0}=\bm{0}_{m}
.
Our goal is to show that for all
t\geq 0
, the stable
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
coloring on variable and constraint nodes refines the intermediate primal-dual solutions of PDHG updates:

\displaystyle\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{pq}

\displaystyle\implies X^{t}_{ij}=X^{t}_{pq}

\displaystyle\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{k}=\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{l}

\displaystyle\implies y^{t}_{k}=y^{t}_{l}.

Before the inductive step, we establish three key facts derived from the properties of the stable coloring.

Fact 1
Since the stable coloring
\mathbold{v}^{\infty}
refines the initial coloring
\mathbold{v}^{0}
, and
\mathbold{v}^{0}
is defined by
\mathbold{C}
, it holds that
\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{pq}\implies C_{ij}=C_{pq}
. Furthermore, by applying
Lemma

C.15
to
\mathbold{C}
, if its spectral decomposition is given by
\mathbold{C}=\sum_{h}\lambda_{h}\mathbold{P}_{h}
, then the projection matrices also respect the coloring:
\forall h\colon\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{pq}\implies(\mathbold{P}_{h})_{ij}=(\mathbold{P}_{h})_{pq}
. Besides, we know
\mathbold{c}^{\infty}
refines
\mathbold{b}
by its initialization
\mathbold{c}^{0}_{k}\coloneq\mathsf{hash}(b_{k})
.

Fact 2
Consider any two pairs
(i,j),(p,q)\in[n]^{2}
. According to
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
definition
Section

2.2
, if they have the same stable color, their
\mathsf{hash}
function entries must be identical. Specifically, for all choices of function
f
:

\displaystyle{\mathbold\displaystyle v}^{\infty}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{pq}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{\infty},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{\infty},\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{\infty}\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{k})\mid k\in N(ij)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)=

\displaystyle\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\mathbold{v}_{pq}^{\infty},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{uq}^{\infty},\mathbold{v}_{pu}^{\infty}\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(A_{k,pq},\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{k})\mid k\in N(pq)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{k})\mid k\in N(ij)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)=\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(A_{k,pq},\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{k})\mid k\in N(pq)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in N(ij)}f\left(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{k}\right)=\sum_{k\in N(pq)}f\left(A_{k,pq},\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in N(ij)}A_{k,ij}f\left(\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{k}\right)=\sum_{k\in N(pq)}A_{k,pq}f\left(\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{k}\right),\text{(we overload $f$ for ease of notation)}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in[m]}A_{k,ij}f\left(\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{k}\right)=\sum_{k\in[m]}A_{k,pq}f\left(\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{k}\right)

The last implication holds as
\forall k\notin N(ij)\colon A_{k,ij}=0
, by definition of neighbors. Consequently, for any function
f
mapping the
\mathbold{c}^{\infty}
to real numbers, the summations over these multisets are identical. In the inductive steps, we pick function
f
to map stable coloring to intermediate dual variables,
f(\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{k})=y_{k}^{t}
for some suitable
t
, as we will show later.

Fact 3
Similarly for constraint nodes, consider
k,l\in[m]
. According to
Section

2.2
, if two constraint nodes have the same stable colors, we have, for any function
g
:

\displaystyle{\mathbold\displaystyle c}^{\infty}_{k}=\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{l}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{k},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\left(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{\infty}\right)\mid(i,j)\in N(k)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)=\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{l},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\left(A_{l,ij},\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{\infty}\right)\mid(i,j)\in N(l)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\left(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{\infty}\right)\mid(i,j)\in N(k)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)=\mathsf{hash}\Bigl(\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\left(A_{l,ij},\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{\infty}\right)\mid(i,j)\in N(l)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in N(k)}g\left(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{\infty}\right)=\sum_{(i,j)\in N(l)}g\left(A_{l,ij},\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in N(k)}A_{k,ij}g\left(\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{\infty}\right)=\sum_{(i,j)\in N(l)}A_{l,ij}g\left(\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{\infty}\right),\text{(we overload $g$ for ease of notation)}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in[n]^{2}}A_{k,ij}g\left(\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{\infty}\right)=\sum_{(i,j)\in[n]^{2}}A_{l,ij}g\left(\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{\infty}\right)

In the inductive steps, we will pick function
g
to map stable coloring to intermediate primal variables, similar to the choice of
f
.

Proof by induction

We now prove the theorem by induction on
t
.
At initialization
t=0
, we have
\mathbold{X}^{0}=\bm{0}
and
\mathbold{y}^{0}=\bm{0}
. The implications
\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{pq}\implies X^{0}_{ij}=X^{0}_{pq}
and
\mathbold{c}_{k}^{\infty}=\mathbold{c}_{l}^{\infty}\implies y_{k}^{0}=y_{l}^{0}
hold trivially.

Suppose the hypothesis holds for iteration
t\geq 0
. We show it holds for iteration
t+1
.

1. Primal Update.

In the primal update step, we must evaluate the term
\mathcal{A}^{\!*}(\mathbold{y}^{t})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}y^{t}_{k}\mathbold{A}_{k}
.
We define the function
f
in Fact 2 as
f\left(\mathbold{c}_{k}^{\infty}\right)=y^{t}_{k}
. This function is well-defined because, by the inductive hypothesis,
y^{t}_{k}
is refined by the color
\mathbold{c}_{k}^{\infty}
, i.e.,
\mathbold{c}_{k}^{\infty}=\mathbold{c}_{l}^{\infty}\implies y^{t}_{k}=y^{t}_{l}
.
Applying Fact 2 with this
f
, we obtain:

\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{pq}\implies\sum_{k\in[m]}A_{k,ij}y^{t}_{k}=\sum_{k\in[m]}A_{k,pq}y^{t}_{k}.

The implication above shows that
\mathcal{A}^{\!*}(\mathbold{y}^{t})
is refined by the coloring
\mathbold{v}^{\infty}
.
Now consider the full term inside the PSD projection in
Equation

26
:

\mathbold{Z}^{t}\coloneq\dfrac{\mathbold{X}^{t}-\alpha_{t}\mathcal{A}^{\!*}(\mathbold{y}^{t})-\alpha_{t}\mathbold{C}}{1+\alpha_{t}\varepsilon}.

Since
\mathbold{X}^{t}
(by hypothesis),
\mathbold{C}
(by Fact 1), and
\mathcal{A}^{\!*}(\mathbold{y}^{t})
(by derivation) are all refined by the coloring
\mathbold{v}^{\infty}
, their linear combination
\mathbold{Z}^{t}
also is.
Finally, the update is
\mathbold{X}^{t+1}=\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}}(\mathbold{Z}^{t})
. The PSD projection depends only on the spectral decomposition as detailed in
Algorithm

1
. Specifically, spectral decomposition is performed on
\mathbold{Z}^{t}
, and the Frobenius covariants corresponding to negative eigenvalues are removed. By
Lemma

C.15
, we know
\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{ij}=\mathbold{v}^{\infty}_{pq}\implies X^{t+1}_{ij}=X^{t+1}_{pq}
, that is,
\mathbold{v}^{\infty}
refines
\mathbold{X}^{t+1}
.

2. Dual Update.

In the dual update step, we evaluate the term inside the operator
\mathcal{A}
:

\mathbold{W}^{t+1}\coloneq\mathbold{X}^{t+1}+\theta_{t}\left(\mathbold{X}^{t+1}-\mathbold{X}^{t}\right).

Since both
\mathbold{X}^{t+1}
and
\mathbold{X}^{t}
are refined by
\mathbold{v}^{\infty}
, so is
\mathbold{W}^{t+1}
.
We define the function
g
in Fact 3 as
g\left(\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{\infty}\right)=\left(\mathbold{W}^{t+1}\right)_{ij}
. This is well-defined because the value of
\left(\mathbold{W}^{t+1}\right)_{ij}
is refined by the color
\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{\infty}
.
Applying Fact 3 with this
g
, we obtain:

\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{k}=\mathbold{c}^{\infty}_{l}\implies\left[\mathcal{A}(\mathbold{W}^{t+1})\right]_{k}=\left[\mathcal{A}(\mathbold{W}^{t+1})\right]_{l}.

Since
\mathbold{y}^{t}
(by hypothesis) and
\mathbold{b}
(by Fact 1) are both refined by the coloring
\mathbold{c}^{\infty}
, the sum
\mathbold{y}^{t+1}
is refined by the coloring.

In summary, we have proven by induction that if the stable coloring
\mathbold{v}^{\infty}
and
\mathbold{c}^{\infty}
refine
\mathbold{X}^{t}
and
\mathbold{y}^{t}
in PDHG algorithm, they also refine
\mathbold{X}^{t+1}
and
\mathbold{y}^{t+1}
.

Since PDHG is guaranteed to converge to
\mathbold{X}^{*}
and
\mathbold{y}^{*}
, this completes the proof.
∎

Remark C.17
.

Notably, this result extends to comparisons across distinct SDP instances. The pairs
(i,j)
and
(p,q)
do not need to belong to the same problem: provided consistent initialization, if their stable
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
colorings are identical, their corresponding optimal values are guaranteed to be equal. This implies a form of universality, suggesting that the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
hierarchy captures fundamental structural invariants that generalize across the whole space of SDP problems.

C.5
Proof for complexity analysis

Lemma C.18

(Restatement of Theorem 12 of
Berkholz et al. 2017
)
.

Let
G=(V,E,c)
be an edge colored digraph with
N=|V|
and
M=|E|
. In time
O((N+M)\log(N+M))
, a canonical coarsest edge-color stable coloring can be computed for
G
.

Proposition C.19

(Restatement of
Proposition

2.4
)
.

Given an SDP of
n\times n
variables and
m
constraints,
\text{nnz}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}})
denotes the number of non-zero entries in
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
, then
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
converges in time

\mathcal{O}\left((n^{3}+\text{nnz}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}))\log n\right).

Proof.

We analyze the computational complexity of
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
by demonstrating that its update rule is algorithmically equivalent to standard
1\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
(color refinement) operating on a directed, edge-colored auxiliary graph. This equivalence allows us to invoke the tight complexity bounds established in
Lemma

C.18
.

Auxiliary graph construction

Let
G_{\text{aux}}=({V},{E})
be the implicit auxiliary graph defined by the update rules in
Section

2.2
.
The node set
{V}
is the disjoint union of variable nodes and constraint nodes, and a set of higher-order nodes:
{V}=V_{\text{var}}\cup V_{\text{con}}\cup V_{\text{tri}}
.

1.

V_{\text{var}}
consists of the
n^{2}
variable nodes. These are initialized with colors
\mathbold{a}_{ij}^{0}\coloneq\alpha_{\text{var}}(C_{ij})
, where
\alpha_{\text{var}}
is a canonical mapping for variables.

2.

V_{\text{con}}
consists of the
m
constraint nodes. These are initialized with colors
\mathbold{b}^{0}_{k}\coloneq\alpha_{\text{con}}(b_{k})
, where
\alpha_{\text{con}}
is distinct from
\alpha_{\text{var}}
.

3.

We further construct a node set
V_{\text{tri}}
. For every variable node with index
(i,j)
, we construct
n
nodes with index
(i,u,j)
for
u\in[n]
. These are initialized with colors
\mathbold{c}^{0}_{iuj}=\alpha_{\text{tri}}(\{\kern-2.24998pt\{C_{iu},C_{uj}\}\kern-2.24998pt\})
.

Since the initialization functions
\alpha_{(\cdot)}
have disjoint ranges, the color classes of three node sets remain disjoint throughout the execution. The total number of nodes is
N=n^{2}+m+n^{3}
.

The edge set
{E}
decomposes into the following:

1.

E_{\text{tri-v}}\subseteq V_{\text{tri}}\times V_{\text{var}}
: These edges connect nodes from
V_{\text{tri}}
to
V_{\text{var}}
. Specifically, every variable node indexed
(i,j)
gets links from
\{(i,u,j)\mid u\in[n]\}
. This requires
n^{3}
edges. The edge colors can be a constant.

2.

E_{\text{v-tri}}\subseteq V_{\text{var}}\times V_{\text{tri}}
: This is the other direction of above. Every higher order node
(i,u,j)
gets links from variable nodes
(i,u)
and
(u,j)
, thus
2n^{3}
edges. The edge colors can be a constant.

3.

E_{\text{v-c}}\subseteq V_{\text{var}}\times V_{\text{con}}
: These edges encode the
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
structure. An edge exists from
(i,j)\in V_{\text{var}}
to
k\in V_{\text{con}}
if and only if
A_{k,ij}\neq 0
. The edge colors are defined by the tensor entries:
e_{ij,k}=\alpha_{A}(A_{k,ij})
. The size of this set is
\text{nnz}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}})
.

4.

E_{\text{c-v}}\subseteq V_{\text{con}}\times V_{\text{var}}
: These edges are the opposite direction from
E_{\text{v-c}}
. The edge colors are the same:
e_{k,ij}=\alpha_{A}(A_{k,ij})
.

The total number of edges is
M=3n^{3}+2\text{nnz}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}})
.

Update equivalence

We now show that the
1\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
update on this edge-colored graph is equivalent to the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
update.

For a constraint node, it updates in the sense of
1\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
as:

\mathbold{b}_{k}^{t+1}\coloneq\mathsf{hash}\left(\mathbold{b}_{k}^{t},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\left({e}_{ij,k}^{t},\mathbold{a}_{ij}^{t}\right)\mid(i,j)\colon A_{k,ij}\neq 0\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\right)

which has exactly the same form as in
Section

2.2
.

For a variable node, we first update the higher-order nodes:

\mathbold{c}_{iuj}^{t+1/2}\coloneq\mathsf{hash}\left(\mathbold{c}_{iuj}^{t},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t},\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t}\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\right),

regardless of the edge colors. Then, we update the variable nodes:

\mathbold{a}_{ij}^{t+1}\coloneq\mathsf{hash}\left(\mathbold{a}_{ij}^{t},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{c}_{i0j}^{t+1/2},\cdots,\mathbold{c}_{inj}^{t+1/2},(e_{k,ij},\mathbold{b}_{k}^{t})\cdots\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\right)

Since the node types
V_{\text{tri}}
and
V_{\text{con}}
are distinguished by disjoint color spaces, we can partition the multiset into two distinct multisets without ambiguity:

\mathbold{a}_{ij}^{t+1}\coloneq\mathsf{hash}\left(\mathbold{a}_{ij}^{t},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{c}_{iuj}^{t+1/2}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\},\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(e_{k,ij},\mathbold{b}_{k}^{t})\mid k\colon A_{k,ij}\neq 0\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\right).

This form matches the variable update in
Section

2.2
. Therefore, the variable update in
Section

2.2
can be implemented with 2 steps of
1\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
.

Hence, we have shown the equivalence of our
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
and standard
1\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
. Applying the bound in
Lemma

C.18
to our setting:
N=|{V}|=n^{2}+m+n^{3}
and
M=|{E}|=\mathcal{O}(n^{3}+\text{nnz}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}))
. Since
m
is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
as we assume linearly independent constraints,
\mathcal{O}((N+M))=\mathcal{O}(n^{3}+\text{nnz}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}))
, and
\mathcal{O}(\log(N+M))=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
. Substituting these values yields the total time complexity:
\mathcal{O}\big((n^{3}+\text{nnz}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}))\log n\big)
.
∎

C.6
Proof for neuralization

Remark C.20
.

It is important to clarify the terminology regarding the
\mathsf{hash}
function utilized in the
\mathsf{WL}
test aggregation step. In standard computer science contexts, cryptographic or data-structure hash functions are inherently discrete and non-continuous. However, in the theoretical framework of GNN expressivity, a hash function simply denotes an injective mapping that assigns a unique, deterministic representation to any distinct multiset of neighbor features. Crucially, this injective mapping is not required to be discrete. As established in the foundational GNN expressivity literature
(
Morris et al. 2019
;
Xu et al. 2018
)
, there exist continuous, injective functions capable of perfectly resolving bounded multisets. Because these idealized
\mathsf{WL}
aggregation functions can be chosen to be strictly continuous, they satisfy the necessary conditions to be arbitrarily approximated by MLPs via the universal approximation theorem.

Proposition C.21

(Restatement of
Proposition

2.5
)
.

There exists a set of parameters for the functions
\mathsf{INIT},\mathsf{UPD},\mathsf{MSG},\mathsf{MAP}
, such that
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FMPNN}
has maximal expressivity equal to the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
.

First, we state a lemma.

Lemma C.22
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact set, and let
\mathcal{X}
be the space of finite multisets of bounded maximum size with elements drawn from
\mathcal{K}
. There exists an integer
p\geq 1
and a continuous, injective function

f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}

such that for any two multisets
X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
,

f(X_{1})=f(X_{2})\iff X_{1}=X_{2}.

This is exactly a restatement of Lemma 4 of
Xu et al. 2018
, if the multiset space is discrete and countable; or of Proposition 1 of
Maron et al. 2019a
, if it is a multiset of real number space, by using the power-sum multi-symmetric polynomial technique. In our case, unlike discrete graphs, we assume that the problem coefficients
\mathbold{C},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}},\mathbold{b}
as well as the intermediate outputs of the neural networks lie within a compact subset
\mathcal{K}
, i.e., real space where the coefficients don’t go to infinity. This ensures that the inputs are bounded and allows for the application of the Stone-Weierstrass theorem for approximation. So we can reuse the proof technique in
Maron et al. 2019a
. Now let’s prove
Proposition

2.5
.

Proof for
Proposition

2.5
.

We prove the equality of expressivity by showing both directions of refinement.

\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}\sqsubseteq\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FMPNN}
:
First,
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FMPNN}
is no more expressive than
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
. It is easy to verify with the same logic as Theorem 1 of
Morris et al. 2019
. The
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FMPNN}
updates are deterministic functions of the unrolling trees defined by the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
color refinement. Therefore, if
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
produces identical colors for two variable pairs,
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FMPNN}
must produce identical embeddings.

\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FMPNN}\sqsubseteq\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
:
We show that
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FMPNN}
can distinguish any two instances distinguishable by
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
at every iteration
t
.

At initialization
t=0
, the initial features are directly encoded from the problem data. Since we assume the input space
\mathcal{K}
is compact, there exist MLPs by the universal approximation theorem that can implement the initialization functions injectively, preserving the distinction between different initial values of
C_{ij}
or
b_{k}
.
At iteration
t>0
, we consider the update for constraint and variable nodes, respectively.

Constraint node
Consider the update of a constraint node
k
. The update requires an injective mapping of the tuple containing the constraint node’s previous feature
\mathbold{h}^{t-1}_{k}
and the multiset of its variable neighbors’ features together with the edge weights:
\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\left(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{v}_{ij}^{t-1}\right)\mid(i,j)\in N(k)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}
.
According to
Lemma

C.22
, there exists an injective function that uniquely encodes this multiset. As shown in the constructive proof of
Maron et al. 2019a
, this injective function can be realized as a summation of power-sum polynomials.
Consequently, the MLP in the term

\mathbold{m}_{k,\text{v}\rightarrow\text{c}}^{t}\coloneq\sum_{(i,j)\in N(k)}\mathsf{MSG}^{t}_{\text{v}\rightarrow\text{c}}\left(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{h}_{ij}^{t-1}\right)

can be parameterized to approximate these continuous mappings, ensuring that the summation yields a unique representation of the multiset.
Furthermore, the injective combination of the node’s previous state
\mathbold{h}_{k}^{t-1}
and this multiset embedding
\mathbold{m}_{k,\text{v}\rightarrow\text{c}}^{t}
can be approximated arbitrarily well by concatenation of them and application of another MLP:

\mathbold{h}^{t}_{k}\coloneq\mathsf{UPD}_{\text{c}}^{t}\left(\mathbold{h}^{t-1}_{k},\mathbold{m}_{k,\text{v}\rightarrow\text{c}}^{t}\right).

Since the intermediate space
\mathcal{K}
is compact, there exist MLPs that universally approximate the injective
\mathsf{hash}
function in
Section

2.2
.

Variable node
Consider the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
update for a variable index
(i,j)
.
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
updates the variable color based on the variable node’s previous feature, the multiset of constraint neighbors and the multiset of variable neighbor pairs. For the multiset of constraint neighbors, it aggregates the multiset
\{\kern-2.24998pt\{(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{c}^{t-1}_{k})\mid k\in N(ij)\}\kern-2.24998pt\}

Lemma

C.22
once, and the term

\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{c}\rightarrow\text{v}}^{t}\coloneq\sum_{k\in N(ij)}\mathsf{MSG}^{t}_{\text{c}\rightarrow\text{v}}\left(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{h}_{k}^{t-1}\right)

implements the injective mapping from constraint neighbors to a vector embedding.
The multiset of variable pairs are formed by
\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t-1},\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t-1}\}\kern-2.24998pt\}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\}
.
To complete this injectively, we invoke
Lemma

C.22
twice.
First, for a specific
u
, the unordered pair
s_{u}=\{\kern-2.24998pt\{\mathbold{v}_{uj}^{t-1},\mathbold{v}_{iu}^{t-1}\}\kern-2.24998pt\}
constitutes a 2-multiset. By
Lemma

C.22

\mathsf{MAP}^{t}\left(\mathbold{h}_{uj}^{t-1}\right)+\mathsf{MAP}^{t}\left(\mathbold{h}_{iu}^{t-1}\right)

Second, we must aggregate these encoded pairs over all
u\in[n]
. The target structure is the outer multiset
S_{ij}=\{\kern-2.24998pt\{s_{u}\mid u\in[n]\}\kern-2.24998pt\}

Lemma

C.22
again, there exists an injective mapping with MLP. Thus, the term

\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{v}\rightarrow\text{v}}^{t}\coloneq\sum_{u\in[n]}\mathsf{MSG}_{\text{v}\rightarrow\text{v}}^{t}\left(\mathsf{MAP}^{t}\left(\mathbold{h}_{uj}^{t-1}\right)+\mathsf{MAP}^{t}\left(\mathbold{h}_{iu}^{t-1}\right)\right)

injectively encodes the nested multiset structure for the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
update. Finally, one can simply concatenate the embeddings
\left(\mathbold{h}_{ij}^{t-1},\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{v}\rightarrow\text{v}}^{t},\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{c}\rightarrow\text{v}}^{t}\right)
and update it with the MLP
\mathsf{UPD}_{\text{v}}^{t}

\mathbold{h}_{ij}^{t}\coloneq\mathsf{UPD}_{\text{v}}^{t}\left(\mathbold{h}_{ij}^{t-1},\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{v}\rightarrow\text{v}}^{t},\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{c}\rightarrow\text{v}}^{t}\right).

In summary, every component of
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
in
Section

2.2
can be approximated by an MLP. By picking the output approximation error
\epsilon
to be smaller than the separation margin of the distinct
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
colors,
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FMPNN}
maintains the injectivity of the update step.

Thus,
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FMPNN}
is at least as expressive as
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FWL}
, which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma C.24
.

Consider a standard form SDP with data
(\mathbold{C},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}},\mathbold{b})
and a triplet of primal, dual and slack solution
(\mathbold{X}^{*},\mathbold{y}^{*},\mathbold{S}^{*})
. If the vector
\mathbold{b}
is scaled by a positive scalar
\alpha>0
to
\alpha\mathbold{b}
, then
\alpha\mathbold{X}^{*}
is an optimal primal solution to the scaled problem.

Proof.

Optimality in linear SDPs is characterized by the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions. The original solution satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\mathbold{X}^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathbold{b}

(28)

\displaystyle\mathbold{C}+\mathcal{A}^{\!*}(\mathbold{y}^{*})-\mathbold{S}^{*}

\displaystyle=\bm{0}

\displaystyle\mathbold{X}^{*}

\displaystyle\succeq 0

\displaystyle\mathbold{S}^{*}

\displaystyle\succeq 0

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbold{X}^{*},\,\mathbold{S}^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=0

It is not hard to find that the following also holds:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\alpha\mathbold{X}^{*})

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathcal{A}(\mathbold{X}^{*})=\alpha\mathbold{b}

(29)

\displaystyle\mathbold{C}+\mathcal{A}^{\!*}(\mathbold{y}^{*})-\mathbold{S}^{*}

\displaystyle=\bm{0}

\displaystyle\alpha\mathbold{X}^{*}

\displaystyle\succeq 0

\displaystyle\mathbold{S}^{*}

\displaystyle\succeq 0

\displaystyle\left\langle\alpha\mathbold{X}^{*},\,\mathbold{S}^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=0.

Therefore,
\alpha\mathbold{X}^{*}
is the solution to the scaled SDP problem; thus, it completes the proof.
∎

Corollary C.25
.

The architecture
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{MPNN}
is not a universal approximator for SDPs. Specifically, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists an SDP instance such that for any parameters of a
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{MPNN}

\mathbold{X}^{\prime}

\mathbold{X}^{*}

\lVert\mathbold{X}^{*}-\mathbold{X}^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\geq\epsilon.

Proof.

According to
Proposition

C.23

\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{MPNN}
is bounded by the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
coloring algorithm.

Consider the counterexample from the proof of
Proposition

2.1
. In this instance, variables
X_{11}
and
X_{33}
are structurally indistinguishable under the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{WL}
algorithm, and thus
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{MPNN}
forces the network to predict identical values, i.e.,
X^{\prime}_{11}=X^{\prime}_{33}=v
. However, in this case, the unique optimal solution satisfies
X^{*}_{11}\neq X^{*}_{33}

\delta=|X^{*}_{11}-X^{*}_{33}|>0

\alpha\mathbold{b}
with
\alpha=2\epsilon/\delta

C.24

Therefore,

∎

Appendix D
SDP application

D.1
Relaxation of CO

In reality, SDP serves as the continuous relaxation of several graph CO problems.

Max-Cut

(
Goemans & Williamson 1995
)
. Let
G
be a graph with nodes
V
and edges
E
with edge weights
w_{ij}\geq 0,\{i,j\}\in E
. The goal of the max-cut problem is to partition the nodes into two disjoint sets such that the sum of weights of edges crossing the cut is maximized. The problem formulation is

\max_{\mathbold{x}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}\quad\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}(1-x_{i}x_{j}),

(31)

where
1
and
-1
denote different partitions. Instead of integer domain
\{-1,1\}
, we relax each variable
x_{i}
to a unit vector
\lVert\mathbold{v}_{i}\rVert=1
, which defines
X_{ij}=\mathbold{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbold{v}_{j}

(
Yau et al. 2024
)
. Thus, SDP relaxation is

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbold{X}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}(1-X_{ij})

(32)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle X_{ii}=1,\forall i\in[n]

Max clique problem

Let
G
be a graph with nodes
V
and edges
E
, let
x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
denote whether
x_{i}
is included in a clique. The max clique problem can be formulated as

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbold{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}x_{i}

(33)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle x_{i}x_{j}=0,\forall\{i,j\}\notin E.

We know that the Lovász function
(
Galli & Letchford 2017
)
is defined as the optimal value of the SDP relaxation of max-clique problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbold{X}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}}

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbold{J},\,\mathbold{X}\right\rangle

(34)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle\Tr(\mathbold{X})=1

\displaystyle X_{ij}=0,\forall\{i,j\}\notin E,

where
\mathbold{J}
is a ones matrix.

Max independent set

The max independent set problem is the complement of the max clique problem. Formally,

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbold{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}x_{i}

(35)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle x_{i}x_{j}=0,\forall\{i,j\}\in E.

and its relaxation:

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbold{X}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}}

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbold{J},\,\mathbold{X}\right\rangle

(36)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle\Tr(\mathbold{X})=1

\displaystyle X_{ij}=0,\forall\{i,j\}\in E.

Graph coloring

The goal of the graph coloring problem is to assign colors to nodes such that no adjacent nodes share the same color. Let
y_{k}\in\{0,1\},k\in[K]
indicate whether the color
k
is used, where
K
is an upper bound, e.g.,
K=|V|
. And let
x_{ik}\in\{0,1\}
indicate whether the node
i
is assigned to color
k
. The graph coloring problem in ILP form is defined as:

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\sum_{k}y_{k}

(37)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{k}x_{ik}=1

\displaystyle x_{ik}+x_{jk}\leq y_{k},\forall\{i,j\}\in E

\displaystyle x_{ik}\leq y_{k}.

We have the SDP relaxation
(
Charikar 2002
)
:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbold{X}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}}

\displaystyle k

(38)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle X_{ij}=-\dfrac{1}{k-1},\forall\{i,j\}\in E

\displaystyle X_{ii}=1.

Min vertex cover

A standard formulation of the minimum vertex cover problem as a quadratic integer program is as following:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbold{x}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{1}{2}(1+x_{i})

(39)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle(1-x_{i})(1-x_{j})=0,\forall\{i,j\}\in E

The SDP relaxation is as following
(
Kleinberg & Goemans 1998
;
Hatami et al. 2009
)
, where we introduce an extra variable:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbold{X}\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{+}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{1}{2}(1+X_{0i})

(40)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle X_{ij}+X_{00}-X_{0i}-X_{0j}=0,\forall\{i,j\}\in E

\displaystyle X_{ii}=1,i\in\{0\}\cup V.

Max 2-SAT

A max SAT problem is given a number of variables
n
, that takes on the values
\{-1,1\}^{n}
, and a collection of clauses
\{C_{1},C_{2},\cdots,C_{k}\}
, each clause is a disjunction (logical OR) of a subset of literals. A literal is either a variable or its negation. Each clause may be assigned a weight, and the goal of Max SAT is to find an assignment of variables that maximizes the total weight of satisfied clauses. A max
k
-SAT problem is one in which each clause has at most
k
literals.

An instance with
n
variables and
k
clauses can be represented by a matrix
\mathbold{A}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{k\times n}
, where
A_{ij}=1
if clause
i
includes the positive literal of variable
j
,
A_{ij}=-1
if it includes the negated literal, and
A_{ij}=0
otherwise. We consider the unweighted setting, where all clause weights are equal, and the objective is to maximize the number of satisfied clauses. The quadratic assignment formulation of the max 2-SAT problem is
(
de Klerk & Warners 2002
)

\min_{\mathbold{x}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}\quad\dfrac{1}{8}\left(\mathbold{x}^{\intercal}\mathbold{A}^{\intercal}\mathbold{A}\mathbold{x}-2\bm{1}^{\intercal}\mathbold{A}\mathbold{x}\right)\\

(41)

whose SDP relaxation is

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbold{X}\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{+}}

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{8}\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbold{A}^{\intercal}\mathbold{A}-\text{diag}\left(\mathbold{A}^{\intercal}\mathbold{A}\right)&-\mathbold{A}^{\intercal}\bm{1}\\
-\bm{1}^{\intercal}\mathbold{A}&0\\
\end{bmatrix},\,\mathbold{X}\right\rangle

(42)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle X_{ii}=1,\forall i\in[n].

D.2
Quadratic assignment problem

Besides, SDP is also a common relaxation for quadratic assignment problems (QAP).

Traveling salesman problem
It is already known that the symmetric traveling salesman problem (TSP) is a special case of QAP. We first introduce the notation of circulant matrices on
n
vertices
\mathcal{C}^{n}
. A circulant matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
has the form

\begin{bmatrix}c_{0}&c_{1}&c_{2}&c_{3}&\cdots&c_{n-1}\\
c_{n-1}&c_{0}&c_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&c_{n-2}\\
c_{n-2}&c_{n-1}&c_{0}&c_{1}&\cdots&c_{n-3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{1}&c_{2}&c_{3}&c_{4}&\cdots&c_{0}\\
\end{bmatrix}

We have a basis for symmetric circulant matrices consisting of
\{\mathbold{C}_{1}^{n},\mathbold{C}_{2}^{n},\cdots,\mathbold{C}_{d}^{n}\}
, where
\mathbold{C}_{i}^{n}
is a matrix with
c_{i}=c_{n-i}=1
and 0 elsewhere. Besides, we have a symmetric matrix
\mathbold{D}
where
D_{ii}=0
and
D_{ij}=D_{ji}>0
is the cost per edge. The objective of TSP is

\min_{\mathbold{X}\in\mathbb{P}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\mathbold{D}\mathbold{X}\mathbold{C}_{1}^{n}\mathbold{X}^{\intercal}),

(43)

where
\mathbb{P}
is the permutation matrix group.
\mathbold{X}\mathbold{C}_{1}^{n}\mathbold{X}
can be interpreted as the adjacency matrix of the tour.

(Sub)graph isomorphism problem
Given adjacency matrices
\mathbold{A},\mathbold{B}
, supposed to be the same shape, we seek the largest isomorphic subgraphs by

\min_{\mathbold{X}\in\mathbb{P}^{n}}\lVert\mathbold{X}\mathbold{A}-\mathbold{B}\mathbold{X}\rVert_{F}.

(44)

With some transformations, we have
\lVert\mathbold{X}\mathbold{A}-\mathbold{B}\mathbold{X}\rVert_{F}=\lVert\mathbold{A}\rVert_{F}-2\Tr(\mathbold{B}\mathbold{X}\mathbold{A}\mathbold{X}^{\intercal})+\lVert\mathbold{B}\rVert_{F}
, thus, the problem is equivalent to

\max_{\mathbold{X}\in\mathbb{P}^{n}}\Tr(\mathbold{B}\mathbold{X}\mathbold{A}\mathbold{X}^{\intercal}).

(45)

The TSP and GIP are special cases of QAP. The relaxation of SDP for QAP has been well-studied
(
Povh & Rendl 2009
;
Oliveira et al. 2018
)
.

D.3
Subsumption of convex optimization

SDP subsumes a lot of convex optimization problems, including second-order conic programming (SOCP), quadratically-constrained quadratic programming (QCQP), linearly-constrained quadratic programming (LCQP), and linear programming (LP)
(
Dattorro 2010
, p. 220)
.

LP
It is straightforward to show that LP is a special case of SDP. Given an LP instance

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbold{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}}

\displaystyle{\mathbold\displaystyle c}^{\intercal}\mathbold{x}

(46)

\displaystyle\text{ s.t. }

\displaystyle{\mathbold\displaystyle A}\mathbold{x}=\mathbold{b},

we can turn it into an SDP instance by constructing
\mathbold{C}=\text{diag}(\mathbold{c})
and
\mathbold{A}_{i}=\text{diag}(\mathbold{A}_{i})
, and extra constraints
X_{ij}=0,i\neq j
. The positive semidefiniteness of SDP variables perfectly captures the positivity of the LP variables, as the SDP variables are forced to form a diagonal matrix.

SOCP
The formulation of an SOCP is

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle{\mathbold\displaystyle c}^{\intercal}\mathbold{x}

(47)

\displaystyle\text{ s.t. }

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbold{A}_{(i)}\mathbold{x}+\mathbold{b}_{(i)}\rVert_{2}\leq\mathbold{c}_{(i)}^{\intercal}\mathbold{x}+d_{(i)},\forall i\in[m],

where
\mathbold{A}_{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{i}\times n},\mathbold{b}_{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{i}},\mathbold{c}_{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},d_{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}
. The positivity constraints of variables can also be captured by such forms. Each constraint is in a second order cone
\mathcal{L}^{k+1}\coloneq\{(\mathbold{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}|\lVert\mathbold{x}\rVert_{2}\leq t\}
, hence the name. Using Schur decomposition, we turn the second order cone
\lVert\mathbold{x}\rVert_{2}\leq t
into a PSD cone
\begin{bmatrix}t&\mathbold{x}^{\intercal}\\
\mathbold{x}&t\mathbold{I}\\
\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0
. So we can turn the SOCP problem into an SDP problem
(
Lobo et al. 1998
)

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle{\mathbold\displaystyle c}^{\intercal}\mathbold{x}

(48)

\displaystyle\text{ s.t. }

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbold{c}_{(i)}^{\intercal}\mathbold{x}+d_{(i)}&\left(\mathbold{A}_{(i)}\mathbold{x}+\mathbold{b}_{(i)}\right)^{\intercal}\\
\mathbold{A}_{(i)}\mathbold{x}+\mathbold{b}_{(i)}&\left(\mathbold{c}_{(i)}^{\intercal}\mathbold{x}+d_{(i)}\right)\mathbold{I}\\
\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,\forall i\in[m].

Note that it is not the standard primal form of
Equation

1
, but a dual form

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle{\mathbold\displaystyle b}^{\intercal}\mathbold{y}

(49)

\displaystyle\text{ s.t. }

\displaystyle{\mathbold\displaystyle C}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}\mathbold{A}_{i}\succeq 0.

QP
To show that LCQPs and QCQPs are also special cases of SDP, we just need to show they are included in the SOCP family. Given a QCQP instance

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle{\mathbold\displaystyle x}^{\intercal}\mathbold{Q}_{(0)}\mathbold{x}+2\mathbold{q}_{(0)}^{\intercal}\mathbold{x}+q_{(0)}

(50)

\displaystyle\text{ s.t. }

\displaystyle{\mathbold\displaystyle x}^{\intercal}\mathbold{Q}_{(i)}\mathbold{x}+2\mathbold{q}_{(i)}^{\intercal}\mathbold{x}+q_{(i)}\leq 0,\forall i\in[m],

for the problem to be convex, we assume
\mathbold{Q}_{(i)}
to be PSD matrices. This allows us to transform the QCQP in the following form
(
Lobo et al. 1998
)
:

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbold{Q}_{(0)}^{1/2}\mathbold{x}+\mathbold{Q}_{(0)}^{-1/2}\mathbold{q}_{(0)}\rVert_{2}^{2}+c_{(0)}-\mathbold{q}_{(0)}^{\intercal}\mathbold{Q}_{(0)}^{-1}\mathbold{q}_{(0)}

(51)

\displaystyle\text{ s.t. }

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbold{Q}_{(i)}^{1/2}\mathbold{x}+\mathbold{Q}_{(i)}^{-1/2}\mathbold{q}_{(i)}\rVert_{2}^{2}+c_{(i)}-\mathbold{q}_{(i)}^{\intercal}\mathbold{Q}_{(i)}^{-1}\mathbold{q}_{(i)}\leq 0,\forall i\in[m],

and further in SOCP form

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle t

(52)

\displaystyle\text{ s.t. }

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbold{Q}_{(i)}^{1/2}\mathbold{x}+\mathbold{Q}_{(i)}^{-1/2}\mathbold{q}_{(i)}\rVert_{2}\leq\left(\mathbold{q}_{(i)}^{\intercal}\mathbold{Q}_{(i)}^{-1}\mathbold{q}_{(i)}-c_{(i)}\right)^{1/2},\forall i\in[m]

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbold{Q}_{(0)}^{1/2}\mathbold{x}+\mathbold{Q}_{(0)}^{-1/2}\mathbold{q}_{(0)}\rVert_{2}\leq t.

And LCQP is a special case of QCQP.

D.4
Control theory

Linear Matrix Inequality

Lyapunov stability analysis is a fundamental problem in control theory used to verify if a dynamical system
\dot{z}=\mathbold{A}_{sys}z
is stable. A necessary and sufficient condition for stability is the existence of a quadratic Lyapunov function
V(z)=z^{\intercal}\mathbold{P}z
with
\mathbold{P}\succ 0
such that the derivative
\dot{V}(z)<0
. Formally, this is the Linear Matrix Inequality (LMI):

\displaystyle\text{Find }

\displaystyle{\mathbold\displaystyle P}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}

(53)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle{\mathbold\displaystyle A}_{sys}^{\intercal}\mathbold{P}+\mathbold{P}\mathbold{A}_{sys}\prec 0.

To optimize numerical properties or check for specific performance bounds, this is cast as a Semidefinite Program. We adopt the formulation from
Boyd et al. 1994
, enforcing a trace constraint to bound the solution scale and discretizing the matrix inequality into
m
scalar linear constraints:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbold{P}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}}

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbold{C},\,\mathbold{P}\right\rangle

(54)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle\Tr(\mathbold{P})=1

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\mathbold{P})=\mathbold{b}.

where each constraint matrix
\mathbold{A}_{k}
represents the projection of the Lyapunov operator onto a random direction vector
v_{k}
:

\mathbold{A}_{k}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbold{A}_{sys}v_{k}v_{k}^{\intercal}+v_{k}v_{k}^{\intercal}\mathbold{A}_{sys}^{\intercal}).

We employ an inverse strategy to generate feasible problem instances with known stability certificates. We first sample a random positive definite matrix
\mathbold{P}_{true}
and normalize it such that
\Tr(\mathbold{P}_{true})=1
. Instead of sampling a random system
\mathbold{A}_{sys}
(which might be unstable), we construct a stable system compatible with our certificate. We solve the inverse Lyapunov equation
\mathbold{A}_{sys}^{\intercal}\mathbold{P}_{true}+\mathbold{P}_{true}\mathbold{A}_{sys}=-\epsilon\mathbold{I}
to recover
\mathbold{A}_{sys}
. We sample
m
random sparse direction vectors
v_{k}
. We compute the RHS scalars
\left\langle\mathbold{A}_{k},\,\mathbold{P}_{true}=\mathbold{b}\right\rangle
. This ensures that the ground truth
\mathbold{P}_{true}
lies exactly on the boundary of the feasible region defined by the hyperplanes.

IGN

As an alternative instantiation of the variable update mechanism, we consider the 2-order Invariant Graph Network (
\text{2-}\mathsf{IGN}
), based on the theoretical characterization of permutation equivariant layers by
Maron et al. 2019b
.

The
\text{2-}\mathsf{IGN}
operates on second-order tensors. For a general linear layer mapping
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
(feature dimension omitted for simplicity), the space of permutation equivariant operations is spanned by a basis of size
b(2+2)=15
, where
b(\cdot)
denotes the Bell number. However, our application to SDPs imposes a strict geometric constraint: both the input feature matrix and the output updated features must lie in the symmetric space
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. This symmetry constraint reduces the dimension of the equivariant basis from 15 to 9. The reduction occurs through two mechanisms:

1.

Input symmetry
: Since
\mathbold{X}_{ij}=\mathbold{X}_{ji}
, operations that distinguish rows from columns become redundant, e.g., summing over rows yields the same vector as summing over columns.

2.

Output symmetry
: To guarantee the output is symmetric, the corresponding basis operations must share weights. For instance, broadcasting a vector to rows must be paired with broadcasting to columns.

Formally, let
\bm{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the all-ones vector,
\mathbold{I}
be the identity matrix,
\mathbold{d}=\text{diag}(\mathbold{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the vector of diagonal entries, and
\mathbold{s}=\mathbold{X}\bm{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the vector of row sums. We identify the following 9 basis operations
\mathcal{B}=\{\mathsf{Op}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{Op}_{9}\}
that map a symmetric
\mathbold{X}
to a symmetric output:

1.

\mathsf{Op}_{1}(\mathbold{X})=\mathbold{X}
. Identity.

2.

\mathsf{Op}_{2}(\mathbold{X})=\Tr(\mathbold{X})\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\intercal}
. Global trace broadcast.

3.

\mathsf{Op}_{3}(\mathbold{X})=(\bm{1}^{\intercal}\mathbold{X}\bm{1})\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\intercal}
. Global sum broadcast.

4.

\mathsf{Op}_{4}(\mathbold{X})=\text{diag}(\mathbold{d})
. Copy diagonal.

5.

\mathsf{Op}_{5}(\mathbold{X})=\text{diag}(\mathbold{s})
. Row sums to diagonal.

6.

\mathsf{Op}_{6}(\mathbold{X})=\Tr(\mathbold{X})\mathbold{I}
. Trace to diagonal.

7.

\mathsf{Op}_{7}(\mathbold{X})=(\bm{1}^{\intercal}\mathbold{X}\bm{1})\mathbold{I}
. Global sum to diagonal.

8.

\mathsf{Op}_{8}(\mathbold{X})=\mathbold{s}\bm{1}^{\intercal}+\bm{1}\mathbold{s}^{\intercal}
. Row/Col sums broadcast.

9.

\mathsf{Op}_{9}(\mathbold{X})=\mathbold{d}\bm{1}^{\intercal}+\bm{1}\mathbold{d}^{\intercal}
. Diagonal entries broadcast.

In the context of deep learning, we extend these operations to multi-dimensional inputs
\mathbold{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times f}
. For each basis operation
\mathsf{Op}_{b}\in\mathcal{B}
, we assign a learnable weight matrix
\mathbold{W}_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{f\times f^{\prime}}
. The
\text{2-}\mathsf{IGN}
layer update is defined as the aggregation of these transformed bases:

\mathbold{X}^{\prime}=\sum_{b=1}^{9}\mathsf{Op}_{b}(\mathbold{X})\mathbold{W}_{b}\hskip 11.49994pt\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times f^{\prime}}.

This formulation ensures that the neural network remains strictly equivariant and preserves the symmetric matrix structure required by the SDP relaxation.

We wrap this up as a neural network
\mathsf{IGN}
and plug it into our V-C bipartite graph message passing, and we have the following form of
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{IGN}
:

\displaystyle\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{IGN}}^{t}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{IGN}^{t}(\mathbold{H}^{t-1})_{ij}

\displaystyle\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{c}\rightarrow\text{v}}^{t}

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{k\in N(ij)}\mathsf{MSG}^{t}_{\text{c}\rightarrow\text{v}}\left(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{h}_{k}^{t-1}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbold{m}_{k,\text{v}\rightarrow\text{c}}^{t}

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{(i,j)\in N(k)}\mathsf{MSG}^{t}_{\text{v}\rightarrow\text{c}}\left(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{h}_{ij}^{t-1}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbold{h}_{ij}^{t}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{UPD}_{\text{v}}^{t}\left(\mathbold{h}_{ij}^{t-1},\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{IGN}}^{t},\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{c}\rightarrow\text{v}}^{t}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbold{h}^{t}_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{UPD}_{\text{c}}^{t}\left(\mathbold{h}^{t-1}_{k},\mathbold{m}_{k,\text{v}\rightarrow\text{c}}^{t}\right)

where
\mathbold{H}^{t-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times d}
is the tensor of variable features
\mathbold{h}_{ij}^{t-1}

E.4
Edge Transformer

We adapt the Edge Transformer (ET) module introduced by
Müller et al. 2024
. The ET architecture is structurally analogous to standard
2\text{-}\mathsf{FWL}
. While the original ET operates on graph node pairs, our framework operates on variables in a matrix, sharing the same pairwise form. Furthermore, the ET has been rigorously proven to possess the expressive power of the
2\text{-}\mathsf{FWL}
algorithm. These properties make it a perfect candidate to compare with our
\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{FMPNN}
.

The ET operates on a second-order tensor
\mathbold{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times d}
, where
d
is the embedding dimension and
\mathbold{X}_{ij}
denotes the feature vector of the variable indexed at
(i,j)
.
Formally, the layer update is defined as:

\mathbold{X}^{t}_{ij}\coloneqq\mathsf{FFN}^{t}\bigl(\mathbold{X}^{t-1}_{ij}+\mathsf{TriAttn}^{t}\bigl(\mathsf{LN}^{t}\bigl(\mathbold{X}^{t-1}_{ij}\bigr)\bigr)\bigr),

where
\mathsf{FFN}
is a feed-forward neural network,
\mathsf{LN}
denotes layer normalization
(
Ba et al. 2016
)
and
\mathsf{TriAttn}
is defined as

\mathsf{TriAttn}(\mathbold{X}_{ij})\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{n}\alpha_{ilj}\mathbold{V}_{ilj}.

(59)

This operation computes a linear combination of a
value vector

\mathbold{V}_{ilj}
, with weights given by scalar attention scores
\alpha{ilj}
, over the summation index
l
.
The attention scores are computed via a scaled dot-product mechanism:

\alpha_{ilj}\coloneqq\underset{l\in[n]}{\mathsf{softmax}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathbold{X}_{il}\mathbold{W}^{Q}\bigl(\mathbold{X}_{lj}\mathbold{W}^{K}\bigr)^{\intercal}\Bigr)\in\mathbb{R}

(60)

which captures the relevance of the triplet
(i,l,j)
, and the value vector is computed via the
value fusion
of the intermediate representations

\mathbold{V}_{ilj}\coloneqq\mathbold{X}_{il}\mathbold{W}^{V_{1}}\odot\mathbold{X}_{lj}\mathbold{W}^{V_{2}},

(61)

where
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication, and
\mathbold{W}^{Q},\mathbold{W}^{K},\mathbold{W}^{V_{1}},\mathbold{W}^{V_{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are learnable projection matrices.

Integrating this module into our framework yields the
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{ET}
architecture. The initialization follows
Equation

5
. For the update step, we substitute the variable-to-variable message passing with the ET layer. Let
\mathbold{H}^{t-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times d}
denote the tensor of variable features
\mathbold{h}_{ij}^{t-1}
. The update rules are:

\displaystyle\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{ET}}^{t}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{ET}^{t}\left(\mathbold{H}^{t-1}\right)_{ij}

(62)

\displaystyle\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{c}\rightarrow\text{v}}^{t}

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{k\in N(ij)}\mathsf{MSG}^{t}_{\text{c}\rightarrow\text{v}}\left(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{h}_{k}^{t-1}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbold{m}_{k,\text{v}\rightarrow\text{c}}^{t}

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{(i,j)\in N(k)}\mathsf{MSG}^{t}_{\text{v}\rightarrow\text{c}}\left(A_{k,ij},\mathbold{h}_{ij}^{t-1}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbold{h}_{ij}^{t}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{UPD}_{\text{v}}^{t}\left(\mathbold{h}_{ij}^{t-1},\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{ET}}^{t},\mathbold{m}_{ij,\text{c}\rightarrow\text{v}}^{t}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbold{h}^{t}_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathsf{UPD}_{\text{c}}^{t}\left(\mathbold{h}^{t-1}_{k},\mathbold{m}_{k,\text{v}\rightarrow\text{c}}^{t}\right).

Thus,
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{ET}
effectively replaces the explicit
2\text{-}\mathsf{FWL}
-based aggregation with the learnable, triangular attention of the Edge Transformer.

Neural architecture and hyperparameters

\mathsf{VC}\text{-2-}\mathsf{IGN}

E.3
, parameterizing each basis operation with a single linear layer. For
\mathsf{VC}\text{-}\mathsf{ET}
, the feed-forward network
\mathsf{FFN}
consists of a single linear layer, and the
\mathsf{TriAttn}
module of 1 attention head is implemented with learnable weight matrices. For all neural architectures, we insert a LayerNorm
(
Ba et al. 2016
)
between the GNN convolutional layers.

To ensure fair comparison, we maintain consistent hidden dimensions and layer counts across models for each dataset. We fix the hidden dimension to
96
for all architectures and all datasets. Regarding number of GNN layers, we employ 6 layers for the Max Clique, MIS, and LMI datasets; and 10 layers for the max-cut (both ER and regular graphs, and
SdpLib
), Vertex Cover, and max 2-SAT datasets.

As

shows, the downstream CO gaps produced by our neural solver perfectly mirror the inherent gaps of the exact SDP relaxations. For Max-Cut, the SDP solution of our GNN performs almost identically to exact SDP solver outputs, with a negligible 0.06% difference. The observations have validated our core claim: the neural approach successfully learns the underlying continuous SDP geometry, rather than directly learning the discrete integer solutions.

However, the neural solver’s high quality solution and fast inference time enable massive potential for CO solvers. Modern exact branch-and-bound methods for Max-Cut
(
Hrga & Povh 2021
)
require solving thousands of intermediate SDPs, which is the primary computational bottleneck. Replacing these expensive exact solvers with our cheap neural proxy to guide branching or pruning decisions, similar to the seminal work
Gasse et al. 2019
for LPs, is a highly promising future direction.
