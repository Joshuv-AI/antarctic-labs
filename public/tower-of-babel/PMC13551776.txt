Vestibular cortex-related effective connectivity signatures: characterization and differentiation of vestibular migraine patients via magnetic resonance imaging and machine learning
Authors: Lingling Dai, Guangzheng Li, Yixiang Lin, Chao Zheng, Wen Chen, Qifang Feng, Xing Xiong, Yang Yu, Hongru Zhao, Jun Ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeuroimaging studies of vestibular migraine (VM) have revealed abnormal functional connectivity in the central vestibular system. However, it remains to be determined whether effective connectivity (EC) of the vestibular cortex is specifically disrupted in VM and whether this brain measure can aid in the differential diagnosis of VM patients.MethodsIn 56 VM patients, 58 episodic migraine (EM) patients, and 63 healthy controls (HCs), the directional influences between the vestibular cortex and the whole brain were examined via resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging and Granger causality analysis, with bilateral parietal operculum cortex 2 (OP2) as the seed regions. Statistical analyses were performed to investigate the group differences and the associations of directional influences with clinical variables. Classification models based on linear support vector machine (SVM) analysis were established to assess the performance of effective connectivity in discriminating VM patients from HCs and EM patients.ResultsRelative to the EM group and HC group, the VM group presented elevated positive influence from the left OP2 to the right middle frontal gyrus (MFG), as well as increased negative influence from the right MFG and inferior parietal lobule (IPL) to the left OP2. Furthermore, the EM group exhibited increased positive influence from the bilateral medial superior frontal gyrus (SFGmed) to the left OP2 compared with the VM group and HC group. The EC-derived SVM models showed favorable and moderate performance for distinguishing VM patients from EM patients (area under the curve = 0.8264, p < 0.001) and HCs (area under the curve = 0.7764, p < 0.001).ConclusionsThe causal flow from and to the left OP2 was altered in the prefrontal and inferior parietal lobes, regions closely implicated in cognitive control and multisensory integration. Abnormal EC associated with the right MFG and IPL appears to be specific to VM, whereas altered EC linked to SFGmed suggests a resilience trait in EM. The EC of the OP2 may serve as a neuroimaging biomarker for VM identification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-026-02430-y.

Background

Vestibular migraine (VM) is a debilitating and complex neurological disorder. The characteristic symptom is episodic vertigo, which has a close association with migraine symptoms, such as headache, photophobia, or phonophobia [1]. As a subtype of migraine, VM was formally listed as a distinct diagnostic entity in 2012, when the consensus criteria were jointly proposed by the Bárány Society and the International Headache Society [2]. Epidemiological data indicate that the lifetime prevalence of VM in adults ranges from 1% to 3%, with a marked female predominance [1, 3, 4]. Although VM severely affects patients, its underlying pathophysiological mechanisms have not been well elucidated.

Over the past decades, multiple hypotheses based on current understanding of migraine have been put forward to explain the pathogenesis of VM. Specifically, previous investigations on VM have suggested the involvement of vasoactive neuropeptides related to pain transmission, neurogenic inflammation, the trigemino-vascular system, and functional alterations in brain areas related to multimodal sensory integration and processing [5]. The nociceptive and vestibular pathways interact at the trigemino-vestibulocochlear system, brainstem (e.g. vestibular nuclei, trigeminal spinal nucleus), thalamus, and cortical areas (e.g. insula, parietal operculum), and dysfunction of nodes at each of these levels may contribute to the genesis of VM [5, 6]. Supporting the central mechanism, neuroimaging studies on VM have revealed alterations of regional brain activity in multiple brain areas associated with multisensory processing of vestibular and nociceptive information, including the thalamus [7, 8], posterior insular-opercular area, and temporoparietal junction [9].

Recently, functional imaging studies have indicated that VM might be better considered as a disorder of disrupted communication between regions rather than of alterations in isolated regions [10]. For instance, functional connectivity (FC) studies utilizing independent component analysis have reported disrupted interaction within or between resting-state networks, such as the sensorimotor network, default mode network and salience network, in VM patients compared with healthy controls (HCs) or patients with episodic migraine (EM) [11–13]. Additionally, in resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) research using seed-based analysis, FC in VM patients was found to be abnormal in several brain regions, including the thalamus [7, 14], insula [15], cingulate cortex [16] and medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) [17]. With respect to the core structure of the central vestibular system, Chen et al. found altered FC between the parietal operculum cortex 2 (OP2) and anterior cingulate cortex, middle frontal gyrus (MFG), and precuneus in VM [18]. However, it should be noted that the direction of interregional or inter-network FC, which may provide valuable information on the neural basis, could not be delineated in the existing VM studies. Furthermore, as the EM group was not included in most previous studies, the comparisons were consequently only conducted between the VM patients and HCs. It is apparent that such study designs prevented us from determining whether the identified brain functional alterations are specific to VM. By contrast, several studies included three groups of subjects including episodic migraine, but their findings remain to be further validated as the sample sizes were relatively small.

Therefore, in this study, we examined the effective connectivity (EC) of the central vestibular system in VM patients and HCs by combining resting-state functional MRI and Granger causality analysis (GCA). A group of EM patients was also included to elucidate the specificity of any detected differences between VM patients and HCs. We hypothesized that abnormal EC would be revealed in cerebral regions involved in vestibular and pain processing, and EC differences may exist between the two patient groups. To bridge the gap between characterizing functional alterations in VM and identifying specific, sensitive neuroimaging biomarkers, we further established brain classification models based on EC with significant group differences using machine learning algorithms, thereby distinguishing individual VM patients from HCs and EM patients.

Methods

Subjects and clinical assessment

Fifty-six patients with VM and fifty-eight patients with EM without aura were consecutively recruited from the population referring to the vertigo and migraine outpatient center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. VM was diagnosed according to the criteria for definite vestibular migraine proposed by the Bárány Society and the International Headache Society (ICHD-3 beta version) [2, 19]. EM without aura was diagnosed on the basis of the criteria established in the ICHD-3 beta guidelines [2]. The diagnoses of VM and EM without aura were made by the same experienced neurologist (Zhao HR). A neuro-otologist (Zhang YM) provided assistance in differentiating VM from other vestibular disorders. Demographic information was acquired for all individuals, including gender, age, and education level. For VM patients, clinical data were collected, including migraine and vertigo disease duration, headache frequency (days per month), a 10-point Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for headache, Dizziness Handicap Inventory (DHI), Migraine Disability Assessment Scale (MIDAS), and Headache Impact Test-6 (HIT-6). For patients with EM without aura, clinical information including migraine disease duration, headache frequency, and headache VAS score was obtained. All enrolled VM patients and EM patients were not under regular preventive or therapeutic medications during 3 days before magnetic resonance examination. In addition, we managed to perform the MRI scanning for all patients during interictal periods. Accordingly, the patients were free of migraine or vertigo attacks from 3 days before to 1 day after the MRI acquisition.

The HC group comprised 63 healthy individuals matched for gender and educational level with VM patients. Recruited HCs had no personal or family history of diagnosed migraine, vestibular diseases, or any other types of primary headache or chronic pain. Participants satisfying any of the following criteria were excluded: left-handedness; age < 18 or > 80 years; other current active neurological or psychiatric disorders; systemic illnesses including diabetes and hypertension; substance or alcohol abuse; MRI contraindications; and excessive head motion during scanning (translational displacement > 3 mm, rotational movement > 3° in any plane, or mean framewise displacement [FD] > 0.3 mm) [20, 21]. Notably, based on the ICHD‑3 beta criteria for VM, participants with active peripheral or central vestibular disorders at enrollment were excluded. Prior vestibular disorders with full recovery, 3 consecutive months without relapse and normal vestibular function tests were not exclusion grounds. The current study was approved by the ethics committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University and carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All subjects provided written informed consent prior to their participation.

MRI Acquisition

MRI data were acquired on a 3.0 Tesla scanning system (MAGNETOM Skyra, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 16-channel head and neck joint coil. The resting-state functional images were obtained using an echo-planar imaging sequence, each comprising 240 brain volumes of 33 interleaved axial slices (repetition time = 2000 ms, echo time = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, field of view = 256 × 256 mm2, matrix size = 64 × 64, slice thickness = 4 mm, no intersection gap). The sections were positioned approximately parallel to the anterior commissure-posterior commissure line. High-resolution T1-weighted anatomic images were acquired using a fast spoiled gradient recalled echo sequence (sagittal orientation, repetition time = 2300 ms, echo time = 2.98 ms, matrix size = 256 × 256, field of view = 256 × 256 mm2, slice thickness = 1 mm). During functional image acquisition, participants were instructed to lie still with their eyes closed, while remaining awake and avoiding any specific cognitive activity. The anatomic images were evaluated by two experienced radiologists to exclude clinically silent lesions.

Data preprocessing

For functional images, data preprocessing was conducted using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12, http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/) and a toolbox for Data Processing and Analysis of Brain Imaging (DPABI, http://rfmri.org/dpabi). First, the original DICOM data were converted to NIfTI format. The first 10 volumes of functional data were discarded to minimize the effects of signal instability caused by initial magnetization. The remaining images underwent slice-time correcting, realignment, and co-registering with the individual T1-weighted images. The co-registered anatomic images were then segmented into gray matter, white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and spatially normalized into standard Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space with a final size of 3 × 3 × 3 mm3. Tissue segmentation was implemented via the New Segment module in SPM12, followed by DARTEL for nonlinear spatial normalization. The resulting normalization matrix was then applied to the corresponding functional images. After that, detrending was performed to remove slow signal drifts, and temporal band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz) was applied to reduce low-frequency drift and physiological high frequency noise. The obtained images were then spatially smoothed using an isotropic Gaussian kernel with full-width at half maximum = 6 mm. To reduce confounding effects, several nuisance variables, including the 24 head motion parameters, global signal, WM signal, and CSF signal, were regressed out from the time series.

Voxel-wise GCA

With the aim of examining the EC between the vestibular cortex and the rest of the brain, we selected the left and right OP2 as the regions of interest. The coordinates for bilateral OP2 (left: -40/-22/16; right: 40/-22/16) were derived from a previously published study on meta-analytical definition and FC of the human vestibular cortex [22]. Two spherical seed regions with a radius of 6 mm were created at the predefined coordinates (Supplementary Fig. 1). Then voxel-wise bivariate first-order coefficient-based GCA was conducted within the gray matter mask using Resting-State fMRI Data Analysis Toolkit software (REST, http://www.restfmri.net). Specifically, the lag length was set to 1, corresponding to one repetition time. This setting conforms to the physiological hemodynamic delay of resting-state functional MRI signals, maximizes temporal resolution for estimating directed neural interactions, and avoids overfitting of vector autoregressive model caused by excessive parameters given the limited functional MRI time points. GCA evaluates the causal influence from the seed region to every other voxel in the brain (X to Y effect), as well as the reverse causal influence from each brain voxel to the seed region (Y to X effect). A positive X-to-Y coefficient indicates that activity in region X exerts a causal influence on the activity of region Y in the same direction (i.e. positive or excitatory influence). By contrast, a negative X-to-Y coefficient indicates that activity of region X predicts opposite-directional changes in the subsequent region Y activity (i.e. negative or inhibitory influence) [23]. Fisher’s z-transformation was applied to the resulting Granger causality coefficients to convert them into z-score maps for subsequent statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 22.0 (Chicago, IL, USA) was utilized to examine group differences in the demographic and clinical variables, with the significance level set at p < 0.05. Specifically, the chi-squared test was performed to analyze gender distribution across the three groups. The Kruskal‑Wallis H test was used for nonparametric comparisons of age and education level, as these data were not normally distributed. For headache frequency, an independent t-test was conducted to analyze differences between the VM group and EM group. Since migraine disease duration and headache VAS scores were non-normally distributed, the Mann‑Whitney U test was applied for nonparametric comparisons between the two patient groups.

The Granger causality maps of the three groups were compared in a voxel-wise way using ANOVA in SPM12, with age, gender, education level, and mean FD as covariates. Multiple comparison corrections were conducted on the basis of Gaussian random field theory (voxel-level p < 0.001, cluster level p < 0.05, two-tailed) within the gray matter mask using the DPABI package. For each detected region (four regions in total) showing significant group differences, the mean Granger causality coefficient was extracted for each participant. Post hoc t-tests were conducted in SPSS 22 to identify pairwise differences in the mean Granger causality coefficients across the three groups, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons (p < 0.05/12). In addition, partial correlation analyses, controlling for age, gender, education level, and mean FD, were separately performed in all patients and in the VM patients to explore the association between the mean Granger causality coefficient of each region and clinical variables. Correlation analyses were performed using SPSS 22, with a significant threshold of p < 0.05 (not corrected).

Machine learning analyses

Although numerous machine learning algorithms have been developed for classification in recent years, support vector machine (SVM) analysis is widely adopted as a benchmark in neuroimaging research owing to its robust performance in small sample settings [24] and interpretable feature weights [25]. Therefore, in this preliminary exploratory study, we applied SVM to examine whether EC measures could distinguish VM patients from EM patients and HCs. The identified EC measures exhibiting significant group differences in voxel-wise GCA were fed to a linear SVM, which was implemented via the scikit-learn package in Python. Two binary classification models were constructed instead of a single ternary model, as our primary objective was to individually differentiate the VM group from each of the other two groups. Hyperparameter optimization for the linear SVM was performed using grid search to identify the optimal penalty parameter C. All classification models were based on a leave-one-out cross-validation strategy, with performance quantified by accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and decision curve analysis (DCA) curves were also plotted to illustrate the diagnostic performance of the constructed models.

To assess the stability and reliability of the established brain classification models, two types of validation analyses were further conducted. One was permutation tests with 1000 iterations performed for the linear SVM classification to assess the statistical significance of accuracy and area under the ROC curve (AUC). The second was stratified 5-fold cross-validation analysis applied for each SVM classification model, based on which accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated using the default threshold and subsequently averaged across the five iterations, while predicted probabilities from all test folds were pooled to generate a single ROC curve and DCA curve.

Validation by propensity score matching (PSM)

Considering the evident age imbalance across the three groups, additional PSM was performed to balance demographic and clinical variables. Group comparisons of demographics, clinical data, bilateral OP2 Granger causality, and imaging-clinical correlations were further validated using the PSM-matched dataset. Specifically, pairwise 1:1 nearest neighbor matching was conducted using SPSS 22.0. Propensity scores were calculated via binary logistic regression for VM vs. EM and VM vs. HC, with a caliper of 0.05 times the standard deviation of the propensity score. Age, gender and education level were set as matching covariates, and a standardized mean differences < 0.1 was defined as adequate balance.

Notably, PSM was used primarily to evaluate age confounding on group differences, so no new SVM models were reconstructed using the matched subsample. On one hand, age had been corrected via ANCOVA during feature selection and was not included as an input feature for SVM. On the other hand, PSM verified that intergroup differences in EC were not attributed to age imbalance. Furthermore, around 21% of participants were excluded after matching, and such sample attrition could reduce statistical power and impair model generalizability.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the VM, EM, and HC groups were presented in Table 1. There were no significant differences in gender distribution (p = 0.684) and education level (p = 0.488) among the three groups. The age of the EM group was significantly lower than that of the VM group and HC group (p = 0.009). No significant differences were detected between the VM group and EM group in migraine disease duration (p = 0.878), headache frequency (p = 0.178), and headache VAS (p = 0.201). The VM group had a mean vertigo disease duration of 7.74 ± 9.17 years. The average score of the DHI, MIDAS, HIT-6 was 52.75 ± 16.57, 11.20 ± 11.44, and 55.75 ± 9.50, respectively.

Causal influence from whole brain to OP2

There were significant differences in the causal flow to the left OP2 from the right MFG, bilateral medial superior frontal gyrus (SFGmed), and the right inferior parietal lobule (IPL) (Table 2; Fig. 1B). Compared with the EM group and HC group respectively, the VM group showed increased negative influence from the right MFG to the left OP2 (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table 1). In each group, the right MFG exerted a negative influence to the left OP2. No significant difference in causal flow from the right MFG to left OP2 was found between the EM group and HC group. The EM group exhibited increased positive influence from the bilateral SFGmed to the left OP2 when compared with the VM group and HC group, separately (Fig. 2C). Positive influence was found from the bilateral SFGmed to the left OP2 in the EM group, while negative influence was found in each of the other two groups. As for causal flow from the right IPL to the left OP2, the result was similar to that found for causal flow from the right MFG to the left OP2 (Fig. 2D). No significant differences were detected in the causal influence from the rest of the brain to the right OP2 among the three groups.

Correlation analysis

After controlling for age, gender, education, and motion parameters as covariates, partial correlation analyses in this study revealed no significant associations between Granger causality coefficients in brain areas showing significant group differences and any of the collected clinical variables, either in the VM group or in the entire patient cohort.

Findings in the PSM-matched cohort

After matching, age, gender and education level were comparable across groups. The matched sample sizes were VM = 49, EM = 39, HC = 52. For this matched dataset, there were no significant differences between the two patient groups in migraine disease duration, headache frequency and headache VAS scores (Supplementary Table 2). The group comparisons of EC of OP2 revealed similar results to those detected in our original analyses. Of note, significant differences existed in the causal flow from the left OP2 to the right IPL, but not in the opposite direction (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 3). However, the difference in the causal flow from the right IPL to the left OP2 remained significant under a lenient voxel-level threshold (p = 0.005) with GRF correction (cluster size > 23 voxels, corresponding to a two-tailed cluster-level p < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 3). Pairwise comparisons after PSM showed consistent results across all EC metrics relative to the original analyses. The findings regarding causal flow from left OP2 to right IPL were comparable to those from the left OP2 to the right MFG (Supplementary Table 4, Supplementary Fig. 4). Furthermore, a positive correlation was found between DHI scores and the directional influence from the left OP2 to the right IPL (not corrected).

Classifier construction

Two classification models were established in the current study: Model 1 for classification of VM versus EM, and Model 2 for classification of VM versus HC. Model 1 showed good overall performance with an AUC of 0.8264 (Fig. 3A), accuracy of 0.7632, specificity of 0.7931, and sensitivity of 0.7321, indicating favorable efficacy in distinguishing VM patients from EM patients. In Model 1, the causal flows from the left OP2 to the right MFG, from the right MFG to the left OP2, from the bilateral SFGmed to the left OP2, and from the right IPL to the left OP2 had feature weights of 0.6102, −0.7202, −0.7495, and −0.8025, respectively (Fig. 3B). DCA curves showed that Model 1 achieved higher net benefit than the “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies within a wide range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 3C). Model 2 demonstrated moderate performance in distinguishing VM patients from HCs, with an AUC of 0.7764 (Fig. 3D), accuracy of 0.6975, specificity of 0.6825, and sensitivity of 0.7143. In Model 2, the causal flows from the left OP2 to the right MFG, from the right MFG to the left OP2, from the bilateral SFGmed to the left OP2, and from the right IPL to the left OP2 had feature weights of 1.4409, −0.2887, −0.2446, and −1.1197, respectively (Fig. 3E). As shown in the DCA curve, Model 2 also yielded higher net benefit relative to the “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies across a wide range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 3F).

Model validation

The permutation tests revealed that accuracy and AUC of both Model 1 and Model 2 were statistically significant (both p < 0.001). Furthermore, the results acquired with 5-fold cross-validation analysis remained highly consistent with those obtained with a leave-one-out cross-validation strategy. Specifically, Model 1 achieved an AUC of 0.8408, accuracy of 0.7538, specificity of 0.7759, and sensitivity of 0.7321. In Model 1, the causal flows from the left OP2 to the right MFG, from the right MFG to the left OP2, from the bilateral SFGmed to the left OP2, from the right IPL to the left OP2 had feature weights of 0.5803, −0.7731, −0.8592, and −0.7508, respectively. Model 2 achieved an AUC of 0.7734, accuracy of 0.6884, specificity of 0.6825, and sensitivity of 0.6964. In Model 2, the causal flows from the left OP2 to the right MFG, from the right MFG to the left OP2, from the bilateral SFGmed to the left OP2, from the right IPL to the left OP2 had feature weights of 1.7530, −0.0803, −0.2910, and −1.1638, respectively.

Discussion

In the present study with a relatively large sample size, we investigated the directed FC between the bilateral OP2, the processing center of human vestibular cortex, and the rest of the brain in VM patients and EM patients by combining resting-state functional MRI and GCA. Disrupted EC of the left OP2 was revealed in multiple regions involved in processing of pain and vestibular information, including the right MFG, IPL, and bilateral SFGmed in the VM group compared with the EM group or the HC group. Machine learning results indicated that directed brain connectivity of the left OP2 had good and moderate discriminative ability in distinguishing VM patients from EM patients and HCs.

Compared with the EM group and the HC group, separately, the VM group showed increased negative influence on the left OP2 exerted by the right MFG, and increased positive influence from the left OP2 to right MFG in the present study. Partially consistent with our findings, previous studies reported increased regional activity during the resting state [26, 27] and decreased gray matter volume [28–30] in the MFG in VM patients relative to EM patients and HCs. In addition, enhanced FC of the MFG with several regions has been revealed in the VM versus HC comparison in prior literature [14, 18, 31]. In particular, Chen et al. observed increased FC between the right OP2 and right MFG [18]. Our study extended their connectivity findings by further delineating the directed interaction between these two brain regions. As a key node of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, the MFG is involved in cognitive and affective processing of sensory information including pain. Functional and structural brain alterations in this region have been considered to reflect disrupted cognitive modulation of pain in migraine patients. The MFG is also thought to be an important part of the vestibular circuitry, given that it has direct fibers to the vestibular nuclei [32, 33]. Taken together, alterations in the bidirectional FC between the OP2 and MFG may indicate increased bottom-up inputs from sensory to cognitive processing areas for better capture of external sensory stimuli, and greater cognitive inhibition of nociceptive and vestibular perception, which may represent compensatory mechanisms and adaptive responses in VM patients. This speculation was in line with previous findings of greater activation in the frontal lobe among patients with the worst performance during visuospatial processing [34]. Notably, in the current study, no significant EC differences with regard to these two regions were found between the EM group and the HC group, suggesting that the observed abnormalities were unique to VM patients.

We also found increased negative influence from the right IPL to the left OP2 in the VM group compared with the other two groups. The IPL is important for visual-spatial information integration [26]. Together with the adjacent temporal perisylvian areas, the IPL belongs to the multisensory vestibular cortical network [35, 36]. It is also a component of the default mode network, which is implicated in multiple cognitive processes including memory, attention, and perception and processing of painful stimuli [37]. During vestibular stimulation, the IPL in healthy individuals contributes to maintaining attention toward ongoing task goals, as well as detecting and responding to salient or novel stimuli in the environment [38]. Therefore, our finding of altered causal influence from the IPL to OP2 may suggest aberrant sensory integration within the vestibular network, and increased demand for cognitive modulation of vestibular information in VM patients. Although altered brain function and structure in the IPL have been documented in both VM and EM patients [26, 28–30, 39, 40], the aberrant EC between these two regions identified here appeared to be unique to VM: significant differences were detected when comparing the VM group with HCs or the EM group, whereas no differences existed between the EM and HC groups.

In contrast to multiple previous studies showing significant brain differences with regard to SFGmed between the VM group and the HC group [7, 17, 41], our data revealed no such differences between VM and HC. Instead, the EM group showed enhanced positive influence from the bilateral SFGmed to the left OP2 compared with both VM and HC groups. The SFGmed pertains to the dorsal part of the mPFC, which is usually considered to play critical roles in regulation of emotional responses as well as pain perception [42, 43]. It is also involved in attention responses, evaluation of stimuli and actions [41, 42]. Accumulating evidence supports that the dorsal part of mPFC is more related to aspects of cognition that require information integration, while the ventral mPFC is important for reward and affection processing [44]. In humans, the prefrontal lobe, including the superior frontal gyrus, has been demonstrated to participate in the integration of somatosensory and vestibular information [45]. Collectively, we speculate that our finding of increased positive causal flow from SFGmed to OP2 may reflect improved appraisal and processing of vestibular information, which could be explained as a resilience mechanism in EM patients that protects them from developing vestibular dysfunction.

Interestingly, all EC alterations were observed only in the left OP2, but not in the right OP2. This finding is in accordance with a recent meta-analysis of structural brain imaging data showing gray matter reduction exclusively in the left posterior insula and operculum regions [46]. Previous studies on vestibular processing have reported that the right hemisphere generally dominates vestibular function in right-handed individuals [36]. Collectively, our findings indicate functional lateralization of OP2: this region presents physiological right-sided dominance, while the left OP2 is more susceptible in individuals with VM. The restriction of EC alterations in OP2 to the left side is unlikely to be fully accounted for by headache laterality, given that several patients in our cohort reported bilateral headaches. Unfortunately, headache laterality data were not available for all patients in the current study, as has been noted in some prior investigations, underscoring the need for future studies to adopt more standardized and comprehensive assessments of laterality.

The partial correlation analyses revealed no significant association between any of the collected clinical variables and GC coefficients of regions showing altered EC with the left OP2. The negative findings may be due to the fact that functional alterations do not always follow a dose-response relationship across brain regions. Some changes in EC may serve as trait markers rather than state markers. In addition, abnormal causal brain activity may represent early central nervous system alterations, whereas clinical parameters progress slowly alongside disease duration, resulting in asynchronous changes between imaging and clinical measurements. On the other hand, since OP2 is predominantly implicated in vestibular processing, its imaging metrics are expected to correlate with vestibular-function-based rating scales instead of headache-related clinical indices. Interestingly, supplementary analyses after PSM revealed a positive correlation of DHI scores with the directional influence from the left OP2 to the right IPL, which lends partial support to our aforementioned speculations.

With respect to the classifier construction, our findings were robust. Specifically, the SVM models on the basis of OP2-related EC patterns showed good and moderate discriminatory power for distinguishing VM patients from EM patients and HCs, respectively. For linear SVM, positive feature weights denote disease-promoting effects, whereas negative weights reflect protective effects against VM. The absolute magnitude of each weight quantifies its predictive importance for VM classification. Accordingly, Model 1 results suggest that stronger positive EC from the left OP2 to the right MFG is more indicative of VM than EM. By contrast, stronger negative EC from the bilateral SFGmed to the left OP2 is more typical of VM versus EM, with this pathway exerting stronger protective effects than the pathway from the right MFG to the left OP2. Therefore, our findings indicate that GCA of the OP2 using resting-state functional MRI data not only provides valuable insights into the neural mechanisms underlying VM and migraine, but also could yield promising objective brain biomarkers for disease classification. Overall, as auxiliary objective imaging biomarkers, these models help improve the accuracy and objectivity of clinical diagnosis, especially for patients with concurrent vestibular manifestations and headache who cannot adequately recall or detail their symptoms.

Nevertheless, our study still has several limitations. First, the mean age of the EM group was significantly lower than that of the other two groups. However, age exerted limited influence on the overall results, as it was adjusted for as a covariate in primary analyses and further validated using the PSM-matched subsample. Even so, future studies with better age matching across groups are still warranted. Second, while our study employed a relatively large sample size (achieving a statistical power of 0.85 at a medium effect size), studies with expanded sample size are encouraged to validate our findings, and independent multicenter data are necessary to verify the generalizability of the classification model. Third, our classification model was confined to interrogating OP2-related EC that differed significantly across groups; thus, we may have overlooked other important regions whose metrics could have further improved our classification accuracy. Fourth, neuropsychological assessment was not sufficiently comprehensive to fully evaluate the disorder-related impacts across all patients. In addition, some individuals had missing clinical information, including headache laterality, as well as MIDAS, HIT-6 scores (EM group). Fifth, the cross-sectional nature of the study design precluded causal inferences regarding the association between abnormal EC and VM as well as migraine attacks.

Conclusions

In summary, the present study demonstrates that the OP2 contributes significantly to the pathophysiology of VM. In VM patients, causal flow from and to the left OP2 is altered in the prefrontal and inferior parietal lobes, regions closely associated with cognitive control and multisensory integration. Abnormal EC of the left OP2 with the MFG and IPL seems to be VM-specific, while aberrant EC between the left OP2 and SFGmed may reflect a resilience character in EM patients. The GCA-derived EC biomarkers hold the potential to differentiate individual VM patients from both HCs and EM patients.
