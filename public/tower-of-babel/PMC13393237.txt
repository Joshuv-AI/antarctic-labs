Factors influencing vaccine uptake during pregnancy: A systematic literature review
Authors: Dasha Shamarina, Lina María Cortes Rueda, David Alderson, Anne T Collins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA systematic literature review was conducted to build a comprehensive evidence base of qualitative/quantitative studies to identify barriers and enablers influencing maternal immunization (MI). Searches of seven databases on 20/10/2023 and 26/10/2023 yielded 87,367 records. Two reviewers performed full-text screening of 484 records. English-language articles from countries that had national recommendations for pertussis-containing maternal vaccines and reported on maternal vaccine acceptance, trust, and hesitancy were eligible. Overall, 184 studies were included. Barriers involving individual and group influences like lack of knowledge and reduced awareness of MI, pre-existing anti-vaccination beliefs and attitudes, and perceived concerns about vaccine safety and efficacy were the most prominent. Proactive communication and recommendations from healthcare professionals and public health authorities were major enablers, alongside trusting the information provided by healthcare professionals. Our results show that vaccine hesitancy is a complex phenomenon. Addressing both practical and perceptual barriers to MI might increase vaccination coverage among pregnant individuals.KEYWORDS: Vaccination hesitancy, vaccination refusal, prenatal education, maternal health, vaccination, pregnancy

Introduction

The majority of recommended childhood vaccines are administered earliest at 6–8 weeks of age,1 leaving infants vulnerable to infections during the first months of life. During this time, infants are only (partially) protected by the maternal antibodies that were conveyed through transplacental transfer.2 Immunization during pregnancy helps protect both mother and infant from serious infections.3,4

Tetanus-diphtheria-acellular pertussis, inactivated influenza, COVID-19, and respiratory syncytial virus vaccines are routinely recommended to pregnant individuals.5,6 Additional vaccines can also be used in specific circumstances; of these, hepatitis B, meningococcal, and inactivated poliomyelitis vaccines are commonly offered to pregnant individuals at risk of infection.3

Even though maternal immunization (MI) has had a profound impact on reducing infectious disease morbidity and mortality in infants,7 vaccine hesitancy remains a substantial barrier to achieving optimal immunization coverage.8,9 Concerns regarding vaccines’ potential short and long-term adverse effects (AEs) on their children complicate the decision-making process for pregnant individuals.

Understanding the underlying causes of hesitancy is crucial to facilitate the development of efficient strategies to improve vaccine uptake in pregnant individuals across the globe and to identify gaps where further studies are warranted. The objective of this systematic literature review (SLR) was to build a comprehensive evidence base of qualitative and quantitative studies to identify the enablers and barriers influencing MI, from the perspective of pregnant individuals.

Methods

This SLR adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.10 A protocol was established before conducting the literature search.

Sources

Searches were conducted in PubMed (NCBI), MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase and Scopus (Elsevier), Web of Science (Clarivate), Health & Medical Collection (ProQuest), and CINAHL (EBSCO) on 20/10/2023 and 26/10/2023 using a combination of free-text and controlled vocabulary terms specific to each database to identify all relevant publications about vaccine acceptance, trust, and hesitancy (Supplementary Methods).

Only English-language articles that focused on countries with national recommendations for pertussis-containing vaccines for pregnant individuals (i.e., the United States [US], the United Kingdom [UK], Australia, New Zealand, Vietnam, India, South Korea, European and South American countries) and reported on maternal vaccines were eligible. Only primary studies (qualitative/quantitative/cross-sectional/cohort studies) that reported on pregnant individuals’ knowledge on vaccine safety, efficacy, or their attitudes toward MI, and were published since inception of MI were considered. Eligibility criteria are detailed in Supplementary Methods.

Study selection

Citations were uploaded onto the systematic review web app Rayyan.ai11 for deduplication before exporting them into Microsoft Excel for title and abstract screening by two reviewers, independently, using the prespecified study eligibility criteria. Subsequently, two independent reviewers assessed the full text of eligible articles and documented the reasons for exclusion. Data extraction was performed by two reviewers and verified by two other independent reviewers. Any discrepancies between reviewers in eligibility assessment and data extraction were resolved through consensus.

A predefined Excel data grid was used to extract the study characteristics (study type, country, vaccines, sampling method), study population demographics (age, sample size, gestational age), and outcomes (enablers and barriers to MI). For qualitative studies, participants’ quotes were extracted verbatim.

The analysis of barriers was mainly based on the Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization Working Group (SAGE-WG) Vaccine Hesitancy Determinants Matrix.12,13 This model categorizes determinants of vaccine hesitancy into “Contextual influences,” “Individual and group influences,” and “Vaccine/vaccination-specific issues.” Variables (e.g., gravida, parity, number of children, trimester) that could not be characterized according to any SAGE-WG themes were categorized as “Others.”

As there is no globally recognized framework to categorize enablers for MI, we categorized enabling factors into seven themes: “External sources of information”; “Socio-economic parameters”; “Internal sources of information”; “Pro-vaccination belief and attitudes”; “Awareness (benefit of vaccine/risk of disease)”; “Trust in health system and healthcare professional (HCP)/government recommendations”; “Others” (Supplementary Methods).

A simple scoring methodology was followed to quantify barriers and enablers. Each factor identified from a study was assigned a unit score; cumulative scores of individual factors determined the overall score of the determinant in each study. High determinant scores were considered as the most influential.

Quality assessment

Eligible articles were critically appraised by two independent reviewers using the Joanna Briggs Institute quality assessment tool,14 which assesses the methodological quality of each study (based on study design) and determines the extent to which a study addresses the possibility of bias in its design, conduct, and analysis. Reviewers judged each domain as Yes/No/Unclear/Not applicable based on the acceptability of the study characteristics, representation of adequate search strategies, and data synthesis mode. A quality score was assigned based on individual responses (Yes = 2/No = −2/Unclear = −1/Not applicable = 0). A consensus score was derived from individual quality scores from two reviewers.

Results

The search identified 87,367 records. After removing duplicates, 18,134 records were imported into Microsoft Excel and screened based on title and abstract. Full-text screening was performed for 484 records. In total, 184 studies15–198 were included in this SLR (Figure 1).

Study characteristics

The 184 studies were published between 1995 and 2023. Twelve cohort studies, 142 cross-sectional studies and 30 qualitative studies (focus group discussions [n = 8] or interviews [n = 22]), were included in this SLR. The sample size ranged between 10– 28,609 individuals (Supplementary Table 1). Studies investigating determinants influencing MI were distributed across Europe (n = 77), North America (n = 59), Oceania (n = 19), Asia (n = 15), South America (n = 9), and transcontinental (n = 5) (Figure 2).

Determinants influencing maternal immunization

We identified 171 studies reporting on barriers,15–37,39–43,45–51,53,54,56–65,67–84,86–88,90–96,98–108,110–141,143–156,158–175,177–184,186–198 and 176 studies reporting on enablers15–19,21–42,44–49,52–75,77–143,145,147–187,189–198 to MI (Figure 4). The most notable barriers reported across included studies involved individual and group influences, with lack of knowledge and awareness of MI, pre-existing anti-vaccination beliefs and attitudes, and perceived concerns about vaccine safety and efficacy being the most prominent (Figures 4 and 5). Europe: Overall, the knowledge or awareness of MI, the disease risk, and vaccine benefit accounted for 74% of all the reported factors affecting vaccine uptake among the 77 European studies. A general lack of knowledge or awareness regarding the existence of immunization programs for pregnant individuals was reported in several studies.24,28,49,113,135,146 In a cross-sectional study from Italy, almost 40% of participants were unaware that influenza vaccines were recommended during pregnancy.113 A prospective observational study from the Czech Republic also reported that 79.2% of participants were unaware of the possibility to get vaccinated against influenza during pregnancy.111 Although in Spain, a study showed that 91.7% of pregnant individuals were aware of maternal influenza vaccines,155 concerns about the hidden economic interests of various stakeholders fueled influenza and pertussis vaccine hesitancy.18

Trust in HCPs and the strength of their recommendation were influential determinants for MI, as reported in 60% of the European studies. Recommendations by public health authorities or HCPs increased vaccine uptake,26,28,30,44,115,120,136,147,181,184,197 as pregnant individuals trusted such recommendations, overall.26,184 Conversely, lack of counseling from HCPs was the main reason for influenza vaccine refusal in two European studies.136,137

In the UK, some racial and ethnic minorities exhibited lower vaccine uptake compared to White ethnic groups.128,167 Level of education was an important determinant of MI. In Italy, a low-level education (middle/high-school) was associated with high-risk perception, reduced knowledge, and lower willingness to be vaccinated.121,146 In Romania, higher education levels were associated with vaccine acceptance.42 However, other studies from the Czech Republic and the Netherlands reported that higher education levels were not always correlated with a greater intention to vaccinate.111,184 Employment status was an enabler for vaccine uptake among pregnant individuals in Italy30,47,120 and Germany.160

North America: In 68% of the 59 North American studies, vaccine uptake was influenced by race, age, and level of education, collectively grouped under socio-economic parameters. Race or ethnicity were determinants for vaccine uptake in 36% of US-based studies.21–23,25,32,33,39,48,60,71,75,103,108,124,126,142,148,150,152,170,174 Having medical insurance was another socio-economic parameter facilitating the uptake of maternal influenza vaccine in the US.39,98,124 Medically uninsured individuals were less likely to receive a provider recommendation, resulting in lower uptake of maternal influenza vaccine.39 US pregnant individuals of younger age (e.g., 18–24 y) were generally more hesitant than older individuals toward both maternal influenza32 and COVID-19 vaccines.27,140,143,152,163,173 Yet, older pregnant individuals were also hesitant toward receiving maternal influenza vaccine22,124 and the postpartum human papillomavirus vaccine, even if the latter was offered free of charge.23 In one US-based study, pregnant individuals declined influenza immunization due to false beliefs that the “vaccine was not protective” and “they were not at risk for influenza.”63

In 54% of the included studies, individuals who trusted HCPs and the strength of their recommendation were more likely to get vaccinated during pregnancy. Pregnant individuals reported increased vaccine uptake if recommended by public health authorities or HCPs.60,62,86,88 Trust in national immunization guidelines was reported as an enabler for MI.87,180

Oceania: Among 19 studies from Oceania, 89% identified trust in HCPs and the strength of their recommendation as important factors influencing MI decisions. Although HCP recommendations favored the uptake of maternal vaccines, there were some reports where HCPs had actively dissuaded pregnant individuals from vaccination,156,187,188 both for newer vaccines (e.g., COVID-19)187 and older vaccines, such as influenza vaccines.156,188 In a survey of pregnant individuals’ perceptions and intentions toward COVID-19 vaccination, 56% of respondents had not received either dose of a COVID-19 vaccine; of these, 28% claimed that vaccination was never discussed and 15% reported being dissuaded from vaccination by an HCP.187 Similarly, a cross-sectional study (investigating the reasons for pandemic influenza vaccine refusal) reported that, of 443 unvaccinated pregnant individuals from Western Australia, 63.9% had not been offered a vaccine despite multiple visits to their general practitioner, and 19.6% had been advised by their general practitioner against vaccination in pregnancy, mainly due to different antenatal care practices prevalent in the region.188 A longitudinal study on maternal COVID-19 vaccination reported that vaccine hesitancy among Australian pregnant individuals increased throughout the six-month study period; individuals who sought treatment for mental health during pregnancy and the postnatal period were more likely to be dissatisfied with their life and were also hesitant to get vaccinated.50 Yet, another Australian prospective study found a significantly higher probability for maternal pertussis (adjusted prevalence ratio: 1.16) and influenza (adjusted prevalence ratio: 1.32) vaccine uptake in pregnant individuals with a history of major depressive disorder.134

MI was influenced by unique factors like geographic region or ethnicity.50,139 Lacking knowledge of vaccine recommendations, HCPs not offering the vaccine universally, and pregnant individuals refusing vaccination, despite it being offered, were some of the underlying barriers for MI among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people.139

Asia: In 87% of the 15 Asian studies, the perceived risk related to the disease or vaccination was found to influence decisions regarding MI (irrespective of the vaccine type). Vaccine safety and efficacy, fear of self-harm or harm to the fetus, risk of AEs during pregnancy (e.g., miscarriage, increased stress levels), and vaccine unavailability were the major barriers to MI.81,99,106,138,164 In an observational, cross-sectional, knowledge, attitude, and practice study from India, 52.5% of 652 pregnant individuals believed that the receipt of COVID-19 vaccine during pregnancy “will cause COVID-19 and it will transmit to the baby.”164

Also, a lack of knowledge regarding vaccines was associated with unwillingness to get vaccinated during pregnancy.80,82 According to an Indian study, registration by the HCP before 29 weeks of gestation, being ≤5 km from the vaccination facility, having ≥2 contacts with the HCP, and being educated were independently associated with complete tetanus immunization.82

Included studies also indicate that pregnancy itself remained an important barrier to MI, despite mothers’ increased awareness of MI.100,138 An Indian study conducted on 300 participants (including pregnant individuals and healthcare workers) reported that 56% of them considered vaccination as the required treatment for COVID-19 and 84% regarded COVID-19 vaccines as safe during pregnancy; however, only 48% were vaccinated.138

South America: In the nine Latin American studies, the two key factors influencing vaccine uptake during pregnancy were knowledge or awareness about MI,35,65,77,78,83,131,153 and perceptions of disease risk and vaccine benefits.35,78,153 The knowledge level on maternal influenza vaccine varied by country.35,65,77–79,83,131,153 For example, in Peru, poor knowledge on MI led to low vaccination coverage.35,83,153

A survey conducted in Brazil showed that although pregnant individuals were open toward MI in general, safety concerns arose after a rumor that vaccines caused microcephaly emerged during the Zika outbreak, leading to vaccine refusal.162 Vaccine-hesitant pregnant individuals further reported suspicions over government initiatives.162

The SAGE-WG’s 3Cs model was used in two multi-national studies and one single-center study, all conducted across South American countries.77–79 The 3Cs model consists of three vaccine hesitancy indicators (confidence, complacency, convenience), which focus on multiple sub-components revolving around individual-based, vaccine-related beliefs and attitudes, concerns, and experiences.77–79 Confidence indicators like safety concerns for the maternal influenza vaccines due to the lack of safety data in pregnancy were among the most common barriers to MI.79 Complacency emanated from misbeliefs and misinformation, which led to false safety and efficacy allegations.

Transcontinental: A multi-country study which recruited 1,290 pregnant individuals through two social media platforms (WhatToExpect, Twitter) reported that safety concerns associated with accelerated development of COVID-19 vaccines and lack of safety data in pregnancy precluded mothers from receiving such maternal vaccines, as they preferred to wait until after giving birth to be vaccinated. Individuals’ beliefs that they were young and healthy, or already had COVID-19, and misinformation gave rise to false safety and efficacy allegations, or even conspiracy theories.76 A second cross-sectional study that evaluated the acceptability of a hypothetical group B Streptococcus (GBS) vaccine among 502 pregnant individuals in the US and Ireland found higher awareness and understanding of GBS in the US (52%) than in Ireland (30%).71 In both countries, vaccine acceptability was driven by the mother’s desire to protect the infant from severe infections.71 Three other transcontinental studies found that country of residence, comorbidities, history of COVID-19, parity, and concerns regarding risk of disease or vaccine were key determinants for vaccine uptake.19,91,168

Targeted interventions

Twenty studies reported specific interventions aiming to increase awareness and uptake of maternal vaccines. The types of interventions were categorized as text messages (n = 3),32,98,101 educational classes (n = 13),41,47,69,89,90,119,133,142,155,165,178,179,185 interactive content (n = 2),85,159 and others (n = 2).38,157 Targeted interventions, such as tailored text messages,32,98,101 childbirth education classes with an emphasis on MI,47,179 lectures165,178,185 and digital interventions85,90,159 (e.g., Qualtrics intervention survey)85 were found to enhance vaccine uptake among pregnant individuals. Digital platforms were preferred by pregnant individuals over leaflets, pamphlets, and other paper-based modes of communication as reported in three studies.85,90,159

Impact of the pandemics on maternal immunization

Eight studies reported the impact of two pandemics (2009 H1N1 influenza pandemic158,180 and 2019 COVID-19 pandemic)16,31,66,89,125,145 on MI. Two studies identified the H1N1 influenza pandemic as an enabler for pandemic influenza vaccination.158,180 Two studies reported the COVID-19 pandemic as a barrier that promoted general vaccine hesitancy;66,89 the remaining four studies identified the pandemic as an enabler both for routine maternal vaccines16,145 and COVID-19 vaccine.31,125

Discussion

This SLR provides a detailed overview of the different barriers and enablers impacting maternal vaccine uptake across the globe. By focusing on these key factors, this manuscript aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of the determinants of MI and to propose recommendations for improving vaccine uptake among pregnant individuals.

Barriers involving individual and group influences were identified as most prominent. Of these, lack of knowledge and reduced awareness about MI, pre-existing anti-vaccination beliefs and attitudes, and perceived concerns about vaccine safety and efficacy were most notable. These findings are in line with an SLR conducted in 2019 and 2021 on 321 studies from various countries aiming to understand barriers and facilitators to MI using the SAGE-WG Vaccine Hesitancy Determinants Matrix.199 Although the searches were partly conducted in different databases, both SLRs found that lacking HCP recommendations, safety concerns, reduced access to prenatal care, and social disparities were barriers to MI, while HCPs’ recommendations and the desire to protect one’s infant were major enablers.199 Similar results were obtained in another SLR conducted in 2022 on 28 qualitative studies from high-income countries (HICs) published in peer-reviewed journals and gray literature, which aimed to identify barriers and facilitators to MI against influenza, pertussis, and COVID-19.9 The referenced SLR found that concerns about vaccine safety and efficacy, limited awareness about vaccine benefits and necessity, fear of AEs, and low disease severity perception were barriers to MI; HCP recommendations, vaccination accessibility, clear communication on vaccine benefits and safety, and family and friends’ encouragements were facilitators to MI.9

In this SLR we found that lack of knowledge about MI is a common barrier in pregnant individuals from both HICs111,113,146,183 and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs),80,82 with variability in its occurrence. In addition, lack of HCP recommendations regarding MI was often identified as a barrier, especially when pregnant individuals viewed HCPs as a trusted source of information.47,107,111,130,172,176,186 In some studies, HCPs either did not mention MI to their patients,51,137,187,188 or actively dissuaded them from getting vaccinated.156,187,188 This underlines HCPs’ critical role, especially during disease outbreaks when pregnant individuals need reassurance about the need to vaccinate.30,62,97,113

Perceived concerns regarding risk of vaccination and harm to self and fetus were notable barriers.81,99,101,119,164,191,193 Safety concerns persisted despite extensive testing and evidence supporting the safety of vaccines during pregnancy. Such concerns propagated anti-vaccination beliefs and attitudes with respect to conventional vaccines (e.g., pertussis and influenza vaccines) and extended to newer vaccines (e.g., COVID-19 vaccines). While concerns regarding COVID-19 vaccines’ safety and efficacy could be explained by the fast-track approval process necessitated by the magnitude of the pandemic, both maternal pertussis and influenza vaccines have been recommended by the World Health Organization200,201 and have been included in the national guidelines of countries from North America,3 Europe,202 Australia203,204 and, to some extent, South America205,206 and Asia.207

Besides racial disparities faced by ethnic minorities in the US, Europe, and Australia,25,60,75,108,124,128,139,148,167 lower educational qualifications and socio-economic status49,50,112,146,152 also constituted worldwide barriers to MI. However, there is no consensus in the available literature. As reported in a study from the Netherlands, higher education did not always result in increased vaccine uptake among pregnant individuals.184 In a previous SLR, higher education was associated with both lower and higher levels of vaccine acceptance, depending on the study.12 Another study associated higher education with parents’ disapproving attitude to comply with new vaccinations for their children.208 Furthermore, a cross-sectional study found that lower educated and lower health-literate parents were more willing to vaccinate their infant against rotavirus if the vaccine was accessible on the free market compared to higher educated and higher health-literate parents.209

In addition, pre-existing anti-vaccination beliefs and attitudes contributed to vaccination hesitancy among pregnant individuals. Pregnant individuals were vulnerable to rumors and misinformation circulating across the internet and social media platforms.42,43,162 These contribute to a greater distrust in existing health systems, as reflected in a study from Brazil in which pregnant individuals harbored suspicions on the hidden interests of different stakeholders (e.g., pharmaceutical companies, government bodies) in driving maternal influenza vaccination campaigns.162

While it was seen that pregnant individuals generally exhibited high information-seeking behaviors, the results in this SLR revealed that they also frequently looked for additional information about MI from various sources like the internet, talking to friends, family, and peers, or relied on their personal experiences. In this situation, evaluating the quality of the information demands awareness, judgment, and knowledge, all of which are influenced by socio-cultural norms, age, experience, and educational background.210,211 Previous studies have demonstrated that adequate knowledge and accurate information about MI from reliable sources helps mitigate anxieties and misconceptions about immunization, thereby increasing the uptake of maternal vaccines.212,213

This review also identified major enablers of MI, which included proactive communication and recommendations from HCPs and public health authorities. Trust in the information provided by HCPs, which is an integral part of the overall health system, was identified as a major enabler.35,52,55,58,60,79,105,117,125,166,180,184,197,198 While assessing knowledge and awareness of MI among HCPs was beyond the scope of the present SLR, a US-based study reported that 32% of individuals in the first trimester of pregnancy had already been vaccinated with a tetanus-diphtheria-acellular pertussis vaccine, as opposed to guidelines that recommend the vaccination during the third trimester.108,214 This finding underlines the importance of HCPs’ knowledge and awareness of MI in providing timely recommendations regarding vaccination.

In this SLR, we were able to identify the challenges and trends associated with two major global pandemics (the 2009 H1N1 and 2019 COVID pandemics) and their impact on MI. Since the COVID-19 pandemic may have led to “immunity gaps” among infants and pregnant individuals, mainly due to reduced MI and delayed infant immunization,215 it is necessary to understand the factors that influence vaccine uptake in the post-pandemic period.

Globally, the main barriers to MI were lack of knowledge and awareness, anti-vaccination beliefs, concerns about vaccine safety and efficacy, socio-economic parameters, and limited HCP recommendations. Major enablers included trust in HCPs and public health recommendations, awareness of disease risk and vaccine benefits, and positive attitudes toward vaccination. Regional analyses showed that the nature of barriers to MI varied across regions, ranging from lack of knowledge and awareness in Europe, socio-economic parameters and misinformation/conspiracy theories in North America, lack of HCP recommendations in Oceania, concerns regarding pharmaceutical or governmental motives in South America, to fears of fetal harm and vaccine safety concerns in Asian studies, highlighting the context-specific drivers of maternal vaccine hesitancy.

In the face of evolving public health challenges, advocating for MI remains paramount. The authors support MI as an important strategy to protect both pregnant individuals and infants. Based on the learnings from this SLR, a set of recommendations can be proposed. First, specific, tailored, and strategic information should be provided from a consistent and trusted source for a lay audience to meet the high information needs of pregnant individuals across different socio-demographic settings. Second, health systems should consider playing a proactive role in dispelling rumors around maternal vaccines. In LMICs, adequate focus should be given to reaching out to pregnant individuals by increasing the participation of midwives in vaccination campaigns. Also, periodic assessments should be undertaken to update HCPs’ knowledge base (including antenatal care providers) regarding national guidelines and global recommendations on MI. In addition, policymakers should document the results of these assessments for further exploration. Large-scale studies are needed to unravel the causes of ethnic or racial disparities that persist in the healthcare systems of HICs. Finally, governments should ensure that maternal vaccines are available and affordable to the entire population.

The current SLR has some limitations. First, we only included English-language articles, which might limit the generalizability of our findings. Also, the search focused on countries with in-house recommendations for maternal pertussis vaccination. Consequently, pregnant individuals from HICs were over-represented in this SLR, and findings related to influenza and other maternal vaccines may have been limited by this selection criterion. It is possible that we underestimated health system barriers due to the lack of data from LMICs. Furthermore, most studies were quantitative and survey based. This approach is helpful in identifying possible factors that affect uptake; however, to fully comprehend the reasons behind these factors and guide subsequent solutions, qualitative and mixed methodology studies are required. We also limited our search and excluded conference abstracts and other gray literature; including these could have increased the comprehensiveness of the evidence by including interim or updated results from ongoing studies and help mitigate publication bias. Another limitation is that this review only included studies from the perspective of pregnant individuals, whereas previous SLRs on MI have also included HCPs’ perspectives. The inclusion of studies focusing on HCPs and policymakers would have added to the already existing large body of research, but it was beyond the scope of this review. The strength of this study is that the quality appraisal identified only five very low-quality studies.

This SLR adds to the growing evidence on maternal vaccine hesitancy and is among the first to highlight regional differences in barriers and enablers from pregnant individuals’ perspectives, complementing the findings of an SLR conducted by Geoghegan (2022)199 on the barriers and facilitators to MI in the context of RSV and GBS. Although similar, the two SLRs focused on different maternal vaccines. Unlike other previous SLRs which focused on single vaccines, qualitative studies, or high-income settings,9,212,213 our review integrates evidence from 184 qualitative and quantitative studies and applies a structured scoring methodology to identify the most influential barriers and enablers. While our findings were largely consistent with previous SLRs, this study additionally highlights the persistence of vaccine hesitancy even in countries with established MI programs, and demonstrates that misinformation, distrust in health systems, and socio-economic disparities remain important determinants across both established and newer maternal vaccines. Our results demonstrate the importance of strong provider recommendations, equitable access to prenatal care, building trust in health authorities and vaccines, and clear communication with an emphasis on infant safety. Further research is needed to explore the dynamic nature of vaccine hesitancy and to develop targeted interventions that address specific barriers and leverage identified facilitators.
