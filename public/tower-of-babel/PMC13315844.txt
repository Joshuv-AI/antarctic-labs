One Scientist’s Noise Is Another’s Signal: Rethinking Physiological Noise in Addiction Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging
Authors: Matthew T Sutherland
Published: 2026

In the 1997 movie Contact, which was adapted from Carl Sagan’s science fiction novel, radio astronomers detect a structured signal originating from the Vega star system. As the signal is examined, a technician who is listening carefully says, “There’s a lot more here, folks!,” and the scientific team realizes that there is not one signal, but another embedded within it. This movie moment captures a durable scientific lesson: new insights can emerge when we reconsider whether something that is initially dismissed as background noise may carry meaningful information. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has long faced a similar signal-separation challenge. The blood oxygen level–dependent (BOLD) signal, which is the foundation of fMRI, is an indirect measure of neural activity that also reflects vascular, respiratory, autonomic, and other systemic physiological processes, as well as scanner instability and head motion (1, 2, 3). While the neuroimaging field has made substantial progress characterizing fMRI correlates of neuropsychiatric conditions [e.g., (4)], including substance use disorders [e.g., (5)], translating these findings into clinically actionable tools remains challenging. One reason may be that the field has drawn too sharp a boundary between neural signal and background physiological noise. Such background signals are commonly treated as nuisance variance to be modeled, regressed, or ignored. That strategy has value when the goal is to isolate neural activity, but it also risks discarding information that may itself be clinically relevant.

A recent article by Welsh et al. (6) published in Biological Psychiatry: Global Open Science invites neuroimagers to rethink the boundary between signal and noise by providing a compelling demonstration that some physiological fMRI signals may contain clinically and cognitively relevant information. Their article focuses on the systemic low-frequency oscillation (sLFO), a slow BOLD signal fluctuation that reflects whole-body physiological processes (e.g., heart rate, respiration, CO2 levels, vasomotion, and autonomic state) and propagates through the brain’s vasculature with measurable time delays (7). This signal may be particularly relevant for addiction neuroimaging because substance use, drug withdrawal, cue reactivity, and craving are not just brain-based events. They are brain-body states accompanied by coordinated changes in physiological arousal, autonomic function, and vascular tone that shape subjective experience and behavior. Using Regressor Interpolation at Progressive Time Delays (RIPTiDe), an analytic method designed to estimate and model the time-lagged systemic component of the BOLD signal (8), Welsh et al. demonstrate that the sLFO can be recovered from legacy datasets and studied as more than nuisance variance. A notable strength of their study is the use of multiple independent datasets involving distinct experimental manipulations expected to alter physiological state, including cigarette cue exposure, smoking satiety versus abstinence, and psychostimulant administration. Importantly, the authors describe multiple brain-behavior relationships linking sLFO alterations to nicotine dependence severity, cue-induced craving, and objective task performance (Figure 1). Together, these findings position the sLFO as a candidate marker of arousal-relevant brain-body physiology and a signal that may capture meaningful state dynamics missed by standard regional activation analyses.

A unifying theme across the article is that lower sLFO amplitude appears to correspond to greater arousal-relevant physiology. This pattern was particularly apparent in the article’s cigarette cue-reactivity findings. Welsh et al. observed that cigarette smokers viewing smoking cues displayed an initial decrease in brain-wide sLFO amplitude, consistent with an acute cue-induced shift toward heightened physiological arousal. As the task progressed, sLFO amplitude gradually increased, indicative of habituation or partial recovery from that initial arousal. Importantly, associations between sLFO dynamics and smoking-related measures emerged at both trait and state levels. At the trait level, lower average sLFO values over the entire task were associated with greater nicotine dependence severity, suggesting that more severely addicted smokers showed a stronger arousal-related physiological profile during cue exposure. At the state level, smaller increases in sLFO from the beginning to the end of the task (i.e., less habituation) were associated with greater cue-induced craving, suggesting that sustained arousal or reduced recovery may track subjective craving. This distinction between sLFO average amplitude and its change over time is one of the article’s more intriguing findings because it links the same physiological signal to both trait-like clinical severity and state-like craving. Critically, these sLFO findings appear to provide information distinct from task-induced regional activations. Smoking cues elicited activity in canonical regions, including nodes of the default mode and salience networks (9), and this cue-evoked activation pattern was largely unchanged whether the sLFO signal was retained or removed.

Beyond cue-induced alterations, sLFO amplitude also appears sensitive to recent smoking versus abstinence. In another dataset, Welsh et al. compared matched controls and chronic cigarette smokers who either smoked ad libitum up until fMRI scanning or abstained from smoking for at least 48 hours prior. Consistent with the authors’ interpretation of reduced physiological arousal during abstinence, abstinent smokers showed higher sLFO values than both ad libitum smokers and matched controls. That is, sLFO did not simply distinguish smokers from nonsmokers because ad libitum smokers did not differ from controls. Rather, sLFO appeared to track physiological state among smokers, with lower values following ad libitum smoking and higher values following abstinence. This is another intriguing finding because it suggests that sLFO is more sensitive to brain-body state changes (i.e., abstinence vs. satiety) than to static group membership (i.e., smoker vs. nonsmoker). Together with the cue-reactivity findings, these results suggest that sLFO tracks context-dependent arousal-relevant physiology, whether in response to cigarette cues or during abstinence.

Beyond smoking-related contexts, sLFO also appears to be altered by methylphenidate administration and linked with objective task performance among non-substance-using participants. In a third dataset, Welsh et al. considered whether a pharmacological manipulation expected to alter physiological arousal also impacted sLFO among nonsmokers. Paralleling the abstinence-versus-satiety outcomes described above, methylphenidate administration reduced sLFO amplitude compared with placebo. Critically, during performance of a multisource interference task, methylphenidate-induced sLFO changes correlated with objectively measured improvements in cognition. Specifically, participants who showed larger psychostimulant-induced reductions in sLFO also showed the greatest improvements in reaction time and accuracy following drug administration. This is an intriguing finding because it further links sLFO dynamics to objective task performance. In other words, sLFO may not only vary with clinically relevant features among cigarette smokers (i.e., self-reported dependence severity and craving) but also with physiological state variation associated with cognitive performance in non-substance-using individuals. These findings broaden the article’s impact, suggesting that sLFO may index brain-body states that shape both addiction-specific subjective experiences and cognition more generally.

The article’s methodological contribution is also noteworthy. Global fMRI signals have long been a source of contention because they contain both neural and nonneural components, including vascular, respiratory, motion-related, and other systemic sources of variance (10). The appeal of RIPTiDe is that it attempts to extract a specific time-lagged component from the BOLD signal that moves through the brain with vascular delays. One attractive feature is that this method can extract the sLFO from standard fMRI data, including legacy datasets without heart rate, respiration, pupillary, or CO2 measures. Extracting sLFO signals may then yield both cleaner estimates of regional task-evoked activity and a meaningful physiological state measure. Therefore, sLFO estimation could be viewed not only as a denoising strategy but also as a potential form of phenotyping. This may prove valuable for existing fMRI datasets that include rich clinical, behavioral, and neuroimaging measures but limited peripheral physiological recordings. In addiction research, this could include revisiting datasets to ask whether systemic physiological signals predict withdrawal severity, relapse risk, medication response, or cessation outcomes.

Such possibilities come with cautions. The sLFO cannot be treated as a pure measure of arousal because it is a systemic aspect of the BOLD signal influenced by multiple processes, which may respond differently across task, pharmacological, and clinical contexts. Future studies may be strongest when sLFO measures can be compared and contrasted with direct physiological recordings. Multimodal validation may be important for better characterizing what the sLFO most strongly reflects (e.g., autonomic arousal, vascular tone, respiratory physiology, or some combination thereof) and how it should be treated in a given analysis (e.g., as a nuisance confounder, mediator, biomarker, or outcome). Finally, characterizing reliability, specificity, and predictive ability is important: are sLFO changes reproducible within individuals, can they distinguish different forms of physiological arousal, and do they prospectively predict meaningful outcomes?

Welsh et al. deployed RIPTiDe across multiple datasets to “listen” to the BOLD signal differently. They highlight brain-behavior relationships at multiple levels, suggesting that some signals typically treated as background noise may contain meaningful information about brain-body states relevant to addiction, clinical measures, and cognition. The article’s broader contribution is perhaps to encourage neuroimagers to be more curious about the physiological structure embedded in the BOLD signal and to consider when background signals are best treated as variance to manage versus meaningful information to explore. Carl Sagan was a passionate advocate for combining imaginative possibilities with disciplined skepticism in science, as captured in the aphorism popularized in his book Broca’s Brain, “extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence.” Welsh et al.’s article brings a similar spirit to neuroimaging research by providing multiple sources of evidence and asking us to listen more carefully before deciding what is signal and what is noise. Sometimes, there is a lot more there.
