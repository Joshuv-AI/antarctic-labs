Shallow Representation of Option Implied Information
Jimin Lin

Abstract
Option prices encode the market’s collective outlook through implied density and implied volatility. An explicit link between implied density and implied volatility translates the risk-neutrality of the former into conditions on the latter to rule out static arbitrage. Despite earlier recognition of their parity, the two had been studied in isolation for decades until the recent demand in implied volatility modeling rejuvenated such parity. This paper provides a systematic approach to build neural representations of option implied information. As a preliminary, we first revisit the explicit link between implied density and implied volatility through an alternative and minimalist lens, where implied volatility is viewed not as volatility but as a pointwise corrector mapping the Black-Scholes quasi-density into the implied risk-neutral density. Building on this perspective, we propose the neural representation that incorporates arbitrage constraints through the differentiable corrector. With an additive logistic model as the synthetic benchmark, extensive experiments reveal that deeper or wider network structures do not necessarily improve the model performance due to the nonlinearity of both arbitrage constraints and neural derivatives. By contrast, a shallow feedforward network with a single hidden layer and a specific activation effectively approximates implied density and implied volatility. Keywords: implied volatility; implied risk-neutral density; machine learning; shallow neural network

Option Pricing Simplified

For simplicity, we consider assets with positive prices. For treatment of negative prices, see
(
18
)
. Let
S_{t}
denote the price of the underlying asset at
t
. We assume the instantaneous risk-free interest rate
r_{t}
and the continuous dividend rate
d_{t}
are deterministic and positive. The forward price of the asset from
t
to some future time
T
is given by
F_{t,T}=S_{t}e^{\int_{t}^{T}(r_{u}-d_{u})\differential u}
. A European vanilla put (resp. call) option with expiry
T
and strike price
K
offers the right to sell (resp. buy) the asset with price
K
at time
T
, which gives a payoff of
(K-S_{T})^{+}
(resp.
(S_{T}-K)^{+}
).

The market price of vanilla European put option,
P_{t}(T,K)
, and call option,
C_{t}(T,K)
, are given by the discounted expected payoff under the market risk-neutral measure
\mathbb{Q}
:

\displaystyle P_{t}(T,K)=e^{-\int_{t}^{T}r_{u}\differential u}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[(K-S_{T})^{+}|S_{t}\right],

\displaystyle C_{t}(T,K)=e^{-\int_{t}^{T}r_{u}\differential u}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[(S_{T}-K)^{+}|S_{t}\right].

(2.1)

Innate dimension reduction and data standardization

The following changes of variables translate the above prices expressed in absolute calendar time and dollar values into relative and dimensionless quantities. Define the tenor
\tau:=T-t
and set
F_{\tau}:=F_{t,T}
. Define

\displaystyle X_{\tau}:=\log\frac{S_{T}}{F_{\tau}},

\displaystyle\kappa:=\log\frac{K}{F_{\tau}},

(2.2)

where
X_{0}=0
. They have intuitive economic interpretations.
X_{\tau}
is the
dividend adjusted logarithmic excess return
(
return
for short).
\kappa
is the
required excess return to hit the strike
, which is commonly called the log-forward-moneyness (
moneyness
for short).

Options quoted by expiry and strike are readily expressed in tenor and moneyness by
(T,K)=(\tau+t,F_{\tau}e^{\kappa})
. Dividing Equation (
2.1
) by dividend adjusted spot price
S_{t}e^{-\int_{t}^{\tau}d_{u}\differential u}
, we define

\displaystyle p(\tau,\kappa):=\frac{P_{t}(\tau+t,F_{\tau}e^{\kappa})}{S_{t}e^{-\int_{t}^{\tau}d_{u}\differential u}}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\left(e^{\kappa}-e^{X_{\tau}}\right)^{+}\right],

\displaystyle c(\tau,\kappa):=\frac{C_{t}(\tau+t,F_{\tau}e^{\kappa})}{S_{t}e^{-\int_{t}^{\tau}d_{u}\differential u}}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\left(e^{X_{\tau}}-e^{\kappa}\right)^{+}\right].

(2.3)

p
is the
put-to-(dividend-adjusted-)spot price ratio
and
c
is the
call-to-(dividend-adjusted-)spot price ratio
. For convenience, we still refer to them by put price and call price.

This transformation abstracts away
r
,
d
, and
S_{t}
and aligns
X_{\tau}
,
\kappa
,
p
, and
c
on the same scale. Thus, it yields inherent dimension reduction and data standardization.

Generic form of pricing formula

Let
\psi(\tau,\cdot)
and
\Psi(\tau,\cdot)
denote the probability density function (PDF) and cumulative distribution function (CDF, or distribution) of
X_{\tau}
under the
\mathbb{Q}
-measure. Note that
X
is an exponential
\mathbb{Q}
-martingale, so it holds that
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[e^{X_{\tau}}]=\int e^{x}\psi(\tau,x)\differential x=1
, which implies that
\widetilde{\psi}(\tau,x):=e^{x}\psi(\tau,x)
is already an Esscher exponential tilting of
\psi
. In other words,
\widetilde{\psi}
is also a PDF. Let
\widetilde{\Psi}
be the corresponding CDF of
\widetilde{\psi}
. Also define the complement of the CDF by
\overline{\Psi}=1-{\Psi}
. Then Equation (
2.3
) can be simplified to the following form:

\displaystyle p(\tau,\kappa)=e^{\kappa}\Psi(\tau,\kappa)-\widetilde{\Psi}(\tau,\kappa),

\displaystyle c(\tau,\kappa)=\overline{\widetilde{\Psi}}(\tau,\kappa)-e^{\kappa}\overline{\Psi}(\tau,\kappa).

(2.4)

Equation (
2.4
) explicates the relation between option price and the implied CDF, and also indicates that the essence of an option pricing model is to specify the risk-neutral CDF.

Sufficient conditions on price to exclude static arbitrage

16
lists sufficient conditions on option prices to exclude static arbitrages. Here we express them in terms of the put option:

i.

Calendar spread: for any
\Delta\tau>0
,
p(\tau+\Delta\tau,\kappa)\geq p(\tau,\kappa)
due to convexity of vanilla payoff;

ii.

Vertical spread: for any
\Delta\kappa>0
,
p(\tau,\kappa+\Delta\kappa)-p(\tau,\kappa)\geq 0
since the first payoff dominates the second;

iii.

Butterfly spread:
p(\tau,\kappa+\Delta\kappa)-2p(\tau,\kappa)+p(\tau,\kappa-\Delta\kappa)\geq 0
as the portfolio has possible positive payoff.

These conditions can be expressed with partial derivatives as follows:

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}p(\tau,\kappa)\geq 0,

\displaystyle\partial_{\kappa}p(\tau,\kappa)\geq 0,

\displaystyle\partial_{\kappa\kappa}p(\tau,\kappa)\geq 0.

(2.5)

Calculating these partial derivatives from Equation (
2.4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}p(\tau,\kappa)

\displaystyle=e^{\kappa}\partial_{\tau}\Psi(\tau,\kappa)-\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{\Psi}(\tau,\kappa),

(2.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{\kappa}p(\tau,\kappa)

\displaystyle=e^{\kappa}\Psi(\tau,\kappa),

\displaystyle\partial_{\kappa\kappa}p(\tau,\kappa)

\displaystyle=e^{\kappa}\Psi(\tau,\kappa)+e^{\kappa}\psi(\tau,\kappa).

Notice that the positive vertical spread condition
\partial_{\kappa}p\geq 0
and positive butterfly spread condition
\partial_{\kappa\kappa}p\geq 0
inherit from the risk-neutrality of the distribution. Only the last positive calendar spread condition
\partial_{\tau}p\geq 0
requires a specific term structure of the distribution.

Black-Scholes model

In the Black-Scholes model, the marginal distribution of the underlying return follows a risk-neutral normal distribution, whose variance increases linearly with tenor and whose mean equals negative one-half the variance,
X_{\tau}\sim N(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\tau,\sigma^{2}\tau)
. The volatility
\sigma>0
is the only free parameter. Defining the total volatility
\omega
absorbs the term structure and simplifies expressions:

\omega(\tau):=\sigma\sqrt{\tau}

(2.7)

The corresponding Black-Scholes PDF, CDF, tilted PDF, and tilted CDF are

\displaystyle\psi_{BS}(\tau,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega(\tau)}\phi\big(z_{+}(\tau,x)\big),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}_{BS}(\tau,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega(\tau)}\phi\big(z_{-}(\tau,x)\big),

(2.8)

\displaystyle\Psi_{BS}(\tau,x)

\displaystyle=\Phi\big(z_{+}(\tau,x)\big),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{BS}(\tau,x)

\displaystyle=\Phi\big(z_{-}(\tau,x)\big),

(2.9)

where
\phi
and
\Phi
are respectively the PDF and CDF of the standard normal distribution
N(0,1)
and

z_{\pm}(\tau,x):=\frac{x\pm\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}(\tau)}{\omega(\tau)}

(2.10)

are statistical pivots of
N(\mp\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}(\tau),\omega^{2}(\tau))
. Here
\omega^{2}(\tau)=(\omega(\tau))^{2}
is a shorthand.

The Black-Scholes pricing formula is readily obtained by plugging Equation (
2.8
) into Equation (
2.3
) and using the symmetry of normal distribution:

\displaystyle p_{BS}(\tau,\kappa)

\displaystyle=e^{\kappa}\Psi_{BS}(\tau,\kappa)-\widetilde{\Psi}_{BS}(\tau,\kappa)=e^{\kappa}\Phi\big(z_{+}(\tau,\kappa)\big)-\Phi\big(z_{-}(\tau,\kappa)\big),

(2.11)

\displaystyle c_{BS}(\tau,\kappa)

\displaystyle=\overline{\widetilde{\Psi}}_{BS}(\tau,\kappa)-e^{\kappa}\overline{\Psi}_{BS}(\tau,\kappa)=\Phi\big(-z_{-}(\tau,\kappa)\big)-e^{\kappa}\Phi\big(-z_{+}(\tau,\kappa)\big).

(2.12)

Note that the pivots
z_{-}
and
z_{+}
are rewritten forms of classical Black-Scholes terms
-d_{1}
and
-d_{2}
to make their financial and statistical interpretations explicit:
z_{+}
(resp.
z_{-}
) is the location-scale standardized return under the Black-Scholes PDF (resp. tilted PDF).

Additive logistic model as the benchmark model

We will use the additive logistic model as our benchmark model, which was introduced by
(
17
)
and extensively studied by
(
4
;
5
;
40
)
. Distinguished from models built bottom-up from stochastic differential equations, the additive logistic model provides direct top-down access to the risk-neutral density of the underlying asset return. For every tenor, three parameters can be specified to control the dispersion, right tail, and left tail of the marginal distribution, which is logistic-beta
(
6
)
. The term structure is customized by the choice of interpolating every parameter into a continuous function of tenor, and by such, the underlying martingale process is easily constructed. It has an explicit pricing formula and is flexible to fit the real data in usual market conditions. Those features make the additive logistic model an ideal benchmark for new model development.

In the additive logistic model, every marginal of the return follows a risk-neutral logistic-beta distribution,
X_{\tau}\sim LB(\mu(\tau),\varsigma(\tau),\alpha(\tau),\beta(\tau))
. The location parameter is fixed due to martingality as

\displaystyle\mu(\tau)=\log\left(\frac{B(\alpha(\tau)+\varsigma(\tau),\beta(\tau)-\varsigma(\tau))}{B(\alpha(\tau),\beta(\tau))}\right),

(2.13)

where
B
is the beta function. The three free parameters
\varsigma
,
\alpha
, and
\beta
are functions of
\tau
that respectively determine the term structure of dispersion, right tail, and left tail. The PDF
\phi_{LB}
and CDF
\Phi_{LB}
of standard logistic-beta distribution
LB(0,1,\alpha,\beta)
are given by

\displaystyle\phi_{LB}(z;\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\phi_{L}(z)(\Phi_{L}(z))^{\alpha-1}(1-\Phi_{L}(z))^{\beta-1},

(2.14)

\displaystyle\Phi_{LB}(z;\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\Phi_{L}(z)}u^{\alpha-1}(1-u)^{\beta-1}\differential u,

where
\phi_{L}
and
\Phi_{L}
are the PDF and CDF of standard logistic distribution
L(0,1)
:

\displaystyle\phi_{L}(z)=\frac{e^{z}}{(1+e^{z})^{2}},

\displaystyle\Phi_{L}(z)=\frac{e^{z}}{1+e^{z}}.

(2.15)

Define
z(\tau,x):=\frac{x-\mu(\tau)}{\varsigma(\tau)}
as the pivot. The risk-neutral PDF, tilted PDF, CDF, and tilted CDF of
X_{\tau}
are

\displaystyle\psi_{LB}(\tau,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varsigma(\tau)}\phi_{LB}\big(z(\tau,x);\alpha(\tau),\beta(\tau)\big),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}_{LB}(\tau,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varsigma(\tau)}\phi_{LB}\big(z(\tau,x);\alpha(\tau)+\varsigma(\tau),\beta(\tau)-\varsigma(\tau)\big),

(2.16)

\displaystyle\Psi_{LB}(\tau,x)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{LB}\big(z(\tau,x);\alpha(\tau),\beta(\tau)\big),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{LB}(\tau,x)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{LB}\big(z(\tau,x);\alpha(\tau)+\varsigma(\tau),\beta(\tau)-\varsigma(\tau)\big),

(2.17)

Plugging Equation (
2.14
) into Equation (
2.3
) and using the symmetry of the logistic-beta distribution yields the option pricing formula:

\displaystyle p_{LB}(\tau,\kappa)

\displaystyle=e^{\kappa}\Psi_{LB}(\tau,\kappa)-\widetilde{\Psi}_{LB}(\tau,\kappa)

(2.18)

\displaystyle=e^{\kappa}\Phi_{LB}\big(z(\tau,\kappa);\alpha(\tau),\beta(\tau)\big)-\Phi_{LB}\big(z(\tau,\kappa);\alpha(\tau)+\varsigma(\tau),\beta(\tau)-\varsigma(\tau)\big),

\displaystyle c_{LB}(\tau,\kappa)

\displaystyle=\overline{\widetilde{\Psi}}_{LB}(\tau,\kappa)-e^{\kappa}\overline{\Psi}_{LB}(\tau,\kappa)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{LB}\big(-z(\tau,\kappa);\beta(\tau)-\varsigma(\tau),\alpha(\tau)+\varsigma(\tau)\big)-e^{\kappa}\Phi_{LB}\big(-z(\tau,\kappa);\beta(\tau),\alpha(\tau)\big).

To ensure valid densities and to exclude static arbitrage, the following conditions on the term structures should hold
(
17
, Proposition 4.1)
: (i)
\varsigma<\beta
; (ii)
\alpha,\beta>0
; (iii)
\varsigma
is nondecreasing and
\varsigma(0)=0
; and (iv)
\frac{\alpha}{\varsigma}
and
\frac{\beta}{\varsigma}
are nonincreasing.

For
X\sim LB(\mu,\varsigma,\alpha,\beta)
, the first four central moments, expectation, variance, skewness, and (excess) kurtosis, are given by
(
6
)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X]

\displaystyle=\varsigma(\gamma(\alpha)-\gamma(\beta))+\mu,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[X]

\displaystyle=\varsigma^{2}(\gamma^{\prime}(\alpha)+\gamma^{\prime}(\beta)),

(2.19)

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}[X]

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)-\gamma^{\prime\prime}(\beta)}{(\gamma(\alpha)+\gamma(\beta))^{3/2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}[X]

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime\prime}(\alpha)+\gamma^{\prime\prime\prime}(\beta)}{(\gamma(\alpha)+\gamma(\beta))^{2}},

where
\gamma^{(n)}
are the polygamma functions of order
n
.

Implied Volatility Reframed

Implied volatility is understood at a practical level as the quantity that matches the option price produced by the Black-Scholes formula (
2.11
) to the market option price. It changed from a constant to a function of tenors and moneynesses after the 1987 Black Monday crash. That is, to reproduce the same option price from the market, one has to amend pointwise the parameter
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
with respect to both tenor and moneyness, into
\sigma:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
(\tau,\kappa)\mapsto\sigma(\tau,\kappa)
. Equations (
2.7
)-(
2.11
) have to be modified accordingly. The total implied volatility becomes

\displaystyle\omega(\tau,\kappa):=\sigma(\tau,\kappa)\sqrt{\tau},

(3.1)

and the new pivots are

\displaystyle z_{\pm}^{\omega}(\tau,x;\kappa):=\frac{x\pm\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}(\tau,\kappa)}{\omega(\tau,\kappa)},

\displaystyle z_{\pm}^{\omega}(\tau,x):=z_{\pm}^{\omega}(\tau,x;x).

(3.2)

The pricing formula is reformulated with
\omega
as

\displaystyle p_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)=e^{\kappa}\Phi\big(z_{+}^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)\big)-\Phi\big(z_{-}^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)\big),

\displaystyle c_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)=\Phi\big(-z_{-}^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)\big)-e^{\kappa}\Phi\big(-z_{+}^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)\big).

(3.3)

The modified Black-Scholes PDF and CDF are given by

\displaystyle\psi_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,x;\kappa)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega(\tau,\kappa)}\phi\big(z_{+}^{\omega}(\tau,x;\kappa)\big),

\displaystyle\psi_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,x)

\displaystyle=\psi_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,x;x),

(3.4)

\displaystyle\Psi_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,x;\kappa)

\displaystyle=\Phi\big(z_{+}^{\omega}(\tau,x;\kappa)\big),

\displaystyle\Psi_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,x)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,x;x).

The tilted PDF
\widetilde{\psi}_{BS}^{\omega}
and CDF
\widetilde{\Psi}_{BS}^{\omega}
are modified in the same way. Noticing that
\psi_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,x)
(resp.
\Psi_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,x)
) without a fixed
\kappa
is not a valid PDF (resp. CDF), we call it the Black-Scholes quasi-PDF (resp. quasi-CDF).

We also insert the Black-Scholes vega
v_{BS}^{\omega}
here:

v_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa):=\partial_{\sigma}p_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)=\partial_{\sigma}c_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)=\phi\big(z_{-}^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)\big)\sqrt{\tau}.

(3.5)

It is useful in calibration to convert price differences into volatility differences, motivated by the first order Taylor expansion
\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{0}=(\partial_{\sigma}p)^{-1}(p_{1}-p_{0})+O(p_{1}-p_{0})^{2}
.

Volatility reductionism

Modern option study relies heavily on the notion of volatility to both form intuition and describe underlying dynamics, such as local volatility, stochastic volatility, local stochastic volatility, and rough volatility. However, from the lens of marginal distribution, the term volatility is unexpressive on the tail behavior that is more relevant to the documented smile shapes of implied volatility. The contrast between the risk-neutral logistic-beta distribution and the risk-neutral normal distribution provides a clear example.

Implied volatility conflation

It is now clear how, without skewness and kurtosis, volatility is impassive for distributional information. From this point of view, we understand that implied volatility is an overloaded term that compresses dispersion, asymmetry, and heavy tails altogether. We henceforth exemplify this by assuming that the true underlying return follows the additive logistic model. The correct risk-neutral density is

Implied scale as corrector

Based on the above observation, a more holistic interpretation of the implied volatility surface can be inferred: rather than volatility, implied volatility is a correction function to pointwisely morph the Black-Scholes quasi-density into the correct density. We formalize this idea by the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\psi
and
\Psi
be the market risk-neutral PDF and CDF,
p
be the relative put option price defined in Equation (
2.4
),
\omega
be the total implied volatility surface defined in Equation (
3.1
). The following equalities hold:

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}p(\tau,\kappa)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\psi}_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)\omega(\tau,\kappa)\partial_{\tau}\omega(\tau,\kappa),

(3.7)

\displaystyle\Psi(\tau,\kappa)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)+\zeta^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa),

(3.8)

\displaystyle\psi(\tau,\kappa)

\displaystyle=\psi_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)\xi^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa),

(3.9)

where
\zeta^{\omega}
is the correction addend and
\xi^{\omega}
is the correction multiplier given by

\displaystyle\zeta^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)

\displaystyle=\psi_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)\omega(\tau,\kappa)\partial_{\kappa}\omega(\tau,\kappa),

(3.10)

\displaystyle\xi^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\kappa}{\omega(\tau,\kappa)}\partial_{\kappa}\omega(\tau,\kappa)\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\big(\omega(\tau,\kappa)\partial_{\kappa}\omega(\tau,\kappa)\big)^{2}+\omega(\tau,\kappa)\partial_{\kappa\kappa}\omega(\tau,\kappa).

\psi_{BS}^{\omega}
,
\widetilde{\psi}_{BS}^{\omega}
, and
\Psi_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)
are the Black-Scholes quasi-PDF, tilted quasi-PDF, and quasi-CDF defined around Equation (
3.4
).

Equation (
3.7
) relates to the term structure of
\omega
. In Equation (
3.8
), the addend
\zeta^{\omega}
pointwisely matches the Black-Scholes quasi-CDF with the true CDF. Equation (
3.9
) corrects the Black-Scholes quasi-PDF with the true PDF through the multiplier
\xi^{\omega}
. Figure
4
visualizes all of these related quantities.

Remark 3.2
.

In the same spirit of seeing

as the corrector, we can imagine other possible pricing formulas with correctors and other analogs of Proposition

Sufficient conditions on implied volatility to exclude static arbitrage

Combining Proposition
3.1
, Equation (
2.5
), and (
2.6
) readily conveys the risk-neutrality of implied density to sufficient conditions imposed on implied volatility to exclude static arbitrages, which we casually collect in the following corollary. For more detailed treatments, we refer to
(
44
, Theorem 2.9)
.

Corollary 3.3
.

An implied volatility surface
\omega
admits

i.

no calendar spread arbitrage if, for every
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
,
\omega(\cdot,\kappa)
is nondecreasing;

ii.

no vertical spread arbitrage if, for every
\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\Psi_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,\cdot)+\zeta^{\omega}(\tau,\cdot)
is a valid CDF;

iii.

no butterfly spread arbitrage if, for every
\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\psi_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,\cdot)\xi^{\omega}(\tau,\cdot)
is a valid PDF.

Because we only focus on data from the bound region
(\tau,\kappa)\in[\underline{\tau},\overline{\tau}]\times[\underline{\kappa},\overline{\kappa}]
in the next section, we leave out asymptotics of expiring tenor and extreme moneyness. Hence, the conditions we need from Corollary
3.3
boil down to the following:

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\omega(\tau,\kappa)\geq 0,

\displaystyle\zeta^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)\geq-\Psi_{BS}^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa),

\displaystyle\xi^{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)\geq 0.

(3.11)

The nonlinearity of
\zeta^{\omega}
and
\xi^{\omega}
still hinders direct parameterization of
\omega
, which stimulates the use of a neural network to approximate
\omega
.

4
Neural Representation Shallowed

The expressibility of neural networks has been extensively studied in the past decades.
(
34
;
35
;
36
)
provided very general conditions for standard multilayer feedforward networks with sufficiently smooth activation to well approximate functions and their derivatives with respect to Lebesgue space. Although theoretically verified, expressibility relies on a particular design of neural network structure, including depth, width, and choice of activation functions. For instance,
(
24
)
prioritizes depth of network over width for better expressibility, while
(
15
)
shows that one can reduce the depth of the network with an appropriate choice of activation functions. Nonetheless, building a neural representation of implied volatility and implied density falls outside the standard setting considered above; its viability should be tested with extensive experiments.

In contrast to previous successful implementations in
(
1
;
49
;
47
;
48
)
with deep or sophisticated neural networks, our goal is to find the simplest neural representations for option implied information upon the representative additive logistic model. It is motivated by the following rationales. Firstly, both implied density and implied volatility are, after all, smooth bivariate functions, and they seem to have been consistently behaving under the usual market conditions for a long time
(
21
)
. So, we expect that a simple neural network is sufficient for expressibility. Second, the additive logistic model is highly customizable for both implied density and implied volatility, and is representative of usual market conditions
(
40
)
. We suggest that it is preliminary to fit the neural representation before real data; a more holistic experiment can be conducted, such as goodness of fit to implied density that is not accessible in the real situation; the well-trained neural representation can be the initial guess for learning with real market data. With such a goal, we design the neural representation framework and the experiments.

Framework

Let
\widehat{f}
denote the neural network approximation of function
f
. The neural representation framework feeds on an option chain
\mathfrak{D}
as the input data to learn the implied density
\widehat{\psi}(\cdot,\cdot)
that is backed by the implied total volatility
\widehat{\omega}(\cdot,\cdot)
. The framework of neural representation of option implied information consists of a simple feedforward neural network plus several auto-differentiable modules, outlined in Figure
5
. We now describe each module.

:
Neural representation framework.

Data.

The dataset includes tenors, moneynesses, and the corresponding option prices in the option chain. We denote the dataset by
\mathfrak{D}=\{(\tau,\kappa,p(\tau,\kappa)\}_{(\tau,\kappa)\in\mathfrak{T}\times\mathfrak{K}}
, where the tenor set
\mathfrak{T}=\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\dots,\tau_{N}\}
contains
\absolutevalue{\kT}=N
number of tenors with uniform grid difference
\Delta\tau
and the moneyness set
\mathfrak{K}=\{\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots,\kappa_{M}\}
has
\absolutevalue{\kK}=M
number of moneynesses with uniform grid difference
\Delta\kappa

\absolutevalue{\kD}=\absolutevalue{\kT}\absolutevalue{\kK}=NM
data points. In the real scenario, the data grid is uneven - number of listed moneynesses varies among different tenors - so here, setting the data points into the Cartesian product of tenor set and moneyness set is a simplification.

Volatility module:
(\tau,\kappa)\mapsto\widehat{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)
.

We approximate the total implied volatility with a feedforward neural network
\widehat{\omega}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
L
hidden layers and an output layer indexed by
L+1
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)=\mathcal{V}(\tau,\kappa)=\mathcal{A}_{L+1}\circ\mathcal{F}_{L+1}\circ\cdots\mathcal{A}_{1}\circ\mathcal{F}_{1}(\tau,\kappa)

(4.1)

where, for
1\leq l\leq L+1
,
\mathcal{F}_{l}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{l-1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}
that
\mathcal{F}_{l}(x_{l-1})=W_{l}x_{l-1}+b_{l}
is the affine map with
W_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{l-1}}
being the weights and
b_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}
being the bias,
\mathcal{A}_{l}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}
is the element-wise activation function, and
n_{l}
is the width of the layer.
l=0
corresponds to the input
(\tau,\kappa)
of dimension
n_{0}=2
. The output layer
l=L+1
gives a scalar so
n_{L+1}=1
. As the implied volatility is nonnegative, the last activation function should output nonnegative values.

Price module:
(\tau,\kappa,\widehat{\omega})\mapsto\big(\widehat{p}(\tau,\kappa),\widehat{v}(\tau,\kappa)\big)
.

With total implied volatility
\widehat{\omega}
calculated, we calculate the approximated option price
\widehat{p}
and in addition the vega
\widehat{v}
by plugging
\widehat{\omega}
into Equation (
2.11
) and Equation (
3.5
):

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(\tau,\kappa)=p_{BS}^{\widehat{\omega}}(\tau,\kappa),

\displaystyle\widehat{v}(\tau,\kappa)=v_{BS}^{\widehat{\omega}}(\tau,\kappa).

(4.2)

Derivative module:
(\tau,\kappa,\widehat{\omega})\mapsto\big(\partial_{\tau}\widehat{\omega}(\tau,\kappa),\partial_{\kappa}\widehat{\omega}(\tau,\kappa),\partial_{\kappa\kappa}\widehat{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)\big)
.

It is easier to express partial derivatives of
\widehat{\omega}
starting from the recursive form of Equation (
4.1
):

\begin{cases}x_{0}=(\tau,\kappa),\\
x_{l}=\mathcal{A}_{l}(y_{l}),\quad y_{l}=\mathcal{F}_{l}(x_{l-1}),\quad 1\leq l\leq L+1,\\
\widehat{\omega}=x_{L+1}.\end{cases}

(4.3)

Differentiating Equation (
4.3
) we obtain the first order partial derivatives
\partial_{\tau}\widehat{\omega}
and
\partial_{\kappa}\widehat{\omega}
,

\begin{cases}\partial_{\cdot}\widehat{\omega}=\partial_{\kappa}x_{L+1}\\
\partial_{\cdot}x_{l}=\mathcal{A}_{l}^{\prime}(y_{l})\odot(W_{l}\partial_{\cdot}x_{l-1}),\quad 1\leq l\leq L+1,\\
\partial_{\cdot}x_{0}=(\partial_{\cdot}\tau,\partial_{\cdot}\kappa),\end{cases}

(4.4)

and the second order partial derivative
\partial_{\kappa\kappa}\widehat{\omega}
,

\begin{cases}\partial_{\kappa\kappa}\widehat{\omega}=\partial_{\kappa\kappa}x_{L+1},\\
\partial_{\kappa\kappa}x_{l}=\mathcal{A}_{l}^{\prime\prime}(y_{l})\odot(W_{l}\partial_{\cdot}x_{l-1})^{\odot 2}+\mathcal{A}_{l}^{\prime}(y_{l})\odot(W_{l}\partial_{\kappa\kappa}x_{l-1}),\quad 1\leq l\leq L+1,\\
\partial_{\kappa\kappa}x_{0}=0,\end{cases}

(4.5)

where
\odot
is Hadamard product,
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}
are the element-wise first and second derivatives of
\mathcal{A}
.

Corrector module:
(\tau,\kappa,\widehat{\omega},\partial_{\tau}\widehat{\omega},\partial_{\kappa}\widehat{\omega},\partial_{\kappa\kappa}\widehat{\omega})\mapsto\big(\widehat{\zeta}(\tau,\kappa),\widehat{\xi}(\tau,\kappa)\big)
.
The correctors are calculated by plugging
\widehat{\omega}
into Equation (
3.10

\displaystyle\widehat{\zeta}(\tau,\kappa)=\zeta^{\widehat{\omega}}(\tau,\kappa),

\displaystyle\widehat{\xi}(\tau,\kappa)=\xi^{\widehat{\omega}}(\tau,\kappa).

(4.6)

Density module:
(\tau,\kappa,\widehat{\omega},\widehat{\zeta},\widehat{\xi})\mapsto\widehat{\psi}(\tau,\kappa)
.
The estimated implied density
\widehat{\psi}
is calculated by substituting
\widehat{\omega}
into Equation (

Arbitrage module:
(\tau,\kappa,\widehat{\omega},\partial_{\tau}\widehat{\omega},\widehat{\zeta},\widehat{\xi})\mapsto\big(\epsilon_{C}(\tau,\kappa),\epsilon_{V}(\tau,\kappa),\epsilon_{B}(\tau,\kappa)\big)
.
Errors corresponding to calendar spread arbitrage, vertical spread arbitrage, and butterfly spread arbitrage are

\displaystyle\epsilon_{C}(\tau,\kappa)=\left(-\partial_{\tau}\widehat{\omega}(\tau,\kappa)\right)^{+},

\displaystyle\epsilon_{V}(\tau,\kappa)=\left(-\Psi_{BS}^{\widehat{\omega}}(\tau,\kappa)-\zeta^{\widehat{\omega}}(\tau,\kappa)\right)^{+},

\displaystyle\epsilon_{B}(\tau,\kappa)=\left(-\xi^{\widehat{\omega}}(\tau,\kappa)\right)^{+},

(4.7)

where
\zeta
and
\xi
are calculated based on Equation (
3.10
) with
\widehat{\omega}
plugged in.
\epsilon_{\{C,V,B\}}
quantifies the degree to which inequalities (
3.11

Loss module.

Evaluated over the dataset
\mathfrak{D}

\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{D})
is the sum of losses caused by pricing loss,
\mathcal{L}_{P}(\mathfrak{D})
, and arbitrage violations,
\mathcal{L}_{C}(\mathfrak{D})
,
\mathcal{L}_{V}(\mathfrak{D})
,
\mathcal{L}_{B}(\mathfrak{D})

\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{D})=\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{P}(\mathfrak{D})}_{\text{weighted price}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{C}(\mathfrak{D})}_{\text{calendar spread}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{V}(\mathfrak{D})}_{\text{vertical spread}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{B}(\mathfrak{D})}_{\text{butterfly spread}}

(4.8)

where each loss is chosen to be the root mean square error as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{P}(\mathfrak{D})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\kD}}\sum_{(\tau,\kappa)\in\mathfrak{T}\times\mathfrak{K}}\frac{1}{\widehat{v}(\tau,\kappa)}\big(\widehat{p}(\tau,\kappa)-p(\tau,\kappa)\big)^{2}\right)^{1/2},

(4.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\{C,V,B\}}(\mathfrak{D})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\kD}}\sum_{(\tau,\kappa)\in\mathfrak{T}\times\mathfrak{K}}\big(\epsilon_{\{C,V,B\}}(\tau,\kappa)\big)^{2}\right)^{1/2},

A valid trained model should hold that

\mathcal{L}_{\{C,V,B\}}(\mathfrak{D})\equiv 0.

(4.10)

Also notice that the vega weighted price loss
\mathcal{L}_{P}
is equivalent to the implied volatility loss.

Extra density loss module.

The market risk-neutral distribution is not observable in practice. We exploit the known ground truth density in the synthetic data experiment to examine the goodness of fit to the implied density of the neural representation. Density deviation is measured with the density loss
\mathcal{L}_{D}
over the dataset
\mathfrak{D}
that is designed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{D}(\mathfrak{D})=\left(\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\kT}}\sum_{(\tau,\kappa)\in\mathfrak{T}\times\mathfrak{K}}\big(\widehat{\psi}(\tau,\kappa)-\psi(\tau,\kappa)\big)^{2}\Delta\kappa\right)^{1/2}.

(4.11)

In the later section, we will report a case where the
\widehat{p}
and
\widehat{\sigma}
well approximate

\sigma

\widehat{\psi}
significantly deviates from

Neural network structure.

The core module in the system is the volatility module
\mathcal{V}
defined in Equation (
4.1

\omega

\mathcal{A}_{L+1}=\mathcal{A}_{\text{Softplus}}
, whose formula and derivatives are

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{Softplus}}(x)=\ln(1 + e^x),

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{Softplus}}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{Softplus}}^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{e^{x}}{(1+e^{x})^{2}}.

(4.15)

Notice that the first and second derivatives of the softplus activation coincide with the logistic CDF and PDF given in Equation (

\partial_{\kappa}\widehat{\omega}
and
\partial_{\kappa\kappa}\widehat{\omega}

The twice differentiability of
\widehat{\omega}
is backed by the softplus activation, so we have a wide choice of activations for hidden layers. For simplicity, we let all
\{\mathcal{A}_{l}\}_{1\leq l\leq L}
have the same activation, and choose from the following candidates: ReLU, quadratic ReLU, cubic ReLU, ELU, and Tanh.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{ReLU}}(x)=(x)^{+},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\text{ReLU}}(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{x>0\}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}_{\text{ReLU}}(x)=0,

(4.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{ReLU2}}(x)=\frac{1}{2}((x)^{+})^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{ReLU2}}^{\prime}(x)=(x)^{+},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{ReLU2}}^{\prime\prime}(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{x>0\}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{ReLU3}}(x)=\frac{1}{6}((x)^{+})^{3},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{ReLU3}}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{2}((x)^{+})^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{ReLU3}}^{\prime\prime}(x)=(x)^{+},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{ELU}}(x)=(e^{x}-1)\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\leq 0\}}+x\mathbbm{1}_{\{x>0\}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{ELU}}^{\prime}(x)=e^{x}\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\leq 0\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{x>0\}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{ELU}}^{\prime\prime}(x)=e^{x}\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\leq 0\}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{Tanh}}(x)=\tanh(x),

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{Tanh}}^{\prime}(x)=\sech^{2}(x),

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{Tanh}}^{\prime\prime}(x)=-2\tanh(x)\sech^{2}(x).

The motivation for testing these activation functions is as follows.
\mathcal{A}_{\text{ReLU}}
has the simplest form and derivatives that simplifies the calculation of
\partial_{\cdot}\widehat{\omega}
and
\partial_{\kappa\kappa}\widehat{\omega}
in Equation (
4.4

4.5

\mathcal{A}_{\text{ReLU2}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\text{ReLU3}}
is motivated by
(

to facilitate discovery of possible shallow networks.
\mathcal{A}_{\text{ELU}}
slightly enhances the smoothness of
\mathcal{A}_{\text{ReLU}}
.
\mathcal{A}_{\text{Tanh}}
is smooth and, in contrast to the other four, bounded.

Noticing the trade-off between the expressivity of the neural network and the increasing complexity of the nested derivative in Equation (
4.4

4.5

,

, and

5
Discussion

This study revisits the representation of option implied information from three complementary perspectives. Section
2
simplified classical option pricing by reassembling familiar variable transformations, leading to an inherent standardization of data. Section
3
reframed implied volatility as a pointwise corrector that morphs the Black-Scholes quasi-density into the market-implied risk-neutral density, thereby establishing an explicit density-volatility parity and clarifying the theoretical basis for arbitrage-free constraints. Section
4
demonstrated that this parity can be operationalized within a neural-network framework and that even shallow feedforward structures are capable of encoding both realistic implied volatility and implied density under arbitrage conditions.

Building a neural representation of option implied information is, however, a delicate task in two respects. First, conventional goodness of fit to implied volatility does not ensure a correct implied density, since the latter remains unobservable in practice. The
\mathcal{N}_{\text{ReLU}}
case exemplifies this modeling risk: excellent price fitting may coexist with an incorrect implied density, potentially leading to mispricing of exotic derivatives. Second, there is no universal rule of thumb for network design. Because of the nonlinearity in the arbitrage constraints and neural derivatives, the effects of depth, width, and activation smoothness vary across architectures. Upscaling networks does not necessarily improve performance, as seen in patterns such as
\mathcal{N}_{\{\text{ELU},\text{Tanh}\}}^{128,3}\prec\mathcal{N}_{\{\text{ELU},\text{Tanh}\}}^{64,3}
and
\mathcal{N}_{\text{ReLU2}}^{\{32,64,128\},3}\prec\mathcal{N}_{\text{ReLU2}}^{\{32,64,128\},2}\prec\mathcal{N}_{\text{ReLU2}}^{\{32,64,128\},1}
. Increasing activation smoothness does not always flatten the effective representation, and higher-order ReLU variants do not outperform the shallower quadratic case. Nonetheless, the performance of
\mathcal{N}_{\text{ReLU2}}^{\{64,128\},1}
confirms that a simple, single-hidden-layer network with appropriate activations can provide an accurate and arbitrage-free representation of option implied information.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that shallow neural networks offer a concise yet expressive basis for modeling option implied information. The proposed representation flexibly captures a potentially wide range of realistic implied-volatility and implied-density surfaces at a given point in time and naturally lends itself to future investigation of their temporal evolution.
