On single-frequency asymptotics for


Abstract
We consider damped driven Maxwell–Bloch equations which are finite-dimensional approximation of the damped driven Maxwell–Schrödinger equations. The equations describe a single-mode Maxwell field coupled to a two-level molecule. Our main result is the construction of solutions with single-frequency asymptotics of the Maxwell field in the case of quasiperiodic pumping. The asymptotics hold for solutions with harmonic initial values which are stationary states of averaged equations in the interaction picture. We calculate all harmonic states and analyse their stability. The calculations rely on the Bloch–Feynman gyroscopic representation of von Neumann equation for the density matrix. The asymptotics follow by application of the averaging theory of the Bogolyubov type. The key role in the application of the averaging theory is played by a special a priori estimate.

Introduction

The Maxwell–Bloch equations (MBE) were introduced by Lamb
[

]

[

,

,
17
,

,

,

,

]
.
The equations
are the Galerkin approximation of the Maxwell–Schrödinger system

[
5
,
16
,
19
,
21
,
22
,
27
,

Our main goal is construction of solutions with single-frequency asymptotics. The asymptotics seem to correspond to
the laser coherent radiation, which
remains a key mystery of laser action since its discovery around 1960.
The damped driven MBE for mixed states read

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\dot{A}(t)&\!\!=&\!\!B(t),\quad\dot{B}(t)=-\Omega^{2}A(t)\!-\!\gamma B(t)+cj(t)\\
\\
i\hbar\dot{\rho}(t)&\!\!=&\!\![H(t),\rho(t)]\end{array}\right|,\qquad j(t)={2}\varkappa\,{\rm Im{\hskip 1.42262pt}}\rho_{21}(t).

(1.1)

Here
A(t),B(t)\in{\mathbb R}
,
\Omega>0
is the resonance frequency,

\gamma>0
is the dissipation coefficient,

c
is the speed of light, and
\hbar
- the Planck constant.
Further,

H(t)
is the Hermitian matrix, and

\rho(t)=\rho
is a
nonnegative Hermitian
2\times 2
-density matrix,

H(t)=\left(\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{cc}\hbar\omega_{1}&ia(t)\\
\\
-ia(t)&\hbar\omega_{2}\end{array}\!\!\!\right);\qquad\rho=\left(\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{cc}\rho_{11}&\overline{\rho}_{21}\\
\\
\rho_{21}&\rho_{22}\end{array}\!\!\!\right),\qquad\mathop{\rm tr\,}\nolimits\rho=1,\quad\rho\geq 0,

(1.2)

where
\hbar\omega_{2}>\hbar\omega_{1}
are the energy levels of active molecules, and
the function
a(t)
is given by

a(t):=\frac{\varkappa}{c}[A(t)+A^{e}(t)],\qquad\varkappa=p\,{{\omega}},\quad{\omega}=\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}>0,

(1.3)

where
p\in{\mathbb R}
is proportional to the molecular dipole moment.
The pumping
A^{e}(t)
we suppose to be a quasiperiodic function:

A^{e}(t)={\rm Re{\hskip 1.42262pt}}[{\bf A}^{e}e^{-i\Omega t}]+{\rm Re{\hskip 1.42262pt}}\sum_{1}^{N}{\bf A}^{e}_{k}e^{-i\Omega_{k}t},\qquad{\rm where}\qquad{\bf A}^{e},{\bf A}^{e}_{k}\in{\mathbb C},\quad\Omega_{k}\in{\mathbb R}\quad{\rm and}\quad\Omega_{k}\neq\Omega.

(1.4)

For solutions to the MBE,
the conservation

{\rm tr{\hskip 1.42262pt}}\rho(t)=\mathop{\rm const}\nolimits

holds
since the trace of commutators vanish. We will consider
solutions with
\mathop{\rm const}\nolimits=1
; see (
1.2
).
We use the Heaviside–Lorentz units and
recall the introduction of the equations in Appendix
B
.

In Appendix
A
, we
prove
the a priori bound for solutions
X(t)=(A(t),B(t),\rho(t))

to (
1.1
) with
|p|/\gamma=r

satisfy the following a priori bounds:

|X(t)|\leq D_{r}(|X(0)|),\qquad t\geq 0,

(1.5)

which is proved in Appendix
A
.
The bound implies
the well-posedness of the MBE.

Our main goal is asymptotics of the Maxwell amplitudes
A(t)
and
B(t)

for solutions
X(t)
to the MBE
as

|p|,\gamma\to 0
.
Define complex Maxwell amplitudes by
M(t)=A(t)+iB(t)/\Omega
.
Then the first two equations of the MBE are equivalent to

\dot{M}(t)=-i\Omega M(t)-i\gamma M_{2}(t)+2icp\omega\,{\rm Im{\hskip 1.42262pt}}\rho_{21}/\Omega,\quad{\rm where}\quad M_{2}(t)={\rm Im{\hskip 1.42262pt}}M(t).

(1.6)

Note that the parameters
|p|,\gamma
are very small for many types of lasers, see Appendix
C
.
For
p=\gamma=0
, all solutions to (
1.6
)
are single-frequency:
M(t)=e^{-i\Omega t}M(0)
.
For small
p
and
\gamma
, equation (
1.6
) implies that

M(t)=e^{-i\Omega t}M(0)+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}R(s)ds,

where
\sup\limits_{s\geq 0}|R(s)|={\cal O}(|p|+\gamma)
by (
1.5
). Hence,
M(t)=e^{-i\Omega t}M(0)+{\cal O}(|p|t)

for
p/\gamma=r
with arbitrary
r\neq 0
.
In particular, for solutions with any fixed initial states
M(0)
,

\max_{t\in[0,|p|^{-1/2}]}|M(t)-e^{-i\Omega t}M(0)|={\cal O}({|p|^{1/2}}),\qquad{p\to 0},\quad{p}/\gamma=r.\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad

(1.7)

Our main results show that in the resonance case, when

\Omega=\omega
,
the time scale
p^{-1/2}
in this asymptotics can be extended
to
|p|^{-1}
and even more in the case of special
harmonic states

of the MBE.

We will represent the density matrix via the Pauli matrices as

\rho(t)=\frac{1}{2}[E+S(t)\cdot\sigma],\qquad{\rm where}\quad S(t)\in{\bf B}=\{S\in{\mathbb R}^{3}:|S|\leq 1\},\quad\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}),

(1.8)

and accordingly, solutions
X(t)
to MBE are represented by

(M(t),S(t))\in{\mathbb X}={\mathbb C}\times{\bf B}
.
The harmonic states are defined via the

interaction picture
of the MBE
and
the corresponding
averaged equation
with the structure

\dot{\bf X}(t)=p{\bf F}_{r}({\bf X}(t)),\qquad t\geq 0;\qquad r=p/\gamma.

(1.9)

We define
the harmonic states
{\bf X}\in{\mathbb X}
of the MBE
as stationary solutions of (
1.9
).

We
calculate all harmonic states
{\bf X}^{r}=({\bf M}^{r},{\bf S}^{r})\in{\mathbb X}
of the MBE with
p/\gamma=r
and show that
the set is a union
Z^{r}=Z^{r}_{1}\cup Z^{r}_{2}
of two smooth 1D manifolds.
We linearise the dynamics (
1.9
) at the harmonic states and
calculate the spectra of the linearisations.
The calculations show that
for
\Omega=\omega
and

c|r|>|{\bf A}^{e}|
, there exist a
nonempty submanifold
Z^{r}_{+}\subset Z^{r}
which is attractive under the averaged dynamics (
1.9
).
Denote the matrix

\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad V_{\Omega}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\Omega&0\\
-\Omega&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right).

(1.10)

Our main results are the following asymptotics
for solutions
X(t)=(M(t),S(t))
to equations (
1.1
) in the representation (
1.8
),
with a
fixed quotient
p/\gamma=r
. Everywhere below we consider the case
p>0

to
p<0

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega=\omega
and the pumping be quasiperiodic. Then for any
r>0
,
the following asymptotics hold.

i) Let the initial state
X(0)=({\bf M},{\bf S})\in Z^{r}
. Then
the
corresponding
solutions
admit the following adiabatic asymptotics:

\max_{t\in[0,p^{-1}]}\Big[|M(t)-e^{-i\Omega t}{\bf M}+|S(t)-e^{V_{\Omega}t}{\bf S}|\Big]={\cal O}(p^{1/2}),\qquad p\to 0.\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad

(1.11)

ii)
Let

cr>|{\bf A}^{e}|
, and
D^{r}_{d}
denote a suitable subset of the
tubular
d
-neighborhood of the stable submanifold
Z^{r}_{+}

with sufficiently small

. Then
for initial states
X(0)\in D^{r}_{d}
,
the corresponding solutions
admit the following asymptotics uniformly
in initial values
X(0)\in D^{r}_{d}

\max_{t\in[0,p^{-1}]}\Big[|M(t)-e^{-i\Omega t}{\bf M}_{*}|+|S(t)-e^{V_{\Omega}t}{\bf S}_{*}(t)|\Big]={\cal O}[{p^{1/2}}+d],\qquad p\to 0,\qquad

(1.12)

where
({\bf M}_{*},{\bf S}_{*}(t))\in Z^{r}_{+}
and

{\bf M}_{*}=-{\bf A}^{e}
does not depend on
r
and on
X(0)\in D^{r}_{d}
.
The Lebesgue measure

|D^{r}_{d}|\sim d^{4},\qquad d\to 0.

(1.13)

Remark 1.2
.

Without
the constraint
p/\gamma=r
, the
a priori bounds (
1.5
) and the asymptotics (
1.11
)–(
1.12
) do not hold.
For example, the bounds must be different for small and big
|p|
for a given dissipation
\gamma>0

The asymptotics
do not hold without this restriction since the limiting amplitudes

depend on

The asymptotics (

reads

S(t)=e^{V_{\Omega}t}{\bf S}+{\cal O}(p^{1/2}),\qquad t\in[0,p^{-1}]

S(t)
about axis
S_{3}

Let us comment on
our approach.
The asymptotics (
1.11
) mean that
the MBE admits solutions

M(t)=e^{-i\Omega t}{\mathbb M}^{r}(t)
,
S(t)=e^{V_{\Omega}t}{\mathbb S}^{r}(t)

with
slowly varying
enveloping amplitudes
{\mathbb M}^{r}(t)\in{\mathbb C}
and
{\mathbb S}^{r}(t)\in{\mathbb R}^{3}

for small
p
and
\gamma
with
p/\gamma=r
.
The amplitudes are solutions to the corresponding dynamical system
which is the interaction picture (or “rotating frame representation”) of the MBE.
The slow variation of the amplitudes for small
p
and
\gamma
is equivalent to the fact that
the initial state
({\mathbb M}(0),{\mathbb S}(0))=(M(0),S(0))

is a harmonic state, i.e.,
a stationary solution of (
1.9
) with
p/\gamma=r
.

The calculation of harmonic states
and of spectra of the linearisations
rely on
the Bloch–Feynman representation
[
7
,
14
]

of the von Neumann equation via the vector

S(t)
from (
1.8
).
We prove that the harmonic states
({\bf M},{\bf S})
with
{\bf M}\neq 0

exist only in the resonance case
\Omega=\omega
,
and only
if
{\bf A}^{e}\neq 0
.
In particular, for the single-frequency pumping

A^{e}(t)={\rm Re{\hskip 1.42262pt}}[{\bf A}_{p}e^{-i\omega_{p}t}]
,
the harmonic states with
{\bf M}\neq 0
exist only in the case
{\bf A}_{p}\neq 0

and triple resonance

\Omega=\omega=\omega_{p}.

(1.15)

The asymptotics (
1.11
) follows for solutions with
every initial state
(M(0),S(0))\in Z^{r}

by the averaging theory of Bogolyubov type
[
30
, Theorem 4.3.6]
.
To prove asymptotics (
1.12
), we combine
the theorem with
the stability of the branch
Z^{r}_{+}
.
Our calculations show that for all harmonic states
({\bf M},{\bf S})\in Z^{r}_{+}
,
the component
{\bf M}=-{\bf A}^{e}
. This is why
the limiting amplitude
{\bf M}_{*}
in (
1.12
) does not depend on

r>0
and on

X(0)\in D^{r}_{d}
. The key role
in the application of the averaging theory
is played by the special a priori estimate (

Let us comment on related results.
The problem of existence of time-periodic solutions

The first results in this direction were obtained recently in

[

of the MBE.
In

For the phenomenological model
[

3
]
,

The solutions are obtained as the result of a bifurcation

In
[

In
[

system (

) in the present paper,

Up to our knowledge,
the single-frequency asymptotics for the MBE with mixed states were

The Bloch-Feynman “gyroscopic” representation

Here we represent von Neumann equation from MBE
as a “gyroscopic equation” (
2.5
)
applying the Bloch, Feynman & al approach
[
6
,
7
,
14
,
15
]
.
Hermitian density matrices
\rho
with
\mathop{\rm tr\,}\nolimits\rho=1

admit the representation

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\left(\!\!\begin{array}[]{cc}1+S_{3}&S_{1}-iS_{2}\\
S_{1}+iS_{2}&1-S_{3}\end{array}\!\!\right),\qquad(S_{1},S_{2},S_{3})\in{\mathbb R}^{3}.

(2.1)

Hence, using the Pauli matrices and the nonnegativity
\det\rho=1-S_{1}^{2}-S_{2}^{2}-S_{3}^{2}\geq 0
, we obtain (
1.8
):

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\left(\!\!\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\!\!\right)+\frac{S_{1}}{2}\left(\!\!\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\!\!\right)+\frac{S_{2}}{2}\left(\!\!\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-i\\
i&0\end{array}\!\!\right)+\frac{S_{3}}{2}\left(\!\!\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\!\!\right)=\frac{1}{2}[E+S\cdot\sigma].

(2.2)

In particular, we can expand the “Hamiltonian operator”
(
1.2
) as

H(t)=\frac{\hbar}{2}(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})E+\frac{\hbar}{2}(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})\sigma_{3}-a(t)\sigma_{2}=\frac{\hbar}{2}(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})E+\frac{\hbar}{2}(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})e_{3}\cdot\sigma-a(t)e_{2}\cdot\sigma,

(2.3)

where
e_{3}=(0,0,1)
and
e_{2}=(0,1,0)
.
Now the von Neumann equation in MBE for
\rho(t)=\frac{1}{2}[E+S(t)\cdot\sigma]
reads as

i\hbar\dot{S}(t)\cdot\sigma=2i\frac{\hbar}{2}(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})[e_{3}\wedge S(t)]\cdot\sigma-a(t)[e_{2}\wedge S(t)]\cdot\sigma=2i\Big[\big(\frac{\hbar}{2}(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})e_{3}-a(t)e_{2}\big)\wedge S(t)\Big]\cdot\sigma,

(2.4)

where we have used the formula
[a\cdot\sigma,b\cdot\sigma]=2i(a\wedge b)\cdot\sigma
for
a,b\in{\mathbb R}^{3}
.
This implies
the “gyroscopic equation” of type
[
14
, (4)]
:

\dot{S}(t)=\theta(t)\wedge S(t),\qquad\theta(t)=-\omega\,e_{3}-2\frac{a(t)}{\hbar}e_{2},\quad\omega=\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}.

(2.5)

The equation can be also written as

\dot{S}(t)=\Theta(t)S(t),\qquad\Theta(t)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\theta_{3}(t)&\theta_{2}(t)\\
\theta_{3}(t)&0&-\theta_{1}(t)\\
-\theta_{2}(t)&\theta_{1}(t)&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\omega&-2a(t)/\hbar\\
-\omega&0&0\\
2a(t)/\hbar&0&0\end{pmatrix}\in so(3),

(2.6)

which corresponds to the cross-product representation
of the Lie algebra
so(3)
.
In particular, the following conservation law holds,

|S(t)|=\mathop{\rm const}\nolimits,\qquad t\geq 0.

(2.7)

The current
j(t)=\varkappa S_{2}(t)
, so
the MBE (
1.1
) reduces to (cf. (
1.6
))

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\dot{M}(t)&\!\!=&\!\!-i\Omega M(t)-i\gamma M_{2}(t)+ic\varkappa S_{2}(t)/\Omega\\
\\
\dot{S}(t)&\!\!=&\!\!{\Theta(t)}S(t)\end{array}\right|,\qquad M(t)=A(t)+iB(t)/\Omega.

(2.8)

Dynamics in the interaction picture

By (
2.6
), for small
p,\gamma>0
,
the system (
2.8
) is a small perturbation of the unperturbed one,

\dot{M}(t)=-i\Omega M(t),\qquad\dot{S}(t)=V_{\omega}S(t),

(3.1)

where
V_{\omega}
is the matrix
(
1.10
) with
\Omega=\omega
which coincides with
(
2.6
) in the case
a(t)=0
.
Solutions to this system are given by

M(t)=e^{-i\Omega t}{\mathbb M},\qquad S(t)=e^{V_{\omega}t}{\mathbb S},\qquad{\rm where}\qquad{\mathbb M}\in{\mathbb C},\quad{\mathbb S}\in{\bf B}.

(3.2)

Our goal is construction of similar solutions
to the perturbed system (
2.8
),

M(t)=e^{-i\Omega t}{\mathbb M}(t),\qquad S(t)=e^{V_{\omega}t}{\mathbb S}(t)

(3.3)

with slowly varying enveloping amplitudes: for a wide interval of time
[0,T(p)]

\sup_{t\in[0,T(p)]}\big[|{\mathbb M}(t)-{\mathbb M}(0)|+|{\mathbb S}(t)-{\mathbb S}(0)|\big]\to 0,\qquad p\to 0,\quad p/\gamma=r\neq 0.

(3.4)

Substituting (
3.3
) into (
2.8
), we obtain the dynamical equations
for the enveloping amplitudes (the “interaction picture”):

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\dot{\mathbb M}(t)&=-ie^{i\Omega t}\big[\gamma{\rm Im{\hskip 1.42262pt}}(e^{-i\Omega t}{\mathbb M}(t))-\tilde{\varkappa}(e^{V_{\omega}t}{\mathbb S}(t))_{2}\big]\\
\\
\dot{\mathbb S}(t)&=e^{-V_{\omega}t}({\Theta(t)}-V_{\omega})e^{V_{\omega}t}{\mathbb S}(t)\end{array}\right|,\qquad\tilde{\varkappa}=\frac{c\varkappa}{\Omega}.

(3.5)

The equations are called as the interaction picture of (
2.8
).
By (
2.5
), (
2.6
), and (
1.3
),

\Theta(t)-V_{\omega}=\frac{2a(t)}{\hbar}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&-1\\
0&0&0\\
1&0&0\end{array}\right),\qquad a(t):=\frac{\varkappa}{c}[A(t)+A^{e}(t)],\qquad\varkappa=p\,{{\omega}}.

(3.6)

Note that
e^{V_{\omega}t}
is he dynamical group of the gyroscopic equation (
2.5
) with
the angular velocity

\theta(t)=-\omega e_{3}
. Hence,
e^{V_{\omega}t}
is the rotation about
e_{3}
with
the angular velocity

\omega
:

e^{V_{\omega}t}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\cos\omega t&\sin\omega t&0\\
-\sin\omega t&\cos\omega t&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right)

(3.7)

Substituting (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) into (
3.5
), we obtain

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\dot{\mathbb M}(t)=&\!\!-pie^{i\Omega t}\!\big[\gamma_{1}({\mathbb M}_{2}(t)\cos\Omega t-{\mathbb M}_{1}(t)\sin\Omega t)-\varkappa_{1}(-{\mathbb S}_{1}(t)\sin\omega t+{\mathbb S}_{2}(t)\cos\omega t)\big],\\
\\
\dot{\mathbb S}(t)=&\!\!-pb[A(t)\!+\!A^{e}(t)]\big[{\mathbb S}_{3}(t)(e_{1}\cos\omega t+e_{2}\sin\omega t)-e_{3}({\mathbb S}_{1}(t)\cos\omega t+{\mathbb S}_{2}(t)\sin\omega t)\big],\end{array}\right.

where

\gamma_{1}=\frac{\gamma}{p}=\frac{1}{r},\qquad\varkappa_{1}=\frac{\tilde{\varkappa}}{p}=\frac{c\,{\omega}}{\Omega},\qquad b=\displaystyle\frac{2\omega}{c\hbar}.

(3.8)

The equations can be written as

\dot{\mathbb M}(t)=p\,f_{r}({\mathbb M}(t),{\mathbb S}(t),t),\qquad\dot{\mathbb S}(t)=p\,g_{r}({\mathbb M}(t),{\mathbb S}(t),t),

(3.9)

where the functions
f
and
g
are given by

\!\!\!\!\left\{\!\!\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{rl}f_{r}({\mathbb M},{\mathbb S},t)\!\!=&\!\!\!\!\!\big[\!-\!i\cos\Omega t\!+\!\sin\Omega t\big]\big[\gamma_{1}({\mathbb M}_{2}\cos\Omega t\!-\!{\mathbb M}_{1}\sin\Omega t)\!-\!\varkappa_{1}(\!-{\mathbb S}_{1}\sin\omega t\!+\!{\mathbb S}_{2}\cos\omega t)\big]\\
\\
g_{r}({\mathbb M},{\mathbb S},t)\!\!=&\!\!\!\!\!-b\big[{\mathbb M}_{1}\cos\Omega t\!+\!{\mathbb M}_{2}\sin\Omega t\!+\!A^{e}(t)\big]\big[{\mathbb S}_{3}(e_{1}\cos\omega t\!+\!e_{2}\sin\omega t)\!-\!e_{3}({\mathbb S}_{1}\cos\omega t\!+\!{\mathbb S}_{2}\sin\omega t)\!\big]\end{array}\!\!\!\!\right|\!.\!\!

(3.10)

.

It is important that

.
Hence,
for any fixed

4
The averaging

The
averaged equations (
3.9
) read

\dot{\bf M}(t)=p\,\overline{f}_{r}({\bf M}(t),{\bf S}(t)),\qquad\dot{\bf S}(t)=p\,\overline{g}_{r}({\bf M}(t),{\bf S}(t)),

Appendix C
On possible treatment of the laser action

Here we discuss possible treatment of the laser action
relying on the obtained results.

On the smallness of the parameters.

Note that the dipole moment
p
and dissipation coefficient
\gamma
are very small
for many types of lasers. In particular,
the dissipation coefficient for the Ruby laser is the electrical conduction of corundum
which is

\gamma\sim 10^{-14}
in the Heaviside–Lorentz
units

[
13
,
33
,
34
]
.
For the dipole moment typically

|p|\sim 10^{-18}
according to

[
26
]

that agrees with the classical dipole moment
ed/2
, where

d\sim 10^{-8}
cm is the molecular diameter, and

e\sim 10^{-10}
is the elementary
charge in the same units.

On the laser threshold.

The asymptotics (
1.11
) hold for solutions
to (
1.1
)
with harmonic initial states
X(0)\in Z^{r}
, and the Lebesgue measure
|Z^{r}|=0
. On the other hand, (
1.12
) hold for solutions with initial states from an open
domain of attraction. Hence,
by (
1.13
),
the asymptotics (
1.12
) appear with a “nonzero probability” in contrast to
(
1.11
). This fact, provisionally, clarifies the existence of a

laser threshold

to ignite the laser action: the intensity of random pumping must be sufficiently large to bring the solution to
the domain
of attraction, and then
the solution is captured in the domain with the single-frequency asymptotics.

On the laser amplification.

The equations (
1.1
) describe one molecule coupled to
the Maxwell field.
The limiting amplitudes of the Maxwell field in the asymptotics
(
1.11
), (
1.12
)
do not depend on non-resonance harmonics in the pumping (
1.4
)
with the frequencies
\Omega_{k}\neq\omega
.
This means that the dynamics (
1.1
) acts as a filter, selecting only the resonant harmonics, that itself cannot explain the
amplification of the Maxwell field in laser devices.
The amplification could be explained by a large number of active molecules, typically
N\sim 10^{20}
,
under the traditional assumption
that the molecules
interact with the Maxwell field but do not interact with each other
[
28
]
.
In this case, the amplitude of the total Maxwell field is multiplied by
\sqrt{N}\sim 10^{10}
by the Law of Large Numbers.

This conclusion essentially depends on the fact that
the phases of the amplitudes
{\bf M}^{r}
and
{\bf M}_{*}

in asymptotics (
1.11
)–(
1.12
)
for all molecules are approximately uniformly distributed.
The uniformity takes place
if it holds for phases of
{\bf A}^{e}
.
This follows from the asymptotics and
Remark
5.2
since
e^{i\phi}e^{-i\Omega t}{\bf M}
differs from
e^{-i\Omega t}{\bf M}

by a shift of time.

Self-induced transparency.
The harmonic states (
5.6

{\bf A}^{e}e^{-i\Omega t}
