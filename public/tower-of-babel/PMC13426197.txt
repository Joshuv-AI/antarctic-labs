A diagnostic pitfall on cervical ultrasound: Lipoplastic lymphadenopathy mimicking malignancy
Authors: Ibrahim A. Bakari, Isaya Munisi, Gustave Buname, Mwajabu Saleh, Lulu Sakafu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lipoplastic lymphadenopathy is an uncommon benign condition characterized by fatty replacement of lymphoid tissue with reactive hyperplasia, which can mimic malignant or granulomatous disease on imaging. We report a 25-year-old female presenting with a gradually enlarging posterior cervical swelling and low-grade fever. Ultrasound demonstrated multiple enlarged cervical lymph nodes with loss of the normal fatty hilum and heterogeneous features suggestive of malignancy, prompting excisional biopsy. Histopathological analysis revealed reactive follicles with fat metaplasia without evidence of malignancy, establishing the diagnosis of lipoplastic lymphadenopathy. This case highlights the limitations of imaging in differentiating benign from malignant lymphadenopathy and emphasizes the indispensable role of histopathology in achieving a definitive diagnosis. Awareness of benign nodal mimics is essential to avoid misdiagnosis, unnecessary interventions, and inappropriate management.

Introduction

Lipoplastic lymphadenopathy is a rare benign condition characterized by fatty replacement of lymphoid tissue with associated reactive changes. It has been reported in various anatomical regions, including intra-abdominal, mesenteric, pelvic, and generalized nodal distributions, and may mimic malignancy clinically and radiologically. Due to its rarity and unfamiliarity, it often presents a diagnostic challenge and may lead to unnecessary invasive procedures.

Cervical lymphadenopathy is a common clinical and radiologic finding with a broad differential diagnosis, including reactive, infectious, granulomatous, and malignant causes. Ultrasonography is typically the first line imaging modality, with features such as nodal enlargement, round shape, cortical thickening, and loss of the fatty hilum considered suspicious for malignancy. However, significant overlap exists between benign and malignant appearances, limiting diagnostic specificity.

We report a case of cervical lipoplastic lymphadenopathy in a young adult female with highly suspicious ultrasound features, highlighting the limitations of imaging and the essential role of histopathological confirmation in establishing the diagnosis.

Case presentation

A 25-year-old female presented to JR Hospital in Mwanza, Tanzania, in January 2026 with a history of low-grade fever for 1 month and a gradually increasing in size solitary swelling in the right posterior neck region for 3 months. She also reported an itchy sensation and ear fullness for over 10 months, accompanied by headaches and joint pains. In addition, she complained of gastrointestinal discomfort, including gaseous abdominal distension and heartburn, as well as tensional chest pain over the sternum. She further noted heat intolerance and loss of appetite.

The patient denied cough, palpitations, weight loss, night sweats, vomiting, diarrhea, dysphagia, and voice changes. Her menstrual cycles were regular. There was no history of diabetes, hypertension, thyroid disease, chronic illness, or familial malignancies, including cancer or thyroid disorders.

On physical examination, the patient was clinically stable. Vital signs were within normal limits, with a temperature of 36.9°C, blood pressure of 124/62 mmHg, pulse rate of 79 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, oxygen saturation of 98% on room air, and a body mass index of 20.3 kg/m². General examination revealed no signs of acute distress. Head, eyes, ears, nose, and throat examination showed bilateral cerumen impaction with debris, while the oral cavity and pharynx were normal. Neck examination revealed an enlarged lymph node in the right posterior triangle, with no supraclavicular or axillary lymphadenopathy. Systemic examination of the respiratory and abdominal systems was unremarkable.

High-resolution neck ultrasound demonstrated multiple enlarged cervical lymph nodes in Levels I, II, and IV, with loss of the normal fatty hilum. The largest lymph node measured up to 1.8 cm in diameter. A small cystic lesion was noted in the right submandibular gland, while the thyroid gland and major vessels appeared normal. These findings were suggestive of malignancy, with chronic granulomatous disease considered in the differential diagnosis. Excision biopsy and further imaging were therefore recommended.

Histopathological examination of an excised cervical lymph node measuring 1.2 × 1 × 0.5 cm revealed reactive follicles with germinal centers and areas of fat metaplasia, with no evidence of granuloma formation or malignant features. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of lipoplastic (lipomatous) lymphadenopathy was established.

The primary diagnosis was lipoplastic lymphadenopathy of the cervical lymph nodes. In view of the benign histological findings, further management focused on regular follow up, monitoring, and patient reassurance.

Discussion

Lipoplastic lymphadenopathy is a rare benign lymph node disorder characterized by partial or complete replacement of normal lymphoid tissue by mature adipose tissue, frequently accompanied by reactive follicular hyperplasia. Since its initial description in the 1970s, only a limited number of cases have been documented in the literature, and the true incidence remains unknown. Published reports have described involvement of cervical, pelvic, retroperitoneal, ovarian, pulmonary, and generalized lymph node groups, highlighting the rarity and diverse anatomical distribution of the condition [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. The scarcity of contemporary publications reflects the uncommon nature of this entity, with much of the available evidence derived from isolated case reports and small case series [[1], [2], [3], [4],[6], [7], [8]].

The exact pathogenesis of lipoplastic lymphadenopathy remains uncertain. Several mechanisms have been proposed, including age-related fatty involution of lymphoid tissue, chronic inflammatory stimulation, persistent immune activation, obesity, endocrine disorders, and previous inflammatory insults [1,2,9,7,8]. Histologically, prolonged stromal remodeling is believed to promote gradual replacement of lymphoid tissue by mature adipocytes while preserving nodal architecture [1,2,9]. Most reported cases have involved middle-aged and elderly individuals, supporting a role for involutional changes. However, occurrence in younger patients, such as in the present case, suggests that chronic antigenic stimulation or subclinical inflammatory processes may also contribute significantly to its development [1,7,8].

Clinically, lipoplastic lymphadenopathy most commonly presents as persistent lymph node enlargement without specific constitutional manifestations [[1], [2], [3],[6], [7], [8]]. The presenting symptoms observed in our patient were nonspecific and, in retrospect, could not be directly attributed to lipoplastic lymphadenopathy. Nevertheless, these symptoms were clinically relevant because they prompted further evaluation and imaging studies, ultimately leading to tissue diagnosis. Radiological assessment of lipoplastic lymphadenopathy remains challenging because no imaging features are pathognomonic. Ultrasonography in the present case demonstrated enlarged lymph nodes with loss of the fatty hilum and heterogeneous echotexture, findings commonly associated with malignant lymphadenopathy [[10], [11], [12]]. Similar sonographic appearances have been reported in both benign and malignant lymph node disorders, limiting their specificity [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. Reactive lymph nodes may also exhibit loss of the fatty hilum and altered vascular patterns, resulting in considerable overlap with malignant disease [[14], [15], [16]].

Although ultrasonography was the primary imaging modality used in our patient, other imaging modalities have been described in the literature. Computed tomography may demonstrate enlarged low-attenuation or fat-containing lymph nodes resulting from fatty replacement of nodal tissue [1,7,8]. These appearances can mimic lymphoma, metastatic disease, cavitating tuberculous lymphadenopathy, fungal infections, or other infiltrative disorders [1,2,7,8]. Magnetic resonance imaging may similarly demonstrate signal characteristics consistent with intranodal fat; however, published MRI descriptions remain scarce because of the rarity of the condition. Historical lymphographic studies have also reported appearances that simulated malignant lymphadenopathy [3]. Consequently, despite advances in imaging, no radiological modality has demonstrated sufficient specificity to reliably distinguish lipoplastic lymphadenopathy from malignant lymphadenopathy [10,12,18,19].

An important observation in the present case was the relatively small size of the affected lymph nodes, measuring less than 2 cm. Several previously reported cases have described substantially larger lymph nodes, often exceeding 2-3 cm [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. This discrepancy suggests that nodal size alone is an unreliable discriminator between benign and malignant processes. The substantial overlap in nodal size among reactive, lipoplastic, and malignant lymphadenopathies reinforces the need for comprehensive clinical, radiological, and pathological correlation rather than reliance on size criteria alone [12,18,16].

Histopathological examination remains the gold standard for diagnosis. Characteristic findings include preservation of overall nodal architecture, replacement of lymphoid tissue by mature adipose tissue, reactive follicular hyperplasia, and absence of cytological atypia or malignant infiltration [1,2,4,9,7,8]. In the present case, these features established the diagnosis and excluded lymphoma and metastatic disease. This histopathological-radiological discordance highlights one of the principal diagnostic challenges of lipoplastic lymphadenopathy and underscores the necessity of tissue diagnosis whenever imaging findings remain suspicious or inconclusive.

Management of lipoplastic lymphadenopathy is generally conservative following histopathological confirmation. No disease-specific treatment has been described because the condition is benign and non-neoplastic [1,2,7,8]. In most reported cases, lymph node excision or biopsy has been performed primarily for diagnostic purposes rather than therapeutic intervention [[1], [2], [3],[6], [7], [8]]. Published reports indicate a favorable prognosis, with no documented evidence of malignant transformation. Therefore, routine clinical follow-up is generally sufficient unless new symptoms emerge or progressive lymph node enlargement occurs.

This case contributes to the limited body of literature describing cervical lipoplastic lymphadenopathy in a young adult. It highlights the potential for benign lipoplastic lymphadenopathy to mimic malignancy on imaging and demonstrates the limitations of radiological assessment alone. Awareness of this rare entity may help clinicians, radiologists, and pathologists avoid unnecessary aggressive interventions and emphasize the importance of histopathological confirmation in atypical lymphadenopathy.

Conclusion

Lipoplastic lymphadenopathy is a rare benign lymph node disorder that may closely mimic malignant lymphadenopathy clinically and radiologically, posing a significant diagnostic challenge. This case highlights that suspicious imaging findings, including loss of the fatty hilum and heterogeneous nodal appearance, are not always indicative of malignancy and that lymph node size alone has limited diagnostic value. Histopathological examination remains the gold standard for diagnosis and is essential for excluding malignant disease and preventing unnecessary interventions. Given the rarity of reported cases and the limited contemporary literature, further case reports and clinicopathological studies are needed to enhance understanding of its pathogenesis, imaging characteristics, optimal diagnostic approach, and long-term outcomes.

Learning points
