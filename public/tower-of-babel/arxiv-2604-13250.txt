Continuation of Hamiltonian dynamics from the plane to constant-curvature surfaces
Cristina Stoica

Abstract
We investigate the deformation of symmetry on cotangent bundles from the Euclidean plane to two-dimensional constant-curvature surfaces and the continuation of local dynamics aspects in Hamiltonian systems. For a fixed curvature sign σ ∈ { + 1 , − 1 } \sigma\in\{+1,-1\} , the curved problem is set up either on the sphere ( σ = + 1 ) (\sigma=+1) or on the hyperbolic plane ( σ = − 1 ) (\sigma=-1) , both with radius R = 1 / ε R=1/\varepsilon , recovering flat space in the limit ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 . The symmetry of these spaces is taken into account by using the Inönü–Wigner contraction of Lie algebras from 𝔰 ​ 𝔬 ​ ( 3 ) \mathfrak{so}(3) or 𝔰 ​ 𝔬 ​ ( 2 , 1 ) \mathfrak{so}(2,1) to 𝔰 ​ 𝔢 ​ ( 2 ) \mathfrak{se}(2) . We use Riemannian exponential coordinates centred at the North pole together with the pull-back the associated momentum map and the symplectic form. Within this geometric setting we use a local slice construction and prove the persistence from flat to curved spaces of non-degenerate relative equilibria and relative periodic orbits of general cotangent bundle Hamiltonian systems. We apply the resulting framework to the Newtonian n n -body problem.



Keywords
: symmetry, Lie algebra Inönü–Wigner contraction, Hamiltonian dynamics, curved
n
-body problems, continuation of local dynamics

1
Introduction

A natural question in mathematical physics is whether known solutions of a
dynamical system persist when the underlying geometry is deformed.
In the case of the
Newtonian
n
-body problem, such a deformation replaces the Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}

with a constant-curvature surface
M_{R}^{\sigma}
of radius
R=1/\varepsilon
, where
\sigma=+1

corresponds to the sphere and
\sigma=-1
to the hyperbolic plane, recovering the flat space in the limit

\varepsilon\to 0
.
The curved Newtonian
n
-body problem has attracted sustained attention over the past two decades; see for instance
[
Chernoivan & al. 1999
,
Diacu & al. 2012a
,
Diacu & al. 2012b
,
Diacu 2012c
,
Martínez & al. 2017
,
Diacu et al. 2018
,
Tibboel 2018
,
Pérez-Chavela & al. 2020
,
Hernández-Garduño & al. 2022
,
Bengochea & al. 2022
,
Zhu 2024
,
Begonchea & al. 2025
]
, and the survey
[
Diacu 2013
]
for background and motivation.

The central results of this paper are local continuation theorems. We prove that relative
equilibria (RE), periodic solutions and relative periodic solutions (RPOs) of Hamiltonian systems defined on the Euclidean plane persist to the corresponding curved problem on
M^{\sigma}_{1/\varepsilon}
for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small provided standard nondegeneracy conditions hold. As a special case, we apply our results to the Newtonian
n
-body problem. Thus, the classical three-body Lagrange and Euler RE, the (non-degenerate) regular
n
-gon RE, as well as the figure-eight choreography
[
Chenciner and Montgomery 2000
]
, all satisfy the required hypotheses and continue to the corresponding constant-curvature surface.

Our approach rests on three ingredients that together reduce the problem to a perturbative argument on a fixed symplectic manifold.

The first is the use of Riemannian exponential coordinates centred at the North pole of
M^{\sigma}_{1/\varepsilon}
. In these coordinates the curved surface is described by a single chart covering all but the antipodal point in the spherical case and globally in the hyperbolic case. After pull-back by the exponential chart the metric and momentum map admit explicit
\varepsilon
-expansions, whereas the canonical symplectic form is
\varepsilon
-independent. The
n
-body curved Hamiltonian expands as
H_{\varepsilon,\sigma}=H_{0}+\sigma\varepsilon^{2}H_{2}+O(\varepsilon^{4})
, where
H_{0}
is the standard planar Newtonian Hamiltonian and
H_{2}
is the explicit first curvature correction.

The second ingredient is an Lie algebra Inönü–Wigner contraction
[
Inönü & al. 1953
]
to
\mathfrak{se}(2)
, a procedure employed as well in
[
Montaldi & al. 2013
]
in the case of point vortices. By introducing an
\varepsilon
-dependent Lie bracket on a fixed three-dimensional vector space that interpolates between
\mathfrak{so}(3)
or
\mathfrak{so}(2,1)
at
\varepsilon>0
and
\mathfrak{se}(2)
at
\varepsilon=0
. Note that for
\sigma=+1
the symmetry group of the curved problem is
SO(3)
, while for
\sigma=-1
it is
SO^{+}(2,1)
. Thus, by employing the deformations of Lie algebra, the symmetry and associated momentum map on the curved surfaces vary smoothly with
\varepsilon
. Furthermore, rotations or boosts about horizontal axes converge to planar translations as the curvature vanishes. and the momentum map converges to the standard
SE(2)
momentum map.

The third ingredient is a local slice construction that, given any compact collision-free set in the chart phase space, produces smoothly varying slices representing locally the symplectic reduced spaces. On these slices, the reduced symplectic forms, reduced Hamiltonians, reduced Hamiltonian vector fields, and reduced Poincaré maps all depend smoothly on
\varepsilon
. Consequently, the continuation results follow from standard implicit function theorem arguments applied to a Hamiltonian context; we refer to
[
Marsden 1992
]
,
[
Holm & al. 2009
]
and
[
Meyer & al. 2009
]
for more background on Hamiltonian systems’ symmetry, reduction, and continuation methods.

The technique of Lie algebra deformations was also employed by
[
Montaldi & al. 2013
]
in the case of point vortex dynamics on hyperbolic, Euclidean and spherical surfaces. The authors provide a unified picture of the symmetry via smooth deformations
\mathfrak{sl}(2)\to\mathfrak{se}(2)\to\mathfrak{so}(3)
. They use stereographic projection to provide a uniform coordinate system for constant-curvature surfaces, with the symplectic form encoded via the conformal factor. Note that the phase space of
n
vortices on a surface
M
is the product
M^{n}
, while in our case we consider cotangent bundle phase spaces
T^{*}(M^{n})
, as is appropriate for
n
-body problems. The present paper carries this constant-curvature viewpoint over to cotangent-bundle Hamiltonian systems and, in particular, to the Newtonian
n
-body problem. Finally, note that when
[
Montaldi & al. 2013
]
consider the continuation arguments for RE, they restrict to the rotational symmetry
SO(2)
, while we study the general context of the full symmetry groups.

Our paper generalises the work of
[
Bengochea & al. 2022
]
. There the authors prove the continuation of the Newtonian
n
-body problem considering the rotational
SO(2)
symmetry only. They use stereographic projection to provide a uniform coordinate system and, by employing a slice theorem, they prove the continuation of central configurations (as base point configurations of RE). In their setting, the momentum map and the local slice model are independent of curvature and the persistence argument is applied to the RE condition only. In contrast, we consider the full symmetry groups, i.e.
SE(2)
on the plane and either
SO(3)
or
SO^{+}(2,1)
on the curved surface, in the framework of Lie algebra deformations. After introducing the exponential (Riemannian normal) coordinates, we pull back the geometry and thereby deal with the
\varepsilon
-dependence of the symplectic form, momentum maps and the local slice model. We provide a general theorem applicable to any smooth family of
G_{\varepsilon}
-invariant cotangent-bundle Hamiltonians of the form
H_{\varepsilon}=H_{0}+{\cal O}(\varepsilon^{2})
, with the Newtonian
n
-body problem being treated as a case study.

While developing the applications we noticed a less known fact: in the planar
n
-body problem, if the full symmetry group
SE(2)
is considered then an RE with non-zero rotational angular velocity must necessarily have zero translational momentum. So, for example, in the
SE(2)
-symmetric context, the Lagrangian triangular RE is not a RE unless its centre of mass is at rest in the origin of the chosen system of coordinates; otherwise, this “RE” is in fact an RPO.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we develop the geometric
framework: exponential coordinates, the Lie algebra Inönü–Wigner contractions, group actions, the
momentum map, and the symplectic form. Section
3
contains the main
results: the local reduction theory and the continuation theorems. In Section
4
we present the explicit
Hamiltonian expansion for the
n
-body problem and apply our results to specific configurations.

2
Geometry, Scaling and Symmetry

2.1
Exponential coordinates at the North pole

Fix a curvature sign
\sigma\in\{+1,-1\}
and, for
\varepsilon>0
let
R=1/\varepsilon
. We consider the constant-curvature surface

M_{R}^{\sigma}=\begin{cases}S_{R}^{2}=\left\{{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,\Big|\,\,\|{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}\|=R\right\},&\sigma=+1,\\[2.84526pt]
\,\\
H_{R}^{2}=\left\{{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}=(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,\Big|\,\,(X^{\varepsilon})^{2}+(Y^{\varepsilon})^{2}-(Z^{\varepsilon})^{2}=-R^{2},\ Z^{\varepsilon}>0\right\},&\sigma=-1.\end{cases}

(1)

with North pole
\mathbf{N}=(0,0,R)
. We write

\langle{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\rangle_{\sigma}:=x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}+\sigma x_{3}y_{3},

(2)

so that
M_{R}^{\sigma}=\{{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\mid\langle{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{q}}\rangle_{\sigma}=\sigma R^{2}\}
, together with
Z>0
when
\sigma=-1
. The induced metric on
M_{R}^{\sigma}
is Riemannian in both cases. We identify
T_{\mathbf{N}}M_{R}^{\sigma}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
via
(u,v,0)\leftrightarrow(u,v)
.
For later use we define the curvature-dependent trigonometric functions

C_{\sigma}(x):=\begin{cases}\cos x,&\sigma=+1,\\
\cosh x,&\sigma=-1,\end{cases}\hskip 18.49988pt\,\,\,\,S_{\sigma}(x):=\begin{cases}\sin x,&\sigma=+1,\\
\sinh x,&\sigma=-1.\end{cases}

(3)

Let
{\mathbf{w}}=(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
\rho=\|{\mathbf{w}}\|=\sqrt{u^{2}+v^{2}}
, and for
\rho\neq 0
let
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}=(u/\rho,v/\rho,0)
. The unit-speed geodesic starting at
\mathbf{N}
with initial direction
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
is

\gamma(t)=R\,C_{\sigma}\!\left(\frac{t}{R}\right)\,\frac{\mathbf{N}}{R}+R\,S_{\sigma}\!\left(\frac{t}{R}\right)\,\widehat{\mathbf{w}}\,=\,\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\,R\,S_{\sigma}\!\left(\frac{t}{R}\right),\frac{v}{\rho}\,R\,S_{\sigma}\!\left(\frac{t}{R}\right),R\,C_{\sigma}\!\left(\frac{t}{R}\right)\right).

(4)

We further define

s_{\sigma}(\rho)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{S_{\sigma}(\varepsilon\rho)}{\varepsilon\rho},&\rho\neq 0,\\[5.69054pt]
1,&\rho=0,\end{cases}\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994ptc_{\sigma}(\rho)=C_{\sigma}(\varepsilon\rho).

(5)

Introducing the cut locus of the North pole
\mathbf{N}

\mathrm{Cut}(\mathbf{N}):=\begin{cases}\text{South pole},&\,\,\,\,\sigma=+1,\\
\text{the void set},&\,\,\,\,\sigma=-1,\end{cases}

the Riemannian exponential map reads

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}\,:

\displaystyle\,\,\,\mathbb{R}^{2}\,\,\,\,\longrightarrow M_{R}^{\sigma}\setminus\mathrm{Cut}(\mathbf{N}),

\displaystyle(u,v)\longrightarrow\,\,\,\,\,\,{\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}(u,v)=(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon}):=\left(u\,s_{\sigma}(\rho),\ v\,s_{\sigma}(\rho),\,\frac{c_{\sigma}(\rho)}{\varepsilon}\right)=:{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}

(6)

with
{\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}(0,0)=\mathbf{N}
. In polar coordinates
(\rho,\varphi)
we have

\displaystyle(\rho,\varphi)\longrightarrow\,\,\,\,\,\,{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}=(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\left(\rho s_{\sigma}(\rho)\cos\varphi,\ \,\rho s_{\sigma}(\rho)\,\sin\varphi,\,\frac{C_{\sigma}(\varepsilon\rho)}{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\left(\dfrac{\sin(\varepsilon\rho)}{\varepsilon}\,\cos\varphi,\ \,\dfrac{\sin(\varepsilon\rho)}{\varepsilon}\,\sin\varphi,\,\dfrac{\cos(\varepsilon\rho)}{\varepsilon}\right),&\sigma=+1,\\[5.69054pt]
\left(\dfrac{\sinh(\varepsilon\rho)}{\varepsilon}\,\cos\varphi,\ \,\dfrac{\sinh(\varepsilon\rho)}{\varepsilon}\,\sin\varphi,\,\dfrac{\cosh(\varepsilon\rho)}{\varepsilon}\right),&\sigma=-1.\end{cases}

(7)

Remark 1
.

In the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
we have

{\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}(u,v)\longrightarrow(u,\,v,\,+\infty)
in the ambient picture. Thus the horizontal coordinates
(X,Y)
reduce to
(u,v)
and the curved surface recedes to a plane.
In particular,
(u,v)
become standard Cartesian coordinates on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and the exponential map approaches the identity.

Remark 2

(Injectivity radius)
.

For
\sigma=+1
, the Riemannian exponential map
{\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}
is a diffeomorphism from
B_{\pi/\varepsilon}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
onto
S^{2}_{1/\varepsilon}\setminus\{\text{South pole}\}
; the injectivity radius is
\pi/\varepsilon
. For
\sigma=-1
, the exponential map is a global diffeomorphism
\mathbb{R}^{2}\to H^{2}_{1/\varepsilon}
. For any compact collision-free set
K\subset T^{*}U^{n}
with
\sup_{z\in K}\max_{i}\|q^{0}_{i}\|\leq\rho_{\max}<\infty
, choosing
\varepsilon
sufficiently small ensures that all configurations in
K
lie inside the spherical injectivity ball when
\sigma=+1
; for
\sigma=-1
no such cut-locus restriction is needed.

For later use we calculate the pull-back of the metric on
M_{R}^{\sigma}
under
{\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}
. First, we calculate

\frac{\partial(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon})}{\partial(u,v)}=s_{\sigma}(\rho)\,\mathbb{I}+\frac{s_{\sigma}^{\prime}(\rho)}{\rho}\,{\mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{w}}^{T},

(8)

where
s_{\sigma}^{\prime}(\rho)={\mathrm{d}}s_{\sigma}/{\mathrm{d}}\rho
. Passing to polar coordinates, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon})}{\partial\rho}=\left(C_{\sigma}(\varepsilon\rho)\cos\varphi\,,C_{\sigma}(\varepsilon\rho)\sin\varphi\,,-\sigma S_{\sigma}(\varepsilon\rho)\right)

(9)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon})}{\partial\varphi}=\left(-\frac{S_{\sigma}(\varepsilon\rho)}{\varepsilon}\sin\varphi\,,\frac{S_{\sigma}(\varepsilon\rho)}{\varepsilon}\cos\varphi\,,0\right)\,.

(10)

These two vectors are orthogonal with respect to the induced metric, with squared norms

\left\|\frac{\partial(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon})}{\partial\rho}\right\|^{2}=1,\hskip 18.49988pt\left\|\frac{\partial(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon})}{\partial\varphi}\right\|^{2}=\left(\frac{S_{\sigma}(\varepsilon\rho)}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}.

Thus in polar coordinates the pulled-back metric is

{\mathrm{d}}s^{2}={\mathrm{d}}\rho^{2}+\left(\frac{S_{\sigma}(\varepsilon\rho)}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\varphi^{2}\,.

(11)

Denoting

A_{\varepsilon,\sigma}(\rho):=s_{\sigma}(\rho)^{2},\hskip 18.49988ptP({\mathbf{w}}):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{w}}^{T}}{\rho^{2}},&\rho\neq 0,\\[3.09999pt]
\displaystyle 0,&\rho=0,\end{cases}

(12)

where
P({\mathbf{w}})
is the orthogonal projection onto the radial direction spanned by
{\mathbf{w}}
, the pulled-back metric is represented in the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

G_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{w}})=P({\mathbf{w}})+A_{\varepsilon,\sigma}(\rho)\,\left(\mathbb{I}-P({\mathbf{w}})\right)=A_{\varepsilon,\sigma}(\rho)\,\mathbb{I}+\left(1-A_{\varepsilon,\sigma}(\rho)\right)\,P({\mathbf{w}}).

(13)

Hence

G_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{w}})^{-1}=P({\mathbf{w}})+\frac{1}{A_{\varepsilon,\sigma}(\rho)}\,\left(\mathbb{I}-P({\mathbf{w}})\right).

(14)

Small-curvature expansion.

On compact subsets of
U
, one has

A_{\varepsilon,\sigma}(\rho)=1-\sigma\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\rho^{2}}{3}+O(\varepsilon^{4}),\hskip 18.49988pt\frac{1}{A_{\varepsilon,\sigma}(\rho)}=1+\sigma\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\rho^{2}}{3}+O(\varepsilon^{4}),

(15)

and therefore

G_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{w}})^{-1}=\mathbb{I}+\sigma\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\rho^{2}}{3}(\mathbb{I}-P({\mathbf{w}}))+O(\varepsilon^{4}).

(16)

In particular,

G_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{w}})^{-1}=\mathbb{I}+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

(17)

We also define the inverse map of the exponential chart. For
{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}=(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon})\in M_{R}^{\sigma}\setminus\mathrm{Cut}(\mathbf{N})
define

\psi=\begin{cases}\arccos\!\left(\varepsilon Z^{\varepsilon}\right)\in[0,\pi),&\sigma=+1,\\
\operatorname{arccosh}\!\left(\varepsilon Z^{\varepsilon}\right)\in[0,\infty),&\sigma=-1.\end{cases}

(18)

Then the geodesic distance from
N
to
{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}
is
d(N,{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon})=R\psi=\psi/\varepsilon
, while

\|(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon})\|=\frac{S_{\sigma}(\psi)}{\varepsilon}.

Therefore the inverse is

{\mathrm{Log}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon})=\frac{\psi}{S_{\sigma}(\psi)}\,(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon})\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

(19)

with the convention
\psi/S_{\sigma}(\psi)=1
at
\psi=0
. Notice that

\|{\mathrm{Log}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon})\|=\psi/\varepsilon
and that

{\mathrm{Log}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon})\to(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

2.2
Inönü–Wigner scaling

Let
\{{\mathbf{b}}_{1},\ {\mathbf{b}}_{2},\ {\mathbf{b}}_{3}\}
be a fixed basis of a three-dimensional real vector space

\mathfrak{g}\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
For each
\varepsilon\geq 0
we define a Lie bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\varepsilon}

on
\mathfrak{g}
by

\displaystyle[{\mathbf{b}}_{1},{\mathbf{b}}_{2}]_{\varepsilon}=\sigma\varepsilon^{2}\,{\mathbf{b}}_{3}\,,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,[{\mathbf{b}}_{2},{\mathbf{b}}_{3}]_{\varepsilon}={\mathbf{b}}_{1}\,,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,[{\mathbf{b}}_{3},{\mathbf{b}}_{1}]_{\varepsilon}={\mathbf{b}}_{2}.

(20)

As before, skew-symmetry determines the remaining brackets, and one checks directly that the Jacobi identity holds for all
\varepsilon\geq 0
.

Lemma 1
.

For each
\varepsilon>0
fixed, the Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\varepsilon})
is
isomorphic to
\mathfrak{so}(3)
when
\sigma=+1
and to
\mathfrak{so}(2,1)
when
\sigma=-1
.
For
\varepsilon=0
, the Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\cdot,\cdot]_{0})
is isomorphic
to
\mathfrak{se}(2)
.

Proof.

For
\varepsilon>0
let

\mathbf{f}_{1}:=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}{\mathbf{b}}_{1},\hskip 18.49988pt\mathbf{f}_{2}:=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}{\mathbf{b}}_{2},\hskip 18.49988pt\mathbf{f}_{3}:={\mathbf{b}}_{3}.

(21)

Then

[\mathbf{f}_{1},\mathbf{f}_{2}]_{\varepsilon}=\sigma\mathbf{f}_{3},\hskip 18.49988pt[\mathbf{f}_{2},\mathbf{f}_{3}]_{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{f}_{1},\hskip 18.49988pt[\mathbf{f}_{3},\mathbf{f}_{1}]_{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{f}_{2}.

For
\sigma=+1
these are the standard commutation relations of
\mathfrak{so}(3)
, while for
\sigma=-1
they are those of
\mathfrak{so}(2,1)
. For
\varepsilon=0
one has

[{\mathbf{b}}_{1},{\mathbf{b}}_{2}]_{0}=0,\hskip 18.49988pt[{\mathbf{b}}_{2},{\mathbf{b}}_{3}]_{0}={\mathbf{b}}_{1},\hskip 18.49988pt[{\mathbf{b}}_{3},{\mathbf{b}}_{1}]_{0}={\mathbf{b}}_{2},

which are the standard
\mathfrak{se}(2)
relations.
∎

Remark 3
.

The family
\{\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\geq 0}:=\{(\mathfrak{g},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\varepsilon})\}_{\varepsilon\geq 0}

is an Inönü–Wigner contraction of either
\mathfrak{so}(3)
or
\mathfrak{so}(2,1)
to

\mathfrak{se}(2)
. In both cases the normalized commutator

[{\mathbf{b}}_{1},{\mathbf{b}}_{2}]_{\varepsilon}
tends to zero while
[{\mathbf{b}}_{3},{\mathbf{b}}_{1}]_{\varepsilon}
and

[{\mathbf{b}}_{3},{\mathbf{b}}_{2}]_{\varepsilon}
remain independent of
\varepsilon
.

We now choose ambient generators so that
{\mathbf{b}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{b}}_{2}
become translations in the exponential chart as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Let
\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\sigma},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\sigma},\mathbf{E}_{3}
be generators in the Lie algebra of the isometry group of
M_{R}^{\sigma}
satisfying

[\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\sigma},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\sigma}]=\sigma\mathbf{E}_{3},\hskip 18.49988pt[\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\sigma},\mathbf{E}_{3}]=\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\sigma},\hskip 18.49988pt[\mathbf{E}_{3},\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\sigma}]=\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\sigma},

and acting on ambient coordinates by

\displaystyle\exp(\varepsilon a\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\sigma})\cdot(X,Y,Z)

\displaystyle=\bigl(XC_{\sigma}(\varepsilon a)+ZS_{\sigma}(\varepsilon a),\ Y,\ ZC_{\sigma}(\varepsilon a)-\sigma XS_{\sigma}(\varepsilon a)\bigr),

(22)

\displaystyle\exp(\varepsilon b\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\sigma})\cdot(X,Y,Z)

\displaystyle=\bigl(X,\ YC_{\sigma}(\varepsilon b)+ZS_{\sigma}(\varepsilon b),\ ZC_{\sigma}(\varepsilon b)-\sigma YS_{\sigma}(\varepsilon b)\bigr).

(23)

Here for
\sigma=+1
these are ordinary rotations about horizontal axes, while for
\sigma=-1
they are boosts in the corresponding Lorentzian planes. We set

{\mathbf{b}}_{1}:=\varepsilon\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\sigma},\hskip 18.49988pt{\mathbf{b}}_{2}:=\varepsilon\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\sigma},\hskip 18.49988pt{\mathbf{b}}_{3}:=\mathbf{E}_{3},

(24)

and write
J:={\mathbf{b}}_{3}
when we wish to emphasise the planar rotation generator.

Thus, for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
fixed,

\displaystyle\exp(a{\mathbf{b}}_{1})=\exp(\varepsilon a\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\sigma}),

(25)

\displaystyle\exp(b{\mathbf{b}}_{2})=\exp(\varepsilon b\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\sigma}).

(26)

In both cases an isometry by a
small
parameter of size
O(\varepsilon)
produces a displacement of arc-length
R\cdot O(\varepsilon)=O(1)
.

Remark 4
.

The symbols
\mathbf{f}_{1},\mathbf{f}_{2},\mathbf{f}_{3}
and
\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\sigma},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\sigma},\mathbf{E}_{3}
are related, but they serve different purposes.
The basis
\{\mathbf{f}_{1},\mathbf{f}_{2},\mathbf{f}_{3}\}
is introduced only for each fixed
\varepsilon>0
in order to recognise the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon}
as
\mathfrak{so}(3)
for
\sigma=+1
and as
\mathfrak{so}(2,1)
for
\sigma=-1
.
By contrast,
\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\sigma},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\sigma},\mathbf{E}_{3}
are the actual infinitesimal generators of the isometric action on
M_{R}^{\sigma}
, written as vector fields in ambient coordinates, and these are the generators used in the momentum-map calculation.
Under the identification of
\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon}
with the corresponding isometry algebra, one has for
\varepsilon>0

\mathbf{f}_{1}=\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\sigma},\hskip 18.49988pt\mathbf{f}_{2}=\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\sigma},\hskip 18.49988pt\mathbf{f}_{3}=\mathbf{E}_{3}.

The basis
\{{\mathbf{b}}_{1},{\mathbf{b}}_{2},{\mathbf{b}}_{3}\}
is nevertheless retained because it is this basis, rather than
\{\mathbf{f}_{1},\mathbf{f}_{2},\mathbf{f}_{3}\}
, that has a finite limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
and hence yields the contraction to
\mathfrak{se}(2)
.

2.3
Group actions

For each
\varepsilon>0
let
G_{\varepsilon}
be the connected Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon}

G_{\varepsilon}\simeq SO(3)
when
\sigma=+1
and
G_{\varepsilon}\simeq SO^{+}(2,1)
when
\sigma=-1

. We define the

g\ast{\mathbf{w}}:={\mathrm{Log}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}\!\left(g\,\cdot{\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{w}})\right),\hskip 18.49988pt{\mathbf{w}}\in U,

(27)

that is,

{\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}(g\ast{\mathbf{w}})=g\cdot{\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{w}}),\hskip 18.49988pt{\mathbf{w}}\in U.

(28)

From (
5
) and (

s_{\sigma}(\rho)=1-\sigma\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\rho^{2}}{6}+O(\varepsilon^{4}),\hskip 18.49988ptc_{\sigma}(\rho)=1-\sigma\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\rho^{2}}{2}+O(\varepsilon^{4}).

Hence from the Riemannian exponential formula (

X^{\varepsilon}=u+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\hskip 18.49988ptY^{\varepsilon}=v+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\hskip 18.49988ptZ^{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}-\sigma\frac{\varepsilon\rho^{2}}{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3}).

(30)

Lemma 2

(The first horizontal generator becomes translation in

)

Fix

Applying (

) then gives (

).

Lemma 3

(The second horizontal generator becomes translation in
v
)
.

Fix
b\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, for
{\mathbf{w}}=(u,v)
in any fixed bounded set,

2.4
The momentum map

We now calculate the momentum map in exponential coordinates. For the cotangent-lifted
G_{\varepsilon}
–action on
T^{*}M_{R}^{\sigma}
, the momentum map is characterized by

\langle\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon},{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon}),\xi\rangle=\langle{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon},\xi_{M_{R}^{\sigma}}({\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon})\rangle_{\sigma},\hskip 18.49988pt\xi\in\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon},

(34)

where we identify covectors and tangent vectors by the induced metric on
M_{R}^{\sigma}
.

For the ambient generators introduced in (
22
)–(
23
) and Remark
5
, differentiation at the identity gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\sigma}\cdot(X,Y,Z)

\displaystyle=(Z,0,-\sigma X),

(35)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\sigma}\cdot(X,Y,Z)

\displaystyle=(0,Z,-\sigma Y),

(36)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{3}\cdot(X,Y,Z)

\displaystyle=(-Y,X,0).

(37)

Therefore, if
{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon}=(p_{x}^{\varepsilon},p_{y}^{\varepsilon},p_{z}^{\varepsilon})\in T_{{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}}M_{R}^{\sigma}
, then

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\sigma}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon},\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\sigma}\cdot{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\sigma}=Z^{\varepsilon}p_{x}^{\varepsilon}-X^{\varepsilon}p_{z}^{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\sigma}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon},\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\sigma}\cdot{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\sigma}=Z^{\varepsilon}p_{y}^{\varepsilon}-Y^{\varepsilon}p_{z}^{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{E}_{3}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon},\mathbf{E}_{3}\cdot{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\sigma}=X^{\varepsilon}p_{y}^{\varepsilon}-Y^{\varepsilon}p_{x}^{\varepsilon}.

(38)

Using the contracted basis (
24
), namely

{\mathbf{b}}_{1}=\varepsilon\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\sigma}
,
{\mathbf{b}}_{2}=\varepsilon\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\sigma}
,
{\mathbf{b}}_{3}=\mathbf{E}_{3}
, we obtain

\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon},{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon})=\bigl(\varepsilon(Z^{\varepsilon}p_{x}^{\varepsilon}-X^{\varepsilon}p_{z}^{\varepsilon}),\varepsilon(Z^{\varepsilon}p_{y}^{\varepsilon}-Y^{\varepsilon}p_{z}^{\varepsilon}),X^{\varepsilon}p_{y}^{\varepsilon}-Y^{\varepsilon}p_{x}^{\varepsilon}\bigr).

(39)

Fix
({\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon},{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon})\in T^{*}M_{R}^{\sigma}
in the image of the exponential chart and write

({\mathbf{w}},\bm{\alpha})=(u,v,p_{u},p_{v})\in T^{*}U
with

{\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{w}})={\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}
. We now express (
39
) in these chart coordinates.

From (
30
) we have

{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}=(X^{\varepsilon},Y^{\varepsilon},Z^{\varepsilon})=\bigl(u+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\ v+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\ \tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}-\sigma\tfrac{\varepsilon\rho^{2}}{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3})\bigr).

(40)

In the exponential chart the canonical momenta are the covector

\bm{\alpha}=p_{u}\,{\mathrm{d}}u+p_{v}\,{\mathrm{d}}v
.
Writing the tangent vector
{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon}
in the coordinate basis of the chart, the relation between
\bm{\alpha}
and its coordinate components is

\bm{\alpha}=G_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{w}})\,{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon},

(41)

where
G_{\varepsilon}
is the pulled-back metric (
13
). Hence

{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon}=G_{\varepsilon}^{-1}({\mathbf{w}})\bm{\alpha}
, and by (
17
) we obtain

p_{1}^{\varepsilon}=p_{u}+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\hskip 18.49988ptp_{2}^{\varepsilon}=p_{v}+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

(42)

Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{u}^{\varepsilon}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial u}{\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}\Big|_{(u,v)}
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{v}^{\varepsilon}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial v}{\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}\Big|_{(u,v)}
.
Using (
8
), (
29
), and the identity

\partial Z^{\varepsilon}/\partial u=-\sigma\varepsilon u\,s_{\sigma}(\rho)
,

\partial Z^{\varepsilon}/\partial v=-\sigma\varepsilon v\,s_{\sigma}(\rho)
,
we have

\mathbf{e}_{u}^{\varepsilon}=\bigl(1+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\ O(\varepsilon^{2}),\ -\sigma\varepsilon u+O(\varepsilon^{3})\bigr),\hskip 18.49988pt\mathbf{e}_{v}^{\varepsilon}=\bigl(O(\varepsilon^{2}),\ 1+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\ -\sigma\varepsilon v+O(\varepsilon^{3})\bigr).

Therefore

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon}=p_{1}^{\varepsilon}\,\mathbf{e}_{u}^{\varepsilon}+p_{2}^{\varepsilon}\,\mathbf{e}_{v}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=(p_{x}^{\varepsilon},p_{y}^{\varepsilon},p_{z}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\left(p_{u}+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\ p_{v}+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\ -\sigma\varepsilon(up_{u}+vp_{v})+O(\varepsilon^{3})\right).

(43)

Substituting (
40
) and (
43

39

\displaystyle\varepsilon(Z^{\varepsilon}p_{x}^{\varepsilon}-X^{\varepsilon}p_{z}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\left(\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+O(\varepsilon)\right)\left(p_{u}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\right)-\left(u+O(\varepsilon^{2})\right)\left(-\sigma\varepsilon(up_{u}+vp_{v})+O(\varepsilon^{3})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=p_{u}+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

\displaystyle\varepsilon(Z^{\varepsilon}p_{y}^{\varepsilon}-Y^{\varepsilon}p_{z}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\left(\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+O(\varepsilon)\right)\left(p_{v}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\right)-\left(v+O(\varepsilon^{2})\right)\left(-\sigma\varepsilon(up_{u}+vp_{v})+O(\varepsilon^{3})\right)\right)

\displaystyle X^{\varepsilon}p_{y}^{\varepsilon}-Y^{\varepsilon}p_{x}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=up_{v}-vp_{u}+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

Hence the momentum map in exponential coordinates satisfies

\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(u,v,p_{u},p_{v})=\left(p_{u}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\,,p_{v}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\,,up_{v}-vp_{u}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\right).

(44)

Thus the following proposition holds.

Proposition 4

(Limit momentum map is the
\mathfrak{se}(2)^{*}

Under the identification

2.5
Configuration and phase space of n particle dynamics

In what follows we consider a system of
n
mass points with a binary interaction, not necessarily gravitational.

On the Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
the configuration space is

\displaystyle Q^{0}=\left(\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)^{n}\setminus\Delta,

where
\Delta
is the collision set, possibly empty. A configuration is written

\displaystyle{\mathbf{q}}^{0}=({\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{1},{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{2},\dots,{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{n})\in Q^{0}.

The associated phase space is the cotangent bundle

M^{0}:=T^{*}Q^{0}\simeq\left(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\setminus\Delta\right)\times\mathbb{R}^{2n},

(47)

with canonical coordinates
({\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i},{\mathbf{p}}^{0}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
for each
i
, and canonical symplectic form

\displaystyle\omega^{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathrm{d}}{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}\wedge{\mathrm{d}}{\mathbf{p}}^{0}_{i}.

The Euclidean group
SE(2)
acts diagonally on
Q^{0}
and by cotangent lift on
M^{0}
:

({\mathbf{a}},\Theta)\cdot({\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i},{\mathbf{p}}^{0}_{i})=(\Theta{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}+{\mathbf{a}},\Theta{\mathbf{p}}^{0}_{i}),\hskip 18.49988pt({\mathbf{a}},\Theta)\in SE(2),\ \ i=1,\dots,n.

(48)

Via the identification
G_{0}\simeq SE(2)
and
\mathfrak{g}_{0}\simeq\mathfrak{se}(2)
from Lemma
1
, this is the
G_{0}
–action on
M^{0}
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, the configuration space is

\displaystyle Q^{\varepsilon}=\left(M^{\sigma}_{1/\varepsilon}\right)^{n}\setminus\Delta^{\varepsilon},

where
\Delta^{\varepsilon}
is the collision set, possibly empty. The phase space is

M^{\varepsilon}:=T^{*}Q^{\varepsilon}.

(49)

In the ambient model, one may write points of
M^{\varepsilon}
as tuples

({\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}_{i},{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\hskip 18.49988pt\langle{\mathbf{q}}_{i}^{\varepsilon},{\mathbf{q}}_{i}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\sigma}=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon^{2}},\hskip 18.49988pt\langle{\mathbf{q}}_{i}^{\varepsilon},{\mathbf{p}}_{i}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\sigma}=0,

with
Z_{i}^{\varepsilon}>0
when
\sigma=-1
. The phase space carries the canonical symplectic form

\displaystyle\omega^{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathrm{d}}{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}_{i}\wedge{\mathrm{d}}{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon}_{i}.

For
\sigma=+1
, the symmetry group is
SO(3)
and acts diagonally by cotangent lift:

A\cdot({\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}_{i},{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon}_{i})=(A{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}_{i},A{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon}_{i}),\hskip 18.49988ptA\in SO(3),\ \ i=1,\dots,n.

(50)

For
\sigma=-1
, the symmetry group is the identity component
SO^{+}(2,1)
, acting in the same ambient way:

A\cdot({\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}_{i},{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon}_{i})=(A{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}_{i},A{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon}_{i}),\hskip 18.49988ptA\in SO^{+}(2,1),\ \ i=1,\dots,n.

(51)

Using Lemma
1
, we identify
G_{\varepsilon}
with the corresponding symmetry group for each fixed
\varepsilon>0
, so this is the
G_{\varepsilon}
–action on
M^{\varepsilon}
.

Writing a phase point as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}=({\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon},{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon})\in M^{\varepsilon},

the associated momentum map is the sum over particles:

\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}_{i},{\mathbf{p}}^{\varepsilon}_{i}),

(52)

and the asymptotic formula (
44
) holds term-wise for each particle and hence for the full
n
-body system.

2.6
Symplectic form in exponential coordinates

Proposition 5
.

Let
\vartheta^{\varepsilon}
denote the canonical Liouville
1
-form on
\displaystyle{M^{\varepsilon}=T^{*}Q^{\varepsilon}}
, and let
\displaystyle{\omega^{\varepsilon}=-{\mathrm{d}}\vartheta^{\varepsilon}}
be the canonical symplectic form. Let
\vartheta^{0}
and
\omega^{0}
denote the corresponding canonical forms on the fixed phase space
T^{*}U^{n}
. Then

(\Phi^{\varepsilon})^{*}\vartheta^{\varepsilon}=\vartheta^{0}

(53)

and therefore

(\Phi^{\varepsilon})^{*}\omega^{\varepsilon}=\omega^{0}

(54)

for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon
for which the exponential chart is defined on
U
.

Proof.

We first consider one particle. Let

\phi^{\varepsilon}:={\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}:U\longrightarrow M^{\sigma}_{1/\varepsilon}

(55)

and let
\displaystyle{T^{*}\phi^{\varepsilon}:T^{*}U\longrightarrow T^{*}M^{\sigma}_{1/\varepsilon}}
be its cotangent lift.
Recall that the canonical Liouville
1
-form on a cotangent bundle
T^{*}Q
is defined by

\vartheta_{Q}{}_{(q,p)}(V)=p\bigl({\mathrm{d}}\pi_{Q}(V)\bigr),

(56)

where
\displaystyle{\pi_{Q}:T^{*}Q\to Q}
is the bundle projection.
For any diffeomorphism
\displaystyle{\phi:Q_{1}\to Q_{2}}
one has

(T^{*}\phi)^{*}\vartheta_{Q_{2}}=\vartheta_{Q_{1}}.
(
[
Marsden and Ratiu 2007
]
)
Applying this to
\phi^{\varepsilon}
gives,
for one particle,

(T^{*}\phi^{\varepsilon})^{*}\vartheta^{\varepsilon}=\vartheta^{0}.

(57)

Taking exterior derivatives, we obtain

(T^{*}\phi^{\varepsilon})^{*}\omega^{\varepsilon}=(T^{*}\phi^{\varepsilon})^{*}(-{\mathrm{d}}\vartheta^{\varepsilon})=-{\mathrm{d}}\bigl((T^{*}\phi^{\varepsilon})^{*}\vartheta^{\varepsilon}\bigr)=-{\mathrm{d}}\vartheta^{0}=\omega^{0}.

(58)

For
n
particles, the map
\displaystyle{({\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon})^{n}:U^{n}\to(M^{\sigma}_{1/\varepsilon})^{n}}
is again a diffeomorphism onto its image, and
\Phi^{\varepsilon}
in (
60
) is its cotangent lift. The canonical Liouville form on a product cotangent bundle is the sum of the one-particle Liouville forms, so applying the one-particle argument componentwise gives
\displaystyle{(\Phi^{\varepsilon})^{*}\vartheta^{\varepsilon}=\vartheta^{0}}
. Taking exterior derivatives yields (
54
).
∎

Remark 7
.

Proposition
5
shows that, in exponential coordinates, the symplectic structure is fixed: all
\varepsilon
-dependence is carried by the Hamiltonian, not by the symplectic form. Thus the curved Newtonian
n
-body problem becomes, on the fixed phase space
T^{*}U^{n}
, a smooth family of Hamiltonian functions on a single symplectic manifold.

in the reduced space.

It remains to check smooth dependence. By construction, the pulled-back slices

\displaystyle{\widehat{S}_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}=(\Phi^{\varepsilon})^{-1}\!\left(S_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}\right)\subset K}

depend smoothly on
\varepsilon
. Moreover, by Proposition
5
, the pulled-back ambient symplectic form is exactly fixed:

\displaystyle{(\Phi^{\varepsilon})^{*}\omega^{\varepsilon}=\omega^{0}}
for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon.

Hence, after transport to the fixed model space
K
, the only
\varepsilon
–dependence in the reduced symplectic forms comes from the smoothly varying slices and the smoothly varying quotient maps

\displaystyle{\pi_{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}}.}

The reduced symplectic form on
P^{\varepsilon}_{\mu^{\varepsilon}}
is defined by

[\omega]^{\varepsilon}:=\left(\pi^{\varepsilon}\big|_{S^{\varepsilon}_{\mu^{\varepsilon}}}\right)^{*}\!\left(\omega^{\varepsilon}\big|_{S^{\varepsilon}_{\mu^{\varepsilon}}}\right),

(65)

where
\pi^{\varepsilon}|_{S^{\varepsilon}_{\mu^{\varepsilon}}}
is a local diffeomorphism
onto a neighbourhood of
[z]^{\varepsilon}\in P^{\varepsilon}_{\mu^{\varepsilon}}
by the
preceding argument. Since by Proposition
5

(\Phi^{\varepsilon})^{*}\omega^{\varepsilon}=\omega^{0}

and the pulled-back slices

\hat{S}^{\varepsilon}_{\mu^{\varepsilon}}\subset K
depend smoothly on
\varepsilon
,
the pulled-back reduced form
(\Phi^{\varepsilon})^{*}[\omega]^{\varepsilon}
depends
smoothly on
\varepsilon
. At
\varepsilon=0
, the form
[\omega]^{0}
is the
standard reduced symplectic form on
P^{0}_{\mu^{0}}
, which is nondegenerate at

[z]^{0}
by the Marsden–Weinstein theorem. By smooth dependence,
[\omega]^{\varepsilon}

is nondegenerate at the continued point for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small.

In slice coordinates, the reduced Hamiltonian is represented by

[H]_{\varepsilon}=H_{\varepsilon}\circ\left(\pi_{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}}\right)^{-1}

and the reduced symplectic form (
65
).
Since
H_{\varepsilon}
is smooth in
\varepsilon
, the slices depend smoothly on
\varepsilon
, and the ambient symplectic form becomes the fixed form
\omega^{0}
on
K
, it follows that
[H]_{\varepsilon}
and
[\omega]_{\varepsilon}
depend smoothly on
\varepsilon
. The reduced Hamiltonian vector field is defined by

\displaystyle{\iota_{X_{[H]_{\varepsilon}}}[\omega]_{\varepsilon}={\mathrm{d}}[H]_{\varepsilon},}

hence also depends smoothly on
\varepsilon
. Standard smooth dependence of local flows, return times, and return maps then yields smooth dependence of the reduced Poincaré maps.
∎

Remark 8

(Local picture)
.

Locally near the chosen point, the geometry is

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\widehat{S}_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}\subset\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon})\subset K\subset T^{*}U^{n}&\xrightarrow{\ \Phi^{\varepsilon}\ }&S_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon})\subset M^{\varepsilon}\\[6.2pt]
&&\downarrow\scriptstyle{\pi_{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}}}\\[4.64996pt]
&&P_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}\end{array}

Thus the quotient is always taken in the actual phase space
M^{\varepsilon}
, while the exponential chart provides fixed local coordinates for the slices representing the reduced space.

Continuation of dynamics

3.1
Continuation of relative equilibria (RE)

Definition 1

(Relative equilibrium)
.

A point
{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}\in M^{\varepsilon}
is a
relative equilibrium (RE)
if there exists

\bm{\xi}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon}
such that

X_{H_{\varepsilon}}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon})=\bm{\xi}^{\varepsilon}_{M^{\varepsilon}}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}).

(66)

Equivalently,
{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}
is a critical point of the augmented Hamiltonian

H_{\varepsilon}^{\bm{\xi}^{\varepsilon}}:=H_{\varepsilon}-\bigl\langle\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon},\bm{\xi}^{\varepsilon}\bigr\rangle.

(67)

Definition 2

(Nondegenerate RE on a slice)
.

Let
{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}
be an RE with generator
\bm{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
and momentum

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}:=\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}).

Choose a slice

\displaystyle S_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon})

through
{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}
as in Lemma
6
. We say that
{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}
is
nondegenerate on a slice
if

{\mathrm{d}}^{2}\!\left(H_{\varepsilon}^{\bm{\xi}^{\varepsilon}}\big|_{S_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}}\right)({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon})\hskip 9.24994pt\text{is nondegenerate on }\ T_{{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}}S_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}.

(68)

Since
S_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon})
, the functions

\displaystyle H_{\varepsilon}^{\bm{\xi}^{\varepsilon}}\big|_{S_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994ptH_{\varepsilon}\big|_{S_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}}

differ by the constant
-\langle\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon},\bm{\xi}^{\varepsilon}\rangle
, so the condition is equivalent to nondegeneracy of the Hessian of the reduced Hamiltonian at the projected equilibrium.

Theorem 7

(Continuation of Newtonian relative equilibria)
.

Let
{\mathbf{z}}^{0}\in M^{0}\cap T^{*}U^{n}
be a planar Newtonian relative equilibrium with generator

\displaystyle{\bm{\xi}^{0}\in\mathfrak{g}_{0}\simeq\mathfrak{se}(2),}

and let

\displaystyle{\bm{\mu}^{0}:=\mathcal{J}_{0}({\mathbf{z}}^{0}).}

Assume that the hypotheses of Lemma
6
hold for the point
{\mathbf{z}}^{0}
, and let

\displaystyle{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{z}}}^{\varepsilon})}

be the corresponding moving momentum value furnished by (
63
). Assume further that
{\mathbf{z}}^{0}
is nondegenerate on a slice in the sense of Definition
2
. Then there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and smooth maps

\varepsilon\longmapsto\bigl({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon},\bm{\xi}^{\varepsilon}\bigr),\hskip 9.24994pt0\leq\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0},

with

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}\in M^{\varepsilon},}

\displaystyle{\bm{\xi}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{\varepsilon},}

such that
{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}
is a Newtonian relative equilibrium of
(M^{\varepsilon},H_{\varepsilon})
with momentum
\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}
, generator
\bm{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
, and

\displaystyle{({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon},\bm{\xi}^{\varepsilon})\to({\mathbf{z}}^{0},\bm{\xi}^{0})}

\displaystyle{\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.}

Moreover,
{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}
is unique up to the
G_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}
–action among relative equilibria sufficiently close to
{\mathbf{z}}^{0}
.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle{[{\mathbf{z}}]^{0}:=\pi_{0}({\mathbf{z}}^{0})\in P_{\bm{\mu}^{0}}^{0}}

be the projected equilibrium. By Lemma
6
, the moving reduced spaces

\displaystyle{P_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon})/G_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}}

admit smooth local slice coordinates near
[{\mathbf{z}}]^{0}
, and in these coordinates the reduced Hamiltonians and reduced symplectic forms form smooth families in
\varepsilon
. By Lemma
6
,
[\omega]^{\varepsilon}
is nondegenerate for
\varepsilon
small, so the reduced Hamiltonian vector field
X_{[H]^{\varepsilon}}
is well defined near
[z]^{0}
.

Because
{\mathbf{z}}^{0}
is a relative equilibrium, the point
[{\mathbf{z}}]^{0}
is an equilibrium of the reduced Hamiltonian flow on
P_{\bm{\mu}^{0}}^{0}
. By Definition
2
, the Hessian of the reduced Hamiltonian at
[{\mathbf{z}}]^{0}
is nondegenerate.
Hence, in local slice coordinates, the equation

\displaystyle{{\mathrm{d}}[H]_{\varepsilon}([{\mathbf{z}}])=0}

has a unique smooth solution

\displaystyle{[{\mathbf{z}}]^{\varepsilon}}

near
[{\mathbf{z}}]^{0}
for
\varepsilon
small, by the implicit function theorem.

Lift
[{\mathbf{z}}]^{\varepsilon}
to the slice

\displaystyle{S_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}}

using the local inverse of

\displaystyle{\pi_{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}}.}

This gives a smooth family

{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}\in S_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}).

In particular,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon})=\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}.}

Since
[{\mathbf{z}}]^{\varepsilon}
is an equilibrium of the reduced flow, the vector

\displaystyle{X_{H_{\varepsilon}}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon})}

projects to zero in the reduced space. Therefore

\displaystyle{X_{H_{\varepsilon}}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon})\in T_{{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}}\!\bigl(G_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}\cdot{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}\bigr).}

\mathfrak{g}_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}\longrightarrow T_{{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}}\!\bigl(G_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}\cdot{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}\bigr),\hskip 9.24994pt\bm{\eta}\longmapsto\bm{\eta}_{M^{\varepsilon}}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}),

is an isomorphism. Hence there exists a unique

\displaystyle{\bm{\xi}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}}

such that

\displaystyle{X_{H_{\varepsilon}}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon})=\bm{\xi}^{\varepsilon}_{M^{\varepsilon}}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}).}

Thus

is a relative equilibrium. Smooth dependence of
\bm{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
follows from smooth dependence of

, the action, and the above linear isomorphism.

Uniqueness of the reduced equilibrium in slice coordinates gives uniqueness of
[{\mathbf{z}}]^{\varepsilon}
near

{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}
up to the

–action.
∎

3.2
Continuation of periodic orbits and relative periodic solutions (RPOs)

In the Hamiltonian setting, continuation of periodic orbits must be formulated on a regular energy surface. Indeed, for an autonomous Hamiltonian flow, the Floquet multiplier
1
is always present in the full monodromy matrix, so the relevant nondegeneracy condition is imposed on the Poincaré return map restricted to an energy level.

Definition 3

(Nondegenerate Hamiltonian periodic orbit)
.

Let

\displaystyle\gamma^{\varepsilon}(t)=\phi_{t}^{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon})

be a periodic orbit of the Hamiltonian flow of
H_{\varepsilon}
on
(M^{\varepsilon},\omega^{\varepsilon})
, with minimal period
T^{\varepsilon}>0
, and let

\displaystyle E^{\varepsilon}:=H_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}).

Assume that
E^{\varepsilon}
is a regular value of
H_{\varepsilon}
. Choose a local submanifold

\displaystyle\Sigma_{E^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}\subset H_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(E^{\varepsilon})

through
{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}
, transverse to
X_{H_{\varepsilon}}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon})
inside the energy surface, and let

\displaystyle P^{\varepsilon}:\Sigma_{E^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}\to\Sigma_{E^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}

be the corresponding local Poincaré return map. We say that
\gamma^{\varepsilon}
is a
nondegenerate Hamiltonian periodic orbit
if

\det\!\bigl({\mathrm{d}}P^{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon})-I\bigr)\neq 0.

(69)

Equivalently, the Floquet multiplier
1
of the full monodromy matrix has algebraic multiplicity exactly
2
.

Proposition 8

(Continuation of Hamiltonian periodic orbits)
.

Let
\gamma^{0}
be a periodic orbit of the Hamiltonian flow of
H_{0}
contained in the regular energy surface

\displaystyle{H_{0}^{-1}(E^{0})}\,,

E^{0}:=H_{0}({\mathbf{z}}^{0}),

and assume that
\gamma^{0}
is nondegenerate in the sense of Definition
3
. Assume further that, for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small,

1.

E^{0}
remains a regular value of
H_{\varepsilon}
near the continued orbit;

2.

the continued orbit remains in the exponential-chart domain;

3.

the continued orbit remains collision-free.

Then there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for all
0\leq\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, the Hamiltonian flow of
H_{\varepsilon}
possesses a periodic orbit

\displaystyle\gamma^{\varepsilon}\subset H_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(E^{0})

near
\gamma^{0}
, depending smoothly on
\varepsilon
and unique up to phase shift along the orbit.

Proof.

Fix a point
{\mathbf{z}}^{0}\in\gamma^{0}
. Since
E^{0}
is a regular value of
H_{0}
, there exists a local hypersurface

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}\subset M^{0}

through
{\mathbf{z}}^{0}
, transverse to
X_{H_{0}}({\mathbf{z}}^{0})
, such that

\displaystyle\Sigma^{0}:=\widehat{\Sigma}\cap H_{0}^{-1}(E^{0})

is a local Poincaré section inside the energy surface. After identifying nearby phase spaces in the exponential chart, regularity of
E^{0}
for
H_{\varepsilon}
implies that

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\varepsilon}:=\widehat{\Sigma}\cap H_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(E^{0})

is a smooth family of local sections for
\varepsilon
small. Let

\displaystyle P^{\varepsilon}:\Sigma^{\varepsilon}\to\Sigma^{\varepsilon}

be the corresponding local return map.

Using local coordinates
u
on
\Sigma^{0}
and the smooth identifications
\Sigma^{\varepsilon}\simeq\Sigma^{0}
, define

\displaystyle F(u,\varepsilon):=P^{\varepsilon}(u)-u.

At the point
u^{0}
corresponding to
{\mathbf{z}}^{0}
we have

\displaystyle F(u^{0},0)=0.

By (
69
),

\displaystyle{\mathrm{d}}_{u}F(u^{0},0)={\mathrm{d}}P^{0}(u^{0})-I

is invertible. The implicit function theorem therefore yields a unique smooth branch of fixed points

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}

of
P^{\varepsilon}
for
\varepsilon
small. Each
u^{\varepsilon}
determines a periodic orbit
\gamma^{\varepsilon}
of
X_{H_{\varepsilon}}
contained in
H_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(E^{0})
. Uniqueness of the fixed point on the section gives uniqueness of the periodic orbit up to phase shift.
∎

Definition 4

(Hamiltonian relative periodic solution)
.

A trajectory
{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}(t)
of the Hamiltonian flow of
H_{\varepsilon}
is a
Hamiltonian relative periodic solution
if there exist
T^{\varepsilon}>0
and
g^{\varepsilon}\in G_{\varepsilon}
such that

{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}(t+T^{\varepsilon})=g^{\varepsilon}\cdot{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}(t)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for all }t.

(70)

Let

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}:=\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}(t)).

By conservation and equivariance of the momentum map, the drift necessarily belongs to the isotropy subgroup

\displaystyle g^{\varepsilon}\in G_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}.

Thus RPOs are periodic solutions in the reduced space

P_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon})/G_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}.

(71)

Equivalently,
{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}(t)
is an RPO if and only if its projection
[{\mathbf{z}}]^{\varepsilon}(t)
to
P_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}
is a periodic solution of the reduced Hamiltonian flow.

Definition 5

(Nondegenerate Hamiltonian RPO)
.

A Hamiltonian relative periodic solution
{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}(t)
is called
nondegenerate
if its projection to the reduced space
P_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}
is a nondegenerate Hamiltonian periodic orbit in the sense of Definition
3
.

Theorem 9

(Continuation of Newtonian Hamiltonian RPOs)
.

Let
{\mathbf{z}}^{0}(t)
be a planar Newtonian Hamiltonian relative periodic solution, with period
T^{0}
, drift

\displaystyle{g^{0}\in G_{\bm{\mu}^{0}},}
and momentum
\displaystyle{\bm{\mu}^{0}:=\mathcal{J}_{0}({\mathbf{z}}^{0}(t)).}

6
hold for the base point
{\mathbf{z}}^{0}(0)

\displaystyle{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{z}}}^{\varepsilon})}

be the corresponding moving momentum value provided by (
63
). Let

\displaystyle{[\gamma]^{0}\subset P_{\bm{\mu}^{0}}^{0}}

be the reduced periodic orbit associated with
{\mathbf{z}}^{0}(t)
, and let

\displaystyle{[E]^{0}:=[H]_{0}([{\mathbf{z}}]^{0})}

be its reduced energy. Assume that

1.

[E]^{0}
is a regular value of the reduced Hamiltonian
[H]_{0}
near
[\gamma]^{0}
, and remains a regular value of
[H]_{\varepsilon}
near the continued reduced orbit;

2.

[\gamma]^{0}
is a nondegenerate Hamiltonian periodic orbit of the reduced Hamiltonian flow on
P_{\bm{\mu}^{0}}^{0}
.

Then there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for all
0\leq\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, the Newtonian Hamiltonian flow on
M^{\sigma}_{1/\varepsilon}
possesses a Hamiltonian relative periodic solution

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}(t)}

with momentum
\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}
and period
T^{\varepsilon}

Moreover, there exists a smooth family of drift elements

\displaystyle{g^{\varepsilon}\in G_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}}
with

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}(t+T^{\varepsilon})=g^{\varepsilon}\cdot{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}(t).}

The reduced periodic orbit is unique near
[\gamma]^{0}
up to phase shift, and the lifted RPO is unique up to phase shift and the
G_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}
–action.

Proof.

By Lemma
6
, the moving reduced spaces

\displaystyle{P_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon})/G_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}}

admit smooth local slice coordinates near the reduced orbit
[\gamma]^{0}
, and in these coordinates the reduced Hamiltonians and reduced symplectic forms form smooth families in
\varepsilon
. Since
[E]^{0}
is a regular reduced energy value and
[\gamma]^{0}
is nondegenerate in the sense of Definition
3
, Proposition
8
applies to the reduced dynamics. Hence, for
\varepsilon
small, there exists a unique nearby reduced periodic orbit

\displaystyle{[\gamma]^{\varepsilon}\subset P_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}}

depending smoothly on
\varepsilon
, with period
T^{\varepsilon}\to T^{0}
.

Choose the slice

\displaystyle{S_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon})}

from Lemma

, and choose a smooth family of points

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon}\in S_{\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}}

projecting to a chosen base point of
[\gamma]^{\varepsilon}
. Then

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon})=\bm{\mu}^{\varepsilon}.}

Let

\displaystyle{\phi_{t}^{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{z}}^{\varepsilon})}

be the corresponding full Hamiltonian trajectory. Its projection to the reduced space is precisely
[\gamma]^{\varepsilon}(t)
.

Since

is periodic with period

4
Application to the Newtonian n-body Hamiltonian

4.1
The Newtonian n-body Hamiltonian in exponential coordinates

In this subsection we work on the fixed chart phase space
T^{*}U^{n}
for a fixed curvature sign
\sigma\in\{+1,-1\}
. Recall that

\displaystyle{\Phi^{\varepsilon}=T^{*}\bigl(({\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon})^{n}\bigr):T^{*}U^{n}\to M^{\varepsilon}}

denotes the product cotangent lift of the exponential chart and that by Proposition
5
one has

\displaystyle{(\Phi^{\varepsilon})^{*}\omega^{\varepsilon}=\omega^{0}}

for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon
. Thus the pulled-back Newtonian systems form a family of Hamiltonian systems on the single symplectic manifold
(T^{*}U^{n},\omega^{0})
. To simplify notation, we write

\displaystyle{H_{\varepsilon,\sigma},\,\,K_{\varepsilon,\sigma},\,\,U_{\varepsilon,\sigma},\,\,\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}}

for the pulled-back Hamiltonian, kinetic energy, potential energy, and momentum map on
T^{*}U^{n}
.

Kinetic energy in exponential coordinates

Using the exponential chart of Section
2.1
, let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a fixed bounded open set containing the origin. For
\sigma=+1
we assume additionally that
\varepsilon\|{\mathbf{w}}\|<\pi/2
for all
{\mathbf{w}}\in U
and all
\varepsilon
under consideration, so that

{\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}:U\to M^{\sigma}_{1/\varepsilon}
is a diffeomorphism onto its image. For an
n
-particle configuration we write

{\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon}_{i}={\mathrm{Exp}}_{\mathbf{N}}^{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}),\hskip 18.49988pti=1,\dots,n,

(72)

so that
({\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{n})\in U^{n}
provides a chart on
(M^{\sigma}_{1/\varepsilon})^{n}
.

For masses
m_{1},\dots,m_{n}
, the kinetic energy in exponential coordinates is

K_{\varepsilon,\sigma}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{0},{\mathbf{p}}^{0}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2m_{i}}\,\left({\mathbf{p}}^{0}_{i}\right)^{T}\,G_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i})^{-1}\,{\mathbf{p}}^{0}_{i}\,,

(73)

where
G_{\varepsilon}^{-1}
is the cometric (
14
). Using

G_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{w}})^{-1}=P({\mathbf{w}})+A_{\varepsilon,\sigma}(\rho)^{-1}(\mathbb{I}-P({\mathbf{w}}))

one obtains

K_{\varepsilon,\sigma}({\mathbf{q}}^{0},{\mathbf{p}}^{0})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2m_{i}}\left(\frac{\left({\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{p}}^{0}_{i}\right)^{2}}{\|{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}\|^{2}}+\frac{1}{A_{\varepsilon,\sigma}\left(\|{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}\|\right)}\left(\|{\mathbf{p}}^{0}_{i}\|^{2}-\frac{\left({\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{p}}^{0}_{i}\right)^{2}}{\|{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}\|^{2}}\right)\right),

(74)

with the evident interpretation at
{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}=0
by continuity. Using (
15
), we obtain

K_{\varepsilon,\sigma}({\mathbf{q}}^{0},{\mathbf{p}}^{0})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2m_{i}}\|{\mathbf{p}}^{0}_{i}\|^{2}+\sigma\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{6m_{i}}\left(\|{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}\|^{2}\|{\mathbf{p}}^{0}_{i}\|^{2}-({\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{p}}^{0}_{i})^{2}\right)+O(\varepsilon^{4}),

(75)

with
O(\varepsilon^{4})
uniform on compact collision-free subsets of
T^{*}U^{n}
.

The curved Newtonian potential and its flat limit

For
\varepsilon>0
the Newtonian interaction on
M^{\sigma}_{R}
is given by

U_{\varepsilon,\sigma}({\mathbf{q}}^{\varepsilon})=-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}m_{i}m_{j}\,\varepsilon\,\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}(\psi_{ij}),

(76)

where

\mathcal{C}_{+1}(t)=\cot t,\hskip 18.49988pt\mathcal{C}_{-1}(t)=\coth t,

and
\psi_{ij}=\varepsilon d_{ij}^{\varepsilon}
with
d_{ij}^{\varepsilon}
the geodesic distance on
M_{R}^{\sigma}
between
{\mathbf{q}}_{i}^{\varepsilon}
and
{\mathbf{q}}_{j}^{\varepsilon}
.
Let

r_{ij}:=\|{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}-{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{j}\|,\hskip 18.49988ptA_{ij}:=\left\|{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}\right\|^{2}\left\|{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{j}\right\|^{2}-\left({\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{j}\right)^{2}.

(77)

A direct expansion of the exponential chart gives

C_{\sigma}(\psi_{ij})=1-\sigma\frac{\varepsilon^{2}r_{ij}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{24}\!\left(r_{ij}^{4}+4A_{ij}\right)+O(\varepsilon^{6}),

(78)

whence

\psi_{ij}=\varepsilon r_{ij}-\sigma\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{6\,r_{ij}}\,A_{ij}+O(\varepsilon^{5}),

(79)

and therefore

\varepsilon\,\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}(\psi_{ij})=\frac{1}{r_{ij}}+\sigma\varepsilon^{2}\left(\frac{A_{ij}}{6\,r_{ij}^{3}}-\frac{r_{ij}}{3}\right)+O(\varepsilon^{4}).

(80)

Substituting into (
76
) yields, in exponential coordinates,

U_{\varepsilon,\sigma}({\mathbf{q}}^{0})=-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{r_{ij}}+\sigma\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}m_{i}m_{j}\left(\frac{r_{ij}}{3}-\frac{A_{ij}}{6\,r_{ij}^{3}}\right)+O(\varepsilon^{4}),

(81)

where
O(\varepsilon^{4})
is uniform on compact collision-free subsets of
U^{n}
.

Hamiltonian expansion

The pulled-back Hamiltonian on the exponential chart phase space
T^{*}U^{n}
is

H_{\varepsilon,\sigma}({\mathbf{q}}^{0},{\mathbf{p}}^{0})=K_{\varepsilon,\sigma}({\mathbf{q}}^{0},{\mathbf{p}}^{0})+U_{\varepsilon,\sigma}({\mathbf{q}}^{0}).

(82)

The planar Hamiltonian is recovered at
\varepsilon=0
:

H_{0}({\mathbf{q}}^{0},{\mathbf{p}}^{0})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|{\mathbf{p}}^{0}_{i}\|^{2}}{2m_{i}}-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{\|{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}-{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{j}\|}.

(83)

Using (
75
) and (
81
), we obtain on compact collision-free subsets

H_{\varepsilon,\sigma}({\mathbf{q}}^{0},{\mathbf{p}}^{0})=H_{0}({\mathbf{q}}^{0},{\mathbf{p}}^{0})+\sigma\varepsilon^{2}\,H_{2}({\mathbf{q}}^{0},{\mathbf{p}}^{0})+O(\varepsilon^{4}),

(84)

where the first curvature correction is

\displaystyle H_{2}({\mathbf{q}}^{0},{\mathbf{p}}^{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{6m_{i}}\Bigl(\|{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}\|^{2}\|{\mathbf{p}}^{0}_{i}\|^{2}-({\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{p}}^{0}_{i})^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}m_{i}m_{j}\left(\frac{\|{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}-{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{j}\|}{3}-\frac{\|{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}\|^{2}\|{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{j}\|^{2}-({\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{j})^{2}}{6\,\|{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{i}-{\mathbf{q}}^{0}_{j}\|^{3}}\right).

(85)

In particular, on any compact subset

4.2
The
SE(2)
-symmetric
n
-body problem REs and RPOs

Recall that
{\mathbf{b}}_{1},{\mathbf{b}}_{2}
represent the infinitesimal generators of planar translations and
{\mathbf{b}}_{3}
the infinitesimal generator of planar rotations, that is, the standard basis of
\mathfrak{se}(2)
obtained in the contraction limit.

Proposition 10

(Vanishing linear momentum for planar REs)
.

Let
{\mathbf{z}}^{0}
be a relative equilibrium of the planar Newtonian
n
–body problem for the full
SE(2)
–action,
with generator
\bm{\xi}^{0}=a{\mathbf{b}}_{1}+b{\mathbf{b}}_{2}+\omega{\mathbf{b}}_{3}
and
\omega\neq 0
.
Then the total linear momentum vanishes:

\displaystyle{\mu_{1}:=\langle\bm{\mu}^{0},{\mathbf{b}}_{1}\rangle=0,}

\mu_{2}:=\langle\bm{\mu}^{0},{\mathbf{b}}_{2}\rangle=0.

Consequently, the momentum value is
\bm{\mu}^{0}=(0,0,L)\in\mathfrak{se}(2)^{*}

with
L=\langle\bm{\mu}^{0},{\mathbf{b}}_{3}\rangle
the total angular momentum,
and the centre of mass coincides with the centre of rotation.

Proof.

Since
{\mathbf{z}}^{0}
is an RE with generator
\bm{\xi}^{0}
, equivariance of the momentum map gives

\mathrm{Ad}^{*}_{\exp(t\bm{\xi}^{0})}\bm{\mu}^{0}=\bm{\mu}^{0}
for all
t
, hence
infinitesimally
\mathrm{ad}^{*}_{\bm{\xi}^{0}}\bm{\mu}^{0}=0
.

For any test element
\bm{\eta}=c{\mathbf{b}}_{1}+d{\mathbf{b}}_{2}+\gamma{\mathbf{b}}_{3}\in\mathfrak{g}_{0}
the
\varepsilon=0

brackets give

\displaystyle[\bm{\xi}^{0},\bm{\eta}]_{0}=(b\gamma-\omega d)\,{\mathbf{b}}_{1}+(\omega c-a\gamma)\,{\mathbf{b}}_{2},

so that

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\mu}^{0},[\bm{\xi}^{0},\bm{\eta}]_{0}\rangle=c\,\omega\mu_{2}-d\,\omega\mu_{1}+\gamma\,(b\mu_{1}-a\mu_{2}).

Setting this to zero for all
(c,d,\gamma)
yields the three conditions

\omega\mu_{2}=0
,
\omega\mu_{1}=0
, and
b\mu_{1}-a\mu_{2}=0
.
Since
\omega\neq 0
the first two conditions force
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=0
.
∎

Remark 9

(Linear momentum on the curved surface)
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, the analogous coadjoint computation using the bracket

[{\mathbf{b}}_{1},{\mathbf{b}}_{2}]_{\varepsilon}=\sigma\varepsilon^{2}{\mathbf{b}}_{3}
gives the modified stationarity conditions

\mu_{1}=\sigma\frac{\varepsilon^{2}a\,L}{\omega}\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\,\mu_{2}=\sigma\frac{\varepsilon^{2}b\,L}{\omega},

(87)

where
\bm{\xi}^{\varepsilon}=a{\mathbf{b}}_{1}+b{\mathbf{b}}_{2}+\omega{\mathbf{b}}_{3}
.
Thus for an RE on the curved surface the “linear momentum” components

\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
can be
O(\varepsilon^{2})
nonzero.
Geometrically, this reflects the fact that the curved-space momentum vector
need not point exactly along
Oz
;
its tilt away from
Oz
manifests as nonzero
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
of order
\varepsilon^{2}
.
In the flat limit
\varepsilon\to 0
these components vanish, consistently with
Proposition
10
above.

Remark 10

(Reduction in stages and the role of linear momentum)
.

In the case of planar motion, the
SE(2)=\mathbb{R}^{2}\rtimes SO(2)
symmetry reduction by stages (
[
Marsden & al. 2007b
]
) is applied as follows:

Stage 1:
Reduce by translations
\mathbb{R}^{2}
at momentum
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
.
The centre of mass decouples and drifts at constant velocity
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})/M
, where
M
is the total mass,
M=\sum\limits_{i=1,...n}m_{i}.

In Jacobi coordinates, the internal dynamics is independent of
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
.

Stage 2:
The residual
SO(2)
acts on the translation-reduced phase space.
Lagrange, Euler, and
n
–gon configurations appear as REs of this
reduced

SO(2)
–action,
for
any
value of
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
.

We note the following:

•

If
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=(0,0)
, the reduced RE lifts to an RE of the full
SE(2)
–action,
with generator
\bm{\xi}^{0}=\omega{\mathbf{b}}_{3}
.
The entire configuration rotates rigidly about the (fixed) centre of mass.
This is the setting of Theorem
7
.

•

If
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\neq(0,0)
, the reduced RE lifts to an
RPO
of the full
SE(2)
–action.
The configuration rotates about its centre of mass, while the centre of mass drifts uniformly.
After one full revolution (period
T=2\pi/\omega
), the configuration has returned to its original shape and orientation
but has been displaced by
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\,T/M
.
This is an RPO with translational drift, and its continuation is determined by Theorem
9
.

In either case, no passage to Jacobi coordinates is needed to apply the continuation results:
Theorem
7
handles the
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=0
case directly in the full phase space,
while Theorem
9
handles
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\neq 0

Remark 11

(Translational drift becomes rotational drift)
.

As mentioned above in Remark
10
, in the planar problem a RPO arising from a reduced RE with nonzero total linear momentum
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\neq 0

performs a translational drift.
On
M^{\sigma}_{1/\varepsilon}
, Euclidean translations do not exist: the drift element
g^{\varepsilon}\in G_{\varepsilon}

is necessarily a
rotation
. This is consistent with the Inönü–Wigner structure:
the translational drift
\exp(t_{0}(a{\mathbf{b}}_{1}+b{\mathbf{b}}_{2}))
in
SE(2)
corresponds, for
\varepsilon>0
,
to the corresponding curved-space isometry generated by
a{\mathbf{b}}_{1}+b{\mathbf{b}}_{2}
, which is a small rotation when
\sigma=+1
and a small boost when
\sigma=-1

about a nearly horizontal axis.
The commutator
[{\mathbf{b}}_{1},{\mathbf{b}}_{2}]_{\varepsilon}=\sigma\varepsilon^{2}{\mathbf{b}}_{3}
further implies that the interplay
between the internal rotation (about
Oz
) and the drift rotation (about a horizontal axis) produces
an
O(\varepsilon^{2})
precession of the overall angular momentum vector.

Application to the figure-eight choreography.

In the planar Newtonian three-body problem with equal masses and centre of mass fixed, a special periodic solution (zero total angular momentum) is the figure-eight choreography

5
Concluding remarks

The continuation results established in this paper rest on three geometric
ingredients: the exponential chart, the Inönü–Wigner scaling of
the symmetry, and the local slice construction of Lemma
6
.
Together, these reduce all continuation questions to a fixed symplectic
manifold on which standard implicit function theorem arguments apply.
Our
continuation theorems apply to any smooth family of
G_{\varepsilon}
-invariant
Hamiltonians of the form (
59
), and the
Newtonian
n
-body problem is treated as the primary example.
All results are local in nature, and are applicable to compact
collision-free subsets of the exponential-chart phase space
corresponding to configurations remaining in a bounded planar region.

A natural further research direction concerns the behaviour near
collisions. Since the metric on
M^{\sigma}_{1/\varepsilon}
is locally
Euclidean at small scales and curvature effects enter only at higher
order, the regularised vector field near a collision at the North pole
should be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-small perturbation of the corresponding
planar regularised field. One therefore expects that the invariant sets arising in the planar three-body problem
via blow-up techniques, as in the work of
[
McGehee 1974
]
and

[
Devaney 1980
]
to persist for

\varepsilon
sufficiently small. Making this precise requires verifying
that the regularisation procedure commutes appropriately with the
exponential chart and the curvature expansion.
Finally, one may consider the numerical continuation of specific REs and RPOs and choreographies.
[
Bengochea & al. 2022
]
accomplished this for the Lagrangian RE in the
SO(2)
symmetric case. In our full symmetry context, the work of
[
C. Wulff 2008
]
may be relevant although the non-compactness of
SE(2)
may lead to additional challenges. These topics are left for future work.
