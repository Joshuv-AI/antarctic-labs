Green functions of the Regge-Wheeler and Teukolsky equations in Schwarzschild spacetime
David Q. Aruquipa, Marc Casals

Abstract
We present a calculation of the full retarded Green functions of the Regge-Wheeler and Teukolsky equations obeyed by gravitational field perturbations of Schwarzschild spacetime. We perform the calculations for spacetime points along: (i) a timelike circular geodesic (where null-separated points are not at caustics); and (ii) a static worldline (where null-separated points are at caustics). These Green functions show a 4 4 -fold singularity structure away from caustics, and 2 2 -fold at caustics (similarly to the case of scalar field perturbations, which we also reproduce). Physical oscillations near the singularities appear in the gravitational case, which were not present in the scalar case. We obtain our results by developing various numerical and analytical methods.



I
Introduction

The time evolution of field perturbations of a black hole background spacetime can be obtained via the retarded Green function (GF)
of the wave equation obeyed by the perturbations.
The GF is thus a very important object for understanding black hole properties and
field propagation.
Classically,
the GF is
valuable, for example, for analysing the stability properties of black hole spacetimes (e.g.,
20
);
calculating the self-force (e.g.,
66
for a review) on a point particle moving on a background spacetime via the so-called MiSaTaQuWa equation
70
;
71
;
understanding the merger-ringdown stage of a gravitational waveform (e.g.,
9
;
47
;
76
).
Also, quantum communication between particle detectors on a background spacetime is determined, to leading order in the coupling with the field,
by worldline integrals of the GF
44
;
12
.

Let us henceforth focus on the case that the background spacetime is that of a spherically-symmetric, Schwarzschild black hole.
In the particular case of scalar field perturbations of Schwarzschild spacetime, the GF has already been used for the analysis
of the time evolution of initial data (e.g.,
50
;
29
;
27

19

79

46

The calculation (and application) of the GF in the case of
gravitational
perturbations of Schwarzschild spacetime, which are the relevant ones for modelling black hole inspirals, is, however, significantly more scarce.

In
Schwarzschild spacetime, the components of the metric perturbation obey
coupled
equations.
Unfortunately, it is the GF of these coupled equations which appears in the MiSaTaQuWa equation for the gravitational self-force.
However, there exist two types of quantities, involving the metric perturbation and its derivatives, which do obey decoupled and separable equations.
One type are the Regge-Wheeler (RW)
72

85

84

The second type are the Weyl scalars, which are projections of the Weyl tensor
onto a null tetrad and obey the so-called (Bardeen-Press-)Teukolsky equation (BPT)

To the best of our knowledge, the calculation of the GF of
the
four
-dimensional

only the specific
\ell=2
multipolar mode of the GF of the RW equation

64

In this paper, we present, for the first time,
the calculation of the
full
(i.e., after summing over “all”
\ell
-modes, if performing a multipolar decomposition) GF of both the RW (for RW spin
s=2
) and BPT (for BPT spin
s=-2
) equations.

The calculation of
GFs is
plagued
with a myriad of technical difficulties, mainly due to their distributional character and divergences.
GFs are functions of two spacetime points and
it is well-known that
they diverge
when the two
points
coincide
39

Importantly, GFs also
diverge when the two points are separated but connected by a null geodesic

;
42

It has been shown

;

4

Our results in this paper show that these
2

4

We show this by explicitly calculating these GFs for the two spacetime points on the following two settings: (i) along a timelike circular geodesic (where divergences of the GF are not at caustics); and (ii) along a static worldline (where divergences of the GF are at caustics).

In order to achieve our calculation of the RW and BPT GFs, we use and develop a combination of analytical and numerical methods.
As for the numerical methods, we use
numerical evolution of characteristic initial data in the time domain as well as
(inverse-)Fourier integrals.
As for the analytical methods, we obtain the equations satisfied by the biscalars in the so-called Hadamard form

;
36

We do not derive such an expansion for the BPT GF and, because of that,
while we achieve the calculation of the RW GF throughout the whole time domain,
we do not achieve the calculation of the BPT GF for points very near coincidence – we leave that for future work.
Another analytical method that we use is
the calculation of BPT
\ell
-modes
via a spectroscopical decomposition on the complex-frequency plane from the
poles (quasinormal modes, QNMs) and
a branch cut of the Fourier modes; we only do it for the dominant contributions from the QNM and branch cut, and as a check for one
\ell
-mode of our Fourier-integral results.

The Hadamard form may be used as the integrand in Eq. (

Within the context of the worldline integral in Eq. (

We next proceed to describe methods for calculating the direct and tail terms in the RW Hadamard form, Eq. (

III.1.1
Direct term in the Hadamard form for RW GF

Eqs. (
11
) and (
III.1
) show that the direct term
U\delta_{+}
has support only on the lightcone, where it is singular. Despite this singularity, it is useful in certain situations to be able to calculate the Hadamard coefficient
U
or to provide a multipolar decomposition of the whole direct term. For example,
U\delta_{+}
was used in Ref.
82
to model the direct image of a source near a (rotating) black hole.
Also, it was shown in Ref.
45
that

is a key component in determining the signal strength of a quantum communication channel.
In its turn, Ref.
21
showed that subtracting the multipoles of the direct term from the multipoles of the full GF, greatly improves the
\ell
-sum convergence for calculating the GF from its multipoles for spacetime points near –but not exactly at– coincidence (which, the integral in Eq. (
10

The
\ell
-multipoles of the direct term may also be used for helping to model more accurately the early stage of the ringdown of a gravitational waveform (e.g.,
60

2

76

The direct term being spin-independent, methods for calculating it and its
\ell
-modes have already been developed for the spin-0 field and are readily valid for general RW spin

In this subsection, we briefly describe already-existing methods for calculating the
direct term and its
\ell

An approach proposed in

\displaystyle\frac{\textrm{d}\Delta^{1/2}}{\textrm{d}\tau}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\tau}{Q^{\mu}}_{\mu}\Delta^{1/2},

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{\textrm{d}{Q^{\mu}}_{\nu}}{\textrm{d}\tau}=

\displaystyle\,u^{\alpha}\left({Q^{\mu}}_{\beta}{\Gamma^{\beta}}_{\alpha\nu}-{\Gamma^{\mu}}_{\alpha\beta}{Q^{\beta}}_{\nu}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\tau}\left({Q^{\mu}}_{\alpha}{Q^{\alpha}}_{\nu}+{Q^{\mu}}_{\nu}\right)-\tau{R^{\mu}}_{\alpha\nu\beta}u^{\alpha}u^{\beta}.

(25)

Here,

{Q^{\mu}}_{\nu}\equiv{\delta^{\mu}}_{\nu}-{\sigma^{\mu}}_{\nu}

\left[\Delta^{1/2}\right]=1

III.1.2
Tail term in the Hadamard form for RW GF

In order to calculate the Hadamard tail term
V_{s}
,
we carry out an expansion in the coordinate distances. By considering the symmetries of Schwarzschild spacetime, we may write the expansion
61

V_{s}=V_{s}(x,x^{\prime})=\sum\limits_{i,j,k=0}^{\infty}{}_{s}v_{ijk}(r)\left(\Delta t\right)^{2i}(1-\cos\gamma)^{j}\left(\Delta r\right)^{k},

(29)

where

even inside a normal neighbourhood

In practise, of course, the infinite sums in (

Thus, within this approach, the QL region is given by the region where the truncated expansion in Eq. (

In the spin-0 case, the so-called Hadamard-WKB method for calculating

was proposed in Ref.
3

61
and implemented in Ref.
17
.
Within this method, one obtains the coefficients

by first Euclideanizing the metric, then decomposing the Euclidean Green function into Fourier- and
\ell
-modes, and finally carrying out WKB asymptotics for large-
\omega
and/or large-
\ell

We availed ourselves of the publicly available code provided in
17

. With this generalized code, which we make publicly available in
1

necessary to obtain Eq. (

Additionally, assuming that the points
x

lie on a worldline

with light-crossings as described in Sec.

, we construct a Padé approximant for Eq. (

We first isolate the singularity of the GF, and of

,

(30)

for some

,

corresponding to the first light-crossing, which marks the end of the normal neighborhood of

\mathcal{V}_{s}\equiv(\Delta t-t_{\textrm{NN}})^{N}\sum\limits_{i,j,k=0}^{\infty}{}_{s}v_{ijk}(r)\Delta t^{2i}(1-\cos\gamma)^{j}(r-r^{\prime})^{k}.

(31)

The form of (

of the singularity at the first light-crossing given in Eq. (

Next, given some fixed point

(and so fixed radius

and

as corresponding to the worldline

of interest
on the right hand side of Eq. (

\Delta t
(with

for the numerator and

for the denominator.
The result of using such Padé approximant of

in Eq. (

may extend the region where

The choices of

We note that this Padé approximant that we use is slightly different from that used in previous literature like

III.2
Calculation of RW GF in the Distant Past

In the DP, we exploit the spherical symmetry of Schwarzschild spacetime in order to perform a multipolar decomposition of the RW GF as

\displaystyle{}_{s}G_{\textrm{ret}}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{r\,r^{\prime}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}{}_{s}\mathcal{G}_{\ell}(x,x^{\prime}),

(32)

\displaystyle{}_{s}\mathcal{G}_{\ell}(x,x^{\prime})\equiv 4\pi{}_{s}G_{\ell}(r,r^{\prime};\Delta t)\sum\limits_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)Y_{\ell m}^{*}(\theta^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(2\ell+1){}_{s}G_{\ell}(r,r^{\prime};\Delta t)P_{\ell}(\cos\gamma),

where
{}_{s}G_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}
are the
\ell
-modes of
{}_{s}G_{\textrm{ret}}
and

r_{*}\equiv r+2M\ln\left(\frac{r}{2M}-1\right)\in\mathbb{R},

(33)

is the so-called tortoise coordinate.
The spherical harmonics

are normalized so that

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)\,Y^{*}_{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(\theta,\varphi)\textrm{d}\Omega=\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}.

(34)

By substituting Eq. (
32

5

and using

\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}P_{\ell}(\cos\gamma)=-\ell(\ell+1)P_{\ell}(\cos\gamma),

(35)

it can be seen that the
\ell
-modes of the GF satisfy the following (1+1)-dimensional partial differential equation (PDE):

\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{*}^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}-4\mathcal{Q}_{s}(r)\right){}_{s}G_{\ell}=-\delta(\Delta t)\delta(\Delta r_{*}),

(36)

where
\Delta r_{*}\equiv r_{*}-r_{*}^{\prime}
and

\mathcal{Q}_{s}(r)\equiv\frac{f(r)}{4r^{2}}\left(\ell(\ell+1)+\frac{2M(1-s^{2})}{r}\right).

(37)

The boundary conditions for Eq. (
36

{}_{s}G_{\textrm{ret}}
obeys.

We describe two methods for calculating the
\ell
-modes

Full RW GF in the DP

Once the
\ell
-modes
{}_{s}G_{\ell}

In practice, the infinite sum in Eq. (
32

This produces two issues. First, similarly to when truncating the Fourier integral at some finite value

56

sharply cutting off the infinite

We similarly smooth out
these spurious oscillations by introducing a smoothing factor in the summand, which in this case is

\exp\left(-\frac{\ell^{2}}{2\ell_{\textrm{cut}}^{2}}\right),

(57)

for some appropriate choice of the parameter
\ell_{\textrm{cut}}
. The smoothing factor (
57
) was already used in
19
;
18
;
21
;
25
;
80
;
15
;
46

19
;
40
;
18
;
80
. A second issue with truncating Eq. (
32

x=x^{\prime}
), the truncated mode sum approaches the Dirac-
\delta
singularity in
{}_{s}G_{\textrm{ret}}
at
x=x^{\prime}
(see Eq. (
11
)) as a Gaussian distribution which narrows as more
\ell
-modes are included in the sum.
Here is where the method of matched expansions and the QL calculation comes –partly– to the rescue:
the DP is then the region where the truncated sum in Eq. (
32

V_{s}
, and so without including the Dirac-
\delta
at coincidence, and so with no smearing issues) in the QL region.
However, not only the divergence of the GF at coincidence is smeared, but also are its global divergences at light-crossings.
In particular, the
\delta(\sigma)
divergences in
Eq. (
2
) become smeared into a Gaussian distribution (see Eq. 6.3 in

\text{PV}(1/\sigma)

19

80

In order to extend the region where the DP calculation of the GF is accurate enough, to as closely as possible to coincidence, we implemented a further technique originally introduced in
21
. This technique consists of subtracting
G^{\textrm{dir}}_{\ell}
from
{}_{s}G_{\ell}
in the mode sum in Eq. (

-sum at some finite value

-sum in (

-sum in Eq. (

IV
Bardeen-Press-Teukolsky Green function

The so-called Bardeen-Press-Teukolsky (BPT) equation

;
77
;
7
, containing a BPT spin
8
8

8

In BPT, “
s

s
parameter, is obeyed by scalars
\Psi_{s}
which represent scalar (
s=0
), electromagnetic (
s=\pm 1
) and gravitational (
s=\pm 2
) field perturbations of Schwarzschild spacetime.
In the case
s=0
, the BPT and RW equations coincide and are just the scalar wave equation.
In the case
s\neq 0
, the various BPT scalars

\Psi_{s}

are constructed within the Newman-Penrose formalism
57
by projecting the various field tensors onto a Newman-Penrose tetrad.
In the gravitational case of interest here, it is

\Psi_{2}=\psi_{0}
and
\Psi_{-2}=r^{4}\psi_{0}
, where
\psi_{0}
and
\psi_{4}
are Weyl scalars –which are projections of the Weyl tensor– and
\Psi_{\pm 2}
are gauge-invariant.
The BPT GF
{}_{s}G_{\textrm{ret}}^{T}
obeys the BPT GF equation:

\hat{\mathcal{T}}{}_{s}G_{\textrm{ret}}^{T}(x,x^{\prime})=-4\pi\delta_{4}(x,x^{\prime}),

(62)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}\equiv\Box+\frac{2s}{r^{2}}\left(\frac{3M-r}{f}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+(r-M)\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right.+\quad\quad\quad

\displaystyle\left.i\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi}+\frac{1-s\cot^{2}\theta}{2}\right).

(63)

Similarly to the RW GF, the causality condition on the BPT GF requires that
{}_{s}G_{\textrm{ret}}^{T}(x,x^{\prime})
is the solution of Eq. (
62
) such that it is equal to zero if the field point
x
is not in the causal future of the base point
x^{\prime}
.

An important difference between the BPT operator
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
in (
IV.3.1
) and the RW operator

in (
6

\Box
(thus establishing their hyperbolic character), the first-order derivatives in the RW operator coincide with those in
\Box
but not with those in the BPT operator.
In fact, the coefficient of a first-order derivative (namely, that of
\partial_{\varphi}
)
in

is purely imaginary. The fact that a coefficient is complex comes from the fact that elements of the Newman-Penrose tetrad on which the Weyl tensor is projected to construct the BPT scalars
\Psi_{s}
are complex-valued.
This of course means that the BPT GF
{}_{s}G_{\textrm{ret}}^{T}
will generally be complex-valued, as opposed to the RW GF, which was real-valued.

It will prove useful later to re-express the BPT operator in
(
IV.3.1

\hat{\mathcal{T}}=\Box+A^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}+B,

(64)

where

\displaystyle A^{\mu}\equiv\,

\displaystyle\frac{2s}{r^{2}}\left(\left(-r+\frac{M}{f}\right)\delta^{\mu}_{t}+(r-M)\delta^{\mu}_{r}+i\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin^{2}\theta}\delta^{\mu}_{\varphi}\right),

\displaystyle B\equiv\,

\displaystyle\frac{s}{r^{2}}(1-s\cot^{2}\theta).

(65)

In the next subsection we describe the Chandrasekhar transformation between homogeneous solutions of the RW and BPT equations. In the subsequent two subsections we
develop the Hadamard form for the BPT GF in the QL region and present a method (in the frequency domain via the Chandrasekhar transformation) for calculating the BPT GF in the DP.
In the fourth, last subsection, we present our calculation of the BPT GF and its radial derivative in the same settings as for the RW GF: a timelike circular geodesic and a static worldline. We note that, in this BPT case, we do not carry out a calculation of the GF in the QL (nor a calculation of its direct part) – we leave that for future work.

IV.1
Chandrasekhar transformation

Remarkably, Chandrasekhar
30
found that, for
s\leq 0
, solutions
\Psi_{{}_{s}}
of the homogeneous version of the BPT Eq. (
62

\hat{\mathcal{T}}\Psi_{{}_{s}}=0
) are related to solutions

of the homogeneous version of the RW Eq. (

\hat{\mathcal{R}}\,\chi_{{}_{s}}=0

(66)

where

(67)

is referred to as the Chandrasekhar operator
and

\eth=-\left(\partial_{\theta}+\frac{i}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi}-s\cot\theta\right)

(68)

is the spin-raising (angular) operator. Similarly, the spin-lowering (angular) operator is

\bar{\eth}=-\left(\partial_{\theta}-\frac{i}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi}+s\cot\theta\right).

(69)

When applied to spin-weighted spherical harmonics
{}_{s}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)

38
, the spin-raising and lowering operators yield

\displaystyle\eth\left({}_{s}Y_{\ell m}\right)=\,

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)-s(s+1)}\,{}_{s+1}Y_{\ell m},

(70)

\displaystyle\bar{\eth}\left({}_{s}Y_{\ell m}\right)=\,

\displaystyle-\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)-s(s-1)}\,{}_{s-1}Y_{\ell m},

(71)

and

\bar{\eth}\,\eth\,{}_{s}Y_{\ell m}-\left(\ell(\ell+1)-s(s+1)\right){}_{s}Y_{\ell m}.

(72)

We choose the normalization of the spin-weighted spherical harmonics so that the orthonormality relation is

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}{}_{s}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)\,{}_{s}Y^{*}_{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(\theta,\varphi)\textrm{d}\Omega=\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}

(73)

and the completeness relation is

\sum_{\ell=|s|}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}{}_{s}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)\,{}_{s}Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\theta^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})=\delta(\cos\theta-\cos\theta^{\prime})\delta(\varphi-\varphi^{\prime}).

(74)

Constructing BPT solutions via the Chandrasekhar operator is advantageous because it maps the BPT equation to the RW equation, which has a short-range potential. This simplifies the imposition of boundary conditions at the horizon and at infinity, improves numerical stability, and facilitates both analytical and numerical treatments of the solutions.

V
Discussion

In this paper, we have achieved the calculation of the
full
spin-2 RW and BPT GFs.
We have seen that the singularity structures of these GFs follow the same
4
- and
2
-fold patterns as the spin-0 GF, given in, respectively, Eqs. (
2
) and (
II
), for when the divergences occur away from caustics and at caustics.
This result for the global divergences is probably not surprising, given that all these GFs admit the Hadamard form in normal neighbourhoods (even if the direct and tail Hadamard bitensors may differ between these GFs), so that they all display the same form for the local divergence.
On the other hand, new physical oscillations near the light-crossing divergences appear for the spin-2 RW GF, which were not present in the spin-0 case, and which become enhanced for the spin-2 BPT GF.

While for RW we achieved the calculation in both the QL region and DP,
for BPT
we achieved it
in a DP but only gave the equations obeyed by the Hadamard biscalars in a QL region.
Thus, in the BPT case, more
work still needs to be done with the Hadamard form for the calculation in a QL region to be matched with a DP calculation.
We next give some ideas of how that could be done.

One idea is to make progress on the Hadamard form for BPT.
In particular, and following the calculations in RW, it would be good to do the following two calculations in BPT.
First, given the improvement in extending the DP in the RW case by subtracting the
\ell
-modes of the direct part from those of the GF, it would be important to be able to calculate the
\ell
-modes of the direct part in the BPT case and subtract them from the full BPT GF.
Second, in order to calculate the BPT GF in the QL region, one could try to calculate
V_{s}^{T}
by carrying out an expansion in the coordinate distance, similarly to the way it is done in the RW case in
61
;
17
for spin-0 and in Sec.
III.1.2
here for spin-2.

A valuable, alternative method for calculating GFs and which offers some level of analytical control was originally suggested in Ref.
50
and was already indicated in Sec.
IV.3.2
. It consists of first decomposing the GF into Fourier modes, then continuing the integration contour on to the complex frequency plane and finally using the residue theorem to pick up the contributions from the poles of the GF Fourier modes.
This method is long-known to be very useful in the DP region, where the contour is closed on the lower plane and contributions to the GF come from poles (quasinormal modes) and a branch cut integral along the negative imaginary axis.
Indeed, this method was applied to calculate the full scalar GF in the DP in
19
and in App.
E
here we have applied it to the one BPT
\ell=2
mode using only the dominant contributions from the branch cut an QNM.
Interestingly, a variation of this method has been recently suggested in
5

and applied to one
\ell
(=2) mode of the spin-2 RW GF in
76
to calculate the GF in a QL region (rather than DP). In this variation, the contour is closed on the
upper
(instead of lower) plane and the contribution to the GF comes from a branch cut along the positive imaginary axis (which arises in the Schwarzschild limit of the so-called Matsubara poles in the case of Schwarzschild-de Sitter spacetime). We leave the calculation of the
full
GF in the QL region using a ‘Matsubara branch cut’ for future work.

Going beyond the calculation of the full GFs and looking at applications, an important one is in the calculation of the self-force. In the scalar case, the self-force may be obtained via the worldline integral in (
10
), where the GF has been ‘regularized’ by subtracting from it a singular (Detweiler-Whiting) GF
33
;
66
. While a similar worldline integral is known for calculating the
gravitational
self-force from a regularized GF of the
metric perturbation
equations (in the Lorenz gauge), it is not yet known how the RW or BPT GFs should be regularized in order to obtain (via some metric reconstruction procedure
31
) a regularized metric perturbation (in some radiation-like gauge). We leave the important task of appropriate regularization of the RW and BPT GFs for gravitational self-force purposes for future work.
