Abstract
. Industrial advertising recommender models are continuously improved through architecture evolution . Upgrades such as RankMixer, TokenMixer-Large, and MixFormer show that better structures remain a key source of quality and business gains. Yet developing such upgrades in production is expert-intensive and difficult to scale. Existing automation is insufficient: AutoML mainly tunes hyper-parameters, while effective gains often require cross-module changes under strict constraints; generic LLM coding agents optimize for runnable code, but runnable code does not imply a valid recommender architecture. Candidates may pass local tests while causing silent failures that degrade performance. We present NOVA , a level-aware agent harness for verification-aware architecture evolution. NOVA uses an architecture gradient , an SGD-inspired, non-differentiable update signal that aggregates prior modifications, verification diagnostics, metric feedback, and trajectory memory to guide the next modification. A verification cascade checks structure semantics, local executability, offline effectiveness, and online impact; invalid candidates are blocked early, with failure patterns recorded as forbidden directions. L1–L4 task-level control matches automation to task complexity and risk, routing high-risk tasks to Copilot for human oversight. Deployed in an industrial advertising system, NOVA achieves the highest effective pass rate on L2 ScaleUp and L3 Literature-to-Production tasks ( 54.5 % 54.5\% and 60.0 % 60.0\% ), reduces silent failures compared with coding-agent baselines, and shortens one literature-to-production cycle by over 13 × 13\times in human-attended time. In online A/B testing, the selected L3 candidate improves GMV on three pCVR objectives by + 1.25 % +1.25\% , + 1.70 % +1.70\% , and + 2.02 % +2.02\% , while reducing pCVR bias by 58.8 % 58.8\% , 66.7 % 66.7\% , and 37.3 % 37.3\% .

Introduction

The evolution of large-scale recommendation models has largely been a history of architectural innovation: from early feature-crossing and shallow models such as LR, FM and FFM
(

;

;
10

, to deep architectures with explicit memory and cross networks such as Wide & Deep, DeepFM, and DCN

, to user-behavior sequence modeling such as DIN, DIEN, and SIM
(
30
;
29
;

)
, and more recently to ranking backbones that introduce Transformer-attention mechanisms and tokenized interactions, such as RankMixer, TokenMixer-Large, and MixFormer-style interaction models
(

;
9
;

;
8
;

. Across this trajectory, a clear pattern emerges: as simple feature-engineering improvements become harder to obtain, further gains in recommendation quality and business impact increasingly depend on more expressive backbones and richer interaction mechanisms. This makes architecture evolution a natural target for automation in industrial recommender development.

Despite its critical importance, architectural evolution in production still faces numerous challenges, ranging from combinatorial parameter tuning under capacity budgets to research-paper reproduction. Each demands expert intuition to navigate vast or even unbounded search spaces, careful assessment of structural compatibility, and validation through extensive offline-to-online pipelines. The process remains difficult to scale due to its operational complexity in industrial production environments and heavy reliance on expert judgment.

Existing AutoML and generic coding-agent approaches only automate fragments of this workflow. AutoML methods
(
20
;
5
;
1
;
12
)
mainly optimize local variables such as learning rate, hidden size, embedding dimension, or layer depth. However, effective model improvements often require cross-module topology changes. For example, a model update may upgrade target attention into joint Seq-Token and Non-Seq-Token modeling, redesign the logit-fusion path, or replace a standard residual connection with an AttentionRes module
(
19
)
. Such changes must further preserve input-output shape compatibility, dtype consistency, feature dependencies, training stability, and online inference compatibility across the production pipeline.

LLMs have greatly improved generic coding agents, enabling them to generate and modify code according to user intent. Their success, however, is usually measured by software-level signals such as compilation success and unit-test passing
(
25
;
23
)
. In industrial recommender systems, an implementation may run without errors while still violating architecture semantics, for example by incorrectly removing sequence masking, degenerating self-attention into a simple MLP, or altering the logit-fusion path. Such runnable-but-negative candidates can degrade offline and online recommendation performance, including AUC, calibration, and business metrics; we refer to them as
silent failures
.

Recent self-evolving recommender systems
(
21
)
have shown that LLM agents can participate in production model optimization by proposing model changes and validating them through offline and online feedback. NOVA builds on this direction, but focuses on auditable architecture-level modifications rather than general model-change proposals. To make these modifications effective under production constraints, NOVA introduces two key mechanisms. First, it organizes architecture evolution as a feedback-driven search: previous modifications, metric changes, verification diagnostics, and trajectory memory are aggregated into an
architecture gradient
that guides the next modification direction. Second, it addresses
silent failures
by checking architecture semantics before expensive training and feeding diagnostics back as reusable forbidden directions. As a result, verification not only rejects the current invalid candidate, but also shapes later search by discouraging similar failure patterns. Figure
1
illustrates how NOVA connects architecture generation, verification, evaluation, and feedback into a production agent harness.

In summary, this paper makes three contributions:

•

Architecture-gradient-guided evolution.

We present NOVA, a level-aware agent harness for verification-aware architecture generation in industrial recommender systems. NOVA introduces the
architecture gradient
, an SGD-inspired, non-differentiable update signal that converts prior modifications, verification outcomes, metric feedback, and trajectory memory into the next modification direction. Combined with L1–L4 task levels and AutoRun/Copilot control, the architecture gradient enables NOVA to progressively generate higher-quality architecture candidates while keeping the evolution process auditable and risk-aware in production.

•

Silent-failure-aware verification.
We introduce a multi-stage verification cascade that catches runnable but structurally invalid candidates before costly training or online trials. Its diagnostics are converted into reusable forbidden directions, making verification part of the search signal rather than a post-hoc filter.

•

Production deployment.

We deploy NOVA in a large-scale industrial advertising system serving over one billion users and evaluate it against human expert loops, AutoML, and generic coding-agent baselines. On the L3 Literature-to-Production task, NOVA achieves
86.7\%
LPR and
60.0\%
EPR, substantially outperforming coding-agent baselines and more than doubling the EPR of the human expert loop. It also shortens one Literature-to-Production cycle by over
13\times
in human-attended time. Online A/B testing further shows that the selected candidate delivers positive business impact in live traffic, improving GMV across the top three pCVR objectives while reducing prediction bias.

Related Work

Related work around NOVA spans four lines: self-evolving and agent-optimized recommender systems, LLM-feedback-based optimization methods, AutoML/NAS/HPO, and coding agents. We begin with the closest agentic recommender systems, followed by broader methods that motivate NOVA’s design.

Self-evolving recommender systems.

Recent agentic recommender systems show that LLM agents can participate in production model optimization. YouTube’s
Self-Evolving Recommendation System

(
21
)
uses offline and online agents to generate, evaluate, and deploy model-improvement proposals. AgenticRecTune
(
24
)
and Meta’s Ranking Engineer Agent (REA)
(
11
)
further demonstrate the value of agent-assisted configuration tuning and ads-ranking experimentation. NOVA builds on this line of work with a focus on auditable architecture-level modifications, including changes to model structure, feature configuration, and interaction modules under production constraints. The main distinction is how verification is used: NOVA performs architecture-semantic checks before training and feeds the resulting diagnostics into the architecture gradient as forbidden directions. This allows later iterations to avoid repeated structural failure patterns and reduces runnable-but-negative candidates during architecture evolution.

LLM feedback as gradient.

ProTeGi
(
16
)
and TextGrad
(
27
)
use LLM-written feedback as a textual gradient for improving prompts, code, and other artifacts. NOVA adopts the high-level idea of feedback-driven updates, but applies it to constrained production architecture evolution. In this setting, the update signal must refer to concrete architecture modifications and aggregate multiple sources: the previous modification, verification diagnostics, metric changes, and trajectory memory. Therefore, NOVA’s architecture gradient is not a standalone textual suggestion, but a structured signal for selecting the next feasible modification. We include a feedback-ablated variant in Section
5
to isolate the value of feeding diagnostics and historical outcomes into the next search direction.

AutoML, NAS, and HPO

Traditional AutoML, neural architecture search, and hyper-parameter optimization search over hyper-parameters or restricted operator spaces
(

;
5
;
1
;
14
;
12
;
17
)
. They are effective for local, well-defined choices, but industrial recommender architecture evolution often requires cross-module topology changes under strict production constraints. NOVA therefore uses modifications to model structure, feature configuration, and interaction modules as the search unit rather than only hyperparameters or pre-defined operators.

Coding agents.

General-purpose coding agents such as SWE-agent and OpenHands
(
25

optimize for software-level correctness, such as compilation success, unit-test passing, and task completion. They are useful for code generation and repair, but they do not verify whether a runnable implementation preserves recommender-specific architectural semantics. NOVA targets this gap by adding architecture-semantic verification and feeding its diagnostics back into the search process.

3.
Problem Formulation

NOVA studies automated architecture evolution for production recommender systems. Given an existing production model, the goal is to iteratively propose, verify, and evaluate feasible architecture modifications that improve recommendation quality and business metrics under production constraints. These modifications form the search units of NOVA and cover model topology, feature routing, interaction modules, and structural hyper-parameters.

Architecture state and feasible modification space.

NOVA represents each candidate architecture as
A_{t}=(G_{t},\phi_{t},F_{t})
, where
G_{t}
is the model graph,
\phi_{t}
contains structural hyper-parameters, and
F_{t}
contains feature configuration. The initial state
A_{0}
is derived from the production codebase
C
, while paper sources
P
and a static knowledge base
KB
provide architecture priors and historical evidence about effective or ineffective modification directions. The search is evaluated on data
D
and bounded by a budget
B
over rounds, LLM tokens, GPU hours, and wall-clock time. We include
F_{t}
in the architecture state because industrial recommender improvements often require coordinated changes to features, model structure, and feature–structure interactions.

Each iteration applies a modification
e_{t}\in\mathcal{E}
to transform the current architecture into a new candidate:

(1)

A_{t+1}=\mathrm{Apply}(A_{t},e_{t}).

where
\Omega

4.2.
Initialization and Signal Sources

Before the iterative search begins, NOVA constructs the initial architecture state
A_{0}
and gathers the initial signals used to compute the first architecture gradient. This initialization can be written as

(9)

g_{0}\leftarrow\mathrm{Grad}(\emptyset,V(A_{0}),\mathrm{baseline\ metrics},H_{0}),

where
\emptyset
indicates that no previous modification exists in the current run,
V(A_{0})
denotes initial diagnostics of the production model, baseline metrics provide the reference performance, and
H_{0}
is the trajectory memory initialized for the current run.

Initialization uses three signal sources. First, production code analysis parses the codebase
C
to obtain
A_{0}=(G_{0},\phi_{0},F_{0})
and the feasible modification boundary. Second, source-document analysis extracts architecture priors from paper sources
P
and converts them into production-adaptable modification directions. Third, knowledge loading retrieves historical evidence from the static knowledge base
KB
about effective and ineffective directions. Unlike
KB
, the trajectory memory
H
is updated during the current run with modifications, diagnostics, failures, and metric feedback.

4.4.
Silent-Failure-Aware Multi-Stage Architecture Verification

Figure 2.
Silent-failure-aware verification cascade.
K
candidate architecture modifications pass through structure-semantic checking, local executability testing, offline evaluation, and online validation. Failed candidates are recorded in trajectory memory
H
and fed back as forbidden directions for the next architecture gradient

.

NOVA uses
silent failure
to refer to architecture modifications that pass explicit engineering checks and can run or train, but fail to deliver the expected offline or online improvement. Such failures arise from two sources: architecture-semantic errors that can be detected before training, and structurally valid modifications that are ineffective for the business objective. As shown in Figure
2
, NOVA addresses both cases with a progressive verification cascade: early stages perform lightweight semantic and executability checks to filter invalid candidates, while later stages apply offline and online evaluation to a smaller set of verified candidates.

•

Structure-semantic gate.
Checks modification logic, shape/dtype, feature-to-token mapping, attention direction, mask semantics, and logit fusion. It catches runnable but structurally wrong candidates before training.

•

Local testing gate.
Checks single-machine executability and blocks engineering failures before offline training.

•

Offline inner loop.
Uses AUC to evaluate verified candidates before deployment.

•

Online outer loop.
Uses GMV/Bias for production validation.

Why the semantic gate helps before training.

A candidate can be executable while still violating recommender-specific architecture semantics. For example, the training framework can execute a mask, branch connection, or feature fusion path as long as tensor shapes are compatible, but it cannot determine whether the masking direction, feature routing, or logit-fusion logic matches the intended architecture. Thus, compilation and local tests show that a graph can run, not that the architecture modification is semantically valid. The semantic gate checks these constraints according to skill specifications, rather than relying only on executability.

Verification as gradient denoising.

Verification diagnostics are fed back into the architecture gradient defined in Section
3
. When a candidate is rejected, its failure pattern is stored in trajectory memory
H
and used as a forbidden direction in later rounds. This feedback denoises the search by blocking false-positive directions before training and reducing repeated semantic failures.

Origin and generalization of skill specifications.

The semantic gate is driven by skill specifications rather than standalone lint rules. These specifications combine historical seed rules, accumulated forbidden patterns, and LLM-assisted checks against the intended design and model context. For uncovered cases, the gate avoids overconfident rejection and leaves the candidate to offline and online evaluation. The mechanism remains fixed across tasks, while improvements to skill specifications in one task can be reused by later tasks with similar architectures or failure patterns.

Capability boundary.

The semantic gate does not judge whether a semantically valid modification is effective for the business objective; such performance-related failures are handled by offline and online evaluation.

4.5.
System Implementation

NOVA is implemented as a Main Agent orchestrating specialized sub-agents. This execution layer is not a separate algorithmic contribution; it operationalizes the architecture-gradient search and verification cascade in production.

Stage dependencies.

The sub-agents form a directed workflow: Initialization
\rightarrow
Solution Design
\rightarrow
Code Generation
\rightarrow
Quality Assessment
\rightarrow
Local Testing
\rightarrow
Offline Training & Evaluation
\rightarrow
Online Experiment & Evaluation. A downstream agent can consume outputs from multiple earlier stages. For example, Code Generation uses the structural hypothesis from Solution Design together with production context from Initialization; Quality Assessment and Local Testing verify the
K
generated candidates; Offline Training & Evaluation returns metric feedback and diagnostics to the Main Agent, which updates
H
and
g
for the next round.

Exception handling.

NOVA distinguishes three failure types:

•

Temporary execution failures
come from the environment rather than the candidate. NOVA retries the same step with a bounded retry budget and prevents such noise from polluting the architecture gradient.

•

Candidate-level failures
include structural-semantic violations and trainable candidates with negative offline feedback. NOVA records them in
H
as forbidden or low-value directions and returns to Solution Design.

•

Rule-uncovered or high-risk failures
are routed to Copilot mode to avoid autonomous extrapolation beyond covered skill specifications.

Iteration budget and selection rule.

The offline stage runs for at most
N
rounds or until the stopping criterion is met. Each round reports offline AUC and updates
H
and
g
. After the offline search stops, the Main Agent selects the feasible candidate with the best offline AUC from the trajectory and sends it to online experimentation. This implements low-cost offline exploration followed by selective online validation.

5.
Experiments

5.1.
Research Questions

We evaluate NOVA around four research questions:

•

RQ1: Effectiveness.
Does NOVA find more AUC-positive architecture modifications than competitive baselines under the same budget?

•

RQ2: Mechanism ablation.
How do NOVA’s main components, including paper reproduction, solution design, multi-candidate generation, quality assessment, and architecture-gradient feedback, contribute to its effective pass rate?

•

RQ3: Production-code modification.
How does NOVA actually modify real production recommender code in a literature-to-production architecture transfer task?

•

RQ4: Production validation.
Do the offline-selected candidates improve online GMV/Bias in production A/B tests?

5.2.
Experimental Settings

Tasks.

We evaluate NOVA on two architecture-modification settings.
L2 ScaleUp
uses a production RankMixer-style
(
31
)
backbone and searches over coupled structural hyper-parameters, including
token_cnt
,
token_dim
, and the number of RankMixer layers, while keeping the total model size within a
\pm 10\%
range of the production baseline.
L3 Literature-to-Production
tests whether a method can transplant TokenMixer-Large
(
9
)
into the same production backbone by modifying token interaction modules while preserving the feature pipeline, tensor shapes, training stability, and inference compatibility.

Dataset.

Experiments are conducted on a large-scale industrial advertising recommendation dataset collected from production traffic. The training corpus spans one month and contains billion-scale user–item interaction records. Each record is associated with over one thousand feature fields, covering both sequential and non-sequential signals. Every candidate model produced by every method is trained from scratch on the same dataset.

Budget and protocol.

All LLM-based methods are powered by the same base model,
Claude Sonnet 4.6

(
2
)

, so performance differences reflect the harness design rather than raw LLM capability. All methods are evaluated under the same offline budget. For each method, we run
N_{\mathrm{task}}=10
independent tasks, with at most
N_{\mathrm{iter}}=10
iterations per task. In each iteration, a method may generate up to
K
candidate modifications; candidates that pass the required gates are evaluated by offline AUC. The offline-selected architecture
A_{\mathrm{off}}^{*}
is the best feasible candidate discovered within this budget.

Metrics.

We report metrics from four perspectives.

Recommendation quality.

Offline quality is measured by
\Delta\mathrm{AUC}
relative to the production baseline. A candidate is considered AUC-positive if
\Delta\mathrm{AUC}>0.001
. Online quality is measured by
\Delta\mathrm{GMV}
and
\Delta\mathrm{Bias}
in production A/B tests.

Engineering validity.

Local Pass Rate (LPR) measures the fraction of generated candidates that pass local executability checks:

(10)

\mathrm{LPR}=\frac{N_{p}}{N_{g}},

where
N_{g}
is the number of generated candidate modifications and
N_{p}
is the number that pass local testing.

Silent-failure rate.

Silent Failure Rate (SFR) measures runnable but ineffective candidates:

(11)

\mathrm{SFR}=1-\frac{N_{+}}{N_{p}}=\frac{N_{p}-N_{+}}{N_{p}},

where
N_{+}
is the number of local-pass candidates that are AUC-positive.

Effective pass rate.

Effective Pass Rate (EPR) measures the end-to-end success rate from generated candidate to positive offline result:

(12)

\mathrm{EPR}=\frac{N_{+}}{N_{g}}=\mathrm{LPR}\cdot(1-\mathrm{SFR}).

5.3.
Baselines

We compare NOVA with human, coding-agent, and AutoML baselines under the same evaluation protocol.

•

Human Expert Loop.

Historical iteration logs of senior engineers on the same production model, including human-authored design, code modification, debugging, training, and analysis, replayed under our unified evaluation protocol.

•

ReActAgent-only.

A single LLM agent under the standard ReAct
(
26
)
paradigm. It does not use NOVA’s skill specifications, multi-candidate generation, trajectory memory, semantic verification, or architecture-gradient feedback. This baseline isolates the value of NOVA’s harness beyond a vanilla reasoning-and-acting agent.

•

OpenHands.

A state-of-the-art open-source coding agent
(
23
)
that reads the repository, modifies files, and iterates against execution feedback. It optimizes for runnable code rather than recommender-specific architecture validity.

•

Optuna-TPE.

An AutoML baseline using Tree-structured Parzen Estimator search
(
3
)
with population-based asynchronous early stopping over a predefined switch space, including learning rate, hidden dimension, block number, dropout, token dimension, and token count. This baseline is applicable only to the L2 ScaleUp setting.

•

NOVA.

The full system with architecture-gradient feedback, semantic verification, trajectory memory, skill specifications, and level/mode control.

L2 ScaleUp.

The L2 ScaleUp task is conducted on a production RankMixer-style backbone. Although it appears to be simple parameter tuning, the target variables are structurally coupled; for example,
token_cnt

token_dim
due to the tokenization and interaction design

These constraints directly affect LPR. Human experts achieve high LPR, while AutoML, generic coding agents, and ReActAgent-only baselines often ignore such architectural couplings and produce invalid configurations. NOVA avoids these failures through architecture-aware rules and verification feedback, and further improves SFR/EPR by using historical optimization knowledge to identify effective scaling directions. This shows that L2 ScaleUp requires architecture-aware constraint handling rather than blind parameter search.

L3 Literature-to-Production.

L3 tasks require adapting paper-derived modules to an existing production backbone while preserving feature routing, tensor shapes, training stability, and inference compatibility. Human experts can usually form semantically valid designs after understanding the paper, but translating the design into production code and passing local debugging often requires repeated trial and error, which lowers LPR. ReActAgent-only and OpenHands-style coding agents mainly optimize for runnable code, but do not reliably preserve production recommender semantics across such structural changes.

NOVA achieves substantially higher LPR and EPR by translating the research architecture into production-compatible modification plans, generating executable implementations, and using verification feedback to correct incompatible directions across rounds. This shows that literature-to-production transfer requires both architecture-aware adaptation and reliable production-code realization, rather than runnable code generation alone.

5.6.
Production-Code Modification Case Study for RQ3

To understand how NOVA modifies real production recommender code, we analyze multiple L3 tasks based on TokenMixer-Large and report two representative cases. Both cases are validated by online experiments.

Case 1: Parameter-efficient architecture variant.

NOVA adapts TokenMixer-Large to a production RankMixer-style backbone under parameter, FLOPs, and serving constraints. A direct paper-aligned variant uses four TokenMixer blocks with inter-residual connections and auxiliary loss. In contrast, NOVA discovers a lighter two-block variant that achieves comparable offline effectiveness with about
43\%
of the dense parameters.

The final variant reduces the number of TokenMixer blocks, narrows the FFN expansion ratio, and adds an additional
norm + FFN + residual
path beyond the standard mixing/reverting structure. This modification preserves the paper’s token-interaction intent while better matching the capacity and latency constraints of the production backbone. The case shows that NOVA performs architecture adaptation rather than paper reproduction: it searches for a feasible modification that works under production constraints.

Case 2: Architecture-gradient correction through auxiliary loss.

The second case shows how NOVA uses target-level feedback to revise a production modification. The initial transfer brings positive signals overall, but
task1
remains below the desired lift. NOVA therefore explores whether auxiliary supervision should be shared globally or specialized for specific targets.

The trajectory contains three stages. First, NOVA transfers the main TokenMixer components but observes insufficient lift on
task1
. Second, it removes the auxiliary loss to reduce interference, but this hurts
task2
and
task3
, suggesting that auxiliary supervision is still useful for part of the model. Third, NOVA reintroduces auxiliary loss with a smaller weight and adds a
task1
-specific auxiliary signal. When this still produces limited
task1
gain and negative
task3
impact, NOVA diagnoses two issues: the
task1
auxiliary loss may read from the wrong target representation, and the global auxiliary objective may interfere with
task3
. The next modification corrects the
task1
auxiliary-loss index and masks
task3
from the global auxiliary objective.

After this correction, NOVA further tests whether
task1
can be improved with a stronger target-specific branch, adding an extra task-token MLP and increasing the auxiliary-loss weight. This more aggressive refinement produces negative results, indicating over-specialization to
task1
. NOVA therefore returns to the better previous trajectory, lowers the
task1
auxiliary-loss weight, and obtains the expected positive result. This case illustrates the role of architecture-gradient feedback: historical outcomes and diagnostics help NOVA revise modification directions, avoid repeated mistakes, and converge toward a production-compatible design.

6.
Conclusion and Future Work

We present NOVA, a level-aware agent harness for industrial recommender architecture evolution. NOVA formulates architecture evolution as a verification-aware architecture-gradient search, embeds silent-failure suppression into the optimization loop, and validates the resulting system in production.

Figure 3.
Future evolution roadmap of NOVA across full-funnel scope expansion, full-lifecycle R&D automation, self-evolving skills, and gradient-aware resource scheduling.

Future work will extend NOVA in four directions. First, NOVA will evolve from fine-ranking pCTR/pCVR optimization to full-funnel recommender model optimization, covering retrieval, pre-ranking, fine-ranking, and re-ranking. Second, NOVA will evolve from model-centric optimization to full-lifecycle recommender R&D automation, coordinating agents for business analysis, data discovery, feature generation, architecture evolution, engineering optimization, and online experimentation. Third, NOVA will upgrade its stage-level skills from manually maintained prompts and rules to self-evolving skills that continuously record task traces, diagnose failures, update skills, and evaluate skill effectiveness. Fourth, NOVA will make architecture exploration more resource-aware by adaptively allocating GPU and training budgets according to verification confidence, early-training signals, historical promise, and cluster availability. This can reduce wasted training on low-value candidates and increase the throughput of offline search.
