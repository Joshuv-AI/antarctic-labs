Physician burnout assessed by the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory - the impact of the Covid-19 pandemic: meta-analysis
Authors: Romana Ulbrichtová, Tibor Baška, Mária Tatarková, Eva Malobická, Nika Štekláčová, Simona Kelčíková, Henrieta Hudečková, Viera Švihrová
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of our meta-analysis was to determine the global prevalence of burnout among physicians measured by the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI).MethodsA meta-analysis performed following the PRISMA guidelines. PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science were searched for trials on the prevalence of physician’s burnout syndrome. A literature search (October–November 2025) was performed, and pooled prevalence was estimated using a random-effects model. Joanna’s Briggs Institute (JBI) quality assessment tool was used to evaluate the risk of bias. Cochrane I-squared test was used to evaluate the heterogeneity among studies. The meta-analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 29.0.2.0.).ResultsThirty-nine cross-sectional studies containing 17,963 physicians were finally included in the analysis. The global prevalence of burnout was 52% (95% CI: 46%–57%). Pre-pandemic studies from Europe yielded a pooled prevalence of 34% (95% CI: 23%–46%), compared with 47% (95% CI: 34%–61%) during the COVID-19 era. In Asia, the pooled prevalence increased from 55% (95% CI: 46%–64%) before the pandemic to 66% (95% CI: 57%–74%) during the pandemic.ConclusionsA large number of physicians were identified as having moderate–high burnout levels. The findings of this analysis highlight the need for policy measures aimed at enhancing the working environment of healthcare professionals and mitigating burnout.Trial registrationThe protocol was registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251177705).Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14886-4.Keywords: Burnout, Physicians, Meta-analysis, Copenhagen burnout inventory

Introduction

Worldwide, the density of physicians was 17.2 per 10,000 population in 2022, including generalists, specialist medical practitioners and medical doctors not further defined [1]. Across the European Union (EU), there were estimated 1.98 million practising physicians in 2023 [2].

In upcoming decades, population ageing is expected to be a major challenge for the health care sector. The demand for health care will probably increase substantially as a result of an ageing population and at the same time the proportion of working people will probably decline. For physicians in particular, this creates a widening structural gap: the number of practising doctors is not keeping pace with epidemiological and demographic needs [3]. The shortage is further intensified by the growing clinical complexity of patients with multiple chronic conditions, which increases both diagnostic and therapeutic demands. At the same time, the continual rise in administrative responsibilities reduces the time available for direct patient care. Together, these pressures create a persistent risk of physician overload and occupational fatigue, contributing to burnout and threatening both workforce retention and the long-term sustainability of healthcare services. Physicians´ burnout is a serious condition that can manifest through emotional exhaustion, diminished professional effectiveness, depersonalization, cognitive impairment, and a heightened likelihood of medical errors [4, 5]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), burnout is a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, with healthcare professionals being among the most affected groups [6].

It is therefore essential to identify this syndrome at an early stage using targeted psychometric instruments that can support timely preventive interventions. There are several instruments that assess burnout using standardized measures, among which the most well-known include Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI), Oldenburg Burnout Inventory, etc [7–9]. Within this context, Kristensen et al. (2019) introduced the CBI, instrument that broadens the conceptualisation of burnout by assessing three domains of an individual’s functioning: personal burnout, work-related burnout, and client-related burnout [8]. The CBI questionnaire was selected because this instrument is increasingly used by professionals worldwide, while comprehensive data are still lacking.

The primary aim of this study was to estimate the prevalence of physician burnout using the CBI, while the secondary aim was to examine differences by geographic region and COVID-19 era.

Methods

Study guidelines

The study was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The study protocol was registered with the international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD420251177705) [10].

Definitions

Our definition of burnout has been based on the CBI, which characterizes it as s fatigue and exhaustion with three sub-dimensions: personal burnout, work-related burnout, and client-related burnout. Most importantly, the three scales can be used in different domains, the three scales can be used independently in accordance with the populations being studied and the theoretical questions being elucidated. We selected second domain (D2, work-related burnout) for the assessment of burnout, which is defined by the authors of the instrument as ‘‘The degree of physical and psychological fatigue and exhaustion that is perceived by the person as related to his/her work’’ [11]. Responses on the CBI were scored on a 0–100 scale (0 = never/to a very low degree, 25 = seldom/to a low degree, 50 = sometimes/to some degree, 75 = often/to a high degree, 100 = always/to a very high degree), and mean scores were calculated for each subscale. The Cronbach’s alphas for internal reliability are very high (0.85–0.87), with a value of 0.87 for D2; the inter-item correlations range from 0.36 to 0.65, and the item-scale correlations range from 0.54 to 0.76 [12].

Search strategy

Systematic literature search was performed to identify relevant peer-reviewed studies published in English in the following databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, regardless of the year of publication during October and November 2025, with the final search performed on 3 November 2025. The databases were searched from inception to this date. A combination of controlled search words (e.g., Medical Subject Headings/MeSH) and free-text words were used. To capture the instrument, we used terms “Copenhagen Burnout Inventory” or “Copenhagen Burnout Questionnaire “. To define participants of the studies, we considered a spectrum of physicians, including “doctor”, “clinician”, “physician”, “medical doctor”, “intern”, “junior doctor”, “house officer”, and “resident”.

A full description of the search strategy can be found in Appendix 1.

Study selection and eligibility criteria

Two reviewers independently performed study screening and data extraction. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus, with involvement of a third reviewer when necessary. Strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to identify original and observational studies relevant to our research objectives. Two reviewers independently screened all titles and abstracts to assess for relevance. Full texts of articles identified for further review were then assessed against the inclusion criteria. In cases of disagreement about the study eligibility of studies, a third reviewer was consulted for resolution.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) observational studies (including cross-sectional studies) published in English and peer-reviewed journals; (2) physicians working around the world; (3) burnout assessed using D2 of the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory; (4) provided raw data. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) nonrelevant articles, reports, editorials, letters, and protocols, systematic reviews and meta-analyses; (2) duplicates; (3) full text not available; (4) other health-care workers or students (nurses, midwives, dentists, etc.); (5) other than Copenhagen Burnout Inventory used; (6) missing data on gender.

Studies were classified as pre-pandemic or pandemic era based on the reported year of data collection. Studies with data collected before 2020 were considered pre-pandemic, whereas those with data collected in 2020 or later were classified as pandemic era studies.

Study risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers assessed the integrity, confirmability, and quality of the cross-sectional studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for studies reporting prevalence data [13]. Studies were included in the meta-analysis only if they met at least two-thirds of the JBI checklist items (‘Yes’ for ≥ 5 of 9 items) and did not fail key methodological domains such as sampling method, sample representativeness, measurement validity, or response rate. Any differences in the results were discussed between the two researchers. If necessary, a third researcher was consulted to reach an agreement.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 29.0.2.0). Pooled prevalence estimates were calculated using standard meta-analytic procedures for proportions implemented in SPSS. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s I² statistic, and a random-effects model was applied due to the expected heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was classified as high when I² exceeded 75%, moderate when I² was greater than 50%, and low when I² was below 25%. When substantial heterogeneity was present, a random-effects model was employed. To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were performed according to geographic region (Europe and Asia), and study period (pre–COVID-19 era and COVID-19 era) [14]. Additionally, temporal trends were examined using meta-regression analysis. Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s regression test and by visual inspection of funnel plots.

Results

Through the PRISMA strategy, a total of 901 articles were obtained from the three databases. After removing duplicates, 471 studies were included for meta-analyse. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were used for screening. Thirty-nine studies, selected for their relevance, were included in our analysis (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

Thirty-nine cross-sectional studies including 17,963 physicians were ultimately included in the analysis, of whom 8,854 were women (Appendix 2). 94.9% of them reported the time of data collection. In the studies, we had outcome data, including sample sizes, year of data collection, country, gender, and mean score of the second domain. In the studies, 66.7% of them reported the mean scores of the D2. The average score across all domains was 47.2 ± 10.3. 48.7% of the studies included information on age, with a mean 33.8 ± 7.1 years (Table 1).

Global prevalence of burnout syndrome among physicians

The pooled prevalence was 52% according to D2 (score above 50) (95% CI: 46%–57%; I² = 99%), indicating substantial between-study heterogeneity (Q = 11,323.17, p < 0.001). The overall effect was statistically significant (z = 18.15, p < 0.001). Despite the high level of heterogeneity, the distribution of individual effect sizes remained relatively concentrated around the overall estimate of 0.52. Most study-level prevalence estimates ranged between approximately 40% and 60%, suggesting a stable central tendency despite methodological and contextual variability across the included studies (Fig. 2).

Forest plot assessing global prevalence of burnout syndrome among physicians

Subgroup analysis

In the subgroup analysis (29 studies), prevalence differed meaningfully both by geographic region (Europe and Asia) and by the COVID-19 era. In Europe, the pooled prevalence was 34% in the pre-pandemic period (95% CI: 23%–46%) and 47% during the COVID-19 era (95% CI: 34%–61%), with substantial heterogeneity observed in both subgroups (I² > 75%). In Asia, the pooled prevalence increased from 55% before the pandemic (95% CI: 46%–64%) to 66% during the pandemic (95% CI: 57%–74%), again with high heterogeneity across studies (I² > 75%).

A formal test of between-subgroup heterogeneity showed statistically significant differences across the four subgroups (Q = 18.86, df = 3, p < 0.01), indicating that both geographic region and the COVID-19 era contributed to variability in prevalence estimates. Meta-regression analysis did not reveal significant temporal effects (Fig. 3, Appendix 3).

Discussion

The analysis showed 52% global prevalence of physician’s burnout according D2 of CBI (95% CI: 46%–57%). Taken together, these patterns suggest that the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with a shift toward higher prevalence estimates, particularly in Asia, where the pooled prevalence increased from 55% to 66%. Although the absolute change was modest, the consistent upward movement across regions, combined with significant between-subgroup heterogeneity, indicates that pandemic-related factors most probably contributed to the elevation in prevalence during the COVID-19 era. Several studies corroborate that the COVID-19 pandemic substantially exacerbated burnout among physicians and other healthcare workers. According to studies carried out in Mexico, the prevalence of severe burnout increased markedly from 30.4% before the pandemic to 63.2% during COVID-19 care, and further declined to 34.5% after the pandemic period [15]. In the Brazilian cohort, the prevalence of physician burnout rose from 18.9% before the pandemic to 31.3% during the pandemic, indicating a significant increase associated with pandemic-related stressors [16]. A large‑scale scoping review of 69 COVID-19 era studies found burnout rates among healthcare professionals varying between 4.3% and 90.4%, reflecting substantial heterogeneity across countries, care settings and timing within the pandemic [17]. Several studies employed the CBI to assess burnout during the pandemic, highlighting its utility in capturing both personal and work-related dimensions. In the Euro‑Burn I multicentre survey, 67.9% of participants met criteria for burnout via CBI [18].

According to Rotenstein et al. (2018), who primarily used the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) to assess burnout, the overall burnout prevalence of physicians ranged from 0% to 80.5%. The authors explained that such large variability stems from substantial heterogeneity in how burnout is defined and measured in the literature, noting that at least 142 distinct definitions for overall burnout or its subscales have been identified [4]. The methodological heterogeneity observed across the studies in this systematic review may originate partially from evolving definitions of burnout and ongoing debate about its underlying conceptual framework. Although most of studies employed an MBI-based inventory, our meta-analysis included those using the CBI, which represents one of the most widely employed methods for assessing burnout. Another meta-analysis comparing burnout measured by the CBI and MBI showed that the overall percentage of burnout in CBI studies (53%) was notably higher than in MBI studies (35%), highlighting the differences in sensitivity and conceptual approaches between these two instruments [19]. According to a meta-analysis evaluating burnout with various tools, including the MBI and CBI, the highest prevalence among healthcare workers was reported in the Middle East and North Africa at 66.6%, followed by Europe at 48.8%, and South America at around 42% [20]. Conversely, a study from Central Asia conducted during the same timeframe found a work-related burnout prevalence of 28.3% among healthcare professionals [21]. In our analysis, the mean age of participants was 33.8 ± 7.1 years, and we identified an alarmingly high rate of burnout within this younger cohort. This indicates that younger physicians may be particularly susceptible to burnout, potentially due to limited professional experience and fewer coping strategies for managing high workloads and maintaining work–life balance; however, this observation is based on limited age-stratified data and should be interpreted with caution. These findings underscore the need for more detailed research to accurately identify the factors contributing to burnout in this age group and to develop suitable interventions. Although several recent studies have employed the MBI or CBI to assess burnout among healthcare workers, only a few have stratified results by age, limiting our understanding of how age influences burnout development. Some studies that reported demographic data observed higher burnout rates among younger professionals, though these differences were not always statistically significant. In recent years, research focusing specifically on physicians has revealed significant burnout levels, particularly highlighting the issue among younger age groups. For instance, a survey of physicians in paediatric intensive care units in Bulgaria reported a mean respondent age of 36.6 ± 7.6 years, with a large proportion experiencing work-related exhaustion and burnout [22]. Similarly, Azi et al. (2025) found that 46.5% of respondents aged 30–39 reported burnout symptoms [23]. In a nationwide cross-sectional study of general practitioners in the Czech Republic, the mean age was 49.9 years. The higher mean age of general practitioners likely reflects the broader demographic trend of an aging primary-care workforce, which may influence both the prevalence and presentation of burnout in this group [24].

Similar methodological approaches in reporting mental health prevalence have been highlighted in previous systematic reviews [25].

This study has several limitations. First, the prevalence estimates are based on self-reported questionnaire data obtained using the CBI, which may be subject to reporting bias. Second, the included studies were conducted across different countries, healthcare systems, and physician populations, which may limit comparability. Finally, the high level of between-study heterogeneity observed in the analysis indicates substantial variability across studies and may have influenced the pooled prevalence estimates.

This study presents, to our knowledge, the first meta-analysis that exclusively includes studies assessing burnout using the CBI. By focusing on a single, conceptually consistent measurement instrument, our analysis overcomes a major limitation of previous reviews, which often combined heterogeneous burnout tools and thereby reduced the interpretability and comparability of pooled estimates. The exclusive use of CBI allows for a more robust and methodologically sound synthesis of burnout prevalence.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified a high prevalence of physician burnout as measured by the CBI. Subgroup analyses further revealed significant variation in burnout prevalence across geographic regions, with higher levels observed during the COVID-19 pandemic period compared with the pre-pandemic era. These findings highlight the substantial burden of burnout among physicians and underscore the need for targeted preventive strategies and systemic interventions.
