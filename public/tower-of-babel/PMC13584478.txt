Clinical effects of surgical delay in older patients with intertrochanteric fractures: analysis from the National Inpatient Sample Database
Authors: Zhijie Pan, Jia Chen, Yuning Lin, Haitang Deng, Wenjie Feng, Qingfeng Shao, Kai Zou, Yang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe clinical effect of surgical timing in older patients with intertrochanteric fractures is controversial. Our objective was to assess the association between surgical timing and in-hospital mortality and postoperative complications in these patients.Materials and methodsIFs patients accepted internal fixation surgery were obtained from the 2002 to 2014 Nationwide inpatient sample database. They were categorized into 3 subgroups according to the time of admission to surgery: early (within 24 h), shorter delays (1 to 2 days), and longer delays (3 to 7 days). The chi-squared test and Kruskal-Wallis test were performed to measure differences among these 3 subgroups. Survey-weighted methods accounted for the complex sampling design. Introduction

Intertrochanteric fractures (IFs) are common among the older population. Worldwide, 4.5 million people are disabled from hip fracture each year, with projections indicating a potential increase to 6.3 million by 2050 [1, 2]. The IFs, one of the most prevalent types of hip fractures, accounts for approximately 50% of all hip fractures [3] and exerts a significant financial burden on healthcare expenses and societal costs worldwide. Surgical treatment utilizing internal fixation, permitting immediate postoperative weight-bearing and reducing complications, is considered the “gold standard technique” for managing such fractures [4, 5].

Guidelines on hip fractures management across various countries recommend surgery within 48 h or even as little as the same day or the time of admission [6, 7]. Early surgery may reduce the time patients are exposed to those harmful states [8, 9], which may be associated with increased risks of medical complications and mortality. Numerous institutions schedule these procedures after hours to prompt the execution of such fracture surgeries [10, 11]. However, sleep deprivation and fatigue significantly compromise surgeons’ neurocognitive abilities, heightening the potential for serious medical mishaps [12]. On the other hand, patients afflicted with numerous comorbidities frequently reside in a fragile condition, exhibiting clinical signs of diminished physiological resilience, heightened mortality risk, and enhanced sensitivity to stress [13]. Delaying surgery to optimize surgical planning and perioperative management based on the preoperative patient status is a viable approach for improving patient outcomes.

The surgery timing for patients with IFs remains controversial. A single-center study [14] suggests that surgery within 24 h did not reduce mortality or major complications in hip fracture patients. Similarly, another single-center study [15] found that very long delays (over 120 h) may be associated with increased the risk for mortality and some medical complications after adjusting for confounding factors. In contrast, a study based on the Danish Database [16] revealed that delays over 24 h significantly raised the adjusted mortality rate. However, previous studies [14–16] on this subject were limited by factors such as small patient populations, which hindered a comprehensive understanding of patient demographics and early surgical outcomes. Therefore, we embarked on an investigation utilizing a database from the United States between 2002 and 2014 to depict the demographics of IFs patients with internal fixation and the surgical delay on clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Data source

Data were acquired from the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database, a resource offered by the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP). The NIS stands as the largest all-payer database in the United States, containing discharge data garnered from a stratified selected 20% sample of community hospitals, comprehensively covering 95% of the US population. Each hospitalization record in the NIS database encompasses comprehensive data, spanning patient demographics, hospital characteristics, over 20 distinct diagnosis and procedure categories (defined by the International Classification of Diseases (ninth revision) Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM)), accompanying comorbidities, length of stay, and the total associated charges.

Ethics statement

All data were obtained through request to the Online Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) Central Distributor, which administers the database. This study conforms to the NIS data-use agreement with HCUP (certification: #HCUP-6GXX48I31). Because this study analyzed secondary data from the NIS database, patients and the public were not involved directly. The study protocol was submitted to the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of our hospital, which exempted the study from IRB approval. Since all data in the NIS database are de-identified, the requirement for informed consent was also waived.

Study population

The study population comprised hospitalizations in the United States from 2002 to 2014 for intertrochanteric fracture that received internal fixation treatment. We included all admissions with a diagnosis code for intertrochanteric fractures (ICD-9-CM code 820.21) and a procedure code for internal fixation (ICD-9-CM codes 79.15, 79.35, and 78.55) [17]. Patients with missing data, aged less than 60, and undergoing procedures over 7 days were excluded.

Subsequently, we categorized the patients into 3 subgroups based on recommended surgical timing [6, 7] after admission in the guidelines:1) early (within 24 h), 2) shorter delay (1 to 2 days), and 3) longer delay (3 to 7 days). The detailed flow can be visualized in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of the study participants selection process

Study variable

Patient demographics and hospital characteristics were evaluated. Outcome measures included length of stay (LOS), total hospitalization costs, in-hospital mortality, and postoperative complications. We searched the database for postoperative complications before discharge, utilizing the ICD-9-CM diagnostic code. We determined cerebrovascular, cardiovascular, pulmonary, venous thromboembolism (including deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism), mechanical wound (including surgical wound dehiscence and hematoma), systemic complication (including postoperative shock and hemorrhage), post-operative infection, urinary complication, as well as post-operative anemia as in-hospital postoperative complications in IF patients. Frailty was identified using 10 diagnosis-based indicators, including the Hopkins ACG frailty indicator, derived from ICD-9-CM codes at admission [18]. The specific ICD-9 codes for complications are listed below SUPPLEMENTARY Table 1.

A total of 29 Elixhauser comorbidities were readily available within the database from 2002 to 2014. The calculation of Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was as shown in previous studies [19]. Meanwhile, frailty and acute medical instability, which including septicemia, systemic inflammatory response syndrome, acute hepatic failure, acute respiratory failure and acute renal failure, were incorporated as components of baseline patient health status in this study.

Data analysis

The statistical software, SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) and R version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used to perform statistical analysis.

All results were compared with the findings for the early group, which served as the reference group for this study. To further assess the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were performed by modeling time-to-surgery as both a continuous variable and a dichotomous variable (> 48 h vs. ≤48 h). We summarized the continuous variables by calculating their mean and standard deviation. Categorical variables were compared using chi-squared tests and continuous outcomes among 3 groups were compared using Kruskal-Wallis test. The Dunnett test and the Bonferroni correction were used for post hoc assessment. We then performed multiple logistic and linear regression to compare the risk-adjusted associations of different surgical timing with in-hospital mortality and postoperative complications. Complex survey analyses were conducted by simultaneously incorporating discharge-level weights (DISCWT) and hospital identifiers (HOSPID) to generate nationally representative estimates. Because hospital stratification variables were unavailable in the NIS database during the study period (2002–2014), this survey-weighted approach was adopted as a validated alternative to conventional hierarchical mixed-effects models to mitigate inter-hospital clustering bias and optimize the accuracy of population-level estimates. The covariates considered in the multivariate regression included statistically significant demographics (age, sex, race, primary expected payer, admission day, Charlson Comorbidity Index, comorbidities), and hospital characteristics (location of hospital and teaching status of hospital).

To enhance the clinical relevance of the study, additional multivariable analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with prolonged surgical delay and to explore potentially modifiable contributors to delayed care.

Statistical significance was defined using an alpha level of 0.001 due to the large sample size.

Results

Prevalences of intertrochanteric fractures and internal fixation

A total of 369,116 geriatric patients with Ifs were treated surgically in the NIS database between 2002 and 2014, while 326,835 (88.521%) received an internal fixation. 228,725 people were eventually included in this study. Discussion

We evaluated a large national cohort of more than 200,000 IF patients who underwent internal fixation procedures between 2002 and 2014 to determine the rate of in-hospital postoperative complications and mortality associated with different surgery timing. In this study, we found that delay surgery for elderly patients with IFs, compared with surgery performed early, was consistently associated with adverse in-hospital outcomes. When surgical delay exceeds 48 h, it was associated with a higher risk of postoperative complications during hospitalization. Meanwhile, the association between delayed surgery and in-hospital mortality was strengthened. These findings highlight the potential importance of timely surgical intervention in improving short-term clinical outcomes in this vulnerable elderly population.

Hip fractures include femoral neck fractures and IFs, and the preferred surgical methods for both are different. Internal fixation is widely regarded as the “gold standard” for treating IFs [4, 5], but hip arthroplasty is more appropriate for elderly patients with femoral neck fractures [17]. Previous studies have reported early operative time and the prognosis of hip fractures [14–16, 20, 21]. Early surgery may be appropriate for younger and healthier patients [22–24], whereas hip fractures primarily affect older patients with comorbidities, which may have contributed to selection bias in previous studies. In this study, we precisely defined age, fracture type, and surgical procedure. In addition, the larger sample size allowed us to further explore the relationship between surgery timing and clinical outcomes.

The guidelines recommend that surgery for IFs should be performed within 48 h of admission, or even on the day of admission [6, 7]. In practice, the implementation of surgery is often influenced by institutional philosophy, logistics, and resource availability. In the UK health care system, out-of-hours surgery is generally reserved for situations involving a genuine threat to life or limb [25]. When investigating the causes of delayed surgery in hip fracture patients, Maria et al. found that more than half of patients with hip fractures lacked operating room availability for surgical treatment within 48 h of admission [15]. In addition, reduced availability of experienced surgeons and supporting staff during off-hours admissions may affect the timely execution of surgery [11, 26]. In the present study, the findings support the potential role of system-level factors, as hospital characteristics such as location (OR shorter: 1.134; longer: 1.347) and teaching status (OR shorter: 1.017; longer: 1.106) were independently associated with delayed surgery.

Our study discovered a statistically significant difference in the proportion of patients experiencing delayed surgery on non-elective admission and weekend admission. The association between weekend admissions and surgical delay may be related to reduced availability of experienced surgeons and supporting staff during these periods [26]. Contrary to our initial suspicions, teaching hospitals were independently associated with prolonged surgical delay. Teaching hospitals play important role in patient care, medical education, and clinical training. The participation of surgical residents and trainees, as well as the complexity of patient management and institutional workflows, may partially contribute to longer surgical scheduling times. Additionally, complex medical insurance payment systems and administrative processes in teaching hospitals may also influence surgical scheduling. Our study found that individuals with private insurance or self-pay were associated with a lower risk of prolonged surgical delay, whereas patients with Medicaid were more likely to experience delayed surgery. These findings may reflect differences in healthcare administrative processes and resource allocation.To reduce the time to surgery, healthcare systems may benefit from increasing operating room availability, optimizing staffing levels, and improving perioperative coordination.

Elderly patients often have multiple comorbidities, which independently increase the risk of postoperative complications and require multidisciplinary evaluation [27–31]. Preoperative optimization is therefore essential, particularly for major clinical abnormalities such as respiratory failure, coagulopathy, and heart failure, which may appropriately delay surgery to allow clinical stabilization [29, 32]. Previous research [33, 34] suggests that preoperative heart tests might not be essential, but further research is still warranted. Anne et al. used the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score to evaluate patients’ pre-surgery conditions and found that those with higher scores waited longer for surgery [16]. The CCI is widely regarded as the most reliable indicator for quantifying vulnerability [35, 36]. Rijckevorsel et al. used CCI to evaluate older patients with hip fractures before surgery and found no significant difference among the three groups [37]. Oppositely, We discovered statistically significant variance in CCI among the three groups, and patients in the delayed groups exhibited a greater comorbidity burden. Furthermore, we discovered acute medical instability (OR shorter: 1.041; longer: 1.575; both P < 0.001) was independently associated with prolonged delay, supporting the possibility that surgical timing may partly reflect the need for preoperative stabilization. After adjustment for these confounders, we found that the association between delayed surgery (especially longer delays) and poorer outcomes (higher risk of complications) persisted, suggesting that this relationship may not be fully explained by measured comorbidity and clinical acuity alone.

Previous studies have shown conflicting effects of early surgery on mortality after hip fracture surgery [38–40]. Our study is among the first to specifically evaluate the association between prolonged surgical delay and in-hospital mortality in patients with IFs. In our study, the in-hospital mortality rates were 2.110% and 4.214%, respectively, which were higher than those reported in previous studies [41, 42]. This discrepancy may be related to the higher CCI observed in our cohort, suggesting greater vulnerability among patients. Additionally, our study had a relatively lower proportion of female patients compared to earlier research, and it was well-established that female may serve as a protective factor against mortality following intertrochanteric fracture surgery [43]. Multiple logistic regression analysis showed that shorter delays were not significantly associated with in-hospital death (OR: 1.007; CI: 0.937–1.082), whereas longer delays were associated with a higher risk of in-hospital mortality (OR: 1.348; CI: 1.218–1.492). Moreover, Our study found that longer delays were associated with longer hospital stays and more hospital costs, potentially increasing the burden on both patients and healthcare systems. These findings support the potential benefit of timely surgical management following appropriate preoperative optimization.

Unavoidably, our study had several limitations that attributable to the use of a large administrative database. First, the NIS database did not record the specific time of admission, so the impact of out-of-hours admission on postoperative outcomes could not be assessed. Second, the NIS database for the study period (2002–2014) did not provide hospital stratification variables, which may limit the ability to fully account for hospital-level clustering and may have affected variance estimation. Similarly, important dimensions of patient vulnerability, such as functional status and pre-fracture mobility, were not available in the NIS database, which may have result in residual confounding and limited the ability to fully capture clinical severity at presentation. Third, it was difficult to obtain the details of surgical procedure, and these may influence ultimate complication rates. Also, the coding and documentation in the large database may be discrepancy or misclassification, which result in underestimating or overestimating the incidence of postoperative adverse events [19]. Fourth, the impact associated with the surgical timing definition used in this study may have been under- or overestimated because the time from injury to surgery failed to be included in the NIS database [37]. Finally, given the lack of post-discharge follow-up in the NIS database, these findings should be interpreted within the context of in-hospital outcomes, with caution in extrapolating to longer-term prognosis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, prolonged waiting time for surgery was significantly associated with a higher risk of postoperative complications, longer hospital stays and higher hospital charges, thereby potentially contributing to a greater burden on patients and the healthcare system. Moreover, our study showed that longer delays were associated with increased in-hospital mortality among older patients with IFs. Therefore, for individuals with clinical instability, reasonable preoperative optimization is important. For those without medical contraindications, early surgery may be beneficial.
