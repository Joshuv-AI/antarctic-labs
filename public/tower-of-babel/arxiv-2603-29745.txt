Abstract
Driven by the MagNet Challenge 2025 (MC2), increased research interest is directed towards modeling transient magnetic fields within ferrite material. An accurate time-resolved and temperature-aware H-field prediction is essential for optimizing magnetic components in applications with quasi-stationary / non-stationary excitation waveforms. Within the scope of this investigation, a selection of model structures with varying degrees of physically motivated structure are compared. Based on a Pareto investigation, a rather black-box gated recurrent unit (GRU) model structure with a graceful initialization setup is found to offer the most attractive model size vs. model accuracy trade-off, while the physics-inspired models performed worse. For a GRU-based model with only 325 325 parameters, a sequence relative error of 8.02 % 8.02\,\% and a normalized energy relative error of 1.07 % 1.07\,\% averaged across five different materials are achieved on unseen test data. With this excellent parameter efficiency, the proposed model won the first place in the performance category of the MC2.



I
Introduction

Magnetics are usually one of the bulkier parts of modern electronics and are responsible for considerable losses
[
33
]
. The continuously growing demand toward efficiency, miniaturization, and sustainability places increasing emphasis on optimized designs for magnetic components.
The optimization, in turn, requires accurate modeling and understanding of the electromagnetic behavior and corresponding core losses of the magnetic material.
The modeling is difficult, because the waveforms resulting from different inputs exhibit varying degrees of hysteresis and saturation that are dependent on the type of material, the core temperature, and the shape, amplitude and frequency of the input waveform
[
33
,
40
,
39
,
25
]
.
This, paired with the lack of a satisfactory first-principle model for the underlying processes, has led commonly utilized models to be only accurate approximations for specific operation conditions
[
25
]
. While physical first-principle models for the micromagnetic material behavior exist,
a transfer of the microscopic physical framework to macroscopic magnetic effects and to the prediction of magnetic component behavior is insufficiently investigated.

I-A

Related work

For steady-state operation (e.g., in a DC-DC converter), the usual process to estimate core losses is to employ specific approximation equations, e.g., in the form of the Steinmetz equation
[
38
,
37
]
, its various improvements
[
2
,
22
,
42
,
31
,
32
]
, and more recent developments such as the Stenglein method
[
39
]
.

While these steady-state core loss models are valid approximations and very effective tools under the appropriate conditions, it has been shown in the context of the
MagNet Challenge 2023 (MC1)
, that they can reach their limits when faced with extensive and varied real-world data
[
19
,
5
]
.
In
MC1
, this was addressed by encouraging the development of data-driven material models that predict power losses based on waveform, frequency, and temperature in ferrites in steady-state operation
[
5
]
.
Much progress towards general and accurate models for core loss prediction in steady-state has been made utilizing the tools from
machine learning (ML)
in the context of the challenge (see, e.g.,
[
25
,
5
,
19
,
13
,
46
]
).

Beyond steady-state operation, there is a vast class of applications in which quasi-stationary or semi-arbitrary excitation of the material is performed due to switched excitation waveforms, e.g.,
power factor correction (PFC)
circuits, electric motor drives, and power amplifiers
[
21
]
.
There are simple models based on the Steinmetz equation utilizing the superposition of the major and minor loop core losses for prediction of the core losses in quasi-stationary operation of
PFC
circuits
[
33
,
15
]
.
However, these concepts cannot easily be extended to semi-arbitrary excitation conditions where the frequencies for major and minor loops constantly change during operation.
For instance, for the electric drive, the rotation frequency may change during operation, making the superposition inaccurate.
Additionally, superposition inherently assumes linearity which is a simplifying assumption for the nonlinear material behavior. Time-resolved modeling allows for more accurate consideration of the nonlinearity.
Furthermore, it allows modeling of transients, and fully enables circuit simulation in the time domain
[
25
]
.

Classical approaches for predicting time-resolved hysteretic material behavior include the Preisach
[
34
]
and
Jiles-Atherton (JA)

[
16
]
models, both of which are in essence phenomenological approximations, i.e., models that aim to mathematically describe observed data trajectories and are inspired by physical ideas but are not actually faithful to a complete physical framework
[
30
]
.

In practice, the
JA
model has been used, e.g., for the estimation of core losses in electric machines with non-sinusoidal excitations
[
36
,
6
]
and for modeling of the material behavior in
finite element method (FEM)
simulations
[
11
,
10
,
3
]
.
Some issues have been identified with the initial formulation of the model.
There is a risk of a non-physical negative differential permeability
[
28
]
and, furthermore,
non-closure of minor loops and consequential drifting of the estimation is a known problem when the excitation is not a closed loop
[
30
]
.
Multiple extensions of the
JA
model have been proposed that aim to improve the prediction quality including more precise prediction of minor loops
[
4
,
24
,
20
,
12
]
.
However, most of the existing applications of the
JA
model are limited in the scope of operating conditions.
Some first approaches with adaptable parameters have been proposed in order to allow more general application (see, e.g.,
[
14
,
28
]
).

The Preisach model has been used for iron loss estimations with different excitation waveforms (e.g.,
[
7
,
47
,
26
]
) and also has been compared to the
JA
model in prediction performance for DC-biased hysteresis behavior
[
26
]
.
The Preisach model was found to be more accurate especially for increasing DC-bias, but also required more computational effort
[
26
]
.
In
[
27
]
, it was found that the proposed inverse Preisach was more accurate and required less computational effort. However, they utilized a closed form for the underlying Everett function, reducing the flexibility of the Preisach model.

Ferrite materials are made up of an arbitrarily oriented and shaped grain structure on the micrometer scale that heavily dominates the macroscopic magnetic behavior.
Physical models based on micromagnetic particle interactions such as the
Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert equation (LLG)

[
23
,
9
]
operate at the nanometer scale.
Transferring those to real-world magnetic material probes with heterogeneous, defective structures remains an open question.

In summary, a first-principle model to explain magnetic hysteresis that is reasonably applicable on a macroscopic scale is missing
[
25
]
.
Existing phenomenological, data-driven models are only employed to different, small subproblems and never really thoroughly compared on various operating conditions. This makes it difficult to determine which model types are most effective for varied and semi-arbitrary excitations.

II
General modeling setup

The modeling task is to estimate the scalar magnetic field
\hat{H}_{k}
with
k\in\left[k_{1},k_{2}\right]
based on the previously observed magnetic field
H_{k}
with
k\in\left[k_{0},k_{1}\right)
, the magnetic flux density
B_{k}
with
k\in\left[k_{0},k_{2}\right]
, and the core temperature
\vartheta
.
That is, a model of the form

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{H}}_{k_{1}:k_{2}}=\bm{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{B}_{k_{0}:k_{2}},\bm{H}_{k_{0}:k_{1}-1},\vartheta)

(7)

is to be conceptualized and implemented (cf.,
[
43
]
).
The notation
\bm{H}_{k_{0}:k_{1}-1}
describes a time series of field values

\bm{H}_{k_{0}:k_{1}-1}=\begin{bmatrix}H_{k_{0}},H_{k_{0}+1},\dots H_{k_{1}-1}\end{bmatrix},

(8)

and it is utilized analogous for all other quantities.

In the following, concepts that are shared among most of the considered models are presented.
Whenever the training setup for a model varies from the described form, it is noted explicitly in the respective section of the model in Sec.
III
.

II-A

Feature Engineering

The term feature engineering encompasses all preprocessing, normalization, and derivation of additional features in an observational data set.
At this point it should be noted that it is possible to extract the switching frequency
f_{\mathrm{sw}}
from the considered data set. This has explicitly not been done as the presented model is intended to be applicable for excitation signal shapes that are as arbitrary as possible.

II-A
1
Normalization

The normalization is performed by first computing the maximum absolute value for the raw magnetic field, magnetic flux, and temperature values
H_{\mathrm{max}}
,
B_{\mathrm{max}}
, and
\vartheta_{\mathrm{max}}
, respectively, for each material data set independently. The normalization is then performed by simple division:

\displaystyle\tilde{z}=\frac{z}{z_{\mathrm{max}}}\quad\text{with}\quad z\in\{H,B,\vartheta\}.

(9)

II-B

Training cost functions

An adapted
root mean squared (RMS)
error was utilized as the loss function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RMSE}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{k=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}\left[\left(\tilde{H}_{k}-\hat{\tilde{H}}_{k}\right)^{2}\cdot\left|\tilde{B}_{k}-\tilde{B}_{k-1}\right|\right]}{k_{2}-k_{1}+1}}.

(11)

Additionally the loss is normalized with the
RMS
value of the full sequence
\bm{H}_{0:k_{3}}
from which
\tilde{\bm{H}}_{k_{0}:k_{2}}
and
\tilde{\bm{B}}_{k_{0}:k_{2}}
are sampled.
Specifically, the weighted loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{RMSE}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RMSE}}\cdot H_{\mathrm{max}}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{k_{3}+1}\sum_{k=0}^{k_{3}}H_{k}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}

(12)

\bm{\theta}

The cost function (
11

SRE

NERE

The normalization by the
RMS

H

II-D

Parameter updates

Each material-specific model is trained for multiple epochs over the training dataset.
For a single epoch, the training data is split into subsequences of the same length
l\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, and then
b\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
subsequences are randomly grouped to form a mini-batch

\mathcal{B}=\left\{\mathbf{B}_{k_{0}:k_{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times l},\mathbf{H}_{k_{0}:k_{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times l},\bm{\vartheta}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times 1}\right\}.

(13)

This way, a list of mini-batches is precomputed for the whole training data set.
These mini-batches are iterated over once and for each mini-batch the model
\bm{\mathcal{M}}
is used to perform a prediction
\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{k_{0}:k_{2}}
.
The training loss (
12
) is evaluated for
\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{k_{1}:k_{2}}
and
\mathbf{H}_{k_{1}:k_{2}}
, the gradient with respect to the model parameters
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{RMSE}}
is computed via backpropagation, and the parameter update is computed based on it.
Then finally, a new set of sequences and ordering is computed for the following epoch.

Additionally, arbitrary warmup phase lengths (as part of the model initialization) are supported through this approach.

III-A
2
Magnetization GRU (GRU-M)

This variant works similarly to the
GRU-P
.
The main difference is that it explicitly predicts the magnetization instead of directly predicting
H

\hat{H}_{k}=\hat{H}_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{g}_{k-1}\right)=\frac{{B}_{k}}{\mu_{0}}-\bm{g}_{k}^{(0)}H_{\mathrm{max}},

(21)

where
\mu_{0}
is the magnetic permeability in vacuum.
The idea is that the prediction function provides a comparatively direct and physically founded way for the flux to impact the prediction. However, the computation is numerically suboptimal, as
\frac{{B}_{k}}{\mu_{0}}
is in the range of roughly
10^{6}
and the resulting field prediction is usually below a scale of
10^{2}
. Furthermore, it might be an issue that the permeability may introduce not only a scaling but also a time shift between
B
and
H
, which cannot be represented with (
21
). Overall the performance of this model is very poor and training is unstable.

For better numerical stability, another version with

\hat{\tilde{H}}_{k}=\hat{\tilde{H}}_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{g}_{k-1}\right)=\mathrm{tanh}\left(\tilde{B}_{k}-\bm{g}_{k}^{(0)}\right),

(22)

is tested.
The warmup for this model is done by inverting the prediction function (
22
)

\bm{g}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{B}_{k}-\mathrm{tanh}^{-1}(\tilde{H}_{k})\\
\bm{g}^{(1:)}_{k-1}\end{bmatrix}.

(23)

The latter setup is provided as
magnetization GRU (GRU-M)
in Sec.
IV
.

III-A
3
GRU parameterizes linear model (GRU-L)

This variant utilizes the first element of the hidden state of the
GRU
to predict the magnetic permeability of the material. Specifically, the normalized inverse of the permeability is predicted in each step:

\hat{\tilde{H}}_{k}=\hat{\tilde{H}}_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{g}_{k-1}\right)=\bm{g}_{k}^{(0)}\cdot\tilde{B}_{k}=\hat{\tilde{\mu}}^{-1}_{k}\cdot\tilde{B}_{k},

(24)

where the estimate for the physical permeability can be calculated as

\hat{\mu}_{k}=\frac{B_{\mathrm{max}}}{\bm{g}_{k}^{(0)}H_{\mathrm{max}}}=\hat{\tilde{\mu}}_{k}\frac{B_{\mathrm{max}}}{H_{\mathrm{max}}}.

(25)

Analogous to the previous variants, the warmup can then be performed as the inverse of the prediction function (
24
)

\bm{g}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{H}_{k}\tilde{B}_{k}^{-1}\\
\bm{g}^{(1:)}_{k-1}\end{bmatrix}.

(26)

Similarly to the
GRU-M
, this setup does not incorporate the time shift characteristic of the material.
An option to circumvent this is to provide multiple past/future elements of the
B
trajectory instead of just
B_{k}
, however, this places
a stronger demand on the hidden state, as each input element requires an element of the hidden state as factor.
In the end, the
GRU parameterizes linear model (GRU-L)
as described in (
24
) and (
26
) is provided for Sec.
IV
.

III-B

LSTM with direct prediction (LSTM-P)

An
LSTM
cell is defined as
[
8
]

\displaystyle\bm{i}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\bm{W}_{\mathrm{i}}\bm{x}_{k}+\bm{b}_{\mathrm{i}}+\bm{U}_{\mathrm{i}}\bm{g}_{k-1}\right)

(27)

\displaystyle\bm{f}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\bm{W}_{\mathrm{f}}\bm{x}_{k}+\bm{b}_{\mathrm{f}}+\bm{U}_{\mathrm{f}}\bm{g}_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tanh}\left(\bm{W}_{\mathrm{m}}\bm{x}_{k}+\bm{b}_{\mathrm{m}}+\bm{U}_{\mathrm{m}}\bm{g}_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle\bm{o}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\bm{W}_{\mathrm{o}}\bm{x}_{k}+\bm{b}_{\mathrm{o}}+\bm{U}_{\mathrm{o}}\bm{g}_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle\bm{c}_{k}

\displaystyle=\bm{f}_{k}\odot\bm{c}_{k-1}+\bm{i}_{k}\odot\bm{m}_{k}

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{k}

\displaystyle=\bm{o}_{k}\odot\mathrm{tanh}(\bm{c}_{k})

where
\bm{c}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}}
is the cell state,
\bm{g}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}}
is the hidden state,
\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{x}}}
is the input,
\bm{W}_{\mathrm{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}\times d_{\mathrm{x}}}
,
\bm{b}_{\mathrm{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}}
,
\bm{U}_{\mathrm{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}\times d_{\mathrm{g}}}
are the input gate parameters,
\bm{W}_{\mathrm{f}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}\times d_{\mathrm{x}}}
,
\bm{b}_{\mathrm{f}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}}
,
\bm{U}_{\mathrm{f}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}\times d_{\mathrm{g}}}
are the forget gate parameters,
\bm{W}_{\mathrm{m}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}\times d_{\mathrm{x}}}
,
\bm{b}_{\mathrm{m}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}}
,
\bm{U}_{\mathrm{m}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}\times d_{\mathrm{g}}}
are the input squashing parameters, and
\bm{W}_{\mathrm{o}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}\times d_{\mathrm{x}}}
,
\bm{b}_{\mathrm{o}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}}
,
\bm{U}_{\mathrm{o}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}\times d_{\mathrm{g}}}
are the output gate parameters.
In the following (
27
) will be written as:

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{g}_{k}\\
\bm{c}_{k}\end{bmatrix}=\mathrm{LSTM}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{g}_{k-1},\bm{c}_{k-1}),

(28)

where
\bm{\theta}
holds the tunable parameters of the
LSTM
cell.

The prediction and the warmup for the
LSTM with direct prediction (LSTM-P)
are implemented through the use of the same mechanism as
GRU-P
(see Sec.
III-A1
):

\hat{\tilde{H}}_{k}=\hat{\tilde{H}}_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{g}_{k-1},\bm{c}_{k-1}\right)=\bm{g}_{k}^{(0)}.

(29)

Note that for the
LSTM
it is not clear how the propagation of
\bm{c}_{k}
is influenced by the warmup via (
29
), compared to the
GRU
where a much smaller amount of operation is applied to the singular state of the model (cf. (
14
)).

III-C

JA variants

While the
JA
equations do not actually seem to be physically founded and can be inaccurate especially regarding the temporal behavior, it is still a common tool for the construction of quasi-static hysteresis phenomena
[
48
]
.
In the following, multiple models that aim to incorporate the
JA
formulas are presented.

III-C
1
Basic (inverse) JA

According to the
JA
model, the change in magnetization w.r.t. changes in the magnetic field can be approximated as
[
48
,
45
]

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}H}=\frac{\delta_{\mathrm{M}}(M_{\mathrm{an}}-M)+ck^{\prime}\delta\frac{\mathrm{d}M_{\mathrm{an}}}{\mathrm{d}H_{\mathrm{e}}}}{k^{\prime}\delta-\alpha_{\mathrm{W}}\left[\delta_{\mathrm{M}}(M_{\mathrm{an}}-M)+ck^{\prime}\delta\frac{\mathrm{d}M_{\mathrm{an}}}{\mathrm{d}H_{\mathrm{e}}}\right]},

(30)

where
\alpha_{\mathrm{W}}
is related to the interaction of Weiss’ domains,
a
is a form factor,
c
is a coefficient related to the reversibility of the domain walls,
k^{\prime}
is a coefficient related to hysteresis losses, and
M_{\mathrm{s}}
is the saturation magnetization
[
45
]
.
Furthermore,
H_{\mathrm{e}}=H+\alpha_{\mathrm{W}}M
is the effective magnetic field,
M_{\mathrm{an}}
is the anhysteretic magnetization calculated as

M_{\mathrm{an}}=M_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\mathrm{coth}\left(\frac{H_{\mathrm{e}}}{a}\right)-\frac{a}{H_{\mathrm{e}}}\right),

(31)

\delta
is a directional parameter with
[
48
]

\delta=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}t}>0$}\\
0&\text{if $\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}t}=0$}\\
-1&\text{if $\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}t}<0$,}\\
\end{cases}

(32)

and
\delta_{\mathrm{M}}
is
[
48
]

\delta_{\mathrm{M}}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}t}<0$ and $M_{\mathrm{an}}>M$}\\
0&\text{if $\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}t}>0$ and $M_{\mathrm{an}}<M$}\\
1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(33)

Based on the derivations in
[
48
]
, it can be followed that

\frac{\mathrm{d}H}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\left[1-\frac{\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}H}}{1+\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}H}}\right],

(34)

which is integrated using an
ordinary differential equation (ODE)
solver to provide estimates for the magnetic field.
Here, it is integrated using the explicit Euler method

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{k}+\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{H}}{\mathrm{d}t}

(35)

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{k}+\Delta\hat{H}_{\bm{\theta}}(\hat{H}_{k},B_{k},B_{k+1}),

where
\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{H}}{\mathrm{d}t}
is approximated using (
30
) and (
34
).

In training of this model (and following
JA
variants), the physical parameters presented in (
36
) are not directly tuned.
Instead, the tunable parameters are wrapped in the sigmoid function
\sigma
and scaled with individual factors to produce the physical parameters.
This results in the tunable parameters

\bm{\theta}=\sigma^{-1}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\frac{M_{\mathrm{s}}}{\eta_{M_{\mathrm{s}}}},\frac{a}{\eta_{a}},\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{W}}}{\eta_{\alpha_{\mathrm{W}}}},\frac{k^{\prime}}{\eta_{k^{\prime}}},\frac{c}{\eta_{c}}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\right).

(36)

where
\eta_{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

\forall z\in\{M_{\mathrm{s}},a,\alpha_{\mathrm{W}},k^{\prime},c\}
are heuristically chosen scaling factors, and
\sigma^{-1}
is the inverse function of the sigmoid function that is applied element-wise to the contents of the input vector. This mapping is done to ensure that the parameters stay greater than zero and roughly within their physical value ranges during numerical optimization.

Note that this basic
JA
model is unable to properly discern between different operating points (e.g., changes in material behavior due to temperature or frequency), and provides the parameters that best describe all conditions on average.
This circumstance is addressed in the following model variant.

III-C
2
GRU directly parameterizes JA (GRU-JADP)

In this setup, a
GRU
provides the parameters to the
JA

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{JA}}

\displaystyle=\bm{g}_{k}^{(:5)}=\mathrm{GRU}_{\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{GRU}}}(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{g}_{k-1})^{(:5)}

(37)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{k}+\Delta\hat{H}_{\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{JA}}}(\hat{H}_{k},B_{k},B_{k+1}).

The first five elements of
\bm{g}_{k}^{(:5)}
are interpreted as the parameters of the
JA
model, while the remaining elements
\bm{g}_{k}^{(5:)}
may carry further information.
The idea is that the adaptation in parameterization to the current operation point is learned by the
GRU
.

III-C
3
JA and residual GRU

This version aims to use the basic behavior of the
JA
model and correct the prediction with a
GRU
cell.
This is implemented by summing the outputs of (
35
) and (
20
)

\hat{H}_{k+1}=\underbrace{\hat{H}_{k}+\Delta\hat{H}_{\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{JA}}}(\hat{H}_{k},B_{k},B_{k+1})}_{\mathrm{JA}}+\underbrace{\Delta\hat{H}_{\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{GRU}}}\left(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{g}_{k-1}\right)}_{\mathrm{GRU}}.

(38)

This model has multiple issues, which is why it is omitted from the final comparison.
The model training is fairly unstable when both models are trained together and requires double precision floating point computations, heavily increasing the time required for training the model.
Additionally, it performs worse this way compared to the setups without the
JA
.
It seems that the information provided by the
JA
part is negligible or even a hindrance for the
GRU
reinforcing the theoretical objections.

III-C
4
Physics-informed neural network with JA regularization

Here, the prediction of a
JA
model is used to add a regularization for the training loss function of the
GRU
inspired by physics-informed machine learning and
physics-informed neural network (PINN)
(see, e.g.,
[
17
,
29
]
), which is an intensively researched topic where prior knowledge in the form of physical equations is incorporated into data-driven models. The underlying idea is that the physical equation supports the training process in terms of convergence speed and robustness against measurement noise. Furthermore, it should provide a better extrapolation accuracy compared to a purely data driven model.

The prediction function for this model type is the same as in (
20
)

\hat{\tilde{H}}_{k}=\hat{\tilde{H}}_{\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{GRU}}}\left(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{g}_{k-1}\right)=\bm{g}_{k}^{(0)},

(39)

but the training loss is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{JA},\mathrm{GRU}}=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{RMSE}}+\lambda_{w}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{JA}},

(40)

where the regularization term
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{JA}}
is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{JA}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{k_{2}-k_{1}+1}\sum_{k=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}\left|e_{\mathrm{JA}}\right|^{2}},

(41)

with

e_{\mathrm{JA}}=\Delta\hat{H}_{\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{JA}}}(\hat{H}_{k-1},B_{k-1},B_{k})-(\hat{H}_{k}-\hat{H}_{k-1}).

(42)

The weighting factor between the two loss components is
\lambda_{w}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
Note that
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{JA}}
and
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{GRU}}
are trained at the same time with this specific setup.

In the presented setup, the potential of the PINN approach is likely not properly utilized.
This approach should be providing the
GRU-P
with an extrapolation railing, the
JA
is likely incapable of providing significant help due to its relatively poor prediction performance.

III-D

Preisach

The Preisach model
[
34
]
is a phenomenological approximation of the hysteresis effect observed in magnetic materials.
A differentiable version of the Preisach model akin to the one presented in
[
35
]
has been implemented
6
6

6

Open source repository corresponding to
[
35
]
for the differentiable Preisach model:
https://github.com/roussel-ryan/diff_hysteresis
.
. Due to its differentiability, the model may be optimized via gradient descent in the same framework as the other models.

The main components of the Preisach model are the hysteron density function
\mu(\alpha,\beta):\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and the hysteron operator
\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}
.
The prediction function of the differentiable Preisach model is
[
35
]

B_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\bm{\mu}_{i}\gamma(H_{k},H_{k-1},\bm{\gamma}_{k-1,i},\alpha_{i},\beta_{i},T)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\bm{\mu}_{i}\bm{\gamma}_{k,i},

(43)

where
\bm{\mu}_{i}=\mu(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
is a discretized approximation of the hysteron density function with
i\in\{0,\dots N-1\}
, and

\displaystyle\gamma(H_{k},H_{k-1},\gamma_{k-1},\alpha_{i},\beta_{i},T)

(44)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathrm{min}\left(\gamma_{k-1}+\mathrm{tanh}\left(\frac{H_{k}-\beta_{i}}{\|T\|}\right),1\right)&\text{if $H_{k}>H_{k-1}$}\\
\mathrm{max}\left(\gamma_{k-1}-\mathrm{tanh}\left(\frac{-H_{k}+\alpha_{i}}{\|T\|}\right),-1\right)&\text{if $H_{k}\leq H_{k-1}$}\\
\end{cases}

is the differentiable hysteron operator (proposed in the supplementary material of
[
35
]
),
T\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a parameter controlling the rate of change for the hysteron operator (
T=10^{-3}
was utilized for all computations), and
\bm{\gamma}_{k-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
are the values that were computed by the hysteron operators in the last step.

The model was implemented with a static, equally spaced grid of
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
values and the corresponding values for the hysteron density vector
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
were optimized using trajectory rollout and stochastic gradient descent (see
[
35
]
).
Furthermore, the output function (
43
) was adapted to

\hat{\tilde{B}}_{k}=\omega_{2}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\bm{\mu}_{i}\bm{\gamma}_{k,i}\right)+\omega_{1}\tilde{H}_{k}+\omega_{0},

(45)

resulting in the following vector of tunable parameters for the differentiable Preisach model:

\bm{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\omega_{0}&\omega_{1}&\omega_{2}&\bm{\mu}\end{bmatrix}.

(46)

In a preliminary test, the Preisach model (
45
) was compared to a basic
GRU-P
.
Both were trained on the same training data and evaluated on the same unseen test data from the material 3C90 that was gathered with
f_{\mathrm{sw}}=$320\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}$
at
\vartheta=$25\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
.
Exemplary trajectories from the training data are depicted in Fig.
4

and multiple metrics for the prediction performance are reported in Tab.
I
.
The considered metrics are the
mean squared error (MSE)

\mathrm{MSE}=\frac{1}{k_{2}-k_{1}+1}\sum_{k=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}e_{k}^{2},

(47)

with
e_{k}=\hat{\tilde{H}}_{k}-\tilde{H}_{k}
,
the
mean absolute error (MAE)

\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{k_{2}-k_{1}+1}\sum_{k=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}|e_{k}|,

(48)

and the
worst case error (WCE)

\mathrm{WCE}=\max\left(\left\{|e_{k_{1}}|,|e_{k_{1}+1}|,\dots,|e_{k_{2}}|\right\}\right).

(49)

III-E

LLG-based modeling

The idea of this approach is once more to utilize physics-informed machine learning (cf. Sec.
III-C4
) to incorporate a physical equation with the hidden state of the
RNNs
.
This could provide a physical interpretation for the hidden state, which could yield insights into the internal structure of the material, and give the
RNN
a guideline on how the hidden state should propagate.

The
LLG
equation deals with the transfer of spin torques on a micromagnetic scale
[
1
]
:

\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial t}=-\frac{\gamma}{1+\alpha_{\mathrm{d}}^{2}}\bm{m}\times\bm{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}-\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{d}}\gamma}{1+\alpha_{\mathrm{d}}^{2}}\bm{m}\times\left(\bm{m}\times\bm{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}\right),

(50)

where
\alpha_{\mathrm{d}}
is a dimensionless damping parameter,
\gamma
is the reduced gyromagnetic ratio,
\bm{m}
is the unit-vector field representation of the magnetization, and
\bm{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
is the effective magnetic field resulting from all relevant interactions within the material
[
1
]
.
The effective field is dependent on the location of the magnetization in the material (and local magnetization components), therefore, (
50
) is a
partial differential equation (PDE)
governed via

\frac{\partial\bm{m}(\bm{z})}{\partial t}=f\left(\bm{m}(\bm{z}),\bm{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{z})\right),

(51)

which may be approximated on a discretized space (exemplary in 2D)

\frac{\partial\bm{m}(i,j)}{\partial t}=f\left(\bm{m}(i,j),\bm{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}(i,j)\right),

(52)

where
(i,j)
corresponds to the coordinates
\bm{z}=\Delta z\cdot\begin{bmatrix}i&j\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}
.
The discretized
PDE
may be solved using typical numerical methods such as
ODE
solvers.

III-E
1
LLG PDE simulation

As a preparation step, it is tested how well a simulation of the
LLG
equation may approximate macroscopic hysteresis effects by summing over the microscopic magnetization. For this, the Python micromagnetic simulation NeuralMag
7
7

7

Open source repository for the utilized micromagnetic simulator:
https://gitlab.com/neuralmag/neuralmag

[
1
]
was utilized.

The main issue is that not only the spatial scale is not properly fitting, but also the time scale.
The considered data was measured with
\tau=$62.5\text{\,}\mathrm{ns}$
, while such micromagnetic simulations are generally performed at
\tau\approx$10\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}$
or with even smaller time steps (Fig.
10
in
[
41
]
), which would require roughly
6000
steps per data point from the
MC2
data base.
This makes the simulation at the required timescale computationally infeasible.

III-E
2
Vector field GRU (GRU-V)

The hidden state
\bm{g}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{g}}}
for this model is arranged in a 2D grid of 2D vectors which are intended to approximate a discretized vector field of the magnetization, i.e.,
\bm{g}_{k}(i,j)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

\forall

i\in\{0,1,\dots,N_{\mathrm{g}}\}
and
j\in\{0,1,\dots,N_{\mathrm{g}}\}
with
N_{\mathrm{g}}=\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{g}}/2}-1\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
(cf. Fig.
5
).
The prediction function for this model was intended to be

\hat{H}_{k}=\hat{H}_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{g}_{k-1}\right)=\frac{{B}_{k}}{\mu_{0}}-\hat{M}_{k},

(53)

where

\hat{M}_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{\mathrm{g}}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\mathrm{g}}}\bm{g}_{k}(i,j)^{(0)}\right).

(54)

Due to the numerical issues already encountered for the
GRU-M
in (
21
), the prediction function

\hat{\tilde{H}}_{k}=\hat{\tilde{H}}_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{g}_{k-1}\right)=\tilde{B}_{k}-\sum_{i=0}^{N_{\mathrm{g}}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\mathrm{g}}}\bm{g}_{k}(i,j)^{(0)}\right)

(55)

was utilized instead. Here,
\bm{g}_{k}(i,j)^{(0)}
refers to the first element of the two-dimensional vector at grid position
(i,j)
, which is interpreted as the magnetization direction that coincides with the measurement data.

Initially, the plan was made to utilize the
LLG
as a regularization term for the hidden state of this model so that the states can interact with each other and, therefore, provide the state with physical interpretability.
However, due to the infeasibility of the isolated
LLG
simulation, the idea was not investigated further.
Instead, the
vector field GRU (GRU-V)
is essentially a different prediction function compared to
GRU-P
.
One further issue is that due to the complexity of the hidden state’s interpretation, it is not feasible to warm it up the same way as the
GRU-P
.

IV-A

Model selection for submission to MC2

The final model selection for the submission to
MC2
was done before
\mathcal{D}^{(m_{2})}_{\mathrm{test}}
was available in order to have a true training-test split.
Therefore, the following considerations are done purely on
\mathcal{D}^{(m_{2})}_{\mathrm{train}}
for each of the materials.

From previous tests, the decision was already made to utilize the
GRU-P
model.
An important aspect of this model is the size, which is defined by the number of hidden units and the number of inputs.
In the following, the model size will be changed by varying the number of hidden units in the
RNNs
.
The tradeoff between the model size measured by the number of parameters and the model accuracy is to be investigated.
The model accuracies are measured by the
SRE
(
5
) and
NERE
(
6
) score on unseen test data.
For the following test, for each of the materials
\mathcal{D}^{(m_{2})}_{\mathrm{train}}
is split into a separate training
\mathcal{D}^{(m_{2})}_{\mathrm{train,train}}
, evaluation
\mathcal{D}^{(m_{2})}_{\mathrm{train,eval}}
, and test set
\mathcal{D}^{(m_{2})}_{\mathrm{train,test}}
.

Overall, the choice for the submitted models is made for a good tradeoff between model size and accuracy motivated by the preliminary Pareto investigation depicted in Fig.
6
.
However, it is not claimed that this is the best choice for the general case. For instance, if the chosen model application permits a higher computation budget and memory footprint or if there are specific accuracy constraints to the performance of the model, a larger or even smaller model might be better suited.
Hence, the good model size vs. accuracy scalability of the GRU-based approach through the choice of cell count is one of its strengths and allows for easy adaptation depending on application requirements.

V
Conclusion

A variety of modeling structures for the prediction of time-resolved magnetic material behavior were developed, implemented, and compared in the scope of this work.
It was found that an almost purely data-driven model utilizing a single
GRU
layer could provide excellent parameter efficiency in terms of model size to model accuracy for small model sizes.
As further incorporation of physically motivated ideas could not improve the performance of this model,
it remains an open question how to properly inject physically motivated structure into the model and what kind of physical equations are actually helpful for the macroscopic problem at hand.

To complement the presented work in the future, interesting directions might be to perform a hyperparameter optimization for the proposed model structures in order to ensure that they perform as best as possible.
Furthermore, the presented Pareto investigations should be extended with the other models that were submitted for
MC2
to enable proper comparison throughout different parameterizations.
Additionally, it would be interesting to consider transfer learning for the provided model structures such that the model can be simply adapted to a new material with only limited measurement data (cf.
[
25
]
for a steady-state example of this).
