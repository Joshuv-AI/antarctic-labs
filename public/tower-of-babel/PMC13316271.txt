Olvi-Vec oncolytic immunotherapy and reversal of platinum resistance in ovarian cancer
Authors: Yong A Yu, Robert W Holloway, Alberto A Mendivil, Sarfraz Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study investigated immune and gene expression modulation of tumor microenvironment (TME) by oncolytic vaccinia virus Olvi-Vec as an immunotherapy and subsequent clinical impact on platinum sensitivity in ovarian cancer (OC).Methods. We studied synergy between platinum and Olvi-Vec in mouse model of platinum-resistant OC (PROC). Subsequently, phase 1b/2 VIRO-15 study was completed in heavily pretreated patients with platinum-resistant/refractory OC (PRROC). To assess Olvi-Vec infection of tumor cells, VIRO-15 patients' ascitic fluid was analyzed by cytology. To evaluate changes in gene expression patterns, paired tumor biopsies obtained before and after Olvi-Vec treatment were analyzed by multiplex immunohistochemistry (IHC) and RNA profiling. Progression-free survival (PFS) from phase-2 patients were compared to their last line of platinum therapy.ResultsIn the preclinical study, durable antitumor effect was achieved through combination treatment with Olvi-Vec and cisplatin in mouse model of PROC. Cytologic analysis in VIRO-15 showed viral infection and eradication of tumor cells in patients' ascitic fluid. Multiplex IHC analysis demonstrated large influx of CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes activated by Olvi-Vec. RNA profiling analysis identified expression of genes in tumors that were down-regulated or up-regulated after Olvi-Vec treatment. These transcriptional changes suggest four biologically relevant expression patterns associated with immune activation, viral infection, sensitization to chemotherapy, anticancer activity and survival. Combination therapy of Olvi-Vec and platinum-doublet chemotherapy resulted in a clinically meaningful benefit in the VIRO-15 study, reversing the typical trend of deteriorating PFS, and with clinical reversal of platinum resistance.ConclusionOlvi-Vec treatment can favorably modify the TME in OC and may explain the apparent reversal of platinum resistance following platinum rechallenge.Trial Registration:ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT02759588.Keywords: Ovarian cancer, Oncolytic immunotherapy, Immunochemotherapy, Platinum-resistant, Platinum-refractory, Immune modulation and activation, Reverse platinum resistance or refractoriness

Introduction

Olvimulogene nanivacirepvec (Olvi-Vec; aka GL-ONC1; laboratory name: GLV-1 h68) is a modified oncolytic vaccinia virus that selectively infects, replicates, and kills malignant cells (Yu et al., 2004; Zhang et al., 2009; Ascierto et al., 2011). Preclinical models have demonstrated that as an oncolytic immunotherapy, Olvi-Vec activates both innate immunity via a proinflammatory response and adaptive immunity (Worschech et al., 2009; Yu et al., 2009) and modifies the tumor microenvironment (TME). The immune and gene expression modulation of the TME may promote an environment that favors the re-sensitization of treatment-resistant tumors to chemotherapy rechallenge (Yu et al., 2009; Binz et al., 2017). In a previous phase 1b dose escalation study, Olvi-Vec monotherapy was administered by intraperitoneal infusion to patients with heavily pretreated platinum-resistant/refractory ovarian cancer (PRROC) and demonstrated: (1) oncolysis and eradication of tumor cells in ascites, (2) immune activation, and (3) disease control (Manyam et al., 2021). In another phase 1 dose escalation study, Olvi-Vec administered by intrapleural infusion to patients with malignant pleural effusions showed a correlation of Olvi-Vec treatment to a decrease in tumor cell density and an increase in immune cell density (Chintala et al., 2023).

Based on the promising phase 1b study results in ovarian cancer (Manyam et al., 2021), a phase 2 study (VIRO-15) was conducted to investigate Olvi-Vec followed by platinum-doublet chemotherapy with or without bevacizumab in PRROC. This trial demonstrated promising anticancer activity and a survival benefit that exceeded historically expected outcomes from platinum rechallenge in a similar patient population. Notably, an objective response rate of 54% was reported from the VIRO-15 study, which more closely approximates the expected response in platinum-sensitive patients. This clinically important observation suggests that Olvi-Vec can induce a clinical reversal of platinum resistance or refractoriness in patients with ovarian cancer (Holloway et al., 2023a).

Materials and methods

Pre-clinical in vivo study

The human high-grade serous OVCAR3 cell line, with known resistance to platinum, was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. OVCAR3 tumors were developed in 6–8-week-old female nude mice (NCI:Hsd:athymic nude-Foxn1nu; Harlan) by implanting 5 × 106 OVCAR3 cells subcutaneously on the right hind leg. Tumor growth was recorded once a week in three dimensions using a digital caliper. Tumor volume was calculated as: [(length × width × height) / 2] and reported in mm3. Both the Olvi-Vec monotherapy group and Olvi-Vec and cisplatin combination group received an intravenous dose of 2 × 106 pfu of Olvi-Vec virus at 41 days after OVCAR3 tumor implantation. Cisplatin doses (5 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection once per day on days 10, 11, 13, and 14 after the virus injection; total of 20 mg/kg, equivalent to a total of 60 mg/m2 human dose) were given in corresponding groups, either as monotherapy or as combination therapy.

Phase 2 VIRO-15 clinical study design and participants

Patients with PRROC, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 or 1 and documented progressive disease at screening were enrolled in a phase 2 prospective study (VIRO-15 trial, NCT02759588). Between September 2016 and September 2019, 27 patients with PRROC who experienced disease progression within six months of their most recent platinum-based treatment were treated with Olvi-Vec, administered intraperitoneally via a catheter at 3 × 109 pfu/day on two consecutive days. The median number of prior lines of chemotherapy was 4 (range 2 to 9). Intraperitoneal Olvi-Vec was followed by platinum-doublet chemotherapy with or without bevacizumab in approximately 6 weeks. Non‑platinum single-agent therapies with or without bevacizumab were encouraged as continued therapy after discontinuation of platinum at the investigator's discretion. The primary endpoints were objective response rate (ORR) by RECIST1.1 & CA-125, and progression-free survival (PFS) by RECIST1.1. Details of the phase 2 study are described in Holloway et al. (Holloway et al., 2023a).

Translational analyses in the VIRO-15 study

Olvi-Vec infection of cancer cells in ascitic fluid was analyzed using a vaccinia-virus-specific polyclonal antibody (ab117453 from Abcam; brown staining) and counterstained with methyl green (light green staining). Paired tumor biopsies were obtained before and after Olvi-Vec treatment (before any subsequent chemotherapy and bevacizumab) in patients who consented to biopsies. The translational analyses on tumor biopsies included measurement of intra-tumoral T-cells by multiplex immunohistochemistry (IHC; Precision for Medicine), and the immune-related genetic signatures in tumors by RNA profiling with PanCancer IO 360™ Gene Expression Panel of 770 genes (NanoString). In addition, cytology analysis of ascitic fluid was performed in consented patients.

Results

Pre-clinical study of Olvi-Vec in a mouse model with platinum-resistant ovarian cancer

In vivo animal study was conducted to investigate the therapeutic effect of Olvi-Vec administered with or without cisplatin on the progression of OVCAR3 tumors (Fig. 1A; n = 6–8 mice per treatment group). The best response and most durable tumor reduction was achieved in the combination therapy group, which showed synergistic antitumor effect, as compared to cisplatin monotherapy or Olvi-Vec monotherapy alone. Visual examination of the tumors in the cisplatin alone group showed the typical hemorrhagic appearance (Fig. 1B), commonly associated with rapid, invasive growth patterns in xenograft tumor models. The virus-treated group demonstrated less hemorrhage than the cisplatin alone group. The virus and cisplatin combination-treated group showed no hemorrhage, but rather necrotic tumor cells, indicating more complete cell death, confirmed by histology (Fig. 1C) (Frentzen et al., 2009).

Olvi-Vec and Cisplatin Treatment in an Animal Model of Platinum-Resistant Ovarian Cancer. A: Combination therapy of Olvi-Vec and cisplatin results in a synergistic antitumor effect in an animal model of platinum-resistant ovarian cancer. B: Tumors from the combination treatment group (right panel) did not show a hemorrhagic appearance, as those in the cisplatin group (left panel). C: Tumors from the combination treatment group (left lower panel) were found to contain non-viable remnant tumor tissue by histology.

Olvi-Vec infection of tumor cells and induction of T-cell influx in patients with PRROC

To investigate Olvi-Vec infection of tumor cells and induced immune activation, cytology and histology analyses were performed in patients' ascitic fluid. Ascitic fluid obtained immediately before Olvi-Vec infusion (n = 21) typically contained a low number of lymphocytes in comparison to cancer cell aggregates (Fig. 2A). In contrast, a notably and consistently higher number of lymphocytes compared to cancer cells was detectable as early as 2 days after initiation of Olvi-Vec treatment (Fig. 2B). Antibody staining confirmed virus infection of tumor cells (Fig. 2B).

A: Ascites samples obtained immediately before Olvi-Vec infusion contain a relatively low number of lymphocytes in comparison to cancer cell aggregates. B: In contrast, a notably higher number of lymphocytes compared to cancer cells (infected by Olvi-Vec) is detectable as early as 2 days after initiation of Olvi-Vec treatment. Both samples were stained in parallel using a vaccinia-virus-specific polyclonal antibody preparation (ab117453; brown) and counterstained with methyl green (light green). Exemplary cancer cell aggregates and lymphocytes are indicated by asterisks and arrows, respectively.

Multiplex IHC analysis of paired tumor biopsies (n = 8) from pre- and post-treatment demonstrated an influx of CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), suggesting activation by Olvi-Vec (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, a “left-shift” of CD8+ T-cells on the HALO™ Infiltration Histogram charts (Fig. 3B) indicates an increased number of CD8+ T-cells penetrating deeper into the epithelial region of tumor tissue where the cancerous cells were located, away from stroma. Best overall responses per RECIST 1.1 criteria in these representative patients with paired biopsy comparisons (#15 A-16, 15 A-18, 15 A-19, 15 A-23) were partial response, progressive disease, stable disease, and partial response, respectively. PFS and overall survival for these patients were 13.0, 4.3, 9.6, & 11.4+, and 23.5, 12.8, 23.8+ & 47.3+ months, respectively.

A “left-shift” on the histograms of CD8+ T-cells before and after Olvi-Vec treatment indicates T-cells entering deeper into the epithelial region of tumor tissue, where the tumor cells are located, away from the stroma.

Olvi-Vec-induced modification of tumor gene expression

To further evaluate changes in the TME following Olvi-Vec administration in patients, gene expression analysis was performed using the NanoString PanCancer IO 360 Gene Expression Panel. This RNA profiling platform assesses 770 genes representing key biological pathways involved in the dynamic interplay between the TME and the immune response in cancer. Paired tumor biopsies were collected at baseline and within six weeks following virotherapy from four consenting patients to assess treatment-associated transcriptional changes.

The expressions of BRD3, CD276, CDH11, DTX3L, DUSP1, EIF2AK2, HERC6, IFIT1, ITGA6, MX1, OAS1/2/3, RSAD2, and SFRP4 were found to be down-regulated, and BBC3, H2AFX, ICOSLG, NBN, RELB, SFXN1, STAT1, and TNF were up-regulated in tumors after Olvi-Vec treatment compared with baseline. These transcriptional changes, unanimous among patients, suggest four biologically relevant expression patterns with potential clinical implications.

• Immune activation and T-cell recruitment.

Upregulation of ICOSLG, RELB, TNF, and STAT1, together with downregulation of CD276 and DTX3L, suggests enhanced immune activation and T-cell recruitment. STAT1 and TNF are well-established mediators of inflammatory signaling and immune activation, while ICOSLG promotes T-cell co-stimulation. Downregulation of CD276, an immune checkpoint molecule, may further support conversion of immunologically “cold” tumors into more inflamed or “hot” tumors.

• Suppression of antiviral response and enhanced viral permissivity.

Downregulation of DTX3L, EIF2AK2, HERC6, IFIT1, MX1, RSAD2, and OAS1/2/3 suggests attenuation of interferon-mediated antiviral signaling. These genes are classical interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) involved in antiviral defense, and their suppression may further facilitate viral replication and enhance oncolytic activity.

• Tumor cell death and chemotherapy sensitization.

Upregulation of BBC3, H2AFX, NBN, and STAT1, along with downregulation of BRD3, CDH11, and DUSP1, is consistent with activation of DNA damage responses and apoptotic signaling. BBC3 promotes apoptosis, while H2AFX and NBN are markers of DNA damage and repair. Downregulation of DUSP1 and CDH11 has been associated with increased sensitivity to chemotherapy and reduced tumor survival signaling.

• Enhanced anticancer activity and favorable prognostic biology.

Upregulation of BBC3, SFXN1, and STAT1, combined with downregulation of BRD3, DUSP1, HERC6, ITGA6, MX1, SFRP4, and EIF2AK2, is consistent with gene expression patterns associated with reduced tumor survival signaling and increased antitumor activity. Several of these genes, including STAT1 and BBC3, have been associated with improved treatment response and favorable prognostic biology.

Overall, these RNA profiling data suggest enhanced viral permissivity, immune activation, tumor cell death, and favorable TME remodeling following Olvi-Vec treatment. These findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating that oncolytic viruses can modulate antiviral defenses, promote immune activation, and enhance sensitivity to chemotherapy and other anticancer therapies (Alwithenani et al., 2025).

Olvi-Vec-primed immunochemotherapy generated a meaningful clinical benefit of PFS

PFS is a well-validated and clinically relevant endpoint in ovarian cancer. In each subsequent line of cancer therapy, PFS generally decreases due to increasingly therapy-resistant disease. To investigate the benefit of Olvi-Vec, a PFS ratio analysis was performed per the von Hoff method (Von Hoff et al., 2010), which compares patients' treatment and response for a new therapy to the patient's last line of therapy, such that the patient's most recent history serves as their own control. The VIRO-15 clinical dataset was used to compare PFS+1 from post-Olvi-Vec treatment to PFS−1 from pre-Olvi-Vec treatment. The Kaplan-Meier survival curves (left) show the median PFS+1 post-Olvi-Vec of 11.0 months vs. the median PFS−1 pre-Olvi-Vec of 4.5 months in the same patients (Fig. 4A). The right panel illustrates that 74% of patients are on or to the left [i.e., PFS ratio (PFS+1/PFS−1) ≥1.3] of the ‘von Hoff line’, defined at a PFS ratio of 1.3 (Von Hoff et al., 2010). This clinically relevant finding suggests that Olvi-Vec-primed immunochemotherapy may reverse the trend of diminishing PFS outcomes with increased lines of treatment (Hanker et al., 2012; Garzon et al., 2020; Sallinen et al., 2021) and provide a potential new treatment alternative for patients with PRROC.

Intra-patient comparisons of PFS. A: Comparison Before and After Olvi-Vec Treatment in the Same Patients. The Kaplan-Meier survival curves (left panel) show the median PFS (i.e., PFS+1 post-Olvi-Vec) of 11.0 months from subsequent chemotherapy after Olvi-Vec vs. the median PFS (i.e., PFS−1 pre-Olvi-Vec) of 4.5 months from the last prior line of therapy before Olvi-Vec in the same patients. The right panel illustrates that 74% of patients are on or to the left of the ‘von Hoff line’ of PFS ratio (PFS+1/PFS−1) at 1.3, suggesting a clinically meaningful benefit following Olvi-Vec-primed immunochemotherapy relative to the last prior line of therapy. B: A comparative analysis of PFS from the current platinum-based VIRO-15 regimen against the last prior platinum-based regimen provides a more stringent benchmark than comparisons to heterogeneous therapies in the last prior line. The VIRO-15 platinum regimen nearly doubled the median PFS: 11.0 months compared to 5.9 months (p < 0.001) for the last prior platinum line, providing evidence of clinical reversal of platinum resistance. Abbreviations: PFS, progression-free survival; mPFS, median progression-free survival; mos, months.

The last prior line of therapy often includes single-agent or investigational treatments, which may not represent the most robust benchmarks for clinical efficacy. Given that platinum remains the most active agent even in platinum-resistant settings (Yerram et al., 2026), an intra-patient comparison of platinum-based therapies offers a more clinically relevant assessment. Specifically, comparing the PFS of the platinum-based regimen in the VIRO-15 study to the PFS of each patient's most recent prior platinum-based regimen based on medical records reveals a significant clinical benefit. Median PFS was 11.0 months (95% CI: 6.7–13.0) for the VIRO-15 platinum regimen versus 5.9 months (95% CI: 4.1–7.3; p < 0.001) for the last prior platinum regimen (Fig. 4B), indicating clinical reversal of platinum resistance. In a more stringent analysis, assuming no censorship on PFS for the VIRO-15 patients, median PFS was 10.0 months (95% CI: 6.4–11.4) for the VIRO-15 platinum regimen versus 5.9 months (95% CI: 4.1–7.3; p = 0.002) for the last prior platinum regimen, with a net improvement of ∼4 months on median PFS. In a further subgroup analysis of patients with 2–3 vs. 4–9 prior lines, a similar PFS [10.0 months (95%CI: 1.1 – NA) vs. 11.0 months (95%CI: 9.6–13.0); p = 0.951] and OS [15.9 months (95%CI: 2.1–23.8) vs. 15.7 months (95%CI: 12.3–23.8); p = 0.724] were observed, indicating a broad clinical benefit in PRROC patients regardless of prior treatment history.

Discussion

Conventional cytotoxic chemotherapies have been the cornerstone of management for advanced, recurrent ovarian cancer. It is clear that platinum response and PFS diminish with each subsequent recurrence (Hanker et al., 2012). Identifying agents that re-sensitize conventional chemotherapies in patients with chemotherapy-resistant tumors is a much sought-after goal in oncology drug development.

Olvi-Vec is a modified vaccinia virus designed for oncolytic immunotherapy of cancers. Olvi-Vec selectively replicates in malignant cells due to inactivation of viral thymidine kinase, an enzyme that is critical for the lifecycle of vaccinia in senescent cells, but not cancer cells. After tumor cell infection by Olvi-Vec, the vaccinia life cycle progresses in the tumor cells' cytoplasm and ultimately results in lysis of tumor cells, release of tumor (neo)antigens, and the establishment of long-term antitumor immunity. In preclinical studies of Olvi-Vec, robust antitumor activities were observed in a wide range of tumor types (Haddad et al., 2017). Olvi-Vec has been shown to activate both innate and adaptive immune responses (Worschech et al., 2009; Yu et al., 2009). The immune activation and changes in tumor gene expression may promote a condition that favors the re-sensitization of tumors to platinum-based chemotherapy (Yu et al., 2009; Binz et al., 2017; Emens and Middleton, 2015; Wang et al., 2016). In addition, previous preclinical and single-arm clinical studies provide evidence that the combination of Olvi-Vec with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and/or radiotherapy may result in synergistic antitumor activities (Yu et al., 2009; Binz et al., 2017; Frentzen et al., 2009; Buckel et al., 2013; Dai et al., 2014; Mell et al., 2017; Smith et al., 2019).

This paper provides preclinical evidence for the synergistic antitumor effect of Olvi-Vec and platinum chemotherapy in a platinum-resistant ovarian cancer model in vivo. Translational analyses of patient-derived tissues in the VIRO-15 clinical study demonstrate the immune modulatory effects and gene expression changes by Olvi-Vec to the TME. RNA profiling data from the VIRO-15 study suggests that Olvi-Vec acts as a living primer. Downregulation of survival and antiviral genes (DUSP1, EIF2AK2, HERC6, MX1, and OAS1/2/3) is consistent with enhanced viral permissivity and tumor vulnerability (Ascierto et al., 2011; Matveeva and Chumakov, 2018). Concurrent upregulation of BBC3, ICOSLG, RELB, STAT1, and TNF indicates transformation of the TME into a pro-apoptotic, immune-active state (Wang et al., 2016; Song et al., 2007). These findings provide further insights into the mechanisms of action (MOA) of Olvi-Vec as an oncolytic immunotherapy (Fig. 5). The VIRO-15 clinical study results suggest that sequential treatments of Olvi-Vec followed by a platinum-based regimen can produce a significant antitumor effect not expected in heavily treated patients with PRROC. Furthermore, a comparative analysis of PFS from the current platinum-based VIRO-15 regimen against the last prior platinum-based regimen provides a more stringent benchmark than comparisons to heterogeneous therapies in the last prior line. In this intra-patient analysis, the VIRO-15 platinum regimen nearly doubled the median PFS: 11.0 months compared to 5.9 months (p < 0.001) for the last prior platinum line (or 10.0 vs. 5.9 months, p = 0.002, if with more stringent analysis without censorship), providing evidence of clinical reversal of platinum resistance.

The VIRO-15 study's high response rate and clinically meaningful prolonged survival in heavily pre-treated patients with PRROC (Holloway et al., 2023a) may be the result of a mutual sensitization between oncolytic vaccinia virus and chemotherapy ± bevacizumab. Song et al. (Song et al., 2007) demonstrated the mechanism of prime and boost from vaccinia virus in combination with chemotherapy in animal studies. The authors showed that cisplatin or cyclophosphamide after vaccinia virus led to complete regression of established tumors, which was associated with increased tumor-specific CD8+ T-cell immune response. These observations mirror what was clinically shown in the VIRO-15 trial. In the Song et al. study, cytotoxic therapies alone failed to induce a significant enhancement of the tumor-specific CD8+ T-cell immune response, indicating that such agents, including cisplatin or cyclophosphamide, do not have enough immuno-adjuvancy (i.e., result in sufficient presentation of tumor neoantigens) to prime a tumor-specific naive CD8+ T-cell. The authors concluded that immune priming by vaccinia, followed by a boost from chemotherapy, is particularly effective against established tumors by driving tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T-cell immune response.

Pre-clinical and clinical data support the staggered approach of oncolytic immunotherapy and chemotherapy (Binz et al., 2017; Manyam et al., 2021; Holloway et al., 2023a; Song et al., 2007; Wennier et al., 2012; Lauer et al., 2018; Russell et al., 2019). In addition to “Prime & Boost” as described above, it is known that certain chemotherapy agents can cause immunogenic cell death (ICD) through diverse pathways (Emens and Middleton, 2015). Carboplatin, paclitaxel, and gemcitabine are known to decrease tumor-induced immune suppression by abrogating myeloid-derived suppressor cells and T-reg activities (Emens and Middleton, 2015; Weir et al., 2011). In the VIRO-15 study, Olvi-Vec treatment was associated with an influx of CD8+ TILs, which has typically been considered a favorable prognostic factor in ovarian cancer (Mai et al., 2025; Pinto et al., 2018; Santoiemma et al., 2015). It has been shown that the presence of CD8+ effector T cells, via activated STAT1 signaling, abrogates stromal-mediated chemoresistance to platinum in ovarian cancer by altering glutathione and cystine metabolism in fibroblasts (Wang et al., 2016). Based on preclinical, clinical and translational assessments, Olvi-Vec converts immunologically ‘cold’ tumors to ‘hot’ tumors (Alwithenani et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2016; Ochoa de Olza et al., 2020) with induced TILs, alters tumor gene expression, relieves hypoxia, re-polarizes tumor-associated macrophages from M2 to M1 type (Kilinc et al., 2016), and favorably modifies the TME to abrogate platinum resistance (Manyam et al., 2021; Holloway et al., 2023a; Wang et al., 2016).

PRROC is typically considered a ‘cold’ tumor with low intraepithelial infiltration of CD8+ T-lymphocytes (Mariya et al., 2014), which could explain the poor efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) as monotherapy (Hamanishi et al., 2015; Matulonis et al., 2019). As pointed out by Le Saux et al. (Le Saux et al., 2021), “The main challenge for ICI in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer is to turn this ‘cold’ tumor into ‘inflamed’ by favoring infiltration of functional cytotoxic T-cells.” Combination therapies with checkpoint inhibitors have been investigated in PRROC with modest results (Konstantinopoulos et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2019; Pujade-Lauraine et al., 2021). The potential reasons for the lack of efficacy may be due to insufficient immune priming and activation of effector T-cells, and/or lack of T-cell trafficking into tumors. Recently, Colombo et al. (Colombo et al., 2025) reported interim results from the phase 3 ENGOT-ov65/Keynote-B96 trial of pembrolizumab vs. placebo, + weekly paclitaxel ± bevacizumab in PROC patients, showing OS improvements (18.2 vs. 14.0 months in the PD-L1 CPS ≥ 1 population; HR = 0.76, p = 0.005). It has been theorized that weekly paclitaxel may alter the TME, accounting for improved outcomes in PROC. Therefore, alternative strategies are needed to overcome such challenges facing immunotherapy in the PRROC setting. Olvi-Vec oncolytic immunotherapy is an ideal immune-modulating agent to induce an inflamed tumor immune microenvironment by induction of proinflammatory cytokines/chemokines and to drive the intraepithelial infiltration of CD8+ T-cells (Manyam et al., 2021; Holloway et al., 2023a; Lauer et al., 2018; Holloway et al., 2023b). The combination of virus-mediated immune priming with ICD and further boost by subsequent platinum-doublet chemotherapy together with anti-angiogenic therapy may generate significantly enhanced anti-tumor activity. The currently enrolling phase 3 OnPrime/GOG-3076 randomized clinical trial will provide more answers to Olvi-Vec's activity in PRROC [41; NCT 05281471]. Based on the MOA of Olvi-Vec, it is also warranted to combine Olvi-Vec with other therapeutic modalities, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, other cytotoxic chemotherapeutic agents, and radiotherapy, to potentially further enhance clinical outcomes.
