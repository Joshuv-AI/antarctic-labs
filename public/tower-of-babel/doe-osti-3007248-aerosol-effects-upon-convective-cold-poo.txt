 Establishing General Trends

1. Abstract

This project explored how tiny airborne particles—those that help form cloud droplets and
those that can trigger ice formation—affect the precipitation created by thunderstorms, which
in turn can affect the development of thunderstorms. Using a combination of field observations
from a major research campaign in Argentina (CACTI) and high-resolution, 3D computer
simulations of the storms, the studies examined how variations in cloud-forming aerosols
influence when storms begin to rain, how much ice they produce internally, and how strongly
they generate “cold pools,” the outflows of cool air that spread across the ground beneath
storms. The first study focused on cloud-condensation nuclei (CCN), showing that higher
concentrations of these particles can delay the onset of rain in ordinary thunderstorms and
weaken the resulting cold pools, which are important for subsequent storm growth and
organization. The second study developed a new method for representing ice-nucleating
particles (INPs) inside weather models—an approach that tracks how these particles become
incorporated into raindrops and ice particles as they grow. This improved treatment revealed
that thunderstorms are generally more sensitive to changes in CCN than to changes in INPs,
once this realistic accumulation process is included. Together, the studies highlight how the
microscopic world of aerosols can exert a surprisingly large influence on the evolution of
storms, while also demonstrating that storm type and environmental conditions determine how
strong these effects can be.

2. Brief technical descrip5on of most relevant projects/publica5ons

This project examined how aerosol microphysics — specifically cloud condensation nuclei (CCN)
and ice-nucleating particles (INPs, hereafter IF-INPs for immersion-freezing INPs) — affect
mixed-phase convective storms and the cold pools they produce. The motivation was twofold:
(1) cold pools produced by latent cooling of precipitating hydrometeors can organize and
promote upscale convection, so microphysical controls on cold-pool timing/strength may
matter for convective organization and parameterizations; (2) the possible effect of IF-INPs
upon cold pools have rarely been addressed, and prior modeling studies often treated raindrop
freezing in a way that neglects aerosol accumulation into raindrops, possibly biasing INP
impacts. Field observations (CACTI/RELAMPAGO) were combined with idealized numerical
modeling to address these questions.

Original hypotheses

 1. CCN hypothesis (MWR study): Increased environmental CCN delays the onset of first
 surface rainfall in developing convective storms and therefore delays/weakens the first

 cold pools produced by those storms (with potential ramifications for upscale
 convective growth).
 2. INP vs CCN hypothesis (JGR study): The relative roles of IF-INPs and CCN in deep mixed-
 phase convection depend on how INPs are represented; explicitly accounting for IF-INPs
 contained within both cloud drops and raindrops (i.e., aerosol accumulation into large
 drops) will alter the sensitivity of storm precipitation and cold pools to INP
 concentration relative to CCN. In particular, prior volume-dependent raindrop freezing
 parameterizations may overestimate INP effects because they neglect raindrop
 accumulation of IF-INPs.

Data sources and field observations

 • Field campaigns: CACTI observational datasets were primarily used: aircraft CCN and IF-
 INP measurements, shallow convection observations on nine flight days, radar and
 surface networks to estimate cold-pool initiation times for a subset of cases that later
 developed deep convection. Observational analysis focused on drizzle formation and
 timing of first cold-pool detection. Some radar and ground-based measurements from
 CACTI’s sister project, the NSF-based RELAMPAGO field campaign, were also used.

Modeling approaches & algorithm development

 • Idealized numerical simulations (single case and sensitivity suites) were run to
 reproduce representative ordinary-storm and supercell morphologies observed in
 CACTI/RELAMPAGO. Microphysics was altered systematically (e.g., varying CCN number
 concentrations from observed values) to isolate aerosol effects on precipitation
 production, latent cooling, and cold-pool properties.

 • A NewIF immersion-freezing (IF) algorithm was developed and embedded into the NSSL
 bulk microphysics scheme to distribute measured IF-INP activity spectra across both
 cloud drops and larger hydrometeors (raindrops, graupel, hail) by estimating how many
 cloud drops are contained within larger particles (i.e., accounting for aerosol
 accumulation through collisions and riming). Key implementation details and
 assumptions (documented and used in sensitivity experiments) include:
 o The algorithm operates only in cloudy updraft grid boxes.
 o Total number of cloud drops in a grid box is estimated by summing contributions
 from cloud drops and the effective number of cloud drops contained within
 rain/graupel/hail using mean particle volumes and an assumed uniform cloud-
 drop volume. This lets the scheme estimate how many IF-INPs become
 sequestered in larger hydrometeors.
 o The same IF-INP activity spectrum (from measurements) is applied to freezing of
 both cloud drops and raindrops, yielding a physically consistent immersion-
 freezing treatment that includes raindrop aerosol loading.

 • Two axes of sensitivity were explored: (i) CCN number concentration (varied up/down
 from observed values) and (ii) IF-INP activity (magnitude and temperature dependence
 of IF-INP spectra). Simulations examined resulting changes in graupel production, riming
 rates, frozen-hydrometeor mass, rainfall, latent cooling, and cold-pool strength/timing.

Key assumptions and analysis methods

 • The observed CCN and IF-INP number concentrations from earlier on a given day were
 assumed valid in storms that developed later that day.
 • Observational inference of cold-pool initiation time: multi-sensor approach combining
 radar RHI scans (velocity signatures), low-level reflectivity, and collocated surface
 station time series (temperature falls and wind shifts) to identify the first detectable
 cold-pool passage in case studies. This cross-referencing reduces timing uncertainty.

Main findings (scientific & technical results)

 • Aircraft CCN and shallow-convection observations across nine days indicated
 environments with higher CCN tended to have suppressed drizzle formation (fewer
 drizzle drops). Of the cases that later developed deep convection (three cases), two
 (ordinary thunderstorms) showed an apparent delay in first cold-pool timing in the
 higher-CCN environments; however, the relationship was not universal.
 • Idealized simulations of an ordinary storm suggested that increasing CCN reduced
 graupel production and latent cooling in downdrafts, producing weaker, shallower, and
 slower-expanding cold pools — and small delays in cold-pool onset. In contrast,
 simulations of a supercell case indicated that supercell dynamics (strong organized
 updraft/downdraft structures) can dominate over CCN microphysical effects, making
 CCN impacts on cold pools negligible for such storms. The results imply a strong inverse
 relationship between CCN and cold-pool strength/depth for ordinary storms, but not
 necessarily for supercells.
 • When IF-INPs are allowed to accumulate in larger hydrometeors, compensating
 depletion of supercooled liquid water occurs — i.e., increased INP activity in upper
 levels does not necessarily translate into larger storm-scale increases in frozen
 hydrometeor mass at lower levels because IF-INPs become sequestered in falling drops.
 As a result, simulated thunderstorms were relatively insensitive to changes in IF-INP
 magnitude/slope (temperature dependence) compared to CCN variations.
 • Increasing CCN systematically reduced graupel riming rates and thus reduced frozen-
 hydrometeor production, which in turn substantially weakened surface rainfall and cold
 pools in the simulated ordinary convection case. In the JGR simulations, rainfall and cold
 pools were substantially and systematically weakened with increased CCN but showed
 relative insensitivity to IF-INP changes because of the accumulation/compensation
 processes in raindrops.

 Technical/interpretive conclusions & recommendations for the community

 • CCN should be considered in cold-pool parameterizations used in larger-scale models
 (weather and climate), especially for environments where ordinary (non-supercell)
 thunderstorms are favored — because CCN can delay/weakens first precipitation and
 cold pools, thereby affecting convective organization.
 • Representation of INPs matters: traditional volume-dependent raindrop freezing
 parameterizations that neglect aerosol accumulation into raindrops can overestimate
 the sensitivity of storms to INP variations. Models should consider the partitioning of
 INPs between cloud drops and larger hydrometeors (i.e., accumulation processes) when
 assessing INP effects on mixed-phase convection. The NewIF algorithm is an example
 implementation that distributes measured IF-INP activity spectra across hydrometeor
 types to account for accumulation. In the simulations performed here, IF-INP numbers
 had less effect on precipitation and cold pool amounts relative to CCN.
 • Storm morphology matters: supercell dynamics can overwhelm microphysical (aerosol)
 controls on precipitation and cold pools — thus aerosol effects will be case-dependent
 and must be interpreted in the context of storm type and environmental dynamics.

Limitations and assumptions that affect interpretation

 • The NewIF algorithm uses simplifying assumptions (uniform cloud-drop volume, quasi-
 steady updraft assumptions in parts of the derivation) that influence the estimated INP
 sequestration; sensitivity to those assumed values was discussed and is a potential
 source of uncertainty.
 • Observational sample sizes (nine aircraft days, three deep-convection follow-ups) are
 limited; the observational evidence supports the hypothesis for ordinary storms in some
 cases but is not universal — hence the need for complementary modeling and further
 observational studies.
 • Idealized simulations, while useful for mechanistic insight, do not fully represent the full
 spectrum of environmental variability; interactions among CCN, INPs, dynamics, and
 larger-scale forcing can produce different outcomes in other regimes.
