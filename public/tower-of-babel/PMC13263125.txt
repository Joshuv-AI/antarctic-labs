Aerosol‐Generating Potential of Topical Nasal Preparation Before Nasoendoscopy: A Quantitative Study
Authors: Andy J. Chua, Yue Yu, Edward Z. Zhang, Vanessa Y. Tan, Irvan Luhung, Stephan C. Schuster, Anqi Sng, Anton V. Sadovoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe aerosol‐generating capacity of nasal sprays is an important but controversial topic, with mixed evidence in the literature. This study aims to quantitatively analyze the amount of aerosol generated by the use of topical nasal sprays prior to nasoendoscopy.MethodsA cohort study was conducted in a simulated outpatient ambulatory clinic. Healthy volunteers were recruited and underwent nasal spraying with normal saline containing 0.1% w/v fluorescein, using a commercially available spray system (Co‐Phenylcaine Forte; ENT Technologies Pty Ltd., Victoria, Australia), within an enclosed cabin. Cabin air was sampled with a filter that trapped fluorescein, which was extracted and quantified with a fluorescence spectrometer. This was compared to background noise, represented by fluorescence quantified in cabin air sampled before nasal spraying. Statistical analysis was performed with the Wilcoxon signed rank test. Multi‐variable analysis was performed with a quantile regression model.ResultsA total of 30 healthy volunteers were recruited. The median (IQR) concentration of fluorescein quantified with NSF spray using the standard nozzle was 20.9 (8.3–53.5) ng/mL, which was significantly higher than background noise [1.7 (1.0–2.2) ng/mL; median difference 18.9, 95% CI (10.8, 36.7), p < 0.001]. Multi‐variable analysis showed that age, gender, and ethnicity were not significantly associated with the amount of fluorescein aerosolization.ConclusionPreparing the nose with topical sprays could be associated with aerosol generation. Clinicians and stakeholders will need to weigh the risks and benefits of using pre‐endoscopic nasal preparation sprays and institute mitigation measures as necessary.Level of Evidence3.

Introduction

The COVID‐19 pandemic has led to increased scrutiny on the risks of infectious aerosol generation during otolaryngologic procedures, such as nasoendoscopy [1]. Topical anesthesia and/or vasoconstrictive agents are commonly used to prepare the nose pre‐procedurally, and have been shown to improve endoscopic visualization and reduce pain experienced by the patient [2]. Although there is mixed evidence for its routine use in flexible nasoendoscopy, it may have a greater role prior to rigid endoscopy [3]. From the patients' perspective, when given the option, a significant number of them do opt to have topical nasal preparation prior to nasoendoscopy [4].

However, despite these benefits, there have been concerns regarding the potential for generating aerosols when spraying the nose with topical solutions. These have led some groups of clinicians to either reduce or completely discontinue this practice [5]. Recent studies have attempted to investigate the aerosol generating potential of nasal sprays; however, these have shown contrasting results [6, 7, 8].

In this study, we aim to add to the body of knowledge concerning aerosol generation by topical nasal sprays in the ambulatory setting. To do so, we used a standard metered‐dose spray system (Co‐Phenylcaine Forte; ENT Technologies Pty Ltd., Victoria, Australia) to deliver fluorescein‐stained normal saline sprays in awake healthy volunteers, with ambient conditions as closely reflective of outpatient settings as possible and quantified the amount of fluorescein released as a surrogate for the amount of aerosol generated by the spray.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This was a prospective cohort study with healthy volunteers recruited by the research unit of a tertiary hospital in Singapore. To meet inclusion criteria, participants had to be aged 21 and above, have no history of nasal vestibulitis or furunculosis, no previous nasal surgery (open or endoscopic), and were fully vaccinated against COVID‐19 as defined by the Ministry of Health, Singapore. Those who had known allergies or previous adverse reactions to fluorescein were excluded, as were those with pulmonary or swallowing disorders.

This trial was approved by the SingHealth Centralized Institutional Review Board (Ref 2021/2783), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment.

Procedures

All participants were asked to don a surgical mask over their nose and mouth, and sat in an aerosol‐proof cabin (SG‐SAFE, The Biofactory Pte Ltd., Singapore), positioned comfortably with their nostrils 5 cm away from an air sampler (Figure 1A,B). Participants were then asked to hold their breath and lower their surgical mask to expose the nares. A board‐certified otolaryngologist would spray each nostril with one puff of normal saline containing 0.1% w/v fluorescein (NSF), using a standard short Flexi Nozzle (ENT Technologies Pty Ltd.). Normal saline was used in lieu of co‐phenylcaine in order to minimize potential adverse effects experienced by participants. Care was taken to ensure the entire tip of the nozzle was within the nostril. After administration of the spray, participants would immediately raise the surgical mask to ensure coverage of the entire nose and mouth, and leave the cabin. Aerosol sampling was performed as described below, then the cabin was washed out for 15 min between each participant.

Aerosol Sampling

We sought to quantify the amount of aerosol generated by the NSF sprays by measuring the amount of fluorescein present in the cabin. To achieve this, an air sampler (SASS 3100, Research International Inc., Monroe, WA, USA) was placed in the cabin. Air was sampled at a rate of 300 L per minute, through a single‐use, snap‐on filter which captured all suspended particles above 0.5 μm in diameter with 90% efficiency. To generate baselines for comparison, air from the empty cabin was sampled for 10 min. The filter was then removed and exchanged for a fresh one. The participant would then enter the cabin, have the spray administered, and cabin air was sampled for 5 min thereafter. Positive controls were also developed by spraying two puffs of NSF into an unoccupied cabin, with the spray bottle and nozzle similarly held 5 cm above the air sampler.

Fluorescein Quantification

To extract the fluorescein from the filter, the entire filter paper was placed in a 5 mL flat bottom tube. Two milliliter of distilled water was added, and the tube was put in a roller mixer for 24 h, followed by vortexing (Vortex‐Genie 2 mixer) at level 10 for 60 s. Aliquots were then taken to perform quantification of fluorescein using a fluorescence spectrometer (HORIBA Duetta). The following settings were used: excitation wavelength 470 nm, emission wavelength 490–750 nm, excitation and emission slit 3 μm, excitation time 1 s. The intensity signal between 507 and 517 nm was averaged over 10 technical replicates and recorded. A calibration curve was developed using serially diluted NSF stock solution and fitted with a linear function.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome measure was to determine if the amount of fluorescein that could be detected in the cabin air after spray had been administered was significantly different from background noise.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 29. The fluorescein concentrations detected with the nasal spray were compared with baseline readings using the Wilcoxon matched‐pairs signed rank test. The fluorescein concentrations detected with the positive controls were compared against those of the negative controls and nasal spray using the Mann–Whitney U test. Multi‐variable analysis was performed using a quantile regression model. All tests were performed with a significance level set at 0.05.

Results

A total of 30 healthy volunteers were recruited, with a mean age of 37.2 years old (SD = 13.5). There were seven males, with an ethnicity breakdown of 19 Chinese, 5 Malays, and 6 Indians. The median (IQR) concentration of fluorescein quantified with NSF spray using the standard nozzle was 20.9 (8.3–53.5) ng/mL (Figure 2). This was significantly higher than in the negative controls [1.7 (1.0–2.2) ng/mL; median difference 18.9, 95% CI (10.8, 36.7), p < 0.001]. The median fluorescein concentration detected with the positive controls was 3103 (911–5806) ng/mL. This was significantly greater than that of the negative control and standard nozzle (p < 0.001 for both). Multi‐variable analysis showed that age, gender, and ethnicity were not significantly associated with the amount of fluorescein aerosolization.

Discussion

The aerosol‐generating nature of some ENT procedures has come under significant scrutiny in the light of the COVID‐19 pandemic and has led to the development of masks [9] and hoods [10] in an attempt to prevent aerosol dispersal during office‐based procedures.

Several studies have investigated the aerosol generating potential of topical nasal sprays. Workman found that the use of atomizers in the clinic to deliver nasal sprays created significant aerosols, similar to that of a sneeze [6]. In the operating room, Dhillon found that the use of co‐phenylcaine sprays led to aerosol generation about 40‐fold greater than background noise when quantified with an aerodynamic particle sizer [7]. In contrast, Bastien found that topical nasal anesthetics were not associated with significant aerosol generation when sampling clinic room air using an optical particle sizer; however, the type of spray (atomizer or metered dose spray bottle) was not specified [8].

Optical particle sizers are a commonly used tool to measure and quantify aerosols; however, they can be inaccurate due to sizing errors and miscounts as they are calibrated against polystyrene latex spheres, rather than respiratory aerosols and droplets [11]. Furthermore, they generally quantify particles up to 10 μm in diameter, and larger droplet‐sized particles which can also transmit disease are not picked up.

In our study, we aimed to evaluate the risk posed by nasal sprays in a broader manner by utilizing a methodology that would capture not only aerosols but also droplets. We also chose to evaluate awake participants in an outpatient setting to maximize our findings' clinical applicability and gave step‐by‐step instructions to our participants to minimize the potential for aerosol generation by normal respiration alone. With this set up, we found that using the co‐phenylcaine spray system led to a 12‐fold increase in fluorescein levels compared to background noise, suggesting that significant amounts of aerosol and droplets are dispersed by the act of spraying. Having eliminated exhalation as a source of aerosolization, we postulate that aerosols could be ejected from the ipsilateral nostril due to turbulence caused by airflow resistance from nasal structures such as the inferior turbinate or septum. An alternative, less likely scenario could be aerosolization from the contralateral nostril after the spray jet has passed through the nasopharynx. Further studies are needed to clarify the mechanism by which aerosolization occurs.

Our study has several limitations. First, our quantification method does not allow us to analyze dispersal patterns and distances of various particles, since the entire cabin air is sampled through a single intake port position. Furthermore, particles were not sorted by size, and therefore predictions of dispersal distances also cannot be made. Also, as our participants were asked to leave the cabin in order not to exhale fluorescein‐containing nasal secretions into the sampled air, there was an inevitable escape of cabin air, which could potentially lead to an underestimation of quantified fluorescein. Finally, we are unable to ascertain if the degree of aerosolization is sufficient to cause disease transmission.

Conclusion

Aerosol quantification is a complex science, made even more challenging by the dynamics of patient respiration. In our study, we used a quantitative methodology in a setting that closely mimics that of an outpatient otolaryngology clinic, and found that the use of topical nasal sprays could lead to aerosol generation. Clinicians and other stakeholders will need to balance the risks of possible infectious disease transmission against the benefits that nasal anesthetics and vasoconstrictors offer, and also against the risks of non‐usage (e.g., inducing a sneeze during endoscopy). Risks mitigation strategies may be necessary, and further studies are needed to assist otolaryngologists in making an informed decision regarding the use of topical nasal sprays.
