Fine-grained pixel-level crack mapping in earthen heritage structures
Authors: Hafsa Matich, Jamal Attmani, Hajar Mousannif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The reliable smart tools that detect surface cracks in an efficient and accurate manner are needed for the heritage structure preservation. In this paper, an entirely automated framework is proposed in which high-resolution images have to be processed and relevant structural information and cracks are extracted in real-time. Image preprocessing, Skeleton-based analysis, and a lightweight deep learning model efficiently detect controlled and real environments in such a way that he workflow is integrated. An exclusive application was developed, through which users can also upload images, if needed, or snap shot images directly through a live camera so that the whole system can Then be applied in reality for practical site inspections. The model showed very high accuracy and very consistent performance under real-time on-field testing. Detection is not all that the system can do; scantling would also be offered on activities like crack width, average width, orientation, and severity. Thus, the proposed approach is just a proper and scalable answer to structural assessment while enabling a more rationalized action for maintenance decision-making in heritage management.

Introduction

Due to concerns over their being most prevalent and potentially therefore the most disturbing forms of phenomena in all historical and contemporary buildings, cracks receive a consensus1,2. They usually signify much deeper Freudian matters behind the wall that, if left unattended, can become dangerous to the very strength of the building and, culturally and aesthetically, bring down the considered institution from among heritage sites. Therefore, cracks observed on different members are considered singularly as signs of material deterioration or decrease in load-carrying capacities or external action, whether the action is environmental or seismic, Recent studies have also emphasized that crack patterns are not merely indicators of material degradation but can be quantitatively correlated with the structural stability and load-bearing behavior of heritage systems. For instance, relationships between crack morphology, propagation, and structural response have been explored to support more informed structural assessment and conservation strategies3–5. in assessing the health of structures6,7. In such instances, the presence of hairline cracks, whether trivial or predominant, may be highly detrimental in ancient masonry and earthen buildings. In addition to structural integrity, they carry aesthetic, symbolic, and cultural aspects which deserve urgent and accurate detection by engineers, conservators, and heritage managers8–10.

The primary mode of crack detection before now has been manual checks. Often, experts routinely studied the surface in question, determining the fragmentation via rulers, in conjunction to strain gauges or vibrating wire meters. Also, surveying instruments of the theodolite or level type will have been put to common use11,12. These methods are relatively economical and straight to the point, yet they have several disadvantages. Often, the credibility of such processes depends upon the skill of the inspector, and several external conditions can render the results very inconsistent13. While some inspections can be continuous for large areas or complex sites, the whole process can become very boring and tiring for an inspector; hence, it still remains a serious impediment towards monitoring the large heritage sites. Moreover, manual inspections are inherently subjective, difficult to reproduce, and poorly suited for long-term monitoring of extensive or inaccessible heritage structures14. In response to these limitations, recent research has explored the integration of smart monitoring systems, combining sensor networks and data-driven analysis to enable continuous structural assessment. Such systems, often validated on scaled structural models, provide valuable insights into damage evolution and structural behavior, thereby bridging the gap between crack detection and structural interpretation15.

The world of advanced technology has recently focused on research in different sorts of activities to probe the limits available. Both Terrestrial Laser Scanning (TLS) and LiDAR and close-range photogrammetry swiftly yield detailed models of the surface captured. TLS indeed produces a dense point field recording fine details of the surface and high-resolution 2D and 3D images from overlapping images. The outcome has been reasonable in all sorts of contexts from historic bridges to castles to earth monuments. However, these techniques often require expensive equipment, complex post-processing workflows, and highly trained operators, which limit their large-scale or routine deployment in heritage conservation projects.

Some challenges still arise. Actually, some point-based methods that are more sophisticated for this application analyze geometric properties-curvature, local gradients, or surface roughness-in crack detection. All other confusions, such as noise or irregular distribution of points and loss of internal defect detection, may hinder efficiency to some extent. It works almost for all image-based detection systems, but here too, though systems are getting hampered by illumination, occlusions, and surface patterns mimicking cracks, resulting in false positives. These limitations highlight the need for automated methods that are robust to illumination variations, complex surface textures, and environmental noise, particularly in the context of heterogeneous heritage materials such as earthen and masonry structures. Moreover, many existing datasets used to train deep learning models are primarily derived from concrete structures, which differ significantly in texture, composition, and degradation patterns from earthen and masonry heritage materials. This domain discrepancy can affect model generalization and reduce detection reliability when applied to historical structures.

In the last decade, the field of artificial intelligence has created ripples in the approach of crack detection. Crack detection has found application in deep learning, especially convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which are able to automatically learn to identify cracks from raw data16. Initial attempts aimed at classifying pictures into cracked and intact categories using classical networks, such as VGGNet or AlexNet17,18; however, the more recent methods apply segmentation algorithms that aim to localize cracks at the pixel level19. U-Net is certainly the most popular architecture for this kind of task, as it benefits from the design of an encoder-decoder type of structure with skip connections for accurate segmentation20. Nevertheless, many existing deep learning models rely on heavy backbone networks, resulting in high computational cost and limited real-time performance, which restricts their deployment on portable or on-site inspection devices commonly used in heritage conservation21.

Different U-Net versions are created depending on different needs. For example, U-Net-ResNet introduces residual connections to capture complicated crack patterns more effectively22,23. U-Net-MobileNetV2 is lightweight and thus suitable for implementation on mobile or field devices24. Since U-Net-InceptionV3 employs multi-scale feature extraction for crack detection at various widths and orientations, it turns out to be handy25. In addition to U-Net models, Faster R-CNN, YOLOv5, and Vision Transformers outperformed classical image processing techniques. The performance comparisons focused on speed, accuracy, and robustness, forming a balance between the various criteria26.

More recently, transformer-based semantic segmentation models have been introduced for structural condition assessment, offering improved global contextual modeling through self-attention mechanisms. Studies such as27 report enhanced crack localization performance in complex structural imagery by capturing long-range spatial dependencies. Despite their effectiveness, these models typically require large, annotated datasets and substantial computational resources, which can hinder real-time deployment in heritage conservation contexts.

In parallel, generative AI–assisted damage detection approaches have been proposed to mitigate data scarcity by synthesizing realistic damage patterns or augmenting training datasets. The framework presented in28 demonstrates the potential of generative models to improve detection robustness under limited data conditions. Nevertheless, the transferability of such generative approaches to heterogeneous heritage materials remains challenging due to domain shift and material-specific visual characteristics.

Considering the practical constraints of heritage conservation such as limited computational resources, the need for real-time analysis, and the complexity of surface textures, this study adopts U-Net-MobileNetV2 as the core segmentation model. This architecture achieves a favorable trade-off between segmentation accuracy, computational efficiency, and robustness, making it particularly suitable for field deployment and large-scale monitoring of historical structures.

In this context, it is essential not only to detect cracks accurately but also to ensure that the detected patterns can support meaningful structural interpretation, particularly in heritage conservation where material behavior differs significantly from modern construction systems.

This research aims to develop a fully automated, reliable, and deployable pipeline for surface crack detection on historical Tabiya water basins. The motivation stems from the need to overcome the limitations of manual inspection and computationally heavy deep learning models, providing heritage managers and conservators with tools for reproducible, efficient, and timely monitoring. By leveraging U-Net-MobileNetV2, the proposed framework seeks to combine accuracy, robustness, and practical deployability, advancing automated assessment processes for heritage conservation decision-making.

Materials and methods

While effective planning is critical for the successful execution of reliable and accurate image-based analyses, in this investigation, the methodological framework will revolve around site preparation, data acquisition strategy, and preprocessing methods for the optimization of the image. The proposed framework is designed for pixel-wise crack segmentation and subsequent geometric analysis, rather than image-level crack classification.

Field inspections were completed to evaluate environmental conditions at the locations and identify the best places to capture images. These visits enabled the identification of optimal viewpoints, appropriate lighting windows, and positioning of control points. Environmental factors-rainfall variability, amplitude of illumination changes, shadow maps in the view seen above, as well as surface reflectance-were considered as they directly affect the clarity of an image and the correctness of all subsequent crack detection and structural interpretation.

Such considerations are particularly critical for heritage earthen structures, whose heterogeneous textures and weathered surfaces may visually resemble crack patterns.

In order to obtain consistently high quality images, the data collection was carried out during periods of stable and diffuse natural light, avoiding direct sunlight because of the likelihood of creating glare, color distortion, or variable illumination at a particular location. A parallel-movement methodology was adopted for the purpose of uniformly framing the different surfaces of the Tabiya structures thereby ensuring consistent coverage with around 50–60 overlapping images per wall. This acquisition strategy supports reliable pixel-level annotation and segmentation by reducing perspective distortion and illumination artifacts.

Previous studies have shown that image quality, consistency, and overlap most often exert a more significant influence on the reconstruction accuracy than the number of images captured. As such careful attention was paid to ensuring optimal conditions for the environment and acquisition rather than necessarily increasing the size of the dataset29. This approach ensures that the resulting images are suitable for deep learning–based semantic segmentation and subsequent crack width estimation. “Fig. 2” illustrates this point.

The automatic and mechanical detection of surface cracks in ancient earthen structures is demonstrated through application on Sahrij Labgar, located in the Kasbah district of Marrakech. The reservoir was constructed between the 11th and 12th centuries and formed one of the most important components of the hydraulic infrastructure of the city during Almoravid times30. Sahrij Labgar contributed to this supply system by functioning with the Khattaras1, a network of subterranean galleries and storage basins designed for large-scale irrigation. Together, these systems delivered water to royal gardens and agricultural areas. Today, this structure stands as a monument to the highly advanced environmental engineering and hydrological proficiency which were attained by Almoravid builders.

Architecturally, Sahrij Labgar, a large quadrangular basin of about 3.8 m deep and with a storage capacity exceeding 40,000 m³, would be said to contain perimeter walls, about 2.3 m thick, built in the traditional tâbiya (rammed-earth) technique, where successive layers of an earth-lime-straw-gravel mix are compacted to become highly durable monolithic walls. The interior surfaces of the basin were finished with a lime-based plaster and tadelakt, a polished waterproof coating well known for its longevity and resistance to water penetration shown in “Fig. 3”.

Sahrij Labgar, despite the strength of its traditional materials, has suffered deterioration from aging, environmental exposure, and moisture fluctuations, resulting in surface cracks and material loss. Its complex and vulnerable surfaces make it an ideal case for AI-based crack detection and automated defect mapping to support non-invasive monitoring and preventive conservation of historic earthen structures.

Image dataset and preprocessing methods.

The set of data considered in this research represents an amalgamation of real-time image acquisition and public crack-image datasets to maintain variability and a visualization close to reality with respect to the structural conditions. A total of 2,500 images were collected, composed of both concrete surface images and Tabiya (earthen heritage) surface images, including 1,625 images showing visible cracks and 875 images of uncracked surfaces. These public datasets, largely derived from concrete structures, were primarily used to ensure sufficient data volume and diversity for robust deep learning model training. The dataset thus generated is robust enough to be used for training and testing deep learning models for automatic crack detection31.

To address the specific context of heritage conservation, a dedicated subset of images acquired from the Sahrij Labgar earthen heritage structure was incorporated into the dataset. These images are used primarily for testing and cross-domain evaluation, as they differ significantly from concrete surfaces in terms of texture, color distribution, surface irregularities, and crack morphology.

For segmentation purposes, ground-truth crack masks were manually annotated at the pixel level using the LabelMe tool. The annotation process was conducted by trained researchers with experience in structural inspection32. Ambiguous regions were reviewed jointly to ensure consistency and labeling accuracy. This manual annotation procedure ensures reliable supervision for pixel-wise crack segmentation33,34.

The integration of images from concrete structures and earthen heritage surfaces introduces a potential domain shift, stemming from inherent differences in material properties, visual appearance, and crack patterns. Rather than avoiding this heterogeneity, the proposed framework explicitly accounts for it by assessing model robustness across heterogeneous domains. This cross-domain evaluation is particularly relevant to heritage conservation scenarios, where annotated data are scarce and models must generalize beyond their original training domain.

Two experimental scenarios were designed to evaluate the impact of training data availability on model performance under a cross-domain setting. In Scenario 1, the dataset was split into 65% training and 35% testing, while in Scenario 2, it was split into 75% training and 25% testing. In both scenarios, the training data consists of a mixture of concrete and Tabiya images, while the testing data consists exclusively of Tabiya images from the Sahrij Labgar site. These configurations allow evaluation of how training data proportion influences generalization performance on the target heritage domain35.

For preprocessing prior to model training, all images were standardized to help with feature extraction. The color images were initially converted into grayscale to reduce inter-channel covariance and enhance the emphasis on patterns of structural crack. Then thresholding and binarization methods were used to separate crack pixels from the background in terms of brightness through established image processing workflows. The thresholding approaches included global, Otsu, Triangle, and Isodata. Among these, GMM thresholding was regarded as being very strong against illumination and shadow effects, making it the most suitable method for this task. However, the GMM calculates local thresholds for each pixel, boosting the clarity of minute cracks through noise suppression, rendering inappropriate textures, and yielding input data that is pure and meaningful for deep learning models36,37. For it performs local thresholds to each and every pixel into two distinct categories: reducing the perceivability of fine cracks through noise removal and irrelevant texture elimination. Here, the GMM-endowed input data are pristine but extremely useful for deep learning models.

C.Architecture of MobileNetV2.

C.Architecture of MobileNetV2.

Architecture of MobileNetV2.

MobileNetV2, the backbone of this entire study, was chosen because it can balance between computational efficiency and effective feature extraction for automated surface crack detection most especially for such resource-limited environments like mobile or field inspection systems. Starting with a 3 × 3 convolutional layer, this network has 32 filters to extract very basic low-level features such as edges and textures-important for the detection of fine crack patterns38. Bottleneck residual blocks come on top of this with their inverted residual structure: first, an expansion of channels by 1 × 1 convolutions, then realized using depthwise separable convolutions, and finally compressed back to their original dimensions. This architecture is said to greatly reduce the computational demands of a system while keeping all those necessary features intact for cracks of all widths and orientations39.

All bottleneck blocks are with linear activation in order to keep details structural, while there are other blocks where ReLU activations are used, allowing inserting non-linearity and improving feature representation. On top of the net is the global average pooling layer that pools spatial information and helps prevent overfitting, along with preserving spatial invariance. Then, the pooled output is used as feature maps within a U-Net encoder–decoder segmentation architecture rather than being passed to a classification head, ensuring pixel-wise prediction capability for crack localization40.

The compact architecture of MobileNetV2, yielding a small-sized model with few parameters compared to traditional CNNs, also ensures speedy training and inference, allowing deployment on mobile platforms for real-time inspections. In the proposed framework, MobileNetV2 is integrated as the encoder backbone of a U-Net segmentation model, rather than being used as an independent classifier. The segmentation output is produced through a decoder network that reconstructs spatial resolution to generate pixel-wise crack masks. MobileNetV2 was evaluated for its accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, and the results were compared with those of a conventional CNN to demonstrate its efficiency and robustness in detecting fine and complex crack patterns. The overall layout of the network architecture and the comparative CNN model is shown in “Fig. 4”.

D.Crack Skeleton Extraction and Localization.

D.Crack Skeleton Extraction and Localization.

Crack Skeleton Extraction and Localization.

Skeleton extraction has been based on the Medial Axis Transform (MAT). MAT is strong in theoretical foundation and is able to maintain the geometric fidelity of long structures, such as cracks. In our workflow, MobileNetV2 represents the primary deep-learning model for crack detection and segmentation purposes. Next, MAT is applied to this segment output to derive a real one-pixel-width skeleton. This integraion ensures that the geometrical analysis is based on accurate and well-localised crack masks from MobileNetV2.

Originally introduced by Blum41, MAT defines the skeleton as the locus of points that have more than one closest point on the object boundary. These points correspond to the centers of maximally inscribed circles within the shape. In operation, MAT computes a distance map over the crack pixels and employs a ridge-point-detection criterion, where the distance gradient reaches local maxima42,43. The identified points form the medial centerline capturing the topology, curvature, and overall structure of the crack.

The configuration of MAT and MobileNetV2 provides a steady and robust skeleton representation for downstream crack-parameter estimation. Once extracted, as shown in “Fig. 5”, this skeleton provides a precise structural representation for accurate crack analysis.

The experimental setting comprises a high-resolution image acquisition system for the purpose of on-site crack detection on heritage structures. A Canon EOS 1100D DSLR camera described in “Table 3”, equipped with a 30 mm lens and a 12.2 MP CMOS sensor, was mounted on a rigid tripod for consistent object framing and parallel movement on the wall. The images were taken in controlled lighting conditions early in the morning, avoiding direct sunlight and minimizing shadows to ensure good quality for the later processing. The camera settings were thus locked at f/8 aperture, 1/125 s shutter speed, and ISO 100 to give uniform exposure to the whole dataset. The setup provided enough overlap between the adjacently captured frames so as to add redundancy and allow correct 3D reconstruction where needed.

All procedures revolving model development and training were performed in a workstation having an AMD Ryzen 9 with 7950 × 16-core CPU, 128 GB DDR5 system memory, and four NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs, giving a total of 96 GB of dedicated GPU memory for deep learning of large image datasets with efficient parallel processing. Operating system was Ubuntu 22.04 LTS along with CUDA 12 and cuDNN 8.6.

The entire image processing and deep learning workflow was made with Python, version 3.10, as it holds the advantage of many AI libraries and much more scientific ones adapted for it. For example, OpenCV 4.8 was used for image preprocessing and feature extraction such as grayscale conversion, which helps reduce channel-wise covariance while enhancing structural patterns of cracks. Brightness-based isolation of the crack pixels from the background was achieved using thresholding and binarization techniques. In addition, NumPy 1.25 was used for basic array and pixel-level manipulation, while Matplotlib 3.7 was employed for visualization of intermediate processing results and output from the applied model. Principal Component Analysis was undertaken to consider the main orientation in which each skeleton point was oriented.

Deep-learning model creation and training was via Tensorflow version number 2.12, where architecture of MobileNetV2 was specified for detection of cracks. Rotating, flipping, scaling, and brightness-adding augmentation were some of the data augmentation routines expected to enable the models generalization.

This included a deployment of a web application in Python Flask 2.3, developed on VPS Ubuntu 22.04, using Gunicorn 20.1 as WSGI server and Nginx 1.24 as reverse proxy and request handler. Other third-party libraries have been added; such ones include pandas 2.1 for data handling, scikit-image 0.21 for advanced image processes, and SciPy 1.11 for numerical operations. For high-demand GPU processing capacity, CUDA 12.1 and cuDNN 8.6 were used to run the developed applications, allowing model training and inference to be achieved with high efficiencies in multi-GPU systems.

Results and discussion

This study aims at enhancing the detection along with severity assessment of structural cracks in historical Tabiya basin structures, for which detection models need to be highly robust and sensitive in order to preserve earthen heritage materials. The proposed system was trained and validated24 using the custom dataset composed of 2500 color images:1625 images of cracked Tabiya surfaces (ranging from micro-fissures to severe structural cracks) and 875 un-cracked reference images. To enhance dataset variability and mitigate overfitting, heavy data augmentation was done before feature extraction using our optimized MobileNetV2 architecture.

As shown in “Table 5”, with a split of 65/35 split the dataset, the experimental finding demonstrated a good performance overall-wise, with all classifiers yielding accuracies above 90%. It was then determined that MobileNetV2 had the highest accuracy (98.45%) and sensitivity (98.90%), showing that it is able to detect very fine and low-contrast cracks present in Tabiya surfaces with a good degree of reliability. The SVM classifier gave the highest specificity (98.91%), which implies that it can correctly identify intact areas and reduce the probability of false detections.

“Table 6” summarizes model performance based on a 75/25 dataset split. As compared to a 65|35 split, the 75|25 split was favorable for all classifiers. In particular, the XGBoost classifier showed the best results, scoring 99% on accuracy as well as sensitivity and specificity. In contrast, in the case of the 65|35split “Table 5”, MobileNetV2 scored 98.45% accuracy, with sensitivity being 98.90%, while SVM had the highest specificity of 98.91%. The performance values here for the 75|25 split were derived from the confusion matrix illustrated in “Fig. 8” where increasing the training data proportion led to better model performance and generalization.

Although only two train/test split configurations (65/35 and 75/25) were evaluated, these settings were selected to analyze model sensitivity to training data availability under controlled conditions. Both configurations are widely used in deep learning evaluation to assess generalization behavior. Moreover, the study focuses on cross-domain evaluation (concrete-to-Tabiya), which introduces higher variability than standard random splitting. Future work will extend this evaluation using k-fold cross-validation and additional dataset partitions to further strengthen statistical robustness.

The crack width estimation and severity grading were validated using manually annotated ground-truth measurements. Crack widths were computed from binary segmentation masks by converting pixel distances into real-world units using a calibrated scale factor. Severity classes (micro, moderate, severe) were defined based on threshold ranges validated against expert-labeled annotations. The system outputs were compared with manual measurements performed by structural inspection experts to ensure reliability of severity classification.

However, according to the results of the confusion matrix, MobileNetV2 still does not surpass XGBoost in terms of prediction accuracy. While MobileNetV2 generates seven misclassified instances, XGBoost only generates three mistakes. Its increased performance is mainly due to the gradient boosting process that XGBoost follows, where the residual errors are corrected by each model in the sequence left by the previous models, ensuring an improvement and reduction of mistakes along the way. Also, the 75|25 split used while dividing the dataset into different training and testing data contributes a lot to the model’s stability and accurate predictions. These factors hence make the XGBoost model, which has been trained utilizing the dataset, perfectly implemented onto resource-constrained platforms such as single-board computers.

MobileNetV2 is not able to outperform XGBoost in prediction accuracy according to the results gleaned through confusion matrix evaluation. While MobileNetV2 produces seven instances of error, XGBoost records only three mistakes. The improved performance is attributable mainly to the gradient boosting strategy employed by XGBoost in which each model in the series is trained to correct residual errors of previous models ensuring display of progressive refinement and lessening of overall missteps. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that portable and lightweight deep learning architectures, specifically a MobileNetV2-based framework integrated with an efficient image processing pipeline, can significantly enhance the monitoring and preservation of heritage structures. The proposed approach was evaluated on a mixed dataset of 2,500 images composed of concrete and Tabiya (earthen) surfaces, including 1,625 cracked and 875 non-cracked samples.

Experimental results show that, under a 65/35 training–testing split, the MobileNetV2 model achieved 98.45% accuracy, 98.90% sensitivity, and 97.93% specificity, demonstrating its ability to detect very fine and low-contrast cracks. When increasing the training proportion to a 75/25 split, overall performance improved, with the XGBoost classifier achieving up to 99.12% accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, indicating strong generalization capability under increased training data.

The models were validated in both controlled and real-world environments. The MobileNetV2-based system achieved real-time performance at 48 FPS, generating accurate crack segmentation masks and extracting key structural features such as crack width, average width, orientation, and severity. These results confirm that the proposed framework is not only highly accurate but also suitable for deployment on resource-constrained platforms, such as single-board computers.

Beyond detection, the system provides quantitative characterization of crack geometry and severity, supporting informed decision-making for conservation and maintenance. The developed Flask-based interface enables non-expert users to capture images, process them automatically, and obtain immediate results, demonstrating strong usability in field conditions.

Overall, this work highlights the potential of combining lightweight deep learning models with geometric analysis to deliver an interpretable, efficient, and scalable solution for heritage conservation. Future work will focus on extending the framework to multi-temporal monitoring, GIS integration, and hybrid models combining visual and structural data, with the goal of improving predictive maintenance and broadening applicability to other heritage materials.
