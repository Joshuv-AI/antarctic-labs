The O-GlcNAc modification of PRRC2C at S2238 promotes SG formation and nasopharyngeal carcinoma metastasis
Authors: Haimeng Yin, Shumei Ding, Han Deng, Ying Shan, Yumo Han, Yuntong Liu, Juping Sheng, Bo You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMetastasis remains the leading cause of mortality in patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), yet its precise mechanism has not been fully elucidated.MethodsIn this study, we established high metastatic (HM) and low metastatic (LM) sublines of NPC cells using the Transwell system, aiming to systematically investigate the metabolic reprogramming events that occur during NPC metastasis.ResultsMetabolomics sequencing results revealed that HM NPC cells have undergone metabolic profile remodeling, leading to increased levels of O-linked N-acetylglucosamine (O-GlcNAc) modification substrates UDP-GlcNAc and UDP-GalNAc, consequently, HM cells exhibited a significantly higher global O-GlcNAc modification level than LM cells. Through the construction of OGT-overexpressing cells and O-GlcNAc modification sequencing, we identified a significant elevation in the O-GlcNAcylation level of Proline-Rich Coiled-Coil 2 C (PRRC2C), a protein associated with stress granule (SG) formation. By transfecting PRRC2C WT and PRRC2C S2238A (serine 2238-to-alanine substitution) plasmids, we mimicked the characteristics of HM and LM cells and found that the O-GlcNAc modification of PRRC2C at S2238 site could promote the formation of SG at mitochondrial platform. Mechanistically, NPC cells transfected with the PRRC2C S2238A plasmids maintained mitochondrial functional homeostasis, evidenced by intact mitochondrial membrane potential and balanced mitochondrial dynamics compared to PRRC2C WT cells. In the nude mice orthotopic transplantation model, the use of epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) could modulate the metastatic potential of HM cells via the inhibition of SGs.ConclusionCollectively, this study identifies targeting O-GlcNAcylation of PRRC2C at S2238 and SG formation as a promising therapeutic strategy for patients with metastatic NPC.Registry and the registration no. of the study/trialN/A.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13402-026-01242-1.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a significant regional subtype of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma that originates from the nasopharyngeal epithelial mucosa, with distinct geographic distribution patterns worldwide [1]. It is endemic in southern China, Southeast Asia, and North Africa [2]. The primary characteristics of NPC include Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection and extensive lymphocytic infiltration. Although radiotherapy is the standard treatment for non-metastatic NPC and achieves satisfactory therapeutic effects, over 50% of patients are diagnosed with advanced-stage disease, posing a major clinical challenge [3]. While numerous metabolic alterations, such as the Warburg effect, have been observed in other tumor types, NPC displays distinct metabolic features in certain aspects [4]. Given the unique pathological characteristics of NPC and the shared metabolic traits with other tumors, exploring metabolic abnormalities in NPC could facilitate the development of more effective personalized therapeutic strategies.

In the nutrient-deprived tumor microenvironment, cancer cells rely on metabolic reprogramming to sustain uncontrolled growth and proliferation — an adaptive process tightly regulated by nutrient-sensing mechanisms that enable cells to respond to environmental fluctuations [5]. O-GlcNAc post-translational modification emerges as a key mediator of this nutrient-sensing axis: catalyzed by O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT), a single O-GlcNAc moiety is covalently attached to the hydroxyl groups of serine and/or threonine residues on target proteins [6]. This modification regulates critical biological processes, including cell cycle progression [7], translation, transcription [8], nutrient sensing [9], stress response [10] and cell differentiation [11]. While elevated O-GlcNAc levels have been linked to increased aggressiveness in multiple cancer types and implicated in metabolic syndrome pathogenesis, its functional role and underlying mechanisms in NPC metastasis remain poorly characterized, representing a critical knowledge gap.

Since O-GlcNAc modification is pivotal for cellular stress responses, its interplay with stress-induced cellular structures, especially SGs, merits in-depth exploration. SGs are membraneless RNA aggregates formed in eukaryotic cells in response to various stressors, such as hypoxia, heat shock, viral infection, and oxidative stress [12]. These granules consist of ribonucleoproteins, translation factors, 40 S ribosomal subunits [13], and several other proteins, including G3BP1, TIA1, and NUFIP2 [14, 15]. The formation of SGs results in metabolic reprogramming, translation inhibition, and alterations in normal cellular signaling to promote cell survival. Although the biology of SGs remains an emerging field, cumulative evidence shows that cancer cells exhibit enhanced SG formation capacity compared to normal cells, a heightened dependency on SGs for survival under adverse conditions, and tumor reliance on SGs for growth and multidrug resistance [16, 17].

In this study, we employed an integrated approach combining metabolomics and O-GlcNAc modification sequencing to systematically elucidate the role of PRRC2C in NPC metastasis. Mechanistically, NPC cells with enhanced O-GlcNAc modification of PRRC2C at S2238 exhibit a predisposition to promote mitochondrial SG formation. This process is supported by enhanced mitochondrial dynamics, which sustain the energy supply required for HM NPC cell functions. Notably, in vivo experiments demonstrated that EGCG inhibits tumor metastasis by suppressing SG formation within the tumor stress microenvironment. Collectively, our findings identify O-GlcNAc modification of PRRC2C at S2238 as a novel metastatic driver in NPC and establish a mechanistic link between metabolic reprogramming, O-GlcNAc signaling, and SG-mediated stress adaptation — providing a theoretical framework for developing SG-targeted therapeutic strategies for metastatic NPC.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture

The human NPC cell lines CNE2 and C666-1 were purchased from Shanghai Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and TW03 cells were kindly provided by Sun Yat-sen University. All cell lines were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco: 10099141) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco: C118775500BT) under the culture conditions of 37 °C with 5% CO2. The STR profile of the CNE2 cell line was verified prior to use, and all cells were routinely confirmed to be free from mycoplasma contamination before conducting the experiments.

Establishment of high and low metastatic sublines of NPC cells

Based on previous studies, two subpopulations of CNE2/C666-1/TW03 cells with different metastatic potentials were developed. An in vitro invasion model was constructed using Transwell (Corning, CLS3422) with 8 μm pore size filters and inserted into 24 well plates. Briefly, CNE2/C666-1/TW03 cells, which were 60–70% confluent, were serum-starved for 24 h before being digested with trypsin and resuspended in serum-free medium. The cell density was set at 5 × 105 cells/mL, and 0.2 mL of the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with DMEM containing 20% FBS. After incubating for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2, cells from the upper (U) and lower sides (L) of the chamber membrane were collected and cultured separately. After four rounds of continuous separation, only the upper cells of the fourth-generation U subpopulation and the lower cells of the L subline were retained. A set of sublines derived from CNE2, C666-1 and TW03 cells were thus generated and defined as low metastatic (LM) and high metastatic (HM) sublines, respectively. The migration ability of the cell subpopulation was verified through adhesion, Transwell and scratch assays.

Cell adhesion experiment

A sterile 6-well plate was used, and the wells were pre-coated with Matrigel (Corning, CLS354234) before cell seeding. A total of 4.5 × 105 cells were subcultured, with approximately 104 cells seeded into each well. Plates were incubated for 30 min, followed by removal of the culture medium. Loosely attached cells were gently rinsed off with PBS. Each well was then fixed with 1 mL of methanol for 30 min. After fixation, the wells were washed three times with PBS, and the number of adherent cells was counted under a microscope.

Transwell migration assay

Transwell assays were conducted using Corning Transwell plates (24 well; 8 μm pores). NPC cells (5 × 104) in 200 µL of serum-free medium were seeded in the upper chambers, with 500 µL of medium containing 10% FBS added to the lower chambers. After 12–16 h of incubation, non-migrating cells on the upper chamber surface were removed, while the remaining cells were fixed, stained, and imaged. Digital images were captured from the membranes, and three random fields per chamber were counted.

Scratch assay

Confluent monolayers of NPC cells were established in 6-well plates. Scratch wounds were introduced, and the extent of wound closure was determined by measuring the cell-free regions at 0 h and 24 h using captured images.

Western blot analysis

Cells were harvested and lysed on ice, and their protein concentrations were measured using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo, 23227). An aliquot of 10 µg of lysate was then denatured by boiling and separated by SDS-PAGE. Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were subsequently blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the appropriate primary antibodies. The membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Protein signals were visualized using the enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system (Thermo, 32106). The specific antibodies used are listed in Table S1.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

According to the protocol, cell lysates were prepared using a Co-IP assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 26146) and incubated with the corresponding antibody at 4 °C for 12 h. Protein A/G magnetic beads were then added to the antibody-bound lysates, and the mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C. Protein bands were detected using an ECL chemiluminescent substrate and imaged accordingly.

BALB/c nude mice animal models

In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, orthotopic NPC xenografts were established using four-week-old BALB/c mice. The mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and placed in a supine position in a stereotactic frame. The tongue was gently retracted and the mouth was held open, and a 50 µL PBS suspension containing 2 × 106 CNE-2/C666-1 cells was injected distal and lateral to the hard palate using a sterile 1 mL syringe. Drug treatment was initiated seven days after injection. Tumor growth at the orthotopic site and NPC metastases were monitored using an in vivo BLI system (PerkinElmer, IVIS Lumina Series III). After euthanasia by CO2 asphyxiation, tumors were harvested, fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for subsequent analysis.

In addition, to assess lung metastasis, 1 × 106 luciferase-labeled TW03 and C666-1 cells suspended in 200 µL RPMI 1640 medium were injected into the tail veins of four-week-old nude mice. Bioluminescence imaging (BLI) was performed over a one-month period to evaluate lung metastasis in each mouse.

UDP-GlcNAc assay

The concentration of UDP-GlcNAc in HM and LM cells was quantified following the standard protocol provided with the ELISA kit (JINGMEI BIOTECHNOLOGY, JM-5449H1). The required flat-bottomed microplate was removed from the aluminum foil packaging and equilibrated to room temperature. Standard and sample wells were prepared by adding 50 µL of diluent to each well. Then, 100 µL of HRP-conjugated antigen was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 60 min. After the washing procedure, 50 µL of substrates A and B was added to each well, and the plate was incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 15 min. Finally, stop solution was added, and the absorbance at 450 nm (OD450 nm) was measured for each well.

Immunofluorescence (IF) assay

Cells were seeded onto slides and cultured overnight before being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. After fixation, cells were permeabilized using 0.1%–0.2% Triton X-100 buffer. Following permeabilization, cells were blocked for one hour and then incubated with the specified primary antibodies. The next day, the cells were incubated for one hour with a fluorescently labeled secondary antibody. After thorough washing, an anti-fading mounting medium containing Hoechst was applied. Stained cells were then imaged using a Zeiss confocal microscope (LSM900, USA). The specific antibodies used are listed in Table S1.

Lentiviral transfection

Lentiviruses carrying OGT-targeting CRISPR-Cas9 and negative control lentiviruses were purchased from Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Logarithmically growing cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 20%. The CRISPR-Cas9 system was used to knock out OGT in cancer cells respectively. After the cells attached to the plate, the required amounts of culture medium and virus for each well were calculated according to the lentivirus transfection instructions, and infection-enhancing reagent was added and mixed thoroughly. Fresh complete medium was replaced 16–24 h later based on the cell infection status, followed by continuous culture. Three independent sgRNA targets were designed for OGT knockout. Following verification of knockout efficiency at the mRNA and protein levels, the cell line with the highest and most stable knockout efficacy was chosen for subsequent functional assays.

Fixation and embedding for electron microscopy

For Epon embedding, cells were fixed at 4 °C for 1 h in 1.6% glutaraldehyde diluted with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.3). After fixation, cells were scraped from the culture dish and pelleted by centrifugation. The cell pellets were dehydrated using a graded series of ethanol and subsequently embedded in Epon. Polymerization occurred at 64 °C for 48 h. Ultrathin sections were mounted on 200-mesh Formvar carbon-coated copper grids, double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined with a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope. Digital images were acquired using a CCD camera.

JC-1 assay

For the JC-1 assay (Beyotime, Cat. No. C2003S), cells were seeded onto confocal dishes and incubated until fully adherent. Subsequently, 1 mL of JC-1 working staining solution was added and mixed well, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min in a humidified cell incubator. After incubation, the supernatant was aspirated, and cells were rinsed twice with JC-1 staining buffer. Finally, 2 mL of complete culture medium was added, and cells were observed and imaged using a laser scanning confocal microscope.

Metabolomics analysis

We isolated HM and LM subpopulations of CNE2 cells for subsequent experiments. After sample collection, 200 µL of prechilled ultrapure water and 800 µL of prechilled methanol were added, followed by vortexing. The mixtures were sonicated in an ice bath for 20 min and incubated at − 20 °C for 1 h to precipitate proteins. The samples were then centrifuged at 16,000 × g and 4 °C for 20 min. The upper supernatants containing metabolites were collected for further processing. The remaining protein pellets were dried and resuspended in 100 µL of SDT lysis buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM DTT, 150 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0). Protein quantification was performed using the BCA assay. The volume of each metabolite supernatant was normalized according to the total protein content to ensure equal cell loading across all samples. The normalized supernatants were concentrated to dryness in a vacuum concentrator at 4 °C. The dried residues were redissolved in 30 µL of methanol/water (1:1, v/v) and centrifuged at 20,000 × g and 4 °C for 15 min. The final supernatants were subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis.

O-GlcNAc modification sequencing

For glycosylation modification profiling, we conducted Co-IP experiments using anti-OGT antibody in both experimental groups (OGT overexpression) and negative control groups (NC, transfected with empty vector). The Co-IP eluates containing OGT-associated protein complexes were collected. These samples were subsequently submitted for O-GlcNAc modification sequencing.

All sequencing services including quantitative proteomics, O-glycosylation site analysis using LC-MS/MS, and bioinformatics analysis (including WiKiPathway enrichment and protein interaction network construction) were performed by Shanghai Bioprofile Technology Co., Ltd.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times, and typical results are displayed in the figures. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical evaluations were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.0, with specific descriptions provided in the corresponding figure legends. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Construction of cell models with high/low metastatic potential

To elucidate the carcinogenic mechanisms underlying metastatic NPC, we developed cellular models with high and low metastatic potentials. HM and LM sublines of CNE2/C666-1/TW03 cells were established with the transwell model (Fig. 1A). The established LM cells exhibit a typical epithelial morphology characterized by polygonal shapes, whereas the HM cells facilitate the formation of vasculogenic mimicry (Fig. 1B), a phenomenon closely associated with cancer cell growth, invasion, and metastasis. Subsequently, we evaluated the adhesion and migration capabilities of the two cell subtypes. The HM cells demonstrated reduced adhesion and enhanced migration abilities (Fig. 1C-E). Furthermore, in vivo tail vein metastasis experiments revealed that HM cells possess an increased propensity for lung metastasis (Fig. 1F-G). These findings confirmed the successful establishment of HM and LM NPC cell lines.

HM cells exhibit enhanced O-GlcNAc modification levels

To gain deeper insights into the relationship between metabolic reprogramming and tumor metastasis, a comprehensive metabolomics analysis was performed on the two CNE2 subpopulations. This analysis revealed distinct metabolic profiles between HM and LM cells (Fig. S1A; Table S2). Pathway enrichment analysis further identified several differentially regulated metabolic pathways (Fig. S1B). Compared with the LM group, the HM group exhibited elevated levels of pyrimidine nucleotides (Fig. S1C), purine metabolites (Fig. S1D) and glycolytic precursors (Fig. S1E). In contrast, the levels of lipid metabolits and TCA cycle intermediates were decreased in HM cells (Fig. S1F-G). In a series of metabolic changes, we found that Uridine diphosphate N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc) and Uridine diphosphate N-acetylgalactosamine (UDP-GalNAc) were significantly enhanced in HM cells (Fig. 2A-B), which are important precursors for the synthesis of glycosaminoglycans and glycoproteins. Similarly, kit-based detection revealed a marked increase in UDP-GlcNAc levels in the HM cells (Fig. 2C). Considering these findings, HM cells exhibited enhanced glycolysis and nucleotide metabolism, accompanied by reduced TCA cycle activity (Fig. 2D). Compared with LM cells, we hypothesized that the UDP-GlcNAc synthesis pathway acted as a central hub connecting glycolipid and nucleotide metabolism. It functioned as a “nutrient sensor” by supplying donor substrates for protein O-GlcNAc modification. Western blot analysis confirmed the expression of OGT, the key enzyme responsible for O-GlcNAc modification. Higher OGT protein levels and stronger O-GlcNAc signals (detected using the RL2 antibody) were observed in HM cells relative to LM cells (Fig. 2E).

O-GlcNAc modification of PRRC2C increases SG formation

We further performed O-GlcNAc modification sequencing on OGT-overexpressing and control groups to identify key molecules underlying cell migration. Wikipathway analysis showed the differential proteins involved in several oncogenic and metabolic pathways, such as pathways affected in adenoid cystic carcinoma, apoptosis-related network, purine metabolism, and mitochondrial gene expression, etc. (Fig. 3A). Combined with the upregulation and elevated O-GlcNAc sites, a total of 13 molecules were identified (Fig. 3B; Table S3). PRRC2C, a critical protein in stress granule (SG), caught our attention [18]. We sought to determine whether PRRC2C promotes SG formation via O-GlcNAc modification, thereby facilitating NPC metastasis. In line with this, PRRC2C expression was elevated in the HM cells (Fig. 3C), and the Co-IP assay revealed that although the overall differences between HM and LM cells were relatively modest in the Co-IP assay, the level of the O-GlcNAc modification of PRRC2C in the immunoprecipitated fraction was significantly higher in HM cells than in LM cells (Fig. 3D). Notably, G3BP1, a core scaffold protein of SGs, is closely linked to cell migration and tumor metastasis and serves as a canonical marker for SGs. Furthermore, through IF analysis and WB, we verified that in HM cells, the expression levels of PRRC2C and G3BP1 were closely related and significantly increased (Fig. 3E-G). These observations collectively suggest that PRRC2C and G3BP1 not only exhibit coordinated expression patterns but also physically associate within SG structures in HM cells, which may synergistically enhance SG formation —providing a structural and functional basis for the enhanced stress adaptation and metastatic potential of HM NPC cells.

The O-GlcNAc modification of PRRC2C S2238 supports SG formation at mitochondria

Given that PRRC2C O-GlcNAc modification was increased in HM cells and OGT knockdown impaired mitochondrial SG formation (Fig. S2A-C), we sought to identify the key O-GlcNAc modification site responsible for SG formation. The results of the proteomic sequencing revealed the PTM flanking region QQSSSLTSVPPTTFS and predicted serine 2238 as the key O-GlcNAc-modified site (Fig. S2D; Table S3). Subsequently, we engineered a site-directed mutagenesis plasmid to replace S2238 with alanine, aiming to attenuate the modification activity for subsequent experimental analyses. Western blot analysis showed that PRRC2C site mutation markedly abolished its O-GlcNAc modification (Fig. 4A). Our subsequent functional assays and in vivo experiments demonstrated that this mutation not only markedly impaired the migratory capacity of tumor cells (Fig. S3A-B) but also abrogated the enhanced metastatic potential of NPC observed in the PRRC2C-WT group (Fig. S3C-E). We also detected the expression level of other SG core proteins in the PRRC2C-WT and PRRC2C-S2238A group, as expected, the WT group exhibited enhanced NUFIP2 and G3BP1 expression (Fig. 4B). These results suggest that O-GlcNAc modification at serine 2238 of PRRC2C promotes SG formation. We performed IF analysis and found that, compared to the PRRC2C-S2238A group, the PRRC2C-WT group cells exhibited an increase in the expression of the SG core protein G3BP1, and more SGs formed at mitochondrial platforms (Fig. 4C-D). Interestingly, we observed the WT cells tend to form more SGs at the mitochondria compared to S2238A cells via transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 4E). This mitochondrial enrichment of SGs in PRRC2C-WT cells is functionally relevant, as mitochondria are the central organelles for cellular energy metabolism and stress adaptation — key processes supporting NPC metastasis. In brief, PRRC2C S2238 O-GlcNAcylation controls location-specific SG formation to drive NPC metastasis.

The O-GlcNAc modification of PRRC2C S2238 promotes mitochondrial functional homeostasis

It is well known that balanced mitochondrial dynamics are essential for mitochondrial quality control and play a central role in tumor metastasis [19, 20]. It has been noted that tumor cells can boost their survival under metabolic stress, facilitate content redistribution, and further encourage metastasis. Consistently, more SGs on the mitochondrial platforms were observed in WT cells. We evaluated mitochondrial membrane potential using JC-1 assay. In WT cells, JC-1 aggregates produced red fluorescence owing to elevated mitochondrial membrane potential, which indicates enhanced mitochondrial dynamics. In contrast, S2238A cells retained normal mitochondrial function (Fig. 5A-B). Western blot analysis revealed a decrease in MFN1 (mitochondrial fusion protein) expression and an increase in MFF (mitochondrial fission protein) expression in the WT cells (Fig. 5C). Consistently, elevated mitochondrial membrane potential and increased MFF expression were also detected in HM cells relative to LM cells (Fig. 5D-F). The IF results also demonstrated that WT cells tended to assemble SGs in mitochondria (Fig. 5G-H). Collectively, our data demonstrated that O-GlcNAc modification at PRRC2C S2238 augments mitochondrial dynamics and induces SG formation in mitochondria. Inhibition of SG effectively suppresses NPC metastasis dirven by PRRC2C O-GlcNAcylation

We aimed to investigate whether the inhibition of SGs could suppress PRRC2C O-GlcNAc modification-induced NPC metastasis both in vitro and in vivo. After a 24-hour exposure to different doses of EGCG (20 µM, 40 µM), which is recognized as an SG inhibitor by inhibiting the G3BP1-cGAS signaling pathway [21]. Transwell migration assays revealed a significant suppression of the enhanced migration in the HM cells (Fig. 6A-B). Western blot analysis further confirmed that EGCG treatment resulted in decreased expression of SG core proteins (Fig. 6C), which means a decrease in SG formation. Concurrently, IF and Co-IP assays demonstrated that EGCG administration also suppressed SG formation and decreased O-GlcNAc modification of PRRC2C (Fig. 6D-E). In parallel, we employed a clinically relevant, imaging-capable orthotopic NPC mouse model to assess the metastatic behavior of the tumor in vivo. In vivo imaging and H&E staining of lung tissues demonstrated that the NC group exhibited a significantly higher rate of tumor metastasis and a lower survival rate compared to the EGCG group (Fig. 6F-H). These findings suggested that despite the aggressive behavior exhibited by HM subline cells, their invasiveness was notably suppressed by the effect of EGCG. Combined with our earlier data, these results support the hypothesis that suppressing SG formation attenuates PRRC2C O-GlcNAcylation-mediated NPC metastasis, offering a potential therapeutic target for NPC treatment. Discussion

The metastasis of tumors is one of the important biological characteristics of malignant tumors, and its process involves multiple complex molecular mechanisms. And O-GlcNAc modification, as a dynamically reversible protein post-translational modification, has been found to be closely related to tumor metastasis in recent years [22]. Our research focused on the metabolic alterations in HM NPC cells, and observed that HM cells exhibit increased glycolytic activity and impaired TCA cycle function, resulting in the production of more substrate UDP-GlcNAc required for O-GlcNAc modification. In subsequent experiments, we demonstrated that the O-GlcNAc modification of PRRC2C at S2238 promotes the metastasis of NPC by mediating SG formation.

Our study identified O-GlcNAc modification of PRRC2C at S2238 as a critical nexus integrating metabolic reprogramming and SG formation in NPC metastasis. The site-specific modification of PRRC2C not only stabilizes its interaction with SG components but also spatially coordinates SG formation at mitochondria, thereby bridging metabolic adaptation to cellular motility. The formation of SGs has become an important stress-response mechanism to various environmental insults [23–25]. However, how SGs selectively recruit proteins to trigger stress-specific responses remains elusive. Overexpression of OGT induces a sharp increase in PRRC2C, leading to more SG formation. It is known that NUFIP2 relocates to SG under stress conditions [26]. SGs have become a system that integrates oncogenic signals and tumor-associated stress stimuli to promote cancer cell adaptability [27]. Protein-protein interactions, facilitated by scaffold RNA, are thought to drive the formation of SGs. For example, the interaction between G3BP1 and the 40 S ribosomal subunit is essential for G3BP-mediated SG formation [28]. Ataxin-2 interacts with the DEAD/H-box RNA helicase DDX6 in SGs, and the level of ataxin-2 is critical for SG formation [29]. The O-GlcNAc modification of PRRC2C at S2238 may promote SG formation to exert its function. In this study, high expression of the SG core proteins G3BP1, NUFIP2 and PRRC2C is associated with unfavorable prognosis in HNSCC (Fig. S4A-C). A detailed future structural dynamic analysis of the interactions between these molecules is necessary.

In parallel, we observed enhanced mitochondrial dynamics, marked by increased fission and elevated membrane potential—adaptive changes to maintain stability and connectivity in the tumor microenvironment. It is reported that STMP1, a mitochondrial micropeptide, facilitates mitochondrial fission to support tumor spread [30]. In hepatocellular carcinoma cells, fragmented mitochondria promoted the remodeling of actin filaments and the formation of lamellipodia or invadopodia to aid in cell metastasis [31].

Increasing evidence suggests that dietary polyphenols (such as curcumin, resveratrol, and tea polyphenols) have chemopreventive properties and may be used in combination with conventional therapies for more effective cancer treatment [32–34]. EGCG, a major polyphenol in green tea, has been shown to possess potential anticancer or cancer-preventive effects by binding to endogenous G3BP1 in tumor cells [35]. Previous reports have shown that EGCG can regulate protein kinases and phosphatases to induce apoptosis and inhibit tumorigenesis in cancer cells [36]. In this study, EGCG suppressed NPC cell invasiveness by inhibiting SG formation in a dose-dependent manner in CNE2 and C666-1 cells. In a nasopharyngeal cancer nude mouse model, EGCG injection reduced metastasis and significantly improved survival, supporting its potential in clinical trials.

Overall, our study provides compelling evidence that in response to metabolic stress of NPC, the O-GlcNAc modification of PRRC2C at S2238 promotes SG formation and the enhanced mitochondrial dynamic favor the SG formation in mitochondria. Additionally, EGCG can impede the metastasis of NPC cells by inhibiting SG formation, indicating that the O-GlcNAc modification of PRRC2C and SG formation may represent promising therapeutic targets for metastatic NPC.
