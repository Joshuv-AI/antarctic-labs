Harnessing oleic acid for facilitating topical delivery of lipid nanoparticle to the ocular posterior segment and improving anti-angiogenesis effect
Authors: Guihua Fang, Aiwen Zhang, Sifan Peng, Xiaojie Wu, Zhuoya Xu, Guowei Zhang, Bo Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fundus neovascularization is a leading cause of vision loss. Axitinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, has significant antiangiogenic effects but suffers from extremely low aqueous solubility and faces multiple ocular barriers when topically instilled. Here, we incorporated oleic acid into a lipid nanoparticle for the topical ocular delivery of axitinib. Meanwhile, we systemically investigated the impact of oleic acid and its content on the performance of lipid nanoparticles in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo. The lipid nanoparticle with 2% oleic acid achieved a 2125-fold increase in axitinib solubility. Pharmacokinetic analysis in rat tears showed that oleic acid did not compromise ocular retention. An ex vivo ocular tissue permeability study revealed concentration-dependent enhancement of corneal permeability and scleral retention by oleic acid. An in vivo biodistribution study indicated that oleic acid facilitated the transport of the lipid nanoparticle into the posterior segment. Consequently, compared to a lipid nanoparticle without oleic acid, this one containing 2% oleic acid showed higher drug bioavailability in posterior segment tissues and a stronger anti-angiogenesis effect in a laser-induced choroidal neovascularization animal model. In addition, the lipid nanoparticle containing 2% oleic acid level exhibited excellent ocular biocompatibility. Overall, our findings highlight the crucial role of oleic acid in non-invasive ocular drug delivery and provide valuable insights into developing fatty acid-containing topical drug delivery systems to treat fundus diseases.

Introduction

Fundus neovascularization disorders, such as wet age-related macular degeneration and diabetic retinopathy, represent leading causes of visual impairment worldwide (Steinmetz et al., 2021). It has been well established that vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and its tyrosine kinase receptors, VEGFR1 and VEGFR2, constitute critical signaling pathways driving neovascularization (Pérez-Gutiérrez and Ferrara, 2023). The limited capacity of topical and systemic drug administration to deliver therapeutics to the posterior segment of the eye has spurred rapidly growing interest in alternative routes, such as intravitreal (Bourzac, 2025). Clinically, intravitreal injection of anti-VEGF antibodies (e.g., ranibizumab) remains the standard treatment regimen (Le Du and Ronco, 2025). This route indeed achieves high local drug concentrations in posterior ocular tissues, including the vitreous body, neural retina, retinal pigment epithelium, and choroid. Nevertheless, as the most invasive mode of posterior segment drug delivery, intravitreal injection carries a high risk of complications such as retinal detachment and endophthalmitis (Cao et al., 2023). Moreover, a subset of patients does not even respond to the anti-VEGF agents (Krebs et al., 2013). Accordingly, there is an urgent need to develop both novel therapeutic candidates and a non-invasive mode of administration.

Axitinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, was originally approved by the U.S. FDA in 2012 for the treatment of advanced renal cell carcinoma (Cohen et al., 2021; Yang and McKeage, 2012). A growing body of research has underscored its promising therapeutic potential in the management of ocular diseases (Annuryanti et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026). Notably, axitinib exhibits higher affinity for VEGFR2 and lower affinity for epidermal growth factor receptor, thus demonstrating favorable ocular safety (Jansook et al., 2024). Several axitinib-based ophthalmic preparations have been or are in clinical trials, including CLS-AX (NCT05891548) and AXPAXLI™ (also known as OTX-TKI, NCT06495918), for the treatment of fundus neovascularization disorders (Cohen et al., 2021). Most of them are administered via ocular injection (e.g., suprachoroidal space injection) or implant (e.g., vitreous implant). Compared with these invasive routes of administration, non-invasive ocular topical instillation is always preferred due to minimal side effects. Nevertheless, the extremely poor aqueous solubility (about 0.4 μg/mL at pH 6.8) in physiological conditions (e.g., tear fluid) restricts dissolution in tear fluid (Praphanwittaya et al., 2020), and multiple ocular barriers, including fast tear elimination, corneal barriers, and conjunctival barriers, lead to insufficient drug accumulation in the fundus tissue and poor therapeutic efficacy (Fang et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2024).

To address the aforementioned challenges, nanocarriers, including polymer- and lipid-based nanocarriers, have shown great promise in topical ocular drug delivery owing to their attractive attributes, such as enhanced solubility, prolonged precorneal retention, and improved ocular penetration (Han et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026). Among these, lipid-based nanocarriers offer advantages in cost-effectiveness and high biocompatibility (Abla and Mehanna, 2023; Ahmed et al., 2025). Particularly, several lipid-based nanocarrier formulations are already commercially available for the management of ocular diseases. For example, Restasis® (cyclosporine anionic nanoemulsion, Allergan) is indicated for patients with chronic dry eye diseases, whereas Ikervis® (cyclosporine cationic nanoemulsion, Santen) is approved for the treatment of severe keratitis associated with dry eye disease (Singh et al., 2020). Beyond the effective management of anterior segment diseases, Recent evidence has demonstrated that lipid-based nanocarriers with appropriate physicochemical properties, such as reduced size, can reach the posterior segment following topical instillation (Puglia et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024). Collectively, these findings indicate that lipid-based nanocarriers hold great potential for the topical delivery of ocular therapeutics targeting fundus diseases.

Oleic acid is a common monounsaturated fatty acid consisting of 18 carbon atoms and one double bond in alkyl chains. Previous research had demonstrated that free oleic acid could enhance corneal permeability of fluorescent probes in ex vivo corneal tissues without causing eye irritation (Gao et al., 2014). Nevertheless, its incorporation into lipid nanoparticle formulations specifically designed for posterior segment delivery remains largely uncharted. This represents a critical knowledge gap, as the potential of oleic acid to function simultaneously as a nanocarrier component and a biological permeation enhancer for fundus-directed delivery remains entirely unexplored.

In this study, we systematically investigated the impact of varying oleic acid content on the in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo performance of axitinib-loaded lipid nanoparticle (ALN). Initially, a screening process for solid lipid was conducted to optimize the ALN formulation. Oleic acid was then incorporated at graded concentrations to generate a series of ALN formulations with varying oleic acid content. The physicochemical properties of these ALN formulations were comprehensively characterized, including particle size, zeta potential, drug encapsulation efficiency, morphology, internal microstructure, in vitro drug release behavior, and storage stability. Simultaneously, the effects of oleic acid content on ex vivo corneal and scleral permeability, as well as in vivo precorneal retention and ocular bioavailability, were evaluated. The in vivo anti-angiogenesis effect of the optimal ALN was further validated in a laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (CNV) animal model (Scheme 1). Materials and methods

Materials and animals

Axitinib was purchased from Shanghai Dibai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Egg yolk lecithin PL-100 M was purchased from AVT (Shanghai) Pharmaceutical Tech Co., Ltd. Precirol® ATO 5 (glyceryl palmitostearate) and Compritol® 888 ATO (glyceryl behenate) were kindly gifted from Standard Pharma Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Stearic acid, oleic acid, and Tween 80 were purchased from Xilong Co., Ltd. (Shantou, China). Naringenin and fluorescein sodium were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Deionized water was produced using a Direct-Q5® UV water purification system. All other chemicals were of analytical grade.

The female Sprague-Dawley rats (weighing 200 ± 20 g, 8 weeks old), male New Zealand white rabbit (weighing 2–3 kg, 8 weeks old), and female C57BL/6 mice (weighing 20 ± 2 g, 8 weeks old) were obtained from the animal center of Nantong University. The animals were maintained in an environment with a 12-h day-night cycle, a temperature of 22 °C, and a relative humidity of 50% with access to food and water ad libitum. After at least 3 days of acclimatization, the experiments were initiated. The entire animal study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nantong University (approval number: S20220308–013), and the study was conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Preparation of ALN

ALN was prepared using the emulsification-solvent evaporation technique (Sun et al., 2025). Initially, PL-100 M (1.2%, m/v), Tween 80 (4.8%, m/v), solid lipids (stearic acid, Precirol® ATO 5, or Compritol® 888 ATO) (6.0%, m/v), and oleic acid (0, 0.5%, 2%, m/v) were accurately weighed and transferred into a beaker. In parallel, a specified amount of axitinib (0.1%, m/v) was weighed into an Eppendorf tube and dissolved in an appropriate volume of anhydrous ethanol. The resulting axitinib ethanol solution was then added to the beaker containing PL-100 M, Tween 80, and the solid lipids. The beaker was subsequently placed in a 75 °C water bath to form the oil phase. Simultaneously, 5 mL of deionized water was heated to 75 °C to serve as the aqueous phase. Upon complete evaporation of anhydrous ethanol under continuous magnetic stirring, the aqueous phase was gradually added dropwise to the oil phase, forming a coarse emulsion. The coarse emulsion was then ultrasonicated using an ultrasonic cell disruptor (Scientz, China) at 400 W for 4 min, operating in pulsed mode (2 s on, 3 s off). The treated emulsion was subsequently solidified in an ice-water bath for 30 min, resulting in the formation of ALN. To obtain the optimal ALN formulation, we initially examined the influence of the selected solid lipids on drug loading and entrapment efficiency. Subsequently, using the optimized solid lipid matrix, oleic acid was incorporated at three varying levels (0, 0.5%, 2%), yielding ALN formulations designated as ALN0, ALN0.5%, and ALN2%, respectively.

Physicochemical characterization of ALN

Size, polydispersity index, and zeta potential

The particle size, polydispersity index, and zeta potential of ALN were measured using a dynamic light scattering analyzer (90 Plus Zeta, Brookhaven, USA). Briefly, an appropriate amount of ALN was diluted with deionized water in a cuvette, and triplicate measurements were conducted at room temperature with a light scattering angle of 90°.

Transmission electron microscope observation

An appropriate volume of the ALN sample was collected, diluted with deionized water, and deposited onto a 200-mesh copper grid. After air-drying at room temperature, the sample was stained with a 2% phosphotungstic acid solution, re-dried, and examined under a transmission electron microscope (TEM) (Hitachi, Japan) to observe its morphology.

Entrapment efficiency and drug loading

The encapsulation efficiency and drug loading of ALN were determined by the ultrafiltration method (He et al., 2026). A certain volume of ALN was taken into an ultrafiltration tube (molecular weight cut-off: 10 kDa). After centrifuging at 2000 rpm for 30 min, the ultrafiltrate was removed, diluted to a certain concentration with deionized water, and then filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane. The sample was then injected into the Agilent high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system to quantify the free drug concentration in the ALN. To determine the total drug content, 0.1 mL ALN was transferred into a 10 mL volumetric flask, anhydrous ethanol was added, and the mixture was ultrasonicated for 30 min. Then, the mixture was centrifuged at 10000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and injected into the HPLC system to determine the total drug concentration in the ALN. Triplicate measurements were conducted. Chromatographic separation was conducted utilizing a C18 column (4.6 mm × 100 mm, 2.7 μm) maintained at 25 °C. A mobile phase comprising water and acetonitrile in a 50: 50 (v/v) ratio was employed, with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. UV detection was performed at 330 nm, and the injection volume was 10 μL. The retention time is around 6.4 min, and the total run time is 9.5 min. The calibration curves (y = 150.35×-3.8046) were linear from 1 to 10 μg/mL with a coefficient of R2 = 0.9998. The limits of detection and quantitation are 0.00038 μg/mL and 0.0013 μg/mL, respectively. The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL) of ALN were subsequently calculated using the following equations.EE%=mtotal−mfreemin×100DL%=Ctotal−Cfree×Vmmw×100where mtotal: measured total mass of the drug; mfree: measured mass of free drug; min: actual input mass of the drug; Ctotal: measured total concentration of the drug; Cfree: measured concentration of free drug; Vm: measured actual final volume of the ALN; mw: total input mass of all the excipients and the drug.

Differential scanning calorimetry

The physical state of axitinib within lipid-based nanoparticles was investigated using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) (STA 449F, Germany). Specifically, freshly prepared formulations were initially pre-frozen in a − 80 °C refrigerator (Thermo Scientific, USA) for a duration of 48 h. Subsequently, they underwent lyophilization in a − 50 °C vacuum freeze-drying machine (Beijing Songyuan Huaxing LGJ-10, China) for another 48 h. The lyophilized samples were then subjected to analysis at a heating rate of 10 K/min, spanning a temperature range from 20 to 250 °C.

Coarse-grained molecular dynamic simulation

The Martini Force Field 3.0, incorporating a water model, was employed to simulate the axitinib-loaded lipid nanoparticle. The coarse-grained (CG) structures of axitinib, Precirol® ATO 5, oleic acid, PL-100 M, and Tween 80 were manually parameterized in accordance with the Martini mapping principle, where three to four atoms were grouped into a single CG bead (Khalkhali et al., 2019). The initial system configuration was constructed within a cubic box using the Packmol software package. All simulations were performed using the GROMACS 2020.3 software package. The systems were maintained at 348 K using a velocity-rescaling thermostat. Energy minimization was performed using 50,000 steps of the steepest-descent method, followed by a 10 ns equilibrium run with a 20 fs time step, under a Parrinello−Rahman semi-isotropic barostat. The compressibility was set to 3 × 10−4 bar−1, and the pressure was held constant at 1 bar throughout the simulation. Production runs of 250 ns and 500 ns were conducted for ALN0.5% and ALN2%, respectively. For Lennard-Jones interactions, a potential-shift Verlet scheme was adopted with a cutoff radius of 1.1 nm. Electrostatic interactions beyond 1.1 nm were treated using the reaction field method.

In vitro drug release behavior

A dialysis method was adopted to study the in vitro drug release behavior of ALN. 1 mL of ALN formulation (equivalent to 20 μg) was transferred into dialysis bags (with a molecular weight cut-off of 3.5 kDa) and then placed in 40 mL of pH = 7.4 simulated artificial tear fluid (composition per 100 mL: NaCl 0.678 g, NaHCO3 0.218 g, KCl 0.138 g, CaCl2 0.00634 g, deionized water to 100 mL) containing 1% Tween 80. The release experiment was conducted in a water bath shaker at 35 ± 0.5 °C and 100 rpm. At predetermined time points, 1 mL of the release medium was taken out and replaced with an equal volume of fresh release medium. 1 mL of the release medium was transferred into an Eppendorf tube, centrifuged at 10000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was taken for HPLC analysis as described in Section 2.3.3. All experiments were carried out in triplicate. Free axitinib solution (equivalent to 20 μg) was prepared by dissolving an amount of axitinib in DMSO solution. The cumulative release (%) was calculated using the following equation:Cumulative release%=Cn×Vmdrug×100where Cn is the drug concentration in the release medium at the nth sampling interval, V is the volume of the release medium, and mdrug is the initial total amount of drug in the dialysis bag.

Stability

The storage stability of ALN formulations was evaluated at 4 °C by monitoring drug content and particle size at predetermined time intervals. For formulations exhibiting a rapid decline in drug concentration, monitoring was discontinued at subsequent time points. Specifically, ALN0 was assessed at days 0, 0.25, 0.5, and 1; ALN0.5% at days 0, 1, 2, and 4; and ALN2% at days 0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 14, and 29. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Precorneal retention study

The Schirmer strip approach is used to study tear pharmacokinetics (Fang et al., 2025). 12 female Sprague-Dawley rats were randomly assigned into 3 groups: ALN0, ALN0.5%, and ALN2% (n = 4 rats per group). Sprague-Dawley rats received a 10 μL topical instillation of ALN to the lower conjunctival sac in one eye at a concentration of 0.8 mg/mL and no instillation in the fellow eye. At different time points (5 min, 10 min, 15 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 12 h), pre-weighed filter paper strips were inserted into the conjunctival sac to collect tears, kept for 20 s, taken out immediately, and weighed, and the weight difference before and after was recorded. The collected tears were then added to 200 μL of 1 μg/mL internal standard naringenin methanol solution, vortexed for 90 s, centrifuged for 15 min at 10000 rpm and 4 °C, and the drug concentration in the supernatant was determined by HPLC. For the separation of the chromatographic peaks of axitinib and naringenin in the HPLC system, the chromatographic conditions were the same as in Section 2.3.3, except that the mobile phase was water-acetonitrile (40:60, v/v). Under these conditions, the retention times for axitinib and naringenin were around 12.8 min and 11.1 min, with a total run time of 14 min. The average extraction recoveries of axitinib and naringenin from tear fluid exceeded 80%, meeting the acceptance criteria for quantitative analysis. Furthermore, the pharmacokinetic parameter, area under the curve (AUC), was calculated. After completion of the experiment, the rats were euthanized and then transferred to the Laboratory Animal Center for centralized disposal.

Ex vivo ocular tissue permeability

The ocular tissue permeability of ALN (ALN0, ALN0.5%, and ALN2%) was studied using a vertical Franz diffusion cell with an effective diffusion area of 0.785 cm2 and 3 corneas/sclera for each ALN formulation (n = 3). Specifically, five healthy adult male New Zealand white rabbits were euthanized, and their eyeballs were removed. The cornea or sclera was excised and washed with artificial aqueous humor (Solution A: NaCl 12.4 g, KCl 0.716 g, NaH2PO4 0.18 g, NaHCO3 4.908 g per 1000 mL distilled water; Solution B: CaCl2 0.1737 g, MgCl2·6H2O 0.318 g, glucose 1.8 g, oxidized glutathione 0.184 g per 1000 mL distilled water; mixed 1:1 before use). The receptor chamber was filled with 7 mL of artificial aqueous humor. The cornea or sclera was fixed between the donor chamber and the receptor chamber of the Franz diffusion cell. Considering that the axitinib concentration in the tear fluid is relatively low, the ALN formulation needs to cover the entire surface of the cornea/sclera. 1 mL of ALN with an axitinib concentration of 100 μg/mL was added to the donor chamber. The entire apparatus was then placed in a transdermal diffusion tester (RYG-6B, Huanghai Instruments, China) at 34.5 ± 0.5 °C. At predetermined time points (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 h), 100 μL of the sample was taken from the receptor chamber, and an equal volume of fresh artificial aqueous humor was supplemented. To the removed sample, 10 μL of methanol was added, vortexed for 90 s, centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was taken for HPLC analysis, as described in Section 2.3.3, to determine the drug concentration at each time point. The cumulative permeation (Qn) and apparent permeability coefficient (Papp) were calculated using the following equations:Qn=V0ACn+VV0∑i=1n−1CiPapp=△Q△t∙C0where Cn is the measured drug concentration at time t, Ci is the measured concentration before time t, V0 is the volume of solution in the receptor chamber, V is the sampling volume, C0 is the initial drug concentration in the donor chamber, and A is the effective permeation area. ΔQ/Δt is obtained from the slope of the steady-state part of the cumulative permeation-time curve.

In vivo ocular biodistribution

Healthy female Sprague-Dawley rats weighing 200 ± 20 g were randomly assigned to three groups (ALN0, ALN0.5%, and ALN2%), with three rats per group. Sprague-Dawley rats received a 10 μL topical instillation of ALN to the lower conjunctival sac in two eyes at a concentration of 0.8 mg/mL. The eyelids were closed for 20 s to avoid spillage of the formulations and allow uniform distribution of the drugs on the ocular surface. At predetermined time points (15 min, 30 min, 60 min, 120 min, 240 min, and 480 min), aqueous humor was collected using a capillary tube, and other ocular tissues, including the cornea, crystalline lens, conjunctiva (unless specified otherwise, the term “conjunctiva” hereafter refers to the bulbar conjunctiva), sclera, and vitreous-retina-choroid complex, were excised after CO2 euthanasia. The excised solid ocular tissues were thoroughly rinsed with saline, blotted dry using filter paper, and precisely weighed. Subsequently, these tissues were finely chopped and transferred into tissue grinding tubes (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Each tube was supplemented with 200 μL of a 1 μg/mL naringenin internal standard solution, then homogenized at 4 °C for 2 min using a low-speed grinding instrument (Servicebio, China). Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. After centrifugation, 100 μL of the supernatant was collected for HPLC analysis. For the aqueous humor, 40 μL of the sample was mixed with 80 μL of 1 μg/mL naringenin internal standard solution. The mixture was vortexed for 2 min and centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, 100 μL of the supernatant was taken for HPLC analysis as described in Section 2.4. The pharmacokinetic parameter AUC was also calculated by non-compartmental analysis.

In vivo anti-angiogenesis effect in a laser-induced choroidal neovascularization animal model

The bioavailability of drugs in ocular tissues is a critical indicator of therapeutic efficacy. Ocular tissue distribution analysis revealed that drug exposure in fundus tissues for the ALN0.5% group was comparable to that of the ALN0 group, whereas the ALN2% group exhibited a significantly higher AUC than both of the other groups. Consequently, ALN2% rather than ALN0.5% was selected for subsequent pharmacodynamic studies. In brief, female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of ketamine (90 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), followed by pupil dilation with 1% tropicamide. The Bruch's membrane was ruptured at the 3, 6, 9, and 12 o'clock positions using a 532 nm diode laser for photocoagulation (duration: 50 ms, power: 200 mW). Immediate bubble formation after laser application indicated successful model establishment. Laser-induced burns were imaged using a SARIS system (Robotrak, China). On day 5 after successful establishment of the CNV model, the 24 mice were divided into three groups (n = 8 each) randomly: the normal saline group, the ALN0 group, and the ALN2% group. 10 μL of each formulation containing 8 μg was topically instilled into the ocular surface twice daily for 7 consecutive days.

To evaluate therapeutic efficacy, anesthetized mice received an intraperitoneal injection of 0.1 mL of 2% sodium fluorescein. On day 12, fundus fluorescein angiography was performed using a SARIS system (Robotrak, China). The assessment of CNV leakage was based on the size of vascular leakage, and the area of sodium fluorescein leakage was quantified using ImageJ software.

Following the 12-day observation period, the mice were euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation, and the choroid-retina complexes were collected for further analysis, including hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining, immunofluorescence staining, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), and RNA sequencing. The primer sequences used in qRT-PCR are shown in Table S1. RNA sequencing was performed using the IDNBSEQ-T7 platform. Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2, with thresholds set at Log2 (Fold Change) ≥ 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.

Ocular safety

Single-dose ocular irritation test

Three healthy male New Zealand White rabbits (6 eyes) were enrolled, and each eye was assigned to one of two groups in a paired design. Specifically, one eye of each rabbit received 40 μL of ALN2% (0.8 mg/mL) via topical instillation into the conjunctival sac, while the contralateral eye received an equal volume of normal saline, serving as the control (Hao et al., 2024). Before administration, the conjunctiva, cornea, and other tissues of each rabbit's eyes were observed using a portable handheld slit lamp (KJ5S2, Kangjie, China) (white light) and fluorescein sodium staining (cobalt blue light), and photographed for the record. Then, the eyes were examined and scored at fixed time points of 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h after administration. At these fixed time points, a drop of 0.2% fluorescein sodium solution was dripped into both eyes, and the slit lamp light source was adjusted to cobalt blue light to check for ocular surface damage. If there were damaged areas in the cornea or conjunctiva, fluorescein sodium would be retained in those damaged areas under cobalt blue light, showing green. Conversely, no green fluorescence would appear. The detailed scoring criteria and classification of ocular irritation are listed in Table S2 (Wilhelmus, 2001) and Table S3 (Sun et al., 2023). After completion of the observation period, the rabbits were euthanized and transferred to the Laboratory Animal Center for centralized, humane disposal.

Multiple-dose ocular irritation test

The experimental grouping, pre-administration inspection, and dosage were consistent with those in Section 2.8.1. The administration was performed twice daily for 7 consecutive days. Observations and scoring were recorded before each daily administration and after the final administration. After completion of multiple administrations, optical coherence tomography (iVue, Optovue, USA) was performed on the eyes, followed by photography. Subsequently, the rabbits were euthanized, and the eyeballs were quickly removed and immersed in Eyeball Fixed Liquid (Phygene, China). After being fixed for 24 h, the eyeballs were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and deparaffinized. The sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin for histological observation. Histological observations and analyses of the cornea and retina were performed using an optical microscope (Leica, Germany).

Statistical analysis

All results are presented as mean ± standard deviation, with the exception of pharmacodynamic data. The statistical analysis was performed using an unpaired Student's t-test. The p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Preparation of ALN

Axitinib exhibits extremely low solubility in aqueous media, with intrinsic solubility in the microgram-per-milliliter range (Reyner et al., 2013). This poor aqueous solubility restricts dissolution in tear fluid, limiting the drug concentration available for intraocular absorption and resulting in low bioavailability following topical administration. Therefore, improving drug solubility with lipid nanoparticles is a crucial prerequisite for ensuring ocular bioavailability. Ethanol was selected as a suitable solvent because of its good solubilizing effect for the lipids and surfactants used in this study, and it is also classified as a Class 3 residual solvent in the ICH Q3C guideline. Considering axitinib is slightly soluble in ethanol (<1 mg/mL at 25 °C) (NCBI Compound Database, 2016). Consequently, axitinib was first dissolved in ethanol before the addition of lipids and emulsifier during the preparation process. The type of solid lipid played a significant role in improving the aqueous solubility of axitinib. Initially, three solid lipids, including stearic acid, Precirol® ATO 5, and Compritol® 888 ATO, were used to prepare ALN with the constant use of axitinib and other ingredients (including PL-100 M and Tween 80) in the formulation. Subsequently, the changes in the encapsulation efficiency and drug loading of ALN were investigated and shown in Table 1. The experimental findings demonstrated that formulations prepared with stearic acid as the solid lipid matrix exhibited a highly viscous cream-like consistency and large particle size (> 1 μm). Precirol® ATO 5 outperformed Compritol® 888 ATO as the solid lipid matrix, achieving higher encapsulation efficiency (65.17% vs. 51.03%) and drug loading (0.540% vs. 0.420%). In vitro physicochemical characterization

Notably, all ALN formulations exhibited a narrow particle size distribution, with polydispersity index values below 0.3. To identify the possible function of oleic acid in ALN, a DSC test was conducted. As shown in Fig. 1c, the DSC curve of axitinib showed a characteristic endothermic peak at 221 °C, and this peak disappeared in the DSC curves of ALN0, ALN0.5%, and ALN2%, indicating that axitinib was present in an amorphous state in the ALN. Additionally, the DSC curves of ALN0, ALN0.5%, and ALN2% exhibited an endothermic peak at 58 °C, corresponding to the melting temperature of Precirol® ATO 5. The melting enthalpy (ΔHm) of ALN0, ALN0.5%, and ALN2% was obtained from the area of the corresponding melting peaks at 58 °C. In comparison with ALN without oleic acid (ALN0), the relative ΔHm of ALN0.5% and ALN2% was reduced to 89.2% and 69.1%, respectively (Fig. 1d). This change in ΔHm indicates that incorporating oleic acid into lipid nanoparticles induced lipid crystal lattice defects and reduced the lipid crystallinity (Shu et al., 2024). This finding explains the mechanism by which incorporating oleic acid enhances the drug loading capacity of lipid nanoparticles.

Although the morphology of ALN can be discerned through TEM, its internal microstructure remains elusive. Coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulation is a valuable approach for visually elucidating the internal microstructure of ALN. The internal structure of ALN is shown through longitudinal cutting after simulation (Fig. 1e). It was found that, although most oleic acid is incorporated within the Precirol® ATO 5 lipid matrix, a proportion remains concentrated at the oil-water interface of the nanoparticle. Additionally, an increase in oleic acid leads to greater distribution both within the lipid matrix and at the oil-water interface. More recently, Ramona Jeitler et al. found that oleic acid exhibited superior miscibility with stabilizers such as Tween 80. As a result, it tended to coat the surface of the lipid nanoparticle (Jeitler et al., 2024). Our findings are consistent with their research.

In vitro drug release behavior in artificial tear fluid was studied under sink conditions using the dialysis bag method. As shown in Fig. 1f, ALN exhibited sustained drug release over 24 h, with a cumulative release of approximately 45%. The in vitro release profiles among ALN formulations with varying oleic acid content showed no significant difference, indicating that oleic acid had no substantial effect on drug release from ALN. Notably, drug precipitation was observed within the dialysis bag of free axitinib solution during the experimental period, resulting in incomplete release profiles in the present study. This finding is generally consistent with previous investigations (Shi et al., 2019). In contrast, no such precipitation was detected for any of the ALN formulations throughout the release study. Although the cumulative release from ALN did not reach 100%, the absence of drug precipitation, supports a sustained-release mechanism.

The stability study evaluated the effect of varying oleic acid content within the ALN formulation on physical properties, specifically drug concentration and particle size. As shown in Fig. S1, the oleic acid content markedly influenced the stability of ALN when stored at 4 °C. Notably, ALN2% exhibited no appreciable changes in either drug concentration or particle size over a 29-day period. In contrast, ALN0.5% showed a rapid decline in drug concentration of approximately 53% by day 1, displaying a stability profile comparable to that of ALN0. These findings indicate that increasing oleic acid content enhances the physical stability of ALN. An appropriate amount of oleic acid appears to substantially improve the colloidal stability of lipid nanoparticles and mitigate drug expulsion during storage, consistent with previous research findings (Ghezzi et al., 2021; Teng et al., 2014).

Effect of oleic acid on ALN ocular residence ability

Blinking and fast tear renewal result in short precorneal retention and low intraocular bioavailability (Fang et al., 2021). Therefore, prolonged ocular retention is a prerequisite for drug-loaded nanocarriers to penetrate ocular tissues. The ocular retention capacity of ALN was quantitatively evaluated by tear pharmacokinetics. Linearity was assessed by plotting the peak area ratio of axitinib to internal standard against the nominal concentration in blank tear fluid supernatant. The calibration curves demonstrated good linearity over the concentration range of 0.002 to 10 μg/mL (R2 = 0.9997).

The concentration-time profiles of axitinib in tears following topical administration of ALN are presented in Fig. 2a. Tear axitinib concentrations for all three formulations (ALN0, ALN0.5%, and ALN2%) peaked at 5 min post-instillation and subsequently decreased, with detectable levels sustained up to 12 h. Additionally, no significant difference in axitinib concentration was observed among the three formulations at any time point. The axitinib exposure in tear fluid (AUC) also exhibited similar values (Fig. 2b), which demonstrated that oleic acid had little impact on the ocular residence time of ALN. In the present study, incorporating oleic acid into lipid nanoparticles led to a modest increase in size, from 118.04 nm to 199.38 nm. Importantly, this variation in particle size had no discernible effect on their ocular retention time. Beyond size alterations, oleic acid induced a notable change in the zeta potential of the lipid nanoparticle, shifting the value from −26.24 mV to −34.87 mV. Given that both the mucus layer and epithelium possess negative charges, this change in the zeta potential of the lipid nanoparticle did not obviously alter its electrostatic interactions with these negatively charged surfaces. Effect of oleic acid on ALN corneal and scleral permeability

Generally, topically administered ocular drugs enter intraocular tissues via either the corneal route or non-corneal route, where the physicochemical properties of the drug are known to exert a dominant effect on its preferred transport route (Macwan et al., 2016; Thomas et al., 2022). In the present study, both ex vivo corneal permeability and scleral permeability assays were performed to systemically evaluate the transmembrane penetration capability of ALN with varying oleic acid content across these two distinct ocular routes.

In the corneal permeation study, the mean cumulative amount of drug permeated (Qn) from ALN2% was 1.6- and 1.3-fold higher than that from ALN0 and ALN0.5% at 6 h (Fig. 3a). Similarly, the mean value of Papp from ALN2% was 1.7- and 1.5-fold higher than those from ALN0 and ALN0.5%, respectively (Fig. 3b). These results indicate that oleic acid can enhance ALN corneal permeability in a concentration-dependent manner.Fig. 3Ex vivo corneal and scleral permeability of ALN0, ALN0.5%, ALN2%. Before delving into the mechanisms by which oleic acid increases corneal permeability, it is instructive to review the structure and components of the cornea. The cornea is generally composed of five distinct layers: the epithelium, Bowman's layer, stroma, Descemet's membrane, and endothelium, each with its specific function. The corneal epithelium, which consists of nonkeratinized, stratified, squamous epithelial cells, serves as the primary barrier to corneal drug absorption. It is well established that phospholipids are the main constituents of cell membranes. Studies have reported that the cornea contains phospholipids, including neutral zwitterionic and negatively charged phospholipids, plasmalogens, lyso-derivatives, and sphingolipids (Greiner and Glonek, 2024). Furthermore, both saturated (C16:0 and C18:0; 48 mol%) and unsaturated fatty acid chains (C18:1; 42 mol%) are present in the phospholipid fatty acyl chains of the corneal epithelium (Graff, 2004). Additionally, it is a well-established observation in biological membranes that saturated fatty acids typically occupy the sn1 position, whereas polyunsaturated fatty acids preferentially reside at the sn2 position (Huang, 2001). Based on these findings, Fig. 3c provides a simplified schematic illustration of the phospholipid bilayer structure of the corneal epithelium. According to coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulation results, a part of oleic acid is located at the ALN interface, especially for ALN at a 2% concentration (Fig. 1e). Given the cis double bond configuration of oleic acid, it is speculated that it may preferentially partition into the phospholipid bilayer of the corneal epithelium upon contact with ALN. This interaction may enhance cell membrane fluidity, which could potentially contribute to the increased corneal permeability (Fig. 3c) (Kurniawan et al., 2017).

The scleral permeability trend was opposite to that observed for corneal permeability. The mean cumulative amount of drug permeated (Qn) from ALN2% was 2.3- and 2.1-fold lower than that from ALN0 and ALN0.5% at 6 h (Fig. 3d). Similarly, the mean value of Papp from ALN2% was 2.4- and 2.1-fold lower than those from ALN0 and ALN0.5%, respectively (Fig. 3e). These findings indicate that oleic acid incorporation reduced ALN scleral permeability. Distinct from the multicellular architecture of the cornea, the sclera is predominantly composed of fibrous connective tissue (Alshaikh et al., 2022). Scleral permeability has been reported to correlate closely with molecular size, molecular weight, and lipophilicity, with particle size being a particularly dominant factor (Ambati et al., 2000; Boote et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2026). Consistent with this, the incorporation of oleic acid into lipid nanoparticles resulted in a moderate increase in particle size, from 118.04 nm to 199.38 nm (Fig. 1a), which likely contributed to the reduced scleral permeability observed for ALN2% relative to ALN0. Notably, despite the lower permeability, a higher oleic acid content was associated with greater axitinib retention within the sclera after 6 h (Table S4). Given that the sclera is avascular, it may serve as a reservoir, potentially contributing to sustained delivery to the posterior segment.

The calibration curves exhibited linearity with high correlation coefficients (R > 0.99) across various ocular tissues (Table S5). Moreover, the relative biodistribution of ALN in ocular tissues was quantitatively assessed by calculating the ratio of the average AUC value for each individual ocular tissue to the sum of AUC values for all measured ocular tissues (Fig. 5b). It was observed that the relative drug exposure of ALN ranking in these ocular tissues was as follows: conjunctiva > cornea > vitreous-retina-choroid > sclera > aqueous humor ≳ crystalline lens. Generally, it has been reported that free drug molecules can penetrate from the ocular surface into the posterior segment via the corneal pathway (i.e., cornea-aqueous humor-posterior segment (vitreous-retina)) and non-corneal pathway (conjunctiva-sclera-choroid-retina-vitreous) (Fig. 5c) (Jiang et al., 2019; Wei et al., 2025). The drug exposure (AUC) was higher in tissues of the non-corneal route than in tissues of the corneal route. Collectively, we may tentatively infer that ALN absorption could proceed via both the corneal and non-corneal pathways, and it is plausible that the non-corneal route might serve as a notably important contributor to ALN delivery toward the posterior ocular segment. While incorporation of oleic acid may enhance the concentration of axitinib in the aqueous humor, outflow from this humor and the physical lens barrier (Fig. S2) could lead to a decrease in the drug's direct entry into the posterior segment. However, it is believed that the uveoscleral outflow of aqueous humor aids in the transport of axitinib into the sclera and subsequently into the choroid (Toris et al., 2021). Additionally, the conjunctiva has a greater pore size than the cornea (3.0 nm vs 2.0 nm) and higher pore density, which is beneficial for conjunctival transport (Kaur et al., 2023).

Above all, the incorporation of oleic acid into lipid nanoparticles has been demonstrated to enhance the bioavailability of axitinib across a variety of ocular tissues. Most crucially, oleic acid enables more efficient delivery of ALN to the posterior segment of the eye, leveraging both the corneal pathway and the conjunctiva-scleral route. It is worth noting that the mean axitinib concentration at 480 min was 198, 169, and 560 ng/g for ALN0, ALN0.5%, and ALN2%, respectively, which are 1650×, 1408×, and 4667× higher than the in vitro (VEGFR autophosphorylation inhibition) IC50 value (0.12 ng/mL) (Ciulla, 2020). Hence, this axitinib-loaded lipid nanoparticle holds great promise for treating fundus diseases.

Anti-angiogenic effect of ALN in an animal model of laser-induced choroidal neovascularization

Inspired by the enhanced drug exposure in the posterior segment tissues, we selected ALN2% as a representative formulation to assess its therapeutic efficacy in an animal model of laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (Fig. 6a). The pathogenesis of choroidal neovascularization is initiated by abnormally increased microvascular permeability, which drives the proliferation of new blood vessels that sprout from pre-existing choroidal vessels, and this uncontrolled neovascular growth eventually causes pathological intraocular hemorrhage (Dudley and Griffioen, 2023; Zhang et al., 2012). Pathological vascular leakage and the size of CNV lesions were evaluated using fundus fluorescein angiography (Fig. 6b). After 7 days of treatment, a reduction in fluorescein leakage area was observed across all treatment groups, with the ALN2% group showing a notably smaller residual leakage area than the other groups. Quantitative analysis of the fluorescein leakage area was further performed (Fig. 6c). The results demonstrated that the relative leakage area in the ALN2% group was reduced to 33.9%, whereas the ALN0 group showed a reduction to 49.2% of the initial value, indicating that the ALN2% exhibited a superior inhibitory effect on neovascularization. Meanwhile, HE staining was used to observe histopathological changes in retinal and choroidal tissues. As illustrated in Fig. 6d, the most pronounced retinal structural damage was observed in the saline group, which presented distinct bulging and deformation, accompanied by disrupted and disarranged retinal architectural layers. After ALN treatment, the extent of tissue damage was obviously alleviated, and the ALN2% group in particular presented minimal tissue injury. Immunofluorescence staining was further applied to assess the anti-angiogenic effect of ALN (Fig. 6e). Apart from angiogenesis, inflammation is also a crucial factor driving the development of choroidal neovascularization (Zhao and Lai, 2024). The mRNA expression levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were assessed via qRT-PCR. As shown in Fig. 6f-h, topical instillation of ALN0 and ALN2% downregulated the gene expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α compared with the saline group, with ALN2% demonstrating a more potent downregulating effect than ALN0. Notably, ALN2% reduced IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α expression to approximately 10%, 19%, and 17% of the saline group levels, respectively, indicating the most prominent anti-inflammatory response among the tested groups.

To obtain profound insights into the molecular mechanisms for ALN2% inhibition of angiogenesis, transcriptome sequencing was performed in saline- and ALN2%-treated groups. The gene expression levels related to angiogenesis were analyzed. It was found that the genes related to vascular development (Fig. S3a) and the migration of vascular endothelial cells (Fig. S3b) were downregulated in the ALN2%. All these results suggested that ALN2% was capable of delivering drugs to the posterior segment of the eye, effectively inhibiting CNV and preventing vascular leakage.

Ocular safety

The Draize test was employed to evaluate the ocular safety profile of ALN. Initially, the integrity of the rabbit's ocular surface and adjacent tissues was assessed using a slit lamp following a single topical administration. As depicted in Fig. 7a, no discernible abnormalities, edema, or secretions were detected in the cornea, conjunctiva, iris, or other ocular tissues subsequent to a single instillation of ALN2%. Moreover, the irritation scores of ALN2% were uniformly below 1. Typically, healthy corneal epithelial cells exhibit intact structures with tightly interconnected intercellular spaces, serving as a barrier that limits the penetration of fluorescein sodium. Consequently, under normal physiological conditions, fluorescein sodium does not permeate corneal or conjunctival epithelial cells, but it stains damaged or inflamed ocular surface tissues (Bandamwar et al., 2014). Therefore, fluorescein sodium staining was used to determine the ocular surface injury. Notably, the rabbit's ocular surface tissues did not exhibit green staining with fluorescein sodium, suggesting that the corneal and conjunctival structure remained intact (Fig. 7a). Subsequently, the ocular safety of ALN2% was further evaluated through repeated topical administration, with twice-daily dosing. Rabbit eyes were examined and photographed using a handheld slit lamp before the initial dosing and after multiple administrations on days 0, 1, 5, and 7. As illustrated in Fig. 7b, no significant irritant responses were observed in the cornea, conjunctiva, or iris of the rabbits throughout the 7 consecutive days of administration. Concurrently, the eye irritation scores were recorded following multiple administrations (Table S6), yielding a mean score of ≤3, further supporting the favorable safety profile of ALN2%. On day 7, optical coherence tomography revealed that the cornea and retina in the ALN2%-treated eye exhibited no morphological or structural alterations relative to the saline-treated control (Fig. 7c). Immediately thereafter, corneal and retinal tissues were harvested and processed for hematoxylin and eosin staining to observe potential pathological alterations. As shown in Fig. 7d, the cornea treated with ALN2% maintained intact epithelial and stromal layers during the experiment period, with no evidence of inflammation or tissue damage. Likewise, the retina in the ALN2% group displayed no obvious structural abnormalities compared with the saline control group (Fig. 7d). In conclusion, both single- and multiple-administration safety evaluations indicated that ALN2% does not induce significant irritation. These findings collectively suggest that ALN is a safe topical ocular delivery system.

Conclusion

In this study, ALN with varying oleic acid contents were formulated to explore the impact of oleic acid content on the in vitro physicochemical properties, ex vivo permeability, and in vivo biodistribution, as well as pharmacodynamic behavior following topical administration. The results revealed that oleic acid at a 2% level increased ALN not only in encapsulation efficiency but also in storage stability. Corneal permeability assays demonstrated that oleic acid significantly improved ALN corneal permeability, with higher oleic acid content yielding progressively enhanced permeability. In contrast, scleral permeability demonstrated an opposite trend. Notably, oleic acid had minimal impact on the retention of ALN on the ocular surface. Additionally, oleic acid improved the ocular bioavailability of ALN. The higher the oleic acid content, the more efficient the posterior delivery. In a laser-induced choroidal neovascularization animal model, ALN containing 2% oleic acid content demonstrated a superior anti-angiogenic effect compared to one without oleic acid. Moreover, this ALN demonstrated excellent ocular biocompatibility. In summary, oleic acid incorporation could facilitate ALN delivery to the ocular posterior segment and boost its anti-angiogenesis effect following topical instillation.
