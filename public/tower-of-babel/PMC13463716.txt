In Situ Construction of Fascial Analogues Induced by Low-Growth-Factor Matrigel
Authors: Jianming Yue, Yumeng Guo, Haixiang Huang, Zhenwei Zhang, Lu Mei, Qiusheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Simple SummaryFascia is a thin layer of connective tissue that wraps around muscles and organs and supplies cells that help repair damaged skin. However, fascia is too thin and fragile to collect from laboratory animals in useful amounts. In this study, we injected a soft, commercially available gel containing a low dose of a natural growth-stimulating protein under the skin of mice. The animals’ own cells gradually moved into the gel and rebuilt it into a new tissue that closely resembled natural fascia in structure and cell composition, without forming scar tissue. Cells taken from this newly formed tissue remained active in culture and, when placed back onto skin wounds of the same animals within a supportive gel, helped the wounds close faster. This approach offers a simple way to obtain fascia-like tissue and repair cells from an animal’s own body, and may support future studies on tissue repair and regenerative therapies.

1. Introduction

Fascia, a continuous connective tissue network distributed throughout the body, has long been regarded as more than just a structural sheath surrounding muscles, vessels, and nerves in anatomy and physiology [1]. Recent advances in regenerative medicine have fundamentally redefined this view. Emerging evidence reveals that fascia is not only a mechanical scaffold but also a highly active biological microenvironment and a reservoir of endogenous cells. Notably, during the repair of skin and tissue injuries, fascia has been identified as a primary source of wound-healing cells and neo-vasculature [2,3]. Therefore, establishing an experimentally accessible fascial tissue model would significantly accelerate research progress in areas such as tissue repair. However, fascial tissues from experimental animals such as mice are characterized by their thinness and fragility, resulting in low yield during anatomical dissection and a high risk of contamination from adjacent muscle and adipose tissues, which poses challenges for in vitro studies of fascia.

Among the key cellular constituents within the fascia, stromal cells expressing CD34 and PDGFRα have garnered significant attention [4,5]. These cells have been described as Telocytes (TCs), characterized by exceptionally long, slender cytoplasmic extensions known as telopodes. Studies have shown that TCs are widely distributed throughout the fascial stroma, where they form a three-dimensional interstitial network and exhibit multipotent differentiation, paracrine activity, and mechanosensory features. These traits allow them to play important roles in various aspects of fascial physiology [6,7]. As a result, acquiring abundant fascia rich in TCs has become a key goal in TC research.

In exploring methods for in vivo cell recruitment and tissue creation, the Matrigel plug assay has long been considered the gold standard for evaluating angiogenesis [8]. By injecting growth factor-enriched Matrigel subcutaneously, this method facilitates robust endothelial ingrowth and allows the resulting plug to be easily retrieved surgically. As a reconstituted basement membrane matrix with high biocompatibility, Matrigel generally prevents strong foreign-body reactions [9]. However, previous studies have mainly focused on angiogenesis, often overlooking matrix remodeling and the role of non-endothelial cell populations. We observe that upon reducing the concentration of factors added to induce explosive vascular growth in experiments, the Matrigel matrix may provide a unique, low-inflammatory microenvironment. Under these conditions, TCs within the fascia can transmigrate into the matrix and remodel it in a non-fibrotic manner.

Based on these observations, we hypothesized that a low-growth-factor Matrigel microenvironment may provide a permissive niche that promotes the recruitment and organization of fascia-associated stromal cells while avoiding excessive fibrotic encapsulation. To test this hypothesis, we modified the conventional Matrigel plug assay and established an in situ induced fascial analogue (iFA) model. We systematically characterized the structural organization, cellular composition, and immune microenvironment of the generated tissue, with particular attention to CD34+/PDGFRα+ stromal cells and their association with fascial features. Furthermore, we evaluated whether cells derived from the iFA possess regenerative potential in a full-thickness skin wound model. Through these analyses, this study aims to provide a mechanistic understanding of how an engineered extracellular matrix environment can facilitate fascial-like tissue formation and to explore its potential application as a source of autologous regenerative cells.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Experimental Animals

Female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Nanjing Qinglongshan Animal Breeding Center and housed in the Laboratory Animal Center of Nanjing Agricultural University (laboratory animal facility license: SYXK(SU)20210086). All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Agricultural University (Approval No. NJAU.No20220427087).

2.2. Construction of the In Situ Induced Fascial Analogue (iFA)

A volume of 300 μL low-growth-factor Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA, Cat. No. 354230, lot No. 20524002) containing a final concentration of 200 ng/mL of bFGF (Novoprotein, Shanghai, China, Cat. No. C044) was prepared on ice and injected into the inguinal region, where it solidified at body temperature to form a single gel plug in situ. Mice received either a unilateral injection (300 μL per mouse) or bilateral injections (300 μL per side, 600 μL in total per mouse), depending on the amount of iFA tissue required for downstream analyses. At designated time points post-injection, mice were euthanized, the inguinal skin was incised, and the iFA was carefully dissected from the surrounding connective tissue using a surgical scalpel. The harvested tissues were rinsed in sterile saline, and any residual connective tissue was gently removed. iFA harvested at day 14 was used for histological, ultrastructural, and cell isolation experiments, whereas tissues collected at days 3 and 7 were used for time-course histological and qPCR analyses. A high-growth-factor control group was established using Matrigel containing 800 ng/mL of bFGF under identical experimental conditions.

2.3. Fascia-Oriented Cell Migration Assay

Sterile medical-grade silicone tubes (1 cm in length) were filled with 20 μL of low-growth-factor Matrigel containing 200 ng/mL of bFGF and allowed to solidify. Following anesthesia, the inguinal skin of each mouse was incised, and the silicone tube was implanted subcutaneously with both ends positioned in direct contact with the fascia. The tube openings were sutured to the adjacent fascia to secure the tube and minimize displacement during normal animal activity, forming a directional migration conduit. After 14 days, samples were collected and rinsed in saline, and the fibrous capsule surrounding the tube was carefully removed for further analysis.

2.4. Histological Staining

Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, followed by trimming, dehydration, clearing, paraffin infiltration, and embedding. Paraffin blocks were sectioned at 6 μm thickness, dried, deparaffinized, and rehydrated. Adjacent sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Stained sections were mounted with neutral resin and imaged using a light microscope. To quantitatively evaluate the fibrotic response, the thickness of the fibrotic capsule or dense collagen deposition layer was measured using ImageJ software (ImageJ 1.48v, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). Capsule thickness was defined as the perpendicular distance from the surface of the implanted material or Matrigel interface to the outer boundary of the collagen-rich fibrotic layer. For each section, measurements were obtained from five randomly selected fields, and the average of the five measurements was used as the value for that section in statistical analysis.

2.5. Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR)

iFA tissues were collected on days 3, 7, and 14 post-injection for qPCR analysis. Total RNA was extracted from the harvested tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the isolated RNA were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Equal amounts of total RNA were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Kusatsu, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on a real-time PCR system (CFX Connect, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The mouse-specific primer sequences used in this study were as follows: CD11b F: GACTGGAGGCAACCATGGAA, R: GCCTGCGTGTGTTGTTCTTT; CD206 F: GTCAGAACAGACTGCGTGGA, R: AGGGATCGCCTGTTTTCCAG; GAPDH F: GAAGGTCGGTGTGAACGGAT; R: ACTGTGCCGTTGAATTTGCC. The amplification efficiency of all primer pairs was verified to be between 90% and 110%, and a single peak in the melting curve analysis confirmed the specificity of the PCR products.

2.6. Immunofluorescence Staining

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by heat-mediated antigen retrieval and blocking with 5% BSA. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD34 (Abclonal, Woburn, MA, USA, Cat. No. A13929,1:200), PDGFRα (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, USA, Cat. No. sc-398206,1:200), and F4/80 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK, ab300421,1:200). After PBS washes, sections were incubated at room temperature in the dark with secondary antibodies: CoraLite 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, Cat. No. SA00013-2,1:200) or CoraLite 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, Cat. No. SA00013-3,1:200). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min, and multi-channel fluorescence images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany).

2.7. Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Tissues were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 48 h and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide at 4 °C for 2 h. Samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (10 min each), infiltrated, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were prepared and stained sequentially with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Images were obtained using a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi High-Tech, Tokyo, Japan).

2.8. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

Tissues were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 48 h and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide at 4 °C for 2 h. Samples were dehydrated through graded ethanol, then immersed in isoamyl acetate for 15 min as the transitional solvent for critical point drying. Critical point drying was performed using liquid CO2, followed by ion sputter coating with gold. Samples were imaged using a Hitachi Regulus 8100 (Hitachi High-Tech, Tokyo, Japan) cold field-emission scanning electron microscope.

2.9. iFA-Derived Cell Culture

The iFA tissue was minced into small fragments, mixed with Matrigel, and cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 50 ng/mL of bFGF, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. After 7 days of culture, when cells had grown to confluence in the 24-well plate, the Matrigel blocks were removed to obtain cells derived from iFA.

2.10. Establishment of Full-Thickness Skin Wound Model and Wound Healing Assessment

Mice were anesthetized by inhalation of isoflurane. A 4 mm diameter circular area was marked on the dorsal skin using a punch biopsy instrument under even vertical pressure. The full-thickness skin within the marked area was then sharply excised with scissors, exposing the underlying dorsal muscle. A silicone splint was sutured around the wound to maintain tension, and the wound was covered with an occlusive dressing to maintain a sterile environment.

To investigate the effect of iFA-derived cells on wound healing, mice were randomly divided into 3 groups (n = 3 per group): no treatment (Control), GelMA alone, and GelMA loaded with iFA-derived cells (GelMA+iFA). For the GelMA+iFA group, an autologous strategy was used: each mouse first received Matrigel injection as described in Section 2.2; 14 days later, the iFA plug was harvested from the same animal, and iFA-derived cells were isolated and expanded as described in Section 2.9. After establishment of the full-thickness skin wound, interventions were performed on day 3. This time point was selected because day 3 represents a critical transition from the inflammatory phase toward the proliferative phase of wound healing, during which the wound microenvironment becomes more permissive for cell migration, survival, and tissue regeneration. Briefly, 2 × 106 autologous iFA-derived cells were mixed with 10% GelMA (EFL, Hangzhou, China) and 0.2% LAP to prepare a 20 μL suspension, which was applied to the wound and cross-linked by exposure to UV light (405 nm, 25 mW/cm2) for 10 s.

Wound healing was monitored and photographed with a digital camera on days 0, 3, 6, 8, and 10 after wounding. Wound areas were quantified using ImageJ software by measuring the area inside the inner edge of the silicone splint, and the wound healing rate was calculated accordingly.

2.11. Statistical Analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), and image processing was performed using ImageJ and Adobe Photoshop CC (version 20.0; Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). For all in vivo experiments, individual animals served as the experimental unit, and n refers to the number of independent animals (biological replicates). Technical replicates (e.g., multiple microscopic fields from the same section) were averaged before statistical analysis and were not treated as independent samples. No animals or samples were excluded from the analyses. Comparisons among multiple groups with a single factor (capsule thickness) were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Gene expression data obtained from independent animals at different time points were analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Longitudinal wound closure data, consisting of repeated measurements from the same animals over time, were analyzed using a mixed-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood, REML) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test to account for within-subject correlation; day 0 was excluded from this analysis because wound closure was 0% by definition. The assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated before statistical analysis. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. The specific statistical method used for each experiment is indicated in the corresponding figure legend.

3. Results

3.1. Construction of the iFA and Histological Characterization

We injected Matrigel matrix containing 200 ng/mL bFGF into the subcutaneous tissue of the inguinal region in female C57BL/6 mice to establish an in situ induced fascial analogue (iFA). Macroscopically, the iFA appeared as a pale yellow, gel-like tissue that could be easily and completely excised during surgery (Figure 1A). H&E staining of serial sections containing skin, iFA, and abdominal musculature revealed that the Matrigel matrix had not yet contacted the fascia on day 3 post-injection; however, by day 14, it was closely apposed to both the superficial and deep fascial layers, suggesting a potential cellular origin for the iFA (Figure 1B).

Histological analysis showed that by day 14, the iFA exhibited a dense and directionally organized collagen matrix accompanied by the infiltration of uniformly elongated spindle-shaped cells, morphologically resembling the native superficial fascia. In contrast, the high-dose Matrigel group triggered massive, disordered cellular infiltration and excessive, irregular collagen deposition throughout the matrix. Meanwhile, the foreign body fibrous capsule induced by silicone implants displayed dense, parallel collagen bundles encapsulating abundant immune cells, representing a structurally distinct pattern (Figure 1C). To quantitatively assess the fibrotic response, we measured the capsule thickness across different groups (Figure 1D). The capsule thickness in the iFA group was highly comparable to that of the native fascia group. Conversely, both the high-dose Matrigel and Silicone developed significantly thickened fibrotic capsules, demonstrating prominent fibrotic tissue remodeling.

Additionally, qPCR analysis indicated a significant temporal decrease in CD11b expression within the iFA, while the M2-associated marker CD206 was markedly upregulated (Figure 1E). These findings indicate a temporal shift in macrophage-associated marker expression within the iFA toward a CD206+ pro-reparative phenotype. Collectively, these results demonstrate that low-growth-factor Matrigel plugs possess favorable biocompatibility and are capable of generating fascia-like tissue in situ, supporting their potential for further investigation.

3.2. Ultrastructural Analysis of the iFA

To further validate the structural similarity between the iFA and native fascia, we examined their ultrastructural features using electron microscopy. TEM revealed that the iFA exhibited typical interstitial tissue characteristics, including numerous well-spread TCs displaying their signature long telopodes in close contact with surrounding collagen fibers. Macrophages with M2-associated morphology were also observed within the matrix, collectively forming a stable stromal niche alongside the TCs. This cellular composition closely mirrors that of natural superficial fascia (Figure 2A). Notably, TCs within the iFA displayed exceptionally long telopodes and adhered tightly to undigested Matrigel matrix, a morphological feature consistent with TCs observed at the fascia–muscle interface (Figure 2B). SEM further illustrated the three-dimensional network of the iFA, revealing a dense collagenous scaffold comparable to that of superficial fascia. At higher magnification, mechanosensitive sheet-like TCs were widely distributed and attached to the collagen fiber network, forming complex cell–matrix interaction interfaces (Figure 2C). These ultrastructural observations demonstrate that the iFA exhibits several nanoscale features comparable to those observed in native fascia, including collagen organization and stromal cell–matrix interactions.

3.3. Directed Fascial Migration Assay Supports the Contribution of Fascia-Derived TCs to iFA Formation

To investigate the contribution and phenotype of fascia-associated cells within the iFA, we adapted the Directed In Vivo Angiogenesis Assay (DIVAA) and established a modified fascial-directed migration model [10]. The schematic illustrates how the device employs a physical barrier to restrict lateral cell infiltration, thereby favoring recruitment of cells from the adjacent fascial region through the silicone tube openings into the Matrigel matrix (Figure 3B).

The implanted silicone tube remained well secured in the subcutaneous space without dislodgement or infection. At 14 days post-implantation, the Matrigel within the tube appeared yellowish, and a continuous stream of cells from both fascial layers was observed migrating through the tube openings into the matrix, forming interconnected cellular migration bands (Figure 3A,C). Histological staining revealed abundant deposition of newly formed collagen matrix within the migration band, accompanied by extensive cellular and vascular infiltration (Figure 3D,E). Double-staining immunofluorescence further confirmed that the stromal cells migrating into the Matrigel matrix co-expressed CD34 and PDGFRα (Figure 3F). These findings provide evidence that fascia-associated cells contribute substantially to cellular recruitment into the iFA and exhibit molecular and morphological characteristics consistent with TCs.

3.4. In Vitro 3D Culture Evaluates Sustained Migratory Activity of iFA-Derived Cells

To evaluate the potential applications of iFA-derived cells, we minced the iFA harvested at day 14 post-injection and embedded it in fresh Matrigel for 3D in vitro culture (Figure 4A). Continuous observation showed that the iFA fragments did not exhibit organoid-like cystic expansion or epithelial growth; instead, they displayed prominent outward stromal cell migration. From day 1 to day 8, large numbers of cells migrated outward from the edges of the tissue fragments, progressively forming a radial cellular network (Figure 4B). High-magnification imaging revealed two major morphological populations among the migrating cells: spindle-shaped TCs with elongated telopodes and round macrophages (Figure 4C). Immunofluorescence staining on day 8 identified CD34+ TCs and F4/80+ macrophages among the outgrown cells (Figure 4D). These findings indicate that the iFA can maintain its cellular viability and migratory capacity in vitro while preserving inherent cellular heterogeneity, demonstrating potential for autologous tissue repair. This suggests that iFA provides a feasible in vivo enrichment strategy and a potential source for the isolation of functional fascial cells.

3.5. In Vivo Evaluation of iFA-Derived Cells in Wound Healing

To evaluate the regenerative potential of iFA-derived cells in tissue regeneration, we established a full-thickness dorsal skin wound model in mice. Following wound creation, GelMA hydrogel alone or GelMA loaded with iFA-derived cells (GelMA+iFA) was applied on day 3 post-injury. Macroscopic observation revealed that wounds treated with GelMA+iFA exhibited accelerated closure compared to both the Control and GelMA-only groups (Figure 5A). Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the GelMA+iFA group achieved significantly higher wound closure rates on day 6 and day 8 post-wounding, with nearly complete healing observed by day 10 (Figure 5B).

Histological examination via H&E staining on day 10 confirmed enhanced re-epithelialization in the GelMA+iFA group, as evidenced by the restoration of a continuous epidermal layer and reduced wound gap compared to the Control and GelMA groups (Figure 5C). These results provide preliminary in vivo evidence that iFA-derived cells, enriched in TCs and macrophages exhibiting M2-associated characteristics, can promote wound closure and re-epithelialization in this acute wound model.

4. Discussion

Fascia, as the pivotal hub of the soft-tissue system, is regarded as a cellular reservoir for tissue regeneration due to its abundance of progenitor cells and stem cells [11]. In this study, we modified the classical Matrigel plug assay by using a low-growth-factor formulation. We successfully created an in situ induced fascial analogue called iFA, which closely mimics the structural organization and selected cellular features of native fascia. This achievement provides a new experimental model and methodological platform for advancing fascia biology and soft-tissue regeneration research.

The main reason why iFA develops an organized collagen architecture rather than a foreign body fibrotic capsule lies in the precise regulation of the “inflammation-remodelling” balance within the low-growth-factor microenvironment. Conventional biomaterial implantation often triggers a strong acute inflammatory response, during which M1 macrophages release pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6. This causes fibroblasts to differentiate into myofibroblasts, resulting in excessive collagen deposition and the formation of dense scar tissue to encapsulate the implant [12,13]. In contrast, the low-dose growth-factor Matrigel used in this study created a minimally stimulatory environment. Our qPCR results showed that, as iFA matured, the immune niche rapidly shifted toward a phenotype associated with increased expression of the M2-associated marker CD206. Macrophages with reparative phenotypes have been reported to secrete anti-inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-10) to suppress pathological fibrosis and also produce matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) that enable dynamic ECM remodeling [14,15]. This optimized immune microenvironment may facilitate fascia-associated stromal cell recruitment into the matrix through a normal migratory process rather than a defensive encapsulation response, ultimately forming a connective-tissue network that closely resembles natural fascia.

A particularly notable finding of this work is the enrichment of CD34+/PDGFRα+ cells within iFA, which ultrastructurally manifest as TCs with long processes. TCs are widely distributed in stromal tissues such as the heart, skin, and fascia [16,17]. Through their extensive telopode networks, TCs establish physical interactions with extracellular matrix components and neighboring cells, suggesting potential roles in intercellular communication and tissue homeostasis. Interestingly, the spatial organization of fascial connective tissues has been discussed in relation to various anatomical communication networks, including those described in traditional medicine [18,19]. However, the biological significance and potential relationship between TC networks and these macroscopic tissue communication concepts remain largely unexplored [20]. The iFA model established in this study may provide a valuable platform for future investigations into the structural organization and long-range communication mechanisms of connective tissues.

For a long time, fascia research has been constrained by sampling difficulties, whether in humans or experimental animals. iFA offers a novel “non-destructive harvesting” strategy: through minimally invasive injection and retrieval, it’s possible to obtain an intact, viable, and autologous fascia-like tissue without damaging native fascia. To validate the regenerative potential of iFA, we established a full-thickness skin wound model and demonstrated that iFA-derived cells significantly accelerated wound closure and promoted re-epithelialization when delivered via GelMA hydrogel. This finding suggests that iFA-derived cells may serve as a potential cellular source for wound coverage and soft tissue repair. The enhanced healing efficacy is likely attributed to the synergistic action of enriched TCs and macrophages exhibiting M2-associated characteristics within the iFA cell population, which have been reported to promote angiogenesis, modulate inflammation, and facilitate ECM remodeling [21]. Notably, the GelMA hydrogel served as an ideal vehicle for cell delivery, providing structural support while allowing for efficient cell migration and paracrine signaling. In vitro, our outgrowth experiments further confirmed the robust migratory activity of iFA-derived cells, supporting their capacity to populate wound beds and establish a pro-regenerative niche. We propose that iFA could be further developed as a seed tissue for creating true “fascia organoids” by optimizing 3D culture conditions—such as by applying mechanical stimulation or specific cytokine cues [22]. Such organoids would retain the complexity of the in vivo microenvironment and serve as an ideal model for antifibrotic drug screening and mechanistic studies of fascia-related disorders [23].

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although iFA exhibited structural and cellular similarities to native fascia, its biomechanical properties, including tensile strength and elasticity, remain to be determined. Second, the long-term remodeling process of the Matrigel-based scaffold requires further investigation to clarify its degradation and replacement by endogenous extracellular matrix. Third, although iFA-derived cells exhibited regenerative potential, the individual contributions of CD34+/PDGFRα+ TCs and macrophages exhibiting M2-associated characteristics, as well as their specific paracrine mechanisms, remain to be elucidated. Fourth, although the selected bFGF concentrations generated distinct tissue responses, systematic dose–response studies including growth-factor-free Matrigel controls are required to clarify the specific contribution of exogenous bFGF. Finally, the tumor-derived origin of Matrigel limits direct clinical translation, highlighting the need for clinically applicable biomaterials that reproduce the favorable microenvironment required for fascial cell recruitment.

5. Conclusions

In conclusion, this study presents an in situ induction method that successfully produces a fascia-like tissue enriched with CD34+/PDGFRα+ TCs and demonstrates its regenerative potential in accelerating wound healing. By establishing a non-destructive harvesting strategy coupled with functional validation in vivo, this work bridges the gap between fascial biology and regenerative tissue engineering research. The iFA not only serves as a biomimetic tissue model but also functions as a potential ‘living patch’ for future soft tissue repair applications, providing a solid experimental foundation for investigating the structural organization and communication networks of connective tissues while offering an experimental platform for future regenerative studies.
