Abstract
Mediumship is a complex human phenomenon that can be investigated by cognitive psychiatry and consciousness
neuroscience without theological assumptions or psychopathological reductionism. This study aimed to formulate and
theoretically substantiate a neuroconnectomic, genetic, and psychofunctional hypothesis of mediumship as a network
phenotype characterized by greater interhemispheric integration and lower functional segregation of cognitive-affective
processes, and to establish formal guidelines for a comparative multimodal experimental protocol to test it empirically.
This theoretical-heuristic essay presents a narrative synthesis of genomic, neurophysiological, psychometric, and
preclinical evidence and delineates a prospective case–control protocol. The historical hypothesis of greater
interhemispheric integration was compared with a 2025 exome study that identified recurrent rare variants in candidate
genes, including EPHB6 and GPRIN2, without demonstrating causality or a genotype–connectome association. Studies
published from 2023 to 2026 indicate state-dependent changes in spectral power and network topology during
mediumistic trance and hypnosis, heterogeneous neural correlates across trance practices, metric-dependent sex
differences in connectivity, and no replicable stable association between trait absorption and brain connectivity.
Preclinical evidence supports a role for EphB6/EphrinB2 in cortical neuronal spacing but cannot be extrapolated to
human connectivity or mediumship. The model is biologically plausible and empirically testable, but remains
hypothetical. Independent replication requires prespecified phenotyping, equivalent active controls, confirmatory
genotyping, DTI, resting-state fMRI, high-density EEG, psychometrics, and rigorous control of ancestry, brain size,
motion, task, and multiple comparisons.
Keywords: Spirituality; Hypnosis; Electroencephalography; Connectome; Neuroimaging.

1. Introduction
Mediumship constitutes a complex human phenomenon that can be investigated scientifically through clinical,
neuropsychological, neurophysiological, neuroanatomical and genetic approaches, without the epistemological necessity of
assuming theological positions a priori or of prematurely reducing it to psychopathology, dissociation or simulation. Within the
fields of the Neuroscience of Consciousness and Cognitive Psychiatry, the investigation of mediumship experiences seeks to
identify whether there are neurobiological and functional network peculiarities that differentiate individuals with highly
developed mediumship from the general population.
Previous functional studies suggest state-dependent alterations during psychography. In a preliminary study using
single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) in ten psychographers, the more experienced participants showed
reduced regional cerebral blood flow in areas related to monitoring, language and motor control during writing in trance, despite
preservation of textual complexity (Peres et al., 2012). The small sample, absence of an external control group and subgroup
analyses require exploratory interpretation. The finding is compatible with a state-dependent functional reorganization, but does
not establish its mechanism, origin or specificity for mediumship.
On 19 June 2015, in a theoretical formulation posted on a personal webpage, Caixeta proposed that individuals with
high mediumship capacity could exhibit lower functional segregation and greater integration between systems, particularly
between the cerebral hemispheres. The proposition was based on clinical observations and on an exploratory
electroencephalographic record from the 1990s with about ten mediums from the Federação Espírita do Estado de Goiás (FEGO),
described as greater beta activity on the right and theta on the left. Because the primary data, protocol and analysis of this EEG
are not available in a peer-reviewed publication, this material should be regarded as a historical antecedent of the hypothesis, not
as confirmatory evidence.

In molecular genomics, Gattaz et al. (2025) compared 54 mediums to 53 first-degree relatives who did not report
mediumship and examined an additional unmatched cohort of 12 mediums. After exome filtering, the authors retained 15,669
variants absent in the family controls, distributed across 7,269 genes, and highlighted 33 genes with variants in at least one-third
of the mediums. This procedure identifies recurrent candidates but does not, by itself, equate to a conventional genetic association
nor demonstrate causality. The additional cohort allows checking internal recurrence, but does not constitute an independent
case–control replication.
Among the candidates, EPHB6 had variants in 27 of 54 mediums (50%) and in 10 of 12 participants of the additional
cohort (83.3%); the annotation used by the authors indicated higher expression in the cerebral cortex and functions in cell
adhesion and migration. GPRIN2 was included among the genes with variants in at least one-third of the sample and has a
functional annotation related to neurite outgrowth (National Center for Biotechnology Information, 2025). However, the variants
were not functionally validated, there was no association with specific mediumship modalities, and much of the signal fell on
immunological genes, mucins and olfactory receptors—families subject to high variability and nonspecific findings.
The central scientific problem is to determine whether the presence of rare variants in neurodevelopmental and neuralarchitecture genes (EPHB6 and GPRIN2) actually translates into a brain connectivity pattern characterized by greater
interhemispheric integration and reduced network segregation in mediums. The identified knowledge gap is experimental and
methodological: the genetic study of Gattaz et al. (2025) was confined to exome sequencing and did not perform direct
neuroanatomical or neurofunctional investigations. The work did not carry out diffusion tensor imaging (DTI/tractography),
resting-state fMRI, corpus callosum volumetry or graph analysis of brain networks in the studied mediums. Consequently, there
is no empirical demonstration that carriers of variants in EPHB6 and GPRIN2 have higher density of transcallosal fibers,
differences in operationalized measures of sensory filtering, or greater interhemispheric homotopic connectivity.
The relevance of this work lies in converting a clinical proposition into measurable and falsifiable hypotheses,
distinguishing levels of evidence. The 2015 publication documents the historical antecedence of the formulation, while the 2025
exome provides exploratory candidates; this temporal sequence does not prove causal independence nor validate the model. The
scientific contribution consists of proposing direct connectomic tests and criteria that will allow confirmation, refutation or
reformulation of the hypothesis without presuming a theological or psychopathological explanation a priori (Lakatos & Marconi,
2003).
This study aimed to formulate and theoretically substantiate a neuroconnectomic, genetic, and psychofunctional
hypothesis of mediumship as a network phenotype characterized by greater interhemispheric integration and lower functional
segregation of cognitive-affective processes, and to establish formal guidelines for a comparative multimodal experimental
protocol to test it empirically.
The specific objectives were to:
1. Conceptually articulate Caixeta's (2015) interhemispheric integration hypothesis with the findings of variants in the
neurodevelopment genes EPHB6 and GPRIN2 mapped by Gattaz et al. (2025).
2. Examine cognitive absorption, hypnotic responsiveness, nonpathological dissociation, openness to experience and
interoception as psychofunctional dimensions potentially related to brain network dynamics.
3. Outline an observational case–control protocol that combines confirmatory genotyping, diffusion tractography (DTI),
resting-state fMRI, high-density quantitative EEG and standardized psychometric scales, with adjustment for
intracranial volume, total brain volume and other prespecified confounders.

2. Methodology
2.1 Study nature and design
This study is a theoretical-heuristic essay (Abraham, 1970), with a narrative literature synthesis (Ogassawara et al.,
2025; Fernandes, Vieira & Castelhano, 2023), not a systematic review nor an original empirical study, but using a qualitative
investigation (Risemberg et al., 2026a; Risemberg et al., 2026b; Pereira et al., 2018). In its prospective dimension, it delineates
an analytical, cross-sectional, case–control observational protocol. Evidence is organized according to its proximity to the
hypothesis—direct, adjacent or preclinical—and limitations are made explicit to avoid undue causal inferences.
The hypothetical-deductive method of Karl Popper and Mário Bunge (cited in Lakatos & Marconi, 2003) is adopted.
The investigation starts from a theoretical problem and knowledge gaps—specifically, whether the rare variants mapped in
neurodevelopment genes in mediums translate into alterations in the architecture of brain networks. From this premise,
conjectures (hypotheses) are formulated that deduce particular neuroanatomical and neurofunctional predictions amenable to
empirical testing and attempted falsification by neuroimaging and neurophysiology. The comparative method is used (to verify
symmetries and divergences among mediums, their first-degree relatives and unrelated controls) and the statistical method (for
quantitative reduction and inferential analysis of neurophysiological, genomic and psychometric data).
2.2 Target population and sampling
The target population comprises adults who report recurrent mediumship experiences and practice mediumship in
established institutions, their first-degree blood relatives who do not meet the operational criterion for mediumship, and healthy
unrelated population controls.
Non-probabilistic purposive sampling in three groups: (1) Case Group (G1), experienced mediums with active practice
for more than five years; (2) Family Control Group (G2), first-degree relatives who do not meet the operational criterion for
mediumship; and (3) Population Control Group (G3), unrelated controls without personal or family history of mediumship
experiences according to a standardized instrument. G1 and G3 will be matched by sex, age, education level, socioeconomic
status and religiosity. Intracranial volume and total brain volume will be treated primarily as covariates, with sensitivity
allometric analyses, rather than rigid matching. Sample size will be defined by prespecified primary outcomes, with separate
calculations for neuroimaging, EEG and rare-variant genetic analyses.
2.3 Eligibility criteria
Inclusion criteria: age between 18 and 65 years; regular mediumship practice for more than five years and a score above
a prespecified threshold on a psychometric instrument with validity evidence for G1. For G2, first-degree kinship with a G1
participant and absence of personal mediumship experience or practice according to the same operational criterion. For G3,
absence of personal mediumship experience and of first-degree family history, in addition to the planned matching.
Exclusion criteria: previous or current diagnosis of primary psychotic disorder or bipolar disorder; structural
neurological disease; epilepsy; traumatic brain injury with loss of consciousness; contraindications to 3-tesla magnetic resonance
imaging, such as pacemaker, ferromagnetic implant or severe claustrophobia; and ongoing use of psychotropic medications or
psychoactive substances within a time window previously defined in the protocol.
2.4 Study variables and controls
According to the systematization of Lakatos and Marconi (2003) on the causal structure of variables:

head motion, signal quality, substance use and duration of practice. Adjustment for brain size will be accompanied by sensitivity
allometric analysis, since a single linear correction may not eliminate all scaling effects.
Moderating variables (M): sex recorded at birth and self-reported gender, absorption, openness to experience, hypnotic
responsiveness, nonpathological dissociation and interoception. These dimensions will be measured separately, will not be
treated as equivalent to mediumship, and will only be modeled as moderators when an interaction hypothesis is prespecified.

3. Results and Discussion
3.1 Results of the narrative synthesis
This article does not present results of the proposed experimental protocol. The section synthesizes available evidence,
including genomic, neurophysiological and psychometric studies, and differentiates findings directly related to mediumship from
adjacent evidence regarding hypnosis, absorption and neurodevelopment. Because the search was not systematic and there was
no meta-analysis, the synthesis does not allow estimation of an aggregate effect nor exclusion of publication bias.
In the exome sequencing of Gattaz et al. (2025), 33 genes were selected because they had rare potentially functional
variants in at least 18 of the 54 mediums and not in the respective family controls. Pathway analysis found enrichment mainly
in immune-related pathways. The filtering criteria generated an exploratory candidate list; there was no per-gene genomic
association test that would authorize classifying EPHB6 or GPRIN2 as established mediumship genes. None of the 7,269 genes
analyzed associated significantly with specific mediumship modalities.
EPHB6 presented variants in 27 of 54 mediums (50%), in the two monozygotic twins participating, and in 10 of 12
mediums of the additional unmatched cohort (83.3%). GPRIN2 was part of the group of genes with variants in at least one-third
of the sample. In a murine model, manipulation of EphB6/EphrinB2 altered neuronal soma spacing in the developing cortex (He
et al., 2023), offering indirect neurodevelopmental plausibility. This preclinical result does not demonstrate effects on longdistance connectivity, interhemispheric integration or human behavior.
In the neurophysiological domain, the exploratory unpublished recording from the 1990s remains only a historical
antecedent. In peer-reviewed evidence, Peres et al. (2012) observed perfusion differences during psychography in a sample of
ten participants. More recently, Plácido et al. (2026) identified changes in topological indices in delta, theta and alpha during a
trance task in 14 female mediums compared with 14 controls in a prayer task; Pinto et al. (2026), with nine participants per
group, found spectral differences in theta, alpha, beta1 and beta2 during writing in trance versus simulated writing. Small samples
and non-equivalent control conditions limit generalization and do not demonstrate a specific neural signature.
Across the three peer-reviewed studies directly examining mediumistic performance, the observed neural measures
were task- and modality-dependent. Peres et al. (2012) reported lower regional cerebral blood flow in experienced
psychographers during trance writing, whereas Plácido et al. (2026) identified changes in graph metrics in delta, theta, and alpha,
and Pinto et al. (2026) observed spectral-power differences in theta, alpha, beta1, and beta2. These studies used different

The adjacent literature on hypnosis and absorption separated state-related network reconfiguration from stable
individual traits. Niedernhuber et al. (2024) found that interhemispheric frontoparietal connectivity, theta power, and network
centrality distinguished hypnotic states from control conditions in 30 experienced practitioners. By contrast, Girn et al. (2023)
found that exploratory associations between trait absorption and brain measures did not replicate across independent samples
and that global modularity was not associated with absorption. Within this synthesis, the more consistent signal concerns transient
changes during induced states rather than a validated resting-state biomarker of absorption.
Gosseries et al. (2024) synthesized trance research across phenomenological and neuroimaging methods and described
recurring changes in attention, agency, body perception, and absorption alongside heterogeneous EEG, fMRI, and SPECT
findings. The review did not identify a unitary neural signature shared by all trance practices. Instead, the reported correlates
varied with induction procedure, practitioner expertise, cultural setting, and recording method, highlighting these variables as
potential sources of between-study heterogeneity.
The studies addressing sex-related differences also yielded metric-specific rather than global results. Ingalhalikar et al.
(2014) reported relatively greater interhemispheric structural connectivity in women, but Martínez et al. (2017) showed that brain
size accounted for part of the apparent sex effect. Kang, Adamson and Yoon (2025), after controlling for brain size in 111 healthy
young adults, found regional differences across structural and functional connections without a uniform sex-specific connectomic
pattern. Rudski et al. (2004) reported only a small mean sex difference in hypnotizability, while Monteiro de Barros et al. (2025)
found epidemiological associations between some spiritual experiences and female sex. Together, these findings identify sex,
gender, brain size, and sociocultural context as covariates or moderators, not proxies for mediumship.
The evidence map therefore yielded four distinct levels: one historical unpublished observation; four peer-reviewed
studies directly involving mediumship or mediumistic tasks (Peres et al., 2012; Gattaz et al., 2025; Plácido et al., 2026; Pinto et
al., 2026); human evidence adjacent to the hypothesis from hypnosis, absorption, trance, and normative connectomics; and one
preclinical study of cortical organization (He et al., 2023). Convergence was observed only at the broad level of state-dependent
functional reorganization and the need to model interindividual variability. No cited study jointly measured mediumship
phenotype, rare variants, structural connectivity, resting-state functional connectivity, task EEG, and psychometric traits in the
same sample.

3.2 Discussion
The central problem is to verify whether the mediumship phenotype is associated with differences in interhemispheric
integration and functional segregation, and whether this profile relates to variants in EPHB6 and GPRIN2 and to dimensions
such as absorption and hypnotic responsiveness. The results of Gattaz et al. (2025) should be read as candidate generation:
variants were selected by rarity filters, absence in family controls and recurrence among cases, without demonstration of
genotype–connectome association or functional effect of the variants. There is therefore no evidence of callosal hypertrophy,
increased synapses or greater structural connectivity attributable to these genes.
The formulation disclosed by Caixeta in 2015 predates the genomic study of 2025 and documents the historical origin
of the hypothesis. This antecedence reduces the possibility that the proposition was created solely to accommodate the exome,
but does not demonstrate its correctness and does not replace prospective validation. The clinical observation, the unpublished

In interpreting the exome, it is necessary to avoid retrospective selection only of the genes most compatible with the
hypothesis. Many candidates belong to highly variable families such as HLA, mucins and olfactory receptors. EPHB6 and
GPRIN2 merit investigation because of their neurobiological annotation, but their presence on the list does not demonstrate an
etiological “core.” Specific variants must be confirmed by orthogonal sequencing, annotated for ancestry and molecular effect,
and tested in independent cohorts before any mechanistic inference.
Recent experimental evidence makes the hypothesis regarding EPHB6 more specific, but not more conclusive. He et
al. (2023) showed in developing murine cortex that overexpression of EphB6 or EphrinB2 promoted clustering of neuronal
somata and that coexpression attenuated this phenotype, suggesting participation of these molecules in cortical cell spacing. The
study offers a plausible bridge between Eph/ephrin signaling and cortical spatial organization, but did not evaluate human
variants, synapses, long-distance connectivity, the corpus callosum or mediumship. Thus, EPHB6 should remain a mechanistic
candidate to be tested, not an established explanation of the phenotype.
The literature on sex differences in the connectome provides context, not confirmation of the model. Ingalhalikar et al.
(2014) reported relatively greater interhemispheric connectivity in women, while Martínez et al. (2017) showed that part of these
differences is reduced after adjustment for brain size. In parallel, Rudski et al. (2004) observed a mean difference in
hypnotizability by sex and Monteiro de Barros et al. (2025) reported higher frequency of some spiritual experiences in women.
These results do not authorize inferring a “female brain” of mediumship. Sex, gender, brain volume, age and sociocultural context
should be modeled separately as prespecified covariates or moderators.
Recent multimodal data reinforce the need to abandon global dichotomies. Kang, Adamson and Yoon (2025) examined
structural and functional connectivity in 111 healthy young adults, controlling for brain size, and observed sex differences
distributed across intra- and interhemispheric connections, without a global and uniform superiority of one pattern over the other.
Women showed greater connectivity in specific parcels of the left hemisphere, including language, somatomotor and defaultmode networks, but also lower functional connectivity in symmetrically distributed connections. The result confirms that sex
effects are regional, dependent on the metric and the sample, and therefore cannot be used as substitutes for individual
connectome measurement nor as an indirect explanation of mediumship.
Two 2026 studies bring the debate directly closer to brain dynamics during mediumistic trance. Plácido et al. (2026)
analyzed EEG and graph theory in 14 female mediums and 14 controls and observed topological changes in the delta, theta and
alpha bands in the medium group; however, the contrast between trance and prayer did not equate the tasks between groups.
Pinto et al. (2026), with nine participants per group, found power differences in theta, alpha, beta1 and beta2 between writing in
trance and simulated writing, without intergroup differences in overall textual quality. Together, the studies support statedependent electrophysiological alterations in small samples, but do not demonstrate a universal signature, structural
interhemispheric integration or a relationship with EPHB6/GPRIN2.
The review by Gosseries et al. (2024) offers a broader framing for these results by showing that trance states from
different traditions share changes in attention, bodily perception, agency and absorption, but present heterogeneous brain
correlates across EEG, fMRI and SPECT. The authors highlight that induction technique, practitioner experience, cultural context
and recording method modify the observed patterns. This synthesis supports the neurophenomenological and multimodal
investigation proposed in this essay, while advising against searching for a single neural signature of trance or automatically
equating hypnosis, psychography and other spiritual practices.
Recent literature on hypnosis also recommends separating state effects from trait characteristics. Niedernhuber et al.
(2024) observed in 30 experienced practitioners that interhemispheric frontoparietal connectivity, theta power and network

In contrast, Girn et al. (2023) did not find a replicable association between trait absorption and brain structure or resting-state

Taken together, the seven recent studies incorporated here outline a coherent hierarchy of hypotheses without forming
a demonstrated causal chain. He et al. (2023) provide preclinical plausibility for EphB6 participation in cortical organization;
Girn et al. (2023) show that trait absorption lacks a demonstrated stable brain correlate; Gosseries et al. (2024) document the
phenomenological and neural heterogeneity of trance states; Niedernhuber et al. (2024) identify state-dependent interhemispheric
reorganization under hypnosis; Kang, Adamson and Yoon (2025) show that sex differences in connectivity are regional and
metric-dependent; and Plácido et al. (2026) and Pinto et al. (2026) document electrophysiological changes during mediumship
tasks. This combination supports a study design that separates between-individual effects, within-individual state effects, practice
experience, and genotype, using repeated sessions, equivalent active controls, and pre-specified outcomes. The current
convergence strengthens the experimental logic of the model, not a biological conclusion about mediumship.
The psychofunctional dimension should be operationalized by measurable constructs, not by labels based on femininity.
Such terms conflate sex, gender, and cognitive style, favor essentialist interpretations, and lack sufficient psychometric definition
in the manuscript. It is recommended to assess, separately, absorption, openness to experience, nonpathological dissociation,
interoception, empathy, and hypnotic responsiveness with validated instruments and pre-registered hypotheses. Individual
biographical observations should not be used as evidence of neurobiological association.
The model proposes to investigate mediumship as a possible network phenotype, that is, as a difference in the balance
between integration and segregation rather than as a function of an isolated brain region. The “reducing valve” metaphor may be
retained only as a historical device to formulate predictions about perceptual filtering and executive control. It should not be
presented as an established neurobiological mechanism nor as evidence of access to non-ordinary information.
The model has potential implications for consciousness science and for clinical differentiation between culturally
organized spiritual experiences and mental disorders. Its main limitations are the absence of neuroimaging in genotyped
participants, the lack of independent genetic replication, variant heterogeneity, small neurophysiological sample sizes, lack of
equivalence between some control tasks, and the possibility of confounding by expectation, practice, religiosity, and motion
artifacts. De-pathologization should not be presumed universally; it requires assessment of suffering, functionality, voluntary
control, and cultural context in each case.
The next step should be a confirmatory, multicenter, pre-registered study with three groups, an operational definition of
the phenotype, equivalent task controls, and blinded evaluators. The protocol should combine pre-specified and confirmed
variants in EPHB6/GPRIN2, DTI/transcallosal tractography, resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI), HD-EEG at rest and during tasks,
and validated scales of absorption and hypnotic responsiveness. Primary outcomes, power analysis, quality control, genetic
ancestry, intracranial volume, motion, and correction for multiple comparisons should be defined before data collection. The
central test will be to verify whether connectivity differences replicate across samples and associate with the phenotype after
controlling for confounders, rather than merely appearing in an exploratory comparison.

4. Conclusion
This theoretical essay articulated a connectomic hypothesis of mediumship to genomic, neurophysiological, and
psychofunctional findings. Recent literature makes the proposal more testable by documenting EEG and network topology

specific neural signature, or validation of ontological interpretations of the mediumship experience.
