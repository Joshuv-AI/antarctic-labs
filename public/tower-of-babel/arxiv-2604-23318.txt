Hidden States Know Where Reasoning Diverges: Credit Assignment via Span-Level Wasserstein Distance
Xinzhu Chen, Wei He, Huichuan Fan, Wenzhe Niu, Zhongxiang Sun, Xuanru Wang, Jiuchong Gao, Jinghua Hao, Renqing He, Weijie Yu

Abstract
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) performs coarse-grained credit assignment in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) by assigning the same advantage to all tokens in a rollout. Process reward models can provide finer-grained supervision, but they require step-level annotation or additional reward modeling. We show that hidden-state distributions contain a useful signal for local reasoning quality that can be extracted using only outcome-level correctness labels available in RLVR. Specifically, within each GRPO group, the Wasserstein distance between span-level hidden state distributions of correct and incorrect rollouts increases around regions where their local reasoning quality diverges. This association holds both across examples and within individual trajectories, suggesting that hidden-state distributional divergence can serve as a self-supervision signal for fine-grained credit assignment. We formalize this observation with a separation theorem showing that, under mild structural assumptions, post-divergence spans have larger Wasserstein distances than pre-divergence spans whenever the population-level distributional gap exceeds finite-sample noise. Motivated by this result, we propose S pan-level H idden state E nabled A dvantage R eweighting (SHEAR), which modifies GRPO by using span-level Wasserstein distances to scale token-level advantages, amplifying updates on tokens whose hidden states are more separated from the opposing group. The method requires no additional model and only minimal changes to the training pipeline. Experiments on five mathematical reasoning benchmarks and five code generation benchmarks show improvements over standard GRPO and strong performance relative to supervised process reward models, while requiring no additional annotation or reward model training.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) is a widely used paradigm for improving reasoning in large language models
26
;
38
, with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
24
being one of its most commonly adopted algorithms. In GRPO, the model samples multiple reasoning trajectories for each problem, receives outcome-level correctness feedback, and updates its policy based on the relative quality of these trajectories. A central challenge in this setting is credit assignment, that is, identifying which parts of a reasoning chain are responsible for success or failure. GRPO assigns the same advantage to every token in a rollout, leaving this finer-grained problem unresolved. This limitation may become more severe on the complicated tasks, which require more thinking and execution steps
16
. A single erroneous step can derail an otherwise sound trajectory, yet all tokens receive the same gradient signal regardless of their contribution to the final outcome. This is especially problematic in long-form mathematical reasoning
1
;
20
;
21
and code generation
10
;
12
;
17
, where chains routinely span hundreds of tokens.

The dominant approach to fine-grained credit assignment is the
process reward model
(PRM)
4
;
5
;
16
, which assigns rewards to individual reasoning steps. PRMs have shown clear advantages over outcome-level supervision, but they rely on costly step-level supervision. Training a PRM typically requires either human-annotated intermediate labels or an auxiliary reward model trained on such annotations, making the resulting credit signal dependent on the quality of the supervision.
This issue is particularly acute on complicated tasks, where the correctness of intermediate steps is often ambiguous even to experts
5
. These considerations motivate a natural question. Can we obtain process-level credit signals without step-level annotation or an additional reward model?

We approach this question from a representational perspective. Instead of training an external model to score individual reasoning steps, we examine whether the language model’s own hidden states contain a signal correlated with local reasoning quality. Hidden states summarize the evolving reasoning context, so reasoning errors may induce systematic distributional differences between correct and incorrect trajectories. If such differences are detectable at the span level, they could provide a self-supervised signal for process-level credit assignment using only the outcome-level correctness labels available in RLVR. We investigate this possibility by measuring the Wasserstein distance
2
;
22
between span-level hidden state distributions of correct and incorrect rollouts within each GRPO group.

Our empirical analysis suggests that such a signal indeed exists. By comparing span-level hidden state distributions between correct and incorrect rollouts within the same GRPO group, we find that the Wasserstein distance provides an effective proxy for local reasoning quality. It remains low where correct and incorrect trajectories are distributionally similar and increases where they diverge, with the two trends exhibiting aligned transition zones (Section
2
). Building on this finding, we propose a simple modification to GRPO in which span-level Wasserstein distances are used as token-level weights in the advantage function, amplifying the existing advantage direction at positions where correct and incorrect trajectories are most distinct. The method requires no additional model, no step-level labels, and only minimal changes to the existing training pipeline (Section
3
). We further formalize the underlying phenomenon with a separation theorem showing that, under mild structural assumptions, post-divergence spans have larger Wasserstein distances than pre-divergence spans whenever the population-level distributional difference exceeds finite-sample noise (Section
4
).

Our contributions are three fold:

1.

Empirical finding.
We show that span-level Wasserstein distance between hidden state distributions of correct and incorrect rollouts serves as a self-supervised signal that tracks reasoning quality without requiring any step-level annotation. This finding suggests that the model’s own representations carry process-level information that can be exploited without training a separate reward model (Section
2
).

2.

Theoretical foundation.
We prove a separation theorem establishing that post-error spans exhibit larger Wasserstein distances than pre-error spans whenever the population-level distributional difference exceeds finite-sample noise, which is a condition we verify empirically. We further extend this result to the group-level minimum distance used in practice and provide a principled justification for using Wasserstein distance as a credit assignment signal (Section
4
).

3.

Method and empirical validation.
We propose

a simple modification to GRPO that uses span-level Wasserstein distances to weight token-level advantages. Experiments on five mathematical reasoning benchmarks and five code generation benchmarks show consistent improvements over standard GRPO and strong performance relative to supervised process reward models, while requiring no additional annotation or reward model training (Section

2
Distributional Divergence in Hidden States

Before presenting our method, we establish an empirical observation that motivates and grounds the entire approach— in a controlled diagnostic setting, hidden-state distributional structure is strongly associated with local reasoning quality, and Wasserstein distance provides a useful way to measure this structure.

Setup.

We use MATH500
16
as the evaluation benchmark with Qwen2.5-Math-7B
23
as the base model. For each problem, we sample
G{=}8
rollouts and retain only problems where the model produces a mix of correct and incorrect answers. This filtering is required because our diagnostic compares correct and incorrect trajectories within the same rollout group and it excludes uniformly easy or uniformly hard problems where no opposing group is available. The retained subset comprises
83.8\%
of MATH500 problems, covering a range of difficulty levels (Please refer to Appendix
I
for details).

For each retained rollout, we extract prefixes at varying truncation points using a sliding window (starting length
w{=}100
tokens, stride
s{=}25
tokens), excluding spans that contain the final answer to avoid trivial leakage from answer-revealing tokens. At each truncation point, we estimate a
continuation success rate
by letting the model complete the reasoning 16 times from that prefix and recording the fraction of correct completions. We use this quantity as a proxy for local reasoning quality where prefixes that preserve a valid reasoning trajectory should admit a higher probability of correct completion.
Independently, at each prefix position we collect the hidden states of the most recent
w
tokens and compute the Wasserstein distance between this local hidden state distribution and the closest matching distribution from the opposing group (correct vs. incorrect). We adopt Wasserstein distance as our primary metric due to its ability to capture distributional differences beyond mean shift. The comparison with alternative metrics is provided in Section
5.4
. Full details of the distance computation are described in Section
3.3
.

To examine whether the association holds at the local level, we analyze stride-wise changes in both quantities within each trajectory. For each pair of consecutive prefix positions within the same rollout, we compute the change in continuation success (
\Delta\text{Accuracy}

\Delta\text{Accuracy}

\Delta W

This within-trajectory association suggests that the distributional divergence signal contains a genuine local component, such that local deterioration in reasoning is accompanied by local divergence of hidden states from the opposing group.

Implications.

These two analyses provide complementary evidence that hidden state distributions carry a signal associated with reasoning quality. The aggregate trend (Figure
1(a)
) shows that Wasserstein distance and continuation success move in opposite directions along the reasoning chain. The local analysis (Figure
1(b)
) further shows that this association is not solely an aggregate phenomenon but reflects within-trajectory dynamics. At positions where continuation success locally shifts, Wasserstein distance shifts concurrently in the opposite direction. If this distributional divergence signal can be reliably detected, it could serve as a self-supervised proxy for process-level credit without any step-level annotation.

This observation raises two questions that the remainder of the paper addresses. First, can we exploit this signal to improve credit assignment during training? We describe our method in Section
3
. Second, under what conditions is this
distributional separation theoretically guaranteed? We formalize a separation theorem in Section
4
and validate the full approach empirically in Section
5
.

3
Method

3.1
Preliminaries: GRPO

We consider reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) for reasoning tasks. Given an input
x
(e.g., a math problem), a policy
\pi_{\theta}
generates a rollout
y^{(i)}=(y_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,y_{T_{i}}^{(i)})
and receives a binary reward
r^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}
indicating correctness. In GRPO, a group of
G
rollouts
\mathcal{G}(x)=\{y^{(1)},\ldots,y^{(G)}\}
is sampled for each input, and the policy gradient takes the form

\nabla_{\theta}J=\mathbb{E}_{y^{(i)}\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}A^{(i)}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}^{(i)}\mid y_{<t}^{(i)},x)\right],

(1)

where
A^{(i)}=\frac{r^{(i)}-\bar{r}_{\mathcal{G}}}{\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}+\epsilon}
is the group-normalized advantage, with
\bar{r}_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}
denoting the mean and standard deviation of rewards within the group.

A fundamental limitation of Eq.
1
is that every token in a rollout receives the
same
advantage
A^{(i)}
. For example, if an incorrect rollout contains mostly correct reasoning but deviates at some step
\tau
, then tokens before
\tau
are penalized in the same way as those after
\tau
.

3.2
Span-Level Wasserstein Distance

Our method builds on the observation from Section
2
that correctness-relevant local differences induce detectable shifts in the model’s hidden-state distributions. We measure these shifts using the Wasserstein distance between local spans of hidden states.

Span decomposition.

Given a rollout
y^{(i)}
of length
T_{i}
, we partition it into overlapping spans using a sliding window of size
w
and stride
s
(
s\leq w
):

\mathcal{S}^{(i)}=\left\{S_{k}^{(i)}=[a_{k}^{(i)},b_{k}^{(i)}]\;\middle|\;a_{k}^{(i)}=1+(k-1)s,\;\;b_{k}^{(i)}=\min(a_{k}^{(i)}+w-1,T_{i})\right\}.

(2)

Each span
S_{k}^{(i)}
defines an empirical distribution over hidden states

\widehat{P}_{k}^{(i)}=\frac{1}{|S_{k}^{(i)}|}\sum_{t\in S_{k}^{(i)}}\delta_{h_{t}^{(i)}}
,
where
h_{t}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the hidden state at the last transformer layer before LM-head for token
y_{t}^{(i)}

30
;
33
, and
\delta_{h_{t}^{(i)}}
denotes the point mass at
h_{t}^{(i)}
.

Wasserstein distance.

The 1-Wasserstein distance between two distributions
P
and
Q
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is

W_{1}(P,Q)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(P,Q)}\int\|x-y\|_{2}\,d\gamma(x,y),

(3)

where
\Gamma(P,Q)
is the set of all couplings with marginals
P
and
Q
. Intuitively,
W_{1}
measures the minimum cost of transporting one distribution into the other. As observed in Section
2
, correctness-relevant local differences are reflected not only in shifts of hidden-state means but also in broader changes in span-level distributional shape. We therefore adopt
W_{1}
as a distribution-level discrepancy rather than a point-estimate similarity such as cosine distance between span means (see Section
4
for theoretical justification and Section
5.4
for empirical comparison with alternative metrics).

In practice, we compute the entropically regularized approximation
W_{\epsilon}
via the Sinkhorn algorithm
6
, which is efficient to compute on GPUs. The approximation satisfies
W_{1}\leq W_{\epsilon}\leq W_{1}+\epsilon\log n
for discrete measures on
n
points (Appendix
B
).

Step 1: Span-level minimum distance.
For each span
S_{k}^{(i)}
in rollout
y^{(i)}
, we compute its minimum Sinkhorn distance to all spans in the opposing set
\mathcal{O}^{(i)}
(line 4 in Algorithm
1
). This minimum makes the signal conservative with respect to multiple valid solution paths so that a span receives a large discrepancy only if it remains dissimilar to all candidate alternatives in the opposing set. We compare this choice against alternative aggregation rules in Section
5.4
.

Step 2: Global norm normalization.
To stabilize magnitudes across varying latent scales, we divide all span-level discrepancies by the global mean hidden-state norm

\bar{n}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathcal{G}}}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}\|h_{t}^{(i)}\|_{2}
,
where
N_{\mathcal{G}}=\sum_{i=1}^{G}T_{i}
is the total number of tokens in the rollout group. Since
\bar{n}
is a positive constant shared across the entire group, this rescaling preserves the ordering of span discrepancies within the group, and the ranking-based separation established for
W_{1}
in Section
4
transfers to the normalized weights. A side effect is that different rollouts may receive different total gradient magnitudes, introducing a form of cross-rollout reweighting. We isolate this effect in the ablation study (Section
5.4
).

Step 3: Token-level aggregation via max-pooling.
Considering that each token may belong to multiple overlapping spans, we assign token
t
in rollout
y^{(i)}
the maximum normalized discrepancy among all spans that cover it (line 10 in Algorithm
1

\omega_{t}^{(i)}=\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\max_{k:t\in S_{k}^{(i)}}d_{k}^{(i)}

This choice emphasizes the strongest local discrepancy signal associated with each token, which is especially useful in local transition regions where overlapping spans may mix low- and high-discrepancy patterns. We compare max-pooling against mean-pooling in Appendix
H
.

Step 4: Weighted advantage.
The token-level weighted advantage is obtained by multiplying the rollout-level GRPO advantage by the token weight

\widetilde{A}_{t}^{(i)}=A^{(i)}\cdot\omega_{t}^{(i)}
.
For incorrect rollouts (
A^{(i)}<0
), tokens in high-discrepancy regions receive larger negative updates. For correct rollouts (
A^{(i)}>0
), tokens whose hidden-state distributions are more strongly separated from the opposing set receive larger positive updates.

4
Theoretical Analysis

The observations in Section
2
suggest that span-level Wasserstein distances align empirically with local reasoning quality. In this section, we ask under what conditions such a separation can be expected theoretically. Our goal is not to model the full complexity of real reasoning trajectories, but to isolate a minimal stylized setting in which post-divergence spans become distinguishable from pre-divergence spans once the population-level distributional signal exceeds finite-sample noise. Under this view, the theory provides conditional support for the weighting mechanism used in Section
3.3
. In particular, the global normalization by
\bar{n}
preserves the ordering of span-level discrepancies within a rollout group, so the separation established below is consistent with the intended weighting pattern.

For readability, the theorems are stated for the exact Wasserstein distance
W_{1}
, whereas the algorithm uses the entropically regularized approximation
W_{\epsilon}
. As discussed in Appendix
B
, the approximation satisfies
W_{1}\leq W_{\epsilon}\leq W_{1}+\epsilon\log n
, for discrete measures on
n
points.
In our experiments, this approximation gap is small relative to the empirical separation margins observed between pre- and post-divergence spans, provided the separation gap exceeds
\epsilon\log n
.

Assumptions.

We consider a correct rollout
y^{+}
and an incorrect rollout
y^{-}
generated for the same input. Let
h_{t}^{+},h_{t}^{-}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote their token-level hidden states. The analysis is based on the following stylized assumptions.

(A1) Divergence structure.

There exists a divergence point
\tau
such that, for
t\leq\tau
, the hidden states
h_{t}^{+}
and
h_{t}^{-}
are independent draws from the same marginal distribution
P_{t}
, while for
t>\tau
, the correct rollout remains distributed as
P_{t}
and the incorrect rollout is distributed as
Q_{t}\neq P_{t}
. This assumption requires distributional equivalence rather than token-level alignment: two rollouts may differ in surface form while still sharing the same hidden-state distribution at the same reasoning stage. As with any stylized model, exact distributional equivalence is an idealization. In practice a small residual gap may persist before
\tau
. Figure
1(a)
provides empirical support for treating this gap as negligible where the mean Wasserstein distance in the first quartile of prefix positions (
\approx 0.67
) is smaller than in the last quartile (
\approx 0.75
), and the separation theorems below remain valid whenever this residual pre-divergence gap is dominated by the post-divergence signal
D(S)
—a condition verified empirically in Section
5.1
.

(A2) Bounded support.

For all positions
t
, the hidden states satisfy
\|h_{t}\|_{2}\leq M
.
This is satisfied in practice by modern transformer architectures, which apply LayerNorm
14
or RMSNorm
32
at each layer, bounding the magnitude of hidden state vectors.

(A3) Finite-sample concentration.

For independent samples
X_{t}\sim P_{t}
with
\|X_{t}\|_{2}\leq M
(in this assumption,
P_{t}
denotes a generic bounded-support distribution and is not restricted to the correct-rollout marginals in (A1), in particular, the bound applies equally when
P_{t}
is replaced by
Q_{t}
), let

\widehat{P}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{t}},\qquad\bar{P}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}P_{t}.

We assume

\mathbb{E}\!\left[W_{1}(\widehat{P}_{n},\bar{P}_{n})\right]\leq\tilde{C}_{d}Mn^{-1/d}.

For i.i.d. samples, bounds of this form are classical
8
. Our setting is independent but not necessarily identically distributed, so we adopt this rate as an assumption capturing the finite-sample noise floor relevant to our analysis and Appendix
C.2
discusses why this assumption is plausible in our setting.

We define the corresponding noise floor

\eta(n,d):=2\tilde{C}_{d}Mn^{-1/d},

(5)

which bounds the expected Wasserstein distance between two empirical measures drawn from the same span-level population mixture up to sampling variability. The practical role of (A3) is to separate finite-sample noise from population-level signal. Whether
\eta(n,d)
is small relative to the observed discrepancy is evaluated empirically in Section
5.1
.

Pairwise separation.

Let
S=[a,b]
be a span of length
n=b-a+1
. Define the corresponding span-level population mixtures

\bar{P}_{S}^{+}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=a}^{b}P_{t},\qquad\bar{P}_{S}^{-}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=a}^{b}Q_{t},

(6)

where, in the pre-divergence regime,
\bar{P}_{S}^{-}=\bar{P}_{S}^{+}
. We define the population distance

D(S):=W_{1}(\bar{P}_{S}^{-},\bar{P}_{S}^{+}).

(7)

Let
\widehat{P}_{S}^{-}
and
\widehat{P}_{S}^{+}
denote the corresponding empirical span distributions.

Theorem 1

(Pairwise Separation)
.

Under assumptions (A1)–(A3), for a span
S=[a,b]
of length
n
in
y^{-}
paired with the corresponding span in
y^{+}
, the following hold:

(i)

Pre-divergence
(
b\leq\tau
):

\mathbb{E}\!\left[W_{1}(\widehat{P}_{S}^{-},\widehat{P}_{S}^{+})\right]\leq\eta(n,d).

(ii)

Post-divergence
(
a>\tau
):

\mathbb{E}\!\left[W_{1}(\widehat{P}_{S}^{-},\widehat{P}_{S}^{+})\right]\geq D(S)-\eta(n,d).

(iii)

Strict separation.
If
D(S)>2\eta(n,d)
, then the expected post-divergence distance is strictly larger than the expected pre-divergence distance.

Proof sketch.

For (i), both empirical measures are drawn from the same span-level population mixture, so by the triangle inequality and assumption (A3),

\mathbb{E}\!\left[W_{1}(\widehat{P}_{S}^{-},\widehat{P}_{S}^{+})\right]\leq\eta(n,d).

For (ii), the reverse triangle inequality gives

W_{1}(\widehat{P}_{S}^{-},\widehat{P}_{S}^{+})\geq W_{1}(\bar{P}_{S}^{-},\bar{P}_{S}^{+})-W_{1}(\widehat{P}_{S}^{-},\bar{P}_{S}^{-})-W_{1}(\widehat{P}_{S}^{+},\bar{P}_{S}^{+}).

Taking expectations and applying (A3) to the two deviation terms yields

\mathbb{E}\!\left[W_{1}(\widehat{P}_{S}^{-},\widehat{P}_{S}^{+})\right]\geq D(S)-\eta(n,d).

Part (iii) follows by subtracting the pre-divergence bound from the post-divergence lower bound. Full proofs are given in Appendix
C
.
∎

The quantity
D(S)
captures distributional differences beyond first-moment shifts. By the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality,

D(S)=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=a}^{b}\left(\mathbb{E}_{Q_{t}}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{t}}[f]\right)\right|.

Restricting
f
to linear functions recovers first-moment differences, whereas the supremum over all 1-Lipschitz functions also captures changes in variance, shape, and other aspects of span-level distributional structure. In this sense, Wasserstein distance is strictly more general than first-moment comparisons based only on span means (Appendix
D
; see also Section
5.4
).

Group-level separation.

Algorithm
1
uses the minimum span-level discrepancy to the opposing set rather than a single paired comparison. To analyze this construction, consider an incorrect rollout
y^{-}
and a set of correct rollouts
\{y^{+,1},\ldots,y^{+,K^{+}}\}
. For each correct rollout
y^{+,k}
, let
\tau_{k}
denote its divergence point relative to
y^{-}
. Define the latest divergence point

\tau^{*}:=\max_{k}\tau_{k},

that is, the largest position up to which
y^{-}
remains distributionally aligned with at least one correct rollout.

For a span
S
in
y^{-}
, define the group-level empirical minimum discrepancy

d_{\min}(S):=\min_{k,j}W_{1}\!\left(\widehat{P}_{S}^{-},\widehat{P}_{S_{j}}^{+,k}\right),

(8)

where
\widehat{P}_{S_{j}}^{+,k}
denotes the empirical distribution of span
S_{j}
from correct rollout
y^{+,k}
. Define the corresponding population quantity

D^{*}(S):=\min_{k,j}W_{1}\!\left(\bar{P}_{S}^{-},\bar{P}_{S_{j}}^{+,k}\right).

(9)

Theorem 2

(Group-Level Separation)
.

Under assumptions (A1)–(A3), the following hold:

(i)

If

, then

\mathbb{E}[d_{\min}(S)]\leq\eta(n,d).

(ii)

Proof sketch.

If
b\leq\tau^{*}
, then there exists at least one correct rollout with a matching pre-divergence span, so the minimum discrepancy admits an expected upper bound of
\eta(n,d)
by the pairwise argument. If
a>\tau^{*}
, then for every opposing span the reverse triangle inequality implies a lower bound of the form

W_{1}(\widehat{P}_{S}^{-},\widehat{P}_{S_{j}}^{+,k})\geq W_{1}(\bar{P}_{S}^{-},\bar{P}_{S_{j}}^{+,k})-\eta(n,d),

and taking the minimum over all opposing spans yields the stated result. Full proofs are provided in Appendix
E
.
∎

The condition
D^{*}(S)>2\eta(n,d)
asks whether a post-divergence span is distributionally distinguishable from every opposing span up to finite-sample noise. A small
D^{*}(S)
does not indicate failure of the method; rather, it means that the span is not globally distinctive from correct reasoning in the model’s representation space, in which case assigning a low weight is appropriate. We do not claim that all post-divergence spans are separable. The theorem is explicitly conditional on the population-level separation exceeding the finite-sample noise floor. Extensions to multiple divergence points and the symmetric analysis for correct rollouts are provided in Appendices
G
and
F
.

Comparison with PRM-based methods.

A central question is whether a self-supervised hidden-state signal can compete with explicit step-level supervision from a trained process reward model.
Empirically, the answer is yes on the mathematical reasoning benchmarks considered here. As shown in Table

This effect is amplified on the 14B backbone which is reflected in the larger standard deviations of PRM variants. By deriving the credit signal directly from the policy model’s own hidden-state representations, SHEAR avoids dependence on a separately trained reward model.

Comparison with Entropy adv.

Entropy adv. is the most relevant baseline conceptually, as it also performs token-level advantage reweighting without external supervision. SHEAR outperforms it on every backbone for mathematical reasoning. This supports our hypothesis that distributional divergence in hidden states carries strictly richer process-level information than per-token entropy, which conflates uncertainty with reasoning quality and treats high-entropy exploratory tokens identically to high-entropy erroneous ones.

Code generation.

Ablation Studies

We conduct ablations to isolate the contribution of two key design choices: (i) the choice of distance metric used to measure span-level distributional divergence, and (ii) the role of global norm normalization in Algorithm
1
.

5.4.1
Distance Measure Comparison

Setup.

Section
4
motivates the use of the 1-Wasserstein distance on the grounds that, via Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality, it captures all 1-Lipschitz functionals of the distributional difference, including mean shift, variance, and higher-order shape changes. We test this claim empirically by replacing the Sinkhorn-approximated
W_{1}
in Algorithm
1
with three alternatives that differ in how much distributional information they retain:

(i)

Cosine distance between span means: a pure mean-shift, direction-only signal that discards both magnitude and shape.

(ii)

Chamfer distance between span hidden-state sets: a point-cloud distance that captures geometric coverage but is not a proper transport metric and is sensitive to outliers.

(iii)

MMD (Maximum Mean Discrepancy) with an RBF kernel: a kernel embedding distance that captures all moments through the kernel feature map but lacks the metric structure of optimal transport.

All variants use identical span construction, normalization, max-pooling, and weighting; only the inner distance computation differs.

Result.

Fig.
5(a)
shows the training curves for the four variants alongside the GRPO baseline. Wasserstein, Chamfer, and MMD form a top tier with peak accuracies above the GRPO baseline.
SHEAR (Wasserstein) achieves the highest peak, but the margin over SHEAR (Chamfer) and SHEAR (MMD) is small, suggesting that any sufficiently expressive distributional metric is able to capture the dominant component of the credit signal. This is consistent with the theoretical view in Section
4
: the population-level signal
D(S)
reflects a distributional gap that is rich enough to be detected by multiple measures, with optimal-transport providing the tightest characterization but not the only viable one.

The behavior of SHEAR (Cosine) is more revealing.
SHEAR (Cosine) plateaus at
0.4837
, falling below the GRPO baseline (
0.4884
) throughout the latter half of training.
This is a striking negative result: using only the angular separation between span means as a credit signal actively hurts learning. We interpret this as evidence that mean-direction shifts in hidden states are not a faithful proxy for reasoning quality; they correlate with surface-level lexical variation and other reasoning-irrelevant factors, and weighting tokens by such a signal injects noise into the policy gradient. The fact that the three distribution-aware metrics succeed where the mean-only metric fails directly supports the central claim of Section
4
: capturing the full distributional difference, rather than a low-order summary, is what makes hidden states a reliable source of process-level credit.

5.4.2
Cross-Rollout Reweighting Ablation

Setup.

Algorithm
1
normalizes span distances by the global mean hidden-state norm
\bar{n}
computed across the entire group. This rescaling is intended to stabilize magnitudes across varying latent scales, but it has a side effect: because
\bar{n}
is shared across rollouts rather than computed per rollout, the total gradient magnitude received by each rollout still depends on the absolute scale of its span distances. This introduces a form of cross-rollout reweighting that is conceptually distinct from the within-rollout token reweighting that the method is designed to deliver. A natural question is whether this cross-rollout effect contributes meaningfully to the observed gains, or whether the method would behave differently if span distances were rescaled in a way that suppresses it.
To probe this, we compare our default formulation against a variant in which the cross normalization step is replaced by per rollout normalization:

(i)

SHEAR (cross-rollout) (
default
): span distances are divided by the global mean hidden-state norm
\bar{n}
as in Algorithm
1
.

(ii)

SHEAR (per-rollout): each span distance is rescaled by a per-rollout normalizer, equalizing the average weight magnitude across rollouts within a group. This explicitly removes any cross-rollout reweighting and isolates the within-rollout token-level signal.

Result.

As shown in Fig
5(b)
, both variants of SHEAR substantially outperform the GRPO baseline. Two observations follow.
First, the dominant source of improvement is clearly the within-rollout token-level credit signal: even after explicitly equalizing per-rollout weight magnitudes, SHEAR (per-rollout) still improves over GRPO by a comfortable margin. This indicates that the method’s effectiveness does not hinge on cross-rollout magnitude differences—the per-token ranking of credit within a rollout, which the separation theorem in Section
4
directly addresses, is what carries the learning signal.
Second, the default formulation nonetheless retains a measurable edge over the per-rollout-equalized variant, suggesting that allowing rollouts with larger absolute distributional gaps to receive proportionally larger total gradient mass is mildly beneficial. Such rollouts contain more discriminable reasoning errors and plausibly merit more aggressive updates; collapsing the magnitude variation across rollouts gives up a small amount of useful signal. We therefore adopt SHEAR (cross-rollout) as the default in all main experiments. Importantly, both variants sit comfortably above the GRPO baseline, indicating that the method is robust to this design choice.
This ablation reinforces the interpretation in Section
4
: the value of the Wasserstein signal lies primarily in where within a rollout it concentrates credit, with cross-rollout magnitude variation playing a complementary role.

Related Work

Credit assignment in RLVR.

Credit assignment, namely identifying which tokens or intermediate steps in a reasoning chain contribute to the final outcome, is a central challenge in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. GRPO
24
assigns uniform advantages across all tokens which is coarse-grained
15
. Process reward models (PRMs)
4

5

16
provide finer-grained step-level supervision and have shown clear benefits over outcome-level feedback. However, PRMs require either human-annotated intermediate labels or an auxiliary reward model trained on such annotations, introducing both annotation cost and a potential transfer gap when the policy model’s capacity exceeds that of the PRM. Cui et al.
5
proposed learning process rewards through implicit signals, while Cheng et al.
4
introduced min-form credit assignment to mitigate reward hacking. Our work departs from the PRM paradigm entirely by extracting process-level credit from the policy model’s own hidden states, requiring no additional annotation or reward model.

Token-level advantage reshaping.

Several recent methods seek to differentiate token-level advantages within GRPO relying on internal signals.
3

35
exploit per-token entropy to reshape advantages, effectively improving credit granularity and exploration. Despite these benefits, a shared limitation remains: a scalar entropy metric cannot reliably distinguish between productive reasoning pivots and genuine erroneous steps. To overcome this representational bottleneck, recent methods leverage continuous latent representations to model and optimize reasoning
13

7

31
. These works share a core insight: high-dimensional latent spaces inherently capture richer semantic signals that clearly separate correct reasoning patterns from erroneous ones. By operating in this continuous space—via latent diffusion
13
, direct latent reward modeling
7
, or hybrid policy optimization
31
, these approaches enable more precise credit assignment and diverse exploration than scalar-driven heuristics. Building upon this shared insight, SHEAR directly computes the span-level Wasserstein distance between the model’s native hidden state, rather than designing complex latent generation architectures or training auxiliary latent reward models. This allows us to extract a highly expressive, self-supervised credit signal that shares the fine-grained evaluation advantages of continuous spaces, while eliminating the reliance on auxiliary models.

8
Conclusion

This paper introduces SHEAR, a self-supervised credit assignment method for RLVR that exploits the model’s own hidden-state representations to provide fine-grained, token-level training signals. We first identify an empirical phenomenon that span-level Wasserstein distance between hidden-state distributions of correct and incorrect rollouts tracks local reasoning quality, without requiring step-level annotation or an external reward model. We formalize this observation with a separation theorem showing that post-divergence spans exhibit provably larger Wasserstein distances than pre-divergence spans, under a verifiable condition on the population-level distributional gap. Moreover, we translate this insight into a practical algorithm that rescales token-level GRPO advantages using span-level Wasserstein distances, requiring only minimal modifications to existing training pipelines. Experiments across five mathematical reasoning benchmarks and five code generation benchmarks demonstrate consistent improvements over standard GRPO and competitive or superior performance relative to PRM-based methods that rely on a separately trained 7B reward model, while incurring less than 16% computational overhead.

Appendix A
Detailed Analysis of the Wasserstein Signal

While Section
2
establishes a strong macro-level anti-correlation between empirical accuracy and Wasserstein distance, this appendix investigates the robustness and dynamic sensitivity of this signal. Specifically, we ask two questions: Is the W-distance signal merely an artifact of longer sequence lengths? And how does it respond to shifts in reasoning quality as the trajectory unfolds?

A.1
Cross-Sectional Analysis: Controlling for Sequence Length

As a reasoning chain progresses, empirical accuracy naturally decreases while hidden state drift increases. To ensure W-distance is an independent indicator of reasoning quality rather than a simple proxy for token depth, we conducted a cross-sectional analysis as shown in Fig
9
.

By strictly grouping trajectories into distinct prefix position bins and dividing them into W-distance quantiles, a consistent pattern emerges. Notably, even in the early stages of reasoning, W-distance already demonstrates a preliminary discriminative ability: within the same early position group, a higher W-distance weakly but consistently corresponds to lower mean empirical accuracy. More importantly, trajectories that fall into high W-distance bins early in the process are significantly more prone to error accumulation, leading to more frequent and severe reasoning failures at later positions. This confirms that an elevated W-distance serves as an effective early warning signal for vulnerable reasoning state.

A.2
Dynamic Step-wise Tracking of Reasoning Mutations

To verify that W-distance captures structural shifts in reasoning, we analyzed the step-wise derivatives of both metrics (
\Delta
Accuracy and
\Delta
W-Distance) across consecutive strides.

Computing
\Delta
Accuracy and
\Delta
W-Distance.

Using the same per-prefix measurements from Section
2
, we compute first-order differences between consecutive prefix positions within each rollout:

\Delta\text{Acc}_{t}=\text{Acc}(s_{t})-\text{Acc}(s_{t-1}),\quad\Delta W_{t}=W(s_{t})-W(s_{t-1})

where
s_{t}
and
s_{t-1}
denote two consecutive prefix positions within the same rollout. These step-wise differences isolate the
local change
in reasoning quality and representational drift at each stride, as opposed to the cumulative trends analyzed in Section 2.

Defining mutation events.

We isolate "mutation events"—moments where a trajectory experiences a significant shift in local continuation success. Since the continuation accuracy at each prefix is estimated by completing the reasoning 16 times and recording the fraction of correct completions (Section 2), the minimum detectable change in accuracy is
1/16=0.0625
. We therefore adopt
\Delta|\text{Acc}|\geq 0.0625
as the threshold for identifying mutation events: changes below this magnitude are indistinguishable from the sampling noise inherent in a 16-trial estimate, while changes at or above this level correspond to at least one completion flipping between correct and incorrect, indicating a meaningful shift in reasoning quality.

As shown in Fig
1(b)
, there is a clear inverse relationship between local accuracy mutations and representational drift (Spearman correlation =
-0.42
). When a trajectory suffers a drop in reasoning quality (
\Delta
Accuracy
<0
), the W-distance tends to exhibit a corresponding local increase (
\Delta
W-Distance
>0
). This confirms that Wasserstein distance is a sensitive monitor of local reasoning failures, closely tracking shifts in structural reasoning logic as they unfold.

Appendix B
Sinkhorn Approximation

The Sinkhorn algorithm
6
computes the entropically regularized optimal transport cost:

W_{\epsilon}(P,Q)=\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(P,Q)}\left\{\int\|x-y\|_{2}\,d\gamma(x,y)+\epsilon\,\mathrm{KL}(\gamma\|P\otimes Q)\right\}.

(10)

Lemma 1

(Approximation Bound)
.

For discrete measures on
\leq n
points within a ball of radius
M
:

W_{1}(P,Q)\leq W_{\epsilon}(P,Q)\leq W_{1}(P,Q)+\epsilon\log n.

The Sinkhorn bias
W_{\epsilon}-W_{1}\in[0,\epsilon\log n]
varies across pairs (depending on the transport plan structure). Therefore,
W_{\epsilon}
does not automatically preserve the
W_{1}
ordering. However, when the true separation gap exceeds
\epsilon\log n
, the ranking between pre-divergence and post-divergence spans is robust. With our hyperparameters
\epsilon\log n\approx 20.8
, which is negligible relative to the observed separation gap (see Section
5.1
).

Strict Positivity of

Remark C.1

(Non-cancellation)
.

D(S)=0
would require

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t}Q_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t}P_{t}

as measures, meaning that distributional differences at individual positions exactly cancel when averaged over the span. This cancellation requires a precise anti-correlation structure across positions in distribution space. We treat

as a regularity condition and verify it empirically: the monotonically increasing AUC between correct and incorrect spans in Section
5.1
(Figure

C.2
Concentration Assumption: Justification

Assumption (A3) states that the empirical Wasserstein convergence rate
O(Mn^{-1/d})
holds for independent non-identical samples with shared bounded support. The standard proof for the i.i.d. case
8
proceeds via: (i) a covering-number bound on the support, depending only on diameter
2M
and dimension
d
; (ii) concentration of cell-wise empirical frequencies via Hoeffding’s inequality, which applies to sums of independent bounded random variables regardless of whether they are identically distributed. Since both ingredients are agnostic to the heterogeneity of
\{P_{t}\}
, the rate carries over with a potentially larger constant
\tilde{C}_{d}\geq C_{d}
.

Additionally,
W_{1}(\widehat{P}_{n},\bar{P}_{n})
satisfies McDiarmid-type concentration: changing any single
X_{t}
affects
\widehat{P}_{n}
by at most
2M/n
in
W_{1}
, yielding:

\mathbb{P}\left[W_{1}(\widehat{P}_{n},\bar{P}_{n})>\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\widehat{P}_{n},\bar{P}_{n})]+u\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{nu^{2}}{2M^{2}}\right).

(18)

We validate the practical implication of this assumption in
Section
5.1
, where the high discriminability
(AUC up to 0.99) between correct and incorrect spans confirms
that finite-sample noise does not overwhelm the distributional signal.

Appendix D
Why Wasserstein Distance?

Proposition D.1

(Population Distance Bounds)
.

The population distance
D(S)=W_{1}(\bar{P}_{S}^{-},\bar{P}_{S}^{+})
satisfies:

(a) First-moment bound:

D(S)\geq\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=a}^{b}(m_{t}^{-}-m_{t}^{+})\right\|_{2},

where

m_{t}^{+}=\mathbb{E}_{P_{t}}[h],\qquad m_{t}^{-}=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{t}}[h].

(b) Shape bound:

D(S)\geq\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=a}^{b}\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{Q_{t}}[\|h-c\|]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{t}}[\|h-c\|]\bigr)\right|

for any reference point
c
.

(c) Optimal bound:

D(S)=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t}(\mathbb{E}_{Q_{t}}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{t}}[f])\right|

(KR duality).

(d) Relationship to TV:

W_{1}(P,Q)\leq\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{supp})\cdot\mathrm{TV}(P,Q)

(upper bound, not useful as a lower bound on
D
).

Proof.

(a) Choose
f(h)=v^{\top}h
with
\|v\|_{2}=1
, which is 1-Lipschitz, in the KR dual, then optimize
v
to align with
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t}(m_{t}^{-}-m_{t}^{+})
.

(b)
\phi_{c}(h)=\|h-c\|_{2}
is 1-Lipschitz by the reverse triangle inequality. Apply this in the KR dual.

(c) Definition of
W_{1}
via KR duality.

(d) Standard result: transport cost per unit of mismatched mass is upper bounded by the support diameter.
∎

Appendix G
Multiple Divergence Points

The single-divergence model (Assumption A1) is an analytical simplification. Real reasoning chains may contain multiple divergences or partial self-corrections.

Definition 2

(Piecewise Divergence)
.

Rollout
y^{-}
has regime boundaries

0=\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<\cdots<\tau_{2m+1}=T

alternating between
aligned regimes
(
h_{t}^{-}\sim P_{t}
) and
drifted regimes
(
h_{t}^{-}\sim Q_{t}^{(j)}\neq P_{t}
).

Proposition G.1

(Piecewise Separation)
.

Spans fully contained within an aligned regime satisfy the pre-divergence bound. Spans fully contained within a drifted regime satisfy the post-divergence bound with regime-specific
D(S)
. Spans that straddle a regime boundary exhibit intermediate distances proportional to the drifted fraction.

The algorithm operates identically regardless of the number of divergence points: it computes Wasserstein distances for all spans and lets the distances encode the local alignment/drift structure.

Appendix H
Design Choice Justifications

H.1
Max-Pooling vs Mean-Pooling

For a token
t
at the divergence boundary (belonging to both pre- and post-divergence spans):

•

Max-pooling assigns
\omega_{t}=\max_{k:\,t\in S_{k}}d_{k}\geq d_{\mathrm{post}}
, preserving the post-divergence signal.

•

Mean-pooling assigns
\omega_{t}=\frac{1}{|\{k:t\in S_{k}\}|}\sum_{k:\,t\in S_{k}}d_{k}
, diluted by pre-divergence spans.

Max-pooling is the conservative choice: it ensures boundary tokens are weighted by their most discriminative span, which is where credit assignment is most uncertain and impactful.

H.2
Monotonicity

Under the natural expectation that distributional divergence accumulates over time (later errors compound earlier ones),
D(S)
should increase for later post-divergence spans. We do not formally prove this (it requires that the KR dual’s optimal witness behaves consistently across span positions), but observe it empirically in Section 5.1.

H.3
Connection to Process Reward Models

Let
\mathrm{PRM}(t)=\mathbb{P}[\text{correct}\mid\text{reasoning up to step }t]
. Under the divergence model,
\mathrm{PRM}(t)
is high for
t\leq\tau
and decreases for
t>\tau
. The token-level weight
\omega_{t}^{(i)}
(Algorithm
1
, line 13) qualitatively tracks
1-\mathrm{PRM}(t)
for incorrect rollouts: small when reasoning is on track, large after errors. This connection is interpretive; the key practical advantage is that
\omega_{t}^{(i)}
requires no step-level supervision.
