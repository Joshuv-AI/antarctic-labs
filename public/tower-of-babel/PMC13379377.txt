Bioprospecting of bacteria from textile effluents for enhanced dye biodegradation
Authors: Gebreselema Gebreyohannes, Berihu Zenawi, Gebremedhin Gebreslassie Hidru, Solomon Fitsum, Desta Berhe Sbhatu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The textile industry is a major contributor to water pollution, releasing effluents containing 10–15% unused synthetic dyes. These dyes have complex aromatic structures that resist biodegradation, posing serious risks to aquatic ecosystems and human health. This study evaluated the physicochemical properties of wastewater from the Maa Garment Textile Factory and investigated the bioremediation potential of indigenous bacterial isolates. Effluent samples collected from different treatment points were analyzed for pH, temperature, total suspended solids (TSS), total dissolved solids (TDS), biological oxygen demand (BOD), and chemical oxygen demand (COD). The wastewater exhibited a neutral to slightly alkaline pH (7.2–7.5), favorable for microbial activity, but had elevated temperatures (up to 38 °C) and high TDS (2633–2866 mg/L), indicating thermal and chemical pollution. A total of 30 bacterial isolates were screened for their ability to biodegrade reactive red, blue, and yellow dyes under varying conditions of temperature (25–40 °C), pH (5–9), and dye concentration (50–150 mg/L). Sixteen isolates demonstrated biodegradation capacity, with optimal performance (mean 89.4%) observed at 37 °C, pH 7, and 50 mg/L dye concentration, and significant variation across tested conditions (p < 0.001). The effective isolates included Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Shigella species, and Salmonella Typhimurium. Among them, P. aeruginosa strains (H5P, C2P, and C4P) showed the highest efficiency, achieving up to 90% biodegradation and maintaining strong performance under alkaline conditions (pH 9) and higher dye concentrations (150 mg/L). These findings highlight the adaptability of indigenous bacteria for dye degradation and identify P. aeruginosa as a promising candidate for sustainable, cost-effective industrial bioremediation, although further field validation across diverse dye systems is recommended.

Introduction

Rapid industrialization has intensified environmental pollution, with the textile industry emerging as a major contributor to global water contamination1. Effluents from textile operations contain synthetic reactive dyes with complex aromatic structures, making them resistant to biodegradation2. These dyes reduce light penetration, lower dissolved oxygen, and disrupt aquatic photosynthesis, causing both aesthetic and serious ecological and health impacts3.

In Ethiopia, the textile sector is one of the fastest-growing industries and a significant source of water pollution4. Many areas lack functional wastewater treatment, and existing plants are often poorly maintained. In Tigray, factories such as the Maa Garment and Textile Factory discharge untreated or inadequately treated effluents containing reactive, azo, and disperse dyes. These effluents persist for months or years, posing carcinogenic, mutagenic, and toxic risks. High pH, elevated temperatures (up to 38 °C), and dye concentrations (50–150 mg/L) further inhibit microbial degradation, disturb fish endocrine systems, and reduce algal photosynthesis5.

Bioremediation using dye-degrading bacteria like P. aeruginosa and K. pneumoniae has shown high potential, achieving up to 90% biodegradation under optimal conditions6. This approach provides an eco-friendly and cost-effective alternative to conventional physicochemical treatments, which are often energy-intensive and generate secondary sludge. However, despite these advances, two key research gaps remain: (i) limited understanding of the performance of indigenous bacterial strains isolated directly from Ethiopian textile effluents, and (ii) insufficient evaluation of their biodegradation efficiency under environmentally relevant stress conditions such as variable pH, temperature fluctuations, and increasing dye loads.

Addressing these gaps, this study investigates locally adapted bacterial isolates obtained from the Maa Garment and Textile Factory for their ability to degrade reactive dyes. Biodegradation efficiency was assessed spectrophotometrically under varying operational parameters, including temperature (25–40 °C), pH (5–9), and dye concentrations (50–150 mg/L). The novelty of this work lies in the application and evaluation of indigenous, stress-tolerant bacterial communities directly sourced from real industrial effluents, aiming to identify optimal degradation conditions and assess their potential integration into existing wastewater treatment systems. Ultimately, this study contributes to the development of sustainable, low-cost, and locally applicable strategies for managing industrial dye pollution in Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and dyes selection

Aseptically, 10 mL effluent samples were collected from 13 sites, including homogenization, biological treatment, and clarification units of Maa Garment and Textile Factory, Mekelle, Ethiopia. The samples were transferred into sterile plastic bottles, transported to the laboratory on ice at 4 °C, and stored at 4 °C until analysis to minimize physicochemical and biological alterations. Three reactive dyes (blue 19, yellow 145, and red 120) (Table 1) were selected for the study based on their extensive use in local textile industries and their significant contribution to elevated BOD, COD, and toxic pollutants in textile effluents. Physicochemical analysis of effluents

Wastewater samples were analyzed for key physicochemical parameters following APHA (2005) standard methods. Samples were collected in sterile polyethylene bottles, transported in an ice box, and analyzed within 24 h. Temperature was measured on-site using a calibrated digital thermometer, while pH was determined with a calibrated pH meter standardized with buffer solutions (pH 4.0, 7.0, and 9.2). Color was assessed spectrophotometrically at 450 nm after filtration, using distilled water as blank, and odor was evaluated organoleptically by a trained panel and classified as none, mild, or strong. COD was determined using the closed reflux method by digesting 50 mL of sample with potassium dichromate and sulfuric acid containing silver sulfate at 150 °C for 2 h, followed by absorbance measurement at 600 nm and calculation using potassium hydrogen phthalate standards. BOD₅ was measured by diluting 10 mL of sample with 90 mL of oxygen-saturated water, determining initial dissolved oxygen, incubating at 20 °C in the dark for five days, and measuring final DO, with BOD calculated from the DO difference according to APHA guidelines7.

Isolation of bacteria from effluents

Bacterial strains were isolated using serial dilution and the pour plate method. Effluent samples were diluted (10⁻¹ to 10⁻⁶) in sterile 0.85% NaCl, mixed with nutrient agar (Peptone 5 g/L, Yeast extract 1.5 g/L, Beef extract 1.5 g/L, NaCl 5 g/L, Agar 15 g/L, pH 7), and poured into sterilized Petri dishes. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and discrete colonies were sub-cultured to obtain pure isolates, which were maintained on nutrient agar slants at 4 °C for further testing8.

Primary screening for dye-degrading bacterial isolates

Pure bacterial isolates were screened for dye-degrading activity using a liquid enrichment assay. Each isolate was sub-cultured on nutrient agar to ensure purity and then inoculated into 50 mL of sterile nutrient broth containing 50 mg/L reactive dye in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks. The medium pH was adjusted to 7.0 prior to autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min, and after cooling, flasks were inoculated with 1–2% (v/v) standardized overnight cultures. Incubation was carried out at 37 °C for 24 h under static or shaking conditions. A dye-containing uninoculated flask served as the negative control, while a dye-free inoculated broth was used as a growth control. After incubation, decolorization was evaluated by visual comparison with controls and, where applicable, confirmed by centrifugation at 8,000 rpm for 10 min followed by spectrophotometric measurement of the supernatant at the dye’s maximum absorption wavelength. Isolates showing marked reduction in color intensity were selected as potential dye degraders for further characterization and optimization. Jamee and Siddique9.

Optimization of biodegradation parameters

A loopful of the selected bacterial isolate was inoculated into 50 mL sterile nutrient broth and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h under shaking conditions (120–150 rpm). The actively growing culture was used as inoculum for all biodegradation experiments. Reactive dye stock solution (1000 mg/L) was prepared in distilled water, sterilized, and diluted to working concentrations of 50, 100, and 150 mg/L. Optimization experiments were conducted in triplicate using 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 100 mL nutrient broth supplemented with reactive dye. For all experimental setups, a negative control (uninoculated flask containing only sterile nutrient broth with the respective dye concentration) was included and incubated under identical conditions to account for abiotic dye decolorization. To evaluate temperature effects, cultures containing 100 mg/L dye at pH 7 were incubated at 25, 30, 37, and 40 °C for 24–72 h. For pH optimization, the medium was adjusted to pH 5, 7, and 9 using 0.01 M HCl or KOH and incubated at 37 °C. Dye concentration effects were evaluated using 50, 100, and 150 mg/L under optimized pH and temperature conditions. After incubation, samples were centrifuged at 8,000–10,000 rpm for 10 min to remove biomass, and the absorbance of the clear supernatant was measured at λmax using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer. Percentage biodegradation was calculated as: Biodegradation (%) = (A0−At)/A0 × 100, where A0 is the initial absorbance and At is the absorbance after incubation10.

Dye biodegradation assay

Dye biodegradation was measured using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. Cultures grown in nutrient broth with dyes were centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 30 min), and the supernatant absorbance was recorded at λmax. Percentage biodegradation was then calculated. % Biodegradation = [(Initial absorbance – final absorbance)/Initial absorbance] X 10011.

Identification of bacterial isolates

Following Bergey’s Manual, potential dye-degrading isolates were characterized through cultural, morphological, and biochemical profiling12. Morphological assessments included microscopic observation of cell shape and arrangement13. Biochemical identification utilized the hanging drop method for motility, H2O2 bubble formation for catalase activity8. KOH-induced gelatinous thread formation was performed to confirm Gram-negative status. Metabolic characterization was carried out using the indole test with Kovac’s reagent, citrate utilization on Simmons citrate agar, and the Triple Sugar Iron (TSI) test to assess carbohydrate fermentation and H₂S production14.

Data analysis

Data on the biodegradation efficiency of bacterial isolates were analyzed using one-way ANOVA to evaluate differences among treatments after verifying the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance prior to analysis. Statistical significance was determined at p ≤ 0.05 and p ≤ 0.01 using Minitab 19 software. All experiments were conducted in triplicates, and results were presented as Mean ± Standard deviation.

Results and discussion

Physicochemical characterization of effluent

Wastewater from the Maa Garment Textile Factory exhibited variable physicochemical characteristics that necessitate tailored bioremediation (Table 2). The effluent pH (7.2–7.5) fell within the optimal range for most dye-degrading bacteria, suggesting that the wastewater can naturally support the metabolic activity of indigenous microflora and making in-situ bioremediation feasible15. Most samples were near ambient temperatures (26–26.3 °C), but a localized spike of 38 °C indicated thermal pollution, favoring thermotolerant microbial strains. Moderately elevated COD (58.3–59 mg/L) and BOD (26–27.6 mg/L) levels reflect a consistent organic load, which, while indicative of industrial inefficiency, provides a stable nutrient source for specialized microbial biofilms16,17. A BOD/COD ratio of approximately 0.45 further demonstrates that the effluent is highly amenable to biological treatment despite the presence of complex synthetic dyes.

However, high TDS levels (2866 mg/L) pose ecotoxicological risks by inducing osmotic stress in aquatic life, necessitating the use of halotolerant bacterial strains for effective treatment. The diverse coloration and malodorous black effluent indicate insufficient oxygenation and active anaerobic decomposition, emphasizing the need for optimized, sustainable wastewater management strategies. These characteristics align with typical textile effluents reported globally, underlining the critical importance of implementing effective effluent treatment to mitigate environmental impacts. These parameters are consistent with typical textile effluents reported worldwide18–20 and highlight the environmental need for effective effluent treatment.

Isolation and screening of bacterial isolates

A total of 30 bacterial isolates were screened from three wastewater treatment points of the textile factory using selective media (Table 3). All isolates grew on nutrient agar, confirming viability. On deferential media, a majority was lactose fermenters on MacConkey agar, while certain a group (B5, C2-C10) were non-lactose fermenters. Noticeably, isolates H1–H10 produced a feature green metallic sheen on eosin methylene blue agar, a characteristic feature of E. coli. The diversity in cultural phenotypes pinpointed an enormous microbial community adapted to the textile factory wastewater ecology. This prior enrichment is a critical step, as selective media foster the isolation process of metabolically versatile microbial strains with certain potential dye-degrading activity, an approach evidenced by previous studies (Singh et al., 20171, .

The isolation of 30 distinct bacterial strains from diverse factory treatment points reveals a well-established, niche-adapted microbial community capable of thriving amidst industrial pollutants21. The presence of both lactose and non-lactose fermenters, including E. coli-like phenotypes, underscores a high degree of metabolic versatility and suggests a strong likelihood of specialized enzyme activity, such as azoreductase production, for dye mineralization. While the survival of these enteric bacteria indicates potential public health risks requiring disinfection, it also confirms that the factory’s effluent acts as a natural selective enrichment environment22. Indigenous bacteria adapted to harsh industrial effluents exhibit enhanced metabolic flexibility and stress tolerance. This resilience makes them robust biotechnological tools and highlights the bioprospecting potential of industrial sites for developing sustainable, cost-effective wastewater treatment systems.

Screening for dye-degradation potential

Screening against red, blue, and yellow dyes identified 16 of 30 isolates (53%) as positive for biodegradation (Table 4; Fig. 1). Isolates from the homogenization tank (H1 to H10) and five clarifier tanks (C2, C4, C6, C8, C10) showed strong activity, while those from the biological unit and other clarifier tanks did not, indicating niche-specific traits. The ability of these isolates to use complex azo dyes as a primary energy source highlights their bioremediation potential, consistent with the role of azoreductases in dye degradation23,24.

The concentration of active isolates in the homogenization and clarifier tanks identifies these units as selective hotspots where high chemical stress has driven indigenous microbes to evolve specialized metabolic machinery. The finding that 53% of isolates successfully biodegraded red, blue, and yellow reactive dyes demonstrates substantial metabolic adaptability and enzymatic potential for dye transformation, likely mediated by azoreductase and related oxidative–reductive enzymes that cleave stable azo (–N = N–) bonds, enabling the utilization of these complex dye compounds as carbon and energy sources15. This substrate versatility is essential for treating complex, multi-color textile effluents and supports a biological approach as a low-cost, sustainable alternative to conventional chemical treatments25. Moreover, the high frequency of active strains indicates rapid evolutionary adaptation within the factory’s microbial community, highlighting the potential to develop robust microbial consortia that eliminate the need for non-native species or costly nutrient supplementation in industrial-scale bioremediation26.

Quantitative assessment of biodegradation efficiency

Effect of temperature on biodegradation

The biodegradation efficiency of bacterial isolates showed significant variation depending on temperature, dye type, and strain (p < 0.001). Across all isolates, biodegradation increased from 25 °C to 37 °C, peaking within this mesophilic range, before declining at 40 °C, reflecting the temperature sensitivity of dye-degrading enzymes (Table 5). The average biodegradation reached 89.4% at 37 °C, compared to 55.0% at 30 °C and 31.9% at 25 °C, highlighting the physiological optimum for enhanced enzymatic activity, likely mediated by azoreductases27. Among the isolates, H5P, C2P, and C4P achieved the highest mean biodegradation efficiencies of 71.4%, 70.0%, and 68.0%, respectively, and maintained significant activity even at 40 °C, demonstrating considerable thermotolerance. In contrast, isolates such as C2SH, H2SH, H4SAL, and Salmonella/Shigella species showed marked declines at elevated temperatures, with biodegradation falling below 50%.

Across the red, blue, and yellow reactive dyes, the isolates exhibited high biodegradation efficiency. For instance, H5P achieved 90% biodegradation of red and yellow dyes at 37 °C while retaining over 68% efficiency at 40 °C. Moderate performers (H1E, H2E, H3E) reached 86–96% at 37 °C but declined to 54–58% at 40 °C, whereas lower-performing isolates dropped below 50%, reflecting strain-specific limitations under thermal stress. Screening across the 25–40 °C range identified 37 °C as the physiological optimum, with several strains, including H1E, C2SH, and H5SAL, achieving near-complete biodegradation of up to 98%. P. aeruginosa strains consistently outperformed other isolates across all dye types and temperatures, indicating robust azoreductase systems capable of degrading diverse chromophores.

These results have critical implications for industrial wastewater treatment. The mesophilic optimum at 37 °C confirms that primary dye-degrading enzymes, likely azoreductases, require precise temperature regulation to maintain consistent performance28,27. H5P, C2P, and C4P are highly thermotolerant and broadly active, making them well-suited for variable-temperature, multi-colored effluents while minimizing the need for multiple strains or extra nutrients. Strain-specific performance variations support the design of tailored microbial consortia, combining high-performing and moderate strains to maximize efficiency and operational stability under realistic industrial conditions29.

Moreover, the superior resilience of P. aeruginosa positions it as a robust biotransformation agent capable of withstanding the thermal shocks typical of textile processing30. Isolates with narrow functional ranges require strict temperature regulation to prevent enzymatic failure, whereas robust non-pathogenic strains such as H5P provide safer and more sustainable alternatives to pathogenic yet active species like Salmonella and Shigella. These results underscore the bioprospecting potential of indigenous industrial effluent isolates, offering a foundation for resilient and scalable wastewater treatment systems31.

Effect of pH on biodegradation of dyes

The biodegradation efficiency of bacterial isolates was strongly influenced by pH, strain type, and dye color (Table 6). Across all isolates, maximal biodegradation occurred at neutral pH (pH 7), with values ranging from 84 to 98%, while acidic (pH 5) and alkaline (pH 9) conditions generally resulted in lower efficiencies (17–57%), demonstrating pH sensitivity of the dye-degrading enzymes32. Among the isolates, H5P, C2P, and C4P consistently showed superior performance, achieving 86–90% biodegradation at pH 7 and maintaining 51–58% efficiency even at pH 9, indicating both promising biodegration activity and resilience to suboptimal pH. Moderate performers such as H1E, H2E, and H3E achieved high biodegradation (86–96%) at pH 7 but dropped to 41–44% at pH 9, while lower-performing isolates, including H2SH, C2SH, and H5SAL, consistently remained below 30% at acidic and alkaline pH levels, revealing strain-specific limitations.

The isolates displayed promising biodegradation activity across red, blue, and yellow reactive dyes. Notably, H5P achieved 51–52% at pH 5, 87–90% at pH 7, and 55–58% at pH 9 across all three dyes, demonstrating both thermal and chemical robustness. C2P and C4P also maintained high biodegradation across all dye types, with slightly lower but comparable efficiencies. In contrast, isolates such as H1K, H2K, and H4K showed moderate activity at pH 7 (82–93%) but were significantly less effective under acidic or alkaline conditions (26–38%). These patterns suggest that pH exerts a strong regulatory effect on enzymatic biodegradation activity, with neutral pH being optimal for most isolates9.

The superior performance of H5P, C2P, and C4P across a broad pH range demonstrates their suitability for treating effluents with variable pH and complex, multi-colored dye compositions. Their activity reduces the need for multiple specialized strains or chemical additives, enhancing cost-effectiveness and simplifying operations33. Incorporating moderate performers like H1E, H2E, and H3E into tailored microbial consortia alongside high-performing strains can enhance overall treatment efficiency and system stability.

The pronounced pH-dependence of most isolates underscores the importance of maintaining near-neutral conditions (pH 7) for consistent biodegradation, while the resilience of P. aeruginosa strains under acidic and alkaline conditions indicates their potential for fluctuating effluent environments34. In contrast, pathogenic strains like Salmonella (H5SAL) and Shigella (C2SH) exhibited low activity at non-neutral pH, highlighting both functional limitations and biosafety concerns, and supporting the prioritization of robust, non-pathogenic strains for dye biodegradation. These results emphasize the bioprospecting potential of indigenous bacterial isolates for sustainable, cost-effective, and operationally reliable textile effluent treatment, aligning with previous studies on pH- and strain-specific variations in azo dye biodegradation35.

Effect of initial dye concentration on biodegradation

The quantitative assessment of 16 bacterial isolates across a range of dye concentrations (50 mg/L to 150 mg/L) revealed a significant inverse correlation (p < 0.001) between pollutant load and biodegradation efficiency (Table 7). At the 50 mg/L baseline, isolates such as C2SH, H5SAL, and H1E achieved near-complete degradation, reaching up to 98%; however, their performance declined sharply to a mean of 41.1% at the 150 mg/L threshold. This pronounced decrease suggests a saturation effect, where high dye concentrations overwhelm microbial azoreductase systems, while the accumulation of intermediate metabolic products, such as aromatic amines, likely exerts toxic pressure that inhibits further cellular growth and enzymatic turnover.

Unlike the sensitive Shigella and Salmonella species, whose substrate utilization is inhibited at high dye concentrations due to saturation effects or increased toxicity36, the P. aeruginosa strains (H5P, C2P, and C4P) displayed remarkable metabolic stability and resilience. Notably, H5P retained 59.16% biodegradation efficiency even at the demanding 150 mg/L load. In contrast to the erratic responses of Klebsiella and E. coli under rising concentrations, the Pseudomonas isolates maintained a linear and predictable degradation profile across all tested reactive dyes, suggesting either more robust enzyme systems or superior cellular efflux mechanisms that effectively manage high substrate toxicity37.

These findings have important implications for industrial wastewater management, particularly in textile operations where effluent concentrations fluctuate38. The results highlight the need for dilution pre-treatments or fed-batch strategies to prevent sudden “slug” loads from causing a total collapse of the bioremediation process. Moreover, selecting H5P over highly efficient but sensitive and potentially pathogenic strains like H5SAL provides a strategic advantage; its balanced profile of high load resilience and safer industrial applicability enhances operational reliability. Employing such robust indigenous strains can significantly reduce costs and operational complexity by enabling a single-strain system to effectively treat complex, multi-colored effluents39.

Biochemical profiling and identification of isolates

Comprehensive biochemical characterization of the 16 bacterial isolates revealed a diverse microbial community dominated by Gram-negative, catalase-positive bacilli representing five distinct genera with specialized metabolic adaptations (Table 8). Within the Enterobacteriaceae family, E. coli and K. pneumoniae demonstrated significant fermentative versatility through A/A reactions on TSI, while S. Typhimurium and Shigella spp. exhibited K/A reactions indicative of glucose-driven, non-lactose fermentation. In contrast, P. aeruginosa isolates (C2P, C4P, and H5P) displayed K/K reactions, highlighting a robust non-fermentative, oxidative metabolism. These distinct profiles, further validated by SIM and citrate utilization tests, suggest that the textile effluent serves as a selective enrichment medium, effectively priming indigenous microflora for the degradation of complex synthetic azo dyes.

While the presence of pathogenic genera like Salmonella and Shigella necessitates stringent containment and disinfection protocols, their high biodegradation potential underscores the significant bioprospecting value of industrial sites. The biochemical diversity spanning the fermentative capacity of enteric bacteria and the oxidative resilience of Pseudomonas establishes a strategic blueprint for designing synergistic microbial consortia capable of treating multi colored cocktail effluents. P. aeruginosa consistently outperformed other isolates under thermal, pH, and concentration stressors, likely due to its metabolic flexibility and robust azoreductase activity. Integrating these resilient species with fermentative agents such as Klebsiella and E. coli offers a scalable and cost-effective framework for industrial bioremediation. This approach leverages combined anaerobic and aerobic pathways to ensure predictable and thorough pollutant mineralization40.

Conclusion

This study highlights both the ecological risks of textile wastewater from the Maa Garment Textile Factory, including high total dissolved solids and strong coloration, and its potential to support effective bioremediating microbes. Thirty bacterial strains were isolated, with 53% demonstrating strong dye decolorizing activity, confirming the value of indigenous microbial communities for sustainable wastewater management. Among these, P. aeruginosa H5P, C2P, and C4P showed high degradation efficiency across reactive dyes and resilience under varying environmental conditions. Optimal bioremediation occurred at 37 °C, pH 7, and 50 mg/L dye concentration, although substrate inhibition and sensitivity to environmental factors indicate the need for well optimized treatment systems.

Future research should focus on pilot scale validation of promising strains in bioreactors, integration of multi omics approaches to elucidate azoreductase mediated pathways, and development of microbial consortia to improve stability and broaden substrate utilization. Biosafety considerations, particularly regarding P. aeruginosa, require strict risk mitigation strategies, including attenuation or transfer of degradation pathways to safer hosts. In addition, advances in bioprocess engineering such as immobilized cell systems, biofilms, and hybrid treatment technologies will be essential to address toxicity and environmental variability. Overall, these findings support a circular bio economy approach for Ethiopia’s textile industry by leveraging indigenous microbial resources for scalable, safe, and cost-effective wastewater treatment.
