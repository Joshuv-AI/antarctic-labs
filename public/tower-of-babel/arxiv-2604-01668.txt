The Landau–Lifshitz–Bloch equation with spin diffusion: global strong solution and finite element approximation
Agus L. Soenjaya

Abstract
. The spin-diffusion Landau–Lifshitz–Bloch (SDLLB) system is a nonlinearly coupled system of quasilinear vector-valued PDEs which models the interaction between spin-polarised currents and magnetisation at high temperatures. The aim of this paper is twofold. Firstly, assuming the initial data is sufficiently small, we show the existence of a unique global strong solution to the SDLLB equation in a bounded domain Ω ⊂ ℝ d \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} , where d ≤ 3 d\leq 3 , thus ensuring well-posedness of the model. Secondly, we propose a decoupled linearised fully-discrete finite element scheme to solve the problem. Despite the strong nonlinearity of the system, the proposed scheme only requires the solution of two completely decoupled linear systems per time-step. Assuming adequate regularity of the exact solution and a certain time-step constraint, we rigorously show that the numerical scheme converges at an optimal rate. Several numerical experiments corroborate our theoretical results.

1.
Introduction

Micromagnetics is a field of physics dealing with the prediction of magnetic behaviour at sub-micrometre length scales. More recently, it was found that magnetisation can be manipulated by spin-polarised currents, even in the absence of any external magnetic field. This gives birth to the field of spintronics. Modern applications of this theory include the development of magnetoresistive random access memory (MRAM) and heat-assisted magnetic recording (HAMR) devices. It is important to note that in various applications, the temperature inside the devices may exceed the Curie temperature of the material. This underlies the importance of a model which incorporates interactions between magnetisation and spin-polarised currents at high temperatures. One such model is given by the spin-diffusion Landau–Lifshitz–Bloch (SDLLB) system, first analysed in
[
25
]
. In the absence of currents, one recovers the Landau–Lifshitz–Bloch (LLB) equation
[
13
,
20
,
28
]
.

Fix a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d=1,2,3
) with boundary
\partial\Omega
. Let
\boldsymbol{m}:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\boldsymbol{s}:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the local magnetisation and the spin accumulation, respectively.
The dynamics of the local magnetisation coupled with the spin accumulation in the regime above the Curie temperature can be described by a coupled system of vector-valued quasilinear equations
[
25
]
, which we refer to as the SDLLB system:

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}=-\gamma\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{H}+\alpha\boldsymbol{H}-\gamma^{\prime}\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{s}\qquad

for
(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\Omega
,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}=\alpha^{\prime}\Delta\boldsymbol{m}-\kappa\mu\boldsymbol{m}-\kappa|\boldsymbol{m}|^{2}\boldsymbol{m},\qquad

for
(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\Omega
,

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{s}=-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}-\frac{D_{0}}{\tau_{\mathrm{sf}}}\boldsymbol{s}-\frac{D_{0}}{\tau_{\mathrm{J}}}\boldsymbol{s}\times\boldsymbol{m}\qquad

for
(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\Omega
,

(1.1d)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{J}=\beta^{\prime}\boldsymbol{m}\otimes\boldsymbol{j}-D_{0}\nabla\boldsymbol{s}+\beta D_{0}(\boldsymbol{m}\otimes\boldsymbol{m})\nabla\boldsymbol{s}\qquad

for
(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\Omega
,

(1.1e)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{m}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}),\;\boldsymbol{s}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{s}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for }\boldsymbol{x}\in{\Omega},

(1.1f)

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\boldsymbol{m}=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\boldsymbol{s}=\boldsymbol{0}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for }(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\partial\Omega.

A more detailed discussion about this model can be found in Section
2.2
.
Here,
\boldsymbol{n}
is the outward pointing normal vector to
\partial\Omega
and
\gamma^{\prime}:=\gamma\delta
. The physical meanings of other coefficients in (
1.1
) are as follows:
\alpha^{\prime}
is the exchange field intensity,
\kappa=(2\chi)^{-1}
is a positive constant related to the longitudinal susceptibility
\chi
of the material,
\mu
is a constant related to the equilibrium magnetisation and temperature,
\tau_{\mathrm{sf}}
is the spin flip relaxation time,
\tau_{\mathrm{J}}
is the spin transfer torque characteristic time, and
\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in(0,1)
are spin polarisation parameters. All numerical coefficients in (
1.1
) are positive.

For physical reasons, we assume that the function
\boldsymbol{j}:=\boldsymbol{j}(t,\boldsymbol{x})
is a prescribed current density vector field which is tangential to the boundary (
\boldsymbol{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
on
\partial\Omega
), while
D_{0}:=D_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})
is a given diffusion scalar field which is bounded above and below by positive constants.
As is commonly done in the literature, we only consider energy contribution from the exchange interactions
[
13
,
28
,
41
]
:

(1.2)

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{m}):=\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|{\nabla\boldsymbol{m}(\boldsymbol{x})}\right|^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{4}\int_{\Omega}\left|{\boldsymbol{m}(\boldsymbol{x})}\right|^{4}+\kappa\mu\int_{\Omega}\left|{\boldsymbol{m}(\boldsymbol{x})}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},

leading to the effective field
\boldsymbol{H}
in (
1.1b
). Lower-order contributions from the first-order anisotropy field and the applied field could be considered without difficulty, but is omitted here for simplicity. Contribution from the demagnetisation field poses no significant problem analytically, but may be a source of numerical bottleneck. We refer the reader to
[
15
,
30
,
47
]
for further details about these issues.

We note that the spin diffusion equation coupled with the Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert equation (SDLLG) is proposed in
[
21
,
22
]
as a model for micromagnetics in the presence of spin currents for temperatures much lower than the Curie temperature. Numerical methods to solve such problem, focusing on multi-layer system, are proposed in
[
1
,
21
,
38
]
, where convergence (along subsequence) to a weak solution is shown (see also
[
6
,
16
,
18
,
32
]
, among others, for numerical integrators to the LLG equation without spin diffusion). Analysis of the SDLLG problem focusing on various mathematical issues such as regularity, long-time behaviour, and optimal control are done in
[
4
,
15
,
33
,
34
,
36
]
. More detail about the physical background can be found in
[
37
]
. On the other hand, the SDLLB system is much less studied. While the issues of well-posedness and numerical approximation of the Landau–Lifshitz–Bloch equation (without spin diffusion) have been considered in
[
8
,
28
,
29
,
31
,
42
]
, not much is known about the SDLLB system, especially for
d=3
. The existence of a smooth solution to (
1.1
) (which is global for sufficiently small initial data) is shown in
[
25
]
for
d\leq 2
. As far as we know, numerical integrator for (
1.1
) has not been considered before.

One of the aims of this paper is to continue the study in
[
25
]
for the case
d=3
. Specifically, under certain smallness assumption on the initial data, we show the existence and uniqueness of global strong solution to the SDLLB in a smooth and bounded domain. We further propose a fully-discrete numerical scheme based on the Galerkin finite element method in space and the linearised Euler method in time to approximate the solution. We remark that despite the nonlinear coupling in (
1.1
), the proposed scheme only requires solving two completely decoupled linear systems in each time-step, which is an advantage in micromagnetics simulation
[
44
]
. Differently from the analysis of numerical schemes for the LLG or the SDLLG equation where only convergence along subsequence without rate is shown
[
1
]
, here we proceed to show an error estimate for the approximation, since the solution to the SDLLB is expected to be regular for a smooth and sufficiently small initial data (or small
\beta
) in a regular bounded domain. Assuming adequate regularity of the exact solution, an optimal rate of convergence is shown, as corroborated by several numerical experiments.

In contrast with the SDLLG equation, the problem (
1.1
) does not automatically admit a pointwise bound on the magnetisation magnitude
\left|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right|
, which complicates the analysis. On the other hand, the SDLLB equation possesses a stronger damping term (given by
\alpha\boldsymbol{H}
) which can be exploited to show a better regularity for the solution, at least for sufficiently small initial data (or small spin polarisation parameters) and in a smooth domain. Note that a certain smallness assumption on
\boldsymbol{m}
is expected for well-posedness, otherwise, at least formally, the last term in (
1.1d
) will dominate, turning (
1.1c
) into a backward diffusion equation for which ill-posedness of the associated initial-value problem is known.

The existence and uniqueness results for the SDLLB are established by proving several uniform a priori estimates for the solution, which require careful analysis due to the nonlinearities involved in the coupled system. The error analysis of the numerical integrator is performed by defining appropriate elliptic projections tailored to the problem at hand and analysing their properties. Here, we work with a single-layer magnetic domain
\Omega
as in
[
15
,
25
,
33
,
34
,
36
]
, which is physically relevant. We briefly remark that for a multi-layer domain, the magnetisation vector field
\boldsymbol{m}
on the smaller domain needs to be extended by zero to the larger domain, potentially creating discontinuity in the coefficients of the drift-diffusion equation (cf.
[
15
, Remark 3.1]
). This causes substantial difficulties in the theoretical and numerical analysis, and will be a subject for future research.

To summarise, the main contributions of this paper include:

(1)

proving the existence and uniqueness of a global strong solution to the SDLLB system with small initial data (or small spin polarisation parameters) in spatial dimensions
d\leq 3
(Theorem
3.10
),

(2)

proposing a linear, decoupled, fully-discrete finite element method for solving the SDLLB system (Algorithm
4.1
),

(3)

establishing an error estimate for the proposed numerical scheme under a suitable time-step restriction and assumptions on the norm of the initial data (Theorem
4.12
).

The paper is organised as follows:

•

Section
2
discusses the model in more detail, and gathers the notations and auxiliary results used throughout the paper;

•

Section
3
establishes the global well-posedness for the SDLLB system;

•

Section
4
presents and analyses the fully decoupled finite element approximation of the SDLLB system;

•

Section
5
contains numerical experiments that verify the theoretical convergence rates of the proposed numerical scheme.

Preliminaries

2.1.
Notations

We begin by defining some notations used in this paper. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a domain. The function space
\mathbb{L}^{p}:=\mathbb{L}^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
denotes the space of
p
-th integrable functions taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\mathbb{W}^{k,p}:=\mathbb{W}^{k,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
denotes the Sobolev space of
functions on
\Omega
, taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. As usual,
\mathbb{H}^{k}(\Omega):=\mathbb{W}^{k,2}(\Omega)
. The space
\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{-1}(\Omega)
denotes the dual of
\mathbb{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, with the duality pairing defined as the extension of the
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
inner product. For brevity, we write
\mathbb{L}^{p}
,
\mathbb{W}^{k,p}
, or
\mathbb{H}^{k}
in lieu of
\mathbb{L}^{p}(\Omega)
,
\mathbb{W}^{k,p}(\Omega)
, or
\mathbb{H}^{k}(\Omega)
respectively. Denote
\Omega_{T}:=(0,T)\times\Omega
. Let
\Delta
be the Neumann Laplacian operator acting on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
-valued functions with domain

\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\boldsymbol{n}}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}:\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}=\boldsymbol{0}\text{ on }\partial{\Omega}\right\}.

For a Banach space
\mathbb{X}
, the spaces
L^{p}(0,T;\mathbb{X})
and
W^{k,p}(0,T;\mathbb{X})
denote respectively the Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces of functions on
(0,T)
taking values in
\mathbb{X}
. The space
C([0,T];\mathbb{X})
denotes the space of continuous function on
[0,T]
taking values in
\mathbb{X}
. For simplicity, we will write

L^{p}_{T}(\mathbb{X}):=L^{p}(0,T;\mathbb{X})\quad\text{and}\quad W^{k,p}_{T}(\mathbb{X}):=W^{k,p}(0,T;\mathbb{X}).

We do not distinguish between the scalar product of
\mathbb{L}^{2}
vector-valued functions taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the scalar product of
\mathbb{L}^{2}
matrix-valued functions taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
, and denote them both by
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
.

Finally, the constant
C
in the estimate denotes a
generic constant which may take different values at different occurrences. If
the dependence of
C
on a variable, e.g.
T
, is emphasised, we will write

C_{T}
. The notation
a\lesssim b
means
a\leq Cb
for some constant
C
.

2.2.
The model

We now provide a more detailed discussion of the SDLLB model under consideration.
Recall that
\boldsymbol{m}
and
\boldsymbol{s}
represent the local magnetisation and the spin accumulation, respectively. The vector field
\boldsymbol{H}:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the effective field, which is defined as the negative variational derivative of the micromagnetic energy
\mathcal{E}
, i.e.

\boldsymbol{H}[\boldsymbol{m}]:=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{m}}\mathcal{E}.

The time evolution of the magnetisation vector field at elevated temperatures can be described by the LLB equation
[
13
,
19
]
:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}=-\gamma\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{H}+\frac{\gamma\alpha_{\parallel}}{|\boldsymbol{m}|^{2}}\,(\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{H})\boldsymbol{m}-\frac{\gamma\alpha_{\perp}}{|\boldsymbol{m}|^{2}}\,\boldsymbol{m}\times(\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{H}),

where
\alpha_{\parallel}
and
\alpha_{\perp}
are, respectively, the longitudinal and transverse damping parameters, and
\gamma
is the gyromagnetic ratio. The damping parameters
\alpha_{\parallel}
and
\alpha_{\perp}
are related to the temperature
\Theta
, the Curie temperature
T_{\mathrm{c}}
, and the Gilbert damping parameter
\alpha_{G}
in the standard Landau–Lifshitz equation via

(2.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\alpha_{\parallel}=\frac{2\alpha_{G}\Theta}{3T_{\mathrm{c}}},\quad\alpha_{\perp}=\alpha_{G}\left(1-\frac{\Theta}{3T_{\mathrm{c}}}\right),&\text{ if $\Theta<T_{\mathrm{c}}$},\\[4.30554pt]
\alpha_{\parallel}=\alpha_{\perp}=\frac{2\alpha_{G}}{3},&\text{ if $\Theta\geq T_{\mathrm{c}}$}.\end{cases}

In contrast to the LLG equation, equation (
2.2
) does not preserve the magnitude of the magnetisation vector in general.
However, note that at zero kelvin,
\alpha_{\parallel}=0
and
\alpha_{\perp}=\alpha_{G}
, thus equation (
2.1
) reduces to

(2.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}=-\gamma\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{H}-\frac{\gamma\alpha_{G}}{|\boldsymbol{m}|^{2}}\,\boldsymbol{m}\times(\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{H}).

Formally taking the inner product of (
2.3
) with
\boldsymbol{m}
shows that
\left|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right|
is a constant in this special case, reducing (
2.3
) to the standard Landau–Lifshitz equation.
In the high-temperature regime, noting that
\alpha_{\parallel}=\alpha_{\perp}=2\alpha_{G}/3
for
\Theta\geq T_{\mathrm{c}}
from (
2.2
), by the vector triple product identity the LLB equation simplifies to

(2.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}=-\gamma\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{H}+\alpha\boldsymbol{H},

where
\alpha:=2\gamma\alpha_{G}/{3}
. This is the form of the LLB equation we are considering in this paper. A derivation of (
2.1
) and (
2.4
) within the continuum thermodynamic framework can be found in
[
9
]
.

In the presence of an electric current, the local magnetisation experiences a torque due to the spin accumulation
\boldsymbol{s}
, whose dynamics is described by a nonlinear drift–diffusion equation
[
39
,
49
]
. Following
[
10
,
49
,
48
]
, the coupling between the magnetisation and spin accumulation, with strength
\delta>0
, can be introduced by appending the term
\delta\boldsymbol{s}
to the effective field
\boldsymbol{H}
in the LLB magnetisation equation (
2.4
). This coupling generates additional torques in (
2.4
): a transverse (precessional) torque
-\gamma\delta\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{s}
and a longitudinal torque
\alpha\delta\boldsymbol{s}
.

For analytical tractability, we consider a simplified model in the regime above the Curie temperature. In this regime, strong thermal agitation reduces the efficiency of a spin-polarised current in generating a net spin accumulation, resulting in a relatively small effective coupling
\delta
. Moreover, spin relaxation rates increase with temperature due to enhanced scattering, causing
\boldsymbol{s}
to decay rapidly and remain small compared with other contributions to the effective field
[
10
]
. Since
\alpha_{G}\ll 1

[
50
]
and
\delta
is small, the longitudinal torque
\alpha\delta\boldsymbol{s}
is therefore negligible relative to the damping term
\alpha\boldsymbol{H}
or the precessional torque
\gamma\delta\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{s}
, and it is dropped in our model. This leads to the SDLLB system (
1.1
) studied here, which coincides with the model in
[
25
]
.

2.3.
Problem formulations

Let
{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded smooth or a convex polytopal domain. The following standing assumptions are used throughout the paper:

(i)

the function
\boldsymbol{j}:\Omega_{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is given such that
\boldsymbol{j}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})
and
\boldsymbol{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
on
\partial\Omega
;

(ii)

the function
D_{0}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is given such that
D_{0}
is bounded above and below by positive constants, i.e. there exist positive constants
D_{\ast}
and
D^{\ast}
such that
D_{\ast}\leq D_{0}\leq D^{\ast}
.

Further regularity assumptions will be introduced as necessary in the corresponding theorems. In the analysis, we always assume
d=3
for ease of presentation, noting that similar argument holds for
d\leq 2
. We introduce the following notions of solution to (
1.1
).

Definition 2.1

(weak solution)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{m}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{1}
and
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{1}
be given. A
weak solution
to (
1.1
) is a pair
(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{s})
, where
\boldsymbol{m}:\Omega_{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\boldsymbol{s}:\Omega_{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
satisfy:

(i)

\boldsymbol{m}\in H^{1}(0,T;\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{-1})\cap C([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbb{L}^{\infty})\cap L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{H}^{1})
;

(ii)

\boldsymbol{s}\in H^{1}(0,T;\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}^{-1})\cap C([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2})\cap L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{H}^{1})
;

(iii)

\boldsymbol{m}(0)=\boldsymbol{m}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{s}(0)=\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
;

(iv)

for almost all
t\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\gamma\alpha^{\prime}\left\langle\boldsymbol{m}\times\nabla\boldsymbol{m},\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\rangle-\alpha\alpha^{\prime}\left\langle\nabla\boldsymbol{m},\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\rangle-\alpha\kappa\mu\left\langle\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\rangle

(2.5)

\displaystyle\quad-\alpha\kappa\left\langle|\boldsymbol{m}|^{2}\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\rangle-\gamma^{\prime}\left\langle\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\rangle,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathbb{H}^{1},

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{\psi}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\beta^{\prime}\left\langle\boldsymbol{m}\otimes\boldsymbol{j},\nabla\boldsymbol{\psi}\right\rangle-\left\langle D_{0}\nabla\boldsymbol{s},\nabla\boldsymbol{\psi}\right\rangle+\beta\left\langle D_{0}(\boldsymbol{m}\otimes\boldsymbol{m})\nabla\boldsymbol{s},\nabla\boldsymbol{\psi}\right\rangle

(2.6)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{sf}}}\left\langle D_{0}\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{\psi}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{J}}}\left\langle D_{0}\boldsymbol{s}\times\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{\psi}\right\rangle,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathbb{H}^{1};

A weak solution is local if the solution exists only for sufficiently small
T
. It is global if the solution exists for arbitrary positive
T
.

Definition 2.2

(strong solution)
.

A
strong solution

(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{s})
to (
1.1
) is a weak solution with additional regularity:

(i)

\boldsymbol{m}\in H^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{H}^{1})\cap C([0,T];\mathbb{H}^{2})\cap L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{H}^{3})
;

(ii)

\boldsymbol{s}\in H^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{H}^{1})\cap C([0,T];\mathbb{H}^{2})\cap L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{H}^{3})
.

In this case,
(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{s})
satisfies (
1.1
) for almost every
(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in\Omega_{T}
.

The above definition of weak solution is in the spirit of
[
4
,
28
]
, noting that
\boldsymbol{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
on
\partial\Omega
by assumption.
Strong solution of the SDLLB system satisfies the following energy equality for all
t\in(0,T)
:

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{m}(t))+\alpha\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{H}(\tau)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{m}_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\gamma^{\prime}\left\langle\boldsymbol{m}(\tau)\times\boldsymbol{s}(\tau),\boldsymbol{H}(\tau)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\tau,

where
\mathcal{E}
is the micromagnetic energy functional defined by (
1.2
).
The energy equality (
2.7
) can be motivated as follows: Formally taking the inner product of (
1.1a
) with
\boldsymbol{H}
, and of (
1.1b
) with
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}
, then subtracting the resulting equations, we obtain

(2.8)

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{4}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}^{4}+\kappa\mu\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\alpha\left\|{\boldsymbol{H}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}=\gamma^{\prime}\left\langle\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{H}\right\rangle.

Integrating this over
(0,t)
then yields (
2.7
).

For ease of presentation, in the analysis we set all numerical coefficients in (
iv
) and (
iv
), except for
\beta
, to 1.

2.4.
Finite elements

Let
\big\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\big\}_{h>0}
be a family of shape-regular and quasi-uniform triangulations of
{\Omega}
into triangles or tetrahedra with maximal mesh-size
h
.
Next, we introduce the conforming finite element space
\mathbb{V}_{h}\subset\mathbb{H}^{1}
given by

(2.9)

\mathbb{V}_{h}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{C}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3}):\left.\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h}\right|_{K}\in\cal{P}_{r}(K;\mathbb{R}^{3}),\;\forall K\in\cal{T}_{h}\right\},

where
\cal{P}_{r}(K;\mathbb{R}^{3})
denotes the space of polynomials of degree at most
r
on
K
taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
For a technical reason (see Lemma
4.7
), we assume that in (
2.9
) the polynomial degree
r
in
\mathbb{V}_{h}
is:

(2.10)

\begin{cases}r\geq 1,&\text{if }d\in\{1,2\},\\
r\geq 2,&\text{if }d=3.\end{cases}

Due to the regularity of the triangulation, we have the following best approximation property: for
p\in[1,\infty]
, there exists a constant
C
independent of
h
such that for any
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{W}^{r+1,p}
,

(2.11)

\displaystyle\inf_{\chi\in{\mathbb{V}}_{h}}\big\{\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\chi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}+h\left\|{\nabla(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\chi})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}\big\}\leq Ch^{r+1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{r+1,p}}.

In the analysis, we will use the finite element projection operator
P_{h}:\mathbb{L}^{2}\to\mathbb{V}_{h}
defined by

(2.12)

\displaystyle\left\langle P_{h}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\chi}\right\rangle=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\chi}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}.

Let
p\in[1,\infty]
. The projector
P_{h}
satisfies the following boundedness and approximation properties
[
14
,
17
]
:

(2.13)

\displaystyle\left\|{P_{h}\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}},

(2.14)

\displaystyle\left\|{P_{h}\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,p}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,p}},

(2.15)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}-P_{h}(\boldsymbol{v})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+h\left\|{\nabla\big(\boldsymbol{v}-P_{h}(\boldsymbol{v})\big)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{r+1}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{r+1}}.

Other projection operators used in this paper will be introduced and analysed in Section
4
.

Finally, the following inverse estimates are well-known: there exist constants
C_{\textrm{inv}}
and
\widetilde{C}_{\textrm{inv}}
(which depend on the regularity of the triangulation, but is independent of
h
) such that

(2.16)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\textrm{inv}}h^{-d/2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{V}_{h},

(2.17)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{C}_{\textrm{inv}}\ell_{h}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{V}_{h},

where

(2.18)

\ell_{h}:=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }d=1,\\
\left|{\log h}\right|^{1/2},&\text{if }d=2,\\
h^{-1/2},&\text{if }d=3.\end{cases}

The inverse estimates hold under the quasi-uniformity assumption on the triangulation.

3.
The existence and uniqueness of global strong solution

To establish the global existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
1.1
), one could apply the Faedo–Galerkin method: derive suitable uniform estimates for the approximate solutions and apply weak compactness argument to extract a subsequence which solves the problem. However, in order to streamline the presentation, we will work directly (and formally) with smooth solution
(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{s})
of problem (
1.1
) in lieu of its Faedo–Galerkin approximations. The formal a priori estimates presented below can be rigorously justified by appealing to the Galerkin approximation framework, following the approach in
[
28
,
29
]
. Uniqueness of strong solutions (in the sense of Definition
2.2
) will be shown by an energy-based argument.

The main result of this section is stated in Theorem
3.10
. For clarity of exposition, we recall that all numerical coefficients in (
1.1
) have been set to 1, except for
\beta
. We begin with a lemma that demonstrates the exponential decay of the
\mathbb{L}^{p}
norm of the solution
\boldsymbol{m}
. This decay corresponds to the eventual loss of magnetisation above the Curie temperature (see also Remark
3.4
, and
[
5
,
27
,
29
]
for supporting evidence).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{s})
be a smooth solution of (
1.1
) with initial data
\boldsymbol{m}_{0}\in\mathbb{L}^{p}
, where
p\in[2,\infty]
. Then for all
t\in[0,T]
,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}.

Moreover, for
p\in[2,\infty)
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{|\boldsymbol{m}(\tau)|^{\frac{p-2}{2}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{m}(\tau)|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq C,

where
C
depends on the coefficients of the equation and
\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}
.

Proof.

Taking the inner product of (
1.1a
) with
|\boldsymbol{m}|^{p-2}\boldsymbol{m}
for
p\geq 2
, and noting (
1.1b
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}^{p}+\left\langle\nabla\boldsymbol{m},\nabla(\left|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right|^{p-2}\boldsymbol{m})\right\rangle+\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}^{p}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p+2}}^{p+2}=0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}^{p}

\displaystyle+\left\|{|\boldsymbol{m}|^{\frac{p-2}{2}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{m}|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+(p-2)\left\|{|\boldsymbol{m}|^{\frac{p-4}{2}}|\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{m}|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}^{p}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p+2}}^{p+2}=0.

Integrating this over
(0,t)
gives (
3.2
). Furthermore, dropping irrelevant terms, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}^{p}+p\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}^{p}\leq 0,

so that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big(e^{pt}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}}^{p}\Big)\leq 0.

Integrating over
(0,t)
and taking
p
-th root show (
3.1
) for
p\in[2,\infty)
. Letting
p\to\infty
in (
3.1
) completes the proof for the case
p=\infty
.
∎

In the next lemma, we assume a certain smallness condition on
\beta
or
\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}
to derive a uniform estimate for
\boldsymbol{s}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{s})
be a smooth solution of (
1.1
) with initial data
\boldsymbol{m}_{0}\in\mathbb{L}^{\infty}
and
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}
, such that
\beta D^{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}<D_{\ast}
. Then for all
t\in[0,T]
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{s}(\tau)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau\leq C,

where the constant
C
depends on the coefficients of the equation,
\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}
, and
\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}
. Furthermore, we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle{\lim_{t\to\infty}\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}=0.}

Proof.

Taking the inner product of (
1.1c
) with
\boldsymbol{s}
, noting (
1.1d
) and our standing assumptions, gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\langle D_{0}\nabla\boldsymbol{s},\nabla\boldsymbol{s}\right\rangle+\left\langle D_{0}\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}\right\rangle=\left\langle\boldsymbol{m}\otimes\boldsymbol{j},\nabla\boldsymbol{s}\right\rangle+\beta\left\langle D_{0}(\boldsymbol{m}\otimes\boldsymbol{m})\nabla\boldsymbol{s},\nabla\boldsymbol{s}\right\rangle

Noting the assumption on
D_{0}
, by Young’s inequality we obtain for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+D_{\ast}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{s}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+D_{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\boldsymbol{j}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}+\beta D^{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{s}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{s}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

(3.5)

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{-2t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}+\beta D^{\ast}e^{-2t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{s}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{s}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2},

where in the last step we used the assumption on
\boldsymbol{j}
and (
3.1
). Now, if
\beta D^{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}<D_{\ast}
, then noting that
e^{-2t}\leq 1
and choosing
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small we can absorb the last two terms on the right-hand side. Integrating over
(0,t)
then yields (
3.3
).

Furthermore, (
3
) and the assumption
\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}<D_{\ast}/(\beta D^{\ast})
imply

(3.6)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2D_{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{Ce^{-2t}D_{\ast}}{\beta D^{\ast}}.

By a variant of the Gronwall inequality
[
35
, Theorem 2.1.5]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}e^{-2D_{\ast}t}+\int_{0}^{t}Ce^{-2D_{\ast}(t-\tau)}\frac{e^{-2\tau}D_{\ast}}{\beta D^{\ast}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}e^{-2D_{\ast}t}+\frac{CD_{\ast}}{\beta D^{\ast}(2D_{\ast}-2)}\left(e^{-2t}-e^{-2D_{\ast}t}\right).

In particular, we have (
3.4
). This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Further estimates on the norms of spatial derivatives of
\boldsymbol{m}
are derived in the following lemmas.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{s})
be a smooth solution of (
1.1
) with initial data
\boldsymbol{m}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{1}\cap\mathbb{L}^{\infty}
and
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}
, such that
\beta D^{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}<D_{\ast}
. Then for all

,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{m}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{m}(\tau)}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau\leq C,

where the constant
C
depends on the coefficients of the equation,
\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}\cap\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}
, and
\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}
.

Proof.

Taking the inner product of (
1.1a
) with
-\Delta\boldsymbol{m}
and integrating by parts, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{|\boldsymbol{m}||\nabla\boldsymbol{m}|}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{s},\Delta\boldsymbol{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Integrating both sides over
(0,t)
, noting (
3.1
) and (
3.3
), we obtain the required result.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Physically, (
3.1
) and (
3.4
) indicate that above the Curie temperature, both the local magnetisation and the spin accumulation decay to zero in a certain sense. Under the LLB framework considered here,
\left|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right|
can vary in space and time since
\boldsymbol{m}
represents the
local magnetisation
(or macrospin) at high temperatures. Indeed, above the Curie temperature,
\boldsymbol{m}(t)
even decays exponentially to zero. We emphasise that this does not mean that the individual
magnetic spin
vectors vanish (they are still of unit length), but rather that they become sufficiently disordered so that the averaged (or local) magnetisation
\boldsymbol{m}
at any point tends to zero, corresponding to the loss of macroscopic magnetisation above the Curie point
[
5
,
27
]
. The loss of magnetisation and the enhanced scattering weaken the coupling between
\boldsymbol{m}
and
\boldsymbol{s}
in such a way that spin relaxation mechanisms dominate over spin current injection, resulting in an eventual decay of
\boldsymbol{s}
to zero as well
[
10
]
.

Remark 3.5
.

We comment on the physical plausibility of the assumption
\beta D^{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}<D_{\ast}
used in Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
, and later in Theorem
3.10
to establish the existence of a global weak solution. In the SDLLG system, a similar smallness condition on
\beta
is typically imposed. Notably, if
\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}=1
, then our assumption simplifies to
\beta<D_{\ast}/D^{\ast}
, which is consistent with the condition intended in
[
1
]
. We note that the statement of
[
1
, Lemma 5]
contains a minor inaccuracy, but once corrected, the resulting constraint for parabolicity reduces precisely to
\beta<D_{\ast}/D^{\ast}
in our notation. If moreover
D_{0}
is constant in space, then this assumption reduces to
\beta\in(0,1)
.
Equivalently, for a given
\beta\in(0,1)
, the assumption holds if
\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}<D_{\ast}/(\beta D^{\ast})
, which can occur naturally in a sufficiently ‘weak’ ferromagnet (small local magnetisation almost everywhere) or at a sufficiently high temperature.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{s})

where in the last step we used Young’s inequality. Rearranging the terms, we obtain

(3.22)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Next, taking the inner product of (
3
) with
\boldsymbol{r}
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\langle D_{0}\nabla\boldsymbol{r},\nabla\boldsymbol{r}\right\rangle+\left\langle D_{0}\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{j}),\boldsymbol{r}\right\rangle-\left\langle D_{0}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{r}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\beta\left\langle D_{0}(\boldsymbol{m}\otimes\boldsymbol{m})\nabla\boldsymbol{r},\nabla\boldsymbol{r}\right\rangle+\beta\left\langle D_{0}(\boldsymbol{m}\otimes\boldsymbol{u})\nabla\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\nabla\boldsymbol{r}\right\rangle+\beta\left\langle D_{0}(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime})\nabla\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\nabla\boldsymbol{r}\right\rangle

(3.23)

\displaystyle\quad=:I_{1}+I_{2}+\cdots+I_{5}.

We estimate each term in the following. Firstly, by Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities,

\displaystyle\left|{I_{1}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{j}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbb{L}^{\infty})}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{j}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbb{W}^{1,4})}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}+C\left\|{\boldsymbol{j}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2},

where in the last step we used the Sobolev embedding
\mathbb{H}^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{W}^{1,4}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{L}^{\infty}
. For the second term, similarly we have

\displaystyle\left|{I_{2}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|{D_{0}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}D^{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

For the term
I_{3}
, by the same argument we obtain

\displaystyle\left|{I_{3}}\right|\leq\beta D^{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq\beta D^{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2},

where in the last step we used (
3.1
). For the terms
I_{4}
and
I_{5}
, by Young’s inequality and (
3.1
) again, we have for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\left|{I_{4}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\beta\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\beta D^{\ast}}{4\epsilon}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle\left|{I_{5}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\beta\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\beta D^{\ast}}{4\epsilon}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Altogether, continuing from (
3
) we have for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+D_{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}D^{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\big(\beta D^{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}+2\epsilon\beta\big)\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\beta D^{\ast}}{4\epsilon}\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\right)\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Now, by the assumption (
3.8
), since
\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}<D_{\ast}/(\beta D^{\ast})
, we can choose

\epsilon=\frac{1}{4\beta}\left(D_{\ast}-\beta D^{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\right)>0,

to absorb the term containing
\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}
to the left-hand side. This yields

(3.24)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq C\left\|{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}+C\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Adding (
3.22
) and (
3.24
), and rearranging the terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right)\leq C\left(1+\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,\infty}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,\infty}}^{2}\right)\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right).

Noting the regularity of the strong solution, by the Gronwall lemma and the Sobolev embedding
\mathbb{H}^{3}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{W}^{1,\infty}
, we have the continuous dependence estimate

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq C\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{r}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right),

where
C
depends on
T
,
\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
, and
\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
. This concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

4.
Finite element approximation

Let
\mathbb{V}_{h}
be the finite element space in (
2.9
), and let
k
be the time-step size. Let
(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n})\in\mathbb{V}_{h}\times\mathbb{V}_{h}
be the numerical approximation of
(\boldsymbol{m}(t_{n}),\boldsymbol{s}(t_{n}))
, where
t_{n}:=nk\in[0,T]
and
n\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
, and
N:=\lfloor T/k\rfloor
.
We denote
\boldsymbol{v}^{n}:=\boldsymbol{v}(t_{n})
. For any discrete function
\boldsymbol{v}
, define for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{t}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}:=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}^{n}-\boldsymbol{v}^{n-1}}{k}.

Let
(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{0},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{0})
be a suitable approximation of the initial data. For ease of presentation, suppose that
\boldsymbol{j}\in C\big([0,T];\mathbb{H}^{1}\big)
so that the expression
\boldsymbol{j}^{n}
are well-defined for
n\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
. It is possible to replace
\boldsymbol{j}^{n}
by a numerical approximation
\boldsymbol{j}_{h}^{n}
without changing the analysis significantly.
A fully discrete linearised finite element scheme to solve (
1.1
) can be described as follows.

Algorithm 4.1
.

Input:
(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{0},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{0}),\{\boldsymbol{j}^{n}\}_{0\leq n\leq N}
.

For
n=1,2,\ldots,N
, iterate:

(1)

compute
\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathrm{d}_{t}\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1}\times\nabla\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n},\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h}\right\rangle-\left\langle\nabla\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n},\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h}\right\rangle-\left\langle\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h}\right\rangle

(4.1)

\displaystyle\quad-\left\langle|\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1}|^{2}\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h}\right\rangle-\left\langle\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n}\times\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n-1},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h}\right\rangle,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h};

(2)

compute
\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathrm{d}_{t}\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1}\otimes\boldsymbol{j}^{n},\nabla\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\right\rangle-\left\langle D_{0}\nabla\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n},\nabla\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\right\rangle-\left\langle D_{0}\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\right\rangle

(4.2)

\displaystyle\quad+\beta\left\langle D_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1}\otimes\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1}\big)\nabla\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n},\nabla\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\right\rangle-\left\langle D_{0}\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n}\times\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\right\rangle,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h};

Output: a sequence of discrete functions
\big\{(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n})\big\}_{1\leq n\leq N}
.

We remark that while (
1.1
) is a nonlinearly coupled system of quasilinear PDEs, the above scheme only requires solving two completely decoupled linear systems per time-step. Note that, in contrast to the scheme presented in
[
1
]
for the SDLLG equation which solves for
\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n}
and then
\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n}
sequentially, Algorithm
4.1
for the SDLLB equation allows
\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n}
to be computed
in parallel
, owing to the explicit treatment of
\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1}
in (
2
).
This decoupling provides a significant computational advantage, particularly in large-scale simulations or parallel computing environments.

To derive an error estimate in this section, for simplicity we take
(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{0},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{0})=(P_{h}\boldsymbol{m}_{0},P_{h}\boldsymbol{s}_{0})
, where
P_{h}
is the
\mathbb{L}^{2}
projection operator defined in (
2.12
). Note that other initial inputs are possible as long as they approximate the initial data with sufficient accuracy. We further assume adequate regularity for the exact solution
(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{s})
to (
1.1
) to derive an optimal order of convergence, namely

(4.3)

\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{s}\in L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbb{H}^{3})\cap W^{1,\infty}_{T}(\mathbb{H}^{r+1})\cap W^{2,\infty}_{T}(\mathbb{L}^{2}),

where
r
is the degree of polynomials used in
\mathbb{V}_{h}
.

We now define several bilinear forms and discuss their properties to facilitate subsequent analysis.

Definition 4.2
.

Given
\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathbb{H}^{1}\cap\mathbb{L}^{\infty}
and
\beta>0
, together with
D_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{j}
satisfying the assumptions stated in Section
2.3
, we define bilinear forms on
\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{H}^{1}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{1}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle:=\left\langle\nabla\boldsymbol{v},\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle+\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle:=-\left\langle\boldsymbol{\phi}\times\nabla\boldsymbol{v},\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\psi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle:=\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi})\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle:=-\left\langle\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{2}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle:=\left\langle D_{0}\nabla\boldsymbol{v},\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle+\left\langle D_{0}\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle:=\left\langle D_{0}\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\psi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle:=-\beta\left\langle D_{0}(\boldsymbol{\phi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\psi})\nabla\boldsymbol{v},\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle:={-\left\langle\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{\phi},\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle.}

Furthermore, let

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}):=\mathcal{D}_{i}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})+\mathcal{C}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})+\mathcal{B}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}),\text{ for }i=1,2.

With the above notations, the weak formulations (
iv
) and (
iv
) for the continuous problem now read as:

(4.5)

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right\rangle+\mathcal{A}_{1}(\boldsymbol{m};\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{\phi})+\mathcal{L}_{1}(\boldsymbol{s};\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathbb{H}^{1},

(4.6)

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{\psi}\right\rangle+\mathcal{A}_{2}(\boldsymbol{m};\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{\psi})+\mathcal{L}_{2}(\boldsymbol{j};\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{\psi})

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathbb{H}^{1}.

The weak formulations (
1
) and (
2
) for the discrete scheme can be written as:

(4.7)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathrm{d}_{t}\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h}\right\rangle+\mathcal{A}_{1}(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1};\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h})+\mathcal{L}_{1}(\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n-1};\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h})

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h},

(4.8)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathrm{d}_{t}\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\right\rangle+\mathcal{A}_{2}(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1};\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h})+\mathcal{L}_{2}(\boldsymbol{j}^{n};\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h})

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}.

We collect some important estimates on the previously defined bilinear forms in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

The following statements hold true for the bilinear forms defined in Definition
4.2
:

(i)

\mathcal{A}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{\cdot},\boldsymbol{\cdot})
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{\cdot},\boldsymbol{\cdot})
are bounded, i.e. there exist constants
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}>0
depending only on the coefficients of the problem (
1.1
),
\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}
, and
\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}
, such that

(4.9)

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{A}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{H}^{1},

(4.10)

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{A}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{H}^{1};

(ii)

\mathcal{A}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{\cdot},\boldsymbol{\cdot})
is coercive, i.e. there exist a constant
\mu_{1}>0

independent
of
\boldsymbol{\phi}
, such that

(4.11)

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{A}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}^{1};

(iii)

If
\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\leq D_{\ast}/(2\beta D^{\ast})
, then
\mathcal{A}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{\cdot},\boldsymbol{\cdot})
is also coercive, i.e. there exist a constant
\mu_{2}>0

independent
of
\boldsymbol{\phi}
, such that

(4.12)

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{A}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}^{1};

(iv)

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathbb{W}^{1,4}
,
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}^{1}
, and
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\boldsymbol{n}}
,

(4.13)

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}},

(4.14)

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\Delta\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{w})\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}};

(v)

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
D_{0}\in W^{1,4}(\Omega)
,
\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathbb{W}^{1,4}
,
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}^{1}
, and
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\boldsymbol{n}}
,

(4.15)

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{B}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\psi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\right)\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}};

(vi)

For any
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathbb{L}^{\infty}
,
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}
, and
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{H}^{1}
,

(4.16)

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

Proof.

Inequalities (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) follow immediately by Hölder’s inequality, while inequality (
4.11
) is obvious. Finally, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\left\langle D_{0}\nabla\boldsymbol{v},\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle+\left\langle D_{0}\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle-\beta\left\langle D_{0}(\boldsymbol{\phi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\phi})\nabla\boldsymbol{v},\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq D_{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\big(D_{\ast}-\beta D^{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\big)\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\geq\mu_{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2},

where
\mu_{2}:=D_{\ast}/2
, thus showing (
4.12
).
Next, we show (
4.13
) and (
4.14
). Integrating by parts, we infer that

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\boldsymbol{\phi}\times\boldsymbol{v},\Delta\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi}\times\boldsymbol{v},\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle.

By Hölder’s inequality, we then have

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}},

from which (
4.13
) follows by the embeddings
\mathbb{H}^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{W}^{1,4}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{L}^{\infty}
.
Inequality (
4.14
) follows by similar argument.
Next, using integration by parts, we also have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\psi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})=\beta\left\langle D_{0}(\boldsymbol{\phi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\psi})\boldsymbol{v},\Delta\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle+\beta\left\langle\boldsymbol{v}\nabla\big(D_{0}(\boldsymbol{\phi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\psi})\big)^{\top},\nabla\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle.

Therefore, by Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{B}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\psi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\beta D^{\ast}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\beta\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{D_{0}(\boldsymbol{\phi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{4}},

which implies (
4.15
) by the assumptions and the embeddings
\mathbb{H}^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{W}^{1,4}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{L}^{\infty}
.
Finally, by Hölder’s inequality we have

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},

showing (
4.16
), thus completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

To aid in the analysis, we next introduce the following elliptic projection operators, a technique originating in
[
46
]
.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
t\in[0,T]
. Let
(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{s})
be a strong solution to (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
2.2
, with initial data
(\boldsymbol{m}_{0},\boldsymbol{s}_{0})
satisfying

(4.17)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\leq D_{\ast}/(2\beta D^{\ast}).

The
\mathcal{A}_{1}
-elliptic projection operator
\Pi_{h}:=\Pi_{h,\boldsymbol{m}(t)}:\mathbb{H}^{1}\to\mathbb{V}_{h}
is defined by

(4.18)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}\mapsto\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{v}\quad\text{satisfying}\quad\mathcal{A}_{1}(\boldsymbol{m}(t);\boldsymbol{v}-\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h})=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}.

The
\mathcal{A}_{2}
-elliptic projection operator
\Lambda_{h}:=\Lambda_{h,\boldsymbol{m}(t)}:\mathbb{H}^{1}\to\mathbb{V}_{h}
is defined by

(4.19)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}\mapsto\Lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{v}\quad\text{satisfying}\quad\mathcal{A}_{2}(\boldsymbol{m}(t);\boldsymbol{v}-\Lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h})=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}.

Note that the bilinear forms defining
\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{v}
and
\Lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{v}
in (
4.18
) and (
4.19
) depend on the given exact solution
(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{s})
of the problem (
1.1
). In light of the regularity assumption on
(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{s})
, estimate (
3.19
) and the assumption (
4.17
), as well as the coercivity and boundedness properties (
4.9
), (
4.10
), (
4.11
), and (
4.12
), the elliptic projections are well-defined by the Lax–Milgram theorem.

Next, for any
\boldsymbol{v}\in L_{T}^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{1})

Lemma 4.6

(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{s})
is a strong solution of (

(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{s})
be a given strong solution with initial data satisfying (
4.17
). The existence of
\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)\in\mathbb{H}^{1}
satisfying (
4.42
) and of

4.43

\mathcal{A}_{2}

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}\leq C\left\|{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

Similarly, making use of the coercivity of

\displaystyle\left\|{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}\leq C\left\|{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}.

Next, we establish (
4.44

4.45

\displaystyle\leq\left|{\mathcal{B}_{1}(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{m};\Delta\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right|+\left|{\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{m};\Delta\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right|+\left|{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\Delta\boldsymbol{\psi}\right\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle\quad+C\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,4}}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\Delta\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

where we in the last step we also used the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality, Sobolev embedding, and Young’s inequality. This, together with (

) and the elliptic regularity result

]
, implies (
4.44

(thus also of Proposition

).

We now proceed with the analysis of numerical scheme proposed in Algorithm (
4.1
).
To this end, the approximation errors are decomposed as:

(4.51)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n}-\boldsymbol{m}^{n}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n}-\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{m}^{n})+(\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{m}^{n}-\boldsymbol{m}^{n})=:\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}+\boldsymbol{\rho}^{n},

(4.52)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n}-\boldsymbol{s}^{n}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n}-\Lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{s}^{n})+(\Lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{s}^{n}-\boldsymbol{s}^{n})=:\boldsymbol{\xi}^{n}+\boldsymbol{\eta}^{n},

where
\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{m}^{n}
and
\Lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{s}^{n}
are, respectively, the
\mathcal{A}_{1}
-elliptic projection of the solution
\boldsymbol{m}(t_{n})
and the
\mathcal{A}_{2}
-elliptic projection of the solution
\boldsymbol{s}(t_{n})
introduced in Definition
4.4
.

The error analysis proceeds by induction, showing the existence of solution and then the error estimate alternatingly. We outline the general strategy now. First, we show a criterion for the unique existence of
(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n})
in terms of the numerical solution at the previous time-step, namely:

(4.53)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}<D_{\ast}/(2\beta D^{\ast}).

Next, we prove that if
(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{j},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{j})
exists for all
j\leq n
for some
n\leq N
, then these discrete solutions necessarily satisfy a certain stability property for all
j\leq n
. Under the assumption that the criterion (
4.53
) holds (and thus
(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n})
exists), we derive auxiliary error estimates for
\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n}-\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{m}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n}-\Lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{s}^{n}
. These auxiliary error estimates are then used to prove, under a suitable restriction on the time-step size, that
\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}<D_{\ast}/(2\beta D^{\ast})
.
This guarantees the unique existence of
(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n+1})
. Proceeding inductively, we thus establish the unique existence of
(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{j},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{j})
along with the corresponding auxiliary error estimates for all
j\leq N
. Finally, these auxiliary estimates, together with the error decompositions in (
4.51
) and (
4.52
), and the projection error estimates in Proposition
4.5
yield the desired full error estimates.

We now proceed to implement this strategy, starting with the following proposition.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n-1})\in\mathbb{V}_{h}\times\mathbb{V}_{h}
be given such that condition (

) holds.

(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n})\in\mathbb{V}_{h}\times\mathbb{V}_{h}
to the numerical scheme at time step

, as described in Algorithm
4.1

Proof.

Given
(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n-1})
and
\boldsymbol{j}^{n}
, our numerical scheme is seeking for
(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathbb{V}_{h}\times\mathbb{V}_{h}
which satisfies

\displaystyle\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h}\right\rangle+k\mathcal{A}_{1}(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h})+k\mathcal{L}_{1}(\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n-1};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h})=\left\langle\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h}\right\rangle,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\phi}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h},

\displaystyle\left\langle\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\right\rangle+k\mathcal{A}_{2}(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1};\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h})=\left\langle\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n-1},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\right\rangle-k\mathcal{L}_{2}(\boldsymbol{j}^{n};\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}),\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}.

It is clear that for any
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}

\displaystyle\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle+k\mathcal{A}_{1}(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})+k\mathcal{L}_{1}(\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n-1};\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\geq\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}.

Moreover, if (
4.53
) holds, then for any
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}

\displaystyle\left\langle\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle+k\mathcal{A}_{2}(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n-1};\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{w})\geq(1+kD_{\ast})\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+k(D_{\ast}-\beta D^{\ast}\delta)\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\gtrsim\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}^{2}.

) and (

The existence of a unique solution
(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n})\in\mathbb{V}_{h}\times\mathbb{V}_{h}

∎

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
n\leq N
. Let
(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{s})
be a strong solution to the problem (
1.1
) with regularity given by (
4.3
). Suppose that the initial data (or
\beta

(4.71)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}^{2}\leq\frac{D_{\ast}}{18C_{\Pi}^{2}\beta D^{\ast}}\;\text{ and }\;\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\leq\frac{D_{\ast}}{2\beta D^{\ast}}.

Assume further that
h
and
k
are sufficiently small such that
k=O(h^{-d/2})
. Then the scheme defined by Algorithm
4.1
is well-posed and stable (in the sense of Lemma
4.9
). Furthermore, the following error estimates hold:

(4.72)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n}-\boldsymbol{m}(t_{n})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n}-\boldsymbol{s}(t_{n})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(h^{2(r+1)}+k^{2}\right),

\displaystyle k\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{j}-\nabla\boldsymbol{m}(t_{j})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{j}-\nabla\boldsymbol{s}(t_{j})}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(h^{2r}+k^{2}\right),

where
C
depends on
T
, but is independent of
n
,
h
or
k
.

Proof.

Let
\delta:=D_{\ast}/(2\beta D^{\ast})
. We will prove the theorem by induction.

Firstly, if (
4.71
) holds, then a unique solution
(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{1},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{1})
to the numerical scheme exists (by Proposition
4.8
), satisfying the auxiliary error estimate (
4.67
) for
n=1
. By (
4.22
) and (
4.23
), together with (
4.67
) and the triangle inequality, we then have (
4.72
) for
n=1
.

For the inductive step, suppose that for some
n\leq N
,

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq j\leq n-1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{j}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\leq\delta.

Under this assumption, we will show that the conclusions of the theorem hold at time step
n
and
\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\leq\delta
, thus completing the induction. Indeed, the existence of a unique solution
(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}^{n})
follows from Proposition
4.8
. The auxiliary error estimate (
4.67
) also holds. Therefore, by (
4.22
), (
4.23
), and the triangle inequality, we obtain (
4.72
).

Furthermore, by the definition of
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}

2.16
), the stability estimate (
4.48
), and the auxiliary error estimate (
4.67
),

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}+\left\|{\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{m}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\textrm{inv}}h^{-\frac{d}{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+C_{\Pi}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\left(C_{\textrm{inv}}h^{-\frac{d}{2}}\right)\widetilde{C}e^{\widetilde{C}(1+\delta)T}\left(h^{r+1}+k\right)+C_{\Pi}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\textrm{inv}}\widetilde{C}e^{\widetilde{C}(1+\delta)T}h^{r+1-\frac{d}{2}}+C_{\textrm{inv}}\widetilde{C}e^{\widetilde{C}(1+\delta)T}kh^{-\frac{d}{2}}+C_{\Pi}\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}.

Suppose that
h
and

6.
Conclusion

We have shown the existence and uniqueness of global solution to the spin-diffusion Landau–Lifshitz–Bloch equation, which is a system of coupled quasilinear PDEs modelling the evolution of the magnetisation vector field in the presence of spin-polarised currents at high temperatures. We then propose a finite element scheme which only involves solving two decoupled linear systems and perform a rigorous error analysis, assuming adequate regularity of the exact solution and sufficiently small initial data. Some numerical results are presented which confirm our theoretical analysis.

For future research, we will consider the problem posed in a multi-layer domain which leads to a system with discontinuous coefficients. Higher-order time-marching scheme involving the backward difference formulae (as in
[
3
,
24
,
26
]
) will also be considered in a future work.
