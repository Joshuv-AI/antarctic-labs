Protocol for high-throughput, automated, solid-phase extraction of sphingolipids from human urine
Authors: Rebekah J Nicholson, J Alan Maschek, Trevor Lonergan, Scott A Summers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUrinary sphingolipids may serve as biomarkers of renal diseases. Here, we present a high-throughput, automated protocol for solid-phase extraction and tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) quantification of lipids from urine. We describe steps for sample preparation and lipid extraction, and then detail procedures for targeted LC-MS/MS analysis of urinary sphingolipids. In this protocol, we focus on the analysis of urine samples; however, this platform can be optimized for alternative biospecimens. Additionally, this method allows for tandem preparation of metabolomic and lipidomic samples.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Nicholson et al.1Subject areas: Health Sciences, High Throughput Screening, Metabolism, Metabolomics, Protocols in Metabolomics and Lipidomics

Before you begin

Ceramides and related sphingolipids are emerging biomarkers of metabolic stress and organ injury.2,3 Although circulating sphingolipids have been widely profiled in large human cohorts and incorporated into clinical risk scores for cardiovascular disease, comparatively little is known about urinary sphingolipid dynamics. Because urinary sphingolipids are likely derived from renal tubular metabolism, their quantification may provide a readout of renal cellular stress in the setting of acute kidney injury (AKI) and related renal pathologies.

Urinary sphingolipids are present at extremely low abundance—typically orders of magnitude lower than in plasma1—making sensitive and reproducible detection challenging. Conventional single-phase liquid extraction methods, while effective for plasma and serum, are limited by the volume that can be processed, often forcing a trade-off between sensitivity and convenience when working with urine. These limitations restrict the discovery and validation of urinary sphingolipids as biomarkers of kidney injury.

This protocol uses a solid-phase extraction (SPE) workflow based on Agilent Captiva EMR-Lipid sorbent chemistry,4,5,6 which allows the user to overcome barriers in sensitivity and provides the additional benefit of high-throughput scalability and automation. EMR-Lipid material selectively binds hydrophobic lipid species, while minimizing matrix interference from salts, urea, and other polar urinary components that suppress ionization or otherwise complicate quantification. When paired with a positive-pressure automation platform (e.g., Tecan Resolvex A200), SPE enables: 1) processing of larger urine volumes, 2) concentration of low-abundance sphingolipids such as dihydroceramides, 3) improved lipid recovery beyond expected gains from increased input volume along, 4) reduced sample handling steps and sample loss, 5) high-throughput, 96-well automation compatible with large clinical cohorts, and 6) collection of a secondary “metabolomics fraction” that can be archived or analyzed in parallel with lipidomic samples.

This workflow has been used to quantify 44 urinary sphingolipids for a cohort of patients undergoing cardiopulmonary bypass surgery.1,7 However, the same SPE method and LC-MS protocol can be applied to other biological samples and lipid analytes.

Preparation of internal standards

Note: This protocol lists internal standards used for urinary sphingolipid quantification. Internal standards should be selected based on target lipid species and optimized for the biological matrix and instrument performance. In this study, concentrations were empirically determined by titrating deuterated standards into representative urine samples prior to final method implementation. Working concentrations were chosen to generate peak areas within the instrument’s linear dynamic range and within approximately two-fold of median endogenous sphingolipid signal intensities, minimizing matrix effects while avoiding detector saturation or proximity to the lower limit of quantification. For the non-deuterated dihydroceramide internal standard, endogenous levels in unspiked urine were consistently below the lower limit of quantification; the spike concentration therefore exceeded any potential endogenous contribution. A deuterated dihydroceramide standard was not commercially available at the time of data acquisition but has since become available and has been adopted for ongoing analyses.

Note: Internal standard mixes are prepared twice a year, on average, and stored at −20°C. It is not recommended to use stored internal standards after 9 months of storage. Powdered internal standards are resuspended in MeOH (to 0.2–1 mg/mL) and diluted in MeOH to working stock concentrations, as listed in in the lipidomic internal standards recipe. Working stocks are combined as a master mix and further diluted in MeOH to be distributed to samples in 10 μL aliquots. Working stocks should be prepared fresh, as needed.

Timing: Variable, depending on sample number

1.Thaw urine samples on ice.

2.Prepare internal standards according to the “materials and equipment” section.

3.Vortex and transfer 800 μL of urine to a 1 mL deep-well 96-well plate. Be sure to maintain careful documentation of sample plate positions.

Vortex and transfer 800 μL of urine to a 1 mL deep-well 96-well plate. Be sure to maintain careful documentation of sample plate positions.

Note: Although 800 μL was used in this protocol to balance sensitivity and throughput, exploratory loading experiments indicated that urine volumes up to 4 mL could be processed through a single well without clogging or observable flow impairment under the described pressure conditions (loaded sequentially in aliquots). Formal recovery scaling and sorbent capacity were not systematically evaluated beyond the 800 μL condition. For smaller sample volumes, urine may be diluted with PBS to maintain consistent loading conditions across wells. Internal standard spike volumes were maintained constant during troubleshooting experiments; users applying substantially different input volumes should consider proportional adjustment if quantitative comparability is required.

Note: Urine lipid levels are influenced by urine concentration and hydration levels. It is recommended to account for this by normalizing LC-MS data to urinary creatinine or specific gravity. As such, additional aliquots of urine can be allocated for normalization at this time to avoid repeated freeze/thaw cycles.

Note: Although the importance of normalization for urinary metabolomic assessments in clinical cohorts is widely supported, there is little consensus on a single method. The use of urinary creatinine concentrations as a proxy for urine concentration is the most common method, as creatinine is produced at relatively constant rates, freely filtered by the glomerulus, and minimally reabsorbed.8 However, circulating creatinine levels are influenced by muscle mass, and renal creatinine handling is altered with kidney dysfunction. Thus, other experimental and statistical normalization methods have been reported.9,10,11,12 Importantly, the method of normalization could significantly impact findings and data interpretation, and should thus be considered and reported carefully.

Automated solid-phase extraction of polar and lipid metabolites

Timing: ∼4–5 h total for thawing, extraction, and processing

This section describes the automated solid-phase extraction of polar and lipid metabolites from human urine. This extraction method was adapted from the solid-phase extraction methods described in Apffel et al.,4 Schwaiger-Haber et al.,5 and Blumenreich et al.6 and optimized for the extraction of metabolites from 800 μL of urine; however, the workflow can be adjusted and scaled, as necessary, for extraction from other biospecimens or urine volumes. Sample preparation begins with a 1–2 h thaw at 4 °C. The automated extraction workflow then requires approximately 2 h, including activation of the Tecan A200, purging of solvent lines, loading samples into plate wells, and running the method. Following extraction, the lipid fraction is dried under nitrogen in a TurboVap at 30 L/min gas flow and 35 °C plate temperature for ∼1 h. Re-suspension of each well requires an additional ∼10 min before samples are ready for downstream analysis.

Note: Make fresh extraction solutions on the day of each study. Use MS grade solvents whenever possible.

Note: For all loading and elution steps, pass samples through the SPE column over 100 s, beginning with a manifold pressure of 0 psi and linearly increasing to a peak pressure of 50 psi at 85 s. After reaching maximum pressure, progressively reduce manifold pressure back to 0 psi by the end of the 100-s cycle to ensure complete transfer to the collection plate. Under these conditions, all wells consistently completed loading and elution within the programmed cycle, and no delayed wells or flow impairments were observed during method implementation. The linear pressure increase compensates for typical variability in urine viscosity; samples with visible particulate content should be centrifuged prior to loading.

Note: Dispense all solvents at a dispense rate of 50 mL/min.

6.If necessary, thaw urine samples on ice. Vortex thoroughly or sonicate to ensure that internal standards are in solution and any urinary sediment is resuspended.

If necessary, thaw urine samples on ice. Vortex thoroughly or sonicate to ensure that internal standards are in solution and any urinary sediment is resuspended.

Place the Captiva 96-well plate containing urine and internal standards on a positive pressure manifold (e.g., Tecan Resolvex A200) over a 96-well collection plate.

Note: We did not include a protein precipitation (PPT) step prior to urine lipid extraction, as urinary protein content is low and did not cause clogging of the EMR-lipid sorbent pores or reduced lipid recovery in our hands. However, for samples with high-protein matrices, a PPT step should be incorporated prior to SPE. Although EMR-Lipid sorbent selectively retains hydrophobic lipids rather than proteins, excessive protein loads can physically obstruct the sorbent pores, leading to impaired flow and reduced extraction efficiency.

Expected outcomes

This protocol details the high-throughput, automated quantification of urinary sphingolipids by SPE following by LC-MS/MS. The solid phase extraction process allows for collection of both the polar metabolome and lipidome from the same samples. The LC-MS/MS methods described target 44 urinary sphingolipids for analysis, which can be adapted for the detection and quantification of other lipid species. Our platform outperforms standard liquid extraction methods and consistently improves urinary sphingolipid recovery beyond the gains predicted solely by increased input volume (Figure 1). This enhancement reflects the combined effects of selective sorbent–lipid interactions, efficient removal of matrix components that interfere with ionization, optimized retention and elution conditions for hydrophobic species, and reduced sample handling steps that limit lipid loss. As a result, low-abundance sphingolipids such as dihydroceramides are more reliably detected. These advances support more sensitive and reliable assessment of urinary ceramides as biomarkers of kidney injury and related pathologies.

Limitations

Urinary sphingolipid levels can vary widely due to differences in urine concentration, making normalization essential for cross-sample comparison. However, there is no consensus on the best normalization method for urine metabolomics, and each approach has drawbacks. We recommend normalizing to urinary creatinine, to maintain consistency with existing urine metabolomics literature; however, urinary creatinine excretion can be altered in kidney disease and may introduce bias in pathological cohorts. In such cases, alternative normalization approaches (e.g., specific gravity) may be considered. Although the monitored fragment ions are class-diagnostic and retention times were confirmed using internal standards, the use of single quantifier transitions without qualifier ions may limit orthogonal confirmation of analyte identity compared to dual-transition methods. Additionally, quantification was performed using class-matched isotopic internal standards rather than exact chain-length–matched standards for all analytes. While isotopic dilution minimizes variability from extraction and ionization, differences in retention time and matrix effects between analytes and non-identical internal standards may introduce minor quantitative bias. This protocol is therefore optimized for reproducible comparative quantification across samples rather than formal absolute bioanalytical validation. Users requiring chain-length–specific response correction may incorporate external calibration curves in addition to isotopic normalization. For long-term or multi-batch clinical studies, potential lot-to-lot variability in SPE plates or LC column batches should be considered. When feasible, a single sorbent and column lot should be used for the duration of a study. New lots should be qualified using pooled QC samples to confirm retention time stability, signal intensity, and recovery consistency prior to analyzing study samples. Continuous inclusion of pooled QC samples throughout analytical batches is recommended to monitor for drift across plate or column changes. Additionally, although our SPE workflow can theoretically support simultaneous extraction of polar metabolites and lipids, analytical conditions must be independently optimized to ensure reliable detection and quantification of the specific metabolite classes relevant to each experiment.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Less than 800 μL of urine is available for a subset of experimental samples (related to step 3).

Potential solution

Our SPE protocol can reliably quantify urinary sphingolipids from as little as 40 μL, and even smaller volumes may be determined to be sufficient to extract and detect more abundant urinary metabolites. As such, urine samples may be diluted in PBS to maintain consistent extraction volumes across sample sets. Also note that quantification of urine creatinine or specific gravity for data normalization should also be performed in the diluted sample.

Problem 2

Low or inconsistent flow through the Captiva EMR-Lipid plate during the SPE cleanup step.

Potential solution

Reduced or uneven flow is often caused by high urine viscosity, elevated protein or particulate content, or partial obstruction of the EMR-Lipid sorbent. Centrifuging samples before loading or performing a brief dilution with water or PBS can decrease viscosity and remove debris that would otherwise impede flow. Ensure that the positive-pressure system applies uniform pressure across the entire plate, as insufficient or uneven pressure can slow or stall individual wells. If specific wells exhibit persistent obstruction, the sample can be reloaded after gentle mixing, or the affected well may need to be excluded or transferred to a fresh sorbent plate. Consistent flow across all wells is essential for reproducible recoveries and balanced elution volumes.

Problem 3

Beading or dripping from the Tecan Resolvex A200 dispense manifold causing cross-contamination between the polar and lipid SPE fractions.

Potential solution

Cross-contamination commonly results from misalignment of the SPE plate on the positive-pressure manifold, uneven flow or air bubbles in the solvent lines, or sorbent overload that allows breakthrough into subsequent fractions. Confirm that the plate is correctly seated and that the dispense head is fully engaged before running the method, using the system’s alignment check if available. Poor sealing or mis-positioning can lead to leaks, beading, or dripping from the manifold that may contaminate adjacent wells. After any solvent change or instrument downtime, purge the solvent lines and manifold to remove residual liquid and ensure proper priming, as trapped droplets or air pockets can disrupt flow when the run resumes. Program pressure settings appropriate for each step, since insufficient pressure can cause uneven flow while overly aggressive pressure may promote splashing or solvent bypass. Avoid exceeding the sorbent capacity, as high matrix load or excessive sample volume can cause early breakthrough of polar components into the lipid fraction. If contamination persists, evaluate fraction purity using blank wells or polarity-specific standards, and re-optimize solvent order, wash volumes, or pressure profiles to maintain clean separation of polar and lipid eluates.

Problem 4

Poor chromatographic peak shape, unstable retention times, or unexpected pressure increases during LC-MS/MS analysis.

Potential solution

Ensure that the LC column is fully equilibrated by running the system at starting mobile phase conditions for an adequate period and by including several conditioning injections (e.g., PrOH or blank solvent) before beginning the sample queue. Prepare fresh mobile phases and confirm that all salts are completely dissolved, as aged or partially dissolved reagents can alter retention and degrade peak shape. After instrument downtime, purge all solvent lines to remove trapped air and verify that autosampler wash lines and system degassing are functioning properly. Monitor the LC pressure trace for any instability, gradual pressure buildup, or sudden spikes. Rapid fluctuations often indicate an air bubble in the pump heads, which can be resolved by running a prime cycle on the autosampler. A large difference between mainpass and bypass pressures suggests a clogged needle or needle seat, which may require cleaning or replacement. High pressure is also frequently caused by blockages in the capillary line between the autosampler and the column. If peak shape continues to deteriorate, flush the column with high organic solvent to remove accumulated hydrophobic material and replace the guard column if backpressure has increased. Confirm that the sample injection solvent matches the strength of the initial gradient, as overly strong organic content can distort peak shape; reducing the injection volume may also improve performance. Finally, maintain a stable autosampler temperature throughout the run and inspect all fittings and column connections for leaks or partial blockages that may contribute to retention time instability.

Problem 5

Retention time drift across the batch leading to mis-integration, incorrect peak assignment, or missing peaks in MassHunter Quant.

Potential solution

Retention time drift may cause MassHunter Quant to misassign peaks or fail to integrate analytes whose observed retention times fall outside the expected RT window. When drift is consistent across the batch, a practical corrective strategy is to use an internal standard as a time-reference compound. In MassHunter Quant, this allows the software to adjust expected analyte retention times based on the observed shift of the internal standard, effectively normalizing peak assignment to real chromatographic conditions. After selecting the appropriate ISTD as the time reference, reprocess the batch so that analyte peaks are aligned to the updated retention times rather than the original method values. Retention Time Flagging can also be used to identify compounds whose peaks fall outside the allowed window and require adjustment. Following RT updates, reintegration should be reviewed to confirm correct peak boundaries and proper association of analytes with their ISTDs. If substantial drift persists despite these corrections, the issue may reflect underlying LC variability, and additional inspection of mobile phases, temperature stability, or pump performance may be necessary.
