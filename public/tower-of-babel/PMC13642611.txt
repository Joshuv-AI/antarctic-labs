Dynamical Modulation of Dual‐Band Thermal Emission in a Graphene‐2D‐hBN‐LiV2O5 Heterostructure
Authors: Yuhui Cai, Minru Zhu, Peiyuan Huang, Qinmiao Chen, Xuening Wang, Bingfeng Fan, Yongyao Li, Jiayao Huang, Dongxu Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDynamic control of thermal emission across multiple infrared bands is crucial for thermal camouflage, molecular sensing, and reconfigurable infrared energy management. However, most emitters rely on broadband, incoherent radiation, limiting spectral precision, and practical tunability. Here, we demonstrate a dynamically tunable dual‐band thermal emission platform based on a van der Waals heterostructure integrating graphene, two‐dimensional hexagonal boron nitride (2D‐hBN) and LiV2O5. Plasmon–phonon coupling produces five hybrid polariton branches (LP1, UP1, LP2, MP2 and UP2), enabling narrowband emission in two mid‐infrared windows within a compact stack. To quantify the coupling physics and guide reconfiguration, we develop coupled harmonic oscillator models using two‐mode and three‐mode interaction frameworks. These models enable extraction of Rabi splitting energies, mode composition and strong‐coupling regimes, and they show excellent agreement with numerical simulations. We obtain Rabi splittings of 8.42 meV in the 2D‐hBN channel and 4.32 meV in the LiV2O5 channel. Increasing the hBN thickness strengthens short‐wavelength confinement and raises the maximum splitting to 27.72 meV. The emitter achieves Q up to 138.97 and FOMλ up to 49.33, providing a practical and compact platform for tunable mid‐infrared sources for adaptive thermal control, molecular detection, and multispectral imaging.

Introduction

Thermal emission, driven by thermally induced electromagnetic fluctuations, governs radiative heat transfer and photon‐based energy conversion. Its broadband, isotropic and incoherent nature, however, hampers applications that require tailored directionality, spectral response or polarization. Dynamic and real‐time modulation of thermal radiation has therefore become central to adaptive thermal management [1], multispectral infrared imaging [2], and nonreciprocal thermal photonics [3]. Graphene is particularly appealing for such active control because it combines a strong electro‐optic response with low thermal mass and excellent integration compatibility. Electrical tuning of its Fermi level, together with nanostructure engineering [4], enables fast and reversible mid‐infrared emission control with spectral selectivity [5, 6], supporting dynamically reconfigurable thermal photonic systems. Although much of the early work concentrated on single‐band modulation, emerging applications increasingly demand dual‐band operation to address separated infrared windows within one device [7, 8]. Existing dual‐band emitters remain largely confined to static designs with emission bands fixed after fabrication [7, 9, 10], or phase‐change‐based implementations that switch between discrete emissive states [8, 11, 12]. As a result, dual‐band thermal emission that is both continuously tunable and independently controllable remains elusive.

A central requirement for independently controllable dual‐band emission is a pair of polaritonic channels that are spectrally separated. Two‐dimensional hexagonal boron nitride (2D‐hBN) satisfies this requirement in the short‐wavelength mid‐infrared by supporting low‐loss transverse optical (TO) phonons and narrowband thermal features near 7 μm, whereas anisotropic LiV2O5 complements it with broadband TO and longitudinal optical (LO) phonon responses around 10–12 μm [13, 14]. This combination provides two distinct phonon‐defined windows that can be engineered as separate emission channels within one platform. Both materials are fabrication compatible with van der Waals integration and multilayer infrared optical stacks. The hBN flakes can be obtained by mechanical exfoliation and assembled through mature transfer and encapsulation routes [15, 16]. In addition, the hBN is widely used as an atomically smooth buffer that helps retain graphene mobility and narrow‐linewidth plasmonic responses in complex stacks. Thickness controlled LiV2O5 films can be produced by exfoliating flux grown crystals [14]. Their bulk phonon‐driven infrared response enables a continuous, unpatterned interlayer that preserves robust Reststrahlen band characteristics on dielectric or metal substrates. These phononic platforms further enable coherence engineering through polaritonic mode coupling. Spatial coherence can be improved via Surface Phonon Polariton (SPhP) [17], Surface Plasmon Polariton (SPP) [18], and hybrid modes, and temporal coherence is set by linewidth and benefits from spectral narrowing [19]. Coherence tailoring has been demonstrated using grating resonators, subwavelength grooves and photonic crystals structures [20]. Combining tunable plasmonic elements with phonon‐active media has emerged as a route to spectrally selective, mode‐defined thermal emission [21, 22].

In this work, we realize a continuously and reversibly reconfigurable dual‐band thermal emitter by integrating graphene nanoribbons with 2D‐hBN and LiV2O5 in a van der Waals heterostructure. The design exploits the spectrally separated phonon resonances of hBN and LiV2O5 to address two atmospheric transparency windows near 7 and 10 μm within a single compact platform. Graphene provides active control through electrostatic gating, which tunes the plasmon dispersion, whereas the nanoribbon geometry offers an independent handle that regulates near‐field confinement and symmetry matching. This architecture enables channel‐resolved control over both emissivity strength and resonance position, directly targeting the need for independent and continuous dual‐band modulation highlighted above. We model the thermally excited spectral response and field‐phonon interactions using rigorous coupled‐wave analysis (RCWA), and use coupled‐oscillator descriptions to track modal evolution and identify systematic modulation trends governed by graphene's Fermi level and ribbon geometry. To compare all resolved branches on equal footing, we quantify performance with a unified set of metrics, including the quality factor (Q), modulation contrast (Cε and Rε), and figures of merit (FOMλ). These results establish a dual‐band mid‐infrared emissivity platform that is tunable, reversible, and compact, providing a route towards adaptive dual‐band thermal photonics.

Models and Principle

The dual‐band emission platform in Figure 1a is engineered for dynamic thermal radiation control in the mid‐infrared. The van der Waals heterostructure consists of graphene nanoribbons, atomically thin 2D‐hBN, and anisotropic LiV2O5 layers, integrated with BaF2 spacers for optical isolation and a metallic Ag back‐reflector to enhance emissive out‐coupling. We determine the BaF2 spacer thicknesses d1, d2, and the grating periodicity Λ using physics guided RCWA parametric optimization. The design places hybrid plasmon–phonon resonances in the atmospheric windows near 7 and 10 μm and strengthens the Rabi splitting features. Spacer thickness d1 sets the evanescent field overlap between graphene plasmons and LiV2O5 phonons, and d2 reduces Ag back reflector dissipation while maintaining field confinement in the active layers. The grating periodicity Λ supplies the in‐plane momentum that couples normally incident radiation into graphene plasmons and therefore defines the hybridization condition. Throughout this work, the geometric parameters are fixed at d1=50nm, d2=1200nm, and Λ=300nm. Two phonon‐mediated emission channels enable spectrally selective modulation. These two phonon‐mediated emission channels remain spectrally independent across the full 6–12 μm window, as verified in Supporting Information S1: Section S1, with the hBN channel yielding narrowband emission near 7 μm and the LiV2O5 channel providing richer spectral features and tunability around 10 μm. Spectral emissivity ελ is derived from reflectivity Rε using Kirchhoff's law, as ελ=αλ=1−Rλ, under thermal equilibrium conditions [23, 24].

As shown in Figure 1b, the dual‐band thermal emission response arises from two phonon–plasmon hybridization channels. The first originates from the interaction between graphene SPP and the TO phonon of 2D‐hBN at a wavelength of 7 μm, yielding two emissive branches corresponding to the upper and lower hybridized modes [25]. The second involves the simultaneous coupling between graphene SPP and both TO and LO phonons of LiV2O5 at a wavelength around 10 μm. This three‐channel hybridization leads to the formation of three distinct emissive polariton branches, namely lower (LP), middle (MP), and upper (UP). Conventional dual‐band emitters combine distinct resonators or switch between discrete phase change states. In our graphene–2D‐hBN–LiV2O5 platform, both bands are independently tuned in a continuous and reversible manner through electrostatic gating, providing real time spectral control without moving components or phase transitions [8, 9, 26].

Figure 1c,d show wavelength‐dependent complex permittivity of 2D‐hBN and LiV2O5 along the out‐of‐plane direction, where real and imaginary parts are indicated by solid and dashed lines. In monolayer 2D‐hBN, the infrared response is set by a dispersive complex permittivity and is dominated by a TO phonon at 7.2 μm. This resonance defines the Type II Reststrahlen band spanning 6.2–7.3 μm and supports an SPhP‐like channel at the graphene–hBN interface [27, 28]. The LO phonon at 6.2 μm has negligible out of plane dipole activity and therefore contributes little to infrared emission. By contrast, LiV2O5 exhibits both TO and LO phonon modes along the a‐ and c‐axes, whereas the b‐axis response is comparatively weak. For the c‐axis response used in this work, these phonon modes define a Reststrahlen band from approximately 9.8–10.2 μm, where the real permittivity becomes negative [14]. This region enables strong confinement of electromagnetic energy through phonon‐supported modes with low losses. Both the SPhP in hBN and the phonon polaritons in LiV2O5 have been directly imaged in recent near‐field experiments, corroborating the physical reality of the polaritonic channels used here [14, 28]. The hybrid resonances translate into strong emissivity enhancement. The peak emissivity approaches unity at the polaritonic resonances and the constituent‐only baselines, as quantified by blackbody‐referenced benchmarks in Supporting Information S1: Section S5. We further verify the modal character through near‐field profiles in the X‐Z plane, as shown in Figure 2c–g. The dominant confinement appears across the ribbon width and is consistent with short‐edge confinement [35]. The LP1 and UP1 modes in Figure 2c,d are localized at the graphene hBN interface, whereas the LP2, MP2, and UP2 modes in Figure 2e–g exhibit pronounced confinement inside the phonon‐active region of LiV2O5. The edge‐enhanced intensity originates from boundary‐induced charge accumulation in width‐quantized plasmon eigenmodes [36]. In our heterostructure, this edge‐weighted localization increases the overlap with the phonon‐active layers and reduces the effective modal area, which reinforces plasmon–phonon hybridization and supports the tunability of the LP, UP, and MP branches in Figure 2a,b. We note that the edge enhancement reflects the intrinsic spatial structure of the hybrid eigenmodes rather than parasitic edge‐radiative channels. This lateral mode selectivity, combined with longitudinal uniformity, supports spectrally selective emission and may improve spatial coherence [24].

Both nanoribbon width modulation and electrostatic gating reconfigure the LP and UP branches, which enables branch‐selective reshaping of the emissivity spectrum. To quantify this behavior, we evaluate three standardized metrics for all configurations, including the quality factor Q, the modulation contrasts Cε and Rε, and the figures of merit FOMλ. Definitions are provided in Supporting Information S1: Section S8. In the graphene‐2D‐hBN channel, the quality factors reach 49.50 for the LP branch and 95.78 for the UP branch. The same platform offers substantial agility, with FOMλ ranging from 1.95 to 13.29 under geometric and electrostatic tuning. The emissivity redistribution is branch‐selective and depends on the tuning route. Under geometric modulation, the most evident change is the suppression of the UP branch, with Cε=−0.25 and Rε=0.75, whereas the LP branch remains nearly unchanged. Under electrostatic gating, the LP branch shows the stronger enhancement, with Cε=0.18 and Rε=1.18. These representative values indicate that geometric tuning mainly redistributes emission between the two hybrid branches, whereas electrostatic gating provides a more direct enhancement of the lower branch. The eigenvector‐derived mixing fractions are calculated using Supporting Information S1: Formula S4 and S5. Figure 4e,f reveal a continuous exchange of modal character under both tuning routes. Increasing the graphene width drives the LP branch from SPP‐like to phonon‐like behavior, and drives the UP branch in the opposite direction. The crossover occurs close to the point of minimal branch separation, indicating maximal hybridization. Varying the Fermi level induces a complementary evolution. The LP branch becomes more SPP‐like whereas the UP branch becomes more phonon‐like. The ability to reversibly steer these mixing fractions provides a practical mechanism to reconfigure infrared emission by redistributing energy between plasmonic and phononic components, which directly impacts the emission spectrum and its coherence.

The layer‐dependent dielectric constants of hBN indicate that the out‐of‐plane dielectric response evolves continuously from N = 2 to N = 4 and becomes nearly saturated above N = 5 [40, 41]. Accordingly, the monolayer sheet model is used for N = 1, the layer‐dependent 3D anisotropic thin‐film model is used for N = 2 to 4, and the bulk‐like 3D anisotropic model is used for N ≥ 5 [42]. To highlight the thickness effect, Figure 5b,c compare the thermal emissivity spectra for hBN layer number N = 1 to 9 in the absence and presence of graphene. In Figure 5b, without graphene, the spectrum is dominated by a resonance near the hBN TO phonon around 7.2 μm. As the number of hBN layers increases, this resonance becomes sharper and stronger, whereas its spectral position remains nearly unchanged. This trend is consistent with the gradual strengthening of the hBN dielectric response from the monolayer to the multi‐layer regime. After introducing graphene, Figure 5c shows that the hBN phonon resonance is transformed into several SPP–phonon hybrid resonances. For N = 1, the spectrum is mainly characterized by a hybrid peak near 7.5 μm, together with narrow phonon‐related features around the hBN phonon region. When the hBN thickness increases to N = 2 to 4, the LO‐related channel begins to participate in the hybridization process, leading to a rapid reorganization of the hybrid spectrum. The LP branch shifts towards approximately 6.0 μm and becomes substantially narrower, whereas the MP branch remains near 6.4–6.6 μm with only a weak spectral displacement. The UP branch shifts continuously from around 7.5 μm towards 8.3–8.5 μm as N increases. Meanwhile, the narrow hBN phonon‐like feature near 7.2 μm persists and becomes more pronounced as the hBN layer number increases. For N ≥ 5, the dielectric constants of hBN are nearly saturated, and the spectra gradually approach the bulk regime, where the main hybrid branches evolve more smoothly with thickness. The maximum Rabi splitting energy reaches 27.72 meV in N = 9.

Figure 5d,e summarize the extracted resonance positions and linewidths of the hybridized modes as a function of hBN layer number. As N increases, the separation between the hybrid branches becomes larger, confirming that thicker hBN layers strengthen the SPP–phonon interaction. The linewidths of the LP, MP, and UP branches show different trends. The LP linewidth decreases rapidly after the monolayer case and then remains relatively narrow. The MP branch keeps a small linewidth over the full thickness range. In contrast, the UP linewidth increases more clearly with N, indicating stronger plasmonic participation and stronger radiative broadening in thicker hBN structures. As discussed in Supporting Information S1: Section S10, the abrupt change of γLP in the few‐layer region mainly originates from the dielectric‐model transition and the corresponding branch reassignment. With increasing ribbon width, the LP branch evolves from SPP‐dominated to phonon‐dominated character with a dominant LO contribution. The MP branch remains largely phononic with only weak SPP weight, whereas the UP branch becomes increasingly plasmonic and reduces its TO participation. Figure 7c quantifies the corresponding coherent coupling by tracking the Rabi splitting energy and the cumulative linewidth‐weighted loss. The threshold criterion follows the standard non‐Hermitian three‐mode coupled‐oscillator formalism and confirms that the observed splittings arise from coherent mode mixing rather than accidental spectral overlap, thereby distinguishing genuine hybridization from simple resonance crossing [43, 44, 45].

Specifically, the coupling condition is expressed as a threshold criterion: (11) Ω>WUPγUP+WMPγMP+WLPγLP

Here, Ω denotes the Rabi splitting energy, whereas γUP, γMP, and γLP correspond to the linewidths of the UP, MP, and LP branches, respectively. WUP, WMP and WLP represent the fractional contributions in each hybrid mode, obtained through eigenvector decomposition of the system's Hamiltonian. Strong coupling is set by the balance between coherent exchange and dissipative loss. This three‐mode form is required because the graphene SPP is simultaneously coupled to the TO and LO phonons of LiV2O5. The criterion compares the effective coupling strengths with the corresponding loss rates and incorporates branch‐dependent mixing weights, thereby capturing unequal coupling strengths and loss asymmetry among the three bare modes [43, 44]. The analytical expressions for WUP, WMP and WLP can be expressed as follows: (12) WUP=γUPγUP+γMP+γLPWMP=γMPγUP+γMP+γLPWLP=γLPγUP+γMP+γLP

Given the linewidths γSPP=3.25meV and γTO=γLO=0.48meV, we calculate γUP=0.96meV, γMP=0.69meV, and γLP=2.44meV. These values yield corresponding Hopfield weights of WUP=0.235, WMP=0.169, and WLP=0.596. Substituting these values into the multi‐mode threshold criterion in Equation (11), we obtain a minimum Rabi splitting of: (14) Ω>0.235γUP+0.169γMP+0.596γLP

This criterion reflects a physical balance between coupling‐driven energy exchange and total system loss. Unlike simpler detuning‐based estimators, it accounts for both loss asymmetry and unequal coupling strengths across modes. The Rabi splitting extracted from the dispersion relation (Ω=4.32meV) exceeds this threshold, confirming the system's entry into the hybridization regime. Higher Fermi level increases the SPP fraction in LP, whereas UP becomes more phonon‐like, enabling continuous steering of the multiband emission landscape. Figure 7f further shows a nonmonotonic evolution of the Rabi splitting energy and linewidth‐weighted loss with gating, underscoring reconfigurable hybridization under electronic control. Fabrication Feasibility and Process Considerations

The proposed graphene–2D‐hBN–LiV2O5 heterostructure is compatible with established van der Waals assembly and mid‐infrared nanofabrication techniques. LiV2O5 can be incorporated a continuous and unpatterned phonon‐active interlayer, since its infrared response is governed by bulk anisotropic phonon modes rather than lateral resonator geometry. Thickness‐controlled LiV2O5 films can be obtained from flux‐grown crystals by mechanical or polymer‐assisted exfoliation, and recent studies have shown that such films retain clear Reststrahlen‐band features and low‐loss phonon‐polariton responses on dielectric or metallic substrates [46, 47, 48]. Moderate Nanoscale thickness nonuniformity is therefore expected mainly to broaden the long‐wavelength resonance, without suppressing the LiV2O5‐mediated modulation channel.

The graphene–2D‐hBN stack can be assembled on the BaF2/LiV2O5/BaF2/Ag platform using dry‐transfer or polymer‐assisted transfer methods. hBN serves both as the short‐wavelength phononic medium and as an atomically flat buffer for graphene, helping to preserve mobility and reduce substrate‐induced disorder [15, 16, 49, 50]. Graphene nanoribbons with submicrometer linewidths can then be patterned by electron‐beam lithography or nanoimprint lithography, which are compatible with CVD‐grown or exfoliated graphene [51]. Electrostatic tuning can be implemented using bottom‐gate or dual‐gate architectures; in this design, the Ag reflector can serve as a global bottom electrode, whereas BaF2 acts as a low‐loss infrared spacer and gate dielectric [50, 52, 53, 54].

The predicted hybridization can be verified by tracking gate‐ and geometry‐dependent far‐field spectra. Since the Ag reflector suppresses transmission, the emissivity can be extracted from reflectance through Kirchhoff's law, ε(λ)=1−R(λ), using FTIR‐based measurements [24, 55]. Split peaks near the hBN phonon band and multibranch features within the LiV2O5 Reststrahlen band would provide direct evidence of avoided crossings and plasmon–phonon hybridization. Their systematic shifts with graphene Fermi level and nanoribbon width would further confirm dynamic, reversible dual‐band thermal‐emission modulation.

The main practical challenges are maintaining graphene mobility, minimizing transfer residues and interface contamination, and preserving LiV2O5 crystallinity and thickness uniformity. These issues can be addressed by hBN encapsulation, clean transfer, post‐transfer annealing and optical preselection of uniform LiV2O5 flakes [15, 16, 46, 47, 48, 49, 50]. Residual imperfections may broaden resonances or reduce modulation depth, but they should not alter the plasmon–phonon coupling mechanism underlying the proposed dual‐band emitter [53, 54, 55].

Conclusion

We demonstrate a dual‐band tunable thermal emission platform based on a graphene–2D‐hBN–LiV2O5 heterostructure for dynamic control of mid‐infrared radiation. RCWA calculations combined with coupled‐oscillator modeling reveal spectrally tunable emission near 7 and 10 μm enabled by band‐selective plasmon–phonon hybridization. Low‐loss TO phonons and hyperbolic dispersion in 2D‐hBN underpin narrowband coherent emission around 7 μm, whereas broadband anisotropic phonons in LiV2O5 support multimode interactions that yield tunable emission near 10 μm. Independent tuning of nanoribbon width and graphene Fermi level provides dynamic control over both emissivity and resonance wavelength, with LP and UP branches exhibiting opposite shifts that facilitate mode‐resolved emission shaping. Multilayer 2D‐hBN further enhances confinement and emission efficiency. The resulting non‐monotonic spectral evolution is captured by a multimode coupling criterion, demonstrating robust and flexible dual‐band operation. These findings outline a general design strategy for reconfigurable mid‐infrared thermal emitters and support radiative control in nanophotonic systems.
