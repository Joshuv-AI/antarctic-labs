Introduction

We consider Schrödinger operators formally defined by

\displaystyle-\Delta+\lambda\delta_{0}.

(1.1)

This model has been studied in nuclear physics, solid-state physics, and electromagnetic theory
[
44

13
,
59
]

Berezin and Faddeev gave the first rigorous analysis of (

d=3

[

\displaystyle{\mathrm{H}}_{0}:=-\Delta|_{C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}-\{0\})}

in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
for
d=3
. Krein’s theory shows that these self-adjoint extensions form the one-parameter family
\{-\Delta_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in(-\infty,\infty]}

[
2
, I. Theorem 1.1.1.]
. Also,
-\Delta_{\alpha}
could be approximated by scaled short-range interactions in the sense of norm resolvent convergence
[
2
, I. Theorem 1.2.5]
,
[

,
7
,
4
]

\widetilde{V}:{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a measurable function satisfying
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\frac{|\widetilde{V}(x)||\widetilde{V}(x^{\prime})|}{|x-x^{\prime}|^{2}}\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}x^{\prime}<\infty

converges to

For the two-dimensional case, the same result holds after replacing the coupling constant by

[

For the one-dimensional case,

The fundamental solutions of heat equations and Schrödinger equations with one-point interaction have been discussed in

1.1
The zero-energy resonance

In this paper, we fix the potential function
\widetilde{V}
given in (
1.8
) below. This
\widetilde{V}
is suitable to approximate
-\Delta_{\alpha}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{\varepsilon}:=-\Delta+\frac{\lambda(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\widetilde{V}\left(\frac{\cdot}{\varepsilon}\right)

for
\lambda(0)=1
and
\lambda^{\prime}(0)\in{\mathbb{R}}
approximates
-\Delta_{\alpha}
in norm convergence sense
[
2
, I. Theorem 1.2.4]
or
[
4
]
.

To see this, we give a quick review of the approximation in
[
2
]
.

The potential kernel (or the Green function) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi|x-y|}=\Delta^{-1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{2t}(x,y)\text{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{t}(x,y)\text{\rm d}t,\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3},x\not=y,

where
p_{t}(x,y)=p_{t}(x-y)=\frac{1}{(2\pi t)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{2t}\right)
is a Gaussian density function.

Let
\widetilde{V}
be an
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
-function belonging to Rollnik class, i.e.

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\text{\rm d}x\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\text{\rm d}y\frac{|\widetilde{V}(x)||\widetilde{V}(y)|}{|x-y|^{2}}<\infty.

Then, we set

\displaystyle v(x)=|\widetilde{V}(x)|^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad u(x)=|\widetilde{V}(x)|^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{sgn}[\widetilde{V}(x)].

Suppose that there exists an
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
function
\phi
such that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}u(x)\widetilde{G}(x,y)v(y)\phi(y)\text{\rm d}y=-\phi(x).

Then, we define

\displaystyle\psi(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\widetilde{G}(x,y)v(y)\phi(y)\text{\rm d}y.

(1.6)

If
\psi\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
but
\psi\not\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
,
\psi
and the spectral point
0
are called a zero-energy resonance function and a resonance of
\mathrm{H}_{1}
, respectively.

Theorem 2.1 in
[
4
]
tells us that if
\mathrm{H}_{1}
has a zero-energy resonance function and
(1+|x|)^{2}\widetilde{V}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
(resp.
(1+|x|)^{4}\widetilde{V}(x)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
), then
\mathrm{H}_{\varepsilon}
converges in norm resolvent (resp. strong resolvent) sense to
-\Delta_{\alpha}
for suitable
\lambda(\varepsilon)
.

1.1.1
Choice of
\widetilde{V}

We fix a function
\widetilde{V}
for which
\mathrm{H}_{1}
has a resonance and a resonance function.

We define

\displaystyle H(a,x)=\int_{a}^{\infty}p_{t}(x)\text{\rm d}t=\frac{1}{2(\pi)^{\frac{3}{2}}|x|}\int_{0}^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{2a}}u^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-u}\text{\rm d}u

(1.7)

for
a\geq 0
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.
Then, we define a spherical symmetric potential
\widetilde{V}
by

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}(x)=-\frac{2}{(2\pi)^{\frac{3}{2}}H(1,x)}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}\right)=-\frac{2}{H(1,x)}p_{1}(x).

(1.8)

It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\widetilde{G}(x,y)\widetilde{V}(y)H(1,y)\text{\rm d}y=-2\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}p_{2t}(x,y)p_{1}(y)\text{\rm d}y\text{\rm d}t=-H(1,x).

(1.9)

Thus, if we set

\displaystyle\phi(x)=\sqrt{\widetilde{V}(x)}H(1,x)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}),

then
\phi
is an eigenfunction of the Hilbert-Schmidt operator
u(x)\widetilde{G}(x,\cdot)v(x):L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
with eigenvalue
-1
.

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
\widetilde{V}
be given by (
1.8
). Then,
\psi
defined by (
1.6
) is a zero-energy resonance function of
\mathrm{H}_{1}
.

Proof.

We know from (
1.9
) that
\psi(x)=H(1,x)
. Also, it is easy to see from (
1.7
) that

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to\infty}|x|H(1,x)=\frac{1}{2(\pi)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi},

where
\Gamma(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{s-1}e^{-x}\text{\rm d}x
is the Gamma function. Therefore, we find that
\psi\not\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
.

Since
\lim_{|x|\to 0}H(1,x)=\frac{1}{2^{\frac{1}{2}}\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}}
,
\psi\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
.
∎

Thus
\widetilde{V}
satisfies the assumptions of
[
4
, Theorem 2.1]
, and hence
-\Delta+\frac{\lambda(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\widetilde{V}\left(\frac{\cdot}{\varepsilon}\right)
converges to
-\Delta_{\alpha}
in strong resolvent sense as
\varepsilon\to 0
for some
\alpha\in(-\infty,\infty)
.

To prepare for the Feynman–Kac formula, it is convenient to set
G(x,y)=G(x-y)=2\widetilde{G}(x,y)
for
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
V=\frac{\widetilde{V}}{2}
.

The following properties of
H
are easily derived from the definition:

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to\infty}|x|H(a,x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}

(1.10)

\displaystyle H(a,0):=\lim_{x\to 0}H(a,x)=\pi^{-\frac{3}{2}}(2a)^{-\frac{1}{2}}

(1.11)

\displaystyle\lim_{a\to 0}H(a,x)=G(x)

(1.12)

\displaystyle H(a,x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}H\left(1,\frac{x}{\sqrt{a}}\right).

(1.13)

In particular,
H(a,x)
is decreasing in
|x|
, and hence
we have

\displaystyle H(a,x)\asymp\frac{C}{\max\{\sqrt{a},|x|\}}

(1.14)

for
a>0
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.

It is easy to see from the scaling property of Gaussian density functions that
for
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle V^{\varepsilon}(x):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}V\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)=-\frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}h^{\varepsilon}(x)}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\right)=-\frac{1}{h^{\varepsilon}(x)}p_{\varepsilon^{2}}(x),

where

\displaystyle h^{\varepsilon}(x):=\int_{\varepsilon^{2}}^{\infty}p_{t}(x)\text{\rm d}t=\frac{1}{2(\pi)^{\frac{3}{2}}|x|}\int_{0}^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}}u^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-u}\text{\rm d}u=H(\varepsilon^{2},x).

Also, we write
h(x)=h^{1}(x)=H(1,x)
.

Then,
h^{\varepsilon}
and
V^{\varepsilon}
have the following properties.

(P-1)

(Asymptotics)

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to 0}h^{\varepsilon}(x)=\pi^{-\frac{3}{2}}(2\varepsilon^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}=:h^{\varepsilon}(0),\quad\lim_{|x|\to\infty}|x|h^{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}

and hence,
h^{\varepsilon}\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
but
h^{\varepsilon}\not\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
.

(P-2)

(Convolution) For
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}G(x,y)V^{\varepsilon}(y)h^{\varepsilon}(y)\text{\rm d}y=h^{\varepsilon}(x).

(P-3)

(Scaling) We have

\displaystyle V^{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}V\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad h^{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}h\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\text{for $x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}$.}

In particular,

\displaystyle h^{\varepsilon}(x)\to\frac{1}{2\pi|x|}:=h^{0}(x)=G(x),\quad x\not=0,\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

1.2
Main result

We consider the heat equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)-\lambda(\varepsilon)V^{\varepsilon}(x)u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)

(1.15)

for
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)\in C^{2}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\{0\})
, where we retake
\lambda(\varepsilon)=1+\gamma\varepsilon
for simplicity of notations. Then, a Feynman-Kac formula leads to

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)

\displaystyle={\mathrm{E}}^{x}\left[u_{0}(B_{t})\exp\left(-\lambda(\varepsilon)\int_{0}^{t}V^{\varepsilon}(B_{s})\text{\rm d}s\right)\right],

where we denote by
{\mathrm{P}}^{x}
and
{\mathrm{E}}^{x}
the probability measure of Brownian motion started at
x
and the expectation w.r.t.
{\mathrm{P}}^{x}
.

Our choices of potential
V
and coupling
\lambda(\varepsilon)
differ from those in
[
26
]
. Nevertheless, their convergence argument can be adapted to our setting, so
u^{\varepsilon}
converges to
u_{\alpha}
, where
u_{\alpha}
is the solution of the associated heat equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{\alpha}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}(t,x).

(1.16)

Our main result gives a Feynman–Kac formula for (
1.16
).

Theorem 1.3
.

For any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\{0\}
and
t>0
, there exist a function
G_{t}^{\alpha}(x)
and a probability measure
\mu_{t,x}^{\alpha}
on the Wiener space
\left(\Omega=C([0,\infty),{\mathbb{R}}^{3}),\mathcal{B}(C([0,\infty),{\mathbb{R}}^{3}))\right)
such that

\displaystyle u_{\alpha}(t,x)=G_{t}^{\alpha}(x)\mu_{t,x}^{\alpha}\left[u_{0}(\omega^{t,x}(t))\right]

for
u_{0}\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
.

In the following, we will see
G^{\alpha}_{t}(x)
and
\mu_{t,x}^{\alpha}
.

We define the Green function as the function which satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}G_{\varepsilon,t}(x,y)f(y)\text{\rm d}y={\mathrm{E}}^{x}\left[\exp\left(-\lambda(\varepsilon)\int_{0}^{t}V^{\varepsilon}(B_{s})\text{\rm d}s\right)f(B_{t})\right]

(1.17)

and we set

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon,t}(x)={\mathrm{E}}^{x}\left[\exp\left(-\lambda(\varepsilon)\int_{0}^{t}V^{\varepsilon}(B_{s})\text{\rm d}s\right)\right].

Then, we focus on a Gibbs measure on the Wiener space
\left(\Omega=C([0,\infty),{\mathbb{R}}^{3}),\mathcal{B}(C([0,\infty),{\mathbb{R}}^{3}))\right)
by

\displaystyle\mu_{\varepsilon,t,x}(\text{\rm d}\omega)=\frac{1}{G_{\varepsilon,t}(x)}\exp\left(-\lambda(\varepsilon)\int_{0}^{t}V^{\varepsilon}(\omega_{s})\text{\rm d}s\right)\mu(\text{\rm d}\omega),

(1.18)

where
\mu
is the Wiener measure on the Wiener space.

The proof of Theorem
1.3
is completed if we show the convergence of
G_{\varepsilon,t}(x)
and the weak convergence of
\mu_{\varepsilon,t,x}
.

Remark 1.4
.

Our proof is purely an analysis of
G_{\varepsilon,t}(x)
and
\mu_{\varepsilon,t,x}
, which are determined by
V^{\varepsilon}
. Therefore our analysis should extend to the countably many point interactions discussed in
[
2
, Chapter III]
.

Remark 1.5
.

\mu_{\varepsilon,t,x}
can be regarded as the law of Brownian motion in a deterministic obstacle. Sznitman’s textbook (which treats random obstacles) gives an SDE description of the dynamics of
\omega
under
\mu_{\varepsilon,t,x}

[
58
, Chapter 6, (1.8)]
:
Let
\{\omega_{s}\}_{0\leq s\leq t}
be the canonical path under
\mu_{\varepsilon,t,x}
. Then
\omega
is the solution of the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle\text{\rm d}\omega_{s}=\text{\rm d}B_{s}+\frac{\nabla G_{\varepsilon,t-s}(\omega_{s})}{G_{\varepsilon,t-s}(\omega_{s})}\text{\rm d}s,\quad 0\leq s\leq t,

\displaystyle\omega_{0}=x,

where
\{B_{s}\}_{0\leq s\leq t}
is a standard Brownian motion in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.

We can see that
G_{\varepsilon,t}(x)
and
\nabla G_{\varepsilon,t}(x)
converge to
G_{t}^{\alpha}(x)
and
\nabla G_{t}^{\alpha}(x)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
for
x\not=0
. Thus we may expect the limit process to satisfy the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle\text{\rm d}\omega^{t,x}_{s}=\text{\rm d}B_{s}+\frac{\nabla G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(\omega^{t,x}_{s})}{G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(\omega^{t,x}_{s})}\text{\rm d}s\quad 0\leq s\leq t

(1.19)

\displaystyle\omega_{0}=x.

This means the process under
\overline{P}_{\gamma,T}^{x}
can be regarded as a Brownian motion with a time-dependent singular drift
\frac{\nabla G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(\cdot)}{G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(\cdot)}
.
Such a process has been studied in the literature of the stochastic differential equations, and the existence and uniqueness of the solution of (
1.19
) is known under some conditions on the drift.
For instance, in
[
9
]
the drift term belongs to some Kato class measure, and in
[
45
]
, the time dependent drift term satisfies
L_{q-}L_{p}
-integrability.
However, we will see that
\frac{\nabla G_{t}^{\alpha}(x)}{G_{t}^{\alpha}(x)}
is singular at the origin and they does not satisfies these assumptions.
So, the existence and uniqueness of the solution of (
1.19
) is not covered by the existing results, and it still remains open.
Mian discussed (
1.19
) in
[
50
]
.

In the two-dimensional case, Chen
[
22
]
gives an SDE description of the process under
\overline{P}_{\gamma,T}^{x}
.
He approximates
\overline{P}_{\gamma,T}^{x}
using transformations of Bessel processes, focusing in particular on excursions.
Recently Clark and Mian
[

studied the local time of the process at the origin under

1.3
Interpretation of
\mu_{\varepsilon,t,x}
via time-space Markov process.

In this subsection, we will interpret the process
\omega
under
\mu_{t,\varepsilon,x}
via another process
\left\{W^{\varepsilon,t,x}(s):s\geq 0\right\}
.

First, we look at the expansion

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon,t}(x,y)

\displaystyle=p_{t}(x,y)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n\geq 1}(-\lambda(\varepsilon))^{n}\int_{\Lambda_{n}([0,t])}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3n}}p_{s_{1}}\left(x,x_{1}\right)V^{\varepsilon}(x_{1})\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(p_{s_{k+1}-s_{k}}\left(x_{k},x_{k+1}\right)V^{\varepsilon}(x_{k+1})\right)p_{t-s_{n}}(x_{n},y)\text{\rm d}\mathbf{x}_{n}\text{\rm d}\mathbf{t}_{n}

\displaystyle=p_{t}(x,y)+

\displaystyle 0,\quad&s\in R_{\widetilde{T}^{x}\backslash\{0\}}\\

Fix
T>0

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{W}_{m}\}_{m\geq 1}\text{is a $C([0,\infty),{\mathbb{R}}^{3})$-Cauchy sequence, $\mathbb{P}(\cdot|\widetilde{T}^{x})$-a.s.}

Proof.

It is clear that for each
m\geq\ell\geq 1

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{m}(s)-\mathcal{W}_{\ell}(s)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sqrt{|I_{n}|}B^{\mathrm{Bri}}_{n}\left(\frac{s-\inf I_{n}}{|I_{n}|}\right)\quad&s\in I_{n}\text{ for }N_{\ell}<n\leq N_{m}\\
0,\quad&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Hence, we can see that for each

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{W}_{m}(s)-\mathcal{W}_{\ell}(s)\right|>\varepsilon\right)\leq\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{m}\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{N_{j}<n\leq N_{j+1}}\left\{\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\left|B^{\mathrm{Bri}}_{n}(s)\right|>\varepsilon\sqrt{j}\right\}\right).

Since

is given by

The second statement follows from the

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon,t}(x)=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\lambda(\varepsilon)^{n}h^{\varepsilon}(x)\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1\{T_{n}^{\varepsilon,x}\leq t\}}{h^{\varepsilon}(S_{n}^{\varepsilon,x})}\right].

First, we remark from Lemma
2.11

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{h^{\varepsilon}(S_{n}^{\varepsilon,x})^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathtt{E}_{x}\left[\left|M_{n}^{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\right]+C\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle=C\int_{0}^{\infty}R\mathtt{P}_{x}\left(\left|M_{n}^{\varepsilon}\right|\geq R\right)\text{\rm d}R+C\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\leq Cn\int_{0}^{\infty}R\frac{\left({\max\{|x|,\varepsilon\}}+\max\{R,\varepsilon\}\right)}{\varepsilon}\frac{\max\{R,\varepsilon\}}{\varepsilon}{e^{-\frac{R^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}}}\text{\rm d}R+C\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\lambda(\varepsilon)^{n}h^{\varepsilon}(x)\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1\{T_{n}^{\varepsilon,x}\leq t\}}{h^{\varepsilon}(S_{n}^{\varepsilon,x})}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\frac{\delta}{a_{\varepsilon}}}\lambda(\varepsilon)^{n}h^{\varepsilon}(x)\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1\{T_{n}^{\varepsilon,x}\leq t\}}{h^{\varepsilon}(S_{n}^{\varepsilon,x})}\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=\frac{\delta}{a_{\varepsilon}}+1}^{{\frac{T}{a_{\varepsilon}}}}\lambda(\varepsilon)^{n}h^{\varepsilon}(x)\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1\{T_{n}^{\varepsilon,x}\leq t\}}{h^{\varepsilon}(S_{n}^{\varepsilon,x})}\right]

5
Comments

In the end of this article, we give some remarks on our Feynman-Kac formula associated with (
1.2
).

We shall recall that we prepared the process
\omega^{t,x}
with the law
\mu_{t,x}
and the functions
G^{(\alpha)}_{t}
to describe the solution of (
1.2
). Comparing with the standard Feynman-Kac formula (
1.17
), the normalizing constant
G^{(\alpha)}_{t}
seems to be unnatural.

To avoid
G^{(\alpha)}_{t}
, it would be better to see the Feynman-Kac formula via super-Brownian motion introduced by
[
60
,
29
]
.

Let
M_{F}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be a set of finite measure on
({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
with the topology of weak convergence. We denote
\mu(f)=\int f(x)\mu(\text{\rm d}x)
for
f\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
\mu\in M_{F}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. A super-Brownian motion
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is an
M_{F}(E)
-valued process which is characterized by
(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta,\alpha,\beta,\gamma,D)
and their initial measures, where
\alpha\in(0,1]
is a constant,
\beta
and
\gamma
are bounded continuous functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
\gamma(x)\geq 0
for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a domain.

Then, it is known that for
u_{0}\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,

\displaystyle\mathtt{E}_{\delta_{x}}\left[X_{t}(u_{0})\right]=E_{x}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\beta(\omega_{s})\text{\rm d}s\right)u_{0}(\omega_{t})\right],

where we denote by
\mathtt{P}_{\mu}
and
\mathtt{E}_{\mu}
the law and the expectation of super-Brownian motion with initial condition
\mu

[
54
, Exercise II.5.2(b)]
.

If we consider a family of super-Brownian motions
\{X^{\varepsilon}\}
with
\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta,-\lambda(\varepsilon)V^{\varepsilon},1,{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\right)
, then

\displaystyle\mathtt{E}_{\delta_{x}}\left[X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(u_{0})\right]=E_{x}\left[\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(\varepsilon)V_{\varepsilon}(\omega_{s})\text{\rm d}s\right)u_{0}(\omega_{t})\right].

By Lemma
3.5
, we know that the right-hand side converges to the non-trivial limit for
x\not=0
, and hence we may believe that there exists a super-Brownian motion
\{\mathcal{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
such that for
u_{0}\in C_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\{0\})
,

\displaystyle\mathtt{E}_{\delta_{x}}[\mathcal{X}_{t}(u_{0})]=G_{t}(x)\mathbb{E}\left[u_{0}\left(W^{t,x}(t)\right)\right]=u_{\alpha}(t,x)

for any
t>0
and any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\{0\}
.

Indeed, Fleischman and Mueller constructed the super-Brownian motion
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
with log-Laplace transition functional satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial_{t}}v=\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{\alpha}v-\eta v^{1+\beta}\quad\text{on }(0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\backslash\{0\}

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+0}v(t,x)=v(x)\geq 0\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\backslash\{0\}

for
\beta\in(0,1]
when
d=2
and
\beta\in(0,1)
when
d=3
.

Their result may suggest a physical perspective of super-Brownian motions via Schrödinger operator as Nagasawa already predicted in his textbook
[
51
]
.

Also, there are some known results suggesting non-trivial relationships between super-Brownian motions and Schrödinger operators as follows.
Let
\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a compact set.

Definition 5.1
.

\Sigma
is a removable set for
\Delta
in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
if and only if
\left.\Delta\right|_{C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\backslash\Sigma)}
is essentially self-adjoint in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

Definition 5.2
.

\Sigma
is a polar set for super-Brownian motion
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
if and only if
\mathtt{P}_{\delta_{x}}\left(\mathrm{supp}(X_{t})\cap\Sigma=\emptyset\textrm{ for all }t>0\right)=1
for any
x\not\in\Sigma.

Definition 5.3
.

\Sigma
is a removable singularity for all nonnegative solutions of equations
\Delta u=u(x)^{2}
if and only if
u\geq 0
and

\displaystyle\Delta u(x)=u(x)^{2},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\backslash\Sigma,

then
u=0
.

Definition 5.4
.

The Bessel capacity of a compact set
\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with index
(\gamma,p)
for
p>1
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cap}_{\gamma,p}(\Sigma)=\sup\left\{\nu(\Sigma):\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\text{\rm d}x\left(\int_{\Sigma}G_{\gamma}(x,y)\nu(\text{\rm d}y)\right)^{q}\leq 1\right\},

where
q=\frac{p}{p-1}
and

\displaystyle G_{\gamma}(x,y)=a_{\gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\frac{\gamma-d}{2}}e^{-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{2t}}\frac{e^{-\frac{t}{2}}}{t}\text{\rm d}t

(

is a constant) and the supremum is taken over all finite measures.

Then, it is known that for any compact set
\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the followings are equivalent:

(1)

\Sigma
is a removable set for
\Delta
.

(2)

\Sigma
is a polar set for super-Brownian motion.

(3)

\Sigma
is a removable singularity for all nonnegative solutions of equations
\Delta u=u(x)^{2}
.

(4)

\mathrm{Cap}_{2,2}(\Sigma)=0
.

The equivalence
(1)
\Leftrightarrow
(4)
is proved in
[
42
]
,
(2)
\Leftrightarrow
(3)
is proved in
[
34
]
, and
(3)
\Leftrightarrow
(4)
is proved in
[
8
]
. Le Gall proved the one-to-one correspondence between all solutions of
\Delta u=u^{2}
and the finite measures on
\Sigma
that do not charge sets of zero capacity if
\Sigma
is not a removable singularity in
[
46
]
.

In particular,
[
42
]
pointed out the characterization of the removability of
\Sigma
via
two-parameter additive Brownian motion
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-valued two parameter Brownian sheet
. Since
(1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2)
tells the removability of
\Sigma
can be characterized in terms of super-Brownian motion, it is plausible to generalize the result by Fleischman-Mueller
[
36
]
as follows:

Conjecture 5.5
.

Let
\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a compact set which is not a removable set for
\Delta
in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Then, there exist a self-adjoint extension of

\displaystyle\|P^{n}(x,\cdot)-\pi(\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\to 0,\quad\pi\text{-a.a.$x$,}

(A.1)

as
n\to\infty
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
denotes the total variation of the measure. In particular, we are interested in the rate of this convergence.

The Markov chain
X
is called
V
-uniform ergodic
if there exist a positive, Borel measurable function
V\geq 1
with
\int V(x)\text{\rm d}\pi<\infty
,
M>0
, and
q\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{|f|\leq V}\|P^{n}(x,f)-\pi(f)\|=MV(x)\rho^{n}

for all
x\in X
, where the supremum is taken over all measurable
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying
|f(x)|\leq V(x)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. In particular,
V
-uniform ergodicity implies (
A.1
).

In
[
10
]
, a sufficient condition for

-uniform ergodicity for the Markov chain

is given.

Theorem A.1
.

(
[
10

)
Suppose

(i)

Minorization condition. There exists

,
\tilde{\beta}>0
and a probability measure
\nu

\displaystyle P(x,A)\geq\tilde{\beta}\nu(A)

for any

.

(ii)

Drift condition. There exist a measurable function
V:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[1,\infty)
and constants

and
K<\infty
satisfying

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}V(y)P(x,\text{\rm d}y)\leq\begin{cases}\lambda V(x),\quad&x\not\in B\\
K,\quad&x\in B.\end{cases}

(iii)

\beta>0

\displaystyle=|x|\int_{|y|\leq 1}\frac{2(\pi)^{\frac{3}{2}}p_{1}(y)}{2\pi|x-y|\int_{0}^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}}u^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-u}\text{\rm d}u}\text{\rm d}y
