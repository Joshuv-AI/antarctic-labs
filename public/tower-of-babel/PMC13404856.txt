Availability, treatment costs and affordability of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs for diabetes in 10 countries: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zhuangqi Li, Shuting Zhang, Bao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes complicated by cardiovascular diseases and chronic kidney diseases poses a growing global burden. Sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) are increasingly recommended, yet their availability, treatment costs and affordability across countries of different income levels remain poorly understood.ObjectivesTo evaluate the regulatory availability, treatment costs and affordability of all marketed SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs across 10 countries, including Nepal, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, South Africa, Brazil, China, Türkiye, Italy and France.Research design and methodsWe examined 22 SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs across 10 countries using publicly available sources. Regulatory availability was defined as marketing authorisation in a given country. Monthly treatment costs were estimated using minimum 28-day retail medicine prices based on recommended dosing regimens. Affordability was assessed as the proportion of the national minimum monthly wage required to purchase 1 month of treatment. Differences in regulatory availability across income groups were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with subgroup analyses by regulatory capacity. Associations between treatment costs and gross domestic product (GDP) per capita were examined using Spearman correlation. Given the small sample size, analyses were exploratory and hypothesis-generating.ResultsRegulatory availability ranged from 2 medicines in Nepal to 15 in China (9.1%–68.2%). No statistically significant differences in regulatory availability were observed across or within income groups. Regulatory availability of SGLT-2 inhibitors did not vary by regulatory capacity, whereas countries with higher regulatory capacity had greater regulatory approval coverage for GLP-1 RAs (p=0.045). Minimum monthly treatment costs ranged from US$3.68–50.30 for SGLT-2 inhibitors and US$22.95–220.80 for GLP-1 RAs. SGLT-2 inhibitor treatment costs increased with GDP per capita (ρ=0.939; p<0.001), while no statistically significant association was observed for GLP-1 RAs. SGLT-2 inhibitors were more affordable than GLP-1 RAs, requiring 0.7%–5.1% versus 1.6%–110.4% of monthly minimum wages.ConclusionSubstantial cross-country variation exists in the regulatory availability, treatment costs and affordability of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs. Higher regulatory capacity was associated with greater regulatory availability for GLP-1 RAs. Coordinated efforts to strengthen regulatory capacity and improve affordability may help improve the regulatory availability and affordability of these therapies across countries.

Introduction

Diabetes, particularly its cardiovascular and chronic kidney complications, poses an increasing global public health burden.1 In 2021, 537 million adults worldwide were living with diabetes,2 3 of whom approximately 90% had type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), according to the International Diabetes Federation.4 Among individuals with T2DM, cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death, accounting for around 50% of all deaths.5 6 In addition, approximately half of patients with T2DM and one-third of those with type 1 diabetes develop chronic kidney disease (CKD), which substantially contributes to excess mortality and further amplifies the risk of diabetes-related cardiovascular complications.7–10 Preventing and managing CVD and CKD has therefore become a central objective of comprehensive diabetes care.11 As the prevalence of diabetes continues to rise, there is an urgent need for effective and accessible therapeutic options that can mitigate these complications, reduce treatment burden and improve patient outcomes.

Evidence has demonstrated that sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) reduce the risks of atherosclerotic CVD events, including myocardial infarctions and strokes, as well as heart failure exacerbations and progression of CKD.12–15 These drugs have therefore become established second line, and in some clinical contexts arguably first line, glucose-lowering therapies for patients with type 2 diabetes and cardiorenal disease, irrespective of baseline glycaemic control.16 Clinical practice guidelines from the American Diabetes Association,17 European Association for the Study of Diabetes,18 Chinese Diabetes Society,19 International Diabetes Federation and several regional entities consistently recommend SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs in preference to sulfonylureas for individuals with atherosclerotic CVD, heart failure, CKD and compelling needs to minimise hypoglycaemia.20 21 Furthermore, GLP-1 RAs are also recommended for patients with type 2 diabetes without established CVD but with indicators of high risk, to reduce the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events.18 22

Despite their well-established clinical benefits, access to SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs remains highly uneven across countries and populations.23 24 The WHO has repeatedly highlighted that equitable access to medicines and a reliable supply of essential therapies are frequently hindered by multiple systemic factors. Most notably regulatory barriers that influence the timing and condition of market entry, followed by medicine pricing.25 For newer glucose-lowering agents, GLP-1 RAs and SGLT-2 inhibitors, delayed regulatory approval, high prices and limited inclusion in public reimbursement schemes often translate into substantial out-of-pocket expenditures for patients.26 27 These constraints disproportionately affect low- and middle-income countries and vulnerable populations, leading to marked disparities in the availability and affordability of therapies with proven clinical benefits. Numerous studies have compared medicine prices, treatment costs and affordability across countries, consistently documenting the challenges faced by low- and middle-income countries in accessing essential medicines and selected newer therapies.28 29 However, relatively few studies have focused specifically on newer diabetes medicines,30–32 and the limited existing evidence is largely restricted to a small number of countries or regions.33 34 Although these studies provide important insights, they remain insufficient to comprehensively characterise the availability, affordability and access to evidence-based newer diabetes therapies across diverse global settings. A comprehensive understanding of these dimensions is essential to inform more effective policy design and governance of diabetes care globally.35

Therefore, we conducted a cross-sectional assessment of all GLP-1 RAs and SGLT-2 inhibitors that were marketed globally as of 30 November 2025 and examined their regulatory availability, treatment costs and affordability across 10 countries spanning different income levels. These countries comprised four lower- and middle-income countries (Nepal, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka), four upper-middle-income countries (South Africa, Brazil, China and Türkiye) and two high-income countries (Italy and France). Low-income countries were not included because of limitations in data availability.

The selection of regulatory availability, treatment costs and affordability was guided by the established WHO/Health Action International (HAI) framework for assessing medicine access, which typically incorporates price, availability and affordability as key dimensions. Building on this framework, we adapted these indicators to better reflect the characteristics of newer diabetes therapies, where regulatory approval status is an important prerequisite for market entry.

Countries were selected to capture heterogeneity in regulatory systems, pharmaceutical market structures, pricing policies and health system contexts across different settings. Income group classification was used as a structured comparative framework to facilitate cross-country analysis rather than to assume that national income is the primary determinant of regulatory availability, treatment costs or availability of medicines. The final sample was determined by data availability and the need to ensure variation across development levels.

Methods

Sample identification

We identified all registered SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs worldwide, using their generic names for consistency. Combination formulations containing SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs were excluded, as such products are generally indicated for more complex conditions and single-agent formulations are typically approved before their combination counterparts. In total, 22 medicines were included in our analysis (online supplemental table S1).

We examined the marketing status of those medicines across 10 countries. According to the World Bank income classification for 2024–2025,36 four countries were classified as lower-middle-income countries (Nepal, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka), four as upper-middle-income countries (South Africa, Brazil, China and Türkiye) and two as high-income countries (France and Italy). See more details of included countries in online supplemental table S2.

Data on marketing authorisation were obtained from the official websites of the national drug regulatory authorities of each country. List price, defined as publicly available retail spot prices without discounts or reimbursement adjustments, was used. List price data were primarily collected from official national sources of retail medicine prices. Data for China were obtained from Menet, a well-established pharmaceutical database in China. When official price data were unavailable, we adopted a substitution approach: prices were obtained from three major online pharmacy platforms, and the lowest available price was used for analysis. Details on the data sources for marketing status and price information are provided in online supplemental table S3.

Publicly available list prices and online pharmacy data were used because they were the most consistently accessible sources across countries and enabled standardised cross-country comparisons. Online pharmacy price data were only used for a small number of medicines when official price sources were unavailable. Potential limitations related to the use of list prices and online pharmacy data have been further discussed in the limitation section.

Availability

Because comprehensive real-world utilisation data for SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs are not consistently available across countries, availability in this study was defined as regulatory availability, measured by whether these medicines had received marketing authorisation in each country. This indicator reflects the initial step for a drug to enter the national market, rather than actual availability at healthcare facilities or pharmacies. This approach aligns with previous studies,37 38 although it differs from the WHO/HAI standardised methodology, which typically evaluates medicine availability at the facility level.39 Therefore, the regulatory availability estimates in this study may overestimate real-world patient access, particularly in settings where procurement systems, reimbursement coverage, or supply chains remain limited. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to assess differences in regulatory availability across income groups and pharmaceutical regulatory capacity groups, with additional comparisons conducted among countries within each income group where appropriate. Regulatory capacity was classified into three levels—high, medium and low—based on WHO benchmarking frameworks and publicly available evidence regarding the performance of national regulatory authorities. Countries were categorised as having high regulatory capacity if their agency had achieved WHO Global Benchmark Tool maturity level three or four for pharmaceuticals or vaccines or had been designated as a WHO-listed Authority (WLA). Medium regulatory capacity referred to settings where available evidence indicated that the national regulatory authority was progressing toward higher maturity levels but had not yet achieved WHO-recognised maturity status. Low regulatory capacity was assigned when publicly available sources showed substantial deficiencies in the regulatory system. Based on this assessment, Italy, France, Türkiye, China and South Africa were classified as having high regulatory capacity; Brazil and Pakistan as medium; and Nepal, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka as low. More details are provided in online supplemental table S4.

Medicines treatment costs

Given that type-2 diabetes is a chronic condition requiring lifelong pharmacological treatment, our analysis evaluated the minimum monthly treatment cost required for patients to maintain therapy. Treatment costs were estimated using publicly available medicine list prices and recommended dosing regimens. In some countries, generic versions of certain medicines, such as dapagliflozin and empagliflozin, were already available during the study period. For each medicine, when generic versions were available in a given country, the lowest available generic retail price was used. When no generic product was available, the corresponding brand-name medicine retail price was applied. This hierarchical approach was used to ensure that the lowest available market price was captured in each setting. To compare the treatment costs of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs across countries, monthly (28-day) treatment cost for type 2 diabetes was derived based on the recommended dosing regimens specified in the approved drug labelling (online supplemental table S1). The lowest monthly treatment cost for each medicine was estimated separately. Because not all medicines were approved in every country and medicines targeting the same mechanism of action were considered therapeutically interchangeable,40 41 we compared the range of monthly treatment costs of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs by class across countries. Price data were collected from October to November 2025, standardised to 2024 base year using the annual Consumer Price Index (CPI), and converted to US dollars according to the official exchange rates for 2024. CPI data were available through October 2025 for all countries except South Africa, for which data were only available up to September 2025. Further details are provided in online supplemental table S2.

Spearman rank correlations were used to assess the associations between medicine treatment costs and national gross domestic product (GDP) per capita in 2024. Data on GDP per capita were obtained from the World Bank.

Medicine affordability

To assess the affordability of medicines in each country, we estimated the number of days’ minimum wage required to purchase 1 month (28 days) of treatment at nominal prices, following the method developed by the WHO and HAI. While the WHO/HAI approach measures affordability using the daily wage of the lowest-paid unskilled government worker,39 we instead relied on national minimum wage data, as in previous studies,42–44 because such data were more consistently available and standardised across countries, thereby improving comparability in this multicountry analysis. In contrast, definitions and availability of wage data for the lowest-paid government worker vary substantially across settings, which may limit cross-country comparability. However, this approach may limit direct comparability with studies applying the WHO/HAI methodology based on the wage of the lowest-paid government worker.

Data on national minimum wages were obtained from the International Labour Organization (ILO)45 (online supplemental table S2). Italy has no statutory minimum wage; instead, minimum wages are established through sectoral collective bargaining agreements, and nearly 100% of employees are covered by a collective bargaining agreement.46 We therefore used Eurofound’s database of minimum pay regulated in collective agreements to obtain sector-level minimum pay rates for Italy. The overall minimum wage was calculated as the average minimum wage of all sector collective agreements. This approach is consistent with the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development guidance47 on using collectively bargained wage floors in countries without statutory minima and with ILO discussions on minimum wages through collective bargaining.48

Statistical analysis

We used two-sided statistical tests, with statistical significance defined as p<0.05. Differences in regulatory availability across income groups and regulatory capacity groups were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Associations between treatment costs and GDP per capita were examined using Spearman rank correlation. All data analyses were conducted using SPSS V.22 (IBM SPSS Statistics). Statistical analyses were performed between September and November 2025.

Given the small sample size (10 countries), statistical analyses were primarily exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Therefore, findings from inferential analyses, particularly non-significant results, should be interpreted cautiously.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Regulatory availability of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs across countries

A total of 22 medicines included in this study have been approved globally, comprising 12 SGLT-2 inhibitors and 10 GLP-1 RAs. Among these, six were initially approved in the USA, five in the European Medicines Agency (EMA), five in China, four in Japan and one in India (online supplemental table S1). To date, no country had marketing authorisation for all 22 medicines. The total number of registered medicines per country ranges from 2 in Nepal to 15 in China, corresponding to a regulatory availability proportion of 9.1%–68.2% (online supplemental table S5).

Figure 1 illustrates the regulatory availability of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs across all 10 countries. Except for Nepal, where only two SGLT-2 inhibitors had received marketing authorisation, all other countries had regulatory approval for both types. When stratified by income level, lower-middle-income countries had a median regulatory availability of 25.0% (range: 9.1%–50.0%) for SGLT-2 inhibitors and 29.2% (range: 16.7%–50.0%) for GLP-1 RAs. In the upper-middle-income group, the corresponding median regulatory availability was 29.6% (range: 27.3%–68.2%) and 20.9% (range: 16.7%–50.0%), respectively. In high-income countries, the median regulatory availability reached 47.8% (range: 45.5%–50.0%) for SGLT-2 inhibitors and 33.3% for GLP-1 RAs (table 1). These findings indicated that high-income countries generally had broader regulatory approval coverage for newer antidiabetic medicines. China also demonstrated a relatively high level of regulatory availability, comparable to that observed in high-income countries. However, regulatory availability reflects market authorisation status only and does not necessarily indicate actual patient access.

We further assessed the regulatory availability of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs across income groups and among countries within each group. For both classes, no statistically significant differences were observed across or within income levels (table 1). However, given the small sample size and descriptive nature of the analysis, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Regulatory availability also varied by pharmaceutical regulatory capacity. No statistically significant differences were observed for SGLT-2 inhibitors across regulatory capacity groups (p=0.549), whereas statistically significant differences were identified for GLP-1 RAs (p=0.045). Countries with higher regulatory capacity generally demonstrated higher regulatory availability of GLP-1 RAs (table 1). Given the small sample size, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered direct evidence of differences in actual patient access.

Monthly treatment costs of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs across countries

A cross-country comparison showed substantial variability in monthly treatment costs for SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs, with GLP-1 RAs therapies generally being more expensive. Detailed distributions of monthly treatment costs are presented in figure 2 and table 2. For SGLT-2 inhibitors, all 10 countries had at least two products available, with China and Pakistan having the largest number of products on the market. Minimum monthly treatment costs ranged from US$3.68 in Nepal to US$50.30 in Italy. Most countries exhibited relatively narrow treatment cost ranges; however, China, Bangladesh and Italy showed substantial within-country variation, with monthly treatment costs ranging from US$14.35 to US$119.00, US$6.74 to US$67.40 and US$50.30 to US$92.97, respectively. This wider variation largely reflects the fact that a larger number of SGLT-2 inhibitors available in these markets and the price information for these products was more comprehensively available. For GLP-1 RAs, Nepal had no products available, whereas China had nine products on the market as of the end of November 2025. Minimum monthly treatment costs ranged from US$22.95 in Pakistan to US$220.80 in Sri Lanka. Maximum monthly treatment costs exceeded US$800 in Italy, China and Pakistan, largely reflecting access to newer GLP-1 RAs products that remain under patent protection, such as tirzepatide. Price data for several products were missing in Pakistan, Sri Lanka and France, which may lead to an underestimation of within-country cost variation.

In further analyses, we examined whether cross-country differences in the treatment costs of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs were associated with national income levels. A strong positive correlation was observed between SGLT-2 inhibitors treatment costs and GDP per capita (ρ=0.939; p<0.001; figure 3), indicating that higher-income countries tended to have higher treatment costs. By contrast, no statistically significant association was observed for GLP-1 RAs (ρ=−0.183; p=0.637), although this should not be interpreted as evidence of no association. These differences may be related to variations in pricing regulation, market access timing and product patent status across countries, although these factors were not directly assessed in this study.

Affordability of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs across countries

We calculated the proportion of the minimum monthly wage required to pay for 1 month of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RA therapy, revealing substantial variation in affordability across countries (figure 4). Overall, SGLT-2 inhibitors were more affordable than GLP-1 RAs in all 10 countries. The lowest proportion of wages required for SGLT-2 inhibitors ranged from 0.7% in Türkiye to 5.1% in Brazil, compared with 1.6% in France to 110.4% in Sri Lanka for GLP-1 RAs. The most affordable SGLT-2 inhibitors were dapagliflozin and empagliflozin, which were available in all countries and had generics at lower prices. In contrast, the least affordable SGLT-2 inhibitors was ganagliflozin marketed in China, an originator product unavailable elsewhere, requiring 21.9% of the monthly minimum wage. For several GLP-1 RAs, monthly treatment costs exceeded a full month’s wage in some countries, for example, tirzepatide required 294.2%, 203.2%, 196.1% and 135.5% of the minimum monthly wage in Pakistan, China, Italy and Brazil, respectively, highlighting substantial affordability challenges for newer GLP-1 RA therapy.

Discussion

Therapies with strong clinical evidence for diabetes complicated by CVD and CKD are not always available in a timely, affordable and equitable manner across countries. Using cross-sectional data from 10 countries, we examined cross-country differences in the regulatory availability, treatment costs and affordability of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs. Countries with higher income levels and greater pharmaceutical regulatory capacity generally had higher regulatory availability of a broader range of medicines. However, no country in our analysis had regulatory approval for all 22 marketed products. Treatment costs also differed markedly across countries. Countries with higher GDP per capita tended to incur higher treatment costs for SGLT-2 inhibitors, whereas no statistically significant association was observed for GLP-1 RAs. Overall, SGLT-2 inhibitors were consistently more affordable than GLP-1 RAs. In many settings, particularly in lower-middle-income countries, the high monthly treatment costs of certain new therapies translated into a substantial financial burden, even when these medicines were formally available on the market. Together, these findings highlight persistent cross-country disparities in regulatory availability, treatment costs and affordability of new diabetes therapies, which may contribute to broader inequities in access-related outcomes worldwide.

National regulatory capacity appeared to be an important factor associated with regulatory availability, as it directly shapes how medicines are evaluated, approved and brought to market.25 Countries with stronger regulatory capacity tended to approve a greater number of products, a pattern that was particularly pronounced for GLP-1 RAs. Consistent with previous studies, new medicines are more likely to obtain marketing authorisation and to do so more rapidly in high-income countries,38 49 which generally have stronger regulatory capacity.50 However, according to the WHO, approximately 70% of countries worldwide continue to face substantial challenges in the evaluation and authorisation of pharmaceuticals and medical products,51 largely due to weak or underdeveloped regulatory systems. To address these constraints, the WHO promotes regulatory collaboration and reliance through the WLA framework,52 enabling national regulatory authorities, particularly those in low- and middle-income countries, to rely on the scientific assessments and regulatory decisions of WLA-designated reference agencies when making their own approval decisions.53 In this context, and alongside other strategic considerations, pharmaceutical companies typically prioritise submissions to stringent regulatory authorities, such as the US Food and Drug Administration and the EMA.38 49 Approval by these agencies often facilitates and accelerates subsequent market entry in other countries and regions worldwide, a pattern that has been consistently documented in previous studies.54–56

In fact, regulatory capacity is only one of several factors influencing the regulatory availability of new medicines. Price regulation has become increasingly complex and may contribute to both direct effects on launch delays within the regulating country and indirect spillover effects on launch timing in other markets.57 Previous studies have shown that price regulation is associated with delayed market entry,57 58 as price controls or administrative pricing mechanisms can reduce manufacturer’s expected prices and, consequently, the net present value of anticipated revenues. Moreover, in the context of widespread use of external reference pricing across countries, pricing decisions in one market may have far-reaching implications beyond national borders.57 59 Beyond pricing policies, pharmaceutical supply chains and distribution systems represent another important determinant of medicine access.60 In many low-income countries, limited fiscal capacity, lower household purchasing power and, in some cases, fragile supply chain infrastructure constrain access to new medicines. These settings are more likely to rely on lower-priced products,42 61 which may further reduce manufacturer’s incentives to introduce newly developed therapies, contributing to lower availability. In parallel, the concentration of clinical trials in high-income countries may further reinforce structural inequities in drug development and subsequent market access.62 Taken together, these dynamics may help explain cross-country differences in regulatory availability and may also contribute to disparities in broader access-related outcomes for new diabetes medicines.

Beyond differences in availability, our analysis revealed distinct income-related patterns in the treatment costs of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs. Specifically, SGLT-2 inhibitors treatment costs were positively associated with GDP per capita, whereas no statistically significant association was observed for GLP-1 RAs; however, this should not be interpreted as evidence of no association. The observed difference may reflect the fundamental disparities in pharmaceutical characteristics, market competition and regulatory frameworks. SGLT-2 inhibitors are small molecule chemical medicines,63 whereas GLP-1 RAs are biologic or biosimilars.64 Biologics typically require longer development timelines, higher manufacturing complexity and face more prolonged and robust patent protection and market exclusivity,65 all of which may contribute to higher treatment costs. Moreover, prices of chemical medicines usually decline substantially following patent expiry owing to rapid generics entry and then intensified market competition,66 as illustrated by dapagliflozin and empagliflozin, both of which have been approved in 10 countries. By contrast, prices of biologic medicines often remain high even after biosimilars enter the market,67 reflecting regulatory complexity, limited clinical and market substitutability and weaker price competition. Previous studies have provided substantial evidence on these structural differences between small molecule medicines and biologics,63–67 and our findings extend this evidence to SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs.

These mechanisms also help explain why SGLT-2 inhibitors were consistently more affordable than GLP-1 RAs across countries. Nevertheless, GLP-1 RAs continue to pose substantial affordability challenges, with some medicines requiring the equivalent of several months’ wages to cover 1 month of treatment. Affordability constraints were particularly pronounced in lower-middle-income countries, where treatment costs represented a disproportionately high financial burden compared with upper-middle- and high-income settings, highlighting substantial disparities in affordability and potential financial barriers to accessing new diabetes medicines.

Our study provides important insights for national policymakers and international health organisations by characterising cross-country disparities in regulatory availability, treatment costs and affordability of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs across 10 countries. However, the limited availability of publicly accessible price data from low-income countries constrains our ability to comprehensively characterise global inequities in the availability, treatment costs and affordability of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs. Medicine price transparency remains particularly poor in low-income settings, as our analysis found that no low-income country had publicly accessible price data. Price transparency is also limited, to varying degrees, in countries at other income levels. Indeed, debates surrounding medicine price transparency have long been contentious.68 Proponents argue that greater transparency facilitates the application of EPR, strengthens procurement and price negotiations, reduces medicine prices69 and helps contain rising pharmaceutical expenditures. In contrast, the pharmaceutical industry views price discrimination through confidential discounts as a key mechanism for promoting access in lower-resourced countries,70 while full price transparency, particularly disclosure of confidential discounts, may undermine differential pricing strategies worldwide, weaken incentives for voluntary discounts and ultimately limit affordable access in lower-resourced countries. However, policymakers increasingly recognise that reliance on confidential discounts has not contributed to better affordability and availability,71 and may even have counterproductive effects. While stakeholders hold differing positions on this issue, there is broad agreement that improving transparency in medicines pricing can support more informed regulatory decisions and policy design aimed at addressing inequities in access to medicines.72 Although achieving full transparency of actual transaction prices across countries remains highly challenging, greater disclosure of prices at some points along the pharmaceutical supply chain, particularly list price, may represent a pragmatic and necessary step forward.

In this cross-sectional study, we observed substantial variation in the regulatory availability, treatment costs and affordability of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs across 10 countries. We also examined their associations with selected national-level characteristics and discussed key influencing factors. These findings underscore the need for international strategies aimed at improving regulatory availability and affordability of new diabetes medicines and reducing cross-country disparities. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously when extrapolating to low-income countries, which were not included in this analysis and may face substantially greater barriers to medicine access and affordability. Strengthening regional regulatory coordination may facilitate more timely market entry and improve medicine availability.52–56 In addition, inclusion of high-value SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs in national essential medicine lists and reimbursement schemes may help improve affordability and reduce financial barriers to access, particularly in lower-resource settings. Regarding pricing, cross-regional joint procurement initiatives have been conducted in several settings, such as Latin America, East Africa, the Gulf Cooperation Council, the Organization of Eastern Caribbean States and several European Union countries,73 to enhance price negotiation and reduce pharmaceutical expenditure. Similar efforts have recently been undertaken in China and the Association of Southeast Asian Nations.74 However, the effectiveness of these joint procurement mechanisms has been largely confined to generic medicines, with originator medicines often constrained by patent protection. In this regard, the Medicines Patent Pool (MPP) represents a potentially important policy instrument.75 Through voluntary licensing arrangements with patent holders, the MPP seeks to promote the diffusion of essential pharmaceutical products in low- and middle-income countries, especially in smaller markets where commercial incentives for entry may be limited.76 Initially focused on HIV/AIDS, hepatitis C and tuberculosis, the MPP has progressively expanded its mandate to new disease areas such as cancer, CVDs, diabetes and COVID-19,77 highlighting its evolving role in addressing global inequities in access to high-value SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs. Following patent expiry, promotion of generic medicines and biosimilars may further enhance market competition and contribute to long-term reduction in treatment costs.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the analysis relied on publicly available price data; as a result, price information was unavailable for several medicines that had been launched in specific countries, including sotagliflozin, bexagliflozin and lixisenatide in Pakistan; dulaglutide in Sri Lanka; and ertugliflozin, tirzepatide and albiglutide in France. Second, this study did not include low-income countries because publicly accessible price data for SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs were unavailable in these settings, which limits a comprehensive assessment of global disparities in regulatory availability, treatment costs and affordability. This exclusion represents an important limitation because it may introduce systematic selection bias in our cross-country comparisons. Low-income countries are likely to face the greatest barriers to regulatory approval, medicine affordability and healthcare access; therefore, excluding these settings may lead to underestimation of the true magnitude of global inequities. Consequently, the findings may overrepresent countries with relatively stronger regulatory systems and better healthcare infrastructure, limiting the external validity and generalisability of our conclusions regarding international equity. Future studies incorporating data from low-income countries and alternative data sources are needed to provide a more comprehensive assessment of global disparities. Third, we examined the regulatory availability of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs based on their marketing authorisation status, which reflects market approval rather than actual access. Regulatory availability should not be interpreted as equivalent to real-world access, as market authorisation represents only one component of access. Actual access is also influenced by multiple downstream factors, including procurement systems, reimbursement coverage, supply-chain efficiency, geographic accessibility, prescribing practices and medicine utilisation. Therefore, our findings on regulatory availability should be interpreted as reflecting differences in market authorisation status across countries rather than measures of patient access. Fourth, our affordability analysis was primarily based on publicly available list prices and online pharmacy data and did not account for health insurance coverage, confidential rebates, procurement arrangements, differences between public and private sector pricing and other price concessions. As a result, these prices may not fully reflect actual patient out-of-pocket expenditure or transaction prices across countries, given substantial variation in insurance coverage, reimbursement arrangements, confidential rebates and other price concessions. Although this may affect absolute affordability levels, the use of list prices is unlikely to substantially distort cross-country comparisons. Similar data limitations exist across countries, although the magnitude of pricing opacity may vary. Consequently, the affordability estimates may overestimate the financial burden faced by patients in some settings. Further studies incorporating real-world reimbursement arrangements are therefore needed to better characterise the true financial burden of these therapies. In addition, affordability was assessed using statutory minimum wage, which may not fully capture actual household income or purchasing power. In countries with large informal sectors or substantial income inequity, this approach may overestimate affordability, particularly among economically vulnerable populations. Fifth, given the small sample of only 10 countries, the statistical analyses in this study were primarily exploratory and hypothesis-generating. The limited sample size reduced statistical power and may have increased the risk of type II error. Therefore, non-significant findings should not be interpreted as evidence of no association, and further studies including larger and more diverse samples are needed to validate these findings. Sixth, this study was based on country-level aggregate data therefore represents an ecological analysis. As a result, observed associations at the country level should not be interpreted as reflecting individual-level relationships, and the potential for ecological fallacy should be considered.

Conclusion

Our findings reveal substantial cross-country disparities in the regulatory availability, treatment costs and affordability of SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs. These disparities may reflect differences in regulatory capacity, pharmaceutical market dynamics and pricing policies across countries. Strengthening international regulatory collaboration and exploring coordinated pricing and procurement strategies may help improve the regulatory availability and affordability of new diabetes therapies worldwide.
