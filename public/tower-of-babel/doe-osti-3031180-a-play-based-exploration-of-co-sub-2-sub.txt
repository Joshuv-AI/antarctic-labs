EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report presents a stakeholder mapping analysis conducted as part of the Play-Based
Exploration of CO₂ Storage in the Illinois Basin project (DOE Cooperative Agreement DE-
FE0032366). The analysis was undertaken to systematically identify, categorize, and
characterize stakeholder groups and community conditions relevant to the planning and
deployment of carbon capture and storage (CCS) infrastructure across the Illinois Basin. Its
purpose is to establish a contextual baseline that informs targeted engagement strategies,
supports equitable project planning, and strengthens the social dimensions of CCS site screening.
The report begins by outlining the rationale for stakeholder mapping in the context of CCS
project development. Stakeholder classes, including governmental and regulatory bodies, local
authorities, community organizations, environmental advocates, industry partners, academic
institutions, landowners, and the general public, are defined and their roles in influencing or
being influenced by project outcomes are described. The report recommends establishing a
formal stakeholder register, interest–influence segmentation matrices, and data governance
protocols to support engagement throughout the project lifecycle.
The core of the report is a regional vulnerability profile that examines existing health burdens,
environmental exposures, and socioeconomic conditions across the Illinois Basin. Health
indicators analyzed include disability prevalence, life expectancy, cardiovascular disease,
asthma, and cancer rates. Environmental exposure metrics encompass wastewater discharge
patterns, toxic air releases, and drinking water non-compliance. Socioeconomic factors addressed
include educational attainment, household income, and unemployment. For each indicator, the
geographic distribution across Illinois counties and census tracts is mapped and discussed, with
particular attention to concentrations in the greater Chicagoland area and parts of central and
southern Illinois.
Key findings include the following: Communities historically burdened by industrial emissions,
legacy contamination, and socioeconomic disadvantage exhibit heightened sensitivity to new
industrial development, including CCS projects. Disability prevalence is elevated in southern
Illinois, requiring accessibility-focused engagement practices. Low life expectancy clusters
correlate with areas of concentrated industrial activity and limited healthcare access. Respiratory
and cardiovascular disease rates are highest in regions proximate to industrial corridors and
major transportation infrastructure. Drinking water non-compliance and wastewater discharge
burdens are geographically dispersed, with notable concentrations in northern Illinois. Lower
educational attainment and higher unemployment, particularly in the Chicagoland region and
selected rural areas, indicate populations that may face barriers to participation in public
processes and to accessing project-related employment opportunities.
The report concludes that effective CCS deployment in the Illinois Basin requires integration of
environmental, health, and socioeconomic data into siting, design, and engagement decisions.
Recommended actions include establishing pre-activity baselines for health and environmental
conditions; incorporating health-protective design criteria into siting and construction plans;
coordinating with local utilities and agencies on infrastructure improvements; deploying
accessible, multilingual communication strategies; developing workforce pipelines aligned with
project phases; and publishing transparent monitoring data throughout the project lifecycle.
These measures are intended to build trust, reduce opposition, and ensure that CCS project
outcomes align with local conditions and stakeholder priorities across the region.

INTRODUCTION
Industrial and power-generation activities have historically been concentrated in parts of the
Illinois Basin. Accordingly, project objectives must encompass not only technical analysis and
geological data collection but also the formal definition, segmentation, and prioritization of
stakeholders to enable disciplined engagement. From a technical perspective, the
Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Special Report on Carbon Capture and
Storage indicates that, when appropriately sited and managed, CO₂ capture and storage generally
present low environmental risk. Nevertheless, project outcomes are strongly conditioned by local
and regional stakeholder dynamics, including stakeholder acceptance, distribution of benefits,
and perception of potential impacts.
To support these requirements, a structured stakeholder-mapping and categorization process was
executed to systematically identify entities capable of influencing, or being influenced by,
project implementation. The process delineated affected parties; assessed relative influence and
benefit pathways; and documented interests, concerns, and prospective roles. Stakeholder classes
included governmental and regulatory bodies, local authorities, community organizations,
environmental advocates, industry partners, academic institutions, landowners, and the public.
Governmental and regulatory entities oversee permitting, compliance, and long-term engagement
obligations. Local authorities manage land use, utilities, emergency coordination, and right-of-
way considerations that affect siting and construction. Community organizations and faith-based
groups surface resident priorities, help calibrate engagement formats, and support two-way
information flow. Advocates provide technical scrutiny of design choices, monitoring plans, and
cumulative-impact analyses. Industry partners and trade associations contribute operational
expertise, supply-chain capacity, and workforce pathways. Academic institutions and extension
programs offer research support, independent review, and training pipelines. Landowners control
access and easements and are central to compensation frameworks and dispute resolution.
Stakeholders will ultimately confer social acceptance and potentially provide comments on risk
perception, benefits, and performance over the project lifecycle. Categorization by interest and
influence enabled the design of targeted communication and engagement strategies characterized
by proactivity, transparency, and role clarity. This methodology strengthens governance,
facilitates trust-building, and promotes inclusive participation, factors that are critical for
regional CCS execution.
Preliminary profiles of demographic, economic, and health characteristics within the Illinois
Basin were developed to inform engagement planning. Multiple data sources were integrated,
including socioeconomic indicators, infrastructure proximity, and historical industrial
development patterns. An initial screen using U.S. Department of Energy mapping tools
supported identification of stakeholder groups within the defined geography. The resulting
stakeholder-definition and categorization framework establishes a foundational reference for
subsequent engagement activities, informing planning decisions, communication protocols, and
risk-management practices. However, expansion beyond these initial groupings remains
necessary to capture the full spectrum of stakeholders and to preserve inclusivity and technical
rigor. Through this approach, transparency and collaboration can be advanced, potential
stakeholder support will be facilitated, and benefits are more effectively
communicated/accessible across the Illinois Basin.

IMPACT OF STAKEHOLDER MAPPING
Key Stakeholder Mapping Components
Stakeholder mapping is a critical step for ensuring the technical viability of CCS projects in the
region, as it enables precise identification, prioritization, and understanding of the interests and
influences of diverse stakeholders. Effective stakeholder mapping directly supports the technical
success of CCS projects by fostering informed decision-making, reducing uncertainties, and
minimizing potential conflicts during project implementation. Accurately mapping stakeholders
can also provide the project team with essential insights into technical and regulatory
requirements specific to the region, ensuring compliance with local, state, and federal
regulations. Engaging stakeholders such as regulatory agencies, environmental groups, industry
associations, and technical experts early in the planning stages facilitates smoother permitting
and approval processes. Stakeholder engagement also ensures alignment with existing
infrastructure capabilities and integration with other regional industrial activities, thus enhancing
operational efficiency and overall project feasibility.
Additionally, stakeholder mapping enables the identification of potential challenges and
technical risks associated with public perception and acceptance. Understanding stakeholder
perspectives helps the project team to anticipate concerns regarding safety, environmental
impacts, and long-term monitoring of CCS activities. By proactively addressing these concerns
through targeted communication and engagement strategies, the team can mitigate opposition
and build trust, thereby reducing delays and facilitating timely project implementation.
Furthermore, stakeholder mapping assists in clearly defining stakeholder roles, expectations, and
responsibilities, ensuring effective communication and collaboration throughout the project
lifecycle. Clear delineation of roles among stakeholders, including technology providers,
operators, government agencies, and local communities, promotes transparency and
accountability, essential elements for successful project management and operation. Robust
stakeholder mapping contributes significantly to the development of comprehensive risk
management and contingency plans. By identifying stakeholders who can significantly impact
project outcomes, the team can more accurately assess and prepare for various scenarios. This
preparation includes establishing communication protocols, aligning stakeholder expectations,
and developing effective response strategies to potential technical and operational disruptions.

Stakeholder Concern Documentation
In addition, a documented stakeholder register, and associated issues log should be created as
controlled, versioned artifacts to provide auditability across the project lifecycle. The register
ought to specify points of contact, statutory authorities, decision rights, preferred communication
channels, and dependencies among agencies, thereby clarifying interfaces for permitting,
monitoring, and emergency coordination. Maintaining this register as a living record supports
continuity through staff turnover and contributes to schedule discipline during critical-path
activities such as site characterization, Class VI applications, pipeline authorizations, and
construction sequencing. To ensure methodological rigor, stakeholder identification should be
complemented by a formal segmentation analysis (e.g., interest–influence matrices and salience
assessments) that links each stakeholder class to concrete benefit and risk pathways. Findings
from this segmentation should inform engagement cadence, document production (fact sheets,
MRV summaries, emergency-response quick cards), and escalation protocols, with explicit

triggers tied to field milestones and regulatory deadlines. Integration of authoritative datasets—
demographic profiles, EJ indicators, land tenure, groundwater protection zones, drainage
districts, emergency-service coverage, and legacy industrial footprints—strengthens the evidence
base and improves the precision of outreach.

Stakeholder Sentiment Data Management
Data governance is central to effective stakeholder mapping. All engagement materials should be
maintained under version control with traceable commitments and a “you said / action taken” log
to enable transparent closeout of issues. Quantitative performance indicators, such as response
times to inquiries, closure rates for commitments, grievance-resolution timelines, attendance
diversity at public sessions, and sentiment trends derived from meeting notes or comment
analysis, provide ongoing feedback loops that can be used to recalibrate engagement tactics and
demonstrate continuous improvement to regulators and local representatives.
Additionally, stakeholder mapping should be explicitly linked to risk management. Identified
stakeholders and their concerns should be cross-referenced within the project risk register, with
pre-agreed communication and coordination steps embedded in contingency plans for technical
anomalies (e.g., drilling variances, transport interruptions) or monitoring excursions. Tabletop
exercises with emergency responders and relevant agencies, conducted at defined intervals, can
validate roles and information flows and thereby enhance readiness. When executed to this
standard, stakeholder mapping functions as an operational control that reduces uncertainty,
aligns technical design with regulatory and stakeholder expectations, and improves the
probability of timely, compliant, and accepted CCS deployment in the Illinois Basin.

EXISTING HEALTH IMPACTS AND DISPARITIES
Analyzing existing health burdens through a stakeholder impact lens is critical for understanding
and addressing potential opposition to project implementation, particularly concerns rooted in
local stakeholder perceptions such as "Not In My Backyard" (NIMBY) reactions. Residents
historically exposed to environmental hazards, including industrial emissions, waste disposal
sites, and transportation-related pollutants, frequently experience compounded and cumulative
health impacts, which can result in heightened local sensitivity and caution regarding new
developments, including CCS projects.1 Chronic conditions commonly observed within these
communities, such as asthma, cardiovascular diseases, respiratory illnesses, and certain types of
cancers, may be aggravated by multiple local factors, including deteriorating housing conditions,
increased exposure to airborne contaminants (e.g., particulate matter, nitrogen oxides, and
volatile organic compounds), soil and groundwater contamination, limited healthcare access, and
inadequate nutrition.2 These combined environmental and socioeconomic health stressors result
in an increased overall health burden, which may amplify stakeholder concerns or opposition if
not proactively anticipated and addressed through thorough project planning and stakeholder

Functional Limitations and Accessibility Considerations
Stakeholder analyses should explicitly consider and integrate the specific vulnerabilities and
perspectives of individuals with disabilities, who often face distinct health-related challenges and
unique barriers to stakeholder participation and healthcare access. 6 These barriers can include
mobility constraints, sensory sensitivities to environmental changes, transportation limitations,
and accessibility challenges in local resources and healthcare facilities. 7 Considering these
distinct needs in the stakeholder impact analysis helps ensure a comprehensive assessment of
potential project effects and promotes project outcomes that support local communities. 8
As illustrated in Figure 1, populations with disabilities are widespread throughout Illinois, with
higher concentrations observed in certain regions, notably in southern Illinois. Understanding
these geographic patterns and specific local concerns can help mitigate potential opposition by
ensuring the project's planning and implementation processes meaningfully address local needs
and sensitivities. By identifying and proactively engaging stakeholders, project developers can
effectively address potential objections, build trust within communities, and enhance overall
project acceptance and technical viability.9
To address concerns and barriers experienced by disabled residents, project planning and
execution could incorporate accessibility commitments that go beyond minimum code
requirements. Public-facing materials and notices can be provided in plain language and multiple
accessible formats (large-print, screen-reader–compatible PDFs, WCAG-compliant webpages,
audio summaries, and ASL/CART services for meetings). Engagement activities can be
scheduled at accessible venues along transit routes, with reserved seating, assistive listening
devices, accessible restrooms, and remote participation options. Comment intake can include
phone hotlines, text/SMS, and mailed forms in addition to online portals, with accommodation
requests accepted in advance and acknowledged within defined service levels. Construction and
field operations can be organized to limit mobility and sensory disruptions. Traffic detours,
sidewalk closures, and work zones can be designed with accessible routes, temporary curb cuts,
tactile warnings, and clear wayfinding; advance notice can be provided through multiple

Malignancy Risk Landscape
Local industrial communities are often disproportionately exposed to carcinogenic substances
due to their closer proximity to landfills, industrial facilities, refineries, and transportation
corridors that emit harmful pollutants. These environmental stressors—coupled with limited
access to quality healthcare, restricted economic opportunities, and historical underinvestment in
infrastructure and public health interventions—contribute to a cumulative increase in cancer
risks within local populations. These risks may influence how CCS projects are perceived by
local communities and stakeholders, highlighting the need to consider and mitigate these impacts
during project development.
When examining the Illinois Basin, it is critical to consider cancer prevalence rates as these
metrics can serve as indicators of underlying, spatially distributed health disparities. As shown in
Figure 5, throughout most regions of the Illinois Basin, the incidence of non-skin cancer meets or
exceeds 8.51%, suggesting that significant proportions of the population may be experiencing
heightened exposure to environmental risk factors. The spatial distribution of these higher
prevalence rates is not uniform; they are most heavily concentrated in the northeast, west, and
southwest portions of the state. These patterns may reflect historical industrialization, legacy
pollutants, and ongoing emissions that have not been adequately remediated. By integrating
cancer prevalence data with other environmental and socioeconomic indicators, it is possible to
better understand how existing burdens intersect with local vulnerabilities. Such analyses support
the identification of priority areas where targeted interventions may be needed to reduce
disproportionate cancer risks and promote more equitable health outcomes during project
development, fostering trust with stakeholders that may perceive CCS as a carcinogenic risk.

ENVIRONMENTAL EXPOSURE CHALLENGES
Wastewater Discharge Risk Indicators
Communities are often disproportionately burdened by exposure to pollutants and other
environmental stressors, which may influence public perception of carbon management
initiatives and highlight areas where strategic stakeholder engagement planning could mitigate
adverse effects. Exposure to pollutants stemming from wastewater discharge is one such
concern. Defined as the release of untreated or insufficiently treated effluent from industrial
operations, sewage treatment facilities, and storm water runoff channels into natural water
systems, wastewater discharge introduces various contaminants (e.g., pathogens, metals, and
organic chemicals) into the environment. These contaminants can adversely affect aquatic
ecosystems, degrade drinking water sources, and contribute to heightened health risks in
downstream communities.
In Illinois, wastewater discharge is not uniformly distributed, as shown in Figure 6. The heaviest
concentrations occur in the northern regions of the state, aligning with urbanized and
industrialized corridors associated with higher industrial throughput and larger population
centers. Additionally, moderate levels of wastewater discharge can be observed in southeastern
Illinois, suggesting spatial variability in environmental burdens. This disproportionate burden by
exposure to pollutants may influence public perception of carbon management initiatives,
highlighting a key area where strategic stakeholder engagement planning could mitigate adverse
effects and reduce stakeholder opposition.

Air Quality Risk-Toxic Constituents
Toxic releases to the air disproportionately affect communities situated near regions
characterized by intensive industrial operations and major transportation corridors, resulting in
increased exposure to airborne contaminants. Continuous exposure to these pollutants in
emission-intensive areas often experience chronic and acute impacts, characterized by increased
incidence and severity of respiratory symptoms (such as asthma attacks and chronic bronchitis),
cardiovascular conditions (including hypertension, arrhythmias, and heart attacks), and long-term
risks of developing cancers associated with sustained inhalation of carcinogenic substances. 29,30
Persistent exposure also increases local susceptibility to infectious diseases and can negatively
impact cognitive function and developmental health, particularly among vulnerable populations
such as children and the elderly.31 As such, industrial operations associated with CCS
infrastructure development may raise concern amongst stakeholders.
As illustrated in Figure 7, within the Illinois Basin, the highest concentrations of toxic air
releases predominantly occur in northern Illinois, particularly within densely populated urban
and suburban areas such as the greater Chicagoland region and adjacent industrial corridors. 32

trust with local stakeholders concerning the health impacts of CCS infrastructure. These efforts
may include deploying real-time air quality monitoring networks, adopting cleaner industrial
processes, implementing stricter emission controls on vehicles, upgrading infrastructure to
reduce fugitive emissions, and fostering interagency collaboration for coordinated pollution
management.35

Drinking Water Non-Compliance Patterns
In the context of new industrial or infrastructure activity, including carbon-management projects,
attention to drinking water compliance is consequential for local risk perception and for
cumulative-impact assessments. Drinking water non-compliance arises when local water systems
fail to adhere to standards established under the Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA), which
governs allowable levels of microbial, chemical, and radiological contaminants. Non-compliance
may involve exceedances of maximum contaminant levels, failures to meet required treatment
techniques, or lapses in monitoring and reporting. Each pathway carries distinct implications for
public health protection and for system operations. For example, exceedances related to acute
microbial contamination trigger rapid public notification and corrective actions, whereas
chemical exceedances associated with chronic exposure necessitate longer-term infrastructure
and process upgrades. Monitoring and reporting violations can mask underlying water-quality
issues and erode public confidence even when acute health risks are not immediately present,
underscoring the importance of data integrity and timely communication. In older systems,
legacy lead service lines and premise plumbing materials complicate compliance management,
as water chemistry changes intended to address one contaminant may inadvertently affect
corrosion control and mobilize metals.
Violations commonly involve contaminants such as lead, nitrates, and E. coli, as well as a range
of disinfection byproducts, industrial chemicals, and naturally occurring elements. Populations
dependent on non-compliant water sources, including children, the elderly, and individuals with
compromised immune systems, face increased risk of health complications, ranging from
gastrointestinal illnesses to neurological disorders and, in some cases, chronic diseases

 17

associated with prolonged exposure. Operational and financial capacity strongly influence
compliance performance. Small and rural systems frequently face staffing constraints, limited
access to certified operators, and capital shortfalls that delay replacement of deteriorated mains,
storage tanks, and treatment equipment. Seasonal demand swings and source-water variability,
particularly in systems reliant on shallow groundwater or surface water influenced by
agricultural runoff, introduce additional complexity in maintaining disinfectant residuals,
controlling disinfection byproducts, and managing turbidity. Addressing these challenges often
requires coordinated planning across multiple entities, including local governments, utilities,
public-health agencies, and technical assistance providers, to align asset-management priorities,
pursue funding, and stage construction to limit service disruptions.
As illustrated in Figure 8, within Illinois, moderate levels of drinking water non-compliance are
geographically dispersed. This distribution reflects a variety of underlying factors, such as
outdated water distribution systems, varying enforcement rigor, and differences in local resource
availability for implementing advanced treatment technologies. Baseline characterization of
source-water quality, distribution-system integrity, and historical violation patterns can inform
siting, construction sequencing, and contingency planning that assuages stakeholder concerns.
Where feasible, project-related investments, such as improvements to monitoring networks,
backup power for treatment facilities, or replacement of high-risk service lines in construction
corridors, can be coordinated with ongoing utility plans to yield durable public-health benefits.
Transparent public communication, clear points of contact, and consistent reporting on both
compliance status and remedial progress further support trust and facilitate efficient resolution of
issues that may arise during project development and operation.

Educational Attainment Regional Profile
Communities with elevated proportions of residents who have not completed high school often
experience amplified economic vulnerability, as lower educational attainment can limit
employment opportunities, reduce earning potential, and restrict access to resources. Historically,
the needs and priorities of these groups may have received less attention in policy and planning
discussions, potentially resulting in inadequate social services, diminished political influence,
and persistent structural inequities. Given disparities in the region, considering local education
level distribution while developing project communication and stakeholder participation
strategies is critical for reducing project opposition.
In Illinois, clusters of areas with a high percentage of individuals lacking a high school diploma
are notable within northern and central regions, particularly in the Chicagoland area (Figure 9).
Additionally, moderate levels of educational attainment challenges are evident in certain parts of
southern Illinois. These spatial patterns reflect a range of underlying factors, including evolving
local industries, historical disinvestment, and varying availability of adult education or job-
training programs.
Lower educational attainment can also interact with other barriers—limited broadband access,
transportation constraints, childcare responsibilities, and health challenges—to dampen
participation in public processes and reduce uptake of training or employment opportunities.

CONCLUSION
Overall, the data and analyses compiled for the Exploration of CCS Potential of the Illinois Basin
project underscore a complex interplay between environmental burdens, demographic
characteristics, health disparities, and socioeconomic conditions. Local stakeholders frequently
experience heightened exposure to pollutants due to proximity to industrial facilities,
transportation networks, and legacy contamination sites, resulting in adverse health outcomes
such as respiratory illnesses, cardiovascular diseases, increased cancer risks, and overall life
expectancy. These environmental impacts are often intensified by infrastructure challenges,
including aging water systems, deteriorating housing stock, and limited transportation options,
which collectively affect public health. In many jurisdictions, additional stressors, such as
limited availability of primary care providers, uneven access to specialty services, and
insufficient preventive screening, exacerbate the severity and duration of environmentally
mediated illnesses. Taken together, these conditions create feedback loops in which
environmental exposure, constrained access to remediation or care, and deteriorating housing
quality reinforce one another over time.
In addition to environmental factors, demographic and socioeconomic variables play critical
roles in shaping local resilience. Conditions such as lower educational attainment, reduced
employment opportunities, economic instability, and constrained access to healthcare resources
amplify local health vulnerabilities and limit the capacity to effectively respond to environmental
stressors and industrial developments. Economic indicators such as unemployment rates, cost-
burdened households, and household energy burdens further illustrate the broader socioeconomic
pressures communities face, affecting disposable income, housing stability, and the ability to
invest in risk-reducing improvements. Social infrastructure, public transportation reliability,
broadband access, community-based organizations, and local public-health capacity, also
functions as a determinant of resilience by influencing information flow, emergency
preparedness, and participation in planning processes. Where these systems are underdeveloped,
particular regions encounter higher barriers to engaging with complex technical initiatives and to
realizing potential benefits from new investment.
Recognizing these detailed patterns through comprehensive analysis is vital for informing
effective carbon management strategies and infrastructure development throughout the Illinois
Basin, ensuring project viability and positive public sentiment from local stakeholders. Insights
from this assessment should guide targeted environmental monitoring, regulatory compliance,
and infrastructure planning, ensuring that future CCS projects do not inadvertently intensify
existing environmental and public-health issues. Priority actions include establishing baseline
measurements prior to field activities, integrating health-protective design criteria into siting and
construction plans, coordinating with utilities and local agencies on water and housing upgrades

 25

where relevant, and publishing accessible metrics that document progress and enable course
correction. By systematically integrating environmental, health, and socioeconomic
considerations into CCS project planning and decision-making processes, stakeholders can
proactively manage risks, prevent the worsening of current stakeholder concerns, and ensure
project outcomes align with the local context and impacted region conditions.
As a practical next step, the findings summarized here could be translated into a structured
implementation roadmap that links data-driven risk indicators to specific design controls,
engagement commitments, and monitoring, reporting, and verification requirements. It could
also establish phasing and decision gates, integrate a permitting crosswalk with statutory
timelines, and map dependencies among engineering, field activities, and stakeholder
engagement. To maintain transparency, the roadmap could set a cadence for public reporting and
dashboards, describe plain-language communication artifacts, and outline verification steps with
third-party partners. Taken together, these findings translate analysis into accountable execution,
advancing CCS development that is technically sound, publicly transparent, and measurably
beneficial across the Illinois Basin.
