Distinctive T Cell Patterns Defining Lymphocytic Alopecia and Alternate Autoimmune and Stem Cell Pathways, Linked to Pleiotropic Functions of Regulatory T Cells Within iSALT
Authors: Steven Kossard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract:The spectrum defining T-cell-mediated alopecias is an expression of autoimmune, innate, regenerative, and effete pathophysiological pathways. Four main folliculotropic T-cell patterns can be integrated into clinicopathologic aspects and define the majority of these alopecias. Lymphocytic inflammation targeting bulbs defines alopecia areata, and pan follicular lymphocytes sparing the hair bulbs and perifollicular concentric fibrosis define lichen planopilaris. Non-destructive lymphocytic folliculitis localized to the bulge modulating stem cell function is introduced as an unrecognized spectrum without defined alopecia. Pan follicular lymphocytic alopecia targets all levels of the follicle, including the bulb and sebaceous glands, and occurs in lupus erythematosus, follicular mucinosis, folliculotropic T-cell dyscrasia, and lymphoma. These form an evidence-based platform due to their inherent programmed pathways. Major advances have occurred in defining inducible mesenchymal skin associated with lymphoid tissue incorporating the pleiotropic functions of regulatory T cells integrated into the JAK/STAT activating transducer pathway. Beyond immunology, the integrated Tregs have non-immunologic functions that modulate stem cell pathways, regulate follicular cycles and follicular inflammation, and repair. In all T-cell alopecias, the central role of both CD8+ and Treg memory cells is pivotal in creating local, multifocal, and diffuse patterns of hair loss. The relationship between the stem cell niche localized to the bulge and supported by the stem cell niche forming progenitor bulbs that induce rapid transit amplifying basaloid cells and trichogenesis are explored in reference to both physiologic and pathogenic pathways. The expanding evidence-based studies defining the pleiotropic functions of the iSALT incorporated Treg/JAK/STAT pathways have led to the development of a conceptual framework to address T-cell alopecias.

INTRODUCTION

Major advances have occurred in the complex integration of physiological homeostatic processes and pathogenic inflammatory skin diseases.1

The spectrum defining lymphocytic-mediated alopecias are an expression of autoimmune, innate, regenerative, and effete pathophysiological pathways. Four main folliculotropic T-cell patterns can be integrated to the clinical aspects and define most lymphocytic alopecias. Lymphocytic inflammation targeting bulbs defines alopecia areata, and pan follicular lymphocytes sparing the hair bulbs and perifollicular concentric fibrosis define lichen planopilaris. Non-destructive lymphocytic folliculitis localized to the bulge modulating stem cell function is introduced as an unrecognized spectrum without defined alopecia. Pan follicular lymphocytic alopecia targets all levels of the follicle, including the bulb and sebaceous glands, and occurs in lupus erythematosus, follicular mucinosis, folliculotropic T-cell dyscrasia, and lymphoma.

Lymphocytic alopecias are viewed as T-cell-mediated autoimmune diseases linked to a breakdown of immune privilege. Inducible skin-associated lymphoid tissue (iSALT) complexes were defined in 1983 by Streilin2 in reference to contact hypersensitivity. The incorporation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) with their pleiotropic functions3,4 within these hubs have resulted in a paradigm shift when exploring both tissue and immunologic homeostatic and pathogenic pathways. iSALT5,6 mesenchymal tissue is an integral component of the pilosebaceous complex. In this context, Tregs have been shown to have non-immunologic functions that modulate stem cell pathways, regulate follicular cycles and follicular inflammation, and repair.7 A central aspect in all T-cell-defined alopecias is the role of both CD8+ and Treg memory cells that transcribe the spectrum that is clinically expressed as local, multifocal, and diffuse hair loss.8,9 Recently, the immunologic and non-immunologic Treg functions have been shown to be integrated and modulated by JAK (Janus Kinase)/STAT (signal transducer and activator of transcription) pathways.10

In contrast to mucosal-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) that is defined by the presence of lymphoid follicles, SALT is not readily apparent on routine histopathologic sections as resident T cells are sparse and the massive numbers of rapidly transiting skin homing T cells linked to immunosurveillance are not apparent. Tregs with their pleotropic functions are key members of iSALT with scattered dendritic cells, including antigen processing histiocytes complementing intra-epidermal Langerhans cells, mast cells, and telocytes linked to tissue regeneration.11

The integrated Treg and JAK/STAT cytokine pathways are central to tissue and immunological homeostasis and modulate autoimmunity. Major advances have occurred in exploring the molecular mechanisms of stem cell dynamics in hair follicle regeneration.12

This offers an opportunity to explore alternate pathogenic pathways anchored to the 4 main T-cell folliculotropic patterns.

DEFINING FOUR MAIN FOLLICULOTROPIC T-CELL PATTERNS

A: Lymphocytic inflammation targeting bulbs in AA (Fig. 1A)

Four patterns of T-cell lymphocytic folliculitis: (A) Peribulbar/bulbar (AA); (B) pan follicular, sparing bulb (LPP); (C) emerging bulb, sebaceous gland at bulge/isthmus level (stem cell folliculitis); and (D) pan follicular, including bulb.

The clinical features and evolution of AA are distinctive, and scalp biopsies are not routinely required for diagnosis. Often, there are episodic solitary or multifocal patches of alopecia that are non-scarring, which often resolve but may progress to persistent patches and total hair loss. Distinctive hair loss targeting marginal scalp loss produces non-scarred pale skin with retained follicular orifices (Fig. 2A). Active AA patches may display distinctive tapering exclamation-mark-like hairs because of acute lymphocyte mediated interruption of anagen growth with narrowed hair shafts at the scalp surface (Fig. 2B).

(B) Photograph showing tapering exclamation mark hairs as an expression of dystrophic hair production. (C) Image showing synchronous diffuse peribulbar and bulbar lymphocytes in deep dermis/subcutis (H&E, ×50). (D) Image showing perifollicular hyaline sheaths defining catagen separating lymphocytes from involuting follicular bulbs in AA (H&E, ×50).

The principal dermatopathological feature defining AA13–15 is synchronized lymphocytic inflammation targeting follicular bulbs and the immediate adjacent proximal nucleated trichogenic segment (Fig. 2C). Despite alopecia patches emerging because of synchronous cytotoxic lymphocytic disruption of anagen, the follicles still progress through a catagen phase as a short transitory state and enter a complex AA-related telogen phase incorporating a distinctive subset of dystrophic follicles. Within catagen, AA can still be diagnosed by the presence of lymphocytes outlining thick perifollicular hyalin sheaths that define catagen (Fig. 2D). These hyaline sheaths may serve as a physical block for mesenchymal signals driving anagen and may limit the autoimmune impact of cytotoxic lymphocytes in AA, arresting anagen and ultimately preserving the follicular cycle with entry into telogen.

Telogen is defined as a resting phase and characterized by retention of hair shafts that are histologically anchored by distinctive spiked trichilemmal hyaline keratin to the external root sheaths. The hair shafts are shed after the induction of hair bulbs after a progenitor phase in telogen. Distinctive dystrophic telogen phase incorporating a distinctive subset of dystrophic follicles in AA appear as dendritic and crenated epithelial sheaths lacking retained hair shafts. The absence of hair shafts in these dystrophic follicles is a direct consequence of the sudden lymphocyte-mediated termination of anagen and expulsion of hair shafts. This is manifested clinically as alopecia compounded by the loss of fragile exclamation mark hairs. Both trichomalacia and pigment hair casts are histopathological signs of sudden dystrophic trichogenesis in anagen follicles.

The most challenging aspect in exploring the pathogenesis of AA is the subsequent delayed transition from arrested telogen to extended anagen cycles marking recovery. For this reason, AA is currently viewed as an autoimmune-mediated disorder of the follicular cycle targeting rapidly replicating cells produced by secondary germinative bulb stem cells derived from the bulge.16,17

Telogen Arrest Incorporating Dystrophic Follicles and Recovery of Hair Growth with Extended Anagen

The telogen phase linked to innate follicular cycles lasts up to 3 months. Rapid spontaneous recovery in AA within this time frame often represents normal synchronized telogen to anagen switch and may not display numerous dystrophic telogen follicles.

The arrested telogen phase with dystrophic telogen follicles is distinctive in AA and is expressed as multiple dendritic and crenated dystrophic telogen follicles lacking retained hair shafts (Figs. 3A–C). Telogen arrest phase with prominent dystrophic telogen follicles can define AA, even in the absence of bulbar lymphocytic inflammation, but is not entirely specific as it is a marker of sudden abnormal interruption of trichogenesis. Telogen and dystrophic telogen follicles have the capacity to transform into dystrophic miniaturized anagen follicles that fail to enter a prolonged anagen cycle. The distinctive dystrophic telogen phase was initially observed by David Whiting18 in his seminal study of biopsies from a large clinically defined cohort of patients with AA in all its phases.

A–C) AA demonstrating synchronous dominant transition to arrested dystrophic telogen follicles with distinctive and complex dendritic outlines, minimal lymphocytic inflammation, and lack of retained hair shafts.

The Hair Cycle is Driven by an Integrated Dual System Incorporating Bulge Stem Cells and Bulb Rapid Transit Amplifying Cells to Sustain Anagen

Ultimately, AA is an enigmatic hair cycle disorder that represents a synchronous focal or extensive lymphocyte-mediated shift of anagen follicles to telogen follicles that persist for a variable period. An integrated relationship exists between the low proliferative labelling stem cells in the bulge with the high proliferative stem cells in the hair bubs (secondary germ), particularly in the switch of telogen to sustained anagen. The bulge acts as a pan follicular immune protected stem cell hub not only for cyclical trichogenesis but also for the permanent infundibular, isthmus, and sebaceous elements of the follicle.

Although telogen is defined as a resting phase, it incorporates an active progenitor phase that is not defined by standard histology but is mesenchyme-driven. This resting period is required for the complex process for progenitor cells that are transformed into programmed and integrated basaloid cells that form follicular bulbs. Bulbar basaloid cells are prototypic examples of transit amplifying cells19 and play a central role in tissue regeneration including the multifaceted integrated complex forming the bulbs linked to trichogenesis and enabling prolonged anagen.19 In AA, telogen incorporates an active progenitor phase that may be disrupted within dystrophic telogen follicles. In longstanding arrested telogen and dystrophic telogen complexes, follicles may enter a prolonged dystrophic anagen phase where miniaturized follicles fail to recover their capacity to re-enter into prolonged anagen and recovery. This is clinically expressed as short fine evanescent hairs that are shed.

The outer root sheaths surrounded by the adjacent supporting iSALT mesenchyme are directly linked to the bulge and the stem cells forming and regenerating this sheath. iSALT mesenchyme also plays a central role in inducing both the telogen progenitor phase and the induction of a rapidly proliferating secondary stem cell niche, forming bulbs that can sustain prolonged anagen growth and recovery in AA.

B: Pan follicular lymphocytes sparing the hair bulbs and perifollicular concentric fibrosis (Fig. 1B)

Pan follicular lymphocytes sparing the hair bulbs with perifollicular concentric fibrosis define the T-cell pattern for LPP. Classic LPP represents a prototypic autoimmune lymphocytic-mediated alopecia with the loss of self-tolerance and targeted destruction of follicles.

Despite the prominent lymphocytic inflammation, the concentric cicatricial elimination of follicles in all presentations of LPP minimizes gross scarring and leads to fine clinically imperceptible vertically orientated sclerotic fibrous tracts and permanent hair loss. LPP in glabrous skin often clinically appears as non-scarring resembling the hair loss in AA.

Progressive LPP with the elimination of multiple contiguous follicles producing irregular pale patches of alopecia with only isolated sentinel terminal hairs (Fig. 4A). The loss of follicular orifices produces a featureless skin surface with variable perifollicular scale and erythema around the sentinel hairs (Fig. 4B). Biopsies of LPP show a histopathology combining concentric perifollicular fibrosis outlined by eccentric clusters of lymphocytes and lichenoid lymphocytic inflammation at the interface of the external root sheaths3 (Fig. 4C) without peribulbar lymphocytic inflammation. The concentric cicatricial elimination of individual follicles minimizes gross scarring as the interfollicular dermis is spared. Both the eccentric lymphocytes and concentric fibrosis in LPP (Fig. 4D) can be observed in subcutis (Fig. 4E) extending beyond the currently recognized permanent follicle infundibular and isthmus regions incorporating the bulge as the main target in LPP. This indicates that the regenerative follicle and specifically the external root sheath, but not the bulb, are also targeted in LPP. The loss of the external root sheaths with their integrated inductive vascular mesenchyme for folliculogenesis and its replacement by sclerotic fibrous tracts contributes to the permanent follicular loss in LPP.

Progenitors for the External Root Sheaths are Linked Directly to the Bulge and Not the Bulb

The sparing of the hair bulbs in LPP is linked to the distinct stem cell line for the external root sheaths. Studies have shown that the slowly replicating stem cell compartment within the bulge generates the outer root sheath stem cells and that the external root sheaths remain directly integrated with the bulge. The bulge is also the central element for the induction of follicular bulbs,12 with highly replicating stems cells in the bulbs producing rapid transit amplifying cells that differentiate into trichogenic complexes. The bulbs directly form the inner root sheaths complex, molding and propelling trichogenesis within integrated external root sheaths. This anagen phase often lasts years and is modulated by accelerated cycle times that define pattern alopecia.

The bulge-derived outer root sheaths play an important role as they are simultaneously integrated with the inner root sheaths and are supported by the perifollicular iSALT mesenchymal tissue housing Tregs modulating hair cycles. A major function of the outer root keratin sheath during telogen is to transform the inner root sheath into distinctive spiked hyaline keratin anchoring hair shafts within follicles during telogen in non-dystrophic telogen follicles.

Complex Relationship of LPP and Pattern Alopecia

The complex spectrum of LPP developing in the setting of pattern alopecia is reviewed by Olsen.20 Frontal fibrosing alopecia (FFA) has become the major enigmatic clinical variant of LPP.21–23 This develops particularly in post-menopausal women with a progressive patterned hair loss driven by LPP. The process is dominated by recession of the frontotemporal hairline that can be marked (Fig. 5A). The frontal hairline loss is significant as female pattern alopecia usually has a preserved frontal hairline even in progressive stages (Fig. 5B).

(A) Photograph showing an elderly woman with FFA and marked recession of the frontotemporal hairline, leaving pale skin with loss of follicular orifices and eyebrows. (B) Illustration of the stages of female pattern hair loss with preservation of frontal hairline compared with (C) the common marked frontotemporal recession defining male pattern alopecia that shares clinical features with FFA in women but may rarely occur as an identical and independent LPP-driven phenomenon in men.

The clinical presentation of FFA shares features with relatively common forms of male pattern hair loss with marked recession of the frontotemporal hairline20 (Fig. 5C). Both male and female pattern alopecia are dominated by progressive miniaturization of follicles and shortening of the hair cycles linked to polygenetic traits and hormones but are not driven by follicular lymphocytic autoimmunity that defines LPP.

One possibility is that FFA occurs predominantly in postmenopausal women with this male genetic trait resulting in patterned frontal recession. This trait is inhibited in women by the premenopausal female hormone milieu, resulting in preservation of the frontal hairline. Conceptually, the alteration of the surface conformation of follicular receptors due to changes in post-menopausal hormone binding may set the stage for FFA as an autoimmune response. This may be triggered by a variety of catalysts, including topical agents such as sunscreens and other agents. The boundaries of FFA have been extended with its onset in premenopausal women, occurrence in men,24 and with the emergence of familial cases. FFA is a clinically distinct variant of LPP but can be expressed as a hybrid form together with multifocal hair loss, more extensive marginal occipital loss, cutaneous LPP in glabrous skin, and oral LP.

The relationship of LPP with androgenic alopecia is more complex because LPP can develop in areas of androgenetic alopecia as discrete foci or hair loss25,26 or combined with more extensive LPP. In these presentations, LPP represents a secondary phenomenon and is not the primary driving force producing pattern alopecia as in FFA.

Loss of eyebrows is frequently present in FFA and is an indication that the process is complex and not purely LPP, inducing a late onset patterned distribution and can in addition be combined with the wider spectrum of LPP and LP. Ultimately, this is an expression of the expansion of programmed circulating and resident memory T cells that are initiated in the setting of FFA as a distinct subset of LPP but can be linked to broader manifestation of LPP and LP.27,28

LPP as an Expression of Autoimmunity with Links to Innate Pathways Governing Tissue Repair

Although LPP is a distinctive autoimmune process and reflects a breakdown of immune tolerance,29 LPP may also represent a homeostatic pathway that is innate and responsible for eliminating effete or compromised follicles. The latter is best illustrated by the LPP-like histopathology observed in the setting of destructive inflammatory folliculitis.

Folliculitis decalvans is a scarring alopecia that is pustular and localized to the permanent infundibular/isthmus complex incorporating the bulge stem cell niche. Compound follicles and tufted hairs are produced and define the healing and regenerative phase of follicular disruption with fusion of the grouped follicular unit infundibular canals.3,30 The secondary LPP-like histopathology is also integral to the pathogenic pathway in folliculitis decalvans and conceptually may be an expression of innate autoimmune–lymphocytic-mediated permanent removal of compromised and effete follicles.

Centrifugal cicatricial alopecia (CCCA)31–33 is distinct from folliculitis decalvans and is also linked to secondary LPP-like histopathology in its repair phase. CCCA is seen particularly in black women and shares clinical features with FPHL. In contrast to staphylococcal folliculitis that drives folliculitis decalvans, hot combs, pomades, and hair styling triggers compromised and effete follicles with progressive permanent follicular elimination that may represent an innate LPP-like pathway linked to repair and establishing tissue homeostasis.

C: Stem cell folliculitis: Non-destructive lymphocytic folliculitis localized to the bulge region modulating stem cell function (Fig. 1C)

Stem cell folliculitis (SCF) was originally introduced as a term to define a distinctive histopathology, with eccentric lymphocytic inflammation at the follicular isthmus incorporating bulge related stem cells responsible for trichogenesis and sebaceous gland formation. This lymphocytic inflammation resulted in sebaceous gland involution in multiple follicles (Fig. 6A). A key feature defining SCF was lymphocytes that targeted emerging bulbs at the isthmus sebaceous duct level, inhibiting trichogenesis and resulting in arrested nascent bulbs (Fig. 6B). These histopathology figures were included in the index publication defining SCF.34 The non-scarring alopecia had developed in a 34-year-old woman over 8 years in the wake of a postpartum telogen effluvium. The diffuse alopecia slowly evolved and shared features with FPHL (Figs. 6C, D).

Subsequently, these distinctive histopathological features defining SCF (Figs. 7A–D) were found in the clinical settings of androgenic alopecia and alopecia areata. SCF may occur without clinically distinctive features as a diffuse hair loss. SCF may also account for the sebaceous gland involution that has been reported in scalp biopsies from patients with psoriasis,35 with or without alopecia or seborrheic dermatitis.36Alopecia is often non-distinctive but can share features with androgenetic alopecia. The histopathologic combination of sebaceous gland involution with arrested trichogenesis in these settings fulfills the definition of SCF. More recently, this phenomenon has been observed in a patient with contact dermatitis of the scalp37 and observed in patients receiving TNF inhibitors even without the presence of psoriasis.38

This form of SCF represents a non-destructive T-cell-mediated inhibitory process modulating the bulge-related stem cells in contrast to the destructive nature of LPP. In contrast to LPP, in SCF, infundibular differentiation remains intact and SCF is a reversible non-scarring process similar to AA and may resolve spontaneously or with treatment.

Lymphocytic Stem Cell Folliculitis Expressed as a Dystrophic Basaloid Folliculogenesis with a Distinctive Aberrant Histopathology

Lymphocytic stem cell folliculitis is not restricted to a stem cell inhibitory process but can be expressed as a dystrophic stem cell pathway with highly distinctive follicles that have undergone basaloid transformation that resembles basal cell carcinoma. This rare distinctive histopathology was initially defined as basaloid folliculolymphoid hyperplasia. The index case developed in a 70-year-old woman with longstanding mycosis fungoides and progressive alopecia with scaling and erythema39 (Fig. 8 A). Multiple biopsies showed a prominent folliculotropic infiltrate of lymphocytes and transformation of numerous hair follicles into pan follicular dystrophic dedifferentiated basaloid structures that extended into the subcutis and lacked follicular and sebaceous differentiation (Figs. 8B, C). The lymphocytes were cerebriform on ultrastructure.

A subset of follicular mucinosis also represents dystrophic stem cell folliculitis with basaloid differentiation, distinguished by intrafollicular mucin and prominent dilated infundibulocystic cavities (Fig. 9B). More frequently, the lymphocytes in follicular mucinosis are not targeting the bulge region and are pan follicular, with intrafollicular spongiosis forming mucinous pools (Fig. 9) and basaloid follicular dystrophy being absent. Basaloid folliculolymphoid hyperplasia is currently recognized as a rare manifestation of follicular mucinosis and folliculotropic mycosis fungoides.40

This spectrum of pan follicular and basaloid dystrophic follicular mucinosis was originally defined by Pinkus.41

D: Pan follicular lymphocytic alopecia (Fig. 1D)

In pan follicular lymphocytic alopecia, T-cells target all levels of the follicle, including the bulb and sebaceous glands. This occurs in both follicular mucinosis and folliculotropic MF without evidence of lymphocyte-induced basaloid follicular dystrophy (Fig. 9C). A closely related pan follicular lymphocytosis lacking prominent mucinosis is folliculotropic T-cell lymphocytosis42 that is currently recognized as a distinct form of pilotropic T-cell dyscrasia.43

The lichenoid lymphocytic folliculitis in lupus erythematosus is often pan follicular and targets hair bulbs in contrast to LPP. In the setting of lupus panniculitis, inflammation can be restricted to the hair bulbs without a pan follicular inflammation. The histopathology on horizontal scalp sections in this setting shares features with AA,44 but the lymphocytic infiltrate is denser and not restricted to the bulbs and induce lupus panniculitis (Fig. 10A). Furthermore, lupus panniculitis localized to the scalp may clinically share features with AA. However, in contrast to AA, the patches of hair loss in lupus are often tender with variable erythema and often resolve with lipoatrophy.

(B) Illustration showing a vertical section with pan follicular lichenoid lymphoid hyperplasia without concentric perifollicular fibroplasia and extensive interfollicular dermal inflammation (H&E, ×50) in contrast to LPP. (C) Photograph showing discoid lupus with irregular scarred patch of alopecia that is eroded with erythema and dyspigmentation in contrast to LPP.

The lymphocytic inflammation in LE is dense and concentric, obscuring the boundary of the external root sheaths (Fig. 10B) without the intervening concentric fibrosis and focal eccentric lymphocytes defining LPP. The interfollicular epidermis is usually involved in LE in contrast to scalp LPP and this is clinically expressed as erosive crusts and erythema in the areas of alopecia (Fig. 10C) in contrast to the pale featureless skin in LPP.

A purely folliculotropic clinicopathologic variant of LE has not been defined in contrast to AA, LPP, follicular mucinosis, folliculotropic lymphocytosis, folliculotropic MF, and the spectrum of stem cell folliculitis.

Second, LPP-like pathology may occur in LE alopecia as a potential reflection of innate autoimmune elimination pathway, removing compromised and effete follicles. Both AA and LPP may occur in patients with LE, but these combinations require additional historical data defining the clinical features and biopsy findings for AA or LPP. The diagnosis should not be based on hybrid patterns with features of AA or LPP that may be observed in LE biopsies and do not necessarily support 2 dermatoses.45

Constructing a Conceptual Tregs Framework for the Role of the Pleiotropic Functions within iSALT in Modulating Folliculotropic T-Cell Alopecias

The integration of Tregs as a family with the diverse JAK/STAT pathways modulating pleiotropic functions inherent in iSALT represents a paradigm shift.10,46–48 This provides an opportunity to construct a conceptual framework to integrate the physiological and pathological elements that modulate folliculotropic T-cell alopecia. This framework encompasses innate homeostatic physiological processes linked to the 2 major follicular stem cell niches governing follicular differentiation and follicular cycles.49 Recently, the involvement of ILC1-like innate immune lymphocytes in human autoimmunity has been linked to AA as a dynamic regulatory pathway that may not be triggered by an autoantigen.50

A key aspect of the integration of JAK/STAT pathways is their role in inducing on naïve T cells, the defining phenotype expression of CD4+CD25+and FoxP3, for Tregs.48 Together with Tregs, the multiple combinations, defining JAK/STAT signaling pathways in iSALT, extend beyond maintaining and modulating immune response and include their central role in restoring immune tolerance or privilege and both tissue and immunological homeostasis.

Recently, low-level expressions under homeostatic conditions of the complexes defined as JAK/STAT1 and JAK/STAT2 have been linked to the activation of stem cell developmental pathways that would include follicular differentiation.2,51,52 These functions have significant epigenetic and transcriptional implications for both innate and pathogenic aspects of folliculotropic T-cell alopecias.

The detailed clinicopathological analysis of the 4 main folliculotropic patterns that define the majority of folliculotropic alopecia, the physiological aspects of follicular cycles, and the 2 follicular stem cell niches formed the basis for constructing an integrative framework incorporating the pleiotropic functions of regulatory T cells within iSALT (Fig. 11).

Integrated framework incorporating the pleiotropic functions of Tregs within iSALT, summarizing major modulated innate homeostatic follicular spheres and modulatory immunological spheres used to analyze the pathogenesis of T-cell alopecia defined by differing folliculotropic T-cell patterns.

The analysis is anchored to this inherent evidence-based platform furnished by the key programmed dermatopathological features linked to follicular stem cell physiological and pathological pathways that are repeated and define prototypic T-cell alopecias. These cannot be integrated and duplicated experimentally, given the complexity of these pathways.

The integrative framework is inherently simplistic, given the reality that pleiotropic functions of Treg are the result of highly complex molecular pathways that have been mapped through evidence-based experimental studies. This complexity is simplified by incorporating spheres and designating each with pleiotropic functions of Tregs linked to iSALT and innate follicular based spheres relevant to the analysis of T-cell alopecias.

In addition, the resident Tregs within the mesenchymal iSALT servicing the follicles are members of a highly complex family of Tregs, including resident memory T cells, and circulating as a reflection of their pleiotropic functions.

A central aspect of iSALT is the dynamic peripheral induction and presence of residential Tregs and resident memory T cells to facilitate and underscore pathogenic pathways,53,54 particularly those linked to autoimmunity and loss of self-tolerance. The innate physiological role of Tregs in maintaining follicular homeostasis and modulating follicular cycles is currently subject to ongoing investigative studies. The scant lymphocytic inflammation observed on scalp biopsies incidentally around the stem cell niches within the follicular bulge and bulb may play a role in this innate physiological sphere.

Both AA and LPP are models for autoimmunity but are distinguished by their relationship to totally distinctive innate follicular paths. Ultimately, LPP may represent a pathogenic expression of prototypic innate autoimmune process that targets and eliminates compromised and effete follicles and a homeostatic process incorporated within the sphere defined as follicular repair. The clinical expression of LPP is broad and not apparent if single follicles are gradually eliminated, such as those linked with follicular senescence.

AA is a more complex process as it ultimately is a disorder of the follicular cycle. AA is a controlled destructive process as the hair cycle is preserved and the main impasse for AA recovery is the failure to exit telogen and enter a sustained anagen phase.

In both AA and LPP, the autoimmune process has been linked to a breach of immune privilege/tolerance in concert with a loss of MHC class II expression on endogenous antigen presenting cells.55,56 Tregs modulate MHC Class by inhibiting both I and II expressions on antigen-processing cells. This has particular significance as within the follicular mesenchyme, Tregs have been shown to be reduced in anagen and increased in telogen,4,6 with a shift in MHC expression and degree of immune tolerance. This modulation of Tregs during telogen may protect the follicle in the critical resting and progenitor phase.

The pharmacological development of oral JAK/STAT inhibitor treatment has provided evidence that destroying the cytotoxic lymphocytes precipitating anagen and stimulating the transition of arrested telogen follicles into prolonged anagen are required for the successful outcome in patients with AA.57 This is a modulatory force as JAK/STAT signaling jump starts the innate hair cycles and activates the dermal papillae. Currently, therapies that modulate Tregs are also emerging.58

Both LPP and stem cell folliculitis are folliculotropic T-cell alopecias that target the bulge related niche in the isthmus bulge region. The inclusion of stem cell folliculitis within the framework is not based on autoimmunity but as a T-cell-modulated inflammatory process inhibiting bulge-related stem cells, inducing arrested anagen progression and inhibiting sebaceous gland differentiation or inducing basaloid dystrophic follicles. This conceptual stem cell pathway was born by analysis of distinctive histopathological events that ultimately could not be linked to a distinct pattern of alopecia.

Constructing integrative frameworks based on the pleomorphic functions of Tregs in iSALT offers an opportunity to provide a simplified analysis of some of the enigmatic and paradoxical aspects of dermatopathology. This model has been previously applied to explore Louis Brocq's definition of parapsoriasis as a possible alternative lymphomatoid inflammatory pathway akin to the recognized relationship of lymphomatoid papulosis with mycosis fungoides.59
