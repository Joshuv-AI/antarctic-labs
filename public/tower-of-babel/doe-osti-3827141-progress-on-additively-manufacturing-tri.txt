 SUMMARY
 This report details the FY 2026 research progress achieved by Oak Ridge National Laboratory
(ORNL), in collaboration with Idaho National Laboratory, under the Advanced Fuels Campaign for the
Intertwined Nuclear Fuel Lattice for Uprated heat eXchange (INFLUX) project. The INFLUX
architecture abandons traditional rod-and-pellet and pebble core geometries in favor of mathematically
derived triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) lattices, wherein fuel, cladding, and coolant occupy
separate, continuously curved, intertwined domains. To overcome the significant processing challenges
associated with co-depositing multimaterial metallic and ceramic elements in a single build, ORNL
adopted a universal “shell-and-fill” manufacturing approach. In this framework, a single-material TPMS
lattice can is additively manufactured, then filled with ceramic fuel kernels or surrogate particles, and
subsequently consolidated into an integrated, hermetic fuel element via a secondary thermal step.
 The FY 2026 experimental scope was executed as a fail-fast screening campaign using non-uranium
surrogate materials to downselect viable processing pathways across light-water reactor (LWR) and high-
temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) product lines. For the metallic LWR pathway, process
optimization for laser powder bed fusion of Zircaloy-4 and surrogate Ti-6Al-4V established a volumetric
energy density processing window to consistently produced cladding walls with >99% relative density
while eliminating lack-of-fusion defect networks. To resolve geometric overmelting and sealing
difficulties associated with complex lattice openings, the cladding was reengineered into a single-port
Stout TPMS design. After the TPMS can was vibropacked with yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) ceramic
spheres, the Stout loading port was hermetically sealed via precision laser seam-welding of a fitted metal
foil cap. Subsequent hot isostatic pressing consolidation demonstrated isotropic clad shrinkage around the
YSZ core without structural breaching or wall buckling, confirming full interior ceramic densification and
sound metallurgical bonding.
 For the carbonaceous HTGR pathway, sacrificial polymer balloon templates were evaluated as
temporary containment structures for overcoated TRISO surrogates before carbonization, but both
candidate routes were formally terminated on demonstrated failure. Fused filament fabrication using
20 vol % carbon fiber reinforced nylon suffered brittle structural collapse during room-temperature cold
isostatic pressing at 15 MPa and severe viscous slumping at 300°C; stereolithography thermoset
photopolymers are still being investigated for proper decomposition temperature. Direct printing of
graphite via powder bed ink jetting (PBiJ) was consequently downselected as the baseline HTGR
pathway. By combining PBiJ-printed Stout graphite cans, rotary spray overcoating of surrogate
tristructural-isotropic particle fuel kernels, and chemical vapor infiltration of pyrolytic carbon, the
gaseous precursor successfully infiltrated the interconnected open pore network of the green body,
locking the kernels, capping powder, and outer lattice into a fully integrated, monolithic carbon/carbon
fuel element.

 1. Introduction
 This report documents FY 2026 fabrication research performed by ORNL under the AFC INFLUX
project. The sections that follow establish, in turn, what the INFLUX fuel design is and why it warrants
investment, why additive manufacturing is the only credible route to producing it, and how the ORNL
work package was structured to determine which specific processes can meet the design's requirements.
Because the fabrication challenge differs fundamentally between reactor classes, the technical work is
organized by product line rather than by processing step.
 1.1 The INFLUX Design
 Nuclear fuel exists to arrange fissile material such that a chain reaction can be sustained, fission
products retained, and heat efficiently removed. These functions compete, and the geometries used to
balance them, such as cylinders, spheres, or plates, have remained essentially unchanged since the first
commercial reactors came online in the 1950s and 1960s—not because they are optimal but because they
are manufacturable. The Intertwined Nuclear Fuel Lattice for Uprated heat eXchange (INFLUX) concept
developed at Idaho National Laboratory (INL) abandons that constraint (Martin et al., 2023; Shah et al.,
2024; Woolstenhulme et al., 2025).
 INFLUX replaces the rod-and-pellet architecture with a triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS)
lattice in which fuel, cladding, and coolant occupy separate, continuously curved, intertwined domains
that repeat on a cubic unit cell. The mathematically derived surfaces that have made TPMS structures the
preferred architecture for compact heat exchangers yield high surface-area-to-volume ratio, enhanced
turbulent mixing, and continuously curved walls with no stress-concentrating corners (Gado et al., 2024;
Li et al., 2025; Prussack et al., 2024) and are applied here to a fuel form. TPMS unit cells are defined
parametrically by unit cell type, cell pitch, and wall thickness (Li et al., 2025); therefore, the fuel-to-
moderator ratio can be tuned independently and graded spatially, providing a single design framework
that adapts across reactor classes rather than a family of unrelated fuel designs.
 The performance case for INFLUX has been established through out-of-pile experiment and
simulation (Ferney & Martin, 2025; Martin et al., 2023; Woolstenhulme et al., 2025). Self-heated thermal
hydraulic experiments and computational fluid dynamics models have demonstrated an approximately
threefold increase in heat transfer coefficient relative to conventional fuel geometries (Prussack et al.,
2024; Shah et al., 2024). Monte Carlo transport calculations show that the intertwined geometry reduces
neutron leakage and mitigates self-shielding relative to pin-cell configurations (Ferney & Martin, 2025;
Martin et al., 2023), and system-level estimates project a 5%–10% improvement in uranium utilization
with roughly 25% additional energy throughput per fuel cycle (Ferney & Martin, 2025; Martin et al.,
2023). Finite element modeling in BISON indicates resilient thermomechanical behavior arising from the
continuously curved geometry (Woolstenhulme et al., 2025). These results place the barrier for INFLUX
not in reactor physics or thermal hydraulics but in making design come to life in manufacturing.
 Overcoming this barrier is the purpose of the herein-reported fabrication scope, which targets the
three Advanced Fuels Campaign (AFC) product lines: UO2 in zirconium-based cladding for light-water
reactors (LWRs), U–Zr alloy in steel-based cladding for sodium fast reactors, and uranium oxycarbide
tristructural-isotropic (TRISO) particle fuel in a graphite matrix for high-temperature gas reactors
(HTGRs). Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) fills a role within INFLUX to help resolve the
manufacturing barrier. Drawing on established capability in metallic and ceramic additive manufacturing
(AM), powder processing, and high-temperature consolidation, ORNL leads fabrication of the TPMS fuel

cans across all three product lines and determines which processing routes can meet the geometric,
hermeticity, and densification requirements the design imposes.
 1.2 Additive Manufacturing as the Enabling Technology
 The INFLUX geometry cannot be produced by conventional fabrication routes. Internal surfaces are
obscured from line of sight throughout the lattice, the intertwined domains cannot be assembled from
separately fabricated parts, and no machining operation can generate the internal channels. Therefore, AM
is not only a preferred route but also the only viable one. Because no commercially mature process can
co-deposit fuel and cladding in a single build, ORNL adopted a shell-and-fill approach across all reactor
classes: a single-material TPMS can is printed first, loaded with kernels or surrogate fill, and consolidated
into an integrated fuel form in a subsequent thermal step. This process decouples AM from fuel chemistry
and allows the printing process to be selected on the requirements of the cladding material alone.
 Powder bed fusion (PBF) offers the geometric resolution and near-full density needed to produce
thin-walled, hermetic lattice structures from metals. Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) was selected over
electron beam powder bed fusion (EBPBF) primarily because EBPBF requires a partially sintered powder
bed for charge conduction, and removing sintered powder from the enclosed internal channels of a TPMS
can is impractical. Additionally, LPBF operates at ambient pressure and achieves finer geometric
resolution.
 Carbonaceous cans for the HTGR design cannot be produced by any melting-based process because
carbon/graphite does not melt at atmospheric pressure and only really sublimates at high temperatures
(>2000°C). Therefore, ceramic materials are often printed from ink-jetting slurries or binders using
computer-controlled nozzles for deposition. This work investigated two routes for achieving a carbon-
based structure Route 1 involves fabricating a sacrificial polymeric balloon that holds the fuel in the
desired geometry throughout the early stages of the thermal cycle and pyrolyzes during high-temperature
treatment, leaving a carbonaceous residue that contributes to the final matrix. Both fused filament
fabrication (FFF) and stereolithography (SLA) were evaluated for this purpose, not only because both are
mature polymer printing processes but also because they represent distinct polymer classes with different
failure modes: FFF deposits thermoplastics that soften and creep under load at elevated temperature,
whereas SLA cures thermosets that decompose rather than flow. Route 2 involves printing graphite
directly and densifying the resulting structure by chemical vapor infiltration (CVI).
 Several processes can produce ceramic components in near-net shape, including vat
photopolymerization and paste extrusion of loaded slurries, but powder bed ink jet (PBiJ) was selected for
the graphite route. PBiJ is a solid-state process: a binding agent is applied selectively to a powder bed at
ambient temperature, mirroring the layer-wise logic of LPBF without a melting source. Two
characteristics make it uniquely suited to this application. First, it uses a minimal binder fraction, so the
calcination step required to remove the binder introduces correspondingly little dimensional change and
residual porosity. Second, and more importantly, the resulting green body retains an interconnected open
pore network throughout the printed structure; this open pore network is the necessary precondition for
pyrolytic carbon CVI to infiltrate and densify the can from within rather than sealing only its outer
surface.
 1.3 ORNL Scope within INFLUX
 ORNL’s role in the INFLUX project is determining a fabrication route that converts the lattice
designs optimized through INL’s modeling and simulation efforts into physical fuel forms. The ORNL
work package is organized into four main tasks: Task 1, optimizing LPBF of Zircaloy-4 (Zry-4) relevant
cladding material for LWRs; Task 2, optimizing graphitic overcoating process for ceramic kernels that
will promote bonding and densification during densification; Task 3, addressing polymer-assisted carbon
structure development, using resin-based AM to produce TPMS architectures that convert to carbon on

 2

heat treatment; Task 4, densifying cladding material and kernels by sintering, spark plasma sintering
(SPS), or hot isostatic pressing (HIP).
 The FY 2026 scope was deliberately structured as a fail-fast screening campaign rather than a single-
route optimization. All work reported here was performed with surrogate materials (i.e., no uranium-
bearing specimens were fabricated). The objective was to establish which of the candidate fabrication
pathways can produce a hermetic, dimensionally stable TPMS can capable of surviving consolidation and
to terminate those that cannot. This report is organized as follows. Section 2 covers metal TPMS
fabrication for the LWR design, including sealing method downselection in Ti-6Al-4V (Ti64), two
completed HIP cycles, and Zry-4 process development. The sodium fast reactor design would likely be
produced similar to that of the LWR route, but with a different metal cladding; therefore, it was chosen to
not be the focus and optimize the LPBF+densification route with Zry-4 and Ti64. Section 3 covers
carbonaceous TPMS fabrication for the HTGR design, spanning kernel overcoating, the polymer-derived
route and its termination, PBiJ of graphite, and pyrolytic carbon (PyC) deposition using CVI to densify
the fuel form.
 2. Metal TPMS Fabrication for the LWR Design
 The LWR product line pairs UO₂ fuel with a zirconium-based cladding, which places the INFLUX
fuel can squarely in the domain of metallic AM. Two questions govern whether this pathway is viable.
First, can a Zr alloy be processed by LPBF to the density and dimensional accuracy that a thin-walled
lattice demands. Second, once such a can is filled with ceramic fuel, can it be hermetically sealed and
consolidated without collapsing, breaching, or losing the lattice geometry that gives INFLUX its
performance advantage? Section 2.1 addresses the first question through parameter development in Zry-4
and TPMS can fabrication in a Ti64 surrogate; Section 2.2 addresses the second through vibropacking,
sealing trials, and HIP consolidation with YSZ as a surrogate fuel.
 2.1 Laser Powder Bed Fusion
 The LPBF work proceeded in two stages. Parameter development was performed first on simple
cubic geometries, where volumetric density and defect populations can be measured directly and related
to processing conditions without the confounding influence of complex geometry. The resulting
processing window was then applied to fabrication of TPMS cans, where wall thickness, overhang angles,
and internal channel access impose constraints that cubes do not reveal.
2.1.1 Zircaloy Process Optimization
 LPBF is a melting-based AM technique that can be used to fabricate complex, near-net-shape metallic
components with high dimensional accuracy. In LPBF, a high-power laser selectively scans and melts
pre-deposited layers of metal powder to additively build a part layer-by-layer, as demonstrated in Figure
1a. Each melted track solidifies rapidly, and then the build platform lowers incrementally to allow a
recoater blade to spread the next layer of powder, iteratively building the part layer by layer. Because
LPBF operates at ambient build-chamber temperatures with localized melt pools, it offers superior
geometric freedom compared to EBPBF, which, due to the natural heating of the electron beam, often
requires partially sintering the powder bed to facilitate electron conduction.

2.1.2 TPMS Can Fabrication
 LPBF processing of Zry-4 has not been previously studied by the authors and required process
optimization before moving to TPMS structure fabrication. In the meantime, Ti64 was chosen as a
suitable surrogate to study. Ti64 has been well studied in the AM literature and is highly processable into
complex structures. Ti64 powders with a given particle size of 15–45 µm were acquired from Renishaw
and were processed with the same Renishaw AM250, following the same conditions mentioned
previously for processing of Zry-4.
 TPMS structures are to be fabricated from Zry-4, and UO2 fuel powder will be loaded into the can
using a vibration table, aka vibropacking. High-temperature sintering would encourage the powders to
bond, but no mechanical force is imposed to push the particles closer together, so the likely result would
be significant internal porosity in the fuel matrix. Advanced processes such as SPS and HIP induce
mechanical pressure under temperature; however, densification of the UO2 fuel loaded inside a Zry-4
shell would most likely cause deformation of the shell itself using these processes. Three different
miniature rectangular TPMS cans were fabricated from Ti64, as shown in Figure 2, using parameters to
achieve a dense structure, a high VED for creating spherical porosity, and a low VED to partially melt
powders together. Thus, varying levels of volumetric density in the walls of the can could be
experimentally explored during SPS or HIP.

 2.2 Ceramic Compaction
 Yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) beads (30 µm in diameter) were chosen as the surrogate fuel for
experimental exploration into densifying and compacting ceramic fuel into a printed TPMS metal shell.
The miniature rectangular design was originally chosen for study based on the INFLUX designs from
previous years (Hyer et al., 2025). Each can was filled/packed with the YSZ beads and vibropacked until
the can was full to the top, as shown in Figure 3a. SPS was not pursued due to the likelihood that the
structure would be deformed without an intermediate solid that could fill the open space of the lattice.
Therefore, HIP was chosen as the compacting method. However, HIP requires the TPMS can to be
hermetically sealed for argon to equivalently apply pressure around all surfaces of the can.

Figure 3. Processing steps for filling, sealing, and densification results for the TPMS cans. (a) Miniature rectangular and
(b) Stout designs fabricated from Ti64 using LPBF with YSZ filling.

 Three different attempts were made to seal the miniature rectangular can. The first included using the
laser from a blown powder direct energy deposition (DED) unit to melt Ti64 powders resting on top of
the YSZ, as shown in Figure 3a. Dialing in the parameters for the DED’s laser was less trivial than
expected, and the can suffered more damage than expected. The second attempt moved to use the AM250
to perform the laser welding, but this process required manually adding layers to the top of the can. Five
layers in total were added to the top of the can without any damage to the can itself, as shown in Figure
3a. However, the sealing layer appeared to be too porous and was not hermetically sealed; the poor path
of heat conduction with the YSZ beads underneath was likely not conducive to ideal melting, wetting, and
solidifying of the Ti64 powders.
 The final attempt to seal the miniature rectangular TPMS involved first cutting out similarly S-shaped
pieces from a 300 µm pure titanium foil and then welding them using an electron-beam system. Figure 3a
shows the final component after welding; the component happened to be hermetically sealed. An
American Isostatic Presses HP630 HIP unit was used in this work with a graphite furnace and argon as
the chamber gas. The can underwent HIP at a more rigorous industry standard for Ti64: operating below
1000°C (Tammas-Williams et al., 2016). Zry-4 and Ti64 both have an α→β transition; the Ti64 α→β
transition is around 980°C. The HIP strategy keeps the temperature low enough to minimize β stability.
However, this strategy did not work, and minimal compaction of the can was observed, regardless of
using a dense or porous-walled can.
 To be more fitting to the original INFLUX design, the Stout design (Figure 3b) was chosen. This
manageable scaled version is similar to the miniature rectangular can, but with the funnel on top.

 6

Moreover, the circular hole on top of the funnel makes sealing with a foil significantly easier. Figure 3b
shows the Stout v1 fabricated from Ti64 (dense parameters for the walls), vibropacked with the YSZ
beads, sealed with the titanium foil, and subsequently processed via HIP. A more aggressive HIP strategy
was performed, similar to a classic HIP for YSZ: going to temperatures over 1000°C at higher pressures
>100 MPa (Chaim & Hefetz, 1998). As shown in Figure 3b, the Stout can shrank roughly 10% in all
directions, and the YSZ appears to have densified. Further characterization of the YSZ/Ti64 interface is
ongoing and will be performed before selecting the HIP strategy for Zry-4 TPMS cans loaded with
ceramic.
 3. Carbonaceous TPMS Fabrication for the HTGR Design
 The HTGR product line replaces the metallic can with a carbonaceous one, and in doing so replaces
every process assumption that governs the LWR pathway. Graphite cannot be melted, so no powder bed
fusion route applies; TRISO particles carry coatings that must survive consolidation intact, so the
aggressive pressures used for ceramic compaction in a metal can are not available. The work reported
here therefore addressed three coupled problems in parallel: preparing the kernels so that they can be
densified without damaging their coatings, producing a carbonaceous lattice capable of containing them,
and densifying the assembly into a monolithic fuel form. Section 3.1 covers kernel overcoating, which
serves both candidate lattice routes. Sections 3.2 and 3.3 cover those two routes: (1) sacrificial polymer
balloons and (2) directly printed graphite.
 3.1 Kernel Overcoating
 Creating a continuous graphitic matrix surrounding the kernels within the HTGR carbonaceous
TPMS lattice is crucial. TRISO particle fuel was chosen as the desired fuel form for the TPMS fuel
loading. Although TRISO fuel already comprises inherently coated ceramic kernels (Pappano et al., 2008;
Terrani et al., 2015), the kernels were overcoated with a sacrificial graphitic matrix to promote uniform
densification rather than risk cracking the critical layers surrounding the fuel kernel (Pappano et al., 2008;
Terrani et al., 2015). Multiple routes for producing a carbonaceous TPMS structure were proposed,
including fabrication of a polymer balloon that can be charred during a cold or low-temperature isostatic
pressing or direct printing of graphite and densification with CVI. The overcoat would be beneficial as a
stress absorber for isostatic pressing or would more readily infiltrate during CVI for improved changes of
high-quality bonding between the kernel and matrix.
 Figure 4a illustrates the overcoating process, which uses a wet-granulation technique inside a custom
rotary mixer. Either surrogate TRISO particles or noncoated YSZ kernels were loaded into an inclined
tumbling drum, which rotated at a constant speed to establish a continuous rolling bed. A liquid slurry
consisting of graphitic powder suspended in an organic binder system (aka graphite matrix phenolic
[GMP] slurry) was prepared and delivered into the tumbling bed via a pneumatic spraying system. As the
kernels tumble, surface tension and capillary forces drive the progressive, layer-wise accumulation of the
slurry onto the spherical substrates, building up a concentric green overcoat of uniform thickness (t)
across the batch, as shown in Figure 4b. The green overcoated granules are then subjected to a thermal
carburization step in an inert atmosphere at high temperature. Carburization encourages thermal
decomposition and pyrolysis of the organic binder phase, thereby converting the green coating into a
rigid, carbonaceous/graphitic matrix shell surrounding each individual kernel, as shown in Figure 4c.
Carburization does not fully densify the coating, but it does achieve densities near 50% that keep the
kernels from being fragile during handling or loading. Figure 4d shows the porosity of the carburized
layer compared to the dense, uniform coatings on the TRISO surrogate.

 3.2 Polymer Balloon Fabrication and Densification
 Polymer AM has become ubiquitous across industrial and research applications due to its low cost
and rapid prototyping flexibility. The most common thermoplastic printing process is FFF, which relies
on the extrusion of a polymer filament through a heated nozzle above its softening temperature to
selectively deposit material that solidifies upon cooling. Commercially available short carbon fiber
reinforced filaments offer enhanced stiffness and elevated carbon char yields compared to neat polymers.
For this study, a 20 vol % short carbon fiber reinforced nylon (CF20) filament was procured from Fiberon
(New London, North Carolina) and processed on a Bambu Lab (Shenzhen, China) X1 Carbon 3D printer.
 Figure 5a shows the initial evaluated as-printed CF20 mini-rectangular TPMS lattices loaded with
overcoated TRISO surrogate kernels and capped with a GMP slurry. The GMP slurry uses ethanol as a
volatile carrier solvent that evaporates upon drying to consolidate the capping matrix. Cold isostatic
pressing (CIP) was evaluated to compact the kernel bed before thermal processing. Because CIP uses
water to create the surrounding pressure, the loaded assembly was vacuum-sealed inside a flexible
polymer bag to prevent fluid contact during pressurization. The rigid lattice broke under 15 MPa during
CIP; this fracture punctured the bag and allowed water infiltration of the GMP, as shown in Figure 5a.
 CIP was a good first step, but pressure needed to be applied by a gas, likely at an elevated
temperature near the glass transition temperature of the polymer. Therefore, warm isostatic pressing
(WIP) was performed in the same unit as was used for HIP of the Ti64. A sealed CF20 loaded lattice
underwent WIP at 15 MPa at 100°C, 200°C, and 300°C. Figure 5b shows that no dimensional change was
observed after 100°C or 200°C. At 300°C, the lattice melted in the crucible. This result suggests that the

 8

glass transition temperature of the filament was reached, but fibers were likely not interconnected enough
to maintain a structure at that temperature.

Figure 5. FFF polymer AM. (a) FFF fabricated CF20 miniature rectangular TPMS can that was subsequently filled with
overcoated TRISO surrogate particles and sealed with the GMP slurry. (b) Densification of the CF20 filled lattice was first
performed with CIP, but the rigid lattice broke, so WIP was performed at various temperatures: no change was observed at

 One of the main issues with fused deposition modeling prints is hermeticity. Although no
dimensional change of the FFF-printed CF20 structure was visible at 200°C, this temperature is close to
the reported glass transition temperature of the material. Increasing the pressure might have caused a
similar issue to what occurred in the WIP: the part may have fractured. The other concern was whether
the structure was hermetically sealed. Fused deposition modeling/FFF parts are well known to have small
pores between filament layers. SLA is another process for fabricating polymer structure that uses
photocuring of a resin to make a structure layer by layer. The photocuring process is inherently well
controlled and often results in hermetic components. An acrylonitrile butadiene styrene–like clear resin
was acquired from Anycubic (Shenzhen, China) and processed on an Anycubic Photon Mono M7 Pro
SLA unit. Partial mini-rectangular TPMS shells were fabricated from the clear resin, as shown in Figure
6a, filled with the overcoated surrogate TRISO particles, and hermetically sealed with the GMP slurry.

Figure 6. SLA polymer AM. (a) SLA printed mini-rectangular TPMS structure fabricated from a clear resin that was then filled
with overcoated TRISO surrogate and sealed with GMP. (b) Thermogravimetric analysis performed on a piece of the SLA
material to determine where the decomposition temperature initiates.

 The thermal decomposition behavior of the cured resin was characterized via thermogravimetric
analysis (TGA) in an inert atmosphere up to 1,000°C; Figure 6b shows the decomposition profile and the
resulting piece in the crucible after being subjected to the 1,000°C cycle. The TGA thermogram
demonstrates thermal stability up to approximately 300°C, after which the cross-linked polymer network
undergoes rapid thermal degradation and pyrolysis between 300°C and 450°C. By 500°C, the mass loss
plateaus, yielding a final residual carbon char fraction of less than 15%–20% of the initial resin mass.
This substantial mass loss translates to extreme nonuniform volumetric contraction during carbonization.
Physical examination of the pyrolyzed SLA specimens reveals severe structural macrocracking, but the
sample still remains as carbonaceous remnant. At the time of this reporting, the SLA filled TPMS
structures have not undergone WIP but will likely be subjected to a temperature near the decomposition
temperature at low pressure to encourage softness without causing structure failure while managing to
drive off the organics during the process.
 3.3 Direct Graphite Printing and Densification
 In tandem with the polymer balloon routes, direct printing of a graphite matrix TPMS structure is
being investigated. PBiJ is a solid-state process operating at ambient temperature, in which an inkjet
printhead selectively deposits a liquid polymeric binding agent onto a bed of fine graphite powder, as
shown in Figure 7a. Two key characteristics make PBiJ uniquely suited for graphitic TPMS fabrication:

 10

1. Low binder fraction: The process requires a minimal volume fraction of liquid binder, ensuring
 that subsequent binder burnout and calcination produce minimal dimensional distortion or macro-
 shrinkage.
2. Interconnected open porosity: The green body retains an open, interconnected pore network
 throughout the printed lattice; this open network is a necessary precondition for internal gas-
 phase infiltration during CVI.

 11

Figure 7. Direct graphite printing and densification. (a) PBiJ fabrication of graphite parts that are then processed in a CVI
system to infiltrate and deposit PyC within the porous structure toward densification. (b) Loading of printed graphite components
into a custom graphite support fixture and placed inside a vertical hot-wall CVI reactor. (c) Demonstration of filling a printed
graphite Stout v2 TPMS can with overcoated kernels with packed powder on top that is being densified by CVI.

 Figure 7a shows initial printing trials that successfully produced high-resolution green graphite
coupons, disc specimens, and test bars with sufficient green strength for manual handling and fixturing.

 12

Graphite powder was acquired from Fisher Scientific with a −325 mesh particle size cut. The graphite
powders were processed with an ExOne Innovent system using an Aquafuse binder.
 The permeable, low-density green graphite bodies were converted into dense, high-integrity
carbonaceous structures via CVI. Printed graphite components were loaded into a custom graphite support
fixture and placed inside a vertical hot-wall CVI reactor, as shown in Figure 7b. During CVI processing, a
gaseous hydrocarbon precursor (such as methane or acetylene) flows through the heated reaction chamber
at elevated temperatures (>1,000°C). The gas trickles into the interconnected open pore network of the
green graphite parts and thermally pyrolyzes, depositing a matrix of PyC directly onto the internal pore
walls. Unlike liquid sintering or surface coating, CVI densifies the printed structure from within without
sealing off the outer surface prematurely. Figure 7b shows a comparison of pre-CVI and post-CVI test
fixtures, confirming complete retention of part geometry and sharp feature boundaries after PyC matrix
deposition.
 PBiJ and CVI processes were combined with kernel overcoating to establish a complete fabrication
workflow for the HTGR Stout TPMS fuel design shown in Figure 7c:
 1. Lattice printing: A monolithic, single-port Stout TPMS graphite can is printed via PBiJ. The
 Stout v1 printed from Ti64 was not conducive to the larger size of the overcoated kernels;
 therefore, Stout v2 was designed to accommodate the loading of the larger kernels.
 2. Kernel loading: Carburized, overcoated TRISO surrogate kernels are packed into the internal
 fuel domain through the top loading neck.
 3. Powder capping: A top layer of graphitic matrix powder is applied over the neck port to fill
 residual headspace and seal the opening.
 4. CVI densification: The entire loaded assembly undergoes CVI processing, where PyC deposition
 locks the overcoated kernels, capping powder, and outer printed Stout lattice into a fully
 integrated, monolithic carbon/carbon fuel element.
 This direct graphite PBiJ + CVI pathway avoids the severe thermal softening and low carbon yields
that caused the failure of the polymer routes, providing a viable baseline pathway for additively
manufactured HTGR fuel forms.
 4. Future Work
 Building upon the FY 2026 fail-fast screening campaign downselection, FY 2027 research activities
will concentrate on advancing the two validated manufacturing pathways—Route A (carbonaceous
HTGR fuel) and Route B (metallic LWR fuel)—from technology readiness level 2/3 to a defensible
technology readiness level 4 using prototypic feedstock.
 1. For the LWR metallic pathway (Route B), effort will focus on scaling LPBF process optimization
 from Ti64 surrogates to Zry-4. A primary metallurgical objective is controlling interstitial oxygen
 and hydrogen pickup, hydride precipitation, and solidification of grain texture during LPBF to
 ensure long-term corrosion resistance and mechanical integrity. To resolve the central tension
 between achieving ceramic fill compaction and preserving thin-walled lattice geometry during
 HIP, a secondary containment strategy using a ceramic jacket inside an outer SS304 can will be
 deployed. Hermetic cap-sealing protocols developed on Ti64 will be transferred to Zry-4 Stout
 lattices. Crucially, the HIP consolidation objective for Route B is intentionally reframed: full
 theoretical density is not the target. Instead, FY 2027 parameter sweeps will target a specified,
 controlled retained porosity window (~90% theoretical fill density) to accommodate fission gas
 release and early-life UO2 swelling without unacceptably straining the Zry-4 cladding. High-
 resolution X-ray computed tomography paired with deep learning reconstruction algorithms will
 be used to map 3D pore network connectivity, ensuring the retained voids remain interconnected

 13

 for gas relief, rather than isolated. Furthermore, in situ process monitoring using the Peregrine
 machine learning framework will be integrated into the Renishaw LPBF platform to establish an
 anomaly-detection inspection basis for complex lattice cladding.
 2. For the HTGR carbonaceous pathway (Route A), process development will concentrate on
 optimizing PBiJ of synthetic graphite and subsequent PyC CVI. Key technical barriers include
 improving green-body handling strength and dimensional fidelity of PBiJ Stout lattices,
 maximizing overcoated kernel packing fractions within line-of-sight obscured internal channels,
 and mitigating CVI surface closure (i.e., premature sealing of surface pores before full internal
 matrix infiltration). CVI precursor gas partial pressures, flow rates, and dwell temperatures will
 be systematically mapped to ensure PyC matrix deposition achieves greater than 90% matrix
 density relative to traditional pressed-compact benchmarks without inducing internal matrix
 cracking or TRISO coating degradation. Although initial packing and CVI trials will use YSZ and
 Al2O3 surrogate kernels, FY 2027 will culminate in a final demonstration using qualified,
 industrial-grade TRISO particles. Cross-cutting activities across both routes will close the loop
 between design and manufacturing, coupling experimental thermal hydraulic loop testing and
 Monte Carlo neutronics modeling with measured as-fabricated yields to refine full-core
 performance and technoeconomic cost–benefit assessments.
 5. Conclusions
 The FY 2026 fail-fast screening campaign successfully identified and validated viable manufacturing
routes for additively manufactured TPMS nuclear fuel elements across LWR and HTGR product lines:
 1. Zry-4 LPBF bounding and Stout design validation: LPBF process windows for Zry-4 and
 Ti64 were successfully bounded. Processing at VED levels to consistently produce near-fully
 dense (>99%) cladding walls. Reengineering the lattice into a single-port Stout geometry resolved
 geometric overmelting and sealing issues.
 2. Hermetic cap-sealing and HIP consolidation: DED and LPBF laser reflow were ruled out for
 lattice cap sealing due to severe thermal erosion and stress cracking. Precision laser seam-welding
 of fitted metal foil caps over the Stout neck port provided a leak-tight hermetic seal. Subsequent
 HIP consolidation produced isotropic clad shrinkage around the YSZ ceramic surrogate core,
 achieving full internal compaction without wall breaching.
 3. Termination of sacrificial polymer routes: Both the FFF thermoplastic (CF20) and SLA
 thermoset photopolymer routes were definitively terminated. Thermoplastic lattices undergo
 catastrophic viscous flow and melting at 300°C, and SLA photopolymers produce low char yields
 (<20%) that lead to severe structural cracking and loss of lattice geometry during thermal
 decomposition.
 4. Baseline direct graphite PBiJ + CVI route: Direct PBiJ printing of graphite lattices combined
 with rotary spray overcoating of TRISO kernels and PyC CVI matrix deposition was established
 as the primary fabrication pathway for HTGR fuel forms. CVI PyC deposition successfully
 densifies the interconnected open pore network of the printed graphite Stout can from within,
 producing an integrated, monolithic fuel element.
