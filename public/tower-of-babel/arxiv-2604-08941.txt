Predictive Entropy Links Calibration and Paraphrase Sensitivity in Medical Vision-Language Models
Binesh Sadanadan, Vahid Behzadan

Abstract
Medical Vision-Language Models (VLMs) suffer from two failure modes that threaten safe deployment: miscalibrated confidence and sensitivity to question rephrasing. We show they share a common cause, proximity to the decision boundary, by benchmarking five uncertainty quantification methods on MedGemma-4B-IT across in-distribution (MIMIC-CXR) and out-of-distribution (PadChest) chest X-ray datasets, with cross-architecture validation on LLaVA-RAD-7B. For well-calibrated single-model methods, predictive entropy from one forward pass predicts which samples will flip under rephrasing (AUROC = 0.711 on MedGemma, 0.878 on LLaVA-RAD; p < 10 − 4 p<10^{-4} ), enabling a single entropy threshold to flag both unreliable and rephrase-sensitive predictions. A five-member LoRA ensemble fails under the MIMIC → \rightarrow PadChest shift (42.9% ECE, 34.1% accuracy), though LLaVA-RAD’s ensemble does not collapse (69.1%). MC Dropout achieves the best calibration (ECE = 4.3%) and selective prediction coverage (21.5% at 5% risk), yet total entropy from a single forward pass outperforms the ensemble for both error detection (AUROC = 0.743 vs. 0.657) and paraphrase screening. Simple methods win.



1
Introduction

A radiologist asks a Vision-Language Model (VLM) whether a chest X-ray shows pleural effusion. The model answers “Yes” with 94% softmax confidence. The radiologist moves on. But the model is wrong, and its confidence provides no warning. Worse, if the radiologist asks the same question in slightly different words, the model might flip its answer to “No.” Miscalibrated confidence in medical AI isn’t a technical nuisance; it’s a patient safety problem. When clinicians can’t distinguish reliable predictions from overconfident errors, they lose the ability to exercise appropriate skepticism, request second opinions, or escalate ambiguous cases
[
28
,
24
]
. The promise of medical VLMs for clinical decision support
[
18
,
37
,
33
]
therefore depends on a capability that current evaluations largely ignore: well-calibrated uncertainty.

Three compounding problems make Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) especially urgent for medical VLMs. First, standard softmax probabilities are poorly calibrated; modern deep networks tend to be overconfident
[
19
]
, and this miscalibration worsens with scale and domain-specific fine-tuning. Second, clinical deployment inevitably involves distribution shift: new scanner hardware, different patient demographics, or degraded acquisition quality. Calibration that looks adequate in-distribution can deteriorate under even mild corruption
[
41
]
. Third, medical VLMs have a separate fragility that calibration alone doesn’t address: they often give contradictory answers when a question is rephrased, even if the meaning hasn’t changed
[
4
]
. Prior work treats miscalibration and paraphrase sensitivity as independent failure modes. We argue they are symptoms of the same underlying problem.

Despite growing interest in medical VLMs, existing evaluations focus almost exclusively on accuracy. Benchmarks like VQA-RAD
[
31
]
and SLAKE
[
34
]
report accuracy and F1 but not whether a model’s confidence tracks its actual correctness. Recent robustness evaluations confirm this gap: VLMs degrade under common imaging artifacts, yet no study measures whether their uncertainty scores remain reliable under these shifts
[
27
]
. Meanwhile, the UQ literature has developed mature methods: temperature scaling
[
19
]
, MC Dropout
[
14
]
, deep ensembles
[
30
]
, and conformal prediction
[
1
]
. Recent work has proposed Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) ensembles as a parameter-efficient route to calibrated uncertainty in large language models
[
49
,
38
]
. These studies report improved calibration in-distribution, but deep ensembles perform best on obvious far-distribution shifts and can fail on more subtle near-distribution data
[
47
,
44
]
. No prior work has tested LoRA ensembles under the cross-site shifts that define real clinical deployment, where the distribution change is subtle enough to evade detection but large enough to break calibration.

We evaluate five UQ methods on MedGemma-4B-IT
[
18
]
across in-distribution (MIMIC-CXR) and out-of-distribution (PadChest) chest X-ray datasets, with cross-architecture validation on LLaVA-RAD-7B
[
6
]
. We discover that predictive entropy serves as a unified proxy for both error detection and paraphrase vulnerability. This paper makes four contributions:

1.

Uncertainty predicts paraphrase sensitivity.
For well-calibrated single-model methods, predictions with high predictive entropy are significantly more likely to flip under rephrasing (area under the ROC curve, AUROC = 0.711;
p<10^{-26}
). Paraphrase instability is not a separate failure mode; it’s a symptom of decision-boundary proximity that entropy already captures. A single entropy threshold can flag both unreliable and rephrase-unstable predictions, giving clinicians one abstention criterion instead of two.

2.

LoRA ensembles fail out-of-distribution.
A five-member LoRA ensemble
[
22
]
performs well in-distribution but fails under the MIMIC
\rightarrow
PadChest shift, producing an Expected Calibration Error (ECE) of 42.9% and accuracy of just 34.1%. Per-member analysis reveals that only one of five adapters generalizes; probability averaging lets four poorly performing members outvote the one that works. This failure inverts the uncertainty-paraphrase bridge signal (AUROC = 0.377).

3.

Entropy decomposition is method-dependent.
Total predictive entropy detects errors well (AUROC = 0.743). MC Dropout at standard dropout rates yields negligible epistemic uncertainty, while the deep ensemble produces meaningful mutual information (0.082 nats), elevated for errors
[
9
,
45
]
. Yet total entropy from a single model still outperforms.

4.

Selective prediction under distribution shift.
MC Dropout covers 21.5% of samples at 5% target risk on out-of-distribution data versus 7.3% for softmax. The deep ensemble achieves zero coverage at this threshold.

Together, these results point to a practical recommendation: a well-fine-tuned single model with softmax entropy handles calibration, error detection, and paraphrase sensitivity screening in one forward pass. Cross-architecture validation on LLaVA-RAD-7B confirms the bridge generalizes (AUROC = 0.878 on MIMIC), supporting the geometric explanation: near-boundary predictions have higher entropy and flip more, regardless of architecture. LoRA ensembles trained on single-site data should not be deployed cross-site without per-member validation.

Related Work

Calibration in deep learning.

19
showed that modern neural networks are poorly calibrated: confidence scores systematically overestimate correctness. Temperature scaling
[
42
]
remains the dominant post-hoc fix, and ECE
[
39
]
the standard metric, though binning artifacts have prompted adaptive variants
[
40
]
. Calibration degrades under distribution shift
[
41
]
, a routine setting in clinical deployment where patient populations, imaging hardware, and protocols vary across sites.

Bayesian and ensemble uncertainty.

MC Dropout
[
14
]
reinterprets test-time dropout as approximate variational inference, yielding epistemic uncertainty at low cost. Deep ensembles
[
30
]
consistently outperform single models on calibration and out-of-distribution detection. Decomposing predictive uncertainty into epistemic and aleatoric components
[
9
]
informs safe deployment: epistemic uncertainty should trigger abstention, while aleatoric uncertainty reflects irreducible noise. Mutual information provides a principled epistemic estimator
[
45
,
21
]
. Recent work extends this to parameter-efficient settings:
49
propose LoRA ensembles for LLM calibration,
38
show they can match explicit ensembles on vision tasks, and
51
apply Laplace approximation to LoRA parameters for calibrated posteriors without multiple forward passes. None of these evaluate cross-site medical deployment. Our bridge finding depends on total predictive entropy and is agnostic to which UQ method produces it, so BayesLoRA-style posteriors could serve as an alternative source; we leave this comparison to future work. More broadly,
44
identify failure modes for ensembles of fine-tuned transformers under distribution shift in a large-scale evaluation on WILDS benchmarks.

Uncertainty in medical AI.

Prior work has studied UQ for medical image classification, including skin lesion diagnosis
[
8
]
, retinal disease grading
[
32
]
, and chest X-ray pathology detection
[
17
]
. These efforts address unimodal, image-only classifiers. For OOD detection specifically,
47

[
33
,
7
,
52
,
18
]
introduce text-conditioned reasoning, yet evaluation stays focused on accuracy benchmarks. The CARES benchmark
[
50
]
measures trustworthiness dimensions like fairness and safety but omits calibration. No prior study systematically evaluates calibration or UQ for medical VLMs.

Selective prediction.

Selective prediction lets a model abstain on low-confidence inputs, trading coverage for accuracy
[
11
,
15
]
. The Area Under the Risk-Coverage Curve (AURC)
[
16
]
summarizes this trade-off. Conformal prediction
[
48
,
1
]
and learn-then-test frameworks
[

provide finite-sample risk guarantees.
35

Paraphrase sensitivity.

The PSF-Med benchmark
[
4
]
showed that medical VLMs produce contradictory answers to paraphrased questions at high rates.
12
and
43
established that such linguistic sensitivity failures are pervasive across language models. No prior work has tested whether paraphrase sensitivity and poor calibration share a common mechanism.

Our contribution.

Prior work treats calibration, selective prediction, and paraphrase sensitivity as separate problems. To our knowledge, no study has (i) systematically evaluated UQ quality for medical VLMs, (ii) formally connected paraphrase sensitivity to predictive uncertainty, or (iii) shown that a single entropy threshold can flag both unreliable and rephrase-unstable predictions.

3
Background

This section defines the quantities used throughout. Standard definitions (calibration, ECE, Brier score, temperature scaling) appear in Appendix
A
.

3.1
VLM as Binary Classifier

MedGemma-4B-IT
[
18
]
pairs a SigLIP vision encoder (
K=256
image tokens) with a Gemma 2 language model (34 transformer layers). Given a chest radiograph
\mathbf{x}
and a binary diagnostic question
\mathbf{q}
(e.g., “Is there a pleural effusion?”), the model generates a response autoregressively. We extract the logit vector
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{W}|}
at the first generated token position and keep only the “Yes” and “No” logits:

z_{+}=\mathbf{z}[\text{Yes}],\quad z_{-}=\mathbf{z}[\text{No}].

(1)

The softmax probability for the positive class is then

\hat{p}=\sigma(z_{+}-z_{-})=\frac{\exp(z_{+})}{\exp(z_{+})+\exp(z_{-})},

(2)

reducing the generative VLM to a binary classifier suitable for calibration analysis.

3.2
Predictive Uncertainty Decomposition

For a single deterministic model, total predictive uncertainty is the entropy of the predicted distribution:

\mathbb{H}[Y\mid\mathbf{x}]=-\hat{p}\log\hat{p}-(1-\hat{p})\log(1-\hat{p}).

(3)

When multiple stochastic forward passes are available (from MC Dropout or a deep ensemble of
S
members with parameters
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}\}_{s=1}^{S}
), total uncertainty decomposes into aleatoric and epistemic components
[
9
,
45
]
. Let
\hat{p}_{s}=p(Y{=}1\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})
and
\bar{p}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s}\hat{p}_{s}
. Total uncertainty is the entropy of the mean prediction:

\underbrace{\mathbb{H}[Y\mid\mathbf{x}]}_{\text{total}}=-\bar{p}\log\bar{p}-(1-\bar{p})\log(1-\bar{p}).

(4)

Epistemic uncertainty is the Mutual Information (MI) between the prediction and the model parameters:

\underbrace{\mathbb{I}(Y;\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\mathbf{x})}_{\text{epistemic}}=\mathbb{H}[Y\mid\mathbf{x}]-\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\mathbb{H}[Y\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}].

(5)

The subtracted term is the aleatoric uncertainty: the expected entropy across individual members, reflecting irreducible noise. Epistemic uncertainty captures model ignorance and should be high on out-of-distribution inputs
[
23
]
.

3.3
Selective Prediction

A model can abstain on inputs where its uncertainty score
u(\mathbf{x})
exceeds a threshold
\tau
, trading coverage for accuracy
[
11
]
. Varying
\tau
traces a risk-coverage curve. We summarize selective prediction quality with two metrics: AURC (lower is better)
[
16
]
, and coverage@risk
α
, the maximum fraction of samples the model can answer while keeping error rate below a target
\alpha
. Formal definitions appear in Appendix
A
.

4
Methods

4.1
Uncertainty Estimation Methods

We evaluate five UQ methods that span single-pass, post-hoc, and ensemble-based approaches. All methods operate on the decision margin
m(\mathbf{x},q)=\ell_{\texttt{Yes}}-\ell_{\texttt{No}}
and softmax probability
\hat{p}=\sigma(m)
defined in Section
3.1
.

Softmax entropy (baseline).

A single forward pass yields the predictive entropy
\mathbb{H}[\hat{p}]
(Eq.
3
) and confidence
c=\max(\hat{p},1-\hat{p})
. This method adds no computation beyond standard inference.

Temperature scaling.

We learn a scalar
T>0
on a held-out calibration partition (15% of evaluation data) by minimizing negative log-likelihood via L-BFGS
[
19
]
. At test time,
\hat{p}_{T}=\sigma(m/T^{*})
. Temperature scaling preserves the decision boundary while reshaping the confidence distribution;
T^{*}>1
corrects the overconfidence typical of large language models
[
26
]
. All reported metrics come from the remaining 85%.

MC Dropout.

Our LoRA adapters include dropout (
p_{\text{drop}}=0.05
) on each low-rank projection. At test time we keep dropout active and run
K=10
stochastic forward passes, producing probability samples
\{p^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}

[
14
]
. The predictive mean
\bar{p}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k}p^{(k)}
and entropy decomposition (Eqs.
4
and
5
) follow directly.

Deep ensemble.

We train
M=5
LoRA adapters independently with different random seeds
[
30
]
. We average ensemble predictions in probability space and apply the same entropy decomposition as MC Dropout. Because each adapter follows a different optimization trajectory, the ensemble captures functional diversity that MC Dropout cannot
[
13
]
.

Margin magnitude.

The absolute decision margin
|m(\mathbf{x},q)|
serves as a non-parametric confidence score. Large margins mean the “Yes” and “No” logits sit far apart; small margins mean near-equipoise. Because margin derives
\hat{p}
from the same forward pass as softmax, their calibration metrics (ECE, Brier, NLL) are identical by construction; the methods differ only in the ranking used for selective prediction.

4.2
UQ-Paraphrase Sensitivity Bridge

Our central methodological contribution connects two failure modes that prior work has treated independently: miscalibrated uncertainty and paraphrase sensitivity.

Paraphrase protocol.

For each test sample
(\mathbf{x}_{i},q_{i})
we collect predictions
\{\hat{y}_{i}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{P_{i}}
under
P_{i}
semantically equivalent paraphrases from the PSF-Med benchmark
[
4
]
. A
flip
occurs if any paraphrase produces a different binary prediction:

f_{i}=\mathbb{1}\!\Bigl[\exists\,j\in\{1,\ldots,P_{i}\}:\hat{y}_{i}^{(j)}\neq\hat{y}_{i}\Bigr].

(6)

Geometric intuition.

The connection rests on a simple observation. When the decision margin
m
is large, the “Yes” and “No” logits sit far apart; rephrasing shifts the internal representation slightly, but not enough to cross the boundary. When
m\approx 0
, even a small perturbation can flip the answer. High predictive entropy signals exactly this near-boundary regime. If this reasoning holds, uncertainty scores should be systematically higher for flip-prone predictions than for stable ones.

Testing the bridge.

We test this with two analyses. First, a group comparison: we split the test set into flipped (
f_{i}=1
) and stable (
f_{i}=0
) subsets and compare entropy distributions via the Mann-Whitney
U
test. Second, a discrimination test: we treat flip prediction as binary classification and report the AUROC of each method’s uncertainty score as the positive-class predictor. If both confirm the hypothesis, paraphrase sensitivity can be recast as a symptom of predictive uncertainty rather than an independent failure mode, and a single entropy threshold can serve as a unified abstention criterion.

Discussion

The bridge between uncertainty and paraphrase sensitivity.

The connection between predictive entropy and paraphrase sensitivity is our central finding. On MedGemma-4B-IT under the MIMIC
\rightarrow
PadChest shift, flip-prone predictions carry higher entropy across all single-model methods (AUROC = 0.711,
p<10^{-26}
), and the same pattern holds on LLaVA-RAD-7B (AUROC = 0.878 on MIMIC, 0.706 on PadChest). The explanation is geometric: predictions near the decision boundary produce elevated entropy and flip more under rephrasing. Consistency training
[
4
]
reduces flip rates by sharpening entropy distributions, suggesting these are two views of the same phenomenon. A deployment system gets paraphrase sensitivity screening for free alongside uncertainty estimation.

Why LoRA ensembles fail out-of-distribution.

The MedGemma ensemble’s OOD failure runs counter to the standard finding that deep ensembles provide strong uncertainty under shift
[
41
,
30
]
, though recent evaluations document similar failures for fine-tuned transformers
[
44
]
. The LLaVA-RAD ensemble does
not
exhibit this failure (69.1% accuracy vs. MedGemma’s 34.1%), suggesting the mechanism is model-specific. For MedGemma, only seed 42 generalizes; probability averaging lets four poorly performing members dilute its signal (Appendix
G
). The ensemble produces meaningful MI (0.082 nats, elevated for errors), but this diversity does not improve error detection because OOD accuracy is too poor. Neither logit averaging nor majority vote rescues performance (Appendix
G
).

Clinical deployment recommendations.

\rightarrow
PadChest shift point toward a two-tier deployment protocol, summarized in Table

.

Robustness under image corruption.

Targeted LoRA calibration is remarkably stable under synthetic corruption (Table

improves
from 0.091 (clean) to 0.084 at severity 5, because corruption pushes the model toward conservative predictions that reduce undetected failures. Conformal coverage guarantees also hold: calibrating on clean data and testing on severity-5 corruptions produces over-coverage (92.4% empirical vs. 90% target; Appendix
J.1
). By contrast, Base model AUGRC degrades by +0.020 and conformal coverage drops by 7.5 percentage points. This supports deploying Targeted LoRA in settings where image quality varies across clinical sites.

Limitations.

Both models share the single cross-site transition (MIMIC-CXR
\rightarrow
PadChest); other site pairs may show different calibration properties. We evaluate binary yes/no questions only; free-text generation requires semantic entropy
[
29
]
. The geometric bridge argument should generalize to meaning-space boundaries but we have not tested this.

The ensemble failure is model-specific (LLaVA-RAD maintains 69.1% OOD accuracy). MC Dropout’s negligible MI extends to
p_{\text{drop}}=0.20
(Appendix
H
), suggesting the bottleneck is adapter-only scope (4.38M/4B parameters). Our entropy threshold can be viewed as a simple routing policy; more sophisticated routing to stronger models or retrieval-augmented pipelines is a natural extension. The MIMIC-CXR subset (
n=98
) limits standalone statistical claims, which we mitigate through bootstrap CIs. Our synthetic corruptions approximate but do not capture the full complexity of real clinical distribution shifts. Future directions appear in Appendix
O
.

Appendix A
Standard Definitions

This appendix collects standard metric definitions referenced in the main text.

Calibration.

A probabilistic classifier is calibrated if its predicted confidence matches its empirical accuracy. Formally, for confidence
c\in[0,1]
:

P\bigl(\hat{y}=y\mid\hat{p}=c\bigr)=c,\quad\forall\,c\in[0,1],

(7)

where
\hat{y}
is the predicted label,
y
is the ground truth, and
\hat{p}
is the model’s predicted probability.

Expected Calibration Error (ECE).

We partition predictions into
B=15
uniform-width bins by confidence
c_{i}=\max(\hat{p}_{i},1-\hat{p}_{i})
and compute:

\text{ECE}=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{|B_{b}|}{N}\bigl|\text{acc}(B_{b})-\text{conf}(B_{b})\bigr|,

(8)

where
|B_{b}|
is the number of samples in bin
b
,
\text{acc}(B_{b})
is the empirical accuracy within the bin, and
\text{conf}(B_{b})
is the mean confidence
[
39
]
. Lower ECE indicates better calibration.

Brier score.

The Brier score is a strictly proper scoring rule that decomposes into calibration and refinement components:

\text{BS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(\hat{p}_{i}-y_{i}\bigr)^{2},

(9)

where
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is the ground-truth label (1 for “Yes”). A perfect model achieves BS = 0.

Negative log-likelihood (NLL).

The average NLL measures the sharpness of correctly calibrated predictions:

\text{NLL}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl[y_{i}\log\hat{p}_{i}+(1-y_{i})\log(1-\hat{p}_{i})\bigr].

(10)

Temperature scaling.

Temperature scaling learns a single scalar
T>0
on a held-out calibration partition by minimizing NLL via L-BFGS
[
19
]
:

T^{*}=\argmin_{T>0}\;-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{cal}}}\Bigl[y_{i}\log\sigma\!\bigl(m_{i}/T\bigr)\\
+(1-y_{i})\log\bigl(1-\sigma(m_{i}/T)\bigr)\Bigr].

(11)

At test time,
\hat{p}_{T}=\sigma(m/T^{*})
. Temperature scaling preserves the decision boundary (
\hat{y}
stays the same) while reshaping the confidence distribution;
T^{*}>1
spreads probability mass away from the extremes.

Selective prediction risk.

Given a confidence threshold
\kappa
, the selective risk is the misclassification rate on retained samples:

r(\kappa)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}[\hat{y}_{i}\neq y_{i}]\cdot\mathbb{1}[c_{i}\geq\kappa]}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}[c_{i}\geq\kappa]}.

(12)

Area Under the Risk-Coverage Curve (AURC).

AURC integrates the selective risk over all coverage levels
[
15
]
:

\text{AURC}=\int_{0}^{1}r(\kappa)\;d\kappa.

(13)

Lower AURC means the uncertainty method better separates correct predictions from incorrect ones.

C.3
Deep Ensemble Construction

The deep ensemble consists of
M=5
independently trained LoRA adapters, each initialized with a different random seed (42, 123, 456, 789, 2024) controlling both parameter initialization and data shuffling order. The base model weights stay frozen and shared across all ensemble members; only the LoRA adapter weights differ. At inference, each adapter produces logits
\ell^{(k)}_{\text{yes}}
and
\ell^{(k)}_{\text{no}}
for
k=1,\ldots,5
. The ensemble predictive distribution is the uniform average of the individual softmax distributions:

\bar{p}(y\mid x,q)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}p_{k}(y\mid x,q),

(14)

where
p_{k}(y\mid x,q)=\mathrm{softmax}(\ell^{(k)}_{\text{yes}},\ell^{(k)}_{\text{no}})
and
y\in\{\text{Yes},\text{No}\}
.

C.4
MC Dropout Configuration

MC Dropout reuses the
p_{\text{dropout}}=0.05
already present in the LoRA adapter layers. At inference, we keep dropout active (rather than the default evaluation-mode behavior of disabling it) and perform
T=10
stochastic forward passes for each input. The predictive distribution is:

\hat{p}(y\mid x,q)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta_{t}}(y\mid x,q),

(15)

where
\theta_{t}
denotes the model parameters with dropout mask
t
. We use a single trained LoRA adapter (seed 42) as the base for MC Dropout inference.

C.5
Temperature Scaling

Temperature scaling learns a single scalar parameter
\tau>0
that rescales logits before the softmax:
p_{\tau}(y\mid x,q)=\mathrm{softmax}([\ell_{\text{yes}},\ell_{\text{no}}]/\tau)
. We optimize
\tau
by minimizing the negative log-likelihood on a held-out calibration set (15% of the evaluation data, randomly selected, minimum 20 samples). Optimization uses L-BFGS with a maximum of 50 iterations, converging in all cases.

Appendix E
Statistical Testing Details

E.1
Bootstrap Confidence Intervals

We compute all confidence intervals via the nonparametric bootstrap
[
10
]
. For a metric
\hat{\theta}
computed on a test set of
n
samples, we:

1.

Draw
B=2{,}000
bootstrap replicates by sampling
n
observations with replacement from the test set.

2.

Compute the metric
\hat{\theta}^{*(b)}
on each bootstrap replicate
b=1,\ldots,B
.

3.

Report the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution as the 95% confidence interval:
\mathrm{CI}_{95}=[\hat{\theta}^{*}_{(0.025)},\hat{\theta}^{*}_{(0.975)}]
.

For metrics computed on paired data (e.g., ECE difference between two methods on the same test set), we bootstrap the pairs jointly to preserve the correlation structure.

E.2
Pairwise Method Comparisons

To test whether one UQ method significantly outperforms another on a given metric, we use a paired bootstrap test. Under the null hypothesis
H_{0}:\theta_{A}=\theta_{B}
, we compute the bootstrap distribution of the difference
\Delta^{*(b)}=\hat{\theta}_{A}^{*(b)}-\hat{\theta}_{B}^{*(b)}
and report the fraction of replicates where
\Delta^{*(b)}\leq 0
(for metrics where lower is better) as the
p
-value. This is a two-sided test implemented as
p=2\min(p_{\text{left}},p_{\text{right}})
.

E.3
Multiple Comparison Correction

For the UQ-PSF bridge analysis, we compare five UQ methods on the flip-prediction AUROC. The raw
p
-values from the Mann-Whitney
U
test range from
2\times 10^{-26}
(softmax) to
5\times 10^{-10}
(deep ensemble). Even a conservative Bonferroni correction (multiplying by 5) does not change any significance conclusion. We report raw
p
-values throughout.

E.4
UQ-PSF Bridge: Mann-Whitney
U
Test

We test the association between predictive uncertainty and paraphrase sensitivity using the Mann-Whitney
U
test
[
36
]
, a non-parametric test comparing the distributions of uncertainty scores between two groups: predictions that flip under paraphrasing and predictions that remain stable.

Let
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n_{f}}\}
denote the entropy values for the
n_{f}
flipped predictions and
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n_{s}}\}
the entropy values for the
n_{s}
stable predictions. The test statistic is:

U=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{f}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{s}}\mathbf{1}[u_{i}>v_{j}]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}[u_{i}=v_{j}],

(16)

which counts the number of pairwise comparisons where a flipped prediction has higher entropy than a stable prediction. Under
H_{0}
(no difference in distributions),
U
follows a known null distribution. For large samples, we use the normal approximation with continuity correction. We report exact
p
-values when sample sizes permit.

We also report the rank-biserial correlation
r=1-2U/(n_{f}\cdot n_{s})
as an effect size measure, where
r=1
indicates perfect separation (all flipped predictions have higher entropy) and
r=0
indicates no association.

E.5
AUROC for Flip Prediction

To evaluate how well uncertainty predicts paraphrase sensitivity as a binary classification task, we compute the AUROC using the predictive entropy of each sample as the score and the flip/stable label as the binary outcome. The AUROC equals the probability that a randomly chosen flipped prediction has higher entropy than a randomly chosen stable prediction, providing a threshold-free measure of discriminative ability.

E.6
Effect of Severity on Calibration

We evaluate whether calibration degrades monotonically with corruption severity by computing ECE at severity levels 1, 3, and 5 for each corruption type and method. As reported in Section
5.2
, the relationship is not monotonic: Gaussian noise shows a spike at severity 1 followed by recovery, while other corruption types produce modest and largely flat ECE trajectories.

Appendix F
Entropy Decomposition Details

For completeness, we provide the full derivation of the entropy decomposition used in the main text. The total predictive uncertainty (predictive entropy) for the ensemble is:

\mathbb{H}[\bar{p}]=-\sum_{y\in\{\text{Yes},\text{No}\}}\bar{p}(y)\log\bar{p}(y),

(17)

where
\bar{p}(y)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}p_{k}(y)
is the ensemble mean prediction. This total uncertainty decomposes as:

\underbrace{\mathbb{H}[\bar{p}]}_{\text{total}}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mathbb{H}[p_{k}]}_{\text{aleatoric (expected entropy)}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{I}[y;k\mid x,q]}_{\text{epistemic (mutual information)}},

(18)

where the aleatoric component is the average entropy of individual ensemble members (reflecting irreducible noise in the data), and the epistemic component is the mutual information between the predicted label
y
and the ensemble member index
k
(reflecting disagreement among ensemble members due to model uncertainty). The mutual information is:

\mathbb{I}[y;k\mid x,q]=\mathbb{H}[\bar{p}]-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mathbb{H}[p_{k}].

(19)

For MC Dropout, the same decomposition applies with the dropout mask index
t
replacing the ensemble member index
k
, and
T
replacing
M
.

Appendix O
Future Directions

Five directions extend this work.

Higher MC Dropout rates.

Dropout rates of 0.1–0.2 may produce meaningful epistemic decomposition with LoRA-adapted medical VLMs. The key question is at what cost to base accuracy, since the adapters were trained with
p_{\text{drop}}=0.05
. Retraining with matched dropout rates would clarify this trade-off.

Diverse ensemble training.

Each adapter in our ensemble sees the same training data. Training each on a different data subset, with a different LoRA rank, or targeting different layers could prevent the shared-bias failure we observe. This tests whether diversity in the training procedure prevents the OOD failure rather than simply hoping that random seed variation provides sufficient functional diversity.

Semantic entropy for free-text VQA.

Our binary reduction enables clean uncertainty extraction but excludes open-ended generation. Free-text output requires aggregating token-level uncertainties into meaning-level estimates. Semantic entropy
[
29
]
clusters generations by meaning and computes entropy over clusters, extending the UQ-PSF bridge to settings where paraphrase sensitivity manifests as semantic drift rather than binary flips.

Conformal prediction with conditional guarantees.

Appendix
J
provides a preliminary conformal baseline. Distribution-free coverage guarantees
[
1
]
complement the heuristic thresholds in our selective prediction analysis. Conditional extensions that ensure guarantees hold across clinical subgroups
[
35
]
(e.g., by disease prevalence, patient demographics, or imaging site) would strengthen the fairness properties of the abstention criterion.

Real-world distribution shift validation.

Our five synthetic corruption types approximate but do not capture the full complexity of clinical distribution shifts. Validation on multi-site clinical data with natural shifts across scanner manufacturers, imaging protocols, and patient demographics would test whether the calibration stability we observe transfers to deployment. Multi-center studies with prospective data collection would provide the strongest evidence.

Four themes run through these results. First, LoRA fine-tuning is the primary calibration mechanism; no post-hoc UQ method applied to the base model comes close. Second, deep ensembles of LoRA adapters can fail OOD, but the failure is model-specific: MedGemma’s ensemble collapses on PadChest (34.1% accuracy) while LLaVA-RAD’s maintains 69.1%. Third, simple UQ methods suffice. Softmax entropy from a single forward pass achieves the best flip-prediction AUROC (0.711 on MedGemma, 0.706 on LLaVA-RAD for PadChest OOD) and the best error-detection AUROC (0.743). MC Dropout adds value for selective prediction at strict risk thresholds (21.5% vs. 7.3% coverage at 5% risk). Fourth, the UQ-paraphrase bridge generalizes across architectures: the geometric argument that near-boundary predictions produce both high entropy and paraphrase vulnerability holds on fundamentally different model families. The cost-effective recommendation: a well-fine-tuned single model with softmax entropy, reserving MC Dropout for scenarios that require strict selective prediction guarantees.
