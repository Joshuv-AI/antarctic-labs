Project Title: High-Voltage DC MULTI-terminal SIMulation (HVDC MULTISIM)

Project Objectives: The project goals are to (i) de-risk multi-terminal (MT)-HVDC technology
 in the context of U.S. offshore wind integration through development of
 innovative controls and protection strategies, (ii) build a new distributed
 software-in-loop (SIL) simulation platform, MULTISIM at the National
 Lab of the Rockies (NLR, formerly NREL) for cost-effective and fast
 validation and (iii) validate the developed controls and protection
 algorithms against standardized functional requirements.

Executive Summary
Expansion of the power grid in the USA is essential to fulfilling the rising energy demand of the country.
In particular, the transmission grid, a network of electrical energy corridors that enable the flow of large
amounts of energy from the point of generation to the point of consumption, needs significant
expansion to cater to this growth of electrification. The current infrastructure is also dated, and any new
transmission corridor should be based on a vision of building a modern infrastructure that is future-
proof. High voltage DC (HVDC) transmission technology falls in this category: it is the most economical
way to build large transmission lines and relies on sophisticated electronics and flexible controls, rather
than just passive components like switchgear and transformers.

This project is aimed at establishing the technical and economic feasibility of a network of HVDC lines
that are interconnected to form a multi-terminal DC network. HVDC transmission is not a new
technology and United States has a number of such lines; however, these are point to point
transmissions and do not form a DC grid.

Modeling and analyzing DC grids is challenging since there are no established modeling tools and the
traditional ways of modeling AC grids fall short of providing the fidelity required the fast dynamics of the
DC grid. This project aims to build a software-in-the-loop simulator for a multi-terminal HVDC grid,
called MULTISIM and, establish new control and protection algorithms to operate such a connected DC
system. NLR, a key partner, established the core framework of the MULTISIM simulator and validated its
functionality. The GE Vernova team, created a full-scale model of a four-terminal HVDC network using
PSCAD (Power Systems Computer Aided Design) software and established the baseline performance of
the system during normal and fault operation.

The project was started on 10/12024 with a kick-off meeting held on 12/11/2024 and ran through two
quarters till termination. All the tasks, milestones and deliverables during this period were met. Several
interim reports detailing the various tasks were submitted. The following sections provide details on the
completed tasks till the project pause and termination after Q2.

Task 2.0: Derive Preliminary Functional, Control, Protection and Grid-Interconnect Requirements.
(M1-M3)

Subtask 2.1: Draft preliminary functional, control, protection, and grid interconnect requirements.
(M1-M3)

Subtask Summary: The preliminary model requirements for the MT-HVDC system were documented.
These requirements, as mentioned below, focus on modeling needs for EMT simulation studies and
include HVDC converter station valves, control, protection, cables, surge arrestors, and grid codes. These
requirements guide the four-terminal system model development under Task 3.1 and Task 3.2.

The valve modeling requirements define the details of submodule (SM) basic circuits as well as SM-level
protection circuits necessary for system studies. Additionally, to reduce computational burden without
compromising accuracy, an equivalent circuit model is recommended.

The control system modeling requirements cover four layers of the MT-HVDC system control, from top
to bottom: dispatch control, upper-level output control, lower-level valve control, and SM switching
control. Due to the complexity and various configurations of HVDC control, the control system model
requirements focus on functional and performance criteria for each layer rather than documenting
detailed control designs.

The protection system modeling requirements outline the basic protection algorithms and include DC
circuit breakers and dynamic braking systems. The protection requirements for the MT-HVDC system
were under development and was planned to be explored further in the project; however, this could not
be completed due to the project termination. For HVDC system protection studies, cables,
transmissions, and surge arrestors are two critical passive elements. These have been well-studied in the
literature; therefore, industrial standard models are recommended for these elements.

Although grid codes vary among system operators, the ENTSO-E network code has been widely adopted
as a baseline or minimum requirement by many system operators, including VDE, TenneT, and the UK
National Grid. In this project, the ENTSO-E network code will serve as the benchmark for grid code
requirements and as the test requirement for model development.

Subtask 3.2: Derive reduced order models and test requirements. (M4-M6)

Subtask Summary: A preliminary reduced order 4 terminal MT-HVDC system model was developed
using PSCAD MMC library with (i) averaged MMC control and (ii) switching MMC control. In this
preliminary model, 3 terminals of the MT-HVDC system are controlled by DC voltage (Vdc) control and
the other terminal is controlled using power (P) control. In the current version of the switching model,
the MMC uses circulating current suppression control (CCSC).

The following tasks were completed to develop the system:

I.  Selection of model parameters: system voltages and power ratings of HVDC substations and
 transformers; system frequency; capacitance, reactance, and sizing of the modular multilevel
 converter (MMC) sub-modules. This was done from previous literature review.
II. Design-of-experiments to study the response of the 4 terminal MT-HVDC system when subjected to
 faults. These faults are applied to both the AC and DC sides of the terminals, at multiple points in the

 4

 system and under varying levels of severity. These studies were carried out with both averaged and
 switching MMC models.
After ensuring the 4 terminal MT-HVDC model was stable in steady-state operation for both MMC
configurations, the system was studied during fault events to observe how the system responds when
there is no protection present in the system. This served as a baseline result to aid future controls
development. The design-of-experiments included AC and DC faults that were applied to the system at
each terminal for 150 milliseconds (ms). Multiple scenarios for residual fault voltages and fault types
were simulated for the AC fault cases, and both line-to-ground and line-to-line cases were simulated for
the DC fault cases.

Instantaneous active power, instantaneous reactive power, DC instantaneous voltage, three-phase
voltage, and three-phase current were recorded at each terminal. At a minimum, the resources should
be stable in the aftermath of the grid event (the grid voltage and frequency should be nominal, and
oscillations should be at least slightly positively damped). In the averaged MMC configuration, the
system recovers back to steady-state during less severe AC (single-phase-to-ground) fault cases and
oscillations are present for more severe AC fault cases (line-to-line, three-phase-to-ground) and both
line-to-ground and line-to-line DC fault cases. In the switching MMC configuration, the resource is no
longer stable for any AC fault applied to the terminal with P control and significant oscillations are
present after the fault clears for most AC fault and all DC fault cases. This showed that switching models
were indeed necessary to understand the true implication of fault situations in the system.

In parallel, the state-of-the-art control strategies for MT HVDC systems were studied and tested on a
simple average 4 terminal MTDC model with 2 wind farms and 2 grid connected converters [1-3]. All
these controls were designed assuming a higher-level control for power scheduling. The following
control methods were evaluated.

i. Voltage Margin Control: In this control, one of the converters acts a slack converter and the other
 converter acts as a constant power source. In an event the slack bus is saturated or the AC grid is
 weak, the other converter goes into DC voltage regulation mode with slightly different reference
 voltages to avoid control conflict. This way, the DC voltage is always controlled, however only one
 converter is responsible for dynamically maintaining the power balance. There is no power sharing.
 This also imposes a strong AC grid requirement on the converter which is maintaining the DC
 voltage.

ii. Droop Control: The control works with a voltage-current (VI) droop characteristics implemented on
 the grid connected converters such that they can share the power variation dynamically. This
 control uses DC voltage as a global signal to communicate with other converter, therefore this
 control works by varying DC voltage to share the power. This also makes the control susceptible to
 line resistance. The DC variation due to the control can be controlled by designing the droop
 characteristics accordingly. A higher layer of control is required to control the power at each
 converter since the droop control acts as a proportional control.

iii. Ratio Control: This is a modified version of the droop control where different converters have
 different droop gains to allow power sharing proportional to the converter ratings.

iv. Priority Control: This control gives a precedence to a particular converter over the other and once
 the precedented converter is saturated, the other converter starts transferring power with a droop

 5

 characteristic. The steady-state behavior is similar to the voltage margin control. However, with the
 saturated converter, the DC voltage varies according to droop characteristics while in voltage margin
 control it is controlled by the other converter.

A simplified model was developed in PLECS (Piece-wise Linear Electrical Circuit Simulation) simulation
platform to implement and understand the control methods. This also allows to simplify the
implementation to swiftly move to the PSCAD platform and integrate with the full system simulation.

There are multiple layers of control like low level converter control, system level voltage control and
higher-level power scheduling control. Considering this, some test cases are defined to test these
system level control algorithms. The important dynamic situations are wind power variation (to zero
power output) and a weak grid condition. The wind power variation can be implemented by changing
the wind converter power reference and the weak grid conditions are indirectly implemented by
controlling the current limits of the grid connected converter.

A final control strategy selection was planned with more detailed study, especially on the dynamics of
higher-level control and fault scenarios. Under the steady state behavior, the voltage margin control
looked favorable since it keeps the DC voltage regulated.

Task 4.0: Design and Procure MULTISIM Platform Hardware and Software. (M1-M9)

Subtask 4.1: Develop multi-workstation computational platform and order hardware and software.
(M1-M6)

Subtask Summary: The NLR team focused on Task 4 during Q1 and Q2 to establish the MULTISIM
platform for simulating multi-vendor MT-HVDC transmission networks.

The MULTISIM platform consists of hardware and software solutions for performing high-fidelity EMT
simulations of complex HVDC transmission systems whose models are divided among different
computing resources. Such platform consists of hardware components including multiple workstations
that are interfaced by high-speed communication network and software solutions that enable seamless
simulation of an HVDC system whose model is divided among multiple workstations. In Q1, NLR
procured majority of the hardware required for establishing the MULTISIM platform and demonstrated
simulation of a CIGRE point-to-point HVDC transmission model divided between two workstations.
Preliminary analysis was performed on the impact of communication channel used between the two
workstations on the speed and accuracy of the simulations. In addition to the technical challenges
related to setting up the simulation of a power system model divided between two computers, the team
worked with NLR’s IT departments to obtain necessary approvals for establishing a direct
communication channel between the two workstations. The team also worked on evaluating the impact
of the location and method for splitting an HVDC network model between two workstations on the
simulation speed and accuracy. Specific activities executed during Q1 in this task are listed below:

  i. For establishing the MULTISIM platform for simulating multi-vendor multi-terminal HVDC
 transmission networks, the NLR team procured a high-performance workstation with AMD
 Ryzen Threadripper PRO 7985WX processor that can support 128 digital threads (processes) in
 parallel. This workstation was interfaced with an existing workstation to simulate an HVDC
 transmission system model divided between the two workstations.

 6

  ii. NLR procured and installed two high-speed network cards (Mellanox), one each in the two-
 workstation setup, to establish high speed communication between the two workstations. The
 network cards support 200 Gb/s throughput. They were connected directly to each other using
 active optical cable that supports up to 100 Gb/s transmission rate.
 iii. The NLR team studied and explored two different methods offered by PSCAD for establishing
 simulation on a cluster of workstations. The first method is called Cluster Launch System (CLS); it
 uses computational resources of multiple computers to simulate a power system model located
 on a particular host computer. While this method is very useful for simulating large power
 system models, it does not segregate models from different vendors on different workstations.
 That is, it does not offer IP separation, which is critical for testing multi-vendor multi-terminal
 HVDC transmission systems. The second method is called Parallel Network Interface (PNI), which
 allows interfacing of PSCAD models located on different workstations to form a complete
 system and its simulations. The NLR team demonstrated both these methods using a CIGRE
 benchmark model of a point-to-point HVDC transmission line.

In Q2, the team completed the development of the multi-workstation computational platform with all
the necessary hardware (such as workstation, communication cables, network card, etc.) and software
(PSCAD) configured. The impact of communication channel used for interfacing the two workstations
was evaluated in detail including simulation timestep, and the location for dividing a model on the speed
and accuracy of EMT simulations of a PSCAD model. More details of platform implementation and tests
results were included in the Deliverable 4.1 report.

Deliverable 6.1: Report on developed control strategies of the preliminary system. (M12)
An interim report “Report: Multi-Terminal HVDC Multi-Resource Simulation Study Initial Results” for 6.1
was submitted in Q2. The report first described the PSCAD model of a four-terminal HVDC network with
two onshore and two offshore terminals. It identified a set of fault scenarios through a “design of
experiment” and established pass/fail criteria for performance evaluation. A set of AC and DC fault
scenarios were simulated. For the AC fault cases, the system was found stable for all events applied at
terminals 1 and 4; however, when a fault was applied at terminal 3, the reactive power measured at
terminal 3 showed a stable oscillatory response post fault clearing. All faults applied at terminal 2
resulted in an unstable response after fault clearance. In the case of the DC faults, system level voltage
oscillation was observed after fault clearance.

This modeling task was critical to understanding the system dynamics during faults. The objective was to
analyze these results and design system levels controls and protection for stabilizing the network. Due
to the termination of the project, these could not be completed.

CONCLUSIONS
The project ran for two quarters before it was terminated. All the milestones and deliverables during
this period were met. In Q1, Task 2 (Derive Preliminary Functional, Control, Protection and Grid-
Interconnect Requirements) was completed, and a comprehensive report (Multi-Terminal HVDC Model
Requirement) was submitted as a deliverable. Task 3 (Design Preliminary MTDC System and Build
Reduced Order Models) was started ahead of schedule in Q1 and completed in Q2 with the submission
of a detailed report (Multi-Terminal HVDC Multi-Resource Simulation Study Initial Results). NLR led Task
4 on the design of the MULTISIM platform hardware and software. NLR demonstrated a working
prototype of the platform and submitted an interim report (Development of Platform for High-Voltage
DC MULTI-terminal SIMulation). However, the full scope of Task 4 could not be completed due to project
termination. Task 6 (Preliminary Design of MT-HVDC Controls and Protection) was initiated along with
Virginia Tech with significant progress in a short period of time. An interim report augmenting the initial
simulation study report was submitted. However, this task was also not completed due to project
termination.

Modeling, simulation and analysis of multi-terminal HVDC network remains a technology gap in the
industry, primarily because of the lack of a suitable high performance simulation platform. The
MULTISIM platform was being designed to address this challenge. The platform was also being
developed to support interoperability, where the models of the HVDC terminals (substations) can be
created, maintained and owned by different HVDC vendors. Even though a proof-of-concept was
designed and demonstrated by NLR in a short period of time, the platform could not be fully developed
for a full-scale system simulation and validation. If completed, this platform could be a de-facto standard
for the HVDC industry.
