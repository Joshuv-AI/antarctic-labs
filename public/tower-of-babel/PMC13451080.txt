The use of pumpkin seeds as an alternative anthelmintic to reduce parasite burden in naturally infected lambs
Authors: M Tacuri Vera, M Schramm, A E Relling, B A Wenner, B J Campbell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParasite control continues to be an important management consideration for small ruminant producers worldwide because of the increased cost associated with anthelmintic treatment and the growing risk of parasite resistance to commercially available anthelmintics. As an alternative, pumpkin seeds (PS) have demonstrated anthelmintic properties in vitro that may be applied on-farm to control parasitic infection. Therefore, the objective of these experiments was to evaluate lamb growth and monitor parasite burden in lambs offered PS supplemented growing diets by measuring lamb body weight (BW), dry matter intake (DMI), average daily gain (ADG), fecal egg count (FEC), FAMACHA eye score (FAM), and packed cell volume (PCV). We hypothesized that the supplementation of PS would decrease parasite burden in growing lambs. Analysis of data included fixed effects of pen and treatment, random effect of lamb, with repeated effect of week. In Experiment 1, all lambs were fed a common pelleted diet for 28 days. The control group (CON) was supplemented with 0.21 kg as fed/hd/day of whole shell corn while the PS group was supplemented with 0.45 kg as fed/hd/day. These treatments were isocaloric and limit fed. At the conclusion of the supplementation period (d 28), lamb BW was lesser for those lambs offered PS when compared with CON (P < 0.01). In addition, there was evidence of compensatory gain as lamb ADG tended to be greater post-treatment for PS lambs when compared with CON lambs (P = 0.14). Lamb FAM and log10(FEC) did not differ between treatments (P > 0.05). However, lamb PCV was greater (P < 0.01) for PS supplemented lambs when compared with CON lambs during the duration of the supplementation period (Week 1–4). In Experiment 2, lambs were fed a common meal diet with differing concentrations of ground PS (0-g [A], 100-g [B], and 200-g [C], respectively) for 14 days. Lamb BW at the conclusion of the supplementation period (d 14) and lamb ADG had linear effects whereas the concentration of PS in the diet increased, lamb BW decreased (P < 0.01). Additionally, there was no evidence of post-treatment compensatory growth (P < 0.33) and no differences in lamb PCV, log10FEC, and FAM (P > 0.12). Under the conditions of these experiments, animal growth decreased, and parasite burden remained unchanged for lamb offered PS. Additional research may be considered to better understand a preferred method of PS feed delivery that does not negatively impact lamb DMI and growth.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) parasitic infection continues to be an animal health concern in the small ruminant (ie sheep and goat) industry today. Those of most interest in the U.S. are strongyle-type parasites (eg Haemonchus contortus, Trichostrongylus axei, Nematodirus), which are mainly responsible for causing anemia by creating abrasions in the abomasum and GI lining. This damage leads to anemia in addition to weight loss, lethargy, and even death if not treated (VanHoy 2025). The most common method of controlling parasitic infection in small ruminants is the combination of chemical dewormers and pasture management (Höglund and Gustafsson 2023). However, there is growing concern with the continued reliance and use of anthelmintic products as parasite populations are becoming more resilient to previously effective anthelmintic treatments (Ježek et al. 2021). Previous literature has reported resistance to anthelmintic drug families including macrocyclic lactones (ML—Ploeger and Everts 2018; Bosco et al. 2020; Bassetto et al. 2024) in the Netherlands, Italy, and Brazil; benzimidazoles (BZD—Bosco et al. 2020; Voigt et al. 2022) in Germany and Italy; and Nicotinic agonist (IMID—Kotze et al. 2014; Kashyap et al. 2021) in America and Australia; further supporting the need for investigating alternative parasite control strategies.

Plant-based nutraceuticals and feed additives have been previously explored for natural anthelmintic properties. It is known that pumpkin seeds (PS) contain cucurbitacins, tannins, and terpenes (Grzybek et al. 2016) that interfere with parasite biology by increasing the rigidity of the nematode cuticle. In turn, this mode of action prevents the molting of larvae (Greiffer et al. 2022) and promote the generation of reactive oxygen species which signal autophagic cell death (Izumi et al. 2012). Before conducting a feeding experiment, Grzybek et al. (2016) investigated pumpkin composition and isolated berberine and palmitine, alkaloids that hinder parasite growth by inhibiting cell division and replication (Osorio et al. 2008). Formerly, the use of PS to reduce parasitic burden has been limited to in vitro and in vivo experiments rodent (Grzybek et al. 2016) models; with limited experiments conducted using ruminant livestock species. However, the challenge with applying in vivo experiments to real world feeding management strategies is understanding the proper concentration and inclusion rates of PS in a livestock diet. For example, Matthews et al. (2016) demonstrated that increasing concentrations of PS oil in the diets of growing lambs and kids reduced dry matter intake.

Application of these concepts to a small ruminant feeding operation would be much more challenging in both practice and economics. However, the broad availability of pumpkins for byproduct feeding to small ruminants makes PS delivery possible. Therefore, the focus of these two feeding experiments was to investigate the effects of PS supplementation and concentration for use as a control of parasitic infection in growing lambs. Therefore, we hypothesized that PS may be used as a natural anthelmintic to decrease parasitic burden within naturally infected lambs without negatively affecting dry matter intake and resulting average daily gain. Our aim was to evaluate the effects of the PS supplement on parasite resilience and growth in young, growing lambs by monitoring body weight, fecal egg count, packed cell volume, and FAMACHA eye score.

Materials and methods

Lambs utilized for this experiment were housed and managed at The Ohio State University’s Small Ruminant Research Center (SRRC) located in Wooster, Ohio, USA. Procedures were done in accordance with The Ohio State University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) guidelines (Animal Use Protocol: #2023A00000082).

Experiment 1

Animals and housing

In Experiment 1, the experimental period took place from November 13, 2023, to January 8, 2024, which encompassed a 28-day feeding period and a 28-day post-treatment observation period. Lambs (n = 20; 8-month-old females; Hampshire × Dorset) used for this experiment were allocated to their respective treatment groups by balancing individual lamb fecal egg count (FEC) and body weight (BW). The power of this experiment was estimated based on a previous undergraduate pilot project at the University of Maine where a 400 egg/mL difference was observed in the lambs offered whole pumpkins over a six week period. Blocking was completed one week prior to the beginning of the feeding experiment with a total of 32 lambs being evaluated, and among this group 20 were selected and blocked according to initial BW and FEC. The selected lambs were randomly allotted to 10 indoor pens (1.5-m in width and 4.9-m in length) with two lambs per pen and five replicates per treatment.

Treatments

The diets were formulated using the small ruminant module of the Cornell Net Carbohydrate and Protein System software (CNCPS v6.55, Agricultural Modeling and Training Systems, Groton, NY, USA). Two dietary treatments were limit fed; a control diet (CON) and a treatment diet that offered pumpkin seeds (PS), with both diets containing 0.91 kg/hd/day of a basal pelleted ration (Table 1). The CON diet supplemented the basal diet with 0.20 kg/hd/d (as fed) of whole shelled corn (WSC); whereas the PS diet supplemented 0.45 kg/hd/d (as fed) of whole pumpkin seeds (Table 2). Initial dosage of PS was estimated based on pilot studies (personal communication), average seeds/pumpkin of ∼0.45 kg/pumpkin in a small subsample population (data not shown), and practicality of substitution on farm between WSC and PS. The differing inclusion rates of WSC and PS ensured that treatments were isocaloric. The nutrient values used for WSC were library values from the CNCPS 6.55 database (88% DM, and on a DM basis: 9% CP, 75% starch, 4% EE, and 9% NDF). whereas the nutrient values for PS were generated using pooled wet chemistry (Cumberland Valley Analytical Services, Waynesboro, PA) analyses provided in Table 2.

At feeding, both the WSC and PS were provided as a top-dress over the basal pelleted ration. Prior to feeding, small perforations were made at the bottom of each bag of PS to drain excess fluid and minimize saturation to the pelleted feed (see pumpkin seed collection below). From d9 onward 0.45 kg of hay was additionally offered daily to prevent ruminal acidosis in accordance with IACUC protocols. Water was offered ad libitum. Feed refusals were weighed, recorded daily, and retained in plastic freezer bags to quantify dry matter intake (DMI). Furthermore, each CON treatment group was pair fed with a corresponding PS treatment group within block. Therefore, if one pen experienced three consecutive days of feed refusals, the amount of basal pelleted feed offered decreased by 0.11 kg/pen across both treatments. Inversely, if both pens in the pair experienced three consecutive days of no feed refusals, the basal pelleted feed was increased by 0.11-kg/pen.

Pumpkin seed collection

Pumpkin seeds were collected directly from approximately 600 pumpkins (Cucurbita pepo) growing at the Western Agriculture Research Station (WARS; South Charleston, Ohio, USA) and at the Waterman Agricultural and Natural Resources Laboratory (WANRL; Columbus, Ohio, USA) on four harvest dates in 2023: October 09, October 31, November 11, and November 14. Pumpkins were dissected into halves and seeds were collected. into a 64.35-L plastic tub, transported to the lab, and then separated into 0.45-kg samples in plastic freezer bags (Ziploc sandwich bags; S. C. Johnson & Son, Inc., Bay City, MI, USA) and froze at −20°C until feeding (one bag/lamb/d). Of note, PS were not washed; therefore, residual pulp containing carotenoids and carbohydrates coating the seeds were also offered to PS treatment lambs during feeding. From this initial sampling period, two subsamples of PS were immediately submitted for nutrient analysis (Cumberland Valley Analytical Services; Waynesboro, PA, USA) from each harvest location (WARS and WANRL) for dry matter (DM), crude protein (CP), neutral-detergent fiber (NDF), acid-detergent fiber (ADF), lignin, ash, ether extract (EE), and mineral content (Table 2).

Lamb growth

Lamb body weight (BW) was collected every seven days prior to feeding utilizing a digital scale (Avery Weigh-Tronix ZM301, Fairmont, MN, USA). Lamb average daily gain (ADG) was calculated by dividing the weight gained each week by the weight collection interval (7 d). Lamb post-treatment ADG was determined by dividing the weight gained in the period after treatment concluded, divided by the number of days (28 d). Gain to feed ratio (G:F) was determined by dividing individual weight gain by DMI.

Lamb health measurements

Lamb health parameters were collected and analyzed each week of the 28-day feeding period and consisted of FAMACHA eye score (FAM), fecal egg count (FEC), and packed cell volume (PCV). According to the Standard Operating Procedures (SOP) of the SRRC, sheep and lambs with a PCV of 21% or less are considered for anthelmintic treatment after a fecal sample has been evaluated to support the presence of a parasitic challenge. Lamb FEC were performed to quantify the number of parasitic eggs shed per individual and were reported as eggs/gram. Each parameter was analyzed on the day of collection utilizing the same techniques as described by Campbell et al. (2017).

Experiment 2

Animals and housing

In Experiment 2, data were collected from July 8, 2024, to August 22, 2024. Experiment 2 was comprised of a 14-day feeding period and a 28-day post-treatment observation period. Weaned lambs (n = 30; 3-month-old ewes and wethers; Hampshire × Dorset) used for this experiment were allocated to their respective treatment groups by blocking lamb FEC, BW, and sex. Blocking was completed seven days prior to the beginning of the experiment with a total of 36 lambs being weighed and fecal sampled, and among this group 30 were selected. The 30 lambs were randomly assigned within block to15 indoor pens (1.5-m in width and 4.9-m in length) with 2 lambs per pen and 5 replicates per treatment.

Treatments

Three dietary treatments were a ground meal ration (Table 3) with differing concentrations of PS at a concentration of 0 g (A), 100-g (B), and 200-g (C)/hd./d. The PS (Natural Foods Inc., Freeport, NY, USA) used in this experiment were purchased whole and ground using a 2 m screen (Wiley Mill, Thomas Scientific, Swedesboro, NJ, USA) to be incorporated into the ground meal ration to reduce sorting behavior. Feed that was offered daily aimed to achieve an intake of 1.36-kg/hd./d. Refusals were weighed and recorded daily. To achieve the target dose of PS per day the animals in treatment B received 0.340-kg/hd./d of the diet containing PS (Table 2) and the rest was the basal diet (Table 2), and the animals in treatment C received 0.680-kg/hd./d of the diet containing PS and the rest was the basal diet. Feeding procedures followed slick bunk management. In the event of feed refusal, if a pen yielded refusals weighing at least 0.45-kg, the basal ground meal diet was decreased by 0.11-kg; meanwhile the concentration of ground PS remained consistent. If a pen had three consecutive days of no feed refusals, the basal ground meal diet increased by 0.11-kg. Samples of both the basal ground meal diet and PS meal were collected weekly and sent for nutrient analysis as shown in Table 3 (Rock River Laboratory, Inc., Wooster, OH, USA). All lambs were offered water ad libitum throughout the duration of this experiment.

Lamb growth

Lamb BW was collected on d 0, 7, and 14. Lamb BW and ADG were collected and reported using the aforementioned metrics as outlined in Experiment 1.

Lamb health measurements

Lamb FAM and PCV were collected on d 0, 7, and 14; whereas lamb FEC were collected twice a week (d 0, 3, 7, 10, and 14). Parameters related to animal health were evaluated as previously outlined in Experiment 1.

Statistical analysis

In Experiment 1, data were analyzed using PROC MIXED (SAS v9.4, Cary, NC, USA) with the fixed effects of treatment, time, and their interaction, and the random effect of block and pen within block. Lamb FEC was analyzed on a logarithmic scale of log10 to normalize the distribution of data. Repeated measures were used considering the auto regressive covariance structure.

In Experiment 2, lamb data were analyzed using PROC MIXED with the fixed effects of treatment, day, and their interaction, and the random effect of block and pen within block. Lamb FEC was analyzed on a logarithmic scale of log10. Lamb ADG and G:F were analyzed with the fixed effect of treatment and random effect of pen. For those variables with no time × treatment interaction (no time effect or the interaction P > 0.15) a polynomial orthogonal contrast was used to separate means in a linear or quadratic effect.

When there was a treatment × time interaction (P < 0.05), means were separated at each time point using the SLICE statement in SAS. Data are presented as least squares means (LSMEANS) ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Treatment by interaction differences were determined using the SLICE and PDIFF procedures in SAS, with differences declared at P ≤ 0.05 and tendencies considered when 0.05 < P ≤ 0.15.

Results

Experiment 1

Data discussing lamb growth in Experiment 1 are shown in Table 4. At the initiation of the feeding period, there were no differences in lamb BW (P > 0.05); however, there was a difference observed at the conclusion of the feeding period whereas PS lambs had a lesser BW when compared with CON lambs (P < 0.01). Furthermore, at the conclusion of the 28-d observation period post treatment, lamb BW did not differ (P = 0.26). There was a treatment × time interaction for DMI (P < 0.01); whereas CON lambs consumed less feed when compared with PS lambs during Week 1, with no differences between treatments in terms of DMI for Weeks 2 and 3, and CON out consumed PS lambs in Week 4. Additionally, PS lambs lost weight during the 28-d treatment period whereas CON lambs gained weight (P < 0.01). When evaluating post-treatment ADG, there was a trend for PS lambs to gain a greater amount of weight when compared with CON lambs (P = 0.14). Furthermore, there was a difference in gain to feed ratio (G:F; P = 0.01), where CON lambs were more efficient when compared with PS lambs.

Discussion

Data supporting the use and efficacy of natural or non-chemical anthelmintics in the livestock feed industry are limited. In this experiment, the use of pumpkin seeds (PS) was evaluated for their use in mitigating parasitic infection in naturally infected, growing lambs. It was hypothesized that PS may be used as a natural anthelmintic to decrease parasite burden within naturally infected lambs without negatively affecting feed intake. Within the context of these experiments, lambs offered PS exhibited decreases in BW and DMI, whereas parasite burden as evaluated by lamb health parameters showed no differences between control and PS treatments.

Lamb growth performance and feed intake

Within both experiments reported, lambs experienced decreased growth, as noted by lamb BW at the conclusion of each feeding period and ADG, which is supported by decreased DMI. Feed refusals were greater for lambs offered PS when compared with CON lambs. This resulted in decreased BW and ADG for PS lambs in Experiment 1. This pattern was also observed in Experiment 2 with lamb ADG decreasing as PS inclusion increased in the diet. These results are consistent with the work of Matthews et al. (2016) who reported that goat kids offered ground PS (5-g PS/kg BW) had greater feed refusals than goat kids that were not offered PS. Moreover, Matthews et al. (2016) conducted three additional experiments that offered PS as (i) an oral drench (5-g PS/kg BW) every other day; (ii) PS oil (2.0-mL/kg BW) either once a week; or (iii) PS oil (2.0-mL/kg BW) three times a week to Boer crossbred goat kids and Katahdin lambs, all of which resulted in decreased DMI when compared with controls. Furthermore, tannins, which are naturally present in PS (Grzybek et al. 2016), may also negatively contribute to a reduction in lamb DMI. For example, Biz et al. (2023) offered lambs a quebracho tree extract that is known to contain increased concentrations of tannins. These authors offered lambs diets with increasing concentrations of quebracho tree extract and observed a reduction in DMI. This reduction in DMI may suggest that tannins and other chemical compounds that are naturally present in PS could contribute to a reduction in lamb DMI.

While the current experiments did not measure the concentrations of cucurbitacin, tannins, and other chemical compounds in the offered PS, these compounds could decrease intake by negatively impacting palatability or via anti-nutritional effects. Grzybek et al. (2016) identified saturated and unsaturated fatty acids of palmitic, palmitoleic, stearic, oleic, and linolenic acids in PS. The denaturation of unsaturated fatty acids may induce bitterness caused by oxidation (Usuki and Kaneda 1980). According to Goatcher and Church (1970), sheep are the most responsive to bitterness compared to the other flavors of sour, salty, sweet, and umami. Within the context of Experiment 1, this feeding period occurred after the pumpkin season, so to aid in the preservation of harvested seeds they were stored in a −20°C chest freezer. The seeds began thawing two days prior to feeding. Because of the abundance of unsaturated fatty acids coupled with the manner in which PS were collected, stored, and subsequently fed, PS in Experiment 1 may have developed a bitter flavor, thus contributing to a reduction in DMI and consequent lamb growth. To avoid these aforementioned challenges in Experiment 2, PS were ground and offered in meal form. However, regardless of the method of feed delivery, lamb DMI still decreased. Additionally, it’s valuable to consider the limitations of availability and preservation challenges. Ensuring consistent supply and maintaining the nutritional availability of PS through preservation strategies is essential for the practical use of PS as a feed ingredient.

Lamb health

The FAM system is commonly used as a quick field indicator to assess parasitic infection, but anemia can also be further assessed using PCV as it quantifies the concentration of red blood cells within a blood sample (Kendall 2024). In Experiment 1, lambs offered PS had a greater PCV when compared with CON lambs. This difference in PCV may be attributed to an increased dietary CP availability in PS when compared with WSC. Previous literature (Felix et al. 2012; Campbell et al. 2021) has also reported that an increase in dietary CP has shown to improve lamb PCV and FAM. Because the parasites of interest in the current experiments are hematin (ie blood-consuming) parasites, a loss of blood would result in a reduction in protein availability. Therefore, resupplying the system with supplemental protein may lessen or negate the effect of blood loss as a result of parasitic infection.

Overall, there were no differences in lamb FEC between treatments for both Experiments 1 and 2. Of note, the frequency of FEC collection increased in Experiment 2 when compared with Experiment 1 (2 times per week vs. 1 time per week). It is known that coprological samples can vary daily because of stool consistency, differences in feed digestibility, and nematode maturity (Shulaw 2011). However, under the circumstances of the current experiments, lamb FEC did not differ based upon collection frequency. In terms of practical application, surveyed farmers shared their wariness of coprological analysis as the cost of fecal testing may outweigh the benefits of incorporating the tests into their respective operations (Sautier and Chiron 2023). Therefore, additional or more periodic FEC collections to better estimate fecal egg output may not be necessary or realistic. Furthermore, it was observed that in both Experiments 1 and 2 that there was evidence of proglottid shedding that was only present in those treatment groups offered PS. However, Moniezia expansa (tapeworm) eggs were not quantified; therefore, the effectiveness of PS as a natural treatment for the control for this species of parasite cannot be discussed, thus highlighting the need for additional research focusing on additional parasite species.

Conclusion

Within the constraints of the current experiments, the practical use of PS in lamb diets decreased growth by decreasing DMI and was not effective as a natural dewormer as shown by negligible differences in lamb PCV and no differences in lamb FEC and FAM. Of note, one limitation of the current experiments is the lack of bioactive-compound quantification of PS used and how these compounds may have been impacted by ruminal digestion. The lack of antiparasitic activity noted in these experiments garners additional insight using a ruminant model. Although tapeworm eggs were not quantified, the observation of proglottids underscores the need for specific quantifications of parasite populations in future work when considering PS as natural anthelmintic.
