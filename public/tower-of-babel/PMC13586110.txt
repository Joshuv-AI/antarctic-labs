OsWRKY11 Regulates Rice Leaf Angle Via Transcriptional Activation of OsGSR1
Authors: Lian Wu, Xiuqing Gao, Dekai Wang, Shuhui Sun, Jing Li, Yuxin Cao, Jinfang Chu, Yu Li, Ran Xu, Yunhai Li, Na Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeaf angle is a key agronomic trait that determines plant architecture and grain yield in rice. Although brassinosteroids (BRs) are known to play a central role in regulating leaf angle, the upstream transcriptional regulatory network remains elusive. Here, we characterized the lla (large leaf angle) mutant which carried a T-DNA insertion in the promoter of OsWRKY11 (a WRKY transcription factor) and exhibits significantly enlarged leaf angles at both seedling and mature stages. We demonstrated that the large leaf angle phenotype of lla was caused by the T-DNA insertion in the promoter of OsWRKY11, leading to its pronounced upregulation. Overexpression of OsWRKY11 recapitulated the lla phenotype, whereas knockdown of OsWRKY11 in the lla background restored leaf angle to wild-type levels, establishing OsWRKY11 as a positive regulator of this trait. Interestingly, the lla mutants are hypersensitive to exogenous BR treatment and accumulate higher levels of the bioactive BR castasterone. Further analyses showed that OsWRKY11 directly binds to W-box elements in the promoter of OsGSR1(a GAST family protein), a known positive regulator of BR biosynthesis, and activates its transcription. Genetic analysis revealed that OsWRKY11 acts upstream of OsGSR1 in the same genetic pathway to regulate leaf angle. Together, our findings uncover an OsWRKY11-OsGSR1 module that fine-tunes leaf angle by modulating BR homeostasis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12284-026-00929-1.Keywords: Rice, Leaf angle, Brassinosteroid signaling, WRKY transcription factor, OsGSR1

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa) plant architecture is a critical determinant of grain yield, particularly under high-density planting conditions (Khush 2013). Among architectural traits, leaf angle, the inclination of the leaf blade from the stem, directly influences light penetration into the canopy and overall photosynthetic efficiency (Faralli and Lawson 2020; Cao et al. 2022). Erect leaves facilitate deeper light distribution, enhancing canopy photosynthesis and enabling higher planting densities, a key strategy for yield improvement in modern rice breeding (Sakamoto et al. 2005; Tian et al. 2024) Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms that govern leaf angle establishment is of fundamental importance for designing high-yielding rice varieties with optimized canopy architecture.

Leaf angle is primarily determined by asymmetric cell elongation and division at the lamina joint, a process tightly regulated by phytohormones, particularly brassinosteroids (BRs) (Sun et al. 2015; Luo et al. 2016). Exogenous BR application promotes lamina joint bending, and mutants with impaired BR biosynthesis or signaling typically exhibit erect leaves (Bittner et al. 2015; Kim et al. 2023). Key BR biosynthetic genes, such as OsDWARF4 (a cytochrome P450 gene involved in BR biosynthesis), OsDWARF11 (a BR biosynthetic cytochrome P450), and OsBRD1 (BRASSINOSTEROID DEFICIENT DWARF 1), have been extensively characterized, and their loss-of-function mutants display reduced leaf angles and more compact plant architecture (Mori et al. 2002; Hong et al. 2003; Tanabe et al. 2005; Li et al. 2013; Guo et al. 2021). Despite extensive knowledge of the BR biosynthetic pathway, the upstream transcriptional regulators that directly control these BR biosynthetic genes remain largely unidentified.

Transcription factors from various families have been implicated in BR-mediated leaf angle regulation. The HLH/bHLH family, for instance, forms a complex antagonistic network that fine-tunes BR signaling output (Zhang et al. 2009; Lu, M.M. et al. 2025). More recently, WRKY transcription factors have emerged as important nodes integrating BR signals with other developmental and environmental cues. OsWRKY72 directly activates the BR receptor gene OsBRI1 (BRASSINOSTEROID INSENSITIVE 1), and its activity is enhanced by OsMAPK6 (MITOGEN-ACTIVATED PROTEIN KINASE 6)-mediated phosphorylation, thereby promoting leaf inclination (Wang, F.X. et al. 2025). Similarly, OsWRKY53 functions downstream of a MAPK cascade and interacts with OsBZR1 (BRASSINAZOLE-RESISTANT 1) to regulate BR-responsive genes (Tian et al. 2021). Notably, these and other previously characterized WRKY factors primarily target components of BR signaling or response, rather than the biosynthetic machinery itself. Whether any WRKY transcription factor directly regulates BR biosynthesis to modulate endogenous BR homeostasis has remained an open question.

We previously identified the large leaf angle (lla) mutant which carried a T-DNA insertion in the promoter region of OsWRKY11 (Wang et al. 2005). Here, we report the characterization of lla and prove that the enlarged leaf angles of lla was due to overexpression of OsWRKY11. Through physiological, transcriptomic, and genetic approaches, we demonstrate that OsWRKY11 functions as a positive regulator of leaf angle. We show that OsWRKY11 directly binds to the promoter of OsGSR1, a known positive regulator of BR biosynthesis (Wang et al. 2009), and activates its transcription. Genetic epistasis analysis confirms that OsGSR1 acts downstream of OsWRKY11 in the same genetic pathway. Our study uncovers a novel OsWRKY11-OsGSR1 transcriptional module that directly links a WRKY transcription factor to the control of BR biosynthesis. This work provides new insights into the hormonal and transcriptional coordination of rice plant architecture and offers a potential target for breeding rice varieties with ideal plant architecture.

Results

OsWRKY11 Positively Regulates Leaf Angle in Rice

The lla mutant was isolated from a rice T-DNA insertion mutant library (Wang et al. 2005). Phenotypic analysis showed that the leaf angle of lla plants was significantly larger than that of the wild type (WT) at both the seedling and mature stages (Fig. 1A-E). The lamina joint region of lla exhibited pronounced elongation compared to WT (Fig. 1F). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of the lamina joint further revealed a marked increase in the length of adaxial cells in lla, together with a slight increase in cell width (Fig. 2). Quantitative analysis showed that the average adaxial cell length was 20.0 ± 1.2 μm in WT and 32.0 ± 1.5 μm in lla, representing a 1.6‑fold increase (Fig. 2E).

Our previous study indicated that the T-DNA was inserted in the promoter region of OsWRKY11 (Wang et al. 2005). Here, we confirmed that the insertion site is located 66 bp upstream of the OsWRKY11 start codon (Fig. 3A). qRT-PCR showed that OsWRKY11 expression was strongly upregulated in lla (Fig. 3B). To test whether elevated OsWRKY11 expression is responsible for the enlarged leaf angle, we generated transgenic rice plants overexpressing OsWRKY11 under the control of the CaMV 35 S promoter. Three independent overexpression lines with the highest transcript levels all displayed significantly increased leaf angles (Fig. 3C-E). Conversely, when OsWRKY11 expression was knocked down in lla via RNA interference (RNAi), the leaf angle was substantially reduced (Fig. 3F-H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that OsWRKY11 functions as a positive regulator of leaf angle in rice.

OsWRKY11 is Involved in Brassinosteroid-Mediated Leaf Angle Regulation

Brassinosteroids (BRs) play a crucial role in regulating leaf inclination in rice. To investigate whether OsWRKY11 is involved in BR signaling, we treated the lamina joints of wild-type (WT) and lla seedlings with various concentrations of 2,4-epibrassinolide (2,4-epiBL) and measured the resulting leaf angles (Fig. 4A, B). Following 2,4-epiBL treatment, the lla mutant exhibited significantly enlarged leaf angles compared to the WT (Fig. 4A, B).

The lla lamina joint showed hyper-sensitive to BL treatment. A Lamina joint bending response to 24-epiBL. B The second leaf angle after treatment with 24-epiBL. The data were presented as means ± SE (n ≥ 20). C Endogenous castasterone content in the lamina joint of WT and lla seedlings. Data are presented as means ± SD (n = 3)

To determine whether the lla mutation affects endogenous BR levels, we quantified BR content in WT and lla seedlings. Although brassinolide (BL) was not detected in our samples, the level of castasterone (CS), one of the most active forms of BR, was significantly higher in the lla mutant than in the WT (Fig. 4C). These findings suggest that OsWRKY11 may regulate rice leaf angle by modulating endogenous BR biosynthesis or accumulation.

Identification of Downstream Genes Regulated by OsWRKY11

To elucidate the transcriptional network through which OsWRKY11 regulates leaf angle, we performed transcriptomic analysis using lamina joint tissues from wild-type (WT) and lla seedlings. RNA-seq identified 1,339 upregulated and 666 downregulated genes in the lla mutant compared to WT (Fig. 5A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed that the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were primarily associated with phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, plant hormone signal transduction, and plant–pathogen interaction pathways (Fig. 5B).

Among the DEGs, four genes implicated in BR signaling or biosynthesis were of particular interest: OsDWARF4, OsXTR1, OsGA20ox2, and OsGSR1 (Fig. 5C). To validate the RNA-seq findings, we conducted RT-qPCR on WT and lla samples. Consistent with the transcriptomic data, the expression level of OsGSR1 was significantly increased in the lla mutant, whereas the transcript levels of OsDWARF4, OsXTR1, and OsGA20ox2 were reduced (Fig. 5D).

OsWRKY11 Binds to the OsGSR1 Promoter to Activate its Expression

Previous studies have established that WRKY family transcription factors regulate the transcription of their target genes through specific binding to W-box cis-elements present in their promoter regions. Interestingly, we identified two W-box elements in the promoter sequence of OsGSR1 (Fig. 6A). To test whether OsWRKY11 binds to the OsGSR1 promoter, we performed an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA). The results show that MBP-OsWRKY11 binds to a DNA fragment from the OsGSR1 promoter that contains the W-box core sequence. This binding could be effectively competed by the addition of increasing amounts of unlabeled wild-type probe. In contrast, a probe containing a mutated W-box core sequence could not compete with the wild-type probe for binding (Fig. 6B). Together, these results demonstrate that OsWRKY11 specifically binds to the W-box motif in the OsGSR1 promoter in vitro.

To further examine whether OsWRKY11 can transcriptionally activate OsGSR1, we conducted a transient expression assay using the SGR1-based reporter system (SRS) in Nicotiana benthamiana (Zhou et al. 2024). Co-expression of the effector p35S::OsWRKY11 with the reporter pOsGSR1::AtSGR1 resulted in a clear color shift from green to yellow, whereas negative control infiltrations showed no obvious change (Fig. 6C). To quantify this effect, we monitored chlorophyll content using SPAD measurements. The co-expression of OsWRKY11 with pOsGSR1::AtSGR1 led to a significant decrease in SPAD values over time, comparable to the positive control (35 S::AtSGR1), indicating enhanced chlorophyll degradation (Fig. 6D).This visual readout indicates that OsWRKY11 specifically activates the OsGSR1 promoter in planta.

OsWRKY11 Acts Upstream of OsGSR1 to Regulate Leaf Angle

Previous studies established that OsGSR1 positively regulates leaf angle by promoting BR biosynthesis, and OsGSR1-RNAi plants exhibit reduced leaf angles and BR-deficient phenotypes (Wang et al. 2009). To test whether OsWRKY11 functions through OsGSR1, we first generated an osgsr1 mutant via CRISPR-Cas9 (Fig. 7A). This line carries a 1-bp insertion in the second exon of OsGSR1 and, like the RNAi plants, displayed a decreased leaf angle compared to WT. We then crossed osgsr1 with the lla mutant to obtain an lla osgsr1 double mutant. In this genetic background, the enlarged leaf angle of lla was fully suppressed by the osgsr1 mutation (Fig. 7B, C). These results demonstrate that OsWRKY11 promotes leaf angle in a manner dependent on functional OsGSR1, placing OsWRKY11 upstream of OsGSR1 in the regulatory pathway.

To further test whether the enhanced BR sensitivity of lla also requires OsGSR1, we treated WT, lla, osgsr1, and lla osgsr1 with 24-epiBL and examined lamina joint bending (Fig. 7D) as well as leaf angle (Fig. 7E). WT showed a weak BR response (from ~ 18° to ~ 28°), while lla exhibited a dramatically enhanced response (from ~ 26° to ~ 55°). The osgsr1 single mutant displayed a weak BR response comparable to WT, with leaf angles increasing only slightly upon 100 ng/µL treatment. Importantly, the lla osgsr1 double mutant showed a nearly identical response to osgsr1, with no significant differences between these two genotypes at any concentration. In contrast, the lla mutant alone displayed a strongly enhanced BR response. These data further confirm that OsGSR1 is required for the lla-mediated increase in BR sensitivity, supporting that OsGSR1 acts downstream of OsWRKY11.

Discussion

Brassinosteroid (BR) signaling is central to leaf angle regulation in rice, and many genes in BR biosynthesis and signaling have been characterized (Luo et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2025a, b; Chen et al. 2026; Tao et al. 2026). However, the upstream transcriptional regulators of this pathway remain largely unknown. We show that OsWRKY11 directly activates OsGSR1, a key BR biosynthetic gene, and thereby positively regulates leaf angle (Wang et al. 2009). The lla mutant with enhanced OsWRKY11 expression accumulated higher levels of bioactive BR castasterone (Fig. 4C) and showed enhanced sensitivity to exogenous BRs (Fig. 4A, B). Genetic analysis confirmed that OsGSR1 functions downstream of OsWRKY11, as the osgsr1 mutation fully suppressed the lla phenotype (Fig. 7B, C). These findings establish an OsWRKY11-OsGSR1 module that modulates BR homeostasis to control leaf angle in rice.

The identification of OsWRKY11 as a direct transcriptional activator of OsGSR1 reveals a new layer of transcriptional regulation in BR biosynthesis. Key BR biosynthetic genes such as OsDWARF4, OsBRD1, and OsDWARF11 have been extensively characterized (Mori et al. 2002; Hong et al. 2003; Tanabe et al. 2005). Despite extensive characterization of these genes, the transcription factors that directly regulate their expression have remained largely elusive until recently. For instance, Tao et al. showed that OsPIL13, a bHLH transcription factor, directly binds to OsDWARF4 (Tao et al. 2026). Our study now reveals that OsWRKY11, a WRKY family member, targets OsGSR1 (Fig. 6A-C), a BR biosynthetic gene acting at an intermediate step (Wang et al. 2009). These findings suggest that transcription factors from different families target specific nodes of the BR pathway to fine-tune BR levels under diverse conditions (Li et al. 2025).

WRKY transcription factors interface with BR pathways at multiple levels. OsWRKY72 directly activates the BR receptor gene OsBRI1, with its activity enhanced by OsMAPK6-mediated phosphorylation (Wang, F.X. et al., 2025). OsWRKY53 functions downstream of a MAPK cascade and interacts with OsBZR1 to regulate BR-responsive genes (Tian et al. 2021). In contrast to these WRKYs that primarily regulate BR signaling components, OsWRKY11 directly targets the BR biosynthetic gene OsGSR1, highlighting the functional diversification within the WRKY family. In Arabidopsis, WRKY46/54/70 interact with BES1 to regulate BR-responsive genes (Chen et al. 2017), suggesting that the crosstalk between WRKY transcription factors and BR signaling is evolutionarily conserved. OsWRKY72 and OsWRKY53 are phosphorylated by MAPKs, which enhances their transcriptional activity. Whether OsWRKY11 undergoes similar post-translational regulation, and how this might affect its function in BR biosynthesis, remains an open question.

OsGSR1 functions at the intersection of BR and GA signaling pathways (Wang et al. 2009). It promotes BR biosynthesis and positively regulates GA signaling. In OsGSR1-RNAi plants, reduced GA sensitivity and elevated OsGA20ox2 expression were observed (Wang et al. 2009). GA also promotes lamina joint cell elongation (Cao et al. 2022), and BR and GA synergistically regulate cell elongation in rice (Cheng et al. 2022). Given that OsWRKY11 activates OsGSR1 transcription, it may indirectly influence GA responses. Whether this module contributes to BR-GA crosstalk during lamina joint development could be tested in future experiments by assessing GA sensitivity in lla and OsWRKY11 overexpression lines.

From a practical perspective, leaf angle is a key determinant of yield potential in high-density planting (Wang et al. 2020). Sakamoto et al. showed that erect leaves resulting from BR deficiency increase grain yield in rice (Sakamoto et al. 2005). Tian et al. recently demonstrated that optimizing canopy architecture enhances maize yield at high planting densities (Tian et al. 2024). Modulating OsWRKY11 expression could provide a strategy for optimizing rice architecture, although BR manipulation often has pleiotropic effects on grain size and stress responses (Tong and Chu 2018). Fine-tuning OsWRKY11 expression or exploring natural allelic variations may offer practical approaches for breeding.

Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrate that OsWRKY11 positively regulates rice leaf angle by directly activating OsGSR1 transcription, thereby modulating BR homeostasis. The OsWRKY11–OsGSR1 module identified in this study provides new insights into the transcriptional control of BR biosynthesis and offers a potential target for breeding rice varieties with ideal plant architecture.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

The lla mutant was identified from a rice T-DNA insertion mutant population in the japonica cultivar ZH11. This mutant was derived from a T-DNA insertion library constructed using the vector pDsBar1300H, which contains a CaMV 35 S promoter driving the bar gene (Wang et al. 2005). All transgenic plants used in this study were generated via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation in the same ZH11 genetic background. Rice plants were cultivated under natural field conditions at experimental stations in Beijing (116°20′E, 40°22′N) and Lingshui (110°03′E, 18°51′N).

Measurement of Leaf Angle and Cellular Analysis

The lamina joint inclination assay was performed based on established methods with minor modifications (Jang et al. 2017). Surface-sterilized rice seeds were germinated and seedlings were grown in a growth chamber for 8 days under a long-day photoperiod (16 h light/8 h dark) at 28 °C. Uniform seedlings were selected, and a segment approximately 2 cm in length, containing the lamina joint, leaf blade, and leaf sheath from the second leaf, was excised under low-light conditions. The excised segments were floated on distilled water for 10 min and then transferred to Petri dishes containing either 24-epibrassinolide (24-epiBL) solutions at specified concentrations (0, 10, and 100 ng/μL) or a water control. After incubation in the dark at 28–29 °C for 48 h, the segments were photographed. The angle between the leaf blade and sheath was measured from digital images using ImageJ software. Each treatment included at least three independent biological replicates. For cellular analysis, the treated lamina joint samples were processed for scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Cell length and cell number on the adaxial (upper) side of the lamina joint were quantified from the SEM images using ImageJ software. For cell length quantification, one cell per plant was measured, and a total of 30 independent plants were analyzed for each genotype.

RNA Extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from rice tissues using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green chemistry on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Each reaction was performed with three technical replicates, and data were obtained from three independent biological experiments. The OsACTIN1 gene was used as an internal control for normalization. All gene-specific primers used for RT-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Plasmid Construction

For the 35 S:OsWRKY11 overexpression construct, the coding sequence (CDS) of OsWRKY11 was amplified from ZH11 cDNA and recombined into the pMDC32 vector. The OsWRKY11-RNAi construct was generated by amplifying a 300-bp fragment of the OsWRKY11 CDS and recombining it into the pK7GWIWG2II vector. To produce the MBP-OsWRKY11 fusion protein for biochemical assays, the OsWRKY11 CDS was cloned into the pMAL-c2x vector. The CRISPR/Cas9 construct for knocking out OsGSR1 (pC1300-cas9-OsGSR1cr) was generated as previously described (Wang et al. 2015), using the sgRNA target sequence 5′-GGCGGCGGCGGAGGGGTGGC-3′. All primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Hormone Treatment and Leaf angle Measurement

To assess BR sensitivity, hormone treatments were conducted at the early three-leaf stage, when the second leaf was fully expanded. A volume of 1 µL of 24-epibrassinolide (24-epiBL) solution at concentrations of 0 (solvent control), 10, or 100 ng/µL was applied directly to the lamina joint of the second leaf. The hormone was prepared in ethanol and subsequently diluted with water, while control plants received the solvent only. After 72 h, segments containing the treated lamina joint were excised and photographed. The leaf angle, defined as the angle between the leaf blade and sheath, was measured from digital images using ImageJ software. At least three independent biological replicates were analyzed for each genotype and treatment.

Quantification of Endogenous Brassinosteroids

The quantification of endogenous BR was performed based on the method reported previously with some simplifications in sample pretreatment (Xin et al. 2013). Two hundred milligrams of the sample powder was extracted with 90% aqueous methanol (MeOH) in an ultrasonic bath for 1 h. Simultaneously, D₃-CS was added to the extract as an internal standard for BR measurement. After the MCX cartridge was activated and equilibrated with MeOH, water, and 40% MeOH in sequence, the crude extracts reconstituted in 40% MeOH were loaded onto the cartridge. The MCX cartridge was washed with 40% MeOH, and then BRs were eluted with MeOH. After drying with a N₂ stream, the eluent was redissolved with acetonitrile to be derivatized with 2-methoxypyridine-5-boronic acid (MPyBA) prior to UPLC‑MS/MS analysis. Analysis of BRs was performed on a quadrupole linear ion trap hybrid MS (QTRAP 6500, AB SCIEX) equipped with an electrospray ionization source coupled with a UPLC system (Waters) (Xin et al. 2020). The MRM transitions of CS (582.4 > 178.1) and D₃-CS (585.4 > 178.1) were used for quantification.

RNA Sequencing and Transcriptome Analysis

Lamina joint tissues were collected from wild-type (ZH11) and lla mutant seedlings at the three-leaf stage. Total RNA was extracted using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). Each sample consisted of tissues pooled from 30 to 40 seedlings, with three independent biological replicates prepared for library construction. Sequencing was conducted by Novogene (Beijing, China) using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were processed with fastp to remove adapters and low-quality bases. The clean reads were aligned to the rice reference genome (Nipponbare IRGSP-1.0) using HISAT2. Gene expression levels were quantified using the StringTie2 software (version 2.0.4). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2. Genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log₂(fold change)| ≥ 1 were considered significant. Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of the DEGs were performed using the clusterProfiler R package (Lu et al. 2025a, b). The expression levels of selected DEGs were validated by RT-qPCR.

Electrophoresis Mobility Shift Assay

For the EMSA, the full-length coding sequence of OsWRKY11 was cloned into the pMAL-c2x vector to generate an MBP-OsWRKY11 fusion construct. The recombinant protein was expressed in Escherichia coli and purified using amylose resin. Biotin-labeled double-stranded DNA probes encompassing the putative W-box element within the OsGSR1 promoter were synthesized. Unlabeled oligonucleotides of identical (wild-type) or mutated sequence were used as specific competitors in binding reactions. EMSA was performed using the LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Jiang et al. 2019). Protein-DNA complexes were separated on a native polyacrylamide gel, transferred to a nylon membrane, and detected by chemiluminescence. The sequences of all probes and competitors are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

SRS Assay for Protein–DNA Interaction Validation

To verify the interaction between the rice transcription factor OsWRKY11 and the OsGSR1 promoter, we performed an activation assay using the SGR1-based reporter system (SRS) in Nicotiana benthamiana (Zhou et al. 2024), where expression of the Arabidopsis thaliana STAY-GREEN1 (AtSGR1) reporter gene causes chlorophyll degradation and visible leaf yellowing. A 2.0‑kb fragment upstream of the OsGSR1 start codon (containing the two W‑box elements shown in Fig. 6A) was used as the promoter. We constructed a dual-function vector harboring both the OsGSR1 promoter::AtSGR1 reporter cassette and the 35 S::OsWRKY11 effector cassette, along with control vectors including 35 S::AtSGR1 (positive control), OsGSR1::AtSGR1 alone (reporter-only control), and 35 S::OsWRKY11 alone (effector-only control). All vectors were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, and bacterial suspensions (OD₆₀₀ ≈ 0.6) were individually infiltrated into leaves of 4–5-week-old plants. Following infiltration, plants were kept in darkness at 22–24 °C to enhance the phenotype. Specific leaf yellowing, observed only in areas infiltrated with the dual-function vector compared to all controls, indicated that OsWRKY11 activates the OsGSR1 promoter. Each treatment included at least three independent biological replicates.
