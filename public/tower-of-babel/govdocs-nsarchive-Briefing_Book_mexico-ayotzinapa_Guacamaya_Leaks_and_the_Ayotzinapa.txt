<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-right-sidebar field--type-text-long field--label-hidden field__items">
              <div class="field__item"><div style="padding:8px; background-color:#eee">
<h3>Guacamaya Background</h3>

<p>Guacamaya is an anonymous hacking collective that targets extractive industries and government agencies in Latin America. The group published its first data set on March 6, 2022, with the release of a collection on mining operations in Guatemala. In public statements and interviews, Guacamaya advocates for resistance to government repression and environmental devastation. On September 30, 2022, the group released ten terabytes of emails from the security forces of five Latin American countries under a project called “<a href="https://enlacehacktivista.org/index.php?title=Fuerzas_Represivas">Fuerzas Represivas</a>.” In a <a href="https://enlacehacktivista.org/comunicado_guacamaya4.txt">manifesto</a>, the Guacamaya collective accuses military and security forces in the Americas of exercising a “monopoly” on violence “under the pretext of guaranteeing internal order, freedom and well-being.” Guacamaya says the hacked records should be used “to free us from state terrorism.”</p>exercising a “monopoly” on violence “under the pretext of guaranteeing internal order, freedom and well-being.” Guacamaya says the hacked records should be used “to free us from state terrorism.”</p>

<p>The largest data set released from the Fuerzas Represivas leak consists of six terabytes—over four million documents—from the Mexican Defense Ministry (<em>Secretaría de la Defensa Nacional</em> - Sedena). The <a href="https://ddosecrets.com/wiki/Distributed_Denial_of_Secrets">Distributed Denial of Secrets</a> (DDoS) collective, which administers access to the Sedena data set, has designated portions of the data containing personal information as limited distribution and has provided access only to verified journalists, human rights groups, and investigators. The Mexican government’s response to the largest hack in Mexico’s history has been to minimize and even <a href="https://elpais.com/mexico/2022-10-05/la-sedena-rompe-el-silencio-tras-la-filtracion-masiva-de-guacamaya-y-niega-las-acusaciones-de-espionaje-a-periodistas.html">deny</a> some of the information reported from the leak. The U.S. government has also remained tight-lipped in its response to the hack, <a href="https://english.elpais.com/international/2022-10-13/white-house-on-mexicos-defense-ministry-leaks-all-governments-are-vulnerable-to-being-hacked.html">acknowledging</a> that “data leaks are a global problem.”</p>
</div>
</div>
              <div class="field__item"><div style="padding:8px; background-color:#eee">
<h3>A Note from the National Security Archive:</h3>a> that “data leaks are a global problem.”</p>
</div>
</div>
              <div class="field__item"><div style="padding:8px; background-color:#eee">
<h3>A Note from the National Security Archive:</h3>

<p>This posting concerns a human rights case the Archive has been researching since 2015: the forced disappearance of 43 students from the Ayotzinapa rural school on September 26, 2014. After receiving access to the Sedena data set in October 2022, we felt an obligation to review the Guacamaya records for material related to the Ayotzinapa case. The Archive has been monitoring the publication of the Sedena documents by Mexican journalists and investigative outlets. Along with our Mexican counterparts, we are navigating how to share this information publicly while also protecting personal privacy. The Archive is developing collaborations with Mexican institutions for the future publication of the documents found in the Guacamaya data set.</p>

<article about="/briefing-book/mexico-ayotzinapa/2023-03-10/guacamaya-leaks-and-ayotzinapa-case" class="node node--type-briefing-book node--promoted node--view-mode-full">

<div class="node__content clearfix">about="/briefing-book/mexico-ayotzinapa/2023-03-10/guacamaya-leaks-and-ayotzinapa-case" class="node node--type-briefing-book node--promoted node--view-mode-full">

<div class="node__content clearfix">

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-body field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p><strong>Washington, D.C., March 10, 2023</strong> - Years before 43 young men from the Ayotzinapa teachers’ college were attacked and forcibly disappeared in Iguala, Guerrero, the Mexican military had the school under surveillance and considered its students to be subversives, according to internal communications and documents from the Mexican armed forces published today by the National Security Archive.</p>

<p>The military records also reveal that Mexico’s National Defense Ministry worked to shield the institution from civilian scrutiny during the investigation into the disappearance of the 43 students; that military intelligence routinely lumped together dangerous drug traffickers and parents of the missing students in the same reports on “conflict” in Mexico; and that Mexico's Defense Secretary oversaw a propaganda campaign to discredit the parents, their lawyers, and a group of experts assigned to assist in the case by a United Nations commission.</p>

<p>The 20 documents posted by the Archive today are among an estimated four million emails and records that were stolen from the Mexican Defense Ministry (<em>Secretaría de la Defensa Nacional</em>—Sedena) by an anonymous collective of hackers known as “Guacamaya.” Following Guacamaya’s leak of the massive data set in late September 2022, journalists in Mexico and elsewhere have used the six terabytes of information to publish a steady stream of breaking-news stories about diverse topics, including:</p>data set in late September 2022, journalists in Mexico and elsewhere have used the six terabytes of information to publish a steady stream of breaking-news stories about diverse topics, including:</p>

<ul style="margin-left:30px;">

<p>As part of the National Security Archive’s long-term investigative project on the Ayotzinapa case (and <a href="https://revealnews.org/article/after-ayotzinapa/">the podcast we co-produced with Reveal from the Center for Investigative Reporting</a>), the Archive has spent months examining the data set for references to the Ayotzinapa School, the night of the attacks in Iguala, the disappearance of the students, and the investigations that followed. Today's posting is an initial selection of documents from this data set intended to give our readers a sense of what we are learning about the Mexican military’s internal communications related to Ayotzinapa.</p>

<p>The records reveal that the Mexican armed forces were closely monitoring the Ayotzinapa School long before 43 of its students disappeared in 2014. In a 2006 secret intelligence report from the headquarters of the 35th Military Zone (based in Chilpancingo, the state capital of Guerrero), the school is described as a breeding ground for “subversives,” which, “from its beginnings [in 1926] was identified with social groups antagonistic to the government.” The military’s language on Ayotzinapa is the language of counterinsurgency: Due to “the ideological penetration” of foreign ideas, notes the report, “the school has an active and important participation in social conflicts, at the local and national level.” The example given in the report is the armed revolutionary (and Ayotzinapa graduate) Lucio Cabañas, who was assassinated by army forces in 1974 (and who is the subject of a National Security Archive <a href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB105/index.htm">Electronic Briefing Book from 2003</a>)&nbsp;[Document 1].</p>rmy forces in 1974 (and who is the subject of a National Security Archive <a href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB105/index.htm">Electronic Briefing Book from 2003</a>)&nbsp;[Document 1].</p>

<p>No guerrilla leader has emerged from Ayotzinapa in the nearly 50 years since Cabañas’s death, but multiple military records from the Sedena data set continue to associate not only the school but its teachers, students, alumnae, and diverse supporters with what the military calls “the agrarian conflict.” Networks of individuals and institutions believed “connected” to the school and its mission appear in military intelligence documents, such as a 2007 report showing a “diagram of Ayotzinapa links.”&nbsp;The report identifies a variety of armed groups, well-known journalists, <em>campesino</em> organizations, local politicians, and members of one of the major political parties, the center-left <em>Partido de la Revolución Democrática </em>(PRD) – including future president Andrés Manuel López Obrador – as Ayotzinapa supporters&nbsp;[Document 2].</p>

<p>Other records, such as the “Itzamna” report's annex on the “Criminal and Social Agenda” in Iguala&nbsp;from April 2020, conflate the “problematics” of organized crime (in this case, <em>Guerreros Unidos</em> – the criminal gang involved in the kidnapping of the Ayotzinapa students) and social movements (in this case, the students themselves and their parents and advisors) in ways that blur the difference between them. Both are perceived as a field of conflict for the military – though criminals are categorized as a “risk” and activists a “challenge” – and both are analyzed and visualized in exactly the same ways: through maps, timelines, links to other groups, and individual profiles of leaders. And the military’s conclusions about both are very similar:</p>

<p><a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/special-exhibit/ayotzinapa-investigations">The Ayotzinapa case began on September 26, 2014</a>, when a group of students from the school were attacked by security forces and members of a criminal gang as they traveled in buses through the town of Iguala. A night of violence and gunfire left three students and three bystanders dead and dozens of people wounded; 43 students were taken away by police and never seen again. The parents of the missing boys mobilized, calling on then-President Enrique Peña Nieto to find their sons and demanding the government investigate those responsible.</p>police and never seen again. <p>From the beginning, <a href="https://www.eleconomista.com.mx/politica/Informes-del-ataque-sembraron-el-caos-en-el-cuartel-20150930-0109.html">questions were raised as to the actions of the Mexican military</a> during the attacks. Iguala hosts a major army installation – the headquarters of the 27th Military Infantry Battalion – and soldiers were seen circulating throughout the various crime scenes as the violence unfolded for hours, from the evening of September 26 into the early morning of September 27. When international investigators loaned by the Inter-American Commission for Human Rights to assist in the case (known as the group of experts, <em>Grupo Interdisciplinario de Expertos Independientes</em>—GIEI) asked to interview soldiers from the battalion, then-Secretary of Defense Salvador Cienfuegos Zepeda refused, <a href="https://youtu.be/FbKTeQTpPNg">telling the press:</a> “I can’t permit my soldiers to be treated like criminals or to be interrogated so that they are later made to feel that they had something to do with [the night of Iguala] instead of being supported.”</p>an’t permit my soldiers to be treated like criminals or to be interrogated so that they are later made to feel that they had something to do with [the night of Iguala] instead of being supported.”</p>

<p>Emails and internal memos found in the Sedena data set make clear why the experts and other investigators wanted to question the military. Four documents in this posting&nbsp;are a sampling of the communications that passed among members of the 27th Infantry Battalion and between the battalion and the 35th Military Zone in Chilpancingo during and immediately after the attacks [Documents 4-7]. As the <em>Parte de Novedades</em> (“Report of latest developments”)<strong>&nbsp;</strong>from that night indicates, there was constant movement on and off base by soldiers of the 27th battalion, including by those who would later become key suspects in the investigation. Yet testimonies from surviving students and eyewitnesses agreed that the military never intervened to stop the violence or rescue the students.<a href="#_edn1" name="_ednref1"><sup>[1]</sup></a></p>. Yet testimonies from surviving students and eyewitnesses agreed that the military never intervened to stop the violence or rescue the students.<a href="#_edn1" name="_ednref1"><sup>[1]</sup></a></p>

<p>Captain José Martínez Crespo, for example, a unit leader at the time, was patrolling Iguala from 12:30 to 5:55am, hours during which a second violent attack took place in the center of town, leaving the bodies of two students lying in the streets [Document 7]. According to a message written by battalion commander Col. José Rodríguez Pérez, at one point, Capt. Martínez Crespo and his men entered a medical clinic called Cristina to locate a group of students – one of whom was badly wounded – and later reported that the boys “expressed their gratitude to [him] for the support rendered, saying they did not wish to remain in that place anymore; that they would leave, and they would give their wounded companion the medical attention he needed themselves” [Document 5].<strong>&nbsp;</strong>Interviews with students who survived that night tell a very different story, with Martínez Crespo berating the boys and refusing to help them get medical attention.<a href="#_edn2" name="_ednref2"><sup>[2]</sup></a></p>ents who survived that night tell a very different story, with Martínez Crespo berating the boys and refusing to help them get medical attention.<a href="#_edn2" name="_ednref2"><sup>[2]</sup></a></p>

<p><a href="https://lopezdoriga.com/nacional/jose-martinez-crespo-el-primer-militar-detenido-por-caso-ayotzinapa/">Martínez Crespo was arrested in November 2020</a> on suspicion of involvement with <em>Guerreros Unidos</em>, the criminal group that participated in the attack on the boys and that may have killed them. The retired officer is now being held in a federal prison awaiting trial. Although the current Defense Minister, Gen. Luis Cresencio Sandoval, has publicly cooperated with the arrest of Martínez Crespo [Document 18], he has also communicated privately and directly with President López Obrador<strong>&nbsp;</strong>about the difficulties the retired officer is experiencing in prison - describing him as “depressed” but “firm in his determination that his innocence must be recognized”&nbsp;[Document 19].</p>

<p>As soon as the group of international experts arrived in Mexico in early 2015, they began to find <a href="https://b10466f9-f2d6-4eb5-84f2-5dd2f725233d.filesusr.com/ugd/3a9f6f_e1df5a84680a4a8a969bd45453da1e31.pdf">major inconsistencies in the government’s version of the crimes</a> – a version that came to be known as the “historical truth” (<em>la verdad histórica</em>). Attorney General Jesús Murillo Karam and his chief investigator Tomás Zerón described coordinated actions between corrupt local police and criminals to kidnap the students, bring them to a garbage dump in the nearby municipality of Cocula, kill them, and burn their bodies overnight into ash and tiny fragments of bone. The GIEI’s findings challenged almost every aspect of the official story – including the government’s failure to investigate the role of the armed forces, a demand the parents had made immediately after their sons went missing.</p>almost every aspect of the official story – including the government’s failure to investigate the role of the armed forces, a demand the parents had made immediately after their sons went missing.</p>

<p>Records in the leaked Sedena data set reveal the extent to which the Defense Ministry sought to circle the wagons and fend off any attempt at turning the inquiry on the military itself. When the parents and their lawyers requested access to the Iguala headquarters of the 27th battalion in early 2015, a senior advisor to Defense Secretary Cienfuegos counseled that if there were jail cells there, “it’s necessary to remove them and take them off base” before any outsiders visit; furthermore, if asked, the commander should not permit any kind of excavation or removal of flooring&nbsp;[Document 8]. Shortly after the GIEI released their first damaging report in September 2015, that same advisor – Humberto Guillermo Aguilar, a retired three-star general – suggested that the parents and their supporters were trying to reopen the investigation as a way of “buying time until 2018, so that the Federal Government will be facing a problem it has been unable to resolve, and in that manner they seek to discredit the PRI-Government in the presidential elections” [Document 9].</p>

<p>Although these internal memos repeatedly declare the military’s commitment to supporting the investigation, they also express fury and disbelief that five foreigners could be allowed to intervene in a case as sensitive as Ayotzinapa. Military and civilian advisors call the members of the GIEI “supposed experts”; their work is characterized as “foreign meddling” [Document 14]; and they frame the GIEI’s efforts to communicate their findings outside Mexico as “illegal” and “unjustified” [Document 12].</p>erts”; their work is characterized as “foreign meddling” [Document 14]; and they frame the GIEI’s efforts to communicate their findings outside Mexico as “illegal” and “unjustified” [Document 12].</p>

<p>According to the documents, the military used a covert strategy to undermine the GIEI and any others who questioned the “historical truth.” The Sedena data set contains evidence that the highest levels of the Defense Ministry ordered and oversaw a propaganda campaign targeting not only the group of experts, but the parents of the disappeared 43 students and their lawyers. In a memorandum to Cienfuegos written in 2016, Juan Ibarrola Carreón – a columnist with <em>Milenio</em> and <a href="https://contralinea.com.mx/interno/semana/juan-ibarrola-columnista-de-milenio-en-la-nomina-de-la-sedena/">paid consultant to the Mexican military</a> – repeats accusations published in several Mexican news outlets about two members of the GIEI. The articles claim that Claudia Paz y Paz – “when she was a judge in Guatemala” [she was attorney general] – had deep ties to leftist guerrilla organizations in Central America, and Ángela Buitrago – a former federal prosecutor from Colombia – fabricated evidence to put a high-ranking Colombian military officer in prison.</p>

<p>Paz y Paz and Buitrago, Ibarrola reminds Cienfuegos, “are the ones insisting on interrogating the military for the Iguala case.” The <em>Milenio</em> columnist suggests that Sedena ask the Colombian defense attaché in Mexico for more information about Buitrago, “so that at the right time it can be fully documented and disseminated in a strategic manner within Mexico” [Document 11].</p>Colombian defense attaché in Mexico for more information about Buitrago, “so that at the right time it can be fully documented and disseminated in a strategic manner within Mexico” [Document 11].</p>

<p>Following publication of the second and final report of the GIEI – which found the military was withholding information about what happened in Iguala – the Intelligence Directorate of the National Defense General Staff concluded that the Defense Ministry needed to influence public opinion more aggressively. The unidentified intelligence officer recommended that Sedena: “leak a narrative to friendly opinion leaders that confirms the ministry’s position”; continue an “exhaustive monitoring” of media for negative articles; “interact with simulated users” on social media sites; carry out “propaganda campaigns against the members of GIEI”; and spread messages against the leaders of the parents of the 43 disappeared boys&nbsp;[Document 13].</p>

<p>In 2018, President López Obrador reopened the Ayotzinapa case and ordered his government agencies to cooperate with the new investigation. In particular, the president required the Defense Ministry to respond to requests for information from the reconstituted group of international experts. The GIEI has since had access to some military installations, such as the 27th Infantry Battalion in Iguala. They have also been given partial access to military archives. However, according to the GIEI’s recent reports and press conferences, even the president’s order has not forced the Defense Ministry to turn over all the records GIEI believes exists about the disappearance of the Ayotzinapa 43. While the families wait, perhaps in vain, for the military to open its files, today’s posting offers a glimpse of what we are learning from the leaked Sedena data set and provides some illuminating details about what the Mexican military was saying and doing behind closed doors.</p>

<p>Following the usual statistics, this Itzamna report on Iguala analyzes organized crime, reviewing homicides and other crimes in the first four months of 2020. Six years after the kidnapping of the Ayotzinapa students, this report identifies Guerreros Unidos (GU) as the continued leading criminal group in the city, controlling more territory than its rival gang, Cartel de la Sierra. José Ángel Casarrubias Salgado, “El Mochomo,” is called the “maximum leader” of the GU, and Iguala is identified as “a location with important corridors for the trafficking of drugs and weapons.” The report contains recommendations for military, police, intelligence, and civilian authorities on how to reduce criminal activities but makes no mention of local corruption or the well-known complicities between some members of the security forces and the criminals.</p>
