A fine-grained defect-sensitive local–global multiscale vision transformer framework for urban visual pollution prediction
Authors: Peng Xu, Jingyi Shen, Taotao Zou, Fa Shen, Yalun Lei, Digong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urban visual pollution is an increasing concern in rapidly growing cities, affecting environmental quality, public perception, and urban sustainability. Existing visual pollution assessment approaches often rely on manual inspection, conventional image processing, or generic deep learning models that exhibit limited sensitivity to small-scale degradation patterns, insufficient contextual understanding, and poor capability for continuous environmental severity assessment. Manual inspection methods are slow and subjective. To address these challenges, this study proposes a transformer-based deep learning framework for multi-class visual pollution detection using street-level imagery. The model integrates fine-grained urban patch encoding, defect-sensitive feature enhancement, Local–Global Dual Branch learning, hierarchical feature fusion, and a learnable Visual Pollution Index (VPI) layer to quantify degradation severity. The proposed approach classifies seven urban pollution categories, including garbage, graffiti, potholes, construction road damage, and cluttered sidewalks. Experimental results demonstrate that the model achieves the highest overall accuracy of 95.12%, outperforming baseline transformer models. The framework also attains a precision of 95.22%, recall of 95.03%, and F1-score of 95.08%. The proposed Visual Pollution Index reaches an overall VPI score of 95.07%, indicating strong consistency between classification confidence and environmental degradation assessment. Explainable AI techniques, including Grad-CAM, LIME, attention rollout, and counterfactual analysis, confirm that the model focuses on meaningful pollution regions rather than background features. The findings demonstrate that integrating defect-sensitive transformer learning with environmental severity quantification provides an effective and interpretable solution for large-scale urban visual pollution monitoring and smart-city environmental management.

Introduction

Rapid urbanization has significantly transformed the physical and visual landscape of cities worldwide. As urban environments expand and infrastructure ages, visual degradation in the form of graffiti, potholes, faded signage, broken billboards, garbage accumulation, and façade deterioration increasingly affects the aesthetic quality and perceived livability of urban spaces1. Visual pollution, although less frequently studied than air or water pollution, grid protection2, directly influences public perception, psychological well-being, urban attractiveness, and infrastructure safety3. Traditional approaches to assessing visual pollution rely on manual field inspections or survey-based evaluations, which are labor-intensive, subjective, time-consuming, and difficult to scale across large metropolitan regions4. Consequently, there is a growing need for automated, data-driven frameworks capable of monitoring urban visual quality efficiently and consistently5.

The widespread availability of street-level imagery collected from vehicle-mounted cameras and public road monitoring systems has opened new opportunities for large-scale urban environmental analytics6. Advances in deep learning, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and transformer-based architecture, have demonstrated remarkable performance in object detection7,8, scene classification9, disease detection10 and infrastructure inspection tasks11. Recent studies have applied these techniques to isolated problems such as road damage detection, litter identification, façade disorder analysis, and streetscape aesthetic evaluation. However, most existing approaches address single-category detection or narrowly defined infrastructure defects, limiting their ability to model complex urban scenes where multiple forms of visual pollution coexist12.

With urban visual pollution, there is natural multi-class and context sensitivity. Small imperfections including potholes or worn-out signage usually show up in crowded backgrounds with buildings, vehicles, plants and people13. These properties are very much problematic to traditional deep learning models, especially in cases where smaller irregularities are dwarfed by the prevalent scene objects. In addition, most of the previous structures do not have systems of retaining hierarchical features representations, refine defect-cognitive regions, or block non-informative environmental context14. Thanks to this, model generalization and model interpretability continue to be issues of concern in real-world urban monitoring systems15.

To address these challenges, this study proposes a multi-class deep learning framework for urban visual pollution prediction based on a modified Multiscale Vision Transformer (MViT). The framework enhances sensitivity to small-scale degradation patterns through fine-grained urban patch encoding, defect-sensitive feature refinement, local–global feature learning, and hierarchical information preservation. An environmental context regularization strategy is further incorporated to reduce background dominance and improve robustness under diverse street-level conditions16. In addition to multi-class classification, the framework introduces a learnable Visual Pollution Index (VPI) that transforms class probabilities into a continuous measure of environmental degradation. This integrated approach enables both pollution identification and environmental quality assessment, supporting scalable urban monitoring, infrastructure maintenance planning, and data-driven smart-city management.

The primary novelty of this study lies not in the individual architectural components, but in their task-specific integration for urban visual pollution assessment. Unlike existing transformer-based and hybrid deep learning approaches that mainly focus on generic image classification, object detection, or single-category defect analysis, the proposed framework is specifically designed for multi-class urban visual pollution recognition and continuous environmental degradation assessment. By jointly combining defect-sensitive representation learning, contextual background suppression, and learnable pollution quantification within a unified architecture, the framework enables both accurate pollution classification and environmental quality evaluation, addressing a research gap that remains insufficiently explored in current urban visual analytics literature. Dataset description and problem formulation

The data in this research is a large-scale street-level RGB data together gathered through vehicle mounted cameras that work in a limited area of urban environment. The pictures reflect the actual urban road set ups in the real-life settings and with different levels of light intensity, traffic congestion, weather changeability, and motion effects. Both images have elaborate background compositions, including buildings, roads, sidewalks, signage, and poles of lighting and clutter. The data is marked into seven categories of visual pollution. These types are different types of urban visual pollution of the aesthetic environment and quality of infrastructure. Since the imagery is taken by a moving platform, it represents real-world monitoring conditions, which are experienced by environmental management systems in smart cities. Weighted cross-entropy loss and balanced mini-batch sampling were used to minimize the impact of the majority bias in the training of the model. The data was split into 70%, 15%, and 15% for training, validation, and testing, respectively, for model evaluation. To maintain the original distribution of the classes, stratified partitioning was adopted to maintain all seven categories of visual pollution in each subset, both when training and hyperparameter tuning, and when performing the final performance test. This approach ensures the reliability and repeatability of results reported.

Data preprocessing

The street-level RGB images were first brought to a standard to create consistency and strength prior to being inputted into the proposed model. The original images were down sampled to a constant size of (224 × 224) pixels with bilinear interpolation to maintain small scale structural features i.e. cracks, graffiti lines and surface features. The pixel values were brought to the range ([0,1]) and then normalized to the channel-mean and channel-standard deviation measures that were calculated on the training set to stabilize the optimization and enhance convergence. To improve the generalization of the real-world urban capture scenarios, data augmentation methods were also utilized on top of the training process such as random horizontal flipping, slight rotation (± 10°), random cropping with scaling, and gentle brightness and contrast manipulations to represent different lighting conditions. Such augmentations were limited to the training to eliminate evaluation bias. The final processed images were then changed into the form of tensors and divided into balanced mini batches to ensure that the difference in the classes between the 7 categories of visual pollution was reduced.

Proposed model

The suggested framework is based on the extension of Multiscale Vision Transformer (MViT) to recognize fine-grained urban visual pollutants on street-level images taken by a moving car. The model starts by transforming the input image into high-resolution fine-grained patch tokens such that minute and texture-rich defects (e.g., potholes, graffiti strokes, broken signage edges) not smooth at the first stage44. These tokens are then fed through a hierarchical multiscale transformer backbone that gradually accumulates the context over the street scene at the same time reducing the number of tokens and the richness of features. To explicitly bias the representation towards defect-like patterns, an attention refinement block that is sensitive to defects is placed between two intermediate MViT stages, which increases the magnitude of high-frequency and irregular structures and reduces the extent of visually smooth background regions45. Concurrently, a lightweight branch of a local CNN that captures low-level texture information replaces under-emphasized transformer-based early Downsampling. The CNN features are combined with multi-level transformer properties (early/mid/late) to maintain the local degradation details and global semantics of scenes. Lastly, the network generates (i) a multi-class pollution forecast and (ii) learnable VPI, a transformer of class likelihoods into one continuous urban pollution indicator that can be used in environmental monitoring. After attention, an MLP further transforms token features. The full transformer block can be written compactly as5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widetilde{\mathrm{Z}}}^{\left(l\right)}={\mathrm{Z}}^{\left(l\right)}+\mathrm{M}\mathrm{S}\mathrm{A}(\mathrm{L}\mathrm{N}({\mathrm{Z}}^{\left(l\right)})),{\mathrm{Z}}_{\mathrm{out}}^{\left(l\right)}={\widetilde{\mathrm{Z}}}^{\left(l\right)}+\mathrm{M}\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{L}\mathrm{N}({\widetilde{\mathrm{Z}}}^{\left(l\right)}))$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{L}\mathrm{N}(\cdot )$$\end{document} is layer normalization. A key property of MViT is that between stages it performs token pooling (downsampling) so that the token count decreases \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{l+1}<{N}_{l}$$\end{document} while embedding dimension increases \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${D}_{l+1}>{D}_{l}$$\end{document}. In the context of visual pollution detection, this hierarchy is valuable because late stages encode global scene context (e.g., street layout, facade context, road conditions), while earlier stages retain the localized irregularities required to recognize pollutants such as potholes or graffiti.

Defect-sensitive attention module (DSAM) for texture irregularities

Although transformers learn attention maps implicitly, small defects can still be dominated by broad background content47. Therefore, after an intermediate stage (typically stage-2 or stage-3), we insert a Defect-Sensitive Attention Module (DSAM) to explicitly emphasize irregular, high-frequency structures. Let the intermediate feature representation be reshaped into a 2D feature map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{F}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{C\times {H}^{^{\prime}}\times {W}^{^{\prime}}}$$\end{document}, where C is channel dimension and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left({H}^{^{\prime}},{W}^{^{\prime}}\right)$$\end{document} is spatial size. DSAM begins by applying a depthwise convolution to capture fine texture patterns:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{F}}_{dw}={\mathrm{D}\mathrm{W}\mathrm{C}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{v}}_{3\times 3}(\mathrm{F})$$\end{document}where depthwise convolution operates independently per channel, allowing the module to detect edge-like and crack-like micro-patterns without heavy parameter overhead. Next, DSAM computes a channel importance vector through global average pooling,7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{g}=\frac{1}{{H}^{^{\prime}}{W}^{^{\prime}}}\sum_{u=1}^{{H}^{^{\prime}}}\sum_{v=1}^{{W}^{^{\prime}}}{\mathrm{F}}_{dw}(:,u,v)$$\end{document}followed by a gated transformation:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{a}}_{c}=\sigma \left({\mathrm{W}}_{2}{\hspace{0.17em}}\delta ({\mathrm{W}}_{1}\mathrm{g})\right)$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta (\cdot )$$\end{document} is ReLU, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (\cdot )$$\end{document} is the sigmoid function, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{W}}_{1},{\mathrm{W}}_{2}$$\end{document} are trainable matrices. In parallel, a spatial attention map is computed to localize defect-prone regions by compressing channels and learning a spatial mask:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{a}}_{s}=\sigma \left({\mathrm{C}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{v}}_{7\times 7}([{\mathrm{A}\mathrm{v}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}}_{c}(\mathrm{F});{\mathrm{M}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}}_{c}(\mathrm{F})])\right)$$\end{document}

Finally, DSAM refines the representation by reweighting features in both channel and spatial dimensions:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{F}}^{^{\prime}}=\mathrm{F}\otimes {\mathrm{a}}_{c}\otimes {\mathrm{a}}_{s}$$\end{document}where ⊗ denotes element-wise multiplication with broadcasting. Intuitively, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{a}}_{c}$$\end{document} learns which feature channels are most informative for pollutant textures (e.g., roughness, cracks, stain-like patterns), while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{a}}_{s}$$\end{document} learns where such patterns are spatially concentrated (e.g., road surface regions for potholes, facade regions for graffiti). This step strengthens discrimination by ensuring that the transformer representation remains defect-focused rather than scene-biased.

The presence of urban visual pollution in urban scenes often comes as small, irregular, texture dominated patterns, in the middle of complex urban scenes, is the reason why the Defect-Sensitive Attention Module (DSAM) is introduced. The light architecture like Mobile Net, real-time detection framework like YOLO is very efficient for deployment related applications, but they are mainly designed to detect objects or be fast. The goal of this study is to recognize the pollutants and to assess the environmental quality at a level of detail where both subtle variations in the texture and the context relationships are of critical importance. By explicitly increasing the representation of the high-frequency defects, and attenuating the visually dominant background regions, the capability of discriminating between similar categories of pollution is enhanced. This adds a moderate computational burden but the increased accuracy of classifications, localization of features and assessment of environment ability due to this addition in the proposed framework are justified. While DSAM enhances the accuracy of defect localization and classification, it adds extra complexity in terms of computation compared to architectures that are light. Efficient attention mechanisms and edge optimized implementations for real-time urban monitoring applications will be explored in the future.

Local–global dual branch for complementary feature extraction

To further increase sensitivity to texture irregularities, the model employs a dual-branch strategy that combines a global transformer pathway with a local convolutional pathway. The transformer branch produces global scene-aware representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{F}}_{global}$$\end{document} derived from the refined MViT output. In parallel, a lightweight CNN branch extracts local cues directly from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{X}$$\end{document} using stacked convolutional blocks:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{F}}_{local}=\phi (\mathrm{X})=\mathrm{C}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{v}\to \mathrm{B}\mathrm{N}\to \mathrm{R}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{L}\mathrm{U}\text{(repeated for}\text{ 3 blocks)}$$\end{document}

This branch is particularly useful for pollutants whose signatures are mainly local gradients and textures rather than long-range context, such as pothole edges, faded signage strokes, and garbage clutter textures. The local branch therefore complements the transformer branch by providing stable fine-detail descriptors that are less affected by token pooling.

The Local–Global Dual Branch architecture was chosen because of the nature of urban visual pollution, which is typically small, in terms of texture, and is embedded in larger, complex, street scene images. Architectures like Swin Transformer excel at capturing global context and scaling up efficiently, but hierarchical token merging can help mitigate the impact of more nuanced local irregularities48. Hence, the proposed framework consists of two branches, a light-weight CNN branch for representation of edge, texture and structural defect information, and a transformer branch for capturing long-range contextual relationships. This complementary integration obtains the balance between the preservation of local texture information and global semantic information and effectively enhances the discrimination ability between visually similar pollution categories as well as the robustness to different urban locations.

Hierarchical feature fusion to preserve degradation cues

A major risk in hierarchical transformers is that repeated pooling can attenuate micro-cues that matter for defect recognition49. To counter this, we fuse features across multiple transformer stages. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{F}}_{early}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{F}}_{mid}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{F}}_{late}$$\end{document} denote pooled representations extracted from early, intermediate, and final transformer stages (for example, after stage-1, stage-2/3, and stage-4). These stage features are combined with the CNN local feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{F}}_{local}$$\end{document}. Fusion is performed by concatenation followed by a learnable projection:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{F}}_{concat}=[{\mathrm{F}}_{early}{\hspace{0.17em}}\parallel {\hspace{0.17em}}{\mathrm{F}}_{mid}{\hspace{0.17em}}\parallel {\hspace{0.17em}}{\mathrm{F}}_{late}{\hspace{0.17em}}\parallel {\hspace{0.17em}}{\mathrm{F}}_{local}],{\mathrm{F}}_{fusion}={\mathrm{W}}_{f}{\mathrm{F}}_{concat}+{\mathrm{b}}_{f}$$\end{document}where ∥ denotes concatenation and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{W}}_{f},{\mathrm{b}}_{f}$$\end{document} are learnable parameters. This design ensures that the classifier receives both (i) low-level detail cues from early stages and CNN features and (ii) high-level context cues from late transformer stages, which collectively strengthens class separability across visually similar categories (e.g., broken signage vs. faded signage) and clutter-like categories (e.g., garbage vs. clutter sidewalk).

To counter the cumulative loss of information that can happen during multiple token pooling and feature abstraction between the transformer layers, a Hierarchical Feature Fusion was added. While contemporary representations of the world, as produced by a modern transformer, will pick up the information of the world, subtle information such as cracks, graffiti strokes, surface damage, and small clutter regions may be reduced in deeper layers. However, this limitation was addressed by the proposed framework, which used early, intermediate and final transformer features, as well as local CNN features, to combine and use the low-level texture information along with the high-level semantic context50. This fusion strategy simulates the preservation of degradation-specific characteristics, it has better discrimination ability for similar pollution types in visual level, and it has higher robustness in the complex urban environment.

Environmental context regularization (ECR)

Street imagery often contains large non-informative regions such as sky, empty road segments, or visually uniform surfaces that can inadvertently dominate learning. To suppress this effect, we apply Environmental Context Regularization (ECR) to the fused feature representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{F}}_{fusion}$$\end{document}. In practice, we use structured spatial dropout (DropBlock) to remove contiguous regions in the feature map, expressed as13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{F}}_{reg}={\mathrm{F}}_{fusion}\odot \mathrm{M}$$\end{document}where ⊙ is element-wise multiplication and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{M}$$\end{document} is a binary mask sampled such that blocks of features are randomly dropped with probability p. This encourages the network to rely on diverse local evidence rather than a single dominant background cue. To further stabilize training, we incorporate a mild feature-energy penalty.14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}}_{norm}={\lambda}_{1}\parallel {\mathrm{F}}_{reg}{\parallel}_{2}^{2}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda}_{1}$$\end{document} controls regularization strength. Together, these operations reduce background overfitting and improve robustness across lighting, perspective shifts, and road scene variations typical in vehicle-based capture.

Classification head for multi-class visual pollution prediction

The regularized fused feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{F}}_{reg}$$\end{document} is fed into a linear classifier to compute logits \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{s}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{7}$$\end{document} and class probabilities \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{y}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{7}$$\end{document}:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{s}={\mathrm{W}}_{c}{\mathrm{F}}_{reg}+{\mathrm{b}}_{c},\mathrm{y}=\mathrm{S}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{f}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{x}(\mathrm{s})$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{W}}_{c}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{b}}_{c}$$\end{document} are trainable parameters. Each component \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${y}_{i}$$\end{document} represents the predicted probability that the input corresponds to pollution class i among the 7 categories (graffiti, potholes, garbage, etc.). Model training uses cross-entropy loss:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}}_{CE}=-\sum_{i=1}^{7}{y}_{i}^{\left(true\right)}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}({y}_{i})$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${y}_{i}^{\left(true\right)}$$\end{document} is the one-hot ground truth label.

Learnable visual pollution index (VPI) layer for quantification

Beyond categorical classification, the framework produces a continuous pollution score that is meaningful for urban environmental assessment51. After obtaining the probability vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{y}$$\end{document}, the Visual Pollution Index is computed using a learnable weight vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{w}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{7}$$\end{document}:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{V}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{I}={\mathrm{w}}^{{\top}}\mathrm{y}=\sum_{i=1}^{7}{w}_{i}{y}_{i}$$\end{document}

In this equation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w}_{i}$$\end{document} represents the learned environmental contribution of the i-th pollutant class, and the dot product aggregates probabilistic evidence into a scalar score. Since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{w}$$\end{document} is trainable, the system can adaptively assign higher weight to pollution types that are consistently associated with more visually degraded scenes in the dataset. This layer makes the overall output directly usable for mapping and ranking urban segments based on predicted visual pollution intensity.

The proposed VPI is based on the class probability distribution of the model and is a continuous environmental degradation indicator. The higher the value of VPI the higher the chance of visually disruptive urban conditions, the lower the value of VPI, the cleaner and aesthetically better the environment. VPI can also be used as a prioritization tool for city planners to prioritize urban locations based on levels of visual degradation, which can be used to inform maintenance scheduling and resource allocation.

Objective function and end-to-end learning

The final learning objective integrates classification loss, context regularization, and standard weight decay. The total loss is defined as:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}}_{total}={\mathcal{L}}_{CE}+{\lambda}_{1}{\mathcal{L}}_{norm}+{\lambda}_{2}\parallel \theta {\parallel}_{2}^{2}$$\end{document}where θ denotes the set of all learnable parameters in Fine-Grained Patch Encoding (FG-PE), MViT backbone, DSAM, CNN branch, fusion layer, classifier, and VPI weights, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda}_{2}$$\end{document} controls \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{2}$$\end{document} regularization. Because all modules are differentiable, the model is trained end-to-end, allowing the fine-grained patch encoding, defect-focused attention refinement, and hierarchical fusion to jointly shape representations that are discriminative for complex urban visual pollution categories.

The MViT’s architecture was chosen as a base architecture because it needs to model the local visual pollution and the global environmental context at the same time for urban visual pollution detection. MViT is different from existing Vision Transformers which have a fixed token resolution across the network, by introducing hierarchical token pooling and progressive feature scaling to reduce computation while maintaining multi-scale information. This property is especially relevant when using images from street level where pollutants like graffiti, potholes, and trash can only fill up a small part of the image and therefore are highly dependent on the context of the image. Despite their typically high computational needs, all images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels and passed to efficient multiscale token representations of the transformer. In addition, the proposed framework results in a reduction in computational needs compared to some high-capacity transformer models and still yields good predictive performance as illustrated by the resource consumption analysis.

Several steps were taken to address the problem of overfitting and were used to minimize background-based learning, since this is typical of street-level imagery where large areas (e.g., sky, empty road) can take over the representation. To begin with, systematically regularizing the model with DropBlock within the ECR module (drop probability 0.15) was performed, which imposes the condition on the network to learn distributed evidence and does not use the one salient region. Second, the decay of weights (0.01) was facilitated on all the trainable parameters to discourage unnecessarily intricate solutions and enhance generalization. Third, the fusion and classification layers were used with dropout (0.2) to minimize co-adaptation of features especially the dense layers on projection. The validation loss was employed to indicate early termination of training when the generalization began to plateau because overfitting might occur at later training epochs. Also, the training was augmented with data flips (random) and small rotations (random) and with random crops/scales and mild brightness/contrast shifts to enhance resistance to changes in viewpoint and illumination caused by vehicle motion. The unbalanced classes between the categories were corrected with weighted cross-entropy and balanced mini-batch sampling, so that the optimizer would not reach a biased decision boundary that is dominated by common classes. Mini batches of 32 trained the model to achieve stable gradients whilst keeping the computations viable. The maximum number of epochs trained was 100, and the best model in terms of validation performance was checked to guarantee reproducibility and consistent reporting. All the implementations were written in PyTorch in deterministic environments where feasible (fixed seeds during initialization and data shuffling) and run on an NVIDIA GPU setup to provide efficient training and deterministic performance.

Results and discussion

The results of the experimental study prove a definite and steady increase in the performance of the developed model compared to the multi-class visual pollution recognition by the existing transformer-based models. As can be seen in the general comparison table, the proposed model has an overall accuracy of 95.12, which is better than CoAtNet (92.36%), CrossViT (90.23%), and LeViT (88.52%). The same tendencies are seen in measures which prove the strength and extrapolative ability of improved architecture. This 2.76% improvement over CoAtNet and almost 6.6% over LeViT is large when the base architectures are already strong pointing to the fact that the architectural modifications are effective in capturing fine-grained patterns of urban degradation. EfficientFormer has moderate stable accuracy with almost all class-wise accuracy in the range of 87–89%. It has its highest performance for Clutter Sidewalk (89.12%) and Garbage (88.93%) and good powers to detect common urban pollution patterns. It has, however, poorer performance in Construction Road (87.84%) and Graffiti (87.95%) because of its lack of ability to recognize subtle variations in texture. Compared to the other models, ConvNeXt is relatively underperforming with accuracy scores generally between 84–87%. It works best with Garbage (86.83%) and Clutter Sidewalk (86.44%) and it does not fare so well with Graffiti (84.97%) or Construction Road (85.31%). Overall, these results demonstrate that EfficientFormer outperforms ConvNeXt, whereas they are still outperformed by the proposed model, due to the limited defect-sensitive contextual learning and multi-scale contextual learning. The superiority of the proposed framework is also evidenced by a detailed analysis by the classes, as shown in Table 6. The accuracy and F1-score are improved significantly by implementing the FG-PE module (A2) (+ 1.15% and + 1.17% respectively). This improvement is evidence that the more granular the spatial level of tokenization, the more sensitive the model is to fine structural abnormalities, such as cracks and edges of graffiti as small debris. The problem of degradation in a coarse token representation is not present in FG-PE, as is the case in patch embedding. This is to confirm that spatial resolution is one of the main factors in the detection of defects in the city. Once the DSAM is added to it, the overall accuracy is enhanced to 92.74% (2.51% higher than baseline). The DSAM selectively brings out feature activations based on texture anomalies than when a uniform background area like sky or clean pavement is being activated. All precision, recall and VPI values have increased evenly, demonstrating that this module enhances the level of confidence in detection and the stability of the class separation. The introduction of the Local CNN Branch (A4) adds complementary low-level texture modeling with the global transformer pathway, and the accuracy goes to 93.61. The CNN branch can efficiently capture edge patterns and micro-textural distortions that transformers can miss out because of its global receptive field. The recorded + 3.38% improvement over the baseline proves the significance of combining local and global features representation especially in heterogeneous urban environment where degradation is delivered on various spatial scales. One of the largest improvements is made with Hierarchical Feature Fusion (A5) having an accuracy of 94.43. The model is also able to capture fine-grained structural information by summing multi-stage representations of the early, middle and deep layers and preserve a high-level sematic insight. Such overlaying aggregation provides greater resistance to inter-class similarities, e.g. between construction road and potholes or between clutter sidewalk and garbage. The steady increase in VPI also presents a greater degree of reliability in the quantification of pollution.

Incorporation of ECR (A6) is again an additional refinement in the performance to 94.92, which diminishes performance background dominance and avoids overfitting to non-pollution patterns. The module improves the capability of generalization when there is a variety of street conditions, changes in the amount of illumination and clutter in the scene. The last configuration (A7) with the Learnable VPI head and the highest overall performance of 95.12% accuracy and 95.08% F1-score, is an overall improvement of + 4.89% to the baseline. Even though VPI head mainly helps to score but not classify quantitatively in the environmental aspects, its union guarantees that there is consistency in the mapping of foregone possibilities and the levels of urban pollution severity. The FG-PE makes the sensitivity to small-scale pollutants, the DSAM to defect-focused attention and the Local CNN branch to texture/edge cues, which the transformers can underemphasize. The largest gain has been given by hierarchical fusion which conserves early mid and late degradation information, whereas, ECR diminishes background dominance, enhancing generalization. The learnable VPI layer does not introduce a direct change on the classification difficulty, but it increases the reliability and consistency of the continuous pollution scoring result, providing the most favorable total monitoring utility. Overall, the evidence of the ablation findings does indicate that there is no single change in architecture that leads to improvement in performance, but to the synergy of the fine-grained encoding, defect-sensitive attention, local global fusion, hierarchical feature integration, and environmental regularization. All these elements in turn help to enhance the capacity of the model to identify, categorize, and measure the level of urban visual pollution, which justifies the structural design decisions of the suggested framework to be used in environmental monitoring practices.

Conclusion and future work

This research introduced a powerful, interpretable deep learning approach for multi-class visual pollution identification, to assist the automated monitoring of urban environment. The proposed model utilized a transformer-based backbone, feature enhancement using pollution-aware transformer, hierarchical feature fusion, and a learnable VPI, and demonstrated a higher accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score than the current vision architectures. The results of the confusion matrix analysis showed good separation between the classes and low misclassification rates between classes where possible, especially for classes that were visually similar and had to be separated. Furthermore, the use of Explainable AI methods, such as Grad-CAM, LIME, attention rollout, layer-wise feature visualization, and counterfactual analysis, confirmed that the model’s predictions were causally linked with relevant aspects of urban pollution and non-countable background features. This enhances trust, transparency and applicability in smart-city governance and in decision-making systems in municipalities. The framework can help authorities focus their cleaning efforts, track the trends of environmental degradation, and inform urban planning decisions based on data. While the proposed framework has shown promising results, there are some drawbacks that should be noted. A single dataset of urban visual pollution was used to evaluate the study, and this may reduce the assessment of cross-city/cross-region generalization. Furthermore, the framework has not yet been tested on external data, and has not been evaluated against other datasets, therefore its robustness in other cities, other countries, other weather conditions, other seasons, and other image acquisition platforms is not yet fully explored. Additionally, although the proposed architecture improves defect sensitivity and classification performance, the integration of multiple transformers and attention modules increases computational requirements compared with lightweight deployment-oriented models. Furthermore, the current framework focuses primarily on image-level pollution assessment and does not explicitly delineate pollution objects at the pixel or instance level, while also excluding complementary spatial, temporal, geospatial, and sensor-based information that may further enhance environmental monitoring. While the proposed VPI is a quantitative assessment of the degree of visual pollution, it is based on model predictions and not on human perception scores. Thus, the present study does not propose a validated relationship between VPI and the subjective human aesthetic judgement, nor did it attempt to formally validate the relationship by using an assessment based on the expert judgement of urban planning or a survey of user perceptions. Future research focuses on cross domain testing in various urban settings, real-time air pollution monitoring using video technology, adaptation to other city domains, and severity-level regression for fine-grained environmental impact assessment. Furthermore, the synergistic combination of object-based image analysis, semantic segmentation, and instance segmentation with transformer-based feature learning has the potential to enhance the spatial interpretability and environmental assessment capabilities by facilitating more accurate localization and boundary delineation of pollution regions. The practical applications of VPI values to urban planning and decision-making will gain in strength if future studies further explore relationships between VPI values and human perception, via expert annotation campaigns, citizen surveys, and psychometric validation frameworks. The integration of multimodal geospatial and sensor information, lightweight attention mechanisms, edge-optimized deployment approaches, and continuous learning algorithms will further boost scalability, robustness, and real-world application in large-scale smart city monitoring networks.
