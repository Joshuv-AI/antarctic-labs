More than a ring: the emerging role of heme in angiogenesis
Authors: Francesco De Giorgio, Luca Munaron, Emanuela Tolosano, Sara Petrillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeme is an essential iron-containing porphyrin of vital importance for all cells, including endothelial cell (EC). Indeed, due to its involvement in several biological processes, including gene transcription regulation, energy production, anabolic processes, heme metabolism is critically required in high-energy–demanding processes like angiogenesis. Over the past decade, growing evidence has identified heme metabolism as a central regulator of the angiogenic process. Hence, targeting heme synthesis and homeostasis could offer valuable therapeutic opportunities to modulate pathological neovascularization, as in ocular neovascularization diseases and cancer, and, potentially, to support neo-vascular growth in conditions characterized by insufficient blood supply as ischemia and stroke.The present review summarizes the literature on heme and angiogenesis, emphasizing the novel functions of heme, beyond its more canonical role as cofactor in hemoproteins and addressing how heme homeostasis can orchestrate a plethora of endothelial functions indispensable for angiogenesis.Keywords: Heme, Porphyrins, Angiogenesis, Metabolism, Retinopathies, Cancer

The evolutionary journey of heme metabolism

Porphyrins are macrocyclic organic compounds with a tetrapyrrole ring structure capable of binding metal ions to form metallo-porphyrins, which are essential in many biological processes [1]. Porphyrins have long been considered crucial molecules in the origin of life. Indeed, experimental studies simulating primordial volcanic conditions suggest that porphyrins could form abiotically [2]. Hence, abiotically formed porphyrins may have actively supported prebiotic metabolism and proto-photosynthesis, thus contributing to the emergence of life [3].

The biochemical complexity of life was largely enabled by the ability to transform free energy, whether derived from light or chemical compounds. In this view, heme and chlorophyll were pivotal due to their ability to coordinate metals—iron and magnesium, respectively—and to mediate electron transfer [4]. Phylogenetic analyses showed that heme-containing proteins emerged prior to the Archaea/Bacteria divergence and, thus, are proposed to represent “pre-Last Universal Common Ancestor (LUCA)” enzymes. A contrary proposal suggests a heme-free LUCA based on fundamental differences in the ways Archaea and Bacteria synthesize heme. While the origins of heme biosynthesis and utilisation remain contested, the presence of multiple heme biosynthetic pathways throughout prokaryotes and eukaryotes show that heme biosynthesis has undergone extensive remodelling over the course of evolution. Interestingly, evolution of heme and oxygen metabolisms are closely interconnected. Indeed, at a certain point during evolution, chlorophyll emerged from the same ancestral biosynthetic pathway that also produced heme in ancient bacteria. Therefore, heme synthesis might have contributed to the introduction of oxygen into the atmosphere [3, 5].

Heme synthesis in eukaryotes evolved from a key evolutionary event that occurred in α-proteobacteria, marked by the emergence of a new branch at the beginning of the biosynthetic pathway with the first appearance of the enzyme aminolevulinic acid synthase (ALAS) [5]. This enzyme catalyses the one-step formation of the first intermediate in heme biosynthesis, 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA), from succinyl-CoA and glycine, replacing the earlier two-step process. Remarkably, following mitochondrial endosymbiosis, it was retained in eukaryotic cells and became the rate-limiting step in the heme synthesis pathway that is used by our cells today [3, 6].

Interestingly, some organisms, such as Caenorhabditis elegans, have evolved specialized mechanisms to acquire heme from extracellular sources, compensating for their inability to synthesize heme. These trafficking pathways are conserved in higher eukaryotes and play a central role in maintaining intracellular heme homeostasis and intercellular porphyrin/heme exchange [7, 8].

As well as being contained in hemoproteins, as in the electron transport chain (i.e. in the respiratory cytochromes), heme is involved as an electron transfer component in numerous specialised redox reactions including anabolic metabolism of fats, steroids and other secondary metabolites, and the catabolism of xenobiotics and compounds of endogenous metabolism (e.g. peroxidase, catalase) [9]. Moreover, heme iron has the capacity to bind diatomic gases (e.g. O2, CO2). Indeed, as a component of hemoglobin and myoglobin, heme is responsible for transporting and storing oxygen in animals, including mammals, making it an essential molecule in the evolution of high-metabolic-demand species, including humans [10]. Finally, heme is an important regulatory molecule reversibly binding transcription factors, ion channels and its homeostasis is crucial for maintaining a healthy cellular metabolic state [1].

Accordingly, dysregulated heme metabolism has been addressed to multiple pathological conditions including porphyrias, retinopathies, diabetes and cancer [11–15]. Hence, heme homeostasis is preserved at multiple levels, including biosynthesis, trafficking and catabolism [6].

Given the central role of heme in multiple cellular processes, heme synthesis is essentially required in highly proliferating cells. Indeed, the targeting of ALAS is embryonically lethal, underscoring the necessity of endogenous heme synthesis during development [16]. Furthermore, heme production remains actively required in adult tissues, indicating that dietary heme alone is not sufficient to meet organ-specific demands [17, 18].

In this view, heme is critically required in endothelial cells (EC), where it supports multiple processes such as redox balance, mitochondrial respiration and biosynthetic processes [19]. In cardiovascular research, heme has long been studied for its role in vascular homeostasis, particularly in the contexts of hemolytic anemia and intravascular hemolysis [20, 21]. However, accumulating evidence over the past decade has implicated heme metabolism as an important modulator of angiogenesis, although the reported effects can be highly context-dependent. Proper heme synthesis and homeostasis appear to be required during vascular development and remodeling to support the energetic and biosynthetic demands of angiogenic programs. Consistently, altered heme handling has been associated with both physiological vascular formation and multiple pathological settings, including ocular neovascularization, cardiovascular disorders, and cancer [11, 22–25].

Here, we summarize the roles of heme in angiogenesis by focusing on mechanisms supported by experimental evidence while also discussing emerging, more exploratory concepts, including potential regulatory effects mediated by porphyrin intermediates.

The dual-edged role of heme synthesis in angiogenesis

Heme is synthesized through a series of eight enzymatic reactions partly occurring in the cytosol and in the mitochondria (Fig. 1). The first and rate-limiting enzyme of the heme biosynthetic pathway is aminolevulinic acid synthase 1 (ALAS1) which catalyses the condensation of succinyl-CoA, a tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediate, and glycine to produce 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA). Then, two molecules of ALA are condensed by ALA dehydratase (ALAD) to form porphobilinogen (PBG), which is subsequently assembled to form the porphyrin ring. Four PBG molecules are used to synthesize the first porphyrin intermediate hydroxymethylbilane (HMB), a reaction catalysed by HMB synthase (HMBS). HMB is successively converted into uroporphyrinogen III (UPG), coproporphyrinogen III (CPG), and protoporphyrinogen IX (PPG), finally leading to the formation of protoporphyrin IX (PPIX). The final step involves mitochondrial ferrochelatase (FECH), which incorporates ferrous iron into PPIX ring, to synthetize de novo protoheme [6, 19, 26]. Then, de novo synthesized heme undergoes modifications required for its incorporation as prosthetic group into key endothelial proteins, including mitochondrial respiratory complexes II–IV of the electron transport chain (ETC), endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), and cytochrome P450 [6, 19, 27, 28]. Moreover, porphyrins and heme actively shape endothelial function and metabolism, all key determinants of vascular growth, as it will be discussed in the following Sect. [29].

Consistent with a requirement for heme supply during endothelial angiogenic programs, multiple studies have reported that angiogenic EC show increased expression of genes involved in heme biosynthesis (e.g., ALAS1, ALAD, HMBS, FECH) as well as enzymes contributing to glycine production (e.g., phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase, PHGDH, and phosphoserine aminotransferase 1, PSAT1). Notably, the magnitude and functional relevance of this transcriptional upregulation may vary depending on the angiogenic context and experimental model [25, 29]. Consistently, FECH expression was found increased in human patients affected by wet age-related macular degeneration (AMD), underscoring the importance of heme synthesis also in aberrant vascular growth [23, 30]. This is further supported by in vitro evidence showing higher intracellular heme levels in highly angiogenic tumor-derived human EC compared to less angiogenic dermal EC [29].

In this view, available evidence suggests that inhibiting heme biosynthesis can impair angiogenesis [11]. For instance, the genetic deletion of endothelial PHGDH in mice is embryonically lethal and post-natal targeting leads to multi-organ (i.e. brain, heart, kidney, retina) angiogenic defects and defective vascular integrity. PHGDH deficiency in EC reduces glycine availability for ALAS1 function, leading to heme deficiency, mitochondrial dysfunction, and impaired angiogenesis [25]. Similarly, pharmacological inhibition of ALAD by succinyl acetone (SA) inhibits angiogenesis due to heme deficiency and impaired mitochondrial fitness [11, 25, 29]. Moreover, genetic targeting of FECH or its pharmacological inhibition with N-methyl protoporphyrin or with the antifungal drug griseofulvin impairs EC function and reduces pathological ocular angiogenesis in experimental models of laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (L-CNV) and oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR). Interestingly, the antiangiogenic effect due FECH targeting was likely due to heme deficiency rather than upstream porphyrins (e.g. PPIX) accumulation [11, 23, 30]. Overall, these data critically highlight the importance of sufficient heme synthesis and levels to accomplish angiogenesis.

Despite the requirement for heme biosynthesis during endothelial angiogenic programs, heme production must be tightly balanced, as overactivation of the pathway can become detrimental [12]. Accordingly, tight control of heme biosynthetic flux is ensured by multiple regulatory layers acting at different levels and at distinct nodes along the pathway, including transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation, enzyme activity modulation, and substrate/transport availability. One prominent example of this multilayer regulation is ALAS1, whose function is controlled by heme at multiple levels, including transcriptional regulation and mitochondrial activity, thereby modulating ALA synthesis to meet cellular heme demand. Additionally, the flux through the heme biosynthetic pathway is optimized to ensure rapid conversion of porphyrin intermediates into heme, avoiding potential toxic buildup [22, 26, 31]. In this context, our works highlight the detrimental effects of “overdriven” heme metabolism on EC functions. Indeed, pharmacological stimulation of heme biosynthesis by ALA supplementation promote massive porphyrin and heme buildup in EC and impaired both physiological and pathological angiogenesis in the OIR retina [27, 29, 32]. Although strong activation of the heme biosynthetic pathway markedly affects EC functions, porphyrin species were found enriched in tumor EC isolated from the brain of glioblastoma patients [33], highlighting that the role of porphyrins in the tumor vascular context remains poorly defined and warrants further investigation. Nevertheless, these findings align with the growing concept of “porphyrin overdrive” as a unique feature of the tumor microenvironment (TME), which will be discussed later [34, 35]. In this view, the biological effects of porphyrins on EC are likely to be dose-dependent, such that different degrees of heme biosynthesis stimulation/inhibition may yield distinct outcomes in angiogenic modulation. Taken together, these considerations have important translational implications. Since heme synthesis is a ubiquitous process required to sustain physiological heme levels across tissues, systemic inhibition of the pathway poses significant challenges for clinical translation [18, 36]. Accordingly, tissue-restricted or locally acting strategies may represent more valuable therapeutic approaches to inhibit heme synthesis in pathological contexts. Clinically, this concept is exemplified by Givosiran, a liver-specific siRNA targeting ALAS1, approved for treatment of acute hepatic porphyrias [37, 38]. Furthermore, ALA is already used clinically for local photodynamic therapy (PDT) in the treatment of skin cancers. In this setting, ALA administration results in porphyrins accumulation, which in turn act as photosensitizers that, upon light exposure, selectively kill cancer cells. Notably, because ALA is preferentially taken up by cancer cells and light delivery can be confined to the target area, systemic toxicity is minimized [39–42]. Accounting for these considerations, modulating heme synthesis may represent a promising strategy to suppress pathological angiogenesis; however, its translational potential will likely depend on addressing key challenges, including systemic effects, tissue specificity, and the feasibility of achieving sufficiently localized modulation.

Heme trafficking and homeostasis in endothelial cells

Coordinated porphyrin/heme synthesis and trafficking have emerged as essential mechanisms for preserving intracellular heme and porphyrin homeostasis in EC (Fig. 2) [29]. Active heme synthesis is required by proliferating cells, including angiogenic EC. However, heme and porphyrins build up could have harmful effects. In this view, a “coordinated” heme synthesis–export axis might be a central mechanism to ensure a “safe” active heme synthesis [43]. Accordingly, we demonstrated that hemopexin (HX), a heme export facilitator, promotes endogenous heme biosynthesis meanwhile facilitating heme export. In this way, HX ensures an active heme synthesis by mobilizing the neo-synthetized heme pool and thus favours EC proliferation and angiogenesis [29]. These data are consistent with previously reported pro-angiogenic function of HX in the rat ischemic brain [44].

EC can also release porphyrin intermediates and heme into the extracellular environment, particularly in response to elevated heme synthesis, as in condition of ALA exogenous administration. These findings may be particularly relevant in the context of porphyria, where EC could actively contribute to the systemic release of porphyrin intermediates into the circulation [29, 32]. Importantly, the combination of ALA with HX, which can also facilitate porphyrin trafficking, further increased porphyrin export and reduced EC proliferation compared with ALA alone [29]. Overall, these findings suggest that the impact of HX on endothelial cells is highly context-dependent. Under basal conditions, HX may support EC function by mobilizing the intracellular heme pool and promoting heme synthesis to an extent that helps preserve heme–porphyrin homeostasis and angiogenic capacity. In contrast, under conditions of excessive porphyrin production, such as after ALA administration, HX may further enhance the export of newly synthesized heme/porphyrins, leading to their accumulation in the extracellular microenvironment at levels that become detrimental to EC function and ultimately exert anti-angiogenic effects. However, the mechanisms through which HX regulates heme and porphyrin trafficking under these different conditions remain to be fully elucidated.

Besides their ability to export porphyrin intermediates, EC can release substantial levels of PBG, a porphyrin and heme precursor, likely to maintain sufficient flux within the heme biosynthesis pathway [26, 29]. However, the mechanisms by which PBG crosses mammalian cell membranes, as well as its potential bioactive properties, remain unknown [29, 45].

Cells can also import heme precursors and heme itself from extracellular sources to support endogenous de novo heme synthesis [12]. For instance, ALA importer Peptide Transporters 2 (PEPT 2) is highly expressed in angiogenic EC from the postnatal developing murine retina [29]. However, this metabolic reliance represents metabolic vulnerability that could exploited to foster heme synthesis and inhibit neovascularization as previously discussed [29]. Moreover, EC are sensitive to changes in extracellular porphyrins and heme profile. For instance, intravascular hemolysis leads to intracellular heme overload and EC dysfunction [20]. Similarly, extracellular porphyrins (i.e. PPIX) inhibit in vitro angiogenesis in human and murine EC models [29].

Although passive diffusion might occur due to the lipophilicity of porphyrins and heme, a more rapid or reduced leakage has been observed. Indeed, EC express multiple plasma membrane porphyrins and heme transporters, including ATP-binding cassette sub-family G member 2 (ABCG2) and ATP Binding Cassette Subfamily B Member 6 (ABCB6), which undergo modulation upon heme synthesis dysregulation [29, 46, 47].

The role of ABCG2 in angiogenesis has been investigated primarily in tumors, where its overexpression in cancer cells correlates with increased Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF) production and increased angiogenesis [48]. Nevertheless, owing to the low specificity of these transporters, their precise function in EC during angiogenesis and in heme metabolism remains unclear.

The most comprehensive insights into heme trafficking and homeostasis during angiogenesis arises from studies on the Feline Leukemia Virus Subgroup C Receptor 1 (FLVCR1) and the paralog FLVCR2 [49, 50], as discussed below. Initially characterized as heme exporter and importer, respectively, their roles as bona fide heme transporters are currently debated. Indeed, their recent recognition as ubiquitous choline and ethanolamine importers poses novel level of complexity in the regulation of heme metabolism and homeostasis [6, 51].

Feline leukemia virus subgroup C receptor 1

The FLVCR1 gene locus encodes two distinct isoforms through alternative splicing. In particular, the FLVCR1a isoform localizes to the plasma membrane and contributes to choline and ethanolamine import [49, 51]. The FLVCR1 gene is transcriptionally regulated by hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), key mediators of the hypoxic response and angiogenesis [52]. Consistently, FLVCR1a expression is markedly increased in angiogenic EC, including embryonic and tumor-derived EC. The endothelial-specific deletion of FLVCR1a in mice impairs developmental angiogenesis leading to widespread hemorrhages and intrauterine death. Moreover, FLVCR1a contribute to pathological tumor angiogenesis, supporting a role for this transporter in sustaining aberrant neovascularization. Remarkably, the conserved function of FLVCR1a was also demonstrated in zebrafish and human EC, supporting the view that its contribution to angiogenic programs is evolutionarily preserved [27, 28]. FLVCR1a-deficient EC display increased intracellular heme levels, accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction, ER stress, and cell death by ferroptosis [27, 53]. In line with this, FLVCR1a-targeted EC display a higher ability to accumulate heme under conditions of increased heme synthesis, such as following ALA treatment [27]. Although the direct intervention of FLVCR1a in heme export is now reconsidered, its targeting may affect the activity of other heme transporters or influence activity of biosynthetic enzymes [51].

Changes in the intracellular heme pool regulate ALAS activity and/or expression, which are altered upon FLVCR1 targeting [27, 53]. Thus, we can speculate that FLVCR1a affects ALAS activity by indirectly controlling heme levels. For instance, FLVCR1a might regulate ALAS activity by controlling substrate availability for heme synthesis. Indeed, the heme precursor succinyl-CoA is an intermediate of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, whose flux is indeed modulated in FLVCR1a-deficient EC [43]. Moreover, FLVCR1a, by controlling choline and/or ethanolamine entry into the Kennedy pathway could contribute to shaping mitochondrial membranes, whose composition is crucial for maintaining mitochondrial activity, including heme synthesis [27, 54].

Overall, these findings highlight the critical role of FLVCR1a in maintaining heme homeostasis. Whether this function is independent of, or mechanistically linked to, its choline/ethanolamine import function, remains to be elucidated. Notably, FLVCR1a-mediated regulation of heme metabolism profoundly rewires EC metabolism, a major driver of the angiogenic process, as will be discussed below.

Feline leukemia virus subgroup C receptor 2

Like FLVCR1, the involvement of FLVCR2 paralogue in endothelial heme regulation is still a matter of debate. Initially, FLVCR2 was identified as a plasma membrane heme importer, suggesting it could balance de novo heme synthesis with heme uptake. Indeed, FLVCR2 targeting was associated with reduced heme/porphyrin import and increased sensitivity to exogenous heme [50]. However, recent structural analyses have ruled out direct heme transport through FLVCR2, although heme binding at the N-terminus has been detected [55, 56]. To date, FLVCR2 function has been mostly characterized in brain EC, where it is considered to mediate choline uptake across the blood–brain barrier. Specifically, FLVCR2 is critical for proper brain vascularization and development [50, 55–57]. Indeed, loss of function mutations in this gene are associated with the development of proliferative vasculopathy with hydranencephaly syndrome (PVHH), also known as Fowler syndrome [58]. Genetic targeting of endothelial FLVCR2 in mice impairs brain angiogenesis, resulting in cerebral vascular abnormalities, tissue hypoxia and embryonic or post-natal lethality, thereby recapitulating the human syndrome. Moreover, impaired vascularization due to FLVCR2 loss is associated with dysregulated angiogenic gene expression program, as indicated by increased Delta-like ligand 4 (Dll4), Endomucin (Esm), and Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor Receptor 3 (VEGFR3), along with reduced expression of Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor Receptor 2 (VEGFR2) [50].

FLVCR2 has also been studied in the lung, where it is highly expressed. Notably, FLVCR2 was found to protect against hemolysis-induced lung damage, addressing for the first time a role for this protein in hemolytic diseases. Indeed, Flvcr2 expression decreases in lungs from hemolytic mice, including pulmonary EC, and its depletion aggravates hemolytic-related lung damage. These data are in contrast with previous notion of FLVCR2 in promoting heme uptake, indicating that FLVCR2 may protect from hemolysis in an alternative mechanism [59]. Further studies are needed to clarify how heme and choline homeostasis are involved in driving these vascular phenotypes.

Heme catabolism in endothelial cells

Heme can exert pro-angiogenic effects through multiple mechanisms, including the modulation of the cell redox status or modulation of angiogenic signaling [60]. In this view, heme catabolism appears to contribute to angiogenic regulation [61]. Indeed, heme degradation by heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1) is upregulated in post-natal and OIR murine retinas, as well as in wounds and tumor angiogenesis [29, 61, 62]. Although HO-1 is activated as a response to heme fluctuations aimed at maintaining intracellular heme homeostasis, it can also be induced by other stimuli, including VEGF signaling [29, 60]. Moreover, recent work identified HO as a component of a signaling cascade mediated by the endothelial protein C receptor (EPCR) through the AKT–Nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (NRF2) pathway, uncovering a novel regulation of endothelial heme catabolism by angiogenic membrane receptors [62]. Hence, heme catabolism may represent a mechanism by which EC regulate angiogenesis.

HO catalyses heme conversion into carbon monoxide (CO), biliverdin and iron (Fig. 2) [6]. CO functions as a pro-angiogenic mediator by sustaining EC proliferation, migration, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression. Consistently, HO-1 activation, whether by heme or various other stimuli, was found to induce VEGF expression [63]. Biliverdin can also promote angiogenesis in both in vitro EC and in vivo models of ischemic skin flaps. This effect is mediated by its conversion to bilirubin and by the sustained induction of HO-1 via the PI3K/NRF2 pathway [64]. However, another study reported an anti-angiogenic effect of biliverdin in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), suggesting that direct biliverdin administration may exert dual, context-dependent effects on angiogenesis [63, 65].

In this view, direct modulation of HO-1 emerges as a promising strategy to regulate angiogenesis. Indeed, HO-1 overexpression improves angiogenesis in several vascular disorders, including ischemia and aneurysm, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target [63]. Interestingly, HX has been reported to enhance post-ischemic angiogenesis through upregulation of HO-1 [44]. Conversely, HO-1 inhibition was effective in inhibiting aberrant angiogenesis in retinal neo-vascularization (RNV) [62].

Heme-mediated transcriptional regulation of angiogenesis

BACH1

Heme can also function as a signaling molecule through its ring structure (Fig. 2). For instance, heme can promote the degradation of BTB and CNC homology 1 (BACH1), a repressor of gene transcription. In EC, this mechanism might be involved in regulating angiogenesis [66]. For instance, BACH1 is a key repressor of HIF-1α, a major inducer of angiogenic process [67]. Consistently, BACH1 deletion enhances perfusion and vascular formation in both ischemic injury and tumor models by activating Wnt/β-catenin signalling and upregulating VEGF expression [68–70]. BACH1 is also a key regulator of cell metabolism (e.g. glycolysis) which could influence angiogenesis, as discussed in the following Sects. [66, 71]. While these studies establish BACH1 as an important node in angiogenic regulation, further studies are needed to directly address the contribution of heme fluctuations in modulating angiogenesis.

REV-ERBα

Heme has also been identified as a physiological agonist of the nuclear hormone receptor REV-ERBα (nuclear receptor subfamily 1, group D, member 1; NR1D1), a core component of the molecular circadian clock [72]. By enabling the interaction of REV-ERBα with its corepressor nuclear receptor corepressor 1 (NCoR1), heme supports its transcriptional repressive activity, which underlies the circadian regulation of several cellular functions, including angiogenesis [73]. For instance, EC migration and proliferation, production of pro-angiogenic factors (i.e. VEGF; Fibroblast Growth Factor, FGF), extracellular matrix remodelling, pericyte recruitment, all key processes during angiogenesis, are modulated by circadian clock machinery [74, 75]. Consistently, targeting components of the clock machinery, such as BMAL1 and PER2, impairs retinal angiogenesis in mice [76]. Moreover, clock genes regulate multiple aspects of cellular metabolism, including glucose and lipid utilization, whose proper balance and handling is a critical determinant of angiogenesis [77–79]. Further studies are required to gain a deeper understanding of heme-mediated regulation of circadian rhythms and its implications for angiogenesis. In this context, such insights could inform the development of therapeutic strategies to target pathological angiogenesis and may help optimize the scheduling and timing of anti-angiogenic drug administration [80].

Heme regulation of miRNA biogenesis

Interestingly, heme has emerged as a key modulator of the RNA post-transcriptional regulatory process. By directly binding to the RNA-binding protein DiGeorge Critical Region 8 (DGCR8), a key component of the microprocessor complex involved in the microRNA (miRNA) maturation process, heme promotes DGCR8 dimerization, thereby facilitating miRNA processing and maturation [81]. Moreover, recent study identified cytosolic ALAS to limit the assembly and activity of the Argonaute RNA interference complex machinery, independent from the canonical function related to heme synthesis [82]. In this view, the targeting of heme metabolism might represent a novel strategy to interfere with RNA interference process. Moreover, modulation of heme levels, might influence cytosolic ALAS fraction, coupling heme-dependent and heme independent roles of ALAS. Much work is still required to better address the roles of heme and the newly identified function of ALAS in miRNA biology, and to elucidate their biological relevance during the angiogenic process.

Biological functions of endogenous porphyrins and porphyrin analogues

As mentioned earlier, porphyrins serve as intermediates in the heme biosynthetic pathway, and their intracellular availability is strictly controlled to avoid endothelial toxic buildup [29]. Although porphyrins share the same tetrapyrrole ring structure as heme, the absence of a central iron may confer distinct biological specificities to these molecules. Indeed, iron coordination induces a slight curvature of the ring, which may underlie functional differences compared with heme [83]. Moreover, variations in the side chains of endogenous porphyrins, as well as in their chemical derivatives, named porphyrin analogues, further contribute to the functional specificity of these compounds [84].

The relevance of porphyrins during angiogenesis has been mostly studied in the field of ocular neovascularization and PDT. Indeed, porphyrins analogues are delivered as photosensitizers that preferentially accumulate in highly proliferating EC, making them sensitive to light irradiation [85–87]. In this view, verteporfin is clinically employed in PDT to hamper aberrant angiogenesis in eye-related vasculopathies (i.e. polypoidal choroidal vasculopathies, PCV) and wet AMD. Interestingly, verteporfin administration followed by PDT promoted higher VEGF and VEGFR3 expression [88]. In line with this, PDT combined with anti-VEGF treatment improved clinical outcome of patients affected by eye-related vasculopathies [89]. In this context, ALA is currently used in PDT as a safer strategy to induce accumulation of endogenous porphyrins, although its use remains limited in cancer therapy [42].

The potential use of porphyrins as direct modulators—and possibly inhibitors—of angiogenesis has only recently begun to emerge. Indeed, zinc protoporphyrin IX (ZnPPIX), another porphyrin analogue, has been reported to suppress angiogenesis in retinopathy. Specifically, ZnPPIX, by inhibiting HO activity, decreases carbon monoxide (CO) production and pro-angiogenic activity [62]. Similarly, tin protoporphyrin IX (SnPPIX), another HO inhibitor, inhibits VEGF expression in a dose-dependent manner, thus impacting EC angiogenic potential [90]. Furthermore, verteporfin has been shown to inhibit Yes-associated protein (YAP)/transcriptional co-activator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ) signaling, contributing to impaired EC function and angiogenesis. Notably, the inhibitory effect on Hippo signalling was specific to the verteporfin and not observed with endogenous PPIX [91, 92]. In addition, 5,10,15,20-tetrakis(methyl-4-pyridyl)-21 H,23 H-porphine-tetra p-tosylate salt (TMPP) was found to impair vascular growth likely due to inhibition of key angiogenic signalling pathways as Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor Receptor 2 (VEGFR2) and Fibroblast Growth Factor Receptor 1 (FGFR1) [93].

The interest in modulating endogenous porphyrin metabolism is steadily increasing. In this view, PPIX was effective in inhibiting in vitro and ex vivo angiogenesis, although its bioactive properties require further investigation [29]. Notably, “porphyrin overdrive” has been identified a key feature of the TME, as discussed below, and its targeting may influence tumor growth and angiogenesis [29, 35].

Overall, this evidence indicates that porphyrins have a wide range of biological functions. Nevertheless, further research is needed to understand the bioactive roles of endogenous porphyrins in angiogenesis and to explore how the modulation of porphyrin metabolism could be exploited to target aberrant neovascularization.

Heme as a key regulator of endothelial cell metabolism

Mitochondrial metabolism

Targeting heme synthesis and homeostasis has been shown to profoundly shape EC metabolism [25, 43]. Indeed, heme groups are required for anchorage (i.e. complex II) or activity (i.e. complexes III and IV) of the ETC complexes (Fig. 2). Hence, heme is indispensable for mitochondrial function and oxidative energy metabolism [25, 94].

Although angiogenic EC predominantly rely on aerobic glycolysis, recent work highlighted the essentiality of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) for sufficient energy production [77, 95–97]. Consistently, heme deficiency due to PHGDH targeting causes mitochondrial defects and decreases OXPHOS leading to inefficient mitochondrial ATP synthesis required for angiogenesis. Moreover, the concomitant increase in glycolysis observed following PHGDH inhibition was insufficient to recover endothelial energetic failure [25]. In this view, glycolysis is transcriptionally regulated by BACH1, which may partially explain the changes in glycolytic capacity observed following alterations in heme levels [98]. Defective mitochondrial respiration was also observed upon endothelial inhibition of ALAS1, ALAD or FECH, addressing the importance of sufficient heme levels for efficient mitochondrial OXPHOS [11, 25]. In line with this, heme analogue supplementation was sufficient to rescue mitochondrial defects due to heme deficiency [25].

ETC dysfunction due to heme depletion is also associated with reduced activity of dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH), an enzyme involved in pyrimidine synthesis, thereby contributing to decreased cellular pyrimidine nucleotide levels and EC proliferative defects [25].

Interestingly, heme biosynthesis and homeostasis can influence mitochondrial ATP dynamics independently of conventional bioenergetic pathways. Indeed, heme and PPIX interact with the adenine nucleotide translocator (ANT), an ADP/ATP exchanger located in the inner mitochondrial membrane, thus influencing ATP dynamics [99, 100]. Consistently, disruption of heme homeostasis following FLVCR1a-targeting in cancer cells is associated with dysregulated ANT activity and an altered cytosolic-to-mitochondrial ATP ratio [43]. Also, FLVCR2 regulates mitochondrial respiration and ATP production in pulmonary EC. Mechanistically, heme binding at the N-terminus of FLVCR2 promotes its dissociation from ETC complexes, thereby uncoupling mitochondrial electron transport from ATP synthesis [55, 59]. Altogether, these data underscore the crucial role of heme in preserving ETC function and coordinating ATP synthesis and dynamics in EC.

Beyond its direct role as a cofactor in ETC complexes, heme synthesis can modulate mitochondrial energy metabolism by drawing on succinyl-CoA through ALAS1, thereby directly coupling heme production to the TCA cycle. When ALAS1 activity is high, succinyl-CoA (a TCA cycle intermediate) is consumed more rapidly for ALA synthesis, reducing its availability within the cycle. Under this condition, the cell compensates by modulating upstream cataplerotic/anaplerotic inputs to replenish succinyl-CoA and sustain both TCA cycle flux and heme production. Consistently, in Acute Intermittent Porphyria (AIP), loss of HMBS causes heme deficiency and induces ALAS1 activity; the resulting elevated demand for succinyl-CoA is associated with enhanced TCA flux [101, 102]. Conversely, when ALAS1 activity is reduced, as by ALA/hemin administration or upon FLVCR1a or ALAS1 genetic targeting, succinyl-CoA is consumed less by heme biosynthesis, increasing its availability to the TCA cycle. In this setting, the higher intra-mitochondrial succinyl-CoA pool can also support increased TCA cycle flux and remodelling of interconnected pathways [24, 43].

Collectively, these findings highlight a metabolic coordination between heme biosynthesis and the TCA cycle, which in turn could influence mitochondrial energy production beyond its direct role as a cofactor in ETC.

Lipid metabolism

Recent evidence also highlighted the importance of adequate de novo heme synthesis and homeostasis in controlling lipid metabolism, a major driver in angiogenesis (Fig. 2). Indeed, proper lipid membrane composition, including adequate cholesterol and sphingolipids levels, are crucially required for angiogenic signalling, particularly in pathways involving VEGFR2 and Notch [103–106].

The first evidence for a tight connection between heme and lipid metabolism emerged in the 1980s from studies in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, showing that heme synthesis and availability could influence sterol metabolism [107, 108].

Subsequent studies in mammalian systems have further supported this link. Indeed, heme serves as a cofactor for CYP51A1 (lanosterol 14α-demethylase), a key enzyme in cholesterol biosynthesis that catalyzes lanosterol demethylation. Notably, CYP51A1 is downregulated upon protoporphyrinogen oxidase (PPO) targeting [109, 110]. Moreover, active cholesterol synthesis is necessary to produce the farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP), a cholesterol precursor, which is required for the modification of newly synthesized protoheme [84]. Consistent with these observations, RNA-seq analysis of liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSEC) from healthy and injured rat livers revealed a correlation between the expression of heme- and cholesterol-related genes [53].

The relationship between heme metabolism and cholesterol homeostasis remains controversial. Indeed, increased heme levels have been reported to promote cholesterol metabolism. For instance, ALA treatment increases cholesterol levels and restores membrane fluidity in a model of cholesterol-deficient LSEC [53]. Similarly, exogenous heme supplementation promotes cholesterol buildup and lipid droplets production, the main sites of cholesterol storage, in a breast cancer cell model [111]. However, heme deficiency caused by ALAS or UROD inhibition has also been associated with increased expression of genes involved in steroid metabolism and cholesterol biosynthesis, respectively [82, 112]. Also, FECH-deficient mice and patients affected by AIP display hyperlipidaemia and altered systemic cholesterol homeostasis [113, 114]. Overall, these findings highlight the complex relationship between heme synthesis, heme availability, and cholesterol regulation. This evidence supports the idea that fluctuations in heme synthesis and levels can affect cholesterol homeostasis, with the outcome likely depending on the delicate balance between these opposing effects.

Beyond its role as a cofactor for CYP51A1, heme can also modulate cholesterol metabolism at the transcriptional level. In particular, heme can suppress cholesterol synthesis and uptake through the binding of the transcription factor REV-ERBα [79, 108, 115].

Also, HO-1 activity, by modulating intracellular heme levels, could influence cholesterol metabolism too. Indeed, previous study identified increased cholesterol synthesis and efflux in HO-1 overexpressing astroglia [116]. Moreover, HO-1 induction by pharmacological inducers (i.e. ALA supplementation) or viral gene transfer successfully attenuated the progression of atherosclerotic plaques in hypercholesterolemic animal models [117, 118].

Another possible explanation of how heme regulates cholesterol synthesis and homeostasis lies in its intersection with multiple metabolic pathways. Indeed, ALAS, by consuming succinyl-CoA, configures as a central metabolic hub in cell metabolism, influencing TCA cycle intermediates availability and shuttling into cataplerotic pathways, including citrate and acetyl-CoA for cholesterol synthesis [43]. In line with this, the ability of ALA to increase cholesterol levels could be associated with its function in modulating TCA cycle flux [53, 101].

Moreover, we recently identified FLVCR1a as a key modulator of cholesterol synthesis and homeostasis in EC by influencing ALAS activity and TCA cycle. The targeting of FLVCR1a increases cholesterol synthesis and accumulation in multiple EC types, highlighting the critical role of FLVCR1a in maintaining endothelial cholesterol homeostasis. Consistent with the essential role of FLVCR1a in controlling cholesterol homeostasis, FLVCR1 expression is rapidly modulated upon cholesterol depletion [119]. Moreover, hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA synthase 1 (HMGCS1), the first enzyme in the cholesterol biosynthetic pathway, has been identified as an essential gene in FLVCR1a-deficient cells [120]. Mechanistically, we propose that the reduced ALAS activity upon FLVCR1a-targeting could, at least in part, contribute to the observed increased TCA cycle flux and enhanced acetyl-CoA entry into the mevalonate pathway [43, 53]. In agreement with this, inhibition of the mitochondrial citrate transporter restored cholesterol synthesis in FLVCR1a-deficient EC [53]. In line with alterations in the TCA cycle and changes in acetate utilization, ketogenesis, another acetyl-CoA–consuming pathway, was found increased in FLVCR1a-deficient tumor-EC [24].

Alterations in lipid metabolism were also addressed upon FLVCR2 deletion in pulmonary EC. Increased lipid uptake and elevated levels of lipid species – including medium- and long-chain fatty acids within triglycerides (TG) and phosphatidylethanolamines – were observed following endothelial FLVCR2 targeting. These changes likely represent a compensatory response to impaired mitochondrial metabolism, supporting a role also for FLVCR2 in regulating lipid handling in EC [59]. Further studies are needed to clarify how FLVCR proteins contribute to the modulation of lipid metabolism and how heme and choline are involved in these processes.

Collectively, these data highlight a crucial role of heme synthesis and homeostasis in regulating cholesterol metabolism. However, the numerous conflicting observations on heme-dependent regulation of lipid homeostasis may reflect the interplay between ALAS activity—and thus de novo heme synthesis—and the intracellular heme pool in controlling cholesterol levels. Further studies are needed to clarify the role of heme metabolism in regulating cholesterol homeostasis during angiogenesis. In addition, the contribution of porphyrin intermediates to cholesterol regulation remains poorly understood and may be particularly relevant in the context of porphyria.

Heme and porphyrins in the tumor microenvironment

Growing evidence led to the concept of a “porphyrin overdrive” state as distinct feature of multiple cancer types. Indeed, porphyrin accumulation has been reported in several cancer types, including liver and prostate cancer, and has been suggested to associate with tumor adaptation and progression [33, 34, 121–123] (Table 1). Interestingly, patients with acute hepatic porphyrias (AHP) having elevated urinary levels of ALA, PBG and other uroporphyrins, display an increased risk of developing primary liver cancer [124, 125]. In this pathological context, high porphyrin levels seem to be associated with the formation of a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment [125]. Consistent with an emerging role of porphyrins in cancer, a preliminary human case–control study suggests that faecal porphyrin levels may serve as a sensitive, non-invasive biomarker for prostate cancer [127].

In line with this, genes regulating porphyrin and heme biosynthesis/homeostasis, are frequently upregulated in cancer (e.g. acute myeloid leukemia, lung cancer, uveal melanoma). For instance, recent transcriptomic analyses revealed enrichment of porphyrin biosynthetic pathways in metastatic in uveal melanoma (UM), suggesting that rewiring of porphyrin metabolism may be associated with disease progression [123]. Consistently, higher HMBS expression correlates with poor prognosis in lung cancer patients [34]. Conversely, FECH levels were often found downregulated in cancer cells compared to normal cells, reflecting the high requirement of porphyrins in the TME [35, 43, 126]. This is further supported by CRISPR–Cas9 in vivo metabolic screens, which identified porphyrin metabolism genes (i.e., HMBS, CPOX) as metabolic dependencies in pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PDAC) [121]. Importantly, porphyrin accumulation was not restricted to cancer cells but was also observed in other components of the TME. For instance, metabolomic analysis of EC isolated from glioma patient tumor specimens revealed increased porphyrin levels [33, 35]. Nevertheless, the discrepancy between in vitro and in vivo metabolic requirements should be considered when studying porphyrin “overdrive” in the TME [121].

Why tumors accumulate porphyrins, and how this contributes to disease progression, remains an open question. A possible explanation is related to maintain sufficient heme levels within TME. Indeed, previous work highlighted that cells with a high rate of heme synthesis can establish a metabolic symbiosis with neighbouring cells by shuttling porphyrin intermediates likely to sustain adequate intra-tumoral heme synthesis [121]. In this view, we also demonstrated that EC can actively participate in the release of porphyrin intermediates and heme, thus possibly contributing to the modulation of the porphyrin metabolic landscape of the TME [29]. Moreover, endothelial heme dynamics emerge as a critical determinant of the metabolic landscape of neighbouring cancer cells. Indeed, we previously demonstrated that targeting FLVCR1a in TEC induces metabolic reprogramming in neighbouring cancer cells. Specifically, FLVCR1a-deficient TEC display increased synthesis and release of ketone bodies (e.g., β-hydroxybutyrate) within the TME, which modulate the oxidative metabolism of neighbouring cancer cells [24].

Remarkably, targeting porphyrin overdrive has been proposed as metabolic vulnerability in cancer. Indeed, ALA supplementation was effective in reducing proliferation of multiple cancer cell types [29, 127]. The growth-inhibitory effect of ALA on cancer cells was accompanied by increased sensitivity to metformin, an inhibitor of mitochondrial Complex I, suggesting that ALA-treated cells might become highly dependent on OXPHOS [127].

Collectively these findings support the idea that porphyrin overdrive is a crucial event in cancer development. Furthermore, the available literature indicates that endothelial heme dynamics can shape TME and drive cancer cell behaviour and metabolism, both by directly affecting the porphyrin/heme balance and by releasing metabolites and/or angiocrine factors [128, 129].

Overall, targeting heme and porphyrin metabolism within the TME may reveal exploitable metabolic vulnerabilities that could help restrain tumor growth and angiogenesis. Moreover, this strategy may indirectly affect tumor perfusion by altering tumor vascular function, thereby shaping nutrient and oxygen availability.

Conclusions

By the present review, we aim to provide a comprehensive overview of the literature on heme in EC and during the angiogenic process, spanning from its canonical role as a cofactor in endothelial hemoproteins to its emerging functions in gene transcription, cell metabolism and modulation of angiogenesis (Fig. 2).

Strategies aimed at promoting “safe” heme synthesis (i.e., without causing porphyrin accumulation), as well as enhancing heme trafficking or catabolism, may represent promising approaches to stimulate angiogenesis in conditions characterized by vascular loss, such as myocardial infarction, cerebral ischemia, or impaired wound healing. On the other hand, inhibition of heme synthesis or driving porphyrin accumulation may represent complementary approaches to dampen pathological angiogenesis. Current porphyrin-based therapies are employed in PDT to treat ocular neovascularization diseases, in which synthetic porphyrins (i.e. verteporfin) act as photosensitizers to render angiogenic EC sensitive to light irradiation [86]. However, based on the evidence discussed here, porphyrin overdrive per se may represent a valuable alternative strategy to modulate aberrant angiogenesis, avoiding discomfort and side effects associated with PDT [29].

Despite the advances in the field of heme metabolism and vascular development, many molecular mechanisms remain poorly uncovered in EC, as the importance of heme metabolism in modulating angiogenic signalling, the interplay between heme and RNA biology, as well as the biological relevance of endogenous porphyrins.
