Tetrodotoxin (TTX) reshapes the functional potential of the gut microbiome in juvenile tiger pufferfish (Takifugu rubripes) across salinity gradients
Authors: Mai A. Wassel, Yoko Makabe-Kobayashi, Md Mehedi Iqbal, Can Huang, Masafumi Amano, Aika Shimizu, Mary Anne E. Mandario, Tomohiro Takatani, Yoshitaka Sakakura, Koji Hamasaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe gut microbiota of aquatic organisms responds dynamically to environmental stressors such as salinity fluctuations. However, how microbial communities respond to combined environmental and dietary stressors, and how these interactions influence functional potential, remains incompletely understood. Here, we investigated whether dietary administration of tetrodotoxin (TTX), a neurotoxin naturally accumulated by juvenile tiger pufferfish (Takifugu rubripes), alters gut bacterial community composition and functional potential across salinity gradients.ResultsJuvenile T. rubripes were reared under four salinity conditions (34.0, 17.0, 8.5, and 2.1 ppt) and fed either a control or TTX-containing diet (1.22 MU/g). Integrated 16S rRNA gene amplicon and shotgun metagenomic analyses revealed that salinity was the primary driver of gut microbiota structure, with only 5.1% of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) shared across salinity levels. In contrast, TTX ingestion induced salinity-dependent shifts in specific bacterial taxa rather than broad community restructuring. Core taxa, including Arcobacteraceae, Mycoplasma, Brevinema, and Vibrio, were consistently detected across treatments but exhibited pronounced changes in relative abundance and functional potential under salinity and toxin stress. Metagenomic profiling indicated that Arcobacteraceae encode genetic modules for amino acid and B vitamin biosynthesis that are absent or incomplete in the host genome, suggesting metabolic complementarity. TTX ingestion reduced the genetic representation of these biosynthetic pathways at specific salinities, particularly those associated with Arcobacteraceae. Conversely, phenylalanine biosynthesis potential enriched in TTX-fed fish, primarily associated with Vibrio spp., indicating a possible microbial functional adaptation to toxin administration. Despite these microbiome and functional shifts, TTX ingestion did not affect host growth.ConclusionsDietary neurotoxin administration reshaped gut microbiome functional profiles in a salinity-dependent manner, highlighting microbiome plasticity and improving our understanding of host–microbiota–environment interactions relevant to aquaculture health management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-026-00572-7.

Introduction

The gut microbiota plays essential roles in animal physiology, contributing to nutrient acquisition, immune development, and protection against pathogens [1–5]. In aquaculture species, stable gut microbial communities are associated with improved growth performance, reduced stress-related conditions, and enhanced disease resistance [6–8]. Because aquatic vertebrates are continuously exposed to dynamic environmental conditions, their gut microbiota is particularly sensitive to stressors such as salinity fluctuations, temperature changes, and chemical exposure [9–14]. Consequently, gut microbial composition and function may reflect both host physiological status and environmental adaptation.

The tiger pufferfish Takifugu rubripes represents an excellent model for investigating host–microbe–environment interactions. This species exhibits ecologically specialization, substantial economic value in aquaculture, and a fully sequenced genome [15–17]. Juvenile T. rubripes naturally inhabit estuarine environments characterized by pronounced salinity fluctuations [18–23], and exposure to extreme low salinity (≤ 5 ppt) impairs growth and osmoregulatory performance [24–26]. Despite these well-characterized physiological responses, the microbiome dynamics underlying salinity adaptation remain incompletely understood.

Uniquely, T. rubripes accumulates tetrodotoxin (TTX), a potent neurotoxin obtained through dietary sources [27, 28]. TTX is thought to confer ecological advantages during early life stages by deterring predation [29, 30]; however, the microbial consequences of chronic dietary TTX administration remain largely unexplored. Previous studies have characterized baseline gut microbiota diversity and spatial organization in pufferfish [31–37], and some evidence suggests associations between specific bacterial taxa and TTX accumulation [37]. Our previous research demonstrated that dietary TTX alters bacterial community structure and predicted functional pathways in juvenile T. rubripes [38]. However, that study relied exclusively on 16S rRNA gene sequencing, which provides limited resolution for accurate inference of strain-level gene repertoires and the specific metabolic functions contributed by individual bacterial taxa. Moreover, separate fish cohorts were used for different salinity conditions, precluding robust evaluation of interactive effects between salinity and TTX.

To address these limitations, we combined 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing with shotgun metagenomic sequencing to characterize gut bacterial composition and functional potential under controlled salinity and dietary conditions. Specifically, we aimed to (1) determine how dietary TTX modulates gut bacterial communities across salinity gradients; (2) identify salinity-sensitive functional pathways, particularly those involved in nutrient biosynthesis; and (3) pinpoint metabolic functions altered by TTX ingestion. By integrating microbial gene repertoires with host genomic data, we further evaluated potential metabolic complementarity between host and microbiome. Collectively, this study provides a comprehensive framework for understanding how environmental and dietary stressors jointly shape gut microbiome structure and function in marine fish, with implications for enhancing resilience and health management in aquaculture systems.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and fish husbandry

In June 2023, hatchery-reared, non-toxic juvenile tiger pufferfish (Takifugu rubripes; mean body length 39.6 mm; mean weight ~1.6 g) were obtained from the Nagasaki Fishery Public Corporation (Sasebo, Nagasaki, Japan). Upon arrival at Nagasaki University’s Graduate School of Integrated Science and Technology on June 30, the fish were transferred to eight experimental tanks (30 fish per tank) equipped with a recirculating aquaculture system (RAS) at 34.0 ppt salinity (Fig. S1). Fish were maintained at 25 °C with a continuous oxygen supply and were fed a commercial control diet (Otohime C1; 580–840 μm, Marubeni Nisshin Feed, Tokyo, Japan) twice daily to satiation. On Day 0 (July 2), six tanks were gradually acclimated from 34.0 ppt to 17.0 ppt over 2 days. Subsequently, four tanks were adjusted to 8.5 ppt (over 2 days), and two tanks reached 2.1 ppt via a transitional step at 4.3 ppt (over 2 days). Target salinities (34.0, 17.0, 8.5, and 2.1 ppt) were achieved by Day 6 (July 8). This experimental design resulted in relatively rapid salinity transitions (6 days total). In natural estuarine environments, transitions typically occur more gradually; therefore, these results reflect acute acclimation responses rather than gradual environmental adjustment. From Day 8 (July 10) onward, half of the tanks at each salinity were fed a TTX-containing diet (1.22 MU/g = 268.4 ng/g), formulated according to Sakakura et al. [26] and Amano et al. [30], while the other half received the control diet. The experiment continued for an additional 10 days, concluding on Day 18 (July 20).

This experimental design permitted only 10 days of dietary TTX administration. In natural environments, T. rubripes is chronically exposed to TTX through prey consumption throughout its life; therefore, our short-term (10-day) results may not fully reflect long-term ecological and host–microbiota interactions.

Sample collection and processing

Five individuals were sampled at the initial sampling time point (IS; before salinity adjustment), followed by 2–3 individuals per tank on Days 7, 12, and 18 (Fig. S1). Fish were euthanized with 200 ppm MS-222 (Sigma), and their body weight and standard length were recorded. Intestinal tracts were dissected aseptically and stored at −80 °C. Gut contents were extracted using sterile syringes with Tris−EDTA buffer (100 mM Tris−HCl, 200 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) and stored at −20 °C for DNA analysis [38–40]. TTX concentrations in TTX-fed fish were quantified by LC-MS/MS following the protocols of Nakashima et al. [41] and Gao et al. [42].

Rearing water (1 L per tank), fecal material collected by sterile siphoning, and subsamples of control and TTX-containing diets were collected. Liquid samples were filtered through 0.22 μm membranes (Sterivex-GP or Millex units, Merck Millipore, Germany), and all samples were stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing

DNA was extracted from gut contents, feed, water, and fecal samples using the ISOIL DNA Isolation Kit (Nippon Gene Co., Ltd., Japan), with modifications, and DNA quality was verified as described by Wassel et al. [38]. The V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primers 515F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) [43]. Each 25 µL PCR reaction contained 3 µL of template DNA (60 ng/µL), 1 µL of each primer (10 µM), 2.5 µL of PCR-grade water, 12.5 µL of Platinum™ SuperFi™ PCR Master Mix, and 5 µL of SuperFi™ GC Enhancer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Thermocycling conditions were as follows: 94 °C for 3 min; 28 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 57 °C for 45 s, and 72 °C for 1 min; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Triplicate PCR reactions were pooled, verified by agarose gel electrophoresis, purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Inc., Beverly, MA, USA), and quantified using the Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen). Indexed libraries were prepared using the Nextera® XT Index Kit v2 (Illumina) and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform (600 cycles; 2 × 300). A total of 115 samples were sequenced.

Amplicon sequence processing and statistical analysis

Raw reads were processed using QIIME2 (v2022.2, https://qiime2.org/) [44], following the methods of Iqbal et al. [45] and Wassel et al. [38]. Sequences were quality filtered, denoised, and rarefied to 24,601 reads per sample. Rarefaction curves were generated to assess sequencing depth and diversity coverage.

Microbial analyses were conducted in R (v2022.02.3) using phyloseq, microbiome, vegan, and ggplot2 packages [46–51]. Alpha diversity (Chao1 and Shannon indices) was compared using ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post hoc test. Beta diversity was calculated using Bray–Curtis and unweighted UniFrac distances, visualized using PCoA and NMDS, and tested with PERMANOVA (999 permutations) [52, 53].

Differentially abundant genera between control and TTX-fed groups were identified using ANOVA and linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) (LDA score > 3.5, p < 0.05) [54]. Heatmaps and core microbiome plots were generated using qiime2R, tidyverse, and MicrobiomeAnalyst 2.0 [55–57]. UpSet plots depicting shared and group-specific ASVs across salinity and dietary treatments were generated using the UpSetR package (v1.4.0), with intersections ordered by frequency. Venn diagrams were created using Venny 2.1.0 [58].

Growth performance and toxin accumulation

Growth parameters (standard length (mm) and body weight (g)) and TTX accumulation (MU/fish) were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc comparisons [59, 60].

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing

For each treatment, DNA from three individuals was pooled (5 µL per sample) for shotgun metagenomic sequencing. To enrich bacterial DNA and reduce host DNA contamination, the NEBNext® Microbiome DNA Enrichment Kit (Cat. No. E2612L, New England Biolabs Inc., USA) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions [61]. DNA quality was assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, the Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit, and agarose gel electrophoresis. Libraries were prepared using the MGIEasy PCR-Free DNA Library Prep Kit (MGI) with 1 µg input DNA and were quality-checked using an Agilent 4200 TapeStation with a High Sensitivity DNA chip (Agilent Technologies, USA). Paired-end sequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ-G400 platform (MGISEQ-2000RS; GeneBay, Inc., Japan).

Metagenomic assembly, annotation, and functional profiling

Forward and reverse FASTQ sequences were processed using the MAPLE Submission Data Maker (MSDM v1.03; JAMSTEC) pipeline for quality filtering, read assembly, and translation into amino acid sequences. Filtering criteria included a minimum base quality score of Q20, ≥ 80% high-quality bases per read, and a minimum merged read length of 400 bp, resulting in high-quality amino acid (AA) sequences with a minimum length of 50 amino acids in FASTA format.

The resulting AA sequence file was analyzed using Genomaple™ v2.3.2 [62–64]. The GHOSTX engine assigned KEGG Orthology (KO) identifiers to amino acid sequences using the KEGG GENES database [65]. The T. rubripes reference genome (49,630 protein-coding sequences, 96.1% ribosomal completeness) was retrieved from NCBI and used for host−microbiome comparisons. KO-assigned genes were mapped to KEGG functional modules, and Genomaple computed module completion ratios (MCRs), Q-values, and taxonomic abundance at the individual taxonomic rank (ITR) level, following Takami et al. [62, 63].

Host–microbiome functional complementarity analysis

The comprehensive functional profiles (functionomes) of the T. rubripes genome and gut bacterial metagenomes were compared to explore potential metabolic complementarity across salinities (34.0, 17.0, 8.5, and 2.1 ppt) under both control and TTX-fed conditions.

KEGG modules incomplete in the host genome (MCR < 100%) were identified. Subsequently, modules that exhibited 100% MCR and Q-values below 0.5 in the gut microbiome datasets were selected to determine bacterial contributions to host metabolic functions. Bubble plots were generated using Python-based libraries: ‘numpy’ [66] and ‘pandas’ [67] for data handling and numerical operations, and ‘seaborn’ [68] and ‘matplotlib’ [69] for visualization. Histograms of module-level functional profiles were generated using MAPLE Graph Maker (v2.3.1; JAMSTEC) to visualize differences between control and TTX-fed fish.

Results

Dynamic shifts in bacterial composition and diversity across salinity levels and time

Bacterial composition shifted substantially across the experimental timeframe and salinity gradient (Fig. 1A; Table S1). At the initial sampling (IS; 34.0 ppt), the dominant genera were uncultured Arcobacteraceae (61.5 ± 21.7%) and Rubritalea (23.3 ± 18.8%), both of which declined sharply by D7. By D18, Arcobacteraceae reappeared across salinities, while Mycoplasma dominated at the lowest salinity (2.1 ppt) (Fig. 1A).

Two-way ANOVA revealed significant effects of both salinity and sampling day on alpha diversity (Fig. S2). Chao1 richness was influenced by salinity (p < 0.05), while both Chao1 and Shannon indices were affected by sampling day (p < 0.05). Shannon diversity declined from D7 to D18 in 34.0, 17.0, and 8.5 ppt groups, but not in the 2.1 ppt group (Fig. S2B). Beta diversity analysis using unweighted UniFrac distances revealed significant effects of salinity, sampling day, and their interaction (PERMANOVA, p < 0.01) (Fig. S3).

The Venn diagram analysis showed pronounced salinity-specific bacterial communities: 189 ASVs were unique to 34.0 ppt, 219 to 17.0 ppt, 45 to 8.5 ppt, and 92 to 2.1 ppt, with only 40 ASVs (5.1%) shared across all salinity levels (Fig. S4). LEfSe analysis identified salinity-associated bacterial biomarkers (Fig. S5). On D12, five ASVs were enriched at 34.0 ppt, including members of Micrococcales, Actinobacteriota, Microbacteriaceae, and Candidatus Aquiluna. At 17.0 ppt, three biomarkers from the phylum Pseudomonadota were detected, namely Alteromonadales, Alteromonadaceae, and Glaciecola. The 8.5 ppt and 2.1 ppt groups were characterized by unique ASVs from the phyla Bacteroidota (NS3a marine group) and Firmicutes (Bacillaceae and Bacillales), respectively (Fig. S5A). On D18, distinct biomarkers were observed at 34.0 ppt (two Pseudomonadota ASVs) and 17.0 ppt (one Bacteroidota ASV) (Fig. S5B).

TTX accumulation and growth performance

TTX was detected in all TTX-fed juveniles across salinity levels, confirming successful ingestion (Fig. S6). TTX accumulation did not differ significantly among salinity treatments (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p > 0.05). Importantly, dietary TTX administration did not significantly affect growth performance (standard length or body weight) within each salinity level (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p > 0.05).

Effects of TTX ingestion on bacterial community composition

Comparison of control and TTX-fed fish revealed salinity-dependent shifts in bacterial genus abundance (Fig. 1). At D12, pronounced differences between treatments were observed. At 34.0 and 17.0 ppt, Arcobacteraceae decreased from 41% to 21% and from 38% to 17%, respectively, accompanied by an increase in Brevinema. In contrast, at 8.5 and 2.1 ppt, Arcobacteraceae increased from 14% to 48% and from 15% to 27%, respectively, with corresponding declines in other genera. By D18, community patterns shifted further. At 8.5 ppt, this trend reversed, with Brevinema increasing from 12% to 57% and Arcobacteraceae declining. At 2.1 ppt, Arcobacteraceae increased from 30% to 71%, accompanied by reductions in other dominant taxa (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis of dominant genera revealed that at D12, no significant differences were observed between control and TTX-fed groups at 34.0, 17.0, or 2.1 ppt (Fig. S7A). However, at 8.5 ppt, the TTX-fed group showed a significantly higher relative abundance of uncultured Arcobacteraceae (UAB) compared with the control group (ANOVA, p < 0.05). By D18, no significant differences were detected between treatments at 34.0 or 17.0 ppt (Fig. S7B). At 8.5 ppt, the pattern changed, with the TTX-fed group exhibiting a significantly higher relative abundance of Brevinema and a lower abundance of Arcobacteraceae compared with the control group (p < 0.05). At 2.1 ppt, TTX administration was associated with a significant increase in uncultured Arcobacteraceae and significant decreases in Vibrio and Brevinema relative to the control group (p < 0.05).

LEfSe analysis identified multiple ASVs that were differentially enriched between control and TTX-fed fish across salinity levels (Fig. 2; Table S2). At 34.0 ppt (D12), TTX-fed fish were characterized by enrichment of ASVs affiliated with Bacteroidota (e.g., Chitinophagales, Tenacibaculum), Verrucomicrobiota (e.g., Puniceicoccaceae), Planctomycetota (e.g., SM1A02, uncultured Planctomycetaceae), Pseudomonadota (e.g., Rhodobacteraceae, Thalassobius, Enterobacter, Nautella, and Francisella), and Spirochaetota (Brevinema). In contrast, the NS3a marine group and Rubritalea were enriched in control fish. At 17.0 ppt, TTX-fed fish showed enrichment of ASVs belonging to Planctomycetota (Rubinisphaera), Bacteroidota (Polaribacter), Verrucomicrobiota (Haloferula), and Actinomycetota (Candidatus Aquiluna), whereas uncultured Chlamydiaceae (Chlamydiota) and Nautella (Pseudomonadota) were more abundant in the control group. At 8.5 ppt, TTX-fed fish were enriched in Pseudomonadota, particularly members of Vibrionaceae (Vibrio ASVs), Alteromonadaceae (Alteromonas), Oceanospirillaceae (Marinomonas), and uncultured Arcobacteraceae, whereas Mycoplasma (Mycoplasmatota) was associated with control fish. At 2.1 ppt, TTX administration was strongly associated with enrichment of Actinomycetota (Candidatus Aquiluna) and a Planctomycetota-related ASV (uncultured Pirellulaceae), whereas Vibrio (Pseudomonadota) was enriched in the control group (Fig. 2A). By D18, the LEfSe profiles indicated a shift in discriminatory taxa across salinity levels (Fig. 2B; Table S2). At 34.0 ppt, TTX-fed fish were enriched in multiple ASVs affiliated with Pseudomonadota (e.g., Francisella, Alteromonas, Pseudohongiella, and Enterovibrio), Planctomycetota (Blastopirellula, SM1A02, and Lentibacter), Verrucomicrobiota (Persicirhabdus, Rubritalea, and Roseibacillus), and Actinomycetota (Candidatus Aquiluna), in addition to several uncultured lineages, including members of Cellvibrionaceae, Methyloligellaceae, Micavibrionaceae and Rubinisphaeraceae. In contrast, control fish were characterized by enrichment of Glaciecola, Gammaproteobacteria, and a Rhodobacteraceae-related ASV. At 17.0 ppt, most discriminatory taxa were enriched in control fish, including ASVs affiliated with Glaciecola, Rhodobacteraceae, Nautella, Pseudomonas, Maribacter, Acinetobacter and Lentibacter, while TTX-fed fish showed enrichment primarily of Planctomycetota (SM1A02) and an uncultured Micavibrionales-related ASV. At 8.5 ppt, TTX-fed fish were characterized by enrichment of Brevinema (Spirochaetota) and a Rhodobacteraceae-related ASV, whereas control fish were strongly associated with two ASVs affiliated with uncultured Arcobacteraceae (Campylobacterota). At 2.1 ppt, TTX-fed fish showed enrichment of several ASVs, including Vitellibacter, Fimbriimonadaceae, Mesorhizobium, Reyranella, Romboutsia, Devosiaceae, Cetobacterium, and two ASVs affiliated with uncultured Arcobacteraceae. In contrast, control fish were characterized by enrichment of Vibrio, Cetobacterium, Candidatus Aquiluna, Gemmata, and a Planctomycetota-related ASV (OM190). Overall, by Day 18 (D18), TTX-associated differences in the gut microbiome were strongly salinity-dependent, with TTX-fed fish showing enrichment of diverse taxa, including several low-abundance and uncultured bacterial lineages.

Heatmap analyses revealed clear differences in bacterial community composition between control and TTX-fed fish at both D12 and D18 (Fig. 3; Table S3). On D12, significant shifts in gut microbiota were observed between control and TTX-fed fish at each salinity level (Fig. 3A). At 34.0 ppt, TTX-fed fish showed higher abundances of Pir4_lineage (Pirellulaceae), Blastopirellula, Muricauda, Rhodobacteraceae, Marinobacter, Marinomonas, Alteromonas, EC3 (Gammaproteobacteria), Maritalea, Pseudohongiella, uncultured Chitinophagales, uncultured Rubinisphaeraceae, and Nautella. At 17.0 ppt, Rubinisphaera, Saccharimonadales, Chryseolinea, Haloferula, Rhizobiaceae, Pir4_lineage (Pirellulaceae), Blastopirellula, Candidatus Aquiluna, EC3, Persicirhabdus, Aquibacter, Maritalea, the NS3a marine group, and Cetobacterium were more abundant in TTX-fed fish. At 8.5 ppt, increased abundances of Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, Pseudoalteromonas, Marinobacter, Alteromonas, and Marinomonas were observed in TTX-fed fish, whereas at 2.1 ppt, Pir4_lineage (Pirellulaceae), Candidatus Aquiluna, uncultured Chitinophagales, and Glaciecola were enriched in the TTX-fed group. On D18, heatmap analysis continued to demonstrate pronounced differences in gut microbiota composition between control and TTX-fed fish across salinity levels (Fig. 3B). At 34.0 ppt, Pseudomonas, Aeromonas, uncultured Gimesiaceae, Rubritalea, Blastopirellula, uncultured Methyloligellaceae, and Persicirhabdus were more abundant in TTX-fed fish. At 17.0 ppt, Fusibacter and Cetobacterium dominated the gut microbiota of TTX-fed fish. At 8.5 ppt, TTX-fed fish exhibited higher abundances of the NS3a marine group, Candidatus Aquiluna, Flavobacteriaceae, Rhodobacteraceae, Rhizobiales, Mesorhizobium, Acinetobacter, Bradymonadales, Marinomonas, Photobacterium, Stenotrophomonas, Cetobacterium, and Brevinema. At 2.1 ppt, Gordonia and Mesorhizobium were enriched in TTX-fed fish.

The UpSet analysis revealed clear patterns of group-specific and shared ASVs across control and TTX-fed fish at different salinity levels (Fig. 4A). The largest intersections corresponded to ASVs unique to individual salinity–treatment groups. Among TTX-fed fish, the 34_TTX group harbored the highest number of unique ASVs (135), followed by 2.1_TTX (60 ASVs). In the control groups, 17_Control contained 108 unique ASVs, while 2.1_Control harbored 49 ASVs. Beyond single-group uniqueness, several intersections of moderate size (approximately 6–13 ASVs) were observed, representing ASVs shared among subsets of salinity levels within the same dietary treatment or across both control and TTX-fed groups. Smaller intersections (≤ 5 ASVs) reflected highly specific overlaps among limited combinations of salinity and treatment conditions. To further characterize these shared taxa, core microbiome analysis was performed and visualized as a heatmap (Fig. 4B). Despite the overall variability, a small set of ASVs—belonging to the genera Mycoplasma, uncultured Arcobacteraceae, Brevinema, and Vibrio—was consistently shared across multiple salinity levels and treatments.

Distinct microbial communities in the fish gut and rearing environment

The bacterial compositions of rearing water and feed were distinct from those of the fish gut across both control and TTX-treated groups. Rearing water samples were consistently dominated by Pseudomonadota and Bacteroidota, followed by Verrucomicrobiota, Actinobacteriota, and Patescibacteria (Fig. S8). Key genera included members of Glaciecola, Rhodobacteraceae, and Rubritalea (Fig. S9). Feed microbiota were dominated by Pseudomonadota and Firmicutes, with prominent genera including Paracoccus, Photobacterium, Lactobacillus, Stenotrophomonas (Figs. S8; S9C). Complementary metabolic potential between the host genome and gut microbiome

Comparative KEGG module analysis demonstrated that the T. rubripes host genome lacks complete modules (MCR = 100%) for multiple amino acid biosynthetic pathways (Table 1A; Fig. S10). Complete modules were absent for ornithine (25%), chorismate (0%), leucine (0%), cysteine (0%), methionine (14.3%), lysine (0%), threonine (0%), and phenylalanine (0%), while isoleucine (40%) and valine/isoleucine (25%) pathways were also incomplete in the host genome. In contrast, the gut microbiome exhibited substantial genetic capacity for these pathways under several salinity conditions. At 34.0 ppt (control), complete modules (MCR = 100%) were detected for most amino acid biosynthesis pathways, including ornithine, chorismate, leucine, cysteine, methionine, lysine, and threonine. Similar patterns were observed at 8.5 ppt (control), where multiple amino acid biosynthetic modules again reached full completion.

A comparable pattern was observed for vitamin-related pathways (Table 1B; Fig. S10). The host genome lacked complete modules for biotin (0%) and riboflavin (25%) biosynthesis. By contrast, the microbiome achieved complete module representation (MCR = 100%) for these pathways at 34.0 ppt and 8.5 ppt. NAD biosynthesis (60% in the host) and C1-unit interconversion (33.3% in the host) were partially represented in the host genome but frequently exhibited higher completion levels in the microbiome depending on salinity.

Importantly, module completion ratios reflect the presence of required gene sets but do not directly indicate transcriptional activity, protein expression, metabolite production, or host utilization. Therefore, these findings indicate metabolic potential rather than confirmed metabolic activity.

Salinity-dependent variation in microbial metabolic potential

Across salinity gradients, the gut microbiome displayed pronounced variation in its metabolic potential for biosynthetic pathways that were incomplete or absent in the host genome (Table 1; Fig. S10). At full-strength seawater (34.0 ppt), microbial communities showed broad representation of amino acid biosynthesis modules, including ornithine (M00028), chorismate (M00022), branched-chain amino acids such as leucine (M00432) and valine/isoleucine (M00019), sulfur-containing amino acids including cysteine (M00021) and methionine (M00017), lysine via the succinyl-diaminopimelate pathway (M00016), and threonine (M00018). At physiologically isosmotic salinity (8.5 ppt), most of these modules remained fully represented. In contrast, at intermediate salinity (17.0 ppt), fewer modules achieved complete representation. At the lowest salinity (2.1 ppt), complementary amino acid biosynthesis modules were not detected at complete levels.

Vitamin biosynthesis pathways exhibited a similar salinity-dependent pattern (Table 1B; Fig. S10). Biotin (M00123) and riboflavin (M00125) biosynthesis modules reached full completion at 34.0 ppt and 8.5 ppt, whereas module completion declined at 17.0 ppt. At 2.1 ppt, vitamin-related biosynthetic modules were not detected at complete levels. In contrast, NAD biosynthesis (M00115) and C1-unit interconversion (M00140) were present at 8.5 ppt but showed reduced completeness at 34.0 ppt and 2.1 ppt.

TTX-associated changes in metabolic potential

TTX ingestion were associated with reduced completeness of complementary biosynthetic modules across salinities. At 34.0 ppt, all amino acid biosynthetic modules identified in control fish were incomplete in TTX-fed fish (Table 1A; Fig. S10A). Similar reductions were observed at 17.0 and 8.5 ppt. Notably, however, phenylalanine biosynthesis at 17.0 ppt was enriched in TTX-fed fish relative to controls, representing a clear exception to the overall declining trend. In addition, riboflavin and C1-unit interconversion at 8.5 ppt remained represented (Table 1A; Fig. S10).

Taxonomic contributors to biosynthetic functions

Taxonomic assignment of functional genes revealed that Arcobacter spp. (Campylobacteriota; Epsilonproteobacteria) and Mycoplasma sp. were the dominant contributors to enriched metabolic pathways in control fish. Arcobacter species were particularly associated with amino acid biosynthesis pathways, including chorismate, cysteine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, ornithine, threonine, and valine/isoleucine (Fig. 7A). In cofactor and vitamin metabolism, both Arcobacter and Mycoplasma spp. contributed substantially to the detected pathways (Fig. 7B).

TTX ingestion was associated with alterations in both taxonomic composition and functional contributions, with reduced Arcobacter abundance coinciding with increased abundance of other bacterial taxa (Figs. 8 and 9). Notably, phenylalanine biosynthesis was enriched in TTX-fed fish and was primarily associated with Vibrio spp. At 34.0 and 8.5 ppt, a reduced prevalence of Epsilonproteobacteria and the emergence of alternative bacterial contributors were observed, corresponding to changes in the biosynthetic profiles (Figs. 8 and 9).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that TTX ingestion significantly alters the gut microbiota of juvenile T. rubripes in a salinity-dependent manner. Although environmental salinity and host physiology are well-established determinants of bacterial community structure, this study specifically examined how dietary TTX modulates bacterial composition and metabolic potential across varying osmotic conditions. Using shotgun metagenomic sequencing and comparative genomic analyses, we show that TTX perturbs specific bacterial taxa and reshapes key metabolic pathways, particularly those involved in amino acid and vitamin biosynthesis. These microbial shifts may have implications for host physiology and stress tolerance, providing new insight into toxin–microbiome interactions in marine organisms.

Temporal and salinity-driven bacterial dynamics

The gut microbiota of juvenile T. rubripes underwent pronounced restructuring over time and across salinity treatments, highlighting the plasticity and environmental sensitivity of intestinal bacterial communities (Fig. 1). At the initial sampling (IS) under full-strength seawater (34.0 ppt), the microbiota was dominated by uncultured Arcobacteraceae and Rubritalea, taxa previously reported as core members of the T. rubripes gut microbiome [33, 37, 38]. However, these dominant taxa declined sharply by Day 7 across all salinity treatments, indicating rapid community restructuring. This shift likely reflects a combination of host developmental changes in immune function and intestinal physiology, salinity-driven selective pressures, and competitive interactions among bacterial lineages. By Day 18, Arcobacteraceae partially re-emerged across all salinity treatments, suggesting ecological resilience and a sustained role in the T. rubripes gut microbiota [33, 37, 38]. In contrast, Mycoplasma became the dominant genus at the lowest salinity (2.1 ppt), replacing Arcobacteraceae. This shift may reflect a selective advantage for Mycoplasma under hypoosmotic conditions, although alternative explanations such as competitive exclusion of salinity-sensitive taxa or host immune responses favoring Mycoplasma persistence cannot be excluded.

Alpha diversity metrics supported these compositional changes. While Chao1 richness primarily responded to salinity gradients, both richness and Shannon diversity were influenced by temporal dynamics (Fig. S2). Most treatment groups (34.0, 17.0, and 8.5 ppt) exhibited substantial declines in Shannon diversity between Day 7 and Day 18, consistent with ecological succession toward more specialized or stable microbial communities. In contrast, the 2.1 ppt treatment maintained relatively stable diversity, possibly reflecting the establishment of a low-diversity but well-adapted microbial community under extreme hypoosmotic conditions. Beta diversity analysis further supported these patterns at the community level. Using unweighted UniFrac distances, salinity, time, and their interaction significantly influenced bacterial community composition (PERMANOVA, p < 0.01; Fig. S3). These findings indicate that microbiota assembly in T. rubripes is shaped by the combined effects of environmental osmotic stress and host developmental processes rather than by a single dominant factor. The extent of salinity-driven differentiation was striking: only 5.1% of ASVs were shared across all treatment groups, whereas 94.9% were treatment-specific (Fig. S4). This pattern suggests that osmotic stress functions as a strong ecological filter structuring microbial community assembly. LEfSe analysis further identified distinct salinity-associated bacterial biomarkers with differing functional capacities (Fig. S5). At 34.0 ppt, communities were enriched in Actinobacteriota and Candidatus Aquiluna, taxa commonly associated with oligotrophic or oxidative-stress environments [70]. In contrast, lower-salinity conditions (8.5 and 2.1 ppt) favored Bacteroidota and Firmicutes, groups known for their capacity to degrade complex polysaccharides and produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [71, 72]. These findings suggest that salinity not only selects for osmotic tolerance but also shapes functional specialization within the gut microbiota. High-salinity communities may rely on oligotrophic resource conservation strategies, whereas lower-salinity communities may exploit carbohydrate-rich substrates that are potentially more available under hypotonic conditions. Such niche differentiation could contribute to the improved growth observed in fish maintained at 8.5 ppt salinity. This salinity-mediated assembly pattern is not unique to T. rubripes. Euryhaline fishes, including striped catfish, black molly, Chinook salmon, and Nile tilapia, all exhibit marked bacterial turnover linked to salinity exposure [9, 10, 73, 74], and similar shifts have been documented in mandarin fish [11]. These convergent patterns across diverse host species suggest that osmotic stress represents a fundamental ecological force shaping microbiota assembly in aquatic vertebrates.

TTX-induced compositional shifts across salinity gradients

The administration of TTX led to clear alterations in the bacterial community composition, with the degree and direction of change varying depending on salinity and time post-administration. These findings indicate that the microbial response to TTX is strongly modulated by environmental salinity. At the genus level, uncultured Arcobacteraceae increased in TTX-fed fish at 8.5 ppt on D12 and at 2.1 ppt on D18, accompanied by declines in Vibrio and Brevinema (Fig. S7). This pattern is consistent with previous observations under low-salinity conditions [38], supporting the hypothesis that hyposaline environments may promote Arcobacteraceae proliferation in the presence of TTX. However, this trend reversed at 8.5 ppt by D18, when Arcobacteraceae declined and Brevinema increased. Such temporal shifts suggest that the effects of TTX are not only salinity-dependent but also time-sensitive, reflecting the dynamic nature of host–microbiome interactions under toxic stress. The dynamics of Brevinema are particularly noteworthy. While some studies associate Brevinema with beneficial metabolic functions, including acetogenesis, hemicellulose degradation, and nitrogen fixation in herbivorous invertebrates [75–77], other reports link this genus to growth retardation [78], intestinal disorders [79], and pathogenicity in terrestrial hosts [80]. The fluctuating abundance of Brevinema across treatments therefore suggests a potential role in microbial community restructuring in response to both environmental and toxicological stressors. UpSet analysis revealed substantial salinity- and treatment-specific microbial composition, with TTX-fed groups consistently exhibiting higher numbers of unique ASVs than control groups (Fig. 4A). Nevertheless, several ASVs affiliated with Mycoplasma, Arcobacteraceae, Brevinema, and Vibrio were consistently detected across salinity levels and treatments (Fig. 4B).

Core microbiota resilience and potential functions

Four bacterial genera, Arcobacteraceae, Mycoplasma, Brevinema, and Vibrio, persisted across all treatment conditions (Fig. 4B), suggesting ecological importance and potential functional relevance within the gut ecosystem of juvenile T. rubripes. This consistency aligns with earlier research documenting microbiota stability and functional roles in T. rubripes [33, 37, 38]. Their persistence, regardless of salinity variation or TTX ingestion, highlights their resilience and suggests that these taxa represent core members of the host gut ecosystem. Consequently, they may serve as promising candidates for future investigation as biomarkers or targets for microbiome-based management strategies in pufferfish aquaculture. Among these taxa, Arcobacteraceae exhibited particularly dynamic abundance patterns in response to both salinity and TTX administrations. Members of the phylum Campylobacterota are widely distributed among marine organisms [81–84] but are especially prevalent in the gut microbiota of T. rubripes [33, 37, 38]. Their fluctuating abundance across treatments suggests sensitivity to environmental and toxicological conditions, while maintaining a persistent ecological role within the gut microbial community. Mycoplasma, belonging to the phylum Mycoplasmatota, showed increased relative abundance in artificial seawater at lower salinities, potentially reflecting sensitivity to osmotic stress. Members of this genus are commonly found in marine fish microbiomes and are often described as “simple but successful parasites” due to their highly reduced genomes and streamlined metabolic capabilities [85–88]. Despite these characteristics, Mycoplasma has also been reported to form mutualistic associations in salmonid hosts [89], indicating that their ecological role may vary depending on host physiology and environmental context. Brevinema (phylum Spirochaetota) has been detected in both wild and farmed Takifugu species [34, 90–92], as well as in TTX-bearing clams and freshwater fish [93–96]. In diseased seahorses (Hippocampus kuda), this genus has been identified as a conditional pathogen associated with intestinal disorders [79]. However, Brevinema is also reported as a free-living bacterium widely distributed in marine environments [97, 98], suggesting that it may exhibit dual ecological roles ranging from commensalism to opportunistic pathogenicity depending on host condition and environmental factors. Members of the genus Vibrio (phylum Pseudomonadota) were particularly prevalent under brackish salinity conditions, reflecting their well-known ecological flexibility and tolerance to fluctuating environmental parameters. Vibrio species can function as either beneficial symbionts or opportunistic pathogens in aquaculture systems, and their presence in fish microbiomes often requires careful monitoring due to their potential dual roles in host health and disease [99–101].

Host selection and control of core taxa

A striking separation between gut microbiota and the microbial communities associated with rearing water, feed, and feces (Fig. 5) indicates that the host exerts strong selective pressure on its intestinal bacterial community independent of TTX treatment. The limited impact of TTX on environmental microbiota further suggests that the toxin primarily influences gut communities through host–microbe interactions rather than through direct modification of surrounding microbial environments. Although the precise mechanisms underlying microbial selection in T. rubripes remain incompletely understood, several host-mediated processes are known to regulate microbial colonization in fish. The host immune system, particularly mucosal immune responses involving specialized lymphocytes and immunoglobulin-like molecules, plays a central role in controlling microbial composition within the gastrointestinal tract [102, 103]. In addition, intestinal mucus—composed primarily of mucins and glycoproteins—acts as both a physical barrier and a chemically selective habitat that can promote or inhibit microbial adhesion and growth [104, 105]. Host pattern-recognition receptors, including Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptors (NLRs), further contribute to microbial regulation by recognizing conserved microbial-associated molecular patterns and initiating immune responses that shape community structure [106, 107]. Moreover, physicochemical conditions within the fish gut—such as pH gradients, oxygen availability, digestive enzyme activity, and bile acid concentrations—create strong ecological filters that favor well-adapted microbial taxa while excluding poorly adapted lineages [108, 109]. Together, these host-derived selective mechanisms likely explain the persistence of Arcobacteraceae, Mycoplasma, Brevinema, and Vibrio across diverse salinity conditions and TTX treatments. Their consistent presence suggests that these taxa possess physiological or ecological traits that enable them to tolerate host immune defenses and successfully colonize the gut environment, potentially contributing to stable host–microbiome interactions within T. rubripes.

Functional complementarity: amino acid biosynthesis

Beyond compositional changes, comparative KEGG module analysis revealed strong functional complementarity between the T. rubripes genome and its gut microbiome, particularly in amino acid biosynthesis (Table 1A; Fig. S10). Bacterial genes contributed substantially to the biosynthesis of ornithine, chorismate, leucine, isoleucine, cysteine, methionine, lysine, and threonine— nutrients essential for growth, immune competence, and metabolic homeostasis in T. rubripes. This metabolic partnership reflects an increasingly recognized principle across aquatic organisms: gut microbiota frequently supplement host nutritional requirements by providing essential metabolic products [110–116]. Bacterial amino acids represent far more than simple nutritional supplementation. Once absorbed by the intestinal epithelium, these metabolites contribute not only to protein synthesis but also to energy balance, protein turnover, and serve as precursors for various bioactive compounds [110–114]. For instance, bacterial production of ornithine has been associated with enhanced growth and improved intestinal health in fish [115, 116]. Additionally, bacterial synthesis of chorismate through the shikimate pathway may compensate for the host’s inability to produce aromatic amino acids (AAAs), including phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan [117, 118]. This metabolic gap-filling is particularly significant in an organism dependent on dietary sources for these essential nutrients. Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), particularly leucine and isoleucine, represent another important component of this host–microbiome metabolic interaction [119–126]. These amino acids play central roles in protein synthesis, muscle development, and energy metabolism—processes that are especially critical in rapidly growing juvenile fish. Other amino acids also perform specialized physiological functions. Cysteine, for instance, has been shown to support intestinal morphology and modulate microbiota composition in golden pompano (Trachinotus ovatus) [127], suggesting a reciprocal relationship in which improved gut structure facilitates microbial colonization and stability. Methionine plays key roles in cellular metabolism and epigenetic regulation through its involvement in methylation pathways [128–130], potentially influencing host gene expression under environmental stress conditions. Lysine and threonine, often limiting in commercial aquaculture feeds, can also be synthesized by gut microbes and contribute to protein synthesis, immune responses, and the maintenance of intestinal mucosal integrity [123–126, 131]. These functions are particularly important because a healthy mucosal barrier helps prevent pathogen invasion while maintaining a stable and selective environment for beneficial microbial communities.

Functional complementarity: vitamin and cofactor metabolism

In addition to amino acids, bacterial metabolic activity contributed to the biosynthesis of several vitamins and cofactors essential for host physiology. Comparative KEGG analysis identified microbial genes associated with the biosynthesis of biotin and riboflavin (Table 1B; Fig. S10), two vitamins involved in energy metabolism, antioxidant defense, and immune function. Biotin (vitamin B7) functions as a coenzyme in carboxylation reactions central to fatty acid and lipid metabolism, and its deficiency has been linked to impaired growth and reduced immune competence in aquatic animals [132, 133]. Riboflavin (vitamin B2) plays a critical role in cellular respiration and oxidative metabolism and has been associated with improved immune responses and reduced oxidative stress in fish [134–136]. The presence of these biosynthetic pathways within the gut microbiota suggests that T. rubripes may partially rely on microbial vitamin production to meet its metabolic requirements—an advantageous trait in aquatic environments where dietary vitamin availability may fluctuate.

Bacterial contributions to cofactor production also extend beyond vitamin biosynthesis. Notably, genes associated with the production of NAD (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) were detected. NAD is a central redox cofactor involved in numerous dehydrogenase reactions and plays a pivotal role in cellular energy metabolism. It is synthesized from niacin (vitamin B3), which can be obtained through diet or microbial metabolism [137, 138]. Bacterial NAD biosynthesis may therefore help maintain stable cofactor availability under conditions where dietary supply fluctuates— an especially important factor for juvenile fish with high metabolic demands. The detection of NAD biosynthetic modules within the microbiota suggests that bacterial metabolism may contribute to host energy balance and oxidative stress regulation [137, 138], processes that are particularly relevant under conditions of osmotic stress and TTX ingestion. Furthermore, bacterial genes involved in C1-unit interconversion pathways were detected. These pathways support the biosynthesis of nucleotides and amino acids while also participating in methylation reactions that regulate gene expression and epigenetic processes [139]. The presence of these metabolic functions suggests that the microbiota may influence not only nutrient availability but also the regulatory pathways controlling host metabolism and gene expression under changing environmental conditions.

Arcobacter and Mycoplasma species as key metabolic contributors

Recent studies have identified several core gut bacterial taxa in pufferfish [33–38]. However, the specific symbiotic microorganisms contributing to host-associated metabolic processes, as well as the functional roles they perform, remain poorly understood. Among the bacterial taxa contributing to biosynthetic functions, Arcobacter spp. and Mycoplasma sp. were particularly prominent (Fig. 7). Despite historical associations of Arcobacter with pathogenicity [140–143], its presence in T. rubripes appeared non-pathogenic and potentially beneficial, with evidence suggesting a mutualistic or commensal relationship with the host. Our findings indicate that Arcobacter species contributed substantially to the biosynthesis of amino acids and B vitamins. These results are consistent with studies in other marine organisms, where Arcobacter has demonstrated capabilities in sulfur oxidation, carbon fixation, and even resistance to viral infections [81, 144–146]. Such functions extend beyond basic amino acid production and may contribute to broader metabolic resilience within host-associated microbial communities. Mycoplasma species, which are commonly found in the gut microbiota of fish [85], demonstrated equally important biosynthetic capacities. Their ability to produce amino acids, detoxify ammonia, and generate acidic environments through lactic and acetic acid production supports both nutrient acquisition and pathogen suppression [86, 87, 147, 148]. Ammonia accumulation can impair neurological function and impose physiological stress on the host; therefore, ammonia detoxification by Mycoplasma may reduce a significant metabolic burden on developing juvenile fish. Additionally, the inverse correlation observed between Mycoplasma abundance and pathogenic Vibrio spp. highlights its potential protective role in maintaining intestinal health [94, 149].

Functional disruption of bacterial metabolism by TTX

While the core microbiota and its metabolic partnerships appeared stable under some salinity conditions, TTX ingestion fundamentally disrupted this functional landscape. Shotgun metagenomic analysis revealed that TTX ingestion significantly altered the metabolic potential of the gut microbiome in a salinity-dependent manner. In particular, TTX suppressed bacterial pathways involved in the biosynthesis of key amino acids, including chorismate, cysteine, methionine, lysine, and threonine, as well as several B vitamins and cofactors such as biotin, riboflavin, and NAD (Table 1; Fig. S10). These disruptions were closely associated with a reduced abundance of Arcobacter species, which were identified as major contributors to these metabolic pathways (Figs. 8 and 9). This pattern suggests a mechanistic link whereby TTX-induced compositional shifts directly compromise the metabolic capacity of the microbial community. The interaction between TTX and salinity revealed a more complex pattern. At low salinity (2.1 ppt), both control and TTX-fed fish exhibited reduced genetic potential for essential metabolic pathways (Table 1), indicating that salinity alone may constrain microbiome functionality. This observation highlights the multi-stressor nature of the experimental conditions, in which osmotic stress independently limits bacterial metabolic flexibility. Interestingly, although the relative abundance of Arcobacteraceae increased under TTX ingestion at lower salinity, this increase did not correspond to enhanced functional capacity. This dissociation between taxonomic abundance and functional output suggests context-dependent interactions among TTX administrations, environmental salinity, and microbial activity. In such cases, bacterial taxa may remain present but become metabolically constrained or physiologically impaired, limiting their ability to express key biosynthetic pathways.

Interestingly, despite the general suppression of biosynthetic capacity, the phenylalanine biosynthetic pathway was enriched in TTX-fed fish, potentially driven by the proliferation of Vibrio (Fig. 8). This selective enrichment is notable, as previous studies have identified members of the class Gammaproteobacteria—such as Vibrio and Shewanella—as potential producers of TTX [150]. The proliferation of these taxa following TTX administration may indicate a positive feedback mechanism in which TTX favors microorganisms capable of producing or tolerating the toxin. Members of the family Vibrionaceae are also known to synthesize diverse bioactive secondary metabolites, suggesting that the microbiome may mount an adaptive metabolic response to TTX-induced stress [151]. Given that phenylalanine functions both as an antioxidant and as a precursor for neurotransmitter synthesis [152, 153], the observed enrichment of phenylalanine biosynthesis may represent a microbial response aimed at mitigating oxidative stress and maintaining physiological balance under TTX administrations. Rather than reflecting a simple loss of microbial function, these results suggest that the gut microbiota may attempt compensatory metabolic adjustments in response to toxin-induced physiological demands. Nevertheless, the overall suppression of other amino acid and vitamin biosynthesis pathways indicates that this compensation remains incomplete and insufficient to fully offset the metabolic disruption caused by TTX. This incomplete compensation may explain why growth performance was not significantly affected by TTX administration despite clear evidence of microbiome-level metabolic perturbation. The host may possess sufficient metabolic buffering capacity or receive adequate nutritional supplementation from the diet to compensate for microbial functional losses. Alternatively, the physiological endpoints measured in this study may not have been sensitive enough to detect subtle metabolic consequences of microbiome disruption. Together, these findings highlight that toxin ingestion reshapes not only microbial composition but also the functional architecture of the gut microbiome in a context-dependent manner.

Study limitations and future research

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Shotgun metagenomic analyses were conducted on pooled gut DNA from a limited number of individuals per treatment, which precluded the assessment of inter-individual variation and reduced the statistical power to detect functional differences among treatments. In addition, functional predictions were based on gene presence inferred from reference genomes and therefore represent potential metabolic capacity rather than confirmed in vivo activity. Consequently, the detection or reduction of biosynthetic pathways should not be interpreted as direct evidence of changes in metabolite production or host utilization. Furthermore, the relatively short experimental duration and the use of a single TTX concentration limit the ability to infer long-term microbiome adaptation or evaluate potential dose-dependent effects under natural exposure scenarios. Future studies should incorporate greater biological replication, extended experimental periods, and a range of TTX concentrations to better characterize microbiome responses under environmentally relevant conditions. Integrating multi-omics approaches—including metatranscriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics—will be particularly important for linking microbial gene content with functional expression and host physiological outcomes. Such integrative approaches will help clarify whether observed genomic shifts translate into measurable metabolic activity and will be essential for elucidating the mechanistic basis of TTX-mediated microbiome restructuring and its implications for host health across variable environmental conditions. Addressing these limitations will be critical for advancing our understanding of toxin–microbiome interactions and their ecological and physiological significance in marine organisms.

Conclusions

This study provides new insights into how environmental and dietary factors interact to shape the gut microbiota of Takifugu rubripes. Our results demonstrate that tetrodotoxin (TTX) ingestion significantly alters both the composition and functional potential of the gut microbial community, with these effects strongly modulated by environmental salinity. Distinct, salinity-dependent shifts in bacterial community structure indicate that environmental conditions influence how dietary toxins affect host–microbe interactions. Because T. rubripes naturally consumes TTX-bearing prey, its gut microbiota is likely exposed to this neurotoxin throughout its life in the wild. Consequently, the patterns observed in this study likely reflect ecologically relevant host–microbe dynamics rather than purely experimental phenomena. Metagenomic analyses further revealed that the gut microbiota contributes key metabolic functions complementary to host physiology, particularly in the biosynthesis of amino acids and B vitamins essential for growth, immunity, and metabolic homeostasis. TTX ingestion disrupted several of these biosynthetic pathways, likely through reductions in beneficial taxa such as Arcobacteraceae, potentially affecting nutrient acquisition, immune regulation, and detoxification capacity. At the same time, evidence of compensatory microbial responses was observed, including increased contributions to phenylalanine biosynthesis, suggesting that the microbiome may partially adjust to TTX-induced physiological stress. However, this compensatory response appeared limited and strongly dependent on environmental salinity. Comparisons with rearing water and feed microbiota demonstrated that the gut microbial community is clearly distinct from environmental microbial sources, indicating strong host-mediated selection. The minimal effects of TTX on environmental microbiota further support the conclusion that TTX primarily reshapes gut communities through host-driven processes rather than direct environmental alteration. Collectively, these findings support our hypothesis that TTX ingestion induces salinity-dependent restructuring and functional reprogramming of the gut microbiome. Monitoring core bacterial taxa and their metabolic potential may therefore provide useful indicators of fish health and microbial stability in aquaculture systems. Nevertheless, while this study identifies substantial shifts in microbial functional potential, the direct physiological consequences for the host remain unresolved and warrant further investigation.
