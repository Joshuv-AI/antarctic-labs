Dynamics of H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 histone modifications in response to powdery mildew infection in common wheat
Authors: Guoliang Chen, Shaoshuai Liu, Ziying Wang, Yuqing Che, Tianhua Chen, Zhongyin Deng, Dada Cui, Xinyu Zou, Wanxin Xu, Xingchen Kong, Huabao Chen, Junshan Qi, Long Mao, Aili Li, Shuaifeng Geng, Min Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPowdery mildew, caused by the biotrophic fungus Blumeria graminis f. sp. tritici (Bgt), is one of the major pathogens that reduces wheat yield. Epigenetic regulation underlying wheat defense against Bgt infection, however, remains poorly characterized. Here, we conducted an integrative transcriptomic and histone H3 modification analysis on wheat leaves infected by mixed races of Bgt.ResultsRNA-seq revealed extensive transcriptional reprogramming in wheat leaves upon Bgt infection, with significant changes in a number of functional genes including those associated with histone modification. CUT&Tag analysis of two key activating histone marks, H3K4me3 and H3K36me3, showed an overall decrease in both modifications after infection. Nevertheless, H3K4me3 modification was notably increased in promoter regions, whereas H3K36me3 peaks were more enriched in the genic regions, indicating that Bgt infection induced a redistribution of these active histone marks. Integrative analysis revealed that only a subset of genes exhibited concordant changes between transcriptional levels and either H3K4me3 or H3K36me3 enrichment. Defense-related genes, including TaARF4, TaWRKY19, and TaCIPK19, were consistently down-regulated at both transcriptional and histone modification levels after Bgt infection, while TaCHIT8, TaPOD, and Calreticulin (TaCRT) were up-regulated. Consistently, virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) of TaCIPK19, TaWRKY19, and TaARF4 enhanced resistance to Bgt, whereas silencing of TaCRT and TaCHIT8 increased susceptibility.ConclusionOur integrative analysis shows that Bgt infection induces widespread transcriptional changes and alters the distribution of active histone marks, H3K4me3 and H3K36me3. Together, our results suggest that Bgt infection triggers coordinated epigenetic and transcriptional reprogramming, exemplified by defense-related genes such as TaCIPK19, TaWRKY19, and TaARF4, whose roles in wheat resistance was confirmed by VIGS assays.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09192-4.Keywords: Wheat, Powdery mildew, Histone modification, Omics

Background

Common wheat (Triticum aestivum) is one of the staple crops that provides up to 20% of human calorie intake [1]. Powdery mildew, caused by Blumeria graminis f. sp. tritici (Bgt), is a major fungal disease affecting wheat yield production [2, 3]. While chemical and biological methods can help mitigate the impacts of this disease, utilizing effective disease resistance genes remains the most economical, efficient, and environmentally friendly strategy [4]. As of now, over 240 wheat Bgt resistance genes/loci have been designated, over 60 major genes (Pm1-Pm71) or alleles located on 18 different chromosomes [5–7]. For instance, Pm12 [8], Pm21 [9], Pm24 [10, 11] and Pm36 [12] have exhibited excellent Bgt resistance. Nevertheless, continuous exploration and characterization of novel Bgt resistance genes remain essential for sustaining durable resistance in wheat.

Histone methylation is a key modification of the epigenetic mechanisms that regulate chromatin structure and gene accessibility [13]. It can occur in any of the components of the nucleosome octamer, although it primarily targets lysine residues. For H3 histones, the fourth lysine (H3K4) and the 36th lysine (H3K36) are the main methylation targets [14]. Histone trimethylation marks (H3K4me3 and H3K36me3) have been extensively utilized to investigate the epigenetic regulation of development, vernalization, biotic and abiotic stress tolerance, as well as nitrogen use efficiency in plants such as Arabidopsis, rice and wheat [15–22]. For instance, H3K27me3 and H3K36me3 are crucial in regulating vernalization response and its maintenance and resetting in winter wheat [23].

Over the past few decades, a number of "omics" techniques have become effective technologies for plant systems. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) advancements have opened the door for a new generation of omics, including transcriptomics and genomics. Transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) is a valuable technique for understanding gene expressions in plants and plays a significant role in the field of stress response research. While single-omics approaches (like genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, or metabolomics) have provided valuable insights into specific aspects of these responses, they often fall short in capturing the full complexity [24, 25]. A single omics approach is insufficient to systematically explain the mechanisms of plant stress responses, making the combination of multi-omics an inevitable trend for future development. Thus, joint analysis of the transcriptome and epigenome has garnered increasing interest.

CUT&Tag (Cleavage under targets and Tagmentation) is a relatively popular genomics approach that maps the genomic site of chromatin modifications [26] using a Tn5 transposase. With Tn5, users can precisely cleave DNA at specific genomic sites identified by a particular chromatin characteristic, eliminating the requirement for sonication or crosslinking [27]. Prior research has shown that CUT&Tag is an appealing substitute in many circumstances because it provides higher specificity, higher signal to noise ratios, requires fewer cells as input, and can be more affordable than ChIP-Seq [26]. Multi-omics enables new functional gene discovery and regulatory network resolution. For instance, CUT&Tag technology was used to investigate the enrichment of H3K4me3 where the H3K4me3 level was strongly positively correlated with soybean recombination [28]. Transcription and chromatin dynamics during early regeneration of immature endosperm were analyzed using RNA-seq, ATAC-seq and CUT&Tag technologies and genes like TaDOF5.6 and TaDOF3.4 were found to significantly enhance the efficiency of genetic transformation in various wheat varieties [29]. Transcriptome and epigenome profiling were also performed across key endosperm developmental stages to investigate regulatory mechanisms, where wheat abscisic acid insensitive 3-A1 (TaABI3-A1) was isolated and shown to participate in the coordinated regulation of starch and seed storage protein synthesis [30]. Despite these advancements, understanding of epigenetic mechanisms underlying wheat disease infection process remains limited.

Here, we conducted a comparative analysis of the dynamic transcriptional and epigenetic changes in response to Bgt infection in the susceptible wheat variety Chinese Spring. By integrating CUT&Tag and RNA-seq analysis, we comprehensively examined transcriptomic and epigenomic alterations underlying wheat responses to Bgt infection. The results significantly expanded our understanding of the mechanisms about how wheat adapts to Bgt infection.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and Bgt treatment

Common wheat Chinese Spring (CS) variety was grown in growth chambers set at 22 °C/16-h in the light and 18 °C/8-h in the dark conditions. For Bgt infection, wheat seedlings at the two-leaf stage were inoculated with Bgt race E09 by gently brushing diseased wheat leaves over the test plants, ensuring uniform deposition of conidiospores on leaf surfaces [12]. After inoculation, the plants were placed in a high-humidity chamber (> 95% RH) in the dark for 24 h to promote spore germination and appressorium formation [31]. Thereafter, plants were returned to the growth chamber under the above-mentioned condition. Leaf tissues were collected at 24 h post-inoculation from both Bgt-inoculated and mock-treated plants for RNA-seq and CUT&Tag analysis. Three biological replicates per condition.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the fourth leaves using the Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). A total of 1 μg RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (RR047A, Takara, Dalian, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (RR820A, Takara) following the manufacturer’s protocol. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene [32, 33]. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Each sample included three biological replicates.

RNA-seq and CUT&Tag experiment

Total RNA was extracted from wheat leaf tissues using Trizol reagent. RNA concentration was measured with the Qubit® RNA Assay Kit and Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer, while integrity was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit (Agilent Technologies, California, USA). Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform, generating paired-end reads of 150 bp.

CUT&Tag experiment was performed according to the user manual of Hyperactive® Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, TD903). The plant nuclei were extracted with HBM buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, 0.44 M sucrose, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.1% Triton, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 2 mM spermine, 1 mM PMSF, EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail). Nuclei were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific to H3K4me3 and H3K36me3. The samples were then incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h and washed twice with wash buffer. Add 2 μL of pA/G-Tnp to 98 μL of Dig-300 buffer and mix. Incubate the mixture at room temperature for 1 h. After washing twice with Dig-300 buffer, add 5X TTBL and incubate at 37 °C for 1 h to fragment the DNA. Finally, extract the DNA using beads and construct the library.

RNA-seq data processing

Reference genome sequence (IWGSC RefSeq v2.1) was downloaded from URL (https://urgi.versailles.inrae.fr/download/iwgsc/IWGSC_RefSeq_Assemblies/v2.1/) [34]. Hisat2 (v2.0.5) was used to compare with the reference genome, and Samtools (v1.5) was used for sorting the compared files. Quantitative analysis was performed using feature Counts (v2.0.1) software with the parameter’- p- P- B- C’. Differential analysis was performed using DESeq2 software, with thresholds set to corrected P value < 0.05 and abs (log2FoldChange) > 1. Cluster Profiler R package was used for enrichment analysis. All expression genes with FPKM > 1 in at least one sample were used for PCA using the prcomp function in R, with default settings. We calculated Pearson correlation coefficients between pairwise samples using the FPKM values of all expressed genes.

CUT & Tag data processing

For CUT&Tag data, raw paired-end reads were trimmed to remove adapters and low-quality bases, then aligned to the wheat reference genome (IWGSC RefSeq v2.1) [34] using the BWA-MEM algorithm (v0.7.17) with the “-M” flag to mark shorter split alignments as secondary. The initial alignments were filtered to retain high-quality, uniquely mapped read pairs. Specifically, SAMtools (v1.9) was used to remove reads with low mapping quality (MAPQ < 30), improperly paired reads, and potential PCR duplicates. Remaining duplicate reads were further removed by Picard MarkDuplicates (Picard v2.20.5) to obtain a set of non-redundant unique fragments for each sample. Biological replicate BAM files (post-deduplication) were then merged for downstream analysis. To visualize H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 profiles, normalized coverage tracks (BigWig files) were generated using deepTools bamCoverage with RPKM normalization and a 10 bp bin size. Histone modification peaks were identified using MACS2 (v2.1.2): H3K4me3 was called in narrow-peak mode (reflecting its promoter enrichment) with input DNA or IgG controls as background (q < 0.05), whereas H3K36me3 was called with the –broad option (q < 0.05) due to its broad gene-body distribution. In parallel, SEACR (v1.3) was applied as an alternative CUT&Tag-optimized peak caller at a stringent threshold of 0.05. Consensus peak sets were defined as the intersection of peaks identified by both replicates and both peak callers. Peaks were annotated using ChIPseeker [35], with promoter regions defined as 3 kb upstream of the transcription start site (TSS). For peak–gene association analysis, genes located within gene bodies or within 4 kb upstream or downstream of gene bodies were linked to their nearest peaks. Genes associated with differential histone modifications after infection were then compared with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from RNA-seq [36, 37] to identify candidates exhibiting coordinated transcriptional and epigenetic responses.

BSMV-mediated gene silencing

To investigate the roles of candidate genes in the interaction between wheat and Bgt, we employed a barley stripe mosaic virus (BSMV)-mediated gene silencing (VIGS) system [38]. BSMV:γ was used as the empty vector control, and BSMV:PDS (phytoene desaturase) served as a positive control for virus infection. All BSMV vectors were mechanically inoculated onto the second leaf of wheat seedlings following previously published protocols. A mock treatment was performed by rubbing the leaves with 1 × GKP buffer (50 mM glycine, 30 mM K₂HPO₄, 1% bentonite, 1% celite). After inoculation, seedlings were misted with DEPC-treated water and kept at 100% humidity in the dark at 25 °C for 24 h to promote infection. The plants were then transferred to a standard growth chamber under controlled conditions. At 9 days post-inoculation, plants inoculated with BSMV:PDS displayed photobleaching symptoms, indicating successful virus infection. At this point, the fourth leaf was collected for gene silencing efficiency assessment via qRT-PCR, after which Bgt conidiospores were inoculated. Disease symptoms were evaluated 12 days after fungal inoculation. Each treatment included ten biological replicates.

Biomass analysis

To quantify fungal biomass, DNA was extracted from infected wheat leaves at 12 days post-inoculation using the CTAB method. Fungal biomass was determined by calculating the ratio of Bgt Actin gene copy number to wheat Actin gene copy number in 100 ng of total DNA using real-time PCR [39]. The same qPCR reagent (TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II, Takara) and 2−ΔΔCt method were used. Each treatment was conducted with three biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was used for single-factor experiments, whereas two-way ANOVA followed by Šidák’s multiple comparisons test was applied to experiments involving two independent variables. P values are denoted as * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.001, *** P < 0.0001, ns, not significant. Data is presented as mean ± SD.

Results

Transcriptome analysis of wheat leaves after Bgt infection

RNA-seq was conducted on RNA isolated from Bgt-inoculated and mock-treated leaves collected at 24 h after infection (Fig. 1A), so as to ensure that observed differences reflect treatment effects rather than temporal variation. After removing adapter sequences and low-quality reads, a total of 280 million clean reads were retained, with over 90% of reads passing Q30, indicating good sequencing quality (Table S2). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation between Bgt-inoculated and control samples, confirming the reliability of the biological replicates and indicating strong transcriptional divergence at 24 h (Fig. S1). Analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified 2,275 downregulated and 2,705 upregulated genes in response to Bgt infection (Fig. 1B). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that the downregulated DEGs were mainly enriched in lipid metabolic process, signal transduction, DNA-templated transcription initiation, and hormone response (Fig. 1C). By contrast, upregulated DEGs were enriched in translational elongation, chitin catabolic processes, signal peptide processing, and cellular amino acid biosynthetic process (Fig. 1D).

Bgt infection altered expression levels of H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 regulators

To investigate whether H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 modifications are involved in wheat's response to Bgt, we analyzed the expression of methyltransferases and demethylases using transcriptome data. The heat-map indicated that Bgt infection markedly altered the expression of these epigenetic regulators. SET domain genes (SDGs) associated with histone methylation have been studied in various plant species [40]. Notably, the expression of histone methyltransferase genes TaSDG27 (TraesCS5B03G0442200) and TaSDG2 (TraesCS7A03G0684200) (H3K4me3) along with TaSDG7 (TraesCS5D03G0440400) and TaSDG8 (TraesCS2A03G0013900) (H3K36me3) was significantly suppressed [41]. Conversely, the expression of histone demethylases displayed opposite patterns. TaJMJ11 (TraesCS1A03G0342300), which removes H3K4me3 trimethylation marks [42], showed reduced expression (Fig. 1E). TaJMJ710 (TraesCS7B03G0917600), which removes H3K36me3 trimethylation marks [43], showed increased expression (Fig. 1E). Together, these results suggest that Bgt infection influences wheat responses by dynamically regulating both H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 modifications through the altered expression of their methyltransferases and demethylases.

Dynamic changes in H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 histone modifications during Bgt infection

We then performed a CUT&Tag analysis to evaluate the roles of H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 histone modifications during Bgt infection. To capture epigenetic events in the early host response, we chose CS leaves that were infected by Bgt infection for 24 h, a time when haustoria form and nutrient uptake and effector delivery by the pathogen begin. CUT&Tag assays were performed using H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 antibodies. For H3K4me3, Qubit analysis confirmed that the DNA from both Bgt infected leaves (designated as Bgt) and the control (CK) samples met quality requirements for CUT&Tag experiments. High-throughput NGS sequencing yielded 23.2 million and 27.7 million mapped reads for Bgt and CK groups, respectively (Table S3). A total of 6,346 H3K4me3 peaks were identified in Bgt samples and 2,423 in the CK. Peak distribution analysis indicated that H3K4me3 signals were mainly enriched near transcription start sites (TSSs), and reads were concentrated around peak centers (Fig. 2A). Peaks were annotated into five genomic categories: distal, genic, 3'UTRs, 5'UTRs, and promoter regions. In CK, H3K4me3 peaks were primarily concentrated in distal regions (38.5%), followed by promoters (23.8%) and genic regions (18.3%). After Bgt infection, the proportion of peaks increased in promoters (to 28.1%) and distal regions (to 41.2%) but decreased in the genic regions (to 13.9%) (Fig. 2B). Differential peak analysis identified 4,861 upregulated and 460 downregulated H3K4me3 peaks (|log2FC|> 1, p < 0.05). Differential analysis identified 4,861 upregulated and 460 downregulated peaks (|log2FC|> 1, p < 0.05). Binding and Expression Target Analysis (BETA) predicted 4,718 upregulated and 458 downregulated target genes, located within gene bodies or within 4 kb upstream/downstream regions (Table S4). Functional categorization using MapMan revealed that many of these genes were associated with biotic stress responses (Table S6). Upregulated targets were enriched in defense-related pathways, including hormone signaling (Auxin, ABA, Ethylene, JA), cell wall modification, proteolysis, redox regulation, and transcription factors such as WRKY and MYB (Fig. S3). Genes encoding PR proteins and secondary metabolite biosynthetic enzymes were also induced, whereas only a few signaling and proteolysis-related genes were downregulated.

For H3K36me3, NGS sequencing generated 26.9 million (Bgt) and 27.1 million (CK) high-quality reads, with > 98% mapping to the reference genome. As with H3K4me3, peaks were enriched around TSSs with signal concentration at peak centers (Fig. 2A). In CK, H3K36me3 peaks were predominantly located in genic regions (36.1%) and promoters (18.4%) (Fig. 2B). After infection, the distribution shifted, with increased proportions in genic (38.7%) and distal regions (33.2%), and a decrease in promoters (14.2%). Differential analysis identified 1,585 upregulated and 507 downregulated peaks (|log2FC|> 1, p < 0.05). BETA predicted 1,513 upregulated and 507 downregulated target genes (Table S5). MapMan analysis showed that many defense-related genes were activated, including heat shock proteins, secondary metabolite biosynthetic genes, and transcription factors (e.g. WRKY, MYB) (Table S7; Fig. S4). Hormone signaling modules such as ethylene and auxin were also upregulated, while some MAPK cascade components, redox-related genes, and primary metabolism genes were downregulated. These results indicate that Bgt infection extensively reprogrammed histone-marked genes, with H3K4me3 primarily linked to rapid activation of defense pathways and H3K36me3 associated with transcriptional reprogramming of metabolic and stress-related genes.

Integration of CUT&Tag and RNA-seq data revealed epigenetically regulated target genes

Although CUT&Tag identified the genomic binding sites of H3K4me3 and H3K36me3, showing thousands of differential peaks between infected and control samples. Histone modification alone is insufficient to elucidate regulatory function. Therefore, we integrated CUT&Tag and RNA-seq datasets to identify genes with both differential expression and histone modification changes. For H3K4me3, 23 genes exhibited increased H3K4me3 signals and upregulated expression, while 32 showed decreased signals and downregulated expression (Fig. 3A and Table S8). Examples of target genes identified include Auxin response factor 4 (TaARF4), Chitinase 8 (TaCHIT8), and Peroxidase 2 (TaPOD2). For H3K36me3, 27 genes displayed coordinated upregulation of both histone modification and gene expression, and 22 genes were downregulated at both levels (Fig. 3B and Table S9). Examples of target genes identified include Calreticulin (TaCRT), TaWRKY19, and CBL-interacting protein kinase 19 (TaCIPK19), among others.

GO enrichment analysis revealed that H3K4me3 targets were mainly involved in transcriptional regulation, defense responses, and ROS metabolism (Fig. 3C). In contrast, H3K36me3 targets were enriched in lipid metabolism, protein processing, metal ion homeostasis, and ER stress-related pathways (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, peak annotation indicated that H3K4me3 signals were predominantly located in promoter regions (within 1 kb upstream of TSS), consistent with its general association with activation, whereas H3K36me3 was more commonly enriched in the gene body regions, reflecting its function in transcription elongation. These findings suggest that H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 modifications contribute to wheat responses to Bgt through distinct but complementary mechanisms. H3K4me3 is likely responsible for rapid immune gene activation, while H3K36me3 contributes to transcriptional stability of metabolic regulators.

Functional validation of candidate genes by VIGS

To investigate the biological functions of the candidate genes identified from multi-omics integrative analysis, we applied VIGS system to knock down five selected genes. At 10–12 days post-inoculation with the VIGS vector, newly emerged leaves displayed typical viral infection symptoms. In particular, BSMV:PDS-treated plants showed a characteristic photobleaching phenotype, indicating successful infection (Fig. 5A, left). qRT-PCR analysis confirmed significant downregulation of all target genes compared to the BSMV:γ control (Fig. 5B-F). Fourteen days after inoculation with Bgt, differences in disease symptoms were observed among the silenced lines. Plants with silenced TaCIPK19, TaWRKY19, and TaARF4 displayed markedly fewer Bgt colonies and reduced lesion development, whereas silencing of TaCRT and TaCHIT8 resulted in more severe disease symptoms (Fig. 5A, right and 5G). Results from quantification of fungal biomass based on the ratio of fungal to wheat genomic DNA (Fig. 5H) were consistent with phenotypic observations. Both fungal coverage and biomass were significantly reduced in TaCIPK19, TaWRKY19, and TaARF4-silenced plants, consistent with their roles as negative regulators of resistance. In contrast, TaCRT and TaCHIT8 silencing enhanced fungal growth, consistent with their roles as positive regulators of defense.

Discussion

Prior studies have indicated that epigenetic alterations can influence gene expression, genomic rearrangements, and the rate of evolution of genomic DNA sequences to adapt to disease stresses [44–46]. Particularly, posttranslational modifications of histones are critical for stress-responsive gene activation and for growth and development. Histone methylation is a crucial epigenetic modification that dynamically regulates gene expression, and its status is tightly controlled by the counteracting activities of histone methyltransferases and demethylases. In plants, this balance is largely maintained by SET domain-containing proteins (SDGs) and Jumonji C domain-containing proteins (JMJs), respectively [42]. In line with their roles in stress responses, our analysis showed that Bgt infection profoundly altered the expression of key histone modification enzymes. Specifically, TaSDG29 and TaSDG7, which mediate H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 methylation, were significantly downregulated. In contrast, the H3K4me3 demethylase TaJMJ11 was suppressed while the H3K36me3 demethylase TaJMJ710 was induced. These findings suggest that dynamic regulation of H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 marks plays an important role in the early defense against Bgt infection.

H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 are dynamically regulated in response to various biotic stresses and contribute to chromatin remodeling through changes in their spatial distribution patterns. Changes in the modification state at promoter regions of histone mark-related genes can influence the expression of genes involved in biotic stress responses [47–49]. In this work, genome-wide profiling of H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 data showed that binding sites are mainly enriched in genic and distal regions, which is consistent with previous findings [23], and this distribution pattern is closely related to gene expression and regulation. H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 are associated with active transcription at promoter regions and with transcriptional elongation, respectively. Differential enrichment of these marks in target gene regions correlates with up- or down-regulation of gene expression [50]. This pattern is distinct from transcription factors (TFs), which typically recognize specific DNA motifs [51]. H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 exhibit distinct enrichment patterns between down-regulated and up-regulated target genes, highlighting their regulatory flexibility in modulating gene expression. These dynamic histone modifications may facilitate wheat adaptation to disease stress.

CUT&Tag has significant advantages, it requires only a small amount of starting material, achieves a high signal-to-noise ratio with low background and features a simple and rapid workflow. Our integrative analysis of RNA-seq and CUT&Tag identified 23 up-regulated and 32 downregulated H3K4me3 target genes, as well as 27 up-regulated and 22 downregulated H3K36me3 target genes during Bgt infection. According to GO and KEGG pathway analyses of the H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 target genes, H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 modifications play critical roles in signal transduction and disease resistance, which is consistent with RNA-seq findings. Moreover, the status of H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 modifications were closely associated with target gene expression. Here, six genes, including TaCIPK19, TaWRKY19, TaARF4, TaCRT, TaPOD and TaCHIT8, were identified as candidates showing concordant changes in both transcriptional activity (RNA-seq) and histone modification (CUT&Tag). To validate their roles in disease resistance, we performed VIGS experiments, which confirmed that these genes contribute to wheat responses to Bgt infection. These findings provide a framework for identifying additional disease resistance genes through integrative epigenomic and transcriptomic approaches. While we prioritized the 24 h time point to capture the critical initiation of defense during haustorium formation, we recognize that the epigenetic landscape is dynamic and can vary over time. Therefore, future time-course studies are essential to distinguish between transient responses and sustained regulation, and to assess the long-term stability of these modifications.

Conclusions

In summary, our integrative analysis of RNA-seq and CUT&Tag reveals that Bgt infection induces widespread transcriptional reprogramming and dynamic redistribution of the active histone marks H3K4me3 and H3K36me3. These modifications play distinct yet complementary roles in wheat defense by directly influencing the activation or repression of stress-responsive genes. Functional validation by VIGS confirmed that key candidates, including TaCIPK19, TaWRKY19, TaARF4, TaCRT, and TaCHIT8, contribute to resistance modulation. Together, these findings highlight the importance of H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 in regulating host–pathogen interactions and demonstrate the effectiveness of multi-omics approaches for mining resistance genes and providing a foundation for future functional exploration.
