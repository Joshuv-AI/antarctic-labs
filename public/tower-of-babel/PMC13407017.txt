Introduction

Physical and mental distress is common among patients who undergo bilateral inguinal orchiectomy, the standard treatment for bilateral testicular tumors. Although curability is important, the treatment plan must also consider post‐treatment QOL parameters, such as fertility preservation.

Case Presentation

A 46‐year‐old man visited our hospital for two palpable bean‐sized hard masses in his right testis. He had no smoking or drinking history, and he had no noteworthy family or medical history. He had been divorced once but had no children.

Treatment history: Before 9 years, he was referred to our hospital by a local hospital for painless swelling of the left testis. He was subsequently diagnosed with a left testicular tumor and underwent left inguinal orchiectomy. Histopathological examination led to the diagnosis of seminoma (pT1, N0, M0, S0) (Figure 1). Postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy with carboplatin was not administered and the patient was followed up as left testicular tumor, stage I A.

The weight of the excised specimen after left inguinal orchiectomy was 50 g. The tumor size was 25 × 28 × 10 mm (↓), and the cut surface was yellowish‐white (a). The tumor was histopathologically diagnosed as seminoma (HE stain, ×200) (b).

On admission, his height was 171 cm; weight, 74 kg; blood pressure, 143/96 mg/Hg; pulse, 88 beats per min (regular); and body temperature, 36.2°C. Parameters, including the tumor makers LDH, HCG, and AFP, were all within the normal range. The testosterone and LH levels were normal at 3.2 ng/mL and 2.79 mIU/mL, respectively, but the FSH level was elevated to 11.95 mIU/mL.

Ultrasound examination revealed two internally heterogeneous masses in the right testis measuring 11 and 8 mm (Figure 2). MRI revealed low signal intensity on T2 and ADC map and high signal intensity on DWI (Figure 3). Chest and abdominal CT findings were not suggestive of distant metastasis.

Scrotal ultrasound examination showing two hypoechoic masses (11 and 8 mm) (↑) on the right testis.

MRI of the right scrotum and testis. The tumors showed lower signal intensity than the testicular parenchyma on the T2 (a: Sagittal) (→) and ADC (b: Coronal) (→) map and high signal intensity on DWI (c: Coronal) (→).

Based on the above findings, a malignant tumor could not be ruled out. As the patient had a strong desire to have children in the future, it was decided to perform testis‐sparing surgery for fertility preservation after obtaining informed consent. Preoperative semen analysis revealed a sperm concentration of 4.7 million/mL, indicating severe oligozoospermia. Therefore, washed and concentrated sperm were cryopreserved.

Surgical findings: In July 200X, under lumbar anesthesia, an approximately 4‐cm incision was made in the right scrotum and the testis was dislocated. Thereafter, the spermatic cord was clamped, and the tunica albuginea was incised, resected the both tumors along with the approximately 2–3 mm of surrounding testicular tissue, and the tissues around the tumors were sent for biopsy. The tunica albuginea was sutured with 3–0 absorbable sutures to complete the surgery. The warm ischemic time was 32 min.

Histopathological examination: The resected tumors were pathologically diagnosed as seminomas (Figure 4). The biopsy results did not indicate any GCNIS.

Two tumors after excision. The cut surfaces were yellowish‐white (a). The tumors were histopathologically diagnosed as seminomas (HE stain, ×200) (b).

Postoperative course: Although it has been only 22 months after postoperatively, no evidence of local recurrence or distant metastasis was observed. After 16 months postoperatively, the testosterone level was 2.74 ng/mL, and the LH level was 4.04 mIU/mL; however, the FSH level was high at 14.82 mIU/mL. Sexual function was preserved, but semen examination revealed the same azoospermia persisted as 1 month after surgery.

Discussion

The incidence of testicular tumors is 0.7–1.8 per 100 000, and bilateral testicular tumors, metachronous as well as synchronous, account for approximately 2% of all testicular tumors, making this a relatively rare disease. Patients with unilateral testicular tumors reportedly are at a 12–17 times higher risk of developing a tumor in the contralateral side [1, 2]. Mrinakova et al. [3] conducted a 14.9‐year follow‐up study on 2124 patients with testicular tumors and reported that 87 (4.0%) patients had metachronous testicular tumors. Of the cases with bilateral testicular tumors, 80% were metachronous and required clinical attention [4]. In a study involving 7102 patients with testicular tumors, Andreassen et al. reported that the concordance rates of histopathological findings between the unilateral and contralateral testes were 83% for seminomas and 43% for non‐seminomas [5]. Our patient also had bilateral seminomas.

Considering that testicular tumors tend to occur in young and middle‐aged men, numerous reports on the efficacy and safety of testis‐sparing surgery to preserve fertility have been published [6, 7, 8]. However, reports have shown that GCNIS occurs in many patients who underwent testis‐sparing surgery [9]. To avoid the risk of local recurrence, Patrikidou et al. [10] further limited the indications, stating that testis‐sparing surgery should only be recommended for patients with a single testis, excellent compliance, a single tumor measuring < 2 cm in the lower pole of the testis, normal preoperative endocrine function, and the ability to understand sufficient explanations.

Grogg et al. [11] reported that GCNIS was detected in 43% of 285 patients who underwent testis‐sparing surgery and that the incidence of local recurrence was significantly lower in those who received 16–20 Gray radiation therapy to the testis after surgery (2% vs. 50%, p < 0.001).

In our patient, biopsy of the tissue around the tumors did not indicate GCNIS; although it has been only 22 months after postoperatively, strict follow‐up will be necessary, and postoperative follow‐up will be similar to that for typical testicular tumors. In addition, we believe that MRI and ultrasound examinations of the testis should be performed.

Patients who have developed testicular tumors should be informed of the importance of self‐palpation of the contralateral testis to ensure timely detection as in our case, wherein detection helped perform timely testis‐sparing surgery. However, in our case, spermatogenesis could not be maintained despite the preservation of sexual function. Patients with testicular tumors often experience male infertility before treatment, with 6%–24% having azoospermia and 50% having oligozoospermia [12]. Heidenreich et al. [9] reported that 62 of 73 patients who underwent testis‐sparing surgery did not require testosterone supplementation, but only 5 were able to have children. Our patient had severe oligozoospermia before surgery. Although cold ischemia is recommended [13], the surgery was performed under warm ischemia, believing that it could be completed in a short time, which may have resulted in azoospermia. According to Izzard's report [14], in sexually mature men, Leydig cell function can be maintained even with radiation exposure up to 30 Gray, and in the future, if patients do not wish to have children, we are considering radiation therapy to the testis.

Owing to their rarity, it is difficult to establish a definitive treatment strategy for bilateral testicular tumors. However, by accumulating patients, a treatment method could be established that considers not only curability but also post‐treatment QOL parameters, such as fertility preservation.

Conclusion

Testis‐sparing surgery should be considered for patients with metachronous bilateral testicular tumors, particularly for those with onset in young adulthood, as it was thought to have the potential to maintain a favorable prognosis and fertility.
