Predictors of Response to Growth Hormone Treatment and Final Height in Medulloblastoma Survivors
Authors: Eve Zipporah Stern, Michal Ben-Ami, Yael Levy-Shraga, Amos Toren, Gadi Abebe-Campino, Michal Lurye, Michal Yalon, Dalit Modan-Moses
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAbnormal growth patterns and impaired final height (FH) are well-recognized long-term complications of childhood medulloblastoma; however, data regarding response to growth hormone treatment (GHT) and determinants of FH are scarce. We aim to describe growth characteristics and response to GHT in survivors of childhood medulloblastoma, and to identify factors associated with FH outcome.MethodsA retrospective study comprising all pediatric patients treated for medulloblastoma in a tertiary care center between 1987 and 2023 and followed up for at least 1 year following diagnosis.ResultsA total of 65/67 patients included in the final analysis (97%) exhibited growth retardation, of whom 38 (58%) were diagnosed with GH deficiency, and 26 were treated with GH. Fifteen of the GH-treated patients achieved FH, with a mean FH-SDS of −1.42 ± 1.47. Only eight (53%) achieved FH within the normal range. FH-SDS of survivors not treated with GH (−1.72 ± 1.25) was lower than that of GH-treated survivors, but this difference did not reach statistical significance. The most important predictor of FH outcome in GH-treated patients was height-SDS at initiation of treatment.ConclusionGrowth retardation and GH deficiency are observed in the vast majority of medulloblastoma patients. Our results suggest that early identification and treatment of GH deficiency are crucial for optimization of FH outcome. Long-term safety of GHT is still a concern and should be discussed with the family prior to initiating treatment.

Introduction

Medulloblastoma is one of the most common malignant brain neoplasms seen in the pediatric population, accounting for 15%–20% of pediatric brain tumors [1]. Historically, medulloblastoma was treated with surgery alone with poor survival outcomes [2]. With the addition of craniospinal irradiation (CSI), followed by introduction of adjuvant chemotherapy in the 1980s, and risk-stratified therapy in the 1990s, survival rates now reach 85% [1, 3, 4].

With increasing survival rates there is an increased risk for treatment-related adverse events, some of which may manifest years after the completion of treatment, necessitating long-term follow-up for these patients [5]. Multiple studies showed poor growth outcomes in survivors of childhood medulloblastoma, with reports of growth disturbance in up to 75–100% of this population [6–17]; growth hormone deficiency (GHD) has been reported in 50–97% of survivors [6–14, 16].

Multiple factors may impact growth trajectories and final height (FH) in survivors medulloblastoma. During intensive treatment, patients may experience poor growth due to undernutrition, cachexia of malignancy and the inflammatory state induced by oncological therapies [18]. In the longer term, radiation therapy may damage the somatotrophs of the anterior pituitary resulting in GHD [19]. Additionally, CSI can result in attenuated growth of the vertebrae contributing further to reduced FH [20–22]. Chemotherapy treatment, specifically Lomustine (CCNU), has also been associated with subsequent growth retardation [23, 24]. Precocious puberty, a recognized complication of cranial irradiation, can result in reduced FH due to premature fusion of the growth plates [25]. Finally, delayed or absent puberty, requiring supplementation with sex hormones could affect the pubertal spurt and as a result FH. Indeed, studies have shown that patients with GHD who are treated with recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) may not reach their true height potential as a result of these compounding factors [13, 17, 26].

Despite the large number of studies describing growth patterns and FH outcomes in survivors of childhood medulloblastoma, data regarding response to rhGH treatment (GHT) and determinants of FH in these patients are scarce. The aim of the current study was to characterize growth patterns in survivors of childhood and adolescent medulloblastoma, and to identify factors associated with FH outcomes and predictors of response to GHT.

Methods

This was a retrospective study, comprising all patients diagnosed with medulloblastoma treated in our center between 1987 and 2023, who had not achieved FH at the time of diagnosis and were at least 1 year from completion of oncological therapy. Most patients were referred to the endocrine clinic shortly after diagnosis, often before commencement of anticancer therapy. The endocrine evaluation included height and weight measurements and assessment of pubertal status at each visit according to Tanner staging [27, 28] documentation of menstrual status, and routine measurements of thyroid stimulating hormone, free thyroxine, cortisol, insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), luteinizing hormone, follicle-stimulating hormone, and estradiol or testosterone. Dynamic tests such as GH stimulation test and ACTH test were performed according to the clinical judgment of the treating endocrinologist.

Clinical data were extracted from the medical charts up to the point of the most recent clinic visit for all patients, including those who had died and those lost to follow-up. GHD was defined, according to guidelines published by the Israeli Society of Pediatric Endocrinology, as peak serum GH <7.5 ng/mL in response to a pharmacological GH provocation test (clonidine, arginine, or glucagon). Girls older than 11 years were primed with estradiol and boys older than 13 years were primed with testosterone. As patients included in this cohort had a clear organic cause for GHD, a single GH stimulation test was used [29]. Normal IGF-1 levels did not exclude GHD, since IGF-1 levels may be normal and thus misleading for the diagnosis of GHD in patients who have received radiotherapy [30]. In keeping with current guidelines, within our clinical practice we recommend deferring GHT until the patient has been disease-free for at least 1 year following completion of oncological therapy, and discussing the appropriateness of GHT and its timing with the patients, their families, and their oncologists [31].

Data Analysis

The initial analysis included estimations of mean, standard deviations, and frequency distribution. Comparison between patients’ height-SDS and mid-parental height (MPH)-SDS were done using the unpaired T test. The associations between potential predictors and FH-SDS were assessed by calculating Pearson correlation coefficients for continuous variables such as age at diagnosis, MPH-SDS, height-SDS at the time of diagnosis and height-SDS at the initiation GHT, and the χ2 test for categorical variables such as gender, CSI protocol, endocrine deficiencies, and GHT. Variables significantly associated with FH-SDS were included in regression models to identify independent predictors of FH.

Results were considered significant if the two-sided p value was <0.05. Calculations were performed using SPSS Statistics 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), software package.

GHD and Early Response to GHT

Thirty-eight patients had a formal diagnosis of GHD based on an abnormal GH stimulation test (Fig. 1). Mean time between diagnosis of GHD and the end of oncological therapy was 2.8 ± 3.6 years. Notably, a further 15 patients had suspected GHD based on abnormal growth velocity with or without low IGF1 levels but did not undergo dynamic testing as they were not considered candidates for GHT. Of the 38 patients with GHD, 26 (68%) patients received treatment with rhGH (Fig. 1). Of the remaining twelve patients, nine did not receive GHT due to oncologists’ and/or family concerns about tumor recurrence, and one patient due to social circumstances affecting medication access. One patient was tested for GHD shortly before the study census date and has since initiated GHT, and one patient was lost to follow-up after undergoing the GH stimulation test. Four of the GH-treated patients were not included in further analyses (two who were treated for only a short period due to relapse and two who were lost to follow-up a short time after commencement of GHT). Mean age at start of GHT was 10.7 ± 4.95 years, and mean duration of GHT was 6.6 ± 2.5 years.

At the initiation of GHT, mean height-SDS was −1.96 ± 1.3, significantly lower (p < 0.001) compared to height-SDS at the time of completion of anticancer treatment (−0.82 ± 0.98). During the year preceding the initiation of GHT, height-SDS decreased by a mean of −0.32 ± 0.3. By comparison, during the first year of GHT, height-SDS increased by a mean of 0.36 ± 0.37. During the second year of GHT, height-SDS increased by a further 0.16 ± 0.31 SD and during the third year by 0.06 ± 0.31SD. All in all, over the first 3 years of GHT height-SDS increased by 0.66 ± 0.59 SD (from −1.96 ± 1.3 to −1.54 ± 1.16, p < 0.001), however, response varied greatly, from −0.38 to 1.47 SD.

FH Outcome in the Whole Cohort

FH data were available for thirty patients (Fig. 1). FH-SDS of these patients was −1.57 ± 1.38, corresponding to a mean FH of 164.5 ± 11.3 cm in males, and 153.5 ± 7.2 cm in females (compared to 174.8 ± 6.6 cm in healthy Israeli males and 162.9 ± 5.9 cm in healthy Israeli females) [32]. FH-SDS of these 30 patients was significantly (p < 0.001) shorter compared to height-SDS at diagnosis of medulloblastoma (−0.26 ± 1.1, delta height-SDS = −1.1 ± 0.81).

FH was available for only 1 of the 4 patients treated with chemotherapy only. This patient showed growth retardation throughout childhood, had early puberty, and reached a FH of 146 cm (−4.25 SD), significantly below his MPH (183 cm, 0.87 SD). Notably, the patient was treated with CCNU at a cumulative dose of 600 mg/m2, which has been associated with impaired growth.

FH Data in Patients with GHD

Of the rhGH-treated patients, fifteen had achieved FH by the time of the last clinic visit. Mean FH-SDS of these patients was −1.42 ± 1.47 (range −3.81 to +1.01), significantly shorter than their MPH-SDS (0.092 ± 0.77, p = 0.004), as well as from their height-SDS at diagnosis of medulloblastoma (−0.27 ± 0.87, p = 0.008). Mean change in height-SDS from initiation of rhGH-treatment to FH was 0.60 ± 0.80; however, there was a wide range in height gain (from −0.43 to 1.91 SD).

Only eight (53.3%) of the rhGH-treated patients achieved FH within the normal range (>−2 SDS). Compared to treated patients whose FH was below the normal range, these patients had a greater height-SDS at the start of rhGH-treatment as well as across all other time points (Fig. 3). Four of the patients treated with rhGH were also treated with gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analog for precocious puberty and 4 other patients required induction of puberty; however, these numbers were too small for their impact on FH to be evaluated.

Factors Associated with FH Outcome

Analyzing the characteristics of all patients in our cohort who had achieved FH (n = 30), we found a significant correlation between FH-SDS and age at diagnosis (r = 0.373, p = 0.042) as well as height-SDS at diagnosis (r = 0.785, p < 0.001). No significant correlation was seen between FH-SDS and MPH-SDS (r = 0.37, p = 0.083).

There was no significant difference in FH-SDS between males and females (−1.61 ± 1.56 vs. −1.51 ± 1.15, p = 0.861), between those treated with high-risk vs standard-risk radiation protocol (−1.59 ± 1.46, vs. −1.31 ± 1.12, p = 0.6), or between patients with treated hypothyroidism and those with normal thyroid function (−1.73 ± 1.59 vs. −1.25 ± 1.10, p = 0.373). FH-SDS of patients treated with rhGH was higher compared with that of untreated patients, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (−1.42 ± 1.57 vs. −1.72 ± 1.25, p = 0.578). Stepwise regression analysis identified height-SDS at the time of diagnosis of medulloblastoma as the most significant predictor of FH-SDS (p < 0.001).

Within the rhGH-treated group, we found significant correlations between FH-SDS and age at diagnosis (r = 0.64, p = 0.01), height-SDS at diagnosis (r = 0.745, p = 0.009), age at rhGH-treatment initiation (r = 0.579, p = 0.024), and height-SDS at rhGH-treatment initiation (r = 0.875, p < 0.001). There was a borderline significant negative correlation between FH-SDS and length of time from completion of oncological treatment to rhGH-treatment initiation (r = −0.459, p = 0.086).

We found no significant correlation between FH-SDS and MPH-SDS (r = 0.452, p = 0.19) or between FH-SDS and duration of GHT (r = −0.409, p = 0.131). Stepwise regression analysis identified height-SDS at the time rhGH-treatment initiation as the most significant predictor of FH-SDS (p < 0.001) in GH-treated patients. Age at diagnosis and age at initiation of rhGH-treatment were not significant in the regression analysis in this patient group.

Adverse Effects and Safety of GHT

Of the patients treated with GH 2/26 (7.7%) had a recurrence of their primary tumor compared with 17/78 (21%) of the remainder of the cohort, who were not treated with GH. In addition to recurrence of the primary tumor, 3/26 (11.5%) of the rhGH-treated group were diagnosed with papillary thyroid carcinoma in contrast to 2/78 (2.6%) of the untreated survivors. In the untreated survivors, 3 (3.8%) patients were diagnosed with meningioma and one (1.3%) was diagnosed with two secondary neoplasms, as compared to no patients in the GH-treated group. One (3.8%) patient treated with GH was diagnosed with slipped capital femoral epiphysis and with impaired fasting glucose (Table 3).

Discussion

In the current study, we described growth characteristics and FH of one of the largest reported cohorts of medulloblastoma patients. Similar to previous reports [6–16], we found abnormal growth patterns and GHD in the vast majority of survivors. FH-SDS ranged from −4.25 to 1.01, with a mean of −1.57 ± 1.4, significantly lower than the expected in the normal population and compared to their MPH. While many of the previous studies described growth characteristics only of survivors with GHD [8, 10, 15, 17] resulting in an incomplete picture of growth patterns seen in survivors of medulloblastoma, we described growth characteristics of patients both with and without a formal diagnosis of GHD. In addition, we reported in detail response to rhGHT across the treatment period, and identified predictive factors of FH in these patients.

Growth Hormone Deficiency

Thirty-eight (57%) patients in our cohort were formally diagnosed with GHD. This is likely an under representation as only those considered candidates for GHT underwent dynamic testing [7–9]. Previous studies reported GHD in 55–97% of their patients [6, 8–14]. This wide range may be attributed to differences in sample size, length of follow-up, timing and methods of assessment of GHD, and cancer treatment era. Dose and type of CSI do not seem to affect the incidence of GHD. Indeed, even in a study comparing patients treated with low-dose CSI (18 Gy) to those treated with conventional dose, there was no difference in the incidence of GHD between the two groups [9]. As well, there was no difference in the incidence of GHD between patients treated with proton-beam therapy compared to those treated with conventional CSI (56.76% vs. 52.5%) [7], in keeping with the outcome of the proton-beam treated survivors in our cohort, who all subsequently developed GHD.

In the current study, growth deceleration was noted in most patients (87%) during the first year after diagnosis, similar to the findings of Brown et al. [14], with no catch-up growth after completion of anticancer treatment. Yet, mean time from completion of therapy to diagnosis of GHD in our cohort was 2.8 ± 3.6 years. This delay is likely due to timing of dynamic GH testing, as patients were referred to stimulation tests mostly when initiation of GHT was considered. Indeed, Uday et al. [8], who routinely tested all patients, reported median time of 1.7 years from completion of therapy to the diagnosis of GHD, with a wide range of 0.5–15 years.

GHT and Response to Treatment

Early response to GHT has been little described before. In our cohort, height-SDS decreased by a mean of 1.16 ± 0.69 from the time of completion of anticancer treatment to the time of initiation of GHT (p < 0.001). During the first 3 years of GHT patients showed a modest but significant and persistent increase in height-SDS, with an increase of 0.36 ± 0.37 SD during the first year of GHT, 0.16 ± 0.31 SD during the second year of GHT, and 0.06 ± 0.31 SD during the third year.

Our findings are consistent with the findings of Chae et al. [15] who observed in their cohort of 28 survivors an increase of 0.5 SD in height-SDS during the first year of GHT, and of 0.2 SD during the second and third years of treatment. Similarly, Ranke et al. [33], reported an increase of 0.4 SD in height-SDS during the first year of GHT.

FH Outcomes

Multiple studies reported decreased FH in survivors of medulloblastoma, with FH-SDS ranging from −2.12 ± 0.77 to −1.0 ± 1.4, despite GHT [8–13, 15, 17, 33]. Notably, older studies showed considerably lower FH-SDS outcomes, ranging from −5 to −2.7 SD [9]. In the current study, FH data were available for 30 patients. Mean FH-SDS of these patients was −1.57 ± 1.4, significantly lower compared to the general population (about 10 cm difference for both males and females), as well as compared to their height at the time of diagnosis of medulloblastoma (−0.26 ± 1.1, delta height-SDS = −1.1 ± 0.81, p < 0.001).

In our GH-treated patients, mean FH-SDS was −1.42 ± 1.47 (range −3.81 to +1.01), similar to several other studies [8, 10, 15]. Mean change in height-SDS from initiation of GHT to FH in the current study was 0.6 ± 0.8; however, there was a wide range in height gain (−0.43 to 1.91 SD). The increase in height-SDS was modest, and FH-SDS was significantly (p = 0.008) lower compared to height-SDS at diagnosis of medulloblastoma (−0.27 ± 0.87). Still, it should be noted that prior to the initiation of GHT, height-SDS decreased by 1.16 ± 0.69 from the time of completion of anticancer treatment, suggesting that GHT prevented a further decrease in height-SDS which would result in an even shorter FH. Ranke et al. [33] reported a less favorable response to GHT, with an overall loss in height-SDS of −0.3 from initiation of GHT. This was significantly lower compared to patients with idiopathic GHD, who showed a height gain of +1.6 SD during GHT. This was likely due to delayed diagnosis and treatment of GHD and additional factors which can negatively impact growth in medulloblastoma survivors such as compromised vertebral growth following CSI or precocious puberty.

We observed that the FH-SDS of 15 patients treated with rhGH (−1.42 ± 1.57) was higher compared to the 15 patients not treated with GH (−1.72 ± 1.25), but not significantly so (p = 0.578). Similar findings were observed by other investigators [10–12]. Only one previous study reported significantly higher FH-SDS in GH-treated medulloblastoma survivors vs. those not treated [17].

The recurring finding of little or no difference in FH outcome between patients treated with rhGH and those not treated is counter-intuitive. This finding may reflect selection bias when deciding which patients to test and treat for GHD, as well as timing of rhGH-treatment initiation. Patients who are diagnosed with medulloblastoma at an older age may achieve a reasonable FH without rhGH-treatment despite having growth retardation, and hence may not be offered GHT (as demonstrated in Fig. 4a). Conversely, patients diagnosed at a younger age may initiate treatment when they already show significant growth retardation and may achieve low FH despite having good response to treatment (Fig. 4b).

Factors Associated with FH

When assessing all 30 patients who achieved FH, we found that younger age at diagnosis of medulloblastoma (r = 0.373, p = 0.042) and lower height-SDS at diagnosis of medulloblastoma (r = 0.785, p < 0.001) were associated with poorer FH outcomes. Several previous studies reported an association between younger age at diagnosis and lower FH [9, 11, 33]. However, in the current study regression analysis identified height-SDS at the time of diagnosis as the most significant predictor of FH-SDS (p < 0.001).

We found no significant difference in FH-SDS between patients treated with high-risk compared to standard-risk radiation protocol. This issue was not addressed in previous studies. However, Xu et al. [9] reported that patients treated with low-dose (18GY) CSI achieved significantly greater FH-SDS (−1.01 ± 1.11) than those treated with conventional dose (−2.04 ± 0.77), suggesting some correlation between radiation dose and magnitude of impact on growth. Importantly, these patients were not representative of the majority of medulloblastoma patients who are treated with higher doses of CSI. There are some reports looking at height outcomes of patients treated with proton-beam therapy [7]. However, the number of patients who reached FH was small. In the current study, only 1 patient treated with proton-beam radiation reached FH. He showed significant growth retardation, but did not undergo GH stimulation test due to the family’s preference. His FH was 155.5 cm, significantly shorter than his MPH. Further studies are required to assess the benefit of proton-beam therapy in preserving growth potential.

We found no correlation between FH-SDS and MPH-SDS, and there was no significant difference in FH-SDS between males and females. FH-SDS of the fifteen patients with proven GHD, who were treated with rhGH was significantly correlated with age at diagnosis (r = 0.64, p = 0.01), height-SDS at diagnosis (r = 0.745, p = 0.009), age at rhGH-treatment initiation (r = 0.579, p = 0.024), and height-SDS at rhGH-treatment initiation (r = 0.875, p < 0.001). However, stepwise regression analysis identified height-SDS at initiation of GHT as the most significant predictor of FH-SDS (p < 0.001). Similarly, Ranke et al. [33] observed that height-SDS at start of rhGH-treatment was the major determinant of FH. This finding highlights the importance of timely diagnosis of GHD and initiation of rhGH-treatment, particularly in patients diagnosed with medulloblastoma at a younger age. This point is illustrated in Figures 4b and c, which present two patients with similar initial characteristics. Both patients were diagnosed with medulloblastoma at a comparable young age (3.7 and 5.2 years) and had similar height-SDS at diagnosis (−0.66 and −0.25, respectively). However, their outcomes differed significantly. In one patient (Fig. 4b), where GHT was delayed due to concerns about tumor recurrence, FH was significantly compromised (FH-SDS −2.89). In contrast, the patient who received GHT early on (Fig. 4c) achieved a normal FH (FH-SDS −0.25), matching her pre-disease height percentile.

Adverse Effects and Safety of GHT

Similar to other cohorts, incidence of adverse events related to GHT was low. Recurrence of the primary tumor was lower in the rhGH-treated group, likely due to careful selection of patients to whom GHT was offered. One rhGH-treated patient had slipped capital femoral epiphysis and impaired fasting glucose, both well-recognized complications of GHT. A higher incidence of secondary thyroid carcinoma was observed in the rhGH-treated group (11.5% vs. 2.6%). We are not aware of this association described previously. Although absolute numbers were small, these are important considerations when counselling patients and their families regarding GHT. Notably, other secondary tumors were observed only in patients not treated with rhGH.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study describes a large cohort of patients, many of whom have been followed from soon after diagnosis of medulloblastoma, with extensive growth data collected until achievement of FH. We assessed multiple parameters including MPH, treatment protocols and associated endocrine abnormalities. Unlike many previous cohorts, we described growth characteristics of patients both with and without a formal diagnosis of GHD, and identified predictors of FH outcomes.

Still, some limitations should be considered. First, the exploratory stepwise regression analysis for predictors of FH in GH-treated patients included only 15 patients. This small sample size, coupled with the inherent limitations of a stepwise approach, poses risks of overfitting and unstable predictors. Hence, our findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Furthermore, the limited sample size precluded the construction of a comprehensive, fully adjusted multivariate model including all potential covariates. Second, we did not have information on sitting height in our survivors. Third, dynamic testing for GHD was not performed in all of the patients, which likely resulted in underestimation of the rate of GHD. Finally, we did not have information regarding radiation dosimetry to the pituitary/hypothalamus. However, given that there was no association between the crude measure of “high-risk” vs. “standard-risk” radiation and the risk for developing GHD, we believe that dosimetry measurements would not have significantly affected our findings.

Conclusions

In the current study we found, similar to other studies, high rates of growth abnormalities, GHD, and decreased FH in survivors of childhood medulloblastoma. We showed that in survivors treated with rhGH, the strongest predictor for poor FH outcome was lower height-SDS at commencement of rhGH-treatment. Our findings suggest that in contrast to other patient groups with GHD, survivors of medulloblastoma often do not achieve optimal catch-up growth. Hence, delaying assessment of the GH-axis and initiation of GHT in young children until height is severely compromised may result in poor FH outcome.
