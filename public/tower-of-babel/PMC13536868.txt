Factors associated with quality of work life among colorectal cancer survivors: a cross-sectional study after returning to work
Authors: Zhen Li, Jingwen Cao, Sinuo Chen, Ge Chai, Yanyan Dong, Luna Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo facilitate the vocational rehabilitation of colorectal cancer patients, more information is needed on the influencing factors related to the quality of work life of colorectal cancer patients after their return to work. This study examines the Quality of Working Life (QWL) among the patients with colorectal cancer (CRC) survivors after returning to work and identifies influencing factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study from January to May 2023 followed 290 CRC survivors from a Chinese hospital’s oncology outpatient department. The General Questionnaire, Quality of Working Life Questionnaire for Cancer Survivors (QWLQ-CS), Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire, and the Social Support Revalued Scale were used for data collection. Descriptive statistics, T-tests, ANOVA, correlation, and multiple linear regression analyses were conducted with the Statistical Package for the Social Science (SPSS) version 25.0.ResultsThe QWL score of CRC survivors was (67.72 ± 12.21). Factors such as the per capita monthly family income, medical payment method as self-employment, basic medical insurance system for urban residents, stoma status, positive coping strategies, passive coping strategies, and social support utilization emerged as significant QWL determinants, while passive coping strategies was inversely related to QWL.ConclusionCRC survivors have greater room for improvement in their work life (QWL) after returning to work. To enhance their reintegration and QWL, healthcare professionals should implement individualized interventions that reflect the various challenges these individuals face. It is important to provide special attention to colorectal cancer survivors who pay for their medical care directly, have residential health insurance, have an ostomy, or have a per capita family income of less than 281⁄ per month, as they tend to report worse levels of quality of work life. Clinical personnel should rapidly detect these risk factors and offer the required assistance and resources to ensure that these patients are adequately prepared to resume employment and enhance their overall quality of work life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16400-9.Keywords: Colorectal Cancer survivors, Return-to-work, Quality of working Life, Factor

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a significant global health concern, ranking third in incidence and second in mortality according to the GLOBOCAN 2020 data [1]. It is estimated that by 2035, the number of cases worldwide will reach 1.36 million in men and 1.08 million in women [2]. With the gradual improvement of screening, diagnosis and treatment technology, the 5-year survival rate for early-stage CRC post-surgery has increased to 90.6% [3], prompting many survivors to consider returning to work during or following their treatment. The increased prevalence of colorectal cancer in younger patients, coupled with an increase in retirement age, means that more patients are being diagnosed with colorectal cancer at working age [4]. Studies have shown that 37.2% of patients return to work within 1 year and up to 67.5% within 2 years [5].This decision is often influenced by the extended survival prospects and the financial burden of care. The ability to resume occupational roles is not only essential for the financial relief and self-esteem of survivors but also serves as a critical step toward societal reintegration. Beyond personal benefits, their return to work can enhance the labor force and contribute to economic growth [6].

Patients with colorectal cancer often undergo surgical treatment, adjuvant chemotherapy, and other methods, and some patients will carry the stoma [7, 8]. Survivors of colorectal cancer may experience gastrointestinal dysfunction [9], sexual dysfunction [10], cancer-related fatigue [11], negative self-image [12], etc. The symptoms will continue to persist during the years following the cancer diagnosis and treatment, leading to a decrease in their work ability [13]. In particular, the intestinal problems of colorectal cancer and the care of the stoma-related care not only seriously affect their psychological social adaptation, but also reduce their quality of life [14]. After undergoing treatment, which may include surgery and the potential need for a stoma, survivors of colorectal cancer (CRC) often experience reduced work ability and psychological difficulties such as worry, shame, and fear of recurrence [15]. These challenges are compounded by the need to balance work and family responsibilities while managing the financial implications of their condition. Cancer patients experience support from family, friends, colleagues, etc., after going through a long-term cancer treatment process, and this support enables patients to maintain a positive and optimistic mindset, build confidence in returning to work, and cope with the challenges of returning to work more comfortably [16–18].

Quality of Working Life (QWL) reflects an individual’s broader life experience during their prime working years and is indicative of their job satisfaction and subjective work experiences [19]. For CRC survivors, the ability to work and their QWL upon return is influenced by many factors, such as poor psychosocial adjustment, ongoing disease management, and uncertainty about disease outcomes [20, 21]. Previous research has demonstrated that poor QWL can lead to negative emotions, insufficient coping strategies, increased turnover, and can even adversely affect prognosis [22, 23]. Most existing studies have focused on the psychological adjustment and the return-to-work rate of CRC survivors, with few examining their QWL in the context of their return to the workforce. Understanding how CRC survivors perceive their QWL can offer valuable insights into improving their work adjustment and overall life quality.

The objective of this study is to evaluate the quality of work life (QWL) among colorectal cancer (CRC) survivors who have resumed employment and to specify the elements that influence their QWL. We suggest that socio-demographic traits, coping strategies, and social support play a crucial role in determining quality of life. It is postulated that colorectal cancer survivors who employ effective coping mechanisms and leverage strong social support will report greater quality of work life.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted from January to May 2023. A total of 290 colorectal cancer (CRC) patients, who had resumed work post-treatment, were recruited from a Chinese hospital’s oncology outpatient department. Convenience sampling was utilized for participant selection. Eligibility criteria included: (1)had a diagnosis of colorectal cancer made through histopathology and had completed active treatment, such as surgery and adjuvant treatment; (2) completed surgical treatment > 6 months; (2) age between 18 and 55 years; (3) proficiency in both oral and written Chinese; (4) currently employed or self-employed, with at least one month of work post-return; (5) provision of informed consent to participate in the study. We excluded patients with comorbidities of other serious diseases, other primary malignancies, or psychiatric disorders.

Sampling and inclusion criteria

Participant enrollment was based on convenience sampling. The calculation of the sample size adhered to the guidelines for impact factor analysis [24], which recommend a minimum of 5–10 subjects for each independent variable. Given the 20 independent variables in this study and allowing for a 20% contingency for non-responses or invalid data, the estimated sample size ranged from 120 to 240 participants. To strengthen the study’s statistical power and account for potential dropouts, the final sample size was increased to 290 cases.

Data collection

Between January and May 2023, this study conducted an initial screening of colorectal cancer patients for disease information through outpatient screening, ward follow-up, and stoma clinics, and screening for return-to-work information through on-site counseling and telephone calls, to include 300 patients who met the inclusion criteria and gave informed consent to participate in the study, with a final validated questionnaire obtained in 290 cases. The other parts of the questionnaire (except disease information) were completed by the respondents themselves. Information on disease (e.g., cancer stage) in the questionnaire was collected from medical records by researchers after obtaining informed consent from respondents.

Data collection was carried out by means of on-site surveys deployed promptly upon obtaining consent. Interviewers were accessible to aid participants just at their direct request. To guarantee precision and accuracy, questionnaires were promptly reviewed with participants upon completion to rectify any mistakes or areas of ambiguity. Measurements

Demographics, disease-related information and work-related information

This section was designed by the researcher himself through a literature review and the following variables were collected through a questionnaire. Demographics collected in this study included age, gender, marital status, number of children, residency, living situation, religion, education level, monthly household income per capita, and provider payments. Disease-related information included the presence of chronic diseases, the presence of stomas, and the cancer stage. Work-related information includes mode of employment, weekly work hours, and length of employment.

Quality of working life for cancer survivors (QWLQ-CS)

QWLQ-CS, created by de Jong [25], is a 23-item instrument measuring five facets of work life: meaning of work (4 items), perception of the work situation (5 items), atmosphere in the work environment (3 items), understanding and recognition in the organization (7 items), and Problems due to the health situation (4 items). Responses are captured on a six-point Likert scale, where higher scores denote greater QWL. The QWLQ-CS’s Chinese version has demonstrated reliable psychometric properties among Chinese cancer patient populations, evidenced by a Cronbach’s α of 0.895 for the overall scale and α ranging from 0.83 to 0.86 for its subscales [26].

Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ)

The SCSQ, developed by Xie Yaning [27], is composed of 20 items divided into two categories: positive coping strategies (12 items) and passive coping strategies (8 items). Each item is assessed using a 4-point Likert scale with options “0 - never,“"1 - occasionally,“"2 - sometimes,” and “3 - often.” A higher aggregate score indicates a stronger tendency towards the corresponding coping style. The SCSQ has demonstrated high internal consistency, with a total Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.90.

Social Support Revalued Scale (SSRS)

The SSRS, crafted by Xiao Shuiyuan [28], is a 10-item measure that evaluates three facets of social support: objective support (3 items), subjective support (4 items), and the social support utilization (3 items ). The total score is 12 to 64, the total score ≤ 22 is low level,>22 to 44 is moderate level, and > 44 indicates a high level of social support. The higher the score, the better the social support. The SSRS boasts a high reliability with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.90.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using theStatistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25.0. Categorical data were summarized using frequencies and percentages, while normally distributed continuous variables were reported with means and standard deviations. Differences in the quality of working life among patients with varying characteristics were assessed using independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA. Pearson’s correlation analysis was utilized to explore relationships between continuous variables. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted with significant variables from the univariate analyses as independent predictors and the quality of work-life score as the outcome variable to determine factors associated with quality of work-life. A p-value of 0.05 or less was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Discussion

This study aimed to delineate QWL among CRC survivors and investigate the influence of demographics, clinical, social, and coping factors on it. The results of our study show that the quality of work life of CRC survivors after returning to work needs to be further improved. The mean QWL score of CRC survivors in this study was (67.72 ± 12.21), which was lower than that reported in the study de Jong’s study [29]. This variation in QWL during the workforce reintegration of cancer survivors may be attributed to differences in individual experiences, organizational support, societal attitudes, and policy provisions that vary significantly across countries.

The results of our study revealed a clear and favorable relationship between active coping, objective support, and subjective support with quality of life (QWL) after returning to work. This highlights the crucial importance of effective coping strategies and adequate support networks. In contrast, passive coping strategies were inversely related to QWL, shedding light on the negative impact of avoidant behaviors. Participants in this study scored lower on “Atmosphere in the Work Environment” and “Understanding and Recognition in the Organization” than on other dimensions. Unlike de Jong’s study [29], They only found low scores on the dimension “Problems due to the health situation”. This may be due to the differences between the two research objects and the different national conditions. The majority of the subjects selected for the study were patients who had last been treated for more than one year were better adapted physically and psychologically, and were easier to understand and gain recognition from the work organization [30]. Compared to China’s late start in providing policy support and benefits to cancer survivors, foreign countries have shown more maturity in this area of research and practice [31, 32]. Individuals with a stoma experience unique challenges that can impede their work efficiency and job retention, potentially hindering their successful workplace reintegration [33]. However, the sample size of this study on stoma patients only accounted for 8.3%, which may be somewhat under-representative, and the conclusion can be further validated by referring to more studies on stoma patients in the future.

Underscoring the need of holistic treatment, multidisciplinary rehabilitation programs such as those established in the Netherlands, which combine vocational counseling with physical training, have demonstrated efficacy in improving the rates at which cancer patients return to work [28]. In line with other research [34], this study validates the crucial role of social support in enhancing quality of life for survivors of colorectal cancer (CRC). Research has revealed that family members of cancer survivors and patients hold contrasting perspectives on the idea of resuming work [35]. Furthermore, it has been shown that a deficiency of social support from the family will hinder cancer survivors from resuming their employment. A work environment that provides assistance, together with empathy from both employers and colleagues, can greatly enhance the confidence of survivors, therefore facilitating their reintegration into formal employment [36]. A positive coping strategies was singled out as a key contributor to higher QWL, while passive coping strategies emerged as risk factors. This underscores the need for interventions that promote resilience and adaptability, enabling CRC survivors to face work-related challenges more effectively. Restricted physical activity, reduced work capacity, symptom distress, and changes in social relationships lead to negative effects such as anxiety, depression, fear, and shame in colorectal cancer survivors, which in turn are detrimental to their return to work [37]. Positive psychological adjustment, enhanced social support, and physical activity exercise can promote the effective adaptation of stoma survivors to society [38, 39]. The psychological state of CRC survivors warrants close attention; fostering positive coping strategies and providing timely counseling can play a crucial role in enhancing their QWL [40, 41].

Furthermore, our findings spotlight the profound influence of economic status on QWL. Survivors from lower-income households who self-fund their healthcare, those reliant on basic insurance, and particularly those managing a stoma, reported lower QWL. Corroborating earlier research [42, 43], which found that stomas can greatly disrupt self-perception and career performance. The economic burden, which includes expenses for long-term therapy and stoma maintenance that are not covered by China’s insurance system, significantly impacts quality of life. Research has indicated that individuals who have survived stomas may experience a persistent condition of self-denial and hold the belief that they are unable to carry out their professional responsibilities because of concerns related to the stoma, negative body image, inadequate sleep quality, and limited social acceptance [44, 45]. This is consistent with research conducted in the United States that emphasizes the significant financial burden faced by a considerable number of cancer survivors during their treatment [46]. Such financial constraints underscore the importance of policy intervention to support CRC survivors’ return to work and enhance their quality of life [47, 48]. Patients with preparation seriously lack their rehabilitation cognition and have a low degree of acceptance. They believe that they are not competent to work and adapt to society. They have a lower return to work [33]. At the same time, medical workers should provide patients with appropriate individualized rehabilitation guidance promptly, and ultimately promote their physical, psychological, and social comprehensive recovery, thereby improving the quality of their work and life.

In conclusion, the QWL for CRC survivors is shaped by a constellation of individual, social, and economic elements. Our study highlights the importance of a nuanced approach to supporting survivors as they navigate back into their work environments. Future initiatives must focus on establishing comprehensive support systems that extend beyond facilitating work re-entry, striving to ensure the well-being and economic security of CRC survivors in the long term. This approach will not only help in making their post-treatment life sustainable but will also contribute to a more rewarding and fulfilling life experience.

Conclusions

The current study shows that the QWL of CRC survivors who return to work leaves something to be desired. We have identified positive coping mechanisms, objective support, subjective support, and the utilization of social support as significant enhancers of QWL for these individuals. Conversely, engaging in passive coping strategies has emerged as a detrimental factor. The process of reintegration for CRC survivors is intricate, demanding the implementation of multi-faceted, efficacious, and systematically structured interventions.

These observations establish the foundation for developing and implementing comprehensive programs targeted at enhancing quality of life among survivors of colorectal cancer. By bolstering their reintegration and promoting their ongoing participation in the labor market, we not only contribute to the improvement of their professional life but also to the furtherance of their general welfare. Subsequent efforts should expand on these discoveries in order to offer colorectal cancer survivors the necessary assistance for a smooth integration into the workforce and an improved standard of living after their recovery.

Limitations and strengths

This research provides foundational data and contributes valuable insights into enhancing the work-life quality of CRC survivors as they return to work. It also serves as a guide for governments and policymakers in crafting effective reintegration policies for cancer survivors re-entering the workforce. However, this study is not without its limitations. The scope was limited to an oncology center at a single hospital in China, which may affect the general ability of the findings. The study lacked a comprehensive component concerning basic information about survivors of colorectal cancer, including specific treatment methods and the unique characteristics of their work. Additionally, it was not sufficiently representative of stoma patients. Subsequent investigations could include these study factors to examine their influence on the work life quality of individuals who have survived colorectal cancer upon their reintegration into the workforce. Hence, the inferences made may not accurately reflect the experiences of all victims of colorectal cancer returning to their professional positions. Potential future research efforts should expand the range of the study by augmenting the sample size and include several sites situated in diverse geographical regions. In addition, longitudinal study is necessary to monitor the development of quality of life in colorectal cancer survivors and to identify long-term predictive variables. Conducting such research would improve our comprehension of the long-lasting effects on the quality of work-life for survivors and the efficacy of different support systems over a continuous period.
