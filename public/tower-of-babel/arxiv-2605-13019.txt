Categorical (Co)Limits of Quantum Graphs
Jennifer Zhu

Abstract
We begin with the characterization of quantum graphs as left ideals in ℳ ⊗ e ​ h ℳ \mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M} (the extended Haagerup tensor product of ℳ \mathcal{M} with itself) to avoid technicalities surrounding representation dependence of quantum graphs. These left ideals roughly correspond to a canonical complement of a quantum graph. Using these left ideals and some operator space theory, we find a new, representation-free characterization of a morphism of quantum graphs compatible with previous representation-dependent morphisms. A notion of categorical (co)limit of quantum graphs follows. We also briefly explore an alternative quantization of graphs as bimodules over C ∗ C^{*} -algebras ( C ∗ C^{*} -graphs), mostly to emphasize the point that a morphism of C ∗ C^{*} -graphs is not a morphism of C ∗ C^{*} -correspondences.

Introduction

Many authors have explored the notion of quantum relations set out by Weaver in
[
44
]
from a variety of perspectives. A quantum relation by Weaver’s definition is an
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
-bimodule
\mathcal{S}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H})
(where
\mathcal{M}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H})
is a von Neumann algebra). When

[

. The

(known by many names) used in
[
28
]

[
19
]
for an enjoyable explanation and Appendix A of the author’s thesis for a brutally short summary). Thus Weaver’s quantum relations provide a natural setting for infinite dimensional extensions and versions of these diagrammatically defined objects. The motivation for this paper was to develop a notion of limit for quantum graphs to build a (possibly) infinite quantum graph from finite quantum graphs. At first blush, quantum relations seem representation dependent but Weaver immediately shows that they only depend on the von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{M}
, not the ambient space
B(\mathcal{H})
(
[
44
, Theorem 2.7]
). Generally, however, one works in a particular representation and then appeals to the fact that the relevant properties of quantum graphs are representation independent.

If we were to use this characterization of quantum graphs in to define a categorical limit, however, it is unclear how a morphism of quantum graphs
\mathcal{S}_{1}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H}_{1})
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H}_{2})
should behave on the ambient spaces
B(\mathcal{H}_{1})
and
B(\mathcal{H}_{2})
. Furthermore, we may encounter issues regarding the uniqueness of the categorical limit. We are thus pushed to avoid the usual characterization of a quantum graph as an
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
-bimodule

, leading to a new notion of morphism of quantum graphs in Definition

. This new notion is equivalent to a CP morphism of quantum graphs in
[
10

and a classical morphism of quantum graphs in
[
28
, Definition 5.4]
under the appropriate assumptions. It also has the advantage of giving a clean visualization of categorical limits: compare the diagrams in Section
5

6.5
where we define the morphism on the level of bimodules.

Although this paper is not focused on the quantum information background or implications, we will see some comments sprinkled through the paper referring to the quantum information theoretic roots. We will introduce the connection between quantum channels and quantum graphs here, but the reader may wish to consult the original paper

The

\displaystyle Q_{N}(T):=\sum_{a\in A,b\in B}\underbrace{\sqrt{N(b|a)}|b\rangle\langle a|}_{K_{ab}}\,T\,\underbrace{\sqrt{N(b|a)}|a\rangle\langle b|.}_{K_{ab}^{\dagger}}

Note that for

so the confusability graph of

\mathcal{S}:=\text{span}\{K_{a_{1}b_{1}}^{\dagger}K_{a_{2}b_{2}}:a_{1},a_{2}\in A,\,b_{1},b_{2}\in B\}\subseteq B(\mathbb{C}^{|A|}).

because
|a_{1}\rangle\langle a_{2}|\in\mathcal{S}
if and only if there exists some

. The authors of
[

\displaystyle Q:B(\mathcal{H}_{1})

\displaystyle\to B(\mathcal{H}_{2})

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{i\in I}K_{i}TK_{i}^{\dagger}

and defining its
quantum confusability graph

[
12

These quantum confusability graphs are (the canonical) examples of quantum graphs to which we refer in this paper.

2
Preliminaries

By limits in this paper we refer to categorical limits (projective limits) and colimits (inductive limits); see
[
24
, Chapter III.4]
,
[
24
, Chapter III.3]
. Such limits are defined in purely categorical terms (i.e., by objects and morphisms), so we need only identify the correct notion of quantum relations and their morphisms to apply the definition of categorical (co)limit.

Our objects will of course be quantum relations as defined in Definition
2.1
. As mentioned in the introduction, we refer to Weaver’s quantum relations as quantum graphs. Weaver’s quantum graphs will be specified as symmetric, reflexive quantum graphs.

A quantum graph in
[
44
]
is given by a von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{M}
(roughly, the quantization of the vertices) and an
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
-bimodule
\mathcal{S}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H})
, where
B(\mathcal{H})
houses a representation of
\mathcal{M}
(roughly, the quantization of the edges). However, our morphism uses alternate characterization of quantum graphs – keeping the same von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{M}
as our quantization of vertices, we will quantize the complementary edges of the graph instead. These complementary edges live in
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
, the extended Haagerup tensor product of
\mathcal{M}
with itself. We will introduce the extended Haagerup tensor product
\otimes_{eh}
in Section
2.3
and state more precisely which subspaces correspond to quantum graphs.

The symbol
\otimes
will denote the algebraic tensor product. We will be using both
∗
and
†
in this paper. The former will denote the involution in a
*
-algebra and the latter will denote the adjoint of a bounded linear map between Hilbert spaces. At times these notions will coincide, but the context will hopefully be clear enough to avoid confusion. When a predual of a space
X
exists and is unique, we will denote it by
X_{*}
. We will denote the topology on
X^{*}
induced by a space
X
by
\sigma(X)
. The unit of an algebra
\mathcal{A}
will be denoted
1_{\mathcal{A}}
.

2.1
Operator Algebras

We will assume the reader is familiar with operator algebras, but we set down some notation and naming conventions here. Throughout this paper
\mathcal{H}
will denote a Hilbert space and

the bounded operators on

by

. The

We will at times identify the trace class operators in

\displaystyle\{\text{trace class operators}\}

\displaystyle\to B(\mathcal{H})_{*}

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\mapsto\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}(T\cdot).

The compact operators in

A von Neumann algebra

will be a

-algebra with a (unique) Banach space predual

2.3
Operator Space Theory

As mentioned in the introduction, we will need some operator space theory in order to quantize the complementary edges of a quantum graph. The full explanation of this quantization is given in Subsection
3.1
, but at the end of this subsection we will see how
\otimes_{eh}
recovers the complementary edges of a finite classical graph. We will also make use of the topologies laid down here to ensure we have the correct continuity of various operations in Section

Basic definitions from operator space theory are given below, but the following is by no means an in-depth exploration or explanation. A few standard references for operator spaces are
[
15
]
,
[
33
]
, and
[
30
]
.

Definition 2.5
.

A
concrete operator space
is a norm-closed subspace
X\subseteq B(\mathcal{H})
for some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 2.6
.

An
abstract operator space
is a vector space
X
with a sequence of norms
\|\cdot\|_{M_{n}(X)}:M_{n}\otimes X\to[0,\infty)
such that

(OS1)

the norm
\|\cdot\|_{M_{1}(X)}:M_{1}\otimes X\to[0,\infty)
endows
M_{1}\otimes X\cong X
with a Banach space structure,

(OS2)

for
x\in M_{m}\otimes X
and
y\in M_{n}\otimes X
,

\|x\oplus y\|_{M_{m+n}(X)}=\max\{\|x\|_{M_{m}(X)},\|y\|_{M_{n}(Y)}\},

(OS3)

and for
\alpha,\beta\in M_{m}
and
x\in M_{m}\otimes X

\|\alpha x\beta\|_{M_{m}(X)}\leq\|\alpha\|\|x\|_{M_{m}(X)}\|\beta\|.

We may occasionally abbreviate
\|\cdot\|_{M_{m}(X)}
by
\|\cdot\|_{m}
when the context is clear. This sequence of norms is the operator space structure (OSS) on the operator space
X
.

Concrete and abstract operator spaces are “the same.” To state this precisely we need some terminology.

Definition 2.7
.

Given a linear map
\theta:X\to Y
between two abstract operator spaces, we can define an
amplification
of
\theta
:

I_{M_{n}}\otimes\theta:M_{n}\otimes X\to M_{n}\otimes Y.

We say
\theta
is
completely bounded
if

\|\theta\|_{cb}:=\sup_{n}\{\|Id_{n}\otimes\theta\|\}<\infty,

where
\|Id_{n}\otimes\theta\|
is the operator norm of the amplification between the normed spaces
(M_{n}\otimes X,\|\cdot\|_{n})
and
(M_{m}\otimes Y,\|\cdot\|_{m})
. We say
\theta
is
completely contractive
if
\|\theta\|_{cb}\leq 1
and
completely isometric
if for
I_{M_{n}}\otimes\theta
is an isometry for each
n
.
Finally
\theta
is a
completely isometric isomorphism
if
\theta
is a surjective complete isometry.

Remark 2.8
.

Every
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
admits a unique norm such that

\|a^{*}a\|=\|a\|^{2}\qquad\forall a\in\mathcal{A}.

Fundamentally, this is because the norm is induced by an algebraic property of the elements. Therefore
M_{n}\otimes\mathcal{A}
has a unique norm
\|\cdot\|_{n}
that ensures it is a
C^{*}
-algebra, and we always have a canonical OSS on a
C^{*}
-algebra.
∎

Every concrete operator space therefore is an abstract operator space. The reverse implication comes from Ruan’s thesis.

Theorem 2.9
.

[
37
, Theorem 3.1]

Every abstract operator space is completely isometrically isomorphic to a concrete operator space.

It thus generally suffices to assume an operator space is embedded in some
B(\mathcal{H})
.

2.3.1
Operator Space Tensor Norms

The compatibility of the morphisms in the limit of quantum graphs relies heavily on a particular operator space tensor product, the extended Haagerup tensor product
\otimes_{eh}

\otimes_{\sigma h}

Given two operator spaces

which are perhaps better understood as algebraic tensor products completed under a predual topology. In particular, they are not constructed in the usual norm-completion procedure, and so are not technically operator space tensor products according to some authors. \otimes_{h},\otimes_{eh},\otimes_{\sigma h}

Definition 2.28

[

For a von Neumann algebra

Under the identification

[

Remark 2.32
.

The topologies above may require explanation. The space
B(\ell^{2})\overline{\otimes}\mathcal{M}
is the usual von Neumann algebraic tensor product (see Subsection
2.1
) whose unique Banach space predual
B(\ell^{2})_{*}\widehat{\otimes}\mathcal{M}
is the operator space projective tensor product of the predual
B(\ell^{2})_{*}
and the von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{M}
.

The space
B(\ell^{2})\overline{\otimes}(\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M})
is closed in a predual topology induced by the operator space projective tensor product the predual
B(\ell^{2})_{*}
and the extended Haagerup tensor product
\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*}
. To give a flavor of how this topology works, it may be best to contrast the “natural” predual topologies of
B(\ell^{2})\overline{\otimes}(\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M})
and
B(\ell^{2})\overline{\otimes}(\mathcal{M}\otimes_{\sigma h}\mathcal{M})
. We begin with the former. Assume that
\mathcal{M}=B(\mathcal{H})
. We have that

B(\ell^{2})\overline{\otimes}(\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M})=(B(\ell^{2})_{*}\widehat{\otimes}(CB(K(\mathcal{H}),B(\mathcal{H})))_{*})^{*}

and since
CB(X,Y^{*})=(X\widehat{\otimes}Y)^{*}
(see
[
33
, Theorem 4.1]
for a reference),

(B(\ell^{2})_{*}\widehat{\otimes}(CB(K(\mathcal{H}),B(\mathcal{H})))_{*})^{*}=(B(\ell^{2})_{*}\widehat{\otimes}(K(\mathcal{H})\widehat{\otimes}B(\mathcal{H})_{*}))^{*}.

Using the symmetry and associativity of
\widehat{\otimes}
(
[
6
, Chapter 1.2.2]
),

\displaystyle(B(\ell^{2})_{*}\widehat{\otimes}(K(\mathcal{H})\widehat{\otimes}B(\mathcal{H})_{*}))^{*}

\displaystyle=((K(\mathcal{H})\widehat{\otimes}B(\ell^{2})_{*}\widehat{\otimes}B(\mathcal{H})_{*}))^{*}

\displaystyle=CB(K(\mathcal{H}),(B(\ell^{2})_{*}\widehat{\otimes}B(\mathcal{H})_{*})^{*})

\displaystyle=CB(K(\mathcal{H}),B(\ell^{2})\overline{\otimes}B(\mathcal{H}))

\displaystyle=CB(K(\mathcal{H}),B(\ell^{2}\otimes_{2}\mathcal{H})).

Every element
[z_{ij}]_{i,j}\in B(\ell^{2})\overline{\otimes}(\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M})
can be considered as an infinite matrix with entries
z_{ij}\in\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
. Every operator
T\in K(\mathcal{H})
turns
[z_{ij}]_{i,j}
into an infinite matrix with
B(\mathcal{H})
entries by

[z_{ij}]_{i,j}\mapsto[\Phi_{z_{ij}}(T)]_{i,j}\in B(\ell^{2})\overline{\otimes}B(\mathcal{H}).

A net
(z^{\lambda}:=[z^{\lambda}_{ij}]_{i,j})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
converges in the
\sigma(B(\ell^{2})_{*}\widehat{\otimes}(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*}))
topology to
z=[z_{ij}]_{ij}
, then for any
T\in K(\mathcal{H})
and functional
\phi\in B(\ell^{2}\otimes_{2}\mathcal{H})_{*}
we have

\phi\bigg([\Phi_{z^{\lambda}_{ij}-z_{ij}}(T)]_{i,j}\bigg)\overset{\lambda\in\Lambda}{\longrightarrow}0.

(The above convergence is a priori a coarser topology than
\sigma(B(\ell^{2})_{*}\widehat{\otimes}(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*}))
, but we hope this demystifies the symbols in the predual.) The natural topology on
B(\ell^{2})\overline{\otimes}(\mathcal{M}\otimes_{\sigma h}\mathcal{M})
is almost exactly the same except one replaces
K(\mathcal{H})
by
B(\mathcal{H})
and
\otimes_{h}
by
\otimes_{eh}
. Although the range of
\odot
is
B(\ell^{2})\overline{\otimes}(\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M})
, the topology is the predual topology from
B(\ell^{2})\overline{\otimes}(\mathcal{M}\otimes_{\sigma h}\mathcal{M})
.

Representation-Free Characterization of Quantum Graphs

We can now show how the
\otimes_{eh}
tensor product allows us to quantize the complementary set of edges of a graph. We begin with an example of how this works for a finite classical graph. The example also works for classical graphs on infinitely many vertices, but we only present the finite case to avoid an extra topological consideration. The infinite case will in any case be subsumed in the general characterization in Theorem
2.34
.

Example 2.33
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite graph, and let
\mathcal{M}=\ell^{\infty}(V)
,
\mathcal{S}\subseteq B(\ell^{2}(V))
be its quantization as in Example
2.4
. Note that the map
\Phi
(Theorem
2.12
) turns
B(\ell^{2}(V))
into a left
\ell^{\infty}(V)\otimes_{eh}\ell^{\infty}(V)
-module. We can therefore speak of annihilators of
\ell^{\infty}(V)
-bimodules inside
\ell^{\infty}(V)\otimes_{eh}\ell^{\infty}(V)
. For
v\in V
denote the indicator function on
\{v\}
by
\chi_{v}
.

\displaystyle\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle:=\{z\in\ell^{\infty}(V)\otimes\ell^{\infty}(V):\Phi_{z}(\mathcal{S})=0\}

\displaystyle=\text{span}\{\chi_{v}\otimes\chi_{w}:(v,w)\notin E\}.

∎

(Part of)
[
17
, Theorem 3.3]
shows that correspondence above generalizes to a bijection between quantum graphs on an arbitrary von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{M}
and
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-closed left ideals.

Theorem 2.34
.

[
17
, Theorem 3.3]

Let
\mathcal{M}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H})
be a von Neumann algebra. There is a bijection between
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-closed left ideals in
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
and quantum graphs on
\mathcal{M}
. Namely, each quantum graph corresponds uniquely to its annihilator in
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
:

\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S})=\{z\in\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}:\Phi_{z}(T)=0,\,T\in\mathcal{S}\}

where
\Phi
is the map in Definition
2.12
.

As a result of this theorem, we may at times call a pair
(\mathcal{M},\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}))
a quantum graph, where
\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S})
is a
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-closed left ideal.

Remark 2.35
.

We pause here to marvel at this correspondence. A common observation (or objection) when encountering the definition of a quantum graph is the dependence on the representation of
\mathcal{M}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H})
. One of the first results
[
44
, Theorem 2.7]
regarding quantum graphs establishes a bijection between quantum graphs on isomorphic von Neumann algebras
[
44
, Theorem 2.7]
. The need for representation independence also drove the development of
intrinsic quantum relations
in
[
44
, Definition 2.24]
, although the definition uses the weak operator topology. We shall also see in Subsubsection
3.3.1
that the definition of quantum graphs from
[
28
]
implicitly chooses an inner product structure for their (necessarily finite-dimensional) von Neumann algebras. However, since the
\otimes_{eh}
tensor product and the topology
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})
are representation independent, we can call these left ideals “quantum graphs” without worrying about the ambient space
B(\mathcal{H})
.
∎

Remark 2.36
.

As the author emphasizes in
[
17
, Section 3]
, the topology under which the annihilators are closed is
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})
, not
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*})
. See Remark
2.17
for the distinction between these predual topologies. The choice of topology is necessary because quantum graphs are subsets of
B(\mathcal{H})
, not
K(\mathcal{H})
(the compact operators). Compare the two following topologies:

\displaystyle z_{i}\overset{\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})}{\longrightarrow}z

\displaystyle\implies\langle\phi,\Phi_{z_{i}-z}(T)\rangle=0\qquad\forall T\in B(\mathcal{H}),\,\phi\in B(\mathcal{H})_{*}

\displaystyle z_{i}\overset{\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*})}{\longrightarrow}z

\displaystyle\implies\langle\phi,\Phi_{z_{i}-z}(T)\rangle=0\qquad\forall T\in K(\mathcal{H}),\,\phi\in B(\mathcal{H})_{*}.

A left ideal in
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
that is the annihilator of a quantum graph must be able to distinguish whether an arbitrary
T\in B(\mathcal{H})
is in the quantum graph. Even though every
T\in B(\mathcal{H})
is the
B(\mathcal{H})_{*}
-limit of compact operators, one would need to interchange two limits in order for the two topologies above to be equivalent.
∎

Remark 2.37
.

A recent preprint has defined
Hilbert Schmidt quantum relations

[
11
, Definition 4.1]
. These quantum relations are still
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
-bimodules
\mathcal{S}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H})
, but they are
\|\cdot\|_{HS}
-closed rather than
\sigma(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))_{*}
-closed. In this case, the
\otimes_{eh}
is the incorrect tensor product to define quantum graphs in terms of their annihilators. The key point is that for each

is a
\sigma(B(\mathcal{H})_{*})
-closed subspace. Hence the subspace annihilated by any subset of

is a
\sigma(B(\mathcal{H})_{*})
-closed subspace.
∎

2.4
Categorical (Co)limits

If we are to take categorical (co)limits of quantum graphs, we will necessarily need candidates for the “vertices” and “complementary edges” of the quantum graph in the limit. These will be furnished by the limit of von Neumann algebras and (a closure of a ) limit of operator spaces respectively. A reader who believes that one can always take the inductive/projective limits of von Neumann algebras and operator spaces may wish to skip this section of the preliminaries. The definitions below are not always the canonical category theoretic ones; we will at some times make assumptions that match the more intuitive understanding of categories. First, some vocabulary.

. Moving forward, this categorical machinery will mostly be swept under the rug so the upfront cost of establishing limits along any directed set seemed frugal.
∎

To summarize: the categories
OpSp
and
\mathbf{W^{*}}
have all small limits and colimits. Every quantum graph can be characterized in terms of a von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{M}
(roughly, the quantization of the vertices) and an operator space in
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
(roughly, the quantization of the complementary edge set). The (co)limits above give us the candidates for the (co)limits of the quantum graphs.

3
Quantum Graph Morphisms

We continue with a section on quantum graph morphisms rather than quantum graphs, the reason being our definition of connectedness (Definition
4.7
) uses the morphism in Definition
3.8
.

Various other authors have given definitions for a morphism of quantum graphs. In Subsection
3.3
we will see that any two of the notions are equivalent when taking the more restrictive set of hypotheses. We begin with morphisms of classical graphs to give intuition for all these definitions.

3.1
Morphisms of Classical Graphs

In this subsection the classical graphs will be on a finite set of vertices. The following could be extended to infinite classical graphs as well, but this section mainly serves as motivation for the quantum case. As mentioned above, the case of infinitely many vertices will be subsumed in the quantization.

Definition 3.1
.

A graph
G=(V,E)
consists of a set of vertices
V
and and a set of edges
E\subseteq V\times V
.

Definition 3.2
.

If
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
and
G_{2}=(V_{2},E_{2})
are two classical graphs, we say a function between the vertices
V_{1}\overset{f}{\leftarrow}V_{2}
induces a
morphism of classical graphs
if

(f(v),f(w))\in E_{1}\Longleftarrow(v,w)\in E_{2}

or equivalently,

(f(v),f(w))\not\in E_{1}\implies(v,w)\not\in E_{2}.

In preparation for the quantization of the morphisms above, we demonstrate that a morphism of two classical graphs with (finite) vertex sets can be defined in terms of their annihilators in
\ell^{\infty}(V_{i})\otimes_{eh}\ell^{\infty}(V_{i})\cong\ell^{\infty}(V_{i})\otimes\ell^{\infty}(V_{i})
. (The isomorphism follows from the fact the respective operator spaces are finite dimensional.)

Let
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
and
G_{2}=(V_{2},E_{2})
be two classical finite graphs, and consider them as quantum graphs on
\mathcal{M}_{1}:=\ell^{\infty}(V_{1})
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}:=\ell^{\infty}(V_{2})
as in Example
3.8
. Their respective annihilators
\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}_{i})
are left ideals in
\mathcal{M}_{i}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{i}
and a function
V_{1}\overset{f}{\leftarrow}V_{2}
on their vertices induces a unital
*
-homomorphism
\mathcal{M}_{1}\overset{\theta}{\to}\mathcal{M}_{2}
by sending
g\mapsto g\circ f
. Explicitly, for the indicator functions
\chi_{u}:=\chi_{\{u\}}\in\ell^{\infty}(V_{1})
and
\chi_{v}:=\chi_{\{v\}}\in\ell^{\infty}(V_{2})
,

\theta(\chi_{u})=\sum_{f(v)=u}\chi_{v}.

If we consider each von Neumann algebra as a Hilbert space with inner product induced by
\langle\chi_{u}|\chi_{v}\rangle=\delta_{uv}
, then we can consider the adjoint of
\theta
as well:

\theta^{\dagger}(\chi_{v})=\chi_{f(v)}.

Definition 3.3
.

[
10
, Proposition 6.12]

If
\mathcal{H}
is a finite dimensional Hilbert space, the orthogonal complement of a quantum graph
\mathcal{S}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H})
is

\mathcal{S}^{\perp}:=\{T\in B(\mathcal{H}):Tr_{\mathcal{H}}(S^{*}T)=0\,\forall S\in\mathcal{S}\}.

Remark 3.4
.

If
G=(V,E)
is a (finite) graph and
\mathcal{S}\subseteq B(\ell^{2}(V))
is its quantization as in Example
2.4
, then

\mathcal{S}^{\perp}=\text{span}\{|v\rangle\langle w|:(v,w)\notin E\}.

In other words, the orthogonal complement corresponds precisely to the complement of the edges of a classical graph. Since the annihilator also corresponds to the complement of the classical graph (Example
2.33
), the following proposition may be unsurprising.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
and
G_{2}=(V_{2},E_{2})
be finite classical graphs, and let
\mathcal{S}_{1}\subseteq B(\ell^{2}(V_{1}))
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}\subseteq B(\ell^{2}(V_{2}))
be their quantizations. A function
V_{1}\overset{f}{\leftarrow}V_{2}
induces a morphism
G_{1}\leftarrow G_{2}
if and only if

\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\perp})\supseteq\theta^{\dagger}\otimes\theta^{\dagger}(\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}_{2}^{\perp})).

Proof.

From the discussion above, the annihilator and orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{S}_{k}
have concise forms:

\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}_{k})=\text{span}\{\chi_{v}\otimes\chi_{w}:(v,w)\notin E_{k}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{S}_{k}^{\perp}=\text{span}\{|v\rangle\langle w|:(v,w)\notin E_{k}\}.

Hence

\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}_{2}^{\perp})=\text{span}\{\chi_{v}\otimes\chi_{w}:(v,w)\in E_{2}\}.

Since
\theta^{\dagger}(\chi_{v})=\chi_{f(v)}
, we have

\theta^{\dagger}\otimes\theta^{\dagger}(\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}_{2}^{\perp}))=\text{span}\{\chi_{f(v)}\otimes\chi_{f(w)}:(v,w)\in E_{2}\}.

Thus the elementary tensors
\chi_{f(v)}\otimes\chi_{f(w)}
annihilate the operator space
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\perp}
if and only if
(f(v),f(w))\in E_{1}
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

The previous proposition is a direct way to characterize a classical graph morphism using
\ell^{\infty}(V)
instead of
V
and is the intuition behind other notions of morphisms in
[
28
, Proposition 5.3]
and
[
10
, Definition 7.1]
. Note, however, that it relies on the existence of an orthogonality relation in the ambient space of
B(\mathcal{H})
of the operator spaces and an inner product structure on the von Neumann algebra. The following characterization of morphisms does not use the orthogonal complement or inner product and corresponds to the fact that
V_{1}\overset{f}{\leftarrow}V_{2}
induces a morphism of classical graphs if and only if
(f(v),f(w))\notin E_{1}\implies(v,w)\not\in E_{2}
. This will become our definition of a morphism of quantum graphs in Definition
3.8
.
∎

Proposition 3.7
.

The map
V_{1}\overset{f}{\leftarrow}V_{2}
induces a classical graph morphism
G_{1}\leftarrow G_{2}
if and only if
\theta\otimes\theta(\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}_{1}))\subseteq\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}_{2})
.

Proof.

For
\chi_{u}\otimes\chi_{t}\in\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}_{1})
we have

\theta\otimes\theta(\chi_{u}\otimes\chi_{t})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f(v)=u,\\
f(w)=t\end{subarray}}\chi_{v}\otimes\chi_{w}.

If
f
induces a morphism of graphs, none of the edges
(v,w)
can be in
E_{2}
else
(u,t)=(f(v),f(w))\in E_{1}
. By linearity of
\theta\otimes\theta
, we have

\theta\otimes\theta(\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}_{1}))\subseteq\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}_{2}).

On the other hand, as a left ideal in
\ell^{\infty}(V_{2})\otimes\ell^{\infty}(V_{2})
, the annihilator
\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}_{2})
contains all such sums of elementary tensors if and only if it contains each elementary tensor.
∎

\displaystyle=K_{v}|v\rangle\langle w|K_{w}^{\dagger}\in\overline{\text{span}}^{w^{*}}\{|f(v)\rangle\langle v|y|w\rangle\langle f(w)|:v,w\in V_{2},y\in\mathcal{S}_{2}\}.

Thus
V_{1}\overset{f}{\leftarrow}V_{2}
induces a classical graph morphism if and only if

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}

\displaystyle\supseteq\overline{\text{span}}^{w^{*}}\{|f(v)\rangle\langle v|y|w\rangle\langle f(w)|:v,w\in V_{2},\,y\in\mathcal{S}_{2}\}

\displaystyle=\overline{\text{span}}^{w^{*}}\{K_{v}\mathcal{S}_{2}K_{w}^{\dagger}:v,w\in V_{2}\}.

∎

In preparation for the following equivalence, we record a lemma from
[

. The lemma is stated for finite dimensional
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}

Lemma 3.20

(
[
45
, Lemma 8.3]
)
.

Using the same notation as in Definition
3.18
, let
A,B\in B(\mathcal{H}_{1})
be positive,
T\in B(\mathcal{H}_{2})
, and
K_{i},K_{j}\in B(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
. Then

(K_{i}^{\dagger}AK_{i})T(K_{j}^{\dagger}BK_{j})=0\quad\iff\quad(AK_{i})T(K_{j}^{\dagger}B)=0.

Proof.

One direction is obvious, so we only address the
\Rightarrow
implication. Let
D=T(K_{j}^{\dagger}BK_{j})
. From our assumption we have

(K_{i}^{\dagger}AK_{i})\underbrace{T(K_{j}^{\dagger}BK_{j})}_{D}=0\implies D^{\dagger}(K_{i}^{\dagger}AK_{i})D=0.

Since we assumed
A
was positive, we can take its square root and

0=D^{\dagger}(K_{i}^{\dagger}AK_{i})D=(D^{\dagger}K_{i}^{\dagger}\sqrt{A})(\sqrt{A}K_{i}D)=(\sqrt{A}K_{i}D)^{\dagger}(\sqrt{A}K_{i}D).

In any
C^{*}
-algebra
a^{*}a=0
if and only if
a=0
so we have that
\sqrt{A}K_{i}D=0
. Multiplying from the left by
\sqrt{A}
we get

0=AK_{i}D=AK_{i}T(K_{j}^{\dagger}BK_{j}).

Performing an analogous procedure for
D=AK_{i}T
yields the desired desired result.
∎

Lemma 3.21
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H})
be a von Neumann algebra and suppose

\sum_{i\in I}x_{i},\sum_{j\in J}y_{j}\in B(\ell^{2})\overline{\otimes}\mathcal{M}

are
\sigma(B(\ell^{2})_{*}\widehat{\otimes}\mathcal{M}_{*})
convergent sums such that each
x_{i},y_{j}
is positive. For each
T\in B(\ell^{2})\overline{\otimes}B(\mathcal{H})
,

\bigg(\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\bigg)T\bigg(\sum_{j\in J}y_{j}\bigg)=0\quad\iff\quad x_{i}Ty_{j}=0\quad\forall i,j.

Proof.

Since
B(\ell^{2})\overline{\otimes}B(\mathcal{H})
is a dual Banach algebra, multiplication is separately
w^{*}
continuous. We can therefore write

\bigg(\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\bigg)T\bigg(\sum_{j\in J}y_{j}\bigg)=\lim_{G\Subset J}\lim_{F\Subset I}\sum_{i\in F}x_{i}T\sum_{j\in J}y_{j}.

Thus if we assume
x_{i}Ty_{j}=0
for all
i,j
then we have one direction of the statement. For the other direction, denote
T_{Y}:=T\sum_{j}y_{j}
. The above equation reduces to

\bigg(\lim_{F\Subset I}\sum_{i\in F}x_{i}\bigg)T_{Y}=0,

where the limit converges in the
\sigma(B(\mathcal{H})_{*})
topology. Again, using the continuity of multiplication,

T_{Y}^{\dagger}\bigg(\lim_{F\Subset I}\sum_{i\in F}x_{i}\bigg)T_{Y}=0\implies\lim_{F\Subset I}\sum_{i\in F}T_{Y}^{\dagger}x_{i}T_{Y}=0.

Since the latter limit is an increasing net of positive operators, for all
i
we have

\displaystyle T_{Y}^{\dagger}x_{i}T_{Y}=0\implies x_{i}^{1/2}T_{Y}=0\implies x_{i}T_{Y}=0.

(5)

Similarly, Equation
5
implies for all
i

\displaystyle x_{i}T\bigg(\lim_{F\Subset J}\sum_{j\in F}y_{j}\bigg)=0\implies x_{i}Ty_{j}=0.

(6)

∎

showing that

4
Quantum Graphs

Recall that in Subsubsection
2.3.1
we saw that we have a representation-free way to express quantum graphs. We repeat it here for convenience.

Theorem 4.1
.

[
17
, Theorem 3.3]

Let
\mathcal{M}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H})
be a von Neumann algebra. There is a bijection between
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-closed left ideals in
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
and quantum graphs on
\mathcal{M}
. Namely, each quantum graph corresponds uniquely to its annihilator in
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
:

\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S})=\{z\in\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}:\Phi_{z}(T)=0,\,T\in\mathcal{S}\}

where
\Phi
is the map in Definition
2.12
.

4.1
Properties of Quantum Graphs

In this subsection we establish some properties of quantum graphs (namely, reflexivity, symmetry, and connectivity) that are easily detected in their annihilators. The following lemma is surely known (or obvious) to experts, but we explicitly record it here to make sense of the involution
\ddagger
on
\otimes_{eh}
. (Recall
(x\otimes y)^{\ddagger}:=y^{*}\otimes x^{*}
on simple tensors.)

Lemma 4.2
.

The involution on
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
extending

(x\otimes y)^{\ddagger}=y^{*}\otimes x^{*}

is
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-continuous and
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-continuous.

Proof.

We will represent
\mathcal{M}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H})
and only prove
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-continuity since
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-continuity follows the same proof by replacing the operator
B
below by a compact operator. By definition, a net
(z_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
in
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
converges to
z\in\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
in the
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-topology if and only if for every
\phi\in\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*}
we have

\phi(z_{\lambda}-z)\to 0.

From
[
17
, Lemma 2.3]
, every linear functional
\phi\in\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*}
there exists a unique
B\in B(\ell^{2}\otimes_{2}\mathcal{H})
and a trace class operator
T\in B(\ell^{2}\otimes_{2}\mathcal{H})_{*}
such that for
z\in\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
,

\phi(z)=Tr_{\ell^{2}\otimes_{2}\mathcal{H}}(T^{*}(I_{\ell^{2}}\otimes\Phi(z))(B)).

Since
Tr_{\ell^{2}\otimes_{2}\mathcal{H}}(S^{*})=\overline{Tr_{\ell^{2}\otimes_{2}\mathcal{H}}(S)}
,

\text{Tr}(T^{*}(Id\otimes\Phi(z_{\lambda}-z))(B))\to 0\iff\text{Tr}(T(Id\otimes\Phi(z_{\lambda}^{\ddagger}-z^{\ddagger}))(B^{*}))\to 0.

Since the trace class operators
B(\ell^{2}\otimes_{2}\mathcal{H})
and
B(\ell^{2}\otimes_{2}\mathcal{H})
are closed under involution, the latter statement suffices to show that
z_{\lambda}^{\ddagger}\to z^{\ddagger}
in the
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})
topology.
∎

[
10
, Corollary 5.13]
provides the finite dimensional version of the proposition below, but note that the correspondence that the author of
[

uses between quantum graphs and projections is order-preserving while the correspondence between quantum graphs and annihilators is order-reversing. To be precise, the projection

\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S})=(\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M})(1-e).

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a quantum graph on a von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{M}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H})
, and let
\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S})
be its annihilator in
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
. The following properties of
\mathcal{S}
are reflected in properties of its annihilator:

1.

\mathcal{S}
is reflexive quantum graph if and only if for every
z\in\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S})
we have
m(z)=0
.

2.

\mathcal{S}
is symmetric quantum graph if and only if
\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S})
is closed under the involution
(x\otimes y)^{\ddagger}=y^{*}\otimes x^{*}
.

Proof.

For (1): Since
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
embeds completely isometrically into
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{\sigma h}\mathcal{M}
(Remark
2.17
), the multiplication map
m:\mathcal{M}\otimes_{\sigma h}\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
from Corollary
2.29
is also defined on
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
. We have that
z\in\text{Ann}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime})
if and only if for all
T\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}

0=\Phi_{z}(T)=\sum_{i}x_{i}Ty_{i}=T\sum_{i}x_{i}1y_{i}=T\Phi_{z}(1).

Since
1\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, we have

\sum_{i}x_{i}y_{i}=\Phi_{z}(1)=0\iff z\in\text{Ann}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime}).

For (2): From the definition of a symmetric quantum graph,
T\in\mathcal{S}
if and only if
z=\sum_{i}x_{i}\otimes y_{i}\in\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S})
annihilates
T^{*}
as well:

\Phi_{z}(T^{*})=\sum_{i}x_{i}T^{*}y_{i}=0

where the partial sums are uniformly bounded and converging in the WOT topology
[

. Hence the last equality holds if and only if

\sum_{i}y_{i}^{*}Tx_{i}^{*}=0.

That is,
\sum_{i}y_{i}^{*}\otimes x_{i}^{*}\in\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S})

It is at this point we must confront an uncomfortable truth. In order to take the projective limit of quantum graphs, we must restrict ourselves to the quantum graphs whose annihilators are
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-closed left ideals. This is a consequence of the following facts.

1.

The projective limit of the quantum graphs
(\mathcal{M}_{j},\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}_{j}))
must be a quantum graph
\mathcal{S}
on
\mathcal{M}
(that is, a
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-closed left ideal in
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
) in order to satisfy the universal property of projective limits.

2.

The annihilator
\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S})
must contain
\mathcal{A}
in order to satisfy the universal property.

3.

We are only guaranteed that
\mathcal{A}
absorbs left multiplication by
\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
.

4.

If we take the
\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}):=\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})}
, then
\mathcal{A}
is not necessarily a left ideal in
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
. We do have that for every

and
a\in\mathcal{A}
that

. Using
w^{*}
continuity of multiplication in dual Banach algebras, we even have that

n(\lim a_{\lambda})\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})}

for
a_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{A}
and the limit taken in the
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})
topology. However, since
\mathcal{N}
is only a
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*})
-dense subspace of
\mathcal{M}
, the algebraic tensor product
\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{N}
is not
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-dense in
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
. We therefore cannot conclude that
\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})}
is a left ideal with respect to
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
by taking the appropriate limits of elements of
\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{N}
.

5.

However, the tensor product
\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{N}

is

\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-dense in
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
(Lemma
5.1
). We can then use continuity of multiplication to conclude that

(\lim_{\mu}n_{\mu})(\lim_{\lambda}a_{\lambda})

converges (where the limits are taken in the
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*})
topology). Since every element of
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
can be written as
\lim_{\mu}n_{\mu}
, we can thusly show that
\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*})}
is a left ideal in
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
.

6.

Finally, every object is the projective limit along the directed set
(\{1\},\leq)
(the directed set of one element that is comparable to itself). Hence we must necessarily exclude the
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-closed left ideals if we would like to take arbitrary projective limits of a class of quantum graphs.

See Subsection
5.1
for examples and a non-example of such quantum graphs. This class of graphs does include all quantizations of classical graphs and quantum Cayley graphs of discrete quantum groups
[
43
]
, for example. With this caveat out of the way, we proceed to prove that the projective limit of
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*})
quantum graphs exists.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{M}
is a von Neumann algebra. If
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
is a
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*})
-dense unital
*
-algebra then
\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{N}
is a
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-dense subset of
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{*}
is norm-dense in
\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*}
(Definition
2.20
) and that
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}
is
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-dense in
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
(Corollary
2.25
). It thus suffices to show that we cannot separate
\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{N}
from
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}
by functionals in
\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{*}
, and this is precisely the property guaranteed by the
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*})
-density of
\mathcal{N}
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

If

.

Proof.

There must exist

such that

Since

\displaystyle=\theta_{j,\infty}^{(2)}(z_{j})=\theta_{\ell,\infty}^{(2)}\circ\theta_{j,\ell}^{(2)}(z_{j})

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\theta_{k,\infty}^{(2)}(a_{k})=\theta_{\ell,\infty}^{(2)}\circ\theta_{k,\ell}^{(2)}(a_{k}).

∎

5.2
Implications of Quantum Graph Limits

Here we make some observations on the projective limit above and collect some easy corollaries.

Remark 5.10
.

The work above is done for the projective limit of quantum graphs. The same procedure can be done for the
inductive
limit of quantum graphs. Recall the categories of
{\mathbf{W^{*}}}
and
{\mathbf{OpSp}}
contain all small limits as well (Section
2
). That is,

(\mathcal{N},\varphi_{k}):=\varprojlim_{\bf W^{*}}\mathcal{M}_{k}\qquad\mathcal{B}:=\varprojlim_{\bf OpSp}\mathcal{\text{Ann}}_{k}

both exist. The algebraic tensor product
\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{N}
is still
\sigma(\mathcal{N}_{*}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{N}_{*})
-dense in
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
. Following Lemma
5.2
, we can show the set

\bigg\{b\in\mathcal{B}:\exists k\in I,b_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}^{(2)}\text{ s.t. }\varphi_{k}^{(2)}(b_{k})=b\bigg\}

absorbs left multiplication by the following set

\bigg\{z\in\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{N}:\exists k\in I,z_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}^{(2)}\text{ s.t. }\varphi_{k}^{(2)}(z_{k})=z\bigg\}.

The remainder of the argument follows similarly by using separate
w^{*}
continuity of multiplication and approximation of elements.
∎

Corollary 5.11
.

The inductive or projective limit of reflexive quantum graphs is not necessarily reflexive.

Proof.

Example
5.9
demonstrates that although the multiplication map
m:\mathcal{M}\otimes_{\sigma h}\mathcal{M}
(Corollary
2.29
) can be defined on
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
, it is not
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-continuous. Hence we cannot conclude that the projective limit
\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{w^{*}}
in Theorem
5.4
is the annihilator of a reflexive quantum graph even if the quantum graphs in the projective system are reflexive.
∎

Corollary 5.12
.

The inductive or projective limit of symmetric quantum graphs is symmetric.

Proof.

This follows from the
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-continuity of the involution
(x\otimes y)^{\ddagger}
(Lemma
4.2
).
∎

Since we framed connectedness in terms of morphisms (Definition
4.7
), we also immediately have the following corollary.

Corollary 5.13
.

If
(\mathcal{M}_{k},\text{Ann}_{k},\varphi_{j,k})
is an inductive system of quantum relations that are each not strongly connected, the inductive limit is also not strongly connected.

Proof.

If each
(\mathcal{M}_{k},\text{Ann}_{k})
is a strongly disconnected quantum relation, for each
k\in I
there exists a
\mathbf{W^{*}}
morphism
\mathbb{C}^{2}\overset{\psi_{k}}{\to}\mathcal{M}_{k}
inducing a non-trivial morphism from
\text{Ann}(T_{2})
to
\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}_{k})
. By the universal property of the
OpSp
limit, there must be a non-trivial morphism from
\text{Ann}(T_{2})
to
\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{w^{*}}
.
∎

6
Operator C*-Spaces

Working with annihilators
\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S})
instead of the bimodules
\mathcal{S}
directly seems rather unintuitive. Moreover, much of the motivation for quantum graphs arises from the finite-dimensional case in which the dualization of the vertex set (i.e.,
C(V)
or
\ell^{\infty}(V)
) is both a
C^{*}
-algebra and a von Neumann algebra. This section explores the implications of alternative choices for these conventions; that is, we will generalize graphs as
C^{*}
-algebra bimodules and attempt to define a morphism strictly in terms of the bimodules.

A belated acknowledgement: This entire paper was initially inspired by
[
27
]
in which the authors take some projective limits along
\mathbb{N}
of finite classical graphs and define their graphs on
C^{*}
-algebras instead of von Neumann algebras. We sketch their construction below and use it to inspire our
C^{*}
-bimodule graphs.

6.1
Operator C*-Systems

The authors of
[
27
]
define the category of
operator
C^{*}
-systems
which we will denote
{\mathbf{OC^{*}Sy}}
. The objects in this category consist of an operator system
\mathcal{S}
and a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathcal{S}
is an
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule. The authors use the representation-independent notion of operator system consisting of an order unit and cones, but for this section it will suffice to know that every operator system “is” a unital,
*
-closed subspace of some unital
C^{*}
-algebra where the unit in
\mathcal{S}
is the same unit as the
C^{*}
-algebra. A morphism of this category is a pair
(u,\pi):(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{1})\to(\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{A}_{2})
such that
u:\mathcal{S}_{1}\to\mathcal{S}_{2}
is a UCP map and
\pi:\mathcal{A}_{1}\to\mathcal{A}_{2}
is a

morphism (i.e., a unital
*
-homomorphism) such that for all
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
and

\displaystyle u(a_{1}\cdot s\cdot a_{2})=\pi(a_{1})\cdot u(s)\cdot\pi(a_{2})

(9)

where we use
\cdot
to indicate the bimodule action here. The authors then use the category above to define the inductive limit of graph operator systems (these are assumed to be undirected graphs with all self-loops). Let
\mathcal{S}_{k}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H}_{k})
be classical finite graph operator systems which will necessarily be bimodules over the diagonal algebras
\mathcal{D}_{k}
in each
B(\mathcal{H}_{k})
. Assume there are
{\bf{C^{*}}}
morphisms
\pi_{k,k+1}:B(\mathcal{H}_{k})\to B(\mathcal{H}_{k+1})
such that we get the following inductive system
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B(\mathcal{H}_{1})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B(\mathcal{H}_{2})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B(\mathcal{H}_{3})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces...}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1,2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{2,3}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{3,4}}

and that

. Being unital
*
-homomorphisms, the

maps induce an inductive systems of the operator systems in the category
{\bf{OS}}
of operator systems
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{S}_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{S}_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{S}_{3}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces...}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1,2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{2,3}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{3,4}}

and of the diagonal algebras in each

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}_{2}}

where the last inductive system is taken in

. The authors then go on to show that

(\varinjlim_{\bf{C^{*}}}\mathcal{D}_{k},\overline{\varinjlim_{\bf{OS}}\mathcal{S}_{k}}^{\|\cdot\|},\varinjlim_{\bf{C^{*}}}B(\mathcal{H}_{k}))

is the inductive limit of

in

and also define a corresponding inductive limit graph.

Remark 6.1
.

Note that not every classical graph morphism
G_{1}\leftarrow G_{2}
induces a UCP map
\mathcal{S}_{1}\to\mathcal{S}_{2}
on the graph operator systems. Consider the two graphs

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle=(V_{1}=\{v\},E_{1}=\{(v,v)\})

\displaystyle G_{2}

\displaystyle=(V_{2}=\{w_{1},w_{2}\},E_{2}=\{(w_{1},w_{1}),(w_{2},w_{2}),(w_{1},w_{2})\}).

The map

\displaystyle V_{1}\overset{f}{\leftarrow}V_{2}

such that
v=f(w_{1})=f(w_{2})
induces a graph morphism
G_{1}\leftarrow G_{2}
, but the corresponding induced map
\mathcal{S}_{1}\overset{\phi}{\leftarrow}\mathcal{S}_{2}
is not trace-preserving. For instance, we have

|v\rangle\langle v|=\phi(|w_{1}\rangle\langle w_{2}|).

The (pre)adjoint
\phi^{\dagger}:\mathcal{S}_{1}\to\mathcal{S}_{2}
therefore cannot be unital. However, this is essentially the only obstruction. A classical graph morphism
G_{1}\overset{f}{\leftarrow}G_{2}
will induce a UCP map
\mathcal{S}_{1}\to\mathcal{S}_{2}
if and only if

(u,v)\in E_{2}\text{ and }u\neq v\implies f(u)\neq f(v).

∎

Although classical graph morphisms (between finite graphs) do not generally induce morphisms of operator
systems
, they do always induce morphisms of operator
spaces
. To reframe the
[
27
]
work for arbitrary classical graph morphisms, we introduce
operator
C^{*}
-spaces
in analogy with operator
C^{*}
-systems.

6.2
Operator C*-Spaces

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
and
G_{2}=(V_{2},E_{2})
be finite classical graphs. Let
V_{1}\overset{f}{\leftarrow}V_{2}
,
C(V_{1})\overset{\pi}{\to}C(V_{2})
, and
C(V_{1})\otimes C(V_{1})\overset{e}{\leftarrow}C(V_{2})\otimes C(V_{2})
be defined as above. Then

\displaystyle a_{1}\cdot e(s)\cdot a_{2}=e(\pi(a_{1})\cdot s\cdot\pi(a_{2}))

(10)

and
f
induces a graph morphism if and only if
\mathcal{S}_{1}\supseteq e(\mathcal{S}_{2})
.

Proof.

We first check that Equation
10
is does indeed hold. Since we are working in finite dimensions, it suffices to show equality for
a_{1}=\chi_{t}
,
a_{2}=\chi_{u}
and
s=|v\rangle\langle w|
. In this case, Equation
10
becomes

\chi_{t}\cdot|f(v)\rangle\langle f(w)|\cdot\chi_{u}=e\bigg(\bigg(\sum_{t=f(v^{\prime})}\chi_{v^{\prime}}\bigg)|v\rangle\langle w|\bigg(\sum_{u=f(w^{\prime})}\chi_{w^{\prime}}\bigg)\bigg).

The LHS is 0 if and only if
f(v)\neq t
or
f(w)\neq u
. Therefore either

\bigg(\sum_{t=f(v^{\prime})}\chi_{v^{\prime}}\bigg)|v\rangle=0\qquad\text{or}\qquad\langle w|\bigg(\sum_{u=f(w^{\prime})}\chi_{w^{\prime}}\bigg)=0

so the equation holds in that case. On the other hand, the LHS is non-zero if and only if

\chi_{t}\cdot|f(v)\rangle\langle f(w)|\cdot\chi_{u}=|f(v)\rangle\langle f(w)|\iff f(v)=t\text{ and }f(w)=u

in which case the RHS becomes

e\bigg(\bigg(\sum_{t=f(v^{\prime})}\chi_{v^{\prime}}\bigg)|v\rangle\langle w|\bigg(\sum_{u=f(w^{\prime})}\chi_{w^{\prime}}\bigg)\bigg)=e(|v\rangle\langle w|)=|f(v)\rangle\langle f(w)|

just as claimed. Finally, we see that
f
induces a graph morphism if and only if for every
|v\rangle\langle w|\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
we have
|f(v)\rangle\langle f(w)|\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
, which is clearly exactly the condition that
\mathcal{S}_{1}\supseteq e(\mathcal{S}_{2})
.
∎

We now generalize to arbitrary dimensions. In such cases, we cannot always impose an inner product structure on the
C^{*}
-algebra
C(V)
, and so the adjoint maps
\pi^{\dagger}
and
e=\pi^{\dagger}\otimes\pi^{\dagger}
are not defined. There is also the issue that the algebraic tensor product
C(V)\otimes C(V)
is not necessarily an operator space. We will therefore simply ask for a
C^{*}
-algebra morphism
\pi
and an operator space morphism
e
that satisfies Equation
10
in order to define a morphism of these bimodules. (For a classical, finite graph this would correspond to asking for both a map
V_{1}\overset{f}{\leftarrow}V_{2}
and a map
E_{1}\overset{e}{\leftarrow}E_{2}
such that

(f(v),f(w))=e((v,w)).

There may be other ways around the ill-definedness of
†
of which I am unaware.)

Definition 6.3
.

A
C
∗
-graph
is a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
and an operator space
\mathcal{S}
such that
\mathcal{S}
is an
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule. If
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{1})
and
(\mathcal{A}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{2})
are C

-graphs, a pair of maps

\mathcal{S}_{1}\overset{e}{\longleftarrow}\mathcal{S}_{2}\qquad\mathcal{A}_{1}\overset{\pi}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{A}_{2}

is a

from

to

if
e
is completely contractive and
\pi
is a
*
-homomorphism such that

\displaystyle a_{1}\cdot e(s)\cdot a_{2}=e(\pi(a_{1})\cdot s\cdot\pi(a_{2}))

(11)

for all

and
s\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
.

Remark 6.4
.

Note that Equation
11
is
not
the usual notion of morphism of
C^{*}
-correspondences (or of operator bimodules). Generally, one would expect a morphism
X_{\mathcal{A}}\to Y_{\mathcal{B}}
of right modules to be given by two maps
e:X_{\mathcal{A}}\to Y_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\pi:A\to B
such that

e(x\cdot a)=e(x)\cdot\pi(a).

In particular,
e
and
\pi
are
covariant
. However, we see that our maps
e
and
\pi
in Definition
6.3
above must be contravariant to recover the classical case.
∎

Naturally, we would like to see if this category has limits. We will show that projective limits exist. See
[
34
, Chapter 2.6]
for a quick construction of the (amalgamated) free product of
C^{*}
-algebras; we hope this convinces the reader that one can take inductive limits in the category

Theorem 6.5
.

If
((\mathcal{A}_{j},\mathcal{S}_{j}),(\pi_{j,k},e^{j,k}))_{j\in J}
is a projective system of

\bigg((\mathcal{A}:=\varinjlim_{\mathbf{C^{*}}}\mathcal{A}_{j},\pi_{j,\infty}),(\mathcal{S}:=\varprojlim_{\mathbf{OpSp}}\mathcal{S}_{j},e^{j,\infty})\bigg)

is its projective limit.

Proof.

The uniqueness of the object
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{S})
in the projective limit follows from the uniqueness of the limits in
\mathbf{OpSp}
and
\mathbf{C^{*}}
. We will verify that
\mathcal{S}
is an
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule in a canonical way and each
(e^{j,\infty},\pi_{j,\infty})
induces a

-graph morphism from

to

. We thus need only show that

is an
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule. There are many ways to write down the inductive/projective limits (again, see

\mathcal{A}:=\varinjlim_{\mathbf{C^{*}}}\mathcal{A}_{j}=\overline{\bigcup_{j}\pi_{j,\infty}(\mathcal{A}_{j})}^{\|\cdot\|}.

where

is the norm on

is well-defined, as showing the right action is precisely analogous. Fix

We will define

for an arbitrary

. Since

. We claim the action

7
Concluding Remarks

We dub quantum graphs “
W^{*}
-graphs” in this subsection for aesthetic symmetry.

7.1
C^{*}
-Graphs

Remark 7.1
.

Since
W^{*}
-graphs quantize the vertices as
\ell^{\infty}(V)
, there are no extra topological considerations on
V
. If we are to quantize the vertices as
C(V)
, however, Gelfand duality requires
V
be compact (or at least locally compact) and
f
be continuous. The dual category to
C^{*}
-graphs on commutative
C^{*}
-algebras is therefore no longer quite the category of graphs.
∎

Remark 7.2
.

An argument in favor of
C^{*}
-algebraic route is that the inductive limit of C
∗
-algebras generally preserves more of the structure of the algebras. For instance, the enveloping von Neumann algebras of any two uniformly hyperfinite (UHF) algebras
\varinjlim_{\mathbf{C^{*}}}M_{n_{k}}
and
\varinjlim_{\mathbf{C^{*}}}M_{n_{j}}
are isomorphic
[
31
, Corollary 5.2]
. For UHF algebras,
\varinjlim_{\mathbf{W^{*}}}M_{n_{k}}
and
\varinjlim_{\mathbf{W^{*}}}M_{n_{j}}
are precisely the enveloping von Neumann algebras of the respective
C^{*}
-algebraic limits. (One can see this by 1) noting the adjoint to the inclusion function
F:\mathbf{W^{*}}\to\mathbf{C^{*}}
is the functor that sends each
C^{*}
-algebra to its double dual and 2) recalling left adjoints preserve colimits.) The relationship between
\varinjlim_{\mathbf{C^{*}}}\mathcal{M}_{j}
and
\varinjlim_{\mathbf{W^{*}}}\mathcal{M}_{j}
for arbitrary von Neumann algebras
\mathcal{M}_{j}
is generally not as neat, however. We again refer to reader to
[
18
]
.
∎

Remark 7.3
.

Could one define a morphism of
C^{*}
-graphs using annihilators as in Section
3
? No, at least not with the
\otimes_{eh}
tensor product. The key property of
\otimes_{eh}
is that for any unital subalgebras
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H})
(with no topological assumptions)

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=CB_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}(K(\mathcal{H}),B(\mathcal{H}))=CB^{\sigma}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}(B(\mathcal{H}))

[
3
, Theorem 4.2(ii)]
. Thus
\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
naturally identifies the
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
bimodules, but this does not necessarily hold for bimodules over other algebras. It may be possible to characterize
C^{*}
-graphs with annihilators in other tensor products.
∎

Remark 7.4
.

Could one define a morphism of
W^{*}
-graphs by replacing the
\mathbf{C^{*}}
morphisms with
\mathbf{W^{*}}
morphism and using a similar map
e
to induce the mapping of edges/operator spaces? Possibly. However, map
e
would need to be relaxed to a
w^{*}
-continuous morphism so we must descend to a subcategory of
{\bf{OpSp}}
where the objects are dual operator spaces.
∎

7.2
W^{*}
-Graphs

Remark 7.5
.

As noted above, one can take the projective (or indeed, inductive limit) of
W^{*}
-graphs along an index category that is a directed set. The construction above does require that the index be directed so that in Lemma
5.2
the space
\mathcal{A}
naturally absorbs left multiplication by a
w^{*}
-dense subset of the ambient von Neumann algebra.

Given the fuss about products and coproducts in the preliminaries (Section
2
), in retrospect it may have been more elegant to find the (co)product and (co)equalizer in the category of
W^{*}
-graphs to prove such all small (co)limits exist. See Question
8.3
.
∎

Remark 7.6
.

In quantum information theory, quantum channels can be characterized as UCP maps between von Neumann algebras (or dually, CPTP maps). More precisely, the quantum channel is a UCP map on the observables that generate a von Neumann algebra. One would naturally try to take a (co)limit in the category of von Neumann algebras with normal UCP maps as morphisms. However, I do not know whether the category of von Neumann algebras with UCP maps has (co)limits.

In any case, this category of von Neumann algebras and normal UCP maps may be the wrong interpretation in the setting of quantum channels. Let
\Phi:\mathcal{M}_{1}\to\mathcal{M}_{2}
be a quantum channel (i.e., a normal UCP map) with a Kraus form
\Phi(T)=\sum_{i\in I}K_{i}^{\dagger}TK_{i}
and define

\mathcal{S}:=\overline{\text{span}}^{\sigma(B(\mathcal{H})_{*})}\{K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{j}:i,j\in I\}

to be its quantum confusability graph. (I do not know if confusability graphs have been defined for quantum channels beyond the finite dimensional case, but this seems like a reasonable definition.) Similarly, let
\mathcal{T}
be the quantum confusability graph for a quantum channel
\Psi:\mathcal{N}_{1}\to\mathcal{N}_{2}
. A morphism from
\mathcal{S}
to
\mathcal{T}
is implemented by a
\mathbf{W^{*}}
morphism
\theta:\mathcal{N}_{1}\to\mathcal{M}_{1}
. The algebraic relations of
\mathcal{N}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{1}
are a fundamental property of the observables in the respective von Neumann algebras – namely, observables commute if and only if they can be simultaneously measured. A UCP map would not preserve these relations.
∎

8.1
Transitivity of Quantum Graphs

A specter that has haunted many investigations of quantum graphs returns to the question of transitivity. A quantum graph
\mathcal{S}
on a von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{M}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H})
is
transitive
if
\mathcal{S}^{2}\subseteq\mathcal{S}

[
44
, Definition 2.4]
. In
[
17
, Remark 3.4(iii)]
, the author asks how one might obtain
\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S}^{2})
from
\text{Ann}(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\mathcal{M}\otimes_{eh}\mathcal{M}
. It also plays a role in abstractly defining a
quantum distance operator
. Ideally, such an operator should have a categorical characterization as adjacency operators do in Appendix
A
.

8.2
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*})

Given the significance of
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{*}\otimes_{h}\mathcal{M}_{*})
-closed graphs in Section
5

5.7
, all quantum graphs on a von Neumann algebra of the form
\mathcal{M}=\prod_{i\in I}^{\infty}M_{n_{i}}
are examples.

8.3
Coproduct of Quantum Graphs

The categorical product of two finite classical graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
is the so-called
tensor product
of graphs, so-called because the adjacency operator of
G_{1}\prod G_{2}
is the tensor product of the adjacency operators of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. The vertex set of this categorical product is
V_{1}\otimes V_{2}
. A naive quantization of the vertex set might be
\ell^{\infty}(V_{1})\otimes\ell^{\infty}(V_{2})
.

In the category of quantum graphs, however, the “vertex set” of the categorical product of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
is the free product of von Neumann algebras (see Remark
2.60
). Without going into precise detail about how to form the free product, it is without a doubt much bigger than
\ell^{\infty}(V_{1})\otimes\ell^{\infty}(V_{2})
. However, this is not so much an obstruction as an interesting feature of non-commutative algebras. The coproduct in the category of
commutative
associative algebras is the tensor product, which aligns with the classical case. Allowing our algebras to be non-commutative necessitates free product-like constructions in the coproduct, a byproduct of which is that a product of quantum graphs must be a much larger object than in the classical case.

8.4
t
-Morphisms of Quantum Graphs

The morphisms explored in Subsection
3.3
are considered
classical
morphisms of quantum graphs because no external quantum resources are involved in the morphisms. Allowing the use of quantum resources naturally leads to the graph homomorphism game (see, for example,
[
4
]
). Non-local game theory distinguishes among the types of quantum resources available to the players in the non-local game.
[
26
, Definition 4.1]
generalizes
t
-homomorphisms from
[
4
]
for
t\in\{loc,q,qa,C^{*},alg\}
via
{\bf W^{*}}
morphisms. Namely, a
t
-homomorphism from
(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{1})
to
(\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{M}_{2})
for finite dimensional von Neumann algebras
\mathcal{M}_{k}
is a unital
*
-algebra
\mathcal{A}_{t}
and a unital
*
-homomorphism
\theta:\mathcal{M}_{2}\to\mathcal{M}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{t}
satisfying

\theta^{\dagger}(\mathcal{S}_{1}\otimes 1_{\mathcal{A}_{t}})\theta\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{2}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{t}

and
\mathcal{A}_{t}
is an algebra whose type depends on
t
. (The notation
\theta^{\dagger}
does
not
indicate the adjoint of a map between Hilbert spaces; we refer the reader to
[

for the definition.) As of this writing there does not seem to be a translation for

8.5
Quantum Cayley Graphs of (Profinite) Quantum Groups

A classical theorem states that a Cayley graph of a profinite group is always a profinite graph
[
35
, Example 2.1.12]
. The question of whether this statement still holds if “quantum” is inserted before every noun is still open. As of this writing, there does not seem to be a definition for the Cayley graph of a general quantum group, though we have one for discrete quantum groups from
[
43
]
. In
[
17
]
, the author proposes a definition for the invariant quantum relations (invariant quantum graphs, according to the conventions of this paper) of a quantum group. The author proves that his proposed definition works in the case in which the quantum group is a classical locally compact group and claims that the proof works the same when replacing the relevant objects by their analogues in
[
20
]
.

We have a notion of profinite quantum graphs from Section
5
. If one allows Cayley graphs to be degenerate (i.e., suppose that the generators of the Cayley graph do not generate the group), then
G
-invariant relations in
G\times G
are precisely the Cayley graphs of
G
. We thus have candidates for the quantum Cayley graphs of a profinite quantum groups. It remains to see if the classical theorem holds in the quantum case.

Appendix A
A Taste of Categorification

The purpose of this appendix is to introduce just enough terminology to show that classical homomorphisms of finite quantum graphs introduced in
[
28
, Section 5.2]
are equivalent to a morphism of finite quantum graphs in Definition
3.8
.

The authors of
[
36
]
quantize (finite) sets, functions, and (finite) graphs through categorification. Roughly, one characterizes the classical objects or properties via morphisms in the category
Set
and interpret them in another category (
FHilb
, the category of finite dimensional Hilbert spaces and linear maps, for instance) to obtain their quantum analogs.

Definitions will be accompanied by motivating examples. The authors in
[
28
]
develop quantum graphs by quantizing the adjacency matrix of a graph via string diagrams. This is part of a broader trend of categorification: by rephrasing familiar objects and phenomena in categorical terms, we achieve a new intuition of the categorified object. In this appendix we will categorify undirected graphs on finitely many vertices. We will not introduce the graphical methods here. For short overviews on graphical methods, see
[
28
]
and
[
16
, Chapter 4]
. For an excellent textbook, see
[
19
]
.

Our conventions: in this appendix, all Hilbert spaces are finite dimensional. If
\varphi:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
is a map between Hilbert spaces, we denote its adjoint by
\varphi^{\dagger}:\mathcal{H}_{2}\to\mathcal{H}_{1}
.

Definition A.1
.

An
algebra
is a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with a multiplication map
m:\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
and unit map
u:\mathbb{C}\to\mathcal{H}
satisfying the following equations:

\displaystyle(m\otimes I_{\mathcal{H}})\circ(m\otimes I_{\mathcal{H}})

\displaystyle=(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes m)\circ(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes m)

\displaystyle(\text{associativity)}

\displaystyle m\circ(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes u)

\displaystyle=I_{\mathcal{H}}=m\circ(u\otimes I_{\mathcal{H}})

\displaystyle(\text{unitality}).

A
coalgebra
is a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with a comultiplication map
\delta:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
and a counit map
\varepsilon:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying the following equations:

\displaystyle(\delta\otimes I_{\mathcal{H}})\circ\delta

\displaystyle=(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes\delta)\circ\delta

\displaystyle(\text{coassociativity})

\displaystyle(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes\varepsilon)\circ\delta

\displaystyle=I_{\mathcal{H}}=(\varepsilon\otimes I_{\mathcal{H}})\circ\delta

\displaystyle(\text{counitality)}

As the names suggest, if
(\mathcal{H},m,u)
is an algebra then
(\mathcal{H},m^{\dagger},u^{\dagger})
is a coalgebra.

Example A.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space with orthonormal basis
\{|i\rangle\}_{i\in[n]}
. The following maps make
\mathcal{H}
an algebra:

\displaystyle m(|i\rangle\otimes|j\rangle)

\displaystyle=\langle i|j\rangle|i\rangle

\displaystyle u(1_{\mathbb{C}})=\sum_{i\in[n]}|i\rangle.

Their adjoints are given by

\displaystyle m^{\dagger}(|i\rangle)

\displaystyle=|i\rangle\otimes|i\rangle

\displaystyle u^{\dagger}(|i\rangle)=1_{\mathbb{C}}.

∎

The objects of interest are both algebras and coalgebras. Namely, the structures
m
and
u
are compatible with
m^{\dagger}
and
u^{\dagger}
in the following way.

Definition A.3
.

A
dagger Frobenius algebra
is an algebra
(\mathcal{H},m,u)
such that

\displaystyle(m\otimes I_{\mathcal{H}})\circ(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes m^{\dagger})=m^{\dagger}\circ m=(I_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes m)\circ(m^{\dagger}\otimes I_{\mathcal{H}}).

Define the swap map
\sigma:\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
by
\sigma(|i\rangle\otimes|j\rangle)=|j\rangle\otimes|i\rangle
. Such an algebra is
special
if

m^{\dagger}\circ m=I_{\mathcal{H}}.

symmetric
if

u^{\dagger}\circ m\circ\sigma=u^{\dagger}\circ m

and
commutative
if

m\circ\sigma=m.

Definition A.4
