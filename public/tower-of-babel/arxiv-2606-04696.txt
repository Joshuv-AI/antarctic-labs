The No-Core Principle for Stationary Actions and Ends of Stationary Random Subgroups
Yair Hartman, Nadav Kalma

Abstract
We prove a no-core principle for stationary actions of countable groups. Namely, if a Borel set intersects almost every orbit in finitely many points and has positive measure, then it is supported, modulo null sets, on the finite-orbit part of the action. This extends to stationary actions a basic regularity phenomenon known for measure-preserving actions. We apply this principle to the geometry of Stationary Random Subgroups. For a finitely generated group, we prove that the Schreier graph of a stationary random subgroup has almost surely 0 , 1 , 2 0,1,2 , or infinitely many ends. Finally, we contrast this probabilistic regularity with the topological notion of Boomerang subgroups: for every k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 , including k = ℵ 0 k=\aleph_{0} , we construct a Boomerang subgroup of 𝔽 3 \mathbb{F}_{3} whose Schreier graph has exactly k k ends.



1
Introduction

In the theory of group actions on measure spaces, one considers probability measure-preserving (p.m.p.) actions and non-singular actions, namely those that preserve the measure class. Non-singular actions provide a broad framework requiring minimal rigid structure. While every p.m.p. action is inherently non-singular, the former exhibits a wide variety of strong regularity properties.

Of particular interest to our work is the fact that, on the infinite-orbit part, p.m.p. actions do not admit Borel selectors of positive measure.

Stationary actions occupy an intermediate realm; these are actions equipped with a measure that is invariant under convolution with respect to a probability measure
\mu\in\operatorname{Prob}(\Gamma)
(a “random walk”) on the group. Every p.m.p. action is
\mu
-stationary, for every
\mu
, and every
\mu
-stationary action is non-singular, assuming that
\mu
is
generating
, namely, the support of
\mu
generates
\Gamma
as a semigroup. A key advantage of stationary measures over p.m.p. actions is their guaranteed existence on compact spaces, even for actions of non-amenable groups. At the same time, stationary measures are more “regulated” than general non-singular actions.

A natural question arises: which of the properties and regularities of p.m.p. actions extend to the broader context of stationary actions?

Our first result shows that if a stationary action admits a positive-measure “core”, then that core is necessarily supported on finite orbits.

Theorem 1.1

(No-core principle for stationary actions)
.

Let
\mu\in\operatorname{Prob}(\Gamma)
be a generating measure, and let
(\Gamma,\mu)\curvearrowright(X,\nu)
be a stationary action. Let
C\subseteq X
be a measurable set with
\nu(C)>0,
such that for
\nu
-a.e.
x\in X
,
|\Gamma x\cap C|<\infty
. Then for
\nu
-a.e.
x\in C
,
|\Gamma x|<\infty
.

Equivalently, almost every point in
C
has a finite orbit.

We call a set
C
as in the theorem above a
core
.

Our result holds in particular for p.m.p. actions, as they are
\mu
-stationary for every generating
\mu
. This result for p.m.p. actions is known using tools of equivalence relations and Descriptive Set Theory (see, for example
[
8
]
). It is interesting that our proof uses random walks to prove this result, even in the p.m.p. setup. One should note that the no-core principle does not hold for general non-singular actions.

The No-Core Principle was first introduced in
[
2
]
to prove a result regarding the space of ends for unimodular random manifolds. We apply this principle to address a similar question in the theory of Stationary Random Subgroups (SRSs).

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete countable group,
let
\text{Sub}_{\Gamma}
denote the compact space of all subgroups of
\Gamma
, equipped with the Chabauty topology, and with the conjugation action of
\Gamma
. A random subgroup is a
probability measure on
\text{Sub}_{\Gamma}
.
An Invariant Random Subgroup (IRS), introduced in
[
3
]
, is a conjugation-invariant probability measure on
\text{Sub}_{\Gamma}
. Equivalently, the conjugation action
\Gamma\curvearrowright(\text{Sub}_{\Gamma},\nu)
is p.m.p.
Similarly, a
\mu
-Stationary Random Subgroup (SRS) is a
\mu
-stationary measure on
\text{Sub}_{\Gamma}
.

By viewing a subgroup geometrically via its Schreier graph, one can ask which geometric properties of normal subgroups generalize to IRSs, and subsequently, which properties of IRSs extend to SRSs. In this article, we address this question concerning the ends of a graph.

For a locally finite graph, the space of ends is a topological invariant that formalizes the notion of “directions to infinity”. The classical theorem of Freudenthal
[
7
]
and Hopf
[
11
]
establishes that the Cayley graph of a finitely generated group has exactly
0,1,2
, or infinitely (uncountable) many ends. Furthermore, this number is independent of the choice of generating set, making it a group invariant. Building on this, Stallings
[
16
]
proved that the number of ends reveals deep algebraic information about the group; for example, a group is 2-ended if and only if it is virtually
\mathbb{Z}
.

Motivated by these classical results, we say a collection of graphs is
end-rigid
if each of its graphs has exactly
0,1,2
, or infinitely many ends. Thus the collection of Cayley graphs of finitely generated groups is end-rigid. Equivalently, the Schreier graphs associated with normal subgroups (which are Cayley graphs of quotients), are end-rigid.
Abels
[
1
]
proved that the collection of quasi-transitive graphs (i.e., all graphs with a finite number of orbits under their automorphism group) is end-rigid. In terms of
\text{Sub}_{\Gamma}
, all Schreier graphs of subgroups with finite conjugacy classes are end-rigid.

Consequently, given a probability measure on
\text{Sub}_{\Gamma}
, one may ask whether the Schreier graph of almost every subgroup
\Delta<\Gamma
is end-rigid. For IRSs, a theorem of Lyons and Schramm
[
15
]
regarding unimodular graphs provides an affirmative answer. Moreover, Curien
[
6
, Corollary 24]
proved that the stationary random graphs, with respect to nearest neighbor transitions, are end-rigid as well.

In this article, we prove that this rigidity phenomenon also holds for general SRSs.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group generated by a finite symmetric set
S
, and let
\mu\in\operatorname{Prob}(\Gamma)
be a generating measure. Let
\nu\in\operatorname{Prob}(\text{Sub}_{\Gamma})
be a
\mu
-stationary random subgroup.
Then

|\operatorname{Ends}(\operatorname{Sch}(H\backslash\Gamma,S))|\in\{0,1,2,\infty\}

for
\nu
-almost every
H
.

Here,
\operatorname{Ends}(\operatorname{Sch}(H\backslash\Gamma,S))
denotes the ends space of the Schreier graph of subgroup
H
with respect to the generating set
S
.

For IRSs, unimodularity gives the stronger conclusion that the infinite case is uncountable. Our argument for SRSs rules out finite numbers of ends larger than two, but does not rule out countably infinitely many ends.

Simultaneously with and independently of our work, Levit and Silman
[
14
]
proved a related result in a slightly different setting, using different tools. Their argument applies to the general framework of random metric-measure spaces and they also show that when the end space is infinite, it is topologically, then it is a Cantor set. Moreover, they classify
the homeomorphism types of surfaces, generalizing the work of Biringer-Raimbault
[
5
]
on unimodular Riemannian manifolds
to the stationay setup.

In the finitely generated group setting, however, the approach taken in
[
14
]
requires the random walk to be finitely supported and symmetric, assumptions that are not needed in our argument.
This leaves open the following natural question: can a
\mu
-SRS for an infinitely supported or non-symmetric measure have countably infinitely many ends almost surely?

While SRSs provide a probabilistic generalization of IRSs, one can also consider the topological related notion of Boomerang subgroups. Introduced in
[
9
]
, a subgroup
\Delta\leq\Gamma
is called a Boomerang subgroup if for every
g\in\Gamma
, the sequence of conjugates
(g^{n}\Delta g^{-n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
admits a subsequence converging to
\Delta
in the Chabauty topology. The relation to IRSs is the following: if
\lambda
is any IRS on
\Gamma
, then
\lambda
-almost every subgroup is a Boomerang subgroup; that is,
\lambda(\mathrm{Boom}(\Gamma))=1
, where
\mathrm{Boom}(\Gamma)
denotes the space of all Boomerang subgroups of
\Gamma
.

In contrast to the almost sure end-rigidity established for IRSs and SRSs, we demonstrate that end-rigidity does not hold for general Boomerang subgroups.

Theorem 1.3

(Failure of end-rigidity for Boomerang subgroups)
.

For any integer
k\geq 3
or
k=\aleph_{0}
, there exists a Boomerang subgroup of the free group
\mathbb{F}_{3}
whose Schreier graph has exactly
k
ends.

Preliminaries

Non-singular actions, invariant measures, and stationary measures

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete countable group acting on a compact topological space
X
by measurable automorphisms, making
X
a
\Gamma
-space. This action naturally extends to the space of Borel probability measures
\operatorname{Prob}(X)
via the formula
(g.\nu)(E)=\nu(g^{-1}E)
.

A measure
\nu\in\operatorname{Prob}(X)
is
ergodic
if every
\Gamma
-invariant Borel set has measure
0
or
1
. The action of
\Gamma
on
(X,\nu)
is
non-singular
if
g.\nu
and
\nu
are mutually absolutely continuous (meaning they share the same null sets) for all
g\in\Gamma
.

A measure is
invariant
if
g.\nu=\nu
for all
g\in\Gamma
. However, invariant measures on compact spaces are only guaranteed to exist when
\Gamma
is an amenable group.

A meausre
\mu\in\operatorname{Prob}(\Gamma)
is called
generating
if the support of the measure generates
\Gamma
as a semigroup, meaning

\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{supp}(\mu^{*n})=\Gamma
.
Given a generating probability measure
\mu\in\operatorname{Prob}(\Gamma)
, a measure
\nu\in\operatorname{Prob}(X)
is
\mu
-stationary
if it satisfies the convolution equation
\nu=\mu\ast\nu=\sum_{g\in\Gamma}\mu(g)g.\nu
.

Stationary measures are highly useful because a
\mu
-stationary measure is guaranteed to exist on any compact
\Gamma
-space, even when invariant measures do not. Furthermore, if
\mu
is a generating measure,
any
\mu
-stationary measure is automatically non-singular. This establishes a natural hierarchy: every invariant measure is
\mu
-stationary, and every
\mu
-stationary measure is non-singular.

Markov chains

We will rely on standard elements of Markov chain theory on infinite state spaces.

Let
S
be a countable state space and
P:S\times S\to[0,1]
be a transition probability matrix, satisfying
\sum_{y\in S}P(x,y)=1
for all
x\in S
. We denote by
Z_{n}
the position of the chain at time
n
.
For every
x\in S
, let
\mathbb{P}_{x}
denote the Markovian probability measure on the path space
S^{\mathbb{N}}
conditioned on the initial state
Z_{0}=x
, with corresponding expectation
\mathbb{E}_{x}
.

We define the number of visits to a state
y
as the random variable
V(y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{n}=y\}}
. Its expectation,
\mathbb{E}_{x}[V(y)]
, is the expected number of visits to
y
given
Z_{0}=x
.
Similarly, we define the first hitting time of
y
as
\tau_{y}^{+}=\inf\{t\geq 1\mid Z_{t}=y\}
, making
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{y}^{+}]
the expected first return time to
y
starting from
x
.
A chain is called
irreducible
if for all
x,y\in S
, there exists an integer
t\geq 1
such that
P^{t}(x,y)>0
.

Recall the classical classification of an irreducible Markov chain with transition matrix
P
. The chain is called:

•

Transient
if for some (equivalently all)
x,y\in S
,
\mathbb{E}_{x}[V(y)]<\infty
.

•

Recurrent
if for some (equivalently all)
x,y\in S
,
\mathbb{E}_{x}[V(y)]=\infty
. Recurrent chains are further classified as:

–

Positive recurrent
if for some (equivalently all)
x\in S
,
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{x}^{+}]<\infty
.

–

Null recurrent
if for some (equivalently all)
x\in S
,
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{x}^{+}]=\infty
.

The following classical lemma is a key ingredient in our proof of the No-Core Principle.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
P
be an irreducible Markov chain on an infinite state space
S
. If
P
is
null recurrent
or
transient
, then for any
x,y\in S
,

P^{n}(x,y)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Proof.

If
P
is transient, the expected number of visits to
y
is finite. Thus,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{n}=y\}}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{x}[V(y)]<\infty.

Since the infinite series converges, the general term must tend to zero, yielding
P^{n}(x,y)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0
.
For the case where the chain is null recurrent, we refer to
[
13
, Theorem 21.19]
.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
2.1
, for any finite subset of states
F\subseteq S
and any initial state
x\in S
,

P^{n}(x,F):=\sum_{y\in F}P^{n}(x,y)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Proof.

Since
F
is a finite set, the limit of the finite sum is the sum of the limits. Applying Lemma
2.1
directly yields the result.
∎

We apply this general theory to the setting of group actions. Given a
\Gamma
-action on a space
X
, a probability measure
\mu\in\operatorname{Prob}(\Gamma)
induces a Markov chain on each orbit
\mathcal{O}_{x}=\Gamma x
. The transition probabilities are given by

P_{\mathcal{O}_{x}}(x,y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\Gamma\\
g.x=y\end{subarray}}\mu(g).

Note, the original random walk on
\Gamma
is a
right
random walk (the position at time
n
is
g_{1}g_{2}\cdots g_{n}
), while the induced Markov chain on
X
operates via
left
multiplication (the position at time
n
is
g_{n}g_{n-1}\cdots g_{1}.x
).

\text{Sub}_{\Gamma}
, IRS and SRS

We denote by
\text{Sub}_{\Gamma}\subseteq 2^{\Gamma}
the space of subgroups of
\Gamma
, equipped with the subspace topology (commonly referred to as the Chabauty topology). Note that
\text{Sub}_{\Gamma}
is a compact metrizable space, and
\Gamma
acts on it by conjugation:
g.H=gHg^{-1}
.

An
Invariant Random Subgroup
(IRS) is a Borel probability measure
\lambda\in\operatorname{Prob}(\text{Sub}_{\Gamma})
which is invariant under the conjugation action of
\Gamma
.
An IRS can be seen as a probabilistic generalization of a normal subgroup, in the sense that the Dirac measure
\delta_{N}
is an IRS if and only if
N\unlhd\Gamma
is normal.

Given a probability measure
\mu\in\operatorname{Prob}(\Gamma)
, a
\mu
-Stationary Random Subgroup
(SRS) is a Borel probability measure
\nu\in\operatorname{Prob}(\text{Sub}_{\Gamma})
which is stationary with respect to
\mu
and the conjugation action.
Any IRS is an example of an SRS, but there are many more SRSs than IRSs.

Cayley and Schreier graphs

Let
S
be a finite symmetric generating set of
\Gamma
. We denote by
\operatorname{Cay}(\Gamma,S)
the associated Cayley graph, whose vertex set is
\Gamma
and whose edges are given by

g\xrightarrow{s}gs\qquad(s\in S).

For a subgroup
H\leq\Gamma
, we denote by
\operatorname{Sch}(H\backslash\Gamma,S)
the associated Schreier graph, whose vertex set is
H\backslash\Gamma
and whose edges are given by

Hg\xrightarrow{s}Hgs\qquad(s\in S).

Both
\operatorname{Cay}(\Gamma,S)
and
\operatorname{Sch}(H\backslash\Gamma,S)
are connected, locally finite graphs.

Fixing
S
, we denote
\operatorname{Sch}(H):=\operatorname{Sch}(H\backslash\Gamma,S)
and by

\operatorname{Sch}(\Gamma):=\{(\operatorname{Sch}(H),Hx)~:~H\in\text{Sub}_{\Gamma},x\in\Gamma\}

we denote the space of all rooted Schreier graphs of
\Gamma
.
This is also a compact metric space when equipped with the local topology (often called the pointwise or Gromov-Hausdorff topology), where two rooted Schreier graphs are at a distance of at most
1/n
if their metric balls of radius
n
around the roots are rooted-isomorphic. This “geometric” topology coincides with the topology on
\text{Sub}_{\Gamma}
.

The space
\operatorname{Sch}(\Gamma)
is also a
\Gamma
-space, where the action of
g\in\Gamma
corresponds to changing the root:

g\cdot(\operatorname{Sch}(H\backslash\Gamma,S),Hx)=(\operatorname{Sch}(H\backslash\Gamma,S),Hxg^{-1})\quad\text{for all }g\in\Gamma.

Observe that the rooted Schreier graph of a conjugate subgroup is canonically isomorphic to the original Schreier graph with a shifted root.

\displaystyle(\operatorname{Sch}(H),Hx)\cong(\operatorname{Sch}(x^{-1}Hx),x^{-1}Hx)\quad

\displaystyle\forall H\in\text{Sub}_{\Gamma},x\in\Gamma

\displaystyle Hg\mapsto x^{-1}Hg\quad

\displaystyle\forall Hg\in H\backslash\Gamma

The discussion above summaries as follows:
The map
\text{Sub}_{\Gamma}\to\operatorname{Sch}(\Gamma)
given by

H\mapsto(\operatorname{Sch}(H\backslash\Gamma,S),H)

is a
\Gamma
-equivariant homeomorphism.

Moreover, for every
H\in\text{Sub}_{\Gamma}
the relation between conjugation of
H
and isomorphic Schrier graphs is given by:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
H\in\text{Sub}_{\Gamma}
and let
\operatorname{Aut}(\operatorname{Sch}(H))
denote the group of label-preserving graph automorphisms of
\operatorname{Sch}(H)
.
Then there is a natural bijection between the conjugacy class
H^{\Gamma}=\{x^{-1}Hx:x\in\Gamma\}
and the orbits of the vertices of
\operatorname{Sch}(H)
under the action of
\operatorname{Aut}(\operatorname{Sch}(H))
.

Indeed, the vertices of
\operatorname{Sch}(H)
are the right cosets
Hx
for
x\in\Gamma
. Two vertices
Hx
and
Hy
belong to the same
\operatorname{Aut}(\operatorname{Sch}(H))
-orbit if and only if the rooted Schreier graphs
(\operatorname{Sch}(H),Hx)
and
(\operatorname{Sch}(H),Hy)
are isomorphic.
Because changing the root of the Schreier graph corresponds to conjugating the subgroup, the subgroup corresponding to the Schrier graph with root at
Hx
is exactly
x^{-1}Hx
, this isomorphism holds if and only if
x^{-1}Hx=y^{-1}Hy
.
Thus, the orbits of
\operatorname{Aut}(\operatorname{Sch}(H))
uniquely correspond to the distinct conjugates of
H
.

Ends of graphs and random subgroups

The space of ends of a locally finite graph
G
, denoted by
\operatorname{Ends}(G)
, formalizes the “ways to go to infinity” in the graph. It is constructed as the inverse limit of the connected components of the complement of finite balls.
This construction endows
\operatorname{Ends}(G)
with a compact topological structure (see for example,
[
12
]
).

The classical Freudenthal-Hopf
[
7
,
11
]
theorem dictates that the number of ends of a finitely generated Cayley graph is always
0,1,2
, or
2^{\aleph_{0}}
. Abels
[
1
]
later generalized this result, proving that the same holds for any quasi-transitive graph.

One gets a measurable function
\operatorname{Ends}:\text{Sub}_{\Gamma}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{0,\infty\}
assigning
H
the cardinality of
\operatorname{Ends}(\operatorname{Sch}(H\backslash\Gamma,S))
. Because conjugating
H
merely re-roots the graph without altering its global end structure, this function is
\Gamma
-invariant. Consequently, for any ergodic measure on
\text{Sub}_{\Gamma}
, the number of ends is almost surely constant.

For normal subgroups (whose Schreier graphs are Cayley graphs) and subgroups with finitely many conjugates (which yield quasi-transitive Schreier graphs), the Freudenthal-Hopf and Abels theorems directly guarantee that the number of ends is
0,1,2
, or
2^{\aleph_{0}}
.

Lyons and Schramm
[
15
]
extended this result by showing that any unimodular random graph almost surely has
0,1,2
, or
2^{\aleph_{0}}
ends. Since the Schreier graph of an Invariant Random Subgroup (IRS) inherently corresponds to a unimodular random graph
[
5
]
, it follows that any IRS almost surely satisfies this same restriction on its ends. For an analogous generalization to unimodular random manifolds, see
[
4
,
2
]
.

The no core principle for stationary actions

3.1
Markov chains on orbits

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete countable group. Let
\mu\in\operatorname{Prob}(\Gamma)
be a generating measure
and let
X
be a measurable
\Gamma
-space. For every orbit
O_{x}:=\Gamma.x
, there is a naturally defined
Markov Chain
, induced by the random walk on the group itself. The transition probabilities are given by,

P_{O_{x}}(y,z)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\Gamma\\
g.y=z\end{subarray}}\mu(g)

for any
y,z\in O_{x}
.

Note that, as usual, we consider a right random walk on
\Gamma
, the resulting Markov Chain on an orbit becomes a left one.
One can see that

\displaystyle P_{O_{x}}^{n}(y,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\Gamma\\
g.y=z\end{subarray}}\mu^{*n}(g).

Since we assumed that
\mu
is generating, the Markov chain is irreducible.

Lemma 3.1
.

Fix some
x\in X
, and consider the Markov Chain
P_{O_{x}}
. Then, either

1.

|O_{x}|<\infty
.

2.

for every finite set
F\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{x}
and every
y\in\mathcal{O}_{x}
,

P_{O_{x}}^{n}(y,F)=\sum_{z\in F}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\Gamma\\
g.y=z\end{subarray}}\mu^{*n}(g)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0

Proof.

We show that
|O_{x}|<\infty
if and only if
P_{O_{x}}
is positive recurrent.

Note that this would finish the proof, as the other case, that the orbit is infinite, implies that
P_{O_{x}}
is either null recurrent or transient. In both cases, we have that (2) as we showed in Corollary
2.2
.

It is easy to see that a finite irreducible Markov chain is positive recurrent.

Assume conversely that
P_{\mathcal{O}_{x}}
is positive recurrent. Let

K=\operatorname{Stab}_{\Gamma}(x)
, and let
Z_{n}=g_{n}\cdots g_{1}
be the left
\mu
-random walk on
\Gamma
.
Then the orbit chain started at
x
is given by
Z_{n}x
. Therefore the
first return time of the orbit chain to
x
is exactly

\tau_{K}^{+}=\inf\{n>0:Z_{n}\in K\}.

Thus positive recurrence of
P_{\mathcal{O}_{x}}
implies that
K
has
finite expected return time for the left
\mu
-random walk.

Passing to inverses gives the usual right
\check{\mu}
-random walk, where

\check{\mu}(g)=\mu(g^{-1}).

By Kac’s formula
[
10
, Theorem 1.2]
,

[\Gamma:K]=\mathbb{E}_{\check{\mu}}[\tau_{K}^{+}]<\infty.

Hence

|\mathcal{O}_{x}|=[\Gamma:\operatorname{Stab}_{\Gamma}(x)]<\infty.

∎

3.2
Core and stationary actions

In
[
2
, Theorem 1.15]
, the authors introduce the notion of a
core
for a random manifold and formulate the “No-Core Principle” which characterizes the conditions under which a core may or may not exist.

Drawing inspiration from their work, we generalize the definition of a core to arbitrary
\Gamma
-spaces as follows.

Definition 3.2

(Core)
.

Let
(X,\nu)
be a probability
\Gamma
-space. A Borel subset
C\subseteq X
is called a Core of the action if for
\nu
a.e.
x\in X
,

|\Gamma.x\cap C|<\infty

In words, a Core is a way to select from each orbit a finite set, in a Borel fashion.

For example, if every orbit is finite, then every measurable set is a core.

More interestingly, consider a Borel selector. Namely, let
(X,m)
be a probability measure preserving action of
\mathbb{Z}
, and let
C
be a Borel set that intersects every
\mathbb{Z}
exactly at one point. It is easy to see that by (the classical) Kac’s formula, in that case,
m
-almost every orbit must be finite.

We think of this setup, when the orbits are finite, as trivial. To get a non-trivial example of an action with a core, one can consider a transitive
\Gamma
-action (for some
\Gamma
) on an infinite (necessarily countable) set
X
, equipped with a fully supported measure
\eta\in\operatorname{Prob}(X)
. In that case, every finite subset is a core, although the orbit is infinite.

Our main theorem states that for stationary actions, the only case in which there is a (non-trivial) core, it is due to a trivial reason: finite orbits.

Theorem 3.3

(No-core principle for stationary actions)
.

Let
\mu\in\operatorname{Prob}(\Gamma)
be a generating measure, and let
(\Gamma,\mu)\curvearrowright(X,\nu)
be a stationary action. Let
C\subseteq X
be a core with positive measure. Then for
\nu
-a.e.
x\in C
,
|\Gamma x|<\infty
.

In words, almost every point in a core has a finite orbit.

Remark 3.4
.

Recall that every p.m.p. action is
\mu
-stationary (for any generating
\mu\in\operatorname{Prob}(\Gamma)
), so this proof implies a no-core principle for p.m.p. actions. For p.m.p. action of countable groups, one can prove it using tools from Descriptive Set Theory (see, for example, Gaboriau’s
[
8
, Section 1.3]
). It is interesting to note that our proof uses random walks to prove this result regarding pmp actions. Of course, the setup of
\mu
-stationary is strictly larger than pmp actions, but unlike pmp or non-singular actions, it is not clear how to model it in terms of Descriptive Set Theory.

Proof.

Let
C\subseteq X

The Boomerang counter example

A Boomerang subgroup, as introduced in
[
9
]
is a subgroup
\Delta<\Gamma
such that for every
g\in\Gamma
the sequence
(g^{n}\Delta g^{-n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
has a subsequence which converges to
\Delta
. If
\lambda\in\operatorname{Prob}(\text{Sub}_{\Gamma})
is an IRS, then
\lambda
-a.e. subgroup is Boomerang.

That is, Boomerang is a topological property (in
\text{Sub}_{\Gamma}
), it is “the area in which IRSs are happening”. Hence, classifying the Boomerang subgroups, yields a classification of the possible IRSs, bypassing the very nature of an IRS as a probabilistic object.
It is therefore natural to ask whether IRS end-rigidity (the IRS version of Theorem
1.2
) can be proved by first proving a pointwise statement for all Boomerang subgroups. The following example shows that this strategy cannot work.

.

For any integer

Let

be sufficiently large and odd, and let
\Gamma
be an infinite 3-generated Burnside group of exponent

. Let
