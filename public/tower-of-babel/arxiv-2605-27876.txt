Q Q -difference analogue of the Stothers-Mason theorem
Jian-Tang Lu, Xing-Xing Lu, Zhi-Tao Wen

Abstract
In this paper, we give a new definition of the q q -weight of zeros, which reduces to the multiplicity of zeros as q → 1 q\to 1 . Furthermore, we obtain a q q -difference version of the Stothers-Mason theorem by means of the new definition of the q q -difference radical, which covers the classical Stothers-Mason theorem as q → 1 q\to 1 . As applications, we study the polynomial solutions of q q -difference Fermat type functional equations. Keyword : q q -weight of zeros, q q -difference radical, Stothers–Mason theorem, Fermat type functional equation 2020MSC : 39B32, 30D35

Introduction

Let
P
be a polynomial. The radical
{\rm rad}(P)
is the product of distinct linear factors of
P
. Let
a
,
b
and
c
be relatively prime polynomials such that not all of them are identically zero. The Stothers–Mason theorem
[
12
]
,
[
14
]
, see also
[
13
]
, states that if they satisfy
a+b=c
, then

\max\{\deg(a),\deg(b),\deg(c)\}\leq\deg({\rm rad}(abc))-1.

A difference analogue of the Stothers-Mason theorem or difference
abc
theorem for polynomials was given by Ishizaki et al. in
[
7
, Theorem 3.1]
. Let
a,b
and
c
be relatively prime polynomials in
\mathbb{C}[z]
, which are not all constants, satisfying
a+b=c
. Then,

\max\{\deg(a),\deg(b),\deg(c)\}\leq\tilde{n}_{\kappa}(a)+\tilde{n}_{\kappa}(b)+\tilde{n}_{\kappa}(c)-1,

where
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, and

\tilde{n}_{\kappa}(p)=\sum_{w\in\mathbb{C}}(\text{ord}_{w}(p)-\min\{\text{ord}_{w}(p),\text{ord}_{w+\kappa}(p)\})

for a polynomial
p
by
\text{ord}_{w}(p)
denoting the order of zeros of
p
at
z=w
.

Another difference analogue of the Stothers-Mason theorem was given by Ishizaki and Wen in
[
8
, Theorem 3.5]
. Let
a,b
and
c
be relatively shifting prime polynomials, see
[
8
, pages 736-737]
, such that
a,b
and
c
are not all constants satisfying
a+b=c
. Then

\max\{\deg(a),\deg(b),\deg(c)\}\leq\deg(\text{rad}_{\Delta}(abc))-1,

where
\text{rad}_{\Delta}(abc)
is difference radical of
abc
defined in
[
8
]
.

One of the purposes of this paper is to introduce a
q
-difference analogue of the radical of polynomials and a
q
-difference analogue of the Stothers-Mason theorem, which are different from the results in
[
3
]
. These
q
-difference counterparts reduce to the classical radical of polynomials and Stothers-Mason theorem, respectively, as
q\to 1
. As applications, we study polynomial solutions of
q
-difference Fermat type functional equations.

In Section 2, we define the
q
-weight of a zero of an analytic function in a domain
G
, and investigate its properties
via
q
-difference calculus. We then introduce the
q
-difference radical by means of the
q
-weight of zeros and focus on the
q
-difference analogue of the Stothers-Mason theorem by using the

q
-difference radical in Section 3. Section 4 is concerned with an extension of the aforementioned
q
-difference Stothers-Mason theorem. Finally, in Section 5 we study the polynomial
solutions of
q
-difference version of the Fermat type functional equations.

Q
-weight of zeros

Following
[
11
]
, we denote
q
-number which is the
q
-analogue of the natural
number
n
by

[n]_{q}=\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}=q^{n-1}+\cdots+q+1.

We set the
q
-analogue of factorial
n!
by

[n]_{q}!=\begin{cases}1\quad&n=0;\\
[n]_{q}\times[n-1]_{q}\times\cdots\times[1]_{q}\quad&n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}.\end{cases}

Let us set the
q
-analogue of power function
(z-a)^{n}
by

[z-a]^{n}_{q}=\begin{cases}1\quad&n=0;\\
(z-a)(z-aq)\cdots(z-aq^{n-1})\quad&n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}.\end{cases}

We note that if
a=0
, then
[z]^{n}_{q}=z^{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\cup\{0\}
. For a given function
f
, we define
q
-derivative of
f

[
9
]
by

D_{q}f(z)=\frac{f(qz)-f(z)}{qz-z}

for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
q\neq 1
. In addition,
q
-derivative of
f
at zero is defined by

D_{q}f(0)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(z^{\prime}q^{n})-f(0)}{z^{\prime}q^{n}}

for
z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. Note that if
f(z)
is analytic at
z=0
, then
D_{q}f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)
.
Moreover, if
f
is an analytic function, then
\lim_{q\to 1}D_{q}f=f^{\prime}
, and
D_{q}
is known as the
Jackson operator
, see e.g.,
[
10
, Page 41]
. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, set
D_{q}^{n}f=D_{q}^{n-1}(D_{q}f)
and
D_{q}^{0}f=f
.
Obviously, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
we have

D_{q}([z-a]_{q}^{n})=[n]_{q}[z-a]^{n-1}_{q},

which is
q
-analogue of differential calculus
((z-a)^{n})^{\prime}=n(z-a)^{n-1}
.
It is a fact that
D_{q}
reduces the degree of a polynomial by one.

Let
f
be analytic in a domain
G\subset\mathbb{C}
and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
q\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.
Suppose that
z_{0},qz_{0},\ldots,q^{n}z_{0}\in G
.
The point
z_{0}
is called a

with
q
-weight

in
G
provided

and all
q
-derivative
D_{q}^{k}f(z_{0})
vanish for all
0\leq k<n
, but
D_{q}^{n}f(z_{0})\neq 0
. In general, the point
z_{0}
is called an
a
-point of
f(z)
with
q
-weight
n
in
G
provided
f(z_{0})-a
and all
q
-derivative
D_{q}^{k}f(z_{0})
vanish for all
0\leq k<n
, but
D_{q}^{n}f(z_{0})\neq 0
.
In particular, if
z_{0}
is an
a
-point of
f
with
q
-weight 1, then we also call
z_{0}
is an
a
-point of
f
with simple
q
-weight. Obviously,
z_{0}=0
being a zero of
f
with
q
-weight

is equivalent to
z_{0}=0
being a zero of

with multiplicity

.

We emphasize here that

-weight of zeros is a generalization of multiplicity of zeros of an analytic function. As
q\to 1
, we see that

-weight of zeros reduces to the multiplicity of zeros of an analytic function.

Example 2.1

Suppose that
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and
a,q\in\mathbb{C}
such that
q\neq 0
, and
f(z)=[z-a]_{q}^{n}
. Then we have
D_{q}^{k}f(a)=0
for any

satisfying
0\leq k<n
, but
D_{q}^{n}f(a)\neq 0
. Thus, it is obvious that

is a zero of
f
with
q
-weight

. In addition, we see that

becomes a zero of
f
with multiplicity

as
q\to 1
.

The following example illustrates that a transcendental function may have a zero with infinite
q
-weight.

Example 2.2

Suppose that
0<|q|<1
, and a function

f(z)=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}(1+q^{n}z),

which converges uniformly on any compact subset of
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<R\}
. The entire function

is a solution of linear
q
-difference equations

f(qz)-(1+qz)f(z)=0.

In addition,

is of logarithmic order
\rho_{\log}(f)=2
, see
[
15
, Theorem 1.1]
.
The logarithmic order is defined by

\rho_{\log}(f):=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\log M(r,f)}{\log\log r}.

Obviously, the distinct zero sequence of

is
z_{n}=-(1/q)^{n}
for

. Thus,
z_{0}=-1
is a zero of

with infinite
q
-weight.

It is well known that
z=z_{0}
is a zero of an analytic function
f
with multiplicity
n
if
and only if
f
is of the form
f(z)=(z-z_{0})^{n}g(z)
, where
g(z)
is analytic at
z=z_{0}
and

g(z_{0})\neq 0
. The
q
-analogue of this result is given as follows.

Theorem 2.1

Let
f
be analytic in the domain
G\subset\mathbb{C}
and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
q\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. Suppose that
z_{0},qz_{0},\ldots,q^{n}z_{0}\in G
.
The point
z_{0}
is a zero of
f(z)
with
q
-weight
n
in
G
if and only if there exists an analytic function
g(z)
in
G
such that

f(z)=[z-z_{0}]_{q}^{n}g(z)

(2.1)

and
g(q^{n}z_{0})\neq 0
.

Before the proof of Theorem
2.1
, let us recall a lemma, see e.g.,
[
2
, Pages 12-13]
, which reveals the relation between

and

Lemma 2.2

For any

,

, we have

f(q^{k}z)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}q^{j(j-1)/2}(qz-z)^{j}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}k\\
j\end{array}\right]_{q}D_{q}^{j}f(z),

where

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}k\\
j\end{array}\right]_{q}=\frac{[k]_{q}!}{[j]_{q}![k-j]_{q}!}

are called
q
-binomial coefficients. In addition, for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

D_{q}^{k}f(z)=\frac{1}{q^{k(k-1)/2}(qz-z)^{k}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}q^{(k-j)(k-j-1)/2}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}k\\
j\end{array}\right]_{q}f(q^{j}z).

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.
It is easy to get our assertion when
q=1
. In the following, we assume that
q\neq 1
.
Let
z_{0}\neq 0
be a zero of
f
with
q
-weight
n
in a domain

, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
D_{q}^{k}f(z_{0})
vanish for

, by using Lemma

, we have

f(q^{k}z_{0})=\sum_{j=0}^{k}q^{j(j-1)/2}(qz_{0}-z_{0})^{j}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}k\\
j\end{array}\right]_{q}D_{q}^{j}f(z_{0})=0.

In addition, we found that
f(q^{n}z_{0})=q^{n(n-1)/2}D_{q}^{n}f(z_{0})(qz_{0}-z_{0})^{n}\neq 0
.
Hence,

is of the form (
2.1
).
If

is of the form (
2.1
), we see that
f(q^{k}z_{0})=0
for all
0\leq k<n
, but

f(q^{n}z_{0})\neq 0
. From Lemma

, we have

D_{q}^{k}f(z_{0})=\frac{1}{q^{k(k-1)/2}(qz_{0}-z_{0})^{k}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}q^{(k-j)(k-j-1)/2}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}k\\
j\end{array}\right]_{q}f(q^{j}z_{0})=0

for any
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and

, but
D_{q}^{n}f(z_{0})\neq 0
. Therefore,

z_{0}
is a zero of
f(z)
with
q
-weight
n
in
G
.

If
z_{0}=0
, then
z_{0}
is a zero of
f
with

-weight

is a zero of

\hfill\square

Obviously, the analytic function

, and let

Now let us state our main result in this paper as follows, which is
q
-difference analogue of the Stothers-Mason theorem.

Theorem 3.2

Let
q\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
such that
|q|\neq 1
, and
a
,
b
and
c
be relatively
q
-prime polynomials such that

a+b=c

and such that
a
,
b
and
c
are not all constants. Then

\max\{\deg(a),\deg(b),\deg(c)\}\leq\deg({{\rm rad}_{q}(abc)})-1.

(3.6)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let us assume that
\deg(c)=\max\{\deg(a),\deg(b),\deg(c)\}
.
Since
a+b=c
, we have
D_{q}a+D_{q}b=D_{q}c
. Multiplying
the first equation by
D_{q}a
, the second by
a
, and then subtracting them, we obtain

bD_{q}a-aD_{q}b=cD_{q}a-aD_{q}c.

(3.7)

Suppose that
bD_{q}a-aD_{q}b\not\equiv 0
. It follows from (
3.7
) that

{\rm gcd}(a,D_{q}a)
,
{\rm gcd}(b,D_{q}b)
and
{\rm gcd}(c,D_{q}c)
all divide

bD_{q}a-aD_{q}b
. Since
a,b,c
are relatively
q
-prime polynomials, from Lemma
3.1
,
{\rm gcd}(a,D_{q}a)
,
{\rm gcd}(b,D_{q}b)
and
{\rm gcd}(c,D_{q}c)
are relatively
q
-prime polynomials, which are the factors of
bD_{q}a-aD_{q}b
. Therefore,

\deg({\rm gcd}(a,D_{q}a))+\deg({\rm gcd}(b,D_{q}b))+\deg({\rm gcd}(c,D_{q}c))\leq\deg(a)+\deg(b)-1.

We add
\deg(c)
to both sides, and use Corollary
2.3
and (
3.3
),

\begin{split}\deg(c)\leq&\big\{\deg(a)-\deg({\rm gcd}(a,D_{q}a))\big\}+\big\{\deg(b)-\deg({\rm gcd}(b,D_{q}b))\big\}\\
&+\big\{\deg(c)-\deg({\rm gcd}(c,D_{q}c))\big\}-1\\
=&\deg({\rm rad}_{q}(a))+\deg({\rm rad}_{q}(b))+\deg({\rm rad}_{q}(c))-1\\
=&\deg({\rm rad}_{q}(abc))-1.\end{split}

Therefore, we prove our assertion when
bD_{q}a-aD_{q}b\not\equiv 0
. In the following, we proceed to prove our theorem when
bD_{q}a-aD_{q}b\equiv 0
. Hence, we have

\frac{D_{q}a}{a}=\frac{D_{q}b}{b}=\frac{D_{q}c}{c}=Q(z),

where
Q(z)
is a rational function. Polynomials
a,b,c
are solutions of linear
q
-difference equations

f(qz)=(1+(q-1)zQ(z))f(z).

There exist
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
such that
b=\pi_{1}a
and
c=\pi_{2}a
, where
\pi_{j}(qz)=\pi_{j}(z)
for
j=1,2
, see e.g.,
[
1
]
. Since
b
and
c
are polynomials, the only possibility is
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are constants. It yields that all zeros of
a,b,c
are of simple
q
-weight. Hence,

\deg(a)+\deg(b)+\deg(c)=\deg({\rm rad}_{q}(abc)).

It is easy to see that (
3.6
) holds. We proved our assertion.

\Box

Theorem
3.2
can be seen as a generalization of Stothers-Mason theorem. As
q\to 1
,
Theorem
3.2
is the classical Stothers-Mason theorem.
The following example shows that the assertion in Theorem
3.2
is sharp.

Example 3.2

Let
a=[z-1]_{q}^{2}
,
b=-[z+1]_{q}^{2}
and
c=-2(q+1)z
. We see that
a
,
b

and
c
are relatively
q
-prime polynomials satisfying
a+b=c
. Moreover,
it is shown that
\max\{\deg(a),\deg(b),\deg(c)\}=2
,
{{\rm rad}_{q}(abc)}=z(z^{2}-1)
,
and
\deg({{\rm rad}_{q}(abc)})=3
.

4
Extension of the Stothers-Mason theorem with
q
-difference radical

We write a polynomial
P
in the form (
3.1
), i.e.,
P(z)=A\prod_{j=1}^{N}[z-z_{j}]_{q}^{n_{j}}
.
The
q
-radical of truncation level
\mu\in\mathbb{N}
for a polynomial
P(z)
is denoted by

{\rm rad}_{q}^{\mu}(P)={\rm gcd}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{N}[z-z_{j}]_{q}^{n_{j}},\ \prod_{j=1}^{N}[z-z_{j}]_{q}^{\mu}\bigg).

(4.1)

Obviously, it is a generalization of
q
-difference radical. When
\mu=1
in (
4.1
), it reduces to the
q
-difference radical of
P
given in (
3.2
).
We note that
{\rm rad}_{q}^{\mu}(P)={\rm rad}_{q}(P)
holds for any
\mu\geq 2
if and only if all the zeros of
P
are of simple
q
-weight.
It follows from (
4.1
),

\deg({\rm rad}_{q}^{\mu}(P))=\deg\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}[z-z_{j}]_{q}^{\min(n_{j},\mu)}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\min(n_{j},\mu).

(4.2)

In addition, we have an inequality

\deg\left({\rm rad}_{q}^{\mu}(P)\right)\leq\mu\cdot\deg\left({\rm rad}_{q}(P)\right).

(4.3)

The following theorem extends Theorem
3.2
for
m+1
polynomials, where
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\geq 2
. We state it as follows.

Theorem 4.1

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\geq 2
and let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m+1}
be pairwise relatively
q
-prime polynomials with

\min_{1\leq i\leq m+1}\{\deg f_{i}\}\geq m-1

satisfying the following functional equation

f_{1}+\cdots+f_{m}=f_{m+1},

and such that
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{C}
. Then

\begin{split}\max_{1\leq i\leq m+1}\{\deg f_{i}\}&\leq\deg({\rm rad}_{q}^{m-1}(f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{m+1}))-\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)\\
&\leq(m-1)\deg({\rm rad}_{q}(f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{m+1}))-\frac{1}{2}m(m-1).\end{split}

(4.4)

In order to prove Theorem
4.1
, we need some lemmas below. Let
[x]^{+}=\max\{x,0\}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 4.2

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
P
be a polynomial with
\deg P\geq n
represented by (
3.1
). Then

\deg(\gcd(P,D_{q}P,...,D_{q}^{n}P))=\sum_{j=1}^{N}[n_{j}-n]^{+}

(4.5)

and

\deg(P)-\deg(\gcd(P,D_{q}P,...,D_{q}^{n}P))=\deg({\rm rad}_{q}^{n}(P)).

Proof.

Let
z_{j}
be a zero of
P
with
q
-weight
n_{j}
such that
P(q^{-1}z_{j})\neq 0
. From Theorem
2.1
, we write
P(z)=[z-z_{j}]_{q}^{n_{j}}H_{j}(z)
, where
H_{j}(z)
is a polynomial with
H_{j}(q^{n_{j}}z_{j})\neq 0
for some
j\in\mathbb{N}
. By Corollary
2.3
,
z_{j}
is a zero of
D_{q}P
with
q
-weight
n_{j}-1
, that is
D_{q}P(z)=[z-z_{j}]_{q}^{n_{j}-1}H_{j,1}(z)
, where
H_{j,1}(z)
is a polynomial with
H_{j,1}(q^{n_{j}-1}z_{j})\neq 0
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
1\leq k\leq n
. If
n_{j}\geq n+1
,
D_{q}^{k}P(z)=[z-z_{j}]_{q}^{n_{j}-k}H_{j,k}(z)
, where
H_{j,k}(z)
is a polynomial satisfying
H_{j,k}(q^{n_{j}-k}z_{j})\neq 0
, which implies
{\gcd(P,D_{q}P,...,D_{q}^{n}P)}
is divided by
[z-z_{j}]_{q}^{n_{j}-n}
.
If
n_{j}\leq n
, the factors in
[z-z_{j}]_{q}^{n_{j}}
never contribute to
{\gcd(P,D_{q}P,...,D_{q}^{n}P)}
. From the argument above, we have

\gcd(P,D_{q}P,...,D_{q}^{n}P)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}[z-z_{j}]_{q}^{[n_{j}-n]^{+}},

which implies (
4.5
). By (
4.5
) and (
4.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\deg(\gcd(P,D_{q}P,...,D_{q}^{n}P))=\sum_{j=1}^{N}[n_{j}-n]^{+}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\max\{n_{j}-n,0\}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{j=1}^{N}n_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\min\{n_{j},n\}=\deg(P)-\deg({\rm rad}_{q}^{n}(P)).

This completes the proof of Lemma
4.2
.

\Box

For any nonconstant polynomials
P
and
Q
, we have the following estimate

\deg({\rm rad}_{q}^{n}(PQ))\leq\deg({\rm rad}_{q}^{n}(P))+\deg({\rm rad}_{q}^{n}(Q)),

(4.6)

where
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, if
P
and
Q
are relatively
q
-prime, then

\deg({\rm rad}_{q}^{n}(PQ))=\deg({\rm rad}_{q}^{n}(P))+\deg({\rm rad}_{q}^{n}(Q)).

Let now
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
f_{j}(z),j=1,2,...,m
be polynomials. Let us recall
q
-Casorati determinant of entire functions
f_{1},f_{2},...,f_{m}
by, see e.g.,
[
6
, Pages 4282-4283]
or
[
1
]
,

\begin{split}W_{q}(z)=W_{q}(f_{1},f_{2},...,f_{m})&=\begin{vmatrix}f_{1}(z)&f_{2}(z)&\cdots&f_{m}(z)\\
D_{q}f_{1}(z)&D_{q}f_{2}(z)&\cdots&D_{q}f_{m}(z)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
D_{q}^{m-1}f_{1}(z)&D_{q}^{m-1}f_{2}(z)&\cdots&D_{q}^{m-1}f_{m}(z)\end{vmatrix}\\[7.0pt]
&=\frac{\begin{vmatrix}f_{1}(z)&f_{2}(z)&\cdots&f_{m}(z)\\
f_{1}(qz)&f_{2}(qz)&\cdots&f_{m}(qz)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
f_{1}(q^{m-1}z)&f_{2}(q^{m-1}z)&\cdots&f_{m}(q^{m-1}z)\end{vmatrix}}{(qz-z)^{m-1}}.\end{split}

(4.7)

The
q
-Casorati determinant is a useful tool for analyzing the solutions of
q
-difference equations, see
[
15
]
. By the properties of the determinant, we have

Lemma 4.3

If
q\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}
, then the
q
-Casorati determinant vanishes identically on
\mathbb{C}
if and only if the functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}
are linearly dependent over the field of functions

\pi(z)
satisfying
\pi(qz)=\pi(z)
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.
Let
W_{q}(z)
be the
q
-Casorati determinant of polynomials
f_{1},f_{2},...,f_{m}
defined by (
4.7
). It yields that
W_{q}(z)\not\equiv 0
by
Lemma
4.3
and the fact
f_{1},f_{2},...,f_{m}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{C}
.
Now let us write

f_{j}(z)=A_{j}\prod_{k=1}^{N_{j}}[z-z_{j,k}]_{q}^{n_{j,k}},

where
A_{j}
are constants,
N_{j},n_{j,k}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and
f_{j}(z_{j,k}q^{-1})\neq 0
for

j=1,\ldots,m+1
. It implies that
f_{j}(z)
is divided by
\gcd(f_{j},D_{q}f_{j},...,D_{q}^{m-1}f_{j})=\prod_{k=1}^{N_{j}}[z-z_{j,k}]_{q}^{[n_{j,k}-(m-1)]^{+}}
for any
j=1,\ldots,m+1
. Obviously, every
f_{j}(z)~(j=1,2,...,m)
can be replaced by
f_{m+1}=f_{1}+f_{2}+\cdots+f_{m}
in
W_{q}(z)
by the properties of
determinant.
Moreover,
W_{q}(z)
is divided by
\gcd(f_{j},D_{q}f_{j},...,D_{q}^{m-1}f_{j})
for
j\in\{1,2,...,m+1\}
, and
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m+1}
are pairwise relatively
q
-prime polynomials, it gives that
W_{q}(z)
is divided by

Q(z):=\prod_{j=1}^{m+1}\prod_{k=1}^{N_{j}}[z-z_{j,k}]_{q}^{[n_{j,k}-m+1]^{+}},

which implies that there exists a polynomial
P(z)
such that
W_{q}(z)=P(z)Q(z)
.

By the assumption
\min_{1\leq j\leq m+1}\{\deg f_{j}\}\geq m-1
, we have
\deg(D_{q}^{n}f_{j})=\deg f_{j}-n\geq 0
with
0\leq n\leq m-1
. This implies that
\deg W_{q}(z)
is never greater than any sum of distinct
m
of the
\deg f_{j}(z),1\leq j\leq m+1
, minus
\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}n=m(m-1)/2
as the sum of
\deg(D_{q}^{n}f_{j_{v}})
for the mutually distinct
m
integers
j_{v}\in\{1,2,...,m+1\}
. Since
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
f_{1},f_{2},...,f_{m+1}
are pairwise relatively
q
-prime, by Lemma
4.2
and (
4.6
), we have

\displaystyle\min_{1\leq h\leq m+1}\bigg\{\sum_{1\leq j\leq m+1,j\neq h}\deg f_{j}\bigg\}-\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)\geq\deg\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m+1}\gcd(f_{j},D_{q}f_{j},...,D_{q}^{m-1}f_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m+1}(\deg(f_{j})-\deg({\rm rad}_{q}^{m-1}(f_{j})))=\sum_{j=1}^{m+1}\deg(f_{j})-\deg({\rm rad}_{q}^{m-1}(f_{1}f_{2}\cdots f_{m+1})),

which implies the first inequality of (
4.4
). Combining (
4.3
) and the first inequality of (
4.4
), we have the second inequality of (
4.4
) follows. We have proved Theorem
4.1
.
\hfill\square

In the following, let us state an example to show the inequality in (
4.4
) is sharp.

Example 4.1

Let
0<|q|<1
. Set
f_{1}=[z-1]_{q}^{5}
,
f_{2}=-[z+1]_{q}^{5}
,
f_{3}=2[5]_{q}z^{4}
. It is obvious that
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
and
f_{3}
are linearly independent. We define

f_{4}=f_{1}+f_{2}+f_{3}=-2q^{3}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}5\\
2\end{array}\right]_{q}z^{2}-2q^{10}.

Let us choose suitable
q
such that
\pm q^{-1},0,\pm q,\pm q^{2},\pm q^{3},\pm q^{4},\pm q^{5}
are not zeros of
f_{4}
. It yields that
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
,
f_{3}
and
f_{4}
are pairwise relatively
q
-prime.
According to (
4.1
), we have
\deg({\rm rad}_{q}^{2}(f_{1}))=2
,
\deg({\rm rad}_{q}^{2}(f_{2}))=2
,

,
\deg({\rm rad}_{q}^{2}(f_{4}))=2

) is sharp.

5
Polynomial solutions of
q
-difference Fermat functional equations

In this section, we apply Theorem
3.2
and Theorem
4.1
to
q
-difference Fermat type functional equations for investigating nonexistence of polynomial solutions.
We adopt the expression
[P]_{q}^{n}:=P(z)P(qz)\cdots P(q^{n-1}z)
instead of
P^{n}
for a polynomial
P
in the Fermat type functional equations. For the Fermat type functional equations, see e.g.,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
.

Theorem 5.1

Let
a
,
b
and
c
be polynomials, not all constants,
q\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
satisfying
|q|\neq 1
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
[a]_{q}^{n}
,
[b]_{q}^{n}
and
[c]_{q}^{n}
are relatively
q
-prime and satisfy

[a]_{q}^{n}+[b]_{q}^{n}=[c]_{q}^{n}.

(5.1)

Then
n\leq 2
. In addition, if one of
a
,
b
and
c
is a constant, then
n=1
.

Proof.

Let us first assume that
a
,
b
and
c
are all nonconstant polynomials.
By Theorem
3.2
, we have

\begin{split}n\deg(a)=\deg([a]_{q}^{n})&\leq\max\{\deg([a]_{q}^{n}),\deg([b]_{q}^{n}),\deg([c]_{q}^{n})\}\\
&\leq\deg({\rm rad}_{q}([a]_{q}^{n}~[b]_{q}^{n}~[c]_{q}^{n}))-1\\
&=\deg({\rm rad}_{q}([a]_{q}^{n}))+\deg({\rm rad}_{q}([b]_{q}^{n}))+\deg({\rm rad}_{q}([c]_{q}^{n}))-1\\
&\leq\deg(a)+\deg(b)+\deg(c)-1.\end{split}

(5.2)

Similarly, for
b
and
c
, we have

n\deg(b)\leq\deg(a)+\deg(b)+\deg(c)-1

(5.3)

and

n\deg(c)\leq\deg(a)+\deg(b)+\deg(c)-1.

(5.4)

Combining inequalities (
5.2
), (
5.3
) and (
5.4
), we obtain

n(\deg(a)+\deg(b)+\deg(c))\leq 3(\deg(a)+\deg(b)+\deg(c))-3,

which implies that
n\leq 2
.

We secondly assume one of
a
,
b
and
c
is a constant. Without loss of generality, we assume that
c
is a constant. Then (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) yield

n(\deg(a)+\deg(b))\leq 2(\deg(a)+\deg(b))-2,

which shows that
n\leq 1
. We proved our assertion.

\Box

Theorem 5.2

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\geq 2
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
q\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
such that
|q|\neq 1
. Suppose that there exist
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m+1}
nonconstant polynomials satisfying

[f_{1}]_{q}^{n}+[f_{2}]_{q}^{n}+\cdots+[f_{m}]_{q}^{n}=[f_{m+1}]_{q}^{n}.

Further suppose that
[f_{1}]_{q}^{n}
,
\ldots
,
[f_{m+1}]_{q}^{n}
are pairwise relatively
q
-prime and
[f_{1}]_{q}^{n},\ldots,[f_{m+1}]_{q}^{n}
are linearly independent.
Then

n\leq m^{2}-1-\frac{m(m-1)}{2\max_{1\leq i\leq m+1}\{\deg(f_{i})\}}.

Proof.

By using Theorem
4.1
, we have

\begin{split}n\max_{1\leq j\leq m+1}\{\deg(f_{j})\}&\leq(m-1)\deg\left(\text{rad}_{q}\left([f_{1}]_{q}^{n}[f_{2}]_{q}^{n}\cdots[f_{m+1}]_{q}^{n}\right)\right)-\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)\\
&\leq(m+1)(m-1)\max_{1\leq j\leq m+1}\{\deg(f_{j})\}-\frac{1}{2}m(m-1).\end{split}

Since
\max_{1\leq j\leq m+1}\{\deg(f_{j})\}\geq 1
, we proved our assertion.

\Box
