Conjugation-induced π-electron modulation in pyridazine-integrated covalent organic frameworks for SO2 capture and upcycling
Authors: Yongfang Qu, Fengqing Liu, Linyu Zhuo, Shouchao Zhong, Liping Zheng, Qiliang Zhu, Yong Zheng, Anmin Zheng, Wei Lin, Fujian Liu, Lilong Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSulfur dioxide (SO2) capture is of critical importance for environmental protection and sustainable sulfur resource cycling. This process demands sorbents that exhibit high SO2 capacity, precise molecular recognition, and robust tolerance under extreme conditions. Here, we report a sp2 carbon-conjugated covalent organic framework engineered with densely aligned and ordered pyridazine groups (DpTb-COF). This design enables efficient SO2 capture, achieving an ultrahigh capacity of 19.4 mmol/g (25 °C, 1.0 bar) and exhibiting precise selectivity gradients (SO2/H2S = 11.3, SO2/COS = 13.1, SO2/CO2 = 55.3, SO2/N2 = 730.1, 0.1/0.9=v/v). Its exceptional stability is evidenced by negligible performance loss over 50 adsorption–desorption cycles. This enhanced performance is achieved by strategically anchoring pyridazine motifs into the highly conjugated DpTb-COF framework. Unlike their non-conjugated counterparts, DpTb-COF’s planar conjugated structure promotes framework electron delocalization, which synergistically enhances SO2 affinity via enriched π-electron density while weakening interaction with CO2 due to reduced basicity. Furthermore, DpTb-COF exhibits remarkable resilience against humidity and corrosive conditions, maintaining its structural integrity and performance. The captured SO2 can be efficiently transformed into cyclic sulfites over the Cu1@DpTb-COF catalyst. This work thereby establishes a rational design strategy for high-performance multifunctional materials that enable both capture and upcycling of acid gases.Subject terms: Pollution remediation, Conjugated polymers, Organic molecules in materials science, Supramolecular polymers

Introduction

Corrosive, toxic acidic waste gases (e.g., SO2) are emitted during shale gas development, natural gas purification, fossil fuel combustion, and metal smelting1,2. Despite their hazards, these emissions can act as valuable feedstocks for high-value-added chemicals3,4, making efficient capture, storage, and conversion technologies imperative for concurrent emission control and resource recovery. Conventional limestone-based wet flue gas desulfurization, effective for high-concentration SO2, performs poorly for dilute streams, generating substantial wastewater, solid waste, and high energy and treatment costs5–7. In contrast, solid sorbent-based adsorption with lower capital investment, simpler operation, and reduced energy consumption is better suited for low-concentration SO2 capture8–10. A key challenge is designing adsorbents with high efficiency and long-term stability, particularly given industrial flue gases’ complex composition (typically CO2, NOx, NH3, and humidity) that compete for adsorption sites and may degrade sorbent structure11. Zeolites, for instance, have limited SO2 capacity and poor stability under acidic, humid conditions12,13, while activated carbons, despite high capacity, show weak host–guest interactions and low SO2/CO2/N2 selectivity14,15.

Porous sorbents achieve efficient SO2 capture via high specific surface area and tunable active sites, which synergistically enhance interfacial adsorption16–18. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), for example, exhibit ultrahigh SO2 capacities due to tunable porosity and dense metal sites but suffer irreversible metal center sulfidation under realistic flue gas conditions, leading to structural degradation and poor industrial durability19–21. In contrast, N-functionalized porous materials offer superior thermal and chemical stability, making them more adaptable to complex industrial SO2 adsorption scenarios22–24. Among these, N-doped porous organic polymers excel with tunable surface chemistry and pore architecture, supporting high SO2 uptake and excellent regeneration25–30. However, their broad N-basic site distribution induces competitive adsorption from other flue gas components, reducing SO2 selectivity11. Addressing this requires rational sorbent design integrating precise N-site control, stable porous framework, and tailored electronic modulation—key to high selectivity and efficient capture in complex industrial streams.

Covalent organic frameworks (COFs), an emerging class of crystalline porous materials, feature robust covalent linkages, tunable nanopores, and high specific surface areas, making them ideal for anchoring N-containing functional groups31–33. N-rich COFs have demonstrated promising potential in gas adsorption/separation, energy storage/conversion, and heterogeneous catalysis. Notably, sp2 carbon-conjugated COFs exhibit superior thermal and chemical stability due to their fully conjugated frameworks and delocalized π-electron systems34; their structural and functional stability is retained even under harsh conditions such as high temperature, humidity, and acidic/alkaline atmospheres, underscoring their potential for industrial desulfurization35. However, current COFs still need improvements in SO2 adsorption capacity, selectivity, and separation precision. Furthermore, the regulatory mechanism by which COF conjugated skeletons modulate the electron delocalization of N sites is critical for efficient SO2 recognition and capture in complex industrial streams, yet it remains unclear and understudied, necessitating systematic in-depth investigation.

Herein, we report a pyridazine-functionalized, sp2 carbon-conjugated COF (DpTb-COF) synthesized via one-step melt polymerization. It possesses a high specific surface area and well-ordered porosity. Structural and electronic analyses confirm its highly conjugated framework enhances bulk electron delocalization, redistributes electron density around pyridazine units, and moderately tunes their basicity. This modulation enriches the framework with π-electron-rich sites to strengthen affinity for strongly acidic SO2, while suppressing pyridazine basicity to reduce competitiveness toward weakly acidic CO2. The resulting electronic configuration maintains high SO2 affinity and markedly improves gas recognition selectivity, enabling precise trace SO2 capture in complex industrial streams. Adsorption measurements show DpTb-COF achieves a high SO2 uptake of 19.4 mmol/g at 25 °C and 1.0 bar. In a 0.1/0.9 (v/v) mixed gas system, it exhibits excellent selectivity: SO2/H2S = 11.3, SO2/COS = 13.1, SO2/CO2 = 55.3, and SO2/N2 = 730.1. This superior performance stems from the conjugated framework’s synergistic regulation of skeleton π-electron density and pyridazine basicity. Additionally, its sp2 carbon-conjugated structure ensures exceptional stability, retaining structural integrity and adsorption performance over repeated cycles under harsh conditions including high humidity, hydrothermal treatment, and corrosive gases (NOx, NH3). Under such conditions, it sustains high SO2 capacity and separation precision, outperforming nearly all reported SO2 sorbents. Post-synthetic anchoring of atomic copper onto DpTb-COF yields Cu1@DpTb-COF, which exhibits superior catalytic activity for direct conversion of captured SO2 into high-value cyclic sulfites under ambient conditions. The synergistic integration of excellent adsorption, high stability, and efficient catalysis establishes DpTb-COF as a versatile platform for deep removal and upcycling of industrial acid gases.

Results

Structural characterizations

Pyridazine, featuring two adjacent N atoms as the basic sites, was incorporated into COFs via double-bond linkages. Commercially available 3,6-dimethylpyridazine (Dp) was reacted with 1,3,5-triformylbenzene (Tb) and 1,3,5-tri(4-formylphenyl)triazine (Tz), respectively, using benzoic anhydride and benzoic acid as catalysts at 200 °C for five days, yielding the corresponding sp2 carbon-conjugated pyridazine-based COFs. For comparison, 2,5-dimethylpyrazine (Da), which contains para-oriented N atoms in the pyrazine ring, was synthesized under identical conditions. After the reaction, residual catalysts were removed by washing with an excess dimethylformamide/ammonium hydroxide mixture, followed by Soxhlet extraction with methanol/acetone, affording brownish-yellow (DpTb-COF), wine-red (DpTz-COF), and yellow (DaTz-COF) solid powders (Fig. 1a).

The crystal structures of the three COFs were systematically characterized by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD). For DpTb-COF, characteristic diffraction peaks were observed at 4.7 °, 8.0 °, 9.3 °, 12.2 °, 16.4 °, and 26.3 °, which can be indexed to the (100), (110), (200), (210), (220), and (001) crystal planes, respectively (Fig. 1b)36. Structural modeling and Pawley refinement based on the experimental PXRD data yielded unit cell parameters of a = b = 22.9 Å, c = 3.4 Å, α = β = 90°, γ = 120 °, with refinement agreement factors of Rwp = 7.96% and Rp = 10.19%. The experimental pattern showed excellent consistency with the simulated eclipsed AA stacking mode. In the PXRD pattern of DpTz-COF, diffraction peaks at 2.8 °, 4.8 °, 5.6 °, 7.4 °, 9.7 °, and 26.0 ° correspond to the (100), (110), (200), (210), (220), and (001) planes, respectively (Fig. 1c)37. Pawley refinement confirmed a hexagonal AA-stacked structure with unit cell parameters a = b = 37.9 Å, c = 3.4 Å, α = β = 90 °, γ = 120 °, and reliability factors of Rwp = 7.33% and Rp = 5.77%, demonstrating high crystallinity. Similarly, DaTz-COF exhibited distinct diffraction peaks at 2.93 °, 4.94 °, and 5.77 °, assigned to the (100), (110), and (200) planes, respectively (Fig. 1d)38. Pawley refinement using an AA-stacked model produced unit cell parameters a = b = 37.2 Å, c = 3.2 Å, α = β = 90 °, γ = 120 °, with refinement indices Rwp = 5.03% and Rp = 4.13%, further indicating excellent crystallinity.

The morphology and pore architecture of DpTb-COF were investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM). SEM imaging revealed a monolithic topology with a micro–nanoscaled porous surface texture (Supplementary Fig. 1). HR-TEM analysis further confirmed the high crystallinity of the material, displaying distinct lattice fringes with interplanar spacings of 1.9 nm, 1.1 nm, and 0.9 nm, corresponding to the (100), (110), and (200) planes, respectively (Fig. 1e, and Supplementary Fig. 2). Additionally, clearly resolved fringes with a spacing of 0.34 nm were observed, assignable to the (001) plane (Fig. 1f, g), which corroborates a well-ordered two-dimensional layered structure. Elemental mapping via energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) verified the homogeneous distribution of pyridazine-type N sites throughout the COF framework (Supplementary Fig. 3).

The porosity of the synthesized COFs was evaluated by N2 physisorption measurements at 77 K (Fig. 2a). All isotherms displayed typical Type I behavior, characterized by a sharp uptake at low relative pressure (P/P0 = 0.05 ~ 0.25), confirming the presence of abundant and uniform micro-/mesopores39. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface areas were calculated to be 877, 1700, and 1024 m2/g for DpTb-COF, DpTz-COF, and DaTz-COF, respectively. Their pore size distributions, derived from non-local density functional theory (NLDFT), showed primary peaks at 1.1 nm, 2.7 nm, and 2.1 nm, correspondingly (Supplementary Fig. 4). These high surface areas, coupled with their tunable pore diameters, render these COFs highly advantageous for gas adsorption and separation applications.

The chemical structures of the synthesized COFs were characterized by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and 13C cross-polarization magic angle spinning (CP/MAS) NMR spectroscopy. FTIR spectra exhibited characteristic trans-vinylene stretching vibrations at 1635 cm−1 and 960 cm−1; the disappearance of the C–H stretching peak at 2927 cm−1 (methyl groups on pyridazine/pyrazine rings) and reduced intensity of the C = O stretching vibration at 1694 cm−1 (aldehyde monomers) confirmed a high degree of polycondensation (Fig. 2b)40,41. Solid-state 13C NMR spectroscopy was used to further characterize the COFs (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 5). For all three COFs, characteristic signals at 125 ~ 129 ppm and 135 ~ 140 ppm were assigned to vinyl and benzene ring carbon atoms, respectively. In DpTb-COF and DpTz-COF, signals at 134 ~ 136 ppm and 155 ~ 156 ppm corresponded to pyridazine ring carbons, whereas a signal at 148 ppm in DaTz-COF was attributed to pyrazine ring carbons42–45. Additionally, a distinct signal at 169 ~ 170 ppm (triazine ring carbons) was observed in both DpTz-COF and DaTz-COF46. Collectively, these results confirm the successful synthesis of COFs incorporating pyridazine and pyrazine units.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) further elucidated the chemical composition of the COFs. Survey spectra confirmed the presence of C and N (Supplementary Figs. 6a–c, and Supplementary Table 1). Deconvolution of high-resolution C 1 s spectra revealed two primary components: a 284.4 eV peak ascribed to C–C/C = C bonds and a 286.1 eV peak corresponding to C = N bonds (Supplementary Figs. 6d–f)47,48. All COFs showed a dominant 399.4 eV peak in N 1 s spectra, characteristic of N atoms in pyridazine/pyrazine rings (Fig. 2d)49; additionally, DpTz-COF and DaTz-COF exhibited an extra 398.6 eV peak, indicative of triazine ring N atoms50. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) demonstrated high thermal stability, with no significant weight loss below 450 °C (Supplementary Fig. 7). The COFs also displayed excellent chemical stability, retaining crystallinity after 24 h in strongly acidic/basic solutions, as verified by PXRD and FTIR (Fig. 2e, and Supplementary Figs. 8, 9).

The degree of π-conjugation within the framework of COFs significantly governs the distribution of π-electrons and basicity of N sites, which in turn dictates their performance in applications such as adsorption and catalysis. To probe the contribution of pyridazine group to the conjugation effect within the COF framework compared to its amorphous polymeric network counterpart (DpTb-POP, Supplementary Fig. 10), we employed solid-state UV-Vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy. A significantly red-shifted absorption edge (653 nm for DpTb-COF vs. 562 nm for DpTb-POP) in the UV-Vis spectrum indicates a more extended π–π conjugation in the crystalline framework (Fig. 2f). This conclusion is further corroborated by the fluorescence spectrum, where the maximum emission wavelength of DpTb-COF (444 nm) is also red-shifted compared to that of DpTb-POP (439 nm), collectively confirming the enhanced electronic conjugation in DpTb-COF (Supplementary Fig. 11).

The influence of conjugation on pyridazine basicity was probed via in-situ FTIR spectroscopy using pyrrole as a probe molecule, by comparing the intrinsic basicity of DpTb-COF and DpTb-POP. This method exploits the hydrogen-bonding sensitivity of the pyrrole N–H bond, whose interaction with basic sites induces a characteristic red shift of the νN–H vibration51, with the shift extent providing a quantitative measure of base strength. Upon adsorption, the pyrrole νN–H band on both materials showed a significant red shift from its gas-phase position (~3400 cm−1). This shift arises from electron density transfer from the basic site (donor) to the N–H bond (acceptor), where the shift magnitude directly reflects hydrogen-bonding strength and thus the material’s intrinsic basicity.

As shown in Fig. 2g, pyrrole adsorption on DpTb-COF surfaces resulted in a distinct new peak at ~3243 cm−1, alongside the characteristic signal of free pyrrole at ~3400 cm−1. This phenomenon is attributed to electron transfer from the pyridazine groups of DpTb-COF to the pyrrole molecules; such electron-donating behavior enhances interfacial interactions, thereby stabilizing pyrrole adsorption on the material surface. Similar trends were observed for DpTb-POP and DaTz-COF, but with notable discrepancies among the three materials. Compared to DpTb-COF, the new peak derived from pyrrole adsorption on DpTb-POP exhibited significant enhancement and a red shift, indicating stronger basicity. In contrast, DaTz-COF showed a weaker interaction-induced peak with a blue shift after pyrrole adsorption, suggesting relatively weaker basic strength. Furthermore, pyrrole desorbed gradually with increasing temperature: its signal completely vanished at 210 °C for DpTb-POP, 180 °C for DpTb-COF, and 150 °C for DaTz-COF. This desorption order is consistent with the basic strength trend, validating that the conjugation effect within the COF skeleton can be utilized to precisely tune the basicity of N-containing functional groups, with DpTb-COF displaying intermediate basicity between DpTb-POP and DaTz-COF (Fig. 2h).

SO2 adsorption, separation and upcycling

Endowed with optimal porosity, high crystallinity, robust chemical stability, and abundant pyridazine/pyrazine sites with moderate basic strength, the SO2 adsorption-separation performance of DpTb-COF was systematically evaluated and compared with DpTb-POP, DpTz-COF, and DaTz-COF. Notably, DpTb-COF exhibited a remarkable SO2 uptake of 19.4 mmol/g at 25 °C and 1.0 bar (Fig. 3a), which significantly outperforms conventional sorbents (e.g., activated carbon, zeolites) and numerous reported porous materials (Fig. 3b, and Supplementary Fig. 12). This high adsorption capacity is attributed to its ordered nanoporosity and high density of pyridazine-N sites with optimal basic strength. In contrast, amorphous DpTb-POP with an identical chemical composition, exhibited a lower SO2 uptake of 9.6 mmol/g (Supplementary Fig. 13). Similarly, DpTz-COF and DaTz-COF, despite their structural similarity to DpTb-COF, showed relatively lower SO2 capacities of 10.5 mmol/g and 9.5 mmol/g, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 14). Notably, the SO2 adsorption-desorption isotherms of the as-synthesized COFs are dominated by a Type I isotherm with a secondary Type IV feature, accompanied by a distinct H4-type hysteresis loop in the desorption branch, indicating the presence of hierarchical micro- and mesopores and slit-like/lamellar pore morphologies in the COFs frameworks (Supplementary Fig. 15). Specifically, the obvious desorption hysteresis was observed in DpTb-COF, revealing the strong specific interactions between the SO2 molecules and the pyridazine active sites in the COF skeletons21. Furthermore, DpTb-COF exhibited a high SO2 adsorption capacity of 8.0 mmol/g even at low pressure of 0.1 bar, outperforming most reported porous sorbents (Fig. 3b, and Supplementary Table 2). The gradual decrease in SO2 capacity with increasing temperature indicates a typically exothermic process. Consistent with this, the corresponding enthalpies for DpTb-COF, DpTz-COF, and DaTz-COF were calculated to be –37.8, –30.1, and –28.5 kJ/mol, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 16). The notably more negative enthalpy of DpTb-COF points to a stronger interaction with SO2 compared to the other COFs.

Given that CO2, H2S, COS and N2 are major components of many industrial streams, we measured their adsorption isotherms on these materials. This evaluation is crucial for assessing the materials’ selectivity towards SO2 and their overall separation capability. As shown in Fig. 3a, and Supplementary Fig. 17, at 25 °C and 1.0 bar, DpTb-COF exhibited capacities of 6.8, 5.8, 1.72 and 0.11 mmol/g for H2S, COS, CO2 and N2, respectively, which are substantially lower than its SO2 uptake. In line with the trend observed for SO2, the adsorption of these gases also decreased with increasing temperature. Other comparative materials—DpTb-POP, DpTz-COF, and DaTz-COF—exhibited the same selective adsorption behavior, with high capacity for SO2 but lower uptake of H2S, COS, CO2, and N2 (Supplementary Figs. 18–21). The respective uptake capacities (mmol/g) were as follows: DpTb-POP: 3.1, 1.31, 0.83, 0.04; DpTz-COF: 3.6, 3.57, 1.11, 0.10; DaTz-COF: 2.4, 2.07, 0.84, 0.05. Ideal Adsorption Solution Theory (IAST) selectivity calculations further affirmed the superior performance of DpTb-COF. At 25 °C and 0.1/0.9 v/v ratio, its selectivities for SO2/CO2, SO2/N2, COS/CO2, COS/N2, SO2/COS and SO2/H2S reached 55.3, 730.1, 10.8, 141.8, 13.1 and 11.3 respectively. Compared with the reference materials—DpTb-POP (36.9, 698.3, 6.0, 113.4, 23.8, 17.5), DpTz-COF (25.6, 302.3, 7.3, 86.5, 7.9, 8.0), and DaTz-COF (25.2, 464.0, 6.1, 111.5, 10.2, 9.3)—DpTb-COF exhibits superior selectivity in the vast majority of gas pairs, with its overall performance significantly surpassing that of most high-performance sorbents reported in the literature. (Fig. 3b, c, Supplementary Figs. 22–26 and Supplementary Table 2)5,7,8,28–30,52–65.

The exceptional selectivity toward sulfur-containing gases of DpTb-COF arises from its unique conjugated framework that acts as an active electronic regulator, precisely tuning the electron delocalization of its pyridazine groups. The extended π-conjugation enhances π-electron density while lowering N basicity, creating a differential affinity for acid gases. This fine-tuning selectively weakens interactions with CO2—which are highly sensitive to electron-donating capacity—while preserving strong, specific binding with SO2. Thus, the material favors SO2 and COS adsorption, which involves more covalent-like bonding, over the more electrostatic interaction-dependent CO2. By incorporating the pyridazine groups into the COF, a suitable electronic structure is constructed, establishing a gradient discrimination mechanism for precise acid gases separation.

The adsorption kinetics of these COFs for SO2, a key factor in desulfurization processes, were evaluated using pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order models. As shown in Fig. 3d, DpTb-COF reached its equilibrium SO2 uptake within 8.5 minutes, whereas DpTb-POP, DpTz-COF, and DaTz-COF required longer saturation times of 11.9, 9.8, and 13.8 minutes, respectively. The kinetic data were well fitted by the pseudo-second-order model, with high correlation coefficients (R2 > 0.999; Supplementary Fig. 27, Supplementary Table 3), indicating that chemisorption is the dominant adsorption mechanism.

Reusability is another critical parameter for assessing the practical potential of sorbents. Cycling tests demonstrated that DpTb-COF maintains high SO2 adsorption performance over 50 consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles (Fig. 3e). After regeneration under vacuum at 100 °C, the material retained more than 98.4% of its initial capacity, confirming excellent reversibility and structural stability. This performance is attributed to the moderate basicity of pyridazine groups, which facilitates effective SO2 binding and release. To further verify the cycling stability, DpTb-COF was characterized by XPS, PXRD and N2 physisorption measurements before and after SO2 adsorption. The N 1 s XPS spectra showed negligible changes after fifty cycles, and no sulfur species were detected in the S 2p region (Supplementary Fig. 28), confirming complete SO2 desorption and the chemical integrity of the framework. In addition, the PXRD patterns remained virtually unchanged after multiple cycles (Supplementary Fig. 29), with well-retained crystallinity and sharp diffraction peaks. N2 isotherms confirm that the porous structure is well retained after multiple cycles, with negligible changes in BET surface area and pore size distribution (Supplementary Fig. 30), underscoring the structural robustness of DpTb-COF under repeated adsorption–regeneration conditions. These results collectively validate its exceptional cycling stability for practical SO2 capture.

To gain deeper insight into the reversible SO2 adsorption mechanism of DpTb-COF, we performed in-situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) to dynamically monitor the adsorption process (Fig. 3f). The spectral data revealed characteristic absorption peaks at 2451, 1687, 1334, and 1143 cm−1, which are attributed to the symmetric/asymmetric stretching and bending vibrations of SO211,16,66. Meanwhile, the vibrational signals at 1043, 981, and 865 cm−1 indicate the formation of a stable N–S bond between the pyridazine group and SO227,30,67. As the temperature increased, the intensities of all these characteristic peaks gradually decreased and eventually disappeared at 100 °C, indicating the complete cleavage of formed N–S bonds enables efficient SO2 desorption. The effective and complete desorption of SO2 during repeated cycling is mainly ascribed to the moderate and well-balanced binding affinity between SO2 and DpTb-COF. These findings not only elucidate, at the molecular level, the critical role of the pyridazine group in achieving reversible SO2 capture through dynamic host–guest interactions, but also provide solid spectroscopic evidence for the outstanding structural stability and regenerability of the DpTb-COF.

To evaluate the practical potential of DpTb-COF in desulfurization, breakthrough experiments were conducted using a ternary gas mixture (SO2/CO2/N2). As shown in Fig. 4a, CO2 and N2 broke through the column almost immediately, whereas SO2 exhibited a breakthrough initiation at 361 min/g and saturation at 1212 min/g, corresponding to a saturation capacity of 1.85 mmol/g. Remarkably, the adsorption performance remained nearly unchanged over five consecutive adsorption-desorption cycles, significantly surpassing those of DpTz-COF (0.83 mmol/g) and DaTz-COF (0.49 mmol/g) (Supplementary Fig. 31). These results are consistent with the static adsorption data, collectively confirming the excellent selectivity and recyclability of DpTb-COF for SO2 capture under industrial conditions. Meanwhile, DpTb-COF also achieves gradient sieving of acid gases (SO2, COS, CO2, and N2), as directly evidenced by its breakthrough behavior in a quaternary gas mixture. The material exhibits a distinct separation profile: immediate breakthrough of CO2/N2, intermediate capture of COS (breakthrough: 41 min/g; saturation: 178 min/g; capacity: 0.19 mmol/g), and the longest retention for SO2. Its COS capacity notably surpasses those of DpTz-COF, DaTz-COF, and DpTb-POP, which exhibit capacities in the range of 0.07 ~ 0.08 mmol/g, confirming its superior separation selectivity across multiple gases (Supplementary Fig. 32). Dynamic breakthrough tests under humid conditions further confirmed the practical advantage of DpTb-COF over the metal-based Mg-gallate. DpTb-COF maintains high SO2 adsorption performance at 30% and 50% relative humidity (RH) with the saturation capacity of 1.84 mmol/g (Supplementary Fig. 33). Even under high humid condition (96% RH), DpTb-COF exhibited only a minor decrease in SO2 breakthrough time (358 min/g) and capacity (1.83 mmol/g), with excellent recyclability over five cycles, significantly surpassing Mg-gallate (1.27 mmol/g, Fig. 4b, and Supplementary Fig. 34). This superior humidity resistance originates from its hydrophobic polymer network, as evidenced by an ultralow water uptake of 0.1 mmol/g, which is orders of magnitude lower than that of Mg-gallate (11.3 mmol/g, Supplementary Fig. 35). Unlike such hydrophilic metal-based sorbents, where water absorption promotes H2SO3 formation and structural decay68, DpTb-COF remains stable, ensuring high desulfurization efficiency.

Furthermore, DpTb-COF demonstrated exceptional hydrothermal stability, maintaining its dynamic SO2 breakthrough performance even after 12 hours of treatment at 100 °C in 3% water vapor atmosphere. The DpTb-COF also exhibited remarkable cycling stability, with nearly unchanged breakthrough capacity over five consecutive cycles, significantly outperforming Mg-gallate (Fig. 4c, and Supplementary Fig. 36). In the presence of NOx, the SO2 breakthrough curves of DpTb-COF remained virtually unchanged over five cycles, confirming its robustness under corrosive conditions (Fig. 4d, and Supplementary Fig. 37). The material also showed high tolerance to corrosive NH3, as verified by breakthrough experiments following treatment with NH3 (Supplementary Fig. 38). Overall, under harsh conditions, DpTb-COF delivered comprehensive performance superior to benchmarks such as HOF-NKU-1, Mg-gallate and NKU-201-Y3,9,60, highlighting its strong potential for industrial gas desulfurization applications (Fig. 4e).

Inspired by its exceptional SO2 adsorption performance at low pressures, we further explored its potential for sensing applications under low-concentration conditions. Thus, the luminescence response of DpTb-COF toward SO2 in anhydrous THF (1×10-4 mM SO2) was performed (Supplementary Fig. 39). Upon exposure to SO2, the material exhibits markedly enhanced photoluminescence intensity accompanied by a slight fluorescence peak redshift, revealing a prominent fluorescent response that enables sensitive SO2 detection. These findings confirm that DpTb-COF serves as a promising dual-functional material for simultaneous SO2 capture and detection.

Importantly, anchored single-atom copper on DpTb-COF (Cu1@DpTb-COF, Supplementary Figs. 40 and 41, Supplementary Table 4) enables efficient cycloaddition of captured SO2/COS with epoxides under ambient conditions. The catalyst achieved high conversions of epichlorohydrin (ECH) (98.9% for SO2 and 97.6% for COS) with nearly 100% product selectivity within 24 hours. Its performance significantly surpassed that of all control catalysts with similar copper content (~3.0 wt%): Cu1@DpTz-COF (86.2%; 76.1%), Cu1@DaTz-COF (85.2%; 59.6%), and Cu1@DpTb-POP (88.6%; 79.3%, Fig. 4f). Notably, the SO2 catalytic cycling tests demonstrate that Cu1@DpTb-COF retains its catalytic activity after five consecutive runs without noticeable loss, indicating its robust stability in the reaction (Supplementary Fig. 42). The postcycling characterizations, including PXRD, BET and ICP-OES, confirmed that its crystallinity and porous structure are well preserved with no significant Cu leaching, accounting for the excellent cycling stability (Supplementary Figs. 43 and 44, Supplementary Table 5). This superior performance is attributed to the synergistic combination of high SO2 uptake, optimal SO2 affinity of the DpTb groups, and well-defined single-atom Cu sites, which collectively facilitate reactant adsorption, activation, and product desorption. The detailed reaction pathways for the cycloaddition of ECH with SO2 on the Cu−N2 site are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 45. Briefly, the reaction proceeds via preferential adsorption and activation of ECH at the Cu−N2 site through strong coordination, followed by Br⁻-mediated nucleophilic ring-opening at the β-C atom. SO2 then inserts into the resulting intermediate, and subsequent intramolecular cyclization affords cyclic sulfites, with Br⁻ regenerated to tetrabutylammonium bromide (TBAB). Finally, the product desorbs from the active site, completing the catalytic cycle4.

Understanding superior performance of DpTb-COF for SO2 capture

To elucidate the critical role of the conjugated structure in DpTb-COF for SO2 adsorption, we constructed structural models of both the multi-layer conjugated ordered DpTb-COF and the single-layer non-conjugated disordered DpTb-POP (Supplementary Fig. 46). Analysis using the Independent Gradient Model based on Hirshfeld partition (IGMH, Fig. 5a, b) revealed that, compared to the non-conjugated disordered DpTb-POP, the multi-layer conjugated ordered DpTb-COF enables synergistic SO2 adsorption through pyridazine-N sites from two layers, resulting in a larger interaction area and stronger binding. Furthermore, IGMH analysis indicated that the interaction of DpTb-COF with CO2 is significantly weaker than that with SO2 (Supplementary Fig. 47), preliminarily explaining its high selectivity for SO2.

To further clarify the nature of the interaction between the multi-layer conjugated ordered DpTb-COF and SO2, we investigated the influence of conjugation degree on the electronic properties of the framework pyridazine-N atoms using Localized Molecular Orbitals (LMOs) analysis. The results show that the pyridazine-N in DpTb-COF interacts with SO2 primarily through the lone pair electron orbital and the delocalized electrons in the N-N-C π orbital. The electron delocalization index for the N lone pair orbital in DpTb-COF is 79.78, higher than the 77.57 in DpTb-POP, indicating that the lone pair electrons in DpTb-COF are less prone to delocalization, thereby reducing their interaction ability with weakly acidic CO2. This observation aligns with the weaker Lewis basicity of pyridazine-N in DpTb-COF, as indicated by pyrrole-IR spectroscopy. Conversely, the electron delocalization index for the N-N-C π orbital in DpTb-COF is 27.48, lower than the 29.30 in DpTb-POP (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Figs. 48, 49, and Supplementary Table 6), suggesting a stronger π-electron delocalization effect in DpTb-COF, which provides more interaction sites for SO2 adsorption. This explains the higher SO2 adsorption capacity and selectivity of DpTb-COF despite the lower basicity of its pyridazine-N. Moreover, SO2 can simultaneously interact with both the delocalized π orbitals and the lone pair electron orbitals of the double-layer pyridazine-N in DpTb-COF, whereas DpTb-POP can only interact with SO2 via the less delocalized π orbitals and lone pair orbitals of its single-layer N atoms (Fig. 5d-g, Supplementary Figs. 50, 51).

The interaction energies between SO2 and the active sites of the two materials were calculated using periodic Density Functional Theory (DFT, Supplementary Figs. 52, 53). After geometric optimization, the binding energy of SO2 on multi-layer conjugated ordered DpTb-COF was –66.4 kJ/mol, and that of CO2 was –42.6 kJ/mol, both higher than the corresponding values on single-layer disordered non-conjugated DpTb-POP (SO2: –45.1 kJ/mol; CO2: –22.9 kJ/mol). The significantly higher binding energy of SO2 compared to CO2 on DpTb-COF further validates its high selectivity for SO2. The planar, multi-layer, ordered conjugated structure of DpTb-COF effectively promotes framework π-electron delocalization, increases adsorption site density, and finely tunes the basicity of the pyridazine-N. This synergy maintains a moderate and reversible binding strength for SO2 that is sufficient for high-capacity capture yet weak enough to permit facile desorption upon mild heating, while simultaneously suppressing the co-adsorption of competing gases like CO2, thereby achieving a unification of high adsorption capacity, superior selectivity and excellent regenerability.

To further investigate the effect of conjugation degree on SO2 adsorption performance, we compared DpTb-COF with DpTz-COF, which features a triazine ring structure offering greater planarity and a higher degree of conjugation69. Owing to their planar multi-layer nature, both structures were analyzed using simplified single-layer periodic models, thereby avoiding the complexities associated with aperiodic disorder in non-conjugated polymers. IGMH analysis showed that the interaction between SO2 and the pyridazine-N in DpTb-COF is stronger (Supplementary Fig. 54). Intrinsic Bond Orbital analysis further revealed significant distortion of the N lone pair orbital in DpTb-COF due to charge transfer to the empty orbital of SO2, indicating a stronger N–S interaction compared to DpTz-COF (Supplementary Fig. 55). DFT calculation results confirmed that the interaction energy of SO2 with single-layer DpTb-COF (–47.0 kJ/mol) is higher than with single-layer DpTz-COF (–40.5 kJ/mol), and the S–N distance is shorter (2.60 Å vs. 2.75 Å; Supplementary Fig. 56), collectively demonstrating a stronger interaction between DpTb-COF and SO2. These findings indicate that although DpTz-COF possesses a higher conjugation degree, its interaction with SO2 does not correspondingly strengthen. Moderate basicity derived from the medium conjugation degree endows DpTb-COF with unique advantages in SO2 capture.

Additionally, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to further investigate the adsorption behavior of SO2 within the DpTb-COF framework. The adsorption structure (Supplementary Fig. 57) shows that the sulfur atoms of SO2 preferentially orient toward the pyridazine-N sites of the DpTb-COF. This observation is further supported by the adsorption density distribution (Supplementary Fig. 58), which indicates that SO2 molecules are predominantly localized around N atoms, suggesting strong intermolecular interactions. The resulting radial distribution function (Supplementary Fig. 59) shows that the dominant S–N interaction distance is ~3.2 Å, further confirming the strong interaction between SO2 molecules and pyridazine-N sites in the DpTb-COF framework.

Discussion

In summary, we developed pyridazine-functionalized sp2 carbon-conjugated COFs as robust sorbents for efficient SO2 capture and precise sieving. The strategic integration of dense pyridazine sites into the sp2 carbon-conjugated framework endows the materials with high SO2 capacity and selectivity. This exceptional performance originates from unique conjugation-induced electron delocalization, which enhances the overall π-electron density of the framework while moderately reducing the basicity of pyridazine units. This electronic modulation weakens acid-base interactions with CO2 while retaining strong, selective affinity for SO2, thereby establishing distinctive gradient sieving capability. Under real-world conditions, DpTb-COF exhibits superior anti-interference performance, with high tolerance to complex gas components (humidity, CO2, NOx) and hydrothermal stress. Furthermore, DpTb-COF retains over 98.4% of its initial SO2 uptake after 50 consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles; mild SO2 desorption is achieved via reversible acid-base interactions, enabling efficient regeneration while preserving structural integrity. Its overall performance outperforms nearly all SO2 sorbents reported in the literature. By fabricating Cu1@DpTb-COF as a catalyst, we realized efficient upcycling of captured sulfur-containing gases, introducing a resource-recovery loop. This study establishes a design strategy for high-performance multifunctional materials, advancing desulfurization technology from laboratory research toward industrial application and providing a sustainable solution for deep desulfurization and resource utilization of sulfur-containing gases.

Methods

Samples preparation

Preparation of DpTb-COF and DpTb-POP

Typically, 3,6-dimethylpyridazine (Dp, 0.75 mmol, 81.0 μL), 1,3,5-triformylbenzene (Tb, 0.5 mmol, 0.081 g), benzoic anhydride (1.0 mmol, 0.225 g) and benzoic acid (0.1 mmol, 0.0122 g) were weighed to a 10 mL Pyrex tube. The reaction mixture was subjected to three freeze-pump-thaw cycles at 77.3 K using a liquid nitrogen bath, followed by vacuum sealing. The sealed tube was then heated at 200 °C for 5 days. After cooling to room temperature, the obtained brown solid was crushed, finely ground, and then immersed in an NH3·H2O/DMF mixture (v/v = 1:1) for 24 hours. The solid was further purified by Soxhlet extraction with acetone/methanol (v/v = 1:1) for 12 hours. Finally, the product was dried at 80 °C under vacuum overnight to afford DpTb-COF. By contrast, DpTb-POP was synthesized via the same procedure, with only polymerization conditions modified (120 °C, 2 days).

Preparation of DpTz-COF and DaTz-COF

As control samples, DpTz-COF and DaTz-COF were synthesized following a reported protocol35. Briefly, 3,6-dimethylpyridazine (Dp, 0.75 mmol, 81.0 μL) or 2,5-Dimethylpyrazine (Da, 0.75 mmol, 81 μL), 1,3,5-tris(4-formylphenyl) triazine (Tz, 0.5 mmol, 0.197 g), benzoic anhydride (1.0 mmol, 0.225 g) and benzoic acid (0.1 mmol, 0.0122 g) were transferred into a 10 mL Pyrex tube. The subsequent freeze-pump-thaw, polymerization, and purification procedures were identical to those described for DpTb-COF.

Preparation of Cu1@DpTb-COF, Cu1@DpTz-COF, Cu1@DaTz-COF and Cu1@DpTb-POP

DpTb-COF (200 mg) and CuCl2·2H2O (80 mg) were separately added to 30 mL of methanol solution. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 12 hours. After that, the precipitate was collected by filtration and washed with ethanol and methanol and dried under vacuum at 80 °C for 12 hours to obtain Cu1@DpTb-COF powder. The synthesis methods for Cu1@DpTz-COF, Cu1@DaTz-COF and Cu1@DpTb-POP are identical to that of Cu1@DpTb-COF, except for the substitution of supports. The copper contents of these four materials, as determined by ICP-OES, are 3.5, 3.9, 2.8 and 3.0 wt%, respectively.

Gas adsorption and separation

SO2 adsorption isotherms of various samples were measured via a custom-built corrosion-resistant volumetric adsorption apparatus equipped with a storage chamber and an adsorption chamber, where the system temperature was regulated by a thermostatic water bath. Prior to testing, all samples were degassed under vacuum at 120 °C for 12 hours to remove residual impurities. The equilibrium adsorption capacities were calculated based on pressure changes before and after adsorption. Mixed-gas breakthrough experiments were performed using a Micromeritics AutoChem 2920 system equipped with a Hiden HPR-20 R&D gas-phase mass spectrometer. Specifically, the adsorbent (100 mg) was loaded into a U-shaped quartz tube (6 mm × 190 mm) and installed in the experimental system. The sample was purged with He flow (30 mL/min) and thermally activated at 120 °C for 2 hours, followed by cooling to 25 °C. The gas flow was switched to a SO2/CO2/N2 mixture (0.3 v%/15 v%/84.7 v%) at a total flow rate of 20 mL/min. The outlet gas composition was continuously monitored online by mass spectrometry, and the corresponding breakthrough curves were acquired from the concentration profiles. Detailed instrument parameters and experimental procedures are illustrated in the Supplementary Information.

Theoretical calculations

Theoretical calculations were performed to investigate the adsorption mechanism of SO2 in DpTb-COF, DpTz-COF and DpTb-POP. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations, including geometry optimizations and adsorption energy analysis, were carried out using the ωB97XD exchange-correlation functional. Localized Molecular Orbital (LMO), independent gradient model based on Hirshfeld partition (IGMH), and intrinsic bond orbital (IBO) analyses were further conducted to elucidate the intermolecular interactions and electronic origins of SO2 adsorption. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations combined with grand canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) calculations were performed to investigate the adsorption behavior and diffusion characteristics of SO2 within the framework. All DFT calculations were carried out using Gaussian 16, while Multiwfn, VMD software package and Materials Studio 2020 were used for electronic structure analysis and molecular simulations. Detailed computational procedures are summarized in the Supplementary Information.
