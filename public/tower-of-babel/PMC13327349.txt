How vaccine research took me on an international journey
Authors: Michael Schotsaert
Published: 2026

I grew up on the countryside in the Dutch-speaking part of Belgium in the 1980s & 1990s. Already as a child, I was intrigued by science and technology and always wanted to explore and know how things worked (and, for one reason or another, how much it would cost). As a grandson of a medical doctor whose practice was in the same house where I spent my childhood, I got in touch with the interesting world of medicine very early. My grandfather introduced me to the fascinating world of science. My grandmother’s parents were farmers, and my grandfather showed me how to take care of plants and fruit trees in the old orchard, and how to treat the beehives he maintained on the farm so they would not become infested with bee mites (Varroa destructor). It was also here that I did my first vaccination of a sheep. As I was always curious, he loved to explain me how things worked, for example that the vaccine given to sheep was different from the jabs I got in my arm but may work in the same way to keep me healthy. That vaccines protected you, so you would not get sick twice from the same virus, but in the case of vaccines you did not have to get sick first. He loved to explain the biology behind vaccination, the chemistry behind antiseptics, the technology of the machinery we worked with and so on. I took it all in with eager ears. From spending my weekends with him, it was clear to me that I wanted to study bio-engineering sciences. To me, this seemed like the ideal combination of science and technology. Knowing how injecting a killed pathogen, or a piece thereof, can protect against this same pathogen was as interesting to me as being able to calculate the performance of an engine. Moreover, as an engineer, you can apply a systems approach to basically any process. In engineering, you measure, calculate and adjust parameters to make the process run smoothly or find the extremes that allow maximum performance before the system becomes unstable. It was with true fascination that I learned that you could apply engineering approaches to model biological processes, from virus infection to the whole immune system. It is therefore with great interest that I have followed the field of Systems Vaccinology since the seminal paper from the Pulendran lab that used the Yellow Fever vaccine to study host responses to infection and vaccination that correlate with protection.1–3 Systems Vaccinology approaches allow us to understand vaccine responses by simultaneously focusing on multiple variables, which then can inform further vaccine development. Given the need to integrate the vast amount of scientific and clinical data generated on a daily basis, Systems Vaccinology has become a new branch in the vaccine research field that helps us understand how old vaccines work and guides development of the next generation of vaccines.

My first master thesis brought me to the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine at Ghent University (Belgium) in 2002. I had the honor to spend almost a year in the virology laboratory to work on Aujeszky’s disease virus, also known as pseudorabies virus (PRV). One of the focuses of the lab was how the virus could evade neutralizing antibodies, for example by internalizing viral proteins from the surface of infected cells after the binding of antibodies.4,5 The virus could also travel long distances in the body by hijacking axonal transport, which was elegantly visualized two years before with a recombinant GFP-expressing PRV reporter virus.6 This was the first time I experienced first-hand the power of molecular biology in real life. It was eye opening to me that one of the strengths of veterinary research is that you have access to animal models to study virus-host interactions in the natural host, without the requirement for bridging studies and difficult extrapolation exercises as is often the case for preclinical and clinical studies for humans. It was in this laboratory that I was exposed to virus-host interaction research. My mentor imprinted in my brain that you cannot uncouple virology from immunology, or the host from the pathogen. Although my research was not yet focused on vaccines, it was in this lab that I got familiar with the concepts of DIVA (Differentiating Infected from VAccinated individuals) by gene-deleted or subunit vaccine strains7 and spillover infections from wild-roaming animals into the human population or farm animals. PRV is eradicated from the domestic pig population in Belgium; however, it remains endemic in wild boars, and continuous monitoring is required to estimate the risk of spillover and reintroduction events. Although it would be very difficult, mass vaccination of wild roaring animals to eradicate PRV in the natural reservoir sounded like a wild idea. The fact that this might require oral vaccination in the field rather than injectable vaccines,8 a vaccination strategy suggested also for African Swine Fever,9 further added to the appeal.

I then moved on to Ghent University’s Department of Biomedical Molecular Biology and the Flemish Institute for Biotechnology (VIB) for a second master’s degree and PhD in molecular medical biotechnology. I joined the “Unit Molecular Virology,” under the supervision of Xavier Saelens as principal investigator. It was here that I got the chance to work with Walter Fiers and Willy Min Jou. Walter Fiers was a pioneer in biotech who had worked at the Carlsberg laboratory in Copenhagen (Denmark) before moving to the USA to work with Robert L. Sinsheimer at CalTech and Gobind Khorana at the University of Wisconsin in the 1960s. He and his team were the first to determine the full sequence of virus and bacteriophage genes and genomes, like the MS2 phage and SV40 virus.10,11 In 1980, my birth year, Willy Min Jou, Walter Fiers and colleagues published the full sequence of the influenza virus hemagglutinin (HA) in the journal Cell, which was the H3 HA from A/Victoria/3/1975 (an H3N2 influenza virus).12 This sequence was aligned with hemagglutinins from other influenza viruses, and allowed one to determine the transmembrane region, the conserved domains, as well as the variable regions in the HA globular head domain.13 This information made it possible to map back the HA antigenic sites to the genetic sequence when crystal structures of the HA molecule became available one year later.14,15 A key contributor to the studies describing the structural biology of the HA molecule is Ian Wilson, currently Hansen Professor of Structural Biology and chair of the Department of Integrative Structural and Computational Biology at the Scripps Research Institute in San Diego, California, United States. It is an honor for me that more than 45 years later, I was able to publish with Professor Wilson on another virus: MPXV.16 The insights from the genetic and structural studies of influenza virus HA have been crucial for influenza vaccine design until this day, with many universal vaccine approaches targeting the conserved areas of this viral spike protein. I had this Min Jou et al. Cell paper printed out hanging on the wall of the laboratory in Ghent, and designed the cloning strategies based on the typewriter written sequence. One of the H3N2 influenza viruses that I used for the mouse studies during my PhD is X47, which is a reassortant virus with internal genes of A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (PR8, an H1N1 influenza virus) and the HA and neuraminidase (NA) from A/Victoria/3/1975 (H3N2). In 1999, another paper from the Fiers research group provided proof of principle in mice that vaccination with a virus-like particle (VLP) that presented the conserved ectodomain of the influenza matrix 2 protein (M2e) to the immune system could protect against challenge with influenza viruses of different subtypes (H1N1 and H3N2 were tested in this manuscript).17 It had been shown 10 years before by Robert Lamb’s group that a monoclonal antibody targeting M2e (14C2) could limit influenza virus growth.18 However, making M2e immunogenic as part of a vaccine was a challenge taken on by the research group in Ghent. This universal vaccine approach was published in Nature Medicine, and was the beginning of a strategic collaboration with Sanofi. My PhD mentor Xavier Saelens, to whom I owe a great deal of gratitude, was involved early on and was leading the universal vaccine effort when I joined his lab. A very interesting editorial commentary was written on this publication by Edwin Kilbourne in which it was suggested that true heterosubtypic protection should involve viruses that did not share the same internal genes (PR8 and X47 were used in this study).19 Kilbourne also suggested that a true universal vaccine approach ideally took advantage of immunity that targets multiple influenza virus antigens, like the HA and NA in addition to M2e, an opinion that I share. In the years that followed, several avenues were explored to make M2e immunogenic through cloning the 23 amino acid-encoding sequence into different vaccine vectors. Inspired by the DNA vaccine work done at the Merck laboratories in the nineties,20 my initial research project consisted of designing and validating DNA vaccines that express multiple copies of M2e in the backbone of HA. Other approaches were presentation of the M2e on the tetrameric zipper GCN421 and in the antigenic loop of hepatitis B core protein.22 Working with DNA vaccines brought three new interesting aspects to my research: vaccine safety, vaccine delivery and antigen presentation. Initially, there were concerns about the safety of DNA vaccines with respect to integration in the genome and the induction of DNA-specific antibodies. Time and much research by teams that were better equipped to answer these questions showed that DNA vaccines are very safe. The aspects of vaccine delivery and antigen presentation by nucleic acid-based vaccines at the site of injection is still a hot topic today as similar questions are asked for the new generation of mRNA and DNA vaccines. In 2005, we did preclinical testing with DNA vaccines as naked plasmids, eventually followed by in vivo electroporation. The developments in the field of lipid nanoparticle formulation and delivery allow us to use much less nucleic acid-based vaccine and to even target their delivery to different tissues and antigen-presenting cells upon injection.23 My first scientific publication was on vaccine formulation and delivery, with the Ghent University Department of Pharmaceutical Sciences, using a mucosal Carbopol-based delivery system.24 I was a couple of years into my PhD when Sanofi asked the Saelens laboratory to investigate the contribution of T cells in universal influenza virus vaccine-mediated protection. Because DNA vaccine-encoded antigen has the potential to induce strong T cell responses, I started looking into that branch of the adaptive immune response. Around that time, the laboratory of Niels Lycke, in collaboration with the Saelens laboratory, had reported a CD4 T-cell epitope in M2e for BALB/c mice.25 However, the extent to which a CD4 T-cell response against M2e could contribute to protection was unclear and hard to uncouple from the antibody response. It is also important to understand that M2e-targeting antibodies do not provide sterilizing immunity against influenza virus infection. The viral M2 protein is abundantly expressed on the surface of infected cells, but is hardly present in the virion. Therefore, antibodies against M2e do not result in virus neutralization, but rather they clear infected cells by mechanisms like antibody-mediated cellular cytotoxicity.26–28 Hence, vaccine-induced immunity against M2e is protective, but also infection-permissive. This means that by using M2e-based vaccines, you modulate disease rather than prevent it. A similar reality is true for NA-specific immunity, which interferes with viral egress rather than with virus entry. This is why we looked to the extent to which host immune responses to virus infection, and T-cell induction in particular, was affected by pre-existing immunity induced by M2e- and NA-based vaccination.29,30 Sterilizing immunity is often presented as conferring immediate viral neutralization upon host exposure, but this oversimplifies reality. The concept is primarily based on in vitro neutralization assays that do not reflect in-vivo dynamics. During early infection, viruses inevitably replicate for several rounds before neutralizing antibodies achieve control. Effective immunity against respiratory viruses like influenza and coronaviruses, thus depends on maintaining high, durable titers of neutralizing antibodies in the respiratory mucosa – the critical barrier to infection. The concept of disease modulation by pre-existing immunity is a recurring theme that would define my scientific research focus since then. My first pandemic as a vaccine researcher was in 2009, when a swine-origin H1N1 influenza virus started spreading around the world. I remember the excitement, as strange as it sounds, that we felt to test the M2e-based approach. M2e-specific antibodies were protective, despite some amino acid differences between the human consensus sequence before the pandemic and the M2e sequence of the new pandemic virus. This was the second virus available for my preclinical research that was actually isolated in Belgium. The first one was a safe research version of an H5N1 highly pathogenic avian influenza virus isolated from smuggled crested hawk-eagles at the Brussels International Airport.31 Viruses respect no country borders.

My first experience with international mobility was during an Erasmus student exchange Program in 2002 at the University for Natural Resources and Life Sciences in Vienna, Austria. This was during my engineering studies and my then mentor at the Faculty for Veterinary Medicine in Ghent inspired me when talking about his postdoctoral research stay in the United States. Similarly, the stories from Walter Fiers about crossing the Atlantic Ocean by boat from Antwerp to New York, followed by crossing the USA from the East Coast to the West Coast to start working at CalTech, made me apply for a fellowship from the Belgian American Educational Foundation. This allowed me to start postdoctoral studies, in December 2013, in the Department of Microbiology at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai (ISMMS), in the laboratory of Adolfo García-Sastre. This was a big change on both the personal and professional level. The financial constraints early career researchers are put through when supporting a family in an expensive city like New York is what kills many research careers. The amazing science that was done at Mount Sinai, however, was worth the struggle. This department was known for virology, and the research to which I had been exposed was mainly molecular biology and immunology. The García-Sastre lab focuses a lot on viral pathogenesis and immune evasion, and I wanted to learn about innate immunity. It was this interest in innate immune signaling that drove me to the adjuvant field, which also allowed me to connect with amazing colleagues for injectable and mucosal adjuvant platforms.32,33 Small defective interfering (SDI) RNA from Sendai virus was characterized as a potent inducer of interferon (IFN) responses through sensing by the Retinoic acid-Inducible Gene I (RIG-I) receptor in the García-Sastre lab, and we are now investigating its potential as a standalone or combination adjuvant for vaccines delivered through different routes.33–35 It was an interesting time to join Mount Sinai, since a universal influenza virus vaccine approach was being developed here as well. Instead of focusing on M2e, the Mount Sinai approach focused on the highly conserved stem region of influenza virus HA. Interestingly, this epitope had similar challenges as M2e as a vaccine antigen, and correlates of protection were hard(er) to define, and for sure were to be found beyond virus neutralization.36

I joined the Microbiology faculty as a research assistant professor in 2017. Not so long before that, Zika virus (ZIKV) had emerged in the Americas, causing a severe epidemic in Brazil with many cases of microcephaly in newborns due to infection of their immunologically naive pregnant mothers.37 A befriended Belgian postdoctoral fellow at ISMMS was working on human brain organoids, and had specific interest in the impact of ZIKV infection on the developing human brain. Our combined expertise allowed us to establish infection and analysis protocols to evaluate the epigenetic imprinting of ZIKV infection on the developing human brain.38,39 This would not have been possible if both of us had been working in scientific silos, which emphasizes the importance of collaboration. In January 2020, I established my independent laboratory at ISMMS. This coincided largely with the start of the COVID-19 pandemic in the U.S.A. The whole world went into lockdown, and we were dragged into high containment laboratories to find preclinical models to test new and old vaccines, antivirals and immunotherapies. Access to a biosafety level 3 laboratory became crucial for scientists to continue their work, since only COVID-19-related work was permitted on site, and the whole world moved from in-person to online meetings and web conferences. Personally, I experienced digitization of meetings as a great leap in productivity. What I valued most was the opportunity to join national and international networks of experts dealing with the emerging virus, for example the Center for Excellence for Influenza Research and Response (funded by the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases). These networks were often already established before the COVID-19 pandemic and were further strengthened by corona-virologists whose expertise stemmed from previous SARS-CoV and MERS-CoV outbreaks. Fast establishment of new networks and engagement of already existing ones was invaluable for sharing knowledge and data as it became available from academic research laboratories, public health authorities, vaccine developers, and other stakeholders who might otherwise operate in isolation. Over the years, some of these connections deepened substantially, and resulted in long-lasting collaborations. The urgency to protect vulnerable populations and control the pandemic – to unlock society and restart economic activity – accelerated vaccine development across multiple platforms and adjuvants. It was also clear that vaccination serves two critical functions: preventing severe disease in infected individuals, and crucially, breaking transmission chains. For respiratory pathogens like influenza virus and SARS-CoV-2, this requires reducing both the quantity and duration of viral shedding from the respiratory tract. My laboratory’s focus on mucosal immunity aligned directly with this need, as many promising vaccine candidates target mucosal immune responses with the potential to block or reduce virus shedding from the respiratory tract. During the pandemic, we established transmission models for influenza viruses and SARS-CoV-2, and investigating how adjuvanted mucosal vaccines block viral transmission has become a major research topic in our laboratory.

Finally, I want to close with another aspect of which I became much more aware in the past years: the importance of public information campaigns. The persistently low vaccine uptake that we are seeing in some areas where this was not a problem before, reflects not only vaccine hesitancy rooted in misinformation, but also pandemic fatigue and communication gaps. It is frightening that children are exposed to vaccine-preventable diseases with potential long-term consequences (think of measles). Clear, transparent messaging about vaccine benefits and risks, coupled with direct acknowledgment of vaccine hesitancy, is essential. Such messaging requires sustained community engagement and deliberate trust-building over time. Organizations like the International Society for Vaccines, CEPI, PATH, Gavi, WHO and the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, to name a few, are indispensable to this effort, advocating for equitable global vaccine access, investing in the transparent communication that helps protecting vulnerable populations and creating awareness about the importance of vaccination.
