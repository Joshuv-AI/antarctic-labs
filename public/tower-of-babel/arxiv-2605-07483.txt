Does Your Neural Network Extrapolate? Feature Engineering as Identifiability Bias for OOD Generalization
Leonel Aguilar, Jan Nagler, Christoph Hoelscher, Nino Antulov-Fantulin

Abstract
Successful deep neural networks discover salient features of data. We show when and why they fail to learn out-of-distribution (OOD)-relevant representations from an in-distribution (ID) training window. This requires decoupling feature learning from data-generating-process (DGP) identifiability. From a single training window, OOD extrapolation is non-identifiable: infinitely many DGPs are ε \varepsilon -observationally equivalent on the training data but diverge arbitrarily outside it, and no in-distribution criterion alone reliably breaks the tie. A structural commitment, the feature map, label map, and model class ( φ , ψ , ℳ ) (\varphi,\psi,\mathcal{M}) , dictates the assumed DGP and governs OOD generalization while leaving ID performance essentially unchanged. When architecture, pretraining, augmentation, input formats, or domain knowledge implicitly inject the missing commitment, the model succeeds. When it cannot infer OOD-relevant structure from ID evidence, it fails. Changing only the representation can make the same architecture, at the same in-distribution loss, differ by ∼ 520 × {\sim}520\times out of distribution. When the commitment is correct and identifiable, OOD error vanishes. For example, Fourier coordinates turn periodic extrapolation into interpolation on 𝕊 1 \mathbb{S}^{1} . The same mechanism predicts outcomes in three natural-science settings (mass-action chemistry; Kepler’s-third-law exoplanet prediction, n = 2,362 n=2{,}362 ; and cross-species coding-DNA detection) and in a 264-run positional-encoding study across Transformer, Mamba, and S4D. Finally, a controlled study shows: correct features are necessary but not sufficient. The model class must express the target, and the transformed training data must cover the relevant representation space. Thus, feature engineering is not a departure from what makes deep learning successful. It is the explicit structural commitment that makes extrapolation identifiable.

While the Universal Approximation Theorem guarantees that neural networks are exceptional interpolators within the support of their training data, they struggle with out-of-distribution extrapolation. Frameworks from Statistical Learning Theory provide guarantees for in-distribution generalization, however, achieving mathematically guaranteed extrapolation in deep learning remains an unsolved problem. Proposition 1

(ERM is blind to observationally-equivalent processes)
.

Let

Scope: Three Necessary Conditions and the Limits

When is the right representation not enough?
We construct a periodic

). Three conditions emerge as jointly necessary:
(a)

(b)
a model class expressing the residual interaction order

(c)

with

. A practitioner with correct

fails OOD; one with both but

also fails. The torus benchmark localises where the commitment runs out and the rest of the OOD problem (identifiability, capacity, sampling) takes over.

Noise
\sigma
does not break the commitment (d).

Section

3
bounds the worst-case correct-decision probability of any
W

\tfrac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\sqrt{n}/(4\sigma)

\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\ll\sigma
and vacuous below. Measuring the empirical in-window gap as

at

places the noise threshold near

. Sweeping

); raw and learned-Fourier baselines are bias-floored in the vacuous regime, then rise with

in the informative regime as the over-parameterised MLPs fit noise. Critically, the canonical commitment retains a clear OOD advantage in both regimes (

lower OOD error at low

smaller OOD error at high

recovers it.

8
Discussion and Conclusion

Conclusion.

OOD risk on a single training window is non-identifiable from data alone (
Section

3
); the structural commitment
(\varphi,\psi,\mathcal{M})
is the only information the modeller supplies that resolves it.
A fixed architecture at identical ID loss can differ by
{\sim}520\times
OOD when only
\varphi
changes (
Table

1
), and the mechanism transfers, untouched, to chemistry (
0.0\%
vs.
24.4\%
OOD,
Table

3
), physics (
2.1\%
vs.
79.0\%
,
Table

4
), biology (AUROC up to
0.96
vs. below-chance phase-equivariant readouts,
Table

5
), and Transformer/Mamba/S4D over
264
runs (
Section

6.3
).
This sharpens “feature engineering is dead”: deep learning supersedes hand-crafted features ID once samples and noise permit, but
(\varphi,\psi,\mathcal{M})
remains the only ID-invisible signal that selects one extrapolation from the observationally-equivalent Rashomon class.
Pretraining hides the commitment in the input format (
Section

4.3
) without removing it; near-boundary loss and the SINDy
\delta_{\mathrm{OOD}}
recover it as operational selection signals (
Section

6
).

Limitations.

Section

3
’s information-theoretic bound is tight only when
\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\ll\sigma
, our experiments sit in the signal-dominated regime where the bound is vacuous, though the multiplicity it characterises still binds CV in practice (
Section

6.1
).
Near-boundary validation requires accessible boundary samples, the SINDy diagnostic is silent on non-polynomial dynamics (e.g.,
G_{2}{=}\tanh(\cdot)
):
\delta_{\mathrm{OOD}}{>}0
flags “no sparse coordinate” but cannot rank.
Correct
\varphi
is necessary, not sufficient: at
N\lesssim M_{\mathrm{crit}}^{d}
(
M_{\mathrm{crit}}{\approx}4
) or with a model class missing the residual interaction order
q
, OOD collapses regardless (
Section

7
).
The CDS head fails on high-GC organisms (yeast-trained amino-acid weights mis-fire on
M. tuberculosis
’s skewed codon usage).

Future work.

Two directions stand out: (i) a finite-sample OOD bound parametrised by sparse identifiability (
\delta_{\mathrm{OOD}}
of
Section

6.2
), extending
Appendix

C
beyond exact realisability and (ii) robust automatic
\varphi
discovery with near boundary information connecting with multi-environment domain advancements.

Appendix A
ERM is blind to observationally-equivalent processes

Proposition 1

(ERM is blind to observationally-equivalent processes, restated)
.

Let
D_{n}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
with
x_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
on
W
and
y_{i}=P_{k}(x_{i})+\eta_{i}
,
\eta_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
, for unknown
k\in\{1,2\}
where
P_{1},P_{2}
are
\varepsilon
-observationally-equivalent on
W
.
For any (deterministic or randomised) test
T:D_{n}\to\{1,2\}
,

\min_{k\in\{1,2\}}\Pr_{D_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{k}^{n}}[T(D_{n})=k]\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{n}}{4\sigma}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}+O(\varepsilon\sqrt{n}).

(3)

In particular, in the regime
\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\ll\sigma
, no
W
-supported test reliably distinguishes
P_{1}
from
P_{2}
.
Selection criteria such as cross-validation, training loss, or held-out loss on
W
are special cases.

Proof.

We use Le Cam’s two-point method
[
14
]
.
Let
\mathcal{D}_{k}
denote the joint distribution of a single observation
(x,y)
under hypothesis
P_{k}
, i.e.,
x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
and
y\mid x\sim\mathcal{N}(P_{k}(x),\sigma^{2})
, and let
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{n}
be the product measure over
n
i.i.d. samples.

Le Cam’s two-point bound.

For any (possibly randomised) test
T:D_{n}\to\{1,2\}
,

\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\Pr_{1}[T=2]+\Pr_{2}[T=1]\bigr)\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1-\mathrm{TV}(\mathcal{D}_{1}^{n},\mathcal{D}_{2}^{n})\bigr),

(4)

hence the worst-case correct-decision probability satisfies

\min_{k}\Pr_{k}[T=k]\;\leq\;\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{TV}(\mathcal{D}_{1}^{n},\mathcal{D}_{2}^{n}).

(5)

KL between joint distributions.

The
x
-marginal of
\mathcal{D}_{k}
is
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
(independent of
k
), so the joint KL reduces to a conditional KL:

\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{D}_{1}\,\|\,\mathcal{D}_{2})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}}\bigl[\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(P_{1}(x),\sigma^{2})\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(P_{2}(x),\sigma^{2})\bigr)\bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}}\!\left[\frac{(P_{1}(x)-P_{2}(x))^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right].

(6)

Since
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
is supported on
W
and
\sup_{x\in W}|P_{1}(x)-P_{2}(x)|\leq\varepsilon
,

\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{D}_{1}\,\|\,\mathcal{D}_{2})\;\leq\;\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}},

(7)

and tensorisation gives

\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{D}_{1}^{n}\,\|\,\mathcal{D}_{2}^{n})=n\cdot\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{D}_{1}\,\|\,\mathcal{D}_{2})\;\leq\;\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}.

(8)

Pinsker’s inequality

[
14
, Lemma 2.5]
then yields

\mathrm{TV}(\mathcal{D}_{1}^{n},\mathcal{D}_{2}^{n})\;\leq\;\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{D}_{1}^{n}\,\|\,\mathcal{D}_{2}^{n})}\;\leq\;\sqrt{\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}\;=\;\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{n}}{2\sigma}.

(9)

Combining the bounds,

\min_{k}\Pr_{k}[T=k]\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{n}}{4\sigma}.\qed

(10)

Remark on the regime.

The quantity
\varepsilon\sqrt{n}/\sigma
is the effective signal-to-noise ratio after
n
samples (signal scale
\varepsilon
, noise scale
\sigma/\sqrt{n}
).
The bound is informative when
\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\ll\sigma
(the
noise-dominated
regime, where the per-sample gap is drowned by noise; this is the proposition’s content and the regime characteristic of finite-window OOD problems) and vacuous (trivially
\leq 1
) when
\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\gg\sigma
(the
signal-dominated
regime, where
n
samples
can
reveal the gap).
Selection criteria (cross-validation, training loss, held-out loss on
W
) are functions of the same
W
-supported sample, hence special cases of a
W
-supported test, and the bound applies to them by inclusion.

Provenance.

The proof technique (Le Cam two-point + Pinsker) is classical
[
14
]
and the multiplicity it characterises is the Rashomon effect of
2
,
17
. Our contribution is the application to single-window OOD identifiability under the
(\varphi,\psi,\mathcal{M})
commitment, not the inequality.
The toy battery (Exp. 1.1, 1.2) and Exp. 3.1 sit in the signal-dominated regime where the bound is vacuous; the multiplicity it captures still binds in practice because the candidate hypothesis classes are observationally near-equivalent on
W
, and ERM/CV select among them with the residual ambiguity that follows (Exp. 3.1,
Section

6.1
).

Appendix B
Near-boundary data exits the agreement zone

Proposition 2

(Near-boundary data exits the agreement zone, restated)
.

Let
P_{1},P_{2}
be
\varepsilon
-observationally equivalent on
W=[a,b]
and diverge at rate
r>0
outside:
|P_{1}(b+\delta)-P_{2}(b+\delta)|\geq r\delta
for
\delta>0
small.
Then for any near-boundary segment
V=[b,b+\delta]
with
\delta\geq\varepsilon/r
,

\sup_{x\in V}|P_{1}(x)-P_{2}(x)|\;\geq\;\varepsilon,

(11)

i.e.,
V
exits the
\varepsilon
-agreement zone of
P_{1},P_{2}
, while by
Section

3

W
supports no reliable test for
\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\ll\sigma
. The threshold
\delta\geq\varepsilon/r
is tight.

Proof.

By the divergence assumption,
|P_{1}(b+\delta)-P_{2}(b+\delta)|\geq r\delta
. Substituting the threshold width
\delta\geq\varepsilon/r
:

|P_{1}(b+\delta)-P_{2}(b+\delta)|\;\geq\;r\cdot(\varepsilon/r)\;=\;\varepsilon.

(12)

Since
b+\delta\in V
,
\sup_{x\in V}|P_{1}(x)-P_{2}(x)|\geq\varepsilon
, so
V\not\subseteq\{x:|P_{1}(x)-P_{2}(x)|<\varepsilon\}
.
∎

Tightness (existence).

The threshold
\delta=\varepsilon/r
is sharp.
Take
P_{1}\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{R}
and
P_{2}(x)=\max(0,\,r(x-b))
. Then

\sup_{x\in W}|P_{1}(x)-P_{2}(x)|=0\leq\varepsilon,\qquad|P_{1}(b+\delta)-P_{2}(b+\delta)|=r\delta,

(13)

saturating the divergence assumption with rate
r
.
For any
\delta^{\prime}<\varepsilon/r
,
V^{\prime}=[b,b+\delta^{\prime})
has
\sup_{x\in V^{\prime}}|P_{1}(x)-P_{2}(x)|=r\delta^{\prime}<\varepsilon
, so
V^{\prime}
remains in the
\varepsilon
-agreement zone. The width
\delta=\varepsilon/r
is therefore the smallest near-boundary segment guaranteed to exit the agreement zone, validating the heuristic threshold of
Section

6.1
.

Appendix C
Realisable-case canonicalisation and three linearising instances

These three lemmas instantiate the
(\varphi,\psi,\mathcal{M})
commitment of
Section

3
with classical examples: the math is textbook (linear OLS in transformed coordinates), and the contribution is the unified framing under realisability.

Lemma 3

(Realisable-case canonicalisation)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\varphi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}_{x}
a feature map,
\psi:f(\mathcal{X})\to\mathbb{R}
a strictly monotonic (hence invertible) label map, and
\mathcal{M}
a hypothesis class on
\mathcal{Z}_{x}
. Suppose

(i)

realisability
: there exists
g^{\star}\in\mathcal{M}
such that

g^{\star}(\varphi(x))=\psi(f(x))\quad\forall\,x\in\mathcal{X}\quad\text{(not merely on $W$);}

(14)

(ii)

noise-free finite-sample identifiability
: ERM on
\mathcal{M}
from
n
i.i.d. samples
\{(\varphi(x_{i}),\psi(y_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{n}
with
x_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
and
y_{i}=f(x_{i})
recovers
g^{\star}
with probability
1
,
\hat{g}_{n}=g^{\star}
.

Then the predictor
\hat{f}_{n}=\psi^{-1}\circ\hat{g}_{n}\circ\varphi
satisfies

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{OOD}}(\hat{f}_{n})=0\quad\text{for any test distribution }\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}\text{ on }\mathcal{X}.

(15)

The case
\psi=\mathrm{id}
recovers the standard formulation.

Proof.

By realisability,
\psi\circ f=g^{\star}\circ\varphi
on
\mathcal{X}
. Composing with
\psi^{-1}
gives

f=\psi^{-1}\circ g^{\star}\circ\varphi\quad\text{on }\mathcal{X},

(16)

so
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{OOD}}(\psi^{-1}\circ g^{\star}\circ\varphi)=0
for every
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}
.
By identifiability,
\hat{g}_{n}=g^{\star}
with probability
1
, hence

\hat{f}_{n}=\psi^{-1}\circ\hat{g}_{n}\circ\varphi=\psi^{-1}\circ g^{\star}\circ\varphi=f\quad\text{w.p.~$1$,}

(17)

and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{OOD}}(\hat{f}_{n})=0
.
∎

Discussion.

Realisability is the load-bearing assumption: absent it,
\hat{g}_{n}
converges to the
\mathcal{M}
-best approximant on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
, which need not extrapolate.
Identifiability is a generic regularity condition; for the linear instances below it reduces to invertibility of the design Gram matrix.
The label map
\psi
canonicalises targets that are not naturally linear in
\varphi(x)
(e.g.,
\psi=\log
for multiplicative families), at the cost of a noise-model caveat addressed in
Remark

3
.

Lemma 4

(Sinusoidal instance: Fourier features)
.

Let
f(x)=\sin x
on
\mathbb{R}
and
\varphi(x)=(\sin x,\cos x):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The linear function
g(u,v)=\mathbf{w}^{\top}(u,v)
with
\mathbf{w}=(1,0)
satisfies

g(\varphi(x))=\sin x=f(x)\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R},

(18)

so
Appendix

C
applies with
\psi=\mathrm{id}
,
\mathcal{M}
the linear functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
g^{\star}=(1,0)^{\top}
; hence
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{OOD}}(g^{\star})=0
for any
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}
supported on
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Realisability is the displayed identity;
Appendix

C
(i) is satisfied with
\psi=\mathrm{id}
.
∎

Remark 1

(Phase-amplitude generalisation)
.

The same instance with
g^{\star}=(A\cos\phi,A\sin\phi)^{\top}
realises any
f(x)=A\sin(x+\phi)
, since

A\sin(x+\phi)=A\cos\phi\cdot\sin x+A\sin\phi\cdot\cos x=g^{\star\top}\varphi(x).

(19)

The 2-parameter family
\{A\sin(x+\phi):A\in\mathbb{R},\phi\in[0,2\pi)\}
is exactly the linear span of
\{\sin x,\cos x\}
, so realisability is automatic for any
A,\phi
.

Lemma 5

(Power-law instance: log-log features)
.

Let
f(x)=ax^{\beta}
for
a>0
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, on
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, with
\varphi(x)=(1,\log x):\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\psi(y)=\log y:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
.
The linear function
g^{\star\top}(u,v)=(\log a)\,u+\beta\,v
, with
g^{\star}=(\log a,\beta)^{\top}
, satisfies

g^{\star\top}\varphi(x)=\log a+\beta\log x=\log(ax^{\beta})=\psi(f(x))\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},

(20)

so
Appendix

C
applies with
\mathcal{M}
the linear functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
; hence
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{OOD}}(\psi^{-1}\circ g^{\star}\circ\varphi)=0
for any
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}
supported on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

Proof.

Realisability is the displayed identity;
Appendix

C
(i) is satisfied with
\psi=\log
.
∎

Lemma 6

(Exponential instance: log-
y
features)
.

Let
f(x)=ae^{\beta x}
for
a>0
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, on
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}
, with
\varphi(x)=(1,x):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\psi(y)=\log y:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
.
The linear function
g^{\star\top}(u,v)=(\log a)\,u+\beta\,v
, with
g^{\star}=(\log a,\beta)^{\top}
, satisfies

g^{\star\top}\varphi(x)=\log a+\beta x=\log(ae^{\beta x})=\psi(f(x))\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R},

(21)

so
Appendix

C
applies with
\mathcal{M}
the linear functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
; hence
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{OOD}}(\psi^{-1}\circ g^{\star}\circ\varphi)=0
for any
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}
supported on
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Realisability is the displayed identity;
Appendix

C
(i) is satisfied with
\psi=\log
.
∎

Corollary 7

(Unified OLS recovery in canonical coordinates)
.

For each instance
Appendices

C
,
C
and
C
, let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
be absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on a window
W
of positive Lebesgue measure (with
W\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{>0}
for
Appendix

C
and
W\subseteq\mathbb{R}
otherwise).
Then ordinary least squares on
n\geq 2
i.i.d. samples
\{(\varphi(x_{i}),\psi(y_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{n}
recovers
g^{\star}
with probability
1
:

\hat{g}_{n}=g^{\star}\;\;\text{w.p.~$1$},\qquad\varepsilon_{\mathrm{OOD}}(\hat{f}_{n})=0\quad\text{for any }\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}},

(22)

where
\hat{f}_{n}=\psi^{-1}\circ\hat{g}_{n}\circ\varphi
.

Proof.

We verify identifiability (
Appendix

C
(ii)) for OLS on
n\geq 2
samples in each instance.
The OLS estimator is

\hat{g}_{n}=(\Phi^{\top}\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^{\top}\mathbf{z},

(23)

with design matrix
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 2}
having rows
\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}
and target
\mathbf{z}_{i}=\psi(y_{i})=\psi(f(x_{i}))
.
By realisability,
\mathbf{z}_{i}=g^{\star\top}\varphi(x_{i})
, so
g^{\star}
is a zero-residual solution; uniqueness holds whenever
\Phi^{\top}\Phi
is invertible, which fails only if two rows of
\Phi
are collinear.
The collinearity event in each instance is:

•

Sinusoidal
(
Appendix

C
):
\sin x_{i}\cos x_{j}-\sin x_{j}\cos x_{i}=\sin(x_{i}-x_{j})=0
, i.e.,
x_{i}\equiv x_{j}\pmod{\pi}
;

•

Power-law
(
Appendix

C
):
\log x_{i}=\log x_{j}
, i.e.,
x_{i}=x_{j}
;

•

Exponential
(
Appendix

C
):
x_{i}=x_{j}
.

Each collinearity event is a finite union of measure-
0
subsets of the joint sample space and so has probability
0
under absolutely-continuous
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
.
Hence
\Phi^{\top}\Phi
is invertible w.p.
1
,
\hat{g}_{n}=g^{\star}
, and
Appendix

C
closes the argument.
∎

Remark 3

(Noise model under
\psi=\log

.

When
