On Some Properties of LCM-Lattices of Edge Ideals of k-Uniform Hypergraphs
Muneeba Mansha, Sarfraz Ahmad

Abstract




1.
Abstract

In this article, we investigate the combinatorial and algebraic properties of the lcm-lattice associated with the edge ideal of a hypergraph. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a hypergraph,
I(\mathcal{H})
its corresponding edge ideal in a polynomial ring in
n
variables, and
\mathrm{Icm}(I(\mathcal{H}))
the associated lcm-lattice. We establish conditions under which the lcm-lattice of an edge ideal is Boolean, modular, or complemented. Furthermore, we extend these results to the case of the product of lcm-lattices in the complemented case. Additionally, we study the effects of polarization on the lcm-lattices of
I(\mathcal{H})
and its polarized ideal.

AMS Classification: 06A06, 06A07, 06A11.

Keywords: posets, Boolean, modular, complemented lattices.

2.
introduction

Hypergraphs and their associated algebraic structures have long been central objects of study in combinatorial commutative algebra. Given a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
, its edge ideal
I(\mathcal{H})
, defined in a polynomial ring over
n
variables, encodes the combinatorial structure of
\mathcal{H}
in an algebraic form. The study of the
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattice, denoted by
\mathrm{Icm}(I(\mathcal{H}))
, provides a lattice-theoretic perspective on the generators of
I(\mathcal{H})
.

The structural properties of
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattices such as being Boolean, modular, or complemented which play a crucial role in determining the algebraic and combinatorial behavior of the underlying ideal. In this article, we identify precise conditions under which the
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattice of a hypergraph’s edge ideal possesses these properties. We further investigate how these properties extend to the product of
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattices in the complemented case and analyze the effect of polarization on the
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattice structure.

Foundational background on monomial ideals can be found in the work of Herzog and Hibi
[
7
]
. Classical results on posets and enumerative techniques, which are central to the study of monomial ideals and their associated lattices, are presented in Stanley’s monograph
[
11
]
. More recently, Dorang
[
3
,
4
]
investigated structural properties of
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattices of monomial ideals. The concept of the
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattice of a monomial resolution was introduced by Gasharov, Peeva, and Welker
[
12
]
, who showed that if there exists a map between two
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattices that is bijective on atoms and preserves joins, then a resolution of one ideal induces a resolution of the other. In particular, if the map is an isomorphism, the two ideals share the same total Betti numbers and projective dimension.

Kuei-Nuan Lin and Sonja Mapes
[
10
]
established a connection between the dual hypergraph and the
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattice of a given square-free monomial ideal. Moreover, Kimura, Rinaldo, and Terai
[
8
]
showed that the projective dimension of a monomial ideal depends on the
1
-skeleton structure of its dual hypergraph. Some results on the homotopy of complemented lattices are also given in
[
5
]
.

3.
Results and Discussions

This section presents the basic concepts, results, and discussions. Let
G=(V,E)
be a simple undirected graph with vertex set
V=\{1,\dots,n\}
and edge set
E
. More generally, a
k
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{H}=(V,E)
consists of a vertex set
V=\{1,\dots,n\}
and an edge set
E
, where each edge is a
k
-element subset of
V
. The corresponding edge ideal
I(\mathcal{H})
is the monomial ideal in the polynomial ring
S=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
over a field
\mathbb{K}
, generated by the square-free monomials

x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}\quad\text{for each }\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\in E

(see
[
9
,
6
]
for details).

Definition 3.0
.

A poset is a pair
(S,\preceq)
, where
S
is a set and
\preceq
is a binary relation on
S
satisfying:

•

Reflexivity:
x\preceq x
for all
x\in S
;

•

Antisymmetry: if
x\preceq y
and
y\preceq x
, then
x=y
;

•

Transitivity: if
x\preceq y
and
y\preceq z
, then
x\preceq z
.

If these conditions hold, the relation
\preceq
is called a
partial order
.

Definition 3.0
.

A
lattice

\mathcal{L}
is a poset
(E,\leq)
such that, for any two elements
a
and
b
in
\mathcal{L}
, there exists a unique greatest lower bound, denoted by
a\wedge b
and called the
meet
of
a
and
b
, and a unique least upper bound, denoted by
a\vee b
and called the
join
of
a
and
b
.

A lattice can be visualized through its Hasse diagram, where elements are arranged according to their rank, i.e., smaller elements (lower rank) appear below larger ones (higher rank). Line segments are drawn to connect each element with those it directly covers. For a detailed introduction to lattices and order theory, we refer to
[
1
,
2
]
.

Definition 3.0
.

The
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattice
of
I(G)
, denoted
\mathcal{L}(I(G))
, is the lattice whose elements are all least common multiples of subsets of the minimal generators of
I(G)
, partially ordered by divisibility. We denote the unique maximal element (the least common multiple of all generators) by
\hat{1}
and the minimal element by
\hat{0}
.

3.1.
Hypergraphs and Boolean Lattices

Definition 3.0
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the set of all subsets of
[r]
, ordered by inclusion. Then
\mathcal{L}
is a lattice, called the
Boolean lattice of rank
r
, and is defined as

\mathcal{L}=\{A\mid A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r\}\},

ordered by inclusion.

Theorem 3.0
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=(V,E)
be a
k
-uniform hypergraph, and let
I(\mathcal{H})=(x_{a_{1}}\cdots x_{a_{k}}\mid\{a_{i},\ldots,a_{k}\}\in E(\mathcal{H}))
be its edge ideal with
\mathcal{L}(I(\mathcal{H}))
to be its
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattice. Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

\mathcal{L}(I(\mathcal{H}))
is isomorphic to a Boolean lattice

(b)

every hyperedge
e\in E(\mathcal{H})
contains at least one vertex
v_{e}\in e
such that
v_{e}\notin e^{\prime}
for all other hyperedges
e^{\prime}\in E(\mathcal{H})\setminus\{e\}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}(E(\mathcal{H}))
be the power set of the edge set
E(\mathcal{H})
of the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
. Let
f
be a map from
\mathcal{P}(E(\mathcal{H}))
to the elements of the
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattice
\mathcal{L}(I(\mathcal{H}))
, defined as follows:

\text{for}\,\,\,\,A\subseteq E(\mathcal{H}),\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,A\mapsto\mathrm{lcm}(A).

Then the
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattice
\mathcal{L}(I(\mathcal{H}))
is Boolean if and only if
f
is bijective.

a)\implies b)

Suppose
\mathcal{L}(I(\mathcal{H}))
is Boolean and let
e_{1}
be a hyperedge in
E(\mathcal{H})
such that for each
v_{e_{1}}\in e_{1}
, there exist at least one hyperedge
e_{2}\in E(\mathcal{H})
such that
v_{e_{1}}\in e_{2}
. Thus, every vertex of
e_{1}
lies in some other hyperedge, hence

\cup_{e\in E(\mathcal{H})}e\;=\;\cup_{e\in E(\mathcal{H})\setminus\{e_{1}\}}e,

contradicting injectivity of the map
f
.

b)\implies a)

Conversely, the map
f
from
\mathcal{P}(E(\mathcal{H}))
to the elements of the
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattice
\mathcal{L}(I(\mathcal{H}))
is surjective by definition. Now for injectivity, assume we have two subsets
A
and
B
from the edge set
E(\mathcal{H})
such that
A\not=B
. We need to show,

f(A)\not=f(B).

To prove this, it is sufficient to show,

\cup_{e\in A}e\;\not=\;\cup_{e\in B}e,

i.e.,
A\not=B
, so there exist at least one hyperedge
e\in A
, such that
e\not\in B
. Since every hyperedge
e\in E(H)
contains at least one vertex
v_{e}\in e
such that
v_{e}\notin e^{\prime}
for all other hyperedges
e^{\prime}\in E(\mathcal{H})\setminus\{e\}
, therefore,
\cup_{e\in A}e
cannot be equal to
\cup_{e\in B}e
and hence
\mathrm{lcm}(A)\not=\mathrm{lcm}(B)\iff f(A)\not=f(B)
, as required.
∎

Theorem 3.0

.

A lattice

is modular if and only if it does not contain a sublattice isomorphic to the pentagon lattice

Let

k

Definition 3.0
.

If

34
, but
34
is not there. Also,
123
would need complement
4
, that is also not there.
Thus, not every element has a complement. So, the lattice is not a complemented lattice.

∎

Definition 3.0
.

A lattice
L
is a
relatively complemented lattice
if every interval
[x,y]
(viewed as a sublattice) of
L
is complemented.

Remark 3.0
.

In a distributive lattice, all complements and relatively complements of poset are unique.

Theorem 3.0
.

Let
G
be a connected graph and
I(G)
be its edge ideal, then
lcm(I(G))
is
relatively complemented
if and only if there exists no induced subgraph
G|_{W}
on the vertex set
W\subseteq V
such that
{G|_{W}}
is of path length
\geq 3
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathrm{lcm}(I(G))
is relatively complemented.
To the contrary, assume that there exists an induced subgraph
W\subseteq V
having a path of length
3
, where

W=\{x_{1}x_{2},\,x_{2}x_{3},\,x_{3}x_{4}\}
.
Then, the interval

[a,\,\{x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}\}]

is not complemented for
a\in\{x_{1}x_{2},\,x_{3}x_{4}\}
.

Conversely, suppose that there exists no induced subgraph of path length
3
.
This means that
G
has a maximum induced path of length two.
We now show that
\mathrm{lcm}(I(G))
is relatively complemented.
Since the
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattice of a graph having path length at most
2
is Boolean, and every Boolean lattice is relatively complemented, we conclude that
\mathrm{lcm}(I(G))
is relatively complemented.

∎

3.4.
Product of
\mathrm{lcm}
-Lattices

We start with following basic property of the
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattices.

Definition 3.0
.

Given two lattices
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, then the product of lattices
L_{1}\times L_{2}
forms a lattice in a natural way. The partial order is defined by:

(x_{1},y_{1})\leq(x_{2},y_{2})\quad\text{if and only if}\quad x_{1}\leq x_{2}\ \text{in }L_{1}\ \text{and}\ y_{1}\leq y_{2}\ \text{in }L_{2}.

Proposition 3.0
.

Let
L_{1},\ldots,L_{r}
be the lattices then
L_{1}\times L_{2}\ldots\times L_{r}
be complemented if and only if
L_{1},\ldots,L_{r}
be complemented.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)

Let
L=L_{1}\times L_{2}\times\cdots\times L_{r}
be complemented. Let
x_{i}\in L_{i}
and consider the element
a=(0_{1},\dots,0_{i-1},x_{i},0_{i+1},\dots,0_{r})\in L
.
Since
L
is complemented, there exists
a^{\prime}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{r})\in L
such that

a\vee a^{\prime}=1_{L}\quad\text{and}\quad a\wedge a^{\prime}=0_{L}.

Now the
i
-th component gives

x_{i}\vee y_{i}=1_{L_{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad x_{i}\wedge y_{i}=0_{L_{i}}.

Thus
y_{i}
is a complement of
x_{i}
in
L_{i}
. Since
x_{i}
was arbitrary,
L_{i}
is complemented.

(\Leftarrow)

Now let
L_{i}
is complemented for all
i
. Let
u=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{r})\in L
.
Now for each
x_{i}\in L_{i}
, there exist a complement
x_{i}^{\prime}\in L_{i}
such that:

x_{i}\vee x_{i}^{\prime}=1_{L_{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad x_{i}\wedge x_{i}^{\prime}=0_{L_{i}}.

Set
u^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},\dots,x_{r}^{\prime})\in L
.
Then from above:

u\vee u^{\prime}=(x_{1}\vee x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,x_{r}\vee x_{r}^{\prime})=(1_{L_{1}},\dots,1_{L_{r}})=1_{L}

and

u\wedge u^{\prime}=(x_{1}\wedge x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,x_{r}\wedge x_{r}^{\prime})=(0_{L_{1}},\dots,0_{L_{r}})=0_{L}.

Therefore
u^{\prime}
is a complement of
u
in
L
.

∎

3.5.
Impact of Polarization on
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattices

In the above discussion, we have considered it
I
to be a square-free monomial ideal. To extend these results to the case of a general monomial ideal
I
in the polynomial ring
S
, we use the concept of
polarization
, as defined below. In particular, we aim to show that the
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattice of
I
and that of its polarization
I^{p}
are isomorphic. Consequently, all the above results will also hold for any monomial ideal
I
.

Definition 3.0
.

Let
S=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over a field
\mathbb{K}
, and let

I=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{m})\subseteq S

be a monomial ideal, where each
u_{j}=x_{1}^{a_{1j}}x_{2}^{a_{2j}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{nj}}
is a monomial in
S
. The
polarization
of
I
, denoted
I^{p}
, is a square-free monomial ideal obtained in a larger polynomial ring

S^{p}=\mathbb{K}[x_{i1},x_{i2},\ldots,x_{ia_{i}}\mid 1\leq i\leq n],

where
a_{i}=\max_{j}\{a_{ij}\}
. Each monomial
u_{j}
is replaced by the square-free monomial

u_{j}^{p}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{a_{ij}}x_{ik}.

Then,

I^{p}=(u_{1}^{p},u_{2}^{p},\ldots,u_{m}^{p}).

Example 3.0
.

Consider the polynomial ring
S=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},x_{2}]
and the monomial ideal

I=(x_{1}^{2}x_{2},\;x_{2}^{3}).

To polarize, we introduce new variables
x_{11},x_{12}
for
x_{1}^{2}
and
x_{21},x_{22},x_{23}
for
x_{2}^{3}
. Thus, in

S^{p}=\mathbb{K}[x_{11},x_{12},x_{21},x_{22},x_{23}],

the polarized ideal is

I^{p}=(x_{11}x_{12}x_{21},\;x_{21}x_{22}x_{23}).

Here,
I^{p}
is square-free, and it preserves many algebraic properties of
I
.
So, for
I
, polarization gives
I^{p}
, preserving the
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattice structure.

The following result shows that polarization preserves the
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattice isomorphism between monomial and square-free ideals. This enables us to extend our characterizations of Boolean and modular properties to any monomial ideal.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
I\subset S=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a monomial ideal and let
I^{p}
denote its polarization. Let
\mathcal{L}(I)
and
\mathcal{L}(I^{p})
be the
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattices of
I
and
I^{p}
, respectively. Then,

\mathcal{L}(I)\cong\mathcal{L}(I^{p}).

Proof.

The
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattice of a monomial ideal is atomic, and two monomials are equal if and only if their polarizations are equal. It suffices to show that the polarization of the join of two monomials equals the join of their polarizations; that is
(m_{1}\vee m_{2})^{p}=m_{1}^{p}\vee m_{2}^{p}
.
Let
m_{1}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
and
m_{2}=x_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
. Then,

m_{1}\vee m_{2}=x_{1}^{\max(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})}\cdots x_{n}^{\max(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})}.

The polarization of
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are given by:

m_{1}^{p}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{\alpha_{i}}x_{ij},\quad m_{2}^{p}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{\beta_{i}}x_{ij}.

Therefore,

m_{1}^{p}\vee m_{2}^{p}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{\max(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})}x_{ij},

which is exactly the polarization of
m_{1}\vee m_{2}
. Hence,

(m_{1}\vee m_{2})^{p}=m_{1}^{p}\vee m_{2}^{p}.

This shows that the
\mathrm{lcm}
-lattices
\mathcal{L}(I)
and
\mathcal{L}(I^{p})
are isomorphic.
∎
