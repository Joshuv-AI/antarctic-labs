CSN3 promotes gastric cancer progression and is associated with immune infiltration and inflammatory signaling
Authors: Gang Wang, Dalai Xu, Lei Qiu, Feng Lu, Yongchang Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCasein kappa (CSN3) is a milk protein-encoding gene, and its expression pattern and biological significance in gastric cancer (GC) remain unclear, this study aims to explore expression and function of CSN3 in GC.MethodsCSN3 expression was assessed in GC tissues and cell lines. Its biological effects were examined in vitro and in vivo. STRING and AlphaFold 3 were used to explore CSN3-associated proteins. RNA sequencing was performed to investigate mechanisms associated with CSN3. Key molecules and inflammatory cytokines related to NOD/TLR signaling were validated by western blotting and reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). Immune infiltration was analyzed using Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER) and preliminarily validated by immunohistochemistry.ResultsCSN3 expression was elevated in GC and was positively associated with deeper tumor invasion, lymph node metastasis, advanced Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) stage, and poorer prognosis. Knockdown of CSN3 inhibited GC cell proliferation and migration while promoting apoptosis. CSN3 overexpression promoted tumor growth in vivo. Protein-protein interaction analysis identified CALM3 as a potential CSN3-interacting protein. RNA sequencing revealed that CSN3 overexpression was associated with the NOD-like receptor and Toll-like receptor signaling pathways. Western blotting showed increased NOD1, RIP2, and p-p65 levels in CSN3-overexpressing cells. RT-qPCR demonstrated upregulation of interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). Moreover, CSN3 expression was positively correlated with immune infiltration, particularly CD8+ T cells.ConclusionsCSN3 is upregulated in GC and is associated with malignant progression and poor prognosis. CSN3 may promote GC progression partly through modulation of NOD1/RIP2/NF-κB-related inflammatory signaling and remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment. These findings suggest that CSN3 may serve as a potential biomarker in GC.Keywords: Gastric cancer (GC), casein kappa (CSN3), immune cell infiltration, prognosis

Key findings

• High casein kappa (CSN3) expression was associated with aggressive clinicopathological features and poor prognosis in gastric cancer (GC).

• CSN3 promoted GC cell proliferation and migration and inhibited apoptosis.

What is known and what is new?

• GC has a poor prognosis, and reliable biomarkers are still needed.

• This study suggests that CSN3 may act as a tumor-promoting factor in GC and may be related to nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain/Toll-like receptor signaling and immune infiltration.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• CSN3 may be a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for GC, but further validation is needed.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a major global health challenge and is the fifth most common cause of cancer-related death worldwide. Owing to frequent recurrence and poor outcomes in patients with advanced disease, GC continues to impose a substantial public health burden (1). In China, it is estimated that 679,100 GC patients were diagnosed each year and most of them are diagnosed at an advanced stage (2). Although advances in diagnosis and treatment have improved the management of GC, many patients are still diagnosed at advanced stages, resulting in limited therapeutic efficacy and an overall 5-year survival rate of approximately 30% (3). In contrast, the survival rate can approach 90% among patients diagnosed at early or intermediate stages (4). Thus, reliable biomarkers with high sensitivity are needed to improve early diagnosis and enable more accurate prognostic assessment of GC.

Caseins (αS1-, αS2-, β-, and κ-casein) make up about 80% of the protein in milk and are encoded by four related genes: casein alpha s1 (CSN1S1), casein alpha s2 (CSN1S2), casein beta (CSN2), and casein kappa (CSN3) (5,6). The CSNs (the casein family genes) are conserved between plants and mammals. These genes are located in a cluster of around 250 Kb on chromosome 6 in cattle (5). Each of these genes exhibits a high level of polymorphism, which has different effects on dairy cow productivity and the quality of milk (7). Out of these genes, CSN3 is the longest, approximately 13 kb long, consisting of five exons, and its polypeptide chain comprises 169 amino acids (8). The protein coded by the CSN3 gene plays an important role in milk composition and processing properties (9). Recent research has revealed that CSN3 is significant not only nutritionally but also possesses other features. For example, earlier studies showed that CSN3 from milk has molecular chaperone activity and can prevent target protein precipitation (10). Chaperone activity is vital for normal brain functioning and neural cell differentiation (11). In human, protein aggregation and misfolding are linked to many neurodegenerative disorders, such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases (12,13). Various studies have shown that molecular chaperones help prevent protein aggregation and are crucial in preventing these diseases (14,15). A specific study found that CSN3 expression is quickly induced during neural differentiation in P19 cells treated with all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA), indicating that CSN3 is regulated by ATRA during neural differentiation (11).

Recently, evidence regarding the role of CSN3 in human cancers remains limited. In this study, we aimed to systematically investigate the expression pattern, biological function, and potential molecular mechanism of CSN3 in GC. First, CSN3 expression in GC tissues and cell lines was evaluated, and its associations with clinicopathological characteristics and patient prognosis were analyzed to determine its clinical relevance. Next, in vitro and in vivo functional experiments were performed to assess the effects of CSN3 on malignant phenotypes of GC. To further explore the underlying mechanisms, protein–protein interaction network analysis, structural modeling, and RNA sequencing were conducted to identify potential interacting partners and downstream signaling pathways associated with CSN3. In addition, considering the possible involvement of the tumor immune microenvironment and inflammatory signaling, immune cell infiltration analysis and validation of key inflammation-related molecules were further performed. Through this integrated design, we sought to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the role of CSN3 in GC progression. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR and ARRIVE reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1-0141/rc).

Methods

Clinical samples

All participants were recruited from The Second People’s Hospital of Lianyungang between January 2024 and March 2025. A total of 79 GC tissue samples were randomly selected for this study. The patients ranged in age from 34 to 87 years, and the sex of all participants was recorded. Histological evaluation was independently performed by a certified pathologist, and TNM staging was determined according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM Classification System, 7th edition. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second People’s Hospital of Lianyungang (No. 2024K056). The requirement for informed consent was waived because this was a retrospective study, and all patient data were anonymized prior to analysis.

The sample size was determined based on the availability of eligible GC cases during the study period and was consistent with sample sizes used in similar retrospective studies; therefore, no formal power calculation was performed. GC tissue samples were randomly selected from the pathology archives for inclusion. Histological evaluation and pathological scoring were performed in a blinded manner, with the pathologist unaware of the clinical and molecular data. Patients with pathologically confirmed GC and available formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissues were included, whereas those with incomplete clinical information, insufficient tissue material, or poor-quality samples unsuitable for histological or molecular analysis were excluded.

Cell lines and cell culture

The normal gastric epithelial cell line GES-1 (CL-0563), GC cell lines AGS (CL-0022), and 293T (CL-0005) cells were purchased from Procell Life Technologies Ltd., Wuhan, China. GC cell lines SGC-7901 (G6769) and BGC-823 (C6123C) were purchased from Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). All cells were cultured in 90% Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, Shanghai, China) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) under conditions of 37 ℃ and 5% CO2.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

A total of 79 GC tissues and their adjacent non-tumorous tissues were detected by IHC. The paraffin-embedded specimens were cut into 4 µm sections. After being baked at 60 ℃ for 2 hours, the samples were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated using a series of graded alcohols. Then, the antigens were retrieved using 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a microwave oven. Subsequently, the samples were incubated overnight with a primary antibody, CSN3 (Novus Biologicals, CO, USA; NBP2-58781; dilution 1:500), in a humidified container at 4 ℃. Afterwards, the sections were washed in PBS 3 times, incubated with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (Zhongshan Golden Bridge, Beijing, China; ZB-2301; dilution 1:500), and analyzed using immunohistochemical kits (Zhongshan Golden Bridge, Beijing, China), followed by signal development using a diaminobenzidine (DAB, Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China; DA1010) detection system according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunohistochemical staining was evaluated using an Olympus microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The immunohistochemical score is calculated by combining the proportion score (the percentage of positively stained cells) with the staining intensity score. The proportion score ranges from 0 to 4, specifically: 0 (no staining), 1 (1–24%), 2 (25–49%), 3 (50–74%), 4 (more than 75%). The staining intensity is scored as follows: 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), 3 (strong). The proportion score and staining intensity score are then multiplied to obtain the total IHS score for each case. A total score between 4 and 12 is considered high expression.

Immunohistochemical staining for CD20 and CD8

Immunohistochemical staining for CD20 and CD8 was performed on the same paraffin-embedded GC tissues and adjacent non-tumorous tissues using the procedure described above. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies against CD20 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; ab27093; dilution 1:500) and CD8 (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA; MBS684124; dilution 1:500), followed by incubation with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology, Beijing, China; ZB-2301; dilution 1:500). Immunoreactivity was visualized using an immunohistochemical kit (Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology, Beijing, China) and a diaminobenzidine detection system (DAB, Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China; DA1010) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The stained sections were then observed under a light microscope to evaluate the infiltration patterns of CD20+ B cells and CD8+ T cells in normal and tumor tissues.

Cell transfection

For knockdown experiment, GC cells were transfected with CSN3 siRNA (siCSN3) or negative control siRNA (siCtrl) using Lipo2000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China; 11668019). The target sequence for CSN3 was 5’-TGCAATAATGAAGAGTTTTCTTC-3’. For overexpression experiment, the 293T cells were co-transfected with pLVX-CSN3 and the lentiviral packaging systems psPAX2 (Addgene plasmid 12260, Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) and pMD2.G (Addgene plasmid 12259, Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) to generate cell lines that stably overexpress CSN3. After 48 hours of infection, the cells were treated with 2 mg/L puromycin for 2 weeks to eliminate non-infected cells. The knockdown and overexpression of CSN3 were validated by reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from GC cells using the EZ-10 total RNA mini-preps kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China, B610583) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Then, using the reverse transcript kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China; B600005), the total RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA. RT-qPCR was performed with SYBR Green qPCR Kit (Jiangsu Cowin Biotech Co., Ltd., Taizhou, China, CW3008). The primer sequences are as follows: GAPDH-F: 5’-ACAACTTTGGTATCGTGGAAGG-3’, GAPDH-R: 5’-GCCATCACGCCACAGTTTC-3’; CSN3-F: 5’- ATTCCTCAGCGGCAATACCT-3’, CSN3-R: 5’- TGCTCGTGATGATGGACTCT -3’; IL-1β-F, 5’- ATGGCAGAAGTACCTGAGCT -3’ and IL-1β-R, 5’- GAGGTGGAGAGCTTTCAGTT -3’; IL-6-F, 5’- ATCTCAGCCCTGAGAAAGGA -3’ and IL-6-R, 5’- GAGATGAGTTGTCATGTCCT -3’; IL-8-F, 5’- ATTTCTGCAGCTCTGTGTGAAGGTGC -3’ and IL-8-R, 5’- TTGTGGATCCTGGCTAGCAGAC -3’; TNF-α-F, 5’- AGGCAGTCAGATCATCTTCT -3’ and TNF-α-R, 5’- AGATAGATGGGCTCATACCA -3’.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell viability was assessed with the CCK-8 from Beyotime Biotechnology (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, C0038). Transfected cells were planted in 96-well plates, and at designated time intervals (day 0, 1, 2, 3, 4), 10 µL of CCK-8 solution were introduced into each well. The plates were then incubated for 4 hours. Absorbance was recorded at 450 nm to evaluate cell viability.

Apoptosis assay

After transfection, GC cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5×105 cells/mL and incubated overnight to facilitate cell attachment. Subsequently, the cells were treated with trypsin and washed three times with cold PBS. Annexin V-FITC and PI (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, C1065) were used to incubate the cells at room temperature for 15 minutes. Then, 200 µL of 1× binding buffer was added to each well, and apoptosis was detected using a flow cytometer (Becton-Dickinson, USA) and analyzed with FlowJo software (FlowJo, USA).

Transwell assay

The rate of cell migration was investigated using a Transwell chamber (Corning, Shanghai, China; 3422). The 600 µL of DMEM medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber, while the transfected GC cells were suspended in 100 µL of serum-free medium and injected into the upper chamber. The entire procedure was carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Finally, paraformaldehyde was used to fix the cells located on the lower surface of the upper chamber. The cells were analyzed under a microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) after staining with crystal violet (Beijing Solaibao Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China; G4740).

Xenograft model

BALB/c nude male mice (6 weeks old, 18–20 g) were purchased from Jiangsu Huachuang Sino Pharma Tech Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). All animal experiments were conducted in the animal facility of Kangda College of Nanjing Medical University. The animal study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Kangda College of Nanjing Medical University. All procedures were performed in compliance with the Regulations on the Administration of Laboratory Animals issued by the National Science and Technology Commission of China, the Measures for the Administration of Laboratory Animals in Jiangsu Province, and the institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. The mice are athymic immunodeficient mice without genetic modification. All animals were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. BGC-823 cells overexpressing CSN3 or control cells were subcutaneously implanted into the right flank of BALB/c nude mice (injection volume: 50 µL, cell density: 1×106 per mouse) to establish a GC xenograft model (n=5 per group). Mice were randomly allocated to experimental groups, and all animals were handled and measured under identical conditions to minimize potential confounding effects. Tumor dimensions were measured daily using digital calipers for dynamic monitoring of growth curves, with volume calculated strictly by the formula: short diameter2 × long diameter/2 (measurement error controlled within ±0.1 mm). On day 20 post-transplantation, whole-tumor bioluminescence imaging was performed using the IVIS® Lumina III small animal imaging system. On day 21 post-transplantation, tumor-bearing mice underwent CO2 gradient euthanasia (flow rate: 30% chamber volume/min), followed by complete tumor resection and three independent measurements of tumor mass using a balance. Tumor measurements, imaging analysis, and data processing were performed in a blinded manner, with the investigators unaware of the group allocation.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis

PPI analysis was performed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org). Additionally, structural predictions of PPIs were carried out using AlphaFold Multimer (v2.3.2) implemented in ColabFold (v1.5.5) with canonical sequences of CSN3 (UniProt ID: P49368) and candidate interactors (CALM3: P0DP25, COPS5: Q92905) retrieved from UniProtKB (2023_02 release). Multiple sequence alignments were generated with MMseqs2 (v13) using default parameters for multimer complexes, and top-ranked models were selected based on confidence metrics and validated against experimental PPI databases.

RNA-sequence

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed by Novogene (Beijing, China). Control and CSN3 overexpressing BGC-823 cells were collected with TRIzol® Reagent (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China; B610409). The samples were sent to Novogene (Beijing, China) for RNA-seq analysis. Sequencing libraries were constructed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the NEBNext UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina. Library quality was assessed using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Then, gene expression profiles of each group were analyzed using the Illumina Hiseq platform, followed by data analysis of differentially expressed genes. GO functional annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were conducted using the online tool DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/home.jsp). A P value of <0.05 and a gene count of ≥1 were used as screening thresholds. All RNA-seq data have been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE317661.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

Immune cell infiltration was evaluated using TIMER 2.0 (http://timer.cistrome.org/). The relationship between CSN3 and immune cell infiltration was determined using Spearman’s correlation. A correlation coefficient greater than 0.1 denotes a positive correlation, while a coefficient less than -0.1 signifies a negative correlation.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China; P0013B), and the protein concentration was measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China; P0012). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies against NOD1 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; ab189409; dilution 1:1,000), RIP2 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; ab155529; dilution 1:1,000), NF-κB p65 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; ab32536; dilution 1:1,000), phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser536) (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; ab76302; dilution 1:1,000), and GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; #2118; dilution 1:1,000). The membranes were then incubated with HRP-linked anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; #7074; dilution 1:2,000) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by signal detection using an ECL kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China; P0018S).

Kaplan-Meier plotter database

The significance of CSN3 as a prognostic marker in GC was assessed using the Kaplan-Meier plotter, specifically analyzing overall survival (OS), post-progression survival (PPS), and first progression (FP). The data for these analyses were sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://kmplot.com/analysis/).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed continuous variables, comparisons between two groups were conducted using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. The associations between CSN3 expression and clinicopathological characteristics were analyzed using the chi-square test. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All in vitro experiments were independently repeated three times, and the data represent biological replicates.

Results

CSN3 is up-regulated in GC tissues and cell lines

Immunohistochemical analysis was performed to assess CSN3 expression in tumor tissues, normal gastric mucosa, and intestinal metaplasia tissues. As illustrated in Figure 1A, representative images demonstrated positive CSN3 protein expression in GC tissues (tumor), whereas negative expression was observed in normal mucosa or intestinal metaplasia tissues. Notably, lymphocytic infiltration was evident within tumor regions (Figure 1A-1D). Furthermore, quantitative analysis revealed significantly elevated CSN3 mRNA levels in human GC cell lines (AGS, SGC-7901, and BGC-823) compared to the normal gastric epithelial cell line GES-1 (Figure 1E). These findings collectively indicate a tumor-specific upregulation of CSN3 in gastric carcinogenesis.

CSN3 expression is associated with pathological characteristics and survival

The relationship between clinicopathological parameters and CSN3 protein expression in GC patients was further analyzed. The results showed that CSN3 expression was significantly correlated with invasion depth (P=0.004), lymph nodal status (P=0.006) and TNM stage (P=0.01) (Table 1). To determine the impact of CSN3 expression on the prognosis of GC patients, survival analysis was conducted using data from the Kaplan-Meier plotter website. It was found that the expression level of CSN3 was significantly positively correlated with the survival time of GC patients, including OS (P=4.9E−6), PPS (P=2.6E−6), and FP (P=1.1E−6) (Figure 2A-2C). With the increase in CSN3 expression in patients, the survival period significantly shortened. These results suggest that high CSN3 expression indicates poor prognosis and is associated with tumor progression in GC patients.

The roles of CSN3 in GC cells

To assess the impact of CSN3 on cell biological behaviors, in vitro experiments for overexpression and knockdown were performed. GC cells underwent infection with a lentivirus carrying the CSN3 overexpression vector. In contrast, CSN3 expression was reduced in GC cells by transfection with CSN3 siRNA. The effectiveness of both overexpression and knockdown was evaluated using RT-qPCR (Figure S1A,S1B). The CCK-8 proliferation assay showed that, compared to the control group, the cell proliferation ability was significantly enhanced in the CSN3 overexpression group (Figure 3A). Conversely, transfection with CSN3 siRNA significantly knocked down CSN3 expression, inhibiting the proliferation of GC cells (Figure S2A). Further flow cytometry analysis indicated that CSN3 overexpression significantly decreased apoptosis in GC cells (Figure 3B), while CSN3 knockdown significantly promoted apoptosis in these cells (Figure S2B). Transwell assay results indicated that CSN3 overexpression led to enhanced migratory capability in GC cells (Figure 3C). Conversely, CSN3 knockdown inhibited the migration of GC cells (Figure S2C). These data showed that CSN3 inhibited cell apoptosis, and promoted cell proliferation and migration.

Fluorescence living imaging showed the CSN3 overexpressed tumors had a dramatically more vigorous fluorescence intensity than the control tumor (Figure 4A,4B). Biweekly caliper measurements showing accelerated volume increase in CSN3-OE group from day 11 onward (Figure 4C). On day 20, tumors were collected and weighed. Tumor size and weight in the CSN3 overexpression group were significantly larger than those in the oe-Ctrl group (Figure 4D-4G).

CSN3 interacts with CALM3

Physical interaction mapping via the STRING database (http://string-db.org) identified candidate interacting proteins for CSN3. CALM3 was validated as a potential interacting protein (Figure 5A). Structural prediction using AlphaFold-Multimer (ColabFold v1.5.5) suggested a potential interaction between CSN3 and CALM3, with pTM and ipTM scores of 0.44 and 0.38, respectively (Figure S3). Given the relatively low confidence scores, this predicted interaction should be interpreted with caution. Putative interface regions were identified in the CSN3-CALM3 complex, providing preliminary structural information on the possible binding mode. Nevertheless, structural prediction alone could not determine the actual interaction strength, and further biochemical validation is needed. Considering that CALM3 is a calcium-dependent signaling-related protein, this predicted interaction may provide a preliminary clue for subsequent mechanistic investigation.

CSN3 regulates immune- and inflammation-related signaling pathways in GC cells

To further investigate the potential mechanisms by which CSN3 influences the progression of GC, RNA sequencing was conducted on BGC-823 cells overexpressing CSN3 and on control cells. The analysis identified 502 differentially expressed genes, with 76 genes upregulated and 426 downregulated (Figure 5B). KEGG and GO pathway analyses were performed on these genes using the DAVID platform. KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to the immune system, including the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway and Toll-like receptor signaling pathway (Figure 5C). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that, following CSN3 overexpression, there was significant enrichment in responses to viruses, negative regulation of viral genome replication, type I interferon-mediated signaling pathways, defense responses to viruses, and negative regulation of macrophage apoptotic processes (Figure S4). These results suggest that CSN3 may influence the progression of GC by regulating immune-related pathways.

To further validate the RNA-seq findings, we examined key molecules involved in inflammation-related signaling in both CSN3-overexpressing and CSN3-silenced cells. Western blot analysis showed that CSN3 overexpression increased the protein expression levels of nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-containing protein 1 (NOD1), receptor-interacting serine/threonine-protein kinase 2 (RIP2), and p-p65, whereas CSN3 knockdown showed the opposite trend, while total p65 expression did not change significantly (Figure 6A,6B). The original uncropped western blot images are provided in Figure S5A,S5B. In addition, RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that CSN3 overexpression significantly upregulated the mRNA expression levels of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α, while CSN3 silencing led to their downregulation (Figure 6C,6D). These results suggest that CSN3 may influence GC progression partly through modulation of NOD1/RIP2/NF-κB-related inflammatory signaling.

CSN3 related to tumor infiltrating immune cells

The tumor microenvironment of GC contains various immune cells, such as T lymphocytes, B cells, natural killer cells, and tumor-associated macrophages. These immune cells interact through multiple signaling pathways, forming a complex immune network that can both promote and inhibit the occurrence and development of GC (16). By modulating the functions of these cells, it may be possible to enhance anti-tumor immune responses and improve treatment outcomes. KEGG enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes from RNA-seq showed that immune signaling pathways were significantly enriched in the high CSN3 expression group. Therefore, we explored the impact of CSN3 on immune infiltration in GC. Through the TIMER database, we investigated the relationship between CSN3 and different types of immune cells. We found that CSN3 expression levels were positively correlated with the infiltration of B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, macrophages, and neutrophils (Figure 7A). Interestingly, we observed lymphocytes infiltration was frequently occurred in GC tissue with immunohistochemical staining of CSN3 (Figure 1A-1D). We further explored the correlations between CSN3 and immune cell markers in GC through the TIMER database. CSN3 is significantly correlated with B cells, CD8+ T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and exhausted T cells (Table 2). These findings suggest that CSN3 may be an immune-related molecule. We further assessed immune cell infiltration in gastric tissues and found that CD20+ B cells were mainly localized in follicle-like structures of normal tissues, whereas CD8+ T cells were predominantly observed in tumor tissues (Figure 7B,7C). Collectively, these results indicate a potential association between CSN3 and the immune microenvironment in GC. Subsequently, we further investigated whether CSN3 expression affects prognosis in GC patients through immune cell infiltration through KM plotter online database. The results showed that, in patients with high infiltration of CD4+ memory T cells, CD8+ memory T cells, B cells, and mesenchymal stem cells, high CSN3 expression was significantly associated with poor prognosis (Figure 8). This finding suggests that the prognostic significance of CSN3 may depend, at least in part, on the immune microenvironment.

Discussion

GC ranks among the most common cancers worldwide, characterized by poor prognosis and high incidence and mortality rates (1). The challenge of early detection often means that many cases are identified at advanced stages, which contributes to unfavorable outcomes and elevated recurrence rates even after surgery (17). Consequently, discovering new therapeutic targets and biomarkers for GC diagnosis is essential for enhancing patient prognosis.

CSN3, also known as kappa-casein, is a protein primarily recognized as a key component of casein, the predominant protein in milk (18). It is essential for the nutritional and functional properties of milk, contributing significantly to the formation of micelles that facilitate the transport and digestion of calcium and phosphate (19,20). While its traditional role is well-established in dairy science, research has begun to explore CSN3’s broader biological functions, such as its involvement in nervous system development (11). However, its potential role in GC biology has not yet been reported.

Our data indicates that CSN3 is significantly upregulated in GC tissues and cell lines and not expressed in normal mucosa, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for GC diagnosis. Kaplan-Meier plotter database reflected that the high expression of CSN3 led to a significantly poorer prognosis in GC patients. Moreover, it was also found that high CSN3 expression is associated with deeper invasion depth, lymph node metastasis, and higher TNM staging in GC patients. Cell function assays revealed that CSN3 enhances proliferation and migration while inhibiting apoptosis, suggesting that CSN3 influences the progression and metastasis of GC. In vivo assays revealed that overexpressing of CSN3 in GC cells promoted tumor growth.

The CALM3 gene encodes calmodulin 3 (CALM3), a pivotal cellular regulatory factor. Its longest isoform, Calm3L, contains a 3′UTR-retained intron that mediates dendritic mRNA localization in neurons through Staufen2 binding at four intronic clusters, with this localization being specifically enhanced by NMDA receptor activation (21,22). In hypertrophic cardiomyopathy research, analysis of 3,189 patients revealed no direct association between CALM3 and this condition but identified differential expression patterns (23). He et al. reported CALM3 upregulation in hemorrhoidal tissues and downregulation in leukemia (24). An et al. demonstrated that CALM3 cooperates with KIF5C to promote neural commitment during neuronal differentiation of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUC-MSCs) (25). Notably, the role of CALM3 in GC and its association with CSN3 remain unreported. In this study, structural prediction suggested a possible association between CSN3 and CALM3, which may provide an initial clue for understanding the molecular context of CSN3 in GC. Because the confidence of the predicted complex was limited, the current model should be regarded as a preliminary structural hypothesis rather than definitive evidence of a stable protein-protein interaction. However, this finding is still of potential biological interest. CALM3 belongs to the calmodulin family and is involved in calcium-dependent signaling, which has been linked to inflammation-related pathways and GC progression (22,26). Moreover, calcium/calmodulin signaling has been linked to NF-κB activation, whereas NF-κB is a recognized mediator of inflammation-associated GC progression (27,28). Thus, the predicted CSN3-CALM3 interaction raises the possibility that CSN3 may participate in the regulation of inflammatory signaling through a calcium-related mechanism. This interpretation is consistent with our observations on inflammation-associated molecules, although the exact biological significance of this interaction still requires further experimental confirmation.

Next, we conducted RNA sequencing on GC cells with CSN3 overexpression to further explore the mechanisms by which CSN3 contributes to GC progression. KEGG pathway analysis of differentially expressed genes identified two significantly enriched (P<0.05) biological pathways related to the immune system: the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway and the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway. As part of the pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), NOD-like receptors play a critical role in the innate immune response. They are expressed not only in innate immune cells but also in tumor cells, where they are involved in tumor initiation and development (29,30). Suarez et al. found that pre-activation of the NOD1 receptor can significantly reduce gastric epithelial cell damage induced by Helicobacter pylori, thereby reducing the incidence of related cancers (31). However, they also discovered that in Colombian patients with intestinal-type GC, the level of NOD1 is significantly lower in tumor tissues compared to non-tumor tissues (31). The research conducted by Peng Wang et al. found that the genetic polymorphisms of NOD1 and NOD2 may be associated with Helicobacter pylori infection and may play an important role in promoting the development of GC in the Chinese population (32). Therefore, the role and regulatory mechanisms of NOD-like receptors in GC require further investigation. Toll-like receptors (TLRs) also belong to the large family of PRRs. By activating nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB), TLRs play a key role in immune responses, inflammation, and tissue repair, thus regulating the expression levels of several important cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules (33). The stimulation of TLRs is also associated with cellular proliferation and apoptosis (34). Studies have found that different gene polymorphisms of TLRs are associated with GC. It has been confirmed that TLR1 and TLR10 gene polymorphisms are associated with a higher risk of GC in individuals infected with Helicobacter pylori (35). In-depth research into the specific mechanisms of the TLR pathway will aid in the development of new therapeutic strategies for GC. Therefore, we speculate that CSN3 may influence the progression of GC by regulating the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway and the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway. To further validate the RNA-seq findings, we examined the changes in inflammation-related signaling molecules after CSN3 overexpression and knockdown. The results showed that CSN3 overexpression increased the protein levels of NOD1, RIP2, and p-p65, whereas CSN3 knockdown showed the opposite trend, while total p65 expression remained largely unchanged. Meanwhile, CSN3 overexpression significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α, whereas CSN3 silencing suppressed their expression. These results were generally consistent with the transcriptomic enrichment analysis, suggesting that CSN3 may partly contribute to GC progression through modulation of NOD1/RIP2/NF-κB-related inflammatory signaling.

The tumor immune microenvironment is composed of diverse and complex cellular and non-cellular components, and immune infiltration plays a critical role in tumor initiation and progression. Although GC is not generally considered a highly immunogenic tumor, it still contains abundant tumor-infiltrating immune cells that contribute to tumor development and therapeutic responses. For example, infiltrating CD4+ T cells exert antitumor functions not only by supporting the activation of CD8+ T cells but also by promoting the generation and maintenance of memory cytotoxic T-cell responses (36,37). In contrast, neutrophils promote GC progression by weakening T-cell immunity (38). Previous studies have shown that κ-casein can modulate macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory, M2-like phenotype, likely in association with suppression of NF-κB signaling. Consistent with the established role of NF-κB/TLR signaling in macrophage activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production, these findings suggest that CSN3/κ-casein may influence adaptive immune responses indirectly through macrophage reprogramming (39).

In this study, we investigated the association between CSN3 expression and immune infiltration in GC. CSN3 expression was significantly correlated with the infiltration of multiple immune cells and with markers of B cells, CD8+ T cells, TH1 cells, TH2 cells, regulatory T cells, and T-cell exhaustion, suggesting a potential role for CSN3 in immune modulation. High CSN3 expression was also associated with poor prognosis in patients with high infiltration of CD4+ memory T cells, CD8+ memory T cells, B cells, and mesenchymal stem cells. Immunohistochemical analysis further showed that B-cell infiltration was mainly observed in lymphoid follicles of normal tissues, whereas CD8+ T-cell infiltration was more prominent in tumor tissues, indicating altered immune cell composition between normal and tumor tissues. Although increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration is generally considered a favorable prognostic factor in GC, its clinical significance depends not only on cell abundance but also on functional status and spatial distribution (40). Our findings suggest that CSN3-related CD8+ T cells may not be fully effective antitumor cells, but may instead exhibit functional exhaustion or spatial restriction. Therefore, CSN3 may contribute to GC progression partly through shaping a functionally constrained immune microenvironment. Further studies incorporating exhaustion-related markers and spatially resolved analyses are needed for validation.

There are limitations that consist in this article. First, the physical interaction between CSN3 and CALM3 and their downstream signaling pathways require experimental validation. Second, the exploration of how CSN3 modulates immune infiltrates was conducted using public databases, with preliminary support from immunohistochemical analysis. Although these databases provide valuable insights, experimental validation through direct in vivo and in vitro experiments is necessary to confirm these findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study suggests that CSN3 is involved in GC progression. CSN3 was significantly upregulated in GC tissues and cells, and its high expression was associated with adverse clinicopathological features and poor prognosis. Functional experiments showed that CSN3 promoted proliferation and migration, inhibited apoptosis in vitro, and enhanced tumor growth in vivo. RNA sequencing and validation analyses further indicated that CSN3 may partly contribute to GC progression through regulation of NOD1/RIP2/NF-κB-related inflammatory signaling. In addition, immune infiltration analysis and preliminary immunohistochemical validation suggested that CSN3 may be associated with remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment. These findings suggest that CSN3 may serve as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target in GC.
