Adaptive Estimation and Inference in Conditional Moment Models via the Discrepancy Principle Thanks: Vasilis Syrgkanis was supported by NSF Award IIS-2337916.
Jiyuan Tan

Abstract
We study adaptive estimation and inference in ill-posed linear inverse problems defined by conditional moment restrictions. Existing regularized estimators such as Regularized DeepIV (RDIV) require prior knowledge of the smoothness of the nuisance function—typically encoded by a β \beta -source condition—to tune their regularization parameters. In practice, this smoothness is unknown, and misspecified hyperparameters can lead to suboptimal convergence or instability. We introduce a discrepancy-principle-based framework for adaptive hyperparameter selection that automatically balances bias and variance without relying on the unknown smoothness parameter. Our framework applies to both RDIV 29 and the Tikhonov Regularized Adversarial Estimator (TRAE) 4 and achieves the same rates in both weak and strong metrics. Building on this, we construct a fully adaptive doubly robust estimator for linear functionals that attains the optimal rate of the better-conditioned primal or dual problem, providing a practical, theoretically grounded approach for adaptive inference in ill-posed econometric models.

Moreover, CV typically selects the parameter that minimizes the weak metric, which does not directly imply a bound in the strong metric. This paper addresses this gap by introducing a discrepancy-principle framework for adaptive hyperparameter selection that does not require knowing the exact

Our main technical tool is the Discrepancy Principle (DP)

33

from classical inverse problems. DP adaptively selects the regularization parameter

(
18

In this paper, we provide a rigorous theoretical justification showing that our choice of
\lambda
achieves the same rate as previous work without prior information about smoothness. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows.

•

First, we develop a general discrepancy principle for hyperparameter selection in ill-posed conditional moment problems. This framework is not limited to a single estimator and provides a general principle for constructing fully adaptive estimators.

•

Second, we demonstrate the broad applicability and power of this principle by developing and analyzing two specific adaptive estimators: one based on the Regularized DeepIV (RDIV) estimator
29
and the other based on the Tikhonov Regularized Adversarial Estimator (TRAE)
4
. We show that both adaptive estimators match the optimal rates in both strong and weak metrics without relying on knowing the exact source-condition parameter
\beta

•

Third, leveraging these adaptive techniques, we construct a fully adaptive doubly robust (DR) estimator for

•

Fourth, we empirically verify our hyperparameter-tuning method on synthetic data. The experiments show that our method efficiently finds effective regularization parameters for RDIV and TRAE, consistent with our theory.

Preliminary

Problem formulation.
We study estimation of linear functionals with unknown nuisance functions defined by LIPs. More formally, given observations of a random variable
W
, we are interested in estimating

\displaystyle\theta_{0}=\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{m}(W;h_{0})],

with a known linear functional
h\mapsto\widetilde{m}(W;h)
. Let
X
and
Z
be two
W
-measurable random variables, and let
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}
be closed linear subspaces of
L_{2}(X)
and
L_{2}(Z)
, respectively, where
L_{2}(X)
denotes square-integrable functions of
X
. Let
\mathcal{H}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
and
\mathcal{Q}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}
be subsets of these function spaces. In our context,
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{Q}
are common classes in machine learning, such as Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS), neural networks, and parametric families. We define the linear operator
\mathcal{T}h=\Pi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}}\mathbb{E}[h(X)\mid Z=\cdot]
, where
\Pi_{S}
denotes mean-square projection onto the space
S
. The nuisance function
h_{0}
is defined as the minimum-norm solution in
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
to the LIP

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}h_{0}=r_{0},

(2.1)

where
r_{0}
is the Riesz representer of a known linear functional
q\mapsto m(W;q)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[m(W;q)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[r_{0}(Z)q(Z)],\quad\forall q\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}.

We make the following assumption to ensure the existence of solutions.

Assumption 2.1
.

We have
r_{0}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{T}):=\{\mathcal{T}h:h\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}\}
.

Given an estimated solution of (
2.1
)
\widehat{h}
, the strong and weak metrics are defined as

\displaystyle\|\widehat{h}-h_{0}\|_{\text{strong}}=\|\widehat{h}-h_{0}\|_{L_{2}},

\displaystyle\quad\|\widehat{h}-h_{0}\|_{\text{weak}}=\|\mathcal{T}(\widehat{h}-h_{0})\|_{L_{2}}.

Note that the weak metric is the error after applying
\mathcal{T}
. Since the problem is ill-posed, a small weak metric does not necessarily imply a small strong metric. The adjoint operator of
\mathcal{T}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T^{*}}q

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}}\mathbb{E}[q(Z)\mid X=\ \cdotp].

Let
a_{0}
be the Riesz representer of
\widetilde{m}(W;h)
, and let
q_{0}
denote the minimum-norm solution of the following dual inverse problem.

\displaystyle\mathcal{T^{*}}q_{0}

\displaystyle=a_{0}.

Given estimates of
h_{0},q_{0}
, we can construct a DR estimator
4
for
\theta

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\widetilde{m}(W;\widehat{h})+m(W;\widehat{q})-\widehat{q}(Z)\widehat{h}(X)\right].

Linear inverse problems.
In classical LIP, we usually observe only
r_{\delta}
in (
2.1
), which is a noisy measurement of
r_{0}
. It is often assumed that
\|r_{\delta}-r_{0}\|\leqslant\delta
and that both the noise magnitude
\delta
and the operator
\mathcal{T}
are known. Due to noise, solving (
2.1
) directly may be inconsistent when the operator
\mathcal{T}
is ill-posed. To illustrate this point, suppose that the operator
\mathcal{T}
admits a countable singular value decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}h

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{i}\langle h,v_{i}\rangle u_{i},

(2.2)

where
\sigma_{1}\geqslant\sigma_{2}\geqslant\cdots
are singular values,
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
form orthogonal bases of
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}
respectively. The solution of (
2.1
) is

\displaystyle h_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\langle r_{0},u_{i}\rangle}{\sigma_{i}}v_{i}.

A small perturbation of
r_{0}
at
u_{k}
for a small
\sigma_{k}
can result in a large estimation error in the solution. To mitigate the influence of noise, several regularization methods have been introduced in the literature. A standard remedy is to use Tikhonov regularization, i.e.,

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}^{\delta}=\arg\min_{h}

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}h-r_{\delta}\|^{2}+\lambda\|h\|^{2}.

(2.3)

The choice of
\lambda
is crucial for consistency. If
\lambda
is too large, the solution (
2.3
) is biased toward smaller
h
and can have large error. The discrepancy principle is a classical method for selecting the regularization parameter
\lambda
.

Definition 2.2

(Discrepancy Principle)
.

Given constants
l,u\in(1,\infty)
and
k\in(0,\infty)
, the regularization parameter
\lambda
should be chosen as follows:

1.

If
\|r_{\delta}\|\leqslant k\delta
, choose
h=0
, which corresponds to
\lambda=\infty
.

2.

If
\|r_{\delta}\|>k\delta
, choose
\lambda
such that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}h_{\lambda}^{\delta}-r_{\delta}\|

\displaystyle\leqslant k\delta\leqslant\|\mathcal{T}h_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{\delta}-r_{\delta}\|,

for some
\lambda^{\prime}\in[\lambda,l\lambda]
.

The first rule applies when the noise level is too large, and thus there is no way to beat the default estimator
h=0
. The intuition for the second rule is that, given a noise level
\delta
, the reconstruction error should be on the same order as the noise,
O(\delta)
, since this is the best error we can achieve in the presence of noise. It is well known that the discrepancy principle provides an a priori way to select the hyperparameter and achieve the optimal convergence rate.

Our problem is much harder than the classical LIP setting in the sense that both
\mathcal{T}
and
r_{0}
are unknown. We need to estimate
\mathcal{T}
to solve the inverse problem. Suppose that the estimated operator is
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
, and denote
\epsilon_{n}=(\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{n}-\mathcal{T})h_{0}
; then we have
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{n}h_{0}=r_{0}+\epsilon_{n}
, which reduces our problem to the standard setting. However, for adversarial estimators, we do not explicitly estimate
\mathcal{T}
; instead, we transform the problem into a minimax problem and implicitly estimate the operator
\mathcal{T}
, which poses additional challenges for analysis.

To analyze Tikhonov regularization, we introduce the source condition, a standard assumption in the inverse-problem literature.

Assumption 2.3

(Source Condition)

Problem (
2.1
) satisfies the

-source condition for some

such that the minimum-norm solution of (

If

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\langle h_{0},v_{i}\rangle^{2}}{\sigma^{2\beta}_{i}}<\infty,

meaning that the minimum-norm solution has some degree of smoothness. This assumption is widely used in previous work

13

29
;
3

3
Discrepancy-Principle-Based Adaptive Regularization

In this section, we introduce our adaptive approach for solving the conditional moment problem (
1.1
). We begin with the RDIV estimator, which provides an explicit and intuitive formulation of the conditional expectation operator. This serves as a motivating example for applying the Discrepancy Principle (DP) in the context of data-driven hyperparameter tuning. We then generalize this idea to a broader class of estimators, including the TRAE, which implicitly estimates the conditional operator through an adversarial minimax formulation and achieves the best sample complexity in the literature. Finally, leveraging these results and insights from
4
, we further develop an adaptive doubly robust estimator that inherits optimal statistical efficiency while automatically adjusting to the degree of well-posedness of both the primal and dual problems.

3.1
Adaptive Regularized DeepIV Estimator

In the IV setting, the goal is to solve the conditional moment problem
\mathbb{E}[h_{0}(X)\mid Z=z]=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid Z]
. We set the linear moment
m(W;q)=Yq(Z)
and the Riesz representer of
m
is
r_{0}(z)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid Z=z]
. As mentioned in the Introduction, in (
1.1
), the conditional expectation operator
\mathcal{T}=\Pi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}}\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid Z=z]
is unknown, which makes the equation difficult to solve directly. The RDIV method explicitly estimates the conditional operator
\mathcal{T}
by solving

\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{n}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{E}_{n}[\log g(X\mid Z)],

where
\mathcal{G}\subset\{g:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\int_{\mathcal{X}}g(x\mid z)\mu(dx)=1,\ \forall z\in\mathcal{Z}\}
is a function space. We define
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}f(Z)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{g}_{n}(X\mid Z)}[f(X)]
and let

\displaystyle L_{n}(h)=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[(Y-(\widehat{\mathcal{T}}h)(Z))^{2}\right],

\displaystyle\ L(h)=\mathbb{E}\left[(Y-(\mathcal{T}h)(Z))^{2}\right].

The RDIV method then plugs in
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
and solves

\displaystyle\widehat{h}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}L_{n}(h)+\lambda\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[h(X)^{2}\right].

For consistency, we use empirical penalties (e.g.,
\mathbb{E}_{n}[h(X)^{2}]
) in the optimization objectives and population norms (e.g.,
\|h\|^{2}
) in theoretical bounds.

As shown in
29

\beta
(see Assumption
2.3

\lambda=O\!\left(\delta_{n}^{\frac{\min\{\beta,1\}}{1+\min\{\beta,1\}}}\right)
yields a
O\!\left(\delta_{n}^{\frac{\min\{\beta,1\}}{1+\min\{\beta,1\}}}\right)
convergence rate. However, this "oracle" choice is impractical, as the smoothness parameter
\beta
is rarely known in advance. This motivates the central challenge: to design a procedure that selects
\lambda

\beta

To address this challenge, we turn to the
Discrepancy Principle
(
Definition

\displaystyle L_{n}(\widehat{h}_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leqslant\delta\leqslant L_{n}(\widehat{h}_{\lambda^{\prime}})

The intuition behind (
3.1

\delta
as a data-driven calibration target. Using a standard concentration inequality
42

\displaystyle|L_{n}(h)-L(h)|

\displaystyle\leqslant O(\delta),

for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
. Since statistical error from finite samples is inevitable, the population loss will, in the worst case, be at least on the order of

\delta

This principle is formalized by two inequalities. The left-hand condition,
L_{n}(\widehat{h}_{\lambda})\leqslant\delta

3.2
Adaptive Tikhonov Regularized Adversarial Estimator

The RDIV estimator, although simple, achieves only a suboptimal convergence rate. By contrast, TRAE solves the conditional moment problem by transforming it into a minimax optimization problem and thus solving the LIP implicitly. In the following, we consider general linear moment functional
m(W;f)
. In TRAE, the empirical loss is defined as

\displaystyle L_{n}(h)

\displaystyle=\max_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[2m(W;f)-2h(X)f(Z)-f(Z)^{2}\right],

where the function class
\mathcal{F}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}
. Similarly, the population loss is defined as

\displaystyle L(h)

\displaystyle=\max_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\left[2m(W;f)-2h(X)f(Z)-f(Z)^{2}\right].

(3.6)

The TRAE estimator is the solution to the penalized empirical risk minimization problem:

\displaystyle\widehat{h}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}L_{n}(h)+\lambda\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[h(X)^{2}\right].

To ensure the population loss
L(h)
corresponds to the mean-squared error of the conditional expectation, we rely on the following assumption.

Assumption 3.9
.

(Closeness) For all
h\in\mathcal{H}
,
\mathbb{E}[h_{0}(X)-h(X)\mid Z=\cdotp]\in\mathcal{F}
and
\frac{5}{4}\mathbb{E}[h_{0}(X)-h(X)\mid Z=\cdotp]\in\mathcal{F}
.

Notice that
3.9
is slightly stronger than the corresponding assumption in
4
. We require this stronger condition for technical reasons in the subsequent analysis. Under
3.9
, the population loss simplifies to the desired squared error:

\displaystyle L(h)

\displaystyle=\max_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\left[2(r_{0}(Z)-h(X))f(Z)-f(Z)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\max_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\left[-(f(Z)-(r_{0}(Z)-\mathbb{E}[h(X)\mid Z]))^{2}+(r_{0}(Z)-\mathbb{E}[h(X)\mid Z])^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbb{E}[h(X)\mid Z]-r_{0}(Z))^{2}\right]=\|\mathcal{T}(h-h_{0})\|^{2}.

To analyze the sample complexity of this estimator, we also need the following assumptions.

Assumption 3.10

(Mean-squared continuity) For all

,

(Critical radius) Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\cdotp\mathcal{F}=\left\{(s,z)\rightarrow h(x)f(z):h\in\mathcal{H},f\in\mathcal{F}\right\}

3.3
Application for Double Robust Estimation

Using the same method as in Section
3.2
, we can solve the dual problem:

\displaystyle\theta=\mathbb{E}[m(W;q_{0})],\quad\Pi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}}\mathbb{E}[q_{0}(Z)\mid X=x]=a_{0}(X),

where
a_{0}
is the Riesz representer of the linear functional
h(X)\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{m}(W;h)\right]
and
\Pi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}}
is the projection operator. We similarly define the empirical loss

\displaystyle L_{n}^{\text{dual}}(q)

\displaystyle=\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[2\widetilde{m}(W;s)-2q(Z)s(X)-s(X)^{2}\right]

and the adversarial Tikhonov regularization estimator,

\displaystyle\widehat{q}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}L_{n}^{\text{dual}}(q)+\lambda_{\text{dp}}^{\text{dual}}\|q\|^{2},

where
\lambda_{\text{dp}}^{\text{dual}}
is selected using the discrepancy principle. Given estimates of
h_{0}
and
q_{0}
from the adaptive Tikhonov method, we define the DR estimator of
\theta
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}(h,q)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\widetilde{m}(W;h)+m(W;q)-h(X)q(Z)\right].

The following corollary establishes the asymptotic normality of our estimator.

Corollary 3.14
.

Suppose that the assumptions of
Theorem

3.13
hold for both
h_{0},q_{0}
with (potentially different) source conditions
\beta_{h},\beta_{q}>0
, and that
\widetilde{m}
satisfies the mean-squared-continuity property, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widetilde{m}(W;h_{1})-\widetilde{m}(W;h_{2})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=O\left(\|h_{1}-h_{2}\|^{\gamma_{0}}\right)

for some
\gamma_{0}>0
. Also let
\beta_{m}=\max\{\beta_{h},\beta_{q}\}
and assume that
\delta_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\delta_{n}

\displaystyle=o\left(n^{-\alpha}\right),\alpha:=\frac{1+\min\{\beta_{m},1\}}{2+4\min\{\beta_{m},1\}}.

Let
\widehat{h},\widehat{q}
be the adaptive adversarial Tikhonov estimators of the minimum-norm solutions
h_{0},q_{0}
using separate samples. Then,

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}(\widehat{h},\widehat{q})-\theta_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{0}(W)+o_{p}(1),

where
\rho_{0}(W)=\widetilde{m}(W;h_{0})+m(W;q_{0})-q_{0}(Z)h_{0}(X)-\theta_{0}
.

Note that this corollary does not assume we know the smoothness parameters
\beta_{h},\beta_{q}
. The algorithm automatically adjusts to the smoothness of the primal and dual problems and achieves the convergence rate of the more well-posed problem. If
\beta_{m}>1
, it is sufficient to have

, which allows some nonparametric classes. When
\beta_{m}
is close to

\delta_{n}=n^{-1/2}
, which implies a parametric class.

5
Conclusion

This paper develops a unified, discrepancy-principle–based framework for adaptive hyperparameter selection in ill-posed conditional moment problems. Classical approaches to nonparametric IV estimation critically on a priori knowledge of smoothness parameters—such as
\beta
-source conditions—to tune regularization and achieve optimal rates. In contrast, our method provides a fully data-driven mechanism that selects the regularization level to balance bias and variance without having the exact smoothness parameter.

We demonstrate that this principle applies broadly across modern estimators. For both RDIV and TRAE, our adaptive rule matches the optimal strong- and weak-metric rates previously available only with oracle tuning. Finally, building on these results, we construct an adaptive doubly robust estimator for linear functionals that automatically adjusts to the degree of well-posedness of the primal and dual inverse problems, attaining the rate of the more favorable problem without knowing which one is better conditioned. Our empirical study on the proxy negative-control example confirms that the discrepancy principle yields stable and competitive performance across sample sizes. More broadly, our results highlight that classical ideas from inverse problems can be successfully extended to modern machine-learning-based estimators, offering principled, computationally efficient, and theoretically grounded methods for adaptive regularization.

\displaystyle=R_{n}^{\lambda_{2}}(\widehat{h}_{\lambda_{2}})-R_{n}^{\lambda_{2}}\left(h_{\lambda_{2}}^{*}\right)+\left(R^{\lambda_{2}}(\widehat{h}_{\lambda_{2}})-R^{\lambda_{2}}\left(h_{\lambda_{2}}^{*}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\left(R_{n}^{\lambda_{2}}(\widehat{h}_{\lambda_{2}})-R_{n}^{\lambda_{2}}\left(h_{\lambda_{2}}^{*}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(R^{\lambda_{2}}(\widehat{h}_{\lambda_{2}})-R^{\lambda_{2}}\left(h_{\lambda_{2}}^{*}\right)\right)-\left(R_{n}^{\lambda_{2}}(\widehat{h}_{\lambda_{2}})-R_{n}^{\lambda_{2}}\left(h_{\lambda_{2}}^{*}\right)\right),

where we use
\widehat{h}_{\lambda_{2}}=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}R_{n}^{\lambda_{2}}(h)
in the second inequality. By
[
4
, Lemma 3]
, with probability at least
1-\xi
,

\displaystyle R^{\lambda_{2}}(\widehat{h}_{\lambda_{2}})-R^{\lambda_{2}}\left(h_{\lambda_{2}}^{*}\right)-\left(R_{n}^{\lambda_{2}}(\widehat{h}_{\lambda_{2}})-R_{n}^{\lambda_{2}}\left(h_{\lambda_{2}}^{*}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant O(\delta_{n}).

Thus,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}\left(\widehat{h}_{\lambda_{2}}-h_{\lambda_{2}}^{*}\right)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant O(\delta_{n}).

Combining with (
B.4
),
Lemma

B.1
and
Lemma

B.3
, we get

\displaystyle L_{n}(\widehat{h}_{\lambda_{2}})\geqslant\frac{1}{2}L(\widehat{h}_{\lambda_{2}})-O(\delta_{n})

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{4}L\left(h_{\lambda_{2}}^{*}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{T}\left(\widehat{h}_{\lambda_{2}}-h_{\lambda_{2}}^{*}\right)\|^{2}-O(\delta_{n})\geqslant\Omega(1)-O(\delta_{n}).

Therefore, for sufficiently large
n
, we have

\displaystyle L_{n}(\widehat{h}_{\lambda_{2}})

\displaystyle\geqslant\Omega(1)-O(\delta_{n})\geqslant c_{d}\delta_{n}^{2}.

Let
k^{*}=\min\{k\in\mathbb{N}:{L}_{n}(\widehat{h}_{2\rho^{k}})\leqslant c_{d}\delta_{n}^{2}\}\leqslant O(\log(n))
. By the previous argument, we have
0<k^{*}<\infty
. Therefore, with probability at least
1-\xi
,

\displaystyle{L}_{n}(\widehat{h}_{2\rho^{k^{*}}})\leqslant c_{d}\delta_{n}^{2}\leqslant{L}_{n}(\widehat{h}_{2\rho^{k^{*}-1}}).

∎

For the proof of
Theorem

3.13
, note that by
Lemma

B.5
, without further assumptions, we can only obtain the suboptimal upper bound
{\lambda_{\text{dp}}}\leqslant\delta_{n}
. However, if we use the analysis in
4
with this upper bound, we still obtain a suboptimal rate. A key observation is that although the bound on

is loose, the bound on

Additional Experiment Details

We adopt the proxy negative-control design used in the Regularized DeepIV experiments, implemented as follows.
Fix dimensions
d_{s},d_{q},d_{w}\in\mathbb{N}
and structural parameters

\mu_{0},\kappa_{0},\kappa_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}},\qquad\mu_{s},\kappa_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}\times d_{w}},\qquad\Gamma_{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}\times d_{w}},

together with covariance matrices

\Sigma_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}\times d_{w}},\quad\Sigma_{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}\times d_{w}},\quad\Sigma_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{q}\times d_{q}}.

Let

\sigma(x)=1/(1+e^{-x})
the logistic link.
For each

we generate:

\displaystyle S_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\!\big(0,\,0.5\,I_{d_{s}}\big),

\displaystyle A_{i}\mid S_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}\!\left(\sigma\!\left(0.125-0.125\,\mathbf{1}_{d_{s}}^{\top}S_{i}^{\prime}\right)\right),

and independent noises

\varepsilon_{u,i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{u}),\quad\varepsilon_{w,i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{w}),\quad\varepsilon_{q,i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{q}),\quad\varepsilon_{y,i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

The latent confounder
U_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}}
, the proxy for
(A_{i},U_{i})
denoted
Q_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{q}}
, and the proxy for
U_{i}
denoted
W_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}}
are generated by

\displaystyle U_{i}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{s}^{\top}S_{i}^{\prime}+\kappa_{a}A_{i}+\varepsilon_{u,i},

\displaystyle Q_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0.2\,\mathbf{1}_{d_{q}}+B_{q}^{\top}S_{i}^{\prime}+\mathbf{1}_{d_{q}}A_{i}+C_{q}^{\top}U_{i}+\varepsilon_{q,i},

\displaystyle W_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}+\mu_{s}^{\top}S_{i}^{\prime}+\Gamma_{w}U_{i}+\varepsilon_{w,i},

where
B_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}\times d_{q}}
and
C_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}\times d_{q}}
are fixed loading matrices (sampled once at the start of the experiment).
The outcome is

Y_{i}=A_{i}+\mathbf{1}_{d_{s}}^{\top}S_{i}^{\prime}+\mathbf{1}_{d_{w}}^{\top}U_{i}+\mathbf{1}_{d_{w}}^{\top}W_{i}^{\prime}+\varepsilon_{y,i}.

The observed proxies are nonlinear transformations of the latent variables:

S_{i}=g(S_{i}^{\prime}),\qquad Q_{i}=g(Q_{i}^{\prime}),\qquad W_{i}=g(W_{i}^{\prime}),

where

is applied componentwise; in our experiments we fix

All models are implemented in PyTorch and trained with the Adam optimizer using full-batch updates. For RDIV we follow the two-stage architecture in
22
,
29
. Stage 1 estimates

via a Mixture Density Network with two fully connected hidden layers of width
64

20

10^{-3}

h

10^{-3}

Monte Carlo samples from the learned MDN for each instrument value and average
h
over these samples. In the fixed-
\lambda

300

100

\lambda

Appendix D
Auxiliary Lemmas

Lemma D.1
.

Under
2.3
and
3.1
, given
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in(0,2]
, we have

\displaystyle\|h_{\lambda}^{*}-h_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{*}\|

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{h}\|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}\|^{\gamma},

where
\gamma=\min\{\beta/2,1\}
and the constant
c_{h}
only depends on
h_{0}
.

Proof.

Suppose that the compact linear operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
admits a countable singular value decomposition
\displaystyle\{\sigma_{i},v_{i},u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
with
\displaystyle\sigma_{1}\geqslant\sigma_{2}\geqslant\cdots
and

\displaystyle h_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}v_{i}.

We have the following closed-form solution for
\displaystyle h_{\lambda}^{*}
.

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda}a_{i}v_{i}.

Write

h_{\lambda}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}s_{i}(\lambda)\,a_{i}v_{i},\qquad s_{i}(\lambda):=\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda}.

Then for
\lambda>0
,

s_{i}^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\Bigl(\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda}\Bigr)=-\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{(\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda)^{2}}.

Thus

\partial_{\lambda}h_{\lambda}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}s_{i}^{\prime}(\lambda)\,a_{i}v_{i}=-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{(\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda)^{2}}\,a_{i}v_{i}.

By orthonormality of
\{v_{i}\}
,

\|\partial_{\lambda}h_{\lambda}^{*}\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{(\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda)^{2}}\Bigr)^{2}a_{i}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{i}^{4}}{(\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda)^{4}}\,a_{i}^{2}.

Case 1:
0<\beta\leqslant 2
.

Rewrite

\|\partial_{\lambda}h_{\lambda}^{*}\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2\beta}}\,\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2\beta+4}}{(\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda)^{4}}.

Set
s=\sigma_{i}^{2}>0
and define

\psi_{\beta}(s,\lambda):=\frac{s^{\beta+2}}{(s+\lambda)^{4}},\qquad s>0,\ \lambda>0.

Let
s=\lambda t
; then

\psi_{\beta}(s,\lambda)=\lambda^{\beta-2}\,\phi_{\beta}(t),\qquad\phi_{\beta}(t):=\frac{t^{\beta+2}}{(1+t)^{4}},\quad t>0.

For
0<\beta\leqslant 2
, one checks that
\sup_{t>0}\phi_{\beta}(t)\leqslant 1
:
if
0<t\leqslant 1
, then
\phi_{\beta}(t)\leqslant t^{\beta+2}\leqslant 1
; if
t\geqslant 1
, then

\phi_{\beta}(t)\leqslant t^{\beta+2}/t^{4}=t^{\beta-2}\leqslant 1
since
\beta\leqslant 2
.
Hence

\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2\beta+4}}{(\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda)^{4}}=\psi_{\beta}(\sigma_{i}^{2},\lambda)\leqslant\lambda^{\beta-2}.

Therefore,

\|\partial_{\lambda}h_{\lambda}^{*}\|^{2}\leqslant\lambda^{\beta-2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2\beta}},

and thus

\|\partial_{\lambda}h_{\lambda}^{*}\|\leqslant C_{0}\,\lambda^{\beta/2-1},\qquad C_{0}:=\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2\beta}}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

Let
0<\lambda^{\prime}<\lambda
. Then

h_{\lambda}^{*}-h_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{*}=\int_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\partial_{\tau}h_{\tau}^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

so

\|h_{\lambda}^{*}-h_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{*}\|\leqslant\int_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\|\partial_{\tau}h_{\tau}^{*}\|\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leqslant C_{0}\int_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\tau^{\beta/2-1}\,\mathrm{d}\tau=\frac{2C_{0}}{\beta}\Bigl(\lambda^{\beta/2}-(\lambda^{\prime})^{\beta/2}\Bigr).

For
0<\alpha\leqslant 1
and
x,y\geq 0
we have
|x^{\alpha}-y^{\alpha}|\leqslant|x-y|^{\alpha}
. Taking
\alpha=\beta/2\in(0,1]
, we obtain

\|h_{\lambda}^{*}-h_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{*}\|\leqslant\frac{2C_{0}}{\beta}\,|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|^{\beta/2}.

Symmetry in
(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})
gives the stated bound for all
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}>0

with exponent
\gamma=\beta/2
.

Case 2:
\beta>2
.

We now exploit a simpler bound on the derivative. Observe that

|s_{i}^{\prime}(\lambda)|=\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{(\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda)^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{4}}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}},

since
\lambda>0
. Thus

\|h_{\lambda}^{*}-h_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{*}\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(s_{i}(\lambda)-s_{i}(\lambda^{\prime}))^{2}a_{i}^{2}\leqslant|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sup_{\tau\in[\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}]}(s_{i}^{\prime}(\tau))^{2}a_{i}^{2}\leqslant|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{4}}.

Hence

\|h_{\lambda}^{*}-h_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{*}\|\leqslant\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{4}}\Bigr)^{1/2}\,|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|.

It remains to see that
\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{i}^{4}<\infty
follows from the
source condition with
\beta>2
. Since
\sup_{i}\sigma_{i}\leqslant 1
, we have

\sigma_{i}^{2\beta-4}\leqslant 1
for all
i
, and

\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{4}}=\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2\beta}}\,\sigma_{i}^{2\beta-4}\leqslant\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2\beta}}.

Therefore

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{4}}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2\beta}}<\infty.

Thus, the Lipschitz bound with constant

\widetilde{C}=\bigl(\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{i}^{4}\bigr)^{1/2}
holds, which
corresponds to exponent
\gamma=1
.

Combining the two cases, the lemma follows.

∎

Lemma D.2

,

Lemma D.3
.

Suppose that
h_{0}\neq 0
. Then the following lower bound holds:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}\left(h_{\lambda}^{*}-h_{0}\right)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant c_{0}\lambda^{2},

for all
\lambda\in(0,2)
, where
c_{0}>0
is a constant that only depends on
h_{0}
.

Proof.

Suppose that the compact linear operator
\mathcal{T}
admits a countable singular value decomposition
\{\sigma_{i},v_{i},u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
with
\sigma_{1}\geqslant\sigma_{2}\geqslant\cdots
and

\displaystyle h_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}v_{i}.

We have the following closed-form solution for
h_{\lambda}^{*}
.

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda}a_{i}v_{i}.

Let
i^{*}=\arg\min_{i}\{i:\sigma_{i}\neq 0,a_{i}\neq 0\}
. Since
h_{0}\neq 0
and it is the minimum-norm solution,
h_{0}\notin\text{Null}(\mathcal{T})
,
i^{*}<\infty
.

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}\left(h_{\lambda}^{*}-h_{0}\right)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}I(\sigma_{i}\neq 0)\frac{a_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}\lambda^{2}}{\left(\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}I(\sigma_{i}\neq 0)\frac{a_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}\lambda^{2}}{\left(\sigma_{i}^{2}+2\right)^{2}}=:c_{0}\lambda^{2}.

By the choice of
i^{*}
, at least one term in the sum is strictly positive, so
c_{0}>0
and it depends only on
h_{0}
.
For non-compact operators, note that
\mathcal{T}h=\mathbb{E}[h(X)\mid Z]
is a bounded linear operator (
\|\mathcal{T}\|\leqslant 1
). One can prove the lower bound similarly using spectral measure.

∎
