Perturbation Analysis for Preconditioned Normal Equations in Mixed Precision
James E. Garrison, Ilse C.F. Ipsen

Abstract
For real matrices of full column-rank, we analyze the conditioning of several types of normal equations that are preconditioned by a randomized preconditioner computed in lower precision. These include symmetrically preconditioned normal equations, half-preconditioned normal equations, seminormal equations and not-normal equations. Our perturbation bounds are realistic and informative, and suggest that the conditioning depends only mildly on the quality of the preconditioner; however, it does depend on the size of the least squares residual – even if the normal equations do not originate from a least squares problem. We illustrate that a randomized preconditioner can deliver a solution accuracy comparable to that of Matlab’s mldivide command, is efficient in practice, and well-suited to GPU implementations. For the computation of the preconditioner, we propose an automatic selection of the precision, based on a fast condition number estimation in lower precision.

Introduction

Given a matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
with
\rank(\boldsymbol{A})=n
, and
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we consider
the conditioning of the normal equations

\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{b}.

(1)

Although normal equations represent a simple and effective solution of
least squares problems

\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}},

(2)

they can be illconditioned and are not recommended in many applications
[
9
, Section 5.3.7]
.
For instance, once the condition number of
\boldsymbol{A}
with respect to left inversion exceeds
10^{7}
,
then
\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}
is numerically singular in IEEE double precision.
Hence, for the normal equations to be well conditioned and safe to use, the condition number of
\boldsymbol{A}
must be small,
ideally close to 1.

We analyze two preconditioning approaches from
[
12
]

for improving the conditioning of the normal equations, but now with the preconditioner computed in lower precision.

Preconditioned Normal Equations (PNE).

Precondition the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
with a randomized preconditioner
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}
so that the preconditioned matrix

\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\equiv\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}
is well conditioned
with high probability. Then solve the preconditioned normal equations and recover the original solution,

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\boldsymbol{y}&=\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{b}\\
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\boldsymbol{x}&=\boldsymbol{y}.\end{split}

(3)

Half Preconditioned Normal Equations (HPNE).

Dispense with the triangular system solution by preconditioning only the left instances of
\boldsymbol{A}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{b}.

(4)

Since
\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}
is non-symmetric, the linear system
has to be solved by an LU factorization
with partial pivoting, a QR factorization, or an iterative solver.
The HPNE (
4
) represent a special case of the not-normal equations
[
21
]
,

\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{b},

where
\boldsymbol{B}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
is a full column rank matrix with the same column space as
\boldsymbol{A}
.

The perturbation analysis in
[
12
]
suggests that PNE and HPNE, with an effective preconditioner so that
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\lesssim 10
, are as well conditioned as
\boldsymbol{A}
, and
their solution can be almost as accurate as that from the Matlab backslash (
\verb|mldivide|
) command. However, the bounds
from
[
12
]

are not informative if the preconditioner is computed in a lower precision. In contrast, our new bounds for PNE and HPNE in Sections
3
and
4
are realistic, as illustrated in Figure
1
.

Figure 1
:
Relative errors
\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|/\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|
in the computed solutions
\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}

and perturbation bounds versus logarithm of relative

\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|/(\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|)
for

.

)
of

, Theorem 3.1]
(green circles); and our new bound (
35
) (black x’s).

1.2
Notation

For a matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
with

\rank(\boldsymbol{A})=n
, the Moore-Penrose inverse is
\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\equiv(\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{T}
, and
the two-norm condition number with respect to left inversion is

\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\equiv\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\|
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean two-norm.

To put subsequent bounds in context, we review perturbation bounds for least squares problems (
2
), and the normal equations (
1
).

Lemma 1
(Fact 5.14 in
[
11
]
).

Let
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
with
\rank(\boldsymbol{A})=\rank(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E})=n
and

\epsilon_{A}\equiv\|\boldsymbol{E}\|/\|\boldsymbol{A}\|
. Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{*}
be the solution
to
\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|}
and
\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
the solution to
\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}{\|(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E})\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|}
.
Then

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\,\epsilon_{A}\,\left(1+\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\frac{\|\boldsymbol{b}-(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E})\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}\right).

We do not consider perturbations in the right hand side
\boldsymbol{b}
, because perturbations in
\boldsymbol{A}
tend to be more influential on the sensitivity of the least squares problems.

The following bound for the normal equations makes no assumptions on the perturbation
\boldsymbol{E}
, so that
\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E}
can be rank deficient.

Lemma 2
(Lemma A.1 in
[
12
]
).

Let
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
with
\rank(\boldsymbol{A})=n
;

\epsilon_{A}\equiv\|\boldsymbol{A}\|/\|\boldsymbol{E}\|
; and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{*}=\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{b},\qquad(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E})^{T}(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E})\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}=(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E})^{T}\boldsymbol{b}.

If
\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
then

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})^{2}\epsilon_{A}\>\left(\frac{\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}{\|\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}+1+\epsilon_{A}\right).

Lemma
2
suggests that the accuracy
of the normal equations only depends on the least squares residual when it is too large,

\frac{\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}>1
.

2
Existing Work

The use of normal equations is often discouraged due to the ’matrix squaring problem,’ where

\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})=\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})^{2}
, leading to a potentially much worse conditioned linear system. Preconditioning the normal equations is not obvious, as effective preconditioners for
\boldsymbol{A}
do not necessarily make effective preconditioners for
\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}

[
20
]
.

Nonsymmetric systems
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{b}
can be solved
by applying preconditioned iterative methods such as CGNE to the normal equations

[
9
, Section 11.3.9.]
,
[
17
]
. In
[
14
]
, CGNE preconditioners are constructed for
linear systems that arise from certain PDE discretizations.
In
[
21
]
, the iterative solution of the not-normal equations
\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{b}
is proposed, where
\boldsymbol{B}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
has the same column space of
\boldsymbol{A}
and can be obtained from an LU factorization of
\boldsymbol{A}
.

The iterative solution of preconditioned normal equations with iterative refinement via backward stable algorithms is
presented in
[
7
,
22
]
. The corresponding
FOSSILS/SPIR and SIRR algorithms have an operation count of
\mathcal{O}(mn+n^{3})
.
An alternative use of iterative refinement for least squares problems is to iteratively refine the seminormal equations
\boldsymbol{R}^{T}\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{b}

[
4
]
, where it is observed
that the seminormal equations, when combined with iterative refinement, are not sensitive to the size of the relative least squares residual.

Although algorithms like FOSSILS and SIRR are numerically stable, the cost of computing the preconditioner remains an issue. Mixed-precision approaches can reduce this cost. For linear systems, Higham and Pranesh
[
10
]
propose
Cholesky factorizations in lower precision as preconditioners for iterative refinement and extend this to the solution of least squares problems via normal equations. Similarly, Scott and Tůma
[
19
]
compute incomplete Cholesky factors in lower precision to precondition LSQR. Li
[
15
]
also investigates mixed-precision within the LSQR algorithm for solving discrete linear ill-posed problems via regularized least squares.

Beyond low-precision factorizations, many randomized sketching techniques are inherently parallelizable and can benefit from GPU acceleration. Chen et. al.
[
6
]
give a thorough benchmarking of a GPU-based sketch-and-precondition solver based on a sparse sign embedding. Carson and Daužickaitė
[
5
]
compute a mixed-precision sketch as the preconditioner for GMRES with iterative refinement.

We extend an existing PNE perturbation bound to mixed precision (Section

) and then present an improvement (Section

).

3.1
Extension of an existing PNE perturbation bound to mixed precision

A previous bound
[
12
, Theorem 2.1]

shows that the conditioning of the PNE depends on the least squares residual of the original least squares problem.
Theorem
3
below is a small extension where
the preconditioner can be computed in a different precision.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
have
\rank(\boldsymbol{A})=n
;
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be nonsingular;
\boldsymbol{E}_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
,
\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
;

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\equiv\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{R}_{s}+\boldsymbol{E}_{s})^{-1},\qquad\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\equiv\boldsymbol{A}_{p}+\boldsymbol{E}_{p},\qquad\epsilon_{s}\equiv\frac{\|\boldsymbol{E}_{s}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\|},\qquad\epsilon_{p}\equiv\frac{\|\boldsymbol{E}_{p}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|};

and
\|\boldsymbol{E}_{s}\|\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}\|<1
. The computed solutions corresponding
to (
3
) are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{b}

(5)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}.

(6)

If
\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}_{*}-\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\,\nu\,\left(\epsilon_{p}+\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\,\eta_{s}\left(\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}-\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}\|}+\epsilon_{p}\right)\right),

where

\displaystyle\nu\equiv\frac{\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}\leq 1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\eta_{s}\equiv\frac{\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\epsilon_{s}}{1-\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\,\epsilon_{s}}.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to that of
[
12
, Theorem 2.1]
, but omits
the simplification
\epsilon\equiv\max\{\epsilon_{p},\epsilon_{s}\}
.

If the perturbation
\epsilon_{s}
in the preconditioner is large compared to
\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})^{-1}
, then the bound in Theorem
3
can be dominated by the least squares residual. Section
7.3
illustrates that this makes this bound uninformative in mixed-precision.

3.2
Improved PNE perturbation bound

We improve Theorem
3
with a bound that is informative in both mixed precision and a single working precision.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
have
\rank(\boldsymbol{A})=n
;
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be nonsingular;
\boldsymbol{E}_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
and
\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
;
\|\boldsymbol{E}_{s}\|\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}\|<1
, and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\equiv(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E}_{A})(\boldsymbol{R}_{s}+\boldsymbol{E}_{s})^{-1},\qquad\epsilon_{s}\equiv\frac{\|\boldsymbol{E}_{s}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\|},\qquad\epsilon_{A}\equiv\frac{\|\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|}.

The computed solutions corresponding to (
3
) are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{b}

(7)

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{R}_{s}+\boldsymbol{E}_{s})\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}.

(8)

If
\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\epsilon_{A}\left(\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}+1+\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\epsilon_{A}\right).

Proof.

Rearrange (
7
) and insert (
8
)
to find the computed least squares residual

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{r}=-\boldsymbol{A}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\boldsymbol{r}\equiv\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{b}.

(9)

Since
\|\boldsymbol{E}_{s}\|\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}\|<1
, the Banach lemma
[
9
, Lemma 2.3.3]
implies that
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}+\boldsymbol{E}_{s}
is nonsingular, so that
\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
is well defined. Because
\boldsymbol{A}
has full column rank,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E}_{A}=(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger})\boldsymbol{A}=(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{H})\boldsymbol{A},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\boldsymbol{H}\equiv\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}.

(10)

To relate
\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
to
\boldsymbol{A}_{p}
, we combine (
10
) with
(\boldsymbol{R}_{s}+\boldsymbol{E}_{s})^{-1}=\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1})^{-1}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{1}=(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{H})\boldsymbol{A}_{p}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{E}_{s}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1})^{-1}.

Multiply (
9
) by
(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{E}_{s}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1})^{T}
on both sides, and apply the above expression of
\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{H})^{T}\boldsymbol{r}=-\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{H})^{T}\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}.

Isolate
\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{r}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{r}=-\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{H}^{T}\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{H})^{T}\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}},

and multiply both sides by
(\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{p})^{-1}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{*}=-(\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{p})^{-1}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-T}\boldsymbol{E}_{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{H})^{T}\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}.

Rewrite the left hand side as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{*}=\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\boldsymbol{x}_{*}=\boldsymbol{R}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}),

which implies

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}=\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}\left(-(\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{p})^{-1}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-T}\boldsymbol{E}_{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{H})^{T}\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\right).

(11)

Bound the norm of
\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}
by bounding the norm of each summand on the right hand side of (
11
) in turn.
First,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-T}\boldsymbol{E}_{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{r}\|\leq\frac{\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\|\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\|}\|\boldsymbol{r}\|.

(12)

Abbreviating
\alpha\equiv 1+\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\epsilon_{A}
gives

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{H})^{T}\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}})\|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{\dagger}\|\|\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|.

(13)

Combine (
11
), (
12
), and (
13
),

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}\leq\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}\|\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\left(\frac{\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})}{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|^{2}}\,\frac{\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\|\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\|}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{r}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}+\alpha\frac{\|\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|}\right).\

From
\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\|}\leq\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|}
follows

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}\|\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\left(\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\epsilon_{A}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{r}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}+\alpha\frac{\|\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\epsilon_{A}\left(\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\frac{\|\boldsymbol{r}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}+\alpha\right).

Theorem
4
implies that to first order the error is bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}_{*}-\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}\lesssim\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\epsilon_{A}\max\left\{\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|},1\right\}

(14)

With an effective preconditioner, so that
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\lesssim 10
, this bound resembles the one in Lemma
1
since
\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\approx\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})
. Theorem
4

is not limited to triangular matrices but
holds for any nonsingular preconditioner
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}
.

Unlike Theorem
3
, the bound in Theorem
4
does not depend on the perturbation
\epsilon_{s}
in the preconditioner,
as long as
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}+\boldsymbol{E}_{s}
is nonsingular. Thus, Theorem
4
is more informative than Theorem
3

if
\epsilon_{s}
is large.

4.1
Extension an existing HPNE perturbation bound to mixed precision

Theorem 5
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
have
\rank(\boldsymbol{A})=n
;
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be nonsingular;
\boldsymbol{E}_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
,

, but

\epsilon=\max\{\epsilon_{A},\epsilon_{s}\}
.

If the perturbation
\epsilon_{s}
in the preconditioner is large

\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})^{-1}
, then the bound in Theorem

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
have
\rank(\boldsymbol{A})=n
;
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}

\boldsymbol{E}_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
,

;

, and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\equiv(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E}_{A})(\boldsymbol{R}_{s}+\boldsymbol{E}_{s})^{-1},\qquad\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\equiv\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E}_{A},\qquad\epsilon_{s}\equiv\frac{\|\boldsymbol{E}_{s}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\|},\qquad\epsilon_{A}\equiv\frac{\|\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|}.

The computed solutions corresponding to (
4
) are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}=\boldsymbol{A}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{b}.

(16)

If
\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})\nu\epsilon_{A}\left(\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}+1+\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\epsilon_{A}\right),

(17)

where

\displaystyle\nu\equiv\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|\|\boldsymbol{A}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\|}\geq 1.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
4
, the Banach lemma
[
9
, Lemma 2.3.3]
implies that
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}+\boldsymbol{E}_{s}=(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{E}_{s}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1})\boldsymbol{R}_{s}
is invertible, and that
\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
is well defined. Since
\boldsymbol{A}

has full column rank,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{1}=(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger})\boldsymbol{A}_{p}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{E}_{s}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1})^{-1}=(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{H})\boldsymbol{A}_{p}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{E}_{s}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1})^{-1},\qquad\boldsymbol{H}\equiv\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}.

Find the computed least squares residual in (
16
) as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{r}=-\boldsymbol{A}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\qquad\text{where}\qquad\boldsymbol{r}\equiv\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{b},

multiply both sides by
(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{E}_{s}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1})^{T}
and use the above
expression for
\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{r}=-\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-T}\boldsymbol{E}_{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{H})^{T}\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}.

At last, multiply both sides by
(\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}
and take norms,

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\|}\left(\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\|\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\|\frac{\|\boldsymbol{r}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}+(1+\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\epsilon_{A})\|\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|\|\boldsymbol{A}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\|}\left(\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\epsilon_{A}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{r}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}+(1+\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\epsilon_{A})\epsilon_{A}\right),

where the last inequality follows from
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}
.

Theorem
6
shows that to first order the HPNE error is bounded by

\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}\lesssim\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})\nu\epsilon_{A}\max\left\{\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|},\,1\right\}.

(18)

With an effective preconditioner, so that
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\lesssim 10
, this bound resembles the one in Lemma
1
since
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})\approx\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})
, see
[
12
, Section 3]
for details on this approximation. Similar to the PNE bound in Theorem
4
, the bound in Theorem
5
does not depend on the perturbation
\epsilon_{s}
in the preconditioner, as long as
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}+\boldsymbol{E}_{s}
is nonsingular.

5
Seminormal and not-normal equations

We consider two alternative approaches for normal equations,
and present perturbation bounds for the seminormal equations (Section
5.1
) and the not-normal equations (Section
5.2
).

5.1
Seminormal equations

If
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R}
is a thin QR factorization where
\boldsymbol{Q}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
has orthonormal columns and
\boldsymbol{R}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
is upper triangular, the seminormal equations
[
2
]
are

\boldsymbol{R}^{T}\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{x}_{*}=\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{b}.

(19)

The seminormal equations can be used for solving least squares problems (
2
) when
\boldsymbol{A}
is large and sparse,
or when
\boldsymbol{Q}
is expensive to access or store
[
2
]
.

The perturbation bound in Theorem
7
below makes no assumptions on the perturbation
\boldsymbol{E}
, so the perturbed matrix
\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E}
can be rank-deficient.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
have
\rank(\boldsymbol{A})=n
;
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be nonsingular;
\boldsymbol{E}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
,
\epsilon\equiv\|\boldsymbol{E}\|/\|\boldsymbol{A}\|
;
and
\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E}=\hat{\boldsymbol{Q}}\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}
a thin QR factorization where
\hat{\boldsymbol{Q}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
has orthonormal columns.
The computed solutions corresponding to (
19
) are

\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}=(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E})^{T}\boldsymbol{b}.

(20)

If
\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\neq\mathbf{0}
then

\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}||}\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})^{2}\epsilon\left(\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}+1+\epsilon\right)

(21)

Proof.

Because
\hat{\boldsymbol{Q}}
has orthonormal columns, we can write

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E})^{T}\boldsymbol{b}=\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{Q}}\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}=(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E})^{T}(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E})\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}.

Now apply Lemma
2
.

The bound in Theorem
7
is the same as the one for the normal equations in Lemma
2
. This is not surprising because
\boldsymbol{R}^{T}\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}
in exact arithmetic. The roundoff error analysis in
[
2
, Theorem 3.1]
also shows that the seminormal equations depend on
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})^{2}
, though
the perturbation analysis here assumes exact arithmetic after the
perturbations to the inputs.

Theorem
7
shows that, to first order, the relative error in
\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}
is bounded by

\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}_{*}-\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}\lesssim\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})^{2}\,\epsilon\,\max\left\{\frac{\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|},1\right\}.

Thus, like the normal equations, the conditioning of the seminormal equations
depends on the least squares residual when it is too large, that is, if

\frac{\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}>1
. This quantifies the claim that the seminormal equations together with iterative refinement are insensitive to the size of the least squares residual in
[
4
, Section 8]
; the seminormal equations are sensitive to the size of the least squares residual, but only when it is large. However, much like the normal equations in Lemma
1
, the error depends on the square of the condition number of
\boldsymbol{A}
.

5.2
Not-normal equations

For a matrix
\boldsymbol{B}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
with
\rank(\boldsymbol{B})=n
and the same column space as
\boldsymbol{A}
, the not-normal equations are
[
21
]

\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{*}=\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{b}.

(22)

The not-normal equations, combined with an iterative linear system solver, represent a potential approach for solving least squares problems that are large and sparse
[
21
]
.

The perturbation bound in Theorem
8
below makes no assumptions on the perturbations
\boldsymbol{E}_{A}
and
\boldsymbol{E}_{B}
, so the perturbed matrices
\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E}_{A}
and
\boldsymbol{B}+\boldsymbol{E}_{B}
can be rank-deficient.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
have
\rank(\boldsymbol{A})=\rank(\boldsymbol{B})=n
;

\boldsymbol{C}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be nonsingular with
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{C}
;
\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
,
\boldsymbol{E}_{B}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
;
\epsilon_{A}\equiv\|\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\|/\|\boldsymbol{A}\|
, and

\epsilon_{B}\equiv\|\boldsymbol{E}_{B}\|/\|\boldsymbol{B}\|
.
The computed solutions corresponding to (
22
) are

(\boldsymbol{B}+\boldsymbol{E}_{B})^{T}(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E}_{A})\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}=(\boldsymbol{B}+\boldsymbol{E}_{B})^{T}\boldsymbol{b}.

(23)

If
\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
then

\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}||}\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})\nu\left(\epsilon_{B}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}|+(1+\epsilon_{B})\epsilon_{A}\right),\qquad\nu\equiv\frac{\|\boldsymbol{B}\|\|\boldsymbol{A}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\|}\geq 1.

(24)

Proof.

Rearrange (
23
) to find the computed least squares residual,

(\boldsymbol{B}+\boldsymbol{E}_{B})^{T}\boldsymbol{r}=-(\boldsymbol{B}+\boldsymbol{E}_{B})^{T}\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\boldsymbol{r}\equiv\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{b}.

Rearrange the left side and use the fact that
\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{*}
,

\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*})=-\boldsymbol{E}_{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{r}-(\boldsymbol{B}+\boldsymbol{E}_{B})^{T}\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}.

(25)

From
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{C}
follows
\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{C}
where

\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{B}
is symmetric positive definite since
\boldsymbol{B}
has full column rank. Thus
\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}
, as the product of two nonsingular matrices
\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{B}
and
\boldsymbol{C}
, is also nonsingular.

Multiply (
25
) by
(\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}
on both sides and take norms,

\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|\leq\|(\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}\|\left(\|\boldsymbol{E}_{B}\|\|\boldsymbol{r}\|+(\|\boldsymbol{B}\|+\|\boldsymbol{E}_{B}\|)\|\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|\right).

At last, factor out
\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{B}\|
and divide by
\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|\neq 0
,

\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})\frac{\|\boldsymbol{B}\|\|\boldsymbol{A}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\|}\left(\epsilon_{B}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{r}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}+(1+\epsilon_{B})\epsilon_{A}\right).

Theorem
8
shows that, to first order, the relative error in
\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}
is bounded by

\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}\lesssim\kappa(\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})\left(\epsilon_{B}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}+\epsilon_{A}\right).

(26)

The above bound suggests that, like the HPNE, the
conditioniong of the not-normal equations depends on the least squares residual.

Remark 5.1
(HPNE as a special case of the not-normal equations).

If
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{A}_{p}
in (
22
) then HPNE is a special case of the not-normal equations.

Though the two bounds are identical to first order,
Theorem
8
does not directly reduce to Theorem
6
.
The small difference comes from the inability of Theorem
8
to account for the perturbation in the preconditioner
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}
, since it assumes an additive perturbation in
\boldsymbol{B}
.

To see this, let
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{A}_{p}
and
\boldsymbol{E}_{B}=\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}
, so that
\boldsymbol{B}+\boldsymbol{E}_{B}=(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E}_{A})\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}
. Inserting these matrices into (
24
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|\|\boldsymbol{A}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\|}\epsilon_{A}\left(\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}+1+\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\,\epsilon_{A}\right)

(27)

since
\epsilon_{B}=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{E}_{A}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|}\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\epsilon_{A}
. If
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\approx\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})
, then (
27
) and the HPNE bound (
17
) are almost the same. Moreover, the first order approximation of (
27
) is

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}\lesssim\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|\|\boldsymbol{A}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\|}\epsilon_{A}\left\{\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|},\,1\right\},

(28)

which is identical to the first order approximation (
18
) of the HPNE bound.

Remark 5.2
(Comparison with PNE and HPNE).

If
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
are effective preconditioners with

\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\lesssim 10
and

\kappa(\boldsymbol{B})\lesssim 10
, then
the not-normal equations bound in Theorem
8
resembles the
PNE and HPNE bounds in Theorems
4
and
6
, respectively.
From
[
12
, Section 3]

follows

\displaystyle\kappa(\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})\approx\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})\approx\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\approx\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s}).

With these approximations, the first order bounds of the PNE (
14
), HPNE (
18
) and not-normal equations (
26
) are all approximately equal to

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}_{*}-\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|}\lesssim\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\,\nu\,\epsilon_{A}\max\left\{\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\|},1\right\}.

If also
\nu\approx 1
, then this bound resembles that of the original least squares problem in Lemma
1
, suggesting that
all linear systems are well conditioned.

6
Preconditioning

We discuss the motivation behind the low-precision
preconditioner for PNE and HPNE (Section
6.1
), and
present the randomized preconditioner for the numerical experiments (Section
6.2
).

6.1
A low precision preconditioner

Theorems
4
and
5

suggest that the PNE and HPNE
conditioning depends only weakly on the perturbation in the preconditioner.
Hence we can accelerate the linear system solution by computing the preconditioner in lower precision.

Lemma
9
shows that a
perturbation
\boldsymbol{E}
in the preconditioner has little effect on the condition number of the preconditioned matrix
\boldsymbol{A}_{p}
, as long as
\boldsymbol{E}
remains sufficiently small.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
with
\rank(\boldsymbol{A})=n
;

\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
nonsingular;
\boldsymbol{E}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
;

\epsilon\equiv\frac{\|\boldsymbol{E}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}\|}
;

\|\boldsymbol{E}\|\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}\|<1
; and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\equiv\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1},\qquad\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\equiv\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{R}_{s}+\boldsymbol{E})^{-1}.

Then

\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{1})\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\>\frac{1+\epsilon\ \kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})}{1-\epsilon\ \kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})}.

Proof.

Since
\|\boldsymbol{E}\|\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}\|<1
, the Banach lemma
[
9
, Lemma 2.3.3]
implies that
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}+\boldsymbol{E}
is nonsingular,
so that
\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
is well defined.
To relate
\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
to
\boldsymbol{A}_{p}
, factor out
\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{1}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1})^{-1}=\boldsymbol{A}_{p}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1})^{-1}.

The Banach lemma
[
9
, Lemma 2.3.3]
also implies

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\|\leq\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|}{1-\|\boldsymbol{E}\|\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}\|}=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|}{1-\epsilon\ \kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})}.

(29)

From
\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
having full column rank follows that its
left inverse equals

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{1}^{\dagger}=(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1})\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{\dagger}.

Take norms,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{A}_{1}^{\dagger}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{\dagger}\|\ (1+\|\boldsymbol{E}\|\|\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}\|)=\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{\dagger}\|\ (1+\epsilon\ \kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})),

and combine the above with (
29
),

\displaystyle\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{1})=\|\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\|\|\boldsymbol{A}_{1}^{\dagger}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\|\|\boldsymbol{A}_{p}^{\dagger}\|\frac{1+\epsilon\ \kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})}{1-\epsilon\ \kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})}=\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\ \frac{1+\epsilon\ \kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})}{1-\epsilon\ \kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})}.

Lemma
9
shows that if
\boldsymbol{A}_{p}
is well conditioned and if
\epsilon<\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})^{-1}
, then

\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
is also well conditioned.
The numerical experiments in Section
7
confirm
that
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{1})\lesssim 10
as long as
\epsilon\ll\kappa(\boldsymbol{R}_{s})^{-1}\approx\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}
.
We control the size of
\epsilon

by adjusting the precision in which the preconditioner is computed.

Algorithm
1
presents a pseudo code for solving the PNE or HPNE with a preconditioner computed in lower precision.
The precision is automatically selected
by a fast condition number estimate

\kappa_{0}\approx\log_{10}(\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}))

computed in single precision. We use the Hager 1-norm condition number estimator
\verb|condest|
in Matlab and the CONDITION package
[
3
]
in C
++
to estimate the condition number of
\boldsymbol{A}
. In particular,
we use the result from
[
11
, Exercise 2, Section 2.6]
and estimate the 1 norm condition number of
\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}
to find the upper bound

\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})^{2}=\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})\leq n\|\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\|_{1}\|(\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}\|_{1}.

The selected precision is half precision if
\kappa_{0}<4
, and is single precision if
\kappa_{0}<8
.
If
\kappa_{0}
overflows or is greater than 8, the selected precision is double precision. If
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})
is known in advance, than the same precision selection heuristic applies without the need for estimation.

It is important to promote
the preconditioner back to double precision prior to preconditioning
\boldsymbol{A}
.
Experiments illustrate that the solution accuracy suffers if
the computation of
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}
happens in low precision.

6.2
A randomized preconditioner

The ideal preconditioner is the upper triangular matrix

\boldsymbol{R}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
from a thin QR decomposition
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R}
, where

\boldsymbol{Q}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
has orthonormal columns. However, the computation of
\boldsymbol{R}
can be too expensive if
\boldsymbol{A}
has large dimension.

A popular approach
[
1
,
4
,
7
,
8
]
reduces the cost of computing a randomized preconditioner without a sacrifice in accuracy.
This is done by sketching
\boldsymbol{A}
with a random matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}

to produce the smaller matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{s}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}
.
With an appropriate choice of
\mathbf{\Omega}
,
the preconditioned matrix
\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\equiv\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{R}_{s}^{-1}
is likely
to have a low condition number.

Motivated by the least squares solver Blendenpik
[
1
]
,
the numerical experiments in Section
7

use a subsampled trigonometric transform as the sketching matrix,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Omega}\equiv\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{D},

(30)

where
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
is a Fourier Transform,

\boldsymbol{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal elements
are equal to -1 or 1 with equal probability 1/2, and the rows
of
\boldsymbol{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
are sampled from
\boldsymbol{I}_{m}
uniformly and with replacement.
The purpose of the randomized transform
\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{D}
is to
ensure that
\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{A}
has an optimal coherence
\mu\approx n/m
.

Among the probabilistic bounds for the condition number of the preconditioned matrix based on (
30
)
[
1
,
12
,
13
,
18
]
, we choose the following.

Lemma 10
(Theorem 4.1 in
[
12
]
).

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
with
\rank(\boldsymbol{A})=n
and thin QR factorization
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R}
, where
\boldsymbol{Q}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
with
\boldsymbol{Q}^{T}\boldsymbol{Q}=\boldsymbol{I}_{n}
. Let
\boldsymbol{S}
in (
30
) sample
d
rows uniformly
and with replacement, and let
\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{Q}
have coherence

\mu\equiv\max_{1\leq i\leq m}{\|\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{Q}\|_{2}^{2}}
.

For any
0<\epsilon<1
and
0<\delta<1
, if
d\geq 2m\mu(1+\tfrac{\epsilon}{3})\tfrac{\ln(n/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}

then with probability at least
1-\delta

\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\leq\sqrt{\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}}.

(31)

Section
7
illustrates that a sampling amount of
d=3n
tends to produce preconditioned matrices with
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}_{p})\lesssim 10
.

)
and in mixed precision (Section

matrices
\boldsymbol{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}

m\leq 2^{17}\approx 10^{5}

columns.

and
