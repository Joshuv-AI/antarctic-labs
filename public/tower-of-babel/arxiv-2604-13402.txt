Abstract
. We study the intersection statistics of affine subspaces in the hypercube 𝔽 2 n \mathbb{F}_{2}^{n} , motivated by recent work of Alon, Axenovich, and Goldwasser on the intersection statistics of axis-aligned subcubes of an n n -dimensional cube. Let d ⩾ 1 d\geqslant 1 and 0 ⩽ s ⩽ 2 d 0\leqslant s\leqslant 2^{d} be nonnegative integers. For a subset A ⊆ 𝔽 2 n A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n} where n ⩾ d n\geqslant d , define λ ∗ ​ ( n , d , s , A ) \lambda^{*}(n,d,s,A) to be the fraction of affine d d -flats in 𝔽 2 n \mathbb{F}_{2}^{n} that intersect A A at exactly s s points. Let λ ∗ ​ ( n , d , s ) = max A ⊆ 𝔽 2 n ⁡ λ ∗ ​ ( n , d , s , A ) \lambda^{*}(n,d,s)=\max_{A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\lambda^{*}(n,d,s,A) and let λ ∗ ​ ( d , s ) = lim n → ∞ λ ∗ ​ ( n , d , s ) \lambda^{*}(d,s)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda^{*}(n,d,s) . We show that when s = j ⋅ 2 k s=j\cdot 2^{k} with j j odd and k ⩾ 1 k\geqslant 1 , we have λ ∗ ​ ( d , s ) → 1 − Θ ⁡ ( 2 − k ) \lambda^{*}(d,s)\to 1-\Theta(2^{-k}) as d → ∞ d\to\infty . 1.
Introduction

Recently, work of Alon, Axenovich, and Goldwasser
[
1
]
initiated the systematic study of the hypercube statistics problem defined as follows. For a subset
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
and nonnegative integers
d\leqslant n
and
s\leqslant 2^{d}
, define
\lambda(n,d,s,A)
as the fraction of
d
-subcubes
Q_{d}\subseteq Q_{n}
such that
|Q_{d}\cap A|=s
. Then let
\lambda(n,d,s):=\max_{A\subseteq Q_{n}}\lambda(n,d,s,A)
and let
\lambda(d,s):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda(n,d,s)
. Note that
\lambda(n,d,s)
is monotone non-increasing in
n
by a simple averaging argument, so the limit
\lambda(d,s)
is well-defined. Except for very specific values of
s
and a few pairs of small
s
and
d
, the exact values for
\lambda(d,s)
are poorly understood.

In this paper, we study the analogous intersection statistics problem for affine subspaces rather than axis-aligned subcubes. From this, we can also obtain results for the hypercube statistics problem. For nonnegative integers
n\geqslant d\geqslant 1
and
s\leqslant 2^{d}
, define for a subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
the affine subspace statistics
\lambda^{*}(n,d,s,A)
as the fraction of affine
d
-subspaces
Q\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
(i.e.
Q=x_{0}+U
where
x_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and
U\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is a
d
-dimensional linear subspace) such that
|Q\cap A|=s
. For convenience, we refer to an affine
d
-subspace as a
d
-flat from now on. Similarly we can define
\lambda^{*}(n,d,s):=\max_{A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\lambda^{*}(n,d,s,A)
and
\lambda^{*}(d,s):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda^{*}(n,d,s)
. Note that the limit is well-defined because
\lambda^{*}(n,d,s)
is also monotone non-increasing. Furthermore, observe that we have

\lambda^{*}(d,s)\leqslant\lambda(d,s).

Indeed, taking a construction in the
d
-flat setting and picking the best coordinates would give a construction for
d
-cubes that performs as well as for
d
-flats. The flat setting removes the dependence on the coordinate system which allows the study of the intersection statistics problem under full affine symmetry. Thus, the flat setting provides a basis-invariant version that identifies the obstructions which any improvement in the cube setting must overcome by exploiting the coordinate-dependent structure.

Our main result is to show that for even
s
, we have

\lambda^{*}(d,s)=1-\Theta(2^{-\nu_{2}(s)}),

where
\nu_{2}(s)
denotes the exponent of the largest power of
2
divisible by
s
(i.e. the
2
-adic valuation of
s
). More specifically, we show the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d\geqslant 1
and
1<s<2^{d}
. Suppose
s=j\cdot 2^{k}
where
j
is odd and
k\geqslant 0
, then we have

\lambda^{*}(d,s)\leqslant 1-\frac{2}{3}(1-2^{-(d-k)})\cdot 2^{-k}+O(2^{-2k})+o_{d}(1).

In particular, for fixed
k
and
d\to\infty
, we have
\lambda^{*}(d,s)\to 1-\frac{2}{3}\cdot 2^{-k}+O(2^{-2k})
.

On the other hand, a lower bound for
\lambda^{*}(d,s)
can be obtained by the same linear algebraic construction presented in Alon–Axenovich–Goldwasser
[
1
]
.

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
s\leqslant 2^{d}
be nonnegative integers. For
s=2^{k}\cdot j
with
j
odd and
k\geqslant 1
, we have

\lambda^{*}(d,s)\geqslant 1-2^{-k}.

For
s
odd, we have
\lambda^{*}(d,s)\geqslant(1+o_{d}(1))\cdot 0.2887
.

For completeness, we include a proof of
1.2
in
Section

2
which follows similarly to the proof of Theorem 2 in
[
1
]
.

Combining
Theorem

1.1
and
1.2
, for
s=j\cdot 2^{k}
with
j
odd and
k\geqslant 1
, we have

\lambda^{*}(d,s)=1-\Theta(2^{-k}),

which determines
1-\lambda^{*}(d,s)
for all even
s
up to an absolute constant. In particular, this shows that
\lambda^{*}(d,s)
is controlled by the
2
-adic valuation of
s
rather than the magnitude of
s
. This behavior is in sharp contrast to the current known upper bounds for the cube statistics from
[
1
]
which depend directly on the magnitude of
s
.

Now we present our result for odd
s
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
d\geqslant 1
and
1<s<2^{d}
. Suppose
s
is odd, then

\lambda^{*}(d,s)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}.

We note that
Theorem

1.3
does not hold for
\lambda(d,s)
as there are specific values for
d
and odd
s
where
\lambda(d,s)>1/2
(see
Section

5
). In fact, to prove
Theorem

1.3
, we will prove a strictly stronger statement that for any subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, the fraction of affine
d
-flats that intersect
A
at an odd number of points is at most
\frac{1}{2}+o(1)
as
n\to\infty
. This is not true in the axis-aligned cube setting, as one can construct a set that intersects every
d
-cube in an odd number of points (see
Remark

4.2
).

Furthermore, we also determine the exact value of
\lambda^{*}(d,2^{d-1})
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
d>1
be an integer. We have

\lambda^{*}(d,2^{d-1})=1-2^{-d}.

Note that in contrast we have
\lambda(d,2^{d-1})=1
, which is achieved by taking the set of points with Hamming weight of the same parity.

Finally, we observe that
\lambda^{*}(d,s)
cannot change too drastically between neighboring values. Namely, we show that
\lambda^{*}(d,s)
and
\lambda^{*}(d,s+1)
differ by a multiplicative factor of at most
e
.

Theorem 1.5
.

For
d>1
and
2\leqslant s\leqslant 2^{d}
, we have

\frac{\lambda^{*}(d,s)}{\lambda^{*}(d,s-1)}\leqslant\left(\frac{s}{s-1}\right)^{s-1}\leqslant e,

and for
0\leqslant s\leqslant 2^{d}-2
, we have

\frac{\lambda^{*}(d,s)}{\lambda^{*}(d,s+1)}\leqslant\left(\frac{2^{d}-s}{2^{d}-s-1}\right)^{2^{d}-s-1}\leqslant e.

As a direct corollary, we also obtain better lower bounds for certain odd values of
s
.

Corollary 1.6
.

For
d>1
and
1<s<2^{d}
where
s=j\cdot 2^{k}
with
j
odd and
k\geqslant 1
, we have

\lambda^{*}(d,s+1)\geqslant\frac{1}{e}\cdot(1-2^{-k})\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda^{*}(d,s-1)\geqslant\frac{1}{e}\cdot(1-2^{-k}).

In particular, for
k\geqslant 3
and
d
large, we have
(1-2^{-k})/e>0.2887
, improving on
1.2
for specific values of
s
.

1.1.
Comparison to
\lambda(d,s)

In this subsection, we compare our results for
\lambda^{*}(d,s)
with current known results for
\lambda(d,s)
. We define
c_{d}
as the probability that a
d\times d
matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
where each column is an independent random nonzero vector in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
is nonsingular. Note that we have

c_{d}=\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\left(1-\frac{2^{i}-1}{2^{d}-1}\right),

and
c_{d}\to 0.2887
as
d\to\infty
. We also define
c(d,k)
to be the probability that a
(d-k)\times d
matrix where each column is an independent random vector in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d-k}
having rank
d-k
. Note that we have

c(d,k)=\prod_{i=0}^{d-k-1}\left(1-\frac{2^{i}}{2^{d}}\right)

and for fixed
k
, we have
c(d,k)\to 1-O(2^{-k})
as
d\to\infty
.

Now we can recall the current known bounds for
\lambda(d,s)
in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.7

(
[
1
]
)
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
s\leqslant 2^{d}
be nonnegative integers.

(1)

\lambda(d,s)=1
if and only if
s\in\{0,2^{d-1},2^{d}\}
.

(2)

For
1<s<2^{d-1}
, we have

\lambda(d,s)\leqslant\lambda(d+2,d,s)\leqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{4s-1}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{d+1}\right).

(3)

For
s=1
, we have
\lambda(d,1)\geqslant(1-2^{-d})^{2^{d}-1}.
In particular,
\lambda(d,1)\to(1+o(1))\frac{1}{e}
as
d\to\infty
.

(4)

For any
1\leqslant s\leqslant 2^{d}
, we have

\lambda(d,s)\geqslant c_{d}.

(5)

For
s=2^{k}\cdot j
, we have

\lambda(d,s)\geqslant c(d,k).

We remark that
Item

1
in
Theorem

1.7
was first proved by Goldwasser and Hansen in
[
4
]
. On the lower bound side, we remark that
c_{d}>c(d,0)
as
c_{d}
is defined to be the nonsingular probability of a
d\times d
matrix with
nonzero
independent columns. On the other hand, the bounds in
Theorem

1.7
imply that if
s
is divisible by a power of
2
that is
\Theta(s)
, then
\lambda(d,s)=1-\Theta(1/s)
. Most recently, Bodnár and Pikhurko determined in
[
2
]
three exact values for
\lambda(d,s)
where
\lambda(d,s)\neq 1
using the Flag algebra method. Specifically, they proved that
\lambda(3,2)=8/9
,
\lambda(4,2)=264/343
, and
\lambda(4,4)=26/27
. However, obtaining tight bounds for essentially all other values of
d
and
s
remains open.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\textup{Gr}(n,d)
denote the set of
d
-dimensional linear subspaces in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and let
\textup{Aff}(n,d)
denote the set of
d
-flats in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. Note that every
Q\in\textup{Aff}(n,d)
can be written as
Q=x_{0}+U
for some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and
U\in\textup{Gr}(n,d)
. For a linear subspace
U\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, we use
U^{\perp}:=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\mid\langle x,u\rangle=0\text{ for all }u\in U\}
to denote its orthogonal complement. Recall the
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-analogue of
\binom{n}{d}
is defined as

\binom{n}{d}_{2}:=\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}\frac{2^{\,n-i}-1}{2^{\,d-i}-1},

which is the number of
d
-dimensional linear subspaces in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. Namely, we have
|\textup{Gr}(n,d)|=\binom{n}{d}_{2}
. Furthermore, we have

|\textup{Aff}(n,d)|=2^{n-d}\binom{n}{d}_{2}.

Note that for a subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, we can interpret
\lambda^{*}(n,d,s,A)
as the probability of a uniformly random
d
-flat
Q\sim\textup{Aff}(n,d)
intersecting
A
at exactly
s
points.

Recall that in the introduction, we defined the quantity
c(d,k)
. This is the probability of a random
(d-k)\times d
matrix with columns being independent random vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d-k}
having rank
d-k
. Specifically, we have

c(d,k)=\prod_{i=0}^{d-k-1}\left(1-\frac{2^{i}}{2^{d}}\right)=\prod_{i=0}^{d-k-1}(1-2^{i-d}).

For our setting, it is more natural to consider the quantity
c_{n}(d,k)
defined as follows. Let
B:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d-k}
be a surjective linear map. Define

c_{n}(d,k)=\mathbb{P}_{U\in\textup{Gr}(n,d)}[\textup{rank}(B|_{U})=d-k].

Then we have

c_{n}(d,k)=2^{(d-k)(n-d)}\cdot\frac{\binom{n-d+k}{k}_{2}}{\binom{n}{d}_{2}}.

Indeed, for
U\in\textup{Gr}(n,d)
to satisfy
\textup{rank}(B|_{U})=d-k
, we have
\dim(U\cap\ker B)=k
. So we can first choose the
k
-dimensional subspace
W=U\cap\ker B
from
\ker B
in
\binom{n-d+k}{k}_{2}
many ways. Then for each fixed
W
, the possible
U\in\textup{Gr}(n,d)
with
U\cap\ker B=W
are the lifts of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}/\ker B
to a
d
-dimensional subspace containing
W
. There are
2^{(d-k)(n-d)}
such lifts because they are parametrized by the linear maps
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}/\ker B\to\ker B/W
where
\dim\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}/\ker B=d-k
and
\dim\ker B/W=n-d
.
Expanding the
2
-binomial coefficients, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n}(d,k)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{d-k-1}(1-2^{i-n})\right)\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{1-2^{i-(n-d+k)}}{1-2^{i-n}}\right)\left(\prod_{i=0}^{d-k-1}(1-2^{i-d})\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{i=0}^{d-k-1}\lim_{n\to\infty}(1-2^{i-n})\right)\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1-2^{i-(n-d+k)}}{1-2^{i-n}}\right)\prod_{i=0}^{d-k-1}(1-2^{i-d})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{d-k-1}(1-2^{i-d})=c(d,k)

since the factors in the first two finite products both tend to
1
as
n\to\infty
.

We will also use the discrete Fourier transform over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. For a function
f:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, it is more convenient for us to use the
unnormalized
Fourier transform
\hat{f}:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

\hat{f}(\xi)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}f(x)(-1)^{\langle\xi,x\rangle}.

Thus we have the inversion formula

f(x)=2^{-n}\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\hat{f}(\xi)(-1)^{\langle\xi,x\rangle}.

Recall Parseval’s identity as

\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\hat{f}(\xi)^{2}=2^{n}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}f(x)^{2}.

For functions
f,g:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, the convolution of
f
and
g
is defined as

(f*g)(x):=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}f(y)g(x-y),

and recall that we have
\widehat{f*g}(\xi)=\hat{f}(\xi)\hat{g}(\xi)
.

We will also use some standard facts about the projective space over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Let
\textup{PG}(n,2)
denote the
n
-dimensional projective space over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, consisting of the nonzero vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n+1}
. Namely, we identify
\textup{PG}(n,2)\cong\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
. Moreover, note that a projective
k
-dimensional subspace in
\textup{PG}(n,2)
corresponds to a
(k+1)
-dimensional subspace in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n+1}
. We recall the definition of a blocking set.

Definition 2.1

(Blocking set)
.

A set
B\subseteq\textup{PG}(n,2)
is called a blocking set with respect to
k
-subspaces if
B\cap U\neq\varnothing
for all
k
-dimensional projective subspaces
U\subseteq\textup{PG}(n,2)
.

We will use the Bose–Burton theorem that gives a lower bound for the size of a blocking set in
\textup{PG}(n,2)
.

Theorem 2.2

(Bose–Burton
[
3
]
)
.

Let
B\subseteq\textup{PG}(n,2)
be a blocking set with respect to
k
-subspaces. Then

|B|\geqslant|\textup{PG}(n-k,2)|=2^{n-k+1}-1,

where equality holds when
B
is an
(n-k)
-dimensional projective subspace.

2.1.
Lower bound construction.

For completeness, we include a proof of
1.2
which is similar to the proof of Theorem 2 in
[
1
]
.

Proof of
1.2
.

Let
s=j\cdot 2^{k}
for
j
odd and
k\geqslant 0
. Let
B:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d-k}
be a surjective linear map and let
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d-k}
be a subset of size
|S|=j
. Then consider the subset

A:=B^{-1}(S)=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\mid B(x)\in S\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}.

We show that
\lambda^{*}(n,d,s,A)\geqslant c_{n}(d,k)
. Let
Q=x_{0}+U
be a
d
-flat where
x_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and
U\in\textup{Gr}(n,d)
. Then note that

|Q\cap A|=2^{d-\textup{rank}(B|_{U})}\cdot|S\cap B(Q)|,

where
B(Q)=\{B(x)\mid x\in Q\}
. It is clear that
|Q\cap A|=s
if
\textup{rank}(B|_{U})=d-k
. (In fact, if
k>0
, we have
|Q\cap A|=s
if and only if
\textup{rank}(B|_{U})=d-k
.) Thus by definition, we have

\lambda^{*}(n,d,s,A)=\mathbb{P}[|Q\cap A|=s]\geqslant\mathbb{P}[\textup{rank}(B|_{U})=d-k]=c_{n}(d,k).

Thus, we can conclude that
\lambda^{*}(d,s)\geqslant\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda^{*}(n,d,s,A)=\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n}(d,k)=c(d,k)
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

We remark that for the axis-aligned cube statistics, one can choose
B
to be a linear map defined by a
(d-k)\times n
matrix of rank
d-k
with all columns being nonzero vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d-k}
. Then the lower bound can be improved to the probability of a
(d-k)\times d
matrix with each column being an independent random nonzero vector in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d-k}
having rank
d-k
as remarked in
[
1
]
. In the affine flat setting, a
(d-k)\times d
matrix arises from restricting a surjective linear map
B
to a random
d
-subspace
U
. Since the basis vectors of
U
may lie in
\ker B
, the induced columns are not forced to be nonzero, so one cannot get such an improvement.

s
even

In this section, we present the proofs of
Theorem

1.4
and
Theorem

1.1
, giving upper bounds for
\lambda^{*}(d,s)
when
s
is even. Let us begin with the simpler special case where
s=2^{d-1}
.

3.1.
The case
s=2^{d-1}

From now on, fix
d>1
and
n\geqslant d
. In the case where
s=2^{d-1}
, we observe a clear distinction between the cube statistics and flat statistics. Recall that when
s=2^{d-1}
, in the cube statistics setting, we have
\lambda(n,d,s)=1
which is achieved by taking the set
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
containing all the points in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with an even number of
1
’s or all the points with an odd number of
1
’s. Equivalently,
A
is the hyperplane defined by the linear equation
\langle x,\vec{1}\rangle=0
or
\langle x,\vec{1}\rangle=1
. Additionally, it was remarked in
[
1
]
that one can get more constructions of
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
by choosing an
(n-d+t)
-subcube
Q
for
1\leqslant t\leqslant d-1
and replacing the set
A\cap Q
with its complement.

First we compute the flat statistics for the set
A
of the points on the hyperplane
\langle x,\vec{1}\rangle=0
. For convenience, let
L(x)=\langle x,\vec{1}\rangle
. For a
d
-flat
Q=x_{0}+U\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
where
U\in\textup{Gr}(n,d)
, note that if
L|_{U}\not\equiv 0
, then
|Q\cap A|=2^{d-1}
; if
L_{U}\equiv 0
, then
|Q\cap A|\in\{0,2^{d}\}
. Thus we have that

\lambda^{*}(n,d,s,A)=1-\mathbb{P}_{U\sim\textup{Gr}(n,d)}[U\subseteq\ker L]=1-\frac{\binom{n-1}{d}_{2}}{\binom{n}{d}_{2}}=1-\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}\frac{2^{n-1-i}-1}{2^{n-i}-1},

where the probability
\mathbb{P}_{U\sim\textup{Gr}(n,d)}[U\subseteq\ker L]
is over a uniformly random element
U
in
\textup{Gr}(n,d)
. In particular, we have

\lambda^{*}(d,s)\geqslant\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1-\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}\frac{2^{n-1-i}-1}{2^{n-i}-1}\right)=1-2^{-d}<1.

We show that this is optimal in the following lemma using a second moment argument.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
d>1
and
n\geqslant d
, for any
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
we have

\lambda^{*}(n,d,2^{d-1},A)\leqslant 1-\frac{\binom{n-1}{d}_{2}}{\binom{n}{d}_{2}}.

Consequently,

\lambda^{*}(d,2^{d-1})=1-2^{-d}.

Proof.

First note that to sample a uniformly random
d
-flat
Q
from
\textup{Aff}(n,d)
, one can first sample a uniformly random linear
d
-subspace
U\in\textup{Gr}(n,d)
and then sample a uniformly random
x_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. Then taking
Q=x_{0}+U
gives a uniformly random
d
-flat. In this proof, we will consider a uniformly random
Q\in\textup{Aff}(n,d)
sampled in this way.

Let
f=1_{A}
and consider
h:=1-2f
. Note that
h:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
where
h(x)=1
if
x\not\in A
and
h(x)=-1
if
x\in A
. For a linear
d
-subspace
U\in\textup{Gr}(n,d)
define

H_{U}(x):=\sum_{u\in U}h(x+u)=(h*1_{U})(x),

and notice that for a
d
-flat
Q=x_{0}+U
with
x_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
we have

\sum_{y\in Q}h(y)=H_{U}(x_{0})=2^{d}-2|A\cap Q|.

In particular,
|A\cap Q|=2^{d-1}
if and only if
H_{U}(x_{0})=0
.

Now we fix
U\in\textup{Gr}(n,d)
and let
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a uniformly random element. Since
|H_{U}(x)|\leqslant 2^{d}
for all
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, we have
H_{U}(x)^{2}\leqslant 2^{2d}1_{H_{U}(x)\neq 0}
and thus

\mathbb{P}[H_{U}(x)=0]\leqslant 1-\frac{\mathbb{E}[H_{U}(x)^{2}]}{2^{2d}}.

Note that for
U\in\textup{Gr}(n,d)
, we have
\widehat{1_{U}}(\xi)=|U|\cdot 1_{\xi\in U^{\perp}}
, so we have

\widehat{H_{U}}(\xi)=\widehat{(h*1_{U})}(\xi)=\widehat{h}(\xi)\cdot\widehat{1_{U}}(\xi)=\widehat{h}(\xi)\cdot|U|\cdot 1_{\{\xi\in U^{\perp}\}}=2^{d}\cdot\widehat{h}(\xi)\cdot 1_{\{\xi\in U^{\perp}\}}.

Applying Parseval’s identity gives

\mathbb{E}[H_{U}(x)^{2}]=2^{-n}\sum_{x}H_{U}(x)^{2}=2^{-2n}\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\widehat{H_{U}}(\xi)^{2}=2^{2d-2n}\cdot\sum_{\xi\in U^{\perp}}\widehat{h}(\xi)^{2}.

Thus we have

\mathbb{P}[H_{U}(x)=0]\leqslant 1-\frac{2^{2d-2n}}{2^{2d}}\sum_{\xi\in U^{\perp}}\widehat{h}(\xi)^{2}=1-2^{-2n}\sum_{\xi\in U^{\perp}}\widehat{h}(\xi)^{2}.

Recall that we can sample a uniformly random
Q\in\textup{Aff}(n,d)
by first choosing
U\in\textup{Gr}(n,d)
uniformly at random, and then choosing
x
uniformly from
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and setting
Q=x+U
. So we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}(n,d,2^{d-1},A)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{Q\sim\textup{Aff}(n,d)}[|A\cap Q|=2^{d-1}]=\mathbb{E}_{U}[\mathbb{P}_{x}[|A\cap(x+U)|=2^{d-1}]]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}[\mathbb{P}_{x}[H_{U}(x)=0]]\leqslant 1-2^{-2n}\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\widehat{h}(\xi)^{2}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{U}[\xi\in U^{\perp}],

where the last inequality comes from exchanging the order of expectation and summation.

For a uniformly random
U\in\textup{Gr}(n,d)
, if
\xi\neq 0
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{U}[\xi\in U^{\perp}]=\frac{\binom{n-1}{d}_{2}}{\binom{n}{d}_{2}};

and if
\xi=0
, we have
\mathbb{P}_{U}[0\in U^{\perp}]=1
. Therefore, we have
\mathbb{P}_{U}[\xi\in U^{\perp}]\geqslant\binom{n-1}{d}_{2}/\binom{n}{d}_{2}
.

Furthermore, by Parseval’s identity on
h
, we have

\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\hat{h}(\xi)^{2}=2^{n}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}h(x)^{2}=2^{n}\cdot 2^{n}=2^{2n}.

Thus, we can conclude that

\lambda^{*}(n,d,2^{d-1},A)\leqslant 1-2^{-2n}\cdot\frac{\binom{n-1}{d}_{2}}{\binom{n}{d}_{2}}\cdot\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\hat{h}(\xi)^{2}=1-2^{-2n}\cdot 2^{2n}\cdot\frac{\binom{n-1}{d}_{2}}{\binom{n}{d}_{2}}=1-\frac{\binom{n-1}{d}_{2}}{\binom{n}{d}_{2}}.

Consequently, we have

\lambda^{*}(d,2^{d-1})\leqslant\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{\binom{n-1}{d}_{2}}{\binom{n}{d}_{2}}\right)=1-2^{-d}.

Combined with the lower bound construction presented previously, we can conclude that
\lambda^{*}(d,2^{d-1})=1-2^{-d}
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

In fact, the proof of
Theorem

1.4
shows that for any subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with size
|A|\neq 2^{n-1}
we have
\lambda^{*}(n,d,2^{d-1},A)<1-\binom{n-1}{d}_{2}/\binom{n}{d}_{2}
. Indeed, since
\hat{h}(0)=2^{n}-2|A|
, we have
\hat{h}(0)^{2}>0
if
|A|\neq 2^{n-1}
.

3.2.
The case
s=j\cdot 2^{k}

In this subsection, we present the proof of
Theorem

1.1
. We first prove a weaker upper bound for
\lambda^{*}(d,s)
for all
s=j\cdot 2^{k}
with
j
odd and
k\geqslant 0
stated in the following proposition. In particular, the following proposition already gives the correct asymptotics for
1-\lambda^{*}(d,s)
for even
s
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
d\geqslant 1
and
1<s<2^{d}
and
n\geqslant d+1
. Suppose
s=j\cdot 2^{k}
where
j
is odd and
k\geqslant 0
, then for any
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
we have

\lambda^{*}(n,d,s,A)\leqslant 1-\frac{2^{d-k}-1}{2^{d+1}-1}.

Note that
3.3
already gives a slightly weaker upper bound on
\lambda^{*}(d,s)
since we have

\lambda^{*}(d,s)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\max_{A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\lambda^{*}(n,d,s,A)\leqslant 1-\frac{2^{d-k}-1}{2^{d+1}-1}=1-2^{-(k+1)}+O(2^{-(d-k)}).

In particular, this already determines the correct asymptotics for
1-\lambda^{*}(d,s)
where
s=j\cdot 2^{k}
with
j
odd and
k\geqslant 1
. Our proof is an averaging argument over
(d+1)
-flats.

Proof of
3.3
.

Fix
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and
s=j\cdot 2^{k}
where
j
is odd and
k\geqslant 0
. For each
(d+1)
-flat
F\in\textup{Aff}(n,d+1)
, we define the local statistics

\lambda_{F}:=\mathbb{P}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subset F\\
\dim Q=d\end{subarray}}[|Q\cap A|=s],

where the probability is over a uniformly random
d
-flat
Q
contained in
F
. Note that by definition, we have

\lambda^{*}(n,d,s,A)=\mathbb{E}_{F\sim\textup{Aff}(n,d+1)}[\lambda_{F}],

where the expectation is over a uniformly random
(d+1)
-flat
F
. Therefore, it suffices to upper bound
\lambda_{F}
for every
F\in\textup{Aff}(n,d+1)
. We will show that for every fixed
F\in\textup{Aff}(n,d+1)
, we have

\lambda_{F}\leqslant 1-\frac{2^{d-k}-1}{2^{d+1}-1}.

Fix any
F\in\textup{Aff}(n,d+1)
and let
S=F\cap A
. From now on, we identify
F
with
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1}
. For
\xi\neq 0\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1}
, we define the hyperplanes
H_{\xi,0}=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1}\mid\langle\xi,x\rangle=0\}
and
H_{\xi,1}=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1}\mid\langle\xi,x\rangle=1\}
.
Then note that
H_{\xi,0}
and
H_{\xi,1}
are two disjoint parallel
d
-flats whose union is
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1}
. Thus we have

|S|=|S\cap H_{\xi,0}|+|S\cap H_{\xi,1}|.

Now we bound
\lambda_{F}
by distinguishing two cases depending on whether
|S|=2s
.

Case 1:
|S|\neq 2s
.
In this case, notice that in every possible hyperplane direction
\xi\neq 0\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1}
, at most one of
|S\cap H_{\xi,0}|
and
|S\cap H_{\xi,1}|
can be equal to
s
. Therefore, it is clear that
\lambda_{F}\leqslant 1/2
. Since
1-\frac{2^{d-k}-1}{2^{d+1}-1}\geqslant 1/2
for all
d>k\geqslant 0
, this case is proved.

Case 2:
|S|=2s
.
In this case, for every hyperplane direction
\xi\neq 0\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1}
, either both
|S\cap H_{\xi,0}|
and
|S\cap H_{\xi,1}|
are equal to
s
, or neither of them are equal to
s
. Therefore it suffices to show a lower bound for the number of
\xi\neq 0
such that
|S\cap H_{\xi,0}|
and
|S\cap H_{\xi,1}|
are both not equal to
s
. For convenience, we denote
B_{F}=\{\xi\neq 0\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1}\mid|S\cap H_{\xi,0}|\neq s\}
and note that

\lambda_{F}=1-\frac{|B_{F}|}{2^{d+1}-1},

where the
2^{d+1}-1
in the denominator is the number of directions
\xi\neq 0\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1}
of the hyperplanes. Let
f:=1_{S}
be the indicator function of
S
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1}
.

Claim 3.4
.

We have
B_{F}=\textup{supp}(\hat{f})\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

Notice that by definition, for
\xi\neq 0\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1}
we have

\hat{f}(\xi)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1}}1_{S}(x)(-1)^{\langle\xi,x\rangle}=|S\cap H_{\xi,0}|-|S\cap H_{\xi,1}|.

Then indeed
|S\cap H_{\xi,0}|=|S\cap H_{\xi,1}|=s
if and only if
\hat{f}(\xi)=0
. Thus we can conclude that
B_{F}=\textup{supp}(\hat{f})\setminus\{0\}
.
∎

Now it suffices to lower bound
\textup{supp}(\hat{f})
. The crucial observation is that
\textup{supp}(\hat{f})
is a blocking set with respect to projective
(k+1)
-subspaces in
\textup{PG}(d,2)
, as shown by the following claim.

Claim 3.5
.

Let
L\subseteq F\cong\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d+1}
be a
t
-dimensional linear subspace for some
0\leqslant t\leqslant d
. If
\hat{f}(\xi)=0
for all
\xi\in L\setminus\{0\}
, then every coset of
L^{\perp}
contains exactly
|S|/2^{t}
points of
S
. In particular,
|S|
is divisible by
2^{t}
.

Proof.

Consider the orthogonal complement
L^{\perp}
of codimension
t
and note that
F
is a disjoint union of
2^{t}
cosets of
L^{\perp}
. For each coset
a+L^{\perp}
where
a\in F/L^{\perp}
, we define

c(a)=|S\cap(a+L^{\perp})|.

Note that we have
|S|=\sum_{a\in F/L^{\perp}}c(a)
.

Fix
\xi\in L
, note that the value
(-1)^{\langle\xi,x\rangle}
is constant on each coset of
L^{\perp}
. Indeed, for
x
and
x^{\prime}
lying in the same coset, we have
x-x^{\prime}\in L^{\perp}
. So we must have
\langle\xi,x^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\xi,x\rangle+\langle\xi,x^{\prime}-x\rangle=\langle\xi,x\rangle
. Therefore, we can rewrite
\hat{f}(\xi)
as

\hat{f}(\xi)=\sum_{a\in F/L^{\perp}}\sum_{x\in S\cap(a+L^{\perp})}(-1)^{\langle\xi,x\rangle}=\sum_{a\in F/L^{\perp}}c(a)(-1)^{\langle\xi,a\rangle}.

By assumption, for every
\xi\neq 0\in L
, we have

\hat{f}(\xi)=\sum_{a\in F/L^{\perp}}c(a)(-1)^{\langle\xi,a\rangle}=0.

On the other hand,
\hat{f}(0)=\sum_{a\in F/L^{\perp}}c(a)=|S|
. Thus, for any fixed
a_{0}\in F/L^{\perp}
we have

\displaystyle|S|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in L}\hat{f}(\xi)(-1)^{\langle\xi,a_{0}\rangle}=\sum_{\xi\in L}\sum_{a\in F/L^{\perp}}c(a)(-1)^{\langle\xi,a\rangle}(-1)^{\langle\xi,a_{0}\rangle}=\sum_{\xi\in L}\sum_{a\in F/L^{\perp}}c(a)(-1)^{\langle\xi,a+a_{0}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in F/L^{\perp}}c(a)\sum_{\xi\in L}(-1)^{\langle\xi,a+a_{0}\rangle}.

Note that the inner sum
\sum_{\xi\in L}(-1)^{\langle\xi,a+a_{0}\rangle}
is
2^{t}
if
a=a_{0}
and
0
otherwise. Therefore we have

|S|=c(a_{0})\cdot 2^{t}.

Furthermore, the above holds for any arbitrary
a_{0}\in F/L^{\perp}
. So, we have
c(a)=|S|/2^{t}
for every
a\in F/L^{\perp}
. On the other hand, since
c(a)=|S\cap(a+L^{\perp})|
is an integer, we have
|S|
must be divisible by
2^{t}
.
∎

For any
(k+2)
-dimensional subspace
L
, by
3.5
, we must have
B_{F}\cap(L\setminus\{0\})\neq\varnothing
since
2s
is not divisible by
2^{k+2}
. In particular,
B_{F}
is a blocking set with respect to
(k+1)
-subspaces in
\textup{PG}(d,2)
. Thus by
Theorem

2.2
, we have

|B_{F}|=|\textup{supp}(\hat{f})\setminus\{0\}|\geqslant 2^{d-k}-1.

Thus we can conclude

\lambda_{F}\leqslant 1-\frac{2^{d-k}-1}{2^{d+1}-1},

for the case where
|S|=2s
. This concludes the proof
∎

Now we are ready to prove
Theorem

1.1
. The proof proceeds by bootstrapping the upper bound given in
3.3
. More specifically, in the proof of
3.3
, we upper bound
\lambda^{*}(d,s)
by upper bounding the local statistics
\lambda_{F}
for
(d+1)
-flats
F
. We distinguish two cases based on whether
|A\cap F|=2s
and then we simply took the maximum of the two upper bounds instead of computing the average of
\lambda_{F}
over all
F\in\textup{Aff}(n,d+1)
. The point is that we can upper bound the fraction of
F\in\textup{Aff}(n,d+1)
such that
|A\cap F|=2s
using
\lambda^{*}(d+1,2s)
. Since
3.3
gives an upper bound for all
d^{\prime}
and even
s^{\prime}
, we can iterate this argument and further improve on the constants for
1-\lambda^{*}(d,s)
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

We will show that

(1)

\lambda^{*}(d,s)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2^{d-k}-1}{2^{d+i+1}-1}\right).

Note that the above expression evaluates to
1-\frac{2}{3}(1-2^{-(d-k)})\cdot 2^{-k}+O(2^{-2k})+o_{d}(1)
. For convenience, let
a_{i}=\frac{2^{d-k}-1}{2^{d+i+1}-1}
and note that we have

a_{i}=\frac{2^{d-k}-1}{2^{d+i+1}-1}=(1-2^{-(d-k)})2^{-k-i-1}+O(2^{-2k-(d-k)-2i}).

So we have

\lambda^{*}(d,s)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{1}{2}(1-2a_{i})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{-m}\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(1-2a_{i}).

Since for all
m
, we have
\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}a_{i}\leqslant 2^{-k+1}
, we have

\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(1-2a_{i})=1-2\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}a_{i}+O(2^{-2k}).

Thus we have

\lambda^{*}(d,s)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{-m}-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{-m}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}a_{i}+O(2^{-2k}).

Note that the first sum is equal to
2
and by changing the order of summation, the second sum evaluates to

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}\sum_{m=i+1}^{\infty}2^{-m}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}\cdot 2^{-i}=(1-2^{-(d-k)})2^{-k-1}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}4^{-i}=\frac{2}{3}(1-2^{-(d-k)})2^{-k}.

Thus we can conclude that for fixed
k
we have

\lambda^{*}(d,s)\leqslant 1-\frac{2}{3}(1-2^{-(d-k)})\cdot 2^{-k}+O(2^{-2k})+o_{d}(1).

as desired.

Now we show
Eq.

1
. For each
t\geqslant 0
, we define
s_{t}:=2^{t}\cdot s=j\cdot 2^{k+t}
and define
\lambda_{t}:=\lambda^{*}(d+t,s_{t})=\lambda^{*}(d+t,j\cdot 2^{k+t})
. We will derive a recurrence for
\lambda_{t}
by applying the same analysis in the proof of
3.3
to
(d+t+1)
-flats and
s_{t}
. Fix
t\geqslant 0
and
n\geqslant d+t+1
, and let
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. Define for each
(d+t+1)
-flat
F\in\textup{Aff}(n,d+t+1)
the local statistics

\lambda_{F}^{(t)}:=\mathbb{P}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subseteq F\\
\dim Q=d+t\end{subarray}}[|Q\cap A|=s_{t}],

where the probability is over a uniformly random
(d+t)
-flat contained in
F
. It follows by averaging over all
(d+t+1)
-flats that we have

\lambda^{*}(n,d+t,s_{t},A)=\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{F}^{(t)}].

Now fix
F\in\textup{Aff}(n,d+t+1)
and let
S:=F\cap A
. We also distinguish two cases depending on whether
|S|=2s_{t}
. By the same argument as in proof of
3.3
, we obtain the following:

•

If
|S|\neq 2s_{t}
, we have
\lambda_{F}^{(t)}\leqslant 1/2
.

•

If
|S|=2s_{t}
, we have

\lambda_{F}^{(t)}\leqslant 1-\frac{2^{(d+t)-(k+t)}-1}{2^{d+t+1}-1}=1-\frac{2^{d-k}-1}{2^{d+t+1}-1}.

Thus we have

\lambda^{*}(n,d+t,s_{t},A)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}[|F\cap A|\neq 2s_{t}]+\left(1-\frac{2^{d-k}-1}{2^{d+t+1}-1}\right)\mathbb{P}[|F\cap A|=2s_{t}],

where
F
is a uniformly random
(d+t+1)
-flat. Note that since
\mathbb{P}[|F\cap A|=2s_{t}]=\lambda^{*}(n,d+t+1,s_{t+1},A)
, we obtain

\lambda^{*}(n,d+t,s_{t},A)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2^{d-k}-1}{2^{d+t+1}-1}\right)\lambda^{*}(n,d+t+1,s_{t+1},A).

For convenience, let
c_{t}:=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2^{d-k}-1}{2^{d+t+1}-1}
. Then taking the maximum over all
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and taking
n\to\infty
, we get the recurrence

\lambda_{t}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+c_{t}\lambda_{t+1}.

Note that
0\leqslant\lambda_{t}\leqslant 1
for all
t\geqslant 0
and we have
\lambda_{0}=\lambda^{*}(d,s)
, so it now suffices to show that
Eq.

1
is the solution to
\lambda_{0}
.

For any integer
m\geqslant 1
, by repeated substitution, we get

\lambda_{0}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}c_{i}+\left(\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}c_{i}\right)\lambda_{m}.

To see this, we induct on
m
. The base case
m=1
is exactly the recurrence for
\lambda_{0}
.
Assuming the inequality holds for
m
, we apply the recurrence for
\lambda_{m}
and obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}c_{i}+\left(\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}c_{i}\right)\lambda_{m}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}c_{i}+\left(\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}c_{i}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}+c_{m}\lambda_{m+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}c_{i}+\left(\prod_{i=0}^{m}c_{i}\right)\lambda_{m+1}.

Note that for all
t\geqslant 0
, we have

0<c_{t}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2^{d-k}-1}{2^{d+t+1}-1}<\frac{1}{2}.

Since
0\leqslant\lambda_{m}\leqslant 1
, we have

0\leqslant\left(\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}c_{i}\right)\lambda_{m}\leqslant\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}c_{i}\leqslant 2^{-m}\to 0.

Thus, taking
m\to\infty
, we obtain

\lambda^{*}(d,s)=\lambda_{0}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}c_{i},

which is exactly
Eq.

1
.

∎

Together with the lower bound in
1.2
, for
s=j\cdot 2^{k}
for odd
j
and

, we have

\lambda^{*}(d,s)=1-\Theta(2^{-k}).

This shows that in the flat model, the
2
-adic valuation
\nu_{2}(s)
controls the statistics
\lambda^{*}(d,s)
. However, the behavior is likely different for the axis-aligned cube statistics as illustrated in the case where
s
is odd.

4.
s
odd

By
1.2
we know that if
s>1
is odd, then
\lambda^{*}(d,s)\geqslant c(d,0)
where
c_{d}\to 0.2887
as

. On the other hand,

1.1
implies that when

as

. In fact, for

odd, we have an alternative simpler proof of an upper bound of

. We prove

by proving the following stronger statement.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and
1\leqslant d<n
, for a uniformly random
d
-flat
F\in\textup{Aff}(n,d)
we have

\mathbb{P}[|A\cap F|\text{ is odd}]\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2(2^{n-d+1}-1)}.

Proof.

Fix
U\in\textup{Gr}(n,d-1)
and note that there are
2^{n-d+1}
affine
(d-1)
-flats parallel to
U
given by
x+U
where
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}/U
. Then for convenience, define for each

the value

to be the parity of
|A\cap(x+U)|
, i.e.
|A\cap(x+U)|\pmod{2}
.

Notice that for a
d
-flat
F=(x+U)\sqcup(y+U)
for distinct
x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}/U
, we have that
|A\cap F|\equiv p(x)+p(y)\pmod{2}
and thus
|A\cap F|
is even if and only if
p(x)=p(y)
. Let
m
be the number of
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}/U
such that
p(x)=1
, i.e. the number of affine
(d-1)
-flats parallel to
U
that intersect
A
in an odd number of points and for convenience let
N=2^{n-d+1}
. We sample a uniformly random
d
-flat
F
by first sampling a uniformly random
U\in\textup{Gr}(n,d-1)
, then sample distinct
x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}/U
, and setting
F=(x+U)\sqcup(y+U)
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|A\cap F|\text{ is even}\mid U]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{N}{2}}\cdot\left(\binom{m}{2}+\binom{N-m}{2}\right)=\frac{m(m-1)+(N-m)(N-m-1)}{N(N-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{N(N-1)-2m(N-m)}{N(N-1)}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2(N-1)}.

Thus we have

\mathbb{P}[|A\cap F|\text{ is odd}\mid U]\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2(2^{n-d+1}-1)}.

Averaging over all possible
U
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

Theorem

1.3
follows directly since for odd
s
and any
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
we have

\lambda^{*}(n,d,s,A)=\mathbb{P}_{F\in\textup{Aff}(n,d)}[|A\cap F|=s]\leqslant\mathbb{P}_{F\in\textup{Aff}(n,d)}[|A\cap F|\text{ is odd}]\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2(2^{n-d+1}-1)}.

Taking
n\to\infty
shows that
\lambda^{*}(d,s)\leqslant 1/2
.

Remark 4.2
.

Note that
Lemma

4.1
does not hold in the axis-aligned cube setting. In particular, one can construct
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
such that every
d
-subcube intersects
A
in an odd number of points. Consider the degree-
d
symmetric polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
defined by

f(x)=\sum_{T\in\binom{[n]}{d}}\prod_{i\in T}x_{i}.

Let
A=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\mid f(x)=1\}
. Fix any
d
-subcube
Q\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and note that it can be written as

Q=\{a+u_{1}e_{i_{1}}+\dots+u_{d}e_{i_{d}}\mid u_{1},\dots,u_{d}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}\}

with distinct
i_{1},\dots,i_{d}\in[n]
and
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
satisfying
a_{i_{1}}=\dots=a_{i_{d}}=0
. We claim that in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, we have

\sum_{u\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}}f(a+u_{1}e_{i_{1}}+\dots+u_{d}e_{i_{d}})=1.

For convenience, we denote for each
T\in\binom{[n]}{d}
, its corresponding monomial as
g_{T}(x)=\prod_{i\in T}x_{i}
. We compute the contribution from each monomial. Note that if
T\neq\{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}\}
, then there exists
r\in[d]
such that
i_{r}\not\in T
. Therefore summing over
u\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
, each value appears twice and thus contribute
0
to the sum over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. In the case where
T=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}\}
, since
a_{i_{1}}=\dots=a_{i_{d}}=0
, we have
g_{T}(a+u_{1}e_{i_{1}}+\dots+u_{d}e_{i_{d}})=u_{1}\dots u_{d}
which takes value
1
only when
u_{1}=\dots=u_{d}=1
. This proves the claim and thus each
d
-subcube contains an odd number of points from
A
.

Finally, we present the proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

Fix
0\leqslant s\leqslant 2^{d}
and let
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a subset that achieves
\lambda^{*}(n,d,s,A)=\lambda^{*}(n,d,s)
.

For
2\leqslant s\leqslant 2^{d}
, consider a subset
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
obtained from randomly deleting each point in
A
with probability
1/s
independently. Then for a uniformly random
d
-flat
Q
, we have

\mathbb{P}[|A^{\prime}\cap Q|=s-1\mid|A\cap Q|=s]=s\cdot\frac{1}{s}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{s}\right)^{s-1}=\left(1-\frac{1}{s}\right)^{s-1}=\left(\frac{s-1}{s}\right)^{s-1}.

Thus we have

\mathbb{P}[|A^{\prime}\cap Q|=s-1]\geqslant\left(\frac{s-1}{s}\right)^{s-1}\cdot\mathbb{P}[|A\cap Q|=s]=\lambda^{*}(n,d,s)\left(\frac{s-1}{s}\right)^{s-1}.

Fixing an outcome of
A^{\prime}
that achieves the above inequality, we can conclude that

\lambda^{*}(n,d,s-1)\geqslant\lambda^{*}(n,d,s-1,A^{\prime})\geqslant\lambda^{*}(n,d,s)\left(\frac{s-1}{s}\right)^{s-1}.

Taking
n\to\infty
implies the desired statement.

Similarly, for
0\leqslant s\leqslant 2^{d}-2
, consider the set
A^{\prime\prime}\supseteq A
obtained from randomly adding each point in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus A
to
A
with probability
1/(2^{d}-s)
independently. Then for a uniformly random
d
-flat
Q
, we have

\mathbb{P}[|A^{\prime\prime}\cap Q|=s+1\mid|A\cap Q|=s]=\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{d}-s}\right)^{2^{d}-s-1}=\left(\frac{2^{d}-s-1}{2^{d}-s}\right)^{2^{d}-s-1}.

Thus we have

\mathbb{P}[|A^{\prime\prime}\cap Q|=s+1]\geqslant\left(\frac{2^{d}-s-1}{2^{d}-s}\right)^{2^{d}-s-1}\cdot\mathbb{P}[|A\cap Q|=s]=\lambda^{*}(n,d,s)\left(\frac{2^{d}-s-1}{2^{d}-s}\right)^{2^{d}-s-1}.

Fixing an outcome of
A^{\prime\prime}
that achieves the above inequality, we can conclude that

\lambda^{*}(n,d,s+1)\geqslant\lambda^{*}(n,d,s+1,A^{\prime\prime})\geqslant\lambda^{*}(n,d,s)\left(\frac{2^{d}-s-1}{2^{d}-s}\right)^{2^{d}-s-1}.

Taking
n\to\infty
implies the desired statement.
∎

5.
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we study the intersection statistics of affine subspaces over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
as a natural coordinate-invariant analogue of the axis-aligned cube statistics introduced by Alon, Axenovich, and Goldwasser
[
1
]
. By replacing the axis-aligned
d
-subcubes with all affine
d
-flats, the problem acquires full affine symmetry. This change turns out to have substantial consequences for the behavior of intersection statistics. In this section, we present some natural follow-up questions as well as discussions for the flat statistics versus the cube statistics.

5.1.
Determine exact values for
\lambda^{*}(d,s)

Currently the only cases for
s
where
\lambda^{*}(d,s)
is completely determined are when
s\in\{0,2^{d-1},2^{d}\}
, so determining the exact values for
\lambda^{*}(d,s)
for most values of
s
remains an interesting open problem. In
Theorem

1.1
, we showed that for
s=j\cdot 2^{k}
with
j
odd and
k\geqslant 1
, we have

\lambda^{*}(d,s)=1-\Theta(2^{-k}),

which determines
1-\lambda^{*}(d,s)
for all even
s
up to an absolute constant. In particular, the lower bound is roughly
1-2^{-k}
and the upper bound is roughly
1-\frac{2}{3}\cdot 2^{-k}
as
d\to\infty
, so the constants in front of the
2^{-k}
term differ by a multiplicative factor of
2/3
. It remains an interesting question to determine the exact leading constant. We conjecture that the lower bound is the correct answer.

Conjecture 5.1
.

For
d\geqslant 1
and
s=j\cdot 2^{k}
with
j
odd and
1\leqslant k\leqslant d
, we have

\lambda^{*}(d,s)=(1+o_{d}(1))c(d,k).

We consider the case where
k
is fixed and
d\to\infty
in the above conjecture. We suspect that in order to prove
5.1
, one would need to prove a strong structural statement that the extremizer of
\lambda^{*}(d,s)
must be close to a union of
j
flats of codimension
d-k
. Our current proof does not give any structural information on the set
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
achieving
\lambda^{*}(n,d,s)
, so new ideas are required.

In the case where
s
is odd, we currently have the general bound

0.2887\leqslant\lambda^{*}(d,s)\leqslant 0.5.

In the case where
s=2^{k}\pm 1
for
k
large,
Corollary

1.6
implies that
\lambda^{*}(d,s)\geqslant c(d,k)/e\geqslant(1-2^{-k})/e
which approaches
1/e
as
k\to\infty
. However, there remains a big gap in the current lower bound and upper bound for all values of odd
s
. Similar to the axis-aligned setting conjectured in
[
1
]
, we conjecture that for
s=1
, the optimal construction should be Poisson, which is also consistent with
Theorem

1.5
.

Conjecture 5.2
.

For
d\geqslant 1
, we have

\lambda^{*}(d,1)=(1+o_{d}(1))\cdot\frac{1}{e}.

5.2.
Affine flat statistics versus cube statistics

Our study also provides insights into the hypercube statistics problem. Although we showed that the 2-adic valuation of
s
controls
\lambda^{*}(d,s)
, several observations imply that the same cannot be true in the axis-aligned cube setting. First, even for parity, analogous behavior fails in the axis-aligned cube setting as illustrated in
Section

4
. We also point out two constructions observed in
[
1
]
that give evidence that one should exploit the fixed coordinates in the cube statistics problem.

In
[
1
]
, it was observed that if one takes
A\subseteq Q_{n}
consisting of every third layer of
Q_{n}
, then in the case where
s\in\{\lceil 2^{d}/3\rceil,\lfloor 2^{d}/3\rfloor\}
, we have
\lambda(d,s)\geqslant 2/3-o_{d}(1)
. This is a significantly better lower bound than
0.2887
in the case when
s
is odd. Moreover, this surpasses the
1/2
upper bound for
\lambda^{*}(d,s)
for these values of
d
and
s
, suggesting very different behaviors for
\lambda^{*}(d,s)
and
\lambda(d,s)
.

Another example observed in
[
1
]
is the following. When
s=2^{d-2}
, consider
A=B^{-1}(x)
where
B:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2}
and
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2}
. If we take
B
to be described by a random
2\times n
matrix with independent nonzero column vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2}
, then notice that
\lambda(n,d,s,A)
is at least
1-3^{1-d}
and thus we get that
\lambda(d,2^{d-2})\geqslant 1-3^{1-d}
. This is an asymptotically better lower bound than
\lambda^{*}(d,2^{d-2})=c(d,d-2)
which is on the order of
1-2^{-(d-2)}
. These examples suggest that the cube statistics is heavily dependent on the fixed coordinates and exhibits genuinely different behavior than affine flat statistics.
