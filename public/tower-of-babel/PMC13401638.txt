Combination Therapy, Drug Interactions, and Survival Outcomes in Metastatic Breast Cancer: A Pooled Analysis of Phase II Trials
Authors: Faruque Azam, Tanisha Momtaz, Farzana Islam, Kazi Fatema Rahman, Sohana Shobnam, Pulock Paul, Md. Jawat Alam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relationship between regimen complexity, treatment sequencing, and survival outcomes in metastatic breast cancer (MBC) remains poorly characterized.ObjectivesTo quantify how treatment complexity, line of therapy, and baseline performance status (PS) differentially affect progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS), and to identify synergistic and antagonistic drug combinations across MBC phase II trials.MethodsA systematic PubMed search (1992–2022) identified 892 eligible studies comprising 1,088 treatment arms and 53,672 patients. Objective response rate (ORR), median PFS, and median OS were extracted per arm along with molecular subtype, treatment size, therapy type, line of therapy, and weighted ECOG PS. Drug-drug interactions were inferred using a null model of non-interacting agents.ResultsAdding agents improved both PFS and OS, but the PFS benefit was approximately twice the OS benefit (effect size, ε2 = 0.24 vs. 0.11; both p < 0.0001). Combination therapy progressively reduced the survival disadvantage associated with prior treatment, with PFS effect sizes declining from rrb = 0.55 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.44–0.64) for single agents to 0.27 (0.09–0.44) for triplets. OS followed a similar pattern, decreasing from rrb = 0.58 (0.48–0.66) to 0.32 (0.14–0.48). ECOG PS showed an escalating correlation, from negligible for ORR (rs = −0.09, 95% CI −0.15 to −0.03) to medium for PFS (−0.23, −0.29 to −0.16) and very large for OS (−0.41, −0.48 to −0.35). Nine combinations showed response-based synergy, eight of which were cytotoxic doublets involving anthracycline-taxane or alkylator-anthracycline pairings; five pairs showed response-based antagonism with no consistent drug-class pattern.ConclusionCombination therapy confers a substantially larger benefit for PFS than OS and partially compensates for the treatment-line penalty in patients with pretreated MBC. Patient fitness appears to become increasingly critical for long-term survival and may help guide regimen planning.

Introduction

Metastatic breast cancer (MBC) remains an incurable disease with a median overall survival (OS) of approximately two to three years.1,2 Treatment is guided by molecular subtype—hormone receptor–positive/HER2-negative (HR+/HER2−), HER2-positive (HER2+), and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)—each following distinct therapeutic pathways.3,4 The treatment landscape has expanded substantially over the past decade, with CDK4/6 inhibitors transforming first-line HR+/HER2− disease, antibody-drug conjugates redefining HER2+ management, PARP inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors entering the TNBC treatment options.3,5 The increase in effective agents has produced many possible treatment combinations, but survival time decline with each new line of therapy as resistance builds up.6,7 Nevertheless, the rapidly expanding number of possible combinations has outpaced the capacity of randomized trials to test them, so many clinically used regimens remain incompletely evaluated.

Phase II trials are essential for assessing new agents and combinations before large-scale confirmatory testing, with randomized design is preferred for time-to-event endpoints.8,9 These trials primarily use early efficacy endpoints such as objective response rate (ORR) and/or progression-free survival (PFS), enabling quick assessment across various regimens, patient populations, and treatment settings. 9 Since phase II designs are more common, quicker to finish, and require fewer resources than phase III trials. 8 they collectively cover a wide range of drug combinations and treatment lines.

Previous work has shown that this large body of phase II evidence can be pooled at scale to reveal higher-level signals: Kang et al 10 aggregated 1,163 response rates across cancer types to infer drug-drug synergy and antagonism, and later Azam and Vazquez 11 extended this approach to over 2,100 response rates, identifying both synergistic and antagonistic combinations. But apparent benefits observed in combination trials are not necessarily driven by true drug-drug interaction, Palmer and Sorger 12 argued that effects of many reported cancer drug combinations can be explained by independent drug action rather than synergy or additivity. Nonetheless, these analyses relied exclusively across multiple cancer types and were not designed to address MBC as a specific disease context.

Beyond the difficulty of evaluating combinations, the endpoints used to assess treatment efficacy have inherent limitations. PFS has become the primary endpoint in MBC trials, yet its relationship with OS remains uncertain. Trial-level analyses have found only moderate correlations between treatment effects on PFS and OS, 13 and a meta-analysis of 11 randomized-controlled trials (RCTs) concluded that neither PFS nor ORR could be validated as a reliable surrogate for OS in MBC. 14 These findings underscore the need for analyses that examine PFS and OS jointly across treatment categories rather than relying on a single endpoint.

While pooled analyses of trials exist for various cancer types, none have simultaneously evaluated the impact of regimen size, molecular subtype, therapy line, and performance status (PS) on efficacy endpoints in MBC trials. In this study, we address these gaps by pooling data from 1,088 treatment arms in published phase II MBC trials. We characterize efficacy patterns across monotherapy, doublet, and triplet regimens by subtype, evaluate how treatment size and PS interact with line of therapy for PFS and OS, and infer response-based synergistic and antagonistic combinations, generating hypotheses about which combinations merit further investigation and which warrant caution.

Methods

Study Design

This study employed an arm-level pooled analysis of efficacy outcomes reported in phase II clinical trials of advanced or MBC. Published trials were identified through a systematic search, and efficacy data from each treatment arm were extracted and analyzed as aggregate arm-level observations rather than individual patient data. The initial 2,000 records were assessed for eligibility. The present study focused on characterizing treatment patterns, quantifying the interplay between regimen complexity, treatment line, and molecular subtype, and inferring drug-drug interactions from the aggregate tumor response data.

The reporting of this pooled analysis of aggregate, arm-level data conforms to the MOOSE (Meta-analysis Of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. 15 This analysis was based exclusively on aggregate, arm-level data extracted from previously published phase II clinical trials in the public domain. As it involved no individual patient data, human participants, or human tissue/samples, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required.

Efficacy Outcomes

The primary efficacy endpoints extracted were median overall survival (mOS, in months), median progression-free survival (mPFS, in months), and objective response rate (ORR, as percentage). Median PFS and OS were recorded as reported or converted to months when originally presented in weeks or days. The ORR comprised complete and partial responses as per RECIST method for measuring response in solid tumors. 16

Explanatory Variables

The following variables were extracted or derived from each treatment arm to capture regimen-level and patient-level characteristics. Treatment-related variables — molecular subtype, treatment size, therapy type, and treatment line — were classified as defined in Supplementary Table S1. Weighted ECOG performance status (wECOG) was derived as a weighted average of the ECOG PS distribution reported within each treatment arm. For example, if a trial reported 60% ECOG 0 and 40% ECOG 1, the wECOG was calculated as “0.60 × 0 + 0.40 × 1 = 0.40”. This continuous metric enabled correlation analysis of baseline body function with efficacy endpoints. Trials/arms that did not report ECOG distribution were excluded from wECOG-related analyses. Drug names were recorded for each treatment arm to enable regimen-specific stratification and drug-drug interaction analysis.

Data Source and Search Strategy

In May 2023, the PubMed database was searched using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms combined with free-text keywords, including “advanced or metastatic”, “breast cancer”, and “phase II clinical trial”. Results were restricted using the publication date filter (1992–2022) and the “Clinical Trial” article type filter, yielding 3,383 records. Searches were run through the PubMed web interface, with automatic MeSH terms; no specialized bibliographic search software was used. Reference lists of retrieved articles were not hand-searched, and study authors were not contacted, as the analysis relied exclusively on data reported in the published articles.

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they were phase II single-arm or RCT evaluating the efficacy of anticancer agents in patients with advanced or metastatic breast cancer (MBC) and reporting at least one endpoint of interest (ORR, mPFS, or mOS). Exclusion criteria were: (i) phase I or phase III trial design; (ii) early-stage or non-metastatic phase II MBC; (iii) adjuvant, neoadjuvant, or radiotherapy containing phase II MBC trials; (iv) systematic reviews, meta-analyses, or pooled analyses of multiple phase II MBC trials; and (v) non-English publications (excluded to ensure accurate and consistent data extraction). Only full-text, peer-reviewed articles were eligible; conference abstracts and unpublished reports were not considered. After screening, 892 studies comprising 1,088 treatment arms met the eligibility criteria (Supplementary Figure S1). Efficacy endpoints and associated explanatory variables were extracted from each qualifying arm, and the final compiled dataset, assembled in February 2024, encompassed 2,622 efficacy observations representing 53,672 patients with advanced or metastatic breast cancer.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Data extraction was performed by two reviewers independently who screened and extracted data from the identified articles. The extracted information included molecular subtype, previous treatment status, number of participants, ORR, mPFS, mOS, treatment size, therapy type, drug names, ECOG PS distribution. An in-house extraction protocol was developed prior to data collection to ensure standardized and uniform coding across investigators. Discrepancies in extracted values were resolved through discussion with F.A. Following initial extraction, data preprocessing and compilation were performed by F.A. The curated dataset was then independently verified for consistency by T.M., F.I., K.F.R. Formal risk-of-bias or methodological quality assessment of individual trials was not performed, because the analysis used aggregate, arm-level summary data rather than individual patient data; eligibility was instead restricted to peer-reviewed phase II trials reporting predefined efficacy endpoints.

Statistical Analysis

Between-group comparisons of efficacy endpoints were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test (two groups) or Kruskal-Wallis test (three or more groups) at a two-sided significance level of p < 0.05. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s test with Holm adjustment to control overall Type I error rate. Effect sizes were computed to quantify the magnitude and practical significance of group differences beyond statistical significance. For ordinal comparisons across treatment size categories (1-agent, 2-agent, 3-agent), the epsilon-squared statistic (ε2) was used as the effect size measure for the Kruskal-Wallis test. For binary comparisons, the rank biserial correlation coefficient (rrb) was computed as the effect size for the Mann–Whitney U test. The association between weighted ECOG (wECOG) PS and each efficacy endpoint was assessed by Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ). The 95% CI for Spearman’s rank correlation (rs) was calculated using the Fieller et al correction. 17 The magnitudes of effect size and correlation coefficients were interpreted according to the benchmarks established in the “effectsize” R package. 18 Because outcomes were analyzed at the arm level rather than pooled into a single summary effect, no fixed or random-effects model was fitted and study-level variability was addressed through stratification by molecular subtype, treatment line, and therapy type, and through non-parametric methods and effect-size estimates with confidence intervals that make no homogeneity-of-variance assumptions. No formal sample-size calculation was performed, as the analysis included all eligible phase II trials identified within the search period rather than a sample enrolled to achieve a prespecified power.

Drug-drug interactions were inferred using the null model framework of non-interacting agents proposed by Kang et al. 10 This approach compares the observed tumor response of a combination against the response expected under the assumption that the constituent agents act independently. For a 2-agent regimen comprising drugs A and B, the expected ORR (ORRE) was derived from the probability of independent response as follows: ORRE=100%×[1‐(1‐ORRA/100%)×(1‐ORRB/100%)], where ORRA and ORRB represent the mean ORR across all treatment arms in which drugs A and B were administered as single agents, respectively. The observed ORR (ORRO) for the combination of drug A and drug B included mean ORR of studies where both drugs were tested together. Consequently, the ORRO of each combination was compared against the ORRE using a two-sided one-sample t test.

Combinations were classified as showing response-based synergy if the ORRO significantly exceeded the expected value (ORRO > ORRE; p < 0.05) and response-based antagonism if the ORRO fell significantly below it (ORRO < ORRE; p < 0.05). These terms denote statistical departures from the null model of independent drug action inferred from objective response rates, 10 and do not imply experimentally confirmed pharmacodynamic interaction. Only combinations with a minimum number of contributing treatment arms (n > 5) were retained for the interaction test to ensure sufficient statistical power. This null model was chosen because ORR is a bounded probability measure (0–1) for which the independence assumption yields a tractable and interpretable expected value.

All analyses were performed in R Statistical Software (version 4.5.1 or higher) using the following packages: “ggplot2″ for visualization, “ggstatsplot” for statistical graphics with embedded hypothesis tests, “effectsize” for effect size estimation and interpretation, and “gtsummary” for tabular summaries.

Results

HR-positive populations comprised the largest subtype category (49%), followed by mixed (30%), HER2-positive (14%), and TNBC (6.8%). Most arms enrolled pretreated patients (71%) with a weighted median ECOG of 0.52 (IQR: 0.38–0.71). Two-agent regimens were most common (47%), followed by single-agent (40%) and three-agent (13%). Subtype-specific Patterns of Regimens and Survival Outcomes

To examine how these survival estimates varied across molecular subtypes, we stratified treatment regimens by subtype and compared mPFS and mOS distributions (Figure 1). In HER2-positive disease, paclitaxel-trastuzumab (n=9) combination achieved the highest mPFS (∼10 months) and OS (∼28 months), while lapatinib monotherapy (n=5) consistently yielded the lowest values for both endpoints. In HR-positive disease, anthracycline-containing doublets, docetaxel-epirubicin (n=6) and docetaxel-doxorubicin (n=6), ranked highest for both mPFS and mOS. Endocrine monotherapies (fulvestrant, letrozole, exemestane) showed moderate mPFS but relatively preserved mOS, with fulvestrant ranking higher in mOS than its mPFS position. Cytotoxic agents (eribulin, ixabepilone, cisplatin-gemcitabine) occupied the lower end of both distributions. The Mixed category showed gemcitabine-paclitaxel (n=5) combination highest and doxorubicin monotherapy (n=5) lowest for both survival endpoints. Treatment Size and Survival Outcomes

After evaluating regimen performance across subtypes, we assessed the influence of the number of agents in combination on survival outcomes and quantified the corresponding effect size (Figure 2). Median PFS increased progressively from 4.4 months for 1-agent (n=431) to 7.1 months for 2-agent (n=514) and 10 months for 3-agent (n=143) trials. The medians between mPFS groups differ significantly with an ordinal trend and a large effect size (epsilon-squared, ε2 = 0.24, 95% CI [0.20, 1.00], p < 0.0001; Figure 2A). The median OS followed a parallel pattern: 14.1, 19, and 24.5 months for 1-agent, 2-agent, and 3-agent trials respectively, but with a medium effect size (ε2 = 0.11 [0.08, 1.00]; p < 0.0001; Figure 2B). The effect size for number of treatments was approximately twice as large for mPFS as for mOS (ε2 = 0.24 vs. 0.11). Interaction Between Treatment Size and Line of Therapy

Subsequently, we examined how line of therapy and treatment size influenced survival outcomes. First-line therapy outperformed pretreated settings across all agent categories, but the effect size of the treatment line diminished with increasing treatment size (Figure 3). For PFS, the median difference between first-line and pretreated patients was 3.0 months for single agents (7 vs. 4), 2.6 months for doublets (8.8 vs. 6.2), and around 2.0 months for triplet trials (10.6 vs. 8.65). These differences were statistically significant (Figure 3A). The biserial effect sizes for treatment line decreased consistently: rrb = 0.55 (95% CI 0.44, 0.64) indicating a very large effect for single agents, 0.47 (0.38, 0.55) also showing a very large effect for doublets, and 0.27 (0.09, 0.44) representing a medium effect for triplet trials (Figure 3A). The difference in OS between first-line and pretreated patients decreased from single agents to doublet therapies but increased slightly with triplet therapies (Figure 3B). For example, the first-line conferred a significant OS benefit of 9.3 months over pretreatment for 1-agent trials (22 vs. 12.7; p < 0.0001), around 6 months for doublets (22.9 vs. 17; p < 0.0001), and 7.5 months for triplets (27.6 vs. 20; p < 0.001). However, the effect size kept declining: from 0.58 (95% CI 0.48–0.66; very large) to 0.43 (0.34–0.51; very large), and then to 0.32 (0.14–0.48; large). ECOG Performance Status Correlations

Weighted ECOG performance status (wECOG) was significantly correlated with all three efficacy endpoints, but the strength of association escalated markedly across endpoints. The correlation with ORR was negative but negligible (rank correlation, rs = –0.09; 95% CI [−0.15, −0.03]; p = 0.003; n = 767). For PFS, the correlation was medium (rs = −0.23; [−0.29, −0.16]; p < 0.0001; n = 887). For OS, the correlation was very large (rs = −0.41; [−0.48, −0.35]; p < 0.0001; n = 661). The increasing gradient, minimal for ORR, moderate for PFS, and substantial for OS, shows that baseline PS has a growing prognostic effect throughout the disease course. Moreover, this finding has direct relevance to treatment planning: although baseline fitness minimally affects initial therapy response, it plays a growing role in the duration of disease control and survival.

Drug-Drug Interactions Based on Tumor Response

Given that treatment size significantly improved survival, we next investigated whether these benefits arose from additive or synergistic drug-drug interactions. Using a null model of non-interacting agents, nine combinations showed response-based synergy and five showed response-based antagonism (Table 2, Figure 4). Among synergistic pairs, doxorubicin-paclitaxel showed the largest absolute gain (expected 52.0%, observed 67.9%; p = 0.038), followed by cyclophosphamide-epirubicin (expected 50.9%, observed 67.3%; p = 0.012). The only triplet identified as synergistic was cyclophosphamide-docetaxel-doxorubicin (expected 65.3%, observed 74.0%; p = 0.029). Antagonistic interactions were most pronounced for lapatinib-vinorelbine, where observed ORR was less than half the expected value (expected 45.3%, observed 16.6%; p < 0.001). Other antagonistic combinations included abemaciclib-tamoxifen (expected 49.1%, observed 34.6%; p = 0.041), fulvestrant-neratinib (expected 38.8%, observed 28.4%; p = 0.006), and capecitabine-vinorelbine (expected 49.6%, observed 41.3%; p = 0.005). Discussion

This pooled analysis of 1,088 treatment arms examined how regimen complexity, treatment line, and performance status relate to efficacy endpoints in metastatic breast cancer. Our principal findings were that the PFS-to-OS effect attenuated selectively for regimen-level but not patient-level characteristics, that combination therapy partly offset the treatment-line penalty, and that baseline performance status showed an escalating association across ORR, PFS, and OS. We observed a twofold attenuation in effect size from PFS (ε2 = 0.24) to OS (ε2 = 0.11) for the treatment size comparison, yet this pattern did not extend to the treatment-line analysis, where PFS and OS effect sizes were comparable within each agent category (1-agent: rrb = 0.55 vs. 0.58; 2-agent: 0.47 vs. 0.43; 3-agent: 0.27 vs. 0.32). That the number of agents in a regimen shows a larger effect on PFS than OS is consistent with the established role of post-progression survival (PPS) as a confounder of between-regimen OS comparisons. Because a regimen acts only during the progression-free phase, while PPS reflects survival driven largely by subsequent lines of therapy, a long PPS adds a treatment-independent component to OS that is shared across comparison arms—diluting between-regimen OS differences and inflating their variance. Consistent with this, Broglio and Berry 19 showed in a simulation study that when median PPS is over 12 months—as is common in first-line MBC—the power to detect an OS benefit drops below 20%, even when PFS differences are large. Empirically, Saad et al 20 analyzed 76 phase III MBC trials (1998–2007) and found that PPS accounts for nearly two-thirds of OS, indicating that subsequent therapies substantially dilute between-regimen OS differences. Trial-level PFS-OS correlations have consistently been moderate—rank correlation of 0.69 in a meta-analysis 14 of 11 first-line MBC trials and a regression slope of 0.44 in HER2-positive MBC 21 —meaning OS effects were roughly half of PFS effects. But while these prior studies characterized PFS-OS attenuation as a general phenomenon, our data reveal that it operates selectively: the ∼2× attenuation applied to the regimen-level treatment size effect but was absent from the patient-level treatment-line effect. Saad et al 20 additionally reported that PPS varied little across treatment lines but differed significantly between hormone therapy and chemotherapy trials—an observation that directly reflects our findings. Therefore, the ∼2× attenuation observed for treatment size, but not for treatment line, suggests OS captures patient-level prognostic differences yet is less effective for regimen-level comparisons. This highlights the importance of choosing endpoints tailored to the specific treatment class.

The correlation between wECOG PS and efficacy outcomes escalated from negligible for ORR (rank correlation, rs = −0.09) through medium for PFS (rs = −0.23) to very large for OS (rs = −0.41). This escalating gradient indicates that baseline ECOG PS is a weak predictor of tumor response but an increasingly strong determinant of overall survival. That ECOG PS carries greater prognostic weight for OS than for PFS has been observed in individual trial-level analyses: in the CLEOPATRA trial, multivariate modeling identified ECOG as a significant prognostic factor for OS but not for PFS in HER2-positive MBC. 22 In advanced cancer more broadly, survival approximately halves with each worsening ECOG level, demonstrating an exponential rather than linear relationship. 23 A meta-analysis of 3,600 non-small cell lung cancer patients confirmed this endpoint-specific gradient quantitatively: ECOG PS ≥ 2 was associated with worse OS, PFS, and ORR, with the largest effect size observed for OS. 24 However, the endpoint-specific gradient we observed was negligible for ORR, moderate for PFS, substantial for OS; this has not been previously demonstrated in a pooled analysis of MBC trials. This escalation can be explained by the regimen-versus-patient-level framework: ORR mainly reflects immediate tumor response to treatment, PFS adds the influence of patient PS over time, and OS further includes factors like PPS and other health risks, which are more pronounced in patients with poor baseline fitness. Therefore, the increasing ECOG-outcome gradient from ORR to PFS to OS shows that prognostic factors accumulate across disease progression, supporting the selective attenuation framework and highlighting ECOG PS as a key confounder in efficacy comparisons between trials.

Our analysis indicated that the negative impact on survival linked to previous treatment exposure gradually declined with increasing regimen complexity, with PFS effect sizes declining from rrb = 0.55 for 1-agent to 0.47 for 2-agent and 0.27 for 3-agent trials. The existence of a treatment-line penalty itself is well established: in HER2-negative MBC, prospective data documented a monotonic PFS decline across successive treatment lines. 6 A Cochrane review pooling 43 trials with 9,742 women demonstrated that combination regimens confer a significant OS advantage over monotherapy, with improved PFS and response rates. 25 But neither of these analyses examined whether the treatment-line penalty itself is modulated by the number of agents—a question our data addresses directly. The strong ECOG-OS correlation (rs = −0.41) further suggests that PS deterioration across successive treatment lines may partly mediate the treatment-line penalty, since pretreated patients typically present with worse baseline fitness. Therefore, our finding that the treatment-line penalty attenuates with increasing treatment size provides novel evidence that multi-agent regimens may narrow the survival disadvantage of pretreated populations, a finding with direct implications for treatment sequencing decisions. Nonetheless, the contribution of declining PS to this penalty warrants further investigation through mediation analyses.

We identified a notable pattern in the drug-drug interaction analysis: eight of nine synergistic combinations consisted exclusively of cytotoxic pairs with bevacizumab-docetaxel being the sole exception involving a targeted agent. By contrast, the five antagonistic combinations showed no consistent class-based pattern. The predominance of cytotoxic synergy is consistent with cross-cancer evidence from a Phase II analysis of 2,165 ORRs that used the same null model approach and identified 4 synergistic and 10 antagonistic combinations. 11 However, the antagonism we identified for lapatinib-vinorelbine in HER2 subtype is particularly notable because it has direct clinical confirmation: Sim et al 26 conducted an RCT comparing lapatinib plus vinorelbine versus vinorelbine alone in HER2-positive MBC and found that adding lapatinib failed to demonstrate any benefit—median OS was numerically worse in the combination arm (15 vs. 18.9 months). Moreover, antagonism does not follow clear drug class patterns, as shown in a pooled analysis of 619 phase II trials where targeted therapy led to shorter OS than chemotherapy in 1-agent and 2-agent regimens. 27 Altogether, our analysis, combined with pooled cancer data and trial results, shows cytotoxic-cytotoxic drug combinations offer the most consistent response-based synergy, while antagonism is linked to certain drug pairs rather than broad drug classes.

In our analyzed trials, treatment-size categories differ substantially in their mix of cytotoxic, endocrine, and targeted agents. Because post-progression survival varies across these drug classes, it adds differential noise that selectively dilutes the OS signal when comparisons are made across treatment sizes. Our three main analyses point to a consistent explanation. The treatment-size effect (a regimen-level characteristic) was attenuated from PFS to OS; the treatment-line effect (a patient-level characteristic) was preserved across both endpoints; and the ECOG effect (also patient-level) was amplified for OS. Together, these results indicate that PFS-to-OS attenuation is not a uniform property of OS, but depends on whether the variable being studied is a property of the regimen or of the patient. This underscores the need to choose endpoints suited to the specific comparison, and cautions against assuming that PFS benefits translate proportionally into OS gains when comparing regimens of differing complexity.

We observed that the absolute OS difference between first-line and pretreated patients narrowed from 9.3 months for 1-agent trials to 6.0 months for 2-agent trials, but widened again to 7.5 months for 3-agent trials—even as the effect size continued to decline from rrb = 0.58 to 0.43 to 0.32. The gap between absolute difference and effect size for triplets indicates a ceiling effect, where higher regimen intensity yields smaller benefits due to toxicity limits. A systematic review 28 of 189 MBC RCTs showed that polychemotherapy conferred a statistically significant but modest survival advantage over monotherapy; however, adding more agents yields diminishing returns and may harm quality of life. A Cochrane review of 17 trials testing whether adding drugs to existing combinations improved outcomes found that more agents increased tumor response rates but also escalated toxicity without demonstrating a clear PFS or OS benefit, 29 the most direct evidence for a ceiling effect in regimen escalation. But the non-monotonic OS widening for triplets introduces an additional complexity: it may reflect selection bias where first-line triplets are reserved for patients with aggressive, high-burden disease, while pretreated patients receiving triplets face cumulative toxicity that limits post-progression therapy options. Because triplet regimens require good baseline PS, their patient populations are enriched for favorable ECOG scores, which independently predict longer OS regardless of treatment efficacy. In a pivotal trial, the capecitabine-docetaxel doublet improved OS over monotherapy but at the cost of markedly higher grade 3 adverse events (71% vs. 49%). 30 Therefore, the non-monotonic OS gap likely results from selection effects and ceiling effects, underscoring the need to examine both absolute differences and standardized effect sizes in treatment-line interactions.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, all analyses were performed at the treatment-arm level using aggregate summary statistics instead of individual patient data, which may lead to ecological bias as arm-level associations might not reflect individual outcomes. Consequently, the ECOG-outcome correlations and treatment size-survival relationships reported here should be interpreted as between-arm trends rather than within-patient effects, and causal inference is not possible from this design. Second, the drug-drug interaction analysis relied exclusively on ORR as the efficacy metric, because the null model framework requires a bounded probability measure; extending this approach to time-to-event endpoints such as PFS and OS would require fundamentally different statistical methodology. The resulting classifications are response-based statistical inferences—departures from an assumption of independent drug action—rather than experimentally or clinically validated pharmacodynamic interactions, and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating; combinations identified as showing response-based synergy or antagonism therefore require confirmation in dedicated pharmacodynamic or survival-based studies and may not exhibit the same patterns for survival outcomes. Third, publication bias is an inherent concern in any pooled analysis of published trials, as arms with unfavorable results may be underreported or selectively omitted, potentially inflating the overall efficacy estimates. Fourth, the exclusion of non-English publications may have introduced a degree of language bias, although the large number of included trials makes a material effect unlikely.

Further limitations concern the representativeness of the included data. The TNBC subtype comprised only 6.8% of the dataset, and no TNBC-specific regimen met the minimum study threshold for subtype-stratified analysis; consequently, the subtype-specific findings reported here are predominantly informed by HR+ and HER2+ disease. In addition, the inclusion period (1992–2022) spans distinct therapeutic eras, from pre-trastuzumab and early taxane use, through the CDK4/6 inhibitor era, to contemporary antibody-drug conjugates (ADC), so absolute survival estimates partly reflect evolving standards of care, and raw cross-era survival comparisons should be interpreted with caution. The principal findings, however, concern structural relationships between endpoints and regimen- or patient-level characteristics—the selective PFS-versus-OS attenuation, the diminishing treatment-line penalty with added agents, and the escalating ECOG–outcome gradient—rather than absolute survival magnitudes, and such relational patterns are less sensitive to temporal shifts in therapy; stratification by molecular subtype and therapy type further separates older cytotoxic-only regimens from modern targeted- and ADC-containing combinations. Finally, the “Mixed” subtype category aggregates treatment arms with heterogeneous or non-separable receptor statuses and may attenuate genuinely subtype-specific associations; it was nonetheless retained because it comprised approximately 30% of treatment arms, and excluding these arms would have substantially reduced statistical power for the treatment-size, treatment-line, performance status, and drug-interaction analyses, for which receptor subtype is not the variable of interest. Subtype-specific interpretations are therefore confined to the receptor-defined categories, whereas the Mixed group chiefly supports the regimen- and patient-level analyses. Despite these limitations, the large sample of 1,088 treatment arms and the consistency of findings across multiple independent analytical contrasts provide reasonable confidence in the robustness of the observed patterns. Nonetheless, these findings should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating: they describe associations at the level of trial arms and are not suitable for causal interpretation or inference about individual patients.

Conclusion

This pooled analysis of 892 phase II trials provides a quantitative framework for understanding how combination therapy shapes survival in MBC. The key finding is that treatment effect improves PFS about twice as much as OS, meaning combination trials using PFS as the main endpoint may overstate long-term survival benefits. Combination therapy also progressively reduces the survival disadvantage of prior treatment, supporting multi-agent regimens in later-line settings, though the non-monotonic OS trend with triplets suggests limited benefit beyond doublets and is consistent with guidelines favoring sequential doublet-based strategies. The response-based interaction analysis suggested that synergy is concentrated among cytotoxic doublets — particularly anthracycline–taxane and alkylator–anthracycline pairings — while antagonistic combinations followed no predictable class-level pattern. These response-derived signals are hypothesis-generating and warrant confirmation through dedicated pharmacodynamic or clinical evaluation of the specific drug pairs. Finally, the escalating influence of baseline ECOG PS from ORR through PFS to OS indicates that patient fitness increasingly relates to outcomes along the disease trajectory, warranting further study of its role in treatment selection and prognostication. Together, these exploratory, arm-level findings can inform endpoint selection, guide rational combination screening, and provide context for interpreting cross-trial efficacy comparisons across the MBC treatment landscape. Future research using individual patient data and expanding this framework to novel targeted therapies will further clarify these results and evaluate their applicability to the evolving MBC treatment landscape.
