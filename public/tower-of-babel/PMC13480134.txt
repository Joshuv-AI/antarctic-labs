Acute Pericarditis After Targeted Kinase Inhibitor Therapy in Acute Leukemia
Authors: Marina Pérez Fernández, Ana Rosa Carranco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) inhibitors improve outcomes in acute myeloid leukemia cases but may cause cardiovascular toxicity. Inflammatory complications are rarely described.Case SummaryA 56-year-old man with FLT3-mutated acute myeloid leukemia developed acute pleuritic chest pain 3 days after the initiation of FLT3 inhibitor therapy. Electrocardiogram showed diffuse ST-segment elevation, and inflammatory markers were elevated (C-reactive protein 244 mg/L; normal <5 mg/L). Echocardiography revealed mild pericardial effusion. No viral prodrome or infectious cause was identified. Renal function and leukocyte count allowed standard therapy. The drug was discontinued, and treatment with ibuprofen and colchicine led to rapid resolution.DiscussionFLT3 inhibitors have been associated with cardiotoxicity although pericardial inflammation is uncommon. This case supports a probable drug-related mechanism.Take-Home MessagesFLT3 inhibitors may cause acute pericarditis. Early recognition and drug withdrawal are essential for recovery.

History of Presentation

A 56-year-old man with acute myeloid leukemia, French-American-British subtype M5, harboring NPM1 mutation and FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) internal tandem duplication, was admitted electively for consolidation chemotherapy and initiation of FLT3 inhibitor (midostaurin). Baseline electrocardiogram (Figure 1) and transthoracic echocardiography were normal.Figure 1Acute Pericarditis

Acute Pericarditis

Three days after starting midostaurin, the patient developed acute pleuritic chest pain which was partially relieved by sitting forward and associated with mild dyspnea. There was no prior cardiovascular history. No viral prodrome or infectious cause was identified.

Investigations

Electrocardiogram demonstrated diffuse ST-segment elevation without reciprocal changes. High-sensitivity troponin levels were normal. C-reactive protein was elevated (244 mg/L; normal <5 mg/L) (Figure 2).Figure 2Baseline Electrocardiogram

Baseline Electrocardiogram

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed mild circumferential pericardial effusion without hemodynamic compromise.

Computed tomography angiography excluded pulmonary embolism and aortic dissection. No microbiological evidence of infection was identified. The patient denied recent viral prodrome symptoms.

Renal function was preserved (creatinine 1.01 mg/dL), and leukocyte count was 0.55 × 103/μL.

Management

Given the temporal relationship with FLT3 inhibitor initiation and the absence of alternative causes, drug-induced pericarditis was suspected.

FLT3 inhibitor therapy was discontinued. Anti-inflammatory treatment with ibuprofen (600 mg every 8 hours) and colchicine (0.5 mg twice a day, weight-adjusted) was initiated.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Symptoms resolved rapidly after treatment initiation. Follow-up electrocardiogram normalized, and repeat echocardiography showed complete resolution of the pericardial effusion.

Renal function remained stable throughout the follow-up period; therefore, colchicine therapy was continued for 3 months, while anti-inflammatory treatment was progressively tapered according to European Society of Cardiology guideline recommendations.

Discussion

Midostaurin is a multikinase inhibitor targeting FLT3, KIT proto-oncogene receptor tyrosine kinase, and platelet-derived growth factor receptor pathways and is approved for FLT3-mutated acute myeloid leukemia. Cardiovascular adverse effects are uncommon and primarily include QT prolongation and ventricular dysfunction.1, 2, 3 Inflammatory cardiac complications are rarely reported, although emerging evidence suggests a possible association between FLT3 inhibitors and pericardial or myocardial inflammation.

Recent case-based evidence supports this signal. Kalkan et al.4 described a case of severe perimyocarditis associated with midostaurin, highlighting a potentially life-threatening inflammatory cardiotoxicity and reviewing previously reported cases. In addition, pharmacovigilance data from the World Health Organization database have identified cardiovascular adverse events associated with midostaurin, including inflammatory manifestations, further supporting a possible drug-related effect.5

FLT3 signaling influences immune cell activation and cytokine pathways. Its inhibition may dysregulate inflammatory cascades and promote serosal inflammation. In addition, off-target kinase inhibition may disrupt cardiomyocyte survival signaling and trigger localized inflammatory responses.6, 7, 8

Causality assessment in this case supports a probable adverse drug reaction. The temporal relationship, exclusion of alternative etiologies, and positive dechallenge fulfill key pharmacovigilance criteria. Although rechallenge was not ethically feasible, the pattern aligns with previously reported cases of perimyocardial inflammation associated with FLT3 inhibition. Compared with prior reports, our case presented predominantly as isolated pericarditis without significant myocardial involvement, suggesting that the clinical spectrum may range from mild pericardial inflammation to more severe perimyocarditis.4

Management followed current recommendations from the European Society of Cardiology, which support nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and colchicine as first-line therapy. In refractory cases, interleukin-1 inhibition may be considered. Agents such as anakinra and rilonacept target the interleukin-1-mediated inflammatory cascade, which plays a central role in pericardial inflammation.

This case expands the phenotype of targeted therapy cardiotoxicity beyond arrhythmic and myocardial dysfunction toward inflammatory manifestations. Recognition is particularly important in cardio-oncology practice, where multiple competing etiologies for chest pain coexist, and awareness of this potential association may facilitate earlier diagnosis and management.

Conclusions

Acute pericarditis may represent a rare manifestation of FLT3 inhibitor-associated cardiotoxicity. Prompt recognition and drug withdrawal, combined with anti-inflammatory therapy, can result in complete recovery.
