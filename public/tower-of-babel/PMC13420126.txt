Disproportionality analysis of off-label intravitreal bevacizumab in the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System database
Authors: Homero Contreras-Salinas, Janet Cristina Vázquez-Beltrán, Gisela García-Sánchez, Sebastian Quirarte-Justo, Sarahí del Carmen Gómez-Macías, Lourdes Yolotzin Rodríguez-Herrera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Bevacizumab, an anti-vascular endothelial growth factor agent initially approved for cancer treatment, is widely used off-label in retinal vascular diseases; however, this use may carry safety risks, particularly when evidence from controlled clinical trials is limited. In this context, real-world evidence derived from large pharmacovigilance databases plays a critical role in evaluating its safety profile.Objectives:Evaluate the safety profile of off-label intravitreal bevacizumab for the treatment of retinal vascular diseases using the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database.Design:Disproportionality analysis using data mining of the FAERS database.Methods:Individual case safety reports (ICSRs) of intravitreal bevacizumab from Q1 2015 to Q2 2025 were extracted, and data mining methods (reporting odds ratio, proportional reporting ratio and Bayesian confidence propagation neural network) were applied to identify statistical disproportionality. In addition, a comparative risk analysis (odds ratio) was performed against ranibizumab.Results:Of the 1495 ICSRs (5232 adverse events (AEs)), 114 preferred terms were statistically significant in the disproportionality analyses; 52% were expected, 15% were related to lack of efficacy and 33% were unexpected. Furthermore, the comparative analysis suggests a higher risk of serious cardiovascular, ocular and systemic events with ranibizumab.Conclusion:Most of the disproportionately reported AEs associated with bevacizumab were expected or related to product handling, underscoring the importance of using approved single-dose formulations. While this analysis suggests a lower risk of serious systemic AEs with bevacizumab than with ranibizumab, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as disproportionality analyses based on spontaneous reporting systems cannot confirm causal associations. Further evidence is needed to confirm the observed associations.Keywords: adverse event, bevacizumab, disproportional analyses, FAERS, off-label, ranibizumab

Introduction

Neovascular ocular diseases are significant causes of vision loss worldwide.1,2 These conditions are characterized by abnormal growth of new blood vessels and increased vascular permeability, a process driven mainly by vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF).2–5

Currently, standard treatment relies on anti-VEGF agents.6,7 These drugs bind specifically to VEGF, neutralize its activity, and block its interaction with endothelial cell receptors, thereby reducing pathological vascularization.4,5,8 Bevacizumab, a humanized anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody originally approved for cancer treatment, is widely used off-label for intravitreal injections in retinal vascular diseases.3,4,8–10

The extensive off-label use of bevacizumab is supported by clinical effectiveness and favourable economic considerations. Although no differences in serious adverse events (AEs) or mortality have been observed between anti-VEGF agents and bevacizumab,2,11 some clinical trials have reported increased risk for ocular AEs compared with approved alternatives.8,9 These risks may arise from the diverse and unvalidated methods used for preparing, storing and handling bevacizumab.1,8

Off-label drug use can address unmet medical needs and bridge gaps between research and regulatory approval. 12 However, studies indicated that off-label use is often associated with new AEs, as seen with bevacizumab.8,9,11,13 This highlights the inherent risks of using medications for unregulated indications, particularly when there is insufficient information.13,14 When controlled clinical trials do not yield sufficient data, real-world evidence (RWE) generated through large databases becomes essential. 15 An example is the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), which collects Individual Case Safety Reports (ICSRs) and includes over 28 million AEs reports as of 2023. 16

FAERS data are primarily used for post-marketing surveillance. Insights from real-world reports are crucial for characterizing a drug’s benefit–risk profile over its lifecycle.16,17 This information improves understanding of drug safety,18,19 particularly for off-label drugs like bevacizumab, whose use in retinal diseases has increased in recent years.12,20,21 Such widespread off-label use raises important questions regarding systemic effects, optimal dosages for specific populations and ocular safety.12,20,22

Although the first intravitreal formulation of bevacizumab for neovascular age-related macular degeneration, Lytenava® (Outlook Therapeutics, New Jersey, USA) (approved by the European Medicines Agency in May 2025), 23 is now available, there is still no information regarding its use in uncontrolled populations, and it has not yet received official FDA approval. Therefore, off-label bevacizumab pharmacovigilance is crucial for better understanding its safety profile in real-world clinical practice. We aim to perform an off-label safety analysis of intravitreal bevacizumab utilizing the FAERS database.

Methods

This study is a pharmacovigilance disproportionality analysis based on spontaneous ICSRs retrieved from the FAERS database and was conducted in accordance with the Reporting of Disproportionality Analysis for Drug Safety Signal Detection Using Individual Case Safety Reports in Pharmacovigilance (READUS-PV) statement. 24

Data processing

Data from the first quarter of 2015 (Q1 2015) to the second quarter of 2025 (Q2 2025) were downloaded from the FAERS database, which is publicly available in American Standard Code for Information Interchange (ASCII) format through the official FDA website. 25 The downloaded data consist of several tables: DEMO, which contains patient demographic and administrative information; DRUG, which includes details of drugs and biological products; REAC, which contains reported AEs; OUTC, which provides for clinical outcomes; RPSR, which contains the sources of the reports; THER, which includes the start and end dates of drug therapy; INDI, which contains the indications for use or associated diagnoses; and DELETE (available from Q1 2019 onwards), which provides cases deleted by the FDA or the drug registration holder. Subsequently, all files were imported and managed in RStudio software v. 4.3.3 (Posit, Massachusetts, USA). For processing and analysis. Analogous files from all quarters were merged using key identifiers. R packages implemented were ‘data.table’ for fast aggregation of large datasets, 26 ‘dplyr’ for data wrangling, 27 ‘ggplot2’ for data visualizations 28 and ‘openxlsx’ to write Excel files. 29

De-duplication and filtering

Duplicate records were removed in three sequential steps. First, the case identifiers (caseids) listed in the DELETE table were deleted. Second, the de-duplication procedure described by Banda et al. 30 was applied. This procedure filters the DEMO table based on the case identifier (caseid), the date the report was received by the FDA (fda_dt) and the unique submission identifier (primaryid), retaining only the most recent version for each caseid. Third, reports containing bibliographic references in the lit_ref column of the DEMO table, corresponding to cases from the scientific literature, were excluded.

The data were filtered by prod_ai as bevacizumab and role_cod as primary suspect (PS). For the appropriate filtering of neovascular ocular diseases, the INDI table was filtered by the indi_pt column based on the following indications: ‘Age-related macular degeneration’, ‘Diabetic retinal oedema’, ‘Choroidal neovascularization’, ‘Neovascular age-related macular degeneration’ and ‘Retinal neovascularization’ to avoid bias in the analysis of other therapeutic indications in the role_cod column (Figure 1).

Categorization

All AEs were categorized into preferred term (PT) and System Organ Class (SOC) according to MedDRA version 28.1. (Virginia, USA).

The determination of whether an AE was expected or unexpected was based on the summary of product characteristics (SmPC) for Lytenava®, 31 which served as the reference document for expected AEs associated with intravitreal administration.

Statistical methods

For the selected ICSRs, demographic data (gender, age ± SD, and reporter) and notifications per year were filtered in the DEMO table. Additionally, a 2 × 2 contingency table was constructed for PT data mining, including only data with a frequency of more than three reports (threshold). Various disproportional analyses were applied to bevacizumab, including the FDR-adjusted chi-square tests (q-value (q) < 0.05), reporting odds ratio (ROR) ± CI (ROR lower CI > 1), proportional reporting ratio (PRR) ± CI (PRR lower CI > 1), and Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN) + IC025 (IC025 > 0; Supplemental Data 1). The statistical methods and signal-detection thresholds are in accordance with international guidelines.32,33

Additionally, a descriptive analysis was performed, filtering the medications used in conjunction with bevacizumab and categorizing them as secondary suspect (SS) in the DRUG table.

Sensitivity analysis

To address the influence of co-exposure to multiple anti-VEGF agents, a sensitivity analysis was performed that excluded ICSRs involving more than one anti-VEGF agent, restricting the analysis to bevacizumab monotherapy cases. The DRUG table for bevacizumab was filtered by prod_ai to identify anti-VEGF agents as ‘SS’ or ‘Concomitant (C)’: ‘ranibizumab’, ‘aflibercept’, ‘faricimab-svoa’, ‘faricimab’, ‘brolucizumab-dbll’, ‘pegaptanib’, ‘conbercept’ and ‘abicipar pegol’. Subsequently, all ICSRs containing any of these anti-VEGF agents were excluded from the analysis. Only cases in which bevacizumab was reported without other anti-VEGF exposure were retained for the disproportional analysis.

Comparative demographics of bevacizumab and ranibizumab

To assess population differences that could bias the analysis, we compared the demographic characteristics (age, gender and adults aged 65 or older) of patients treated with bevacizumab and ranibizumab. For comparisons between groups, quantitative variables were analysed using Welch’s t-test, with effect size estimated by Cohen’s d. Qualitative variables were analysed using the chi-squared test, with effect size assessed using odds ratios with 95% CI and Cramér’s V.

Comparative risk analysis between bevacizumab and ranibizumab

Two comparative risk analyses were performed: PT and SOCs associated with bevacizumab compared to those observed with another anti-VEGF agent, such as ranibizumab. The ranibizumab as PS in the database was filtered using the methodology described earlier. The frequency of more than one report was used for the 2 × 2 contingency table. The q-value and the base-10 logarithm of the odds ratio (log10OR) with confidence intervals (CI) were used as contrast measures.

Demographics

Analysis of ICSRs related to off-label intravitreal bevacizumab use revealed that most cases involved women (49.1%), followed by men (34.9%), while the sex was unspecified in 15.9% of instances. The primary reporters were physicians and consumers, with similar proportions (39.2% and 39.1%, respectively), followed by other health professionals (16.0%) and pharmacists (2.5%). Reports from lawyers or unknown sources were less frequent (1.1% and 2.0%, respectively). Most reports were from the United States (57.9%), followed by Canada (13.5%) and Italy (10.5%), while the remainder were distributed among other, less-represented countries (Figure 2). A sustained increase in reporting was observed over time, especially since 2016, peaking in 2024 (17.9%) (Figure 3). Of the total 1495 Individual Case Safety Reports (ICSRs), 45% (n = 672) included medications that could have contributed to the reported AEs, in addition to bevacizumab (also known as SS). Ranibizumab was the most frequently used (n = 378, 25.3%). It was followed by faricimab (n = 75, 5.0%), brolucizumab (n = 22, 1.5%), and aflibercept (n = 16, 1.1%). Other suspected medications were less common, primarily consisting of drugs from various therapeutic classes (n = 181, 12.1%).

Data mining

Using multiple disproportionality analyses, 114 PTs showed statistically significant disproportionality for intravitreal bevacizumab. The most prominent disproportionalities were observed for vitritis infective (ROR = 4615.31; PRR = 2865; IC025 = 11.48; q < 0.001), iris injury (ROR = 1505.26; PRR = 1254.55; IC025 = 10.29; q < 0.001), and subretinal fluid (ROR = 714.25; PRR = 653.09; IC025 = 9.33; q < 0.001), followed by ciliary hyperemia, diabetic retinal oedema, and endophthalmitis (Supplemental Data 2).

Unexpected AEs

Of the 114 statistically significant PTs, 59 (52%) were classified as expected, 17 (15%) related to drug efficacy issues, and 38 (33%) as unexpected based on the bevacizumab intravitreal SmPC for Lytenava®. 31 The unexpected categorized EAs include the following: problems related to the procedure or product, vascular changes of the retina, lens changes, other eye problems, neurological, vascular events and angiopathies, and other non-ocular (Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis

In the sensitivity analysis, 3454 AEs were identified (−33.9% without filtering). Additionally, 78 PTs met the disproportionate criteria, with a 31.6% reduction from the original analysis. Among these, 74 PTs remained with a disproportionate number after excluding concomitant anti-VEGF treatment, suggesting that the primary data mining remains robust. However, four new significant PTs emerged in the monotherapy analysis. Besides, 34 PTs no longer met the disproportionate criteria after filtering concomitant anti-VEGF therapies (Table 2).

Demographic analysis of bevacizumab versus ranibizumab

The mean age was 72.5 ± 12.7 years with bevacizumab and 72.4 ± 15.3 years with ranibizumab. This difference was not statistically significant (Welch’s t-test: p = 0.837), and the effect size was negligible (Cohen’s d = 0.007), indicating no significant age difference between the groups. Statistically significant differences were observed in the distribution by sex (58.4% women in the bevacizumab group vs 54.7% in the ranibizumab group; χ2 = 6.14, p = 0.013) and in the proportion of patients ⩾65 years of age (77.8% vs 73.1%; χ2 = 6.70, p = 0.010), with bevacizumab being more frequently associated with reports from older patients compared with ranibizumab. However, the associated effect sizes remained insignificant, as indicated by the odds ratios (sex: OR = 1.16 (95% CI: 1.03–1.31); age ⩾65: OR = 1.29 (95% CI: 1.07–1.56)) and Cramér’s V coefficients (V = 0.023 and V = 0.031, respectively; Table 3). These coefficients suggest a very weak association, indicating that the statistical significance is likely due to the high statistical power provided by the large sample size.

Comparison with ranibizumab

In the analysis of both drugs, the most frequently reported outcome fell under other important medical event, being the most frequent for both bevacizumab (51.84%) and ranibizumab (44.06%). Nevertheless, relevant differences were observed between the two drugs; deaths were considerably more frequent with ranibizumab (20.34%) compared to bevacizumab (2.41%), as were hospitalizations (13.37% vs 10.17%) and life-threatening events (1.25% vs 0.47%). On the other hand, bevacizumab had a higher proportion of cases associated with disability (5.89% vs 2.92%) and those requiring medical intervention (1.54% vs 0.04%). Reports of congenital anomalies were uncommon in both groups (<0.1%). Cases with no important medical events accounted for 27.63% with bevacizumab and 18.00% with ranibizumab.

Analysis by PTs

Head-to-head analysis suggests a higher risk of serious cardiovascular, ocular, and systemic events for ranibizumab such as death (logOR = −1.26; q < 0.001), cerebrovascular accident (logOR = −0.47; q < 0.001), macular oedema (logOR = −0.47; q < 0.001), myocardial infarction (logOR = −0.58; q < 0.001), and vitreous haemorrhage (logOR = −0.31; q = 0.023). Discussion

Safety analysis of intravitreal bevacizumab

This study provides a pharmacovigilance overview of disproportionate AEs associated with off-label intravitreal bevacizumab reported to FAERS. The analysis revealed a broad range of AEs, including ocular and systemic reactions, with most events classified as expected according to the SmPC, followed by unexpected or ineffective outcomes. Although bevacizumab’s efficacy is well supported in clinical trials, its off-label administration from multidose vials and non-pre-filled syringes introduces safety and efficacy risks. This analysis highlights the need to distinguish intrinsic pharmacologic risks from issues related to product interpretation of post-marketing disproportionality signals from spontaneous reporting systems.

AEs such as suspected transmission of an infectious agent through the product, inadequate aseptic technique during product use, product contamination, product counterfeit, product use issue, suspected counterfeit product, and procedural complications have been documented in the literature, particularly in association with prepared or repackaged bevacizumab used for intravitreal administration.34–38 Multiple infectious ophthalmic processes have been attributed to contaminated vials prepared in compounding pharmacies, underscoring the risks associated with the use of multidose vials and off-label compounding practices.36,37 Reports of falsified bevacizumab vials distributed through unvalidated supply chains further highlight the vulnerabilities in product distribution and handling. 35 These findings suggest that inadequate aseptic technique and improper handling of multidose formulations represent a significant source of unexpected events.

AEs, such as cataract and cataract nuclear, have been observed following intravitreal administration. Although cataract formation is not an expected AE of bevacizumab, mechanical trauma related to the injection procedure or repeated intravitreal injections may contribute to lens damage,34,39,40 indicating the possibility of inadvertent lens trauma or local effects associated with the injection technique.

Retinal vascular events, including retinal aneurysm and diabetic retinopathy, have also been reported as unexpected events. Although bevacizumab is used therapeutically to inhibit neovascularization in diabetic retinopathy, paradoxical progression or worsening of vascular lesions has occasionally been observed.34,41 Diverse authors indicate these retinal events underlying disease progression, individual variability in VEGF suppression or misclassification of outcomes as AEs rather than a lack of efficacy.37,42

Some vascular events and angiopathies showed a statistically significant association with anti-VEGF exposure; however, the indications analysed in this study mainly comprise age-related and diabetic oedemas, both of which are well-established risk factors for vascular disease. Robust evidence from a meta-analysis of 103 studies demonstrates that patients with diabetes have a substantially increased risk of vascular events. 43 In parallel, a meta-analysis by Rajati et al. 44 reported that the incidence of vascular events increases significantly with age. Moreover, the frequent coexistence of hypertension in both populations further amplifies the risk of ischemic vascular outcomes.44–47 Therefore, the occurrence of angiopathy, transient ischemic attack or cerebrovascular accident in patients treated with bevacizumab should be interpreted within the context of elevated baseline vascular risk related to diabetes, hypertension and aging, rather than as an isolated treatment-related effect. This underscores the importance of comprehensive cardiovascular assessment and strict blood pressure control in patients who are candidates for anti-VEGF therapy.

Other non-ocular and neurological AE categories, such as cranial nerve disorder, third nerve paresis, angiopathy and poor peripheral circulation, are extremely rare following intravitreal bevacizumab administration; however, they are biologically plausible given the systemic absorption of bevacizumab and its established vascular effects when administered systemically.34,41,42 Previous oncology studies have described microvascular complications and central nervous system ischemic events following systemic exposure to bevacizumab,34,42 which may partially explain these unexpected events reported in FAERS.

For other non-ocular AEs, diabetes mellitus inadequate control, increased insulin requirement, cutis laxa, diabetic foot, lymphocyte count increased, myasthenia gravis and thyroid disorder, current evidence does not support a causal association between intravitreal bevacizumab and these events. To date, no published pharmacovigilance studies or clinical trials have reported an association between intravitreal bevacizumab and these outcomes. Existing evidence suggests that most of the unexpected non-ocular AEs observed in patients receiving intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy are primarily attributable to the progression of diabetes rather than to the drug itself.48,49 Likewise, diabetic foot is better explained as a manifestation of chronic micro- and macrovascular diabetic complications rather than a drug effect, as bevacizumab is not known to cause peripheral vasoconstrictive or neuropathic toxicity.50,51 Similarly, cutis laxa has not been linked to anti-VEGF agents and is usually associated with autoimmune or inflammatory conditions, which are common in patients with diabetes. 52 The increase in lymphocyte count observed in some reports also lacks established causality. One study evaluating inflammatory biomarkers in patients with diabetic macular oedema found differences in lymphocyte and monocyte counts, but no clear evidence of systemic lymphoproliferation or immune activation. 53 Additionally, thyroid disorder may be associated with diabetes through mechanisms including disruption of the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis, changes in thyroid-stimulating hormone secretion, peripheral conversion of T4 to T3, metabolic stress and interference from antidiabetic medication. However, this relationship has been extensively studied, with inconclusive evidence.54,55 Overall, these findings suggest that most of these unexpected systemic events are more consistent with underlying disease rather than true cause–effect associations.56,57 Still, they provide a basis for future research to investigate whether these events may be associated.

Given the presence of other anti-VEGF agents, as secondary or concomitant treatments, and the potential risk of confusion when determining the causes of AEs when patients have been treated with multiple drugs with similar mechanisms of action, a sensitivity analysis was conducted and showed that the overall pattern of bevacizumab disproportion remained largely unchanged, supporting the robustness of the main findings. However, some terms that were previously statistically significant were no longer significant when focusing solely on bevacizumab use; this suggests that these disproportions may be influenced by concurrent use of other anti-VEGF agents, potentially leading to overlapping AEs (Table 2). Additionally, new disproportionation emerged, while statistically significant, should be interpreted with caution due to the exploratory nature of the sub-analysis, the low frequency of cases, and the borderline significance levels (PRR(−) and ROR(−) > 1.3; Supplemental Data 3). These findings indicate that concomitant anti-VEGF therapies may be a relevant confounding factor in analyses based on databases such as FAERS, underscoring the need for sensitivity analyses to improve the specificity of the results.

Comparative risk analysis

The higher frequency of serious AEs observed in our study in patients treated with ranibizumab is consistent with the findings of the RISE and RIDE trials, which reported a higher incidence of serious AEs, such as central nervous system haemorrhage, congestive heart failure, hypertension, gastrointestinal perforation, proteinuria and impaired wound healing, in the ranibizumab-treated groups58,59; although the IVAN trial observed more atherothrombotic events and heart failure in the bevacizumab group, this was not statistically significant 60 ; likewise the meta-analysis by Virgili et al. 61 did not identify significant differences in the incidence of serious AEs across anti-VEGF treatments. On the other hand, the CATT study reported a higher proportion of serious systemic AEs in patients treated with bevacizumab versus ranibizumab (24.1% vs 19.0%), 9 and a subsequent meta-analysis described a higher frequency of some serious systemic AEs such as infections and gastrointestinal disturbances with this agent, which contrasts with our findings. 2

While the most recent meta-analyses did not show statistically significant differences in the most serious AEs for either drug, nor in comparisons with other anti-VEGF agents,11,61 most clinical trials and meta-analyses have reported inconsistent results when comparing bevacizumab and ranibizumab regarding the risks of mortality and arterial thrombotic events (acute myocardial infarction and cerebrovascular events).2,9,11,34,58,60 However, our study observed higher relative risks and odds ratios for death, myocardial infarction, stroke and hypertension for ranibizumab.

On the contrary, bevacizumab showed a disproportionate risk related to product handling and off-label use, such as contamination, poor drug quality, suspected transmission of infectious agents and post-procedure complications. Because bevacizumab is used off-label, it requires handling before intravitreal administration and carries additional risks compared to ranibizumab (previously evaluated).62,63 Furthermore, reports of therapeutic inefficacy, such as subretinal fluid, retinal oedema, macular oedema, and visual impairment, should be interpreted with caution. These could reflect natural disease progression or variations in individual response; furthermore, in head-to-head trials, bevacizumab has shown efficacy equivalent to that of ranibizumab.9,60,64

The existing literature suggests that the systemic and ocular safety profiles of bevacizumab and ranibizumab are similar but with relevant distinctions. The variations observed in this analysis may reflect differences in the exposed population, clinical use conditions and factors related to off-label administration of bevacizumab. Nonetheless, we found that bevacizumab and ranibizumab were reported in broadly comparable populations within FAERS. Although bevacizumab was statistically significant in elderly patients (⩾65 years), the effect size was negligible (Cramér’s V = 0.031). Therefore, the demographic data analysed are unlikely to explain the observed differences in AE profiles. Regardless, FAERS does not capture the full comorbidity burden, and confounding by unmeasured clinical factors remains a limitation.

However, it is crucial to emphasize that most studies used to contrast our research are based on clinical trials performed in controlled populations. The discrepancies observed may stem from factors not analysed in clinical trials. Therefore, further research on real-world evidence regarding bevacizumab and ranibizumab is recommended. This is necessary to determine whether the observed differences in results are due to non-causal relationships with the drugs, as intrinsic characteristics of the study subjects, or limitations of the FAERS database.

Limitations

FAERS is an essential tool for early detection of safety signals; however, it has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its results. First, presenting variables such as drug names and dosages in free-text format increases the risk of analysis errors. Additionally, incomplete reporting, in which only the suspected drug and AEs are documented while omitting critical information such as dosage, treatment duration, comorbidities, concomitant medications and causality, can occur. In addition, because FAERS is essentially a passive notification system, reporters’ perceptions of cause and effect can influence whether they report, making it challenging to establish direct causal relationships between a drug and a reported event. Other contributing factors include differences in regulatory policy requirements across countries, the evolution of coding dictionaries and inconsistencies in database structures. These variations can lead to temporal and geographical differences in the data. Furthermore, ranibizumab, an FDA-approved ophthalmic drug, may be subject to different regulatory scrutiny and reporting standards than an off-label product like bevacizumab. In summary, the analysis of these factors primarily generates hypotheses rather than confirms risks.

Conclusion

The findings indicate that most statistically disproportionate AEs were consistent with those reported in clinical trials and expected according to the product monograph. In turn, unexpected events were primarily attributed to factors related to the handling or intravitreal administration of bevacizumab rather than to its pharmacological toxicity. Hence, ocular formulations of bevacizumab in single-dose vials for intravitreal administration are recommended to reduce the risk of handling-related AEs.

In our comparative risk analysis, we found that bevacizumab is associated with a lower frequency of serious systemic AEs than ranibizumab. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as previous studies do not support a superior systemic safety for bevacizumab. Some meta-analyses have reported no statistically significant serious AEs associated with bevacizumab or ranibizumab. This discrepancy between our results and previous findings underscores the need for real-world investigations, as these are often excluded from meta-analyses. Such studies will be essential to determine whether the lower risk observed with bevacizumab represents a systemic safety advantage or is influenced by variations in clinical practice. Clarifying this issue is critical for decision-making and improving patient safety.
